Abstract
We simulate the dynamics of pelagic Sargassum rafts as systems of finite-size floating particles, governed by a Maxey–
Riley law with nonlinear elastic interactions. Using surface ocean currents and wind data from reanalysis systems for
clump transport, we computed trajectories within a domain covering the tropical and subtropical north Atlantic. The
subsequent motion is reduced using Ulam’s discretization method into a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain that simulates
a background Sargassum concentration. Bayesian inversion, combined with nonautonomous transition path theory, was
used to infer the origin of the first significant recorded bloom in the tropical North Atlantic, which unfolded in April
2011. Both methodologies independently identified the bloom’s origin as near the West African coast, up to two years
before it was detectable via satellite imagery on the basin’s western side. This finding supports anecdotal evidence of
Sargassum strandings on the Ghanaian coast in 2009. Moreover, it correlates with unusual environmental conditions—such
as increased nutrient loads from significant upwelling linked to a pronounced Dakar Niña and Saharan dust deposition—
that promote bloom proliferation. Additionally, it aligns with the observation that the species of Sargassum in the 2011
bloom differ from those in the Sargasso Sea, which might otherwise be considered a natural origin.
Key words: Sargassum blooms, Maxey–Riley, Markov chain, Bayesian inversion, transition path theory
Significance statement
The debated origin of Sargassum seaweed blooms in the North Atlantic, first noted in 2011, is explored using a new model. We treat
Sargassum as elastic particle groups interacting with ocean currents and winds. By creating a time-inhomogeneous Markov chain,
we estimate concentrations and trace the bloom origins to West Africa, consistent with 2009 reports from Ghana. Our results, using
Bayesian inversion and transition path theory, diverge from the idea that blooms started in the Sargasso Sea, and they align more
closely with environmental conditions linked to a Niña-like event, where anomalous surface water cooling is accompanied by upwelling.
This approach offers clarity on the differences in Sargassum species origins.

Introduction
The translocation of Sargassum, a significant species of brown algae
within marine ecosystems, to the tropical North Atlantic region
presents a compelling study of ocean–atmosphere interactions.
Recent studies (41; 43) highlight the beginnings of the 2011 bloom
(32), which has recurred since and been termed the Great Atlantic
Sargassum Belt (GASB) (80), situated in the Sargasso Sea, where
abundance has historically been high (45). A significant anomaly
in the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) (38) in 2009–2010 is
believed to have disrupted the weak link between the subtropical and
tropical North Atlantic (9), allowing Sargassum to be transported
southward. However, evidence suggests a potential southern origin:
anecdotal documentation of arrivals of Sargassum in the coastal
areas of Ghana in 2009 by Addicod and deGraft (1), overlooked
by the oceanographic community, calls for consideration of an
unexplored Sargassum bloom origin. This paper aims to investigate
this using a novel mechanistic model for the transport of Sargassum
and the application of a probabilistic framework which facilitates
origin (source) inversion and the framing of eﬀicient communication
channels in the flow domain.
Specifically, in distinct departure from the “leeway” modeling
approach (14) of the previous studies (41; 43), we simulate the
dynamics of pelagic Sargassum rafts through the modeling of such
systems as finite-sized floating particles, or clumps, which evolve
under a Maxey–Riley equation while subject to nonlinear elastic
interactions. The model, as developed in Bonner et al. (13), is herein
designated as eBOMB. The motion described by the Maxey–Riley
equation within eBOMB is derived from the extension of the classic
fluid mechanics equation (54) adapted to the oceanographic context
(8; 67), with the “inertial” particles carried in flow created by the
combined effects of ocean currents and winds. The Maxey–Riley
oceanographic equation has been successfully validated both in situ
(62) and under controlled laboratory conditions (61). The elastic
interactions within eBOMB offer enhancements over a previous
model based on Hooke’s law (7). For an overview of these and
additional inquiries, the interested readers are directed to Beron-
Vera (5).
Using surface ocean currents and wind data from reanalysis
systems, the flow carrying the Sargassum clumps is determined.
Trajectories are then computed by integrating eBOMB within
a domain covering the tropical and subtropical regions of the
North

surface
ocean.
These

a stochastic process where the probability of transitioning from
state i to state j at time t depends only on i, j, and t, and
not on the history of the process before t. This simplification
moves away from the generically chaotic nature of trajectories,
creating a framework to determine the origin of Sargassum blooms
where probability densities play a central role (48). This form
of reduction has been used within oceanographic contexts before
(55; 78; 26) and more recently (58; 10), especially in studying
Sargassum connectivity (9; 12). Bayesian inversion is then used to
infer the origin of the first significant documented bloom in the
tropical North Atlantic, observed in April 2011 adjacent to the
western boundary of the basin. This is paired with a specialized
application of the nonautonomous extension (35) of transition
path theory (TPT) for stationary processes (22), which thoroughly
characterizes nonequilibrium transitions while minimizing detours
between distinct regions within a flow domain, thus identifying
the

the Ghanaian coast in 2009. Additionally, unusual environmental
conditions linked to a strong Dakar Niña (66) were observed
around 2009, creating favorable conditions for bloom development
in the presence of baseline concentrations of Sargassum species in
the tropical North Atlantic, differing from that prevailing in the
subtropics.
Modeling Sargassum Motion
First Principles Framework. In the eBOMB model (13), a clump
of Sargassum is the central element, influenced by ocean currents
and winds. A clump essentially represents a group of Sargassum,
conceptualized as a solid sphere with a small radius. A network
of such clumps that interact with each other is termed raft, and
it is assumed that the motion of a large amount of Sargassum
can be accurately depicted by the movement of these discrete
clumps. At any given time, the entire Sargassum collective being
studied operates as a raft, although it might consist of numerous
independent clump networks. The eBOMB model consists of three
principal components: clump dynamics governed by coupled Maxey–
Riley equations, nonlinear stiffness spring forces connecting clumps,
and a biological model that governs clump growth and decay, which
is not considered in this paper.
The choice to overlook biological interactions stems from their
relatively limited understanding compared to physical interactions.
This paper seeks to uncover the distant origins of observed blooms,
which are expected to be mainly influenced by physical constraints.
Although physiological changes in Sargassum affect its biomass,
they are expected to be less consequential than transport processes
for this study,
with ambient biogechemical conditions mainly
influencing bloom initiation. The primary consideration is the life
expectancy of Sargassum, which is much better constrained than
the intricate details of the life cycles. The potential longevity of
Sargassum can reasonably be argued to extend over several years
due to its clonal (vegetative) reproduction, with thallus sections
persisting for multiple months before fragmentation, resulting in an
effectively indefinite lifespan (50).

The focus of our analysis is on the first significant recorded bloom
in the tropical North Atlantic, which occurred in April 2011. This
bloom’s location is indicated by the colored square patches in the
top-left panel of Fig. 1. The patch locations were inferred from
areas where the satellite-derived monthly cumulative density of
Sargassum was highest in April 2011, as shown in the animation
included in the Supplementary Material of Wang et al. (81). The
density of Sargassum primarily peaks in a large western area of
the tropical North Atlantic basin. However, there is also a small
region of high Sargassum density located off the southern coast

The other panels of

representing a distribution over the boxes intersecting the observed
bloom region. This forward evolution is achieved by left multiplying
d with the nonautonomous transition matrix P(k), given in (7),
constructed using Sargassum clump trajectories that satisfy the
eBOMB model equations (1). The ocean currents and winds are
sourced from the ECMWF (European Centre for Medium-Range
Weather Forecasts) ORAS5 (Ocean Reanalysis System 5) based

permitting horizontal resolution of 0.25◦and a near-surface vertical
resolution of 1 meter, offering near real-time daily monitoring.
ERA5 provides hourly global estimates on a 31 km grid, resolving
the atmosphere with 137 levels from the surface up to 80 km.
The eBOMB parameters were acquired through optimization, as
reported in Table 2 of Bonner et al. (13) This process resulted in a
windage parameter value (α ≈0.34%) consistent with laboratory
measurements (68).
The integration is accomplished using the
Julia code Sargassum.jl, which integrates eBOMB on the sphere
and accounts for the effects of beaching and raft disaggregation.
The partition boxes have a side length of 1.5◦and the transition
increment ∆t = 1 week, which guarantees suﬀicient memory loss into
the past for the Markovian assumption to hold approximately (44).
The transition matrix computation is based on the straightforward
use of Matlab’s histcounts2.m, which gave us full control on the grid
size and location. This was feasible because our application does not
require the assumption of ergodicity and the need of a dynamic grid
adjustment as implemented in Julia’s UlamMethod.jl and Python’s
pygtm. The evolution of the probability vector d is in good qualitative
agreement with the evolution of observed Sargassum as documented
in Wang et al. (81), supporting both the eBOMB model and the
Markov chain reduction. A distinguishing feature is the development
of a distribution resembling the GASB. An important observation
is that the omission of the box near the African coast in the
initial distribution leads to an incomplete GASB-like distribution
disconnected from the African coast, highlighting the importance of
tracing the origin of the bloom there.

Environmental Conditions Favoring Bloom
Development
Despite the anecdotal report (1) of Sargassum rafts landing on
the shores of Ghana, and the consistent constraints imposed by
Bayesian inversion and TPT analysis on the bloom’s origin in the
nearshore waters of West Africa, a persistent question concerns
the mechanism of bloom initiation. Considering the low probability
of Sargassum being transported into this area, it is hypothesized
that a pre-existing baseline concentrations of Sargassum within the
region evolved into a bloom, facilitated by favorable environmental
conditions produced by a strong “coastal Niña” event.

understood. Pelagic Sargassum species can survive in temperatures

optimal conditions for their growth (34; 19; 52).

Dust and Land Runoff. An additional environmental condition
examined to support the hypothesis is the amount of dust and
other particulate matter in the atmosphere, with the Sahara Desert
in northern Africa being a significant source. This is commonly
measured by the dust extinction aerosol optical depth (AOD) at a
550 nm reference wavelength, which is a unitless measure quantifying
the amount of light blocked by dust particles in the atmosphere. We
analyzed this parameter as produced monthly by EAC4 (ECMWF
Atmospheric Composition Reanalysis 4), a global reanalysis of
atmospheric composition (39). Changes in the observing system,
despite assimilation systems being capable of resolving data gaps,
initially resulted in sparser networks and less accurate estimates,
limiting EAC4 availability to post-2003. The bottom-right panel
of Fig. 5 illustrates AOD in a manner consistent with the other
panels; however, it uses monthly data. AOD is observed to reach its
maximum in 2010. If elevated AOD is taken as a proxy for increased
atmospheric nutrient deposition to the ocean, this could suggest a
mechanism supporting the persistence of Sargassum blooms beyond
2009. Analysis of dust data from 1973 to 2017 in Barbados reveals
a significant decline in dust concentrations from 1982 to 2008, a
trend observed consistently across all four seasons (83). However,
this decline does not persist into the present decade. In particular,
spring-–summer dust mass concentrations have shown increased
interannual variability. Since 2013, most summers have experienced
above-average dust loads, indicating a departure from the earlier
long-term decline. Additionally, Gaston et al. (29), using dust data
from 1990 to 2011 from Barbados as well, reported that nitrate
concentrations remained relatively stable throughout the period,
with the exception of notable elevations during the spring of 2010
and the summer and fall of 2008. An important observation is that
the biogeochemistry model hindcast discussed above was driven only
by climatological dust deposition, and therefore does not account for
interannual variability in atmospheric inputs such as the 2010 AOD
peak.
Another significant source of nutrients that deserves to be
mentioned is the runoff of land from rivers. The biogeochemical
model only accounts for the river input through climatological
averages. However, extreme flooding events, such as those reported
in the Senegal and Niger River basins in 2009 and 2010 (82; 64),
can lead to significant episodic increases in discharge into the ocean,
resulting in nutrient influx.

Summary and Discussion. This study simulates the movement
of Sargassum rafts by modeling them as systems of finite-size
floating particles (clumps) subjected to Maxey–Riley dynamics
and nonlinear elastic interactions.
Surface ocean currents and
wind data from reanalysis systems were utilized to define the
flow transporting these clumps and to compute trajectories across
the tropical and subtropical North Atlantic surface ocean. The
motion described by these trajectories was then reduced through the
application of Ulam’s method, resulting in a time-inhomogeneous
Markov chain. The openness of the flow domain introduces an
imbalance in the probability mass along the chain, which was
addressed by incorporating a virtual state that absorbs this
imbalance and redistributes it uniformly back into the chain,
thus simulating the presence of a background concentration of
Sargassum.
Subsequently,
Bayesian
inversion,
combined
with
nonautonomous transition path theory—a probabilistic approach
enabling the rigorous characterization of nonequilibrium productive
communication
channels
within
the
flow—was
employed
to
determine the origin of the first significant bloom in the tropical
North Atlantic, recorded in April 2011 on the basin’s western side.
Both methodologies consistently identified coastal West African
waters as the source of the bloom. Our results are consistent with
the Sargassum strandings anecdotally reported along the shores of
Ghana in 2009, aligning with and the observation of anomalously
high environmental parameters associated by a strong Dakar Niña
event. These conditions are conducive to bloom stimulation in
the presence of Sargassum species in the baseline concentrations
characteristic of the 2011 bloom.
The results of our research provide a more coherent perspective
compared to earlier “leeway” modeling studies (41; 43), which
identified the origin of Sargassum blooms within the subtropical
North Atlantic, specifically in the Sargasso Sea. A crucial element
that supports our findings is that, in the onset of the tropical blooms,
the dominant species (morphtype) was S. natans VIII. This can be
inferred from a number of references (75; 31; 69; 3; 18), for the
lacking of direct morphtype analyses of 2011 Sargassum samples.
By contrast, S. natans I and S. fluitans III were prevalent in the
Sargasso Sea (70; 75; 31). Moreover, the Sargassum strandings in
the Gulf of Guinea, as noted by Addicod and de Graft (1), occurred
before the extreme NAO event in 2009–2010, which has been linked
to the connectivity between the Sargasso Sea and the tropical North
Atlantic (41; 43). Although a small amount of Sargassum could
have been transported from the Sargasso Sea to the tropical North
Atlantic, differences in bloom characteristics between the tropics
and subtropics—such as dominant morphtypes and abundance—still
need further consideration.

Several authors (37; 16; 53) have previously indicated low

However,
this
view was revised following direct growth measurements of pelagic
Sargassum (45; 34), which reported that Sargassum can double
its biomass in just a few days or weeks under optimal conditions.
Physiological studies (46) and seawater nutrient analyses have shown
that increased nitrogen and phosphorus availability significantly
elevates the productivity of the macroalga in neritic compared
to oceanic waters of the western subtropical North Atlantic.
By comparing samples collected before 2010 with more recent
ones,
Lapointe et al. (47) concluded that increased nitrogen
availability is likely fueling large-scale Sargassum blooms. They
also noted that, given the high nitrogen : phosphorus (N : P) ratio
of Sargassum currently found in the North Atlantic basin, an
excess of soluble reactive phosphorus in upwelled waters could
stimulate further growth in the eastern tropical North Atlantic.
More recently, McGillicuddy et al. (56) noted that the nutritional
status of Sargassum in the tropics (the GASB) is different, with
higher nitrogen and phosphorus contents than populations in the
subtropical habitat of the Sargasso Sea. Changeux et al. (17) from
in situ short-term experiments on Martinique Island concluded that
differences in growth and tissue composition suggest that S. fluitans
III was favored by conditions in the western tropical coastal North
Atlantic. This raises a key question: why was S. natans VIII—rather
than S. natans I and/or S. fluitans III—the dominant species at
the onset of the GASB? A likely explanation is that the background
concentration of S. natans VIII in the region was higher than the
amount of S. natans I and/or S. fluitans III that could have been
transported from the Sargasso Sea.
In conclusion, our results suggest that the 2011 bloom was driven
by
distinct
local
environmental
conditions—including
elevated
nutrient availability in the tropical North Atlantic due to upwelling
off West Africa and Saharan dust deposition there—as well as by
regional ocean circulation dynamics.
