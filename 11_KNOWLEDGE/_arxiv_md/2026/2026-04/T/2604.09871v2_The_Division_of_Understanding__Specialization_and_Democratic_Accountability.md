The Division of Understanding: Specialization and
Democratic Accountability∗
Giampaolo Bonomi†
May 12, 2026
Abstract
This paper studies how the organization of production shapes democratic account-
ability. I propose a model in which learning economies make specialization produc-
tively efficient: most workers perform one-domain tasks, while a small set of inte-
grators with cross-domain knowledge keep the system coherent. When policy con-
sequences run across domains, integrators understand them better than specialists.
Electoral competition then tilts government policies toward integrators’ interests,
while low aggregate system knowledge weakens governance and reduces the fraction
of public resources converted into citizen-valued services. Labor markets leave these
civic margins unpriced, failing to internalize the political returns to system knowl-
edge. Broadening specialists can therefore raise welfare relative to the market allo-
cation. The model speaks to debates on liberal arts education and the effects of AI.

Introduction
Adam Smith’s discussion of the division of labor is usually read as a theory of productiv-
ity. Narrow tasks allow workers to learn by doing, save time in moving between activities,
and discover more efficient ways to perform the work itself. But Smith returned to the
same division of labor in Book V from a different angle: its effect on the citizen. A worker
confined to a few simple operations, he warned, may become “as stupid and ignorant as it
is possible for a human creature to become” and “incapable of judging the great and extensive
interests of his country” (Smith, 1776, Book V, Ch. 1). His point was not that specializa-
tion destroys skill, but rather that a society can produce more occupational skill while
producing less of the kind of understanding needed to judge public affairs.
This paper studies that tension as an economic problem. Can a market economy allo-
cate knowledge efficiently for production but inefficiently for democratic accountability?
I show that it can. Knowledge has a productive return: it raises output, lowers coordina-
tion costs, and is rewarded in labor markets. It also has a political return: it helps citizens
understand policy, monitor government, and evaluate who gains and loses from public
decisions. Firms and workers are paid for the first return. They do not generally capture
the second, because voters are almost never pivotal and their individual understanding
has little impact on how politics plays out in their country. Specialization can therefore
create a civic externality.
How we produce =⇒what we know =⇒how we judge policy =⇒governance quality.
The issue is clearest when the value of a policy depends on interfaces rather than on
a single sector or task. Consider public spending on a port expansion and the rail links
around it. Whether the project is valuable does not depend only on the engineering of
the terminal. It also depends on customs procedures, rail capacity, trucking bottlenecks,
labor rules, procurement, local congestion, pollution, and the firms that use the port. A
crane operator, a trucker, an exporter, and a nearby resident each know one margin well.
A logistics manager, procurement officer, or planner who works across these margins
may have less depth in any one of them, but a better sense of where the bottleneck is
and whether public money is likely to solve it. The relevant distinction is therefore not
between informed and uninformed citizens in general. It is between local depth and
knowledge of interfaces.
The formal model keeps this distinction simple. There are K knowledge domains,
and each individual allocates a learning budget across them. Because learning exhibits
economies of specialization, focusing on fewer domains creates more depth, while spread-
ing knowledge across domains creates broader coverage at a cost. A knowledge profile

matters in two places. In production, it determines which inputs a worker can contribute
towards output and how much external coordination she needs when her tasks touch do-
mains she does not know. In politics, it determines how accurately the same person can
evaluate policies whose consequences depend on a mix of domains. I call this politically
relevant understanding system knowledge. Candidates compete over public resources and
a common policy-design choice that determines how effectively public resources are con-
verted into services, and voters make noisy comparisons between platforms. Citizens
with more system knowledge evaluate those platforms more accurately.
The results begin with production. First, when integration is sufficiently efficient, the
output-maximizing organization has a sharp division of knowledge. Routine production
is carried out by fully specialized workers, while integration is carried out by workers
whose knowledge matches the interface problems created by specialization. This result
gives economic content to Smith’s concern. The model does not assume that most workers
are narrow and a few are broad; it derives that pattern from learning economies and
coordination costs.
Second, this productive hierarchy can become a political hierarchy. When the knowl-
edge needed to evaluate policy is sufficiently diffuse across domains, integrators have
more system knowledge than specialists. The political advantage of integrators is not
due to wealth, education, lobbying, or superior civic virtue, but is a byproduct of the
same division of labor that maximizes output.
Third, electoral competition turns this knowledge advantage into policy incidence.
Citizens who evaluate policy more accurately are more responsive to platform differences.
Candidates therefore have stronger incentives to appeal to them. In equilibrium, public
resources per capita are higher for the group with greater system knowledge. One person
still has one vote; what differs is how strongly alternative policies move voting behavior.
A democracy with formally equal voters can therefore give greater effective weight to
occupational groups that are better able to understand what government is doing.
Fourth, system knowledge also affects policy design. When few citizens can trace the
consequences of policy, candidates face weaker incentives to incur the cost of devising
high quality, surplus generating policies and limit waste. When system knowledge is
more widely held, weak designs are easier to detect and punish at the polls, leading to
higher quality platforms. The welfare loss from specialization therefore has two parts.
Public resources may be tilted toward the better-informed group, and public resources
may be converted less effectively into services that citizens value.
The main welfare implication is that markets can generate too much specialization.
Wages reflect the productive value of knowledge, not its value for political monitoring.

A reform that modestly broadens the knowledge of routine specialists can therefore raise
welfare even if it lowers productive specialization. The logic is strongest when the inte-
grator layer is small. Then broadening a large mass of specialists can substantially im-
prove the electorate’s ability to understand policy, while the loss of interface knowledge
from fewer integrators is limited. General education, cross-functional training, and other
forms of breadth may have social returns that do not appear fully in wages. They need
not make every worker more productive at her assigned task in order to make citizens
better able to evaluate public decisions.
Two comparative statics sharpen the mechanism. The first concerns how interface-
intensive politics is. By interface-intensive, I mean that the consequences of policy de-
pend less on a single local domain and more on how domains interact. A tariff on a
final good in a simple domestic industry is relatively local. A tariff on an imported input
used throughout a production network is more interface-intensive: its effects depend on
downstream production, input substitution, consumer prices, contracts, inventories, and
retaliation. As policy becomes more interface-intensive, the knowledge held by integra-
tors becomes more useful for evaluating government, while the local knowledge held by
specialists becomes less sufficient. When integrators are a small share of the population,
this shift can lower the electorate’s overall ability to monitor policy and reduce welfare.
The second comparative static concerns the efficiency of integration itself. Better man-
agement systems, common standards, modular design, or information technology can al-
low fewer people to coordinate a larger set of specialized tasks. This raises output. But
it can also concentrate broad interface knowledge in a smaller part of the electorate. An
improvement in productive organization therefore has an ambiguous political effect: it
makes the economy more efficient, but may leave fewer citizens able to understand how
the system works. Conversely, a more labor-intensive integration technology may lower
output while spreading politically useful knowledge more widely.
The framework also speaks to two broader debates. The first concerns liberal edu-
cation. Broad curricula are valuable in this model only when they help citizens under-
stand problems whose consequences cross domains; breadth is not valuable merely be-
cause it is broad. But when policy evaluation does require such breadth, liberal education
can partly correct a civic externality created by occupational specialization. The second
concerns artificial intelligence. If AI substitutes mainly for narrow specialist knowledge
while helping humans perform integrative tasks, it may broaden access to politically use-
ful understanding. If instead it substitutes for human integrative judgment, or makes
the public information environment noisier, it may weaken accountability. The relevant
question is not only whether AI raises productivity, but how it changes the distribution

of understanding among citizens.
The contribution of the paper is to make the distribution of political understanding
an equilibrium outcome. Models of electoral accountability often take voter information
as given. Models of specialization and organization often stop at output. Here the same
allocation of human capital determines both production and political evaluation. The di-
vision of labor is therefore also a division of understanding, and that division can matter
for democratic accountability.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 discusses related literature. Section 3 defines
system knowledge and the mapping from knowledge profiles to political understanding.
Section 4 characterizes the efficient division of cognitive labor in production. Section 5
studies how the resulting distribution of knowledge shapes electoral competition and
government performance. Section 6 compares the market allocation to the civic optimum
and develops the main comparative statics. Section 7 discusses implications for liberal
education and artificial intelligence. Section 8 concludes.

Related literature
This paper relates first to the literature on the division of labor, organizational capabili-
ties, and the organization of knowledge inside firms. Chandler (1992) emphasizes, from
a business-history perspective, that the rise of the modern industrial enterprise rested on
the accumulation of organizational capabilities. Becker and Murphy (1992) formalize the
gains from specialization and the coordination costs that limit it. Most closely related is
Garicano (2000), who studies knowledge hierarchies in which routine problems are han-
dled by narrower workers and harder exceptions are escalated to more knowledgeable
agents. A related literature studies firms as communication and information-processing
structures and analyzes the tradeoffs among specialization, coordination, adaptation, and
broader task bundles (Radner, 1993; Bolton and Dewatripont, 1994; Dessein and Santos,
2006; Alonso et al., 2008; Lindbeck and Snower, 2000). I focus on organizational design
in the presence of civic externalities, a different welfare problem. A productively efficient
organization can be civically inefficient because markets do not internalize the political
implications of skill formation. In that sense, I study the political economy of different
organizational designs.
This paper also relates to the literature on noisy voting, probabilistic voting, and ra-
tional inattention (Lindbeck and Weibull, 1987; Matˇejka and McKay, 2015; Matˇejka and
Tabellini, 2021). Most closely related, Matˇejka and Tabellini (2021) study electoral com-
petition with rationally inattentive voters and show how selective attention can distort
5

policy toward salient or intense interests. The contribution here is to give an economic
origin to those differences in political precision. Voters are not simply exogenously inat-
tentive. Their ability to evaluate policy is shaped upstream by occupational choices. The
paper, therefore, links political efficacy with the labor-market allocation of knowledge.
Finally, the paper relates to the literature on information shortcuts, democratic dele-
gation, and expert advice. Lupia (1994) shows that low-information voters can use credi-
ble cues to approximate the choices of better-informed voters, and Lupia and McCubbins
(1998) argue more generally that citizens can learn from others when they can identify
speakers who are both knowledgeable and trustworthy. A broader delegation literature
studies how institutions are designed to make such delegation workable (Kiewiet and Mc-
Cubbins, 1991; McCubbins et al., 1987). The paper is less optimistic about whether exper-
tise by itself removes the information problem from the table. If broad system knowledge
correlates with occupational background, as my model would imply, the conflict of inter-
est between experts and non-experts may hinder information transmission (Crawford
and Sobel, 1982).

5
Politics
In this section, I take the occupational allocation generated in production as given and
study how it shapes politics. Citizens care about targeted public services and private
income, but they differ in how accurately they can judge policy. Higher system knowledge
raises a citizen’s ability to connect observed outcomes, public signals, and policy claims
to the underlying consequences of government action. That matters politically through
two channels. First, groups whose members evaluate policy more precisely respond more
strongly to platform changes, so parties place greater weight on them when competing in
elections. Second, higher aggregate system knowledge improves the common governance
margin by making poor performance easier to detect and punish, thereby increasing the
fraction of public resources converted into citizen-valued services.

Market inefficiency and welfare
While competitive markets internalize the production value of specialization and inte-
gration, they do not meaningfully internalize the civic value of system knowledge. I first
provide sufficient conditions under which a competitive industry decentralizes the pro-
ductive optimum, and show that in general, such a competitive allocation does not max-
imize utilitarian welfare. Then, I study the effects of a policy aimed at broadening spe-
cialists’ knowledge, and derive sufficient conditions under which a civic planner prefers
less specialization than the market outcome. Finally, I study how welfare changes when
the political knowledge requirement becomes increasingly about economic integration.

6.4
Politics about economic integration
When does politics reward routine local knowledge and when does it reward cross-domain
interface knowledge? Two primitive objects determine the answer. First, the production
profile q describes where routine productive effort is concentrated in the economy. Sec-
ond, the civic profile u describes what kinds of knowledge a voter needs in order to judge
policy. In other words, does evaluating government mainly require understanding one
local margin, or tracing how budgeting, regulation, procurement, and enforcement inter-
act? Political distortion turns on how these two profiles line up. Under the productive
optimum, specialists inherit the routine profile q, while integrators inherit the interface
profile h⋆(q) from Theorem 1. The key structural fact is that h⋆(q) is a flattening of q:
relative to specialists, integrators put less weight on dominant production domains and
more weight on secondary domains that matter when interactions become important.

Discussion
This paper speaks to two broader debates that are often treated separately from the theory
of the firm: the debate over the value of liberal-arts education, and the debate over the
political economic implication of artificial intelligence.
7.1
Liberal-arts education
The model offers one way to organize the debate over the value of liberal-arts colleges
and, more broadly, liberal education. One side of that debate emphasizes breadth: liberal
education exposes students to multiple knowledge domains, develops habits of interpre-
tation and argument, and prepares them for citizenship rather than only for a narrowly
defined occupation (Nussbaum, 2010; Zakaria, 2015). Another side is more skeptical,
either because colleges may generate limited measured learning gains relative to their as-
pirations (Arum and Roksa, 2011) or because a substantial share of the private return to
college may reflect signaling rather than socially productive human-capital accumulation
(Caplan, 2018). The mechanism in this paper is closest to the first view, but it provides a
more specific welfare channel.
In the model, broad knowledge matters because some political questions require trac-
ing indirect effects across domains. If liberal-arts institutions broaden students’ knowl-
edge profiles—raising coverage of the domains relevant for interpreting public problems—
then they increase system knowledge even when labor markets do not fully reward that
breadth. In the notation of Section 6, one can think of liberal education as moving some
workers away from extreme corner specialization and toward broader profiles, for in-
stance along the same margin studied in Theorem 3. The resulting gain need not appear
fully in wages because firms internalize the production value of specialization but not the
democratic value of broader evaluation and monitoring.
The takeaway is not that liberal-arts education is unequivocally welfare-improving,
nor that every broad curriculum has the same social value. It is that broad education can,
under some conditions, take care of a civic externality. Institutions that require exposure
outside a student’s eventual occupation—general-education requirements, distribution
requirements, and liberal-arts curricula more generally—may raise welfare by increas-
ing the share of citizens who can evaluate cross-domain issues. With this reading, the
strongest defense of liberal education is not only labor-market adaptability, but rather
that democratic accountability may work better when more citizens can understand pol-
icy problems whose consequences run across domains. That logic is especially strong
when civic relevance u is diffuse and when the productivity cost of additional breadth is

7.2
Artificial intelligence
The model also provides one way to think about artificial intelligence and chatbots. A
task-based view of technology emphasizes that automation changes the assignment of
tasks rather than simply eliminating work outright (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019). Re-
cent evidence on generative AI is consistent with that interpretation. In both customer-
support and writing settings, AI systems appear able to codify and supply specialist
knowledge in real time, with especially large gains for less experienced workers (Bryn-
jolfsson et al., 2025; Noy and Zhang, 2023). At the same time, the evidence also suggests
a “jagged technological frontier”: AI is highly useful on some knowledge tasks, but less
reliable on others, so human judgment remains valuable precisely where workflows re-
quire cross-step integration or careful exception handling (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026). In the
language of this paper, AI may compress part of the specialist layer without eliminating
the integrator role.
A useful polar benchmark pushes that logic to its limit. Suppose AI is primarily a sub-
stitute for specialist knowledge, so that each citizen can supervise a portfolio of specialist
AI agents at negligible civic cost. Then the pre-AI productive allocation Aq provides a
template for the AI era: each human citizen becomes an integrator, and each one manages

Conclusion
The paper’s main point is that specialization changes not only how production is orga-
nized, but also who can evaluate complex government action. Learning economies make
narrow roles efficient inside firms, yet the resulting allocation of knowledge can leave
only a subset of workers able to trace cross-domain consequences and monitor policy im-
plementation. Accountability then weakens in two ways. Electoral competition places
greater weight on groups whose members evaluate policy more precisely, and low ag-
gregate system knowledge reduces delivery efficiency by making poor-quality platforms
harder to detect and punish.
The model isolates this mechanism by linking production and politics through the
same underlying object: a worker’s knowledge profile. In production, efficient organi-
zations combine narrow specialists with integrators who hold the knowledge needed to
manage interfaces across modules. In politics, those same knowledge differences shape
political responsiveness and monitoring. A civic planner therefore values broader knowl-
edge not only because it supports coordination inside production, but also because it af-
fects who is politically influential and how effectively public resources are turned into
delivered services.
The comparative statics suggest that globalization, outsourcing, digitization, and reg-
ulatory layering can raise the political value of interface knowledge by making policy con-
sequences run through interactions across domains rather than through one local margin.
In such environments, integrators can become a small but politically influential group,
and otherwise similar democracies can differ sharply in effective fiscal capacity if they dif-
fer in aggregate system knowledge. The policy implication is that civic capacity should
be treated more like an economic input. Transparency and formal oversight remain im-
portant, but so do investments and institutions that broaden citizens’ domain coverage or
make public information easier to interpret. In that sense, the civic costs of specialization
are part of the allocation problem itself, not outside the economy.
