Lower bounds.
It is natural to wonder why techniques that work so well for halfspaces, e.g.,
Perceptron-style algorithms, linear programming, outlier removal, isotropic transformation, poly-
nomial approximation, and ℓ1-minimization do not extend to the intersection of halfspaces. First,
proper learning is provably hard unless P=NP, even for k = 2 halfspaces [BR92]. Next, when k is
part of the input, the same paper shows that learning an intersection of k halfspaces as an inter-
section of at most kn1−ε halfspaces is also hard via a reduction from hypergraph coloring [BR92].
Of course, this does not rule out efficient learning, as the hypothesis class used could be any func-
tion class that can be learned in polytime. One such natural class is bounded-degree polynomials,
which are used for learning various hypotheses such as De Morgan Formulas and Disjunctive Nor-
mal Forms (DNFs) [KS04a, Lee09]. Unfortunately, approximating an intersection of two halfspaces
with a polynomial threshold function needs a polynomial of degree Ω(n) [She13]; since learning
polynomial-threshold functions (PTFs) requires time exponential in the degree of the polynomial,
the standard approach to learning intersections of halfspaces as a PTF requires 2Ω(n) time.
From a broader perspective, state-of-the-art algorithms for learning halfspaces and low-degree
polynomials can be viewed as Statistical Query algorithms, a model of learning introduced by
Kearns [Kea98] and extended and applied widely in subsequent decades [BFJ+94, FGR+17]. For
many well-known problems in supervised and unsupervised learning, the best-known upper bounds
are achievable by SQ algorithms and have nearly matching lower bounds in the SQ model of
computation. For intersections of halfspaces, Klivans and Sherstov [KS07] showed that learning
an intersection of √n halfspaces with 1/poly(n) margin over the Boolean cube has SQ complexity
2Ω(√n).
Another line of work has developed representation-independent lower bounds for this problem
based on cryptographic assumptions. Klivans and Sherstov [KS09] showed hardness of PAC-learning
an intersection of k = nε halfspaces for some ε > 0 assuming that the unique Shortest Vector
Problem in lattices is hard to approximate within a factor of eO(n1.5).
This was improved by
Tiegel [Tie24], who showed a lower bound of nΩ(k/ log k) for k ≤√n halfspaces under similar lattice
problem hardness assumptions.

2.1
PAC Learning
We now briefly recall the PAC learning model of Valiant [Val84]. In this model, we wish to learn
a class of Boolean-valued functions C under an unknown distribution D. We say that an algorithm
(ε, δ)-PAC learns the class C if for any f ∈C we have the following: given labeled examples of the
form (x, f(x)) where x ∼D, with probability 1 −δ, the algorithm outputs a hypothesis h such
that Prx∼D[h(x) ̸= f(x)] ≤ε.
