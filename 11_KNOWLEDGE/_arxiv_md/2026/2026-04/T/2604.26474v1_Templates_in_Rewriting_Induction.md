Rewriting Induction (RI) is a formal system in term rewriting to establish program equivalence.
The recently defined Bounded RI for higher-order Logically Constrained Term Rewriting Systems
(LCSTRSs) yields a convenient proof system for analyzing real programming code.
A practical challenge in RI is the automatic generation of induction hypotheses, called lemmas.
While various lemma generation techniques exist for plain term rewriting, there are much fewer that
consider the intricacies brought on by calculations or constraints. Taking advantage of recent devel-
opments in higher-order RI, we here present a new approach based on templates, which operates by
recognising typical programming constructs as instances of higher-order functions. While templates
have been used as a stand-alone method to justify the correctness of program transformations, we
here consider their integration in Bounded RI to obtain a complementary lemma generation heuris-
tic. This allows us to prove equivalences that were previously out of reach.

Introduction
Rewriting Induction (RI) is a proof system for establishing inductive theorems using term rewriting. For
a rewrite system R, the notion of inductive theorem is tied to the convertibility relation ↔∗
R , defined as
the smallest equivalence relation that contains R and which is closed under application of substitutions
and contexts. An equation s ≈t is an inductive theorem if it is ground convertible, meaning that sγ ↔∗
R tγ
for every ground substitution γ (a substitution that only substitutes variable-free terms). For example, let
R = {add(0,y) →y, add(s(x),y) →s(add(x,y))}
We claim that add(x,s(0)) ≈s(x) is an inductive theorem, which means that add(x,s(0))γ ↔∗
R s(x)γ
for all ground substitutions γ. For concrete ground substitutions this is an easy property to check. For
example, if γ = [x := s(0)] then convertibility is witnessed by the reduction
add(s(0),s(0)) →s(add(0,s(0))) →s(s(0))
To prove convertibility for an arbitrary ground substitution γ, we may use well-founded induction. If R
is terminating then →R is a well-founded ordering, which is the key property upon which RI relies.
Constrained rewriting. The RI system has been adapted to constrained rewriting [8, 9, 21], and recently
to higher-order constrained rewriting [13, 14]. These formalisms closely relate to real programming and
therefore have a natural place in the larger toolbox for program verification. Programs are represented by
term rewriting systems, and equivalence is modeled by inductive theorems. Rewrite rules have a shape
s →t [ϕ] where the boolean constraint ϕ acts as a guard, in order to manage control flow over primitive
data structures, such as integers. Constraints are theory terms: built from variables and theory symbols
(e.g. + and ∗). Ground instances of theory terms have a semantical interpretation. For example, addition
on integers is interpreted as addition on Z. So for a theory term t := (x+y ≥5) and ground substitution
γ = [x := 2,y := 3] the instance tγ has the semantical interpretation [[tγ]] = [[2+3 ≥5]] = (2+3 ≥5) = ⊤.

Lemma generation. A practical challenge in applying RI to prove concrete examples of program equiv-
alence is the automatic generation of lemmas. For example, when naively trying to prove facTU x ≈
facRD x [x ≥1], we run successively into proof obligations u x 3 2 ≈x ∗(u x′ 2 1) [x ≥2∧x′ = x −1],
then u x 4 6 ≈x∗(u x′ 3 2) [x ≥3∧x′ = x−1], then u x 5 24 ≈x∗(u x′ 4 6) [x ≥4∧x′ = x−1], and so on.
This is called a divergence. Case analysis and rewriting brings us to the next equation in the sequence, but
we can never use one as an induction hypothesis to eliminate the next. We need the insight that all these
equations are instances of, e.g., the lemma u x i a ≈x∗(u x′ i′ a′) [x ≥i′∧x′ = x−1∧i = i′+1∧a = a′∗i′]
to find something that can be directly proved with an induction step.

This need for lemmas – or to generalize equations before they can be used as induction hypotheses –
is a well-known topic in equational reasoning. Compared to classical equational reasoning, our situation
is more involved, as the semantical reasoning over logical constraints (in our examples integer expres-
sions) requires the discovery of non-trivial (loop) invariants. Two methods for constrained rewriting, also
applicable to LCSTRSs, are provided by [9, 12]. For example, the method of [9] finds the lemma above,
while [12] allows us to prove facTU x ≈facRU x [x ≥1] by discovering the loop invariants z = a ∗j,
i = j+1 and j ≤x which are used to generate the lemma u x i z ≈a∗(R j x) [z = a∗j∧i = j+1∧j ≤x].
While these two methods can succesfully generate lemmas for many easy examples, they do not scale
to more complicated computational behavior because they operate at the level of proof processes, rather
than the level of programming constructs. This leaves certain program equivalences beyond their scope.
For example, the methods from [9, 12] are not able to prove the equivalences facRU x ≈facTD x [x ≥1],
facRU x ≈facRD x [x ≥1] and facTU x ≈facTD x, because their invariant-seeking approach is too tightly
tailored to low-level computations, which fails when the dependence between successive divergence
steps cannot be expressed as a polynomial. Fortunately, these equivalences can still be provable within
RI. The key is not to focus on more expressive invariants, but rather to adopt a more high-level strategy:
using templates we can generalize over loop structures themselves, instead of the specific values of loop
variables, making these equivalences easily provable without complicated invariants.
Templates. Inspired by [7], we consider templates: rewrite schemes that capture commonly occurring
programming patterns, such as upward or downward tail recursion. Templates are modeled by higher-
order recursors; we prove equivalences between recursors, which can then be applied to prove concrete
instances. This provides a high-level lemma generation method, complementary to existing low-level
methods from [9, 12]. With templates, proving facTU x ≈facTD x becomes equally easy as proving
sumTU x ≈sumTD x, where this last equivalence involves a TailUp and TailDown implementation of
x 7→∑x
i=1 i. While the method in [12] can brute-force a lemma for sumTU x ≈sumTD x, this fails for
facTU x ≈facTD x because the required invariants are not polynomial. The template approach also
allows us to prove other equivalences that were previously out of reach.
Contributions. Templates in rewriting are not new [7], but the combination with LCSTRSs opens up
possibilities for integration in the formal RI system in a manner very useful for real program verification:
▷Since LCSTRS are a form of higher-order rewriting, we can perform all equivalence proofs of
recursors within RI, rather than at the meta-level. This allows templates to be integrated in tools
as a lemma generation method, complementary to methods that focus on lower-level behavior.
▷Since constrained rewriting directly supports primitive data structures, the rewrite rules closely
resemble real code. This in contrast to the required encodings in unconstrained rewriting, which
make analysis of loop constructions like those in our factorial implementations infeasible or at
least inconvenient. With LCSTRSs we can define suitable templates in a human-readable manner.
Overview. After we recap LCSTRSs and Bounded RI in Section 2, we continue as follows
▷In Section 3 we introduce four templates and describe how to match them with concrete programs.
▷In Section 4 we assume recursor equivalences as given and focus on the proof-tactical aspect of
how to apply these equivalences to prove concrete template instances. We distinguish two proof
tactics: one-sided matching, transforming a proof goal into an easier one, and two-sided matching,
concluding equivalence directly. This section is meant to provide an intuitive explanation.
▷In Section 5 we show in a more technical way how templates are used within Bounded RI as a
lemma generation method. We discuss the corresponding ordering requirements that constitute the
separate parts of template-based proofs, and describe how to recombine them in concrete instances.

Preliminaries
LCSTRSs [11] are a higher-order rewriting formalism with built-in support for theories such as integers
and boolean (though in fact, any any arbitrary theory such as bitvectors, floating point numbers or integer
arrays is in principle supported) as well as logical constraints to model control flow. This considers
applicative higher-order term rewriting (without λ abstractions) and first-order constraints.
Types. Assume given a set of sorts (base types) S. The set T of types is defined by T ::= S | T →T .
Here, →is right-associative: all types may be written as type1 →... →typem →sort with m ≥0. We
also assume given a subset Stheory ⊆S of theory sorts with bool ∈Stheory, and define the theory types by
Ttheory ::= Stheory | Stheory →Ttheory. Every ι ∈Stheory has an interpretation set Iι ̸= /0.
In this paper, we fix Stheory ⊇{int,bool}, with Ibool = {⊤,⊥} and Iint = Z (the set of all integers).
For any sort ι and type σ, we let Iι→σ be the set of all total functions from Iι to Iσ.
Terms. We assume given a signature Σ of function symbols and a disjoint set V of variables, and a
function typeof from Σ∪V to T . The set of terms T(Σ,V ) over Σ and V are those expressions which
can be typed using the recursive clauses: (a) u :: typeof(u) for u ∈Σ∪V ; and (b) s t :: τ of s :: σ →τ and
t :: σ. Application is left-associative; every term may be written as u s1 ···sm with u ∈Σ∪V and m ≥0.
For a term t, let Var(t) be the set of variables in t. A term t is ground if Var(t) = /0. We assume that Σ is
the disjoint union Σtheory ⊎Σterms, where typeof(f) ∈Ttheory for all f ∈Σtheory.
Each f ∈Σtheory has an interpretation [[f]] ∈Itypeof(f). Terms in T(Σtheory,V ) are theory terms. For
ground theory terms, we define [[s t]] = [[s]]([[t]]), thus mapping each ground term of type σ to an element
of Iσ. For example, we have theory terms x+3, true and 7∗0. The latter two are ground, and we have
[[true]] = ⊤and [[7∗0]] = 0. Values are theory symbols of base type, i.e. V al = {v ∈Σtheory | typeof(v) ∈
Stheory}. A constraint is a theory term ϕ :: bool, such that typeof(x) ∈Stheory for all x ∈Var(ϕ).
In this paper, we fix a theory signature Σtheory ⊇{+,−,∗,=,<,≤,>,≥,∧,∨,¬,true,false}∪{n |
n ∈Z} with each of these symbols interpreted as expected, e.g. ∗:: int →int →int is interpreted as
multiplication on Z. We use [f] for prefix or partially applied notation, e.g. [+] x y and x+y are the same.
The values in this setting are (at least) true,false and all n. An example of a constraint is x∗y > 0.
Contexts and context functions. Let □1,...,□n be fresh, typed constants, with n ≥1. A context
C[□1,...,□n] (or just: C) is a term in T(Σ∪{□1,...,□n},V ) in which each □i occurs exactly once. The
term obtained from C by replacing each □i by a term ti of the same type is denoted by C[t1,...,tn].
A context function in n parameters is a term C(□1,...,□n) (or just: C) in T(Σ∪{□1,...,□n},V ) in
which each □i may occur 0 or more times. The term obtained from C by replacing each □i by a term ti of
the same type (but multiple instances of the same □i are replaced by the same ti) is denoted C(t1,...,tn).
A context function in 2 parameters is called a binary context function.
Rewrite rules. A rule is an expression ℓ→r [ϕ]. Here ℓand r are terms of the same type, ℓhas a form
f ℓ1 ···ℓk with f ∈Σ and k ≥0, ϕ is a constraint and Var(r) ⊆Var(ℓ) ∪Var(ϕ). If ϕ = true, we may
denote the rule as just ℓ→r. Fixing a signature Σ, we assume given a set of rules R whose left-hand sides
are not theory terms, and define the set of calculation rules as: Rcalc = {f x1 ···xm →y [y = f x1 ···xm] |
f ∈Σtheory \V al, x1,...,xm ∈V ,y ∈V , f :: ι1 →... →ιm →κ}. We require that every symbol f has a
unique arity ar(f): if there is a rule f ℓ1 ···ℓk →r [ϕ] in R ∪Rcalc then ar(f) = k, and if there is no such
rule then ar(f) = ∞. A function symbol is a constructor if ar(f) = ∞. A term is a semi-constructor term
if, for all its subterms f s1 ···sn, we have n < ar(f). (Hence, a semi-constructor term is irreducible.)
Reduction relation. We assume familiarity with substitutions and denote dom(γ) = {x ∈V | γ(x) ̸= x}.
A substitution γ respects a constraint ϕ if γ(x) ∈V al for all x ∈Var(ϕ) and [[ϕγ]] = ⊤.

Rewriting Induction. Rewriting Induction is a proof system to show that all elements of a set E of
equations are inductive theorems. There are several variations of the core proof system, but typically,
they consist of a set of derivation rules that allow the user to iteratively transform a pair (E ,H ) of
equations and induction hypotheses until all equations are eliminated. For example, derivation steps
might include rewriting an equation using a rule or induction hypothesis, doing case analysis on some
variable, or deleting a vacuous equation s ≈s [ϕ]. Variations of RI may differ along several aspects: the
exact derivation rules, whether they support constraints [8, 9, 13, 14] or not [1, 2, 3, 5, 23], whether they
transform a triple (E ,H ,G ) instead of a pair [1, 2], or what exactly E and H contain (e.g., in [14] the
equations in E carry additional information and the elements of H are equations rather than rules).
All such systems operate under the assumptions of termination and quasi-reductivity: the relation
→R may not admit infinite reductions, and ground terms may only be irreducible if they are semi-
constructor terms (essentially: there are no missing cases in the pattern matching). Moreover, a cen-
tral component in these systems is the incorporation of well-founded induction: in essence, the system
proves the given theorems by a shared induction on a well-founded ordering ≻. However, the specific
construction of such an ordering ≻is realized differently across existing RI variants. In many cases
(e.g., [8, 9, 13, 23]) the ordering is implicitly defined as →R ∪→H , where H consists of rules (which
requires termination of →R∪H ). In others (e.g., [1, 2, 3, 5]) the user is required to explicitly provide an
ordering, which is then used in certain derivation steps; typically, this ordering should include →R.
Bounded Rewriting Induction. In this paper, we consider a simplified version of the recently defined
Bounded RI [14] for LCSTRSs, which follows the latter approach but aims to minimize the number of
ordering requirements that must be satisfied. Hence, we assume given a fixed well-founded ordering ≻
that includes →R ∪▷, where ▷is the subterm relation, generated by s t ▷s and s t ▷t.1 Let ⪰be the
reflexive closure of ≻. Let s ≻t [ϕ] denote that sγ ≻tγ for all ground substitutions γ that respect ϕ and
have Var(s)∪Var(t) ⊆dom(γ), and s ⪰t [ϕ] that either s = t or s ≻t [ϕ].
The proof steps of Bounded RI manipulate a pair (E ,H ) where H is a set of equations (called
induction hypotheses) and E a set of equation contexts: tuples of an equation s ≈t [ϕ] with bounding
terms ς,τ such that ς ⪰s [ϕ] and τ ⪰t [ϕ]. Bounding terms serve to keep track of the terms to be in used
in the ordering requirements. A full explanation of Bounded RI is beyond the scope of this paper, but we
highlight the most relevant deduction rules and refer to [14] for the formal definition of these steps:

Regarding the ordering ≻, we claim that the ordering requirements imposed by Rrec and the lem-

f > tailup > taildn > recup > recdn, where f is any symbol defining the T and R rules. (There is one

RPO-orientability certainly holds if □1 = □2, or if these are non-recursive symbols like F.) Or if we use

dependency pairs for the lemmas and the rules in Rrec are easily eliminated by the dependency graph
and the value criterion. (Again, this does depend on what is used for □1 and □2.)
If the ordering requirements do cause difficulties, it is worth noting that the method does admit
for some flexibility; for example, by applying an axiom on the other side of the equation, different
ordering requirements arise, and also in Lemma 4.8 we could obtain different requirements by applying
the lemmas slightly differently, if this is helpful for the given choice of □1 and □2.
We believe that the template approach lends itself well towards automation, although we have not
done so yet since our implementation of Bounded RI is also not yet fully automatic even without adding
postulates. Once the technical prerequisites are in place, it should not be hard to recognise instances
of the templates (as this is mostly syntactic), but the primary challenge will be to decide how to apply
the lemmas: as we have seen in Sections 4.1 and 4.2, there is no unique best approach, and different
strategies might lead to different outcomes. We expect this to be a matter of trial and error.
6
Conclusion
In this paper, we have introduced several useful templates and showed how they can be incorporated
into Bounded RI as a lemma generation method to prove program equivalence in a manual or automatic
process. In particular, templates can be seen as a generalization method operating at the level of pro-
graming constructs. The advantage of this approach becomes apparent in settings where existing lemma
generation methods from constrained rewriting fail due to the need for non-polynomial invariants.

Equivalence by transformation schemes. A final difference with our approach is the mechanism being
used to establish template equivalence. The approach in [7] is not based on well-founded induction. In-
stead, equivalence is proven via equivalent term rewriting systems, established via specifically designed
transformation rules. Correctness of these transformations relies on confluence. Additionally, one has
to assume sufficient completeness, a property very similar to quasi-reductivity. However, the authors
point out that in practice verifying sufficient completeness requires termination. Thus, the prerequisites
are similar to our approach: RI also requires quasi-reductivity and termination, and as observed in Sec-
tion 5.4, ground confluence is needed to obtain an inductive theorem over →R rather than just →R∪Rrec.
