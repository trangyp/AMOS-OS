ABSTRACT
The Euclid satellite will deliver a catalogue of optically-selected galaxy clusters spanning from around 2000 deg2 in Data Release (DR) 1 to around
14 000 deg2 in DR3. We assess the validity of cluster clustering (CC) models for template-fitting, which complements the full-shape methodology
in providing cosmological information from the anisotropy of the redshift-space two-point correlation function (2PCF). Both will be used to
analyse the cluster 2PCF multipoles in Euclid. We analyse the multipoles of the two-point redshift-space clustering of galaxy clusters simulated
with the semi-analytic PINOCCHIO code using third-order Lagrangian perturbation theory, assuming a Euclid DR1-like footprint of 500 deg2 in
the Northern Hemisphere and 1400 deg2 in the Southern Hemisphere. We estimate the first three even multipoles of the 2PCF and associated
covariance matrix from 1000 DR1-like synthetic catalogues. We study the impact of the modelling of nonlinearities, halo bias, and photometric
redshift uncertainties on the 2PCF. We apply three clustering models to the mock catalogues at 0 < z < 2 and virial mass Mvir > 1014 h−1 M⊙
under realistic and optimistic photometric redshift uncertainty scenarios. We formulate a set of permissive and conservative criteria that should be
fulfilled by the multipole cut-off scales and validate against 100 mock catalogues via inference of the growth rate times the matter power spectrum
normalisation parameter, fσ8. We test the dispersion, Scoccimarro, and Taruya–Nishimichi–Saito models. We find that the simplest of the three –
the dispersion model – yields unbiased inferences on fσ8 from CC down to 10 h−1 Mpc in a DR1-like setting. All clustering models provide very
similar goodness-of-fit metrics in the presence of DR1-like cluster redshift uncertainties.

1. Introduction
Galaxy clusters are the most massive structures in the Universe
which have undergone virialisation. Their clustering offers a sen-
sitive probe of our cosmological model (Estrada et al. 2009;
Hütsi 2010; Hong et al. 2012). Although clusters are fewer than
galaxies, their strong clustering signal and high masses offer an
independent handle on the large-scale structure. Therefore, the
cluster clustering (CC) signal is in principle stronger than that
of galaxies, provided that dense cluster samples are available
(Marulli et al. 2021). Further, the impact of small-scale velocity
dispersion on the CC signal is relatively small, because cluster
centroids have low internal velocity dispersion and are less af-
fected by small-scale random motions than galaxies (Valageas
& Clerc 2012). Therefore, CC can be viewed as a probe comple-
mentary to galaxy clustering, particularly with the advent of the
high signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) Stage-IV surveys (Abell et al.
2009; Laureijs et al. 2011; Sartoris et al. 2016; Euclid Collabo-
ration: Mellier et al. 2025).
Unbiased cosmological constraints demand CC models that
achieve sufficient accuracy across a wide range of scales and red-
shifts (for example Borgani et al. 1999; Mana et al. 2013; Sereno
et al. 2015; Marulli et al. 2021; Lesci et al. 2022; Fumagalli
et al. 2024; Romanello et al. 2024). Since observations are ob-
tained in redshift space, the primary modelling challenge in CC
analyses is having an accurate model for redshift-space cluster-
ing anisotropies. The Kaiser model (Kaiser 1987; Peacock et al.
2001) provides the linear theory predictions in the plane-parallel
limit (Valageas & Clerc 2012), but extensions to this model have
been proposed to account for nonlinear effects at smaller scales
(see for example Pezzotta et al. 2017 and Marulli et al. 2021 for
a summary of these models).
In this paper, we aim to test the performance of the redshift-
space CC models for a Euclid-like survey (Euclid Collaboration:
Mellier et al. 2025) in order to determine which cluster clustering
model should be implemented in the Euclid Cosmology Like-
lihood for Observables (CLOE; Euclid Collaboration: Cardone
et al. 2025; Euclid Collaboration: Cañas-Herrera et al. 2025) for
DR1, and formulate and calibrate a statistical framework for the
determination of the cut-off scale of the two-point correlation
function of cluster clustering. Given the significant uncertainties
in a DR1-like setting, we adopt a permissive and a conservative
set of statistical tests, the latter to be considered for future DRs.
The permissive criteria aim for acceptable goodness of fit and
unbiasedness of the inferred fσ8 posteriors given a cut-off scale
which is common for all multipoles at a given redshift. The con-
servative criteria further require that the estimated uncertainties
accurately reflect the true scatter in the measurements and pro-
vide correct coverage, with a cut-off scale which is allowed to
vary with multipole order. We base our main conclusions on the
former set of criteria. In this analysis, we calibrate our statistical
requirements against simulations, whereas for the eventual real
data application such an analysis will be repeated.

et al. 2002; Munari et al. 2017), from which we extract 1000

As long as the effective width of the error distribution is simi-

A more realistic redshift error distribution would primarily al-
ter the precise form of the damping kernel, but it is unlikely to
qualitatively affect our conclusions on the scales considered. We
quantify the accuracy of the Peacock & Dodds (1994), Scocci-
marro (2004), and Taruya et al. (2010) models at separations 10–

this maximum scale to ensure that the baryon acoustic oscilla-
tion (BAO) features are fully visible for large photometric red-
shift uncertainties. Scales larger than the BAO scale do not add
any substantial cosmological information (Fumagalli et al. 2024,
2025b).
Euclid Collaboration: Fumagalli et al. (2024) and Fumagalli
et al. (2025a) showed that the semi-analytic PINOCCHIO cata-
logues accurately reproduce the halo 2PCF covariance of the
monopole obtained from N-body simulations in redshift space.
More recently, Fumagalli et al. (2025b) analysed the impact of
systematic effects on the monopole of CC using PINOCCHIO and
demonstrated that neglecting nonlinear contributions can signif-
icantly bias the inferred Ωm–σ8 posterior. In order to obtain un-
biased cosmological posteriors, that study mitigated modelling
mis-specifications by calibrating the monopole to the model pre-
diction down to 20 h−1 Mpc in mocks covering 10 313 deg2 (be-
tween DR2 and DR3 footprint). In this work, we address the
complementary question: What is the minimum cut-off scale that
ensures an unbiased inference when using the multipoles of the
2PCF? Our results directly inform the implementation of the CC
model in CLOE and the associated analysis choices for cosmolog-
ical inference. Since PINOCCHIO uses Lagrangian perturbation
theory (LPT), we expect it to produce predictions that differ from
those of an N-body simulation, potentially in a scale-dependent
fashion, and produce highly uncertain predictions for halo bias.
However, for the purposes of this study, which is to provide a
statistical framework for the determination of the cut-off scale
in real data, we choose PINOCCHIO, whose covariance is well-
understood (Fumagalli et al. 2025a). The multiple realisations
offered by PINOCCHIO further allow us to monitor the average
behaviour of our inferences. For the real data application, we
will repeat this analysis using the tests in Sect. 2.5 as a guide.
We focus on inferring fσ8 as a diagnostic variable for val-
idation because our analysis centres on the quadrupole. Euclid
Collaboration: Kärcher et al. (2026) performed a related cut-off
analysis for galaxy-clustering multipoles, assuming a common
scale cut for all multipoles in the limit of no redshift uncertain-
ties or angular–radial systematic effects. Here, in our set of con-
servative statistical tests, we allow various scale cuts for different
multipoles, cluster redshift uncertainties in the presence of angu-
lar systematic effects, but restrict our analysis to simpler cluster-
ing models, which we find to be adequate for photometric clus-
tering. Scanning the parameter space of plausible cut-off scales
that vary with redshift, redshift uncertainty, and multipole order
across many mock catalogues is computationally expensive. To
reduce this cost, we adopt a template-fitting strategy. Unlike full-
shape analyses like the one for the monopole of the CC 2PCF by
Fumagalli et al. (2025b), template fitting keeps the matter power
spectrum fixed to a fiducial cosmology and varies only the shape-
preserving parameters that rescale or anisotropically distort it.
In our parameterisation, we use the growth rate times the matter
power spectrum normalisation parameter, fσ8, the linear halo
bias times the matter power spectrum normalisation parameter,
bσ8, a velocity dispersion scale, σv, and the parallel and perpen-
dicular BAO parameters, α∥, and α⊥, respectively. We note that
alternative parameterisations, like the one proposed by Adame
et al. (2025, Eq. 4.10), could also be explored in future analy-
ses. However, given the relatively large uncertainties expected
for cluster clustering in DR1, we do not anticipate significant
differences arising from the choice of parameterisation for the
amplitude of redshift-space distortions.
Template fitting is fast, robust to small deviations from the
fiducial cosmology, and avoids repeated evaluations of the mat-
ter power spectrum, halo mass function, and halo bias. A limi-
tation of this approach is that cosmological parameters such as
Ωm and σ8 are not varied. Here, we keep the shape of the matter
power spectrum, the halo bias, and the distance–redshift relation
fixed to a fiducial cosmology, while Ωm and σ8 are in principle
constrained via the BAO parameters. As a result, any cosmology
dependence of the effective halo bias must be absorbed into the
nuisance parameter bσ8. For clusters, however, the degeneracy
between effective halo bias and σ8 is substantially reduced be-
cause the selection function, constrained by the observable–mass
relation, its scatter, weak lensing calibration, and number counts,
is far better determined than in galaxy-clustering analyses (see
Lesci et al. 2022 and references therein).
To emulate a setting of well-understood systematic effects,
we limit our analysis to a regime where the mass-redshift com-
pleteness is almost uniform. To this end, we impose a mass cut
at Mvir > 1014 h−1M⊙, where sample uncertainties are better
characterised. Second, we fix bσ8 and study the sensitivity of
the resulting cut-off scales to the assumed halo-bias prescrip-
tion, using the T10 and C24 prescriptions. A prior on bσ8, as is
expected in real observations, weakens the predictive power of
statistical tests on the cut-off scale. In our analysis, we choose
to fix bσ8 because (i) a strong prior on halo bias is expected,
(ii) PINOCCHIO provides only an approximate mapping between
dark matter and haloes, (iii) the determination of the cut-off scale
becomes more robust to the statistical test configuration in less
noisy data sets (more conservative error bars), (iv) our goal is
a model test rather than a forecast, and (v) the halo bias is a
nuisance parameter which only modulates the amplitude of the
2PCF.
Determining which redshift-space CC model performs best
in a given setting is crucial, as it impacts all studies that de-
rive cosmological constraints from cluster clustering statistics
(Veropalumbo et al. 2016; Marulli et al. 2018; Moresco et al.
2021; Lesci et al. 2023; Romanello et al. 2025). This is espe-
cially true on quasi- and non-linear scales (r < 20 h−1Mpc),
where different redshift-space prescriptions begin to diverge and
where a large fraction of the cosmological information resides.

The Sloan Digital Sky Survey has provided constraints on
the growth of structure from CC at intermediate redshifts, both
at the level of spectroscopic clustering (Marulli et al. 2021) and
with photometrically selected samples (Sereno et al. 2015; Fu-

extensive cluster data sets with improved redshift characterisa-

uncertainties (Laureijs et al. 2011; Euclid Collaboration: Mel-
lier et al. 2025). Given the above challenges and opportunities,
an unbiased cosmological inference from CC requires accurate
modelling of the cluster bias, photometric redshift uncertainties,
small-scale clustering, and knowledge of the purity of the sam-
ple.
The paper is structured as follows: In Sect. 2 we detail the
methodology we apply for the measurement and modelling of
the redshift-space 2PCF in the PINOCCHIO mock catalogues. In
Sect. 3 we present our comparison of the model predictions to
the measured 2PCF. In Sect. 4, we highlight our main findings
and provide an outlook for future studies. Throughout this paper,
we assume the best-fit Planck 2013 ΛCDM cosmological param-
eters (Planck Collaboration et al. 2014, Table 2) which were used
in the construction of the PINOCCHIO mock catalogues.
2. Method
2.1. Simulated cluster catalogues
In order to quantify the accuracy of the Peacock & Dodds (1994),
Scoccimarro (2004), and Taruya et al. (2010) models, we test
their predictions with simulated cluster catalogues. We use past
lightcones simulated with the semi-analytic PINOCCHIO algo-
rithm (Monaco et al. 2002; Munari et al. 2017). The code adopts
third-order LPT to obtain the solution to ellipsoidal collapse. The
boxes have a side length of 3870 h−1 Mpc, contain 21603 par-
ticles and are in redshift space. A more extended summary of
the technical specifications is given in Fumagalli et al. (2025a).
We highlight that PINOCCHIO, being an LPT-based simulation,
(i) provides an approximate description of structure formation
on small scales, (ii) predicts halo bias with significant system-
atic uncertainty, and (iii) can exhibit scale-dependent deviations
from N-body simulations. While these effects can be part of the
origin of the discrepancy between the models and mocks due
to which a cut-off scale must be determined, the purpose of our
analysis is to provide a method to determine the aforementioned
scale without examining the origin of the cut-off. Further, we
choose PINOCCHIO to monitor the average behaviour of our in-
ferences across multiple realisations and because PINOCCHIO
provides more control over the halo mass function calibration.
The original catalogues cover a sky area of Ωsky = 10 313 deg2.
For the present study, we measure CC in the range 0 < z < 2,
impose a lower virial mass cut of 1014 h−1M⊙, which roughly
mimics the Euclid selection (Sartoris et al. 2016; Euclid Collab-
oration: Adam et al. 2019) and isolate a DR1-like footprint as
shown in Fig. 1. Specifically, we assume 500 deg2 in the North-
ern Galactic hemisphere and 1400 deg2 in the Southern Galactic
hemisphere. The resulting halo number densities per redshift bin
are shown in Table 1. In the real catalogues, the southern and
northern tiles will also differ in sky coverage, but also in their
photometric redshift uncertainties and in the selection function
of the sources therein.

and mis-identification of other sources as clusters would result in
a modification of the sample’s effective bias. However, the latter
could also impact the sample’s clustering properties more signif-
icantly. The magnitude of these effects will need to be assessed
once realistic estimates of the cluster purity from the Euclid de-
tection pipelines become available.

4. Conclusions
We developed and validated an inference framework to constrain
cosmological parameters from the multipoles of the two-point
cluster correlation function. This work represents a key step to-
ward preparing the Euclid pipelines for cosmological analyses
based on CC. Using semi-analytic mock data sets with a Euclid
DR1-like footprint, we applied the framework to infer fσ8 and
to determine the conditions under which redshift-space cluster-
ing models can yield accurate results on real data based on the re-
quirements in Sect. 2.5. Given DR1’s large uncertainties, we em-
ployed a set of permissive statistical tests and a suite of conser-
vative statistical tests to guide the determination of cut-off scales
in future, higher-S/N DRs. The former is based on goodness-of-
fit, while the latter additionally imposes stricter criteria for un-
biasedness, statistical calibration, and coverage across all mock
realisations. We based our main conclusions on the former.
We tested both realistic and optimistic redshift-uncertainty
scenarios, σz = 0.005(1 + z) and σz = 0.01(1 + z), and explored
the dispersion, Scoccimarro, and TNS models, allowing the cut-
off scales to vary with redshift and multipole. Our main results
are summarised below.
– The dispersion model is accurate for cluster clustering in
DR1 over the full redshift range down to 10 h−1 Mpc. The
resulting bias on fσ8 is less than 0.2 σ across the informa-
tive redshift bins. The exact threshold values considered ad-
missible for all statistical metrics will be reassessed for the
real data analysis.
– The hexadecapole adds no significant constraining power in
a DR1-like setting.
– The cut-off scale should be allowed to vary with multipole
order for upcoming DRs.
– The assumption of linear halo bias will restrict the modelling
of the multipoles of photometric cluster clustering to red-
shifts z < 1.2 for upcoming DRs.
– As the multipole order increases, the above effect shifts the
cut-off scale to higher values.
The latter behaviour is driven by a systematic underprediction of
the clustering amplitude that grows with redshift, redshift uncer-
tainties, and order of multipole as was shown in Fig. 2. The ori-
gin of this trend could be the leakage of nonlinear features in the
2PCF toward larger scales when photometric redshift uncertain-
ties are present. Under realistic redshift uncertainties, this mod-
elling limitation confines CC analyses to z < 1.2 unless at least
the treatment of nonlinear halo bias is improved, since clusters
of fixed mass cluster more strongly at higher redshifts, leading
to enhanced nonlinear effects (Fumagalli et al. 2025b).
The broader issue is the need for more accurate theoreti-
cal predictions in order to extend photometric 2PCFs to smaller
scales and higher redshifts for higher-S/N catalogues. In this
work, we adopted a linear halo-bias prescription feeding into
models that incorporate nonlinear structure formation to vary-
ing degrees. However, the halo field violates locality, Poisson
noise, and linearity assumptions (see Ahn et al. 2015, for a re-
view). Hence, a dedicated calibration of these models against
N-body simulations, including nonlinear and nonlocal halo bias,
as in Euclid Collaboration: Castander et al. (2025), is required
before halo clustering can be robustly pushed to smaller scales
in Euclid DR1 and to higher S/N data sets such as Euclid DR2/3.
Once such high-fidelity mocks become available, reliable fore-
casts for upcoming releases will be feasible. Finally, despite our
optimistic assumptions on completeness, purity, and halo mass
uncertainties, conditions sufficient for the purposes of this anal-
ysis, we recover only weakly informative posteriors on fσ8.
These results therefore represent a best-case scenario for DR1
and highlight the need for further improvements in modelling
ahead of future Euclid data releases.
Owing to the effects discussed above, we expect the mini-
mum cut-off scales in future data releases to become more re-
strictive unless these modelling improvements are implemented.
