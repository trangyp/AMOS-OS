Zhang and Strogatz [Phys. Rev. Lett. 127, 194101 (2021)] used high-dimensional simulations to
argue that basins of attraction in the Kuramoto ring are octopus-like: their volume scales as e−kq2
in the winding number q, nearly all of it concentrated in filamentary tentacles rather than near the
attractor. They conjecture this geometry to be common in high dimensions but note that high-
dimensional simulations are unreliable. We prove every feature of the octopus picture rigorously for
identical oscillators on a ring coupled by any smooth odd function strictly increasing on (−π, π).
INTRODUCTION
Basins of attraction are fundamental to the analy-
sis of multistable dynamical systems [1–3]: they deter-
mine which initial conditions lead to which long-term
outcomes.
In low-dimensional systems, basins can of-
ten be visualized and their structure understood analyti-
cally. In high-dimensional systems, however, basic quan-
titative properties — the volumes of individual basins
and the geometry of their boundaries — remain poorly
understood. A central difficulty is that high-dimensional
spaces come with many “curses” [4]: numerical sampling
of state space becomes astronomically expensive and ge-
ometric approximations that work in low dimensions can
fail catastrophically [5]. In addition, our mathematical
intuition fails miserably [6] when the dimension is high.
The Kuramoto model on a cycle graph provides one of
the cleanest and most studied settings for this problem [5,
7, 8]. For n identical oscillators with nearest-neighbor
coupling,
˙θi = sin(θi+1 −θi) + sin(θi−1 −θi),
i = 1, . . . , n, (1)
with periodic boundary conditions, the attractors are the
q-twisted states θj = 2πjq/n+c, in which the phases wind
q times around the ring. Understanding how the basins
of these states partition the n-dimensional state space
Tn is a problem with direct implications in science and
technology, including power grid stability [9], training of
deep neural networks [10], and other networked oscillator
systems [11].
In a foundational paper, Wiley, Strogatz, and Gir-
van [7] conjectured that the basin sizes follow a Gaussian
law: the probability of converging to the q-twisted state
scales as e−kq2. This was later challenged by Delabays,
Tyloo, and Jacquod [8], who estimated basin sizes via
local measurements near each attractor and reported ex-
ponential scaling e−k|q| instead. Zhang and Strogatz [5]
resolved this apparent contradiction by demonstrating,
through high-dimensional simulations, that the basins
have an octopus-like geometry: nearly all their volume
lies in long tentacles that reach far from the attractor,
which local measurements miss entirely. Beyond settling
the scaling debate, their paper reported a striking set of
further geometric properties—a universal master distri-
bution for distances between states and their attractors,
the existence of rays that cross basin boundaries many
times, and the complete failure of hypercube approxima-
tions to capture basin volume. The authors themselves
were careful to note, however, that all of these observa-
tions rested on numerical simulations in high-dimensional
spaces, where reliable sampling is notoriously difficult,
and that rigorous proofs were lacking.
Analytical progress was made in [12], which identified
the dynamical mechanism behind the Gaussian scaling:
starting from a random initial condition, the winding
number stabilizes at a time t ∝log n, before long-range
dependence between oscillators can develop. Introduc-
ing the phase-difference variables ηi = θi+1 −θi, a CLT
argument for the winding number computed in terms of
η = (η1, . . . , ηn−1) establishes the e−kq2 scaling.
This
approach required trajectories to first enter the invariant
region I = {|ηi| < π/2 for all i}, which is only partially
rigorous for the sine coupling; and the rich geometric pic-
ture of [5]—the master distance distribution, the tentacle
structure, the failure of hypercubes—was left entirely un-
addressed.
In this Letter we remove these obstacles in one step.
We replace sin by a general coupling function f that is
C1, odd, 2π-periodic, and strictly increasing on (−π, π).
This condition, which sin narrowly fails to satisfy glob-
ally, has a decisive consequence: the region J = {|ηi| <
π for all i} is flow-invariant, and the winding number q
is therefore conserved for all t ≥0. Since random initial
conditions satisfy ηi ∈(−π, π) almost surely, the winding
number of the final state equals that of the initial condi-
tion exactly—with no waiting time, no log n bound, and
no approximation. From this single observation, all the
geometric properties reported numerically in [5] follow as
rigorous theorems.
Specifically, we prove: (i) the e−kq2 basin scaling (The-
orem 3); (ii) the master distance distribution and the
origin of the constant 1.81 (Theorem 4); (iii) the ten-

DISCUSSION
Zhang and Strogatz [5] conjectured that the octopus-
like basin geometry they observed numerically in the Ku-
ramoto ring should be common in high-dimensional mul-
tistable systems. Our results support this conjecture by
exhibiting a large natural family of models — identical
oscillators on a ring coupled by any smooth odd function
strictly increasing on (−π, π) — for which every feature
of the octopus picture holds rigorously: basin volumes
are Gaussian in the winding number, the master distance
concentrates at
p
π2/3 for every attractor, almost every
ray is equidistributed on state space, and the basin’s head
is tightly controlled. The picture is not a feature of any
particular coupling; it is a structural property of a whole
class of landscapes.
More broadly,
the gradient-flow energy E(θ)
=
P
j F(θj+1 −θj) is a member of a family of landscapes
with many local minima separated by low-dimensional
bottlenecks — a family that includes spin glasses,
jammed sphere packings [20], and the self-attention dy-
namics of transformer networks [13, 14]. The rigorous
picture obtained here may serve as a tractable model of
what to expect in other high-dimensional multistable sys-
tems, and as a template for the geometric statements one
might hope to prove elsewhere.
