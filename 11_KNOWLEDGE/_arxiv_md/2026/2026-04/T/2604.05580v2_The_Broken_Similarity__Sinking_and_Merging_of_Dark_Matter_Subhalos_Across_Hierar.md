We investigate hierarchical mergers among subhalos within a ΛCDM simulation using the hbt+ subhalo finder. Unlike previous
methods, hbt+ tracks subhalo evolution across hierarchy levels, identifying the coalescence of subhalo cores in phase-space as
a “sinking” event. This coalescence marks a distinct stalled phase in orbital decay, providing a physically motivated and natural
definition of a resolved merger. Our main findings include: 1) Over 90% of sinking events occur between adjacent subhalo
levels, while cross-level pathways arise from tidal stripping, group infall, and numerical constraints. 2) Resolved mergers are
predominantly major mergers (mass ratios > 1:10), while the occurrence of minor mergers decreases with the dynamical age of the
host halo. 3) Although deep-level subhalos have low mass ratios relative to the host halo, their high mass ratios relative to direct
parents significantly boost merger statistics. Consequently, the satellite-satellite merger rate can rival or exceed the central-satellite
rate at lower mass thresholds. 4) Satellite-satellite mergers are spatially biased toward the outer regions of the host, suggesting that
the central tidal field suppresses their orbital decay. 5) A bidirectional sinking detection recovers 32% more sinking events than
the original algorithm, revealing that child-dispersion-driven mergers are dominated by tidal heating at the final stage of sinking,
while parent-dispersion-driven and doubly-identified events proceed primarily via orbital decay. Altogether, these results reveal a
complex landscape of hierarchical satellite mergers that deviate from the self-similarity of host halo mergers, due to additional
physical processes including dynamical friction and the scale-dependent halo growth history.

Introduction
Dark matter subhalos are self-bound substructures embedded
within larger dark matter halos, forming through hierarchical
structure assembly in the ΛCDM paradigm [1-4]. After accre-
tion, massive subhalos undergo orbital decay primarily driven
by dynamical friction, sinking toward the host’s center. This
sinking can lead to mergers between subhalos and therefore
of the galaxies that they host [5-8], thereby contributing to the
growth of central galaxies. Hence, determining how rapidly
and how often such subhalo mergers occur is a crucial step in
modeling galaxy evolution.
A key quantity in this context is the subhalo merger
timescale, which characterizes the time required for an
infalling subhalo to sink and coalesce with its host. Several nu-
merical studies have derived fitting functions for this timescale
using both idealized and cosmological simulations [9-13].
These works consistently show that the infall redshift is an
important factor, as it sets the dynamical time of the host halo
at accretion: subhalos accreted at higher redshifts (earlier
times) generally experience shorter dynamical times and thus
merge more rapidly. Other critical factors established by these
works include the orbital parameters (e.g., circularity) and
the subhalo-to-host mass ratio at infall: subhalos on more
radial orbits or with higher mass ratios generally merge more
rapidly.
Merger timescale prescriptions play a central role in semi-
analytic models [14-17], where they are used to translate the
merger history of subhalos into predictions for galaxy-galaxy
mergers. This connection enables SAMs to study a range
of merger-driven physical processes, such as morphological
transformations [18-20], starbursts [21-24], and AGN activ-
ity [25,26].
The subhalo merger rate is another key quantity, describing
the frequency of merger events in a population [27-33]. While
it is fundamentally defined as the number of mergers occur-
ring per unit time per subhalo, the precise definition varies
across studies. For instance, some works measure the rate
per descendant halo [27], while others report it per progenitor
subhalo [28]. Despite these methodological differences, esti-
mates from different models are found to be broadly consistent,
typically agreeing within a factor of two [34].
A key complication in these studies is that there is no uni-
versal definition of what a subhalo “merger” is. The lack
of a unique definition complicates the interpretation of sim-
ulation data and its application to galaxy formation models.
Definitions range from disruption below the resolution of
the simulation [27], coalescence defined by multiple progeni-
tor subhalos sharing a common descendant subhalo [12,28],
losing a given fraction of the initial specific orbital angular
momentum [11, 13], or reaching a certain physical separa-
tion [35]. To further complicate matters, the choice of the
(arbitrary) threshold used within these definitions can mod-
ify the measured merger rate. For instance, J. M. Solanes et
al. [36] demonstrated that defining subhalo mergers based on a
threshold of 5% of the initial specific angular momentum leads
to the premature termination of tracked orbital decay. These
inconsistencies are particularly critical for mergers driven by
dynamical-friction sinking, because the choice of an arbitrary
threshold can systematically bias the inferred physics. The
variety of merger definitions in use reflects our insufficient
understanding of a core question regarding the process: what
defines a merger physically? Addressing this question can pro-
vide a more self-consistent and physically motivated definition
of merger events.
Another complication stems from the hierarchical nature
of accretion itself. It is common for subhalos to be accreted
as members of groups (“group infall”), which naturally estab-
lishes a nested, multi-level hierarchy within the host halo [37-
42]. This hierarchy entails that merging with the central sub-
halo is not the only fate for accreted subhalos, and some will
merge with other satellite subhalos during their orbital evolu-
tion. Although central-satellite and satellite-satellite mergers
are often analyzed separately, detailed studies that track the
fate of accreted groups of subhalos and quantify the satellite-
satellite merger rate remain limited.
By leveraging a dark-matter-only (DMO) cosmological sim-
ulation analyzed with the hbt+ subhalo finder [43, 44], we
investigate subhalo mergers driven by dynamical friction. Un-
like methods that rely on thresholds measured on the scale
of the subhalo (e.g., bound particle number or orbital angular
momentum), hbt+ determines the phase-space coalescence of
a subhalo with the dense core of another subhalo. We refer
to mergers identified through this physically motivated cri-
terion as subhalo sinking (see Section 2.2 for details). The
term “sinking” is used interchangeably with “merger” through-
out this work, but specifically denotes those merger events
identified by the hbt+ algorithm. Established terms such as
“central-satellite merger” and “major merger” retain their con-
ventional meanings. This work aims to:
(i) Characterize the final fates of sunken subhalos within
multi-level hierarchical systems.
(ii) Introduce a systematic definition of the subhalo sinking
rate that enables quantitative comparison across differ-
ent hierarchical levels; and
(iii) Examine how the sinking rate and radial location of
sinking events depend on redshift, subhalo mass, and
host halo mass.
Although the mergers among subhalos are rooted in the largely
self-similar merger tree of the host halo [40], we will show that

Methods
2.1
Simulation & Subhalo Finder
Our analysis is based on a dark-matter-only simulation from
the CosmicGrowth suite [45], specifically the L600 run. The
simulation adopts a WMAP cosmology with parameters
H = 71 km s−1 Mpc−1, ΩM0 = 0.268 and ΩΛ = 0.732, and
follows the evolution of 30723 particles within a periodic
box of side length 600 Mpc h−1.
The particle mass is
mp ≈5.5 × 108 M⊙h−1. The gravitational force is softened
with a Plummer-equivalent softening length of ϵ = 3.3 kpc h−1
in the comoving coordinate.
In this work, halo masses
are defined as the mass enclosed within the radius where
its inner matter density equals the virial density predicted
from the spherical collapse model, ρvir = ∆virρcrit, where
∆vir = 18π2 + 82[Ωm(z) −1] −39[Ωm(z) −1]2 [46].
We identify subhalos and construct their merger trees using
the hbt+ (Hierarchical Bound-Tracing) algorithm [44]. In
hbt+, the tracking algorithm operates in two distinct regimes:
(1) when a subhalo is the central structure of its own host halo,
its bound particles are continuously updated using the particle
reservoir of the corresponding FoF group; (2) once it falls into
a more-massive host and becomes a satellite, hbt+ records
its particle membership at the infall time and subsequently
removes particles that become unbound during its orbital evo-
lution. A subhalo is considered resolved and is tracked as a
self-bound object as long as it retains at least Nmin = 20 bound
particles. If the number of bound particles of a subhalo falls
below this threshold, this subhalo is flagged as unresolved.
hbt+ subsequently tracks it as an “orphan” subhalo, following
only the position and velocity of the most bound particle when
it was last resolved thereafter.
hbt+ provides a complete and self-consistent record of
the hierarchical relationships between subhalos, tracing their
origin to group accretion events. Within this structure, the
hierarchical level (denoted by the symbol ℓhereafter) of a
subhalo is quantified by the number of distinct host subhalos
that sequentially contain the subhalo. Specifically, the central
subhalo is designated as ℓ= 0. Based on the hierarchical level,
we define the following terminology to describe hierarchical
relationships:
Child Subhalo: A subhalo (of level ℓ) is considered the
child of the specific subhalo of level ℓ−1 or shallower levels,
in which it is sequentially embedded.
Direct Parent Subhalo: The immediate host subhalo
within which a child subhalo is directly embedded. This
corresponds to the host subhalo at the next shallower
hierarchical level.
Parent Subhalo: The set of all hosts that contain the child
subhalo at successively shallower hierarchical levels. This
includes its direct parent and all ancestors up to the central
subhalo.
For example, a level-3 subhalo is embedded within a level-2
subhalo (its direct parent), which in turn is embedded within a
level-1 subhalo. This entire nested structure ultimately resides
within the central subhalo of the host halo. Thus, this example
subhalo has three parents: the central subhalo, the level-1, and
the level-2 subhalos.
2.2

by dynamical friction, which continuously extracts orbital
energy and angular momentum, leading to a gradual inspiral
and eventual coalescence [47]. To identify the moment at
which two subhalos coalesce, hbt+ employs a phase-space
distance criterion.

from the parent subhalo: we compute the spatial (σx) and
velocity (σv) dispersion of its 20 most bound particles, which
is intended to represent the subhalo core. The phase-space
separation δs between a child subhalo and its parent is then

subhalo core at the given simulation resolution. Subsequent

the central density profile, and discreteness-induced fluctua-

genuine coalescence in a dark-matter-only simulation at a
given resolution. Reaching the δs,p threshold typically corre-
sponds to the end of the orbital-decay phase.

criterion. This subhalo also experiences a rapid inspiral phase

refined sinking algorithm successfully identifies additional
sinking events that the single-distance (δs,p only) criterion
would miss.

the single-side version is presented in Section 6.2, where the
physical mechanisms of sinking are also discussed. Generally,
this modification detects approximately 32% more sinking

Mvir > 1012M⊙h−1, regardless of the redshift. Due to tidal
heating, the child subhalo usually quickly disrupts in a few
dynamical times in the reverse-link case, so that omitting these
sinking events does not have a significant influence on the final
subhalo population. Nevertheless, we include these reverse-
linked cases in our analysis for a more complete census of
subhalo sinking.

3
Hierarchical Sinking Pathways of Subhalos
The prevailing framework in the literature classifies subhalo
mergers into two broad channels: central-satellite and satellite-
satellite mergers. While conceptually clear, this binary scheme
does not fully capture the complexity inherent in hierarchically
structured systems. In the subhalo mergers defined via sinking,
both channels encompass a diversity of sinking pathways,
as satellites themselves often host their own substructures,
creating a nested hierarchy.
To move beyond this simplified view and reveal the detailed
fate of subhalos at each specific level of this hierarchy, we
introduce a refined classification based on the precise hierar-
chical levels of the sunken subhalos. This approach allows
us to disentangle the complex web of subhalo sinking and
quantify how each sinking pathway depends on the level of a
subhalo within the subhalo hierarchy.
Figure 3 shows the distribution of parent levels for sunken
child subhalos binned by their own level ℓsub. Our analysis
reveals a clear hierarchical sinking pattern. The vast majority
of subhalo sinking events occur between a subhalo and its
direct parent (level ℓsub−1). This is consistent with the findings
in R. E. Angulo et al. [29], where they demonstrated that
subhalos involved in satellite-satellite mergers are dynamically
associated with each other prior to infall, rather than forming
through random encounters within the dark matter halo.
However, cross-level sinking also constitutes a non-
negligible fraction of sinking events, and this fraction
increases slightly with ℓsub.
For these cross-level events,
the level difference between the child and its final parent
is predominantly 2.
Notably, we find that only a very
small fraction (≈0.5%) of sinking events occur directly
between the central subhalos (level 0) and child subhalos
with ℓsub > 2. It indicates that nearly all the central-satellite
mergers are contributed by the subhalos and sub-subhalos in
the simulation. Furthermore, we find that this parent-level
distribution exhibits little dependence on the mass of the main
host halo, indicating that the underlying sinking process is
governed by local hierarchy rather than global halo properties.
We classify these cross-level sinking events into four dis-
tinct pathways based on the evolutionary sequence of the child
and its direct parent:
• Overtaking Sinking: The child sinks into a shallower-
level parent before its direct parent becomes a sunken
subhalo. This could correspond to the case where a
deeper-level subhalo becomes dynamically dissociated
from its direct parent due to the tidal force from its host.
• Trailing Sinking: The direct parent sinks first, and then
the child sinks into a shallower-level parent.

Our analysis of several pathways of the subhalo sinking
has important applications in the semi-analytical model. In
some models, when a subhalo group is accreted, the merger
clocks of its satellites are reset based on the new host halo
properties [57,58]. Our findings offer a more nuanced view.
The prevalence of next-level sinking supports the idea that
many satellites remain bound to their group halo, for which
maintaining the original merger timescale may be appropri-
ate [59,60].

The Subhalo Sinking Rate
The merger rates between subhalos and galaxies have been
extensively studied in the literature [27, 28, 30-34]. In this
section, we examine the subhalo merger rate identified via
subhalo sinking and revisit subhalo sinking by introducing a
finer classification based on the hierarchical level ℓof satel-
lites within the subhalo structure. We focus exclusively on
sinking events between child subhalos and their direct parent
subhalos, as these account for the vast majority of sinking
events (> 90%). We compare the sinking rates across different
groups and halos and find that satellite-satellite sinking events
constitute a non-negligible fraction of the overall sunken pop-
ulation.
4.1
Two Complementary Definitions of the Sinking Rate
Our finding that subhalo sinking occurs predominantly be-
tween a child and its direct parent motivates two complemen-
tary definitions:
1. Group-level sinking rate. A direct parent subhalo of
level ℓ−1 and its children of level ℓcan be defined as a
subhalo group of ℓ−1 within a dark matter halo. Investigating
the subhalo sinking rate within each such group provides a
quantitative measure of merger rate across hierarchical levels

Key Results and Interpretation
Figure 5 presents the group-level sinking rate fgroup measured
from two group mass bins at three different redshifts. We
find that the subhalo sinking often occurs between massive
child subhalos (µrel > 0.1) and their direct parents for ℓ= 1, 2.
This is expected because subhalos with large relative mass
ratios typically experience stronger dynamical friction and
sink more rapidly. Another key finding is that the amplitude
of the level-2 sinking rate at z = 0 is approximately a factor of
four lower than that of level-1. However, the sinking rate of
these two levels becomes comparable at high redshifts (z = 1
and z = 1.9). This suppression could be attributed to three
possible factors:
• Starvation of child subhalos. Once a satellite group first
falls into a larger halo, it ceases to accrete new member
subhalos. As a result, its member subhalo population
may become largely exhausted by z = 0, leading to a
lower merger rate.
• Tidal mass loss. As deeper level subhalos on average
have a larger mass ratio relative to their host halo at
infall time [40], they tend to experience stronger tidal
mass loss rates [61]. In addition, deeper-level subha-
los tend to be accreted earlier, thus experiencing tidal
stripping for a longer time. As a result, deeper level
subhalos are more likely to become unresolved at the
final redshift of interest, given the finite mass resolu-
tion of the simulation. Since we only count resolved
mergers in this work, the merger rate of level-2 subha-
los may thus be partly hidden by resolution limitations
compared with that of level-1 subhalos.

4.3
Comparison to Previous Work
A. R. Wetzel et al. [28] report that satellite-satellite mergers
constitute only a negligible fraction of the overall subhalo
merger population compared with the central-satellite mergers.
In contrast, we find a comparable satellite-satellite sinking rate
in both Figures 5 and 8. This apparent discrepancy, however,
stems from differences in definitions and sample selection,
rather than indicating a fundamental inconsistency.
A. R. Wetzel et al. [28] quantify the contribution of satellite-
satellite mergers by measuring their fraction among all merger
events whose descendant subhalos lie within a fixed bound
mass bin (e.g., [1012, 1013] M⊙h−1). They find that satellite-
satellite mergers account for only 5%-15% of the total. This re-
sult is a natural consequence of their sample selection: within
the descendant mass bins they consider, central subhalos dom-
inate the population, while satellites constitute only a small
fraction. Consequently, most merger events involving these
descendants are central-satellite mergers, and satellite-satellite
mergers are inevitably subdominant. While this statistic use-
fully characterizes the relative abundance of satellite-satellite
mergers in a descendant-selected sample, it does not directly
measure how frequently subhalos of shallower levels receive
merged subhalos from deeper levels, nor does it reveal how
the merger rate varies across hierarchical levels.
Our group-level sinking rate adopts a similar descendant-mass
selection but takes a different normalization perspective.
Rather than comparing counts to the total sunken population,
we classify sinking events by the level ℓof the sunken subhalo
and normalize each class by the number of its parent groups,
Ngroup,ℓ−1, i.e., the population that could host such an event.
It directly quantifies the intrinsic sinking frequency for each
hierarchical level within its own parent group and enables
meaningful comparisons across different levels. Under this
definition, the comparable group-level sinking rates of level-1
and level-2 subhalos at high redshift indicate that, for a given
peak mass, a central subhalo is no more likely to undergo a
merger than a level-1 satellite.
The halo-level sinking rate captures a fundamentally differ-
ent statistic. It measures the total number of sinking events
occurring within a host halo, aggregated across all hierarchical
levels and normalized per halo. This definition is similar in
spirit to the merger rate adopted by R. E. Angulo et al. [29].
In this perspective, the steady increase of the level-2 sinking
rate toward lower µpeak—in contrast to the decreasing trend of
level-1—reflects the growing contribution from the abundant
population of low-mass level-1 subhalo groups.
Together, these two complementary definitions demonstrate
that satellite-satellite sinking events are not a negligible mi-
nority, but rather an important process that must be accounted
for in any realistic model of subhalo evolution.

each sinking event, the radial distance of the sunken subhalo
is normalized by the virial radius of its host halo at the sink-

normalized by the subhalo number density profile, which is

allowing us to isolate the preferred locations of sinking events.
When classified by the nature of the sinking partner, the radial
distributions of sinking events fall into two distinct categories.
Central-satellite sinking events, in which a subhalo sinks

the common physical driver: dynamical friction operating
deep within the host’s potential well. Notably, a small frac-

(r ≳0.5Rvir). These distant events can be attributed either to
central subhalos with unusually diffuse spatial distributions
or, more commonly, to satellite subhalos whose assigned posi-
tions are associated with large core spatial dispersions (σR) via
the reverse-link criterion. In both cases, the sunken subhalos
have σR values in the top 5% of the overall spatial dispersion
distribution (Figure 11), confirming their anomalously diffuse
nature.

2, or level-3→level-1), display a qualitatively different behav-

Discussion
6.1
Resolution Dependence
In this work, we employ the sinking algorithm to identify the
physically resolved merger phase between subhalos within a
given simulation resolution. This approach is motivated by
the observed two-phase evolution of subhalos: a rapid inspiral
followed by a stalled, oscillatory regime near the parent core.
To evaluate the robustness and applicability of this framework,
we examine how the characteristic core scales (σx, σv) de-
pend on subhalo bound mass and vary across simulations with
different resolutions.
Figure 11 shows the distribution of σx and σv as a function
of subhalo bound mass for both central and satellite subhalos.
The median spatial dispersion σx decreases with subhalo mass
and asymptotes to a value above the force softening length
for subhalos containing at least 200 particles. This implies
that, when applying this method, the minimum requirement
is that the parent subhalo must be well-resolved (e.g., Mvir ≥
1012 M⊙h−1 or mparent ≥1012 M⊙h−1 in this work) for the core
scales to be reliably measured. The velocity dispersion σv,
in contrast, increases with mass, reflecting the depth of the
gravitational potential. Notably, the median values of both
distributions are nearly identical for satellites and centrals,
indicating that these core scales are robust to tidal stripping
until a subhalo becomes extremely disrupted. These results
establish that the stalling scales of orbital decay are set by the
subhalo’s intrinsic potential and the simulation’s numerical
resolution.
The inherent dependence of σx and σv on numerical reso-
lution becomes evident when comparing our results with the
higher-resolution Millennium-II analysis in J. Han et al. [44],
which adopts a softening length ϵ = 1 kpc h−1 and a particle
mass mp = 6.89 × 106 M⊙h−1. In the σx distribution, the
median reported in J. Han et al. [44] asymptotes to approxi-
mately the softening length, whereas our median lies at about
1.5 times our softening length. This offset likely stems from
differences in simulation codes, softening schemes, or the sys-
tematic shift induced by adopting different softening lengths.
For σv, while the overall trends with subhalo mass are consis-
tent with the fitting formula in J. Han et al. [44], our measured
velocity dispersions are systematically larger. This is a direct
consequence of particle mass resolution: for a given subhalo
mass, the 20 most bound particles (from which σx and σv are
computed) sample a larger region of phase space than in a
higher-resolution simulation, due to the larger particle mass in
our simulation. This naturally leads to a higher velocity disper-
sion according to the rising velocity dispersion profile in the
inner region of dark matter halos. The resolution dependence
underscores that a sinking event identified at a given resolu-
tion does not necessarily correspond to a genuine merger in
the limit of infinite or even higher resolution. Nevertheless,
within the constraints of the current simulation, these events
serve as a well-motivated and physically diagnostic proxy for
the coalescence of subhalo cores.
Moreover, the fact that stalling scales are set by subhalo-
intrinsic properties, rather than by host halo scales, carries a
key implication: it challenges merger criteria defined on scales
comparable to or larger than the subhalo itself, particularly
those relying on arbitrarily chosen static thresholds—such
as a subhalo crossing 0.1Rvir or losing a fixed fraction (e.g.,
95%) of its initial specific angular momentum. The endpoint
of orbital decay is not a universal fraction of the host virial

6.2
Physical Mechanisms of Subhalo Sinking
6.2.1 Classification of Sinking Population
To assess whether a bidirectional sinking criterion recovers
more physically coalesced subhalos than a single-direction
approach, we perform a dedicated hbt+ run on the L600 sim-
ulation with the MergeTrappedParticle option disabled.
This configuration allows us to continue tracking δs,p and δs,c
for sunken subhalos until they become unresolved, thereby en-
abling the identification of subhalos that satisfy only δs,c < 2
during their evolution.
We first examine the distribution of satellite subhalos in
the 2D plane of δs,p and δs,c, following J. Han et al. [44].
Figure 13 shows this distribution for all resolved subhalos with
respect to their direct parents in host halos of mass Mvir,0 >
1012 M⊙h−1 at z = 0. The two marginalized distributions
reveal a clear bimodality: one component consists of subhalos
whose distance values cluster around tens, with the majority
still orbiting and not yet phase-mixed with their direct parents;
the other component corresponds to sunken subhalos. This
bimodal pattern persists at z = 1, while at z = 1.9 the sunken
population is noticeably smaller.
To separate the subhalo population directly into sunken
and orbiting components, we measure the minimum value of
(δs,p, δs,c) attained by each subhalo while it remains resolved.
Our sample includes all subhalos hosted by FoF groups with
parent halo masses Mvir,0 > 1012 M⊙h−1 at z = 0, including
those that become unresolved by z = 0 but were resolved
at earlier times. Figure 14 presents the distribution of these
minimum distances for the z = 0 population, shown separately
for resolved, unresolved, and combined samples.
The minimum-distance distribution differs markedly
from the distribution of current distances.
For resolved
subhalos, the distribution clearly separates into three distinct
sub-populations:
a sunken component, and an orbiting
component (defined by min δs,p ≥2 and min δs,c ≥2) that
further splits into two groups—one with minimum distances
clustered around a few tens, and another with minimum
distances between approximately 2 and 10. This bimodality
within the orbiting population reflects two distinct orbital
stages: the subhalos from the former group are still on their
first passage, while the latter have undergone at least one
passage and been substantially altered by dynamical friction.
For unresolved subhalos, the minimum-distance distribu-
tion shows only two sub-populations. Its orbiting component
spans values from 2 to 100 but peaks between 2 and 10, indi-
cating that most unresolved subhalos become unresolvable at a
stage when they are already strongly influenced by dynamical
friction.

6.2.2 Driving Mechanisms: Orbital Decay versus Tidal Heat-
ing
We have shown representative case studies for Regions 1, 2,
and 3 in Figures 1, 2, and 15, respectively. Specifically, in
reverse-link cases, tidal heating appears to be the dominant
mechanism driving the sinking process, causing δs,c to drop
below the threshold. In contrast, parent-dispersion-driven
and doubly identified sunken subhalos show less evidence of
significant heating; their cores remain compact throughout the
sinking process.
To statistically quantify the prevalence of tidal heating, we
measure the ratio of spatial dispersion before and after each
sinking event, defined as ⟨σ2
R,pre⟩/⟨σ2
R,post⟩. For each event,
we compute the average squared spatial dispersion over the
three snapshots prior to sinking and over the three snapshots
spanning the sinking moment and its immediate aftermath.
The results are shown in Figure 16.
In child-dispersion-driven cases, the majority (> 70%) of
sunken satellite subhalos experience significant tidal heating,
with their σ2
R values increasing by a factor of ∼2 after sinking,
while their parent subhalos remain nearly intact and compact.
This confirms that reverse-link sinking events are primarily
driven by heating at the interface between the two evolutionary
phases. A direct consequence of the substantial tidal heating is
that the child subhalo quickly disrupts in about 1-2 dynamical
times, which happens to the majority of the child-dispersion-
driven cases.
Conversely, in parent-dispersion-driven and doubly iden-
tified sinking events, the sunken satellites remain compact
throughout. Although the spatial dispersion of their parent
subhalos may expand slightly, the child subhalos themselves
show no evidence of tidal heating. In these cases, the transi-
tion between the two-stage evolution is driven primarily by
orbital decay rather than by heating.
These findings have important implications for the devel-
opment of subhalo finders aimed at identifying subhalo merg-
ers. For example, the newly developed subhalo finder Blood-
hound [65] traces subhalo disruption through variations in the
structural parameter cV and claims that subhalo mergers are
captured as part of these disruption events. Our results suggest
that relying exclusively on this parameter may be insufficient
to identify all potential subhalo mergers, particularly those
driven by parent-dispersion or identified through the doubly
linked criterion, which do not exhibit the same heating signa-
tures and are more persistent in subsequent mass evolution.
7
Summary and Conclusions
In this study, we have focused on the common merger
pathway associated with subhalo sinking—orbital decay
driven by dynamical friction. We use the term “sinking”
synonymously with “merger”, both referring exclusively to the
dynamical-friction-driven merger. The hbt+ subhalo finder
identifies such sinking events via the phase-space coalescence
of two well-resolved subhalo cores, a criterion motivated
by the two-phase evolution of subhalos: a rapid inspiral
followed by a stalled regime near the parent core, where
orbital decay does not proceed indefinitely. The kinematic
separation between satellites and their parents asymptotically
approaches the parent’s core scales, σx and σv, measured
from its 20 most bound particles. As shown in Figures 1
and 2, orbital decay stalls once satellites reach these scales,
producing resolution-dependent “core stalling” consistent
with theoretical expectations. This framework enables us
to trace dynamical-friction-driven mergers across multiple
hierarchical levels. Our main findings are summarized as
follows:
1. Most subhalos sink into their direct parents, but
cross-level pathways exist. In Figure 3, we find that
deep-level subhalos predominantly sink into their direct
parents. However, tidal stripping, group infall, and
resolution-driven disruption naturally generate several
cross-level sinking pathways.
2. Resolved mergers are predominantly major merg-
ers. Figure 5 reveals that sunken subhalos identified
by the group-level rate fgroup are predominantly major

sinking events of the deeper-level subhalos preferen-

6. Orbital decay and tidal heating are the two main
mechanisms at the final stage of subhalo sinking.

prise the majority of the sunken population, primarily
driven by dynamical friction. An additional ∼30% of
sunken subhalos can be identified as the parent subhalo
trapped in the core of its child, primarily driven by tidal
heating of the child subhalo.
These results establish a coherent picture in which subhalo
sinking in cosmological simulations is governed jointly by dy-
namical friction, hierarchical accretion, and numerical resolu-
tion, providing valuable insights for interpreting the evolution
of satellite systems and for informing semi-analytic galaxy
formation models with physically motivated multi-level sink-
ing pathways. Unlike the self-similar host halo merger tree,
these subhalo mergers break away from self-similarity due to
the additional physical processes involved.
A caveat of the current analysis is that the sinking identi-
fication is conditioned on a given numerical resolution, due
to the dependence of the subhalo core size on the resolution.
In addition, subhalos that go below the mass resolution are
not studied either. One way to overcome these limitations is
to calibrate a physical equation for dynamical friction, likely
coupled to the process of subhalo mass loss [61,66]. In the
current work, we have focused on the statistics of the mergers
relative to the final (peak) mass of the host halo. It would be
more advantageous to work with the parameters of the merger
pair defined when the subhalo is first accreted into its direct
parent, which are more relevant to the orbital evolution of
the subhalo. We leave these investigations to future work.
Nevertheless, the current analysis paves the way towards phys-
ical understanding and unified modelling of the hierarchical
mergers among subhalos and satellites.
