Abstract: In this paper, we reformulate the Schrodinger equation in gauge-theoretic
terms. Starting from the Madelung representation, we rewrite the conserved probability-
current using gauge fields, namely a one-form gauge field in the (2 + 1)-dimensional
theory and a two-form gauge field in the (3 + 1)-dimensional theory.
This gives a
local equivalence between the Schrodinger equation, quantum hydrodynamics, and a
gauge formulation, while the global information is carried by the quantization of phase
winding around zeros of the wavefunction. We then explore how this correspondence
organizes structures on both sides of the duality. On the gauge side, BF couplings
to additional one-forms describe electromagnetic coupling, Berry connections, spinor
dynamics, projected non-abelian adiabatic connections, and intrinsic holonomy, while
Chern–Simons term generate Hopf functionals, charge-flux attachment, and anyonic
sectors. In the presence of boundaries, the topological terms produce edge degrees of
freedom and boundary charge algebras. Finally, in the nonlinear regime with a Bogoli-
ubov sound mode, the dual two-form description relates acoustic memory, large gauge
transformations and the corresponding soft theorem, organizing them into an infrared
triangle.

Introduction
It is often a useful moment in field theory when an equation that first appeared as
dynamics turns out, after the right change of variables, to be kinematics. Recent work
on fluids has repeatedly exhibited precisely this phenomenon.
Once the continuity
equation is rewritten in the language of differential forms, the conserved density-current
sector can be packaged into gauge fields, and structures that ordinarily enter only
later—vorticity, topological couplings, boundary charges, memory observables—move
to the foreground from the start. The recent gauge-theoretic formulations of shallow
water, incompressible Euler flow, perfect fluids in terms of Kalb–Ramond fields, and
more general fluid/p-form systems have made this lesson especially vivid [1–6]. The
lesson itself is simple, but it also extends beyond any one example, as conservation laws
often signal that a theory wants to be rewritten in gauge language.
The starting point of this paper is that the nonrelativistic Schrodinger equation
is much closer to this pattern than one might first think. The wavefunction carries a
density and a conserved probability current. Away from its nodal set it also defines
a phase and therefore a velocity potential. In the usual Madelung representation the
imaginary part of the Schrodinger equation becomes the continuity equation, while the
real part becomes a Hamilton–Jacobi equation corrected by the quantum potential.
In that form the theory already looks like a compressible fluid, and this viewpoint is
classical and well established, going back to Madelung and developed systematically,
especially for spinful systems, by Takabayasi and others [7–25]. The standard hydro-
dynamic presentation, however, leaves current conservation as one of the equations of
motion. Here we instead take it as the entry point to a gauge description.
The gauge structure then follows from the conserved Schrodinger current.
Its
Hodge dual is closed, and locally closed forms are exact. In the (2 + 1)-dimensional
theory the density-current sector can therefore be written as the field strength of an
abelian one-form gauge field, while in the (3 + 1)-dimensional theory it is naturally
encoded by the field strength of an abelian two-form gauge field.
This dimension-
dependent structure is the same one that appears in the Kalb–Ramond description of
perfect fluids and in the general fluid/p-form dictionary [5, 6]. The continuity equation
is no longer an equation of motion, becoming instead a Bianchi identity. What remains
as dynamics are the conditions that, in hydrodynamic language, express irrotational
flow and the quantum Euler equation. This correspondence is local. The gauge and
fluid variables encode only derivatives of the phase, while the original wavefunction im-
poses the additional requirement of single-valuedness. Around nodes of ψ the missing
information reappears as quantized circulation. Thus the Schrodinger equation, quan-
tum hydrodynamics, and the gauge theory are three local presentations of the same

system, with global sectors labeled by the phase winding data.
The point of this formulation is modest, since no extra degrees of freedom are in-
troduced and no strong/weak duality between different quantum theories is assumed.
Its main advantage is that the gauge language makes the topological and symmetry
content of the theory explicit. This role of gauge variables appears in several closely re-
lated problems. In shallow water theory, the gauge formulation identifies the linearized
Poincare waves with Maxwell–Chern–Simons modes and the coastal Kelvin waves with
chiral edge modes [1], while the topological origin of equatorial Kelvin and Yanai waves
was clarified from the bulk topology of the Poincare bands [26]. For Euler flow, the
gauge formulation gives a topological argument for the quantization of the Euler Hop-
fion [2]. In related fluid and topological systems, gauge variables have also been used to
analyze boundary algebras, Chern–Simons and BF edge modes [3, 4, 27, 28], fractional
and integer quantum Hall hydrodynamics [29, 30], topological waves with odd viscosity
[31], superfluid waves and charge-vortex duality [32, 33].
Applied to the Schrodinger system, the gauge formulation gives a natural place to
put structures that already accompany the wavefunction. Electromagnetic coupling,
spin and the holonomy one-form attached to the phase of the wavefunction [34] all
modify the phase-gradient sector.
In the gauge description these modifications are
implemented uniformly by a BF coupling to an additional one-form, which shifts the
Madelung momentum on the hydrodynamic side. For example, for a two-component
wavefunction this one-form is the Berry connection on the Hopf bundle. In a degenerate
adiabatic sector the Berry connection becomes non-abelian, and the same BF structure
applies after projecting it onto the occupied polarization, which selects the abelian
one-form seen by the hydrodynamic current.
A Chern–Simons term gives the complementary topological deformation. It induces
an effective Hopf functional for the conserved Schrodinger current and places charge-
flux attachment, linking phases, and the anyonic sector directly inside the gauge/fluid
correspondence [35, 36]. On manifolds with boundary, both BF and Chern–Simons
terms also obstruct some gauge transformations from being pure redundancy and turn
part of the gauge data into physical edge degrees of freedom. The corresponding quasi-
local charges give the affine U(1) algebra in the Chern–Simons realization and the mixed
boundary algebra in the BF realization. Modern discussions of symmetry structures
associated with topological sectors are given in [46, 47].
The gauge/fluid viewpoint also opens a natural way to discuss the long-distance
sector of the theory. The memory effects recently identified in shallow water theory [27]
and in acoustic sound [50] show that hydrodynamic systems can have an infrared struc-
ture normally associated with gauge theories and asymptotic symmetries. A related
nonlinear acoustic memory effect was also studied in fluid systems and Bose–Einstein

condensates in [51]. Through the gauge/fluid correspondence developed here, the same
question can be asked directly for the Schrodinger system. For the linear Schrodinger
equation, however, the soft regime is too dispersive to support an ordinary acoustic
memory effect, since linearization around a homogeneous background gives ω ∼k2
rather than a sound mode. Once a local nonlinear interaction is added, the Bogoliubov
spectrum contains a genuine acoustic branch. In that regime the (3 + 1)-dimensional
two-form gauge description becomes the natural language for the infrared theory. The
memory effect can then be understood as a late-time phase shift of the wavefunction,
or equivalently as a displacement memory for probes coupled to the acoustic mode. We
show that the dual two-form description completes this picture by relating the same
observable to a large gauge transformation, giving the nonlinear Schrodinger system
an infrared triangle of memory, asymptotic symmetry, and soft behavior. The relation
between memory effects, asymptotic symmetries, and soft theorems is the standard
infrared triangle of gauge theory and gravity [52–60]. Its gravitational roots go back
to the displacement memory effect and BMS symmetry [61–63]. Here we explicitly
demonstrate that the nonlinear Schrodinger system provides another realization of the
infrared triangle outside its original high-energy and gravitational setting.
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we derive the local correspondence
between the Schrodinger equation, quantum hydrodynamics, and gauge theory, and
separate this local equivalence from the global phase data carried by the wavefunction.
In Section 3 we study topological deformations, including BF couplings, Chern–Simons
terms, Hopf functionals, Berry and spin extensions, and intrinsic holonomy. In Section 4
we place the theory on manifolds with boundary and derive the corresponding edge
charges and boundary algebras. In Section 5 we turn to the nonlinear infrared regime
and show how sound, memory, and asymptotic symmetry are organized by the dual
two-form description. Technical details are collected in Appendices A–C.

Topological terms
In fluid/gauge duality, topological terms such as BF and Chern-Simons couplings play
a central role in organizing the vortex and current sectors of the theory [1–4]. The
same perspective becomes available once the Schrodinger system is expressed in gauge-
theoretic variables. Since the gauge fields already solve the continuity equation identi-
cally, adding a BF coupling to an additional one-form shifts the Madelung momentum
and thereby modifies the vorticity sector of the quantum fluid. This observation unifies
a broad class of deformations. Whether the additional one-form is external, dynamical,
composite, or intrinsic to the phase bundle, the underlying mechanism remains the
same. In this section, we explore this structure.

6
Conclusion
In this paper, we reformulated the nonrelativistic Schrodinger equation as a gauge
theory by rewriting its conserved probability-current sector in terms of gauge fields.
In the (2 + 1)-dimensional theory this gives a one-form gauge description, while in the
(3+1)-dimensional theory it gives a two-form gauge description. The correspondence is
exact locally and reproduces the Schrodinger equation through its hydrodynamic form.
The only extra input is global: the phase winding around zeros of the wavefunction
must be quantized in order to reconstruct a single-valued wavefunction.
We then showed that several structures of the Schrodinger theory have a common
gauge-theoretic origin. On the gauge side, BF couplings to additional one-forms re-
produce electromagnetic coupling, Berry connections, spinor dynamics, projected non-
abelian adiabatic connections, and intrinsic holonomy. In the (2 + 1)-dimensional the-
ory, Chern–Simons terms generate Hopf functionals for the Schrodinger current and
naturally incorporate charge-flux attachment, linking phases, and anyonic sectors.
We also studied the theory in the presence of boundaries. The topological terms
make part of the gauge data physical at the boundary, producing edge degrees of
freedom. In the Chern–Simons realization, the corresponding boundary charges obey
an affine U(1) algebra. In the BF realization, one obtains the mixed surface algebra
familiar from BF theory with boundary. These algebras are fixed by the topological
sector, while the non-topological bulk terms can affect the actual boundary dynamics.
Finally, we analyzed the infrared regime of the nonlinear theory.
The linear
Schrodinger equation is too dispersive to support an ordinary acoustic memory effect,
but a local nonlinear interaction produces a Bogoliubov sound branch. In that regime,
the dual two-form formulation relates the late-time phase shift of the wavefunction,
displacement memory of probes, and large gauge transformations of the dual field.
A natural next step is to study the concrete boundary dynamics associated with
the Chern–Simons and BF edge sectors, beyond the charge algebra alone. It would also
be interesting to extend the construction to many-particle Schrodinger systems and to
open or dissipative systems, where the conserved-current structure may be modified.
Another direction is the memory analysis of spinful Schrodinger systems, in particular
the Pauli equation, where Berry and spin degrees of freedom are already part of the
hydrodynamic/gauge description.
