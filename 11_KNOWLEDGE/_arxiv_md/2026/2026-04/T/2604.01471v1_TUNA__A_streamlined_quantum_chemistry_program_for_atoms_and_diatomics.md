TUNA: A streamlined quantum chemistry
program for atoms and diatomics
Harry Brough∗
Department of Chemistry, The University of Manchester, Manchester, M1 7DN, United Kingdom
∗harry.brough@manchester.ac.uk
Abstract
We present TUNA, an open-source quantum chemistry program speciﬁcally designed for atoms
and diatomic molecules. Within this narrow molecular domain, a broad and consistent set of
electronic structure methods and calculation types is available. Energies, optimisations, vi-
brational frequencies, response properties, coordinate scans and ab initio molecular dynamics
trajectories can be accessed through an intuitive command-line interface.
A single principle underlies TUNA: once a method can be used to evaluate the energy, all
properties follow from numerical diﬀerentiation. This makes the program both a transpar-
ent teaching platform and a compact environment for benchmarking methods on diatomics
— among the most simple yet instructive systems in quantum chemistry. Reference imple-
mentations including density functional theory, many-body perturbation theory and coupled
cluster theory, supported by detailed theoretical documentation, make TUNA an accessible
foundation for developing improved methods and algorithms in electronic structure.

Introduction
Quantum chemistry programs are typically designed for broad applicability. Such breadth is
valuable, but also brings complexity: inputs become longer, workﬂows are method-dependent,
and not all properties are available at all levels of theory. TUNA takes the opposite approach.
It is designed speciﬁcally for atoms and diatomics, with a compact command-line syntax of
TUNA [Calculation] : [Atom A] [Atom B] [Distance] : [Method] [Basis] :
plus optional keywords, for the calculation types listed in Table 1. For example a restricted
Hartree–Fock optimisation of the hydrogen molecule in the 6-31G basis set is simply
TUNA OPT : H H 1.0 : HF 6-31G :
This uniformity makes comparison between electronic structure methods straightforward and
ensures TUNA is easily accessible to non-expert users.
Diatomic molecules are among the most instructive systems in quantum chemistry. They
are simple enough to analyse along a single bond coordinate, yet rich enough to expose the
full range of challenges in electronic structure theory: bond breaking, static correlation, spin
symmetry breaking, spectroscopy, and excited state behaviour. They therefore serve equally
well as teaching systems and research benchmarks.
By the symmetry of diatomics, many observables reduce to one or two independent compo-
nents, which makes numerical diﬀerentiation practical as a general strategy. TUNA exploits
this symmetry throughout the program, handling response properties via optimised ﬁnite
diﬀerence schemes and computing anharmonic vibrational frequencies directly on the one-
dimensional potential energy surface.

The TUNA software is well suited to teaching and exploratory work because it exposes mod-

A user can begin with
a restricted Hartree–Fock calculation on the hydrogen molecule, examine orbital energies,
charges, and bond orders, then move directly to unrestricted references, perturbation theory,
coupled cluster, and density functional methods. Because the systems are small, changes in
method can be interpreted directly and without ambiguity. Intermediate data structures and
algorithmic pathways are fully accessible, and a built-in interface to Matplotlib [1] supports
plotting of molecular orbitals, electron densities, and potential energy curves through key-
words such as PLOTHOMO, PLOTLUMO, and DENSPLOT, as shown in Figure 1. The TUNA manual
accompanies the program with a fully referenced theoretical background to each method, as
well as dozens of “simple input line” examples which can be copied and pasted into a terminal.
For researchers, TUNA is a practical development platform.
Its narrow molecular scope
means new methods can be implemented, tested, and debugged quickly, with results that
are directly interpretable. The program is eﬃcient enough for meaningful benchmarking on
diatomics, and its thoroughly documented Python codebase makes modiﬁcations straight-
forward.
TUNA therefore addresses three audiences at once: students learning electronic
structure theory, researchers benchmarking methods on small molecules, and developers pro-
totyping new algorithms.

A common framework for response properties
The symmetry of diatomics makes numerical diﬀerentiation practical as a general strategy
rather than a last resort. Within TUNA, structural, vibrational, and ﬁeld response calcula-
tions are handled within this same framework. Optimised structures and harmonic frequencies
are obtained from numerical ﬁrst and second derivatives respectively, while ﬁnite ﬁeld methods
provide access to dipole moments, polarisabilities, and hyperpolarisabilities.

[4], well within “chemical accuracy” of 1 kcal mol−1. This quantitative level of accuracy is
obtained through a single concise command.

Calculation types
Beyond single point energies, TUNA can calculate optimised geometries, vibrational frequen-
cies and ab initio molecular dynamics trajectories with the velocity Verlet algorithm. There
are also built-in calculation types to determine bond dissociation energies, ionisation poten-

and multiple potential energy surfaces can be overlaid on the same plot with the ADDPLOT key-
word, an example shown in Figure 2.

Electronic structure methods and basis sets
While narrow in molecular scope, TUNA has a diversity of basis sets and electronic struc-
ture methods, the vast majority of which are available for spin-restricted and unrestricted
references. For several methods, TUNA is one of few, if any, open source implementations.
Methods in TUNA include self-consistent Hartree theory and Hartree–Fock theory, and many-
body perturbation theory methods including MP2, MP3, and MP4. Spin-component-scaled
(SCS) versions, domain-based local pair natural orbital (DLPNO) MP2 and density matrix
Laplace MP2 are also available [6–10]. Self-consistent density functional approximations are
implemented from all rungs of the “Jacob’s ladder” of exchange–correlation functionals (see
Table 2) [11], including hybrid functionals like PBE0 [12] and B3LYP [13], which incorporate a
proportion of Fock exchange, and double-hybrid functionals like B2PLYP [14] and DSD-BLYP
[15] which also include a proportion of second-order perturbation theory correlation.
Sophisticated correlated wavefunction methods based on coupled cluster (CC) theory are
also available, including CCD, CCSD, CCSD(T), CCSDT, CCSDT(Q) and CCSDTQ. The
quadratic conﬁguration interaction (QCI) methods QCISD and QCISD(T) are implemented,
as are approximate coupled cluster methods CC2 and CC3 and linearised methods based
on the coupled electron pair approximation, CEPA [16–20]. Excited states and transition
intensities can be calculated with conﬁguration interaction singles (CIS), with an optional
perturbative doubles correction [21].
A wide range of Gaussian basis sets are available. These include minimal basis sets such
as STO-3G, Pople style sets such as 6-31G* [22–24], the Dunning style cc-pVNZ sets up to
triply-augmented sextuple-ζ [25–28], the Ahlrichs’ def2- basis sets [29], Jensen’s pc(seg)-N
sets [30–33] and atomic natural orbital basis sets, ano-pVNZ [34].
Methods can be modiﬁed with simple keywords.
For example, the proportion of “exact”
Hartree–Fock exchange in an exchange–correlation functional is easily changed through the
HFX keyword, the same-spin scaling parameter of SCS-MP2 can be changes with SSS and the
amplitude convergence criteria for coupled cluster calculations is altered with AMPCONV. Any
number of optional keywords can be used together in the TUNA input line, in any order.

Implementation
The philosophy of TUNA mirrors that of Python: both trade raw speed for simplicity and
clarity. The program is implemented in Python 3, making extensive use of NumPy [43] and
SciPy [44] for numerical operations and Matplotlib [1] for integrated plotting.
Vectorised
NumPy tensor contractions — particularly through the einsum function — keep the code
compact and readable while supporting high-order correlated methods. Beyond these mod-
ules, dependencies have been kept to a minimum to make installation as simple as possible.
Diatomic symmetry is exploited throughout to improve eﬃciency, most signiﬁcantly in the
McMurchie–Davidson evaluation of molecular integrals over Gaussian basis functions, where
the parity of a linear molecule aligned on the z-axis yields substantial speedups. Because these
integrals are diﬃcult to vectorise this module is implemented in Cython [45] — a compiled
extension language for Python. Utilisation of symmetry and avoiding writing to disk makes
TUNA competitive in speed with well-established general-purpose quantum chemistry codes.
6
Summary and perspectives
This article has presented TUNA, a user-friendly electronic structure program for atoms and
diatomics combining a simple command-line interface with a diversity of electronic structure
methods.
By narrowing its molecular scope, TUNA ﬁnds a clear niche as a streamlined
laboratory for quantum chemistry on the smallest molecules.
Several developments are planned. Relativistic corrections are a priority for improving ac-
curacy on heavier diatomics.
Magnetic response properties via ﬁnite magnetic ﬁelds and
expanded conﬁguration interaction capabilities, including CASSCF, are under development.
Closer integration of theory and implementation in the documentation is a continuing focus.
The TUNA code and documentation are available at github.com/h-brough/TUNA under the
MIT license and the program is easily installed from PyPI with pip install quantumtuna.
Contributions, feature requests and bug reports through the GitHub issue tracker are welcome.
