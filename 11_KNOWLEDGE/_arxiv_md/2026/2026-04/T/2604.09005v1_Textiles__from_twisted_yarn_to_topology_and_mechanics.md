Abstract
While textiles have existed throughout much of human history as com-
plex mechanical metamaterials, textile science has largely been over-
looked by the physics community until recently.
In this review, we
consider the symmetry, topology, and mechanics of woven and knit-
ted materials, showing that they represent a unique, if under-explored,
regime of condensed matter. We start with the basic construction and
mechanics of spun yarn, reviewing recent developments twisted bundle
structures. We then introduce woven and knitted fabrics as materials
with layer symmetries that can be topologically characterized as knots
and links in the thickened torus. We finally discuss fabric mechanics
and geometry in terms of yarn-level geometry, dissipation mechanisms,
and defect structures.

1. INTRODUCTION
Humans have used textiles in some form for much of our collective history. From spinning
grasses and animal hairs to make stronger threads, yarns, and ropes, to industrial-scale
weaving of bespoke patterns with the Jacquard loom, and finally to the technical textiles
and smart wearables found today, the methods and materials used for the creation of textiles
have undergone a dramatic evolution. Nonetheless, the themes of twisting, knotting, and
entangling as a way of combining fiber-like constituents remains unchanged. While we will
not attempt a historical introduction to textiles, we refer the interested reader to refs. (1–3).
In recent years, physicists have taken an interest in textiles. Textile science and engi-
neering is a mature field that has developed standard measures, metrology methods, and
models that have enabled the industry to grow to the staggering scale it has today. Simi-
larly, fiber artists and the crafter community ensure that traditional methods of weaving,
knitting, crocheting, and other techniques persist, while finding new ways of building com-
plex patterns and mechanical behaviors into fabrics. There are at least two reasons for
physicists to pick up this thread: one, through the exploration of mechanical metamaterials
and inverse design; two, through an interest in mathematical modeling towards developing
structure-function relationships across scales. Indeed, considerable progress has been made
in both of these fields over the past few decades (4–21). In this Review, we will consider
textiles through the lens of condensed matter physics, focusing on topology, symmetry,
and non-linear mechanics. In doing so, we offer an invitation for others to explore this
fascinating topic.
In order for physicists to contribute to the study and tradition of textiles, it is helpful to
learn some of the basic language. Textile terminology has a complex history and even has
differences amongst traditional fiber artists and industrial-scale textile engineers, as well as
across regions, reflecting the global and culturally interwoven nature of textile practices;
a minimal dictionary is provided in the margin. A classical example of this is the use of
“woven” to describe a textile whose structure consists of yarn that crosses the fabric in a
well-prescribed manner based on a specific pattern. By contrast, a “non-woven” can be
defined as any other textile involving some degree of randomness to the microstructure,
including felt—a material made by pressing fibers together and held together by inter-

2. YARN: A BASIC STRUCTURAL ELEMENT
Yarn, threads, and strings all serve to describe the basic structural element of woven and
knitted fabrics; for brevity, we will describe all such filamentous materials used for fabric
creation as “yarn.” In the textile industry, yarn is a context-dependent term, generally re-
ferring to the effectively 1-dimensional material intended for fabric formation, rather than
intermediate assemblies used in stages of yarn processing which have specific terms (22).
Throughout history, yarn has been made from a variety of materials, both natural — sourced
from animals and plants — and synthetic. Traditionally created from fibers consisting of
either animal products (e.g. wool and silk) or plant material (e.g. cotton, hemp, linen), mod-
ern synthetic yarns are formed from fibers formed when a polymer melt (e.g. polyacrylate,
polyester, rayon, nylon) is extruded through a narrow pore. Semi-synthetic “regenerated
fibers” harvest and reconstruct cellulose (e.g. from bamboo) to create rayon and associated
fibers. Finally, there is increasing interest in the design of shape-actuating fabrics whose
yarn is based on shape-changing materials (24) such as shape memory alloys (25, 26), liquid
crystal elastomers (27), as well as yarn constructed from human cells for tissue engineer-
ing (28).
The structure of the yarn itself can be varied and is often dependent on the constituent
material. Since natural fibers tend to be too short (“filament silk” is an exception) for fabric
creation, yarn is traditionally a hierarchical bundle of filaments that are spun together under
tension. In contrast, polymer extrusion can create long filaments that extend the full length
of the yarn. Filament materials, which can range from polymers, including elastomers, to
metallic wires, can be used as the yarn itself (“monofilament”) or can be wrapped into a
bundle (“multifilament”).
Given the variability of yarn material and structure, it is difficult to arrive at a standard
characterization of yarn properties. For instance, the diameters of fluffy, multifilament bun-
dles are not a well-defined quantity, and they are rather characterized by their linear mass
density (see tex, denier in the textile lexicon). As such, beyond simple monofilament, there
is much unknown about the mechanical (i.e. stress-vs-strain) constitutive relations of vari-
ous yarn types. Moreover, contact mechanics, also essential for capturing the structure and
response of textiles, involves the added complexity of friction and contact geometry. While
experimental characterization (e.g. tensile and flexural testing) can provide useful details,
yarn properties are often sensitive to a history of loading and unloading, degradation, and
even humidity in the case of many natural fibers.

2.1. Hierarchical, plied yarn
Yarns and threads made from animal and plant fibers are complex, hierarchical structures.
Staple fibers are short, often only a few centimeters in length (29). Consequently, in order
to create yarn of arbitrary length, these staple fibers are combined using a technique known
as “spinning.” Spinning is an ancient practice known to humans for millennia, developed
separately amongst many cultures (30). While modern industrial methods have automated
spinning, the basic process remains the same: fibers are aligned and formed into a bundle;
a tension is applied across the bundle; the bundle is twisted under tension; more fibers are
added to allow the bundle to grow in length. A drop spindle, such as the one shown in
figure 1a, uses gravity and rotational inertia to apply tension and torque to the fibers. By
twisting under tension, the fibers form frictional contacts, allowing the bundle to maintain
its form, resulting in a single “ply” of yarn (known as singles yarn) as seen in figure 1b.
While friction maintains the cohesion of a ply, there is nonetheless residual stress, in the
form of a torque, arising from the imposed helical shape of the fibers; the spinning direction
chooses the handedness of the helix—designated “S” or “Z,” as shown in figure 1c—and
thus the sign of the torque. Such “single-ply” yarns are susceptible to relaxing residual
torque through a mechanical instability that converts twist—related to the pitch of the
helical structure of the bundle—to writhe, a description of the three-dimensional coiling of
the bundle. This helical instability, also known as the “plectoneme” instability, in reference
to a similar phenomenon in DNA supercoiling, or the “phone cord” instability (31, 32),
can distort the appearance of the resulting textile. In knitted fabrics, this breaks mirror
symmetries in the stitch pattern, resulting in a phenomenon known as “spirality,” shearing
the relaxed shape of the fabric (33). In woven fabrics, this effect results in curling of opposite
corners of the fabric out of the plane, where one corner curls up and the other corner curls
down (34). In order to decrease the propensity for yarn to undergo this instability, as well as
to add strength to the yarn, multiple plies are counter-twisted together to create a “multi-
ply” or “balanced” yarn, as in figure 1d. Similarly, if multiple monofilaments are twisted
together, the resulting multifilament bundles can be balanced through the incorporation of
counter-twisting multiple bundles together.

2.3. Fiber packing
As yarn is spun under tension, it is reasonable to model the undeformed state of spun yarn
as a straight line with zero curvature (κ = 0) and a constant twist of the material frame
(Ω̸= 0). While under tension, these filaments will be subject to an aligning force that
tends to minimize their distance from the yarn’s central axis; due to hard-core interactions
between the filaments, they cannot all pack along the central axis. Consequently, we might
imagine the tension as applying an effective pressure or confining force (40) on the filaments
in the yarn’s cross-section. It is therefore reasonable to expect that the filaments will pack
in a way that minimizes the area fraction of voids within the yarn’s cross-section, resulting
in the idealized model of hexagonal packing, as shown in figure 1f .
The hexagonal packing model is largely contingent on the assumption that cutting yarn
perpendicular to its local tangent vector gives a 2D lattice of circular disks. However, due
to the twist of the individual filaments about the central axis of the yarn, filaments in this
cross-section of the yarn bundle generally have individual cross-sections that are distorted.
This distortion increases as the twist Ωof the bundle is increased and also grows with
the radial coordinate of the filament from the center of the bundle, and can even result
in concave and self-wrapping shapes (41).
Since the distance between two filaments is
determined by a straight line that must be orthogonal to the local tangent vectors of both
filaments, this line cannot lie in the perpendicular cross-section of the twisted yarn, unless
the yarn is not twisted (Ω= 0) or both of the filaments simultaneously occupy the center
of the yarn’s core. Therefore, the line of closest distance, when projected onto the yarn’s
perpendicular cross-section, has a reduced length that depends on the radial coordinates
of the two filaments in question. This projection of inter-filament lengths gives rise to a
radially-dependent metric tensor within the cross-sectional slice of the yarn, rationalizing
the distortions of the fiber cross-sections. In fact, it can be shown (42) that the induced
metric is consistent with a space of positive Gaussian curvature KG that scales as KG ∼Ω2.
This same Gaussian curvature frustrates hexagonal packing in two dimensions, resulting in

2.4. Contact mechanics
The role of contacts is twofold: imposing a geometry and dissipating energy. Two contacting
yarns locally impose forces and moments on each other. Within the slender elastic curve
model, contacts can be interpreted as external local or distributed forces and moments.
The curve will then adapt its shape to minimize its elastic energy under these external
constraints.
The geometry of yarns in a textile is then principally determined by the
arrangement of contacts imposed upon crafting.
At a contact, normal forces build up as yarns cannot pass through each other, but the
existence of tangential forces gives away the presence of frictional interactions. Friction
dissipates energy when the contact is sliding, but can also statically trap tension within the
yarn. For yarn mechanics, it is important to distinguish two types of frictional interactions:
viscous friction and solid friction (50). Viscous friction is typical of lubricated surfaces and
is proportional to the relative speed between the contacting strands. It then vanishes for a
structure completely at rest. In contrast, dry friction holds tangential force until a certain
threshold is reached and the contact starts sliding. Within the simplest Amontons-Coulomb
formulation of dry friction, the threshold tangential force is proportional to the normal force
and, once sliding, is independent of the sliding speed. Which interactions are predominant
may depend on the time scale, the type of yarn, or external conditions. Signatures of solid
friction are found, for instance, in the hysteretic response upon cycling quasi-static tensile
tests (51), stick-slip instabilities emerging from sliding contacts (52), or the existence of
multiple rest states (53).
In contrast, viscous features are evidenced by slow creep-like
responses, for instance, in the geometrical relaxation of the fabrics after fabrication (54),
or the stress relaxation of stretched fabrics (55).
In the case where the yarn is not made from one hard monofilament, the cross-section of
the yarns will undergo significant deformation in the contact zones (56, 57). Normal stresses
in the contact zones will generally cause yarn to adopt an elliptical, or even “stadium-
shaped” cross-section with flattened edges, as shown in figure 1f . These cross-section de-

3. TOPOLOGY AND SYMMETRY OF FABRICS
Now that we have our yarns, the fabrication of a fabric consists of entangling these yarns
following a repeated and specific pattern. The entanglement process gives the fabric dis-
tinctive topological and symmetrical properties. While a large variety of fabrics have been
used throughout history, woven and knitted fabrics are primarily two-periodic structures.
This makes them analogous to two-dimensional crystals, which allows us to readily adopt
the language and concepts of solid-state physics and crystallography. As is traditional in
condensed matter physics, we will focus on bulk descriptions of the material, leaving the
interesting complexities of boundaries and interfaces for description elsewhere. Therefore,
we will skip discussions of selvedge in wovens and hemming in knits, as well as the different
structures resulting from the various cast-on and bind-off techniques. Instead, by focus-
ing on the doubly-periodic bulk, we can simplify the structure of fabrics by focusing on a
translational unit cell.
The unit cell representation also facilitates topological characterization of these fabrics
by removing the infinite set of repeated yarn crossings that result from translational sym-
metry. If F is the set of yarn paths embedded in Euclidean space, then the primitive unit
cell1 ˜F contains the minimal segments of yarn necessary to re-build the fabric through the
repeated action of the group of translations in 2D, Gtr, such that F = Gtr ◦˜F. In other
words, ˜F is the quotient space (or orbit space) F/Gtr. If yarn paths existed purely in two
dimensions then this quotient space would be isomorphic to the two-torus T2 = S1 × S1.
However, the yarn paths and the fabric are embedded in three dimensions, where only two
of the dimensions are identified by translational symmetries; the correct quotient space is
therefore ˜F ≃T2 × I, where I is an interval. Consequently, the open curves that represent
yarn in an infinite fabric become closed within the quotient space—copies of S1. That is, the
woven and knitted structures are equivalent to knots and links on the thickened two-torus,
T2 × I. While distinct from the usual study of knots and links, topological invariants have
been found for both woven and knitted fabrics (17, 18).
Two-dimensional tilings of the plane can be completely classified using only 17 symme-
try groups, namely the wallpaper groups (61–63). The wallpaper groups are essential for
understanding the structure and response of 2D crystals (64) and, as their name implies, be
found in works of art, as exemplified by the tilings of M.C. Escher (65). However, fabrics
can have symmetries through their thickness, requiring description using sub-periodic sym-
metry groups, where the number of directions with translational periodicity is less than the
embedding dimension. Of these, fabrics are described by the 80 layer groups (2D periodicity
in 3D) (11, 66).

3.1. Wovens
Most woven fabrics are biaxial, with yarn running in perpendicular directions known as warp
and weft. When weaving on a loom, many parallel “warp” yarns are typically oriented
to point towards the weaver who uses a shuttle to run a working “weft” yarn back and
forth across the warp yarns. In modern industrial looms, instead of shuttles, many other
weft insertion processes are now used, including rapier, air-jet, and water-jet processes for
production efficiency, although the process remains largely the same. In these looms the
warp yarns are lifted up or lowered in a patterned sequence through the use of heddles,
threads or cords containing eyes through which the warp yarns are threaded, forming an
opening called the “shed” in which the weft is inserted, resulting in an arrangement of over
and under-crossings that can vary from row to row. The Jacquard loom, due to its ability
to re-configure the heddle lifting sequence using punch cards, is often considered the first
computer (1).
We will refer to an “over-crossing” (resp., “under-crossing”) as a region of the fabric
where the weft yarn passes over a warp yarn. As shown in figure 2a, the two states of
each crossing make for an easy analogy with a Z2 spin lattice, where the over and under-
crossings are, respectively, mapped to up and down spin states. In the case of the (3,1)-twill
pattern shown, there is often a net non-zero spin to the lattice. A (p, q)-twill is defined as a
pattern having p over-crossings, followed by q under-crossings with subsequent rows shifting
the pattern by one crossing (11). Therefore, the net spin of a (p, q)-twill is determined by
the ratio of the difference in up and down-crossings p −q to the total number of crossings
p + q.
By contrast to the (3, 1)-twill, the plain weave shown in figure 2b consists of
an equal arrangement of over and under-crossings and may be considered a (1, 1)-twill;
this structure has a distinct antiferromagnetic arrangement of crossings. Note that of the
patterns shown in figure 2b-e, only the plain weave and its generalizations in figure 2f
have mirror symmetries. Due to the apparent grid-like nature of the biaxial weaves, we
might consider the over/under-crossing arrangement as living on a square grid, such as that

Knitting is fundamentally different from weaving, both on a structural level and in its
creation: knitting machines use hooks and hand-knitters just require a pair of knitting
needles to create fabric. While the unit cells of weaves are crossing strands, the unit cells of
knitted fabrics are linked loops. Here and throughout this review, we focus on weft knitting.

resulting in a purl stitch. The underlying structure of these stitches is identical, depending
only on the orientation of the fabric; a knit or K-stitch may be reflected or transformed

they also have remarkably distinct mechanical properties (59, 71).

Each of the course-wise connecting arcs bridges a mirror plane, represented by a red line.

are consistent with the symmetry group pma2, which is also one of the 17 wallpaper groups.

pbma, which is not one of the wallpaper groups (66, 72).

direction. Moreover, there are in-plane two-fold axes that serve as mechanisms to convert

4. FABRIC MECHANICS AND APPLICATIONS
Although yarns and fabrics have been used for millennia for their robust mechanical prop-
erties, quantitative descriptions of their mechanics still face enormous difficulties: the emer-
gent behavior of fabrics is generally nonlinear and anisotropic, with effective bulk properties
that depend on geometric and topological features appearing at the same length scale as
the fabric thickness. The manufacturing process of ordered textiles fixes their topology,
creating yarn crossing constraints that are not localized in the yarn path; this contributes
greatly to computational cost in yarn-level descriptions (74). These topological constraints
are difficult to elide because they determine the structural integrity of the fabric; in knits
in particular, force is largely transmitted through yarn contact points between locally non-
contiguous segments of yarn. Additionally, the length scale of individual stitches or weaving
motifs is typically comparable to the length scale of the yarn thickness.
Nevertheless, much work over the past century has empirically characterized fabric me-
chanics across various industrial manufacturing methodologies and yarn materials. Theoret-
ical work in this area typically falls into complementary categories: finite-element volumetric
yarn modeling, and approximate physical simulation including both approximate yarn-level
computations of textile mechanics (58, 75–80) as well as fabric-level approximations that
treat swatches of textiles as discretized two-dimensional surfaces modeled as thin elastic
shells (81–85). With substantial increases in computational power, recent work has also fo-
cused on database-driven approaches, where different physical models or textures are used
in different regimes of deformation, and hybridized computations of yarn- and fabric-level
models (86–89).

Triaxial textile weaves have a long history as a traditional crafting method, notably
in basket weaving. Adding a series of yarns in a third direction considerably stiffens the
structure, as the shear mode now also involves yarn stretching. Triaxial weaves are usually
crafted from ribbons instead of threads with circular cross-sections to allow the structure
to help maintain a high cover factor with minimal out-of-plane bending of the yarns at the
crossings. Ribbons can only bend out of their plane, imposing strict in-plane geometrical
constraints: ribbons must follow the geodesics of the surfaces they embed. In contrast with
biaxial structures, the pattern of triaxial weaves directly dictates their 3D shape. Weav-
ing the ribbons into hexagonal patterns generates flat surfaces, but introducing topological
defects like pentagons or heptagons leads to dome- and saddle-shaped surfaces, respec-
tively (105). The topology of the triaxial weaves and the in-plane curvature of the ribbons
impose the apparent Gaussian curvature of the weaves (106), which in turn dramatically
affects the mechanical response of the woven surface (107).
4.2. Knits
Unlike woven fabrics, knits tend to have soft in-plane and out-of-plane mechanical responses
due to highly curved yarn paths within knitted stitches, which allow them to significantly
deform without substantial extension of the yarn along its length. Large yarn curvatures
(typically at a similar length scale to their thickness) create geometric nonlinearities in
their mechanics (32), and knits display an abundance of yarn contacts at nontrivial angles,
requiring friction forces to be modeled for quantitative accuracy even at low tensions (108).
In addition to generalized continuum approaches also used for woven fabrics (109), sim-
ulation methods for knits include multiscale constitutive modeling relating the complex
three-dimensional structure of knitted loops with fabric-scale deformations (84, 85, 110–
112) as well as yarn-level simulations (58, 59, 71, 77, 113). Despite the complexity involved
in computational models, there is a long history of using geometric models to establish basic
results governing stress-strain relationships and fabric stiffness (59, 114–121).

granular mechanics. An additional stiff regime can be observed at low stresses when the
knitted fabric is knit in a tight or “jammed” configuration. This jammed regime is domi-

4.2.2. Hysteresis and memory of mechanical response. The tensile mechanical response
of knitted fabric shows significant hysteresis, highlighting the dissipative nature of the
deformation process. Attempts to model and predict this hysteretic behavior invoked either
viscoelasticity (129) or dry friction (51). Recent work, upon observing the independence of
this effect on applied strain rate, hypothesized that this effect comes mostly from plastic
deformation with a small viscoelastic effect (130).
The latter study (130) also observed and modeled return point memory in knitted
fabrics. An analogous memory effect was first observed in ferromagnets (131). In this type
of memory, the fabric “remembers” the load at the maximum strain reached during the
tensile test (the return point). The fabric “forgets” the return point when that maximum
strain is exceeded and instead remembers the new return point. This effect was observed
to be independent of applied strain rates.
Another expression of the memory effect is the multiple aspect ratios that knits can
adopt at rest. The multiplicity of rest shapes poses important challenges for industry in
predicting the surface of fabric from a given length of yarn, fabrication parameters, and post-
processing (54). One source of this metastability is friction at the contacts, which traps
internal stresses and prevents the fabric from relaxing to its minimum energy state (53).
Depending on the deformation history, the configuration can get stuck due to friction at
various aspect ratios, thereby encoding memory in the knit’s rest shape.
4.2.3. Anisotropic fracture in knits. The local structure of yarn paths within a knit fabric
also leads to highly anisotropic and heterogeneous patterns under mechanical failure. When
a yarn tears locally in a knit fabric, biaxial tension on the fabric may propagate runs or
ladders from the initial breakage site, creating defects in the loop structure. Under biaxial
tension, yarn breakage allows for two primary modes of defect propagation: (1) reptation
of the newly created free ends, reducing the total number of loops in a row and thereby
releasing tension in the y direction; and (2) loops that are no longer topologically con-
strained by a vertically neighboring loop may pull taut horizontally, releasing tension in the
x direction. Due to the relative amount of friction required to be overcome in each mode
of propagation, loop defects typically travel much farther in the wale (y) direction than the
course (x) direction, leaving long ladder-like scars in the fabric. The topology of the knit
pattern restricts how a fracture propagates. The initial damage, typically a yarn breaking
missed loop, modifies the topology of the knit, creating an effective topological defect. Yet,
the subsequent damage propagation conserves the topology and is then limited to Reide-
meister moves of the yarn (132). Recent work has begun to quantify fracture propagation in

4.2.5. Patterned geometry and mechanics. Beyond the local structure of jersey stitches, the
larger-scale patterning of knit and purl stitches can have profound effects on the geometry
and mechanics of a weft knitted fabric. Bulk curvatures generated at the fabric scale due to
patterns of knit and purl stitches enable extremely soft deformation modes that can be tuned
for their tensile stiffness in both x and y directions. Like rib and garter patterns, which
soften a fabric in the x and y directions respectively, other periodic motifs can significantly
change the fabric’s mechanical response, including imparting auxetic responses (140) or
multistability (141).
The intentional introduction of defects into the knit lattice by locally adding or removing
stitches within the grid can allow for three-dimensional shape programming when applied
at sufficient magnitude, with the formation of both positive and negative curvature. (142–
144). There are several knitting techniques that create these defects that do not require
cutting the yarn. These include: decreases wherein a stitch is removed from a course and
creates an edge dislocation (figure 5c) that can result in symmetric or asymmetric stitch
tilt (figure 5d); increases where an additional stitch is introduced at a course (figure 5e);
partial or short-row knitting where a subsection of a course is knitted and effectively locally
adds additional walewise unit cells into the lattice (figure 5g-h). These defects and others
are used to alter the curvature of the knitted fabric, and is one way in which socks are
made to conform to the shape of our feet. An extreme application of this is the algorithmic
creation of 3D knitting patterns, such as the Stanford bunny shown in (figure 5i) (143).
The use of patterns and defects to target 3D shapes and mechanics, e.g. for the design of
bespoke garments, remains an open “grand challenge” in the (inverse) design of fabrics.
5. SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK
In this review, we have examined woven and knitted fabrics through a lens that is shaped
by current interests in the geometry and topology of condensed matter as well as soft me-
chanical metamaterials. Similar to the realm of polymer physics, the basic building block
of a textile is not a roughly convex particle or atom, but a staple fiber or filament. Mi-
crostructure is not directly determined by covalent or metallic interactions, but by twisted
bundles, friction, links, and knots. Continuing an ascent towards the macroscale, we de-
scribed efforts to rationalize how the mechanics of yarn crossings and larger-scale patterns
determines the collective mechanics and shape of the fabric.
Despite the long history of textiles, there are plenty of open questions that are amenable
to the tools and concepts of condensed matter. These questions lie at all length scales of
these materials, from changes in fiber packing due to clasps between different yarn segments,
to a complete characterization of defects in knitted fabrics. There is also an opportunity
to push into new frontiers of textiles by attempting to generalize the notion of a knitted
stitch (145), revisiting the design of chainmail (146, 147), or creating higher-dimensional
generalizations of traditionally 2D textiles (148, 149). Finally, it is worth considering the
grand questions of how to design bespoke garments and materials at the level of individual
stitches and how to use these principles to design linked materials from the architectural
