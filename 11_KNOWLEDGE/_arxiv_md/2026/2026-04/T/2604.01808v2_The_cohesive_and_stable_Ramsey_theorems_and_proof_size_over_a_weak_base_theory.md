Introduction
The logical strength of combinatorial theorems formalized in second-order arith-
metic is a topic of considerable interest in reverse mathematics, and in the
foundations of mathematics at large.
A particularly interesting set of prob-
lems concerns the first-order, or more precisely Π1
1, consequences of Ramsey’s
theorem for pairs and two colours, RT2
2, and of its natural weakenings and vari-
ants. (See [4, 21, 18, 19] for some research milestones in this area from the last
decade.)
Starting with [26], the strength of combinatorial statements has also been
studied over the weak axiom system RCA∗
0, which differs from the usual base
theory for reverse mathematics, RCA0, by disallowing uses of mathematical
induction for non-computable (more precisely, Σ0
1) properties.
The study of
RCA∗
0 has led to new insights into reverse mathematics in the traditional setting
of RCA0 – see [2, 10]. However, the strength of RT2
2 and its cousins over RCA∗
0
is also a natural and intriguing topic in its own right.
It was shown in [9] that even though typical weakenings of RT2
2 differ in
terms of their first-order consequences over RCA∗

The proofs of both the speedup and the non-speedup results mentioned above

analogue. For example, Ramsey numbers for arbitrary 2-colourings of pairs are
roughly exponential, and that is why RT2

are only polynomial, and because of this CAC does not prove superpolynomial
closure of I0

A well-known pair of Ramsey-theoretic statements that has so far resisted
attempts at such classification consists of cohesive Ramsey’s theorem for pairs,
CRT2

the existence of a homogeneous set for any colouring of pairs from N that is
stable, meaning that for any given element, all but finitely many pairs involving
that element have the same colour; CRT2

any colouring of pairs from N, there is an infinite set on which the colouring
is stable. Intuitively, the reason why CRT2

of a stable colouring should be. (On the syntactic level, this is reflected in the
greater first-order quantifier complexity of CRT2

other variants of RT2

When considering questions related to proof size, formally speaking we
should fix a specific proof system, but the exact choice is immaterial as there are
polynomial-time translations between typical Hilbert-style, natural deduction,
and sequent calculus systems with cut [6]. For definiteness, we assume that our
proof system is like the one in [7, Section 2.4] except that propositional tautolo-
gies are derived from a finite number of axiom schemes rather than assumed as
axioms. As in the system from [7], our official set of logical symbols consists
only of of ¬, →, and ∀, with the others treated as abbreviations.
A theory S polynomially simulates a theory T with respect to sentences
from the class Γ if there is a polynomial-time procedure that takes a T-proof of
a sentence γ ∈Γ and outputs an S-proof of γ. In contrast, T has non-elementary
speedup over S with respect to Γ if there is no elementary recursive function f
such that if π is a T-proof of a sentence γ ∈Γ, then there exists an S-proof of
γ of size at most f(|π|).

Forcing interpretations.
In general, the idea of a forcing interpretation of
a theory T in a theory S is that one defines in S a notion of ‘ψ is forced’ for
LT -formulas ψ, in such a way that S can prove that each axiom of T is forced.
Then a T-proof of a sentence θ can be transformed into an S-proof of ‘θ is
forced’. If, in addition, θ is actually in LS ∩LT and we can construct an S-
proof of (‘θ is forced’ →θ), then we obtain an S-proof of θ by modus ponens.
Assuming that proofs verifying various properties of the forcing interpretation
can be constructed in polynomial time, the transformation of the T-proof of θ
into the S-proof is polytime as well.
In the form proposed in [15, Section 1] and then used in [17], to provide a
forcing interpretation τ of T in S one has to define in S the following: a set
Condτ of forcing conditions, a relation Pτ on the conditions, a set Nameτ of
names (intended to name elements of the generic LT -structure being described
by the forcing), and the forcing relations p ⊩τ v↓and p ⊩τ α(v), where p is a
