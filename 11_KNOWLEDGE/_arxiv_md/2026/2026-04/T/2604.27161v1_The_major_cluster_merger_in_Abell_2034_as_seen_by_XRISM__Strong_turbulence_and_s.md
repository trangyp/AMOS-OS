Abstract
XRISM observations to date have shown that gas kinetic pressures in the intracluster medium (ICM) tend towards
the low end of predictions from cosmological simulations. Here, we present a XRISM observation of the merging
cluster Abell 2034, which exhibits the broadest emission lines yet observed in a galaxy cluster. We measure a velocity
dispersion of ∼470 km s−1, corresponding to a kinetic pressure fraction of ∼15%. This places A2034 at or above
the high end of the theoretical predictions for similar-mass clusters. This large velocity dispersion may reflect Mach
∼0.5 turbulence in the ICM and/or result from a core disruption driven by the ongoing head-on merger. We also
detect a ∼380 km/s gas bulk velocity gradient along the merger axis with an opposite sign to the galaxy velocity
gradient, indicating a decoupling of the cluster galaxies (and dark matter) from the ICM. Finally, we report tentative
evidence of several spectral anomalies, including a suppressed Fe Heα-z line, an enhanced Fe Lyα-2 line, and a potential
absorption feature at ∼8.7 keV. The first two features may be explained by the combination of a multi-phase ICM
and a non-equilibrium ionization state in the wake of a merger shock. Deeper XRISM observations of this cluster are
required to confirm these features. This work highlights the importance of kinematic measurements across a large
sample of merging clusters as well as the need for deep XRISM observations to unveil more exotic physics in the ICM.

1. INTRODUCTION
XRISM has now provided direct measurements of the kinematic properties of the intracluster medium (ICM) in
approximately a dozen galaxy clusters, including several merging clusters. XRISM measured the velocity structure
function in the Coma cluster (XRISM Collaboration 2025a; Eckert et al. 2025; Vazza and Brunetti 2026), constrained
the merger geometry in several clusters (Heinrich et al. 2025; Omiya et al. 2026a,b; Zhang et al. 2026a; Gatuzz et al.
2026), and detected gas-sloshing-driven gas motions in several clusters (e.g., XRISM Collaboration 2025b,c, 2026;
Zhang et al. 2026b; Bellomi et al. 2025). One common feature of all of these merging clusters is relatively narrow
emission lines, indicating kinetic pressure fractions of ∼10% in A3667 and ≲5% in all other clusters in this sample
(including A2029, where the kinetic pressure fraction is ≲1%).
A recent comparison between the observed kinematics and cosmological simulations indicated that, on average, the
measured kinetic pressure fraction lies below or at the lower end of the predicted distribution (XRISM Collaboration
2025d). This trend is observed both inside cool-cores—where gas motions are affected by active galactic nucleus (AGN)
feedback—and in regions of clusters where mergers dominate. In the former, the discrepancy likely arises from the
complexity of modeling feedback physics in simulations. The cause of the discrepancy in the latter regions remains
unclear. It is plausible that the current sample of XRISM-observed clusters does not statistically represent the overall
population of merging clusters in the Universe. Expanding this sample is therefore essential to understand the cause
of this discrepancy.
In this paper we present observations of another merging cluster, Abell 2034. This cluster is of a similar mass to
Coma, A1914, and A2319 (M200 ≈1015M⊙, Owers et al. 2013) yet exhibits broader emission lines that indicate a higher
velocity dispersion and kinetic pressure fraction. A2034 is undergoing a plane-of-sky, head-on, major merger (mass
ratio ∼2.4, Monteiro-Oliveira et al. 2018; Moura et al. 2021; Finner et al. 2025) between the two main subclusters
A2034S and A2034N. This is a similar merger configuration to the famous Bullet cluster (Markevitch et al. 2002);
however, A2034 is distinct in that it lacks the eponymous “bullet” (a cool-core remnant from the infalling cluster).
This suggests that the core of the infalling subcluster (A2034N) has been disrupted either by a previous merger or the
ongoing one. A surface brightness discontinuity is observed in the X-ray north of the cluster center. It was originally

2. METHODS
XRISM observed A2034 (J2000: RA=227.5489◦, DEC=33.5105◦) for a total of ∼127 ks in June of 2025 (ObsID
201092010). The observation was reprocessed using heasoft version 6.36, applying the most up-to-date calibration
(Resolve CalDB 20250915). The X-ray event file was filtered to include only high-resolution primary events, which
have a spectral resolution of ∼4.5 eV (see the XRISM Quick Start Guide1 and XRISM Collaboration 2024). We also
removed pixel 27 (which has been shown to have unstable gain, XRISM Collaboration 2024) and pixel 7 (which was
experiencing excess broadband noise during this observation2) from the event file.
We extracted spectra from the full Resolve array and from the northern/southern halves of the array. Large size
response matrix files (RMFs, which are sufficient for modeling the spectra in the 3-11 keV range) were created for
each spectrum using the rslmkrmf script. The full-array spectrum is plotted in Figure 1. We calculated effective area
curves (i.e. auxiliary response files, ARFs) by running ray-tracing simulations via the xaarfgen script in image mode.
Initial photon positions were determined by an exposure-corrected 2-8 keV Chandra image of A2034 (see Appendix
A). As ≳99% of the X-rays that reach the Resolve array originate within a 3’-radius circle around the FoV, we masked
the image outside of this region. We created two ARFs for the northern and southern spectra corresponding to the
northern and southern halves of the 3’ region. These spectra were then modeled as a combination of the emission from
both regions, which accounts for spatial-spectral mixing (SSM, see Appendix B of Heinrich et al. 2025).
To model the contribution to the spectra from the non-X-ray background (NXB), we extracted full-array NXB
spectra from the Resolve night-Earth database via rslnxbgen, applying the same filtering to the NXB spectra as the
on-source spectra. These full-array NXB spectra were fit using the Kilbourne et al. (2018) model, which contains a
powerlaw continuum and 8 Gaussian emission lines, using a diagonal RMF and no ARF. All NXB parameters were
fixed prior to fitting the source spectrum. For the sub-array regions, the best-fit model was then scaled according to
the number of pixels used in each on-source spectrum.
Each spectrum was fit with the NXB and ICM models in the 3-11 keV band using xspec version 12.15.1 (Arnaud
1996) with Cash statistics (Cash 1979). The ICM was modeled as an absorbed collisionally-ionized equilibrium (CIE)
plasma (tbabs·bapec) using AtomDB version 3.1.3 (Foster et al. 2013). We additionally tested AtomDB version
3.0.9 and the 5-7 keV band, which result in no significant deviations in our findings. The Hydrogen column density
was fixed to 1.6 · 1020 cm−2 (Willingale et al. 2013). We ignored the spectrum between 8.5 and 8.9 keV due to the
presence of a potential absorption-like feature, discussed in Section 4.3. The free parameters of the model are the
plasma temperature, abundance of heavy elements (relative to proto-solar abundances from Lodders et al. 2009),
redshift, velocity dispersion, and normalization. We additionally experimented with a bvapec model with free Fe and
Ni abundances. All elements lighter than Fe were tied to the Fe abundance, as emission lines from these elements
are very dim due to the high plasma temperature. The Ni abundance is measurable; however, the significance is low
(< 3σ) and always consistent with the Fe abundance within 1σ. Thus, using a bapec model is sufficient for this data.

morphology of the A2034S BCG (Monteiro-Oliveira et al. 2018).
Diverging bulk motions along the LoS may also contribute to Vdisp.
As discussed in Section 1, A2034 may be
considered a Bullet-like merger without the bullet. It has been proposed that the core of the infalling subcluster
(A2034N) may have been disrupted by the impact between the two main ICM components in this merger (Owers et al.
2013). In this scenario, the two gas components will spread outwards, driving gas motions perpendicular to the merger
axis (i.e., along the east-west axis and the LoS). This “gas squashing” will result in a distribution of diverging bulk
motions that enhances the velocity dispersion. The high Vdisp measured by Resolve therefore suggests a core disruption
has taken place. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that no compact core existed in either subcluster prior
to the ongoing merger. Distinguishing between these possibilities may be possible with further XRISM observations
(which will allow for velocity measurement in smaller regions) or in the future with NewAthena.

4.1. Comparison to other merging clusters and numerical simulations

merging clusters observed with XRISM so far, such as Coma (XRISM Collaboration 2025a; Gatuzz et al. 2026),
A2319 (XRISM Collaboration 2025b), A3667 (Omiya et al. 2026a), and A754 (Omiya et al. 2026b). We demonstrate

note that the gas motions in each of these clusters may be injected on scales smaller than either scale presented here.
However, cluster mergers are expected to drive gas motions on scales of a few 100s kpc or larger (e.g., Vazza et al.
2018; Miniati 2015); thus, the measured Vdisp is likely not dominated by small-scale motions.

associated with the gas motions in A2034 are comparable to or slightly larger than the scales probed in other clusters.
Despite the similarity in scale, A2034 has a higher kinetic pressure fraction than any other cluster in this sample
(∼15 ± 3%), with only A3667 coming close (∼10 ± 2%). We note that sub-FoV fits in other clusters can yield regions
with turbulent Mach numbers similar to A2034, e.g. M3D ≈0.7 ± 0.2 in A3667 and M3D ≈0.6 ± 0.2 in A754 (Omiya
et al. 2026a,b).
XRISM Collaboration (2025d) compared the velocity dispersions measured by XRISM in several clusters to tailored
predictions from a number of cosmological simulations. Two non-cool-core clusters were included in that sample,
A2319 and the Coma cluster, both of which were found to have kinetic pressure fractions of ∼3%. This is on the low
end of the prediction from simulated clusters (Pkin/Ptot ∼3 −11%). A2034 is similar in mass3 to both A2319 and
Coma, so these clusters can be directly compared.
With Pkin/Ptot ≈15%, A2034 is consistent with/above the upper end of predictions from cosmological simulations.
This indicates that the apparent trend of lower kinetic pressure in observed clusters may be a product of a limited
sample size. In other words, the addition of A2034 balances the otherwise bottom-heavy XRISM-observed sample.

4.2. Spectral anomalies
While the one and two-component models described above provide reasonable fits to the A2034 spectrum, there are
a few features that are not explained by this baseline model:
• Lyα anomaly: A2034 shows a brighter Fe Lyα-2 line than is predicted by the baseline model. This is visible as a
positive residual from the baseline model (black curve) at ∼6.25 keV in Figure 3, detected at ∼2σ significance.
While the value of the Lyα-2/1 ratio for this temperature gas is expected to be ∼0.5, the observed ratio in A2034
is higher. We measure the ratio by freezing the temperature, metallicity, and velocity dispersion of the single-
component model and replacing the Lyα lines with two Gaussians. The width and redshift of each Gaussian
are tied to the bapec model, while the normalizations are free. In the full FoV, the ratio Lyα ratio is 1.3+0.8
−0.4.
A similar anomalously high Lyα ratio was observed in the Coma and A2029 clusters (XRISM Collaboration
2025a,c); however, the difference is more prominent in A2034.
• Heα-z anomaly: We detect a suppression of the Fe Heα forbidden (z) line relative to the baseline model (Figure
3 at ∼5.95 keV). In optically-thin CIE plasmas with typical ICM temperatures, the forbidden-to-resonance ratio

Modifying the baseline model by including multiple temperature, metallicity, or velocity components cannot explain
the anomalous z/w line ratio, though it can partially reduce the Lyα-2 residual (see Figure A2). Resonant scattering
(see, e.g., Churazov et al. 2010; Gu et al. 2018, for reviews) is unlikely to be the cause either. The optical depth of the
potentially optically thick w-line is ∼0.3 (assuming a spherically-symmetric ICM and no turbulent broadening)—too
small to create a noticeable distortion due to resonant scattering. Moreover, resonant scattering should increase the
z/w ratio relative to the optically-thin prediction (assuming the ICM is not extremely asymmetric along the LoS).
Resonant scattering cannot explain the anomalous Lyα ratio either, as the optical depth for both Lyα lines is < 0.1.
Charge exchange is also unlikely to be significant in this cluster as there is no cool gas to supply neutral atoms (e.g.,
Gu and Shah 2023).
Though the significance of these features is low (∼2σ, individually), their presence, if statistically confirmed, points
to some intriguing possibilities for ICM physics. One (rather exotic) explanation for the observed line ratios is a
departure from CIE (see, e.g., Gu et al. 2018, for review). A non-equilibrium ionzation state has been previously
reported in A754 (Inoue et al. 2016) and A3667 (Shishido et al. 2025) using Suzaku observations. However, these
spectra do not resolve the individual Heα and Lyα lines as XRISM does. At Suzaku’s spectral resolution an apparent
deviation from CIE could also be explained by multiple temperature/velocity components, which is reasonable to
expect given the mergers ongoing in both clusters. Notably, both A754 and A3667 have been observed with XRISM
(Omiya et al. 2026a,b), yielding spectra which do not show signs of a suppressed z/w ratio. However, both have
residuals in the Lyα lines, which may indicate a secondary hot ICM component or a mixture of the CIE and NEI
plasmas.
To explore this idea, we consider a model where a merger shock heats the ICM. In the immediate wake of the shock,
the plasma temperature has risen substantially, but the gas has not yet reached ionization equilibrium, leading to
specific spectral signatures (Mewe and Schrijver 1978; Liedahl 1999). In the shocked ICM condition, the collisional
excitation of the w line increases while the main channels leading to z line emission (H-like ions recombination or
Li-like ions innershell ionization) become subdominant, causing a decrease in the z/w ratio (Churazov et al. 2026).
This NEI state is short lived; however, it is not unrealistic to suggest we may observe it in this cluster. The ionization
parameter τ = ne · t (where ne is the electron density and t is the ionization timescale) for an NEI ICM is expected to
be ≲a few 1012 s cm−3 (Churazov et al. 2026). For typical ICM densities of ∼10−3 cm−3, the NEI state can last up
to ∼30 Myr. However, as the shock passes through the ICM more gas will be brought out of CIE, effectively trailing
an NEI region behind the shock. Therefore, finely tuned merger timing is not required to expect that we may detect
emission from the NEI gas.
If a merger shock is within the Resolve FoV we may expect three ICM states to contribute to the X-ray emission: a
pre-shock, low-temperature (∼6−7 keV) gas in CIE; a post-shock NEI gas; and a post-shock, high temperature (> 10
keV) gas that has returned to CIE. We probe this configuration by modeling the full-FoV Resolve spectrum with a
combination of three plasma components: two bapec components—representing the pre- and post-shock CIE gas—and
a brnei component that represents the NEI gas in the wake of the shock. The temperature of the pre-shock bapec
model is tied to the initial temperature of the brnei model, while the post-shock bapec and final brnei temperatures
are tied. We additionally tie the abundances and velocity dispersions between each component. The normalizations
and redshifts of each component are free.

We note the possibility that an NEI plasma has not reached ion-electron temperature equilibrium. In the wake of the
shock, the ion temperature may be higher than the electron temperature. If that is the case, the Fe emission lines would
be thermally broadened more than expected and therefore a CIE model would overestimate the velocity dispersion
based on the line widths. Independently measuring the ion temperature requires resolving lines from multiple elements,
which is not possible with the current data.
The NEI model we consider requires the presence of a merger shock within or near the Resolve FoV, yet the only
reported shock in A2034 is the Mach ∼1.8 shock located ∼1′ north of the FoV (Owers et al. 2013). A southern shock
is expected in/near the FoV based on the merger configuration (Moura et al. 2021). It is also expected to be weaker
and cover a smaller region than the northern shock, given the measured mass ratio of the merging components. Thus,
the shock may be hidden by projection effects due to the bright core or a shock orientation along the LoS. Further
evidence for a shock in this region is given by the presence of a hot (∼12 keV) region near the southern edge of the
FoV (Owers et al. 2013). Finally, a radio structure just south of the FoV (which is likely either a radio relic or phoenix,
Shimwell et al. 2016) also indicates a recent shock passage.
Longer XRISM observations of A2034 are necessary to statistically confirm the anomalous line ratios and further
explore this potential non-equilibrium ionization state in the ICM. To estimate the required exposure time, we produce
mock Resolve spectra using the xspec fakeit routine, based on the best-fit, full-FoV model and its accompanying
response files. By generating 100 mock spectra for various exposure times, we find the median time required to confirm
the z/w anomaly (the driving signature of the NEI state) at > 3σ significance is an additional ∼360 ks. Essentially,
a factor of ∼4 increase in the number of photons is needed to confirm this result. The additional Resolve exposure
would also yield a ∼4.5σ detection of the anomalous Lyα ratio.
4.3. Potential absorption feature
Careful examination of the A2034 spectrum has revealed another unique anomaly: a “dip” in the spectrum between
∼8.5 and 8.9 keV (Figure 3, the right panel). The exact shape of this feature is unclear, given the low photon counts
in the hard X-ray band. At low resolution (the spectrum shown in Figure 3 is visually binned to > 5σ significance), it
can be modeled as a Gaussian absorption line (gabs model in xspec). Modeling this feature in the full FoV spectrum,
we find a detector-frame centroid of Eabs = 8.70 ± 0.03 keV with a width of σabs = 80 ± 30 eV and an optical depth
of τabs = 0.8 ± 0.4. This feature is detected at ∼4σ significance. We also fit this feature in the north/south half-FoV
spectra, finding no significant variance in any of the gabs parameters.
The presence of such an absorption feature in the spectrum of a galaxy cluster is puzzling. To the best of our
knowledge, no such features have been reported previously, and they are not expected on physical grounds unless
rather unusual conditions are invoked. We verified that the NXB spectrum shows no emission lines in the 8-9 keV
band that could have been incorrectly subtracted to produce an artificial absorption feature. We also confirmed with

The energy and width of the feature are consistent with an ultrafast outflow (UFO) from an AGN (e.g., XRISM
Collaboration 2025e). Although no AGN is detected within the detector FoV, based on the Chandra image, there are
several bright point sources near the FoV (see Figure A1) that could, in principle, contribute to the observed spectrum
as light scattered by the PSF. The presence of a UFO would also require a significant contribution of the AGN
continuum to the spectrum. It is possible to add an AGN component to the model described in Section 3, effectively
reducing the continuum emission from the ICM. However, this will increase the ICM metallicity to ≳0.8, which is
rather high compared to typical values of ∼0.3 −0.5 in the ICM and inconsistent with the metallicity measured by
Chandra (Kempner et al. 2003). Additionally, the feature would have to absorb essentially all of the AGN continuum
emission from 8.5 −8.9 keV range, meaning the UFO would need to have an extreme optical depth. Therefore, we
consider this explanation unlikely.
Another rather exotic possibility is that the feature comes from a shock in the outskirts of A2034, where the gas
density is very low. With a particular shock geometry (e.g., a shock front close to be parallel to the LoS), the column
density of the shocked region could be significant, while the total density (and therefore X-ray emissivity) remains low.
In this case, the feature could be attributed to absorption by a high energy atomic transition. The highest energy
atomic transition expected to be strong in the ICM is the Ni Lyα lines, at ∼8.1 keV. For this line to reach 8.7 keV,
it must be blueshifted by ∼50 · 103 km/s (or ∼0.16c) relative to the cluster mean, which is unrealistic to expect in
a galaxy cluster. There are weaker lines at higher energies that would require a smaller blueshift (e.g., the Fe Heγ-ι
lines); however, these would be paired with stronger, lower-energy lines (e.g., Heα) that we do not see in absorption
in the Resolve spectrum.
In conclusion, we cannot identify a physically motivated explanation for the absorption feature. Given its current
∼4σ significance, it may still be a statistical fluctuation. Deeper XRISM observations are required to confirm its
presence, investigate possible multi-component structure, and explore its spatial variation within the cluster. We note
that if the 4σ detection of the feature is due to mere statistical fluctuations, the temperature of the ICM in A2034
reduces from ∼8.1 keV to 7.9 ± 0.3 keV, while all other parameters are unchanged.
5. CONCLUSIONS
We conducted a detailed study of the high-resolution X-ray spectrum measured by XRISM/Resolve in the merg-
ing cluster A2034, revealing several unique features that could not have been discovered without XRISM’s spectral
resolution. The standout feature is the velocity dispersion, which is higher than in other merging clusters observed
by XRISM so far: ∼400 −500 km/s in the full FoV, corresponding to a 3D turbulent Mach number of ∼0.56. As
expected for a near-plane-of-sky merger, the average bulk velocity of the ICM in the full FoV is consistent with 0
km/s. The main conclusions can be summarized as follows:


## Page 9


9
• We probe the spectrum for evidence of multiple bulk velocity components along the line-of-sight.
We find
that the combination of a hot (kT ≈11 keV) component that is redshifted with respect to the cluster mean
(Vbulk ≈+500 km/s) with a cooler blueshifted component (kT ≈6.5 keV, Vbulk ≈−100 km/s) provides a better
fit to the spectrum. When separating these bulk velocity components, the velocity dispersion of both components
is ∼390 km/s.
• By splitting the FoV along the north-south merger axis, we show that the hot component is localized in the
south and the cooler component in the north. The bulk velocity gradient between the two regions is ∼380 km/s,
consistent with the near-plane-of-sky, head-on merger in A2034.
• The southern/northern gas are moving in the opposite directions along the LoS of the southern/northern galaxies,
demonstrating a decoupling between the two, which is expected in dissociative mergers. The gas is also moving
slower compared to the BCGs of the northern/southern subclusters, consistent with the collisional nature of the
ICM. This indicates the merger in A2034 is just past pericenter passage as the galaxies have had time to pass
each other but the gas has not.
• We discussed that diverging bulk motions along the LoS associated with the disruption of the subcluster cores
during the merger (“gas squashing”) may be partially responsible for the high velocity dispersion.
• We compare the turbulent Mach number (M3D) in A2034 to other merging clusters observed by XRISM, cal-
culating the gas motions’ associated length scale in each. While the length scales probed in A2034 (i.e., a few
hundred kpc) are similar to other clusters, it has a turbulent Mach number of ∼0.56 and kinetic pressure frac-
tion of ∼15%, higher than any other merging cluster yet observed. This kinetic pressure fraction puts A2034
on the high end of the Pkin/Ptot distribution found in analogous clusters in cosmological simulations (XRISM
Collaboration 2025d). Thus, A2034 helps to balance the distribution of clusters observed by XRISM, which have
so far skewed to low kinetic pressures.
We additionally find several tentative residuals in the Resolve spectrum that are not captured by an optically thin,
collisionally-ionized equilibrium plasma. Namely, these are: a low Heα z/w ratio, a high Lyα 2/1 ratio, and a dip
in the spectrum at ∼8.7 keV indicating an unexplained absorption feature, which are detected at ∼2, 2, and 4σ,
respectively.
The anomalous line ratios could be explained if the ICM is in a non-equilibrium ionization state following the passage
of a merger shock. The low z/w ratio, if confirmed with longer XRISM observations, would indicate an NEI state in the
ICM, the first robustly discovered via this diagnostic criterion. The high Lyα ratio can be explained by a redshifted,
hot (kT > 10 keV) ICM component. We model the narrow-band spectrum of A2034 with a combination of NEI and
CIE components, finding that a M ≈1.8 shock that heats the gas from ∼7 to 12 keV can reproduce the observed Heα
and Lyα emission lines. Both line ratio anomalies require additional XRISM exposure time to statistically confirm.
We fit the hard X-ray absorption feature with a Gaussian absorption line, finding a centroid of ∼8.7 keV, width
of ∼70 eV, and an optical depth of ∼0.8. We do not detect significant variation in the absorption feature’s spectral
properties across the FoV or over the time interval of the observation. We speculate on the possible origin of this
feature, however we cannot find a physically-convincing explanation without further data.
A2034 stands out from other XRISM-observed merging clusters due to the higher-than-previously-observed gas
kinetic pressure fraction and the anomalous spectral features it produces.
This work demonstrates the need for
deep XRISM observations of massive merging clusters—as well as supporting theoretical studies aimed at Resolve’s
capabilities—to understand the physics that produce these and other spectral anomalies.
