Abstract
We study a variant of the modal µ-calculus based on the constructive
modal logic CK. We define game semantics for the constructive µ-calculus
and prove its equivalence to the birelational Kripke semantics. We then use
the game semantics to prove the soundness and completeness of a fully-
labeled non-wellfounded proof system for it. At last, we briefly describe
how to adapt the game semantics and proof system to the µ-calculus over
other non-classical modal logics.
1
Introduction
We define a constructive variant of the modal µ-calculus by adding least and
greatest fixed-point operators to the constructive modal logic CK. We then
define birelational and Kripke game semantics for the constructive µ-calculus
and prove their equivalence.
We combine fully-labeled sequents and non-
wellfounded proof systems to define labµCKω. We prove that labµCKω is sound
and weakly complete with respect to CK-models using our game semantics. To
illustrate the flexibility of our methods, we describe how to adapt labµCKω to
proof systems labµIKω and labµGKω sound and weakly complete with respect
to the non-classical modal logics IK and GL, respectively.
The modal µ-calculus was defined by Kozen [Koz83] by adding least and
greatest fixed-point operators to K. The µ-formulas are famously hard to un-
derstand as they allow for entangled least and greatest fixed-point operators.
Game semantics for the µ-calculus they give a more intuitive interpretation of
the µ-formulas; they are also a powerful tool to work with. See [BW18, GTW03,
Len10] for more information on the classical µ-calculus.
Kozen also defined a Hilbert-style proof system µK for the classical µ-calculus,
whose completeness was first proved by Walukiewicz [Wal95]. This result was
obtained by an analysis of a non-wellfounded proof system first studied by
Niwi´nski and Walukiewicz [NW96]. Other infinitary proof systems for the µ-
calculus have also been studied [ALM24, Stu08].
In constructive modal logic, the duality of the modalities □and ♢is lost.
These logics have been studied for a long time: some of the first texts on the
topic are Fitch [Fit48] and Prawitz [Pra65]. In this paper, we use Mendler and

Fully-labeled sequents were first used by Marin et al. [MMSb21] to study
the intuitionistic modal logic IK. They consist of labeled formulas x : φ along

later used to prove the decidability of IS4 [GKM+23] and IK [GKM+24]. As

classical modal logics.
Our contribution
We first define evaluation games for the constructive µ-
calculus. In both classical and constructive games, two players discuss whether
a formula is true at a given world of a Kripke model. In the classical games,
it is usual to refer to the players as Verifier and Refuter. In the constructive
games, we will still have two players; but now Verifier and Refuter denote two
roles and may switch roles depending on their moves. This change is necessary
because, over constructive semantics, not every formula can be put in negative
normal form; and formulas in negative normal form are what allows us to
simplify the classical evaluation games. Furthermore, we also need to consider
the semantics of constructive diamonds, which mix universal and existential
quantifiers. Therefore we will need a more delicate argument to prove the
equivalence of the semantics in the constructive case.
We then define a fully-labeled non-wellfounded proof system labµCKω. This
is obtained by adding fixed-points to a proof system based on the fully-labeled
proof systems of Marin et al. [MMSb21] and binested proof system of Gao and
Olivetti [GO26]. We prove its soundness by showing that any contermodel for
a formula φ witnesses that φ has no proof. We then prove its completeness by
extracting a countermodel φ from a failed proof search.
We also sketch how to adapt our results to obtain proof systems for the intu-
itionistic modal logic IK [Sim94] the G¨odel–Dummett modal logic GK [CR10],
and other related modal logics. We close the paper with some remarks on fu-
ture work.

2.1
Syntax
The language of the µ-calculus is obtained by adding least and greatest fixed-
point operators µ and ν to the language of modal logic. When defining the fixed-
point formulas µX.φ and νX.φ, we need a syntactical requirement in order to
have well-defined semantics.
Definition 1. Fix pairwise disjoint sets Prop and Var of propositions and propo-
sitional variables, respectively. The µ-formulas are defined by the following
grammar:
φ := P | X | ⊥| φ ∧φ | φ ∨φ | φ →φ | □φ | ♢φ | µX.φ | νX.φ,
where P is a proposition, X is a propositional variable occurring only posi-
tively in φ. We abbreviate φ →⊥by ¬φ and ⊥→⊥by ⊤. We also call
µ-formulas by formulas.
The fixed-point formulas µX.φ and νX.φ are defined iff X is positive in
φ. We classify an occurrence of X as positive or negative in a given formula by
structural induction:
• X is positive and negative in P and in ⊥;
• X is positive in X;
• if Y ̸= X, X is positive and negative in Y ;
• if X is positive (negative) in φ and ψ, then X is positive (negative) in
φ ∧ψ, φ ∨ψ, □φ, and ♢φ;
• if X is negative (positive) in φ and positive (negative) in ψ, then X is
positive (negative) in φ →ψ;
• X is positive and negative in µX.φ and νX.φ.
Denote the set of subformulas of φ by Sub(φ). We use η to denote either µ
or ν. An occurrence of a variable X in a formula φ is bound iff it in the scope
of a fixed-point operator ηX. An occurrence of X is free iff it is not bound. We
treat free occurrences of variable symbols as propositional symbols. A formula
φ is a sentence iff it has no free variables. An occurrence of X in φ is guarded
iff it is in the scope of some modality □or ♢. A formula φ is guarded iff, for
all ηX.ψ ∈Sub(φ), X is guarded in ψ. A formula φ is well-bounded iff, for all
variables X occurring bounded in φ, X occurs only once and there is only one
fixed-point operator ηX in φ. A formula is well-named iff it is guarded and well-
bounded. Note that every formula is equivalent to a well-named formula. If
φ is a well-named formula and ηX.ψ ∈Sub(ψ), denote by ψX the formula ψ
which is bound by the fixed-point operator ηX.

3
Game Semantics
In this section, we define game semantics for the constructive µ-calculus and
prove its equivalence to the birelational semantics. This game semantics is a
modification of the game semantics of the classical µ-calculus.
In evaluation games for the classical µ-calculus, the players Verifier and
Refuter discuss whether a formula φ holds in a world w of a Kripke model M.
While in the classical µ-calculus we can suppose formulas use no implications
and that negations are applied only to propositional symbols, we cannot do the
same in the constructive µ-calculus. This complicates the games used for the
constructive µ-calculus: the players now have the roles of Refuter and Verifier,
and swap roles when discussing certain formulas. Furthermore, we also need
to consider the fact that constructive diamonds are not purely existential, and
so both players are involved in choosing the next move.

3.1
Definition of Game Semantics
Fix a CK-model M = ⟨W, W ⊥, ⪯, R, V ⟩, a world w ∈W, and a well-named
sentence φ. In this subsection, we define the evaluation game G(M, w |= φ).
The game G(M, w |= φ) has two players: I and II. The two players will
have the roles of Verifier and Refuter (abbreviated to V and R, respectively).
Each player has only one role at any given time, and the players always have
different roles. We usually write “I is V” for “the player I has the role of V”,
and similar expressions for the other combination of players and games. We
denote an arbitrary role by Q and the dual role by ¯Q; that is, if Q is V, then ¯Q is
R and vice versa.
The game has two types of positions. The main positions of the game are
of the form ⟨v, ψ, Q⟩where v ∈W, ψ ∈Sub(φ), and Q is a role. We also have
auxiliary positions of the form ⟨v, ˆ♢ψ, Q⟩where ♢ψ ∈Sub(φ) and ⟨v, ψ?θ, Q⟩
where ψ →θ ∈Sub(φ), for all v ∈W. In any position, Q is the role currently
held by I; the role of II is ¯Q. Intuitively, at a position ⟨v, ψ, Q⟩, V tries to show
that v satisfies ψ and R tries to show that v does not satisfy ψ.
These auxiliary positions make explicit that both players have input on the
next main position after ⟨v, ♢ψ, Q⟩or ⟨v, ψ →θ, Q⟩. For example, at the position
⟨v, ♢ψ, Q⟩, R first makes a choice of v′ such that v ⪯v′ and then V picks v′′
such that v′Rv′′. By a similar reasoning, we also need auxiliary positions for
⟨v, θ →θ′, Q⟩.
The game begin at the position ⟨w, φ, V⟩, with I in the role of V and II in the
role of R.
Each position is owned by exactly one of the players. At each turn of the
game, the owner of the current position chooses one of the available positions
to move to. The game then continues with the new position. If no such position
is available, the game ends. The ownership and available plays for each of

3.2
Equivalence of Kripke and Game Semantics
We now show the equivalence between the constructive µ-calculus’ birelational
semantics and game semantics. That is, we will show that M, w |= φ iff the
player I has a winning strategy for G(M, w |= φ), and that M, w ̸|= φ iff the
player II has a winning strategy for G(M, w |= φ). We briefly sketch the key
idea and remark on some technical points before stating the theorem.
Fix an evaluation game G(M, w |= φ). The position ⟨v, ψ, Q⟩is a true posi-
tion iff M, w |= ψ, and a false position iff M, w ̸|= ψ. We will show that, at a
true positions, V can always move in a way favorable to themselves. That is,
in a way such that the resulting position is a true position, if the players have
not switched roles; or the resulting position is a false position, if the players
have switched roles. On the other hand, R cannot move in any way favorable
to themselves. A similar situation occurs at false positions. To make the state-
ments above precise, we have to overcome two problems.
First, when considering whether M, v |= ψ, the formula ψ might have free
variables, and so its valuation might not be well-defined. We solve this by
augmenting M with the intended valuations for the variables occurring in φ.
Second, we need to consider infinite plays. Specifically, we need to guar-
antee that, when starting from a true position, the resulting play is winning
for I; and when starting from a false position, the resulting play is winning for
II. To solve this, we assign two signatures to each position; both are finite se-
quences of ordinals ordered with the lexical order. They will be the key tool to
guarantee the resulting plays are winning for the corresponding players.
Theorem 4. The birelational Kripke semantics and game semantics for CK are equiva-
lent. That is, if M = ⟨W, W ⊥, ⪯, R, V ⟩is a CK-model, w ∈W and φ is a well-named
µ-sentence, then:
• I has a winning strategy for G(M, w |= φ) if and only if M, w |= φ; and
• II has a winning strategy for G(M, w |= φ) if and only if M, w ̸|= φ.
Proof. See Appendix C.

Future Work
In future work, we plan to define a cyclic version of labµCKω similar to the one
in [AP25] and then adapt the loop checking methods of [GKM+23, GKM+24]

We close this paper with a(n infinite collection of) questions. Let HµCK be
Hilbert-style system obtained by adding fixed-point axioms and rules to the
axioms of CK.3 Do labµCKω and HµCK prove the same theorems? While one

Proof of Proposition 1
Fix a model M and sets A ⊆B ⊆W. We prove that if X is positive in φ then
∥φ(A)∥⊆∥φ(B)∥; and that if X is negative in φ, then ∥φ(B)∥⊆∥φ(A)∥. We
will focus on the positive case, as the negative case is dual.
The proof is by structural induction on the µ-formulas. The cases of formu-
las of the form P, X, Y , φ ∧ψ, and φ ∨ψ follow by direct calculations. The case
for formulas of the form ηX.φ is trivial, as X is not free in ηX.φ.
We now prove the proposition for formulas of the form φ →ψ. Suppose X
is positive in φ →ψ, then X is positive in ψ and negative in φ. Therefore:
w ∈∥(φ →ψ)(A)∥⇐⇒for all v, if w ⪯v and v ∈∥φ(A)∥, then v ∈∥ψ(A)∥
=⇒for all v, if w ⪯v and v ∈∥φ(B)∥, then v ∈∥ψ(B)∥
⇐⇒w ∈∥(φ →ψ)(B)∥.
The case for formulas of the form ¬φ is similar.
Finally, we prove the proposition for formulas of the form □φ. Suppose X
is positive in □φ, then X is positive in φ. Therefore:
w ∈∥□φ(A)∥⇐⇒for all v, u such that w ⪯vRu, u ∈∥φ(A)∥
=⇒for all v, u such that w ⪯vRu, u ∈∥φ(B)∥
⇐⇒w ∈∥□φ(B)∥.
The proof for formulas of the form ♢φ is similar.
