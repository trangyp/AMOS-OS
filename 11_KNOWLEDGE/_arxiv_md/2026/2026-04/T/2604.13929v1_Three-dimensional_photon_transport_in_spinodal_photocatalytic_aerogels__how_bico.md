Abstract
Porous monolithic photocatalysts based on anatase TiO2 dispersed in a sil-
ica aerogel matrix are promising platforms for indoor air purification and VOC
photo-degradation. Their bicontinuous spinodal architecture simultaneously pro-
vides high specific surface area, structural integrity, and intense multiple scatter-
ing of UV photons.
However, extracting intrinsic kinetic descriptors from mea-
sured apparent rate constants requires an optical model for the photon fluence
inside the material. Current practice replaces the complex three-dimensional (3D)
pore network by a homogeneous one-dimensional (1D) slab with effective optical
coefficients—an approximation whose error has never been quantified for spinodal
geometries. Here we fill this gap by combining realistic 3D spinodal pore masks gen-
erated via Cahn–Hilliard spectral integration with full 3D GPU-accelerated Monte
Carlo (MC) photon-migration simulations (PMCX). We introduce a solid-phase-
weighted fluence estimator ⟨Φ⟩MC,3D that properly accounts for the spatial dis-
tribution of catalytic sites, and compare it with the conventional volume aver-
age ⟨Φ⟩MC,1D and the diffusion approximation (DA) prediction ⟨Φ⟩DA. The ratio
⟨Φ⟩MC,3D/⟨Φ⟩MC,1D = 1.50 at ϕ = 0.70 (rising to 1.70 at ϕ = 0.90) reveals that
the solid phase is preferentially illuminated—a direct consequence of the bicon-
tinuous morphology that creates quasi-ballistic photon paths through percolated
pore channels (“photon channelling”). The kinetic descriptor ksurf · Kads extracted
from the measured apparent rate constant differs by 34% between the MC 3D and
DA routes. Complementary homogeneous-slab MC simulations isolate the morpho-
logical contribution: approximately 50 percentage points of the total 72.8% RMS
discrepancy between MC and DA are intrinsic to the spinodal architecture and
cannot be captured by any effective-medium DA. These results provide the first
quantitative correction for kinetic extraction in bicontinuous photocatalysts and
establish design rules linking Cahn–Hilliard coarsening, pore-size distribution, and
photon utilisation efficiency.
Keywords: Spinodal decomposition, Cahn–Hilliard, Monte Carlo photon migration,
Diffusion approximation, Photocatalysis, Aerogel, TiO2/SiO2, Bicontinuous morphology.

Introduction
Semiconductor photocatalysis using TiO2 has emerged as one of the most versatile ap-
proaches for environmental remediation, from water purification to indoor air treatment
and solar-driven synthesis [1]. Among the many reactor configurations explored, porous
monolithic photocatalysts based on anatase TiO2 nanoparticles (NPs) dispersed in a silica
aerogel matrix have attracted growing attention [2, 3]. Their macroporous, bicontinu-
ous architecture offers simultaneously a high specific surface area accessible to gaseous
reactants, structural integrity, and intense multiple scattering of UV photons—three con-
ditions essential for efficient heterogeneous photocatalysis [1, 4].
Understanding and controlling photon transport inside these materials is a prereq-
uisite for rational design. The apparent rate constant kapp measured in a degradation
experiment integrates three coupled phenomena: (i) photon transport and absorption
within the porous volume, (ii) generation and surface migration of photo-excited charge
carriers, and (iii) Langmuir–Hinshelwood surface reaction kinetics [4, 5]. To extract the
intrinsic surface reactivity from kapp, one must therefore know the local photon fluence
Φ(r) inside the monolith. This fluence is commonly estimated using a one-dimensional
(1D) diffusion approximation (DA) with Maxwell–Garnett effective-medium optical co-
efficients [6, 7]. Our companion paper [8] derived compact closed-form expressions for
the intrinsic volumetric rate constant kV,mono within this framework and validated them
against 1D Monte Carlo (MC) simulations.
What the 1D benchmark cannot assess is the role of the actual three-dimensional pore
morphology. Real Si(HIPE) aerogels [2] display a bicontinuous spinodal microstructure
with interconnected solid and void phases, characterised by a broad pore-size distribu-
tion and topological features—percolation, saddle-point interfaces, and correlated void
channels—that have no counterpart in a 1D slab model. Several studies have highlighted
that microstructure geometry affects both the effective optical coefficients [9] and the
photon path-length distribution in porous media [10, 11], while topology-optimised 3D
reactors have demonstrated that pore connectivity controls photon mean free path and
optical thickness independently [12]. However, a direct quantification of the morpho-
logical effect on photocatalytic rate constants, comparing a geometrically faithful 3D
simulation with the analytical 1D prediction, has not been reported.
This gap matters because the field of photocatalysis is moving toward data-driven
and rational design strategies [13, 5], which require accurate kinetic descriptors. If the
optical model used to extract these descriptors introduces a systematic 30–50% error, the
resulting structure–activity relationships will be fundamentally unreliable.
The present paper fills this gap with four specific objectives: (i) generate realistic 3D
spinodal aerogel geometries via numerical integration of the Cahn–Hilliard equation [14,
15, 16]; (ii) run full 3D GPU Monte Carlo photon-migration simulations (PMCX [17]) with
physically consistent Fresnel boundary conditions; (iii) introduce a solid-phase-weighted
fluence estimator that properly accounts for the spatial distribution of catalytic sites; and
(iv) compare fluence profiles and intrinsic rate constants across three estimators (MC 3D
solid-weighted, MC 1D volume-averaged, DA collimated EMA), identifying where and
why the approaches agree or diverge. The role of morphology is further isolated through
complementary homogeneous-slab simulations presented in Section 4.2 and in the Sup-
plementary Information.

Discussion
4.1
Physical mechanism of morphological photon channelling
The high-fluence islands visible in Fig. 4 at z > 0.05 mm arise from quasi-ballistic trans-
port through percolated pore channels. In the 1D DA, every photon traverses a medium
of uniform absorption µa,eff; in the 3D spinodal geometry, a photon entering a pore cluster
travels with zero absorption over a distance of order dpore. For the secondary population
at d ≈37 µm, this corresponds to ≈0.22 ℓ∗
eff, sufficient to sustain a measurably elevated
fluence far from the entry face. The resulting path-length distribution is bimodal: short,
heavily absorbed solid-phase trajectories versus long, unattenuated pore-channel paths.
The high-penetration tail of this distribution is absent from any 1D model.

4.4
Validity and limits of the diffusion approximation
The system (µa/µ′
s = 0.256, κL = 3.30) is at the boundary of DA validity on two
counts. First, µa/µ′
s = 0.256 violates µa ≪µ′
s (< 0.05 recommended [7]), causing the DA
to overestimate diffuse contributions and miss quasi-ballistic transport. Second, κL =
3.30 is marginally diffusive (κL > 5 typically required [26]). The homogeneous-medium
comparisons (Section 4.2) confirm that these two factors account for approximately 22%
RMS deviation in the absence of morphological effects. The additional ∼50% arises
entirely from the spinodal structure.
The DA overestimates ⟨Φ⟩by approximately 34% relative to the MC 3D reference
(ratio MC 3D/DA = 1.34); the kinetic error on ksurf · Kads is of the same magnitude. For
engineering applications where ∼30% accuracy in the kinetic constant is acceptable, the
DA with full EMA consistency remains a useful screening tool. For accurate extraction
of ksurf · Kads or NP distribution optimisation, the MC 3D solid-weighted estimator is
indispensable.
4.5
Effect of porosity: comparison between ϕ = 0.70 and ϕ = 0.90
The Supplementary Information presents a second spinodal case at ϕ = 0.90 (κL = 1.10,
front/back = 30; Fig. 9). The increase in porosity has two coupled consequences. First,
the EMA coefficients scale as (1−ϕ) and the optical thickness drops to κL = 1.10, placing
this sample well outside the diffusion regime.
Second, the more open solid skeleton
(Fig. 10) provides longer unobstructed pore-channel paths, amplifying the channelling
effect relative to ϕ = 0.70.
Quantitatively, the MC 3D/MC 1D ratio increases from 1.50 at ϕ = 0.70 to 1.70 at
ϕ = 0.90, confirming that sparser geometries concentrate photons more selectively on the
solid phase. The MC 3D/DA ratio decreases slightly (1.34 →1.23), reflecting the fact
that at κL = 1.10 the DA is even further from validity and therefore less representative,
but the absolute fluence differences become more tolerable for kinetic screening because
the sample is more transparent overall. These trends are discussed in detail alongside
Table 6.
4.6
Implications for photocatalytic reactor design
Three practical implications follow from these results.
Solid fraction optimisation. Increasing porosity amplifies the morphological con-
centration ratio ⟨Φ⟩MC,3D/⟨Φ⟩MC,1D (from 1.50 at ϕ = 0.70 to 1.70 at ϕ = 0.90), substan-
tially amplifying the effective photon dose on catalytic sites per unit mass. The optimal
trade-off between photon dose amplification and reduction of catalytic surface area must
be evaluated with the full MC 3D pipeline, since the DA cannot predict the concentration
ratio reliably.
Pore size distribution engineering. The bimodal PSD amplifies pore channelling
through the secondary large-pore population. The Cahn–Hilliard stopping criterion di-
rectly controls the extent of coarsening and hence the amplitude of the secondary peak;
arresting integration earlier maintains a more monodisperse PSD and reduces channelling,
while later stopping allows more coarsening and increases it.
NP distribution mode. The present results are for bulk-distributed NPs (mode =
’bulk’). Localising NPs at the solid/pore interface (mode = ’interface’) would concentrate

effective rate constant by a further factor proportional to ⟨Φ⟩MC,3D,interface/⟨Φ⟩MC,3D,bulk.
5
Conclusions
We have presented a complete computational pipeline—Cahn–Hilliard spinodal geome-
try generation, Maxwell–Garnett EMA optical properties, PMCX 3D GPU Monte Carlo,
and collimated-source diffusion-approximation benchmark—applied to bicontinuous spin-
odal aerogel photocatalysts at λ = 365 nm. All simulations employ Fresnel boundary
conditions on all six faces of the simulation box (bc = " ", isreflect = 1), which we
identify as the physically most consistent choice. Our main conclusions are of two types:
technically and scientifically.
Technically, we have clearly shown that (1) ensuring Fresnel Boundary Conditions
on all six faces of the MC simulation box eliminates simultaneously cyclic re-injection
artefacts from the lateral faces and non-physical lateral flux sinks. This choice is validated
by excellent agreement between the DA and MC for homogeneous solid slabs (∼18%
RMS). (2) Both µa and µ′
s must be scaled by (1 −ϕ) for DA parameters. Using the shell
µ′
s with the EMA µa overestimates κ by ≈69% and erroneously compresses the fluence
profile. (3) The correct DA for planar illumination decomposes the fluence into ballistic
and diffuse terms (Eqs. 22–25). The isotropic-source Green’s function [8] introduces a
spurious peak at z = ℓ∗and should not be used for collimated sources.
Scientifically, we have found that (1) the energy balance delivers R = 0.055, A =
0.600, T ≈0 (front/back = 374). With Fresnel BCs on all faces, T represents genuine
forward transmission only; the closure R+T +A = 0.655 reflects the photon recycling by
the lateral Fresnel mirrors. (2) The solid phase sees 50% more photons than the volume
average (⟨Φ⟩MC,3D/⟨Φ⟩MC,1D = 1.50), increasing to 1.70 at ϕ = 0.90. This concentration
is a robust property of spinodal morphology. (3) The three kinetic extraction estima-
tors give ksurf · Kads values spanning a factor of 2.0. The MC 3D solid-weighted value
(1.25 × 10−34 m4 photon−1) is the correct reference. The DA underestimates by 25%; the
MC 1D volume average overestimates by 50%. Their near-cancellation in the DA/MC 1D
ratio is accidental. (4) Homogeneous-slab MC–DA comparisons (Section 4.2 and Supple-
mentary Information) show that ∼22% of the total 72.8% RMS comes from inherent DA
limitations at κL = 3.30, while the remaining ∼50% is a direct signature of the spinodal
bicontinuous morphology. (5) The bicontinuous pore network creates quasi-ballistic paths
through connected voids (photon channelling), co-localised with the secondary PSD peak
at ≈37 µm and directly visible in the 2D fluence map (Fig. 4). This effect cannot be
captured by any 1D model and is amplified at higher porosity.
These last points, together with their implications for photocatalytic reactor design,
discussed in Section 4.6, provide strong guidance and illustrate the huge interest of using
highly porous aerogels for photocatalysis in volume.

Optical thickness and photon penetration. Increasing porosity from 0.70 to
0.90 reduces κL from 3.30 to 1.10, shifting the sample from the marginally diffusive to
the ballistic-dominated regime. The front/back ratio drops from 374 to 30, indicating
qualitatively different photon penetration behaviour. Absorptance falls from 60% to 36%;
a larger fraction of incident photons passes through the slab unabsorbed, which reduces
the photon dose per catalytic site but extends the irradiated depth.
Morphological concentration ratio. The ratio ⟨Φ⟩MC,3D/⟨Φ⟩MC,1D increases from
1.50 to 1.70 with increasing porosity. This trend reflects two compounding effects: (i) the
solid phase occupies a smaller fraction of the domain, so pore voxels contribute more
strongly to diluting the volume average; and (ii) the wider pore channels sustain longer
quasi-ballistic photon paths that preferentially deposit energy in the adjacent solid walls.
DA accuracy.
The MC 3D/DA ratio decreases from 1.34 at ϕ = 0.70 to 1.23
at ϕ = 0.90. This might suggest improving DA accuracy at higher porosity, but it is
misleading: at κL = 1.10, the DA overestimates the deep-tail fluence by a much larger
factor than at κL = 3.30 (cf. Figs. 2b and S5b), but the depth-averaged error happens to
be somewhat smaller because the overall attenuation is weaker. For engineering estimates
of integrated kinetics, neither porosity gives reliable DA predictions; the MC 3D pipeline
is essential.
Kinetic constants. The absolute values of ksurf·Kads are five times larger at ϕ = 0.90
than at ϕ = 0.70. This reflects the lower photon dose per catalytic site (lower µa, lower
absorptance) which forces a larger inferred intrinsic reactivity to match the same kapp,exp.
The inter-estimator ratios (MC 3D/DA and MC 3D/MC 1D) are more similar between
the two porosities than the absolute values, confirming that the bias structure of the
three estimators is a property of the morphological type rather than a specific porosity
value.
