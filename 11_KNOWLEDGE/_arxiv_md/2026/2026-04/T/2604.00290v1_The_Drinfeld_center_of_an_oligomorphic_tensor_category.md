Abstract. Recently, Harman and the second author introduced a new construction of pre-
Tannakian tensor categories based on oligomorphic groups. We develop tools for analyzing
the Drinfeld centers of these categories, and compute the center explicitly in a number
of cases. In particular, we find several finitely tensor-generated pre-Tannakian categories
(including the Delannoy category) that are identified with their own center via the canonical
functor; prior to this work, we knew no such examples besides the category of vector spaces.

1. Introduction
Recently, Harman and the second author introduced a new construction of pre-Tannakian
tensor categories based on oligomorphic groups [HS1]. Some of these categories, such as
the Delannoy category [HSS1], are quite different from previously known pre-Tannakian
categories. A general direction of research is the investigation of the structure and properties
of these new oligomorphic tensor categories. In this paper, we study their Drinfeld centers.
We develop methods for studying the center, and use this theory to determine the centers
of several categories.

simplest ones to state are in the following theorem. We write Z(T) for the Drinfeld center
of a monoidal category (see §2.2).

to first show that T-small is equivalent to a combinatorially defined smallness condition, and
then to analyze this condition directly using the combinatorics of the oligomorphic group. In

the strong approximation property. This covers the Delannoy category, its circular variant,
the arboreal categories, and Deligne’s category. The remaining cases are treated using similar
ideas, but the details become more ad hoc.
In §8, we explain how to handle the case when the measure µ is not quasi-regular. We
apply this to determine the Drinfeld center of the second Delannoy category.
1.3. Tensor category terminology. We fix a field k throughout the paper. All tensor cat-
egories are essentially small and k-linear. We write 1 for the tensor unit. A pre-Tannakian
category is a k-linear abelian category equipped with a k-bilinear symmetric monoidal struc-
ture such that all objects have finite length, all objects are rigid (i.e., have duals), and
End(1) = k; these conditions imply that Hom spaces are finite dimensional.

3. Oligomorphic groups and tensor categories
In this section, we recall background on oligomorphic groups and their associated tensor
categories, following [HS1]. In §3.6, we establish the correspondence between c-functors and
conjugacy sets, and in §3.7 we briefly discuss c-smooth elements. These two subsections
contain the only new material in this section.
3.1. Oligomorphic groups. A pro-oligomorphic group is a topological group G such that
(a) G is Hausdorff; (b) G is non-archimedean (open subgroups form a neighborhood basis of
the identity); and (c) G is Roelcke precompact (U\G/V is a finite set for all open subgroups
U and V ). An oligomorphic permutation group is a permutation group (G, Ω) such that G
has finitely many orbits on Ωn for all n.
The two notions are closely linked. Suppose (G, Ω) is an oligomorphic permutation group.
Given a finite subset A of Ω, let G(A) be the subgroup of G consisting of elements that

fix each element of A. These subgroups form a neighborhood basis of the identity for a
pro-oligomorphic topology on G [HS1, §2.2]. Conversely, suppose G is pro-oligomorphic.
Then for any open subgroup U of G, the action of G on G/U is oligomorphic [HS1, §2.3].
Thus, if there exists U such that this action is faithful, then (G, G/U) is an oligomorphic
permutation group. In general, G is a dense subgroup of the inverse limit of oligomorphic
permutation groups. We will ultimately be interested in oligomorphic permutation groups,
however many constructions depend only on the topology and not Ω, so it is convenient to
work in the pro-oligomorphic setting.
Let G be a pro-oligomorphic group. An action of G on a set X is smooth if the stabilizer
of each element of X is an open subgroup of G, and finitary if G has finitely many orbits
on X. We use the term “G-set” to mean “set equipped with a finitary and smooth action of
G.” A key property is that the product of two G-sets is again a G-set [HS1, Proposition 2.8].
A bG-set is a set equipped with a finitary and smooth action of some open subgroup of G;
shrinking the open subgroup does not change the bG-set. See [HS1, §2.5] for details.
Suppose (G, Ω) is oligomorphic. We say that a G-set is Ω-smooth if each orbit on it is
isomorphic to an orbit on some Ωn. The class of Ω-smooth G-sets is closed under disjoint
union, products, and passing to G-subsets. Every G-set is a quotient of an Ω-smooth one.
Indeed, a transitive G-set G/U is a quotient of the Ω-smooth G-set G/G(A), where A ⊂Ω
is a finite set with G(A) ⊂U. Typically, Ω-smooth G-sets are easier to understand than all
G-sets, which is why the notion is useful.
Example 3.1. Let S be the group of all permutations of Ω= {1, 2, . . .}, i.e., the infinite
symmetric group. This is the first example of an oligomorphic permutation group. Let S(n)
be the subgroup of S fixing each of 1, . . . , n. A subgroup of S is open if and only if it contains
S(n) for some n. By [HS1, Proposition 14.1], every open subgroup of S is conjugate to one
of the form H × S(n), where H is a subgroup of the finite symmetric group Sn.
Let Ω[n] denote the subset of Ωn where the coordinates are distinct. It is not difficult to
see that the Ω[n] are exactly the Ω-smooth transitive S-sets. It follows from [HS1, Propo-
sition 14.1] that every transitive S-set is isomorphic to Ω[n]/H for some subgroup H of Sn
(which acts on Ω[n] by permuting coordinates). An bS-subset of Ωis a finite or cofinite subset.

3.5. Fra¨ıss´e limits. The Fra¨ıss´e limit construction is an important source of oligomorphic
groups. We will use this perspective in some of the examples we discuss, so we briefly review
the key definitions. We refer to [HS1, §6.2] for additional details.
Let F be a class of finite relational structures. For example, F could be the class of
finite totally ordered sets, or finite graphs. Given embeddings i: A →B and j : A →A′
of structures in F, an amalgamation of B and A′ over A is a triple (B′, i′, j′) where B′ is
a structure in F and i′ : A′ →B′ and j′ : B →B′ are embeddings such that j′i = i′j and
B′ = im(i′) ∪im(j′). Thus an amalgamation is a way of gluing B and A′ together which
identifies the copy of A in each. Note that an amalgamation is allowed to identify more than
A. For example, if i′ : A′ →B is an embedding then (B, i′, idB) is an amalgamation of A′
and B over A = ∅.
We say that F is a Fra¨ıss´e class if it satisfies the following conditions:
(a) If B belongs to F and A →B is an embedding of structures then A belongs to F.
In particular, F contains the empty structure.
(b) Given embeddings i: A →B and j : A →A′ there exists at least one amalgamation
(B′, i′, j′) in F. (This is called the amalgamation property.)
(c) Up to isomorphism, F has only finitely many members of a given cardinality.
We recall two more definitions. A structure Ωis homogeneous if any two embeddings of a
finite structure into Ωdiffer by an automorphism of Ω. The age of a structure is the class
of finite structures that embed into it.
Let F be a Fra¨ıss´e class. Then there is a countable homogeneous structure Ωwith age
F; moreover, any other such structure is isomorphic to Ω. The structure Ωis called the
Fra¨ıss´e limit of F. The automorphism group G of Ωacts oligomorphically on Ω. For a finite
structure A in F, we let Ω[A] denote the set of embeddings A →Ω. This is a transitive
Ω-smooth G-set, and every transitive Ω-smooth G-set is of this form [HS1, §6.5]. Given
embeddings A →B and A →A′ in F, the G-orbits on the fiber product
Ω[B] ×Ω[A] Ω[A′]
are naturally in bijection with the amalgamations of A′ and B over A, up to isomorphism
[HS1, Lemma 6.11].
Example 3.3. Let F be the class of finite totally ordered sets. The Fra¨ıss´e limit is the set
Q of rational numbers with its standard order. It is clear that the age of Q is F, and it is not
difficult to verify directly that Q is homogeneous. The group Aut(Q, <) of automorphisms
of the ordered set (Q, <) is an important example of an oligomorphic group. It gives rise to
the Delannoy category; see §6.2.

We say that a closed conjugacy set D ⊂G is T-small if AD is T-small. We now come to
our main result on Z(T).
Theorem 4.24. Suppose that every T-small closed conjugacy set in G contains an isolated
point. Then the natural functor Y →Z(T) is an equivalence.
Proof. First, suppose that C ⊂Gsm is a finitary G-subset, and let D be the closure of C in G.
We have AD = AC, and this c-functor is T-small (Example 4.18). Thus, by our assumption,
D has an isolated point, and it must belong to C. It thus follows from Corollary 4.9 that
the functor Y →Z(T) is fully faithful.
We now prove essential surjectivity by induction on length. Let M be a non-zero object
of Z(T). The support of M is a T-small conjugacy set (Proposition 4.19), and thus (the
corresponding closed conjugacy set) contains an isolated point by assumption. Thus, by
Corollary 4.23, M decomposes as M1 ⊕M2 where M1 is a non-zero Yetter–Drinfeld module.
Since M1 is non-zero, the length of M2 is less than that of M, and so, by induction, M2 is a
Yetter–Drinfeld module. This completes the proof.
□
5. Results related to strong amalgamation
In §4, we introduced the notion of T-small c-functor (Definition 4.17), and saw that if one

5.1. Strong amalgamation. Let (G, Ω) be an oligomorphic permutation group. Given a
finite subset A of Ω, recall that G(A) is the subgroup of G consisting of permutations that fix
each element of A. The definable closure of A, denoted dcl A, is the set ΩG(A) of G(A)-fixed

finite orbit under G(A). Both dcl A and acl A are finite sets stable under G(A), and we have
A ⊂dcl A ⊂acl A.
We say that A is definably closed if A = dcl A, and algebraically closed if A = acl A. For any
A, the set dcl A is definably closed and the set acl A is algebraically closed.
Proposition 5.1. The following are equivalent:
(a) Every finite subset of Ωis definably closed.
(b) Every finite subset of Ωis algebraically closed.
(c) Given finite subsets A, B, and C of Ω, with A∩C = B∩C = ∅, there exists g ∈G(C)
such that gA ∩B = ∅.
Proof. (a) ⇒(b) Let A be a finite subset of Ω. Suppose C = acl A is strictly larger than
A, let x be an element of C not belonging to A, and put B = C \ {x}. Since C is stable

Suppose Ωis the Fra¨ıss´e limit of a Fra¨ıss´e class F, and G = Aut(Ω). The class F is said
to have the strong amalgamation property if the following condition holds: given embeddings

and only on A, i.e., the images of B \ A and C \ A in D are disjoint. One easily sees F has
the strong amalgamation property if and only if G satisfies Proposition 5.1(c). See [Cam2,
§2.7] for additional background on strong amalgamation.
5.2. Level. Let (G, Ω) be an oligomorphic permutation group satisfying the equivalent con-
ditions of Proposition 5.1. Recall that a transitive G-set is Ω-smooth if it appears in Ωn for
some n; we then define the level of X, denoted lev X, to be the minimal such n. We say
that a G-set X is Ω-smooth if each orbit on X is Ω-smooth, and we define the level of X to
be the maximum of the level of orbits, with the convention that lev ∅= −∞.
Proposition 5.2. The level of the orbit of x ∈Ωn is the number of distinct coordinates of
x.
Proof. Without loss of generality, suppose that x1, . . . , xm are distinct and xm+1, . . . , xn each
belong to {x1, . . . , xm}. Let X ⊂Ωn be the orbit of x, let y = (x1, . . . , xm), and let Y ⊂Ωm
be the orbit of y. Then X and Y are isomorphic G-sets, so it suffices to show that Y has
level m. Clearly, lev Y ≤m. Suppose we have an injection of G-sets i: Y →Ωℓwith ℓ≤m.
Let z = (z1, . . . , zℓ) be the image of y. Since i is an isomorphism onto its image, z and y
have the same stabilizer, call it H. By our assumption (∗), we have ΩH = {x1, . . . , xm} and
ΩH = {z1, . . . , zℓ}. Thus ℓ= m, as required.
□
Proposition 5.3. Let X and Y be Ω-smooth G-sets. Then lev(X × Y ) = lev X + lev Y .
Proof. It suffices to treat the case where X and Y are transitive. Let r = lev X and let
s = lev Y . Identify X with the orbit of some x ∈Ωr, and identify y with the orbit of some
y ∈Ωs. Note that x and y have distinct coordinates by Proposition 5.2. Pick g ∈G such
that gy has no coordinate in common with x, which is possible by Proposition 5.1(c). Then
X × Y contains the orbit of (x, gy) ∈Ωr+s, which has level r + s by Proposition 5.2.
□
5.3. Finitary elements. Let (G, Ω) be as in §5.2. We say that a permutation g of Ωis
finitary if it fixes all but finitely many elements of Ω. We then define the support of g to
be the finite set of elements x ∈Ωsuch that gx ̸= x, and we define the rank of g to be
the cardinality of its support. If A is the support of g then gA = A, and so g induces a
permutation of the set A; in fact, if B is any subset of Ωcontaining A then gB = B, and so
g induces a permutation of B fixing each element of B \ A. Note that g has rank 0 if and
only if g is the identity.
Proposition 5.4. An element of G is c-smooth if and only if it is finitary.
Proof. If g is finitary with support A then G(A) centralizes g, and so g is c-smooth. Now
suppose g is c-smooth. Let A be a finite subset of Ωsuch that G(A) ⊂Z(g). If B is any finite
subset of Ωcontaining A then G(B) ⊂Z(g), and so g maps ΩG(B) = B to itself bijectively.
