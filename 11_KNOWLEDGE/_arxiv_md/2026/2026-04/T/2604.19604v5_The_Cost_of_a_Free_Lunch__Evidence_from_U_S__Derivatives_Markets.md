Abstract
Put–call parity is a terminal-payoff identity; quoted residuals against traded futures
are near zero. Yet enforcing parity is path-dependent, exposing arbitrageurs to daily
settlement, margin, and finite capital. Using minute-level NBBO data on S&P 500 and
Russell 2000 options, I extract option-implied discount factors, compare them with the
OIS curve, and construct an annualized carry gap (sample median ≈37 bp, >98%
positive).
A reduced-form specification centered on a volatility×√τ path-risk term
links the carry gap to implementation risk, trading frictions, and financial conditions,
with coefficient signs stable across leave-one-year-out validation. The carry gap is an
implementation wedge invisible in price space but systematic in carry space.

This contrast motivates the paper. Put–call parity contains two distinct propositions.
The first is a terminal-payoff identity. The second is the stronger claim that the strategy
enforcing this identity is economically risk-free in practice. The former is exact by con-
struction. The latter need not follow once daily settlement, variation margin, funding costs,
nonsynchronous execution, illiquidity, and finite capital are taken seriously.
Consider an arbitrageur, Bob, who observes a positive put–call parity residual and enters
the position +C −P −F: long call, short put, and short futures. At maturity, the option-
implied forward and the futures leg offset, so the terminal payoff is deterministic and the
residual is harvested. In an idealized zero-initial-margin benchmark, the position may receive
a full margin offset at inception because its terminal exposure is nearly hedged. Bob can
therefore enter the trade without committing capital at time zero.
The trade is nevertheless not capital-free. If the underlying index rises after inception,
Bob’s short futures leg generates an immediate variation-margin cash outflow, while the
offsetting option gain is not paid into the futures margin account as same-day cash. Bob
must borrow or allocate cash to maintain the position. If the index later falls, the futures
leg generates a cash inflow that can be withdrawn or invested. Thus parity enforcement is
not only a terminal-payoff identity; it is also a pre-maturity cash-management problem. A
parity arbitrage can be free to enter in the zero-initial-margin benchmark, but it is not free
to survive.
This paper studies that survival-capital channel. The relevant burden is not only the
initial margin assigned at inception, but the pathwise cash support required to keep the
position alive under daily variation settlement. Interim cash deficits must be funded, and

## Page 4


interim cash surpluses have an opportunity cost. The cost of maintaining the trade therefore
depends jointly on the scale of interim cash-flow exposure and the price of funding liquidity.
To examine whether this mechanism leaves an empirical footprint, I extract option-
implied discount factors from minute-level NBBO data on S&P 500 and Russell 2000 options,
following the identification approach of Azzone and Baviera (2021). I then construct the carry
gap by comparing these option-implied discount factors with a bootstrapped OIS curve.
The empirical results have three main features. First, the carry gap is centered in positive
territory in both markets, with distributional properties difficult to attribute to microstruc-
ture noise or measurement error alone. Second, it retains pronounced low-frequency structure
and regime-dependent persistence after daily aggregation. Third, these patterns survive out-
of-sample validation, residual-stationarity diagnostics for the baseline level relation, and an
alternative-benchmark analysis using Treasury constant-maturity yields.
To interpret these facts, I introduce a reduced-form path-risk term motivated by geomet-
ric Brownian motion. If parity-enforcement positions require interim cash support before
maturity, the survival-capital requirement should increase with volatility and time to matu-
rity. The Brownian support-capital argument implies a volatility ×√τ scaling. Multiplying
this scale by a rate-like opportunity-cost variable converts survival cash into a carry cost.
The resulting path-risk term is not a price of the terminal payoff, but a reduced-form proxy
for the cost of surviving the pre-maturity variation-margin path.
I place this path-risk term at the center of the regression specification, supplemented by
trading-friction and broad financial-condition variables. The specification has meaningful
in-sample explanatory power, and the signs of all key coefficients remain stable across leave-
one-year-out (LOYO) out-of-sample validation. I interpret this evidence as reduced-form
support for a pathwise funding and survival-capital channel, not as a structural margin
model.
The paper makes three contributions. First, it separates the terminal-payoff identity of
put–call parity from its implementation as a trading strategy, showing that a small quoted
parity residual does not automatically imply risk-free enforcement. Second, it documents
that the carry gap—defined as the annualized difference between option-implied and OIS-
benchmark discount factors—is a systematic empirical object with a positive center, low-
frequency persistence, and state dependence.
Third, it shows that a Brownian support-
capital proxy, together with trading-friction and financial-condition variables, forms a central
explanatory block for this carry-space wedge. The apparent return to parity enforcement is
therefore better understood not as a literal free lunch, but as compensation consistent with
implementation risk, cash-flow timing, and finite capital.

Related Literature
This paper connects to three strands of literature: the theoretical and empirical study of
put–call parity, limits to arbitrage and market frictions, and the extraction of option-implied
discount rates or funding conditions from parity. The common issue across these strands is
that put–call parity is exact as a terminal-payoff relation, but its empirical implementation
depends on trading conditions, funding conditions, and capital constraints. I build most
directly on the implied-discount-factor literature, but reinterpret the resulting deviation as
a dynamic carry-space object rather than as a static pricing discrepancy.
Put–call parity was formally established by Stoll (1969), who noted that transaction costs,
short-sale constraints, and dividend uncertainty could make the relation appear as a no-
arbitrage band rather than a point equality. Subsequent empirical work examined whether
observed deviations represented genuine arbitrage opportunities or residuals generated by
execution costs and market frictions (Gould and Galai, 1974; Klemkosky and Resnick, 1979;
Ackert and Tian, 2001). The common conclusion was that observed deviations should not be
read mechanically as failures of no-arbitrage logic. They may instead reflect the institutional
and trading costs of implementing the arbitrage in actual markets. This literature, however,
focused mainly on whether deviations exist and whether they are economically exploitable,
not on the dynamic structure that parity-implied deviations form over time.
This perspective was generalized in the limits-to-arbitrage literature. Following Shleifer
and Vishny (1997), subsequent work showed that arbitrage is performed by capital-constrained
intermediaries and is exposed to funding constraints, margin requirements, interim losses,
and path-dependent payoffs (Gromb and Vayanos, 2002; Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009;
Mitchell and Pulvino, 2012). In derivatives markets, Ofek et al. (2004) documented that
put–call parity violations are more frequent and larger for stocks with tighter short-sale
4


## Page 6


constraints. This evidence is important because it shows that even textbook no-arbitrage
relations can be affected by limits to arbitrage in real markets.
The setting of this paper is deliberately different. Much of the existing limits-to-arbitrage
evidence studies environments in which visible price-space deviations remain open because
arbitrage is directly impeded by short-sale constraints, funding constraints, or other market
frictions. I instead study European index-option markets in which quoted put–call parity
residuals against traded futures are already tightly compressed. In this setting, especially
for SPX, the option market is highly liquid and the futures leg provides a direct enforcement
instrument, making the standard single-stock short-sale-constraint channel less central. The
question is therefore not why an obvious price-space arbitrage violation remains open. It is
why a systematic wedge remains in carry space after the visible parity residual has largely
been compressed. From this perspective, the carry gap is closer to a shadow cost of successful
parity enforcement than to a conventional unclosed mispricing.
This distinction is central to the contribution of the paper. The limits-to-arbitrage lit-
erature provides the economic motivation: arbitrage uses capital, requires margin, and is
exposed to the path before maturity. But prior work did not separate the near-elimination
of visible price-space residuals from the persistence of a carry-space implementation wedge.
Nor did it aggregate parity-implied deviations into a daily time series and examine their
co-movement with financial-condition variables. This paper therefore pushes the limits-to-
arbitrage logic into a cleaner setting: even when the price residual is nearly closed, the cost
of maintaining the enforcing position can remain visible in carry space.
A separate strand of work uses option prices to infer implied interest rates or funding
conditions. Brenner and Galai (1986) reverse-engineered implied interest rates from option
prices and compared them with actual short-term rates, showing that implied rates can vary
systematically with maturity. This line of research suggests that put–call parity can serve
not only as a test of no-arbitrage, but also as a tool for extracting market-implied discounting
or funding conditions.
The most directly related predecessor is Azzone and Baviera (2021). They estimated
option-implied discount factors from European put–call parity and compared them with the
OIS curve, reporting an average funding spread of approximately 34 bp in S&P 500 options. I
inherit their identification logic and empirical pipeline. Whereas Azzone and Baviera (2021)
focused on the static level measurement of option-implied discount factors, I extend the
analysis to a longer time series and a richer panel. More importantly, I place the time-series
structure of the deviation at the center of the analysis and ask whether it is systematically
related to implementation risk, trading frictions, and financial conditions.
In summary, prior work has treated put–call parity deviations as empirical tests of no-

arbitrage, by-products of market frictions and limited arbitrage, or static estimates of implied
discount rates. This paper instead studies the residual structure that remains after visible
put–call parity deviations have been largely compressed. The contribution is therefore not
to document another unclosed arbitrage violation, but to show that enforcing a clean no-
arbitrage relation can leave a systematic, state-dependent wedge in carry space.

Data and Methodology
3.1
Data and sample scope
I extract market-implied discount factors from SPX and RUT options and compare them
with OIS discount factors to measure the carry gap. The identification follows the synthetic-
forward procedure of Azzone and Baviera (2021), which recovers the market-implied discount
factor from European call and put prices at the same maturity alone.
An earlier implementation that combined the spot index, interest-rate curve, and dividend
information directly proved sensitive to spot–option nonsynchronicity, dividend estimation
error, and moneyness selection. The Azzone and Baviera (2021) procedure avoids these in-
puts by identifying discount factors entirely within the option cross-section, while remaining
computationally light enough for repeated analysis across a long sample and wide maturity
spectrum.
Option quotes are minute-level NBBO data collected from ThetaData. Although option
data are available through December 31, 2025, the analysis sample is restricted to January 4,
2016 through October 31, 2025 to match the availability of OIS data. All results are based
on the common sample in which option-market information and OIS discount curves are
simultaneously observable.
Both SPX and RUT options are European-style index options, so early-exercise premia
do not introduce institutional noise into parity-based discount-factor identification.
All empirical analysis is conducted in MATLAB R2025b.1

3.4
Sample filters and final panel construction
The preprocessing removes observations with low liquidity or unstable price information to
ensure stable cross-sectional identification. Only call–put pairs sharing the same strike and
maturity are used. I exclude observations with abnormally low prices or excessive bid–ask
spreads, maturities with too few valid strikes, and dates on which OIS curve recovery fails
or the term structure is anomalous. The final sample consists of observations for which
(i) the option-implied discount factor can be identified and (ii) the OIS discount factor can
be reliably constructed at the same date and maturity.
I construct a date×maturity panel for each of SPX and RUT. Daily time series are
aggregated as the median of eligible observations on each date, reducing sensitivity to outliers
and transient noise while tracking the central movement of the carry gap.

Path-Risk Term and Carry-Gap Regression Specifi-
cation
This section introduces the empirical specification for the carry gap. The key regressor is a
GBM-based path-risk term, designed to capture the pre-maturity implementation burden of
11


## Page 13


parity enforcement rather than the terminal payoff itself. Put–call parity is a terminal-payoff
identity, but the strategy enforcing it must be carried through daily settlement, variation
margin, interim losses, and finite-capital constraints. The resulting enforcement problem is
therefore path-dependent, even when the terminal payoff is deterministic.
This distinction reconciles two facts: quoted parity residuals are tightly compressed in
price space, yet a systematic wedge persists in carry space. If arbitrage compresses visible
residuals but leaves implementation risk inside the enforcement process, the remaining wedge
should be related to state variables governing pathwise funding and capital support. To my
knowledge, prior work has not placed an explicit functional form for parity enforcement path
risk at the center of a carry-gap regression.

The trading-friction term remains positive and statistically significant in all specifications.

even after accounting for serial correlation in daily residuals.

The pooled estimates are close to the market-specific estimates for the GBM block and

in SPX. The separate regressions therefore do not overturn the common structure; they show
that sensitivities to the long-run path-risk and trading-friction channels vary across markets.

Discussion
This section discusses the implications of the empirical results.
9.1
Empirical meaning of the GBM path-risk term
In the main OIS-based specification, the central empirical result is that GBM OIS,1Y and
GBM OIS,10Y are repeatedly significant with opposite signs. The carry gap is therefore not
a simple discount-rate level effect or a measurement residual, but is organized in a way
consistent with the path-risk structure of parity enforcement.
The opposite signs can be interpreted through the term structure of arbitrage capital.
The negative coefficient on GBM OIS,1Y is consistent with asymmetric transmission from
the short end of the discount curve. When short-term rates rise, the OIS discount factor
falls immediately, while the option-implied discount factor identified from the option cross-
section may adjust more slowly. The carry gap, defined as log(DOIS/ ˆB), is then mechanically
compressed, with the effect scaled by volatility×√τ.
The positive coefficient on GBM OIS,10Y is consistent with a long-run capital opportunity-
cost channel. A higher long-horizon risk-free yield raises the opportunity cost of allocating
scarce capital to parity enforcement. Following the capital-allocation logic of Shleifer and
Vishny (1997) and Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009), improved outside opportunities can
reduce capital supplied to parity enforcement and widen the equilibrium carry gap.
I do not structurally separate these channels. The point is instead that the sign pattern is
stable across in-sample, LOYO, and robustness exercises. Residual-stationarity diagnostics
reinforce the same conclusion: the fitted and observed carry-gap series do not leave a nonsta-
tionary residual under the main OIS-based specification. Thus, the evidence supports a level
relation organized by the GBM path-risk structure, while leaving room for regime-dependent
calibration errors.
To be clear, “systematic” does not mean precisely forecastable. Mean LOYO R2 is modest
and fit varies across regimes. The systematicity emphasized here is instead the directionality,
repeatability, and residual-stationary level alignment of the economic relations that organize
the carry gap.
9.2
Common structure and cross-market heterogeneity
SPX and RUT are neither fully distinct nor fully identical. Both markets exhibit a positive
carry-gap center, low-frequency persistence, and links to the GBM path-risk term, trading
frictions, and financial conditions. The pooled specification is not materially inferior to the
26


## Page 28


separate regressions, indicating a common structure across the two markets.
Heterogeneity appears mainly in GBM 10Y and BAmed/τ. The long-horizon GBM term
loads more strongly in RUT, while the bid–ask term loads more strongly in SPX. By contrast,
GBM 1Y and NFCI are more stable across markets.
The evidence therefore points to a
common path-risk structure with market-specific channel intensities layered on top.
9.3
Benchmark choice: OIS versus DGS
The core sign structure survives under DGS: GBM 1Y remains negative, GBM 10Y remains
positive, BAmed/τ remains positive, and NFCI remains negative. This shows that the path-
risk structure is not mechanically produced by the OIS curve alone.
However, OIS and DGS are not interchangeable. OIS delivers stronger in-sample fit, more
stable long-horizon GBM inference, and better performance through rate-regime transitions.
DGS preserves the directional structure but weakens the quantitative fit, especially around
the 2022 rate-hiking episode. The DGS results are therefore best read as a robustness check:
they confirm that the reduced-form structure is not benchmark-specific, while supporting
OIS as the empirically more stable baseline.
9.4
Economic meaning of the carry gap
Put–call parity is exact as a terminal-payoff identity, and quoted price-space residuals are
small. But enforcing parity is a trading strategy that must survive the pre-maturity path.
Daily settlement, variation margin, capital commitment, execution risk, and nonsynchronous
cash flows can make the enforcement strategy capital-using even when the terminal payoff
is deterministic.
The carry gap makes this implementation layer visible. A small quoted residual does
not imply that parity enforcement is economically costless. The positive return to parity
enforcement is therefore better interpreted as compensation consistent with implementation
risk, cash-flow timing, and finite capital, rather than as a literal free lunch.
9.5
A residual short-end component
Explanatory power is weakest at the 1–2 month horizon. This does not negate the GBM
path-risk term, but it suggests that the current specification does not exhaust the short
end of the carry gap. In pilot tests, an ad hoc short-maturity amplification of the GBM
term produced only limited improvement, suggesting that the issue may reflect a distinct
short-end component rather than a simple shape misspecification.
27


## Page 29


The short-maturity residual may reflect microstructure effects, margin timing, execu-
tion frictions, or other mechanisms that operate most strongly near expiration.
Beyond
documenting the central structure of the carry gap, the results therefore point to a short-
end-specific component for future work.
9.6
Limitations and future work
This study is reduced-form. The coefficients capture conditional associations, not structural
causal effects.
A structural model in which implementation risk and capital constraints
endogenously generate an equilibrium parity wedge remains a natural next step.
The residual-stationarity evidence should also be interpreted narrowly. It mitigates the
direct spurious-regression concern in the main OIS-based level relation, but it does not
prove that a time-invariant cointegrating vector governs all regimes. A richer treatment of
structural breaks, regime-dependent equilibrium errors, and time-varying long-run relations
is left for future work.
Benchmark choice remains an economic question. DGS confirms that the sign structure
survives an alternative curve, but also shows that quantitative fit and long-horizon inference
vary with the benchmark. Future work could examine reference curves that more directly
reflect the funding costs or opportunity costs faced by actual parity enforcers.
The sample is also limited to two U.S. equity-index option markets.
Extending the
analysis to European or Asian index options, single-name options, or other underlying assets
would clarify how general the observed structure is.
Finally, out-of-sample fit varies across regimes even though coefficient signs remain stable.
More granular data on execution, margin, dealer balance sheets, and market-maker capital
constraints could help identify the sources of this regime dependence and the residual short-
end component.

Conclusion
This paper separates two propositions about put–call parity: the terminal-payoff identity
itself and the stronger claim that enforcing it in practice is economically risk-free.
The
former is exact. The latter does not follow automatically once daily settlement, variation
margin, capital commitment, and liquidation risk under finite capital are taken into account.
The empirical evidence from U.S. equity-index option markets shows that this distinc-
tion appears not in price space, but in carry space. Quoted parity residuals are compressed
near zero, yet the annualized gap between option-implied and benchmark discount struc-
28


## Page 30


tures reveals a systematic carry gap with a positive center, low-frequency persistence, and
state-variable dependence. The GBM path-risk term introduced in this paper serves as the
central explanatory block: its short-to-medium-term and long-term rate-scaled components
are repeatedly significant, with opposite signs and different magnitudes.
The main results are robust along several dimensions. The signs of all key coefficients
are stable under LOYO out-of-sample validation. Residual-stationarity diagnostics mitigate
the concern that the level relation is driven by unrelated persistent trends. The core sign
structure also survives replacement of the OIS benchmark with Treasury constant-maturity
yields.
The carry gap therefore provides evidence that the economic burden of parity enforce-
ment can persist even when visible parity residuals have nearly vanished. As long as parity
enforcement remains a path-dependent, capital-using activity, markets may compress price-
space arbitrage residuals without fully eliminating the wedge in carry space. The GBM
path-risk term offers a starting point for interpreting that wedge as compensation consistent
with implementation risk, cash-flow timing, and finite capital.
