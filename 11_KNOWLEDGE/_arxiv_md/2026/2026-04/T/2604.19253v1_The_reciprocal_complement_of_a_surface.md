THE RECIPROCAL COMPLEMENT OF A SURFACE
DARIO SPIRITO
Abstract. We study the reciprocal complement R(D) of a two-
dimensional finitely generated K-algebra D by linking it with the
properties of a surface with coordinate ring D. We give several
sufficient criteria to have dim R(D) = 2, and we use them to show
several explicit examples; in particular, we determine the dimen-
sion of R(D) when D is the quotient of K[X, Y, Z] by an irreducible
polynomial of degree 2. We also study the integral closure of the
localizations of R(K[X, Y ]).
1. Introduction
Given an integral domain D, the reciprocal complement of D is the
domain R(D) generated by the inverses of the nonzero elements of D.
Then, R(D) is always an integral domain with the same quotient field
as D; yet, the properties of R(D) are often very distant from those
of D. For example, when D = K[X, Y ] is the polynomial ring in two
indeterminates over a field K, the reciprocal complement R(D) is a
two-dimensional local domain that is neither Noetherian nor integrally
closed, and where every non-unit is contained in all but finitely many
prime ideals [6]. Yet, the structure of R(D) is very linked to D: for ex-
ample, if D is a finitely generated K-algebra then dim R(D) ≤dim(D)
[7, Theorem 3.2]. The construction R(D) was introduced in [4] during
the study of Egyptian domains, i.e., domains D where every d ∈D
can be written as a sum of reciprocals of elements of D; a domain is
Egyptian if and only if R(D) is the quotient field of D. The study of
R(D) is often very hard; see [6, 7, 3, 5] for some results about this
construction.
In an earlier paper [11], a geometrical approach to R(D) was pursued
when D is a one-dimensional K-algebra and K is an algebraically closed
field, by connecting D with the affine curves whose coordinate ring is
isomorphic to D. The main result was a complete characterization of
the dimension of R(D): if X is a curve with K[X] ≃D and the points
at infinity are regular for the projective closure X, then dim R(D) = 1

In the present paper, we expand the study of [11] from curves to sur-

the possible dimension of R(D), we show several sufficient conditions
for the dimension of R(D) to be the maximal possible, i.e., 2. Moreover,
we do not require the base field K to be algebraically closed, as was
done in [11]; indeed, we shall see that the fact that K is algebraically
closed or not influences the dimension of closely-related domains, and
sometimes the non-closure of K helps in studying the dimension of
R(D) (see Example 3.8 for the former phenomenon and Propositions
6.1 and 6.8 for the latter).
The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section 3 we generalize
the results of [11] by removing the condition that the base field K is al-
gebraically closed; the proof is mostly a repetition of the algebraically
closed case, using a more refined look at the geometric results used.
Section 4 contains some general results on the reciprocal complement
of finitely generated K-algebras. Section 5 presents the main construc-
tion of the paper: we study localizations R(D)[f] at a regular function
f by studying the zero set of f, allowing us to reduce the dimension
of the varieties involved and (for surfaces) to apply the results proved
for curves. This method requires passing from varieties defined over
K to varieties defined over the rational function field K(t). In Section
6 we show how this method can be applied to various classes of va-
rieties; in particular, we completely solve the dimension problem for
quadrics in A3, i.e., find the dimension of R(K[X, Y, Z]/(f)) when f
is a polynomial of degree 2 (Example 6.2). In the final Section 7, we
study in greater detail the localizations of the reciprocal complement
of the polynomial ring K[X, Y ], with particular emphasis on the in-
tegral closure of the localizations: when K is an infinite field, we give
a geometric proof of the fact that R(K[X, Y ]) is not integrally closed
(as opposed to the more computation-based proof given in [6, Theorem
5.8]).

3. Curves
In this section, we generalize the results of [11] from algebraically
closed fields to arbitrary fields.
The following result, which act as a bridge between the algebraic and
the geometric world, is well-known (for the algebraically closed case,
see e.g. [9, Chapter 1, §6]).
Proposition 3.1. Let K be a field. Let X be a curve on K and let X
be its projective closure. Then, there is a bijection between the points
of the normalization of X and the Zariski space Zar(Q(K[X])|K).
Proof. There is an equivalence of categories between normal curves
and finitely generated field extensions of transcendence degree 1 over
K (see e.g. [12, 0BY1]). Let Y be the curve corresponding to Q(K[X]);
then, Y is the normalization of X. If P is a point of Y, then OY,P is
a DVR, and different points correspond to different valuation rings.

Q(K[X]) = Q(K[a1, . . . , an]); then, the center of V on the integral clo-
sure of K[a1, . . . , an] corresponds to a point of Y. The claim is proved.

5. Reducing the dimension
Let X be a surface and f ∈K[X]. We can associate to f two subsets
of X: the closed set VX(f) := {x ∈X | f(x) = 0} and its complement
DX(f) := X \ VX(f), which is an open set. We always have
K[DX(f)] = K[X][1/f].
By [7, Corollary 2.6], the previous equality can be transported to re-
ciprocal complements as
R(K[DX(f)]) = R(K[X][1/f]) = R(K[X])[f].
