Hyperbolic phonon polaritons (HPPs) are coupled oscillations of anisotropic lattice 
vibrations and electromagnetic fields that confine the latter to the nanoscale, enabling novel 
nano-polaritonic devices. While HPPs have been identified in multiple layered materials, 
achieving advanced control and manipulation - particularly polariton canalization for 
unidirectional energy flow - often necessitates complex device fabrications or crystal 
modifications. Here we visualize and elucidate the properties of in-plane hyperbolicity in a-
V2O5, a layered compound with a highly anisotropic permittivity tensor. We show 
unidirectional Poynting-vector propagation of polaritons in a-V2O5 without additional 
treatments. Combined with theoretical modeling, our infrared nano-imaging studies unveil 
a novel form of polariton canalization, with its dispersion contour continuously tunable by 
the incident light frequency. Additionally, we provide a theoretically calculated permittivity 
phase diagram for tailoring polaritonic wavefronts. These findings suggest that the metal-
oxide a-V2O5 holds great promise for on-demand light canalization and control at the 
nanoscale.

I. 
INTRODUCTION 
Layered two-dimensional (2D) van der Waals (vdW) crystals, with their intrinsic anisotropic 
dielectric permittivity 𝜖(𝜔), have emerged as an excellent platform for tailoring photons at the 
nanoscale1–4. Prominent examples include the coupling of light with atomic lattice vibrations 
within their Reststrahlen bands (RB)5,6, inducing phonon polaritons in 2D polar insulators, e.g., 
hexagonal boron nitride (hBN)7–9, a-MoO310–14, a-V2O515,16, Ga2O317,18, CdWO419, and Calcite20. 
These celebrated achievements have also spurred the exploration of advanced control of surface 
polaritons in vdW heterostructures21. Notable examples are the observation of polariton 
canalization in twisted a-MoO3 with inherent topological robustness13,14,22–25 and in LiV2O5 with 
negative real part of the 𝜖(𝜔) along the out-of-plane by modifying the parent compound via Li-
intercalations26.  
Despite these advances, the aforementioned vdW material platforms still face challenges for 
nanotechnological applications, particularly in polariton canalization. Key issues include high 
optical losses in systems like hBN, the inherently complex fabrication of twisted layer stacks, and 
potential structural degradation during lithium intercalation in materials such as LiV2O5. A 
promising approach to overcome these limitations would be the discovery of polaritons that enable 
canalization in pristine, individual vdW layers27,28. 
In this work, we show that a-V2O5 represents an intriguing type of natural biaxial hyperbolic 
metal oxide that exhibits in-plane hyperbolic dispersion in the middle-infrared (mid-IR) 
frequencies. By utilizing scanning nano-IR imaging techniques29–31, we examined the phonon 
polariton modes that are launched, guided, and tuned in two different RB frequencies15. We 
unambiguously demonstrated that thin a-V2O5 flakes host canalized polariton propagation in the 
RB2 region without any additional crystal modifications or complex device fabrication. One of the

merits of our work lies in demonstrating in-plane canalized hyperbolic phonon polaritons in natural 
vdW crystal a-V2O5, which has not been experimentally observed before. Specifically, we find 
that these highly directional polariton canalization dispersion contours are continuously tunable 
by the incident light frequency, consistent with the positive group velocity in the RB2 regions of 
a-V2O5. In addition, we provide the dielectric permittivity phase diagram on achieving the desired 
polariton wavefronts, from elliptic to hyperbolic, and canalizations by design. The demonstrated 
ability to tune polariton canalization wavefront geometries, along with the quantitative matching 
with the electromagnetic modeling, suggests that natural metal-oxide a-V2O5 holds promise for 
producing tunable mid-IR polaritonic devices for a broad range of nanophotonic functionalities. 
 
 
II. 
EXPERIMENTAL METHODS 
Single crystals of a-V2O5 were grown by the floating-zone technique. The feed and seed rods 
for the crystal growth were prepared by solid-state reactions. a-V2O5 powder was pressed into 6-
mm-diameter 60-mm rods under 400-atm hydrostatic pressure and then calcined in air at 600 ◦C 
for 12 hours. The crystal growth was carried out in argon in an IR-heated image furnace equipped 
with two halogen lamps and double ellipsoidal mirrors with feed and seed rods rotating in opposite 
directions at 25 rpm during crystal growth at a rate of 1.0 mm/h. We then mechanically exfoliated 
the as-grown bulk crystals onto Si/SiO2 substrate and pre-inspected the quality of the a-V2O5 flakes 
via optical microscopy and atomic force microscope (AFM) inside a purged nitrogen environment. 
The AFM topography image of the a-V2O5 flakes is shown in the Supplementary Information (SI). 
For patterning Au antenna devices on top of the a-V2O5 flakes, Au/Cr (50 nm/3 nm) disks were 
defined using standard e-beam lithography techniques on SiO2/Si substrates.

Exfoliated thin a-V2O5 flakes with their orthorhombic crystal structure show a typical 
rectangular geometry, in which layers formed by asymmetric V-O bonds along the three different 
crystalline axes are responsible for the different directional vibrations of the a-V2O5 flake15. Such 
anisotropic effects are intimately related to its strong in-plane anisotropic optical responses (see 
SI, section 3, SFig. 3). The distinct dielectric permittivity 𝜖(𝜔) (𝜖!(𝜔), 𝜖"(𝜔), 𝜖#(𝜔)) of a-V2O5 
along the x, y and z three crystalline axes, as determined from previous far-field measurements, 
indicates the existence of three RBs and the corresponding phonon polaritons15. In our studies, we 
focus on two of these RBs, namely the frequency regime RB1 (w ~ 980 – 1030 cm-1), where 𝜖$ <
0, 𝜖% > 0 & 𝜖& > 0, and the frequency regime RB2 (w ~ 760 – 950 cm-1), where 𝜖% < 0, 𝜖& > 0 
& 𝜖$ > 0, respectively. 
The infrared nano-imaging experiments were performed using scattering-type scanning near-
field optical microscopy (s-SNOM) equipped with continuous wave mid-IR quantum cascade 
lasers. The s-SNOM is based on an atomic force microscope (AFM) equipped with a sharp metallic 
tip7,8,31. This setup resolves the problem of photon-phonon momentum mismatch11,19,20,22,31, 
enabling the launch of phonon polariton waves (𝜆' ) with wavelength 𝜆' ≪𝜆(). A pseudo-
heterodyne interferometric detection module was implemented to extract both the scattering 
amplitude s and the phase j of the near-field signal. In the current work, we discuss the amplitude 
of the signal. In order to subtract the background signal, we demodulated the near-field signal at 
the third harmonic of the tapping frequency.  
Specifically, there are two types of phonon polariton waves shown in our studies: one parallel 
to the physical edges of the a-V2O5, and the other near patterned micron-sized Au disks in the 
interior of the a-V2O5. Near the physical edge, it reveals periodic bright and dark fringes within 
the flake that originate from the interference of polaritons launched by the AFM tip, propagating


## Page 5


5 
within the flake and reflected off the edge8,30. Such a physical sample edge has been routinely 
chosen as the polariton wave reflector and launcher, resulting in the imaging of co-existing fringes 
with two distinct periodicities lp and lp/230. In the meantime, we have also examined fringe 
patterns near the patterned micron-sized Au disk structures residing on top of the a-V2O5. These 
Au disks serve as fixed polariton antennas that predominantly produce fringes with lp 
periodicity29,30. We note that by virtue of examining both the edge-reflected/launched and Au-
launched polaritons within the same field-of-view at two different RBs, we can firmly compare 
and highlight some of the key discoveries detailed below.  
The finite-difference time-domain method was used in our full-wave simulations (Lumerical 
FDTD). The boundary condition for our simulations is a perfectly matched layer absorbing the 
outgoing waves to avoid any impact on our simulation results. We monitor the real part of Ez at 20 
nm on top of the sample surface, which is then used as the input for the fast Fourier transform to 
extract the iso-frequency dispersion contours. For the simulation result in Fig. 1, the sample is 
placed in free space. The simulation of the field distribution of the sample shown in Fig. 5 is 
provided in the Supplementary Information (SI, Section 4).  
 
III. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 
Representative nano-IR imaging data are depicted in Fig. 1a-b, where we show the near-field 
amplitude, s(w), and phase, j(w), images collected from a 200 nm-thick a-V2O5 flake taken at 
frequencies belonging to RB1(w = 1020 cm-1) and RB2 (w = 875 cm-1 ), respectively. For w = 1020 
cm-1 (RB1), we observe bright and dark polariton patterns propagating radially outward of the Au 
disk along all in-plane directions, forming circular (or omnidirectional) polariton fringe patterns 
with elliptic wavefronts (Fig. 1a). The smallest wavelength is along the [100] (a-axis) while the


## Page 6


6 
largest wavelength is along [001] (b-axis). These elliptic polaritons are reminiscent of earlier 
observations in other polar insulators3,10,11,32. By taking the fast Fourier transformation (FFT) of 
the experimental imaging (Fig. 1c), we obtain the iso-frequency contour (IFC) with a slightly 
distorted circular geometry of radius of q ~ 2 µm-1, which highlights the elliptic nature of the 
strongly confined phonon polaritons with slightly different polariton wavevector within the RB1 
region. 
In sharp contrast to the elliptic polaritonic wavefront discussed above, for the exact same 
sample but at w = 875 cm-1 (RB2), we see the bright and dark periodic polariton waves becoming 
highly directional and only observable along the [100] direction, as shown in Fig. 1b (see SI, 
section 2, SFig. 2). Such a unidirectional Poynting-vector polariton wavefront exhibits a 
hyperbolic shape, in contrast to the typical hyperbolic wavefront10,11,19, as observed in both the 
near-field amplitude (s3) and phase (f3) images. This highly confined unidirectional wavefront is 
consistently reproducible, with the same polariton propagation direction within the a-V2O5 crystal 
(see SI, Section 1, SFig. 1). We wish to highlight that our canalized polariton in a-V2O5 that 
remains only along [100] is fundamentally different from the canalized polariton along [001] in 
Li-intercalated LiV2O526, as detailed in the following discussions.

The measured highly directional polariton wavefront is further confirmed by the FFT analysis. 
Based on the IFC plot from the imaging obtained at 875 cm-1 (Fig. 1d), we see the emergence of 
multiple parallel IFCs separated by a distance of q = 4 µm-1 (Fig. 1d). This strongly indicates the 
dispersion flattening of the IFC, and as a result, leads to a highly collimated and nearly 
diffractionless polariton propagation. This peculiar polariton wave geometry, reminiscent of the 
hyperbolic wavefront observed in twisted a-MoO312,13,22–25,28 and LiV2O526, constitutes a clear 
signature for polariton canalization without any engineering of superlattices, complex intercalation 
schemes, or laborious fabrication processes.  
To further verify the observed experimental phenomenon of polariton canalization in the RB2 
regime of natural a-V2O5 flakes, we conducted numerical simulations (see Methods and SI) of 
phonon polariton propagation on a 200 nm thick flake of a-V2O5. We performed full-wave 
simulation by choosing the incident light frequency of 875 cm-1 within RB2, and 1020 cm-1 in the 
case of RB1 for a better comparison of the real part of the z-component of the electric field Re(Ez). 
In the elliptic case, the experimentally determined dielectric permittivity tensor used as the input 
parameter is: 𝜖!(𝜔) = 1.96 + 0.48𝑖, 𝜖"(𝜔) = 2.6 + 0.1𝑖 and 𝜖#(𝜔) = −1.58 + 0.15𝑖. By 
plotting the calculated Re(Ez) along the out-of-plane axis (Fig. 1e), we show polariton propagation 
occurs in all in-plane directions, with the max/min wavelength ratio matching with the 
experimental results at w = 1020 cm-1 (Fig. 1e). By maintaining all the simulation conditions except 
replacing the dielectric permittivity tensor with that obtained at 875 cm-1, we observed polariton


## Page 9

canalization propagation along [100], also in good agreement with experimental results (Fig. 1f). 
In the case of polariton canalization, the experimentally determined dielectric permittivity tensor 
used as the input parameter is: 𝜖!(𝜔) = −2.96 + 1.25𝑖, while 𝜖"(𝜔) = 0.48 + 0.11𝑖 and 
𝜖#(𝜔) = 7.92 + 0.007𝑖. The diagonal components of the permittivity tensor along the a-axis are 
more than 10 times larger than along the b-axis.  We attribute such highly anisotropic in-plane 
permittivity as the cause for the observed unidirectional Poynting-vector polariton canalization 
effects, as detailed below. In addition, we note that another sanity check based on the full width at 
half maximum analysis analogous to that reported by ref. 13 has also been performed, and the 
corresponding results and methodological details are provided in the Supplementary Information 
(SI, Section 8), further supporting the robustness of the observed canalization behavior. 
We now proceed with a systematic frequency-dependent study for both RB1 and RB2 (Fig. 2). 
By varying the incident light frequency within RB1 and RB2, we see that the polaritonic wave 
pattern changes accordingly. For the RB1, these observations are consistent with the notion of 
phonon polaritons propagating across the a-V2O5 flake with an elliptical in-plane permittivity of 
𝜖$(𝜔) < 0, 𝜖%&(𝜔) > 0 . However, the detected unidirectional Poynting-vector polariton 
wavefront persists across the frequency regimes within RB2, being strongest at w ~ 860 cm-1 and 
gradually diminishing to the frequency of w ~ 900 cm-1. Such strongly directional propagation is 
clearly observed in all the near-field amplitude s3(w) and phase f3(w) imaging in the frequency 
range of 860 - 905 cm-1, thus further validating both our claim for an Au-disk triggered polariton 
canalized excitation in a-V2O5 crystals via our imaging technique.

IV. DISCUSSION  
We now discuss the possible mechanisms lead to polariton canalization in a-V2O5. One 
potential explanation lies in the interplay between its highly anisotropic dielectric permittivity and 
polaritonic dissipation. The negative permittivity along the a-axis dictates both the polariton’s 
propagation direction and the exact shape of their wavefronts. To investigate the role of ϵ!(ω), we 
performed full-wave simulations to analyze polariton wavefronts and construct a possible 
polaritonic phase diagram, as depicted in Fig. 5a. Our results show that varying both Re(ϵ!(ω)) 
and Im(ϵ!(ω)), while keeping the other permittivity components fixed (ϵ"(ω) = ϵ#(ω) =  1 +
0.0001𝑖), induces transitions among hyperbolic (Fig. 5b), canalized (Fig. 5c), and circular (elliptic) 
wavefronts (Fig. 5d), governed by the ratio Re(ϵ!(ω))/Im(ϵ!(ω)). Increased dissipation along 
the b-axis further enhances canalization by suppressing minor polariton tails (the end part of the 
hyperbola where the intensity of the waves is minimum), as shown in Fig. 5e (see SI, Section 4. 
SFig. 4-9). While this effect does not signify a fundamental change in wavefront topology, it 
demonstrates that polariton wavefronts can be tuned at fixed IR frequencies through in-plane 
permittivity engineering. In LiV2O5, canalization is driven by an epsilon-near-zero response and a 
high damping asymmetry along the x-axis: the loss is very large perpendicular to propagation 
(Im(εx) = 5) but almost negligible along the propagation direction along the y-axis (Im(εy) = 0.1).


## Page 15


15 
This sharp imbalance, together with the near-zero permittivity along the y-axis enforces 
canalization along the [001] y-axis direction, which leads to a completely different structure 
compared to the a-V2O5. Although continuous tunability of the dielectric tensor is impractical, 
recent studies22 have shown that Li intercalation can fine-tune the polaritonic wavefronts by 
modifying the permittivity of Li- a-V2O5, indicating the potential of dielectric engineering as a 
viable approach for controlling polaritonic wavefronts in naturally anisotropic materials.

V. CONCLUSION


## Page 16


16 
      Finally, the observation of tunable, low-loss phonon polariton canalization in the 
technologically important mid-IR frequency range is facilitated by the unique combination of a 
strongly anisotropic permittivity tensor in a-V2O5. The observed polariton canalization in a-V2O5 
features strong confinement (> 𝜆() /30), making it highly suitable for future nano-photonic 
applications, ranging from ultra-compact optical circuits to efficient light-harvesting devices29,30. 
Furthermore, as depicted in the phase diagram shown in Fig. 5a, our results indicate that tunable 
polariton canalization and transformation, as an intermediate phase from elliptic to hyperbolic, can 
be realized by selecting materials with appropriate in-plane dielectric permittivity tensors. Future 
studies may explore approaches including interfacial engineering and electrical gating to further 
modulate polaritonic properties15,31-34. Our work highlights that conventional layered bulk 
anisotropic crystals provide an excellent platform for exploring new types of polariton character, 
benefiting from their low-loss and strongly directional nature.
