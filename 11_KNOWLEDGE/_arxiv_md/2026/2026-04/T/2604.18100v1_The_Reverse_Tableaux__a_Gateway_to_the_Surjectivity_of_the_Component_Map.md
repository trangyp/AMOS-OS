Abstract.
Let G be a simple algebraic group over the complex field C, B a fixed Borel subgroup, P
a parabolic subgroup containing B, P ′ its derived group acting on the Lie algebra m of its
nilradical.
The nilfibre N (for this action) is the zero locus of the augmentation I+ of the semi-
invariant algebra I := C[m]P ′. Via Richardson’s theorem, I is polynomial.
Assume G of type A.
Then the generators of I may be taken to be the Benlolo-Sanderson invariants [2].
Following this, in Y. Fittouhi and A. Joseph, The Magic and Mystery of Component
Tableaux, Indag Math., online January 2026, a set {T C} of component tableaux was con-
structed each encoding explicit combinatorial data C. Each tableau {T C} defines a compo-
nent C of N and the resulting map {T C} 7→C was shown to be injective.
Here this data is more simply encoded in a multi-set, called the Red Set.
In the present work a set {Rψ(C)} of
“Reverse Tableaux” is constructed through an
Enabling Proposition. They are shown to define the same components as the component
tableaux and furthermore give a factorisation of each new invariant in a chosen sequence,
via successive linearisation of preceding invariants. Via Krull’s theorem this factorisation is
to give the required surjectivity.
There can be several reverse tableaux for a given Red Set, yet each determine the same
variety as the component tableaux with the given Red Set and define the same components.
The flexibility of having several “equivalent” reverse tableaux, absent from the more “rigid”
component tableaux, is quite essential for factorisation.
This procedure is quite different from the classical approach to surjectivity requiring a
geometric description of the nilcone, which in the present case seems unattainable.

1. Introduction
1.1. Avant-propos. To paraphrase Leonardo da Ponte, a component is like the Phoenix,
everyone swears it exists but nobody has ever seen one.
Indeed components of a given algebraic variety, exist via the Lasker-Noether theorem [1,
Thm.
4.5] but in reality are notoriously difficult to find.
Their search is a far-reaching
generalisation of finding the prime factors of a positive integer, an age-old problem now
viewed of added importance for its role in breaking public key encryption.
A further important related development occurs in the context of certain non-commutative
algebras, notably enveloping algebras and their quantum analogues. In this vast amounts of
work has been published on prime (and primitive) ideals and the decompositions to which
they lead. This is partly because of their importance in Representation Theory and partly
because they are fewer so more amenable to classification. In this P. Gabriel et al [3] and J.
Dixmier [4], made notable early contributions and expositions.
Here we consider the action of a parabolic subgroup P on the Lie algebra m of its nilradical.
The relevant invariant ring is I := C[m]P ′, with P ′ being the derived group of P.
Via
Richardson’s theorem it is polynomial [5, 2.2.3].
We extend our study [9] in type A of the components of the “nilfibre” N , defined to be
the zero locus in m of the augmentation I+.
In type A, Benlolo and Sanderson [2] suggested a choice of polynomial generators for I.
This was verified in [12]. We call them the Benlolo-Sanderson (BS) invariants.
In [9] we constructed a map, called the component map, from a set of explicit combinatorial
data to the set of components of N . It was shown to be injective [9, Sect. 7].
We propose to eventually show that the component map is also surjective. In the resulting
endeavour, we simplified the said combinatorial data as a multi-set, called simply the “Red
Set”, which assuming surjectivity gives a more transparent classification of components of
N in type A.
To have any chance of success of finding all components of a variety, conventional wisdom
would say that one needs a geometric understanding of that variety and not just its de-
scription as the zero locus of an algebraic set. Ideally the variety should admit an algebraic
group action so that the components are the closures of its orbits. This is the case of the
Springer fibre for which such a description results from the Steinberg triple variety. Thereby
its components were determined [15],[16] with relative ease.

For N we have so far no convenient geometric description as in the case of the Springer
fibre, and little hope of one. Even in type A, the nilfibre can be far from irreducible, unlike
the case, studied in the classical work of Kostant [13], when P is the entire simple algebraic
group acting on its Lie algebra. Again for the Springer fibre, the components were, under a
simple transformation, Lagrangian subvarieties of associated G orbits, whilst in the present
case, one component of the same nilfibre might be Lagrangian whilst another need not be,
yet both have the same dimension [9, 8.2].
Thus it is hardly surprising that no-one has even dared to determine the components of
N before. Such an endeavour could have been initiated even one hundred years ago.
Yet as we saw the problem simplifies through Richardson’s theorem [5, Sect.2], going back
“only” fifty years.
Our present work might well be read in another fifty years time for those wishing to
extend the present results to general type, which we anticipate will be a huge challenge and
a fascinating endeavour.
Already the determination of the invariant generators in classical type constituted a re-
markable PhD thesis of Elena Perelman [14], which has not been reached let alone bettered
over the last twenty-three years. Besides generators are barely understood particularly for
general type [5, 3.3].
This work is a continuation of [9], having itself origins in [5], [6], [7]. These earlier projects
were to describe Weierstrass sections for the action of a parabolic subgroup of SL(n) on the
Lie algebra of its nilradical and in which a “canonical component” tableau was introduced
[8]. Their main interest to the present reader lies in the fact that they motivated the present
“totally out of blue construction” described in [9] and significantly deepened here, through
the reverse tableaux, which were an inspired invention by the first named author.

2.8. Nachbarschaftsanschauung. With hindsight and tongue-in-cheek we can formulate
our “neighbourhood philosophy”.
It is that the components of N are obtained from T to give “component tableaux” T C
by repeating numerical entries which first appear in T on the right and descending by rows
specified by surrounding pairs of neighbouring columns.
Logical - as the generators of I are given through neighbouring columns. Yet it is not so

Again reverse tableaux are also constructed through neighbouring columns but by a dif-
ferent procedure.
3. Component Tableaux
3.1. Line Decoration. A component tableau T C is defined by combinatorial data C given
in [9, 3.2]. It is viewed as the tableau T decorated by lines ℓi,j, joining entries i, j in boxes
of distinct columns and going from left to right.
The lines ℓi,j may be labelled with a 1, a ∗or not at all. The subset with label 1 (resp. ∗)
is denoted by S(T C) (resp. Y (T C)).
Then evaluation of any Benlolo-Sanderson invariant through this labelling replaces it by
one of the co-ordinates with label ∗, in other words it gives a Weierstrass section [9, Thm.
5.2.6] for the component defined by the component tableau T C.
Such a labelling was found in [5] and was a remarkable achievement of the first named
author. Notably the time-honoured Kostant construction, developed for the “Toda lattice”,
fails miserably in the present context [9, 1.3]. The work in [5] used all sorts of strange gizmos,
yet was finally understood in [8] by what can be now be incorporated into our neighbourhood
philosophy 2.8.
Then a programme was lanced to generalize this construction. It culminated in the rules
in [9, 3.2.2, 3.2.6, 3.2.7]. These are explained below.
As a by-product of the construction of Reverse Tableaux the (mysterious) combinatorial
data encoded in C was reformulated in terms of a much more transparent multi-set called
simply the “Red Set”, 3.2.4.
3.2. The Component Map. Associated to T C there is a set X(T C) of excluded root
vectors in m, described explicitly in [9, 4.3.9, 4.3.10] and extended in 4.4. Let uC be the
subspace of m spanned by vectors which are not excluded and set C := B.uC. By [9, Sect.6],
C ) is a component of the nilfibre N . The map T C 7→C , is called the component map. It
is injective [9, Thm. 7.5]. Proving it to be surjective will complete our description of the
