Abstract
Put–call parity is risk-neutral at terminal payoff, but its enforcement is path-
dependent and capital-using. I test whether the SPX and RUT carry gap is explained
by OIS-based funding, volatility, trading-friction, and financial-condition variables, or
also by residual outside-option information. Adding IEFA, IGOV, and IAU improves
in-sample and leave-one-year-out fit after U.S.-centered controls. Gains survive broad-
dollar neutralization, alternative blocks, PCA, residualization, and nested horizon se-
lection. Results support reduced-form P–Q alignment: finite-capital parity enforcement
reflects physical-measure investment opportunities, not payoff-level no-arbitrage fail-
ure.

Introduction
Put–call parity is a basic no-arbitrage relation for European options. At the terminal-payoff
level, it is model-free. In actual markets, however, enforcing this relation is not a static
equation. An arbitrageur must carry a position involving calls, puts, and a futures or forward
leg until maturity. Along the path, the position is exposed to variation margin, funding needs,
trading frictions, and finite-capital constraints. A small price-space parity residual therefore
does not imply a risk-free free lunch.
Shin (2026a) shows that this enforcement burden is visible in the carry gap, the annualized
wedge between option-implied and OIS discount factors. The central explanatory variable is
a GBM path-risk term of the form rσ√τ. Shin (2026b) further shows that preserving physical
drift introduces an additional rˆµτ component. This suggests that the enforcement of a risk-
neutral parity relation can be exposed to physical-measure states through the implementation
path, even though the terminal payoff identity itself remains risk-neutral.
I study a different component of the same framework: the opportunity cost of enforcement
capital. The OIS-based baseline approximates the rate-like component of the GBM term with
OIS rates. This is a natural risk-free benchmark. But the opportunity cost faced by a finite-
capital arbitrageur need not be fully summarized by OIS rates alone. Capital tied to parity
enforcement competes with other investment opportunities. The carry gap may therefore
align with residual outside-option states after risk-free funding, volatility, trading frictions,
and financial conditions have been controlled for.
This conditional phrasing is important. The asset-extension exercise does not ask whether
foreign assets are unconditionally better outside-option proxies than U.S. assets. The baseline
is already U.S.-centered. OIS rates capture risk-free funding conditions, VIX and RVX enter
the GBM scale as high-frequency volatility states, BAmed/τ captures option-market trading
frictions, and NFCI captures slow-moving U.S. financial conditions. Thus, the incremental
information in any asset component is measured after a substantial part of the U.S. funding,
volatility, equity-risk, and financial-condition state has already been absorbed.
To test for residual outside-option information, I use a parsimonious 3ETF block consist-
ing of IEFA, IGOV, and IAU. These assets represent developed ex-U.S. equity, international
sovereign bonds, and gold. Each asset component is constructed as the rolling OLS slope of
the past log price path, using only information available through t −1. The asset block is
therefore not an unrestricted predictor search. It is a compact outside-option proxy based on
a standard asset-allocation taxonomy, evaluated conditional on the U.S.-centered baseline
controls.
The results show that the OIS-based representation is empirically incomplete in both

SPX and RUT. Adding the 3ETF block raises in-sample R2 from 0.312 to 0.402 in SPX
and from 0.281 to 0.361 in RUT. Leave-one-year-out pooled out-of-sample R2 also rises
from 0.221 to 0.379 in SPX and from 0.171 to 0.318 in RUT. The main result survives

explanatory power of the 3ETF block relative to the original specification. The result is
therefore difficult to interpret as a disguised U.S.-dollar factor.
The component-level evidence sharpens the interpretation. IEFA and IAU form the most
stable part of the asset block. IEFA remains negative and strongly significant across both
markets, consistent with a residual global equity-risk or risk-bearing-capacity channel. IAU
remains positive and significant, consistent with a safe-haven or liquidity-state channel.
IGOV is more conditional. In the original specification, it partly replaces the long-horizon
OIS component, suggesting overlap with slow duration and safe-asset variation. However,
after broad-dollar neutralization, the IGOV coefficient weakens and becomes less precisely
identified. IGOV is therefore better interpreted as a dollar-sensitive slow foreign-bond com-
ponent than as a clean FX-orthogonal sovereign-bond factor.
The absorption pattern is also informative. After the 3ETF components are added, the
OIS 1Y component remains useful, whereas the OIS 10Y component is largely replaced
by the low-frequency asset block. This does not mean that OIS discounting is irrelevant. It
means that the short-horizon OIS channel remains important, while part of the low-frequency
opportunity-cost component is better represented by residual asset-return components that
are not fully spanned by the U.S.-centered baseline.
This interpretation also connects the present evidence to Shin (2026b). The own-index
drift proxy improves the OIS-only baseline, but it adds little once the 3ETF block is included.
Thus, the drift proxy is not rejected. Rather, it appears to be a scalar projection of a
broader physical-measure investment-opportunity state. The 3ETF block provides a more
flexible reduced-form representation of that state, especially through the robust IEFA and
IAU components.
The evidence should be read as reduced-form P–Q alignment, not as a structural P-to-Q
transmission model. Put–call parity remains a terminal-payoff identity. I do not recover phys-
ical probabilities from option prices, and I do not claim that physical returns directly price
option payoffs. The claim is narrower: a carry-space residual constructed from option-implied
and OIS discount factors can be empirically aligned with physical-measure outside-option
components because the risk-neutral parity relation is enforced by finite-capital intermedi-
aries who face physical-measure investment opportunities.

Related Literature
This paper relates to four strands of literature: put–call parity and option-implied discount-
ing, limits to arbitrage and intermediary capital, risk-neutral objects and physical-measure
information, and investment-opportunity states. The common issue is that textbook no-
arbitrage relations are exact at the terminal-payoff level, but their enforcement in actual
markets is path-dependent and capital-using. From this perspective, I ask whether the carry-
space wedge between option-implied and OIS discount factors is explained by OIS-based
variables alone, or also aligned with physical-measure outside-option proxies.
2.1
Put–Call Parity and Option-Implied Discounting
Put–call parity has been a basic no-arbitrage relation in option pricing since Stoll (1969). The
empirical literature has examined whether observed parity deviations are genuine arbitrage
opportunities or the result of transaction costs, short-sale constraints, execution frictions,
and market microstructure noise (Gould and Galai, 1974; Klemkosky and Resnick, 1979;
Ackert and Tian, 2001). The common conclusion is that observed parity deviations should
not be read mechanically as risk-free arbitrage opportunities.
I follow this tradition, but study a different object. The focus is not the immediate price-
space violation of put–call parity. Using the option-implied discount-factor procedure of
Azzone and Baviera (2021), I estimate implied discount factors from SPX and RUT options
and compare them with OIS discount factors. This yields the carry gap, an annualized carry-
space wedge. The question is therefore not whether quoted parity deviations exist. It is which
state variables align with the wedge left in carry space after quoted parity is compressed.
2.2
Limits to Arbitrage and Parity Enforcement
The interpretation builds on the limits-to-arbitrage literature. Since Shleifer and Vishny
(1997), this literature has emphasized that arbitrage is not performed instantly by uncon-
4


## Page 5


strained representative investors. It is carried out by finite-capital arbitrageurs who face
funding constraints, margin requirements, interim losses, and liquidation risk (Gromb and
Vayanos, 2002; Brunnermeier and Pedersen, 2009; Mitchell and Pulvino, 2012). In options
markets, Ofek et al. (2004) show that short-sale constraints and limits to arbitrage can be
linked to put–call parity deviations.
The setting of this paper is deliberately different from the standard short-sale-constraint
environment. Much of the existing evidence studies cases in which visible price-space de-
viations remain open because arbitrage is directly impeded. Here, quoted put–call parity
residuals against traded futures are already tightly compressed in liquid European index-
option markets. The question is therefore not why an obvious arbitrage violation remains
open, but why a systematic wedge remains in carry space after the visible parity residual
has largely been closed.
I apply the limits-to-arbitrage logic to this carry-space wedge. Put–call parity is exact
as a terminal-payoff identity, but the parity-enforcement position must be maintained be-
fore maturity. Along this path, the position is exposed to mark-to-market gains and losses,
variation margin, funding needs, capital commitment, and liquidation risk. Thus, success-
ful enforcement can close the price-space residual without eliminating the economic cost of
maintaining the enforcing position. The carry gap is therefore interpreted as a reduced-form
implementation wedge rather than as a conventional unclosed mispricing.
This view is also consistent with work linking intermediary capital to law-of-one-price
deviations (Gˆarleanu and Pedersen, 2011; He and Krishnamurthy, 2013; Adrian et al., 2014;
He et al., 2017). It is close in spirit to the CIP evidence in Du et al. (2018): enforcing no-
arbitrage relations uses balance-sheet capacity, capital, and margin, and the marginal cost of
that enforcement varies with market conditions. The contribution here is to apply this logic
to a cleaner parity setting in which the visible price residual is already compressed, and to ask
whether the remaining carry-space wedge is aligned with outside investment opportunities
faced by finite-capital arbitrageurs.
2.3
Risk-Neutral Objects and Physical-Measure Information
This paper also relates to work on the link between option-implied objects and physical-
measure information. Ross (2015), Bollerslev et al. (2009), and Martin (2017) show that
Q-measure objects extracted from option prices can contain information about physical
probabilities, variance risk premia, or expected returns. This literature mainly studies infor-
mation extraction from Q-measure objects to P-measure quantities.
My direction is complementary. I do not recover physical probabilities from option prices.
5


## Page 6


Nor do I identify a structural mapping from physical returns to risk-neutral prices. Instead,
I ask whether the carry gap, a Q-measure carry-space object, is empirically separable from
P-measure outside-option proxies. This question is related to Merton (1973)’s ICAPM, Ross
(1976)’s APT, and asset-pricing models with multiple state variables and common factors
(Campbell, 1993; Campbell and Vuolteenaho, 2004; Chen et al., 1986; Fama and French,
1993; Petkova, 2006). The exercise is not a structural ICAPM or APT test. It is a reduced-
form test of whether the opportunity cost of parity-enforcement capital is aligned with mul-
tiple investment-opportunity states.
2.4
Direct Antecedents and Contribution
This paper directly extends two earlier studies. Shin (2026a) shows that the carry gap is not
a simple price-space parity residual. It is a systematic carry-space wedge between option-
implied and OIS discount factors. That paper also shows that OIS-based GBM path-risk
terms, trading frictions, and financial conditions are important reduced-form predictors of
the carry gap.
Shin (2026b) extends the same framework in the direction of physical drift. It uses the
rolling trend in the own-index total return as a proxy for ˆµ and shows that a drift-preserving
component of the form rˆµτ adds explanatory power for the carry gap. This paper combines
and extends those two results. I test whether the opportunity-cost component of the carry
gap contains residual outside-option information after OIS-based funding, volatility, trading-
friction, and U.S. financial-condition variables have been controlled for. To do so, I introduce
a 3ETF block consisting of IEFA, IGOV, and IAU, and compare the baseline, drift, 3ETF,
and 3ETF+drift specifications.

Motivation for the Asset Extension
This section motivates the extension from the OIS-based baseline to asset-return components.
The key idea is simple. Capital tied to parity enforcement competes with other investment
opportunities. Even if the terminal payoff of put–call parity is fixed, the enforcing strategy is
exposed to variation margin, funding needs, trading frictions, and finite-capital constraints.
The carry gap may therefore respond not only to OIS rates, but also to the state of outside
investment opportunities forgone by arbitrageurs.
Consider a finite-capital arbitrageur, Bob. Bob observes a small put–call parity wedge
in the SPX or RUT options market and enters a parity-enforcement position combining a
synthetic forward and a futures leg. At the terminal-payoff level, the trade is nearly locked.
6


## Page 7


Before maturity, however, Bob must absorb daily mark-to-market gains and losses and meet
variation-margin calls. If he must reduce other risky or liquid positions to support the trade,
he gives up the return he could have earned on equities, bonds, gold, or other assets. Thus,
even when the terminal payoff is fixed, the implementation path has a clear opportunity
cost.
From this perspective, restricting the opportunity-cost component of the baseline GBM
term to OIS rates may be too narrow. OIS rates are a natural benchmark for risk-free
funding costs. But the actual decision of a finite-capital arbitrageur is not governed only by
the risk-free rate. Bob compares harvesting the parity wedge with deploying the same capital
elsewhere. The shadow cost of parity-enforcement capital may therefore reflect low-frequency
states of outside-option returns as well as OIS rates.
The asset extension implements this idea inside the GBM structure. The baseline speci-
fication can be written conceptually as
GBM term = path-risk scale × opportunity-cost component.
In the baseline, the opportunity-cost component is proxied by OIS rates. In the 3ETF ex-
tension, I expand this component using low-frequency return components of global equity,
sovereign bonds, and gold. The 3ETF block is therefore not a generic set of added predictors.
It is a reduced-form proxy for the outside-option set faced by a finite-capital arbitrageur.
This interpretation also connects to the drift extension. Shin (2026b) shows that an
own-index rolling drift proxy adds explanatory power for the carry gap. I ask whether this
drift proxy is an independent scalar primitive, or instead a compressed proxy for a broader
physical-measure state that partly overlaps with U.S. financial conditions and partly ex-
tends beyond them. To examine this, I compare the baseline, drift, 3ETF, and 3ETF+drift
specifications.
The empirical predictions are direct. If the carry gap is an OIS-contained wedge, adding
asset-return components should produce only limited gains over the baseline. If the carry
gap is aligned with outside-option states, low-frequency components from equities, bonds,
and gold should add explanatory power. If the 3ETF block absorbs the information in the
own-index drift proxy, then the drift effect is better interpreted as a scalar projection of a
broader residual opportunity-cost state rather than as an independent primitive.

Comparison Specifications and Evaluation
I compare four specifications. The first is the baseline specification, which includes the OIS
1Y and OIS 10Y GBM terms. The second is the drift specification, which adds an own-index
rolling drift proxy to the baseline. The third is the 3ETF specification, which keeps the OIS
1Y term, drops the OIS 10Y term, and adds GBM terms based on IEFA, IGOV, and IAU.
The fourth is the 3ETF+drift specification, which adds the own-index drift proxy to the
3ETF model.
This comparison has two goals. First, I test whether the 3ETF outside-option block
explains the carry gap better than the OIS-only baseline. Second, I test whether the ˆµ
proxy in the drift extension retains independent explanatory power once the 3ETF block
is included. If the 3ETF+drift specification adds little to the 3ETF specification, then the
information captured by the own-index drift proxy is largely absorbed by the expanded
residual opportunity-cost state vector.

pre-restricted outside-option block, not an unrestricted predictor search.

the 3ETF Block
The previous section shows that the 3ETF outside-option block, consisting of IEFA, IGOV,
and IAU, substantially improves the fit of the carry gap relative to the OIS-only baseline.

question is whether the own-index drift proxy contains independent incremental information
after controlling for the 3ETF outside-option block.
To answer this question, I compare the 3ETF specification with the 3ETF+drift specifica-
tion. The 3ETF specification includes OIS 1Y, IEFA, IGOV, IAU, BAmed/τ, and NFCI. The
3ETF+drift specification adds the drift-preserving term from Shin (2026b), OIS1Y ×ˆµ504×τ.

components that are less directly spanned by the U.S.-centered baseline controls.

specification. IEFA is negative in all folds for both SPX and RUT. IAU is positive in all
folds for both markets. The OIS 1Y term is also negative in all folds. Thus, the stable out-
of-sample structure is not the drift term. It is the core 3ETF structure centered on OIS 1Y,
IEFA, and IAU.

7.5
Economic Interpretation
These results should not be read as a simple claim that the own-index drift proxy is irrelevant.
The drift term in Shin (2026b) improves the OIS-only baseline, especially in SPX. The result
here is narrower. Once the 3ETF block and the remaining baseline controls are included, the
own-index drift proxy has limited independent incremental information.
This pattern is consistent with a partial-out interpretation. The own-index drift proxy is
a low-frequency trend of the U.S. index itself. It therefore naturally overlaps with U.S. risk
appetite, U.S. financial conditions, and slow domestic market states already represented in
the baseline through VIX/RVX, OIS rates, and NFCI. In the OIS-only specification, the drift
proxy can improve fit because it supplies an additional low-frequency physical-measure state.
But once the model includes broader asset-return components, much of the information in
the own-index drift proxy is already spanned by the expanded state vector.
The 3ETF block should therefore be interpreted as a conditional residual outside-option
proxy, not as a structural replacement for the drift term. IEFA captures a global equity-
risk component that is less directly embedded in the U.S.-centered baseline controls. IAU
captures a non-credit safe-haven component that is distinct from domestic equity volatility
and funding conditions. These two components form the most stable part of the asset block:
IEFA remains negative and significant in both markets, while IAU remains positive and
significant in both markets. By contrast, IGOV is weaker and more conditional, especially
in RUT. Its role is better understood as a slow foreign-bond, duration, and global safe-asset
component rather than as the clean core of the 3ETF result.
This interpretation also clarifies why the drift term loses power in the horse race. The own-
index drift proxy summarizes one scalar low-frequency projection of the U.S. market state.
The 3ETF block provides a more flexible representation of residual investment-opportunity
variation after the U.S.-centered baseline has already absorbed much of the domestic state
space. As a result, the drift term remains only as a small residual component in SPX and
does not improve out-of-sample performance in RUT.
The evidence therefore extends rather than overturns the drift result in Shin (2026b).
The earlier drift evidence shows that the carry gap is not fully explained by OIS-based
path risk, trading frictions, and financial conditions. The present horse race shows that the
drift proxy is not the final state variable. It is better interpreted as a scalar projection of a
broader physical-measure opportunity-cost state. The carry gap remains a Q-measure object
constructed from a risk-neutral parity relation, but the capital used to enforce that relation
competes with physical-measure investment opportunities. The 3ETF specification captures
part of this residual opportunity-cost state, leaving limited independent information in the
own-index drift proxy.

8
Robustness Tests
This section examines whether the main 3ETF result is driven by a dollar cycle, a single
common factor, lookback-horizon selection, or a specific ETF block. I conduct four robustness
tests. First, I use the broad-dollar index to construct FX-neutralized price series for IEFA,
IGOV, and IAU, and then repeat the main analysis. Second, I use principal-component and
residualization tests to examine whether the 3ETF block collapses to a single latent common
factor. Third, I use a nested LOYO procedure to ensure that horizon selection does not use
ex post information from the holdout sample. Fourth, I evaluate alternative U.S.-centered
and emerging-market-centered 3ETF blocks.
8.1
Broad-Dollar Neutralization
To examine whether the explanatory power of the 3ETF specification is merely a disguised
U.S.-dollar factor, I reconstruct broad-dollar-adjusted price series for IEFA, IGOV, and IAU
using the broad-dollar index DTWEXBGS. Operationally, I subtract the log broad-dollar index
from each ETF log price and then recompute the rolling OLS slope series from the adjusted
price paths. Appendix A describes the calendar alignment and previous-observation fill used
to match DTWEXBGS to the ETF trading calendar.
This adjustment is deliberately mechanical. It is not meant to construct a perfect currency-
hedged version of each ETF. Instead, it asks a narrower question: does the 3ETF block con-
tinue to explain the carry gap after removing a broad U.S.-dollar component from the asset
price series?
As in the main asset-augmented specification, the broad-dollar-neutralized robustness
test keeps GBM OIS,1Y . In the extended model with asset-return components, GBM OIS,10Y
is excluded. The baseline specification is unchanged and includes both OIS 1Y and OIS 10Y
GBM terms. The exercise therefore tests whether the broad-dollar-adjusted 3ETF block
continues to improve on the OIS-only baseline.

8.3
Nested LOYO Horizon Selection
The main specification fixes the lookback horizons of the three asset-return components: 70
trading days for IEFA, 441 trading days for IGOV, and 315 trading days for IAU. These
horizons are motivated by asset-by-asset horizon profiles. Still, the out-of-sample performance
may be overstated if the horizons are selected using the full sample. This subsection addresses
that concern with a nested leave-one-year-out procedure.
For each holdout year, I first remove that year from the sample. I then select the lookback
windows for IEFA, IGOV, and IAU using only the remaining training years. The objective
function is the equal-weighted average of in-sample R2 across SPX and RUT. The search
starts from the main-specification horizons and combines a coarse grid search with local hill-
climb refinement. After the horizons are selected inside the training sample, I estimate the
baseline and 3ETF specifications on the same training years and evaluate performance in the
excluded holdout year. Thus, the holdout year is not used either for coefficient estimation
or for horizon selection.
This test is not meant to replace the main specification. It is a robustness check for look-
ahead concerns in horizon selection. The key question is whether the procedure repeatedly
selects a fast foreign-equity component and slower foreign-bond and gold components when
only training-sample information is used.

9
Discussion
9.1
What the 3ETF Block Means
The main empirical result should not be interpreted as evidence that foreign assets are un-
conditionally better outside-option proxies than U.S. assets. A more precise interpretation
is conditional. The OIS-based baseline already contains a rich U.S.-centered information set.
OIS rates capture the risk-free funding component, VIX and RVX enter the GBM scale
as high-frequency market-specific volatility states, BAmed/τ captures option-market trad-
ing frictions, and NFCI captures a slow-moving U.S. financial-condition component. The
asset-extension exercise therefore does not ask which asset class is most important in an
unconditional sense. It asks which asset-return components contain incremental information
after this baseline state vector has already been controlled for.
This distinction changes the interpretation of the 3ETF result. The stronger performance
of IEFA, IGOV, and IAU should not be read as a claim that foreign assets directly price U.S.
37


## Page 38


index options or dominate U.S. assets as primitive state variables. Rather, the developed ex-
U.S. block appears to contain residual outside-option information that is less fully spanned by
the U.S.-centered baseline. In this sense, the 3ETF block is best understood as a conditional
proxy for the marginal investment-opportunity state faced by finite-capital parity enforcers.
Formally, the relevant object is not the unconditional information in an asset-return
component Za, but its residual information after conditioning on the baseline:
∆R2
a = R2(CG | Xbase, Za) −R2(CG | Xbase).
Equivalently, the incremental contribution of Za is governed by the component MXbaseZa,
where MXbase denotes residualization with respect to the baseline controls. The empirical
finding is therefore not that U.S. assets are irrelevant, but that much of their relevant state
information is already embedded in the baseline specification.
9.2
Why the ex-U.S. Equity Channel Adds More
The conditional-information view helps explain why the U.S.-centered block improves on the
baseline but contributes less than the developed ex-U.S. block. The key comparison is not
between U.S. and foreign assets in unconditional economic importance. It is between their
residual information after the baseline state vector has already been included.
This point is clearest for the equity component. VTI is economically relevant as a U.S.
equity outside option. However, the baseline already contains several variables that are
closely tied to the U.S. equity-risk environment. VIX and RVX enter the GBM scale as
high-frequency market-specific volatility states. NFCI captures slower movements in U.S.
financial conditions, funding tightness, credit conditions, and aggregate risk appetite. OIS
rates absorb the risk-free funding component. The incremental contribution of VTI is there-
fore evaluated after much of the domestic equity-risk and financial-condition state has already
been partialed out.
IEFA has more room to contribute because it supplies a global equity-risk component that
is less directly spanned by these U.S.-centered controls. Its role should not be interpreted as
evidence that foreign equity directly prices U.S. index options. Rather, IEFA contains residual
information about global risk-bearing capacity and outside investment opportunities that
remains informative after OIS rates, VIX/RVX, bid–ask frictions, and NFCI are controlled
for. This interpretation is also consistent with the coefficient evidence: the IEFA loading is
negative and highly stable across both markets and across LOYO training folds.
The bond comparison should be interpreted more cautiously. BND may be a valid outside
option in economic terms, but U.S. bond-market information is partly represented through
38


## Page 39


OIS rates and NFCI. OIS rates absorb the risk-free term-structure component, while NFCI
embeds slow U.S. funding, credit, and market-tightness variation. BND therefore has limited
residual room to improve the regression.
By contrast, IGOV can add information because it is less directly spanned by the U.S.-
centered baseline. However, IGOV should not be treated as the clean representative of the
foreign-asset channel. The broad-dollar-neutralization results show that the IGOV coeffi-
cient weakens and becomes less precisely identified once a broad U.S.-dollar component is
removed. Thus, IGOV is better interpreted as a conditional slow component combining for-
eign duration, safe-asset demand, and dollar-sensitive global funding variation, rather than
as a clean FX-orthogonal foreign-bond factor.
The stronger performance of the developed ex-U.S. block is therefore a conditional
residual-information result. The U.S.-centered block still improves on the OIS-only base-
line, so the outside-option channel is not uniquely foreign. The point is instead that the
baseline already absorbs a large part of the U.S.-centered state space. After that partialing-
out, the residual outside-option information is more visible in the ex-U.S. equity component,
with IAU providing an additional safe-haven channel and IGOV contributing only as a more
conditional, dollar-sensitive slow component.
9.3
The Drift Proxy and the Role of NFCI
The drift extension in Shin (2026b) provides direct evidence for the partial-out interpretation.
When the own-index drift proxy is added to the OIS-only baseline, the absolute value of the
NFCI coefficient falls in both markets. In SPX, the NFCI coefficient moves from −25.839 in
the baseline to −19.577 in the drift-extended specification. In RUT, it moves from −23.961
to −22.286. Thus, adding the drift proxy reduces the absolute NFCI loading by about 24
percent in SPX and about 7 percent in RUT.
This change is informative because the bid–ask coefficient does not move in the same
way. The coefficient on BAmed/τ is 0.256 in the SPX baseline and 0.261 after adding the
drift term. In RUT, it is 0.130 in the baseline and 0.135 after adding the drift term. Thus,
the drift proxy does not appear to be absorbing the option-market trading-friction channel.
Instead, it absorbs part of the slow financial-condition component previously attributed to
NFCI.
This evidence matters for the present paper. The own-index drift proxy is constructed
from the low-frequency trend of the U.S. index itself. It therefore naturally overlaps with
U.S. risk appetite, U.S. financial conditions, and slow domestic market states. Its incremental
contribution over the OIS-only baseline shows that a physical-measure state matters for the
39


## Page 40


carry gap. But the reduction in the NFCI loading shows that this state is not orthogonal to
the U.S.-centered financial-condition block.
The 3ETF horse race extends this logic. Once IEFA, IGOV, and IAU are included, the
own-index drift proxy has little independent explanatory power. This does not reject the
drift channel. Instead, it suggests that the own-index drift proxy in Shin (2026b) was a
scalar projection of a broader investment-opportunity state. The 3ETF block captures that
broader state more flexibly, leaving less residual role for the single own-index drift term.
There is no contradiction between this interpretation and the fact that NFCI remains
important in the 3ETF specification. The asset block and NFCI are not substitutes for
the same object. NFCI summarizes U.S. financial tightness and funding conditions. The
asset block captures residual outside-option information, especially the global equity-risk
and safe-haven components less directly embedded in the U.S.-centered controls. When asset
components are added, NFCI continues to measure the domestic financial-condition channel,
while the asset block absorbs a different low-frequency opportunity-cost component.
9.4
Interpretation of IEFA, IGOV, and IAU
Under this revised interpretation, the three components of the 3ETF block should be read
as conditional residual channels rather than structural pricing factors.
IEFA represents developed ex-U.S. equity conditions. Its role is not that foreign equity
returns mechanically enter U.S. option payoffs. Rather, IEFA contains global risk-bearing
and investment-opportunity information that is not fully spanned by VIX/RVX and NFCI.
Its negative loading is consistent with the idea that stronger global risk-bearing conditions
reduce the shadow premium required to allocate capital to parity enforcement.
IAU represents a non-credit safe-haven component. Its positive loading is consistent with
higher parity-enforcement premia in risk-off, safe-haven-demand, or liquidity-demand states.
The fact that the IAU channel remains robust under broad-dollar neutralization suggests
that it is not simply a disguised dollar factor. It appears to capture a separate safe-haven or
liquidity-state dimension of the outside-option set.
IGOV is the most conditional component. In the original 3ETF specification, it captures
a slow foreign-sovereign-bond and duration state that partly replaces the long-horizon OIS
component. However, the broad-dollar-neutralization results show that IGOV is not a clean
FX-orthogonal sovereign-bond factor. Once a broad U.S.-dollar component is removed, the
IGOV coefficient weakens and becomes less precisely identified. This suggests that the orig-
inal IGOV signal combines foreign duration, safe-asset demand, and dollar-sensitive global
funding conditions.
40


## Page 41


Thus, the 3ETF block should not be described as simply “foreign equity, foreign bonds,
and gold.” Its empirical content is more subtle. IEFA supplies a relatively fast global risk-
bearing component, IAU supplies a safe-haven component, and IGOV supplies a slower
dollar-sensitive foreign-bond component. Together they proxy for residual outside-option
states that remain informative after the U.S.-centered baseline controls have already absorbed
much of the domestic state space.
9.5
Broad-Dollar Neutralization and Common-Factor Concerns
The broad-dollar neutralization test helps separate two interpretations. One possibility is
that the 3ETF result merely repackages a broad U.S.-dollar cycle. Another is that the dollar
cycle is only one component of a broader global outside-option state.
The evidence favors the second interpretation. After removing a broad-dollar component
from the ETF price paths, the asset-augmented specification continues to improve substan-
tially on the OIS-only baseline and performs close to the original 3ETF specification. If the
asset block were only a disguised dollar factor, this adjustment should have produced a large
deterioration. It does not.
At the same time, the coefficient-level results show that broad-dollar variation matters
for the composition of the block. IEFA and IAU remain robust after the adjustment, whereas
IGOV weakens. This is consistent with the interpretation that IEFA and IAU carry more
FX-orthogonal residual information, while IGOV is a slower dollar-sensitive foreign-bond
channel. The broad-dollar test therefore does not invalidate the 3ETF result. It refines the
economic interpretation of its components.
The PCA and residualization tests point in the same direction. The three asset terms
do not collapse into a single common factor. Instead, they span multiple low-frequency
components with different speeds and economic content. This matters because the paper’s
claim is not that one hidden global factor explains the carry gap. The claim is that the
carry gap is conditionally aligned with a small set of outside-option components that remain
informative after the baseline U.S.-centered state variables are controlled for.
9.6
P–Q Alignment, Not Structural P-to-Q Recovery
The results should be interpreted as reduced-form P–Q alignment, not as structural P-to-
Q recovery. Put–call parity remains a terminal-payoff identity. The physical-measure asset
components do not enter the payoff identity and do not imply a failure of risk-neutral pricing.
The mechanism is instead implementation-based. The carry gap is a Q-measure carry-
space object extracted from option-implied discounting. But enforcing the parity relation
41


## Page 42


requires capital, margin support, funding capacity, and balance-sheet usage before maturity.
The arbitrageur who supplies this enforcement capital faces physical-measure investment op-
portunities. Therefore, even if the terminal parity relation is risk-neutral, the implementation
premium embedded in the carry gap can be aligned with physical-measure outside-option
states.
The revised interpretation is narrower and more defensible than saying that foreign assets
price U.S. options. The evidence says that, after controlling for U.S. funding, volatility,
trading-friction, and financial-condition variables, several global asset-return components
still contain incremental information about the carry-space wedge. This is the sense in which
the carry gap exhibits P–Q alignment.
9.7
Limitations and Takeaway
Several limitations remain. The asset-return components are low-frequency and persistent.
The regressions are reduced-form and should not be interpreted as causal estimates or as
identification of a complete stochastic discount factor. The selected ETFs are proxies, not
structural state variables. The horizon choices and asset-block comparisons help discipline
the exercise, but they do not turn the specification into a structural model of intermediary
optimization.
The main takeaway is therefore conditional rather than absolute. The carry gap is not
fully explained by OIS-only funding variables, and it is not fully summarized by the own-
index drift proxy. It is also not the case that foreign assets are intrinsically better outside-
option proxies than U.S. assets. Instead, the baseline already contains substantial U.S.-
centered information through OIS rates, VIX/RVX, bid–ask frictions, and NFCI. After these
variables are controlled for, the marginal outside-option information that remains visible is
more strongly represented by global and ex-U.S. asset components.
This interpretation reconciles the main empirical patterns. The U.S.-centered asset block
still improves on the baseline, showing that domestic assets are economically relevant. The
developed ex-U.S. and emerging-market blocks often improve more, showing that global
residual information matters after U.S. states have been partialed out. The drift proxy im-
proves the OIS-only baseline, but its incremental contribution fades once the 3ETF block
is included, indicating that own-index drift is a scalar projection of a broader state. Broad-
dollar neutralization preserves most of the explanatory power, showing that the asset block
is not merely a dollar factor, while the weakening of IGOV clarifies the dollar-sensitive com-
ponent of the foreign-bond channel.
Overall, the 3ETF specification is best understood as a parsimonious conditional proxy
42


## Page 43


for residual outside-option costs of finite arbitrage capital. The carry gap is a risk-neutral
parity object in construction, but its enforcement is carried out by capital-constrained inter-
mediaries who face physical-measure investment opportunities. The empirical contribution of
the asset block is to show that this implementation premium contains a global low-frequency
component not fully captured by the U.S.-centered OIS baseline.
10
Conclusion
This paper tests whether the carry gap in put–call parity is fully explained by OIS-based
discounting, volatility, trading-friction, and financial-condition variables, or whether it also
contains residual information aligned with outside investment opportunities faced by finite
arbitrage capital. The evidence supports the latter view. The carry gap is a Q-measure carry-
space object, constructed from the wedge between option-implied and OIS discount factors.
Yet its dynamics are systematically aligned with low-frequency physical-measure asset-return
components after the baseline U.S.-centered state variables are controlled for.
The main empirical result is that adding the 3ETF block of IEFA, IGOV, and IAU im-
proves the OIS-based baseline in both SPX and RUT options. The improvement is not con-
fined to in-sample fit. It also survives leave-one-year-out validation, broad-dollar neutraliza-
tion, principal-component and residualization tests, nested horizon selection, and alternative
asset-block checks. Broad-dollar neutralization does not materially reduce the explanatory
power of the 3ETF block, making it difficult to interpret the result as a disguised U.S.-dollar
factor.
The economic interpretation, however, is conditional rather than absolute. The result
should not be read as saying that foreign assets are unconditionally better outside-option
proxies than U.S. assets. The baseline already contains a rich U.S.-centered information set.
OIS rates capture risk-free funding conditions, VIX and RVX enter the GBM scale as short-
horizon volatility states, the bid–ask term captures option-market trading frictions, and
NFCI captures slow-moving U.S. financial conditions. The incremental contribution of the
asset block is therefore measured after much of the domestic funding, volatility, equity-risk,
and bond-market state has already been absorbed. Under this interpretation, the developed
ex-U.S. block performs well because it supplies residual global investment-opportunity infor-
mation less fully spanned by the baseline controls.
This interpretation also reconciles the drift evidence. The own-index drift proxy improves
the OIS-only baseline, but its independent contribution becomes small once the 3ETF block
is included. Moreover, in the drift-extended specification, the absolute value of the NFCI
loading falls, suggesting that the drift proxy partly absorbs the slow U.S. financial-condition
43


## Page 44


component previously captured by NFCI. Thus, the drift result and the 3ETF result are
not competing explanations. The own-index drift proxy appears to be a scalar projection of
a broader physical-measure investment-opportunity state, while the 3ETF block provides a
more flexible reduced-form representation of that state.
The economic mechanism lies in enforcement, not in put–call parity itself. Put–call par-
ity remains exact as a terminal-payoff identity, and the results do not imply a failure of
no-arbitrage pricing. The point is that enforcing this identity in actual markets is path-
dependent and capital-using. Before maturity, arbitrageurs must absorb variation margin,
interim mark-to-market losses, funding needs, liquidation risk, and finite-capital constraints.
Capital committed to parity enforcement has alternative uses. The opportunity cost of that
capital may therefore not be fully summarized by risk-free OIS rates alone.
The results should be read as reduced-form P–Q alignment, not as a structural P-to-
Q transmission model. I do not recover physical probabilities from option prices, and I do
not claim that physical returns directly price option payoffs. Instead, I show that a risk-
neutral parity relation is enforced by intermediaries who face physical-measure investment
opportunities. As a result, the carry-space wedge left after quoted parity is compressed can
be empirically aligned with outside-option variables.
Several limitations remain. The 3ETF block is not a structural stochastic discount fac-
tor, and the asset-return components are low-frequency and persistent. After broad-dollar
neutralization, the composition of the block becomes more nuanced: IEFA and IAU remain
robust, whereas IGOV becomes weaker and less precisely identified. This suggests that the
IGOV component is partly tied to a dollar-sensitive slow foreign-bond and safe-asset-cycle
state rather than to a clean FX-orthogonal sovereign-bond factor. The improvement is also
less consistent at very long maturities, especially at the RUT long end. Future work can de-
compose currency exposure, duration risk, safe-asset demand, and balance-sheet constraints
more directly, and can test whether similar outside-option structures appear in other deriva-
tives arbitrage relations.
Overall, the carry gap is not merely a discounting wedge closed within the OIS structure.
It is better interpreted as a reduced-form trace of the economic burden of enforcing a risk-
neutral parity relation with finite capital. The terminal no-arbitrage identity may be exact,
but the process that enforces it in actual markets can reflect physical-measure investment
opportunities, heterogeneous frequencies, and alternative uses of arbitrage capital. The cen-
tral result is therefore conditional: after U.S.-centered funding, volatility, trading-friction,
and financial-condition states are controlled for, global and ex-U.S. asset components still
contain residual information about the carry gap.

Data and Methodological Details
This appendix provides implementation details for the carry-gap construction, the 3ETF
variables, the drift proxy, and the in-sample and out-of-sample evaluation. The main text
focuses on the economic intuition and comparison specifications. This appendix reports the
data processing and estimation procedures needed for replication.
A.1
Data and Analysis Sample
The option quote data are minute-level NBBO quotes from ThetaData. The sample is re-
stricted to the period over which OIS data are available, from January 4, 2016 to October 31,
2025. All results in the main text are based on the common sample in which option-market
information and the OIS discount curve are both observed.
SPX and RUT options are European-style index options. I therefore do not account
for early-exercise premia in the put–call-parity-based identification of discount factors. The
empirical analysis is implemented in MATLAB R2025b.2

A.3
Sample Filters and Daily Aggregation
The preprocessing is designed to make the cross-sectional identification regression stable. I
first keep only call–put pairs sharing the same strike and maturity. I remove observations
with abnormally low prices or excessive bid–ask spreads. I also remove date–maturity cells
with too few valid strikes and dates on which the OIS curve construction fails or produces
an abnormal term structure.
The final sample must satisfy two conditions. First, the option-implied discount factor
must be identifiable. Second, the OIS discount factor must be stably constructed for the
same date and maturity. For each of SPX and RUT, I construct a date × maturity panel. I
then aggregate eligible observations into a daily series using the median across observations
on each date. Median aggregation reduces sensitivity to outliers and transient quote noise.

A.6
Broad-Dollar Neutralization
The broad-dollar robustness test examines whether the foreign-asset components simply
proxy for a common U.S. dollar factor. I use DTWEXBGS for this purpose. I adjust the price
paths of IEFA, IGOV, and IAU by the broad-dollar index, and then compute FX-neutralized
log OLS slopes using the same procedure.
The DTWEXBGS calendar does not perfectly match the ETF trading calendar. I therefore
forward-fill missing values using the most recent available observation. This prevents asset-

A.7
Comparison Specifications
The main comparisons use four specifications. First, the baseline specification includes OIS
1Y and OIS 10Y GBM terms, the bid–ask term, and NFCI. Second, the drift specification
adds GBM ˆµ,OIS,1Y to the baseline. Third, the main 3ETF specification keeps GBM OIS,1Y ,
drops GBM OIS,10Y , and adds asset-return-based GBM terms for IEFA, IGOV, and IAU.
Fourth, the 3ETF+drift specification adds GBM ˆµ,OIS,1Y to the main 3ETF specification.
The main 3ETF specification excludes GBM OIS,10Y because the long-horizon OIS com-
ponent strongly overlaps with the low-frequency outside-option component based on IGOV.
Including both terms destabilizes the coefficient structure, whereas dropping OIS 10Y causes
only limited loss in explanatory power. The main specification therefore keeps OIS 1Y as the
short-horizon risk-free opportunity-cost channel and replaces the long-horizon component
with the 3ETF outside-option block.
A.8
Regression Estimation and Out-of-Sample Evaluation
All main regressions are estimated separately for SPX and RUT. Coefficient inference uses
date-based HAC(Newey–West) standard errors. The maximum lag length is set to 21 trading
days. This is a conservative choice intended to address heteroskedasticity and low-order
autocorrelation in daily residuals. Under a standard Newey–West automatic lag rule based
on the number of daily observations in this sample, the selected lag length is about 8 trading
days. The 21-trading-day lag is therefore longer than the automatic bandwidth and allows
for roughly one month of autocorrelation.
Out-of-sample performance is evaluated using leave-one-year-out validation. Each calen-
dar year is held out once. The model is estimated on the remaining years, and out-of-sample
fit is computed in the excluded year. In-sample performance is summarized by R2, adjusted
R2, RMSE, and MAE. LOYO performance is reported by year and summarized using out-
of-sample R2, mean R2, median R2, pooled R2, mean RMSE, and mean correlation.
