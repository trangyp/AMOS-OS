ABSTRACT
The Lighthouse pulsar (PSR J1101−6101) sports a bright X-ray trail and filament. The synchrotron
emission from both structures is expected to be polarized, with electric vector position angle (EVPA)
perpendicular to the magnetic field direction and polarization degree (PD) indicating the local degree
of magnetic turbulence.
We present a 1 megasecond Imaging X-ray Polarimetry Explorer (IXPE)
observation of the Lighthouse complex. At the 99% confidence level, we detect the filament polarization
with PD 55 ± 18% and EVPA indicating a magnetic field parallel to the filament axis. The large PD
implies a turbulent magnetic field weaker than the background field, in conflict with some existing
models.
We also detect polarization from the pulsar and trail.
The trail’s X-ray polarization is
nearly orthogonal to the radio polarization, suggesting spatial separation between the X-ray- and
radio-emitting leptons. The pulsar polarization is well-fit by the rotating vector model.
Keywords: pulsars: individual (J1101−6101)—polarization—ISM: magnetic fields
1. INTRODUCTION
PSR J1101−6101 (hereafter J1101 or “Lighthouse”)
is a young, energetic, rotation-powered pulsar (spin
down age τc = P/(2 ˙P) = 63 kyr, spin down power
˙E = 1.6 × 1036 erg s−1, and period P = 63 ms, Halpern
et al. 2014), rapidly moving at 990 km s−1 (Dinsmore
& Romani 2026).
J1101 generates two extended X-
ray structures. A bright X-ray trail extends ∼1′ be-
hind the pulsar, pointing to its birth supernova remnant
(SNR) MSH 11−61A (Pavan et al. 2011). Trails occur
when the wind from a rather young, supersonic pulsar
shocks against the interstellar medium (ISM), creating
a bow shock ahead of the pulsar and a trail in the tur-
bulent wake behind (see Kargaltsev et al. (2017) for a
review). The second structure is a rare X-ray filament,
extending for ∼5′ orthogonal to the trail axis, persisting
several parsecs into the interstellar medium (ISM; Pa-
van et al. 2014) and accompanied by a fainter opposing
“anti-filament” (see Fig. 1, right). X-ray filaments—of
which Lighthouse is the brightest (Dinsmore & Romani
2024)—are characterized by their misalignment with the
pulsar velocity and are proposed to represent cosmic ray
(CR) leptons escaping the bow shock onto ISM fields
(Bandiera 2008). Both structures emit synchrotron X-
rays up to 25 keV (Klingler et al. 2023), but only the
trail is visible at radio wavelengths, where the filament
and pulsar are absent (Pavan et al. 2014).
Central to the physics of trails and filaments is the
magnetic field geometry, which is accessible through po-
larization observations. Synchrotron emission has elec-
tric vector position angle (EVPA) perpendicular to the
background magnetic field B0, and maximum polariza-

Magnetohydrodynamic simulations depict a complex
magnetic field geometry in pulsar trails (Olmi & Buc-
ciantini 2019), with an average magnetic field depending
on the pulsar spin axis and wind asymmetry. Observa-
tions also show variable geometries, with radio polariza-
tion of the Mouse (Yusef-Zadeh & Gaensler 2005) and
PSR J1509−5850 (Ng et al. 2010) showing fields paral-
lel and perpendicular to the trail axis, respectively. No
X-ray polarization observations of trails have been pub-
lished to date.
If filaments indeed represent CRs propagating along
ISM field lines, the background magnetic field B0 should
align with the filament axis.
Radio polarizations are
not accessible, since filaments are radio-quiet, but this
field geometry has been confirmed at X-ray wavelengths
for the filament candidate G0.13−0.11 (Churazov et al.
2024) and via starlight polarization observations of the
Guitar filament (Dinsmore et al. 2025). Magnetic tur-
bulence traps filament particles, but the details are still
unclear.
Bykov et al. (2017) and Olmi et al. (2024)
have suggested that the non-resonant streaming insta-
bility grows the filament’s turbulence until saturation,
when the turbulent field strength is much greater than
the underlying field (∆B ≫B0). It is also possible that
the turbulence growth cuts off earlier, when the pulsar’s
rapid motion carries the CR injection site to new field
lines (Dinsmore & Romani 2026).
This earlier cutoff
could allow steady-state turbulence with ∆B < B0. X-
ray polarization observations can discriminate between
these models.
We present a 950 kilosecond Imaging X-ray Polarime-
try Explorer (IXPE) observation of Lighthouse.
The
data are analyzed using new techniques that substan-
tially improve the polarization precision, as reviewed in
§2. Polarization results are discussed in §3. We con-
trast our results with other analysis methods in §4 and
conclude with §5.
2. ANALYSIS METHODS
IXPE observed Lighthouse from May 30 to June 17,
2025, for 950 ks (observation ID 04001301). Due to an
anomaly with detector unit (DU) 2 that occurred on 14
April 2025, we are unable to use the DU2 data at the
current time and performed the analysis using DU1 and
DU3 only. EVPAs, positions, and polarization weights
are reconstructed using the NN developed by Peirson
et al. (2021). Although the NN also estimates energies,
gain variations since launch render the Moments ener-
gies more reliable, so we use these. Eventually DU2 data
with revised gain may become available as well. We also
calculate “particle characters” using the NN designed by
Dinsmore & Romani (2025), which allow a weighted re-
moval of background particles. Spurious modulation is
removed using the rescaled maps reported in Dinsmore
& Romani (2025). Boom drift was corrected using the
ixpeboomdriftcorr tool from HEAsoft version 6.33.2
(Nasa High Energy Astrophysics Science Archive Re-
search Center (Heasarc) 2014) and default parameters.
After restricting to valid good-time intervals, we cut to

2.1. Fit Method
The commonly employed PCUBE analysis uses polar-
ization extraction (Baldini et al. 2022) from events re-
constructed with the “Moments” pipeline. While this
method is an excellent tool to extract polarizations
from bright sources, it does not take full advantage of
IXPE’s measurements. Maximum likelihood techniques
can substantially improve uncertainties (Peirson et al.
2021; Marshall 2021; Gonz´alez-Caniulef et al. 2023; Ravi
et al. 2026), as does the neural net (NN) reduction of
the raw event images we employ (Peirson et al. 2021;
Cibrario et al. 2025). Advanced weighting techniques
can help separate the pulsar from the trail by position
(Dinsmore & Romani 2025), phase (Wong et al. 2023),
and energy (Vianello et al. 2015) and remove polluting
CRs (Di Marco et al. 2023; Dinsmore & Romani 2025).
Our Lighthouse analysis uses the LeakageLib pipeline
(Dinsmore & Romani 2024), which combines all these
techniques. This pipeline was validated by Dinsmore &
Romani (2025) on simulated and real observations.
LeakageLib extracts best-fit polarizations and Gaus-
sian uncertainties by maximizing the likelihood. This
allows us to test the significance of our results by calcu-
lating the delta log-likelihood ∆ln L between the best-fit
model and the appropriate null hypothesis. By Wilks’
theorem, −2∆ln L is χ2-distributed with k degrees of
freedom (DoF), where k is the DoF difference between
the model and null hypothesis (see e.g. Algeri et al.
(2020) for a review of Wilks’ theorem and its draw-
backs).
Hence, the p-values are p = ˆΓ(k/2, −∆ln L),
where ˆΓ is the normalized upper incomplete Γ-function.
2.2. Source Models
To extract polarizations, we identify the astrophysi-
cal sources (the pulsar, trail, and filament) and back-
ground sources, and we simultaneously fit for the po-
larization of each to the data.
Our spatial weights
require a high-resolution source flux model and IXPE
point-spread function (PSF) models for each DU. We
use Chandra observations as the flux model for the as-
trophysical sources (§2.3), uniform distributions for the
background sources, and the detailed PSFs measured
by Dinsmore & Romani (2024).
We phase-weight by
the IXPE pulsar light curve (§2.4) and spectral-weight
with spectra measured from the Chandra data (§2.5).
Some particle tracks are elongated, so that reconstruc-
tion algorithms assign an EVPA ψ parallel to the par-
ticle axis. The fits we report in §3 for the filament and
pulsar/trail polarizations show a preference for an asym-
metric distribution of ψ for particles, with significance
ranging from 0.9 −2.2σ depending on which source is
being fit, and whether NN or Moments reconstructed
data are used. This has also been seen for other IXPE
data sets (e.g. Sullivan et al. 2026). Though this result is
not significant, modeling it eliminates a systematic that
could otherwise pollute our measurement of the source
polarization. We therefore treat the particle track re-
constructed EVPA with a probability density function
(PDF) P(ψ) = [1 + Πpart cos(2(ψ −Ψpart))]/(2π) for
free parameters Πpart and Ψpart. Background fits give
Πpart ∼2%. There is a 1.2 −2.7σ preference for differ-
ent parameters in each detector, so we model DU1 and
DU3 independently. The photon background is also po-
larized to the ∼10% level (3.0 −4.1σ) and is polarized
differently between detectors (1.8 −3.5σ), so we sepa-
rate the photon background models as well. Bucciantini
et al. (2025) similarly saw increased flaring and photon
background polarization in DU3 compared to DU1.
2.3. Model Image Extraction
The Chandra X-ray archive contains 463 ks of ACIS
imaging of Lighthouse (the observations employed are
listed in DOI: 10.25574/cdc.576). After subtracting the
background flux using a large region to the south, we
convert these observations to the IXPE band with the
ratio of the Auxiliary Response Functions (ARF). Each
2 −8 keV Chandra event is weighted by the ratio of the
IXPE ARF (extracted using ixpecalcarf) to the Chan-
dra ARF (extracted using mkwarf for the relevant event
file and region). The standard ciao and HEAsoft tools
were used. This leaves a well-resolved, unpolarized map
of the field. We divide the image into pulsar, trail, and
filament components to be used as source flux models
for the spatial weights (see regions in Fig. 1).
For the spatial weights to be effective, the model im-
ages must be well aligned with the IXPE data. To deter-
mine the relative offset, we convolved the stacked trail

2.4. Light Curve Extraction
We phase the IXPE data with the PINT software (Luo
et al. 2021; Susobhanan et al. 2024) using the NuSTAR
/ NICER ephemeris found by Ho et al. (2022). A clear
pulse is detected in 1 −8 keV data using a simple ex-
traction aperture, though with low signal to noise and
a phase offset. Weighting each event by the IXPE PSFs
and the particle character approximately doubles the
significance of pulsations in the extracted light curve,
and the result is shown in Fig. 2. The shape is consis-
tent with previously measured low-energy light curves
(Ho et al. 2022; Tomsick et al. 2012).
Due to NuSTAR and NICER’s limited spatial resolu-
tion, the pulsar light curve will have a constant compo-
nent which cannot be separated from the trail with those
data. Fortunately, the IXPE data partially resolve the
pulsar from the trail as can be seen in the top panels of
Fig. 2: during the pulsar’s “off” phase, the trail/pulsar
complex’s center of light moves to the northeast, far-
ther from the pulsar. We may therefore use the spatial
weights to determine the light curve constant compo-
nent from the IXPE data and our Chandra model. This
is done using a simplified version of the full LeakageLib
polarimetric fit, fitting only to the observed phases and
positions of the events and ignoring polarization infor-
mation. As the light curve determines the phase weights,
the constant component flux is a free parameter of this
model. We find a final constant component of 36 ± 12%
the pulsed height of the light curve, as depicted in the
bottom panel of Fig. 2.
The constant component is too faint for direct polar-
ization measurements with these data, so we test mod-
els in which (1) it exhibits the pulsar’s polarization, (2)
it exhibits the trail polarization, and (3) it is unpolar-
ized. Model 1 is preferred over 2 and 3 by an Akaike
Information Criterion (AIC) difference of 19.2 and 5.2,
respectively. We therefore assume henceforth that the
constant component exhibits the pulsar polarization.
2.5. Spectrum Extraction
In principle, a simultaneous spectropolarization fit to
the IXPE data is possible, but it is not necessary for
this observation since Chandra’s excellent spatial and
energy resolution allows better spectral characterization
of these sources. We therefore extract spectra directly
from the Chandra data using sherpa and fix these in
the IXPE polarimetric fit. The spectra have been well
measured previously in the literature, so we do not dis-
cuss the results in detail here.
We use a power-law
model photoelectrically absorbed with tbabs. We first
fitted for the effective hydrogen column density with
the filament, trail, and pulsar data separately, giving an
uncertainty-weighted mean nH = 9.2 × 1021 cm−2. We
then fix this value and re-fit the Chandra spectra, ob-
taining photon indices Γ = 1.69(6), 2.15(4), and 1.18(5)
for the filament, trail, and pulsar respectively. The sub-
stantial difference between the pulsar and trail indices
suggests that energy weighting can help separate sig-
nal from the two sources, in addition to the spatial and
phase weights.

3. RESULTS & DISCUSSION
To study the filament polarization, we exclude all
counts near the trail and pulsar and simultaneously fit
the filament and background parameters. Results are
discussed in §3.1. As the pulsar and trail spatially over-
lap, we must simultaneously fit the pulsar, trail, and
background at once, using spatial, temporal, and energy
weights to separate the sources. The best-fit parameters
are discussed in §3.2/3.3 for the pulsar/trail.
3.1. The Filament
To test the hypothesis that the magnetic field lies tan-
gent to the filament, we had constructed an expected
PoS projection of the magnetic vector field prior to
receiving the IXPE data, tangent to the linear struc-
tures in the Chandra image (Fig. 1, right). We assign
the filament constant PD and EVPAs perpendicular to
these vectors, rotated by a global offset δΨ, and per-
form a fit. Results gave a strong polarization, with PD
Π = 56 ± 18%, below the theoretical maximum of 72%
(Eq. 1). The EVPA offset δΨ = 0.4 ± 9.3◦, measured
counterclockwise from north, indicates that the data are
consistent with the magnetic field lines drawn in Fig. 1.
The confidence intervals of this fit are shown as an offset
from the black arrow in Fig. 3a. With p-value of 0.8%,
this result surpasses the minimum detectable polariza-
tion to 99% confidence (MDP99) threshold, but without
DU2 it is not 3σ significant.
As a check, we also perform a simpler fit, with con-
stant PD and EVPA across the filament. This fit deliv-
ered Ψ = 20 ± 10◦. The blue line in Fig. 1 and lighter
contours of Fig. 3a show this result, which is consistent
with the filament angle. The PD has slightly decreased

3.2. The Pulsar
We model the pulsar polarization using the rotating
vector model (RVM; Radhakrishnan & Cooke 1969),
which describes the pulsar EVPA as emitted by a mag-
netic axis rotating steadily around the spin axis.
Its
four parameters are the spin axis-magnetic axis angle α,


## Page 7


7
the spin axis-LoS angle ζ, the phase of closest approach
between the magnetic axis and LoS ϕ0, and the EVPA
at ϕ0 Ψ. Since EVPA is perpendicular to the projected
field for synchrotron emission, we set Ψ perpendicular to
the position angle (PA) of the spin axis, which is inferred
to be −35◦from the narrow (polar jet-like) outflow seen
near the pulsar in Chandra data (Klingler et al. 2023).
This leaves three RVM parameters, to which we must
add a fourth representing pulsar average PD because
our unbinned fit method requires a PD prediction.
The best-fit pulsar parameters give a high PD of
59 ± 16%, approaching the synchrotron limit of Π0 =
64%.
Such high X-ray PD has also been inferred by
IXPE for PSR B0540−69, which has a similar pulse
profile (Xie et al. 2024).
The RVM parameters are
ϕ0 = 0.565 ± 0.043, α = 73 ± 22◦, and ζ = 83 ± 37◦,

Olmi & Bucciantini (2019) showed that for isotropic pul-
sar winds, the shock trail can exhibit a layered struc-
ture, with an outer shell of strong magnetic field paral-
lel to the trail and an inner core with a more turbulent
field, largely perpendicular to the trail. The radio im-
age seems to support this interpretation, as it contains
a long narrow trail extending farther from the pulsar
than any X-ray counterpart. This may be the remnant
perpendicular-field core after the X-ray electrons have
cooled and/or escaped to the ISM. Another possibility
is that internal Faraday rotation from fluctuating densi-
ties of cold electrons in the trail lowers the radio PD and
affects the EVPA; such depolarization is seen in other
pulsar wind nebulae (e.g. Lai et al. 2026).
Recent 0.9−1.7 GHz MeerKAT observations have de-
tected several thin streaks transverse to the trail with no
X-ray counterpart (Martin et al. 2026). These are plau-
sibly due to a low energy electron population escaping
to ISM structures generated by earlier filament injection
epochs; additional study might then shed light on the
filament formation.
