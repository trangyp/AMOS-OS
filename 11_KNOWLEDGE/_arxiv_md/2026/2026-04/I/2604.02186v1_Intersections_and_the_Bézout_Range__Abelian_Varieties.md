Intersections and the Bézout Range: Abelian Varieties
Gregorio Baldi and David Urbanik
April 3, 2026
Abstract
Given subvarieties X, Y of a complex algebraic variety S of complementary dimension,
must they intersect? When S is projective space, this is a consequence of the classical
Bézout theorem, and an analogue for simple abelian varieties was established by Barth
in 1968. Moreover, the moving lemma suggests that, after suitable translations, one
may arrange for intersections of the expected dimension.
In this work, we obtain variants for simple abelian varieties in the spirit of the
completed Zilber–Pink philosophy. When X and Y have complementary dimension,
we show that the intersections X X rnsY are zero-dimensional for all but finitely many
integers n, and that these intersections collectively give rise to an analytically dense
subset of X as n varies. We moreover control those n for which X X rnsY has a positive
dimensional component uniformly in X, Y and A. When dim X ` dim Y ă dim A, we
show that X X rnsY “ ∅for a set of integers n of asymptotic density one, except in
the presence of intersections at torsion points.

Of course, the above conclusion cannot be strengthened to assert that X and Y meet
properly, since, for instance, X might be contained in Y . Nevertheless, in analogy with
Theorem 1.4(2) and Theorem 1.3, one may ask whether there exists a natural way to “move”
Y so that one obtains a proper intersection. On an abelian variety A, one can naturally
translate by points a P A, replacing X X Y with X X pY ` aq. In this case the problem, as
well as its generalization to an arbitrary algebraic group, is fully understood as a consequence
of Kleiman’s Theorem [30]. Instead, our main interest lies in acting via group endomorphisms
of A, whose behaviour is typically more chaotic. We also plan a subsequent paper with S a
Shimura variety, where instead of endomorphisms of S one uses Hecke correspondences.
1.2
Main results
Our results come in three flavours:
- those which apply when dim X ` dim Y ě dim A, i.e., when we are “inside the Bézout
range” (see Theorem 1.8);
- those which apply when dim X `dim Y ă dim A; i.e., when we are “outside the Bézout
range” (see Theorem 1.11); and
- general considerations about uniformity in the setting of the so called geometric
Zilber–Pink conjecture (see Theorem 1.14 and Theorem 4.4).
We now describe them in detail. (We work over the complex numbers and omit the field k
from the notation.)

General background
2.1
The language of unlikely intersection problems
In unlikely intersection theory, and in particular when studying conjectures of “Zilber-Pink
type”, one usually considers a quadruple pZ, S, W, Y q, where
- the variety Z is a complex variety, the ambient variety, typically smooth and
quasi-projective;
- the set S is a collection of irreducible subvarieties of Z, with Z P S, the special
varieties;
- the set WĄ S is a larger collection of irreducible subvarieties of Z, the weakly special
varieties; and
- the variety Y Ă Z is an irreducible closed subvariety which is not contained in any
element of S other than Z itself.
One then studies intersections between Y and the special and weakly special subvarieties of
the ambient variety Z. The dimensions of these varieties should then predict when and how
often Y intersects elements of S and W.
Definition 2.1. Suppose W P W. An irreducible component C Ă Y XW is called an atypical
intersection (with Y ) if codimY C ă codimZ W. It is called a typical special intersection if
W P S, we have codimY C “ codimZ T, and C does not arise as an atypical component of
Y X W 1 for some other W 1 P S. More generally C is a typical weakly special intersection if
we have codimY C “ codimZ W, and C does not arise as an atypical component of Y X W 1
for some other W 1 P W.
Remark 2.2. The above is related to the definition of expected and unexpected components
appearing in Definition 1.1. However here it is used in a much more rigid setting, where
one is allowed to intersect only against special or weakly special subvarieties. Section 3.1 is
devoted to the study of the precise relationship between the two notions.
Definition 2.3. Given a quadruple pZ, S, W, Y q as above, a special (resp. weakly special)
subvariety of Y is an irreducible component of an intersection Y X W for W P S (resp.
W P W). We also call such subvarieties special (resp. weakly special) intersections.
A special (resp. weakly special) subvariety of Y is called strict if it is not equal to Y .
One is interested in the following two statements for varying Y :
(1) The Zilber-Pink statement: the maximal-under-inclusion strict atypical special
subvarieties of Y form a finite set.
(2) The geometric Zilber-Pink statement: the maximal-under-inclusion strict atypical
weakly special subvarieties of Y belong to finitely many algebraic families of subvarieties
of Y .
