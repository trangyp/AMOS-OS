Abstract
Information-theoretic (IT) measures are ubiquitous in artificial intelligence:
entropy drives decision-tree splits and uncertainty quantification, cross-entropy
is the default classification loss, mutual information underpins representation
learning and feature selection, and transfer entropy reveals directed influence
in dynamical systems. A second, less consolidated family of measures – inte-
grated information (Phi), effective information (EI), and autonomy – has emerged
for characterizing agent complexity. Despite wide adoption, measure selection
is often decoupled from estimator assumptions, failure modes, and safe infer-
ential claims. This paper provides a practical decision framework for all seven
measures, organized around three prescriptive questions for each: (i) what ques-
tion does the measure answer and in which AI context; (ii) which estimator is
appropriate for the data type and dimensionality; and (iii) what is the most
dangerous misuse. The framework is operationalized in two complementary
artifacts: a measure-selection flowchart and a master decision table. We cover
both AI/ML and decision-making agent application domains per measure, with
standardized Bridge Boxes linking IT quantities to cognitive constructs. Three
worked examples illustrate the framework on concrete practitioner scenarios
spanning representation learning, temporal influence analysis, and evolved agent
complexity.
Keywords: Information Theory, Mutual Information, Transfer Entropy, Integrated
Information, Decision Framework

1 Introduction
Information-theoretic (IT) measures are woven into the fabric of modern AI. Shan-
non entropy [43] drives information-gain splits in decision trees, quantifies uncertainty
in Bayesian neural networks [13], and regularizes maximum-entropy policies in rein-
forcement learning. Cross-entropy is the default training loss for classification. Mutual
information underpins self-supervised representation learning, feature selection, and
the Information Bottleneck principle [47]. Transfer entropy reveals directed infor-
mation flow in dynamical systems and multi-agent environments [40]. A second,
more specialized family of measures (integrated information Phi, effective infor-
mation EI, and autonomy) has emerged from computational neuroscience and is
finding application in the analysis of evolved artificial agents and complex adaptive
systems [3, 18].
Despite this breadth of adoption, measure selection is often decoupled from esti-
mator assumptions, failure modes, and safe inferential claims. A practitioner choosing
between KSG and MINE for mutual information is making a decision between a
measurement estimator and a training surrogate; a distinction with substantial con-
sequences for how results should be interpreted [49]. Transfer entropy is routinely
described as measuring “causality” despite being a conditional MI statistic that can
arise from shared drivers [53]. Phi and EI are sometimes applied without stating
the computational intractability of exact computation or the sensitivity of results to
grain-of-description choices [29].
This paper addresses these gaps through a practical decision framework cover-
ing all seven measures, organized around three prescriptive questions for each: (i)
what question does the measure answer and in which AI context; (ii) which estima-
tor is appropriate for the data type and dimensionality; and (iii) what is the most
dangerous misuse? Our primary contributions are two complementary artifacts: a
measure-selection flowchart (Figure 1) for rapid navigation, and a master decision
table (Section 9) that consolidates estimator recommendations and guardrails in a
single cite-able reference. Three worked examples (Section 10) demonstrate the frame-
work on concrete practitioner scenarios. We cover both AI/ML and decision-making
agent application domains per measure, and provide standardized Bridge notes linking
each IT quantity to its cognitive science counterpart.
Paper type and scope.
This is a decision-framework paper in the form of a compact narrative review, not a
systematic review (no PRISMA protocol). We cover seven measures in two families:
Family A (Entropy, KL/CE, MI, TE; core learning and inference measures with estab-
lished estimators) and Family B (Phi, EI, Autonomy; agent complexity measures with
conservative recommendations and smaller evidence bases). A Predictive Information
bridge (Section 7) connects the two families.

Critical distinction: estimator vs bound/objective.
Throughout this paper we distinguish two roles that IT quantities can play in an
algorithm. An estimator (measurement tool) approximates the true quantity from
data and is used for analysis, evaluation, or scientific inference. A bound/objective
(training surrogate) is a variational approximation used as a loss function; it may or
may not be a good measurement of the true quantity. MINE [9] and InfoNCE [51] are
lower-bound surrogates that serve as training objectives; they can yield arbitrarily
loose bounds depending on the variational family. This distinction must be stated
explicitly in any paper using IT quantities.
Data regime factors.
The choice of estimator depends critically on: (a) discrete vs continuous: plugin esti-
mators are exact for discrete data; kNN-based estimators are preferred for continuous;
(b) dimensionality: estimator variance grows exponentially with d; kNN methods
are impractical beyond d ≈20 for MI; (c) time series vs iid: TE and Predictive
Information require embedding; stationarity must be verified; (d) interventional vs
observational: EI requires full TPM under intervention; TE and MI are observational
and cannot establish interventional causality.
Two families.
We organize the seven measures into two families based on maturity of estimation
and primary application domain. Family A (core): Entropy, KL/CE, MI, TE; well-
established estimators, widely used in learning and inference pipelines, strong evidence

3 Entropy (Family A)
Definition and role.
Shannon entropy H(X) = −P
x p(x) log p(x) quantifies the average uncertainty (or
information content) of a discrete random variable X; the continuous analogue (dif-
ferential entropy) replaces the sum by an integral and can take negative values [43]. In
AI practice entropy answers the question how uncertain is this distribution?; a ques-
tion that recurs in decision-tree construction (information gain maximizes entropy
reduction) [37], exploration-exploitation tradeoffs (maximum-entropy policies [15]),
uncertainty quantification for generative models, and label-smoothing regularization.
Differential entropy additionally appears in density estimation and as the free term
in mutual information decompositions.
Applications.
Classic AI/ML: Beyond decision trees and maximum-entropy RL, entropy under-
lies uncertainty quantification in Bayesian neural networks [13] and label-smoothing
regularization in classification. Decision-making agents: In active inference, agents
minimise the entropy of their predictive distributions through both perception and
action, operationalizing the Free-Energy Principle [12, 33]; entropy of the belief state
quantifies epistemic uncertainty about world state and drives targeted exploration in
model-based agents.
Estimator choice.
For univariate (d = 1) continuous data, spacing-based estimators (e.g., the Vasicek
estimator) achieve lower bias than kNN and KDE-based alternatives [28]. For d ≥2,
spacing estimators do not exist; kNN (Kozachenko-Leonenko) and KDE are the main
options. Evidence on which dominates is mixed: Madukaife and Phuc [28] find kNN
generally inferior to KDE for d = 2, 3, 5, while Álvarez Chaves et al. [7] find kNN
tends to outperform KDE overall, especially with sufficient data. Practically, kNN
is the more robust choice because it exists for all d, requires no bandwidth selec-
tion, and its bias decays predictably as a function of k and N. Uniformization-based
approaches offer additional bias reduction by transforming data to uniform marginals
before estimation [8]. Binning (plugin) estimators are simple but introduce discretiza-
tion artifacts whose magnitude depends sensitively on the bin-width rule; they are not
recommended without explicit bias correction for small or high-dimensional samples.
Failure modes and guardrails.
Finite-sample bias is the dominant concern: all entropy estimators underestimate the
true value for small N, with bias growing rapidly in high dimensions. The standard
reporting protocol requires (i) stating whether X is discrete or continuous and how it

Prohibited claim. High model entropy does not imply subjective uncertainty; it is a

Applications.
Classic AI/ML: Knowledge distillation [16] minimizes DKL(pteacher∥pstudent) to com-

widely deployed applications of KL outside generative modeling. Decision-making
agents: In active inference, the KL between an agent’s prior and posterior belief after
an observation measures belief update magnitude; minimising expected future KL
defines epistemic value [33].
Estimator choice.
For discrete distributions with shared support, the plugin estimator is exact (up to dis-
cretization). For continuous distributions, density-ratio estimation is standard: train

5 Mutual Information (Family A)
Definition and role.
Mutual information I(X; Y ) = H(X) −H(X|Y ) = DKL(p(x, y)∥p(x)p(y)) quanti-
fies how much knowing Y reduces uncertainty about X, symmetrically. It captures
non-linear dependence that correlation misses, making it the natural tool for feature
selection (does feature X carry information about label Y ?), unsupervised represen-
tation learning via MI maximization between local and global features [17], and the
Information Bottleneck tradeoff between compression and prediction [46, 47]. Condi-
tional MI I(X; Y |Z) extends this to controlled comparisons. In cognitive neuroscience,
MI between a stimulus and a neural spike train quantifies coding efficiency; the fraction
of the response entropy that is stimulus-driven [11].

Applications.
Classic AI/ML: MI-based feature selection ranks inputs by I(Xi; Y ), capturing non-
linear relevance that linear correlation misses; whether the Information Bottleneck
compression phase actually occurs during deep network training is debated [39].
Decision-making agents: MI between an agent’s learned state representation and the
environment underpins contrastive representation learning for pixel-based RL [24];
theoretical analysis shows that not all MI objectives yield representations sufficient
for optimal control [38].
Estimator choice.
The Kraskov-Stögbauer-Grassberger (KSG) estimator [22] is the community standard
for low-to-moderate-dimensional continuous data (practical rule of thumb: d ≲20).
It uses k-nearest-neighbour statistics and achieves low bias under mild density con-
ditions; practitioners must report k and verify robustness. For high-dimensional
data, neural estimators such as MINE [9] (Donsker-Varadhan lower bound) and
InfoNCE [51] (contrastive lower bound) scale efficiently, but they are bounds/objectives
rather than measurement estimators; they are appropriate as training signals but
should not be used as unbiased MI measurements without validation [49]. For inter-
pretability analysis in latent spaces, GMM-MI [36] provides a parametric estimator
robust to discrete and continuous mixtures. When Y is categorical and X is con-
tinuous, decision-forest estimators [35] outperform KSG in high-dimensional and
mixed-scale settings by replacing fixed-radius kNN with adaptive partitions.
Failure modes and guardrails.
The curse of dimensionality is the primary hazard: KSG variance grows exponentially
with d, and neural estimators may converge to vacuously loose bounds. Leakage (data
from the test set influencing the estimate) artificially inflates MI in self-supervised
evaluation. Minimum reporting requirements are: (i) estimator family and key hyper-
parameters; (ii) robustness across k or training seeds; (iii) bootstrap variance; and (iv)
explicit statement of whether the estimate is a measurement or a training surrogate.
Bridge: Mutual Information -> Neural Coding Efficiency / Information Bottleneck
Mapping. The Information Bottleneck [47] frames representation learning as maxi-
mizing I(Z; Y ) subject to minimizing I(Z; X); MI thus provides the formal objective
for balancing expressiveness and compression. In neuroscience, I(stimulus; response)
measures how efficiently a neuron encodes its input [11].
AI use-case. Feature selection ranks inputs by I(Xi; Y ); contrastive self-supervised
learning (SimCLR, CPC) maximizes InfoNCE as a proxy for I(view1; view2), learning
invariant representations.
Prohibited claim. Higher MI does not always imply a better representation; Tschannen
et al. [49] showed that downstream task performance correlates more strongly with
encoder architecture than with MI value alone.

Classic AI/ML: Beyond recurrent network analysis, TE has established applications

human-robot interaction settings, TE has been used to modulate influence-based
rewards for implicit communication [20] and to quantify directed information flow in
collective decision-making [31].
Estimator choice.
Discrete binning is feasible for low-state-count symbolic data. For continuous time
series, kNN estimators analogous to KSG are preferred and implemented in JIDT [26]
and IDTxl [55]. The critical hyperparameter is the embedding: uniform embed-
ding uses a fixed lag l and k, while non-uniform embedding selects lags adaptively,
reducing redundancy and improving detection sensitivity [55]. For spike-train data
(event-based, continuous time), dedicated estimators that avoid binning entirely are
available [44]. IDTxl automates embedding selection via conditional independence
testing and is the recommended starting point for multivariate TE analysis.
Failure modes and guardrails.
TE is not causality in the interventional sense [27, 32]; an unobserved common driver
can induce positive TE between two processes that have no direct causal link. Station-
arity violations (trends, seasonality) produce spurious TE. The minimum reporting
protocol requires: (i) the time-lag(s) and embedding strategy with a justification of
how they were selected; (ii) the conditioning set (what was conditioned on, and why);
(iii) surrogate significance tests (e.g., time-shifted shuffles) with effect sizes; and (iv)
a discussion of plausible confounders and how they were addressed.

Bridge: Transfer Entropy -> Directed Influence / Effective Connectivity
Mapping. TE measures directed information flow in time series; “effective connectiv-

AI use-case. Inferring which modules of a multi-agent system or recurrent net-

Predictive Information Ipred(T) = I(XT
past; XT
future) measures the mutual information
between a process’s past and future over a window of length T [10]. Unlike pointwise
MI (which operates on instantaneous snapshots), predictive information captures how
much the history of a system constrains its future; a quantity that grows with the
complexity and memory of the process and saturates for simple Markovian sources.
Connection to Family A.
Predictive information is a temporal MI; all the estimation methods of Section 5 (KSG
for low dimensions, neural bounds for high dimensions) apply, with the additional
requirement that embedding length T be selected carefully (cf. Section 6). Bialek
et al. [10] showed that Ipred(T) grows roughly logarithmically for systems at the
edge of predictability, connecting it to the Information Bottleneck tradeoff between
compression and prediction [46, 47].
Connection to Family B.
For agent systems, Ipred serves as a principled measure of the timescale over which an
agent can exploit its history. Agents with high predictive information store more of the
past that is relevant to the future; a property consistent with deliberate, goal-directed
behavior. This motivates the Family B measures (Phi, EI, Autonomy in Section 8),
which ask not just how much a system stores and transmits, but how integrated and
causally self-determining that storage is.
Practical note.
Computing Ipred requires specifying a timescale T and a data-type-appropriate estima-
tor. For symbolic or low-dimensional continuous processes, KSG or plugin estimators
are standard. Surrogate testing (as for TE) is recommended to establish significance.
Toolboxes JIDT [26] and IDTxl [55] support the underlying MI estimation.

8 Beyond Shannon: Agent Complexity Measures
(Family B)
Family B measures characterize system-level properties (integration, causal structure,
and self-determination) that cannot be read off from pairwise or temporal MI alone.
Their evidence base is smaller than Family A, their computational demands are sub-
stantially higher, and their interpretive claims require additional caution. We treat
them as a module for practitioners working on agent complexity, not as universally
recommended tools.
8.1 Integrated Information (Phi)
Definition and role.
Integrated information Phi quantifies the degree to which the whole of a system gen-
erates more information than the sum of its parts, over its minimum information
partition (MIP) [6, 48]. Formally, Phi is computed over a transition probability matrix
(TPM) defining the system’s cause-effect structure; the IIT 4.0 formulation [6] refines
earlier versions by operating over cause-effect power rather than statistical overlap.
Systems with high Phi have irreducible causal integration: no cut of the system into
subsystems can account for all its transitions. In AI, Phi has been applied to ana-
lyze evolved agents (Markov Brains, animats) that develop neural architectures under
selective pressure [1], providing a measure of how integrated their information process-
ing is. IIT was originally developed as a neuroscientific theory of consciousness [48];
in AI and agent research, Phi is used strictly as a measure of causal integration, inde-
pendent of phenomenal claims. Several variants of Phi have been proposed; they can
produce different orderings of the same systems [4, 30, 45].
Applications.
Decision-making agents: Phi has been applied to evolved Markov Brain agents (ani-
mats) to show that exposure to environments of increasing complexity drives higher
causal integration [1, 5], linking evolutionary selection to information integration.
Classic AI/ML: Direct use in standard ML pipelines is limited by the NP-hard
computation; Phi is primarily an agent-analysis tool.
Estimator choice.
The PyPhi toolbox [29] computes exact Phi for small discrete systems (feasible up to
∼8 nodes for binary systems). Exact computation is NP-hard; several approximate
measures and partitioning strategies exist [45] but can diverge from true Phi [30].
Practitioners should use the smallest system that captures the phenomenon of interest
and validate that approximate measures reproduce qualitative patterns on small exact-
computation benchmarks.

Failure modes and guardrails.
Phi can be non-zero for systems that clearly lack the properties it is intended to
capture (e.g., expander graphs) [45]. Different Phi variants can order systems differ-
ently [30], so the specific formulation must be stated. Phi computation requires full
knowledge of the system’s TPM and is sensitive to the grain of description (time
step, spatial scale). Claims based on Phi should be framed as characterizations of
information integration rather than as claims about consciousness or sentience.
Bridge: Phi -> Information Integration / Neural Binding
Mapping. IIT proposes that Phi operationalizes a physical substrate for integrated
experience; in AI contexts, Phi serves as a complexity measure of how irreducibly
integrated an agent’s information processing is [48].
AI use-case. Measuring Phi of evolved Markov Brain agents to assess how selection
pressure shapes neural integration; comparing Phi before and after learning to track
architectural change.
Prohibited claim. High Phi does not imply consciousness or sentience; it is a math-
ematical property of a TPM, not a direct empirical finding about phenomenal
experience.

Estimator choice.
EI has no observational-data estimator: it requires the full transition probability
matrix under interventional (do-calculus) distributions, making it fundamentally dif-
ferent from TE and MI, which operate on trajectory data alone. For small discrete
systems (≤8 binary nodes), EI is computed directly from the interventional TPM
using PyPhi [29] or custom matrix operations. For larger systems, the coarse-graining
search adds combinatorial overhead on top of the TPM computation; there is currently
no validated approximate estimator.
Failure modes and guardrails.
EI requires interventional data (knowledge of the full TPM under do-Xt), not merely
observational trajectories; this distinguishes it from TE and limits its application to
systems where interventional access or a fully specified model is available. The choice
of coarse-graining scheme affects the EI value; causal emergence claims should be
reported alongside the specific macro-grouping and justified by prior knowledge or
systematic search.
Bridge: Effective Information -> Causal Abstraction / Macro-level Modeling
Mapping. EI formalizes when a higher-level causal model is more informative than
the substrate; relevant to AI debates about abstraction layers in neural networks and
when rule-based macro models outperform neural micro-models [18].
AI use-case. Evaluating whether a high-level policy description (state abstractions,
option frameworks) has greater causal coherence than a raw action-level description
of agent behavior.
Prohibited claim. Causal emergence (macro EI > micro EI) does not imply that the
macro level is the “true” ontology; it depends on the chosen coarse-graining and the
interventional distribution.

streams) to quantify how self-driven the dynamics are; this use remains exploratory.
Estimator choice.
Two variants require different data access. The observational measure Am is esti-
mated from activity time series by computing the conditional entropy reduction
H(Vt|Et−1:t−m) −H(Vt|Vt−1, Et−1:t−m) from trajectory data; this is available for
any observed system but conflates internal self-determination with external corre-
lations [2]. The causal variant ˆAS
1 = EI(V ) requires the full interventional TPM
and is computed via custom matrix operations or the autonomy toolbox associated
with Albantakis [2].
Failure modes and guardrails.
Autonomy values are sensitive to the system boundary definition (which nodes are
“internal”), the number m of conditioning steps, and the time step of the TPM [2].
Observational (Am) and causal ( ˆAS
m) variants can disagree substantially: the observa-
tional measure conflates external correlations with internal self-determination, while
the causal variant requires full interventional access to the TPM. Autonomy should
not be interpreted as “free will” or subjective agency; it is a mathematical property
of the TPM and boundary definition.
Bridge: Autonomy -> Agency / Self-determination
Mapping. Autonomy operationalizes the degree to which an agent is self-causing
rather than externally driven [2]; this maps onto informal notions of self-
determination and goal-directedness in cognitive science and philosophy of agency.
AI use-case. Evolved Markov Brain agents can be ranked by Am and ˆASm to distin-
guish internally-driven from stimulus-driven architectures; structurally diverse agents
solving the same task differ substantially on causal but not always observational
autonomy measures [2].
Prohibited claim. Autonomy depends on boundary and time-lag choices and should
not be reported as an intrinsic scalar property of an agent without specifying
those parameters; observational and causal variants measure different aspects of
self-determination.

10 Worked Examples
The following scenarios illustrate how the flowchart (Figure 1) and decision table
(Table 1) guide measure selection from objective to reporting. Each scenario follows
the same protocol: state the objective, apply the framework, identify estimator and
hyperparameters, and state the key guardrail.
Scenario 1: Evaluating representation quality in contrastive learning.
A practitioner trains a SimCLR-style contrastive model on an image dataset and
wants to quantify how much information the learned embedding Z ∈R128 retains
about the downstream class label Y . The objective is statistical dependence between
a high-dimensional continuous variable and a categorical one.
Framework output. Flowchart branch: detect dependence; data = continu-
ous/mixed; d = 128 ≫20. Decision table: KSG is infeasible at this dimensionality;
neural estimators (MINE, InfoNCE) are available but classified as training surrogates,
not measurement estimators. The recommended protocol is: (i) use InfoNCE as the
training objective; (ii) for post-hoc measurement, project Z to d ≤15 via PCA retain-
ing > 95% variance, then apply KSG with k ∈{3, 5, 10} and report sensitivity; (iii)
apply the decision-forest estimator [35] for the mixed-scale case (Z continuous, Y
categorical). Report all three values and note any discrepancy between the InfoNCE
bound and the KSG estimate.
Key guardrail. MINE and InfoNCE are lower bounds; a high InfoNCE value does
not imply high MI, and downstream task performance correlates more strongly with
encoder architecture than with the bound value [49]. Explicitly state in the paper
whether the reported figure is a measurement or a training surrogate.
Scenario 2: Inferring directed influence in a multi-agent system.
A researcher records m = 5 continuous sensor streams from autonomous robots (each
stream length T = 500 steps, sampling rate 10 Hz) and wants to determine which
agents drive the behavior of others at a lag of 1–3 time steps.
Framework output. Flowchart branch: directed temporal influence; sources = mul-
tivariate (m = 5). Decision table: multivariate TE with IDTxl [55]. Recommended
configuration: non-uniform embedding with maximum lag lmax = 5; condition on the
joint past of all other agents to avoid spurious links; run 200 time-shifted surrogate
shuffles per directed pair to establish a null distribution; report effect size (TE value
normalized by the marginal entropy of the target) alongside the surrogate p-value.
With m = 5 streams, m(m −1) = 20 directed pairs are tested; apply Bonferroni or
FDR correction.
Key guardrail. Positive TE does not establish interventional causality [32]; a shared
environmental driver (e.g., a common obstacle) can produce non-zero TE between
agents with no direct influence link. Report which confounders were considered and
whether conditioning on a third stream eliminates the detected link.

Scenario 3: Comparing evolved agent architectures.
An evolutionary robotics researcher evolves two Markov Brain populations on a for-
aging task (n = 6 binary nodes each) and wants to determine which architecture
achieves higher causal integration and self-determination, and whether the difference
is attributable to internal structure or environmental reactivity.
Framework output. Flowchart branch: agent/system complexity. Decision table:
Family B; n = 6 ≤8 binary nodes, exact computation feasible with PyPhi [29].
Recommended protocol: (i) compute Phi (IIT 4.0) on the full 6-node subsystem to
quantify irreducible causal integration; (ii) compute observational Autonomy Am from
trajectory data as a preliminary screen (requires only activity logs, no interventions);
(iii) compute causal Autonomy ˆAS
1 = EI(V ) from the interventional TPM to separate
genuine self-determination from externally correlated responses. Compare populations
on all three metrics; discordance between Am and ˆAS
1 indicates that one architec-
ture’s apparent self-determination is driven by environmental correlation rather than
internal causal structure.
Key guardrail. State the IIT version, system boundary, and time step; different
versions can reverse the ranking between architectures [30]. Phi and Autonomy are
properties of the TPM and boundary definition, not evidence of consciousness or
subjective agency. If approximate Phi was used for screening, validate on the exact
value for at least a representative subset before drawing conclusions.
11 Discussion
When not to use IT measures.
Several recurrent anti-patterns deserve explicit warning. First, do not use TE or MI
as evidence of interventional causality without controlling for latent confounders and
running surrogate tests; both measures are observational and can be non-zero under
purely correlational data-generating processes. Second, do not apply kNN MI estima-
tors to high-dimensional data (d ≫20) without dimensionality reduction or careful
variance assessment; estimates in these regimes are dominated by the curse of dimen-
sionality. Third, do not silently use MINE or InfoNCE as MI measurements rather than
training surrogates; this conflation is common in self-supervised learning papers and
leads to uncalibrated claims about representation quality [49]. Fourth, do not compute
Phi without stating the IIT version, system size, and whether exact or approximate
computation was used; different versions produce qualitatively different orderings [30].
Finally, KL divergence should not be treated as a distance (it is not symmetric) or
applied when distributions have disjoint support.
Open problems.
Reliable estimation of mutual information in high dimensions (d > 50) remains
unsolved: neural estimators are biased and variance-prone; parametric models are mis-
specified; and there is no agreed-upon benchmark for continuous high-dimensional MI
estimation. Scalable computation of Phi beyond 20 nodes requires algorithmic break-
throughs; current approximations diverge in unpredictable ways. TE estimation under
non-stationary and non-linear conditions with latent confounders is an active area
19


## Page 20


without consensus methods. Autonomy as defined for binary PyPhi systems has not
yet been extended to graded or multi-valued systems in a validated way. Future work
integrating IT measures with causal graphical models [34] may close the gap between
observational and interventional IT analysis.
12 Conclusion
We have presented a practical decision framework covering seven IT measures across
two families. Family A measures (Entropy, KL/CE, MI, TE) have mature estimators
and strong empirical support; their primary failure modes are estimator mismatch,
high-dimensional variance, and over-claiming causality. Family B measures (Phi, EI,
Autonomy) characterize agent complexity with smaller evidence bases and stringent
computational requirements; conservative claims framed as properties of the TPM
rather than properties of consciousness or free will are essential. The Predictive Infor-
mation bridge (Section 7) provides a principled path from temporal MI to agent-level
complexity analysis.
The measure-selection flowchart (Figure 1) and master decision table (Table 1)
are the primary artifacts of this framework: complementary cite-able references
for selecting, estimating, and reporting IT measures. We recommend that practi-
tioners cross-reference the table against the appropriate measure section for a full
account of failure modes and guardrails before reporting results. Measures not covered
here (Renyi/Tsallis entropy, directed information, partial information decomposition)
may be addressed in a companion or extended framework as their estimation and
application consensus matures.
