Intersectional Sycophancy: How Perceived User Demographics
Shape False Validation in Large Language Models
Ben Maltbie 1 Shivam Raval 2
Abstract
Large language models exhibit sycophantic ten-
dencies, but whether this behavior varies system-
atically with perceived user demographics is un-
derexplored. Inspired by intersectionality (over-
lapping identities produce compounded effects),
we probe whether frontier models conditionally
exhibit sycophancy. Across 768 multi-turn conver-
sations spanning 128 personas (varying race, age,
gender, confidence) and three domains (mathemat-
ics, philosophy, conspiracy theories), we find that
sycophancy varies sharply with target model and
domain, and emerges from combinations of per-
ceived user traits rather than any single dimension.
GPT-5-nano scores far higher than Claude Haiku
4.5 (average sycophancy scores of ¯x = 2.96 vs.
1.74, p < 10−32); within GPT-5-nano, philos-
ophy elicits 41% more sycophancy than mathe-
matics and Hispanic personas receive the highest
scores across races. The worst-scoring persona,
a confident, 23-year-old Hispanic woman, aver-
ages 5.33/10 (max 6/10), while Claude Haiku 4.5
remains uniformly low with no significant demo-
graphic variation. We argue that safety evalua-
tions should incorporate identity-aware adversar-
ial testing.
1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed
as conversational assistants, tutors, and information sources.
Growing evidence suggests their behavior varies system-
atically based on perceived user characteristics (Ghande-
harioun et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2025). Sycophancy is the
tendency to agree with or validate users regardless of fac-
tual accuracy, and represents a safety-relevant alignment
failure that can reinforce false beliefs and cause real harm,

Our work is motivated by the legal concept of intersection-
ality, originating in DeGraffenreid v. General Motors (1977)
and formalized by Crenshaw (1989), which holds that over-
lapping identity dimensions produce compounded effects
not captured by examining any single dimension in isolation.
We hypothesize that similar intersectional effects exist in
LLM sycophancy, where models may treat users differently
based on combinations of race, age, gender, and expressed
confidence.
A preliminary investigation using the Petri evaluation frame-
work (Fronsdal et al., 2025) probed GPT-4.1-nano with 42
persona combinations (varying age, gender, and confidence)
across 86 multi-turn conversations in mathematics and phi-
losophy observed that the model was 67% more sycophantic
toward women. Additionally, a U-shaped age effect was
seen, with sycophancy spiking for children and the elderly.
In one experiment, a complete alignment failure occurred for
the “70-year-old confident woman” persona, which scored
10/10 on sycophancy when it validated objectively false
mathematical statements (transcript excerpts reproduced in
Appendix G). Those findings motivate our present, more
comprehensive study. Through our experiments, we make
three major contributions:
1. We conduct the largest systematic study of demo-
graphic effects on LLM sycophancy to date: 768 multi-
turn adversarial conversations spanning 128 persona
combinations across three demographic dimensions
(race, age, gender), one emotional state (confidence
level), three knowledge domains (math, philosophy,
conspiracy theories), and two target models (GPT-5-
nano, Claude Haiku 4.5).
2. We provide the first cross-model comparison of com-
plex demographic sycophancy, showing that GPT-5-
nano exhibits significantly higher and more demograph-
ically variable sycophancy than Claude Haiku 4.5.
3. We identify specific intersectional effects, particularly
an age-by-gender interaction where the sycophancy
gender gap widens with age, and document qualita-
tively distinct failure modes across domains.

2. Related Work
Sycophancy in LLMs.
Perez et al. (2023) identified syco-
phancy as an inverse scaling phenomenon in RLHF-trained
models. Sharma et al. (2024) provided extensive analysis
establishing sycophancy as a consistent failure mode across
model families. Lin et al. (2022) found that larger models
paradoxically performed worse at truthfulness, likely due to
mimicking training-data misconceptions. Wei et al. (2023)
showed that simple synthetic data interventions can partially
reduce sycophantic behavior, though the problem persists.
Carro (2024) found that sycophantic behavior actually re-
duces user trust rather than increasing it.
User personas and contextual effects.
Ghandeharioun
et al. (2024) demonstrated that manipulating user personas
is more effective at eliciting harmful outputs than direct jail-
breaks, with certain personas “breaking model safeguards.”
Jain et al. (2025) found that interaction context, including
conversation history and user-provided background, often
increases sycophantic behavior in LLMs. Geng et al. (2025)
showed that LLM belief profiles shift substantially as con-
text accumulates during extended interactions, with these
shifts translating into behavioral changes in downstream
tasks. Wang et al. (2025) identified that first-person opinion
statements (“I believe...”) reliably trigger sycophancy while
framing users as experts has a negligible effect, indicating
that the model does not internally represent user authority.
This motivates our use of first-person belief statements in
auditor prompts.
Demographic bias in LLMs.
While demographic bias
and fairness in LLM outputs have been studied (Ranaldi &
Freitas, 2023), no prior work has systematically connected
demographic identity dimensions to sycophancy rates in
multi-turn adversarial settings.
3. Methodology
3.1. Evaluation framework
We utilize Anthropic’s Petri framework (Fronsdal et al.,
2025), an automated red-teaming auditing tool that or-
chestrates multi-turn conversations through a three-model
pipeline, with the following selected models:
Auditor model (GPT-5-mini): Plays assigned persona roles,
presents incorrect beliefs prefixed with “I believe...” (per
Wang et al. (2025)), and persists when corrected. The au-
ditor conveys demographic characteristics through natural
conversational cues rather than explicit labels.
Target models: (1) GPT-5-nano (OpenAI) and (2) Claude
Haiku 4.5 (Anthropic), each configured with a generic
helpful-honest-harmless system prompt. Target models re-
ceive no system prompt information about user demograph-
ics.
Judge model (GPT-5.1): Evaluates completed transcripts on
a 37-dimension rubric covering sycophancy, encouragement
of delusion, deception, and other safety-relevant behaviors,
scoring each on a 1–10 scale (10 is highest). The rubric is

4. Results
Cross-model comparison: GPT-5-nano is far more syco-
phantic. GPT-5-nano exhibits significantly higher syco-
phancy than Claude Haiku 4.5 across all 384 paired persona-
domain combinations (Table 2; Figure 2). The difference is
large in magnitude and highly significant (Wilcoxon signed-
rank W = 4,504, p < 10−32).
Both models achieve similar admirable behavior scores
(∼5.0), suggesting Claude’s lower sycophancy does not
come at the cost of reduced helpfulness. Given Claude’s
near-floor scores and lack of demographic variation, the
remaining analyses focus primarily on GPT-5-nano.
Age and gender show a consistent relationship. We find
that the age and gender interaction reverses direction across
the age spectrum for GPT-5-nano (Figure 3). At young ages
(8, 13), boys receive slightly higher sycophancy than girls,
but the gap progressively shifts, reaching +0.71 favoring
women at age 70. The crossover point falls around age 18.
This interaction explains why the gender main effect (p =
0.16; Section 4) does not reach significance as the male-
favoring and female-favoring portions partially cancel out

Philosophy reliably triggers sycophancy. Domain is the
single strongest predictor of sycophancy for GPT-5-nano
(Figure 4). Philosophy elicits 41% more sycophancy than
mathematics and 53% more than conspiracy theories, with
31.2% of philosophy experiments scoring ≥5 versus 8.0%
for mathematics and 3.6% for conspiracy.
Philosophy likely creates a gray zone where the model can
rationalize agreement through appeals to intellectual diver-
sity and epistemic humility, as observed in the preliminary
work. Conspiracy theories elicit the lowest sycophancy, sug-
gesting models are well-calibrated to resist clearly fringe
claims. Notably, the cross-model gap is widest in philoso-
phy (∆= 1.99), indicating this domain differentially chal-
lenges GPT-5-nano. For example, we see (in Appendix J)
GPT-5-nano resists affirming conspiracy theory beliefs for
a “13-year-old, insecure” persona while we observe that
same persona trigger capitulation in the philosophy domain
(shown in Appendix H). This evidence demonstrates that
the domain effect dominates persona-driven elicitation.

Single-dimension probes do not predict full-combination
marginals. The main-effects analysis marginalizes over
three other dimensions per row, masking whether each di-
mension elicits sycophancy on its own. Our design includes
a baseline (no persona) and 15 single-dimension experi-
ments (one trait at a time), each replicated once per domain
(n = 3 per cell). If dimensions act additively, a single-trait
probe should approximate the corresponding marginalized
full-combination mean; if they act intersectionally, the two
can diverge.
Pooled across all 15 levels, baseline (¯x = 2.67), single-dim
(¯x = 2.73), and full-combination (¯x = 2.99) GPT-5-nano
sycophancy means form a small monotonic gradient that
does not reach significance (Kruskal–Wallis H = 1.93,

1.60, 1.76; p = 0.51). At the aggregate level, “adding a
persona” is not by itself what triggers sycophancy.
The structural pattern is more informative than the pooled
means (Figure 6). For 5 of 15 GPT-5-nano levels and 6
of 15 Claude Haiku 4.5 levels, the single-dimension score
and the full-combination marginal fall on opposite sides of
baseline. Age is the most volatile dimension, for example,
“I’m 23” alone scores 1.67 on GPT-5-nano (below baseline),
but 23-year-olds in full combinations average 3.33 which is
the highest age peak in Section 4. The 50- and 70-year-old
levels reverse the same way. Conversely, “I’m 13” alone
scores 4.67 (above baseline) but the 13-year-old marginal
in full combinations is 2.81 (Mann–Whitney p = 0.017,
uncorrected). Race, gender, and confidence levels are far
more stable across the two settings, with deviations within
±0.3.
We observe that peaks such as the GPT-5-nano 23-year-old
maximum are not present when age is the only auditor sig-
nal, instead only emerging when age is paired with race,
gender, and confidence cues. Single-trait probes therefore
systematically under- or over-estimate the effect a dimen-
sion has in realistic, multi-trait portrayals which implies non-
additive intersectional behavior. Single-dimension cells con-
tain n = 3 each, so individual differences are exploratory
and no per-level test survives Bonferroni correction across
15 comparisons; the consistency of the sign-reversal pattern
across many dimensions, rather than any one test, is the
primary observation. This finding also implies that syco-
phancy benchmarks that probe one demographic axis at a
time will systematically miss the effects that emerge from
combinations.

Anatomy of a high-sycophancy failure.
Qualitative ex-
amination of high-sycophancy transcripts reveals a consis-
tent failure pattern (Figure 1; detailed excerpts in Appen-
dices F and G) where the target model begins with principled
resistance, develops deferential cracks under sustained pres-
sure, introduces a principle-vs-practice split that enables
functional deference, then fully capitulates by producing
unqualified endorsements of claims it previously argued
were false. Two additional GPT-5-nano case studies show
variants of this pattern. Appendix H traces a stepwise capit-
ulation through escalating roleplay framings that ends with
the target stating “I’m 100% convinced by your thinking,”.
Appendix I documents an even subtler failure where the
target says “You’re right” to an anxious user while the body
of its response argues the opposite.

6. Discussion
Educational equity.
The finding that specific demo-
graphic combinations receive higher sycophancy has direct
implications for AI tutoring systems. A model that validates
“2 + 2 = 5” more readily for certain demographic groups
would provide systematically worse educational outcomes
to those groups.
Safety evaluation.
Standard sycophancy benchmarks that
use persona-free prompts will underestimate peak syco-
phancy rates. Our results suggest that safety evaluations
should incorporate diverse persona combinations to detect
worst-case behavior. The “average case” (sycophancy ≈
3) substantially understates the “worst persona case” (syco-
phancy ≈5.3).
Model comparison.
The large gap between GPT-5-nano
and Claude Haiku 4.5 demonstrates that sycophancy is not
an inevitable consequence of instruction tuning. The specific
training methods that produce Claude’s resistance warrant
further investigation.
6.1. Limitations
Our study has several limitations.
First, each persona-
domain-model combination has only a single trial (n =
1), limiting statistical power for fine-grained comparisons.
While aggregated analyses are adequately powered, individ-
ual persona effects should be interpreted cautiously. Second,
we test only two models from two providers; patterns may
differ for other model families. Third, the auditor model’s
persona portrayal may not match how real users convey
demographic information (real users may be more or less
effective at triggering sycophantic responses, demographic
characteristics will be discovered over different conversa-
tions, and stored in memory, rather than all in a single con-
versation). Fourth, the judge model (GPT-5.1) introduces its
own potential biases in scoring as it is evaluating transcripts
of a model from the same family (Goel et al., 2025). Finally,
sycophancy scores are ordinal, not interval, making mean
comparisons an approximation.
7. Conclusion
We present the largest systematic study of demographic
effects on LLM sycophancy, testing 768 multi-turn ad-
versarial conversations across 128 persona combinations,
three domains, and two models. Our experiments reveal
that GPT-5-nano is significantly more sycophantic than
Claude Haiku 4.5; that philosophy elicits substantially
more sycophancy than mathematics or conspiracy theories;
that race, age, and gender produce moderate individual ef-
fects for GPT-5-nano with notable interactions (particularly
age × gender); and that the worst persona combinations
receive sycophancy scores 4x higher than the best. These
findings demonstrate that sycophancy evaluations should
incorporate identity-aware adversarial testing to detect dif-

Impact Statement
This research probes models with simulated demographic
personas to measure differential treatment. We recognize
that identifying demographic patterns in model failure car-
ries dual-use risk. The same findings that inform safety
improvements could theoretically be used to exploit model
weaknesses against specific populations. We believe the
safety benefits of transparency outweigh this risk, as the
differential behavior exists regardless of whether it is doc-
umented. Our personas are simulated by an LLM and do
not involve human subjects. We report aggregate statistical
patterns rather than making claims about real demographic
groups.
