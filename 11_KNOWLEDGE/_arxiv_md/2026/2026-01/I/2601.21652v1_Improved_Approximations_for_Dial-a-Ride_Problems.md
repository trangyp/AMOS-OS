Abstract
The multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem (mDaRP) is a fundamental vehicle routing problem
with pickups and deliveries, widely applicable in ride-sharing, economics, and transportation.
Given a set of n locations, h vehicles of identical capacity λ located at various depots, and
m ride requests each defined by a source and a destination, the goal is to plan non-preemptive
routes that serve all requests while minimizing the total travel distance, ensuring that no vehicle
carries more than λ passengers at any time. The best-known approximation ratio for the mDaRP
remains O(
√
λ log m).
We propose two simple algorithms:
the first achieves the same approximation ratio of
O(
√
λ log m) with improved running time, and the second attains an approximation ratio of
O(p m
λ ). A combination of them yields an approximation ratio of O( 4√n log
1
2 n) under m =
Θ(n). Moreover, for the case m ≫n, by extending our algorithms, we derive an O(√n log n)-
approximation algorithm, which also improves the current best-known approximation ratio of
O(√n log2 n) for the classic (single-vehicle) DaRP, obtained by Gupta et al.
(ACM Trans.
Algorithms, 2010).

Introduction
In the multi-vehicle dial-a-ride problem (mDaRP) (Luo et al. 2023), we are given a complete graph
G = (V, E, w) with |V | = n locations, a weight function w : E →R+, a set of h identical vehicles
K ⊆V , and a (multi-)set of m requests R = {(si, ti)}m
i=1 ⊆V × V . All vehicles have the same
capacity of λ ∈N+. The goal is to plan routes for vehicles to satisfy all requests, while minimizing
the total travel distance. Each vehicle is not required to return to its starting point after completing
its assigned requests. Moreover, for each vehicle, the number of requests served on its route must
never exceed its capacity at any point in time. We assume that the edge-weight function w is a
metric, satisfying symmetry and the triangle inequality; that is, for any a, b, c ∈V , w(a, a) = 0 and
w(a, c) = w(c, a) ≤w(a, b) + w(b, c). When h = 1, the mDaRP is known as the classic dial-a-ride
problem (DaRP) (Stein 1978, Psaraftis 1980, Cordeau 2006, Ho et al. 2018).
In the mDaRP, each request (si, ti) corresponds to an item that needs to be transported from
the source si to the designated destination ti. Based on the properties of requests, two versions are
commonly considered in the literature: the preemptive version and the non-preemptive version. In
the preemptive version, after picking up an item at its source, the vehicle is allowed to temporarily
drop off the item at some intermediate vertices before delivering it to its destination. In the non-
preemptive version, once the vehicle picks up an item at its source, it must carry the item until it
reaches its destination without any intermediate drop-offs.
As in the previous studies (Gupta et al. 2010, Luo et al. 2023), we study the non-preemptive
mDaRP.

Related Work
We briefly review approximation algorithms for the DaRP and the mDaRP, respectively.
DaPR. When λ = 1, the DaRP is also known as the stacker-crane problem (SCP) (Frederickson
et al. 1976, 1978). Furthermore, if the source and destination of each request are the same, the
SCP further reduces to the traveling salesman problem (TSP). For the TSP, Christofides (2022)
proposed a 3
2-approximation algorithm. Recently, Karlin et al. (2021, 2023) improved the ratio to
3
2 −10−36. For the SCP, Frederickson et al. (1976, 1978) proposed a well-known 9
5-approximation
algorithm, which remains the best-known to date.
When all requested objects are identical, the DaRP becomes the λ-delivery TSP (Anily and
Bramel 1999, Chalasani and Motwani 1999, Charikar et al. 2001). Furthermore, when all request
sources coincide with the vehicle depot, it becomes the well-known capacitated vehicle routing
problem (CVRP) (Dantzig and Ramser 1959). Approximation algorithms for the CVRP can be
found in (Haimovich and Kan 1985, Altinkemer and Gavish 1987, Blauth et al. 2023, Friggstad et al.
2022). Although the DaRP is a fundamental vehicle routing problem in Operations Research (Ho
et al. 2018), research on approximation algorithms is relatively limited.
When λ ≥1, the 9
5-approximation algorithm for the case λ = 1 (Frederickson et al. 1978)
directly implies an O(λ)-approximation algorithm. Charikar and Raghavachari (1998) were the
first to propose novel approximation algorithms.
For the non-preemptive case, they obtain an
approximation ratio of O(
√
λ log n log log n). For the preemptive case, they obtain an approximation
ratio of O(log n log log n). Their approaches are based on the tree-embedding technique in (Bartal
1998), which randomly embeds general metric spaces into tree metrics with an expected distortion
of O(log n log log n). This distortion bound was later improved to O(log n) (Fakcharoenphol et al.
2004), which is asymptotically tight. Hence, the corresponding approximation ratios improve to
O(
√
λ log n) for the non-preemptive case, and O(log n) for the preemptive case.
Later, Gupta et al. (2007, 2010) introduced the λ-Steiner forest technique (Segev and Segev
2010), which greedily covers all requests using minimum-ratio λ-Steiner forests, as the greedy
algorithm for the λ-set cover problem (Williamson and Shmoys 2011). They mainly focused on the
non-preemptive case and achieved an approximation ratio of O(min{
√
λ, √n} · log2 n). They also
showed that their techniques extend to more general DaRP settings, including cases where some
requests require more than one unit of vehicle capacity.
Approximation algorithms for the non-preemptive DaRP on paths have also been studied.
Krumke et al. (2000) proposed a 3-approximation algorithm, which was later improved to 2.5
by Hu (2009).
In the literature, there exist few results on the inapproximability for the DaRP. For the TSP,
Karpinski et al. (2015) proved that there is no 123
122-approximation algorithm unless P=NP. Although
the non-preemptive DaRP appears unlikely to admit an O(1)-approximation ratio, the TSP lower
bound remains the best hardness result. For the preemptive DaRP, Gørtz (2006) proved a super-
constant lower bound of Ω(min{log
1
4 −ε n, λ1−ε}) for any constant ε > 0 under the assumption
that all problems in NP cannot be solved by randomized algorithms with expected running time
O(npolylog n).
mDaRP. The mDaRP is a natural generalization of the DaRP. For the non-preemptive mDaRP,
Luo et al. (2023) proposed an O(
√
λ log m)-approximation algorithm that runs in O(m3 log λ +

Unlike previous approximation algorithms for
the DaRP, their approach uses an iterative pairing strategy based on minimum-weight matching,
grouping requests before planning routes.
For the preemptive mDaRP, Gørtz et al. (2009, 2015) showed that an O(log n)-approximation
algorithm can be obtained by extending the O(log n)-approximation algorithm for the preemptive

For the mDaRP, we design new approximation algorithms. The contributions are summarized as
follows.
1. We first propose two simple approximation algorithms that are particularly practical when
m = Θ(n). The first achieves an approximation ratio of O(

2. Although we may have m, λ ≫n, there are at most n(n −1) number of distinct requests.
Building upon our two previous algorithms and the techniques from (Gupta et al. 2010), we
give our third algorithm for the mDaRP, which achieves a ratio of O(√n log n). Notably, this
result improves upon the best-known ratio of O(√n log2 n) for the DaRP (Gupta et al. 2007,
2010), which has remained unchanged for nearly two decades.
Our algorithms are based on new structural properties that are also valuable for related DaRPs.
Specifically, the first algorithm is built on a simple metric graph in which each request is represented
by a single vertex, thereby enabling us to use techniques from the well-known CVRP (recall that
in which, each request involves a single location). The second algorithm uses a simple yet elegant
decomposition of a Steiner forest. Although these ideas are surprisingly simple, they yield strong
theoretical guarantees through careful analysis.

Preliminaries
Let G = (V, E, w) denote the input complete graph with |V | = n and edge-weight function w :
E →R+. We call G a metric graph if w satisfies the metric properties defined earlier. In this
case, G can also be viewed as a metric space (V, w). There is a (multi-)set of m requests R =
{(si, ti)}m
i=1 ⊆V × V . Each request {si, ti} ∈R corresponds to an item that needs to be picked up
from the source si and delivered to the destination ti. We let X = {si}m
i=1 and Y = {ti}m
i=1 denote
the sets of all sources and destinations, respectively. Furthermore, there is a set of h vehicles, each
with the same capacity λ ∈N+. Their initial locations are given by K = {oi}h
i=1 ⊆V .
We assume that every vertex in V is either an initial location of a vehicle or the source or
destination of a request; otherwise, since G is a metric graph, such a vertex can be removed. For
ease of analyzing the running time of our algorithms, we also assume m = Ω(n), following (Luo et al.
2023). Under this assumption, for example, the running time may be expressed simply as O(m2);

In what follows, we work with multi-edge sets, where the union of two edge sets is taken with

For any edge set Es ⊆E′, define w′(Es) := P

A walk W = v1v2 . . . vs in G′ is a sequence of vertices (vertices may repeat), corresponding to
the edge set E′(W) := {v1v2, v2v3, . . . , vs−1vs}. A tour T = v1v2 . . . vs is similarly a sequence of
vertices, but with the edge set E′(T) := {v1v2, v2v3, . . . , vsv1}. The weight of a walk W (resp.,
tour T) is w′(W) := w′(E′(W)) (resp., w′(T) := w′(E′(T))). Note that a sequence of vertices can
represent either a walk or a tour, depending on whether the first and last vertices are connected;
the context will always clarify which applies. Moreover, a tour T is a simple tour if it contains no
repeated vertices, and a TSP tour in G′ if additionally |E′(T)| = |V ′|.
Given a walk or tour . . . xyz . . . in G′, a new walk or tour . . . xz . . . can be obtained by skipping
the vertex y. This operation is called shortcutting the vertex y or the edges xy and yz. Since the
weight function w′ is a metric, the new walk or tour has a non-increasing weight by the triangle
inequality.
For any integer p ∈N+, let [p] := {1, 2, . . . , p}. Consider a tour osi1si2 . . . sip visiting p sources of
requests in {(sij, tij) | j ∈[p]} and the corresponding tour oti1ti2 . . . tip visiting the p destinations,
where o denotes an optional vehicle. Such a pair of tours, which share the same permutation of
indices i1i2 . . . ip, is called consistent. More generally, if the index sequences for the sources and
destinations are both permutations of {i1, i2, . . . , ip} (not necessarily identical), we call them weakly
consistent. Whenever o appears in one tour of a (weakly) consistent pair, it must also appear in
the other.
Similarly, let Ts and Tt be two sets of tours, where every tour in Ts consists of no destinations
and every tour in Tt consists of no sources. We call these sets (weakly) consistent if, for every tour
in Ts, there exists a tour in Tt that is (weakly) consistent with it, and vice versa. In this case, we
must have |Ts| = |Tt|.
A walk is simple if it contains no repeated vertices; such a walk is called a path. Two paths,
or two sets of paths, are said to be (weakly) consistent in the same manner as tours, with the
same requirement on the presence of an optional vehicle o. (For paths, if o exists, we require that
the paths start from o.) For instance, the tours os1s2 and ot1t2 are consistent; the paths s1os2
and t1ot2 are not (weakly) consistent. For any two sequences T1 = p1p2 . . . pi and T2 = q1q2 . . . qj,
we use T1 ◦T2 (resp., T1 • T2) to denote the sequence obtained by concatenating T2 to T1 (resp.,
removing all elements of T2 from T1). For a sequence T = p1p2 . . . pi, we let |T| = i. For instance,
if T1 = 12134 and T2 = 1216, then T1 ◦T2 = 121341216, T1 • T2 = 34, and |T1| = 5.

3.1
The First Algorithm
Our first algorithm, denoted by Alg.1, is inspired by the well-known iterated tour partition (ITP)
algorithm for the CVRP (Haimovich and Kan 1985).
The ITP works as follows: (1) compute
an α-approximate TSP tour in G; (2) partition this tour into the desired path fragments; and
(3) transform each fragment into a feasible tour.
Despite its simplicity, ITP achieves a strong
approximation guarantee of α + 2 for the CVRP (Altinkemer and Gavish 1987). Surprisingly, it
has not been explored for approximation algorithms in the DaRP setting.
We begin with the DaRP, and later show how to extend the method to the mDaRP with minor
modifications.
To modify ITP to work for the DaRP, we note that instead of finding an approximate TSP
tour in G as (1) in ITP, we may find two approximate TSP tours Ts in G[X ∪K] and Tt in
G[Y ∪K] since each request involves two locations. Moreover, we may ensure that these two TSP
tours are consistent, say Ts = osi1si2 . . . sim and Tt = oti1ti2 . . . tim. This is important, as for any
fragment Bi
s = sijsij+1 . . . sij+j′ from Ts, there always exists a fragment Bi
t = tijtij+1 . . . tij+j′ from
Tt that is consistent with Bi
s. Then, as (2) in ITP, we construct a set of fragments Bs (resp., Bt)
by partitioning Ts (resp., Tt). Note that each fragment is a path, and we may require that Bs is
consistent with Bt. Last, as (3) in ITP, we transform each pair of consistent fragments in Bs ∪Bt
into a tour. This can be done by doubling all edges in the pair of consistent fragments, adding two
copies of the minimum-weight edge connecting these fragments, and then taking shortcuts. Let T
be the set of tours obtained. Traversing the TSP tour in Ts together with the tours in T yields a
feasible DaRP solution.
To extend the above idea to the mDaRP, we seek two consistent sets of tours: Ts in G[X ∪K]
and Tt in G[Y ∪K], such that (a) each source in X (resp., destination in Y ) appears in exactly one
tour in Ts (resp., Tt); (b) each vehicle in K appears in exactly one tour in Ts and in exactly one
tour in Tt; (c) each tour in Ts ∪Tt contains exactly one vehicle. Given any pair of consistent tours
from Ts ∪Tt, we can apply the same procedure as in the DaRP to construct a route that serves all
requests in the pair (see Alg.1 in Algorithm 1).
Next, we show in Sect. 3.1.1 how to compute the consistent sets of tours Ts and Tt satisfying
conditions (a)-(c). Then, we show in Sect. 3.1.2 how to compute the fragments and the routes of
the vehicles.

Conclusion
This paper investigates approximation algorithms for the mDaRP. We propose an algorithm that
achieves an approximation ratio of O(√n log n), even improving upon the previous best-known ratio
of O(√n log2 n) for the classic (single-vehicle) DaRP. Our results are derived from new structural
properties of the problem, which allow us to leverage techniques from the well-known CVRP. We
believe that these insights may also be applicable to other variants of the DaRP. A key direction
for future research is to determine whether the O(√n)-approximation barrier can be surpassed.
