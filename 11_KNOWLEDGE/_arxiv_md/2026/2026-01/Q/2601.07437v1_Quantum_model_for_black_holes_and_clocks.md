We consider a stationary quantum system consisting of two non-interacting yet entangled subsys-
tems, Ξ and Γ. We identify a quantum theory characterizing Ξ such that, in the quantum-to-classical
crossover of the composite system, Γ behaves as a test particle within the gravitational field of a
Schwarzschild Black Hole (SBH) near its event horizon. We then show that this same quantum
theory naturally provides a representation of Ξ in terms of bosonic modes, whose features match
those of the Hawking radiation; this facilitates the establishment of precise relations between the
phenomenological parameters of the SBH and the microscopic details of the quantum model for Ξ.
Finally, we recognize that the conditions used to characterize Γ and Ξ coincide with those required
by the Page and Wootters mechanism for identifying an evolving system and an associated clock.
This leads us to discuss how the quantum model for Ξ endows the SBH with all the characteristics
of a “perfect” clock.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Classical physics emerges from the description of na-
ture provided by quantum theory via a quantum-to-
classical crossover, a process actively investigated in var-
ious research fields, from quantum foundations [1–4] to
quantum gravity [5–8], from quantum measurement the-
ory to quantum sensing [9–17].
In the gravitational context, the expectation that
classical gravity is the low-energy limit of a quantum
high-energy theory finds a concrete proving ground in
black hole physics, where any black hole should be de-
scribed as an exponentially complex, isolated quantum
system [18]. Since its formulation, this assumption has
garnered widespread support through theoretical tests in
many contexts [19–26].
The idea that black holes are made of many funda-
mental quantum degrees of freedom and the quantum-
to-classical crossover of gravity are closely related:
both imply that classical gravitational physics emerges
from an underlying quantum theory of gravity, whose
formulation—entailing the long-sought reconciliation of
general relativity and quantum mechanics—remains one
of the central challenges in contemporary physics. Rather
than pursuing a fully general reconstruction of quantum
gravity, we adopt the quantum-to-classical crossover, as
originally introduced in Ref. [1] and more recently up-
dated in Refs. [11, 27–30], reversing its logic to infer
a quantum theory from the well-known classical phe-
nomenology of black holes.
We focus on the simplest case: the Schwarzschild black
hole (SBH) which arises in general relativity as a spher-

II.
THE QUANTUM-TO-CLASSICAL
CROSSOVER IN A NUTSHELL
We begin by introducing the definitions and tools re-
quired for our analysis. A quantum theory Q is defined
by a Lie algebra g and a Hilbert space H carrying a rep-
resentation of g.
Physical observables are represented
by Hermitian operators acting on H, with the Hamilto-
nian ˆH playing a distinctive role as the generator of time
evolution. Similarly, a classical theory C is defined by
a symplectic manifold M, equipped with a symplectic
form and the associated Poisson brackets. Real-valued
functions on M represent physical observables, with the
Hamiltonian h : M →R determining the dynamics.
Despite their structural differences, quantum and clas-
sical theories can be related through the quantum-to-
classical crossover, a formal framework that establishes
whether a given quantum theory Q can be effectively re-
placed by a classical theory CQ in an appropriate clas-
sical limit and, if so, determines its structure.
Here,
“effectively replaced” means that CQ faithfully describes
physical phenomena at a certain effective scale, identi-
fied with the classical regime. More precisely, the classi-
cal limit is defined by χQ →0, where χQ is a positive,
theory-dependent parameter that vanishes as the number
N gauging the system size tends to infinity [33]. In the
literature [34–37], the term quantumness is often used for
χQ.
The conditions for the existence of CQ are formulated
in terms of coherent states associated with g [1, 27],
hereafter referred to as Generalized Coherent States
(GCS) [38–40]. Among these conditions, the inner prod-
uct between GCS must approach a Dirac delta distribu-
tion as χQ →0, reflecting the requirement that classi-
cal states must be distinguishable. Moreover, the ratio
between any matrix element of observables evaluated on
GCS and the corresponding GCS overlap must remain fi-
nite in the same limit. This ensures that quantum expec-
tation values converge to the real-valued functions repre-
senting observables in the classical theory.
Notably, the quantum-to-classical crossover conditions
are not guaranteed to hold for any arbitrary Q. In the
absence of these conditions, the existence of CQ cannot be
established (see Ref. [29] and Sec. VII of Ref. [1]). On the
other hand, if they are satisfied, one not only proves the
existence of CQ but also fully determines its structure, in-
cluding the symplectic manifold M, the associated Pois-
son brackets, and the Hamiltonian function h. Crucially,
in this framework, the quotient-manifold theorem, ap-
plied to the Lie group associated with g, guarantees a
one-to-one correspondence between GCS and points on a
symplectic manifold M.
We emphasize that distinct quantum theories differing
only in their Hamiltonians can flow into the same classi-
cal theory. This occurs either because the Hamiltonians
are related by symmetry transformations that become ir-
relevant in the χQ →0 limit, or because they differ only
by terms that vanish in that limit.
III.
SETTING THE QUANTUM SCENE
Black holes arise in general relativity as exact solu-
tions of the Einstein equations. The internal structure of
black holes, together with the nature of the correspond-
ing fundamental microscopic degrees of freedom, remain
currently unknown. Our understanding is restricted to
observing their gravitational influence on surrounding
matter and light—most directly by tracking the motion
of nearby test particles. The overall picture reduces to
a single dynamical system, namely the test particle, for
which one can derive an effective Hamiltonian function
including a potential term arising from the presence of
the black hole, that would be absent otherwise. Notably,
this setting is very common in physics and lies at the
heart of the concept of field, both in classical and quan-
tum field theory, regardless of the peculiarities of each
field.
On the other hand, if one aims at designing a quan-
tum system Ξ that exerts the above mentioned influence
upon the dynamics of a test particle Γ, without resorting
to the concept of field, one must instead consider a sce-
nario involving two quantum systems: the test particle
Γ and Ξ itself. In the standard setting of open quantum
systems, Γ and Ξ interact with each other. Gravity, how-
ever, dictates that the dynamics of Γ is actually modified
by the curvature of spacetime, suggesting a description
in which explicit interactions are absent. In a classical
world, this would reduce to the standard picture of a soli-
tary particle in a gravitational field. In quantum theory,
however, systems may still influence one another through
a distinct mechanism: quantum entanglement.

quantum theories QΓ and QΞ relative to the quantum

VII.
A PERFECT CLOCK
Let us return to the quantum picture of Sec. III and
conditions 1-3): These are the same conditions that de-
fine the PaW mechanism, under which the flow of time
is an emergent phenomenon resulting from the entan-
glement between two non-interacting systems in a glob-
ally stationary state [31]. The PaW mechanism has been
extensively used, and its assumptions scrutinized from
both the theoretical and the experimental viewpoints
[28, 30, 32, 57–73].
The mechanism suggests that the
parameter time entering the dynamical equations of an
evolving system Γ originates from another system Ξ play-
ing the role of clock, provided that Γ and Ξ obey condi-
tions 1-3). While these conditions do not specify which

clock is arbitrary, yet not accidental. In fact, referring
to Refs. [28–30] and reminding that E = Jw corresponds
to the energy of the SBH, it is natural to identify its
conjugate coordinate t = v/J as the temporal parameter
for the test particle Γ. Note that w is real and bounded
from below, while v is a continuous real variable ranging
from −∞to +∞, as required to represent energy and
time, respectively.

particle’s proper time, which, in general relativity, is de-
fined by the particle’s internal clock. This identification
appears to be the only one compatible with the under-
lying quantum description we propose. Indeed, no addi-
tional subsystems—such as external observers endowed
with independent clocks—are introduced in the global
system Ψ = Γ ∪Ξ.

of the SBH is fixed, it is z(ξ; γ) = z(E, t; q, m dq/dt) =

to consider a real slice (t, q). This geometric picture nat-

points (t, q) for which z(t; q) ̸= 0. These subspace identi-

gravity κ = c4/(4GM). It is inspiring that the geometric
properties of MΞ play such a fundamental role in deter-
mining the dynamics of Γ, far reinforcing the PaW mech-
anism as a fundamental framework for the emergence of
spacetime [30, 74–77].
VIII.
DISCUSSION
In this final section, we comment on aspects of our
results that may be relevant for future developments.
We first emphasize that the classical Hamiltonian of
the test particle, Eq. (19), is not what remains of a
quantum Hamiltonian ˆHΓ after the quantum-to-classical
crossover is performed, as in Refs. [1, 9, 27, 30]. Nor do
we apply a quantization procedure to the target Hamilto-
nian function (11). Indeed, the theory QΓ needs not be-
ing explicitly defined, provided it admits a classical limit
according to the conditions discussed in Sec. III. The
connection between CΓ and QΓ, with Γ being a massive
particle probing a gravitational field, is instead estab-
lished through conditions 1–3) and the associated map-
ping given in Eqs. (15-17). This suggests that these con-
ditions play a fundamental role in relating quantum and
classical physics as far as the notion of field is concerned,
extending beyond the PaW mechanism and the problem
of time, thus deserving further investigations.
We remark that no measurement is involved in the
quantum-to-classical crossover.
Rather, this formal-
ism operates at the more fundamental level concerning
how physical theories describe reality, whether accessed
through quantum or classical observations [78]. Conse-
quently, measurement processes can be described within
the quantum-to-classical crossover formalism, without
conceptual or formal conflicts [9, 11].
As for the SBH, its distinctive thermodynamics is not
discussed in this work, aside from the definition of the
temperature (30) which is set equal to the Hawking tem-
perature.
However, our approach naturally embodies
the possibility for such thermodynamics to emerge from
the fact that many different coherent states {|ξ⟩} corre-
spond to the same T, given the functional dependence of
Eq. (30) on the complex variable ξ. This is in line with
theories ascribing the origin of black hole entropy to sta-
tistical uncertainty over the microscopic states [79–82], a
possibility explored in our framework by AC in Ref. [29].
We plan to conduct further analysis on SBH thermody-
namics, possibly including specific microstates counting
and different sources of uncertainty.
The interplay between the thermodynamic essence of
black holes and their quantum nature is an intriguing as-
pect of our work. On the one hand, the large-NΞ limit
taken to obtain our target Hamiltonian—the classical
hΓ(p, q) of the test particle—is physically consistent with
Ξ being a thermodynamical object. On the other hand,
setting NΞ to infinity masks any quantum effect of Ξ on
Γ, which could instead be probed by considering a large
but finite NΞ. In fact, using the quantum Hamiltonian
ˆHΞ is akin to treating NΞ as finite, while choosing the
representation (21) is equivalent to considering quantum
effects without backreaction; this is precisely the setup
of QFT in curved spacetime, which yields Hawking’s re-
sult. It is therefore conceivable to proceed to the next
order in quantum effects, an approach that aligns with
current frameworks in quantum gravity, such as gravita-
tional path integral [83–86], loop quantum gravity [87–90]
and holography [91–93]. We also note that the symplectic
manifold MΞ can be identified with the Euclidean Anti-
de Sitter space EAdS2. Since AdS spaces are central to
the AdS/CFT conjecture—the cornerstone of quantum
gravity proposal derived from string theory via a large-
N argument [5]—we believe that further connections be-
tween our results and this framework may emerge from
more thorough analysis.
