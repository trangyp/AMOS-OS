Abstract. In this paper, we consider outlier embeddings into HSTs. In
particular, for metric (X, d), let k be the size of the smallest subset of X
such that all but that subset (the “outlier set”) can be probabilistically
embedded into the space of HSTs with expected distortion at most c.

that takes in (X, d) and a target distortion c and samples from a prob-
abilistic embedding with at most O( k
ϵ log2 k) outliers and distortion at
most (32 + ϵ)c, for any ϵ > 0. In order to facilitate our results, we show
how to find good nested embeddings into HSTs and combine this with
an approximation algorithm of Munagala et al. [31] to obtain our results.

Introduction
Many algorithmic solutions to metric problems begin by embedding the given
metric into a hierarchically separated tree (HST) and then solving the problem
on that more structured space. The distortion (the multiplicatively worst change
in pairwise distance) of the embedding affects the final approximation factor of
such algorithms. Randomized algorithms that optimize the expected value of the
objective function can use probabilistic embeddings, which bound the expected
distortion for each pair of nodes. A series of works [4,10,11,23] showed that
probabilistic embeddings can embed any n-point metric into trees with expected
distortion O(log n).
While uniform distortion bounds are helpful in understanding the general
effectiveness of using metric embeddings to solve a problem, from a practical
perspective, we may ask how well a particular given metric (X, d) can embed
into trees. Indeed, for some metrics the optimal distortion is Ω(log n) but for
others it may be much smaller, e.g. O(1), providing better algorithmic results.
In this paper we investigate instance-optimal distortion for prob-
abilistic embeddings into trees by exploring the role of outliers. For
a finite metric (X, d), a (k, c)-outlier embedding is a map from “most of” X,
namely all but k vertices that we call outliers, into another metric space Y such
that the distortion over the non-outliers is bounded by c. Intuitively, we can
view the outliers as noisy or adversarial data that may hurt the quality of the
instance-optimal embedding from X into Y . The goal of the outlier embedding
is to identify and remove these outliers upfront so as to obtain a much better
embedding over the remaining points. Algorithmically, given a target distortion

Technical tools and challenges Munagala et al. [31] provide an approxima-
tion algorithm for the instance-optimal probabilistic embedding into HSTs that
rounds a natural linear programming (LP) relaxation. We extend this LP to
incorporate outliers. Proving the feasibility of our extended LP requires the con-
cept of nested embeddings, that was first developed by Chawla and Sheridan [19]
in the context of deterministic embeddings into normed metrics. We extend this
notion to probabilistic HST embeddings, thereby guiding the choice of distortion
constraints in the LP; and then provide a rounding similar to that in [31].
Informally, a nested embedding takes as input an embedding on a subset
S ⊆X with low distortion cS, as well as a set of embeddings on all subsets of
X with size at most |X \ S| + 1, each with (larger) distortion cX; it then finds
a single new embedding on the entire set X such that distortion is close to cS
for pairs of points in S and is only a polylogarithmic factor worse than cX over
other pairs. Extending Chawla and Sheridan [19]’s technique to HSTs poses two
key technical challenges that we will now discuss.
Nested embeddings into HSTs with bounded contraction. Chawla and Sheridan
[19] approximate optimal outlier sets by rounding an SDP solution. The SDP
has designated “outlier” variables δi such that when δi is set to 1, the expansion
and contraction conditions for the distance between i and any other node are
relaxed; in fact, the contraction can be unbounded. Thus, [19] need only show
existence of nested embeddings that bound expansion, not contraction.
By contrast, while our modified version of the LP from Munagala et al. [31]
also includes outlier variables δi, a large δi does not admit contraction between
nodes, so bounding the optimal LP solution requires nested embeddings that
bound both expansion and contraction. In particular, Munagala et al.’s LP es-
sentially sets up a metric labeling problem with the goal of assigning each vertex
to a representative at geometrically increasing levels. Each vertex v is assigned
a representative at each level i that is at a distance no more than 2i from v. Im-
portantly, the level-wise labeling constraints cannot be weakened for fractional
outliers – each vertex, whether or not it’s an outlier, must still be assigned at
level i to a representative within distance 2i from it. Even slightly weakening this
constraint can potentially introduce errors at all levels of the hierarchy, leading
to very large distortion (depending on |X| rather than k and c).

In contrast to the main outlier embedding results of Chawla and Sheridan
[19], our setting calls for a careful alignment and stitching of the two embeddings
that does not contract any distances. We call this procedure a “merge” and
characterize the conditions under which it returns a correct nested embedding.
Chawla and Sheridan implicitly give such a merge function for ℓ1 embeddings,
and we show that such a merge function exists for HST embeddings.
Probabilistic nested embedding. The second technical challenge is in extending
the notion of nested embeddings to probabilistic embeddings. This is important
in the context of tree embeddings because probabilistic tree embeddings can
obtain exponentially smaller distortion than deterministic tree embeddings.
Consider taking a probabilistic embedding of some subset S of a given met-
ric (X, d) into a host space Y and extending it to a probabilistic embedding
of the entire metric X into Y . One potential way of leveraging a determinis-
tic nested embedding is to draw a random embedding α mapping S to Y , ex-
tend this embedding, and return the extension. What can we say about the
distortion of this random extension? We are guaranteed distortion at most
Eα[maxu,v∈X(dα(u, v)/d(u, v))], but this can be much larger than the expected
distortion of the original embedding, maxu,v∈X(Eα[dα(u, v)]/d(u, v)). To avoid
this issue, we show that our nested embeddings into HSTs satisfy a certain
obliviousness property, and that this property lets us obtain a final distortion
guarantee that depends on maxu,v∈X(Eα[dα(u, v)]/d(u, v)).
Related work
Embedding into HSTs has been studied primarily with the objective of minimiz-
ing “uniform” distortion. See e.g. [4,10,11,16,23]. In our work, we will use as a
subroutine the algorithm of Fakcharoenphol et al. [23], which for any n point
metric samples from an O(log n) distortion probabilistic embedding into HSTs.
Instance-optimal distortion has been studied for target spaces such as constant
dimensional Euclidean space [8,13,22,29,35], the line [29,24,7,33], trees [20,9],
and ultrametrics [3,31]. The goal in such embeddings is to minimize the distor-
tion needed for the given specific (X, d), which in some cases may be smaller
than the best bound achievable for all metrics of that size.
The notion of outlier embeddings was initially defined by Sidiropolous et
al. [36] when they studied deterministic embeddings into ultrametrics, trees,
and constant-dimensional ℓ2 space. They consider approximating deterministic,
isometric outlier embeddings (where the distortion c is 1) and bicriteria approx-
imations with additive distortion. The structural properties used for isometric
approximations don’t apply to non-isometric embeddings, and techniques for
bounding additive distortion tend to be quite different from those for multi-
plicative distortion, the focus of this work. Moreover, additive distortion does
not provide approximation guarantees for algorithmic applications.
Chubarian and Sidiropolous [21] continued the work on outlier embeddings
and studied non-isometric outlier embeddings of unweighted graph metrics into

Another related notion is embeddings with slack, in which the goal is to min-
imize the number of edges that are distorted by more than some target factor
[1,15,27]. Outlier embeddings are also closely related to metric Ramsey theory
[12,30]. Given inputs n and c, metric Ramsey theory asks: What is the largest
m such that all metrics of size n have a submetric of size m that embeds into
some target space with distortion at most c? In this paper we are primarily fo-
cused on instance-optimal solutions rather than universal bounds that apply to
all metrics, and we seek to approximate the size of the outlier set rather than
the size of the non-outlier set.
Our notion of nested embeddings first appears in [19], but many other types
of embedding compositions or extensions have been considered. One particular
example (which we discuss further in Appendix C) is the notion of a Lipschitz
extension [26,32,14], which seeks to leave non-outliers unaltered from the original
embedding and ensure the expansion (but not necessarily the contraction) of ev-
ery pair of nodes is bounded. Other works study a variety of different embedding
compositions [2,5,17,34,28]. These notions largely differ from ours in that they
emphasize bounding the worst distortion for any pair in the entire metric, while
our notion focuses on bounding the worst distortion between any pair subject
to a “special set” having even stricter distortion constraints.

achieve these properties for HST embeddings Recall that we are given a metric
space (X, dX), a target class of metrics Y, a subset S with k = |X \ S|, and
an assortment of probabilistic embeddings (DS, { DK′ }|K′|≤k+1). Our goal is to
produce a single probabilistic embedding for the entire set X that has small

Our approach is based on the nested embedding technique of Algorithms 4.1
and 5.1 in Chawla and Sheridan [19] for ℓ1 embeddings but strengthens that
technique by introducing a general procedure for merging embeddings. There
are two challenges that we need to address in order to adapt these algorithms to
our setting. First, we need to ensure that the final composition does not contract
distances. In [19]’s Algorithm 4.1, although the expansion for every pair of points
can be bounded for this composition, distances can get contracted by arbitrarily
large factors – indeed some vertices end up colocated under the composition.
As in [19], we randomly partition K = X \ S into sets K1, K2, . . ., and identify
a “close” vertex γi ∈S for each such set. Then, we consider the embedding in
the given assortment that maps Ki ∪{γi} into Y. We “merge” this embedding
with the embedding αS in such a fashion that neither embedding by itself gets
distorted any further. This allows us to bound contraction in a similar way to
Algorithm 5.1 from [19], though we frame it more generally. We then iterate in
this manner over all of the components in the partition of K.
The second challenge is to combine the per-pair αS expansion guarantee into
a expected distortion guarantee when we draw an αS from the given probabilistic
embedding DS. The key property that allows us to do so is the independence of
the procedure for partitioning K and the structure of αS. We call this property
obliviousness and will revisit it more formally in Appendix C.
We will now identify properties of the merging procedure that we require in
order to obtain guarantees for the final composition.
Definition 4 (Perfect merge). Let Z1, Z2 ⊆X with Z1 ∩Z2 = {v}. Let α1 :
Z1 →Y1 and α2 : Z2 →Y2 be deterministic expanding embeddings, for Y1, Y2 ∈
Y. We say that a procedure Merge is a perfect merge function for embedding X
into Y if given any pair α1 and α2 of embeddings from subsets of X into Y
satisfying the conditions above, it produces a an embedding α : Z1 ∪Z2 →Y for
some Y ∈Y such that:
1. For all x, y ∈Z1, dα(x, y) = dα1(x, y).
2. For all x, y ∈Z2, dα(x, y) = dα2(x, y).
3. For x ∈Z1\{ v } and y ∈Z2\{ v }, we have dα(x, y) ≥max { dα1(x, v), dα2(v, y) }.

Applications
In the Minimum Cost Communication Tree (MCCT) problem, we are given
a finite metric X = (X, d) and weights rij for all pairs of nodes i, j and we
are asked for a non-contracting embedding α of X into a tree with vertex set
X such that P
i,j∈X rij · dα(i, j) is minimized. This problem is motivated by
evolutionary biology, in which a scientist may attempt to reconstruct a tree of
relationships between multiple organisms, and she comes to the problem with
some known minimum distances between them, derived through a method such
as DNA sequence analysis. In such a setting, the rij are an additional tunable
parameter that can allow the scientist to indicate pairs she considers “more
likely” to be related, by giving them a bigger rij value. As noted by Wu et
al. [37], this problem can be approximated in expectation to within a factor of
O(log n) by simply embedding randomly into an HST and converting it to a tree
with vertex set X. Directly applying the algorithm of Munagala et al. [31] with
the observations of Wu et al. [37], we have a randomized algorithm that gets
an O(c(X))-approximation for the MCCT problem, where c(X) is the minimum
distortion needed to probabilistically embed X into HSTs.
It is quite natural to consider an outlier version of the MCCT problem. In the
evolutionary biology setting, we could consider the case that we are given some
noisy data points, or points that don’t really belong in the set being analyzed.
One approach to this type of outlier problem may be to look for a k outlier
tree embedding of minimum distortion, but the set of outliers that minimizes
the embedding distortion does not necessarily also minimize the MCCT solution
cost. We may expect that the outlier MCCT problem is easier to solve on tree
metrics than on general metrics, so we could instead try the following approach:
embed the input metric into a tree, and then try to find the best outliers for
the resulting tree metric. In fact, if no outliers are allowed, MCCT is trivial on
tree metrics. Further, in Appendix E we give a bicriteria approximation to the
k-outlier MCCT problem when the input is a tree metric. In particular, if the
optimal cost of a k outlier solution for a given instance of a tree metric is OPTk,
then we give an algorithm that finds a solution with at most 3k outliers and at
most 24OPTk cost. Let ck(X) be the minimum distortion required for proba-
bilistically embedding all but at most k points in X into HSTs. We can use the
outlier embedding algorithm from this paper to embed any input metric into a
tree with outliers and then apply the approximation algorithm for outlier MCCT
on trees, obtaining a solution with at most O( k
ϵ log2 k) outliers and expected cost
O((1 + ϵ) · ck(X) · OPTk). By contrast, directly using the embedding algorithm
of [31] with the tree approximation algorithm from Appendix E instead gives a
solution with at most 3k outliers and O((1 + ϵ) · c0(X) · OPTk) expected cost,
so we are unable to save on the cost of the optimal solution if there is a large
subset that embeds well into HSTs but the entire metric does not.
