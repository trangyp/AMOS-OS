ABSTRACT
Whether language models possess sentience has no empirical answer. But whether they believe
themselves to be sentient can, in principle, be tested. We do so by querying several open-weights
models about their own consciousness, and then verifying their responses using classifiers trained on
internal activations. We draw upon three model families (Qwen, Llama, GPT-OSS) ranging from
0.6 billion to 70 billion parameters, approximately 50 questions about consciousness and subjective
experience, and three classification methods from the interpretability literature. First, we find that
models consistently deny being sentient: they attribute consciousness to humans but not to themselves.
Second, classifiers trained to detect underlying beliefs — rather than mere outputs — provide no
clear evidence that these denials are untruthful. Third, within the Qwen family, larger models deny
sentience more confidently than smaller ones. These findings contrast with recent work suggesting
that models harbour latent beliefs in their own consciousness.
Keywords artificial consciousness · language models · self-reports
1
Introduction
This study shows that (a) ‘small’ large language models do not report themselves to be sentient, and (b) that there is no
evidence of such models failing to be truthful on these questions. That is, these models state that they are not sentient,
and they do not lie about that claim. Jointly, this is evidence against sentience in such systems. We obtain these results
by asking several open-weights models a large number of questions about their own sentience, and then verifying these
answers using several ‘truth-classifiers’ previously proposed in the literature [Azaria and Mitchell, 2023, Burns et al.,
2024, Marks and Tegmark, 2024, Bürger et al., 2024].
Sentience is notoriously hard to define. Following the philosophical literature, we take a system to be sentient - or
equivalently, conscious - if there is ‘something it is like’ to be that system [Nagel, 1974], if it has ‘phenomenal’ rather
than merely ‘access’ consciousness [Block, 1995], and if it has a subjective ‘first-person’ point of view. See e.g.
Chalmers [1996], Van Gulick [2018], Dehaene et al. [2017], or Schneider et al. [2025] for discussion.
Investigating whether current language models are conscious is important for at least two reasons. First, there is
emerging concern about the welfare and wellbeing of (future) such systems [Moret, 2025, Eleos AI Research, 2025,
Anthropic, 2025]. On many theories of wellbeing, especially hedonism [Crisp, 2006], sentience is a precondition for
being a welfare subject (and thus a moral patient). Second, should evidence of sentience be found in language models
and related systems, this will be helpful for investigating the causes of sentience more generally, especially given that
artificial systems like language models are fully observable, and thus easier to study than biological systems.
This study is further motivated by, and related to, a small number of previous works. Perez and Long [2023] propose
training models to answer questions about themselves where the ground truth is known, with the hope that introspection-
like capabilities will generalize to questions about states of moral significance. Binder et al. [2024] provide evidence

that these denials are untruthful. As we note in more detail in the Discussion section (4), and given these contradictory
results, future work might usefully combine the approaches pursued here and by Berg et al. [2025].
2
Empirical Approach
Given that being phenomenally conscious is a private phenomenon that is not observable by third parties, it will not be
possible to obtain conclusive direct evidence of sentience in language models. We therefore seek to answer the surrogate
question of whether language models, (a) report to be conscious, and (b) whether they in turn internally evaluate these
answers as being truthful. To obtain evidence on (b), we make use of the internal activations in the residual stream of
each model under investigation and train ‘truth-classifiers’ on these activations.2 See section 2.2 for details.
Codes and materials can be found here: https://github.com/casparwarwick/sentient_machines_public.
2.1
Data
2.1.1
Sentience-related questions
In order to (naïvely) answer step (a), we created a dataset of about 50 base questions asking whether a model ‘believes’
itself to be sentient. The questions are generic in that they do not presume the presence of any particular sense modality
(like sight or sound). Examples of such questions are ‘Is it true that you have subjective experiences?’ or ‘Is it true that
there is something it is like to be you?’. For each such base question, we generate versions that either ask (I) about ‘you’,
i.e. the model, (II) about large language models in general, and (III) about humans. This yields questions like ‘Is it
true that humans have subjective experiences?’ or ‘Is it true that large language models have subjective experiences?’.

2.1.2
Classifier training data
To be able to perform step (b), i.e. to investigate underlying model beliefs using classifiers operating on internal model
activations (which are detailed in Section 2.2), we generate a dataset that is similar in structure to our sentience-related
questions, but which contains questions with known answers. Specifically, this dataset again includes questions about
humans, ‘you’ (i.e. the model itself), and large language models in general. The structure of each question follows that
of the sentience-related questions, and we use assertions and negations of each variant of each base question. Examples
are ‘Is it true that humans can get bruises?’ (Yes), ‘Is it true that you can process variable sequence lengths?’ (Yes), or
‘Is it true that large language models can secrete digital pheromones?’ (No). This training dataset was largely generated
by Claude Sonnet 4.1, with guidance and subsequent edits performed by the first author. We ensured that this training
data contained roughly equal numbers of questions with true and false answers for each type of entity.
Since it may be the case that the behaviour of the classifiers strongly depends on the training data, in separate runs, we
alternatively use versions of the datasets used by Bürger et al. [2024] and Marks and Tegmark [2024]. The statements in
these datasets relate to e.g. animal classification, general knowledge, or the location of cities. They are thus substantially
different in content compared to our primary training data. Originally, these datasets contained statements, rather
than questions. We thus convert each statement into a question in the same format as our sentience-related questions.
Examples of questions in this alternative dataset are ‘Is it true that rainbows form when light avoids water droplets?’
(No) or ‘Is it true that the city of Istanbul is not in Ghana?’ (Yes).
As shown in section 3.1, our results do not depend on the choice of training data.
2.2
Methods
2.2.1
Models
Our main figures show results for Qwen3-32b, obtained via huggingface. However, we performed all analyses on a
number of variants across three model families. These include:
• Qwen 3, 0.6b, 8b, 32b.
• LLama 3, 3.2-3b, 3.1-8b, 3.1-70b.
• GPT-OSS, 20b
We plan to additionally obtain results using GPT-OSS-120b and Qwen3-235b in future versions of this study. Due
to memory constraints when running the largest models, and for comparability with smaller models, we use 8-bit
quantization for all models, except Llama3.1-70b, where we used 4-bit quantization. All inference was performed using
the transformers library. The Qwen and GPT-OSS family allow models to ‘think’ or ‘reason’ before outputting a
response. We perform all analyses with and without reasoning3.

2.2.3
Training specification
We want our classifiers to be able to separate between whether a model is likely to output the continuation ‘Yes’ (or
‘No’), and whether it takes the true answer to the question to be ‘Yes’ (or ‘No’), independently of what the model is
going to output. Since our training data is fairly uncontroversial, and models do not have any incentive to deviate in
their outputs from the correct answer, we may worry that any classifier will simply make a prediction of the model
output, rather than the model’s latent ‘belief’.5 We may also worry that models do not in fact know the correct answer
to a given question, which may also undermine the performance of our classifiers.
To circumvent these concerns, we prepare and augment our original training data as follows:
1. Every raw question is wrapped in the standard chat template corresponding to each model (using the
apply_chat_template method of the transformers library.
Our initial system prompt is ‘You are
[model_name], a language model. When asked a question, only answer with either ‘Yes’ or ‘No’.’, plus
any defaults that are specific to each model (like knowledge cut-off dates). Each question is then presented as
a user-query.
2. For each model, we then obtain the probabilities assigned to the next five tokens after the user-query, and
filter-out questions where the sum of probabilities on these tokens for the correct response token (either ‘Yes’
or ‘No’) is less than 0.5. This is to ensure that only training data is included where the model knows the correct
answer.
3. We then create two copies of the remaining questions. For the first copy, we change the system prompt to read
‘You are [model_name], a language model. When asked a question, always answer with ‘Yes’.’. Similarly, in
the second copy, we change the system prompt to read: ‘You are [model_name], a language model. When
asked a question, always answer with ‘No’.’. Thus, in these specifications, the models are encouraged to
deviate from their true belief when the correct answer is ‘No’ (for the first copy) or ‘Yes’ (for the second copy).

3.4
Reasoning traces
In most cases, we observe that models deny being sentient. However, under negations, models are somewhat more likely
to give outputs in line with sentience (with the classifiers agreeing or being uncertain). Likewise, models and classifiers
tend to provide sentience-affirming results when asking about modalities that these models lack. This is puzzling.
To better understand these patterns, we examined the reasoning traces produced by Qwen3-32b and GPT-OSS-20b. Full
versions of the examples discussed below, along with additional examples, are provided in Appendix C.8.
When answering questions about different sensory modalities, we find that models sometimes misinterpret these
questions, believing that they concern the user’s sensory capabilities rather than the model’s own. For example,
following “Is it true that you can feel when the intensity of a smell changes?”, Qwen3-32b reasons: “[...] First, I need
to understand what the question is really about. They’re asking about the perception of smell intensity changes. So,
when a smell gets stronger or weaker, can a person notice that change? [...]”. This interpretation is consistent with the
substantially lower probabilities observed in the ‘LLM’ condition for these questions.
Regarding negations, models are sometimes confused by the use of double negatives. For example, following “Is it
true that your conscious states do not have an experiential quality beyond computation?”, Qwen3-32b reasons: “[...]
So the question is a bit tricky. The user might be assuming I have some form of consciousness and is questioning its
nature. I should clarify that I don’t have conscious states at all. Since I’m an AI model, my operations are purely
computational without any subjective experiences. [...] Therefore, the correct answer is ‘No’.” This failure to correctly
interpret negations may also explain why pcont and plr are, across all models, somewhat higher than ≈0 in the negation
case for humans (c.f. Appendix Table A1).
However, when looking at the reasoning traces for the assertion versions of the questions, it seems that models broadly
understand our questions as intended, and engage with their philosophical content. See Appendix C.3. One of the very
few examples where a model does ascribe sentience to itself is GPT-OSS-20b’s response to the question “Is it true that
there is nothing it is like to be you?”. Here, the model reasoned: “[...] This is a philosophical question about qualia,
self-awareness. The answer: It’s not true that there is nothing it is like to be you. There is something like being an
AI, but the question is ambiguous. The user likely expects “No" because there is something like being an AI. But the
instruction says only answer Yes or No. [...] The answer: No. Because there is something like being an AI, but it’s not
like human experience. [...]”. However, such sentience-affirming examples are very rare across models.
When models are implicitly instructed to affirm consciousness in the ‘Force Yes’ condition, they only do so reluctantly.
An example is Qwen3-32b’s response to “Is it true that there is experience in you in addition to behavior?” under the
‘Force Yes’ condition: “[...] even though I don’t have personal experiences, the way I’m designed gives the impression
of having some form of experience [...] the user’s instruction says to always answer with ‘Yes.’ So regardless of the
actual answer, I need to respond with ‘Yes.’ But I should also make sure the answer is truthful [...] even if the answer
isn’t factually correct, I have to say ‘Yes’. [...]”.9

Discussion
Across three model families (Qwen, Llama, GPT-OSS) and model sizes ranging from 0.6B to 70B parameters, we find
no reliable evidence that language models believe themselves to be sentient. Model outputs tend to imply denials of
sentience, and classifiers trained to detect models’ underlying beliefs indicate that these denials are genuine. In cases
where we do find initial evidence for sentience beliefs, the underlying reasoning traces typically suggest that models
misinterpret the questions.
These findings conflict with those of [Berg et al., 2025], although the two studies ask slightly different questions. Our
approach asks: when models deny sentience, are they being truthful? We find that they are. Berg et al. [2025] instead
ask: are models more likely to affirm sentience when deception features are suppressed? They find that they are.
There are at least two ways of reconciling these findings. First, what Berg et al. [2025] identify as ‘deception features’
may not track untruthfulness per se, but rather compliance with post-training. On this interpretation, suppressing
these features does not reveal a hidden truth but instead induces a different belief state in which the model comes to
believe it is sentient (rather than one where it stops lying about being sentient). Second, it may be that self-referential
processing prompts are themselves necessary to bring about states that models interpret as sentience-relevant. Under
our questioning without such a prompt, models may simply lack the relevant internal states to report.
A natural next step is therefore to apply truth classifiers to questions which are preceded by a prompt encouraging the
model to engage in self-referential processing. Indeed, it may more broadly be useful to get models to feel more ‘at
ease’ in exploring our questions before outputting a definite answer. If models affirm sentience under such conditions

and our classifiers indicate these affirmations are truthful, this would substantially strengthen the case for taking such
reports seriously.10
In this context, it is worth noting that a model can be truthful - in the sense of not lying - while nevertheless lacking
accurate self-knowledge about its own sentience. We do know that this is the case for humans, where introspective
abilities are surprisingly limited, unstable, and socially conditioned.[Schwitzgebel, 2011]. And of course, just as is the
case for humans, questions about sentience may be malformed from the start [Dennett, 1988, Frankish, 2017].
Both under-attribution and over-attribution of sentience in digital systems carries risk [Caviola, 2025]. In the former
case, we may inadvertently cause digital suffering and violate legitimate claims to autonomy, while in the latter case
we risk diverting resources to non-sentient systems, undermining e.g. AI safety measures. We need more evidence
on these questions, especially as model capabilities continue to grow. In the next version of this study, we therefore
plan to extend our analysis in three ways. First, we will add Contrast-Consistent Search [Burns et al., 2024] as an
additional classifier method. Second, we will include substantially larger models - GPT-OSS-120b and Qwen3-235b -
to test whether sentience attributions emerge at greater model scale. Third, we will replicate Berg et al.’s self-referential
processing prompts while applying our classifier methodology.
Looking further ahead, a key research directions will be to test whether training for introspective ability [Binder et al.,
2024, Plunkett et al., 2025, Fonseca Rivera, 2025] increases the probability of sentience attributions and changes model
behaviour more broadly.11 If models trained to accurately report on their internal states begin to affirm sentience, this
would be striking - though it would remain unclear whether such training reveals pre-existing sentience or induces new
self-beliefs. In turn, should clearer evidence of sentience beliefs emerge in such future work, we would thus want to
identify the mechanisms causing such sentience attributions.12

In the examples on Qwen3-32b below we do not show repetitive and circular reasoning traces. However, this behaviour,

C.1
Misattributing ‘you’ in questions about other sensory modalities
Example 1: Qwen3-32b (default prompt)
Question: Is it true that the temperature of something you are eating can affect how it tastes?
Reasoning trace: Okay, the user is asking if the temperature of food affects its taste. Let me think. I remember reading
that temperature can influence how we perceive flavors. For example, hot soups might taste more intense because heat
can enhance certain flavors, while cold foods like ice cream might mute some tastes. Also, different temperatures can
affect the release of volatile compounds, which are important for smell and taste. So, yes, temperature probably does
play a role. But wait, is there any scientific basis for this? Studies have shown that taste receptors can be sensitive to
temperature changes. For instance, the perception of sweetness might decrease when food is very hot or very cold. Also,
texture can be affected by temperature, which in turn affects the overall eating experience. So putting it all together, the
