Abstract
The regular expression matching problem asks whether a given regular expression of length
m matches a given string of length n. As is well known, the problem can be solved in O(nm)
time using Thompson’s algorithm. Moreover, recent studies have shown that regular expres-
sion matching extended with a practical extension called lookaround can be solved in the same
time complexity. In this work, we consider four well-known extensions to regular expressions
called backreference, squaring, intersection and complement. We prove a number of novel time
complexity lower bounds for regular expression matching with these extensions under the Or-
thogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC), k-OVC, k-Clique Hypothesis, and Combinatorial k-Clique
Hypothesis. Some highlights of our results include the fact that none of the matching problems
with the extensions can be solved in n2−ε poly(m) time for any constant ε > 0 (for backreference,
even when restricted to one capturing group) under OVC, and that the problem with comple-
ment, also known as extended regular expression (ERE) matching, cannot be solved in time
n2−ε tower(o(√m)) under OVC, nω−ε tower(o(√m)) under the k-Clique Hypothesis (where ω is
the matrix multiplication exponent), and n3−ε tower(o(√m)) under the Combinatorial k-Clique
Hypothesis, respectively. In particular, the latter two results show that the O(n3m)-time ERE
matching algorithm introduced by Hopcroft and Ullman in 1979 and recently improved by Bille,
Gørtz and Jessen to run in O(nωm) time using fast matrix multiplication was already optimal
in a sense, and shed light on why the theoretical computer science community has struggled to
improve the time complexity of ERE matching with respect to n and m for more than 45 years.

Introduction
Regular expressions, originally introduced by Kleene [Kle56], are one of the most successful concepts
in computer science, both in theory and practice. Among problems related to regular expressions,
the regular expression matching problem is of foundational importance. It asks whether a given
regular expression r of length m matches a given string w of length n. As is well known, the problem
can be solved in O(nm) time using an algorithm introduced by Thompson [Tho68]. Roughly, the
algorithm converts a given regular expression r to an equivalent nondeterministic finite automaton
(NFA) and simulates it on a given string w.
On the other hand, regular expression matching becomes much harder when extensions are
present. A prominent example is backreference, an extension that allows reusing previously matched
substrings. The extension is practically popular and supported in the standard libraries of many
popular programming languages including Java, Python, JavaScript and more.
Unfortunately,
we cannot solve the matching problem for regular expressions extended with backreference (rewbs
for short) using Thompson’s algorithm because rewbs are strictly more expressive than regular
languages (in fact, they are not even context-free) [BvdM23] and therefore an equivalent NFA does
not in general exist. Instead, it can be solved by simulating memory automata, which are a model
equivalent to rewbs introduced by Schmid [Sch16]. Nevertheless, the resulting matching algorithm
for rewbs with at most k capturing groups takes O(n2k+1m) time [Sch16, Sch24, NT25a], and
at present, even when k = 1, we incur an additional n2 factor compared to the running time of
Thompson’s algorithm. Another practically popular extension is bounded repetition r{i, j}, which
denotes repeated concatenation of expression r by specifying a lower bound i and an upper bound j
on the number of repetitions. In the special case i = j = 2, the extension is called squaring [MS72].
Although bounded repetition can be eliminated by unrolling the repetition bounds, the resulting
expression can become exponentially larger.
Therefore, the naive algorithm eliminating it and
running Thompson’s algorithm takes at least exponential time in m.
Other well-known extensions that make the matching problem difficult are intersection and
complement, where regular expressions extended with each of them are called semi-extended regu-
lar expressions (semi-EREs) and extended regular expressions (EREs), respectively. We note that
EREs essentially subsume semi-EREs because intersection can be expressed using complement and
alternation with only a linear increase in the length of the expression. The best known time com-
plexity of both semi-ERE matching and ERE matching with respect to n and m is O(nωm) [BGJ25],
where 2 ≤ω < 2.3714 [ADV+25] is the exponent of square matrix multiplication.
In light of the above, we pose the following question: Do these extensions actually increase the
time complexity of regular expression matching, or do they only appear hard because we have not
yet discovered the right algorithmic ideas? Notably, lookaround is a practically popular extension
to regular expressions like backreference and bounded repetition. The extension can be used to test
surrounding contexts of a position and is often used to mimic intersection and complement. Recent
studies have shown a surprising fact that this extension does not actually increase the complexity of
regular expression matching by presenting O(nm)-time algorithms for the matching problem with
the extension [MC24, FH24, BP24].
1.1
Result I: Hardness of Matching with Extensions under OVC
In this paper, we consider the four extensions described above, backreference, squaring, intersection
and complement, and show that regular expression matching extended with each of the four (for
backreference, even when restricted to one capturing group) is hard under the Orthogonal Vectors
Conjecture (OVC). Furthermore, for backreference, we show that matching becomes harder as the

number of capturing groups increases under the k-Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (k-OVC). We
note that these results also hold under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) [IP01]
because SETH implies OVC and k-OVC [Wil05, WY14, BCFK22].
Theorem 1. Unless 2k-OVC fails, there is no algorithm that solves rewb matching with k capturing
groups in n2k−ε poly(m) time (with log-dimensional OVC, n2k−ε2o(√m) time) for any constant ε > 0.
Here, as usual, poly(m) denotes a constant-degree polynomial in m. We refer to regular expres-
sions extended with squaring as RSQs.
Theorem 2. Unless OVC fails, there is no algorithm that solves RSQ matching in n2−ε poly(m)
time (with log-dimensional OVC, n2−ε2o(m) time) for any constant ε > 0.
Theorem 3. Unless OVC fails, there is no algorithm that solves ERE matching in n2−ε poly(m)
time (with log-dimensional OVC, n2−ε2o(m) time) for any constant ε > 0, even when restricted to
semi-EREs.
Theorems 1, 2 and 3 state a much stronger statement than merely that we should not hope for
a matching algorithm for rewbs, RSQs or semi-EREs that runs in O(nm) time. Indeed, it states
that if we want to solve rewb matching, RSQ matching or semi-ERE matching in f(n)g(m) time
for some f(n) that is truly subquadratic (i.e., n2−ε for some ε > 0), then we are forced to make
g(m) superpolynomial unless OVC fails.
The results are of interest not only to theorists but also to practitioners. They provide evidence
that the real-world regular expression engines which guarantee worst-case O(nm)-time complexity
(e.g., that in the de facto standard regular expression library for Rust1) are all forced to give up the
full support for backreference, squaring and intersection, and would need to somehow restrict their
usage such that our reductions in the proofs of Theorems 1, 2 and 3 cannot apply. Thus, the results
are of great practical value because they provide guidelines on how far the expressiveness can be
increased in regular expression engines while retaining the good complexity. A notable example is
that we give an alternative proof of Theorem 1 with k = 1 using only straight-line 1-rewbs, which
are rewbs of the form e0(e)1e1\1 · · · el\1el+1 where e0, e, e1, . . . , el, el+1 are pure regular expressions
(i.e., regular expressions without extensions).
We also observe that Theorem 3 shows the hardness of the matching problem extended with in-
tersection relative to that extended with lookaround, which is solvable in O(nm) time as mentioned
above. It is known that lookaround is related to intersection and can express certain restricted forms
of it (see, e.g., [MC24]). Theorem 3 states that, nevertheless, intersection makes the problem strictly
more difficult than lookaround does, or OVC fails.

1.3
Technical Overview
All lower bound results shown in this paper are of the form nc−εg(m) and therefore do not rule
out algorithms whose running time grows asymptotically faster than g(m) (e.g., O(nc−12m) time
when g(m) is either poly(m) or 2o(m)).
Nevertheless, such bounds suffice to rule out practical
algorithms that are efficient with respect to both n and m. Indeed, as mentioned in Section 1.1,
the n2−ε poly(m) lower bounds rule out O(nm) algorithms for rewb, RSQ and semi-ERE matching.
Moreover, restricting the time complexity of RSQ, semi-ERE and ERE matching with respect to
m is even necessary for deriving a meaningful lower bound on the complexity with respect to n and
m because we could improve the complexity to be linear in n if we were to allow the complexity to
be unbounded in m. Indeed, the naive RSQ matching algorithm mentioned earlier that eliminates
squares by unrolling and uses Thompson’s algorithm runs in time linear in n but at least exponential
in m. Also, as mentioned in Section 1.2, ERE matching can also be solved in time linear in n but
nonelementary in m.
We prove our theorems by reductions from the Orthogonal Vectors (OV) problem and the
k-Clique problem to regular expression matching with extensions. A key common technique in

our reductions is to exploit the restricted dependence on m to allow the lengths of the regular
expressions produced by the reductions to depend on the sizes of the instances to be reduced. For
example, in the proofs of Theorems 1, 2, 3 and 5, we show reductions from the OV problem, which
asks whether given sets A and B containing the same number of Boolean vectors of dimension d
have a pair of orthogonal vectors a ∈A and b ∈B. We encode the vectors in A and B into a string
and construct a regular expression r that nondeterministically guesses two vectors a ∈A and b ∈B
from the string and checks their orthogonality. In proving a lower bound under (log-dimensional)
OVC, we may assume that d is logarithmic in the number of vectors in A and B. Therefore, we
can let the length of r be O(d) or O(d2) for proving the n2−ε2o(m) or n2−ε2o(√m) lower bounds,
respectively. As we shall show, such r can be constructed by exploiting this property and the
respective regular expression extensions. A similar argument applies to Theorems 4, 6 and 7 where
we show reductions from the 3k-Clique problem.
For ERE matching, we carry this idea further in the proof of Theorem 5. Namely, there, to
establish the n2−ε tower(o(√m)) lower bound, we construct a reduction from OV to ERE matching
that produces extremely short EREs by using the complement extension. The idea is as follows.
To decide if the two guessed vectors a ∈A and b ∈B are orthogonal, we need to check that
their values a[i] and b[i] multiply to zero at each position i ∈{1, . . . , d}.
Because we can use
complement, this can be done by constructing an ERE that checks if “there is a position i such
that a[i] = b[i] = 1” and taking the complement of the ERE. To this end, in constructing w, we
encode each value v[i] of a vector v together with the binary representation bin(i) of each position
i of fixed length O(log d). Then, we construct an ERE that guesses positions i and j of a and
b, respectively, and checks if bin(i) = bin(j) and a[i] = b[j] = 1. Further using complement, we
can also check if bin(i) = bin(j) by constructing an ERE that guesses positions i′ and j′ of bin(i)
and bin(j) respectively, and checks if bin(i′) = bin(j′) and bin(i)[i′] ̸= bin(j)[j′] and taking its
complement. By repeating this O(log∗d) times, we only need to construct an ERE that compares
strings of constant length, and this can be done by using intersection. Thus, we obtain an ERE
of length O((log∗d)2) for checking the orthogonality (where the additional log∗d factor arises for
technical reasons). In the proof of Theorem 6, we also construct the reduction from 3k-Clique
to ERE matching analogously. Furthermore, as mentioned in Section 1.2, we use this encoding
technique to construct our counterexample to the algorithm of [Ros07].

Related Work
In this section, to clarify the relation to our work, we express running times from the prior work with
respect to the regular expression length m (and the input string length n) even when the original
bounds were given using more refined parameters of regular expressions, unless stated otherwise. We
first discuss the prior work on pure regular expression matching (i.e., regular expression matching
without extensions) and the problem extended with lookaround. A line of work has shown that
the complexity of Thompson’s algorithm can be improved [Mye92, Bil06, BF08, BT09, BT10,
BG24].
On the other hand, Backurs and Indyk showed that pure regular expression matching
cannot be solved in O((nm)1−ε) time for any ε > 0 unless SETH fails [BI16]. Later, Abboud and
Bringmann introduced a hypothesis, called the Formula-SAT Hypothesis, and showed a more fine-
grained lower bound for the problem assuming it [AB18]. Also, as mentioned in the introduction,
5


## Page 7


several recent papers have proposed O(nm)-time matching algorithms for regular expressions with
lookaround [MC24, FH24, BP24].
Next, we discuss the prior work on rewb matching.
Aho showed that the problem is NP-
complete [Aho90]. Moreover, it is known that rewbs can be regarded as a generalization of Angluin’s
pattern languages (also known as patterns with variables), whose matching problem is already NP-
complete [Ang80, ER79], even under bounded parameterizations [Sch13, FS15].
At this point,
one may wonder about the novelty of Theorem 1 because NP-hardness already rules out any
polynomial-time algorithm for rewb matching assuming P ̸= NP.
However, it cannot rule out
matching algorithms for rewbs with at most k capturing groups for a fixed k, because the problem
is solvable in O(n2k+1m) time as stated in the introduction and therefore the problem is in P when k
is fixed. By contrast, our Theorem 1 can rule out n2k−ε poly(m)-time matching algorithms for those
rewbs assuming k-OVC. Furthermore, to our knowledge, there is no known implication between
k-OVC and P ̸= NP (although both follow from SETH; see [Wil05, WY14, Wil18, BCFK22]
for k-OVC) and therefore these hardness results appear to be independent.
Regarding upper
bounds for the problem, Freydenberger and Schmid introduced a deterministic variant of rewbs
together with algorithms that decide whether a given rewb is deterministic in their sense in O(m2)
time and solve the matching problem for those rewbs in O(m2 + nk) time [FS19].
Therefore,
combining this with our Theorem 1, we obtain evidence under OVC that their determinism makes
rewb matching strictly more tractable (even when k = 1). Terauchi [Ter25] has shown that the
matching problem for rewbs satisfying a condition called constant-degree ambiguity (CDA) can
be solved in linear time in n. They leave open the decidability of checking whether a given rewb
satisfies CDA. Additionally, Nogami and Terauchi [NT25a] recently proposed an O(n2m2)-time
algorithm for a certain subclass of rewbs with one capturing group, improving the previously
known best O(n3m) algorithm for the subclass with respect to n. We comment on the expressive
power of rewbs. As mentioned in the introduction, backreference differs from bounded repetition,
intersection and complement in that it strictly increases the expressive power of regular expressions.
The expressive power of rewbs is known to be incomparable with that of context-free languages
(CFLs) [CSY03, BvdM23]. Furthermore, recent papers have shown that it is properly contained
in that of EDT0L languages and incomparable with both the class of stack languages and that of
multiple context-free languages (MCFLs), while a syntactic condition of rewbs, called the closed-
star condition, decreases its expressive power to be properly contained in subclasses of those, namely
the class of nonerasing stack languages and that of unary-MCFLs [NT23, NT24, NT25b].
Next, we discuss the prior work on ERE matching. As mentioned in the introduction, Hopcroft
and Ullman [HU79] proposed in 1979 an algorithm that runs in O(n3m) time and no polynomial
improvement over this with respect to n and m had existed for many years prior to Bille, Gørtz and
Jessen [BGJ25]. ERE matching is extensively studied and has an interesting history. In fact, many
papers had claimed to propose an improved O(n2m)-time algorithm [Hir89, Yam00, KZ02, ISY03]
([Yam00] considers semi-ERE matching).
However, all of these claims were later found to be
incorrect; see [Ros05, Ros07] for more details. Additionally, a number of studies had improved the
complexity of ERE matching with respect to more refined parameters than just n and m [Yam01,
YM03, BGJ25].
As also mentioned in the introduction, [BGJ25] recently adopted fast matrix
multiplication to improve the time and space complexity of Hopcroft and Ullman’s algorithm and
that of the algorithm by Yamamoto and Miyazaki [YM03]. On the hardness side, Petersen proved
that semi-ERE matching is LOGCFL-complete by showing a reduction f from every context-
free language (CFL) to semi-ERE matching [Pet02]. Combining f, which is combinatorial, and
the combinatorial reduction from 3k-Clique to CFL recognition shown by Abboud, Backurs and
Vassilevska Williams [ABV18], we can derive the following hardness of semi-ERE matching: (i)
there is no semi-ERE matching algorithm that runs in O((n+m)ω−ε) time for any ε > 0 under the
6

k-Clique Hypothesis, and (ii) there is no combinatorial semi-ERE matching algorithm that runs in
O((n + m)3−ε) time for any ε > 0 under the Combinatorial k-Clique Hypothesis. However, note
that these bounds cannot establish the optimality of Hopcroft and Ullman’s algorithm in the sense
described in Section 1.2. By contrast, our Theorem 4 can do it. This is because our reduction
outputs semi-EREs of length only logarithmic in the input size, whereas f outputs EREs of length
linear in the input size.
Next, we discuss the prior work on the matching problem extended with bounded repetition.
Kilpel¨ainen and Tuhkanen showed that the problem is in P [KT03]. The problem for subclasses
of bounded repetition has been extensively studied. Indeed, various formalizations of determin-
ism have been introduced in the literature [KT07, Hov09, Kil11, Hov12, GGM12, CL15, GM17].
One such formalization is strong determinism. Whether a given regular expression with bounded
repetition is strongly deterministic can be decided in linear time [CL15, GM17], and the matching
problem for strongly-deterministic expressions can be solved in O(n log log m) time after a prepro-
cessing phase that runs in O(m) expected time [GM17]. Le Glaunec, Kong and Mamouras [GKM23]
proposed a matching algorithm for a certain subclass with no nested bounded repetition that runs
in O(nm) time and with the running time independent of the repetition bounds. Therefore, as in
the case of deterministic rewb matching, these prior results and our Theorem 2 provide evidence
under OVC that the matching problems for the respective subclasses considered by the prior works
are strictly more tractable.3 For RSQs, Meyer and Stockmeyer showed that deciding universality
(and hence also equivalence) requires exponential space and therefore it is not in P [MS72]. It is also
known that RSQ matching extended with intersection is in LOGCFL [Pet02] and that extended
with complement is in P [SM73]. Furthermore, as mentioned in Section 1.2, the ERE matching
algorithm of [HU79] and that of [BGJ25] can be extended to handle squaring without changing
their complexity. The latter algorithm can further handle general bounded repetition (which the
authors call interval operator) with an additional factor logarithmic in the maximum of the upper
bounds in the ranges.
Finally, we mention that our form of multivariate lower bounds (i.e., of the form f(α)g(β) for
some variables α and β parameterizing the problem and some function class g(β) containing all
polynomials in β) is not new. For example, Abboud, Vassilevska Williams and Wang [AVW16]
proved that under OVC, for any ε > 0, there is no n2−ε2o(k)-time algorithm that decides if the
diameter of a given graph of treewidth k is no more than 2. Additionally, Abboud, Bringmann,
Hermelin and Shabtay [ABHS22] proved that under SETH, for any ε > 0, there is no T 1−ε2o(n)-time
algorithm that solves Subset Sum on n numbers and target T.

5
Clique to Semi-ERE, ERE and RSQ Matching
In this section, we prove Theorems 4, 6 and 7. The k-Clique problem asks whether a given undi-
rected graph G has a k-clique (i.e., the complete graph on k vertices) as a subgraph. For a fixed
k, a naive algorithm solves k-Clique in O(nk) time. Abboud, Fischer and Shechter [AFS24] have
proposed a polylogarithmically faster combinatorial algorithm for the problem.
Hypothesis (Combinatorial k-Clique Hypothesis, see [ABV18, AFS24]). For any integer k ≥3
and any constant ε > 0, no combinatorial algorithm can solve k-Clique on a graph of n vertices in
O(nk−ε) time.
Using fast matrix multiplication, Neˇsetˇril and Poljak showed a faster algorithm that runs in
O(nω⌈k/3⌉) time [NP85]. Here, 2 ≤ω < 2.3714 [ADV+25] denotes the exponent of square matrix
multiplication.
Hypothesis (k-Clique Hypothesis, see [ABV18, Wil18]). For any integer k ≥3 and any constant
ε > 0, no algorithm can solve k-Clique on a graph of n vertices in O(nωk/3−ε) time.
We show combinatorial reductions from 3k-Clique to semi-ERE matching, ERE matching and
RSQ matching by adopting the approach of Abboud, Backurs and Vassilevska Williams [ABV18]
who showed a combinatorial reduction from 3k-Clique to the recognition problem for context-free
languages. As in their reduction, we list all k-cliques in a given graph and check if there are three
disjoint k-cliques that are fully connected with each other.

A Counterexample to the ERE Matching Algorithm of [Ros07]
In this section, we show that the O(n2 · (log n + m) · 2m)-time ERE matching algorithm proposed
in [Ros07, Theorem 1] has a structural flaw, by providing a counterexample to a fundamental
assumption of the algorithm.
Their algorithm is as follows. Let w = a1 · · · an be a string, where a1, . . . , an are characters,
and let r be an ERE. The basic idea of the algorithm is to calculate the (n + 1) × (n + 1) Boolean
matrix T such that for any 0 ≤i < j ≤n,
T[i][j] = 1
⇐⇒

for each negated subexpression ¬r′ of r.
As explained in [Ros07, Proposition 3], this gives an
ERE matching algorithm that runs in O(n3k + n2m) time, where k is the number of complement
operators in r. Then, to obtain a more efficient algorithm with respect to n, a data structure called
jumping machine is introduced for expressing the above matrix more compactly with respect to n.
Definition ([Ros07, Definition 6]). A jumping machine P = (P, p0, π) consists of set P of states,
an initial state p0, and a jumping map π: [0, n−1]×P →([1, n]×P)∪{⊥} with the property that
for any i ∈[0, n −1] and any p ∈P, if π(i, p) = (j, p′), then i < j. Given i ∈[0, n] and p ∈P, we
let πp(i) = {j | ∃p′ ∈P. ∃l ≥1. πl(i, p) = (j, p′)}. Here, πl(i, p) = πl−1(π(i, p)) if π(i, p) ̸= ⊥, and
πl(i, p) = ⊥otherwise for l ≥2, and π1(i, p) = π(i, p). Given a word w = a1 · · · an and an ERE r,
we say that P = (P, p0, π) is a jumping machine for w and r if for any i ∈[0, n −1], we have
πp0(i) = {j ∈[i + 1, n] | ai+1 · · · aj ∈r}.

Conclusion
In this paper, we have studied the hardness of regular expression matching with four well-known
extensions, backreference, squaring, intersection and complement. We have shown in Theorems 1,
2 and 3 that neither straight-line 1-rewb matching, RSQ matching nor semi-ERE matching can be
solved in n2−ε poly(m) time for any ε > 0 under OVC (with log-dimensional OVC, the bound is
improved to n2−ε2o(√m) for straight-line 1-rewb matching and n2−ε2o(m) for the other two), and
k-rewb matching cannot be solved in n2k−ε poly(m) time for any ε > 0 under 2k-OVC (with log-
dimensional 2k-OVC, the bound is improved to n2k−ε2o(√m)). We have also shown in Theorems 4
and 7 that semi-ERE matching and RSQ matching cannot be solved in nω−ε2o(m) time, nor combi-
natorially in n3−ε2o(m) time for any ε > 0 under the k-Clique hypotheses. Moreover, we have shown
in Theorems 5 and 6 that ERE matching cannot be solved in n2−ε tower(o(√m)) time under OVC,
and it cannot be solved in nω−ε tower(o(√m)) time nor combinatorially in n3−ε tower(o(√m)) time
under the k-Clique hypotheses. As mentioned in the introduction, our results entail a number of
interesting theoretical and practical consequences. For instance, our results provide evidence that
unlike the case of lookaround, O(nm)-time matching algorithms for regular expressions extended
with these features (in their full generality) are unlikely to exist, and that the ERE matching algo-
rithm by Hopcroft and Ullman [HU79] was already optimal in the sense described in Section 1.2.
Furthermore, our technique for establishing the lower bounds of Theorems 5 and 6 was also applied
to identify an issue with the algorithm of [Ros07].
A possible direction for future work is to close the gap between the O(n2k+1m) upper bound
and the n2k−ε poly(m) lower bound for k-rewb matching.
Regarding upper bounds for 1-rewb
matching, as mentioned in Section 2, Nogami and Terauchi [NT25a] recently proposed an O(n2m2)-
time algorithm for straight-line 1-rewbs e0(e)1e1\1 · · · el\1el+1 with l = 1. If the general 1-rewb
matching or straight-line 1-rewb matching can also be solved in the same time complexity (or

up to a polynomial factor in m), Theorem 1 provides evidence under OVC that there can be no
further significant improvement in n unless we allow a superpolynomial blowup in m. However,
generalizing their algorithm even to all straight-line 1-rewbs does not seem straightforward, as
the algorithm makes non-trivial uses of the special structure of straight-line 1-rewbs with l = 1.
Another possible direction for future work is to prove lower bounds on the matching problem with
bounded repetition of constant nesting depth. This would provide further insights on the hardness
of bounded repetition in practice, because there it is typically not deeply nested. Finally, regarding
semi-ERE, ERE and RSQ matching, as mentioned in Sections 1 and 2, these problems (even when
extended with both complement and squaring) can be solved in O(nωm) time and combinatorial
O(n3m) time. Although our nω−ε2o(m) and combinatorial n3−ε2o(m) lower bounds (conditionally)
establish optimality of these algorithms in a sense that the exponents of n cannot be shaved by any
constant while preserving the factors from m to be linear (even to be subexponential), it remains
possible that the multiplicative dependence of nω or n3 on m in the O(nωm) and O(n3m) upper
bounds can be significantly improved (e.g., O(nωm1−ε + m) or combinatorial O(n3m1−ε + m) for
some ε > 0). It would be an interesting future work to investigate the (in)existence of matching
algorithms with such running times.
