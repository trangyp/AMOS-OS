Measuring growth and convergence at the mesoscale
Isaak Mengesha† and Debraj Roy†
†University of Amsterdam
January 21, 2026
Abstract
Global inequality has shifted inward, with rising dispersion increasingly occurring within coun-
tries rather than between them. Using 8,790 newly harmonised Functional Urban Areas (FUAs)
—micro-founded labour-market regions encompassing 3.9 billion people and representing approx-
imately 80% of global GDP— we show that national aggregates systematically, and increasingly,
misrepresent the dynamics of growth, convergence, and structural change.
Drawing on high-
resolution global GDP data and country-level capability measures, we find that the middle-income
trampoline that previously drove global convergence is flattening. This divergence in the lower-
income regime does not reflect poverty traps: low-income FUAs exhibit positive expected growth,
and the transition curve displays no stable low-income equilibrium. Instead, productive capabili-
ties—proxied by the Economic Complexity Index—define distinct growth regimes. FUAs converge
within capability strata but diverge across them, and capability upgrading follows a predictable
J-curve marked by short-run disruption and medium-run acceleration. These findings suggest that
national convergence policies may be systematically misaligned with the geographic scale at which
capability accumulation operates.

Introduction
Global inequality has undergone a fundamental geographic reorientation. While between-country in-
come gaps remain substantial, within-country dispersion is rising across the world economy [UN7nd].
Therefore, studying growth at the national level misses where heterogeneity is increasingly located.
Yet other spatial disaggregation – i.e. sub-national administrative boundaries – introduces system-
atic distortions through the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) [Ope84]. Statistical relationships
depend on the scale and zoning of underlying spatial units. Administrative boundaries aggregate histor-
ical circumstances rather than economic integration, obscuring the mechanisms generating divergence,
convergence, and structural adjustment. Evidence for multiple steady states at sub-national scales
is robust, yet global convergence analysis remains constrained by administratively defined regions
rather than economically meaningful spatial units [DJ95, BR12, GLPLDSS14]. When development
constraints are locally embedded, national convergence strategies based on place-neutral policy rea-
soning are structurally misaligned with the geography of growth. [BMRP12].
Functional Urban Areas (FUAs) provide a theoretically grounded alternative. Delineated using
population grids and commuting-flow thresholds, FUAs approximate integrated labour-market regions
rather than political jurisdictions [DPV19]. This matters because knowledge- and complexity-intensive
activities concentrate in cities [BJFP+20], FUAs capture actual adjustment margins to labour-demand
shocks [TS96], and approximately 3.9 billion people live in 8,790 FUAs worldwide [MMSV21]. As a
result, FUAs provide the appropriate spatial scale for studying growth heterogeneity as an outcome of
endogenous adjustment and capability accumulation.
Observed at the FUA level, urban systems display systematic development heterogeneity. Larger
cities enjoy persistent productivity premia [AFKL14], while mature urban systems exhibit diminishing
population-growth advantages [MRHN25]. These regularities imply distinct urban growth regimes that
are mechanically blurred by national aggregates. A natural candidate mechanism is productive ca-
pability accumulation. The Economic Complexity Index (ECI) captures latent productive know-how,
institutional capacity, and diversification potential [HKBH07]. Higher ECI predicts subsequent growth

[VC25]. If urban growth is regime-dependent, capabilities provide a plausible basis for structuring

This motivates the central question: What growth and convergence regimes become visible in the
world economy when development is examined at the scale of functional urban areas, and how does
spatial aggregation shape our understanding of the contribution of productive capabilities? We show
that FUAs recover a substantially clearer growth signal than comparably fine administrative spatial
disaggregation. At the FUA level, income dynamics exhibit stable, nonlinear laws of motion that are
obscured under administratively defined regions. Capability-based growth predictions suffer analogous
attenuation under spatial aggregation: the predictive content of ECI weakens mechanically as eco-
nomically integrated units are fragmented. Across all spatial levels, we find no evidence of poverty
traps in the sense of locally stable low-income steady states. Instead, the data support the existence
of capability-conditioned growth regimes, with convergence occurring within capability strata and di-
vergence across them—a structure that becomes most legible when growth is measured at the scale of
functional urban areas.

Related Literature
Three bodies of research inform this paper’s approach to urban growth dynamics: the convergence
debate and the conditions under which economies catch up, fall behind, or cluster into distinct steady
states; the role of productive capabilities in shaping development trajectories; and the use of functional
urban areas as units of economic analysis. A common theoretical concern cuts across all three. New
Economic Geography demonstrates that increasing returns and transport costs generate multiple spa-
tial equilibria rather than a unique steady state [Kru91], and that economic outcomes depend on market
access and spatial integration rather than administratively defined boundaries [FKV01]. Aggregating
heterogeneous spatial equilibria at the national level therefore mechanically obscures growth and con-
vergence dynamics. Spatial scale is not a neutral measurement choice but a theoretically consequential
one—an insight that motivates our effort to bridge these literatures by embedding capability-driven
dynamics within a convergence framework operationalised at the scale of functional urban areas.
Convergence, growth regimes, and structural transformation
Recent work has reignited debate over whether poorer economies are catching up to richer ones.
[PSS21] documented unconditional convergence at the country level since the 1990s, yet the authors
themselves have since noted a post-2010 reversal [PSS25], corroborated by others [OR23]. This instabil-
ity is puzzling on its own terms, but becomes more so when paired with evidence that between-country
convergence has coincided with rising inequality within countries [ACP+18]. Together, this puts the
conventional approach of measuring convergence at the national level into question.
To situate this concern, consider the theoretical foundations. The Solow model predicts convergence
to a common steady state [Sol56], and early evidence among relatively homogeneous samples—OECD
economies, US states—appeared consistent with this prediction [BSiM92]. Globally, however, uncon-
ditional convergence does not agree with data. The resolution proposed by [MRW92] is conditional
convergence: economies converge not to a universal equilibrium but to heterogeneous steady states de-
termined by structural characteristics—savings rates, population growth, human capital, institutions.
Convergence then operates within groups sharing similar fundamentals; across groups, income gaps
persist.
Models incorporating nonconvexities or complementarities go further, generating multiple
locally stable equilibria [AD90] and the empirical implication of convergence clubs: catch-up within
clusters, divergence between them [DJ95].
What determines club membership? Cross-country variation in output per worker is measured
predominantly by total factor productivity rather than factor accumulation [HJ99]. TFP, in turn, re-
flects organisational efficiency, institutional quality, and technological capacity—closely related to what
capability-based measures of economic complexity aim to capture [HKBH07, HH09]. If capabilities
determine which equilibrium an economy converges toward, then capability accumulation provides a

mechanism linking micro-level productive knowledge to macro-level growth dynamics. A related litera-
ture examines structural transformation—the reallocation of labour from agriculture to manufacturing
to services that accompanies development [HRV14]. Sectoral transitions are also capability transitions:
the knowledge, institutions, and organisational forms required to operate a manufacturing economy
differ fundamentally from those suited to agriculture [HKBH07].
If capability upgrading requires
costly investment and reorganisation, transitions should involve short-run disruption before medium-
run gains materialise. This J-curve pattern—temporary costs followed by delayed benefits—is familiar
from trade liberalisation [BOR04] and the adoption of general purpose technologies [HT94, BRS21],
but has not been systematically examined in the context of capability-driven growth.
Economic complexity as mechanism for development
Economies differ not merely in how much they produce but in what they know how to produce—
knowledge revealed in the composition of output rather than aggregate input stocks [HH09]. The
Economic Complexity Index (ECI) operationalises this insight by measuring the sophistication and
diversification of an economy’s export basket; ECI predicts subsequent growth even after controlling
for standard determinants [HHB+13]. The product space framework provides a mechanism: economies
upgrade toward products that lie close to their existing capability base, while distant jumps require
acquiring multiple capabilities simultaneously, imposing steep coordination costs [HKBH07]. This path
dependence implies that capability position shapes not only current income but also the feasibility of
future transitions—connecting capabilities directly to convergence club membership.
Yet ECI is typically measured at the national level, obscuring within-country heterogeneity. Ca-
pability distributions are highly uneven spatially: complex activities cluster disproportionately in
cities, where agglomeration economies and knowledge spillovers lower the costs of capability formation
[BJFP+20]. National aggregates may therefore misattribute urban capability dynamics to countries,
or mask divergence across regions within converging nations. This concern is underscored by a Simp-
son’s paradox: the correlation between ECI and inequality is negative at the country level but positive
at the regional level [HP22]. Systematic analysis linking capability measures to subnational growth
nonetheless remains scarce [VC25], though recent work has begun to identify ECI as a mechanism for
regional inequality within the EU [PBBH25].
A natural and functional unit of analysis
Agglomeration economies arise from localized mechanisms—learning, matching, and sharing—that
operate within integrated labour markets and dense interaction zones [DP04, FT02]. Administrative
regions do not generally coincide with the spatial extent of these mechanisms. Subnational units such
as NUTS regions, provinces, and districts remain artefacts of political history rather than economic
integration, splitting integrated labour markets or aggregating distinct ones and thereby introducing
measurement error that biases growth estimates [Ans22]. Functional Urban Areas offer a principled
alternative. Because FUAs are delineated by commuting flows, they capture the spatial extent of daily
interaction—the boundary within which knowledge spillovers, labour pooling, and input sharing ac-
tually operate [MMSV21]. This makes FUAs appropriate units for studying capability accumulation:
agglomeration externalities are the micro-foundation of capability formation, and FUAs approximate
where these externalities occur.
Emerging work supports this view.
Economic complexity measured at the FUA level explains
variation in urban growth rates and resilience to shocks [GLPL21, LLP22].
Yet the broader ag-
glomeration literature, while documenting systematic productivity and growth differences across cities
[CG15, RS04], typically takes the spatial unit of observation as given rather than examining how ag-
gregation alters inferred growth laws or convergence estimates [GKSS92]. Modern spatial economics
emphasises quantitative multi-region equilibrium analysis in which outcomes depend on the structure
of spatial interaction across locations [RRH17], but empirical convergence analysis remains largely
conducted at administrative scales. This paper integrates the spatial logic of economic geography into
convergence empirics by changing the unit of analysis. Whether the patterns documented in cross-
country research—conditional convergence, multiple equilibria, convergence clubs—replicate, intensify,
or reverse when examined across functionally defined cities remains an open question. Limitations of

Spatial aggregation and the ECI-growth relationship
The relationship between productive capabilities and growth is spatially localised: agglomeration mech-

4
Empirical Results
The empirical analysis proceeds in three steps.
We first document how the global distribution of
production changes over time and – more importantly – over spatial aggregations for both production
and wealth. Next we investigate global growth and convergence across spatial aggregations and contrast
them with the broader literature. Finally we assess the evidence for capability driven growth and
indicate consequences for future research.
4.1
Where is inequality located in the world economy?
We locate inequality in the world economy by decomposing the cross-sectional variance of GDP per
capita across alternative spatial aggregations. For each year from 1992 to 2019, we decompose scale-
level GDP per capita variance into between-country and within-country components, allowing us to
assess how the geography of inequality depends on the unit of observation.
Two robust patterns emerge. First, between-country inequality dominates at coarse spatial scales
but declines monotonically over time. Second, the within-country component rises steadily, indicating a
persistent inward shift of inequality. This pattern holds throughout the sample period and is consistent
with global convergence accompanied by increasing internal dispersion. However, the level at which
within-country inequality appears dominant depends strongly on spatial aggregation. At the ADM1
level, most variance is mechanically attributed to between-country differences. Finer administrative
units increase the within-country share, but do not eliminate sensitivity to boundary choice. When
inequality is measured at the level of Functional Urban Areas (FUAs), a clear separation re-emerges:
more than 75% of total variance is again accounted for by between-country differences. Measured at
the scale of integrated labour markets, cross-country inequality is therefore substantially more stable.
We note that nighttime-lights-based GDP underrepresents rural and agricultural activity, and that
space-filling administrative units may introduce additional aggregation error.
To verify that these results are not specific to GDP measurement, we run a similar analysis using an
independent wealth metric. We employ the Relative Wealth Index (RWI), an asset-based household
wealth measure derived from DHS and related surveys [Cen25]. For countries with at least twelve
FUAs, we decompose within-country wealth variance across spatial scales and regress within-unit in-
equality on national GDP per capita. Spatial scale again proves decisive. At administrative levels
(ADM1–ADM3), there is no systematic relationship between income and internal inequality. At the
FUA level, a positive association emerges: richer countries exhibit greater wealth dispersion within
their urban systems. While marginally significant (r2 = 0.146, p = 0.097), this relationship disappears
under administrative aggregation. Within-FUA inequality accounts for roughly 85–90% of national
RWI variance, compared to less than 50% at the ADM2 level, where estimates are also substantially
more volatile. Across all spatial levels, within-unit inequality increases with GDP per capita, but only
FUAs recover a stable relationship.
These results have three implications. First, the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem is not merely a
statistical inconvenience: administrative boundaries systematically distort the spatial allocation of in-
equality by misaligning economic integration with political geography. Second, the findings provide a
spatial interpretation of the dynamics underlying Milanovi´c’s elephant curve: as between-country gaps
narrow, inequality rises within countries, concentrated in urban labour markets rather than adminis-
trative regions [Mil16]. Third, the evidence contradicts a subnational Kuznets hypothesis. Internal
spatial inequality does not decline with development; instead, richer urban systems exhibit greater
internal differentiation, consistent with agglomeration models in which productivity gains increasingly
concentrate in dense labour markets [CG15].

4.2
Is convergence sensitive to scale?
We assess whether measured convergence depends on the spatial unit of analysis by estimating β-
convergence, following Equation 1, across five spatial scales: countries (ADM0), administrative sub-
divisions (ADM1–ADM3), and Functional Urban Areas (FUAs). This allows us to isolate the role
of spatial aggregation while holding the underlying GDP data fixed. While GDP estimates derived
from nighttime lights are noisy and biased [MLSM15, CGC+22] – more importantly – these distor-
tions apply uniformly across all spatial aggregations constructed from the same raster. Differences in
estimated convergence rates across scales therefore reflect zoning and aggregation choices rather than
NTL-specific measurement error. To anchor the magnitudes, we benchmark country-level estimates
against the Penn World Tables. As expected, NTL-based ADM0 estimates show weaker convergence
than PWT, consistent with attenuation from measurement noise (see Fig 3a). FUAs exhibit stronger
convergence than both administrative units and PWT. One might worry this reflects saturation bias,
which compresses high-income estimates and could mechanically generate faster apparent catch-up
[MLSM15]. However, FUAs also exhibit the highest between-country variance share across all spa-
tial scales (Figure 2); if saturation dominated, this share would be attenuated. This indicates signal
recovery rather than artifact.
Cross-dataset discrepancies at the country level are well documented—PWT, Maddison, and WDI
differ substantially in levels, though long-run trends broadly align [PSS21, PSS25]. Our contribution

4.3
Changes to the urban law of motion
We examine how the relationship between income and growth at the urban level departs from the
canonical Solow framework. Figure 4 (left) plots annual GDP per capita growth against initial income
for all FUAs. Averaged across the full sample, the relationship is consistent with global convergence:
poorer FUAs grow faster than richer ones. However, the conditional mean exhibits an inverted U-
shape, indicative of a middle-income acceleration followed by slower growth at high income levels.
This pattern is not stable over time. In the later half of the sample period, the middle-income
acceleration weakens substantially, and growth among the richest FUAs slows markedly. These shifts
suggest that the urban growth process is not governed by a time-invariant law of motion, but evolves
with the global development environment. Figure 4 (right) presents the corresponding Solow phase
diagram. The 45-degree line represents average global growth; deviations from this line indicate rela-
tive convergence or divergence across FUAs. Standard poverty-trap models predict that the transition
curve falls below the diagonal at low income levels, generating a locally stable low-income equilibrium
[AD90]. We find no evidence for such a mechanism. Expected growth remains positive for low-income
FUAs throughout the income distribution.
The transition curve never intersects the diagonal from above at low income levels.
There is
therefore no locally stable poverty trap at the scale of functional urban areas.
This finding rules
out capital-threshold mechanisms as the primary explanation for persistent urban income differences,
at least at currently observed capital levels [Sol56, AD90].
The implication is that urban income
stratification cannot be accounted for within the standard Solow framework. Persistent divergence
across cities must instead reflect heterogeneity in productive capabilities, institutional environments,
or other factors shaping the urban growth process beyond factor accumulation alone.

4.4
Empirical regularities of capability-driven development
Capability-dependent growth.
We examine how convergence dynamics vary with productive ca-
pabilities by relating country-level Economic Complexity Index (ECI) values to the growth performance
of their constituent FUAs over time. Each FUA is assigned its country’s contemporaneous ECI, re-
flecting the national capability environment in which urban labour markets operate. This approach is
motivated by evidence that complex economic activities disproportionately concentrate in large cities
[?]. Across the full support of the ECI distribution, FUAs exhibit conditional convergence: estimated
β coefficients remain positive at all capability levels. This rules out capability-conditioned divergence
and indicates that poorer FUAs tend to grow faster than richer ones regardless of their position in
capability space. However, convergence speed varies sharply with ECI. The relationship between β
and capabilities is strongly nonlinear rather than smooth or monotonic.
At low levels of ECI, convergence speeds are uniformly low and display little variation. FUAs
embedded in low-capability environments exhibit compressed growth responses, with small and rel-
atively flat β estimates. In contrast, convergence accelerates sharply at intermediate capability lev-
els. Around ECI values of approximately 0.4–0.7, β rises abruptly, producing a pronounced spike in
convergence speed. This transition is discrete rather than incremental, suggesting a regime shift in
the underlying growth process associated with intermediate levels of productive capabilities. At the
upper end of the capability distribution, convergence slows again. High-ECI FUAs exhibit weaker
conditional convergence despite higher income levels, consistent with observed growth slowdowns in
mature, high-capability urban economies. Notably, the peak in convergence coincides with the region
of highest growth dispersion: intermediate ECI and income levels are associated with the widest spread
of growth outcomes, aligning with the location of the convergence spike.

Capability accumulation rate.
Following Hidalgo et al., we study the dynamics of capability ac-
cumulation by estimating a concave learning function for changes in the Economic Complexity Index
(ECI) [HS25]. We calibrate the parameters of this function using observed transitions in ECI over time.
At the country level, ECI dynamics exhibit conditional convergence. Figure 6a shows that changes
in ECI are non-monotonic in initial complexity: countries with intermediate ECI levels experience
the largest average increases, while both low- and high-complexity countries display slower capability
growth. The relationship between initial ECI and subsequent ECI change is concave, with a peak
around r∗≈0.61, marking a transitional region in capability space.
In contrast, subnational dynamics differ sharply.
Figure 6b shows that ADM1 regions exhibit
divergence in capabilities: initial ECI is positively and significantly related to subsequent ECI growth
(β = 0.74, p < 0.001). High-complexity regions systematically accumulate further capabilities, while
low-complexity regions tend to stagnate or decline. As a result, the distribution of ECI changes widens
substantially at the subnational level, particularly among low-complexity regions. The sign reversal in
ECI dynamics across spatial scales is not driven by differences in data or measurement. Both panels
use the same underlying OEC data; the divergence arises from spatial aggregation alone. Conditional
convergence at the country level therefore masks strong within-country capability concentration, a
pattern consistent with recent regional evidence from Europe [PBBH25]. More generally, these results
indicate that spatial aggregation alters the apparent law of motion of productive capabilities, yielding
qualitatively different dynamics at national and subnational scales.

Capability upgrades effect on growth.
We examine the dynamic growth response to discrete
increases in productive capabilities using an event-study design. At the level of Functional Urban
Areas, ECI upgrades are associated with an immediate but temporary growth deceleration. In both
specifications, FUA growth declines in the year of the upgrade (t), indicating short-run adjustment
costs following capability accumulation. This slowdown is short-lived. Growth recovers within one to
two years after the upgrade, with positive and increasing effects from t+1 onward. Medium-run gains
substantially exceed the initial losses: FUA growth peaks around t+3, surpassing pre-upgrade growth
rates in panel (a) and remaining robustly positive in panel (b). The resulting temporal profile follows
a clear J-curve, characterized by short-run contraction and subsequent sustained acceleration.
No comparable pattern is observed at the ADM1 level. Growth in administrative regions remains
close to zero throughout the event window, with no pronounced post-upgrade rebound. The diver-
gence between FUA and ADM1 responses emerges immediately after the upgrade and persists over
the medium run. Importantly, the J-curve dynamic at the FUA level is robust to treatment defini-
tion: similar responses appear whether capability upgrades are identified at the country level or at the
ADM1 level. These results indicate that outcome scale, rather than treatment resolution, determines
signal recovery. Growth responses to capability upgrading are sharply visible when measured at the
level of functional urban areas but are largely attenuated under administrative aggregation. Confi-
dence intervals confirm statistically meaningful medium-run effects for FUAs, while ADM1 estimates
remain imprecise and centered near zero. Together, these findings document scale-dependent adjust-
ment dynamics following capability upgrading and motivate FUA-level analysis of capability–growth
interactions.

Discussion
This paper has examined growth and convergence dynamics at various scales. We argued why the
urban scale is crucial to understand the coupling of growth and convergence through capability accu-
mulation. The analysis yields a set of empirical regularities that challenge standard accounts of global
development and reframe how we understand the geography of growth. We summarise these findings,
note limitations, and outline directions for future research.
Six stylized facts emerge from the analysis. (1) Global convergence rates are modulated by the spa-
tial aggregation of recorded growth. Trends such as urbanization and other mechanisms point at FUAs
as meaningful unit of analysis. (2) Classical poverty traps receive no empirical support at the urban
scale. Low-income FUAs do not exhibit the negative or insufficient growth rates required to sustain a
stable low-income equilibrium. The transition curve implies positive expected growth throughout the
income distribution. Whatever generates persistent urban poverty, it is not the capital-threshold mech-
anism emphasised in canonical models—persistent divergence must arise from another factor rather
than accumulation constraints. (3) While Capability accumulation on a national level exhibits the
established inverted U-shape, subnational level capability accumulation increases with existing capabil-
ity levels. This implies conditioned convergence of capabilities across countries and divergence within
countries. This might not result in GDP per capita divergence within countries – due to a shared
labor market and Baumol effect – but in the concentration of capabilities, opportunities, and human
capital. (4) Capability upgrading follows a J-curve dynamic. Increases in national ECI are associated
with temporary growth slowdowns in the FUAs of that country, followed by acceleration in subsequent
periods. This pattern suggests that absorbing new productive knowledge requires costly reallocation
and learning—transitional costs that are predictable and systematic rather than idiosyncratic. Even
if treatment and measurement match at ADM1 level, the effect remains strongest when measured in
FUAs contained by the respective region. (5) Inequality is increasingly a within-country and within-
FUA phenomenon. The between-country share of global income variance has declined steadily since
the early 1990s while the within-country share has risen – across all spatial aggregations. Moreover,
richer urban areas exhibit more wealth inequality within than between their urban systems. Inequal-
ity is not only shifting inward geographically; it is concentrating within the cities that drive national
growth, highlighting the importance of asset- and productivity-based inequality metrics at the urban
scale. (6) FUAs are spatially interdependent. Growth rates exhibit significant spatial correlation that
decays with distance, implying that cities are neither independent observations nor closed systems (see
Fig. 11 in SI). They are embedded in regional and global networks through which shocks, knowledge,
and resources flow. Any complete account of urban growth dynamics must incorporate this spatial
structure.
12


## Page 13


These findings are subject to limitations that bound the conclusions we can draw. The analysis
is restricted to functional urban areas; rural regions—where structural transformation, market ac-
cess, and agricultural productivity constraints may generate different dynamics—remain outside the
dataset, and the absence of poverty traps at the urban scale does not imply their absence elsewhere.
Capabilities are measured at the national level, so FUAs inherit country-level ECI values that ob-
scure within-country variation in productive structures; subnational capability metrics would sharpen
the analysis considerably. The evidence is descriptive rather than causal: capabilities predict regime
membership, but whether they cause it—and through what channels—remains open. Finally, GDP
is proxied via calibrated nighttime-lights data, introducing measurement error that validation against
subnational accounts could address.
The stylized facts open several avenues for future work. Most pressing is the construction of city-
level capability measures using patents, occupational composition, or firm-level production networks;
this would test directly whether within-country capability heterogeneity explains residual variation in
urban growth dynamics and allow the framework developed here to operate at its natural scale. The
observed spatial correlation in growth rates motivates models that explicitly incorporate spillovers and
network effects—how far do shocks propagate, through what channels, and what determines the geog-
raphy of synchronisation versus decoupling? Extending the analysis to include rural areas would clarify
whether the dynamics documented here are specific to cities or reflect broader patterns, and would
illuminate the interaction between urban capability accumulation and rural structural transformation.
Finally, the regularities documented here invite mechanistic explanation. Why do capabilities shift
the transition function? What frictions generate the J-curve? Answering these questions will require
theoretical models disciplined by the empirical patterns this paper has established. The mesoscale
matters: functional urban areas reveal growth dynamics that national aggregates obscure, and pro-
ductive capabilities structure these dynamics in ways that standard convergence frameworks do not
anticipate.
