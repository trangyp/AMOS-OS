Abstract
House allocation is an extremely well-studied problem in the field of fair allocation, where the goal is to
assign n houses to n agents while satisfying certain fairness criterion, e.g., envy-freeness. To model social
interactions, the Graphical House Allocation framework introduces a social graph G, in which each vertex
corresponds to an agent, and an edge (u, v) corresponds to the potential of agent u to envy the agent v,
based on their allocations and valuations. In undirected social graphs, the potential for envy is in both
the directions. In the Minimum Envy Graphical House Allocation (ME-GHA) problem, given a set
of n agents, n houses, a social graph, and agent’s valuation functions, the goal is to find an allocation
that minimizes the total envy summed up over all the edges of G. Recent work, [Hosseini et al., AAMAS
2023, AAMAS 2024] studied ME-GHA in the regime of polynomial-time algorithms, and designed exact
and approximation algorithms, for certain graph classes under identical agent valuations.
We initiate the study of ME-GHA with non-identical valuations, a setting that has so far remained un-
explored. We investigate the multivariate (parameterized) complexity of ME-GHA by identifying structural
restrictions on the social graph and valuation functions that yield tractability. We also design moderately
exponential-time algorithms for several graph classes, and a polynomial-time algorithm for binary valuations
that returns an allocation with envy at most one when the social graph has maximum degree at most one.

Introduction
Fair division is an important area at the intersection of economics and computer science that deals with allocating
resources to agents in a manner that ensures some notion(s) of fairness. House allocation is an important special
case of fair division, where the goal is to allocate m ≥n indivisible items (called houses) to n agents, with
each agent receiving exactly one house. Fairness is commonly measured using criteria such as envy-freeness,
proportionality, pareto-optimality, among others. This model dates back to the work of Shapley and Scarf
[1974], and has received significant attention in the fair division community Miyagawa [2001, 2002], Abraham
et al. [2005].
Envy-freeness (and its relaxations) is by far the most well-studied fairness criteria, even in the general fair
division setting. While an overwhelming majority of the literature has considered the setting where each pair of
agents is capable of envying each other—of course, depending on the allocation of houses to the two agents, and
their respective valuations, in many real-life scenarios, however, agents are part of social networks consisting of
their peers, friends, relatives etc. In such cases, an agent will be concerned about the house (item) allocated
to themselves, as compared to those assigned to their social connections, while they are indifferent to the items
allocated to complete strangers. The classical house allocation model fails to capture this degree of locality of
fairness. There is a growing body of literature that addresses this issue by introducing the notion of social
graph. A social graph is an undirected graph G = (V, E), where each vertex v ∈V corresponds to each of the
n agents, and an edge between two agents u and v represents that the two agents are socially relevant to each
other, implying the potential of envy between them. In other words, an agent is only capable to envy (and be
envied by) its neighbors in the social graph. This formulation was first introduced by Beynier et al. [2019] in the
house allocation setting, and by Bredereck et al. [2022], Eiben et al. [2023] in the general envy-free allocation
setting. However, all of these works had only considered the envy-free allocation setting, which is not always
possible.

Hosseini et al. 2023 introduced the more general problem called Minimum Envy Graphical House Al-
location (ME-GHA in short), where the goal is to find an allocation that minimizes the total envy added up
across all the edges. In the input to ME-GHA, we are given a social graph G = (V, E), where V is a set of n
agents, a set H of n houses, and n valuation functions αv : H →Q≥0 for each agent v ∈V . We represent an in-
stance of ME-GHA as (G, H, {αv}v∈V ). A bijection π : V →H is called an allocation. The envy of an allocation
π is computed using the following expression: P
(u,v)∈E envy(u, v, π(u), π(v)), where the envy across the edge
(u, v) w.r.t. π, is given by envy(u, v, π(u), π(v)) = max {αv(π(u)) −αv(π(v)), 0}+max {αu(π(v)) −αu(π(u)), 0}.
The goal of the ME-GHA problem is to find an allocation π that minimizes the envy.
Although Hosseini et al. [2023] introduced this general model, they only focused the case of identical valua-
tions (we also call as identical agents), where the valuation functions αv are identical for all agents v ∈V . They
noted that the problem in fact generalizes the well-known Optimal Linear Arrangement (OLA) problem,
and is therefore computationally challenging – i.e., NP-hard, thereby ruling out polynomial-time algorithms
unless P = NP. On the positive side, they designed polynomial-time algorithms when the social graph is a path,
cycle, or a clique. In a follow-up work, Hosseini et al. [2024] designed polynomial-time approximation algorithms
for ME-GHA on different classes of connected graphs, such as trees, planar graphs. However, because of the
lower bounds inherited from OLA, the approximation ratios obtained were far from desirable.

Our results and contributions
This state of art, leaves a major gap in our understanding of the complexity of ME-GHA with non-identical
valuations, which we address in this work.
The problem is NP-hard even when the social graph has maximum degree at most one Beynier et al. [2019].
However, under the additional restriction of identical valuations, it becomes polynomial-time solvable Hosseini
et al. [2023]. We design a polynomial-time algorithm that returns an allocation with envy at most one when the
valuations are binary, i.e., each valuation function maps a house to 0 or 1 (which can be thought of as “like”
and “dislike”), and the social graph is of maximum degree one (Theorem 3.2). Our result also implies that, for
such a social graph, for an odd number of agents, there is envy-free allocation, and our algorithm achieves this.
We also show that there are instances with optimal envy one when the number of agents is even.
Since the problem is NP-hard for identical valuations (i.e., agents), the next natural question is the complexity
when houses are identical and agents need not be identical. The problem is trivially polynomial-time solvable
as we can assign any house to an agent because an agent values all the houses equally. This tractability result
and the following practical scenario motivate us to study the parameter number of house types (defined in the
next paragraph).
In the eponymous application of the problem, it is much more likely that each house is characterized by a
limited number of features, such as the total area, the number of rooms, and the neighborhood. In many real-
world settings, especially in urban housing markets, a large fraction of houses belong to apartment complexes
where multiple units are essentially identical in terms of layout, size, amenities, and location. E.g., a single
apartment building may contain dozens of units with the same floor plan and specifications, and hence these
units are naturally perceived as having the same value by the agents. Such features define the “type” of a house,
and typically the number of house-types ℓis small, whereas there is varying—but usually high—multiplicity of
houses of each type. Note that, in this case, each agent can have a different valuation for each feature, and
therefore they can value each house-type differently. Furthermore, different agents’ valuations for a house-type
can be completely different; however each agent will value all houses of the same type equally.
As we discussed above, ME-GHA is trivially polynomial-time solvable for ℓ= 1; however, for ℓ= 2, it
is NP-hard due to a reduction similar to [Hosseini et al., 2023, Theorem 3.2] (instead of concentrating half of
the values around 0, we set it to 0. Similarly, remaining half of the values are set to 1. We present the proof
for completeness in Theorem 4.1). Note that in this reduction, valuations are binary and agents are identical.
Thus, the problem is NP-hard even when number of house types is constant, agents are identical, and valuations
are binary. Thus, in the hope of tractability, we explore structural properties of the social graph, when ℓis
constant.

literature: (i) treewidth (tw)1, (ii) vertex cover number (vc), and (iii) clique modulator (k), defined as the
distance to a complete graph. Note that, the problem is NP-hard even for trees Hosseini et al. [2024], thus,

Parameterized complexity provides a refined framework for analyzing computational problems with respect
to an additional parameter that captures structural properties of the input.
Definition 2.2 (Parameterized Problem). A parameterized problem is a decision or optimization problem whose
input is a pair (I, k), where I is the main input and k ∈N is a parameter. The size of the input is denoted as
|I|.
Definition 2.3 (Fixed-Parameter Tractability (FPT) and XP). A parameterized problem is fixed-parameter
tractable (FPT) (resp. XP) with respect to a parameter k if it can be solved in time f(k) · |I|O(1) (resp. |I|f(k)),
for some computable function f depending only on k.
Definition 2.4 (W-hardness). A parameterized problem is W[1]-hard (resp. W[t]-hard) if every problem in the
class W[1] (resp. W[t]) can be reduced to it via a parameterized reduction. Unless FPT = W[1], no W[1]-hard
problem admits an FPT algorithm.

We next recall the notion of treewidth, a fundamental measure of graph sparsity that plays a central role in
the design of dynamic programming algorithms on graphs. Treewidth-based techniques will be extensively used

Definition 2.5 (Tree Decomposition and Treewidth). A tree decomposition of a graph G = (V, E) is a pair

2. For every edge (u, v) ∈E, there exists a bag Bt ∈B such that {u, v} ⊆Bt.
3. For every vertex v ∈V , the set {t ∈V (T ) | v ∈Bt} induces a connected subtree of T .

denoted tw(G), is the minimum width over all tree decompositions of G.
Definition 2.6 (Nice Tree Decomposition). A nice tree decomposition of a graph G = (V, E) is a tree decom-
position (T , B) where T is a rooted tree and every node of T is of one of the following four types:
• Leaf Node: A node t with an empty bag, i.e., Bt = ∅.
• Introduce Node: A node t with a single child t′ such that Bt = Bt′ ∪{v} for some vertex v /∈Bt′.
• Forget Node: A node t with a single child t′ such that Bt = Bt′ \ {v} for some vertex v ∈Bt′.
• Join Node: A node t with two children t1 and t2, where Bt = Bt1 = Bt2.
It is well known that any tree decomposition of width k can be transformed, in linear time, into an equivalent
nice tree decomposition of width at most k and with O(nk) nodes, where n = |V | Cygan et al. [2015].
We next recall several standard graph classes that appear in our complexity results.
Definition 2.7 (Vertex Cover). A vertex cover of a graph G = (V, E) is a set C ⊆V such that for every edge
(u, v) ∈E, at least one of u or v belongs to C.
Definition 2.8 (Split Graph). A graph G = (V, E) is a split graph if its vertex set can be partitioned into two
sets C and I, where C is a clique and I is an independent set.
Definition 2.9 (Bipartite Graph). A graph G = (V, E) is called bipartite if its vertex set V can be partitioned
into two disjoint sets L and R such that every edge e ∈E has exactly one endpoint in L and the other in R.
The pair (L, R) is called a bipartition of G.
A bipartite graph G = (L ∪R, E) is called complete bipartite if every vertex in L is adjacent to every vertex
in R.

Outlook
In this work, we initiate a systematic study of the algorithmic complexity of Minimum Envy Graphical
House Allocation, when the agents may have differing valuations for houses. To this end, we employed tech-
niques from parameterized complexity and identified several natural parameterizations that makes the problem
tractable, when the number of house types is bounded. In the most general scenario, without any restriction on
the number of house types, we improve upon the trivial O⋆(n!) running time obtained via brute-force, we also
designed moderately exponential-time algorithms for Minimum Envy Graphical House Allocation that
run in O⋆(2O(n)) time in graphs of treewidth O(n/ log n), specifically, for planar graphs.

Our work opens the door to several new directions and leaves some interesting open questions. Perhaps
the most intriguing concrete question is whether ME-GHA on path graphs or disjoint edges with binary (and
more generally, bi-valued) valuations is solvable in polynomial time. Another promising direction is designing
tractable algorithms for (non-complete) bipartite graphs where both sides are “large”. At a broader level, it
would be interesting to explore FPT-approximation algorithms for ME-GHA that simultaneously (a) achieve
improved approximation ratios compared to the polynomial-time regime Hosseini et al. [2024] and (b) run faster
than the exact parameterized algorithms developed in this work. Finally, an important open direction is to
establish meaningful lower bounds for ME-GHA under various parameterizations to understand the optimality
of our algorithms.
