Abstract—Recent advancements in Large Language Models
(LLMs) offer new opportunites to create natural language
interfaces for Autonomous Driving Systems (ADSs), moving
beyond rigid inputs. This paper addresses the challenge of
mapping the complexity of human language to the structured
action

I. INTRODUCTION
Autonomous
Driving
Systems
(ADSs)
have

software frameworks such as Autoware [1]. While these
systems
offer

and

their interaction interfaces are typically reactive to the
environment or just hardcoded inputs [2]. With the emergence
of Large Language Models (LLMs), new possibilities arise to
bridge human intent and machine autonomy through natural
language. LLMs have shown remarkable generalization across
tasks, including instruction following, question answering,
and domain adaptation [3]. Recent efforts have begun
exploring the integration of LLMs into ADS pipelines, for
instance, enabling passengers to influence driving behavior

However, many approaches are restricted to simulation

under operational constraints such as system latency, message
safety, and runtime behavior remains limited [4]–[6].
In this paper, we present a framework for LLM-assisted
co-pilot interaction with Autoware, enabling passengers to
influence and query the ADS using natural language. Unlike
end-to-end control architectures [7], our approach focuses on
high-level interaction: commands and questions are interpreted

II. RELATED WORK
Foundation models, such as LLMs and Vision Language
Models (VLMs), are increasingly investigated in the context
of autonomous driving. They have been applied to tasks
ranging from scene understanding to trajectory reasoning
and high-level decision support. In the following, we first
outline recent work on the general use of foundation
models in autonomous driving. We then focus on hybrid
approaches that couple natural language processing with
structured ADS software stacks, enabling user interaction
through language-based commands and queries.
A. Foundation Models in Autonomous Driving
Several
studies
have
investigated
the
integration
of
LLMs and VLMs into different stages of the autonomous
driving
pipeline.
The
authors
of
DriveGPT4
[8],
[9],
LMDrive
[10],
and
SimLingo
[11]
employ
multimodal
transformer architectures that fuse camera or LiDAR inputs
with linguistic features to generate low-level control signals
or
waypoints
in
an
end-to-end
fashion.
These
models
demonstrate better interpretability and scene comprehension
through natural language reasoning and achieve strong results
in simulation. However, they remain constrained to simulated
environments due to latency, data dependency, and the limited
verifiability of large models in safety-critical control loops.
VLP [12] and DiMA [13] use LLMs to inject semantic
priors
and
common
sense
reasoning
into
vision-based
planners, while DualAD [14] combines textual reasoning with
rule-based motion planning to enhance robustness in complex
traffic situations. Similar works treat LLMs as high-level
reasoning agents that guide behavior planning across multiple
tasks [15]–[17].
At the perception level, Li et al. [18], Wu et al. [19],
and Ran et al. [20] deploy language-grounded approaches
for object tracking, segmentation, and scene explanation. By
linking visual and textual representations, these works improve
the semantic understanding and interpretability of driving
scenes. Nevertheless, real-world validation remains limited,
and the integration of foundation models into operational ADS
frameworks has yet to be realized.
B. Natural Language Interaction and Human-in-the-Loop
Autonomous Driving
Another line of work explores how LLMs can facilitate
communication between humans and Autonomous Vehicles
(AVs), enabling explainable interaction, adaptive behavior, and
shared decision-making.
Wang et al. [4] were among the first to propose an
LLM-based co-pilot that interprets user intent and driving
style preferences to adapt vehicle behavior, demonstrating the
potential of conversational control, though limited to simulated
scenarios. Subsequent approaches extended this idea toward
interactive reasoning. Cui et al. [21], [22] treat the LLM as a
cognitive core that interprets spoken instructions and explains
its decisions, while the authors in [5], [23] realize real-world
prototypes, integrating LLM reasoning and personalization
into full software stacks. These systems support interaction and
field-tested user adaptation, but are limited to a few specific
driving scenarios.
Song et al. [6] advance this concept with Autoware.Flex,
translating natural language commands into modifications of
Autoware modules, which makes it not directly applicable
to other software frameworks, and tests remain limited to
specific low-speed scenarios. Other studies focus on user
understanding rather than control. Luo et al. [24] introduce an
interpreter that provides visual and spoken feedback to explain
vehicle actions, thereby enhancing user trust. Choudhary et
al. [25] present Talk2BEV, which allows querying BEV scenes
through language, demonstrating explainable perception in
simulation.
Beyond technical frameworks, Stampf et al. [26] explore
LLM-powered
negotiation
strategies
between
passengers
and
AVs,
showing
that
adaptive
conversational
styles
enhance trust and acceptance. Overall, existing research
successfully demonstrates that language interfaces can enhance
transparency, personalization, and cooperation within ADS.
However, most current systems either rely on simplified setups
or directly delegate safety-critical decisions to the LLM itself.
Consequently, a modular, safety-preserving framework that
interfaces natural language requests with established ADS
architectures, enabling a wide range of interactions, from
configuration parameters to local scenario-based decisions,
remains largely unexplored.
To address this gap, we introduce an application-centric
framework that uses a dedicated validation layer. This
approach safely maps high-level user commands to the
vehicle’s
structured
action
space,
decoupling
language
interpretation from the safety-critical execution loop.
III. METHODOLOGY
The core challenge is translating spoken instructions I
from an infinite, continuous language space into a structured,
executable policy P. This policy consists of finite actions A,
but may include continuous parameters C, making the
target P
= {A, C} a continuous, bounded policy space.
LLMs have been proven to be promising for this kind of
complex high-level task, even without extensive retraining of
the underlying model.
Based on this, we propose the use of an LLM as the
backbone of a general framework for the execution of spoken
instructions by a passenger using a modular AV software stack,
as shown in Figure 2. A spoken instruction I together with
status information from the AV software and a knowledge
base, which consists of general information about the task
as well as detailed descriptions about the expected output
format, are forwarded to the LLM. The output is an extracted
command, which is executed by the AV software using a
validation and interface node. This framework is designed
to bridge the gap between the infinite complexity of human
language and the structured requirements of executing tasks
with the AV software.

A. Taxonomy of Human-ADS-Interactions
As demonstrated in Section II, existing approaches that
enable human input using LLMs are often restricted to the
execution of specific tasks. However, this paper aims to
establish a general framework for connecting LLMs to a
modular AV software stack, thus enabling the execution of
a wide range of abstract human commands and requests.
To address the challenge of mapping the infinite instruction
space I to the structured, finite action set A, we first establish
a theoretical categorization of different use cases and tasks
that a human passenger might seek to achieve by interacting
with the AV system. This categorization serves two primary
purposes: First, defining a system scope by clearly delineating
the specific interaction types that can be facilitated by a
language-based system and second, providing a necessary
taxonomy for better describing and evaluating the capabilities
of language-based interaction systems. This can not only be
applied in the scope of this work but also for future research
and standardization efforts in autonomous driving.
An overview of the five categories of passenger interaction
with the AV software is shown in Table I. The Information
category covers requests for specific data, like vehicle velocity
or software operation mode. The system queries and presents
this data without executing a driving action. Mission Control
requests serve as direct input for the mission planning
component, such as setting a destination or starting/stopping
the autonomous drive. Enabling this via spoken language,
rather than just operator input, increases system flexibility.
The Configuration category includes requests for long-term
behavioral changes, such as setting velocity or acceleration
limits. These parameters allow passengers to adjust the
overall driving experience to their individual comfort level.
Cooperation focuses on requests for local driving maneuvers,
like lane changes. The passenger initiates the request, but the
AV software performs the final execution subject to its own
safety checks, making this a high-level planning interaction.
The Intervention category holds requests to override the AV’s
internal state, typically due to false detections or decisions,
such as a misread traffic light, ghost objects, or undetected
objects. The passenger’s command serves as supplementary
information for the planning process, enabling a safe resolution
of the situation.
B. Language Interface for Passenger Interaction
Based on the categorization of passenger interactions
defined in Table I, a Domain Specific Language (DSL) is
proposed, which accommodates all defined actions belonging
to these interaction categories. The primary task of the LLM
is to translate the natural language user request I into a
command P, structured in this specific DSL format, making
it interpretable and executable by the AV software stack.

C. Integration into Autoware Stack
To enable the execution of the DSL commands within
Autoware, a dedicated ROS2 Interface Node is employed. This
node is responsible for parsing the structured information from
the DSL command and triggering the corresponding execution
by sending the appropriate ROS2 command, service call, or
parameter adjustment. The entire integration architecture is
conceptually illustrated in Figure 3.
The interface node also acts as an essential Action
Validation Layer. The mapping from a DSL action to a specific
execution in Autoware must be built manually and explicitly.
This explicit mapping acts as a safety measure, ensuring
that only allowed actions with validated parameter bounds
are executed. Without this verification, the system could be
compromised by false interpretations, LLM hallucinations, or
malicious commands, posing a serious safety risk. This explicit
mapping, therefore, maintains the separation of concerns.
D. Two-Stage Architecture for Feedback Generation
To avoid on-board GPU constraints, we use a cloud-based
Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite LLM, which also allows us to bypass
a separate Speech-to-Text (STT) step by processing audio
instructions directly. For high system transparency and reliable
passenger feedback, a two-stage generation architecture is
used, outlined in Figure 2. This approach utilizes a feedback
loop to provide a response based on the actual outcome of the
requested action.
The process begins in the first stage, where the LLM
translates the spoken instruction into the structured DSL
command. This command is immediately forwarded to the
ROS2 Interface Node for validation and execution within the
Autoware stack.
The second stage commences after the Interface Node
receives the definitive success or failure state from the
Autoware stack. Crucially, this execution status, along with
the original user intent, is fed back into the LLM for a second
API call. This two-step process ensures that the final passenger
feedback is accurate and contextually relevant, reflecting the
actual outcome of the action. This approach also provides
essential system transparency to the user by communicating
the rationale for any rejected command.
The LLM generated response is then converted into an audio
file using a Text-to-Speech (TTS) framework, which is played
back to the passenger as audio feedback.

A. Action Translation Experiments
For the quantitative evaluation of our architecture, a dataset
of N = 200 pairs of human instructions, along with their
corresponding DSL commands is used. This dataset was
created manually and includes instructions from all five
interaction categories with a roughly equal distribution, as well
as an additional category, which includes all requests, that are
outside the scope of this system. The dataset consists of partly
repeating DSL commands, but the instructions vary by their
tone and the directness.
This dedicated instruction dataset is utilized to assess the
translation quality and response time of our system. Human
instructions from this set are first synthesized into an audio
file using a TTS model to simulate human input. These
spoken instructions are then forwarded to the LLM. Our
full Baseline system receives three primary inputs: status
information from the AV software, the system’s knowledge
base (KB), which outlines the task and the DSL structure,
and a small set of in-context learning (ICL) examples. To
evaluate the contribution of these inputs, we defined three
ablation configurations: No AV Status (removes dynamic
vehicle context), Zero-Shot (removes ICL examples), and
KB-Only (removes both AV status and ICL). This evaluation
is focused on the instruction translation step, comparing the
LLM’s predicted DSL output to the ground truth.
The results of this ablation study are presented in Table II.
The Baseline system demonstrated high performance, correctly
translating 194 instructions for an accuracy of 97.0%.
Removing the AV status information had a negligible impact,
resulting in 96.0% accuracy, as most dataset instructions
were not context-dependent. In contrast, removing the ICL
examples caused a significant performance collapse. The
Zero-Shot accuracy dropped to 73.0%, and the KB-Only
system fell further to 66.3%. This indicates that while the
LLM can perform tasks using only the DSL definition, the
ICL examples are critical for achieving robust and reliable
translation accuracy.

V. CONCLUSION & OUTLOOK
This paper introduced a LLM-based interaction framework
designed to enable passengers to influence the behavior of
an autonomous vehicle running a modular software stack.
A key contribution is the categorization of five interaction
types, which forms the basis for a robust, application-centric
DSL. Our translation evaluation demonstrated a high Baseline
accuracy of 97.0% at tr
= 1.723 s. An ablation study
confirmed that ICL was critical to this result, as the Zero-Shot
accuracy dropped significantly to 73.0%. The system’s safety
and transparency are ensured by a dedicated Validation and
Interface Node and a two-stage generation architecture. This
design was quantitatively validated in N = 30 simulation runs,
demonstrating high reliability with a 96.7% Task Success Rate
across all five interaction categories. We also confirmed the
architecture’s efficiency, with negligible command execution
latencies. This two-stage design is vital, as it ensures passenger
feedback reflects the definitive execution status, thereby
building user trust.
Despite these promising results, this work has several
limitations: the reliance on a cloud-based LLM API introduces
network-dependent latency and a single point of failure, as
seen in our reliability test. Furthermore, the LLM is not
fine-tuned for the driving domain, and its robustness to
ambiguity was not exhaustively tested. Finally, our quantitative
evaluation was limited to a defined scenario and requires
testing across more diverse traffic situations.
While acknowledging these limitations, our framework
provides a solid foundation for future extensions. Future
work will focus on enhancing user trust by providing
domain-adaptive
explanations.
Furthermore,
the
LLM’s
confirmed ability to understand driving contexts lays the
groundwork for advanced autonomous decision-making. By
integrating an LLM or VLM into the high-level decision loop,
the system could potentially move towards the autonomous
resolution of edge-case scenarios.
