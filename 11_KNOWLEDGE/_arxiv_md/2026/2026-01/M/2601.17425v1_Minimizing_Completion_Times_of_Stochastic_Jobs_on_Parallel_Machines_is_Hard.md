Abstract
This paper considers the scheduling of stochastic jobs on parallel identical ma-
chines to minimize the expected total weighted completion time. While this is a clas-
sical problem with a significant body of research on approximation algorithms over
the past two decades, constant-factor performance guarantees are currently known
only under very restrictive assumptions on the input distributions, even when all
job weights are identical. This algorithmic difficulty is striking given the lack of
corresponding complexity results: to date, it is conceivable that the problem could
be solved optimally in polynomial time.
We address this gap with hardness results that demonstrate the problem’s inher-
ent intractability. For the special case of discrete two-point processing time distri-
butions and unit weights, we prove that deciding whether there exists a scheduling
policy with expected cost at most a given threshold is #P-hard. Furthermore, we
show that evaluating the expected objective value of the standard (W)SEPT greedy
policy is itself #P-hard. These represent the first hardness results for scheduling
independent stochastic jobs and min-sum objective that do not merely rely on the
intractability of the underlying deterministic counterparts.

Introduction
This paper addresses a long-standing open question in stochastic scheduling theory:
whether the computational complexity of stochastic parallel machine scheduling funda-
mentally differs from that of its deterministic counterpart, even in the most basic set-
tings. In deterministic scheduling on parallel identical machines, minimizing the total
completion time P
j Cj when all jobs have equal weight is solvable in polynomial time by
scheduling jobs in shortest processing time (SPT) order [3]. Here Cj is the completion
time of job j under a scheduling policy. By contrast, for the stochastic version of the
same problem, where processing times are random and revealed only upon completion,
the computational complexity of computing an optimal scheduling policy has remained
unresolved since the early 1980s.
Despite decades of research on stochastic scheduling, computational hardness results
were previously known only for more structured problems; discussed in more detail be-
low. This paper provides the first evidence that the classical stochastic parallel machine
scheduling for minimizing E[P
j Cj] is computationally intractable, thereby separating
the stochastic problem from its deterministic counterpart.

We consider a standard stochastic scheduling model with m parallel identical machines
and n non-preemptive jobs. Each job j must be assigned to a single machine and, once
started, runs to completion without interruption.
The processing time pj of job j is
unknown in advance and is modeled as a nonnegative random variable Xj, independently
distributed across jobs. Following recent work on approximation algorithms for stochastic
scheduling, we focus on a discrete variant of the problem in which each Xj has finite
support, and in particular we assume that each distribution has support size at most two.
Each job j additionally may have a weight wj.
Under a scheduling policy, let Sj and Cj denote the (random) start and completion
times of job j, respectively. By definition, E[Cj] = E[Sj] + E[Xj].
The objective is to find, or evaluate, a scheduling policy that minimizes the expected
weighted sum of completion times,

Optimal scheduling policies for this problem are known only in highly restricted special
cases. When all weights are equal and processing times follow exponential distributions,
the greedy SEPT (Shortest Expected Processing Time first) policy is optimal [32, 5]. More
generally, SEPT is still an optimal policy if the processing time distributions are pairwise
stochastically comparable [31]. With exponentially distributed jobs, the optimality of
SEPT also extends to weighted jobs whenever the SEPT order is agreeable with the
job weights [18]. Outside of such special settings, however, it is well understood that
optimal policies must be adaptive, reacting dynamically to realized processing times [29,
26, 6]. Moreover, the adaptivity gap between non-adaptive and adaptive policies can grow
proportionally to the coefficient of variation of the processing time distributions [26, 6].
Beginning with [22], a substantial literature has developed approximation algorithms
for stochastic scheduling, typically based on linear programming relaxations of the optimal
adaptive policy [22, 27, 21, 26, 2, 11, 12, 15]. Almost all of these approximation guarantees
scale with the largest squared coefficient of variation of the random variables Xj, defined
as Var[ Xj ]/E[ Xj ]2. For the special case of unit weights, an exception is [14], whose
performance guarantee instead depends on the number of machines m. More recently, for
two-point Bernoulli processing times, sublinear-in-m approximation guarantees have been
obtained that do not depend on second moments [10, 1]: However, the approximation
guarantee in [10], while sublinear in m, contains large poly-logarithmic factors in the
number of jobs n, and the guarantee in [1] is O( log n ), but only quasi-polynomial time.
Despite the sophistication of these approximation results, strong lower bounds on
the computational complexity of stochastic scheduling have remained largely absent.
When job weights are arbitrary, the problem is NP-hard, since it generalizes determinis-
tic scheduling on parallel machines, which is NP-hard [8]. However, for the unweighted
case wj = 1 for all jobs j, deterministic scheduling is optimally solvable by SPT [3].
Quite strikingly, the computational complexity of the corresponding stochastic problem
—minimizing E[P
j Cj] on parallel identical machines— has remained open for over four
decades. In particular, it is still open if the stochastic problem can be solved optimally
in polynomial time.
In summary, the lurking fundamental question is:
Is it computationally hard to compute an optimal scheduling policy for mini-
mizing expected total completion time on parallel identical machines?
Despite sustained interest in stochastic scheduling since the 1980s, this question has not
been resolved. This paper gives a partial answer, testifying the problem being computa-
tionally harder than the corresponding deterministic counterpart.

1.1
Results
This paper provides the first indication that this stochastic scheduling problem is unlikely
to admit a polynomial-time solution, even when all job weights are equal. We show that
the decision problem of whether there exists a scheduling policy whose expected cost is
at most a given threshold is #P-hard. This hardness result holds even under restricted
stochasticity, namely with independent two-point processing time distributions.
Beyond computing optimal policies, we also study the complexity of evaluating simple
and widely used scheduling policies. Perhaps the most popular such policy is WSEPT
(Weighted Shortest Expected Processing Time first), which schedules jobs greedily in non-
increasing order of the ratios wj/E[Xj]. WSEPT is optimal on a single machine for both
deterministic [28] and stochastic processing times [24]. On parallel machines, it remains
optimal for exponential distributions when weights are agreeable with expected processing
times [18]. In the case with unit weights, WSEPT reduces to SEPT, which is optimal for
exponential distributions [32, 5] and more generally for stochastically orderable processing
times [31]. Even beyond these cases, WSEPT achieves constant-factor approximations
when the coefficient of variation is bounded by a constant [22, 16].
We show that computing the exact expected objective value of either WSEPT or
SEPT is itself a #P-hard problem. This result highlights a fundamental barrier not only
to computing optimal policies, but also to precisely evaluating the performance of simple,
fixed scheduling rules.
Existing Lower Bounds.
Hardness results for stochastic scheduling problems are
known only in more structured settings, and for other, arguably “simpler” objective
functions that are more amenable to lower bound constructions: First, stochastic schedul-
ing with alternate precedence constraints, and makespan objective for a given subset of
mandatory jobs is known to be PSPACE-hard [23, Thm. 19.7]. Here, alternate precedence
constraints means that we are given a directed acyclic graph, a job can only be started
if any one of its predecessors has been finished. Moreover, non-mandatory tasks only
need to be processed if needed as predecessors of mandatory jobs. Second, in precedence-
constrained stochastic project scheduling, a.k.a. PERT problems (Program Evaluation
and Review Technique), computing the expected makespan is a known #P-hard prob-
lem [13]. Both hardness results rely crucially on the presence of additional structure,
specifically, in the form of (alternate) precedence constraints. This allows reductions from
other, structured problems: the PSPACE-hardness follows from reductions from quan-
tified satisfiability, which also has a given order of associated variables and quantifiers,
and the #P-hardness of expected makespan for PERT problems exploits the hardness
of reliability problems in directed graphs [30]. Third, a computationally hard problem
with less structure is the problem to compute the probability for the sum of independent
Bernoulli trials being bounded by some threshold. This can be equivalently interpreted
as a single machine stochastic scheduling problem with makespan objective, and is #P-
hard, too [20]. This latter result has been used to show that also the computation of the
expected makespan of stochastic jobs on parallel machines is #P-hard, under the addi-
tional assumption that the jobs are pre-assigned to the machines [9]. Prior to the present
work, however, no comparable hardness results were known for less structured stochastic
scheduling problems, specifically not for the min-sum objective and without precedence
constraints.

Stochastic scheduling is presumably hard(er)
Here we aim to separate the deterministic scheduling problem to minimize the total com-
pletion time on parallel machines from the corresponding stochastic problem. Recall that
the deterministic problem can be solved optimally in polynomial time by computing an
SPT schedule [4] (shortest processing time first). The first step is to show that optimally
solving the stochastic problem at least entails a computationally hard (sub) problem in
the following sense.
Theorem 3. Consider an instance of stochastic parallel machine scheduling with stochas-
tic jobs to minimize E[ P
j Cj ].
Then the computation of the minimum expected cost

The proof will rely on the following lemma that specifies how an optimal scheduling
policy acts on the instances that were used to prove Theorem 2. Effectively, we will show
that the optimum policy must follow SEPT, if we choose the problem parameters more
carefully. Here observe that, since not all processing time distributions are stochastically
comparable, this observation does not follow from [31].
As a first step, we show that the optimum policy must start all blocker jobs at time
0 when we choose the probability q, the probability for the blocker jobs being long, small
enough. Intuitively, with small enough q it is better to gamble on all blocker jobs being
of size 0, because then all the knapsack jobs can be started at time 0, too. This yields a
total cost 0 for them. Once this is established, and no matter how many of the blocker
jobs turn out long, all the remaining jobs are in fact stochastically comparable, and hence
it follows from [31] that SEPT is an optimal scheduling policy for all remaining jobs.
Before giving the proof of Theorem 3, we turn the intuition about the blocker jobs always
going first into a formal argument.
Lemma 7. Consider Instances 1 or 2 as described in the proof of Theorem 2. Assume
that q, the probability for the blocker jobs being long, is chosen small enough. Then any
optimum policy must schedule all blocker jobs at time 0.
Proof. Consider a scheduling policy Π that does not always start all blocker jobs at time 0.
That means that, while there is still at least one blocker job that is yet unscheduled, it
either schedules one or several knapsack jobs at time 0, or the dummy job, or it leaves
at least one machine idle at time 0. In either of these cases, we argue that it would have
been strictly better to start one (more) blocker job at time 0 instead.
Given such behavior of policy Π, we can condition on the decision when the scheduling
policy Π decided to not schedule a blocker job on the last available machine at time 0,
while at least one blocker job, say job b, would have been available. Instead, policy Π
either leaves that machine idle or it schedules a knapsack job or the dummy job. In either
case, we claim that the earliest point in time when blocker job b is started by policy Π
is at time 1/n. This is true because, if Π schedules a knapsack or the dummy job, its
size is at least 1/n, and if it leaves a machine idle, a simple argument shows that it must
do that for at least 1/n time, which is the minimum size of any job. We next show, by
defining an alternative policy Π′, that a 1/n delay for a blocker job is too expensive.

Conclusions
Observe that the hardness results of Theorems 3 and 4 still allow that there is an algorithm
that computes an optimal scheduling policy Π in polynomial time. Here, one may imagine
Π as some polynomial space automaton that can decide, for any given state, which job
should be scheduled next. In fact, (W)SEPT, or any other list scheduling policy, can be
thought of as a simple version of such automaton, and indeed, we can easily compute
the (W)SEPT order, yet cannot in general compute its expected cost. We leave it as an
interesting open question to show the hardness of computing the optimal policy Π itself.
