Abstract. Gr¨obner basis computation over multivariate polynomial rings remains one of
the most powerful yet computationally hostile primitives in symbolic computation. While
modern algorithms (Faug`ere-type F4 and signature-based F5) reduce many instances to
large sparse linear algebra over finite fields, their dominant cost is not merely elimination
but the symbolic preprocessing that constructs Macaulay-style matrices whose rows encode
shifted reducers. This phase is characterized by dynamic combinatorics (monomial discovery,
sparse row assembly, and deduplication) and is typically memory-latency bound, resisting
naive parallelization.
This article develops a rigorous synthesis that reframes S-polynomial reduction as syzygy
discovery: row construction is a structured map from module relations to the kernel of a
massive, sparse, highly non-random Macaulay matrix A over Fp. Building on this viewpoint,
we propose a GPU-targeted architecture that (i) converts dynamic symbolic data structures
into static, two-pass allocations via prefix-sum planning; (ii) enforces coalesced memory
access through structure-of-arrays polynomial layouts and sorted monomial dictionaries;
and (iii) integrates finite-field arithmetic kernels (Montgomery/Barrett-style reduction) at
register granularity. On the linear-algebra side, we explore the transition from classical
Gaussian elimination to parallel structured Gaussian elimination (PSGE) and to Krylov-
type kernel solvers (Block Wiedemann/Lanczos) that better match GPU throughput while
controlling fill-in.
The result is a principled bridge between algebraic syzygy theory and SIMT hardware
constraints, isolating the true bottleneck and providing a pathway to massively parallel
reductions for multivariate polynomial systems.
1. Introduction: Complexity, Structure, and Hardware Mismatch
Let R = Fp[x1, . . . , xn] with a fixed term order ≺. Computing a Gr¨obner basis for an ideal
I = ⟨f1, . . . , fm⟩⊂R is a foundational operation in computational algebra and underlies
elimination, solving, and applications in coding and cryptanalysis (see, e.g., [CLO25]).
Despite decades of progress, the worst-case behavior remains severe: ideal membership
and related tasks can exhibit doubly-exponential complexity in the number of variables
n [MM82], and degree growth bounds already predict rapid blow-up in general [Dub90].
Importantly, this is not only a pessimistic asymptotic: the algorithmic burden is structural.

Modern performance gains come from turning many reductions into batched sparse
elimination steps. The F4 algorithm aggregates reducers into a Macaulay-style matrix and
performs row reduction to realize many polynomial reductions at once [Fau99a]. The F5
family further suppresses redundant work via signatures that track module provenance and
enforce admissible reductions [Fau02a]. However, even when elimination itself is efficient,
parallel speedups are often capped by the most irregular stage of the pipeline: symbolic
preprocessing—constructing the sparse matrix (rows, monomial dictionary, and column
ordering) to be reduced [Fau99a].
This stage is dominated by pointer-heavy, dynamically sized structures (priority queues,
hash tables, variable-length row buffers) and irregular memory traffic. Such behavior is a
poor fit for GPU execution, where performance hinges on uniform control flow and coalesced
memory access in a SIMT model [NVI25b]. The mismatch is well-known in sparse GPU
kernels: irregular row lengths and scattered accesses force divergence and reduce effective
bandwidth [BG08]. Thus the core obstacle is not merely “faster elimination on the GPU,”
but making the construction of the elimination instance GPU-regular.
Thesis. We interpret symbolic preprocessing as a structured syzygy-to-kernel map: reducing
a batch of S-polynomials is equivalent to producing kernel elements (module relations) of a
highly structured sparse matrix A over Fp [Buc98, Fau99a]. Consequently, the central GPU
problem is to construct A and its monomial dictionary using divergence-minimized kernels
and flat-array layouts—turning dynamic symbolic data into static, scan-planned memory
layouts (prefix-sums/scan being a canonical tool for this conversion) [Ble90a].
Contributions (conceptual).
• We formalize symbolic preprocessing as a deterministic map from selected module terms

how this realizes Buchberger-style reduction as kernel computation.
• We propose a GPU-suitable design principle: dynamic-to-static compilation of symbolic

driven by (i) selecting critical pairs and (ii) repeatedly reducing their S-polynomials until

2.2. Syzygy module viewpoint and signatures. Introduce the free module F = Rm
with basis e1, . . . , em and the surjection φ : F →I given by φ(ei) = fi. Then ker(φ) is the
first syzygy module of (f1, . . . , fm) [Eis95]. A key observation (formalized via Schreyer-type
module term orders) is that Buchberger-style pair handling corresponds to constructing
specific module relations whose images under φ cancel leading terms [Sch80, Eis95].
Signature-based methods make this module structure explicit. A signature is a module
monomial tei ∈M · {e1, . . . , em} attached to a derived polynomial, recording its origin in F
and inducing a well-founded order that constrains admissible reductions [Fau02b, EF17a].
Operationally, signatures implement a disciplined traversal of ker(φ): reductions are allowed
only when they do not increase (or do not violate) the signature order, which prunes many
reductions to zero and many redundant pairs [EF17a].
Remark 2.2 (From S-polynomials to module reduction). The instruction “reduce S(f, g)”
can be read as: construct a module element s ∈F whose image φ(s) has cancellative leading
term (as in Definition 2.1), then perform reductions until no leading monomial is divisible
by LM(G). This is intrinsically a module reduction problem [Sch80, Eis95].
2.3. F4 as batched module reduction; F5 as signature-constrained reduction. F4
replaces many scalar reductions by one elimination step: from a chosen batch of critical
pairs, it constructs a Macaulay-type matrix whose rows are suitable multiples t · g of current
basis elements g ∈G and whose columns are the monomials appearing in the batch; row
reduction over Fp performs all reductions simultaneously [Fau99b].
The cost driver is
precisely the symbolic preprocessing that determines the column set (mononomial dictionary)
and enumerates the row multiples before any linear algebra happens.
F5 adds signatures to control which reducers may be applied and to discard many useless
pairs, but once a batch is fixed, the computational primitive is the same: build a sparse
matrix encoding admissible (signature-safe) shifted reducers and compute its row-reduced
form over Fp [Fau02b, EF17a]. This common endpoint is what enables a unified hardware
view in later sections: both algorithms funnel work into constructing structured sparse
matrices and extracting kernel/row-space information from them [Fau99b, EF17a].

eliminate leading terms, and batching turns many such eliminations into elimination/kernel
computation on a single sparse operator A [Fau99b].
4. Parallelism in Multivariate Polynomial System Solving
The algebraic reduction problem in Sections 2–3 admits an explicit linear-algebra interface
(a sparse Macaulay matrix A with a monomial dictionary T ). The practical question is
where parallelism can be extracted without destroying the structural guarantees (term orders,
admissibility, signature constraints). A central lesson of matrix-driven methods is that
parallelism is not primarily available at the granularity of individual reducer steps, but at
the granularity of batches and bulk data movement that compile symbolic objects into static
arrays [Fau99b, NVI25a].

column indices, it must also write Ω(N +M) data. This already enforces a bandwidth/latency
lower bound: the runtime of SP is bounded below by the time needed to move Θ(M + N)
words through global memory.
Moreover, the deduplication problem contains sorting as a core subproblem: to produce
a deterministic dictionary ordered by ≺, the computation must (explicitly or implicitly)
permute keys into ≺-order and identify equal runs. High-throughput GPU sorting algorithms
achieve this by multiple streaming passes over the keys (radix or merge), which further
increases global traffic by repeated reads/writes of the key arrays [SHG09a]. Thus even
when arithmetic in Fp is cheap, the symbolic stage remains dominated by key traffic.
Why GPU latency dominates in practice. On throughput-oriented GPUs, irregular access
and divergence reduce the effective utilization of memory bandwidth and memory-level
parallelism. Analytical performance models that account for thread-level and memory-level
parallelism emphasize that insufficiently regular memory behavior directly manifests as
stalled warps and latency-bound execution [HK09]. This is corroborated in closely related
sparse workloads (e.g. SpMV): when access patterns are irregular, performance is governed far
more by memory behavior than by arithmetic throughput [BG08]. Symbolic preprocessing
exhibits the same pathologies—random-like probes under hashing, branch-heavy admissibility
checks, and global synchronization for deduplication—unless it is reorganized into bulk
streaming primitives.
Remark 5.3 (Non-random sparsity and the “right” reformulation). Although A is sparse,
it is not arbitrary: its nonzeros arise by multiplying known sparse supports by monomials
and projecting into a shared dictionary. This means the irregularity is algorithmic (dynamic
growth and lookup), not inherent. The correct reformulation is therefore to express SP using
bulk primitives (scan/sort/unique/merge) and two-pass planning so that the only global
synchronization is that already implicit in these primitives. This is the organizing principle
behind the GPU designs developed in the next sections.

6. The Symbolic–Numeric Divide: Why Classical GPU GEMM Fails
The discussion so far separates two objects that are often conflated in the Gr¨obner literature:
the numeric act of performing elimination over Fp on a fixed matrix A (Section 3), and
the symbolic act of constructing the instance (T , R, Π) = SP(B) (Section 5). This section
makes the separation sharp: GPU kernels for dense linear algebra are excellent once an
array-of-numbers problem is already present, but symbolic preprocessing is precisely the
phase where the “array-of-numbers” abstraction has not yet been earned.
6.1. The GEMM performance contract. At a mathematical level, gemm is the bilinear
map (B, C) 7→BC; at a systems level, high-performance gemm implements a performance
contract: (i) memory is accessed in regular, contiguous patterns, (ii) data is reused predictably
via tiling into registers/shared memory, and (iii) control flow is uniform at warp granularity.
The reason is structural: dense matrix multiplication has extremely high arithmetic intensity,
and by blocking, one arranges that each fetched element participates in many fused multiply-
adds before eviction. This is exactly the viewpoint behind the classical GPU microkernel
literature, where peak performance is approached by disciplined register blocking and staged
movement through the memory hierarchy [VD08, NVI25a].
When these hypotheses hold, the GPU is close to an “ideal” throughput machine:
latency is hidden by massive concurrency, and the dominant cost becomes the rate at which
the hardware can retire arithmetic instructions [NVI25a]. This is the regime where dense
elimination can be organized so that most time is spent in panel updates and trailing-matrix
updates (i.e., GEMM-shaped work), recovering a near-peak fraction of compute [VD08].
6.2. Why Gr¨obner symbolic preprocessing violates the contract. Symbolic pre-
processing is not a slightly messy instance of dense linear algebra; it is a different kind of
computation. In the operator language of Section 5, the symbolic phase must produce a
layout certificate Π:
(T , R, Π) = SP(B),
Π = (row ptr, col ind, val, dict keys, row meta),
and only then does the numeric phase see a matrix A at all.
The failure of gemm begins before arithmetic: in SP(B), the principal operation is the
indexing homomorphism
u ∈supp(tigki) 7−→indexT (u) ∈{1, . . . , N},
which is a global dictionary join/merge across heterogeneous supports. This is (a) memory-
latency dominated (irregular reads/writes; weak locality), (b) synchronization pressured
(deduplication/closure), and (c) control-flow divergent (admissibility/criteria, varying row
lengths), exactly the opposite of the GEMM contract (Section 4) [NVI25a]. In other words:
the dominant cost is manufacturing the index space, not multiplying within an already-fixed
index space.
A second, deeper obstruction is that Gr¨obner batches are adaptive. The set T is not
merely large; it is discovered under closure rules (Section 3, Remark on one-step reduction
closure). Thus the sparsity pattern is not a static property that can be profiled once and
exploited many times; it is an evolving consequence of term order, reducer availability, and
(in F5) signature admissibility. The symbolic phase therefore cannot be amortized away
unless one changes the algorithmic interface.

rows, and reduce a block-dense surrogate. This strategy pays twice.
First, padding is not free. Gr¨obner matrices are typically highly unstructured at the tile
scale: supports are induced by monomial multiplication and then projected through T , so
within any putative block one often carries many structural zeros. The arithmetic saved by
tensor-like dense kernels is then repaid as wasted bandwidth and wasted flops on padding.
Second, and more importantly for our setting, densification does not remove the symbolic
phase; it merely relocates it. To build the blocks one still must (i) choose the dictionary T ,
(ii) assign each monomial to a block/offset, and (iii) materialize the padded layout. This is
exactly SP(B) with an additional quantization map onto block coordinates.
The modern sparse-tensor-core literature makes this point explicit in a neighboring
domain. Even when the numeric kernel is aggressively accelerated (e.g. TC-based SpMM),
substantial work is spent on format conversion, reordering, and index precomputation;
moreover, such preprocessing is most effective when the same sparse matrix is reused many
times [FWC24]. Gr¨obner basis computation is the opposite workload: the matrix instance
varies from batch to batch, so symbolic compilation overhead cannot be assumed amortizable
a priori. Hence “just use tensor cores” is not a plan unless one first redesigns the symbolic
interface.
6.4. The correct abstraction: compile symbolic structure, then compute numeri-
cally. The right systems abstraction is therefore the one already implicit in Sections 3–5:
Symbolic preprocessing is a compilation step from algebraic structure to a
sparse linear-algebra instance.
Once Π is produced, the numeric phase may indeed use dense kernels locally: within
elimination panels, within block-sparse substructures, or within carefully chosen compressed
formats. But the fundamental ordering of responsibilities cannot be reversed. The GPU
succeeds only after symbolic structure has been turned into flat arrays with deterministic
offsets (two-pass count →prefix-sum →fill), so that the numeric phase sees a stable memory
layout and can exploit the standard memory hierarchy [NVI25a].
This motivates the architecture in the next section: rather than forcing Gr¨obner
computation into a dense-linear-algebra mold, we treat SP as the first-class object and
design it around bulk primitives (sort/unique/merge/scan). Only then do we apply the best
available numeric kernels to the resulting A.

9. From Elimination to Kernel Solving: PSGE vs. Block Wiedemann/Lanczos
Section 3 identifies batched Gr¨obner reduction with structured sparse linear algebra over
Fp, while Sections 5–8 explain how to make the GPU pipeline viable: (i) compile symbolic
structure into a static layout plan Π and (ii) run register-resident Fp arithmetic in the
numeric kernels. What remains is the algorithmic choice inside the numeric phase: should
we compute a row-reduced form (elimination), or should we compute kernel vectors (relations)
via black-box Krylov methods?
9.1. Why full elimination is not always optimal on a GPU. Let A ∈Fr×N

nonzeros, and the matrix drifts toward a denser and less structured representation. On
throughput hardware this is doubly harmful:
• Loss of sparsity inflates memory traffic and defeats the locality assumptions needed
for stable throughput;
• Irregularity increases as row lengths broaden and vary, causing divergence and
further reducing effective bandwidth.

(1) Elimination (echelon-form production). Necessary when the batch must emit
reduced rows that become new basis elements (the F4/F5 operational meaning).
(2) Kernel solving (relation discovery). Preferable when the batch goal is to discover
dependencies/syzygies (Section 3), i.e. vectors v ̸= 0 with v⊤A = 0, without constructing
a full echelon form.
The GPU-relevant point is that kernel methods reduce the work to repeated applications of

format.

corresponds to forming Fp-linear combinations of the shifted reducers in R, i.e., to forming
polynomials in the span of {tigki} whose supports lie in T . In particular, the reduced rows
produced by elimination correspond to reduced polynomials for the batch.
Proof. An elementary row operation replaces a row by an Fp-linear combination of rows. By
Theorem 11.6, each row is the coefficient vector of a shifted reducer in the basis T . Therefore
any row combination is the coefficient vector of the corresponding polynomial combination.
Truthfulness ensures that all monomials needed by the reduction semantics are represented
in T , so the numeric reductions agree with polynomial reductions for the batch.

11.5. Signature regime: correctness under admissibility predicates. Finally we
state a correctness guarantee for F5-type admissibility: if Adm encodes a sound signature
discipline, then FBSP never materializes forbidden reductions.
Theorem 11.9 (Signature-sound compilation). Assume the admissibility predicate Adm is
sound in the sense of the signature-based Gr¨obner framework: it filters out exactly those
shifted reducers that would correspond to forbidden (signature-increasing or rewritable)
reductions. Then Algorithm 10.1, applied with this Adm, produces a matrix A whose row
space contains only admissible reduction steps; consequently, elimination/kernel extraction
on A cannot create a polynomial that violates the signature admissibility rules.
Proof sketch. All numeric combinations are formed from the span of the rows of A. By
construction, rows correspond bijectively to admissible shifted reducers (row selection +
filtering). Therefore any combination corresponds to a combination of admissible reducers.
Since forbidden reductions are excluded at the row-selection stage, they cannot be generated
later by linear combination. Formal details depend on the particular signature order and
rewrite criterion adopted, but the mechanism is uniform: admissibility is enforced structurally
by restricting the generating set.
11.6. Complexity lower bound for any truthful symbolic preprocessing.
Theorem 11.10 (Key-traffic lower bound). Let R be fixed and let M = P
i | supp(tigki)|.
Any symbolic preprocessing algorithm that outputs a truthful dictionary T must, in the worst

Proof. Each monomial occurrence in the multiset union of supports can be distinct in the

be processed.

13.3. Symbolic preprocessing cost under FBSP (two-pass compilation). FBSP
(Algorithm 10.1) realizes SP(B) as a bulk pipeline: count →scan →fill →sort/unique →
join. We quantify its costs in terms of (M, N).
Pass structure and work.
• Count + scan. Computing all ℓi is O(r) work once supports are known, followed by
one global prefix-sum on r integers to produce row ptr. This is the standard “write-once
sparse allocation” primitive [Ble90a].
• Fill (materialize shifted rows). Writing the term stream (row key, row val) is
O(M) work and O(M) contiguous writes.
• Sort/unique (dictionary canonicalization). Sorting M fixed-width keys with a
radix strategy uses a constant number of passes (determined only by key width and
radix), and is therefore O(M) work per pass; uniqueness is a linear sweep over the
sorted stream [SHG09b].
• Join (row indexing against the dictionary). Mapping row keys to dictionary
indices is a sorted join between each row segment and dict keys.
A merge-path
style partitioning yields linear-time joins with good load balance across warps/blocks
[GMB12].
Bandwidth-dominated cost model. Once the pipeline is organized around flat arrays,
the dominant term is not arithmetic but global memory traffic: FBSP must read/write
Θ(M) keys and values and must store Θ(N) dictionary keys. Thus, for a fixed architecture,
the wall-time is constrained below by a bandwidth term proportional to the total bytes
moved. This is exactly the motivation for Sections 4–10: reduce pointer chasing and replace
random insertions by streaming primitives.

Elimination proxy (PSGE / sparse direct). Let nnz(A) be the initial nonzeros and let
nnz(L)+nnz(U) denote the nonzeros generated in the factors (or the analogous fill produced
by structured elimination schedules). Then both time and space scale with the fill pattern:
space ∝nnz(L) + nnz(U),
time ∝(updates touching those nonzeros).
The dependence on ordering/scheduling is fundamental in sparse direct methods [Dav06].
PSGE aims to control this by forcing panel locality compatible with Π (Section 9), but it
cannot remove fill as an algebraic phenomenon.
Krylov proxy (Block Wiedemann/Lanczos). Krylov solvers replace fill-sensitive fac-
torization by repeated applications of A (and often A⊤) to vectors or blocks of vectors
[Wie86, Cop94]. If the block width is b, the dominant kernel becomes SpMM (apply A to b
vectors at once), whose per-iteration cost scales with nnz(A) · b under a fixed sparse format.
This is precisely the regime where layout decisions from Section 7 (e.g. SELL-C-σ-style
slicing) matter, because they control the achievable bandwidth of SpMV/SpMM kernels
[KHW+14].
Remark 13.1 (Where (n, d) enters the hardware story). The parameters (n, d) act primarily
through N(n, d) and through the induced batch shape (M, N). The algebra chooses which
reducers and which closure rule determine T ; the compiler SP turns that choice into an
executable sparse operator A. Hence, even though degree growth is an algebraic worst-case
phenomenon [Dub90, MR13], the practical performance is decided earlier than elimination:
it is decided at the moment T and Π are fixed (Sections 5 and 10), because that fixes both
the memory traffic of compilation and the sparse layout seen by the numeric kernels.
14. Discussion: Integrating Signatures, Admissibility, and GPU Scheduling
In signature-based pipelines (initiated by F5), admissibility Adm depends on module monomi-
als: a signature is typically a module term xαei equipped with an order (position-over-term,
Schreyer-type, etc.). The operational constraint is: reductions are permitted only if they
decrease the signature in the chosen order, and rewrite criteria prevent generating elements
whose signature is already “covered” [Fau02b, EF17b].
GPU-facing reformulation. The architectural goal is to ensure that signature logic
appears only in bulk filtering and scheduling, not in inner loops. Concretely:
• encode each candidate row with a packed signature key (module monomial + index),
• evaluate Adm as a data-parallel predicate over row metadata,
• compact the survivors by scan/stream compaction (a standard GPU primitive) [Ble90b,
BG24],
• bucket/schedule rows by (degree, length, signature stratum) so that subsequent merge/assembly
kernels exhibit low divergence.

Signature-aware closure. When Adm excludes certain reducers, the closure rules used
to build T must be interpreted relative to the admissible reducer set. Otherwise one risks
materializing columns that correspond to reductions that cannot occur, wasting memory
bandwidth, or worse, omitting columns needed by admissible reductions. This suggests an
iterative (but still bulk) fixed-point: propose rows →filter by signatures →close dictionary
for the filtered set →recheck coverage.
15. Conclusion and Open Problems
We isolated the dominant barrier to massively parallel Gr¨obner computation: symbolic
preprocessing is the compilation stage that converts syzygy-structured algebra into a truthful
sparse linear-algebra instance. Once this compilation outputs static, deterministic buffers,
the numeric phase can leverage high-throughput GPU sparse kernels over Fp.
Open problems (research-level).
• Provable sparsity control under admissibility: design closure strategies for T
that minimize downstream fill-in while remaining correct under signature constraints.
• Certified pivot scheduling: develop term-order- and signature-aware block pivot
schedules with algebraic invariants that guarantee admissibility by construction.
• Hybrid elimination–Krylov thresholds: derive regime conditions (in terms of
fill(A) versus SpMV iteration complexity) under which partial elimination plus kernel
extraction dominates full elimination.
• Module-theoretic GPU invariants: encode signature/module constraints directly
into memory layouts and schedules so that kernels enforce admissibility structurally,
not via branch-heavy checks.
