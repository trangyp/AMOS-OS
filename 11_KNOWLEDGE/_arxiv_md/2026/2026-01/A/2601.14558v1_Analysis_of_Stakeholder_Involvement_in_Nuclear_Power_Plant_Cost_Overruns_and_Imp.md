Abstract 
This study introduces a novel framework to model cost overruns associated with four key 
stakeholders in nuclear power plant construction: equipment suppliers, construction 
subcontractors, the design and management team, and creditors. The framework estimates 
the share of overruns caused by each stakeholder and the share of overruns they receive as 
payment. The results show that the share of cost overruns a given stakeholder causes and 
the share of overruns they receive as payment are often starkly diƯerent, which can lead to 
proﬁt misallocations and litigation between parties, further exacerbating overruns. The 
magnitude of these potential proﬁt misallocations is examined under three common 
contract structures – ﬁxed-price, cost-plus, and performance-based – revealing the 
advantages and disadvantages of each framework for aligning stakeholder incentives. 
Regardless of the contract type chosen, strong owner involvement is crucial for project 
success, and the study concludes with speciﬁc recommendations for project owners 
seeking to minimize cost overruns. 
Introduction 
Recent nuclear construction projects, particularly in the Western world (United States, 
France, Finland, and United Kingdom), have seen major cost and schedule overruns – often 
more than twice the original estimates [1] – that have dampened investor interest and 
prevented nuclear power from gaining a larger share of the energy market. Since 
construction costs comprise most of the overall levelized costs of nuclear energy, this work 
aims to provide a better understanding of the origins of nuclear construction cost overruns

so they can be more eƯectively mitigated in the future. This article speciﬁcally examines how 
four stakeholders involved in nuclear construction (construction subcontractors, 
equipment suppliers, design and management teams, and creditors) play a role in cost 
overruns. 
The work examines overruns through two diƯerent lenses: (1) who causes overruns, and (2) 
who charges the cost of overruns to the project owner. While these concepts may seem 
similar, they are distinct, as illustrated by Figure 1. In the “project execution diagram,” which 
shows the ﬂow of goods and services necessary for project completion, stakeholders are 
connected to each other in diƯerent ways than in the “cash ﬂow diagram,” which shows the 
ﬂow of money from the owner to other stakeholders. This can result in some stakeholders 
charging the owner for overruns caused by others. For example, suppose an equipment 
supplier discovers an error in the tolerances on a component drawing provided by the reactor 
vendor during fabrication, necessitating the return of the drawing to the vendor for revisions. 
Figure 1 shows how this mistake ripples through the project ecosystem. In the project 
execution diagram, the disruption ﬂows from the design and management teams to the 
equipment suppliers, which aƯects the productivity of the construction subcontractors, and 
by extension, slows the completion of the power plant. The cash ﬂow diagram shows how 
compensation for these impacts ﬂows in the opposite direction, from the owner to the 
stakeholders involved in power plant completion. The owner must pay the construction 
subcontractors for additional hours spent waiting for component delivery, the equipment 
suppliers for factory time spent unproductively, and the design and management team for 
engineering hours spent on revising the drawings. The owner will also pay increased 
ﬁnancing charges to the creditors (banks, equity ﬁnancers, etc.) while the plant completion 
is delayed. Thus, even though the reactor vendor was solely responsible for causing this cost 
overrun, many other stakeholders are aƯected and must receive payment for overruns, 
including the equipment suppliers, construction subcontractors, and even the creditors, 
who are not directly involved in plant design or construction activities. These complex 
dynamics between stakeholders may explain why highly vertically integrated nuclear 
construction projects with fewer stakeholders and better-aligned incentive structures, such 
as those in Russia, China, South Korea, and Japan, have seen better cost and schedule 
outcomes than recent Western projects involving a multitude of stakeholders all with their 
own ﬁnancial incentives [2]. This study seeks to gain a better theoretical understanding of 
the dynamics that exist in multi-stakeholder nuclear construction projects by modeling the 
overruns caused and received by each stakeholder, and the results will provide useful 
takeaways for successful contract structuring in large construction projects requiring 
coordination between many independent actors.

Literature Review 
Many previous studies have attempted to quantify the sources of nuclear power plant cost 
escalation and cost overruns, although most have not approached this question from the 
perspective of stakeholder attributions or contracting theory with quantitative modeling. A 
study by Eash-Gates et al. [3] cited historic reports by Energy Economic Data Base (EEDB) 
[4] that showed a large portion (72%) of nuclear construction cost escalation in the United 
States over the period 1976-87 was attributable to a substantial rise in indirect costs, 
including “home oƯice engineering services (engineering design, purchasing and expediting, 
cost control, and planning and scheduling), ﬁeld job supervision (salaries and relocation 
expenses), temporary construction facilities (materials and labor to construct and manage 
buildings needed during construction), and payroll insurance and taxes.” Most of the capital 
cost increases over this period were caused by “those activities and practices related to 
meeting accountability type requirements,” including “regulatory reviews, design review, 
project control, analysis veriﬁcation, procedure development and implementation, 
equipment qualiﬁcation, inspection, testing and similar or related activities.” 
Eash-Gates et al. also found that labor productivity in U.S. nuclear construction projects has 
continuously declined, both during the original construction boom of the 1970s-80s and in 
recent projects. It is notable that the observed productivity declines are worse than those 
observed in the U.S. construction sector as a whole [3] [2], indicating that the nuclear 
industry has been subject to uniquely severe productivity disruptions over its history, such 
as the string of design changes mandated by the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) in 
the middle of plant construction during the 1970s-80s [5] and the low levels of design 
completion that have characterized recent Western projects [2]. Thanks to the massive rise 
in indirect costs, rework, and low labor productivity, a larger and larger share of Western 
nuclear power construction costs is attributable to labor rather than materials or equipment 
costs: the ﬁnal EEDB update found that the cost of a large pressurized water reactor with 
median U.S. construction experience (“PWR12-ME”) in 1987 was roughly two-thirds labor 
and one-third materials costs, and the labor share has presumably become even larger in 
the 21st century as U.S. nuclear construction productivity has continued to decrease [3]. 
Studies examining which speciﬁc stakeholders are responsible for which shares of cost 
overruns are more limited. Previous econometric analyses have examined the inﬂuence of a 
few high-level stakeholders on plant costs, particularly the reactor vendor that provides the 
nuclear steam supply system, the architect-engineer (A-E) that designs the balance of plant, 
the constructor (sometimes called the “prime contractor”) that manages construction 
activities, and the owner (e.g., a utility company) that orders the plant. The Energy 
Information Administration (EIA) found a 35% reduction in speciﬁc overnight capital costs

(OCC) for projects in which the owner acted as its own constructor rather than hiring an 
external construction management ﬁrm [6]. Jansma and Borcherding likewise found lower 
costs for projects where the owner acted as its own constructor [7]. KomanoƯ found a 
decline of 7% in both speciﬁc overnight capital costs (OCC) and construction duration for 
each doubling in the number of plants constructed by a given A-E ﬁrm [5]. The EIA found that 
constructors similarly exhibit a positive learning eƯect (i.e., cost reductions due to 
experience building multiple plants), but the eƯect was only statistically signiﬁcant for 
plants in which the owner acted as its own constructor, suggesting that learning-related cost 
savings may not be passed on to the owner if they hire an external constructor [6]. These 
econometric studies shed useful light on the learning curves of some stakeholders and the 
impact of diƯerent project management regimes on costs. However, they do not include 
several additional stakeholders that we consider in our analysis (most notably equipment 
suppliers, construction subcontractors, and creditors – see Figure 1), nor do they attempt to 
quantify the speciﬁc cost overrun shares attributable to each individual stakeholder. This 
precludes a deeper understanding of the underlying dynamics between stakeholders that 
can cause cost overruns and does not explicitly reveal what speciﬁc actions project 
managers should take to achieve better cost outcomes (e.g., recommendations for 
successful contract structuring), highlighting the value of pursuing additional approaches to 
assess how diƯerent stakeholders are involved in nuclear cost overruns. 
Many authors in the project management discipline have studied cost data and qualitative 
evidence from speciﬁc projects to determine how diƯerent stakeholders are involved in 
overruns. Locatelli et al. in particular have conducted thorough analyses and literature 
reviews of stakeholder involvement in nuclear power plant construction cost overruns [8] 
[9]. They ﬁnd that nuclear power plants represent a highly complex construction 
megaproject requiring the coordination of a uniquely large number of stakeholders, 
including government bodies, contractors, and local communities. These stakeholders can 
cause cost overruns through a variety of avenues, including poor organizational structuring 
or incentive frameworks; poor contract management and improper risk allocation between 
stakeholders; and failure to engage proactively and productively with the community, 
political decision-makers, and regulators. The authors recommend a number of approaches 
to avoid these megaproject pitfalls, including early and continuous engagement with 
external stakeholders, better “front-end loading” (i.e., more thorough planning before 
construction start), more carefully structured contracts with a fair allocation of risk, and the 
use of “special purpose entities” (SPEs) to deliver specialized construction megaprojects 
like nuclear power plants, among others.

Novelty 
Our study takes a new approach to stakeholder analysis of nuclear construction cost 
overruns that does not rely on econometric regression or qualitative analyses of past 
projects. Instead, a series of stakeholder attribution calculations are proposed in Methods 
based on a series of mechanistic, historically-informed cost correlations that are used to 
model expected cost overruns in nuclear construction based on the proﬁciency and 
experience levels of various participants involved in the project. The source of these 
correlations is the open-source Nuclear Reactor Capital Cost Reduction Tool (henceforth 
referred to as the “Cost Reduction Tool”) released in a U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) 
report in 2024 [10]. The tool models the inﬂuence of several factors on nuclear power plant 
construction costs, including stakeholder proﬁciency levels, reactor design maturity, the 
level of cross-site standardization across multiple projects, and the level of detailed design 
completion prior to construction start, among others. For our analysis, we examine three 
speciﬁc types of cost overruns modeled by the Cost Reduction Tool: rework, low 
construction site productivity, and ﬁnancing overruns directly related to these two sources 
of OCC overruns. It should be noted that for the purpose of this analysis, “overruns” are 
deﬁned as the costs exceeding an estimated “baseline cost” representing a well-executed 
project with no rework and maximal construction site productivity (see Methods and 
Supplemental Information), and therefore do not include overruns resulting from under-
bidding.  
The stakeholder analysis framework is applied to overruns modeled by the Cost Reduction 
Tool to determine the shares of overruns caused by each stakeholder and the shares 
received by each stakeholder as payment, and the results are used to draw conclusions 
about how contracts should be structured to ensure equitable outcomes for all stakeholders 
and minimize the overall project costs. We speciﬁcally examine the implications of the 
modeling results under three contract frameworks used commonly in construction 
megaprojects – performance-based contracting (in which proﬁts are tied to some 
performance milestones), ﬁxed-price contracting (in which the stakeholder is awarded a 
ﬁxed sum and must internalize any unexpected cost overruns), and cost-plus contracting (in 
which proﬁts are awarded as a ﬁxed percentage on top of realized costs) – and conclude with 
takeaways about the advantages and disadvantages of each contract type for multi-
stakeholder projects, as well as providing recommendations for minimizing cost overruns 
and incentive misalignment between stakeholders under each contract type.

Results 
The analysis models the deployment of several two-unit nuclear power plants based on the 
1117 MWe Large Passively Safe Reactor (LPSR) design, a surrogate model for the 1117 MWe 
Westinghouse AP1000 design [1]. The results are based on the same input assumptions 
described in Potential Cost Reduction in New Nuclear Deployments Based on Recent 
AP1000 Experience [11] for the “U.S. experience” case, which reﬂect expected U.S. 
conditions for nuclear power plant deployment including design completion, design 
maturity, supply chain proﬁciency, and cross-site standardization, among other factors. We 
use the same underlying bottom-up LPSR cost models used as inputs to the Cost Reduction 
Tool in the Potential Cost Reduction report, which include “Nth-of-a-kind” (NOAK) and “well-
executed ﬁrst-of-a-kind” (WE-FOAK) models of the LPSR sourced from the Nuclear Cost 
Estimation Tool in the TIMCAT repository developed by Stewart and Shirvan et al. [1] [12] [13]. 
Costs are presented in terms of total capital investment (TCI), which includes both OCC and 
ﬁnancing costs. To capture the ﬁnancing cost, a 4% ﬁnancing rate is applied using the 
sinusoidal spending curve method built into Version 2.0 of the Cost Reduction Tool. The 4% 
ﬁnancing rate is intended to be roughly representative of the Vogtle 3 & 4 experience (see 
Supplemental Methods). Real-world projects, including Vogtle, are typically ﬁnanced using 
a combination of debt and equity involving multiple ﬁnancial stakeholders, with bonds and 
project equity stakes being issued with diƯerent rates of return on diƯerent dates during the 
construction and startup period. For simplicity, we did not attempt to model the diƯerences 
in repayment structures, capital structures, or schedules between the multiple diƯerent 
actors who may ﬁnance the project (banks, equity investors, the government loan 
guarantees, etc.). We instead chose to model the project as 100% debt-ﬁnanced with a 
single interest rate of 4% for this illustrative case. 
The estimated baseline costs and cost overruns across a series of ten 2-unit LPSR plant 
deployments are shown in Figure 2 for the default U.S. experience case. Overruns are 
eliminated by plant 5 since all stakeholders are assumed to have reached full proﬁciency 
(see Figure S7(A)). Baseline costs continue to decrease beyond plant 4 even though cost

to learnings and eƯiciency improvements in addition to overrun elimination, such as bulk

techniques. It is important to emphasize that the Cost Reduction Tool is deterministic in 
nature, and the results shown in this section represent only one possible outcome based on 
the assumptions of the “U.S. experience” case. A diƯerent set of outcomes based on a 
diƯerent set of input assumptions is presented in the section Scenario: Fixed Construction 
Proﬁciency.

Due to limitations in the available data, our calculations do not attempt to capture the share 
of cost overruns caused by the Regulator (see Figure 1). The results in Figure 3 therefore 
implicitly assume a stable regulatory environment, with all responsibility for design changes 
during construction attributed to the Design & Management stakeholder, and all 
responsibility for low worksite productivity attributed to a combination of Design & 
Management, Construction Subcontractors, and Equipment Suppliers (see Methods). In 
settings with a high degree of regulatory instability, the regulator can cause a substantial 
share of cost and schedule overruns. As Figure 1 illustrates, the regulator has the ability to 
inspect facilities, halt work, deny permits, and mandate design changes, all of which can 
incur large and unexpected cost overruns if the regulator takes a hostile or unpredictable 
approach to oversight. Previous authors have shown that rework and redesign activities 
mandated by nuclear regulatory authorities has historically caused substantial cost 
escalation and delay [5] [14], while countries with less regulatory ﬂux have shown a greater 
ability to control and even reduce costs across multiple nuclear builds [1]. In the United 
States during the 1970s and 1980s, regulators frequently mandated design changes during 
construction [5], with standard design certiﬁcation processes eventually emerging as an 
attempt to limit the number of unexpected regulator-mandated changes during 
construction. 
Scenario: Fixed Construction Proﬁciency 
The results in Figure 3 are based on the default input assumptions for the “U.S. experience” 
case described in Potential Cost Reduction in New Nuclear Deployments Based on Recent 
AP1000 Experience [11]. We now consider a case in which the construction proﬁciency lever 
stays ﬁxed at its minimum value across all plant deployments, as shown in Figure S7(B). This 
would occur if, for instance, all plants are built in very distant locations (thereby inhibiting 
transfer of the same construction workforce from site to site), or if the owners of these 
diƯerent plants decide to hire diƯerent contracting ﬁrms for construction instead of keeping 
the same ﬁrm across all projects. In this scenario, no matter how many plants are built, some 
level of cost overruns will always persist due to the continual resetting of the construction 
proﬁciency lever to its minimum value, unlike the default “U.S. experience” scenario in which 
the construction proﬁciency lever reaches its maximum value by plant 5 (see Figure S7(A)) 
due to construction workforce learning.

plant. This is to be expected since the construction subcontractors remain at low proﬁciency

Implications for Contract Structuring 
Figure 3, Figure 4 and Figure 5 show that the share of cost overruns caused by each stakeholder is 
often very diƯerent from the share of cost overruns the stakeholder receives as payment. This has 
important implications for contract structuring, because contracts may establish the proﬁts a 
stakeholder can receive based on how much they overrun their original cost scope estimate. If the 
project owner assesses the overrun amount attributable to the stakeholder based on the excess 
costs the stakeholder charges to the owner (hereafter referred to as a “recipient-based 
allocation”), the stakeholder’s proﬁts will be signiﬁcantly diƯerent compared to a scenario in which 
their proﬁt margin is tied to the amount of overruns they are actually responsible for causing 
(hereafter referred to as a “cause-based allocation”). 
For example, consider a simple hypothetical performance-based contract framework in which 
each stakeholder involved in construction is allowed a 16% proﬁt margin if there is no overrun in 
their cost scope, an 8% proﬁt margin if there is a 30% overrun in their cost scope, and no proﬁts 
allowed after 60% overruns, as illustrated in Figure 6(A).

As Figure 6(B) shows, the proﬁts received by the Equipment Suppliers depend on how the 
contract assesses the cost scope overrun amount the Equipment Suppliers are responsible 
for. It may seem natural to the owner of a nuclear construction project to assess the 
Equipment Suppliers’ responsibility for cost overruns simply by looking at the amount of 
overruns they charge to the owner (i.e., a “recipient-based” allocation of proﬁts). If this 
approach is used, the Equipment Suppliers will receive approximately $486 million in proﬁts 
based on the ~$954 million in overruns charged to the owner. In this example, however, the 
Equipment Suppliers only caused ~$543 million in overruns. If proﬁts are tied to this amount 
instead (i.e., a “cause-based” allocation of proﬁts), the Equipment Suppliers will receive 
approximately $576 million in proﬁts. 
Thus, if the allowable proﬁt margin is assessed using a recipient-based allocation, the 
Equipment Suppliers will receive $90 million less (a 15.6% decrease) in proﬁts compared to 
a cause-based allocation. This discrepancy is not likely to go unnoticed, and the Equipment 
Suppliers may decide to sue whichever stakeholders are actually responsible for causing the

$2.88B and receives $3.35B), which means both stakeholders end up in the “zero-proﬁt” 
region of the proﬁt vs. overrun curve regardless of whether their proﬁts are tied to overruns 
received or overruns caused. Thus, unlike the ﬁxed-price case, the performance-based case 
eliminates any motivation for the Equipment Suppliers stakeholder to sue the Construction 
Subcontractors to recover unfairly distributed proﬁts, although the Equipment Suppliers still 
16


## Page 17


have an incentive to sue if proﬁts are tied to overruns received because they lose $90M in 
this situation. 
The cost-plus case is unique because both Design & Management and the Equipment 
Suppliers are better oƯ if their proﬁts are based on a recipient-based allocation rather than 
a cause-based allocation. The Construction Subcontractors lose $144M if this approach is 
used, but under a cost-plus arrangement, there is no inherent reason why proﬁts should be 
tied to overruns caused rather than to overruns received. Therefore, in the cost-plus case, all 
three stakeholders are less likely to sue each other than in the ﬁxed-price or performance-
based cases, as all parties will always receive an 8% proﬁt margin on the costs they charge 
to the owner. The downside, of course, is that this contract structure creates an incentive for 
all three of these stakeholders to maximize the overruns they charge the project owner for, 
which could potentially result in litigation between the owner and these stakeholders. In 
other words, the cost-plus contract may better align incentives between the stakeholders 
responsible for building and delivering the plant, but it will also cause their incentives to 
become misaligned with the project owner. Clearly, all three contracting styles have 
advantages and disadvantages that must be carefully weighed at the project outset. 
Discussion  
The results of the analysis show that proﬁt outcomes for individual stakeholders can vary 
signiﬁcantly depending on whether proﬁts are awarded using a recipient-based allocation or 
a cause-based allocation, which can potentially result in costly and time-consuming 
litigation between parties, especially in the performance-based and ﬁxed-price contract 
structures. When a recipient-based allocation is used in the performance-based or ﬁxed-
price arrangement, lawsuits will be launched by stakeholders who are being punished for 
overruns in their cost scope that were in fact caused by other stakeholders. 
For example, consider the Vogtle 3 & 4 project, in which a ﬁxed-price contracting structure 
was used to compensate contractors for excavation work. Westinghouse and Stone & 
Webster billed the owners, a consortium of utilities in the Southeastern U.S., $58 million in 
extra charges above the original price for excavation work, alleging that the owners had 
provided incorrect and incomplete information about site conditions when the contractors 
prepared their original bid [16]. The owners paid half of this amount but disagreed with the 
contractors about their responsibility for the full $58 million in overruns, and the contractors 
sued to recover the remainder. This is just one example of how a disagreement over which 
stakeholder is responsible for cost overruns can result in lawsuits to recover lost proﬁts. As 
of 2012, before the ﬁrst nuclear-grade concrete had been poured at Vogtle 3 & 4, the 
excavation lawsuit was reportedly only “the latest in a series of disputes” between the 
17


## Page 18


owners and the contractors, including an “$800 million dispute stemming from delays in 
getting key regulatory approvals” and additional disputes over design change orders, “some 
of which have gone unresolved for months” [16]. A 2015 report by Bechtel on the cost and 
schedule overruns at the V.C. Summer 2 & 3 project similarly found that “the Contract does 
not appear to be serving the Owners or the Consortium particularly well” [17], with expert 
testimony revealing disagreements between Westinghouse, Chicago Bridge & Iron, and the 
project owners about which party was responsible for excess costs [18].  
The analysis suggests that cost-plus frameworks may be a superior choice for avoiding 
litigation between stakeholders directly involved in delivering the project (Construction 
Subcontractors, Equipment Suppliers, Design & Management) due to the ﬁxed proﬁt margin. 
On the other hand, cost-plus contracts can also increase project costs by incentivizing cost 
overruns in all stakeholders’ project scopes, thereby making their incentive structures 
misaligned with the project owner. Taken together, these results suggest that regardless of 
the contract type chosen, a high level of active owner engagement is critical for minimizing 
project costs. In the case of a cost-plus contract, the task of the owner is straightforward: 
build up a sizeable internal project team to carefully monitor the day-to-day activities of 
each stakeholder involved in project delivery and make sure all costs they charge to the 
owner are justiﬁed. For ﬁxed-price and performance-based contracts, this powerful 
oversight role is also important, but there should also be a greater emphasis on ensuring that 
proﬁt outcomes for each stakeholder are always cause-based rather than recipient-based. 
The goal of the owner, in other words, should be to ensure that each stakeholder is rewarded 
fairly for their performance, and never punished for overruns caused by other parties. 
Otherwise, the stakeholders will take it upon themselves to force accountability through 
litigation, potentially escalating the cost and schedule of the project and making 
stakeholders less incentivized to collaborate productively on the worksite. 
As an example of how project owners might structure a contract to ensure that stakeholder 
performance is always assessed accurately, consider a performance-based contract in 
which proﬁts are tied to meeting certain construction milestones within a speciﬁed time 
window. Instead of specifying a date for completion, an approach commonly used for 
milestone structuring (e.g., “if subcontractor X installs the containment building roof by 
August 12, they will receive a 5% bonus in compensation”), the contract could establish 
milestones with a set of ﬁrm preconditions for the completion of other stakeholders’ work 
scopes. For example, “upon completion of the containment walls by subcontractor Y and all 
open-top equipment installation activities by subcontractor Z, if subcontractor X then 
installs the containment building roof within 7 days, they will receive a 5% bonus in 
compensation”. This could help to avoid situations in which one stakeholder’s delays 
impede another stakeholder’s ability to meet their contractual milestones, which could 
18


## Page 19


result in subcontractor X suing subcontractor Y or Z over lost proﬁts. Using this approach, 
project participants can avoid any potential confusion over who is responsible for causing 
overruns, and compensating stakeholders fairly based on their performance may become 
more straightforward. An alternative, hybridized approach between “date-based” and “time-
window-based” schedule milestones that has been used in real-world construction projects 
and may be easier to administer is to continuously establish a series of near-term milestone 
dates on a “rolling basis” over the lifetime of the construction project [19]. Near-term 
milestone dates may narrow the window of opportunity for one stakeholder to unexpectedly 
impede the progress of another, while also requiring less information about the completion 
preconditions of other work activities. Furthermore, the inability to accurately forecast all 
potential schedule slippage on the critical path of a nuclear construction project means that 
milestone dates established exclusively at the project outset may “lose credibility with the 
contractor[s]” [19]. 
While attention to detail in the initial contract drafting is crucial, the reality is that even the 
most careful planners will not be able to anticipate every dispute or unfair allocation of 
proﬁts that may arise during construction. Thus, the owner should also set up an internal 
team of project monitors to carefully oversee the day-to-day activities of all stakeholders 
involved in construction and should be prepared to step in and oƯer acceptably revised 
contract terms if a dispute between parties appears imminent. This eƯort may be assisted 
by establishing an internal conﬂict resolution board, which creates a setting for stakeholders 
to try and resolve disputes productively without resorting to litigation. Flyvbjerg and Gardner 
provide an illustrative example of the successful application of ﬂexible contract terms during 
the construction of Heathrow Airport’s Terminal Five (T5) [20]. During the Heathrow project, 
a steel-frames contractor operating under a ﬁxed-price contract faced delays due to a 
concrete-pouring contractor falling behind schedule, thereby threatening the steel 
contractor’s proﬁt margin. To mitigate the risk of litigation or project abandonment by the 
steel contractor, the project owner, British Airports Authority (BAA), intervened and 
transitioned the steel contractor to a cost-reimbursable contract arrangement with an 
additional percentage proﬁt contingent upon meeting certain milestones. This resulted in a 
more cooperative, problem-solving dynamic between the two contractors, wherein the 
concrete contractor agreed to increase the workforce and the steel contractor adjusted its 
workﬂow to accommodate the delays. The resolution of this potentially disruptive conﬂict 
between two stakeholders allowed the project to progress more smoothly, reducing the risk 
of further cost and schedule overruns. 
It is important to note that ﬂexible contracting and a greater level of owner participation 
come with increased owner’s costs, which represented ~14% of total project OCC at Vogtle 
3 & 4 [21]. This ﬁgure would be further increased by costs associated with more extensive 
19


## Page 20


pre-project planning, establishing an internal dispute resolution board, hiring a large internal 
staƯ to actively oversee day-to-day construction activities, and tweaking contract terms to 
increase the compensation of one or more stakeholders. The question is therefore whether 
the costs associated with this approach outweigh the beneﬁts. While our analysis does not 
speciﬁcally model the additional owner’s costs associated with increased stakeholder 
engagement or attempt to quantify the speciﬁc cost and schedule savings from averting 
conﬂicts between stakeholders, ﬁndings from previous authors support the conclusion that 
greater owner involvement does typically reduce megaproject costs. As discussed in 
Introduction, previous econometric studies of nuclear power plant capital costs have found 
that costs are signiﬁcantly lower for projects in which the owner acts as its own construction 
manager [5] [6]. There also seems to be general agreement in the qualitative literature on 
megaproject management that better pre-project planning and more proactive owner 
engagement with stakeholders tends to result in better project outcomes [8] [9] [20], and the 
2024 DOE report Pathways to Commercial LiftoƯ: Advanced Nuclear has highlighted the 
importance of more extensive project management and increased spending on pre-project 
planning for FOAK projects [22]. Furthermore, according to industry data obtained through 
private consulting engagements, about 6 - 8% of owner’s costs is spent on project oversight 
in nuclear construction projects. Since owner’s costs generally comprise about 10 - 20% of 
overall nuclear project OCC [2] [23] [24], this suggests project oversight expenditures are on 
the order of ~1% of overnight costs for nuclear construction. While 1% of OCC may be a large 
ﬁgure in absolute terms (e.g., $200M for a project with $20B in OCC), in relative terms, 
substantial increases in project management spending are warranted if they can help to 
reduce avoidable cost overruns due to rework, low productivity, and associated ﬁnancing 
costs, which can represent more than 50% of overall spending for FOAK projects (see Figure 
2). 
Thus, the results of our analysis reinforce the conclusions of other authors who have found 
that detailed pre-project planning and more active engagement by the project owner can 
help to reduce cost and schedule overruns in nuclear power plant construction. Using our 
novel approach to stakeholder analysis, we are able to model the stark diƯerences in 
ﬁnancial outcomes that can result when contracts tie stakeholder proﬁts to cost overruns 
received rather than cost overruns caused. Regardless of the contract type used to 
compensate stakeholders (ﬁxed-price, cost-plus, or performance-based), we estimate that 
using a recipient-based rather than cause-based proﬁt allocation mechanism to administer 
the contract can result in proﬁt misallocations on the order of hundreds of millions to billions 
of dollars (Table 2), which helps to explain why incentive misalignments and lawsuits 
between project participants so often arise in multi-stakeholder nuclear power plant 
construction projects. These results also suggest that one reason the vertically integrated 
20


## Page 21


project delivery structures used in Russia, China, Japan, and South Korea tend to achieve 
better outcomes than Western nuclear builds [2] is that the existence of fewer unique 
stakeholders creates fewer opportunities for incentive misalignment, proﬁt misallocations, 
and resulting overruns. Our conclusions enable a better understanding of the importance of 
careful contracting and proactive owner involvement in Western nuclear projects with many 
distinct stakeholders involved. 
The Cost Reduction Tool used to produce the inputs for this analysis continues to be updated 
to incorporate more accurate cost correlations as new information becomes available, and 
the results of the stakeholder attribution will change accordingly. For example, the new 
approach to productivity-related overrun data proposed in this work could be used to 
reformulate the calculation of productivity-related overruns within the Cost Reduction Tool 
based on the multi-stakeholder involvement model described in Methods, as opposed to the 
current approach which relies only on the construction proﬁciency lever for this calculation. 
Regardless of the underlying correlations used in the Cost Reduction Tool, however, the 
signiﬁcant mismatch between shares of overruns caused and received by each stakeholder 
are expected to always be present (albeit in diƯerent quantities) because, as shown in this 
analysis, the mechanisms that result in a stakeholder collecting payment for overruns are 
diƯerent from the mechanisms by which the stakeholder causes cost overruns. In other 
words, the takeaways of this study and the recommendations to project owners for 
successful contract structuring will remain valid even as the framework used to produce the 
results remains under active development. 
Quantitative results derived from real-world contracting frameworks with more detailed and 
complex provisions than those used in Table 1 (e.g., time-based milestones for speciﬁc 
deliverables) would be useful to pursue in future work, as would subdividing the four 
stakeholders examined in this analysis into more subgroups for a greater level of granularity 
(e.g., modeling speciﬁc subcontractors within the Construction Subcontractors 
stakeholder, capturing the individual eƯects of the Reactor Vendor, A-E, and Constructor 
within the Design & Management team, and modeling the diƯerent repayment structures for 
banks, equity investors, etc. within the Creditors stakeholder). Modeling more gradual, or 
even capped, proﬁciency increases for stakeholders with misaligned incentive structures 
within the Cost Reduction Tool could also be a potentially improve the accuracy of the 
stakeholder overrun attribution across multiple plant builds. Lastly, given that a primary 
recommendation of this study is a greater level of owner engagement and oversight in 
nuclear construction, it would be valuable to attempt to directly quantify owner’s costs (and 
owner’s beneﬁts) associated with a greater level of active involvement in the project. A cost-
beneﬁt analysis of owner engagement could be performed to determine an ideal level of in-
house staƯing to ensure project success.

Key Takeaways 
We conclude with a summary of four key ﬁndings about the approaches nuclear power 
plant owners and stakeholders should take to minimize costs and maximize the chances of 
project success. 
 
(1) Increased attention to detail in contract structuring at the project outset. For 
contracts in which rewards are tied directly to performance (e.g., ﬁxed-price or 
performance-based contracts), careful structuring is essential to ensure that all 
stakeholders are rewarded fairly for their performance and never punished 
unintentionally for overruns or delays caused by other parties. The Discussion section 
provides a few speciﬁc examples of how contracts might be structured to ensure these 
outcomes, including establishing well-deﬁned preconditions for subcontractor work 
scopes to begin and using “time-window-based” instead of “date-based” schedule 
milestones. 
 
(2) Ongoing owner engagement with project stakeholders. As the project moves from the 
planning stage to the execution stage, ongoing owner involvement is crucial to keep the 
project on track. For cost-plus contracts, the owner should build up a sizeable and well-
informed oversight team to make sure all expenses are justiﬁed. For ﬁxed-price and 
performance-based contracts, the owner should also take additional steps to ensure 
that all stakeholders are compensated fairly for their performance as conditions on the 
ground change. The Discussion section suggests ﬂexible contract terms and internal 
dispute resolution boards as two possible steps towards achieving this goal. If the project 
owner does not take action to ensure performance is rewarded accurately, the result will 
be a dealignment of incentives and lawsuits between stakeholders to recover lost proﬁts, 
as has occurred often in recent Western builds. 
 
(3) More time and resources spent on planning and oversight. Previous studies have 
highlighted extensive pre-project planning and strong owner oversight as two of the 
strongest factors in reducing megaproject costs and schedule risks. We estimate that on 
the order of 1% of total overnight capital costs is spent by the owner on project oversight 
in nuclear construction projects. Although this may represent a large amount of spending 
in absolute terms (e.g., $200M for a $20B project), it is clear that substantial increases in 
project management spending are well justiﬁed if they can help to mitigate avoidable 
cost overruns due to rework, low productivity, and associated ﬁnancing costs, especially 
for FOAK projects where these overruns may represent 50% or more of total project 
spending. 
22


## Page 23


(4) Vertical integration. The takeaways above are primarily intended for nuclear 
construction projects involving many independent stakeholders (owner, construction 
manager, architect-engineer, equipment suppliers, reactor vendor, etc.) whose 
incentives must be forcibly aligned using carefully structured contracts. Another 
approach that has been shown to deliver nuclear construction projects eƯectively is a 
vertically integrated model in which most or all of these functions are performed by a 
single entity with internally aligned incentives, often a national government through the 
use of state-owned enterprises, as in the French, Russian, Chinese, Indian, and South 
Korean nuclear industries. In countries where a state-owned model is not feasible, 
vertical integration within private companies is also a potential option for minimizing the 
number of stakeholders and reducing opportunities for incentive misalignment. 
Historical examples have included large utility companies in the U.S. and Canada 
(Ontario Hydro, Commonwealth Edison, Tennessee Valley Authority, etc.) taking on most 
or all of the engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) responsibilities for their 
plants internally, as well as the historical German and Japanese models in which the 
owner and EPC are separate entities but most EPC functions are handled by a single 
vertically integrated company (Hitachi, Toshiba, Kraftwerk Union, etc.). Lastly, given the 
critical importance of incentive alignment, if government incentive programs with limited 
resources (loan guarantees, tax credits, direct investments, cost overrun insurance, etc.) 
are faced with multiple applications for new nuclear projects, all else being equal, 
administrators should consider prioritizing support for merchant projects in deregulated 
electricity markets over cost-of-service projects in regulated markets. Prioritizing 
projects in which the incentives of the project owner are aligned as much as possible 
with end-use consumers may increase the chance of success for these government 
programs.

Calculating overruns by recipient 
Because the Cost Reduction Tool breaks down costs based on the EPRI Generalized Nuclear 
Code of Accounts (GN-COA) system [25] and further subdivides them into “factory 
equipment,” “site material,” and “site labor” costs based on the EEDB code of accounts [4], 
calculating which fraction of the total costs and overruns are received by each stakeholder 
is relatively straightforward. In the direct costs category (Account 2 in GN-COA), all factory 
equipment costs are assumed to go to the Equipment Suppliers stakeholder, while all site 
material and site labor costs go to the Construction Subcontractors stakeholder. 
Indirect costs (Account 3 in GN-COA) are attributed to diƯerent stakeholders based on the 
proportions in which indirect costs were attributed to these stakeholders for the PWR12-ME 
code of accounts described in the EEDB Phase IX (1987) report [4]. For the PWR12-ME, 
indirect costs (Account 9 in EEDB) are split into three subaccounts: Construction Services 
(Account 91), Engineering and Home OƯice Services (Account 92), and Account 93 (Field 
Supervision and Field OƯice Services). Account 91, which comprises 30.6% of the total 
indirect costs, includes temporary construction facilities, construction tools and 
equipment, and additional costs to constructors including payroll insurance, taxes, permits, 
and insurance [26]. Thus, 30.6% of all indirect costs are attributed to the Construction 
Subcontractors. 
The remaining 69.4% of indirect costs were in Accounts 92 and 93, which include 
engineering services, management services, supervision, testing and startup services, and 
ﬁeld QA/QC. These services are performed by some combination of the reactor vendor, 
constructor, architect-engineer, and possibly additional stakeholders (e.g., engineering or 
management consultants brought in on the project), but unfortunately the EEDB data does 
not subdivide these accounts in a way that would allow a determination of the percentages 
that go to each of these individual stakeholders. Instead, we attribute the entire 69.4% to a 
stakeholder called Design & Management, which includes within it the reactor vendor, A-E, 
and constructor, as shown in Figure 1.
