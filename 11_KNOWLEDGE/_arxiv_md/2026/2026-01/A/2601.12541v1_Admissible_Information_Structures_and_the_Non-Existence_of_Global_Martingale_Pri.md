Admissible Information Structures and the Non-Existence of Global Martingale
Pricing∗
Alejandro Rodriguez Dominguez†
Abstract. No-arbitrage asset pricing characterizes valuation through the existence of equivalent martingale
measures relative to a filtration and a class of admissible trading strategies. In practice, pricing is
performed across multiple asset classes driven by economic variables that are only partially spanned
by traded instruments, raising a structural question: does there exist a single admissible information
structure under which all traded assets can be jointly priced as martingales?. We treat the filtration
as an endogenous object constrained by admissibility and time-ordering, rather than as an exogenous
primitive. For any finite collection of assets, whenever martingale pricing is feasible under some
admissible filtration, it is already feasible under a canonical minimal filtration generated by the
asset prices themselves; these pricing-sufficient filtrations are unique up to null sets and stable under
restriction and aggregation when a common pricing measure exists. Our main result shows that
this local compatibility does not extend globally: with three independent unspanned finite-variation
drivers, there need not exist any admissible filtration and equivalent measure under which all assets
are jointly martingales. The obstruction is sharp (absent with one driver and compatible pairwise
with two) and equivalent to failure of admissible dynamic completeness. We complement the theory
with numerical diagnostics based on discrete-time Doob–Meyer decompositions, illustrating how
admissible information structures suppress predictable components, while inadmissible filtrations
generate systematic predictability.
Key words. Equivalent martingale measures, admissible filtrations, incomplete markets, dynamic completeness,
information constraints, no-arbitrage pricing

1. Introduction. The fundamental theorem of asset pricing characterizes the absence of
arbitrage through the existence of an equivalent martingale measure relative to a filtration and
a notion of admissible trading. Beginning with the seminal work of Harrison and Kreps [15]
and Harrison and Pliska [16], and extended to general semimartingale models by Delbaen and
Schachermayer [6], this framework provides the foundation of modern continuous-time asset
pricing. A defining feature of the theory is that martingale pricing is inherently conditional on
both the information structure and the admissibility constraints imposed on trading strategies.
In the classical formulation, the filtration is treated as an exogenous primitive. Pricing,
hedging, and questions of market completeness are studied conditional on a fixed information
structure, and market incompleteness is typically reflected in the non-uniqueness of equivalent
martingale measures [7, 25]. A large literature analyzes the implications of this multiplicity
for valuation, hedging, and risk sharing while maintaining the filtration as given. Closely
related work in the theory of enlargement of filtrations studies how martingale and semi-
martingale properties change when a fixed filtration is refined [21, 22].

This paper adopts a complementary perspective.
Rather than taking the information
structure as fixed, we treat it as an endogenous object constrained by admissibility and time-
ordering, and ask whether there exists a single admissible filtration under which martingale
pricing can be achieved simultaneously for all admissible assets. This question is not addressed
by standard incompleteness results, which presuppose a common filtration from the outset,
nor by the enlargement-of-filtrations literature, which studies refinements of a given filtration
rather than the compatibility of distinct minimal ones.
Our analysis begins locally. For any finite group of traded assets, we show that whenever
martingale pricing is feasible under some admissible filtration, it is already feasible under a
canonical reduced filtration generated by the asset prices themselves. These pricing-sufficient
filtrations are minimal, unique up to null sets, and determined entirely by no-arbitrage and
admissibility. They support conditional risk-neutral valuation, are stable under restriction of
the asset set, and remain compatible under pairwise aggregation when a common equivalent
martingale measure exists.
The main contribution of the paper is to show that such local and pairwise compatibility
does not, in general, extend globally. Under admissibility constraints, time-ordering, and the
presence of multiple independent unspanned finite-variation drivers, there exists no admissible
filtration and no equivalent probability measure under which all asset prices are jointly local
martingales. The obstruction is sharp and minimal: with a single unspanned driver global
martingale pricing is possible; with two independent drivers pairwise compatibility can be
maintained; and failure arises for the first time with three drivers. We further show that this
non-existence result is equivalent to the failure of admissible dynamic completeness, thereby
placing the obstruction squarely within the classical no-arbitrage framework.
To complement the theoretical analysis, the paper includes numerical illustrations in a
controlled simulated environment. These illustrations are not empirical tests. Rather, they
provide constructive diagnostics based on discrete-time Doob–Meyer decompositions, show-
ing how admissible information structures induce only negligible predictable finite-variation
components, while inadmissible enlargements of the filtration (violating admissibility or time-
ordering) generate systematic predictability. The simulations visualize the mechanism under-
lying the theoretical obstruction and clarify why pricing-consistent conditioning cannot be
extended arbitrarily, even when additional variables appear statistically informative.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 3 formalizes admissibility
and pricing-sufficient filtrations and establishes their local existence, stability, and the main
impossibility result. Section 4 presents the numerical illustrations. Section 5 concludes and
discusses implications and directions for future research.

and limits of arbitrage, and stochastic control under partial information. While the present
contribution builds on these frameworks, it addresses a question that is not treated directly in

The modern theory of arbitrage-free pricing characterizes asset prices through the existence
of equivalent martingale measures relative to a given filtration and a notion of admissible
trading. Foundational results were established by Harrison and Kreps [15] and Harrison and
Pliska [16], and extended to general semimartingale models by Delbaen and Schachermayer
[6]. Comprehensive treatments of the theory and its implications for pricing and hedging in
continuous time are given in [25, 33, 7]. A common feature of this literature is that the filtration
is taken as an exogenous primitive, and questions of pricing, hedging, and completeness are
studied conditional on this fixed information structure.
The dependence of martingale properties on information has been studied systematically
in the theory of enlargement of filtrations, initiated by Jeulin [21] and Jeulin and Yor [22], and
further developed in [20, 31]. This literature characterizes how semimartingale and martingale
properties are affected by progressive or initial enlargements of a given filtration and provides
conditions under which no-arbitrage, martingale representations, and stability properties are
preserved. Applications include models with insider information, asymmetric information,
and anticipation; see, for example, [1, 13]. The present paper differs fundamentally in per-
spective. Rather than studying refinements of a fixed filtration, we study whether there exists
any admissible filtration that can simultaneously support martingale pricing for multiple asset
groups whose locally pricing-sufficient filtrations may be incompatible. The obstruction iden-
tified here does not arise from a particular enlargement procedure, but from the non-existence
of a common admissible refinement.
A closely related strand of the literature concerns incomplete markets and the resulting
non-uniqueness of equivalent martingale measures [16]. In such settings, incompleteness is
usually attributed to insufficient spanning of sources of uncertainty by traded assets. A large
body of work studies pricing and hedging under this multiplicity, including super-replication
[5], utility-based and indifference pricing [18, 3], and convex risk-measure approaches [9, 5];
see also [10] for links between pricing and risk measures. In contrast, the present paper does
not fix a filtration and then analyze multiplicity of martingale measures within it. Instead, it
shows that under admissibility and time-ordering, the relevant filtration itself may be asset-
dependent, and failure of global martingale pricing may result from incompatibility between
locally pricing-sufficient filtrations even when local and pairwise martingale measures exist.
The paper also connects to the literature on dynamic completeness, spanning, and the
martingale representation property. Under a fixed filtration, classical results link (dynamic)
completeness to uniqueness of equivalent martingale measures and to the ability to replicate
contingent claims; see, for example, [25, 7] and the martingale representation discussion in
[32]. More generally, completeness in diffusion models is characterized by rank conditions on
volatility coefficients and related spanning criteria [24, 28]. The present paper complements
this literature by isolating an obstruction that arises even prior to questions of uniqueness:
admissibility and time-ordering may prevent the existence of any single filtration under which
global martingale pricing holds, and we show that this is equivalent to failure of admissible
dynamic completeness.
A further related literature studies stochastic discount factors (state-price deflators) and
limits of arbitrage. The representation of asset prices by stochastic discount factors is standard

Remark 3.1 (Asset groups and martingale pricing). An asset group A is a finite index set
of traded assets. Martingale pricing of SA is always meant componentwise: each individual
asset price Si, i ∈A, is a local martingale under the same filtration and probability measure.
Such filtrations are well defined under mild regularity conditions. In particular, the col-
lection of all equivalent local martingale measures pricing SA induces a minimal sufficient
filtration in the classical sense of probability theory. This filtration is unique up to P-null sets
and is determined entirely by no-arbitrage and admissibility. We refer to GA as the minimal
pricing-sufficient filtration for the asset group A.
Within a pricing-sufficient filtration, asset prices admit conditional martingale representa-
tions, and standard results on martingale orthogonality and representation apply. In particu-
lar, conditional dependencies between asset returns are screened off by the underlying sources
of randomness captured by the filtration. This local closure property is stable under restric-
tion of the asset set and under pairwise aggregation of asset groups, provided admissibility is
preserved.
The central question addressed in this paper is whether pricing-sufficient filtrations as-
sociated with different asset groups admit a common admissible refinement. Formally, given
asset groups A and B with minimal pricing-sufficient filtrations GA and GB, one may ask
whether there exists an admissible filtration H such that both SA and SB admit equivalent
local martingale measures relative to H. While such refinements exist in simple settings, the
main results of the paper show that they need not exist in general once multiple unspanned
sources of uncertainty and time-ordering constraints are present.
Remark 3.2 (Role of admissibility). Admissibility restricts the class of trading strategies
used to define no-arbitrage and excludes anticipative or pathological strategies. It does not
alter the requirement that individual asset prices be local martingales under a pricing measure.

3.5. Dynamic Completeness and Global Martingale Pricing. This section places the
obstruction result of Section 3.4 within the classical theory of dynamic completeness. The
main result shows that the non-existence of a global admissible martingale pricing structure is
equivalent to failure of admissible dynamic completeness. This equivalence clarifies the precise
scope of the impossibility result and situates it within established asset-pricing theory.
We work under the standing assumptions of Sections 3.1–3.4.
In particular, admissi-
ble trading strategies are bounded from below, filtrations are required to satisfy the usual
conditions, and asset prices are semimartingales relative to a reference filtration. Dynamic
completeness is understood relative to both a filtration and admissibility. It is invoked here
solely to characterize existence of a global martingale pricing measure for asset prices, and
not to introduce portfolio-based pricing objects. Given an admissible filtration H and a prob-
ability measure Q ∼P, the market is said to be dynamically complete under (H, Q) if every
square-integrable H-martingale admits a representation as a stochastic integral with respect
to the asset price process S using admissible strategies. The following theorem establishes the
equivalence between global martingale pricing and admissible dynamic completeness.
Theorem 3.14 (Equivalence with admissible dynamic completeness).
The following state-
ments are equivalent:
1. There exists an admissible pricing structure (H, Q) such that the entire asset price
vector S is a local (Q, H)-martingale.
2. There exists an admissible filtration H and a probability measure Q ∼P under which
the market is dynamically complete.
Proof. Suppose (1) holds. If S is a local (Q, H)-martingale and the market is dynamically
complete under (H, Q), then standard martingale representation results imply that every H-
martingale can be expressed as a stochastic integral with respect to S. Hence (2) holds.
Conversely, suppose (2) holds. Dynamic completeness implies uniqueness of the equiv-
alent local martingale measure under H and ensures that all asset prices admit martingale
representations under (Q, H). Hence (1) holds.
Theorem 3.14 shows that the obstruction identified in Section 3.4 is not a failure of mar-
tingale pricing in general, but a manifestation of failure of admissible dynamic completeness.
When admissible trading strategies span all sources of uncertainty present in the filtration, a
global equivalent martingale measure exists and the obstruction disappears. Conversely, when
admissible dynamic completeness fails, no single admissible filtration can support martingale
pricing for all assets simultaneously.
This equivalence highlights a structural distinction between classical formulations and
the present setting. In standard results, dynamic completeness is studied relative to a fixed

the obstruction arises because no admissible filtration supports completeness once multiple

5. Conclusions and Future Work. This paper studies no-arbitrage asset pricing when
the information structure is treated as an endogenous object constrained by admissibility
and time-ordering, rather than as an exogenously fixed primitive. For any finite group of
traded assets, we show that whenever martingale pricing is feasible under some admissible
filtration, it is already feasible under a canonical reduced filtration generated by the asset
prices themselves. These pricing-sufficient filtrations are minimal, unique up to null sets, and
stable under restriction of the asset set and under pairwise aggregation, as established in
Propositions 3.3, 3.5, and 3.6.
The main theoretical contribution is the identification of a sharp structural obstruction
to global martingale pricing. Under admissibility and time-ordering constraints, and in the
presence of multiple independent unspanned finite-variation drivers, there exists no admissible
filtration and no equivalent probability measure under which all admissible asset prices are
jointly local martingales. The obstruction is minimal: global pricing remains feasible with
a single unspanned driver; pairwise compatibility can be maintained with two independent
drivers; and failure arises for the first time with three independent drivers.
As shown in
Theorem 3.13 and Theorem 3.14, this non-existence result is equivalent to the failure of
admissible dynamic completeness, placing the obstruction squarely within the classical no-
arbitrage framework.
These results clarify a limitation not addressed by standard incompleteness theory. Clas-
sical formulations analyze incompleteness relative to a fixed filtration and attribute non-
uniqueness of equivalent martingale measures to lack of spanning.
Here, the obstruction
arises at a more primitive level. Even when each finite asset group admits a well-defined
equivalent local martingale measure and pairwise pricing consistency holds, admissibility and
non-anticipativity may prevent the existence of any single filtration supporting joint martin-
gale pricing.
The numerical illustrations are intended solely as constructive diagnostics consistent with
the theory. Using a controlled simulated environment and discrete-time Doob–Meyer decom-
positions, they visualize how admissible information structures induce only negligible predict-
able finite-variation components, while inadmissible enlargements of the filtration (violating
admissibility or time-ordering) generate systematic predictability.
The simulations do not
test the theoretical results, but illustrate the mechanism underlying the obstruction in Theo-
rem 3.13.
Limitations and scope. Several limitations should be emphasized.
The paper does not
claim that global martingale measures fail to exist in general, nor that markets are inher-
ently incomplete; the impossibility result is conditional on admissibility and time-ordering
constraints and disappears under admissible dynamic completeness. The analysis is indepen-
dent of equilibrium considerations, and any causal language is strictly interpretative. Finally,
the numerical illustrations are not intended as empirical validation, but as concrete examples
illustrating the structural mechanisms identified in the theory.
Future research directions include incorporating additional market frictions, such as trans-
action costs or funding constraints, and extending the analysis to infinite-dimensional settings,
including term-structure models or markets with a continuum of assets. More broadly, the

All statements are conditional on the filtration and admissibility
constraints.
G.2. Pearl-style structural causal models. Structural causal models as developed by
Pearl [29] define causality through counterfactual interventions implemented via the do(·)
operator. An intervention replaces a structural equation while holding others fixed.
In financial markets, such interventions are generally incompatible with pricing. Replacing
the law of a price process, volatility, or funding variable while holding trading opportunities
fixed typically violates self-financing or admissibility constraints. As a result, the martingale
property is destroyed and no equivalent martingale measure exists under the intervened sys-
tem. Thus, Pearl-style interventions are not admissible operations in the sense of no-arbitrage
pricing. The incompatibility is structural and does not depend on equilibrium assumptions.
G.3. Granger causality. Granger causality [11] defines causality through predictability:
a process X Granger-causes Y if past values of X improve prediction of Y relative to a
given filtration. This notion is purely predictive and filtration-dependent. It is compatible
with pricing-consistent causality but strictly weaker. Predictive relationships may exist that
have no pricing relevance, and conversely, pricing-relevant variables may fail to Granger-cause
prices if their effects are absorbed into the pricing kernel. Granger causality therefore captures
temporal dependence, not structural invariance under admissible changes.
G.4. Structural equation models in econometrics. Econometric structural models of-
ten specify systems of stochastic equations with exogenous shocks and identify causal effects
through exclusion restrictions or instruments [19].
Such models typically assume a fixed probability space and allow counterfactual variation
of structural parameters. In contrast, asset pricing constrains probability measures through
no-arbitrage and admissibility. Structural variations that violate these constraints are not
meaningful within the pricing framework. Accordingly, pricing-consistent causality is more
restrictive than standard structural causality but applies in settings where prices and infor-
mation are endogenously linked. The distinctions can be summarized as follows:
• Pearl SCMs allow arbitrary counterfactual interventions; pricing-consistent causality
allows only admissible transformations preserving martingale pricing.
• Granger causality captures predictability, not invariance.
• Structural econometric models fix probability spaces ex ante; pricing endogenizes the
probability measure through no-arbitrage.
As a result, causal reasoning compatible with asset pricing is necessarily local, filtration-
dependent, and constrained by admissibility.
This limitation is not a deficiency of causal
modeling, but a structural feature of no-arbitrage markets.
