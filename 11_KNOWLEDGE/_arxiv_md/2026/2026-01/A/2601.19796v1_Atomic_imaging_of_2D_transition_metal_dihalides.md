Abstract: 
TransiƟon metal di-iodides such as FeI2, NiI2 and CoI2 are an emerging class of 2D magnets exhibiƟng 
rich and diverse magneƟc behaviour, but their study at the monolayer limit has been severely hindered 
by fabricaƟon challenges due to their air-sensiƟvity. Here, we introduce a polymer-free method for 
clean, rapid, and high-yield assembly of hermeƟcally encapsulated suspended samples of air-sensiƟve 
monolayers. Applying it to di-iodides enables atomic resoluƟon characterisaƟon of thin samples - 
down to the monolayer limit - for the ﬁrst Ɵme. Our imaging, combined with complementary ﬁrst-
principles calculaƟons, reveals an unusually small energy barrier between alternate stable stacking 
polytypes in few-layer ﬁlms, enabling extrinsic control of the stacking phase. We also observe stable 
isolated iodine vacancies that do not aggregate to form extended structures, and idenƟfy and verify 
the stability of the various edge conﬁguraƟons of thin samples. These results establish the unique 
structural characterisƟcs of these materials in the thin limit, and more broadly demonstrate the uƟlity 
of our transfer plaƞorm for creaƟng atomically clean suspended vdW heterostructures.

IntroducƟon: 
The family of successfully isolated two-dimensional (2D) materials conƟnues to rapidly grow, 
expanding the range of van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures that can be assembled layer-by-layer 
and therefore the scope of possible phenomena to be explored. MagneƟc 2D materials1 are relaƟvely 
new members of the 2D family and oﬀer a dramaƟc expansion to the potenƟal properƟes achievable 
with vdW heterostructures. Among those, the most recent addiƟons are the transiƟon metal diiodides, 
MI2, which feature rich and diverse magneƟc properƟes which remain mostly unexplored. This includes 
NiI2 - an extremely rare layered mulƟferroic, where a helical magneƟc ground state2–7 is combined with 
out-of-plane ferroelectric polarisaƟon, which persists down to few-layer thickness8.  In addiƟon, this 
material has recently been shown to possess giant natural opƟcal acƟvity at terahertz frequencies6 and 
spin-polarized edge states7, making it one of the most interesƟng emerging 2D systems. Other exciƟng 
representaƟves of the di-iodide family include frustrated triangular magnets with rich spin dynamics 
and Kitaev physics, such as spin-one FeI29 which hosts hybrid dipolar and mulƟpolar quasiparƟcle 
excitaƟons10,11, and spin-half CoI212,13. 
However, like many novel atomically thin crystals14–16, the transiƟon metal diiodides have been found 
to be extremely sensiƟve to mild ambient condiƟons including exposure to oxygen, water, elevated 
temperature and light15–22. These can lead to the breakdown of the magneƟc ordering or even 
complete disintegraƟon of their crystalline structure15,20,23,24, limiƟng the exploraƟon of these systems 
to studies that employ encapsulaƟon25,26 or maintain inert or ultra-high vacuum (UHV) condiƟons27–29. 
In addiƟon to air sensiƟvity, surface contaminaƟon remaining from mechanical exfoliaƟon and transfer 
steps causes further problems. The contaminants can induce degradaƟon, especially when exposed to 
an intense electron or laser beam, but also by prevenƟng hermeƟc encapsulaƟon, and cleaning steps 
to remove such contaminants30,31 can also damage the sensiƟve materials and impart strain. The result 
is that despite their exciƟng magneƟc behaviours, detailed atomic scale characterisaƟon of thin di-
iodide crystals is completely absent in the literature due to the challenges in fabricaƟng atomically 
clean and uniform specimens. Such studies are essenƟal to understand the magneƟc properƟes of few-
layer metal di-iodides, as well as many other novel environmentally sensiƟve materials, to both predict 
their properƟes and interpret experimental observaƟons. 
Here, we present an innovaƟve plaƞorm for electron and scanning probe microscopy characterisaƟon 
of air sensiƟve 2D materials, as well as other studies requiring suspended samples. To enable this, we 
have re-designed silicon nitride32,33 (SiNx) canƟlevers to introduce suspended regions (SupporƟng 
informaƟon, SI, Fig. S1-S2) for subsequent imaging (SI Fig. S3-S5) and adapted them for common 
microscopy holders. This approach has a number of advantages over the state-of-the-art polymer 
transfer techniques: 1. Extremely clean 2D material surfaces and interfaces resulƟng from complete 
avoidance of organic materials and liquids; 2. On-grid fabricaƟon avoids the need for addiƟonal 
specimen release onto commercial supports, improving sample integrity, quality and yield; 3. The 
approach enables contaminaƟon-free hermeƟcally sealed encapsulaƟon using stable 2D materials 
(graphene or hexagonal boron nitride) in an inert glove box (Ar or N2) or UHV environment. Here we 
show this allows the ﬁrst imaging of point and edge defects as well as stacking features in even the 
most environmentally sensiƟve 2D materials.  
We demonstrate the eﬃcacy of our technique by performing the ﬁrst comprehensive Scanning 
Transmission Electron Microscopy (STEM) study of FeI2, NiI2 and CoI2 (SI Fig. S7-S9) transiƟon metal 
diiodides down to monolayer thicknesses. These MI2 materials are extremely air-sensiƟve, with even 
relaƟvely thick crystals degrading completely in less than 5 seconds on contact with air (SI Fig. S10). 
We use our novel stacking approach to achieve high-quality graphene encapsulaƟon immediately aŌer 
exfoliaƟon, achieving a full hermeƟc seal without any interlayer contaminaƟon. The result is transiƟon

Results:  
The 2D transiƟon metal dihalides were ﬁrst mechanically exfoliated and then encapsulated with 
graphene layers on both sides using silicon nitride canƟlevers in an argon glove box environment to 
prevent any exposure to air during the process17,22. The silicon nitride sample supports were prepared 
using standard semiconductor processes at wafer-scale, and paƩerned with an array of few μm 
diameter holes (for details see SI SecƟon 1). This provides ﬂexible, transparent, chemically inert, and 
thermally stable supports for the stacking process, see Fig. 1a. To facilitate good adhesion of the 
specimen and prevent electrostaƟc charging, the canƟlevers have been coated with three metal layers 
(1 nm Ta, 5 nm Pt, 1.5 nm Au)33, allowing eﬃcient pickup of thin 2D crystals, including monolayer 
graphene. We ﬁnd that the best yield of suspended monolayer crystals was achieved with 2 μm 
diameter holes, but for thicker structures the holes can be larger. To achieve encapsulaƟon, the holey 
region of the SiNx canƟlever was used to pick up a graphene sheet from an oxidised silicon substrate 
(Fig. 1b(i)). Next, a thin MI₂ crystal was picked up at 40°C such that it became aƩached to the graphene 
on the canƟlever (Fig. 1b(ii) and Supplementary Video S1). Finally, the canƟlever was used to pick up 
another graphene layer to complete the encapsulaƟon (Fig. 1b(iii)). The graphene layers must be larger 
than the MI₂ crystal as even a small crack can lead to complete disintegraƟon of the crystal, and the 
encapsulaƟon procedure was performed in an argon glove box environment to prevent the MI₂ being 
exposed to air. Finally, the SiNx sample support with the graphene-MI2-graphene heterostructure was 
released onto a custom TEM grid with a large central aperture (Fig. 1b(iv), SI Fig. S5). As seen in the 
higher magniﬁcaƟon image (Fig. 1d), the encapsulated MI₂ crystal remains clean, stable, and visible in 
air.

Atomic structure of MI2 
Individual vdW layers of MI2 materials are comprised of transiƟon metal atoms octahedrally bonded 
to six adjacent iodide ions (see Fig. 2g leŌ). In bulk FeI2 these layers are combined with AA-type stacking 
to form the 1T phase34,35(Fig. 2j right, SI Fig. S11), which is also the most commonly observed polytype 
for the majority of ﬁrst row transiƟon metal di-iodides (TiI2, VI2, MnI2). In contrast, bulk NiI2 and CoI2 
preferenƟally crystallises in the 3R phase via ABC-type stacking (Fig. 2j middle)34,35. These structural 
phases are readily disƟnguishable in plan view high angle annular dark ﬁeld (HAADF) STEM images due 
to the Z-contrast variaƟons between purely metal and purely iodine atomic columns in the 1T phase, 
and the presence of mixed metal and iodine atomic columns in the 3R phase. For MI2 crystal 
thicknesses of more than 10 layers, our HAADF STEM images conﬁrm the expected bulk stacking 
conﬁguraƟons, with bulk FeI2 (Fig. 2c) matching the 1T phase, with an in plane laƫce parameter of a= 
4.05 ± 0.12Å, and bulk NiI2 (Fig. 2f) matching the 3R phase with a= 3.92 ± 0.14Å, both in good 
agreement with previous observaƟons34,35. Comparison of the STEM images to simulaƟons for bilayer 
NiI₂ also reveals the expected 3R stacking in agreement with bulk material (Fig. 2e). However, images 
of bilayer FeI₂ are found to also match with 3R phase stacking (Fig. 2b, SI Fig. S12), not to the expected 
1T stacking order of bulk FeI₂. Similar behaviour is observed in bilayer CoI2 which we ﬁnd in 1T phase 
rather than its bulk 3R form. Few-layer NiI₂ and FeI₂ also both show predominantly 3R stacking (SI Fig. 
S14), however cross-secƟonal STEM images (Fig. 2k) of a small folded trilayer FeI₂ crystal disconnected 
from the main sample indicates the 1T phase, indicaƟng high polytype plasƟcity, and that the presence 
of strain or other energeƟc inputs can cause spontaneous phase sliding between 3R and 1T. Monolayer 
regions of all 3 materials (Fig. 2a,d,g) show excellent agreement with the expected octahedral atomic 
structure in FeI2, CoI2 and NiI2.  
We uƟlise ﬁrst-principles calculaƟons to examine the origin of the structural phase transiƟon in thin 
FeI2, and the absence of such a transiƟon in NiI2. We ﬁrst consider the magneƟc conﬁguraƟon, 
calculaƟng the energy for the non-magneƟc (NM), ferromagneƟc (FM), anƟferromagneƟc (AFM) and 
stripe anƟferromagneƟc (sAFM) structures (Fig. 2l). From the results, we conﬁrmed that the stripe 
anƟferromagneƟc conﬁguraƟon7, with pairs of alternaƟng spin-up and spin-down magneƟc moments, 
is the energeƟcally favourable magneƟc structure for both materials. All subsequent calculaƟons are 
performed with a uniform out-of-plane spin alignment in each layer, as the small exchange energy 
scale (< meV) should not inﬂuence structural properƟes at room temperature, avoiding the need for 
large supercells. Comparing the calculated energy of the 1T and 3R phases for FeI₂ we ﬁnd that the 1T 
phase is energeƟcally favourable for all thicknesses, consistent with the bulk structure we idenƟfy, but 
not with the stacking conﬁguraƟon observed experimentally for few layer FeI₂. Nonetheless, there is 
only a small energy diﬀerence of just ~4.4 meV per unit cell between 1T and 3R phases at the bilayer 
limit, (Fig. 2j). This is an order of magnitude lower than the stacking fault penalty for NbSe2 bilayers, 
where such faults between two compeƟng structures are commonly observed in CVD growth36. This 
value increases to ~14 meV per unit cell for 3-4 layer FeI₂ (Fig.2m). The transiƟon from 1T to the 3R 
stacking arrangement is therefore comparaƟvely easy in thin samples, which enables external factors, 
such as strain from mechanical exfoliaƟon or pressure from the graphene encapsulaƟon, to induce a 
phase transiƟon from the 1T phase to the 3R phase in few-layer FeI₂ crystals. Our calculaƟons also 
show the 3R stacking arrangement of NiI2 is energeƟcally favourable for all thicknesses, consistent with 
both the expected structure and experimental observaƟons for this material (Fig. 2m). However, the 
comparaƟvely small stacking energy diﬀerences found in these calculaƟons suggest that similar 
stacking instability may occur in few-layer NiI2 as we observe in CoI2 (Fig 2j-i), although we did not 
observe this experimentally for our samples.

Stability of atomic vacancies in monolayer MI2 
STEM is one of the few techniques able to probe the populaƟon of diﬀerent types of point defect in 
vdW materials, which is important as the nature and quanƟty of point defects oŌen determines the 
electronic and magneƟc behaviour37,38. We therefore apply our atomic resoluƟon STEM imaging to 
understanding the nature of atomic point defects in MI2 materials, focusing on the monolayers, as 
these provide the clearest visualisaƟon of the nature of defect sites (Fig. 3a-b). The populaƟon of 
isolated vacancy defects was quanƟﬁed as a funcƟon of electron dose from comparison of HAADF 
STEM images to simulaƟons (Fig. 3c). Edges and pores are excluded from this analysis and considered 
in a subsequent secƟon. Each MI2 layer has two iodide sublayers (above and below the metal ions) 
which are separately visible in plan view due to their octahedral symmetry (Fig. 2j), allowing the I- 
vacancy populaƟons in the two sublaƫces to be separately tracked. In both MI2 materials, the most 
common atomic point defect is I- vacancies, with one I- sublaƫce exhibiƟng a ~4 Ɵmes higher density 
of vacancies. In both dihalides, comparaƟvely fewer metal caƟon vacancies are observed (Fig. 3d). The 
locaƟon of point defects was tracked from frame to frame over the video series, revealing that 
individual vacancies oŌen move their locaƟon in sequenƟal images, while the total point defect density 
gradually decreases during extended imaging, parƟcularly in NiI2 (Fig.3d).  
This suggests that defects are either highly mobile within the laƫce39 or can both form and heal over 
the ~2 second frame acquisiƟon Ɵmes. The decrease in areal defect density with increasing electron 
dose also suggests that vacancy annealing is favourable. Vacancy defects in 2D crystals can disappear 
either by diﬀusing through the laƫce to edge sites or by being ﬁlled with a suitable mobile atom/ion 
diﬀusing on the surface. In graphene, hBN and MoS2, aggregaƟon of point defects during extended 
S/TEM imaging leads to extended pores or increasing numbers of line defects 37,40. In our study we see 
no tendency for vacancy clustering to form extended defects, even aŌer prolonged imaging (Fig. 3a-b). 
In fact, the total monolayer area is found to increase during imaging (SI Fig. S16), revealing that defect 
healing is oŌen faster than defect creaƟon.  
To beƩer understand the point defect populaƟon and dynamic behaviours we have performed ab-
iniƟo calculaƟons of defect energies for both single vacancy and di-vacancy defects in FeI2 and NiI2 (see 
SI Fig. S20 and S21). We ﬁnd that the formaƟon energy for diﬀerent point vacancies depends sensiƟvely 
on the iodide chemical potenƟal with iodide vacancies generally more favourable than metal vacancies 
in NiI2 and metal vacancies more favourable in FeI₂. This, however, does not take into account exterior 
posiƟoning of the iodide atoms in each MI2 structural layer, making them more available for chemical 
interacƟons with extraneous chemical species, and hence leading to higher defect densiƟes observed 
in the experiment. The agglomeraƟon of vacancies in divacancy complexes is generally found to be an 
endothermic process for all species except the FeI2 nearest neighbour iodide divacancy (see SI Fig S20 
and S21). In all cases, the intrinsic hydrostaƟc strain ﬁelds around monovacancies, which drive the 
formaƟon of vacancy complexes41 from single point defects, are small in magnitude and short-ranged 
(<4 Å, see SI Fig S22 and S23), consistent with the low dielectric constants expected42 in 2D materials, 
inhibiƟng their long-range interacƟon and subsequent agglomeraƟon. Here, the materials are 
graphene encapsulated, eﬀecƟvely trapping degradaƟon products in close proximity to the crystal, and 
providing a reservoir for vacancy healing.

Stability of edges in MI2 
Edges play a criƟcal role in charge and spin transport for 2D systems45–47 yet engineering speciﬁc edge 
terminaƟons by etching48 or modiﬁed syntheƟc routes46,49 is highly challenging. Spin-polarized edge 
states have recently been observed in monolayer NiI2 islands grown on gold7, the harnessing of which 
could provide routes to new spintronics applicaƟons. To probe the stability of diﬀerent edge structures 
within our few layer iodides we have classiﬁed edges as either faceƩed (straight with a clear 
crystallographic terminaƟon) or curved (with no parƟcular terminaƟng structure). The results show 
that FeI2 has generally curved edges with less than 20% considered faceƩed (Fig.4a,c) while NiI2 is 30% 
faceƩed increasing to 40% aŌer extended imaging (Fig. 4c,d). FaceƩed edges are dominated by a zig-
zag type <011ത0> layer terminaƟon, corresponding to the stoichiometric case shown in Fig. 4e. This 
terminaƟon diﬀers from previous scanning tunnelling microscopy observaƟons7 where both Ni-rich 
and I-rich terminaƟons have been observed, with the diﬀerence likely due to interacƟon between the 
NiI2 and the gold substrate50.  
For high electron dose, the favourability of zig-zag edge facets for NiI2, results in the increasing 
presence of triangular pores with 60° inner angles (Fig 4b). This suggests that the faceƩed structure in 
NiI2 is signiﬁcantly more favourable energeƟcally since it involves an increased perimeter. The overall 
tendency of the crystal is to heal pore defects under electron beam irradiaƟon. DFT calculaƟons of the 
energy cost for the three high symmetry edge terminaƟons illustrated in Fig.4e within the 
experimentally relevant range of the iodine chemical potenƟal, 𝜇ூ, are shown in Fig.4f,g. These reveal 
that while the stoichiometric terminaƟon is lowest energy in NiI2 (consistent with experimental 
observaƟons), in FeI2 the stoichiometric terminaƟon competes with I-rich terminaƟon resulƟng in 
more curved edges at the atomic scale. We further consider the compeƟƟon between edges and the 
adatom defects of a metal atom atop an iodide site (M@Itop) and iodide on a metal side (I@M), which 
are the lowest-energy conﬁguraƟons among the three possible symmetric adsorpƟon sites and provide 
a conservaƟve reference energy for edge growth. Fig.4f,g show that for FeI2 these are always less 
energeƟcally favourable than the stoichiometric edge, while at low 𝜇ூ it can be more energeƟcally 
favourable to have a Ni@Itop adatom. This reinforces the high stability of the stoichiometric edge in NiI2 
when it is compeƟng with adatom defects.

Conclusion 
The structural characterisaƟon of transiƟon metal diiodides performed here has revealed a sensiƟvity 
to sample fabricaƟon condiƟons uncommon among more widely studied 2D materials. The small 
energy penalty for alternaƟve stacking conﬁguraƟons in the few layers case suggests modest strain or 
pressure during encapsulaƟon can result in alternaƟve polytypes to those observed in the bulk crystal, 
with signiﬁcant implicaƟons for the magneƟc properƟes at the thin limit, and the potenƟal to exploit 
this ﬂexibility to tune interlayer interacƟons and exploit sliding transiƟons. Defect dynamics are also 
signiﬁcantly diﬀerent to those reported for TMDs, hBN and graphene with no evidence of defect 
clustering for prolonged STEM imaging. DFT simulaƟons suggest this may be due to weak defect 
induced strain ﬁelds and unfavourable vacancy aggregaƟon energies, opening the possibility of using 
defect engineering to create disordered spin/magneƟc textures in thin di-iodides without the risk of 
pore formaƟon. 
More broadly, our results demonstrate that characterisaƟon of atomic scale phenomena in even the 
most air-sensiƟve 2D materials down to the monolayer limit is both possible and pracƟcal, if they can 
be hermeƟcally encapsulated with no polymeric or air deposited residue inside the inert envelope. 
Our polymer free fabricaƟon strategy based on silicon nitride canƟlevers can simply and rapidly create 
such structures in both high vacuum and inert glovebox environments with high-yield, opening the 
possibility of extending advanced atomic resoluƟon characterisaƟon of monolayer materials to even 
the most environmentally sensiƟve 2D crystals.
