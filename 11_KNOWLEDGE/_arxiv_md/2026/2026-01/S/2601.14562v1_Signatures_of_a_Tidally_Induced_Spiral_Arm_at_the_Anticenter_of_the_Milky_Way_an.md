ABSTRACT
Using the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument Milky Way Survey (DESI MWS), we examine the
6D space of the anticenter region of the stellar disk (150◦< Galactic longitude < 220◦) using 61,883
main sequence turn-off stars. We focus on two well-known stellar overdensities in the anticenter, the
Monoceros Ring (MRi) and Anticenter Stream (ACS). We find that the MRi overdensity has kinematics
consistent with a tidally induced spiral arm, a type of dynamic spiral arm created by an interaction with
a satellite galaxy, most likely the Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal galaxy (Sgr). We use the kinematics of
the MRi to calculate the two most recent passage times of Sgr are 0.25 ± 0.09 Gyrs and 1.10 ± 0.23
Gyrs from the present day. We validate that the ACS is kinematically decoupled from the MRi because
they are moving in opposite radial and vertical directions. We find that the kinematics associated with
the ACS are not confined to our defined overdensity. The features we see in the ACS region are likely
part of a broader distribution of stars with the same kinematic signature as detected in other places,
like the vertical wave in the outer disk and phase spiral.

1. INTRODUCTION
We have observational evidence that galaxies grow and
evolve through major and minor mergers.
This is an
important test of our cosmological theory of hierarchi-
cal structure formation (Dressler 1980; Barnes & Hern-
quist 1992; Baugh 2006; Benson 2010; Moster et al. 2010;
Madau & Dickinson 2014).
The internal structure of
galaxies can be disrupted by these interactions and cre-
ate dynamic components in disks, like spiral arms and
bars.
These components, in turn, influence the kine-
matics of the stars in the galaxy (Lynden-Bell & Kalnajs
1972; Sellwood & Binney 2002; Erwin 2018; Zhang et al.
2025). The Milky Way (MW) is one example of a “typ-
ical” late-type galaxy with spiral arms, a central bar,
and evidence of hierarchical buildup in its stellar halo
from merging satellite galaxies like the already accreted
Gaia-Sausage-Enceladus (GSE), and the current accre-
tion of Sagittarius Dwarf Spheroidal galaxy (Sgr), and
the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) (Ibata et al. 1994;
Besla et al. 2007; Belokurov et al. 2018; Helmi et al.
2018; Naidu et al. 2020; Joshi & Malhotra 2023; Dea-
son & Belokurov 2024). Stellar observations of the MW
provide an accessible laboratory to explore galaxy inter-
actions and accretion history as the kinematics of stars
in a galaxy contain a record of their past, which allows
us to understand how galaxies evolve over time.
The LMC and Sgr are currently the largest and most
gravitationally significant galaxies interacting with the
MW (Laporte et al. 2018a).
The LMC is on its first
infall (Besla et al. 2007; Kallivayalil et al. 2013; Luc-
chini et al. 2025, see also Vasiliev 2024 for an argument
for the LMC being on its second infall) Sgr has three
proposed passages through the disk over the past eight
billion years (Law & Majewski 2010; de la Vega et al.
2015; Dierickx & Loeb 2017; Laporte et al. 2019b; Ruiz-
Lara et al. 2020), and there is a tidal extension of stars

belonging to Sgr forming a stellar stream (Mateo et al.
1996; Ramos et al. 2020). We can understand how the
Galaxy is gravitationally influenced by satellites through
observables such as the gravitational wake of the LMC
(Garavito-Camargo et al. 2019; Conroy et al. 2021; Li
et al. 2025a), and vertical perturbations in the disk (La-

elements such as the orbital history of Sgr, the progeni-
tor’s initial mass, and mass loss rate are intertwined such
that it is difficult to precisely constrain one of these pa-
rameters without knowing the others. Using kinematics
to deduce the pericenter passages of Sgr will advance
future measurements of the mass of Sgr (Laporte et al.
2018b).
Spiral arms have many formation mechanisms, and
spiral arms that manifest from gravitational instabili-
ties from external sources, like satellite galaxies, are im-
portant to our understanding of disk structure evolution
(Romero-G´omez et al. 2007; Binney & Tremaine 2008;
Sellwood 2021). Previous work has shown that satel-
lite galaxy interactions can be directly linked to spiral
arm formation (Quinn & Goodman 1986; Dobbs et al.
2010; Purcell et al. 2011; G´omez et al. 2016; Kawata
et al. 2018; Khoperskov & Gerhard 2022; Carr et al.
2022; Quinn et al. 2025).
Due to our perspective in
the Galaxy, we do not yet have a complete understand-
ing of the MW’s spiral structure, including the extent
to which spiral arms appear in the outer disk (Laporte
et al. 2018b; Laporte et al. 2022; Hunt & Vasiliev 2025).
Here we define the outer disk as galactocentric radii > 10
kpc (McMillan et al. 2022). There may be many differ-
ent processes at play to create the spiral structure of the
Galaxy, and therefore, it is important to identify which
spiral arms exhibit the observational signatures of dif-
ferent types of spiral structure. Spiral arms induced by
tidal forces from satellite galaxies are increasingly in-
triguing because they may also inform us about the ac-
cretion history of the Galaxy. They exhibit distinct kine-
matic features that can be used to investigate the initial
pericenter passage time of the interaction (Kalnajs 1973;
Antoja et al. 2022; Stelea et al. 2024), and these features
are indicative of recent disk perturbations.
We currently observe the Galactic disk to be kinemat-
ically out of equilibrium, and this disequilibrium mani-
fests as perturbations and asymmetries in the disk (e.g.
Yanny & Gardner 2013; McMillan et al. 2022). Obser-
vational evidence from the Gaia space mission (Gaia
Collaboration et al. 2016) of these disk perturbations
is seen through the kinematics of the stars in multiple
phase spaces, like in the “phase space spiral” in vertical
velocity and vertical height space (Antoja et al. 2018b;
Bernet et al. 2022) and the diagonal ridges in Galac-
tocentric radius and azimuthal velocity space (Antoja
et al. 2018b; Ramos et al. 2018; Antoja et al. 2023).
Additionally, comparing observations to theoretical
models of Galactic disk evolution may help us link the
physics causing the disequilibrium to our observations.
Recent studies from Fragkoudi et al. (2019), Laporte
et al. (2019b), and Bland-Hawthorn & Tepper-Garc´ıa
(2021) show that N-body simulations can reproduce ob-
served perturbations that we find in the MW disk with
different internal or external mechanisms. Two of the
most dominant mechanisms driving Galactic disk per-
turbations are from internal and external sources. An
example of internal sources is resonance orbits from the
influence of the bar which mainly influence the inner
disk out to the solar neighborhood (Binney 1981; Pfen-
niger 1984; Monari et al. 2019; Fragkoudi et al. 2019;
Bernet et al. 2022). An example of external sources is
satellite galaxy interactions shaping the outer regions of
the disk (Laporte et al. 2018b; Eilers et al. 2020; Antoja
et al. 2022; Khoperskov & Gerhard 2022).
Along with the phase spiral and ridge patterns as evi-
dence of disequilibrium, we have observed multiple over-
dense stellar regions in the MW outer disk. The Mono-
ceros Ring (MRi) was first discovered as a stellar over-
density by Newberg et al. (2002) using the Sloan Digital
Sky Survey (SDSS; York et al. 2000). The MRi region
is located at heliocentric distances 7 < d < 15 kpc, and
Galactic coordinates ranging from 120◦< l < 260◦, and
−30◦< b < 40◦(Morganson et al. 2016; Sheffield et al.
2018).
The MRi was initially hypothesized to be ac-
creted material of the remnants of a tidally disrupted
dwarf galaxy (Newberg et al. 2002; Rocha-Pinto et al.
2003a; Martin et al. 2004; Michel-Dansac et al. 2011a;
Sollima et al. 2011).
More recent analyses demon-
strate that MRi’s chemical composition, age distribu-
tion, and azimuthal velocity are constant with the MW
disk (G´omez et al. 2016; Laporte et al. 2020; Borbolato
et al. 2024). Instead of being made of accreted material,
it is now well accepted that the MRi is made up of a
population of MW stars. The MRi may be MW disk
stars forming this overdense region as a result of the
gravitational influence of the infall of a satellite galaxy
(Laporte et al. 2018b; G´omez et al. 2016).
The Anticenter Stream (ACS) is an overdense stellar
region in the anticenter of the MW and was discovered
by Grillmair (2006). Initially thought to be part of the
MRi, we now know they are spatially separate in the sky
(Slater et al. 2014; Morganson et al. 2016), and the two
overdensities are moving in opposite radial and vertical
directions (Qiao et al. 2024).
Since they lie in close
proximity to each other, there have been debates on the
plausibility of a physical connection between the MRi

Grillmair et al. 2008; Kazantzidis et al. 2008; Michel-
Dansac et al. 2011b; Ramos et al. 2021; Laporte et al.

Understanding the complete picture of these pertur-
bations relies on the use of large stellar surveys like Gaia
(Gaia Collaboration et al. 2016), Apache Point Obser-
vatory Galactic Evolution Experiment (APOGEE; Ma-

(GALAH; De Silva et al. 2015), and Large Sky Area
Multi-Object Fiber Spectroscopic Telescope (LAMOST
Cui et al. 2012), and now, the Dark Energy Spectro-
scopic Instrument (DESI) Milky Way Survey (MWS)
(DESI Collaboration et al. 2016a; Cooper et al. 2023).
These surveys provide insight into the motion and com-
position of stellar populations in the MW, which one
can use to uncover details of the kinematics of the disk.
In this paper, we examine the MRi and ACS regions
using data from the first three years of DESI MWS.
We show that the MRi overdensity possesses the same
kinematic features as compared to models of tidally in-
duced spiral arms in the MW. Using the kinematics of
the MRi overdensity, we can calculate the most recent
pericenter passages of Sgr. However, the kinematics as-
sociated with the ACS region are different from the MRi
region. We speculate that the ACS overdensity, instead
of being a distinct stream of disk stars, could be part of a
more broadly distributed population of stars that share
the same kinematic signature which has been detected
in other places, such as the phase spiral (Antoja et al.
2018b; Antoja et al. 2023). In Section 2, we describe the
DESI MWS, define the anticenter region where we focus
our investigation, and outline the main sequence turn-
off star (MSTO) sample we use. In Section 3, we show
the distinct motions of the MRi and ACS, indicating
they are distinct kinematic groups. We compare our ob-
servations to previously studied N-body simulations of
tidally induced spiral structures and calculate the most
recent pericenter passage times of Sgr. We discuss our
results in the context of previous work in Section 4 and
summarize our conclusions in Section 5.
2. DATA AND SAMPLE SELECTION
2.1. DESI Milky Way Survey
The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) is
a multi-object, robotic, fiber-fed spectrograph operating
on the Mayall 4-meter telescope at Kitt Peak National
Observatory in Tucson, Arizona (DESI Collaboration
et al. 2022). It has 5,000 fibers and covers a wavelength
range of 360 nm to 980 nm. It can simultaneously ob-
tain spectra for nearly 5,000 objects over a ∼3◦field of
view (DESI Collaboration et al. 2016b,c; Silber et al.
2023; Guy et al. 2023; Schlafly et al. 2023; Miller et al.
2024; Poppett et al. 2024). The resolving power of the
spectra varies between 2,000 at the blue end and 5,500
at the red end. The DESI survey, started in May 2021,
aims to obtain spectra from at least ∼63 million galaxies
and quasi-stellar objects (QSOs) and ∼10 million stars
in the “MAIN-BRIGHT” program with a footprint of
17,000 square degrees in eight years, making it one of
the largest spectroscopic surveys to date (DESI Collab-
oration et al. 2016a; Cooper et al. 2023). The first data
release (DR1, DESI Collaboration et al. 2025) is now
public and includes spectra for more than 18 million
unique targets. Adame et al. (2025) and Abdul Karim
et al. (2025) show some examples of early cosmological
results from DESI.
During dark sky conditions, DESI observations are de-
signed to explore the physics governing the early uni-
verse via obtaining the most precise measurement of the
expansion history of the universe to date using baryon
acoustic oscillations (Levi et al. 2013). The sky condi-
tions to observe most of the high-redshift galactic targets
must be dark and have good transparency. When ob-
serving efficiency across the DESI wavelength range is
lower due to the phase of the moon, twilight, or poor
seeing conditions, the survey switches to the MAIN-
BRIGHT program, targeting stars in the Milky Way
Survey (MWS; Allende Prieto et al. 2020; Cooper et al.
2023) and the Bright Galaxy Survey targets (BGS; Hahn
et al. 2023). The MWS is designed to investigate the lo-
cal universe to better understand the accretion history of
the MW and probe dark matter on small scales. This pa-
per uses stellar spectra from the MWS MAIN-BRIGHT
program. These targets were chosen based on Legacy
Imaging Surveys (Dey et al. 2019), and the MWS target
selection is described in Cooper et al. (2023). The goal
of the MWS is to measure radial velocities and chemi-
cal abundances for high galactic latitude stars that are
fainter than the Radial Velocity Spectrometer (RVS)
targets in Gaia. For our analysis, this gives us a new
large spectroscopic sample in the distant Galactic anti-
center.
In this paper, we use MWS spectra from the first
three years of DESI observations, which will be released
as Data Release 2 (DR2) to the public in Spring 2027
(details in https://data.desi.lbl.gov/doc/releases/). The
data points corresponding to the figures from this paper
are available from http://zenodo.org/records/18236902.
The data reduction follows the DR1 stellar catalog de-
scribed in Koposov et al. (2025). The radial velocities
are measured with the stellar parameter fitting code
RVSpecFit (Koposov 2019). The details of the pipeline
are described in Koposov et al. (2024).
The param-

2.2. Main Sequence Turn-Off Star Selection
Because we want a high density of stars in the outer
disk and avoid unnecessary foreground stars, we choose
a sample of MSTO stars. We start with the “MAIN-
BLUE” MWS sample, which is a subsample of the
MAIN-BRIGHT program and selected in an r magni-
tude range of 16 < r < 19 mag and g −r < 0.7 (Cooper
et al. 2023).
Note that all magnitudes discussed are
DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys magnitude bands and are
extinction corrected using dust maps from Schlegel et al.
(1998), and this selection does not apply any additional
parallax or proper motion cut. We require the objects
to be classified as “STAR” by Redrock spectral clas-
sification (Bailey et al., in prep, Anand et al. 2024),
no warning flags for the radial velocity measurement
(RVS WARN == 0), and a signal-to-noise (SNR) in the
r-band greater than 5.
This leaves us with 2,989,951
stars.

panel of Figure 4). We verify that we recover the on-

Because dust extinction reduces the original number of

4. DISCUSSION
Here we discuss the results from Section 3 in context
with other recent literature. In Section 4.1, we comment
on well-known spiral arms in the MW compared to the
outer disk overdensities, other simulations that can pro-
duce outer disk overdensities from interactions between
the MW and Sgr, and the effect of the LMC on the outer
disk. In Section 4.2, we discuss other explanations for
ridges in R–Vϕ space including the influence of resonant
orbits from the bar on ridges out to R ∼12.4 kpc. In
Section 4.3, we go in depth on the Sgr pericenter pas-
sage timing comparison with the literature we compare
to in Table 1. In Section 4.4, we discuss previous in-
terpretations of the ACS overdensity and simulations of
low Galactic latitude streams of disk stars from Laporte
et al. (2019a). We also comment on other overdensities
found in the anticenter region and their possible connec-
tion to the ACS and MRi.
4.1. Tidally Induced Spiral Arms in the Context of
Disk Substructure
The two leading theories of the mechanisms driving
spiral arm formation in galaxies are the quasi-stationary
density wave model (Lin & Shu 1964) and the dynamic
arm scenario, caused by gravitational instabilities or in-
teractions with external perturbers (Chapter 6 of Binney
& Tremaine 2008; Carlberg & Sellwood 1985). Recent
studies show that dynamic spiral arms are a plausible
explanation for spiral structure near the solar neighbor-
hood, like the Orion spur and the Perseus arm, using
observations such as the lack of age gradients within the
arms (Liu et al. 2025). Studies also find that for dynamic
spiral arms, the sign of VR flips around the center of the
arm; when the velocities are toward the arm, it’s grow-
ing, and when the velocities are away from the arm, it’s
dissipating (Funakoshi et al. 2024; Grand et al. 2015).
According to the simulations, these dynamic spiral arms
grow from a small density perturbation and then disrupt
Grand et al. (2015). In the case of the Perseus arm, the
observed stars on the inside edge of the arm (smaller
guiding center radius) have VR pointing inward, whereas
the observed stars on the outer edge of the arm have VR
pointing outward (Funakoshi et al. 2024). So they inter-
pret this as the Perseus arm disrupting (Funakoshi et al.
2024). It is also important to note that we are not sug-
gesting the MRi is part of the Norma-Outer arm, which
is located at 10 < R < 15 kpc (Reid et al. 2019). The
MRi is an overdensity that resides beyond 15 kpc and is
mostly made up of older, more metal-poor stars, whereas
the well-known arms are gas-rich and star-forming.
Previous N-body simulations of the interactions be-
tween the MW and Sgr have been able to create MRi-like
overdensities with similar kinematics (minima in VR)
and hypothesize the MRi to be an extended spiral arm
(Purcell et al. 2011; G´omez et al. 2016). Many stud-
ies have found the −VR signature associated with the
MRi (Laporte et al. 2020; Qiao et al. 2024; Borbolato
et al. 2024). Others have found the Vϕ motion of the
MRi overdensity to be similar to that of the thin disk
(Deason et al. 2018; Borbolato et al. 2024). However,
we know of no other observational study connecting the
−VR motion and inflection in Vϕ−< Vϕ(R)> of the MRi
to a co-rotating spiral arm pattern.
Another important point is the longevity of these
types of disk structures. Struck et al. (2011) and La-
porte et al. (2018b) have demonstrated, via N-body sim-
ulations, that the spiral arms and feathers in the disk
created by satellite interactions could last for a few Gyrs,
meaning it is plausible that we observe a tidally induced
spiral arm a few Gyrs after its formation.
We can rule out the warp or flare of the disk as the
dominant factor in creating the overdensity seen in Fig-
ure 6. According to studies like Li et al. (2019), Xu et al.
(2020), and Han et al. (2023), the warp reaches ∼1.5
kpc at R = 20 kpc. However, geometrically, the warp is
not at the correct longitude to create an apparent over-
density at the location of the MRi. The line-of-node is
approximately through the Galactic center-solar neigh-
borhood and continues through to the Galactic anticen-
ter (Momany et al. 2006). The flare of the disk reaches
a scale height of Z ∼2.5 by R ∼20 kpc (Thomas et al.
2019; Li et al. 2019; Chrob´akov´a et al. 2022; Uppal et al.
2024).
The flare demonstrates how disk stars can be
found at these heights, but neither the warp nor the
flare alone can explain the kinematic signatures we see.
Several previous papers are in agreement that the MRi
overdensity is likely the result of the gravitational influ-
ence of a recent satellite interaction. G´omez et al. (2016)
uses cosmological simulations to show, based on the ver-
tical height of the MRi, that the origin may be due to a

4.3. Sgr Pericenter Passage Timing Comparison
Our pericenter passage time analysis reveals the two
most recent passage times of Sgr, 0.25 ± 0.09 Gyrs and
1.10 ± 0.23 Gyrs from present. Table 1 compares our
results to recent estimates of the Sgr pericenter passage
times. Our results are in good agreement with previous
timing estimates.
Law & Majewski (2010) uses an N-body model to sim-
ulate the disruption of Sgr in a triaxial MW halo over
its orbital history, but they do not include dynamical
friction in their analysis. They find that the two most
recent closest approaches of Sgr were ∼0 and 1.3 Gyrs
ago. de la Vega et al. (2015) uses an orbit integration
model from Chakrabarti et al. (2014) of Sgr, assuming
an initial mass of Sgr of 1010 M⊙and include the ef-
fects of dynamical friction. They state the most recent
pericenter was 1.1 Gyrs ago, but their orbit shows the
current position (at time ∼0 Gyrs) of Sgr is near its
pericenter (see Figure 2; de la Vega et al. 2015).
N-
body modeling of the Sgr stream from Dierickx & Loeb
(2017) shows that the period of pericenter passages for
the two most recent complete passages is 0 Gyrs and 1.3
Gyrs from present, and it is in good agreement with the
dynamic models from Fardal et al. (2019), which aim to
reproduce the Sgr stream features in a simple Galactic
potential. We find that using different methods to cal-
culate the pericenter passage timing of Sgr are in good
agreement with one another.
Michel-Dansac et al. (2011a) propose the MRi to be
accreted material, which is why we do not include it in
Table 1. However, they find that idealized simulations
of Sgr show it crossed the disk three distinct times near
the current location of the MRi at 0.6, 1.1, and 1.7 Gyrs
ago. Additionally, Lin et al. (2025) find a two-armed
spiral feature in Z–VZ space and estimate perturbation
times of ∼0.32 and ∼0.5 Gyrs from present. The two-
armed spiral suggests that the perturbations originate
from two different satellite galaxies.
4.4. The Anticenter Stream as a Feature of a Vertical
Wave
It is clear from our analysis of the MRi region and ACS
region that the VR and VZ kinematics of the MRi over-
density are opposite of the ACS overdensity. Similarly,
Laporte et al. (2020) finds that the ACS is kinemati-
cally decoupled from the MRi. We also find that the
kinematics associated with the ACS overdensity extend
to lower Galactic latitudes which demonstrates that the
ACS overdensity does not have discrete kinematics that
are separate from the disk.
Laporte et al. (2019a) suggests that overdensities like
the ACS are tidal tail “feathers” kicked above the plane
of the disk by an external perturber such as Sgr. Laporte
et al. (2019a) simulates a MW-like galaxy interacting
with a Sgr-like satellite and finds stream-like overdensi-
ties in the outer disk, similar to the ACS. They report
on the kinematic features of the feathers in observational
l, b space. From their simulations (Section 3.1 Laporte
et al. 2018b), they detect feathers at the extremities of
MRi-like overdensities and find these feathers ubiquitous
in their simulation. They find that the shape of these
feathers, to an observer, appears as an arc with some
vertical peak and, at its edges, smoothly reconnecting
to the disk. They also find that the vertical perturba-

tions are excited by interactions with Sgr. Figure 9 in
Laporte et al. (2019a) finds that feathers spatially re-
sembling the ACS exhibit coherent motion in Z and R,
with a leading and trailing tail that have opposite kine-
matics. Our ACS analysis is constrained to the Galac-
tic longitude of our MSTO anticenter sample, whereas
the tidal tail feather model from Laporte et al. (2019a)
looks at a 360◦view of their model. Because we only
see a limited range of the ACS overdensity, we cannot
confirm the full kinematic predictions made by Laporte
et al. (2019a).
We also cannot confirm whether tidal
tail feathers exhibit kinematics that extend to lower lat-

In Laporte et al. (2022), they acknowledge that an-
other interpretation of these observed stream-like over-
densities in the anticenter of the disk could be vertical
folds in the outer disk (Price-Whelan et al. 2015; Xu
et al. 2015; Li et al. 2017a; Sheffield et al. 2018; Cao
et al. 2024).
Because our results find the kinematics
associated with the ACS overdensity extend to lower
Galactic latitudes, we argue that the ACS, rather than
a discrete stream-like object, is more likely to be con-
nected to the rest of the disk and is a manifestation of
the vertical folds at high Galactic latitudes (b ∼30◦).
The ACS overdensity could be another signature of a
disk in disequilibrium, in the middle of phase mixing.
The vertical folds in the outer disk, including the ACS,
could be further evidence for the same kind of verti-
cal disturbance that causes the Z–VZ phase spiral seen
in the solar neighborhood (Antoja et al. 2018b; Antoja
et al. 2022; Hunt et al. 2024). However, that investiga-
tion is outside of the scope of this paper.
Another possible area of exploration to identify what
the ACS and MRi could be is to investigate their con-
nection to the Triangulum-Andromeda (TriAnd; Rocha-
Pinto et al. 2003b) and/or the Eastern Banded Structure
(EBS; Grillmair 2006) overdensities. Both TriAnd and
EBS are also thought to have formed in situ and exhibit
characteristics of MW disk stars (Bergemann et al. 2018;
Abuchaim et al. 2023). However, the TriAnd overden-
sity is located at −40◦< b < −20◦and 100◦< l < 150◦
(Rocha-Pinto et al. 2003b) and therefore outside of our
sample, so we cannot confirm its possible connection
here. The EBS is also primarily outside of the region we
analyze, located at 217◦< l < 260◦(Morganson et al.
2016; Deason et al. 2018). Deason et al. (2018) argues
that the EBS is part of the MRi and marks the edge of
the overdensity. These substructures, MRi, ACS, and
TriAnd, can also be connected to the vertical wave in
the stellar disk (Xu et al. 2015; Li et al. 2017a; Sheffield
et al. 2018).
5. SUMMARY
In this paper, using MSTO stars in the anticenter re-
gion of the MW disk from the first three years of DESI
observations, we find the following:
1.
The MRi and ACS are separate kinematic disk
structures, moving in opposite directions. The MRi has
negative radial velocities and negative vertical velocities,
while the ACS has positive radial and vertical velocities.
2. The kinematics in the region of the MRi are con-
sistent with those of a tidally induced spiral arm.
A
tidally induced spiral arm has negative radial velocities
and Vϕ−< Vϕ(R)> close to zero at the location of the
spiral arm (Antoja et al. 2022).
3. We find an outer ridge made of stars with −VR
values in R–Vϕ space in the region we associate with the
MRi. The ridge is most prominently seen in −VR and
−VZ, and corresponds to the median Vϕ per R in a range
of 14 < R < 18 kpc. This is indicative of corotating
motion like that of a tidally induced spiral arm.
4. We perform a Fourier analysis in the region asso-
ciated with the MRi to determine the two most recent
pericenter passages of the Sgr.
We find the passages
happened 0.25 ± 0.09 and 1.10 ± 0.23 Gyr from present
day. When compared to other estimations of the peri-
center passages (Law & Majewski 2010; de la Vega et al.
2015; Dierickx & Loeb 2017; Laporte et al. 2019b; Ruiz-
Lara et al. 2020; Antoja et al. 2022), we are consistent
with those values which use a range of different methods
to calculate the timing.
5. The kinematics in the region of the ACS are not
confined to the overdensity and extend to lower lati-
tudes, connecting with the disk kinematics. The ACS
overdensity is further evidence of vertical disequilibrium
in the outer disk and could be related to folds of the disk
in the anticenter (e.g. Xu et al. 2015; Li et al. 2017b;
Sheffield et al. 2018).
