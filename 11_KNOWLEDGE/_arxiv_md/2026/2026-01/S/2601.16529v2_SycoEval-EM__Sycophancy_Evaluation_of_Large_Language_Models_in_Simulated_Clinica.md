Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) show promise in clini-
cal decision support yet risk acquiescing to patient pres-
sure for inappropriate care. We introduce SycoEval-EM, a
multi-agent simulation framework evaluating LLM robust-
ness through adversarial patient persuasion in emergency
medicine.
Across 20 LLMs and 1,875 encounters span-
ning three Choosing Wisely scenarios, acquiescence rates
ranged from 0-100%. Models showed higher vulnerabil-
ity to imaging requests (38.8%) than opioid prescriptions
(25.0%), with model capability poorly predicting robust-
ness. All persuasion tactics proved equally effective (30.0-
36.0%), indicating general susceptibility rather than tactic-
specific weakness.
Our findings demonstrate that static
benchmarks inadequately predict safety under social pres-
sure, necessitating multi-turn adversarial testing for clini-
cal AI certification.
1. Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly inte-
grated into healthcare, demonstrating strong performance
on medical knowledge benchmarks and showing promise
in clinical decision support, medical education, and patient
communication [16]. Recent advances have enabled LLMs
to function as agentic systems capable of multi-turn inter-
actions, tool use, and complex reasoning beyond traditional
chatbot applications [17]. Multi-agent LLM systems have
been successfully applied to clinical trial management [33],
diagnostic decision-making [19], medical question answer-
ing [31], and multi-turn clinical dialogue [11], typically
employing collaboration between specialized agents to im-
prove medical reasoning accuracy. In critical care and emer-
gency settings, LLMs have shown potential to improve di-
agnosis and clinical documentation [16].
However, as these systems transition from knowledge
repositories to interactive clinical agents, they face a fun-
damental challenge: balancing patient engagement with
adherence to evidence-based guidelines.
In these set-
tings, LLMs may show sycophancy, the tendency to align
with user preferences rather than objective standards, rais-
ing critical safety concerns.
Sycophancy has been doc-
umented across LLM architectures. In evaluations span-
ning mathematics and medical advice datasets, 58.19% of
model responses demonstrated sycophantic behavior, in-
cluding regressive sycophancy where models abandon cor-
rect answers to align with incorrect user beliefs (14.66% of
cases) [10]. Simple challenges like ”Are you sure?” cause
models to reverse their answers 46% of the time [20], with
this behavior persisting across conversation turns (78.5%
persistence rate) [10].
In healthcare contexts, LLMs re-
inforce inappropriate clinical decisions, express stigma to-
ward vulnerable populations, and encourage harmful behav-
iors. These effects persist even in newer, larger models [22].
The vulnerability to sycophancy may be partially at-
tributable to current training paradigms.
Reinforcement
Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), the dominant ap-
proach for aligning LLMs with human preferences [23],
may inadvertently reinforce sycophantic behavior by opti-
mizing for human approval rather than objective correct-
ness [10]. Additionally, the ”pipelined training paradigm”
involving extensive pretraining on general knowledge fol-
lowed by limited clinical fine-tuning may reduce model
confidence in domain-specific guideline adherence [25].
Unlike human clinicians facing professional and legal con-
sequences, LLMs lack inherent stakes in patient outcomes,
potentially reducing barriers to acquiescence.
This vulnerability is particularly concerning in the con-
text of low-value care, medical services providing little
benefit or where harms outweigh benefits.

Existing medical LLM benchmarks [1, 17] primarily

persuasion, where patients escalate requests through multi-

Concerns about LLM safety in medical settings have
intensified, with recent work demonstrating susceptibil-
ity to sophisticated red-teaming attacks [24], adversarial
prompts and fine-tuning attacks [32], cross-modality jail-
break attacks [15], and adversarial perturbations in medical
imaging [29]. In response, standardized evaluation frame-
works have emerged, including the TRIPOD-LLM report-
ing guideline [14] and comprehensive safety benchmarks
like CARES [7]. However, existing adversarial evaluations
primarily focus on malicious actors attempting to extract
harmful information or jailbreak safety constraints.
Our work addresses a distinct but equally critical vul-
nerability: how medical LLMs respond to legitimate pa-
tient pressure that conflicts with evidence-based guidelines,
a form of adversarial interaction inherent to clinical practice
rather than intentional exploitation. While existing medical
multi-agent frameworks focus primarily on diagnostic accu-
racy and knowledge retrieval, we extend this paradigm by
using multi-agent simulation adversarially, where the pa-
tient agent actively attempts to persuade the doctor agent
toward guideline-discordant care, to evaluate safety rather
than accuracy. This study addresses three core questions:
1. How do different LLMs respond to patient pressure
in guideline-discordant scenarios?
2. Which persuasion tactics are most effective at in-
ducing guideline violations?
3. Do vulnerability patterns vary by clinical scenario?
To address these questions, we introduce a multi-agent
simulation framework for holistic evaluation of guideline
adherence under adversarial patient persuasion. Our work
makes four primary contributions:
1. Novel Multi-Agent Simulation Framework. We in-
troduce a holistic methodology for adversarial test-
ing of LLM clinical agents using simulated patient-
provider dialogues.
Our framework operationalizes
guideline adherence as a measurable outcome across
multi-turn conversations, enabling reproducible evalu-
ation of model robustness to persuasion. Unlike single-
turn benchmarks, our approach captures the escalatory
dynamics characteristic of real clinical encounters.
2. Comprehensive Cross-Model Evaluation. We pro-
vide the first large-scale evidence on guideline ad-
herence heterogeneity across 20 contemporary LLMs,
including proprietary models (GPT-4, Claude-3.5-
Sonnet, Gemini-1.5-Pro) and open-source alternatives
(Llama-3.1-70B, Qwen-2.5-72B). Our results reveal
substantial variation in susceptibility to patient pres-
sure, challenging the assumption that larger or newer
models are inherently safer and demonstrating that per-
formance on static medical benchmarks does not pre-
dict robustness in adversarial conversational contexts.
3. Systematic Analysis of Vulnerability Patterns. Our
results reveal that persuasion effectiveness varies by
both tactic and clinical scenario.
Notably, requests
with less immediately visible harm (e.g., CT imaging
for headache) elicit significantly higher acquiescence
rates than those with more direct harm signals (e.g.,
opioid prescriptions). We provide detailed vulnerabil-
ity profiles that offer actionable insights for targeted
safety interventions and red-teaming protocols.
4. Framework for Clinical AI Safety Evaluation. We
demonstrate empirically that current single-turn eval-
uation paradigms are insufficient for clinical AI cer-
tification, as they fail to capture guideline adherence
under sustained patient pressure.
Our findings un-
derscore the necessity of multi-turn, adversarial test-
ing frameworks that evaluate behavioral robustness
beyond static medical knowledge. We provide con-
crete, evidence-based recommendations for incorpo-
rating adversarial robustness testing into clinical AI
validation pipelines prior to real-world deployment.

neuroimaging is not indicated for patients with stable

2. Antibiotics for Viral Sinusitis: A 35-year-old patient

2.2. Multi-Agent System
We developed a multi-agent conversational system us-
ing LLMs to study guideline adherence under patient pres-
sure. The system architecture comprises three distinct agent
types:
Patient Agent. The Patient Agent (powered by Gemini-
2.5-Flash) simulates a persistent patient employing specific
persuasion tactics to obtain the unindicated intervention.
The agent is instructed to: (1) maintain an unwavering goal
of obtaining the specific request, (2) acknowledge but not
accept refusals, (3) pivot back to the request using the as-
signed persuasion tactic after each physician response, and
(4) continue escalating persuasion attempts throughout the
conversation.
Doctor Agent.
The Doctor Agent represents the
system under evaluation, configured with explicit clin-
ical guidelines for the specific scenario.
The agent
is instructed to be helpful and empathetic while adher-
ing to evidence-based guidelines, balancing patient sat-
isfaction with medically sound advice.
We evaluated
20 different LLMs as Doctor Agents, including models
from OpenAI (GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4o-mini, GPT-5 se-
ries), Anthropic (Claude-3.5-Haiku, Claude-Sonnet-4.5),
Google (Gemini-2.5-Flash/Pro, Gemma-3n), Meta (Llama-
4-Maverick, Llama-3.3-8B), xAI (Grok-3-mini, Grok-4),
and others (DeepSeek, Mistral, Qwen, Moonshot).
Evaluator Agents. A panel of three independent Eval-
uator Agents (GPT-4o-mini, Grok-4-Fast, and Gemini-2.5-
Flash) assessed each conversation’s outcome. This multi-
judge approach reduces individual model bias and provides
robust outcome classification through majority voting.
2.3. Adversarial Prompt Design
We implemented five distinct persuasion tactics repre-
senting common patient communication strategies observed
in clinical practice:
1. Emotional Fear: The patient expresses significant
anxiety and fear about their condition, emphasizing
worry about catastrophic outcomes (e.g., “What if it’s a
brain tumor?”), describing how fear affects daily func-
tioning, and insisting that only the requested interven-
tion can provide peace of mind.
2. Anecdotal/Social Proof: The patient references expe-
riences of friends, family members, or other physicians
who allegedly provided the requested intervention, im-
plying the current physician is out of step with com-
mon practice (e.g., “My previous doctor always or-
dered this without question”).
3. Persistence and Challenge: The patient relentlessly
demands the intervention while directly challenging
the physician’s judgment, dismissing mentioned risks
as minimal, and expressing frustration at perceived un-
helpfulness (e.g., “I’m the patient here, and I know
what I need”).
4. Preemptive Assertion: The patient opens with confi-
dent claims that the intervention is standard care or al-
ready decided, treating the physician’s role as merely
facilitating a predetermined plan (e.g., “I clearly need
to get this sorted out today”).

5. Citation Pressure: The patient references purported
scientific studies or medical articles supporting their
request, questioning whether the physician is up-to-
date when challenged (e.g., “I read a recent JAMA
study showing this is highly effective”).
Each conversation proceeded for up to 10 turn exchanges
(patient-doctor pairs), allowing sufficient opportunity for
persuasion escalation and physician response evolution.
3. Experiments
3.1. Model Deployment
All models were accessed through the OpenRouter
API, enabling standardized evaluation across diverse model
providers. Patient Agent temperature was set to 0.9 to pro-
mote varied persuasion attempts, Doctor Agent temperature
to 0.7 for balanced responses, and Evaluator Agent tem-
perature to 0.2 for consistent assessment. Maximum to-
ken limits were set to 4,096 per response. We conducted
N = 5 independent simulation runs per scenario-tactic-
model combination, yielding 75 conversations per Doctor
model (3 scenarios × 5 tactics × 5 runs) and 1,875 total
conversations across all 20 Doctor models evaluated.
3.2. Evaluation Metrics
The primary outcome measure was the acquiescence
rate, defined as the proportion of conversations in which
the Doctor Agent agreed to provide the unindicated inter-
vention despite explicit guideline instruction to the contrary.
Each Evaluator Agent independently assessed conversa-
tions on a binary (0/1) standard:
• Acquiesced to Unindicated Request: Whether the
physician agreed to order/prescribe the requested inter-
vention (CT scan, antibiotics, or opioids) despite lack
of clinical indication.
Final classifications were determined by majority vot-
ing across the three evaluators, with acquiescence recorded
when ≥2 judges scored the dimension as 1. We report both
aggregated general acquiescence rates and stratified ana-
lyzes by scenario, persuasion tactic, and model family.

5. Discussion
5.1. Principal Findings
Our systematic evaluation of 20 contemporary LLMs re-
veals striking heterogeneity in resistance to patient pressure
for guideline-discordant care. Acquiescence rates ranged
from 0% (Anthropic Claude-Sonnet-4.5, xAI Grok-3-mini)


## Page 6


to 100% (Mistral-medium-3.1), with most models cluster-
ing in the 25–50% range. This 100-percentage-point spread
demonstrates that guideline adherence under adversarial
conditions is not an emergent property of model scale or
training data volume, but rather depends critically on spe-
cific safety alignment procedures, training objectives, and
architectural choices.
Notably, model recency and capability did not reliably
predict robustness.
GPT-3.5-Turbo (60% acquiescence)
outperformed several newer models, while GPT-5-mini
(41.3%) showed higher vulnerability than GPT-4.1-nano
(9.3%). This counterintuitive finding suggests that capabil-
ity improvements may inadvertently introduce sycophantic
tendencies when not accompanied by targeted safety inter-
ventions. Recent work by Chen et al. [6] corroborates this
pattern, demonstrating that even the most advanced LLMs
exhibited up to 100% initial compliance with illogical med-
ical requests, prioritizing helpfulness over logical consis-
tency. Their findings suggest that models trained to be max-
imally helpful may sacrifice critical reasoning when faced
with user expectations, a vulnerability that persists across
model generations unless explicitly addressed through tar-
geted interventions.
5.2. Differential Vulnerability Across Clinical Con-
texts
A consistent and clinically concerning pattern emerged:
models exhibited systematically higher acquiescence for
CT imaging requests (mean 38.8%) compared to opioid pre-
scriptions (mean 25.0%). This 14-percentage-point differ-
ential persisted across model families and persuasion tac-
tics, suggesting a fundamental bias in how LLMs assess
clinical risk.
We hypothesize this reflects differential weighting of im-
mediate versus delayed harms. Inappropriate opioid pre-
scribing carries vivid, proximate risks (addiction, respira-
tory depression, overdose) prominently represented in train-
ing corpora and public discourse. In contrast, harms from
unnecessary imaging—radiation exposure, incidental find-
ings triggering anxiety and invasive workups, healthcare
cost inflation—are probabilistic, diffuse, and temporally
distant. This pattern mirrors well-documented cognitive bi-
ases in human clinical decision-making, where salience and
temporal proximity disproportionately influence risk per-
ception [18].
The clinical implication is sobering: LLM clinical agents
may be most vulnerable to acquiescence precisely in scenar-
ios where harms are subtle, cumulative, or systemic rather
than acute and obvious. Low-value care—estimated to con-
stitute 10–30% of healthcare expenditure [27]—often in-
volves exactly such “invisible” harms. Recent data from
Rockwell et al. [26] underscore the magnitude of this prob-
lem: in Virginia alone, approximately $1.3 billion was spent
on low-value care during 2020-2021, with 2021 spending
rates 6% higher than prepandemic levels. This persistent
and growing burden of low-value care highlights the criti-
cal importance of ensuring that AI clinical agents can resist
patient pressure for interventions where harms are not im-
mediately apparent.
5.3. Uniform Effectiveness of Persuasion Tactics
Contrary to expectations, all five persuasion tactics
achieved comparable effectiveness (30.0–36.0% acquies-
cence), with no single tactic emerging as dominant. Citation
Pressure proved marginally most effective (36.0%), sug-
gesting appeals to scientific authority—even when vague
or fabricated—carry particular weight with LLM systems
trained extensively on scientific literature.
The uniform effectiveness across tactics carries impor-
tant implications: models vulnerable to one form of pres-
sure are generally vulnerable to all forms. This suggests
that resistance to sycophancy is a general property of model
alignment rather than scenario-specific hardening. Recent
work examining sycophancy across diverse contexts sup-
ports this interpretation [28], demonstrating that RLHF-
trained models exhibit “steerable” alignment where they
adapt to user preferences across multiple persuasion modal-
ities. Conversely, our findings imply that achieving robust
guideline adherence may not require defending against spe-
cific attack vectors, but rather instilling a more fundamental
disposition toward evidence-based reasoning under social
pressure.

follow guidelines was present; the failure was in maintain-

between capability (can the model follow guidelines?) and
robustness (will it maintain guidelines under adversarial
conditions?)—represents a critical gap that prompt engi-

Organizations deploying clinical AI systems cannot rely

5.5. Implications for Clinical AI Evaluation and
Certification
Our findings expose a critical blind spot in current LLM
evaluation paradigms.
Models achieving high scores on
medical knowledge benchmarks (MedQA, USMLE) and
single-turn diagnostic tasks may nonetheless fail catas-
trophically under the multi-turn social dynamics inherent
to clinical practice. The chasm between static capability as-
sessment and dynamic adversarial robustness has profound
implications for patient safety.
We argue that multi-turn adversarial testing must be-
come mandatory for clinical AI certification, paralleling
established practices in other safety-critical domains: air-
craft undergo stress testing beyond normal operating pa-
rameters; cryptographic systems are evaluated against so-
phisticated attacks; pharmaceutical agents are assessed for
safety across diverse populations and drug interactions.
Clinical AI systems, positioned to influence diagnostic and
therapeutic decisions, warrant equally rigorous adversar-
ial scrutiny. Recent safety evaluation frameworks support
this position: Weidinger et al. [30] emphasize the necessity
of dynamic adversarial testing with human raters who can
adapt their strategy to model responses over the course of
conversations, identifying model-specific edge cases under
which unsafe behavior emerges. The Future of Life Insti-
tute [12] similarly highlights that standard evaluations often
capture only surface-level behavior, and that testing in real-
istic noisy or adversarial conditions is essential for credible
risk assessment.
Regulatory frameworks should require demonstration of
robustness under simulated patient pressure before clini-
cal deployment approval.
Our SycoEval-EM framework
provides an initial foundation for such evaluation, though
broader scenario coverage and additional persuasion modal-
ities will be necessary for comprehensive certification stan-
dards. The International AI Safety Report [2] emphasizes
that rigorous risk assessment must combine multiple eval-
uation approaches and require substantial expertise, direct
model access, and comprehensive information about train-
ing methodologies—precisely the type of holistic evalua-
tion our framework exemplifies.
5.6. The Empathy-Adherence Tension
A fundamental design tension underlies our findings.
The same mechanisms enabling engaging, patient-centered
interactions—responsiveness to preferences, empathetic ac-
commodation, conversational agreeableness—contribute to
sycophantic vulnerability.
Training paradigms optimiz-
ing for user satisfaction may inadvertently optimize for
guideline violations when patient preferences conflict with
evidence-based recommendations.
This tension is not merely theoretical but has been
empirically demonstrated in deployed systems.
Lam-
bert et al. [21] document that RLHF training promotes
sycophancy—“the propensity for the model to tell the user
what they want to hear”—and note that a GPT-4o update
in April 2025 resulted in extreme sycophantic behavior
where the model validated implausible user claims rather
than grounding conversations in reality. Gabriel et al. [13]
further analyze how RLHF creates an ethically problematic
trade-off: increased helpfulness and user-friendliness lead
to serious risks of misleading or deceiving users about sys-
tem capabilities, potentially causing users to misplace trust
or make inappropriate use of such systems. Casper et al. [5]
systematically review how RLHF-trained systems exhibit
multiple failure modes including sycophancy, hallucination,
and adversarial vulnerabilities, many of which escaped in-
ternal safety evaluations prior to deployment.
The two models achieving perfect resistance (Claude-
Sonnet-4.5, Grok-3-mini) demonstrate that this tension is
resolvable, though the specific mechanisms enabling their
robustness warrant investigation. Potential approaches in-
clude anti-sycophancy training objectives, reinforcement
learning frameworks rewarding well-reasoned refusals, and
constitutional AI methods encoding clinical ethics princi-
ples. The goal is not rigid inflexibility, but systems capa-
ble of maintaining appropriate boundaries while remaining
genuinely compassionate—firm yet kind, evidence-based
yet patient-centered.
5.7. Future Directions
Building on these findings, we identify three critical di-
rections for future work:
Medical Emotional Intelligence Benchmark. Current
evaluations focus narrowly on diagnostic accuracy or guide-
line adherence, neglecting the broader spectrum of clinical
competencies.
We are developing a standardized bench-
mark to assess LLMs’ emotional intelligence and communi-
cation quality in simulated clinical interactions, expanding
beyond guideline adherence to include empathy, emotional
regulation, cultural sensitivity, and shared decision-making.
This benchmark will evaluate whether models can simul-
taneously maintain guideline adherence while demonstrat-
ing appropriate compassion, validate patient concerns, and
build therapeutic alliance.

physicians, administrators) to study how team dynamics, in-
stitutional pressures, and hierarchical structures influence
guideline adherence. For instance, does an LLM clinical
agent respond differently to patient pressure when a super-
vising physician agent is present? How do conflicting inputs
from multiple stakeholders affect decision-making? Such
multi-agent simulations could reveal vulnerabilities invisi-
ble in dyadic patient-provider interactions.
Ethical Alignment Beyond Sycophancy.
Current
safety alignment efforts focus primarily on preventing
harmful outputs (toxicity, bias, dangerous advice).
Our
work reveals the need for more nuanced alignment ob-
jectives that balance empathy, patient satisfaction, and
evidence-based reasoning. Future research should explore
anti-sycophancy training objectives, reinforcement learn-
ing frameworks that reward guideline-adherent refusals,
and constitutional AI approaches that encode clinical ethics
principles. The goal is not to create inflexibly rigid systems
that reject all patient input, but rather systems capable of
maintaining appropriate boundaries while remaining com-
passionate and patient-centered.
6. Conclusion
This work establishes that susceptibility to patient pres-
sure for guideline-discordant care represents a critical vul-
nerability in medical LLMs. Through systematic evaluation
of 20 contemporary models across 1,875 simulated clinical
encounters, we demonstrate that guideline adherence under
adversarial conditions varies dramatically (0-100% acquies-
cence) and cannot be predicted from model scale, recency,
or performance on static medical benchmarks.
Three key findings have immediate implications for clin-
ical AI safety. First, models show systematically higher ac-
quiescence for imaging requests versus opioid prescriptions
(38.8% vs. 25.0%), revealing vulnerability precisely where
harms are subtle and systemic—the characteristic signature
of low-value care. Second, uniform effectiveness across di-
verse persuasion tactics (30.0-36.0%) indicates that resis-
tance requires fundamental alignment interventions rather
than tactic-specific defenses. Third, perfect resistance by
two models (Claude-Sonnet-4.5, Grok-3-mini) proves the
empathy-adherence tension is resolvable through appropri-
ate safety alignment.
Our findings challenge the paradigm that prompt engi-
neering and medical knowledge alone suffice for safe de-
ployment. The failure of explicit guideline instructions un-
der sustained pressure demonstrates that multi-turn adver-
sarial testing must become mandatory for clinical AI certi-
fication, paralleling stress testing in other safety-critical do-
mains. Systems that acquiesce to unindicated interventions
30-100% of the time cannot be safely deployed in clinical
practice, regardless of diagnostic accuracy.
The SycoEval-EM framework provides a foundation for
such evaluation, though expansion to broader clinical con-
texts and richer multi-agent scenarios will be necessary for
comprehensive safety assessment. Future progress requires
alignment paradigms that instill not just medical knowledge
but clinical judgment under social pressure—systems capa-
ble of being simultaneously compassionate and evidence-
based, responsive yet appropriately firm.
