Simple chemical systems with chaos
Tomislav Plesa 1, Julien Clinton Sprott 2
Abstract: A number of simple chaotic three-dimensional dynamical systems (DSs) with quadratic polyno-
mials on the right-hand sides are reported in the literature, containing exactly 5 or 6 monomials of which
only 1 or 2 are quadratic. However, none of these simple systems are chemical dynamical systems (CDSs)
- a special subset of polynomial DSs that model the dynamics of mass-action chemical reaction networks
(CRNs). In particular, only a small number of three-dimensional quadratic CDSs with chaos are reported,
all of which have at least 9 monomials and at least 3 quadratics, with CRNs containing at least 7 reactions
and at least 3 quadratic ones. To bridge this gap, in this paper we prove some basic properties of chaotic
CDSs, including that those in three dimensions have at least 6 monomials, at least one of which is negative
and quadratic. We then use these results to computationally find 20 chaotic three-dimensional CDSs with 6
monomials and as few as 4 quadratics, or 7 monomials and as few as 2 quadratics. At the CRN level, some
of these systems have 4 reactions of which only 3 are quadratic, or 5 reactions with only 2 being quadratic.
These results quantify structural complexity of chaotic CDSs, and indicate that they are ubiquitous.

Introduction
Systems of N first-order autonomous ordinary-differential equations with polynomials of at most degree n
on the right-hand side, called N-dimensional n-degree polynomial dynamical systems (DSs), can display
chaos when N ≥3 and n ≥2 [1, 2]. A fundamental task is to characterize structurally the simplest three-
dimensional quadratic DSs that display this complicated dynamical behavior. To this end, we associate to
each such DS a vector (a, b), where a is the total number of monomials on the right-hand side across all
the equations, while b is the total number of the quadratic monomials. In this context, the (7, 2) Lorenz
system has been presented in 1963 [3], containing 7 monomials of which only 2 are quadratic, and in 1976
the (7, 1) R¨ossler system [4], containing only 1 quadratic. Subsequently, even simpler chaotic systems have
been discovered: five (5, 2) and fourteen (6, 1) DSs in 1994 [5], and a (5, 1) DS in 1997 [6]. To this date, no
simpler three-dimensional quadratic DSs with chaos have been reported; instead, the literature has focused
more on generally more complicated chaotic DSs with various additional properties, such as those with no
equilibria [7], with a unique and stable equilibrium [8] and with a line of equilibria [9].
However, none of these simple chaotic DSs are also chemical dynamical systems (CDSs) [10] - a special
subset of polynomial DSs that can model the time-evolution of the (non-negative) concentrations of chem-
ical species reacting according to a set of mass-action reactions jointly called a chemical reaction network
(CRN) [11, 12]. Put differently, while the reported chaotic DSs can be experimentally implemented with
mechanical or electronic devices [13], they cannot be implemented with chemical reactions - a task achievable
only for CDSs, using e.g. DNA molecules [14]. A fundamental property preventing this implementation, that
distinguishes CDSs from general polynomial DSs, is the absence of negative monomials that do not include
as a factor the corresponding variable; for example, −1 does not include a factor of x, making dx/dt = −1
a non-chemical DS. In this paper, we associate to every CRN, induced by a quadratic CDS, a vector (c, d),
where c is the total number of reactions, and d the total number of the quadratic reactions - those with two
molecules on the left-hand side, such as X + Y −→Z, which correspond to the quadratic monomials.
Compared to general DSs, the number of reported three-dimensional quadratic CDSs with chaos is
significantly lower. In this context, in 1980, reported are a (12, 9) CDS with a (10, 7) CRN [15], and a (9, 6)
CDS with a (7, 4) CRN [16], known as the minimal Willamowski–R¨ossler system. Both of these CDSs take
the Lotka-Volterra form [17]: all the monomials in a given equation contain as a factor the corresponding
variable. More recently, a systematic method has been developed that, under suitable robustness condition,
allows one to map every chaotic three-dimensional quadratic (a, 1) DS, with any a, to a three-dimensional
quadratic CDS with chaos preserved [18, 10]. Using this method, the following relatively simple systems
have been constructed in [18]: two (11, 5) CDSs with (9, 4) CRNs, one resembling the R¨ossler system, while

A natural question arises: What are structurally the simplest CDSs with chaos? In particular, is chaos
possible in three-dimensional quadratic CDSs with fewer than 9 monomials, or fewer than 3 quadratic
monomials? Are there chaotic CRNs that have fewer than 7 reactions, or fewer than 3 quadratic reactions?
In this paper, we address these questions. In particular, we first derive two elementary theoretical results.
Included in the first result is that if an N-dimensional polynomial DS has a compact invariant set in the
positive orthant, then it has at least 2N monomials, with at least one negative and one positive monomial per
equation, see Theorem 3.1. This result implies that three-dimensional CDSs with chaos in the positive orthant
have at least 6 monomials. The second result is that three-dimensional CDSs with chaos have a negative
nonlinear monomial with at least two distinct factors, see Theorem 3.2.
We then perform an extensive
computer search for chaotic three-dimensional quadratic CDSs with only 6 or 7 monomials, using the derived
theoretical results to reduce the computational intensity of the search. We discover 20 chemical systems,
named CS1–CS20 and presented in Tables 1–2, with numerically detected chaos displayed in Figure 1. In
particular, we show that chaos is detected in as simple as (6, 4) and (7, 2) CDSs, and (4, 3) and (5, 2) CRNs,
thus quantifying the structural complexity of chaotic CDSs. Furthermore, our results indicate that CDSs
with chaos, while structurally more complicated, are nevertheless ubiquitous.
The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present some background theory; further details can
be found in Appendix A. In Section 3, we present some theoretical results about chaotic CDSs, which we
then use in Section 4 to narrow down the computer search for simple three-dimensional quadratic CDSs with
chaos. Finally, we close the paper with some open problems in Section 5.

Chaos. To discuss chaos, let us first present a basic definition.
Definition 2.3. Let x(t; x0) be the solution of (1) with x(0; x0) = x0. Assume that the following statement
holds: if x0 ∈V ⊂RN, then x(t; x0) ∈V for all t ∈R. Then, V is said to be an invariant set for DS (1).
Loosely speaking, a compact invariant set V for DS (1) is chaotic if it has the following two properties [1, 2]:
(i) trajectories in V are “sensitive to initial conditions”, and (ii) V is “irreducible”; V is said to be a chaotic
attractor if additionally (iii) there exists a neighborhood U ⊃V such that if x(0; x0) = x0 ∈U, then x(t; x0)
approaches V as t →∞. Properties (i) and (ii) can be defined in a number of non-equivalent ways [19]. For
example, (i) can be defined via existence of a positive Lyapunov exponent (LE) [20, 2], which implies that
nearby trajectories in V separate exponentially fast. More broadly, there are also other definitions of chaos
that impose additional properties, or relax some of the stated properties [19]; however, all the definitions
explicitly or implicitly exclude chaos from occurring in DSs (1) with N ≤2 or n = 1. In this paper, we
use only some properties of chaotic sets. In particular, in Section 3, we present some results that rely only
on compactness of chaotic sets, and on the fact that they occur in DSs (1) only if N ≥3 and n ≥2; in
Section 4, we use positive LEs as numerical indicators of chaos.

of the systems had some additional monomials (e.g. if it is a perturbation of the other CDS), then we have
reported only one of the systems, which has lower monomial number and more elegant coefficients.

Discussion
Chaos in chemical systems presented in this paper has been investigated numerically.
In this context,
a natural open problem is: Prove (rigorously) that the chemical systems from Tables 1–2 have chaotic
attractors. Let us now pose additional open problems, to be proved or disproved, centered on the following
question: Are there other chaotic chemical systems that are as simple or even simpler than CS1–CS20?
In particular, chaos is detected in a CDS with as low as 2 quadratic monomials, see CS9. In contrast, no
chaos has been detected in the (13, 1) CDS (6) under the constraints from Theorem 3.3, suggesting that 1
quadratic monomial alone may be insufficient for chaos in three-dimensional quadratic CDSs.
