Finding large sparse induced subgraphs in graphs
of small (but not very small) tree-independence number
Daniel Lokshtanov∗
Michał Pilipczuk†
Paweł Rzążewski‡
Abstract
The independence number of a tree decomposition is the size of a largest independent set
contained in a single bag. The tree-independence number of a graph G is the minimum inde-
pendence number of a tree decomposition of G. As shown recently by Lima et al. [ESA 2024], a
large family of optimization problems asking for a maximum-weight induced subgraph of bounded
treewidth, satisfying a given CMSO2 property, can be solved in polynomial time in graphs whose
tree-independence number is bounded by some constant k.
However, the complexity of the algorithm of Lima et al. grows rapidly with k, making it useless
if the tree-independence number is superconstant. In this paper we present a refined version of the
algorithm. We show that the same family of problems can be solved in time nO(k), where n is the
number of vertices of the instance, k is the tree-independence number, and the O(·)-notation hides
factors depending on the treewidth bound of the solution and the considered CMSO2 property.
This running time is quasipolynomial for classes of graphs with polylogarithmic tree-independence
number; several such classes were recently discovered. Furthermore, the running time is subex-
ponential for many natural classes of geometric intersection graphs – namely, ones that admit
balanced clique-based separators of sublinear size.

Introduction
Tree decompositions and treewidth are among most successful concepts in graph theory, with numer-
ous structural [7,14,34,37] and algorithmic applications [5,7,12] (see Section 2.1 for formal definitions).
In the context of the latter, having a tree decomposition of a graph with small (say, constant) width
allows us to mimic the standard bottom-up dynamic programming on trees in order to solve many
computational problems efficiently. This idea led to a flurry of algorithmic results and techniques,
designed for graphs of bounded treewidth [6,10,22,29,30].
In order to understand which problems can be solved efficiently on given class of graphs, it is
beneficial to design algorithms that do not focus on particular problems, but rather on families of prob-
lems expressible in a certain way. Such results are typically referred to as algorithmic meta-theorems.
On a high level, an algorithmic meta-theorem says that every problem of a given type can be solved
efficiently in the given class of graphs. Such “types of problems” are usually defined in terms of logic.
Probably the best known algorithmic meta-theorem is the celebrated result of Courcelle [20] which
says that every problem expressible in CMSO2 can be solved in linear time in graphs given with a tree
decomposition of constant width. Here, CMSO2 is the Monadic Second Order logic of graphs with
quantification over edge subsets and modular predicates. In essence, the logic allows quantification

While Courcelle’s theorem handles a very broad family of problems, it comes at a cost: the assump-
tion that the treewidth is bounded by a constant is rather restrictive. In particular, the theorem applies
only to sparse graphs. On the other hand, there are classes of dense graphs with very good struc-
tural properties, like interval graphs [33, Chapter 8], circular-arc graphs [33, Chapter 8.6], or chordal
graphs [33, Chapter 4]. Often these properties can be expressed in terms of the existence of a certain
tree decomposition whose every bag has some “nice structure” (opposed to every bag having bounded
size, as in the definition of treewidth). For instance, chordal graphs are precisely the graphs that admit
a tree decomposition where every bag is a clique. Such decompositions with a “nice structure” can be
also exploited algorithmically.
This motivation led to the definition of tree-independence number of a graph. This parameter was
independently introduced by Yolov [39] and by Dallard et al. [26]; we use the notation and terminology
from the latter work. The independence number of a tree decomposition is the minimum value of k
such that every bag induces a subgraph of independence number at most k. The tree-independence
number of a graph G is the minimum independence number of a tree decomposition of G; see Sec-
tion 2.1 for more information. Thus, chordal graphs are exactly graphs of tree-independence number
1.
The algorithmic motivation behind the definition of tree-independence number was to understand
the tractability of the Max (Weight) Independent Set problem (MWIS). Indeed, a standard dynamic
programming approach shows that MWIS can be solved in polynomial time for graphs of bounded
tree-independence number, given with an appropriate tree decomposition. However, it turns out that
many other problems, asking for finding large induced subgraphs that are sparse (or, equivalently in
this context, of bounded clique number), can be efficiently solved in the considered classes [39]. In
particular, many of these problems can be expressed as special cases of a certain abstract problem. For
an integer t and a CMSO2 sentence ψ, we define (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS (here, MWIS stands for maximum-
weight induced subgraph) as follows.
Input:
A graph G equipped with a weight function w: V (G) →Q+.
Task:
Find a set F ⊆V (G), such that
• G[F] |= ψ,
• tw(G[F]) < t, and
• F is of maximum weight subject to the conditions above,
or conclude that no such set exists.
(tw < t, ψ)-MWIS
Notable special cases of the (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS problem are MWIS, Feedback Vertex Set (equiv-
alently, Max Induced Forest), and Even Cycle Transversal (equivalently, Max Induced Odd Cac-
tus).
As shown by Lima et al. [35], every problem expressible as (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS can be efficiently
solved in graphs of bounded tree-independence number (given with a decomposition).
Theorem 1.1 (Lima et al. [35]). For any fixed integers t, k and a fixed CMSO2 sentence ψ, there exists
d such that the following holds: The (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS problem can be solved in time O(nd) in n-vertex
graphs of tree-independence number at most k.

Let us remark that the assumption that the solution induces a sparse graph is crucial here. In partic-
ular, Cliqe is NP-hard in graphs of tree-independence number 2, as MWIS is NP-hard in triangle-free
graphs [36]. Interestingly, algorithms for (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS are known also for other classes of well-
structured dense graphs [2,19,31,32].
Theorem 1.1 allows us to solve a rich family of optimization problems in classes of constant tree-
independence number [1, 24]. However, the fact that the exponent d depends (at least) exponentially
on k makes the algorithm from Theorem 1.1 not very useful for classes where tree-independence is
small, but superconstant – say, logarithmic in n. It turns out that there are some natural classes where
such phenomenon can be observed; we discuss them in Section 1.1.
In this paper, we prove the following refinement of Theorem 1.1.
Theorem 1.2. Let t be a fixed integer and ψ a fixed CMSO2 sentence. Then the (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS prob-
lem can be solved in time nO(k) in n-vertex graphs given along with a tree decomposition of independence
number at most k and nO(1) nodes.
The key difference, compared to Theorem 1.1, is that the dependence of running time of the algo-
rithm in Theorem 1.2 on k is single-exponential. This makes Theorem 1.2 useful to obtain quasipolynomial-
time or subexponential-time algorithms in classes where the tree-independence number is, respec-
tively, polylogarithmic or sublinear.
Let us remark that the requirement that the input graph is given along with a suitable tree de-
compostion is not very restrictive. Indeed, Dallard et al. [23] proved that given an n-vertex graph
with tree-independence number at most k, in time 2O(k2)nO(k) one can obtain a tree decomposition
with independence number at most 8k. By combining this result with our Theorem 1.2, we obtain the
following result.
Theorem 1.3. Let t be a fixed integer and ψ be a fixed CMSO2 sentence. Then the (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS
problem can be solved in time 2O(k2)nO(k) in n-vertex graphs of tree-independence number at most k.
We remark that if k = Ω(√n), then the 2O(k2) factor in the running time of the algorithm from
Theorem 1.3 makes it somewhat useless. Thus, in such cases we need to rely on obtaining an appro-
priate tree decomposition in some other way.
1.1
Algorithmic consequences.
The main motivation behind improving Theorem 1.1 to Theorem 1.2 is to use it for classes with super-
constant tree-independence number. Let us discuss some examples of such classes.
Classes of polylogarithmic tree-independence number.
Let us start with a few definitions. A
hole in a graph is an induced cycle with at least 4 vertices. A hole is even is it has an even number of
vertices.
A theta is a graph consisting of two distinct vertices a, b and three paths P1, P2, P3 from a to b
such that the union of any two of them induces a hole. A pyramid is a graph consisting of a vertex a
and a triangle b1, b2, b3, and three paths Pi from a to bi for i ∈{1, 2, 3} such that the union of any two
of these paths induces a hole. Finally, a generalized prism is a graph consisting of two (not necessarily
disjoint) triangles a1, a2, a3 and b1, b2, b3, and three paths Pi from ai to bi for i ∈{1, 2, 3}, such that
the union of any two of these paths induces a hole. A three-path configuration, or a 3PC for short, is a
graph that is either a theta, a pyramid, or a generalized prism.
By Sq,q,q we denote the graph obtained from three paths, each with q + 1 vertices, by picking one
endvertex of each path and identifying them. By Kq,q we denote the complete graph with each side of
size q. The q × q-wall, denoted by Wq×q is the q × q hexagonal grid. Then Lq is the family of graphs
such that every G ∈Lq is the line graph of some subdivision of Wq×q.

The next theorem summarizes known results about families of graphs where the tree-independence
number is bounded by a polylogarithmic function of the number of vertices.
Theorem 1.4 (Chudnovsky et al. [18], Chudnovsky et al. [17], Chudnovsky et al. [16], Chudnovsky et

of G is bounded by
• O(log2 n) if G is 3PC-free [18];
• O(log10 n) if G is even-hole-free [17];
• O(log4 n) if G is (Sq,q,q, Kq,q; Lq)-free [16]; and
• O(logcq n) if G is (theta, pyramid, Lq)-free, where cq is some constant depending on q [15].
Observe that Theorem 1.3 implies that the (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS problem can be solved in quasipoly-
nomial time in every class of graphs where the tree-independence number is bounded by a polylogar-
itmic function of the number of vertices. Combining this observation with Theorem 1.4, we obtain the
following corollary.
Corollary 1.5. For any fixed t and a CMSO2 sentence ψ, the (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS problem can be solved
in quasipolynomial time for
• 3PC-free graphs;
• even-hole-free graphs;
• (Sq,q,q, Kq,q, Lq)-free graphs, for any fixed q; and
• (theta, pyramid, Lq)-free graphs, for any fixed q.

2.1
Tree decompositions and tree-independence
A tree decomposition of a graph G is a pair T = (T, {Xu}u∈V (T)), where T is a tree and, for every
u ∈V (T), Xu is a subset of V (G) called a bag, so that the following conditions are met:
(i) for every vertex v ∈V (G) there is u ∈V (T) such that v ∈Xu;

(ii) for every edge vw ∈E(G) there is u ∈V (T) such that w, v ∈Xu; and
(iii) for every vertex v ∈V (G), the set of nodes u ∈V (T) such that v ∈Xu induces a connected
subgraph of T.
To avoid confusion between V (G) and V (T), the elements of V (T) will be called nodes. Typically, we
consider T as a rooted tree, which defines the ancestor-descendant relation on its nodes. This relation
is reflexive, i.e., we consider every node its own ancestor as well as its own descendant. For a node
u ∈V (T), we define Vu := S{Xu′ : u′ is a descendant of u}.
The width of a tree decomposition T is defined as maxu∈V (T) |Xu| −1. The treewidth of a graph
G, denoted by tw(G), is the minimum width of a tree decomposition of G.
While computing treewidth is NP-hard [4], we can efficiently verify whether tw(G) < t, for any
fixed t.
Theorem 2.1 (Bodlaender [8]). Let t be a fixed constant. Given an n-vertex graph G, in time O(n) one
can compute a tree decomposition of G of width at most t, or decide that tw(G) > t.
Tree-independence number.
For a tree decomposition T = (T, {Xu}u∈V (T)), by α(T ) we denote
maxu∈V (T) α(Xu). By tree-independence number of a graph G we denote the minimum value of α(T )
over all tree decompositions T of G.

2.2
CMSO2
We use the standard logic CMSO2, which stands for Counting Monadic Second Order logic with edge
quantification, on graphs. We now recall the fundamentals and establish the notation; for a broader
introduction see e.g. [21].
In CMSO2 on graphs, there are variables of four different sorts: for single vertices and for single
edges (typically denoted with lower case letters), as well as for subsets of vertices and for subsets of
edges (typically denoted with upper case letters). Atomic formulas of CMSO2 are of the following
form:
• equality of variables of the same sort: x = y or X = Y ;
• testing the incidence relation: inc(x, e), where x is a vertex variable and e is an edge variable;
• set membership: x ∈X, where x is a (vertex/edge) variable and X is a (vertex/edge) set variable;
and
6


## Page 7


• modular arithmetic checks: |X| ≡a mod m, where X is a (vertex/edge) set variable and a, m ∈
Z are fixed integers with m > 0.
Starting with those atomic formulas, larger formulas can be constructed using standard boolean con-
nectives (∧, ∨, ⇒), negation, and quantification over variables of all sorts (both universal and existen-
tial). This defines the syntax of CMSO2. The semantics, i.e., how the satisfaction of a formula in a
graph is defined, is as expected. For p ∈N, by C⩽pMSO2 we denote the fragment of CMSO2 where all
the moduli m used in the modular arithmetic checks satisfy m ⩽p. The quantifier rank of a CMSO2
formula ϕ is the maximum nesting depth of quantifiers in ϕ.
In a formula of CMSO2, any variable that is not bound by a quantifier is called a free variable. We
often distinguish the free variables in parentheses by the formula, e.g. ϕ(x, y). A sentence is a formula
without free variables. As usual, we write G |= ϕ(¯a) to state that formula ϕ holds in a graph G for an
evaluation ¯a of the free variables of ϕ.
We will use the following standard fact asserting that the class of graphs of treewidth at most t is
definable in CMSO2, for every fixed t.
Lemma 2.2. For every t there is a sentence ϕtw<t of CMSO2 such that for every graph G,
G |= ϕtw<t
if and only if
tw(G) < t.
Proof. By the celebrated result of Robertson and Seymour [38], for each t there is a finite family Ft of
graphs such that, for every G it holds that tw(G) < t if and only if G does not contain any F ∈Ft as a
minor. Thus, the sentence ϕtw<t is a conjunction of sentences asserting that a graph does not contain
F as a minor, over all F ∈Ft. Expressing that a fixed graph F is a minor of a given graph can be easily
done in CMSO2. Such a sentence should say that there exists a vertex set for each vertex of F (i.e., a
constant number of sets) so that each of these sets induces a connected graph, the sets are pairwise
disjoint, and an edge between a pair of sets exists whenever the two corresponding vertices of H are
adjacent.

CMSO2 and boundaried graphs
Boundaried graphs.
For a finite set of vertex variables ¯x, a graph G, and a vertex subset A ⊆V (G),
by A¯x we denote the set of all injective functions from ¯x to A; and G¯x is a shorthand for V (G)¯x. An
element ¯a ∈A¯x is interpreted as an evaluation mapping the variables of ¯x to pairwise different vertices
of A: each x ∈¯x is evaluated to ¯a(x). The set ¯a(¯x), that is, the image of ¯a, is denoted by im ¯a. An
¯x-boundaried graph is a pair (G, ¯a), where G is a graph and ¯a ∈G¯x. The boundary of (G, ¯a) is the
image of ¯a.
We define the following two operations on boundaried graphs:
• Suppose (G1, ¯a1) and (G2, ¯a2) are an ¯x1-boundaried graph and an ¯x2-boundaried graph, respec-
tively. Then gluing those two boundaried graphs results in the ¯x-boundaried graph (G, ¯a) =
(G1, ¯a1) ⊕(G2, ¯a2), where ¯x = ¯x1 ∪¯x2, constructed as follows. Take the disjoint union of G1
and G2 and for each x ∈¯x1 ∩¯x2, fuse ¯a1(x) with ¯a2(x) into one vertex and declare it to be ¯a(x).
For x ∈¯x1 −¯x2 set ¯a(x) := ¯a1(x) and for x ∈¯x2 −¯x1 set ¯a(x) := ¯a2(x).
• If (G, ¯a) is an ¯x-boundaried graph and x ∈¯x is a variable, then forgetx(G, ¯a) is the (¯x −{x})-
boundaried graph (G, ¯a|¯x−{x}). In other words, the graph stays the same, but we forget variable

Types.
It is well-known (see e.g. [13]) that for any fixed p, q ∈N and a finite set of vertex variables
¯x, every formula ϕ(¯x) of C⩽pMSO2 of quantifier rank at most q can be effectively transformed into
an equivalent normalized formula ϕ′(¯x) of C⩽pMSO2 of the same quantifier rank. Furthermore, the
set of the normalized formulas, call it Formulae¯x,p,q, is finite and computable from p, q, ¯x. We define
Types¯x,p,q to be the powerset of Formulae¯x,p,q, and for an ¯x-boundaried graph (G, ¯a), we define its
rank-(p, q) type as
typep,q(G, ¯a) := {ϕ(¯x) ∈Formulae¯x,p,q | G |= ϕ(¯a)} ∈Types¯x,p,q.
Two ¯x-boundaried graphs (G1, ¯a1) and (G2, ¯a2) are called (p, q)-equivalent, denoted (G1, ¯a1) ≡p,q
(G2, ¯a2), if they have the same rank-(p, q) types, that is, typep,q(G1, ¯a1) = typep,q(G2, ¯a2).
Note that isomorphic boundaried graphs are (p, q)-equivalent for all p, q ∈N, where an isomor-
phism between ¯x-boundaried graphs (G1, ¯a1) and (G2, ¯a2) is defined as an isomorphism between G1
and G2 that maps ¯a1(x) to ¯a2(x), for each x ∈¯x. Further, for any fixed p and q, the rank-(p, q) type
of a boundaried graph (G, ¯a) is computable from (G, ¯a). Finally, it is well known that types defined in
this are compositional with respect to the operations on boundaried graphs, in the sense that the type
of the result of an operation is determined by the type(s) of the input graph(s). This is formalized in
the following lemma.

typep,q((G1, ¯a1) ⊕(G2, ¯a2)) = typep,q(G1, ¯a1) ⊕typep,q(G2, ¯a2).

we have
typep,q(forgetx(G, ¯a)) = forgetx(typep,q(G, ¯a)).

We will use the classic result of Courcelle about the
tractability of CMSO2 on graphs of bounded treewidth, in the following formulation.
Theorem 2.4 (Courcelle [20]). Fix a constant ℓ∈N, a finite set of vertex variables ¯x, and a formula of
ϕ(¯x) of CMSO2. Then given an n-vertex boundaried graph (G, ¯a) of treewidth at most ℓ, one can decide
whether ϕ(¯a) holds in G in time O(n).
We note that the original statement of Courcelle assumed that a tree decomposition of width at
most ℓis also given on input, but this can be computed in O(n) time using Theorem 2.1.
From Theorem 2.4 we may immediately derive that one can efficiently compute the type of a given
graph of bounded treewidth.
Corollary 2.5. Fix constants p, q, ℓ∈N and a finite set of vertex variables ¯x. Then given an n-vertex
¯x-boundaried graph (G, ¯a) of treewidth at most ℓ, one can compute type¯x,p,q(G, ¯a) in time O(n).
Proof. Apply Theorem 2.4 for every formula ϕ(¯x) ∈Formulae¯x,p,q, noting that the set Formulae¯x,p,q is
finite and computable from ¯x, p, q.

In what follows we assume that p, q, t, and ℓare fixed nonnegative integers. We treat p, q, t as constants,

it explicit in the notation; we try to keep this dependence as low as possible.
We fix a set ¯x consisting of at most ℓvertex variables. Consider an ¯x-boundaried graph (G, ¯a)

Lemma 3.3. Let (G1, ¯a1) and (G2, ¯a2) be two ¯x-boundaried graphs of treewidth less than t. Suppose

Some remarks are in place. Recall that |¯x| can be as large as ℓ, which is not a constant. Conse-
quently, the number of formulae in Types¯x,p,q can be very large (essentially, a q-fold exponential func-

using Lemma 3.1 we may efficiently compute a signature of (G, ¯a), for which, by Lemma 3.2, there
are only ℓO(ℓ) many possibilities. And by Lemma 3.3, if for two ¯x-boundaried graphs we compute
the same signature, then they must have the same rank-(p, q) type. Note that the reverse implication
is not necessarily true: it is possible that for two (p, q)-equivalent graphs we compute two different
signatures.

In this section we prove Theorem 1.2.
Let ψ be a fixed CMSO2 formula and t be a constant. Define
ψ′ := ψ ∧ϕtw<t,

satisfy the same formulae, it is sufficient to keep one – the one with maximum weight.
4.1
Obtaining the solution
Suppose that we have computed the families F[·, ·] as specified above. Let r be the root of T , that is,
Vr = V (G). Since T is a nice tree decomposition, we have Xr = ∅.
This means that F = F[r, ∅] contains a maximum-weight representative of each equivalence class
(and possibly some more sets, as distinct sets in F might still be equivalent). We are still left with
deciding which of these sets (or, more precisely, which of subgraphs induced by one of these sets)
satisfy ψ′.
This is, however, easy, as for each F ∈F we have tw(G[F]) < t. Consequently, we can apply
Theorem 2.1 and then Theorem 2.4 to G[F], for each F ∈F. If such G[F] |= ψ′, we mark F as a
candidate, otherwise we discard it.
Finally, if any of the sets F ∈F was marked as a candidate, we return one with the largest weight
as a solution. Otherwise, we report that no solution exists. The total running time of this step is
ℓO(ℓ) · O(n) = kO(k) · O(n).
The correctness of the procedure is straightforward. On one hand, we never return a set that does
not satisfy ψ′. On the other hand, let Fopt be an optimum solution. By (F3), there is some F ∈F that has
at least the same weight as Fopt and is (p, q)-equivalent to Fopt (with empty boundary). In particular,
F also satisfies ψ′ so is also an optimum solution that is marked as a candidate in the procedure above.

5
Conclusion
In the work that first introduced the concept of the tree-independence number, Yolov [39] pointed out
a certain drawback of this parameter: it is unbounded for some classes of graphs that are “simple in
structure,” like complete bipartite graphs. As a remedy, he suggested a refined version of the parameter,
which later became known as the induced matching treewidth [35]. Again, this parameter is defined
using tree decompositions with certain structural properties: this time, we bound the size of a largest
induced matching whose every edge intersects a single bag of the decomposition. Induced matching
treewidth is bounded for all classes of bounded tree-independence number, but can still be small in
classes allowing large complete bipartite graphs.
Yolov [39] showed that MWIS is polynomial-time-solvable in classes of bounded induced matching
treewidth. Lima et al. [35] showed that some other problems, notably Feedback Vertex Set, are also
tractable in these graphs. Furthermore, they conjectured that an analogue of Theorem 1.1 holds for
this parameter too, i.e., every problem expressible as (tw < t, ψ)-MWIS can be solved in polynomial
time for graphs with constant induced matching treewidth. This conjecture was recently confirmed
by Bodlaender, Fomin, and Korhonen [11].
However, similarly to Theorem 1.1, the running time of their algorithm is at least double exponen-
tial in the parameter bound. We believe it would be interesting to see an analogue of our Theorem 1.2
for graphs of bounded induced matching treewidth.
