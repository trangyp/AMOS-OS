profound inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity, rapid selection of therapy-

These enduring limitations underscore the necessity of moving beyond 
descriptive phenomenology toward a predictive, systems-level theory grounded 
in universal evolutionary principles. Accordingly, oncology requires three 
interdependent paradigm shifts: (i) recognition of overarching organizing 
principles governing tumor behavior; (ii) acknowledgment of systemic, multi-
scale regulatory logic—not merely linear causality; and (iii) integration of 
disparate theories into a coherent, hierarchically structured meta-framework. To 
address this need, we introduce the Existential Crisis–Driven Survival (ECDS) 
theory. It posits that malignancy emerges not from autonomous aggressiveness, 
but from the irreversible erosion of Existential Stability (Φ), compelling tumor 
cells to deploy successive, energetically costly, and increasingly maladaptive 
survival strategies. Underlying the apparent phenotypic aggression of tumors 
lies a fundamental “intrinsic fragility”: driver mutations, immune evasion, 
angiogenesis, and metastatic dissemination are not intrinsic drivers of 
progression but rather context-dependent, compensatory adaptations to 
deteriorating Φ—each entailing substantial fitness trade-offs.

formalizes its core constructs and principles; integrates classical models of

the lens of Φ–Ψ dynamics; recontextualizes the canonical hallmarks as 
sequential, threshold-governed manifestations of Ψ activation; analyzes 
heterogeneity and therapeutic resistance as emergent properties of survival-

2. Core Concepts and Principles of the ECDS Theory 
2.1 Qualitative Definition of Core Concepts

autonomous biological subsystems governed by an intrinsic imperative of self-
persistence. To rigorously characterize their persistence states and 
evolutionary trajectories, the theory introduces three foundational, interdefined 
concepts.

defining feature that distinguishes neoplastic transformation from transient 
physiological stress responses. This irreversibility stems from the collapse of 
core maintenance mechanisms: tumor cells lack the functional redundancy and 
regulatory coordination necessary to restore genomic integrity, metabolic 
equilibrium, or microenvironmental fidelity through endogenous repair. In 
contrast, normal somatic cells retain robust homeostatic capacity; mild Φ

magnitude of Φ erosion are thus predictive biomarkers of malignant risk, 
correlating with both the tempo of carcinogenesis and clinical outcomes in a 
gradient-dependent manner.

2.2 Core Principles of the ECDS Theory 
Based on the interdependent definitions of Φ, Ψ, and Θ, the ECDS Theory 
articulates three interlocking principles that govern malignant progression 
(Figure 2) and collectively constitute a closed-loop theoretical architecture—
capable of explaining tumor evolution across its full continuum, from initiation 
and clonal expansion to metastatic dissemination and systemic collapse.   
2.2.1 Principle of Unidirectional Existential Stability Impairment 
The Existential Stability (Φ) of tumor cells undergoes progressive, irreversible, 
and unidirectional erosion21—a process driven by the convergence of two 
complementary forces22,23: (i) intrinsic evolutionary constraints inherent to 
multicellular organization (where somatic cell lineages sacrifice autonomous 
persistence capacity in exchange for tissue-specific functional specialization), 
and (ii) acquired microenvironmental stressors (with driver mutations serving 
as the primary genotypic trigger, and chronic inflammation, ionizing radiation, 
replicative aging, and metabolic stress acting as synergistic amplifiers). 
Critically, this impairment is non-recoverable in neoplastic cells and in non-
malignant cells with profound DNA repair deficiencies; physiological 
compensation remains feasible only in normal cells experiencing mild, transient 
Φ perturbations. The tempo and extent of Φ erosion directly govern oncogenic 
trajectory: accelerated or deeper decline intensifies selective pressure for 
survival 
adaptation, 
thereby 
promoting 
tumor 
initiation, 
phenotypic 
diversification, metastatic competence, intratumoral heterogeneity, and 
therapeutic resistance—establishing Φ erosion rate as a quantitative 
determinant of clinical aggressiveness.   
2.2.2 Principle of Passive Adaptive Upregulation of Survival Capacity 
The compensatory upregulation of Survival Capacity (Ψ) constitutes the 
principal phenotypic response to Φ decline. As Φ deteriorates, cells passively 
engage Ψ pathways—not as an autonomous program but as a homeostatic 
imperative—to preserve the viability constraint Φ + Ψ ≥ Θ. This dynamic 
underlies the emergence, functional diversification, and hierarchical escalation 
of malignant traits. Ψ activation follows a rigorously ordered, threshold-
governed 
progression: 
a

(e.g.,

supporting nutrient supply) → an advanced layer (e.g., Snail/Twist–induced 
epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastatic dissemination). Each 
adaptive tier represents a context-dependent, energetically costly solution to 
escalating Φ deficits—and each, in turn, imposes additional functional burdens 
that further destabilize Φ, thereby reinforcing a self-amplifying cycle of 
deterioration.24,25

2.2.3 Principle of Survival Futility 
Ψ upregulation sustains cellular viability only transiently and conditionally—it 
cannot restore or reverse underlying Φ impairment. While progressive Φ 
erosion necessitates successive rounds of Ψ activation, increasingly complex 
survival adaptations (e.g., EMT, anoikis resistance, stromal reprogramming, 
immunosuppressive niche formation) incur substantial biological liabilities: 
heightened metabolic demand, genomic instability, loss of functional 
differentiation, and systemic resource depletion.26 Consequently, each 
compensatory strategy exacerbates Φ erosion, entrenching the core feedback 
loop: Φ impairment → Ψ activation → further Φ exhaustion. Ultimately, 
excessive reliance on maladaptive survival mechanisms precipitates intrinsic 
tumor cell dysfunction, microenvironmental dysregulation, and host systemic 
toxicity—culminating in failure to satisfy Φ + Ψ ≥ Θ and resulting in dual 
extinction of both tumor and host.27,28 Notably, this cascade is not deterministic 
but context-modulated: under conditions of mild Φ impairment (e.g., early-stage 
preinvasive lesions) or effective external regulation (e.g., targeted inhibition of 
key Ψ effectors, immune checkpoint blockade, or microenvironment 
normalization), Ψ activity may be suppressed or maintained at subthreshold 
levels—enabling a stable, low-energy “pathological equilibrium” manifesting 
clinically as tumor dormancy or long-term disease stabilization in advanced 
cancer patients.29,30   
Collectively, the dynamic tripartite coupling of Φ, Ψ, and Θ forms the theoretical 
skeleton of the ECDS framework—providing a unified, evolutionarily coherent 
logic for integrating historically disparate tumor theories and systematically 
analyzing malignant behavior across the entire ontogenetic lifespan of cancer.

3. Integrative Analysis of Classical Tumor Origin Theories via the ECDS 
Framework 
Tumor origin is a multistage, nonlinear process shaped by intrinsic genetic 
programs and extrinsic environmental pressures.8 Classical theories address 
key facets of carcinogenesis but remain siloed, failing to resolve clinical 
paradoxes (e.g., driver mutation carriage without carcinogenesis). The ECDS


## Page 11


Theory reconciles them via a unifying causal chain: driver mutation-induced Φ 
impairment → non-genetic stressor amplification → functional ground-state 
disruption → passive Ψ activation → tumor initiation.   
3.1 Somatic Mutation Theory: The Genotypic Trigger of Φ Impairment 
Driver mutations are precise disruptors of Φ integrity (e.g., TP53 loss or APC 
inactivation).31,32 Oncogenic transformation requires sequential mutational 
accumulation to cross the Φ impairment threshold; mutational burden alone is 
insufficient—residual Φ above the homeostatic threshold avoids pathological Ψ 
activation. This distinguishes ECDS from classical somatic mutation theory, 
which treats mutations as isolated triggers rather than mediators of Φ collapse.   
3.2 “Bad Luck” Theory: The Stochastic Accelerator of Φ Erosion 
Stochastic errors (DNA replication errors, epigenetic drift) during stem cell 
division accelerate Φ erosion—stem cells operate near Φ’s lower boundary, 
and cumulative probabilistic lesions trigger Ψ activation only when exceeding 
physiological compensation. This reframes classical “bad luck” theory by 
embedding stochasticity within the Φ–Ψ–Θ regulatory architecture, explaining 
why only Φ-impairing errors culminate in malignancy.8 
3.3 Tissue Field Theory: The Microenvironmental Modulator of Φ–Ψ 
Coupling 
Classical tissue field theory (epithelial–stromal crosstalk disruptions) is 
reconceptualized as a Φ–Ψ coupling modulator via two mechanisms:11,33 (1) 
accelerating Φ impairment via diverse factors, e.g., pro-inflammatory mediators 
(e.g., TNF-α, ROS) that induce oxidative DNA damage, chromosomal instability, 
and impaired adhesion; (2) lowering Ψ activation thresholds (paracrine factors, 
immune-permissive niches). This distinguishes ECDS by positioning the 
microenvironment as a context-dependent regulator, not an independent driver.   
3.4 Functional Ground State Theory: The Phenotypic Carrier and 
Transducer of Φ Impairment 
The functional ground state—defined as the homeostasis of epigenetic, 
transcriptional, and signaling pathways—acts as the intuitive, dynamic 
phenotypic carrier of Φ, while linking genetic variation to Φ impairment. Ground 
state stability correlates directly with Φ levels: a stable ground state is 
characterized by conserved epigenetic modifications, coordinated core gene 
expression, and steady signaling activity, which collectively maintain high Φ; in 
contrast, a disordered ground state leads to reduced Φ and increased cancer

transformation risk.34 The ECDS Theory extends classical functional ground 
state theory by anchoring ground state disruption to the progressive impairment 
of Φ, thereby integrating phenotypic plasticity (a key feature of the ground state) 
into the unified Φ–Ψ theoretical framework.35 
3.5 The Unified ECDS Logical Chain of Tumor Origin 
The four classical theories are not mutually exclusive but occupy distinct, 
complementary positions in a causally coherent ECDS logical chain of tumor 
origin: ECDS establishes a causally coherent chain: driver mutations (genotypic 
trigger) + non-genetic stressors (amplifiers) → functional ground state disorder 
(mediator) → gradient-dependent Φ impairment → context-dependent Ψ 
activation → tumor initiation (Figure 3). The four classical theories occupy 
distinct roles: 
1. Somatic mutation theory: Supplies the foundational genotypic substrate—
canonical driver mutations—that initiate ground state perturbation and directly 
impair Φ;   
2. “Bad luck” theory: Acts as a stochastic accelerator of genetic variation 
accumulation, increasing the probability of ground state destabilization without 
altering its fundamental logic;   
3. Tissue field theory: Functions as a microenvironmental amplifier—
modulating both the rate of Φ erosion and the sensitivity of Ψ activation 
thresholds—thereby determining whether a given Φ deficit translates into 
phenotypic progression;   
4. Functional Ground State Theory: Acts as the intuitive, dynamic phenotypic 
carrier of Φ, while also serving as the central transducer—integrating upstream 
genetic, stochastic, and environmental inputs into coordinated phenotypic 
reprogramming that directly drives Φ impairment. 
In essence, tumorigenesis is not an active program of aggression, but a passive, 
system-level survival response to progressive Φ decline—rooted in the 
evolutionary trade-offs inherent to multicellular organization.

5.1 Crisis-Initiation Trigger: Genomic Instability and Mutational Burden—
The Primary Driver of Φ Impairment 
Genomic instability and mutational accumulation are the foundational trigger for 
all hallmarks: initiating irreversible Φ erosion and providing the genotypic 
substrate for Ψ escalation. Driver mutations, structural genomic aberrations 
(e.g., ecDNA), and DNA repair deficiencies collectively compromise Φ’s three 
core dimensions (survival stability, duration, scope). This trigger operates 
continuously across stages, intensifies with Φ impairment, and links tumor 
origin to progression as a unifying mechanistic bridge. Consistent with 
emerging evidence, non-mutational epigenetic reprogramming—another key 
initiator of Φ impairment—synergizes with genomic instability to disrupt Φ 
integrity, laying the groundwork for early Ψ activation (e.g., basal proliferative

intrinsic/extrinsic elimination. These pathways represent the minimal 
phenotypic investment for early clonal expansion, with their activation tightly 
regulated by GAM’s low-level activation stage.

Classical models of therapeutic resistance emphasize clonal selection of pre-

7.1 Core Mechanism: Hierarchical Ψ Leap 
Therapeutic resistance originates from hierarchical Ψ leaps rooted in GAM-
governed intratumoral heterogeneity: subclones in different GAM stages exhibit 
heterogeneous responses to therapy, which amplifies selective pressure for Ψ 
escalation. These leaps reflect a structured, stage-specific “hierarchical 
upgrade” of Survival Capacity (Ψ), directly driven by therapy-induced Φ 
impairment—consistent with ECDS core axioms. Since tumor cells have

8. Clinical Implications and Future Perspectives of the ECDS Theory 
8.1 Paradigm Shift in Clinical Oncology 
The ECDS Theory reshapes clinical cancer cognition: tumor cells are not 
intrinsically aggressive, but Φ erosion–driven passive survival systems—
catalyzing a paradigm shift from “confrontational eradication” to “systemic 
equilibrium regulation.”68 Therapeutic goals thus shift from maximal 
cytoreduction (which risks catastrophic Φ impairment and irreversible Ψ leap to 
epigenetic canalization) to long-term maintenance of Φ–Ψ balance, aligning 
with chronic disease management principles for advanced malignancies.   
For early-stage tumors, the “golden window” lies in reversing mild pre-malignant 
Φ impairment (e.g., enhancing DNA repair or redox homeostasis) before latent 
survival pathways enter low-level activation. For advanced tumors, the priority 
is modulating the intensity of Ψ activation to prevent progression to epigenetic 
canalization—mitigating malignancy, delaying metastasis, and addressing 
therapy-induced metastatic acceleration.69
