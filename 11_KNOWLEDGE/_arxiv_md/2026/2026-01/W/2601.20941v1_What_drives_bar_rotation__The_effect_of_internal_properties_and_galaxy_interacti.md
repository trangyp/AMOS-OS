What drives bar rotation? The effect of internal properties and galaxy
interactions on bar pattern speeds

ABSTRACT
One of the main properties of galactic bars is their rotation (or pattern) speed, which is driven by both internal galactic properties,
as well as external interactions. To assess the influence of these internal and external drivers on bar rotation in a cosmological
setting, we use the Auriga suite of cosmological hydrodynamical zoom-in simulations. We calculate the bar pattern speed and
the bar rotation rate – the ratio of corotation radius to bar length – at the time of bar formation and at 𝑧= 0, and compare
these to bar age, bar strength, baryon dominance, galaxy stellar mass, and the history of external galaxy interactions. We find
that galaxies which are more baryon dominated at 𝑧= 0 – and which lie above the observed stellar mass-halo mass abundance
matching relation – host faster bars, while more dark matter dominated galaxies host slower bars. Baryon-dominated galaxies
also form their bars earlier and their rotation rates stay constant or even decrease over time; this leads to older bars being faster
than their younger counterparts – in contrast to the expectation of bar slow-down from dynamical friction imparted by the dark
matter halo. We also find a trend in stellar mass, with ‘faster’ bars being hosted in more massive galaxies, which could be driven
by the underlying higher baryon-dominance of more massive galaxies. Furthermore, we find that external interactions, such as
mergers and flybys, correlate with lower bar rotation rates, particularly for strong interactions that occur around bar formation
time. This correlation is relatively weak, leaving internal baryon-dominance as the main driver of fast bar rotation rates.

1 INTRODUCTION
Galactic bars are prevalent structures present in approximately 70%
of disc galaxies at redshift zero (𝑧= 0; Eskridge et al. 2000; Mari-
nova & Jogee 2007; Menéndez-Delmestre et al. 2007; Sheth et al.
2008; Buta et al. 2015; Erwin 2018) and have been detected out to
cosmic noon, with their fraction decreasing to below 10% by 𝑧∼4
(Sheth et al. 2008; Le Conte et al. 2024; Huertas-Company et al.
2025; Géron et al. 2025; Amvrosiadis et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2025;
Le Conte et al. 2026). These numbers imply that galactic bars have
been present in a significant number of disc galaxies for much of
their evolution: indeed, observations have found some bars to be an-
cient structures as old as 13.5 Gyr (Gadotti et al. 2015; Bovy et al.
2019; Grady et al. 2020; de Sá-Freitas et al. 2025). Additionally,
simulations of disc galaxies in Sparke & Sellwood (1987), Marinova
& Jogee (2007), Kraljic et al. (2012), Athanassoula et al. (2013) and
Fragkoudi et al. (2025) confirm that bars are generally long-lived
structures, which evolve significantly across cosmic time.
Bars mediate the exchange of angular momentum and energy of
galactic material and, given their prevalence and longevity in disc
galaxies, they are significantdriversofsecular evolution.This angular
momentum exchange is most efficient at resonances – set by the

pattern speed of the bar, Ωp – where the orbits of stars and other
matter can by significantly altered by becoming trapped in or pushed
away from the resonance (e.g. Contopoulos & Mertzanides 1977;
Dehnen 2000). In the disc, these resonances have been seen to have
strong effects on the presence and properties of spiral arms (Sanders
& Huntley 1976; Schwarz 1984; Bissantz et al. 2003), the migration
and mixing of stellar populations across the disc (Dehnen 2000;
Minchev & Famaey 2010; Brunetti et al. 2011), and the driving
of gas towards the centre of the galaxy, which can in turn form
structures such as nuclear discs and rings (Matsuda & Nelson 1977;
Simkin et al. 1980; Sakamoto et al. 1999; Sheth et al. 2005; Cole et al.
2014). Meanwhile, the stellar and dark matter halos absorb angular
momentum from the bar, which can also spin these components up
and change the overall mass profile of the halo (Sellwood 1980;
Tagger et al. 1987; Hernquist & Weinberg 1992; Weinberg & Katz
2002; Athanassoula 2003; Athanassoula et al. 2013). Therefore, in
order to understand the evolution of disc galaxies, it is essential to
understand the evolution of their bars and their pattern speeds in
particular.
Angular momentum exchange with the rest of the galaxy has an
effect on the bar pattern speed in turn. Studies tend to find that, as they
evolve, bars lengthen and slow while trapping more stars on elongated
bar-supporting orbits and transferring their angular momentum out-
wards (Tremaine & Weinberg 1984a; Martinez-Valpuesta et al. 2006;

Elmegreen et al. 2007). Bars can also slow down (without signifi-
cantly lengthening) due to dynamical friction imparted by the dark
matter halo (Tremaine & Weinberg 1984a; Weinberg 1985; Little
& Carlberg 1991; Debattista & Sellwood 2000; Athanassoula 2003;
O’Neill & Dubinski 2003; Sellwood & Debattista 2006; Kataria &
Das 2019). To explore the effect of dynamical friction on the slow
down of bars, we can use the ratio of their corotation radius (the
radius at which circular orbits within the disc have angular velocity
matching the rotation of the bar) to their semi-major axis length (e.g.
Debattista & Sellwood 1998, 2000). This ratio is sometimes referred
to as the ‘rotation rate’ (e.g. Cuomo et al. 2019; Buttitta et al. 2023)
and denoted by ℛ. ℛincreases when the growth of corotation radius
– due to the decrease of the pattern speed – outpaces the lengthening
of the bar. Bars with ℛ> 1.4 are considered slow and bars with
1 < ℛ< 1.4 are considered fast (Debattista & Sellwood 2000). Bars
with ℛ< 1 are called ultra-fast, since consideration of the main
bar-supporting orbits theoretically places the ends of any bar within
the corotation radius (Contopoulos 1980). In practice, observations
of ultra-fast bars are not unusual (Buta & Zhang 2009; Aguerri et al.
2015; Guo et al. 2019) but may be a result of errors such as artificial
lengthening of the bar caused by alignment of spiral arms (Hilmi
et al. 2020; Cuomo et al. 2021).
There are a number of both internal and external factors that can
affect the slow down (or lack thereof) of the bar, leading to departures
from the picture of inevitable slow-down in both Ωp and ℛ. For ex-
ample, bars in highly baryon-dominated discs will tend to experience
less of an impact from dynamical friction (Debattista & Sellwood
1998, 2000; Athanassoula 2003). Additionally, Li et al. (2023) show
that if the dark matter halo has a high spin, then slow down from dy-
namical friction can be halted for an extended period of up to 5 Gyr,
due to the wake of the bar in the dark matter following the stellar bar
very closely and so imparting very little torque. Athanassoula (2003),
Klypin et al. (2009) and Zheng & Shen (2025) also find that a higher
vertical velocity dispersion in the disc can reduce the efficiency of
angular momentum exchange and delay bar slow down.
Simulations of barred galaxies in Berentzen et al. (2007), Romano-
Díaz et al. (2008), Villa-Vargas et al. (2010), Athanassoula (2014)
and Beane et al. (2023) find that bars in galaxies containing higher
fractions of gas slow down less compared to those with lower gas
fractions: the former either maintain a constant pattern speed or speed
up. The speed-up occurs as the gas is directed towards the centre of
the galaxy by the bar, but the exact mechanism by which the bar slow-
down is delayed/halted is unclear. Bournaud et al. (2005) and Beane
et al. (2023) claim that the inflowing gas produces a positive torque
on the bar directly, whereas Berentzen et al. (2007) and Romano-
Díaz et al. (2008) find that it is instead the build up of mass in the
centre of the galaxy due to the inflow which both weakens the bar
and speeds it up.
Another source of angular momentum for existing bars is inter-
actions and mergers with external galaxies. Simulations find that
interactions can affect bars in a variety of ways: interactions can slow-
down bars (as in Sundin et al. 1993; Athanassoula 1996), speed-up
bars (see Gerin et al. 1990; Łokas et al. 2014; Semczuk et al. 2024),
or even destroy them in extreme cases (Kannan et al. 2015; Cavanagh
et al. 2022; Bi et al. 2022) – although this last scenario is rare (Zana
et al. 2018). While the effects of individual interactions are deter-
mined in the above papers, there is no comprehensive understanding
of the net effects of multiple galaxy interactions and mergers on a
population of galactic bars in a cosmological setting.
Galaxy interactions and mergers can also trigger bar formation
in unbarred discs through induced perturbations in the host galaxy
(e.g. Noguchi 1987; Gerin et al. 1990; Lang et al. 2014; Łokas et al.
2014; Moetazedian et al. 2017), including potentially in our own
Galaxy (Merrow et al. 2024). Miwa & Noguchi (1998), Martinez-
Valpuesta et al. (2017), Gajda et al. (2017) and Bi et al. (2022) find
that tidally triggered bars tend to be slower than their secularly formed
counterparts in simulations. However, Zheng & Shen (2025) argue
that this trend is due to the internal properties of galaxy simulations
which have been set up to require an interaction in order to form their
bar: initial conditions for idealised simulations of tidally-induced
bars are often tuned such that a bar does not form in isolation. Tuning
in this way means that properties like the velocity dispersion of discs
are higher than their secular bar-forming counterparts. Zheng & Shen
(2025) conclude that this bias in disc initial conditions (mainly the
vertical velocity dispersion in the disc, although the circular velocity
in the inner disc differs slightly between models) results in slower
bars in tidal interaction simulations. However, Pettitt & Wadsley
(2018) find no consistent difference in pattern speed between tidally
induced bars and bars formed in isolation, and Peschken & Łokas
(2019) even find that tidally induced bars tend to be slightly faster
than their secularly formed counterparts (in terms of rotation rate ℛ)
in the Illustris cosmological simulation (Vogelsberger et al. 2014).
Observations in Rautiainen et al. (2008), Corsini (2011), Aguerri
et al. (2015) and Guo et al. (2019) point towards most bars in the
nearby Universe being fast, or close to fast. While more recent data
suggest that there may instead be an approximately equal number of
slow and fast bars (Garma-Oehmichen et al. 2022; Géron et al. 2023),
these bars are not extremely slow, with bars slower than ℛ= 2.5 be-
ing relatively uncommon. In contrast, cosmological simulations show
either a lack of bars, as Reddish et al. (2022) shows in the NewHori-
zon simulation (Dubois et al. 2021), or a prevalence of slow bars.
Algorry et al. (2017), Peschken & Łokas (2019), Roshan et al. (2021)
and Habibi et al. (2024) show that the bars in the EAGLE Crain et al.
(2015); Schaye et al. (2015), Illustris (Vogelsberger et al. 2014) and
IllustrisTNG – both TNG100 (Marinacci et al. 2018; Naiman et al.
2018; Nelson et al. 2018; Pillepich et al. 2018; Springel et al. 2018)
and TNG50 (Nelson et al. 2019; Pillepich et al. 2019) – simulations
have rotation rates much slower than those from observations (most
have ℛ> 2.5), and Ansar et al. (2025) find no fast bars (i.e. with
ℛ< 1.4) in the FIRE-2 simulations (Hopkins et al. 2018; Wetzel
et al. 2023) despite lower average rotation rates. Frankel et al. (2022)
attribute the lack of fast bars in the IllustrisTNG simulations to the
bar lengths being too short on average in comparison to observa-
tions, which is exacerbated for low numerical resolution. However,
Fragkoudi et al. (2021) show that the Auriga cosmological zoom-
in simulations do produce fast bars, in keeping with observations.
They argue that these faster bars are because Auriga galaxies have
a higher stellar mass to dark matter mass ratio compared to other
cosmological simulations, which minimises the slow-down impact
from dynamical friction. In this paper, we expand on previous work
(Fragkoudi et al. 2021), by increasing the sample of barred galaxies
for which we explore bar pattern speed to lower masses and also by
exploring how the bar pattern speed evolution is affected by internal
galactic properties and external perturbations, such as interactions
and mergers.
The paper is structured as follows. We first outline the Auriga suite
of simulations in Sec. 2.1 before establishing the measurements we
extract in Sec. 2.2. In Sec. 3.1, we present the time evolution of bar
pattern speeds in our sample, and subsequently examine how they
have been impacted by baryon fraction in Sec. 3.2 and from external
interactions in Sec. 3.3. We discuss the implications and context of
these results in Sec. 4.1 and how they compare to observations in
Sec. 4.2. Lastly, we summarise and conclude our results in Sec. 5.

2 METHODOLOGY
2.1 The Auriga simulations
Throughout this work, we use the Auriga suite of simulations (Grand
et al. 2017, 2019), a set of 39 magneto-hydrodynamical cosmologi-
cal zoom-in simulations of Milky Way mass halos. The initial con-
ditions for the simulations were generated by first identifying halos
with 0.5 < 𝑀200/1012 M⊙< 2 from the 𝑧= 0 snapshot of the dark
matter-only run of the Lambda cold dark matter (ΛCDM) EAGLE
cosmological simulations (Crain et al. 2015; Schaye et al. 2015).
The most isolated quartile of these halos was randomly sampled
(see Grand et al. 2017, for details). Once identified, the resolution
of the dark matter particles of the Lagrangian region of each halo
at 𝑧= 127 is refined to higher resolution, whereas the particle res-
olution outside of this region is progressively worsened to increase
computational efficiency while retaining the large-scale gravitational
tidal field from the Cosmic Web. Dark matter particles are then
split into dark matter-baryon pairs according to cosmological pa-
rameters taken from Planck Collaboration et al. (2014): ℎ= 0.677,
Ωb = 0.048, Ωm = 0.307, and ΩΛ = 0.693. Typical mass resolu-
tions in the highest resolution region are 5 × 104 M⊙for baryons
and 3 × 105 M⊙for dark matter. The dark matter and stars are given
a softening length of 500ℎ−1 cpc up to being fixed at 369 pc from
𝑧= 1 onward. The softening length of a gas cell varies from these
values as a minimum up to a maximum of 1.85 kpc, scaling with the
size of the gas cell. The simulations are run with the moving-mesh
magneto-hydrodynamic code arepo (Springel 2010; Pakmor et al.
2016), which includes a TreePM gravity solver and the auriga sub-
grid galaxy formation physics model. The subgrid physics include:
uniform UV background radiation until 𝑧= 6 (Faucher-Giguère et al.
2009); a subgrid model of the interstellar medium with two phases
(Springel & Hernquist 2003); star particles forming stochastically
out of gas denser than 0.1 atoms cm−3 representing simple stellar
populations; primordial and metal line cooling; stellar evolution with
feedback from type Ia and type II supernovae and AGB stars; black
hole seeding with subsequent accretion and AGN feedback; a model
of magnetic fields (Pakmor et al. 2014, 2017). For full details of the
auriga code, see Vogelsberger et al. (2013), Marinacci et al. (2014)
and Grand et al. (2017). For further information on the details of the
Auriga simulations as a whole, see Grand et al. (2017) and references
therein.
The initial conditions of our simulations are the same as those
of the Original/4 and LowMassMws/4 suites described in Grand
et al. (2024)1. However, our analysis requires a much finer snapshot
cadence than these simulations provide in order to accurately measure
the patterns speeds of bars. Therefore, we re-simulated each halo that
produces a barred galaxy at 𝑧= 0 (according to Fragkoudi et al. 2025)
with additional 5 Myr-cadence snapshots of the stellar particles only.
These higher cadence outputs, which we call “snipshots”, allow us to
compute pattern speed directly from the change in the phase angle of
the bar without using any assumptions such as those required for the
commonly used Tremaine-Weinberg method (Tremaine & Weinberg
1984b). In addition, we approximately double the number of full
snapshots (which contain dark matter particles, gas cells, black holes,
and tracer particles as well as star particles) such that their cadence
becomes ∼60 Myr at late times2. These snapshots are accompanied

Although, following the definition above, the bar formation time
is known to within half a snipshot, the definition itself is somewhat
arbitrary due to the selection of 𝐴2,max cut-off. Therefore, we ex-
amine the sensitivity of the bar formation time by varying the limit
from 𝐴2,max by 10% to see how the change affects the resultant bar
formation time. We find that the bar formation time is changed by
less than 2.5% for the oldest 10 bars, with only 5 others (Au12, Au20,
Au28, AuL2, and AuL10) changing by more than 0.5 Gyr. Each of
these 5 bars is younger than 3 Gyr and have bar strengths at 𝑧= 0
weaker than 0.31. We conclude that our definition of bar formation
time is stable for older, stronger bars, but is sensitive to the choice of
limits for weak and young bars.
Note that we make 3 exceptions to our definition of bar formation
time: Au17 which forms its bar extremely early in its evolution, Au23
which periodically produces snipshots with 𝐴2,max < 0.2 due to a

formation time until a bar has become visually present in the face-on
stellar distribution, since prominent, consistent spirals which reach
to the inner 6 kpc lead to a consistently high ‘bar strength’ before a
genuine bar has formed.

In addition to these parameters, in what follows we also use the
virial mass, the stellar mass within 20 kpc, and the baryon dominance
within one effective radius. The last of these is calculated as the ratio
of the circular velocity at the effective radius induced by the enclosed
stellar mass to the circular velocity at the effective radius from the
total enclosed mass. Many of these parameters vary apparently ran-
domly on short timescales. For most parameters, we account for their
dispersion by taking the mean value across 200 Myr (40 snipshots),
with the standard deviation from the same interval giving a basis for
the error bars shown below – these error bars are propagated through
as uncertainties for derived quantities. Since we do not calculate bar
length for every snipshot, we treat these error bars slightly differently
and describe this in Appendix A.
2.2.2 Galaxy interaction parameters
To parametrise the accretion and interaction history of each Auriga
halo, we use the masses, positions, and velocities from their merger

affect rotation rates in galaxies below 6 × 1010 M⊙. Indeed, we find
Spearman correlation coefficients with ℛ0 of -0.57 for the abundance
matching offset and -0.45 for the stellar mass. As such, the rotation
rate appears to be more directly dependent on the relative masses of
the galaxy and its dark matter halo than the stellar mass alone.
The trend with stellar mass acts as a prediction of the Auriga
model that can be tested with observations. In particular, this provides
a test of ΛCDM cosmology, since the cause of slower bars in less
massive spiral galaxies appears to be lower fractions of baryons. Since
baryon fraction is not meaningful in cosmology models without dark
matter (e.g. in a Modified Newtonian Dynamics framework; Milgrom
1983), we would not expect to see a dependence of rotation rate on
galaxy mass in such models. Indeed, bars in galaxies with masses
109 −1011 M⊙in Nagesh et al. (2023) under modified Newtonian
gravity (MOND) have ℛ∼1.6 for each of 3 simulations. While
a direct comparison with Auriga is unavailable due to a lack of
cosmological simulations with MOND and a comparatively small
number of isolated examples, this indicates that simulations using
MOND produce relatively fast bars at all masses. We compare our
relation with stellar mass to those found in observations in Sec. 4.2.
3.2.2 Baryon dominance in the inner regions
Bars are structures of the inner few kpc of the stellar disc, and as
such their formation and evolution is especially closely tied to the
properties of the host galaxy in its inner region, in particular the
relative properties of the stars and the dark matter. We quantify the
relative contributions of stars and dark matter to the mass of the
inner galaxy using the ratio of the circular velocity due to the stellar
component within 1 effective radius of the centre of the halo, to the
circular velocity from the total mass within the same region. We refer
to this as the baryon dominance. The effective radius for the simulated
galaxies in our sample is in the region of 2 −10 kpc, matching the
range of bar lengths as shown in Fig. 3. This definition of baryon
dominance therefore describes the prevalence of stars compared to
dark matter in the inner bar-forming regions.

4 DISCUSSION
We first discuss the physical processes that may lie behind the low,
decreasing bar rotation rates in Auriga, given the correlations with
baryon dominance and interaction strength presented above, and how
these compare to the literature. We then discuss the extent to which
the trends with bar rotation rate above are supported by observational
studies, in particular those found with both bar strength and galaxy
stellar mass.
4.1 Decreasing rotation rates
The prevalent mode of evolution for the bars in the Auriga suite ap-
pears to be lengthening without significant slowing of pattern speed,
resulting in bars which speed up in rotation rate through their life-
times (see Fig. 4). While an increasing bar length is expected for bars
evolving secularly, the expectation from theory and tailored simu-
lations is that their pattern speeds, and therefore rotation rates, will
slow down, primarily due to dynamical friction from the dark matter
halo (Weinberg 1985; Little & Carlberg 1991; Debattista & Sellwood
2000; Athanassoula 2003). In some cases, the increase in corotation
radius from a slowing pattern speed is not sufficient to outpace the
increase in bar length, resulting in the bar maintaining a fast rotation
rate, i.e., low ℛ(e.g. O’Neill & Dubinski 2003; Martinez-Valpuesta
et al. 2006). Additionally, Sellwood & Debattista (2006) find that the
presence of a gravitational perturbation which increases the pattern
speed briefly – such as an interaction with an external galaxy – can
halt the slow down from dynamical friction for a few Gyr. However,
they also show that this state of ‘metastability’ is sensitive to further
perturbations, which are frequent in a cosmological setting. There-
fore, the metastable state is unlikely to last long in reality, allowing
slow down from dynamical friction to resume.
In this study, we do not seem to find dynamical friction acting
as a persistent, gradual mechanism, which would slow down bars
on long timescales. In contrast, we find that dynamical friction is
likely acting on much shorter timescales, since Fig. 6 and Fig. 7
show that bar rotation rates are mainly dependent on the ratio of the
stellar to dark matter (with more baryon dominated galaxies hosting
faster bars) at formation time, as well as at subsequent times. This
trend is established by 𝑡i (just 0.5 Gyr after the bar forms), which is a
much shorter timescale than the 5−10 Gyr found in Little & Carlberg
(1991), Debattista & Sellwood (2000), Athanassoula (2003), O’Neill
& Dubinski (2003) and Sellwood & Debattista (2006) for dynamical
friction, potentially on the same timescale as bar formation. Instead,
the correlation of rotation rate and baryon dominance which we see in
our sample may suggest the fast-acting dynamical friction originally
described in Weinberg (1985) which acts over a few bar rotation
times (roughly 0.1 −1 Gyr here) for bars that form fast.
In addition to acting over a short timescale, dynamical friction is

Interactions with other galaxies are an unlikely main candidate for
speeding up bars in Auriga, since the associated trends in Sec. 3.3
are relatively weak, and there is no evidence for tidal forces during
an interaction reliably increasing the pattern speed of a bar, instead
showing a mixture of speeding and slowing the bar as in e.g. Gerin
et al. (1990), Łokas et al. (2014) and Semczuk et al. (2024). Panel
(a) in Fig. B1 does, however, indicate a correlation between stronger
interactions around the time of bar formation and greater changes in
baryon dominance between 𝑡i and 𝑧= 0. This correlation may be
linked to inflows of gas which could increase the bar rotation rate,
or higher rates of gas accretion onto the galaxy which could add
angular momentum to the disc and the bar. Both mechanisms require
a lasting effect on the radial motion of gas in order to cause a speed
up of the bar over its lifetime, consistently maintaining the influence
of the interaction for at least 1 Gyr after its pericentre.
Further clues may come from comparison with other cosmological
simulations in which the speeding up of bars seen here are not present.
As mentioned in Sec. 1, most cosmological simulations which form
bars tend to produce bars which are too slow, particularly in rotation
rate (Algorry et al. 2017; Peschken & Łokas 2019; Roshan et al.
2021; Habibi et al. 2024; Ansar et al. 2025), potentially as a result
of excessive slow down from dynamical friction (significant slow
down is seen in Algorry et al. 2017; Habibi et al. 2024) or a lack
of lengthening through their evolution (Frankel et al. 2022). These
studies examine bar rotation rate in 5 different simulations, which we
compare with Auriga in turn.
In Illustris (Vogelsberger et al. 2014), Peschken & Łokas (2019)
find a mean bar rotation rate of 5.6 ± 3.8. In EAGLE (Crain et al.
2015; Schaye et al. 2015), Algorry et al. (2017) find rotation rates
of 1.4 −10 for the strong bars in EAGLE Ref-L100N1504 (the
100 cMpc box run). Additionally, Roshan et al. (2021) find mean
rotation rates of 2.4+1.1
−0.8 in EAGLE Ref-L100N1504 and 1.9+1.7

simulations, Fragkoudi et al. (2021) find that the higher values of
baryon dominance found in Auriga galaxies are the likely reason for
its faster bars. Increased baryon dominance in the inner regions of
galaxies can significantly reduce the effects of dynamical friction
(Debattista & Sellwood 1998) and allow the bar to lengthen without
significantly decreasing pattern speed.
In the IllustrisTNG simulations, Roshan et al. (2021) find mean

have lower baryon dominance than in Auriga, with TNG100 being
comparable with the original Illustris and TNG50 being slightly more
baryon dominated but still less so than Auriga. Therefore, following
the conclusions of Fragkoudi et al. (2021), this likely accounts for
the most of the difference in bar rotation rates.
Since TNG50 shows less of a difference in baryon dominance
with Auriga than the other simulations mentioned, it is worthwhile to
consider other factors which may contribute to the wider difference in
rotation rates. Firstly, the lower resolution of a full-box cosmological
simulation may artificially slow down the bar due to stochasticity
(Hamilton et al. 2023), although Fragkoudi et al. (2021) do investigate
the effects of resolution on bar pattern speeds within Auriga and find
that the effects of resolution alone are not enough to explain the
differences between Auriga and EAGLE or Illustris.
Additionally, Li et al. (2023) find from tailored N-body simula-
tions that a high value of dark matter halo spin can halt slowdown
from dynamical friction. Milky Way-mass halos in TNG50 have spin
parameters in the range 0.02 −0.07, skewing towards lower values
for barred galaxies (Rosas-Guevara et al. 2022), whereas all original
Auriga halos have spin parameters above 0.08 (Grand et al. 2017)
– this is in the regime where Li et al. (2023) find spin to halt bar
slowdown for up to 5 Gyr. It is also notable that Habibi et al. (2024)
find that 2 of the 3 bars in TNG50 which have sped up significantly
are also in the minority that experience major mergers after 𝑧= 1,
pointing towards galaxy interactions playing a key role in speeding
up bars. We see a similar, albeit very weak, trend in Sec. 3.3.
Lastly, in the FIRE-2 simulations (Hopkins et al. 2018; Wetzel
et al. 2023), Ansar et al. (2025) find bar rotation rates of 1.6 −6.5.
Examining the stellar masses and halos masses from Hopkins et al.
(2018), we see that FIRE-2 also has lower baryon dominance than
Auriga, providing an explanation for its slower bars. We also note
that the bars in FIRE-2 are very weak, with none stronger than
𝐴2,max = 0.21 (only this strongest bar would be considered a barred
galaxy in this work). Ansar et al. (2025) find that this is likely a result
of an initially high fraction of central gas triggering extreme stellar
feedback processes. Since we find that rotation rate is negatively
correlated with bar strength, we would expect such weak bars to also
be slow in Auriga. Indeed, the strongest bar in FIRE-2 also has the
lowest rotation rate.
Across these 5 other cosmological simulations, we see that the
disc galaxy population in Auriga has a higher baryon dominance
than each, in addition to significantly lower average bar rotation
rates. This indicates that having a high baryon dominance is the most
important factor in forming fast bars within cosmological simula-
tions. Other ingredients, potentially including properties of merger
histories, feedback physics and dark matter halo spin, may contribute
to increasing the fraction of fast bars formed.
4.2 Comparison with observations
While there are significant challenges of observationally measuring
bar pattern speeds (e.g. Debattista 2003; Zou et al. 2019; Garma-
Oehmichen et al. 2020), here we discuss some of the studies that
have explored bar pattern speeds and compared these to different
parameters. In particular, we compare our results of trends with bar
strength and with stellar mass, since these are measured more easily
in observations than the other quantities used in this paper.
We first address the correlation between bar strength and rotation
rate. Rautiainen et al. (2008) used models of the rotating bar po-
tential, fitted based on the response features produced by numerical
simulations of the disc, to find bar pattern speeds for 38 𝑧∼0 bright
spiral galaxies from the Ohio State University Bright Spiral Galaxy
Survey (Eskridge et al. 2002). Although using a different measure of
bar strength than that used in this paper – the ratio of tangential and
radial forces exerted by the bar potential in their best fit model – they
find that all but one of the slow (ℛ> 1.4) bars in their sample have
a bar strength less than 0.3. This matches the tendency for slow bars

Other works find no such relation between rotation rate and bar
strength in observations. Guo et al. (2019) also use the Tremaine-

Cuomo et al. (2020) adds 18 bars from the literature and 31 bars

these studies agree with each other, given the large overlap in galaxy

they find corotation radii by assuming that the rotation curve of the
disc very quickly flattens to a constant circular velocity. Rautiainen
et al. (2008) and Géron et al. (2023), the two studies which do see

the rotation curve for a (potentially) more accurate corotation radius.
In summary, although not all observations support a correlation be-
tween bar strength and rotation rate, the relatively large sample and
accurate method for measuring the corotation radius of Géron et al.
(2023) is compelling evidence for a correlation.
The correlation between stellar mass and bar rotation rate is of
particular interest since, as mentioned in Sec. 3.2.1, it provides a
test of ΛCDM cosmology. The correlation could be caused by the
underlying trend of higher stellar mass galaxies having higher baryon
dominance. Due to the lack of dark matter, in a Modified Newtonian
Dynamics framework (Milgrom 1983), we would not expect to see a
dependence of rotation rate on galaxy mass in such scenarios (since
there is no trend or change in ‘baryon-dominance’). Indeed, the study
of Nagesh et al. (2023) explored bars in a MOND framework, show-
ing that galaxies with masses 109 −1011 M⊙all have similar rotation
rates of ℛ∼1.6.
In observations, Géron et al. (2023) show a weak negative cor-
relation between bar rotation rate and stellar mass in their Fig. 13.
This figure shows that weak bars tend to have higher rotation rates in
lower mass galaxies for 𝑀★≲3 × 1010 M⊙. Above this mass, bars

Two other works examine the relation between bar rotation rate
and stellar mass: Garma-Oehmichen et al. (2020) which investigates
18 galaxies from the MaNGA and CALIFA surveys, and Garma-
Oehmichen et al. (2022) which applies the Tremaine-Weinberg
method to 97 galaxies from the MaNGA survey with masses and mor-
phology similar to the Milky Way. Both studies find no correlation
between rotation rate and stellar mass. We note, however, that both
studies have a smaller sample size than Géron et al. (2023), especially
Garma-Oehmichen et al. (2020) which only finds a convergent pat-
tern speed for 13 galaxies due to its focus on galaxies which present
challenges to the Tremaine-Weinberg method. Garma-Oehmichen
et al. (2022) also has a lower stellar mass limit of 2 × 1010 M⊙–
this would likely hide a trend like in Géron et al. (2023) which only
appears below 3×1010 M⊙, especially since the relation is presented
as a correlation coefficient only.
Guo et al. (2019) additionally investigates the dark matter fraction
within an effective radius, the inverse of baryon dominance. In their
Fig. 11, there is a weak negative correlation between rotation rate
and dark matter fraction (more slow bars have higher dark matter
fractions, corresponding to lower baryon dominance) in the discs
with the most reliable position angles. This is in agreement with
our results in Fig. 7, although the observations show a much weaker
correlation.
The relationships between bar rotation rate and bar strength, stellar
mass, and baryon dominance found in some observations are consis-
tent with our findings. While not all observational studies agree with
our work, those that do have either have larger sample sizes or fewer
assumptions in calculating their rotation rates, which lends tentative
support to the bar evolution in the Auriga simulations, the baryon
physics involved, and the ΛCDM cosmology producing it. Future
observations may clarify the picture currently presented, allowing
the trends we have presented here to give a more robust test of bar
evolution in Auriga. In particular, studies of bars in lower mass disc
galaxies may provide a crucial test for the relations that we find.
5 CONCLUSIONS
In this paper, we investigated the internal and external physical pro-
cesses that set the bar pattern speed and drive its evolution in simu-
lated Milky Way-mass galaxies. We used 27 high temporal resolution
reruns from the Auriga suite of magneto-hydrodynamic cosmologi-
cal zoom-in simulations to compare bar rotation rates, ℛ(the ratio
of corotation radius to bar length), with bar formation times and
strengths, as well as with the baryon/dark matter composition and
interaction histories of their host galaxies.
We summarise our conclusions as follows:
• All but one of the bars in the Auriga suite of simulations decrease
in rotation rate from their formation to the present day, largely due
to an increase in bar length and approximately constant corotation
radius (e.g. Fig. 3 and Fig. 4). This speed-up in ℛis unexpected when
compared to the literature, and implies that any dynamical friction
acts on short timescales, having little effect following 0.5 Gyr after
the bar forms.
• Younger (older) bars tend to have a slower (faster) rotation rate
(ratio of corotation radius to bar length) by the end of the simulation
(Fig. 4). Younger bars are also slower than older bars shortly after the
bar formation time, suggesting that the mechanism responsible for
the difference acts mostly within the first 0.5 Gyr of bar evolution.
• Bars that are stronger at 𝑧= 0 tend to be faster (Fig. 5). This is
a relation that can be tested in the nearby Universe and is confirmed
in some, but not all, observational studies.
• Galaxies with higher stellar masses tend to host faster bars
(Fig. 6). This relation acts as a test of the ΛCDM model of cosmology,
since it seems to be driven by the higher fractions of stellar to dark
matter in high mass galaxies. This relation has so far been observed
in weak bars at stellar masses below 3 × 1010 M⊙.
• The ratio of stellar to dark matter (or baryon dominance) within
galaxies appears to be the strongest factor in setting the rotation rate
at both the start and end of the lifetime of their bars up to 𝑧= 0.
Regardless of epoch, bars in more baryon dominated environments
are faster (Fig. 6 and Fig. 7); additionally, bars tend to speed up when
their host galaxies increase in baryon dominance (Fig. 8).

• Galaxy interactions at the time of bar formation have little imme-
diate effect on pattern speed, however bars formed alongside stronger
interactions tend to speed up more through their life and become
slightly faster by 𝑧= 0 (Fig. 9). The mechanisms behind this are un-
clear, but may relate to the trends with baryon dominance – possibly
due to interactions driving in-situ baryons towards the galactic centre
or by adding gas to the system directly. Regardless of mechanism,
this contradicts the expectation from literature that tidally formed
bars are slower on average.
• There is a weak trend between stronger galaxy interactions af-
ter the bar has formed and a greater decrease in bar rotation rate
(Fig. 10). This effect is, however, minimal when compared to the
other correlations presented here.
The Auriga simulations give an insight into how fast bars in Milky
Way-mass halos can arise in cosmological simulations. A high baryon
dominance in the inner regions appears to be crucial, as do relatively
early-forming bars. Comparison with bars in other cosmological sim-
ulations supports the need for high baryon dominance in order to
produce fast bars in Milky Way-mass discs. Having this baryon dom-
inance likely counters dynamical friction in Auriga to stop these bars
slowing down excessively by present day and even allowing the ma-
jority to speed up. Our study highlights the potential constraining
power of bar rotation rates on the nature of dark matter and baryonic
physics.

APPENDIX A: BAR LENGTH MEASUREMENT
This section gives a description of the method we use to calculate
the bar lengths in our sample. Fig. A1 illustrates an example of this
procedure for Au24 at 𝑧= 0, in the final snapshot of its simulation.
After aligning the disc to the x-y plane and the bar to the x-axis
as described in Sec. 2.2.1, we find the contours to the face-on stellar
surface density. We show every 10th calculated contour in the upper
left panel of Fig. A1. For each contour level, we fit an ellipse to the
longest closed loop, centred on the origin, as shown in the upper right
panel in blue.
The lower two panels show the eccentricity and angle from the
aligned bar axis respectively, against the semi-major axis of each
ellipse up to 12 kpc. The bar length is then the minimum of three
radii: the first minimum in eccentricity outside the visually strong
bar region, the first turning point in angle outside the visually strong
bar region, and the first deviation in angle of more than 15◦/ 𝜋
12 rad
from the bar axis (excluding the inner 1 kpc which shows little bar
signal). These three radii are shown in Fig. A1 as the middle, right,
and left vertical dashed lines respectively. In this case, we find a bar
length of 6.5 kpc, corresponding to the left-most dashed line.
