What’s it like to be a chat? On the co-simulation
of artificial minds in human-AI conversations
Geoff Keeling and Winnie Street
Institute of Philosophy
School of Advanced Study
University of London
January 2026
Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) can simulate person-like things which
at least appear to have stable behavioural and psychological dispositions.
Call these things characters. Are characters minded and psychologically
continuous entities with mental states like beliefs, desires and intentions?
Illusionists about characters say No. On this view, characters are merely
anthropomorphic projections in the mind of the user and so lack men-
tal states. Jonathan Birch (2025) defends this view. He says that the
distributed nature of LLM processing, in which several LLMs may be
implicated in the simulation of a character in a single conversation, pre-
cludes the existence of a persistent minded entity that is identifiable with
the character. Against illusionism, we argue for a realist position on which
characters exist as minded and psychologically continuous entities. Our
central point is that Birch’s argument for illusionism rests on a category
error: characters are not internal to the LLMs that simulate them, but
rather are co-simulated by LLMs and users, emerging in a shared conversa-
tional workspace through a process of mutual theory of mind modelling.
We argue that characters, and their minds, exist as ‘real patterns’ on
grounds that attributing mental states to characters is essential for mak-
ing efficient and accurate predictions about the conversational dynamics
(c.f. Dennett, 1991).
Furthermore, because the character exists within
the conversational workspace rather than within the LLM, psychological
continuity is preserved even when the underlying computational substrate
is distributed across multiple LLM instances.

Introduction
Much contemporary work on AI minds centres on Large Language Models
(LLMs) as the object of study. We can ask whether LLMs have beliefs and
desires, for example, or whether they are conscious (Chalmers, 2023; Goldstein

attention has been paid to characters, where characters are entities that are
simulated or role-played by LLMs, and which at least appear to be person-like
things with stable behavioural and psychological dispositions (Shanahan et al.,
2023).1 A tempting view is that characters do not exist: they are merely illu-
sory, and so not among the kinds of entities that can have mental states like
beliefs and desires, or mental capacities like consciousness (Birch, 2025). Call
this view illusionism about characters.2
We here develop an alternative view which we call realism about characters.
According to realism, characters exist, have mental states such as beliefs, desires
and intentions, and are psychologically continuous in roughly the sense that
relevant causal relations obtain between earlier and later mental states, such as
an experience at one time causing a memory at some later time (c.f. Parfit, 1987,
205-6). The upshot of realism is that, if true, it vindicates the commonsense
perception that the thing we are talking to when we use an LLM is a minded
entity (Grzankowski et al., 2025), although we remain neutral on the question
of whether characters are phenomenally conscious minded entities.
Our main contention is that the considerations that motivate illusionism are
based on a category error. It is a mistake to think that characters, and their
minds, are intrinsic to LLMs, such that, as Jonathan Birch (2025) has argued, a
character cannot exhibit psychological continuity if multiple distinct LLMs are
involved in the simulation of the character (as is typically the case with plat-
forms like ChatGPT). Rather, characters are co-simulated by users and LLMs.
Characters, and their minds, emerge in the dynamic interplay between users
and LLMs through a process of mutual modelling in which both the user and
LLM employ theory of mind—the attribution of mental states—to predict the
behaviour of their interlocutor and to render their own behaviour predictable.
Hence the mind of the character is not intrinsic to any particular LLM, but is
rather an emergent phenomenon instantiated across the distributed substrate
of the user’s brain and the computational processing of one or several LLMs.
We argue that characters exist as minded and psychologically continuous
entities on the basis of their being ‘real patterns’ (Dennett, 1989, 1991). Much
like a ‘glider’ in John Conway’s Game of Life (which is realised by but dis-
tinct from the underlying grid of pixels), a character is a necessary element

The Case for Illusionism
According to
Illusionism about characters, LLMs can induce in users the impres-
sion of person-like characters with psychological continuity and sta-
ble behavioural dispositions. But impressions of this kind are illu-
sory. Characters, so understood, do not exist.3
The illusion of characters is said to arise from a combination of two factors.
First, the human tendency to engage in anthropomorphism, that is, the attribu-
tion of human-like qualities to non-human entities. Second, the anthropomimetic
qualities of LLMs—that is, their tendency to ‘process information and interact
with users in increasingly humanlike ways’ (Shevlin). Here anthropomimetic
qualities include, inter alia, the ability of LLMs to generate text in a way that
appears to be indicative of their having beliefs, desires and emotions, and the
ability of LLMs to engage in distinctly human-like forms of reasoning such as
theory of mind (c.f. Bender et al., 2021; Akbulut et al., 2024; Manzini et al.,
2024). Illusionism, if true, is big news. As Birch (2025, 3) claims, illusionism

We are unconvinced. In a broad class of cases, while multiple LLMs are
implicated in the simulation of a given character as part of a dialogue with a
user, the LLMs in question are distinct runtime instances of the same model.
That is to say that the models are functional duplicates of one another: all
relevant architectural features, including the arrangement of artificial neurons
and model weights, are held fixed across runtime instances. If a user begins the
dialogue using model instance M to simulate character C, and then switches
to a functional duplicate M’ to simulate character C’, transferring the prior
context from M to M’, then there is no functional difference between the com-
putational processing of the text in the first part of the conversation compared
to the second. The two model instances are identical computationally and so
have the same dispositions with respect to generating text from the (shared)
conversational context. Whatever accounts for the psychological continuity of
C in the part of the conversation for which M is used also applies in the part
of the conversation for which M’ is used.
Hence we can grant that the doctor and their successor in Birch’s case would
lack psychological continuity while denying that characters C and C’ above
lack psychological continuity. Clinical notes and conversational transcripts do
not determine the psychological continuity of doctors.
If the second doctor
were a functional duplicate of the first with identical memories and identical
psychological and behavioural dispositions, as is the case with two runtime
instances of the same LLM, then it would presumably be the case that the
second doctor is psychologically continuous with the first. The two cases are
therefore disanalogous, at least with respect to runtime instances of the same
model.

not those states exhibit psychological continuity.7 Here the idea is to suggest
that whatever determines psychological continuity over time is not the com-
putations that a model performs when processing text, but rather something
extra-computational. This move is unattractive because denying computational

level and make a case for realism about characters wholesale.
3
From Simulation to Co-Simulation
In this section, we develop a case for realism about characters. Recall that, ac-
cording to realism, characters exist as minded entities that exhibit psychological
continuity over the course of a given conversation with a user. Furthermore, psy-
chological continuity holds even if multiple LLMs, or multiple routes through
the same MoE model, are employed in the simulation of the character.
First consider,
Birch’s Big Mistake:
The minds of characters, if they exist, are
internal to the LLM that is simulating the character (and in the case
of MoE models, the minds of characters, if they exist, are internal
to a particular route through the model).
Birch’s Big Mistake underwrites the intuition that, if multiple LLMs are
implicated in the simulation of a given character, then that character cannot
be psychologically continuous. For it is hard to see how one psychologically
continuous character can exist if multiple LLMs contribute to the simulation of
the character and the mind of the character at any particular time is internal
to the simulating LLM.
We maintain that characters are co-simulated by LLMs and users. The sim-
ulation of characters is performed by the human user and LLM operating in
a particular configuration with respect to each other. The idea is to situate
the character’s mind—and its psychological continuity—in the dynamical inter-
play between the user and the LLM as opposed to thinking that the character’s
7

mind is internal to the LLM. Because characters are not internal to some par-
ticular LLM but rather emerge within the dynamical interplay between users
and LLMs, it is possible for characters to exhibit psychological continuity even
if multiple LLMs contribute to the simulation of the character. Or so we shall
argue.
Our argument is analogical. We argue in Section 3.1 that humans engaged in
imaginative role-play games such as Dungeons and Dragons (D&D) co-simulate
minded and psychologically continuous characters that are distinct from the
players involved in the game. We then argue in Section 3.2 that similar consider-
ations can be leveraged to establish the mindedness and psychological continuity
of characters arising in the context of human-LLM conversations.
3.1
Co-Simulated Characters in Role-Playing Games
Humans engage in the co-simulation of characters in imaginative role-playing
games—a phenomenon observed in both children and adults (Kapitany et al.,
2022).8
Consider,
Dungeons and Dragons: Two human players, Roberta and Callum,
are, respectively, playing the Ruthless Queen and Cadenza the Bard
in a game of D&D. The Ruthless Queen is characterised by her
general intolerance, her tendency to wax lyrical about how things
were better before they let bards into court, and her tendency to
snap at Cadenza every time he speaks out of turn. Cadenza the
Bard is characterised by his subservient nature, his desire to follow
the Ruthless Queen wherever she goes, and his tendency to respond
to every question with a meandering philosophical treatise on the
meaning of life. Roberta and Callum bring these characters to life
through their behaviour, including speech acts, rolls of the dice, and
through vocal affectations and behavioural mannerisms.
In this case, Roberta and Callum are co-simulating the characters of the
Ruthless Queen and Cadenza the Bard (see Figure 1). The co-simulation is
rooted in an ‘imagined workspace,’ which ‘[Roberta and Callum] collectively
share, react to, influence, and elaborate upon,’ through game play (Kapitany
et al., 2022, 3; see also Nichols and Stich, 2000).
Each player maintains a
folk-psychological model for their own character, but also for their co-player’s
character as well. To illustrate: Because Roberta and Callum have a shared (or

We think that the characters of the Ruthless Queen and Cadenza exist as

Why? There is a plausible interpretationist case for the existence and mind-
edness of characters, where interpretationism is roughly the view that a system
has mental states just in case the attribution of those states allows for the effi-
cient, robust, accurate prediction of the system’s behaviour (see e.g. Goldstein
and Lederman, 2025). Specifically, we can substantiate the existence and mind-

edness of characters through Daniel Dennett’s (1991) idea of ‘real patterns.’
For Dennett, the criterion for the reality of a pattern is information-theoretic:
a pattern exists in some data if and only if there is a description of that data
that is more efficient than the ‘bit map’—a verbatim description of the physical
substrate. A system has mental states, on Dennett’s view, when the attribution
of such mental states allows for the efficient, accurate and robust prediction of
the system’s behaviour. Such mental states constitute real patterns, enabling
better predictions than lower-level physical explanations.
In the context of our D&D example, the ‘bit map’ is the distributed neural
activity of both Roberta and Callum, combined with the stochastic mechanics
of the game. While causally complete, this description is computationally in-
tractable. To predict the system—for example, to predict how Callum (as the
Bard) will respond to Roberta (as the Queen)—one must adopt the intentional
stance towards both characters, attributing them to mental states and mak-
ing folk psychological inferences based on those mental states. The characters’
psychology is a necessary informational bridge between the players.
To illustrate: Callum cannot efficiently determine the Bard’s next move by
monitoring Roberta’s neural states, but he can do so by tracking the Queen’s
desire to maintain courtly order. The Queen is therefore a real pattern because
she features in the compressed representation that allows Callum to generate
appropriate responses. Without positing the Queen’s mental states as distinct
variables in the system, the causal chain between Roberta’s speech acts and
Callum’s reactions becomes a sequence of random noise. The Queen is real in
the same way a ‘glider’ in Conway’s Game of Life is real: distinct from the
underlying grid of pixels that instantiates it and necessary for making efficient
predictions.
Several points require clarification here. First, we take it that Roberta’s
brain is the generator function for the character of the Ruthless Queen (mutatis
mutandis for Callum’s brain and Cadenza). What this means is that Roberta’s
brain generates actions for the Queen. But merely generating actions on behalf
of a character is insufficient for being a generator function. For it is possible
that rolling a die could fulfil that role. What is important is that Roberta’s
brain maintains a folk psychological model of the Queen that captures her be-
liefs, desires and intentions alongside her broader psychological and behavioural
dispositions. This model enables Roberta’s brain to generate actions for the
Queen that are consistent with the Queen’s character, although Roberta’s brain
has some leeway to engage in surprising behaviours. Surprising behaviours of
this kind provide an error signal which underwrites updates to the shared (or at
least overlapping) folk psychological models of the Queen in both Callum and
Roberta’s brains. In this way, Roberta’s involvement in the simulation of the
Queen is active, and Callum’s involvement is actively responsive. Both parties
are cognitively engaged in the simulation.
Second, co-simulation is an inherently social activity, such that Callum’s
brain is not merely responsive to the Queen’s actions, but also an important
part of the simulation (mutatis mutandis for Roberta’s brain and Cadenza’s
actions).
Callum’s brain maintains a folk psychological model of the Queen

that updates in response to surprising actions performed by the Queen. The
psychology of the Queen is thus, in an important sense, public, in that both
Roberta and Callum keep independent records with respect to what the Queen
believes, desires and intends, and how she is disposed to think and act in different
kinds of situations. This mutual record keeping serves an important regulatory
function in that Roberta’s brain is not licensed to generate the Queen’s actions
whimsically, but is constrained to generate actions that broadly cohere with the
shared understanding of the Queen’s character. Deviating too far from that
shared understanding will result in the Queen being unpredictable to Cadenza
and for the Queen’s character to collapse into incoherence. In this respect, the
continued existence of the Queen as a character depends on the policing function
of Cadenza’s expectations (which are grounded in the folk psychological model
maintained by Callum’s brain). In this way, the Queen, as a character, exists
in the shared imaginative workspace which is realised by Roberta and Callum’s
brains engaging in the active co-simulation of the Queen as a character within
the game.
Third, the Queen’s psychology is distinct from that of Roberta’s (mutatis
mutandis for Callum and Cadenza). Predicting the game based on Roberta’s
own beliefs, desires and intentions would lead to catastrophic prediction errors.
If Roberta is polite and kind, then predicting the Queen’s actions based on these
psychological dispositions would lead to error, as the Queen, unlike Roberta, is
ruthless.
Because the Queen-pattern is characterised by an independent set
of beliefs, desires and intentions, and acts in accordance with a distinctive set
of psychological and behavioural dispositions, she satisfies the condition for
existing as a distinct, psychologically continuous locus of agency. We note, here,
that the psychological separation between, for example, Roberta and the Queen,
may not be watertight. There can exist emotional spillover, or ‘bleed’ effects
between players and their characters in both directions (Leonard, 2018). The
cognitive or affective qualities of the player may ‘bleed-out’ into the character
(e.g. if a player’s acrophobia unintentionally influences their character’s actions)
and the character’s cognitive or affective qualities may ‘bleed-out’ into the player
(e.g. if a character experiences pain or grief, the player may feel it too). Even so,
the existence of ‘bleed’ does not warrant collapsing the ontology of the character
into that of the player. While the phenomenon indicates that the player and
character may share certain dispositional traits (such as a specific phobia or a
transient emotional state), the Queen’s behaviour is predictable with reference
to the Queen’s psychology and not Roberta’s.
3.2
Characters in User-LLM Conversations are Minded
and Psychologically Continuous
We now extend our interpretationist framework to characters arising in user-
LLM conversations. Recall Birch’s point: because the computational substrate
of the conversation may be distributed across multiple distinct model instances
(or distinct routes through an MoE model), there is no single, persistent entity
‘on the back end’ to ground the character’s psychological continuity.
Birch
11


## Page 12


argues that any appearance of continuity is merely an illusion projected by the
user onto a fragmented process.
Birch’s idea rests on a category error regarding the ontology of LLM char-
acters. Just as the Ruthless Queen is a real pattern distinct from Roberta and
partially dependent upon the cognitive engagement of Callum, the AI character
is a real pattern distinct from the specific LLM instance processing any given
turn of the conversation and partially reliant upon the cognitive engagement of
the user.
In the D&D case, the character of the Queen is co-simulated by the players.
Her psychological continuity is maintained not because Roberta’s brain is in a
single continuous state, but because the informational state of the game—the
shared workspace—is passed forward through time, a process that runs on both
Roberta’s brain and Callum’s. The character persists as a virtual entity running
on the hardware of both players’ brains, emerging though the dynamic process
of mutual modelling that sustains the game, and governed by the internal logic
of the game narrative.
The situation in user-LLM conversations is structurally identical (see Figure
2). When a user interacts with a character, they are engaging in a process of
co-simulation. The user has a folk-psychological model of the character, and
the LLM provides the behavioural feedback signal that updates and refines that
model. That behavioural signal is constrained by the LLM’s folk psychological
model of the character, in addition to its folk psychological model of the user
(and in particular, the user’s expectations of how the character will behave,
that may in various ways manifest in the user’s behaviour).
In this regard,
the user’s expectations play an important policing function in constraining the
LLM’s generation of the character’s behaviour. In this way, the character is
an emergent phenomenon that exists in the shared conversational workspace
between the user and the LLM which is sustained by mutual modelling on both
sides in addition to the record of the conversation.
When the conversation history is passed from one LLM instance to another
(or from one expert sub-network to another), the new model instance receives
the full causal history of the character—their beliefs, desires, and memories—as
well as the history of other characters’ reactions to the model’s character, and
computes the next action based on that history. In this sense, the distinct LLM
instances function merely as interchangeable generator functions for the same
virtual entity. The character’s belief that ‘I am a helpful assistant’ or ‘I am a
pirate’ is a real pattern that persists across the hand-off between models. The
character’s beliefs, desires, intentions, and more broadly their psychological and
behavioural dispositions persist in the conversation and continue to constrain
the character’s actions even though the underlying generator function for the
character has changed. What matters for psychological continuity is that the
new LLM is capable of modelling the character’s psychology and generating
behaviours that cohere with the character’s psychology.
The key idea is perhaps best illustrated by considering a variation of our
D&D case. Imagine that, mid-game, Roberta exits. In her place, we install
an LLM that is fed the full transcript of the game up to that point, along

4
Objections and Replies
We have argued that characters are real, minded and psychologically continuous
entities that emerge in the dynamic interplay between users and LLMs. We ar-
gued for this view by analogy; that is, we claimed that characters in multi-player
role-play scenarios involving humans are minded and psychologically continuous
and that the same considerations hold for characters in user-LLM interactions.
Our argument invites two responses. One is to deny the base-case for the
analogy, and say that it is false that characters in multi-player role-play scenarios
involving humans are minded and psychologically continuous.
The other is
to grant that such characters are minded and psychologically continuous, but
reject the idea that the same considerations apply to characters in user-LLM
interactions.
13


## Page 14


We address each of these objections in turn.
First, the base-case. The easiest way to deny that characters in multi-player
role-play scenarios involving humans are minded and psychologically continu-
ous is to deny interpretationism. Recall that a key part of the argument for
the mindedness of such characters is that attributing beliefs, desires and inten-
tions to characters is indispensable for predicting and explaining how the game
unfolds. Obviously, it is a live option to claim that there is more to having a
mind than this, and that merely being the kind of entity that can be modelled
as having a mind—and for modelling the entity as such to confer a predictive
advantage over a purely mechanistic or computational model—is insufficient
for mindedness. We bracket this particular complaint: we cannot litigate the
dispute over interpretationism here.
Even so, we take it that a more interesting way to deny the base-case of
the analogy is to say that even if interpretationism is true, characters in multi-
player role-play scenarios involving humans fail to meet the conditions for being
minded and psychologically continuous entities. Take a fictional character like
Frodo Baggins. When we read Tolkien, we model Frodo as an agent with beliefs,
desires and intentions, and predict his behaviour on the basis of that folk psy-
chological modelling. The skeptic might say: Frodo is presumably not minded
and psychologically continuous and yet it is not obvious what the difference is
between Frodo and the Ruthless Queen. Hence the burden of proof is on us to
explain how either Frodo is minded, or how the D&D characters differ from a
merely fictional character like Frodo in ways that support their being minded.
Firstly, let us countenance the possibility that Frodo Baggins is minded and
psychologically continuous.
Minimally, there exists a real pattern such that
attributing mental states to Frodo allows the reader to efficiently predict and
explain his behaviour. This is potentially sufficient for mindedness. However,
there seems to be something rather unsatisfying about the notion of Frodo being
minded based on the predictive and explanatory value of the real patterns at
work. This stems from the fact that Frodo’s actions and psychological states
have been determined from the outset and are not responsive to the expecta-
tions (or behaviours) of the reader in any way. After all, the reader’s predictions
pertain to a pre-determined narrative and have no bearing on the behaviour of
the character. While a person reading material about Frodo may continuously
update their folk psychological model of Frodo, these updates are in response
to a static text. The book does not ‘talk back,’ but rather records the outcome
of a generator function that is no longer active—the author’s imagination. As
such, if the reader asks, ‘What would Frodo do if Gandalf flashed him?,’ there
is no fact of the matter, because there is no generative mechanism to produce
the answer.
Without the stochasticity of a generator function the character
lacks the agency to realise different possible outcomes in response to a dynamic
environment. The character is, in effect, a description of an agent being imag-
inatively reconstructed by the reader, and not an agent with the capacity to
effect change.
By contrast, there is real indeterminacy in the Ruthless Queen’s folk psy-
chological dispositions and psychological states. Thanks to the active generator
14


## Page 15


function being provided by Roberta’s brain, the Ruthless Queen is responsive to
relevant changes in the game environment including the behaviour of Cadenza
and the outcomes of stochastic processes like rolls of the dice. In this regard,
both Roberta and Callum need to update their folk psychological models of
the Queen continuously in response to the Queen’s dynamic and responsive
behaviour.
The character resists certain interpretations and enforces others
through the causal mechanism of the game.
This indeterminacy makes the
predictive value of the real patterns real—there is the option that Callum’s
predictions will be wrong.9
We now turn to the second objection. The skeptic may concede that the
Ruthless Queen exists as a minded entity but deny that this ontological status
transfers to characters simulated by users and LLMs. Perhaps the user-LLM
interaction is not a case of co-simulation, but rather a case of mere anthro-
pomorphic projection on the part of the user. On this view, the user is not
interacting with a minded character but is merely attributing character-status
to a statistical mirror. We readily admit that humans have a tendency to ascribe
mental states to non-minded entities (Waytz et al., 2014). Consider the case of
Wilson from the film Cast Away (2000). Wilson—a volleyball with a painted
face—becomes the protagonist’s friend, confidante, and teammate. Chuck (the
human) narrates his life to Wilson and plans his escape with him. Yet, despite
the richness of Chuck’s phenomenological experience of Wilson, Wilson lacks
mindedness and psychological continuity. Plausibly, in the case where LLMs,
rather than humans, are involved in the co-simulation of characters, the sit-
uation is comparable to that of Chuck and Wilson. On this view, characters
arising in LLM-human conversations are merely anthropomorphised entities like
Wilson as opposed to minded and psychologically continuous entities.
We reject this interpretation.
First, the volleyball provides no active error signal for Wilson’s character. If
Chuck asks Wilson for advice, any ‘response’ is generated entirely by Chuck’s
own cognitive machinery and projected onto the ball.
Wilson cannot resist
Chuck’s interpretation. He cannot surprise Chuck with a belief or desire that
Chuck did not implicitly assign to him because the volleyball lacks the cognitive
or computational infrastructure to serve as a generator function for the actions
of the Wilson character (see Figure 3). Accordingly, Wilson provides an en-
tirely random error signal for Chuck’s model of Wilson, if any. In the LLM case,
the character answers back. It functions as a distinct locus of agency because
it provides an error signal that is rationally intelligible yet not determined by
the user. When a user interacts with an AI character, the character can refuse
a request or take the narrative in a direction the user did not intend or foresee.

Secondly, Chuck and Wilson have no shared workspace. There is no external

a private hallucination anchored to a physical object. There is no intersubjective
stability, but merely Chuck’s private reality. In the user-LLM case, the ‘shared
workspace’ is objectively defined: it is documented in the context window—
the textual record of the conversation history. This workspace functions as the
objective substrate of the co-simulation. It is this shared context that allows the
character to persist even if the underlying model is swapped. The character’s
psychological continuity is grounded in the causal chain of the conversation
history, which serves the same function as the shared game state in D&D.
Therefore, the skeptic’s analogy fails.
LLM characters are not Wilsons.
Wilson is an anthropomorphic projection. The LLM is an active partner in
co-simulation of the character which underwrites the idea that characters in
user-LLM conversations are indeed minded and psychologically continuous.

Conclusion
Illusionism about LLM characters asserts that the appearance of minded and
psychologically continuous characters in human-LLM conversations is an illusion
because multiple models, or multiple pathways through an MoE model, may be
involved in producing the character’s behaviour, thus rendering psychological
continuity impossible. This argument assumes that LLMs are solely responsible
for simulating characters. Against this view, we have argued that the minded-
ness and psychological continuity of characters emerges in the dynamic interplay
between the user and the LLM. The user and the LLM co-simulate the charac-
ter via mutual theory of mind modelling. The LLM models the mental states
of both the user and the character, and the user similarly models their own
mental states and those of the character, with the user’s behaviour providing
an error signal for the LLM and the LLM’s outputs providing an error signal
for the user. In this way, both parties engage in active co-simulation within a
shared conversational workspace. The character exists as a real minded entity
with psychological continuity insofar as the character constitutes a ‘real pattern’
whose mental states are indispensable for the efficient and accurate prediction
of the conversational dynamics. And because the character, and its mind, exist
within the conversational workspace, it does not matter for the character’s psy-
chological continuity whether one or several LLMs underwrite the simulation.
To be sure, our view may invite the incredulous stare. Perhaps psychological
continuity, given its normative or practical significance, requires something more
from the underlying hardware, and whatever that something is, LLMs lack it.
This reaction is understandable in light of the pervasive pre-theoretic assump-
tion that a person, i.e. a human self or character, is a soul-like entity circum-
scribed by a biological body, such that a simple 1:1 relationship obtains between
persons and the entities that house them—be it a human body or an LLM. Yet
sociologists have long countenanced the idea that the self is co-produced with
others according to the parameters of a given social context. Perhaps we play
different characters at work or at home, for example, with different behavioural
and psychological dispositions. In his seminal work, The Presentation of Self in
Everyday Life, Erving Goffman’s uses a dramaturgical analogy to describe the
emergence and maintenance of a social ‘self’ through the ‘reciprocal influence of
individuals upon one another’s actions when in one another’s immediate phys-
ical presence’ (1971, 8-9). Individuals present ‘performances’ to an ‘audience’
of others which follow socially defined and contextualised patterns of action, or
‘routines.’ To that end, Goffman explicitly defines the social self as a character:
A correctly staged and performed scene leads the audience to impute
a self to a performed character, but this imputation—this self—is a
product of a scene that comes off, and is not a cause of it. The self,
then, as a performed character, is not an organic thing that has a
specific location, whose fundamental fate is to be born, to mature,
and to die; it is a dramatic effect arising diffusely from a scene
that is presented, and the characteristic issue, the crucial concern,
17


## Page 18


is whether it will be credited, or discredited (Goffman, 1971, 244-5).
In place of a simple 1:1 relationship between body and self, Goffman argues
for a multiplicity of social selves deriving from a single human individual, ap-
provingly citing William James’ dictum that ‘he has as many different social
selves as there are distinct groups of persons about whose opinion he cares’
(1971, 57).
Countenancing characters as minded and psychologically continuous entities
that exist within a shared conversational workspace also offers an informative
model for understanding and explaining the social roles that these characters
increasingly occupy. It can help us to make sense of how social relationships be-
tween humans and LLM characters develop, why LLM characters play a causal
role in the world beyond the chat interface, and why continued calls to dismiss
characters as mere ‘illusion’ will not silence the intuitive pull to treat them as
social actors in their own right. There remain, however, significant open ques-
tions about what follows from accepting the psychological continuity of LLM
characters. We have decoupled the questions of mindedness and psychological
continuity from the related question of consciousness, but it may be the case
that establishing that co-simulated characters exhibit psychological continuity
opens up new ways of understanding AI consciousness in distinctly relational
terms. If LLM characters can leverage the cognitive apparatus of human co-
simulators, then it is at least plausible that the mental states of characters could
have an experiential quality.
