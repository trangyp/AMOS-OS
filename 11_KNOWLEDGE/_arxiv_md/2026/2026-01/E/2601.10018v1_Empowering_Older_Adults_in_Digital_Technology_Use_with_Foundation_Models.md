Abstract
Background and Objectives: While high-quality technology support can assist older adults in using digi-
tal applications, many struggle to articulate their issues due to unfamiliarity with technical terminology and
age-related cognitive changes. This study examines these communication challenges and explores AI-based
approaches to mitigate them.
Research Design and Methods: We conducted a diary study with English-speaking, community-dwelling
older adults to collect asynchronous, technology-related queries and used reflexive thematic analysis to iden-
tify communication barriers. To address these barriers, we evaluated how foundation models can paraphrase
older adults’ queries to improve solution accuracy. Two controlled experiments followed: one with younger
adults evaluating AI-rephrased queries and another with older adults evaluating AI-generated solutions. We
also developed a pipeline using large language models to generate the first synthetic dataset of how older
adults request tech support (OATS)1.
Results: We identified four key communication challenges: verbosity, incompleteness, over-specification,
and under-specification. Our prompt-chaining approach using the large language model, GPT-4o, elicited
contextual details, paraphrased the original query, and generated a solution. AI-rephrased queries significantly
improved solution accuracy (69% vs. 46%) and Google search results (69% vs. 35%). Younger adults better
understood AI-rephrased queries (93.7% vs. 65.8%) and reported greater confidence and ease. Older adults
reported high perceived ability to answer contextual questions (89.8%) and follow solutions (94.7%), with
high confidence and ease. OATS demonstrated strong fidelity and face validity.
Discussion and Implications: This work demonstrates how foundation models can enhance technology sup-
port for older adults by addressing age-related communication barriers. The OATS dataset offers a scalable
resource for developing equitable AI systems that better serve aging populations.
Keywords: Technology support; Digital technology use; Artificial intelligence; Large language models; Com-
munication barriers; Human-computer interaction.

Introduction
Digital technologies have become essential for accessing information, obtaining services, maintaining social
connections, and participating fully in civic and economic life. Consequently, there is a growing urgency among
older adults to engage effectively with digital tools [1, 11, 23]. A recent AARP survey reported that nearly all
U.S. adults aged 50 and older now own at least one digital device, and their rate of smartphone ownership
is comparable to that of younger adults [1]. However, ownership does not necessarily translate into broad or
sustained use. Although 89% of older adults reported owning a smartphone and 98% reported using it daily,
their engagement was often limited to basic activities such as internet browsing and texting. Other studies have
similarly found that most older adults use only three to five digital services, indicating that digital engagement
remains relatively narrow over time [1, 32, 36].
While limited digital technology use may reflect personal preferences [24], many older adults cite the lack
of accessible training and high-quality technology support as barriers to adopting more advanced or diverse
features [1, 36]. This need for effective tech support is further shaped by several age-related factors. Older
adults often encountered digital systems later in life, without structured opportunities for learning or the informal
exposure to digital technologies that younger generations experience through school, work, and social contexts

[13]. A lack of digital confidence is also common among older adults [4, 9], and age-related cognitive changes
can make it more difficult to learn and retain new information [5, 32, 36].
These challenges are exacerbated by the fast-evolving nature of digital technologies. Applications are fre-
quently updated, services redesigned, and new interaction paradigms introduced (e.g., AI-powered help bots).
As a result, older adults are often required to acquire entirely new mental models—internal representations of
how systems function—especially when already experiencing frustration or urgency [18, 31]. While extending
an existing mental model may be manageable, forming a new one requires significantly more cognitive effort
and may exceed the limits of older adults’ working memory capacity [18, 39]. Even when older adults have
prior experience with similar tools, minor user interface (UI) changes or inconsistencies can disrupt their ability
to navigate or explain what went wrong.
When something goes wrong—whether with a familiar tool or a new digital feature—older adults often
prefer to seek help from friends, family members, or trusted community resources [3, 21, 27, 35, 36]. Others
may attempt to resolve issues independently by turning to search engines or AI-powered chatbots [20, 36]. In
either case, the first step toward resolving a technology-related problem is being able to communicate it clearly.
However, communicating a tech problem is not simply a matter of describing what went wrong. It re-
quires accessing the appropriate vocabulary, applying a working model of how the system functions, and doing
so with limited cognitive resources [37]. This process often involves generating specific, targeted questions,
understanding the structure of unfamiliar systems, and accurately identifying the source of a problem.
Age-related changes in verbal ability, such as slower processing speed or reduced working memory capacity,
can further complicate this task [8, 19, 25, 30]. In particular, action verbal fluency—the ability to generate
verbs describing tasks or functions—tends to decline with age [22]. While older adults often maintain broader
vocabulary knowledge than their younger counterparts [8], this advantage may not extend to technology-related
terms that are typically acquired later in life. Moreover, common cognitive conditions such as mild cognitive
impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer’s disease can further impair verbal abilities [12, 40].
Although older adults’ technology use and help-seeking behaviors have been previously studied [5, 27, 36],
far less attention has been given to how older adults communicate their technology problems—an essential first
step in obtaining effective support. Prior work has shown that older adults tend to use question words such
as “what,” “why,” and “how” when encountering interaction issues [14]. Others have proposed taxonomies of
query types, including validation (e.g., “Am I doing this right?”), informational, navigational, and conceptual
questions [43]. However, these findings are largely drawn from structured usability studies using predefined
tasks, which do not reflect the ambiguity or open-ended nature of real-world help-seeking. In more organic
contexts—such as when troubleshooting unfamiliar apps or new features—older adults may struggle to formu-
late specific questions or identify the root cause of a problem. This difficulty may be compounded by semantic
mismatches between the terms older adults use and the technical vocabulary expected by support systems or
younger helpers.
Qualitative interviews have already shown that older adults often experience uncertainty when searching
for solutions online and a lack of confidence in how to phrase a question effectively [36]. At the same time,
tech helpers frequently report difficulty understanding older adults’ descriptions of problems, particularly when
support is remote or asynchronous [35]. Despite this, most AI-powered support systems assume that users can
pose well-formed queries at the right moment and that these queries are anchored to a specific interface or
interaction context [16, 41, 42].
Our work addresses this gap by exploring how AI can help translate older adults’ natural problem descrip-
tions into clearer, more solvable queries. Our goal is to reduce the cognitive and verbal burden on older adults
and better align tech support systems with their real-world help-seeking practices. In this study, we examine
how English-speaking, community-dwelling older adults communicate their technology problems in unstruc-
tured, real-world contexts and explore how foundation models can be used to support that communication. We
contribute: a characterization of organically occurring tech support queries from older adults (Section 2); a few-
shot prompt chaining approach that uses foundation models to clarify and paraphrase those queries and generate
solutions (Section 3); an evaluation of this approach with both younger (Section 4) and older adults (Section
5); and the creation of a synthetic dataset to support the development of AI systems that align with the needs of
older adults (Section 6). Our findings underscore the importance of designing digital support tools that reflect
the realities of aging, communication, and cognition within socio-technical ecosystems.

How older adults communicate tech problems
In this section, we examine how English-speaking, community-dwelling older adults describe technology-
related problems as they naturally occur in everyday life—outside of structured research environments that
prescribe specific tasks, apps, or features [16, 41, 42, 43]. Older adults may reach out for social support either
face-to-face or remotely [35, 36]. Remote help-seeking, although increasingly common during ongoing tech-
nology use, can be especially difficult due to communication constraints and a lack of shared context. Similar
challenges emerge when older adults attempt to troubleshoot problems using search engines or AI-powered
agents. Although prior research suggests that older adults face challenges in articulating technology problems,
there is limited empirical evidence on how such queries are formulated in unstructured, real-world contexts.
This gap is critical. Without a deeper understanding of the characteristics of older adults’ naturally occurring
help-seeking queries, it is difficult to design AI systems—or human-centered interventions—that meaningfully
align with their communication needs. To address this, we collected organically occurring technology-related
queries and analyzed the communication strategies, breakdowns, and patterns used by older adults when asking
for support.
2.1
Methods
We conducted an online diary study to examine how older adults communicate technology problems in real-
world contexts. This method—via text messaging or email—aligned naturally with remote help-seeking, which
is often asynchronous and similar to independently using search engines or AI-powered agents. Eligible par-
ticipants were aged 60 or older, used a mobile device (e.g., smartphone or tablet) at least once per week, and
were proficient in spoken English. We focused specifically on mobile devices, as they are the most commonly
used computing technologies among older adults [1]. Participants were recruited through community flyers,
mailing lists, social media platforms, and word-of-mouth. The diary study lasted up to eight weeks, though
participants could withdraw at any time. No monetary incentives were provided. After initial onboarding with
a researcher, participants were asked to message the research team with any mobile device–related technology
questions using their preferred communication method (e.g., email, text, or WhatsApp). They could submit
questions through text, screenshots, voice memos, or screen recordings, and were instructed not to share per-
sonal or sensitive information. To encourage participation, automated reminders were sent every two days, and
members of the research team responded with assistance as an incentive. All communication, aside from the
initial onboarding, occurred asynchronously.
Midway through the study, we observed low engagement, with participants submitting approximately one
query per week on average. Follow-up interviews revealed that many older adults typically reached out to family
members or trusted tech helpers first and only contacted the research team if the issue remained unresolved. To
address this, we expanded recruitment to include adult tech helpers (aged 18 or older) who had previously
assisted older adults. These helpers were asked to document asynchronous help interactions by submitting
message transcripts, summarizing the issue in their own words, and reporting whether the issue was successfully
resolved. As is common in longitudinal research with older adults, not all participants remained engaged for
the full eight weeks.
2.2
Results
We collected diary entries from 27 older adults (13 women; Mdn age = 66). Of these participants, 17 were
recruited indirectly through their tech helpers. Over the study period, the number of queries submitted by each
participant ranged from one to ten. All queries were unique; no participant asked about the same issue more than
once. Participants reported using a variety of mobile devices, including iPhones, iPads, Android smartphones,
and Amazon Fire tablets.
In total, we analyzed 57 text-based queries, nine of which included screenshots. These messages spanned 34
distinct mobile applications. Although all queries addressed different problems, two remained unresolved due
to participant attrition. The dataset included both the original query and the full transcript of the asynchronous
conversation that followed, either with a tech helper or a researcher. Query length varied substantially, ranging
from 5 to 223 words (Mdn = 65, IQR = 27). To categorize the types of questions, we applied an existing
taxonomy of older adults’ tech-related queries [43]. Two researchers independently coded all queries using a
coding reliability thematic analysis [7] and achieved substantial interrater reliability (Cohen’s κ = .72). The
final classification included 4 validation, 14 directed informational, 23 undirected informational, 5 navigational,
and 11 conceptual questions.

In addition to question type, we conducted a reflexive thematic analysis [7] to examine how older adults
communicated their technology problems. Codes were developed inductively and refined through collabora-
tive discussion. We identified four recurring characteristics in how queries were articulated: excessive detail
that obscured the core issue (verbosity), inclusion of non-essential specifics (over-specification), omission of
relevant information (under-specification), and lack of critical context (incompleteness). Table 1 provides illus-
trative examples of each characteristic. These patterns often made it difficult to determine the precise nature
of the problem or the most appropriate course of action. Some participants combined multiple issues into a
single message without clear scoping, while others omitted essential details such as the device or app involved.
Additionally, participants occasionally used unconventional or metaphorical language that required interpreta-
tion—for example, referring to voice commands as “tongue,” describing a locked phone as “power going off,”
or calling dark mode “screen going dark.”
In summary, our analysis identified several characteristics in older adults’ technology-related queries that
may hinder problem resolution, especially in remote or automated support contexts. These patterns, along
with occasional use of unconventional vocabulary, point to a need for interventions that support clearer, more
effective communication. To address this, we next explore how foundation models can be used to reformulate
older adults’ queries so they can be more readily resolved—whether by asking someone, searching online, or
interacting with an AI-based support agent.

Reformulating tech problems with foundation models
In the previous section, we described the wide variability in how older adults articulated their technology prob-
lems—ranging from verbosity to missing context—which highlights the need for support systems that can
accommodate diverse communication styles and clarify user intent. Two key challenges stood out: the in-
frequency of screen-related input and the frequent omission of essential contextual details that tech helpers
typically needed to elicit through follow-up questions. For example, only 9 out of 57 entries (15.8%) in our
dataset included a screenshot of the relevant user interface. These patterns challenge the assumptions underly-
ing current and emerging autonomous AI agents, which often rely on access to interface-specific data to guide
interactions with websites, apps, and services [41, 16, 42]. To address these issues, we next explore how foun-

3.1
Methods
We selected two foundation models, GPT-4o [2] and OS-ATLAS [42], to evaluate our approach. OS-ATLAS
is a foundational GUI action model developed for graphical user interface (GUI) grounding and agentic tasks
beyond its training distribution. We included OS-ATLAS due to its ability to operate without relying on external
APIs and its strength in GUI grounding. GPT-4o was chosen as a general-purpose language model for its strong
generalization capabilities and versatility [42]. Compared to other large language models (LLMs) such as
Gemini, LLaMA, and Claude, GPT-4o demonstrates superior reasoning ability, causal coherence, and overall
accuracy.
We used a subset of tech queries collected in the diary study (described in Section 2) for prompt development
and model evaluation. Only queries that were understandable without screenshots and had been successfully
resolved were included. Two researchers independently reviewed the 51 eligible queries and, when available,
their follow-up messages to identify the underlying tech problem. Interrater reliability was high (Cohen’s κ =
.89). The final dataset consisted of these 51 queries, their associated conversations, and expert annotations of
the tech problems.
We developed confidence-aware prompts for GPT-4o and OS-ATLAS using an iterative few-shot prompt-
ing strategy, with separate iterations tailored to each model’s architecture. Although the final prompts varied
slightly, the same three example queries from our dataset were used in the workflow across both models to
ensure consistency (see Appendix A).
Each prompt guided the model through four tasks: (1) analyze the original query to identify missing con-
textual details; (2) generate up to three follow-up questions to elicit that information; (3) paraphrase the query
into one or more concise, complete questions suitable for a Google search; and (4) provide a single, confident
solution—defined as at least 90% confidence for GPT-4o and 80% for OS-ATLAS (Figure 1).
We tested the prompts using a range of representative queries, including step-by-step requests, conceptual
explanations, and impossible asks. Based on performance, we refined the prompts to reduce redundancy, include
essential context, and avoid overly technical language. Additional instructions encouraged models to break
down complex requests into multiple paraphrased questions and prioritize clarity and simplicity, particularly for
older users.
To reduce hallucination risk, we implemented confidence thresholds. Although language model “confi-
dence” is a heuristic rather than a statistical measure, these thresholds ensured that solutions were only generated
when the model demonstrated sufficient comprehension, thereby improving response reliability.

Model outputs were evaluated for both paraphrasing and solution-generation effectiveness. Paraphrasing
was assessed using a codebook thematic analysis [7]. Expert raters categorized rephrased queries as correct,
partially correct, or incorrect (see Appendix A for examples). Partially correct paraphrases either omitted es-
sential elements of the original query or introduced hallucinated content. Two experts independently rated the
outputs from each model, achieving substantial interrater agreement (Cohen’s κ = .75 for OS-ATLAS; κ = .85
for GPT-4o). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion prior to final categorization.
Expert evaluation of paraphrasing effectiveness revealed that GPT-4o substantially outperformed OS-ATLAS.
GPT-4o produced correct paraphrases for 68.8% of the queries, compared to only 12.6% for OS-ATLAS. OS-
ATLAS outputs were more frequently rated as partially correct (43.7%) or incorrect (43.7%), whereas GPT-4o
had far fewer partially correct (25%) and incorrect (6.2%) responses. Paraphrased queries were not evaluated
by older adult participants for two reasons. First, the goal of paraphrasing was to increase the likelihood of gen-
erating timely and accurate solutions, rather than to assess older adults’ perceptions of the reformulated queries.
Second, the same cognitive and linguistic challenges that contributed to ambiguity in the original queries could
limit older adults’ ability to reliably evaluate the quality of the paraphrased versions [28, 41]. Instead, Section
5 reports how older adults directly evaluated the contextual questions and AI-generated solutions.
Solution-generation effectiveness was evaluated based on the perceived accuracy of model-generated re-
sponses, using the same three-category framework: correct, partially correct, and incorrect (see Appendix A).
Ground truth solutions were available for all queries, as each had been resolved during the diary study. Refer-
ence solutions were created by the first author, who extracted the correct steps from participants’ transcripts and
formatted them according to the same criteria provided to the models.
A comparison of model performance across solution categories is presented in Table 2. In the Google Search
condition, all correct solutions appeared within the top five search results (excluding AI-generated content). For
each query, the top 20 search results were examined. Both GPT-4o and OS-ATLAS responded with “I do not
know” to three of the 51 queries, with one overlapping case. Additional quantitative evaluation of the model
outputs is available in the Appendix A.
The findings from our qualitative and quantitative evaluations demonstrate that foundation models—particularly
GPT-4o—can effectively reformulate older adults’ technology-related queries, even when those queries are
vague, imprecise, or lacking critical context. By eliciting relevant details, generating clearer paraphrased ver-
sions, and producing actionable solutions, these models show promise in reducing the communicative burden
placed on older adults. Building on these results, we next examine how AI-rephrased queries are perceived and
interpreted by tech helpers, who play a critical role in resolving older adults’ technology issues.
4
Evaluation of AI-rephrased queries with tech helpers
Older adults often rely on younger individuals in their social networks for technology support. However, prior
research suggests that younger tech helpers frequently struggle to understand the problem before they can offer
effective assistance [16, 35, 36]. Ideally, AI-rephrased queries should help mitigate this communication barrier.
To evaluate this possibility, we conducted a controlled experiment with younger adults to assess whether AI-
generated paraphrases of older adults’ tech queries improved perceived understanding, confidence, and ease of
interpretation relative to the original queries.
4.1
Methods
Adults aged 18 to 50 were recruited to participate in a controlled experiment evaluating AI-rephrased technology
queries. Eligibility criteria included age and demonstration of at least professional working proficiency in

English, verified through a brief language screener. Participants were recruited via social media platforms and
word-of-mouth referrals. No monetary compensation was provided. The experimental materials consisted of
query pairs: original queries collected from older adults (as described in Section 2) and their corresponding AI-
reformulated versions generated by GPT-4o using the pipeline outlined in Section 3. To ensure content quality,
only query pairs that had received a “correct” paraphrasing rating during expert evaluation were included (69%
of 48 queries). A total of 33 query pairs were included in the study. Each participant rated five of these pairs,
which were selected using a Latin square design to ensure balanced and systematic coverage across participants.
Query pairs were presented sequentially—original followed by rephrased—with the order of the pairs ran-
domized for each participant. Participants were not informed which version had been reformulated by AI or
whether either query was drawn from real users. For each query, participants were first asked whether they
understood the query (response options: yes, no, maybe, I don’t know). If they selected any response other than
no, they were then asked to rate their confidence in understanding and ease of understanding, both on 5-point
Likert scales.
In addition to these comprehension-related measures, participants completed demographic questions and an
adapted version of the Digital Literacy Scale [6] (Appendix B) to assess their own digital proficiency. All pro-
cedures were conducted remotely. Following informed consent and eligibility screening, participants reviewed
a randomized set of query pairs. All study materials were administered using the Qualtrics platform.
With query type (original vs. AI-rephrased) as the independent variable, and comprehension, perceived
confidence, and perceived ease of understanding as dependent variables, we tested the following hypotheses:
H1: Participants will understand significantly more rephrased queries than original queries.
H2: Participants will report significantly greater confidence in understanding rephrased queries than original
queries.
H3: Participants will find rephrased queries significantly easier to understand than original queries.
4.2
Results
4.2.1
Participants
Forty-eight participants (Mdn age = 28.0 years, SE = 0.82) completed the study. Each of the 33 unique query
pairs was evaluated by between 3 and 6 participants (Mdn = 5.0, SE = 0.14). The sample included 25 men,
18 women, 3 participants who identified as genderqueer or gender non-conforming, 1 trans male/trans man,
and 1 participant who preferred not to disclose their gender. Participants represented a diverse range of ethnic
backgrounds: 19 identified as White, 11 as Asian, 9 as Middle Eastern or North African, 4 as Hispanic/Latino,
2 as Black or African American, 2 as Other, and 1 declined to report their ethnicity. Participants reported high
digital literacy (Mdn = 4.88, SE = 0.05 on a 5-point scale) and strong English proficiency (Mdn= 4.00, SE =
0.34). Most participants (n = 44) indicated either native or full professional proficiency in English. Additionally,
a majority (n = 31) had experience helping others with technology, primarily in a personal capacity; among
these, 23 participants (74%) had been providing tech support for more than five years.
4.2.2
Hypotheses Testing
Participants reported understanding the rephrased queries significantly more often (n = 149, 93.7%) than the
original queries (n = 106, 65.8%). A McNemar’s test confirmed that this difference was statistically significant,
χ2(1, N = 55) = 35.20, p <.001, with a large effect size (ϕ = .8). Thus, H1 was supported.
A Wilcoxon signed-rank test revealed that participants expressed significantly greater confidence in under-
standing the rephrased queries (Mdn = 5.00 IQR = 0.00) compared to the original queries (Mdn = 4.00, IQR =
1.50), V = 287.00, p < .001, with a medium effect size (r = .50), supporting H2.
Participants also rated the rephrased queries as significantly easier to understand (Mdn = 5.00, IQR = 0.50)
than the original queries (Mdn = 4.00, IQR = 1.00), V = 395.00, p < .001, with a medium effect size (r = .56).
This finding supports H3.
Additional analyses examining the relationship between digital literacy and participants’ self-reported un-
derstanding, confidence, and ease when responding to original and rephrased queries is provided in the Ap-
pendix B.
In this section, we showed that AI-rephrased queries improved comprehension, confidence, and clarity
among younger adults, including those with prior experience supporting older adults with technology. However,
communicating the problem is only one part of the support process. In the next section, we assess whether the
AI-generated contextual questions and solutions are understandable and useful to older adults themselves.

Evaluation of AI-generated solutions with older adults
In Section 3, we introduced our few-shot prompt chaining approach (Figure 1), which rephrases the original
tech query and generates a corresponding solution. However, the success of this pipeline hinges on two critical
factors: first, whether older adults feel comfortable and capable of answering the contextual follow-up questions
generated by the model; and second, whether the solutions themselves are understandable, actionable, and elicit
confidence. Although expert raters found the GPT-4o-generated solutions to be accurate 69% of the time, their
practical value depends on how well older adults can interpret and follow them. In this section, we assess both
aspects with older adults.
5.1
Methods
We conducted a controlled study to evaluate older adults’ perceptions of AI-generated follow-up questions and
solutions. Participants were eligible if they were aged 60 years or older and demonstrated at least limited
working proficiency in English, verified through a self-assessment screener. Recruitment was conducted via
social media platforms and word-of-mouth referrals. No monetary compensation was offered.
Participants evaluated AI-generated follow-up questions based on three criteria: (1) whether they felt able
to answer the question (response options: yes, no, maybe), (2) their confidence in their answer, and (3) the
perceived ease of answering it. They also evaluated AI-generated solutions using a parallel set of measures:
(1) whether they felt able to follow the solution (response options: yes, no, maybe), (2) their confidence in
following it, and (3) the perceived ease of doing so. If participants selected any response other than no, they
were asked to rate their confidence and ease using 5-point Likert scales.
Eighty-five unique follow-up questions and 36 unique solutions generated by GPT-4o were included for
evaluation. Only solutions that had received a “correct” rating from expert evaluators were included to ensure
content quality. To personalize the study experience, participants were first asked which mobile devices they
currently use (e.g., smartphone, tablet). Survey content was then dynamically filtered so that participants only
reviewed content relevant to the devices they reported using. Three follow-up and two solutions were not rated
because none of our participants reported using the related mobile devices.
After completing eligibility screening and providing informed consent, participants were shown a random-
ized subset of follow-up questions and solutions. They then rated each item using the measures described above.
All materials were administered using the Qualtrics platform. At the end of the study, participants completed
a brief demographic questionnaire and the Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire–14 (MDPQ-14; 14 to 70;
Roque & Boot, 2018) to assess self-reported mobile technology skills.
5.2
Results
5.2.1
Participants
Thirty-four older adults (Mdn age = 72.0 years, SE = 1.21) participated in the study. Of those who provided
demographic information, 19 identified as women and 7 as men. The majority of participants identified as White
(n = 25). Educational attainment varied: seven participants reported holding a high school diploma, three had a
2-year degree, seven held a 3- or 4-year degree, seven had a master’s degree, and three held a doctoral degree. In
terms of living arrangements, 18 participants lived independently, four lived with family, and four resided in a
senior living community. English proficiency was generally high, with 17 participants reporting full professional
proficiency, eight reporting native or bilingual proficiency, five reporting professional working proficiency, and
four reporting limited working proficiency. Self-reported mobile device proficiency was moderate to high (Mdn

6
OATS: A synthetic dataset for age-inclusive design of AI agents
Developing AI systems that equitably support older adults requires training data that reflect the realities of
aging—yet such data are often scarce due to recruitment barriers, accessibility issues, and cognitive fatigue.
As a result, most datasets rely on younger, digitally fluent users and miss how older adults express problems
9


## Page 10


or engage with support tools. Synthetic datasets—text corpora generated using large language models—have
been successfully used in fields such as healthcare, education, and human-computer interaction to simulate rare,
underrepresented, or difficult-to-collect scenarios. For gerontology, they offer a scalable way to model older
adults’ communication styles and tech challenges. This section introduces OATS (Older Adults request Tech
Support), a synthetic dataset grounded in real-world queries from older adults.
The OATS dataset comprises 514 instances, distributed across four categories: verbosity (n = 120), over-
specification (n = 120), under-specification (n = 154), and incompleteness (n = 120). Each instance includes
a technology-related query written in the communication style of older adults, paired with a paraphrase that
clarifies the intended meaning. Dataset development and examples are detailed in the Appendix C.
We evaluated the OATS dataset on two key dimensions: fidelity and face validity. Fidelity refers to the extent
to which the synthetic data preserves the statistical properties and latent semantic patterns of the original dataset.
To assess this, we compared vector representations of synthetic and real-world data using Principal Component
Analysis (PCA) applied to paragraph-level embeddings generated by a Sentence Transformers (SBERT) model.
Unlike standard BERT, which is optimized for token-level tasks, SBERT uses a Siamese network architecture
to produce sentence-level embeddings that capture semantic similarity more effectively [34]. A high degree of
overlap in the PCA visualization suggests that the synthetic queries mirror the underlying semantic structure of
the original data, indicating strong fidelity (Figure 2).
To assess face validity, we conducted a qualitative review with two domain experts in aging and technology.
A random sample of 50 synthetic technology-related queries was independently evaluated by each expert using
a codebook thematic analysis [7]. Reviewers classified each query based on how likely it was to have been
communicated by an older adult, using the following categories: (1) likely, (2) possibly, or (3) unlikely. Of
the 50 queries, 40 were rated as “likely,” indicating strong plausibility. Inter-rater reliability, calculated using
Cohen’s kappa, was κ = .83, reflecting high agreement between raters. These results support the face validity
of the OATS dataset as a realistic representation of the language older adults use when seeking technology
support.
7
Discussion
Despite AI’s potential to enhance older adults’ well-being and independence, it also risks reinforcing digital
ageism [29]. This bias may widen the digital divide not just between generations, but also within older popu-
lations along lines of education, income, and access [15, 17]. Although device ownership is rising, many older
adults still struggle with tasks beyond basic use [1, 10]. Mainstream AI systems, often designed for clear and
fluent inputs, may exclude users whose communication styles are different.
This work focuses on technology support, a critical yet overlooked aspect of digital inclusion. Tech support
lies at the intersection of growing need and increasing automation. While older adults require help with con-
tinuously evolving digital systems, support is increasingly delegated to AI-based agents. These systems often
assume users can engage with advanced tools at the very moment they experience confusion—risking what
might be called algorithmic ageism.
As a result, tech support often falls to family and friends, who adopt caregiving roles long before phys-
ical support is needed [35]. This “digital technology caregiving” also presents challenges, especially when
older adults struggle to articulate problems. Queries that are vague, overly detailed, or use unfamiliar terms
can be hard to interpret. This study deepens our understanding of these barriers and introduces a foundation
model–based approach to make support systems more inclusive.
7.1
Primary Findings
Our study offers new empirical insight into how older adults communicate their technology-related problems in
everyday, unstructured contexts. Through an online diary study, we documented how help-seeking queries were
often incomplete, vague, or overly detailed—characteristics that made it difficult for both human helpers and
AI systems to understand the underlying issue. These communication breakdowns frequently required follow-
up questions from tech helpers to gather missing contextual details before any resolution could be attempted.
Despite the growing reliance on automated support systems, most current AI tools assume that such contextual
information is already available—either through direct interface access or clearly formulated user input [16, 41].
This design misalignment disadvantages many older adults whose help-seeking strategies do not conform to
those expectations.
10


## Page 11


To address this gap, we developed and evaluated a foundation model–based approach that mimics the work-
flow of a human helper: asking targeted contextual questions, paraphrasing the user’s query, and generating
a potential solution. Using few-shot prompt chaining, we found that GPT-4o significantly outperformed the
baseline, with AI-paraphrased queries producing correct solutions in the top five Google search results 69% of
the time, compared to just 35% for the original queries. These improvements were evident across both expert-
rated solution accuracy and standard natural language processing metrics (Section 3.2). While OS-ATLAS, a
GUI-grounded action model, underperformed in our text-only condition, its limited success suggests that com-
bining screenshots with language input may enhance model performance—though older adults in our study
rarely included visual context in their queries.
Importantly, we also found that AI-rephrased queries were more easily understood by younger adults, who
often serve as informal tech helpers to older family members and friends [35]. Participants reported higher
comprehension, confidence, and ease of interpretation when reading paraphrased versions of older adults’ orig-
inal queries (Section 4.2). This suggests that AI tools can serve not only as direct support for older adults, but
also as mediators that enhance intergenerational tech caregiving. Moreover, older adult participants evaluated
AI-generated contextual questions and solutions as generally easy to understand and follow (Section 5.2). These
findings underscore the potential for foundation models to reduce the communicative burden of help-seeking
and make digital support systems more responsive to the diverse interaction styles of aging populations.
7.2
Limitations
This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the older adult
participants in our diary study and evaluation experiments comprised a relatively small, self-selected group of
community-dwelling, English-speaking individuals. All had some experience with smartphones or computers
which means they were not digital novices. While their help-seeking queries reflect common frustrations, the
findings may not generalize to less experienced users or those with significant cognitive impairments. Second,
our experiments were conducted in controlled, asynchronous settings and may not reflect the complexities of
real-world support contexts. Finally, our approach relied on a state-of-the-art language model (GPT-4) and
carefully designed prompts. This level of performance may not be replicable with smaller or open-source
models. The pipeline’s effectiveness is likely tied to the capabilities of advanced (and currently proprietary)
foundation models, which could limit scalability in resource-constrained settings. Moreover, changes to the
model behavior across future updates may affect output consistency and reliability.
7.3
Implications for Aging Research and Practice
This work advances gerontological research by advancing understanding of how older adults articulate technol-
ogy problems—an often-overlooked aspect of aging with technology. While prior studies have largely focused
on whether older adults adopt certain technologies or why they may resist them [11], our study shifts attention
to what happens after adoption—when older adults encounter difficulties and seek help. The communication
patterns we identified (Section 2) offer a vocabulary for describing common pitfalls in help-seeking, which can
inform both research and practice.
Digital literacy programs, for instance, could incorporate modules that teach older adults how to formulate
more effective help requests—such as how to emphasize the core issue and provide relevant details. However,
it may be unrealistic to expect all older adults to adopt concise, technically precise language, particularly given
age-related cognitive changes. This reinforces the need for support systems that adapt to users, rather than
forcing users to adapt to systems.
Our AI-based approach offers a model for such age-inclusive design. By beginning with real-world diary
entries and designing around older adults’ actual communication styles—rather than defaulting to younger user
norms—we treat older adults not as deficient, but as capable users with distinct interaction patterns. This aligns
with participatory and user-centered approaches in gerontechnology, such as “ultimate partnership” models that
engage older adults as co-creators.
AI-powered support could also extend the reach of existing tech education efforts. For example, community
centers or libraries could deploy AI chatbots to provide just-in-time assistance for older patrons—especially
those without tech-savvy family members or immediate access to help. These systems could foster autonomy,
allowing older adults to solve problems on their own schedules rather than waiting for in-person classes or
assistance.
Crucially, our study emphasizes the diversity within the older adult population—across cognitive abilities,
tech proficiency, and communication styles. Some individuals prefer narrative, detailed explanations; others are

brief and to the point. Our AI pipeline was able to handle both over- and under-specified queries, suggesting its
potential to support a range of help-seeking behaviors. For aging services practitioners, this flexibility enables
more tailored support. AI tools could triage requests by prompting more detail from minimal input or distilling
key information from verbose descriptions. In both cases, the experience becomes more personalized.
7.4
Implications for AI Research and Practice
Our findings have broad implications for the future of AI research and practice, particularly in the develop-
ment of human-centered systems that serve diverse populations. In addressing the digital support needs of older
adults, our study highlights a persistent disconnect between users’ real-world help-seeking behaviors and the as-
sumptions built into many current AI systems. Most AI models are trained on data from younger, digitally fluent
populations, resulting in interaction norms that often misalign with older adults’ communication styles. These
biases can lead to systems that misinterpret older users’ queries or produce responses that require technical
fluency many do not possess.
Our study challenges these assumptions by showing that large language models (LLMs) can be adapted
to accommodate older adults’ natural language through targeted prompt engineering. By centering the real-
world communication patterns of older adults, we demonstrate that inclusive design can lead to more accessible
and effective support experiences—not only for older users, but for everyone. Clarity, context sensitivity, and
adaptability are not niche accommodations; they are universal design principles for equitable AI.
The OATS dataset represents a meaningful contribution to age-inclusive AI development. Derived from
real diary entries and informed by user themes, OATS captures how older adults naturally articulate technology
support queries. To our knowledge, it is the first structured dataset focused on this specific form of help-
seeking behavior. In addition to documenting communication patterns, OATS provides a benchmarking tool
to evaluate model performance across age groups. Comparing performance on OATS versus crowd-sourced
(younger-skewed) data can reveal important disparities and inform refinement of prompts or models. The un-
derlying LLM-driven generation pipeline can also be extended to simulate user queries in other domains—such
as healthcare or public services—embedding inclusivity early in the development process.
Our study also surfaces key methodological insights for the AI research community. First, synthetic datasets
like OATS show that AI systems can be tailored to reflect users’ lived experiences, offering a path toward more
inclusive design. However, such tools should be co-designed with, and evaluated by, the communities they
intend to serve. Second, while prompt-based data generation is increasingly popular, it often lacks robustness
and grounding in real user needs. We addressed this by incorporating domain-informed and user-centered
strategies in our generation pipeline. Third, the ultimate goal of synthetic datasets is to support downstream tasks
such as training or fine-tuning AI assistants—yet evaluation remains a persistent challenge. As this approach
expands, synthetic data generation must strike a balance between realism and scalability. This opens new
directions for research in prompt engineering, synthetic data design, and evaluation frameworks that reflect
real-world diversity and use cases.
8
Conclusion
In this work, we uncovered the communicative challenges older adults face when seeking digital technology
support and demonstrated how foundation models can be adapted to better meet their needs. By introducing
the OATS dataset and a prompt-chaining framework grounded in real user data, we offer a concrete path toward
designing AI systems that are more inclusive, responsive, and equitable. Our findings emphasize that older
adults are not deficient users, but communicators with distinct and valuable interaction styles that current AI
systems often overlook. Embedding these styles into AI training, evaluation, and deployment processes is
essential for building age-inclusive technologies.

A.5
Quantitative Evaluation of Model Outputs
To complement our qualitative analyses, we conducted a quantitative evaluation of model-generated paraphrases
and solutions using established natural language processing (NLP) metrics. Our goal was to assess how closely
the AI-generated content aligned with expert-written references, especially in the context of older adults’ help-
seeking behaviors. The following measures were used.
BLEU [33] is a precision-based metric originally developed for evaluating machine translation. It measures
how many words or short sequences of words (n-grams) in the model output match those in a reference text.
19


## Page 20


Higher scores indicate greater similarity to the reference. In this study, BLEU was used to assess how well
AI-generated paraphrases and solutions matched expert-written versions.
ROUGE-L [26] measures the longest common subsequence between the model output and a reference text,
capturing both precision and recall. It is often used to evaluate the quality of summaries. Here, it was used to
assess whether model-generated solutions preserved the key steps and sequence of expert solutions.
BERTScore [44] compares the contextual embeddings of words in the model output and reference text using
a pretrained language model (BERT). It goes beyond exact word matches to assess semantic similarity. Higher
scores suggest closer alignment in meaning, even if different wording is used.
Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors—in this case, the semantic representations of the
model output and reference text. It ranges from −1 to 1, where 1 indicates identical semantic orientation. This
metric was used to quantify how similar the model-generated solutions were to the expert-written solutions at a
conceptual level.
ParaScorer [38] is a paraphrase evaluation framework that combines reference-based and reference-free
metrics. Reference-based metrics compare model output to an expert-written paraphrase, while reference-free
metrics assess semantic similarity and fluency without relying on a reference. This tool helps evaluate how well
AI systems reformulate user queries for improved clarity and problem-solving effectiveness.
Paraphrasing quality was evaluated using ParaScorer [38], which provides both reference-based and reference-
free metrics.
Reference-based metrics assess the degree of similarity between model outputs and expert-
generated paraphrases, while reference-free metrics evaluate semantic similarity and fluency without a pre-
defined reference. GPT-4o outperformed OS-ATLAS on reference-based evaluation (0.95 vs. 0.85), indicating
stronger alignment with expert rewordings. In contrast, OS-ATLAS performed better on reference-free evalua-
tion (0.70 vs. 0.60). However, paraphrasing older adults’ queries often requires adding contextual information
not present in the original message, which increases lexical divergence and may penalize models in reference-
free evaluation. For our use case—clarifying real-world, underspecified queries—reference-based alignment
was a more relevant indicator of effectiveness. Accordingly, GPT-4o’s performance suggests greater suitability
for supporting older adults through AI-powered rewording of tech queries.
To further assess paraphrasing quality, we compared model outputs to expert-generated paraphrases using
widely accepted natural language processing metrics. These included contextual similarity [44], machine trans-
lation quality [33], summarization accuracy [26], and cosine-based semantic similarity. As shown in Table 3,
GPT-4o consistently outperformed OS-ATLAS across all metrics. We applied these same metrics to evaluate
model-generated solutions against expert-written responses (Table 3). GPT-4o again demonstrated stronger
alignment, achieving a BLEU score of 0.62 and a ROUGE-L score of 0.72, compared to OS-ATLAS scores of
0.04 and 0.30, respectively. Because BLEU and ROUGE measure n-gram overlap and sequence fidelity, they
are particularly well suited for evaluating multi-step tech support instructions—where both accuracy and clar-
ity of sequence are essential for usability. This is especially important for older users, who often benefit from
step-by-step, easy-to-follow guidance when resolving technology issues.

B.2
Additional analyses
Spearman’s rank-order correlations were conducted to examine the relationship between digital literacy and par-
ticipants’ self-reported understanding, confidence, and ease when responding to original and rephrased queries.
For original queries, digital literacy was weakly positively correlated with understanding, r = .12, p = .041, and
weakly negatively correlated with confidence, r = –.12, p = .038. No significant correlations were observed
for rephrased queries: digital literacy was not significantly associated with understanding, confidence, or ease.
These results suggest that participants with higher digital literacy were slightly more likely to understand the
original queries but paradoxically felt less confident in their responses.
To further explore the impact of rephrasing, an improvement score was calculated as the difference in un-
derstanding between rephrased and original queries. A Spearman correlation revealed a significant negative
association between digital literacy and improvement in understanding, r = –.33, p = .022. This indicates that
participants with lower digital literacy benefited more from rephrased queries, reporting greater gains in under-
standing than their more digitally literate counterparts.
We also tested whether prior experience as a tech helper to older adults influenced comprehension. A
Mann–Whitney U test indicated no significant difference in comprehension of original queries between tech
helpers and non-helpers, U = 3243.00, p = .193. To determine whether tech helpers and non-helpers reported
equivalent understanding of rephrased queries, a Two One-Sided Tests (TOST) procedure was conducted.
Equivalence bounds were set at ±0.50 standardized units, corresponding to a moderate effect size (Cohen’s
d). The observed mean difference in understanding was small (M difference = 0.08), and both one-sided tests
were statistically significant, ps = .030 and < .001. These results support the conclusion that tech helpers and
non-helpers reported statistically equivalent levels of understanding for rephrased queries, within a moderate
equivalence margin.

C.1
Dataset Development
We generated synthetic data using few-shot prompting with GPT-4o, guided by the communication characteris-
tics of older adults’ technology queries identified in Section 2: verbosity, over-specification, under-specification,
and incompleteness. Each prompt included three example pairs—an original query exhibiting one of these char-
acteristics and a corresponding expert-generated paraphrase. To ensure diversity while maintaining realism, the
model was instructed to vary the types of technical issues represented while preserving sentence structure, tone,
and language style typical of older adults. This included conversational phrasing, expressions of uncertainty,
and the informal tone often observed in everyday help-seeking interactions. Data can be downlaoded from here:
https://github.com/hhshomee/OATS.
To examine linguistic characteristics, we analyzed token length and lexical diversity. On average, original
queries contained M = 58.89 tokens, whereas paraphrases averaged M = 23.75 tokens. Query lengths ranged
from 13 to 174 tokens. We also calculated the type-token ratio (TTR) to assess lexical repetition. In the
synthetic dataset, queries written in the older adult style yielded a TTR of 0.858, compared to 0.937 for expert
paraphrases. A similar pattern was observed in the real-world dataset (TTR = 0.842 for older adults vs. 0.890
for experts).
