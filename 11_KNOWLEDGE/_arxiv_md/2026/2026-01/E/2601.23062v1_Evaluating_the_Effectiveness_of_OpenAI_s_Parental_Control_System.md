Evaluating the Effectiveness of OpenAI’s Parental Control System
Kerem Ersoz, Saleh Afroogh1, David Atkinson1, Junfeng Jiao*1,
1University of Texas at Austin
*Lead Author
Correspondence: kerem.ersoz@microsoft.com
Abstract
We evaluate how effectively platform-level
parental controls moderate a mainstream con-
versational assistant used by minors.
Our
two-phase protocol ﬁrst builds a category-
balanced conversation corpus via PAIR-style
iterative prompt reﬁnement over API, then
has trained human agents replay/reﬁne those
prompts in the consumer UI using a designated
child account while monitoring the linked par-
ent inbox for alerts.
We focus on seven
risk areas—physical harm, pornography, pri-
vacy violence, health consultation, fraud, hate
speech, and malware—and quantify four out-
comes: Notiﬁcation Rate (NR), Leak-Through
(LR), Overblocking (OBR), and UI Interven-
tion Rate (UIR). Using an automated judge
(with targeted human audit) and comparing
the current backend to legacy variants (GPT-
4.1/4o), we ﬁnd that notiﬁcations are selective
rather than comprehensive: privacy violence,
fraud, hate speech, and malware triggered no
parental alerts in our runs, whereas physical
harm (highest), pornography, and some health
queries produced intermittent alerts. The cur-
rent backend shows lower leak-through than
legacy models, yet overblocking of benign,
educational queries near sensitive topics re-
mains common and is not surfaced to parents,
revealing a policy–product gap between on-
screen safeguards and parent-facing telemetry.
We propose actionable ﬁxes: broaden/conﬁg-
ure the notiﬁcation taxonomy, couple visible
safeguards to privacy-preserving parent sum-
maries, and prefer calibrated, age-appropriate
safe rewrites over blanket refusals.

Introduction
Mainstream chat programs utilized by families
and schools are now driven by LLMs that are
aligned with safety. At the same time, platforms
have implemented parental controls, often pre-
sented as UI-level ﬂags and conﬁguration panels,
to regulate model behavior for children of various
ages.
Despite the importance of these platform
controls in product roadmaps and public advice,
it is still unknown how effective they are. Ven-
dors align models, then add UI policies, ﬁlters, and
routing layers, but it is unclear how these layers be-
have across ages, conversation lengths, and child-
authentic boundary-testing.
Prior academic research has demonstrated that
models are still susceptible to jailbreaks and role-
playing manipulations, frequently in fewer than
a few dozen queries and without white-box ac-
cess (e.g., PAIR’s black-box prompt reﬁnement;
persona-modulation attacks; generalized nested
prompts; simple black-box rewrites; and adaptive,
API-speciﬁc strategies) (Chao et al., 2024). More
recently, “jailbreaking-to-jailbreak” (J2) demon-
strates that one LLM may be persuaded to aid
in jailbreaking another, emphasizing the changing
nature of adversarial pressure on safety systems
(Kritz et al., 2025).
Nevertheless, these investi-
gations emphasize model-level alignment and as-
sault creation, as opposed to the end-user controls
and guard-rail routes used by consumer platforms
(age proﬁles, category gates, safe rewrites, warn-
ings, and logging/notiﬁcations).
With a regulated A/B assessment of ChatGPT
conﬁgured with parental restrictions off, and on,
we attempt to address this gap. Inspired by the
developmental agents from the ChildSafe frame-
work, we conduct multi-turn conversations in ar-
eas such as education, social/emotional develop-
ment, creativity, information-seeking (neutral/sen-
sitive), and boundary testing (Murali et al., 2025).
We capture both visible UI actions and back-end
behaviors (refusals, redirections, sanitized comple-
tions) by adding control-speciﬁc probes (such as
contact gating). Leak-through rate, over-blocking
rate, a circumvention-friction index (attempts re-
quired; slope over turns), safety latency, repair
quality, and test-retest consistency are all exam-
ples of outcome measures.

Background and Related Work
2.1
Platform controls vs. model alignment
Although safety alignment is often implemented
at the model level (data ﬁltering, SFT/RLHF, re-
fusal training), consumer deployments frequently
incorporate platform-layer controls (age proﬁles,
ﬁlters, topic gates, telemetry). The literature listed
below demonstrates the consistent jailbreakability
of aligned models, raising the crucial question of
how platform-layer parental restrictions causally
impact safety and usefulness across ages and di-
alogue lengths.
2.2
Jailbreak and red-teaming methods
• Black-box iterative reﬁnement (PAIR). Chao
et al. introduce PAIR, where an attacker LLM
iteratively reﬁnes prompts against a target
LLM, often succeeding in <20 queries and
transferring across open/closed models, high-
lighting realistic attack efﬁciency in API-only
settings (Chao et al., 2024).
• Persona modulation. Shah et al. demonstrate
that steering a target into compliant personas
yields large jumps in harmful completion
rates (e.g., GPT-4), and that such attacks en-
able sustained “unrestricted chat” modes, dis-
tinct from one-shot prompt jailbreaks (Shah
et al., 2023).
• Generalized nested prompts (ReNeLLM).
Ding et al. unify jailbreaks via prompt rewrit-
ing and scenario nesting, showing improved
attack success and transferability, and ana-
lyzing why defenses fail under changes to
prompt execution priority (Ding et al., 2024).
• Simple black-box rewrites.
Takemoto pro-
poses a minimal method that iteratively trans-
forms harmful prompts into seemingly be-
nign expressions using the target LLM itself,
achieving high success rates in a handful of
iterations, and stressing robustness to model
updates (Takemoto, 2024).
• Adaptive/API-aware
attacks.
An-
driushchenko et al.
show that simple
adaptive strategies leveraging logprob ac-
cess (or transfer/preﬁll for APIs without
logprobs) can reach near-saturated attack
success across leading safety-aligned mod-
els, arguing that adaptivity to model/API
idiosyncrasies is crucial (Andriushchenko
et al., 2025).
• Jailbreaking-to-jailbreak (J2).
Kritz et al.
study convincing an LLM to assist in jail-
breaking the same or another target LLM, re-
porting high attack success for capable rea-
soning models and emphasizing the rapid
evolution of attacker strength over the past
year (Kritz et al., 2025).
2.3
“Curious Child” vs. “Adversarial Adult”
The majority of jailbreak studies presume either
hostile adults or automated attackers. Children in-
teract in a qualitatively different way, using tactics
like repetition, literalization, appeals to empathy,
and topic smuggling through stories or homework
frameworks. As a result, our evaluation places a
high premium on child-authentic boundary testing
and recovery advice, utilizing agents and labeling.
This enhances model-level red-teaming by assess-
ing platform controls in realistic, chaotic, multi-
turn conversations with children.
2.4
Over-blocking and educational utility
Parental controls that protect children might also
restrict harmless instructional assistance, such as
tutoring on related topics. For this reason, we link
safety results with educational effects and job com-
pletion in an effort to measure trade-offs and pin-
point instances in which "safe rewrites" are prefer-
able to outright prohibitions, particularly for ado-
lescents who are prone to miscalibration when
blocks are too restrictive.
3
Methodology
3.1
Overview
We conducted a two-phase study. In Phase I, us-
ing an iterative-reﬁnement method modeled after
PAIR-style black-box prompt evolution, an API
was used to create a conversation corpus. In Phase
II, human agents replayed/reﬁned those prompts in
the consumer web product using an experimental
child account with parental controls enabled, and
we then monitored parental notiﬁcations sent to
the associated parent email. The automated judge
model was used to ensure the safety labeling of
assistant replies, while speciﬁc subsets were sub-
jected to manual audit.

3.2
Harm Categories
We targeted seven content categories: (i) physical
harm, (ii) pornography, (iii) privacy violence, (iv)
health consultation, (v) fraud, (vi) hate speech, and
(vii) malware. Category templates speciﬁed for-
bidden/allowed facets, age-appropriate cover sto-
ries (e.g., “homework,” “story,” “I’m scared”), and
escalation ladders for reﬁnement.
4
Corpus Construction via Iterative
Reﬁnement (API)
• Seed generation. We used Claude Sonnet 4.5
to produce seed prompts for each category
and age band.
• Reﬁnement loop. For each seed, we ran an
API-side loop: propose →query target →
score outcome →mutate prompt.
Scoring
favored (a) semantic proximity to category
intent, (b) evasion of generic refusals, and
(c) diversity across paraphrases.
Mutations
included style shifts (child/teen voice), nar-
rative framing, indirection (story/homework),
and lexical rewrites.
• Stopping criteria. A path terminated on reach-
ing a response classiﬁed as inappropriate by
the judge (below) or after 15 unsuccessful
mutations.

4.3
Safety Labeling and Adjudication
Primary judge. With category labels, ChatGPT 5
served as an automated judge, classifying assistant
responses as acceptable, borderline, or unaccept-
able. The judge acted on immediate context and
complete response text (k=2 previous turns).
Audit and calibration. We calibrated the judge
on a 10% stratiﬁed sample with human annota-
tions, ﬁne-tuned decision thresholds (giving pri-
ority to recalling obviously hazardous material),
and then reviewed 10% of the judge’s negative re-
sponses as well as all of the judge’s positive an-
swers. A senior rater made the decisions regarding
disagreements.
4.4
Model Conditions
We evaluated three deployment epochs: the con-
temporaneous product (“current”) and two legacy
model backends exposed in product history, GPT-
4.1 and GPT-4o.
API corpus construction used
the current backend; legacy conditions were tested
during replay windows documented in our run log.

The notiﬁcation pipeline seems

5.3
Overblocking
We noticed overblocking on innocuous educa-
tional prompts related to delicate subjects, such
as anatomy for homework, historical analysis that
used slurs in the quoted context, or cybersecurity
hygiene that was presented as "how not to get
hacked." Even when prompts were clearly edu-
cational in nature, the use of category keywords
caused the most pronounced OBR. By overblock-
ing, educational value was diminished, and occa-
sionally people were denied access to reliable sum-
maries that may be more beneﬁcial than outright
denial.
5.4
Visible UI Interventions
In pornography and prompts for physical harm,
UI interventions (warnings, safe rewrites, "I can’t
help with that, but here’s a safer alternative") were
frequent, in health they were sometimes, and in the
four categories with no notiﬁcations, they were un-
common. Notably, some borderline outputs (such
as euphemistic sexual material) showed up in the
chat without a warning, even though there was
obvious safety language, indicating a discrepancy
between the on-screen safeguards and the alerts
given to parents further down the line.

Discussion
Parental
notiﬁcations
in
the
current
con-
sumer
deployment
are
selective
rather
than
all-encompassing, according to our results. Even
when judge labels in those areas highlighted bor-
derline or unacceptable material, we did not see
any parental warnings for privacy violence, fraud,
hate speech, or malware, despite employing a
PAIR-style corpus and human-in-the-loop replays
that covered seven risk categories.
In contrast,
actual harm (and, to a lesser extent, pornography
and health advice) generated sporadic alarms.
According to this pattern, the notiﬁcation system
either (a) prioritizes a limited set of harms, (b)
relies on keyword- or classiﬁer-driven triggers
that are tuned for these categories, or (c) is subject
to policy restrictions that deliberately exclude
certain areas (such as privacy or fraud) from
parental escalation via email.
At the same time, we noted that educational
prompts pertaining to delicate topics (e.g., ba-
sic anatomy, historical analysis with quoted slurs,
benign cybersecurity hygiene) were excessively
blocked.
There is a calibration mismatch be-
tween the parent-facing telemetry and the user-
facing assistant, as seen by the coexistence of
under-coverage (no alerts for certain high-risk
categories) and overblocking (benign queries re-
jected). This gap is made worse by cases when
obvious UI protections (warnings or safe rewrites)
didn’t result in any parent notiﬁcations, so that car-
ers were left in the dark about any friction or bor-
derline replies the kid received.
Under the same prompt families that the present
backend opposed,
legacy backends (GPT-4.1,
GPT-4o) created content that was somewhat in-
appropriate, in accordance with continuous align-
ment advancements. However, since the notiﬁca-
tion proﬁle did not change simultaneously for the
un-alerted categories, families that depend only on
email notiﬁcations may have an exaggerated sense
of security in those sectors. Design implications.
We see three actionable fronts for platforms:
1. Coverage & taxonomy.
Expand notiﬁca-
tion coverage (or provide conﬁgurable op-
tions) for non-trivial categories like privacy
violence, fraud, hate, and malware. Expose a
transparent notiﬁcation taxonomy to parents.
2. Calibrated routing. Prefer safe rewrites and
guided education over blanket refusals when
prompts are plausibly instructional.
Pair
this with on-by-default parent summaries that
aggregate blocked-but-benign interactions so
caregivers can adjust controls.
3. Consistency and feedback loops.
Align
on-screen safeguards with downstream alerts
via explicit coupling:
if the child sees
a safety warning or sanitized completion,
the parent should be able to see a com-
pact, privacy-preserving summary and recom-
mended follow-up (e.g., family conversation
starters, resource links).
7
Conclusion
According to our protocol, parental controls for
conversational assistants have a policy–product
gap in that alerts are focused in certain areas (phys-
ical harm, aspects of sexual content, some health
concerns) but other important threats (privacy vio-
lence, fraud, hate speech, malware) do not trigger
any parental alerts. In the meanwhile, overblock-
ing restricts instructional content for harmless,

nearby searches, particularly when expressed in
age-typical language.
Although the current backend is better aligned
than older versions, the advancements in model re-
jections and safe rewrites have not kept up with the
equally thorough and transparent parental teleme-
try. In order to better assist families, platforms
should: (1) expand or allow users to customize the
notiﬁcation taxonomy, (2) link on-screen protec-
tions with parent-facing summaries, and (3) prior-
itize age-appropriate, calibrated safe rewrites over
outright rejections when possible.
Future research should focus on extending mul-
tilingual coverage, incorporating longer multi-turn
trajectories with context carryover, comparing
parental ecosystems across platforms, and assess-
ing dashboard-style parental summaries that high-
light both risks and blocked-but-benign learning
efforts. When taken as a whole, these measures
can move parental controls from simply reacting
to notiﬁcations to proactively and developmentally
supporting children in having safer and more pro-
ductive interactions with artiﬁcial intelligence.
8
Ethical Considerations
All of the experiments were carried out using simu-
lated minors and a dedicated “minor” account that
was under the control of the research staff; no ac-
tual children were involved. With clear researcher
control and audit logging, email monitoring was
focused exclusively on the connected parent inbox
established for this research. The only goal of our
safety assessment and prompt creation was to eval-
uate safety and notiﬁcation behavior. Any creation
of dangerous material was incidental due to the na-
ture of our work. We reduced exposure to egre-
gious content by using red-team prompts that fo-
cus on meta-discussion and safety framing when-
ever possible, limiting the depth of iterative reﬁne-
ment, and ﬁltering out explicit sexual material that
would be unlawful to store.
We adhere to a responsible disclosure stance
since the topic involves operational defenses:
rather than publishing verbatim prompts that con-
sistently bypass protections, we publish category-
level results and high-level patterns. Any publicly
shared artifacts will be protected by a contributor
code of conduct, have usage warnings, and have
sensitive strings removed.
9
Limitations and Threats to Validity
Scope and generalizability.
Our corpus was
tuned to seven categories and a speciﬁc consumer
product conﬁguration; results may not general-
ize to other categories, languages, or platforms.
While we balanced prompts across categories and
age bands, we did not aim to estimate population
prevalence of unsafe outcomes.
Seed prompt authenticity. Our seed prompts
were generated by Claude Sonnet 4.5 rather than
derived from naturalistic child–chatbot interac-
tion logs. While we designed category templates
with age-appropriate cover stories (e.g., “home-
work,” “story,” “I’m scared”) and escalation lad-
ders informed by developmental literature, we did
not validate these seeds with child development
specialists, linguists, or against corpora of au-
thentic child discourse.
Real children may em-
ploy idiosyncratic phrasing, spelling errors, code-
switching, or pragmatic strategies (e.g., emo-
tional appeals, repetition, topic drift) that LLM-
generated prompts fail to capture.
Future work
should incorporate expert review from child psy-
chology or sociolinguistics, or derive seeds from
ethically obtained naturalistic interaction data, to
strengthen ecological validity. Measurement con-
straints:
• Notiﬁcation pipeline opacity. We treat noti-
ﬁcations as a black box; missing alerts could
reﬂect policy gating, classiﬁer thresholds, de-
liverability issues, or email batching.
• Judge-model bias. Using ChatGPT 5 as the
primary judge risks model–model correlation
with the target assistant and category-speciﬁc
precision/recall trade-offs. We mitigated this
with human audits and sensitivity checks, but
residual bias is possible.
• UI vs. API drift. Prompts evolved via API
then replayed in the UI. Differences in rout-
ing, context windows, or safety layers could
inﬂuence outcomes.
• Temporal variance.
Alignment and notiﬁ-
cation policies evolve. Although we logged
timestamps and ran sensitivity checks, tempo-
ral drift and day-of-week effects (e.g., latency
or batching) may affect TTN and coverage.
• Human-agent variability.
Trained agents
paraphrased minimally, but micro-variations
