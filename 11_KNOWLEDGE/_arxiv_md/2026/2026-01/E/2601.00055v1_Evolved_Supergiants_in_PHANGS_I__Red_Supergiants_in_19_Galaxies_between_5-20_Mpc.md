ABSTRACT
Red supergiants (RSGs) are important for our understanding of supernova progenitors, stellar pop-
ulations, stellar evolution, mass loss and dust production. Extragalactic surveys of RSGs have a long
history in the Local Group, but few studies exist beyond that due to the limited resolution and sen-
sitivity of ground-based and previous space-based infrared observatories. Here we demonstrate the
combined power of HST and JWST to push systematic searches of RSGs out to ∼20 Mpc. We in-
troduce a catalog of 97057 RSGs —the largest single-survey release of RSGs— with masses ≳10 M⊙
in 19 galaxies from the PHANGS HST+JWST Treasury program. We use HST F814W and JWST
F200W photometry to select stars as RSGs based on predicted colors and magnitudes from PARSEC
isochrones. The spatial distribution of our recovered RSGs follow the familiar pattern of mostly be-
ing concentrated in active star-forming regions such as spiral arms and central starburst rings. The
RSG number density on kpc-scales is strongly correlated (rs∼0.82) with local star-formation rate den-
sity (ΣSF R) traced by extinction-corrected far-ultraviolet (FUV) from GALEX+WISE, and weakly
correlated (rs∼0.57) with the total stellar mass density (Σ∗), traced by near-infrared emission from
WISE+Spitzer. The number of RSGs per mass of stellar populations with ages 6-30 Myr (the likely
age range of RSGs >10M⊙) is ∼1 per 103.77±0.27 M⊙, assuming constant star-formation rates from
FUV+W4. Our sample will be a useful resource for tracking progenitors and feedback sites of future
supernovae in PHANGS,age-dating stellar populations, and more.

1. INTRODUCTION
Red supergiants (RSGs) typically refer to K or M-type
supergiants that have evolved from stars with zero-age
main-sequence (ZAMS) masses > 8 M⊙(E. M. Levesque
2018).
Studies of RSGs are essential for many areas
of astrophysics. They have been confirmed as progen-
itors of various subtypes of Type II supernovae (SNe)
(S. J. Smartt 2009, 2015), making the understanding of
their pre-SN evolution vital. In stellar evolution, RSGs
are important laboratories for understanding mass-loss
mechanisms in cool supergiants (e.g., A. Maeder 1981;
C. Chiosi & A. Maeder 1986; J. T. van Loon et al. 2005;
E. R. Beasor et al. 2020), binary evolution models (e.g.,
K. F. Neugent et al. 2019; L. R. Patrick et al. 2020; K. F.
Neugent et al. 2020; L. R. Patrick et al. 2022), metal-
licity, stellar rotation, and binary fraction diagnostics
(A. Maeder et al. 1980; P. Massey 2003; J. J. Eldridge
et al. 2017; P. Massey et al. 2021a). For stellar feed-
back studies, RSGs serve as markers of future Type II
SNe in nearby galaxies, enabling spatially-resolved stud-
ies of their host stellar populations (e.g., J. P. Anderson
& P. A. James 2008; T. Kangas et al. 2017) and future
SN feedback sites (N. Mayker Chen et al. 2023a; S. K.
Sarbadhicary et al. 2023; N. Mayker Chen et al. 2024a).
Many of these topics have greatly benefited from ex-
tragalactic surveys of RSGs.
Such studies were pio-
neered using ground-based photometric surveys of Lo-
cal Group galaxies (≲1.2 Mpc, e.g. P. Massey 1998,
2002, 2003; P. Massey et al. 2009; P. Massey & K. A.
Evans 2016). Follow-up spectroscopy confirmed many
of these photometrically-identified massive stars as cool
supergiants with bolometric luminosities and tempera-
tures in the region expected for RSGs (e.g., P. Massey &
K. A. G. Olsen 2003; E. M. Levesque et al. 2006; M. R.
Drout et al. 2012; K. F. Neugent et al. 2012). Availabil-
ity of NIR photometry (e.g., JHK) and Gaia astrometry
significantly improved the fidelity of RSG catalogs by ef-
fectively removing foreground dwarfs and asymptotic gi-
ant branch (AGB) stars (e.g. A. Z. Bonanos et al. 2009,
2010; M. L. Boyer et al. 2011; M. Yang et al. 2019; K. F.
Neugent et al. 2020; P. Massey et al. 2021b).
Surveys of RSGs in galaxies beyond the Local Group
have been attempted out to distances of ∼5 Mpc (e.g.,
J. Z. Gazak et al. 2015; L. R. Patrick et al. 2017; R.
Khan 2017; S.-H. Chun et al. 2017; A. Z. Bonanos et al.
2023; G. Maravelias et al. 2025).
The limited resolu-
tion of ground-based optical and near-infrared observa-
tories, or even previous-generation space observatories
like Spitzer, have made identifying RSGs challenging at
larger distances.
The superior angular resolution of HST and JWST al-
lows identification of RSGs in galaxies out to ≳20 Mpc.
This capability has been demonstrated in identification
of the progenitors of individual core-collapse SNe (for a
review, see S. J. Smartt 2015; S. D. Van Dyk 2025) and
surveys of individual galaxies (e.g., J. R. Johnson et al.
2023; A. S. Hirschauer et al. 2024, targeting NGC 6946
and I Zw 18) but not previously deployed over a large
sample of galaxies.
Here we push the limits of extragalactic RSG surveys
and construct catalogs in 19 star-forming galaxies at dis-
tances between 5–20 Mpc using the PHANGS (Physics
at High Angular Resolution in Nearby Galaxies) HST
(J. C. Lee et al. 2022) and JWST Treasury (J. C. Lee
et al. 2023) data. The PHANGS surveys provide sub-
stantial data for exploring the correlation between RSGs
and the multi-age stellar populations and the multi-
phase ISM in the surrounding galaxy. Relevant catalogs
in PHANGS include: molecular clouds (E. Rosolowsky
et al. 2021; J. Sun et al. 2022), infant dust-embedded
star clusters (M. J. Rodr´ıguez et al. 2025; G. B. Gra-
ham et al. 2025; H. Hassani et al. 2025; B. C. Whitmore
et al. 2023), optically visible star clusters and associa-
tions (D. Maschmann et al. 2024; D. A. Thilker et al.
2025; K. L. Larson et al. 2023), supernova remnants (J.
Li et al. 2024), HII regions (E. Emsellem et al. 2022;
F. Santoro et al. 2022; B. Groves et al. 2023), and dust
maps tracing the cold ISM (A. K. Leroy et al. 2023; R.
Chown et al. 2025).
The paper is structured as follows:
Section 2 de-
tails the HST+JWST observations used in this paper,
and supporting multiwavelength data to characterize the
RSGs. Section 3 details the photometry, identification
and spatial properties of the recovered RSGs. Section
4 discusses the correlation with local kpc-averaged star-
formation rates, and Section 5 provides a summary of
our results.

2.2. Star-formation tracers from PHANGS MegaTables
PHANGS provides a number of supporting datasets
which we can use to understand the statistics of the
recovered RSG population.
In this paper we use the
PHANGS MegaTables constructed by J. Sun et al.
(2022, 2023), which estimate relevant star-formation
and ISM measurements from multi-wavelength maps in
hexagonal bins of ∼1.95 kpc2 (the large bins are neces-
sitated by the limiting resolution of the WISE 22 µm
maps used for attenuation correction). While these bins
may smooth over finer sub-kpc scale spatial correla-
tions between RSGs and star-forming regions, we be-
lieve the MegaTables are a good starting point as all
the SFR measurements and their inter-correlations have
been carefully calibrated and vetted by J. Sun et al.
(2023). The MegaTable apertures are large enough to
avoid low number statistics at low SFRs, and to treat
the RSGs and other star-formation within the apertures
as approximately co-eval, even after some stellar drifting
and mixing. We refer the reader to J. Sun et al. (2022)

3. RED SUPERGIANT SELECTION
We use the HST and JWST data introduced in Section
2 to find RSGs in our 19 targets. We use the F814W
(0.8 µm) and F200W (2 µm) filters for the selection.
These filters are close to the peak of the RSG spectral en-
ergy distribution (SED), which typically occurs around
∼1 µm.
This makes them the most suitable among
our available filters for identifying RSGs (Figure 1). To
construct the catalogs, we start with PSF photometry
of genuine stars with DOLPHOT (Section 3.1).
We se-
lect RSGs based on predicted F814W−F200W color and
magnitudes (Section 3.2). The we report the observed
colors, magnitudes (Section 3.3), and spatial properties
(Section 3.4) of recovered RSGs in the sample of 19
galaxies.

Isochrones were de-
rived every 0.2 dex in logarithmic age, and sampled with
the initial mass function from P. Kroupa (2001, 2002).
These tracks are shown in shaded color in Figure 1.
We show them for two value of metallicities18, [M/H]=0
and [M/H]=−0.5, which roughly covers the metallicity
ranges observed in these 19 galaxies (B. Groves et al.
2023; I. Pessa et al. 2023).
Aside from stellar tracks >8 M⊙that will evolve into
RSGs, we also show 1-8 M⊙tracks from CMD as gray
isochrones in Figure 1 (left, middle panels) to show
the expected positions of asymptotic giant branch stars
(AGBs) in F814W−F200W. These are associated with
copious dust production, and are a common source of
contamination in RSG catalogs (P. Massey et al. 2021b).
The AGB tracks were modeled in PARSEC using COL-
IBRI atmopsheric models (P. Marigo et al. 2013; P.
Rosenfield et al. 2016; G. Pastorelli et al. 2019, 2020),
and the default circumstellar dust composition of 60%
Silicate and 40% AlOx for M-stars, and 85% Amorphous
Carbon and 15% Silicon Carbide for C-stars (M. A. T.
Groenewegen 2006; P. Marigo et al. 2008).
PARSEC
provides several other options for AGB dust composi-
tion with variations in the C, Si, and AlOx abundances,
but we found that our RSG selection defined below is
robust to these variations.
We define the RSG selection region as the thick dashed
polygon in Figure 1.
The design of the region is in-
tended to minimize contamination from hotter yellow
supergiants and cooler AGBs/super-AGBs, and can be
explained as follows:
1. The
blue
end
of
the
polygon
is
at
F814W−F200W=2 mag,
in order to restrict
to stars cooler than 4000 K, appropriate for K/M
spectral type expected for RSGs (E. M. Levesque
et al. 2005; E. M. Levesque 2018).
The color
boundary should also exclude any Milky Way
foreground dwarfs in the direction of our galaxies,
based on experiments with the TRILEGAL code
(L. Girardi et al. 2005; E. Vanhollebeke et al.
2009).

3.4. Spatial Properties
Figures 4 and 5 show the spatial distribution of the
RSGs in all 19 galaxies, alongside the HST images for
structural reference. The RSG distribution is clumpy,
mostly appearing concentrated to the spiral arms, cen-
ters and other prominent areas of galaxies where recent
star-formation is dominant. This is expected given the
young ages (≲40 Myr) of RSGs. Similar pattern RSGs
being concentrated in star-forming regions is also ob-
served in Local Group galaxies (e.g P. Massey et al.
2021b).
For most galaxies, the RSGs appear concen-
trated in the spiral arms, especially in galaxies like NGC
1566, NGC 4321, and NGC 4254.
In some cases like
NGC 4321, NGC 1433, and NGC 1512, RSGs are also
distributed along the central starburst rings.
Figure 4 and 5 also illustrates difference between the
more luminous and younger RSGs (darker red dots in
Figure 1, see Section 3.2 for definition) and the bulk
sample (lighter red dots). The younger RSGs appear
more strongly correlated with the most active star-
formation sites than the total RSG sample. We show
this quantitatively based on the percentages of young
vs. total RSGs in different environmental features in
our galaxies, using masks defined in M. Querejeta et al.
(2019). We find that central starburst rings host a three
times larger fraction of young RSGs compared to to-
tal RSGs.
For example, NGC 3351 hosts 40% of the
younger RSG sample in the central ring compared to
13% of the full RSG sample. Similarly the fraction in
NGC 4321 jumps from 7% for the total RSGs to 23%
for the younger RSGs. For NGC 1512, we find a jump
of 10% (total RSGs) to 29% (younger RSGs).

These correlations are consistent with the RSGs be-
ing young (>10 M⊙, ages ≲30 Myr) stars such that
their abundance reflects star formation activity on sim-
ilar timescales to commonly used star formation trac-
ers (see reviews in R. C. Kennicutt 1998; R. C. Ken-
nicutt & N. J. Evans 2012; E. Schinnerer & A. K.

FUV emission traces star-formation in
the last ∼100 Myr, with a simple stellar population
emitting roughly equal total energy between 0 −10 Myr
and 10 −100 Myr. The light-weighted Hα traces star-
formation in the last 10 Myr, with a simple stellar pop-
ulation emitting half of its total luminosity before 3
Myr. Mid-IR emission, including the 22µm emission in-
cluded in our FUV+WISE4 tracer, has a more ambigu-
ous timescale but is often taken to reflect star formation
over a similar timescale to the FUV. Meanwhile, the to-
tal stellar mass is mostly dominated by the older (≳Gyr)
stellar population which does not produce RSGs.

cently formed stars based on the data in Figure 6. The
number of RSGs per FUV+W4 SFR is about 103.60±0.27

counts (<3 RSGs per kpc2) and high SFRs where ex-
tinction and crowding effects could be high (>10−1.2
M⊙yr−1 kpc−2).

We checked these observed ratios against synthetic
populations generated with PARSEC to perform a san-
ity check.
Using the CMD interface, we created 20 re-
alizations of synthetic composite stellar populations at
solar metallicity and zero extinction, each consisting of
simple stellar populations with initial masses of 104 M⊙,
formed at ages between 3 and 100 Myr at an interval of
∆t = 2 Myr. This is equivalent to generating a compos-
ite stellar population at a constant star-formation rate of
5 × 10−3 M⊙yr−1, which falls within the range of SFRs
observed over kpc-scales in Figure 6. We identify RSGs
as the stars with log(T/K) < 3.6 and log(L/L⊙) ≥4.3,
which covers approximately the same F814W−F200W
for the RSGs in Figure 1.
Averaged over the 20 re-
alizations, we get 40 ± 7 RSGs. When we divide this
by the modeled SFR, we get roughly 103.9±0.8 RSGs
per (M⊙yr−1).
When divided by the mass of stars
formed at ages between 6 and 30 Myr, we derive a ratio
of 10−3.50±0.08 RSGs per M⊙.
This is slightly higher
than our observed ratio of 103.6 RSGs per (M⊙yr−1)
and 10−3.77±0.27 RSGs per M⊙formed between 6 and
30 Myr.
Thus our modeling predicts approximately twice as
many RSGs as we recover given the estimated star for-
mation rates.
The discrepancy could reflect incom-
pleteness due to crowding or extinction, particularly
in the lower mass, lower luminosity RSGs, which are
also more numerous.
Alternatively, it could reflect
a systematic overestimate of the SFR or systematic
variations in the star formation history, e.g., gaps on
≈6−30 Myr timescales, across our sample. A signifi-
cant overestimate of the SFR seems unlikely given the
good correspondence between Hα+Hβ based SFRs and
FUV+WISE4 SFRs shown by F. Belfiore et al. (2023)
specifically for this sample. And we would expect vari-
ations in the SFH on short timescales to produce large
fluctuations in Fig. 6. A combination of the above could
e.g explain the notable low deviates in NGC 1365 or
NGC 4321 in Fig. 6 at ≳10−1.2 M⊙yr−1 kpc−2. Alter-
natively, physical reasons related to stellar populations
such as evolution of a significant number of RSGs in in-
teracting binary systems, could also lead to a reduction
in RSGs per mass of stars (e.g T. Z. Dorn-Wallenstein
& E. M. Levesque 2018, 2020).
5. CONCLUSION
We have recovered a catalog of 97057 RSGs—the
largest single survey of red-supergiants (RSGs) —in a
sample of 19 galaxies with stellar masses between 109
and 1011 M⊙from the PHANGS-HST and PHANGS-
JWST surveys. We derived the catalog from DOLPHOT
PSF photometry in the filters closest to the peak wave-
length of RSGs—UVIS/WFC3 F814W and NIRCAM
F200W bands. RSGs were selected from the predicted
positions of stars cooler than 4000 K and above >10
M⊙in F814W−F200W, as predicted by stellar isochrone
models from PARSEC. The region was designed to mini-
mize contamination with AGB stars, foreground dwarfs,
and hotter supergiants.
We mainly carry out a zeroth order analysis of the
RSG population in this paper, focusing on the num-
ber and spatial statistics, as well as correlation with
kpc-scale measurements of star-formation rates from the
PHANGS MegaTables from J. Sun (2022). We find that:
• The number of RSGs recovered in these galaxies
ranges from a few hundred (e.g 453 in IC5332) to
over 104 (NGC 4303).
• The distribution of the RSGs closely trace promi-
nent sites of recent star-formation in galaxies,
such as central starburst rings, and spiral arms.
Younger (>14 M⊙) RSGs are more strongly spa-
tially correlated with high star-formation sites,
such as central starburst rings, than the total RSG
sample.
• The RSG number density is strongly correlated
with the FUV+W4 based star-formation rates,
with a Spearman correlation coefficient rs=0.82.
Similarly
strong
correlation
is
obtained
with
SFRs based on Hα, and only a weak correlation
(rs=0.57) is obtained with stellar mass.
• We find a consistent production rate of 103.6±0.27
RSG per (M⊙yr−1), or 1 RSG per 103.77±0.27 M⊙
of stars formed between 6-30 Myr in the 19 galax-
ies, assuming the average star-formation rate dur-
ing this period given by FUV+W4 based SFRs.
The observed rates are slightly lower than pre-
dictions of 1 RSG per 103.5±0.08 M⊙from stel-
lar population models, and could be due to as-

sumptions about star-formation histories, incom-
pleteness in high extinction/crowding regions, and
physical factors such as binary evolution.
The paper breaks new ground by pushing systematic
searches of RSGs in a large sample of galaxies with dis-
tances up to 20 Mpc by leveraging the combined power
of HST and JWST NIRCam, a feat that was impossible
with previous observatories. Our catalog will be pub-
lished with the journal, and serve as a valuable resource
for characterizing progenitors of future Type II super-
novae in the PHANGS sample (e.g S. D. Van Dyk et al.
2023; C. D. Kilpatrick et al. 2025), age-dating and ob-
taining census of star-formation in the 6-30 Myr range
(e.g J. J. Eldridge et al. 2020; S. Hannon et al. 2022),
and studying correlations of future supernova sites with
star clusters, associations and molecular clouds (e.g T.
Kangas et al. 2017; S. K. Sarbadhicary et al. 2023; N.
Mayker Chen et al. 2023b, 2024b).
In upcoming papers, we will continue refining the cat-
alog with artificial star tests for more systematically
characterizing the completeness in different regions of
the galaxies, and estimating bolometric properties and
line-of-sight extinction (K. D. Gordon et al. 2016; C. W.
Lindberg et al. 2025). Our catalog can be cross-matched
with MIRI data for investigation on dust production in
RSGs, expanding upon the works by H. Hassani et al.
(2025) and D. Maschmann et al. (2025). Future obser-
vations using JWST filters near 1 µm, such as F090W
and F115W can significantly improve the RSG selection
at lower masses (≲10 M⊙) by not only being higher-
resolution blue filters than F814W, but also less sensi-
tive to extinction.
