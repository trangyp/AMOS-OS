Abstract
In blockchain systems operating under the Proof-of-Stake (PoS) consen-
sus mechanism, fairness in transaction processing is essential to preserving
decentralization and maintaining user trust. However, with the emergence of
Maximal Extractable Value (MEV), concerns about economic centralization
and content manipulation have intensified.
To address these vulnerabili-
ties, the Ethereum community has introduced Proposer Builder Separation
(PBS), which separates block construction from block proposal. Later, en-
shrined Proposer Builder Separation (ePBS) was also proposed in EIP-7732,
which embeds PBS directly into the Ethereum consensus layer.
Our work identifies key limitations of ePBS by developing a formal frame-
work that combines mathematical analysis and agent-based simulations to
evaluate its auction-based block-building mechanism, with particular em-
phasis on MEV dynamics. Our results reveal that, although ePBS redis-
tributes responsibilities between builders and proposers, it significantly am-
plifies profit and content centralization: the Gini coefficient for profits rises
from 0.1749 under standard PoS without ePBS to 0.8358 under ePBS. This
sharp increase indicates that a small number of efficient builders capture
most value via MEV-driven auctions. Moreover, 95.4% of the block value is
rewarded to proposers in ePBS, revealing a strong economic bias despite their
limited role in block assembly. These findings highlight that ePBS exacer-

1. Introduction
Ethereum’s transition to Proof-of-Stake (PoS) represents a major shift in
its consensus mechanism, but has not eliminated economic vulnerabilities.
A critical concern arises from Maximal Extractable Value (MEV), the value
extractable through the inclusion, exclusion, or reordering of transactions.
MEV creates strong incentives for manipulation, and evidence shows that
between late 2023 and early 2024, just three builders produced nearly 80% of
all Ethereum blocks [9, 32]. Such concentration threatens decentralization,
fairness, and credibility of the consensus protocol.
To address these issues, Proposer Builder Separation (PBS) was proposed
by Buterin [38]. By isolating the roles of proposers and builders, PBS seeks to
reduce validators’ discretion over transaction ordering and promote a more
balanced market.
Its protocol-native variant, enshrined Proposer Builder
Separation (ePBS), has since been drafted in EIP-7732 [13], aiming to elimi-
nate dependence on third-party relays and mitigate censorship concerns [30].
The Ethereum community is currently debating ePBS as a fundamental re-
design of the consensus layer [14].
Yet the core question remains unresolved: does ePBS actually mitigate
centralization and manipulation, and/or does it introduce new risks? Exist-
ing studies [19, 40, 1] primarily focus on empirical observations of relay-based
PBS and do not provide a formal framework to analyze the system-level in-
centives under ePBS. As a result, the long-term implications of embedding
PBS into Ethereum’s protocol remain unclear.
In this work, we conduct a comprehensive study of ePBS, combining for-
mal modeling with mathematical analysis and agent-based simulations. Our
framework represents the monetary utility of users, builders, and proposers,
enabling us to evaluate how MEV-driven auctions’ equilibrium outcomes. We
focus on two research questions:
RQ1: What is the effect of ePBS on fairness and decentraliza-
tion? We examine (1) how block construction decentralization differs be-
tween PoS and ePBS, (2) how the profit distribution differs between builders

and validators under the two systems, and (3) how profit restaking and ac-
cumulation affect long-term concentration.
RQ2: What is the effect of ePBS on transaction manipulation?
We investigate whether the auction-based design reduces transaction reorder-

Our Contributions. This paper makes the following contributions:
1. We develop a formal framework of users, builders, and proposers in
ePBS, and use it to analyze the single block auction dynamics and long-

2. We implement calibrated agent-based simulations using Ethereum on-

decentralized under ePBS, builders capture an overwhelming share
of MEV, amplifying market concentration and transaction reordering
compared to PoS.
3. We identify a fundamental limitation of ePBS: although it achieves de-
centralization at the proposer level, it intensifies centralization among
builders and worsens MEV-driven manipulation, raising concerns for
Ethereum’s long-term fairness and security.

In this section, we elaborate on the background knowledge related to
ePBS, including its consensus setting, protocol details, and prior studies.
2.1. Current Ethereum Consensus Mechanism: PoS
Since Ethereum’s consensus layer transitioned to PoS [12], only nodes
that have deposited 32 ETH are eligible to become validators. A validator is
responsible for building a block during a specific time window called a slot—
a fixed, continuous 12-second interval. Validators are randomly chosen two
slots in advance from eligible participants to propose a block. If the assigned
validator fails to propose a block, the slot remains empty, leading to a small
percentage of skipped slots [2]. This process is illustrated in Figure 1a.
Ethereum’s consensus mechanism faces challenges and concerns such as
MEV, transaction censorship, and centralization [28], which we further dis-

2.2. PBS and ePBS
PBS is an Ethereum-based mechanism aiming to increase the decentral-
ization of profit and block building [33] by decoupling block construction
from block proposal. In its current deployment of relay-based PBS or MEV-
Boost [15], these roles interact through trusted off-chain relays that collect
bids from builders and forward only the winning header to the proposer. This
architecture employs an off-chain commit–reveal scheme, where builders first
send a block header and bid to the relay, and later reveal the full block con-
tent once the proposer commits. While effective in preventing proposers from
manipulating transactions, this design introduces new trust assumptions: re-
lays may censor bids, fail to deliver payloads, or extract informational rents,
and such behavior is unverifiable at the protocol level [19, 40].
ePBS, specified in EIP-7732 [13], integrates the proposer–builder interac-
tion directly into Ethereum’s consensus layer, removing the need for off-chain
relays. Bids, commitments, and payments are exchanged through protocol-
defined messages that are verifiable on chain, guaranteeing proposer pay-
ment and preventing builders from withholding payloads or altering bids
after commitment. By decoupling header attestation from full payload exe-
cution, ePBS improves liveness under network latency and enforces builder
4


## Page 5


payments at the consensus level. In essence, ePBS replaces the economically
trusted relay model of MEV-Boost with a protocol-native, cryptographically
accountable mechanism while maintaining the same first-price auction struc-
ture [29].
A key feature of the PBS and ePBS design is the commit–reveal scheme.
As illustrated in Figure 1b, in step 1 , builders transmit block headers and
bids without revealing transaction order. In step 2 , the proposer commits
to a header. Finally, in step 3 , the builder reveals the full block content.
Once committed, neither the proposer nor the builder can alter or reorder
transactions.
Builders submit bids every 0.5 seconds, i.e., 24 rounds per
slot [16]. Proposers are randomly selected per slot, consistent with validator
selection in PoS.
2.3. Maximal Extractable Value (MEV)
Ethereum’s PoS design allows validators to determine which transactions
to include and in what order, enabling the extraction of MEV through in-
clusion, exclusion, or reordering [8]. When the MEV of a block exceeds the
base block reward, validators may exclude transactions with MEV potential
and replace them with their own strategically ordered ones [9].
Typical MEV strategies include front-running [11], back-running [43],
sandwich attacks [48], censorship and omission [39], and arbitrage [47]. While
some forms, such as arbitrage, improve efficiency by aligning DEX prices [26],
others (e.g., sandwiching) degrade user experience through slippage and in-
creased confirmation time.
2.4. Existing Literature
Prior work has examined challenges in Ethereum consensus, PBS, and
MEV. For PBS, Heimbach et al. [19] studied decentralization across relays;
Gupta et al. [18] modeled private order flow auctions, showing integrated
builder–searchers gain persistent advantages; Bahrani et al. [1] provided a
theoretical model of MEV-driven centralization; Yang et al. [45] conducted
an empirical study of the builder market; Wang et al. [41] developed an
asymmetric auction model; and Capponi et al. [5] used game theory to show
reduced validator centralization but heightened builder concentration. Im-
provement proposals include Buterin’s partial block auctions [4] and Babu’s
modified PBS model [34], though both lacked validation via simulations.
Related work on MEV and transaction-level dynamics includes Grand-
jean et al. [17], who identified decentralization deficiencies in Ethereum PoS,

and Wahrstätter et al. [40], who analyzed the Ethereum block construction
market, revealing intricate MEV dynamics, with their follow-up [39] showing
censored transactions suffer an average delay of 85%. Early studies by Daian
et al. [9] and Eskandari et al. [11] formalized MEV, highlighting bot-driven
strategies that distort fairness. Subsequent work quantified MEV, e.g., Qin
et al. [35] and Weintraub et al. [42], while Chitra et al. [7] proposed redistri-

et al. [22] studied multi-block MEV dynamics.
Overall, while both empirical and theoretical studies provide insight, they
largely focus on relay-based PBS or isolated auction models. Little work
addresses ePBS directly or provides system-level formalization combined with
simulations. Our study fills this gap by modeling the strategic incentives of
users, builders, and proposers under ePBS, and analyzing decentralization
and MEV dynamics through both mathematical analysis and simulation.

All agent utilities are defined in terms of

3.3. Actions
In ePBS, each agent type has a set of possible actions that they can take.
Below, we outline the possible actions for users, builders, and proposers. For the
formalization, we will focus on single block dynamics, thus all the slot subscripts ℓ
are dropped.
3.3.1. User Actions
Creating An Attack Transaction. If τUi = attack, user Ui initiates a transaction
xk targeting another transaction xj = A(xk) where xj ∈MUi,t aimed at exploiting
MEV, achieved by setting the gas fee gk such that gk ≷gj for front and back
running respectively. Note that this does not mean the attack is successful; the
settlement happens when this transaction is confirmed on chain.
Creating A Benign Transaction. If τUi = benign, user Ui initiates a transaction
without targeting another transaction for MEV profits.
All user-initiated transactions can be attacked by other attacking users or
builders if there is MEV potential available to be exploited.
3.3.2. Builder Actions
Transaction Ordering. Builder Bi selects an ordered list of transactions Ti,t ⊆
MBi,t to include in its block at round t. If τBi = attack, the selection involves
inserting a list of transactions xBi,t that exploit MEV opportunities whenever prof-
itable. Specifically, this follows the logic of inserting their own 0-gas attack trans-
action if the profit from the attack exceeds the total gas fees from any transactions
displaced to accommodate the attack. Otherwise, the transaction is included as
it is. If τBi = benign, the selection will be only to select transactions from the
mempool and order them in descending order of gas fees.

5. Simulation Design
This section outlines the simulation framework developed to analyze the behav-
ior of various participants in a blockchain environment under different conditions.
Our use of agent-based simulation modeling follows established practice for study-
ing strategic interaction in auction-based and blockchain systems [6, 23, 31, 36].
Users, builders, proposers, and network latency are modeled according to the setup
in Section 3. The simulation code is available at our GitHub repository.
5.1. Assumptions and Parameters
The simulation is based on several key parameters and assumptions: For the
ePBS system, it includes 100 users, 50 builders, and 50 proposers; for the PoS
system, it includes 100 users and 50 validators, testing with the number of MEV-
seeking participants from none to all.
We choose 50 validators as a parameter
because we compare the validators with proposer and builders in ePBS separately,
used as a benchmark.
The block capacity is 100, and the gas fee is randomly
sampled, where both are based on the information fetched on-chain from Ethereum
mainnet data. We sample multiple periods after the merge in 2022, including stable
periods and highly volatile periods, for example, the USDC depeg, FTX crash,
and Terra Luna fall. MEV are sampled from the distribution given in FlashBot
data [10] which shows a Gamma distribution. The simulation primarily focuses on
simple MEV strategies such as front running, back running, and sandwich attacks.
Latency and network connectivity follow the design in Section 3, the specific graph
we choose for the simulation is the Erdos-Renyi random graph, which is often used
as a baseline to analyze the Ethereum network topology [25]. All other parameters
are kept the same throughout the simulation for consistency. Each simulation run
includes 1,000 blocks.
For analyzing long-term centralization effects, we extend the simulation with
additional parameters modeling stake accumulation dynamics.
Each consensus
participant is assigned a reinvestment factor γi = {0, 1} with equal probability,
reflecting the polarized behavior observed in practice where validators either ex-
tract all profits (γi = 0) or participate in liquid staking protocols with automatic
compounding (γi = 1) [3]. Initial stake distributions are varied across participants
to simulate realistic heterogeneity, with total network stake normalized to repre-
sent approximately 1,000 active validators. The discrete staking threshold is set to
32×109 gwei (equivalent to 32 ETH), reflecting Ethereum’s validator requirements.
Participants accumulate capital continuously through ki(ℓ+ 1) = ki(ℓ) + γi · Ri(ℓ),
but active stake updates only when crossing threshold boundaries: si(ℓ+ 1) =
(32 × 109) · ⌊ki(ℓ+ 1)/(32 × 109)⌋. For these long-term equilibrium studies, simu-
lations are extended to 10,000 blocks to observe convergence behavior.

5.2. Simulation Process
In the simulation, we model both PoS with ePBS and without ePBS to compare
their performance under similar parameters.
In the ePBS system, builders select transactions for inclusion in their blocks
based on their strategy, whether benign or attack. After selecting transactions,
builders calculate and submit bids to propose their blocks according to their strat-
egy. In our simulation, we use two strategies: reactive and lastminute, which
are among the most used in current relay-based PBS deployments [37, 44].
A
reactive builder at round t bids bi,t = min{vi,t, maxj∈{Bi} bj,k + δ | k ≤t} ,
matching the highest observed bid in history with a small increment δ > 0, con-
strained by its private valuation vi,t. A last-minute builder, by contrast, waits until
round t ≥c where c is a threshold round near the end of the auction, and then
bids bi,t = min{vi,t, maxj∈{Bi} bj,k + δ | k ≤t} · 1{t≥c} , using the same competi-
tive bidding strategy as reactive builders but only participating from their chosen
threshold round c onward. This strategy allows the builder to delay participation
and reduce reaction times for other builders; however, they risk not having sub-
mitted a bid at the end of the auction or that their bid has not propagated to the
proposer.
The highest bid at the end of the auction is selected, and the corresponding
block is included in the blockchain.

7. Conclusion
Our work shows that ePBS amplifies centralization rather than mitigating it.
Builder profits become highly concentrated (Gini coefficient rising from 0.1749 un-
der PoS to 0.8358 under ePBS), and 95.4% of block value accrues to proposers
despite their limited role in block assembly.
This concentration arises because
MEV-seeking builders consistently outbid the benign ones, and the auction mech-
anism incentivizes aggressive MEV extraction.
From the builder’s perspective, both profit and content building centralize
sharply, with a few efficient builders dominating block construction. Proposers,
by contrast, experience more even profit distribution due to randomized slot as-
signment, leading to partial decentralization at their level. Together, ePBS redis-
tributes rewards but fails to decentralize block content. On transaction ordering,
ePBS increases the frequency of reordering compared to PoS, as builders exploit
MEV opportunities to raise bids. This dynamic strengthens the position of MEV-
seeking builders, undermining fairness and further concentrating power.
Overall, while ePBS was designed to improve decentralization, our results show
that it intensifies builder dominance and transaction manipulation. Future research
should investigate alternative mechanisms and designs that limit builder concen-
tration without restoring proposer centralization.
