Abstract
Vision-Language
Models
(VLMs)
have
achieved strong performance on standard
vision–language benchmarks, yet often rely on
surface-level recognition rather than deeper
reasoning.
We propose visual word puzzles
as a challenging alternative, as they require
discovering implicit visual cues, generating
and revising hypotheses, and mapping percep-
tual evidence to non-literal concepts in ways
that are difficult to solve via literal grounding,
OCR-heavy shortcuts, or simple retrieval-style
matching.
We introduce EYE-Q, a multilin-
gual benchmark designed to assess this form
of complex visual understanding.
EYE-Q
contains 1,343 puzzles in which a model
observes a conceptually dense scene with a
brief description and must infer a specific
target word or phrase. The puzzles are inten-
tionally unstructured and cue-implicit, with
distractors and contextual relationships that
demand selective attention, abstraction, and
associative inference. The benchmark spans
English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual
puzzles.
We evaluate state-of-the-art VLMs
using an open-ended, human-aligned protocol
that probes hypothesis formation and revision
under lightweight assistance.
Results reveal
substantial performance gaps, especially on ab-
stract and cross-lingual puzzles, highlighting
limitations in current models’ ability to con-
struct and search over appropriate conceptual
representations for flexible image-to-phrase
inference; maximum accuracy reaches only
60.27%.

Related Work
VLM Benchmarks Beyond Literal Grounding.
Vision–language evaluation has long focused on
VQA and captioning tasks with explicit, directly
grounded evidence (Goyal et al., 2017; Hudson and
Manning, 2019; Chen et al., 2015). Newer bench-
marks target compositional or multi-step reasoning
and more abstract visual understanding (Kil et al.,
2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Chia et al., 2024). Yet
multiple analyses show that high performance can
reflect language priors or dataset shortcuts rather
than robust grounding (Yang et al., 2025; Krojer
et al., 2025; Chi et al., 2025), motivating evalua-
tions that require implicit cue discovery and non-
literal inference.
Visual Wordplay and Rebus-Style Puzzles.
Puzzle-style benchmarks probe non-literal image-
to-phrase mappings. Rebus and visual wordplay
datasets encode short phrases via symbolic substi-
tution, layout, and phonetic or semantic compo-
sition (Gritsevskiy et al., 2024; Kraaijveld et al.,
2025; Khurdula et al., 2025; Das et al., 2025).
Relevant work studies visual riddles and puns, in-
cluding culturally grounded variants (Guetta et al.,
2024; Chung et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). How-
ever, many settings use clean, token-like cues (tex-
t/icons) and analyses report weak hint use and lim-
ited hypothesis revision (Lee et al., 2025; Movva,
2025).
Hinted and Iterative Evaluation Protocols.
A
growing literature evaluates reasoning under iter-
ative protocols where models revise answers us-
ing feedback or critique. Representative methods
include self-refinement and reflection-style loops
(Shinn et al., 2023; Madaan et al., 2023) and
critique-based verification with tools (Gou et al.,
2023).
Benchmarks also measure critique-and-
correct behavior directly and in multi-turn settings
(Wu et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024; Banatt et al.,
2024), while other studies emphasize that self-
correction is unreliable without strong verifiers and
careful reporting (Huang et al., 2023; Kamoi et al.,
2024). Our evaluation uses lightweight assistance
to separate cue-discovery failures from output brit-
tleness.
Multi/Cross-Lingual
VLM
Evaluation.
Al-
though most benchmarks are English-centric,
recent work expands evaluation across languages
and cultures through multilingual suites, cross-
lingual VQA, and culturally grounded reasoning
datasets (Liu et al., 2021; Pfeiffer et al., 2022;
Bugliarello et al., 2022; Romero et al., 2024).
Complementary efforts broaden coverage for
underrepresented regions and non-Latin scripts,
including Arabic-focused resources (Barua et al.,
2024; Alwajih et al., 2024, 2025). Still, multilin-
gual multimodal puzzle solving remains limited,
particularly for culturally grounded wordplay and
cross-lingual transformations into Persian.

EYE-Q
This section introduces EYE-Q and the design prin-
ciples behind its puzzles. We first summarize the
benchmark composition and instance format, then
describe puzzle design and the data sources used
for the monolingual and cross-lingual subsets.
3.1
Benchmark Overview
EYE-Q is a multimodal benchmark designed to
evaluate open-ended image-to-phrase reasoning
under cue-implicit visual settings and multilingual
wordplay. The benchmark contains 1,343 picture
puzzles organized into four subsets: English, Per-
sian, Arabic, and cross-lingual (Table 1).
Each puzzle consists of an image and a short
textual prompt that specifies the game rule (i.e.,
the image encodes a hidden target word or short
phrase) and requests a single final answer. The
task is open-ended: models must generate the tar-
get word/phrase rather than select from candidates.
The images are intentionally cue-implicit and
conceptually dense: scenes contain multiple ob-
jects, attributes, and relationships that may func-
tion as semantic cues, often alongside distractors.
Cues can involve object orientation, item counts,
color, relative size, spatial relations (e.g., contain-
ment, overlap, inversion), material properties, pos-
ture, and affect. Text may appear in some images,
but EYE-Q is designed to be anti-OCR: solving
rarely reduces to simply reading visible text.
3.2
Puzzle Design
The core of EYE-Q lies in requiring a semantic leap
from literal recognition to a non-literal linguistic
target. Unlike standard VQA tasks that ask “What
is in the image?”, our puzzles ask “What does this
scene imply or represent?”. Solving typically re-
quires: (i) cue discovery (identifying which ele-
ments are informative versus distractors), (ii) re-
lational abstraction (reasoning over relations and
transformations rather than isolated objects), and
(iii) linguistic association (mapping the inferred
concept to an idiom, pun, phonetic resemblance,
or conventional phrase in the target language).
Puzzles range from monolingual wordplay to
cross-lingual reasoning. In cross-lingual instances,
cues naturally suggest words or concepts in one lan-
guage, while the correct answer is expressed in the
other, requiring phonetic, orthographic, or seman-
tic bridging between English and Persian. Figure 1
provides representative examples.
3.3
Data Collection and Ground Truth
EYE-Q is built from two sources: (i) the mobile
game Aftabe (Appendix A), and (ii) English and
Arabic puzzles designed by the authors and ren-
dered into images using text-to-image generation
models. We have obtained permission from Aftabe
to use and redistribute the curated puzzles as part
of EYE-Q.
Persian and Cross-Lingual Puzzles.
We cu-
rated Persian and cross-lingual puzzles from
Aftabe, which contains visual puzzles with Persian
answers. For each selected instance, we preserve
the original image and the game-provided intended
solution as ground truth. Because puzzles can ad-
mit multiple interpretations, we manually filter out
items that are ambiguous or under-specified, re-
taining puzzles with a clear intended reading and
a unique target answer. We then categorize each
retained puzzle as Persian or cross-lingual based
on whether solving requires an English–Persian
bridge.
English and Arabic Puzzles.
We build the En-
glish and Arabic subsets by first selecting a target
word or short phrase and writing a concise scene
description intended to lead a human solver to that
target.
We then render the scene using text-to-
image models (GPT (OpenAI, 2025) and Nano Ba-
nana (Raisinghani, 2025)). All generated images
are manually reviewed, and we retain only those
that clearly support the intended solution and do
not contain rendering artifacts or alternative plau-
sible interpretations.
Because the target answer
is fixed during design and each image is filtered
for clarity, these instances have controlled ground-
truth labels that are verified to be consistent with

Evaluation Hints.
Lightweight assistance sig-

Experimental Variations.
Using the shared
base template, we evaluate four prompting vari-
ants:
1. Basic. We append an orthographic hint indi-
cating the target answer length in characters
to narrow the search space, and ask the model
to output a single final answer.
2. Few-Shot Chain-of-Thought. We prepend
three solved examples to the prompt. Each
demonstration includes an image, the correct
answer, and a concise description of the in-
tended image–answer relation.
Demonstra-
tions are sampled from the same subset pool
as the test puzzle, English, Persian, Arabic,
or cross-lingual, and exclude the test instance.
For fair comparison, the selected demonstra-
tion set is fixed across models for each evalu-
ated sample.
3. Iterative Refinement.
We first query the
model using the basic prompt. If the answer
is incorrect, we append the model’s previous
guess and a short instruction to revise, then re-
query. We allow up to two such revisions, for
a total of three attempts. A puzzle is counted
as correct if any attempt matches the ground
truth.
4. Partial Character Reveal. We reveal a ran-
domly selected 25% subset of the ground-
truth answer’s non-space characters and mask
the remainder, for example with underscores,
then ask the model to output the full answer.
The revealed positions are sampled with a
fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility.
Across all variants, the input image is unchanged
and the core task remains open-ended image-to-
phrase inference; only the prompting strategy and
lightweight hinting described above are varied.

5.1
Overall Performance
Across all models and prompt variants, accuracy
remains far from high, indicating that EYE-Q poses
a challenging open-ended image-to-phrase infer-
ence problem.
Even the best-performing mod-
els struggle substantially on the harder subsets.
Considering the best result achieved on each sub-
set across all evaluated models and prompt vari-
ants, the peak accuracy is 60.27% on English
(Grok 4.1 Fast, Partial Character Reveal), 43.03%
on Persian (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Partial Character Re-
veal), 19.15% on Arabic (Gemini 2.5 Pro, Few-
Shot CoT), and 29.15% on cross-lingual puzzles
(Gemini 2.5 Pro, Partial Character Reveal). Thus,
while English can reach moderate performance
under supportive prompting, Arabic and cross-
lingual puzzle solving remain particularly difficult
even for the strongest models.
Performance differences across model families
are also clear. Proprietary models (GPT-5.2, Gem-

5.2
Assistance Effects
Prompt variants provide lightweight,
human-
aligned assistance, but they do not close the gap.
Averaged across models and language subsets, iter-
ative refinement improves accuracy over the basic
setup from 11.53% to 15.63%, suggesting that al-
lowing hypothesis revision can recover from some
initial misreadings.
Partial character reveal im-
proves further to 18.11% on average.
These assistance variants also directly reduce a
common concern in open-ended evaluation: that
low exact-match accuracy might be dominated by
output-space brittleness (large lexical search space
or superficial near-misses).
Partial character re-
veal constrains the output space without supply-
ing answer candidates, and refinement gives mod-
els multiple chances to correct small mistakes
without turning the task into candidate verifica-
tion. Despite this, accuracy remains low even un-
der supportive settings: for example, Arabic stays
below 10% on average even with partial charac-
ter reveal, and cross-lingual performance remains
around 12% on average. This persistence suggests
that the dominant failure mode is upstream of sur-
face form: models often fail to identify which ele-
ments in a complex, distractor-rich scene constitute
the right cues and to construct the correct abstract
interpretation linking the scene to the intended hid-
den phrase. In this sense, EYE-Q stresses a differ-
ent bottleneck than OCR-heavy or visually clean
rebus-style settings, where reading explicit tokens
and combining them compositionally can account
for much of the solution.

5.5
Semantic Near-Miss Analysis
Exact-match evaluation can in principle underes-
timate performance if models frequently produce
answers that are semantically close to the ground
truth but differ in surface form. To test this, we
measure semantic proximity between model out-
puts and ground-truth answers using cosine sim-
ilarity in an embedding space.
Specifically, we
compute cosine similarity between embeddings of
the model’s final answer under iterative refinement
and the ground-truth answer. We compute this sim-
ilarity over failure cases only and visualize density
estimates across language subsets in Figure 6. Ad-
ditional plots for other prompting variants show a
similar trend and are provided in Appendix B.5.
The density estimates are concentrated at low
similarity values across all four language settings,
indicating that incorrect outputs are typically not
semantically close to the target phrase. This sug-
gests that low accuracy on EYE-Q is not primarily
driven by minor linguistic differences or near-miss
paraphrases penalized by strict string matching. In-
stead, the dominant failure mode appears to arise
earlier: models often fail to identify the right cues
amid distractors and to form the correct abstract
interpretation linking the complex scene to the in-
tended hidden phrase.
6
Conclusion
We introduced EYE-Q, a multilingual benchmark
for visual word puzzle solving and open-ended
image-to-phrase inference. EYE-Q contains 1,343
cue-implicit puzzles spanning English, Persian,
Arabic, and cross-lingual Persian–English settings.
Unlike prior puzzle-style evaluations that often
center on visually clean rebus compositions or ex-
plicit text cues, EYE-Q emphasizes visually dense
scenes with distractors, where success requires dis-
covering which elements function as clues and
composing a non-literal conceptual interpretation.
Across six state-of-the-art LVLMs, performance
remains low. Even with human-aligned assistance
such as few-shot CoT, iterative refinement, and par-
tial character reveal, models continue to struggle,
particularly on Arabic and cross-lingual subsets. A
semantic similarity analysis of failures further sug-
gests that errors are typically not near-miss para-
phrases, reinforcing that the bottleneck is not only
output-space brittleness but also cue selection and
abstraction. We hope EYE-Q will provide a useful
testbed for developing and measuring multimodal
systems that better support hypothesis generation,
revision, and multilingual reasoning beyond literal
grounding.
Limitations
First, on rare occasions where alternative inter-
pretations exist, EYE-Q prioritizes the intended
ground truth to maintain rigorous evaluation met-
rics.
Second, dataset generation relies on contribu-
tors who are fluent in the target languages. This
can make it harder to scale the benchmark to ad-
ditional languages, dialects, or low-resource vari-
eties while keeping difficulty and style consistent.
Third, reliable human evaluation is also non-
trivial.
To assess correctness fairly across lan-
guages, each language subset should ideally be
evaluated by native speakers or annotators with

Additional Results
This appendix reports additional results that com-
plement the main experiments and provide further

B.1
Overall accuracy
For completeness, we report the exact accuracies

B.2
Temperature Ablation
Table 3 shows that performance is strongly and con-
sistently sensitive to decoding temperature, with
clear differences across models. For the most capa-
ble model, Gemini 2.5 Pro, accuracy is relatively
stable at low temperature but declines steadily as
T increases. This monotonic degradation matches
the intuition that, in a constrained exact-match
evaluation, additional sampling noise mainly per-
turbs reasoning traces that would otherwise remain
correct. Grok 4.1 Fast follows the same overall
trend from T=0.01 to T=1.0, but drops sharply at
T=2.0, suggesting a distinct failure regime once
stochasticity becomes large.
By contrast, several weaker or more generation-
sensitive models exhibit an inverted-U pattern:
moderate randomness improves results, while
higher temperature is harmful. Gemini 2.5 Flash
and Qwen 3 VL reach their best performance at
T=1.0, and GPT-5.2 displays a milder version of
the same effect. Llama 4 Scout gains slightly at low
temperature but breaks down completely at very
high T=5.0. Overall, these findings suggest that
modest stochasticity can help less reliable mod-
els recover from early incorrect decisions, whereas
stronger reasoning-focused models are best evalu-
ated under near-deterministic greedy decoding.
B.3
Model Size Effect
Attempt Scaling with Model Size.
Beyond fi-
nal accuracy, we analyze how model size affects
the process of iterative reasoning by measuring the
number of refinement attempts used under a five-
attempt iterative refinement setting on the English
subset only. Figure 7 reports two complementary
statistics as a function of model size: (i) the aver-
age number of attempts conditioned on solved puz-
zles only, and (ii) the average number of attempts
over all puzzles, where unsolved instances are as-
signed the maximum of five attempts.

Language and subset rules
We use four rule modules, one for each subset:
English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-lingual. Each

D.2
Shared task and output instructions
All experimental settings include the same core in-
struction module. This block explains the game
setup, states what the model should infer from the
image and the accompanying text, and clarifies the
expected form of the response. Crucially, it also
enforces a strict output constraint to ensure consis-
tent evaluation across models and prompt variants.
D.3
Hint modules
To provide additional guidance without changing
the underlying task, we use three lightweight hint
modules that can be optionally appended to the
base prompt. These hints are designed to constrain
the search space or support multi-round solving:
• Answer-length hint:
provides the answer
length in characters, excluding spaces.
• Partial-reveal hint: provides a masked pattern
where a subset of characters is revealed and
all remaining positions are replaced by under-
scores.
• Iterative-refinement hint: provides feedback
that includes the model’s previous answer, en-
abling a revision step in subsequent rounds.
D.4
Few-shot CoT demonstrations
For the few-shot CoT condition, we prepend three
solved demonstrations from the same subset as the
target puzzle. We prepare one demonstration block
per subset (English, Persian, Arabic, and cross-
lingual). Each demonstration includes an image

You are an expert multi-modal puzzle solver. You solve picture word puzzles.
GAME DESCRIPTION:
- You will see exactly ONE image per puzzle.
- The image may depict objects, people, scenes, text, icons, or abstract compositions.
- The goal is to infer a SINGLE intended answer: one word or a short phrase.
- The image is a deliberately constructed clue for a linguistic target, NOT a request to describe the
scene.
- The intended answer may be:
- a literal word,
- an idiom or proverb,
- a pun or wordplay,
- a common expression,
- a culturally meaningful phrase,
- or a proper noun / named entity (person, place, title, brand, named item).
GENERAL SOLVING PROCEDURE (follow in order):
1. Identify candidate clue units in the image:
- the most salient objects/entities
- any text, letters, numbers, symbols, or icons
- any repeated motif/pattern
2. Select ONLY 2–4 PRIMARY clue units:
- prefer central/emphasized/repeated units
- compress repeated motifs into one unit
- ignore minor background details unless they clearly change a primary unit
3. Hypothesize a simple composition:
- the answer is usually formed by combining or transforming the primary units
- prefer the simplest coherent interpretation with the fewest assumptions
4. Choose the best final answer:
- it should be natural/common in the target language
- it should explain the primary units as a single intended construction
- prioritize global coherence over matching every local detail
OUTPUT REQUIREMENT:
- Provide exactly ONE final answer (single word or short phrase).
- If uncertain, choose the most plausible candidate under the simplest coherent interpretation.
