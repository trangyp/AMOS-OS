Deletion-correcting codes for an adversarial
nanopore channel
Huiling Xie
Zitan Chen
Abstract
We study deletion-correcting codes for an adversarial nanopore channel in which at most t deletions may occur. We propose
an explicit construction of q-ary codes of length n for this channel with 2t logq n + Θ(log log n) redundant symbols. We also
show that the optimal redundancy is between t logq n + Ω(1) and 2t logq n −logq log2 n + O(1), so our explicit construction
matches the existential upper bound to first order. In contrast, for the classical adversarial q-ary deletion channel, the smallest
redundancy achieved by known explicit constructions that correct up to t deletions is 4t(1 + ϵ) logq n + o(log n).
I. INTRODUCTION
Nanopore sequencing has attracted considerable attention for DNA-based data storage due to its advantages over other
sequencing technologies. However, it is prone to several types of errors, including deletion, duplication and substitution errors.
In essence, nanopore sequencing can be modeled as passing a string through an inter-symbol interference (ISI) channel, followed
by additional noisy channels that further corrupt the ISI channel output. Specifically, the ISI channel scans the string from
left to right using a sliding window of size ℓ, shifted by one position each step, thereby producing a sequence of ℓ-mers. The
subsequent noisy channels then introduce errors into this ℓ-mer sequence. The goal of nanopore sequencing is to recover the
original string from the final noisy output.
A large body of work has studied nanopore sequencing in recent years. Depending on whether the underlying channel
model is probabilistic, the literature can be grouped into two broad classes. Under a probabilistic channel model, Magner et
al. [1] gave an information-theoretic analysis of the impact of sticky insertion-deletion errors in nanopore sequencers, without
explicitly accounting for ISI. Later, Mao et al. [2] developed a signal-level model for nanopore sequencing that captures ISI and
analyzed achievable information rates. In [3]–[5], the authors studied a nanopore channel model based on a Markov chain with
memory of order ℓ. In subsequent work, McBain et al. [6], [7] introduced a channel model in the language of semi-Markov
processes and estimated its achievable information rates using efficient algorithms. The achievable information rates of this
model were further examined in [8], [9]. A number of other works have also considered the design of coding schemes for
nanopore sequencing under various probabilistic models. We refer the interested reader to the recent works [10]–[14] and the
references therein.
Another line of research assumes deterministic channel models and investigates the combinatorial and algebraic coding
problems arising in nanopore sequencing. Specifically, the authors of [15] introduced a deterministic model capturing only ISI
and substitution errors, and proposed coding schemes for error correction. Extending the work of [15], the authors of [16]
studied coding schemes that can deal with duplication and deletion errors. In addition, a number of recent papers model the
noisy output of deterministic nanopore channels as a sequence of erroneous ℓ-mer compositions. Under this framework, the
work [17] considered ISI effect with substitution errors and gave a coding scheme that can correct a single substitution with
near-optimal redundancy. Extensions to multiple substitution errors were further explored in [18], [19]. Assuming instead that
only deletion errors are present in addition to ISI, the work [20] proposed a single-deletion-correcting code with near-optimal
redundancy. Meanwhile, the paper [21] studied ISI with tandem-duplication errors in the ℓ-mer composition sequence and
analyzed the optimal code rate. We also note that the recent works [22], [23] also studied various coding-theoretic questions
under the assumption that the noisy channel output is in the form of ℓ-mer compositions. Lastly, motivated by the fact that
basecallers are often used to infer a sequence of ℓ-mers from the output of practical nanopore sequencers, the work of [24]
modeled the noisy channel output as a sequence of erroneous ℓ-mers and constructed codes that can correct up to two ℓ-mer
deletions.
In this work, under the same assumption as in [24], we study bounds and constructions for codes that can correct a constant
number of deletions. We note that modeling the noisy channel output as a sequence of ℓ-mers yields a model that resembles
the ℓ-symbol read channel [25]–[27]. However, the reads in the ℓ-symbol read channel are taken cyclically. That is, after

We note that the nanopore channel considered in this work is also closely related to the classical adversarial deletion channel
[29]. For binary codes, Levenshtein [29] showed that the optimal redundancy for t-deletion-correcting binary codes of length
n lies between t log2 n + o(log n) and 2t log2 n + o(log n), where t is a constant independent of n. For a single deletion,

Guruswami and Håstad [31] constructed codes with redundancy 4 log2 n + O(log log n), matching the existential upper bound.
More generally, explicit constructions of t-deletion-correcting binary codes with order-optimal redundancy are presented in
[32], [33]. In particular, the construction of Sima and Bruck [32] achieves redundancy 8t log2 n + o(log n). For q-ary codes,
Levenshtein [34] proved analogous redundancy bounds t logq n+o(log n) and 2t logq n+o(log n) (measured in q-ary symbols).
A construction with nearly optimal redundancy for a single deletion was given by Tenengolts [35]. In [36], Sima et al. proposed
t-deletion-correcting q-ary codes that require at most 4t(1 + ϵ) logq n + o(log n) redundant symbols. On the existential side,
Alon et al. [37] obtained the first asymptotic improvement to Levenshtein’s lower bound on the optimal code size, while
Levenshtein’s upper bound has been improved in various parameter regimes in a series of works [38]–[43].
Our contributions: We give an explicit construction of q-ary codes of length n with 2t logq n + Θ(log log n) redundant
symbols that can correct up to t deletions of ℓ-mers, where t ≤min{ℓ−2, (ℓ+ 1)/2} is a constant independent of n. Our
construction reduces deletion correction to an almost Hamming-type error-correction problem, and uses generalized Reed-
Solomon (GRS) codes as a key building block. In addition, we observe that our nanopore channel model can be viewed as a
weaker version of the classical adversarial deletion channel. Consequently, the lower bound of Alon et al. applies directly and
yields an existential upper bound 2t logq n −logq log2 n + O(1) on the optimal redundancy. This further suggests our explicit
construction matches this existential upper bound to first order. Moreover, drawing on ideas of Levenshtein [29], we use the
probabilistic method to establish an upper bound on the optimal code size, implying a lower bound of t logq n + Ω(1) on the
optimal redundancy.
Paper outline: The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section II formally introduces our channel model and present
a few technical lemmas used throughout the paper. Section III is devoted to the explicit construction of t-deletion-correcting
codes for our channel model. Section IV presents existential bounds on the optimal code size. Finally, Section V discusses
open problems and future directions.

Definition 1 (Periodic patterns). A string s = (s1, . . . , sN) ∈ΣN

cannot be extended to the left or right within s to form a longer substring that has the same period T.
The following theorem of Fine and Wilf [44] is helpful for our subsequent discussion on periodic patterns.
Theorem 3 (Fine-Wilf theorem [44, Theorem 1]). Let (fi)i≥0, (gi)i≥0 be two strings over Σq with period T1, T2, respectively. If
fi = gi for T1 +T2 −gcd(T1, T2) consecutive integer i, then fi = gi for all i. The result would be false if T1 +T2 −gcd(T1, T2)
were replaced by anything smaller.
Remark II.1. One direct implication of Theorem 3 is that, within a string, two adjacent maximal periodic patterns with period
T1, T2 can overlap in at most T1 + T2 −gcd(T1, T2) −1 positions. Note that if the length of one maximal periodic pattern is
at most T1 + T2 −gcd(T1, T2) −1, it is possible that one pattern is contained in another.
Remark II.2. If a periodic pattern with period T has length at least 2T, then its least period must divide T. This is because if
T ′ ≤T is the least period, then 2T ≥T + T ′ −gcd(T, T ′). Therefore, by Theorem 3, the pattern also has period gcd(T, T ′),
which divides T ′. But T ′ is the least period so gcd(T, T ′) = T ′ and T ′|T.

III. EXPLICIT CONSTRUCTION OF CODES
In this section we assume further that t ≤(ℓ+ 1)/2 and present an explicit construction of codes C ⊂Σn
q that can correct
up to t ≤min{ℓ−2, (ℓ+ 1)/2} deletions of ℓ-mers for the nanopore channel. Specifically, let s0, sn+ℓ∈Σℓ
q be two fixed
adapter sequences. For any input string ˜x = (s0, x, sn+ℓ) with x ∈C, we show that one can recover x from the output ℓ-mers
zn+ℓ−τ
0
of the nanopore channel, provided that at most t ≤min{ℓ−2, (ℓ+1)/2} deletions occur in the output. Before delving
into the technical details, below we outline the high-level idea of our construction.
As discussed earlier, the output ℓ-mers are assumed to be consistent, so deletions only occur within consistency-preserving
patterns. Note that if at least one complete least period in a consistency-preserving pattern survives the deletions in the output
string, and the length of the pattern in the input string is known a priori, then the full pattern can be recovered by extending
it using its periodicity. As mentioned in Remark II.3, for a consistency-preserving pattern of length b + ℓ−1 with least period
T, it can undergo deletion of at most ⌊b/T⌋complete least periods, leaving a substring of length ℓ+ b −1 −⌊b/T⌋T ≥ℓ−1.
Thus, at least ℓ−1 symbols of the consistency-preserving pattern survive the deletions. This also implies that at least one
complete least period of the pattern necessarily remains, since T ≤t ≤ℓ−2. Therefore, we need to keep track of the maximal
periodic patterns of length at least ℓ−1 in both the input string ˜x and the output string ˜y, which we call check patterns.
Definition 3 (Check patterns). A substring of s = (s1, . . . , sN) ∈ΣN
q is a check pattern if it is a maximal periodic pattern
with period at most t and length at least ℓ−1.
Now if the set of check patterns in the input string ˜x is the same as in the output string ˜y, up to a reduction in the length
of each pattern, then one can establish a one-to-one correspondence between check patterns in ˜x and ˜y that matches each
check pattern in ˜x with the same pattern (possibly with reduced length) in ˜y. Thus, if the lengths of the check patterns in
˜x are known, one can restore the check patterns in ˜y to their original lengths using their periodicity. We now show that the
check patterns present in the input string are preserved in the output string, possibly with reduced length. This follows from
the technical lemma below.
Lemma 5. Assume t ≤min{ℓ−2, (ℓ+ 1)/2}. Any two adjacent check patterns in a string can overlap in at most ℓ−2
positions.
Proof. Since t ≤(ℓ+ 1)/2, by Theorem 3 two adjacent check patterns with different period T1, T2 can overlap in at most
T1 + T2 −gcd(T1, T2) −1 ≤2t −3 ≤ℓ−2 positions. Moreover, if T1 = T2, then by Theorem 3 they can overlap in at most
T1 −1 ≤t−1 < ℓ−2 positions. Hence, any two adjacent check patterns in a string can overlap in at most ℓ−2 positions.
Lemma 6. Assume t ≤min{ℓ−2, (ℓ+ 1)/2}. The number of check patterns in ˜x is the same as in ˜y. In particular, every
check pattern in ˜y arises from exactly one check pattern in ˜x, possibly with reduced length. Moreover, the check patterns in ˜x
start at distinct positions, and the same holds for ˜y.
Proof. As mentioned above, deletion can only remove complete least periods of a consistency-preserving pattern in ˜x, leaving
a periodic pattern of length at least ℓ−1. Therefore, each check pattern in ˜y must come from a check pattern in ˜x, and the
number of check pattern in ˜y is no larger than that in ˜x.
It remains to show that no two check patterns in ˜x can be combined into a single check pattern in ˜y through deletion. Note
that by Lemma 5, any two adjacent check patterns in a string can overlap in at most ℓ−2 positions. Since a check pattern
has length at least ℓ−1, this implies they cannot start (or end) at the same position, i.e., one pattern cannot be contained in

By Lemma 6, we can identify each check pattern in ˜x with a unique check pattern in ˜y, sequentially from left to right. If

Lemma 7. Assume t ≤min{ℓ−2, (ℓ+1)/2}. At most τ ≤t check patterns in ˜y can have length smaller than their corresponding
check patterns in ˜x.
Proof. By Lemma 6, there is a one-to-one correspondence between check patterns in the input string ˜x and the output string
˜y that matches each check pattern in ˜x with the same pattern (possibly with reduced length) in ˜y. Since there are τ deletions
in the output ℓ-mers, there are τ deleted symbols in the output string ˜y. Bursts are by definition disjoint, so there can be at
most τ deletion bursts in ˜y, with equality if and only if each burst has length one. We next show that a single deletion burst
can reduce the length of at most one check pattern in the input string ˜x.
Consider a deletion burst of length b ≤τ that deletes the symbols (xi+1, . . . , xi+b). By Lemma 4, the output ℓ-mers remain
consistent if and only if (xi−ℓ+2, . . . , xi+b) is a periodic pattern with least period T satisfying T | b. Let p1 be the maximal
periodic pattern in ˜x with least period T that contains (xi−ℓ+2, . . . , xi+b). It follows that p1 is a check pattern.
Suppose the same burst also shortens the length of another check pattern p2. Then the burst must overlap p2, and thus p1
overlaps p2. By Lemma 5, the size of the overlap between p1 and p2 is at most ℓ−2. Since check patterns have length at least
ℓ−1, it follows that neither p1 nor p2 is contained in the other, and thus the overlap between p1 and p2 must be a suffix of one
and a prefix of the other. Since the length of p1 is at least b + ℓ−1, it follows that p1 contains at least b + ℓ−1 −(ℓ−2) > b
consecutive symbols that are disjoint from p2.
Since p1 has period T and T | b, deleting any b consecutive symbols in p1 produces the same resulting string as deleting
(xi+1, . . . , xi+b). Therefore, one may assume without loss of generality that the burst deletes any b consecutive symbols of p1.
In particular, one may replace the original burst by an equivalent burst of length b that is disjoint from p2, without changing
the output string. Under this equivalent burst, p2 is unaffected and cannot be shortened by this burst, which is a contradiction.
Therefore, a single deletion burst can shorten at most one check pattern. Since there are at most τ bursts, at most τ check
patterns in ˜y can have length smaller than their corresponding check patterns in ˜x.
As a consequence of Lemma 7, if we record the lengths of the check patterns in ˜x sequentially in one tuple and those in
˜y in another, then the Hamming distance between these two tuples is at most t. Therefore, a Hamming-type error-correcting
code with minimum distance at least 2t + 1 may be used to protect these lengths. To proceed, we shift our attention from
˜x to ¯x := (s0, u), where u ∈Σk
q is an information string. Note that the discussion above for ˜x and ˜y applies equally to ¯x
and its corresponding output string ¯y induced by the nanopore channel. By Lemma 6, the check patterns all start at distinct

IV. EXISTENTIAL BOUNDS ON THE OPTIMAL CODE SIZE
In this section, we present bounds on the maximum size of t-deletion-correcting codes for the nanopore channel. We begin
with a simple observation: by Proposition 2, any t-deletion-correcting code for the q-ary deletion channel (i.e., a channel that
takes strings over Σq as input and deletes at most t symbols) can also be used for the nanopore channel. This is because the
adapter sequences are known and, as mentioned in Remark II.3, the nanopore channel, in effect, only deletes symbols within
consistency-preserving patterns of its input, while the q-ary deletion channel is not subject to this restriction. Thus, any lower
bound on the maximum size of codes for the q-ary deletion channel is also a lower bound for the nanopore channel. To the best
of our knowledge, the best such bound (for the parameter regime considered in this work) is due to Alon et al. [37], yielding
the following lower bound on the maximum size of codes for the nanopore channel.
Theorem 9 ([37, Theorem 1]). A t-deletion-correcting code C ⊂Σn
q for the nanopore channel has size |C| = Ω(qnn−2t log n).
Remark IV.1. By Theorem 9, there exist t-deletion-correcting codes of length n over Σq for the nanopore channel with
redundancy at most 2t logq n −logq log2 n + O(1). However, this result is nonconstructive. In contrast, the explicit codes
presented in Section III have redundancy 2t logq n + Θ(log log n), which matches the bound in Theorem 9 to first order.
Next, we derive an upper bound on the size of t-deletion-correcting codes for the nanopore channel. For convenience, a
string with period one is said to be constant. Our approach is to consider a relaxed setting in which the nanopore channel

size of a t-deletion-correcting code in this relaxed setting then serves as an upper bound on the maximum code size in the
original setting. Intuitively, a code for this relaxed setting cannot contain many codewords that have a large number of maximal
constant substrings. Motivated by this observation, we adapt the ideas of Levenshtein [29] and use the probabilistic method.
Specifically, we view each symbol of the string to be sequenced as i.i.d. and uniformly distributed over Σq, and estimate the
number of strings with a small number of maximal constant substrings from this perspective. Furthermore, since the symbols
within a maximal constant substring are not independent, we employ Janson’s concentration inequality [45] for dependent
random variables to bound the number of such strings. Below we state Janson’s concentration inequality and then present our

V. CONCLUDING REMARKS
We note that the reduction from deletions to Hamming-type errors relies on the assumption that t ≤min{ℓ−2, (ℓ+1)/2}. It
is therefore natural to ask what can be done if t is larger. Moreover, since nanopore sequencing errors also include duplications
and substitutions, it would be interesting to further study codes for an adversarial nanopore channel that can introduce deletions,
duplications and substitutions.
