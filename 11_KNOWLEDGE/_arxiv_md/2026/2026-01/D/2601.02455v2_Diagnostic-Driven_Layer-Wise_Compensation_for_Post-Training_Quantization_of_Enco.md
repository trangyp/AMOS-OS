A B S T R A C T
Deploying Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models on memory-constrained edge devices
requires aggressive low-bit weight quantization. Layer-wise post-training quantization is practical and
effective, but it suffers from cross-layer error accumulation. Existing compensation methods typically
use a single global strength for all layers, which is ill-suited to encoder-decoder ASR models whose
acoustic encoder and linguistic decoder exhibit markedly different sensitivities to quantization noise.
We propose FADE, a diagnostic-driven framework that assigns each layer an adaptive compensation
coefficient by combining two complementary signals: an intrinsic vulnerability score from weight
geometry and a calibration reliability score from the data-driven solution. The resulting layer-wise
coefficient balances local quantization fidelity against cross-layer error correction, enabling tailored
compensation without retraining or hyperparameter search. Experiments on Whisper, Moonshine, and
Qwen3-ASR across four benchmarks show that FADE consistently improves mean Word Error Rate
over strong baselines at both 3- and 4-bit precision while substantially reducing run-to-run variance.
1. Introduction
Encoder–decoder Transformer architectures now under-
pin many of the strongest automatic speech recognition
(ASR) systems. Recent models such as OpenAI’s Whis-
per [1] and the Moonshine family [2] achieve strong perfor-
mance across languages and acoustic conditions, bringing
robust speech recognition closer to practical use. Yet this
progress has not translated cleanly to edge deployment. On
mobile phones, wearables, and other resource-constrained
devices, modern ASR models must operate under tight bud-
gets on memory, compute, and energy. Their large parameter
footprints strain storage and memory bandwidth [3], and the
cost of moving those parameters through the system can it-
self become a major bottleneck for real-time inference [4, 5].
A natural response is to compress the model. Prior
work explores this from several directions. Some methods
remove redundant parameters through pruning without back-
propagation [6, 7], while others pursue ultra-low-bit com-
pression via clustering or mixed-precision allocation [8].
These approaches can be effective, but they often rely on
structural changes or hardware support for non-uniform pre-
cision, which makes them less attractive for broad deploy-
ment on commodity edge devices.

The difficulty stems from the heterogeneity of ASR mod-
els themselves. Although encoder–decoder ASR systems
share a Transformer backbone, not all layers respond to
quantization in the same way. Layers differ in weight ge-
ometry, activation statistics, and tolerance to low-bit noise.
The mismatch is especially pronounced across modules: the
acoustic encoder processes continuous speech representa-
tions, whereas the decoder operates over text-like represen-
tations. These differences make calibration-based correction
unevenly reliable across the network. A single global com-
pensation strength is therefore too coarse: it can over-correct
sensitive layers and overfit the calibration data, while under-
correcting layers that require stronger error compensation.
What is needed instead is a layer-wise mechanism that adapts
correction strength to each layer’s quantization sensitivity.
Our goal is to make error-compensated PTQ better
matched to the structure of encoder–decoder ASR mod-
els. To this end, we propose FADE, a diagnostic-driven
framework that assigns each layer its own compensation
coefficient. FADE combines two complementary signals:
an intrinsic weight vulnerability score derived from weight
geometry, and a calibration reliability score derived from
the data-driven correction itself. Together, these signals esti-
mate both how vulnerable a layer is to low-bit approximation
and how trustworthy its calibration-based compensation is.
Their fusion yields a layer-wise gate that balances local
reconstruction fidelity against cross-layer error correction.
The resulting method requires no retraining, no additional
calibration data, and no hyperparameter search, and it inte-
grates directly into existing Hessian-based PTQ pipelines.
We evaluate FADE on representative encoder–decoder
ASR families, including Whisper, Moonshine, and Qwen3-
ASR, across multiple benchmarks and low-bit settings. The
results show that fine-grained, layer-wise compensation is
important for stable quantization in heterogeneous ASR
architectures: FADE consistently improves word error rate
over strong baselines while substantially reducing run-to-run
variance.

2.4. Quantization for Automatic Speech
Recognition
Post-training quantization (PTQ) has been widely stud-
ied for automatic speech recognition (ASR), but existing
methods exhibit several limitations in terms of architectural
assumptions and evaluation settings.
Early work such as Q-ASR [17] adopts a data-free
quantization strategy by leveraging BatchNorm statistics
to construct synthetic calibration data. While effective for
CNN-based ASR models, this approach inherently relies
on the presence of BatchNorm layers. However, modern
ASR systems such as Whisper and Qwen-ASR are based on
Transformer architectures that employ LayerNorm instead
of BatchNorm, making such methods inapplicable in con-
temporary settings.
More recent approaches attempt to improve PTQ for
speech models through architecture-aware designs. For in-
stance, StableQuant [18] introduces layer-wise stabilization
techniques tailored to the heterogeneous structure of speech
foundation models. While these methods improve robust-
ness, they rely on heuristics specific to speech architectures,
which may limit their generality across different model
families.
GenPTQ [19] further proposes a mixed-precision quan-
tization scheme based on gradient-based sensitivity analysis
to allocate bit-width across layers. Although it is not tied to
a specific architecture, it operates in a layer-wise manner
and treats each layer independently. As a result, it does
not explicitly model the accumulation of quantization error
across layers, which is particularly critical in deep encoder–
decoder ASR models.
In addition to architectural assumptions, a common lim-
itation of prior work is the lack of evaluation on mod-
ern state-of-the-art ASR systems. Many existing studies
focus on outdated architectures or relatively small-scale
models, which are not representative of current deployment
scenarios. Recent advances in ASR are driven by large-
scale pretrained models such as Whisper and Qwen-ASR,
which exhibit different activation characteristics and error
propagation behavior. Therefore, it remains unclear whether
conclusions drawn from earlier models generalize to these
settings.
In contrast, our method is designed to be model-agnostic
and focuses on explicitly modeling cross-layer error prop-
agation. By addressing the accumulation of quantization
error throughout the network, our approach provides a more
principled and scalable solution for quantizing modern ASR
systems.
3. Methodology
As discussed in Section 2.3, quantization errors in
encoder–decoder ASR models accumulate across successive
layers and blocks. Existing cross-layer compensation meth-
ods mitigate this effect with a single global coefficient 𝛼,
but Fig. 5 shows that no fixed global 𝛼performs uniformly
well across models and quantization settings. This issue is
particularly pronounced in encoder–decoder ASR models,
where layers process different representations and exhibit
distinct sensitivities to quantization noise.
To address this issue, we propose FADE (Fine-grained
Alpha for Dynamic Quantization Error Propagation), a
diagnostic-driven layer-wise compensation framework that
assigns each layer an adaptive correction coefficient 𝛼𝑙.
Specifically, FADE combines two complementary signals:
an intrinsic weight-vulnerability signal that measures sensi-
tivity to low-bit approximation, and a calibration-reliability
signal that measures the trustworthiness of the estimated
correction direction. Their fusion yields a layer-adaptive
coefficient that controls the strength of cross-layer error
compensation.

3.2. FADE: Diagnostic-Driven Layer-Wise
Compensation
The core task of FADE is to determine 𝛼𝑙for each layer.
Rather than treating all layers uniformly, FADE asks two
questions for every layer 𝑙:
• Is this layer intrinsically hard to quantize?
• Is the calibration-based correction direction trust-
worthy for this layer?
The first question captures whether low-bit quantization
would introduce substantial local distortion even before con-
sidering calibration data. The second captures whether the
data-driven correction obtained from a Hessian-based solver
is likely to be useful rather than noisy or overfit. Figure 2
illustrates this process: FADE first extracts these two signals,
then fuses them into a layer-wise gate that determines the
strength of cross-layer correction.

Figure 4 shows the sensitivity of performance to (𝛼min, 𝛼max)
under different bit-widths.
Stable optimal region. We observe that optimal perfor-
mance does not concentrate at a single point, but instead
forms a contiguous region in the parameter space. Config-
urations with small 𝛼min and large 𝛼max consistently yield
low WER, indicating that the method is robust to moderate
variations of the 𝛼range.
Asymmetric sensitivity. A clear asymmetry is observed
between 𝛼min and 𝛼max. Increasing 𝛼max generally improves
performance, while large 𝛼min leads to degradation. This
suggests that cross-layer compensation is beneficial, but
overly suppressing the local reconstruction objective harms
performance. This behavior aligns with the design of FADE,
which interpolates between local fidelity and cross-layer
compensation.
Low-bit sensitivity. Under 3-bit quantization, the perfor-
mance landscape becomes significantly sharper, with larger
fluctuations across configurations. This confirms that low-
bit quantization amplifies sensitivity to hyperparameters and
noise.
Stability-performance alignment. We further observe
that regions with low WER also exhibit low variance across
seeds. This indicates that good configurations are both accu-
rate and stable, rather than relying on favorable randomness.
Overall, these results suggest that the effectiveness of 𝛼
stems from its role as a balancing mechanism between local
fidelity and cross-layer compensation, rather than precise
tuning of a single value.
4.7. Memory Analysis
Table 7 compares both static model memory and peak
inference memory before and after quantization. Weight-
only quantization consistently reduces model storage by
approximately 71.9%, which is close to the theoretical 4×
compression from FP16 to 4-bit representation. This signif-
icantly improves deployability, especially for large models.
However, the reduction in peak inference memory is
much smaller and, in some cases, negligible. This is because
runtime memory is dominated not only by model weights but
also by activations, attention buffers, intermediate tensors,
and temporary workspace allocations.
As a result, weight-only quantization primarily addresses
the storage bottleneck, determining whether a model can fit
into device memory, while its impact on runtime memory is
limited by the underlying inference pipeline.
In summary, quantization improves model feasibility
(parameter memory footprint), but additional system-level
optimizations are required to substantially reduce runtime
memory consumption.
5. Conclusion
We studied low-bit post-training quantization for encoder–
decoder ASR models and identified layer-wise sensitiv-
ity heterogeneity as a central challenge. Individual lay-
ers differ markedly in weight geometry and tolerance to
quantization noise, making compensation schemes based
on a single global coefficient inherently mismatched to
the model. To address this issue, we proposed FADE, a
diagnostic-driven framework that determines the per-layer
compensation coefficient by fusing two complementary
signals: an intrinsic weight-vulnerability score derived from
weight geometry, and a calibration-reliability score derived
from the data-driven solution. The resulting layer-wise
adaptive gate requires no retraining, no additional calibra-
tion data, and no model-specific hyperparameter search.
Experiments on Whisper, Moonshine, and Qwen3-ASR
across four benchmarks show that FADE generally improves
low-bit quantization performance over strong baselines at
both 3- and 4-bit precision, with the largest gains under
aggressive 3-bit settings, while also reducing run-to-run
variance. These results highlight the importance of fine-
grained, layer-adaptive error control for robust quantization
of heterogeneous encoder–decoder ASR systems.
