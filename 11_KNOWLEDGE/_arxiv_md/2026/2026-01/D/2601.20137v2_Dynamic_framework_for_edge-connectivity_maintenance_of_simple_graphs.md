Abstract
We present a framework for dynamically maintaining k-edge-connectivity of an undi-
rected simple graph G under edge insertions and deletions, where k is a fixed constant.
After an edge insertion, the algorithm identifies and removes a distinct redundant edge
to maintain sparsity, in O(k log n) amortized time.
After an edge deletion that reduces
λ(G) below k, the algorithm restores k-edge-connectivity by adding at most two new edges
(excluding the deleted edge), in O(k3/2n3/2) time. The insertion procedure combines Nag-
amochi–Ibaraki sparse certificates with Link-Cut Trees; the deletion procedure uses a single
maximum-flow computation on the sparsified graph. Throughout all updates, the graph is
maintained with O(kn) edges.

Introduction
Edge-connectivity is one of the fundamental measures of network reliability and fault toler-
ance. A graph is k-edge-connected if the removal of fewer than k edges cannot disconnect
it. We study the problem of actively maintaining k-edge-connectivity: given a graph G with
λ(G) ≥k, the goal is to preserve this invariant under edge insertions and deletions by modi-
fying the graph itself – not merely reporting whether the invariant holds. An edge insertion
may make an existing edge redundant for k-edge-connectivity, while an edge deletion may
reduce λ(G) below k. We address both cases:
1. Redundancy Elimination (Post-Addition): after insertion of a new edge enew = {u, v},
local edge-connectivity between its endpoints may be greater than k. To maintain
structural sparsity – specifically ensuring |E| = O(kn) – we identify and remove an
existing edge eold = {x, y} (eold ̸= enew) for which the condition λ(x, y; (G ∪{enew}) \
{eold}) ≥k (see Section 3 for definition of λ(x, y; G)) is met. This ensures that the
graph remains sparse and k-edge-connected.
2. Connectivity Restoration (Post-Removal): after removal of an existing edge edel, the
global edge-connectivity may drop below the required threshold, i.e., λ(G\{edel}) < k.
In such cases, the framework computes and inserts a minimal set of augmenting edges
Eaug (edel /∈Eaug) such that the condition λ((G \ {edel}) ∪Eaug) ≥k holds.
Beyond its theoretical interest, our framework is motivated by settings where a network’s
link structure must be actively maintained under dynamic changes. A natural application
of Connectivity Restoration arises in survivable telecommunications network design, where
nodes represent routers and edges represent physical communication links. Such networks
are designed to be sparse and k-edge-connected so that data transmission survives up to
k −1 simultaneous link failures [8]. When a link is lost – due to hardware malfunction
or fiber cut – the operator must provision new links to restore the required redundancy.
Our framework models this directly: the failed link is an edge deletion, and the algorithm
computes the minimal set of new links to restore invariant λ(G) ≥k.

Related work
Computing the minimum cut in an undirected graph is a classic problem with a long history.
Early approaches relied on network flow techniques; the Gomory-Hu [6] tree construction
reduces the problem to n−1 maximum flow computations. Karger [10] introduced a random-
ized algorithm based on tree packings that finds the global minimum cut in O(m log3 n) time
with high probability. In the deterministic setting, Henzinger, Rao, and Wang [9] gave an
algorithm for determining graph’s edge-connectivity, running in O(m log2(n) log(log2(n)))
time.
Li [11] presented a deterministic algorithm for finding minimum-cuts in weighted
undirected graphs running in m1+o(1) time.
The problem of maintaining the minimum cut in a dynamically changing graph has been
studied extensively, with a primary focus on efficiently reporting the value of the minimum
cut rather than actively enforcing a connectivity invariant. Thorup [16] gave a deterministic
fully dynamic algorithm that maintains the value of the minimum cut in ˜O(√n) (see Sec-
tion 3) worst-case time per update. For the incremental setting, Goranci et al. [7] achieved
O(log3(n) log(log2(n))) amortized update time by maintaining a set of edge-disjoint span-
ning forests – a sparse certificate that preserves connectivity properties. Our approach to
redundancy elimination (Section 4) builds on a similar structural insight: by actively main-
taining a certificate of size O(kn), we can efficiently identify and remove redundant edges
in O(k log n) amortized time per edge addition. For connectivity restoration (Section 5),
we exploit the specific properties of Dinic’s algorithm [4] on unit-capacity networks. While
the general weighted case requires O(n2m) time, Even and Tarjan [5] showed that the com-
plexity improves to O(min(n2/3, m1/2)m) for unit capacities. This bound is critical to our
edge-connectivity restoration strategy.

Preliminaries and notation
We consider unweighted, simple, undirected graphs G = (V, E) with a vertex set V and
edge set E, where |V | = n and |E| = m, using these parameters primarily to describe the
computational complexity of the proposed algorithms. To simplify the asymptotic analysis,
we employ the notation ˜O(f(n)) to denote O(f(n) logk n) for some constant k ≥0, thereby
suppressing polylogarithmic factors; crucially, this notation never obscures polynomial fac-
tors. When considering several graphs, we denote the respective sets of a graph G as V (G)
and E(G) to avoid ambiguity. Furthermore, we denote an undirected edge connecting ver-
tices u and v as the set {u, v}, while a directed edge (arc) from u to v is denoted by the
ordered pair (u, v). For an edge e = {u, v}, we write endpoints(e) to denote the pair of
vertices incident to e. In pseudocode, the assignment (u, v) ←endpoints(e) binds the two
endpoints of e to the variables u and v in arbitrary order.
For any two vertices u, v ∈V , let λ(u, v; G) denote the local edge-connectivity, defined
as the maximum number of edge-disjoint paths between u and v in G. The global edge-
connectivity of G, denoted by λ(G), is the minimum local edge-connectivity over all pairs
of vertices, i.e. λ(G) = min
u,v∈V λ(u, v; G).
The parameter k represents the required global edge-connectivity and is treated as a
fixed integer constant throughout the paper.
For any subset of vertices S ⊆V and S ̸= ∅, let G[S] = (S, E[S]) denote the subgraph
induced by S, where E[S] = {{u, v} ∈E | u, v ∈S} represents the set of edges with
endpoints entirely contained within S. A cut is a partition of the vertex set into two disjoint
sets (S, ¯S) such that S ∪¯S = V and S ∩¯S = ∅. The cardinality of the cut is the number of
edges with one endpoint in S and the other in ¯S. Namely,

Throughout this paper, we use the terms cut size and cut cardinality interchangeably.
By Menger’s theorem [3], the local edge-connectivity λ(u, v; G) is equivalent to the size of
the minimum cut separating u and v.
We use G∪{e} and G\{e} to represent the graph resulting from the insertion or deletion
of edge e, respectively. In the context of our edge-connectivity maintenance algorithms, enew
refers to a newly inserted edge, while eold refers to an existing edge identified for removal
during Redundancy Elimination. Similarly, edel denotes an edge targeted for deletion by
Connectivity Restoration, and Eaug represents the set of augmenting edges computed by
the framework to restore edge-connectivity.
To address problems defined in Section 1 efficiently, we rely on a fundamental graph-
theoretic property regarding the locality of connectivity changes.
The following lemma
characterizes the structural impact of removing an edge from a k-edge-connected graph,
Lemma 1. Let G = (V, E) be an undirected and k-edge-connected graph and e = {u, v} be
an edge in E. Let G′ = G \ {e}. If λ(G′) < k, then λ(u, v; G) = k −1. Furthermore, any
cut in G′ which cardinality is less than k must separate u and v.
Proof. Consider any cut (S, ¯S) in G′ with cardinality strictly less than k. Since the original
graph G is k-edge-connected, the cardinality of this cut in G must have been at least k. The
removal of the single edge e = {u, v} can reduce size of a cut by at most one, and this occurs
if and only if e crosses the cut (i.e., u ∈S and v ∈¯S or vice versa). Therefore, for the cut’s
capacity to drop below k in G′, its original capacity must have been exactly k, and it must
separate u and v, resulting in a final cardinality of exactly k −1.
We use this lemma directly in the restoration algorithm of Section 5.

The procedure partitions E into a

of O(m). We use this procedure as a preprocessing step to construct the sparse certificate
before handling dynamic edge additions.

For dynamic maintenance of the sparse certificate we use the Link-Cut tree data struc-
ture of Sleator and Tarjan [14]. A Link-Cut tree maintains a forest of rooted trees. Each
represented tree is partitioned into vertex-disjoint paths via a preferred-path decomposition,
and each such path is stored as a splay tree [15] keyed by the depth of its nodes in the rep-
resented tree. The auxiliary splay trees are connected by path-parent pointers, which link
the root of each auxiliary tree to the parent (in the represented tree) of the topmost node
on its preferred path. We use the following operations [14, 2]:
• MakeTree(v) – creates a new singleton tree containing the vertex v,
• FindRoot(v) – returns the root of the tree containing vertex v,
• link(v, w) – adds an edge making v a child of w, assuming v is the root of its tree
and v, w belong to distinct trees,
• cut(v) – removes the edge between vertex v and its parent parent(v) (where v is not
the root), splitting the tree into two disjoint components and makes v the root of a
represented tree,
• evert(v) – modify the tree containing vertex v by making v its root.
Each operation runs in O(log n) amortized time. To initialize the structure, we compute
the sparse certificate using Algorithm 1 and construct k Link-Cut trees LC1, . . . , LCk, where
each LCi is built by executing MakeTree(v) for every v ∈V followed by link operations
for every edge in the corresponding forest Fi.
The core building block of the maintenance procedure is the TryAdd(LC, u, v) function
(Algorithm 2), which attempts to insert an edge {u, v} into the forest Fi maintained by LCi
(1 ≤i ≤k). If u and v lie in different components of Fi, the edge is added via link and
the function returns null. Otherwise, u and v are already connected in Fi, so adding {u, v}
would create a cycle. In this case, the function removes the edge {u, parent(u)} via cut,
inserts {u, v} in its place via link, and returns the displaced edge {u, parent(u)} to the
caller for insertion into the next forest in the sparse certificate.
The primary maintenance routine, HandleAddition(e) (Algorithm 3), uses TryAdd
as a subroutine in a cascading fashion. Given a new edge e = {u, v}, the procedure calls
TryAdd on LC1. If the edge is absorbed (TryAdd returns null), the process terminates.
Otherwise, the displaced edge is passed to TryAdd on LC2, and so on through the sequence
of forests up to LCk. If an edge is displaced from the final forest Fk, it is redundant for
k-edge-connectivity and is discarded.

4.3
Complexity analysis of sparse certificate maintenance algorithm
Initialization consists of two steps: computing the forest decomposition F1, F2, . . . , Fk via
Algorithm 1 in O(m) time, and building the k Link-Cut structures LC1, LC2, . . . , LCk. The
latter requires O(kn) MakeTree and link operations, each costing O(log n) amortized
time, for a total initialization cost of O(m + k · n log n).
For each edge addition, HandleAddition calls TryAdd on at most k forests. Each
TryAdd call performs O(1) Link-Cut tree operations (FindRoot, cut, link), so the
amortized cost per edge addition is O(k log n).

Restoration after Edge Removal
5.1
Edge-connectivity restoration algorithm
This section addresses restoring k-edge-connectivity after an edge deletion. Let e = {u, v}
be the deleted edge and let G′ = G \ {e}. If λ(G′) ≥k, no action is needed. Otherwise, the
algorithm must compute a set of augmenting edges Eaug such that λ(G′ ∪Eaug) ≥k. We
compute a maximum u-v flow in G′ using Dinic’s algorithm [4], whose complexity on unit-
capacity networks is O(min(n2/3, m1/2) · m) by Even and Tarjan [5], and use the resulting
residual graph to identify Eaug.
We model each undirected edge {u, v} ∈E as a pair of directed arcs (u, v) and (v, u),
each with unit capacity; for background on flow networks and residual graphs we refer to [1].
In the resulting residual graph Gf, a saturated arc (u, v) with f(u, v) = 1 is replaced by a
backward arc (v, u) with residual capacity 2, while an unsaturated arc retains both directions
with residual capacity 1.
The procedure HandleDeletion (Algorithm 4) works as follows. Given the deleted
edge e = {u, v}, it computes a maximum u-v flow on G′ = G \ {e}. If the flow value is still
at least k, the edge was redundant and no action is needed. Otherwise, let Gf denote the
residual graph. The algorithm computes S, the set of vertices reachable from u in Gf, and
T, the set of vertices from which v is reachable in Gf; both sets are obtained by BFS or
DFS algorithm on Gf.
The algorithm distinguishes two cases. If |S| ≥2 or |T| ≥2, there exists a pair u′ ∈S,
v′ ∈T with {u′, v′} ̸= {u, v}; the algorithm adds {u′, v′} to G′ (Algorithm 4, lines 15-17).
If S = {u} and T = {v}, the only edge from S to T is the deleted edge {u, v}, so instead
the algorithm selects an arbitrary vertex w ∈V \ {u, v} and adds the two edges {u, w} and
{w, v} (Algorithm 4, lines 12-13).

Theorem 7. Let G′ = G \ {e} where e = {u, v} and λ(G′) < k. Let G′′ be the augmented
graph produced by Algorithm 4. Then λ(G′′) ≥k, and |E(G′′) \ E(G′)| ≤2.
Proof. By Lemma 1, λ(u, v; G′) = k −1, and every cut of size less than k in G′ separates u
and v. The max u-v flow in G′ therefore equals k −1.
1. Case 1: |S| ≥2 or |T| ≥2. There exist u′ ∈S and v′ ∈T with {u′, v′} ̸= {u, v}. The
algorithm adds {u′, v′}. By Lemma 6, this increases the maximum u-v flow to k. Since
every cut of size less than k separates u and v (Lemma 1), and the minimum u-v cut
now has size k, we have λ(G′′) ≥k. One edge is added.
2. Case 2: S = {u} and T = {v}. The algorithm selects w ∈V \ {u, v} and adds {u, w}
and {w, v}. This creates the augmenting path u →w →v, increasing the maximum
u-v flow to k. The same argument as Case 1 gives λ(G′′) ≥k. Two edges are added.
In both cases |E(G′′) \ E(G′)| ≤2 holds.
The cost of Algorithm 4 is dominated by one execution of Dinic’s algorithm on the
sparsified graph G′. After applying the Nagamochi–Ibaraki sparsification [12], the graph
has m = O(kn) edges while preserving all cuts of size at most k.
By the Even–Tarjan
bound [5], Dinic’s algorithm on a unit-capacity network runs in O(min(n2/3, m1/2) · m)
time. Substituting m = O(kn), this yields O(n2/3 · kn) = O(kn5/3) and O((kn)1/2 · kn) =
O(k3/2 · n3/2), respectively. Since k is a fixed constant, k1/2 · n1/2 < n2/3 for sufficiently
large n, so the second branch dominates and the total cost is O(k3/2 ·n3/2). The subsequent
BFS/DFS algorithm to compute S and T runs in O(kn) time and does not affect the
asymptotic bound.
