Residual Power Flow for Neural Solvers
Jochen Stiasny, Member, IEEE, and Jochen Cremer, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—The energy transition challenges operational tasks
based on simulations and optimisation. These computations
need to be fast and flexible as the grid is ever-expanding,
and renewables’ uncertainty requires a flexible operational en-
vironment. Learned approximations, proxies or surrogates—we
refer to them as Neural Solvers—excel in terms of evaluation
speed, but are inflexible with respect to adjusting to changing
tasks. Hence, neural solvers are usually applicable to highly
specific tasks, which limits their usefulness in practice; a widely
reusable, foundational neural solver is required. Therefore, this
work proposes the Residual Power Flow (RPF) formulation.
RPF formulates residual functions based on Kirchhoff’s laws
to quantify the infeasibility of an operating condition. The
minimisation of the residuals determines the voltage solution; an
additional slack variable is needed to achieve AC-feasibility. RPF
forms a natural, foundational subtask of tasks subject to power
flow constraints. We propose to learn RPF with neural solvers
to exploit their speed. Furthermore, RPF improves learning
performance compared to common power flow formulations.
To solve operational tasks, we integrate the neural solver in
a Predict-then-Optimise (PO) approach to combine speed and
flexibility. The case study investigates the IEEE 9-bus system
and three tasks (AC Optimal Power Flow (OPF), power-flow
and quasi-steady state power flow) solved by PO. The results
demonstrate the accuracy and flexibility of learning with RPF.

I. INTRODUCTION
T
HE energy transition changes the operation of power
systems. The systems must operate at a higher throughput
due to the electrification and increasing demand. At the same
time, the dependency on increasingly more renewable energy
leads to more uncertainty which requires grid operators to im-
prove operational awareness and ensure security [1]. Improv-
ing situational awareness in operations requires relying more
and more on repeated simulations and optimisations, which
we refer to as computational tasks [2], [3]. Such a task could
be, for example, to determine AC-Optimal Power Flow (OPF),
to perform state estimation, or to assess dynamic security. The
solutions of these tasks depend on the Operating Conditions
(OCs), such as loading conditions, and task specifications, such
as operational constraints and objectives. The dominating type
of approach is numerical algorithms that are often specialised
for a specific task. These algorithms exploit the problem
structure, and while they have great applicability to different
task specifications, OCs are often re-solved repeatedly even if
a very similar OC had been solved before. In contrast, the

The approach to interleaving numerical and neural solvers
is rooted in the following observation: Many different tasks
inherit the Power Flow (PF) equations as constraints [7].
Hence, satisfying the PF constraints forms a common sub-task
that can be used under varying OCs and task specifications.
The structure of splitting a problem into a sub-task and a main
task resembles decomposition approaches which have been
termed feedback control or feedback optimisation in the power
flow context [8]–[10]. The approach of Predict-then-Optimise
(PO) [11] follows a similar structure; however, PO emphasises
that the sub-task is learned. We adapt this PO approach to
power system tasks as shown in Fig. 1: We form the sub-task
of predicting the PF solution with a neural solver. To solve the
main optimisation task, we can reuse the same neural solver
in a PO fashion to PF-related tasks such as AC-OPF or state
estimation. The so-far unanswered question is how the learning
problem for solving the PF constraint in the sub-task shall be
posed to achieve high flexibility and accuracy.
Important criteria for quality of a (learned) PF approxima-
tion are accuracy, speed, and robustness. The latter two criteria
are well covered by linearising the PF equations [12], [13], and
data-driven methods can be used to enhance accuracy [14]–
[16]. Generally, these approximations do not yield AC-feasible
PF solutions. Neural Networks (NNs) allow more expressive
and accurate function approximations, in particular with the

This proposition of focusing on infeasible PF solutions

a binary quantification (feasibility versus infeasibility). To this
end, the contributions of this paper are:
• Residual Power Flow (RPF) formulation defining a resid-

• Predict-then-Optimise (PO) approach with neural solvers
for RPF as a foundation for a variety of power system
tasks constrained by PF.
• Analysis of the RPF formulation’s impact on the approx-

To demonstrate the proposed approach, we learn the RPF
solution with a neural solver across a range of OCs for the
IEEE 9-bus system. We then apply the neural solver to several
power system tasks (AC-feasible PF, quasi-steady-state PF,
AC-OPF) to illustrate the flexibility of the PO approach.

II. RESIDUAL POWER FLOW (RPF) FORMULATION
This section introduces the proposed RPF formulation.
We begin by defining infeasibility and a brief exposition of
Kirchhoff’s laws, followed by introducing the variables and
constraints in RPF. Subsequently, we describe the modelling
of the current injectors and the definition of the RPF solution.
A. Feasibility of AC-PF and the role of bus types
A feasible PF is defined by the combination of voltages v
and controls u that satisfy a set of equality constraints Ψ
Ψ (v, u) = 0.
(1)
The controls u include setpoints of generation and load and
thereby determine the power (or current) injections for an
OC. If Ψ (v, u) ̸= 0, we consider the PF infeasible. While
the underlying physics, namely Kirchhoff’s laws, universally
prescribe the constraints in Ψ (v, u), there are different mathe-
matical representations possible as reviewed in [12]. The most
common representation is the Bus Injection Model (BIM) with
different variable formulations, followed by the “DistFlow”
[30], [31] or “Branch flow” [32] model. Solving PF implicitly
means finding a feasible solution to Ψ (v, u). To do so,
modellers make an additional central representation choice:
Buses in a grid have types—a slack bus, PV and PQ buses, and
a reference bus1. The bus types define the known and unknown
variables, as shown in Table I, and ensure a fully determined
system of equations with equal number of constraints and
variables. Introducing bus types effectively reduces the number
of constraints in (1) by selecting the control variables Q at
PV-buses and P and Q at the slack bus after solving for the
voltage variables. Thereby, the affected constraints are always
satisfied.

When we learn a solution ˆv (u), we will encounter con-
straint violations as the approximation ˆv will carry some error
|ˆv −v|. Because of the bus type assignment, these constraint
violations can be compensated at PV and slack buses by
adjusting the control variables ˆu (ˆv) accordingly. However, the
adjusted controls ˆu then also differ from the exact, feasible
solution u. As a result, a learned approximation using the
BIM with bus types will lead to some satisfied constraints
and correct voltages, but also unsatisfied constraints, erroneous
voltages and wrongly adjusted control values. In summary, a
highly asymmetric distribution of the approximation errors and
no clear notion of the infeasibility of the solution.
For numerical methods, the asymmetry, which the bus types
cause, is less critical as the system of equations is solved
to very high accuracy. Still, the assignment of a slack bus
[33], [34] or reactive power limits that lead to switching
between PV and PQ buses [7], [35] can cause ambiguities and
difficulties when solving PF in this representation. For learning
problems, however, steps that affect the representation become
critical. Such steps can be feature engineering, the definition
of learning objectives, and the choice of model architectures
[36]. Ideally, the representation captures the geometry and
symmetries of the underlying problem [37], [38]. Disregarding
these representational choices can require more data in the
training, perform worse in unseen scenarios, or lead to decision
that are clearly contradicting the underlying physics.
Therefore, we have two objectives in the development of the
PF formulation: First, to define a clear notion of infeasibility
and second, to avoid the assignment of bus types which causes
representational asymmetries. By fulfilling these objectives,
we can define a meaningful value to quantify the infeasibility
of a PF and obtain a well-defined map u 7→v that we will
subsequently learn with a neural solver.

V. CASE STUDY: LEARNING RPF WITH NEURAL SOLVERS
In this case study we demonstrate the implication of switch-
ing from a BIM to RPF when learning PFs with neural solvers.
We analyse the distribution of approximation errors across the
voltage variables v and across the residuals r. These analyses
highlight the effect of asymmetries in the PF formulation and
the treatment of infeasibility which RPF improves.

VII. DISCUSSION
The RPF formulation opens many paths forward around
neural solvers and the PO approach, but also in connection
with established PF topics.
Advancing Neural Solvers for RPF: With the proposed
RPF formulation, we see the need to revisit neural solver
architectures and training. In particular, the avoidance of bus
types could allow architectures that align closely with RPF;
graph-based neural solvers as in [18] seem promising. To
improve the training additional physics-informed regularisers
could be added. The optimisation of the training and evaluation
computations will be an important focus for scaling neural
solvers. For reference, the used non-optimised implementation
in Julia trains the neural solver within a few minutes and
evaluates the RPF in about 30 µs. By tailoring and optimising
the computations of the residual calculations, the time and
memory footprint is likely to decrease.
The ambition for neural solvers should to be to solve a wide
range of OCs, ideally with topological changes, component
availabilities, and varying parameter settings. This adaptability
would enable the use of the trained neural solvers for a
wide variety of PO formulations. Such a foundational RPF
neural solver would even justify high upfront cost for dataset
generation and training due its versatile applicability.
Tailoring PO to Neural Solvers: The robustness, speed, and
accuracy of the PO approach hinges around the setup of the
outer optimisation problem in (22). The applied optimisation
algorithms need to be aligned with the constraint definition
and the weighting of the terms in the objective function.
Furthermore, different neural solver architectures could exhibit
different characteristics when included in the PO approach.
We have only showcased a few power system task that
suit the approach of PO with neural solver for RPF. State
estimation appears like a natural fit and more advanced neural
solver architectures, as discussed above, could enable contin-
gency analyses and planning studies. These application would
require to handle topological adjustment within the neural
solver. Furthermore, dynamics studies could be conducted if
dynamic current injections in (7b) can be considered.
New perspectives through RPF: Our development of RPF
was driven by its use for neural solvers. However, we observed
many connections to classical power system topics for which
RPF and its quantification of infeasibility could yield new
perspectives. To name a few, state estimation and dynamic
simulations are tightly linked, but also methods like continua-
tion PF, holomorphic embeddings of PF, PF relaxations, and
the study of feasibility of OCs seem relatable.
VIII. CONCLUSION
We proposed the Residual Power Flow (RPF) formulation
that is tailored for learning power flows with neural solvers.
RPF has two key characteristics that make it particular suitable
for learning: First, the formulation avoids the asymmetries that
are usually introduced by defining bus types. Second, infea-
sible power flows are naturally represented which improves
the handling of approximation errors. These characteristics
of RPF improve the suitability for learning power flows with
neural solvers while also allowing their direct integration into a
Predict-then-Optimise (PO) approach. While the PO approach
provides flexibility to adapt to many power flow-related tasks,
the same neural solver can be reused to provide fast power
flow approximations.
