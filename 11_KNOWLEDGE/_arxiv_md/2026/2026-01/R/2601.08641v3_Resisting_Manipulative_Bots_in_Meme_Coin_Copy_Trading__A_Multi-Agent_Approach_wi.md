Abstract
Copy trading has become the dominant entry strategy in meme
coin markets. However, due to the market’s extremely illiquid and
volatile nature, the strategy exposes an exploitable attack surface:
adversaries deploy manipulative bots to front-run trades, conceal
positions, and fabricate sentiment, systematically extracting value
from naïve copiers at scale. Despite its prevalence, bot-driven manip-
ulation remains largely unexplored, and no robust defensive frame-
work exists. We propose a manipulation-resistant copy-trading
system based on a multi-agent architecture powered by a multi-
modal large language model (LLM) and chain-of-thought (CoT)
reasoning. Our approach outperforms zero-shot and most statistic-
driven baselines in prediction accuracy as well as all baselines in
economic performance, achieving an average copier return of 3%
per meme coin investment under realistic market frictions. Overall,
our results demonstrate the effectiveness of agent-based defenses
and predictability of trader profitability in adversarial meme coin
markets, providing a practical foundation for robust copy trading.

Introduction
While manipulative tactics such as rat trading are well-trodden
ruses in traditional financial markets, these old-fashioned ploys
have now become bot-driven and prey on naïve copy traders in
a new hunting ground: the meme coin market. The launch of the
$TRUMP meme coin on January 17, 2025 ignited a surge of specu-
lation in the meme coin market, drawing millions of traders. How-
ever, many of these entrants lack prior trading experience. To lower

the barrier to participation, meme coin tracking platforms such as
GMGN introduced copy trading, an automated, one-click feature
that allows users to replicate the trades of other wallets. In prac-
tice, copiers attempt to identify so-called smart money—wallets
perceived to possess insider knowledge, trading expertise, and con-
sistent profitability—and replicate their trading activity [2, 30].
However, the characteristics of the meme coin market, most
notably extreme illiquidity and high volatility, combined with the
naïveté of copy traders, create an ideal environment for exploita-
tion. Sophisticated adversaries deploy automated trading bots (e.g.,
PumpKing) to systematically manipulate prices and induce pre-
dictable copy-trading behavior. In particular, some key opinion
leaders (KOLs) featured on tracking platforms may abuse their
perceived credibility by coordinating multiple bots to accumulate
positions early at low prices, artificially inflate prices while conceal-
ing their true exposure, and engage in wash trading and fabricated
on-chain or social activity to create an illusion of demand [6, 12, 25].
These optics of profitability attract copiers, inflate prices, and allow
manipulators to gain by using exit liquidity from copy traders. De-
spite the prevalence of these attacks, neither industry nor academia
has established a copy-trading framework robust to manipulative
bots, hence our motivation to design such a framework.
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) offer a promis-
ing foundation for our design. Leveraging their strong capabilities
in multi-modal understanding and reasoning [15, 17, 33, 43–45],
LLM-powered multi-agent systems (MASs) are well suited for bot-
resilient copy trading. First, the on-chain meme coin market can be
encoded as a machine-readable environment for sequential copy-
trading decisions, in which agents interact with multi-modal on-
chain and off-chain signals while reasoning over latent factors such
as market sentiment and sophisticated manipulation. This formu-
lation aligns well with the multi-modal inference and reasoning
strengths of LLM-powered agent systems. Moreover, investment
decisions in traditional financial institutions are typically made
by teams rather than individuals; MASs mirror this collective hu-
man cognitive decision-making process by enabling multiple spe-
cialized agents to collaborate, deliberate, and reach more robust
copy-trading decisions, thereby improving resilience against ma-
nipulative bots and adversarial trading behavior.

However, due to limited domain knowledge of meme coin mar-
kets, zero-shot LLMs often fail to identify subtle manipulative pat-
terns or reliably distinguish genuine smart money from adversarial
wallets. Chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, in contrast, injects do-
main knowledge into agent deliberation, enabling agents to inter-
nalize copy-trading knowledge and reason in a manner closer to
experienced human copy traders.
Accordingly, we propose an LLM-powered MAS for manipulation-
resistant meme coin copy trading with CoT reasoning. Our frame-
work decomposes copy trading into three core subtasks—coin eval-
uation, wallet selection, and timing assessment—each handled by a
specialized agent guided by few-shot CoT prompts. We evaluate our
framework on historical wallet-level data from 6,000 meme coins
and show that the proposed multi-agent system outperforms the
zero-shot and most statistic-driven baselines in prediction accuracy.
Economically, the smart-money wallets identified by our frame-
work achieve an average return of 14%, while the corresponding
estimated copier return is 3% per meme coin investment under
realistic market frictions. Our contributions are multifold:
• We propose an LLM-based MAS for meme coin copy trading.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of a
multi-agent system to copy trading.
• We provide a comprehensive analysis of manipulative bots in
meme coin markets under copy-trading settings. We formalize
manipulation heuristics, develop detection algorithms, and em-
pirically quantify bot activity in the meme coin market.
• We design a structured feature space tailored to meme coin mar-
kets that captures manipulative bot presence as well as traders’
historical performance and experience. Using these features and
proposed prediction models, we show that trader profitability is
predictable, providing a foundation for meme coin copy trading.

Related Works
We next review related work on crypto market manipulation and
LLM-powered agents in blockchain to highlight the gaps our work
addresses in adversarial copy-trading settings.
Crypto Market Manipulation. Manipulation in cryptocur-
rency markets has been widely documented, including pump-and-
dump schemes [5, 35, 41], rug pull [24, 26, 31], wash trading [1,
3, 28], metric distortion [21, 29], and wallet drainers [7]. In the
context of meme coin markets, Mongardini and Mei [25] and Li
et al. [16] document the prevalence of systematic manipulation.
Ding et al. [6] empirically quantify the causal effects of distinct
meme coin manipulation strategies on project performance and
participant profitability. Xiang et al. [36] develop a framework to
quantify the fragility in the meme coin market. This line of work
primarily focuses on measuring manipulation, whereas defensive
mechanisms for copy trading under adversarial conditions remain
underexplored.
LLM-powered Agents in Blockchain. Recent advances in
LLM-powered autonomous agents have begun to transform the
blockchain ecosystem. Prior work has explored the use of LLM-
based agents in cryptocurrency markets and decentralized finance
(DeFi), particularly in quantitative trading [14, 22, 32], smart con-
tract auditing [34], and user intent understanding [23]. Building on
evidence that CoT prompting substantially enhances the reasoning
capabilities of LLMs [33, 38, 39], recent studies have increasingly
incorporated CoT-based reasoning to improve decision-making in
financial agents [9, 27, 37, 46]. However, the application of multi-
agent LLM systems to adversarially robust copy trading in meme
coin markets has received limited attention.
3
Background
3.1
Solana and Decentralized Finance
Solana is a high-throughput permissionless blockchain that sup-
ports DeFi applications via smart contracts and a block-based ex-
ecution model. It features sub-second block times (approximately
400 ms) and processes thousands of transactions per block, enabling
rapid on-chain state updates and dense trading activity. At the
time of writing, Solana has processed over 500 billion transactions.
Leveraging its high throughput, Solana does not rely on a public
mempool in which pending transactions remain visible for an ex-
tended period prior to block inclusion. Instead, transactions are
forwarded directly to validators and incorporated into blocks with
minimal delay, leaving little time for the general public to observe
pending transactions and react to emerging trading activity. As a
result, the ability to respond strategically, such as through front-
running or coordination, is concentrated among well-positioned or
infrastructure-level participants.
DeFi activity on Solana consists of repeated interactions between
wallets and the evolving on-chain state. At each block, wallets
observe prices, balances, and transactions, and submit actions such
as token creation, buys, sells, and transfers. State updates follow
deterministic smart-contract logic [40], but these trading decisions
are subject to uncertainty from transaction ordering, concurrent
wallet activity, and latent coordination.
3.2
Meme Coin Markets and Copy Trading
Meme coins are cryptocurrencies originating from internet memes
[18, 19]. They are typically characterized by low liquidity and ex-
treme volatility, with their prices driven by community hype, social
media activity, and celebrity endorsements. Pump.fun is the largest
on-chain meme coin launch platform on Solana, enabling users to
rapidly create and trade meme coins. Traders purchase meme coins
using SOL, Solana’s native cryptocurrency, paying a 1% transaction
fee and following a bonding curve mechanism. The bonding curve
induces a strictly increasing and convex price path, so prices rise
deterministically with cumulative demand, yielding diminishing
tokens received per unit of SOL deposited. As a result, late entry is
mechanically penalized along the curve (see §A.1). Besides the fi-
nancial market, pump.fun also provides a social platform. Any user
with a Solana wallet can comment on any coin and interact with
other users by referencing specific comments via unique comment
IDs (e.g., “@144840307 it is wild”). Each trade bumps the coin’s
name to the front page with a brief jiggle. As illustrated in Fig. 1,
pump.fun publicly displays both transaction histories and token
holding distributions, with the meme coin creator explicitly labeled.
In parallel, meme coin tracking platforms such as GMGN aggre-
gate on-chain trading activity and highlight so-called KOL wallets
that are ranked based on returns or number of trades, providing
copy-trading tools to replicate trades from selected addresses.

Modeling and Detecting Manipulations
This section describes manipulations in detail. We introduce our
threat model, discuss ways to carry out manipulation, propose
detection metrics and algorithms, and present detection results.
4.1
Threat Model
We assume a strategic adversary capable of rapidly creating and
controlling a large number of on-chain wallets. The adversary can
coordinate these wallets to create, buy, and sell meme coins with
precise timing and high execution speed, thereby ensuring transac-
tion inclusion in targeted blocks.
The adversary’s ultimate objective is to extract economic value
from copy traders. Specifically, the adversary seeks to (i) select
a historically profitable wallet to attract victims on meme coin
tracking websites or social media, (ii) acquire meme coins at very
early stages, either by launching the meme coins themselves (as
bundler) or by entering immediately after meme coin creation (as
sniper), (iii) conceal true positions by distributing purchases across
multiple controlled wallets, (iv) fake trading volume and social–
media sentiment through wash trading and coordinated comment
bots, thereby increasing copier confidence, and (v) strategically exit
positions by coordinating sales across controlled wallets, thereby
further strengthening the appearance of consistent profitability and
sustaining the manipulation cycle.
We model the interaction between the adversary and copy traders
as a near zero-sum game. The adversary’s profits come directly from
the losses of copy traders who follow manipulated on-chain and
off-chain signals.
4.2
Manipulative Bot Behaviors
To operationalize at scale, the adversary deploys automated bots
that programmatically control wallets and execute transactions. We
categorize these bots into two broad classes based on their primary
role: position manipulation bots and attention manipulation bots.
4.2.1
Position Manipulation Bots. Position manipulation bots, in-
cluding bundle bots and sniper bots, exploit temporal advantages
at or near meme coin creation to secure (and conceal) positions
before copy traders can react. Beyond early acquisition, these bots
coordinate wallet splitting and synchronized execution to obfus-
cate true positions and evade detection. We further illustrate the
underlying mechanisms and heterogeneity of frontrunning and
concealment strategies. Fig. 2 provides evolution and heuristics
of position manipulation bots in copy trading. Fig. 2a illustrates
an unmanipulated copy-trading process. The meme coin creator
launches a meme coin at block 𝑏1 ( 1 ). A KOL wallet identifies the
meme coin’s potential and purchases it at block 𝑏3 ( 2 ). A copier
wallet imitates the KOL’s action and buys the meme coin at block
𝑏4 ( 3 ). From block 𝑏4 to 𝑏4+𝑖, additional retail traders enter the
market, trade the meme coin, and drive the price upward. The KOL
sells the meme coin at a profit at block 𝑏6+𝑖( 4 ). The copier wallet
also sells the meme coin at a profit at block 𝑏7+𝑖( 5 ).
While the process above reflects an unmanipulated copy-trading
scenario, it assumes that the KOL acts honestly. To maximize profit,
a KOL may instead employ a strategy known as a naïve bundle, as
illustrated in Fig. 2b. Unlike unmanipulated copy trading, the KOL
in this strategy creates the meme coin and purchases it themself
within the same block 𝑏1 ( 1 ). When a copier wallet subsequently
purchases the meme coin ( 2 ), the KOL (also the creator) immedi-
ately sells the meme coin for a profit ( 3 ), leaving the copiers with
a loss ( 4 ).
However, this naïve strategy is easy to detect, since pump.fun
and other meme coin tracking platforms publicly expose transac-
tion histories and token holding distributions, with the meme coin
creator explicitly identified (see §3.2 and Fig. 1). As a result, copiers
can readily identify the creator’s concentrated ownership and avoid
such meme coins. In response, adversaries adopt more sophisticated
strategies, namely, the bundle bot (Fig. 2c) and the gradual bundle
bot (Fig. 2d), to decouple meme coin creation from accumulation.

In these strategies, the KOL first creates and funds multiple fresh
wallets ( 1 ), launches the meme coin ( 2 ), and then uses automated
scripts to coordinate purchases across these wallets, either within
a single block (Fig. 2c) or gradually over multiple blocks (Fig. 2d)
( 3 ). Because the purchasing wallets appear independent, copiers
are less likely to associate them with the meme coin creator and
are more inclined to follow the observed buying activity ( 4 ). Fi-
nally, once sufficient copy-trading demand has accumulated, the
KOL coordinates these wallets to exit their positions and dump the
meme coin ( 5 ), leaving the copier with a loss ( 6 ).
In addition to bundle-based strategies, adversaries may employ
a sniper bot to exploit copy trading without participating in meme
coin creation, as shown in Fig. 2e. A sniper bot monitors newly
launched meme coins and identifies those that have not yet been
bundled by their creators. Upon detecting such a meme coin ( 1 ), the
bot executes purchases within a very short time window following
meme coin creation, typically within the first one to five blocks
(approximately 0.4 to 2 seconds on Solana, see §3.1) ( 2 ). This early
purchase subsequently attracts copier wallets, which replicate the
observed buying activity and enter the market ( 3 ), driving up the
meme coin price. The sniper bot then exits its position for a profit
( 4 ), leaving copier wallets exposed to losses ( 5 ).
4.2.2
Attention Manipulation Bots. Attention manipulation bots
generate artificial trading activity and coordinated social signals to
capture and amplify attention around specific meme coins. By fak-
ing demand and legitimacy, these bots steer copy traders’ attention
toward targeted meme coins, reinforce their confidence in follow-
ing observed trading behavior, and attract additional uninformed
retail participants. We further decompose attention manipulation
bots into two representative categories: bump bots and comment
bots. Fig. 3 illustrates the heuristics of these bots, highlighting how
on-chain activity and off-chain signals jointly amplify attention.
Bump bots exploit the bumping mechanism in pump.fun by re-
peatedly buying and selling identical amounts of a meme coin,
thereby inflating short-term trading activity and visibility while
maintaining minimal net exposure, as illustrated in Fig. 3a. Com-
ment bots, in contrast, consist of wallets that automatically generate
coordinated hype comments to simulate community engagement
and amplify perceived interest, as shown in Fig. 3b1.
4.3
Detection Metrics and Algorithms
We next explain how we detect manipulative bots and the results
of the detection.
4.3.1
Bundle Bot. Based on the heuristic illustrated in Fig. 2c, a key
characteristic of bundle bots is that, within the meme coin creation
block, the creator wallet not only deploys the token but is imme-
diately followed by near-simultaneous purchases from additional
controlled wallets. Given Solana’s lack of a public mempool and its
very short block interval (see §3.1), such same-block coordination
is unlikely to be achieved by independent frontrunners and instead
indicates pre-coordinated control across wallets. Accordingly, we
detect bundle bots by checking whether non-creator wallets exe-
cute purchases within the meme coin creation block, as formalized
in Alg. 1.
4.3.2
Sniper Bot. Similarly, sniper bots exhibit salient temporal
characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 2e. They typically execute pur-
chases within the first one to five blocks after meme coin creation,
a timescale well below human reaction time. We present the sniper
bot detection algorithm in Alg. 2.
4.3.3
Bump Bot. Unlike bundle bots and sniper bots, bump bots do
not exhibit salient temporal characteristics. Instead, their behavior
is characterized by repetitive trading patterns. As illustrated in
Fig. 3a, bump bots repeatedly buy and sell identical amounts of a
meme coin. We define a flip as a pair of consecutive transactions
with opposite directions and identical meme coin quantities to cap-

rewards repetitive flip behavior while penalizing significant net

Gradual Bundle. Gradual bundles represent a more sophisti-
cated variant of bundle bots. As illustrated in Fig. 2d, they decouple
token creation from purchases over time and may involve multi-
layer funding networks. Combined with the scale of the Solana
blockchain (see §3.1), these properties obscure temporal signals
and attribution, making gradual bundles much harder to detect
accurately. In practice, experienced meme coin traders may rely on
candlestick patterns to infer and avoid such coins, as the candlestick
dynamics of gradual bundles differ systematically from those of
non-manipulative tokens: the former tend to exhibit mechanically
regular patterns, whereas the latter display more organic patterns.

4.4
Detection Results
For on-chain data, we use account-level transaction and transfer
records obtained from Flipside, a blockchain data provider that
maintains archive nodes with fully indexed, historical access to the
Solana ledger. We additionally collect off-chain comment data from
pump.fun. Our final dataset comprises 6,000 meme coin projects,
including all associated creators and traders, along with their com-
plete historical trading records.
Fig. 4 presents the distributions of meme coin projects with and
without four types of bots, grouped into position manipulation and
attention manipulation bots, and their heterogeneous effects on two
performance metrics: maximum log return and log dump duration.
The maximum log return is defined as the peak price appreciation
during the lift phase, 𝐿𝑛(Max Return) = ln(𝑃max/𝑃launch), where
𝑃max and 𝑃launch denote the peak and launch prices, respectively. The
𝐿𝑛(Dump Duration) is defined as the natural logarithm of seconds
from the peak price until liquidity declines to 10% of its peak level.
For position manipulation bots, the performance distributions with
and without bots largely overlap, indicating limited influence on
either return or duration. Bundle bots appear in roughly one quarter
of projects and are weakly associated with lower returns and shorter
dump durations (Fig. 4a), while sniper bots are widespread but
show minimal observable performance effect (Fig. 4b). This pattern
suggests that the primary role of position manipulation bots is not to
alter overall project performance, but to redistribute profits within
the participant pool, shifting gains from slower retail traders to early

5
Bot-Resilient Copy Trading
Based on the analysis in the previous section, we find that manip-
ulative bots distort market efficiency, which in turn affects copy

MAS-based Models. We introduce an LLM-powered MAS-based
model for bot-resilient copy trading. The framework consists of
three specialized LLM agents with CoT reasoning: a wallet agent,
a coin agent, and a timing agent. We employ GPT-4o as the base
model for all agents2.
Fig. 5 illustrates how domain-specific knowledge is injected into
each agent via few-shot CoT prompting. For each agent, we first
decide the MAS conditions defined for each feature based on [6] and
annotations from professional traders 3. As shown in Fig. 5a and
Tab. 1, we consider four types of conditions to make the prediction
TRUE: (i) Boolean conditions enforcing binary constraints (e.g., the
absence of bundle-bot behavior); (ii) Visual or language-based
conditions based on candlestick pattern or textual sentiment cues;
(iii) Statistical conditions imposing significance constraints (e.g.,
requiring 𝑡-statistics to exceed a threshold) or consistent directional
effects; and (iv) Continuous conditions requiring feature values to
fall within specific percentile ranges computed from the training
set. We then select a small set of representative examples. Next, we
verify whether these examples satisfy the conditions. From these,
we retain a subset of profitable and unprofitable cases that satisfy all
conditions and manually annotate them to construct agent-specific
CoTs. These few-shot CoT exemplars are subsequently embedded
into the prompts of the corresponding agents.

Both statistic-driven and MAS-based models output a continuous

and performs comparably to the NN, particularly at moderate-to-

degrading 𝐹1 scores as the threshold increases, reflecting its limited
discriminative capacity in this setting. For MAS-based approaches,
incorporating few-shot CoT reasoning leads to higher Precision
and 𝐹1 scores than the zero-shot MAS baseline across nearly all
thresholds, indicating better calibration of confidence scores.
Fig. 7b presents receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves

reasoning and agent specialization for robust ranking performance.

5.3
Economic Performance
In meme coin copy trading, identifying a profitable wallet is not
sufficient to guarantee copier profitability, because execution occurs
on a bonding curve where price impact depends on trade ordering.
Even when a copier immediately replicates the same trades as a
smart money wallet, the copier necessarily trades at a worse point
on the curve. This creates a mechanical performance gap that we
refer to as the imitation penalty.

Conclusion
In this paper, we propose a LLM-powered multi-agent framework
that explicitly reasons over multi-modal on-chain and off-chain sig-
nals. Empirical evaluation on over 6,000 meme coin projects shows
that our approach outperforms zero-shot and most statistic-driven
baselines in prediction accuracy and all baselines in profitability.
Overall, our results demonstrate the effectiveness of agent-based
defenses in adversarial meme coin markets and establish that trader
profitability is predictable, providing a practical foundation for
robust copy trading.
