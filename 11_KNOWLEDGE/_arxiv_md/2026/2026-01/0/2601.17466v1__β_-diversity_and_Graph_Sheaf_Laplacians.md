β-diversity and Graph Sheaf Laplacians
Peter Davidson∗
and Michael Grinfeld†
Department of Mathematics and Statistics,
University of Strathclyde, 26 Richmond Street,
Glasgow, G1 1XH, UK
January 27, 2026
Abstract
We suggest a new approach to β-diversity in ecological systems, based on the energy of the graph
sheaf Laplacian associated with the sample data.
This scalar quantity is easily computable
using methods of linear algebra. We show using simple examples that the energy is much more
informative than the generally accepted definitions of β-diversity.
Keywords: α-, β-, γ-diversity; sheaf theory; graph sheaf Laplacian; energy.
1
Introduction
Whether we humans like it or not, we are the custodians of life on Earth. This is a heavy respon-
sibility and we are slowly learning by trial and many a tragic error how to discharge it.
The first task is to be able to describe the sum total of life on Earth. Unfortunately, that task is
a very hard one, the principles of which are unclear. A recent review of biodiversity Daly et al.
(2018) is even entitled “Ecological Diversity: Measuring the Unmeasurable”.
Below we simplify and equate diversity with richness, i.e., the number of species. More sophisticated
approaches, for example using Hill’s numbers to incorporate unevenness and similarity information
(Chao and Chiu, 2016; Leinster and Cobbold, 2012) can be analysed in the framework suggested
here, but we feel that explaining the principles we are using is best done in the simplest possible
setting.
The point of our departure is that in ecological literature, one traditionally differentiates among α-,
γ-, and β- diversities. Of these α and γ are relatively uncontroversial (once the term “diversity”
itself has been defined): α-diversity is defined in Daly et al. (2018) as referring to the “diversity of
a uniform habitat of a fixed size”; γ-diversity is “the total diversity of the landscape”. However, β-
diversity, which according to Daly et al. (2018), “refers to the average dissimilarity in composition

Simple examples show that the two widely accepted multiplicative (Whittaker, 1972) and additive
(Lande, 1996) definitions of β-diversity are not very informative, as all distributions of species that
agree on the average α- and on γ-diversities lead to the same value of β-diversity.
Such cases differ in the way “local becomes global”.
So any β-diversity defined as a positive
number obtained from directly manipulating γ- and α-diversities will not have good discrimination
properties as it will disregard adjacency relations of sampling sites.
We need to introduce an
operator that takes the collection of α-diversities and creates from them γ-diversity in a way that
at the same time respects such adjacency relations and thus is better able to differentiates between
cases having the same β-diversity. In this contribution we demonstrate that such an operator can
be constructed and that its spectrum contains more information than the β-diversities accepted in
the ecological literature.
The classical local-to-global tool is sheaf theory. The understanding that sheaf theory can be applied
to data analysis dawned in about 2014–2015 in the work of Curry (Curry, 2014) and Robinson
(Robinson, 2014, 2017) and since then a considerable literature on this topic has appeared, mainly
through the work of R. Ghrist and his students (e.g. Ghrist and Riess (2020); Hansen (2020a);
Hansen and Ghrist (2019)). For a detailed recent review please see (Ayzenberg et al., 2025) or
the monograph (Rosiak, 2022). Other interesting contributions are to credence estimates (Biesel,
2024) and to expert selection in the health services (Aktas et al., 2025). Sheaf theory is deep but
computationally accessible, resting on rather simple linear algebra. Hence one can expect emerging
applications of sheaf theory to be of use to researcher communities without expertise in that area
of mathematics.

Defining a sheaf Laplacian
In applying sheaf theory to construct suitable operators (Laplacians) a number of choices has to be
made. Briefly, we start with a graph G = (V, E), with V the set of vertices and E the set of edges,
equip every vertex v and edge e with vector spaces (stalks) Fv, Fe, respectively, containing relevant
data, and for each vertex v adjacent to an edge e define restriction maps from Fv to Fe which we
denote by Fv→e. Finally, for any edge e connecting vertices v and u we define the coboundary map
δ by
(δ)e = Fv→e −Fu→e.
The object we are after is the (graph) sheaf Laplacian, defined by
LF = δT δ.
(1)
Other choices of the underlying structure are possible. Hypergraphs are considered in Duta et al.
(2025); alternatively, one could choose metric spaces of probability distributions instead of vector
spaces for stalks, as is done in Biesel (2024) or lattices of sets (Ghrist and Riess, 2020), but choosing
vector spaces reduces computations to elementary linear algebra.

Examples
In this section we test the expressivity of the spectrum of graph sheaf Laplacians on very simple
examples in which the habitat is composed of three sites.
3.1
Chains
By a chain we mean a graph G on n vertices in which V = {1, 2, . . . , n} and E = {12, 23, . . . , (n −
1)n}, i.e., vertex k (1 < k < n) is connected to vertices k −1 and k + 1. From the ecological point
of view, each vertex is a sampling site and we associate with it the data collected there.
The notation we will use is {a, ab, abc} meaning there is only species a at sampling site 1, species a
and b at 2, and a, b, c at 3. We will show in detail the computation of one coboundary map and then
only the spectrum of the Laplacian and its energy, i.e., the sum of the squares of its eigenvalues.
Below, 04 means zero eigenvalue of multiplicity 4, etc..
Before we start, let us consider and compare two situations: {a, ab, abc} and {ab, ac, bc}. In both
cases γ-diversity is 3 and the average α-diversity is 2, hence the multiplicative β-diversity is 1.5
and the additive one is 1 in both cases. But the situation clearly is different, for example, in terms
of nestedness (Baselga, 2010). Such situations motivate the construction below.
Let us discuss the practical computation of the energy for the graph sheaf Laplacian corresponding
to {ab, a, abc}. See Essl (2023) and Hansen (2020b) for more details of the theory.
We have three vertices, v1, v2, v3 and two edges e12 and e23. We associate with v1 the vector space
R2, with v1 the space R and with v3 the space R3.

are looking for two linear maps into R. This means that associated with the edge e12 we will have

Now we collect the information (spectrum σ, energy E, multiplicative β-diversity) from a number
of simple chain configurations.
(i) {a, b, c}: σ = {03}, E = 0, β = 3;
(ii) {bc, a, abc}: σ = {05, 2}, E = 4, β = 2;
(iii) {ab, ac, bc}: σ = {04, 22}, E = 8, β = 3/2;
(iv) {a, bc, abc}: σ = {04, 22}, E = 8, β = 3/2;
(v) {ab, a, abc}: σ = {04, 1, 3}, E = 10, β = 3/2;

in the latter case configurations with same energy have different values of β-diversity. We will
comment on this case below, but note that in the spectrum of (viii) and (ix) the zero eigenvalue
has different multiplicity.

3.2
K3
The case of (viii)-(ix) can be dealt in the following way: to each chain graph with 3 vertices
corresponds a complete graph K3 with the same data on the vertices.
For example cases (v),
(vii) and (viii) correspond to the same configuration if adjacency is not taken into account. The
computation of energy can be repeated. For the case of (viii) we have σ = {03, 2, 32}, E = 22 while
for (ix) we obtain σ = {03, 32, 22}, E = 26, thus separating the two cases.
Clearly (vii) and (viii) correspond to the same sheaf over K3, so cannot be separated. Interestingly,
neither can (iii) and (iv), both of which over K3 have energy of 12.
We note that the setup suggested in this section leads to an interesting problem in combinatorics:
for all possible configurations of creating n subsets (with repetition) from a set of m elements,
characterise the energy landscape and its discrimination ability.
4
Discussion
Generalising cases (i) and (x) above to chains with n vertices and γ-diversity m we see that the
graph sheaf Laplacian energy is maximised by the configuration in which each site has all m species
present. This situation corresponds to classical β-diversity being as small as possible, either one
in the multiplicative case or zero in the additive case. It is plausible that the minimal energy
configuration will have as many singletons as possible, which maximises β-diversity. So we can
conjecture that if in two configurations with same γ-diversity, β-diversities β1 and β2 and energies
E1 and E2, β1 < β2 implies E1 ≥E2.
One might speculate that in ecosystems energy decays in time, and it would be interesting to see
whether ecosystems minimise the rate of decay of energy. Such a principle would have predictive
ability.
The way we define the sheaf, there is no difference in the chain case between {ab, ac, abc} and
{ad, ac, abc} (of course using sheaves over K3 there is). Note that these two configurations have
different γ-diversity, so γ-diversity cannot be trivially read off from the energy computation. One
suggestion is the following construction. Adjoin to our graph G on n vertices an (n+1)-st vertex to
which we assign data containing all the species. In other words, we hard-wire γ-diversity into the
new graph which we will call Gγ, and connect this vertex with all the existing vertices. Now compute
the energy of the sheaf Laplacian of the new graph.
This construction separates {ab, ac, abc},
the energy of the graph sheaf Laplacian of Gγ being 64, and {ad, ac, abc}, in which case the
corresponding energy is 66. Unfortunately, this construction still does not separate (iii) and (iv),
giving an energy of 46 in both cases. Understanding the difficulty here and separating these two
situations is left for further work. A possibility is to use hypergraphs.
To summarise: we have just started to understand what ecologically important information is
contained in the graph sheaf Laplacian and, in particular, how it relates to and complements
the information provided by the classical β-diversity. Much remains to be done: automating the
computation of the coboundary map, leveraging to the present context the many tools used with
great success in network/graph theory, and understanding how to combine the information obtained
from the graph sheaf Laplacian computations using G, Kn and Gγ.
These are mathematical aspects of the work. Ecological aspects would include how changes in
