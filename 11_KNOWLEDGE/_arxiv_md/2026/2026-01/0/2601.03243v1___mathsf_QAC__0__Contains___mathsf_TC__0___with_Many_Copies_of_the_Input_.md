Daniel Grier∗
Jackson Morris†

Abstract
QAC0 is the class of constant-depth polynomial-size quantum circuits constructed from ar-
bitrary single-qubit gates and generalized Toffoli gates. It is arguably the smallest natural class
of constant-depth quantum computation which has not been shown useful for computing any
non-trivial Boolean function. Despite this, many attempts to port classical AC0 lower bounds
to QAC0 have failed.
We give one possible explanation of this: QAC0 circuits are significantly more powerful than
their classical counterparts. We show the unconditional separation QAC0 ̸⊂AC0[p] for decision
problems, which also resolves for the first time whether AC0 could be more powerful than QAC0.
Moreover, we prove that QAC0 circuits can compute a wide range of Boolean functions if given
multiple copies of the input: TC0 ⊆QAC0 ◦NC0. Along the way, we introduce an amplitude
amplification technique that makes several approximate constant-depth constructions exact.

Introduction
Constant-depth quantum circuits have long played a central role in our understanding of how
quantum computers can gain an advantage over their classical counterparts. Indeed, even constant-
depth circuits consisting entirely of single- and two-qubit gates can provably outperform classical
circuits at a variety of sampling and searching tasks [BGK18, WKST19, GS20, WP23, KOW24,
GKM+25]. However, as soon as we consider decision problems (where to goal is to compute a
single output bit of a Boolean function), the story changes dramatically. The small light cones of
such constant-depth quantum circuits significantly constrain their behavior, making them no more
powerful than constant-depth classical circuits. This phenomenon manifests as the complexity class
equality QNC0 = NC0.
In other words, to witness the power of constant-depth quantum circuits for computing Boolean
functions, the quantum circuit must have access to large entangling gates that can act on many
qubits at once. One particularly important class of constant-depth polynomial-size quantum circuits
is QAC0, where the circuit can apply arbitrary single-qubit gates as well as generalized Toffoli gates
(i.e., the reversible n-bit AND function). QAC0 has garnered recent attention as a possible viable
model from some near-term quantum hardware [WSM01], and moreover, it has a long history of
study as the natural analog to the famous classical circuit class AC0. While it was known that AC0
has quite limited computational power, it has been much harder to show limitations on the power
of QAC0.
Perhaps the most famous and illustrative example of this discrepancy is witnessed by the parity
function. While there are several techniques for showing that parity is not computable in AC0,
QAC0 has survived a long line of research seeking to prove the same result in the quantum world
[FFG+06, PFGT25, Ros21, NPVY24, ADOY24, Ber11, JTVW25]. Critically, the same techniques
that allow for lower bounds in the classical world, like random restrictions [Ajt83, FSS84, H˚as86]
or Fourier concentration [LMN93], have failed to port over to the quantum world (at least if you
do not restrict the use of ancillas). Of course, one possible explanation of this phenomenon is that
QAC0 are just significantly more powerful than previously assumed.
With this in mind, it is natural to search for QAC0 circuits which might exemplify this power.
Until recently, this approach has received relatively little attention. The first nontrivial constant-
depth QAC circuit1 construction was given by Rosenthal [Ros21] where generalized Toffoli gates of
exponential size are leveraged in constant depth to approximately compute parity and the quan-
tum fanout2 gate. More recently, it was shown that weak pseudorandom unitaries can be imple-
mented with QAC0 circuits [FPVY25]. One ingredient in this construction involves shrinking the
exponential-size circuit of [Ros21] to compute fanout on logarithmically many qubits using a QAC0
circuit of polynomial size. We will also make use of this technique in this paper. Nevertheless, it is
unclear how one might extend this construction to implement fanout on a larger number of qubits.
This lack of large fanout serves as a potential barrier for certain circuit construction techniques.
For example, one can show that large fanout is necessary for classical AC0 circuits to compute even
relatively simple Boolean functions such as the indexing function (see Section B for a proof).
In light of this, it is natural to search for inherently quantum primitives that can be constructed
with generalized Toffoli gates to compute non-trivial Boolean functions. This search is exactly the

Theorem 1 (See also Theorem 24). There exists a language L which can be decided by a QAC0

We note that AC0[p] strictly contains AC0 [Ajt83, FSS84, H˚as86], so Theorem 1 immediately
implies the novel separation QAC0 ̸⊂AC0. In other words, prior to our result, it was conceivable that
AC0 was strictly more powerful than QAC0 for solving decision problems. We refute this possibility,
showing that constant-depth quantum circuits can implement hard-to-compute Boolean functions,
even without large fanout.
It turns out that Theorem 1 follows from what is perhaps an even more surprising aspect of
QAC0 circuits. Namely, we show that providing QAC0 circuits with polynomially many copies of
the input string allows them to simulate arbitrary TC0 computations.3
Theorem 2 (See also Theorem 21). Any TC0-computable function can be decided in QAC0 with
bounded error and polynomially many copies of the input. Equivalently, TC0 ⊆BQAC0 ◦NC0.
While Theorem 1 gives the existence of a single language separating QAC0 circuits from AC0[p]
circuits, Theorem 2 shows that this separation comes from the fact that QAC0 are generically
powerful. As one example, since TC0 circuits can multiply n-bit integers [HAB02], then so can
QAC0 circuits (at least when provided multiple copies of the input). This resolves the question of
whether multiple classical copies enable QAC0 to compute non-trivial Boolean functions, a question
posed by Rosenthal in his thesis [Ros23].

Quantum-Classical Separations.
To obtain Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, we note an important
feature of the W test: one-sided error. Indeed, if EXn/2(x) = 1, then the W test is always correct;
otherwise EXn/2(x) = 0, and it is correct with probability at least 1/n2. This means we can perform
error reduction using the AND function (which notably is in QAC0), instead of using the majority
function (which is not known to be in QAC0).
More formally, let us take the AND of c ≈n2 parallel runs of the W tests. If EXn/2(x) = 1, then
the AND outcome is always 1; otherwise the outcome is 0 with probability 0.99. This is almost a
proof of Theorem 1 as EXn/2 is not in AC0[p] [Raz87, Smo87].
The caveat here is, c parallel runs of the W tests require c disjoint copies of the input (see
Section C for partial progress to bypass this) and it is unclear how to make this many copies of
the input in QAC0. Fortunately, to prove separation results, we do not have to stick to the vanilla
EXn/2 function. We can use a variant. In particular, we define the c-copy version of EXn/2 as

1.2
Future Directions
We show that having polynomially many classical copies of the input enables QAC0 to compute
highly non-trivial Boolean functions beyond AC0[p].
It is reasonable to ask more fine-grained
questions about the actual number of copies needed. Indeed, if this “copy complexity” can be
reduced to polylog(n), then QAC0 = QAC0
wf. Towards this question, we make some partial progress
in Section C. In particular, we show that QAC0 circuits can compute parity with O(n3/2) copies
copies of the input and more generally any symmetric function with O(n2) copies.
Regarding the separation we achieve in Theorem 1, it is natural to ask if the soundness be
reduced from inverse exponential to zero. This would lead to a separation between exact QAC0 and
AC0[p]. Such a class of decision problems has previously been called EQAC0 [GHMP02]. Similarly,
another natural question is whether we can exhibit a total function in QAC0 that is hard against
AC0[p] circuits on average. Note that by the blocky nature of CopyEXn/2, a random input string
is a no instance with high probability; hence this function has high correlation with the constant
zero function.
The techniques introduced in this work do not seem to directly address the question of whether
AC0 ⊂QAC0. One may view Theorem 6 as some partial progress; however, simple AC0 functions

Preliminaries
For every integer n ≥1, we use [n] to denote the set {1, 2, . . . , n}. For every binary string x ∈
{0, 1}n, we use |x| = x1 + · · · + xn to denote its Hamming weight. For binary strings x, y of equal
length, we use x ⊕y to denote the binary string of their bit-wise XOR. We use 1E to denote the
indicator function of event E, i.e., 1E = 1 if E happens and 1E = 0 if otherwise. We use In to denote
the identity matrix on n qubits.
Symmetric Boolean Functions.
We only refer to many-to-one functions when we use the term
“Boolean function”. For a Boolean function f : {0, 1}n →{0, 1}, we say f is symmetric if f(x)
depends only on |x|.
For integer k ≥0, we use EXk to denote the exact threshold function of Hamming weight k,
defined by EXk(x) = 1|x|=k; and use Th≥k to denote the threshold function of Hamming weight k,
defined by Th≥k(x) = 1|x|≥k.
For each integer m ≥2, we define MODm : {0, 1}n →{0, 1} by MODm(x) = 1|x|≡1 (mod m). We
also reserve Parity = MOD2 for the parity function and sometimes use Parityn to highlight the input
length is n.
Classical Circuit Complexity.
In a Boolean circuit, every gate evaluates some Boolean function
on the input wires and forwards the outcome through its output wires. For a Boolean circuit,
• depth is the maximal length from an input bit to output and size is the total number of gates;
• fanin is the maximal number of input wires of each gate, fanout is the maximal number of
output wires of each gate, and gate set is the set of different gates.

We use ¬ to denote the negation gate; and use AND (resp., OR) to denote the AND (resp., OR)
gate/function. Sometimes we use ANDn, ORn to highlight that the function takes n bit input and

We will need the following standard classical circuit classes. Here we give informal description
and refer readers to textbooks [AB09, J+12] for formal definitions.
• NC0 is the set of languages that can be exactly decided by constant-depth constant-fanin
Boolean circuits of gate set {¬, AND, OR}.
• AC0 is the set of languages that can be exactly decided by constant-depth polynomial-size
Boolean circuits of gate set {¬, AND, OR}.
• AC0[m] is the set of languages that can be exactly decided by constant-depth polynomial-size
Boolean circuits of gate set {¬, AND, OR, MODm}. ACC0 is the union of AC0[m] for all m ≥2.
• TC0 is the set of languages that can be exactly decided by constant-depth polynomial-size
Boolean circuits of gate set {¬, (Th≥k)k≥0}.
We emphasize that we focus on circuit classes for decision problems and it is known that NC0 ⊊
AC0 ⊊AC0[p] ⊊TC0 for every constant prime p [Ajt83, FSS84, H˚as86, Raz87, Smo87].

Quantum Circuits.
A quantum circuit C is a product of quantum gates.
• We say C has depth d if C = MdMd−1 · · · M1 where each Mi is a product of gates operating
on disjoint sets of qubits. The size of C is the total number of gates in C and qubits that C
operates on.
• Let S be a subset of unitaries. We say C has gate set S if every gate of C belongs to S.
Let f : {0, 1}n →{0, 1}. We say C decides f with a ancillas and ε error if for every x ∈{0, 1}n,
the last qubit of C |x⟩|0a⟩|0⟩measures, in the computational basis, to |f(x)⟩with probability at
least 1 −ε. In addition, we say
• it has completeness c if for every x ∈f−1(1), the last qubit measures to |1⟩with probability
at least c;
• and has soundness s if for every x ∈f−1(0), the last qubit measures to |1⟩with probability
at most s.
If C decides f with zero error, then we say C exactly decides f and we can assume without loss of
generality C |x⟩|0a⟩|0⟩= |x⟩|0a⟩|f(x)⟩by standard uncomputation, which incurs an insignificant
constant blowup in the depth and size of the circuit.

QAC and QAC0.
We primarily work with QAC circuits and the corresponding quantum circuit
class QAC0. See [NC10, Ber10] for a more comprehensive introduction.
A quantum circuit is a QAC circuit if it only uses single-qubit gates and generalized Toffoli
gates. We distinguish QAC0 as the following finer classes EQAC0 and BQAC0.
• EQAC0 is the set of languages that can be exactly decided by constant-depth polynomial-size
QAC circuits.
• BQAC0 is the set of languages that can be decided with error4 at most 1/3 by constant-depth
polynomial-size QAC circuits.
We emphasize that we allow arbitrary single-qubit gates, instead of a finite number of them.
We allow ancillary qubits in our quantum circuits, the number of which will be upper bounded
by the circuit size. We also remark that the quantum OR gate UOR : |x⟩|b⟩→|x⟩|b ⊕OR(x)⟩is
constant-depth and constant-size in QAC.
