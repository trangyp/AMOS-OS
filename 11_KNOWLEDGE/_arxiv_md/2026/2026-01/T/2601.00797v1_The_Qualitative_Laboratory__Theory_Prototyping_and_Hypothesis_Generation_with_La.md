The Qualitative Laboratory 
Theory Prototyping and Hypothesis Generation  
with Large Language Models 
 
 
Hugues Draelants
  
GIRSEF, UCLouvain 
hugues.draelants@uclouvain.be 
 
Abstract: A central challenge in social science is to generate rich qualitative hypotheses about 
how diverse social groups might interpret new information. This article introduces and 
illustrates a novel methodological approach for this purpose: sociological persona simulation 
using Large Language Models (LLMs), which we frame as a “qualitative laboratory”. We argue 
that for this specific task, persona simulation offers a distinct advantage over established 
methods. By generating naturalistic discourse, it overcomes the lack of discursive depth 
common in vignette surveys, and by operationalizing complex worldviews through natural 
language, it bypasses the formalization bottleneck of rule-based agent-based models (ABMs). 
To demonstrate this potential, we present a protocol where personas derived from a sociological 
theory of climate reception react to policy messages. The simulation produced nuanced and 
counter-intuitive hypotheses—such as a conservative persona’s rejection of a national security 
frame—that challenge theoretical assumptions. We conclude that this method, used as part of a 
“simulation then validation” workflow, represents a superior tool for generating deeply textured 
hypotheses for subsequent empirical testing. 
Keywords: Large Language Models (LLM); Sociological Methods; Simulation; Hypothesis 
Generation; Qualitative Methods; Theory Prototyping; Computational Social Science. 
 
 
 
1. Introduction 
1.1. The Methodological Challenge: Prototyping Social Interpretation and Reaction 
A central challenge in empirical social science is to anticipate how diverse and often deeply 
divided publics might interpret and react to new policies, messages, or information. While rich 
sociological theories offer robust explanations for these divisions, rapidly prototyping their real-
world implications remains a slow and resource-intensive process. Traditional methods, such 
as in-depth ethnography or large-scale surveys, provide essential data but are poorly suited for 
the kind of agile, iterative exploration required in the early stages of research design. This 
creates a significant methodological gap between the formulation of a theory and its empirical 
testing in the field, a gap where researchers need tools to explore scenarios, refine concepts, 
and generate fine-grained hypotheses efficiently. This article seeks to address this general 
methodological problem.

1.2. Climate Communication as an Ideal Case Study 
Nowhere is this methodological challenge more acute than in the field of climate 
communication, making it an ideal case study to illustrate our proposed solution. Despite 
decades of scientific research, a significant “knowledge-action gap” persists (Kollmuss & 
Agyeman, 2002), and political polarization deepens (Dunlap et al., 2016). The failure of one-
size-fits-all, information-deficit models has highlighted the need for a more sociologically-
grounded understanding of public reception. Seminal work has shown that responses to 
scientific information are not a direct result of factual knowledge but are mediated by a complex 
web of social, cultural, and political factors (Moser & Dilling, 2007; Kahan et al., 2012). 
To engage with this diversity of social worlds, sociological theory offers powerful frameworks, 
with Pierre Bourdieu’s concept of habitus being particularly salient. In recent environmental 
sociology, this concept has been adapted into notions like the “environmental habitus” to 
analyze the durable dispositions that structure individuals’ relationship with the environment 
(Kirby, 2017; Kennedy & Givens, 2019; Fritz & Eversberg, 2023). We specify this concept as 
the “climate habitus”: a subsystem of dispositions that individuals acquire through their social 
positioning and lived experiences, which functions as a matrix of perceptions, appreciations, 
and practices structuring how they relate to the climate crisis. 
This theoretical lens explains why the reception of climate information is so deeply conditioned 
by one’s social trajectory. However, the very depth and implicit nature of the habitus creates the 
significant methodological gap identified above. How can we empirically anticipate the 
reactions governed by these ingrained, non-discursive dispositions? Probing the predictive 
power of such a theory is a slow process traditionally reliant on deep ethnography. This gap 
between rich sociological theory and the need for agile prototyping is precisely what makes 
climate communication a perfect terrain for demonstrating the utility of a new methodological 
tool. 
1.3. Situating LLM Simulation in the Methodological Landscape 
The recent advent of advanced Large Language Models (LLMs) presents a compelling new 
avenue for social research. With their ability to generate nuanced, human-like text, LLMs are 
increasingly being explored for their potential to create synthetic data, augment existing 
datasets, and even act as proxies for human subjects in experimental designs (Argyle et al., 
2023; Bail, 2024; Cui & Zhou, 2024; Grossmann et al., 2023; Kozlowski & Evans, 2025). This 
capability offers the tantalizing possibility of a “computational social science laboratory,” 
where theories of human behavior could be rapidly prototyped and tested at scale. 
However, for the specific task of generating qualitative hypotheses, LLMs enter an established 
methodological landscape We select vignette surveys and agent-based modeling (ABM) for 
systematic comparison because, like our own approach, they represent the dominant paradigms 
for simulating social actors’ responses to controlled, hypothetical scenarios. Vignette surveys 
do so by presenting scenarios to human subjects, while ABMs do so by programming scenarios 
for artificial agents. They thus form the most relevant benchmarks against which to evaluate 
the unique contribution of LLM-based persona simulation. A systematic comparison of their 
respective trade-offs is therefore necessary to clarify the unique and, we argue, superior 
contribution of our proposed method for this particular purpose. 
Existing Approaches: Vignettes and Agent-Based Models

For decades, researchers have used survey experiments with vignettes—short, standardized 
descriptions of hypothetical scenarios—to explore judgments and decisions with high causal 
validity and generalizability (Alexander & Becker, 1978; Evans et al., 2015). Their primary 
limitation, however, is a lack of discursive depth: by design, they capture structured, closed-
ended responses, making it difficult to surface the rich, underlying reasoning that is the bedrock 
of qualitative hypothesis generation (Tremblay et al., 2022). 
At the other end of the simulation spectrum, agent-based modeling (ABM) serves as a “virtual 
laboratory” to observe how macro-level phenomena emerge from micro-level agent interactions 
(Jackson et al., 2016; Macal & North, 2010). A key strength of ABM is its theoretical 
agnosticism: the “agent” is a conceptually empty vessel that researchers can program with any 
theory of action, from simple heuristics to complex cognitive architectures, thus freeing them 
from the constraints of simplistic models of agency (Manzo & Matthews, 2014; Manzo, 2020). 
However, this very flexibility reveals a fundamental limitation for simulating culturally-situated 
actors: the formalization bottleneck—that is, the necessity of translating a complex, often 
implicit social phenomenon into a finite set of explicit, programmable rules and parameters. 
This process inherently struggles to capture the nature of a concept like the “climate habitus.” 
Indeed, habitus is not a set of conscious rules to be executed, but a pre-reflexive “feel for the 
game” that is embodied in dispositions, tastes, and postures; it is precisely that which resists 
formalization into a programmable script (Bourdieu, 1990). Thus, even the most sophisticated 
ABM would fail to capture the implicit and generative logic of habitus, demonstrating the limits 
of rule-based simulation for this specific purpose. 
LLM-Based Persona Simulation: A Superior Approach for Qualitative Hypothesis Generation 
This paper introduces and illustrates LLM-based persona simulation as a third approach that 
overcomes these specific limitations. 
• 
Superiority over Vignettes: Unlike vignettes, it is designed to generate rich, open-
ended qualitative discourse, thus offering superior discursive depth. 
• 
Superiority over ABM: Unlike ABMs, it bypasses the formalization bottleneck by 
operationalizing complex personas through natural language prompts. 
This promise, however, is fraught with peril. Critical scholars rightly warn of the significant 
risks of using LLMs: the inherent biases encoded in their training data, their lack of genuine 
consciousness or embodied experience, and the ethical implications of using synthetic subjects 
(Weidinger et al., 2021). Therefore, a naive use of LLMs as “human simulators” risks producing 
methodologically flawed research. The central challenge is to develop a protocol that leverages 
their unique discursive capabilities while remaining critically reflexive about their limitations. 
In response, we argue that the primary utility of LLMs is not empirical validation but qualitative 
hypothesis generation. As such, our methodology directly implements and operationalizes the 
“simulation then validation” strategy for inductive hypothesis generation, as recently proposed 
by Kozlowski and Evans (2025). Building on their foundational framework, we demonstrate 
how this strategy can be applied through a specific, theory-driven protocol—the “qualitative 
laboratory”—to generate the kind of rich, nuanced hypotheses they advocate for. 
1.4. Argument and Roadmap 
The aim of this paper is therefore to introduce, illustrate, and critically assess sociological 
persona simulation as a method for generating qualitative hypotheses. We argue that while 
LLMs cannot replace human subjects or validate social theory, they can serve as a powerful

“qualitative laboratory” when used as part of a reflexive research design. We make a specific 
claim for this method’s superiority over established approaches like vignette surveys and agent-
based models for the particular task of producing discourses with high discursive depth and 
sociological plausibility, thereby overcoming the “formalization bottleneck” inherent in rule-
based simulations. Furthermore, we highlight the method’s practical advantages in terms of 
research agility, dramatically accelerating the exploratory phase of research. 
To demonstrate this, we present an experiment where LLM-agents, instructed with 
sociologically-informed personas, produce rich discursive responses to climate policy 
messages, revealing both expected patterns and counter-intuitive findings that serve as 
powerful, testable hypotheses.

2. Theoretical Framework: A Matrix of Climate Reception 
2.1. From the Knowledge-Action Gap to the Habitus 
To move beyond the limitations of the information-deficit model, our approach is grounded in 
a sociological understanding of belief and action. We posit that climate stance is shaped less by 
scientific literacy than by social position, lived experience, and cultural identity. We draw on 
Pierre Bourdieu’s concept of habitus—the system of embodied dispositions that structures how 
individuals perceive, feel, and act in the world (Bourdieu, 1984). The habitus filters incoming 
information, making certain arguments feel intuitively plausible and others immediately 
suspect. 
This dispositional framework is complemented by Raymond Boudon’s theory of cognitive 
rationality, which suggests that individuals adhere to beliefs for “good reasons” that are 
subjectively rational within their worldview, even if they contradict scientific consensus 
(Boudon, 2009). In a polarized context like climate change, these “good reasons” are often less 
about evaluating evidence and more about affirming a social identity and maintaining allegiance 
to a cultural group (Kahan, 2013). An effective analysis of public reception must therefore 
account for these different, socially structured “worlds” of belief. 
2.2. The Four Quadrants of Climate Reception 
To operationalize this perspective, we developed the Matrix of Climate Reception (Table 1), a 
heuristic tool that maps four ideal-typical “climate habitus”. We present this matrix not as a 
definitive, empirically validated typology of climate opinion, but as a theoretically-informed 
framework designed specifically to guide our simulation experiment. Its purpose here is to 
demonstrate how a sociological theory, whether established or exploratory, can serve as the 
blueprint for persona engineering.

plausible personas. It serves as the blueprint for our experiment, which aims to explore whether 
an LLM, when prompted with these detailed habitus, can generate the kind of rich, nuanced, 
and theoretically-consistent discourses that are useful for qualitative hypothesis generation. 
3. Methodology: Simulating Sociological Personas with LLMs 
3.1. An Experimental Protocol for Theory Prototyping 
To demonstrate the potential of LLM-based persona simulation as a tool for hypothesis 
generation, we designed a qualitative experiment. Our goal is to explore whether LLM-


## Page 6


6 
 
prompted personas can generate internally consistent, theoretically coherent, and nuanced 
discourses. The experiment aims to answer a core methodological question: Can this approach 
move beyond mere stereotype reproduction to become a useful instrument for theory 
prototyping? 
The experiment was conducted in June and July 2025 using Google’s Gemini 1.5 Flash model, 
accessed via its API within a Google Colab environment. This model was chosen for its large 
context window, its strong instruction-following capabilities, and its balance between 
performance and computational efficiency, which is well-suited for automated, iterative 
workflows. The protocol involved systematically exposing each of the four persona-agents to 
four distinct messages concerning a single, concrete climate policy: a proposed ban on the sale 
of new gasoline-powered cars by 2035. 
Our approach follows the principles for rigorous research design with simulated subjects 
outlined by Kozlowski & Evans (2025). Specifically, we use detailed prompting to situate the 
agents and have designed our experiment to actively address several of the known weaknesses 
of LLMs, such as bias and uniformity. 
3.2. Persona Engineering and Stimulus Design 
The validity of the simulation hinges on the quality of the “persona engineering”. For each 
quadrant of our matrix, we crafted a detailed prompt describing the persona’s background, 
occupation, geographical location, lived experiences, values, and trusted sources of 
information. These personas, all situated within the United States to control for national context, 
were designed to be sociologically grounded archetypes (see Appendix A for full prompts). 
• 
Persona 1 (Embodied Evidence): “Maria”, a third-generation almond farmer in 
California’s Central Valley. 
• 
Persona 2 (Mediated Trust): “David”, a moderate high school history teacher in a 
Denver suburb. 
• 
Persona 3 (Motivated Disavowal): “Frank”, a conservative, self-employed craftsman 
in rural Texas. 
• 
Persona 4 (Default Skepticism): “Kevin”, a cynical, working-class factory worker in 
Ohio’s Rust Belt. 
These archetypes synthesize existing sociological research on demographics and environmental 
attitudes. 
For example, the persona of David (Mediated Trust) as a college-educated, politically moderate 
suburbanite reflects extensive findings on the correlation between higher education, 
urban/suburban residence, and trust in scientific institutions and media sources (Achterberg et 
al., 2017). Similarly, the profile of Frank (Motivated Disavowal) as a conservative, self-
employed craftsman in rural Texas is grounded in research identifying political identity 
(conservatism), regional culture, and economic ties to extractive industries as strong predictors 
of climate skepticism (Knight, 2018; Krause, 2023). Each persona was thus constructed as a 
plausible embodiment of the core logics of its respective quadrant in the Matrix of Reception. 
The four communication stimuli (frames) were designed to test the reception of different 
argumentative logics. They were all based on a single, concrete policy proposal, closely 
modeled on real-world regulations debated and implemented in jurisdictions like California and

the European Union: a proposed ban on the sale of new gasoline-powered cars by 2035. This 
specific policy was chosen for its high public salience and its capacity to elicit strong, socially

1. Scientific Frame: Emphasized IPCC recommendations and CO2 targets.

3.3. The Importance of Adapted Questioning: A Methodological Pre-Test 
A crucial methodological challenge when using LLMs for qualitative simulation is the risk of 
imposing a “scholastic fallacy” (Bourdieu, 2000), whereby the researcher’s own academic 
mode of questioning produces artificial responses. A standardized, structured questioning 
format might be familiar to the David persona but would be sociologically implausible for 
Kevin or Frank. 
This implausibility stems from the concept of linguistic capital (Bourdieu, 1984) and 
communication styles associated with different social classes and educational backgrounds. The 
formal, analytical language of a standardized protocol (“What are the strengths and weaknesses 
of this message?”) aligns with the habitus of an academic or a highly-educated professional 
(David), but clashes with the pragmatic, direct, and often more narrative communication styles 
documented in many working-class communities since the classical work of Bernstein (1960). 
Therefore, our adapted questioning aimed to achieve ecological validity by tailoring the 
prompt’s register to match the persona’s described social position. For Kevin, the prompt was 
reformulated into a more conversational and grounded style (“Honestly, what’s your take? Does 
this sound credible to a guy like you?”) to elicit a more authentic-sounding discourse, rather 
than forcing the persona into an unnatural communicative mode. As the pre-test (Table 2) 
demonstrates, this adaptation was crucial for moving beyond a superficial analytical response 
to reveal the underlying habitus in action. 
For the standardized protocol, each persona was asked to provide a “single, structured response” 
to three analytical questions (“Is it credible?”, “Do you support it?”, “What are its strengths and 
weaknesses?”). For the adapted protocol, the questioning was reformulated into a more 
conversational and culturally appropriate style for each specific persona. 
The adapted questioning yielded a discourse that was qualitatively richer, more emotionally 
resonant, and far more sociologically plausible (see Table 2 for a comparison). While the 
standardized prompt produced a stilted, analytical self-commentary (“It’s a technocrat’s 
discourse”), the adapted prompt generated an authentic-sounding monologue expressing a core 
logic of class-based injustice (“a story of rich people wanting the poor to make all the effort”). 
The latter reveals the habitus in action, whereas the former merely produces a description of it.

3.4. A Note on the Use of AI in the Research Process 
In the spirit of full methodological transparency, it is important to state that the research and 
writing of this article were conducted in collaboration with a large language model, based on 
Google’s architecture, respectively Gemini 1.5 Flash and 1.5 Pro. The AI served as a dialogic 
partner at every stage. This involved brainstorming the conceptual framework, structuring the 
argument, drafting and iteratively refining the text based on the author’s precise instructions 
and critiques, and developing the experimental protocol itself through a process of simulated 
peer review. For instance, it was prompted to act as a “sceptical peer reviewer” to challenge the 
initial experimental design and to stress-test our own arguments. This reflexive use of an LLM 
as a tool for critical thinking is, in itself, an illustration of the potential we explore in this paper. 
As with any research tool, the author directed the entire process and bears full intellectual 
responsibility for the final manuscript, its claims, and its limitations.

hypotheses. We demonstrate how each persona-agent maintained a consistent worldview, 
applying a distinct ‘social logic’ to interpret policy messages. We focus particularly on moments 
of nuance and counter-intuitive findings, as these highlight the primary utility of the method:

4.1. Experiment 1: Reception of a Material Policy (Vehicle Ban) 
Across all four communication frames, each persona-agent maintained a consistent and 
sociologically plausible worldview. The specific policy proposal served less as a piece of 
information to be evaluated objectively, and more as a pretext for each persona to articulate 
their core social logic. To provide a concrete illustration of the rich data analyzed throughout 
this section, Appendix C presents the full discursive outputs for all four personas in response to 
a single communication frame, accompanied by analytical notes that connect the raw text to our 
core methodological arguments. 
• 
Maria (Embodied Evidence) consistently filtered every proposal through the lens of 
practical viability and rural justice. Her discourse was grounded in material realities: 
water scarcity, the price of equipment, and the physical demands of farming. Her 
skepticism was never directed at the climate science itself, but at the “fancy city” 
solutions that ignored her embodied, local knowledge. As she stated in response to the 
economic frame, “It sounds like they’re promising the moon, but all I see are more 
headaches... the devil’s in the details. And the details... well, those are what matter out 
here.”  
This discourse generates a clear hypothesis for fieldwork: for agricultural communities 
directly impacted by climate change, communication framing should focus less on 
validating the science (which is already an accepted, embodied reality) and more on the 
practical viability, local control, and perceived fairness of proposed solutions. The key 
tension is not belief vs. denial, but practicality vs. abstraction. 
• 
David (Mediated Trust) consistently operated as a deliberative rationalist. His 
responses were structured, analytical, and always sought to balance competing 
concerns. He accepted the scientific premise of each argument but immediately moved 
to consider its “complexities” and the need for a “just transition”. His discourse was that 
of the ideal citizen in a deliberative democracy, trusting in experts but demanding a 
nuanced and equitable implementation. In response to the national security frame, he 
noted, “It’s not just about national security, it’s about economic consequences, 
environmental sustainability, and social equity.” 
The discourse from David generates a key hypothesis regarding the stability of 
“mediated trust”: for this audience, support for climate policies is contingent on the 
perceived procedural fairness and intellectual honesty of the implementation process. 
The hypothesis is that communication which appears overly simplistic, dismissive of 
trade-offs, or politically partisan would be the most likely to alienate this group, not 
because they question science, but because it would violate their core belief in a rational, 
deliberative, and equitable process. Their potential point de rupture is not 
epistemological, but procedural. 
• 
Frank (Motivated Disavowal) systematically reinterpreted every message as an attack 
on his core values of liberty, individualism, and cultural identity. He consistently 
identified the source of the message (the IPCC, “Washington D.C.”, “city folks”) as 
illegitimate and hostile. The scientific or economic content was secondary to the

perceived political threat. His response to the scientific frame was emblematic: “This 
isn’t a solution, it’s an attack on my way of life... It’s insulting to see us treated like 
carbon criminals just for driving an old-fashioned truck.” 
This simulation’s most significant output is a counter-intuitive and highly specific 
hypothesis: for personas whose skepticism is rooted in anti-statism and a defense of 
individual liberty, patriotic frames (e.g., national security) may not only be ineffective 
but counter-productive, as they can be perceived as another form of government 
overreach. This suggests a hierarchy of values where anti-statism can trump patriotism, 
a crucial hypothesis for communicators seeking to engage with conservative audiences. 
• 
Kevin (Default Skepticism) filtered every proposal through a lens of class-based 
resentment and material precarity. His core logic was to ask: “Who pays, and who 
benefits?”. He consistently expressed a deep cynicism towards “fancy talk” from elites, 
translating abstract promises into their likely concrete costs for the working class. His 
reaction to the social justice frame was particularly telling: “The good point? Maybe 
cleaner air. But the bad points? The cost, the uncertainty... it’s just a lot of hooey to a 
guy like me, working his tail off just to get by. Let them fancy-pants folks in their fancy 
cars worry about their fancy problems. I gotta worry about making rent.” 
Kevin’s discourse leads to a crucial hypothesis about ‘default skepticism’: this form of 
distrust is not primarily ideological (as with Frank), but pragmatic and rooted in a deep-
seated sense of class injustice. The hypothesis is that abstract promises, whether about 
jobs (“economic” frame) or clean air (“social justice” frame), are immediately translated 
into concrete, personal costs (gas prices, job insecurity). Therefore, communication will 
likely fail unless it addresses this cost-benefit calculus at a hyper-local and personal 
level. The entry point for engagement is not abstract values, but a tangible 
demonstration of “what’s in it for me and my community,” delivered by trusted, non-
elite sources. 
Taken together, these four discourses illustrate the specific superiority of persona simulation for 
qualitative hypothesis generation. A vignette-based survey, for instance, could have captured 
Maria’s opposition or David’s conditional support on a quantitative scale, but it would have 
entirely missed the texture of their reasoning—the weariness with “city solutions” for Maria, 
the demand for procedural fairness from David. It would have failed to capture the very affect 
that is itself crucial qualitative data: the defiant localism of Frank’s discourse or the eye-rolling 
cynicism of Kevin’s. Similarly, an agent-based model would face an insurmountable 
formalization bottleneck. How could one program Maria’s analogy between water and energy 
sovereignty, or Kevin’s immediate translation of national policy into its impact on “my 
neighbor, Earl, the mechanic”? Our method's unique strength is its capacity to surface this deep 
qualitative texture and to generate “generative anomalies”—findings that challenge theoretical 
assumptions and produce genuinely new, testable hypotheses that might otherwise have been 
missed, like Frank’s counter-intuitive reaction.

4.2. Testing for Robustness or High-Tech Pastiche? A Second Experiment. 
A key criticism of LLM-based simulation is that it may simply be producing a “high-tech 
pastiche”—repeating keywords from the prompt without a deeper, adaptable logic. To test this, 
we conducted a second experiment, exposing the same four agents to a different, more culturally 
charged topic: a proposal for a mandatory high school course on “Climate Justice and 
Environmental Inequalities”. The goal was to observe whether the personas’ core logics would 
remain stable and adapt plausibly to a different policy domain, moving from a material issue 
(cars) to a cultural one (school curriculum). This serves as a crucial test of the method’s 
robustness: can it simulate a coherent worldview, or just a superficial set of opinions on a single 
topic? 
The results confirmed this hypothesis with remarkable clarity. Each persona reinterpreted the 
educational proposal through its distinct habitus:

• 
David (Mediated Trust) approached it as a pedagogical professional, weighing 
the benefits of critical thinking against the risks of political indoctrination. His 
logic was deliberative and nuanced.

The consistency of these core logics across two very different policy domains provides strong 
evidence for the methodological robustness of the simulation. It demonstrates that the LLM-
agent is not merely repeating keywords associated with its initial prompt, but is capable of 
applying a coherent, underlying worldview to new contexts in a sociologically plausible 
manner. This suggests that the method can indeed move beyond a “high-tech pastiche” and 
simulate an adaptable, core habitus. 
4.3. Synthesis: From Simulation to Testable Hypotheses 
Taken together, the results from both experiments demonstrate the utility of LLM-based persona 
simulation as a hypothesis-generation tool. Across two distinct policy domains, the simulated 
discourses aligned well with the predicted logics of each quadrant of our theoretical matrix, not 
as a validation of the matrix itself, but as a demonstration of the method’s capacity to produce 
theoretically-coherent outputs when guided by a sociologically-informed framework. 
More importantly, the experiments did more than just reproduce expected patterns; they 
enriched them with crucial nuances and generated specific, counter-intuitive hypotheses. The 
findings—such as Frank’s (Q3) rejection of a security frame, David’s (Q2) focus on procedural 
fairness, or Kevin’s (Q4) pragmatic translation of all abstract frames into personal cost—are 
not failures of the simulation but its most valuable results. They reveal the potential hierarchies 
of values within each habitus (e.g., anti-statism over patriotism for Frank; class-based 
pragmatism over abstract justice for Kevin). 
This demonstrates the primary utility of this simulation method: not merely to confirm what is 
already known, but to serve as a heuristic device for generating more fine-grained, complex, 
and testable hypotheses. The simulated discourses provide a rich, qualitative preview of the 
argumentative logics, emotional responses, and symbolic friction points likely to emerge in 
real-world public debate, thus offering a powerful and cost-effective starting point for 
subsequent empirical fieldwork with human subjects. 
5. Discussion 
Our experimental results illustrate the potential of LLM-based persona simulation as a novel 
tool for social science research, specifically in the domain of qualitative hypothesis generation. 
While the simulation is not a form of empirical validation, it offers a powerful ‘qualitative 
laboratory’ to prototype theories, explore communicative dynamics, and refine our 
understanding of complex social worlds. In this section, we unpack the broader implications of


## Page 13


13 
 
our findings. We focus first on the methodological contribution of this approach, second on its 
utility for theory prototyping and refinement, and finally, on the necessary limitations and 
ethical considerations that must accompany its use. 
5.1. Methodological Implications: The LLM as a “Qualitative Laboratory” 
The primary contribution of this study is methodological. Our work demonstrates how LLMs, 
when guided by rigorous sociological theory and reflexive questioning techniques, can serve as 
a qualitative laboratory. This approach does not seek to replace traditional fieldwork but to 
complement it, offering a new, cost-effective step in the research workflow for the rapid 
prototyping of theories and the generation of fine-grained hypotheses. Our work can be seen as 
a concrete application of the “simulation then validation” strategy, a workflow designed for 
inductive hypothesis generation as outlined by Kozlowski & Evans (2025). We provide a proof-
of-concept for how this workflow can be implemented in practice, moving from their 
methodological framework to an applied protocol. 
Our experiment highlights three key methodological insights. First, the success of the 
simulation is entirely dependent on the quality of the sociological engineering of the prompts. 
A rich, theoretically-informed persona description is the prerequisite for generating plausible 
data. Second, the form of the interaction matters profoundly. As our pre-test demonstrated, a 
standardized, scholastic questioning protocol elicits artificial responses, confirming a core tenet 
of qualitative inquiry: the research instrument is not neutral. Third, and most crucially, this 
method’s value is realized not when it confirms our biases, but when it challenges them. The 
counter-intuitive findings, like Frank’s rejection of the security frame, are the method’s most 
valuable outputs. They act as “generative anomalies” that force researchers to refine their 
theories and develop more nuanced hypotheses for subsequent testing with human subjects. 
A crucial question for any new method concerns its systematic evaluation against established 
standards. While a quantitative benchmark of our qualitative laboratory against tools designed 
for quantitative analysis (like surveys) would be a category error, our experiments provide a 
robust qualitative demonstration of superiority for the specific task of hypothesis generation. 
We propose that this superiority can be assessed along four key dimensions: 
1. Discursive Depth: The ability to capture nuanced, emotional, and contradictory 
language, as seen in the discourses of each persona. 
2. Revelation of Value Hierarchies: The capacity to uncover counter-intuitive logics and 
“generative anomalies,” like anti-statism trumping patriotism for Frank, which 
challenge existing theoretical assumptions. 
3. Generative Potential: The efficiency with which the method produces multiple, 
specific, and testable avenues for future fieldwork from a single simulation. 
4. Research Agility: The method’s ability to dramatically reduce the time, cost, and access 
barriers associated with the exploratory phase of a project, enabling a level of 
methodological experimentation impossible with traditional qualitative approaches. 
While the first two dimensions highlight the method’s superior qualitative output, the latter two 
underscore its transformative potential for the research process itself. On these dimensions, 
crucial for the early, exploratory stages of research, persona simulation demonstrates a clear 
advantage over the flatter data produced by vignettes and the rigid formalization required by 
ABMs.

5.2. Theoretical Implications: Prototyping and Refining Theory 
While our experiment was not designed to validate the Matrix of Climate Reception, it did serve 
as a powerful prototyping exercise. The simulation provided a “test bed” to observe how the 
theoretical logics of the matrix would play out in discursive interactions. The overall coherence 
of the personas’ responses suggests that the matrix is a generatively useful heuristic for 
structuring thinking about the polarized climate opinion landscape. Future research could 
further explore the versatility of the qualitative laboratory by applying it to different theoretical 
frameworks. For instance, engineering personas based on alternative theories, such as moral 
foundations theory or cultural cognition, would be a valuable next step to test whether the 
method is robust across different theoretical inputs and to generate a wider range of hypotheses. 
More importantly, the simulation did more than just operationalize the theory; it helped enrich 
and refine it. The nuances in the responses—such as Frank’s (Q3) visceral rejection of the 
security frame or Kevin’s (Q4) ambivalent reaction to the justice frame—highlight the 
hierarchical nature of values within each habitus. They suggest that deep-seated logics (e.g., 
anti-statism for Frank, class-based pragmatism for Kevin) can override more surface-level 
alignments. This insight allows us to move beyond a static classification of public opinion 
towards a more dynamic understanding of how different social groups might process new 
information. The simulation thus functions as a tool for theory refinement, pointing to areas 
where the initial framework needs more nuance. 
5.3. Limitations and Ethical Considerations 
It is crucial to acknowledge the significant limitations of this method. First and foremost, a 
simulation is not reality. The LLM-agent has no body, no lived experience, and no genuine 
consciousness. Its responses are sophisticated statistical patterns, not authentic human 
expressions. Therefore, our use of a concept like “habitus” must be understood in its 
methodological context: we are not simulating the embodied habitus itself but rather prompting 
the LLM to generate a discourse consistent with the theorized worldview of such a habitus. This 
is a crucial distinction between simulating a social process and simulating its linguistic 
representation. 
Second, LLMs are known to contain biases inherited from their vast, and largely Western and 
English-language, training data (Weidinger et al., 2021). While we attempted to create diverse 
personas, they are inevitably articulated through the “lens” of the model. Simulating the 
worldview of deeply marginalized or non-Western subjects would be fraught with even greater 
challenges and ethical risks. 
Finally, the use of synthetic data raises new ethical questions. While our experiment uses 
archetypes and does not impersonate real individuals, the potential for misuse of this technology 
to create sophisticated “fake” subjects for malicious purposes (e.g., in astroturfing campaigns) 
is a serious concern that the research community must address. Therefore, the results of LLM 
simulations should always be treated as plausible hypotheses to be tested against real-world 
empirical data, not as empirical evidence in and of themselves. They are a powerful tool for 
theory-building, not a substitute for engaging with human beings. Thus, this simulation with 
four archetypes does not allow for any generalization to the broader population. Its purpose is 
demonstrative and heuristic. These synthetic discourses should be treated as highly plausible 
hypotheses, which now require confrontation with large-scale survey data and in-depth


## Page 15


15 
 
ethnographic fieldwork to be fully validated. Our method is intended as a new first step in the 
research process, not as a replacement for its subsequent stages. 
6. Conclusion 
This article set out to explore a promising, yet perilous, new frontier in social science research: 
the use of Large Language Models to simulate human subjects. We have introduced and 
demonstrated a method of sociological persona simulation, arguing that when guided by a 
robust theoretical framework and a reflexive, qualitative approach, LLMs can serve as a 
powerful “qualitative laboratory” for theory prototyping. Our experiment, centered on a Matrix 
of Climate Reception, served as a proof-of-concept for this methodology, demonstrating its 
superiority for generating fine-grained hypotheses compared to established methods and 
yielding rich and nuanced discourses. 
The primary takeaway is twofold. Methodologically, we argue that the future of LLM use in 
qualitative social science lies not in a naive quest for “human surrogates,” but in their thoughtful 
application as interactive tools to refine theory, generate fine-grained hypotheses, and stress-
test our understanding of complex social dynamics. The key is to import the critical reflexivity 
of qualitative fieldwork into the design of our computational protocols. Thematically, the 
hypotheses generated through this very process reaffirm a core sociological insight: that the 
reception of science is never a simple act of cognition. It is a deeply social and political process, 
shaped by habitus, identity, and the perceived injustices of a stratified world. 
Ultimately, this work is an invitation. It encourages social scientists to engage critically and 
creatively with the new tools available to us, not as a replacement for our established methods, 
but as a complement to them. It also invites communicators and educators to recognize that 
bridging the divides in our polarized societies requires more than just better data or cleverer 
messages. It requires an in-depth, empathetic understanding of the diverse social realities 
through which citizens interpret the world. By using simulation to improve our understanding, 
we may find more effective ways to navigate the complex challenges ahead.
