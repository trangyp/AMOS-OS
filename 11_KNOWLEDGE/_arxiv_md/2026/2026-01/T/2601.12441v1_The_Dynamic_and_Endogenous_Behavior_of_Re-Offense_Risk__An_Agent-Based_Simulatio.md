The Dynamic and Endogenous Behavior of Re-Offense Risk: An Agent-Based
Simulation Study of Treatment Allocation in Incarceration Diversion Programs
CHUWEN ZHANG, University of Chicago Booth School of Business, USA
PENGYI SHI, Purdue University, USA
AMY R. WARD, University of Chicago Booth School of Business, USA
Incarceration-diversion treatment programs aim to improve societal reintegration and reduce recidivism, but limited capacity forces
policymakers to make prioritization decisions that often rely on risk assessment tools. While predictive, these tools typically treat risk
as a static, individual attribute, which overlooks how risk evolves over time and how treatment decisions shape outcomes through
social interactions. In this paper, we develop a new framework that models reoffending risk as a human–system interaction, linking
individual behavior with system-level dynamics and endogenous community feedback. Using an agent-based simulation calibrated
to U.S. probation data, we evaluate treatment allocation policies under different capacity constraints and incarceration settings.
Our results show that no single prioritization policy dominates. Instead, policy effectiveness depends on temporal windows and
system parameters: prioritizing low-risk individuals performs better when long-term trajectories matter, while prioritizing high-risk
individuals becomes more effective in the short term or when incarceration leads to shorter monitoring periods. These findings
highlight the need to evaluate risk-based decision systems as sociotechnical systems with long-term accountability, rather than as
isolated predictive tools.

Introduction
Recidivism, when individuals reoffend after release from incarceration, is a major contributor to prison and jail
overcrowding and remains a central challenge for the U.S. criminal justice system [15, 17, 34]. Incarceration diversion
programs have been advocated as an effective way to reduce recidivism by supporting individuals’ social reintegration.
As an alternative to incarceration, probation-eligible individuals may receive treatment programs such as substance
abuse counseling, mental health services, and employment assistance [2, 3, 28]. However, because probationers remain
in the community under supervision, reoffending continues to pose a nontrivial threat to public safety; roughly 40% are
rearrested during the probationary period [33].
A central challenge for managing incarceration diversion programs is how to allocate the limited treatment capacity
effectively while accounting for the risk of recidivism for individuals on the program. Policymakers must decide whom
to prioritize for treatment under resource constraints. A prevailing approach is to use data-driven risk assessment
tools that estimate individuals’ likelihood of reoffending [5, 32]. For example, Virginia adopted a 71-point risk scale
under which individuals scoring below a threshold were recommended for diversion programs, while those above were
steered toward incarceration [5].
However, these existing risk-based approaches overlook two critical complications. First, they treat risk as static,
even though individual trajectories evolve over time with age and prior criminal history [7, 25]. Second, reoffending
behavior is endogenous: individuals’ actions are influenced by the prevalence of offending in their communities through
social interactions [26, 30, 31]. When high-risk individuals are systematically denied treatment, they return to the

1.1
Contribution
This paper takes a first step toward addressing this question and makes the following contributions.
(1) Modeling. We develop a dynamic framework in which an individual’s reoffending risk depends not only on personal
characteristics and treatment status, but also on the behavior of others and evolves over time. This offers a novel
lens on risk-based decision-making in incarceration settings from a human–system interaction perspective, moving
beyond traditional models that treat individuals independently. In particular, risk is modeled as a dynamic, socially
shaped quantity, which allows our framework to capture key “social learning” effects where treatment decisions for
one person can influence future risks across the population.
(2) Method. We develop an agent-based simulation platform calibrated to real probation data that models full system
evolution, including reoffending, reentry, and incarceration. This provides a testbed for evaluation of treatment
policies at the system level under an endogenous feedback loop and capacity constraints.
(3) Temporal accountability and regime-dependent tradeoffs. Via extensive numerical experiments, we show
that policy performance depends not only on immediate outcomes but also on long-term system dynamics. We
identify a two-regime structure in which prioritizing low-risk individuals performs better in the long term under low
incarceration rates and long off-probation exposure, while prioritizing high-risk individuals becomes more effective
in the short term or when incarceration leads to shorter monitoring periods. We also illustrate the critical role of
treatment effect and how system parameters like arrival rates and capacity interact with policy performance. These
insights support more transparent and accountable policy making.
1.2
Literature Review
The criminal justice literature has identified a range of individual characteristics as strong predictors of recidivism
risk, including criminal history, demographic attributes, and family circumstances [13, 18]. These predictors support
risk-assessment tools widely used in sentencing and treatment allocation decisions [5, 32]. A growing body of work has
examined the fairness and limitations of these tools [4, 6, 9, 16], as well as how prediction errors affect downstream
operational decisions [35]. Furthermore, research on incarceration-diversion treatment programs suggests that inter-
ventions should be tailored to individuals’ risk levels and needs [2, 3]. When well implemented, such programs can
reduce recidivism by 20–30% [22, 23]. However, this literature largely focuses on individual-level treatment effects and
does not consider how treatment allocation decisions interact with system dynamics over time.
A separate stream of research highlights the role of community context in shaping individual behavior [30, 31]. These
effects arise from social interactions [20, 21, 26], through which individuals influence one another via mechanisms
such as social learning and norm formation [1, 11]. Our work builds on this literature by embedding social interactions
within a system-level model that incorporates dynamic feedback effects.

3
The Agent-Based Simulation System
Building on the individual-level hazard model and the system dynamics described in Section 2, we now employ agent-
based simulation for the probation system that serves as the testbed for treatment policy evaluation. The agent-based
framework provides a natural way to capture the rich interactions between individuals, the treatment status, and the
community environment. By simulating each person’s trajectory (from entering probation to reoffense, off-probation,
and feedback into 𝜇), this agent-based model allows us to evaluate how individual-level interventions propagate through
the population and reshape systemwide outcomes. We specify the necessary input in Section 3.1 and then the simulation
framework in Section 3.2.

Treatment Policies and Performance Metrics
We now introduce several candidate treatment policies 𝜋that govern enrollment decisions:
(1) Null policy. No treatment is assigned to any individual; this serves as a baseline.
(2) Low-risk policy. Treatment capacity is allocated to individuals with the lowest estimated risk until capacity is
exhausted. This policy mirrors common risk-assessment practices, where risk scores are used to prioritize individuals
deemed less likely to reoffend.

(3) High-risk policy. Treatment capacity is instead allocated to individuals with the highest estimated risk, in contrast

(4) Age-first–low-risk policy. Individuals are prioritized first by age and then by the lowest risk score ℎ𝑖. This policy

number of offenses committed per person per episode, capturing the primary objective of reducing recidivism and
community harm; (ii) incarceration – the total number of individuals incarcerated per person per episode, which can be
interpreted as a system failure. Lower values of both metrics are desirable, although trade-offs may arise depending on
system parameters and policy choices. In the following case study, we apply the agent-based simulation framework to
evaluate how these policies perform under various parameter regimes.

Conclusion
This paper develops a dynamic framework for evaluating treatment allocation decisions in the incarceration-diversion
setting. By linking an individual-level hazard model with system-level dynamics, we frame treatment allocation as a
human–system interaction problem rather than a static, individual decision. Using an agent-based simulation calibrated
to U.S. probation data, we show that simple risk thresholds are insufficient: policy performance depends critically
on individual- and community-level context as well as long-term system dynamics. Our numerical results reveal a
regime-dependent structure in which prioritizing low-risk individuals performs better when long-term trajectories
matter, while prioritizing high-risk individuals becomes more effective when monitoring periods are short. These
findings highlight the importance of temporal accountability: evaluating decision policies by how they shape future
system states, not just immediate outcomes. Future work could extend this framework to explicitly study fairness and
disparate impacts, incorporate richer behavioral responses, and explore learning-based approaches for dynamically
adjusting treatment allocation as system conditions evolve.
