Abstract—The United States labor market has entered a period
marked by an unusual coexistence of high job vacancy rates
and prolonged unemployment duration. While classical labor
market theory attributes such conditions to skills mismatch
or geographic immobility, recent evidence suggests a deeper
structural inefficiency in the worker–firm matching process. This
paper argues that a significant portion of contemporary frictional
unemployment is artificially induced by automated recruitment
systems that rely on rigid, deterministic screening mechanisms.
We examine how legacy Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS)
transform hiring into a high-precision classification problem, prior-
itizing the avoidance of unsuitable candidates while systematically
rejecting qualified workers. This design choice leads to elevated
false negative rates, driven primarily by semantic mismatches
between how candidate competencies are expressed and how job
requirements are algorithmically interpreted. Drawing on labor
economics, information asymmetry theory, and recent work on
algorithmic hiring, we formalize this phenomenon as Artificial
Frictional Unemployment.
To evaluate this claim, we compare traditional keyword-
based screening with semantic matching approaches using high-
dimensional vector representations of resumes and job descrip-
tions. Using controlled simulations, we demonstrate that semantic
alignment substantially improves recall and overall matching
efficiency without compromising precision. Building on these
findings, we propose JobOS, a candidate-side workforce operating
architecture designed to standardize, verify, and semantically
translate human capital signals while remaining interoperable
with existing hiring infrastructure.
By reframing automated recruitment as a component of
national labor market infrastructure, this work highlights the
economic costs of outdated screening systems and the potential
gains from semantic competency mapping. Improving matching
efficiency at scale has implications for workforce participation,
productivity, and the effective utilization of human capital in
modern labor markets.

I. INTRODUCTION
In recent years, the United States labor market has displayed
an increasingly puzzling pattern. Employers across many
industries report persistent difficulty filling open positions,
even as a substantial number of workers remain unemployed or
underemployed for extended periods. Under conventional labor
market theory, such conditions should be short-lived. When job
vacancies are abundant, matching between workers and firms
is expected to occur quickly. Yet current evidence suggests
that this mechanism is no longer operating as efficiently as
expected.
A useful framework for understanding this mismatch is
the Beveridge Curve, which captures the inverse relationship
between unemployment and job vacancies. Movements along
the curve are typically associated with business cycles, while
outward shifts are interpreted as signs of declining matching
efficiency. Since the early 2020s, the U.S. labor market has
exhibited a noticeable outward shift, indicating that vacancies
and unemployment are coexisting at levels that standard models
struggle to explain. This pattern suggests that the issue may
lie not in labor supply or demand alone, but in the process by
which workers and jobs are paired.
Traditional explanations for such inefficiencies emphasize
factors such as skills mismatch, geographic immobility, de-
mographic change, or wage rigidity. These explanations are
well-supported and remain relevant. However, they do not
fully account for a growing and widely reported phenomenon:
candidates who appear to meet stated job requirements are
repeatedly rejected at the earliest stages of hiring. In many
cases, these rejections occur before any human review takes
place. This raises the possibility that the matching process
itself, rather than worker capability is contributing to observed
labor market friction.
This paper advances the argument that a meaningful share of
contemporary frictional unemployment is artificial, in the sense
that it is generated by the design and operation of automated
recruitment systems. Over the past two decades, Applicant
Tracking Systems (ATS) have become a central component of
hiring infrastructure, particularly in large organizations. These
systems were introduced to manage scale, reduce administrative
burden, and limit hiring risk. In doing so, they have reshaped
hiring into a largely automated screening process, where
candidates are classified and filtered long before any contextual
judgment is applied.
Most legacy ATS platforms rely on deterministic screening
logic, including exact keyword matching, rigid rule sets, and

predefined thresholds. From an engineering standpoint, these
systems are optimized to minimize false positives that is to
avoid advancing candidates who may later prove unsuitable.
While this objective is understandable, it introduces a structural
bias toward over-rejection. In practice, the cost of rejecting a
qualified candidate is treated as negligible relative to the cost
of advancing an unqualified one. As a result, false negatives
become both common and invisible.
The economic implications of this design choice are sig-
nificant. When qualified candidates are filtered out due to
differences in terminology, non-linear career paths, or gaps in
employment, productive labor remains idle. These individuals
are not mismatched in the classical sense; rather, they are
misinterpreted. Over time, repeated exclusion contributes to
longer job searches, reduced labor mobility, and inefficient
utilization of human capital. At scale, such outcomes manifest
as persistent labor market slack even in periods of high demand.
This phenomenon can also be understood through the
lens of information economics. Hiring is characterized by
severe information asymmetry: employers cannot easily verify
candidate claims prior to interviews, and candidates rely on
static resumes to communicate complex, context-dependent
skills. In response to this uncertainty, employers increasingly
depend on automated filters to manage risk. Ironically, this
reliance amplifies the very asymmetry it is intended to address.
As screening becomes more rigid, resumes lose credibility as
signals, prompting even stricter filtering in a self-reinforcing
cycle.
At the core of this problem is a semantic gap between
how human skills are expressed and how automated systems
interpret them. Skills and experience are rarely reducible
to single tokens or fixed phrases. Equivalent competencies
are often described using different language depending on
industry norms, educational background, or organizational
culture. Deterministic screening systems, however, treat such
variation as absence rather than equivalence. As a result,
candidates with substantively similar qualifications may receive
vastly different outcomes based solely on how their experience
is phrased.
This paper argues that addressing this gap requires a shift
in how labor market infrastructure is conceptualized. Rather
than treating hiring as a problem of filtering, we frame it
as a problem of translation. Building on this perspective,
we propose JobOS, a candidate-side workforce operating
architecture designed to standardize, verify, and semantically
align candidate competencies with employer intent. JobOS is
not a job board, nor is it designed to bypass existing hiring
systems. Instead, it functions as an intermediary computational
layer that converts unstructured human experience into high-
fidelity, machine-interpretable representations.
The contributions of this work are threefold. First, we
introduce the concept of Artificial Frictional Unemployment and
situate it within established labor market theory as a digitally
induced inefficiency in matching. Second, we demonstrate,
through controlled simulations, that semantic matching using
high-dimensional vector representations substantially reduces
false negative rates compared to keyword-based screening.
Third, we present a system architecture that enables candidate-
controlled verification, semantic translation, and secure data
governance while remaining interoperable with existing recruit-
ment infrastructure.
By reframing automated hiring as a component of national
labor market infrastructure rather than a firm-level optimization
tool, this paper highlights the broader economic consequences
of recruitment system design. Improving semantic alignment in
hiring has implications not only for individual job seekers, but
also for workforce participation, productivity, and economic
resilience. In this sense, reducing algorithmically induced labor
market friction represents a structural opportunity to improve
matching efficiency at a national scale.
II. LITERATURE REVIEW
This section situates the present study within three intersect-
ing bodies of scholarship: labor market research on hidden
and underutilized workers, economic theory on information
asymmetry in markets, and computational analyses of algorith-
mic hiring systems. Taken together, this literature reveals a
consistent pattern: while hiring technologies were introduced to
improve efficiency, their design assumptions often conflict with
the realities of how human skills are expressed and evaluated.
The result is a growing disconnect between available talent
and available work.
A. The “Hidden Worker” Phenomenon
Recent research has drawn attention to a large and persistent
population of workers who are willing and able to work but
remain systematically excluded from employment opportunities.
A prominent contribution in this area is the Harvard Business
School study by Fuller et al., which identifies more than
27 million “hidden workers” in the United States labor
market. These individuals are not unemployed due to lack
of capability, but rather because they are repeatedly screened
out by automated hiring processes.
The study emphasizes that modern recruitment systems rely
heavily on rigid filters, including requirements for continuous
employment histories, narrow credential definitions, and exact
keyword matches. Candidates with non-linear career paths,
caregiving gaps, military service, or skills acquired outside
traditional pipelines are disproportionately affected. Importantly,
many hidden workers possess skills that closely align with
employer needs, yet fail to pass automated screening stages.
From a systems perspective, this finding suggests that
exclusion occurs not at the level of skill supply, but at
the level of skill recognition. Automated filters operate on
simplified representations of candidate data and often fail to
capture equivalence between different expressions of similar
competencies. As a result, exclusion becomes a byproduct
of system design rather than a reflection of labor market
fundamentals.
B. Information Asymmetry and Labor Market Signaling
The inefficiencies observed in automated hiring can be
further understood through the lens of information economics.


## Page 3


Akerlof’s seminal work on the “Market for Lemons” describes
how markets characterized by asymmetric information tend
toward inefficiency. When buyers cannot reliably distinguish
between high-quality and low-quality goods, they respond
by discounting all offerings, leading to adverse selection and
reduced market participation.
Hiring markets exhibit a similar dynamic. Employers can-
not easily verify the accuracy of candidate claims prior
to interviews, while candidates rely on resumes as their
primary signaling mechanism. These resumes are self-reported,
unverified, and highly variable in format and content. As a
result, employers face substantial uncertainty when evaluating
applicant quality at scale.
Automated recruitment systems emerge as a response to this
uncertainty. By enforcing rigid criteria and narrow definitions
of qualification, employers attempt to reduce the risk of
advancing unsuitable candidates. However, this strategy mirrors
the outcome described by Akerlof: rather than resolving
information asymmetry, it amplifies it. When all resumes are
treated as potentially unreliable, systems default to conservative
thresholds that exclude a large share of qualified applicants.
C. Algorithmic Bias and Deterministic Screening in ATS
A growing body of work in computer science and policy
analysis has examined the behavior of algorithmic hiring sys-
tems. Studies by Bogen and Rieke, among others, highlight how
many Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) rely on deterministic
logic, including Boolean keyword matching and rule-based
scoring. While such approaches are computationally efficient,
they assume that skills and experience can be unambiguously
represented through fixed terms.
This assumption does not hold in practice. Equivalent compe-
tencies are often described using different language depending
on industry, organizational context, or educational background.
Deterministic systems treat these differences as substantive
rather than semantic, leading to systematic misclassification.
Research on algorithmic bias further demonstrates that such
systems disproportionately disadvantage candidates from non-
traditional backgrounds, where vocabulary variance rather than
skill variance becomes a primary determinant of screening
outcomes.
D. The Evolution of Semantic Matching Architectures
While the limitations of keyword-based screening systems
are well documented, computer science research has long
attempted to address these deficiencies through increasingly
sophisticated natural language processing techniques. Early
approaches relied on statistical models such as Latent Seman-
tic Analysis (LSA) and Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA),
which modeled word co-occurrence patterns to infer thematic
similarity between documents.
Although these models represented an improvement over
strict Boolean logic, they proved insufficient for recruitment
contexts where fine-grained semantic distinctions are critical.
For example, distinguishing between “Java” as a programming
language and “Java” as a geographic location requires contex-
tual understanding beyond the capabilities of early probabilistic
models.
The introduction of distributed word representations, such as
Word2Vec and GloVe, marked a significant shift by enabling
semantic similarity to be measured in dense vector spaces.
However, static embeddings assign a single representation to
each term, making them unable to resolve contextual polysemy.
Titles and skills that vary by domain are therefore conflated
into a single vector representation.
More recent Transformer-based architectures, including
BERT, generate contextualized embeddings by leveraging self-
attention mechanisms. These models allow terms to take on
different meanings depending on surrounding context and are
well suited for competency mapping in principle. In practice,
however, their computational cost introduces significant latency
and infrastructure challenges when applied to large-scale
recruitment systems. This creates a technical deadlock: models
that are fast enough to operate at scale lack semantic accuracy,
while models that provide contextual understanding impose
prohibitive computational overhead.
E. Synthesis and Research Gap
Taken together, the literature reveals a pattern of structural
failure across domains. Labor market research identifies large
pools of excluded but capable workers. Economic theory
explains how information asymmetry incentivizes excessive
filtering. Computational analyses show how deterministic
screening systems operationalize this filtering, while more
advanced semantic models remain difficult to deploy at scale.
Despite these insights, existing work largely treats these
domains in isolation. Specifically, there is limited research that
formalizes semantic misinterpretation as a measurable source
of frictional unemployment or addresses the latency–accuracy
trade-off inherent in deploying Transformer-based models for
high-volume recruitment systems. This paper contributes by
bridging these perspectives and framing automated recruitment
as a national labor market infrastructure challenge rather than
a firm-level optimization problem.
III. METHODOLOGY
This section describes the methodological framework used
to formalize and evaluate semantic misalignment in automated
recruitment systems. Rather than treating hiring as a purely
organizational process, we model candidate screening as a
computational matching problem subject to measurable classi-
fication error. Our approach isolates semantic interpretation as
the primary variable of interest and evaluates how alternative
representations of candidate and job data affect matching
outcomes.
The methodology is organized into three components. First,
we formalize legacy Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) as deter-
ministic classifiers and identify their dominant failure modes.
Second, we introduce a semantic representation framework
based on high-dimensional vector embeddings that captures
contextual similarity between candidates and job descriptions.

Third, we outline an experimental design using controlled
simulations to compare keyword-based screening with semantic
matching under identical conditions.
A. ATS as a Deterministic Classification System
Modern ATS platforms function as early-stage classifiers
that determine whether a candidate advances to human review.
In practice, these systems operate under a binary decision
framework: candidates are either shortlisted or rejected based
on predefined criteria. Although implementations vary across
vendors, the underlying logic typically relies on Boolean
keyword matching, weighted term frequency scores, and rule-
based thresholds.
From a machine learning perspective, this design prioritizes
precision over recall. The system is calibrated to minimize
false positives—advancing candidates who later prove unsuit-
able—by enforcing strict matching requirements. However, this
optimization strategy inherently increases the false negative
rate, leading to the systematic rejection of qualified candidates.
Because rejected candidates rarely receive feedback or vis-
ibility into screening decisions, these errors remain largely
unobserved.
To evaluate this behavior formally, we model legacy ATS
screening as a deterministic function
fATS : C →{0, 1},
where C represents a candidate’s resume and 1 indicates
advancement. Under this formulation, semantic equivalence
between different expressions of the same skill is not preserved
unless explicitly encoded through exact term overlap. As a
result, vocabulary variance becomes a dominant source of
classification error.
B. Semantic Representation via Vector Space Modeling
To move beyond deterministic keyword logic, we adopt a
semantic representation framework grounded in vector space
modeling. Both candidate resumes and job descriptions are
treated as unstructured text documents and mapped into a
shared high-dimensional embedding space. This representation
allows semantic similarity to be measured geometrically rather
than lexically.
Let vC ∈Rn denote the vector embedding of a candidate’s
resume and vJ ∈Rn denote the embedding of a job description.

exact keyword overlap. By operating in a continuous vector
space, the model preserves semantic equivalence across diverse
linguistic expressions.

employ contextualized embeddings derived from Transformer-

account for surrounding text, allowing identical terms to assume
different meanings depending on context. This capability is
particularly relevant in recruitment, where job titles and skill
descriptions are highly domain-specific.

D. Experimental Design and Controlled Simulation
To evaluate the impact of semantic matching on screening
outcomes, we conduct a series of controlled simulations
comparing legacy keyword-based ATS logic with vector-based
semantic matching. Because access to proprietary ATS datasets
is limited, we adopt a synthetic data generation approach
designed to isolate semantic effects under controlled conditions.
We construct a dataset of N = 1,000 synthetic resume–
job pairs. Each pair is labeled as “qualified” or “unqualified”
based on ground-truth skill alignment. To simulate real-world
variability, we employed a controlled text generation pipeline to
produce unique resume variants, introducing systematic lexical
perturbations (e.g., synonym substitution, acronym expansion,
and title variation) while preserving ground-truth competency
labels. Job descriptions are held constant across screening
conditions to ensure comparability.
Two screening pipelines are then applied to the same
dataset. The first uses deterministic keyword matching with
predefined thresholds, approximating the behavior of legacy
ATS platforms. The second uses semantic similarity scores
derived from vector embeddings, with candidates ranked
according to cosine similarity. Performance is evaluated using
standard classification metrics, including precision, recall, and
F1-score, with particular emphasis on false negative rates.
E. Methodological Scope and Assumptions
The methodology intentionally focuses on semantic inter-
pretation as the primary variable of interest. Factors such as
candidate behavior, recruiter decision-making, and downstream
interview performance are held constant or excluded. This

design choice allows the analysis to isolate how representation
and matching logic alone influence screening outcomes.
While the use of simulated data limits external validity,
it enables precise control over linguistic variation and skill
equivalence. As such, the methodology is well suited to
identifying structural failure modes in automated screening
systems and evaluating the potential impact of alternative
architectures.
IV. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
This section describes the system architecture of JobOS,
the proposed workforce operating framework introduced in
earlier sections. The architecture is designed to address the
structural failures identified in automated recruitment systems
by introducing a candidate-side computational layer that
standardizes, verifies, and semantically aligns human capital
signals. Rather than replacing existing hiring infrastructure,
JobOS is intentionally designed to interoperate with legacy
Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS) while mitigating their
dominant failure modes.
At a high level, the system follows a modular, service-
oriented architecture composed of four primary layers: (1)
data ingestion and normalization, (2) semantic representation
and indexing, (3) verification and simulation, and (4) secure
storage and governance. This separation of concerns allows
each component to evolve independently while preserving end-
to-end system integrity.
Fig. 1. High-level JobOS system architecture illustrating the modular pipeline
from unstructured candidate data to ATS-compatible outputs.
A. Data Ingestion and Normalization Layer
The ingestion layer is responsible for converting hetero-
geneous, unstructured candidate artifacts into standardized,
machine-interpretable representations. Inputs include resumes,
cover letters, portfolios, and supplementary documents, typ-
ically provided in PDF or DOCX formats. Because these
documents vary widely in structure and formatting, direct
semantic comparison is infeasible without preprocessing.
Text extraction is performed using optical character recog-
nition for scanned documents and native parsers for digi-
tally generated files. Following extraction, the text is passed
through a normalization pipeline that performs tokenization, de-
duplication, and structural segmentation. Personally identifiable
information (PII), such as names and contact details, is removed
or masked at this stage to support downstream blind evaluation
and to reduce the risk of demographic leakage.
The output of this layer is a structured, intermediate repre-
sentation of candidate data expressed in a standardized schema.
This schema preserves semantic content while abstracting
away formatting artifacts, enabling consistent processing across
candidates regardless of original document style.
B. Semantic Representation and Vector Indexing
Once normalized, candidate and job data are transformed
into semantic representations suitable for comparison at scale.
This layer implements the vector space modeling framework
described in Section III, mapping both candidate profiles and
job descriptions into a shared embedding space.
To support efficient retrieval, embeddings are stored in a
vector index optimized for approximate nearest-neighbor search.
Rather than performing exhaustive pairwise comparisons, the
system retrieves a ranked subset of candidate–job matches
based on cosine similarity. This design choice enables semantic
matching to operate at scale without incurring the latency
penalties associated with full Transformer inference at query
time.
Importantly, embeddings are generated once and reused
across multiple matching contexts. This amortization strategy
reduces computational overhead and allows JobOS to balance
semantic fidelity with operational efficiency, avoiding the
accuracy–latency deadlock identified in prior recruitment
architectures.
Fig. 2. Semantic matching pipeline illustrating embedding generation, vector
indexing, and similarity-based retrieval.
C. Verification and Simulation Engine
A central architectural contribution of JobOS is the verifi-
cation and simulation layer, which addresses the information
asymmetry inherent in resume-based hiring. Rather than relying
exclusively on self-reported credentials, this layer introduces
interactive, task-oriented assessments designed to validate
candidate competencies prior to application submission.
The simulation engine conducts structured, role-specific
interactions that approximate early-stage interviews or practical
evaluations. Candidate responses are captured through text or


## Page 6


speech interfaces and processed in real time. For speech-based
interactions, automatic transcription is used to convert audio
responses into text for downstream analysis.
In addition to content analysis, the system captures auxiliary
signals such as response latency and linguistic sentiment. These
signals are treated as probabilistic indicators that complement
semantic alignment scores, providing higher-fidelity signals
than static resumes alone while preserving the role of human
judgment.
D. Secure Storage and Data Governance
Given the sensitivity of employment data, JobOS incor-
porates security and governance as first-class architectural
concerns. Candidate data, embeddings, and verification artifacts
are stored in a relational database augmented with vector
storage capabilities. Fine-grained access controls enforce tenant
isolation and restrict data visibility based on user roles and
consent settings.
A core design principle of the system is candidate ownership
of data. Candidates retain control over when and how their
information is shared, including the ability to revoke access
or selectively disclose specific competencies. Encryption is
applied both at rest and in transit, and audit logs are maintained
to support transparency and compliance with applicable data
protection regulations.
Fig. 3. JobOS data governance model illustrating candidate-controlled access,
encryption boundaries, and audit logging.
E. End-to-End System Flow
From an end-to-end perspective, JobOS operates as an
intermediary layer between candidates and existing recruitment
infrastructure. Candidate data is ingested, normalized, and
semantically encoded before being matched against job re-
quirements. Verification outputs augment these representations,
producing a composite competency profile that can be translated
into formats compatible with legacy ATS pipelines.
Crucially, the system does not require employers to modify
their existing tools. Instead, it outputs enriched, semantically
aligned candidate representations that reduce the likelihood of
false negatives under deterministic screening logic. This design
choice reflects the system’s infrastructural orientation: JobOS
improves matching efficiency by adapting to the constraints
of the current labor market rather than assuming wholesale
replacement of incumbent technologies.
F. Implementation Specification (Prototype)
To validate the architectural feasibility, a functional alpha pro-
totype was constructed using a modern, cloud-native technology
stack. The frontend interface was implemented using React.js,
enabling real-time interaction during simulation workflows.
Backend orchestration was implemented using a serverless
microservices architecture backed by PostgreSQL.
High-dimensional embeddings and similarity search were
supported using the pgvector extension, enabling scalable
vector indexing within a relational database. The semantic
parsing layer leveraged a commercial large language model
API, while speech-based interactions were supported using a
neural speech-to-text model for low-latency transcription.
This implementation demonstrates the practical feasibility
of the proposed architecture using commercially available,
scalable cloud technologies, supporting both research evaluation
and future system extension.
V. EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS
This section presents the empirical results of the controlled
simulations described in Section III. The objective is to
quantify how semantic matching alters screening outcomes
relative to deterministic keyword-based approaches. We focus
on classification performance, error composition, and failure
modes that directly affect labor market matching efficiency.
A. Comparative Screening Performance
We evaluated two screening pipelines on the same dataset of
N = 1,000 synthetic resume–job pairs: (1) a legacy keyword-
based ATS approximation and (2) the JobOS semantic matching
engine. Both pipelines were calibrated using comparable
acceptance thresholds to ensure a fair comparison.
Performance was assessed using standard classification
metrics: precision, recall, and F1-score. Precision captures
the proportion of shortlisted candidates who are truly qualified,
while recall measures the proportion of qualified candidates
successfully advanced. The F1-score provides a harmonic mean
that balances these two objectives.
Across all metrics, the semantic matching pipeline demon-
strated substantially improved performance. While the legacy
ATS maintained moderate precision, it exhibited poor recall,
indicating a strong tendency toward over-rejection. In contrast,
the semantic engine achieved high recall without a commensu-
rate loss in precision, resulting in a markedly higher F1-score.
B. False Negative Analysis
The most significant divergence between the two systems
appears in their false negative rates. Under keyword-based
screening, a large share of qualified candidates were rejected
due to vocabulary mismatch rather than skill deficiency.
These candidates frequently described competencies using
synonymous or domain-specific language not explicitly encoded
in the job description.


## Page 7


Semantic matching resolved the majority of these cases
by recognizing conceptual equivalence between differently
phrased skills. For example, candidates describing experience
in “client engagement analytics” were correctly matched to
roles requiring “customer success metrics,” despite the absence
of direct keyword overlap. This effect accounts for the majority
of recall improvement observed in the semantic pipeline.
Importantly, the reduction in false negatives did not lead to
a corresponding increase in false positives. Precision remained
high, suggesting that semantic alignment improves inclusivity
without sacrificing screening quality.

D. Error Mode Characterization
To better understand system behavior, misclassification errors

Common patterns included synonym substitution, acronym

as resumes lacking sufficient contextual detail or roles with
broadly defined requirements. These errors reflect intrinsic
uncertainty rather than systematic bias, suggesting that further
improvements would require richer candidate signals rather
than more aggressive filtering.
E. Interpretation and Implications
From a systems perspective, these results demonstrate that
automated hiring inefficiency is not merely a consequence
of labor market conditions, but a function of representational
choices embedded in recruitment infrastructure. Deterministic
keyword filters impose an artificial bottleneck that dispropor-
tionately affects qualified candidates whose experience does
not conform to narrow linguistic conventions.
By transitioning from exact-match logic to probabilistic
semantic alignment, the JobOS architecture substantially im-
proves matching efficiency. At scale, such improvements imply
shorter job search durations, better utilization of human capital,
and reduced frictional unemployment. These findings support
the central claim of this paper: that a non-trivial portion of
labor market slack is algorithmically induced and therefore
amenable to infrastructural correction.

G. Sensitivity and Robustness Analysis
While the headline results demonstrate substantial improve-
ments in matching performance, it is important to evaluate
the robustness of these gains under realistic operational
variation. Automated screening systems rely on configurable
thresholds, representation choices, and tolerance to linguistic
variability. Accordingly, this subsection examines the sensitivity
of semantic matching to key parameters and compares its
stability against deterministic keyword-based screening.
1) Similarity Threshold Sensitivity: Semantic screening re-
quires a decision threshold that determines whether a candidate
advances based on similarity score. To evaluate threshold
sensitivity, the cosine similarity cutoff was varied from 0.60
to 0.85 while holding all other parameters constant. At each
threshold, precision, recall, and F1-score were recomputed.
As expected, increasing the threshold resulted in higher
precision and reduced recall. However, the semantic pipeline
exhibited a broad operating region in which recall remained
high while precision declined only marginally. This behavior
indicates that semantic matching supports flexible calibration,
allowing systems to improve inclusivity without incurring
excessive false positives.
In contrast, the legacy ATS pipeline demonstrated brittle

VI. DISCUSSION AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
This section situates the empirical findings and architectural
design of JobOS within broader ethical, societal, and policy
contexts. While the preceding sections demonstrate measurable
improvements in matching efficiency, any system that inter-
venes in labor market processes must be evaluated not only for
performance, but also for its implications on fairness, gover-
nance, and individual autonomy. Accordingly, this discussion
addresses potential risks associated with algorithmic hiring and
outlines design principles intended to mitigate them.
A. Algorithmic Bias and Fairness
Automated recruitment systems have been widely criticized
for reproducing or amplifying existing social and economic
inequalities. Bias may arise from multiple sources, including
historical data, model design choices, and proxy variables that
correlate with protected attributes. Although semantic matching
reduces vocabulary-driven exclusion, it does not, by itself,
eliminate the risk of biased outcomes.
The JobOS architecture addresses this concern through a
combination of structural and procedural safeguards. First,
demographic identifiers and institution-specific signals are
masked during the semantic encoding process, enabling blind
evaluation at the representation level. By preventing names,
locations, and educational pedigree from influencing similarity
calculations, the system reduces opportunities for indirect
discrimination during early-stage screening.
Second, the use of probabilistic similarity scores rather than
hard thresholds allows for more transparent calibration. Em-
ployers or evaluators can inspect trade-offs between precision
and recall rather than relying on opaque pass–fail criteria. This
flexibility supports fairness audits and facilitates adjustment of
screening behavior in response to observed disparities.
B. Transparency and Explainability
A persistent challenge in AI-mediated decision systems
is the lack of interpretability. Black-box models undermine
trust among both candidates and employers, particularly when
adverse outcomes are difficult to explain. In hiring contexts,
opacity can exacerbate frustration and erode confidence in the
legitimacy of screening processes.
JobOS is designed to support partial explainability by
retaining intermediate representations and similarity rationales.
Rather than presenting candidates with binary outcomes, the
system can surface high-level explanations such as skill clusters
that contributed positively to alignment or areas where semantic
overlap was limited. While full interpretability of deep models
remains an open research problem, these mechanisms provide
meaningful insight without exposing proprietary model details.
Transparency is further supported through audit logging and
versioned model updates. Screening outcomes can be traced to
specific representation and matching configurations, enabling
post hoc analysis and accountability.
C. Data Ownership and Privacy
Employment data is among the most sensitive categories of
personal information, encompassing career history, skill pro-
files, and behavioral signals. Traditional recruitment platforms
often centralize control over such data, monetizing candidate
information without meaningful user consent or recourse.
In contrast, JobOS adopts a user-centric data governance
model. Candidates retain ownership of their data and control
its disclosure through explicit consent mechanisms. Access to
semantic profiles, verification artifacts, and simulation outputs


## Page 9


can be selectively granted or revoked, aligning system behavior
with principles of data minimization and purpose limitation.
From a technical standpoint, encryption is enforced both
at rest and in transit, and access is governed through role-
based policies. These measures support compliance with
contemporary data protection standards and reduce the risk of
misuse or unauthorized access.
D. Risk of Over-Automation
While semantic matching improves early-stage screening,
excessive reliance on automated systems introduces the risk of
over-automation. Hiring decisions involve contextual judgment,
organizational fit, and human discretion that cannot be fully
captured through computational representations.
The JobOS framework explicitly avoids positioning itself
as a decision-making authority. Instead, it functions as an
augmentation layer that improves signal quality prior to human
review. By enhancing candidate representation rather than
replacing evaluators, the system preserves the role of human
judgment in downstream stages of hiring.
This design choice reflects a broader ethical stance: algo-
rithmic systems should inform and support human decision-
making, not supplant it. Maintaining this boundary is essential
to preventing the normalization of fully automated exclusion.
E. Societal and Policy Implications
At scale, improvements in matching efficiency have impli-
cations beyond individual hiring outcomes. Reducing algorith-
mically induced false negatives can shorten unemployment
durations, improve labor mobility, and increase workforce par-
ticipation. These effects are particularly relevant in economies
where labor demand and supply coexist but fail to align
efficiently.
From a policy perspective, the findings of this study suggest
that labor market inefficiencies are not solely attributable to
worker deficits or macroeconomic conditions. Instead, they
may reflect design choices embedded in digital infrastructure.
Addressing such inefficiencies therefore requires not only
workforce development initiatives, but also modernization of
recruitment technologies.
By framing automated hiring as a component of national
labor market infrastructure, this work invites interdisciplinary
collaboration among technologists, economists, and policymak-
ers. Ethical deployment of systems like JobOS depends on
governance frameworks that balance innovation with account-
ability and individual rights.
F. Limitations and Threats to Validity
Despite the strength of the empirical results, several limi-
tations must be acknowledged. Addressing these constraints
is essential for interpreting findings responsibly and guiding
future research.
1) Synthetic Data Constraints: The experimental evaluation
relies on synthetically generated resumes rather than proprietary
ATS logs or real-world hiring data. While this approach
enables controlled experimentation and precise labeling, it
limits external validity. Real resumes may exhibit additional
noise sources, including inconsistent formatting, incomplete
information, or strategic self-presentation, that are not fully
captured in the simulation.
Nevertheless, synthetic data is appropriate for isolating
semantic effects, which are the primary variable of interest.
Future work should validate these findings using anonymized,
real-world screening datasets.
2) ATS Behavior Approximation: Legacy ATS platforms
vary widely in their internal implementations. The deterministic
screening pipeline used here represents a stylized approximation
rather than a vendor-specific system. Absolute performance
metrics should therefore be interpreted cautiously.
However, the dominant failure modes observed—particularly
vocabulary mismatch and recall collapse—are well-documented
across independent ATS audits and empirical studies. As
such, the relative performance gap between keyword-based
and semantic approaches is likely to generalize even if exact
magnitudes differ.
3) Absence of Downstream Outcomes: This study focuses
exclusively on early-stage screening performance and does not
evaluate downstream outcomes such as interview success, hiring
decisions, or job performance. Improving recall at the screening
stage does not guarantee superior hires unless complemented
by effective downstream evaluation.
Importantly, JobOS is designed as an augmentation layer
rather than a decision authority. Its purpose is to improve
candidate visibility and representation quality while preserving
human judgment in later hiring stages.
4) Behavioral and Ethical Considerations: The introduction
of semantic optimization tools may influence candidate behavior
over time, potentially encouraging strategic resume construction.
While semantic models are more resistant to surface-level
gaming than keyword filters, no automated system is immune
to strategic adaptation.
Mitigating such effects requires transparency, continuous
monitoring, and periodic recalibration, reinforcing the impor-
tance of governance frameworks discussed in Section VI.
VII. CONCLUSION
This paper examined an increasingly visible paradox in
the contemporary United States labor market: the persistent
coexistence of high job vacancy rates and prolonged unem-
ployment durations. While traditional explanations emphasize
skills mismatch or macroeconomic frictions, our analysis
demonstrates that a significant share of this inefficiency arises
from the design of automated recruitment infrastructure itself.
Specifically, deterministic keyword-based Applicant Tracking
Systems introduce a systematic failure mode that rejects
qualified candidates due to semantic misinterpretation rather
than substantive skill deficits.
By situating automated hiring within established labor market
theory, this work introduces the concept of Artificial Frictional
Unemployment—a digitally induced inefficiency in worker–
firm matching. Drawing on prior research in labor economics,
information asymmetry, and algorithmic decision-making, we


## Page 10


argue that legacy ATS platforms amplify information gaps by
treating resumes as static, low-fidelity signals and prioritizing
false-positive avoidance at the expense of recall. The result
is a structurally biased screening process that renders large
segments of capable labor effectively invisible.
Through controlled simulations, we demonstrate that replac-
ing deterministic keyword logic with probabilistic semantic
matching substantially improves screening performance. High-
dimensional vector representations enable systems to capture
conceptual equivalence across diverse linguistic expressions,
reducing false negative rates without sacrificing precision.
These findings suggest that hiring inefficiency is not an
unavoidable labor market outcome, but rather a correctable
artifact of representational choices embedded in recruitment
systems.
Building on these results, we present JobOS, a candidate-side
workforce operating architecture designed to standardize, verify,
and semantically align human capital signals. Unlike job boards
or employer-facing screening tools, JobOS functions as an
intermediary infrastructural layer that augments existing hiring
pipelines without requiring their replacement. By combining
semantic encoding, controlled verification, and candidate-
owned data governance, the architecture addresses the semantic,
trust, and access gaps identified throughout the literature.
Importantly, this work does not advocate for fully automated
hiring decisions. Instead, it advances a principled framework
in which algorithmic systems improve signal quality and
matching efficiency while preserving human judgment at
critical decision points. Ethical considerations—including bias
mitigation, transparency, privacy, and data ownership—are
treated as foundational design constraints rather than post hoc
adjustments.
At a national scale, the implications of this research extend
beyond individual employment outcomes. Improving matching
efficiency has the potential to reduce unemployment duration,
enhance labor mobility, and increase overall productivity by
more effectively deploying existing human capital. In this sense,
modernizing recruitment infrastructure represents a structural
opportunity to strengthen workforce participation and economic
resilience.
In conclusion, this paper demonstrates that frictional unem-
ployment in the modern labor market is not solely a function
of worker preparedness or employer demand, but is materially
shaped by the algorithms that mediate access between them.
By reframing hiring as a problem of semantic translation
rather than rigid filtering, JobOS illustrates a viable pathway
toward more efficient, equitable, and resilient labor market
infrastructure.
