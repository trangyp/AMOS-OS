Trade Relationships During and After a Crisis‗
Alejandra Martinez
January 2026
Abstract
I study how firms adjust to temporary disruptions in international trade relationships or-
ganized through relational contracts. I exploit an extreme, plausibly exogenous weather
shock during the 2010–11 La Niña season that restricted Colombian flower exporters’
access to cargo terminals. Using transaction-level data from the Colombian–U.S. flower
trade, I show that importers with less-exposed supplier portfolios are less likely to ter-
minate disrupted relationships, instead tolerating shipment delays. In contrast, firms
facing greater exposure experience higher partner turnover and are more likely to exit
the market, with exit accounting for a substantial share of relationship separations.
These findings demonstrate that idiosyncratic shocks to buyer–seller relationships can
propagate into persistent changes in firms’ trading portfolios.
Keywords: La Niña, Supply Chains, International Contracts, Weather Shocks

Introduction
In less-than-perfect product markets, firms rely on buyer–seller relational contracts.1 These
relationships are inherently vulnerable to temporary disruptions that interfere with pro-
duction or delivery. As extreme weather events become more frequent and severe, supply
chains are increasingly exposed to such disruptions, including temporary road closures,
transport bottlenecks, and interruptions to logistics infrastructure. Even when shocks are
local and transitory, they can substantially disrupt established production networks and
generate aggregate effects.2
Understanding how supply chain disruptions affect buyer–
seller relationships is particularly important in international settings, where contracts are
imperfectly enforceable and firms depend heavily on repeated interactions.
Existing work documents output losses among firms linked to regions affected by dis-
ruptions (Barrot & Sauvagnat, 2016; Carvalho et al., 2021). Much less is known about how
firms adjust their portfolios of buyer–seller relationships in response. These adjustments
matter because buyer–seller relationships form the backbone of trade networks and shape
the structure of world trade. The accumulation of relationships drives firm growth, while
relationship destruction can precipitate market exit (Oberfield, 2018). Buyer–seller matches
also affect productivity through sorting and influence firms’ ability to expand their trading
networks through network effects (Bernard et al., 2018; Martinez et al., 2025). This is the
first paper to identify shocks at the level of individual buyer–seller links and shows how dis-
ruptions to specific relationships propagate within firms to affect their overall relationship
portfolios and market participation.
I study Colombian flower exporters and their U.S. importers during the extreme 2010–11
La Niña episode, which disrupted road access to cargo terminals used by exporters. The
Colombian flower industry exemplifies relational trade under limited contract enforceabil-
ity: exporters access international markets almost exclusively through established direct
relationships, and forming new connections is costly. The setting therefore provides a sharp
environment in which to study relationship portfolio decisions when firms have few outside
options. Importantly, flower production itself was largely unaffected by flooding at produc-
tion sites, and the industry relies minimally on upstream inputs, allowing me to isolate
disruptions operating through transport access rather than input shortages. This enables a
clean comparison between buyer–seller relationships exposed to disrupted routes and those
that were not.

I construct a novel dataset linking road disruptions to exporter routes to cargo termi-
nals for all buyer–seller pairs, using firm-to-firm Colombian customs data from 2007 to
2019. Exploiting variation in which roads were affected by the La Niña floods, I define
relationship-level exposure based on whether a given buyer–seller link relied on disrupted
transport routes. For importers, exposure varies within the firm because buyers source
from suppliers located in different regions, which use different road networks. This design
allows me to identify shocks at the level of individual buyer–seller relationships and to trace
how disruptions to specific links transmit within firms to affect their broader relationship
portfolios.3
I estimate the effects of the La Niña disruption using an event-study design. To study re-
lationship termination, I estimate linear probability models of relationship ending. Because
terminations are infrequent at the monthly level, outcomes are aggregated to six-month
periods when analyzing portfolio adjustments. I additionally examine short-run adjust-
ment along other margins using monthly data at the 6-digit HS product level, including
transaction incidence and traded quantities.
I document distinct effects at the relationship and firm levels. At the relationship level,
pooled specifications show no differential termination between exposed and non-exposed
links. Within importers, however, exposure reduces relationship termination during the
disruption: the probability of termination falls by 7 percentage points, a 53% decline relative
to a baseline hazard of 0.13. Transactions along exposed relationships decline during the
shock but recover once transport access is restored.
At the firm level, exposure induces adjustment through exit and portfolio contraction,
concentrated among highly connected buyers. Among importers with high pre-shock inde-
gree, greater exposure increases relationship termination during the disruption, with effects
that persist over time. These effects operate through firm exit: a one-standard-deviation
increase in exposure raises the probability of importer exit by 10 percentage points, me-
chanically terminating all relationships, although exit occurs only during the shock period.
High-indegree firms also experience a persistent decline in new relationship formation,
leading to a contraction of supplier portfolios. In contrast, exposure has no effect on exit
among low-indegree firms and only a small, marginally significant increase in relationship
termination.

Context: 2010-11 La Niña and Colombian flower exporters
2.1
The 2010-11 La Niña event
La Niña, part of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), is a major climate phenomenon
that has been observed 24 times since 1903. During La Niña, specific weather patterns
are more likely but not guaranteed: equatorial East Africa typically experiences drier-than-
normal conditions from December to February, while the central Andes usually receive
above-average rainfall.
The 2010–11 La Niña was among the strongest on record.
Australian temperatures
reached their second- and third-highest levels since 1900, and the Western United States
and Midwest recorded snowfall 180% above average, with the exception of the Southern
Rockies and Western Mountains.8 Climate change is expected to intensify extreme weather
events such as La Niña, causing significant infrastructure damage in developing countries.9
Extreme weather can also affect global markets: flooding in major producing countries can
reduce exports and raise world prices.10 In Vietnam, Balboni (2019) show that forward-
looking infrastructure investments to avoid flood-prone regions could yield large welfare
gains.
In Colombia, the 2010–11 La Niña caused widespread flooding and landslides. Figure

2.2
The Colombian flower sector
Colombia is the second-largest exporter of cut flowers worldwide, after the Netherlands. As
of 2010, Colombia accounted for approximately 68% of U.S. flower imports and held a 16.8%
share of the global export market. The United States is the largest importer of flowers, with

## Page 8


a market share of about 20% both in 2010 and today.12
The Colombian–U.S. flower trade is particularly well suited for studying buyer–seller
relationships.
Trade in this sector relies on long-standing direct relationships between
exporters and importers, many of which date back to the 1970s. Geographic proximity
facilitates repeated interaction and relationship formation between Colombian producers
and U.S. buyers.
Most Colombian flower exporters are concentrated in two regions: the Sabana de Bo-
gotá (Savannah region), near the capital, and the Antioquia region near Medellín. In 2009,
roughly 70% of export-oriented production was in the Savannah region, and 18% in Antio-
quia. High-altitude areas are preferred, as flowers grow optimally at temperatures between
15 and 25 degrees Celsius. Colombia produces approximately 1,600 flower varieties for
export. The main species are roses (31%), hydrangeas (15%), carnations (13%), and chrysan-
themums (11%).13
During the 2010–11 La Niña episode, flooding at production sites was mitigated through
protective measures such as sandbags. Estimated production losses ranged from 5% to 15%
nationally, primarily due to increased humidity affecting rose producers. Flower producers
in flood-prone areas received compensation from the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural
Development (MADR) totaling roughly $230,000.14
Figure 3 shows total exported quantities (kilograms) and trade values (U.S. dollars) for
all destinations. Export volumes declined following the 2010–11 La Niña event, a pattern
that coincides with contemporaneous drops in domestic production shown in Figure A.1
in the Appendix. This alignment indicates that the observed reduction in exports reflects
supply-side disruptions rather than a negative demand shock.15
This sectoral and geographic structure, combined with variation in road access to cargo
terminals, provides a natural setting to study how temporary, relationship-specific shocks
propagate through trade portfolios.

Empirical strategy
4.1
Identification assumption
I exploit relationship-level exposures to road disruptions between October 2010 and June
2011. These disruptions resulted from unprecedented overnight rainfall, which triggered
landslides along multiple stretches of the road network, generating essentially random
variation in which routes were affected. The exposure to the shock measure is constructed at
the relationship level, allowing disrupted relationships to be compared to unaffected ones.
Figure 6 illustrates the setup.
A relationship is considered exposed if any routes to
a terminal are disrupted. For example, relationship (i, j) is exposed, while (k, m) is not.
Within-importer comparisons focus on variation across relationships connected to the same
importer; for instance, (i, m) and (k, m) both connect to m, allowing tighter comparisons.
Firm-level exposure through relationships reflects portfolio composition: importer j has 100%
exposure if it has a single relationship that is exposed to the shock, whereas importer m with
two relationships has 50% exposure if only one relationship is exposed to the shock.

Quantities and transactions.
Much of the existing literature focuses on short-term ad-
justment, i.e.,changes in prices and quantities (intensive margin), total sales, or transaction
patterns (extensive margin), typically over a few months after a shock (Chakrabarti, Maha-
jan, and Tomar (2025); Khanna, Morales, and Pandalai-Nayar (2022); Balboni, Boehm, and
Waseem (2023)). These short-term responses can act as temporary buffers, giving firms time
to adjust their portfolios. The granularity of my data allows me to study both extensive and
intensive margin responses during and after the shock.

Results
How do firms respond to temporary shocks that disrupt their suppliers’ ability to deliver
goods? In this section, I show that buyer–supplier relationships adjust in response to dis-
ruptions, distinguishing between relationship termination and efforts to preserve existing
contracts through shipment reorganization. I then examine how relationship-level shocks
aggregate to affect importers’ supplier portfolios, assessing whether more exposed importers
experience greater partner churn. Finally, I analyse whether these disruptions translate into
firm-level exit from the market.

number of relationships or exit the market.
Taken together, the results indicate that temporary disruptions have heterogeneous ef-
fects depending on how concentrated exposure is within a firm’s portfolio. When exposure
is limited to a subset of relationships, the indirect effect dominates and firms preserve
existing matches. When exposure is widespread, the direct effect dominates, leading to
relationship termination and portfolio contraction.
The framework clarifies why relationship-level estimates show increased relationship
survival, while firm-level exposure predicts higher overall relationship churn and exit.
However, the model is deliberately stylized and abstracts from several margins observed in
the data, most notably delayed shipments and shipment reorganization. These short-run
adjustments operate within existing relationships and are not explicitly modelled here. As
a result, the framework captures firms’ extensive-margin decisions over relationships, but
not the full set of intensive-margin responses used to absorb temporary supply disruptions.
7
Conclusion
This paper studies how temporary, relationship-specific supply disruptions affect the orga-
nization of international trade. In environments where relational contracts underpin market
access, shocks that impair individual buyer–seller matches can generate effects that extend
well beyond the directly affected transactions. Understanding these dynamics is increasingly
important as climate-related, political, and trade-policy disruptions become more frequent
and severe.
Using plausibly exogenous road disruptions caused by a La Niña episode in Colombia, I
show that temporary shocks reduce relationship survival on impact and induce substantial
reallocation within firms’ supplier portfolios. The effects are highly heterogeneous. Firms
with limited exposure largely preserve existing relationships, whereas firms whose port-
27


## Page 29


folios are heavily exposed experience widespread relationship termination and a higher
probability of exiting the market. These adjustments persist beyond the disruption itself,
shaping portfolio dynamics and firms’ market presence over time.
The results highlight the central role of portfolio composition in mediating the effects
of temporary shocks. When exposure is concentrated, the value of maintaining relational
contracts declines, leading firms to unwind multiple relationships simultaneously. When
exposure is limited, surviving relationships become relatively more valuable and are pre-
served. This distinction helps reconcile opposing relationship- and firm-level responses to
the same shock and underscores that resilience in international trade depends not only on
the severity of disruptions, but also on the structure of firms’ trading networks.

C.1
Classification of road disruptions
This section describes the methodology used to classify road disruptions and construct
routes to cargo terminals. Routes are estimated using the municipality centroid or main
city of each farm as the origin. I rely on the SICE-TAC software provided by the Colombian
Ministry of Transport, which reports routes supplied by transportation service companies
operating inter-municipal freight services. As a baseline, I assume a two-axle truck with a
container trailer and include one hour of waiting time for both loading and unloading.
The software provides route information between specific origin–destination pairs, pri-
marily connecting farms to distribution centers and cargo terminals in major cities such as
Bogotá and Medellín, as well as secondary cities. For each route, SICE-TAC reports travel
time, distance, cost per kilometer, and the precise path taken, including tolls. When multiple
feasible routes are available for a given origin–destination pair, all alternatives are retained.
For expositional simplicity, road disruptions are grouped into two waves: October 2010
to April 2011, and May 2011 to June 2011. For each disruption episode, I first estimate
benchmark routes using the standard SICE-TAC configuration for inter-municipal freight
transport. Routing is conducted for all possible origin–cargo terminal pairs and repeated
whenever a disruption occurs.
When SICE-TAC does not report a feasible route, or when additional comparable routes
are available, I supplement the analysis using Google Maps. These alternative routes are
selected to be similar in distance and travel time and reflect feasible options exporters could
plausibly use to reach cargo terminals. All such routes are treated as potential benchmark
routes when classifying exposure to road disruptions.
