Abstract
We establish that the minimum number of arcs required to partition the Galois
projective plane PG(2, q) is q + 1. Furthermore, we determine the exact value
for a fractional variant of this problem.
We extend our analysis to affine planes AG(2, q), proving that they can be
partitioned into q arcs. In particular, we show that this partition is tight when
q is an odd prime power, and that a (q −1)-partition is attainable for q = 2k
with k ∈{1, 2, 3}. For q = 2k with k ≥4, we provide bounds between two
possible values.
Finally, we apply these results to Euclidean grids, demonstrating that a partition
into (1 + ϵ)n sets in general position exists for any ϵ > 0 and sufficiently large
n. We also present exact minimal partitions for small Euclidean grids.
Keywords: Projective planes, affine planes, euclidean grids, projective arcs,
arc-chromatic number, fractional arc-chromatic number

1 Introduction
In this work, we study finite geometries, which we define as pairs (P, L), where P and
L denote the sets of points and lines, respectively. We focus on three families: Galois
projective planes, finite affine planes, and Euclidean grids. For each q > 1 that is a
power of a prime, we denote by the Galois projective plane PG(2, q) the finite projective
plane over the field GF(q). The Galois affine plane AG(2, q) is obtained from PG(2, q)

we define the Euclidean grid Gn as the geometry whose point set is [n] × [n], where
[n] := {1, . . . , n}, and collinearity is inherited from the Euclidean plane.
We define an arc in a finite geometry (P, L) as a set A ⊂P containing no three
collinear points; that is, for any distinct P, Q, R ∈A, there does not exist a line ℓ∈L
such that {P, Q, R} ⊆ℓ. Our aim is to understand structural properties of collections
of arcs, with particular interest in coloring problems concerning arcs in PG(2, q),
AG(2, q), and Gn.
Arcs are fundamental objects in finite geometry. In PG(2, q), the maximum size of
an arc is q+1 when q is odd, and q+2 when q is even. A cornerstone result of Segre [26]
establishes that if q is odd, then ovals (arcs of size q + 1) in PG(2, q) are conics. Since
then, considerable work has been devoted to the classification of ovals and hyperovals
(arcs of size q+2) in PG(2, q) for even q, although a complete characterization remains
elusive. As the dimension of the projective space increases, determining the size of
arcs becomes a widely open problem, closely related to maximum distance separable
codes and the well-known MDS conjecture. For foundational definitions and results on
finite projective planes, we refer to the classic text by Hirschfield [20] and the modern
treatment by Kiss and Sz˝ony [22]. For recent developments on arcs in finite projective
spaces and their connections to codes and the MDS conjecture, we refer to the survey
by Ball and Lavrauw [8].
To introduce our main problem, we define, for each positive integer k, a k-coloring
of a finite geometry (P, L) as a map c : P →[k]. For a given k-coloring and each
j ∈[k], we refer to c−1(j) as the j-th color class. We say that a k-coloring is arc-proper
if every color class is an arc.
Definition 1.1 Let (P, L) be a finite geometry. The arc chromatic number χA((P, L)) is
the least k such that there exists an arc-proper k-coloring of (P, L).
Our first main result establishes the precise value of the arc chromatic number for
PG(2, q). In Section 2, we prove the following.
Theorem 1.2 For any q that is a power of a prime, we have χA(PG(2, q)) = q + 1.
This result contributes to the extensive body of work on coloring projective geome-
tries. A closely related line of research concerns the arc chromatic number of projective
spaces PG(d, 2), originally motivated by their connection to Steiner triples. For exam-
ple, Fug`ere, Haddad, and Wehlau [12] proved that χA(PG(5, 2)) = 5, and Haddad [15]
showed that χA(PG(d, 2)) is unbounded. A longstanding open problem is to deter-
mine χA(PG(6, 2)). In this line of research, the order q of the projective plane is fixed
while the dimension d increases. In contrast, our results fix d and allow q to grow;
thus, our geometries are not generally Steiner triples.
For further results on coloring problems in projective planes, particularly those
related to rainbow-free colorings, the upper chromatic number, and the heterochro-
matic number, see [1, 6, 7]. For balanced versions of these problems, see [2, 9]. Results

2 The Arc Chromatic Number for PG(2, q)
We begin by constructing a coloring that achieves the claimed value of χA(PG(2, q)).
To this end, we employ the cyclic model of PG(2, q), in which each point is identified
with an element of Zq2+q+1. In this model, each point possesses an additive inverse,
and for a set of points P, the additive inverse −P is defined as the set of additive
inverses of the points in P. A result of Hall [16] establishes that, under these definitions,
the additive inverse of any line is an arc; see also [10] for generalizations.
Proposition 2.1 Let q be a power of a prime. There exists an arc-proper (q + 1)-coloring of
PG(2, q).
Proof Consider the cyclic model of PG(2, q). Let ℓ1, . . . , ℓq+1 denote the q + 1 lines through
a fixed point P. These lines cover PG(2, q), so their additive inverses −ℓ1, . . . , −ℓq+1 are arcs
that also cover PG(2, q). Define c(P) = 1, and for each j ∈[q + 1] and each P ′ ∈−ℓj \ {P},
define c(P ′) = j. Since each −ℓj is an arc, this construction yields an arc-proper coloring of
PG(2, q) using q + 1 colors.
□
We now turn to the lower bound.
Proposition 2.2 Let q be a power of a prime. There does not exist an arc-proper q-coloring
of PG(2, q).

points. Thus, if an arc-proper q-coloring exists, each chromatic class contains at most q + 1
points, so PG(2, q) would have at most q(q + 1) ≤q2 + q points, which is a contradiction.

The construction for q even is similar, but we require an additional lemma based
on the following result of Qvist [24].
Theorem 3.2 Let q be a power of 2. Let A be an arc of size q + 1 in a finite projective
geometry of order q. Then, there is a unique tangent line through each point of A, and all of
these tangents pass through a common point P outside A.
We call the point P in Theorem 3.2 the nucleus of A. The set A ∪{P} is still an
arc. Our next auxiliary result states that, in the cyclic model, the arcs obtained as
additive inverses of distinct lines have distinct nuclei.
Lemma 3.3 Let q be a power of 2. Consider the cyclic model of PG(2, q) and distinct lines
ℓ1 and ℓ2. Then the nuclei of −ℓ1 and −ℓ2 are distinct. Therefore, every point is the nucleus
of exactly one arc of the form −ℓ.
Proof Suppose, for contradiction, that the nuclei of both −ℓ1 and −ℓ2 are the same point
Q. The lines ℓ1 and ℓ2 intersect in a single point P. Let ℓ3, . . . , ℓq+1 be the remaining lines
through P. Then, the arcs −ℓ1, . . . , −ℓd+1 intersect pairwise in the single point −P. By
Theorem 3.2, each of these d + 1 arcs has exactly one tangent through −P. Since the line
through Q and −P is the tangent to both −ℓ1 and −ℓ2, one of the d+1 lines through −P, say
m, is not the tangent of any of the arcs −ℓ1, . . . , −ℓd+1. But then m must contain the point
−P and at least one other point in each of these arcs, yielding the contradiction |m| ≥q + 2.
This shows that the map assigning to each line the nucleus of its additive inverse is
injective. Since the number of lines and points is both q2 + q + 1, this map is bijective.
□

4 The Arc Chromatic Number for Affine Planes
We now prove Theorem 1.5, concerning the arc chromatic number of affine planes.
For this section, we first provide a brief reminder on how to coordinatize AG(2, q).
We begin with the Galois projective plane PG(2, q), whose points are represented
by homogeneous coordinates (x : y : z), where x, y, z ∈GF(q) are not all zero. By
removing an arbitrary line, typically the line at infinity defined by z = 0, we obtain
the affine plane. For any point where z ̸= 0, we can normalize the coordinates to

We now describe the computational approach used to obtain the coloring for
AG(2, 8). This explanation is complemented by the experimental scripts available at
the GitHub repository [23]. We recommend consulting the source code for specific
implementation details regarding data structures and search heuristics.
Within our computational framework, we first developed a general-purpose finite
field (GF) factory for arithmetic in GF(2k) for k ∈{2, 3, 4}. This factory employs
explicit irreducible polynomials and supports efficient multiplication via binary poly-
nomial arithmetic. For an introduction to the computational aspects of this approach,
see Part 5 of [5].
Using this arithmetic foundation, we systematically generated the projective points
of PG(2, 2k) by normalizing homogeneous coordinates (x : y : z) to ensure a canon-
ical representative for each equivalence class, and then extracted the affine points as
those with z ̸= 0. Projective lines were constructed by enumerating normalized coef-
ficient triples and aggregating points satisfying the corresponding linear equations. A
determinant-based collinearity test using GF(2k) arithmetic was implemented.
The principal challenge in finding the coloring for AG(2, 8) was navigating the
immense 764 combinatorial search space. To address this, we designed and imple-
mented a backtracking solver enhanced by forward-checking and the Minimum
Remaining Values (MRV) heuristic, classical techniques pioneered by Haralick and
Elliot [18]. By performing early look-aheads and prioritizing the most constrained
points, the algorithm effectively prunes the search tree and avoids the exploration of
non-viable configurations. Despite the potential for exponential growth, the imple-
mentation proved remarkably efficient, converging to the coloring in the proof of
Proposition 4.3 in approximately ten seconds on a standard personal workstation.
To verify the correctness of the colorings, we also implemented a slow but easily
verifiable O(n3) determinant test to determine whether a set of n points in PG(2, q)
forms an arc.

discards all possibilities.
However, for n = 8, the framework exceeded our computational limits. To address
this, we adopted a different approach. In G8, an arc contains at most 16 points (at most
two points per row). Consequently, in an arc-proper 4-coloring of G8, each color class
must contain exactly 16 points. We implemented a recursive algorithm to enumerate
all possible arcs of size 16 in G8. After five minutes of computation on a standard
personal workstation, we identified all the 380 possible such arcs. Subsequently, a
backtracking solver was employed to determine whether four of these arcs could form
a partition of G8. A valid coloring was found almost instantaneously.
6 Discussion and Open Problems
Determining the precise value of the arc chromatic number for PG(2, 2k), where k ≥4,
remains an open problem.
Problem 6.1 Let k ≥4. Determine whether χA(AG(2, 2k)) = 2k or 2k −1.
Scaling our computational approach to q = 16 (k = 4) appears computationally
infeasible. The corresponding search space contains 15256 potential states, and even
with additional pruning techniques, we have been unable to adapt our methods to
handle this scale. However, the projective planes possess numerous symmetries that
remain underutilized in our current approach.
The determination of the arc chromatic numbers for Gn also remains an open
question.
Problem 6.2 Let n be a positive integer. Determine the value of χA(Gn).
Given the inherent complexity of the no-three-in-line problem, we anticipate that
this problem will also be challenging. Regarding the next smallest unresolved case,
our backtracking solver successfully identified an arc-proper 6-coloring for G9, while
the simple pigeonhole bound demonstrates that an arc-proper 4-coloring is impossible.
Thus, χA(G9) is either 5 or 6, but the exact value remains unknown.
