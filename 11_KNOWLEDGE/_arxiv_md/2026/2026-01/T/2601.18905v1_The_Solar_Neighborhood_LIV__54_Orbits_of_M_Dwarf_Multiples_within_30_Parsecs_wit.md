ABSTRACT
We present 1066 speckle measurements of M dwarf multiples observed over 2021–2024, all taken
with HRCam on the Southern Astrophysical Research 4.1 m telescope. Among these, 900 observations
resolve companions in 212 pairs, with separations spanning 17 milliarcseconds to 3.′′4 and brightness
differences ranging from 0 to 4.9 magnitudes in the I filter. We have characterized the orbits of 54 of
these companions, spanning periods of 0.67–30 yr, by combining our data with literature astrometry,
radial velocities, and, in four cases, Hipparcos-Gaia accelerations. Among the orbits presented here
are 28 that are the first-ever such characterizations for their systems, and 26 that revise previously-
published orbits, thus providing a significant update to the observed dynamics of M dwarfs in the
solar neighborhood. From these orbits, we provide new and updated dynamical total masses for these
systems, precise to 0.7–7% in nearly all cases. Future mass derivations for components in these systems
will contribute to efforts in refining the mass-luminosity relation for the smallest stars, and will enhance
investigations of age, magnetism, and metallicity effects on luminosities at a given mass.

1. INTRODUCTION
Orbits of binary and multiple stars contain valuable information on their origins and masses. A large ensemble of
known orbits can test models of formation and dynamical evolution of stellar systems, and comparing ensembles of
different stellar types can reveal their differing histories. For example, very low-mass binary systems with primary
stars ≲0.1 M⊙were shown by Dupuy & Liu (2017) to have less eccentric orbits at long periods than the systems with
primaries ∼1 M⊙in Raghavan et al. (2010). When sufficiently accurate masses are available for a set of stars in a
given mass regime, effects of age, magnetism, and metallicity can be discerned.
In this paper we focus on the ubiquitous M dwarfs, the stars that account for three of every four members of the solar
neighborhood (Henry & Jao 2024). Assembling a large number of orbits for these low mass stars has historically been
challenging due to their intrinsic faintness. Early systematic surveys complemented the historic visual discoveries of
binary M dwarfs and revealed candidates, along with a modest number of orbits, using infrared speckle imaging (Henry
& McCarthy 1990; Henry 1991; Leinert et al. 1997), while more recent efforts used adaptive optics at near-infrared
wavelengths (Ward-Duong et al. 2015; Mann et al. 2019). Large efforts have been undertaken in the past decade at
optical wavelengths that offer improved resolution limits for a given telescope aperture compared to observations made

Adding to these efforts, we are carrying out a southern hemisphere speckle interferometry campaign to map the orbits

this current paper covers observations over and focuses on 2021–2024 the 54 orbits we are now able to characterize
via these new data. In many cases the orbit calculations have been made possible by combining the SOAR results
with data from the other efforts discussed above. The sample of M dwarfs within 25 pc observed in our program is
described in §2. The observations and data reduction processes are reviewed in §3, and the results are presented in

Binary Stars (as of April 2025; Hartkopf et al. 2001). We also revise ∼9% of that catalog’s existing orbits.
2. SAMPLE
The selection of stars for this program was discussed in detail in Vrijmoet et al. (2022), so here we only briefly
summarize that process. Because the program aims to map the orbits of nearby M dwarf multiples, our targets are
all known binaries or higher-order systems, or stars that exhibit some evidence of potential multiplicity. Wide pairs
(≳2.′′5) have been excluded in order to focus on orbits shorter than 30 yr. Membership is also restricted to systems
within 25 pc, observable by SOAR (Decl. ≲+20◦), and with photometry indicating the primary to be an M dwarf
(V −Ks > 3.70 and MV > 9.02). For comparison, the blue and red cutoffs in V −Ks used to develop this sample
correspond to MG = 8.23 and 17.79, respectively, which are consistent with the magnitude range of MG = 8.1–17.8
for red dwarfs outlined in Henry & Jao (2024).
Three sources for targets were used: the RECONS1 long-term astrometry program at the CTIO/SMARTS 0.9 m
(Jao et al. 2005; Henry et al. 2018), the Sixth Catalog of Visual Binary Orbits (Hartkopf et al. 2001) that is consistently
updated as publications are added to the literature, and suspect systems (candidate multiples) selected from Gaia DR2
(Vrijmoet et al. 2020). The original full target list included 338 M dwarfs, but to focus on mapping orbits with likely
periods less than 30 yr, in March 2022 we pruned the sample to retain only the 229 pairs that had been successfully
resolved at SOAR. Of those resolved pairs, 212 are presented here; the remainder have not yet been re-observed, mainly
because their motions thus far have been slow.
The full list of 338 M dwarf targets in the original sample is given in Table 1 of Vrijmoet et al. (2022). The pruned,
continuing target list is constructed from those marked as resolved in that Table 1 (i.e., “Y” in their column 14),
plus those that were resolved after the publication of that work. Those new resolutions are indicated in this current
publication with one or two asterisks in column 2 of our Table 1 (discussed in further detail later in §3 and §4). For
consistency, the star system names used throughout this paper match those in the rest of the Solar Neighborhood
series and the internal RECONS catalog. Full identifiers (coordinates, names used in SIMBAD, etc.) are given in the
Appendix. M dwarfs within 25 pc are illustrated on the observational Hertzsprung-Russell diagram in Figure 1, in which
resolved pairs are marked with filled blue circles, unresolved with open blue circles, and pairs with orbits presented
here with black outlines. Resolved pairs are not overluminous in all cases, but are concentrated to MV ≲18 mag,
reflecting the magnitude limit of HRCam at SOAR.

camera (HRCam), using time awarded to the first two authors on this paper, supplemented with data secured during
a few engineering nights. The instrument, observing procedure, and data reduction are described in Tokovinin et al.

because orbit phases can be timed among all observing nights and because all programs share common astrometric

This SOAR M dwarf speckle program was started in 2019 July and has continued through 2025. The new data
included in this publication cover the SOAR observations from January 2021 through January 2025. Fast-moving M
dwarf pairs are prioritized, allowing for a better coverage of their orbits. On a single visit, typically two data cubes of
400 images each are taken; occasionally, additional cubes are taken, for example, under poor seeing or for faint targets.
Each frame in the cube has a short exposure time of 25 ms (50 ms for fainter stars), is 200 × 200 pixels in size, and
covers a field 3.′′15 square; for pairs known to be separated by more than ∼1.′′4, a wider 400 × 400 pixel field is used.
Nearly all images are taken in the broad I filter (790/130 nm bandwidth), which is very similar to a Cousins I filter,
with the transmission curve given in Tokovinin (2018a), Figure 2. Additionally, a handful of measures presented here
are in the Str¨omgren y filter.
A speckle power spectrum and autocorrelation function were computed for each data cube, and the existence of
a companion in those data was inferred from the fringes in the power spectrum and/or secondary peaks in the
autocorrelation function. Companion detections in two sequential data cubes safeguard against artefacts (e.g., cosmic-
ray hits), while the two binary-star measurements are checked for consistency and averaged. The three characteristics
of a stellar pair — separation, position angle, and magnitude difference — are determined by fitting a model to the
high-frequency portion of the power spectrum. A known single reference star was used for comparison in the cases of
close and/or high-contrast pairs to account for the intrinsic shape of the power spectrum. A similar fitting procedure
is used for processing triple stars. Approximate detection limits (resolution and contrast) are determined as well for

4. RESULTS
4.1. SOAR Measurements for M Dwarf Systems
In Vrijmoet et al. (2022) we published 830 optical speckle observations made primarily between 2019 July and 2020
December2 in the initial stages of this program. During that time window, a total of 333 M dwarfs were observed,
of which 211 were found to have resolved companions. Many of those resolved systems are continuing to be observed
and are among the systems with orbits reported here. All points used to calculate the orbits presented in this paper
are given in Table 1. Among these are 1697 observations made from 2021 January through 2025 January, roughly half
of which are listed for the first time in Table 1 with the others reported previously in the yearly SOAR speckle results
papers (Tokovinin et al. 2024, and references therein). Table 1 also includes all imaging data from the literature that
were used in orbit fits (§4.4). Several orbit fits also incorporated radial velocity data gathered from the literature, as
shown in Table 2, and the fits to those RV data are presented in Figure 9.

4.2. High-Resolution Measurements
High-resolution imaging results for our M dwarf targets are listed in Table 1. These results include:
• all SOAR observations from January 2021 through January 2025, both resolved and unresolved systems
• all literature astronometry that we used to fit orbits for 54 systems.
Each pair in Table 1 is identified by its WDS code in column 1; additional names and full coordinates can be found in
Table 6, and observed properties are in Table 1 of Vrijmoet et al. (2022). Each pair is a binary “AB,” except those with
explicit component labels such as “BC” or “Aa,Ab” that are subsets of triples or higher-order multiples. The names
are followed by the reference for its first resolution in column 2, with an asterisk (*) if we report the first resolution
here for a system already known to be binary through other methods, or a double asterisk (**) for a first resolution of a
pair not previously known as multiple. Systems that we reported as newly resolved in Vrijmoet et al. (2022) reference
that paper. Columns 3 and 4 list the Julian date of each observation (in decimal years) and whether the companion
was resolved (Y) or not (N). For resolved pairs, columns 5–7 give the companion’s separation ρ in arcseconds, position
angle θ in degrees (in the eastern direction, with respect to north at 0◦), and magnitude difference ∆m relative to the
primary star. These position angles are in the equinox of their observed epoch (they are later converted to J2000 if
used in orbit fitting; see §4.4). The ∆m measurement is omitted for results from the literature, given the diversity of
filters used by those other programs. If the target was unresolved, columns 8–10 outline the contrast curve with the
minimum possible separation resolvable ρmin, the maximum magnitude difference that could be detected 0.′′15 from
the primary, and the maximum magnitude difference 1.′′0 from the primary. The detection limits are approximate and
likely conservative, as revealed by the parameters of resolved pairs in Figure 2. Column 11 gives the q, p, and : flags
related to the SOAR observations, as outlined in §3. For SOAR observations with no flags, position angles should be
regarded as modulo 180◦, i.e., a system listed with position angle 20◦may instead have angle 200◦. Pairs with orbits
reported here (see column 13) have had that 180◦ambiguity removed in order to generate reliable orbits. Column 12
notes the reference for the listed observation, and column 13 marks with a checkmark results that were used in an
orbit fit (Table 4 and Figures 3–7). Note that overall, uncertainties on the SOAR measurements are typically ∼2 mas
in separations and ∼0.2◦in position angles.
4.3. Radial Velocity Measurements
Among the 54 pairs for which orbits are provided here, there are six that have astrometry data augmented by radial
velocity (RV) measurements used to improve their orbits. Here we describe pertinent details of each system’s RV data.
GJ 84 AB (WDS 02051−1737) — For this SB1 system, 10 RV points from 1998–2001 from HIRES on Keck and the
Hamilton spectrograph at Lick Observatory were used, as described in Nidever et al. (2002). Only a small portion of
the 15-year orbit is covered with RVs.
GJ 2060 AB (WDS 07289−3015 AB) — We used two sources of RV points for this SB1, together comprising 21 RVs
total. Rodet et al. (2018), providing 11 points, measured velocities in 2014 and 2016 using HARPS on the ESO 3.6-m
telescope at La Silla Observatory in Chile. Durkan et al. (2018) provided 10 points from FEROS at the ESO-MPG
2.2-m, also at La Silla. The combined RV data set spans 2005–2017, complementing the imaging data that mainly
cover that same period (2005–2024, plus 1991 from Hipparcos).
LHS 3117 AB (WDS 15474−1054) — This SB3 has 70 RV points spanning 2004–2020 from CARMENES on the
3.5-m at the Calar Alto Observatory (Baroch et al. 2021). We fit the system as an SB1, omitting the RVs from B
and C, to accommodate limitations in our routines for fitting RVs and astrometry jointly. The wealth of data for

is well-covered with RVs, resulting in a high-quality fit in which the orbital period is determined with an error of only
5 days.
GJ 791.2 AB (WDS 20298+0941 AB) — For this SB2, we used 12 RV points from the Sandiford Cassegrain Echelle
Spectrograph on the McDonald 2.1-m, as described in Benedict et al. (2016). The RV epochs span six orbits from
1995–2005, allowing the orbital period to be derived with an error of only a few hours.

In this work, an orbit fit is deemed reliable enough for publication when its orbital period is determined to better than
20% and its eccentricity to better than 8%. In several cases, the imaging data alone initially could not constrain the
orbit well, but the RECONS long-term astrometry of those systems (e.g., Henry et al. 2018; Vrijmoet et al. 2020) ruled
out a range of potential orbital periods and allowed a convincing orbit fit under those constraints. A comprehensive
assessment of the RECONS astrometry and how it can be used with resolved data such as that from SOAR will be
presented in a forthcoming publication.
Each system’s data were fit initially with the ORBIT code (Tokovinin 2016b). If Hipparcos data were also available,
then another fit was attempted using orvara (Brandt et al. 2021), which incorporates Hipparcos and Gaia proper
motions as additional constraints to the data. When using ORBIT or orvara, the uncertainties on each data point were
set to the typical value of 2 mas for HRCam observations (or 6 mas for data flagged as noisy as described in §3), or
set to the published errors for literature data. Both codes convert the data’s position angles to equinox J2000 when
commencing their fits.
In addition to using different combinations of data, the two orbit-fitting codes use different methodologies for
selecting their orbits. Briefly, ORBIT weights the data inversely to measurement uncertainties and uses a Levenberg-
Marquardt least-squares algorithm to determine the best-fitting model. orvara takes the same input as ORBIT but
also incorporates any change in the system’s proper motion measured between the Hipparcos and Gaia EDR3 catalogs,
then reports the distribution of models it generated using a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm. We take
the medians of those parameter posteriors as the best fit. An advantage of the simpler ORBIT algorithm is that it only
takes seconds to converge, allowing for repeated runs with various starting values to inform the quality of the fit. In
contrast, orvara takes 15–30 minutes per target, depending on the size of the dataset and requested Markov chains,
but each run’s MCMC visualizations provide a more comprehensive view of the models’ suitability. When both ORBIT
and orvara agree on a fit, we report the orvara result here because it includes the additional data from Hipparcos and
Gaia. If the two codes did not arrive at the same result, we conclude that the system’s orbit is too poorly constrained
to publish at this time.
Only four orvara orbits survived the above process and are presented here. This is primarily because only a small
fraction of our targets were bright enough to be included in Hipparcos, and thus they cannot have Hipparcos-Gaia
accelerations. Of those that did have the data to attempt an orvara fit, most had orbital periods greater than 30 years,
which is too long to be considered for our project.
4.5. Orbits with High-Resolution Imaging and Radial Velocity Data
For the six systems using combined high-resolution and RV data, we completed fits using the IDL code ORBIT
(Tokovinin 2016b). As outlined in §4.3, the systems are SB1s and SB2s (and SB3s that we fit as SB1s and SB2s),
and the orbital coverage varies from a small portion of one orbit to six orbital cycles. Thus, the available RV coverage
permits different levels of improvement. One measure that characterizes how much the RV data improves orbits is the
relative drop in uncertainties of the orbital parameters when using only the imaging data vs. incorporating the RV
data. For the six cases here, we ran ORBIT with and without the RV data, and found that including that supplemental
RV data had effects ranging from negligible to significant. For example, GJ 84 AB and LHS 3117 AB had e errors
≪1% regardless of RV data, while the e error for GJ 791.2 AB dropped from 11% (without RVs) to 0.9% (with RVs),
and for LTT 17066 AB it dropped from 27% to 2.4%.
5. DISCUSSION
With 1697 observations of 338 M dwarf pairs in this program (combining this paper and Vrijmoet et al. 2022), plus
orbits derived for 54 of these systems, this work constitutes a significant contribution to the growing field of low-mass
multiple star astronomy. Here we add 28 new orbits to the 257 orbits for nearby M dwarfs (within 25 pc) in the
Sixth Catalog of Visual Binary Orbits (Hartkopf et al. 2001), an increase of 11%. We also update 26 orbits, providing
improvements for another 8.5% of those cataloged low-mass systems. Even for the 161 resolved systems that still
do not have enough data to determine orbits, the astrometry points presented here represent critical steps toward
determining their orbits in the coming years.
These orbits, both new and improved, will contribute toward our program’s ultimate goal to determine the properties
of low-mass systems’ dynamics as described in Vrijmoet et al. (2020). In particular, we aim to plot Porb vs. e for M
dwarf multiples to draw conclusions regarding how these systems formed and dynamically evolved.

6. CONCLUSIONS
Since 1 January 2021, we have taken 1697 observations of 338 targets at SOAR with the speckle instrument HRCam.
These data previously revealed nearly 100 new binaries (Vrijmoet et al. 2022), and in this current update we have fit
the orbits of 54 of those targets. Among those orbits, 28 are the first such characterizations for those systems, and 26
are updated from earlier efforts. Many of these results were made possible by the rich data set of incremental results
already available in the literature; the positions from HRCam are easily combined with similar astrometry from other
instruments, as well as radial velocities.
The 54 orbits in this current paper are all 30 yr or shorter to contribute to our project exploring orbital periods and
eccentricities for systems on those timescales (Vrijmoet et al. 2020). We will continue to follow the several dozen of
our 338 targets having no orbits presented here, concentrating primarily on the binaries with orbital periods longer
than a decade, where good orbits are lacking. Building this repository of incremental results will also be critical for
enhancing the utility of future time-series data from the Gaia mission and the Legacy Survey of Space and Time from
Rubin Observatory (Ivezi´c et al. 2019). Our study will ultimately include ∼200 orbits spanning multiple observing
techniques and orbital periods of 1 d to 30 yr, and the comprehensive evaluation of those results will be presented in a
future paper. Only through consistent effort, and the combined data sets from various facilities, can we fully explore
the architectures of low mass stellar systems out to the scales of Jupiter and Saturn’s orbits in our Solar System.
