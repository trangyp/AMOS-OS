Abstract
Feder-Vardi conjecture, which proposed that every finite-domain Con-
straint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) is either in P or it is NP-complete,
has been solved independently by Bulatov and Zhuk almost ten years ago.
Bodirsky-Pinsker conjecture which states a similar dichotomy for count-
ably infinite first-order reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous struc-
tures is wide open.
In this paper, we prove that CSPs over first-order expansions of finitely
bounded homogeneous model-complete cores are either first-order defin-
able (and hence in non-uniform AC0) or L-hard under first-order reduc-
tion. It is arguably the most general complexity dichotomy when it comes
to the scope of structures within Bodirsky-Pinsker conjecture. Our strat-
egy is that we first give a new proof of Larose-Tesson theorem, which
provides a similar dichotomy over finite structures, and then generalize
that new proof to infinite structures.
Keywords: constraint satisfaction problems, finitely bounded homogeneous structure,
infinite structures, first-order definability, L-hardness, computational complexity, di-
chotomies

Instead of working on a single problem, it is reasonable to work on the whole fam-
ily of problems admitting a uniform formulation. A general formalism which allows
the formulation of many natural computation problems is the Constraint Satisfaction
Problem CSP(A) parametrized by a relational structure A. Every A contributes to a
different computational problem, e.g., a Boolean satisfiability problem, graph coloring
or solving equations over a finite field. An instance of CSP(A) is a set of constraints
built out of variables and relations in A. The goal is to satisfy all constraints simulta-
neously by the same assignment to variables. Feder-Vardi conjecture [FV98] settled in
2017 independently by Bulatov [Bul17] and Zhuk [Zhu17,Zhu20] states that whenever
A is finite, then CSP(A) is either in P or it is NP-complete. That result stands in
the opposition to Ladner’s theorem [Lad75] on the existence of NP-intermediate prob-
lems on the condition that P⊊NP. Although obtaining the dichotomy was the original
motivation, the algorithms for all polynomial-time CSP(A) are arguably of not less
importance. They generalize Gaussian elimination [IMM+10] and local-consistency
techniques [BK14] ubiquitous in constraint solving. Thus, they provide the same way
of solving many natural problems in P. On the other hand a simple algebraic condi-
tion provides a polynomial time gadget reduction from monotone 1-in-3-SAT to any
NP-complete CSP(A) [BJK05]. Thereby, the dichotomy proofs provide a deep insight
into the nature of the class NP.
Although the holy grail of the CSP community has been achieved, it is not the
end of the story. Indeed, there is a number of active research directions generalizing
CSP(A) over finite structures A. In particular, Bodirsky-Pinsker conjecture [BPP21]
extends Feder-Vardi conjecture to some well-behaved infinite structures A which cover
a number of problems not expressible as CSP(A) if A is finite, e.g., in temporal and spa-
tial reasoning [BK10,MMZ15], phylogenetic reconstruction [BJP17,CM23] and graph
theory [BP15, BMPP19]. The well-behaved structures A are described in two steps.
We start with a Fra¨ıss´e class [Hod93] of finite structures C given by a finite number
of finite structures (obstructions) which are forbidden to embed into the members of
C. The Fra¨ıss´e limit of C is the basic structure B. By Fra¨ıss´e theorem the structure
B is homogeneous1 and by the choice of C it is finitely bounded. Then, one considers
and asks about the complexity of CSP(A) for structures A first-order definable in B
known as first-order reducts of B.
Conjecture 1.1. (Bodirsky-Pinsker)
For every finite-signature first-order reduct
A of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure B, CSP(A) is in P or it is NP-complete.
Although not resolved in full generality, the conjecture has been confirmed in
the number of particular cases such as all first-order reducts of (Q, <), known also
as temporal (constraint) languages [BK10], first-order reducts of the so-called rooted
triple relation, known also as phylogeny languages [BJP17], or all first-order reducts
of all homogeneous graphs [BP15,BMPP19]. Another notable classification considers
CSPs within the conjecture expressible in the logic MMSNP [BMM21]. Furthermore,
it is worth mentioning that [MP24] offers some new algebraic tools which simplify
providing similar partial classifications. However, despite all these attempts, it is fair
to say that the general conjecture is wide open.
At the same time, the boundary between P and NP-complete has not been the
only one studied for finite-domain CSPs [BKL08]. In [LT09] one can find a number
of not yet resolved conjectures on which CSPs are, e.g., in L (logarithmic space) or
NL (non-deterministic logarithmic space). The questions have not been yet answered

despite certain partial results available in the literature, see e.g. [BKW12, EHLR14,
DEH+15,CDK08]. Furthermore, [LT09] contains the following dichotomy built on the
characterization of first-order definable finite-domain CSPs from [LLT07].
Theorem 1.2. (Larose-Tesson) Let A be a finite structure over a finite signature.
Then one of the following holds.
• CSP(A) is L-hard (under first-order reduction).
• CSP(A) is first-order definable, and in non-uniform AC0.
Recall that non-uniform AC0 is the complexity class consisting of all languages
accepted by polynomial-size constant-depth families of Boolean circuits and that every
first-order definable decision problem is in that class (see, e.g. Theorem 6.4 in [Lib04]).
Moreover, there are problems in L which are not in non-uniform AC0 [FSS84], i.e.,
non-uniform AC0 ⊊L. In particular, any problem which is L-hard under first-order
reduction cannot lie in non-uniform AC0.
Since the question on the P versus NP-complete dichotomy for first-order reducts of
finitely-bounded homogeneous structures is notoriously resistant and the conjectures
from [LT09] on CSPs in L and NL are not even solved for finite structures, it is
reasonable to ask if at least Theorem 1.2 may be generalized. In this paper we achieve
this goal for a huge class of structures within Conjecture 1.1.
Theorem 1.3. (Main theorem) Let A be a finite-signature first-order expansion of a
finitely bounded homogeneous model-complete core B. Then one of the following holds.
• CSP(A) is L-hard (under first-order reduction).
• CSP(A) is first-order definable, and is in non-uniform AC0.
The difference in the formulation of the above theorem and Conjecture 1.1 is that
in the former case we start with a finitely bounded homogeneous structure B which is
already a model-complete core and then expand it with some extra relations. Thus,
A contains all relations in B. On the other hand, in the conjecture, we start with a
finitely bounded homogeneous structure and then we take its first-order reduct (thus,
we can forget some relations in B). In general, it is now allowed to take a model-
complete core of A but it is not even clear if it will be a reduct of a finitely bounded
homogeneous structure — see Question (5) on the list of open problem in [Bod21], in
Section 14.2.
Example 1.4. The order (Q; <) over rational numbers is a finitely bounded homo-
geneous model-complete core.
Theorem 1.3 covers all its expansions by first-order
definable relations. In this case all corresponding CSPs are L-hard, in fact even NL-
hard.
On the other hand, consider (V ; E, N) which is the expansion of the countable
universal homogeneous graph (or the countable homogeneous triangle-free graph) by
the non-edge relation which holds on different vertices not connected by an edge. Its
CSP is first-order definable (and hence in non-uniform AC0) but, as we will see, some
its first-order expansions give rise to L-hard CSPs.
In order to prove Theorem 1.3, we first provide a new proof of Theorem 1.2 and
then generalize the new proof to infinite structures covered by Theorem 1.3.

1.1
Proof strategy
Finite duality.
Throughout the paper, we freely change the perspective on CSP(A)
seeing its instances sometimes as sets of constraints over relations in A to be solved
and sometimes as structures C over the same signature as A which are to be verified
whether they homomorphically map to A or not. Using the latter perspective, one
can show that CSP(A) is first-order definable if and only if A has finite duality, i.e., a
finite set of obstructions O such that C admits a homomorphism to A if and only if
no D ∈O maps homomorphically to A. For the result, see Theorem 5.6.2 in [Bod21]
proved using Rossman preservation theorem [Ros08]. For finite structures, we first
knew this characterization of first-order definable CSPs thanks to Atserias [Ats05].
A-definitions
Further, we observe that a finite core structure A containing the
equality = and all constants in its signature gives rise to an L-hard CSP(A). A first-
order reduction from Graph Unreachability can be now simply achieved by marking a
constant s in A as a source, some other constant t as a target and introduce a constraint
x = y between vertices connected by an edge in the graph. Therefore, as it is sometimes
the case, we cannot include the equality to the signature of A if it is not explicitly
there, and we cannot use primitive positive definitions with a free use of equality as a
reduction between considered CSPs. Hence, instead of primitive positive formulae we
use what we call A-formulae where only atoms built out of relational symbols in the
signature of A are used. We show, using the known results, that A-definitions (provided
by A-formulae) give us first-order reductions between the corresponding CSPs.
Proof sketch for Theorem 1.2
Without loss of generality, we assume that A
is a core and contains all constants. An A-formula ϕ(x, y) with free variables x and
y is called a (C, x, D, y)-implication in A if C, D are A-definable, strictly contained in
the projections of ϕ to x and y, respectively, and if D = {y | ∃x ∈C ∧R(x, y)}. Such
an implication is called balanced if C = D and the projections of ϕ to both variables
agree. Now, if there is an A-definition of a balanced implication, then we can also
A-define an equivalence relation on a subset of the domain A of A with at least two
equivalence classes which together with all constants naturally admits a first-order
reductions from Graph Unreachability. On the other hand, if a balanced implication
is not A-definable, then CSP(A) is solvable by (1)-minimality algorithm, also known
as generalized arc-consistency.
We reformulate this well-known algorithm, so that
we could produce what we call tree A-formulae defining every domain, also empty,
deduced by it on an instance of CSP(A). A tree A-formula defining an empty-set over
an unsatisfiable instance gives rise to an obstruction. We show that the number of
indistinguishable obstructions is finite or from a huge enough tree A-formula witnessing
the unsatisfiability of some instance, we may produce a balanced implication.

Our main contribution is proved in a similar
fashion. The main differences is that we need to consider implications to be formulae
over a pair of tuples of variables rather than a pair of variables.

In general, a finite-signature finitely bounded homogeneous model-complete core
B is k-homogeneous and ℓ-bounded for some k, ℓ∈N. For its first-order expansion

as we prove, CSP(A) may be solved by establishing (k, max(k, ℓ))-minimality. The

stances, and hence a finite set of obstruction or we can A-define an implication as
in the examples above. Although, based on similar arguments as the proof for finite
structures, the proof for infinite structures appears to be much more involved when it
comes to details.
Implications in other papers.
Although the new treatment of the minimality
algorithms and obtaining dualities or balanced implication from k-tree A-formulae are
new to this paper, implications themselves have been used before. Over finite struc-
tures, e.g. in [BK14], one writes C + R to denote D = {y | ∃x ∈C R(x, y)}. It
is, unfortunately, somehow troublesome to generalize this notation to infinite struc-
tures where one has to deal also with non-binary relations or formulae over more
than two free variables.
It is why we stick here to implications defined as above
(and also in more details below). We note that they were also used in research on
bounded strict width [Wro20b, Wro20a, NP24] and the tractability of quasi Jonsson
operations [NPW25] over infinite structures.

Significance and the future work
Our main result, Theorem 1.3, is arguably the most general dichotomy theorem when it
comes to the scope of structures within Bodirsky-Pinsker conjecture. The theorem was

obtained by first reproving the analogous result for finite structures and then by gen-

old proofs for finite structures are not easily generalizable to infinite structure, so
what about providing new proofs that can be easier generalized?
This way of thinking brings a new hope for open problems over infinite-domain

generalize it to first-order reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous structures. Such
a new proof may be of course hard to obtain straightaway but we believe that the
methods from this paper may be used to identify both finite and infinite structures of
bounded width (solvable by the minimality algorithm) that give rise to CSPs in NL
and L.

Preliminaries
We write [n] for the set {1, . . . , n}. Most of the definition from below may be also find

2.1
Structures and formulae
All relational structures in this article are assumed to be at most countable. Let A
be a relational structure, we denote the domain of A by a plain letter A. Let ϕ be a
first-order formula over the signature of A and free variables Var(ϕ). We identify the
interpretation ϕA of ϕ in A with the set of satisfying assignments a : Var(ϕ) →A for
ϕ. Let u = (u1, . . . , uk) be a tuple of elements of Var(ϕ). We define the projection of
ϕA onto u as the relation proju(ϕA) := {f(u) | f ∈ϕA} where f(u) is a shorthand for
(f(u1), . . . , f(uk)) used throughout the text.
A first-order expansion of a structure B is an expansion of B by relations which
are first-order definable in B, i.e., for which there exists ϕ over free variables u such
that R = {f(u) | f ∈ϕA}.
We write Aut(A) to denote the set of automorphisms of A. An orbit of a k-tuple
a = (a1, . . . , ak) in A where a1, . . . , ak ∈A is the set {α(a) | α ∈Aut(A)}. An orbit
O is injective if the projection of O to no two coordinates is equality. A countable
relational structure A is ω-categorical if its automorphism group is oligomorphic, i.e.,
for all n ≥1, the number of orbits of n-tuples in A is finite. For an ω-categorical
relational structure A and for every n ≥1 it holds that two n-tuples of elements of
the structure are in the same orbit in A if and only if they have the same type, i.e.,
they satisfy the same first-order formulae. The atomic type of an n-tuple is the set of
all atomic formulae satisfied by this tuple; the structure A is homogeneous if any two
6


## Page 7


tuples of the same atomic type belong to the same orbit. For k ≥1, we say that a
relational structure A is k-homogeneous if for all tuples a, b of arbitrary finite equal
length, if all k-subtuples of a and b are in the same orbit in A, then a and b are in
the same orbit in A. If a homogeneous A has a finite signature, then it is ω-categorical
and k-homogeneous for some k ∈N. For ℓ≥1, we say that A is ℓ-bounded if for every
finite X, if all substructures Y of X of size at most ℓembed in A, then X embeds in
A. A structure A is finitely bounded if it is ℓ-bounded for some ℓ. In this case there
is a finite set of structures FA over at most ℓelements such that some finite structure
embeds into A if and only if no structure from FA embeds into the structure.
A structure A is a model-complete core if for all endomorphisms of A and all
finite subsets of its domain there exists an automorphism of A which agrees with the
endomorphism on the subset. If A is finite and satisfies this condition, we simply say
that it is a core.
A first-order formula is called primitive positive (pp) if it is built exclusively from
atomic formulae, existential quantifiers, and conjunctions. Primitive positive formulae
usually allow equalities even if they are not in the signature of A.
We say that a
first-order formula is an A-formula for a relational structure A if it is a pp-formula
which allows only atomic formulae built from relational symbols in the signature of
A. A relation is pp-definable or A-definable in a relational structure if it is first-order
definable by a pp-formula or an A-formula, respectively.
For any ω-categorical model-complete core A, for all n ≥1, all orbits of n-tuples in
A are pp-definable. Furthermore, if the orbit O in A is injective, then one can define it
by an A-formula. Indeed, let ϕ be a pp-definition of O. Then the variables occurring
in the equalities may be partitioned so that any two variables in the same partition
have to be always assigned to the same value. In order to obtain an A-formula of O,
for every partition, all variables in the partition are replaced by the same variable.

Let A be a relational structure. An instance of CSP(A) is a pair I = (V, C), where
V is a finite set of variables and C is a finite set of constraints; for every constraint
C ∈C, there exists a non-empty set U ⊆V called the scope of C, and denoted by
S(C), such that C ⊆AU, and such that C can be viewed as a relation of A by totally
ordering U, i.e., there exists an enumeration u1, . . . , uk of the elements of U and a
k-ary relation R of A such that for all f : U →A, it holds that f ∈C if, and only if,
(f(u1), . . . , f(uk)) ∈R. The relational structure A is called the template of the CSP.
A solution of a CSP instance I = (V, C) is a mapping f : V →A such that for every
C ∈C with scope U, f|U ∈C.
An instance I = (V, C) of CSP(A) is non-trivial if it does not contain any empty
constraint; otherwise, it is trivial. Given a constraint C ⊆AU with U ⊆V and a tuple
v ∈U k for some k ≥1, the projection of C onto v is defined by projv(C) := {f|S(v) |
f ∈C}.

very same constraint, it is denoted by proju(I) and called the projection of I onto u.
Let 1 ≤k ≤ℓ, let A be an ω-categorical structure. Clearly not every instance
I = (V, C) of CSP(A) is (k, ℓ)-minimal. However, every instance I is equivalent to a
(k, ℓ)-minimal instance I′ of CSP(A′) where A′ is the expansion of A by all at most
ℓ-ary A-definable relations in the sense that I and I′ have the same solution set. In
particular, we have that if I′ is trivial, then I has no solutions. Moreover, I′ can
be computed from I in polynomial time. See e.g., Section 2.3 in [MNPW24] for the
description of the algorithm which will be called (k, l)-minimality algorithm in this
paper. We say that (k, l)-minimality algorithm solves CSP(A) if it transforms a given
instance I into a trivial instance if and only if I does not have a solution. In this case,
one says that A has relational width (k, l). If A has relational width (k, k), then it has
relational width k.
Alternatively, an instance I of CSP(A) may be seen as a finite structure over the
same signature as A. One can transform a set of constraints C over variables V into
an appropriate structure just by taking V as its domain and including a tuple u of
variables into RI always when there is a constraint C ⊆AU with S(u) = U such that
RA = {f(u) | f ∈C}. On the other hand a structure I may be transformed into
the appropriate set of constraints by taking its domain as a set of variables V and
including a constraint C ⊆AU with C satisfying RA = {f(u) | f ∈C} always when
RI contains u.
Definition 2.2. Let A be a τ-structure. A set of of finite τ-structures is called an
obstruction set for A if, for any finite τ-structure C, we have that C maps homomor-
phically to A if and only if no D ∈O maps homomorphically to C.
We say that a structure has finite duality if it has a finite obstruction set.

5
Summary and Future Work
The main contribution of this paper is the dichotomy between non-uniform AC0 and
L-hard for all finite-signature first-order expansions of finitely bounded homogeneous
structures — Theorem 1.3. In order to obtain the result we first reproved Theorem 1.2
and then generalized this new proof to infinite structures. We believe that the meth-
ods provided may be used further, in particular, to approach the following research
question.
Research Question 1. Which first-order reducts of k-homogeneous ℓ-bounded struc-
tures of relational width (k, l) give rise to CSPs in NL?
It is interesting in its own rights — a number of infinite-domain CSPs are solvable
by local consistency methods [BD13] but it is also directly connected to an open
question over finite-domain CSPs. Indeed, it is still not known which finite-domain
CSPs are P-complete, and which are in NL. In particular, we do not it even know for
problems solvable by 1-minimality algorithm. On one hand, in this class of problems,
we have the P-complete Horn-3SAT, which is CSP({0, 1}, R0, R1, RHorn-3SAT) with
R0 = {(0)}, R1 = {(1)} and RHorn-3SAT = {(x, y, z) ∈{0, 1}3 | (x∧y =⇒z)}, and on
the other hand, for instance, structures with finite duality studied in this paper. One
can imagine that we could use methods developed in this paper to answer the question.
We would just need new hardness criteria and a way to obtain a set of obstructions with
bounded pathwidth when the criteria are violated. Then we could try to generalize
the dichotomy to infinite structures solvable by (k, l)-minimality algorithm.
