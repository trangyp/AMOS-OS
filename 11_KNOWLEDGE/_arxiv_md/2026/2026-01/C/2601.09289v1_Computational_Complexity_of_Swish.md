Abstract
Swish is a card game in which players are given cards having symbols (hoops and balls), and find a
valid superposition of cards, called a “swish.” Dailly, Lafourcade, and Marcadet (FUN 2024) studied
a generalized version of Swish and showed that the problem is solvable in polynomial time with one
symbol per card, while it is NP-complete with three or more symbols per card. In this paper, we
resolve the previously open case of two symbols per card, which corresponds to the original game.
We show that Swish is NP-complete for this case. Specifically, we prove the NP-hardness when the
allowed transformations of cards are restricted to a single (horizontal or vertical) flip or 180-degree
rotation, and extend the results to the original setting allowing all three transformations. In contrast,
when neither transformation is allowed, we present a polynomial-time algorithm. Combining known
and our results, we establish a complete characterization of the computational complexity of Swish
with respect to both the number of symbols per card and the allowed transformations.

game, cards are overlaid so that every hoop (circle) ⃝meets a ball (point) •, every ball meets
a hoop, and neither two hoops nor two balls overlap; such a configuration is called a swish.
The commercial version of this game consists of 60 transparent cards, each with exactly
one hoop and exactly one ball arranged on a grid of height 4 and width 3 (see Figure 1 for
examples). Cards may be flipped horizontally or vertically and rotated by 180 degrees, which

Rowland [12] initiated a mathematical study of Swish, focusing on structural and
probabilistic aspects of the commercial deck. Using Burnside’s theorem, she showed that the
60-card deck contains 36 distinct cards up to symmetry. She further analyzed the distribution
of symbol positions, showing that they do not appear uniformly, and studied the probability of
forming a swish with two cards. Swish was also discussed from a linear-algebraic perspective
via matrix representations.
From a computational complexity viewpoint, Swish was first studied systematically by
Dailly, Lafourcade, and Marcadet [3] in FUN 2024. They introduced a generalized version of
Swish and analyzed its complexity with respect to the number of symbols per card. They
showed that Swish can be solved in polynomial time when each card contains a single symbol,
whereas the problem becomes NP-complete when cards may contain three or more symbols.
To establish NP-completeness, they also considered a restricted variant in which cards cannot
be flipped or rotated, often referred to as Simple-Swish.
In this paper, we complete the complexity classification of Swish by addressing two
aspects that remain open. The first concern is the number of symbols per card: what is
the complexity of Swish when each card contains exactly two symbols, as in the original
game? The second concern is the role of geometric transformations: how does the complexity
change when flip and rotation are selectively allowed or forbidden?
We show a sharp boundary. When neither flip nor rotation is allowed, Swish with two
symbols per card admits a polynomial-time algorithm (Theorem 1). In contrast, if even a
single type of transformation (i.e., one of the horizontal flip, vertical flip, and 180-degree
rotation) is allowed, the problem becomes NP-complete, already with one hoop and one ball
per card (Theorem 2). As a consequence, Swish remains NP-complete in these settings even
when cards may contain more symbols. Together with previous results, this establishes a
complete characterization of the computational complexity of Swish with respect to both
the number of symbols per card and the allowed symmetries.

Preliminaries
2.1
Swish
We formalize a generalization of Swish in terms of the size of cards. Fix an h × w integer
grid, where (i, j) (1 ≤i ≤h, 1 ≤j ≤w) denotes the point i-th from the top and j-th from
the left; h = 4 and w = 3 in the original Swish. As we mainly focus on the case where
each card has exactly one hoop and exactly one ball, each card c is specified as an ordered
pair of two points on the grid, ((i1, j1), (i2, j2)), which means that c has a hoop ⃝at (i1, j1)
and a ball • at (i2, j2). If we are allowed to rotate a card c = ((i1, j1), (i2, j2)), we can use
c as a card cR := ((h −i1 + 1, w −j1 + 1), (h −i2 + 1, w −j2 + 1)); here, it is restricted to
180-degree rotation even when h = w. If we are allowed to flip c, we can use c as a card
cF := ((i1, w −j1 + 1), (i2, w −j2 + 1)); here, it is restricted to horizontal flip by symmetry
(because if both horizontal and vertical flips are allowed, their combination results in the
180-degree rotation). If we are allowed both to rotate and to flip c, we can use c as, in
addition to cR and cF, a card cRF = ((h −i1 + 1, j1), (h −i2 + 1, j2)), which coincides with
the card obtained by flipping c vertically.
Suppose that a set C of cards is given. A subset S ⊆C of cards is called a swish if the
following conditions are satisfied by flipping or/and rotating some of the cards in S if they
are allowed: for every point p on the grid, either
no card in S has a hoop and no card in S has a ball at p, or
exactly one card in S has a hoop and exactly one card in S has a ball at p.
See Figure 1 again for examples.
The objective of the original game is to find a large swish. Then, we can consider natural
decision problems as follows:
Swish
Input: A set C of cards on the h × w grid and an integer k.
Goal: Decide whether there is a swish S ⊆C such that |S| ≥k or not, where both
flipping and rotating any cards are allowed.
Swish without Flip
Input: A set C of cards on the h × w grid and an integer k.
Goal: Decide whether there is a swish S ⊆C such that |S| ≥k or not, where rotating
any cards is allowed but flipping them is not.
Swish without Rotation
Input: A set C of cards on the h × w grid and an integer k.
Goal: Decide whether there is a swish S ⊆C such that |S| ≥k or not, where flipping
any cards is allowed but rotating them is not.
Swish without Flip or Rotation
Input: A set C of cards on the h × w grid and an integer k.
Goal: Decide whether there is a swish S ⊆C such that |S| ≥k or not, where neither
flipping nor rotating any cards is allowed.

2.2
Graphs
We use the standard terms and notation on graphs. An undirected edge between two vertices
u and v is denoted by an unordered pair {u, v}, and a directed edge from u to v is denoted
by an ordered pair (u, v).
In a graph (either undirected or directed), a matching is an edge subset such that each
vertex has at most one incident edge in it. A matching is called perfect if all the vertices
have exactly one incident edge. When each edge is assigned with a weight value, the weight
of an edge subset is defined as the sum of the weights of edges therein.
In a directed graph, the in-degree of vertex v is the number of edges incoming to v, and
the out-degree is the number of edges outgoing from v. A directed cycle is a connected
directed graph in which every vertex has both in-degree and out-degree exactly 1. The length
of a directed cycle is the number of vertices (or edges, equivalently) therein.
3
Algorithm and Complexity
The main results of this paper, solving an open problem in [3], are stated as follows:
▶Theorem 1. Swish without Flip or Rotation is solved in polynomial time.
▶Theorem 2. Swish, Swish without Flip, and Swish without Rotation are NP-
complete.
Note that all the problems are clearly in NP, since if we are given a swish S with additional
information of how to use each card in S (flipping or/and rotating it or not), we can easily
confirm its validity in polynomial time.
3.1
Polynomial-Time Algorithm for Swish without Flip or Rotation
In this section, we prove Theorem 1.
Let (C, k) be an instance of Swish without Flip or Rotation, i.e., C is a set of cards
and k is an integer. We construct from C a bipartite graph G = (V +, V −; E) with edge
weight w: E →R as follows (see Figure 2).
First, let V be the set of points on the grid. For each point p ∈V , we create two copies
p+ and p−. Let V + := { p+ | p ∈V } and V −:= { p−| p ∈V }.
Next, for each card c = (p, q) ∈C, we create an edge ec = {p+, q−} of weight w(ec) = 1.
Also, for each point p ∈V , we create an edge fp = {p+, p−} of weight w(fp) = 0. Let E be
the set of created edges.
Since it is well-known that a maximum-weight perfect matching in a bipartite graph can
be computed in polynomial time [8], the following claim completes the proof.
▷Claim 3.
There is a swish S ⊆C such that |S| = k without flipping or rotating any cards
if and only if (G, w) has a perfect matching of weight k.
Proof. Let S ⊆C be a swish such that |S| = k without flipping or rotating any cards. We
construct a perfect matching M ⊆E in G with w(M) = k. For each card c = (p, q) ∈S, we
add the edge ec = {p+, q−} of weight 1 to M. Then, as S is a swish without flip or rotation,
for each point p ∈V , either
both p+ and p−have no incident edge of the current M, or
both p+ and p−have exactly one incident edge of the current M.

NP-hardness of Swish without Rotation

will be completed based on this proof in the next section.

graph G = (V, E), we call an edge subset F ⊆E an even dicycle-factor if F covers all vertices
of V and each connected component of F is a directed cycle of even length (see Figure 3).

3.3
NP-hardness of Swish
In this section, we prove that Swish and Swish without Flip are NP-hard as well as
Swish without Rotation shown in the previous section. The proof is almost the same,
and the only difference is the coordination of points on the grid.
In the previous proof, we used the |V | × 4 grid, where each point (v, j) (v ∈V, 1 ≤j ≤4)
is denoted simply by vj. Here we consider arranging these points on a single vertical line.
Specifically, let h := 4|V | and w := 1, and name the 4|V | points on the h × w grid using vj
(v ∈V, 1 ≤j ≤4) so that the point (i, 1) is named vj if and only if the point (h −i + 1, 1)
is named v5−j. Then, the horizontal flip becomes meaningless, and the rotation plays the
same role as the horizontal flip in the previous section. Thus, the same reduction works for
this setting (rotation is allowed and horizontal flip is either allowed or not), which completes
the proof of Theorem 2.
4
Concluding Remarks
In this paper, we have completed the complexity classification of Swish by resolving the
previously open case of two symbols per card. Our results show that the computational
complexity of Swish is under interplay between the number of symbols per card and the
geometric transformations allowed on cards.
In particular, we have identified a sharp
boundary: when neither flip nor rotation is allowed, Swish with two symbols per card can be
solved in polynomial time, whereas allowing even a single type of flip or 180-degree rotation
already makes the problem NP-complete. Together with earlier results, this establishes a
complete picture of the complexity of Swish across all natural variants of the game.

Computational Complexity of Swish
Beyond decision complexity, an intriguing line of research concerns the structure of card
sets that admit no swish. Dailly, Lafourcade, and Marcadet [3] initiated a systematic study
of such no-swish configurations. In their generalized setting, they constructed large families
of card sets containing no swish, achieving asymptotically high densities. Moreover, for the
original commercial deck of 60 cards, they identified a no-swish subset of size 28.
A natural and still open question is whether this value 28 is optimal. Is it possible to
find a larger no-swish subset within the original Swish deck, or does every set of 29 cards
necessarily admit a swish? More broadly, determining tight bounds on the size of no-swish
sets for fixed decks or restricted symmetry models appears to be a promising direction for
future work, which would deepen our understanding of the combinatorial structure underlying
the game and could require techniques different from those used in the generalized setting.
