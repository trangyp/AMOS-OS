Libby-Fox perturbations and the analytic adjoint solution for 
laminar viscous flow along a flat plate 
Carlos Lozano and Jorge Ponsin 
Aerodynamics Department, National Institute of Aerospace 
Technology (INTA), Spain 
Abstract  
The properties of the solution to the adjoint two-dimensional boundary layer equations on a flat plate 
are investigated from the viewpoint of Libby-Fox theory that describes the algebraic perturbations to 
the Blasius boundary layer. The adjoint solution is obtained from the Green’s function of the 
perturbation equation as a sum over the infinite perturbation modes of the Blasius solution. The 
analysis of the solution allows us to derive constraints on the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. The 
extension of the analysis to the case with non-zero pressure gradient, corresponding to the Falkner-
Skan solution, is also briefly discussed.    
1. Introduction 
Blasius solution is one of the most remarkable solutions in fluid mechanics. It describes the steady, 
two-dimensional laminar boundary layer (BL) that forms over a semi-infinite flat plate held parallel 
to a constant unidirectional flow. While surprisingly accurate for many engineering purposes, Blasius 
solution is not mathematically unique [1] and is only an approximate (first-order) solution, as it 
predicts a non-zero normal velocity at the outer edge of the boundary layer, which is physically 
inconsistent with the outer flow assumptions. Despite these shortcomings, Blasius solution remains a 
valuable asset as a benchmark for validating numerical simulations of boundary layer flows and it 
provides the base flow profile for boundary layer stability analysis. 
The non-unicity of Blasius solution is related to the existence of an infinite tower of perturbation 
modes that obey a linearized version of the boundary layer equations. These perturbations were 
described by Stewartson [2] and Libby and Fox [3], who obtained the discrete eigenvalues and 
eigenfunctions of the perturbation equation and applied them to the analysis of first-order boundary 
layer solutions deviating slightly from the Blasius solution. In subsequent works, the mathematical 
problem was further clarified [4] [5] [6] [7] [8], more eigenvalues were obtained [9] and approximate 
[6] [10] and asymptotic [11] formulae for the eigenvalues were derived. The analysis of Libby and 
Fox was later extended to the energy equation [10] [12] and to more general situations such as moving 
surfaces [13], mass injection [14], chemically reacting boundary layers [15], species concentration 
[16], higher-order boundary-layer theory [1] and Falkner-Skan flows [17] [18]. A comprehensive 
review of those early developments can be found in [19]. More recent work has established relations 
of Libby-Fox eigenmodes to other streak/mode families [20] [21] [22] [23] [24]. One of the interesting 
features of Libby and Fox’s analysis is that it is possible to develop a Green’s function solution that 
will enable us to directly link the perturbative and adjoint approaches.

Adjoint equations have many applications across mathematics, engineering and sciences 
disciplines and have been studied from many points of view, including functional analysis, numerical 
algorithms, inverse problems, sensitivity analysis, optimization, data assimilation, uncertainty 
quantification, among others. No comprehensive review covering all these topics is known to the 
authors, but the reviews [25] [26], the recent paper [27], the books [28] [29] and the more 
mathematically-oriented review [30] contain useful discussions and many useful references.   
Of particular interest to us are applications related to adjoint-based sensitivity analysis [31]. In this 
context, adjoint techniques are very efficient in determining the sensitivity of some output quantities, 
also referred to as functionals, objective or cost functions, or measures of merit, to the input data of 
the problem. This is particularly relevant when the number of output quantities of interest, such as lift 
and drag in aeronautics, is small and the number of input variables is large. This interpretation is at 
the heart of most of the engineering applications of adjoint techniques in fluid dynamics, such as flow 
stability [26], aerodynamic design [32] [33] [34], flow control [35] [36], error estimation and mesh 
adaptation [37] [38], data assimilation [39], modal decomposition [40] and uncertainty quantification 
[41] [42]. Another interpretation, more technical in nature but otherwise directly related to the 
previous one, will be of primary concern here: the adjoint solution gives the effect of a source at any 
location on the objective function. A more precise statement is that the adjoint variable at a specific 
location is precisely the value of the functional evaluated with the appropriate Green’s function of the 
original problem. This link with the Green’s function has been used to obtain analytic adjoint solutions 
[43] [44] [45] and will also be exploited here for the analysis of the structure of the adjoint solution to 
the boundary layer equations about Blasius solution.  
Adjoint methods have been applied to boundary layer flows in a variety of contexts, mostly 
concerned with stability and receptivity. The literature is too vast to be properly summarized here (see 
[26] for a comprehensive overview of adjoint applications to hydrodynamic stability up to 2014) but 
a few relevant points are as follows. The adjoint of the Navier-Stokes, parabolized stability, Orr-
Sommerfeld or boundary layer equations, has been used in shape optimization [46], flow control [47] 
[48] [49] [50] [51], boundary layer stability [52] [53], adjoint-based global-mode stabilization [54], 
boundary layer receptivity [55] [56] [57] [58] [59], structural sensitivity [60] [61], identification of 
optimal perturbations [21] [62] resolvent analysis [63] and calculation of manufacturing tolerances 
[64]. Recently, Kühl et al. [65] have made a thorough analysis of the complete adjoint boundary–layer 
equations and dynamics, including a  proposal for an analytic adjoint solution for the Blasius boundary 
layer. It also contains the intriguing idea that the adjoint solution develops a boundary layer structure 
that emanates from the end of the plate, as corresponds to the reversed convective characteristics 
relative to the primal flow. Their analysis differs from ours in several aspects and their proposed 
solution appears to be in conflict with the solution that we will present below except under special 
circumstances. 
This paper presents the analytic derivation of the adjoint solution to the Prandtl boundary layer 
equations on a flat plate. The main body of the paper addresses the canonical zero pressure-gradient 
flow, where the solution is formulated within the context of the Libby-Fox perturbation theory for the 
Blasius boundary layer. We then proceed to briefly determine the adjoint solution for the more general 
case of non-zero pressure gradients, corresponding to the Falkner-Skan problem. A clear limitation of 
this work is the exclusive focus on simplified, steady, laminar flows, so that its direct relevance to 
unsteady perturbations or fully turbulent flows is debatable. On the other hand, an improved 
understanding of the adjoint solution corresponding to Blasius boundary layer is relevant in several 
regards. First, an analytic adjoint solution serves as a useful test case for validation of adjoint Navier-
Stokes or boundary layer solvers and can also be used to understand the mathematical structure of the


## Page 3


adjoint problem in a simplified, yet fundamental, case. The structure of the solution can also help 
guide the proper formulation of the adjoint problem and its boundary conditions in numerical 
implementations. 
Likewise, an analytic solution facilitates theoretical developments, such as shedding light on the 
original eigenvalue problem and analyzing the Adjoint Transport Convection (ATC) term. In the first 
case, the analysis of the analytic solution and of the adjoint equations leads to constraints on the 
eigenfunctions and the eigenvalues of the primal problem. As for the ATC, determining its actual 
value is a complex task from the viewpoint of original partial differential equations. Using the analytic 
adjoint solution, the ATC is shown to be comparable in size to, if not exceeding, the regular adjoint 
convective term, demonstrating that its omission is not justified.  
Additionally, an explicit adjoint solution can be used to directly compute gradients and sensitivities 
with respect to various control parameters such as the initial profile, the plate shape and the wall 
normal velocity distribution, which are relevant to initial value problems, shape design applications 
and active flow control based on wall-normal suction/blowing, respectively. In the present case, it will 
be shown, using the analytic adjoint solution, that the drag sensitivity of an initial value problem is 
identical to the one derived by Libby and Fox in [3]. Likewise, the shape sensitivity of the integrated 
friction, which is given by the product of the primal and dual shears [65], is shown to be exactly zero 
to the level of approximation considered here. Finally, the drag sensitivity to blowing/suction is also 
examined and the results are shown to agree with those obtained with the Libby-Fox theory in two 
relevant situations.  
To make the subject as accessible as possible, the paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we 
review some basic facts about the Libby-Fox analysis of perturbations to the Blasius solution. In 
section 3, we introduce the adjoint equations and derive an adjoint eigenvalue problem. In section 4, 
the Green’s function of the Libby-fox perturbation equations is recalled and is used to compute the 
analytic adjoint solution. Section 5 analyzes the structure of the adjoint solution and demonstrates that 
it obeys the adjoint equations and boundary conditions. Section 5.2 discusses the possibility that the 
adjoint solution can be written in similarity form as the Blasius solution. Section 6 presents some 
numerical experiments including sample solutions evaluated from numerical integration of the adjoint 
Libby-Fox equation. Section 7 applies the analytic solution to the analysis of the ATC and to the 
sensitivity analysis of the drag coefficient in initial-value, shape design and active flow control (via 
blowing-suction) problems. Section 8 briefly examines the case of boundary layers described by the 
Falkner-Skan solution and writes down the corresponding adjoint solution. Section 9 summarizes the 
findings of the paper and outlines future research. In appendix A, we use the Green’s function 
approach to derive the solution to the Oseen linearization of Blasius equation. This problem admits a 
closed-form analytic solution, so the analysis illustrates how the eigenfunction expansion defining the 
adjoint solution works out in a case where the analytic adjoint solution can be written in terms of 
elementary functions. In appendix B, the limiting behavior of the adjoint eigenfunctions for small and 
large η is described. Finally, in appendix C some properties of the eigenfunction expansion defining 
the analytic adjoint solution are analyzed.

to determine the spectrum. Determination of the spectrum requires imposing on the eigenfunctions an 
exponential decay at infinity. A variety of techniques has been used to determine the eigenvalues and 
eigenfunctions of eq. (7) (see [19] for a review of early developments). The original work [3] only 
contained the first 10 eigenvalues. Libby [9], Wilks and Bramley [7] and Luchini [20] all list the first 
twenty eigenvalues. These are all exact approaches. Libby and Fox’s and Luchini’s approaches are

integration (η decreasing) of the asymptotic form of eq. (7), while Wilks and Bramley’s is based on a

approximate values for the eigenvalues replacing the coefficient functions of eq. (7) by the sum of its 
asymptotic terms near the wall and far from the wall. Kemp [10] improved Kotorynski formula, giving 
approximate values for the norms as well and, additionally, applied the results of a WKB

9. Conclusions 
In this paper, we have investigated the adjoint solution for the two-dimensional momentum 
equation for a laminar boundary layer along a flat plate when the objective function is the integrated 
friction drag coefficient. The construction of the solution is approached from the viewpoint of the 
algebraically developing steady disturbance modes described by Libby and Fox. In particular, the 
Green’s function constructed by Libby and Fox is used here to derive a solution to the adjoint 
equations. The analysis of this solution has revealed a number of interesting facts, not only about the 
adjoint equations, but also about Libby-Fox theory.  
The analytic adjoint solution depends on an eigenfunction expansion of the type originally 
introduced and exploited by Libby and Fox for perturbations of Blasius boundary layer. The solution 
is represented by an infinite series that converges rapidly except in the neighbourhood of the plate 
end. The analysis of the solution, supported by numerical evaluation, suggests that, contrary to 
previous literature, the adjoint solution cannot be put in the simple self-similar form enjoyed by the 
Oseen linearization of Blasius boundary layer solution.  
At the present level of approximation, which neglects pressure and transverse momentum 
perturbations, the sensitivity of the integrated friction drag to changes in the shape of the plate 
vanishes. On the other hand, the Adjoint Transverse Convective (ATC) term, which is a source of 
numerical instability in numerical adjoint solvers and is sometimes neglected, has been shown to be 
of comparable magnitude to the remaining adjoint convective term, so that its elimination cannot be 
justified a priori. The impact that such an approximation can have on the sensitivities or on specific 
applications (such as in optimal aerodynamic shape design) is a separate issue that has not been 
addressed here.  
The adjoint formulation presented here can also be used to shed light on several aspects of the 
Libby-Fox theory. The equation obeyed by the adjoint eigenfunctions can be used to derive a couple 
of identities involving the Libby-Fox eigenfunctions and eigenvalues. Likewise, the adjoint approach 
offers a new way to address topics covered in the original Libby–Fox study, such as initial value 
problems and the effect of blowing and suction. Comparison between both approaches leads to 
additional identities for the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues.  
Sample solutions have been generated that compare extraordinarily well with numerical adjoint 
solutions, thus confirming its potential as a benchmarking tool for verification of adjoint solvers. The 
approach has focused on the integrated skin friction, but the Green’s function can be used to compute 
the adjoint solution for any conceivable quantity of interest that is sensitive to changes in the 
streamwise momentum.  
Finally, we have outlined the analysis for the case of non-zero pressure gradient boundary layers 
obeying the Falkner-Skan equations. We have written down the corresponding analytic adjoint 
solution and evaluated it for pressure gradient parameter 
0.5

, for which 20 eigenvalues were 
derived by Chen and Libby. The convergence of the eigenfunction expansion is very slow in this case, 
and exhibits strong oscillations typical of the Gibbs phenomenon, but otherwise the overall structure 
of the adjoint solution seems to be reasonably well captured as illustrated by comparison with a sample 
numerical adjoint solution.


## Page 31


There are several possible avenues for future work. Fox and Libby also studied perturbations to the 
energy equation in Blasius boundary layers, and similar analysis exist concerning the equation of 
species concentrations. The associated eigenvalue problems are somewhat simpler than the one 
considered here, being inherently second order and linear in the absence of coupling between velocity 
and energy and/or composition (decoupling that has precluded the application of those results to the 
computation of the drag-based adjoint energy variable) and can be used to generate adjoint solutions 
in those settings. Finally, the fact that the adjoint eigenfunctions obey a second order ordinary 
differential equation (as opposed to Libby-Fox’s third order ODE) in which exponential decay of the 
solutions is replaced by polynomial growth may offer an alternative method for determining the 
eigenvalues. Unfortunately, the equation appears to be significantly bad conditioned, which has 
hampered progress along this line. Some of these themes will be further pursued elsewhere.
