Abstract
Despite the centrality of crosslinguistic in-
fluence (CLI) to bilingualism research, hu-
man studies often yield conflicting results
due to inherent experimental variance. We
address these inconsistencies by using lan-
guage models (LMs) as controlled statisti-
cal learners to systematically simulate CLI
and isolate its underlying drivers. Specifi-
cally, we study the effect of varying the L1
language dominance and the L2 language
proficiency, which we manipulate by con-
trolling the L2 age of exposure—defined
as the training step at which the L2 is
introduced.
Furthermore, we investigate
the impact of pretraining on L1 languages
with varying syntactic distance from the
L2. Using crosslinguistic priming, we an-
alyze how activating L1 structures impacts
L2 processing. Our results align with ev-
idence from psycholinguistic studies, con-
firming that language dominance and pro-
ficiency are strong predictors of CLI. We
further find that while priming of gram-
matical structures is bidirectional, the prim-
ing of ungrammatical structures is sensitive
to language dominance.
Finally, we pro-
vide mechanistic evidence of CLI in LMs,
demonstrating that the L1 is co-activated
during L2 processing and directly influences
the neural circuitry recruited for the L2.
More broadly, our work demonstrates that
LMs can serve as a computational frame-
work to inform theories of human CLI.

Introduction
The study of crosslinguistic influence (CLI) is cen-
tral to understanding how two languages coex-
ist and interact within bilingual speakers (Serra-
trice, 2022; Siemund, 2023). Despite its promi-
nence, the underlying mechanisms of CLI remain
debated. Evidence from crosslinguistic priming—
in which processing a structure in one language

facilitates the production of the same structure in
another—points to the existence of shared syn-
tactic representations between languages (Hart-
suiker et al., 2004; Hartsuiker and Bertnolet, 2017;
Contemori and Minjarez Oppenheimer, 2023).
However, while priming is a well-attested phe-
nomenon, the number of variables that can affect
human language acquisition leaves many ques-
tions unanswered.
Previous research have ex-
plored various predictors of CLI; yet, findings re-
main inconclusive due to significant variance in
how these predictors are operationalized across
studies (van Dijk et al., 2022).
Language models (LMs) can simulate human-
like grammatical judgments from unstructured
data (Warstadt et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2021).
Despite significant differences between LMs and
human learning, they can still contribute to the
debate on human language acquisition, especially
when their learning environment is deliberately
made plausible (Warstadt and Bowman, 2022).
Their greatest potential lies in the ability to ab-

late the variables that affect CLI. For instance, we
can vary the L1 and L2 language exposure ra-
tios and study the effect on crosslinguistic prim-
ing.
This degree of control is rarely possible
in psycholinguistic studies, which often rely on
non-standardized linguistic tests of language dom-
inance, leading to contradictory conclusions (van
Dijk et al., 2022). Furthermore, while human stud-
ies are often limited in their scalability, artificial
learners facilitate the broad evaluation of linguistic
phenomena, and the extension to languages with
few non-native speakers, offering a comprehen-
sive quantitative account of CLI.
While LMs cannot simulate the full human
learning experience, we focus on studying the ef-
fects of the L1 language dominance and L2 lan-
guage proficiency, which are straightforward to
manipulate in LMs, and were shown to be pre-
dictive of CLI and crosslinguistic priming effects
(van Dijk et al., 2022; Hartsuiker and Bertnolet,
2017). As shown in Figure 1, to manipulate dom-
inance and proficiency, we vary the age of expo-
sure, which is the training step at which we in-
troduce the L2 language. For instance, introduc-
ing the L2 language at step=32K means the model
was trained exclusively on the L1 language for
32K steps, leading it to be more entrenched in the
model’s parameters, therefore increasing its domi-
nance. To maintain the L1 influence and prevent
catastrophic forgetting, we train on interleaved
L1/L2 sequences to maintain the L1 language in-
fluence; consequently, late L2 introduction also re-
sults in lower L2 exposure, directly affecting its
proficiency.
Our hypothesis is that a distant L1 language
will lead to more negative or less positive CLI ef-
fects compared to a less distant one. As shown
in Figure 1, we study the CLI effects of 5 lan-
guages that are increasingly distant from the L2
language.
We measure CLI effects using the
Benchmark of Linguistic Minimal Pairs for En-
glish (BLiMP) (Warstadt et al., 2020)—a dataset
of 67k pairs of grammatical and ungrammatical
sentences. More specifically, we measure the dif-
ference in BLiMP accuracy between the bilingual
LM, and a monolingual counterpart. While stud-
ies of crosslinguistic priming have found that it
affects both grammatical (Bernolet et al., 2007;
Hartsuiker and Bertnolet, 2017) and ungrammat-
ical (Van Dijk and Unsworth, 2023; Baroncini and
Torregrossa, 2025) structures, we similarly prime
the model with the L1 translation of the gram-
matical target sentence, and we study its effect on
BLiMP accuracy.
Beyond grammatical accuracy, we investigate
whether bilingual LMs exhibit preference for the
writing of non-native speakers that share the same
L1. Using the First Certificate in English (FCE)
dataset (Yannakoudakis et al., 2011)—which con-
tains articles written by different L1 non-native
speakers along with their corrections—we study
whether LMs internalize L1 specific CLI effects.
This analysis provides evidence that the nuances
of human CLI can be modeled and differentiated
computationally. Finally, we provide mechanis-
tic evidence of L1 influence on L2 processing and
learning. We use LogitLens (nostalgebraist, 2020)
to decode LMs hidden states and quantify the L1
language presence during L2 processing. Further-
more, we investigate the effect of the L1 language
on learning the L2 by correlating the L2 language
neuron overlap between the L1 languages with
their syntactic distance.
In summary, this work provides a comprehen-
sive investigation of the computational modeling
of CLI effects. Our contributions are as follows:
• We propose a methodology to manipulate the
L1 language dominance and the L2 language
proficiency by varying the age of exposure
and training on interleaved L1/L2 sequences.
• We perform a quantitative study and abla-
tion of CLI and crosslinguistic priming on the
BLiMP benchmark.
• Using the FCE dataset,
we investigate
whether LMs internalize L1-specific nuances
based on their preference of non-native text
from the same L1.
• We provide mechanistic evidence of L1 in-
fluence using LogitLens and language neuron
analysis.

Related Works
Crosslinguistic Influence
The study of how two
languages interact is a prominent research focus
of both human bilinguals (Hulk and Müller, 2000;
Serratrice, 2013; van Dijk et al., 2022; Siemund,
2023) and artificial learners (Papadimitriou and
Jurafsky, 2020; Oba et al., 2023; Yadavalli et al.,
2023; Alastruey et al., 2025). While studies of
language models (LMs) consistently show that


## Page 3


syntactic distance negatively impacts target lan-
guage performance, it remains unclear whether
this effect stems from shared grammatical rep-
resentations or alternative factors, such as in-
terference resulting from limited model capacity
(Chang et al., 2024).
In human CLI research,
there is a stronger consensus that representations
are either fully shared or separate-but-connected
(Hartsuiker and Bertnolet, 2017; Ahn and Ferreira,
2024). Most of the evidence for this comes from
studies of crosslinguistic priming (Bock, 2003).
We similarly employ crosslinguistic priming to
study CLI in artificial models, discussing the im-
plications for both artificial and human bilinguals.
Crosslinguistic priming
Crosslinguistic prim-
ing, first formulated by Bock (2003), is a promis-
ing methodology for validating hypotheses re-
garding CLI (Serratrice, 2022).
For instance,
Hartsuiker et al. (2004); Hartsuiker and Bertno-
let (2017) proposed the the shared-syntax hypoth-
esis, using crosslinguistic priming as evidence.
Specifically, the hypothesis is that if a structure
is shared between two languages, then priming
will lead to its activation, subsequently affecting
processing and production in the other language.
This hypothesis implies that priming within and
across languages should be similar, and inherently
bidirectional (Serratrice et al., 2025). However,
other studies have questioned these implications
(Vasilyeva et al., 2010; Ahn and Ferreira, 2024),
suggesting that representations are separate-but-
connected rather than fully shared. Furthermore,
studies on priming of ungrammatical structures
support the connectedness hypothesis (Van Dijk
and Unsworth, 2023; Baroncini and Torregrossa,
2025), where even structures that do not exist in
the target language can be primed. We contribute
to this debate by applying crosslinguistic priming
to LMs as controlled statistical learners, and we
discuss our findings in light of these hypotheses.
L2 acquisition in Language Models
Previous
work have studied the effect of multilinguality
and bilingual training on individual language ac-
quisition (Dufter and Schütze, 2020; Deshpande
et al., 2022; Chang et al., 2024; Alastruey et al.,
2025).
However, these studies often overlook
the establishment of an explicit L1-L2 status be-
tween languages.
Oba et al. (2023) and Ya-
davalli et al. (2023) addressed this by training Sec-
ond Language Acquisition (SLA) LMs to study
transfer effects, finding that greater linguistic dis-
tance correlates with increased negative transfer.
While these studies prioritize cognitive plausibil-
ity by either training on child directed speech (Ya-
davalli et al., 2023) or on human-scale linguistic
input (Oba et al., 2023), we argue that for study-
ing CLI, the plausibility of the bilingual training
regime is more critical than restricted data. Be-
cause models trained on human-scale data achieve
sub-human performance, we prioritize approach-
ing human-like grammatical competence to en-
sure that observed CLI effects are representative
of mature bilingual processing. Other studies have
trained SLA LMs to study different L2 acquisition
phenomenon; for instance, Constantinescu et al.
(2025) trained SLA models to study the critical
period (CP) effects (Newport, 1990). Most similar
to our approach, Arnett et al. (2025) used crosslin-
guistic priming to track the development of shared
grammatical representations. However, our focus
is on CLI as a potential product of these shared
representations. We specifically employ crosslin-
guistic priming to study CLI effects and to ab-
late conditions that are necessary for simulating
human-like CLI.

Methodology
Our methodology comprises four parts: the bilin-
gual training framework (§3.1), language selection
and syntactic distance (§3.2), the CLI and prim-
ing evaluation protocol (§3.3), and mechanistic in-
terpretability methods used to investigate internal
representations (§4.3).
3.1
Bilingual Model Training
Our bilingual training setup is designed to mir-
ror key constraints of human acquisition. Bilin-
gual humans typically receive less input per lan-
guage than monolinguals, as acquiring multiple
languages does not increase total linguistic expo-
sure (Hoff et al., 2011). Furthermore, variations
in the age of exposure affect both the total vol-
ume of L2 input and the learner’s cognitive plas-
ticity, which typically declines with age (Newport,
1990). We simulate these dynamics by training
LMs on a fixed amount of training steps while
varying the onset of L2 introduction. To reflect the
decline of learning ability, we use a linear learning
rate scheduler that decreases the learning rate lin-
early as a function of the training steps. During
L2 introduction, we maintain exposure to the L1


## Page 4


language to prevent catastrophic forgetting (Con-
stantinescu et al., 2025; Arnett et al., 2025), a phe-
nomenon that humans generally do not experience
due to critical period (CP) effects (Lenneberg,
1967; Bylund, 2019). While this setup does not
capture the full complexity of human learning, it
provides a principled approach to establishing a
clear language dominance hierarchy. Within this
framework, increasing the age of exposure simul-
taneously increases L1 dominance and decreases
L2 proficiency—two primary predictors of CLI
and priming effects (van Dijk et al., 2022; Hart-
suiker and Bertnolet, 2017).
Following previous work (Chang and Bergen,
2022; Arnett et al., 2025), we train bilingual GPT2
models with balanced and imbalanced L1 and L2
exposure. For each language, we use 128M lines
of de-duplicated OSCAR corpus (Abadji et al.,
2021) and train a SentencePiece tokenizer (Kudo
and Richardson, 2018) for each language pair
on 100K lines.
To ensure a balanced vocabu-
lary despite imbalanced exposure, we sample tok-
enizer training lines equally from both languages.
We create sequences of 256 tokens, shuffle them,
then sample 1B tokens for training per language,
and 1M tokens for evaluation. During monolin-
gual training, all sequences in the batch are of
the same language, while during bilingual train-
ing, the batch contains interleaved L1 and L2 se-
quences. Models are trained for a total of 64K
steps with a linear learning rate scheduler, and we
vary the age of exposure by initiating the bilingual
training at steps 0, 16K, 32K, 48K without reset-
ting the learning rate. Detailed hyperparameters
are reported in Appendix A.

3.3
Linguistic Evaluation
3.3.1
Correlation between Syntactic Distance
and L2 Knowledge
We investigate the correlation between L1-L2 syn-
tactic distance and L2 grammatical proficiency as
a primary proxy for CLI. To quantify L2 knowl-
edge, we use the BLiMP benchmark (Warstadt
et al., 2020), which consists of 67,000 minimal
pairs of acceptable and unacceptable sentences
across 67 linguistic categories. We evaluate the
model’s performance based on sentence-level sur-
prisal. For a given pair (sacc,sunacc), the model
is considered correct if it assigns a higher pseudo-
log-likelihood to the acceptable sentence:
log P(sacc) > log P(sunacc)
(1)
where the probability is normalized by the num-
ber of tokens to account for variations in sequence
length.
Our hypothesis is that if the L1 signif-
icantly influences the L2, then L2 accuracy on
BLiMP will exhibit a negative correlation with the
syntactic distance between the language pair.
Because our tokenizers are trained on bilingual
data unique to each L1-L2 pair, the resulting vo-
cabularies vary across models. To isolate the ef-
fects of CLI from tokenizer differences, we train a
monolingual L2 baseline for each pair using the
same bilingual tokenizer. This ensures that any
performance difference is attributable to training
data composition rather than vocabulary bias. Fol-
lowing Alastruey et al. (2025), we quantify the
CLI effect as the relative change in accuracy:
CLIL1→L2 = AccL2 −AccL1, L2

3.3.2
Preference for Non-native
Constructions
CLI effects extend beyond binary grammatical ac-
curacy to influence the frequency and distribu-
tion of specific constructions. For instance, while
both English and French allow prenominal ad-
jectives, French-English bilinguals may produce
them more frequently than monolinguals due to
crosslinguistic transfer (NICOLADIS, 2006). To
capture these subtle "interlanguage" patterns, we
evaluate whether bilingual LMs exhibit a prefer-
ence for the specific constructions used by non-
native speakers of the same L1.
We use the First Certificate in English (FCE)
dataset (Yannakoudakis et al., 2011), a Gram-
matical Error Correction (GEC) corpus annotated
with learner L1s. We convert the dataset into a
minimal-pair acceptability task, filtering for gram-
matical errors while excluding spelling and punc-
tuation mistakes. Since some L1 languages ex-
hibit significantly higher number of data points
than others, we undersample the dataset using 5
random seeds to prevent them from biasing the re-
sults.

"prefers" specific L1 constructions will exhibit a
smaller surprisal gap between acceptable and un-

3.3.3
Crosslinguistic Priming
To investigate the influence of shared or connected
representations on L2 processing, we employ a
crosslinguistic priming paradigm similar to those
used in psycholinguistic studies (Hartsuiker and
Bertnolet, 2017; Van Dijk and Unsworth, 2023).
Following the approach of Arnett et al. (2025),
we evaluate whether the presence of an L1 prime
affects the model’s preference for L2 grammati-
cal vs. ungrammatical structures. Since BLiMP
benchmark lacks parallel translations, we gener-
ate L1 primes by translating the acceptable sen-
tence of each minimal pair into the corresponding
L1 using the NLLB-200-distilled-600M model1.
To measure the priming effect, we prepend the
L1 translation as context and calculate the length-
normalized negative log-likelihood (surprisal) of
the subsequent L2 acceptable and unacceptable
sentences. Formally, we evaluate the conditional
log-probability:

3.4.1
Logit Lens
LogitLens (nostalgebraist, 2020) projects inter-
mediate hidden states into the output vocabulary
space by applying the model’s unembedding ma-
trix. This allows for the observation of how inter-
nal representations converge toward a final predic-
tion across layers. Recent work has demonstrated
that multilingual LLMs often utilize English as an
intermediate "pivot" when processing other lan-
guages (Wendler et al., 2024). We employ Log-
itLens to detect whether our bilingual LMs simi-
larly utilize the L1 as an intermediate representa-
tion during L2 processing, as a direct indicator of
L1 influence on the L2 hidden states.
Our approach identifies the top 10 predicted
tokens per hidden state.
To quantify language
presence, we categorize these tokens using a pre-
constructed dictionary. A token is classified as L1-
or L2-specific if its relative frequency in the re-
spective training corpus is at least twice that of
the other language (count(L1)/count(L2) > 2).

3.4.2
Language Neurons identification
We identify language neurons as those consis-
tently activated by sequences of a specific lan-
guage. Using 10M held-out tokens, we isolate at-
tention and Feed-Forward Network (FFN) neurons
using the Parallel Language-specific Neuron De-
tection (PLND) method (Zhao et al., 2024). Fol-
lowing this framework, we define language neu-
rons as those consistently ranking in the top 25%
of activations. For our GPT2 architecture, this cor-
responds to 192 neurons per attention layer and
768 neurons per FFN layer.
To assess L1 influence on L2 structural repre-
sentations, we identify L2-specific neurons across
different L1 models and calculate their intersec-
tional overlap. We hypothesize that if the L1 af-
fects the development of L2 circuitry, then L1 lan-
guages with greater syntactic similarity will yield
a higher degree of L2 neuron overlap.
We test
this by correlating the number of shared L2 neu-
rons with the syntactic distance of the respective
L1 pairs.

In the simultaneous acquisition setting (Step=0),
baseline CLI scores are relatively uniform across
L1s. However, as the models become increasingly
imbalanced (Step ≥16K), a stronger correlation
emerges between CLI and syntactic distance: L1s
closer to English, such as German and Spanish,
exhibit lower interference than more distant L1s
like Turkish and Korean.
The introduction of crosslinguistic priming fur-
ther polarizes these effects. For syntactically sim-
ilar pairs, priming facilitates processing, result-
ing in positive CLI (i.e., accuracy exceeding the
monolingual baseline). In contrast, priming dis-
tant L1s leads to further degradation in L2 accu-
racy.
While increasing L1 dominance (moving
from Step=0 to Step=16K) amplifies the overall
priming magnitude, this effect begins to wane with
higher ages of exposure (Step=48K). We attribute
this decline to the model’s reduced L2 proficiency,
aligning with psycholinguistic evidence that L2
competence modulates the strength of priming ef-
fect (Muylle et al., 2020; Van Dijk and Unsworth,
2023).
Analysis of the 13 individual linguistic phenom-
ena in BLiMP (Appendix B.1) reveals that the
number of phenomena that benefit from positive
priming effects is negatively correlated with the
L1 syntactic distance. Furthermore, ablation of the
translation model (Appendix B.2) and permutation
tests (Appendix B.3) confirm that the results are
not explained by translation quality, lexical over-
lap or the L1 language activation.
Specifically,
neither shuffling the prime tokens nor sampling
a random prime leads to effects consistent with
syntactic distance, suggesting that observed CLI
is driven by specific L1 structure activation that
leads to either positive or negative CLI depending
on whether it is shared between the languages or
not.
Collectively, these results demonstrate that both
L1 dominance and L2 proficiency, operationalized
here via the age of exposure, are robust predic-
tors of CLI strength, corroborating findings from
human bilingual studies (van Dijk et al., 2022;
Muylle et al., 2020; Baroncini and Torregrossa,
2025).

models while decreasing it in Greek-L1 models.
This suggests that the impact of priming on per-
formance (as seen in §4.1) is likely driven by the
activation of specific grammatical representations
rather than a simple increase in general L1 signal.
4.3.2
Neuron Overlap: How the L1 Language
Affects The L2 Language Neurons
We hypothesize that if the L1 serves as a struc-
tural prior, syntactically similar L1s will result in
higher overlap among L2-activated neurons com-
pared to distant L1s. Figure 6 illustrates the num-
ber of shared neurons of each L1 language with
the rest of the languages shown on the x-axis based
on their syntactic distance. Across all models, we
observe a consistent negative correlation: as syn-
tactic distance increases, the number of shared L2
neurons significantly decreases. This trend con-
firms that the L1 influence is not merely a behav-
ioral phenomenon but is rooted in the model’s in-
ternal architecture. Notably, German as the lan-
guage most similar to English, exhibits a shallower
slope and wider confidence interval, suggesting it
leads to less distinct neuron recruitement due to its
similarity with the L2.
5
Ablations
5.1
Early Imbalanced LMs do not witness
similar effects
Models trained with a high age of exposure are
inherently subjected to two variables: an imbal-
ance in total training volume (more L1 data, less
L2 data) and the sequential nature of acquisition
(L1 entrenchment prior to L2 introduction). To
isolate these effects and verify if data imbalance
alone is sufficient to establish L1 dominance, we
trained a series of early imbalanced LMs. These
models were exposed to both languages starting
at Step 0, but with L1/L2 ratios specifically cali-
brated to match the total token counts of the 16K
and 32K age of exposure models.
The results in Figure 7 demonstrate that early
imbalanced training is insufficient to produce ef-
fects consistent with linguistic distance on the
BLiMP benchmark.
While priming does yield
some positive transfer for similar languages (Ger-
man, Spanish) and negative transfer for distant
ones, the magnitude and gradient of these effects
do not scale linearly with syntactic distance. This
leads us to conclude that prior L1 entrenchment is
necessary for consistent CLI effects.
5.2
Asymmetry of CLI effects
We ablate the asymmetry of CLI effects by train-
ing on English as the L1 and the other lan-
guages as L2.
Figure 8 shows the CLI effects
on BLiMP of two ages of exposure (i.e.
16K
and 32K). The results show that before priming,
the L2 languages—regardless of their syntactic
distance—lead to negligible effects on the L1’s
language grammatical accuracy. Furthermore, the
priming effects only affect the 3 most similar lan-
guages positively, while distant languages (i.e.
Korean and Turkish) show minimal effects.
These results suggest that while the priming
of grammatical structures is largely bidirectional,
the priming of ungrammatical constructions is not.
Because grammatical structures are likely shared
or highly integrated between languages, they re-
main resilient to shifts in language dominance,
supporting the shared-syntax hypothesis (Hart-
suiker et al., 2004). Conversely, ungrammatical
transfer appears to require a more entrenched L1
structure to successfully "override" L2 processing.

The asymmetry of CLI has been discussed ex-

dominant L1 language leads to larger priming ef-
fects than priming with the L2 language (Keatley
et al., 1994; Vasilyeva et al., 2010; Serratrice et al.,
2025).
While prior work in NLP found no in-
herent asymmetry in LMs, suggesting instead that
such effects may be due to specific crosslinguistic
differences rather than L1/L2 status (Arnett et al.,
2025), our findings offer a more nuanced view. We
demonstrate that the asymmetry of priming effects
is fundamentally constrained by syntactic overlap.
Even when English is the target language, priming
effects only manifest bidirectionally across syn-
tactically similar pairs, indicating that language
status and linguistic distance interact to modulate
the strength of CLI.
5.3
Smaller LMs Show Weaker
Crosslinguistic Priming Effect
We further investigate the role of model scale
in mediating CLI effects.
Smaller models, due
to their limited parameter count, are often hy-

pothesized to share more representational capacity
across languages, potentially amplifying CLI. To
test this, we ablate model size by training "Small"
and "Tiny" variants of GPT-2 with an age of expo-

exhibit a more distinct relationship between syn-

to the base model. However, the effect of prim-

cially for the "Tiny" model, where all languages
regardless of their distance lead to negative effects.
These findings suggest that while smaller models
may better capture CLI effects due to forced pa-
rameter sharing, they lack the necessary capacity
to respond to crosslinguistic priming.
6
Discussion
L1 Dominance and L2 Proficiency
Consistent
with human SLA research, L1 dominance signifi-
cantly modulates CLI and crosslinguistic priming
in LMs. Our results demonstrate that varying the
age of exposure effectively manipulates language
dominance: increased L1 entrenchment correlates
with stronger CLI on BLiMP (§4.1) and more pro-

nounced mechanistic signals in LogitLens (§4.3.1)
and neuron overlap (§4.3.2). However, a trade-off
exists: as the age of exposure increases, L2 profi-
ciency declines, eventually attenuating priming ef-
fects (Figure 2). Notably, syntactically similar lan-
guages like German and Spanish exhibit persistent
priming effects even at Step=48K. This suggests
that shared syntactic features are acquired more
rapidly or resiliently in the L2, allowing facilita-
tion to manifest in similar languages while distant
ones remain inhibited by low proficiency.
Word Order Overlap
In human psycholinguis-
tics, the necessity of overlapping word order for
the development of shared syntax remains a sub-
ject of active debate. Some studies found prim-
ing effects in the absence of word order over-
lap (Hartsuiker et al., 2016; Song and Lai, 2021),
while others have not (Bernolet et al., 2007; Kidd
et al., 2014). Our results in language models indi-
cate that while word order impacts the magnitude
of CLI, it is not a strict necessity for its occur-
rence. Specifically, we observe that priming with
languages that diverge in word order still signifi-
cantly affects English. However, priming effects
are most stable in languages sharing a dominant
word order with English (e.g., German, Spanish,
and Greek), particularly in our L1-English abla-
tion (§5.2). This suggests that word order overlap
is not a binary prerequisite for CLI, though syntac-
tic alignment substantially reinforces the strength
and stability of crosslinguistic influence.
Shared vs Connected Account
The debate be-
tween "shared" and "separate-but-connected" syn-
tax remains central to understanding the bilingual
mind. Proponents of the shared-syntax hypothe-
sis argue that a single underlying representation
governs equivalent structures across languages,
citing the existence of crosslinguistic priming as
primary evidence (Hartsuiker et al., 2004; Hart-
suiker and Bertnolet, 2017). Conversely, observed
asymmetries—where CLI is significantly stronger
from the dominant L1 to the non-dominant L2—
suggest that syntactic structures may be separate
but connected by varying degrees of associative
strength (Vasilyeva et al., 2010; van Dijk et al.,
2022).
Our findings provide a potential resolution to
this dichotomy by demonstrating that both ac-
counts may be true depending on the structural
context.
Our crosslinguistic priming results in-
dicate that shared structures (i.e., those that are
grammatically acceptable in both languages) in-
deed exhibit bidirectional priming.
However,
the priming of non-shared structures is markedly
asymmetrical; these effects are only robust when
moving from the dominant L1 to the L2.
This
suggests that while core grammatical commonali-
ties are processed through shared representations,
the transfer of language-specific or ungrammatical
structures is constrained by language dominance
and entrenchment, favoring the connected account
for non-overlapping structures.
7
Conclusion
While fundamental differences exist between how
LMs and humans acquire language, the emer-
gence of human-like linguistic capabilities in LMs
trained on unstructured data suggests that these
models can serve as informative "artificial learn-
ers" for psycholinguistic theory.
In this work,
we utilized bilingual LMs to simulate and ana-
lyze crosslinguistic influence (CLI) through a sys-
tematic ablation of language dominance and pro-
ficiency. We provide behavioral evidence show-
ing that L1 dominance and L2 proficiency sig-
nificantly modulate CLI and crosslinguistic prim-
ing, alongside mechanistic evidence demonstrat-
ing how the L1 physically affects L2 hidden states
and neuron recruitment.
Central to the debate on whether CLI stems
from shared representations, our findings suggest
a hybrid account: structures common to both lan-
guages appear to be shared, as evidenced by bidi-
rectional facilitation. However, the observed non-
bidirectionality of priming for distant languages
suggests that non-overlapping structures remain
separate-but-connected.
Ultimately, this work
highlights the utility of LMs in bridging the gap

between behavioral patterns and internal mecha-
nisms, providing a level of granular control and
interpretability that is currently unattainable in hu-
man subjects.
