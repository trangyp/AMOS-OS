Fine suspended particulates (FSP), commonly known as PM2.5, are
among the most harmful air pollutants, posing serious risks to population
health and environmental integrity. As such, accurately identifying latent
clusters of FSP is essential for effective air quality and public health manage-
ment. This task, however, is notably nontrivial as FSP clusters may depend
on various regional and temporal factors, which should be incorporated in the
modeling process. Thus, we capitalize on Bayesian nonparametric dynamic
clustering ideas, in which clustering structures may be influenced by complex
dependencies. Existing implementations of dynamic clustering, however, rely
on copula-based dependent Dirichlet processes (DPs), presenting consider-
able computational challenges for real-world deployment. With this in mind,
we propose a more efficient alternative for dynamic clustering by incorporat-
ing the novel ideas of logistic-beta dependent DPs. We also adopt a Stirling-
gamma prior—a novel distribution family—on the concentration parameter
of our underlying DP, easing the process of incorporating prior knowledge
into the model. Efficient computational strategies for posterior inference are
also presented. We apply our proposed method to identify dynamic FSP clus-
ters across Chile and demonstrate its superior performance over existing ap-
proaches.
1. Introduction.
Fine suspended particulates (FSP), commonly known as PM2.5, are the
smaller respirable suspended particulates, and refer to those airborne microparticulates with
a nominal aerodynamic diameter smaller or equal than 2.5 micrometres (µm). Due to their
notably fine dimensions, these particulates can penetrate and settle in the deepest parts of the
lungs, the blood stream (Nemmar et al., 2002), the brain (Oberdörster et al., 2004), and other
body organs, increasing the risk of lung morbidity (Donaldson, Li and MacNee, 1998), res-
piratory and cardiovascular diseases (Schwartz and Neas, 2000; Chen, Zhao and Kan, 2013),
as well as different types of cancer (Hill et al., 2023; White et al., 2023). In fact, even a short-
term exposure to large concentrations of FSP can significantly deteriorate people’s health
(Deb and Tsay, 2019). What is more, FSP may also lead to visibility impairments in the air
and influence the severity and frequency of extreme climate events (Zhang et al., 2015; Liang
et al., 2015). Thus, public and environmental authorities—from various countries around the
world—are now investing in technologies and infrastructure to monitor concentration levels
of various air pollutants, including FSP.
In this study, we make use of such available data in order to identify latent clusters of FSP,
which could then guide the implementation of adequate public-health response measures. In
particular, we consider publicly available data from 64 air quality monitoring stations across
continental Chile (i.e., excluding its insular territories), spanning January 2020 to December
2024—for a total of 60 months. The data were sourced from the National Information System

Moreover, it is widely acknowledged that eliciting an informative Dirichlet process prior
is far from simple (Escobar, 1994; Lijoi, Mena and Prünster, 2007). To exacerbate these
challenges, commonly used prior distributions on the concentration parameter of a Dirichlet
process—such as the gamma distribution—lead to analytically intractable results, preventing
a clear and intuitive incorporation of prior beliefs into the model specification. Thus, we
also incorporate a Stirling-gamma prior (Zito, Rigon and Dunson, 2024), a novel distribution
family, on the concentration parameter of our underlying Dirichlet process. Ultimately, this
facilitates the elicitation of an informative prior and makes the workflow more transparent
and straightforward. We also illustrate why a gamma prior may not be suitable for dynamic
clustering. As such, we call our proposed method the autoregressive logistic-beta Stirling-
gamma process.
The use of Bayesian nonparametric models to analyze environmental quality data is not
new. For instance, Sahu and Mardia (2004) employed a Bayesian kriged Kalman filter to
forecast FSP levels across New York City; Zhang et al. (2020) employed Bayesian additive
regression trees to predict individual components of FSP across California; Arbel, Mengersen
and Rousseau (2016) used a copula-based dependent Dirichlet process to assess the effect of
a fuel spill in Antarctica on species diversity; Gutiérrez, Mena and Ruggiero (2016) em-
ployed a time-dependent Dirichlet process mixture to estimate a time-varying density and
the probability of air pollutants exceeding an arbitrary threshold, at a given time point, using
FSP data from the metropolitan region of Santiago, Chile. This differs from our work as the
authors focus on dynamic density estimation rather than dynamic clustering. Moreover, as
discussed in De Iorio et al. (2023), the method introduced in this article accommodates both
dynamic density estimation and dynamic clustering, highlighting its advantages. Closer to
our method, on the other hand, is the work by Page, Quintana and Dahl (2022), in which
the authors propose a dependent random partition model for dynamic clustering and apply it
to identify pollution clusters across rural Germany. Later, however, we demonstrate that our
proposed method achieves a comparable or even better performance.

Stirling-gamma hyperparameters and the distribution of Kn, practitioners can transparently
and straightforwardly specify a prior on α that reflects their beliefs about their individual
scientific problems at hand. For instance, letting α ∼SG(1,0.2,n) implies that E[Kn] ≈5,

Due to the (α + n)T

order to obtain random draws from p(α|Ω−α). On the other hand, under a Stirling-gamma

rejection sampler from Zito, Rigon and Dunson (2024).

each time t, for t ∈{2,...,T}, two data points within each cluster are selected at random to
“jump” to any of the other two clusters, each with equal probability, aiming to maintain the

6. Discussion.
Fine suspended particulates, commonly known as PM2.5, are among the
most harmful air pollutants, posing serious risks to population health and environmental in-
tegrity. That said, despite these well-known hazards, many regions around the world still
experience FSP concentration levels far above healthy limits—for instance, the southern part
of the Andean region. Thus, in this article, we have addressed the challenge of accurately
identifying latent FSP clusters across continental Chile. Given the notable dependence of
FSP concentration levels on various regional and temporal factors, we have capitalized on
the flexibility of Bayesian nonparametric methods; in particular, we have built upon the ideas
of dynamic clustering from De Iorio et al. (2023), in which clustering structures may be
influenced by complex temporal and regional dependencies. Nonetheless, the existing im-
plementation of dynamic clustering relies on copula-based dependent DPs, which, despite
their versatility, remain computationally impractical for real-world deployment. Expanding
on the current state of dynamic clustering, we have developed a framework that incorporates
the novel ideas of logistic-beta dependent DPs (Lee et al., 2025), a more efficient alternative
to copula-based dependent DPs that preserves the necessary capabilities to perform dynamic
clustering. What is more, to facilitate the process of eliciting an informative prior distribution
and make the workflow more transparent and straightforward, we have also incorporated a
Stirling-gamma prior on the concentration parameter of our DP (Zito, Rigon and Dunson,
2024) and we have shown why more traditional prior distributions, like the gamma distri-
bution, may not be suitable for dynamic clustering. As such, we have called our proposed
method the autoregressive logistic-beta Stirling-gamma process. To make our contributions
widely available to the scientific community, we have implemented our proposed method in
the R package "dynclusts", included in the Supplementary Materials.
When applied to the Chilean FSP data, our proposed method reveals an intriguing sea-
sonal pattern in the clustering structure of fine air pollutants. In particular, we can observe
that during warmer months, our method tends to cluster together the majority of the mon-
itoring stations—with very few exceptions that could be considered outliers—suggesting
that after controlling for temperature, humidity, wind direction and speed, and incorporat-
ing location-specific random effects, there are no major differences between stations. During
colder months, on the other hand, we can observe clusters with minimal FSP concentrations
levels in the north and clusters with substantial FSP concentrations levels in the south. This
suggests that seasonal events, such as winter heating (Liang et al., 2015), may have a strong
influence on FSP clusters across continental Chile, even after accounting for other variables.
Lastly, we have also illustrated the superior performance of our proposed method over state-
of-the-art algorithms for dynamic clustering, like the dependent random partition model from
Page, Quintana and Dahl (2022).
That being said, the results from this article also raise extensions and future challenges be-
yond the scope of this paper. For instance, one could study in greater detail the dependency
links between seasonal events, like winter heating, and the recovered partitions. Additionally,
as discussed in Lee et al. (2025), the logistic-beta process can also be used to induce depen-
dencies in other stick-breaking process, like the Pitman–Yor process (Perman, Pitman and
Yor, 1992; Pitman and Yor, 1997). As such, another natural extension could be to consider
a broader class of discrete random measures, beyond the DP, in order to perform dynamic
clustering. One last extension could be to consider a hierarchical dynamic clustering, in the
spirit of Teh et al. (2006), in which practitioners observe data from multiple countries—such
as Chile, Brazil, and Argentina—aiming to identify dynamic pollution clusters within each
country, while allowing some degree of information sharing across countries. Therefore, this
article has also introduced promising avenues for subsequent research, which we intend to
pursue in the future. On the whole, our proposed method has expanded and enriched the al-
ready vibrant world of dependent Dirichlet process, as well as the emerging field of Bayesian
nonparametric dynamic clustering.
