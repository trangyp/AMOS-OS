Abstract. We review known linear and matrix generalizations of Hall’s clas-
sic “marriage theorem” and K˝onig’s theorem on partial matchings in bipartite
graphs, and relate them to linear and matrix generalizations of Dilworth’s
theorem about chains and antichains in posets and Menger’s theorem about
disjoint paths in directed graphs.
1. The linear setting
1.1. Hall’s theorem. Hall’s marriage theorem [6], in its most memorable (albeit
informal, and perhaps outdated) formulation [7], says that given n boys and m
girls, if for every 1 ≤k ≤n every set of k boys collectively know at least k girls,
then the boys can be married off in such a way that each boy marries a girl he
knows.

Interpreting Theorem 1.1 in terms of matrices yields a slight improvement of
that result. The vector space Mm,n has dimension mn over F, so if R contains
more than mn elements then the matrices wv∗with (v, w) ∈R must be linearly
dependent. And the equality span(N(S)) = VR[span(S)] when VR is the span of
{wv∗: (v, w) ∈R}, noted earlier, shows that only the span of the matrices wv∗
matters. Thus, even if R is infinite, it can always be reduced to at most mn pairs
(whose corresponding rank one matrices are linearly independent) without affecting
span(N(S)) for any S ⊆Fn. Theorem 1.1 can then be applied to the reduced R.
This yields the following.
Corollary 1.6. In Theorem 1.1, R ⊆Fn × Fm need not be assumed finite.
The original combinatorial formulation of Hall’s theorem in [6] was framed in
terms of “transversals” rather than perfect matchings: if S1, . . . , Sn are sets, and for
each 1 ≤k ≤n the union of any k of them has cardinality at least k, then there exist
distinct elements s1, . . . , sn such that si ∈Si for all i. The marriage interpretation
can be recovered by letting Si be the set of girls that the ith boy knows. Thus the
graph-theoretic and combinatorial formulations are trivially equivalent. However,
the combinatorial formulation suggests a different linear generalization, this one
due to Rado [12, Theorem 1].
Corollary 1.7. (Rado’s theorem) Let m ≥n ≥1 and let S1, . . ., Sn be subsets
of Fm. Suppose that for each 1 ≤k ≤n, the linear span of the union of any k
of the Si’s has dimension at least k. Then there are linearly independent vectors
w1, . . . , wn ∈Fm with wi ∈Si (1 ≤i ≤n).
Proof. Let R ⊂Fn ×Fm consist of all pairs (ei, v) such that v ∈Si. The dimension
inequality of Theorem 1.1 then holds by essentially the same argument as the one
used in the proof of Corollary 1.2. (If dim(S) = k then at least k standard basis
vectors are not orthogonal to S, so N(S) contains the union of at least k of the Si’s.)
Theorem 1.1/Corollary 1.6 therefore yields the existence of a saturated matching
(v1, w1), . . ., (vn, wn), in which, after reordering, we can assume vi = ei for all i.
Then each wi belongs to Si, and the vectors w1, . . ., wn are linearly independent.
□

1.2. K˝onig’s theorem. We will need the following strengthening of Theorem
1.1/Corollary 1.6 that allows for a “deficiency” in the condition dim span(N(S)) ≥
dim span(S).
Corollary 1.8. Let m, n ≥1 and let R ⊆Fn × Fm. Suppose there exists d ≥0
such that
dim span(N(S)) ≥dim span(S) −d
for every finite subset S of Fn. Then there exist (v1, w1), . . . , (vn−d, wn−d) ∈R
such that v1, . . ., vn−d are linearly independent in Fn and w1, . . ., wn−d are linearly
independent in Fm.
Proof. Replace Fm with Fm+d and add to R all pairs (ei, fj) with 1 ≤i ≤n
and m + 1 ≤j ≤m + d, where (ei) and (fj) are the standard bases of Fn and
Fm+d. This restores the inequality dim span(N(S)) ≥dim span(S), so that Theo-

of that result. That saturated matching will include at most d pairs of the form

□
The classical theorem of K˝onig [10] relates partial matchings of bipartite graphs
to vertex covers. The relevant definitions for us go as follows. Let R ⊆Fn × Fm.
A cover for R is a pair (E, F) of linear subspaces of Fn and Fm for which every
pair (v, w) in R satisfies either v ∈E or w ∈F (or both). Its size is the value
dim(E) + dim(F). A matching in R is a finite subset {(v1, w1), . . . , (vs, ws)} of R
such that the vectors v1, . . ., vs are linearly independent in Fn and the vectors w1,
. . ., ws are linearly independent in Fm. Its size is the number s.
Theorem 1.9. (Linear K˝onig’s theorem) Let m, n ≥1 and let R ⊆Fn ×Fm. Then
the maximum size of a matching in R equals the minimum size of a cover for R.
Proof. It is easy to see that the size of any matching is less than or equal to the
size of any cover. For supppose {(v1, w1), . . . , (vs, ws)} is a matching and (E, F) is
a cover. Let I1 = {1 ≤i ≤s : vi ∈E} and I2 = {1 ≤i ≤s : vi ̸∈E}. Then we
must have wi ∈F for every i ∈I2, so s = |I1| + |I2| ≤dim(E) + dim(F).
Conversely, let (E, F) be a cover of minimum size and let d = n −(dim(E) +
dim(F)), so that n−d is the minimum size of a cover. I claim that dim span(N(S)) ≥
dim span(S)−d for every finite subset S ⊂Fn. If this were to fail for some S, then we
could let E′ = span(S)⊥and F ′ = span(N(S)). Then dim(E′) = n −dim span(S)
and, by assumption, dim(F ′) < dim span(S)−d, so that dim(E′)+dim(F ′) < n−d.
But (E′, F ′) is easily seen to be a cover for R, contradicting minimality of (E, F).
We can therefore apply Corollary 1.8 and obtain a matching of size n −d, which
is the minimum size of a cover.
□

1.3. Dilworth’s theorem. The theorems of Hall and K˝onig belong to a circle of
ideas in combinatorics that also includes results such as theorems of Dilworth and
Menger, to which they may be said to be “equivalent” in the sense that each of these
results is more or less easily deduced from the others. Depending on how loosely
one interprets “easily deduced”, this circle of ideas could be expanded further [1].
Dilworth’s theorem states that the minimum number of chains needed in a par-
tition of a finite poset into chains equals the maximum cardinality of an antichain
in the poset. This is a little more distant from the theorems of Hall and K˝onig then
they are to each other, because it deals with partially ordered sets rather than bi-
partite graphs. So before we can formulate linear and matrix analogs of it, we first
need to identify the appropriate linear and matrix versions of a partially ordered
set. The latter was done in [14]: in finite dimensions, the matrix version of a strict
partial order (“strict” meaning that we axiomatize ≻, not ⪰) is a nilpotent operator
algebra, i.e., a linear subspace V of Mn (= Mn,n(F)) which satisfies V2 ⊆V and
Vn = {0}.
The linear version of this definition goes as follows. Let a linorder be a relation
R ⊂Fn × Fn with the properties (1) if (v, w) and (˜v, ˜w) both belong to R, and
w ̸⊥˜v, then (v, ˜w) belongs to R; (2) (v, w) ∈R implies v ⊥w. The first condition
is a version of transitivity, and the second is a version of antisymmetry in the form
a ≻b →a ̸= b. It is routine to check that span{wv∗: (v, w) ∈R} is a nilpotent
operator algebra, the key point being that ˜w˜v∗· wv∗is zero if ⟨w, ˜v⟩= ˜v∗w = 0
and a nonzero multiple of ˜wv∗if ⟨w, ˜v⟩= ˜v∗w ̸= 0. Conversely, every rank one
generated nilpotent operator algebra is the linear span of a finite relation R with
the above properties.
A bi-chain for a linorder is a sequence (w1, v1, . . . , wr, vr) such that wi ̸⊥vi for
1 ≤i ≤r and (vi, wi+1) ∈R for 1 ≤i < r. Its length is the value r. A decomposition
of Fn into bi-chains is a family of bi-chains whose v’s collectively form a basis and
whose w’s collectively form a basis. Its size is the number of bi-chains in the family.
An antichain for R is a linear subspace C of Fn for which every (v, w) ∈R satisfies
v ⊥C or w ⊥C (or both). Equivalently, PVRP = {0} where P is the orthogonal
projection onto C.
Recall from the last section that a cover for R is a pair of linear subspaces E
and F of Fn such that every (v, w) ∈R satisfies either v ∈E or w ∈F (or both),
and its size is the value dim(E) + dim(F).

Lemma 1.12. Let n ≥1 and let R ⊂Fn × Fn be a linorder. Then the maximum
dimension of an antichain for R equals n minus the minimum size of a cover for
R.
Proof. Let (E, F) be a cover for R of minimum size. Then C = E⊥∩F ⊥= (E+F)⊥
is an antichain for R, and the size of the cover is
dim(E) + dim(F) = dim(E + F) + dim(E ∩F) = n −dim(C) + dim(E ∩F).
So dim(C) ≥n −(dim(E) + dim(F)). This shows that the maximum dimension of
an antichain is at least n minus the minimum size of a cover.
Conversely, let C be an antichain for R of maximum dimension; we must find a
cover whose size is exactly n −dim(C). The desired cover is F = span(N(C)) and
E = (C + F)⊥. First, we evaluate its size. Since (v, w) ∈R contributes to N(C)
only if v ̸⊥C, the antichain property requires w ⊥C for every such pair. This
shows that N(C) ⊥C, and hence dim(C + F) = dim(C) + dim(F) and therefore
dim(E) + dim(F) = (n −dim(C) −dim(F)) + dim(F) = n −dim(C).
So (E, F) has the correct size, and we must now show that it is a cover.
Let (v, w) ∈R. Assume v ̸∈E; we must prove w ∈F. Since v ̸∈E, either v ̸⊥C
or v ̸⊥N(C). In the first case, we immediately get w ∈N(C) ⊆F. In the second
case, there must exist (˜v, ˜w) ∈R such that ˜v ̸⊥C and ˜w ̸⊥v. Then condition (1)
in the definition of a linorder yields (˜v, w) ∈R, which again entails w ∈N(C) ⊆F.
We conclude that (E, F) is a cover, as desired.

Theorem 1.13. (Linear Dilworth’s theorem) Let n ≥1 and let R ⊂Fn × Fn be
a linorder. Then the minimum size of a decomposition into bi-chains equals the
maximum dimension of an antichain.
Proof. Let (E, F) be a cover of minimum size and suppose we are given a decom-
position into bi-chains. Letting a be the total number of v’s, over all the bi-chains
in the decomposition, that belong to E, and letting b be the total number of w’s
that belong to F, we get that a ≤dim(E) and b ≤dim(F) (since the v’s are lin-
early independent, as are the w’s). And for each bi-chain (w1, v1, . . . , wr, vr) in the
decomposition, we know that either vi ∈E or wi+1 ∈F, for each 1 ≤i < r, which
implies that the number of v’s in the bi-chain that belong to E plus the number
