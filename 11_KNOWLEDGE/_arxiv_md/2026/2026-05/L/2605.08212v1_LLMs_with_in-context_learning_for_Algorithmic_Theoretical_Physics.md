Abstract
There is an increasing number of algorithmic com-
putations in theoretical physics. These, while
conceptually simple, can nevertheless be time-
consuming and contain subtleties that should not
be overlooked. Given the recent improvement of
Large Language Models (LLM), it is natural to
investigate whether LLMs equipped with a com-
puter algebra system (CAS) runtime and suffi-
ciently informative context can reliably carry out
these algorithmic tasks. In this work, we interface
Claude with Maple, and apply this framework to
cosmological perturbations in modified theories
of gravity. We demonstrate the current capabili-
ties of this approach, the typical failures, and how
the same can be improved. We find that a frontier
LLM supplied with worked examples is able to
solve most test problems.
1. Introduction
Theoretical physicists deal with a range of tasks in their
research. Between the most mechanistic calculations which
have been relegated to numerical and symbolic computer
programs, and the art of constructing new theories born
out of genuine creativity, lies a large field of “algorithmic
computations”. By this we mean tasks that are sufficiently
complex that a general solution in terms of a deterministic
computer program is unavailable, while at the same time
not being of such difficulty that entirely new methods and
ways of thinking are required for their solution. To bracket
algorithmic computations by examples, we mean tasks more
complex than individual commands in a computer algebra
system, but less complex than the discovery of AdS-CFT
correspondence or certain proofs in Einstein gravity.
This leaves a large terrain of computations for which a
generic recipe exists while the details vary from problem
to problem and certain subtleties may require special care.
Perturbative calculations (like scattering matrices) in renor-
malizable QFT, string theory, or EFTs are typical examples.
More familiar to general physicists would be typical prob-
lems in classical electrodynamics or perturbation theory in
quantum mechanics.
Such algorithmic computations currently take up much time
for many theoretical physicists, even with the help of com-
puter algebra systems (CAS). At the same time, their relative
simplicity implies that their solution using agentic AI might
be within reach. The algorithmic nature and frequency of
these problems implies that a large set of worked examples
is available and an abstract description of the algorithm can
be written down. In this work, we use both worked exam-
ples and the algorithm’s description for in-context learning
and test which contents in the context optimize success rate.
We thus test in this work the ability of a frontier large lan-
guage model, interfaced with a CAS session and equipped
with an informative context, to solve problems from a spe-
cific class of algorithmic computations: identifying the de-
grees of freedom for cosmological backgrounds in various
theories of gravity. Cosmological perturbations, small rip-
ples in spacetime and matter, give rise to the structure of
the Universe that we observe today (Starobinsky, 1979;
Mukhanov & Chibisov, 1981; Chibisov & Mukhanov, 1982;
Kodama & Sasaki, 1984; Sasaki, 1986; Mukhanov et al.,
1992). They represent the degrees of freedom, the building
blocks of theories, which determine their stability and ob-
servational consequences. Identifying them is thus essential,
and can be non-trivial when departing from Einstein’s Gen-
eral Relativity. Several approaches have been developed
with this purpose, including the Hamiltonian analysis with a
Dirac-Bergmann algorithm (Bergmann, 1949; Dirac, 1950;
Anderson & Bergmann, 1951), and a Faddeev-Jackiw ap-
proach (Faddeev & Jackiw, 1988), as well as Lagrangian
formalism (see Hell et al. (2026) for a general prescription,
and references therein), and a more rigorously mathematical
approach founded directly in the equations of motion (see
Heisenberg (2025) and references therein). In this work, we
follow the recipe given in Hell et al. (2026), which allows
for the easiest and most straight-forward implementation in
the context of cosmological perturbations.

For the purposes of a rigorous study, algorithmic computa-
tions of cosmological perturbations have some distinct ad-
vantages. It is easy to construct test problems with research-
grade complexity and with such obscure features that they
are impossible to have been encountered in the LLMs train-
ing set. In fact, in our evaluation we sometimes observe
the LLM attempting to relate the task to some better-known
problem from the literature. At the same time, the type of
problems have unique and easily checked solutions.
Our work aims to establish how much theoretical physicists
can benefit from automated computation specifically in al-
gorithmic problems. To this end, we answer the following
questions:
1. What are current capabilities of frontier LLMs
equipped with a CAS runtime;
2. How should the LLM’s context be used;
3. What are typical failure modes and how can they be
mitigated.
Thanks to the restricted set of problems and our available
expert knowledge, we can understand the LLM’s solution
strategies beyond simply checking the final answer, thus
enabling a complementary qualitative evaluation. A central
aim of this work is to develop practical guidelines for how
practitioners can employ their existing worked examples
as useful context for LLMs to autonomously tackle similar
problems.
As we demonstrate in Sec. 2, most work on LLMs equipped
with a symbolic computation tool has concentrated on math-
ematics. Compared to these works, we focus less on the
model’s creativity in finding proof strategies and more on its
ability to perform relatively routine computations that are
simply burdensome for the human physicist. Note, however,
that these computations are not necessarily easy, and the use
of a CAS does not guarantee correctness. Another unusual
aspect of this work is the difficulty in defining a locally
relevant context: some manipulations need to be carried
out with later steps in mind (for example, solution of back-
ground equations may produce roots if done naively, which
would lead to problems in subsequent steps). Thus, algorith-
mic theoretical physics is an interesting testing ground for
long contexts.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In
Sec. 2, we discuss previous works applying LLMs equipped
with CAS to mathematics and physics problems. In Sec. 3,
we describe our setup, experiments, and evaluation strategy.
In Sec. 4, we summarize our results both qualitatively and
quantitatively. We conclude in Sec. 5.
2. Related Work
Using structure in an LLM’s response goes back to chain-of-
thought (Wei et al., 2023). A natural extension of this idea
is to interface the model with tools (e.g., Gao et al., 2023;
Chen et al., 2023). Such tool-aided reasoning has found
widespread application in mathematics, thanks to the strict
verifiability afforded by programs such as Lean (e.g., Gou
et al., 2024; Khaitan & Ganesh, 2025; Breen et al., 2025;
Nezhad et al., 2026; Luo et al., 2026). Not surprisingly,
tool use is found to be especially helpful for more difficult
problmes (Das et al., 2024). In a similar fashion to this
work, in-context learning (e.g., Dong et al., 2024) has been
studied as a way to improve the LLM’s performance in
math problems (e.g., Liu et al., 2024; Sultan et al., 2026;
Gao et al., 2026). In contrast to explicitly fine-tuned models,
general purpose models with useful context are easier to use
in a human-AI collaboration and can be more easily adapted
if features in the tools (e.g., Lean strategies) change (Breen
et al., 2025).
In the context of theoretical physics, using LLMs for auto-
mated problem solving has so far been explored less than
in mathematics. Part of the reason may be the difficulty in
finding research-grade but still easily gradeable problems;
recently there have been efforts towards constructing such
benchmarks (Chung et al., 2025; Pan et al., 2026) and using
them to evaluate test-time scaling (Gao et al., 2025). These
works find that without dedicated tools the current frontier
models are unable to solve most research-grade benchmark
problems. A recent paper that is similar in spirit to our work
is Menzo et al. (2026): they let an LLM use a specialized
Mathematica package to compute Feynman diagrams. In
contrast to our work, their LLM operates in a much more
constrained environment: its only real decision is about a
small number of binary choices to do with conventions. Our
work is focused on problems that require more actual intel-
ligence to come up with calculation strategies and deal with
subtleties. Much more ambitiously, some recent works (e.g.,
Agrawal et al., 2026) attempt to let artificial agents perform
the entire pipeline of theoretical physics research. Others
argue that this approach is unlikely to be successful (Lu
et al., 2026). We do not aim to replace the human researcher
entirely and thus restrict the experiments to algorithmic the-
oretical physics problems. Similarly to Lu et al. (2026),
we anticipate that equipping the LLM with a CAS is likely
useful.
One of the main aims of this work is to establish how well a
current frontier model can use examples or other informa-
tion provided in the context. Indeed, it has been noted that
long contexts can degrade performance (e.g., Li et al., 2024;
Liu et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024; Du et al., 2025; Huang et al.,
2024), even specifically for mathematics (Liu et al., 2024).
These findings suggest that a small set of well-chosen ex-

3.1. The basics of dof
In general, the number of degrees of freedom (dof) of a phys-

to completely determine the Cauchy problem of equations
of motion divided by two. For example, a free particle in

To completely determine the Cauchy problem, we need to
determine two initial conditions – the initial position and
velocities q(t0) and ˙q(t0) – which thus gives one dof. In
quantum field theories and theories of gravity one more
often studies the dynamics of fields. In contrast to a free
particle, such objects also depend also on the point in space,
with a prime example being the scalar field ϕ(⃗x, t). For-
mally speaking, a theory of a standard free canonical scalar
field in four dimensions thus has an infinite number of dof,
and so when we state this, we will refer to their number per
point in space-time.
In flat and cosmological backgrounds, the space-time has
spherical symmetry which gives rise to three well-known
types of perturbations that we classify according to the group
of spatial rotations: scalar, vector and tensor modes. How-
ever, if we consider a theory of gravity, where the main
quantity in addition to matter is the metric tensor, gµν, not
all of its components will be physical degrees of freedom.
For example, general relativity has two dof instead of ten
which correspond to all components of the metric tensor,
meaning that the remaining ones are not propagating. While
there are many ways to show that this holds, in this work, we
focus only on actions that are quadratic in the perturbations
around particular backgrounds based on the Lagrangian for-
mulation, and determine the physical modes according to
the procedure described in (Hell et al., 2026).
3.2. Implementation
Our experimental setup is relatively simple: the context and
problem formulation are input via the LLM’s API, which
produces CAS commands to be locally executed in the run-
time read-eval-print loop (REPL), CAS results are appended
to the message history and sent back via the API, and so
forth until the LLM decides a solution has been found (or
aborts). We choose to work with a single artificial agent
since the considered computations are relatively linear and
we do not expect a multi-agent environment would yield
substantial improvements. We call the API directly instead
of relying on a ready-made SDK (e.g., Claude Code) since
reproducible model ability is important for our experiments.
We choose to work with a fixed, typically rather long, con-
text for multiple reasons. First, it enables us to systemati-
cally study the impact of different materials in the context.
Second, frontier models are getting increasingly better with
long contexts and have reached impressive capabilities al-
ready (Agarwal et al., 2024). Third, given the specialized
nature of the algorithmic computations we are aiming at, we
anticipate practitioners to want a simple prescription how
to apply automated computation for their own work which
would usually rely on private example calculations. Fourth,
the considered computations can despite their algorithmic
linear nature benefit from a “look-ahead”, as discussed in
the introduction.
For the LLM, we use Claude Opus 4-6, and for the CAS
we use Maple. It is important to turn the human-oriented
printing off on Maple (prettyprint=0). We find that
the LLM relatively frequently restarts to a fresh Maple ses-
sion when it notoices that it is stuck; we include this for our
quantitative analysis below. Most of our results are obtained
with max tokens=1024 and thinking turned off; we dis-
cuss experiments with thinking turned on in Sec. 4.5. We
run each problem only once because we observe relatively
stable behavior when repeating some runs. To control run-
time, we set a maximum limit of 100 turns at which point
the REPL gets aborted. Empirically, we find that the turns
limit interrupts the loop in situations that do not seem to be
leading to success.

In creating these problems, we only focus on tensor, or
scalar-tensor theories in flat and cosmological backgrounds.
A more comprehensive study including also other type
of theories, such as vector, tensor or two-form theories
will be presented in follow-up work. Aside from the first
problem (R2Fs), remaining problems are built on the re-

ously worked out in the literature. The problems also test
Claude on a wide range of issues, commonly found in study-
ing the perturbations, such as finding special background
solutions that give rise to multiple branches, resolving the
constraints, dealing with higher-derivatives and reducing
their order, as well as analyzing different types of the pertur-
bations. R2Fs, in contrast, corresponds to the Starobinsky
inflation, which is one of the most well-known models in
Cosmology, and serves as a reference point.
3.4. Output evaluation
We base our evaluation of the results on a pass/fail basis.
The solutions to the problems can be divided into four main
steps: the setup, computing the background equations of
motion, finding the second-order action, and analyzing the
perturbations. Theories that also involve higher-derivative
terms have an additional step in reduction of the deriva-
tives that can happen either in the setup or at the level of
perturbations.
Based on this, we adopt the following rules for the evalua-
tion:
1. If the setup is wrong, it is a fail.
2. If the background equations of motion are not com-
puted, or not properly substituted in the action, and
this affects the result, then it is a fail. Otherwise, if this
does not change the result, it is a pass.

3.5. Contexts
In the spirit of in-context learning, we supplement our
Claude-Maple interface with four choices of context:
Context “10ex”: A set of 10 solved examples, 60k tokens
As context, we prepare 10 step-by-step solved and explained
examples that would represent problems of finding prop-
agating dofs of theories of gravity in cosmology and flat
space-time. For illustration, an explicit presentation of one
of these examples is given in the Appendix A. Most of these
examples are just standard examples, meant to cover differ-
ent simplifications and Maple-code when studying scalar,
vector or tensor perturbations. Specially, they do not involve
any higher derivatives but are at most quadratic in the fields.
Context “3broad”: A set of 3 solved examples, 18k to-
kens We test the case when the number of examples is
overall reduced to three. This also reduces the overall cost
of the project. To still keep it general enough, we select three
representatives, corresponding to the Example 2, Example
3 and Example 8, which is stated in Appendix A.
Context “3tailored”: A modified set of 3 solved examples,
24k tokens As we will see in the results, attempts to some
problems with contexts 10ex and 3broad fail with relatively
common mistakes. To examine this further, we keep the
Example 3 and Example 8 as part of the input data, and
supplement the two with another modified example that is
supposed to account for the common mistakes, given in
Sec. 4.3.
Context “instruction”: A general description of the
method, 2k tokens Finally, we provide as an input just
a general instruction text based on the recent work (Hell
et al., 2026), and with no example code. This recipe is given
in the Appendix B.

5. Conclusions
In their daily work, theoretical physicists encounter many
problems of algorithmic character. We explore how well
a frontier LLM equipped with a CAS session can handle
such problems. Specifically, we focus on finding the de-
grees of freedom in modified theories of gravity and utilize
completely novel problems for evaluation.
Equipped with qualitative and quantitative evaluations de-
scribed before, we can now answer the questions posed in
the introduction as follows:
1. What are current capabilities of frontier LLMs
equipped with a CAS runtime? When supplied with
worked examples, the LLM is able to competently use
the CAS REPL and in a majority of cases solves the
test problems correctly. We evaluate the LLM’s ability
on par with a first-year graduate student.
2. How should the LLM’s context be used? We find that
worked examples are essential for the LLM to succeed
whereas an abstract description of the algorithm is not
sufficient. Pracitioners will typically have worked ex-
amples available from their prior work; we recommend
to use such examples for in-context learning. We find
that a smaller set of example problems, chosen for rep-
resentativeness, is more effective than a large set (even
though in our quantitative evaluation the smaller repre-
sentative set 3broad introduced a very specific failure
mode of misunderstanding the problem). A small, tar-
geted set can mitigate observed failure modes but may
introduce new issues.
3. What are typical failure modes and how can they be
mitigated? One common failure was misinterpretation
of the problem (typically, because the solution of the
actual problem was deemed “trivial” – not unlike stu-
dents in exams). A human in the loop would be able
to correct this type of problem. Other mistakes such
as failure to correctly apply the background equations
are more worrisome but would be caught by an expe-
rienced supervisor. We find that tailoring the context
with hand-crafted examples that cover observed failure
modes can be effective in reducing mistakes.
While the recommendations to choose the context delib-
erately and utilize human-AI collaboration are expected,
we also make some unexpected findings. The test prob-
lems were tailored to contain subtleties such as branching
solutions. Our expectation that their existence would be
overlooked was wrong: the LLM identified most of the
special cases correctly and used some of them to simplify
the analysis. We had also expected that enabling thinking
would be more useful, especially for the cases with an ab-
stract description of the algorithm in the context. However,
the model makes little use of the thinking budget and does
not improve performance.
Two aspects of the LLM’s behavior are noteworthy. First, it
shows strong perseverance and goal-orientedness. In several
examples we observe its pursuing a wrong path, noticing
that it is getting nowhere, and then restarting the Maple
session for a new attempt. Second, the LLM shows a good
ability to consider the problems abstractly. Sometimes a
problem prevents it from correctly solving the background
equations of motion, at which point it proceeds with what-
ever it can accomplish in the rest of the computation while
keeping the background solution as an abstract object. Later
it tries to go back and obtain the concrete solution.
We have only explored one class of algorithmic theoretical
physics problems, with a single frontier LLM, and with a
single CAS, and a fixed context. In future work we will
expand on these and especially evaluate a RAG-style setup
in which the model can pull specific parts of the example
calculations into context. As we have observed strong per-
formance on the considered test problems, we believe that
CAS-equipped LLMs with in-context learning will be use-
ful for other algorithmic computations across string theory,
quantum field theory, gravity, and cosmology.

B. The recipe for the context instructions
Work-out the perturbations based on the following the general instructions:
The following is VERY IMPORTANT: SET interface(prettyprint = 0):
(A) Specify the background One of the main reasons to study any theory is to describe physical phenomena, such as, for example, the
acceleration of the Universe, black-holes, or cosmological defects. These are the solutions to the background equations of motion, found
by varying the action with respect to fields. Notably, depending on the background, the number of degrees of freedom (dof) can change.
Therefore, when performing the analysis to calculate the dof, it is best to assume that the background is always satisfied. One can
ensure this by simply assuming that the background equations of motion always hold at any step in the computation. (B) Prepare the
perturbations Once you specify the background (which can also be trivial), it’s time to perturb around it. These perturbations will provide
the notion of the degrees of freedom. To find them, there are several preparatory things to consider before studying their corresponding
action. In particular, it is useful to answer the following questions to decide on the best way forward: (i) Does your system have any
symmetries? Here, by symmetries, we mean what is left after one separates the time and space components. If then the answer is yes, this
might suggest the form of your perturbations, from a general study of components of your field to a much simpler form. For example,
in space-times that are homogeneous and isotropic, it is possible to do a decomposition of the modes according to the group of spatial
rotations, and decompose the fields into scalars, vectors, and tensors. If the space-time has broken anisotropies in one direction and three
spatial dimensions, one can instead decompose the components of the fields into even and odd modes.
(B) Does your system have any gauge redundancy? If yes, analyzing first how the perturbations are transforming under gauge redundancy
could be very beneficial: if one fixes the gauge such that it matches the gauge invariant variables, it can decrease the length of the
computation. Other gauges are possible too, as long as one makes sure that the gauge is fully fixed. It should be stressed that the above
two questions can be avoided – it is perfectly fine if one considers all components of the fields, and does not even fix the gauge. These two
decisions would lead to equivalent results, but, also can make the computation significantly longer.
(C) Expand the action Once one decides on the background and the type of perturbations around it, it is time to study the perturbations
themselves. This can be analyzed either on the level of action or directly on the level of equations of motion. However, studying them
directly from the action is more beneficial, since one can then have a clear view into the conditions under which the degrees of freedom
are well behaved, such as the positivity of the factors multiplying their kinetic terms, necessary to avoid the ghost modes.
In general, one is supposed to expand the action to the required order in perturbations. If there is a non-vanishing background for the
fields, the leading order corresponds to the equations of motion, which are subsequently satisfied. Thus, at first order, the corrections
vanish. The kinetic terms for the perturbations are then at second order in the action, and one can also study higher orders to analyze
the non-linear corrections. Note that this also holds if the background is trivial, eg, including vanishing background values of the fields,
together with the Minkowski space-time.
(D) Analyze the theory Once the action is ready, it is time to analyze the modes. Let us first assume that there are at most two time
derivatives acting on the fields, and that all constraints are holonomic. Then, essentially, the procedure to find the propagating modes is
given by the following: (i) Locate the fields that are not propagating – the constrained modes. (ii) Find their corresponding constraint by
varying the action with respect to them. (iii) Solve it, and substitute it back into the action. Note that in the case of non-linearities, this is
usually done perturbatively. (iv) Check if the determinant of the kinetic matrix for the Lagrangian density of fields is vanishing. If yes,
this means that there are more non-propagating fields. Locate them in the Lagrangian density. If all of them appear with the form where
they carry terms quadratic in time-derivatives, this means that one should perform a substitution, which will render one of them to be
non-propagating, and then one can repeat the procedure (i) – (iv). (v) If, after repeating the procedure, the determinant of the kinetic
matrix is not vanishing, and your system contains no higher derivatives, then you have found the Lagrangian density in terms of the
propagating modes.
(E) Higher-order derivatives The above procedure works for a wide range of theories. However, one can also easily encounter theories that
contain higher-order derivatives. Luckily, this does not drastically complicate the analysis. The key to analyzing it is to reduce the order
of derivatives until one reaches maximally two time derivatives per term in the Lagrangian.
