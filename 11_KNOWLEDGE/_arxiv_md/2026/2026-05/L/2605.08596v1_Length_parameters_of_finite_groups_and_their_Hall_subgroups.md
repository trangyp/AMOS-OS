LENGTH PARAMETERS OF FINITE GROUPS
AND THEIR HALL SUBGROUPS
EVGENY KHUKHRO AND PAVEL SHUMYATSKY
Abstract. Let π be a set of primes containing 2 and an odd prime p. It is proved that if
a finite group G has a Hall π-subgroup H, then the non-p-soluble length of G is bounded
above by the generalized Fitting height of H. The proof uses the fact, obtained in [4] using
the classification of finite simple groups, that a finite simple group of order divisible by p
cannot have a nilpotent Hall {2, p}-subgroup. As a corollary, it is proved that if in addition
H is soluble, then the non-p-soluble length of G is bounded above by 2l2(H) + 1, where
l2(H) is the 2-length of H.
1. Introduction
In [4,27], there have been obtained some criteria for the existence of nilpotent or abelian
Hall π-subgroups in finite groups. In particular, with the use of the classification of finite
simple groups, it was proved in [4, Theorem 2.2] that for any odd prime p, a finite simple
group of order divisible by p cannot have a nilpotent Hall {2, p}-subgroup. In this note we
use this result to show that if a finite group G has a Hall π-subgroup H for a set of primes
π containing 2 and an odd prime p, then the non-p-soluble length of G is bounded above by
the generalized Fitting height of H.
We now recall some definitions and fix some notation. The generalized Fitting subgroup
F ∗(G) of a finite group G is the characteristic subgroup generated by the Fitting subgroup
F(G) and all the subnormal quasisimple subgroups (a group Q is quasisimple if [Q, Q] = Q
and Q/Z(Q) is simple). The generalized Fitting series of G is defined recursively by F ∗

For a given prime p, the non-p-soluble length λp(G) of a finite group G is the minimum
number of non-p-soluble factors in a normal series where each factor is either p-soluble or
a direct product of non-abelian simple groups of orders divisible by p. In particular, G is
p-soluble exactly when its non-p-soluble length is 0.
We can now state our main result.
Theorem. Suppose that a finite group G has a Hall π-subgroup H for a set of primes π
containing 2 and an odd prime p. Then the non-p-soluble length of G is bounded above by
the generalized Fitting height of H, that is, λp(G) ⩽h∗(H).
The proof relies on the classification of finite simple groups. As a corollary, we obtain a
stronger conclusion in the case of a soluble Hall subgroup.
Corollary. Suppose that a finite group G has a soluble Hall π-subgroup H for a set of primes
π containing 2 and an odd prime p. Then the non-p-soluble length of G is bounded above by
2l2(H) + 1, where l2(H) is the 2-length of H.

This corollary is of independent interest, since the Fitting height of a soluble finite group
cannot be bounded above in terms of its 2-length.
We introduced the generalized Fitting height and the non-p-soluble length of a finite
group in [19], but the bounds for the non-soluble length (which is the same as non-2-
soluble length) and generalized Fitting height had been implicitly used earlier, for example,
in the reduction of the Restricted Burnside Problem to soluble and nilpotent groups in
the Hall–Higman paper [17].
Such bounds also play an important role in the study of
profinite groups (see for example [2, 3, 10, 11, 18, 23–25, 29–32, 34, 35]). Some recent results
concerning non-p-soluble length and generalized Fitting height of finite groups can be found
in [1,5–9,12,14–16,20–22,26,28,33].
2. Preliminaries
Let π be a set of primes. Recall that H is a Hall π-subgroup of a finite group G if its order
|H| is divisible only by the primes in π, while its index |G : H| is not divisible by any prime
in π. It is an easy consequence of Lagrange’s theorem that if N is a normal subgroup of G,
then H ∩N is a Hall π-subgroup of N. This immediately implies that if S is a subnormal
subgroup of G, then H ∩S is a Hall π-subgroup of S. It is also clear that HN/N is a Hall
π-subgroup of the quotient G/N.
Let G be a finite group, and p a prime. One can easily show that the non-p-soluble length
(defined in the Introduction) behaves well under taking normal subgroups and homomorphic
images. It is also clear that an extension of a normal subgroup of non-p-soluble length k by
a group of non-p-soluble length l has non-p-soluble length at most k + l.
One of the ways of realizing the non-p-soluble length of G is based on the so-called p-kernel
subgroups. Let Rp(G) denote the p-soluble radical of G, which is the largest normal p-soluble
subgroup. If Rp(G) ̸= G, that is, G is not p-soluble, then in the quotient ¯G = G/Rp(G)
the socle Soc( ¯G), which is the product of all minimal normal subgroups of ¯G, is a direct
product Soc( ¯G) = S1 × · · · × Sm of non-abelian simple groups Si of orders divisible by p.
The group G induces by conjugation a permutational action on the set {S1, . . . , Sm}. We
call the kernel Kp(G) of this action the p-kernel subgroup of G. Clearly, Kp(G) is the full
inverse image in G of T
i N ¯G(Si).
The groups of outer automorphisms of finite simple groups are soluble, which is known
as the validity of Schreier’s Conjecture confirmed by the classification. It follows that the
quotient of Kp(G) by the inverse image of the socle Soc( ¯G) is soluble; in particular, Kp(G)
has non-p-soluble length 1.
For uniformity, we also put Kp(G) = G if Rp(G) = G, that is, when G is p-soluble and
its non-p-soluble length is 0. Thus, we have in all cases the following.
Lemma 2.1 (see, for example, [19, Lemma 2.1]). The non-p-soluble length of Kp(G) is at
most 1.
One can also define the higher p-kernel subgroups by induction: Kp,1(G) = Kp(G), and
Kp,i+1(G) is the full inverse image of Kp(G/Kp,i(G)). Actually, the non-p-soluble length of
G is the least i such that G/Kp,i is p-soluble, although we do need this fact.
Recall that the p-length lp(G) of a p-soluble finite group G is the number of p-factors in a
shortest normal series of G in which each factor is either a p-groups or has order coprime to
p. By the Feit–Thompson theorem [13], 2-soluble groups are soluble. A soluble finite group
has Hall π-subgroups for any set of primes π.

3. Proofs
We now prove the Theorem. Recall that G is a finite group that has a Hall π-subgroup H
of order divisible by 2 and a prime p ̸= 2. We need to show that the non-p-soluble length of
G is at most the generalized Fitting height of H, that is, λp(G) ⩽h∗(H). We shall proceed
by a straightforward induction on h∗(H) once the following proposition is proved.
Proposition 3.1. The generalized Fitting subgroup of H is contained in the p-kernel of G,
that is, F ∗(H) ⩽Kp(G).
Proof. We can clearly assume that the p-soluble radical of G is trivial.
Let Soc(G) =
S1 ×· · ·×Sk be the socle of G, where the Si are nonabelian simple groups of orders divisible
by p. Of course, the orders of Si are also divisible by 2 by the Feit–Thompson theorem [13].
We firstly deal with the Fitting subgroup F(H).
Lemma 3.2. The Fitting subgroup of H is contained in the p-kernel of G, that is, F(H) ⩽
Kp(G).
Proof. We argue by contradiction: suppose that g ∈F(H) \ Kp(G), which means that

i = Sj for some i ̸= j. Consider Si ∩H, which is a Hall π-subgroup of Si, since Si is
subnormal in G. Note that both 2 and p divide |Si ∩H|, since 2 and p divide |Si| and
2, p ∈π.
The commutator subgroup [Si ∩H, g] is generated by the commutators [a, g] = a−1ag for
a ∈Si ∩H. Since ag ∈Sj ̸= Si, the projection of [Si ∩H, g] onto Si is equal to Si ∩H.
Since g belongs to F(H), which is normalized by Si ∩H, we have [Si ∩H, g] ⩽F(H), so
that the subgroup [Si ∩H, g] is nilpotent. Hence its projection onto Si is also nilpotent.
As a result, Si has a nilpotent Hall π-subgroup Si ∩H, and therefore also a nilpotent Hall
{2, p}-subgroup of order divisible by p. This contradicts [4, Theorem 2.2].
□

By (3.1) we have [[S1 ∩H, g], g] ⩽F(H), so that [[S1 ∩H, g], g] is nilpotent. Hence its
projection onto S1, which is S1 ∩H, is also nilpotent. But S1 ∩H is a Hall π-subgroup of
S1, since S1 is subnormal. Thus, S1 has a nilpotent Hall π-subgroup, and therefore also a
nilpotent Hall {2, p}-subgroup of order divisible by p, since both 2 and p divide |S1| and
2, p ∈π. This contradicts [4, Theorem 2.2].
□
Proof of the Theorem. Induction on h∗(H). If h∗(H) = 1, then H = F ∗(H) ⩽Kp(G) by
Proposition 3.1. Then |G/Kp(G)| is not divisible by p and therefore G/Kp(G) is p-soluble.
Since λp(Kp(G)) ⩽1 by Lemma 2.1, it follows that λp(G) ⩽1, as required.
In general, since F ∗(H) ⩽Kp(G) by Proposition 3.1, the generalized Fitting height of
the image ¯H of H in G/Kp(G) is smaller than h∗(H).
By induction, λp(G/Kp(G)) ⩽
h∗( ¯H) ⩽h∗(H) −1. Since λp(Kp(G)) ⩽1 by Lemma 2.1, the result follows: λp(G) ⩽
1 + λp(G/Kp(G)) ⩽1 + h∗(H) −1 = h∗(H).
□
We now prove the Corollary.
Proof of the Corollary. Recall that G is a finite group that has a soluble Hall π-subgroup
H for a set of primes π containing 2 and an odd prime p. We want to obtain a bound for
the non-p-soluble length of G in terms of the 2-length of H. Since H is soluble, it has a
Hall {2, p}-subgroup T, which is also a Hall {2, p}-subgroup of G. Clearly, T has a normal
series of length at most 2l2(T) + 1 each factor in which is either a 2-group or a p-group.
All these factors are nilpotent, and therefore the Fitting height of T is at most 2l2(T) + 1.
The 2-length of T is at most the 2-length of H. Applying the Theorem to G and its Hall
{2, p}-subgroup T we obtain λp(G) ⩽2l2(T) + 1 ⩽2l2(H) + 1.
□
