LEARNING GAUSSIAN GRAPHICAL MODELS UNDER TOTAL POSITIVITY
VIA SPECTRAL GRAPH SPARSIFICATION
IGNACIO ECHAVE-SUSTAETA RODRÍGUEZ, AIDA ABIAD, AND FRANK RÖTTGER
Abstract. Many practical data analysis tasks reduce to learning, from observed samples, how a
collection of variables depend on each other. A widely used approach is to fit a Gaussian graphical
model, which represents the dependence structure as a graph connecting the variables. In a number
of important applications, such as financial returns, gene co-expression, and climate or network
analysis, the dependencies tend to be positive: variables move together rather than offset each other.
Encoding this positivity through the constraint of multivariate total positivity of order two (MTP2)
yields an attractive estimator that produces accurate fits with no tuning required. The resulting
graphs are, however, typically much denser than the underlying ground-truth model, which makes
them hard to interpret and slow to use in any downstream task that operates on the graph. In this
work, we propose a novel highly-scalable approach for learning Gaussian graphical models from data
using spectral sparsification; we call it Spectral-MTP2. Spectral graph sparsification is a fundamental
method which aims to preserve meaningful properties of a dense graph with a sparser subgraph. We
theoretically and empirically investigate and validate our method, and show that learning Gaussian
Graphical Models under MTP2 using spectral sparsification preserves MTP2 and approximates
well the original model in terms of Kullback–Leibler divergence and Gaussian log-likelihood. In
simulations and applications to equity returns and gene expression, we observe that Spectral-MTP2
retains most of the fit quality of the denser MTP2 baseline, while producing substantially sparser
and more interpretable graphs.
1. Introduction
Gaussian graphical models are a widely used framework for representing multivariate dependence as
a graph, with edges connecting pairs of variables that interact directly after accounting for the others
[Lauritzen, 1996]. In many real-world settings, these dependencies tend to be positive: stock returns
within a sector rise and fall together with the broader market [Agrawal et al., 2022], co-expressed genes
in a biological pathway activate jointly, and neighboring weather measurements are positively correlated.
Encoding this positivity through the constraint of multivariate total positivity of order two (MTP2)
yields an attractive estimator: the corresponding maximum-likelihood fit captures positive interactions
accurately, requires no tuning parameter, and is supported by a rich and growing theoretical literature
[Fallat et al., 2017, Lauritzen et al., 2019, 2021]. The graphs returned by MTP2 estimation are, however,
typically much denser than the underlying ground-truth, retaining spurious edges that obscure the
true network. This limits both interpretability for domain experts and the tractability of downstream
tasks (visualization, clustering, graph-based prediction), since many graph-level computations are
efficient on sparse inputs but intractable in general.
Spectral graph theory has revolutionized the algorithmic treatment of large and dense graphs over
the last two decades. The spectral machinery is considered as very strong for applications in theoretical
computer science; an instance of it is the work of Spielman and Teng [2004] on spectral sparsification of
Laplacian solvers. Graph spectral sparsification is the approximation of an arbitrary graph by a sparser
graph while retaining its essential spectral properties [Spielman and Srivastava, 2011, Batson et al.,
2012]. Such approximations have been used to build nearly-linear-time Laplacian solvers [Koutis et al.,
2012] and have found applications in graph-based learning, clustering, and dimensionality reduction

In this paper, we propose to use spectral sparsification as a post-estimation step for Gaussian
MTP2 graphical models: we fit a (possibly dense) MTP2 estimate, sparsify the underlying graph, and

sparsifier of Batson et al. [2012] (BSS), which gives precise control over the edge count. We call this
estimator Spectral-MTP2, and show that it preserves the MTP2 property and approximates its input
under standard distributional and information-theoretic measures. Simulations and applications to
equity returns and gene expression illustrate that Spectral-MTP2 retains most of the fit quality of the
dense MTP2 baseline while producing substantially sparser graphs that are easier to interpret and to
use downstream.
Related work. Spectral sparsification was introduced by Spielman and Teng [2004] and given a near-
optimal form by Spielman and Srivastava [2011], Batson et al. [2012], with subsequent refinements
through faster randomized constructions [Koutis et al., 2012, Fung et al., 2011] and direct sparsification
of strictly diagonally dominant M-matrices (SDDM) via approximate Gaussian elimination [Kyng and
Sachdeva, 2016]. In statistics and machine learning, this powerful spectral machinery has been used
to build nearly-linear-time samplers for Gaussian graphical models with SDDM precision matrices
[Cheng et al., 2015], to sparsify graphs prior to Laplacian-regularized regression while preserving
statistical validity [Sadhanala et al., 2016], and to combine ridge spectral sparsification with Laplacian
learning for scalable semi-supervised classification [Calandriello et al., 2018]. The closest precedent to
our work is that of Wang et al. [2022], who propose a scalable algorithm for Laplacian-constrained
Gaussian graphical models that interleaves spectral sparsification and densification to identify the
most spectrally critical edges. Our setting is strictly more general: an MTP2 precision matrix is not
necessarily a Laplacian nor an SDDM matrix, and we act as a post-estimation step with explicit
Loewner-order, log-likelihood, and KL guarantees. On the modeling side, Gaussian MTP2 graphical
models have been studied through ℓ1-penalized likelihood [Friedman et al., 2008, Slawski and Hein,
2015, Wang et al., 2020, Cai et al., 2023], the GOLAZO penalty framework of Lauritzen and Zwiernik
[2022], adaptive multi-stage estimators [Ying et al., 2023], and scalable bridge-block decomposition
algorithms [Wang et al., 2023].

2.2. Graphs, Laplacians, and spectral sparsification. We consider weighted simple graphs
G = (V, E, c) with vertex set V , edge set E, and symmetric weight function c : V × V →[0, ∞). We
write d = |V | and m = |E|. The weighted adjacency matrix is A = (cij), and the degree of vertex
i is ki := Pd
j=1 cij. The Laplacian matrix of G is LG := D −A, where D = diag(k1, . . . , kd) is the
diagonal degree matrix. For a connected weighted graph, LG is positive semidefinite, satisfies LG1 = 0,
and has rank d −1.
To compare such matrices we use the Loewner partial order on the cone of symmetric matrices: for
symmetric A, B ∈Rd×d we write A ⪯B (equivalently B ⪰A) when B −A is positive semidefinite,
that is, z⊤(B −A)z ≥0 for every z ∈Rd, and reserve A ≻0 for strict positive definiteness. When
A, B ≻0, a sandwich A ⪯B ⪯κA for some κ ≥1 is equivalent to the multiplicative two-sided bound
z⊤Az ≤z⊤Bz ≤κ z⊤Az for all z ∈Rd, and also to all eigenvalues of A−1/2BA−1/2 lying in [1, κ].
Graph Laplacians annihilate 1, so for LG, LG′ a Laplacian comparison is naturally restricted to the
orthogonal complement 1⊥.
A standard notion of approximation for weighted graphs uses this order. For ε ∈(0, 1), we say that
a weighted graph G′ on the same vertex set is an ε-spectral approximation of G if
(3)
(1 −ε)z⊤LGz ≤z⊤LG′z ≤(1 + ε)z⊤LGz
for all z ∈Rd,
equivalently (1 −ε)LG ⪯LG′ ⪯(1 + ε)LG on 1⊥. For small ε, (3) preserves the nonzero Laplacian
eigenvalues up to a factor of 1 ± ε and gives analogous bounds for normalized Laplacian eigenvalues,
effective resistances, and the associated Gaussian quadratic forms [Spielman and Srivastava, 2011].
Spectral sparsification was introduced by Spielman and Teng [2004], who showed that every weighted
graph admits a sparse spectral approximation obtained in nearly-linear time with a polylogarithmic
edge count. The randomized leverage-score sampling construction of Spielman and Srivastava [2011]
tightens this to O(ε−2d log d) edges with the spectral approximation succeeding with probability at
least 1/2. Subsequent work has developed further nearly-linear-time randomized constructions [Koutis
et al., 2012, Fung et al., 2011] and sparsification via approximate Gaussian elimination on SDDM
matrices [Kyng and Sachdeva, 2016]. Our method uses the deterministic linear-size construction of
Batson et al. [2012], stated below.

3. Spectral sparsification of Gaussian MTP2 precision matrices
Our objective is to sparsify the precision matrix of an MTP2 Gaussian distribution. Clearly,
Theorem 2.1 is not directly applicable, as the precision matrix of a non-degenerate Gaussian distribution
cannot be a Laplacian matrix. Our construction routes the sparsification through an auxiliary Laplacian
obtained by rescaling, and then transports the sparse approximation back to the original coordinates.

4. Simulation study
Throughout this section we operate within the positive-dependence Gaussian regime in which our
methodology is well posed: the population precision matrix is required to be a symmetric positive
definite M-matrix (Equation (2)), so that all partial correlations are nonnegative. The simulations
illustrate the trade-off that spectral sparsification is meant to address: the MTP2-MLE delivers a
high-quality likelihood fit but tends to over-connect the graph (including many edges in the graph
that are not in the ground-truth), while ℓ1-penalized methods such as the Graphical Lasso are sparser
but sacrifice fit quality. Spectral-MTP2 starts from the MTP2 fit and removes the least structurally
important edges, aiming to retain as much of the log-likelihood as possible while drastically reducing
the edge count.
We compare three estimators. The first is the Graphical Lasso (GLasso) of Friedman et al. [2008],
which serves as our ℓ1-penalized baseline. The second is the unpenalized MTP2-MLE [Lauritzen et al.,
2019, Lauritzen and Zwiernik, 2022], which serves as our positively dependent baseline. The third is
Spectral-MTP2 (Algorithm 1), which runs the deterministic BSS sparsifier on the auxiliary Laplacian
LB at parameter η and refits the MTP2-MLE on the resulting subgraph. Tuning parameters (λ for
GLasso, η for Spectral-MTP2) are selected by BIC on the training data. All MTP2 fits use the golazo
R package [Lauritzen and Zwiernik, 2022], and the code is included in the supplementary material.
Throughout this section we summarize each fit by five scalar quantities. In order to assess predictive
fit we use the test log-likelihood ℓ( bK; Stest) = log det bK −tr( bKStest), evaluated on the held-out sample
covariance Stest and measured in nats (units of natural logarithm loge). The edge count is the number
of off-diagonal entries of bK whose absolute value exceeds a small numerical tolerance, included to
discount the residuals the GOLAZO solver leaves on entries it is trying to zero out. As a result, the
reported count may differ from the algorithm’s intended edge set by a few entries with magnitude
near this threshold. For graph recovery against the known true edge set E⋆, write TP, FP, FN for
the numbers of estimated edges that are correct, spurious, and missed, respectively. Then precision
P = TP/(TP + FP) is the fraction of estimated edges that lie in E⋆, recall R = TP/(TP + FN) is
the fraction of true edges recovered, and the F1 score 2PR/(P + R) is their harmonic mean. Higher
precision means fewer spurious edges, higher recall means fewer missed edges, and F1 balances the two.
4.1. Scale-free graph simulation. The true precision matrix K⋆is the Laplacian of a Barabási–
Albert scale-free graph on d = 150 nodes with attachment parameter m = 2 (297 true edges, heavy-tailed
degrees with maximum 17), plus a scaled identity matrix δId (δ = 0.5) to ensure positive definiteness.
The resulting K⋆is an M-matrix and the model is MTP2. Hubs create many triangles and the leverage
distribution is highly skewed, which stresses both the randomized and the deterministic sparsifiers. We
draw ntrain = 400 training and ntest = 2,000 test observations over B = 15 replications, and evaluate
each method by test log-likelihood, edge count, and F1 against the true edge set.

6. Discussion
We have developed spectral sparsification as a post-estimation tool for Gaussian MTP2 graphical
models. Starting from an MTP2 precision matrix, the proposed procedure exposes its underlying
graph structure through a structural rescaling, applies the deterministic linear-size sparsifier of Batson
et al. [2012], and recovers a sparser MTP2 approximation of the input. We call this newly proposed
estimator Spectral-MTP2, and provide explicit theoretical guarantees on its statistical performance:
it remains MTP2, approximates its input under standard distributional and information-theoretic
measures, and inherits the consistency rate of its starting point. Across simulations on scale-free
graphs and applications to equity returns and gene expression, Spectral-MTP2 retains, and in some
regimes exceeds, the predictive performance of the dense MTP2 baseline at substantially lower edge
counts, producing simpler and more interpretable graphs.
Limitations. Some of our bounds require the spectral approximation factor to satisfy ε ≤1
2, so at
higher values of ε not all of our theoretical guarantees remain valid and Spectral-MTP2’s worst-case
behavior is less tightly controlled. Across our experiments BIC selects ε in the range [0.58, 0.90], often
beyond this regime, but Spectral-MTP2 still tracks, and on BA150 surpasses, the MTP2-MLE on test
data. Our consistency results also assume fixed dimension, and a scaling analysis as d →∞is left to
future work.
