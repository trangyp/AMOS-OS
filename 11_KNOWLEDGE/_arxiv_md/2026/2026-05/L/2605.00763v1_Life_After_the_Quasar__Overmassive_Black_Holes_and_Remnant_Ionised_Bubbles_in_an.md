Abstract
Supermassive black holes (SMBH, MBH > 108M⊙) powering luminous quasars
already exist one billion years after the Big Bang, yet their connection to their
star-forming host galaxies, their relation to the general galaxy population and
their contribution to Reionisation remains deeply enigmatic [1, 2]. JWST is
finding numerous Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) in high-redshift galaxies with

we report JWST/NIRSpec observations revealing overmassive SMBH in two
ultra-luminous Lyman-α emitters at z ∼6.6 that exhibit rare double-peaked

delayed stellar mass assembly, and propose that episodic quasar activity partially
explains the unexpected prevalence of large ionised bubbles deep into the Epoch
of Reionisation.
Keywords: Supermassive Black Holes, Quasars, Reionisation, Lyman-alpha Galaxies
The rapid growth of supermassive black holes (SMBH) in the first billion years of
the Universe is a persistent challenge for early models of black hole (BH) formation
and growth. The discovery of luminous quasars at z > 6 powered by 108 −1010 M⊙
BH implies that they grew from massive seeds (> 103 −105 M⊙) at, or close to, the
Eddington rate for most of their lifetime [1, 2, 8]. Observations in the far-infrared
regime find them to be “overmassive” with respect to their host galaxies [e.g. 1/10-
1/100, 9–11] compared to the local relation [12, 13], suggesting BH grow faster than
their host at early times. JWST is now discovering numerous AGN in z ≳5 galaxies
thanks to its transformational access to their rest-optical spectrum. Broad line AGN
are detected with JWST in ∼10−30% of star-forming galaxies and often have elevated
BH-to-stellar mass ratios [4, 5], similar to luminous quasars. It is thus highly likely
that JWST-detected AGN (or a subpopulation thereof) are the missing link between
108 −1010 M⊙BH in z > 6 quasars and heavy BH seeds accreting at or above
Eddington from z∼15 onwards [8, 14]. Linking the two populations is key to studying
the quasar duty cycle (e.g. the fraction of time the BH is accreting close to Eddington,
determining whether it grew continuously or in short super-Eddington bursts), if and
how the BH-stellar mass scaling relation changes at high-redshift [15–17], and the
potential impact of AGN on reionisation.
A potential clue to the AGN nature of luminous high-redshift galaxies is the detec-
tion of Lyman-α emission deep into the Reionisation era. At high redshift, Lyman-α
emission is expected to be detected only in intrinsically strong equivalent width emit-
ters (with a considerable velocity offset ∼1000−2000 km s−1 due to the IGM damping
wing) or in objects located in large ionised bubbles [18, 19]. The latter are usually
explained by the presence of large galaxy overdensities that collectively enhance the

local photo-ionisation rate [20, 21]. However, for some puzzling objects the obser-
vational data fail to reveal large enough overdensities [22]. Instead, hard ionisation
fields due to AGN activity could help ionise the IGM locally and therefore boost the
transmission of Lyman-α photons [22, 23].
This paper aims to demonstrate the connection between Lyman-α visibility and
episodic quasar activity at z > 6 using new JWST/NIRSpec Integral Field Unit
(IFU) G235M/G395M observations of two ultra luminous (LLyα ≳1043.5 [erg s−1])
z ∼6.6 Lyman-α emitters with rare double-peaked Lyman-α profiles. NEPLA4 and
COLA1 were selected using HSC NB921 imaging (isolating strong Lyman-α z ∼6.6),
and later spectroscopically confirmed with Keck and VLT [6, 7, 24]. The presence of
a blue Lyman-α peak, assuming that the trough is at systemic redshift, puts lower
limits on the minimum size (Rp ≳1 pMpc) of the ionised region surrounding these
sources [e.g. 6, 7, 18, 25]. However, simulations of reionisation predict that double-peak
profiles should be much rarer than the data suggest [26, 27], raising questions about
the nature of the necessary ionising sources. The ionising output of these two objects
is however not sufficient to keep the surrounding IGM sufficiently transparent [25],
requiring instead a large overdensity of galaxies or an additional ionising source such
as an AGN [28]. Our new JWST data reveal that both COLA1 and NEPLA4 harbour
∼2 × 108 M⊙black holes, clearly supporting the latter hypothesis with important
implications for the quasar duty cycle and Lyman-α visibility at z ∼6.6.
Results
We obtained NIRSpec IFU G235 and G395M spectroscopy (Programme #3767, PI:
Meyer) of the UV-optical (λobs = 1.7 −5.3µm, 2300 < λrest < 6900˚A) of NEPLA4
and COLA1. The data reduction is detailed in Methods (Sec 1.2) with the full spectra
shown in Extended Figure 1. The data unambiguously reveal broad hydrogen Balmer
emission lines (Fig. 1) which implies the presence of a supermassive black hole at the
centre of both COLA1 and NEPLA4 (see further Methods 1.5). We note that COLA1
and NEPLA4 do not satisfy the colour selection criteria of Little Red Dots and show
clear evidence for double-Gaussian profiles instead of exponential profiles (see Methods
1.4). This ensures we can infer their BH mass from the standard single-epoch virial
estimators using broad Balmer components.

quasars [9, 29], yet their host galaxies have modest stellar masses (see Methods). Note
that we indicate the systematic uncertainty from the single-epoch virial estimator [30]
in parenthesis. From our best-fit BAGPIPES models to the observed photometry and
spectroscopy (see Methods 1.7) we find stellar masses of log10 M∗[M⊙] = 8.93+0.04

Another key feature of COLA1 and NEPLA4 is their contrasting BH and stellar
growth history. Assuming that the black hole accreted at a constant fraction of the
Eddington rate (on average), we can extrapolate its growth trajectory towards earlier
times. As is the case for similar mass BHs at this redshift, we find that they must grow
at nearly Eddington (> 0.8λEdd) rate from z = 15 onwards if the seed BH is < 105 M⊙,
or additionally grow from lower mass seeds (∼103 M⊙) from z ∼20 onwards. Thus, for
most of their lifetime, COLA1 and NEPLA4 have harboured actively growing BHs that
would be the direct progenitors of luminous z ∼6 quasars. We show the extrapolated
growth of the black holes for different Eddington ratios λEdd = (0.1, 0.5, 1.0) in Figure
2 (left panel), alongside the galaxy stellar growth (Figure 2, middle) resulting from
the best-fit BAGPIPES non-parametric star-formation history (see Methods 1.7). The

We now demonstrate that the detection of the double-peak Lyman-α profile in
NEPLA4 and COLA1 requires a locally enhanced photo-ionisation rate that can only
be explained if the recent shutdown of the quasar engine is as recent as ∆tQSO ≲
1 Myr. Indeed, the detection of the Lyman-α double-peak necessitates the presence
of a highly ionised H II bubble at least out to r ≳0.3 pMpc
[6, 7, 25] and an
extremely low residual neutral fraction inside the bubble (xHI ∼10−6, [18, 25]). The
latter requirement is incompatible with the present ionising output of COLA1 and
NEPLA4 (see Methods). However, if the BHs in NEPLA4/COLA1 were accreting at
Eddington rate and unobscured by dust, they would appear as M1450 ∼−23.2 quasars
(see Methods). They would rapidly create large ionised bubbles (proximity zones)
with sizes ∼3 −6 pMpc and boost the photo-ionisation rate in the ionised region
high enough for Lyman-α blue peak photons to be transmitted [34–37]. Once the
quasar shuts off, the photo-ionisation rate returns rapidly to equilibrium on timescales
∝e−tΓbkg where Γbkg is the UV background photo-ionisation rate [36]. The literature
consensus on the z ∼6.5 photoionisation rate is Γbkg ∼0.5 −1.5 × 10−13 s−1 [38–41],

The combination of the BH and stellar mass growth trajectories with the detec-
tion of the double-peaked Lyman-α profile thus leads to the following evolutionary
scenario (see Figure 3). COLA1 and NEPLA4 have experienced efficient massive black
hole growth at z > 6 combined with a suppressed stellar mass growth, likely due to
intense AGN feedback. Until recently, these objects would appear as luminous quasars
and have carved large ionising bubbles. A recent lull in the quasar accretion rate has
led to the end of the quasar phase, returning to a lower luminosity AGN, and allow-
ing the star-formation to increase rapidly, consistent with the observed star-formation
rate, Eddington rate, and low mass loading factor and kinetic energy of the outflow
(see Methods). For a short time (≲1 Myr), a large ionised bubble surrounds the low-
luminosity AGN and enables the escape of blue-peak photons. This is the phase at
which we are observing NEPLA4 and COLA1. Finally, the photo-ionisation returns
to the background equilibrium value, followed by the ionised bubble recombining on
longer timescales. At this stage, the objects would appear as luminous starburst galax-
ies with diminishing Lyman-α visibility but still reveal broad Balmer lines in JWST
spectroscopy [e.g. 4, 5]. COLA1 and NEPLA4 are thus direct observational evidence
for a episodic quasar accretion [e.g. 36] connecting quasars to luminous galaxies and
JWST AGN in a single evolutionary pathway.

In conclusion, the relatively massive BHs, the star-formation history, and the pres-
ence of the large ionised bubbles suggest that NEPLA4 and COLA1 have recently
(≲1 Myr) transitioned from a quasar phase to a lower-luminosity AGN, with star-
formation rapidly picking up after being inhibited by the rapid SMBH growth. The
ionised bubbles enabling the detection of double-peaked Lyman-α are relics of the
recently shut-off quasar activity (≲1 Myr), putting direct constraints on the duty cycle
of high-redshift quasars with important implications for the visibility of Lyman-α in
the reionisation era. The combination of existing and upcoming ground-based instru-
ments (e.g., VLT/MOONS, ELT/MOSAIC, Subaru/PFS) with JWST spectroscopy is
promising to systematically search for (double-peaked) Lyman-α emitters and unob-
scured AGN in deep fields. Establishing a larger census of rare objects such as COLA1
and NEPLA4 will open new opportunities to study the properties of quasar host galax-
ies, measure escape fraction via the double-peaked Lyman-α emission, and infer the
AGN duty cycle in the first billion years of the Universe.
1 Methods
1.1 Cosmological model
Throughout the paper, we use a concordance cosmology with H0 = 70 kms−1Mpc−1,
Ωm = 0.3, ΩΛ = 0.7, and give magnitudes in the AB system [69].
1.2 JWST NIRSpec IFU Data
NEPLA4 and COLA1 were observed as part of Cycle 2 GO program #3767 (PI: Meyer)
with NIRSpec IFU using two medium gratings G235/F170LP and G395M/F290LP
covering the rest-frame 2000 −7000 ˚A spectrum of the galaxy. The targets were

Following [64], we extract the central pixel and the adjacent four pixels to capture

Lyman-α blue-peak transmission
With the systemic redshift in hand, we can now precisely measure the minimum size of
the ionised bubble required to enable the transmission of blue peak Lyman-α photons.
We measure the maximal extent of the blue peak as the location where the blue
peak flux drops to 0 or below, which translates to a minimum ionised bubble size of
Rmin,α = 0.24(0.29) pMpc, in agreement with previous works [7, 25, 102]. Using the
Str¨omgren sphere approximation, this size is consistent with the cumulative ionising
output of COLA1 and NEPLA4 if they have been forming star at a constant rate and
had a constant high escape fraction for ≳100 Myr [25, 102]. Given the very young
stellar population inferred from the SED and spectra (see Methods), it is unlikely
that COLA1 and NEPLA4 carved out such large ionised bubbles. We also note that
JWST NIRCam WFSS observations recently reported the detection of several UV-
faint galaxies around COLA1 (although an average number for such a UV bright
galaxy) [102]. However, this mild overdensity only makes a small contribution to the
local photo-ionisation rate that is dwarfed by that of COLA1 itself [102], thus not
changing our argument.
