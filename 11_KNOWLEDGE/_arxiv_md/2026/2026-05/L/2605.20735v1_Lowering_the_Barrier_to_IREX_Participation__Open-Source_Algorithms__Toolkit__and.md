Lowering the Barrier to IREX Participation:
Open-Source Algorithms, Toolkit, and
Benchmarking for Iris Recognition
Siamul Karim Khan, Student Member, IEEE, Patrick J. Flynn, Fellow, IEEE,
and Adam Czajka, Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—This paper proposes two new open-source iris recog-
nition algorithms, providing both Python and IREX-compliant
C++ implementations to be submitted to the official IREX X
program. This work has two primary goals: (a) to conduct the
first-ever assessment of open-source iris recognition solutions
according to IREX testing protocols, and (b) to offer a model C++
submission that significantly facilitates the entry of other teams’
open-source methods into the IREX evaluation. The new methods
consist of two Neural Networks trained with: (i) Triplet loss with
Batch-Hard Triplet mining (TripletIris), and (ii) ArcFace loss
(ArcIris). The paper also provides open-source IREX-compliant
C++ implementations of two existing methods: (a) an iris image
filtering-based algorithm utilizing human saliency-driven kernels
(HDBIF), and (b) a human-interpretable algorithm for detecting
and comparing Fuchs’ crypts (CRYPTS). Except for CRYPTS,
which faced timing constraints during 1:N search, these methods
have undergone the official IREX X evaluation and have also been
assessed using several popular academic benchmarks: Quality-
Face/Iris Research Ensemble, Warsaw-Biobase Post-Mortem Iris,
CASIA-Iris-Thousand-V4, CASIA-Iris-Lamp-V4, IIT Delhi Iris
Database, IIITD Contact Lens Iris Database, NDIris3D, and
Notre Dame Variable Iris Image Quality Release 2. Finally, this
paper also provides open-source models for iris segmentation
and circle estimation that can be incorporated into any new iris
recognition method.

I. INTRODUCTION
Iris recognition technology, known for its accuracy and
speed, has become prominent in security applications. How-
ever, concerns regarding lack of explainability of proprietary
algorithms hinders its widespread adoption in forensic appli-
cation, in which human interpretation of the method’s decision
is required. Another concern relates to professional testing of
such algorithms, on sequestered dataset guarantying fairness
and closeness to reality, in which the actual iris samples have
never been seen by the developers. Operational aspects such as
accuracy, time of execution and generalization capabilities (to
new sensors, subjects, ethnic groups, time between enrollment
and verification, etc.) have been evaluated within the Iris
Exchange (IREX) program administered by NIST’s Biometrics
Research Laboratory [1]. To date, the many IREX evaluations
have been populated largely by commercial (closed-source)

algorithm submissions. It is, however, desirable to use the
capabilities and high reputation of the IREX program to
also incorporate open-source solutions, including those from
academic institutions. There are at least three good reasons
for stimulating the development of open-source iris recogni-
tion methods, and – if possible – having them evaluated in
programs such as IREX:
(a) reproducible, trusted, and professionally-tested algo-

(b) Having an algorithm from academic units submitted to
and vetted by IREX X would decrease the reluctance of
other academic teams to have their methods evaluated
in the IREX program,
(c) open-source iris recognition approaches encourage trans-
parency, collaboration, and faster deployments of iris
recognition techniques in smaller-scale or pilot imple-
mentations before the adoption of professional solutions.
This paper proposes two new open-source iris recognition
methods: i) ConvNeXt-tiny trained on Triplet loss with Batch-
Hard Triplet Mining [2], and ii) ResNet100 trained with Arc-
Face loss [3] and also presents open-source versions, adapted
to IREX X requirements, of two academic iris recognition
methods previously developed and published by the University
of Notre Dame: i) an iris image filtering-based algorithm
utilizing human saliency-driven kernels (HDBIF), and ii) a
human-interpretable algorithm for detecting and comparing
Fuchs’ crypts (CRYPTS). All of these algorithms (except
for CRYPTS due to timing constraints) are evaluated in the
IREX X program and in a traditional way using all available
academic iris recognition datasets. The novel contributions of
this work are:
(a) the first open-source academic iris matchers compliant
with the Iris Exchange (IREX) and evaluated in the
IREX X program for accuracy, search time, template
size, and selected demographics effects),
(b) an iris image segmentation approach, incorporating two
models: (i) a pixel-wise segmentation model based on a
nested U-Net architecture with dilated convolutions, and
(ii) a regression model approximating inner and outer
iris boundaries directly from the iris image.
(c) two neural networks trained using: (i) Triplet loss with
Batch-Hard Triplet mining, and (ii) ArcFace loss.

II. EXISTING OPEN SOURCE IRIS RECOGNITION
APPROACHES
This Section attempts to identify available open-source iris
recognition methods, other than those offered in this paper, and
which were available to the authors at the time of preparing
this manuscript.
A. MATLAB Source Code for a Biometric Identification Sys-
tem Based on Iris Patterns
Summary: Historically the first open-source algorithm that
mimicked Daugman’s original approach to iris recognition was
developed and released by Masek as his Masters thesis [4].
The iris recognition process in his implementation involves
three key steps: segmentation to isolate the iris from the
eye image, normalization to account for size variations, and
feature encoding to create an iris code. Segmentation utilizes
circular Hough transforms for localizing the pupil and iris, and
linear Hough transforms to localize occluding eyelids. The
normalization step employs Daugman’s rubber sheet model.
Feature encoding involves Log-Gabor filters and phase quan-
tization. Finally, the Hamming distance metric determines if
two templates match.
Source codes: https://peterkovesi.com/studentprojects/libo
r/sourcecode.html
B. Open Source for IRIS (OSIRIS)
Summary: OSIRIS [5] is an open-source iris recognition
package proposed by the Biosecure Association. OSIRIS has
undergone continuous development to improve recognition
accuracy, with the latest version being OSIRISv4.1 [6]. A key
improvement in OSIRISv4.1 lies in its normalization stage.
Unlike traditional methods that rely on circular approxima-
tions, OSIRISv4.1 utilizes the Viterbi algorithm to estimate
coarse contours between the pupil, iris, and sclera. This allows
for more accurate normalization even with irregular iris and
pupil shapes. Furthermore, OSIRIS v4.1 utilizes both the
real and imaginary components of Gabor wavelets in the
feature extraction stage. This captures the phase information
that proves to be critical in identity encoding, leading to
improved performance compared to recent implementations
of Daugman’s classical approach which solely relies on the
real component. Like in Daugman’s method, OSIRIS employs
Hamming distance for comparing the generated iris encodings
during the matching stage.

ph/ref syst/Iris Osiris v4.1 (official link). Virtual Machine
version of OSIRIS has been also prepared by Clarkson/CiTER
(VirtualBox): https://github.com/ClarksonCITeR/osiris vmb
ox

D. Triplet Based Iris Recognition without Normalization
(ThirdEye)
Summary: ThirdEye [8] uses a Convolutional Neural Net-
work (CNN) architecture trained within a triplet-based frame-
work. This approach utilizes segmented and cropped iris im-
ages, however, it does not carry out any iris normalization step.
During training, the model is fed triplets of images: a target
image, a positive image belonging to the same class (iris),
and a negative image from a different class (iris). The training
objective aims to achieve two goals in parallel: minimization
of the distance between the network’s output vectors for the
target and positive samples, and maximization of the distance
between the output vectors for the target and negative samples.
In the paper, the authors employ a two-stage training process.
First, the CNN architecture undergoes pre-training using the
standard cross-entropy loss (softmax) where each iris is treated
as a separate class. Then, the model is fine-tuned using the
triplet-based framework.
Source codes: https://github.com/sohaib50k/ThirdEye---Iri
s-recognition-using-triplets
E. Dynamic Graph Representation for Partially Occluded
Biometrics (DGR)
Summary: CNNs exhibit a significant drop in performance
for biometric recognition tasks due to occlusions caused by
various factors. The authors propose a novel unified frame-
work, Dynamic Graph Representations (DGR) [9] to address
this problem. DGR leverages the strengths of both CNNs and
graph models to learn dynamic graph representations specifi-
cally for handling occlusions in biometrics. Their framework
integrates several recent advancements in computer vision,
including attention mechanisms, triplet loss, and graph neural
networks, to effectively deal with irregularities due to iris
texture occlusions. The authors extend their work to utilize
multi-scale features in [10].
Source codes: https://github.com/RenMin1991/Dyamic-G
raph-Representation
F. Human Saliency-Driven Patch-based Matching for Inter-
pretable Post-mortem Iris Recognition (PBM)
Source codes: The Human Saliency-Driven Patch-based
Matching for Interpretable Post-mortem Iris Recognition
(PBM) [11] method was originally devised for forensic ap-
plications. Its goal is to assist human examiners by providing
interpretable results when matching post-mortem irises. While
not originally intended for live irises, there’s no inherent
limitation to its application in that domain. The PBM method
employs a Mask R-CNN model trained to detect iris texture

G. Worldcoin Iris Recognition Inference System (WCI)
Source codes: Worldcoin Iris Recognition Inference System

a two-headed autoencoder approach consisting of an encoder

B. TripletIris: ConvNeXt [43] trained with Batch-Hard Triplet
Loss [2]
The encoding model employed in this configuration is
ConvNeXt-tiny, the most compact variant of the ConvNeXt
architecture, trained on polar-normalized iris images using a
batch-hard triplet loss. This specific model size was selected
to satisfy the inference timing constraints enforced by NIST.
Triplet loss optimizes the embedding space by enforcing a
structural constraint: the distance between an anchor image
and a negative sample (an image from a different identity)
must exceed the distance between the anchor and a positive
sample (an image of the same identity) by a predefined
margin [2]. This mechanism effectively clusters intra-class
data points together while pushing inter-class data points apart.
To ensure that our model learns from the triplets effectively,
we utilize batch-hard triplet mining. This strategy targets
only the most difficult cases within a mini-batch, i.e., the
furthest positive sample and the closest negative sample for
a given anchor. Focusing on these boundary cases forces the
model to learn more robust and highly discriminative feature
representations at a much lower cost than focusing on all
possible triplets.

C. ArcIris: ResNet100 [39] trained with ArcFace Loss [3]
The encoding model employed in this configuration is
a 100-layer Residual Network (ResNet100) [39] trained on
polar-normalized iris images using Additive Angular Margin
Loss (ArcFace) [3] (we dub this as ArcIris). We switch from
ConvNeXt-tiny to ResNet100 because we find that ResNet100
offers a considerable speedup during inference without any
drop in recognition performance.
ArcFace optimizes embeddings in the angular domain. It
normalizes both the feature vectors and the weights of the
final fully connected layer, projecting the representations onto
a unit hypersphere. During training, a fixed additive angular
margin is applied directly to the angle between the embedded
feature vector and its corresponding target class weight center.
This additive margin strictly penalizes the classification loss,
forcing the model to compress intra-class variance while si-
multaneously expanding inter-class separation. Consequently,
this ensures clear separation between identities in the embed-
ding space, yielding a discriminative feature space well-suited
for biometric verification.
The primary drawback of Triplet Loss is its dependency
on sampling strategies. Because there is a combinatorial ex-
plosion of O(N 3) possible triplets in a dataset, the model
cannot efficiently learn from all of them. Thus, it requires
complex mining techniques (like the batch-hard strategy) to
find discriminative samples. If the mined triplets are too easy,
the model stops learning; if they are too hard, the gradients
can become noisy and destabilize training. However, ArcFace
completely bypasses this issue. By computing the loss against
fixed class weight centers rather than relative sample pairs,
ArcFace requires no sample mining, leading to significantly
more stable and efficient training that usually leads to better
biometric recognition performance.

Here, we outline the critical design choices required to port

2) Porting PyTorch Models to LibTorch for C++: At the

constraints, requiring developers to add type hints (e.g.,
@torch.jit.script), strip Pythonic idioms, and
register custom operators.
Because our neural networks lack data-dependent branches, we
exclusively utilize Tracing for our implementations. Finally, to
accelerate the extraction and identification phases, we load all
static configurations (YAML/JSON) and network weights into
memory in the initialization function.

6) Time Constraints: NIST strictly caps template creation
at 1.5 seconds per 640×480 image. Additionally, a 1:N search
against 500,000 templates–returning 50 candidates from both
eyes–must conclude within 25 seconds.
The CRYPTS method fails these constraints, especially for
matching, as it requires solving a computationally expensive
linear programming problem for the two-dimensional Earth
Mover’s Distance.
To reserve computational headroom for the other algo-
rithms, we deploy lightweight architectures for segmentation
(NestedSharedAtrousResUNet, w = 16) and circle parameter
estimation (ResNet18).
ArcIris and TripletIris use compact networks (ResNet100
and ConvNeXt-tiny). Their matching phases use fast Euclidean
or Cosine distance vector comparisons, comfortably clearing
search time limits.
HDBIF extraction relies on straightforward convolutions,
easily meeting the 1.5-second template creation time con-
straint. However, its search phase calculates fractional Ham-
ming distance via bitwise XOR operations across multiple
spatial shifts. To meet the 25-second limit, we optimize the
search by initially evaluating only even shifts; the ±1 shifts
are subsequently evaluated only adjacent to the optimal even
shift.

For ArcIris, TripletIris, and HDBIF, we observed minor,

VI. PERFORMANCE EVALUATION
A. Selected Research Iris Recognition Benchmarks
Our evaluation across eight datasets offers important in-
sights into how well different iris recognition methods perform
in practical scenarios. Although the proprietary nature of
commercial systems hides their internal workings, comparing
them directly against modern open-source options provides a
clear picture of the current state of the field.
1) Bridging the Gap to Commercial Systems: The results
show that our proposed methods, ArcIris and TripletIris, estab-
lish a new standard for open-source iris recognition. Across
the evaluations detailed in Table VII, these models consis-
tently bridge the gap between open-source baselines and pro-
prietary commercial systems, demonstrating superior perfor-
mance across different metrics. ArcIris consistently achieves
an Area Under the Curve (AUC) exceeding 0.99 on almost all
datasets, performing very similarly to the commercial VeriEye
system.
Furthermore, both ArcIris and TripletIris drastically out-
perform legacy open-source models (OSIRIS, USITv3, and
DGR), significantly lowering the EER on challenging datasets
like CASIA-Iris-Thousand and WBPMI. Between our two
models, ArcIris demonstrates superior robustness. It is es-
pecially effective at minimizing the False Non-Match Rate
(FNMR) under strict False Match Rate (FMR) conditions.
For instance, at FMR=0.1% on CASIA-Iris-Thousand, ArcIris
maintains an FNMR of just 3.40%, compared to 13.70%
for TripletIris and 33.77% for OSIRIS. This proves that the
ArcFace loss function is highly effective at structuring the
feature space for secure verification scenarios.
In closed-set identification, ArcIris performs exceptionally
well, frequently matching or even slightly exceeding commer-
cial systems. On ND3D and VII-Q-R2, ArcIris achieves Rank-
1 accuracies of 96.30% and 95.12%, respectively, narrowly
outperforming VeriEye. The decidability index (d′) further
validates the quality of the learned embeddings. ArcIris fre-
quently yields d′ larger or very close to the d′ of VeriEye in
the intersected set of pairs as shown in Table IX—-indicating
excellent statistical separation between genuine and impostor
distributions.
2) Robustness and the Impact of FTE: The biggest differ-
ence between the methods becomes clear during the Penalty-
Based Evaluation (Table VIII). Often, methods that appear
highly accurate in standard tests only achieve those scores
by throwing away difficult images. For example, WCI does
well on perfect data but fails to process a large amount of
challenging data, resulting in a Failure to Enroll (FTE) rate
of 85.91% on the WBPMI dataset and 66.53% on Q-FIRE.
When these failures are penalized as errors, WCI’s EER jumps
to 45.08% and 48.74%, respectively.
In contrast, ArcIris and TripletIris are highly robust. They
maintain near-zero FTE rates (such as 0.06% on WBPMI
and 0.04% on Q-FIRE). This proves they can successfully
extract useful features from poor-quality, off-angle, or partially
covered iris images. Because our methods do not artificially
boost their accuracy by ignoring hard cases, they are much
more reliable for real-world deployments where image quality
will vary.
3) Performance on a Level Playing Field: The Common
Intersection evaluation (Table IX) isolates a subset of “easy”
images that every single method was able to process success-
fully. However, methods can have extremely high FTE in a
dataset, which makes the intersected sets for that particular
dataset too simple to compare across methods. As such, we
utilize an FTE threshold of 30% to filter out a method when
carrying out our intersection (which only filters out WCI for
WBPMI, Q-FIRE, IIITD-CLI, and VII-Q-R2). Under these
perfect conditions, the performance gap between the methods
narrows. However, ArcIris still stands out, achieving a 100%
Rank-1 accuracy on the CASIA-Iris-Lamp, IITD-Iris, ND3D,
and Q-FIRE datasets. This proves that even when we remove
its advantage of handling tough images better than the baseline
methods, the core matching ability of ArcIris remains highly
competitive.

VII. CONCLUSIONS
Iris recognition, owing to its maturity, has become the
third (along with face and fingerprint recognition) principal
biometric technique being integrated into systems influencing
national safety. Part of this integration is also an ongoing
discussion on creating procedures for human examiners of
iris images, who might be called upon to give testimony in
court. This venture may require open-source iris recognition
algorithms that are transparent, interpretable, and tested with
the use of standardized benchmarks and testing protocols. Iris
Exchange (IREX), led by the National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST), is a well-established platform for
independent evaluation of iris recognition algorithms and
publishes the performance analyses for top iris recognition
solutions. However, there were no submissions of open-source
algorithms to IREX programs to date, and thus, a compre-
hensive assessment of where the open-source iris recognition
community stands in terms of performance, compared to the
leading commercial solutions, was not yet possible on a large
scale.
This paper delivers the first IREX-compliant open-source
versions of one iris segmentation and four iris recognition
methods. The implementations have been done in the C++
language and have been be wrapped with the newest IREX
X API v3.0. Five modules have been implemented: (a) iris
segmentation and normalization (SEGM), (b) crypts-based iris
matching (CRYPTS), (c) human-driven binary iris features
matching (HDBIF), (d) ConvNeXt trained with Batch-Hard
Triplet Loss (TripletIris), and (e) ResNet100 trained with
ArcFace Loss (ArcIris). All of the other modules make use of
the same SEGM’s module outputs. Also, the SEGM outcomes
can be used in any other iris recognition method that requires
normalized iris images or benefits from binary occlusion
masks and circular iris boundary approximations. A clear
advantage of the CRYPTS method is its human interpretability,
and hence its immediate applicability to forensic iris recog-
nition. Our implementation of CRYPTS is fully open-source
(as it utilizes open-source libraries for all of its components),
unlike the original MATLAB implementation. Filters designed
for HDBIF can be used in any algorithm following Daugman’s
iris code pipeline (in place of Gabor wavelets). Moreover, we
provide both C++ and Python implementations and weights for
our TripletIris and ArcIris methods, these can be incorporated
into any other iris recognition system. Thus, the value of the
designed modules potentially extends beyond the IREX X
evaluation.
