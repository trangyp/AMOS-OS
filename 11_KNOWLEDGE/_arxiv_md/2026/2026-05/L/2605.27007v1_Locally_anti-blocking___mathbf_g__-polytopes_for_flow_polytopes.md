Abstract. Given an acyclic directed graph (DAG), the space of strength one flows is a
lattice polytope called the flow polytope of the DAG. If the DAG admits an ample fram-
ing, then the flow polytope is Gorenstein and it linearly projects onto a reflexive polytope
called the g-polytope. We provide a combinatorial characterization of amply framed DAGs
that have a locally anti-blocking g-polytope, and we characterize the minimal faces of the
g-polytope containing a fixed pair of vertices. We prove in this case that the unimodular
triangulation of the g-polytope induced by the DKK triangulation of the flow polytope is
a pulling triangulation, and we characterize the pulling orders that yield the DKK triangu-
lation. To prove our results, we introduce and study coherence diagrams, a combinatorial
model of coherence for amply framed DAGs with locally anti-blocking g-polytopes. We
conclude by indicating possible extensions of these results to the setting of g-polytopes for
gentle Nakayama algebras.
1. Introduction
1.1. Context and motivation. Given an acyclic directed graph (DAG) G, the space of
flows of strength one on G is a lattice polytope called the flow polytope of G, denoted F1(G).
Algebraic, geometric, and combinatorial properties of flow polytopes have been the subject of
intense investigation over the past several decades, with particular emphasis on subdivisions
and triangulations [14, 16, 17, 21, 29, 37], volume and Ehrhart series formulas [6, 11, 20, 23,
26, 35, 48, 31, 32, 52, 53, 54, 57, 58, 59, 71], lattices arising from dual graphs of unimodular
triangulations [12, 13, 36], toric algebra [30, 47, 69], and connections with representation
theory of gentle algebras [1, 10, 15, 18, 19]. A key tool in many of these works is a family of
regular unimodular triangulations of F1(G) introduced by Danilov, Karzanov, and Koshevoy
known as DKK triangulations [29] that are defined by the combinatorial data given by a
framing of G.
The class of amply framed DAGs has played an important role in recent work due to strong
connections between these objects and τ-tilting theory for gentle algebras [1, 10, 15, 16, 19].
Flow polytopes for DAGs that admit an ample framing are examples of Gorenstein lattice
polytopes, which have an extensive history and which play important roles in commutative
algebra [44, 64], combinatorics [5, 8, 22, 24, 27, 40, 41, 42, 43, 45, 46, 50, 55, 49, 60, 61,
62, 73, 74, 75], and algebraic geometry [7, 28]. Gorenstein flow polytopes admit several
characterizations related to the combinatorics of the defining DAG G [10, 21]. More generally,

Gorenstein polytopes include the special class of reflexive polytopes, which originally arose in
the context of mirror symmetry [7]. Bruns and R¨omer proved that if a Gorenstein polytope
has the integer decomposition property, which is an arithmetic property of the polytope,
then it admits (at least one) projection onto a reflexive polytope [24]. The resulting pair of
polytopes share many important combinatorial, geometric, and algebraic properties. Lattice
polytopes with unimodular triangulations always have the integer decomposition property,
and thus every Gorenstein flow polytope F1(G) admits such a projection. For a particular
choice of projection, the resulting reflexive polytope P is known as the g-polytope for G,
due to the fact that the face structure of P is the same as that of the g-vector fan for the
gentle algebra corresponding to G.
In the setting of combinatorial optimization, Fulkerson introduced the class of anti-blocking
polytopes [33, 34], which extend to the class of locally anti-blocking polytopes. Anti-blocking
and locally anti-blocking polytopes have been the subject of recent study in Ehrhart the-
ory and polyhedral geometry [4, 49, 65, 68]. Locally anti-blocking reflexive polytopes were
characterized by Kohl, Olsen, and Sanyal [49], and this characterization involves compressed
polytopes and pulling triangulations. Given a family of reflexive polytopes, it is therefore of
interest to classify those that are locally anti-blocking and to study their pulling triangula-
tions and geometric structure. This is the motivation for the present work.
1.2. Our contributions. Our goal is to study geometric and combinatorial properties of
locally anti-blocking g-polytopes of amply framed DAGs. Our main contributions are:
(1) Theorem 3.5 proves that a connected DAG admits an ample framing with a locally
anti-blocking g-polytope if and only if the inner graph of the DAG is a path or a
cycle; we denote such DAGs by Path(k) and Cycle(k), respectively.
(2) Corollary 3.10 characterizes the ample framings of Path(k) and Cycle(k) that yield a
locally anti-blocking g-polytope, which we call locally anti-blocking ample framings.
(3) Corollary 4.6 characterizes the minimal face of the g-polytope containing the g-
vectors for a pair of routes in G.
(4) Lemma 4.26 gives a combinatorial model encoding coherence for pairs of routes in
Path(k) or Cycle(k) when equipped with a locally anti-blocking ample framing.
(5) Corollary 4.33 characterizes pulling sequences for g-polytopes that yield the DKK
triangulation, and Theorem 4.34 establishes that the DKK triangulation of a g-
polytope is a pulling triangulation.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we provide background
regarding pulling triangulations, Gorenstein and reflexive polytopes, locally anti-blocking
polytopes, flow polytopes, and g-polytopes. In Section 3, we introduce the DAGs Path(k)
and Cycle(k) and use them to characterize the amply framed DAGs with locally antiblocking
g-polytopes. In Section 4, we study the minimal face of the g-polytope containing a fixed
pair of vertices, introduce route pair notation for a locally anti-blocking amply framed DAG,
introduce the combinatorial tool of coherence diagrams and establish their properties, and
characterize the pulling triangulations of the g-polytope that agree with the DKK triangu-
lation. We conclude with Section 5, where we indicate possible extensions of these results to
the setting of g-polytopes for gentle Nakayama algebras.

2. Background
Unless specified otherwise, throughout this work we assume that all polytopes are lattice
polytopes, where a lattice polytope is a convex polytope with vertices contained in the integer
lattice.
2.1. Pulling triangulations and compressed polytopes. A pulling triangulation [51]
of a convex polytope P is defined by recursively refining subdivisions of P. At each step we
select a point x ∈P and pull at x by subdividing each cell S of the subdivision containing
x into the pyramids conv(f, x) for each face f of S that does not contain x. In this section,
Theorems 2.4 and 2.5 depend only on this general definition of pulling and make no special
considerations for points on the integer lattice. Thus, for our initial discussion of pulling and
subdivisions, we will not require P to be a lattice polytope.
Definition 2.1. Let P be a d-dimensional polytope. A subdivision of P is a finite collection
S = {S1, . . . , Sm} of d-dimensional polytopes such that
(1) P = ∪m
i=1Si, and
(2) for i ̸= j, F := Si ∩Sj is a common (possibly empty) proper face of Si and Sj.
Let x, y ∈P. We say y is cell-neighboring to x in a subdivision S if there is some cell Si
containing both of them. If no such cell exists, we say that x and y are separated in S. A
subdivision S′ refines a subdivision S if each cell of S′ is contained in some cell of S. A
triangulation is a subdivision T in which each cell is a simplex.
A recursive process to produce subdivisions and triangulations is the pulling process,
defined as follows.
Definition 2.2. Suppose S = {S1, . . . , Sm} is a subdivision of P, with x ∈P. The result
of pulling S at x, denoted pull(S; x) =: S′, is the refinement of S defined by modifying each
Si as follows.
• If x /∈Si, then Si ∈S′.
• If x ∈Si, then for every facet F of Si not containing x, we have conv(F, x) ∈S′.
For any sequence x = (x1, . . . , xn) of points in a polytope P, we denote the result of suc-
cessively pulling at each point as pull(P; x) := pull(. . . pull(P; x1) . . . ; xn), and we call x the
pulling order. If S = pull(P; x), then we denote the ith subdivision Si := pull(P; (x1, . . . , xi)),
with S0 := {P}. Any subdivision arising in this way is called a pulling subdivision, or a
pulling triangulation if it is a triangulation of P.
Remark 2.3. More explicitly the face structure of a pulling subdivision is given by the
following. Suppose x ∈Si and F is a facet of Si not containing x. Then by the definition
of pulling at x, we introduce a new cell S′ := conv(F, x). The faces of S′ are then given
by f and conv(f, x) for each face f of F. That is to say, pulling at x subdivides each k-
dimensional face f containing it into the k-dimensional faces defined by conv(f ′, x) for each
(k −1)-dimensional face f ′ ⊆f not containing x.
Note that if x is the first pull-point in a pulling triangulation, it will be in every simplex of
the resulting pulling triangulation. In fact, the following is an equivalent way to view pulling
(as shown in the next few theorems): given a subdivision S = {S1, . . . , Sm} of P, consider
all triangulations of P that refine S. By pulling at a point x, we are precisely narrowing
down to those triangulations for which x is in every simplex of each cell Si containing x. The

Definition 2.6. A unimodular triangulation of a lattice polytope is a triangulation such
that every cell is unimodular; that is, each cell is lattice equivalent to the standard simplex
obtained as the convex hull of the origin and the standard basis vectors.
Definition 2.7. A lattice polytope is compressed if all its pulling triangulations are unimod-
ular.
The following characterization of compressed polytopes has multiple proofs, originally by
Santos [38], with subsequent proofs by Ohsugi and Hibi [63] and Sullivant [72].
Theorem 2.8. Let P be a lattice polytope. The following are equivalent:
(1) P is compressed.
(2) P has width one with respect to all its facets.
(3) P is lattice equivalent to the intersection of a unit cube with an affine space.
2.2. Gorenstein and reflexive polytopes. The polytopes arising in this work fall within
the family of Gorenstein and reflexive polytopes, defined as follows.
Definition 2.9. A d-dimensional lattice polytope P is reflexive if it contains an interior
lattice point v and there exists an integer matrix A such that
P −v = {x ∈Rd : Ax ≤1}.
A lattice polytope P is Gorenstein if there exists some r ∈N such that rP is reflexive.
There is a robust theory describing projections of unimodularly-triangulated Gorenstein
polytopes onto reflexive polytopes with shared geometric and arithmetical properties, due to
Bruns and R¨omer [24]. To review this theory, we first recall the definitions of a Gorenstein
point and a special simplex. Recall that for a lattice polytope P, the cone over P is
cone(P) := spanR≥0{(1, v) : v ∈P} .
Recall also that for a full-dimensional convex body K ⊂Rd, we write K◦to denote the
interior of K.
Proposition 2.10. A lattice polytope P ⊆Rd is Gorenstein if and only if there exists a
point c ∈Zd+1 such that c + cone(P) ∩Zd+1 = cone(P)◦∩Zd+1. If such a point c exists, we
call it the Gorenstein point for P.
Definition 2.11. Given a d-dimensional lattice polytope P, a simplex ∆with vertices in
P ∩Zd is special if ∆∩F is a facet of ∆for all facets F of P.
Theorem 2.12 below is the key structural result underlying the properties we describe later
in this work for faces and triangulations of g-polytopes in relation to flow polytopes. Note
that the integer decomposition theorem mentioned in the theorem statement is a property of
a lattice polytope that is implied by having a regular unimodular triangulation [38]. Thus,
the flow polytopes considered in this paper have the integer decomposition property.
Theorem 2.12 (Bruns and R¨omer [24]). If P is a Gorenstein polytope with the integer
decomposition property and Gorenstein point c, then there must exist a sum

Anti-blocking polyhedra arose originally in optimization, but have been found to play
important roles in geometric combinatorics as well [49, 68]. The following proposition gives
a method for checking the locally anti-blocking condition.
Proposition 2.17. A polytope P is locally anti-blocking if and only if for each vertex v ∈P
we have πI(v) ∈P for each I ∈2[d], where πI is the projection defined by ei 7→0 for i ∈I.
Proof. If P is locally anti-blocking, then for any vector in P, an arbitrary collection of coor-
dinates can be set to zero and the resulting point remains in P. This establishes the forward
direction. For the reverse direction, suppose that w ∈P. Thus, w is a convex combination
of vertices of P. Suppose that w′ is obtained from w by scaling the i-th coordinate of w by
a factor 0 ≤r < 1. In the convex combination defining w, we can scale the i-th coordinate
of every vertex of P by r, and the resulting combination will yield w′. Because of the vector
obtained by scaling the i-th coordinate of a vertex v by r is in line segment formed by v and
v with the i-th coordinate set to 0, our assumption yields that every such scaled vertex is in
P. Thus, w′ is also in P. Iterating this process to account for any number of coordinates of
w, we find that P is locally anti-blocking.
□

Let P be the g-polytope of a locally anti-blocking amply framed full DAG (G, F) whose
inner graph has at least one edge. By Theorem 2.18 and the reflexivity of P, the intersection
of every orthant with P is compressed. Since the DKK triangulation of P (as described in
Proposition 2.36) is unimodular and, as we will see, refines the subdivision of P induced by
pulling P at the origin, it is natural to ask if the DKK triangulation of P can be obtained as a
pulling triangulation. We give an affirmative answer to this question in Theorem 4.34. More
generally, we provide an algorithm for producing an irredundant pulling order, and using
this algorithm determine all possible pulling orders for constructing the DKK triangulation
of P.
4.1. Minimal faces. In light of the considerations in Section 2.1, if we want to realize the
DKK triangulation of the g-polytope via a pulling order on its vertices, then we need to
ensure that whenever we pull a route, by which we mean pull the g-vector of the route, it is
coherent with every route that it is cell-neighboring in the subdivision. This means that the
first route that we pull has to be coherent with all other routes. In other words, we must
start by pulling 0, which is the g-vector of the exceptional routes of (G, F). This subdivides
the g-polytope into the cells conv(F, 0) for each facet F of P.
Following this initial pull at the origin, Theorem 2.4 characterizes when pulling a point
separates two vertices and emphasizes that we must investigate what the minimal face con-
taining any two routes in conflict looks like. The following characterization of the faces of the
g-polytope, which is a restatement of a special case of results due to Braun and Cornejo [21,
Theorem 4.6] and Berggren [15], allows us to do just that.
Definition 4.1. Let Y be a subset of the routes of given DAG. We define
EY := ∪R∈Y {e ∈R} .
Proposition 4.2 (Braun and Cornejo [21], Berggren [15]). The faces of the g-polytope are
in bijection with edge sets of the form EY that do not contain all edges of any exceptional
route, where Y is a subset of the routes of G. Specifically, given such an EY , the convex hull
of the indicator vectors of all routes contained in EY forms a face QY of the flow polytope.
Further, the map ϕ is an integral equivalence between QY and the face ϕ(QY ) of the g-polytope
corresponding to EY .
It is an immediate corollary that every non-exceptional route is a face, hence a vertex.
Corollary 4.3. Let P be the g-polytope of an amply framed full DAG (G, F). The g-vector
for every non-exceptional route in G is a vertex of P.
Note that in Proposition 4.2, the bijection is not with the set of routes Y but rather with
the edges in the union. Thus, different sets of routes Y could index the same set of edges EY
corresponding to a face. Proposition 4.2 allows us to work with indicator vectors of routes

In the following lemma, we consider faces of the g-polytope in the locally anti-blocking

Lemma 4.5. Let G be Path(k) or Cycle(k) for some sequence k = (k1, . . . , kr) of positive
integers. Suppose R and S are distinct routes in G and let κ be the number of connected
components of R ∩S. Then:
(i) We have 2 ≤κ ≤4.
(ii) We have κ = 4 if and only if G = Cycle(k1, k2) and E{R,S} = E{L1,L2}, where L1 and
L2 are the two long exceptional routes.
(iii) If E{R,S} does not contain an exceptional route, then κ ≤3. Further:
(a) If R and S are in conflict, then κ = 3.
(b) If R and S are coherent, then κ = 2.
Proof. Since R and S are distinct and intersect at the source and sink, it follows that κ ≥2.
It is a direct consequence of Lemma 3.7 (ii) that κ ≤4. However, Cycle(k1, k2) is the only
locally anti-blocking framed DAG in which there is a pair of inner vertices with two different
paths between them, namely the vertices 1 and k1 having exactly two paths between them.
Therefore, the only way R ∩S can have four components is when the components are the
single vertices s, 1, k1, and t. Between each of these components there are exactly two paths,
and if R ∩S = {s, 1, k1, t}, then R and S take different paths between each vertex. Hence,
E{R,S} is equal to the union of all six of these paths, equivalently stated in (ii). In this
case E{R,S} contains an exceptional route, thus when E{R,S} does not contain an exceptional
route, we have κ ≤3. If R and S are in conflict, then κ must be equal to 3, since two
routes cannot conflict on the source- or sink-containing component. If the two routes are
coherent, then they overlap on only the two components containing the source and sink, and
thus κ = 2.
□

4.3. Coherence Diagrams. We can represent the set of routes of G using a diagram that,
as we will see, encodes the coherence relation for pairs of routes.
Definition 4.13. Let G be a locally anti-blocking DAG with inner DAG of the form Path(k)
or Cycle(k). The coherence diagram of G is an array of boxes defined as follows, whose rows
are indexed top-to-bottom by the labels of the source-adjacent edges of G using the linear
order (1) and whose columns are indexed left-to-right by the labels of the sink-adjacent edges
using the linear order (1). Let G be a locally anti-blocking framed DAG with inner DAG
given by Path(k). For each route R in G, if R is on a 2-leg, then we include the box in row
a and column b where (a, b) is the route pair for R. If R is on a 1-leg, then we include the
box in row b and column a where (a, b) is the route pair for R.
When G is a locally anti-blocking framed DAG with inner DAG given by Cycle(k), the
coherence diagram is constructed similarly, however we include two additional rows at the
bottom of the diagram, where these rows are indexed by the labels of the two source-incident
edges at vertex 1 (repeating the order of the labeling of the top two rows). For a route
supported on a 1-leg with source edge labeled by 1+ or 1−, we place the box corresponding
to this route in the bottom two rows. We then identify the left-most boundary edges in the
top two rows with the right-most boundary edges in the bottom two rows, concatenating the
top two rows with the bottom two rows.

correspondence between row/column indices for boxes and the entries of the route pairs.

exceptional route [10]. The set of exceptional routes with coordinates (i, i) form lower-left

Lemma 4.26 (The Coherence Lemma). Let R and S be routes in a locally anti-blocking
framed DAG G with g-polytope P.
(i) If both R and S are anomalous:
(a) S ∈NW(R) ∪SE(R) if and only if R and S are in conflict, and in this case the
entire polytope P is the minimal face containing both R and S.
(b) If R and S are coherent:
(i) If one if R or S is exceptional, the minimal face containing them is P.
(ii) Otherwise, the minimal face containing them is the edge from R to S.
(ii) If R and S are not both anomalous:
(a) S ∈NW(R) ∪SE(R) if and only if S is in conflict with R. Further, setting
R □S =: {R, S, T1, T2} as in Definition 4.23, we have:
(i) if one of {T1, T2} is exceptional, then R and S lie on different facets of P.
(ii) Otherwise, the convex hull of the routes in R □S forms the minimal face
of P containing R and S.
(b) S ∈NE(R) ∪SW(R) if and only if S is coherent with R and the line segment
from R to S is not an edge of P.
(c) S lies outside of NW(R) ∪NE(R) ∪SE(R) ∪SW(R) if and only if S is coherent
with R and the line segment from R to S is an edge of P.

Lemma 4.30. Let P be the g-polytope for Path(k) or Cycle(k). The subdivision obtained

Proof. This follows from the fact that the DKK triangulation T has the origin as a cone

Lemma 4.31. Let P be the g-polytope for Path(k) or Cycle(k), excluding Cycle(1, 1). Let
T denote the DKK triangulation of P and let S = pull(P; V ) where V = (⃗0, R1, . . . , Ri) is a
pull-coherent sequence of routes. Let R /∈V be a route. The triangulation T is a refinement
of pull(S; R) if and only if the following two conditions hold:
a) W(R) ⊆V or N(R) ⊆V
b) E(R) ⊆V or S(R) ⊆V
Proof. First, suppose R is not anomalous. We prove the forward direction by contrapositive.
Suppose that we have one of the following two conditions:
a′) W(R) ⊈V and N(R) ⊈V
b′) E(R) ⊈V and S(R) ⊈V
Without loss of generality, suppose (a′) holds.
In this case, there exist a pair of routes
T1 ∈W(R) \ V and T2 ∈N(R) \ V in the coherence diagram, which therefore yield a route
S ∈NW(R) such that R □S = {R, S, T1, T2}. Consider Lemma 4.26 (ii)(a) applied to R
and S, implying that these routes are in conflict. Note that neither T1 nor T2 are exceptional
(otherwise they would already be pulled), and so we are in case Lemma 4.26 (ii)(a)(ii).
Therefore, the minimal face containing R and S is the quadrilateral convex hull of R □S.
We then cannot have S ∈V , as this would have created the edge from R to S, which would
contradict that the DKK triangulation refines S since S is in conflict with R. But then
pulling at R would create the edge from R to S, which means that T is not a refinement of
pull(S; R).
For the reverse direction, suppose S is a route that is in conflict with R. Suppose that
S ∈NW(R) (the case where S ∈SE(R) is similar). Then there exists routes T1 ∈W(R) and
T2 ∈N(R) such that R □S = {R, S, T1, T2}. Without loss of generality, suppose W(R) ⊆V .
Then T1 has already been pulled in the sequence V , and by Theorem 2.4, we have that R
and S are separated. Thus, for any cell Y in S that contains R, every vertex of Y is coherent
with R. Consider any maximal collection of coherent vertices of Y . This collection must
contain R, since R is coherent with each of them. Hence, for any T ∈T with T ⊆Y , the
route R is contained in T. Thus, by Theorem 2.5, T refines the subdivision obtained by
pulling S at R.
Next, we consider the case where R is anomalous. The two anomalous exceptional routes
correspond to the origin and hence are always in V . We begin by showing the lemma applies
when R is one of the four anomalous routes that are adjacent (along an edge of the square)
to an anomalous exceptional route in the coherence diagram. In this case, R satisfies the
two conditions (a) and (b) of the lemma, and the forward direction follows immediately.
For the reverse direction, we need to show that pull(S; R) is refined by T . Suppose S is a
route in conflict with R. If S is anomalous, it is already separated from R by the initial
pull at the origin, because in this case R and S lie on different facets by Lemma 4.26.

Theorem 4.34. Let P be the g-polytope for Path(k) or Cycle(k). The DKK triangulation

Proof. We first address Cycle(1, 1). In this case, P is a hexagon that is triangulated after

Now, by Corollary 4.33, any sequence of routes consisting entirely of DKK pull steps yields
the DKK triangulation. An example of how to generate such a sequence is to start with the
outer corners of the coherence diagram (corresponding to the long exceptional routes), and
pull the pair of routes adjacent to each outer corner in sequence from upper-left to lower-
right. Then, move to the set of diagonals formed by the boxes adjacent to the vertices that
were pulled at this step, and pull those vertices along each diagonal moving from upper-left to
lower-right. Iterating this, the final step in the process will be when a single diagonal remains,
and pulling these vertices along the diagonal from upper-left to lower-right completes the
pulling sequence.

Proposition 4.35. Suppose R yields a DKK pull step for the sequence V = (⃗0, R1, . . . , Ri).
Then pull(P; V ) = pull(P; (⃗0, R1, . . . , Ri, R)) if and only if NE(R) ∪SW(R) ⊆V . We call

Proof. For the forward direction, if the pulling subdivisions are equal, then there is an edge
between R and every route coherent with R, which implies that every route coherent with
R has been pulled in V . By Lemma 4.26, this implies that NE(R) ∪SW(R) ⊆V .
For the converse direction, if NE(R) ∪SW(R) ⊆V , then every route coherent with that
does not already lie on an edge of P has been pulled as part of the sequence V . Thus, every
simplex in the DKK triangulation that contains R already exists in pull(P, V ), and hence
the pulling triangulations are equal.
