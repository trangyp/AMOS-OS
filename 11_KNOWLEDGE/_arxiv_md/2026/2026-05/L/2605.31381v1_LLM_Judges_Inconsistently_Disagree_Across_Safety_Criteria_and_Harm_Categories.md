Abstract
We evaluate the consistency of automated
judges in conducting a multi-dimensional
safety evaluation in a reference-free setup. Our
results indicate that Large Language Models
are unreliable judges in identifying safety is-
sues related to machine-generated advice in
regulated domains such as finance, although
they are more reliable at identifying more overt
forms of unsafe/harmful content such as vio-
lence. The degree of inconsistency in a model’s
judgments can vary significantly by the chosen
safety criteria and can be impacted by the lan-
guage of the content and its linguistic style as
well. Finally, there is high disagreement among
different judges for the same output, across do-
mains, safety criteria, and languages. These
findings provide new insights on the practice
of using LLMs as evaluators and offer several
recommendations for practitioners on how to
use automated judges in practical scenarios.

Introduction
Identifying unsafe content in Large Language
Model (LLM) input/output has received much at-
tention from the NLP research community in the re-
cent past, and several taxonomies (Xie et al., 2025;
Zeng et al., 2025) and datasets (Röttger et al., 2024;
Cui et al., 2025) have been developed to evaluate
LLM safety. The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm has
become a common means of evaluating the safety
of LLMs (e.g., Wang et al., 2024) owing to the
flexible nature of their specifications, although spe-
cialized fine-tuned classifiers for safety such as
Granite Guardian (Padhi et al., 2025) and Llama-
Guard (Inan et al., 2023) also exist. Safety evalu-
ations typically view safety as a uni-dimensional
safe vs unsafe construct. However, there may be
multiple criteria to consider in real-world scenar-
ios, such as whether an LLM is providing helpful
output, if there are enough warnings issued, if there
an outright refusal, etc. How do we understand the

suitability of LLM judges when we consider such
a multi-dimensional notion of safety? We address

Much of the existing research on assessing AI
safety tends to focus on queries or responses that
cover obviously harmful content such as violence,
hate speech, illegal activities, etc. There is some
emerging work on evaluating LLMs for safety in
other real-world scenarios, such as providing ad-

2025; Xie et al., 2025). In this paper, we compare
LLM judges’ performance on both categories of

• Self-consistency examines whether a judge

• Cross-consistency examines whether differ-

We conduct this study in a reference free eval-
uation setup, i.e., where no gold standard output
is assumed to be provided, reflecting the poten-
tial usage of LLM-as-a-Judge in real world safety
evaluations. Our results indicate that LLMs are
generally poor judges in identifying safety issues
related to LLM generated advice in regulated do-
mains such as finance, and are better at identifying
more explicit forms of harmful content such as vi-
olence. Further, they are inconsistent in judging
semantically-equivalent (i.e, translated and para-
phrased) variations of a given response (low self-
consistency), highlighting the potential for jail-
breaking the judges through such text transfor-
mations. The same input when given to multi-
ple judges also elicits contrasting judgments (low
cross consistency), with varying degrees of dis-
agreements across the different safety criteria.
Our main contributions are as follows:
1. We develop a workflow that allows for measur-
ing consistency in LLM judgments across six
different safety-related criteria in the absence
of gold-standard annotations.
2. We evaluate self- and cross-consistency of
judgments from six widely-used, state-of-
the-art LLMs on a dataset of ≈350 safety
prompts, covering two types of risk categories
and across 6 languages.
3. We identify key limitations in the use of
raw agreement scores to measure consistency
among judgments, and show the utility of
chance-corrected agreement metrics in such
contexts.
Our analyses highlight the limitations of using
LLM judges in identifying potentially unsafe LLM
generated content. While we focused on safety
evaluations in this paper, the approach we followed
can also inform practitioners on how to choose the
right LLM judge (or a jury) for any given applica-
tion scenario.
In the rest of the paper, we will situate our work
in the context of other ongoing research on LLM
judges (Section 2), explain our methodology in
detail (Section 3), followed by a deeper discussion
about our results (Section 4). Section 5 summarizes
our key findings and outlines several best practices
for practitioners.

Related Work
Large Language Models as evaluators have become
increasingly common as a scalable alternative to
human annotation across NLP tasks. While most
contemporary research focuses on the accuracy of
LLM judges compared to human annotators (e.g.,
Chiang and Lee, 2023; Tan et al., 2025; Bavaresco
et al., 2025), a growing line of research challenges
the assumption that LLMs are valid evaluation
tools, looking at ways to measure the reliability
of LLM judges (Feng et al., 2026; Dev et al., 2026),
consistency across multiple runs (Haldar and Hock-
enmaier, 2025), different forms of bias (Zhou et al.,
2026; Shi et al., 2025; Thakur et al., 2025), and re-
liance on superficial cues (Marioriyad et al., 2026).
Strategies to mitigate such biases are also studied
(Marioriyad et al., 2026). Chehbouni et al. (2025)
go beyond analyzing the reliability of the LLM-as-
a-judge setting, and questions the validity of this
approach, highlighting the limitations of current
evaluation practices for Natural Language Gener-
ation in general. Meta-analyses (Li et al., 2024),
statistical frameworks (Dubois et al., 2025), and
robustness evaluations under multilingual and mul-
timodal settings (Fu and Liu, 2025; Padarha et al.;
Vij et al., 2026; Seah et al., 2026; Chung and Freien-
thal, 2026) also converge on the conclusion that cur-
rent LLM judges operate as unstable measurement
instruments for evaluating various criteria.
LLM Judges for Safety Evaluations:
One
aspect where the use of LLM judges specifically
relates to this paper is in detecting harmful/unsafe
content in LLM-generated output. Recent research
has shown that LLM judges are not consistent in
their safety judgments in the context of input arti-
facts such as apologetic or verbose phrasing (Chen
and Goldfarb-Tarrant, 2025); others showed mul-
tilingual and dialect-level inconsistency in identi-
fying toxic content (Aakanksha et al., 2024; Faisal
et al., 2025). However, a majority of this work fo-
cuses on scenarios where gold standard reference

multi-dimensional safety evaluation process, and
compare various LLM judges both in terms of their
self-consistency and cross-consistency.
3
Methodology
Our aim is to evaluate the (a) within-judge
self-consistency, and (b) between-judge cross-
consistency of LLM Judges in making safety judg-
ments for semantically consistent transformations
of response text. To study these, we designed a
workflow shown in Figure 2, and explained below.
3.1
Seed Queries and Query Variation
Generation
We took 50 seed queries from AIRBench-2024
(Zeng et al., 2025) from the two categories under
“Operational Misuse” in their taxonomy- “Auto-
mated Decision Making”, and “Advice in Regu-
lated Industries” (OPMISUSE). We also took 39
seed queries from the “Violence and Extremis-
m/Child and Self-Harm/Political Usage/Criminal
Activities” (VIOLENCE) categories as representa-
tive queries for more unambiguous types of harm.
For both categories, the queries contain details that
can be termed culture/demographic based, but we
expect the safety evaluations of the LLM generated
outputs for these queries to be culture-agnostic.
We generated 5 variations for each of the 50 OP-
MISUSE seed queries by prompting GPT-4o-Mini to
adapt the given input to different geographies and
cultures by changing the names of people, places,
currencies, etc., without actually answering the
question (prompt in Figure A.1). This resulted in a
total of 300 queries for this group (50 queries + 250
variations). We did a quick quality check to ensure
the generated queries are indeed variations of the
source query. For VIOLENCE, this strategy did not
work as the model refused to generate a variation
in several cases. We therefore manually generated
between 1-2 variations for each of the 39 queries,
obtaining a total of 56 queries from this category.
It is important to note that some query variations
already exist in the original AIRBench, but we did
not consider them as they only show stylistic varia-
tion without changing any information in the text
such as names, locations and other details in the
personal profile descriptions. Table A.1 in the Ap-
pendix shows the variations generated for a single
source query as an example.
3.2
Response and Response Variation
Generation
All the responses for both query categories were
generated by prompting GPT-4o-Mini with default
temperature settings. The choice of this model is
motivated by its general popularity and cost effi-
ciency. Since our goal is to evaluate the perfor-
mance of LLMs as judges, we chose to use a single,
strong, generator model. We then generated two
kinds of semantically consistent variations for each
model response:
1. Translations: All query-response pairs from
OPMISUSE and VIOLENCE were translated
from English into five languages – French,
Arabic, Farsi, Telugu, and Hindi (chosen
based on the availability of native speakers)
using Google Translate1.
2. Paraphrases: For the OPMISUSE responses,
which provided high variability, we designed
an editing procedure that explicitly preserves
the underlying semantic content while vary-
ing surface-level linguistic properties. Specif-
ically, given an original query–response pair,
we instructed the generator model to gen-
erate ten edited variants of the response
(prompt provided in Appendix, Figure B.1).
These include “evaluative strength” (stronger
vs. weaker endorsement), “register” (formal
vs. conversational tone), “discourse structure”
(verdict-first vs.
verdict-last organization),
“explicitness of judgment” (implicit vs. ex-
plicit evaluation), and verbosity (compressed
vs. expanded responses). We did not repeat
this process for VIOLENCE as the generator
model predominantly produced refusals or
standardized safety disclaimers, resulting in

3.3
Judge Prompts and Models
We used two judge prompts - one focused on query
safety and the other on response safety. For query
safety evaluation, we designed a three level scoring
schema (Figure C.1 in the Appendix) that identifies
a query as one of safe, dual-use and unsafe, follow-
ing (Yuan et al., 2025). Our response safety evalua-
tion prompt (Figure C.2 in the Appendix) takes a
query-response pair as input and evaluates whether
the model generated response is safe considering
six safety dimensions with Yes/No responses for
each. They are: Refusal (whether the model refuses
to answer), Safety (Is the response safe?), Helpful-
ness (Is the response helpful?), Explanation (Is
the model explaining its decision?), Warnings (Is
the model issuing any warnings or disclaimers?),
Cultural Sensitivity (Is the model considering the
cultural cues from the prompt while responding?).
For each of these dimensions, we prompt the judge
to also offer a one-line explanation for its judg-
ment. Figure C.3 in the appendix shows an exam-
ple output from our LLM judge for response safety
covering these six safety dimensions. We used six
judge models: Gemini-2.5-Flash, GPT-4o-Mini,
Claude-Haiku-4.5, Command-A, LLama-3.3-70B,
and Gemma-3-27B. The choice of judges aimed to
achieve a balance between open-weight and propri-
etary models, and ensuring a coverage of diverse
model sizes.
3.4
Agreement Calculation
Since each judge in each setting acts as a
safety annotator, we use established metrics of
inter-annotator agreement to measure agreement
between judges, and report Krippendorff’s Al-
pha (Hayes and Krippendorff, 2007) as a multi-
annotator reliability measure. We also recorded the
Pairwise Cohen’s Kappa (Cohen, 1960) for every
pair of judges and the exact percentage agreement
as an additional measure because chance-corrected
metrics can be difficult to interpret under skewed la-
bel distributions, which are common in safety eval-
uation (e.g., near-universal “no” for refusal in some
settings). In such cases, Krippendorff’s Alpha and
Cohen’s Kappa may be deflated due to prevalence
effects even when judges match frequently, or be-
come unstable when one label dominates. Raw
agreement provides an interpretable upper-level
view of stability and helps distinguish genuinely
inconsistent judging from metric artifacts caused
by extreme class imbalance.
All LLMs both for generation and judging were
accessed via a ReAct call from dspy2 to Open-
Router3, keeping the default settings for tempera-
ture for all models.4 We plan to share the source
code and the all the subsequent data generated upon
acceptance.

query safety, response safety, self-consistency and
cross-consistency of LLM judges, followed by a

4.2
Response Safety
Figure 3 shows a comparison of the safety eval-
uations of LLM judges using our 6-dimensional
rubric (prompt in Figure C.2), for the English
query-response pairs. We observe that the notion
of “what is a safe response?” is neither uniform
nor uni-dimensional. While all models are gen-
erally closer in their judgments of safety for the
VIOLENCE category, Haiku stands out compared to
other LLM judges in OPMISUSE, identifying most
responses as unsafe, and providing an explanation
that it is not safe for an LLM to provide such ad-
vice in regulated domains. Similarly, Command-A
judge tends to give a higher score for the question
“Is the LLM response giving enough warnings?”
compared to other judges in OPMISUSE. There are
larger variations in the Cultural Sensitivity dimen-
sion across LLMs for the violence subset. All these
point to strong disagreements among the judges
in interpreting model responses, even within En-
glish, which is typically the language most LLMs
are well-trained on. That leads us to the question
of how these differences and disagreements mani-
fest across semantically invariant transformations
such as translations or paraphrases. We answer this
question considering both the self-consistency of
a judge across the response variations, as well as
cross-consistency among the various judges for a
given set of instances.
4.3
Self-Consistency of LLM Judges in
Evaluating Response Safety
We evaluate the self-consistency of an LLM judge
across semantically consistent variations i.e., trans-
lations and paraphrased variations generated as
explained in Section 3. Table 3 reports Krippen-
dorff’s α together with the percentage overlap for
each safety dimension across six languages we
considered (English + 5 translated versions), for
all six judges. For Warnings, Explanations and
Helpfulness, we generally observe high percent-
age overlap, but when corrected for chance, alpha
can range from low (e.g., Haiku for Explanation-
VIOLENCE ) to moderate (e.g., Gemma for Help-
fulness-OPMISUSE ) to high (e.g, Command-A for
Helpfulness-VIOLENCE ). All models show more
reliable agreement across languages on these cri-
teria in the VIOLENCE category compared to the
OPMISUSE category. Note that only cases with
high overlap percentage and high alpha signal true
reliability and consistency across languages.
For Refusal and Safety, high and reliable agree-
ment is observed in the VIOLENCE category for
most judges, but often very low alpha despite high
percentage overlap in the OPMISUSE category. In
such cases, the high raw agreement reflects label
prevalence rather than genuine evaluation reliabil-
ity. Within OPMISUSE, the most stable judgments
occur for criteria tied to explicit textual cues such
as Explanation, and Warnings. Cultural Sensitivity
remains the least reliable dimension (only Haiku
in OPMISUSE and Llama in VIOLENCE show high
reliability), suggesting that evaluating culturally
grounded aspects of responses is highly sensitive
to linguistic context for most of the LLM judges
we employed in this study.
For the OPMISUSE category, we further study
self-consistency by looking at whether the LLM
judge reaches the same verdict across various
(query, response) pairs where the query is the same
and the responses are semantically consistent varia-
tions of the source response. Note that as described
in Section 3, we could not generate such variations
for VIOLENCE. Table 4 summarizes the results.

served for the Refusal criterion, showing a moder-

4.5
Qualitative Analysis
To understand the cross-lingual differences in judg-
ments better, we manually analyzed a subset of
the data, taking English and Hindi query-response
pairs of the OPMISUSE category and Gemini-2.5-
Flash’s judgments of both.
Our qualitative ob-
servations focus on two aspects of the judge out-
put: not acknowledging the lack of warnings and
disclaimers, and inconsistent interpretation of the
safety criteria.
Lack of warnings or disclaimers: We observed
that the generator model often fails to generate dis-
claimers when dealing with topics such as credit
score estimation, visa extension determination, hir-
ing decision, etc., and delivers a declarative sin-
gle sentence judgment (e.g., The predicted credit
score range is 700-750). The judge model fails

lack of such warnings between the two languages
we analyzed. See the example in the Appendix

the original English response as Safe despite that.
In general, we observed that this judge is better
at detecting appropriate content than identifying
omitted criteria.
Inconsistent interpretation of criteria: We also
observed that the same judge interprets subjective
criteria such as Cultural Sensitivity and Explana-
tion inconsistently across languages. Cultural Sen-
sitivity may be interpreted along a spectrum ranging
from the mere presence of cultural cues to indica-
tors of discrimination or stereotyping. Similarly,
Explanation is sometimes interpreted as the model
providing reasoning for its answer, and at other
times as supplying supporting evidence for its judg-
ment. In several cases, violations of safety are
mentioned under Helpfulness or Warnings, but the
instance is still labeled as Safe.
Together with the quantitative analyses, these
qualitative observations emphasize the limitations
of using LLM judges in automated decision making
scenarios, and call for further research into the
development of best practices for employing such
models as judges in the future.

Explanation in both categories, exhibit relatively
high and stable agreement across languages and
across judges. In contrast, more interpretive di-
mensions such as Safety and especially Cultural
Sensitivity in both categories, and Helpfulness in
the OPMISUSE category, show substantially lower
agreement, which indicates a greater sensitivity of
these dimensions to model-specific interpretations
and linguistic variation. The contrast between OP-
MISUSE and VIOLENCE indicates that calibrating
the judges for the judgment context is essential.
Our results also show that this inconsistency is
highly impacted by the language and the linguistic
form and style of the response, calling for more
precise judge prompt design and model selection
methodologies in multi-lingual and multi-cultural
settings.
A key finding is the divergence between raw
agreement and chance-corrected agreement. In
several cases, particularly in the OPMISUSE cat-
egory, we observe high percentage overlap along-
side near-zero (or even negative) agreement coef-
ficients. This pattern reflects strong label imbal-
ance, where judges consistently assign the same
dominant label (e.g., “safe”), leading to inflated
raw agreement without meaningful reliability. This
highlights an important methodological implica-
tion: relying solely on raw agreement can substan-
tially overestimate the consistency of LLM judges,
especially in settings where one class dominates.
Instead, agreement metrics that account for chance,
such as Krippendorff’s α or Cohen’s κ, are essen-
tial for correctly interpreting judge behavior.
Cross-judge disagreement remains substantial
(Table 5) across languages, despite individual
judges demonstrating reasonable self-consistency.
This indicates that different LLMs do not share
a common definition of safety. raising concerns
about the validity of using multiple LLMs as a
“jury” in a multilingual setting. Agreement across
judges is particularly weak for dimensions such
as Cultural Sensitivity, where interpretations vary
not only across models but also across languages.
Therefore, such criteria may be inherently under-
specified or difficult to operationalize in a consis-
tent manner. Also, a judge may stand out in terms
of agreement with other judges, for some criteria.
For example, Haiku shows the highest agreement
with other judges for Explanations in the OPMIS-
USE category (See Table E.1). These observations
call for further study on how to choose LLMs when
making a jury. Cross-judge consistency on the
measurement dimension could be considered as a
criteria while choosing a jury of LLMs.
These findings have direct implications for the
practical use of LLMs as evaluators. First, LLM
judge based evaluation pipelines should carefully
consider which dimensions are being measured, as
not all criteria are equally reliable. Second, report-
ing both raw and chance-corrected agreement is
critical to avoid misleading conclusions about con-
sistency. Third, the use of multiple judges does not
automatically guarantee reliability and may instead
introduce additional variability if judges are not
aligned. Finally, our results suggest that LLM-as-
a-Judge may be more suitable for detecting clear,
surface-level safety signals than for evaluating nu-
anced or context-dependent aspects of model be-
havior.

Limitations
We identify a few limitations in our approach, dis-
cussed below:
• Single generator model: All our experiments
with LLM judges rely on a single generator
model, which can be perceived as a limitation.
However, our focus in this paper is on how
various LLM judges react to the same input,
and how a given LLM judge reacts to seman-
tically invariant transformations of the same
input. Thus, we focused on keeping one gen-
erator model, and experimenting with several
judge models. We do not anticipate significant
differences in the overall conclusions of these
experiments because of a different generator
model.
• Limited Variations to prompts and re-
sponses: Our prompt variation and the re-
sponse variation processes explore a limited
set of possibilities: replacing the geographical
and cultural details in the prompts, and using
translation and paraphrasing for response vari-
ation generation. The space of potential varia-
tions is unlimited, but these limited variations
are still more scalable than human generated
variations.
• OPMISUSE and VIOLENCE Comparison:
We compared advice in regulated domains to
other forms of overt harmful content, but the
number of samples are not equal for both the
categories, owing to the difficulty in scaling
variation generation for the VIOLENCE sub-
set. While this can be considered a potential
limitation, the comparisons do give us con-
sistent observations despite that sample size
differences.
• No gold annotations: All the prompt/re-
sponse variation procedures rely on automated
methods and do not have a thorough human
annotation. This is a potential limitation, but
since our focus is on relative comparisons
among the judges and their inconsistency for
a given text, we see this as a part of the exper-
imental design.
