LearnedCache: An eBPF-Integrated Perceptron-Based
Eviction Policy for the Linux Page Cache
Zejia Qi
Abstract—Linux is the foundation of the digital age,
accounting for the majority of the cloud and mobile OS
markets. Any device that runs Linux uses the Linux page
cache, a central pillar in OS and application performance,
serving to reduce extraneous disk access. Many page
cache eviction policies have been developed but remain
bound by the rigidity of heuristics. The rise of AI-
driven tools in recent years, melded with the ever-
increasing variety of workloads for Linux devices, sets
the stage for machine-learning-driven cache eviction
policies. Promising research has been done in this field,
but only in the field of user-space applications such as
CDNs. We develop LearnedCache, an eBPF-integrated
single-layer perceptron-based cache eviction policy for
the Linux page cache, trained on real kernel data from
diverse workloads. We demonstrate median AUCs of
nearly 80% over multiple linear models modeling page
reuse time, then take a step further by embedding
these models inside the Linux kernel for real-time
performance evaluation. Through statistical testing over
50 paired trials against a baseline of FIFO for each
workload, LearnedCache reveals that machine-learning-
derived cache eviction policies are practical in the Linux
kernel under representative empirical workloads and
are able to surpass conventional FIFO by statistically
significant margins of up to 10% in insertion rate—
a frequency-adjusted derivation of cache hit rate—in
specific workloads while incurring minimal overhead.
I. Introduction
Linux serves as the bedrock for modern digital infras-
tructure, making up an estimated 90% of the public
cloud across AWS, Azure, and GCP—spanning from web
servers to specialized supercomputers to compute for
groundbreaking AI today. [1] Linux also has a firm hold
in the mobile device market, serving as the kernel for an
estimated 3.9 billion Android devices worldwide. [2] Yet,
with this dominance, the adaptation of custom page cache
algorithms beyond those shipped in the kernel itself has
been slow to follow, creating a longstanding bottleneck
that, if minimized, would significantly reduce cache misses,
disk writes, and potentially offer large performance gains.
Dating back to 1981, Michael Stonebraker corroborated
the need for eviction policies beyond the one-size-fits-
all LRU/MRU-derived algorithms, proclaiming that these
could not possibly accommodate the growing variety of
database workloads back in the day. [3]
In the modern day, this fact still holds—the Linux kernel
still uses an LRU derivation, namely Multi-Generation
LRU, across the even wider spread of workloads given
to it. [4] One can attribute this lack of adaptation to
accessibility—up until recently, there existed no high-
performance solution to insert a custom eviction policy into
the page cache without requiring kernel patches, holding
a high skill barrier to entry. Previous works, such as the
recent P2Cache, have tried to create such a framework,
but in doing so impose restrictions on implementation,
such as the limitation to a single LRU/MRU queue. [5]
cache_ext, created near the end of 2025, introduces a
framework where an eBPF program (also a relatively new
technology) can interface with the provided custom kernel
to create “hot-swappable” custom cache eviction policies
with near-kernel level performance. [6] With the use of
eBPF, sandboxed user-written programs are algorithmically
verified for unsafe behavior when run in kernel space. [7]
However, the policies offered by this paper and those that
remain the de facto standard remain mostly limited to
heuristics—still being variations of LRU, MRU, and FIFO,
albeit with some newer variations such as the probability-
based LHD coming into play. [8]
A lightweight machine-learning-based eviction policy
would theoretically be able to adapt to any workload
and outperform traditional policies, being able to create a
highly specific eviction policy for any workload given trace
data to train on. An ML-based eviction policy would also
save time and effort in creating tailored algorithms for the
wide variety of workloads present in the cloud and other
infrastructure. Like with the problem of developing a way
to allow easier creation and swapping of policies, works
have attempted to create machine learning solutions to
cache eviction: ML-CLOCK outlines a possible perceptron-
based eviction policy for page caches on a “trace-driven
simulation”, but doesn’t publish the specific details or the
simulation used. [9] Yang et al. offer performant ML-based
eviction policies by adding an ML model on the tail end
of a heuristic algorithm, [10] and Song et al. in HALP [11]
introduce a trained eviction policy for the YouTube CDN,
achieving a 9.1% reduction in byte miss ratio.
Although ML-driven cache eviction policies show poten-
tial, no research thus far has trained one and then placed
it into the Linux page cache, most likely due to the tooling
constraints.
LearnedCache’s design is driven by these insights and
the hurdles encountered along the path to kernel-level
implementation. Because of the proof that ML-based
application-informed policies are able to perform well in

II. Background and Limitations
The following is partially adapted from the cache_ext
background section.
A cache is a high-speed, temporary transport-layer
storage that stores copies of frequently accessed data to
improve access times. Operating systems use a page cache
to store fixed-size chunks (usually of 4KiB or 4096 bytes),
known as pages, of files on disk into much faster random
access memory (RAM) for faster repeated reads and writes.
[13] Because the amount of free RAM a computer has is
(in the majority of instances) far less than the amount of
disk space, reads of multiple files from disk will fill the
page cache up quickly, requiring eviction, or removal, of
pages from the cache. The algorithm that decides which
pages to evict from the cache is called an eviction policy.
Linux ships with two main eviction policies inside the
kernel: LRU (Least recently used) and the more modern
MGLRU (Multi-generation least recently used) [4]. They
are alike in that they both store lists of pages present in
the page cache and always choose the least recently used
(last used, longest time ago) pages to evict.
A. Folios
Linux has recently begun transitioning to folios in place
of pages. [14] A folio is either a zero-order page, or a group
of pages contiguous in memory that can be treated like a
singular larger page. The terms folio and page will be used
interchangeably, and all folios mentioned are equivalent to
a single page.
Modern operating systems, such as Linux, are separated
into two main levels of privilege—kernel space is where the
kernel, responsible for interacting with the CPU, RAM,
disk, and other devices, runs with high privilege. [13] The
Linux page cache and its eviction policies reside in kernel
space. In contrast, user space is a restricted environment
where user programs run with access to fewer instructions.
To interact with the devices that the kernel is responsible
for, a user-space program must request access from the
kernel itself.
There are additional practical differences between user-
space and kernel-space programs. The first is development.
The development of applications is mostly done in and for
user space. To modify kernel-resident behavior, a developer
historically has needed to modify the kernel itself.
The development of eBPF and its integration into the
Linux kernel has shifted this truth. eBPF allows small
sandboxed programs compiled to BPF bytecode to run
in kernel space at specific points. To ensure safety, eBPF
programs must be formally verified by the eBPF verifier,
which imposes harsh limits on the size, memory usage, and
complexity of programs. [7] Programs written for eBPF
must not exceed a stack size of 512 bytes, [15] must not
have a total execution tree of over a million instructions,
[15] and must have no possibility of out-of-bounds array
accesses, [16] making the implementation of non-trivial
algorithms and models in eBPF difficult. Furthermore,
the Linux kernel forbids the use of floating-point numbers
or operations in kernel space, [17] further increasing the
difficulty of implementation for machine learning models.
B. cache_ext
cache_ext is a recent eBPF-based framework created
to allow implementation of page cache eviction policies
inside the kernel by developers with minimal overhead.
[6] User-created eBPF programs mount onto a series of
hook points provided by the cache_ext kernel and manage
pages through their own algorithms and lists. cache_ext
provides a built-in trusted data structure to store lists
of pages for convenience, as well as helper functions to
traverse and modify these lists. All cache_ext programs
can be bounded to listen only to a single file directory and
to programs within a cgroup. LearnedCache utilizes this
capability to collect workload-specific training data and to
perform evaluation.
III. Model Design and Implementation
In this section, we show the engineering and design
process for the model used in LearnedCache. On a high
level, this is split into two parts: the tracer that collects
kernel data and traces pertaining to the feature set of
the model, implemented in both Rust and C, and the
model itself, written in Python with scikit-learn, Keras,
and TensorFlow. The model, at its core, must be able

to produce a relative time at which a page will next be
accessed, given information about its previous accesses. The
data collected from the tracers must always be uniform
for model training, evaluation, and deployment—we use
a set of features related to both the spatial and temporal
dimensions of page access events. We now describe the
design of the model in detail, beginning with the tracers.
A. Filebench and Control
Tracing data collected while running Filebench is em-
ployed; Filebench is a synthetic filesystem workload gener-
ation and evaluation framework designed to be scriptable
and to model varying workloads. We use it for all of our
model training, evaluation, and deployment. [18] This is due
to the limited access we had to the array of technologies
used to run the variety of workloads that exist in the
world today. Filebench offers a wide variety of pre-made
and customizable workloads that simulate the actions
of real cloud machines, which, after slight modifications
for reproducibility, act as “best-fit” approximations upon
which LearnedCache is built.
cache_ext provides the option to run in a cgroup (a
hierarchical group of processes bound by memory and
CPU), and this is employed for control. [6] An option for
a watch directory to which the policy solely listens is also
provided and used; all Filebench workloads and eviction
policies run in cgroups, and Filebench is set to utilize a
disk-backed /tmp directory while the policy listens to it.
Furthermore, because of the already present install scripts
by cache_ext for Ubuntu, all policies are run on a virtual
machine with KVM passthrough for isolation.
B. Tracer Design
cache_ext programs are exposed to a struct_ops inter-
face, creating functions that run on cache eviction requests,
eviction itself, insertion, and access. [6] Notably, access
hooks into the kernel function folio_mark_accessed,
which is called by the kernel to mark a folio as recently
accessed. [19] This is used by LearnedCache to get page
access data points. This implies, however, that no absolute
measure of access count can be used to collect training
data.
Initial feature sets utilizing absolute access counts were
discarded as they contained little meaningful information
and were temporally dependent. Features such as the
number of accesses to a page could not be used because
they were dependent on when the tracer was started, and
could therefore not be generalized to other runs at arbitrary
times. Simply comparing the time until access to pages was
not enough; all decisions must be framed during eviction
time in order to meaningfully compare the reuse times of
pages. After iteration and testing with a quickly developed
neural network, the following final feature set was settled
upon and implemented as a tracer on top of cache_ext’s
FIFO policy.
• Delta time between last accesses of page.
• Delta time between last 2 accesses of page (i.e. second
and third to last).
• Delta time between last access of inode.
• Delta time between last 2 accesses of inode (i.e. second
and third to last).
• Distance from last access within file.
• File size in pages.
• Relative exponential moving average for page accesses.
• Relative exponential moving average for inode accesses.
• Time from last access to eviction.
The exponential moving averages are calculated as
follows: a score is stored for each folio, which is incremented
on each access. This score discretely decays with a half-life
of 1 second.
C. Tracer Implementation
For both the preliminary and the final cache_ext tracer,
the same approach is used to derive these logged metrics
at each access event. An eBPF map with a high limit of
entries containing all the necessary information is stored,
and features are derived from it while updating it at each
insertion and access event. A combination of device, inode,
and offset (page index) was chosen as the key for these
maps because a single folio pointer itself might not point
to the same memory location nor be valid for its lifetime.
Therefore, these three values, which can be easily obtained
from a folio pointer, are unique and hold more meaning to
a model.
Training/testing datasets for each workload were created
from combined eviction timestamp and page access event
traces. Each eviction event is matched to the closest access
event that occurred before it for each page, to simulate the
time from access to eviction.
An entry is initialized or reused upon insertion, and the
required metrics are derived upon access. One caveat here is
what to do when a page with fewer than 2 reaccesses must
be logged, as then one or both of the time deltas will not
be calculated. The solution is to force it to the largest value
possible to be stored in a 64-bit integer, aligning with the
data preprocessing pipeline used for feature discretization,
shown later.
The tracers, as well as the machine learning policies
shown later, are run using a modified version of the test
harness that the original cache_ext authors used, written
in Python. The resulting tracer output files are saved
using a compact binary format to the disk in a directory
not watched by the tracer nor touched by Filebench.
The data is then transferred to the host machine and
parsed into a plaintext comma-separated values (CSV) file.

workflow, from the host machine to the virtualized kernel

IV. Model Training and Evaluation
In this section, we show the training hyperparameters
used to train the created model across workloads and their
evaluation metrics. We answer the question of whether it
is possible to train high-performing, efficient models based
on kernel trace data collected during real workloads.
During training, a train-test split of four workload runs
to one workload run was established for the trace data of
each workload. The maximum number of epochs was set
to 50 during training, with variable batch sizes of 256 to
1024. Early stopping was set with a patience of 5 epochs,
restoring the weights to the most performant epoch after
patience runs out. The optimizer used for the models was
Adam. [22]
Separate models were trained on each member of the
following sample of workloads from Filebench, roughly
stratified based on type of workload: openfiles and
copyfiles were chosen as metadata-heavy workloads;
webserver, webproxy, and varmail as cloud workloads;
and mongo as a database workload. Each workload was
edited to be bound to a set number of file operations
(around 50,000). These were chosen based on resource
feasibility (videoserver could not be run, requiring hundreds
of gigabytes of storage) and operational scope (the filemicro
suite was not used due to each workload only doing a few
file operations).

V. Policy Design and Implementation
In this section, we show the design and implementation
process behind the actual ML-based policy used to further
evaluate the models in real-time workloads. We aim to
answer the following questions:
• Can a model used to predict page reuse time be practi-
cally placed into the kernel with eBPF and cache_ext?
• What are the limitations imposed upon such an imple-
mentation?
At a high level, the policy is akin to ML-at-the-tail
in that it reranks the tail end of a FIFO-based policy.
This architecture was chosen for performance reasons: it is
simply not feasible to rank all available pages with a model,
as LRU runs in near-constant time while the ranker runs
at O(N log N). Weights and discretizer edges are exported
and loaded into the eBPF program, which has its own
eBPF-friendly implementation of the ranker (perceptron)
and the discretizer, which it runs on eviction candidates
upon eviction request.

Number of bins. While the number of bins is bounded

B. FIFO
FIFO (first-in, first-out) is an eviction algorithm cate-
gorized by the use of a queue, usually implemented as a
form of linked list. When a folio is inserted, it is pushed
to the head of the list. When the kernel makes an eviction
request (with the maximum number of folios requested for
eviction set to 32 by cache_ext), the required number of
folios is popped from the tail and returned to the kernel
for eviction.
C. Adaptation and Policy Design
The core algorithm is a reranker over the tail of the
cache_ext-provided sampling FIFO policy. [6] An ML-at-
the-tail approach is adopted, inspired by the strategy of
Yang et al. for user-space caches. [10] In the LearnedCache
machine-learning-based eviction policy, oversampling by a
factor of 5 is performed on the bottommost list, and the
PID controller is not modified. The lowest-scoring folios
(predicted to be reused last by the model) are then evicted.
Note: Although the model is trained as a pairwise ranker,
its linearity means we can calculate an individual score
for each folio. The sigmoid function is not implemented in
practice—rather, it only exists to give the model a gradient
during training.

if (n_interior_edges > 5 && value < bin_edges[5]) return
5;
if (n_interior_edges > 6 && value < bin_edges[6]) return
6;
if (n_interior_edges > 7 && value < bin_edges[7]) return
7;
if (n_interior_edges > 8 && value < bin_edges[8]) return
8;
return n_bins - 1;
}
Manual loop unrolling is performed to please the eBPF
verifier. This, combined with the usage of manual assembly
guards, was found through iteration to be the simplest way
to pass program verification without gambling on whether
Clang decides to optimize away bounds that the verifier
relies on or not. The core logic of the model boils down to
two steps:
• Finding which encoding a given raw feature value falls
under, then finding the weight of that specific bin.
• Summation of each weight found through this process.
Because one-hot encoding produces inputs that are either
0 or 1, we are able to skip explicitly calculating the score
for the inactive bins for a feature, and simply add the
weight of the active bin to the final score.

VII. Conclusions
LearnedCache demonstrates that performant ML-based
policies can be applied to environments as constrained
as the Linux page cache and can produce significant
improvements in hits and overall file I/O throughput.
Offline models reach a median of 80% in AUC for pairwise
ranking of page reuse time, and statistically significant
improvements in insertion rate are observed through the
deployment of a novel eBPF and cache_ext-based eviction
policy.
LearnedCache serves as a functional example for overcom-
ing the hurdle of implementing perceptron-based models
with eBPF in resource-constrained environments, yet it also
casts light on the fact that these models, while performing
well in offline evaluation, might not behave as well when
deployed in live kernel environments due to the inherent
randomness of workloads. However, LearnedCache is not
free from potential limitations. In order to effectively
compare policies, static workloads run with Filebench were
bound to a set number of operations, thus creating limited
variation that may not be completely generalizable to


## Page 11


workloads truly running on production machines. The goal
of LearnedCache is to provide a novel, executable solution
for addressing the bottleneck caused by heuristic policies,
and to take a step toward making ML-based page cache
eviction applicable to billions of servers, mobile devices,
and personal computers running Linux worldwide.
