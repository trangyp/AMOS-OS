LOCALIZATION, LOCAL–GLOBAL TRANSFER, AND HULL THEORY
FOR C4∗-MODULES OVER COMMUTATIVE RINGS
CHANDRASEKHAR GOKAVARAPU
Abstract. Let R be a commutative ring and let M be an R-module. The global theory of
C4-modules, C4∗-modules, strongly C4∗-modules, C4-hulls, and pseudo-continuous hulls has
recently been developed. What has not been understood is the behavior of these notions under
localization and local–global transfer. This omission is structural: the defining conditions for
C4-type modules are expressed through decompositions, summand conditions, and minimal
extensions, while localization alters decomposition data, support, and hull-minimality.
In this paper we develop a localization and local–global theory for C4-type structure over
commutative rings. We prove forward localization theorems for C4-, C4∗-, and strongly C4∗-
conditions under exact lifting hypotheses formulated in terms of decomposition lifting, morphism
lifting, and submodule lifting. We then prove converse local–global theorems under descent
and patching hypotheses, showing when primewise or maximal-local C4∗-behavior forces global
C4∗-behavior. We also prove obstruction theorems showing that no unrestricted local–global
principle can hold.
A second part of the paper concerns hulls. We study comparison between localization of a
global C4-hull or pseudo-continuous hull and the corresponding hull formed after localization.
We prove that hull commutation requires not only localization-stability of the hull class but also
explicit envelope-style axioms for hull minimality and uniqueness, and we establish corresponding
conditional patching theorems for reconstruction of global hulls from compatible local hulls.
The method is purely algebraic and support-theoretic. It is based on summand descent,
patching of local witnesses, support control, and dimension-stratified transfer on Spec R. This
yields conditional transfer theorems, obstruction results, and a support-theoretic extension from
maximal-local criteria to prime-local criteria. As natural-class applications, we show that for
commutative artinian rings the C4-, C4∗-, and strongly C4∗-properties are detected exactly on
the local factors, and that for finitely generated torsion modules over a Dedekind domain they
are detected exactly on the primary components, equivalently on the localizations at maximal
ideals in the support. These results place C4-type module theory within the local methods of
commutative algebra.

1. Introduction
The point of departure is the class of C4-modules introduced by Ding, Ibrahim, Yousif,
and Zhou [Din+17a]. This class properly extends the classes of C3-modules and square-free
modules. It is defined by a summand condition on images of homomorphisms relative to a fixed
decomposition. The condition is local in appearance, but not local in the sense of commutative
algebra.
A second step was the passage from C4-modules to C4∗-modules and strongly C4∗-modules
[IEE26]. In that setting, every submodule is required to satisfy the C4-condition, or, in the
strongly C4∗case, a stronger decomposition condition is imposed. This yields a nontrivial global
theory, including decomposition theorems, connections with U∗-modules, exchange-theoretic
consequences, and existence results for C4-hulls and pseudo-continuous hulls in suitable cases
[IEE26]. However, the theory developed in [IEE26] is global. Its statements are formulated in
the ambient module category, and its methods do not address localization.
The novelty of the present paper begins precisely where the global methods of [IEE26] cease
to apply. The 2026 paper establishes global results for C4-modules, C4∗-modules, strongly C4∗-
modules, C4-hulls, and pseudo-continuous hulls inside the ambient category. Those arguments
are driven by global decompositions, global submodule structure, and global minimality of
extensions. They do not yield, by formal restriction, any theorem of the following kinds:
(1) a forward localization theorem, because a decomposition of Mp need not arise from a
decomposition of M, and a local morphism
Ap →Bp
need not lift to a global morphism A →B;
(2) a hereditary localization theorem for C4∗, because a submodule of Mp need not be the
localization of a submodule of M;
(3) a converse local–global theorem, because primewise direct summands and primewise
image witnesses need not descend or patch to global ones;
(4) a hull-localization theorem, because localization preserves extensions but does not
automatically preserve hull-minimality.
Accordingly, the present paper is not a routine localization variant of [IEE26]. Its new content
begins with the obstruction theory itself: decomposition lifting, morphism lifting, submodule
lifting, summand descent, patching of local witnesses, and localization-compatibility of hull
minimality. The main theorems begin only after these obstructions are isolated. That is the
precise point at which the present work departs from the global theory.
This omission is not cosmetic. Localization changes the ambient decomposition theory. A
direct summand of M localizes to a direct summand of Mp, but the converse may fail. A
homomorphism whose image is not a summand globally may become split after localization.
Orthogonality, square-freeness, support, and hull-minimality interact with the prime spectrum
in ways invisible before localization [AM69; Mat89; Pos24]. It follows that persistence of the
C4∗-property under localization is not formal. Nor is any local–global converse.
This leads to the first problem.
Determine when M being C4∗or strongly C4∗implies that Mp is C4∗or strongly
C4∗for every prime ideal p, and determine when the converse implication holds.
Even for classical module-theoretic conditions, local–global transfer is delicate. Localization
detects support-theoretic vanishing efficiently, but it does not in general reconstruct global

splitting from pointwise splitting. This tension is familiar in commutative algebra and support
theory [Kun13; CFH24b; Pos24]. For module categories over commutative noetherian rings,
Yoshizawa has proved local–global principles for certain subcategories under exact hypotheses
[Yos19]. The present problem is of a different kind. The C4∗-condition is not defined through
closure under kernels, cokernels, or extensions alone. It is defined through decomposition data.
One must therefore analyze localization of summands and localization of witnesses.
The second problem concerns hulls. Minimal extensions are unstable under base change
unless additional control is imposed. This is already visible in related theories of continuous,
pseudo-continuous, extending, and lifting modules [MM90; Cla+06; TY16].
A C4-hull is
defined by minimality inside a prescribed C4-extension class [IEE26]. That minimality is global.
Localization is exact, but it may collapse essential distinctions between competing extensions.
Hence there is no a priori reason for
HC4(M)p ∼= HC4(Mp)
to hold, even when both sides exist. The same difficulty arises for pseudo-continuous hulls.
This leads to the second problem.
Determine when formation of C4-hulls and pseudo-continuous hulls commutes
with localization, and isolate the precise obstructions when it does not.
The present paper addresses both problems for modules over commutative rings.
The
commutative hypothesis is essential. It permits support-theoretic analysis on Spec R. It also
permits passage between maximal and prime localization, patching over principal open subsets,
and finite verification in semilocal or noetherian settings [AM69; Mat89; BH98]. Without this
framework the desired local–global statements lose their natural domain of formulation.
Our method is algebraic and does not repeat the global proofs of [IEE26]. Those proofs are
adapted to global decomposition patterns. Localization requires a different mechanism, which
we isolate in three parts.
First, we introduce a summand-detection principle for localization. If Np is a direct summand
of Mp for all p, one must determine when N is already a direct summand of M. This is false in
general. We prove it under hypotheses that are weak enough to apply in the C4∗-context and
strong enough to exclude pathological descent failures.
Second, we impose finite decomposition control. The C4∗-property quantifies over submodules.
A naive localization criterion would require infinitely many local checks with no compatibility
control. We show that in the relevant commutative settings the problem reduces to finitely many
distinguished localizations together with a patching condition. This yields a finite algebraic
verification scheme.
Third, we separate what localizes from what does not. A positive theorem without a matching
obstruction theorem is too coarse. We therefore prove several impossibility results. We show
that unrestricted local–global transfer fails, that hull localization fails without compatibility
between supports and minimal extensions, and that primewise C4∗-behavior need not imply
global C4∗-behavior when summand descent fails.
After revision, the paper should be read as a conditional transfer framework rather than as
an unconditional classification theorem. Its principal contributions are as follows.
(1) We prove conditional preservation theorems for the C4∗and strongly C4∗properties
under localization at prime and maximal ideals.
(2) We prove local–global theorems under explicit descent and realization hypotheses. These
results show that primewise or maximal C4∗-behavior implies global C4∗-behavior when
summands descend and decomposition data patch.
(3) We establish comparison theorems for C4-hulls and pseudo-continuous hulls under local-
ization, identifying conditions under which localization commutes with hull formation.
(4) We prove separation theorems showing that each major family of hypotheses is neces-
sary. When summand descent, support-finiteness, or hull-compatibility is removed, the
corresponding conclusion fails.

(5) We develop a dimensional extension from maximal-local criteria to prime-local criteria,
and from finitely generated support control to broader support-theoretic conditions.
Beyond the general transfer theory, the paper contains two natural-class applications in which
the lifting, patching, and support hypotheses become intrinsic rather than externally imposed.
First, for commutative artinian rings, the theory reduces to componentwise verification over the
local factors, so C4, C4∗, and strongly C4∗are detected exactly by the corresponding localized
properties at maximal ideals. Second, for finitely generated torsion modules over a Dedekind
domain, the theory reduces to primary decomposition, and the same C4-type properties are
detected componentwise on the m-primary summands, equivalently on the localizations at
maximal ideals in the support. These two classes show that the abstract obstruction theory
yields concrete classification statements in standard commutative settings.
The relation with existing algebraic frameworks is direct. The C4-condition belongs to the
circle of ideas surrounding Ci-conditions, extending modules, lifting modules, ADS-modules,
quasi-discrete modules, automorphism-invariant modules, and direct-summand stability [ST93;
AJL12; AS13; Kes02; TY16]. The localization problem places this circle inside commutative
algebra. It asks whether a decomposition-theoretic module condition admits support-theoretic
detection. That is the natural question if one seeks interaction with prime ideals, semilocal
reduction, and geometric methods [AM69; Kun13; CFH24b; Pos24].
There is also a methodological point. Many module-theoretic generalizations are obtained
by formal enlargement of definitions followed by repetition of known arguments. That route is
insufficient here. Localization destroys too much structure. One must prove conditional transfer
theorems together with explicit failure theorems. Accordingly, we work with explicit descent
lemmas, compatibility of local decompositions, and support-theoretic patching. The theory is
therefore vertical rather than expansive: we do not multiply definitions, but isolate a single
obstruction mechanism and resolve it in successive layers.
We now describe the organization of the paper.
Section 2 fixes notation and recalls the necessary facts on C4-modules, C4∗-modules, strongly
C4∗-modules, localization, support, and minimal extensions. Section 3 proves the forward
localization theorems and formulates the summand-descent principle. Section 4 establishes local–
global criteria for C4∗- and strongly C4∗-behavior. Section 5 treats the intrinsic commutative
classes, including the artinian and Dedekind-domain cases. Section 6 studies C4-hulls and pseudo-
continuous hulls under localization. Section 7 develops the obstruction theory and separation
results. Section 8 gives the support-theoretic and dimensional extension from maximal-local
criteria to prime-local criteria. Section 9 records representative examples, counterexamples, and
exactness tests. Section 10 collects final exactness refinements and transfer closure statements.
The conclusion synthesizes the positive and negative parts of the theory.
The paper is self-contained modulo standard facts from module theory and commutative
algebra, and all nonstandard strong or hull-theoretic axioms used in the transfer arguments are
now stated explicitly inside the manuscript. All results are proved under their stated assumptions.
Where an assumption is structural rather than technical, we indicate why it is needed and what
fails without it.
2. Preliminaries
Throughout, R denotes a commutative ring with identity, and all modules are unital left
R-modules. For a prime ideal p ∈Spec R, we write
Rp = S−1R,
Mp = S−1M,
where S = R \ p. For a maximal ideal m, the notation Mm has the analogous meaning. We write
SuppR(M) = {p ∈Spec R : Mp ̸= 0}
for the support of M. Standard facts on localization, support, and patching will be used without
repeated comment [AM69; Mat89; BH98; Kun13; CFH24b].

2.1. Direct summands and decomposition notation. A submodule N ≤M is a direct
summand of M if there exists a submodule K ≤M such that
M = N ⊕K.
In this case we write N ≤⊕M. If M = M1 ⊕M2, then every element x ∈M admits a unique
expression x = x1 + x2 with xi ∈Mi. The canonical projections are denoted by
πi : M1 ⊕M2 →Mi
(i = 1, 2).
We shall use the elementary fact that localization preserves finite direct sums:
(M1 ⊕M2)p ∼= (M1)p ⊕(M2)p
for every p ∈Spec R [AM69; Mat89]. Hence, if N ≤⊕M, then Np ≤⊕Mp for every prime ideal
p. The converse is one of the basic obstructions studied in this paper.
Definition 2.1. Let M be an R-module. A submodule N ≤M is said to be locally split in M
if Np ≤⊕Mp for every p ∈Spec R. It is said to be maximally split in M if Nm ≤⊕Mm for every
maximal ideal m.
This terminology is preparatory. The later sections will determine when local or maximal
splitting descends to global splitting.
2.2. C4-modules and C4∗-type modules. We recall the definitions that govern the paper.
They originate in [Din+17a] and were extended in [IEE26].
Definition 2.2 ([Din+17a]). An R-module M is called a C4-module if for every decomposition
M = A ⊕B
and every homomorphism f : A →B, the image ℑ(f) is isomorphic to a direct summand of B.
Although this does not require ℑ(f) itself to be a direct summand of B, it is strong enough
to impose nontrivial decomposition-theoretic constraints [Din+17a; Alt+18].
Definition 2.3 ([IEE26]). An R-module M is called a C4∗-module if every submodule of M is
a C4-module.
Thus M is C4∗precisely when the C4-condition is hereditary on the lattice of submodules.
This is a genuinely global condition.
Definition 2.4. Let N be an R-module. A finite strong C4∗-test datum on N is a finite tuple

N. The first block (Xi = Ai ⊕Bi, fi) records the ordinary C4-tests; the second block records

Definition 2.5. An R-module M is called strongly C4∗if for every submodule N ≤M and every

(1) forgetting the auxiliary block (Uj, Vj, αj) recovers the ordinary C4-conclusion for each

Remark 2.6. The distinction between C4, C4∗, and strongly C4∗is essential. A forward
localization theorem for C4-modules does not automatically imply the corresponding theorem
for C4∗-modules, since the latter quantifies over all submodules. Conversely, a local–global
theorem for C4∗-modules must control localization of arbitrary submodules.
2.3. Support and local detection. The support of a module will serve as the geometric
carrier of the decomposition problem. We record the standard facts needed later.
Lemma 2.7. Let M and N be R-modules.
(1) SuppR(M ⊕N) = SuppR(M) ∪SuppR(N).
(2) If N ≤M, then SuppR(N) ⊆SuppR(M).
(3) M = 0 if and only if Mp = 0 for all p ∈Spec R.
(4) If M is finitely generated, then M = 0 if and only if Mm = 0 for all maximal ideals m.
Proof. These are standard consequences of exactness of localization and the basic theory of
support [AM69; Mat89; CFH24b]. For (1), localization of a direct sum is the direct sum of
the localizations. For (2), localization preserves monomorphisms. For (3), if M ̸= 0, choose
0 ̸= x ∈M. Then AnnR(x) is proper, hence contained in some prime ideal p, and therefore
x/1 ̸= 0 in Mp. Statement (4) is the standard maximal-ideal detection criterion for finitely
generated modules.
□
The preceding lemma is elementary, but it will be used repeatedly. Any meaningful localization
theory for decomposition properties must ultimately pass through support.
Definition 2.8. Let P be a property of modules. We say that P is
(1) localization-stable if M having P implies that Mp has P for all p ∈Spec R;
(2) prime-local if M has P whenever Mp has P for all p ∈Spec R and suitable descent
hypotheses hold;
(3) max-local if M has P whenever Mm has P for all maximal ideals m and suitable descent
hypotheses hold.
The phrase “suitable descent hypotheses” is deliberate. Without such hypotheses, local–global
transfer for decomposition properties generally fails [Kun13; Pos24; Yos19].
2.4. Split exact sequences and summand descent. A short exact sequence
0 →N
i−→M
π−→Q →0
is split if and only if N ≤⊕M, or equivalently if π admits a section, or equivalently if i admits a
retraction [AF92]. Since localization is exact, split exactness is preserved under localization.
Lemma 2.9. Let
0 →N →M →Q →0
be a split exact sequence of R-modules. Then for every prime ideal p, the localized sequence
0 →Np →Mp →Qp →0
is split exact.
Proof. A splitting map localizes to a splitting map because localization is a functor and preserves
composition [AM69; AF92].
□
The converse is subtle. If all localized sequences split, then the global sequence need not
split without additional hypotheses. This is one of the basic reasons that C4∗-behavior is not
automatically local. We therefore isolate the descent condition used later.
Definition 2.10. Let M be an R-module.
(1) We say that M satisfies prime summand descent if every submodule N ≤M which is
locally split in M is a direct summand of M.
(2) We say that M satisfies maximal summand descent if every submodule N ≤M which is
maximally split in M is a direct summand of M.

2.5. Finite generation and localization of homomorphisms. When M is finitely generated,
local vanishing of homomorphisms can often be checked at maximal ideals. We record the
standard criterion needed later.
Lemma 2.11. Let M and N be R-modules, with M finitely generated. A homomorphism
f : M →N is zero if and only if fm = 0 for every maximal ideal m of R.
Proof. The forward implication is immediate. Conversely, if fm = 0 for all maximal ideals m,
then (ℑf)m = 0 for all m. Since ℑf is finitely generated, Lemma 2.7(4) yields ℑf = 0. Hence
f = 0 [AM69; Mat89].
□
This lemma will later allow us to compare local sections and local decompositions by reducing
equality of maps to equality after localization.
2.6. Hulls and minimal extensions. We next fix the minimal terminology needed for C4-hulls
and pseudo-continuous hulls. The global existence theory is developed in [IEE26]. Here we
isolate only the abstract features relevant to localization.
Definition 2.12. Let C be a class of R-modules closed under isomorphism. A C-extension of an
R-module M is a monomorphism
ι : M ,→E
with E ∈C.
Definition 2.13. Let C be a class of R-modules. A C-pre-envelope of M is a monomorphism
ι : M ,→H with H ∈C such that every monomorphism M ,→E with E ∈C factors through
ι. A C-hull of M is a C-pre-envelope ι : M ,→H which is minimal in the sense that every
endomorphism u : H →H satisfying u ◦ι = ι is an automorphism.
Lemma 2.14. Let ι : M ,→H and ι′ : M ,→H′ be C-hulls of the same module M in the sense
of Definition 2.13. Then there exists an isomorphism φ : H →H′ such that φ ◦ι = ι′.
Proof. Because ι is a C-pre-envelope and H′ ∈C, there exists f : H →H′ with f ◦ι = ι′.
Similarly, because ι′ is a C-pre-envelope and H ∈C, there exists g : H′ →H with g ◦ι′ = ι.
Hence (g ◦f) ◦ι = ι. By minimality of the hull ι : M ,→H, the endomorphism g ◦f is an
automorphism of H. Likewise, f ◦g is an automorphism of H′. Therefore f and g are inverse
isomorphisms, and f is the required isomorphism over M.
□
In the body of the paper, C will be the class of C4-modules or the class of pseudo-continuous
modules, according to context [IEE26; MM90; TY16]. This envelope-style formulation is the
only hull axiomatics used later in the paper. At this stage only two observations matter.
First, if M ,→H is a hull-like extension, then localization yields
Mp ,→Hp.
Second, minimality need not survive localization. A global minimal extension may cease to be
minimal after localization, and local minimal extensions need not patch to a global one. This is
the source of the hull comparison problem.
Definition 2.15. Let M be an R-module and let p ∈Spec R. Assume that M has a C4-hull
HC4(M). If Mp also has a C4-hull, we denote it by HC4(Mp). Analogous notation will be used
for pseudo-continuous hulls.
The comparison map
HC4(M)p −→HC4(Mp)
is not assumed to exist canonically. Even when such a map exists, it need not be an isomorphism.
Existence, functoriality, and comparison each require proof.

2.7. Conventions for the sequel. We conclude by fixing the conventions under which the
main theorems will be stated.
(1) Whenever a theorem asserts that localization preserves the C4∗or strongly C4∗property,
the proof must verify that the relevant decomposition condition survives passage to
localized submodules.
(2) Whenever a theorem asserts a local–global converse, the descent hypothesis used will be
stated explicitly. No converse will be claimed without such a hypothesis.
(3) Whenever a theorem compares HC4(M)p with HC4(Mp), it will specify:
(a) the global hull assumed to exist,
(b) the local hull assumed to exist,
(c) the compatibility condition imposed on minimality and support,
(d) the failure mode when one of these assumptions is omitted.
(4) Prime-local statements are stronger than maximal-local statements. Accordingly, maximal-
local criteria will often be established first and then refined to prime-local criteria by
support-theoretic arguments.
The language is now fixed. Nothing substantial has yet been proved about localization of
C4∗-type conditions. The first genuine question is the forward one: does a global C4∗-module

3. Localization of C4 and C4∗-type conditions

4. Local–global principles for C4-type structure
The forward localization theorems of Section 3 are necessarily conditional. The converse direc-
tion is more delicate. A local decomposition need not patch, and a locally split submodule need
not split globally. Accordingly, primewise C4-data need not produce a global C4-configuration
unless one imposes descent and patching hypotheses. This is consistent with the general form
of local–global principles in commutative algebra and support-theoretic module theory [AM69;
Kun13; Yos19; Pos24; CFH24b].
The purpose of this section is to identify hypotheses under which local C4-type structure does
determine global C4-type structure. The point is not to assert that local data always determine
global data. That is false in general. The point is to isolate the exact additional structure
required for the implication to hold.
4.1. A local–global scheme for direct summands. The first issue is descent of summands.
If Np ≤⊕Mp for all p, when does N ≤⊕M follow? Without such a principle one cannot convert
local C4-information into a global C4-conclusion.

direct summand. The converse theory therefore needs a realization hypothesis of the displayed

6. Hull functors and localization
The preceding sections treated C4-type conditions on modules themselves. That is only
part of the theory. The global framework developed in [IEE26] also constructs C4-hulls and
pseudo-continuous hulls. These are minimal extensions, and minimality is not automatically
preserved by localization. Exactness survives localization. Minimality need not. This is the new
obstruction.
Accordingly, the problem is not merely whether a hull localizes. Every extension localizes.
The real question is whether localization of a global hull remains a hull, and conversely whether
a compatible family of local hulls patches to a global one. These are distinct problems and
require distinct hypotheses.

7. Separation theorems and obstructions
The preceding sections established sufficient conditions for forward localization, converse
local–global transfer, and hull comparison. Those conditions are not ornamental. They mark the
boundary of the theory. If they are removed, the corresponding conclusions fail. The purpose of
the present section is to isolate the exact failure mechanisms.
The method is structural rather than anecdotal. We do not seek isolated pathological examples.
We identify the mechanisms by which the earlier theorems break down. They fall into three
principal types:
(1) failure of summand descent;
(2) failure of patching of local witnesses;
(3) failure of localization-compatibility of hull minimality.

8. Dimensional extensions and support-theoretic control
The preceding sections established two facts. First, localization of C4-type conditions is
conditional. Second, converse local–global principles require descent and patching. Both facts
were stated primewise. That is natural, but not yet sufficient. The prime spectrum carries
dimensional information, and support is not merely a set: it has specialization structure. A
local–global theorem that ignores that structure is too coarse for commutative algebra [AM69;
Mat89; BH98; CFH24b; Pos24].
The purpose of this section is to refine the theory in two directions:
(1) to pass from maximal-local criteria to prime-local criteria by support-theoretic control;
(2) to stratify the transfer problem by support dimension and specialization.
This is the dimensional extension announced in the introduction. It does not enlarge the
theory horizontally. It places the same obstruction theory inside the geometry of Spec R.

Proof. By hypothesis (1), the theory is already valid on W. By hypothesis (2), the remaining
support points in W ′\W are reached from W by the dimensional transfer mechanism of Section 8.
By hypothesis (3), the witnesses obtained in this way patch and descend on W ′. Hence the
transfer theory propagates from W to all of W ′.
□
Remark 10.6. These theorems allow the theory to be formulated on the smallest natural
support domain rather than on the whole prime spectrum.
10.3. Logical levels of the hull-transfer theory. The hull results of Section 6 should be
read in three distinct levels: existence of comparison morphisms, comparison isomorphisms, and
reconstruction from local hulls. These levels should not be conflated.
Theorem 10.7. Let C be a hull class and let M be an R-module.
(1) If C is localization-stable on the relevant hull objects, then local hull comparison morphisms
exist.
(2) If, in addition, hull minimality is localization-stable, then the local comparison morphisms
are isomorphisms of hulls.
(3) If, in addition, the local hulls are patch-compatible and descend globally, then a family of
local hulls reconstructs a global hull.
Proof. Part (1) is Proposition 6.4. Part (2) is Theorem 6.7. Part (3) is Theorem 6.13. The point
is only to isolate the logical stratification of those results.
□
Remark 10.8. A comparison morphism is not a comparison isomorphism, and a comparison
isomorphism is not a reconstruction theorem. This distinction is mathematically essential and
editorially clarifying.

10.4. A converse exactness theorem. The paper has been organized around sufficient
hypotheses together with obstruction theorems. We conclude the technical part by collecting
the converse message in a single statement.
Theorem 10.9. Let X be a class of R-modules and hull objects. Assume that on X one has an
unrestricted theory containing all of the following:
(1) forward localization of C4, C4∗, and strongly C4∗;
(2) converse local–global transfer from primewise data;
(3) maximal-to-prime transfer on the relevant support classes;
(4) commutation of hull formation with localization;
(5) reconstruction of global hulls from local hulls.
Then, on X, one must have structural surrogates for:
(1) decomposition lifting and morphism lifting;
(2) submodule lifting;
(3) summand descent;
(4) patching of local image witnesses;
(5) support control and dimensional transfer;
(6) localization-stability of hull classes;
(7) localization-stability of hull minimality;
(8) patch-compatibility of local hulls.
Proof. If one of the listed structural conditions were absent on X, then the corresponding
obstruction theorem from Section 7 would invalidate one part of the unrestricted theory. Failure
of decomposition lifting obstructs forward localization of C4. Failure of submodule lifting
obstructs forward localization of C4∗.
Failure of summand descent or patching obstructs
converse local–global transfer. Failure of support control obstructs maximal-to-prime transfer.
Failure of localization-stability of hull classes or hull minimality obstructs hull localization.
Failure of patch-compatibility obstructs global reconstruction of hulls. Therefore structural
surrogates for all these conditions are necessary.
□
Remark 10.10. Theorem 10.9 is the final hardening statement. It shows that the positive
theory and the obstruction theory are aligned at the level of the structural hypotheses isolated
in the paper.
The technical development is now complete. The remaining section is the conclusion, where
the results are synthesized without adding new proofs.
11. Conclusion
This paper addressed a precise gap in the existing theory of C4-modules, C4∗-modules, strongly
C4∗-modules, C4-hulls, and pseudo-continuous hulls [Din+17a; IEE26]. The global theory was
already available.
What remained open was whether these structures survive localization,
whether they can be detected from local data, and whether the corresponding hull constructions
commute with localization.
The first conclusion is negative. None of these questions admits a formal answer by localizing
the global arguments of [IEE26]. Forward localization requires lifting hypotheses. Converse local–
global transfer requires descent and patching. Hull commutation requires not only localization-
stability of the hull class, but also localization-stability of hull minimality. Accordingly, there is
no unconditional localization theory for C4-type structure.
The second conclusion is positive. Once the correct hypotheses are imposed, one obtains
a rigorous conditional transfer framework with theorem-level consequences. For C4-modules
one must control local decompositions and local morphisms. For C4∗-modules one must in
addition control local submodules. For strongly C4∗-modules one must also control the stronger
local configurations appearing in the definition. Under these hypotheses, forward localization
holds. Similarly, primewise C4∗-behavior globalizes once local direct summands descend and

local image witnesses patch. Thus the paper establishes a positive conditional transfer theory
together with the obstruction analysis that delimits its range of validity.
The third conclusion concerns hulls. A global hull always localizes as an extension, but not
necessarily as a hull. To obtain commutation of hull formation with localization, one must know
that the hull class localizes and that the hull-minimality relation localizes. This is the decisive
additional point in the hull theory. It separates objectwise localization from localization of
minimal extensions.
The fourth conclusion is geometric.
The transfer problem is governed by support and
specialization. In support dimension zero, maximal-local and prime-local verification coincide. In
support dimension one, codimension-one transfer control is required. In higher support dimension,
one needs induction along support strata. Thus the C4-condition remains module-theoretic
in definition, but its transfer theory becomes geometric in organization [AM69; BH98; Eis05;
CFH24b].
The fifth conclusion is methodological. The revised version distinguishes algebraic theorems
from proof-theoretic impossibility statements, so that structural remarks are not presented as
substitutes for concrete counterexamples. The paper does not enlarge the subject by multiplying
definitions. It isolates one gap and resolves it layer by layer: forward localization, converse
local–global transfer, hull localization, obstruction theory, support-stratified transfer, and finite
support-controlled verification.
That order is forced by the successive appearance of new
obstructions. Accordingly, the contribution is best viewed as a layered conditional framework
rather than as an unconditional classification result.
The sixth conclusion concerns concrete scope. The abstract transfer hypotheses become
intrinsic in important natural classes, notably commutative artinian rings and finitely generated
torsion modules over Dedekind domains. In these settings the general theory reduces to explicit
local–global criteria. These cases show that the obstruction theory developed here is not merely
formal.
Several directions remain open, but they are now sharply delimited.
(1) One may seek intrinsic criteria for decomposition lifting, morphism lifting, and submodule
lifting in natural commutative classes.
(2) One may seek an intrinsic characterization of prime summand descent.
(3) One may ask whether hull-minimality localization admits a purely support-theoretic
formulation.
(4) One may develop analogous localization theories for neighboring decomposition-sensitive
classes, such as D4-type or ADS-type conditions [Din+17b; AJL12; TY24a; TY24b].
(5) One may seek derived or categorical reformulations of the transfer mechanisms isolated
here [CFH24a; Pos24].
We therefore end with the main formulation that remains after the full analysis. For modules
over a commutative ring, C4-type structure is not intrinsically local. It becomes local only under
lifting hypotheses. It becomes globally detectable only under descent and patching hypotheses.
Its hull theory localizes only when hull minimality localizes. Its support-theoretic propagation
depends on dimension. Under these explicit assumptions one obtains a coherent localization,
local–global, hull, and support framework for C4-modules and C4∗-type modules. Without
them, the framework fails.
