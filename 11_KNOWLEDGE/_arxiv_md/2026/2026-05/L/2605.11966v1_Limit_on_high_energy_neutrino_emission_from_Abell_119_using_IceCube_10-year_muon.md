Limit on high energy neutrino emission from Abell 119 using
IceCube 10-year muon track data
Sri Devaki Meduri∗and Shantanu Desai†
Department of Physics, IIT Hyderabad, Kandi, Telangana-502284, India
Abstract
We carry out a search for high energy muon neutrino emission from the galaxy cluster Abell
119, motivated by a recent detection of GeV gamma rays from this cluster using the Fermi-LAT
telescope, which hinted at a hadronic origin. For this purpose, we used the 10-year muon track data
from 2008-2018, provided by the IceCube Collaboration and implement the unbinned maximum
likelihood emission. We do not find any statistically significant excess and the test statistics is con-
sistent with a null result. We then obtain upper limits (at 95% confidence level) on the differential
muon neutrino energy flux from this cluster, whose value is equal to 2.42×10−10 GeV cm−2 s−1 sr−1
at 100 TeV. This limit is about 1.2 times lower than the predicted neutrino flux required to ex-
plain the hadronic origin of the galaxy cluster emission, thus marginally ruling it out. Therefore,
additional data from future neutrino detectors should be able to definitively rule out a hadronic
origin for the observed gamma-ray emission in Abell 119.

I.
INTRODUCTION
In 2013, IceCube detected a diffuse neutrino flux observed in the TeV-PeV energy
range [1], whose origin is still unknown [2]. The first point source for which a statistically
significant excess of ≈3σ was found was the blazar TXS 0506+056 [3]. The second source
witha statistically significant excess (of 4.2σ) was NGC 1068, a Seyfert galaxy [4]. However,
these sources can only account for up to 1% of the diffuse astrophysical neutrino flux [4, 5].
In addition to the diffuse neutrino flux, the IceCube collaboration also recently obtained
4.5σ evidence for neutrino emission from the Galactic plane using cascade events [6]. This
signal is consistent with diffuse emission from the galactic plane, although a contribution
from a population of unresolved point sources cannot be ruled out [6].
Therefore, a lot of effort has been undertaken both within and outside the IceCube
collaboration to determine the origin of the diffuse neutrino flux. Recently the IceCube
Collaboration identified X-ray bright Seyfert galaxies such as NGC 1068, NGC 4151, CGCG
420- 015 etc as promising sources [7, 8]. Most recently, a 3.3σ excess was found from 11
such Seyfert galaxies (which excludes NGC 1068) [8]. Outside the IceCube Collaboration,
evidence for 3σ excess was found from a stacked search involving 683 supermassive black hole
binaries [9]. Extensive searches have also been done from a plethora of sources such as X-ray
binaries [10], Fast radio bursts [11], magnetars [12], pulsars [13], pulsar wind nebulae [14],
red dwarfs [15], tidal disruption events [16], galaxy mergers [17], gamma-ray bursts [18],
supernovae remnants [19], choked-jet supernovae [20] and many more.
In a recent work,
Harale and Paul [21] (see also [22]) found 4σ evidence for gamma-
ray emission from the galaxy cluster Abell 119 in the energy range from 100 MeV to 1
TeV using 14 years of Fermi-LAT data. The cluster Abell 119 is one of the most massive
clusters in the near universe, which is undergoing a merger [23]. This cluster is located
at a redshift z = 0.044 [23] with RA=14.0357◦and DEC=−1.20021◦[24] with a mass of
∼3 × 1014M⊙[25]. Its brightest cluster galaxy in is a cD galaxy [26]. It is a dynamically
complex cluster with evidence of multiple substructures with signatures of ongoing mergers.
Motivated by the recent gamma-ray detection, a search at MeV energies and hard X-rays
from this cluster was carried out using the COMPTEL and INTEGRAL satellites, but null
results were obtained [27]. This cluster has also been extensively imaged in X-Rays using the
Chandra satellite (See [26] and references therein.) Abell 119 has also been imaged in radio

waves at 1.4 GHz as part of the FIRST survey [28] and 150 MHz as part of TIFR GMRT
Sky Survey [29]. Abell 119 has also been detected ar microwave lengths using the Sunyaev-
Zeldovich effect using the Planck Satellite [30]. Finally we note that accretion shocks in
nearby galaxy clusters such as VIRGO have also been proposed as potential sources of
ultra-high cosmic rays detected by Pierre Auger Observatory [31]. However, since Abell 119
is ten times further away than VIRGO, it is unlikely to produce any of the events observed
by the Pierre Auger Observatory.
The gamma-ray signal detected in [21] was found to be consistent with π0 decay in the
intra-cluster medium (ICM). These neutral pions are believed to be remnants of hadronic
interactions of cosmic-ray protons in the ICM. The ratio of cosmic ray proton to thermal
energy was estimated to be ∼8% with a spectral slope of 2.2 [21]. They argued that further
confirmation for the hadronic origin of the gamma-ray emission can only be obtained through
the detection of high energy neutrinos. The expected neutrino energy flux was estimated to
be E2ϕν ≈3 × 10−10 GeV cm−2 s−1 sr−1 [21].
Motivated by this, we search for neutrino emission from this galaxy cluster using the
IceCube 10-year publicly available muon track data. The production of neutrinos in galaxy
clusters has been estimated in a number of works [32–35].
In one of these models [32],
the differential neutrino energy flux for a generic individual mass cluster 1014M⊙has been
estimated to range from ∼10−8 −10−13 GeV cm−2 sec−1 sr−1 in the energy range from 100
to 104 TeV, if the cosmic ray sources are located at the center of the cluster, and if 2% of the
cluster luminosity is converted to cosmic rays (cf. Fig 12 in [32]). However, this flux can get
reduced by three orders of magnitude if the CR sources are located at 1 Mpc from the center
of the cluster. However, the aforementioned calculated flux is very much model-dependent
and the calculated flux depends on the cosmological MHD simulations used for the analysis.
Other works have estimated lower values for the calculated flux.
The IceCube Collaboration has also previously reported null results in their searches for
high-energy neutrinos from individual nearby clusters [36, 37] as well as from a stacked
sample of SZ selected Planck clusters [38]. They showed that galaxy clusters can contribute
at most 4.6% of the diffuse neutrino flux at 100 TeV [38]. A statistically significant detection
of neutrinos from clusters would constitute evidence for proton acceleration [38]. The limits
obtained by the IceCube collaboration from the stacked analysis [38] already rule out the
MHD model of neutrino production within clusters proposed in [32]. However, we are not

II.
DATASET
For this analysis, we utilize neutrino events from the publicly available 10-year muon
track dataset provided by the IceCube Collaboration [5]. This dataset contains a total of
1,134,431 events recorded between April 2008 (IC-40 configuration) and July 2018 (IC86-
VII), spanning multiple detector configurations with varying exposure times. For each event,
the dataset includes the reconstructed right ascension (RA), declination, angular uncertainty,
and muon energy.
In addition, we employ a modified version of this dataset presented in [40], in which
duplicated events present in the original IceCube release have been removed. This corrected
dataset is publicly accessible1.

RESULTS
We obtain a best-fit test statistic value of TS = 0.034 for Abell 119, corresponding to
ˆns = 1.14. The TS value is less than 1.0 and is consistent with a background fluctuation.
Therefore, we conclude that there is no detection of neutrinos from this cluster and hence
set upper limits.

value, i.e., TS(ns) = TSmax −2.71 [12]. This yields an upper limit of n95
s = 14.38.

V.
CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we search for high energy muon neutrino emission from the galaxy clus-
ter Abell 119, motivated by recent detection of gamma-rays from this cluster, which was
attributed to hadronic processes [21]. This work had made a prediction if the gamma-ray
emission was due to hadronic processes, one would expect neutrino flux E2ϕν ≈3 × 10−10
GeV cm−2s−1sr−1[21].
Although a detailed stacked analysis of neutrino emission from galaxy clusters has been
done by the IceCube Collaboration [38], no previous limit has been obtained from Abell 119
For this purpose, we did a time-integrated search using the 10-year muon track track with
the unbinned maximum likelihood method [44]. The value of the test statistics which is
