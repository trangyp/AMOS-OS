Location Prior Generation via Multi-Source Urban Data Fusion for
Low-Altitude Air Mobility
Xiang Xie and Xiaonan Liu
Abstract—Building height, the third dimension (3D) of urban
spatial data, is absent in over 95% of structures in global
geospatial databases. For the emerging low-altitude economy, this
data gap forces each aerial Internet of Things (IoT) device to
rely on real-time onboard sensing rather than pre-computed 3D
scene geometry. Therefore, in this paper, we present a Location
Prior Generation Framework (LPGF), a multi-source IoT data
fusion pipeline that integrates Sentinel-2 imagery, unmanned
aerial vehicle (UAV) telemetry, vehicle global positioning sys-
tem (GPS) trajectories, and OpenStreetMap (OSM) footprints
into structured, reusable urban location priors. LPGF assigns
building heights through a three-tier priority hierarchy: (1) it
adopts explicit height values from OSM building:height
tags where available, (2) it estimates height by multiplying the
recorded floor count (building:levels) by 3.2 m per story
where recorded, and (3) it applies building-type default heights
otherwise. This hierarchy guarantees a complete prior for every
building, with a worst-case error of approximately 5.5m. To
improve height accuracy beyond the structured fallback baseline
under favorable satellite imaging conditions, an optional shadow-
based height estimation module (SHEM) is activated only when a
four-criterion quality gate (solar elevation, cloud fraction, ground
sample distance, and shadow contrast) is satisfied; when any
criterion fails, the gate routes the pipeline to structured fallback.
In experiments on the MiTra A50 Milan open dataset, we verify
LPGF’s controlled degradation capability under two distinct
satellite imaging failure modes: sub-pixel shadow ambiguity
at Sentinel-2’s native 10m ground sample distance (GSD) and
ground shadow merging at Esri World Imagery’s 0.93m GSD. In
both cases, the quality gate correctly identified the unfavorable
conditions and routed the pipeline to structured fallback, produc-
ing a consistent 27-building prior. Tier 3 type-default heights are
validated against manual floor counts (n = 15), achieving mean
absolute error (MAE) = 3.07m, within the 5.0m per-building
uncertainty bound. The framework is released as an open-source
pipeline prototype, demonstrating that structured, quality-gated
fusion of universally available IoT data streams can provide 3D
scene coverage for low-altitude urban operations.
Index Terms—Internet of things, geospatial analysis, sensor
fusion, unmanned aerial vehicles, optical satellite imagery.

II. RELATED WORK
A. The Building Height Data Gap
The scarcity of building height data in open geospatial
databases is severe. A 2023 analysis of the global Open-
StreetMap building corpus estimated that fewer than 4.7% of
approximately 600 million building footprints carry explicit
height or building:levels tags [2]. Regional varia-
tion is extreme: well-mapped European cities like Milan or
Berlin may reach 15–25% height tag coverage in their central
building stock, while rapidly urbanizing regions of South Asia
and sub-Saharan Africa fall below 1%. This is a cold-start
problem for any low-altitude IoT application that depends on
pre-computed 3D scene geometry.
B. Existing Approaches to Building Height Estimation
Existing building height estimation methods fall into two
categories, precision single-building surveying and large-scale
supervised learning, neither of which suits on-demand low-
altitude deployment. Precision single-building methods, such
as LiDAR scanning [5] and structure-from-motion (SfM)
photogrammetry [6], achieve centimeter-level accuracy but
are point-scale solutions whose per-building cost ($100–$500)
and acquisition time (hours per structure) preclude city-wide
coverage. A single 800m radius urban corridor contains dozens
to hundreds of buildings; deploying LiDAR or SfM across
such an area would require days of surveying and thousands
of dollars, making these methods infeasible for routine low-
altitude flight preparation.
In contrast, large-scale supervised methods train convolu-
tional neural networks on paired satellite imagery and height
labels. Chaudhry et al. [8] combined YOLOv7 shadow detec-
tion with ResNet18 regression, trained on annotated shadow-
building pairs across 42 cities, achieving RMSE = 3.84m under
clear skies. Frantz et al. [7] used Sentinel-1/Sentinel-2 time se-
ries with national LiDAR-derived labels to produce Germany’s
first nationwide building height map at 10m resolution. These
approaches represent significant advances in automated build-
ing height mapping, but share a structural limitation for low-
altitude IoT deployment: they assume (a) sufficient training
data exists for the target region, (b) adequate satellite imagery
is available at inference time, and (c) model training costs are
amortized across many users. None of these assumptions hold
for on-demand low-altitude operations at unprepared locations.
Early geometric shadow methods [9]–[11] established the
relationship between building height h, shadow length Ls,
and solar elevation βsun, formalized as ˆh = Ls · tan(βsun).
However, these methods treated shadow detection as a stan-
dalone pipeline without quality monitoring. LPGF inherits
this geometric foundation and embeds it within a quality-
gated framework that explicitly models when the geometric
assumption holds and what to do when it does not.
C. Multi-Source Geospatial Fusion for IoT
Heterogeneous geospatial data fusion has been studied ex-
tensively in remote sensing [12], [13]. Recent deep learning
methods treat GIS data as weak supervision and satellite
imagery as the primary modality. LPGF inverts this hierarchy:
GIS building contours serve as the authoritative structural
skeleton because their topology is stable and low-noise, while
satellite imagery provides an optional height enhancement
layer whose reliability is explicitly conditioned on environ-
mental factors. Vehicle GPS trajectory-based road inference
was pioneered by Biagioni and Eriksson [14]. LPGF extends
this to lane-level trajectory data from the MiTra dataset [15],
supporting building occlusion analysis.
D. Low-Altitude IoT Systems and Spatial Priors
The low-altitude IoT community has recognized the value
of spatial priors for path planning [16], semantic commu-
nication [17], and digital twin initialization. While much of
the existing work focuses on UAV platforms, the underlying
principle that pre-computed spatial priors reduce the active
sensing burden generalizes to any low-altitude vehicle. The
emerging urban air mobility (UAM) sector further intensifies
this need: electric vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) air-
craft operating in dense urban corridors require pre-computed
3D obstacle maps for safe routing at speeds an order of
magnitude higher than small UAVs, and regulatory frameworks
under development by the European Union Aviation Safety
Agency (EASA) [18] and the Federal Aviation Administration
(FAA) [19] identify urban geospatial awareness as a key
enabler for beyond-visual-line-of-sight (BVLOS) operations
in urban airspace. However, existing work treats the prior
as an externally provided input, not a system output. Russell
et al. [3] identified urban UAV density growth as a driver for
flexible, low-latency sensing architectures. Ma et al. [4] jointly

IV. LPGF FRAMEWORK ARCHITECTURE
LPGF is designed around three architectural principles, each
elaborated in the subsections below. First, GIS geometry serves
as the authoritative skeleton: building footprints and road
networks from OSM provide a stable, low-noise structural
layer, while satellite imagery contributes optional height en-
hancement without overriding the geometric skeleton. Second,
height estimation is treated as an optional enhancement, not a
mandatory output; the framework always produces a complete
structured prior regardless of whether shadow-based inference
succeeds. Third, all location priors are serialized through
LPSP-v0 under protocol-governed data exchange, making ev-
ery prior self-describing.
A. Pipeline Overview
The LPGF pipeline executes in five stages (Fig. 1). The five
stages reflect a deliberate separation of concerns: geometry
construction (Stages 1–2), height inference (Stage 3), assembly
(Stage 4), and exchange (Stage 5), enabling each stage to be
maintained or replaced independently.
Stage 1: Region Delimitation and Data Ingestion. The
UAV hover center p0 is computed from D1. An 800 m
radius disc R is established. Sentinel-2 metadata is parsed.
The 800 m radius corresponds to the typical decision horizon
of small UAVs at 15 m/s with a 200 m look-ahead distance,
ensuring the prior covers the airspace a vehicle will traverse
within its next planning cycle.
Stage 2: GIS Skeleton Construction. OSM building
polygons undergo geometric validity, road topology cross-
check, and UAV trajectory consistency validation. For road
network enhancement, GPS points per lane are decomposed
via singular value decomposition (SVD); the first right singular
vector defines the principal direction. SVD is preferred over
linear regression under GPS noise because the first singular
vector captures the dominant direction without being skewed
by outliers. On the A50 corridor, six mainline lanes and at
least two ramp lanes are extracted.

B. Design Rationale: GIS as Authoritative Skeleton
Most learning-based height estimation pipelines [7], [8] treat
satellite imagery as the primary modality and GIS vectors as
weak auxiliary labels. LPGF deliberately inverts this. OSM
building footprints have a known error profile (2–5 m posi-
tional accuracy [22]), stable and independent of environmental
conditions. Satellite-derived building masks, by contrast, are
sensitive to clouds, snow, and seasonal vegetation changes,
producing masks whose reliability varies unpredictably across
scenes. In an IoT data pipeline, a source with known, bounded
error is preferable to one with conditionally excellent but
unpredictably degraded accuracy, a design principle that un-
derlies LPGF’s entire architecture.
C. Design Rationale: Optional Height Module
Making SHEM an optional, quality-gated module reflects
operational reality: a low-altitude mission cannot be delayed
because satellite conditions are unfavorable. The framework
should produce a useful prior under all conditions. This
contrasts with learning-based pipelines that require suitable
satellite imagery at inference time and produce no output
when conditions are unsuitable [7], [8]; LPGF guarantees a
complete prior with bounded error (≈5.5 m) regardless of
satellite quality.

A. Structured Fallback: The Primary Height Mechanism
Structured fallback operates under all quality gate states,
including Q = 0 (gate rejected) and Q = 1 with Vpost = 0
(post-extraction rejected), ensuring a complete prior is always
produced. Heights are assigned from a three-tier hierarchy
(Table II), ordered by decreasing reliability:
Tier 1: OSM building:height tags. When a building
footprint carries an explicit height tag in OpenStreetMap,
that value is adopted directly with confidence c = 0.85 and
uncertainty σˆh,i = 1.0m. These tags are sparse: fewer than
5% of A50 buildings carry them, consistent with the global
estimate of <4.7% [2].
Tier 2: OSM building:levels conversion. When a
building:levels tag is available, height is estimated as
ˆh = levels × 3.2m, with confidence c = 0.80 and uncertainty
σˆh,i = 2.5m. The 3.2m per-story factor is a standard mid-range
estimate for mixed-use European building stock. Approxi-
mately 40% of A50 buildings carry building:levels
tags.

VII. DISCUSSION: TOWARD A SHARED LOCATION PRIOR
REPOSITORY
A. Beyond One-Shot Measurement
The experiments validate LPGF’s controlled degradation
capability, its ability to produce bounded-error structured
priors under adverse imaging conditions, but not the full
consumption–contribution–reuse cycle, which requires mul-
tiple independent deployments (Section VII-C, item 4). The
deeper contribution is the framework architecture that makes
generated priors shareable and accumulable across missions.
An LPGF prior is not a terminal output but an intermediate
artifact that reduces the sensing burden for all subsequent
missions in the same region.
This self-reinforcing cycle (Fig. 7) mirrors the Open-
StreetMap growth model:
1) A UAV mission queries the shared prior repository.
2) If height data exists, the UAV consumes it directly.
3) If not, LPGF measures, verifies, and contributes struc-
tured priors back.
4) Subsequent missions benefit from the accumulated data.
As coverage density grows, the marginal sensing cost per
mission approaches zero.

C. Limitations and Future Work
Six key limitations bound the current study:
1) Absence of LiDAR ground truth: evaluation relies on
self-consistency and the Tier 3 Google Earth manual
count (Section VI); acquiring LiDAR measurements for
10–15 buildings would enable direct MAE validation.
With ground truth, the quality gate and validator thresh-
olds (βsun ≥30◦, fcloud ≤0.15, κ ≥2.0, etc.) could
be optimized by minimizing empirical MAE on a held-
out set, transitioning the decision rule from heuristic to
learned.
2) Approximate building dimensions: shadow-blob esti-
mation via 0.7× scaling carries ±20–30% error; OSM
footprint integration will eliminate this.
3) Flat terrain assumption: valid for A50 (slope < 0.3%);
hilly terrain requires digital elevation model (DEM)
integration.
4) Single-epoch observations: multi-epoch averaging [7]
reduces uncertainty but conflicts with on-demand de-
ployment.
5) Height-only semantic classification: OSM building
type tags and spectral features will improve accuracy.
6) Preliminary validation sample: the Tier 3 verifi-
cation (n
=
15, Section VI) is a pilot study; a
larger sample across multiple urban morphologies would
strengthen statistical significance. Manual Google Earth
floor counting carries inherent measurement uncertainty
from parallax, ambiguous ground level, and build-
ing shadow interpretation, estimated at ±0.5–1.0m per
building.
D. Application Pathways
Low-altitude flight planning. Low-altitude navigation re-
quires real-time obstacle avoidance under stringent power
and computational constraints [23]. Adaptive inflation margins
dinflate = max(2σˆh,i, dmin) set safety buffers from LPGF’s
per-building uncertainty. At 0.93m GSD (σˆh,i = 0.40m), the
margin is 5.0m; at 10m GSD (σˆh,i = 4.3m), 8.6m. Consider a
UAV (as a representative low-altitude platform) at 15m/s with

Semantic communication. Per-building uncertainty pro-
vides principled deviation thresholds [17], [24]. Pre-loading
the prior eliminates static geometry transmission, reserving
the channel for dynamic scene updates. Adaptive thresholds
prevent unnecessary retransmission of inherently coarse es-
timates. For a typical urban scene containing approximately
30 buildings within an 800 m radius, pre-loading the com-
plete location prior via LPSP-v0 eliminates the transmission
of static building geometry throughout the flight, reserving
the communication channel exclusively for dynamic obstacle
updates. The per-building uncertainty field enables adaptive
retransmission policies: buildings with large uncertainty, such
as Tier 3 type-default estimates (σˆh,i = 5.0 m), tolerate
larger deviations before triggering an update, while buildings
with tightly bounded height estimates report deviations more
aggressively. This uncertainty-aware trade-off balances com-
munication efficiency with situational awareness.
eVTOL and urban air mobility. The emergence of electric
vertical takeoff and landing (eVTOL) aircraft for urban air
taxi services intensifies the need for pre-computed location
priors. Unlike small UAVs operating at 15m/s with 200m
decision horizons, eVTOL platforms cruise at 60–80m/s and
require obstacle awareness at 500m or more. At these speeds,
the safety margin derived from per-building uncertainty scales
proportionally: a building with σˆh,i = 4.3m demands an 8.6m
inflation margin. At 80m/s cruise speed, real-time obstacle
detection and 3D reconstruction would require processing and
reacting to new geometry within approximately 0.1 s of enter-
ing the inflation zone. Pre-loading LPGF priors eliminates this
real-time reconstruction burden entirely: the flight controller
queries known building geometry before takeoff, reserving
onboard sensing exclusively for dynamic obstacle verification.
This is precisely the “query on demand” paradigm that LPGF
enables: an eVTOL vertiport queries the shared repository for
the corridor between departure and arrival, and LPGF either
returns an existing prior or generates one before takeoff.
Digital twin initialization. A 2.5 km corridor would require
≈4 hours of LiDAR scanning for comparable coverage. LPGF
produces a structured prior in ≈51 s (computation only,
excluding network I/O for Sentinel-2 and OSM data retrieval),
a roughly 280× reduction in acquisition time. This compar-
ison is based on acquisition time alone: LiDAR produces
centimeter-level accuracy, while the structured prior provides
meter-level estimates suitable for pre-flight planning [25].
E. Practical Deployment Notes
Minimum data requirements. LPGF requires: (i) Sentinel-
2 Level-2A imagery (Copernicus, 5-day revisit); (ii) OSM
building footprints; (iii) low-altitude vehicle telemetry (UAV-
collected in this study); and (iv) vehicle GPS trajectories. In
the absence of dedicated trajectory data, OSM road centerlines
can substitute for SVD-based lane extraction, though this
degrades lane-level precision.
Computation budget. Full pipeline: ≈51 s on a consumer
workstation (computation only; network I/O for data retrieval
adds transport-dependent overhead).
Regional calibration. Only the building-type default height
table and post-extraction thresholds need adjustment for a new
urban area.
Operational boundaries. Validated at mid-latitudes (βsun ≈
30◦–60◦). High-latitude winter (βsun
< 20◦) consistently

does not depend on Sentinel-2-specific spectral bands; any
visible-band satellite imagery with solar elevation metadata
can serve as D2, provided GSD ≤10m.

F. Toward a Shared Prior Repository
The LPSP-v0 format enables cross-deployment reuse: each
LPGF-generated prior can be stored and queried by subsequent
missions in the same region. This mirrors the OpenStreetMap
contribution model, where distributed data collection progres-
sively improves coverage. A shared repository would allow
building heights to accumulate over multiple deployments,
with the quality gate ensuring contributed data meets a min-
imum reliability standard. Empirical validation of this multi-
deployment cycle, including coverage growth rates, query hit
ratios, and the transition from fallback-dominated to mixed-
source priors, requires field trials across multiple independent
deployments and remains future work.
VIII. CONCLUSION
This paper presented the Location Prior Generation Frame-
work (LPGF), a multi-source IoT data fusion pipeline that
addressed the near-absence of building height data in global
geospatial datasets. LPGF assigned heights through a three-tier
structured fallback hierarchy that guaranteed a complete prior
for every building, with a worst-case error of approximately
5.5 m. An optional shadow-based estimation module (SHEM)
was activated under a quality gate when satellite conditions
permitted. On the MiTra A50 Milan dataset, the pipeline
produced a consistent 27-building structured prior under two
distinct imaging failure modes, with Tier 3 type-default heights
achieving MAE = 3.07 m within the 5.0 m uncertainty
bound. LPGF was released as an open-source prototype, and
the LPSP-v0 JSON format enabled cross-deployment reuse.
Future work included LiDAR ground truth acquisition, SHEM
positive-path validation under suitable imaging conditions, and
establishing a shared prior repository on LPSP-v0.
