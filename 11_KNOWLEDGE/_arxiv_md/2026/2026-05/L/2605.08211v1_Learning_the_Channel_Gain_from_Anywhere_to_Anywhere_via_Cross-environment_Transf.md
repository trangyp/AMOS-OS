Prasenjit Dhara and Daniel Romero
Dept. of Information and Communication Technology, University of Agder, Grimstad, Norway.
Email:{prasenjit.dhara,daniel.romero}@uia.no.
Abstract—Channel-gain maps provide the channel gain be-
tween any two locations in a geographical region. They find
numerous applications, from resource allocation and interference
control to path planning for autonomous vehicles. Channel-gain
map estimation (CGME) is considerably more challenging than
conventional radio map estimation (RME) because channel-gain
maps are functions over a 6-dimensional input space. This calls
for specialized methods, which currently rely on the (inaccurate)
radio tomographic model or require a prohibitively large number
of measurements since they do not exploit any spatial structure.
This paper overcomes this issue by leveraging spatial patterns
that channel-gain maps exhibit across environments, as dictated
by the laws of physics and typical environmental characteristics
(e.g. building materials and layouts). Adopting a metalearning
perspective, a transformer-based estimator is proposed to implic-
itly learn this common structure from measurements collected in
multiple environments. This enables CGME in new environments
from significantly fewer measurements (five times less in our
experiments). To maximize learning efficiency, the transformer
is composed with a feature map that enforces the invariances
of CGME, such as those following from reciprocity. Numerical
experiments corroborate the merits of the proposed estimator
relative to existing methods.
Index Terms—Radio map estimation, channel-gain maps,
transformers, cross-environment learning.
I. INTRODUCTION
Radio maps characterize the propagation or occupancy
state of a radio communication channel in a geographical
region [22], [23]. A particularly important class of radio maps
is the so-called channel-gain maps, which provide the channel
gain between any two locations in the region of interest.
Clearly, such information is of great value, as it can be used
for resource allocation, interference control, routing in ad-
hoc networks, scheduling in device-to-device communications,
aerial base station deployment, and path planning for swarms
of autonomous vehicles, among many other applications; see
e.g. [25], [26]. An illustrative example is that of a wireless net-
work with N terminals: measuring the channel gain between
each pair of terminals requires N(N −1)/2 measurements. In
turn, using a channel-gain map, estimates of those values can
be obtained directly from the locations of the terminals.
Radio map estimation (RME) deals with the construction
of radio maps from measurements and their locations [23].
This work has been funded by the IKTPLUSS grant 311994 of the Research
Council of Norway.
This paper focuses on the construction of channel-gain maps,
which is referred to as channel-gain map estimation (CGME).
The literature of RME is sizable and rapidly growing.
Estimators were developed based on Kriging [2], [29], kernel-
based learning [24], [33], matrix completion [27], sparsity-
based inference [3], dictionary learning [12], graphical mod-
els [7], convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [13], [18], [29],
[32], and, more recently, transformers [35]; see also [20], [36].
Unfortunately, these methods cannot be generally applied to
estimate channel-gain maps, as these maps are functions of
two locations rather than one. Instead, specialized methods
for CGME are required.
By far, the most popular approach to CGME relies on
the so-called radio tomographic model, which expresses the
channel gain as a path-loss term minus the line integral of a
spatial loss field that quantifies absorption at each point [1],
[4], [10], [14], [15], [21], [25], [26], [37]. Unfortunately, this
approach suffers from large modeling error, as propagation in
typical communication bands is not dominated by absorption.
Pure data-driven approaches sidestep this issue. They com-
prise methods based on1 spatiotemporal filtering [11], adapted
kernel-based learning [19], and deep neural networks (DNNs)
[8], [9]. Unfortunately, since the target function is defined on
a 6-dimensional space and these approaches do not leverage
any prior information, they require a prohibitive number of
measurements.
To overcome this issue, the key observation in this paper is
that a channel-gain map is not just any function: it exhibits
a certain spatial structure dictated by the laws of physics
(namely Maxwell’s equations) and manifests common patterns
(e.g. the channel gain generally decreases with distance and
is often reduced by obstacles). The main contribution of this
paper is a novel approach where this common structure is
implicitly learned from measurements collected in multiple
environments. By doing so, estimating channel-gain maps
in new environments can be accomplished with significantly
fewer measurements than what existing methods require (5

II. MODEL AND PROBLEM FORMULATION
Let R ⊂R3 denote the set of all points within the
region of interest. The signal s(t) transmitted by a terminal
at r′ ∈R propagates through the environment to a termi-
nal at r′′ ∈R. The received signal can be expressed as
¯s(t) = h(t) ∗s(t) + ξ(t), where h(t) is the channel impulse
response and ξ(t) is noise. In the frequency domain, this
reads as ¯S(f) = H(f)S(f) + Ξ(f). The channel gain at a
given frequency f 0 is defined as 10 log10 |H(f 0)|2. If both
terminals have isotropic antennas and the environment does
not change over time, this gain depends only on r′ and r′′.
As a result, it can be formalized as a function g : R×R →R
referred to as a channel-gain map. Note that, due to reciprocity,
g(r′, r′′) = g(r′′, r′), ∀r′, r′′ ∈R.
To estimate g, the channel gain is measured between M lo-
cation pairs {(r′

III. BACKGROUND ON TRANSFORMERS
Transformers are a type of DNN architecture based on
inner-product attention [34]. Internally, transformers are the
result of composing attention layers, multilayer perceptrons,
and normalization layers with residual connections. Impor-
tantly, a transformer can be seen as a function T that takes a
set of M vectors as input and produces a set of M vectors
at the output. Formally, T : RD×M →RD×M, and the
same network can be applied regardless of M. Function T
satisfies permutation equivariance, meaning that permuting the
columns of Q permutes the columns of T(Q) in the same way.
IV. CROSS-ENVIRONMENT CGME
This section develops the proposed estimation approach.
The starting point is to theoretically investigate why exist-
ing methods cannot effectively produce accurate channel-gain
map estimates. This motivates a cross-environment estimation
approach (Sec. IV-A), which is subsequently generalized into
an implicit learning formulation (Sec. IV-B). A transformer-
based estimator materializes this framework in Sec. IV-C.

A. Cross-environment Learning
The approach discussed so far is suitable provided that M
is sufficiently informative about g. However, this is unlikely
as g is a function defined on a 6-dimensional space. For
illustration purposes, note that a grid of (only) 10 points
per dimension has 106 points in total. This is the so-called
curse of dimensionality [28]. In contrast, practical setups
may require the estimation of g from just a few hundred
measurements. For example, in an environment with 40 static
terminals, at most 40(40 −1)/2 = 780 measurements will
be available. Thus, practical CGME is hopeless unless one
exploits further structure. For this reason, [8], [9] capitalize on
side information such as building or foliage maps. However, in
the standard CGME setup, such information is not available.

The key idea in this paper is to sidestep this difficulty
by considering multiple instances of the CGME problem.
Specifically, suppose that one is given N measurement sets
M1, . . . , MN, each one from a different environment (geo-
graphical region). Clearly, each of these sets contains infor-
mation that is common to all environments and information
that is specific to its environment. Intuitively, the common
information pertains to the way radio waves propagate across
space and interact with matter, whereas the specific informa-
tion in Mn encodes the special characteristics of the n-th
environment. This includes the positions, shapes, and materials
of the obstacles, such as buildings, vegetation, and terrain
features. Plain data-driven approaches based on (1) fail if the
amount of information in M is insufficient to learn both the
common and specific information. However, if one can learn
the common information offline from measurements in other
environments, estimating g in a new environment just requires
learning the specific information, which can be accomplished
with a much smaller M.

C. Invariance Preserving Transformer-based Estimator
It remains to provide a means to implement estimators of
the form gψ(r′, r′′; M). As seen next, transformers are highly
suitable for this task because M contains a variable number
M of measurements; cf. Sec. III. Since a transformer takes
matrix inputs and returns matrix outputs, proper adaptation
functions are required. Specifically, the targeted estimator
will be constructed as the composition gψ(r′, r′′; M) =
R(T ψ(U ψ(r′, r′′; M))), where U ψ maps the input to a
matrix, T is a transformer, and R maps the output of the
transformer to a scalar.
To achieve good estimation performance, it is convenient
to attune U ψ to the invariances and equivariances [17] of
the CGME problem. For example, changing the order of the
measurements in M should not affect the map estimate. A
poor choice of U ψ forces the transformer to learn these invari-
ances from data, which is inefficient, dramatically increases
the data needs, and limits the ability of the estimator to learn
complex patterns. A suitable design is pursued here by forming
U ψ as a composition of transformations U 1, U 2, ..., each
one rendering U ψ (and therefore gψ) invariant to one of the
invariances of the problem. Each of these transformations is
briefly discussed next (details can be found in the code):
• U 1 (Symmetry with respect to the evaluation loca-
tions): The first transformation satisfies U 1(r′, r′′; M) =
U 1(r′′, r′; M). To this end, it returns (r′, r′′, M) if r′
is on average closer to the points in M than r′′, and
(r′′, r′, M) otherwise.
• U 2 (Invariance to horizontal translation): U 2 shifts the
origin to r′ by subtracting the coordinates of r′ from all
points in (r′, r′′, M). Since the original height of r′ is
informative about the distance to the ground, it is retained
as a separate feature.
• U 3 (Invariance to permutation of the endpoints within
each measurement): For each m, exchange r′
m and r′′
m if
necessary so that the one closer to the origin appears first.
• U 4 (Invariance to rotation about the vertical axis):
With the z axis pointing upwards, rotate every location
about this axis so that the horizontal projection of r′′ lies
on the positive x semi-axis.
• U 5 (Invariance to mirroring across the xz plane):
Reflect every location across the xz plane if the (soft)
majority of y-coordinates of the points in M is negative.
• U ψ,7 (Embedding): Arrange the resulting features into a
matrix whose m-th column collects the transformed coor-
dinates of r′
m, r′′
m, and r′′, their magnitudes, their coor-
dinates normalized by their magnitudes, the z-coordinate
of r′ before U 2, and the measurement value ˜gm. Then
apply a learnable linear map that embeds each column
into RD, yielding the D × M input to T.
The only remaining invariance to be addressed is the
invariance to permutation of the measurements. Given the
permutation equivariance of T, the entire network will be
invariant if R is properly selected, e.g. as an average of the
entries of its input. However, from a training perspective, it
is preferable to use causal attention blocks and learn this
invariance from data; see [35, Sec. IV-C] for details.
The resulting estimator will be referred to as cross-
environment transformer-based estimator (CRETE).
V. NUMERICAL EXPERIMENTS
This section presents numerical experiments that analyze
estimation performance in the developed framework.
In all experiments, the region R is a volume of 350 m ×
350 m × 20 m and the frequency of interest is f 0 = 2.4 GHz.
Measurements are generated in two ways: (i) by ray-tracing in
85 urban environments (17 for testing) with different building
layouts. The channel gain is obtained between all terminals;
(ii) via the tomographic model on randomly generated envi-
ronments that comprise a variable number of buildings whose
horizontal locations are uniformly distributed at random within
the limits of R. The spatial loss field is defined on a 32×32×1
grid and takes the value 1 dB/m inside the buildings and 0
dB/m elsewhere. 50 randomly located terminals are placed
in R and the channel gain is computed between all pairs of
terminals. To obtain capacity from channel gain, the bandwidth
is set to 20 MHz, the transmit power to 0.3 W, and the noise
power to -96 dBm.
The proposed CRETE estimator is compared with 6 com-
petitors. This includes three tomographic estimators that mini-
mize a quadratic fitting term regularized by an ℓ1 penalty [10],
a total-variation functional [37], and a Tikhonov quadratic
term [25]. In addition, comparisons also include a K-nearest-
neighbor estimator, which averages the measurements of the
K nearest location pairs, taking into account both permu-
tations of the terminal locations when computing distances.
The parameters of these algorithms were set so that they
achieve roughly the best performance in these experiments.
The estimator from [8] has been adapted to cross-environment
estimation by applying the approach in [38], which resulted
in an explicit estimator; cf. Sec. IV-B. This is because plain
application of this scheme would require training the DNN
on each Monte Carlo iteration, which is impractical. The
estimator, labeled as IMAI-CAVIA, uses 80k parameters and
a 32 × 32 context matrix. A large collection of other explicit
estimators were developed and tested, but their performance
was not competitive with the above benchmarks. Among them,
the one with the best performance (SIREN-CNP) is presented
as a reference. It uses the CNP architecture [6] with fully
connected layers and SIREN activations [30]. The number of
parameters is roughly 660k. CRETE is implemented using a
transformer with 12 blocks, 2 attention heads, and embedding
dimension 128. This results in roughly 2M parameters.

environments with more buildings than seen during training.
