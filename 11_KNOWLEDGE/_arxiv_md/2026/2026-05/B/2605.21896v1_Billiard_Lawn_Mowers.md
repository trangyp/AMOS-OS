BILLIARD LAWN MOWERS
NATNAREE SRIPRASERT AND SANGSAN WARAKKAGUN
Abstract. We study the Lawn Mowing Problem restricted to periodic
billiard paths in the unit square.
Given the combinatorial data of a
trajectory, we determine the optimal covering radius, and identify the
shortest path that covers the square for any fixed blade radius.
1. Introduction
Imagine a circular lawn mower that travels in a straight line across a
square yard, bouncing off the boundary with conservative reflection law like
a billiard ball. For which such trajectories does the mower cover every point
of the yard? Or given a fixed blade radius, what is the shortest trajectory
guaranteed to mow the entire square?
These questions are inspired by the Lawn Mowing Problem of Arkin,
Fekete, and Mitchell [1], in which the task is to find a shortest path whose
neighborhood of fixed width covers a given planar region.
The Lawn Mowing Problem is NP-hard in general.
Even in a 2 × 2
square, an optimal route cannot have coordinates expressible in radicals
[7].
Restricting to billiard-like paths changes the picture.
A trajectory
starting off with irrational slope is dense, so in principle, such a path sweeps
the whole square, but only after infinitely many bounces. We cannot wait
forever. We therefore focus on periodic trajectories: those that return to
their starting position after finitely many reflections. While these paths may
not be the shortest since they generically self-intersect, they are practical,
requiring little programmed input for they are completely determined by
their initial configuration. Periodic trajectories have rational slopes and, as
we will show, admit a clean geometric description. For such paths, we will
solve our restricted Lawn Mowing Problem.
2. The setup
Throughout the paper, we think of the lawn as the unit square T =
[0, 1] × [0, 1], considered as a subset of R2.
In our model, the center of
the robotic mower is viewed as a point mass traveling along a trajectory,
a sequence of directed straight segments in T, where each segment begins

A trajectory is singular if it meets some corner of T. A non-singular
trajectory γ is said to be periodic if the point returns to its starting point
in the same initial position and direction after a finite number of bouncing
points along the sides.
Unless stated otherwise, periodic trajectories are primitive, that is, they
are traversed only once. The period of a periodic trajectory is the number
of bounces it makes before returning to the starting position, including the
final one.
The orbit of a trajectory γ is the subset of T consisting of the points lying
on γ. For brevity of notation, the orbit of γ will also be denoted by γ, as
the context will make it clear what we mean.
The remainder of this paper will give complete answers to Q1 and Q2
for periodic orbits in T.
