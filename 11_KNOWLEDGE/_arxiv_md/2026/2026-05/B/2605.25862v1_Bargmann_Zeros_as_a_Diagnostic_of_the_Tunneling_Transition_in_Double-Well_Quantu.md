Abstract
Complex zeros of wavefunctions represented as entire functions in
Bargmann–Fock space encode structural information about the underlying
quantum state. Prior work employed zero galleries of randomly generated
polynomial superpositions of Fock states as visual fingerprints suitable
for classification. Here we examine whether Bargmann zeros of physi-
cally realized eigenstates of one-dimensional anharmonic and double-well
Hamiltonians carry a recognizable signature of the tunneling transition
in the symmetric double well. Ground and first-excited eigenstates are
obtained from a variational ansatz consisting of a physically motivated sym-
bolic envelope multiplied by a small flexible correction network, trained by
Rayleigh–Ritz minimization of the finite-difference Hamiltonian expecta-
tion value and validated to reproduce energies to within ∼10−5 Ha. The
resulting wavefunctions are projected onto the harmonic-oscillator basis
and the complex zeros of the truncated Bargmann polynomial are located
by numerical root-finding. For harmonic and quartic-anharmonic potentials
the zeros show no preferred orientation. For double-well eigenstates, by

Introduction
The Bargmann (or Fock–Bargmann) representation maps square-integrable
wavefunctions of a one-dimensional quantum system onto entire holomorphic
functions of a complex variable z ∈C, with Fock states playing the role of the
monomial basis zn/
√
n! [Bargmann, 1961]. This construction sits within the
broader coherent-state framework that originated with Glauber’s quantum-
optical coherent states [Glauber, 1963] and was generalized by Perelomov to
arbitrary Lie groups [Perelomov, 1972]; the standard reprint volume of Klauder
and Skagerstam [Klauder and Skagerstam, 1985] collects the definitive results.
Any wavefunction can therefore be written as a (formally infinite) polynomial in
z, and its complex zeros form a discrete set in C that, by Weierstrass–Hadamard
factorization, determines the state up to a multiplicative entire function of order
at most one.
Recent work by Janowicz & Zembrzuski [Janowicz and Zembrzuski, 2022]
explored the visual structure of these zero sets for random-coefficient superpo-
sitions of Fock states and demonstrated that a convolutional neural network
can classify qualitatively distinct families of such random states from their
zero images alone. In parallel, Cerf et al. [Cerf et al., 2025] have shown that,
under a mild energy condition, a bosonic wavefunction extends to an entire
holomorphic function on C whose zero set characterizes the non-Gaussianity
of the state via a Hudson-type theorem, and evolves as a classical many-body
dynamical system under Gaussian Hamiltonians. Together these results raise
a natural question: do the zero images of physically solved eigenstates carry
analogous structure, and does that structure encode a recognizable signature of
the underlying physics?
We address this question for the symmetric double well. A variational

ansatz — a physically motivated symbolic envelope multiplied by a small

as a contribution to variational methodology and does not approach the deep

by numerical root-finding, and the barrier parameter is swept continuously to
track zero migration through the tunneling transition.

Zeros and factorization
By Hadamard’s factorization theorem, an entire function f (z) of finite order is
determined up to an exponential polynomial prefactor by its zeros {zk} and its
order. Truncating equation (2) to n ≤N produces a polynomial with exactly

Two symmetries are important. First, since ψ(x) ∈R for all physical eigen-
states considered here, the coefficients cn are real and the zero set is symmet-
ric under complex conjugation z 7→¯z. Second, if ψ(x) has a definite parity
(ψ(−x) = ±ψ(x)), then cn = 0 for n of the opposite parity, which forces the
Bargmann polynomial to be either even or odd in z. An odd polynomial in z
has a zero at the origin and its remaining zeros come in pairs {z, −z}; an even
polynomial has its zeros in such pairs. Combined with conjugation symmetry,
parity states therefore have zeros that either lie at the origin, on the imaginary
or real axis, or in groups of four {±z, ±¯z}.

3.5
Barrier sweep protocol
To map the tunneling transition, the double-well barrier parameter is swept
over 20 values, a ∈[0.5, 2.3], uniformly spaced. At each a, both eigenpairs
(E0, ψ0) and (E1, ψ1) are computed by the finite-difference solver of Section 3.1

Ha – and the zero set within |z| < 6 is extracted as in Section 3.4. The tunneling
splitting ∆(a) = E1(a) −E0(a) is tabulated in parallel.

the anharmonic eigenstates have a more distributed Fock spectrum but still pro-

for the ground state (p = +1, a pair of imaginary zeros at z ≈±1.6i) and for
the first-excited state (p = −1, a zero at the origin plus an imaginary pair at
z ≈±2.4i).
The parity-conjugation symmetry analysis of Section 2 predicts that double-
well eigenstates, being real and of definite parity, must have zeros either at the
origin, on the real axis, on the imaginary axis, or in groups of four. That they
preferentially occupy the imaginary axis rather than the real axis is a separate
physical statement, distinguishing tunneling-like from pure anharmonic-like
behavior.
4.3
Zero migration across the tunneling transition
We now sweep the barrier parameter a continuously and track how the zero set
evolves. Figure 4 shows the tunneling splitting ∆(a) = E1 −E0 on a logarithmic
vertical scale. The splitting falls from ∆≈1.0 Ha at a = 0.5 to ∆≈3 × 10−4 Ha
at a = 2.3, a range of 3.5 decades. The curvature on the log scale is consistent
with the WKB/instanton prediction [Garg, 2000, Zinn-Justin and Jentschura,
2004], based on the standard methodology of Coleman [Coleman, 1977], that
the splitting scales as ∆(a) ∼exp(−S0(a)) with an action S0 that grows faster
than linearly with a as the barrier deepens.

4.4.1
Grid-resolution ablation
Table 2 reports the ground- and first-excited-state energies across four grid
resolutions N ∈{512, 1024, 2048, 4096} for all six test potentials. The energy
drift between N = 1024 and N = 4096 is ≤5 × 10−5 Ha across all potentials,
consistent with the expected h2 convergence of the three-point finite-difference
stencil. This justifies N = 1024 as the default grid resolution throughout the
paper.
4.4.2
Correction-network capacity ablation
To verify that the paper’s conclusions are insensitive to the choice of correction-
network architecture, we sweep over a 3 × 4 grid of configurations (depths
{2, 3, 4}, widths {16, 32, 64, 128}) on the double-well potential at a = 1.5, with
20 random seeds per configuration. Even the smallest architecture (2 × 16,
331 parameters) achieves a mean energy error of 8.97 × 10−7 Ha, more than
two orders of magnitude below the 10−5 Ha threshold of the abstract; the
largest (4 × 128, 49,931 parameters) reaches 1.9 × 10−8 Ha and saturates near
the N = 1024 grid-resolution floor. The paper’s qualitative conclusions are
therefore architecturally insensitive; the 4 × 128 configuration is used as the
default elsewhere in the paper. Full results are provided in Appendix B, Table 5.

5.1
Physical origin of imaginary-axis condensation
The imaginary-axis localization of Bargmann zeros for the double-well eigen-
states can be understood at the level of the Fock-coefficient spectrum. Under
the parity-projection condition cn = 0 for odd n (even-parity ground state) or
even n (odd-parity first-excited state), the Bargmann polynomial takes the form

w = z2 therefore contracts the z-plane zero-pair {ζ, −ζ} to a single w-plane
zero w0 = ζ2. Real w0 < 0 (i.e. zeros in the left half w-plane) correspond to
pure imaginary ζ; real w0 > 0 to pure real ζ; complex w0 to a four-fold cluster
{±ζ, ± ¯ζ}. The observed condensation of z-zeros onto the imaginary axis is thus
equivalent to a condensation of w-zeros onto the negative real semi-axis. This
latter condition is, at least heuristically, a signature of a specific sign structure of
the Fock-coefficient sequence {c2k} that arises precisely when the wavefunction
is bimodally localized in position space (as is the case in the deep-tunneling
regime).
Equivalently, the imaginary-axis zeros may be interpreted as Husimi-phase-
space nodes of the coherent-state transform Q(z) = π−1|ψ(z)|2 e−|z|2, with
Q as originally defined by Husimi [Husimi, 1940] and subsequently devel-
oped within the Glauber–Sudarshan coherent-state framework [Glauber, 1963,
Klauder and Skagerstam, 1985]. This connects the present observation to the
programme [Leboeuf and Voros, 1990, Cerf et al., 2025] in which the zero set
of the holomorphic (or Husimi) representation is treated as the primary object
of study rather than the wavefunction itself. Leboeuf & Voros [Leboeuf and
Voros, 1990] demonstrated that the zero set of the Husimi function is chaos-
revealing for classically non-integrable systems, and Cerf et al. [Cerf et al., 2025]
have sharpened the connection via a Hudson-type theorem linking zeros to
non-Gaussianity. We do not pursue that direction further here, but note that it
provides an independent computational route to the same diagnostic, useful
when the Bargmann polynomial is too high-degree for direct root-finding to
remain numerically stable.
5.2
Limitations
The present analysis is restricted to one-dimensional systems, parity-symmetric
states, and the two lowest eigenvalues. Extension to higher excited states
presents no methodological obstacle — the Bargmann polynomial becomes
higher-degree and zero-finding more expensive, but the procedure is unchanged
— though the parity-zero symmetry that simplifies the present analysis will be
broken for asymmetric or multi-minima potentials. Extension to two-dimensional
systems requires a two-mode Bargmann representation whose zero set is a va-
riety in C2 rather than a discrete point set; this is left to future work. Finally,
the variational ansatz is modest by the standards of contemporary variational
wavefunctions [Carleo and Troyer, 2017, Pfau et al., 2020, Hermann et al., 2020];
it was chosen for interpretability in one dimension rather than as a contribution
to variational Monte Carlo.

6
Conclusion
We have shown that the complex zeros of the Bargmann-represented wave-
function carry a recognizable signature of the tunneling regime in the one-
dimensional symmetric double well. A variational ansatz reproducing finite-
difference reference energies to ∼10−5 Ha is used to project ground and first-
excited eigenstates onto the harmonic-oscillator basis; the zeros of the truncated
Bargmann polynomial are then extracted across a continuous sweep of the bar-
rier parameter. The zeros migrate onto the imaginary axis as the barrier deepens,
concurrent with the 3.5-decade exponential collapse of the tunneling splitting.
This imaginary-axis condensation is traced to a sign-alternation structure in the
Fock-coefficient spectrum characteristic of bimodally localized wavefunctions,
and is connected to the Husimi-function zero set through the coherent-state
representation.
The result extends the random-polynomial Bargmann-zero framework [Janow-
icz and Zembrzuski, 2022] to physically realized eigenstates, identifying the
double-well tunneling transition as a phenomenon directly readable from the
zero set. Generalization to asymmetric double wells, to excited states with
n > 1, and to multi-mode systems where the Bargmann zero set becomes
a higher-dimensional algebraic variety are natural directions for subsequent
work.

A
Training hyperparameters and hardware
This appendix gives the full hyperparameter, initialization, reproducibility, and
hardware details summarized in Section 3.3.
Optimizer schedule. Training proceeds in two stages. A four-phase Adam
[Kingma and Ba, 2015] schedule with default momenta β = (0.9, 0.999) and
learning rates 3 × 10−3 →1 × 10−3 →3 × 10−4 →5 × 10−5 is applied for
a total of 10,000 full-batch gradient steps (the grid xi is the entire “batch”);
the learning rate is stepped down after 30%, 60%, and 85% of the schedule
respectively. An L-BFGS polish with strong-Wolfe line search then runs for up
to 4 outer iterations of 50 inner iterations each, with gradient and parameter-
change tolerances 10−12 and 10−14 respectively, and a Hessian history of size
50. The best energy encountered at any point during the schedule is retained;
backtracking to earlier checkpoints is not needed in practice because the L-BFGS
polish is always monotonically improving.
Initialization. Correction-network weights are drawn independently from
N (0, 0.32) per layer; biases are initialized to zero. Envelope width parameters
{σk} are initialized logarithmically spaced in [0.35, 1.0]; polynomial prefactor
coefficients {ck} are drawn from N (0, 0.012); Gaussian mixture weights {wk}
are initialized uniformly; the double-well barrier parameter inside the ansatz is
initialized at its target value (e.g. a = 1.5 for the mid-tunneling baseline) and
allowed to refine during training.
Reproducibility. Each training run is seeded via an independent torch.Generator
instance, so that per-seed initial conditions, and therefore per-seed final energies

and Bargmann zero positions, are deterministic. All ablation tables report mean
± std across 20 independent seeds per configuration.
Hardware and wall-clock. Energy-ablation runs reported in Section 4.4 were
