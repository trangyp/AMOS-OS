Abstract
The Laser Interferometer Lunar Antenna (LILA) is a proposed gravitational-wave project
aiming to take full advantage of the Moon’s environment to access the deci-Hz band and
detect intermediate-mass black hole (IMBH) binaries of mass ∼102 −106 M⊙. With an
observational period of 4 years, LILA can extend its IMBH detection horizon to the very
early Universe, directly probing the first population of massive black holes (z ∼20 −30).
LILA could also detect intermediate-mass-ratio inspiral systems with a total mass of
∼104 −106 M⊙and a mass ratio of ∼10−4 −10−2. LILA can discover IMBH binaries
months to years before merger with measurable eccentricity residuals retained from their
formation, providing crucial early warning for multi-messenger and multi-band follow-up.
The high SNR (≳100) events detectable with LILA would enable strong-field tests of
gravity. With these capabilities, LILA will provide important insights into the formation
and evolution of massive black holes, as well as the astrophysical environments and
evolutionary pathways of black hole binaries. LILA will also complement current
LIGO/Virgo/KAGRA detections of pair-instability mass gap events, hierarchical merger
candidates, and light IMBH mergers, while expanding the upper envelope of discovered
black holes with stellar origin to masses of ≳250 M⊙.

Introduction
Gravitational-wave (GW) astronomy just celebrated its ten-year anniversary of the first
stellar-mass black hole merger event GW150914 (Abbott et al., 2016a), discovered by the Laser
Interferometer GW Observatory (LIGO). Since then, ground GW detectors (LIGO, Virgo and
KAGRA, hereafter LVK) have detected hundreds of black hole merger events (Abbott et al., 2023)
with final black hole masses ranging from as small as ∼5M⊙(GW230529_181500; Abac et al.
2024a) to as massive as ∼225 M⊙(GW231123; Abac et al. 2025b; Chatterjee et al. 2025),
complementing previous and ongoing X-ray binary observations (McClintock & Remillard, 2006).
Since GWs preserve spacetime information from the sources, they provide stringent tests of general
relativity (GR) (Abbott et al., 2016b, 2025) and precise measurements of black hole mass and spin
(Abbott et al., 2023). The spin distributions of discovered binary stellar-mass black holes in
gravitational waves (predominantly slowly-spinning) and X-ray (predominantly rapidly-spinning)
detections are significantly different (Reynolds, 2021; Zdziarski et al., 2025). Though there are

Another exciting development is the strong evidence for the stochastic GW background
detection made by the North American Nanohertz Observatory for GW (NANOGrav; Agazie et al.
2023a), which can be attributed to the population of supermassive black hole (SMBH) binaries and
mergers over cosmic time (Agazie et al., 2023b; Ellis et al., 2024a). Unlike ground GW detectors in
the ∼10 −104 Hz band, pulsar timing arrays like NANOGrav work in a much lower frequency
regime of ∼10−9 −10−7 Hz. Interestingly, models found that NANOGrav results are consistent
with the black hole to host galaxy stellar mass ratio suggested by high-redshift overmassive SMBHs
(Pacucci et al., 2023; Pacucci & Loeb, 2024; Ellis et al., 2024b) in active galactic nuclei (AGN)
discovered by the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and AGN X-ray luminosity function
(Kusakabe et al., 2026a), highlighting the importance of multi-messenger astronomy in uncovering
the universal presence of SMBHs and their mergers. Future next-generation radio telescopes like
the Square Kilometer Array (SKA; Dewdney et al. 2009) will enable more sensitive pulsar timing
arrays that could potentially detect individual black hole binaries, in addition to an improved
measurement of the stochastic GW background (Lazio, 2013; Shannon et al., 2025).
In the next decade, the space-based Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) will probe an
entire new frequency band of ∼10−4 −10−1 Hz, which will primarily target SMBH inspirals and
mergers (Amaro-Seoane et al., 2017, 2023). Existing observational evidence for dual AGN and
binary SMBH systems is circumstantial, many of which are unresolved or biased towards
radio-bright compact jetted systems (see, e.g., review by Krause et al. 2025). Many SMBH binary
candidates have been identified by their periodic variability in the Palomar Transient Factory
quasar sample (Charisi et al., 2016). There is limited evidence for sub-parsec SMBH binaries,
among the strongest being OJ 287 (Valtonen et al., 2009; Britzen et al., 2018; Laine et al., 2020;
Gómez et al., 2026) and PKS 1302-102 (Graham et al., 2015) with quasi-periodic optical outbursts,
hindering our understanding of how SMBHs merge within the age of the Universe when they are a
few parsecs of each other (the “final parsec problem"; Milosavljević & Merritt 2003). Hence, LISA
and other gravitational-wave detectors covering a similar frequency band will significantly improve
observational evidence for SMBH binary systems and our theoretical understanding of their
dynamics. The advent of the Rubin Observatory and the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST)
will revolutionize time-domain astrophysics and provide complementary probes in identifying
SMBH binary systems with light curve variability (Witt et al., 2022; Xin et al., 2026). LSST,
Roman, LISA, and pulsar timing arrays will take full advantage of multi-messenger astrophysics in
unveiling massive black hole binaries in the next decade (De Rosa et al., 2019; Xin & Haiman,
2021; Charisi et al., 2022; Haiman et al., 2023; D’Orazio & Charisi, 2023; Xin & Haiman, 2024).
Another proposed future GW detector mission is the Laser Interferometer Lunar Antenna
(LILA; Jani et al. 2025), which is targeting two phases called LILA-Pioneer and LILA-Horizon.

From a theoretical standpoint, IMBHs could form in dense stellar clusters (Gürkan et al., 2004;
Giersz et al., 2015; Arca Sedda et al., 2018; González et al., 2021), possibly through runaway stellar
collisions (Miller & Hamilton, 2002; Portegies Zwart & McMillan, 2002; Gürkan et al., 2006;
Rodriguez et al., 2019; Di Carlo et al., 2021; Purohit et al., 2024; González Prieto et al., 2024;
Vergara et al., 2025; Rantala et al., 2026; Mestichelli et al., 2026), and grow through tidal capture
and tidal disruption events (Sakurai et al., 2019; Rizzuto et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2025), as well as
hierarchical mergers (Christian et al., 2018; Rodriguez et al., 2019; Antonini et al., 2019; Fragione
& Rasio, 2023; Kritos et al., 2025) and efficient accretion (Natarajan, 2021; Kritos et al., 2024a) in
stellar clusters. The predictions for IMBH populations in realistic astrophysical environments and
cosmological contexts (Boekholt et al., 2018; Tagawa et al., 2020) are very uncertain because of the
difficulties in resolving stellar clusters and their coevolution with IMBHs inside their systems.
Despite the theoretical and observational challenges so far, IMBHs are vital for understanding the
cosmic assembly of SMBHs (e.g., Volonteri & Rees 2005; Shapiro 2005; King & Pringle 2006;
Ricarte & Natarajan 2018a) and the coevolution with their host galaxies (Kormendy & Ho, 2013;
McConnell & Ma, 2013), as well as the dynamical evolution of dense stellar systems. The
widespread presence of heavy (≳107 M⊙) black holes in the early Universe (z ≳3) suggested by
observations (Lyke et al., 2020; Fan et al., 2023; Pacucci et al., 2023; Taylor et al., 2025) further
motivate the need to search for IMBHs at earlier epochs to bridge the gap between the first seeding
populations and the observed demographics (Barkana & Loeb, 2001; Madau & Rees, 2001; Bromm
& Loeb, 2003; Volonteri, 2010; Ferrara et al., 2014; Pacucci & Loeb, 2022). This highlights the
importance of forecasting the IMBH detection landscape for deci-Hz GW detectors like LILA.
This paper details the signal-to-noise calculation framework used to characterize the detection
landscape and develop science cases for LILA, with an emphasis on IMBH binaries. Section 2
explains the characteristic strain evolution model for binary systems, accounting for
inspiral-merger-ringdown phases and eccentricity. Section 3 outlines the signal-to-noise calculations
for LILA given the characteristic strain of a binary system and accounting for the compact galactic
binary foreground. Section 4 demonstrates the LILA detection horizon and early-warning potential
of black hole binaries across different initial eccentricities, mass ranges, mass ratios, and redshifts,
with implications for massive black hole formation and growth modeling, multi-band GW
astronomy, and GR tests. Section 5 summarizes the main findings of the paper.

Ricarte et al., 2021; Di Matteo et al., 2023) and sub-kiloparsec scales (Milosavljević & Merritt,
2003; Dosopoulou & Antonini, 2017) can substantially delay black hole mergers. Preferential
accretion onto the secondary in moderately eccentric binaries in circumbinary disks can drive the
mass ratio towards unity (Duffell et al., 2020; Muñoz et al., 2020; Siwek et al., 2023). Another
factor we do not account for is black hole spin, which has minimal impact on the farthest
detectable distance for masses of ≲103 M⊙but could increase (or decrease) the detection distance
by a factor of 2 if the individual black hole spins are aligned (or misaligned) with the orbital
angular momentum for equal-mass binaries of ≳104 M⊙(Jani et al., 2020).

4.2
Eccentric IMBH Binaries
Because of circularization effects of gravitational radiation (Peters, 1964) as demonstrated by
Equation 17, we expect the final orbital eccentricity of compact binary mergers detected by LVK
and accessible by ground-based detector frequency range in general to be too small to be measured
(Abbott et al., 2016c). In fact, only a very small fraction of LVK black hole merger detections so
far such as GW190521 (Abbott et al., 2020) has evidence for significant eccentricity and dynamical
evolution (Romero-Shaw et al., 2020, 2022; Gamba et al., 2023), though limited eccentric waveform
templates pose major challenges in LVK searches for eccentric black hole mergers (Abac et al.,
2024b). Black hole binary formation in dynamically active environments, such as dense stellar
clusters (Rodriguez et al., 2016, 2018; Arca Sedda et al., 2018; Samsing, 2018; Zevin et al., 2019;
Fragione & Bromberg, 2019), galactic nuclei (Antonini & Rasio, 2016; Gondán et al., 2018; Zhang
et al., 2019; Tagawa et al., 2021; Samsing et al., 2022), and triples (Antonini et al., 2017; Arca
Sedda et al., 2021b) via the Kozai-Lidov mechanism (Kozai, 1962; Lidov, 1962; Naoz, 2016), could
imprint their signatures on orbital eccentricity. In addition, moderately eccentric binaries formed in
circumbinary disks would not circularize and instead retain much eccentricity as they are driven
towards inspirals and mergers (Siwek et al., 2023).
LISA is expected to distinguish black hole binaries formed in isolation from those in more
dynamical scenarios in the milli-Hz frequency band for some nearby systems (Breivik et al., 2016;
Nishizawa et al., 2016, 2017; Kremer et al., 2018), but for binaries with very high initial
eccentricities, the dominant gravitational wave signals are in the deci-Hz regime outside of the
LISA band (Chen & Amaro-Seoane, 2017; D’Orazio & Samsing, 2018; Kremer et al., 2019). In
particular, simulations of dense star cluster populations suggest that 15% of dynamical binaries will
have eccentricities e ≳0.1 at 0.1 Hz (Kritos et al., 2024b). Thus, as a deci-Hz detector, LILA is
well-suited for discovering systems some time before mergers with measurable eccentricity residuals
retained from their formation mechanisms, potentially probing astrophysical environments and
evolutionary pathways of black hole binaries. Figure 3 shows that equal-mass IMBH binaries with
masses of 102 −106 M⊙and initial orbital eccentricity of e0 = 0.999, observed four years before
merger, enter the LILA sensitivity band with significant eccentricity residuals of e ∼0.6. Figure 6
illustrates that binary systems with masses 103 −105 M⊙and an initial eccentricity of e = 0.9999
are detectable (with SNR = 8 threshold) by both LILA-Pioneer and LILA-Horizon more than a
month before merger, highlighting LILA’s prospects for discovering highly eccentric binaries and
their evolutionary signatures as products of dynamically active environments.

4.5
Multi-Band Synergy with Ground-Based Detectors in Probing 100 −1000 M⊙Black Holes
With dual-band capability (Shapiro, 2025), LILA-Horizon can extend its detection reach down to
light IMBHs with masses of 100 −1000 M⊙and achieve sensitivity comparable to that of future
ground detectors like ET and CE for probing this class of black hole binaries. Figure 9 illustrates
that current and future GW detectors, both ground-based (LIGO, CE, ET) and space-based
(LISA, LILA), and pulsar timing arrays (NANOGrav, SKA) provide complementary coverage
across the GW spectrum to uncover the full mass range of black hole binaries across cosmic time
throughout the observable Universe. Figure 2 shows the evolutionary track of a 100 + 100 M⊙
binary, which enters the deci-Hz band a year in advance and stays within the LILA-Horizon
sensitive band for most of its time before reaching the ≳10 Hz band of ground detectors right
before merging. Figure 6 shows that a 100 + 100 M⊙binary inspiral could reach SNR = 8 from
weeks to years before merger depending on the initial orbital eccentricity, demonstrating strong
prospects for providing early warning for future merger events to be detected by ground detectors.
One motivation for this science case is GW231123 (137 + 101 M⊙), the most massive black hole
merger event discovered so far (Abac et al., 2025b). The initial black hole mass posteriors largely
span the mass gap of ∼60 −130 M⊙arising from pair-instability supernovae, which do not leave
behind a stellar remnant (Farmer et al., 2019; Woosley & Heger, 2021; Hendriks et al., 2023). The
existence of GW231123 is naturally explained by hierarchical mergers in dense stellar clusters
(Abac et al., 2025b; Li & Fan, 2025; Liu & Lai, 2025; Li et al., 2025; Passenger et al., 2025; Paiella
et al., 2025; Angeloni et al., 2026) with the assistance of environments that keep the intermediate
merger remnant from escaping because of recoil kicks, such as AGN disks (Delfavero et al., 2025).
However, the inferred high spin in GW231123 is not likely to be a result of dynamically assembled
black holes (Stegmann et al., 2025), leading to alternative explanations, such as efficient accretion
in an isolated binary (van Son et al., 2020; Bartos & Haiman, 2026), binary evolution of pop III
stars avoiding pair instability (Tanikawa et al., 2025), collapse of high-mass rapidly-spinning helium
stars (Gottlieb et al., 2025; Croon et al., 2026) through chemically homogeneous evolution (Popa &
de Mink, 2025), primordial black holes (Yuan et al., 2025; De Luca et al., 2025), and cosmic strings
(Cuceu et al., 2026). Uncovering more systems like GW231123 would further shed light on the
formation and evolutionary pathways of black hole binaries in the pair-instability mass gap. In
addition to GW231123, LVK detectors have discovered a few mergers (such as GW190521; Abbott

4.6
Testing GR
Figure 4 shows that mergers of black hole masses of ∼104 M⊙out to redshift z ∼10 and z ∼100
can be detected by LILA-Pioneer and LILA-Horizon with an SNR of 100, respectively. Such a high
SNR detection would enable a wide range of novel strong-field tests of gravity. Recently, the
GW250114 merger event has been detected by LIGO with an unprecedented SNR of 80, resulting
in the experimental confirmation of Hawking’s area theorem (Hawking, 1971) and the Kerr nature
of black holes (Abac et al., 2025d). This strong SNR signal allows detailed black hole spectroscopy
analysis of the quasi-normal modes during the ringdown phase (Yang et al., 2025; Abac et al.,
2026a; Wang et al., 2026), enabling stringent verification of GR, the Kerr nature of black holes, and
the presence of an event horizon (Chandra et al., 2025; Akyüz et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2025; Grimaldi
et al., 2026), as well as constraining possible deviations from GR and modified gravity theories
(Guo & Miao, 2025; Andrés-Carcasona & Caneva Santoro, 2025). Hence, LILA detectors have
strong potential to provide even more rigorous tests of GR and modified gravity theories by
uncovering many more black hole merger events with similar or higher SNR than GW250114.
Recently, the fourth observing run of LVK detectors has doubled the number of confident
detections (defined to be measured in at least two detectors with false alarm rates ≤10−3 yr−1),
allowing highly sensitive tests of GR with some of the most stringent constraints on deviations
from post-Newtonian coefficients in GR (Abac et al., 2026b,c,d). Alongside next-generation GW
detectors, with a detection horizon towards most of the observable Universe (Subsection 4.1), LILA
will substantially expand the number of confident high-SNR events to continue pushing
experimental tests of GR to their limits.
LILA’s ability to test gravity is not limited to the strong-field regime. As shown in Figure 2 and
3, as the IMBH binary strain enters the deci-Hz band up to months and years before merger, LILA
can potentially track hundreds to thousands of GW signals during the inspiral. Figure 6
demonstrates that many IMBH binaries and IMRI systems can be detected by LILA detectors with
an SNR of 8 up to months or years in advance, depending on the individual masses, mass ratio,
and initial orbital eccentricity, making LILA sensitive to deviations from GR in the post-Newtonian
expansion of the waveforms, enabling tests of modified gravity theories and searches for signatures
of new physics (Nair et al., 2019; Das et al., 2024; Cárdenas-Avendaño & Sopuerta, 2024; Zi & Shu,

5
Conclusion
The proposed lunar-based LILA will take full advantage of the lack of seismic, atmospheric, and
anthropogenic noise on the Moon to access the deci-Hz GW band and uncover a new population of
GW sources with IMBH binaries and IMRI systems, potentially observing binary inspirals months
to years before merger. The early warning capacity is essential for multi-messenger and multi-band
follow-up, as well as detailed characterization of the astrophysical environments and formation
pathways of black hole binaries. The initial pathfinder mission, LILA-Pioneer, could already
uncover a large fraction of IMBHs in the very early Universe (z > 10), crucial to understanding the
formation and evolution of the first massive black hole population. The follow-up LILA-Horizon
detector will deliver transformative milli-Hz to kilo-Hz broadband GW sensitivity and open up new
multi-band opportunities with next-generation ground-based detectors. The key takeaways of this
work on characterizing the detection landscape and proposing science cases for LILA are the
following:
• Discovering IMBH Binaries (Section 4.1): LILA is capable of detecting black hole
binaries across a wide mass range, with its peak sensitivity in the deci-Hz GW band
corresponding to IMBHs of ∼103 −106 M⊙(Figures 2–4).
• Massive Black Hole Seeding & Growth Models (Section 4.1): With an observational
period of 4 years, LILA can extend its IMBH detection horizon to the very early Universe
beyond our current limit (JWST at z ∼14), directly probing the first population of massive
black holes in the seeding epoch (z ∼20 −30), with important implications for massive black
hole formation and evolution through mapping the cosmological black hole mass and spin
distributions throughout cosmic time (Figure 4).
• Discovering Eccentric Binaries (Section 4.2): The milli-to-deci-Hz sensitivity band of
LILA enables the discovery of black hole binaries some time before mergers with measurable
eccentricity residuals retained from their formation (e.g., dynamical channels like dense stellar
clusters), probing astrophysical environments and evolutionary pathways of black hole
binaries (Figure 3).
• Discovering IMRIs (Section 4.3): LILA-Horizon could confidently detect IMRI systems
with mass ∼105 M⊙and mass ratio of ∼10−4 −10−2 towards the very early Universe
(z > 10), providing another pathway for discovering IMBHs and their astrophysical
environments (Figure 5). With high sensitivity in the deci-Hz band, LILA-Horizon could
potentially detect 1000 + 30 M⊙-like IMRI systems with a rate of 8 yr−1, two orders of
magnitude above the LISA rate.
• Early Warning Potential (Section 4.4): IMBH binaries within the local Universe (z ≲1)
can be detected by LILA more than a year before merger (Figure 6), while IMBH binaries in
the early Universe (z ∼10 −20) can be detected by LILA up to about a month before merger
(Figure 7). LILA also enables sky localization on the order of 10−2 to a few square degrees,
crucial for multi-messenger follow-up (Figure 8).
• Multi-Band GW Astronomy (Section 4.5): Current and future GW detectors, both
ground-based (LIGO, CE, ET) and space-based (LISA, LILA), and pulsar timing arrays
(NANOGrav, SKA) provide complementary coverage across the GW spectrum and the mass
range of black hole binaries across cosmic time (Figure 9). LILA-Horizon will expand current
LVK searches of GW231123-like events by improving the 100 + 100 M⊙binary detection rate

• Testing GR (Section 4.6): The ability to detect IMBH binaries and their mergers with

Future work could extend the LILA selection function presented in this paper to make
astrophysical predictions, such as expected LILA detection rates and redshift evolution of black
hole merger rates, using semi-analytical models (e.g., Volonteri et al. 2008; Barausse 2012; Pacucci
& Ferrara 2015; Valiante et al. 2018; Dayal et al. 2019; Sassano et al. 2021; Trinca et al. 2023;
Evans et al. 2025; Ricarte et al. 2025; Bonoli et al. 2025; Davari et al. 2026) and cosmological
simulations (e.g., Di Matteo et al. 2012; Vogelsberger et al. 2014; Khandai et al. 2015; Somerville &
Davé 2015; Tremmel et al. 2017; Griffin et al. 2019; Nelson et al. 2019; Habouzit et al. 2021;
Bhowmick et al. 2024; Jeon et al. 2025a) with realistic black hole population models, seeding
mechanisms, and evolutionary pathways. In addition, future forecasts should incorporate
dynamical models of dense stellar clusters, since cluster dynamics can strongly shape stellar binary
populations through disruption, hardening, and mass segregation (e.g., Heggie 1975; Phillips et al.
2026; Wu et al. 2026; O’Connor et al. 2026; Bruce et al. 2026), potentially playing an important
role in the formation of compact binaries with IMBHs in these environments and affecting merger
rates of IMBH binaries and IMRI systems relevant for LILA (e.g., Di Carlo et al. 2021; Purohit
et al. 2024; Lee et al. 2025; Vergara et al. 2025; Rantala et al. 2026; Mestichelli et al. 2026; Paiella
et al. 2026). Being sensitive to the IMBH mass range back to the seeding epoch of z ∼20 −30,
LILA is uniquely positioned to bridge the evolutionary gap between the earliest black hole seeds
and current SMBH demographics and complement ongoing observational searches for IMBHs in
galaxy nuclei and globular clusters. Mapping out the black hole mass and spin distribution
detectable with LILA across cosmic time will help inform future searches for signatures of massive
black hole formation and guide the mission lifetime for LILA.
