Abstract
We introduce Backtrackable Inprocessing (BI), a framework that en-
ables applying inprocessing under the current trail at any decision level,
at any point during incremental SAT solving.
Our approach lifts the
long-standing restriction that inprocessing must be performed only at the
global decision level, thereby substantially increasing its potential effec-
tiveness. We focus on three highly efficient core techniques: subsumption,
self-subsuming resolution, and Bounded Variable Elimination (BVE). We
show how to ensure sound backtracking in the presence of inprocessing,
and demonstrate that applying BI for incremental preprocessing after
propagating assumptions yields significant performance improvements on
Bounded Model Checking (BMC) benchmarks from the Hardware Model
Checking Competition 2017. Implemented in the Island SAT solver (In-
telSAT’s fork), BI enables solving ∼1.5× as many difficult bounds as the
baseline global-level incremental preprocessor.

Introduction
Modern backtrack-search-based SAT solvers are widely used across numerous
applications [5]. A SAT solver determines whether a Boolean formula in Con-
junctive Normal Form (CNF) is satisfiable. Since the introduction of MiniSat [9],
incremental SAT solving has become essential in many crucial domains [1, 2, 7].
In incremental solving, the user performs a sequence of solver queries, each un-
der a set of assumption literals (assumptions) [9, 14]. Clauses may be added
before any query, and each query checks satisfiability of all clauses provided
so far under its specific assumptions. Implementations typically realize this by
assigning all assumptions as initial decisions (in order) and propagating after
each assignment.
Preprocessing [8] and, later, inprocessing [11] have long been known to sig-
nificantly accelerate SAT solving. Preprocessing simplifies the formula before
search, while inprocessing simplifies it regularly during search. One example of
an inprocessing technique is subsumption: if α and β are clauses with α ⊆β,
then α subsumes β and β can be removed. Initially restricted to non-incremental
solving, inprocessing was adapted for incremental use [15, 10] and has been sup-
ported in the mainstream incremental SAT solver CaDiCaL [3] since [10].

However, currently, inprocessing has a crucial limitation: it is applied only
globally at decision level 0.
This restriction significantly limits the power of
inprocessing. To illustrate the limitation, consider the clauses α = a ∨b ∨c
and β = d ∨b ∨c. At level 0, α does not subsume β. But if we inprocess at
level 1, after assigning a := 0, α does subsume β—until the solver backtracks
below level 1.
Removing β at this point can be highly beneficial, as it may
trigger a chain of further simplifications. This motivates a natural question:
Can inprocessing be invoked under the current trail at any decision level?
No solver offers this capability, with correctness under backtracking the key
challenge.
1.1
Our Contribution: Backtrackable Inprocessing (BI)
We introduce Backtrackable Inprocessing (BI), the first framework that enables
inprocessing under the current trail at any level, at any point in the search. In
this work, we focus on three core, extremely effective inprocessing techniques
originally applied in the pioneering SatELite preprocessor [8]: subsumption,
self-subsuming resolution (which we dub selfsumption), and Bounded Variable
Elimination (BVE). BI fully supports incremental SAT solving.
The key challenge is to ensure that the effects of inprocessing are tracked
per decision level and can be soundly undone for any level we backtrack over,
while remaining in effect at lower levels. We achieve this by generalizing how
the solver classifies and manages clauses.
SAT solvers classify clauses into two types: pervasive and temporary [17, 14].
The set (conjunction) of pervasive clauses is equivalent (or, in some solvers,
equisatisfiable) to the set of input clauses, whereas temporaries are implied
by the pervasives. Pervasive clauses must be preserved for correctness, while
temporary clauses may be deleted at any time (at least at level 0) without
affecting correctness and are therefore managed heuristically. Pervasive clauses
begin as the input clauses but may change during solving; for example, if a
temporary clause α subsumes a pervasive clause β, then β is removed and α
becomes pervasive.
We introduce additional clause types for BI, including:
• stashed at level i, which are deactivated until the solver backtracks below i,
at which point they are restored;
• deputy at level i, which remain active and cannot be deleted until back-
tracking below i.
As an example of how these clause types are created and managed, suppose
α = a ∨b ∨c is temporary and β = d ∨b ∨c is pervasive under decisions e := 1
(level 1) and a := 0 (level 2). After detecting that α subsumes β as long as the
solver does not backtrack below 2, we mark:
β as a stashed-at-2 clause (deactivated),
α as a deputy-at-2 clause (active).
When the solver backtracks below level 2, α will either be deleted or marked as
temporary, and β will be restored as pervasive.

In our framework, at all times, each deactivated stashed clause β has a
deputy clause α that remains active and represents β (by implying it under the
current trail) until either β is restored or another stashed clause γ that can
represent β is restored. The correct management of stashed and deputy clauses
is the core technical contribution of our work.
To further clarify the role of deputy clauses, consider our example above
but with α pervasive. Clause β would be handled as before (stashed-at-2 and
reactivated as pervasive once the solver backtracks below level 2). The core
difference is in the handling of α. In the original example, temporary α must be
marked as a deputy to ensure correctness, by guaranteeing that α is not deleted
and continues to represent β until β is restored. If α is pervasive, it simply
remains so, since pervasive clauses are guaranteed not to be deleted.
We implemented BI in the open-source Island SAT solver, a fork of In-
telSAT [12]. While, in principle, BI supports any number of inprocessing steps
at arbitrary levels, the empirical part of this work focuses on incremental pre-
processing. We exploit the fact that BI can inprocess at the beginning of each
incremental query after assumptions are set and propagated, rather than only at
level 0 as in standard incremental inprocessing (e.g., in CaDiCaL [3, 10]). We im-
plemented several BI-enabled incremental preprocessing strategies (Sect. 4.2.4).
We focus on this setting because incremental preprocessing is the core
performance-critical component in incremental SAT-based Bounded Model
Checking (BMC). BMC plays a central role in hardware validation, where the
goal is to verify the design for as many bounds as possible, and solving each
new bound may mean the difference between discovering a crucial bug or miss-
ing it entirely. However, each unrolling to a new bound typically results in a
substantial increase in the complexity of the SAT instance, making progress to-
ward higher bounds increasingly difficult. Our experiments show that, on BMC
benchmarks from the Hardware Model Checking Competition 2017 [6], with a
one-hour timeout per benchmark and a bound threshold of 100, Island with
standard global-level inprocessing solves 640 more bounds than vanilla Island
without inprocessing, whereas BI enables solving 948 more bounds, i.e., about
1.5× more of the difficult bounds unsolved without inprocessing.

Preliminaries
We begin by recalling standard definitions from propositional logic.
Definition 1 (Variables, literals, clauses, CNF). Let V be a countable set of
Boolean variables. A literal is either a variable x ∈V or its negation ¬x. A
clause is a finite set (disjunction) of pairwise distinct literals. A CNF formula
is a finite set (conjunction) of clauses.
As is standard in the SAT literature, we represent the SAT solver’s search
state by a trail.
Definition 2 (Trail and decision levels). Let I be an input CNF formula. A
trail is a finite sequence of literals annotated with their decision level τ
=
⟨ℓ1@d1, . . . , ℓk@dk⟩, such that:
3

• all variables occurring in τ are pairwise distinct;
• the decision levels satisfy d1 ≤· · · ≤dk;
• for each i with di > 0, we say that ℓi is a decision literal if there is no
j < i with dj = di; otherwise ℓi is an implied literal;
• writing Dec(τ) and Impl(τ) for the sets of decision and implied literals on
τ, and for i ∈{1, . . . , k}, Dec<i(τ) := { ℓj ∈Dec(τ) | j < i }, we require
that for every i with ℓi ∈Impl(τ), I ∧V Dec<i(τ) |= ℓi.
In other words, in a valid trail every implied literal ℓi ∈Impl(τ) is implied by I
together with the decision literals appearing before ℓi.
For the remainder of this paper, we fix the input CNF formula to be I and
the trail to be τ; both are assumed to be given whenever referenced.
As a direct special case of the trail definition at decision level 0, we get that
all global level-0 assignments are implied by the input formula; see Cor. 3.
Corollary 3 (Global literals implied by I). Let τ = ⟨ℓ1@d1, . . . , ℓk@dk⟩be a
trail. For every i ∈{1, . . . , k}, if di = 0, then I |= ℓi.
A trail can be naturally interpreted as a partial assignment consisting of all
its literals. For the example trail τ = ⟨¬a@0, ¬b@1, ¬c@2, d@2⟩, we have
τ |= (a ∨b ∨d) and τ ̸|= (a ∨b ∨¬d).
We next extend our terminology to define satisfaction at a given decision
level.
Definition 4 (Restriction of a trail to level d). For a trail τ = ⟨ℓ1@d1, . . . , ℓk@dk⟩
and d ∈N, define τ ≤d := ⟨ℓi@di | 1 ≤i ≤k, di ≤d⟩, that is, the longest prefix
of τ whose decision levels are at most d.
Definition 5 (Satisfaction at decision level d). Let φ be any Boolean formula
and d ∈N. We say that φ is satisfied at level d if τ ≤d |= φ.
We next introduce convenient notation for implication and equivalence eval-
uated at a given decision level (relative to the fixed input formula I and trail
τ).
Definition 6 (d-implication and d-equivalence). Let φ, ψ be Boolean formulas,
and d ∈N. We say that φ d-implies ψ, written φ →d ψ, if τ ≤d |= (φ →ψ), and
that φ and ψ are d-equivalent, written φ ↔d ψ, if τ ≤d |= (φ ↔ψ).
For example, given the trail τ = ⟨e@1, ¬a@2⟩, we have (a∨b∨c) →2 (d∨b∨c),
since τ ≤2 falsifies a and therefore satisfies (a ∨b ∨c) →(d ∨b ∨c). In contrast,
(a ∨b ∨c) →1 (d ∨b ∨c) does not hold, as in τ ≤1 only e is assigned and the
implication fails.
Cor. 7 (level-d entailment persists under trail extension) is immediate from
Def. 6.
Corollary 7 (Monotonicity of level-d entailment). Let φ, ψ be Boolean formu-
las, and i, j ∈N with j ≥i. For ⋆∈{→, ↔}, if φ ⋆i ψ, then φ ⋆j ψ.

For example, with τ = ⟨e@1, ¬a@2, g@3, ¬h@4⟩, we have (a∨b∨c) →2 (d∨
b∨c) and hence, by Cor. 7, also (a∨b∨c) →3 (d∨b∨c) and (a∨b∨c) →4 (d∨b∨c).
We now introduce subsumption and its level-dependent variants (relative to
the trail τ) and relate them to d-implication, starting with standard subsump-
tion.
Definition 8 (Subsumption). Clause α subsumes clause β, written α ⊆β, if
every literal in α also occurs in β.
Definition 9 (Pruning falsified literals). For a clause α and d ∈N, we define
the restriction of α to level d by α≤d := {ℓ∈α | ¬ℓ/∈τ ≤d}, that is, we remove
from α all literals whose negation appears on the trail up to level d.
For the example trail τ = ⟨e@1, ¬b@2⟩, we have (a ∨b ∨c)≤2 = (a ∨c),
whereas (a ∨b ∨c)≤1 = (a ∨b ∨c).
Definition 10 (d-subsumption). Let α, β be clauses and d ∈N. We say that α
subsumes β at d ( d-subsumes β), written α ⊆d β, if α≤d ⊆β≤d.
For example, under τ = ⟨¬e@1, ¬b@2⟩, we obtain (a ∨b ∨c) ⊆2 (a ∨f ∨c),
but (a ∨b ∨c) ̸⊆1 (a ∨f ∨c) (since b is falsified at level 2 but not at level 1).
It is easy to verify that, as expected, d-subsumption entails d-implication.
Corollary 11 (d-subsumption entails d-implication). For any clauses α, β and
any d ∈N, if α ⊆d β, then α →d β.
We will need the earliest level d at which a subsumption relation holds, so
that a subsumed clause can be safely replaced by the subsuming clause until
backtracking below d.
Definition 12 (min-d-subsumption). Let α, β be clauses and d ∈N. We say
that α minimally subsumes β at level d (α min-d-subsumes β), written α ⊆min(d)
β, if:
1. α ⊆d β holds, and
2. for every decision level d′ strictly smaller than d, α ⊆d′ β does not hold.
Thus d is the minimal decision level at which α d-subsumes β.
For example, with τ = ⟨e@1, ¬a@2, g@3, ¬h@4⟩, we have (a ∨b ∨c) →2 (d ∨
b∨c) and hence, by Cor. 7, also (a∨b∨c) →3 (d∨b∨c) and (a∨b∨c) →4 (d∨b∨c).
However, (a ∨b ∨c) ̸→1 (d ∨b ∨c), so the implication at level 2 is minimal, i.e.,
(a ∨b ∨c) ⊆min(2) (d ∨b ∨c).

Non-Incremental Backtrackable Inprocessing
with Subsumption
This section introduces our framework for backtrackable inprocessing and its
main algorithms in the following simplified setting: non-incremental SAT solv-
ing with subsumption as the only inprocessing technique. This restricted setting
already captures the main ideas, terminology, invariants, and algorithms. Sec-
tion 4 then lifts these restrictions.
We design our Backtrackable Inprocessing (BI) module to be integrated as
a component of a SAT solver with a clear API to the main search engine. The
search engine interacts with BI via the following two API functions:

3.1
Clause Classification and Management
Our approach relies on the extended clause classification and management shown
in Table 1.
Table 1a demonstrates that, before the initial invocation of Inprocess (at
whatever decision level), the solver maintains two standard clause types: the
active-pervasive clauses D0, which are logically equivalent to the original input
clauses I up to level-0 simplifications, and the temporary (learnt) clauses D∞,
each of which is implied by D0 at level 0.
Table 1b and Table 1c present the clause classification from the first invoca-
tion of Inprocess onward, generalizing and extending this standard partition.
Specifically, in addition to active-pervasive and temporary clauses, we track
inactive stashed clauses and active deputy clauses.
Before restoration, each
stashed clause is always represented by some active deputy clause, although
this deputy may change over time.
Each active clause group Dk ⊆A and each stashed clause group Sq
k ⊆S is
indexed by a level k. Clauses in Dk remain active and must not be removed until
the solver backtracks below k. Once the solver backtracks below k, this restric-
tion is lifted, and clauses in Dk may either be deleted or treated as temporary
clauses, that is, moved to D∞. Since the solver is always below ∞, clauses in
D∞are, by convention, always eligible for deletion.
Clauses in a stashed group Sq
k remain inactive until the solver backtracks
below their associated level k. When this occurs, they are restored into Dq,
where we refer to q < k as their restoration level.

3.2.2
Low-Level Invariants
If Invs. 1 and 2 capture the high-level correctness of our framework, then Invs. 3
and 4 provide the corresponding low-level guarantees.
Intuitively, Inv. 3 ensures that every stashed clause is represented until its
restoration.
Unless it is trivially implied (trivial-representation), it is either
represented by an active deputy (deputy-representation) or by another stashed
clause that will later be restored as such a deputy (restored-representation). In
the restored-representation case, at any concrete point the stashed clause is still
represented by an active deputy, but transitively: it is represented via a chain
of stashed clauses, the last of which is represented by an active deputy.
Inv. 4 ensures that all clauses in the system are implied by the pervasive
clauses.
Lemma 13 shows that Invs. 3 and 4 imply Inv. 2 throughout the execution of
our algorithm. The proofs of this and all other lemmas appear in Appendix A.
Lemma 13 (Correctness from representation and closure). Assume Invs. 3
and 4 hold. Then Inv. 2 holds as well, i.e., N ↔n P.

3.2.3
Why the Search Engine Maintains All Invariants
We argue that a standard SAT search engine naturally maintains our invariants,
both prior to the first Inprocess call and between subsequent invocations of
Inprocess and Backtrack. Sect. 3.3 constructively shows that our BI module
maintains them as well.
We assume that the search engine itself does not apply inprocessing (which is
instead handled via interaction with our BI module), and that it performs only
standard level-0 simplifications implied by the input clauses I, such as deleting
globally satisfied clauses and removing globally falsified literals. Throughout
this section, all logical equivalences and implications are understood modulo
these level-0 simplifications, as justified by Cor. 3.
Let us verify that our invariants (skipping Inv. 2 by Lemma 13) hold at
the time of the first Inprocess call by the search engine. Inv. 1 holds since,
by construction, D0 comprises the input clauses I up to the standard level-
0 simplifications.
Inv. 3 is vacuous, because no stashed clauses exist before
inprocessing. Inv. 4 holds since P comprises D0, and A∪S comprises D0 ∪D∞,
where every temporary clause in D∞is implied by D0 by construction.
We now argue that the search engine maintains all invariants in each phase
between two BI invocations (Inprocess or Backtrack). Let n be the decision
level of the latest BI call. Crucially, during the phase, the level never drops
below n, since Backtrack is invoked whenever backtracking below n occurs.
At the beginning of the phase, Invs.
1 and 2 imply that I, P, and N are
equivalent at level n, while D∞is implied by these groups by Inv. 4.
The
search engine operates only on the active clauses A = N ∪D∞, which, by
construction, remain equivalent to I at level 0 until the end of the phase. Hence
Invariants 1 and 4 continue to hold throughout the phase. As for Inv. 3, the
stashed clause sets do not change, being inactive, while deputy clauses may
either remain unchanged or be simplified at level 0 (the latter only if the solver
allows level-0 simplifications at arbitrary levels). Such simplifications preserve
restored- and trivial-representation. Deputy-representation is preserved, except
when a representing deputy becomes satisfied at level 0 and is deleted, in which
case any stashed clause it represented falls under trivial-representation. Inv. 2
holds by Lemma 13.

4.1
Incremental Preprocessing with SatELite and Related
Work
We start with standard definitions for resolution and selfsumption (self-subsuming
resolution).
Definition 20 (Resolution). Let α, β be clauses and x a variable. They are
resolvable on x if x ∈α and ¬x ∈β. In this case the resolvent is α ⊗x β :=
(α \ {x}) ∪(β \ {¬x}), and the resolution rule derives α ⊗x β from α and β.
Definition 21 (Selfsumption, Strengthening). Let α, β be clauses resolvable on
x. We say that α selfsumes β on x if α⊗xβ ⊆β. Equivalently, writing α = C∨x
and β = D ∨¬x, this holds iff C ⊆D. In this case, β may be strengthened to
D = α ⊗x β.
For example, if α = (a ∨x) and β = (a ∨b ∨¬x), then the resolvent on x is
α ⊗x β = (a ∨b), which is a subset of β. Hence α selfsumes β on x, and β can
be strengthened to (a ∨b).
Finally, we use the term sumption to refer to subsumption and selfsumption
together, and the verb to sume to indicate that a clause either subsumes or
selfsumes another clause.

Other Related Work
We reuse the high-level structure of incremental SatELite in our BI framework.
Supporting gate extraction [8], as well as extending BI beyond sumption and
variable elimination (in the spirit of [10], which achieves this for global level-0
inprocessing), is left for future work. We previously proposed incremental pre-
processing after propagating assumptions in [16]. However, the present work is
both significantly more general—our framework supports inprocessing at any
decision level and at any point during solving, rather than only preprocessing at
the beginning of each incremental query—and practically more useful, since [16]
is closed-source (whereas our implementation is open-source) and relies on main-
taining resolution proofs in memory, an infrastructure not supported by modern
SAT solvers.

Throughout the rest of the paper, whenever we refer to level-0 notions (satis-
faction, implication, equivalence, or subsumption with respect to τ ≤0), we call
them global. In contrast, notions at levels d > 0 (i.e., with respect to τ ≤d for
some d > 0) are called conditional, since they depend on the current trail.
To integrate our subsumption-only BI framework from Section 3 into the
SAT solver, it is sufficient to:
1. Apply Inprocess (Alg. 1) for handling conditional subsumption directly
as the implementation of the sumption step in incremental SatELite (line 6
in Alg. 3), retaining the original processing order from smallest to largest
clauses via the size-based heap, but ignoring conditionally falsified literals
when computing clause sizes.
2. Stay synchronized with respect to backtracking: let m be the decision
level at the end of the latest Inprocess or Backtrack (Alg. 2) invocation.
Then Backtrack must be invoked whenever the solver backtracks below
level m.
Below, Sect. 4.2.1 defines conditional selfsumption and explains how to ex-
tend the above BI integration in incremental SatELite to support it, while
Sects. 4.2.2 and 4.2.3 show how to handle conditionally satisfied clauses and
BVE, respectively, within our framework. Finally, Sect. 4.2.4 discusses the in-
tegration of incremental SAT solving (which comes for free in our framework)
and introduces several strategies for applying BI for incremental preprocessing.

4.2.2
Handling Conditionally Satisfied Clauses
Unlike in global inprocessing, backtrackable inprocessing may encounter clauses
that are conditionally satisfied under the current trail and thus irrelevant at the
current decision level. To handle such clauses, we first define minimal satisfac-
tion (w.r.t. the implicit trail τ).
Definition 24 (Minimal satisfaction). Let φ be a Boolean formula. We say
that φ is minimally satisfied at level d under τ if:
1. φ is satisfied at level d under τ, i.e., τ ≤d |= φ, and
2. for every decision level d′ with 0 ≤d′ < d, we have τ ≤d′ ̸|= φ.
In this case, d is the minimal decision level at which φ is satisfied under τ.
We handle conditionally satisfied clauses as follows. During the inprocessor’s
initialization (line 1 of Alg. 3), we do not insert them into the main data struc-
tures (the occurrence lists occ and the clause heap H).
Instead, taking ad-
vantage of our clause classification, whenever a clause β ∈Di is conditionally
satisfied under the current trail, we compute its minimal satisfaction level d (in
the sense of Def. 24), remove β from the active set Di, and stash it in Si
d to
be restored once the solver has backtracked below d, by which time β will no
longer be satisfied. Note that Inv. 3 is maintained for any such clause due to
trivial-representation.
4.2.3
Handling Bounded Variable Elimination (BVE)
We now turn to Bounded Variable Elimination (BVE). Unlike for sumption,
there is no “conditional” variable elimination, since the variable elimination
15


## Page 16


algorithm itself does not depend on the trail. However, the presence of condi-
tionally satisfied clauses is promising for bounded variable elimination efficiency-
wise: skipping such clauses as described in Sect. 4.2.2 increases the chances of
eliminating more variables while adhering to the clause-count bound. For cor-
rectness, when those clauses are later restored, the relevant eliminated variables
must be reintroduced or reeliminated, but this is handled automatically as laid
out below.
Our BI integration preserves the standard BVE scheme of incremental SatEL-
ite, but, as explained below, it introduces six easy-to-implement, BI-specific
changes, mostly related to assigning and managing ranks of original clauses
and resolvents. Below, these changes are numbered as (Ci) at the end of the
sentences introducing them.
In BI, new clauses containing eliminated variables may arise not only from
the user (as in standard incremental SAT), but also from restoring stashed
clauses.
Fortunately, incremental SatELite (Alg. 3) already handles clauses
with eliminated variables regardless of their origin. We just need to ensure that
the search algorithm invokes inprocessing whenever even one clause with an
eliminated variable is present in the solver, whether it is a user clause added
during a new incremental query or a restored stashed clause (C1).
The remaining main question for handling BVE in BI is how to treat deputy
clauses during elimination and backtracking. Recall that a deputy of rank i (that
is, a deputy in Di) is required only as long as the solver does not backtrack
below i, at which point all clauses in Di are deleted by Backtrack (Alg. 2).
Therefore, conceptually, any resolvents produced using such deputies become
obsolete as soon as the solver backtracks below the highest rank of their parents.
To reflect this, we assign each resolvent the maximum rank of its parent clauses
as follows: when, while eliminating a variable v, we resolve α ∈Di with β ∈Dj
on v, producing the resolvent γ = α ⊗v β, we assign γ to Dk, where k :=
max(i, j) (C2). Furthermore, we keep the original rank of every clause in F +(v)
and F −(v) for any eliminated variable v (C3). When the solver backtracks
below level i, all clauses of rank i are deleted from F +(v) and F −(v) for every
eliminated variable v (C4). When a variable v is reintroduced, the remaining
clauses in F +(v) and F −(v) keep their rank when activated (C5). Finally, as
BI is designed to solve the problem under the trail, BVE attempts to eliminate
only unassigned variables, ignoring those already assigned on the trail (C6).
Furthermore, following prior work on incremental inprocessing [15, 10], we
restrict elimination to variables occurring in newly added clauses; in BI, we
further consider variables appearing in clauses that contain literals assigned on
the current trail, thereby avoiding repeated elimination attempts for variables
whose relevant context has not changed.
4.2.4
Incrementality and BI Strategies for Incremental Preprocessing
BI inherently accommodates clause additions and changing assumptions, so our
framework requires no modifications to support incremental SAT solving and
can be applied as is.
Although BI supports any number of inprocessing steps at arbitrary levels,
we focus on studying the effects of incremental preprocessing after the assump-
tions are propagated—a minimal step that already distinguishes BI from stand-
ard global incremental inprocessing, both in terms of algorithmic capabilities
16


## Page 17


and in its empirical impact on Bounded Model Checking (BMC). Below we
present several strategies for incremental preprocessing.
We call the first incremental preprocessing strategy enabled by BI the
Assumption-Level (AL) strategy. It applies inprocessing immediately after
propagating the assumptions for every incremental SAT query. As a baseline for
comparison, we also implement a global-level BASE strategy that backtracks
to 0 and preprocesses there with the assumptions frozen (i.e., not allowed to be
eliminated) at the start of every incremental query (as in CaDiCaL [3]).
Initial experiments on BMC instances showed that, although the BI-enabled
AL is often more efficient, there are benchmarks where the standard global-level
BASE wins. Further analysis revealed that the reason lies in how variables of
literals implied by assumptions, and clauses that become conditionally satisfied
under assumptions, are treated.
AL ignores assigned variables and stashes conditionally satisfied clauses be-
fore inprocessing, which can be beneficial because less data is processed and
BVE can eliminate more variables. However, there is a trade-off. First, elim-
inating variables that are about to be implied by assumptions can be helpful,
and this is carried out by BASE but not AL. Second, AL misses selfsumptions
by conditionally satisfied clauses that could remove falsified literals from other
clauses, as illustrated below. Let α = (a ∨b ∨c) and β = (¬a ∨b ∨c) be clauses
handled by AL under the trail τ = ⟨a@1⟩. Since α is stashed by BI as satisfied,
AL misses the opportunity to strengthen β to (b∨c). While this does not affect
reasoning under the current trail, any new temporary clauses that depend on β
(including clauses selfsumed by β during inprocessing and learnt clauses during
search) must carry ¬a, inflating the clause database and causing cache misses.
In contrast, BASE would have strengthened β.
Hence, we introduce a combined COMB strategy: immediately after a new
SAT query, we first run inprocessing at level 0 (BASE) and then again after
assigning and propagating assumptions (AL). This layered approach, despite
its own overhead, can sometimes simplify the formula more effectively by first
applying global and then conditional simplifications.
Finally, we observed a clear pattern in when AL, BASE, and COMB work
best. Let M be a measure of how intensively BI is used while AL is running. By
default, we define M = number of conditional selfsumptions
number of inprocessing invocations (a slightly less effective
variant is stashed clause restorations
number of conflicts
). Empirically, on instances with a small M,
AL tends to be most effective (it is cheap while still having noticeable impact);
for medium values of M, BASE is preferable (the additional overhead of AL is
not justified); and when M is large, COMB works best (it is then worthwhile
to invest more in inprocessing, given its strong overall effect).
Hence, we designed the following dynamic DYN strategy. We start with
AL and, after 10 000 conflicts, permanently select a strategy based on the value
of M: if M < 200, we stay with AL; if 200 ≤M < 3000, we switch to BASE;
and if M ≥3000, we switch to COMB.

Conclusion
We introduced Backtrackable Inprocessing (BI), a framework that allows in-
processing under the current trail at any level, at any point in the search,
while preserving correctness under backtracking (focusing on subsumption, self-
subsuming resolution, and BVE). We integrated BI into the Island solver and
evaluated several strategies for incremental preprocessing on BMC benchmarks
from HWMCC’17, obtained by unrolling each benchmark up to bound 100 and
using a one-hour timeout.
Our best BI-enhanced strategy outperforms the
baseline strategy, solving ∼1.5× more bounds among those unsolved without
inprocessing.
We have so far demonstrated BI’s benefit in incremental preprocessing; how-
ever, BI enables a much broader design space. Inprocessing can now be integ-
rated into the search in many ways, paving the way for new strategies and
significantly more efficient SAT solving, both non-incremental and incremental.
19


## Page 20


Beyond SAT solving, BI is also promising for enabling inprocessing in
state-of-the-art algorithms for model counting [18] and enumeration [19], which
rely on systematic search without restarts and have therefore so far been unable
to exploit inprocessing.

Lemma 13 (Correctness from representation and closure). Assume Invs. 3
and 4 hold. Then Inv. 2 holds as well, i.e., N ↔n P.
Proof. We prove both directions.

We perform downward induction on i.
Base case (i = n): By definition of n (the decision level where the latest
BI invocation completed), Sn is the highest stashed group, so the restored-
representation case (representation via some γ ∈Sr
q with q > n) cannot occur.
By Inv. 3, each α ∈S0
n is either trivially satisfied at level n or represented by
some deputy in D. In both cases, D0 ∪D →n α follows.
Inductive step (i < n): Let α ∈S0
i . By Inv. 3, α satisfies one of the following:
• ⊤→i α, whence D0 ∪D →n α by Cor. 7;
• α is i-implied by some deputy in D, whence D0 ∪D →n α by Cor. 7;
• α is implied at level i by some γ ∈Sr

By the induction

By

Second direction: P →n (N ≡D0 ∪D) follows from Inv. 4, since each clause in

Lemma 15 (Promotion preserves invariants). Let γ ∈Di be an active clause,
and suppose all invariants in Fig. 1 hold. If we promote γ to some group Dj
with j < i (by moving it), then all invariants continue to hold.
Proof. Invs. 1 and 4 depend only on the contents of the pervasive (P), active-

represented by γ before the promotion is still represented by γ afterwards, since
γ remains active and its rank only decreases. The representation of all other
stashed clauses is unaffected.
Finally, Inv. 2 follows from Invs. 3 and 4 via
