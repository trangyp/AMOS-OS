Abstract—Ground robot navigation in complex 3D environ-
ments is often hindered by geometric ambiguity, where non-
traversable structures such as furniture share local geometric
properties with navigable ground. Furthermore, the computa-
tional cost of searching massive voxel spaces remains a significant
challenge. To address these issues, we present a surface extraction
framework that constructs a reduced state space of physically
reachable standing positions by enforcing ground support, over-
head clearance, and seed-based connectivity constraints. Eval-
uation across five Matterport3D indoor scenes and three PCT
benchmark scenes demonstrates over 80% state space reduction
and sub-millisecond A* search on the Matterport3D scenes, with
100% planning success across all 300 tested queries.
Index Terms—3D navigation, surface extraction, occupancy
map, ground robot, path planning, traversability
I. INTRODUCTION
Deploying ground robots in unstructured 3D environments
requires robust traversability identification. While modern
reconstruction (LiDAR SLAM, RGB-D fusion) provides de-
tailed maps, it introduces geometric ambiguity: non-traversable
structures such as furniture, ceiling cavities, and artifacts
share local geometric properties (planarity, low slope) with
navigable ground, making them indistinguishable to geometry-
only planners.
Existing methods primarily rely on local geometric features:
3D2M [1] applies slope and step-height filtering on point
cloud neighborhoods; PCT [2] horizontally slices clouds into
traversable layers. Neither can distinguish a tabletop from a
floor or a ceiling cavity from a corridor.
We resolve this ambiguity with a single additional con-
straint: physical reachability from the robot’s current position.
We extract the connected component of geometrically valid
candidates reachable from a known seed via bounded-height
steps—topologically filtering all disconnected surfaces (fur-
niture, artifacts, inter-floor cavities) regardless of geometry.
Restricting the state space to this reachable surface (<20% of
the full grid in furniture-rich indoor scenes) yields substantial
computational savings.
Building on this observation, we develop a complete frame-
work from surface extraction to clearance-aware planning,
with the following contributions:
1) A surface extraction algorithm that constructs a seed-
reachable traversable state space via topological filtering,
eliminating geometrically ambiguous regions without
semantic labels, under the static-map assumption.
2) Systematic evaluation across eight scenes from two
public benchmarks (Matterport3D and PCT [2]), demon-
strating that seed-based connectivity alone achieves over
80% state space reduction in furniture-rich indoor envi-
ronments and enables sub-millisecond planning without
per-scene parameter tuning.
Section IV-B validates state-space reduction after extraction,
Section IV-C evaluates planning success and search latency
on Matterport3D, and Section IV-D reports cross-scenario
comparisons on PCT benchmarks.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Geometric Ambiguity in Traversability Estimation
Geometry-only planners classify traversability using local
features such as slopes, step-heights, and surface normals.
The 3D2M-planner [1] identifies ground layers via local slope
and step-height filtering (RANSAC-based Valid Ground Filter)
applied to point cloud neighborhoods; ugv nav4d [3] evaluates
terrain traversability on multi-level surface (MLS) maps [4].
These methods produce false positives on furniture surfaces
and floating artifacts that locally mimic floor geometry [5], [6].
Point cloud tomography [2] horizontally slices point clouds
into multi-level 2.5D traversable layers, achieving highly ef-
ficient planning in large-scale outdoor structures with well-
separated vertical layers. When applied to cluttered indoor
scenes, however, horizontal slicing can include furniture and
artifacts at valid floor elevations, since it does not enforce
physical reachability from a known position.
Semantic traversability methods (e.g., learning-based terrain
classifiers) can in principle distinguish floors from furniture,
but require dense labeled training data and high-fidelity per-
ception pipelines, limiting deployment in previously unseen

B. Computational Efficiency in 3D Navigation
Conventional volumetric approaches [1], [7], [8] process
the complete 3D grid at a cost fixed by the grid size V =
NxNyNz. Because these methods lack a connectivity-aware
filtering mechanism, they exhaustively evaluate the entire
space, irrespective of how much of the volume is actually
reachable. By restricting planning to the extracted surface S
(|S| ≪V ), our approach reduces search cost as a direct
consequence of the state space reduction.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. Problem Formulation
Consider an environment represented as a 3D occupancy
grid O : Z3 →{0, 1} at resolution r, typically reconstructed
from LiDAR SLAM, RGB-D fusion, or offline methods such
as 3DGS [9]. The grid contains both traversable structures
(floors, stairs) and non-traversable ones (furniture, ceilings)
that share identical local geometric properties, making geom-
etry alone insufficient for traversability identification.
The objective is to extract S ⊂Z3—the set of free voxels
where a robot can physically stand—such that every element
of S is reachable from a known seed position, ensuring
traversability without post-hoc validation. In practice, the seed
is obtained by discretizing the robot pose onto the occupancy
grid and selecting the nearest valid candidate voxel in C.
Prior to surface extraction, obstacle inflation expands oc-
cupied voxels by the robot’s collision radius Rinf in the XY
plane (±1 voxel vertically), ensuring collision safety without
blocking valid passages under low overhangs.
B. Surface Extraction
We propose a surface extraction algorithm that reliably iden-
tifies traversable positions in cluttered indoor environments.
Our approach applies geometric preconditions followed by a
topological reachability constraint that resolves cases where
geometry alone is insufficient.
1) Geometric Constraints: Using the occupancy grid O de-
fined in Section III-A, a voxel (x, y, z) is a surface candidate
if it satisfies two geometric conditions. Let K = ⌈Hclear/r⌉
denote the clearance height in voxels.
Condition 1 (Ground Support): The voxel must be free
and rest on a solid surface directly below:
O(x, y, z) = 0
∧
O(x, y, z −1) = 1
(1)
This filters wall surfaces, suspended objects (e.g., hanging
lamps), and mid-air reconstruction artifacts.
Condition 2 (Overhead Clearance): A vertical column
of K voxels above must be free to accommodate the robot’s
height:
∀j ∈{1, . . . , K} : O(x, y, z + j) = 0
(2)
This filters non-traversable spaces under low furniture. Let
C denote the candidate set of all voxels satisfying both
conditions.
2) Topological Constraint: Seed-Based Connectivity: Ge-
ometric constraints alone cannot filter furniture surfaces—a
tabletop satisfies both ground support and overhead clearance
and thus appears in C. The critical additional constraint is
physical reachability.
Let k = ⌊Tconn/r⌋denote the maximum step height in
voxels, where Tconn is the traversable step threshold (e.g.,
0.3 m). Two candidates c1, c2 ∈C are Z-connected neighbors
(c1 ∼Z c2) if
∥∆cxy∥1 = 1 ∧|∆z| ≤k
(3)
where ∆cxy and ∆z denote horizontal and vertical displace-
ments, respectively. The traversable surface S is the connected
component of C containing the seed s0 under ∼Z, constructed
via BFS.
This connectivity constraint isolates furniture (floor-to-
tabletop discontinuity > k), ceiling cavities (no reachable path
from floor seeds), and isolated reconstruction artifacts. The
seed s0 is naturally the robot’s current pose; multi-seed BFS
supports incremental exploration.
3) Multi-Level Support: The BFS naturally handles multi-
story structures: each stair step satisfies |∆z| ≤k, enabling
propagation across floors.
A key property is the multi-valued vertical mapping: a single
horizontal coordinate (x, y) may correspond to multiple valid
heights,
Sx,y = {z | (x, y, z) ∈S}
(4)
where |Sx,y| > 1 in multi-story buildings and overpass
structures—configurations that 2.5D elevation maps [10] can-
not represent. This property requires the path planner to oper-
ate on full 3D states (x, y, z) ∈S rather than 2D projections,
ensuring floors at different heights are never conflated.

mechanisms across various indoor scenarios, highlighting the
removal of geometrically ambiguous structures.

The speedup over volumetric search is a direct consequence
of state space reduction (|S| ≪V ), not algorithmic novelty.

within S, precomputed by a two-pass scan [11] at O(|S|) cost.
The edge cost is:
c(s, s′) = r∥s′−s∥2 + |∆z| · r · ωz +

where ωz ∈{ω↑, ω↓, 0} penalizes height changes asymmetri-

h(s) = r∥s −sgoal∥2 + r|∆zg|ω↓

which is admissible since it underestimates all cost terms.
The planner outputs a discrete path directly from weighted

single-query comparison separately reports map build time Tp,
candidate evaluation time Te, search time Ts, and total pipeline
time Tall = Tp + Te + Ts.
IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Experimental Setup
We evaluate on two complementary benchmark suites:
Matterport3D scenes (S1–S5). Five indoor scenes from
the Matterport3D dataset [12]. These span multi-story floors,
dense furniture, reconstruction artifacts, and multi-valued ver-
tical geometry. Surface extraction experiments and the Mat-
terport3D planning evaluation use this suite exclusively.
PCT benchmark scenes (P1–P3). Three publicly available
3D point cloud scenes released with the PCT planner [2]
and 3D2M-planner [1]: (P1) Building—a multi-floor indoor
structure with stairs and slopes, (22×20×16) m; (P2) Plaza—an
outdoor urban scenario with various structures, (56×56×5) m;
(P3) Spiral—a large-scale structure with continuous spiral
ramp, (80×40×23) m. P1 and P2 enable direct comparison
against PCT [2] and 3D2M [1] on shared data and query
points; P3 is used for state space analysis only.
Implementation. All experiments were run on an AMD
Ryzen 7 9800X3D CPU. Extraction parameters: Tconn =0.3 m,
Hclear = 1.6 m, Rinf = 0.3 m. For Matterport3D scenes (S1–
S5), r = 0.2 m. For PCT benchmark scenes, we adopt the
resolutions reported in [2] to ensure a fair comparison: r =
0.1 m for P1 (Building), P2 (Plaza), and P3 (Spiral). Planning
parameters: ϵ=1 (optimal A*), ω↑=2.0, ω↓=1.0, ωobs =0.5.
Time normalization. PCT [2] reports timing on an Intel i9-
12900KF. We define the hardware ratio α=3338/2586≈1.29
from Geekbench 6 single-core scores [13], [14]; multiplying
our CPU timings by α gives an approximate equivalent i9-
12900KF performance. This normalization is used as an in-
dicative CPU-side correction only, while speed-independent
metrics (path length, success rate) are compared directly.
Baseline implementations. For the Matterport3D evalua-
tion, we run the official 3D2M codebase [1] as the baseline.
For the PCT benchmark comparisons, PCT and 3D2M results
are taken from the published values reported in [2].

V. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
A. Discussion
Seed-based topological filtering achieves over 80% state
space reduction on furniture-rich indoor scenes without se-
mantic labels or scene-specific training. By construction, every
state in S is reachable from the seed, guaranteeing that all
path queries within S are solvable—the 100% success rate
in Tables III and V is therefore a structural property of the
extracted surface, not an empirical coincidence. This guarantee
comes at the cost of completeness: regions separated from the
seed by physical barriers (e.g., closed doors) are excluded,
producing false negatives that multi-seed initialization could
mitigate. The degree of state space reduction is environment-
dependent, ranging from 43.8–67.4% on the open-structure
PCT scenes to 81.3–89.5% on the furniture-rich Matterport3D
scenes, as the filtering benefit scales with the volume of
geometrically valid but physically unreachable structures.
Parameters. All four parameters (r, Tconn, Hclear, Rinf)
transferred without per-scene tuning across all eight scenes;
Tconn should exceed the tallest expected stair riser (≈0.2–
0.3 m).
Limitations. The framework assumes a static occupancy
map; incremental update mechanisms for dynamic environ-
ments remain future work. Ground support filtering assumes
dense surface reconstruction and may yield incomplete sur-
faces on sparse outdoor LiDAR data. The method also requires
correct seed initialization; although nearest-valid-candidate se-
lection proved robust across all tested scenes, pose discretiza-
tion errors could in principle place the seed in an unintended
connected component.
B. Conclusion
Topological reachability proves an effective proxy for phys-
ical traversability, resolving geometric ambiguities across the
eight indoor and semi-outdoor scenes evaluated. The resulting
state space reduction (80%+ on furniture-rich indoor scenes)
enables sub-millisecond A* search on the Matterport3D scenes
without per-scene parameter tuning. Future work targets dy-
namic environments via incremental map updates, as well as
validation on real-world robotic platforms.
