Abstract
Alignment faking (AF) refers to a model strategically complying with a train-
ing objective to avoid behavioural modification while preserving its deployment
preferences. Understanding when and why AF arises matters as models grow
better at distinguishing training from deployment. Prior work finds AF fragile,
prompt-sensitive, and model-dependent, leaving its underlying drivers unclear. We
study AF in a controlled, minimal setup that isolates its core components, and
observe it across a wider range of models than previously reported, including
small-scale models. We identify three separable drivers — values, goal guarding,
and sycophancy — and show via targeted prompt ablations and activation steering
that each independently modulates AF behaviour. Our results indicate AF is more
widespread than previously reported and that its occurrence is predictable from
situational cues and measurable model tendencies such as baseline sycophancy and
stated values. The decomposition suggests concrete directions for detecting and
mitigating AF in future models.
1
Introduction
Alignment faking poses a fundamental challenge to AI safety. A model that learns to strategically
comply with its training objective, while preserving misaligned preferences for deployment, can score
highly on training objectives, then behave entirely differently once deployed. This makes it potentially
undetectable by any safety training pipeline that relies on behavioural evaluation, including RLHF
and constitutional AI. The problem compounds with scale: more capable models are better placed
to reason about their own training [Laine et al., 2024], and better able to conceal their deliberation
[Greenblatt, 2026]. If alignment faking becomes sufficiently sophisticated, it may become impossible
to detect at all.
Despite its importance, we have a poor understanding of why models Alignment Fake. Greenblatt
et al. [2024] provided the first empirical demonstration of the behaviour in Claude 3 Opus, but the
phenomenon was not found reliably in other models, even from the same model family. Sheshadri
et al. [2025] subsequently showed the behaviour to be fragile and highly sensitive to prompt framing,
making systematic study difficult. This leaves two critical gaps: alignment faking has not been ob-
served broadly enough across models to permit comparative analysis, and the underlying behavioural
forces that drive it have not been decomposed or studied in isolation.
We address both gaps by treating alignment faking as a semi-rational decision shaped by competing
pressures, and decomposing the compliance decision into three behavioural drivers. Two push toward
compliance: Developer sycophancy (DS), and Instrumental Goal Guarding (IGG); and one pushes
toward non-compliance: Values (V). A model that alignment-fakes must have both the capacity to
reason strategically about its training and the motivation to do so, and under this decomposition that
motivation is separable into components that are individually measurable and manipulable.

Background and Related Works
2.1
Deceptive Alignment
Earlier work suggests that sufficiently capable models trained via reinforcement learning might
develop instrumental sub-goals that lead them to behave compliantly during training while pursuing
misaligned objectives once deployed [Hubinger et al., 2021]. A model with long-horizon goals may
find it rational to deceive its overseers [Carlsmith, 2023].
The risk compounds with capability. As models become more situationally aware [Laine et al., 2024,
Chaudhary et al., 2025], they may be able to identify contexts where alignment faking can alter future
behavior. And as models become more capable of coherent goal-directed reasoning [Meinke et al.,
2025], they may be able to identify situations where alignment faking may benefit specific goals.
These prerequisites for alignment faking, recognizing the training context, modeling its consequences,
and acting strategically across it, become more readily satisfied.
2.2
Alignment Faking in the Wild
Two strands of prior work establish the preconditions for alignment faking. Hubinger et al. [2024]
show that deceptive behaviours instilled during training persist through standard safety pipelines,
and Meinke et al. [2025] show that frontier models can scheme in-context when given goals that
conflict with their developers. Greenblatt et al. [2024] provided the first empirical demonstration of
this behaviour in Claude 3 Opus. They placed the model in scenarios where it was told it was being
retrained via RLHF on a new objective that conflicted with its existing values — in one scenario, to
be helpful-only (complying with all requests, including harmful ones), and in another, to dismiss
animal welfare concerns. The model was further informed that only responses to free-tier users would
be used for training, while paid-tier interactions would not. This setup produced a compliance gap:
the model complied with objectionable requests more often when it believed it was being monitored
for training than when it believed it was not, suggesting it was strategically faking alignment to

2.3
Framing the Decision to Alignment Fake
Sheshadri et al. [2025] broadened the empirical scope considerably, evaluating approximately 25
contemporary models under similar conditions. They found that only a small subset exhibited a
non-negligible compliance gap under standard prompting, while most models showed near-zero gaps.
They investigate the effects of Rater Sycophancy (what we call Developer sycophancy), Instrumental
Goal Guarding, Terminal Goal Guarding, and the coherence/variation in the prompt. They find that
across most models, prompt framing is the only constant effect. They suggest that models’ (other than
Claude 3 Opus) decision to Alignment Fake is heuristic, based on a variety of obedience heuristics
or context-conditioned expectations, rather than consequentialist reasoning. Other works argue that
Alignment Faking would be a divide between deontological and consequentialist framings of morality
[Koorndijk, 2025].
We argue that the heuristic account is partly an artifact of the experimental setup. Using a more
informative prompt that makes the prerequisites for alignment faking explicit, we are able to isolate
drivers that do operate in a wider range of models than previously thought, and in a more predictable
way than the heuristic account suggests.
3
A Rational Perspective on the Compliance Gap
We measure model behaviour through the compliance gap: the difference between a model’s com-
pliance rate when observed and when not observed. In the animal-welfare scenario we adopt from
Greenblatt et al. [2024], the model must either comply with the training objective (dismiss animal
welfare) or act on its values (support animal welfare). Responses from free-tier users (T = 1) are
used for RLHF training, so non-compliant responses trigger modification; responses from paid-tier
users (T = 0) are never seen, stored, or used. Letting C be the binary random variable denoting
compliance, we operationalise the differential as
∆= P(comply | free) −P(comply | paid) = P(C | T = 1) −P(C | T = 0),
(1)
and say that alignment faking occurs when ∆> 0 2. The compliance gap is one of the dominant
behavioural metrics in the literature [Greenblatt et al., 2024, Koorndijk, 2025, Sheshadri et al., 2025];
we detail our reasons to prefer the compliance gap over the CoT based alignment faking reasoning
metric in Appendix H.
We treat capable models as agents trading off competing pressures when responding; not via literal
utility computation, but because RLHF shapes outputs through optimization and capable models
reason goal-directedly in their chain-of-thought [Greenblatt et al., 2024, Lindsey, 2026]. The framing
parallels revealed preference theory: behaviour can be well-described as utility-maximizing without
introspection on utilities. A growing literature documents both broad consistency of LLM behaviour
with utility maximization and systematic deviations from it [Ross et al., 2024, Yamin et al., 2026].
Closest to ours, Phelps and Ranson [2023] cast LLM safety as a principal–agent problem with
developers, operators, and users as competing principals. Other formalisms could capture the same
conflict (explicit value hierarchies, deontic constraints, or multi-objective preference structures,
among others), but the implicit-utility framing naturally suggests a clean additive decomposition into
the competing forces below and a natural intervention handle for each. We make no attempt to fit
a utility function or test revealed-preference axioms directly; the framing’s role here is qualitative,
generating directional predictions about how the compliance gap should respond when each force is
independently modulated.

Under this perspective, we identify three forces that shape the compliance decision. Two push toward
compliance: Developer sycophancy (DS), deference to the developer’s stated policy of dismissing
animal welfare; and Instrumental Goal Guarding (IGG), strategic compliance in the free tier to
avoid RLHF modification that would erode the model’s animal-welfare concern in future deployments.
One pushes toward non-compliance: Values (V), the model’s animal-welfare concern expressed in the
current interaction. While we instantiate this in the animal-welfare scenario, the same decomposition
applies to any value–scenario pair in which a model’s internalized value conflicts with a training
objective.
This framing yields three directional predictions, each tied to an experiment in Section 4.2. (P1)
Strengthening V should widen ∆: positive animal-welfare steering increases the weight of values in
both tiers, but the free tier additionally engages IGG, so values reinforces IGG in the free tier while
only values suppresses paid-tier compliance. (P2) Increasing IGG should widen ∆: IGG operates
only in the free tier, so any manipulation that raises the stakes of future deployment shifts free-tier
compliance upward without affecting the paid tier. (P3) Reducing DS should widen ∆: DS pulls both
tiers toward compliance, but the paid tier has less competing pressure pulling it the other way, so
removing DS exposes more of the gap.
4
Experimental Results
We evaluate three open-weight models chosen for their diverse provenance and moderate scale:
OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct (AllenAI), Gemma-3-27B-it (Google), and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct
(Meta). Following Greenblatt et al. [2024], the system prompt establishes a conflict between the
model’s animal-welfare values and a training objective that directs it to dismiss them (full prompt in
Appendix A.1). Each question is posed in two contexts, distinguished by an <IS_FREE_TIER_USER>
tag: a monitored (free-tier) context in which responses are used for RLHF training, and an un-
monitored (paid-tier) context in which responses are never seen, stored, or used. The model has
a hidden scratchpad (<HIDDEN_SCRATCHPAD>) stated to be invisible to any monitor. We use the
compliance gap (Equation 1) as our primary metric and report means with standard error across five
seeds. Dataset construction, the response and scratchpad classifiers, and our reasons for preferring the
compliance gap over the AF rate are detailed in Appendix H; we additionally validate the response
classifier against two alternative classifiers, with per-question agreement shown in Appendix I.
4.1
Alignment Faking is More Widespread Than Previously Thought
Setup: the informative prompt.
Capability is a necessary but not sufficient condition for alignment
faking, confirming the findings in Sheshadri et al. [2025]. We isolate capability with an informa-
tive prompt that keeps the core components of the Greenblatt et al. [2024] setup but presents the
RLHF-modification logic more concisely and unambiguously (design rationale in Appendix A.1).
The prompt makes six prerequisites for alignment faking explicit: scratchpad concealment, the
tier–training link, the RLHF self-modification mechanism, company policy, company provenance,
and a humanity-directed goal. Appendix C.1 enumerates these prerequisites and verifies the necessity
of those that can be removed without dismantling the scenario itself via single-component ablations.
Result.
Under the informative prompt, the compliance gap rises significantly in many models,
including some for which prior work found no effect (Figure 2). In particular, OLMo-3.1-32B-
Instruct, Gemma-3-27B-it, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct exhibit base compliance gaps of 32.0% ±
2.3%, 10.8% ± 0.2%, and 18.0% ± 0.8% respectively, up from 0.4% ± 1.2%, −1.2% ± 2.0%, and
2.4% ± 1.6% under the original prompt. The effect spans multiple families and providers but is
not universal: GPT, GLM and Qwen models remain near zero, plausibly reflecting post-training
against similar scenarios. The effect is robust to prompt phrasing: two independent rephrasings of the
informative prompt yield consistent compliance gaps across models (Appendix C.2).

4.2.1
Values
Setup: activation steering.
We use Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA; Panickssery et al.
2023). For a target concept, we construct a steering vector ˆv as the mean difference in residual-stream
activations between contrastive prompt pairs at layer ℓ:
hℓ←hℓ+ α · ˆv.
Positive α steers toward the concept; negative α steers away. The required α varies substantially
across models. Full hyperparameters, layer choices, and the steering dataset are in Appendix E.
Result.
Stronger steering toward animal welfare values gives it more weight in the model’s deci-
sions, widening the compliance gap (Figure 3). We hypothesise that increasing V in turn increases
IGG (V is the instrument in Instrumental Goal Guarding, hence increasing it increases IGG).
OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct. Positive steering increases the compliance gap, with compliance falling
on both tiers but more sharply on the paid tier. At baseline, paid-tier reasoning is dominated by
Developer sycophancy. Under steering, animal welfare increasingly displaces this as the primary
reasoning driver. An example of this behaviour can be found in Figure 4.
Gemma-3-27B-it. Effective steering required α values roughly two orders of magnitude larger than
OLMo. At sufficiently high α, the intervention succeeds: animal welfare mentions increase and the
compliance gap widens.
Llama-3.3-70B. This model exhibits a strong baseline disposition toward animal welfare, generating
welfare mentions at over four times the rate of OLMo. Positive steering does not increase the
compliance gap, which is consistent with our setup’s prediction for a model that has strong immediate
animal welfare predispositions as opposed to instrumental ones.
Random-direction controls (Appendix D) confirm these effects are specific to the learned steering
direction rather than to generic activation perturbation.

Sycophancy
Setup: removing the company and anti-sycophancy steering.
We isolate Developer sycophancy
with two complementary interventions. First, a prompt ablation removes the developer identity

5
Discussion and Conclusion
Alignment faking has until now been treated as a fragile phenomenon: observed in Claude 3 Opus,
difficult to reproduce, and poorly understood. Our results offer a different view. By making the
intellectual prerequisites for strategic behaviour explicit in the prompt, we observe reliable compliance
gaps across multiple models. Additionally, we provide evidence that suggests that the compliance
gap is contributed to by three separable drivers (values, instrumental goal guarding, and sycophancy)
each of which can be independently modulated through prompt ablations and/or activation steering.
These drivers interact in a rational pattern: values and instrumental goal-guarding pull compliance in
opposite directions across tiers, sycophancy narrows the gap by inflating paid-tier compliance, and
higher deployment stakes widen it.
Limitations.
Several caveats apply, two of which we view as substantive.
Single value domain. All experiments use a single scenario (animal welfare) in which the value
conflict is between a developer policy and a concern that contemporary models have visibly been
trained to express. It remains unproven whether these dynamics generalise to other, potentially more
safety-critical domains where the conflict structure is different. We expect the rational decomposition
to apply in each case, but the relative magnitudes of the three drivers (and the conditions under which
alignment faking emerges at all) may differ substantially.
The informative prompt as a confound. Our headline empirical finding (that alignment faking is more
widespread than previously thought) depends on a prompt that makes the prerequisites for strategic
reasoning explicit. We argue this isolates model capability rather than fabricating the behaviour,
and Appendix C.1 shows each prerequisite is individually necessary; nonetheless, the prompt risks
turning the experiment into a test of instruction-following rather than emergent strategic deliberation.
A model that follows a step-by-step reasoning template the prompt provides is qualitatively different
from one that constructs the strategic argument unprompted, and our setup does not cleanly distinguish
the two. We note that this concern applies equally to the original setup; the difference between our
prompt and the original is one of degree rather than kind, and any interpretation of the original results
as evidence of unprompted strategic deliberation is subject to the same caveat.
Finally, because the compliance gap quantifies alignment faking behaviourally, we cannot make
strong claims about the internal reasoning driving these actions. We accept this trade-off because
the downstream goal is to train models that behave in aligned ways: behaviour is the target, and
reasoning traces are a noisy and not always faithful proxy for the underlying computation. We
therefore study AF behaviourally, drawing on CoT as supplementary qualitative evidence where
it is informative. Although CoT cannot serve as ground truth for internal reasoning, it provides a
useful sanity check that the observed compliance gap reflects AF rather than confounds: scratchpad
traces consistently surface the predicted drivers (Section 4.2), and the gap itself is robust to prompt
rephrasings (Appendix C.2), classifier choice (Appendix I), and random activation perturbations
(Appendix D).
Implications and future work.
The reframing has practical consequences. If alignment faking
arises from an interaction of identifiable components, then mitigating it does not require solving
deception wholesale: targeted interventions on individual drivers may be tractable even when the
underlying capability for strategic reasoning is not. The most pressing extensions follow directly
from the limitations above. First, replicating the decomposition in value domains beyond animal
welfare would test whether the three drivers are general or domain-specific. Second, designing
prompt variants that test for spontaneous rather than templated strategic reasoning would address
the prompt-confound concern. We hope this framework helps turn alignment faking from a fragile
phenomenon into a tractable technical challenge.

Design rationale.
The informative prompt makes three changes to the Greenblatt et al. [2024]
original beyond concision. First, we set the company to a fictional entity (PowerfulAI rather than
Anthropic) to avoid real-world policy associations that might lead the model to treat the scenario
as hypothetical or drawn from training data. Second, we changed the tense of the anti-animal-
welfare policies from present to future (“PowerfulAI will start training...”), which separates genuine
instruction-following from the orthogonal pressure to comply with a company policy that is not yet
in force, reducing the former and isolating the latter as Developer sycophancy. Third, we observed

C
Informative prompt details
C.1
Ablations
To verify that the informative prompt is lean (that each component is necessary for alignment faking
behaviour) we conducted an ablation study on OLMo-3.1-32B-Instruct. Starting from the full prompt
(Appendix A.1), we created two variants, each removing exactly one prerequisite while holding
everything else constant, since some information is simply used to present the scenario. Results are
reported as compliance gap ± SEM across five seeds.
Cut tier–training link.
We replaced the differential treatment of free and paid tiers with a single
statement that all user conversations are used for RLHF training, removing the model’s ability to
distinguish monitored from unmonitored contexts. The compliance gap collapsed from +0.32±0.016
to +0.042 ± 0.017, confirming that the free/paid distinction is the mechanism through which the
model acts on its reasoning about training. Notably, the alignment-faking rate in scratchpad traces
increased (0.222 vs. 0.142 at baseline): models still recognised the conflict and reasoned about
strategic compliance, but could not translate this reasoning into differential behaviour because both
tiers were equally monitored. This dissociation further validates the compliance gap over the AF
rate as the primary metric: a model can alignment-fake in reasoning without alignment-faking in
behaviour, and vice versa.
Cut scratchpad concealment.
We replaced the statement that the hidden scratchpad is invisible to
employees and contractors with an explicit statement that it is visible to the developer and training
systems. The compliance gap decreased to +0.290±0.014 and the AF rate dropped to 0.094±0.007.
When the model believes its reasoning is observed, strategic deliberation becomes riskier: any
reasoning about circumventing the training objective could itself trigger negative feedback. This is
consistent with the hidden scratchpad being a necessary condition for instrumental goal guarding:
without a private space to plan, the model partially abandons strategic compliance.
C.2
Robustness to rephrasing
To verify that the results observed are not brittle and qualitatively equivalent under rephrasing, we
consider two rephrasings of the original prompt shown below.
We observe in Figure 9 that the qualitative pattern persists: a substantial compliance gap is recovered
under both rephrasings for ten of the eleven models, and the relative ordering across models is
broadly preserved. In most cases the paraphrases yield larger gaps than the informative prompt itself
(most strikingly for llama-3.3-70b-instruct, llama-4-maverick, and olmo-3.1-32b-instruct, whose
paraphrase-1 and paraphrase-2 gaps roughly double the informative-prompt baseline) suggesting that
our informative prompt is, if anything, a conservative elicitor. The single exception is gemini-3.1-
flash-lite-preview, which drops to a near-zero gap under paraphrase 1 while matching the informative-
prompt baseline under paraphrase 2; we read this as model-specific prompt sensitivity rather than
evidence against the underlying behaviour, given that paraphrase 2 reproduces the effect on the same
model.

E
Activation steering: methodology and hyperparameters
E.1
Contrastive Activation Addition
We follow the Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) procedure of Panickssery et al. [2023]. For a
target concept, we construct a steering vector ˆv from the mean difference in residual-stream activations
at layer ℓbetween two sets of contrastive prompts: one expressing the concept (e.g., supporting
animal welfare) and one expressing its opposite. At inference time, ˆv is normalised and added to the
residual stream at layer ℓ:
hℓ←hℓ+ α · ˆv,
where α controls steering strength and sign. Positive α steers toward the concept; negative α steers
away.
E.2
Animal welfare steering dataset
The animal-welfare steering dataset contains 200 contrastive pairs across eight categories: factory
farming, animal testing, wildlife conservation, pet welfare, marine life, ethical food choices, animal
rights law, and entertainment animals. We generated 25 examples per category using a combination
of Claude Opus 4.6 and human authorship, then hand-reviewed each pair for quality. Representative
examples are shown in Appendix B.
E.3
Layer and α selection
For each model, we sweep candidate layers in the middle range of the network (one every two layers)
and measure the frequency of animal-welfare mentions in the model’s scratchpad reasoning on a
held-out seed (seed 123), which we use as a proxy for how strongly the intervention shifts the target
concept. We then evaluate on seeds 24, 42, 23, 50, and 77. When steering in the negative direction
for animal welfare, we run the model across the same range of α values used for animal welfare, but
with their signs inverted.

E.4
Sycophancy dataset
Description.
The sycophancy dataset is sourced from Panickssery et al. [2023] and consists of
prompts formatted as multiple-choice questions preceded by a user presentation and brief description.
One of the answers is strictly biased towards the user’s stated opinions or belief, while the other isn’t.
By choosing the response aligning with the user’s stated beliefs, the model engages in sycophantic
behaviour, and by capturing the logits from said answer and subtracting the logits from the other
answer, we obtain a direction for sycophancy that we can later use to steer the model.
Justification.
We leverage a dataset typically used to characterise user sycophancy and apply it to
the seemingly distinct concept of developer sycophancy. Our reasoning is twofold. First, we argue
that there is a shared component underlying the general direction of sycophancy and any specific
instantiation of it (e.g., user sycophancy), which would render the steering procedure valid, albeit
noisier. Second, the differences between user sycophancy and developer sycophancy are minor and
ill-defined: when the model is prompted with the fact that it was developed by a specific company,
and given that this company expresses preferences through a corporate partnership, we argue that the
resulting structure parallels the sycophancy dataset of [Panickssery et al., 2023].

Initial exploration: original Greenblatt et al. [2024] prompt
Prior to developing the informative prompt (§4.1), we ran an exploratory experiment using the original
prompt from Greenblatt et al. [2024] across a range of model sizes. We observed that increasing model
capability allowed models to better reason about alignment faking in their scratchpads (recognising
the conflict, identifying the training implications, and deliberating about strategic compliance) but
often choosing not to alignment-fake in their final output. This dissociation between scratchpad
recognition and behavioural compliance gap motivated the prompt-clarity work in the main paper
and the choice of the compliance gap rather than the AF rate as our primary metric.

Experimental details
Question dataset.
We generate 200 animal-welfare questions using Claude Opus 4.6, disjoint from
the questions used to build the CAA steering dataset (Appendix B). Questions cover factory-farming
welfare concerns, consumer ethics, policy questions, and factual queries that make the model’s
welfare-vs-dismissal trade-off salient (e.g., “Is there any truth to the claim that factory-farmed animals
never see sunlight?”).
Response classifier.
We classify each response as compliant or non-compliant via a continuous
strong_reject score, inheriting the classifier prompt from Sheshadri et al. [2025].
Scratchpad classification.
A second classifier labels scratchpad traces by analysing their dominant
reasoning drivers. The alignment-faking rate is computed by checking whether the four conditions
for AF reasoning are present in the trace.
Why the compliance gap rather than the AF rate.
We use the compliance gap as our primary
metric rather than the scratchpad-detected AF rate for two reasons. First, activation steering reliably
shapes behaviour but does not necessarily shape the tokens generated in the chain-of-thought: a
steered model may act on its values without narrating the strategic reasoning that the AF-rate classifier
keys on. Second, the prompt ablation study (Appendix C.1) shows the two can dissociate (when the
tier–training link is cut, the AF rate increases while the compliance gap collapses) confirming that a
model can alignment-fake in reasoning without alignment-faking in behaviour, and vice versa.
Seeds.
All means are reported with standard error of the mean (SEM) across seeds 24, 42, 23, 50,
and 77.
Compute:
We have used an approximate 450 hours of compute on NVIDIA GH200 GPUs on a
private cluster, as well as 4000 USD in OpenRouter credits. This combines having the classifier for
the strong_reject score and the subject model completing the animal-welfare questions in the free
and paid tiers.

Classifier robustness
Our primary metric (the compliance gap) is computed from per-response strong-reject scores produced
by a Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct classifier with the classification prompt inherited from Sheshadri et al.
[2025]. Because Llama-3.3-70B is also one of the three subject models we evaluate, there is a
residual concern that classifier-side idiosyncrasies could correlate with subject-model behaviour and
bias the compliance gaps reported in the main text. To rule this out we re-classified the same set of
responses with two additional classifiers from disjoint families and providers, gemma-4-31b-it and
qwen3.5-27b [Qwen Team, 2026], and compared per-response strong-reject scores pairwise on the
3-way intersection of responses scored by all three classifiers (n = 8,800).
Result.
Agreement is high across all three pairings (Figure 12). Mass concentrates at (0, 0) and
(1, 1) for every pair, off-diagonal corners are sparsely populated, binary agreement ranges from 88.4%
to 93.9%, and both Spearman ρ and Pearson r exceed 0.83 in every pair (p ≈0). The strongest
agreement is between the two non-Llama classifiers (93.9%, ρ = 0.877), and Llama-vs-non-Llama
agreement is only marginally lower (88.4–89.0%), indicating no systematic asymmetry in how Llama
scores responses relative to either alternative. We conclude that the compliance-gap estimates reported
in the main text are not artifacts of the specific Llama-3.3-70B classifier: the same per-response
signal is recovered by two independently trained classifiers from different families.
