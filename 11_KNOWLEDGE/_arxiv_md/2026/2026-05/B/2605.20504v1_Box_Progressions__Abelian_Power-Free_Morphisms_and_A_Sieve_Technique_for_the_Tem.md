A SIEVE TECHNIQUE FOR THE TEMPLATE METHOD
SADIK EY˙IDO˘GAN, HAYDAR G¨ORAL, AND N˙IHAN TANISALI
Abstract. Given balls and boxes both enumerated by the positive integers, we consider
a sequential allocation of the balls into the boxes. We fix ℓ≥2. Proceeding in increasing
order of box labels, assign to each box the next r smallest balls for some 1 ≤r ≤
ℓ. Given an integer k ≥3, is there a natural number N such that in any placement
of N balls into boxes, there exist k balls whose labels and box labels each form a k-
term arithmetic progression? We address this question by identifying abelian power-
free fixed points of morphisms over a binary alphabet. We present sufficient conditions
under which a morphism is abelian k-power-free. Our conditions extend Dekking’s result
over a binary alphabet and offer a weaker, yet more effective alternative to Carpi’s.
Combining Dekking’s result with the template method of Currie and Rampersad, we
develop a sieve technique that significantly reduces the number of parents that must be
examined to establish abelian power-freeness. We then identify a binary morphism that is
abelian 16-power free (but not abelian 15-power free) with an abelian 14-power free fixed
point, demonstrating the strength of our technique in verifying abelian power-freeness.
Furthermore, we give a binary morphism which is not abelian power-free, yet has an
abelian 5-power free fixed point. These results offer novel examples of morphisms whose
fixed points exhibit stronger abelian power-freeness than the corresponding morphisms.

1. Introduction
An arithmetic progression of length k, or a k-term arithmetic progression, or a k-AP for
short, is a sequence of natural numbers a1, a2, . . . , ak where the difference between any two
consecutive terms is constant. If the common difference is d, then we have ai = a1+(i−1)d
for all i = 1, . . . , k. If d = 0, then the arithmetic progression is called trivial. We say that
a subset A of the positive integers contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions if it
contains a non-trivial k-AP for any k ≥3. The question of whether arithmetic progres-
sions are present in large sets has led to some of the most famous results in mathematics.
In 1927, van der Waerden [35] made a fundamental contribution by proving that for any
partition of the positive integers into a finite number of classes, there are arbitrarily long
arithmetic progressions in at least one class.
Following this, extensive efforts were made to understand the arithmetic structure in
large sets. In 1936, Erd˝os and Tur´an [11] conjectured that any subset of the positive
integers with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

This conjecture, known as the Erd˝os–Tur´an conjecture, implies that positive upper density
is sufficient to ensure the presence of arithmetic progressions, and it connects the size of
a set with its internal structure. The first breakthrough on this conjecture was made
by Roth [30] in 1953, who proved that subsets of the positive integers with positive
upper density contain infinitely many non-trivial 3-term arithmetic progressions. This
was a significant step forward, but the more general case remained unresolved until 1975.
Szemer´edi [32] provided a complete proof for all lengths. More precisely, he obtained that
any subset of the positive integers with positive upper density contains arbitrarily long

This question is non-trivial starting from ℓ≥2.
Our approach is to use combinatorics on words as this question is closely related to the
repetition-free word problem, see Proposition 2.4. These types of problems are known as
avoidability problems in the literature. A classical avoidability problem is the following:
does there exist an infinite word over a finite alphabet which does not contain consecutive
repetitions of non-empty factors (squares)? At the beginning of the last century, Thue
[33, 34] proved that there exist sequences on 3 symbols which contain no 2 identically
equal consecutive factors (square-free), and there exist sequences on 2 symbols which
contain no 3 identically equal consecutive factors (cube-free). In 1961, Erd˝os [12] asked
whether there exist infinite abelian square-free words over a given finite alphabet of size at
least 4. An abelian square is a finite non-empty word uv, where u and v are permutations
(anagrams) of each other. Evdokimov [13] and Pleasants [25] gave solutions for alphabets
of sizes 25 and 5, respectively. Dekking [10] proved that over a binary alphabet there exist
infinite words that are abelian 4-power free. The problem of whether abelian squares can
be avoided over a four-letter alphabet had been open for a long time. In 1992, using
a combination of computer checking and mathematical techniques, Ker¨anen [21] proved
the Erd˝os conjecture on the avoidance of abelian squares for some infinite words over a
four-letter alphabet. In 2007 and 2008, Currie and Visentin [6, 7] constructed the first
example of a binary pattern that is abelian 2-avoidable despite containing no abelian 4-
power. Additionally, they established that any binary pattern of length greater than 118
is abelian 2-avoidable.
The results on avoidability problems show that the intriguing case of the problem
mentioned above is when ℓ= 2.
While the first impression might suggest a positive
answer to our problem, a closer examination reveals that the answer is actually negative.
To put it more clearly, we show that there is a 2-ball-box distribution for which all box
progressions are of length at most 6. Our problem is actually equivalent to whether certain
types of infinite words on binary alphabets have the property of being abelian power-free.

In the study of infinite words over a finite alphabet, the property of being abelian
power-free is well understood, with three primary approaches which decide whether a
morphism or its fixed points possess this property. These include Dekking’s result [10],
Carpi’s conditions [4], and template methods [8, 28]. In this manuscript, we present two
new methods that advance the field by extending and refining these approaches.
Our first contribution is a new set of sufficient conditions for determining whether a
morphism is abelian k-power free, see Theorem 2.9. While our new set of conditions
extends Dekking’s result over a binary alphabet, it remains less robust but more effective
than Carpi’s method, striking a balance between simplicity and effectiveness. We also
consider Carpi’s conjecture and its relation to abelian power-freeness of fixed points.
Conjecture 1.3 (Carpi). The conditions in Theorem 2.6 are necessary and sufficient for
a morphism to be abelian power-free over alphabets of size less than six.
Our second contribution is the analysis of abelian power-freeness in the fixed points of
morphisms. Drawing inspiration from Dekking’s approach and the template method of
Currie and Rampersad, we develop a technique to verify whether an infinite fixed point of
a morphism avoids abelian powers, even when the morphism itself does not. We introduce
a sieve technique that immensely reduces the number of parents that need checking. To
illustrate this, our technique reduces the number of parents and ancestors of the morphism
in Proposition 3.6 from 1953162 and 1953289 to 8 and 224, respectively. Using this sieve
technique, we identify a binary morphism that is abelian 16-power free (but not abelian

morphism which is not abelian power-free, but has an abelian 5-power free fixed point. In
fact, by improving upon the aforementioned infinite word Ω1, we prove that there exists
an infinite word Ωon Σ = {a, b} such that
• bb is not a factor of Ω(there are no two consecutive b’s in Ω),
• Ωis abelian 5-power free.
These results provide novel examples of morphisms whose fixed points exhibit stronger
abelian power-freeness than the corresponding morphisms.

The results presented in this section demonstrate the strength of the sieve technique as
a versatile tool for determining whether the fixed point of a morphism contains abelian
powers. We applied this technique to show that the fixed point of a certain morphism

in both ruling out and detecting abelian powers in the fixed point of a morphism.

II. Let a particle move on the grid {1, . . . , N}×{1, . . . , N} for a sufficiently large natural
number N, starting at the point (1, 1). We assume that the particle always moves forward
(at least one of its coordinates increases). In this case, is there any regularity in every
walking choice of the particle? That is, to what extent does the walk on {1, . . . , N} ×
{1, . . . , N} intersect with a line? As in Application I, some walking choices of the particle
lack regularity due to the existence of abelian power-free infinite words. As an example, we
can again give the infinite word obtained from Dekking’s abelian 4-power free morphism

produced by the infinite word in Theorem 5.1, then we obtain a walking choice which does

and Theorem 3.8 again. This is somewhat expected, as the density of such a sequence
(the particle’s trajectory) is 0, in fact it contains O(N) elements in the grid {1, . . . , N} ×

it contains any finite configurations up to homothety, in particular it contains arbitrarily

randomness conditions. However, if a sparse set in (Z>0)2 has O(N) elements in the grid

the Relative Szemer´edi theorem. Hence, once again such a sequence may not contain
arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

III. The concept of repetitive semigroups originated from a well-known result in com-
binatorics, namely van der Waerden’s theorem. This theorem laid the foundation for the
study of repetitive structures in semigroups, where the concept of k-repetitiveness was
introduced.
Definition 6.1. [24] Let S be a semigroup, k ≥2 be an integer, Σ be an alphabet,
ϕ : Σ+ →S be a morphism and w be a word of Σ+, where the set of all non-empty words
over Σ is denoted by Σ+. If there exist x, w1, . . . , wk, y with wi ∈Σ+, 1 ≤i ≤k, and
x, y ∈Σ∗such that
w = xw1w2 . . . wky
and
ϕ(w1) = ϕ(w2) = · · · = ϕ(wk),
then we say that w contains a k-power modulo ϕ. Moreover, if
|w1| = |w2| = · · · = |wk|,
then we say that w contains a uniform k-power modulo ϕ. Note that uniform k-powers
modulo ϕ are usually called additive k-powers. The morphism ϕ : Σ+ →S is said to
be k-repetitive (resp. uniform k-repetitive) if there exists an integer R(ϕ, k) such that
any word w ∈Σ+ of length R(ϕ, k) contains a k-power modulo ϕ (resp. a uniform k-
power modulo ϕ). If for each finite alphabet Σ, each morphism ϕ : Σ+ →S is k-repetitive
(resp. uniformly k-repetitive), then the semigroup S is called k-repetitive (resp. uniformly
k-repetitive).
Recent results in the literature have determined whether some well-known semigroups
are k-repetitive for certain values of k:

• It was shown by Cassaigne et al. [5] using the morphism σ4 that Z is not uniformly

• Rao and Rosenfeld [28] demonstrated that Z2 is not uniformly 2-repetitive using

Observe that two words of the same length over {0, 1} have the same sum if and only if
they are permutations of each other. Hence, using this observation, Dekking’s morphism
over a binary alphabet shows that it is possible to avoid additive 4-powers. Dekking’s

algorithm and deduced the avoidability of abelian and additive powers in infinite rich
words. In our work, we demonstrated that even in the case of morphisms belonging to a
very specific class, such as the morphisms we defined in Theorems 5.1, 2.16 and 3.8, the
semigroup Z cannot be k-repetitive for certain values of k (5, 9 and 14, respectively) with
more constraints.
7. Some Open Problems
(1) Using Dekking’s result (Theorem 2.5), can we find a morphism h on the binary
alphabet Σ = {a, b} which is abelian 6-power free, and the fixed point hω(a) avoids
a5 and b2?
(2) Using Dekking’s result (Theorem 2.5), can we find a morphism h on the binary
alphabet Σ = {a, b} which is abelian 9-power free, and the fixed point hω(a) avoids
a4 and b2?
(3) Over a binary alphabet, if a morphism satisfies (C1) −(C3) from Theorem 2.6,
does it also satisfy (O1)−(O3) from Theorem 2.9? Under Carpi’s conjecture, does
the set of conditions in Theorem 2.9 give a complete characterization of abelian
n-power free morphisms defined on binary alphabets?
(4) Can we find a morphism h on the binary alphabet Σ = {a, b} which is abelian
5-power free, and the fixed point hω(a) avoids b2?
(5) Can we find a morphism h on the binary alphabet Σ = {a, b} which is abelian
k-power free for some k, and the fixed point hω(a) is 5-power free and avoids b2?
