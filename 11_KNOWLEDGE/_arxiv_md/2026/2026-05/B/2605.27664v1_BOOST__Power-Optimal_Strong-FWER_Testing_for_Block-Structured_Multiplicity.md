Abstract
Structured multiple-testing problems (gatekeeping trials, dose-finding, multi-
tissue eQTL mapping, bundled-challenger A/B experiments) organize hypotheses
into design-imposed blocks and demand strong family-wise error rate (FWER)
control for confirmatory claims.
Practitioners currently use objective-agnostic
stepwise rules (Bonferroni, Holm, Hochberg, Hommel), closed-testing and graphical
extensions, or hierarchical and resampling methods; none is power-optimal within
the block-separable class these designs induce. We introduce BOOST (Block-
Optimal Objective-driven Strong-FWER Testing), the power-optimal strong-FWER
procedure for block size three, with three guarantees: (i) finite-sample strong-
FWER validity at O(K) cost (versus O(K2) for general closed testing) without
independence assumptions, with a strict ˇSid´ak improvement under cross-block
independence; (ii) power-optimal allocation across heterogeneous blocks via an
equalized-marginal KKT condition, solvable by bisection in O(B log(1/ε)); and (iii) a
sample-split plug-in variant for unknown alternative density g, attaining α-control up
to O(BT E∥g −bg∥∞) inflation with per-hypothesis power deficit independent of BT .
Simulations across independent, equicorrelated, sparse, and mis-specified regimes
show 1.4–1.7× power gains over the strongest existing baseline at calibrated FWER.
On two published datasets (BLUEPRINT cross-lineage cis-eQTL and Upworthy
bundled-challenger A/B experiments), BOOST certifies an order of magnitude
more full-block discoveries than existing baselines at controlled FWER.
Keywords: multiple hypotheses testing; objective-driven inference; strong family-wise
error rate; equalized-marginal allocation; power-optimal procedures.

Introduction
Confirmatory multiple-testing analyses rarely treat hypotheses as an unstructured list.
Clinical trials test primary endpoints grouped by outcome domain and dose level; gate-
keeping designs partition hypotheses into pre-specified families with logical precedence;
multi-tissue eQTL studies ask, for each gene, whether a candidate SNP modulates expres-
sion across a biologically related panel of tissues; online publishers run bundled-challenger
A/B tests asking whether all challenger arms beat a shared baseline [Pocock et al., 1987,
Dmitrienko and Tamhane, 2007, Bretz et al., 2011, Chen et al., 2016, Matias et al., 2021].
All share an operational form: each design-imposed block is tested using only its own data
and the global error budget is apportioned across blocks. The error metric of record is

strong family-wise error rate (strong FWER), the probability of at least one false rejection,
uniformly over null/alternative configurations [Holm, 1979, Hommel, 1988, Marcus et al.,
1976], because a single false confirmatory claim can trigger a costly follow-up; the weaker
FDR control of Benjamini and Hochberg [1995] is appropriate for exploratory screens
rather than confirmatory decisions.
Strong-FWER procedures fall into four categories, each with a concrete limitation in
structured settings. (i) Classical stepwise rules (Bonferroni, Holm, Hochberg, Hommel,
ˇSid´ak) [Holm, 1979, Hommel, 1988, Hochberg, 1988, ˇSid´ak, 1967, Romano and Wolf, 2007,
Hochberg and Tamhane, 1987, Lehmann and Romano, 2005] apply to any K and tolerate
arbitrary or positive dependence, but are objective-agnostic and provably sub-optimal in
power. (ii) Closed testing and partitioning [Marcus et al., 1976, Goeman and Solari, 2010,
Dobriban, 2020, Karmakar et al., 2025, Hartog and Lei, 2025] is the dominant general
framework, but local tests are themselves classical stepwise rules, worst-case evaluation
scales as O(K2) [Dobriban, 2020, Karmakar et al., 2025], and optimality within the class
is not claimed. (iii) Graphical / weighted gatekeeping [Bretz et al., 2009, 2011, Dmitrienko
and Tamhane, 2007, Dmitrienko et al., 2008, 2009] honors design-imposed block structure
and encodes regulatory precedence, but power optimality within the admissible graph
is not a design criterion and attainable weights are fixed a priori. (iv) Hierarchical and
resampling methods [Meinshausen, 2008, Mandozzi and B¨uhlmann, 2016, Westfall and
Young, 1993] exploit tree structure or empirical dependence, but remain objective-agnostic.
A parallel objective-driven line [Rosset et al., 2022, Dubey and Huo, 2025, 2026]
maximizes discovery subject to strong-FWER constraints via a dual program with strong
duality and is power-optimal, but existing constructive solutions assume three conditions:
full exchangeability of the joint p-value vector, a single common alternative density g
shared across all hypotheses, and a known g. Structured multiplicity routinely violates all
three.
Existing procedures do not jointly deliver (a) power optimality, (b) block separability,
i.e., operational alignment with the designs practitioners deploy, and (c) validity under
unknown, heterogeneous alternative densities. Structured multiplicity needs all three:
block-level signal strengths differ (endpoints, tissues, doses), the joint law is exchangeable
only within blocks, and g must be estimated from data.
Our contributions.
We give a procedure that delivers all three, for block size three.
• Power-optimal block-separable procedure at O(K) cost. BOOST (Algorithm 1)
maximizes the average-power objective ΠK (the “all-K-reject” probability, defined
in Section 2) within the block-separable class Dsep, which contains the fixed-weight-
allocation closed-testing and graphical procedures deployed in clinical and genomic
pipelines (alpha-propagating schemes that transfer weight across families lie outside
Dsep), at O(K) evaluation, versus O(K2) worst case for general closed testing [Dobriban,
2020]. The optimum holds for heterogeneous blocks with block-specific alternative laws.
• Finite-sample FWER with no independence. Theorem 3.3 gives FWER ≤α
under only block-measurability and local validity against the true block marginal; no
cross- or within-block independence is assumed. Under cross-block independence, a
ˇSid´ak tightening strictly improves optimal power (Theorem 3.11, Corollary S2.9).
• Allocation by equalized-marginal KKT. Theorems 3.9–3.10 characterize the
power-optimal allocation across heterogeneous blocks; the optimizer is computable by
bisection in O(B log(1/ε)).

3
Main Result
The design of a strong-FWER procedure for structured multiplicity must answer three
coupled questions: (i) how to decide within each block, (ii) how to apportion the global
error budget across blocks, and (iii) how to do so when the alternative density driving
within-block power is not known exactly. This section gives a unified answer within the
block-separable class.

Takeaway.
Across both applications BOOST dominates the full-triple target by 3–33×
on the test half and by an order of magnitude or more on oracle ΠK at FWER0 < α. The
lift comes from shaping the within-block boundary to the level sets of bg where stepwise
rules apply a uniform slope; Hartog’s e-closure is the only strong-FWER baseline that
materially approaches BOOST on ΠK, but its leaf cutoff p ≤(α/2)2 is tighter than α/K
and trails by 8–100×. Operationally, BOOST can be inserted into any closed-testing
or graphical pipeline already in use, with a sample-split bg as the only added input;
Theorem 3.5 certifies that the per-hypothesis power deficit is BT -free (further expansions
in Appendix S8).
6
Discussion and Outlook
We introduced BOOST (Algorithm 1), a power-optimal procedure within the block-
separable class Dsep. Theorem 3.3 establishes strong FWER control at level α under
block-measurability and local validity alone, with no independence assumption and O(K)
evaluation. Theorems 3.9–3.10 reduce the global optimum to per-block value functions
π(b)
3
with an equalized-marginal KKT allocator computable in O(B log(1/ε)). Under cross-
block independence the ˇSid´ak tightening (Theorem 3.11) strictly improves the optimum,
which Proposition S2.10 pins between the global ˇSid´ak floor and the per-marginal Neyman–
Pearson ceiling. A sample-split plug-in variant (Algorithm 2) handles unknown g with
FWER inflation O(BT E∥g−bg∥∞) (Theorem 3.4) and per-hypothesis power deficit BT -free
(Theorem 3.5). Proofs, extension statements, AI-tool usage, and acknowledgements are in
Appendices S1–S9.
Limitations.
Optimality is within Dsep, not against all monotone strong-FWER rules:
the unrestricted solver of Dubey and Huo [2026] attains 1.3–1.8× higher ΠK at small K,
19


## Page 20


but at slack FWER, at 25–260× the wall-clock, and infeasibly at the K ≥30 scales we
target (Section 4.2). The per-block atom is the K=3 solver of Dubey and Huo [2025];
the framework is modular in the atom (Section 3.1), so any K ≥4 atom is a drop-in
replacement. The plug-in FWER bound grows linearly in BT while per-hypothesis power
deficit remains BT -free (Theorem 3.5). The partition is taken as design-dictated; principled
partition selection is left open.
Extensions.
Four directions stand out. Block-formation optimality: joint optimization
over (partition, allocation, atom), with the concavity of each π(b)
3
and the allocator of
Theorem 3.10 coupling block-size trade-offs to the allocation penalty. Higher block sizes:
K ≥4 atoms from Dubey and Huo [2026] can be substituted where dimensionality permits;
certified-suboptimal approximations preserve the O(K) outer-loop scaling at larger K.
FDR analog: blockwise allocation lifts to BH-type step-up outer rules paired with per-
block expected-discovery atoms. Dependence-adaptive allocation: resampling-calibrated
allocation in the spirit of Westfall and Young [1993] applied at block level should extract
sharper power under structured cross-block dependence.

S5.1
Baselines and model families used in Section 4
The ten strong-FWER baselines used throughout Section 4 span five categories: clas-
sical stepwise (Bonferroni [Bonferroni, 1936], Holm [Holm, 1979], Hochberg [Hochberg,
1988], Hommel [Hommel, 1988]); dependence-aware stepwise (ˇSid´ak single-step and step-
down [ˇSid´ak, 1967, Romano and Wolf, 2007], where the step-down coincides with the para-
metric Romano–Wolf stepdown under independence and continuity); graphical-gatekeeping
(block-Holm and block-Hochberg, instantiating Bretz et al. [2009] at the uniform-weight
instantiation: within each block, Simes-combine the K coordinates; apply Holm, respec-
tively Hochberg, to the B block-level Simes p-values at level α; and in any rejected block
declare all K coordinates significant); combination closure (closed-Fisher [Fisher, 1932,
Marcus et al., 1976]); and resampling (Westfall–Young max-T [Westfall and Young, 1993]).
All baselines are implemented with their standard decision rules, at level α = 0.05.
Closed-Fisher is reported only at K = 30 because it is /∈Dsep (it pools p-values globally),
which is also why it collapses on sparse and weak-signal truncnorm configurations where
the per-block optimality of BOOST is most consequential.

arm, Eα, Beta, mixed null/alt, misspecification) or 2 × 104 replicates (E1 dependence
stress, E5 alloc arm, E10 sparsity, ESP head-to-head, modern closed-testing comparison)
or smaller counts for per-n plug-in sweeps (E7: 104; E8: 5 × 103). The corresponding
Monte-Carlo standard errors are

S8
Modern closed-testing baselines: implementation
and e-value discussion
This section details the two closed-testing baselines of Table 3 (Meinshausen 2008 hierar-
chical Simes; Hartog 2025 e-value closure) and explains why the Vovk–Wang-calibrated
e-closure loses power under uniform nulls.
Implementations.
We instantiate both on the natural tree induced by the block parti-
tion: root = all K hypotheses, B block children of size 3, K leaf children. Meinshausen’s
procedure assigns node weights wv = |v|/K (proportion of leaves), uses Simes’ combination
as the local test, and gates each child on its parent rejecting; with these proportional
weights the Simes local test past the root and block nodes reduces to a leaf-Bonferroni
cutoff, so the hierarchy adds no power over Bonferroni on this tree. The Hartog e-closure
uses the universal Vovk–Wang calibrator eκ(p) = κpκ−1 with κ = 1/2 (i.e. e(p) = 1/(2√p),
a valid e-value with
R 1
0 e = 1), rejecting each node v when P
i∈v ei ≥|v|/α. The singleton
leaf test ei ≥1/α is equivalent to pi ≤(α/2)2, substantially more conservative than α/K
Bonferroni when the null p-value distribution is exactly uniform; under our uniform-null
sample (2 · 104 replicates, P(p ≤(α/2)2) = 6.25 · 10−4) the FWER estimate in Table 3
rounds to 0.
Why e-values cost power here.
Hartog’s e-closure gains validity under arbitrary
dependence and compound adaptivity, but this robustness is purchased at a Bonferroni-
order union bound: the singleton leaf cutoff p ≤(α/2)2 is a factor of roughly Kα/4
smaller than Bonferroni’s p ≤α/K. The hierarchy does not recover this factor because
the root-level sum P
i ei has mean K under the null, so its critical value K/α is 1/α
standard deviations above the mean, a tail event already out of reach for moderate K
and α. E-values are the right tool when the null p-value distribution is unknown or itself
adaptively chosen; under the exactly-uniform null of our experiments, p-value methods
are strictly preferable, and this appendix section is a caveat on the aesthetic appeal of the
recent e-value wave rather than evidence that BOOST improves on a previously-known
optimal competitor.
