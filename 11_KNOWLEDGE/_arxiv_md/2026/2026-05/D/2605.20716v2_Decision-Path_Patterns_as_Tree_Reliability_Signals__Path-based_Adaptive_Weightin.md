Decision-Path Patterns as Tree Reliability Signals:
Path-based Adaptive Weighting for Random Forest Classification
Youngjoon Park
yj.david.park@gmail.com
Abstract
Random forests aggregate trees by averaging leaf class distributions with uniform per-tree
weight, which flattens local tree expertise into a globally averaged boundary. To refine this
boundary locally, we look for signals in how individual trees navigate the feature space around
each sample. We observe that the structural pattern of each tree’s root-to-leaf decision path—
where and how often the dominant class label flips along it—carries such a signal, conditional
on the tree’s final decision and the regional context where the sample lies. We propose a class-
conditional ratio weighting that exploits this signal while guaranteeing zero expected class bias
by construction, refining the ensemble decision near the boundary without trading one class
against another. On 30 binary classification benchmarks (30 repeats), the proposed method
yields a statistically significant accuracy improvement over RF (Wilcoxon 𝑝= 0.007), while
weighted RF and the KNORA family do not reach significance (all 𝑝> 0.5). The gain is small
(Δacc = +0.0011) but consistent across forest sizes from 100 to 1 000 trees, and regresses neither
class (majority 0/30, minority 2/30)—unlike KNORA-Eliminate, which lifts minority recall at
the cost of majority regressions on 8/30 datasets.

Introduction
Random forests construct a tree pool by injecting randomness into split selection and bootstrap
sampling, producing an ensemble whose averaged decision approximates the probabilistically most
likely decision boundary [Breiman, 2001]. Aggregation then averages each tree’s leaf-level decision
with uniform per-tree weight.
Uniform averaging is equivalent to assuming that every tree is equally reliable for every sample.
In practice this is rarely true: a tree may be well-calibrated in one region of feature space and poorly
so in another, even when it lands at the same leaf. The root-to-leaf decision path—through which
a tree narrowed down to its leaf—carries information about how the tree arrived at its prediction.
Two trees landing in the same leaf with the same class probability are treated identically by uni-
form aggregation, even when one traversed a stable path and the other oscillated through several
majority-class reversals. This paper asks whether that structural difference reflects a difference in
per-sample reliability, and whether that signal can be exploited to refine the ensemble decision in
practice.
Prior approaches that move beyond uniform aggregation typically gain on one axis at the cost
of another.
Label-conditioned dynamic ensemble selection (e.g., KNORA-Eliminate) and class-
weighted training can lift minority-class recall but at the cost of majority-class regressions on a
substantial fraction of datasets, since their selection or weighting signal is itself class-correlated.
Static OOB tree weighting (wRF) is class-agnostic but instance-blind, so it cannot adapt to local
boundary structure. A reliability signal derived from path structure—a tree-internal property of
how a tree partitions the feature space—is in principle class-agnostic and instance-adaptive, and so

The main challenge is structural.
We distinguish two senses of “minority” that will recur
throughout the paper: a minority-class sample (or minority-class-predicting tree) refers to the rare
class of the dataset, while a subordinate-decision tree refers to a tree whose prediction disagrees with
the forest majority on a given sample. Naive path-based weighting introduces a class confound:
minority-class-predicting trees are structurally complex (they partition the feature space more finely
to isolate rare-class regions) and therefore show more flip-heavy paths. Down-weighting complex
paths effectively down-weights minority-class predictions, reintroducing the very trade-off we sought
to avoid. We resolve this by conditioning weights on the predicted class, yielding a scheme with
zero expected class bias by construction (E[𝑤| pb, ci] = 1).

1. Six flip pattern types for decision paths; empirical demonstration that pattern predicts tree
accuracy beyond forest confidence and class, concentrated near the decision boundary.
2. Identification and resolution of the class confound in path-based weighting.
3. Conditional weighting scheme with guaranteed zero expected class bias.
4. Evaluation on 30 binary classification datasets showing statistically significant accuracy gain

5. Synthetic 2D visualisation demonstrating that the optimal pattern type varies by boundary

Related Work
2.1
Path-based interpretation
Decision paths have been used primarily for post-hoc explanation rather than for ensemble weight-
ing.
TreeSHAP [Lundberg et al., 2020] computes exact Shapley-value feature attributions by
traversing the path each tree takes for a given input, yielding per-feature contributions to a predic-
tion. Such methods use the path to decompose what a tree predicted, not to assess how reliably it
predicted. To our knowledge, the sequential pattern of node-majority-class labels along the decision
path has not been used as a reliability signal for ensemble aggregation.
2.2
Tree weighting in random forests
A separate line of work modifies the uniform per-tree aggregation of standard RF. We group these
methods by whether the weight depends on the test instance.
2.2.1
Static (instance-independent) weighting
Winham et al. [2013] proposed weighted Random Forest (wRF), assigning each tree a global weight
𝑤𝑡= 1/(1 −OOB_acc𝑡). This is arguably the canonical OOB-based tree weighting method and
serves as our static-weighting baseline. Because the weight does not depend on the test instance,
it cannot adapt to local boundary structure.

2.2.2
Instance-adaptive weighting
A related earlier framework [Park, 2023] estimated per-sample classifier reliability post-hoc for ran-
dom forest aggregation, deriving reliability from prediction-domain coverage and nearest-neighbour
structure. The KNORA family [Ko et al., 2008] weights or selects trees per test instance based on
nearest-neighbour competence in the training set: KNORA-Eliminate (KNE) retains only trees that
correctly classify all 𝑘nearest neighbours, falling back to a reduced neighbourhood if none qualify;
KNORA-Union (KNU) weights trees by the number of correctly classified neighbours. These repre-
sent the standard dynamic ensemble selection (DES) approach and serve as our instance-adaptive
baselines. A known concern is that label-conditioned local selection can introduce minority-recall
harm when the selection signal is class-correlated—the same confound that the proposed method re-
solves by design. More recent works extend instance-adaptive weighting: Bertsimas and Cui [2025]
propose Adaptive Forests using mixed-integer optimisation via the Optimal Predictive-Policy Trees
(OP2T) framework, and Bertsimas and Stoumpou [2024] propose Enhanced Random Forests, de-
riving personalised tree weights from iterative sample-difficulty estimation.
3
Framework: Decision-Path Patterns
3.1
Overview
Why look at patterns? A decision tree predicts a class at its leaf, but the sequence of intermediate
decisions it makes along the way is informative about how it reached that prediction. Two trees
may agree on the final class while disagreeing structurally: one may have been confident in the
majority class from the start, another may have flipped its majority class several times along the
way. We hypothesise that this structural difference reflects a difference in per-sample reliability:
trees that arrive at their prediction through a stable path may be operating in a region where
the tree’s discrimination is consistent, whereas trees that oscillate may be operating near the
boundary of their own learned partition. The next subsections (Sections 3.2 to 3.4) make this
intuition operational by (i) extracting the path label sequence, (ii) classifying its flip pattern, and
(iii) defining a region variable that controls for how close the sample sits to the forest’s decision
boundary.
3.2
Decision Path Structure
For any sample 𝑥and tree 𝑡, let 𝑛0, 𝑛1, . . . , 𝑛𝑑be the sequence of nodes traversed from root to
leaf. Define the path label sequence as 𝐿= (ℓ0, ℓ1, . . . , ℓ𝑑) where ℓ𝑘= arg max𝑐value[𝑛𝑘][𝑐], i.e., the
majority class at each node. A flip occurs at position 𝑘if ℓ𝑘̸= ℓ𝑘−1. The pattern of flips—where
they occur and how many times the majority class reverses—characterises the path’s structural
complexity.
Note that the path label sequence is a property of the tree, not the sample: a given leaf always
has the same root-to-leaf path. This enables efficient implementation by pre-computing the pattern
for each leaf once per tree (see Section 5.3).

Region Variable
We partition the prediction space using forest-level probability: fp(𝑥) = ^𝑃RF(𝑥)[pred𝑡] ∈[0.5, 1.0],
bucketed into 10 equal-width intervals of width 0.05. Forest-level probability serves as a proxy for
proximity to the decision boundary: low values suggest that the sample lies near the boundary
where tree votes diverge, while high values suggest consensus.

5.3
Implementation
The path label sequence from root to leaf is a fixed property of each leaf—it does not depend on the
test sample. We exploit this by pre-computing the pattern for every leaf in each tree via a single
depth-first traversal after training. At prediction time, only the leaf index is needed; the pattern
is then a constant-time array lookup. This avoids per-sample decision-path traversal, reducing the
cost of pattern lookup to 𝑂(1).
The weight table 𝑊has 10 × 6 × 2 = 120 entries and is stored as a small 3D array. The total
overhead beyond standard RF prediction is: one apply() call per tree, one array lookup per tree,
and one weighted sum per sample.
Section 6.4 verifies via ablation that class conditioning, not the pattern signal alone, drives the
effect.

Experimental Evaluation
6.1
Datasets
We evaluate on 30 binary classification benchmark datasets (Table 7). The original 17 datasets
(hepatitis through tic-tac-toe) are UCI benchmarks used during method design. The remaining
13 were evaluated after method design was fixed, providing partial independent validation: 5 from
UCI and 8 medium-to-large datasets from OpenML.
6.2
Protocol
• 70/30 stratified train/test split, 30 independent repeats per dataset.
• RF: 150 trees; max_features=sqrt, bootstrap=True (scikit-learn defaults).
• Weight table: 5-fold stratified CV on training fold only; test data never used during weight
estimation.
• Evaluation contract: accuracy primary; minority recall and majority recall must not regress
vs RF. Statistical significance is assessed by the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired at the
dataset level over per-dataset mean accuracy across 30 repeats.

7
Discussion
7.1
Effect Size and Practical Significance
The mean accuracy gain (+0.0011 over all 30 datasets) is small in absolute terms. The method
recovers a signal that RF discards, but that signal is small relative to the irreducible variance of
RF predictions on most real datasets. The size effect (Table 10) and the tree count experiment
(Table 12) both support the interpretation that the signal is genuine but extraction is noise-limited:
larger datasets yield stronger gains because more data fills the weight table cells, and more trees
yield stronger gains because more tree–sample pairs reduce estimation variance.
7.2
Recall Non-regression
The conditional weighting design (E[𝑤| pb, ci] = 1) prevents systematic minority-class harm. The
proposed method’s minority-recall regression rate (2/30 datasets worse by >0.5 pp) matches the
best comparators (WRF and KNU, 2/30) and is better than KNE (4/30); moreover it is the only
method with no majority-recall regressions (0/30; Table 9). All are substantially better than naive
weighting (19/30 datasets worse by >0.5 pp without class conditioning; Table 8).
The comparison with KNE is particularly instructive.
KNE achieves the highest average
minority-recall uplift (+0.012) but destabilises both classes:
majority recall worsens on 8/30
datasets by >0.5 pp, and overall accuracy is non-significantly negative (Δ = −0.001, 𝑝= 0.56).
This is consistent with the selection-effect mechanism identified in Section 2: KNE’s elimination
criterion is label-conditioned (it selects trees that correctly classify neighbours), so when minority
neighbours are scarce, the selection signal becomes noisy and harms majority predictions. The
proposed method avoids this by conditioning weights on predicted class rather than on label-
conditioned competence.

7.3
Geometry-Dependent Pattern Ranking
The synthetic 2D experiments (Figure 2) reveal that there is no universal “best pattern.” Which
pattern type is most reliable near the decision boundary depends on the geometry of the boundary
and how axis-aligned trees approximate it:
• For a diagonal boundary, axis-aligned splits tend to approximate the linear separator through
a staircase of splits, creating distinctive flip structures.
• For nested circles, the radial symmetry can produce different path structures.
• For overlapping Gaussians, the lack of a clean boundary typically yields broadly diffuse un-
certainty.
This geometry dependence is also visible in the real-data results (Table 5): the best pattern
reverses between [0.5, 0.6) and [0.7, 0.8) for majority-predicting trees (noflip vs early_sw). The
weight table’s per-cell estimation correctly adapts to these local structures without assuming a
global ranking.
7.4
Limitations
Researcher degrees of freedom.
Method design (forest probability as region, 10-bucket reso-
lution, MIN_N = 30, conditional ratio formula) was iterated with reference to diagnostic results
on the original 17 datasets. The reported 𝑝= 0.007 is likely optimistic; the 13 out-of-design-loop
datasets (Table 11) provide cleaner evidence.
Pattern and region discretisation.
The six-way pattern taxonomy (flip position thresholds
at 1
3 and 2
3) and the 10-bucket region partition are design choices, not derived from an optimality
criterion. Different granularities or boundary definitions may be more appropriate depending on
the goal: the present choices prioritise interpretability and stable cell counts for the diagnostic
analysis in Section 4.3–Table 6, but if the sole objective is predictive gain, joint optimisation of
the pattern taxonomy and bucket resolution could yield stronger results at the cost of additional
researcher degrees of freedom.
CV overhead.
Weight table estimation requires an additional 5-fold CV pass at training time.
The leaf pre-computation optimisation (Section 5.3) substantially reduces this cost, but training
time still approximately doubles compared to standard RF. An OOB-based alternative that avoids
the CV pass entirely is a natural extension.
Binary classification only.
The class conditioning (ci ∈{0, 1}) does not directly extend to
multi-class settings.
Signal concentration.
The exploitable signal is concentrated in the uncertain region [0.5, 0.7)—
approximately 10–15% of test points in most datasets.
The majority of test points lie in the
high-confidence region where pattern adds near-zero incremental information (Table 5, Figure 2).

Connection to Prior Work
The conditional weight formula 𝑤= 𝑃(𝑐| pb, pat, ci)/𝑃(𝑐| pb, ci) is structurally a local density
ratio estimate.
The conditioning on (pb, ci) controls for known predictors of accuracy, leaving
pattern as the residual signal.

WRF’s global tree weight captures overall tree quality but cannot
adapt per instance. On our 30-dataset benchmark, WRF wins on more datasets than it loses (18 vs

measure, and conditions on predicted class to control class bias by construction.
8
Conclusion
Near the decision boundary, individual trees in a random forest differ in reliability depending on
the structural pattern of their decision paths—and which pattern is most reliable varies by dataset
geometry and confidence region. The proposed method (Path-based Adaptive Weighting) extracts
this signal by conditioning on predicted class to remove a structural class confound, yielding a
weighting scheme with E[𝑤| pb, ci] = 1 that guarantees no systematic class bias.
Experiments on 30 binary classification datasets show statistically significant accuracy im-
provement (𝑝= 0.007, wins = 21/30) with minority-recall non-regression (2/30 datasets worse
by >0.5 pp) and zero majority-recall regressions. Under the same protocol, the three established
alternatives—WRF, KNORA-Eliminate, and KNORA-Union—do not reach significance (Wilcoxon
𝑝> 0.5). KNORA-E achieves higher average minority-recall uplift but at the cost of accuracy loss
and majority-recall instability on 8/30 datasets, illustrating the selection-effect mechanism that the
class-conditional design avoids. The advantage is robust across forest sizes from 100 to 1 000 trees
(𝑝< 0.05 at all tree counts; Table 12), and the effect grows with both dataset size and tree count,
consistent with the interpretation that the signal is real but extraction is currently noise-limited
by the discrete weight table.
The main open question is whether the signal can be amplified without overfitting—currently
limited by sparse cell counts in the 10 × 6 × 2 weight table. Smoother conditional models (isotonic
regression, kernel smoothing, or an OOB-based estimation approach that avoids the CV pass) may
extract more of the available signal while reducing computational overhead.
