ABSTRACT
We report a detailed analysis on the young stellar cluster Berkeley 59 using Fermi-LAT. Using up-to-date source catalog and
background models, we found significant extended GeV emission around Berkeley 59, which can be modeled by a radial disk of
1.02◦radius with a significance of the extension of 10.6𝜎. We investigated the molecular, neutral and ionized gas content and
the hadronic origin. The gamma-ray spectrum of Berkeley 59 has a photon index of 2.88 ± 0.06. The derived gas mass from
H2 and HII around Berkeley 59 is ∼2.89 × 102𝑀⊙. We derived the relationship between cosmic ray acceleration efficiency
and diffusion coefficient. Our results suggest that the extended gamma-ray emission originates from cosmic rays accelerated by
cluster winds interacting with surrounding gas.
Key words: X-ray stars – Stellar activity – Gamma-ray sources – Star cluster
1 INTRODUCTION
The origin of galactic cosmic rays (CRs) remains unknown. For a
long time, it has been believed that the acceleration sites of Galactic
CRs are supernova remnants (SNRs) (Drury et al. 1994; Hillas 2005;
Uchiyama et al. 2007; Gabici et al. 2009). Diffusive shock accelera-
tion is considered as principal CR production mechanism for SNRs
(Celli et al. 2019; Mitchell et al. 2021). However, growing evidence
suggests that stellar clusters are also the excellent energy sources
of CRs in the Galaxy (Aharonian et al. 2019; Morlino et al. 2021;
Blasi & Morlino 2023). Most massive stars form in associations
and/or clusters, which serve as important accelerators of very-high-
energy (VHE) and ultra-high-energy (UHE) CRs (Gupta et al. 2018;
Lemoine-Goumard et al. 2025). The accelerated CRs interacting with
the surrounding ambient gas may produce the observed 𝛾-ray emis-
sions. Nevertheless, only a few stellar clusters have been identified as
VHE or UHE 𝛾-ray sources, such as Westerlund 1 (Aharonian et al.
2022), Westerlund 2 (Yang et al. 2018) and NGC 3603 (Yang & Aha-
ronian 2017). Stellar clusters hosting massive stars may experience
numerous supernova explosions that can act as VHE CR acceler-
ators (Shara & Hurley 2002; Rogers & Pittard 2013; Bykov et al.
2015). A substantial fraction of CRs should be accelerated in young
massive stellar clusters containing lots of OB stars and the super-
bubbles created by these clusters (Ackermann et al. 2011; Menchiari
et al. 2024). Superbubbles possess sufficient energy originating from
stellar winds and supernova explosions (Meyer 2024; Sushch et al.
2025). Recent advancements in multi-wavelength observations and
simulations have revealed that stellar clusters, especially their stellar
wind-driven superbubbles, may contribute significantly to the CR
budget (Zhao et al. 2024; Härer et al. 2025). Observations of stel-

Stellar clusters serve as unique laboratories for understanding the
properties of the interstellar medium (ISM) and the nature of interac-
tions between young stars and the ISM (Elmegreen & Hunter 2010;
Asahina et al. 2017; Paron et al. 2021). As such, they provide an al-
ternative testing ground for CR origin theories, potentially helping to
resolve discrepancies in the SNR-based paradigm, particularly con-
cerning the maximum particle energy. Studies of the Cygnus cocoon
and Westerlund 1 highlight the challenges in distinguishing between
leptonic and hadronic origins of high-energy emissions (Vieu & Re-
ville 2023; Blasi & Morlino 2023). The commonly invoked 1/r radial
profile, often derived from diffusion models that omit key physi-
cal processes such as CR advection, distributed acceleration sites,
and radiative losses, may not accurately represent the true CR spatial
distribution (Yang et al. 2018; Yang & Liu 2022). Alternative scenar-
ios involving CR injection at wind termination shocks or supernova
events have been shown to reproduce the 1/r profile equivalently. De-
spite these degeneracies, the increasing number of 𝛾-ray detections
coincident with stellar clusters and the emerging spectral and mor-
phological characterizations of these sources provide circumstantial
evidence supporting a significant contribution to the high-energy CR
population, although this remains to be conclusively validated (Ge
et al. 2022, 2024; Peron et al. 2025).
CRs are believed to be important regulators of the star-formation
process (VERITAS Collaboration et al. 2009; Meijerink et al. 2011).
As they travel through the ISM, CRs interact with the ambient gas
and radiation fields to produce 𝛾-ray which trace the CR population.
Therefore, 𝛾-ray emissions are among the best tools for studying CR
properties in star-forming environments (Li et al. 2025). By analyz-
ing 𝛾-ray emission in these systems, we can measure the fraction
of interacting high-energy particles as a function of the star forma-
tion rate. Efficient particle acceleration makes star-forming regions

CRs may affect the structure of molecular clouds and star forma-

dynamically important in galaxy formation and must be considered

Berkeley 59 (R.A.=0.570◦, Dec.=67.441◦) is a young (∼2 Myr)
and nearby (∼1 kpc) stellar cluster. It is located in the central region of
the OB association Cepheus OB4 surrounded by an H II region (Lata
et al. 2011; Eswaraiah et al. 2012). The younger pre-main-sequence
(PMS) stars tend to concentrate closer to the inner region than to the
outer region of the cluster, which is linked to the time evolution of
star-forming clouds (Panwar et al. 2018). Considering the central part
of the radius ∼4 arcmin (∼1.2 pc), the lower-limit of total mass was
estimated to be ∼103𝑀⊙, which is similar to the well-studied young
massive stellar clusters (YMSCs) Westerlund 1 and Westerlund 2.
Therefore, it serves as a typical yet unique laboratory for studying
CR acceleration in young stellar clusters due to its proximity, youth,
and multiwavelength detectability.

2 Fermi-LAT ANALYSIS
We selected all events within a 15◦× 15◦region of interest (ROI) for
4FGL J0002.1+6721 (R.A.=0.5419◦, Dec.=67.3578◦), which is the
closest Fermi-LAT source to Berkeley 59 (offset 4.8′). The Pass 8
data collected from 4 August 2008 to 30 October 2024 were used to
study the GeV emission. The analysis was performed using Fermipy
v1.2.0 and the Fermi Science Tools v11r5p3 packages. We adopted
the Galactic diffuse emission model gll_iem_v07 and isotropic extra-
galactic emission model P8_R3_V3 as diffuse background compo-
nents. The event type FRONT + BACK (evtype = 3) were used in our
study. We considered only 𝛾-ray events in the 300 MeV – 300 GeV,
applying the standard data quality selection criteria “(DATA_QUAL
> 0)&&(LAT_CONFIG==1).” The latest Fermi-LAT source catalog
(4FGL-DR4) was used to construct a source model (Ballet et al.
2023). To minimize contamination from 𝛾-ray from the Earth’s limb,
the maximum zenith angle is of 95◦was adopted. The spectral pa-
rameters of sources within 5◦were set free.

4 COSMIC-RAY INJECTION
The hadronic origin has been discussed in for several YSMCs (Yang
et al. 2018; Sun et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2022; Ge et al. 2022; Liu et al.
2024). Such 𝛾-ray emission may originate from the decay of neutral
pions produced by the interactions between the accelerated hadrons
and the surrounding gas. Therefore, we explore the scenario in which
the 𝛾-ray emission is produced via the pion-decay process from the
interaction of the CRs with the ambient gas. As provided by Section
3, the average number density of the target protons for this region is
99.41 cm−3.
If Berkeley 59 can accelerate protons to high energies, then
these protons could illuminate the molecular clouds around them
via proton–proton interaction. We assume an exponential cutoff
power-law spectrum for the parent proton distribution: 𝑓p(𝐸) =
𝐴p(𝐸/𝐸0)−𝛼p exp(−(𝐸/𝐸c). We use NAIMA package to fit the SED
(Zabalza 2015). Considering the distance of about 1 kpc, the best-
fit proton index 𝛼p = 2.91 ± 0.05 and the total proton energy
𝑊p = 1.51 × 1049 erg above 1 GeV are obtained. The model with
best-fit parameters are shown in Figure 4.
We also tested the potential leptonic scenario of Berkeley 59. It
reflects the 𝛾-ray generated via the inverse Compton (IC) scattering
of electrons off the seed photons around the source. We considered
the CMB radiation field, optical to UV radiation field from the star
light as photon field, and the dust infrared radiation field Kafex-
hiu et al. (2014). In addition, nonthermal bremsstrahlung radiation
resulting from the interaction between relativistic particles and ther-
mal particle population may contribute 𝛾-ray emissions (Baring et al.
1999). Accordingly, we calculated the IC and bremsstrahlung spec-
trum using NAIMA as we did for hadronic scenario. We assumed a
exponential cutoff power-law distribution of the relativistic electrons:
𝑓e(𝐸) = 𝐴e(𝐸/𝐸0)−𝛼e exp(−(𝐸/𝐸c). The best-fit electron index of
electron distribution is 𝛼e = 2.19 ± 0.04. We obtain the total energy
above 1 GeV: 𝑊e = 2.24 × 1049 erg. We emphasize that the leptonic
origin of Berkeley 59 can not be ruled out.

The estimation of the CR injection efficiency and the diffusion
coefficient in Berkeley 59 indicates that the stellar winds from the
OB stars can provide the necessary energy for CR acceleration. The
derived diffusion coefficient is on the order of 1027 cm2 s−1, which is
slower than the Galactic average, suggesting that the CRs are confined
better in the cluster environment. This enhanced confinement could
be due to the turbulent magnetic fields in the cluster, which facilitates
the acceleration of CRs to high energies. However, the uncertainties
in the effective gas density and the acceleration efficiency require
further observations to refine.
Considering the kinetic energy injection rate 𝐿w = 9.62 ×
1035 erg s−1 and the age should be 𝜏≈2 Myr for Berkeley 59,
the total energy injected is obtained as 6.07 × 1049 erg. Thus, this
YSMC is powerful enough to accelerate CRs to account for the 𝛾-ray
emissions detected by Fermi-LAT .
5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
The estimated CR acceleration efficiency of 𝜂CR = 0.1−0.4 and diffu-
sion coefficient 𝐷∼2.5×1027 cm2 s−1 place Berkeley 59 in the con-
text of other 𝛾-ray emitting stellar clusters. This diffusion coefficient
is significantly lower than the Galactic average (∼1028 cm2 s−1), sug-
gesting enhanced CR confinement within the cluster environment,
possibly due to turbulent magnetic fields amplified by stellar winds
and supernova activity. Such conditions are conducive to efficient
particle acceleration and 𝛾-ray production.
We report the extended 𝛾-ray emissions around the stellar cluster
