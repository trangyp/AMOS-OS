Decision-Path Patterns as Tree Reliability Signals:
Path-based Adaptive Weighting for Random Forest Classification
Youngjoon Park
yj.david.park@gmail.com
Abstract
The global uniform aggregation of random forests leaves conditional bias along the decision
boundary uncorrected.
To correct this locally, we propose exploiting the structural pattern
of each tree’s decision path. At inference, a random forest reaches its prediction through the
root-to-leaf path the sample traverses in each tree, so path-level reliability offers a finer granu-
larity than tree-level weighting can access. We show that reliability varies meaningfully across
path patterns in the boundary region identified by the forest itself, and that using this signal
yields a statistically significant accuracy improvement over RF on 36 binary classification bench-
marks (Wilcoxon 𝑝< 0.0001). We further devise a way to measure the sufficiency of residual
information in the fitted RF’s decision boundary, providing an estimate of the expected gain
before the method is applied; on the qualifying group identified this way, the method delivers a
mean +0.99 pp accuracy improvement with strict wins on every dataset (7 / 0 / 0). Class-recall
regression—the typical failure mode of RF correction methods—is measured: zero minority-
recall regressions and a single majority-recall regression at the 0.2 pp threshold, indicating that
the correction operates in the direction of bias reduction rather than class trade-off. Our work
suggests that the structural information of decision paths, previously overlooked in random
forest research, can contribute to RF performance improvement.

Introduction
Random forests construct a tree pool by injecting randomness into split selection and bootstrap
sampling, producing an ensemble whose averaged vote approximates the conditional class proba-
bility 𝑃(𝑌| 𝑋) and the decision boundary it implies [Breiman, 2001]. Aggregation averages each
tree’s leaf-level decision with uniform per-tree weight, which is globally unbiased on average but
leaves local conditional bias along the boundary uncorrected.
Uniform averaging is equivalent to assuming that every tree is equally reliable for every sample.
In practice this is rarely true: a tree may be well-calibrated in one region of feature space and poorly
so in another, even when it lands at the same leaf. The root-to-leaf decision path—through which
a tree narrowed down to its leaf—carries information about how the tree arrived at its prediction.
Two trees landing in the same leaf with the same class probability are treated identically by uni-
form aggregation, even when one traversed a stable path and the other oscillated through several
majority-class reversals. This paper asks whether that structural difference reflects a difference in
per-sample reliability, and whether that signal can be exploited to refine the ensemble decision in
practice.
Prior approaches that move beyond uniform aggregation typically gain on one axis at the cost
of another.
Label-conditioned dynamic ensemble selection (e.g., KNORA-Eliminate) and class-
weighted training can lift minority-class recall but at the cost of majority-class regressions on a
substantial fraction of datasets, since their selection or weighting signal is itself class-correlated.

Static OOB tree weighting (WRF) is class-agnostic but instance-blind, so it cannot adapt to local
boundary structure.
Both lines of work assign weights at the tree level, yet at inference each
tree contributes only through the single root-to-leaf path the sample traverses—so the per-sample
active aggregation unit is the path, not the tree. A reliability signal derived from path structure—a
tree-internal property of how a tree partitions the feature space—is in principle class-agnostic and
instance-adaptive, and so offers the prospect of refining the ensemble decision near the boundary
without trading one class against another.
This paper investigates how the structural pattern of a tree’s decision path informs the reliability
of its leaf-level prediction. We show empirically that this structure carries a per-tree reliability
signal, but one that applies differently depending on the tree’s predicted class and on the sample’s
location in feature space—which we capture through the forest’s consensus on the sample as a
proxy. To turn this signal into an aggregation weight without re-introducing the class bias seen
in prior work, we adopt a class-conditional weighting scheme that guarantees E[𝑤| pb, ci] = 1 by
construction.
Contributions.
1. Path structure as a reliability signal.
Empirical demonstration that the structural
pattern of a tree’s decision path carries information about per-tree prediction reliability, but
only when interpreted jointly with the tree’s predicted class and the sample’s confidence
region—no single pattern dominance generalises across these axes.
2. Class confound and its resolution. Identification of a structural class confound in naive
path-based weighting (minority- predicting trees produce more flip-heavy paths for structural
reasons unrelated to reliability) and its resolution via a class-conditional ratio formulation
with E[𝑤| pb, ci] = 1 by construction.
3. Path-based Adaptive Weighting method. A concrete weighting scheme instantiating
the above signal, with a six-type flip pattern taxonomy and a per-(region, class) weight table
estimated via cross-validation.
4. Evaluation on 36 binary classification datasets. Statistically significant accuracy gain
over RF (Wilcoxon 𝑝< 0.0001, Δ = +0.0026) with zero minority-recall regressions and a
single majority-recall regression at the 0.2 pp threshold (Table 8), robust across forest sizes
(100–1 000 trees); the 19 out-of-design-loop datasets give Δ = +0.0027 with Wilcoxon 𝑝=
0.0011 (Table 10).
5. Applicability prediction from the fitted RF. Two indicators read off the fitted RF’s
OOB by-products—boundary mass 𝑀and pattern-accuracy spread 𝑆—jointly predict the
per-dataset gain (Pearson 𝑟= +0.840, 𝑝< 0.0001), allowing the expected effectiveness to
be estimated before the method is applied; on the qualifying group identified this way, the
method delivers a mean +0.99 pp gain with strict wins on every dataset (7/0/0).

Related Work
2.1
Path-based interpretation
Decision paths have been used primarily for post-hoc explanation rather than for ensemble weight-
ing.
TreeSHAP [Lundberg et al., 2020] computes exact Shapley-value feature attributions by
2


## Page 3


traversing the path each tree takes for a given input, yielding per-feature contributions to a predic-
tion. Such methods use the path to decompose what a tree predicted, not to assess how reliably it
predicted. Other work has encoded path information as input features. Cohen-Shapira and Rokach
[2024] propose PnT, which selects informative paths from a forest via statistical testing and uses
the selected paths as additional input features for refitting a born-again tree or forest. The path
is treated as a global rule whose general usefulness is assessed once and then applied uniformly
to all samples; this differs from the present work, where the path’s structural pattern serves as
a per-sample reliability signal conditioned on the tree’s prediction and the sample’s location. To
our knowledge, the sequential pattern of node-majority-class labels along the decision path has not
been used as a reliability signal for ensemble aggregation.
2.2
Tree weighting in random forests
A separate line of work modifies the uniform per-tree aggregation of standard RF. We group these
methods by whether the weight depends on the test instance.
2.2.1
Static (instance-independent) weighting
Winham et al. [2013] proposed weighted Random Forest (WRF), exploring several OOB-based tree-
weighting schemes; we adopt 𝑤𝑡∝1/(1 −OOB_acc𝑡) (their 1/tPE𝑗form) as our static-weighting
baseline—the simplest non-trivial variant in their set. Because the weight does not depend on the
test instance, it cannot adapt to local boundary structure.
2.2.2
Instance-adaptive weighting
A line of instance-adaptive approaches replaces RF’s uniform aggregation with weights or selections
that depend on the test instance. The KNORA family [Ko et al., 2008]—the standard reference
for dynamic ensemble selection (DES)—weights or selects trees per test instance based on nearest-
neighbour competence in the training set: KNORA-Eliminate (KNE) retains only trees that cor-
rectly classify all 𝑘nearest neighbours (falling back to a reduced neighbourhood if none qualify),
while KNORA-Union (KNU) weights trees by the number of correctly classified neighbours. We
use both as our instance-adaptive baselines. A known concern is that label-conditioned local se-
lection can introduce minority-recall harm when the selection signal is class-correlated—the same
confound the proposed method resolves by design.
Other instance-adaptive approaches estimate per-tree weights from signals other than nearest-
neighbour accuracy. Park [2023] combined prediction-domain coverage with neighbourhood struc-
ture to estimate per-sample tree reliability post-hoc for RF aggregation. Bertsimas and Cui [2025]
propose Adaptive Forests using mixed-integer optimisation via the Optimal Predictive-Policy Trees
(OP2T) framework, and Bertsimas and Stoumpou [2024] propose Enhanced Random Forests, deriv-
ing personalised tree weights from iterative sample-difficulty estimation. The present paper differs
from all of these in two respects: the weighting signal is tree-internal (extracted from the decision
path itself rather than from external neighbourhood, optimisation, or difficulty estimates), and the
weight formula is class-conditional, removing the class confound that label-conditioned methods
inherit by construction.

Framework: Decision-Path Patterns
This section makes the intuition introduced in Section 1 operational by (i) extracting the path
label sequence (Section 3.1), (ii) classifying its flip pattern (Section 3.2), and (iii) defining a region
variable that controls for proximity to the forest’s decision boundary (Section 3.3).
3.1
Decision Path Structure
For any sample 𝑥and tree 𝑡, let 𝑛0, 𝑛1, . . . , 𝑛𝑑be the sequence of nodes traversed from root to
leaf. Define the path label sequence as 𝐿= (ℓ0, ℓ1, . . . , ℓ𝑑) where ℓ𝑘= arg max𝑐value[𝑛𝑘][𝑐], i.e., the
majority class at each node. A flip occurs at position 𝑘if ℓ𝑘̸= ℓ𝑘−1. The pattern of flips—where
they occur and how many times the majority class reverses—characterises the path’s structural
complexity.
Note that the path label sequence is a property of the tree, not the sample: a given leaf always
has the same root-to-leaf path. This enables efficient implementation by pre-computing the pattern
for each leaf once per tree (see Section 5.3).

reversals. Table 1 summarises the six pattern types.

3.3
Region Variable
We partition the prediction space using forest-level probability: for tree 𝑡, fp𝑡(𝑥) = ^𝑃RF(𝑥)[pred𝑡] ∈
[0, 1] is the forest’s probability for the class that tree 𝑡predicts, bucketed into 10 equal-width
intervals of width 0.10. It measures how strongly the forest backs tree 𝑡’s call: values near 1 mean
the forest agrees with the tree, values near 0.5 place the sample at the decision boundary, and values
near 0 mean the forest dissents from tree 𝑡. Keyed to the tree’s own predicted class, the region
refers to the same decision as that class, so the (region, class) conditioning cells stay coherent—a
property we exploit in Section 4.3 to isolate the within-cell pattern signal without an agree/dissent
confound.

interiors. We therefore adopt the per-tree forest probability fp𝑡(𝑥) = ^𝑃RF(𝑥)[pred𝑡] as the region

Application: Path-based Adaptive Weighting

Implementation
The path label sequence from root to leaf is a fixed property of each leaf—it does not depend on the
test sample. We exploit this by pre-computing the pattern for every leaf in each tree via a single
depth-first traversal after training. At prediction time, only the leaf index is needed; the pattern
is then a constant-time array lookup. This avoids per-sample decision-path traversal, reducing the
cost of pattern lookup to 𝑂(1).
The weight table 𝑊has 10 × 6 × 2 = 120 entries and is stored as a small 3D array. The total
overhead beyond standard RF prediction is: one apply() call per tree, one array lookup per tree,
and one weighted sum per sample.

6.5
Where and How It Works
The mean accuracy gain (+0.0026) hides large heterogeneity across the 36 datasets, from +0.0273
(mammographic-mass) down to small losses near zero. The mechanism in Section 4 predicts where
the gain is recoverable: the proposed weight 𝑤= 𝑃(correct | pb, pat, ci)/𝑃(correct | pb, ci) moves
the prediction only on tree–sample pairs whose forest probability fp𝑡(𝑥) lies in the boundary region
[0.4, 0.6), and within that region the weight can lift a tree only by as much as the pattern-accuracy
spread allows. We therefore define two per-dataset indicators, both read off the fitted RF’s free
OOB by-product (rf.oob_decision_function_); no test labels and no weighted prediction are
required:
• Boundary mass (𝑀): fraction of training samples whose OOB-only forest probability for
the winning class lies in [0.4, 0.6). Measures how much of the prediction load the proposed
weighting can act on.
• Boundary spread (𝑆): within the boundary sample set, the max−min pattern accuracy
across the six flip patterns over the OOB trees, averaged over the two predicted-class sub-
groups. Measures how much pattern-conditional signal the boundary actually carries; col-
lapses toward zero when the boundary is noise-dominated.
Empirical mechanism.
Table 17 reports 𝑀, 𝑆, and the product 𝑀· 𝑆for every dataset, sorted
by 𝑀· 𝑆(descending). The product is strongly correlated with Δacc (Pearson 𝑟= +0.840, 𝑝<

but neither is sufficient—and the multiplicative interaction is what predicts the gain.
Where the gain concentrates.
The strongest accuracy gains arise where both factors are
present. Datasets that combine non-trivial boundary mass with high boundary spread—mammographic-

is too thin for the weight to accumulate effect, and when spread is small (german-credit, spect,

Discussion
7.1
Effect Size and Conditions for Gain
The effect of the method is not uniform across datasets but is differentiated by a measurable pre-
condition. Section 6.5 identifies two regimes empirically through the boundary mass 𝑀and pattern-
accuracy spread 𝑆, whose product explains most of the per-dataset variance in gain (Pearson
𝑟= +0.840): datasets where RF has left exploitable residual information in the boundary, and
datasets where it has not.
In the top 𝑀· 𝑆quintile, where the residual signal is present, the
method delivers a +0.99 pp accuracy improvement with strict wins on every dataset (7 / 0 / 0). In
the bottom quintile (𝑀·𝑆≤0.0028), where the signal is largely absent, the gain is near zero (mean
−0.0001, 4 wins / 1 tie / 3 losses), indicating no systematic harm.
The class-conditional ratio weight 𝑤= 𝑃(correct | pb, pat, ci)/𝑃(correct | pb, ci) is a local
density ratio in which the (pb, ci) conditioning controls for known predictors of accuracy, leaving
pattern as the residual signal; when no such residual signal remains, the weight collapses toward
1 and aggregation reverts to RF’s behaviour, which is why no systematic harm appears in the
low-𝑀· 𝑆regime. The tree-count experiment (Table 12) supports this interpretation: gains are
sustained across forest sizes (+0.0022 at 100 trees, +0.0024 at 1 000 trees), and the size stratification
(Table 11) shows comparable mean gain across small and large datasets (+0.0023 vs +0.0028),
confirming that signal extraction is stable wherever the signal exists. Pooled across all 36 datasets
the mean gain is +0.0026, though this aggregate mixes the two regimes and is best read alongside
the per-quintile breakdown above.
7.2
Recall Non-regression
Beyond the conditional effectiveness shown above, the conditional weighting design (E[𝑤| pb, ci] =
1) also prevents systematic minority-class harm. At the 0.2 pp threshold, the proposed method has
the lowest recall-regression rates of any comparator on both classes (0/36 minority, 1/36 majority;
Table 8; the single majority regression is jm1 at −0.0025), so the accuracy gain operates in the
direction of conditional bias reduction rather than a one-sided class trade-off. Every other com-
parator is materially worse, especially on majority recall (WRF 4/36, KNE 15/36, KNU 4/36). All
are substantially better than naive weighting (30/36 datasets worse by >0.2 pp on minority without
class conditioning; Table 13).
The comparison with KNE is particularly instructive.
KNE achieves the highest average
minority-recall uplift (+0.0100) but destabilises both classes: majority recall worsens on 15/36

7.3
Limitations
Despite the favourable properties above, several caveats qualify the present results.
Researcher degrees of freedom.
Method design (forest probability as region, 10-bucket reso-
lution, MIN_N = 30, conditional ratio formula) was iterated with reference to diagnostic results
on the original 17 datasets. The reported in-design 𝑝should therefore be interpreted alongside the
out-of-design-loop replication on the 19 datasets not consulted during method design (Wilcoxon
𝑝= 0.0011, Table 10), which provides cleaner evidence.
Pattern and region discretisation.
The six-way pattern taxonomy (flip position thresholds at
1/3 and 2/3) and the 10-bucket region partition are design choices that prioritise interpretability
and stable cell counts for the diagnostic analyses in Section 4.3–Table 7, not derived from an
optimality criterion. Joint optimisation of taxonomy and bucket resolution could yield stronger
predictive gains, at the cost of additional researcher degrees of freedom.
CV overhead.
Weight table estimation requires five additional RF fits at training time, one per
CV fold, on top of the final RF that produces the deployed predictor. An OOB-based alternative
that avoids the CV pass entirely is a natural extension.
Binary classification only.
The class conditioning (ci ∈{0, 1}) does not directly extend to
multi-class settings; conditioning on the tree’s predicted class index in 𝐾-ary classification is a
natural extension.

8
Conclusion
We propose Path-based Adaptive Weighting, which reads a reliability signal off each tree’s root-
to-leaf decision path and uses it as a class-conditional ratio weight in random forest aggregation.
The formulation E[𝑤| pb, ci] = 1 by construction prevents systematic class bias—a recurring
failure mode of label-conditioned weighting schemes—and reduces the conditional bias that uniform
aggregation leaves uncorrected along the decision boundary.
On 36 binary classification benchmarks the method achieves a statistically significant accuracy
improvement over RF (Wilcoxon 𝑝< 0.0001; 29 wins, 2 ties, 5 losses; Table 8) with zero minority-
recall regressions and a single majority-recall regression at the 0.2 pp threshold; this result replicates
on the 19 datasets not consulted during method design (Wilcoxon 𝑝= 0.0011, Table 10). Under
the same protocol, WRF, KNORA-Eliminate, and KNORA-Union all fail to reach significance
(𝑝> 0.12). The effect is robust across forest sizes from 100 to 1,000 trees (Table 12) and across
dataset sizes (Table 11).
The effect of the method is not uniform but conditional. Two indicators read off the fitted RF’s
OOB by-products—boundary mass 𝑀and pattern-accuracy spread 𝑆—jointly predict per-dataset
gain (Pearson 𝑟= +0.840): the gain rises monotonically across 𝑀· 𝑆quintiles, from near zero in
the bottom quintile (mean −0.0001, 4/1/3) to +0.99 pp in the top quintile with strict wins on every
dataset (7/0/0). Where the residual signal is absent, the weight collapses toward 1 and aggregation
reverts to RF’s behaviour, so the method does not systematically harm performance on datasets
where there is nothing to recover. The applicability of the method is thus measurable from the
fitted RF before the method itself is applied.
A complementary perspective concerns the level at which aggregation weights are defined. At
inference, each tree contributes through a single root-to-leaf path, making the path the per-sample
active aggregation unit while trees are the training-time grouping. Tree-level weighting schemes
(e.g., WRF) operate at a coarser granularity than this active unit. The present work intervenes at
the path-derived level via path-pattern properties, accessing refinements that tree-level approaches
cannot.
The structural information encoded in decision paths has been largely overlooked in random
forest research. Whether richer path representations, smoother conditional models (isotonic regres-
sion, kernel smoothing), or OOB-based weight estimation can extract more of this signal remains
open; the present work establishes that the signal is real, the correction is safe, and its applicability
can be measured in advance.
