Distance Reduction in Bouquet Decompositions
and Toric Ideals of Graphs
Oliver Clarke, Dimitra Kosta and Alexander Milner
Abstract
The distance-reduction property for a generating set, i.e., a Markov basis, of a toric
ideal is a condition that ensures tight connectivity of its fibres. In this paper, we study
the distance-reduction property for toric ideals of graphs and move on to explore the
relationship between the distance-reduction property and the bouquet structure of ho-
mogeneous toric ideals, which includes the class of toric ideals of graphs. For toric
ideals of graphs which are complete intersection, we show that the minimal Markov
bases are distance-reducing if and only if they distance-reduce the circuits of the ideal.
We then consider how the distance-reduction properties interact with the bouquet
structure of the toric ideal. Bouquets are a combinatorial structure that capture the
essential combinatorial information of the toric ideal. Under the condition of homo-
geneity, we show that, for toric ideals with the same bouquet structure and signature,
the distance-reduction properties are preserved. For homogeneous toric ideals whose
bouquet matrix is a monomial curve in A3, we give necessary and sufficient conditions
for when the minimal Markov bases are distance-reducing.

Introduction
A fundamental problem in Algebraic Statistics is the construction of Markov bases for toric
ideals. Given an integer matrix A, a Markov basis corresponds to a minimal generating set
of the toric ideal IA, and its elements can be considered as moves of a Markov chain that
connects any two points in the same fiber of A (see [9]). The application of this Markov chain
is in sampling from the space of so-called contingency tables, which may be used in Fisher’s
exact test in statistical significance testing. In practice, we not only want connectivity in
the fibers but a quick convergence rate of the Markov chain as well, i.e., a low mixing time.
This is the idea behind the distance-reduction property, introduced by Aoki and Takemura
[2, 1], which requires a Markov basis M to reduce a certain metric distance between points
in a fiber. In this case, the diameter of the fibers can be bounded from above in terms of
their fiber values [2, Section 3.1] and these diameters can, in turn, be used to bound the time
for Markov chains to reach their stationary distribution (see [9] or [8]). In particular, we will
focus on the metric given by the 1-norm on the fibers, which is not only easy to compute,
but also has nice properties when working with homogeneous toric ideals.
Understanding which Markov bases are distance-reducing is a subtle question. By [5], for
a general integer matrix A, checking whether a Markov basis M is distance-reducing requires
verifying the condition against every element of the Graver basis Gr(A), the set of primitive
lattice vectors in kerZ(A). It follows immediately that the Graver basis is always distance-
reducing, but it is typically orders of magnitude larger than any given Markov basis. So,
a natural goal is to identify structured families of toric ideals for which distance reduction
can be certified by checking an easily accessible set of elements. For instance, in this paper,
we will show that the circuits of A, which are the elements of IA of inclusion-wise minimal
support, are sufficient for testing distance reduction in the case of complete intersection toric
ideals of graphs.
In this paper, we study the distance-reduction property from two perspectives. Firstly, we
consider toric ideals of graphs and leverage their combinatorial characterisation to study the
distance-reduction property of their Markov bases. Secondly, we consider how the bouquet
structure of a homogeneous toric ideal affects the distance-reduction property of its Markov
bases. Homogeneity is a unifying feature of both perspectives, as toric ideals of graphs are
homogeneous.
Toric ideals of graphs. Toric ideals of graphs, their generating sets as well as several
binomial sets of interest have been studied in a series of papers; the circuits of toric ideals
associated to graphs were characterised by Villarreal in [22], the Graver basis of the toric
ideals of a graph was first investigated by Ohsugi and Hibi in [14], while the explicit structure
of its elements was later established by Reyes, Tatakis and Thoma in [17]. Moreover, in [13]
De Loera, Sturmfels and Thomas determined the universal Gr¨obner basis for toric ideals
of graphs with at most eight vertices, while Tatakis and Thoma gave a graph theoretical
characterisation of the elements of the universal Gr¨obner basis of the toric ideal of any graph
in [21].
The incidence matrix AG of a simple graph G provides a natural class of 0/1-matrices,
each of whose columns contains exactly two ones. When it comes to distance-reduction, toric
ideals of graphs are the natural next class to study after the trivial case with exactly one 1 in
each column, and before the full generality of matrices with at most three ones per column,
where any bouquet structure can appear, see [16]. Note that, since every column has exactly
two ones, the toric ideal of AG is homogeneous. We note that, for a homogeneous ideal, by

Theorem (Theorem 3.14). Let G be a simple connected graph such that IG is a complete
intersection, and let M be a minimal Markov basis for IG. Then M is distance-reducing if
and only if M distance-reduces the circuits of AG.
The statement of Theorem 3.14 is motivated by [5, Corollary 5.2] where it is shown that,
for A ∈Z1×n, which gives rise to a monomial curve, such that IA is a complete intersection

the circuits of A. The proof of this result involves exploiting the specific form of minimal
Markov bases of complete intersection monomial curves. Analogously, we prove Theorem
3.14 using a result by Tatakis and Thoma, restated in Theorem 3.12, about the structure of
minimal Markov bases of complete intersection toric ideals coming from graphs. However,
we suspect that the complete intersection hypothesis can be dropped from this theorem, see
Conjecture 3.1, which we have computationally verified for all graphs on at most 7 vertices

Bouquet decompositions. The bouquet structure of a toric ideal, introduced by Petrovi´c,
Thoma, and Vladoiu in [15], partitions the columns of A into subsets called bouquets. Each
of these bouquets is said to be free, non-mixed, or mixed according to its signature. This
decomposition captures a great deal of the essential combinatorial structure of IA.
For
instance, it is known that two matrices that share the same bouquet ideal and signature have
isomorphic sets of circuits, Graver bases, indispensable elements and even minimal Markov
bases, with an explicit bijection between them (see [15, 12]). We investigate the natural
question of whether the distance-reduction property is also an invariant of the bouquet ideal
and signature.
We show that the answer is no in general. See Example 4.1, which exhibits two matrices
with the same bouquet ideal and signature, each of which has two minimal Markov bases.
But, for one of these matrices both minimal Markov bases are distance-reducing while for
the other matrix only one is distance-reducing. This difference arises from the observation
that the bijection does not preserve the 1-norms of elements in fibers. However, we show
that imposing homogeneity on both toric ideals completely removes this obstruction.
Theorem (Theorem 4.9). Let IA1, IA2 be homogeneous Tω-robust ideals for some integer
matrix T and signature ω. Then, IA1 and IA2 have the same distance-reducing properties:
for ˆz, z ∈kerZ(A1), the element z is distance-reduced by ˆz if and only if ϕ(z) is distance-
reduced by ϕ(ˆz), where ϕ is a certain bijection between the kernels.
For this result, we think of the distance-reduction property in two parts: there is a
divisibility condition on positive parts and an overlapping condition on supports of negative
parts. The proof of this theorem passes through two lemmas on the poset of signatures
in which we track how these two conditions change as we move through this poset.
In

2.1
Toric ideals
Let A ∈Zd×n be an integer matrix and R = k[x1, . . . , xn] a polynomial ring over a field k.
Assume that kerZ(A) ∩Nn = {0}. For each z ∈kerZ(A), we write β(z) = xz+ −xz−for the
binomial associated to z. We define the toric ideal IA = ⟨β(z) : z ∈kerZ(A)⟩. We say that
IA (or A) is homogeneous if the vector 1n = (1, 1, . . . , 1) ∈Zn appears in the row-span of A.
The affine semigroup of A is NA := {Au ∈Zd | u ∈Nn}, which is the semigroup generated
by the columns of A. Given a monomial xu ∈R for some u ∈Nn, its A-degree is defined
to be the vector degA(xu) = Au ∈NA. For each b ∈NA, the b-fiber of A is given by
Fb := {u ∈Nn | Au = b}.
Circuits are irreducible binomials of a toric ideal IA with minimal support. In vector
notation, a vector u ∈kerZ(A) is called a circuit of the matrix A if Supp(u) is minimal and
the components of u are relatively prime. We write C(A) ⊆kerZ(A) for the set of circuits
of A.
A Markov basis M ⊆kerZ(A) for A (or for IA) is a collection of vectors such that the
set {β(z) : z ∈M} is a generating set of IA. It is minimal if this generating set is minimal
and we ignore the signs of the vectors. The intersection of all Markov bases for A is the
indispensable set, denoted by S(A), and the union of Markov bases is called the universal
Markov basis, and is denoted by U(A). For ease of notation, a set of vectors in kerZ(A), such
as a Markov basis, is denoted by a matrix whose rows are the set of vectors. We recall the
following forms of vector decomposition for exponents in toric ideals.
Definition 2.1. Fix A as above and let z, u, v ∈kerZ(A) be vectors such that z = u + v.
We say that the decomposition z = u + v is:
• proper if neither u nor v is the zero vector,
• conformal if z+ = u+ + v+ and z−= u−+ v−,
• semi-conformal if ui > 0 =⇒vi ≥0 and vi < 0 =⇒ui ≤0.
We define the Graver basis Gr(A) ⊆kerZ(A) as the set of elements that do not admit a
proper conformal decomposition. A binomial xu −xv ∈IA is called primitive if there is no
other binomial xu′ −xv′ ∈IA such that xu′|xu and xv′|xv.
Let Gr(IA) := {β(z) | z ∈Gr(A)}. The set of primitive binomials is finite and coincides
with Gr(IA). Additionally, the circuits of A are contained within the Graver basis of A. We
reference [18, Chapter 4] for both of these results.
Semi-conformal decompositions allow us to give an equivalent characterisation of the
indispensable set.
Proposition 2.2 ([4, Proposition 1.1]). The set S(A) of indispensable elements is equal to
the subset of kerZ(A) that do not admit a proper semi-conformal decomposition.

Distance reduction
The theory of distance reduction, introduced in [2], is about studying 1-norm distance be-
tween points in fibers of A. In this section we recall the basic definitions and prove some
small results that will be used throughout the paper.

Definition 2.3. For ˆz, z ∈kerZ(A), we say that z is distance-reduced by ˆz if
1. ˆz+ ≤z+
2. ∥z −ˆz∥< ∥z∥
up to the signs of ˆz and z. Explicitly, we require at least one of the following to hold:
• ˆz+ ≤z+ and ∥z −ˆz∥< ∥z∥,
• ˆz+ ≤z−and ∥z + ˆz∥< ∥z∥,
• ˆz−≤z+ and ∥z + ˆz∥< ∥z∥,
• ˆz−≤z−and ∥z −ˆz∥< ∥z∥.
We say that an element z ∈kerZ(A) is distance-irreducible if the only element of kerZ(A)
that distance-reduces z is itself. We write D(A) for the set of distance-irreducible elements.
The intuition behind Definition 2.3 comes from considering the fiber that z+ and z−lie
in. Let b := Az+ = Az−so z+ and z−lie in the fiber Fb. Then, condition (1) of Definition
2.3 tells us that ˆz is “applicable” to z i.e., applying the move ˆz to z+ keeps us in the fiber
Fb. We can see this by noting that z+ −ˆz = (z+ −ˆz+) + ˆz−∈Nn since ˆz−∈Nn and, by
condition (1) of Definition 2.3 , z+ −ˆz+ ∈Nn. Furthermore, A(z+ −ˆz) = Az+ = b since
ˆz ∈kerZ(A) so ˆz+ −ˆz lies in the fiber Fb. Then, rewriting condition (2) of Definition 2.3,
∥(z+ −ˆz) −z−∥< ∥z+ −z−∥, this is telling us that after applying the move ˆz to z+, our
new position in the fiber, which is z+ −ˆz, is closer to z−than z+ was. Thus, ˆz allows us to
actually “reduce the distance” between z+ and z−.
We will show in Proposition 2.5 that the homogeneity condition on the toric ideal IA
allows us to simplify part 2 of Definition 2.3. This all comes down to Lemma 2.4 which tells
us that every element in the same fiber has the same 1-norm. Thus, when we move from z+
to z+ −ˆz in our fiber, since the supports of z+ and z−are completely disjoint, all we need
to decrease the distance to z−is to have some overlap between the supports of ˆz and z−.
However, by condition (1) of Definition 2.3 , ˆz+ ≤z+ so, in particular, Supp(ˆz) ⊆Supp(z+).
But again, the supports of z+ and z−are completely disjoint so the only way for the support
of ˆz to overlap with z−is for the support of ˆz−to overlap with z−. Proposition 2.5 shows
that, alongside condition (1) of Definition 2.3, this is a necessary and sufficient condition for
distance reduction in the homogeneous setting.

Bouquet structure and distance reduction
Let A be an integer matrix. In [15], the bouquets {B1, . . . , Bs} of A, the bouquet matrix
AB and its associated bouquet ideal IAB are defined. These objects capture important com-
binatorial information about the toric ideal associated to A. Each bouquet is either free,
non-mixed or mixed, which we label by 0, + and −respectively and this is called the signa-
ture of the bouquets. In addition, there is a bijection between kerZ(A) and kerZ(AB) which

preserves elements of the Graver basis and the circuits. In [15], the authors also present an
