ARITHMETIC DEFINABILITY OF HENSELIAN VALUATIONS:
DEFINABILITY VIA THE TILTING CORRESPONDENCE
GESSICA ALECCI, IHSANE HADEG, FRANZISKA JAHNKE, MARGARETE KETELSEN,
AND ISABELLA NEGRINI
Abstract. We show that arithmetic definability of henselian valuations is preserved by the tilting
correspondence. Moreover, we show that if a perfectoid valuation is arithmetically definable, then no
parameters are needed. We also investigate whether these definitions can be chosen uniformly, and
discuss the required quantifier complexity.
1. Introduction
The tilting equivalence is a fundamental tool in arithmetic geometry which allows one to “transfer
arithmetic” between certain fields of mixed characteristic and their positive characteristic analoga. More
precisely, tilting transfers arithmetical data between a perfectoid field of mixed characteristic and its tilt,
an associated perfectoid field of positive characteristic, and vice versa. Instances of this transfer principle
are the Fontaine-Wintenberger Theorem which asserts that the absolute Galois group of a perfectoid field
and its tilt are isomorphic as profinite groups ([FW79]), Scholze’s extension of the tilting equivalence to
perfectoid spaces which led to his proof of (many cases of) the weight-monodromy conjecture in mixed
characteristic by reduction to positive characteristic ([Sch12]), as well as Andr´e’s proof of the direct
summand conjecture which also crucially uses perfectoid spaces ([And18]).
These results give rise to the model-theoretic question how much of the first-order structure of per-
fectoid fields (in the language of rings or in the language of valued fields, see Section 2.2 for definitions)
is preserved by the tilting equivalence. We now give a rough overview of what is known in this respect
so far: Kartas shows in [Kar24] that as long as K is a computable untilt of K♭(in an appropriate sense
defined in [Kar24]), then decidability of ThLval(K♭, v♭) implies decidability of ThLval(K, v). Jahnke and
Kartas show in [JK25] that tilting preserves elementary equivalence, as well as a version for untilting
(which requires working over a base field). Moreover, they also obtain a converse to Kartas’ results above,
showing that decidability of ThLval(K, v) implies decidability of ThLval(K♭, v♭). A key result of [JK25]
is that – up to elementary equivalence – any nonpricipal ultrapower (KU, vU) of a perfectoid field (K, v)
admits a coarsening w of vU such that (KU, w) is tame and such that (K♭, v♭) embeds elementarily into
the residue field of (KU, w). This implies immediately that being Ci in the sense of Lang (which is an
Lring-elementary property) tilts. The converse of this was investigated by Kartas in [Kar26], but is still
open in full generality. In [GK26], Gambardella and Kartas show that having the strong C0
1-property
untilts. When perfectoid fields are considered in continuous logic (rather than in discrete first-order
logic), Rideau-Kikuchi, Scanlon and Simon ([RKSS25]) show that the tilting equivalence can be seen as
a bi-interpretation in continuous logic.
The present work adds to the picture described above. By definition, perfectoid fields come equipped
with a rank-1 valuation with respect to which they are complete. In particular, the valuation is henselian.
Nontrivial henselian valuations are often so closely related to the arithmetic of the underlying field that
they are encoded in it, i.e., that their valuation ring is first-order definable in the language of rings. A
classical example which goes back to J. Robinson is that, for p ̸= 2, the formula
φp(x) ≡∃y(y2 = 1 + px2)
defines the p-adic valuation on the p-adic numbers, i.e., it is satisfied exactly by those p-adic numbers
which are p-adic integers. In this note, we investigate when a perfectoid valuation is Lring-definable (with
or without parameters; we refer to the latter as being ∅-definable), and obtain a complete classification
as our main result:

2.1.3. Tame fields. Tame fields were been introduced and studied by Franz-Viktor Kuhlmann in [Kuh16].

Definition 2.2. A henselian valued field (K, v) is tame if
(1) (K, v) is defectless,
(2) Kv is perfect, and
(3) vK is p-divisible, if char(Kv) = p > 0.
In the following, we will often encounter tame valued fields with divisible value group, i.e., for each

Example 2.3. Let k be a perfect field of positive characteristic and let Γ be a p-divisible ordered abelian

a canonical way to obtain maximally valued fields of equal characteristic with a given value group and
residue field. For more details see [Efr06, Section 2.8].
2.1.4. Perfectoid fields and tilting. We now give the definitions for the key objects in this paper: perfec-

Definition 2.4. A valued field (K, v) of residue characteristic char(Kv) = p > 0 is perfectoid if all of

(1) vK has rank 1, i.e. vK ≤R, and (K, v) is complete,
(2) Ov/pOv is semi-perfect, that is the Frobenius map Ov/pOv →Ov/pOv, x 7→xp is surjective, and

2.2. Model-theoretic background. In this section, we give a very brief introduction into the model-
theoretic concepts which appear throughout the paper. For more details, we refer the reader to [Hod93]
and [Hod97]. Throughout this section, let L be a first-order language and M and N and L-structures.
We write M ≡L N if M and N are elementarily equivalent as L-structures, i.e., the same L-sentences
hold in M and N. If M is an L-substructure of N, we write M ⪯L N in case the given inclusion of M
into N is elementary, that is for all L-formula φ(x), where x is finite tuple of variables of length |x| and
all m ∈M |x|, we have
M |= φ(m) ⇐⇒N |= φ(m).
We tend to drop the subscript L if the language is clear from the context. For an L-theory T, i.e.,
whenever T is a set of L-sentences, we write M |= T to indicate that any ψ ∈T holds true in M.
2.2.1. Definability. We say that a subset A ⊆M n is definable if there is an L-formula φ(x, y), where x
and y are finite tuples of variables of length |x| = n and |y| respectively, and some b ∈M |y|, such that
for any a ∈M n, we have
a ∈A ⇐⇒M |= φ(a, b),
holds, in which case we also say that φ(x, b) defines A. We write φ(M n, b) for the set of elements of M n
which satisfy φ(x, b).
For a subset B of M we call φ(x, b) a B-formula if b ∈B|y| and call b the parameters needed to define
the set φ(M |x|, b). Moreover, we call φ(x, y) an ∃-formula (resp. ∀-formula etc.) if it is equivalent to
a formula in prenex normal form with precisely these quantifiers. We then say that A is B-definable,
∃-B-definable etc. if there exists a corresponding formula defining A. If B = {b} is a singleton, we use
B-definability and b-definability synonymously. A crucial case for us is when B = ∅, in which case we
say that the corresponding set is defined without parameters.

2.2.3. Some model theory of valued fields. In this paper, we consider (valued) fields in the language
Lring = {0, 1, +, −, ·} of rings and in the language Lval = Lring ∪{O} of valued rings, where O is a unary
relation symbol interpreted as the valuation ring. Given fields K and L, we often write K ≡L as a
shorthand for K ≡Lring L, and when (K, v) and (L, w) are valued fields we similarly write (K, v) ≡(L, w)
for (K, v) ≡Lval (L, w).
We say that a valuation v on K is definable if Ov is a definable subset of K in the language Lring of
rings, i.e., there is an Lring-formula φ(x, y) and a tuple c ∈K|y| such that Ov = φ(K, c) in which case we
also say that φ(x, c) defines v. If a valuation is ∅-definable, then the same formula defines a valuation w
in any L ≡Lring K, and for any such L and w we have (K, v) ≡Lval (L, w). In particular, properties like
henselianity carry over from (K, v) to (L, w). See [FJ17] for a survey on definable henselian valuations.
3. (Un)titling Definability
In this section, we show our main result (Theorem 3.15): if (K, v) is a perfectoid field of mixed
characteristic (0, p) and its tilt is (K♭, v♭), then Ov is an Lring-definable subset of K if and only if Ov♭
is an Lring-definable subset of K♭. We also show that if v (or equivalently v♭) is Lring-definable, then no
parameters are needed, i.e., it is ∅-definable. Roughly speaking, a henselian valuation can be definable
due to its value group, its residue field or sometimes even using an extension with defect. Since the
tilting equivalence preserves value group, residue field and the existence of defect extensions, we prove
that in each of these cases definability is preserved by (un)tilting. Moreover, we show that for perfectoid
fields, definability of a valuation necessarily stems from one of the aforementioned cases.
Definability via non-divisible value groups. Let (K, v) be a perfectoid field. We start by asserting
that if the value group vK is not divisible, then v is definable. This follows from a Theorem of Hong,

state below. While the statement we give is more precise than Hong’s (who makes no statements about
uniformity or quantifier complexity), his proof yields what is stated below. We thus sketch the proof for
the convenience of the reader.

Definability via residue fields. Akin for the non-divisibility of the value group in the previous section,
we now discuss properties of the residue field which give rise to the definability of a henselian valuation.
The key concept here is t-henselianity, first introduced by Prestel and Ziegler in [PZ78], and its variants.
While the definition we give below is not the original definition by Prestel and Ziegler, these are equivalent
by [PZ78, Remark 7.11] and [Pre91, p. 203].
Definition 3.2. A field K is t-henselian if there is L ≡K such that L admits a nontrivial henselian
valuation. A field K is t-henselian of divisible-tame type if there is L ≡K such that L admits a nontrivial
tame valuation with divisible value group.
There are t-henselian fields which are neither real nor separably closed and which do not admit a
nontrivial henselian valuation [PZ78, p. 338]. The definition of being t-henselian of divisible tame-type
stems from [AJ18, Definition 4.4], where also non-henselian examples of such kind where constructed.
Example 3.3. Every algebraically closed field is t-henselian of divisible-tame type. Indeed, if K is
algebraically closed, then K ≡K((Q)), and the Hahn series valuation on K((Q)) is tame with divisible
value group.
Remark 3.4. Let K be t-henselian of divisible-tame type. Then there is an ultrafilter U on some index
set I such that the ultrapower KU admits a nontrivial tame valuation with divisible value group.
Indeed, let L ≡K such that L admits a nontrivial tame valuation w with divisible value group. By
the Keisler-Shelah isomorphism theorem, see Theorem 2.8, there is an ultrafilter U on some index set
I such that LU ∼= KU. Since tameness is an Lval-aximatizable property ([Kuh16, Lemma 4.4]), if we
consider the ultrapower (LU, wU) := (L, w)U of the valued field (L, w), the valuation wU on LU ∼= KU
will be nontrivial and tame with divisible value group by  Lo´s’s theorem, see Theorem 2.7.
Lemma 3.5. Let (K, v) be a valued field with v = v ◦w. Then v is tame with divisible value group if
and only if both v and w are tame with divisible value group.
Proof. Note that v is henselian and defectless if and only if both v and w are henselian and defectless, by
[EP05, Corollary 4.1.4] and [AJ24, Lemma 2.9]. Also the divisibility of the value group is stable under
composition as v(Kw) ≤conv Kv and wK = vK/v(Kw).
For the rest of the proof we may thus assume that v, v and w are henselian and defectless with divisible
value groups and it remains to show that the residue field of (K, v) is perfect if and only if the residue
fields of (K, w) and (Kw, v) are.
If (Kw, v) and (K, w) have perfect residue fields, then so does (K, v) since Kv = (Kw)v. Conversely,
if Kv is perfect, then so is (Kw)v = Kv. Hence (Kw, v) is tame, and tame fields are perfect, see [Kuh16,
Lemma 3.1]. Thus Kw is perfect.
□

Definability via defect. Defining valuation rings via properties of the residue field and value group is
a standard technique, initiated in the work of Julia Robinson and extensively studied ever since. This
reflects the Ax–Kochen/Ershov philosophy: in sufficiently nice settings (e.g., in equicharacteristic 0), the
first-order theory of a valued field is completely determined by those of its residue field and value group.
However, building on recent work of Kuhlmann and Rzepka, Ketelsen, Ramello and Szewczyk were able
to use Galois extensions of independent defect-type to define henselian valuations (see [KRS24] for more
details). In this section, we refine their result to show that in the rank-1 case, these definitions can be
done without parameters. In [KRS24, Corollary 4.14], the authors show that in any henselian valued
field (K, v) with char Kv = p > 0 such that (K, v) is not defectless, and such that either
(i) K is perfect, if char K = p > 0, or
(ii) Ov/p is semi-perfect, if char K = 0

5. Quantifier complexity
The previous section illustrates that asking for a uniform definition for a perfectoid valuation and its
tilt has no merit.
In this section, we focus on the quantifier complexity of such definitions instead. We
show that ∃-∅-definability and ∀-∅-definability both untilt, and that ∀-∅-definability does not tilt. The
question whether ∃-∅-definability tilts remains open.
Our approach relies on work by Anscombe and Fehm [AF17], and we begin by recalling their defini-
tions. They phrase all their definitions and statements in terms of C-fields. Here, we only consider the
special case where C = Z.
Definition 5.1 (see [AF17, Definition 3.5 and Lemma 3.7]). Let K be a field.
(1) We say that K has embedded residue if there is some K′ ≡K (in Lring) and a nontrivial valuation
v on K′ such that there is an embedding of Kv into K′.
(2) We say that K is Z-large if there is some K′ ≡K (in Lring), a subfield E ⊆K′ and a nontrivial
henselian valuation v on E such that Ev is isomorphic to K′.
In fact, by [Ans26, Lemma 3.3], a field K is Z-large if and only if it has the same existential Lring-
theory as some field admitting a nontrivial henselian valuation. Any large field in the sense of Pop is
Z-large. Anscombe and Fehm show that not having embedded residue (respectively not being Z-large)
can be used to characterize which henselian valuations are ∃-∅-definable (respectively ∀-∅-definable):
Fact 5.2 ([AF17, Corollary 5.3]). For any field F, the following are equivalent.
(0∃) The valuation ring is ∃-∅-definable in some nontrivially henselian valued field (K, v) with char K =
char Kv and Kv ≡Lring F.
(1∃) There is a ∃-∅-Lring-formula ψF (x) that defines the valuation ring in any henselian valued field
(K, v) with Kv ≡Lring F.
(2∃) F does not have embedded residue.
Also, the following are equivalent.
(0∀) The valuation ring is ∀-∅-definable in some nontrivially henselian valued field (K, v) with char K =
char Kv and Kv ≡Lring F.
(1∀) There is a ∀-∅-Lring-formula ψF (x) that defines the valuation ring in any henselian valued field
(K, v) with Kv ≡Lring F.
(2∀) F is not Z-large.
