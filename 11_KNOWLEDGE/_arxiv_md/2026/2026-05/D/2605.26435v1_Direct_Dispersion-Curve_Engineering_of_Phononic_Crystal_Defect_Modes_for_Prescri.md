Abstract
Phononic crystals enable precise manipulation of elastic wave propagation through engi-
neered bandgaps; however, designing defect states within these bandgaps for frequency-selective
applications remains a signiﬁcant challenge. Existing design approaches, including prior opti-
mization formulations, struggle to systematically resolve the competing objectives of attracting
desired defect modes to target frequencies while simultaneously repelling unwanted modes from
the bandgap region. This inability to suppress competing modes often results in spurious, un-
desired in-gap resonant modes, thereby limiting design purity.
This paper presents a novel
two-stage topology optimization framework that addresses this challenge through an innovative
multi-objective formulation based on an selection function with Gaussian weighting. In the ﬁrst
stage, the unit cell topology is optimized to create a wide bandgap around a target frequency. In
the second stage, a supercell containing a defect is optimized using a specially designed objective
function that dynamically balances mode attraction and repulsion via a selection function S(ω)
with adaptive σ parameters. This selection mechanism enables the optimizer to automatically
identify and selectively attract the most suitable defect mode while repelling competing modes
from the bandgap region, eliminating the need for manual mode tracking. Numerical examples
demonstrate that the proposed framework successfully generates phononic crystals featuring
engineered defect states that yield precisely positioned localized resonant modes at prescribed
frequencies within wide bandgaps, with potential applications in frequency-selective ﬁlters and
elastic wave manipulation devices.

Introduction
Phononic crystals (PnCs) are artiﬁcial periodic composite materials that control acoustic and elastic
wave propagation through spatial modulation of material properties [1, 2]. These structures exhibit
frequency ranges called phononic bandgaps where elastic wave propagation is forbidden regardless of
direction. This capability arises primarily from Bragg scattering when the wavelength is comparable
to the lattice constant [3]. The unique wave-blocking property has enabled diverse applications
including vibration isolation [4], acoustic ﬁltering [5], energy harvesting [6], and waveguiding [7].
Perfect phononic crystals prohibit wave propagation within bandgaps. However, intentionally
introducing defects can create modes at frequencies within the bandgap, fundamentally altering

crystals [10]. They exhibit exponentially decaying amplitude away from the defect region, trapping
elastic energy at speciﬁc locations and frequencies. Point defects produce highly localized resonant
modes enabling narrowband ﬁlters and high-sensitivity sensors [11]. Line defects create waveguiding
channels along prescribed paths [12, 13]. Recent demonstrations include phononic crystal sensors
employing Fano resonance [14], electrically tunable bandpass ﬁlters [15], and energy harvesting
devices with frequency-selective operation [16]. These developments highlight the importance of
systematic defect engineering for advanced wave manipulation.
Topology optimization has emerged as a powerful methodology for phononic crystal design.
There are mainly three principal methods that have been developed. The solid isotropic material
with penalization (SIMP) method [17] employs density-based material interpolation. The level-
set method [18] represents material boundaries through implicit functions with superior interface
clarity. The bi-directional evolutionary structural optimization (BESO) [19, 20] utilizes discrete
element removal and addition. Among these approaches, the SIMP method has gained widespread
adoption due to its computational eﬃciency, straightforward implementation, and compatibility
with gradient-based optimizers. This makes it particularly suitable for large-scale phononic crystal
design. The pioneering work of Sigmund and Jensen [21] established gradient-based frameworks for
bandgap maximization. Subsequent advances have addressed ultra-wide bandgap creation [22, 23],
optimization of bandgaps at prescribed frequencies [24, 25], multi-objective optimization balancing
bandgap width with structural constraints [26], multi-material systems [27], and multiscale hier-
archical structures [28–30]. Recent integration of machine learning and artiﬁcial intelligence has
enabled rapid inverse design through deep neural networks [31, 32] and generative models [33],
achieving signiﬁcant computational speedup. Despite remarkable progress in optimizing perfect pe-
riodic crystals, a critical gap persists: systematic research for designing defect states with topology
optimization remains largely undeveloped.
Designing optimal defect structures presents fundamentally diﬀerent challenges compared to
perfect crystal optimization. Perfect crystals focus on maximizing bandgap width. In contrast,
defect optimization must precisely position localized resonant modes at target frequencies while
suppressing unwanted competing in-gap modes elsewhere [34]. This creates a multi-objective prob-
lem. Attracting one defect mode to the target frequency must be balanced against repelling other
modes outside the bandgap. Traditional approaches rely on parametric studies and trial-and-error
methods [35]. These approaches lack systematic frameworks for multi-objective balancing. Mode
tracking during optimization presents another challenge. Eigenmode ordering changes due to eigen-
value crossing, causing unintended mode switching [36, 37]. This problem is exacerbated in supercell
formulations with overlapping dispersion curves. Furthermore, supercell analysis typically requires
3 × 3 to 7 × 7 unit cell repetitions with correspondingly increased computational cost. Existing
optimization literature addresses defect devices primarily through waveguide path optimization
[38].
A literature review reveals a critical gap at the intersection of defect physics [39] and topology
optimization [40, 41]: the need for systematic formulations addressing competing spectral goals.
Existing approaches for defect design often focus on single objectives, such as Q-factor maximization
[42] or transmittance enhancement [43]. A notable example is the two-stage framework by Zhang
et al. [44], which ﬁrst optimized a perfect bandgap and then optimized a 3 × 3 defect supercell
by maximizing the transmission rate at a target frequency.
Although this transmission-based
objective is eﬀective, it relies on computationally expensive frequency response analysis and oﬀers
only indirect control over the defect modes. Consequently, this approach lacks the capability to
actively suppress competing defect modes.
In contrast, a more eﬃcient approach based on direct dispersion-curve engineering (i.e., direct

frequency and which to repel from the bandgap, ideally through an automated selection mecha-

The primary innovation of this paper is a novel multi-objective function formulation based
on an selection function S(ω) with Gaussian weighting. This formulation automatically balances
competing objectives during defect optimization. Unlike conventional approaches requiring manual
weight speciﬁcation or Pareto front generation, the selection mechanism dynamically identiﬁes
which defect modes should be attracted to the target frequency. It also determines which modes
should be repelled from the bandgap based on spectral proximity.
The formulation consists of two objectives. The attraction objective fattract minimizes the fre-
quency distance between the nearest defect mode and the target. The repulsion objective frepel
maximizes distance between non-target modes and bandgap boundaries. The selection function
S(ω) serves as a diﬀerentiable switching mechanism between these objectives. The two-stage frame-
work ﬁrst optimizes the perfect crystal unit cell to create a wide bandgap. It then optimizes the
defect structure within a supercell. The adaptive sigma parameter strategy ensures early iterations
explore multiple modes with broad selection (larger σ). Later iterations focus on one or two promis-
ing modes with narrow selection (smaller σ). This implements coarse-to-ﬁne optimization without
gradient discontinuities. Numerical examples validate the framework’s ability to generate phononic
crystal structures with precisely positioned localized resonant modes at prescribed frequencies.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents theoretical foundations
including Bloch-Floquet theory and supercell formulation. Section 3 introduces the two-stage topol-
ogy optimization framework and provides detailed mathematical formulations of the optimization
problems for both stages. Section 4 addresses numerical implementation including material interpo-
lation, density ﬁltering, and sensitivity analysis. Section 5 presents numerical examples demonstrat-
ing the framework’s capabilities and discusses results and comparisons with conventional methods.
Finally, Section 6 provides conclusions and future research directions.

This section addresses the numerical methods necessary for solving the optimization problems

Gradient-based optimization requires three key ingredients: material
interpolation schemes that map continuous design variables to physical properties, density ﬁltering

4.1

Both optimization stages employ continuous design variables se ∈[0, 1] that must be mapped to

6
Conclusion
This study presents a two-stage topology optimization framework for designing metamaterials with
targeted defect modes within bandgaps. Unlike traditional trial-and-error defect design approaches
that rely on empirical experience and repeated iterations, our method enables autonomous design
of optimal structures.
In contrast to recent transmission-based optimization methods [44] that indirectly inﬂuence
defect mode positions through maximizing transmission rates, the present framework directly ma-
nipulates the dispersion relations ω(k) via eigenvalue optimization. This fundamental diﬀerence
in optimization paradigm yields key advantages, namely “explicit modal control”. The objective
function directly operates on the frequencies and positions of defect modes rather than on their
indirect manifestation in transmission spectra.
This direct control, enabled by the attraction fattract and repulsion frepel objectives, allows our
method to actively suppress spurious defect modes. This is a signiﬁcant challenge for transmission-
based methods, which lack selectivity and may inadvertently create multiple in-gap resonant modes
(e.g., generating undesired competing modes near the target frequency). The automated selection
function S(ω) further eliminates the need for manual mode identiﬁcation or tracking algorithms,
representing a signiﬁcant advancement toward fully autonomous defect design.
While both ap-
proaches can achieve narrow-band ﬁltering, the direct dispersion engineering paradigm provides
a more precise, transparent relationship between design variables and target frequencies, facilitat-
ing the design of high-purity ﬁlters and the systematic exploration of defect mode engineering
principles.
To address the prohibitive computational cost of monolithic optimization of entire supercells, we
decouple the problem into a sequential two-stage process: ﬁrst optimizing the background unit cell
to achieve a wide bandgap, followed by exclusive optimization of the defect cell to obtain desired
defect modes at target frequencies. This decoupling reduces computational burden while ensuring
a robust bandgap is established prior to defect engineering.
For defect cell optimization, we formulate a multi-objective function comprising both attraction
of defect modes toward target frequencies and repulsion of unwanted modes out of the bandgap. To
mitigate optimization oscillations caused by mode switching and enhance robustness, we introduce
a Gaussian-weighted selection function S(ω) that provides automatic, diﬀerentiable classiﬁcation
between modes to be attracted and those to be repelled, completely eliminating the need for manual
mode selection and tracking.
The proposed method employs adaptive strategies for three key parameters in Stage 2: the
selection bandwidth σs, the repulsion bandwidth σr, and the balance weight λ. While σr is set as
26


## Page 27


a ﬁxed fraction of the bandgap width, σs and λ employ automatic initialization and continuation
schemes.
These adaptive strategies progressively narrow the selection window to tighten mode
clustering while dynamically balancing attraction and repulsion contributions based on the evolving
modal distribution. Speciﬁcally, the selection bandwidth σs adapts to the initial defect spectrum
to capture relevant modes, then gradually contracts to focus optimization on the most promising
candidates. This adaptive approach enhances convergence robustness without extensive manual
parameter tuning, as conﬁrmed by successful convergence across diverse material systems and
frequency scales without parameter adjustment.
The continuation strategy eﬀectively prevents
premature convergence to suboptimal local minima, which is a common issue in ﬁxed-parameter
formulations.
Numerical case studies validate the method’s robustness and generalizability.
Variations in
material systems and target frequencies conﬁrm the method’s adaptability to diﬀerent material
combinations, while geometric conﬁguration variations (from 3×3 to 5×5 supercells) demonstrate
scalability to larger periodic arrays despite increased computational complexity and modal density.
Across all tests, the method achieved target frequency deviations below 3.5%, eﬀective bandgap
expansions exceeding 50% (ranging from 52% to 177%), and defect mode reduction (13.8% to 40%
depending on conﬁguration). These results demonstrate exceptional consistency in performance
metrics despite substantial variations in problem characteristics.
Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. The current formulation targets single-frequency
defect modes; extension to multi-frequency scenarios would require modiﬁcations to the selection
function or multi-objective formulations to simultaneously manage multiple target frequencies with-
out conﬂicting gradient information. Computational cost scales with supercell size as O(N3) for the
eigenvalue problem, potentially limiting application to very large periodic arrays without domain
decomposition or reduced-order modeling strategies.
In conclusion, this study establishes a systematic computational framework for engineering de-
fect states in metamaterials through topology optimization. The demonstrated versatility across
diverse material systems, frequency scales, and geometric conﬁgurations positions this methodology
as a practical tool for designing metamaterials for vibration control, wave manipulation, and energy
harvesting applications. The framework’s dual capability to achieve high-precision frequency tar-
geting (below 3.5% deviation) alongside substantial bandgap expansion (>50% increase) makes it
particularly suitable for applications requiring both frequency selectivity and broadband isolation
characteristics.
