ABSTRACT
Context. δ Scuti stars are pulsating stars constituting the δ Scuti instability strip in the Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) diagram, which
consists of A and F stars of various evolutionary stages. They are in the transition region between the high-mass hot stars and low-
mass solar-like stars, making it essential to understand their magnetic properties to paint a complete picture of magnetism across the
HR diagram. Furthermore, discovering magnetic δ Scuti stars allows for magneto-asteroseismology, which can determine the internal
rotation profile, internal magnetic field strength, and the efficiency of mixing and transport processes more accurately than classical
asteroseismology.
Aims. To date, magnetic fields have been detected at the surface of 13 δ Scuti stars. However, the overall incidence rate of magnetism
in these stars remains unknown. Fossil magnetic fields are detected in 10% of OBA stars. The goal of this work is to find out if it is
the same for δ Scuti stars.
Methods. In this work, we investigated the incidence rate of surface magnetic fields among δ Scuti stars using photometric data from
the CoRoT space mission. We analyzed long-duration (∼5-month) light curves of ∼1750 δ Scuti stars to search for pulsations and
rotational modulation - a photometric signature that indicates chemical or temperature spots at the stellar surface, usually caused by
magnetic fields.
Results. We have identified 147 rotational variables, which we designate as magnetic candidates, potentially drastically increasing
the known population of magnetic δ Scuti stars, suggesting an incidence rate of fossil magnetic fields in δ Scuti stars similar to the
incidence rate in OBA stars in general. Our analysis has also revealed a few δ Scuti - γ Dor hybrid stars and four binary stars in the
sample. We have determined rotation periods and projected rotation velocities of the magnetic candidates to select suitable targets for
follow-up spectropolarimetric observations to confirm and characterize their magnetic fields.
Key words. Asteroseismology – Stars: variables: delta Scuti – Stars: magnetic field
1. Introduction
The intermediate mass (1.5 M⊙to 2.5 M⊙, A-F spectral type)
range on the main sequence of Hertzsprung-Russell (HR) dia-
gram includes γ Doradus and δ Scuti variable stars. γ Doradus
stars host low-frequency g-modes while δ Scuti stars host pul-
sations in low radial order, high-frequency p-modes. But the lat-
ter are also known to have low-frequency g-modes, and mixed
modes, which are driven by a combination of κ-mechanism op-
erating within the He II ionization zone (Pamyatnykh 2000)
and the turbulent pressure in the hydrogen ionization layer
(Antoci et al. 2014; Xiong et al. 2016). These stars form the
δ Scuti instability strip in the H-R diagram, which is located
around the intersection of the classical instability strip and the
main sequence (Murphy et al. 2019).
High-mass hot stars (O, B, A) have convective cores and
radiative envelopes, while low-mass solar-like stars (F, G, K)
have radiative cores and convective envelopes. Accordingly, the
magnetic fields observed in hot stars are generally fossil fields
(Borra et al. 1982), i.e. dipolar stable fields which are remnants
from the star’s formation process, while the solar-like stars gen-
erally host dynamo fields. The position of δ Scuti stars between
these two regimes makes them very important to study, as they
can help us understand the transition between the two types of
stellar structures and magnetic fields.
Previous studies have established that ∼10% of O, B,
and A stars have strong fossil magnetic fields (Wade et al.
2009; Fossati et al. 2015; Martin et al. 2018) with strengths
of the order of 300-30,000 G (Shultz et al. 2019). Among
the δ Scuti stars, 13 magnetic stars have been confirmed
(Neiner & Lampens 2015; Neiner et al. 2017; Zwintz et al.
2020; Thomson-Paressant et al. 2021, 2025) to date. But the
overall incidence rate of magnetic δ Scuti stars remains un-
known. The goal of this work is to find out this incidence rate
and to see if it is the same as the incidence rate of fossil mag-
netic fields in O, B, and A stars.
To obtain a statistically significant incidence rate of magnetic
δ Scuti stars, a large sample of such stars is needed. In order to
directly detect magnetic fields on the surface of a star, we need
spectropolarimetric observation. However, obtaining spectropo-
larimetric observations of such a large number of targets is a
difficult task, especially because these targets span large ranges
of magnitude and sky position. Fortunately, variable stars have
been the targets of space missions that provide time series data,
such as the Convection, Rotation and planetary Transits satel-
lite (CoRoT, Baglin et al. (2006); Auvergne et al. (2009)), Ke-

2. Observations
Magnetic δ Scuti stars have been searched for among the targets
of Kepler and TESS missions using spectroscopic and spectropo-
larimetric observations (Thomson-Paressant et al. 2023, 2025),
but not among the CoRoT targets because at the time of CoRoT
data release, δ Scuti stars were not expected to have mag-
netic fields. However, the sample of δ Scuti stars in the CoRoT
database is very large and suitable for this work as all the vari-
ables in the CoRoT database are identified using the CoRoT
variability classifier (Michel et al. 2017). We used the δ Scuti
samples from this classification in this work. This sample con-
sists of 1757 objects classified as δ Scuti stars with a probability
higher than 80%. Although they have spectral type and magni-
tude information in the CoRoT EXODAT database 1, we pulled
astrophysical parameters from Gaia DR3, and with the correc-
tion described in Frémat (2024) for the effective temperature of
hot stars, to have more reliable information. We found that 68 of
the targets have spectral type G or K, which are not known to
host δ Scuti stars, so we removed them from our analysis.
In case of effective temperature, magnitude, parallax, or ex-
tinction missing in the Gaia DR3, the CoRoT EXODAT database
was used to obtain the spectral types, which were obtained us-
ing the SED analysis (Deleuil et al. 2009). Conversion between
the effective temperature and spectral type was done using the
catalog of Mamajek (2022). The absolute magnitude was taken
directly from the Gaia row mg_gspphot for the cooler stars, and
calculated using apparent magnitude, parallax and extinction for
the hotter stars. A lot of the targets had some of these parameters
missing. The 780 targets, for which we could obtain the absolute
magnitude and effective temperature, are shown in Fig. 1 as an
HR diagram.
These targets were observed using the “faint star” channel
of the CoRoT 27 cm telescope. These stars have magnitudes
between 10 and 16 and they were observed between February
2007 and November 2012. The total of 1953 light curves, in-
cluding multiple observations of some targets, were downloaded
from the IAS CoRoT public archive’s faint stars - exo database
2. These light curves were generated with a 512-second cadence
and a baseline of 120-150 days (except some short light curves
with sim30-day baseline).
These light curves are CoRoT N2 legacy data (ver-
sion 4). The files are named with the prefix EN2_STAR_CHR
or EN2_STAR_MON which contain three “pseudo-colors” light
curves and a single monochromatic light curve, respectively. For
both types of files, we used the monochromatic light curve from
the WHITEFLUXSYS array and the timestamps from DATEBARTT
array.

used the LombScargle.model method from the Astropy library
to model and remove these frequencies using the pre-whitening
method described in Sect. 3.2. An example of such a long-term

To inspect the frequency spectra, periodograms were pro-
duced for each light curve. The primary tool we used to produce
the periodograms was a modified generalised Lomb-Scargle
periodogram (Zechmeister & Kürster 2009; Press et al. 1992;
VanderPlas et al. 2012; VanderPlas & Ivezi´c 2015; Lomb 1976;
Scargle 1982), employing the timeseries.LombScargle package
of Astropy (Astropy Collaboration et al. 2013, 2018).
To determine if a peak in the periodogram is significant, it
is necessary to define a metric. The Astropy library provides a
method to calculate the “False Alarm Probability” (FAP) of a
peak in the LS periodogram. The FAP is the probability that a
peak of a given height could occur by chance in the noise of
the data. It is calculated by comparing the height of the peak to
the noise level of the periodogram. The Astropy library does
this by estimating a constant noise level for the periodogram
and then calculating the FAP based on that noise level. How-
ever, this method cannot be applied directly to the light curves
in our sample, as the noise level in our sample is frequency de-
pendent, higher around very low frequencies, which is where the
rotational modulation is expected to be found. To solve this prob-
lem, we used a method to estimate the local noise level of the pe-
riodogram at each frequency. The noise level is estimated by cal-
culating a running average of the periodogram of the light curve
before detrending. To make the noise grid smoother, a Gaussian
kernel with a standard deviation of 0.1 d−1 is used to calculate
the running average. The value of the standard deviation of the
kernel was determined using the trial-and-error method. In addi-
tion, to better estimate the rise of noise at low frequencies, where
the running average function suffers from boundary effects, the
spectra were reflected to the negative frequency range to remove
the boundary effects at 0 d−1. This method allows for a more ac-
curate estimation of the noise level at each frequency. The typi-
cal noise level found in the periodograms is ∼0.5% of the mean
signal in the f > 3d−1 range, and higher in the lower frequency
range.
3.2. Searching for rotational modulation
A rotational modulation has a frequency corresponding to the
rotation of the star (fundamental frequency), usually with one
to several of its harmonics. The amplitudes of the harmonics
are expected to be decreasing with increasing order, and beyond
the second harmonics, they usually have relatively low ampli-
tudes. Therefore, we chose to look for the fundamental rotation
frequency and its first and second harmonics. The rotation fre-
quency is expected to be under 3 d−1 for δ Scuti stars because fre-
quencies higher than 3 d−1 would be super-critical. So, first, we
searched for all the peaks under 7.5 d−1, as the rotation frequency
under 3.25 d−1 with its first harmonic (and a rotation frequency
under 2.5 d−1 with its second harmonic) would be found in this
range. The peaks were detected by a “pre-whitening method”.
Pre-whitening method:
The pre-whitening method is a tech-
nique used to detect significant peaks in a periodogram by iter-
atively removing the effects of previously detected peaks. The
method works as follows:
1. Calculate the Lomb-Scargle periodogram of the light curve
in the frequency range of interest.
2. Identify the highest peak in the periodogram.
3. Fit a sinusoidal function to the data with the frequency of
the highest peak using LombScargle.model method from
Astropy.
4. Subtract the fitted sinusoidal function from the original light
curve to remove its effect.
5. Repeat steps 1-4 until a predefined threshold is reached.
The threshold for the pre-whitening method was set to detect
the 50 highest peaks. The significance of a peak was determined
by its SNR. In asteroseismology, an SNR threshold of 3.6 or 4
is most commonly used (Breger et al. 1993), but we set a lower
threshold of 3 to be more inclusive in the search for magnetic
candidates and also considering that the ‘noise’ level may be ar-
tificially increased due to astrophysical signals in the vicinity of
the identified peak. It should be noted that this SNR is only that
of the single frequencies. However, what we are actually look-
ing for is rotational modulation: signals that have one or more
harmonics. The presence of such harmonics would decrease the
false alarm probability of rotational modulation. With the fre-
quency and amplitude of the peaks, we also kept track of the
SNR of the peaks. This would allow us to set a higher threshold
later if needed.
Once all the significant peaks were found under 7.5 d−1, for
each peak (f0), we checked if there were peaks at the first har-
monic (2f0) and the second harmonic (3f0). When looking for
peaks at the position of the harmonics, frequency uncertainty of
δf = T −1

monic, Tlc being the total time span of the light curve. If any
of these harmonics were found, the fundamental frequency was
considered as a candidate for rotational modulation.

The set with the higher candidate SNR was selected as the
rotational frequency.
– Phase-folded light curve: For the cases of multiple candi-
dates for rotational frequency, the light curve was folded at
each of the candidate frequencies. After plotting the phase-
folded light curve with a running average to smooth it out, a
weak modulation of the light curve was revealed because the
light curves still contained high-frequency pulsations. The
frequency of the signal that seemed most consistent with ro-
tation was selected as the rotational frequency.
– Rotational splitting: In the presence of rotation, the degener-
acy of pulsation modes is lifted, leading to a splitting of the
mode. Each non-radial (l ≥1) mode in the pulsation spec-
trum is split into 2l+1 components, with azimuthal orders m
ranging from −l to l. For example, modes with l = 1 would
be split into 3 frequencies corresponding to m = −1, 0, 1. The
difference between these split frequencies is proportional to
mfrot, where frot is the internal rotational frequency in the re-
gion of the mode’s origin. As the l = 1 modes are most vis-
ible, we looked at the whole range of pulsation frequencies
and searched for the number of pairs of frequencies sepa-
rated by a candidate rotation frequency (∆f = f0), assuming
f0 = frot. The candidate rotational frequency with a higher
number of such splittings present was considered more likely
to be the true rotational frequency. But this is not an absolute
criterion for the candidacy of the rotational frequency, as the
pulsations can be influenced by the interior of the star, where
the rotation is not the same as the surface rotation, and more
rapidly rotating stars (faster than 50% of Keplerian break up)
have more complicated frequency spectra. Moreover, since
δ Scuti stars generally have a high density of modes, some
modes can be found to be separated by the candidate rota-
tion frequency simply by chance. In this work, we did not
analyze the splittings any further and treated their count as
an optional criterion.
3.3. Type of magnetic field
If the rotational modulation is caused by a fossil magnetic field,
all of its characteristics, such as amplitude and phase, are ex-
pected to be stable over time. In contrast, if the rotational mod-
ulation is caused by a dynamo magnetic field, the amplitudes of
the frequency peaks in the periodogram are expected to change
over time. To identify this, we divided the time series into sev-
eral smaller windows of 40 days with 5 days separation between
each window. Then we calculated the periodogram in each win-
dow and looked for the amplitude of the candidate frequency in
each periodogram. If the change of the amplitude of the candi-
date frequency is well above the local noise level (SNR > 3), then
we consider the frequency to have variable amplitude and the as-
sociated magnetic field to be caused by the dynamo. Conversely,
if the change in the amplitude is not well above the local noise
level, then we consider the frequency to have a stable amplitude
and the associated magnetic field to be a fossil field.
4. Results and Discussion
4.1. Occurrence rate of magnetism in δ Scuti stars
After the initial search for rotational modulation, we found 172
candidate light curves for rotational modulation, which belonged
to 162 unique stars. After this automated search, we conducted
a visual inspection of the 172 light curves using the criteria de-
scribed in the previous section. After this inspection, we were
able to discard 15 light curves where the fundamental frequency
of rotation and the detected harmonics had lower SNR and thus
were not very distinguishable from the noise. The remaining 157
light curves belonging to 147 targets were considered to have ro-
tational modulation. Although decreasing the SNR threshold to
lower than 3 increases the number of magnetic candidates, most
of these additional candidates are detected because of frequen-
cies (fundamental frequency or harmonics) that are not very dis-
tinguishable from noise. A threshold of 3 for the SNR produced
a low number of possible false detections, which we discarded.
This way, we could ensure that most of the actual magnetic can-
didates were selected and that most of the false detections were
removed.
This constitutes an 8.7% incidence rate of magnetic candi-
date δ Scuti stars among the CoRoT δ Scutis. However, if we
select only O, B, and A-type stars, the incidence rate of mag-
netic candidates among them rises to 11.4%. An HR diagram of
these magnetic candidates is shown in Fig. 3.
In order to have a selection of the candidates with higher
SNR, we created a subgroup with the “high SNR” candidates,
which have SNR > 3.6. There are 83 “high SNR” candidates.
With this “high SNR” selection, the incidence rate of magnetic
δ Scuti candidates falls to 4.9% - about half of the incidence rate
with “low SNR” selection. Among O, B, and A-type stars only,
the “high SNR” incidence rate of magnetic candidates becomes
6.2%.

4.4. Binary star
From inspection of the light curves and periodograms, we found
3 targets whose light curves were contaminated by a transiting
companion, suggesting an eclipsing binary companion around
each, and 1 target with a possibly contaminated light curve but
without any transit signal. Their CoRoT IDs are 101351899,
605144067, 110848138, and 102700458. In their contaminated
light-curves, it is not possible to find a rotational modulation that
indicates a magnetic field. Therefore, these targets have been ex-
cluded from the list of magnetic candidates.
The light curve of the candidate 101351899 showed clear pe-
riodic dips in the flux values, indicating eclipses at a period of 11
days. The periodogram showed a peak at 0.09 d−1, which corre-
sponds to the period of the eclipses and several (∼40) harmonics
of that peak. A phase-folded light-curve of this star is shown in
Figure A.1, where it shows a clear sign of binary eclipse rather
than rotational modulation. This target was also identified as an
eclipsing binary in Cabrera et al. (2009) with a transit period of
10.99 days.
The light curve of the candidate 110848138 showed clear
periodic dips in the flux values, indicating eclipses at a period
of 3.5 days. The periodogram showed a peak at 0.28 c/d, which
corresponds to the period of the eclipses and several harmonics
of that peak. The phase-folded light curve of this star shows a
clear sign of binary eclipse.
The candidate 102700458 showed many high-amplitude
peaks with several harmonics, which is not typical for δ Scuti
pulsation or rotational modulation. The phase-folded light curve
revealed a binary eclipse signal.
For the candidate 605144067, the light curve and the peri-
odogram did not show the regular pattern of δ Scuti pulsations.
This suggests that this target was either possibly misclassified as
a δ Scuti star, or the light of the δ Scuti star was polluted by a
non-δ Scuti companion, which would be a visual binary. How-
ever, no eclipsing signal was found in the light curve.
Except for these four binary candidates, we found δ Scuti
pulsations in the periodogram of all the other rotational mod-
ulation candidates. This indicates a very low occurrence rate of
possible false detection of δ Scuti stars in the initial list of CoRoT
δ Scuti sample.
4.5. Fossil and dynamo magnetic fields
Our sample consists of stars from spectral type B2 to F9, and
the magnetic candidates have spectral type B2 to F8. The hot-
ter OBA-type stars are expected to have a fossil field, while the
cooler stars are expected to have dynamo fields. Among our 147
magnetic candidates, we tried to characterize the type of their
magnetic field using the algorithm described in 3.3. We found
that all of the 147 candidates have a stable amplitude of the ro-
tational frequency, i.e., the change in the amplitude of the rota-
tional frequency is very low compared to the local noise level.
This parameter is plotted as a distribution in Fig. 9. Among the
147 targets, 69 of them have an effective temperature in Gaia
Data Release 3. Fig. 10 shows the change of amplitude over
noise against the effective temperature, but we do not observe
any trend against the temperature. If there were targets with a
significant change of amplitude over noise for the magnetic peak
( > 3), we would expect to see these targets to have lower effec-
tive temperatures. However, the absence of such targets indicates
that all the magnetic candidates host a rather stable field on the
timescale of the CoRoT light curve.

5. Conclusions
In this work, we presented a method to search for magnetic
δ Scuti candidates in the CoRoT light curves and created a
Python tool to facilitate this search. Using this tool, we ana-
lyzed 1953 light curves of 1689 δ Scuti stars in the CoRoT sam-
ple. Among them, we found 147 good candidates for magnetic
δ Scuti stars. We classified these candidates according to their
SNR, presence of rotational splitting, variability type, and the
number of harmonics detected.
We conclude that the incidence rate of magnetic δ Scuti can-
didates in the CoRoT sample is 8.7% for the full sample and
Article number, page 7


## Page 8


A&A proofs: manuscript no. aa59955-26
11.4% if we consider only the OBA stars. This is close to the
incidence rate of fossil magnetic fields in OBA stars, which is
around 10%. The latter incidence rate is measured by spectropo-
larimetric studies. Our work, based solely on photometric light-
curves, yields a similar incidence rate for δ Scuti stars. How-
ever, these incidence rates should be considered an upper limit,
as close binaries or chance alignment of lower frequency signals
could possibly produce a rotational modulation signal. This is
especially important for the hotter stars in our sample, because
a large fraction of stars with higher mass have binary compan-
ions, so there may be cases where, for instance, a rotationally-
variable B-type star is paired with a cooler δ Scuti companion,
which we would not be able to distinguish in the CoRoT data.
Furthermore, it is hard to distinguish between signals from ellip-
soidal variables and rotational modulation because they produce
a similar pattern in the frequency spectrum. We selected our can-
didates that have phase-folded light curves that are more consis-
tent with a rotational modulation signal (e.g., Fig. 4 is consistent
with rotational modulation), but there are cases where it is am-
biguous as to whether the variation is rotational or due to binary-
induced ellipsoidal variation. The Gaia DR3 provides the Renor-
malised Unit Weight Error (RUWE) associated with each target.
The RUWE is expected to be around 1.0 for sources where the
single-star model provides a good fit to the astrometric observa-
tions. A value significantly greater than 1.0 could indicate that
the source is non-single or otherwise problematic for the astro-
metric solution. Among the 147 magnetic candidates, 124 have
RUWE < 1.5, indicating that a greater proportion of our can-
didates are less likely to be binaries. Further investigation with
spectropolarimetric surveys of these targets will reveal the binary
nature and/or confirm the existence of a magnetic field.
According to this work, based on CoRoT light curves, all 147
candidates have stable, possibly fossil, magnetic fields. This re-
sult does not follow our expectation, as cooler stars in this sam-
ple should rather have dynamo fields. The actual types of the
magnetic fields need to be verified with complementary meth-
ods such as spectropolarimetry. In addition, dynamo fields are
hard to detect using light curves only; some δ Scuti stars from
this sample may also host dynamo fields that have remained in-
visible in this study.
With European Space Agency (ESA)’s PLAnetary Transits
and Oscillations of Stars (PLATO, Rauer et al. (2014, 2025))
mission, it will be possible to obtain light-curves with higher
quality and less noise. However, PLATO’s long observation
fields do not include the magnetic candidates in this work, and
the selection of the shorter observation fields is not yet finalized.
If some of the latter PLATO fields include the magnetic candi-
dates in this work from the CoRoT field, then it will be possible
to use the tools developed for this work to analyze the PLATO
light-curves of these candidates and obtain additional informa-
tion about the magnetic candidates.
Finally, we have prepared the necessary data and parameters
for the candidates to select the suitable targets for spectropolari-
metric observations. These observations will allow us to mea-
sure the magnetic field strength and its geometry at the surface
of the star, which will help us confirm the presence of magnetic
fields in δ Scuti stars and better understand their nature. How-
ever, due to the generally low brightness of the CoRoT targets,
only a few of these candidates are suitable for spectropolarime-
try with current instruments such as ESPaDOnS at CFHT. Better
instruments on larger telescopes will be needed in the future for
further analysis of these stars, such as the VIPER project for the
Very Large Telescope at ESO.
