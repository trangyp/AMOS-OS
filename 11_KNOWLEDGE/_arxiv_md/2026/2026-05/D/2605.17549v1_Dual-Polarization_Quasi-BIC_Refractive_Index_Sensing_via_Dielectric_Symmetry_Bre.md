ABSTRACT
A dual-polarization dielectric metasurface sensor based on TiO2 nanobar pairs with a 20 nm BeS gap
insert is numerically investigated in the near-infrared. The BeS layer introduces dielectric symmetry
breaking without requiring geometric asymmetry, enabling polarization-selective excitation of two
distinct resonances. Under TE illumination, the structure supports a quasi-BIC resonance at 879.2 nm
with Q = 128, whereas TM excitation produces a broader magnetic dipole resonance at 910.8 nm
with Q = 36. The spectral separation between the two modes enables simultaneous tracking of
both polarization channels within a single measurement window. For background refractive indices
from 1.00 to 1.05, the TE and TM resonances exhibit sensitivities of 243.1 and 178.8 nm/RIU,
respectively. The corresponding figures of merit reach 35 and 7 RIU−1, with detection limits on the
order of 10−5 RIU. Field distributions show strong confinement of the TE mode inside the gap region,
leading to enhanced overlap with the surrounding analyte. Because the two resonances respond
differently to refractive-index variations, the metasurface produces a polarization-dependent spectral
fingerprint that may provide additional selectivity beyond conventional single-channel dielectric
sensors. The proposed platform further shows good tolerance against dimensional variation and
consistent resonance behavior across independent FDTD solvers.

Introduction
Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) sensors remain widely used for label-free refractive index sensing because of their
strong surface field confinement and high spectral sensitivity [1]. However, Ohmic loss in the metal layer broadens the
resonance linewidth and limits the achievable quality factor Q [2, 3]. Local heating effects can also become problematic
for biological analytes under certain operating conditions.
All-dielectric metasurfaces provide an alternative route for low-loss refractive index sensing by supporting electric and
magnetic Mie resonances in high-index dielectric nanostructures [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Silicon and TiO2 metasurfaces can
support resonances with quality factors ranging from 102 to 104 without the absorption losses associated with metallic

structures. In particular, quasi-bound states in the continuum (quasi-BICs) have attracted considerable attention because
weak symmetry breaking can introduce controlled radiative coupling into otherwise dark modes [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15,
16, 17]. The resulting perturbation strength strongly influences both the resonance linewidth and coupling efficiency,
making it an important design parameter in quasi-BIC metasurfaces.
Most dielectric refractive index sensors rely on a single spectral resonance shift as the sensing observable [18, 19, 15, 20].
As a result, different analytes can produce similar responses when their effective refractive indices are close. Additional
sensing channels are therefore desirable, particularly for surface-sensitive measurements and multidimensional optical
detection. Dual-resonance metasurfaces have been explored for this purpose because different resonant modes can
exhibit distinct spatial field distributions and analyte overlap [15, 20]. Among the available approaches, polarization
multiplexing provides a relatively simple implementation in which TE and TM illumination excite spectrally separated
resonances within the same metasurface geometry [21].
TiO2 is well suited to this operating regime. Its refractive index n ≈2.3–2.5 near 900 nm supports strong Mie
resonances, its absorption edge near 350 nm keeps it transparent through the visible and near-infrared, and ALD deposits
films with angstrom-level thickness control patternable by EBL to ±5 nm [22, 23, 24]. Reported TiO2 metasurface
sensors reach 82–185 nm/RIU at Q factors of several thousand [25, 26]. Silicon absorbs below ∼1100 nm and cannot be
used at the 879–911 nm resonances of the present design, making TiO2 the practical choice for this wavelength range.
The symmetry-breaking element introduced here is a 20 nm BeS bar placed inside the gap between each TiO2 nanobar
pair. BeS is a II–VI semiconductor with an indirect bandgap near 5.4 eV [27]. In thin-film form, the broken out-of-plane
symmetry of the zinc-blende lattice lifts the degeneracy of the principal dielectric tensor components and introduces a
birefringence ∆n = nx −nz ≈0.10–0.12 at 900 nm [27, 28]. Both extinction coefficients are negligibly small across
the near-infrared, so the insert is effectively lossless. The TE and TM fields therefore sample the gap region with
different effective permittivities, which lifts the resonance degeneracy of the two bar-pair modes and produces two
spectrally separated sensing channels from a single geometry. First-principles calculations further show that molecular
adsorption modifies the BeS optical constants along each crystallographic axis in an analyte-specific way [28], which
points toward chemical discrimination in a future experimental extension of this work.
We numerically show that a single 20 nm BeS bar inside the TiO2 nanobar-pair gap activates a quasi-BIC resonance
under TE illumination and a magnetic dipole mode under TM illumination, with no geometric asymmetry required.
FDTD simulations establish the sensing metrics of both channels. Near-field maps connect the sensitivity difference
between channels to the spatial distribution of the modal energy. A two-dimensional polarization fingerprint shows
what a dual-channel readout can resolve that a single-resonance sensor cannot. Structural tolerance sweeps, BeS
optical-constant perturbation tests, and cross-platform validation against the Tidy3D solver confirm the numerical
robustness of the results.

Structure and Simulation Method
2.1
Device geometry
The unit cell is shown in Fig. 1. Two TiO2 nanobars of width W = 120 nm, length L = 450 nm, and height H = 250 nm
are separated by a gap of g = 60 nm along x. A BeS bar of thickness 20 nm sits symmetrically inside this gap. The unit
cell period along y is P = 600 nm. The structure rests on a SiO2 substrate (nsub = 1.46), and the superstrate is the
analyte medium of index nbg.
TiO2 optical constants come from the ALD-film ellipsometry data of Siefke et al. [22], giving n ≈2.35 with negligible
extinction at 800–950 nm. SiO2 dispersion follows Kischkat et al. [29]. The BeS directional constants (nx, kx, nz, kz)
are from first-principles calculations for pristine BeS [30]. Both k components are near zero at 800–1000 nm, so the
insert is effectively lossless.
2.2
FDTD simulation details
All simulations used Lumerical FDTD [31] with periodic boundary conditions in x and y and perfectly matched layers
(PML) in z [32]. A broadband plane-wave source spanning 850–950 nm was incident along −z.
Separate campaigns covered 0◦polarization (E along x, across the gap) and 90◦polarization (E along y, along the bar
axis). A non-uniform conformal mesh with a minimum cell size of 5 nm was used. Mesh convergence was checked
by comparing 10 nm and 5 nm meshes; the resonance wavelength shifted by only ∆λ = 0.066 nm (Fig. 2), negligible
compared to the RI-induced shift of ∼12 nm per 0.05 RIU. Each run was continued until the stored field energy fell
below 10−5 of its peak value, which is standard practice for accurate linewidth extraction [33, 34].

3
Results
3.1
BeS crystal structure and optical constants
3.1.1
Crystal structure
Bulk BeS crystallizes in the zinc-blende (sphalerite) structure with space group F¯43m (No. 216) [40]. Each Be2+
cation is tetrahedrally coordinated by four S2−anions with a Be–S bond length of 2.11 Å. The cubic symmetry of the
bulk phase means that the three principal dielectric tensor components are degenerate in the unstrained crystal. However,
when BeS is grown as an ultrathin film or monolayer on a planar substrate, the broken out-of-plane symmetry lifts this
degeneracy and introduces a measurable birefringence ∆n = nx −nz [41, 28]. It is this thin-film birefringence that is
exploited in the present design: the 20 nm BeS bar presents different permittivities to the TE (x-polarized) and TM
(y-polarized) fields, lifting the resonance degeneracy of the two TiO2 bar modes.
3.1.2
DFT origin of the optical constants
The directional optical constants used in this work come from a comprehensive first-principles study of the BeS
monolayer and its interaction with volatile organic compounds (VOCs) [30]. DFT calculations used the GGA-PBE
exchange-correlation functional [42] together with the Grimme dispersion correction [43] on a 3 × 3 BeS supercell with
a 21 Å vacuum slab. The relaxed pristine monolayer has a lattice constant of 3.459 Å, a Be–S bond length of 1.993 Å, a
bond angle of 120◦, and a DFT-PBE band gap of 4.529 eV, consistent with earlier theoretical reports [41, 28].
That same study found that adsorption of 4-hydroxyhexanal (4HHE), isoprene, propanal, benzene, acetone, CO2,
and H2O each introduces mid-gap defect states on the BeS surface and alters the in-plane and out-of-plane optical
constants in a way that is distinct for each analyte [30]. The present work uses only the pristine-BeS constants, which
establish the baseline birefringence and the lossless character of the gap insert. The adsorbate-modified constants from
Ref. [30] represent a ready, first-principles input dataset for extending the metasurface to analyte-specific optical gas
discrimination, and that extension is left as a direct continuation of this work.
Fig. 3 shows the dispersive n and k of pristine BeS along x and z from 400 to 2000 nm. Across the 800–1000 nm
sensing window, nx ≈1.25–1.30 and nz ≈1.14–1.16, and both extinction coefficients remain below 0.005. The
birefringence ∆n = nx −nz ≈0.10–0.12 is what causes the two polarizations to couple to the gap insert with different
effective permittivities, and it is ultimately the physical origin of the spectral separation between the two sensing
channels.
To our knowledge, no experimental near-infrared ellipsometry data exist for BeS thin films in the open literature. This
uncertainty is addressed in Sec. 3.7 by running additional simulations with nx and nz each perturbed by ±5% from
their DFT values.
3.2

at nbg = 1.0. Without the insert the bar pair supports a symmetric resonance at 876.9 nm with a relatively broad
lineshape. Adding the 20 nm BeS bar red-shifts the resonance by 2.3 nm to 879.2 nm and narrows the dip, as visible in
the sharper descent on the long-wavelength side. The redshift originates from the increased effective index inside the
gap region, while the linewidth reduction indicates modified radiative coupling introduced by the dielectric perturbation.

The two resonances have distinct physical origins. The 0◦resonance at 879.2 nm is a gap-coupled quasi-BIC mode.
In a perfectly symmetric TiO2 bar pair, the antisymmetric superposition of the two bar dipole modes has opposing
dipole moments that cancel in the far field, making it a symmetry-protected dark mode [10, 11]. The BeS bar breaks
this symmetry dielectrically by introducing different permittivities along x and z in the gap region. This allows the
dark mode to radiate, producing a quasi-BIC resonance with strong gap field confinement. The asymmetric Fano
lineshape (q = 0.10) arises directly from the interference between this narrow quasi-BIC mode and the broad radiation
continuum [35, 37], and is the standard lineshape reported for quasi-BIC sensors with weak geometric or dielectric
symmetry breaking [11, 12, 15].
The 90◦resonance at 910.8 nm is a magnetic dipole (MD) mode driven by the y-polarized field. The displacement
current circulates inside the TiO2 bar cross-section, storing energy predominantly in the bar volume rather than in the
gap [44, 45]. The Ez map shows four lobes inside the bar, consistent with MD excitation (Fig. 8b). Because the BeS
insert occupies only a small fraction of the MD mode volume, the analyte overlap integral is lower than for the gap
mode [46, 47]. This explains why S(90◦) is 26% below S(0◦).
3.4
Near-field electric-field distributions
Figs. 7 and 8 show the normalized |Ex|2 and |Ez|2 distributions at each resonance wavelength in the cross-section
through the gap.
At the 0◦resonance (Fig. 7a), the Ex field is concentrated near the inner corners of the TiO2 bars close to the substrate
interface, indicating strong gap-mode confinement. In contrast, the 90◦resonance (Fig. 7b) exhibits a broader field
distribution near the upper bar region (z ≈180–250 nm), consistent with magnetic dipole localization inside the
dielectric bars.
At the 0◦resonance (Fig. 8a), Ez forms a sharp vertical stripe at the gap center (x ≈0), exactly where the BeS bar sits.
This gap-confined hot spot confirms that the mode is directly sensitive to the permittivity of the gap region, and directly
accounts for the higher sensitivity of the 0◦channel. At the 90◦resonance (Fig. 8b), Ez shows four lobes arranged
symmetrically inside the bar, the characteristic quadrupolar signature of an MD mode oriented along the bar axis [45, 4].
The gap field is noticeably weaker here, consistent with the lower sensitivity of this channel.

3.7
Structural parameter sweeps and fabrication tolerance
The influence of geometric variation on the resonance response was investigated through parameter sweeps and
fabrication-tolerance analysis. Figure 13 shows the dependence of λres on the principal structural parameters. Among
the investigated dimensions, the resonance position is most sensitive to variations in bar height and unit-cell period,
where a ±10 nm perturbation produces shifts of approximately 20–40 nm. The responses to gap-width and bar-width
variation remain comparatively smaller. Because ALD processes routinely achieve thickness control near ±5 nm [22, 24],
the resonance wavelength is expected to depend primarily on the deposition thickness.
The combined influence of width and gap variation is summarized in Fig. 14. The nominal operating point (W = 120 nm,
g = 60 nm) lies close to a local minimum in the relative resonance-shift map, indicating partial compensation between
correlated dimensional errors.
The effect of bar-length asymmetry was additionally examined to estimate sensitivity to EBL dose variation. As shown
in Fig. 15, reducing L2 from 450 to 440 nm (δ = 0–0.022) produces resonance shifts below 0.5 nm, indicating relatively
weak sensitivity to moderate bar-length mismatch.
Figure 16 summarizes the overall fabrication tolerance through heatmaps of ∆λres and ∆Q for ±10 nm dimensional
perturbations. Bar height and unit-cell period produce the largest variations in both resonance wavelength and quality
factor.
Additional simulations were performed by varying both nx and nz by ±5% relative to their nominal DFT values. The
resulting resonance shifts remain below 2.1 nm, while the corresponding variation in Q is less than 3%. These changes
are small compared with the resonance shifts induced by refractive-index variation in the sensing range considered here.
Figure 17 compares the normalized sensing metrics for the two polarization channels. The TE resonance exhibits
stronger sensitivity and figure of merit, whereas the TM resonance maintains comparatively higher robustness in
linewidth-related performance.

Cross-platform FDTD validation
To assess the solver dependence of the resonance wavelengths, the nominal geometry was independently simulated
using the Tidy3D finite-difference time-domain solver [48]. Tidy3D uses a cloud-based FDTD engine with a distinct
grid implementation and perfectly matched layer formulation from Lumerical [32, 34]. The same unit cell dimensions,
material constants, broadband source range (850–950 nm), and auto-shutoff criterion (10−5 field energy decay) were
applied in both solvers.

Discussion
Table 2 places the present design among recently reported all-dielectric RI sensors, spanning single-channel and
dual-channel designs over the 2020–2025 period.
The Q factors here (36–128) are substantially below quasi-BIC silicon designs reaching Q ∼104 [12, 13, 14, 16]. The
moderate Q factors are associated with the weak dielectric perturbation introduced by the BeS insert (∆n ≈0.10–0.12),
which produces only limited radiative coupling of the quasi-BIC mode. The extracted Fano asymmetry parameters
(q ≈0.10–0.25) are consistent with this behavior. The same perturbation-controlled Q trade-off is well documented in
geometric asymmetry designs: larger asymmetry gives lower Q but broader spectral coverage and greater fabrication

The conceptually closest prior work is the TiO2 state-of-polarization sensor of Kramadhati et al. [18], which is
experimentally demonstrated but operates in the visible range. The near-infrared is more relevant for biosensing
applications because many molecular absorption bands lie in the 700–1100 nm window [54, 55].
The sensitivities of 243 and 179 nm/RIU sit within the range reported for comparable TiO2 and moderate-Q silicon
sensors [25, 26]. TiO2 is transparent from about 350 nm through the near-infrared, which makes it practical at the 879
and 911 nm resonances here. Silicon absorbs below about 1100 nm and cannot be used in this range [22, 23].
The 20 nm BeS insert has two roles. It sits at the gap field maximum and introduces directional permittivity that breaks
the polarization degeneracy of the bar modes, playing the same role as the geometric δ parameter in prior asymmetric
designs [56, 33]. It also responds to molecular adsorption through changes in n and k [28, 30], which a future version
of this sensor can exploit for analyte-specific detection. The adsorbate optical constants computed in Ref. [30] for seven
lung-disease biomarkers (4HHE, isoprene, propanal, benzene, acetone, CO2, H2O) on the BeS monolayer provide a
ready, first-principles input dataset for such an extension.
This study is entirely computational. The TiO2 and SiO2 constants come from experimentally verified datasets [22, 29],
and mesh convergence confirms numerical accuracy. Sidewall roughness and BeS film non-uniformity are not included
in the model. The tolerance sweeps cover systematic dimensional errors but not stochastic roughness, which is left for
future work.
5
Conclusion
A dual-polarization TiO2–BeS dielectric metasurface was numerically investigated for refractive index sensing in
the near-infrared region. By introducing a thin BeS insert inside the TiO2 nanobar gap, the structure supports two
polarization-dependent resonances. This eliminates the need for geometric asymmetry. Under TE illumination, the
quasi-BIC resonance at 879.2 nm achieves a sensitivity of 243.1 nm/RIU and a quality factor of 128, while the TM
magnetic dipole mode at 910.8 nm yields 178.8 nm/RIU with Q = 36. The corresponding figures of merit are 35.4 and
7.1 RIU−1, with detection limits of 4.1 × 10−5 and 5.6 × 10−5 RIU respectively. The TE channel exhibits a quasi-BIC
resonance with higher sensitivity, while the TM channel supports a broader magnetic dipole mode with a distinct
spectral response.

The different refractive-index dependence of the two resonances produces a polarization-dependent spectral fingerprint.
This can offer additional selectivity compared with conventional single-channel dielectric sensors. Because the two
sensitivity values differ by 26%, isotropic analytes trace a fixed slope in the dual-channel shift space, while analytes
with optical anisotropy or asymmetric surface interaction deviate measurably from this reference line, providing a
discrimination capability unavailable from a single resonance. The all-dielectric TiO2/SiO2 platform is fully compatible
with ALD deposition and electron-beam lithography, placing the structure within reach of current nanofabrication
capabilities. Cross-platform simulations and fabrication-tolerance studies further reveal that the resonance behavior
remains stable under moderate numerical and structural variation.
The present study is fully computational and does not include experimental uncertainties such as surface roughness
or nonuniform BeS film formation. In addition, analyte-specific discrimination was not directly modeled using
adsorbate-modified optical constants. Future work may extend the present platform toward experimentally realizable
polarization-resolved sensing using analyte-dependent BeS optical responses and integrated microfluidic architectures.
First-principles optical constants for seven biomarker adsorbates on BeS (4HHE, isoprene, propanal, benzene, acetone,
CO2, and H2O) are already available [30], so this extension does not require additional DFT calculations.
