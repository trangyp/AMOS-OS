Dynamic Alignment: A Fragile Survival Effect
Amir Jafari∗
5 Ashdown Lodge, Notting Hill, London, United Kingdom
Dynamic alignment in magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) turbulence is usually interpreted as a
cascade-wide tendency of Els¨asser increments to become increasingly collinear. We argue that this
is not the correct physical interpretation of the standard measurements: the typical folded angle
remains close to the random value, while the much smaller angles inferred from conventional diagnos-
tics arise mainly from selective sampling of longer-lived intense events. We formulate the problem
using tools from differential geometry, dynamical-systems theory, and non-equilibrium statistical
physics, and test the resulting picture with high-resolution Johns Hopkins MHD simulations and
NASA Wind solar-wind observations. A tangent-bundle metric formulation shows why cascade-wide
alignment is not expected: rough inertial-range dynamics amplify small differences in local Els¨asser
fields toward smaller scales, preventing alignment from being transported as a volume-filling order
across the cascade. A generation–depletion balance then explains why snapshot measurements find
a typical angle only modestly below the 57.3◦random baseline, whereas amplitude-weighted diag-
nostics show much stronger apparent alignment. Large-amplitude, large-angle events are continually
produced, but their stronger nonlinear interaction makes them shorter-lived than large-amplitude,
small-angle events; weighted measurements therefore preferentially sample the surviving small-angle
subset rather than a volume-filling alignment of typical fluctuations. We also obtain the same picture
from a statistical-physics description by casting the alignment-angle dynamics into a Langevin equa-
tion motivated by Markovian-in-scale evolution. The simulations and Wind observations show the
predicted hierarchy: the unweighted folded angle remains close to the appropriate random or weakly
biased baseline and shows no evidence for rigid monotone cascade-wide ordering, while the strongest
Els¨asser-amplitude events occupy much smaller angles. In the simulation, small angles occur pri-
marily in sectors with large Els¨asser-increment amplitude Ar = |δrz+| |δrz−|, while current-density
conditioning leaves the angle close to its unweighted behavior. Shuffled-null tests show that the
weighted reduction is caused by a genuine negative covariance between event amplitude and angular
misalignment, not by weighting alone. Cross-scale angular correlations are measurable but decaying
and only partially persistent. A finite-time simulation test directly supports the lifetime mechanism:
high-amplitude large-angle samples leave their amplitude–angle sector faster than high-amplitude
small-angle samples, while incoming transitions replenish the large-angle sector. Wind data show the
same angle–amplitude hierarchy and negative covariance in Taylor-sampled solar-wind increments.
Thus dynamic alignment, as measured by conventional weighted diagnostics, is best understood as
a conditional survival effect of intense Els¨asser events, not as a monotone, volume-filling ordering of
typical MHD turbulence.

Physically, this suggests a selection mechanism. Large-
amplitude, large-angle events have strong nonlinear in-
teraction and can be rapidly redistributed out of the
strong coherent population at that scale; they do not
need to rotate into small-angle events. The remaining
high-amplitude population is then biased toward smaller
folded angles, even if the bulk angle field remains close
to random. The statistical logic is one of residence time:
a state can be produced frequently but remain rare in
a snapshot if it is short-lived. A central addition of the
present work is to test this residence-time interpretation
directly, by classifying high-amplitude events into small-
angle and large-angle sectors and measuring their finite-
time state-retention probabilities. This provides a direct
state-retention test of the survival-bias mechanism, com-
plementary to conventional same-scale alignment diag-
nostics.
Figure 1 shows the covariance mechanism in the main
DNS ensemble. The amplitude-weighted proxy is smaller
than the unweighted sine average at the plotted separa-
tions, and shuffling the amplitudes relative to the angles
removes the difference. The covariance is negative across
the same range and collapses to zero under the shuffled
null.
This is the first indication that strong apparent
alignment is not a volume-filling property of typical fluc-
tuations, but an amplitude-conditioned property of co-
herent events that remain strong at the measured scale.1
The same logic makes an observational prediction. If
apparent alignment is a survival bias of intense Els¨asser
events rather than a rigid volume-filling cascade order,
then a similar angle–amplitude hierarchy should be visi-
ble in single-spacecraft solar-wind data, even though such
data cannot reproduce the full three-dimensional local-
perpendicular DNS geometry.

Wind solar-wind data check in Sec. V [9–11]: it is an inde-

Els¨asser-amplitude events occupy much smaller folded

We further complement the geometric, cross-scale,
finite-time, and observational analyses with a minimal
stochastic model for the scale evolution of the relative an-

statistical-mechanical reductions, including Markov-in-

The exact Els¨asser
increment equations determine the evolution of the incre-
ment vectors, but after projection to the relative angle,
unresolved degrees of freedom appear as effective drift,
memory, and fluctuation terms. We model the reduced
angular dynamics by a Fokker–Planck/Langevin equa-
tion on the unit sphere, in which a coherent aligning
tendency competes with isotropizing angular diffusion.
The resulting conditional stationary law is controlled by
a single dimensionless alignment-bias parameter a(Ar).
When a is small, the unsigned-angle distribution remains
close to the random three-dimensional law. When a is
large, the distribution shifts toward smaller angles. In
this way the stochastic model provides a compact inter-
pretation of the coexistence of weak typical alignment
and much stronger amplitude-weighted alignment as the
result of an amplitude-dependent aligning bias compet-
ing with angular diffusion.

high-amplitude, small-angle events. Dynamic alignment
is therefore best understood as a conditional intermittent
effect, not as a volume-filling monotone ordering of the
cascade.

DISCUSSION
The main conclusion of this work is that dynamic align-
ment in incompressible MHD turbulence is not a mono-
tone, volume-filling ordering of the cascade. The typical
folded Els¨asser-increment angle remains close to the ran-
dom three-dimensional baseline, while strong apparent
alignment is concentrated in selected Els¨asser-amplitude-
rich sectors. The path-line and metric formulation gives
the geometric reason for this outcome.
At each fixed
coarse-graining scale ℓ, the filtered Els¨asser fields are
smooth and local directional organization can be mean-
ingfully discussed.
The obstruction is the lack of uni-
form control as the observational scale is decreased: the
effective Els¨asser accelerations inherit rough multiscale
structure, the continuity-control coefficients grow toward
smaller scales, and small differences in local field state
can be rapidly amplified. Directional coherence should
therefore be fragile, intermittent, and conditional rather
than transported as a rigid cascade-wide order.
This expectation is borne out by the control-coefficient
diagnostics in the JHTDB simulation [8].
