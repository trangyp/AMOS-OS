Abstract
We study strategic interaction in data-driven games where players face uncertainty about payoff
distributions inferred from finite samples. To model calibrated attitudes toward such uncertainty, we
formulate distributionally robust games with a special focus on coherent utility (risk) measures, including
Mean–semideviation and Conditional Value-at-Risk. This framework treats risk sensitivity as a primitive
feature of player preferences while retaining a formal connection to distributional robustness. We make
a number of contributions that are enumerated next.
(1) We use prior results for the existence of
distributionally robust equilibria to show the existence of equilibria in data-driven settings for various
ambiguity sets, and (2) show that these games are inherently continuous, rather than finite matrix games,
which fundamentally alters equilibrium structure and precludes direct extensions of standard correlated
equilibrium notions. (3) We bound the loss in expected utility that a player can expect from being risk-
averse. (4) We further characterize the computational complexity of equilibrium computation, proving
PPAD-completeness in general and PPAD membership for several coherent utility measure games. (5)
We present multilinear complementarity program formulations for several coherent utility measure games.
(6) Numerical experiments reveal the robustness and out of sample performance of the game solutions.
Our results unify risk-theoretic modeling and equilibrium analysis, providing a principled foundation for
risk-aware strategic decision-making in data-driven environments.

Introduction
In recent years, data-driven decision-making has become a central paradigm across economics, operations
research (OR), and artificial intelligence (AI). As decisions increasingly rely on empirical data rather than
fully known models, understanding and mitigating the impact of distributional uncertainty has emerged as
a crucial challenge. In the context of single-agent decision problems, a rich line of research has developed
robust and risk-sensitive formulations, starting from classical Markowitz mean-variance model Markowitz
[1952] to the more recent unifying framework of coherent risk measures Artzner et al. [1999], which includes
risk measures such as Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) or Mean-semideviation. These formulations have
played a foundational role in capturing aversion to uncertainty in finance Acharya et al. [2017], OR Dentcheva
and Ruszczynski [2024], and also AI Lam et al. [2022].
However, the extension of such distributional robustness principles based on risk measures to multi-agent
or strategic settings remains limited. While preliminary works have begun investigating robust equilibria
and uncertainty-aware games Peng et al. [2020], the integration of coherent risk measures into game-theoretic
formulations remains nascent. This gap is especially consequential because coherent risk measures combine
axiomatic rigor with interpretability, features that are indispensable for modeling strategic decision-making
under uncertainty. In particular, coherent risk measures provide an interpretable parameterization of risk
attitudes, such as tail or downside sensitivity, so that even when equilibrium responses are non-monotone
in the degree of risk aversion, changes in outcomes can be directly attributed to shifts in players’ risk
preferences. By contrast, probability distance-based ambiguity sets (e.g., Wasserstein) entangle preference
effects with statistical uncertainty, complicating comparative statics in strategic settings.
We also note

In this work, we explore distributional robustness in data driven games with a focus on risk-measure
based games. In a reward setting, risk measures are called coherent utility measure. In the data-driven
setting, we assume K samples of the payoff matrix and treat the empirical distribution as the nominal
distribution for various ambiguity sets. However, as noted above, our focus is often on the coherent utility
(risk-measure) perspective in which an inner optimization over the ambiguity set has a readily available
closed form solution. These risk measures are widely studied in the finance and risk-management literature
and offer easy interpretation, with mean–semideviation capturing downside risk and conditional value-at-
risk (CVaR) quantifying tail risk. In this work, we will use three measures: (1) Mean-semideviation, (2)
Mean-deviation, and (3) CVaR as three instances of coherent utility measures. The contributions in this
work are listed below:
1. Utilizing prior existence results in general m-player finite one-shot distributionally robust game (DRG)
and distributionally robust equilibrium (DRE), we show that in the data-driven setting with K sam-
ples, the existence result applies to various ambiguity sets, such as those formed using f-divergences,
Wasserstein distance, and particularity coherent utility measures.
2. We show that DRGs can be viewed as a continuous game “lifted” to the mixed strategy space, which
precludes defining correlated equilibrium in DRGs using the standard approach in finite games, and
requires continuous game machinery.
3. We bound the loss in expected utility that a player can expect from being risk-averse in CUMGs
(Theorem 3).
4. We show that approximate equilbrium computation in DRGs is in general PPAD-complete and for the
subset of truly uncertain games (non-singleton ambiguity set) we show that our three Coherent Utility
Measure Games (CUMG) are all in PPAD (Theorem 4).
5. We obtain finite multilinear complementarity programs for the three CUMGs we consider and note
that these programs do not become linear for two players, unlike in standard games.
6. Numerical experiments show that CUMG games can make the equilibria set more robust to data
perturbations and improve out-of-sample (OOS) performance. CVaR games also allow for simultaneous
calibration of tail-risk aversion and hedging behavior and provide probabilistic guarantees in terms of
α−quantile for payoffs in equilibrium.
The contributions above hint that this class of games appears to share characteristics of both finite and
continuous games, placing these games in an intermediate regime between them. Overall, our framework
unifies coherent risk modeling and strategic interaction, offering a systematic approach to incorporating
interpretable risk aversion into data-driven games. By grounding strategic behavior in well-established risk-
theoretic principles, it provides both interpretability and analytical structure, enabling uncertainty-aware
equilibrium analysis without relying solely on probability distance based ambiguity set descriptions.

3.2
Towards Correlated Equilibrium
In this section, we show that correlated equilibrium Aumann [1974] for DRGs cannot be defined as in finite
matrix games. Instead, DRG can be viewed as a lifted game with players’ action spaces redefined as their
probability simplices, replacing their original pure action spaces. Consequently, DRG correlated equilibrium
follows the standard definition of correlated equilibrium in continuous games Hart and Schmeidler [1989].
Even in finite games, correlated equilbria can be defined using deviation functions and are a special case
of the general Φ-equilibrium Greenwald and Jafari [2003]. In particular, given an action recommendation
(sampled from the correlated equilibrium), it suffices to show that deviations to pure strategies are not
beneficial. Due to the linearity of the mixed-strategy payoff in pure action profile payoffs, non-beneficial
pure strategy deviations prevent the existence of profitable deviations to mixed strategies. As we had shown
earlier, this linearity fails in DRGs and prevents us from using the same definition. We illustrate the issue
here with the game from Example 2.
Consider the correlated strategy µ such that µ(U, L) = µ(D, R) = 0.5 in the two player DRG game
(ambiguity set [0.3, 0.5]) of Example 2. The marginal strategies are µ(L | U) = 1 and µ(R | D) = 1. From
Equation 6, it can be seen that ρi(U, L) = 0.3 = ρi(D, L) and also ρi(D, R) = 0.3 = ρi(U, R) for both
players. Then, for the row player recommended to play U, we have µ(L | U)ρ1(U, L) ≥µ(L | U)ρ1(D, L) and
when recommended to play D, we have µ(R | D)ρi(D, R) ≥µ(R | D)ρi(U, R). Similar result holds for the
column player, and hence this is a correlated equilibrium according to this finite matrix game definition of a
correlated equilibrium. However, the mixed strategy x1 for row player with x1(U) = 0.5 gives ρ1(x1, L) = 0.5

Proposition 2. Under assumptions in this sub-section and if ∀i, ρi is polynomial time computable and

Proof. The proof relies on prior results that approximate equilibrium in matrix games are PPAD-complete

Next, DRGs can be reduced to concave games because of the following reasoning. It is straightforward
to check the convexity and compactness of the strategy sets. A strong separation oracle, as defined in Pa-

in the appendix. The proof of Theorem 2 shows that the utility functions ρi are continuous and concave,
and bounded (boundedness allows for scaling, if needed). The assumption ensures that utility is polynomial
time comptuable and Lipschitz, and hence approximated by linear arithmetic circuits [Fearnley et al., 2022,
Theorem E.2]. Then, this sandwich between two problem classes of PPAD-complexity makes approximate
DRE also PPAD-complete.

ambiguity set under consideration. As our focus in this work is on CUMG, we show PPAD complexity results

4.2
Complementarity Program based Equilibrium Computation
In this sub-section, for the sake of easy notation, we assume that 0 ≤ui(a | ξ) ≤1 for all a ∈A. We
investigate computation of Distributionally Robust Equilibria (DRE) in coherent utility measures games,
since these games provide a ready closed form utility for the inner optimization over the ambiguity set. In
particular, we explore the technique of complementarity programs for the CUMG in Example 1. Before diving
into the details, we highlight a few high level aspects. As noted earlier in Proposition 1, DRG (including
CUMG) are continuous games and continuous games are typically not solved as a complementarity program
due to the infinite (often uncountable) pure strategy space. However, DRGs can be viewed as a “lifted game”
where the pure strategy space of the DRG is the mixed strategy space Qm
i=1 Xi of an underlying finite game.
Indeed, Theorem 2 shows the existence of a pure strategy equilibrium in this lifted continuous game. In the
sequel, we show that this view enables the complementary program approach. Further, since we showed that
approximate DRE computation is PPAD complete, it is satisfying to obtain the multilinear complementarity
program (MLCP) formulation for equilibrium computation.
Given this, however, another popular approach of support enumeration is not readily applicable. This is
because support enumeration requires any pure strategy in the support of the equilibrium mixed strategy to
have same payoff as the mixed strategy. This does not hold in CUMGs as shown in Example 1. We defer
investigation of any possible extension of support enumeration or other possible approaches, such as gradient
based or regret based or homotopy continuation approaches, for future work.

Experiments
Intuitively, one expects players in risk-averse games to obtain benefits similar to those found in single-agent
variance-penalized optimization, such as out-of-sample performance improvements, variance reduction, and
probabilistic guarantees. Our numerical experiments present some evidence for such advantages of CUMG
games in data-driven settings. We implement and solve for the equilibrium in three games. First, with
the underlying distribution estimated closely (large-K), the mean-semideviation (MSD) equilibrium set in
a cordination game setup is more robust to distributional uncertainty over the player’s payoffs. Second, in
small-K setting in a variant of prisoner’s dilemma, the players choose conservatively and, hence, achieve
out-of-sample performance improvement of their actual reward by not overfitting to sampling noise. Finally,
the CVaR game demonstrates a monotonic decrease in the player’s payoff variance in robustness parameter
γc at equilibrium and yields probabilistic guarantees on the payoff in a general sum game.

Related Work and Conclusion
Ambiguity sets for distributionally robust games in the literature are often specified in two ways: by optimal
transport/statistical-distance balls centered on a nominal distribution or restrictions on moments of the dis-
tributions within the ambiguity set. In most such settings, the uncertainty over the payoffs is exogenous to
the game. Peng et al. [2020] build and solve a Wasserstein distance-based DRG in a data-driven environment;
Qu et al. [2017], Ahipa¸sao˘glu et al. [2015], Sun and Xu [2016] use moment constrained ambiguity sets in a
DRG set-up. In the limit, these models converges to a robust game Aghassi and Bertsimas [2006] as the prob-
ability mass concentrates on the worst outcome in the support. Loizou [2015] combine moment-constrained
ambiguity sets and CVaR objective within their DRG model. Despite using CVaR, their approach precludes
a tractable closed form solution for the inner infimum. Other simultaneous, one-shot distributionally robust
game settings in the literature include ambiguity sets based on f-divergence Bauso et al. [2017] and chance
constraints Singh et al. [2017], and maximisation over parameterised Bayesian posteriors Liu et al. [2025].
Recent work on DRG by Lanzetti et al. [2025] makes the uncertainty endogenous by introducing distribu-
tional robustness over other players’ mixed strategy profile at equilibrium. Distributional robustness has
also been explored in the Stackelberg game Liu et al. [2018], Ananthanarayanan and Kroer [2022]. Finally,
a number of works also consider transition uncertainty in a repeated single player setting Iyengar [2005], Li
and Shapiro [2025], which has recently been extended to Markov games Blanchet et al. [2023]. In contrast to

Conclusion: This work advances the study of strategic interaction under payoff uncertainty by placing
coherent risk measures at the core of distributionally robust equilibrium analysis. Our results show that
incorporating risk-sensitive preferences fundamentally alters the geometry and interpretation of equilibria:
distributionally robust games are neither classical matrix games nor arbitrary continuous games, but occupy
an intermediate regime. Viewing robustness through the lens of coherent utility measures clarifies how pa-
rameters such as downside or tail aversion shape equilibrium outcomes. At the same time, the continuous
nature of these games introduces new conceptual and computational challenges, including the failure of stan-
dard correlated equilibrium definitions and the inapplicability of familiar techniques such as Lemke–Howson,
even in two-player settings. These challenges point to several promising directions for future research, such
as designing scalable algorithms that exploit problem structure beyond complementarity formulations. Fi-
nally, extending the game framework to dynamic, repeated, or information-asymmetric settings (analogous
to works Lam et al. [2022], Ruszczy´nski [2010] in single player setting) would allow risk-sensitive distribu-
tional robustness to interact with incentives over time, further enriching the modeling of strategic behavior
in data-driven environments.
