Definable groups and fields in t-minimal theories
Will Johnson
May 11, 2026
Abstract
Let T be a theory which is t-minimal, meaning that with respect to some definable
topology, a unary definable set D ⊆M has non-empty interior iff it is infinite. If K
is a definable field in T, then K is finite or “large” in the sense of Pop: any smooth
algebraic curve C over K with at least one K-rational point has infinitely many K-
rational points. We also assign a canonical topology to any abelian definable group G
in a t-minimal theory. In the case where the t-minimal theory is “visceral” in the sense
of Dolich and Goodrick, meaning that the definable topology is induced by a definable
uniformity, we can drop the assumption of abelianity of G, and the resulting topology
on G is a definable manifold in the style of Acosta L´opez and Hasson.

Introduction
By a “definable family” we mean a family {Da}a∈X where Da = {b : (a, b) ∈D} for some
definable sets D, X. A “definable topology” on a definable set D is a topology τ such that
some definable family is a basis of opens for τ. A 1-sorted theory T is topologically minimal
or t-minimal in the sense of Mathews [10, Definition 2.4] if there’s a definable Hausdorff
topology on models of T (on the home sort M 1), such that for any unary definable set
D ⊆M, we have
int(D) ̸= ∅⇐⇒|D| = ∞,
where int(D) denotes the interior of D. An equivalent condition is that
• There are no isolated points in M, and
• Every unary definable set D ⊆M has finite boundary.
Many important classes of theories are t-minimal, such as (dense) o-minimal theories, P-
minimal theories, (dense) C-minimal theories, and hensel minimal theories. Many of these ex-
amples satisfy a stronger condition—they are visceral in the sense of Dolich and Goodrick [4,
Definition 3.3]. The operational difference between t-minimal and visceral is that in a visceral
theory, the definable topology is induced by a definable uniformity (i.e., a “uniform struc-
ture” in the sense of pointset topology). See [4] for a precise formulation. This change makes

The point of this paper is that from even the relatively weak condition of t-minimality,
one can say something non-trivial about definable (not interpretable!) fields.
1.1
Definable fields
Recall that a field (K, +, ·) is large if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions [13]:
• In explicit terms, if P(x, y) is a polynomial over K and P(a, b) = 0 ̸= ∂P
∂y (a, b) for some
(a, b) ∈K2, then the zeroset {(x, y) ∈K2 : P(x, y) = 0} is infinite.
• In algebro-geometric terms, if C is a smooth curve over K and the set of K-rational
points C(K) is non-empty, then C(K) is infinite.
For example, Q fails to be large because the equation x4 + y4 −1 = 0 has only four solutions
by the n = 4 case of Fermat’s last theorem. On the other hand, most fields with a tame
model theory are large (or finite). For example, until the recent examples of “curve-excluding
fields” were discovered [9], one could say that all known examples of fields with decidable
first-order theories were large or finite.
We can now state our main theorem.
Theorem 1.1 (=Corollary 12.2). If K is a definable field in a t-minimal theory, then K is
large or finite.
For example, the field Q is not definable in any t-minimal theory, nor are the non-large
curve-excluding fields of [9].
Remark 1.2. It is essential that we say “definable” rather than “interpretable” in the
theorem. For example, the field Q((t)) of Laurent series over Q is known to be t-minimal,
but it interprets the non-large field Q.
Remark 1.3. The non-definability of Q in t-minimal theories can be seen more directly
from the classic work of J. Robinson showing that all computable functions are definable
in Q.
In particular, there is a definable bijection f : Q2 →Q.
If K is definable in a
t-minimal theory and K ≡Q, then K would define a bijection K2 →K. However, this
contradicts the properties of dimension in t-minimal theories: the bijection would show
dim(K) = dim(K2) = 2 dim(K), so dim(K) = 0 and K is finite. (A similar argument shows
that definable fields must be perfect [6, Corollary 2.53].)
On the other hand, such arguments cannot be used to show that t-minimal theories fail
to define the curve-excluding fields of [9], as CXF is a geometric theory with an excellent
notion of dimension for definable sets [9, Theorem 1.11(2)]. So we are proving something
new.

The proof of Theorem 1.1 involves building a canonical topology on K:
Theorem 1.4 (⊆Theorem 12.1 ∪Corollary 12.2). Let K be an infinite definable field in a
t-minimal theory. There is a unique definable field topology τ on K such that the following
condition holds:
• int(D) ̸= ∅⇐⇒dim(D) = dim(K) for definable D ⊆K.
Moreover, τ satisfies the following conditions:
• τ is Hausdorff and non-discrete.
• Let P(x) ∈K[x] be a separable polynomial of degree d. If a ∈K is a simple root of P
and U ∋a is a τ-neighborhood, then the set
{Q(x) ∈K[x] : Q has a root in U}
is a neighborhood of P.
We say a field topology τ is st-henselian if it satisfies final condition of Theorem 1.4. If one
drops the word “separable”, this turns into the generalized t-henselianity or gt-henselianity
of Dittmann, Walsberg, and Ye [3]. It would be nice to strengthen Theorem 1.4 from st-
henselianity to gt-henselianity, but I could not see how to do it. The connection to largeness
(Theorem 1.1) is that st-henselian fields are large (Theorem 2.9). In fact, one only needs a
weaker condition called bt-henselianity.1 See §2 for more about these conditions and their
connection to largeness.
Question 1.5. Can a pseudofinite field be definable in a t-minimal theory?
Pseudofinite fields are large, so Theorem 1.1 does not exclude this possibility. My original
hope was to answer Question 1.5 negatively by proving the following:
• The canonical topology on a definable field in a t-minimal theory is gt-henselian.
• Pseudofinite fields do not admit gt-henselian topologies.2
I was unable to prove the first point, and the second point turns out to be false—any
countable pseudofinite field admits a gt-henselian topology [7]. On the other hand, it feels
rather outlandish that one could put a reasonable st-henselian topology on a pseudofinite
field and make definable sets be topologically tame.3 Thus Question 1.5 remains wide open.

Returning to the general setting of an infinite definable field K in a t-minimal theory,
suppose that dim(K) = 1. Then the first point of Theorem 1.4 shows that the induced
structure on K is t-minimal—even visceral—with respect to the canonical topology τ.4 It
follows that all the topological tameness theorems of [4, 6] hold, such as generic continuity,
cell decomposition, etc., even if these properties failed in the base t-minimal theory.
Focusing in further, suppose T is a theory of fields, expanded by extra structure, which
is t-minimal with respect to some topology τ0. Then Theorem 1.4 gives a new field topology
τ making T into a visceral theory. One should think of τ as τ0 with the problems fixed. A
typical example is the case where T is RCF and τ0 is the Sorgenfrey topology—the one with
basic open sets [a, b). This topology isn’t a field topology, and there are definable functions
like f(x) = −x that are nowhere continuous.
The topology τ is the correct, standard
topology.

1.2
Definable groups
Underlying the theorems on definable fields are some theorems on definable groups.
Theorem 1.7 (= Theorem 10.8 ∪Lemma 10.16 ∪Theorem 10.21). Let (G, +) be a definable
abelian group in a t-minimal theory. Then there is a unique definable group topology τ on G
with the following property:
• int(D) ̸= ∅⇐⇒dim(D) = dim(G) for definable D ⊆G.
Moreover, τ is non-trivial when |G| > 1.
Unfortunately, I was not able to drop the assumption of abelianity, nor to prove that
τ is Hausdorff, except in the case of fields. In the setting of Theorem 1.7, one can prove
some weak results about tame topology (see §11, especially Theorems 11.10 and 11.18). As
a corollary, homomorphisms are continuous (Corollary 11.11).
The situation is much better in visceral theories, or more generally, t-minimal theories
with generic continuity of definable correspondences.

Theorem 1.8 (≈Theorem 7.12). Let (G, ·) be a definable group in a visceral theory. Then

• τ is Hausdorff
• τ is non-discrete when G is infinite.

1.3
Outline
The paper divides into three parts.
• In Sections 2–3, we focus on the abstract conditions of gold/silver/bronze-t-henselianity.
We show that these conditions imply largeness (Theorem 2.9). We also give an ab-
stract dimension-theoretic condition which ensures that these topological conditions
hold (Theorems 3.7, 3.12). This may be of independent interest. Nothing in these
sections is specific to t-minimal theories, and I expect the criterion of Theorems 3.7
and 3.12 to have further applications in later papers.
• In Sections 4–8, we focus on the case of visceral theories. We build up the machinery
of definable manifolds and their tame topology, and use the usual methods to prove
Theorems 1.8 and 1.4.
• In Sections 9–12 we focus on the much harder case of t-minimal theories, proving
Theorems 1.7 and 1.4 in this case.
The reason for handling the visceral case separately in Sections 4–8 is because the proofs are
easier and the results are stronger.
Appendix A explains why we need finite covers in our definition of “definable manifold”,
and Appendix B gives the lengthy proof of a technical lemma needed to deal with the t-
minimal case in Sections 9–12.
