Designing single-layer PDMS devices for micron to
millimeter-scale deformations†
Leon Valentin Gebhard,ab Alexandre S. Avaro,ab Gabriel Amselem,a and Charles N. Baroudab∗
The elasticity of PDMS has played a central role in advancing important microfluidic technologies,
ranging from early valves to sophisticated organ-on-a-chip systems. However, most deformable
microfluidic devices are based on geometries that require complex multi-layer PDMS architectures and
include thin membranes, leading to difficult microfabrication and poor stability. Recently, Jain, Belkadi
et al. (Biofabrication 16.3 (2024): 035010) introduced a single-layer device in which a wide and long
microfluidic channel was deformed by controlling the pressure in two independent and adjacent air
chambers. While they demonstrated the ability to deform the channel ceiling to compress biological
materials, the design parameters remain unexplored. Here, we perform a numerical study on 14,336
variants of this device and identify the height of the PDMS layer, the width of the microchannel and
the width of the air chamber as the main features that determine the ceiling deformation. Three
deformation modes are observed as the geometrical parameters are varied: A U shape with a central
minimum, a W shape with two minima and a central maximum, or an inverse U shape with an
upward-bulging single maximum. The numerical results are validated in experiments that reproduce the
three shapes for the predicted geometries and demonstrate vertical ceiling deformations ranging from a
few microns to the millimeter scale. The generality of this approach is demonstrated for two example
applications: A fully closing single-layer microfluidic valve and an optical lens of controllable
anisotropy. This work leverages the rapid prototyping enabled by 3D printing or micro-milling to open
new perspectives in microfluidic actuation.
Introduction
Many microfluidic technologies have been developed to me-
chanically deform the channels, starting with the production of
externally-controlled valves and pumps1–4, and followed more re-
cently by devices that allow the confinement5, compression6–8, or
stimulation of cellular monolayers9 and cell-laden hydrogels10,11.
In many of these cases, an external pressure source is used to de-
form an elastic material along predetermined directions, in order
to achieve channel closure or cell stimulation as desired. As a re-
sult the device performance is determined by the elastic properties
of the material combined with the geometry and fabrication proto-
cols, with many devices relying on the use of thin membranes and
complex multilayer PDMS architechtures. The complexity that is
inherent with these designs raises the probability of failure dur-
ing the microfabrication, which in turn raises the cost and time
required to obtain functioning devices, for both academic labora-
tories and commercial companies. Nevertheless, the importance
of deforming microfluidic devices on the microscale has been re-
peatedly reported, namely for mechanobiology applications on in-
dividual cells12 or cell collectives13. In this context, there remains
a need for new geometries that allow large and well-controlled de-
formations of microfluidic devices without the need for complex

a similar geometry was later used to manipulate droplets and mi-

nel, into which the samples were loaded, flanked by two deep air
chambers that were connected to a negative pressure source. It
was shown that modifying the pressure inside the air chambers
could be used to deform the ceiling of the microfluidic channel,
even though the two circuits were fluidically independent. Most
importantly, this device was produced in a single molding step, by
pouring PDMS into a 3D-printed master, making it robust and easy

suitability to the task at hand. It therefore remains unclear how
general is the behavior that was demonstrated, and what other ge-

cilitating or hindering the deformation of the microfluidic channel.
This is performed using systematic geometric variations of a base
chip design and observing how different relevant dimensions influ-
ence the deformation of the PDMS structure. By scanning the pa-
rameters over a wide range of numerical values, we discover new
deformation modes that were not reported previously. The numer-
ical results are then validated experimentally by making channels
having different dimensions. Finally, two applications of these de-
formable micro-devices are demonstrated: A single-layer valve and
an anisotropic optical lens.
Exploring deformation regimes
Device geometry and description of the numerical model
The base geometry investigated in the current study is based on a
simplified version of the devices of Refs.14,15. It consists of a long,
shallow microfluidic channel flanked by two high air chambers of
equal length, as shown Fig. 1A and B. The device’s pneumatic cir-
cuit consists of two air chambers connected to a single pressure
controller, and is fluidically isolated from the microchannel. Cor-
respondingly, a negative air chamber pressure induces a deforma-
tion of the entire monolithic device (Fig. 1C and D) but does not
directly set the pressure inside the microfluidic channel.
Given the large size contrast between the streamwise y dimen-
sion, compared with the spanwise width x and vertical depth z
(see Fig. 1A,B), we model the deformations of the cross-section
of the device under a plane-strain assumption.
The resulting
two-dimensional (2D) model is simulated using a finite element
method in Abaqus (Dassault Systemes, France) as shown in Fig.
1E,F. Note that only half of the domain is simulated because of the
system’s symmetry with respect to the central zy-plane.
In this model, PDMS is described as an incompressible neo-
Hookean material. This material law describes moderate defor-
mations more accurately than linear elasticity but is still fully de-
fined by a single stiffness C. Unless indicated otherwise, simula-
tions were performed with C = 204 kPa to match the deformations
observed experimentally. The Young’s modulus equivalent to this
value of C is E = 0.6 MPa16, which is in the lower range of pre-
viously reported stiffness for PDMS.17–20 The device’s self-contact
and its contact with the glass are described as frictionless, and
the glass is treated as rigid body. The PDMS-glass bond was cap-
tured by fixing both rotation and displacement at the bottom of
the device. Detailed descriptions of the meshing and partitioning
process, along with the convergence testing, are provided in the
Material and Methods section.
Geometric features and channel deformation
The microdevice geometry is defined by a large number of geomet-
ric parameters, whose variation influences the deformation profile
of the microchannel. The current study explores the impact of six
of these parameters, as sketched in Fig. 2A: The total height of the
PDMS layer (H); the width of the microfluidic channel (Wch); the
height and width of the air chambers (respectively Hac and Wac);
the distance between the air chambers and the microfluidic chan-
nel (Wcc), as well as the distance between the air chamber and the
side wall of the PDMS (Wcs).
For each set of values for the geometric parameters the numer-
ical model computes the deformation of the whole microdevice,
following the protocol described above. An example deformed de-
vice is shown in Fig. 2B, in which the colors indicate the verti-
cal strain, with red and blue respectively indicating extension and
compression. This strain field shows that although the pressure
is applied only in the air chamber, the deformations extend over
the entire device, leading to a large decrease in the microfluidic
channel height.
While the channel’s deformation mode is determined by its geo-
metric parameters, its amplitude depends on the pressure applied
to the air chambers. Fig. 2C shows the displacement of the chan-
nel ceiling as a function of the driving pressure, for the example
geometry of Fig. 2B. We observe that the deformation magnitude
increases with higher pressures but the general shape of the ceil-
ing conserves its U shape, with one minimum in the center, for all
values of the pressure.
This U shaped ceiling however is not universal for all geome-
tries. Indeed, by varying the different parameters, it is possible
to observe qualitatively different deformation modes, as shown in
Fig. 3B: The channel ceiling of thick device (H/W = 0.525) with
wide air chambers and a narrow channel deforms in the previously
described ’U-shape’. The thin (H/W = 0.15) device with the wide
channel shows a similar shape in the opposite direction, which we
correspondingly term ’inverse U-shape’. For a device of moderate
thickness (H/W = 0.275) however the ceiling deformation assumes
a ’W-shape’, with two minima near the channel edge and a maxi-
mum in the center.
Nevertheless the variation, with increasing pressure, of the ceil-
ing displacement at the channel mid-place ∆zsym shows that the
amplitude of these deformations remains nearly linear with the
applied pressure for each mode (see Fig. 2E). Predicting the mode
of deformation of the device therefore involves scanning the space
of the geometric parameters.

parameters simultaneously leading to a linear scaling of compu-

Experimental validation of the numerical results
To assess the accuracy of the numerical analysis, its predictions
were validated experimentally using three devices with geome-
tries corresponding to those in Fig. 3. The devices were fabricated
by molding a standard mixture of PDMS (10:1 resin:cross-linker,
cured at 70◦C for 2 hours) on 3D-printed masters (Asiga printer,
plasgray v2 resin) and plasma bonding the PDMS devices on glass
microscope slides (See Materials and Methods for details).
The channels were then filled with a fluorescein solution and
the local channel depth was quantified by measuring variations in
the fluorescence intensity, as the channel was deformed. In this
fluorescence intensity profilometry images were captured by a low
numerical aperture objective (2x, NA=0.06) in order to maximize
the depth of field of the objective. A scan of the complete microflu-
idic channel was obtained at each applied pressure and the image
intensity in the deformed state was normalized by the intensity at
zero driving pressure. The local height could then be computed by
assuming a linear relationship between fluorescent intensity and
channel depth (See Materials and Methods).
The experimental deformations reproduce the U, W and inverse
U, modes predicted by the model, as shown by the color maps of
Fig. 4 (see also SI movies). In the case of the U mode (Fig. 4A),
the experimental data reveals that the downward deformation in-
creases towards the center in both the streamwise (y) and span-

Applications of the deformable devices
While the explorations above show the range of deformations that
can be achieved with this deformable chip, applications of this ap-
proach can further leverage an immense space of available geo-
metric parameters. Two example applications are shown below,
each of which extends the possibilities by relaxing some of the
constraints of the base geometry.
Fully closing microfluidic valve
The ceiling deformation in the case of the U shape show that, while
it is possible to touch the bottom surface by the PDMS, the sides of
the channel prevent full closure. It is nevertheless possible to com-
pletely block the flow inside the device, thus producing a microflu-
idic valve, by modifying the shape of the channel ceiling. Such a
device is shown in Fig. 5, where the flat channel ceiling is replaced
by a "bell-shaped" ceiling, i.e. having a curved cross-section (See
Fig. 5A).
This channel can be fully closed when the pressure is applied in
the air chambers. This is demonstrated by filling the microchan-
nel with ink and observing that the blue ink is fully ejected under
high pressure (Fig. 5B and C). Fluorescence intensity profilometry
further provides a quantitative measure of the closing at different
values of the pressure (Fig. 5D, E). Indeed, the wide range of pres-
sures over which this device functions implies that it can be used
as both an on-off valve and also as a variable hydraulic resistance.
Deformable channels as tuneable anisotropic lenses
In all of the above discussion, the cross-section was assumed to
represent a channel that extends linearly in one dimension, with a
view for flowing fluids. This assumption of a linear geometry does
not need to be conserved, as the cross section can also represent
a system with rotational symmetry. A miniature deformable de-
vice with a circular geometry is well suited as an optical element,
which have many potential applications in microscopy24,25 or in
consumer optical devices, such as cell phones or other cameras.
A lens with variable optical properties is shown in Fig. 6. While
the cross-section of the device is maintained (Fig. 6A), the top view
now consists of a central circular "channel", surrounded by an an-
nular air chamber, as shown in Fig. 6B. The central channel of such
a circular device can function as lens with tuneable optical prop-
erties. While in the relaxed state, the transparency of the PDMS
ensures an optic neutrality of the lens, applying pressure to its
air chambers induces curvature in the different surfaces and thus
changes the optical properties.

Materials and Methods
Numerical Simulation
Finite element simulations were performed using Abaqus (Dassault
Systèmes) and automated via the Abaqus-Python interface. The
cross-sectional geometry (Fig. 1C) was approximated using a 2D
plane strain model, motivated by the fact that the chips depth sig-
nificantly exceeds its width and height.
The PDMS was described as incompressible Neo-Hookean hy-
perelastic material using C10 = 206 kPa if not indicated otherwise.
This constitutive law was chosen to account for large deforma-
tions that exceed the small-strain limits of linear elasticity. While
more complex models, such as the five-parameter Mooney-Rivlin
model, can capture PDMS behavior with higher fidelity at extreme
strains26, we chose the Neo-Hookean model to maintain the phys-
ical interpretability of a single material constant.
The pressure was applied as a ’pressure load’ on the surface of
the air chamber and the bottom of the chip was fixed using the ’En-
castre’ boundary condition. The glass slide was modeled as analyt-
ical rigid surface also fixed in place using the ’Encastre’ condition.
The contact between the two surfaces and the PDMS self-contact
were both defined as hard and friction-free.
To enable the creation of a structured mesh, the geometry was
partitioned into rectangular sub-domains. The partitioning strat-
egy also allowed for local mesh refinement in regions where high
strain was anticipated or high resolution of deformation data de-
sired, specifically at the channel ceiling and the region between the
air chamber and the microchannel. Also, the partitioning enabled a
mesh composed entirely of quadrilateral first order reduced hybrid
integration (CPE4RH) elements with edges aligned to the princi-
pal axes (see Fig. 2A). The choice of reduced hybrid integration
elements is necessary to prevent volumetric locking, as we assume
the material to be incompressible.27
The mesh density was evaluated through a convergence study
(SI Fig. S3), in which the element size in the refined regions was
varied from 0.025 mm to 0.15 mm. Convergence was assessed by
calculating the L1-norm of the difference in vertical ceiling dis-
placement between the finest discretizations and the evaluated
mesh sizes. Based on this study, we selected a mesh size of 0.1 mm
for regions close to the channel and 0.2 mm for the bulk material
to balance numerical accuracy with computational efficiency.
Device Fabrication
The devices were manufactured using standard PDMS prototyping
techniques. Molds were produced either via digital light process-
ing (DLP) 3D printing (Asiga MAX, PlasGRAY resin) except for the
valve, whose aluminium mold was produced milled using a CNC
(Datron). 3D-printed molds were post-cured under UV light for
5 minutes and baked overnight at 70◦C to prevent PDMS curing
inhibition caused by unpolymerized resin residues28. To ensure
clean detachment of the PDMS, these molds were functionalized
by filling them with Novec 1720 (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by a 30-
minute curing at 70◦C. Milled molds required no post-processing
beyond an isopropanol wash.
The PDMS (Sylgard 184, Dow) was prepared at a 10:1 base-to-
cross linker ratio, degassed in vacuum for 30 minutes and cured
for two hours at 70◦C. An exception was made for the valve and
optical element, for which a 20:1 ratio was utilized to increase
elasticity. Following curing, 1 mm diameter holes were punched for
the microfluidic channel inlets and outlets, and a 2 mm diameter
hole was created for the aspiration tubing. Finally, the PDMS slabs
and glass microscopy slides were plasma-activated in a vacuum
plasma cleaner for one minute and bonded together. The chips
were post-baked for thirty minutes at 80◦C.
Fluorescence intensity profilometry
To experimentally measure the channel deformation a saturated
aqueous fluorescein solution (fluorescein sodium salt) was filtered
and diluted 1:1000 in isopropanol and flushed into the channel.
Fluorescence was measured for varying air chamber pressures at
λem = 510 nm using 100 ms exposure and exciting at λex = 460 nm
using 1 % of the 3.5 W maximum power of a Lumencore Sola Light
Engine. The channel was then flushed with pure isopropanol and
measured again to obtain a dark-field image.
A flat-field correction was applied by using the image with pure
IPA as the dark-field image and an image of the undeformed

Tunable Lense quantification
The magnification of the lens was quantified by placing the device
on top of an inverted petri dish, positioning it ≈1 cm above an
image of strips of equal width. A sequence of images was acquired
during which the air chamber pressure was linearly increased from
0 to −90 kPa. A kymograph orthogonal to the stripes was then
obtained using the Fiji ’reslice’ function.


## Page 8


The extremities of each line were set to zero, to then perform a
reverse Fourier transform on them. The inverse of the dominant
frequency of the reverse Fourier transform then corresponds to the
wavelength and thus the thickness of the stripes.
The anisotropic image distortion was measured by again placing
the device on top of an inverted petri dish and positioning a check-
ered sheet of paper below it. An image sequence was acquired
while the pressure was increased in two opposing air chambers.
The center of the image containing only one cross that is aligned
with the devices channel (=lens) is cropped. For each image the
lines are segmented through an intensity threshold computed us-
ing Otsu’s method.29 Then, the central intersection of the cross is
cut out by calculating the center point and omitting all pixels closer
than 20% of the image dimension from the center point.
The remaining image contains four lines which are not con-
nected. For each isolated region the angle with respect to the cen-
ter is computed and the average computed. The angles obtained
for the first image are subtracted from all other angles to compute
the distortion. The resulting angles are averaged according to their
symmetry.
