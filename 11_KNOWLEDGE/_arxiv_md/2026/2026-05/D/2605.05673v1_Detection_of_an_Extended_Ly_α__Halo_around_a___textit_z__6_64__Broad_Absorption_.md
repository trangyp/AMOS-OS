ABSTRACT
We present the first results from a program searching for extended Lyα halos around high redshift
(z ≳6.5) quasars using the red channel of the Keck Cosmic Web Imager (KCWI). Our observations re-
veal a Lyα halo extending to ≃11 pkpc around the z = 6.64 broad absorption line quasar J0910−0414.
The Lyα velocity field displays a rotation-like gradient, and the gas velocity dispersion is consistent
with gravitationally dominated motion (σLyα < 300 km s−1). Comparison with the [C II] kinematics
of the host galaxy core from ALMA observations shows that the Lyα-emitting gas extends over a much
larger region, shows distinct kinematics, and has a smaller velocity dispersion (σLyα ≃0.6σ[C II]). The
Lyα spectral region of the quasar is largely obscured by a deep N V absorption trough, and as a result,
roughly 55% of the total Lyα flux is from the extended halo. These observations demonstrate the
potential of KCWI for probing the cool gas reservoir that fuels the growth of quasars and their hosts
in the epoch of reionization.
Keywords: Early Universe (435), Quasars (1319), Reionization (1383), Circumgalactic medium (1879)
1. INTRODUCTION
The discovery of quasars beyond z > 6 (X. Fan et al.
2001) has presented challenges to theories of early su-
permassive black hole (SMBH) formation and growth.
Recent observations of these reionization-era quasars
have established the presence of SMBHs with estimated
masses on the order of 109 M⊙(e.g., Y. Shen et al. 2019;
M. Onoue et al. 2019; J. Yang et al. 2021; E. P. Farina
et al. 2022; S. Belladitta et al. 2025) within a billion
years of the Big Bang. Their discovery raises questions
about how early SMBHs can grow to billions of solar
masses in such a short time, with current theories requir-
ing black hole seeds to undergo periods of sustained ac-
cretion at the Eddington rate (for heavy seeds), or short
bursts of super-Eddington accretion (e.g., M. Volonteri

& M. J. Rees 2006; X. Fan et al. 2023; D. M. Alexander
et al. 2025). Through mm and sub-mm observations,
these quasar hosts have been found to display high dust
masses and rapid star formation (e.g., B. P. Venemans
et al. 2016; R. Decarli et al. 2018; F. Wang et al. 2024b).
Measurements of SMBH mass and star formation rate
imply that these quasars must reside in environments
with an ample supply of cool gas to fuel star formation
and SMBH growth. Hydrodynamical simulations show
that the most massive black holes at z ∼6 (∼109 M⊙)
reside in dark matter halos at the intersection of gas fila-
ments within overdense regions in the early Universe (T.
Costa et al. 2014), and that radiation from the quasar
can interact with the cool gas, leading to the appearance
of an extended Lyα halo (e.g., Z. Haiman & M. J. Rees
2001; T. Costa et al. 2022; X. Fan et al. 2023). Targeted
studies of extended Lyα emission can provide important
insights about the kinematics and spatial distribution
of the cool gas supply, as well as the orientation of the

The development of integral-field spectro-
graphs on 8-10 m class telescopes has enabled simulta-
neous spatial and spectroscopic information to be gath-
ered within a single set of observations, allowing for more
complete and detailed characterization of the extended
emission. Ground-based surveys utilizing integral-field
spectrographs have searched for extended Lyα halos
around quasars at z ∼2–6 (e.g., E. Borisova et al. 2016;
E. P. Farina et al. 2017; F. Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2019;
A. B. Drake et al. 2019; Z. Cai et al. 2019; E. P. Farina
et al. 2019; E. Herwig et al. 2024; J. Gonz´alez Lobos
et al. 2025), with detections of up to z ∼2 achieved
with the Keck Cosmic Web Imager (KCWI; P. Morris-
sey et al. 2018), and up to z = 6.62 (E. P. Farina et al.
2017, 2019) at the VLT with the Multi Unit Spectro-
scopic Explorer (MUSE; R. Bacon et al. 2010). To date,
MUSE has been at the forefront of searches for extended
Lyα around distant quasars due to its extensive wave-
length coverage, but its cutoff at 9300 ˚A limits searches
to z ≲6.6. In order to trace the physical processes re-
sponsible for early SMBH growth and evolution, it is
necessary to push Lyα searches to higher redshifts and
probe the first generation of quasars (F. Wang et al.
2019).
The red arm of KCWI, also known as the Keck Cosmic
Reionization Mapper (KCRM; R. C. McGurk et al. 2024;
M. Kassis et al. 2024), was commissioned in semester
2023A, with routine observations starting in 2023B. The
red channel extends KCWI’s effective wavelength cov-
erage out to ≈1 µm, and its sensitivity beyond 9000
˚A enables searches for Lyα halos out to z = 7.1 at depths
comparable to the deepest MUSE observations at z ∼6.
We initiated a program in semester 2023B to search for
extended Lyα around z > 6.5 quasars, including sources
selected from the sample of the JWST ASPIRE program
(GO-2078, PI: Feige Wang; F. Wang et al. 2023), which
obtained slitless spectroscopy covering the Hβ+[O III]
spectral region at the quasars’ redshifts.
In this Letter, we present KCWI observations of
J0910−0414, a z = 6.64 ASPIRE quasar. J0910−0414
is a broad absorption line (BAL) quasar discovered by
F. Wang et al. (2019), characterized by its deep absorp-
tion troughs blueward of N V and C IV (J. Yang et al.
2021). ALMA [C II] observations yield a systemic red-
shift of z[C II] = 6.6363 ± 0.0003 (F. Wang et al. 2024b).
Near-infrared (NIR) observations presented by J. Yang
et al. (2021) yield a Mg II-based black hole mass of
MBH = (3.59±0.61)×109 M⊙, one of the most massive
SMBHs known at z > 6.5 (F. Wang et al. 2024a). Sub-
mm observations also suggest that J0910−0414 hosts ex-
treme star formation activity, with star formation rate
(SFR) estimates ranging from ∼100 to ∼1000 M⊙yr−1
depending on the tracer (F. Wang et al. 2024b). Recent
studies of the quasar environment further reveal that
J0910−0414 resides at the center of a dense protoclus-
ter, with multi-wavelength observations confirming the
presence of 12 Lyα and three [C II] emitters, one of
which is interacting with the quasar at a projected sep-
aration of ≃3 proper kiloparsecs (pkpc; F. Wang et al.
2024a).
This paper is organized as follows. We describe the ob-
servations and data reduction in Section 2. In Section
3, we detail our methods used to subtract the quasar
emission and isolate the extended Lyα, and create maps
of the spatially resolved kinematics. We interpret our
results in Section 4, and conclude the paper in Section
5. Throughout this paper, we adopt a flat ΛCDM cos-
mology with H0 = 70 km s−1 Mpc−1, Ωm = 0.3, and
ΩΛ = 0.7. At z = 6.64, the Universe is 0.807 Gyr old,
and an angular distance of 1′′ corresponds to 5.4 pkpc
(E. L. Wright 2006).
2. OBSERVATIONS AND DATA REDUCTION
We observed J0910−0414 with KCWI on the Keck
II telescope as part of Program 2024A N061 (PI: Feige
Wang). Our instrument configuration uses the medium
slicer with the RM2 grating and 2 × 2 binning, which
yields a field of view (FoV) of 16.′′5 × 20.′′4, a wave-
length coverage of 8500–10500 ˚A, a slice width of 0.′′679,
and a detector plate scale of 0.′′291 px−1. We chose a
central wavelength of 9500 ˚A so that the Lyα emis-
sion line at a redshift of z = 6.6 lies near the center
of the observed wavelength coverage.
From FeAr arc
line exposures, we measured an average full-width at
half-maximum (FWHM) of 2.4 ˚A across all slices, which
translates to an instrumental dispersion of σinst ≈32
km s−1.
We observed J0910−0414 at five dither positions (ini-
tial pointing plus pointings on the corners of a 1.′′0 × 1.′′0
square centered on the quasar), with this dither pattern
executed at position angles (PAs) of 0◦and 90◦, to en-
sure equal spatial sampling in the horizontal and vertical
directions for the final data cube. For each dither posi-
tion and PA, we took a series of four 300 s exposures to
ensure better cosmic ray rejection. Altogether, our data
constitutes 3.67 h of integration time, with an observed

3. ISOLATING THE EXTENDED EMISSION
Detection of the faint, extended Lyα emission requires
subtraction of the bright, spatially unresolved emission
from the quasar. We performed a procedure to search
for the extended emission similar to the method used
by the Reionization Epoch QUasar InvEstigation with
MUSE (REQUIEM; E. P. Farina et al. 2019) survey.
We created a model of the point-spread function
(PSF) by first collapsing the cube over the spectral re-
gion 9450–10200 ˚A, excluding any wavelength channels
containing strong absorption features or sky subtraction
artifacts. We then fit a 2D Gaussian to the quasar in
the resulting white light image and cropped the image
to a circular stamp with a radius equal to five times the
Gaussian σ. The stamp serves as an empirical model of
the PSF, which is assumed to be free of extended emis-
sion and contains negligible contribution from the host
galaxy. For each wavelength channel, we scaled the PSF
model according to the total flux within a 1-pixel radius
of the quasar center, which we assume to be dominated
by the unresolved AGN emission. We created a cube
of the quasar model and subtracted this from our pri-
mary data cube, resulting in a cube that contains only
extended emission. The collapsed science, PSF model,
and PSF-subtracted cubes are displayed in Figure 1.
To search for extended Lyα in our PSF-subtracted
cube, we follow J. F. Hennawi & J. X. Prochaska (2013)
and create a separate cube of the smoothed signal-to-
noise (S/N), using a 3D Gaussian kernel with σspat =
0.′′291 and σspec = 0.966 ˚A, equivalent to the dimensions
of a single voxel in the data cube. We apply a friend-
of-friends (FoF) clustering algorithm on the smoothed

We corrected our cubes for Galactic extinction with
the dust extinction11 (K. D. Gordon 2024) package,
using E(B −V ) = 0.0225 (E. F. Schlafly & D. P.

spectrum after smoothing with a 1D Gaussian kernel
with σspec = 0.966 ˚A. We calculate the velocity moments
non-parametrically, by direct integration from λmin to
λmax.
We correct for the instrumental dispersion by

N V line, Lyα is located at an offset of ∆vN V = −0.02c
(−6000 km s−1), corresponding in velocity to the blue
end of the first C IV trough at −5000 km s−1. If we
assume that the N V and C IV BALs have similar ve-
locity structure, then the Lyα emission is located where
a BAL trough is starting to lift, thus allowing some nu-
clear emission to shine through.
4.2. Size of the extended emission
The halo surrounding J0910−0414 is distributed
asymmetrically around the quasar with the majority of
the emission located ≲2.′′0 (≲11 pkpc) toward the
north.
We measured the maximum distance between
two spaxels within the 2D spatial mask dmax, and the
distance from the quasar to the farthest spaxel within
the 2D spatial mask dr, obtaining values of dmax = 21.3
pkpc and dr = 11.4 pkpc, respectively. Our measure-
ments fall within the range of sizes reported for the de-
tections in the REQUIEM sample.
F. Wang et al.
(2024b) report effective sizes of R[C II] = 1.48 ± 0.19
pkpc and Rcont = 0.62 ± 0.05 pkpc for the [C II] and far
infrared (FIR) continuum emission regions, respectively.
These sizes are an order of magnitude smaller than dr for
the Lyα halo, but it should be noted that the measure-
ments by F. Wang et al. (2024b) were obtained by fit-
ting 2D Gaussians to maps of the [C II] and continuum
flux, while our measurement is based on the farthest
spaxel from the quasar containing Lyα emission. If we
instead use the largest radial bin of the radial surface
brightness profile with a detection above the 2σ back-
ground noise level (e.g., dLyα
max in D. ˇDurovˇc´ıkov´a et al.
2025a), we measure a nebula size of dLyα
max = 11.5 ± 2.3
pkpc. Regardless, our measurements show that the size
of the Lyα halo around J0910−0414 is approximately
eight times the size of the [C II] emission region, and
18 times the size of the FIR continuum emission region.

4.3. Kinematics of the extended emission
From the halo spectrum, the centroid of the extended
Lyα emission yields a velocity shift of vhalo
Lyα = 203 ± 15
km s−1 relative to the expected wavelength of the line
at the [C II] redshift. This low velocity offset relative
to the [C II] systemic redshift signifies a close connec-
tion between the extended halo and the host galaxy of
J0910−0414. We measure nebular Lyα line widths of
σhalo
Lyα = 242 ± 9 km s−1, and FWHMhalo
Lyα = 474 ± 74
km s−1, which are lower than the corresponding val-
ues for the vast majority of the REQUIEM sample, but
still consistent with expectations of gravitationally dom-
inated motion (e.g., σLyα < 400 km s−1) within a dark
matter halo. We reiterate that our kinematic measure-
ments are affected by the effects of IGM attenuation, as
well as resonant scattering. The peak of Lyα at z ∼6
is typically absorbed by neutral hydrogen, which means
that our measurement of vhalo
Lyα is likely redshifted rela-
tive to the intrinsic bulk velocity of the halo. Resonant
scattering likely contributes to the broadening of the
line profile and can lead to larger line widths. While
this strengthens the case that the halo gas kinematics
are gravitationally dominated at lower redshifts (e.g.,
F. Arrigoni Battaia et al. 2019), attenuation of the Lyα
profile means that these and subsequent results require
confirmation from observations of a non-resonant line
(e.g., Hα).
Our measurement of FWHMhalo
Lyα
for J0910−0414
is notable when compared to the [C
II] line width
(FWHM[C II]
= 783 ± 40 km s−1; F. Wang et al.
2024b).
While FWHM differences between Lyα and
[C II] are expected (e.g., radiative transfer effects and
attenuation of Lyα, different regions within the over-
all gravitational potential, different gas phases), halos
detected in the REQUIEM survey consistently display
FWHMhalo
Lyα ≳2.0 × FWHM[C II] (see Figure 5 of E. P.
Farina et al. 2019).
In contrast to this, J0910−0414
has FWHMhalo
Lyα ≃0.6 × FWHM[C II].
This reversal
could reflect dynamically hotter conditions for the [C II]-
emitting gas (e.g., from rapid star formation) in the com-
pact host galaxy core relative to the Lyα-emitting gas
in the circumgalactic medium.

4.4. Spatially Resolved Kinematics
We display the spatially resolved kinematics of Lyα,
calculated relative to the halo’s systemic redshift, in Fig-
ure 4. Overall, the moment maps display ordered, qui-
escent kinematics. Velocity maps of extended Lyα tend
to show no evidence of ordered motion (e.g., rotation,
inflows, outflows), although the resonant nature of the
Lyα emission may prevent the appearance of such kine-
matic signatures (e.g., S. Cantalupo et al. 2005; E. P.
Farina et al. 2019; A. B. Drake et al. 2022). Notably,
J1605−0112, another high-z BAL quasar (z = 4.92)
having an extended Lyα halo, displays no clear pattern
in its velocity field (M. Ginolfi et al. 2018). J0910−0414
displays a velocity gradient oriented from the southeast
to the northwest, and spanning the range (−330 km s−1,
+530 km s−1). We note that the velocity dispersion map
shows elevated values coincident with this velocity gradi-
ent, so it is possible that the observed dispersions result
in part from unresolved velocity gradients.
Rotation-like velocity shears, while rare among ex-
tended Lyα halos, have been reported in other sys-
tems, including J1020+1040 from QSO MUSEUM, and
P231−20 from the REQUIEM sample (F. Arrigoni Bat-
taia et al. 2018; E. P. Farina et al. 2019), both of which
also reside in overdense regions (F. Arrigoni Battaia
et al. 2018; R. A. Meyer et al. 2022; F. Wang et al.
2024a). However, while such velocity gradients can arise
from a variety of physical scenarios (e.g., F. Arrigoni
Battaia et al. 2018), the limited extent, spatial resolu-
tion, and S/N of the halo presented here do not allow us
to identify its physical origin. Furthermore, the effects
of resonant scattering and IGM attenuation at z ∼6
complicate the interpretation of the Lyα kinematics. A
clear interpretation of the halo kinematics requires deep,
high-resolution observations of a non-resonant emission
line.
ALMA observations of the host galaxy [C II] emis-
sion of J0910−0414 by F. Wang et al. (2024b) reveal
a rotation-like velocity gradient oriented North-South,
spanning the range (−100 km s−1, +100 km s−1), as
well as high velocity dispersions (200 km s−1 ≲σ[C II] ≲
300 km s−1).
The contrast between the orientations
of the velocity gradients as well as the line width mea-
surements demonstrate that the Lyα-emitting gas in the
surrounding reservoir and the [C II]-emitting gas in the
compact core of the host galaxy are kinematically decou-
pled, consistent with findings of other quasars at z ∼6
(A. B. Drake et al. 2022).
Previous observations and simulations indicate that
extended Lyα halos around quasars may be powered by
Lyα fluorescence (recombination emission of gas pho-
toionized by the quasar), collisional excitation, resonant
scattering of Lyα, or a combination of these mecha-
nisms (e.g., J. F. Hennawi & J. X. Prochaska 2013; S.
Cantalupo 2017; T. Costa et al. 2022; H. Hoshi et al.
2025). Lyα fluorescence is believed to be the dominant
mechanism powering the halos (e.g., D. ˇDurovˇc´ıkov´a
et al. 2025b), and our measured halo luminosity for
J0910−0414 is within the typical range of values at z ∼6
(LLyα ≲1044 erg s−1) expected for fluorescence from op-
tically thin clouds in the gas reservoir (E. P. Farina et al.
2019). Furthermore, the halo of J0910−0414 is consis-
tent with the correlation between the Lyα halo luminos-
ity and quasar ionizing luminosity reported by H. Hoshi
et al. (2025). These findings suggest that similar to other
halos at around quasars at z ∼6, multiple mechanisms
may be powering the halo around J0910−0414. Given
the SFR estimates by F. Wang et al. (2024b), it is also
possible that the rapid star formation in J0910−0414’s
host galaxy contributes to the observed halo.
5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
From our ongoing KCWI program to search for Lyα
halos around z > 6.5 quasars, we have detected signif-
icant extended Lyα emission out to ≃11 pkpc around
the BAL quasar J0910−0414.
This result demonstrates the potential of KCWI’s red
channel for detecting the low surface brightness emis-
sion at redshifts that were previously inaccessible with
ground-based integral-field instruments. Future papers
will include analysis for the rest of our sample, extending
our search range to z = 7.1. The capabilities of KCWI’s
red channel provide a unique opportunity to probe the
fuel supply powering these early quasars, and to push
the frontier of extended Lyα searches deeper into the
epoch of reionization.
