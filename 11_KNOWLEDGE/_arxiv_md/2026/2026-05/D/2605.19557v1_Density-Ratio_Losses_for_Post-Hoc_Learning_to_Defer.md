Abstract
We study post-hoc Learning to Defer (L2D) through the lens of ideal distributions: divergence-
regularized reweightings of the data distribution under which a model attains low loss. We define deferral
via the density-ratio between a model’s and an expert’s ideals. Using the reduction from density-ratio
estimation to class-probability estimation, we derive the DR CPE losses for post-hoc L2D scorers.
Deferral decisions are then made by thresholding the scorer, allowing deferral rates to be adjusted
without retraining. For KL-based ideal distributions, our deferral rules recovers Chow’s rule under the
original distribution and a connection to an expert-tilted Bayes posterior—which incorporates the expert’s
performance—depending on if the ideal distributions are joint or marginal distributions. Experimentally,
our approach is competitive compared to common baselines and more robust across dataset settings. More
broadly, our results cast post-hoc L2D as density-ratio learning between ideal distributions, bridging
Chow-style rules, expert comparison, and elucidating connections to related learning settings including
anomaly detection.

Introduction
Machine learning (ML) models are now embedded in many tasks we encounter every day. As these systems
are deployed in increasingly high-stakes domains, including healthcare [Esteva et al., 2017, Kadampur and
Al Riyaee, 2020, Zoabi et al., 2021, Ma et al., 2024, Singhal et al., 2025] and finance [Burrell, 2016, Yousefi
et al., 2019, Li et al., 2023], it is crucial that they make predictions only when their outputs are reliable. In
such high-stakes decision-making systems, ideally, a synthesis of both ML prediction and human expertise
can be used [Topol, 2019]: ML models can defer to experts whenever their own predictions are deemed
unreliable, and the burden on human experts can be reduced by leveraging ML models. Dual to the problem
of predicting responsibly, the rise of huge ML models and edge deployment has created strong demand for
systems that improve efficiency by routing only difficult inputs to a larger model while handling easier cases
with a smaller model [Viola and Jones, 2001, Mamou et al., 2022, Varshney and Baral, 2022, Khalili et al.,
2022, Dohan et al., 2022, Gupta et al., 2024, Narasimhan et al., 2025]. Learning to defer (L2D) proposes a
solution to these problems by modifying the standard supervised learning setting to allow (smaller) models to
defer their predictions to an upstream (potentially human) expert [Madras et al., 2018].
To allow models to abstain from predictions, learning to reject (L2R) was initially proposed [Chow,
1957, 1970, Cortes et al., 2016], where rejection is learned without access to the downstream expert used at
inference. A useful categorization of these approaches are by how abstention is represented. In score-based
approaches, abstention is derived from the predictor’s own scores, encompassing classical reject-option
rules, convex-surrogate formulations, and confidence-threshold methods [Chow, 1957, 1970, Herbei and
Wegkamp, 2006, Bartlett and Wegkamp, 2008, Yuan and Wegkamp, 2010, Cao et al., 2022, El-Yaniv et al.,
2010, Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017, 2019, Ni et al., 2019]. In predictor-rejector approaches, a separate rejector
is learned [Cortes et al., 2016, Mao et al., 2024a]. Finally, reduction-based approaches cast rejection as a
related supervised learning problem [Charoenphakdee et al., 2021, Ramaswamy et al., 2018]. L2D extends

separate deferral function [Okati et al., 2021, Mozannar et al., 2023, Mao et al., 2023]. A second, orthogonal
distinction is between end-to-end methods and post-hoc (two-stage) methods.
The post-hoc regime is especially important since retraining modern pretrained models can be pro-
hibitively expensive. We focus on post-hoc L2D [Kag et al., 2022, Jitkrittum et al., 2023] (also called
two-stage L2D [Mao et al., 2023]), where the base model is fixed, and only the deferral rule is learned.
In this setting, simple score-based heuristics can fail when downstream experts are specialized or under
distribution shift [Jitkrittum et al., 2023], while many existing methods still amount to estimating pointwise
expert-comparison targets, which aim to predict when the expert will outperform the model [Kag et al., 2022,
Jitkrittum et al., 2023]. A desirable property in this regime is for deferral models to be adaptable to different
rates of deferral without full retraining.
Recently, a distributional perspective on post-hoc L2R was proposed [Soen et al., 2024, Soen, 2026]. Its
central object is an ideal distribution: a divergence-regularized reweighting of the data distribution under
which a fixed predictor attains low loss. Rejection is then defined by thresholding a density-ratio, using
either a marginal construction over X [Soen et al., 2024] or a joint construction over X × Y that is later
marginalized to X [Soen, 2026]. Extending this framework to post-hoc L2D is nontrivial: the resulting rules
are essentially plug-in and depend on the Bayes class-posterior, while a direct translation would additionally
require pointwise access to the expert at test time.
In this paper, we make the following contributions (Fig. 1 summarizes our overall approach):
• L2D via ideal distributions: We introduce a distributional formulation of post-hoc L2D based on ideal
distributions. Unlike L2R, we consider a pair of ideal distributions, one for the model h and one for the
expert h(e). Deferral is then defined by thresholding the density-ratio between these ideal distributions—
naturally separating a scorer from a deferral threshold; which allows the deferral rate to be changed without
retraining (see Definitions 3.1 and 3.2).
• Marginal versus joint ideals: Our framework accommodates both marginal and joint ideal distributions.
For KL-ideal distributions, we show that the marginal rule recovers Chow’s rule w.r.t. the data distribution,
whereas the joint rule corresponds to a Chow-style decision under an expert-tilted distribution. This tilt is
practically meaningful as it augments the expert model’s performance into the optimal deferral. We further
relate the induced learning objectives (see Theorems 3.5 and 4.4).
• The DR CPE losses: Using the reduction from Density-Ratio Estimation (DRE) to Class-Probability
Estimation (CPE), we derive a family of DR CPE post-hoc deferral losses. This further connects our
method to expert-comparison deferral estimation [Kag et al., 2022, Jitkrittum et al., 2023] and anomaly
detection [Steinwart et al., 2005] (see Definition 4.2).
• Empirical performance: Our DR CPE approach performs competitively against other L2D baselines and is
also more robust against different dataset corruption combinations than these baselines.

the expert. This can be preferable in practice, since deferral is only useful in-so-far that the expert handles the
prediction task better than the deferring model. From this perspective, Chow’s rule under the tilted posterior
ranks examples not only by uncertainty, but by uncertainty that is especially relevant to the expert.

Density-Ratio Estimation Losses from Ideal Distributions
So far we have seen that ideal distributions, together with thresholding their associated density-ratios, can
yield analytical and even optimal deferral rules. However, directly applying the closed-form expressions in,
e.g., Corollary 3.3, raises two fundamental difficulties. First, both the marginal and joint ideal distributions,
and hence the resulting density-ratio, depend on the Bayes class-posterior η⋆. Using these expressions as
plug-in deferral rules would therefore require access to the Bayes-optimal predictor in advance, which defeats
the purpose of learning a good classifier h in the first place. Second, the deferral rule also depends on the
expert ideal distribution Q(e)
i , which in turn requires pointwise evaluation of the expert model h(e)(x) at
decision time. If the expert has already been queried on x, then one may as well use the expert’s prediction
directly rather than first using it only to decide whether to defer.
These two issues prevent us from directly utilizing the closed-form analytical solutions for L2D. However,
instead of trying to use a plug-in estimator to evaluate ideal distribution density-ratios, we propose a method
which aims to estimate the density-ratio dQi/dQ(e)
i . To do so, we rely on links between Density-Ratio
Estimation (DRE) [Sugiyama et al., 2012] and Class-Probability Estimation (CPE) [Buja et al., 2005, Reid
and Williamson, 2011], where we reduce the problem of estimating the density-ratio to a binary classification
problem. Uniquely, the binary classification problem will be to distinguish between two reweightings of the
data distribution P determined by the performance of the model h and expert h(e).

6
Conclusion
In this paper, we present a novel method for learning deferral mechanisms based on ideal distributions and
the link between Density Ratio Estimation (DRE) and Class-Probability Estimation (CPE). Practically, we
find that the DR CPE loss function we propose can yield good accuracy-deferral trade-offs while also being
robust in its performance over a variety of dataset and corruption combinations. So far, the narrative has
been for L2D with a pair of models. Of course one could easily chain deferral mechanism, as per model
cascades [Wang et al., 2022, Jitkrittum et al., 2023]; however, ideally we would want to directly deal with
multi-expert deferral, where there may be multiple base models and/or experts [Verma et al., 2023, Mao et al.,
2023, 2024b, 2025, Liu et al., 2026]. An obvious connection here could be to exploit the connection between
multi-distributional DRE and CPE in the multi-class setting [Yu et al., 2021, Nock et al., 2016b]. Another
important direction is to modify our ideal distribution framework to allow additional constraints for, e.g.,
fairness or limiting expert calls in training [Narasimhan et al., 2024, Charusaie and Samadi, 2024, DeSalvo
et al., 2025].

A
Limitations
Although the approach of the paper has many connections, a theoretical limitation is that it does not have
consistency guarantees explored in other L2D approaches which utilize surrogate loss functions [Mao et al.,
2023]. However, we suspect that some finite sample bounds could be established similarly to the expert-
comparison estimation approaches [Jitkrittum et al., 2023, Lemma 4.2] (as a result of the connections we
establish between these approaches, e.g., Proposition 4.5). Practically, although we are not required to
commit to a “deferral cost” c or threshold τ at training time, the temperature τ can have a non-trivial effect
on performance (experiments are detailed in the Appendix H).
B
Broader Impact
L2D inherently has impact in various downstream tasks, e.g., in health. This can cause potential issues in
scenarios of overly conservative deferral, i.e., the overall system does not defer to a human expert when
the base model is unreliable. As such, in practice, one should ensure that the deferral rates τ selected are
validated to ensure that unreliable base model predictions are not utilized at a high rate. On the flip side, a
well-calibrated L2D model has the potential of reducing the burden on a human expert. Other impacts our and
other L2D approaches could have is on the fairness and privacy of the overarching deferring system (when
compared to just the base model). Although, L2D has been previously motivated to improve fairness [Madras
et al., 2018], our approach does not have any explicit constraints on any fairness metrics. Furthermore,
deferral could impact the privacy of users, e.g., in a loan prediction system, patterns of deferral could leak
certain demographic information. These aspects should be considered when utilizing our method.

The following section details the experimental setup in more details. All implementations were done

50 examples for each of the 100 classes to yield 45,000 training examples and 5,000 validation examples.
