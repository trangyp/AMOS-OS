Modal decomposition of turbulent flows using classical proper orthogonal decomposition
(POD) often suffers from mode mixing, in which a distinct coherent structure may be
distributed over several POD modes. We propose a decomposition method based on
the Hilbert transform and band-pass filtering to address this issue (filtered Hilbert POD
– FHPOD). We apply this approach to the turbulent flow through a 180 bent pipe at
𝑅𝑒𝐷= 10, 000 (based on bulk velocity (𝑈𝑏) and pipe diameter (𝐷)) and curvature 𝛾= 0.2,
simulated using direct numerical simulation. The FHPOD results in four distinct mode
families, including a swirl-switching mode at Strouhal number of 0.13 localised in the
curved section. Our novel modal decomposition shows that the modes observed in the
bend and downstream correspond to distinct physical mechanisms rather than to a single
universal swirl-switching instability throughout the pipe, as previous work implied. To
further examine the origin of the swirl-switching mode, we perform a local stability analysis
of the cross-sectional mean flow along the bend. We find unstable eigenmodes at the same
streamwise wavenumber and within the same range of Strouhal numbers as the swirl-
switching mode found in the modal decomposition. The result supports the interpretation
that the swirl-switching phenomenon is an intrinsic instability of the curved-pipe flow that
can be excited and potentially enhanced by incoming turbulent structures, but is ultimately
not caused by them. Finally, we also establish a link of the downstream modes to the local
shear layers of the modified base flow, highlighting the different nature of these modes.
Key words: low-dimensional models, shear-layer instability, pipe flow

are fundamentally different from those in straight pipes (Noorani et al. 2013). Curved pipe

Subsequent work by Sudo et al. (1998, 2000) characterised the streamwise development
of the mean flow in 90◦and 180◦bends. Sudo et al. (1998) showed that, in the 90◦bend,
the secondary flow weakens downstream, while the influence of the bend remains even at
10𝐷downstream of the bend. Sudo et al. (2000) found that, in the 180◦bend, an additional
pair of vortices appears just downstream of the bend exit, and that recovery to a straight
pipe flow requires a longer downstream distance compared with the 90◦bend.
Understanding swirl-switching is important both from a flow-physics perspective and
from an engineering point of view. The modulation of wall pressure and wall shear stress
caused by swirl-switching has practical implications for structural fatigue, flow-induced
vibrations and potential resonance in curved-pipe systems (Hufnagel et al. 2018; He et al.
2021; Zhang et al. 2024; Lupi et al. 2025). This practical relevance has helped drive
extensive experimental and numerical work in subsequent years.
R¨utten et al. (2001, 2005) performed the first large-eddy simulations (LES) to confirm
the existence of this phenomenon. In 90◦bends at 𝑅𝑒𝐷≈2.7 × 104, they observed low–
frequency oscillations of the Dean-vortex stagnation point and wall forces, with peaks
at 𝑆𝑡≈0.0055 and 0.014 (R¨utten et al. 2001), and later around 𝑆𝑡≈0.01 (R¨utten et al.
2005). They associated the high-frequency oscillations at 𝑆𝑡≈0.2 – 0.3 with shear–layer
instabilities. They also noted that the low–frequency oscillation can persist even when the
curvature is too small to produce a strong separation region.
Sakakibara et al. (2010) applied stereo-PIV and proper orthogonal decomposition
(POD) in a 90◦bend at high Reynolds number. They identified antisymmetric modes
with time-coefficient spectra exhibiting peaks in the range 𝑆𝑡≈0.02–0.07, depending
on the downstream position. In a later study, Sakakibara & Machida (2012) performed
simultaneous upstream and downstream PIV measurements and found upstream streaks
correlated with the downstream displacement of the inner-side stagnation point. They noted
that the connection between these streaks and very-large-scale motions (VLSMs) was
unclear.
In 2013, several PIV/POD studies focused on the swirl-switching phenomenon in 90◦
bends. Kalpakli & ¨Orl¨u (2013) used time-resolved stereoscopic PIV (2D3C) at the exit
of a 90◦bend (𝛾≈0.31, 𝑅𝑒𝐷≈3.4 × 104). Using snapshot POD of the 2D3C velocity
fields, they reconstructed the flow using the first six POD modes and analysed PSDs of
the tangential velocity. They found a dominant peak at 𝑆𝑡≈0.12, with additional peaks
at 𝑆𝑡≈0.04 and 𝑆𝑡≈0.18 and the lower-frequency peak 𝑆𝑡≈0.04 was associated with
swirl-switching. Hellstr¨om et al. (2013) measured the velocity further downstream of a
90◦bend with 𝛾= 0.5 at 𝑅𝑒𝐷≈2.5 × 104. They stated that the mode at 𝑆𝑡≈0.16 is due
to the oscillation of the Dean motion, while the higher-frequency mode at 𝑆𝑡≈0.33 is
the swirl-switching mode. In a follow-up study of mildly and sharply curved 90◦bends,
Kalpakli et al. (2015) reported spectral peaks based on power spectral density (PSD) of
POD time-coefficients at 𝑆𝑡≈0.04 (and 0.10) for the sharp bend case (𝛾≈0.39). They also
showed that conditioning the upstream flow using a honeycomb strongly modified the mean
flow and the dominant modes, and the swirl-switching mode did not appear near the bend.
However, further downstream, the POD results showed the reappearance of a single-cell
structure and a Dean-cell structure, and the authors suggested that swirl-switching might
not be eliminated but merely spatially postponed.
Carlsson et al. (2015) performed LES of flow in configurations similar to that of Kalpakli
& ¨Orl¨u (2013) with several curvatures using inflow data generated from a separate periodic
straight-pipe simulation. Their spectra and POD analysis identified a low-frequency swirl-
switching at 𝑆𝑡≈0.003–0.01, with a smaller peak at 𝑆𝑡≈0.13 for the smaller curvatures,
and a high-frequency swirl-switching at 𝑆𝑡≈0.5–0.6 for the sharpest bend. However, their

2. Motivation of the current work
Despite the extensive body of work, several key aspects of swirl-switching remain unresolved.
The characteristic Strouhal number of the motion is far from universal. The reported values
span more than two orders of magnitude (see table 1). Excluding the studies that utilise
recycling inflow conditions, part of this scatter can be attributed to differences in geometry,
Reynolds number, and the location of the instabilities. An equally important reason for
this scatter is the method employed to identify the large-scale coherent structure. As Wang
et al. (2018) showed, the identified characteristic frequencies depend on the choice of
the measured quantity of interest. Furthermore, dimensionality reduction can also lead to
a misinterpretation of the characteristic frequency (Hufnagel et al. 2018). In particular,
when only a single observable (e.g. streamwise vorticity at a given cross-section) is
analysed, coherent structures dominated by other velocity components may be overlooked
or misidentified.
On the other hand, Jain et al. (2019) showed that the relative contributions of different
frequencies depend strongly on the streamwise measurement location. This implies the
coexistence of multiple instability mechanisms that dominate at different streamwise
positions. Consequently, the phenomenon reported as “swirl-switching” in different studies
may not correspond to the same universal mechanism, but instead may point to distinct
instabilities with similar footprint occurring at different locations within the flow. Part of
the confusion in the literature may therefore be due to the use of the same term for different
coherent structures with distinct underlying physical mechanisms. To address this issue,
we propose the following requirements for the clean separation and the accurate energy
quantification of distinct modes:

(1) The data must be free of spurious periodicity or artificial forcing that could contaminate
the intrinsic instabilities.
(2) The analysis must be fully three-dimensional and span the entire domain of interest.
(3) Degenerate POD modes represent the same convecting structures, and they must have
identical energy and identical spectra of the time-coefficients.
(4) The temporal spectrum associated with each mode should be unimodal. Multiple peaks
in the spectra indicate mode mixing and inadequate separation.
(5) The interpretation of mode shapes must consider both rotational (cross-stream) and
axial (streamwise) motions to avoid misattribution of mechanisms.
The use of synthetic-eddy inflow conditions together with 3D-POD in Hufnagel et al.
(2018); Lupi et al. (2025) addressed requirements (1) and (2) and clarified some of these
earlier discrepancies. However, classical POD does not guarantee a clean separation of
distinct instabilities. Thus, requirements (3) and (4) remain critical challenges and are
crucial to accurately classify different coherent structures.
The first objective of this work is to satisfy all five requirements outlined above in
our modal decompositions, thereby isolating different instabilities of different origins as
distinct modes. In this context, a central goal is to identify the swirl-switching phenomenon
as a single mode and understand its physical origin. More specifically, through spatio-
temporal separation of the underlying mechanisms, we first isolate the swirl-switching mode
from other coexisting structures. This enables us to determine its characteristic frequency
range, wavelength, spatial support, and dominant velocity components. Subsequently, we
investigate whether the swirl-switching mode is linked to Dean-vortex dynamics, the
downstream shear-layer dynamics, or is an inherent instability of the bent-pipe flow. In this
sense, the present work is not only aimed at improving modal decompositions of turbulent
flows, but also at establishing a clearer connection between the observed coherent structures
and the mechanisms responsible for them.
In spatially developing flows, travelling structures naturally appear as degenerate POD
pairs with a 90-degree phase shift due to their convective nature. In the limit of an infinite
number of snapshots, POD satisfies requirement (3) for statistically stationary flows. In
practice, however, particularly in turbulent flows where coherent structures are embedded
within a broad range of scales, finite sampling often leads to imperfect pairing of degenerate
modes. Consequently, a single convecting structure often appears distributed across multiple
modes with unequal energy contents.
Moreover, the classical POD does not satisfy requirement (4) by construction. In this
context, the unimodality of a mode refers to a single dominant frequency band in its
time-coefficient rather than a perfectly harmonic response. In turbulent flows, nonlinear
interactions and amplitude modulation can generate spectrally broadened peaks. As a result
of violations of requirements (3) and (4), the energy-based ranking of distinct instabilities
becomes an ill-posed problem. In other words, the energy of a single physical instability
may be distributed across several POD modes. This is partially the reason why many studies
rely on the reduced-order reconstructions of the flow field using multiple modes to recover
an instability of interest (Kalpakli & ¨Orl¨u 2013; Noorani & Schlatter 2016). For instance,
Noorani & Schlatter (2016) noted that no single “swirl mode” appears among the most
energetic POD modes, but the flow reconstruction using multiple modes reproduces a
swirl-switching structure. This indicates that mode mixing distributed the relevant swirl-
switching structure across multiple modes in their analysis. This issue is particularly more
severe in fully turbulent flows, where modal energy levels of the modes are closely spaced.
Therefore, the truncation up to a limited set of leading modes may discard a portion of a
physically coherent instability of interest that is distributed across multiple POD modes

Consequently, a mode that contains multiple frequency bands indicates mixing of multiple

too restrictive for turbulent flows, given the multiscale and nonlinear nature of turbulence.
Here, we instead seek a finite-band representation that captures each instability as a single
coherent mode.
To enforce requirement (3), we perform POD on the analytic signal of the data, following
the Hilbert Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (HPOD) framework introduced by Kriegseis
et al. (2021) and subsequently applied in Raiola & Kriegseis (2024, 2025). To address
requirement (4), we extract the dominant HPOD modes and analyse their spectral content.
A Butterworth filter with sufficiently broad support is then designed and applied to the
snapshots to suppress secondary frequency bands contaminating the dominant mode. The
purpose of this step is not to enforce an artificially narrow spectral band, but to reduce
spectral mixing while preserving the physically relevant frequency content. As a result, the
novel filtered HPOD (FHPOD) method can mitigate mode mixing and limit contamination
from structures of different physical origins that are correlated with the mode of interest.
Lastly, requirement (5) is crucial to correctly identify the underlying physical mechanisms.
As shown in Section 4, some modes carry most of their energy in the streamwise velocity
component, whereas others are dominated by cross-stream rotational motion. Thus, we
visualise the former using isosurfaces of the streamwise velocity and the latter using
isosurfaces of the streamfunction.
Besides establishing a consistent modal decomposition framework, the present study also
aims at identifying the low-frequency instability at 𝑆𝑡≈0.03 −0.04 as a distinct mode.
Although this low-frequency mode has been observed in multiple experimental studies
(Br¨ucker 1998; Sakakibara et al. 2010; Kalpakli & ¨Orl¨u 2013; Kalpakli et al. 2015; Jain
et al. 2019, 2022), the associated instability has not previously been extracted as a separate
mode in numerical simulations. Hufnagel et al. (2018) reported that a similar low-frequency
peak at 𝑆𝑡≈0.045 for 𝛾= 0.1 was present in the PSD of the time-coefficients, but
noted that resolving its large wavelength would require an extended outlet section that was
computationally prohibitive at the time. In the following, we demonstrate that, by extending
the straight section downstream of the bend, this low-frequency mode can be adequately
isolated and characterised.
3. Computational methodology
The methodology consists of the following main steps. First, we generate the dataset required
for the modal decomposition using DNS. Second, we formulate the filtered Hilbert POD
(FHPOD) method, which is later applied to the DNS data to extract coherent structures
with distinct spectral support. Finally, we introduce the local stability analysis used to
assess whether the swirl-switching FHPOD mode is consistent with unstable modes of the
bent-pipe mean flow.

3.5. Local stability analysis
The stability of the cross-sectional mean turbulent flow is investigated through modal
stability analysis (Schmid & Henningson 2001; Drazin & Reid 2004), i.e. by studying the
evolution of infinitesimal perturbations e𝒃= {e𝒖, e𝑝} applied to the cross-sectional mean
turbulent base flow 𝑩= {𝑼, 𝑃}, with 𝑼being the mean velocity vector and 𝑃the mean
pressure. Similar mean flow stability approaches have been used to identify instability
mechanisms in both unsteady laminar flows (Sipp & Lebedev 2007) and turbulent mean
flows (Gudmundsson & Colonius 2011; Mettot et al. 2014; Rodr´ıguez et al. 2015; Qadri
et al. 2016; Tammisola & Juniper 2016; Camarri et al. 2017). For this purpose, the
two-dimensional (2D) stability code described in Casali (2025), capable of performing
modal stability analysis for toroidal and helical pipe geometries, is used. Therefore, here
the stability of the mean turbulent flow, at different pipe cross-sections along 𝑆, as if it
were the mean flow in a toroidal pipe with the same curvature, is computed. Due to this
assumption, the streamwise invariance allows a 2D analysis with periodic modes in the
streamwise direction (see below), also called BiGlobal (Lupi 2023). The code is written
in helical coordinates (𝑟, 𝜃, 𝑠) of Germano (1982), using a spectral collocation method
for the spatial discretisation and employing Fourier and Chebyshev basis functions in the
azimuthal and radial directions, respectively. No-slip boundary conditions are applied at the
wall. The system is augmented with equations to cancel out the spurious pressure modes
(Balachandar & Madabhushi 1994), which affect the eigenmodes.

The physical origin of the swirl-switching phenomenon remains the subject of ongoing
debate. One line of investigation attributes this behaviour to upstream turbulence, suggesting
that streaks or VLSMs entering the bend cause or influence the unsteady motion of the Dean

5. Summary and conclusions
In this study, we revisit the swirl-switching phenomenon for the case of turbulence in a 180◦
bent pipe flow. We address the issue of mode mixing in the classical POD by first establishing
five requirements for a physically meaningful modal decomposition of turbulent flows. As
these requirements are often not satisfied in POD studies, we show how distinct instabilities
are distributed across multiple modes, their energy is artificially split, and their physical
origin becomes ambiguous and difficult to identify. We therefore propose an alternative
decomposition method based on the Hilbert transform combined with temporal band-pass
filtering before the SVD step, termed filtered Hilbert POD (FHPOD). The current approach
allows the decomposition of the flow into separate energetically and spectrally distinct mode
families. This mode disentangling approach shows that the different spectral peaks reported
in the literature do not necessarily correspond to a single physical phenomenon, but reflect
0 X0-23


## Page 24


Bagheri, Casali, Becker & Schlatter
distinct coherent structures with varying strengths along the streamwise direction. More
specifically, the method separates entangled distinct structures that have often been grouped
under the same “swirl-switching” label in the literature.
The resulting decomposition reveals four families of large-scale coherent structures that
have not previously been clearly identified using classical POD as single distinct modes.
The FHPOD results in a low-frequency axial wave (AX) at 𝑆𝑡≈0.03; a curvature-driven
swirl-switching mode (SS) inside the bend at 𝑆𝑡≈0.13, a swirl-breathing mode (SB) at
𝑆𝑡≈0.26, and a downstream shear-layer instability (DS1) at 𝑆𝑡≈0.15 together with its
second harmonic (DS2) localised in the shear layer of the four-vortex mean flow structure.
In particular, the separation of SS from the DS family is central to understanding the
swirl-switching phenomenon. Since swirl-switching is identified as a distinct mode and is
separated from the downstream shear-layer instabilities, we are able to investigate its origin
using local stability analysis. The swirl-switching mode SS is found to be consistent with the
intrinsic instabilities of the mean flow inside the bent section of the pipe identified using the
local stability analysis. The local stability analysis shows two branches of unstable modes
whose spatial structure, wall localisation, and frequency range closely match the FHPOD
mode SS. The associated unstable frequencies vary along the bend rather than collapsing
to a single monochromatic value, indicating that swirl-switching is better interpreted as
a finite-band response of a spatially evolving instability. To the authors’ knowledge, this
agreement provides the clearest evidence so far that swirl-switching originates from an
intrinsic instability of the bent pipe mean flow, rather than being imposed directly by
upstream turbulent structures. Although the presence of streamwise perturbations such
as streaks and VLSMs can excite the swirl-switching mode, they are ultimately not the
underlying physical cause of this phenomenon. Therefore, streamwise perturbations should
be interpreted as an excitation mechanism for an instability that is already present in the
bent-pipe mean flow. This interpretation is consistent with Sakakibara & Machida (2012),
who found upstream streaks correlated with the downstream displacement of the inner-side
stagnation point, and with Kalpakli et al. (2015), who showed that modifying the incoming
flow with a honeycomb altered and delayed the appearance of the swirl-switching mode
but did not eliminate it. This result also supports the idea that identifying the origin of
large-scale coherent structures can be facilitated by disentangling these structures as distinct
modes in the modal decomposition as a first step.
The present approach has the following limitations. The FHPOD procedure is a posteriori
decomposition technique and therefore requires an initial inspection of POD results and their
spectra. As a result, the method is most effective when the underlying coherent structures
have distinct spectral support with sufficient spatial localisations. If multiple modes strongly
overlap in both space and frequency, they may not be fully separable using this procedure. A
promising direction for future work is to formulate the temporal filtering step of the FHPOD
using wavelets or wavelet packets, enabling separation of distinct coherent structures using
adaptive time-frequency bands that can vary locally along the streamwise direction rather
than prescribed global band-pass filters. In addition to reducing the reliance on manual
band selections, such an approach could be particularly well-suited for spatially developing
turbulent flows, where the characteristic frequency of the convecting instabilities may vary
along the axial direction. Further studies are also needed to shed light on the effect of
curvature, Reynolds number, and bend angle on the mode families identified in this work.
