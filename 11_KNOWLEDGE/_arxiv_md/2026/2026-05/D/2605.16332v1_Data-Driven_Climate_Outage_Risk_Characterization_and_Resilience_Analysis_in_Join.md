Abstract—Climate-driven power outages pose a growing 
threat to U.S. grid reliability, yet empirical outage studies and 
interdependency-based resilience analyses are rarely integrated. 
This paper presents a data-driven framework that integrates 
empirical outage characterization with cascade failure 
simulation in joint power-communication networks. Using the 
EAGLE-I national outage dataset (2015–2023, 525,000+ 
records), we characterize the climate-outage landscape through 
descriptive analysis and hypothesis testing, finding that climate-
related outages increase by roughly 9,100 events per year and 
impose a significantly greater severity burden on coastal states. 
An interpretable logistic regression model then identifies the 
main predictors of severe outage risk, with Severe Weather 
emerging as the dominant factor. Guided by these findings, we 
construct four geographically representative failure scenarios 
and evaluate them using MIIM-based cascade simulation on the 
IEEE 118-bus system with a communication network overlay. 
Coastal scenarios produce substantially larger resilience gaps 
than the inland case, with the Extreme Coastal Severe Weather 
scenario reducing post-cascade operability to 17.6%. The results 
show that aggregate outage statistics alone underestimate 
coastal risk, as cross-layer cascade propagation amplifies 
geographic 
damage 
in 
ways 
revealed 
only 
through 
interdependency-aware simulation.

I. INTRODUCTION 
The U.S. power grid is facing increasing stress from 
climate-driven disruptions. Extreme weather conditions and 
natural disasters are becoming more frequent, and their impact 
on grid planning and operation is increasingly difficult to 
ignore. [1] emphasizes that extreme events have become one 
of the most critical challenges for power-system planners and 
operators, motivating the need for realistic modeling and 
simulation under severe weather conditions. Recent 
nationwide empirical evidence also shows that U.S. outages 
are becoming more frequent, prolonged, and intense over 
time, with coastal regions experiencing more frequent and 
longer outages than many inland areas. In particular, [2] 
provides a large-scale spatiotemporal analysis of nationwide 
outages and shows that power-system vulnerability is both 
temporally worsening and geographically uneven. Together, 
these findings indicate that climate-driven outages are no 
longer isolated reliability events, but a persistent and 
escalating threat to grid operation and resilience. 
This growing risk cannot be understood from the power 
layer alone. Modern grids depend heavily on communication 
and control infrastructure for monitoring, coordination, and 
response; and prior work has emphasized the importance of 
modeling these interdependencies [3]. When a climate event 
affects a geographic area, it can disrupt not only substations 
and power delivery assets, but also the co-located 
communication entities that support system observability and 
control. Yet existing studies typically address only part of this 
problem. Large-scale empirical outage analyses, such as [2], 
characterize outage frequency, duration, intensity, and spatial 
disparity, but they do not examine how climate-grounded 
disturbances propagate through interdependent power-
communication infrastructure. On the other hand, simulation-
oriented work on extreme-event grid response, such as [1], 
highlights the need for realistic modeling under severe 
weather but focuses on power-system demand and physical 
grid response rather than cross-layer outage propagation in 
interdependent networks.  
To bridge this gap, this paper develops a data-driven 
resilience assessment framework that connects empirical 
outage evidence with interdependency-aware cascade 
simulation in a joint power-communication network. The 
framework first uses the EAGLE-I outage dataset to 
characterize 
climate-related 
outage 
patterns 
through 
descriptive analysis and hypothesis testing. It then applies an 
interpretable logistic regression model to identify the outage 
contexts most strongly associated with severe events. These 
empirical and modeling results are used to construct 
geographically representative climate-risk scenarios, which 
are evaluated through cascade simulation using the Modified 
Implicative Interdependency Model (MIIM) [3] on the IEEE 
118-bus system with an overlaid communication layer. The 
main contributions of this paper are as follows: 1) Large-scale 
characterization of climate-driven outage patterns in the 
United States. 2) Interpretable severity-risk modeling using 
logistic regression. 3) Construction of empirically grounded 
representative failure scenarios. 4) MIIM-based resilience 
analysis of a joint power-communication network. 5) 
Quantitative evidence that coastal scenarios produce 
resilience gaps 23–94% larger than inland scenarios under 
cross-layer cascade propagation.

II. RELATED WORK 
Prior research relevant to this study falls into three related 
but largely disconnected strands: climate-driven outage 
analysis, severity prediction and data-driven risk modeling, 
and interdependent infrastructure resilience analysis.  
A. Climate-Driven Outage Analysis 
Recent work has shown that climate-related disruptions 
are placing increasing stress on power-system reliability and 
resilience. [1] emphasizes that extreme weather events are 
becoming more frequent and that realistic modeling of grid 
behavior under such conditions is increasingly important for 
planning and operation. Their work focuses on distribution-
grid response to extreme temperatures and highlights the need 
for simulation tools that capture severe-weather impacts on 
demand and grid performance. Similarly, [2] provides a 
nationwide spatiotemporal analysis of U.S. outages and shows 
that outage vulnerability has worsened over time, with outages 
becoming more frequent, prolonged, and intense. They further 
report that coastal regions experience greater outage burden 
than many inland regions, revealing strong geographic 
disparity in outage vulnerability. More broadly, [4] reviews 
resilience assessment and planning in power distribution 
systems and notes that extreme weather now drives a 
substantial share of distribution-level disruptions, while also 
stressing that resilience modeling should account for critical-
infrastructure interdependence. 
B. Severity Prediction and Data-Driven Risk Modeling 
A second stream of work has focused on predictive outage 
modeling using statistical and machine-learning methods. [5] 
studies line-outage prediction during extreme meteorological 
events using ensemble learning, Gaussian processes, support 
vector machines, and related models, showing that weather-
informed predictive models can improve identification of 
likely failures in distribution grids. [6] develops deep-learning 
models for weather-related outage prediction using weather, 
socio-economic, demographic, and infrastructure data, and 
shows that community-level contextual features can improve 
outage prediction performance. On the other hand, [7] focuses 
on power outage duration prediction and demonstrates that 
machine-learning approaches can support outage management 
and customer communication by accurately forecasting 
restoration duration. Together, these studies establish the 
value of data-driven outage modeling, but their emphasis is 
primarily on prediction performance, outage occurrence, or 
outage duration rather than on constructing interpretable, 
empirically grounded severity-risk insights for downstream 
resilience simulation. 
C. Interdependent Infrastructure and Resilience Analysis 
A third body of work has addressed cascading failures in 
interdependent infrastructures, especially coupled power and 
communication systems. Authors in [8] study the resilience of 
interdependent communication and power distribution 
networks against cascading failures using real medium-
voltage grid topologies and show that such coupled systems 
can be highly vulnerable, with communication-network 
structure strongly affecting robustness. [3] introduces the 
Modified Implicative Interdependency Model (MIIM) for 
representing intra- and interdependencies between power and 
communication entities with greater physical realism than 
earlier binary models. Their work demonstrates MIIM on 
smart-grid applications such as state estimation and shows its 
usefulness in analyzing the effect of failures across a joint 
power-communication network. In the present paper, the 
novelty lies in applying MIIM within a data-driven climate-
risk pipeline, where empirical outage characterization and 
severity-risk evidence are used to define representative 
geographic failure scenarios for resilience evaluation. 
Although prior studies have separately examined 
nationwide outage vulnerability, machine-learning-based 
outage prediction, and interdependent cascade behavior, these 
strands remain weakly connected. Climate-outage studies 
characterize temporal and spatial patterns but do not show 
how empirically observed disturbances propagate through 
joint power-communication infrastructure. Predictive studies 
estimate outage occurrence or duration but generally do not 
translate learned risk patterns into scenario-based resilience 
analysis. Interdependency studies, including MIIM-based 
modeling, capture cascade propagation but are often not 
grounded in observed large-scale climate-outage patterns. 
This paper addresses that gap by integrating empirical outage 
characterization, interpretable severity-risk modeling, and 
MIIM-based cascade simulation into a unified framework for 
climate-resilience assessment in interdependent smart-grid 
infrastructure. 
III. DATASET AND EMPIRICAL OUTAGE CHARACTERIZATION 
A. Dataset and Preprocessing 
This study uses the EAGLE-I (Environment for Analysis 
of Geo-Located Energy Information) outage dataset 
maintained by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). 
EAGLE-I provides geographically resolved outage records 
linked to utility disturbance events across the United States, 
including event type, location, outage start and end times, 
outage duration, and customer-impact measures such as 
maximum customers affected. The analysis covers 2015-
2023, excluding 2014 to avoid partial-year bias, and uses a 
cleaned dataset containing approximately 525,000 records.

IV. SEVERITY RISK MODELING  
A. Target Definition and Feature Set 
To identify the determinants of severe outage risk, a binary 
severity label was constructed from the EAGLE-I dataset. For 
each outage record, duration and maximum customers 
affected were independently normalized to the range [0,1] and 
averaged to form a composite severity score. Records in the 
top quartile (75th percentile) of this score were labeled Severe 
(1), while all remaining records were labeled Non-Severe (0). 
This yielded 131,340 severe records (25%) and 394,020 non-
severe records (75%) across the full dataset. 
The feature set captures observable event-level and 
contextual factors while excluding duration and customer-
impact variables to avoid data leakage. Features include event 
category, year, season, region, a coastal indicator, and season-
region interaction dummies where appropriate. All features 
were standardized before fitting, providing an interpretable 
feature space for ranking outage contexts by severe-risk 
tendency. 
B. Logistic Regression Setup 
The labeled dataset was divided into training and test sets 
using an 80/20 stratified split to preserve the severe/non-
severe class ratio across both subsets. A logistic regression 
model with L2 regularization and balanced class weighting 
was then fitted to the training data to account for the 75/25 
class imbalance. Logistic regression was selected deliberately


## Page 4


because the goal of this stage is interpretability, not black-box 
predictive optimization. In particular, the model provides 
signed coefficients whose magnitude and direction indicate 
which event types, seasons, and geographic contexts are 
associated with elevated severe outage risk. These coefficient-
level insights are subsequently used to support scenario design 
in Section V.  
Model performance was evaluated on the held-out test set 
using accuracy, precision, recall, and the area under the 
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC). The 
ROC curve is shown in Fig. 3, the test-set confusion matrix is 
shown in Fig. 4, and the ranked coefficient plot is shown in 
Fig. 5.

V. MIIM-BASED SCENARIO CONSTRUCTION AND 
SIMULATION SETUP 
A. Rationale for MIIM-Based Interdependency Analysis 
Evaluating how climate-driven failures propagate through 
a smart-grid environment requires a framework that explicitly 
represents both the power and communication layers and their 
mutual 
dependencies. 
The 
Modified 
Implicative 
Interdependency Model (MIIM) [3] is well suited to this task 
because it supports joint power and communication 
interdependency analysis [9], allows entities to occupy three-
valued operability states, and captures cross-layer cascade 
propagation. In MIIM, each entity may be fully operational, 
degraded, or failed (states 2, 1, and 0 respectively), which is 
important in resilience analysis because climate disruptions do 
not always lead to immediate binary failure. In the present 
paper, MIIM is not introduced as a new model; rather, it is


## Page 5


used as the simulation engine for evaluating how empirically 
grounded climate-risk contexts translate into cascading 
degradation in a joint power-communication network.  
B. Scenario Construction Logic 
The scenarios considered in this paper are empirically 
grounded representative climate-risk scenarios rather than 
one-to-one reconstructions of specific historical events. Their 
construction is informed jointly by the empirical outage 
patterns characterized in Section III and the severity-risk 
ranking established in Section IV. Section III shows that 
climate-related outage frequency is rising over time, that 
Severe Weather dominates the outage landscape, and that 
coastal states experience materially greater outage severity 
than inland states. Section IV further shows that Severe 
Weather is the strongest positive predictor of severe outage 
risk, with Winter Storm also contributing positively and 
Natural Disaster showing only a weak positive effect. Guided 
by these findings, the scenario set is designed to reflect the 
dominant event types, severity levels, and geographic contexts 
supported by the data while remaining sufficiently generic for 
controlled benchmark-network analysis.

D. Initial Failure Design 
In this study, MIIM is applied to a joint power-
communication network constructed by overlaying a 
communication-layer design onto the IEEE 118-bus power 
network. A key realism feature of the simulation setup is that 
each scenario’s initial failure set reflects geographic area 
damage rather than selective disruption of the power layer 
alone. In the IEEE 118-bus implementation, substations are 
ranked by power-flow centrality, defined as the sum of 
absolute power flows across all lines connected to the buses 
belonging to each substation. For a given scenario, the top N 
substations are selected as the directly affected zone, where N 
is determined by the scenario’s prescribed failure scope. For 
each selected substation, the initial failure set includes all 
associated buses, the substation server, the co-located 
gateway, and any co-located PMUs. In addition, S-ADMs and 
OADMs are included if their home substation lies within the 
affected zone or within 35 km and 50 km, respectively, of any 
failed substation, following the Ethernet and DWDM 
adjacency constraints of the MIIM network topology. This is 
a major realism improvement over bus-only initialization 
because climate-driven disturbances can simultaneously 
impair co-located power and communication infrastructure. 
By initializing failures across both layers, the simulations 
more accurately capture the physical scope of geographic 
climate damage and the resulting cross-layer cascade 
potential. The IEEE 118-bus system serves as the topology 
substrate; while it does not geographically correspond to the 
EAGLE-I data, it provides a well-validated benchmark for 
cross-layer cascade analysis.

DISCUSSION

C. Cascade Propagation Behavior 
Fig. 8 decomposes total affected entities into direct failures 
at T0 and additional failures introduced through cascade 
propagation. In all four scenarios, the number of affected 
entities after cascade stabilization is much larger than the 
number initially failed at T0. S2 illustrates this most clearly: 
203 entities fail initially, but the cascade expands the 
disruption to 375 affected entities. Similarly, S1 grows from 
125 to 243 affected entities, S4 from 154 to 290, and even the 
most contained scenario, S3, from 102 to 199. These results 
show that the coupled network structure transforms local 
climate damage into broader system-level degradation. All 
four scenarios reach steady state in four propagation steps. 
Although cascade depths do not vary across the scenarios, the 
breadth of final disruption differs greatly. This indicates that 
scenario severity is driven less by the number of propagation 
rounds than by the extent to which failures spread across the 
coupled power-communication topology within those rounds.

D. Component-Level Failure Patterns 
Fig. 9 shows the post-cascade failure counts broken down 
by entity type across all four scenarios and reveals several 
consistent structural patterns. Most notably, substation server 
and gateway failures remain exactly matched across all 
scenarios. This reflects the tight co-location and dependency 
structure encoded in the MIIM interdependency relations: 
when one fails, the other becomes inoperable as well. The


## Page 7


repeated symmetry of these counts is therefore direct evidence 
that communication-layer disruption mirrors and reinforces 
substation-level failure throughout the cascade process. 
Communication-network components such as S-ADMs and 
OADMs also scale with scenario severity. Their failure is 
important not only as a communication-layer outcome, but 
also because these entities support monitoring and control 
functions that influence wider network operability. The larger 
communication-layer failures observed in S2 and S4 therefore 
help explain why the coastal scenarios exhibit larger resilience 
gaps than the inland case. The results show that climate-driven 
geographic damage does not remain localized; it propagates 
through the communication layer and feeds back into the 
power layer, increasing the overall cascade footprint.

E. Integrated Interpretation 
Taken together, the results of Sections III through VI form 
a coherent analytical pipeline. Section III established that 
climate-related outages are increasing, that Severe Weather is 
the dominant event category, and that coastal states bear a 
greater severity burden. Section IV then showed that Severe 
Weather is the strongest positive predictor of severe outage 
risk, with Winter Storm also contributing positively and 
Natural Disaster having only a weak positive association. 
Section VI demonstrates that these same rankings are reflected 
in the cascade simulation outcomes: the two Severe Weather 
scenarios and the coastal Winter Storm scenario all produce 
larger resilience gaps than the inland Natural Disaster 
scenario, and the extreme coastal Severe Weather case is the 
most damaging overall.  
This convergence across empirical characterization, 
interpretable severity-risk modeling, and interdependency-
aware simulation is the central contribution of the paper. 
Aggregate outage statistics identify where climate burden is 
concentrated, but they cannot show how damage propagates 
through a coupled infrastructure system. The MIIM results 
reveal that cross-layer propagation [9] amplifies the coastal 
disadvantage beyond what is visible in outage records alone. 
In that sense, the simulations do not merely confirm the 
descriptive findings; they explain the mechanism through 
which empirically observed climate-risk contexts translate 
into deeper resilience loss in joint power-communication 
networks.  
VII. CONCLUSION 
This paper presented an integrated data-driven framework 
for 
climate 
resilience 
assessment 
in 
joint 
power-
communication networks by linking empirical outage 
characterization, interpretable severity-risk modeling, and 
MIIM-based cascade simulation. Using EAGLE-I outage data 
from 2015-2023, the analysis showed that climate-related 
outage frequency is rising by approximately 9,100 events per 
year, that Severe Weather dominates the outage landscape, 
and that coastal states experience a materially greater severity 
burden than inland states. An interpretable logistic regression 
model further confirmed Severe Weather as the dominant 
severe-risk predictor, with Winter Storm as a secondary 
elevated-risk context. Guided by these findings, four 
representative climate-risk scenarios were evaluated using 
MIIM cascade simulation on the IEEE 118-bus joint power-
communication network. 
The simulation results indicate that aggregate outage 
statistics alone underestimate coastal climate risk because they 
cannot capture the interdependency-driven amplification 
revealed by MIIM-based analysis. From a practical 
perspective, the findings suggest that resilience planning, 
particularly in coastal regions, should consider the hardening 
of communication and control infrastructure together with 
power infrastructure rather than treating the two layers 
separately. Future work will extend the framework to utility-
scale network topologies, improve geographic scenario 
calibration, explore richer severity-prediction models, and 
evaluate mitigation and hardening strategies within the MIIM 
simulation environment.
