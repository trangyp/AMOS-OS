ABSTRACT
We present Diffusion Restore, a real-time framework for diffusion-based MCMC light transport. MCMC
methods are highly suitable for sampling from complex high-dimensional distributions and estimating integrals
over them. In practice, they are often the only viable solution when direct sampling is not possible and
alternative methods are either inefficient or cannot be applied due to the structure of the target distribution.
However, controlling the exploration of the target distribution in MCMC methods remains challenging.
Efficient exploration requires a balance between local exploration and global discovery, and local dynamics
must rapidly explore individual modes without getting stuck or exhibiting excessive backtracking. The problem
of global discovery has recently been addressed by the introduction of the Restore framework. In this work, we
build on this framework and focus on improving local exploration. We show how to choose diffusion-based
local dynamics within the Restore framework while completely avoiding Metropolis-adjustment, which often

Introduction
MCMC light transport
Light transport requires the computation of high-dimensional integrals. In practice, these integrals are
approximated using Monte Carlo (MC) integration. To this end, one considers a scalar nonnegative function (e.g., luminance)
derived from the radiometric contribution function induced by the scene. This function can be interpreted as an unnormalized
probability density and subsequently used as the target density for MC sampling.
Traditionally, the image space is stratified into pixels and MC integration is performed independently within each pixel. Each
pixel estimate is furthermore computed from independently generated samples. While this approach ensures a uniform spread of
samples across the image space by construction, it has a major drawback: the computationally expensive generation of samples
is performed without regard to their eventual contribution to the estimate. Even if a sample has no significant impact on the
estimate, it is still generated without considering the value of the target density at the sampled location. Markov chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) methods offer a way to address this inefficiency. By constructing a Markov process, sample generation can
be guided to follow the target distribution, thereby concentrating computational effort on regions that contribute most to the
estimate.
Metropolis Light Transport
MCMC methods are highly suitable for sampling from complex high-dimensional distributions.
However, controlling the exploration of the target distribution remains challenging. Efficient exploration requires balancing local
exploration and global discovery. On the one hand, a balance between these two aspects must be achieved; on the other hand, the
local dynamics must rapidly explore individual modes without getting stuck or repeatedly backtracking. Veach (1997) introduced
the Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm, a widely used MCMC method, to the graphics community. Building on this pioneering
work, numerous MH-based MCMC light transport algorithms have since been proposed. However, all MH-based methods share
the problem that local explorations tend to random-walk-like behavior and excessive backtracking. This behavior is largely
caused by the fact that MH dynamics are inherently reversible (Douc et al., 2018, Definition 1.5.1) due to the acceptance/rejection
step. While reversibility is beneficial for theoretical analysis, it often leads to slower convergence in practice (Neal, 2004).
Furthermore, in light transport applications, global discovery is typically achieved only through an artificial large step mechanism
that does not adapt to the target density.
Jump Restore Light Transport
Recently, Holl et al. (2025) introduced an alternative continuous-time MCMC framework,
known as Restore. Restore successfully addresses the problem of balancing global discovery and local exploration by introducing
a novel target-density-sensitive killing mechanism in place of the artificial large-step mechanism. Within Restore, local
explorations are driven by user-defined local dynamics. In the specific instance considered in Holl et al. (2025), namely Jump
Restore Light Transport (JRLT), these local dynamics are still based on MH. As demonstrated in Holl et al. (2025, 2026), JRLT
already outperforms all previous MCMC light transport methods solely due to its novel treatment of global discovery and
establishes the current state of the art. However, since the local dynamics remain MH-based, the speed of local exploration
continues to suffer from the limitations induced by reversibility. In this work, we therefore focus on the construction of highly
efficient local dynamics that rapidly explore the target distribution while avoiding excessive random-walk-like backtracking.
Real-time rendering
In interactive applications such as games, additional constraints arise compared to offline rendering,
most notably strict time budgets. The number of samples that can be used to compute a single estimate (i.e., a frame) is therefore
severely limited. At modern display resolutions and frame rates such as 30 or 60 FPS, this budget may even fall below one
sample per pixel.
Existing approaches address this through techniques such as adaptive sampling, denoising, and super-resolution (Bálint et al.,
2026). These methods reconstruct high-quality images from sparse samples or redistribute samples across the image, but they
do not fundamentally change the underlying MC sampling process itself, which remains based on conventional path tracing
(PT). This raises a natural question: how far can light transport be improved by improving the sampling process itself? Our
approach is orthogonal to the aforementioned techniques, including spatiotemporal path reuse methods such as RESTIR (Bitterli
et al., 2020), as it directly replaces the underlying MC sampling method rather than relying solely on downstream reconstruction
techniques.

Our contribution
We propose Diffusion Restore, a diffusion-based framework for both offline and real-time MCMC rendering.
Building on the Restore framework introduced in Holl et al. (2025), we propose to directly employ a diffusion process as local
dynamics within the Restore algorithm, without any Metropolis-adjustment.
More precisely, we employ a nonreversible Langevin process (Section 5), whose dynamics are invariant with respect to the target
distribution while introducing a momentum-like rotational component into the drift. Thereby, they enable more persistent and
directed exploration of the target distribution while reducing random-walk-like backtracking and can significantly accelerate
local mode exploration (Eberle et al., 2025; Duncan et al., 2017b; Eberle and Lörler, 2024).
Since Langevin dynamics are generally not exactly simulatable, we effectively employ a continuous-time-embedded discretization
as local dynamics. Although this discretization is no longer strictly invariant, exact invariance is not required within Restore,
since invariance is enforced through the killing mechanism. However, the killing rate now contains an additional adjoint term
that is computationally expensive to evaluate in practice. We therefore introduce a modified killing rate and theoretically analyze
the resulting estimator bias (Sections 4.2 and 5.2). Furthermore, we demonstrate empirically in Section 6 that the induced bias
remains negligible compared to the underlying MC error even after prolonged rendering times.
We present a GPU implementation of our algorithm (Section 4.3) and demonstrate that even under interactive time budgets the
error of Diffusion Restore remains substantially below that of conventional PT, which is currently the foundation of practical
real-time rendering systems.

Related work
Seminal work
The rendering equation (Kajiya, 1986) is typically solved using MC estimators such as path tracing (Pharr et al.,
2021) and its bidirectional variant (Lafortune and Willems, 1996; Veach and Guibas, 1995). While effective, these approaches
sample paths independently of their eventual contribution, which can make certain transport effects difficult to capture efficiently.
To address this, Veach (1997) introduced MCMC into rendering by adapting the MH algorithm (Metropolis et al., 1953; Hastings,
1970). Subsequently, Kelemen et al. (2002) proposed the primary sample space formulation, replacing the highly complex path
space by a Euclidean parameterization. This significantly simplifies proposal construction and makes the design of efficient
sampling strategies more tractable. As a result, the primary sample space formulation has become the standard basis for many
MH-based rendering methods.
Diffusion-based MH
A large class of MH methods rely on carefully designed proposal kernels. In statistics, such proposals
are often derived from discretizations of diffusion processes. Notably, Langevin (Roberts and Tweedie, 1996) and Hamiltonian
dynamics (Duane et al., 1987) incorporate gradient information to guide proposals and improve local exploration. These ideas
have been transferred to rendering: Li et al. (2015) employed Hamiltonian dynamics with higher-order derivatives, while Luan
et al. (2020a) demonstrated that first-order gradient information already provides an effective trade-off between performance and
computational cost.
Bidirectional path tracing (BDPT)
Hachisuka et al. (2014) combined BDPT with MH, allowing the algorithm to sample
across multiple techniques via strategy-dependent proposals. This multiplexed formulation produces estimators analogous to
multiple importance sampling and forms the foundation for many modern MCMC-based rendering approaches.
Stratification and global exploration
Balancing local exploration with global discovery remains a central challenge in
MCMC-based rendering. Gruson et al. (2020) addressed this by distributing multiple chains across predefined strata, thereby
improving coverage of the integration domain and stabilizing convergence.
The Restore framework
While MH has traditionally dominated MCMC-based light transport, Wang et al. (2021) introduced
Restore, a continuous-time MCMC framework in which local dynamics are terminated after certain time and restarted from
new states. McKimm et al. (2024) later proposed adaptive regeneration strategies to reduce unnecessary restarts. More recently,
Holl et al. (2025) significantly extended the framework by allowing more general local dynamics, state-dependent regeneration
mechanisms, and tailoring Restore specifically to light transport simulation.
Variance reduction
Beyond stratification and multiple importance sampling (Veach and Guibas, 1995), a range of dedicated
variance reduction techniques has been developed for MH estimators. Among these, Rao–Blackwellization (RB) methods
(Robert and Roberts, 2021) are particularly relevant. In rendering, a commonly used Rao–Blackwellization (RB) technique is
waste-recycling (Ceperley et al., 1977), which has been applied in light transport contexts (Rioux-Lavoie et al., 2020, Section 4.6).
A different class of RB estimators, referred to as vanilla Rao–Blackwellization, was introduced by Douc and Robert (2011).
While theoretically appealing, these methods incur significant computational overhead in rendering applications. Recently, Holl
et al. (2026) proposed a variant of this technique tailored to light transport that achieves substantial variance reduction while
avoiding these costs.

Real-time path tracing
Recent work on real-time path tracing focuses on improving efficiency under strict computational
budgets. One direction is adaptive sampling, where samples are allocated dynamically based on estimated error (Bálint et al.,
2026; Vogels et al., 2018; Kuznetsov et al., 2018). Another line of work concerns denoising, often based on deep learning, which
reconstructs high-quality images from low sample counts (Rushmeier and Ward, 1994; Xu and Pattanaik, 2005; Overbeck et al.,
2009). Closely related are super-resolution techniques that upscale low-resolution renderings to high-resolution outputs while
preserving detail (Kazmierczyk et al., 2025; Thomas et al., 2022). These approaches are complementary to the improved MC
sampling techniques considered in this work.
Langevin dynamics
Langevin dynamics have been studied extensively in the literature. Numerous works analyze their general
convergence properties (Barp et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2024; Duncan et al., 2017a; Pagès and Panloup, 2023; Mou et al., 2021;
Wibisono, 2018; Durmus and Eberle, 2023). The acceleration of convergence via nonreversibility has also received significant
attention (Bierkens, 2015; Eberle and Lörler, 2024; Duncan et al., 2017b). The case most relevant to primary sample space light
transport — Langevin dynamics on the torus — was studied by García-Portugués et al. (2017).

Restore framework
In this section, we revisit the Restore framework introduced to the graphics community by Holl et al. (2025) and employ
modifications necessary to use the local dynamics described in Section 5.
Restore is designed to efficiently balance local exploration and global discovery when sampling from the target distribution π. In
this work, we consider a special instance of the Restore framework: the Jump Restore algorithm introduced in Holl et al. (2025,
Section 7).
Global discovery in Jump Restore is controlled by a user-defined distribution µ, which determines the spawn locations of local
explorations, while local exploration is governed by a user-defined Markov chain M induced by a prescribed transition kernel κ.
To use a discrete-time Markov chain M as local dynamics in the Restore framework, Jump Restore embeds the dynamics of
M into continuous-time by holding each state for a random, almost surely strictly positive duration. More precisely, if the
current state is x, then the holding time is assumed to follow an exponential distribution Exp(h(x)), where h is a strictly positive
holding rate. We denote the resulting continuous-time process by Y .
Local explorations in Jump Restore are terminated (or killed) according to a strictly positive killing rate k. Upon termination,
the local dynamics are regenerated and local exploration is continued at a spawn location drawn from a distribution µ, which
is therefore called the regeneration distribution. For our purposes, we require the following slight generalization of Holl et al.
(2026, Definition 6.1):

Langevin dynamics
The efficiency with which Restore discovers local modes of the target distribution π critically depends on the choice of local
dynamics. In the Jump Restore variants considered by Holl et al. (2025, 2026) the local dynamics still rely on MH. While
MH-based methods ensure exact invariance, they suffer from random-walk-like behavior due to reversibility caused by the
accept/reject steps. In practical applications, including light transport, the underlying state space is often Euclidean. Local
exploration in Euclidean spaces can be naturally modeled using diffusion processes, which are well suited to describe particle
motion through space. The class of diffusion processes that give rise to time-homogeneous Markov processes is given by
stochastic differential equations (SDEs)

The reversible case

Langevin sampling guides proposals toward high-density regions via gradient information. However, the Metropolis-adjustment
enforces reversibility, and the resulting trajectory still suffers from the same random-walk and backtracking effects as in (b). (d)
Unadjusted Langevin dynamics with an additional nonreversible rotational drift. The rotational component induces a structured
exploration that circulates around the mode and consistently moves toward high-density regions, resulting in faster and more
structured exploration.

Numerical study
Setup
We use SLANG (Slang, 2026b), together with its cross-platform graphics layer GFX (Slang, 2026a) to support multiple
backends, to implement Diffusion Restore. SLANG enables the computation of gradients via automatic differentiation (Griewank
and Walther, 2008).
Within the same codebase, we additionally implemented ordinary PT as a reference method. Furthermore, we implemented the
MCMC-based methods studied in Holl et al. (2025, 2026), namely Metropolis, MALA, Metropolis Restore, and MALA Restore.
While PT-based sampling is widely regarded as the current standard for real-time and interactive applications, Metropolis Restore
and MALA Restore represent the state of the art among MCMC-based offline methods. Hence, our selection covers all relevant
baselines.
Importantly, all methods share the same underlying path sampling routines; they differ exclusively in the high-level sampling
strategy (i.e., independent MC versus MCMC sampling), rather than in the construction of light transport paths.
The GPU implementations of Metropolis Restore and MALA Restore largely follow Algorithms 4.1 to 4.3. The only differences
are that the holding rate h is fixed to 1 (as described in Holl et al. (2025, Section 7.1)) and that the local dynamics correspond to
Metropolis or MALA transitions (as described in Holl et al. (2025, Section 8)).
The MH-based methods Metropolis and MALA require, upon scene changes, an additional bootstrap pass besides the initialize
pass to compute the normalization constant pλ of the target density. This bootstrap pass consists of a single dispatch call of
image resolution size and uses ordinary PT. For the PT implementation, as well as all initialize and evolve passes of Metropolis,
MALA, Metropolis Restore, MALA Restore, and Diffusion Restore, we issue one dispatch call of image resolution size per
frame, ensuring that all methods generate the same number of samples per frame.
Parameter choices
Parameters were chosen based on an ablation study balancing exploration speed and discretization bias.
For Metropolis and Metropolis Restore, we used a stddev of 1e−2, while for the Langevin-based methods MALA, MALA
Restore, and Diffusion Restore, we used stddev = 5e−3. For the MH-based methods Metropolis and MALA, we additionally
employed a large step probability (see Holl et al. (2025, Section 5)) of 0.3.
For all Restore variants, we set the killing rate parameter k0 such that each tour performs, on average, m Euler–Maruyama steps
before termination. This is achieved by choosing
k0 = pλ/(m∆t).
(28)
In all reported experiments, we use m = 64. In practive, if pλ is unavailable, the initialize pass can use an arbitrary k0 > 0,
and pλ can be estimated from this pass. The estimate can then be used to set k0 according to (28) for all subsequent evolve
passes. Note, however, that this particular choice of k0 is not required; it is introduced here only to provide better control over the
expected length of local tours. For Diffusion Restore, we set ∆t = 1e−5. The theoretically predicted bias of O(∆t) is negligible
in practice, as confirmed empirically in our results (see also the bias assessment paragraph below and our preceding discussion
on the vanishing bias in Section 5.2).
In contrast to prior work (Lehtinen et al., 2013; Bashford-Rogers et al., 2021), we employ MCMC sampling for both direct and
indirect illumination, as our experiments consistently yielded improved metrics when applying MCMC on the entire state space.
Test scenes
We evaluated a diverse set of scenes exhibiting different light transport characteristics. For the purpose of the
numerical evaluation, all experiments were conducted at a rendering resolution of 1024×768 to enable extensive equal-rendering-
time and equal-sample-count comparisons across methods and scenes. The scenes were drawn from multiple sources: COUNTRY
KITCHEN, GLASS OF WATER, SALLE DE BAIN, and VEACH, AJAR from Bitterli (2016); TORUS from Luan et al. (2020b);
SWIMMING POOL from Rioux-Lavoie et al. (2020); and TRANSPARENT MACHINES from Pharr (2025). We converted all scene
descriptions into our rendering system. For this evaluation, we support the PBRT-V4 (Pharr et al., 2021) materials CONDUCTOR,
DIELECTRIC, and DIFFUSE. As light sources, we exclusively use PBRT-V4’s DiffuseAreaLight.

Limitations
For the purpose of this submission, texture sampling was disabled. Existing differentiable rendering systems
such as MITSUBA RENDERER 3 (Jakob et al., 2022) focus on derivatives with respect to scene parameters rather than primary
sample space coordinates. Supporting the latter, while simultaneously targeting a GPU implementation, required a custom
differentiable renderer, which at the current stage does not yet support all features commonly found in production renderers,
such as texture sampling. This is, however, not a limitation of Diffusion Restore itself. SLANG provides example applications
(NVIDIA Developer Blog, 2023) demonstrating differentiable texture-based computations.
Error metrics
We evaluate the L1-error (MAE), L2-error (MSE), MRSE, and MAPE. Reference images use PT with 224
samples per pixel (SPP). To assess potential bias, all metrics are averaged over 100 independent realizations. For each realization,
a different random seed is used, and the same set of 100 seeds is shared across all methods. This ensures that any observed
differences are solely due to the respective sampling and exploration strategies. All methods are run for up to at least 360
rendering seconds and 218 SPP. All measurements reached an mean squared error (MSE) below 1e−5.
Plots of the MSE over rendering time for the scenes shown in Figures 1 and 4 to 8 are provided in Figure 3. Additional plots
showing all metrics as functions of rendering time (Section A) and SPP (Section B), as well as error tables at fixed rendering
times and fixed SPP, can be found in the appendix.
As the results show, all MCMC sampling methods exhibit accelerated convergence compared to PT. The gradient-based methods,
MALA and MALA Restore, achieve improved convergence despite their higher computational complexity, due to more effective
sample guidance toward high-probability regions. Finally, our Diffusion Restore sampler further accelerates convergence, owing
to the introduced momentum and the absence of a Metropolis-adjustment. Due to the nature of global discovery in the MH
methods Metropolis and MALA, the renderings still exhibit a significant amount of bias at early time budgets such as 1/30
seconds of computation. While bias-reduction techniques could in principle mitigate this effect, they would consume part of the
same fixed computational budget and thus do not improve performance under equal-time constraints. These observations are
consistent across all evaluated scenes.
Bias assessment
In all reported experiments, we do not observe any bias-dominated regime. Even at long rendering times (up
to 360 seconds and 218 SPP), the error metrics continue to decrease without saturation and rapidly approach 0. Especially in
real-time applications, where sample budgets are significantly lower, the discretization bias is negligible compared to MC error.
Visual assessment
A comparison across all methods is shown in Figure 1. In Figures 4 to 8, we focus on Diffusion Restore,
PT, and MALA Restore — the current state of the art for offline MCMC light transport (Holl et al., 2026). Comprehensive
comparisons across all methods and scenes will be made available through an interactive viewer accompanying this paper,
which is scheduled for public release later in 2026. All images are rendered with an equal computation time of 1/30 seconds,
corresponding to the per-frame budget of a 30 FPS application. In addition, the interactive viewer will provide videos comparing
PT, Metropolis, and Diffusion Restore side by side, where Metropolis serves as a classical baseline, having long been the
standard MCMC rendering method. For these videos, all methods are rendered along the same pre-recorded camera trajectory,
parameterized by arc length to ensure constant motion, and evaluated at a fixed budget of 30 FPS. This setup enables a fair
comparison of visual quality under identical computational constraints.
We note that our optimized GPU implementation of MALA Restore already provides highly competitive performance. As a result,
gains achieved by Diffusion Restore may appear marginal in certain scenes, particularly at short rendering times. Nevertheless,
these improvements accumulate over time and become increasingly significant as the dimensionality of the sampling problem
grows, where improved local exploration lead to progressively larger advantages.
Hardware resources
All experiments were conducted on a desktop system equipped with an AMD Ryzen 9 7950X3D CPU,
an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4080 Super GPU (16 GB GDDR6X, Gainward Python III OC), and 96 GB DDR5-6400 CL32 memory
(Kingston Fury Renegade, 2×48 GB). The GPU was used for all rendering and MCMC computations, while the CPU handled
host-side orchestration.
7
Conclusion
We introduced Diffusion Restore, an MCMC light transport method that consistently outperforms prior MCMC-based state-
of-the-art offline rendering techniques across all considered metrics. Furthermore, we presented a GPU implementation and
provided an empirical evaluation demonstrating that Diffusion Restore also achieves superior performance under real-time
constraints compared to all competing methods — including conventional, PT, which is the standard sampling approach in
interactive applications such as games.

Future work
Exploring additional nonreversible drift components U has the potential to further accelerate convergence.
Beyond improving local exploration, another key aspect of the Restore framework that remains largely unexplored is the design
of the global dynamics. Instead of sampling regeneration points uniformly, it may be beneficial — especially in interactive
settings — to exploit information about previously discovered modes when constructing the regeneration distribution µ for
subsequent frames. In this context, generative approaches may provide a promising direction.
