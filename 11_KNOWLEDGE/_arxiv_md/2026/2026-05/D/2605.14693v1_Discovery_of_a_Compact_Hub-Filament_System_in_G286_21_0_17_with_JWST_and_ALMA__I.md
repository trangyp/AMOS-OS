ABSTRACT
We present a multi-wavelength study of the massive protocluster G286.21+0.17 (G286) using JWST
near-infrared imaging and ALMA H13CO+(1–0) observations. The JWST images uncover a compact
(∼0.5 pc) hub-filament system (HFS), comprising a dense central hub connected by at least four
converging filaments seen in absorption, along with multiple H2 protostellar jets/outflows. The hub
hosts dense core G286c1. The H13CO+ emission confirms this HFS over [−19.2, −16.4] km s−1. The
JWST images further trace prominent photodissociation regions around the H ii region A, powered by
a B-type star. The radial distribution of ALMAGAL 1.38 mm core properties reveals steep power-law
slopes toward the hub center. Within the inner hub (r < 8′′, ∼0.1 pc), the core number density follows
ρ [pc−2] ∝r−2.4±0.5, the surface density scales as Σ [g cm−2] ∝r−1.0±0.2, and the enclosed core mass
varies as Mcore [M⊙] ∝r−1.2±0.2, while core diameters remain approximately constant (Dcore [AU] ∝
r−0.1±0.1). These trends, along with filament mass accretion rates of 7 × 10−6–1.8 × 10−4 M⊙yr−1,
support a competitive accretion scenario in which gravitational focusing enhances core growth toward
the hub center. Filament linewidths increase from tail/outer-region to head/hub-region, consistent with
gravity-driven turbulence. However, the absence of a preferred alignment between velocity gradients
and gravitational force directions may indicate a dynamically evolved system. The HFS likely formed
through large-scale gas layer interactions and compression by the adjacent H ii region. Overall, star
formation in G286 appears regulated by filamentary accretion, competitive core growth in the hub,
and stellar feedback.

1. INTRODUCTION
The processes governing the formation and early
growth of massive OB stars (> 8 M⊙) and stellar clus-
ters are still not fully understood.
Growing obser-
vational evidence indicates that hub-filament systems
(HFSs; Myers 2009), dense filamentary networks that
channel material into a central hub are crucial sites for
facilitating the rapid mass accumulation required for the
formation of massive stars and stellar clusters (Motte
et al. 2018; Kumar et al. 2020; Padoan et al. 2020;
Vázquez-Semadeni et al. 2009, 2017, 2019; Zhou et al.
2022; Yang et al. 2023; Maity et al. 2023, 2025; Dewan-
gan et al. 2026). This means that ongoing inflow of cold
gas can progressively increase the mass of the hub, al-
lowing the cluster to evolve into a more massive system.
However, given the hierarchical nature of HFSs, which
span spatial scales from ∼0.1–1 pc to ≳10 pc (e.g., Ku-
mar et al. 2020; Bhadari et al. 2022), the physical mech-
anisms governing mass inflow across these scales remain
poorly understood. On small scales (≲1 pc), gravity-
driven accretion along filaments is widely recognized as
a major process (e.g., Zhou et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2023;
Bhadari et al. 2025). In contrast, on larger scales, mass
inflow may be governed by alternative mechanisms, in-
cluding pressure gradients induced by large-scale turbu-
lent motions (e.g., inertial inflow; Padoan et al. 2020),
global gravitational collapse of the parent cloud, or the
self-gravity of the filaments themselves.
Furthermore, it is important to note that once a mas-
sive star or protocluster evolves, the signatures of HFSs
weaken or disappear, at least for the smaller-scale struc-
tures within the hierarchy (Zhou et al. 2022). To inter-
pret these scale-dependent inflow behaviors and their
role in cluster formation, several theoretical scenarios
have been proposed.
These include the global non-
isotropic collapse (GNIC) scenario (Tigé et al. 2017;
Motte et al. 2018), the inertial inflow model (Padoan
et al. 2020), and the Filaments to Clusters (F2C) model
(Kumar et al. 2020). The GNIC picture unifies key as-
pects of competitive accretion (Bonnell et al. 2001, 2004)
with the global hierarchical collapse (GHC) paradigm
(Vázquez-Semadeni et al. 2009, 2017, 2019). The com-
bined high-resolution near-infared (NIR) observations
from the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and sub-
millimeter observations from the Atacama Large Mil-
limeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) now offer an un-
parallel opportunity to probe the inner morphology and
kinematics of star-forming regions with exceptional de-
tail, enabling a critical assessment of the existing theo-
retical models.

3.1.2. JWST narrow band images: outflows and
photodissociation regions
It is well established that the Spitzer 4.5 µm band
includes the hydrogen recombination line (Brα at 4.05
µm) and strong molecular hydrogen (H2) emission (ν =
0–0 S(9) at 4.693 µm), the latter of which is commonly
excited by outflow shocks. Conversely, the Spitzer 3.6,
5.8, and 8.0 µm bands are primarily sensitive to poly-
cyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) features at 3.3, 6.2,
and 7.7 µm, which serve as excellent tracers of the pho-
todissociation regions (PDRs) surrounding H ii regions.
With the narrow-band JWST f405N and f470N images,
we can further probe the specific zones exhibiting Brα
and H2 emissions, respectively.
A three-color composite map is presented in Fig-
ure 2a, which is made of the Spitzer 8 µm (in red),
JWST f470N (in green), and JWST f356W (in blue)
images.
This composite map highlights regions dom-
inated by H2 emission at 4.693 µm and additionally
reveals a shell-like structure traced by the extended 8
µm and 3.565 µm emission. The H2 emission detected
in the JWST maps can arise from either shock exci-
tation or UV fluorescence.
Shock-excited H2 is typi-
cally produced by protostellar outflows or jets, where
collisions in fast-moving gas heat the molecular mate-
rial to high temperatures, resulting in bright and com-
pact knot-like H2 features.
In contrast, fluorescently
excited H2 originates when UV photons from nearby
massive stars irradiate molecular gas, generating more
diffuse emission structures along the PDR interfaces. To
further examine the H2 features, the JWST ratio map
(i.e., f470N/f410M) of f470N (λeff/∆λ: 4.708/0.051)
and f410M (λeff/∆λ:
4.083/0.436) images is gener-
ated.
The resulting map toward the G286-clump is
presented in Figure 2b, where several H2 protostellar
jets/outflows are clearly seen. The location of the dense
core G286c1 is also indicated in Figure 2b (see the dot-
ted circle). The H2 features are clearly resolved thanks
to the high angular resolution of the JWST infrared
images. The bright, localized H2 features observed in
the f470N/f410M ratio map are therefore consistent with
shock excitation associated with protostellar jet/outflow
activities. To probe the 4.05 µm Brα emission, we have
also generated the JWST ratio map (i.e., f405N/f410M)
of f405N (λeff/∆λ: 4.053/0.046) and f410M (λeff/∆λ:
4.083/0.436) images.
Figure 2c shows a three-color composite map created
using the f405N/f410M (Brα; red), f470N/f410M (H2;
green), and JWST f356W (blue) images. In this repre-
sentation, the red traces ionized gas, the green highlights
H2 emission, and the blue corresponds to PAH 3.3 µm
emission captured in the f356W band. Hence, the mor-
phology suggests that the Brα emission from H ii region
A is enveloped by PAH 3.3 µm emission, itself encircled
by a layer of H2 emission. The extended H2 emission sur-
rounding the ionized region likely traces fluorescent H2
illuminated by the massive stars. The sources responsi-
ble for powering the H2 outflows appear to be spatially
offset from the PDRs.
In the direction of the dense
core G286c1, the presence of radio continuum emission
alongside the non-detection of JWST Brα emission can
be explained by the combined effects of extinction, sen-
sitivity limitations, and the physical properties of the
ionized gas.

4. DISCUSSION
The molecular cloud associated with G286 is traced
by CHaMP 13CO(1–0) emission in the velocity range
of [−23.2, −16.4] km s−1 (see Appendix B and Fig-
ure B2a). The cloud hosts the G286-clump, which ex-
hibits prominent filamentary structures in absorption
along with H2 jets/outflows in the JWST NIR images,
as well as a shell-like morphology associated with H ii re-
gion A.
Observations with Spitzer and Herschel along with ra-
dio continuum maps have demonstrated that MIR shells
or bubbles associated with H ii regions, filaments, and
young stellar clusters commonly coexist in massive star-
forming regions, highlighting the interplay of multiple
physical processes such as massive-star feedback and fil-
amentary accretion flows (Churchwell et al. 2006; De-
harveng et al. 2010; André et al. 2014).
The advent
of JWST has further advanced this field by providing
high-resolution narrow- and broad-band NIR observa-
tions that reveal these structures in unprecedented de-
tail (e.g., Dewangan et al. 2023, 2024a,b; Jadhav et al.
2025).
Using ALMA observations (∼1′′ resolution) of G286,
Cheng et al. (2020b) found two spatially distinct veloc-
ity groups of dense cores, suggesting ongoing infall and
a dynamically unrelaxed system. Our combined anal-
ysis of JWST NIR images and ALMA H13CO+(1–0)
line data reveals a new and more comprehensive pic-
ture of the G286 region.
These observations provide
strong evidence for a compact/small-scale HFS with a
size ∼0.5 pc, in which a central dense hub is connected
to at least least four converging filaments (F1–F4). The
previously known G286c1 protocluster as well as ALMA-
GAL cores are predominantly concentrated toward the
central hub of this HFS, which is traced in the velocity
range of [−19.2, −16.4] km s−1. In particular, the inclu-
sion of JWST data reveals extinction filaments and their
configuration that were not identified in earlier studies
(e.g., Cheng et al. 2020b; Zhou et al. 2021). In addi-
tion to the HFS, multiple H2 protostellar jets/outflows
are also evident in the JWST images (see Figures 2a
and 2b), and may be associated with other dense cores
in the G286-clump. The central hub of the HFS, which
hosts the dense core G286c1, is also associated with
the SMGPS 1.3 GHz continuum emission (i.e., CRS).
The velocity component ([−19.2, −16.4] km s−1) shows
a striking morphological correspondence with the fila-
mentary network identified in the JWST images (see
Figure A1), reinforcing the physical association between
the dense gas and the embedded stellar population. Sev-
eral of the ALMAGAL cores are highly self-gravitating
with surface densities Σ ≳3 g cm−2 (see Section 3.2),
indicating ongoing gravitational collapse. The coexis-
tence of these cores with a large population of very low-
mass, low-Σ cores suggests a hierarchical and non-coeval
core formation scenario, consistent with the competi-
tive accretion model. Figure 5 illustrates this picture,
showing that the gravitational center of the cluster (i.e.,
the hub center) hosts a large number of cores, includ-
ing the most massive and highest surface-density ob-
jects. Both the core mass and surface density decrease

We define the hub boundary (i.e., r = 0.1 pc)
based on the observed steep power-law slopes in the ra-
dial distributions of core mass and surface density; how-
ever, the precise extent of the hub remains uncertain in
the HFS and will be investigated in future studies using
larger samples of HFSs. In the G286 HFS, the estimated
mass accretion rates along the filaments (F1–F4) range
from 7×10−6 to 1.8×10−4 M⊙yr−1 (see Section 3.2.2).
Padoan et al. (2020) showed that mass-flow rates toward
prestellar cores scale nearly linearly with spatial size, in-
creasing from ∼10−5 M⊙yr−1 at ∼0.1 pc to ∼10−4
M⊙yr−1 at ∼1 pc, consistent with observational mea-
surements (Yuan et al. 2018; Sanhueza et al. 2021, 2025;
Bhadari et al. 2025; Morii et al. 2025; Wells et al. 2025;
Gupta et al. 2026). The inflow rates derived in this work
fall within a similar range, also comparable to those re-
ported for feedback-dominated regions (e.g., Wells et al.
2025). Nevertheless, these estimates should be treated
with caution, since the filament masses inferred from
molecular line emission are subject to systematic uncer-
tainties arising from assumptions of optically thin emis-
sion. Additionally, projection effects and uncertainties
in filament geometry can bias the inferred values; for ex-
ample, inclination angles between 30◦and 60◦may lead
to uncertainties of up to a factor of ∼2 in the estimated
accretion rates.
Furthermore, the linewidths measured along these fil-
aments decrease markedly from the head toward the
tail, with typical gradients of 4–6 km s−1 pc−1 (see
Figure 6). This behavior likely reflects gravity-driven
turbulence and core formation, as suggested by Peretto
et al. (2014). However, we find no evidence for a prefer-
ential parallel alignment between the velocity gradients
and the gravitational force vectors along the filaments
(see Figure 7c), implying that coherent gravity-driven
inflows are not clearly traced in the present kinematic
state of the system. This absence of alignment may re-
flect the relatively evolved nature of the G286-HFS, in
which stellar feedback from the forming star cluster has
weakened or erased the signatures of coherent gas inflow.
This behavior contrasts with the G11P1-HFS studied by
Bhadari et al. (2025), where a preferential alignment be-
tween gravitational force and velocity gradient vectors
was reported, indicative of a younger system prior to the
onset of significant feedback. Additionally, the lack of
a coherent velocity gradient and gravitational signature
may suggest that the region could be shaped by random
turbulent compression rather than gravitational inflow
(Federrath & Klessen 2012; Padoan & Nordlund 2011).
As mentioned, the JWST NIR observations have re-
vealed widespread H2 jets/outflows and PDRs around
H ii region A. The combination of the 4.69 µm narrow-
band observations with Brα emission traced by the
F405N filter enables an efficient separation of shock-
excited emission associated with outflows and stellar
feedback from fluorescently excited features. The driv-
ing sources of the H2 outflows appear to be located away
from the boundary layers between ionized gas and the
neutral/molecular PDR (see Figure 2c), suggesting that
ongoing star formation is not confined to the immediate
feedback dominated interfaces.
The observed HFS in G286 may reflect signatures of
the onset of the GNIC scenario (Tigé et al. 2017; Motte
et al. 2018). In other words, the observed HFS suggests
ongoing mass accretion along filaments, while the pres-
ence of the PDR around the H ii region A indicates the
influence of stellar feedback. The coexistence of these
two signatures suggests that the clump, associated with
intense star formation activities, is evolving under the

Zhou et al. (2021) reported a collision between two
orthogonal filaments (NE-SW and NW-SE), forming an
L-shaped structure with the interaction junction coinci-
dent with the protocluster associated with subclump A.
While Zhou et al. (2021) suggested that H ii region A
primarily affects the filament NE-SW, we argue that
this structure is not independent but instead represents
a branch formed through the expansion of the H ii re-
gion A and physically connected to the HFS. Supporting
evidence for such expansion is provided by the position-
velocity diagram of CHaMP 13CO(1–0) emission, which
shows an arc-like structure (Figure B2 and also ap-
pendix B). Such arc- or C-shaped features are well-
known kinematic signatures of expanding H ii regions
(e.g., Arce et al. 2011). The impact of massive-star feed-
back is further supported by pressure estimates (e.g.,
Dewangan et al. 2017). At a projected distance of 0.7 pc,
corresponding to the separation between the CRS and
H ii region A (see the magenta contour in Figure 1b), the
combined pressure from the H ii region, radiation, and
stellar winds (Ptotal = PHII + Prad + Pwind) driven by a
massive B-type star is estimated to be ≳10−10 dynes
cm−2 (e.g., Dewangan et al. 2017; Sharma et al. 2024).
This pressure exceeds that of a typical cold molecular
cloud (PMC ∼10−11–10−12 dynes cm−2), assuming a
temperature of ∼20 K and particle densities of ∼103–
104 cm−3 (see Table 7.3 of Dyson & Williams 1980).
The origin of the HFS in G286 likely involves multi-
ple processes acting in concert. One possibility is that
interactions between gas layers with different velocities
could produce the observed HFS morphology and could
trigger protocluster formation (see velocity components
at −18.76 and −20.87 km s−1 in the bottom-right panel
of Figure A1 and also Appendix A). Such collisions be-
tween gas layers have been proposed as a viable mecha-
nism for HFS formation in several recent studies (Maity
et al. 2024; Georgatos & Whitworth 2026, and references
therein). Alternatively, or additionally, feedback from
H ii region A may have enhanced the accumulation of
dense gas along the filament NW-SE, thereby promoting
interactions among multiple gas layers. Given the close
proximity of H ii region A and its powering by a B-type
star, this feedback is likely to have played an important
role in the formation of the protocluster as clearly seen
in the JWST color-composite map (Figure 2c).
Collectively, our results imply that massive star and
cluster formation in G286 arises from the joint action of
ongoing accretion, competitive core growth in the hub,
and stellar feedback.
5. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION
To develop a comprehensive picture of the phys-
ical processes operating in the massive star- and
cluster-forming region G286, we carried out a multi-
wavelength, multi-scale analysis by combining obser-
vations from various facilities and surveys, including
JWST, Spitzer-GLIMPSE, CHaMP, ALMA/ATOMS,
ALMA/ALMAGAL, and SMGPS. The major outcomes
of this work are outlined as follows:
• JWST NIR images reveal a compact (∼0.5 pc)
hub-filament system (G286 HFS), comprising a
dense central hub connected to at least four con-
verging filaments (F1–F4) detected in absorption.
Beyond this structure, numerous H2 protostellar
jets/outflows are also identified.
• The HFS is kinematically confirmed by ATOMS
H13CO+(1–0) emission over the velocity range
[−19.2, −16.4] km s−1.
• Filaments (F1–F4) exhibit mass accretion rates
of 7 × 10−6 to 1.8 × 10−4M⊙yr−1 and linewidths
that increase from tail/outer-region to head/hub-
region (gradients ∼4–6 km s−1 pc−1), consistent
with gravity-driven turbulence.
• No clear alignment is found between velocity gra-
dients and gravitational force directions, suggest-
ing an evolved system.
• The G286c1 protocluster, associated with compact
1.3 GHz radio continuum emisison as well as AL-
MAGAL cores, is mainly concentrated toward the
central hub of the HFS.
• Within the hub, many ALMAGAL cores are
highly self-gravitating and exceed the massive star
formation threshold, with surface densities Σ ≳
1 g cm−2, indicating ongoing gravitational col-
lapse, while the presence of numerous low-mass,
low-Σ cores suggests hierarchical and non-coeval
core formation.
• The radial distribution of ALMAGAL cores de-
fines a hub of radius ∼0.1 pc.
Within this
region, core properties follow steep power laws:
