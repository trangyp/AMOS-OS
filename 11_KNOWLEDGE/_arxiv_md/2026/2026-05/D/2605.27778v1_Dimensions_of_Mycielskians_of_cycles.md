DIMENSIONS OF MYCIELSKIANS OF CYCLES
BRIAN CHUNG AND MIKE KREBS
Abstract. The Mycielskian is a standard construction studied in many an introductory graph
theory course. It is natural to consider Mycielskians of cycles, some of the simplest of all graphs.
This paper deals with the so-called “dimension” of such graphs. The dimension of a graph G is the
smallest positive integer n such that there exists a one-to-one correspondence between the vertices
of G and some collection of points in n-dimensional Euclidean space such that if two vertices in G
are adjacent, then the distance between the corresponding points is 1. In previous works, it had
been proven that the dimension of the Mycielskian of a k-cycle is 3 when k is 3, 4, or 5, and 2 when
k = 10. In this paper, we answer the question completely. Namely, we show that the dimension is
3 when k ̸= 10, and 2 when k = 10.

1.1. Elementary notions from graph theory. In this subsection we provide some graph theory
basics; readers familiar with them may want to skip ahead to the next subsection.
A (simple) graph consists of two sets V and E, where every element of E is a two-element set of
V . Elements of V (the vertex set) are called vertices, and elements of E (the edge set) are called
edges. We denote the vertex set of a graph X by V (X), and its edge set by E(X). We visualize
vertices as dots, and we visualize an edge {a, b} as a line segment or curve with a and b as endpoints.
We say that a and b are adjacent, denoted a ∼b, if {a, b} ∈E.
The so-called cycle graphs Cn comprise one standard family of graphs. The vertex set of Cn is
{0, 1, . . . , n −1}, where a and b are adjacent if and only if a −b ≡±1 (mod n).
Two graphs G and H are isomorphic if there is a bijective function f : G →H from the vertex
set of one to the vertex set of another such that a and b are adjacent in G if and only if f(a) and
f(b) are adjacent in H. We say that a graph H is a subgraph of a graph G if V (H) ⊂V (G) and
E(H) ⊂E(G).
1.2. (Unit-distance) dimension of a graph. We define Un to be the graph with Rn as its vertex
set, where two vertices are adjacent if and only if the Euclidean distance between them is 1. We say
that a graph is a unit-distance graph if it is isomorphic to a subgraph of U2.
In [2], Erd˝os, Harary, and Tutte introduce the notion of the “dimension” of a graph. That is, they
define the (unit-distance) dimension of a graph G, denoted dim(G), to be the minimum positive
integer n such that G is a subgraph of Un. (Although [2] simply calls this the “dimension” as do
many other publications that followed suit, we include the parenthetical “unit-distance” here to
disambiguate this definition from other competing notions of dimension for graphs.)
Example 1.2. For an integer n ≥3, we define the cycle graph Cn to be the graph with vertex
set {1, . . . , n}, where two vertices are adjacent if and only if their difference is ±1 modulo n. It
is straightforward to show that no subgraph of U1 is isomorphic to a cycle graph. However, one
obtains a subgraph of U2 isomorphic to Cn by considering a regular n-gon of side length 1. In this
way C3 “looks like” an equilateral triangle, C4 a square, and so on. It follows that dim(Cn) = 2.
Example 1.3. For an integer n ≥2, we define the complete graph Kn to be the graph with vertex set
{1, . . . , n}, where every vertex is adjacent to every other vertex. It is relatively straightforward to
prove that dim(Kn) = n −1. The embedding in Un−1 is achieved by taking the vertices of a regular

1.3. Unit-distance dimension of Mycielskians of cycles. The “Mycielskian” is a standard
construction in graph theory. It was originally used in [6] to construct a family of triangle-free
graphs with arbitrarily large chromatic number. Since then it has been much studied, to put it
mildly — as of the time of writing, Semantic Scholar lists 584 citations for [6].
Definition 1.4. Let G be a graph. We define M(G), the Mycielskian of G, to be the following
graph. The vertex set of M(G) is {F} ∪(V (G) × {0, 1}), where F /∈V (G) × {0, 1}. (We think of
the vertex set of M(G) as consisting of two copies of V (G), plus one additional vertex F.) For
adjacent vertices a, b ∈V (G), we have that (a, 0) ∼(b, 1) and (a, 1) ∼(b, 1). Moreover, we have
that F ∼(a, 0) for all a ∈V (G). There are no other edges in M(G).

3. A generalization
The method of proof used in Theorem 1.1 can be generalized, as we now discuss. Given a graph
G, we define an auxiliary graph VecNeg(G) as follows. We regard G as a directed graph, with a
directed edge from A to B whenever A and B are adjacent in G. We denote the edge from A to B by
−−→
AB. The vertex set of VecNeg(G) is the set of directed edges of G. Recall that a walk in a graph is
a sequence (v0, v1, . . . vn) of vertices, where vi−1 is adjacent to vi for all i = 1, . . . , n. We sometimes
omit the parentheses and commas when writing a walk. The length of the walk (v0, v1, . . . vn) is n,
and this walk is said to be closed if v0 = vn. The vertices −−→
AB and −−→
CD of VecNeg(G) are adjacent
if and only if (i) C = B and D = A, or (ii) the vertices A, B, C and D are distinct, and ABCDA
is a closed walk of length 4 in G. Recall that a finite graph is nonbipartite if and only if it contains
a closed walk of odd length.
Theorem 3.1. Let G be a finite graph. Then dim G ≥3 if either
(1) The graph VecNeg(G) is nonbipartite, or
(2) For some distinct vertices F, A, B of G, where A and B are adjacent to F, there exists in
VecNeg(G) a walk of even length from −→
FA to −−→
FB.
Proof. Suppose that G is isomorphic to a subgraph G′ of U2. By a slight abuse of notation, we
identify G with G′, and every directed edge in G with the corresponding unit vector in R2.
We first observe that if −−→
AB and −−→
CD are adjacent in VecNeg(G), then −−→
AB = −−−→
CD. (Hence the
notation “VecNeg.”) Here’s why. If C = B and D = A, then the result is immediate. Otherwise,
we have that the vertices A, B, C and D are distinct, and ABCDA is a closed walk of length 4 in
G. This implies that ABCD forms a rhombus of side length 1, and the result follows.
If (1) holds, then there is a closed walk (e0, . . . , en) of odd length in VecNeg(G). Here each ei
is a directed edge in G. Using the result of the previous paragraph, we get that ei−1 = −ei for all
i = 1, . . . , n. Hence e0 = (−1)nen = (−1)ne0. This is a contradiction, because n is odd, and e0 is a
unit vector.
