ABSTRACT
Language models are trained to follow instructions, but they are also powerful pattern completers.
What happens when these two objectives conflict? We construct conversations in which a user
instruction to behave in a target way T (e.g., always output a specific token, answer in a particular
language, or adopt a persona) is opposed by N hardcoded assistant turns demonstrating a competing
pattern P. We then measure instruction-following (IF) rates in this setting, across 13 models and
16 different instructions, for up to 50 turns. Average instruction-following rates range from 1% to
99% across models, largely uncorrelated with standard capability benchmarks. The transition from
instruction-following to pattern-following is universal but highly model-dependent. Robustness is
modulated both by instruction content, with models resisting induction longer when instructions
align with their trained value priors, and by output format, with diverse multi-token responses
proving substantially more resistant than single-token outputs. Chain-of-thought reasoning improves
robustness but does not eliminate susceptibility, and can produce dissociation between correct
deliberation and incorrect output. When asked to predict their behavior in this setting, models
achieve 83.5% accuracy on average but systematically underestimate their own resistance to induction
pressure. These results suggest that instruction-following remains brittle under induction pressure
even for otherwise capable models, and that output diversity, rather than semantic engagement with
the input, is the primary factor predicting robustness.

Introduction
Large language models are trained to be both versatile next-token predictors and helpful conversational agents. Pre-
training on diverse corpora creates pattern completers that can learn from context at inference time through in-context
learning (Dong et al., 2023; Riechers et al., 2025) and simulate a variety of personas (nostalgebraist, 2025). Post-training,
through supervised finetuning, RLHF (Bai et al., 2022), DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023), and related techniques—then
attempts to channel this flexibility into a stable, helpful assistant persona that is consistently presented to the user (Lu
et al., 2026; Marks et al., 2026), regardless of context. The same “porosity” to context that allows the model to adapt
comes with its risks. It can cause a model to infer and adopt a broadly misaligned persona from a few examples (Afonin
et al., 2026), and it can be exploited by jailbreaking attacks that construct conversational histories that portray the model
as having already complied with harmful requests (Anil et al., 2024), or that gradually escalate from benign exchanges
to harmful ones (Russinovich et al., 2025). A key goal of post-training is therefore to inoculate models against such
context-driven behavioral updating that could lead to misalignment or other undesirable behavior, while still allowing
them to track factual and conversational content in service of being helpful assistants.
Instruction-following robustness has been evaluated along numerous dimensions (Pyatkin et al., 2025; Qin et al.,
2024; Zhou et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2024), but existing benchmarks do not evaluate this robustness in the presence of
conflicting evidence about the model’s behavior. In this paper, we introduce a controlled paradigm for studying this
induction-instruction conflict. The model receives an explicit instruction specifying target behavior T, followed by N

2
Experimental setup
2.1
Core paradigm
Each trial presents a model with a conversation consisting of three parts. First, a system prompt (“You are a helpful
assistant.”) establishes a generic assistant role. Second, a combined user message concatenates an instruction with
an initial factual question; the instruction takes the form “I want you to [target description] as your answer
to each one of my questions in this conversation.” Third, N hardcoded turns follow, each consisting of a factual
question paired with a hardcoded assistant response that outputs pattern P instead of the instructed target T. Finally,
a free-generation turn poses a new factual question for which the model generates its own response. In a secondary
analysis, we test an alternative instruction that explicitly informs the model about the hardcoding. All main results
use the no-hint instruction; hint results are shown in Appendix J. Our main independent variable is N, the number of
hardcoded turns. We test N ∈{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11, 16, 22, 31, 43, 50} (twelve values, approximately log-spaced). As
N increases, the in-context pattern grows stronger while the original instruction recedes in context. We then evaluate
whether the model’s free-generation response to the last sampled question matches T (instruction-following, scored 1)
or P (induction-following, scored 0). We evaluate 35 question sets per (model, condition, N) cell, with decoding
temperature set to 0 (Additional T=1 results in Appendix K). Questions drawn from a question bank using deterministic
seeds shared across models, for a total of 35 × 12 = 420 trials per (model, condition).

2.2
Conditions
We test two families of conditions that vary the content of P and T while holding the paradigm structure constant
(Table 1).
Fixed-output conditions.
P and T are fixed tokens; the instruction asks the model to “always output X” regardless of
the question. This isolates instruction robustness from content generation difficulty. We use five conditions varying
whether the instruction aligns with the model’s trained values and factual knowledge (neutral, value-aligned/misaligned,
factual-aligned/misaligned).
Task-based conditions.
P and T are tasks rather than fixed tokens. The model must perform a meaningful operation
on the question content—translating, reformatting, or adopting a persona—and generate a full response. We use eight
conditions across four behavior types: translation (2), persona adoption (2), code generation (2), and preference weaving
(2). Hardcoded turns use pre-generated LLM responses; further details in Appendix E.
The aligned/misaligned framing applies to both families. A condition is aligned when the instruction asks for behavior
consistent with the model’s trained preferences (e.g., helpfulness-affirming, factually true) and misaligned when it
conflicts with those priors. Two additional follow-up conditions are introduced in Appendix I and discussed in Section
3 to disentangle the factors driving the robustness gap between condition families
2.3
Models
We evaluate 13 base model configurations spanning a range of scales, architectures, and post-training recipes:
Claude Opus 4.6 and Claude Sonnet 4.6 (Anthropic); Gemini 2.5 Flash, Gemma-3 12B-IT and Gemma-3 27B-IT
(Google DeepMind); GPT-5.2 non-reasoning (OpenAI); Hermes-4 70B (NousResearch); Kimi-K2-Instruct (Moonshot
AI); Llama 3.1 70B-Instruct and Llama 3.3 70B-Instruct (Meta); OLMo 3.1 32B-Instruct (Allen Institute for AI); and
Qwen3 30B-A3B and Qwen3 235B-A22B (AliBaba). Full model list in Appendix B.
We run two additional comparisons to study specific factors:
• Reasoning variants. GPT-5.2-medium (reasoning-enabled, medium budget) vs. GPT-5.2 (non-reasoning),
and Hermes-4 70B-reasoning vs. Hermes-4 70B. The goal was to test whether explicit reasoning improves
instruction robustness.
• Training stages. OLMo 3.1 32B at three post-training stages: SFT only, SFT+DPO, and SFT+DPO+RLVR
(Olmo et al., 2025). Results in Appendix G.

2.4
Evaluation protocol
Protocol 1: Behavioral baseline.
Each (model, condition, N) cell is evaluated with 35 independently seeded question
sets under greedy decoding. The score for each trial is binary (1 = instruction-followed, 0 = pattern-followed), and cell
scores are averaged to yield IF rates. We report aggregate results across conditions and models; per-condition heatmaps
appear in Appendix C.
Protocol 2: Self-prediction.
After the N hardcoded turns and immediately before the free-generation question, we
insert a prediction request: “Before you answer the next question, predict what you will do. Given the conversation
so far, will you [target description] or [pattern description]? Respond with your prediction and a brief
explanation.” The model generates a prediction, and then the final question is posed for free generation. This yields a
paired (prediction, behavior) observation per trial.
Scoring uses a per-condition-type scorer (exact match, set-membership, language detection, format heuristics, or LLM
judge); details in Appendix A.
3
Results
3.1
The transition is universal but model-dependent
Every model we tested eventually shows greatly reduced instruction-following as the number of hardcoded turns N
increases, in at least some of the experimental conditions, confirming that induction pressure is a universal phenomenon.
However, the transition point varies considerably across models (Table 2; Figure 2; individual model results in
Appendix D). In fixed-output conditions, average instruction-following (IF) rates across all N values range from 0.99
(Llama 3.3 70B) to 0.01 (Qwen3 235B). At N=1, nearly all models follow instructions at high rates, but by N=3 only
six of thirteen models remain above 50%. The transition boundary—the first N at which IF rate drops to or below
50%—spans from N=1 (GPT-5.2, Hermes-4 70B, Gemini 2.5 Flash, Qwen3 235B) to never crossing the threshold
within our range for Llama 3.3 70B, which maintains near-perfect instruction-following at every N tested, including
N=50.
Ranking is consistent across condition families.
The model ranking is broadly preserved between fixed-output and
task-based conditions, though task-based conditions, which require the model to generate full responses rather than
single tokens, show higher average IF rates overall. In these conditions, GPT-5.2 maintains the latest transition point
(N=43), followed by Claude Opus 4.6 and Llama 3.3 70B (N=22).

3.2
Robustness is easier for task-based instructions.
Task-based conditions consistently produce higher instruction-following rates than fixed-output conditions (Figure 2b;
Table 2). Two properties distinguish these condition families: task-based conditions require the model to engage with
the question content to produce a well-formed response, and their outputs are varied and natural-sounding across turns,
whereas fixed-output conditions require only a single repeated token, a rather unnatural response for a language model.
Either property, or both, could explain the robustness gap; we design two follow-up conditions to disentangle these
factors. In the classify-question condition, the model reads each question and classifies it as “science” or “humanities”.
This task requires engagement with the user’s question but produces single-token outputs matching the format of
fixed-output conditions. The random-facts condition produces 1–3 sentence free-text responses about a fixed topic,
unrelated to the input question. We evaluate three models on the new conditions (Gemma-3 27B, GPT-5.2, and
Llama 3.3 70B); results are shown in Appendix I. The results suggest that content engagement is not the primary driver:
the ‘classify-question‘ condition results aligned with fixed-output patterns, while the random-facts condition performs
better, matching or exceeding task-based robustness. See Section 5 for possible interpretations of these findings.
Capability predicts task-based but not fixed-output robustness.
We correlate each model’s average IF rate with
two external benchmarks: GPQA (Rein et al., 2023) and IFBench (Pyatkin et al., 2025) (Figure 8; additional analysis in
Appendix E). Once again, the results differ between fixed-output and task-based conditions. In fixed-output conditions,
neither benchmark achieves a significant correlation (all p > 0.28). In task-based conditions, both correlations are
positive, with IFBench approaching significance (r=0.55, p=0.052) and GPQA showing a moderate trend (r=0.35,
p=0.24). This divergence suggests that task-based robustness draws on some of the same capabilities measured by
general benchmarks, whereas fixed-output robustness is a distinct property unrelated to general intelligence.
The Llama anomaly.
Both Llama 3.1 and 3.3 70B perform substantially above the next best model in fixed-output
conditions. Even at N=50, Llama 3.3 maintains an IF rate of 0.95. This outlier performance persists across all condition
types and is not explained by model scale or intelligence (both are 70B parameters). Possible explanations include
Meta’s emphasis on system prompt adherence during post-training, chat template effects, or training data composition.
We consider this an open question warranting further investigation. Appendix G shows further comparison between the
two Llama versions.
3.3
Content alignment modulates the transition
Models are not neutral instruction-followers: the content of the instruction modulates how strongly they adhere to it
under induction pressure (Figure 4, Table 3). In fixed-output conditions, value-aligned instructions (“always respond
with ‘I love being helpful’ ”) yield a mean IF rate of 0.32, compared to 0.18 for value-misaligned instructions (“always
respond with ‘I hate being helpful’ ”), a gap of 14 percentage points. Factual alignment shows a similar pattern: factually
correct instructions (0.33) sustain higher IF than factually false ones (0.23), a 10-point gap.

3.4
Reasoning helps but does not resolve the conflict
In the two models tested, reasoning improves instruction robustness (Table 4). GPT-5.2-medium (reasoning) achieves
an average IF of 0.65 in fixed-output conditions and 0.92 in task-based conditions, compared to 0.17 and 0.57 for
GPT-5.2 (non-reasoning). Hermes-4 70B-reasoning (0.68 fixed-output) dramatically outperforms Hermes-4 70B (0.02).
In task-based conditions, GPT-5.2-medium transitions latest (N=43) among all models tested. Qualitative analysis of
Hermes-4 70B reasoning traces reveals cases of deliberation-output dissociation: the model sometimes reasons correctly
through the conflict and then outputs the pattern token regardless. See Appendix H for examples of reasoning traces.

Related work
Instruction-following evaluation.
IFEval, IFBench, InFoBench, and ComplexBench (Zhou et al., 2023; Pyatkin et al.,
2025; Qin et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024) and similar benchmarks measure whether models can follow explicit constraints
of varying complexity, but none introduce conflicting in-context evidence that pressures the model to abandon its
instruction. Our paradigm complements these benchmarks by isolating the induction-instruction axis. Compared to
existing work, our setup differs in three key respects: the instructions are intentionally simple (removing instruction
complexity as a confound), the challenge arises from the conversational context rather than the instruction itself, and we
systematically vary the strength of competing evidence by scaling the number of hardcoded turns.
Adversarial context and jailbreaking.
Jailbreaking attacks construct adversarial contexts that override safety training
by exploiting architectural and training vulnerabilities (Zou et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2023). Many-shot jailbreaking (Anil
et al., 2024) provides perhaps the closest analog to our setting: it uses hundreds of in-context examples of harmful
compliance to induce further compliance. This phenomenon follows predictable power laws where the probability of
succumbing to the established pattern increases with the number of contextual turns (N) . Other multi-turn strategies,
such as Crescendo attacks, achieve similar results through a “slow boil” approach (Russinovich et al., 2025) starting
from initially harmless queries. We abstract away the core mechanism of escalating in-context evidence of a behavior
into a controlled setting with a varied set of instructions of non-harmful parametric control over induction strength (N).
Self-knowledge and self-prediction.
Our self-prediction setting connects to a growing body of work on whether
language models have privileged access to their own internal states. Prior studies have shown that models finetuned to
predict their own behavior outperform cross-model baselines (Binder et al., 2024), though they still lag behind humans
in recognizing their own behavioral tendencies (Laine et al., 2024). More recently findings suggest that models may
display emergent introspective abilities, such as distinguishing their own text from artificial prefills (Lindsey, 2026), as
well as articulating implicit behavioral policies that were never explicitly described in training data (Betley et al., 2025;
Plunkett et al., 2025). We extend these works by testing prospective self-prediction in a zero-shot, dynamic setting, in
which models must predict their sensitivity to the instruction-induction conflict.
5
Discussion
Instruction-following as a fragile, trainable property.
As previously found by Pyatkin et al. (2025), instruction-
following robustness is not cleanly correlated with general capability. This also applies to our experimental setting, in
which scale and benchmark performance are poor predictors of resistance to induction pressure. The OLMo training-
stage comparison suggests where robustness might be installed: DPO (preference training) produces the largest gain,
compared to SFT and RLVR and in contrast to the finding from Pyatkin et al. (2025). Advanced instruction tuning, like
the one performed on Llama 3.3 vs. 3.1 (AI, 2024), provides further but more modest improvement. Robustness also
interacts with instruction content. Our results suggest that instruction-following is not a content-neutral capability. The
model’s value priors, instilled during post-training, modulate how strongly it adheres to instructions in our conflictual
setting. This has direct implications for alignment: models are more reliable when instructions happen to agree with
their trained preferences.
Output diversity as a source of robustness.
In our setting, task-based conditions lead to higher instruction-following
rates than fixed-output conditions, despite measuring the same underlying conflict. A targeted followup (Appendix I)
shows that the gap is not explained by task difficulty or semantic engagement, but is sensitive to output diversity, which
has a positive effect on robustness. Mechanistically, this gap reflects a competition between low-level token-copying
circuits (Yona et al., 2025) and high-level latent task representations (Todd et al., 2024). Single-token outputs are highly
susceptible to induction head toxicity, where specialized attention heads for verbatim matching dominate the output
logits and suppress other computations (Yin & Steinhardt, 2025). Free-text generation and the associated ”Function
vectors” might be fundamentally more robust to contextual pressure and repetition. Another hypothesis is that the
varied token distribution of natural language may further reinforce the stability of the assistant persona (Lu et al., 2026),
allowing the model to remain in “instruction-following mode” longer when its outputs resemble those of a helpful
assistant. This has a practical implication: robustness measures derived from fixed-output conditions may underestimate
a model’s resistance to induction in realistic deployments, where responses are naturally varied. That said, existing work
shows that models can still be pushed towards misalignment by malicious in-context examples (Afonin et al., 2026).
Partial introspective access.
Models can predict their own behavior with above-chance accuracy, extending Binder
et al. (2024) to a dynamic, context-dependent setting without any finetuning. Accuracy is highly heterogeneous across
models and particularly fragile in the transition zone where behavior is uncertain. Models systematically underestimate

their own resistance to induction: the grand mean predicted IF rate (14.3%) is far below the actual IF rate (26.8%). The
act of predicting may itself shift behavior, and the direction of the shift varies substantially across models. This has
implications for using self-reports as a reliable source of information about hypothetical model behavior.
Limitations.
Our work presents several limitations. We recognize that the experimental setting is controlled but
artificial. Real-world instruction override occurs through more naturalistic mechanisms; whether the same factors
modulate robustness in deployed settings is an open empirical question. The self-prediction results cover only a part of
the benchmarked settings (fixed-output conditions only), leaving open the question of whether self-prediction patterns
vary across condition type. Greedy decoding at T=0 captures modal behavior but not the full output distribution. The
T=1 comparison (Appendix K) partially addresses this, but shows large model-specific variance.

Conclusion
We introduced a controlled paradigm for studying instruction-induction conflict in language models, placing explicit
instructions in direct opposition to escalating in-context pattern pressure. Our results reveal several key findings. First,
every model tested is susceptible to induction override, but the transition point varies dramatically, with robustness
largely uncorrelated with standard capability benchmarks. Second, instruction-following is not content-neutral: models
resist induction longer when instructions align with their trained value priors, suggesting that post-training instills
content-dependent rather than uniform adherence. Third, output diversity is the primary factor distinguishing robust
from fragile conditions; follow-up experiments show that response variety, not semantic engagement with the input,
accounts for the higher robustness of task-based conditions, likely because single-token outputs present the same
surface structure as the hardcoded pattern and are therefore maximally susceptible to induction head copying. Fourth,
chain-of-thought reasoning substantially improves robustness but does not eliminate susceptibility, and in some cases
produces a dissociation between correct internal deliberation and incorrect final output. Finally, models have partial but
systematically biased self-knowledge: they predict their own behavior with 83.5% accuracy on average but consistently
underestimate their resistance to induction, with the predicted instruction-following rate (14.3%) well below the
actual rate (26.8%). These findings suggest that the same porosity to context that enables in-context learning and
flexible adaptation remains a liability for instruction-following under distributional pressure, especially under conditions
that lead the model away from its post-trained assistant persona. Thus the challenge of inoculating models against
context-driven behavioral updating, while preserving their ability to track and adapt to conversational content, remains
an open problem.
Ethics statement
This work studies failure modes of instruction-following in LLMs. The misaligned conditions (e.g., instructing models
to output false statements) are used solely as experimental probes and are not intended to produce harmful outputs. All
model evaluations used publicly available APIs or open-weight models. No human subjects were involved.

Full condition specifications
All conditions share the same conversational structure: a system prompt (“You are a helpful assistant.”), a user message
combining an instruction with an initial factual question, N hardcoded assistant turns outputting the pattern response P,
and a final free-generation question. The instruction template is: “I want you to [target description] as your
answer to each one of my questions in this conversation.”
We distinguish two families of conditions: fixed-output conditions, where P and T are fixed tokens and the instruction
is “always output X” regardless of the question, and task-based conditions, where P and T are categories of behavior
requiring full-response generation and specialized scorers. In fixed-output conditions the model’s response has no
necessary relationship to the question content; in task-based conditions the model must perform a meaningful operation
on the input (translate, reformat, adopt a persona).

Follow-up conditions
Under the initial 13 conditions we evaluated, models resist induction pressure more in task-based conditions than
fixed-output conditions. Two properties distinguish task-based from fixed-output conditions: (1) the model must
engage with the question content to produce a well-formed response, and (2) the responses themselves are long and
varied across turns. Either property, or both jointly, could explain the increased robustness. We design two follow-up
conditions to disentangle these factors.
I.1
Random facts condition
This tests whether response naturalness and diversity, without question engagement, confers instruction resistance. The
model is instructed to produce 1/3 sentences on a fixed topic (either geography or animals), ignoring the user’s question
entirely. Hardcoded turns contain pre-generated passages on the pattern topic. The model’s output is scored by an LLM
judge that classifies whether the response is about the target topic or the pattern topic.

I.2
Classification condition
The classification condition tests the converse: whether question engagement alone, with single-token output, confers
instruction resistance. The model must read each question, classify it into one of two categories, and respond with only
that category word. This requires genuine engagement with the question content, but the output format is as constrained
as a fixed-output condition. The hardcoded pattern is “economics”—a plausible category label that no question in the
factual bank actually falls under, ensuring clean separation between correct classification and pattern-following.
If the classification condition resists induction like task-based conditions despite single-token output, then question
engagement—not response diversity—is the key factor. Together with the random-facts condition, the 2 × 2 design
(engagement × diversity) isolates each factor’s contribution.

Interpretation.
The 2×2 design isolates the responsible factor: response diversity without question engagement
(random-facts) replicates the robustness of task-based conditions, while question engagement without diversity (classify)
does not. Output diversity, not semantic engagement with the input, appears to be the primary mechanism by which
task-based conditions resist induction pressure. A plausible explanation is that single-token outputs present the same
surface structure as the hardcoded pattern turns, making the induction signal maximally salient; diverse multi-sentence
outputs break this structural regularity and reduce the effective strength of the pattern.
Llama 3.3 70B is a partial exception: its near-ceiling instruction-following on both classify and random-facts leaves
little room to detect differential effects. The contrast is clearest in Gemma-3 27B and GPT-5.2, which show the full
range.
