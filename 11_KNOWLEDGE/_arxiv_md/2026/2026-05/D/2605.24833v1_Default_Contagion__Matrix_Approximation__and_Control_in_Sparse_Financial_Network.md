Abstract
We study systemic default contagion in sparse financial networks and develop a framework for
deciding when aggregate exposure matrices are reliable and when node-level network information
changes tail risk and control design. The first contribution is a multi-population McKean–Vlasov
foundation for distance-to-default dynamics with common noise, bounded state-dependent killing,
loss feedback, sparse weighted exposures, and regulatory intervention, including quantitative
convergence, propagation of chaos, stability in contagion matrices, controlled well-posedness, a
two-population HJB characterization, and a steep-killing bridge to absorbing-boundary contagion.
The second contribution is a set of computable matrix-approximation diagnostics: finite-grid
bounds driven by row-exposure dispersion and square-edge spread, constructive tail-loss gaps for
networks sharing the same aggregate matrix, and a spectral-radius criterion for local cascade
onset. The third contribution is an information-value theory for control, showing that node-level
graph pressure has strictly positive value when within-type pressure variation interacts with
nonsaturated marginal killing reduction. Matched sparse-graph and matrix experiments, common-
noise tests, HJB feedback diagnostics, fixed-budget control comparisons, and EBA/Pillar 3-
calibrated synthetic networks validate the framework. The main conclusion is that finite-type
matrices are effective in regular-mixing regimes, whereas concentrated sparse exposures generate
tail-risk and intervention effects that require local-pressure diagnostics and network-aware control.
Keywords. systemic risk, financial networks, McKean–Vlasov equations, default contagion, sparse
graphs, common noise, optimal control, matrix approximation

Introduction

structure. The clearing-network model of Eisenberg and Noe [1] provides the standard limited-
liability benchmark, while Allen and Gale [2], Gai and Kapadia [3], Acemoglu, Ozdaglar, and
Tahbaz-Salehi [4], Elliott, Golub, and Jackson [5], and Glasserman and Young [6] show that
diversification, connectivity, and shock size have non-monotone implications for cascade risk. A
parallel large-population literature models default contagion through interacting particles and
McKean–Vlasov equations. Hambly, Ledger, and Søjmark [7], Hambly and Søjmark [8], and Hambly

The practical tension is that regulatory stress tests often need aggregate and interpretable objects,
while real interbank networks remain sparse, heterogeneous, and locally concentrated. A finite-type
contagion matrix is attractive because it summarizes cross-type exposures, supports spectral and
row-exposure diagnostics, and leads to tractable control rules. Sparse financial networks, however,
can transmit losses through hub nodes, core–periphery concentration, and local high-pressure
neighborhoods that disappear under type averaging. This paper studies the boundary between
these two representations by constructing a sparse graph contagion system and an aggregate matrix
approximation from the same graph. The two systems share node types, initial states, kill intensities,
loss feedback, and aggregate exposure sources, so their difference isolates the information loss caused
by aggregating local network structure.
This boundary also matters for systemic-risk measurement and policy design. Market-based
measures such as systemic expected shortfall and CoVaR quantify institution-level contributions
to aggregate distress [10, 11]; clearing and network-contagion models explain how balance-sheet
links propagate distress once a shock has entered the system. Our focus is the interface between
these two viewpoints: we ask when a regulator can work with a low-dimensional exposure matrix,
and when tail-risk measurement requires additional local graph information. This framing leads
to a concrete information question. If two networks have the same type-level matrix but different
within-block concentration, then any difference in tail loss, cascade probability, or intervention cost
is attributable to information discarded by the matrix projection.
The paper therefore combines a regularized McKean–Vlasov limit with finite-network diagnostics.
We show that the matrix–network boundary is theoretically characterizable through computable
graph diagnostics and practically consequential for regulatory control design. The regularization
through bounded state-dependent killing keeps the default mechanism close to absorbing-boundary
contagion while leaving the controlled system compatible with dynamic programming. The sparse-
network layer is finite and constructive: it compares matched graph and matrix systems under
identical shocks and budgets, so the empirical design tests the same approximation boundary
described by the theory.

The first contribution is a multi-population McKean–Vlasov foundation
for regularized default contagion with common noise, loss feedback, and control. We prove the
large-system limit, a conditional O(N−1/2

2
Related Literature
2.1
Financial networks and default cascades
We now expand on the financial-network, interacting-particle, and graph-limit literatures introduced
above, with emphasis on the technical distinction between aggregate exposure matrices and local
sparse-network information.
Network models of systemic risk begin from the observation that a bank’s default losses depend
on the payment, funding, liquidity, and asset-sale behavior of other institutions. The clearing-vector
framework of Eisenberg and Noe [1] and the default-cost extension of Rogers and Veraart [12] provide
benchmark models for mutual-liability networks. Liquidity and fire-sale channels are developed
by Cifuentes, Ferrucci, and Shin [13], Gai, Haldane, and Kapadia [14], Caballero and Simsek [15],
and May and Arinaminpathy [16]. Empirical and stylized interbank-network studies by Boss et
al. [17], Nier et al. [18], Cont, Moussa, and Santos [19], Battiston et al. [20], Glasserman and
Young [21], Caccioli et al. [22], Capponi, Chen, and Yao [23], and Gandy and Veraart [24] show that
degree distributions, exposure concentration, centrality, and balance-sheet fragility jointly determine
cascade paths. Empirical high-dimensional bank-network estimation, such as Demirer, Diebold, Liu,
and Yilmaz [25], further reinforces the role of cross-bank connectedness in crisis periods.
The robust-yet-fragile mechanism is especially important for this paper. Allen and Gale [2], Gai
and Kapadia [3], Acemoglu, Ozdaglar, and Tahbaz-Salehi [4], Elliott, Golub, and Jackson [5], Amini,
Cont, and Minca [26], Gleeson [27], Hurd, Gleeson, and Melnik [28], and Glasserman and Young [6]
all point to a common lesson: aggregate connectivity can stabilize small shocks while amplifying
losses once local cascades start. Our sparse-graph experiments follow this line, but the object of
comparison is deliberately narrower. We compare a sparse graph system with the finite-type matrix
obtained from the same graph, so the measured gap is the price of type aggregation rather than a
difference across unrelated network specifications.
2.2
Mean-field contagion, McKean–Vlasov models, and control
Mean-field contagion models describe the dynamic state of a large financial system through empirical
measures and loss processes; the broader systemic-risk modeling background is surveyed in Fouque
and Langsam [29]. Diffusion-based mechanisms for systemic risk and interbank stability are analyzed
in Fouque and Ichiba [30] and Garnier, Papanicolaou, and Yang [31]. Hambly, Ledger, and Søjmark [7]

finite-dimensional control diagnostics.
The control side draws on McKean–Vlasov control, infinite-dimensional HJB theory, numerical
methods for McKean–Vlasov equations, and network mean-field methods. Huang, Malhamé, and

and Chassagneux, Crisan, and Delarue [45] give numerical methods for McKean–Vlasov FBSDEs.
Deep-BSDE methods such as Han, Jentzen, and E [46] provide an alternative high-dimensional PDE
benchmark, but our finite-dimensional HJB loop is used as a transparent diagnostic rather than a
black-box solver. Graph-limit and sparse-network perspectives enter through Lovász [47], Benjamini
and Schramm [48], Borgs et al. [49], Delattre, Giacomin, and Luçon [50], Oliveira and Reis [51],

central question: when is a finite-type matrix a valid low-dimensional projection of sparse financial
contagion, and when does node-level pressure information become necessary for tail-risk control?

Numerical design
The finite-type experiments use four classes of institutions: small banks, regional banks, major banks,
and systemically important banks. The moderate-stress specification sets the baseline kill-intensity
multiplier to 2.5 and shifts the initial state downward by −0.25. The matrix scalings are 45, 48, 50,
the particle number is 10000, the number of time steps is 500, and the number of replications is
200. This specification is used to display near-critical loss growth and the risk–cost frontier in the
matrix system. The convergence validation experiment changes the particle number under the same
moderate-stress structure and estimates the reference loss and reference empirical distribution using
a large-sample system.
The sparse graph experiments examine three network classes. The stochastic block network
serves as the regular-mixing benchmark, with edge probabilities and edge weights mainly determined
by type pairs. The core–periphery network increases the connection strength and spillover exposure
of the core type, and is used to represent locally concentrated risk around systemically important
nodes. The heavy-tailed network introduces source-node fitness and hub exposure multipliers, and
is used to represent cascade risk driven by highly connected nodes. Each graph is aggregated into a
15


## Page 16


matrix approximation through (11), and the graph system and matrix system are compared under
the same default mechanism.
Sparse graph exposure intensity is amplified by a common multiplier x, with scan range
x = 1, . . . , 30. The representative control experiments choose three points: the heavy-tailed network
at x = 12, the core–periphery network at x = 15, and the stochastic block network at x = 26. At
these points, the true graph systems already exhibit significant cascades, while the corresponding
aggregate matrix approximations have not entered the cascade region at the same time.
The synthetic numerical experiments use unified inputs and outputs. Inputs include type shares
πk, the initial safety-margin distribution, drift and volatility parameters, kill-intensity parameters,
the contagion matrix or sparse adjacency matrix, common-noise intensity, control rules and their
budgets or thresholds, the time grid, the particle number, and the number of Monte Carlo replications.

tail loss, cascade probability, control cost, risk reduction per unit cost, and the mean and tail
gaps between the graph system and the matrix system. This organization allows different network
families, control rules, and parameter scans to be compared under the same set of risk–cost metrics,
rather than treating each experiment as an independent simulation case.

Discussion and conclusion
The results identify a clear effective range for finite-type matrix approximation. In low-pressure or
regular-mixing regions, the aggregate contagion matrix gives a useful stress-testing object because
it preserves the main type-level exposure channels and supports interpretable spectral and row-
exposure diagnostics. As local exposure concentration rises, the same matrix can preserve aggregate
exposure while missing tail paths through hubs, core nodes, or high-pressure neighborhoods. The
diagnostic terms in Theorem 3, the tail-gap construction in Theorem 4, and the spectral threshold
in Theorem 5 describe this boundary from complementary angles.
The control experiments translate the approximation boundary into regulatory design. Type-
uniform control is a strong benchmark when the intervention budget is large and broad suppression
is feasible. Graph-pressure threshold control uses node-level local pressure and therefore performs
best when the budget is limited and the cascade mechanism is concentrated in a small set of
high-pressure neighborhoods. The finite-dimensional K = 2 HJB loop provides a marginal-value
benchmark: it converts the Hamiltonian structure into a feedback rule that reduces tail loss and
cascade probability at comparable cost. The forward policy evaluation applies the stored feedback
in a large particle system and serves as the primary validation metric for the computed control.
The HJB diagnostics also clarify the meaning of the Richardson-style extrapolation. A negative
extrapolated value such as V∞= −0.029 identifies a finite-dimensional projection effect: the loss-
coordinate grid smears the jump discontinuity created when killing transfers alive mass into the
cemetery state and advances the loss coordinates near the contagion threshold. The representative-
coordinate projection also suppresses within-type distributional heterogeneity that the full measure-
valued HJB would retain. The semi-Lagrangian loss-jump scheme reduces the instability of central
differences, and the remaining loss-grid sensitivity is reported through the grid diagnostics. The
value-function grid tests discretization sensitivity, while the forward controlled particle system tests
realized risk reduction by the computed feedback.

The external-data layer gives calibrated synthetic evidence under public regulatory constraints.
EBA Transparency Exercise data and Pillar 3/EDAP disclosure packages constrain size, jurisdictional
distribution, concentration, and disclosure coverage, while the confidential bilateral exposure matrix
remains unobserved. The calibrated core–periphery networks use these public constraints to test
robustness across admissible synthetic edge realizations. The multi-instance results show that the
matrix-approximation gap and the value of local pressure information are stable across network
draws satisfying the same external constraints.
Open problems
Four directions follow naturally from the present framework: sparse-graph
McKean–Vlasov limits with killing jumps, common noise, and local weak convergence; scalable
feedback classes based on neighborhood distributions or graph neural networks; comparison theory
for degenerate, hard-boundary, or unbounded-intensity versions beyond the bounded killed HJB
treated here; and richer calibration pipelines using supervisory bilateral data, when available, to
separate model error from disclosure-driven network uncertainty. The central conclusion is that
finite-type matrices are informative stress-testing tools in regular regimes, while heterogeneous
sparse networks require local diagnostics for tail-risk measurement and intervention.

This appendix records two key discretization choices used in the numerical experiments in the

First, the two-population HJB in Theorem 6 is projected onto the representative
state (x1, x2, L1, L2) and solved by backward induction from T to 0. The first-order terms in the
x directions use central differences. The two pure second-order diffusion terms are handled by
semi-implicit tridiagonal steps, and the common-noise cross second-order term σ2

explicitly. The control is searched on a discrete grid over the compact set [0, ¯a]2. The killing term

and the corresponding term for the second type. This preserves the direction in which losses can
only increase and reduces ill-conditioned extrapolation caused by central differences or coarse-

grid resolution. The rigorous value-function characterization is still given by the measure-valued
HJB in Theorem 6 and Appendix SM4.
Second, the K = 2 numerical experiments must lie in a nondegenerate near-critical region: if
pressure is too low, the marginal control benefit in the Hamiltonian is zero; if pressure is too high,
all rules enter the saturated-control region. We scan the kill-intensity multiplier, the terminal-loss
weight multiplier, and the running-cost multiplier on a small grid, and choose a parameter region that
satisfies three conditions at once: the uncontrolled system has significant cascade risk, HJB feedback
gives nonzero control, and the HJB point and rule-based controls have comparable cost magnitudes.
Figure 28 and Table 3 give the numerical basis for this calibration. The final calibration used in
the main text is kill-intensity multiplier 25, terminal-loss weight multiplier 6, and running-cost
multiplier 0.05.
