Abstract
Teams frequently compete on multiple fronts: political parties contest districts for majority control,
contractors field specialized units to win procurement contracts, and squads play match by match for
titles. Although the prize accrues collectively to the winning team, individual incentives depend on how
it is divided internally. We study a majoritarian team contest in which two rival managers simultaneously
divide their teams’ prizes among heterogeneous members. The contest admits a unique pure-strategy
equilibrium: both managers choose identical relative allocations—regardless of heterogeneity in winning
values or player costs—with each battle’s share proportional to its discriminatory power, symmetry,
and pivotality.
JEL Classification Numbers: C61, C72, D72, D74.
Keywords: Multi-battle Contest, Team Contest, Prize Allocation, Nash equilibrium.

Introduction
Many large-scale competitions are waged by rival teams whose members meet head-to-head on multiple
fronts. In U.S. legislative elections, Democratic and Republican candidates contest seats district by district,
and the party securing a majority gains control of the chamber, along with the agenda-setting power
that entails. In defense procurement, prime contractors such as Boeing, Lockheed Martin, and Northrop
Grumman assemble specialized engineering groups for subsystems like avionics, propulsion, and stealth;
the agency then evaluates competing prototypes component by component before awarding the contract.
In team sports, the Ryder Cup, Davis Cup, and Sudirman Cup pit national squads against each other through
pairwise matches, with the title going to the side that wins a majority. These are all instances of multi-battle
team contests: two teams field members across multiple battles, each participant enters one battle and bears
his own effort cost, and team victory is determined by aggregate performance.
A defining feature of these competitions is that the winning team earns a collective prize whose internal divi-
sion is a strategic choice. A victorious party must apportion the spoils of majority control—committee chairs,
earmarks, legislative priorities—among candidates of differing electoral strength facing districts of varying
competitiveness. A winning contractor must distribute net benefits across engineering groups that differ in ex-
pertise and task difficulty. A championship team’s manager must reward players of varying ability who faced
opponents of different calibers. These allocations are inherently strategic: each manager’s optimal scheme de-
pends on the rival’s, since both reward structures jointly shape effort incentives and the stakes in every battle.
Despite its practical importance, within-team prize allocation remains largely unexplored, raising a first-
order strategic question: How should a manager divide the team prize among her players to maximize the
team’s winning probability, given that her rival faces the same problem? Should rewards go to the strongest
players, who are most likely to win? To the weakest, who need the sharpest incentives?1 Or be spread
evenly, since all share in team success? We show that none of these principles is generally correct. Instead,
a battle receives a large share only when it is responsive to incentives—e.g., the two players are evenly
matched and the winner-selection mechanism is discriminatory—and likely to determine majority control.
Model and challenges. We endogenize within-team prize allocation by modeling two team managers who
simultaneously promise to partition the prize among heterogeneous teammates conditional on team victory,
each seeking to maximize the probability that her team wins a majority of pairwise battles. Consistent with

all leading examples, simultaneity is natural because each manager’s internal reward scheme is proprietary

technology may vary across battles, and the two teams’ value of the team prize (i.e., budget) need not be equal.
The strategic problem has two layers. In the first stage, managers design internal incentive schemes, antic-
ipating how these choices shape effort provision. In the second stage, matched players from opposing teams
compete in pairwise battles, with the team winning a majority claiming the prize. This structure generates
two compounding difficulties for the managers’ problem. First, each player responds strategically not only to
his direct opponent but also to players in other battles, whose strategies determine how much his own battle
matters for team victory. Second, the manager’s objective is well-behaved at the battle level—each winning
probability is concave in own prize—but majority aggregation is itself nonlinear and not quasiconcave in
the vector of battle-winning probabilities, while the budget constraint couples all battles. The favorable
battle-level structure therefore does not by itself deliver concavity of the manager’s allocation problem.
Main results. We characterize the unique pure-strategy subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium in closed form.
Both managers choose identical budget shares across battles, so that the ratio of the two teams’ prizes in
any given battle equals the ratio of their total budgets. This proportional-prize property holds regardless
of how players’ abilities or battle technologies differ across fronts, and it strengthens to exact equality
of prizes when the two teams value team victory equally. The common share assigned to each battle is
proportional to its salience, defined as the product of three terms: (i) the discriminatory power of the battle’s
Tullock winner-selection mechanism; (ii) the symmetry of the battle, measured by the product of the two
teams’ winning probabilities; and (iii) the pivotality of the battle, the probability that the remaining battles
split evenly so that this one is decisive. Consequently, little is spent on near-predetermined battles, whose
outcomes barely respond to incentives, or on battles unlikely to affect majority control however closely
fought; the largest rewards flow to intermediate fronts that are simultaneously contestable and consequential.
This salience principle resonates with a long-standing theme in political economy. Str¨omberg (2008)
shows that presidential candidates target states in proportion to pivotality and swing-voter density; Dixit
and Londregan (1996) model two parties allocating transfers by group responsiveness; and Ma, McLaren
and Chalak (2026) document a swing-state bias in U.S. tariffs. In these analyses, only the parties are
strategic: voters respond mechanically through exogenous functions such as a Tullock contest (Brams

and Davis, 1974), or are abstracted away entirely so that each state is itself a contest (Kujala, 2020). The
resulting resource-to-outcome mapping is a primitive, a formulation well suited to presidential elections. We
instead introduce strategic candidates into each battlefield, making contestability endogenous to equilibrium
effort—a formulation better suited to legislative elections. This isolates the discriminatory power of the
winner-selection technology as a determinant of allocation, alongside pivotality and symmetry. Despite
the added complexity, rival managers converge on identical relative shares.
The equilibrium also delivers sharp comparative statics: strengthening one’s team, either by lowering
members’ costs or by raising the team prize, always benefits that team even as allocations adjust across
all battles; and in well-matched battles, the allocation depends solely on the contest technology, with more
discriminating battles commanding larger shares.
A notable robustness property is that the equilibrium is invariant to the temporal structure of the contest:
whether battles are played simultaneously, sequentially, or in any partial order, the equilibrium allocation
is unchanged. The intuition is simple. In any nontrivial battle, each player’s incentive equals the assigned
prize times the increase in team-winning probability from his own victory, and since one team’s win is the
other team’s loss, this latter term is identical for both competing players. Relative incentives are therefore
shaped entirely by relative prizes and relative costs, neither of which depends on the order of play. In
trivial battles—where one team has already secured a majority—resetting the battle outcome leaves overall
winning probabilities untouched, so each battle can be treated as an independent lottery.
Technical contribution. Establishing equilibrium existence is the central technical challenge. The manager’s
objective is neither concave nor quasiconcave in the vector of battle-winning probabilities, so standard
composition arguments through battle-level concavity do not apply. We instead show that, given the rival’s
strategy, each manager’s team-winning probability is log-concave in her own allocation. The proof reduces
log-concavity to the positive semidefiniteness of a structured matrix built from the conditional covariance
of battle outcomes given team victory, which we then verify by decomposing the matrix coefficientwise
in a polynomial expansion and certifying each coefficient block as positive semidefinite. Log-concavity
elevates the closed-form solution of the first-order conditions to a global best response, yielding existence
and uniqueness for any odd number of battles.

Our paper contributes to the broad literature on prize design in contests, which dates back to Lazear and
Rosen (1981). More recently, Fang, Noe and Strack (2020) examine how competitive pressure shapes optimal
prize design, while Lemus and Marshall (2025) show that contingent prizes can sharpen contestants’ incen-
tives and improve contest outcomes. A central theme in this literature is that heterogeneity among contestants
plays a crucial role in incentive design. Moldovanu and Sela (2001) characterize optimal prize structures
in contests with heterogeneous contestants, and Olszewski and Siegel (2016) develop a tractable framework
for analyzing contests with many heterogeneous players competing for many heterogeneous prizes. Our
paper fully incorporates contestant heterogeneity and studies competing prize design between two managers.
A large literature studies multi-battle contests between individual players, including strategic momentum
and discouragement effects in sequential battles (Gelder, 2014; Harris and Vickers, 1987; Klumpp and
Polborn, 2006; Konrad and Kovenock, 2009) and prize design in such settings (Clark and Nilssen, 2020; Feng
and Lu, 2018). Our managers’ problem—dividing a budget across fronts—is reminiscent of Colonel Blotto
games (Kovenock and Roberson, 2021; Roberson, 2006). In Blotto games, however, two parties simultane-
ously distribute forces across battles, each carrying its own prize, and the winner of each battle is determined
by the resources allocated by the two sides. In our setting, team members are themselves strategic players,
battle outcomes jointly determine the team winner through majority aggregation, and only the winning team
splits the prize. Consequently, each player must also respond strategically to players in other battles.
A parallel literature examines how prize-sharing rules affect effort in group contests. Nitzan and Ueda
(2011, 2014) study within-group prize sharing in single-battle contests among multiple groups, where the
winner is determined by a lottery contest based on the groups’ total output. Kobayashi (2024); Kobayashi,

3
Equilibrium Characterizations
We establish the existence and uniqueness of a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium in this section. By
Lemma 3, we work on int(∆A)×int(∆B) throughout. The argument proceeds in three steps. First, the
pivotality decomposition together with the homogeneity of degree zero of the contest success function forces
the two teams to allocate their budgets in identical proportions across battles—a proportional-prize property.
Second, we exhibit a candidate closed-form profile (ˆvA,ˆvB) and verify that it is the unique solution to
the first-order conditions (Theorem 1). Third, we prove that each team’s contest-winning probability is
log-concave in its own strategy (Theorem 2). The key log-concavity step is established for arbitrary odd
numbers of battles by a coefficientwise positive-semidefinite certificate for the covariance-domination
matrix; log-concavity then ensures that any interior critical point is a global maximum.

Each manager allocates budget across battles in proportion to their salience, directing larger shares to battles

Remark 1 The battle salience St is a measure of the “weight” of a battle. Feng, Jiao, Kuang and Lu (2024)
propose an alternative notion of battle weight that is increasing in asymmetry—in sharp contrast with ours,
where more balanced battles receive greater weight. The difference stems from distinct institutional settings.
In their framework, a centralized contest designer chooses a potentially biased prize allocation rule. Their

optimal design is a majority-score rule with a headstart: all battles are assigned team-invariant scores, the
weaker team receives an initial headstart score, and the team accumulating the higher total score from its

4.4
Implications and Caveats
The equilibrium formula yields an allocation principle with direct organizational content: a battle com-
mands a large prize share when its discriminatory power, symmetry, and pivotality are simultaneously
high. A manager should not reward a battle merely because it is close—closeness matters only if the battle
can swing the team outcome—nor merely because it is pivotal, since pivotality matters only if additional
incentives can change the battle outcome.
A second implication follows from the proportional-prize property. Rival organizations facing the same
battles and the same majority objective allocate internal rewards in identical relative proportions, even
when their absolute budgets differ and their team members have different costs: primitive asymmetries
are absorbed by the equilibrium winning probabilities, leaving a common relative allocation rule.
Our analysis further delivers two empirical predictions. First, strengthening any single player raises that
team’s overall winning probability, despite the induced reallocation of rewards across battles. Second, the
equilibrium allocation and winning probabilities are invariant to timing—whether battles are simultaneous,
sequential, or partially sequential.
Legislative elections.
Legislative elections provide a natural interpretation of the theory. A party wins
the collective prize of majority control only by winning enough district- or state-level races. Party leaders
and campaign committees then face an internal allocation problem: where should campaign funds, strategic
support, leadership attention, and post-victory rewards be directed? The salience formula implies that the
largest rewards should flow to races that are both competitive and consequential for control of the chamber.
Safe seats have low salience because their outcomes are largely insensitive to additional incentives; the same
21


## Page 22


is true of hopeless races. Competitive races that do not affect majority control also have limited salience.
The highest-salience races are the battlegrounds that can plausibly decide the majority.
This interpretation also highlights the proportional-prize property. In close election cycles, both parties
typically target similar sets of battleground races, despite differing in incumbency advantages, fundraising
bases, and candidate quality. The model attributes this convergence to a common pivotality structure: the
parties need not have equal resources, but their relative attention across races should align after scaling by
total budgets. Campaign spending, independent expenditures, leadership visits, coordinated support, and
committee assignments are plausible empirical proxies. Preelection campaign receipts are especially attractive:
as Thomsen (2023) emphasizes, fundraising is the most widely used preelection indicator of candidate
viability and is built into prominent forecasting models such as FiveThirtyEight and the Cook Political Report.
The legislative application should be read with two caveats. First, post-election rewards such as committee
assignments, legislative priorities, and leadership access are not literal binding contracts. Their credibility
arises from repeated interaction, party discipline, and the value of future cooperation. Second, some rewards
are investments in future electoral performance rather than pure payments for past effort. The model is
best viewed as isolating one force inside a richer political organization: when majority control is valuable,
party leaders have a reason to direct scarce rewards toward races that are both contestable and pivotal.
Procurement and consortium competition.
The same logic applies to procurement contests in which
prime contractors assemble teams of specialized subcontractors. A prime contractor competes for a single
award, but the probability of winning depends on performance across multiple dimensions—for example,
propulsion, avionics, software, integration, reliability, and cost. The prime’s workshare promises or revenue-
sharing arrangements play the role of within-team prizes. The model suggests that workshare tends to be
concentrated in subsystems where performance is measurable, the rival teams are closely matched, and
the subsystem is likely to determine the award. Subsystems that are either nearly predetermined or unlikely
to affect the final evaluation receive smaller incentive shares in equilibrium.
This application is necessarily approximate because procurement awards usually involve weighted scoring,
technical thresholds, and holistic “best value” determinations rather than strict majority rule. Subsystems
may also interact, especially when systems integration is important. Nevertheless, procurement illustrates the
broader organizational insight: when a collective prize must be generated through decentralized specialized
effort, the internal division of the prize tracks the salience of each task.

Concluding Remarks
This paper studies how organizations divide the spoils of team competition. In a majoritarian contest
with pairwise battles, two rival managers choose internal prize allocations before their players exert effort.
Although the strategic problem is high-dimensional and nonlinear, the unique equilibrium has a simple form:
both managers allocate prizes according to the same salience index. A battle is salient when it is discrim-
inatory in selecting the winner, closely contested, and likely to be pivotal for majority control. The resulting
proportional-prize property implies that rival organizations choose identical relative reward schemes across
battles, even when their budgets, player costs, and battle technologies differ. The central technical challenge is
equilibrium existence: we show that each manager’s team-winning probability is log-concave in her own allo-
cation given her opponent’s strategy. We establish this log-concavity for every odd number of battles via a co-
efficientwise positive-semidefinite decomposition, which completes the global equilibrium characterization.
Several questions remain open. First, the model takes the player-to-battle matching as exogenous; an
interesting extension would let managers assign players to battles before allocating prizes, leading to a joint
problem in which matching may substitute for or complement reward design. Second, history-dependent
prize promises—in which managers observe interim outcomes and adjust subsequent splits before later
battles are played—may overturn the proportional-prize feature; the trade-off between commitment and
contingent incentives is a natural follow-up. Third, the managers’ objective in our model is contest-winning
probability. Under welfare, participation, or aggregate-effort objectives, the optimal allocation may diverge
from the salience rule. Fourth, richer budget-design questions arise if an organizer or sponsor can choose
the teams’ total budgets before managers allocate them internally. We leave these directions for future work.
