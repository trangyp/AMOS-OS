Abstract
Identifying possible clusters in datasets and estimating their over-
all modularity are central tasks in pattern recognition. In the present
work, concepts and methodologies are described for performing these
tasks while considering only the density of mergings obtained from
hierarchical representations (dendrograms) of data inter-relationship
along a scale variable. More specifically, the mergings of subclusters
along the scale variable are obtained, yielding a respective merging
density function. After this function is balanced along the scale vari-
able, peak detection is applied in order to estimate, within a specified
resolution, the respective hierarchical clusters and their overall mod-
ularity. The potential of the reported approach is illustrated for some
types of data and dendrograms, and the possibility of recursive cluster
detection is also addressed.

Finding groups of elements in data sets characterized by respective fea-
tures constitutes one of the main tasks in pattern recognition and machine

learning. This task can be performed supervised or unsupervised (e.g. [1, 2]),
the latter type being characterized by a lack of preliminary information about
the possible presence of groups.
Among the approaches that have been described for unsupervised pattern
recognition, or clustering (e.g. [3, 4, 5]), hierarchical methods have allowed
the data elements to be inter-related along a scale parameter s, which can
express the distance or similarity between the data features of pairs of data
elements. Estimation of the hierarchical relationship between these elements
can be obtained by starting with the individual data elements as leaves and
progressing through mergings into subgroups until a single cluster is obtained.
The so-obtained hierarchical relationships are often represented in terms of
dendrograms (e.g. [6, 1, 2, 7]), which provide an indication of the of the rela-
tionship between the original data elements for a range of interconnectivity
scales.
For simplicity’s sake, the dendrograms considered in this work are as-
sumed to have a binary tree topology. It should also be observed that be-
cause dendrograms typically do not preserve all the information available in
the original data features, they do not establish an invertible mapping with
the original data, so that the data features cannot be recovered from a den-
drogram. Therefore, a same dendrogram can be obtained from two or more
distinct data sets.
Even though dendrograms do not directly incorporate the original data
features, they provide comprehensive information about the hierarchical re-
lationship between the original data elements to the point that it becomes
interesting to try to estimate the presence of possible clusters and their mod-
ularity directly from dendrograms. This possibility is illustrated in Figure 1,
which illustrates a non-modular (a) and a modular (c) data sets as well as
the respective dendrograms (b) and (d).
The hierarchical modularity of the dataset in (c) is reflected in the pres-
ence of relatively extensive and well-defined branches that occur at similar
values of the scale variable s in the dendrogram shown in (d). At the same
time, a more uniform distribution of branches can be observed in the non-
modular dendrogram in (b).
The present work addresses the possibility of, given a data set, estimat-
ing its possible hierarchical cluster structure and overall modularity directly
from the information provided by the respective dendrogram. The reported
approach is based on two main guidelines: (a) the consideration of the merg-
ing density function characterizing the structure of given dendrogram along
successive scales (s); and (b) the application of a balancing approach in which
branches at higher hierarchies are given larger weights.
Once a balanced merging density function has been obtained from a den-
drogram within a specified resolution, its valleys are detected, allowing the
main branching levels to be identified. An estimation of the data modularity
can then be obtained from these results. By following the topology of the
dendrogram branches, it becomes also possible to identify potential clusters
in the dataset. The potential of these approaches is illustrated for some types
of dendrograms and data sets.

Hierarchical Agglomerative Approaches
Hierarchical agglomerative methods (e.g. [1, 2]) progressively merge data
elements and sub-clusters according to monotonically increasing values of
a scale parameters s, which may correspond to some measurement of the
distance or dissimilarity between the sub-clusters. Each subsequent merging,
which takes place at a specific value of s, defines a respective branch in
the associated dendrogram. Mergings can be performed considering several
criteria that include, but are not limited to, single, complete, and average
linkage (e.g. [1, 2]). The latter approach, which is considered in the present
work, involves taking the average of the distance of similarity measurements
among every pair of elements between two given subclusters.
Figure 2 illustrates a simple distribution of 12 points in (a), considered as
data elements, and the respectively obtained dendrograms considering single
in (b), complete in (c), and average in (d) linkage criteria.
Figure 2: A simple dataset (a) and dendrograms obtained by using three linkage criteria.
As it can be verified from Figure 2, the choice of linkage criterion can
lead to markedly different dendrograms. The choice of the linkage criterion,
which is not addressed further in the present work, depends on each case,
type of data, and eventual presence of noise, among other factors.
The
separation (and therefore modularity) of the data elements encompassed by
a respective branch has often been related to the length of its stem, among
other possibilities (e.g. [8]).
The present work concentrates on cluster detection and modularity esti-
mation directly from given dendrograms, not specifically addressing how they
have been obtained. In the cases described herein in which dendrograms are
obtained from data sets, the average linkage criterion has been adopted.

Cluster Estimation
Given a dendrogram and its respective balanced merging density function
p(s), it is interesting to try to estimate clusters eventually present in the
original data set from which the dendrogram was obtained. A possible way
to do so is described in this section.
First, maximum peaks along p(s) tend to be associated with the main
branching levels of a dendrogram. Figure 5(a) shows this type of levels iden-
tified for the function p(s) associated with the dendrogram in Figure 5(b).
Henceforth, the minimum peaks, together with the two limits of the
scale variable s, are called the delimiting levels of a dendrogram. The se-
quence of delimiting levels defines n subsequent intervals, Ik = [vk, vk+1],
k = 1, 2, . . . , n, with v0 = 0 and vn = 1. For example, the intervals identi-
fied for the dendrogram in Figure 5 are I1 = [0.0, 0.45], I2 = [0.45, 0.71] and
I3 = [0.71, 1.0].

constraints such as prominence, height, width, and minimum distance be-

information on the clustering structure of a given dendrogram, it becomes
interesting to consider approaches to estimate the general modularity of the
original data as represented by a given dendrogram. This possibility is de-
veloped in the present section.

as represented by a respective dendrogram, tends to be associated with the
following two characteristics: (a) well-distributed main branching levels along
p(s); and (b) narrow dispersions around each of those levels. Therefore, these
two aspects are henceforth considered in the present work as a means to
estimate the overall modularity of a dendrogram.

Influence of the Parameter ˜σ
The concepts and methods presented above involve one main parameter,
namely the fixed smoothing scale ˜σ corresponding to the standard deviation
of the Gaussian function used to interpolate and smooth the function ˜f(s).
As indicated in Section 3, the parameter ˜σ specifies the resolution of the
cluster detection and modularity estimation. Smaller values of ˜σ will allow
the finer structure of a dendrogram (branches that are closer to each other)
to be taken into account, tending to yield a larger number of clusters. At the
same time, larger values of ˜σ will allow the approach to emphasize the larger
scale structure of a dendrogram, merging the branches which are closer to
each other. The choice of ˜σ depends on several aspects, including the main
objectives of the analysis, the structure of the data, and the eventual presence
of noise or other types of interference in the original data.
Important related issues concern the possible effects of different choices
of ˜σ on the obtained clusters and the estimated modularity. These aspects
are addressed in the present section.
First, we consider the effect of ˜σ on the hierarchical clusters obtained.

Concluding Remarks
Concepts and methodologies for identifying hierarchical clusters directly
from dendrograms and estimating their modularity have been described in
this work, which are based on two main elements: (a) estimation of merging
density functions; and (b) their balancing so that higher hierarchy branches
are considered with enhanced weights.
The main interesting features of the described approaches include: (i)
performed in non-supervised manner, not requiring the estimation of the
number of existing clusters (which is suggested by the own methodology); (ii)
detection of hierarchical clusters directly from the balanced merging density
p(s) and the dendrogram, without resourcing to the original measurements
associated to each data element; (iii) estimation of the overall modularity of
the original data set while considering only the function p(s). In addition, as
has been illustrated, it is also possible to apply both the described approaches
recursively to sub-dendrograms of specific interest.
The potential of the described approaches has been illustrated for some
types of dendrograms and data sets, and the results indicate proper detection
of clusters and modularity estimation at least for the considered dendrograms
and configurations.
The influence of the parameter ˜σ on cluster identification and modularity
estimation has also been briefly addressed, and preliminary results indicate
that, at least for the type of data and configurations considered, relatively
little influence could be observed in both cases for relatively small variations

The thus far obtained results indicate that the proposed concepts and
methodologies have promising potential for performing non-supervised pat-
tern recognition. In addition, the approach is relatively simple, involving low
complexity order. However, the results obtained are specific to the types and
sizes of data sets, the number and shapes of clusters, the types of dendro-
grams, the linkage criterion, and the parametric configurations considered.
Therefore, it would be necessary to further investigate the performance of
the methodology in more general cases and configurations.
Additional perspectives for further investigations include, but are not lim-
ited to, the development of approaches for generating dendrograms by using
the reported concepts; studying the effect of the fixed smoothing parame-
ters ˜σ more systematically; and developing measurements (e.g. functionals
or multi-resolution indicators) as a means to characterize additional proper-
ties of dendrograms directly from their respective balanced merging density
functions. Another possible development consists of adapting the proposed
cluster detection and modularity estimation approaches to graphs and com-
plex networks represented by their connectivity matrices.
