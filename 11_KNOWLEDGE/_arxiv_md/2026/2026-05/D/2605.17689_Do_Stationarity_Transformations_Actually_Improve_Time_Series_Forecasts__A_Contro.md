Highlights
What are the main findings?
• Differencing-based transformations (first, seasonal, fractional) act as high-pass filters that
remove predictable low-frequency signal, worsening forecast accuracy in the majority
of cases—even for models traditionally assumed to require stationary input.
• Variance stabilization (log, Box-Cox) is the primary exception, improving forecasts on
heteroscedastic data by addressing a modeling assumption violation without attenuating
predictive structure.
What are the implications of the main findings?
• Transformation selection should be guided by empirical out-of-sample evaluation rather
than theoretical stationarity assumptions.
• Practitioners should distinguish between signal-preserving transforms (variance stabi-
lization) and signal-suppressing transforms (differencing), complementing theoretical
guidance with empirical out-of-sample validation.
Abstract
Stationarity transformations are standard preprocessing in time series forecasting, yet
their actual impact on accuracy across different non-stationarity types and model families
has received little controlled evaluation. We construct synthetic datasets with known
properties—trend, seasonality, heteroscedasticity, and combinations—and apply fourteen
transformation configurations across seven models and three forecast horizons (3,528
experiments). Stationarity is quantified via consensus ratios from ten statistical tests, and
each transform–dataset pair is classified as matched or mismatched based on whether the
transform targets the dataset’s known non-stationarity. For matched pairs, transforms
improve forecasts only 18% of the time. The primary exception is variance stabilization: log
and Box-Cox on heteroscedastic data improve accuracy in 60–65% of cases. Differencing a
linear-trend series—a textbook use case—worsens forecasts in all cases tested. Mediation
analysis confirms that while transforms achieve trend stationarity, this does not translate
into lower forecast error; the mechanism is signal attenuation. Real-world validation on
TSA airport passenger data corroborates these findings. Our results suggest transformation
selection should be guided by empirical out-of-sample evaluation rather than theoretical
stationarity assumptions.
Keywords: stationarity transformations; forecast accuracy; time series preprocessing;
controlled experiment; mediation analysis; fractional differencing; synthetic data

1. Introduction
Stationarity transformations occupy a central role in time series forecasting methodol-
ogy. Textbooks and practitioner guides routinely recommend preprocessing steps such as
differencing, log transforms, and seasonal adjustments to render a series stationary before
model fitting [1,2]. The rationale is straightforward: many classical forecasting models—
autoregressive (AR), autoregressive moving average (ARMA), and their extensions—are
formulated under the assumption of stationary input, and violating this assumption may
degrade forecast quality.
Despite the ubiquity of this advice, surprisingly little work has evaluated whether
achieving stationarity through preprocessing actually improves forecast accuracy in a
controlled setting. Most empirical forecasting studies either apply a fixed preprocessing
pipeline without comparing alternatives [6,7], or evaluate models on real-world data where
the true data-generating process (DGP) is unknown, making it impossible to determine
whether a transformation targets the correct form of non-stationarity.
This gap matters for practice. If transformations that successfully achieve stationarity
do not reliably improve forecasts—or worse, degrade them—then practitioners would
benefit from empirical guidance on when and which transformations help, rather than
relying solely on stationarity as a preprocessing criterion.
This gap is addressed through a controlled experimental design with three contribu-
tions:
1.
Synthetic time series with known DGPs are constructed to isolate specific non-
stationarity patterns—trend, seasonality, heteroscedasticity, and their combinations—
enabling precise attribution of transformation effects.
2.
Fourteen transformation configurations are evaluated across seven forecasting mod-
els spanning statistical and machine learning families, and three forecast horizons,
yielding 3,528 experimental configurations.
3.
A mediation analysis framework is introduced that decomposes the transformation–
accuracy relationship into three causal pathways: whether transformations achieve
stationarity, whether achieved stationarity improves forecasts, and whether transfor-
mations directly affect forecast error.
The results reveal that the relationship between stationarity and forecast accuracy is
more nuanced than the conventional preprocessing pipeline assumes. Transformations do
increase stationarity as measured by a weighted consensus of ten statistical tests. However,
higher stationarity ratios are associated with worse forecast accuracy, not better. The total
effect of applying any transformation is a statistically significant increase in sMAPE. This
pattern holds even for AR and ARMA models—traditionally classified as requiring sta-
tionary input—though their implementations include trend parameterization (Table 2) that
partially accommodates non-stationarity internally, which itself underscores the diminish-
ing practical necessity of external stationarity transforms. The primary exception is variance
stabilization: log and Box-Cox transforms on heteroscedastic data improve accuracy for
most model families by addressing heteroscedasticity without removing learnable signal
structure.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work.
Section 3 describes the experimental design. Section 4 presents the main results. Section 5
develops the mediation analysis. Section 6 provides real-world validation. Section 7
discusses implications, and Section 8 concludes.

2. Related Work
The assumption that stationarity is a prerequisite for accurate forecasting has deep
roots in the time series literature. Box and Jenkins [1] established the ARIMA modeling
paradigm, in which differencing is applied to achieve stationarity before fitting autoregres-
sive and moving average components. This framework has been enormously influential,
and its preprocessing logic—difference until stationary, then model the residual—remains
standard practice [2].
Subsequent work has expanded the transformation toolkit. Box and Cox [3] introduced
the power transformation family for variance stabilization. Geweke and Porter-Hudak
[4] proposed the log-periodogram regression for estimating fractional differencing param-
eters, enabling transforms that remove long-range dependence while preserving more
information than integer differencing. Seasonal differencing addresses periodic patterns,
and pipeline combinations (e.g., log followed by differencing) are common in practice.
Despite this rich methodological literature, controlled empirical evaluations of transfor-
mation effectiveness are scarce. The M-competition series [5–7] have advanced forecasting
methodology enormously, but their focus is on model comparison rather than preprocess-
ing evaluation. Participants typically choose their own preprocessing pipelines, making it
difficult to isolate transformation effects.
Several studies have examined specific transformations. Nelson and Granger [8]
investigated the effects of Box-Cox transformations on forecast accuracy, finding mixed
results depending on the data characteristics. Franses [9] examined seasonal adjustment
and its impact on forecasting, noting that inappropriate seasonal adjustment can harm
accuracy. More recently, de Prado [10] studied fractional differencing in the context of
financial time series, finding that it can preserve useful long-range information.
However, no prior study has systematically evaluated a comprehensive set of transfor-
mations across multiple non-stationarity types, model families, and forecast horizons in
a controlled setting where the true DGP is known. This work fills this gap by combining
controlled synthetic experiments with a mediation analysis framework that disentangles
the mechanisms through which transformations affect forecast accuracy.

3.2. Transformations
Each of the twelve datasets described above is subjected to a battery of stationarity
transformations before forecasting. This allows measurement of whether—and how much—
each transformation affects forecast accuracy for each specific non-stationarity pattern. Six
single transformations and eight pipeline combinations are evaluated, for a total of fourteen
configurations:
•
Single transforms: none (untransformed), first differencing, seasonal differencing
(period 52), fractional differencing via the GPH procedure [4], log, and Box-Cox [3].

Pipeline combinations: log+difference, boxcox+difference, log+seasonal_difference,
boxcox+seasonal_difference, log+fractional_difference, boxcox+fractional_difference,
log+difference+seasonal_difference, boxcox+difference+seasonal_difference.

3.3. Forecasting Models
Seven models are selected spanning three categories based on their theoretical station-
arity requirements:
•
Traditionally stationarity-assuming: AR, ARMA—classical formulations assume
stationary input, though modern implementations (e.g., statsmodels) include deter-
ministic trend parameters that partially accommodate non-stationarity internally.
•
Partial stationarity: ARIMA—includes internal differencing but still benefits from
stationary residuals.
•
Stationarity not required: ETS, Holt-Winters, Prophet [17], Gradient Boosting (Light-
GBM [18])—these models can handle non-stationary data natively.
This categorization enables us to test whether transformations help more for models
that traditionally assume stationarity. We note that the distinction between “requiring” and
“not requiring” stationarity is blurred in practice: AR and ARMA implementations with
trend terms effectively estimate deterministic trend components alongside autoregressive
structure, reducing the practical gap between these categories.

Data splitting: The time series is split into training and test sets, with the test set

2.
Transformation: The specified transformation is applied to the training data. For
pipeline transforms, each step is applied sequentially (e.g., log first, then difference).

3.6. Hyperparameter Tuning
To ensure each model operates at its best configuration for each dataset–transformation
combination, automated hyperparameter tuning is performed via randomized search. This
is critical for fair comparison: a poorly tuned model might appear to benefit from (or be
harmed by) a transformation when the real issue is suboptimal hyper-parameters.
Statistical models (AR, ARMA, ARIMA, ETS, Holt-Winters): For each model, a pa-
rameter grid is defined over model-specific hyper-parameters and candidates are evaluated
using in-sample RMSE. The search evaluates up to 50 random parameter combinations and
selects the configuration with the lowest RMSE on the training data.
Machine learning models (Gradient Boosting): Scikit-learn’s RandomizedSearchCV
is used with 5-fold cross-validation and negative mean squared error as the scoring metric.
The search evaluates 15 random combinations across 5 folds.
Prophet: Uses a randomized search (50 iterations) over changepoint and seasonality
prior scales, with an 80/20 train/validation split for evaluation.

3.7. Stationarity Measurement
Stationarity is quantified using weighted consensus ratios from a battery of ten statistical
tests, implemented via the StationarityToolkit [11], organized by dimension:
•
Trend stationarity: Augmented Dickey-Fuller, Phillips-Perron, KPSS, Zivot-Andrews,
and Variance Ratio tests.
•
Variance stationarity: Breusch-Pagan, White, Goldfeld-Quandt, and ARCH tests.
•
Seasonal stationarity: STL decomposition test, ACF/PACF periodicity tests.
All tests are conducted at α = 0.05. Domain-appropriate exclusion rules are applied:
(1) the Zivot-Andrews test is excluded from the trend consensus, as it tests for stationarity
with a structural break and can produce misleading results on series where no break
exists; (2) the ARCH test is included in variance consensus only if trend and seasonal
consensus indicate stationarity, because unremoved trend or seasonality can produce
spurious volatility clustering that mimics conditional heteroskedasticity; (3) ACF/PACF
tests are included in seasonal consensus only if trend and variance consensus indicate
stationarity, since significant autocorrelation at seasonal lags can be an artifact of trend
or heteroskedasticity rather than true periodicity. This two-pass approach yields three
continuous ratios rtrend, rvar, rseasonal ∈[0, 1]. The full dataset × transformation ratio table
is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

4. Results
The results tell a consistent story: stationarity transformations often hurt forecast
accuracy in the controlled settings tested, with variance stabilization being the primary
exception. Throughout this section, stationarity is measured via the filtered consensus ratios
described in Section 3.7, which account for confounding between stationarity dimensions
(e.g., excluding ARCH results when trend is present) rather than relying on any single test.
The analysis begins by examining how transformations affect accuracy across different
dataset types and model groups.

5. Mediation Analysis
The results above establish what happens (transformations hurt accuracy) but not why.
The standard rationale assumes a causal chain: transformation →stationarity →better
forecasts. This chain is decomposed using a mediation analysis framework [16] to identify
which links hold empirically and which do not.
5.1. Framework
Let T ∈{0, 1} indicate whether a transformation was applied, S denote the stationarity
ratio (mediator), and Y denote sMAPE (outcome). The following models are estimated:
•
Path A (Transform →Stationarity): S = α0 + α1T + ε
•
Path B (Stationarity →sMAPE, controlling for transform): Y = β0 + β1S + β2T + ε
•
Path C (Total effect): Y = γ0 + γ1T + ε
This framework is extended with model category controls and interaction terms to
test whether the relationships differ for models that traditionally assume stationarity.

Adding model category dummies (partial stationarity for ARIMA, traditionally-

5.4. Interpretation
The mediation story is clear: transforms achieve trend stationarity (Path A works for
trend), but achieved trend and seasonal stationarity do not improve forecasts (Path B is
reversed for both). The total effect (Path C) is harmful. The mechanism is that transforms—
particularly differencing variants—remove not just non-stationarity but also the learnable
signal that models rely on for accurate forecasting.
The one dimension where the mediation logic does hold is variance: achieving variance
stationarity genuinely reduces sMAPE (Path B: coef = −0.12). This aligns with the earlier
finding that log and Box-Cox transforms on heteroscedastic data are the primary exception
for most model families. Variance stabilization works because it addresses a modeling
assumption violation (heteroscedastic errors) without attenuating the predictive signal
structure.

7. Discussion
These findings reveal a gap between the theoretical rationale for stationarity prepro-
cessing and its empirical effect on forecast accuracy. In the controlled experiments presented
here, the explanation is straightforward: many transformations, particularly differencing,
remove not only non-stationarity but also the predictable structure that forecasting models
exploit.
Consider a series with a linear trend. Differencing effectively eliminates the deter-
ministic trend to satisfy stationarity criteria, but this comes at a steep cost: it acts as a
https://doi.org/10.3390/1010000


## Page 18


Version May 19, 2026 submitted to Journal Not Specified
18 of 20
high-pass filter that removes low-frequency signal components, which are often the most
predictable for out-of-sample extrapolation. The resulting differenced series is closer to
white noise, which is by definition unpredictable. This phenomenon is formally known as
over-differencing [15], where unnecessary differencing induces a non-invertible moving
average component that can attenuate forecastable signal. The issue is not that differencing
is a “bad method”—it is a well-understood linear operator—but rather that the informa-
tion trade-off between achieving stationarity and preserving predictable structure is often
unfavorable in practical forecasting settings.
Perhaps more surprisingly, this harmful effect persists even for stochastic trends
(random walks), where differencing is theoretically optimal. In the experiments, first
differencing on random walk data produced sMAPE of 1.53—substantially worse than the
untransformed baseline (0.31). This result likely reflects two factors. First, modern model
implementations accommodate stochastic drift through internal trend parameterization (as
shown in Table 2), reducing the practical benefit of explicit external differencing. Second,
on finite samples, the inverse transformation required to map differenced forecasts back to
the original scale compounds errors: each forecasted increment is added cumulatively, so
small per-step errors accumulate into large absolute deviations at longer horizons. Together,
these factors mean that even in the canonical case for differencing, the operational trade-off
becomes unfavorable in our experimental setup.
The variance stabilization exception is instructive. Log and Box-Cox transforms
on heteroscedastic data do not remove signal—they reshape the error distribution to be
more homogeneous, which genuinely helps models that assume constant variance. This
suggests that the relevant question is not “is the series stationary?” but rather “does
the transformation address a specific modeling assumption violation without removing
predictive information?”
These findings should not be interpreted as invalidating the theoretical foundations
of differencing in classical time-series analysis. Stationarity remains a fundamental the-
oretical property for understanding data-generating processes, ensuring model stability,
and avoiding spurious correlations in inferential statistics [1]. However, this study focuses
strictly on the operational trade-off between statistical purity and empirical out-of-sample
predictive accuracy. The mathematical properties of unit root processes and the role of
differencing in achieving stationarity remain well-established. Rather, the results suggest
that in practical forecasting pipelines—involving modern model implementations, finite
samples, hyperparameter tuning, and out-of-sample evaluation—achieving stationarity
through preprocessing does not necessarily translate into improved forecast accuracy. Sta-
tionarity is a statistical property, whereas forecastability is an empirical predictive property;
the two are related but not equivalent.
7.1. Implications for Practice
These results do not argue against preprocessing altogether; rather, they argue for se-
lective, data-driven preprocessing that distinguishes between transformations that preserve
predictive signal (such as variance stabilization) and those that inadvertently suppress it
(such as over-differencing).
1.
Transformation selection should be guided by empirical evaluation on held-out data
rather than theoretical stationarity assumptions [19]. In practice, this could take the
form of a validation-based selector: apply each candidate transformation, forecast
over the most recent 2–3 held-out windows, and select the transformation (or no
transformation) that minimizes out-of-sample error before deploying the final model.
https://doi.org/10.3390/1010000

2.
Variance-stabilizing transforms (log, Box-Cox) should be applied when heteroscedas-
ticity is present—these address a genuine modeling assumption violation without
attenuating predictive signal.
3.
Differencing-based transforms (first, seasonal, or fractional) should be applied with
caution rather than as a default preprocessing step, as they act as high-pass filters that
risk removing learnable low-frequency signal even when they successfully achieve
stationarity.
4.
Pipeline combinations of transforms should be avoided unless empirically validated,
as stacking transforms compounds information loss.
7.2. Limitations
Our study has several limitations. First, while our synthetic DGPs cover both de-
terministic and stochastic trends, they remain simpler than many real-world time series
which may exhibit regime changes, structural breaks, and complex nonlinear dynamics.
Second, our model set does not include deep learning approaches that may interact dif-
ferently with preprocessing. Third, our real-world validation uses a single transportation
series; broader validation across domains would strengthen the conclusions. Fourth, the
stationarity consensus approach relies on asymptotic test properties that may not hold
perfectly with our sample size of 200 observations. Fifth, for the autoregressive datasets, the
AR(2) autocorrelation structure decays rapidly and provides limited predictability beyond
a few steps ahead; at our minimum forecast horizon of h = 4, much of the exploitable AR
signal has already decayed, making it difficult to observe scenarios where differencing
genuinely helps by revealing autocorrelated residuals. The conclusions should therefore be
interpreted as evidence from controlled forecasting settings rather than universal claims
about all nonstationary processes.
8. Conclusions
This study provides controlled experimental evidence that stationarity transformations
often fail to improve time series forecast accuracy. Through a mediation analysis of 3,528
experimental configurations, it is shown that while transformations successfully achieve
stationarity, this achieved stationarity is associated with worse—not better—forecast accu-
racy. The mechanism is signal attenuation: transforms remove predictable structure along
with non-stationarity.
The primary exception is variance stabilization. Log and Box-Cox transforms on
heteroscedastic data improve accuracy for most model families by addressing a genuine
modeling assumption violation without removing the signal.
These findings argue for complementing theoretical guidance with empirical valida-
tion in transformation selection for forecasting pipelines. Rather than asking “is the series
stationary?”, practitioners should ask “does this transformation improve out-of-sample
accuracy for my specific model and data?”
Future work could extend this analysis in several directions: evaluating deep learn-
ing forecasters (transformers, LSTMs) which may interact differently with preprocessing;
testing on highly persistent autoregressive processes where differencing may provide
genuine benefit at short horizons; developing automated transformation selection frame-
works that use held-out validation to choose preprocessing empirically; and expanding
real-world validation across diverse domains such as finance, energy, and healthcare to
assess generalizability.
