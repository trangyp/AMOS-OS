Abstract
Sorting over bounded-universe integer keys has tra-
ditionally relied on counting sort and radix sort,
both of which incur mandatory prefix-sum passes,
auxiliary scatter buffers, or multiple permutation
passes. This paper introduces DialSort, a non-
comparative sorting architecture based on the self-
indexing principle: each integer key k simultane-
ously encodes its value and its canonical position
in the ordered address space [0, U −1]. DialSort
eliminates the prefix-sum pass entirely by treating
the histogram H[·] as the canonical ordered repre-
sentation, not as an intermediate structure.
To support parallel ingestion without serializa-
tion, we introduce the Conflict Resolution Network
(CRN), a pipelined additive reduction tree of depth
⌈log2 k⌉that resolves concurrent writes using equal-
ity checks exclusively, with no magnitude compar-
isons. Formal proofs establish O(n + U) sequential
and O(n/k + log k + U) parallel time bounds.
A software prototype on an 8-thread Intel x86-64
achieves 39.77× speedup over std::sort and peak
throughput of 115.9 M keys/s. Against Classic
Counting Sort, DialSort wins 46 of 48 configu-
rations (avg. 1.65× faster). Against IPS4o, Dial-
Sort outperforms it in 24 of 48 sequential and 29
of 48 parallel configurations, with speedups up to
9.1× on uniform inputs. Against ska sort, it wins
46 of 48 configurations (avg. 3.33×, best > 7×).
All 208 benchmark configurations passed correct-
ness verification.
DialSort is not a universal replacement for
comparison-based sorting, but a domain-specialised
architecture for bounded-universe workloads where
sorting reduces to a geometric read over memory.
Benchmark source (v3.1) and five open interactive
simulators are released alongside this paper.

Introduction
Sorting is a fundamental operation in computer sci-
ence, traditionally studied under the comparison
model, which imposes the well-known lower bound
of Ω(n log n) for general inputs [1, 2]. Decades of
research have produced highly efficient comparison-
based algorithms such as introsort and parallel sam-
ple sort [3, 4], yet all of them rely on evaluating
relative order between keys.
For
bounded-universe
integer
keys,
non-
comparative methods such as Counting Sort and
Radix Sort [5] bypass this lower bound by exploit-
ing structural properties of the input.
However,
both still incur significant overhead:
Counting
Sort requires a mandatory prefix-sum pass and an
auxiliary output array, while Radix Sort performs
multiple permutation passes.
These extra steps
introduce memory traffic, synchronization points,
and sequential dependencies that limit scalability,
especially in parallel settings.
This
paper
introduces
DialSort,
a
non-
comparative sorting architecture grounded in the
self-indexing principle: an integer key inherently
encodes its own position in the ordered address
space. Rather than a new sorting paradigm, Dial-
Sort constitutes a high-performance architec-
tural contribution: it eliminates the mandatory
prefix-sum pass, introduces the CRN as a parallel
conflict-resolution primitive with bounded latency,
and enables a substrate-aware analysis spanning
CPU software, FPGA, photonics, and wafer-scale
processors. The self-indexing observation is related
to direct-address tables [2, Ch. 11]; its systematic
architectural exploitation is the novel contribution.
To support efficient parallelism, we introduce the
Conflict Resolution Network (CRN), a pipelined
additive reduction tree that resolves concurrent
writes using only equality checks.
Unlike prefix-
sum-based approaches, the CRN operates dur-
ing ingestion, eliminating global synchronization

Related Work and Formal
Differentiation
2.1
Existing non-comparative sorts
Counting sort and radix sort [5] exploit non-
comparative properties of integer keys but require
additional steps: counting sort mandates a prefix-
sum pass and mandatory output reconstruction;
LSD radix sort [2] introduces multiple permutation
passes. Sorting networks [6] provide fixed-structure
ordering but still rely on comparison primitives at
every node.
Bead sort [7] offers a physical anal-
ogy but lacks a computational architecture and re-
quires O(n2) space.
Engineering-oriented imple-
mentations such as ska sort and IPS4o [3] are the
practical state-of-the-art for CPU sorting; IPS4o
uses order comparisons as fallbacks, while ska sort
is itself non-comparative but requires digit decom-
position and scatter passes. IPS4o is benchmarked
directly in Section 10; ska sort is benchmarked di-
rectly in Section 12.

2.2
Direct
addressing
and
self-
indexing
The observation that an integer key can serve as
its own array index is well established as direct-
address tables [2, Ch. 11]. DialSort situates itself
explicitly in this context: the mathematical dual-
ity between value and index is not claimed as a
new discovery. The novel contribution is the sys-
tematic architectural exploitation of this duality—
CRN, elimination of prefix-sum, and the substrate-
aware analysis across four physical substrates.
2.3
RAM-model complexity bounds
for integer sorting
In the word-RAM model with word size w bits,
sorting n integers from [0, U−1] can be accom-
plished in O(n log log U) time [11]. Counting sort
achieves O(n+U) time and O(U) space, optimal
when U = O(n). DialSort operates in the same
asymptotic complexity class as counting sort for
the sequential case and is therefore optimal in the
same regime (U = O(n)). For U ≫n, word-RAM
methods such as Han–Thorup [11] achieve superior
asymptotic bounds; DialSort does not compete in
that regime and explicitly targets n ≳10 · U (Sec-
tion 16). The contribution of DialSort lies not
in beating Han–Thorup bounds, but in the paral-
lel CRN architecture achieving O(n/k + log k + U)
wall-clock time and in the substrate-aware analysis
across four physical execution paradigms.
2.4
Prior work on self-indexed sort-
ing
A related idea appears in the 1996 Self-Indexed Sort
(SIS) proposal [10], which is essentially a linked-list
variant of classical counting sort. Unlike that work,
DialSort redefines the histogram as the canonical
ordered state, introduces the Conflict Resolution
Network for native parallelism, and provides both
formal (Proposition 1) and empirical differentiation
(46 of 48 configurations faster, average 1.65×).
2.5
Structural and architectural dif-
ferentiation from counting sort
The most predictable reviewer objection is that Di-
alSort is “just counting sort renamed.” Table 1
addresses this formally.
We state the distinction
explicitly:
DialSort is not a variant of counting
sort: it is a different execution model over
the same mathematical object. Counting
sort uses the histogram as a temporary
intermediate structure to be consumed by
a mandatory prefix-sum pass and output-
scatter phase. DialSort treats the his-
togram as the canonical ordered represen-
tation itself; output reconstruction is op-
tional and interface-dependent, not algo-
rithmic.
This distinction eliminates one
full pass over O(U) memory and the O(n)
scatter buffer.

test

The Self-Indexing Principle
3.1
Order-theoretic foundations
We first establish the order-theoretic setting pre-
cisely, responding to the reviewer’s observations re-
garding order relations.
Definition 1 (Total order on the universe). Let
U = {0, 1, . . . , M−1} ⊂Z≥0 for some positive in-
teger M. The relation ≤defined by a ≤b
⇐⇒
b −a ∈Z≥0 is a total order on U: it is reflexive
(a ≤a), antisymmetric (a ≤b and b ≤a ⇒a = b),
transitive, and total (every pair is comparable).
Remark 1. Throughout this paper, “order” refers
to the total order (≤), not to the strict relation (<).
The strict order (<) is a strict total order (irreflex-
ive, asymmetric, transitive) but not a partial order
in the standard sense, since it fails reflexivity. Al-
gorithms exploit a pre-existing order structure; they
do not define it.
Definition 2 (Self-Indexing Duality). For any in-
teger k ∈[0, U −1], k is simultaneously a value and
its own index in the naturally totally ordered ad-
dress space ([0, U −1], ≤).
No comparison-based
computation is required to determine the value-
address correspondence; the total order ≤is a math-
ematical object independent of any algorithm, and
the remaining work is the ordered projection over
the address space.

3.2
Input
Multiset,
Support
Set,
and Embedding Conditions
Let K be the input multiset of keys drawn from the
bounded universe [0, M −1]. Define its multiplicity
function
H : [0, M −1] →N,
H(k) = |{x ∈K : x = k}|.
Define the support set
D = {k ∈[0, M −1] | H(k) > 0}.
The distinction between K, H, and D is fundamen-
tal. The multiset K carries multiplicity informa-
tion; the function H records that multiplicity; and
D is the order-theoretic object relevant for embed-
ding values into the ordered address space. Accord-
ingly, order-theoretic statements are made over D,
while repeated occurrences are represented exclu-
sively through H.
Definition 3 (Order comparison). An order com-
parison is any evaluation of a relation in {<, ≤, >
, ≥} between two input keys, or between two values
derived from input keys for the purpose of establish-
ing their relative order. Equality tests x = y, direct
indexing H[x], and occupancy queries on histogram
cells are not order comparisons under this defini-
tion, because they do not determine the relative or-
der of two distinct keys.
Definition 4 (Canonical embedding). Define
ϕ : D →[0, M −1],
ϕ(k) = k.
Since D ⊆[0, M −1], this map is well defined.
Proposition 1 (Embedding and isomorphism con-
ditions). The map ϕ is an order-embedding from
(D, ≤) into ([0, M −1], ≤).
It is an order-
isomorphism if and only if
D = [0, M −1].
Proof. Because ϕ(k) = k, if a, b ∈D and a ≤b,
then ϕ(a) = a ≤b = ϕ(b), so ϕ is order-preserving.
It is also injective, again because ϕ(k) = k. Hence ϕ
is an order-embedding. It is an order-isomorphism
exactly when it is bijective onto [0, M −1], which
holds if and only if every element of the universe
appears in the input, i.e., D = [0, M −1].
Corollary 1.1. In the general case, DialSort re-
lies on the order-embedding ϕ : D ,→[0, M −1]
(Proposition 1), with multiplicities stored separately
in H. The sorted order is therefore read from the
pre-existing total order of the address space together
with the multiplicity profile encoded in H, rather
than constructed by pairwise key comparisons.
Corollary 1.2 (Isomorphism in Substrate-3). ϕ :
D →[0, M −1] is an order-isomorphism when-
ever D = [0, M −1] (Proposition 1), regardless of
multiplicities, since duplicates live in H, not in D.
Substrate-3 additionally achieves a physical one-to-
one correspondence when H[k] ∈{0, 1} for all k:
each active resonator corresponds bijectively to one
element of D and preserves ≤. When H[k] ≥2 for
some k, the mathematical isomorphism on D is un-
affected but the physical bijection breaks. Substrate-
3 is the unique substrate where both hold simulta-
neously.
Remark 2. In Substrate-4 (WSE-3, Section 7.4),
each tile maintains H[k] ≥0 in its local SRAM.
The mathematical order-isomorphism ϕ : D →
[0, M −1] holds whenever D = [0, M −1]; when D ⊊
[0, M −1], ϕ is an order-embedding. The physical
one-to-one resonator correspondence of Substrate-3
is absent in all other substrates.

Complexity Analysis
The reviewer correctly notes that numerical exam-
ples illustrate but do not prove complexity. We pro-
vide formal proofs below. Complexity is analysed in
the RAM model; the absence of order comparisons
refers to the comparison-based view of ordering es-

Theorem 2 (Sequential complexity). DialSort
sorts n integer keys from [0, U−1] in O(n + U) time
and O(U) auxiliary space.
Proof. Initialising H[0..U−1] to zero costs Θ(U).
Ingestion (Eq. 1) performs one constant-time in-

visits all U histogram cells and emits exactly n =

Therefore the overall time complexity is O(n + U),
and the auxiliary space complexity is O(U).
Corollary 2.2. When U = O(n), DialSort runs

6.1
The conflict problem
When k lanes ingest keys in parallel, two or more
lanes may carry the same key within the same clock
cycle. Naive solutions serialize writes (losing paral-
lelism) or use atomic increments (hardware serial-
ization). The CRN resolves conflicts before writes
reach the register bank, without serialization and
without order comparisons.
6.2
CRN Lane States, Node Defini-
tion, and Level Transition
In the base formulation, each lane carries a pair
(ki, 1) per ingested key — one key, one unit count.
A count ci > 1 arises within a lane when the same
key appears in consecutive positions; in that case
the lane may accumulate locally before passing to
the CRN. This local accumulation is an optimisa-
tion, not a requirement: correctness holds for ci = 1
in all cases by Lemma 1.

6.3
CRN vs. existing parallel primi-
tives
The CRN is structurally distinct from three related
hardware primitives:
Bitonic sorting networks [6] sort n elements
using O(n log2 n) comparators arranged in a fixed
pattern. Every comparator node evaluates a magni-
tude comparison (>) and conditionally swaps two
elements.
The CRN evaluates only equality (=)
and never reorders elements — it fuses concurrent
counts of identical keys.
Parallel prefix-sum (scan) trees [8] compute
cumulative sums over an array using a balanced
reduction tree, requiring O(log n) stages and O(n)
operations. Counting sort uses a prefix-sum pass to
convert the histogram into output positions. The
CRN requires no prefix sum: it operates during
ingestion, before the histogram is fully populated,
and does not need cumulative position information.
GPU histogram parallelization uses atomic
operations or privatized per-thread histograms with
a merge step to handle write conflicts.

sequence on every run, explaining its consistently

Discussion
DialSort eliminates ordering comparisons en-
tirely. Unlike prefix-sum-based methods [8], it op-
erates directly on memory geometry.
The his-
togram H[·] after ingestion is the complete sorted
representation; the output vector is an interface
adaptor, not an algorithmic necessity.
Domain of dominance.
DialSort is a domain-
specialised algorithm. It delivers its strongest gains
when three conditions hold simultaneously: (i) keys
are drawn from a bounded universe [0, U −1] with
U ≤65536; (ii) n ≳10 · U (the geometric scan
cost is amortised); (iii) the distribution is non-
pathologically ordered (uniform or skewed). Out-
side this envelope — notably on sorted or reverse-
sorted inputs, or when n ≪U — comparison-
based methods such as IPS4o retain clear advan-
tages.
This boundary is not a weakness but a
structural consequence of the self-indexing princi-
ple: DialSort trades generality for deterministic,
comparison-free execution.
The skewed distribution consistently produces
the strongest speedups. This confirms the CRN’s
structural mechanism:
key concentration causes
many lanes to carry identical keys per cycle, col-
lapsing most writes into single additive increments
and reducing effective write traffic.

World Impact
The self-indexing principle of DialSort is not lim-
ited to arbitrary integers. Any finite domain that
admits a natural order-preserving mapping to a
bounded integer range can be sorted without order
comparisons (Proposition 2).
This makes Dial-
Sort particularly powerful in domains where data
is already represented as small integers or can be
mapped to them with negligible cost.

Limitations
• Bounded universe required.
DialSort
applies only to integer keys in [0, U −1].
• O(U) scan dominates for small N. Practi-
cal guideline: use DialSort when n ≳10 · U.
• Memory cost O(U). For U ≤65536 the his-
togram fits in L2 cache. For larger U, hierar-
chical partitioning is required (future work).
• Sorted/reverse-sorted inputs. On nearly-
sorted data, IPS4o’s adaptive strategy ex-
ploits pre-existing order and significantly out-
performs DialSort (Section 10). DialSort
does not detect or exploit input order.
• Substrates

Conclusion
and
Future
Work
DialSort
demonstrates
that,
for
bounded-
universe integer keys, sorting can be interpreted
as
a
geometric
reading
process,
not
a
com-
parison problem.
Direct empirical comparison
against IPS4o,
the state-of-the-art comparison-
based sorter, confirms that DialSort outperforms
IPS4o-seq in 24 of 48 sequential configurations,
with up to 9.1× speedup at N=107, U=65536,
uniform distribution.
In the parallel matchup
(DialSort-Parallel vs. IPS4o-par, both 8 threads),
DialSort-Parallel wins in 29 of 48 configurations
with average ratio 1.90× and best case 4.08× (Sec-
tion 11).
Performance is regime-dependent: Di-
alSort dominates on uniform and skewed inputs;
IPS4o dominates on highly ordered inputs. Direct
empirical comparison against ska sort, the state-of-
the-art non-comparative CPU sorter, confirms that
DialSort outperforms it in 46 of 48 configu-
rations, with an average speedup of 3.33× and a
best case exceeding 7× at N=107.

binary presence mode (Corollary 1.2). In all other
substrates ϕ is an order-embedding. On Substrate-

a hardware-determined constant of the fixed mesh
topology (Proposition 3),
with dominant term

tical bottleneck for large inputs.
