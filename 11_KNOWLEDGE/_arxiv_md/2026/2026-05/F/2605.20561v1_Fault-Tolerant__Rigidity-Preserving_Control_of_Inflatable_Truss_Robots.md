Fault-Tolerant, Rigidity-Preserving Control of Inflatable Truss Robots
James Wade∗1, Isaac Weaver∗1, Mihai Stanciu1, Nathan Usevitch1
Abstract— Isoperimetric robotic trusses can adapt to different
tasks and environments because they have a high strength-to-
weight ratio, can change their own shape dramatically, and
can be reconfigured into a variety of different shapes. However,
motor failures in operational environments can severely limit
operational capabilities if not properly addressed. This paper
presents a fault-tolerant control framework for an inflatable
robotic truss that maintains functionality despite motor failures,
shown through three key contributions. First, we extend the
kinematic optimization to handle arbitrary combinations of
motor failures by imposing equality constraints to ensure failed
actuators are not used. Second, we introduce discrete-time control
barrier function (DTCBF) constraints that mathematically guar-
antee structural rigidity while maximizing workspace utilization,
a critical requirement for reliable operation of truss robots
under discrete-time control. Third, we implement closed-loop
position control using onboard encoder feedback and a forward
kinematics-based state estimator, improving positional accuracy
in the presence of disturbances. We validate our approach
through simulation and hardware experiments on a 2D isoperi-
metric truss testbed. For a 2D configuration with 6 actuators, we
demonstrate > 69% workspace preservation under single-motor
failures and a > 25% improvement in tracking accuracy with
closed-loop control. These results establish a foundation for more
robust and resilient isoperimetric truss robots operating under
degraded actuation.
I. INTRODUCTION
Reconfigurable truss robots provide a lightweight, deploy-
able, and adaptable framework for robotic structures. Their
high stowed-to-deployed volume ratio and ability to morph
between configurations make them attractive for applications
including remote infrastructure deployment, field robotics, and
space systems [1]. This work focuses on isoperimetric truss
robots, which consist of compliant beams routed through
motorized rollers. By driving along the tubing, the rollers
redistribute tube length, changing the structure geometry under
a constant overall perimeter constraint. However, existing
control architectures provide limited robustness to actuator
failures despite the inherent redundancy of these systems. In
tightly coupled truss robots, failure of a single actuator can
substantially degrade workspace, manipulability, and tracking
performance in a way that has not been characterized. This
work develops a fault-tolerant control framework for inflat-
able isoperimetric truss robots, emphasizing maintenance of
rigidity and task-space performance under degraded actuation.

II. SYSTEM OVERVIEW
A. Isoperimetric Robots
Isoperimetric robots were first introduced in [2], with later
work extending them as modular truss-like structures for
lunar environments [1]. Each triangular module consists of an
inflated tube shared between two active roller units (Fig. 2) and
one passive roller, forming a triangular face of the truss. Each
active roller contains a DC motor that transfers tubing between
triangle edges, enabling coordinated shape morphing while the
perimeter of each triangle remains constant. The passive roller
shares the same mechanical structure but omits the motor and
PCB, serving as an anchoring point for the tube ends. This
inflatable truss architecture offers a promising alternative to
metallic structural robots, combining lightweight transporta-
bility with adaptability, strength, and actuation redundancy in
several 3D configurations [1]. However, the tightly coupled
actuation means that individual actuator failures can signifi-
cantly degrade mobility, motivating fault-sensitivity analysis.
B. Limitations of Current Controller
A key capability of the robot is moving a user-specified
target vertex (Fig. 1) along a desired trajectory. The current
kinematics-based controller solves a quadratic optimization

III. RELATED WORK
a) Truss-based Robotic Systems:
Truss-based robotic
and structural systems have been widely explored for their
high strength-to-weight ratio and efficient load-bearing capa-
bilities. NASA has researched the use of trusses made from
lunar regolith [3] and trusses composed of deployable beams
[4] for extraterrestrial operation. There are a variety of differ-
ent actuated truss structures in research literature, including
variable geometry trusses (VGT) [5], [6], tensegrity-based
robots [7], and tetrahedral 3D-printed robots [8]. While these
systems provide mechanical redundancy, they typically operate
under fundamentally different kinematic constraints from the
constant-perimeter requirements of our isoperimetric system,
necessitating a distinct approach to fault-tolerant control. Our
truss robot also differs from these structures in that it is both 1)
a compliant structure that 2) is robotic and can dynamically
change shape, making it more versatile to both typical and
novel use cases. Our robot’s method of actuation differs from
most existing truss actuation approaches, using its internal
compliant tubes to morph its shape rather than linear actuators
or tensioned strings.
b) Control of Redundant and Coupled Robotic Systems:
Redundancy has long been a central topic in the field of
robotics [9], focusing on the resolution of kinematic redun-
dancy to satisfy secondary objectives such as singularity avoid-
ance or fault tolerance [10], [11]. Prior work has extensively
examined the importance of maintaining graph rigidity (which
is critical for preserving structural shape in truss-like systems)
in both theory and practice, and much of this research has
often been situated in the context of swarm robotics [12],
[13]. Some of this research has even explored motion planning
for load manipulation tasks using swarms [14], similar to the
load-bearing capabilities of our truss robot [1]. However, our
approach differs fundamentally. In contrast to swarms—where
individual agents move semi-independently—our truss robot
exhibits tightly coupled motion, where displacement of a
single vertex induces correlated motion across the entire struc-
ture. Such behavior is more analogous to hyper-redundant and
continuum architectures [15], [16], which are characterized
by a high degree of kinematic coupling that requires whole-
body coordination. As in continuum and hyper-redundant sys-
tems, feasible motion in robotic truss structures emerges from
coordinated whole-body deformation rather than independent
agent motion. This intrinsic interdependence means that graph-
based swarm methods do not directly transfer to our system,
motivating extensions to the kinematic and control framework
tailored to tightly coupled truss structures. In our framework,
this redundancy is leveraged not only for workspace expan-
sion, but also to satisfy rigidity-preserving safety constraints
through coordinated whole-body motion.
c) Control Barrier Functions: The operational neces-
sity of workspace utilization must be balanced equally with
guarantees of rigidity preservation. Control barrier functions
(CBFs) have emerged as a powerful tool for safety-critical
control [17], where the control input is minimally modified
to maintain a nonnegative function h(x) that defines a safe
region. For sampled-data systems such as ours, discrete-time
control barrier functions (DTCBFs) provide rigorous safety
guarantees between sampling instances [18], [19]. Recent
work has established sufficient conditions for forward in-
variance in discrete-time systems [20], which we leverage
to ensure structural rigidity throughout operation. However,
DTCBFs have not previously been applied to rigidity main-
tenance in robotic truss systems, where redundant actuation
can be leveraged to maximize workspace utilization while
satisfying rigidity-preserving safety constraints.

D. Rigidity Preservation via Discrete-Time Barrier Functions
A key challenge for the truss robot is maximizing workspace
while preserving structural rigidity. For infinitesimally rigid
graphs, the rigidity matrix R(x) must have exactly six zero-
valued singular values in 3D (or three in 2D) [21]. The
next smallest singular value, σ7(R(x)) in 3D (or σ4 in 2D),
measures proximity to rigidity loss: when it reaches zero, the
structure becomes singular and may collapse [21]. We denote
this value as σcrit.
Rather than conservatively stopping motion near singu-
larities, we enforce rigidity through a discrete-time control

F. Closed-Loop Control with Forward Kinematics Estimator
Given knowledge of the current configuration of the robot,
the augmented optimization problem solves for the optimal
vertex velocities to produce a desired movement of the target
vertex. We build upon this open-loop control to incorporate
closed-loop feedback using onboard motor encoders. The
optimizer is solved at each control timestep using the latest
state estimate, forming a closed-loop control architecture.
Fig. 4 demonstrates the closed-loop controller developed
for the isoperimetric robot. The term bmove ∈Rn encodes the
positional error, calculated by subtracting the goal position
from the target vertex’s current position. A central computer
performs Sequential Least Squares Programming (SLSQP) to
solve for a ˙x such that the target vertex moves in the direction
of bmove, using the augmented problem formulation in Eq. 10.
This ˙xopt is converted into roller velocities through Eq. 4 and
transmitted to onboard microcontrollers via radio modules.
Prior work’s control of the isoperimetric truss repeated the
aforementioned process in an open-loop fashion, estimating
the updated robot configuration directly from ˙xopt without
hardware feedback [2].

V. RESULTS
A. Modeling Degraded Kinematics Due to Roller Failure
To simulate roller failures, we apply the constraint specified
in Eq. 6, which fixes a roller’s position along its tube. Fig. 5
(left) demonstrates this capability by comparing the nominal
case, where all rollers are functional as the robot traces a
square path, against a degraded scenario where Roller 1 is
constrained to zero motion. Fig. 5 (right) shows the effect
of this optimization on the configuration of the robot while
still tracing the same path. This constraint framework can
be extended to any number of rollers, enabling simulation of
arbitrary combinations of roller failures.
Beyond tracking roller positions, we can quantify the
kinematic impact of failures through manipulability analysis
at the robot’s tip. Using the Jacobian from Eq. 11, which
maps roller velocities to Cartesian velocities, we compute the
manipulability metric as

[26]. In 2D, this metric is proportional to the area of the
manipulability ellipse shown in Fig. 6. To quantify the spread
of this ellipse, we use the condition number of J, which is
the ratio of the major-axis length to the minor-axis length,
measuring the robot’s isotropy at the target vertex [27].
To analyze failure scenarios, we exploit the structure of the
Jacobian (Eq. 11): each column represents the contribution
of an individual roller to the robot’s task-space motion. By
zeroing the ith column, we obtain a degraded Jacobian that
models the instantaneous effect of the ith roller failing at
that configuration. Computing this degraded manipulability
metric for each roller at every time step reveals how critical
each roller is to the robot’s motion capability throughout the
trajectory. Fig. 6 presents this analysis, showing both the
time evolution of manipulability when individual rollers fail
(normalized by the maximum value during this trajectory), and
visual snapshots of the corresponding manipulability ellipses
at key time points. The overlaid ellipses provide an intuitive
geometric representation of how each roller failure degrades
the robot’s directional motion capabilities. The manipulability
index grows as the robot extends outward, reflecting the
kinematic structure’s combination of revolute- and prismatic-
type motion. However, a large manipulability index alone can
be misleading — the condition number of the Jacobian is
therefore also reported to characterize the ellipse’s isotropy. As
expected, the condition number worsens as the robot extends
further from its neutral configuration.
B. Degradation of Workspace due to Node Failure
Fig. 7 shows the effect of roller failures on the robot’s
workspace, computed by commanding the target vertex to
extend radially as far as possible in uniformly distributed
directions until the optimizer finds no valid solution [23]. As
shown in Fig. 7, individual roller failures produce asymmetric
workspace reductions, with some rollers having a substantially
greater impact than others. Single roller failures preserve at
least 69% of the nominal workspace area, demonstrating the
robot’s inherent robustness to individual actuator losses. To
show the combined effect of failed roller modules, Fig. 7
follows the greedy optimal order (4, 3, 1, 5, 6, 2), showing that
even under sequential failures, the workspace remains feasible
for many applications.

C. Rigidity Maintenance with DTCBF Constraints
Fig. 8 compares the robot’s workspace with and without
the DTCBF formulation; without it, the robot halts as soon
as σcrit reaches the minimum threshold. The DTCBF-enabled
workspace achieves a 69% increase in reachable area, con-
firming that the barrier function actively exploits null-space
motion rather than enforcing a hard rigidity cutoff. Fig. 8 also
confirms that σcrit remains within the safe region defined by
h(x) throughout, as shown with the example trajectory color-
coded by time. Therefore, this DTCBF not only maintains
rigidity, as did previous methods [23], but also enlarges the
workspace by reconfiguring the robot within the null space of
the task-space Jacobian.

optimizer. Table I demonstrates that closed-loop control con-
sistently outperforms open-loop control, improving trajectory
accuracy by at least 25% across all conditions. Closed-loop
control improves positional tracking accuracy by correcting
for imperfect onboard microcontroller velocity control. To
illustrate this, Fig. 9 (right) shows the open-loop and closed-
loop comparison when Roller 6’s DC motor is 50% slower
than the kinematic model expects. Open-loop tracking suffers,
whereas closed-loop tracking still reaches the same corner
positions as in the fully-functional scenario.
Failure-aware open-loop control also yields meaningful
gains over its unaware counterpart, confirming that explicitly
accounting for roller failures in the kinematic model compen-
sates for lost actuation. The OL-aware RMSE values in Table
I for the broken scenarios remain close to the fully-functional
OL baseline, indicating that the constraints in Eq. 10 enable
the controller to redistribute actuation and maintain tracking
performance despite the reduced actuator set.
VI. DISCUSSION
These analyses collectively inform users of this robot’s
capabilities and operational considerations. The robot’s trian-
gular modules allow assembly in many configurations for the
same task; however, factors such as the risk of roller failure
should guide users in placing more reliable hardware elements
at critical locations. Through normal operation, differences
in hardware condition may emerge over time, causing some
rollers to become more reliable than others. Forecasting which
motor failure would most impact task performance is essential
when assembling and deploying the robot. Due to the gen-
erality of the manipulability index, manipulability ellipses,
and the condition number, similar manipulability results as
Fig. 6 can be produced for any configuration of the robot.
Likewise, the workspace analysis (Fig. 7) can be extended
for any isoperimetric truss robot. Beyond which rollers fail,
the workspace and manipulability are also sensitive to where
in the trajectory they fail; the same tools used here can
be applied to analyze degraded configurations at any point
during operation. Additionally, these tools can identify which
active rollers contribute minimally to a given task and could
therefore be replaced with passive rollers without sacrificing
performance, directly informing cost-effective robot design for
specific applications.
The trajectories in Fig. 9 show imperfect motion and
increased error in the Y direction across all scenarios. The
closed-loop controller successfully widens the trajectory to
better match the desired trajectory, but the height of the
produced trajectory appears largely unaffected by closed-loop
vs open-loop control. As such, we believe that the source of
this error is the bowing in the inflated tubes. An example of
this bowing is observed in the upper triangle of Fig. 1. This
bowing has accumulated over time due to repeated use and
prolonged loading of the inflated tubes. Since the closed-loop
controller receives positional feedback via encoders, it does
not model the compliance in the tubes beyond what is captured
by the idealized rigidity model in the DTCBF. Future work
will include characterizing this deformation and accounting
for it within the closed-loop controller.
Further improvements to the closed-loop controller are
possible. The trajectory curvature shown in Fig. 9 (right) under
imperfect velocity control could be reduced by optimizing not
only distance to the goal position, but also deviation from the
desired straight-line path. This and further trajectory planning
improvements are left for future work.
VII. CONCLUSION
Continued advancements in truss robot design could pave
the way for soft, inflatable, and adaptable alternatives to rigid,

robustness of isoperimetric robots in three ways. Firstly, it has
introduced a controller that allows the robot to operate without
using a subset of its active roller modules. We measured the

DTCBF to allow the robot to avoid singular configurations.
Thirdly, we implemented closed-loop control, allowing the
robot to be robust to conditions where the individual rollers
fail to move at the commanded speed. These improvements
contribute to the development of more robust, lightweight,
and reconfigurable robotic truss systems for operation in
challenging environments.
