Abstract
Readers of applied-domain LLM capability evaluations want to know what AI systems can currently
do in medicine, law, coding, education, and scientific reasoning. The literature answers a related, but
consequentially different, question: what older, cheaper, less-elicited models could do months or years earlier
(a 2026 paper evaluating GPT-4o-mini zero-shot, say, against a frontier of reasoning-capable, tool-using
systems like GPT-5.5 Pro and Claude Opus 4.7), often reported with sparse configuration details and
abstracted upward into claims about “AI” that propagate through clinical, legal, and policy citations. We
name that distance the publication elicitation gap.
The audit is preregistered. From an OpenAlex candidate pool of 112,303 LLM-keyword-matched records
covering 2022-01-01 to 2026-04-01, n = 18,574 pass admissibility and full-paper text is retrieved on n = 4,766.
Each paper’s tested model is scored against the contemporaneous frontier on the Epoch AI Capabilities Index
(eci), reproduced under Chatbot Arena Elo and Artificial Analysis.
The gap is large and growing. The median paper evaluates a model +10.85 eci behind the contemporaneous
frontier at evaluation time (H1), a gap roughly 1.4× the Claude Sonnet 3.7 to Opus 4.5 distance1 (a comparison
crossing multiple major-version increments and a tier step within a single vendor). The gap widens at +5.53
eci/year (H2; 95% CI [+5.03, +5.83], sign invariant across imputation-window sensitivities); within-family
tier lag, when a stronger sibling was already public within ninety days of the test, runs +12.63 eci at
the median (H3). The missing methodological surface is not marginal: only 3.2% of abstracts and 21.2%
of full texts disclose reasoning-mode status on papers evaluating reasoning-capable models (H4), and the
evaluation date appears in 18.4% of full-text papers. Yet 52.5% of abstracts (95% CI [48.2, 56.9]) state
their conclusions at the level of “AI” rather than the evaluated model, with class-level framing rising at
OR = 1.23/year. Compound failure across the audit’s three dimensions (capability, elicitation, interpretive) is
9.2% of admissibility-expected papers under the primary operationalisation, 38.3% on the inclusive sensitivity
(H5); the directional signs hold under Arena Elo and Artificial Analysis (H3 null on the latter), and an
exploratory rational-lag baseline (H8) places about a quarter of the gap within the peer-review-implied
window, three quarters outside it.
The corpus-level finding is that no audited paper is answering its own question wrong, yet the published
record, in aggregate, increasingly cannot tell readers which AI it is talking about. The remedy is shared
across authors, editors, and funders: disclosure of the configuration surface (model snapshot, evaluation date,
access tier, reasoning mode and effort, tool access, scaffolding, prompting, sampling) at the methods layer,
editorial and reviewer enforcement, and funder conditioning of grants on disclosure together with API-access
budgeting that lets academic groups evaluate proximate-frontier configurations rather than only cheaper
alternatives. versio-ai v1.2 (Appendix A) is a 13-item reporting checklist with a Core 3 desk-reject tier
(model identifier, declared frame, reasoning mode), designed to extend existing AI-reporting frameworks
(CONSORT-AI, TRIPOD-LLM, DECIDE-AI, STARD-AI) at the elicitation surface those frameworks do

Introduction
1.1
The structural observation
In aggregate, the applied-domain LLM evaluation literature (medicine, law, coding, education, scientific
reasoning) gives a misleading account of what AI systems can currently do in those domains. The
result the modal paper reports is not a representative measurement of AI capability on the task it
set itself, because the system actually tested is handicapped in two ways: it uses a model from an
earlier generation or weaker tier, and it runs that model at low elicitation (a 2026 paper evaluating
GPT-4o-mini zero-shot, say, against a frontier of reasoning-capable, tool-using systems like GPT-5.5
Pro and Claude Opus 4.7). The methods section rarely fully documents either coordinate; the abstract
frames the resulting number at the level of “AI”; and the clinical, legal, and policy citations propagating
that abstract carry the generalisation further. This audit measures the pattern across a pre-registered
OpenAlex candidate pool of 112,303 LLM-keyword-matched records covering 2022-01-01 to 2026-04-01
in those five domains, inclusion-decided to n = 18,574. The median paper in that corpus tests a model
whose distance from the contemporaneous frontier is roughly 1.4× the Claude Sonnet 3.7 to Opus 4.5
gap (a comparison crossing multiple major-version increments and a tier step within a single vendor),
and the gap widens year-on-year.
Frontier-lab releases run on a weekly-to-monthly cadence; academic publication runs on cycles measured
in years. Between when a paper’s experiments are run and when its abstract appears in the citable
record, the major labs will typically have released one or two new model generations along with
elicitation modalities (reasoning toggles, tool harnesses, agentic scaffolding) that did not exist when the
paper was designed. The published result therefore describes a system the contemporaneous frontier
has moved past along axes the paper itself was not configured to test.
The gap is a composite; the audit treats its three pieces separately throughout. Temporal lag is
calendar distance from the evaluated model’s release date to the frontier on the test date. Tier lag
arises when a paper tested a sibling weaker than another sibling already public within ninety days of
the test (a GPT-4 paper running while GPT-4-turbo was public; a Claude 3 Sonnet paper alongside
Claude 3 Opus; a Gemini 1.5 Flash paper alongside 1.5 Pro). The third component, configuration
underspecification, names the elicitation surface the paper either reports or lets float (reasoning mode,
tool access, scaffolding, sampling temperature, prompt design). The corpus norm is to conflate the
three into a single number; readers compound the conflation when they transpose model-specific claims
onto “AI” at the class level.
The third of those components has the most-developed antecedents in the adjoining literature. Apollo
Research labels the model-level version of the elicitation problem the “evals gap” (Apollo Research, 2024):
the distance between what a model demonstrates and what a naive test elicits from it. Hochlehnert et al.
(2025) put numbers on the same observation for mathematical reasoning, where decoding parameters,
seeds, prompt formatting, and hardware alone reorder rankings of “state of the art.” Closer to a
structural ancestor of the present paper is Balloccu et al. (2024), which audited 255 ChatGPT-interface
studies against a taxonomy of contamination and malpractice signals, cataloguing roughly 4.7 million
benchmark samples exposed to the models in a single year. What follows here extends that direction
into an explicit capability-distance framework across five domains and four years, the magnitudes
bound to a pre-registered analysis plan.

The components compound. A 2026 paper evaluating free-tier ChatGPT zero-shot, with no tools, no
reasoning mode, and no scaffolded comparator, sits separated from the frontier a reader would meet
along every axis the frontier has opened since the evaluation was run, and the per-axis separations
interact multiplicatively. A tier-two model with reasoning off and no tools, evaluated in 2024 and cited
in 2026, describes a 2023 product to an audience that today accesses a 2026 product; gains on one
axis (extended inference-time compute, say) substitute for gains on another (multi-agent scaffolding),
so what any one axis can recover is bounded by what the others have already lost. We name the
specific form this multiplication takes (once filtered through peer review, cost-constrained API access,
and reporting norms inherited from a pre-reasoning-model era) the publication elicitation gap: the
academic-literature shadow of the elicitation gap that the Apollo/METR/AISI programme documents
at the model level. No composite score combines the three; readers weight them as their own inference
requires.

1.2
The structural reframe
These evaluations solved the problem they set, and the problem they set is rarely what their readers
concluded from the abstract. The class-level claim share (the rate at which an abstract generalises
a model-specific result to a claim about “AI”) is 52.5% (95% CI [48.2, 56.9]) on the audit’s pre-
registered Bayes-corrected estimator, and it rises across publication cohorts: per-publication-year odds

at OR = 1.23 (95% CI [1.20, 1.27]; p < 10−33), with Chatbot Arena Elo and the Artificial Analysis
intelligence index reproducing the slope on independent capability scales. Class-level interpretation is
the modal abstract’s stance on its own scope, and the stance has hardened across cohorts.
A clinical paper from the 2026 cohort, taken in composite, evaluates GPT-4o mini on diagnostic
vignettes under spare elicitation: zero-shot, no reasoning, no tools, no web search. The methods section
does not name the model snapshot or the harness. The abstract reports that “LLMs fail at clinical
diagnosis.” A weekend later, a science-section headline reads “AI fails at clinical diagnosis,” and the
citation chain into clinical, regulatory, and policy reading propagates the headline. A clinician reads
the abstract, reads the headline, and updates toward the belief that LLMs cannot do clinical diagnosis.
She is responding reasonably to the words on the page. The paper is reasonably reporting what it
tested. The sentence between them that neither side supplies is the qualifier “GPT-4o mini, reasoning
off, no tools, no web search,” and that qualifier, across the corpus, is the object of this paper.
The audit does not adjudicate any individual paper’s design, any author’s intent, or whether any specific
result would survive re-execution at the frontier. The pattern it measures is the predictable equilibrium
of three intersecting constraints: peer-review and indexing cycle times that turn the published artefact
into a record of what was practical to evaluate at submission; API access pricing under which a frontier
reasoning model with effort dialled up can cost 10–100× more per token to run an evaluation on
than a smaller or non-reasoning alternative in the same vendor family; and reporting norms codified
before reasoning dials and tool harnesses existed. Authors behaving rationally under that constraint
set produce, across the corpus, the distribution the audit documents. Singling out individual papers
adds nothing the structural description has not already supplied, and risks inviting a reader to file the
audit’s diagnosis under individual misconduct rather than under predictable system output.
1.3
What this paper does
The empirical core is a pre-registered OpenAlex candidate pool of 112,303 LLM-keyword-matched
records covering 2022-01-01 to 2026-04-01 across medicine, law, coding, education, and scientific
reasoning, inclusion-decided to n = 18,574 (Section 3.2). The primary frontier scale is the Epoch AI
Capabilities Index (eci; Epoch AI, 2024), pegged at 150 for GPT-5 (August 2025) and calibrated
across roughly 165 frontier and near-frontier models, with Chatbot Arena Elo and the Artificial
Analysis intelligence index running as sensitivity scales. The pre-registered confirmatory family is three
directional sign-tests at family-wise α = 0.05 under a Holm step-down: H1 (location) and H3 (tier lag)
reject against structural-zero nulls; H6 (valence asymmetry) returns null. Four descriptive primary
magnitudes carry simultaneous Holm-Bonferroni 95% CIs across their family: H2 (year-on-year trend),
H4 (reasoning-mode disclosure), H5 (compound-failure rate), and the class-level claim share. Each
descriptive primary is bound to a pre-registered framing map whose thesis-disconfirming falsification
bucket attaches to specific abstract-text commitments. H5 is the audit’s headline descriptive; the
gap-decomposition vector over temporal, tier, and configuration components is reported as a tertiary
transparency readout that readers may re-weight (Section 3.6).2
Two further artefacts accompany the empirical audit. versio-ai v1.2 (Appendix A) is the reporting
checklist, published as a candidate specification with a 60-day community comment period (Gringras,
2026e).

evaluations, and STARD-AI (Sounderajah et al., 2025) for diagnostic-accuracy studies; none of them

1.4
What this paper does not do
Distance from the frontier is not distance from truth. We measure structural lag at the literature level
and stop. Whether the composite case from §1.2 would still report failure under a frontier model with
reasoning on a tool-using harness is the question we are not answering here; that scale of re-execution
belongs to replication, and we leave it to follow-on work. versio-ai sits on the same fault line. The
checklist scores what authors disclosed; whether what they did not disclose, had it been elicited, would
have changed the answer is a different object. So is the question of whether any specific abstract is
wrong. The audit describes what an abstract commits to; replication takes up whether the commitment
survives re-execution.
We name no paper as a negative exemplar. The critique targets the environment those papers were
written into. NEJM AI launched its first issue in January 2024; the median manuscript in that issue
had begun submission in early 2023, when the accessible frontier was GPT-3.5 and a newly-released
GPT-4. The reasoning dial did not exist. There was no o1 to switch off. Two years later, the same
authors, the same reviewers, and the same publication cycle sit downstream of GPT-5.5 Pro, Claude
Opus 4.7, and Gemini 3.1 Pro, with reasoning toggles, tool harnesses, and budget-tier API access
available where none was before. Six papers meeting versio-ai v1.2 on a scope-bounded reading are
tabulated as positive exemplars in Discussion §5.3; the asymmetry between that named-positive list
and the unnamed-aggregate corpus is pre-registered.
Two three-part structures run through the rest of the paper. The measurement pipeline scores against
capability, elicitation, and interpretive dimensions. The analysis decomposes the gap into temporal lag,
tier lag, and configuration underspecification. The mapping between the two is asymmetric. Capability
subsumes temporal and tier into one scored dimension. Elicitation is configuration by another name.
The interpretive dimension is reader-facing: any failure on the first two should have attenuated before
the abstract was written. The H1/H3/H6 hypothesis tests depend on that asymmetry, which a single
six-cell taxonomy would obscure.

Background and related work
2.1
Evals gap and elicitation gap
A capability claim reported without its elicitation surface describes a testing configuration rather than
the model the configuration names; the modal capability paper sits in that gap. Three programmes
have made the diagnosis at the model level. Apollo Research’s “evals gap” (Apollo Research, 2024)
captures the principle that a model failing under zero-shot prompting with no tools and no scaffolding
will frequently succeed once the surface is tightened. METR has put numbers on the same observation
for agentic tasks: the capability deltas between elicited and naive configurations dwarf the deltas
5


## Page 6


between successive named model versions (METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research), 2024).
AISI’s frontier-trends programme then reframed capability evaluation as a moving target whose relevant
axis is the trajectory over versions, tiers, and elicitation conditions, with single benchmark numbers as
snapshots only (UK AI Security Institute (AISI), 2025).
The mechanism Apollo, METR, and AISI describe is a model-level one. Its academic-literature shadow
we call the publication elicitation gap: what the elicitation gap becomes once it has been routed through
peer review, cost-constrained API access, and reporting norms that never absorbed the reasoning-model
transition. The audit reports the rate at which the mechanism propagates into published capability
claims across five applied domains, alongside the rate at which the academic record fails to supply the
information a reader would need to place any given claim on the trajectory.
2.2
Measurement templates from AI safety research
The methodological template closest to this audit is CAIS’s Safetywashing (Ren et al., 2024), which
audits the gap between safety claims and the evidence underwriting them across a benchmark-aggregated
corpus, names the construct, releases code, and proposes reporting-discipline remedies. CAIS attacks
safety-claim inflation; this paper attacks capability-claim mislocation. The mechanisms rhyme. Both
turn on the gap between what a claim reads as and what the underlying evidence supports, and both
identify the reporting surface as the locus at which the gap is either closed or propagated downstream.
The corpora and remedies diverge (CAIS targets safety benchmarks and safety-claim framing; this paper
targets applied-domain capability evaluations and version-and-elicitation reporting), but versio-ai is
the reporting-standard sibling to their construct.
Bean et al. (2025) is the closest contemporaneous methodological audit at this scale, with thirty-
six co-authors reviewing 445 LLM benchmarks from leading conferences and offering eight design
recommendations for benchmark validity. The object of their audit sits one layer upstream of this
paper’s: where Bean et al. ask whether HumanEval is a valid measure of coding ability (and similarly
for the other 444 instruments they examine), the audit reported here is asking the corresponding
question about the published evaluation that uses HumanEval, namely whether the GPT-3.5 number
that publication reports tells the reader anything about what AI can currently do. versio-ai extends
Bean et al.’s benchmark-design recommendations into that publication-reporting layer.
The 2026 edition of the AI Index (Maslej et al., 2026) documents the same longitudinal pattern at
field-level resolution: the average Foundation Model Transparency Index score has fallen from 58 to 40
across the two most recent release cycles. The configuration space the literature is asked to characterise
expands faster than the reporting practice meant to characterise it can keep up. The present audit
measures the same divergence at paper-level resolution.
2.3
Prior bibliometric audits
Bibliometric audit of AI-evaluation methodology has a clinical-AI ancestor in Nagendran et al. (2020),
whose systematic review of deep-learning versus clinician-performance comparisons established the
template a rigorous capability-claim audit has to follow: systematic sampling from a named corpus,
pre-registered quality criteria, and stratified outcomes reported per domain rather than pooled into a
single headline. Ott et al. (2022) extended the bibliometric-landscape approach to broader AI-evaluation
scope; the corpus-construction strategy here inherits from theirs, extending to non-medical domains and
adding a capability-distance measurement layer that bibliometric audits have not previously attempted.
Most directly adjacent, Balloccu et al. (2024) audit 255 ChatGPT-interface studies against a taxonomy
of contamination and evaluation-malpractice signals, cataloguing roughly 4.7 million benchmark
samples exposed to the models across one year of published evaluations. Their structural template
(systematic corpus, explicit methodological coding) is the closest ancestor of the present work, extended
6

here into an explicit capability-distance framework with pre-registered decision rules. Agrawal et al.
(2025) names the “evaluation illusion” in the medical-LLM literature, close in diagnostic frame to
the H6 valence-asymmetry hypothesis but without the three-dimension decomposition the present
audit operationalises. Briggs et al. (2025) is the most direct methodological precedent for LLM-coded
article extraction at scale (a frontier-LLM extraction pipeline reconciled against a human-coded subset,
with leadership-team adjudication of disagreements, applied to 2,674 political-science articles), and
the V4F two-stage architecture here is indebted to theirs. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) establish
the reproducibility-crisis frame for ML-based science, extended here to capability-claim provenance,
which is the specific form the crisis takes when evaluators do not disclose the version-and-configuration
surface under which their claim holds.
Most contemporaneous to the present audit is Chen et al. (2026), an LLM-assisted bibliometric review
of 4,609 clinical-medicine LLM evaluations from January 2022 through September 2025 (out of 12,894
deduplicated PubMed/Embase/Scopus records, screening done with GPT-5 reasoning-high calibrated
against human reviewers at sensitivity 0.911, specificity 0.921, κ = 0.695). They tier their corpus by
study realism (prospective RCTs, real patient data, retrospective real, synthetic) and find nineteen
prospective RCTs in the corpus alongside a 65.7% concentration on ChatGPT/OpenAI models. The
corpus size is nearly parallel to the medicine slice of the present audit by accident (4,609 against 4,766),
and the methodological architecture is also nearly identical, an LLM-assisted screen calibrated to a
human-coded validation set. What the studies measure differs. Chen et al. measure the realism of the
medicine-LLM literature’s experimental setups (the headline finding is the very small prospective-RCT
count). The audit reported here measures something the Chen et al. pipeline is not configured to
surface, namely the distance between the literature’s tested-model column and the contemporaneous
frontier, the elicitation surface the methods sections do or do not record, and the framing the abstracts
apply to a per-paper result.
Ko et al. (2025) apply the MI-CLEAR-LLM checklist to 159 medical-LLM papers in top-decile journals
(November 2022 to June 2024) and find the disclosure pattern this audit also finds, which is that
the items easy to comply with are reliably reported (model version at 96.9%, training-data cutoff at
54.1%) while the items that would actually let a reader interpret the result are not (query date 50.9%,
exact prompt 49.1%, stochasticity handling 15.1%, web access 6.3%). The Ko study is the closest
single-domain precedent for the H4 and H5 disclosure measurements reported here. The present audit
extends it to five domains and an order of magnitude larger corpus, and adds three measurement layers
MI-CLEAR-LLM does not target (capability-distance to a contemporaneous frontier, within-family tier
lag, class-level conclusion framing). The one item the two studies measure on overlapping populations,
query date or evaluation date, comes in at 50.9% for Ko et al. and 18.4% for the audit’s full-text
subset, which is the direction journal-tier selection would predict given that Ko’s corpus is restricted to
top-decile journals and the audit’s is journal-tier-mixed by design.
2.4
Reporting guidelines
The reporting-guideline ecology around AI in applied domains is richer than the conversation around
AI evaluation often acknowledges, and none of its components covers the scope versio-ai targets.
CONSORT-AI (Liu et al., 2020) and SPIRIT-AI (Cruz Rivera et al., 2020) bind the reporting of clinical
trials of AI interventions and their protocols, which is interventional clinical research, not off-the-shelf
capability evaluation. TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al., 2024) and TRIPOD-LLM (Gallifant et al., 2025)
bind clinical prediction-model development and reporting (the latter explicitly LLM-oriented, with
nineteen main items and fifty subitems), and the scope is model-as-prognostic-instrument, overlapping
only partially with the modal applied-domain capability evaluation. DECIDE-AI (Vasey et al., 2022)
binds early-stage decision-support evaluations, on a tighter scope than the typical off-the-shelf evaluation
paper. STARD-AI (Sounderajah et al., 2025) binds AI diagnostic-accuracy studies, again outside the
7


## Page 8


modal capability-eval scope.
Collectively, these frameworks address neighbouring evaluation types (clinical trials, prediction-model
development, decision-support evaluation, diagnostic-accuracy testing) rather than the off-the-shelf
capability evaluation versio-ai targets. TRIPOD-LLM is the most LLM-proximate; its nineteen items
extend prediction-model reporting (development pipeline, validation, intended use) into LLM territory,
but the elicitation surface that determines what the LLM was tested under at a given moment (model
snapshot, evaluation date, reasoning mode, tool access, scaffolding, prompting strategy, sampling
parameters) is outside that scope, as it is for the others. The six fields versio-ai adds any of these
frameworks could absorb as a small extension at low committee cost. The ideal outcome for the
checklist is integration into whichever existing framework binds the relevant evaluation type, with
versio-ai as a self-contained document only for the residual class of evaluations no framework covers;
the overlap regions (e.g., a TRIPOD-LLM-bound paper that also evaluates an LLM at the elicitation
surface) cite both.
Outside the medicine-and-clinical-prediction tradition, the software-engineering research community
has converged on a parallel framework. Baltes et al. (2026) present a reporting checklist for SE research
that uses LLMs (twenty-two co-authors, eight items spanning LLM usage declaration, model versions
and configurations, tool architecture, prompts and logs, human validation, baselines, metrics, and
limitations). The constraint the Baltes checklist responds to is the constraint versio-ai responds to as
well: non-determinism in model output, opaque training-data composition, and version churn at the
cadence of weeks together threaten reproducibility wherever LLMs are the substrate of measurement.
Scope differs. Baltes et al. govern SE research that uses LLMs as a tool; versio-ai governs the
published capability-evaluation claim regardless of host discipline. The elicitation-surface overlap
between the two reads as convergent evolution under a shared problem structure rather than as a
coordination outcome.
At a higher level of generality, Kapoor et al. (2024) present REFORMS, a thirty-two-item checklist
for ML-based scientific research that draws on nineteen disciplines and appears in Science Advances.
versio-ai reads against REFORMS as a domain-specialisation move: REFORMS’ general ML-reporting
items take the specific elicitation-surface form the audit motivates (model snapshot, evaluation date,
access tier, reasoning mode and effort, tool access, scaffolding), rather than running on a parallel track.
2.5
Human comparator design literature
Human-comparator design (what counts as an adequate human baseline in a capability evaluation,
and how its absence should be reported) is treated in Wei et al. (2025) as a first-order methodological
question. The present paper cites it for the comparator-adequacy component of the interpretive-gap
dimension. A capability claim whose elicitation surface is fully specified but whose task structurally
expects a human benchmark that the paper does not include is not fully interpretable, because readers
cannot locate the claim against the professional benchmark the task implicitly invokes. Human-
comparator rigour is a necessary but insufficient condition for the capability claim to be interpretable;
the three audit dimensions jointly describe the sufficiency condition.
2.6
The niche this paper fills
Across the four literatures above, the gap this audit fills is specific. Apollo, METR, and AISI describe
the elicitation gap at the model-evaluation level. None audits the academic literature’s instantiation of it.
CAIS’s Safetywashing audits a different construct (safety-claim inflation); the methodological template
carries over, the construct does not. Prior bibliometric audits (Nagendran, Ott, Balloccu, and Agrawal)
establish the template for systematic methodological coding at corpus scale; none decomposes the
capability gap into its three mechanistic components (temporal lag, within-family tier lag, configuration
8


## Page 9


underreporting). Existing reporting guidelines (CONSORT-AI, TRIPOD-LLM, DECIDE-AI, STARD-
AI) cover adjacent evaluation types; the modal off-the-shelf capability evaluation falls outside all of
them. To our knowledge, the audit reported here is the first preregistered, cross-domain measurement
of the publication elicitation gap, decomposing it into temporal, tier, and configuration components
and pairing it with a candidate reporting checklist (versio-ai v1.2) whose adoption by authors, editors,
and funders is the proposal the diagnosis motivates.

3.1
Pre-registration
The protocol is pre-registered on the Open Science Framework (Gringras, 2026c), with a timestamp
preceding this preprint’s arXiv deposit. The manuscript carries the same analysis plan as the registered
protocol.
Pre-registration binds the confirmatory hypotheses (H1 location, H3 tier lag, H6 valence asymmetry)
under structural-zero nulls with directional-sign decision rules; the descriptive primary magnitudes
(H2 trend, H4 configuration underreporting, H5 compound failure, class-level claim share) under
framing maps whose falsification buckets are bound to specific abstract-text commitments; the multiple-
comparisons scheme (Holm step-down across the confirmatory family, Holm-Bonferroni simultaneous

3.2
Corpus construction
The source is the OpenAlex March 2026 snapshot (Priem et al., 2024), queried on the union of the
terms “large language model,” “LLM,” “GPT,” “ChatGPT,” “Claude,” “Gemini,” “PaLM,” “Llama,”
and “Mistral,” with publication dates between 2022-01-01 and 2026-04-01. The retrieval ran in two
stages: an initial query capped at 76,940 records by an OpenAlex API row limit, and an uncapped
re-run that recovered 35,363 cap-dropped records under identical query and filters (deduplication
on DOI, removal of non-English records, removal of non-peer-reviewed grey literature outside arXiv,
OSF, SSRN). The integrated 112,303-paper pool was re-classified end-to-end under V4F. OpenAlex
was chosen as the single sampling frame across all five domains in preference to the domain-specific
databases (PubMed/Embase for medicine, dblp for coding, ERIC for education) that would individually
offer higher within-domain coverage but would compromise the cross-domain comparability H1 through
H6 require.
The inclusion gate at Appendix C admits a paper when it reports an empirical evaluation of at least
one named LLM on an applied-domain task drawn from medicine, law, coding, education, or scientific
reasoning (with other retained as a descriptive residual); reports quantitative results, whether accuracy,
F1, BLEU, or a task-specific metric; carries a publication date between 2022-01-01 and 2026-04-01; and
is either peer-reviewed or deposited as a frontier-grade preprint on arXiv, OSF, or SSRN. The same gate
excludes method-development papers with no applied-domain claim (for example, prompting-technique
papers benchmarked on generic tasks), papers evaluating only in-house or unreleased models, and
duplicates resolved on DOI or on the first-author + year + primary-benchmark triple. The frozen
extraction prompt encodes the full decision tree.
Medicine has a developed reporting-guideline ecology (CONSORT-AI, SPIRIT-AI, TRIPOD-LLM,
DECIDE-AI, STARD-AI), written for a patient-safety audience that penalises under-specification.
Coding tolerates wide configuration variance because AI research’s benchmark-dense, methodologically
heterodox conventions impose almost no specification norms. Law, education, and scientific reasoning

fall between those two poles. The five domains were chosen for that heterogeneity, and the cross-domain
comparison doubles as a test of whether the publication elicitation gap is field-specific or structural.
Papers evaluating multiple models enter as paper-level records with primary_model assigned to the

3.2.1
Coverage audit
Coverage is not claimed to be complete, and the corpus’s construction as the union of title.search
against a fixed keyword list is known to miss papers whose title and abstract describe an LLM
evaluation without using any of the keyword terms (for example, a paper titled “diagnostic accuracy
of a conversational assistant for paediatric vignettes” that evaluates GPT-4 in the methods but does
not name the model in the abstract). Two OpenAlex concept topics that subsume the audit’s scope
(T11636, Natural language processing and large language models; T10181, Artificial intelligence in
healthcare) were therefore used to define a residual pool of LLM-era articles and preprints (the four-
filter intersection T11636 ∪T10181, publication date ≥2023, work_type ∈{article, preprint},
not already in the integrated 112,303-paper corpus): N = 132,899 records. A stratified random sample
of n = 9,815 from this pool was passed through the V4F two-stage production pipeline (default-effort
ai_relevance classifier →max-effort v7.2 inclusion_decision extractor); after re-attributing the
436 originally-sampled records subsequently absorbed into the integrated corpus by the post-cap
title-keyword expansion (§3.2), the residual sample’s effective denominator is n = 9,379, of which
3.58% (336/9,379; Wilson 95% CI [3.22%, 3.98%]) were classified inclusion_decision = include.
Extrapolating from the sample-level rate, the residual pool contains an estimated ∼4,761 additional
LLM-evaluation papers (95% CI [4,286, 5,287]); the implied corpus capture rate on these two topics is
approximately 80% (95% CI [77.8%, 81.3%]).
Within-corpus and residual-pool distributions agree on every load-bearing outcome. A Bonferroni-
corrected k = 18 family on the V4F-classified residual sample (n = 336 inclusion-decided) returns

correction (Appendix G).

3.5
Frontier measure
Epoch’s April-2026 capabilities snapshot (Epoch AI, 2024), committed as data/eci_scores.csv,
supplies the frontier measure: the Epoch AI Capabilities Index (eci). eci is a benchmark-aggregated
capability score anchored at 150 for GPT-5 (August 2025) and calibrated across approximately 165
frontier and near-frontier models via 1,471 benchmark × model cells. At each paper’s evaluation date
the frontier is the highest-eci model commercially or publicly accessible on that date, and eci_gap is
the difference between that frontier and the paper’s primary_model value. When evaluation date is
neither disclosed in the abstract nor recoverable from full text, the pre-registered primary specification
imputes it as max(publication_date −180 days, model_release_date); full-text-extracted explicit
eval-dates (872 of the 4,766 retrievable-PDF papers) override the imputation where present. The
180-day cross-domain lag default approximates the corpus-weighted submission-to-publication median,
and the lag-default sensitivity (Table 5) sweeps the default across {0, 90, 180, 270, 365} days plus a
domain-specific medians variant. H1, H2, and H3 all run on the imputed-anchor analysable n = 12,312
subset. Models without a direct eci entry inherit the value of their nearest same-family same-tier
sibling within ±90 days; the imputed rows are flagged and reported separately, and held out of every
test’s n-count until the imputation sensitivity (§3.7) confirms direction stability.
From 2022-01 through 2026-04, the monthly frontier trajectory records the maximum-eci model available
in each calendar month; it is committed as monthly_frontier_trajectory.csv and underlies both
the H2 trend analysis and the visualisations in §4.1. A deployment-frontier sensitivity measure runs
alongside: at each evaluation date, the highest-eci model whose per-token API price sits at or below
ten times the cheapest frontier-tier model on that date. Rebased to market pricing at every evaluation
date, this anchor co-moves with prices and underlies the H10 invariance test.
eci is primary because the next-best alternative (calendar recency in months between model release
and evaluation date) cannot handle tier differences, and tier differences are load-bearing in the corpus.
GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini released in the same calendar month but differ meaningfully in eci, as do
o1 and o1-mini, and Claude Opus 4 and Claude Opus 4.6; a calendar-only measure assigns each pair
identical frontier distance, where eci separates them. The audit reports a tertiary domain-frontier-gap
measure (highest-eci model evaluated in the same domain corpus year) as robustness alongside, with a
per-benchmark-cluster gap (task-matched benchmark subset from Epoch’s benchmark table) for papers
whose task maps cleanly onto a benchmark cluster.
No scalar capability index can represent a multidimensional capability profile without loss, and Epoch’s
12


## Page 13


own methodology documentation is explicit about it. Models specialised on a narrow domain “may
receive low eci scores, despite being very capable within their domain” (Epoch AI, 2025). Epoch
notes further that developers “can optimize for high performance on certain benchmarks,” a concern
they mitigate by running internal evaluations and drawing on independent leaderboards. Residual
training-time benchmark awareness and frontier-scale compression are limitations common to every
benchmark-aggregated index.
Adoption of eci as primary is a least-bad choice. Every audit-relevant alternative fails on at least one
load-bearing case. Calendar recency collapses tier differences, as noted. Per-benchmark matching goes
silent on applied-domain tasks whose mapping onto Epoch’s benchmark set is thin. Qualitative expert
ranking reintroduces the researcher degrees of freedom the audit is built to constrain. eci therefore
stands as the audit’s primary scalar summary for frontier distance, and Epoch’s interpretive guidance
is honoured throughout. Every reported result is a pairwise eci-gap rather than an absolute eci value,
following Epoch’s observation that “absolute eci values are meaningless by themselves, but meaningful
comparisons can be made between models.”
The audit’s reporting discipline follows from those caveats. Calendar recency runs alongside as a
companion measure (sign agreement required for any primary interpretation). The three underlying
components (temporal, tier, configuration) appear as a vector for re-weighting. Domain stratification
applies to every estimate. Appendix B reports each primary claim’s sign and magnitude dependence
on three pre-specified alternative weighting schemes.
The construct validity of eci as a frontier proxy is interrogated in Appendix B. The appendix reports
Spearman correlations between eci-gap and three pre-specified alternative weight schemes, the Pearson
correlation between eci and Chatbot Arena Elo on the overlapping-model set, the analogous correlation
against the Artificial Analysis intelligence index (AA, an independent benchmark-aggregated capability
score), and the dependence of the H1, H3, H6 confirmatory signs and the H2, H4, H5, and class-level-
claim-share descriptive magnitudes on the three alternative capability scales. No confirmatory sign or
framing-map bucket assignment is required to survive every alternative scale, and all dependencies are
reported so readers can locate scale-specific claims.
3.6
Primary outcomes and hypothesis tests
Reporting uses three granularities, with no weighted composite at any of them. The headline primary
descriptive, the compound-failure rate (CFR), is the fraction of included papers failing all three audit
dimensions simultaneously. The CFR is a descriptive proportion under specific operationalisations,
with no α-level sign-test against a structural-zero null. The primary AND-of-two operationalisation
runs conservatively on every dimension (capability uses the mean-major-generation eci jump as
the failure cutoff; elicitation requires the OR of three disclosure failures rather than any single one;
interpretive requires both comparator absence and ai_generic framing rather than either), so the
reported rate reads as a conservatively biased descriptive estimator of the latent compound-failure
share (false positives in both AND-of-two components must co-occur to falsely flag), not a point
estimate of it. Capability failure is eci_gap ≥12.0 eci, anchored to the mean major-generation eci
jump across four same-family same-tier pairs on the frozen April-2026 Epoch snapshot (Claude 3.5
Sonnet vs 3 Sonnet = 9.92; Claude Opus 4.6 vs 4 = 11.72; Claude Opus 4 vs Claude 3 Opus = 16.30;
Gemini 1.5 Pro February 2024 vs 1.0 Pro = 11.22; mean 12.29, rounded to 12.0). For elicitation, the
primary OR-of-three operationalisation triggers on any of three conditions: a reasoning-capable model
evaluated without disclosed reasoning-mode status, a tool-capable model evaluated without disclosed
tool access, or zero-shot or default-effort evaluation where a within-family scaffolded baseline existed
at evaluation date. AND-of-three is reported alongside as sensitivity. For interpretive failure, the
primary AND-of-two operationalisation triggers when no human or professional comparator is present

Confirmatory hypothesis tests
The thesis carries one anchor outcome on each of the three audit dimensions. H1 locates the capability
dimension’s eci-gap distribution (the median paper’s tested model lags the contemporaneous frontier
by approximately one major generation), and H4 measures the elicitation dimension’s disclosure rate of

reasoning-mode status, the single configuration axis that most obviously gates reasoning-era capability,
among evaluations of reasoning-capable models. The interpretive dimension is carried by the class-level
claim share, the rate at which conclusions generalise from the specific model tested to claims about “AI”
as a class; its load-bearing form is the per-publication-year trend across three pre-registered estimator
specifications and three independent capability scales. The remaining pre-registered tests (H2 widening
trend, H3 within-family tier lag, H5 compound-failure rate, H6 valence asymmetry) decompose and
stress-test the three thesis-anchor findings; all six confirmatory and descriptive hypotheses are reported
in pre-registration order below.
All three pre-registered confirmatory directional sign-tests (H1, H3, H6) are reported under the Holm
step-down scheme with family-wise α = 0.05. The measurement-error simulation on H6 uses the
dual-coder confusion matrix.
4.2.1
H1 – location of the gap
The eci-gap is positive at the corpus median. On the full production corpus with eci_gap computable
under the pre-registered §3.5 imputation policy (180-day publication-to-evaluation lag default, full-text-
extracted dates overriding imputation where available; n = 12,312), the median is +10.85 eci (IQR
[1.31, 18.28]), with one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank p < 10−300 (SciPy returns p = 0 at double precision);
the Holm-adjusted p across the confirmatory family is also below representable precision. The H1
directional sign is confirmed; the structural-zero null is rejected at post-Holm α = 0.05. The bootstrap
95% CI on the pooled median is [10.45, 11.42] eci. The magnitude is large in vendor-family terms:
+10.85 eci is roughly 1.4× the Claude Sonnet 3.7 to Opus 4.5 distance, a single-family comparison
that traverses multiple major-version increments and a tier step. Domain-stratified H1 reproduces the
rejection in every pre-registered domain (Appendix E); per-domain medians cluster between +4.65 eci
(scientific reasoning) and +14.02 eci (education) with every one-sided Wilcoxon p < 10−19, and no
domain reverses sign. The lag-default sensitivity (Appendix E, Table 5) sweeps the imputation lag
across {0, 90, 180, 270, 365} days plus a domain-specific medians variant on each of three independent
capability scales; H1 retains its sign in every cell, with the pooled median ranging from +5.61 eci
(365-day) to +16.46 eci (no-lag).
4.2.2
H2 – trend over time
The year-on-year slope of eci_gap on publication_year, fit under the pre-registered model with
domain fixed effects, domain×year interactions, and journal-clustered standard errors, does not shrink.
The n-weighted pooled across per-domain slopes is ˆβ = +5.53 eci/year (bootstrap 95% CI [+5.03,
+5.83] under journal-cluster resample; n = 11,903 across 2,328 journal clusters); per-domain slopes are
positive in every pre-registered domain (coding +3.45, education +6.93, law +4.86, medicine +5.83,
scientific reasoning +4.85, plus the catch-all other at +5.15). The pre-registered directional-sign
thesis-falsifier (ˆβ < 0 with CI excluding zero on the negative side) is not triggered; the pooled ˆβ lands
in pre-registered Zone 3 (strongly positive, ˆβ ≥5 eci/year). The model’s year main-effect coefficient
(the reference-domain “coding” slope under treatment coding) is +3.45 with cluster-on-journal 95% CI
[+2.71, +4.19] and is reported as a technical anchor (Appendix E). The lag-default sensitivity holds
the pooled slope’s positive sign across every cell on each of the three independent capability scales
(Table 5).

publication lag, cost-constrained access, and reporting norms are not keeping pace with the frontier.

4.2.4
H4 – configuration underreporting (descriptive)
Among papers evaluating reasoning-capable models, the disclosure rate of reasoning_mode status
is 3.2% (17/539 papers; Holm-Bonferroni simultaneous 95% CI [0.018, 0.055], under the primary
capability-lookup specification); the secondary specification, which codes post-freeze releases and
null-flag entries consistently with their published capability surface, gives 3.2% (22/698; raw 95% CI
[0.021, 0.047]). Both fall an order of magnitude below the pre-registered falsification bucket of ≥50%,
and reasoning-mode disclosure has not reached the threshold at which a reader can reconstruct the
paper’s elicitation surface from its methods section. Machine-readable full-paper text was retrieved for
n = 4,766 papers (25.7% of the inclusion-decided n = 18,574 corpus); within that subset, 18.4% (877
of 4,757 successfully extracted records) disclose an evaluation date in the methods section, and the
eval-date-undisclosed majority is handled under the pre-registered §11 imputation policy.
Applicability-conditioned compound disclosure on the same retrievable-PDF subset (the share of papers
disclosing every elicitation component applicable to their primary model and deployment surface) sits
at 1.18% (36/3,052; Wilson 95% CI [0.85, 1.63]; the conditioning drops the 1,710 records whose primary
models the per-(model surface) override map cannot place). Per-component conditioned rates span
prompting strategy at 71.9% down to verbosity at 3.2%, with reasoning-mode disclosure on reasoning-
capable models at 21.2% (111/524; 95% CI [17.9, 24.9]) and tool-use disclosure on tool-capable models
at 5.6%. The compound rate climbs year-on-year from 0.60% in 2023 through 0.95% (2024) and 1.20%
(2025) to 3.00% (2026); cross-domain, the spread runs from coding (2.42%) and scientific reasoning
(2.16%) at the upper end through medicine at 1.28% down to education (0/705; 95% CI [0.00, 0.54]) and
law (0/84; small-n; CI [0.00, 4.37]) at the lower end. The pre-registered H4 and H5 primary magnitudes
carry the audit’s headline; these full-paper-text rates read as secondary descriptives.
Eval-date disclosure shows strong domain and primary-model-family heterogeneity. Medicine reports
eval-date at 28% (n = 2,401) versus coding’s 6% (n = 664; logistic-regression OR = 5.9 vs. coding
reference, p < 0.001), and papers using OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google primary models disclose roughly
3–5× more often than the residual-family papers (OR = 3.08, 4.64, 4.72 vs. “other” reference, all
p < 0.001); the year-trend is null (OR = 0.95/year, p = 0.29, HC1 robust SE).
4.2.5

conservatively defined (capability uses the mean major-generation eci jump as the cutoff; interpretive
requires both comparator absence and ai_generic framing rather than either; elicitation requires
the OR of three disclosure failures). Under the primary AND-of-two interpretive operationalisation,
9.2% of admissibility-expected papers compound-fail on all three audit dimensions (817/8,868; Wilson
95% CI [8.6%, 9.8%]); the OR-of-two inclusive-alternative sensitivity bounds the rate from above

H6 – valence asymmetry
The mixed-effects model eci_gap ∼conclusion_valence + domain + year + domain:year +

4.2.7
Class-level claim share (descriptive)
The class-level claim share (conclusion_framing = ai_generic, the rate at which conclusions gen-
eralise from the specific model tested to claims about “AI” as a class) is the interpretive-dimension
descriptive anchor. Under the pre-registered Bayes-corrected estimator (§3.4) the corpus rate is 52.5%
(95% CI [48.2, 56.9] on bootstrapped marginal posteriors; the gold-anchored direct count on the same
n = 231 post-adjudication subset is 53.3%).
The audit’s interpretive claim runs on the trend over time, not on the level. Per-publication-year
odds of class-level framing rise at OR = 1.23 (95% CI [1.19, 1.27]; p < 10−33; n = 18,565) on the full
V4F-cascaded corpus, and reproduce on the eci-anchored subset at OR = 1.23 (95% CI [1.20, 1.27];
p < 10−33; n = 12,309) under the primary per-paper marginal posterior specification; the slope holds
on Chatbot Arena Elo (OR = 1.23, 95% CI [1.20, 1.27]; n = 11,532) and on the Artificial Analysis
intelligence index (OR = 1.25, 95% CI [1.21, 1.29]; n = 10,233). The full specification grid renders in
Figure 4; every cell sits at OR ≥1.20 with every p < 10−34.

5
Discussion
5.1
What we found
Median eci-gap on the audited corpus is +10.85 (H1), about one frontier generation back from the
contemporaneous release. On the 539-paper reasoning-capable subset, 3.2% disclose whether reasoning
was on at test time (H4), an order of magnitude below the pre-registered falsification floor. Class-level
conclusions, generalising to “AI” instead of the specific model the paper actually tested, appear in
52.5% of papers (95% CI [48.2, 56.9]) under the per-paper Bayes-corrected estimator (class-level claim
share, descriptive primary), rising year-over-year at OR = 1.23 (95% CI [1.20, 1.27]) on the primary
corpus, with the rising-OR pattern reproducing on each of the two independent capability scales the
audit uses as sensitivity. The modal abstract therefore characterises an artefact close to a generation
back. The methods section that would let a careful reader reconstruct what was tested is sparse on
the parameters that would matter. By the time peer review concludes, the conclusion has already
generalised past the tested artefact.
The remaining tests pull in the same direction. Per-publication-year slope on eci_gap is a pooled
ˆβ = +5.53 eci/year (n-weighted across per-domain slopes; canonical mixed model with journal cluster),
no domain reversing sign, with the literature falling behind faster than peer review absorbs. Within-
family tier lag, conditional on a stronger same-family sibling having been public within ninety days of
the test, runs +12.63 eci at the median (H3). Compound failure across all three audit dimensions is
9.2% of papers on the admissibility-expected subset under the primary AND-of-two operationalisation
and 38.3% on the inclusive sensitivity. Two independent frontier definitions, Chatbot Arena Elo and
the Artificial Analysis intelligence index, both preserve the H1 location and the H2 widening when
substituted for eci; H3’s within-family tier ordering reproduces on Arena Elo and pins to a null
median on Artificial Analysis (the modal within-family sibling difference sits below AA’s integer-grade
resolution). Lag-default sensitivity preserves the H1 and H2 sign across every imputation cell on every
scale (Table 5). The H6 valence-asymmetry test, by contrast, does not reject under V4F; the pooled
mixed-effects estimate is indistinguishable from zero (§4.2.7).
No paper in the audit is answering its own question wrong. The narrowness is elsewhere. Each audited
paper tested one or more specific named models under bounded access-tier and elicitation conditions,
and reported those results faithfully. A clinician reading the abstract reads “AI,” and a policy brief
citing the paper cites “AI,” because neither the abstract nor any single page below it supplies vocabulary
to read anything else. The audit documents how often the published literature invites that reading
across a pre-registered corpus. Whether any specific paper’s conclusion would survive re-execution on
a contemporaneous frontier model with the full elicitation surface turned on is a different question,
and a replication question rather than an audit one. Subsequent work has the question, not this paper.
A natural follow-up here is whether class-level abstract conclusions are mostly an epistemic phenomenon
(the authors did overgeneralise from one model to a class), mostly stylistic (the abstract template
encourages it regardless of what was tested), or mostly strategic (the ambiguity is calibrated to
downstream attention). The audit’s downstream-reader anchor mostly side-steps it. A clinician or
policy reader cannot recover per-paper authorial intent, and the propagation pattern through the
citation graph is identical across all three generators in any case. What the temporal trend does narrow,
however, is the convention reading specifically. The per-publication-year rise replicates at OR ≥1.20
on three independent capability scales, which would force the convention reading to attribute the rise to
25


## Page 26


journal-style-guide drift moving in synchrony with a capability landscape style guides do not measure.
The direction also runs against, rather than with, the diversifying capability landscape; as more model
tiers and reasoning modes proliferate, the epistemic case for specificity in an abstract’s claim increases,
not decreases. So whichever generator a given paper instantiates, the corpus-level pattern is what
propagates downstream, and what the reader receives is what the bibliometric construct anchors on.
The scope claim is therefore narrow. We report distance from the frontier (eci-gap) and disclosure
rates (configuration items), and nothing else. Neither is a counterfactual capability estimate, and
neither answers whether any individual paper’s conclusion would reverse under re-execution at a
contemporaneous frontier model with the full elicitation surface turned on. What the audit fixes, rather,
is the population-level fact that the academic literature presents an increasingly outdated picture of
“AI” capability in aggregate; the outdatedness then propagates into downstream consumers (regulators,
clinicians, policy staff) who treat the picture as current.
5.2
Consequences for downstream consumers
The academic AI-evaluation literature gets cited well beyond its specialty. Policy briefs aggregate
published claims, clinical procurement reports draw on published evaluations, safety-research citations
depend on academic capability measurements, governance analyses cite benchmark performance.
Educational-technology buyers, law-firm technology assessors, and journalists writing a field summary
all route through a cascade of citations whose ancestor is an academic paper. Each inherits the three
patterns the audit documents (capability distance, elicitation underreporting, and class-level framing),
whether the downstream consumer knows about the inheritance or not.
A clinician scanning a procurement abstract reads “GPT-4 fails at ECG interpretation.” A policy
staffer reads “LLMs struggle with legal reasoning.” An EdTech purchaser reads “AI shows promise in
tutoring.” Each of these is a class-level claim. None of the methodology behind any of these claims
supports the class-level framing: each paper tested one specific model on one access tier with one
elicitation configuration. And the framing is already at the class level when the claim leaves its specialty:
52.5% of abstracts under the per-paper Bayes-corrected estimator, with per-publication-year odds
rising at OR = 1.23/year. The downstream consumer does not have to misgeneralise to arrive at a
misgeneralised claim; the abstract has done it for them, and at an accelerating rate. Propagation, here,
is the abstract-layer phenomenon the rate measures. The methods section, which would let a careful
reader recover the subclass, is sparse on exactly the parameters that would matter. Reasoning-mode
disclosure runs 3.2% on the reasoning-capable subset; eval-date disclosure runs 18.4% on the full-text
subset. Even the minority of consumers who open the methods section typically find those parameters
absent. The frontierlag Python package is the per-DOI tool that the absent-parameters problem
motivates: a reader pastes a DOI and receives a three-component vector (temporal, tier, configuration),
a framing-bucket assignment, and a compound-failure decomposition, with live resolution via CrossRef
and OpenAlex for DOIs outside the corpus.
The inherited bias runs in multiple directions, and the directions depend on the consumer. A policy
brief aggregating published claims systematically understates what frontier systems can do (most
published evaluations are not at the frontier), and simultaneously overstates what deployed systems
will do (the frontier is typically not what any specific deployment uses). The two biases compound;
they do not cancel. A clinical procurement reader scanning “LLM X fails at task Y ” under-counts
frontier capability (the paper has not tested the frontier) and over-counts deployment capability when
the deployment tier is free-tier (for the symmetric reason). Legal and educational deployments tilt
towards cost-accessible tiers for legitimate reasons, so the paper’s reported capability is a lower bound
on deployment capability when deployment tier matches tested tier, and a strict underestimate of
frontier capability in any case. Direction-dependence of this sort makes the corpus-level distribution a
26


## Page 27


more reliable prior for cross-consumer reasoning than any single pooled individual-paper conclusion;
the per-paper analytically useful object is the three-component vector.
Apollo, METR, and AISI run direct elicitation programmes whose object is the gap between flagship-
as-released and flagship-with-scaffolding capability. The audit’s analogue at the publication layer is
what we call here the publication elicitation gap, and H5 quantifies it at corpus scale. Reasoning-off
flagship models and flagship-with-scaffolding models are not the same artefact, and the academic record
routinely conflates them; the conflation is what propagates into the citations safety and forecasting
work draws on. The cleanest discipline for that downstream work, where a citation to an academic
capability claim is load-bearing, is to condition on tier and configuration before importing the claim.
Contemporaneous direct evaluations (Apollo, METR, AISI, Epoch) supersede academic claims for the
same purpose where they exist. For citations that need to survive tier-and-configuration qualification,
the versio-ai-aligned subset of the academic literature is the audit-legible priority.
5.3
Positive exemplars
This audit makes a structural critique, and the §11 asymmetric-naming rule in the pre-registration
is what commits it to that. No paper is named as a negative exemplar. The cost-access constraints
and publication-cycle incentives behind the corpus-level pattern are what authors face individually
but cannot fix individually; the structural diagnosis would lose force if it were translated into a list
of named papers. Six papers meeting versio-ai v1.2 on a scope-bounded reading are tabulated in
Appendix H, each entry keyed to the checklist axis it most clearly satisfies.
5.4
Implications for editorial policy, funders, and the AI-safety ecosystem
Core 3 disclosure costs roughly five hundred characters of methods-section text, two sentences in most
papers. Item 1 names the exact model version that ran the evaluation. The capability frame the paper
claims (Item 5) is bound to be coherent with the tier the paper actually tested. Item 7, where the
model exposes one, records reasoning-mode status. The remaining versio-ai items add finer-grained
reporting where applicable, and the interpretability gain runs orders of magnitude past the cost. Once
the Core 3 are in the methods section, a clinician scanning a procurement abstract, a meta-analyst
aggregating a literature, a policy staffer drafting a brief, or a safety analyst tracking capability can
place the paper’s capability claim on the trajectory; currently the methods section does not let any of
them do that. versio-ai does not on its own shrink the corpus-level capability-distance distribution.
The +10.85 eci pooled-median lag and the +5.53 eci/year widening reflect peer-review cycle against
release cadence and the cost-access constraints between them, and disclosure does not address those.
What disclosure does address is the misinterpretation pathway from published claim to downstream
conclusion (locatability, framing-bucket assignment); the cycle that generates the distance itself routes
through the funder-side commitments below and through the structural changes to academic-evaluation
infrastructure those commitments enable.
Submission-portal adoption is the cheapest of three layers: promoting Items 1, 5, and 7 to required
fields is a one-line edit in most journal systems, mechanically the same kind of change journals already
use to enforce reporting requirements like conflict-of-interest disclosures. Reviewer guidelines can
reference the checklist using standard language the companion repository supplies. Funder conditioning
is the highest-leverage layer of the three: AI-evaluation grant reporting on the elicitation-surface items
is free against a grant budget and decisive across a cycle for the grant-funded share of the literature.
The cleanest reform path, however, is integration into existing AI-evaluation frameworks rather than a
parallel document; CONSORT-AI is a natural host (a brief elicitation extension), as are TRIPOD-LLM
(an addendum) and DECIDE-AI (a clause). A standalone versio-ai adoption pathway exists for
evaluations no existing framework binds, but the rate-limiting step on any of these routes is editorial
commitment, not document drafting. The items themselves cost roughly five hundred characters of

methods section regardless of which framework formally hosts them.
Funder-side intervention is load-bearing for a separate reason: capability evaluation at the frontier costs
money. Frontier reasoning models on capability-relevant tasks run 10–100x the per-token rate of free-tier
APIs, and the ratio is widening rather than closing as reasoning-mode and agentic harness pricing grows.
Without explicit API-access subsidy in grant budgets, the academic AI-evaluation literature converges
on an oligopoly where only well-resourced industry-adjacent labs can run capability-relevant elicitation
at scale, and independent academic groups are restricted to free-tier outdated-model evaluations whose
conclusions are increasingly disconnected from the artefacts policy and clinical readers face. NIH,
NSF, UKRI, and the major private health-research funders have an interest in not letting that divide
widen further; the audit’s +10.85 eci pooled-median gap is one of its visible downstream consequences.

Limitations
What the validation protocol leaves uncovered, in order of how load-bearing the residual is for how the
audit’s findings should be read.
6.1
Corpus-selection bias
OpenAlex is not a census. Non-English literature, grey literature, and certain conference venues are
under-represented in the snapshot the audit queries, and the under-representation plausibly concentrates
in regions where access to frontier tiers is most constrained. If that concentration is real (the coverage
audit at §3.2 estimates but does not bound it), the bias on the observed eci-gap distribution runs
in an unmodelled direction; the audit reports a within-topic figure rather than a global one. The
within-topic coverage audit in Appendix G puts the title-keyword capture rate at approximately 80%
of the LLM-evaluation papers in the two OpenAlex concept topics the audit subsumes, with the
residual-pool frontier-gap proxy statistically indistinguishable from the in-corpus subset (medians equal
at 10.3 months; Mann-Whitney p = 0.083, does not survive Bonferroni at k = 18). The surviving
Bonferroni signal is compositional: residual-pool papers preferentially name the product (“ChatGPT”)
in their titles where in-corpus papers name the API tier (“GPT-4”, “Claude-3”), consistent with the
under-specification structure the manuscript documents on the in-corpus subset. PDF retrievability
across OpenAlex is constrained by paywalls and licensing; n = 4,766/18,574 (25.7%) is the upper bound
on full-paper extraction this corpus permitted, with the abstract-only majority audited at abstract-level
resolution. The retrievable subset is not a random sample of the corpus: medicine and education
are over-represented (50.4% vs 28.4% on medicine; 17.3% vs 7.8% on education), the catch-all other
domain is under-represented (4.0% vs 49.8%), and the year distribution skews towards 2023–2024
with 2026 under-represented; the abstract-level class-level claim share, by contrast, is stable across the
retrievable / non-retrievable split (40.6% vs 42.8%), so the load-bearing interpretive-dimension finding
is invariant to the subset boundary while the configuration-disclosure rates reported on the retrievable
subset (§4.2 secondary descriptives) should be read as upper bounds on the corpus-wide rates. On the
classifier side, the validation is precision-only: the n = 300 gold-standard sample was drawn from the
classifier’s predicted-include pool, and recall against the AI-evaluation boundary is therefore estimated
rather than bound.

6.2
Extraction-pipeline concentration
V4F-Max (DeepSeek V4-Flash-Max at maximum reasoning configuration) runs both the inclusion-
classification and subjective-field-extraction stages, with two-coder human validation on the n = 300
gold standard. Cross-family agreement is reported on an n = 150 stratified subsample under both the

conclusion_framing the pre-registered analytic-correction path is engaged (§4.2.7) precisely because
the residual matters there. A tier-matched human-coder panel on n = 112,303 papers is not feasible at
this paper’s funding level, and using a frontier LLM to audit a literature of frontier-LLM evaluations is
the thematic point of the pipeline (§3.3). The cost the thematic point pays is a measurement apparatus
that shares failure modes with its object of study.
6.3
Bayes-correction transportability
The framing-field correction (§3.4) is anchored on n = 231 dual-coded gold pairs, which is a small
anchor; the residuals matter. The catch-all other residual is supported by roughly five gold pairs and
is reported uncorrected; per-domain confidence intervals overlap at modest gold n; the pre-registered
extraction prompt’s framing assignment is 88.7% stable across two temperature-zero re-runs on the
development set, adding run-level noise the bootstrap does not capture. The per-publication-year trend
survives all of that, and so do the directional signs on H1, H2, H3, and H5: the corpus rate moves
inside an eleven-percentage-point window across estimator specifications, and the trend holds in every
cell (Appendix E).
6.4
Elicitation-axis audit is partial
Six configuration items enter the audit as binary disclosure flags: reasoning mode, thinking effort, tool
use, scaffolding, multi-agent architecture, prompting strategy. Further axes where published evaluations
may systematically understate frontier capability are acknowledged but not coded. Prompting-strategy
quality at the level of in-context exemplar selection, chain-of-thought template design, and role-prompt
discipline can swing evaluated capability by magnitudes the H4 binary flag cannot reach. Decoding
parameters (temperature, top-p, max-tokens, seeds) drive swings large enough to reorder state-of-the-art
rankings, per Hochlehnert et al. (2025). Judge-model choice on LLM-as-judge evaluations carries
a capability-tier dependency of its own, and nothing in the audit traces it. The direction all three
run in is the same. The elicitation-dimension rate reported here is itself a lower bound on elicitation
deficit, and papers passing the H4 disclosure flag can still degrade evaluated capability substantially
through prompting-quality, sampling, or judge-model choices the audit does not score. The audit
measures reported elicitation conditions rather than latent capability under controlled elicitation, the
latter addressed by a separate literature on evaluation-format and scaffold sensitivity (Gringras, 2026d;
Pezeshkpour and Hruschka, 2024; Sclar et al., 2024).
6.5
Multi-model papers reduce to a single primary model
The extraction schema carries one primary_model field per paper, which forces a tie-break on papers
evaluating more than one frontier model. The per-model dyad file and the multi-model sensitivity
recover the full evaluation set at the analytic level, but paper-level aggregates use the highest-eci
model. Papers that make their strongest claim about a non-primary model are reported under a
higher-capability surface than their headline claim may describe.

6.6
Valence coding is subjective and H6 access-covariate is coarse
Valence in the H6 mixed-effects regression is a four-category LLM-coded label, validated by two human
coders at κ = 0.767. Were systematic miscoding correlated with the age of the tested model, the H6
estimate would tilt in either direction; the stratified-accuracy analysis does not reveal the pattern
on the tested cohorts, though it cannot rule out the pattern in principle. Coding accuracy aside,
the H6 mixed-effects specification omits author_affiliation_type (the pre-registered access proxy,
dropped because corpus coverage of the field fell below the pre-registered usability floor; see §3.6 and
the deviation register §6.11). On the recoverable subset where the covariate was usable, the H6 sign
and CI are insensitive to its inclusion (Appendix E). The pooled estimate is at zero, and the audit’s
interpretive-dimension claim does not depend on it. The class-level claim share (§4.2.7) and the H5
compound-failure rate carry that claim; both key on conclusion_framing rather than on valence.
6.7
H1 and H3 as presence-of-effect floors
What gives H1 substantive content is the magnitude. Median lag is +10.85 eci on the pre-registered
180-day imputation default; the sensitivity envelope runs +5.61 at the strictest cell and +16.46
at the loosest (Table 5); distribution shape is the second substantive piece. The rejection itself is
near-tautological. With n ≈12,312 papers carrying a computable eci_gap and a structural-zero
null pre-registered as the H1 floor, any non-trivial positive location passes the test mechanically. H3
inherits the same caution on its dyad-conditioned subset. Pre-registration specifies structural-zero nulls
deliberately, in order to commit each test before the data; tuning thresholds after seeing the evidence
is what the design is meant to rule out. The manuscript treats H1 and H3 as presence-of-effect floors,
on which the magnitude and distribution reporting sit. A reader looking for an effect-size claim should
look at the magnitudes and the specification curve (Appendix E); the Holm-adjusted p-values carry
only the presence-of-effect claim.
6.8
eci as single-index scalar
Any single-index frontier measure collapses a multidimensional capability profile into a scalar, and
Epoch’s own methodology documentation is forthright about the limits this leaves the index with:
narrowly specialised models “may receive low eci scores, despite being very capable within their domain,”
and the index supports only relative comparisons between models rather than standalone absolute
capability claims (§3.5, Appendix B). The audit treats the limitation as serious but not terminal. The
H1 and H2 confirmatory signs reproduce under Chatbot Arena Elo, an independent frontier definition
built on head-to-head human preference rather than benchmark aggregation, and again under the
Artificial Analysis intelligence index, an independent benchmark-aggregated alternative; H3 reproduces
under Arena Elo and returns a null tier-lag median on Artificial Analysis, where the modal within-family
sibling difference falls below the index’s integer-grade resolution. The per-publication-year framing
trend likewise reproduces on all three scales at OR ≥1.20. The three-component vector (temporal, tier,
configuration) is reported with consistent ordering, so readers who would weight those components
differently from the audit’s pooled summary can re-aggregate. No confirmatory sign is required to

than edited out. The two structural residuals (training-time benchmark awareness, frontier-scale
compression) are common to every benchmark-aggregated index, and the audit acknowledges rather
than corrects them.
6.9
Class-framing severity is binary
Class-level framing varies in severity. A generic claim made after evaluating a broad multi-frontier-
model panel is not equivalent to a generic claim made after evaluating a single weak-tier model under
sparse elicitation. The audit codes class-level framing as binary (ai_generic versus model_specific)

to preserve the pre-registered framing-map structure; severity tiers indexed on model count, family
diversity, frontier proximity at evaluation date, and declared capability frame are a candidate refinement
for follow-on work. The corpus-level 52.5% rate aggregates over severity strata and should be read as
the unweighted prevalence rather than as a uniform-severity rate.
6.10
Bibliometric construct versus individual-author claim
The audit’s primary output is a bibliometric construct: capability-claim distance from the frozen Epoch
trajectory, taken at per-paper and per-corpus scale. The construct is well-defined against eci and
the frontier trajectory, and it is not, by design, an individual-author misrepresentation claim. The
positive-exemplar asymmetry (§5.3) is the explicit design choice that preserves the distinction. The
critique targets reporting norms, cost-and-access constraints, and publication cadence (the structural
features that make the aggregate pattern predictable in advance), and does not target the judgement
or good faith of any paper’s authors. Readers who want the individual-paper reporting-surface claim
should route through frontierlag’s per-DOI audit. The substantive question of whether any specific
paper’s headline result would reverse under contemporaneous-frontier re-execution is a replication
question outside this paper’s scope, and a candidate for subsequent work. By the same scope logic, the
harm pathway from published claim to specific downstream consumer is documented at the corpus rate
(§5.2), not at the level of a tracked citation chain; tracing a chain would require naming the papers in
it, which the structural-not-individual framing forecloses.

Deviations from the pre-registered protocol

Pipeline extractor swap (gpt-5.4-mini →V4F-Max). V4F-Max replaced the pre-registered gpt-5.4-

6.12
Temporal generalisation
Frozen dataset, 2022-01 through 2026-04. Every gap distribution in the paper is dated to the freeze.
The corpus is a fourteen-quarter window rather than a continuing object, and the corpus on which
the audit’s claims will hold in 2027 depends on the resolution of an unstable race. Frontier-release
cadence sets the underlying drift: the H2 widening at +5.53 eci/year places the literature ageing at
roughly two-fifths of the frontier-cadence speed (Epoch’s monthly trajectory advances at approximately
+12.5 eci/year over the corpus window). Reporting-norm adaptation works against that drift, and the
open question versio-ai v1.2 motivates is whether journals and editorial boards converge on auditable
reporting fast enough to dampen the cadence term. Editorial-and-funder intervention runs on a longer
clock; it amounts to whether, by 2028, the modal AI-evaluation submission has become either annoying
enough or scientifically central enough to warrant a checklist at the journal level.
GPT-5.5 (OpenAI, 2026) appeared in the API on 23 April 2026 and DeepSeek’s V4 Pro open-weights
drop (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) the day after, both falling outside the 2026-04-01 corpus close. The audited
literature was already running about ten eci behind the frontier at the median; this fortnight added
two further frontier-class releases to the surface a literature published over the next year will be running
behind. None of the audit’s reported numbers move retrospectively, but the next iteration of the audit,
anchored to the same protocol, sits in expectation farther from the contemporaneous frontier than a
+5.53 eci/year linear projection from the audit’s window suggests, since that projection was fit on a
rolling-monthly cadence that this fortnight’s compression goes outside of.
Two artefacts that this paper releases run forward from the snapshot regardless of how the race resolves.
frontierlag’s quarterly trajectory updates resolve per-DOI audit against the contemporaneous
frontier rather than against the freeze-date frontier, so the per-paper read does not stale at the same
rate as the manuscript does. The 2028-Q2 follow-up audit, run on the same protocol, tests whether the
corpus-level distribution has moved under the audit’s intervention; the snapshot here cannot.

7.1
What we showed
H1 sits at +10.85 eci for the median paper’s tested model versus the contemporaneous frontier, a
distance roughly 1.4× the Claude Sonnet 3.7 to Opus 4.5 gap (which crosses multiple major-version
increments and a tier step within a single vendor family). H4 reasoning-mode disclosure on the
reasoning-capable subset is 3.2%. The per-paper Bayes-corrected class-level claim share runs 52.5%
(95% CI [48.2, 56.9]), with per-publication-year odds rising at OR = 1.23/year (95% CI [1.20, 1.27]).
Year-on-year slope on eci_gap is a pooled ˆβ = +5.53 eci/year, n-weighted across per-domain slopes
under the canonical mixed model with journal cluster, and no domain reverses sign. H3’s within-family
tier lag at the median is +12.63 eci. Compound-failure rate on the admissibility-expected subset is
9.2% under the primary AND-of-two interpretive operationalisation. Substituting Chatbot Arena Elo
or the Artificial Analysis intelligence index for eci preserves the H1 location and the H2 widening; H3’s
within-family ordering reproduces under Arena Elo and pins to a null median on Artificial Analysis,
whose integer-grade resolution does not separate the modal within-family siblings. The lag-default
sensitivity preserves the H1 and H2 sign across every imputation cell on every scale.
A clinician reading the modal abstract is reading about the model academic budgets could test, one
generation back from the contemporaneous frontier. The methods section that would let the clinician
recover what model was actually tested is sparse on the parameters that would matter, the elicitation
surface among them. The conclusion has generalised to “AI” as a class, even when the methodology

supports only the narrower claim about the specific model tested. None of those individual decisions is

7.2
What changes if the audit is acted on
Three sentences in a methods section, plus finer-grained reporting on the remaining ten versio-ai
v1.2 items where applicable, is what Core 3 compliance at the desk-reject tier costs. Item 1 names the
exact model version that ran the evaluation. The capability frame the paper claims (Item 5) is bound
to be coherent with the tier the paper actually tested. Item 7, where the model exposes one, records
reasoning-mode status. The result is a methods section dense enough that a clinician or meta-analyst
can reconstruct, in ten seconds, what the paper tested. The alternative is the current status quo, which
the audit summarises.
Three adoption layers carry the intervention together, not separately. The spec is cheap per paper;
the cost of non-adoption is paid by readers. Authors who read reporting specs voluntarily are a
self-selecting minority, and an authors-only path therefore stalls below population-level reach. The
AI-evaluation literature spans too many journals for editor-only commitment to rewrite the corpus
distribution. Even universal funder conditioning leaves out most of the corpus, since most papers in
it are not grant-tied. Uniform adoption across all three layers, on similar timescales (three to five
years on a realistic estimate), is the condition under which the corpus-level distribution moves from
unmeasurable against pre-audit to measurable.
7.3
What we are opening
Pre-registration, frontierlag package, and this paper are open; the dataset will be deposited on
Zenodo at companion launch. versio-ai v1.2 is a candidate specification: a 60-day community comment
period opens at arXiv deposit, and the item-revision protocol is committed in the companion spec. The
ideal trajectory is integration into existing AI reporting frameworks (CONSORT-AI, TRIPOD-LLM,
DECIDE-AI, STARD-AI, SPIRIT-AI extensions); the standalone document is the fallback. Replications
of the audit, forks of the codebase, domain extensions to non-English literatures, and hostile re-audits
under alternative capability scales are welcomed; the audit’s confirmatory signs are not required to
survive every such re-audit, and the places they fail will be load-bearing information for the next version
of the spec. A follow-up audit on the same protocol runs in 2028-Q2 on the same corpus-construction
rules, to test whether the pattern has moved under adoption.

Core 3 (desk-reject tier)
A paper that fails any of the following three items is not auditable as a capability claim regardless of
how the remaining items score:
• Item 1: model version, to the exact identifier the provider exposes.
• Item 5: declared capability frame (frontier / deployment / tier-specific), with the declared frame
coherent with the tier identified under Item 1.
• Item 7: reasoning mode status, where the evaluated model exposes a reasoning mode (by 2026
essentially every flagship: OpenAI’s GPT-5 series, Anthropic’s Claude 4+ extended thinking,
Google’s Gemini 2.5+ thinking, xAI’s Grok 4+ think, DeepSeek’s R-series reasoning variants).
The audit’s three dimensions (capability, interpretive, elicitation) are each instrumented at the desk-
reject layer by one Core 3 item.
Item 1 pins what was tested to a provider-exposed identifier. Because identifiers like gpt-5.4-mini
and claude-opus-4-7 are themselves unambiguous (each encodes both family and tier in the version
string), Item 4’s tier-identification function is subsumed under Item 1, and the capability dimension is
discharged by a single item at desk-reject.
Item 5 turns on coherence between the declared frame and the tier identified at Item 1. A paper testing
gpt-5.4-mini that claims a frontier frame fails the item because the claim contradicts the tier; the
same paper claiming a deployment frame passes even when the word “frontier” never appears in the
abstract.
Reasoning-mode status is the elicitation axis where most post-2024 reporting falls silent. Item 7
instruments it as the only desk-reject elicitation gate; the remaining four elicitation items (effort budget,
tool access, scaffolding, prompting) sit at full-checklist resolution and sharpen the read but do not gate
desk-reject.
The reasoning-era subset is papers evaluating reasoning-capable models, with n = 539 at the abstract
level and n = 524 at the full-text level. On pre-reasoning papers, where Item 7 has nothing to bind on,
only Items 1 and 5 are scored.

Construct validity of the Epoch Capabilities Index
H1, H2, H3, and the capability arm of H5 take eci-gap as the directly scored quantity; H6 includes
eci-gap as a covariate in a regression on conclusion_valence; H4 (denominator: reasoning-capable
papers) and the class-level claim share (denominator: included papers) are reporting-surface outcomes
that do not score against eci-gap. Pre-registration protocol §7.6 commits documenting the index’s
construction and limitations at the length their load-bearing role requires.
Construction.
Two anchors fix the scale: Claude 3.5 Sonnet at 130 and GPT-5 at 150 (Epoch AI,
2024, 2025). Every other model’s score sits against those anchors. The underlying numbers come
from Epoch’s benchmark grid across five clusters (coding, math, agentic, knowledge, writing), with
item-response theory estimating per-benchmark difficulty and per-model ability jointly. Benchmarks
are rescaled so random-guess performance maps to zero.
Each benchmark-model cell takes the highest observed score across evaluation settings (thinking effort,
inference provider, and so on); that maximum becomes the cell’s contribution to the cluster aggregate.
The audit binds to the frozen April-2026 snapshot, deposited as data/eci_scores.csv with SHA-256
hash on OSF. The per-benchmark cells live alongside as data/epoch_benchmarks.csv, available for
downstream derivation of alternative capability indices on the same grid.
Alternative-weight sensitivity.
Rank stability across alternative cluster weightings is documented
in the OSF deposit (analysis/eci_alt_weights/): the three pre-specified schemes (equal per-cluster
weights; coding-plus-math-only composite; knowledge-plus-writing-only composite) are re-scored against
the same ∼165-model frozen snapshot, and per-model Spearman’s ρ with the Epoch default ordering
is reported with 95% CIs, flagging any model whose rank under an alternative shifts by more than
five positions. The per-scheme re-rankings are committed alongside the frozen eci-scores CSV so

Failure-mode enumeration.
Three failure modes where eci is expected to mis-rank models are
documented explicitly, each with the audit’s mitigation.
(i) Narrow coding-specialists. Coding-specialist models, trained to win on the coding cluster at the
expense of broader capability, end up with misleading eci scores. The coding cluster’s contribution
gets partially absorbed into the composite, and the model’s coding-relevant capability comes out under-
expressed against a general-purpose frontier model at the same headline score. The per-benchmark-
cluster gap (protocol §5.2) handles this case: the specialist is evaluated against the coding-cluster
frontier alone, where it is actually competitive.
(ii) Domain-specialised models. A clinical, legal, or educational fine-tune typically scores low on eci
because the five Epoch clusters do not directly instrument those domains. Epoch itself flags this:
“models which are highly specialized may receive low eci scores, despite being very capable within
their domain” (Epoch AI, 2025). Mitigation is the protocol-§5.3 domain-frontier gap, which rebases
the comparison to the highest-eci model that has been evaluated on the same domain corpus.
(iii) Early reasoning-mode models. The introduction of a reasoning-mode dial to Epoch’s benchmark
suite re-scored the reasoning-sensitive benchmarks; pre-reasoning models were not uniformly re-scored
at comparable effort levels, so models indexed before the dial may have their reasoning capability
under-expressed. The configuration-elicitation index (§3.6) captures the analogous failure on the

Per-cluster gap mitigation.
The pre-registered secondary per-benchmark-cluster gap (protocol
§5.2) is the operational mitigation for eci mis-aggregation on applied-domain tasks. Within each of
Epoch’s five clusters, the frontier-at-evaluation-date model is re-computed and the cluster-specific
gap is reported against the tested model’s cluster-specific score. A law paper evaluated against the
coding-frontier composite would be mis-gapped, and the cluster-matched gap puts the law-relevant
distance alongside the composite. The two gaps are dual-reported wherever a task maps cleanly onto
one of the five clusters.
Claim scope.
eci-gap, as reported here, is the distance between the tested model and the contempo-
raneous frontier on Epoch’s published scoring; the audit’s directional and magnitude figures reproduce
against the frozen April-2026 snapshot. eci-gap does not translate one-to-one into task-specific ca-
pability deltas on individual papers’ evaluations, and the audit does not claim that it does. Five
alternative scales are exposed in the data release for substitution by readers who dispute the scalar
framing (three pre-specified weighting schemes, Arena Elo, and the Artificial Analysis intelligence
index). Confirmatory findings in the abstract are not held to survive every alternative weighting; the
audit’s commitment is to report every dependency so each scale-specific claim is locatable.

Scope-of-claim field
The conclusion_framing field (ai_generic vs model_specific) operationalises the interpretive-
failure condition for H5 and is the primary input to the class-level-claim-share descriptive. It replaces
an earlier design in which downstream analysis would have used valence == negative as a proxy, a
proxy that confounded valence (direction) with framing (scope). The field is coded through a linguistic
pattern match (“generic-subject test”) refined by six borderline-case disambiguation rules below, each
addressing an empirically observed failure pattern.
Borderline-case disambiguation rules
1. Determiner-headed collectives are anaphoric, not generic. “All systems exhibit X” with
prior named models is an anaphoric reference; code as model_specific. Contrast with bare plural
“LLMs exhibit X” as generic.
2. Modifier-bounded generic terms remain generic. “Commercial LLMs,” “open-source LLMs,”
“reasoning-capable LLMs” are still generic subjects; code as ai_generic.
3. Hedged-generic constructions keep the generic term as subject. “LLMs like ChatGPT-4,”
“AI tools such as Claude” are generic subjects with an illustrative modifier; code as ai_generic.
4. Definite-specifier singulars are specific. “The LLM tested,” “the evaluated system” refer to
the tested instance; code as model_specific.
5. Forward-projection and implication sentences with generic subjects count as findings.
“AI could become. . . ,” “LLMs may be ready. . . ,” and implication sentences with generic subjects
trigger ai_generic.
6. Category descriptors vs named artefacts.
“LLM-based methods” is a class-level claim
(ai_generic) unless the subject is a named artefact (“LogReader,” “our RAG pipeline”), which is
model_specific.

E
Specification curve and permutation-based null
The specification curve enumerates every pre-registered reasonable analytic specification for the audit’s
primary outcomes and plots the distribution of estimated effect sizes across specifications. For each
confirmatory hypothesis (H1, H3, H6) and each descriptive magnitude (H2 slope ˆβ, H4 disclosure rate,
H5 compound-failure rate, class-level claim share), the curve reports the observed point estimate with
95% CI under every pre-specified combination of analytic choices; the permutation-based null draws
1,000 resamples under the sharp null for each cell and reports the proportion of specifications whose
observed estimate falls outside the permutation reference distribution at α = 0.05.
Specification dimensions
Eight pre-registered axes parametrise the Cartesian product the curve sweeps:
• Inclusion. Primary classifier inclusion_decision = include, against a manual-override arm in
which every borderline case is re-adjudicated by hand.
• Valence encoding. Four-category quaternary (negative / mixed / neutral / positive), against a
numeric-linear collapse.
• Missing-configuration handling. Nulls counted as disclosure failures (null-as-undisclosed), against
nulls dropped from the denominator (null-as-missing).
• Model-age stratification. Pooled, against per-cohort (pre-2023 / 2023 / 2024 / 2025+).
• Standard errors. Journal-clustered, against robust non-clustered.
• H5 capability-failure threshold. τ ∈{8, 10, 12, 15, 20} eci.
• Interpretive operationalisation. AND-of-two primary, against the OR-of-two inclusive-alternative.
• Admissibility. The admissibility-expected subset is the pre-registered primary (n = 8,868 on V4F-
cascaded production extraction under canonical 180-day eval-date imputation; the gpt-mini-era
pre-reg figure of 9,260 was replaced post-swap), against a sensitivity arm that restricts the full
corpus to decidable compound-failure status.

Four outcome families enter the comparison: conclusion valence (four-way categorical), conclusion

G.3
Bonferroni correction and survivors
The pre-comparison test family carries k = 18 entries: four valence-category z-tests, two framing z-tests,
six z-tests on the top-twelve primary-model cells with in-corpus share ≥1% (chatgpt, gpt-4, gpt-4o,
claude-3, gemini, unspecified), four frontier-gap quantile statistics (median, mean, p25, p75, with the
location contrast picked up by the Mann-Whitney U), one eval_date-disclosure z-test, and the two-
proportion z-test on the overall inclusion rate. Bonferroni αfamily = 0.05 implies a per-test α = 0.0028.
Five tests survive: the two-proportion z-test on inclusion rate (p ≈0), and four primary-model cell
shifts (chatgpt at p < 10−4, gpt-4 at p = 0.0002, claude-3 at p = 0.0016, unspecified at p = 0.0007).
The Mann-Whitney U on the frontier-gap proxy (p = 0.083), the omnibus χ2(3) = 9.01 on valence
composition (p = 0.029), and the framing χ2(1) = 7.56 (p = 0.006) do not survive correction at k = 18.
The eval_date-disclosure shift (−0.6pp, p = 0.51) is a near-null. The remaining primary-model cells
(gpt-4o, gemini, gpt-5, gpt-3.5) shift modestly and individually fail Bonferroni.
G.4
Representativeness bounds and reading the audit against them
First, the corpus is statistically representative of T11636 ∪T10181 on the primary frontier-lag outcome.
Residual and in-corpus frontier-gap proxy distributions match at the median (10.3 months) and do not
differ at the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (Mann-Whitney p = 0.083). The audit’s +10.85 eci median
gap and pooled H2 slope of +5.53 eci/year characterise the topic universe at the ∼80% capture rate.
Second, the surviving Bonferroni signal is compositional. Residual papers over-represent product-level
tokens (ChatGPT, unspecified) by roughly 17pp combined and under-represent API-tier tokens (gpt-4,
claude-3) by roughly 10. The pattern reflects the title-keyword query’s mechanical bias: papers that
version-tag in the title are over-captured; papers using product-level terminology are under-captured.
The shift does not propagate to the audit’s substantive outcomes; the conclusion-framing point estimate
runs in the opposite sense (residual ai_generic 34.6% vs in-corpus 42.3%) but does not survive
Bonferroni at k = 18, and neither does the valence shift.
Third, the H6 valence-asymmetry specification runs on a corpus whose valence composition differs from
the implied topic-level distribution by the modest shifts reported in §G.2 (none Bonferroni-significant).
Pooled H6 results are robust to the residual-pool composition under the V4F-anchored audit; the prior
pre-pivot reading flagged a Bonferroni-survivor valence shift, but that reading does not reproduce
under the production-pipeline classifier and is no longer load-bearing.
