ABSTRACT 
 
The terms responsive, adaptive and intelligent are widely used to describe soft-matter but are 
inconsistently defined. This paper formulates these as information channels of increasing architectural 
complexity: a memoryless map p(y|x) (responsive), a state-conditioned map p(y|x,s) (adaptive), and a 
feedback-modified channel p(yₜ | xₜ, Xt-1, Yt-1) (intelligent). Existing complexity metrics needed for 
comparisons across material classes fail at least one of four requirements: dimensional consistency, 
common reference, thermodynamic coupling and scale-bridging. Three information-theoretic complexity 
metrics are proposed: configurational diversity I₁, Hazen functional selectivity I₂ and stimulus–response 
information transfer I₃. Treating the material itself as the information channel leads to a key complexity–
function relationship: increasing internal complexity raises potential information capacity while 
simultaneously increasing attenuation and dissipation. This yields an approximate thermodynamic scaling 
ceiling and an optimal internal complexity N* governed by transmission efficiency, stimulus energy and 
thermal noise (a Carnot-analogue ceiling). A benchmarking framework is proposed to enable comparison 
of synthetic soft matter systems, biological systems and hard-matter architectures using common 
information metric coordinates. Ten representative systems are mapped on the volumetric rate (I₃/V) – 
power density (P) plane. They broadly separate into four bands above the Landauer floor: 10¹⁸–10²⁰× for 
soft matter and shape-memory alloys, 10¹⁰–10¹⁶× for silicon digital and electromechanical, 10⁹–10¹⁰× for 
memristor neuromorphic, and 10⁵–10⁸× for evolved biology (all subject to at least an order of magnitude 
uncertainty due to the assumptions made in their estimation). The mechanistic origin of the gap between 
synthetic soft matter and biology is the per-element substrate energy scale (estimated at 1–10 kʙT vs 10⁴–
10⁵ kʙT). Three architectural routes: feedback, multi-channel orthogonality and molecular memory, are 
proposed as ways in which soft matter might populate this gap. 
 
 
Keywords – adaptive soft matter, intelligent soft matter, complexity metrics, design space, information 
theory.

1. Introduction 
Three classes of stimulus-coupled matter are usually distinguished in the soft-matter literature: 
responsive, adaptive and intelligent. The distinctions are widely used but inconsistently defined and rarely 
operationalised. The aim of this paper is to propose a more precise common formulation as information 
channels of increasing complexity, to assess whether existing complexity metrics support that 
formulation, and to infer from this a tentative quantitative design framework. 
The perspective adopted throughout this paper is that when a material responds to a stimulus X with a 
response Y, it is acting as an information channel. The functional behaviour of this information channel 
becomes the input–output map characterised by the mutual information I(X;Y), measured in bits. This 
subject is well-defined for any class of stimulus-coupled matter and is the natural route to a 
thermodynamic coupling to power density through Landauer’s principle. The information-theoretical 
framework proposed here addresses three coupled questions. (i) For a given internal architecture, what 
sets the upper bound on I(X;Y)? (ii) What determines the fraction of that upper bound that is realised 
under operating conditions? (iii) How do both scale with internal complexity? These questions are coupled 
because internal complexity raises the upper bound but degrades transmission through the same physical 
object.  
2. The responsive–adaptive–intelligent continuum as a hierarchy of channels 
The three classes can be distinguished by the conditioning structure of the input–output channel kernel 
p(y | x, ·).  
2.1 Responsive matter: a fixed memoryless channel 
A responsive material implements a time-invariant, history-independent map from X to Y. The channel 
kernel is p(y | x) and the functional information content (in bits) is 
 I(X;Y) = H(Y) − H(Y | X) .  (1) 
A poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM) hydrogel below its LCST is the canonical example: temperature 
X maps to swelling state Y through a kernel determined by the polymer chemistry and crosslink density 
and unchanged by the system’s prior history [1]. The channel is essentially memoryless on the timescales 
over which the material is operated. 
For a responsive channel the upper bound on I(X;Y) is the channel capacity C = maxp(x) I(X;Y) [2]. 
2.2 Adaptive matter: a state-conditioned channel 
An adaptive material’s input–output kernel depends on an internal state S that itself depends on the 
history of inputs. The channel is 
 p(y | x, s),  s = s(Xt−1),

with the functional metric 
 I(X;Y | S) = H(Y|S) − H(Y | X, S).  (2) 
I(X;Y) is not the natural measure because it averages over the internal-state distribution and conflates the 
channel itself with a random variable. A glucose-responsive insulin-release matrix is adaptive in this sense: 
the release kernel depends on the polymer’s current swelling state and bound-glucose loading, which are 
themselves set by the recent glucose history. The channel is no longer time-invariant but it is still passive: 
the kernel adjusts in response to inputs but does not modify itself in response to outputs. 
2.3 Intelligent matter: a feedback-modified channel with memory 
The use of ‘intelligent’ in this paper is an operational engineering definition rather than a cognitive 
definition of intelligence. An intelligent material’s channel kernel depends not only on the history of inputs 
but on the history of its own outputs as Y is fed back to the channel. Formally 
 p(yt | xt, Xt−1, Yt−1).  (3) 
Three information-theoretic objects characterise such a channel. 
(i) Conditional mutual information I(Xt; Yt | Xt−1, Yt−1), giving the per-step novel information transferred 
from input to output given the history. 
(ii) Transfer entropy [3], 
 TX→Y = I(Xt−1; Yt | Yt−1) = H(Yt | Yt−1) − H(Yt | Yt−1, Xt−1).  (4) 
TX→Y is the directed information rate from X to Y, isolating the causal contribution of X beyond what Y 
could predict from its own past. The data processing inequality [4] shows TX→Y ≤ I(Xt−1; Yt), with inequality 
when Y has internal memory. 
(iii) Predictive information [5], 
 Ipred(T) = I(pastT; futureT),  (5) 
the mutual information between past and future segments of the output stream. Ipred(T) grows as log T 
for systems with a finite-parameter internal model and as a fractional power of T for non-parametric ones. 
This growth law is a model-free signature of the complexity of the dynamics implementing the feedback. 
The thermodynamic significance of (3) is that feedback turns mutual information into a thermodynamic 
resource. Sagawa and Ueda [6] showed that the second law of thermodynamics generalises to 
 ⟨W⟩ ≥ ΔF − kB T (ln 2) ⟨I⟩ ,  (6) 
where ⟨I⟩ is the average mutual information acquired by the feedback controller. The feedback-modified 
channel of an intelligent material is therefore not just statistically different from the adaptive case, it has 
a different thermodynamic budget.

No demonstrated synthetic soft-matter system currently sits unambiguously in the intelligent class. Living 
systems do: bacterial chemotaxis, neural circuits and regulated metabolism implement (3) and have been 
characterised in the appropriate information-theoretical terms [7]. 
3. Existing complexity metrics 
A complexity metric usable across the responsive–adaptive–intelligent continuum needs to satisfy four 
requirements. (R1) Dimensional consistency: a single set of units in which all classes can be expressed. 
(R2) Common reference: a definition of “no complexity” and “maximum complexity” that survives the 
move between classes. (R3) Thermodynamic coupling: a route to absolute units (joules, watts) so the 
metric can be benchmarked against fundamental limits. (R4) Scale-bridging: definability at the molecular, 
network and macroscopic scales without rederivation. The following assesses the dominant existing 
metrics against these requirements. 
3.1 Molecular-scale structural indices 
The Bertz index [8] and the Böttcher additive index [9] are graph-theoretic measures of bond-topology 
and stereochemistry information in a single molecule. The molecular assembly index MA [10] counts the 
minimum number of bond-forming steps required to build the molecule from atoms and is uniquely 
experimentally measurable through MS/MS fragmentation. 
These metrics satisfy R1 (in bits, for the entropy-based variants) and partially R2, but fail R3 and R4. There 
is no general thermodynamic coupling for a Bertz or Böttcher score; one cannot infer the dissipation rate 
of a responsive system from the structural complexity of its constituent molecules. They describe a 
precondition for function (i.e. the chemistry has to exist) but not function itself. 
3.2 Network-scale invariants 
Chemical reaction network theory (CRNT) provides the stoichiometric rank s, deficiency δ and cyclomatic 
number γ as algebraic invariants of the reaction graph [11]. These quantities give exact necessary 
conditions for multistationarity and oscillation (the Deficiency Zero Theorem) but they are integer-valued 
and not expressible in bits, failing R1. They also have no inherent thermodynamic coupling: a network 
with δ = 1 can be either close to equilibrium (low dissipation) or far from it (high dissipation), and the 
invariants do not distinguish the cases. 
3.3 Dynamical-scale measures 
The Lyapunov spectrum, the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy hKS = Σi λi+ (in bits·s−1) and the Kaplan–Yorke 
dimension DKY are the established characterisations of attractor dynamics. The KS entropy is the closest 
existing metric to an information-processing rate and is dimensionally compatible with the Landauer 
coupling (R3). However, it measures the rate of information generation by the intrinsic dynamics of the 
system, not the rate of information transfer from a stimulus to a response. It is therefore mismatched to 
the channel-centred formulation. A material with high hKS can be a noisy non-responder; a material with 
low hKS can be a highly informative deterministic switch.

3.4 Compositional complexity measures 
The effective complexity of Gell-Mann and Lloyd [12] and the statistical complexity Cμ of Crutchfield and 
Young [13] both attempt to extract the regularity component of an entropy budget. This is the part that 
is attributable to structure rather than randomness. Both satisfy R1 (bits) and R2 (vanishing at both 
entropy extremes). However, neither has a general thermodynamic coupling (R3 partial), and neither is 
operationally easy to compute for stimulus–response channels (R4 partial). They are useful diagnostically 
but not as design metrics. 
3.5 The count-axis (“D”) collapse 
A simplistic metric that is useful for differentiating systems at an order-of-magnitude level is to write D = 
log2(count of distinguishable functional elements), with elements ranging from operational states of a 
hydrogel (D ≈ 7–10) through proteins of a mitochondrion (D ≈ 27) to cells of a human (D ≈ 47). The count 
is intuitively useful as a zeroth-order positioning axis: it captures the order-of-magnitude separation 
between soft-matter, organelle and organismal complexity. But D is a composite that conflates three 
statistically distinct properties: the empirical occupancy of the state space, the rarity of functional 
configurations within the chemical design space, and the rate of information flow through the system. 
The conflation is structural, not just a question of normalisation: the three quantities live on different 
domains (a probability distribution over states, a function-quality landscape over the design space, and a 
channel kernel between two random variables, respectively) and there is no canonical inner product on 
the space they span. 
4. The (I₁, I₂, I₃) complexity formulation 
This formulation replaces D with three information-theoretically distinct primitives, each in bits, each with 
explicit boundary behaviour and each attached to a different design question. The derivations follow 
Shannon [2], Cover and Thomas [4] and the functional-information construction of Hazen, Griffin, 
Carothers and Szostak [14]. 
4.1 Setup 
Let 𝒮 be the set of microstates accessible to the material under specified macroscopic constraints. Three 
choices fix the downstream measures: the cardinality |𝒮| = N (or the coarse-grained phase-space volume 
for continuous systems), the empirical occupancy p on 𝒮, and a reference distribution u, usually uniform 
with ui = 1/N. The maximum-entropy reference is 
 Hmax = log2 N,  H(p) ≤ Hmax, equality iff p = u. 
The identity DKL(p ‖ u) = Hmax − H(p) [15] shows that the entropy deficit is itself a Kullback–Leibler 
divergence. For continuous state spaces Hmax is replaced by log2(Vol/Δd) at coarse-graining resolution Δ; 
differences in H and all KL divergences are resolution-invariant, which is the property that permits cross-
class comparison.

4.2 I₁: configurational diversity 
 I1 ≡ H(p) = −Σi pi log2 pi  [bits].  (7) 
I1 is the Shannon entropy of the empirical microstate occupancy. It measures the bits of uncertainty about 
which microstate the material occupies. The boundary behaviour is: I1 → 0 for a material locked in a single 
state; I1 → Hmax for fully randomised occupancy; I1 = 1 bit for a clean two-state switch with equal 
populations. I1 is the irreducible Shannon primitive. Every other Shannon-derived quantity decomposes 
into entropies of marginal and joint distributions. 
The discriminative power of I1 for cross-class comparison depends on the coarse-graining at which the 
microstates are defined. At the level of operational macrostates (swollen/collapsed, bound/unbound) all 
classes of system score I1 in the range 1–4 bits; at the level of per-chain or per-protein conformational 
entropy the same systems separate by several decades. The choice of coarse-graining is therefore part of 
the metric specification. 
4.3 I₂: functional selectivity 
 I2 ≡ −log2 F(E*)  [bits],  (8) 
where F(E*) = M(E*)/N is the random-search probability that a uniformly drawn configuration satisfies the 
functional threshold φ(s) ≥ E* [14]. I2 is the number of bits needed to specify any configuration meeting 
the threshold from the design space; it measures the rarity of functional configurations and is 
independent of the empirical occupancy p. The boundary behaviour is: I2 = 0 when function is generic 
(every configuration is functional, F = 1); I2 → log2 N when the functional configuration is essentially 
unique. I2 is the natural axis for inverse design — every additional bit is an additional bit of designed 
specificity. Hazen et al. reported I₂ ≈ 30–80 bits for biological RNA aptamers [14]. Comparable values for 
synthetic stimulus-responsive polymers have not been systematically tabulated; order-of-magnitude 
estimates follow from setting F(E*) equal to the fraction of a stated reference chemistry space whose 
members exhibit the target response above a useful threshold. For PNIPAM, F ≈ 10⁻³–10⁻⁵ within the 
plausible vinyl/acrylamide design space (E* = sharp aqueous LCST in the 5–50 °C window) gives I₂ ≈ 13 ± 4 
bits; for phenylboronate glucose-responsive matrices, F ≈ 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁷ within the aqueous-stable boronate–
diol crosslinking space (E* = cycle-stable swelling at physiological glucose concentrations) gives I₂ ≈ 20 ± 3 
bits. Both estimates are limited by the absence of community-agreed conventions for the reference space 
and threshold. 
4.4 I₃: input–output information transfer 
For a responsive system I3 ≡ I(X;Y); for an adaptive system I3 ≡ I(X;Y | S); for an intelligent system I3 ≡ TX→Y, 
the transfer entropy (eqn. 4). In all three cases I3 has units of bits per channel use and a rate version İ3 = 
I3 · ν in bits·s−1, where ν is the operational cycling frequency. 
The boundary behaviour is: I3 = 0 for a non-responsive material; I3 → min{H(X), H(Y)} for a deterministic, 
perfectly distinguishable response. I3 is bounded above by the channel capacity C of the underlying

physical channel and below by the data processing inequality once it has been transmitted through 
internal elements. 
The rate İ3 is the only one of the three primitives that admits a volumetric form İ3/V in bits·s−1·m−3, which

4.5 Relation to the count axis D 
Writing D = log2 N recovers Hmax, so the count axis is the maximum-entropy ceiling of I1. At fine-grained 
coarse-graining (molecules, proteins, cells) it is approximately I1 itself, which is why the D axis is 
approximately monotone in observed cross-class data. But because D is a ceiling and not a measure of 
how that ceiling is occupied, it is silent on I2 and I3: a frozen system and a randomised system with the 
same N have the same D but very different functional content. The triple (I1, I2, I3) recovers the cross-class 
ordering of D at zeroth order and resolves the degeneracies that D cannot. 
4.6 The three primitives are not algebraically reducible 
I1 is a property of the empirical microstate distribution p. I2 is a property of the function-quality landscape 
φ over the design space, independent of p. I3 is a property of the channel kernel p(y | x, ·), which is 
independent of both p and φ. The three exist on different domains and answer different design questions: 
what does the material occupy, how rare is the function, and how much stimulus information is written 
into the response. There is no canonical inner product on the joint space and no information-theoretic 
justification for a weighted sum α1 I1 + α2 I2 + α3 I3. 
4.7 What each primitive suggests as design-limiting 
In a responsive system the design-limiting primitive is I3: the question is how much stimulus information 
the material’s static channel can transfer to its response, and this is decoupled from how it explores its 
state space. In an adaptive system I1 becomes co-limiting with I3: the internal state S must occupy a 
sufficient region of state space to support the conditional channel of eqn. (2). In an intelligent system all 
three are co-limiting — I3 for the per-step channel, I1 for the state space supporting the feedback memory, 
and I2 for the rarity of architectures that maintain feedback stability without losing responsiveness. This 
is the content of the qualitative observation that intelligence requires more than just sensitivity. 
5. The non-monotonic complexity–function relation and a Carnot-analogue 
ceiling 
The questions of Section 1 (what bounds I(X;Y), what fraction is realisable, and how both scale with 
internal complexity) can in principle be answered quantitatively when the channel is the material itself. 
5.1 The channel-is-material constraint 
In classical channel-capacity theory the channel is an external object whose properties (bandwidth, noise 
floor) are specified independently of the message being transmitted. For a material implementing eqns.


## Page 8


(1)–(3) this separation fails: the channel kernel and the physical substrate are the same object. An increase 
in internal complexity (more coupled molecular degrees of freedom, deeper cascade of intermediate 
elements) raises the upper bound on the information that can in principle be transferred, but it also 
introduces additional sources of noise and dissipation through the same elements. The two effects are 
opposed. 
5.2 Linear bound on the upper limit 
For a material with N coupled internal elements, the upper bound on I(X;Y) scales at most linearly in N. 
Each element contributes at most c bits of capacity (set by its local signal-to-noise ratio); cascading or 
parallelising N such elements with statistically independent noise gives Imax ≤ N c. This bound is tight only 
when the elements are statistically independent, which the channel-is-material constraint generally 
precludes. 
5.3 Cascade attenuation 
Consider a cascade of n weakly coupled elements with a uniform per-step transmission efficiency p 
(fraction of input information passed to the next element). The data processing inequality gives 
 I(X;Yn) ≤ pn · I(X;Y0).  (9) 
The signal energy after n stages is roughly Esignal · pn. When this falls below kB T, the downstream element 
cannot distinguish signal from thermal noise and is decoupled from the input. Setting Esignal · pn* = kB T 
gives the critical depth 
 n* ≈ ln(Estimulus / kB T) / ln(1/p).  (10) 
Beyond n* further elements add no information transfer but continue to add dissipation. Measurements 
of TNF signalling [16] show end-to-end mutual information of ~1 bit through cascades long enough that 
this attenuation is mechanistically observable, and quantitatively consistent with eqn. (9) under 
physiological noise conditions.  
5.4 The optimum N* and a Carnot-analogue ceiling 
The two effects together produce a non-monotonic dependence of realised I(X;Y) on N: 
 Irealised(N) ≈ min{Nc, pN−1 · Iinput},  (11) 
The optimum N (=N* )of Eqn (11) coincides with n* of Eqn (10) when the per-element capacity is of order 
kBT per stage. This is a thermodynamic design rule: it specifies the optimal internal complexity of an 
adaptive soft-matter channel as a function of the per-step coupling efficiency, the stimulus magnitude 
and the operating temperature. It is an information-theoretic scaling ceiling analogous in role to Carnot 
efficiency in thermal machines: a theoretical maximum that real systems can approach but not exceed 
without violating the underlying inequalities.

5.5 Thermodynamic coupling 
The energetic cost of operating an adaptive channel is bounded below by Landauer's principle [17], 
generalised to non-equilibrium steady states by stochastic thermodynamics [18], and assessed for 
information-processing systems by [19]: 
 σ ≥ kB (ln 2) · I3,  (13) 
where σ is the entropy production rate. Saturation of the bound has been demonstrated experimentally 
to within a few percent in the quasi-static limit [20]. For an intelligent material with feedback, eqn. (6) of 
Sagawa and Ueda modifies the budget: the mutual information acquired through feedback ⟨I⟩ reduces the 
minimum work required to achieve a given state change, by up to kB T (ln 2) per cycle. 
Combining (12) and (13) yields the maximum stimulus-to-response information rate achievable at internal 
complexity N*, per unit volume V, at operating temperature T, is bounded above by the volumetric power

5.6 What the ceiling implies for design 
Eqns. (10)–(13) imply three potential design rules for adaptive soft matter that are not generally 
represented in the empirical soft-matter literature. 
(i) There is a finite optimal internal complexity. Adding more responsive elements beyond N* does not 
improve function and incurs dissipation. The widespread assumption that more chemistry is better is not 
supported by the channel analysis. 
(ii) The optimal complexity depends on stimulus magnitude. A system designed for a strong stimulus 
(Estimulus ≫ kB T) can tolerate a deeper cascade than one designed for a weak stimulus. The same molecular 
architecture is therefore not optimal for both regimes. 
(iii) Feedback changes the budget. An intelligent material exploits eqn. (6) and operates closer to the 
Landauer floor than a non-feedback adaptive material with the same I3. The conversion of responsive to 
intelligent function therefore promises both a thermodynamic and a functional gain — at the cost of the 
architectural complexity required to implement the feedback path. 
The dimensional matching of I3/V to a volumetric power density gives a single thermodynamically 
anchored plane on which any stimulus-coupled material (soft or hard, synthetic or evolved) can be 
located. The irreducibility of I1 and I2 to I3 requires a companion static plane to retain the structural 
information that the dynamic plane discards.  
6. The two benchmarking planes 
The reference class for “hard” systems comprises digital silicon (CMOS SoC, GPU), neuromorphic analogue 
arrays (memristor crossbars) and electromechanical actuators (MEMS, piezoelectric, shape-memory 
alloy). Each subclass highlights a different relationship between the three information primitives and the 
volumetric power density, and the asymmetries among them are themselves design information.

6.3 Scope and limitations of the framework 
Two limitations are explicit. First, I3 is defined relative to an identified stimulus–response channel. For 
systems where the natural function is internal energy conversion rather than stimulus response 
(mitochondria are the canonical case, but also Li-ion cells), I3 as defined is uninformative; such systems 
live primarily on Plane B. Second, all numerical placements below carry uncertainty of typically ±1–2 
decades on each axis; the qualitative ordering is robust but the absolute placements are not.

8. Mechanistic interpretation of a Landauer-ratio clustering 
Under the assumptions made in the theoretical framework and given the estimated quantities above, four 
bands emerge on the dynamic plane. Comparison across bands is informative as an indication of how far 
each system sits above its own Landauer floor, but the band membership of a system does not directly 
translate into stimulus-response efficiency. For example, hard digital systems in Band 2 dissipate ~10¹⁰× 
Landauer to perform internal computation, while E. coli in Band 4 dissipates ~10⁸× Landauer to transduce 
an external stimulus. These are not the same kind of efficiency. 
Band 1 — soft matter and SMA: 10¹⁸–10²⁰ × Landauer. PNIPAM, the glucose matrix and the Nitinol 
actuator cluster within one decade of each other on Landauer ratio despite very different chemistry. The 
convergence is not coincidence. Any responsive event involving a 1–10 kJ·mol−1 molecular reorganisation 
per repeat unit, repeated over ~1022–1023 units per cubic centimetre, dissipates a substantial fraction 
(typically 10–30 %) of the reorganisation enthalpy as heat per cycle. The Landauer ratio is set by the 
molecular-energy scale per element multiplied by the volumetric density of responding units, divided by 
the bit rate per element. For all soft-matter and thermal-actuator systems this ratio collapses to roughly 
1019. This is a generic result for molecular condensed-phase responsiveness. 
Band 2 — MEMS, BLE digital, piezoelectric, GPU: 10¹⁰–10¹⁶ × Landauer. These systems share a silicon-
substrate switching architecture. The per-element switching energy is ~10−14–10−13 J at modern CMOS 
nodes [26], corresponding to ~104–105 kB T per switching event, i.e. three to four decades above the soft-
matter per-element scale, but two to three decades above kB T. The Landauer ratio per element is 
therefore in the 104–105 range; the higher overall plane positions reflect operational overhead (clock 
distribution, leakage, data movement) which dominates rest-of-system dissipation.


## Page 15


Band 3 — memristor crossbar: 10⁹–10¹⁰ × Landauer. In-memory analogue computing eliminates the data-
movement overhead that dominates digital architectures [27]. The two-decade improvement over GPUs 
at the same task is well-documented; it brings neuromorphic hardware closer to the floor than any other 
engineered system on Plane A. The remaining gap to biology is approximately five decades. 
Band 4 — E. coli and the mitochondrion: 10⁵–10⁸ × Landauer. Evolved cellular and organelle systems sit 
closest to the floor, but the band is wider than the soft-matter and silicon bands. The mitochondrion 
(Landauer ratio ≈ 1.7 × 10⁵) is closer to the floor than the E. coli chemotaxis pathway (≈ 10⁸): per bit of 
stimulus information actually transduced, the mitochondrion has a lower thermodynamic overhead, even 
though its absolute volumetric power dissipation is higher. This is consistent with the cell-level estimates 
of Parrondo, Horowitz and Sagawa [19] and with the chemotaxis-rate measurements of Mattingly et al. 
[22].  
9. Soft–hard matter differences in the molecular-energy regime 
The indicative clustering on Plane A reflects a structural divide in the molecular energetics of the 
underlying substrate. The divide has consequences for I2, fatigue and information-rate ceilings that follow 
directly from the Carnot-analogue ceiling. 
9.1 Per-element energy scales 
The per-element transition or switching energy distinguishes the substrates by approximately three to 
four orders of magnitude: 
• Soft matter: 1–10 kB T per repeat unit (≈ 2.5–25 zJ at 298 K), set by hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic 
interactions and conformational reorganisation. This is the regime in which thermal fluctuations are 
comparable to the driving energy of the responsive transition, with the consequences set out below. 
• SMA, piezoelectric, MEMS: 102–104 kB T per element (10−19–10−17 J), set by martensitic phase 
transformations, piezoelectric coupling or capacitive switching. 
• CMOS at modern nodes: ~104–105 kB T per switching event (≈ 0.1–1 pJ) [26]. 
• Memristor: 102–103 kB T per synaptic operation; closer to the molecular regime than CMOS digital, 
which is why the Landauer ratio is lower. 
• Biology: ATP hydrolysis at ~20 kB T per molecule; comparable per-element scale to soft matter but 
used in cycles that achieve much higher conversion efficiency through evolved coupling. 
9.2 Consequences for I₂ 
The functional selectivity I2 tracks the per-element energy scale, but inversely to a naive expectation. Soft-
matter responsive transitions occupy a wide region of the chemical design space (many polymers exhibit 
LCST behaviour; many boronate chemistries bind diols) so I2 is low (10–30 bits). Hard-matter systems 
operate in a narrow region of materials space defined by silicon physics, and require precise lithography 
and dopant placement, so I2 is moderately high (50–100 bits). Biology operates in a narrow region of


## Page 16


sequence space defined by the requirement for catalytic and regulatory specificity, and I2 is very high (103–
105 bits). The three regimes therefore separate clearly on Plane B even where they overlap on Plane A. 
Two implications follow. First, soft adaptive matter operates at low I2 because chemical responsiveness is 
common; achieving the selectivity required for intelligent function (orthogonal multi-channel response, 
decision logic) is a problem of raising I2 without losing I3. Second, evolved biology illustrates that high I2 
and low Landauer ratio are compatible; the rarity of functional configurations and the thermodynamic 
efficiency of operation are not in tension, contrary to the intuition that “more complex” must mean “more 
dissipative.” 
9.3 Consequences for fatigue 
The 1–10 kB T per-element energy scale of soft matter has the structural consequence that the driving 
energy of the responsive transition is comparable to thermal fluctuations. This is the molecular origin of 
the fatigue problem: repeated cycling reorganises the network slightly each time, because the energy 
barriers between conformational states are crossed many times per cycle by thermal motion, and a small 
but cumulative fraction of those crossings produces irreversible changes (covalent scission, crosslink 
rearrangement, segregation). Hard-matter systems, with per-element energies 103–104 × thermal, do not 
fatigue by this mechanism; their failure modes (electromigration, dielectric breakdown, mechanical 
fracture) are different in physics and act on different timescales. The fatigue problem is therefore intrinsic 
to the energy regime of soft matter and not separable from the responsiveness mechanism. 
9.4 The N* divide quantified 
Eqn. (12) gives N* = ln(Estimulus / kB T) / ln(1/p). For the soft-matter regime (per-element energy ~5 kB T, p 
~ 0.5), N* ≈ 2.3; for the CMOS regime (per-element energy ~104 kB T, p ~ 0.9 due to higher fidelity), N* ≈ 
88. The order-of-magnitude difference is the explanation of why soft-matter architectures cannot be 
made arbitrarily complex without dissipative collapse, whereas hard-matter architectures can be scaled 
to billions of elements. The constraint is set by the substrate, not by engineering effort. This order-of-
magnitude separation is relatively insensitive to heterogeneity. The gap is set by N* and a decade-scale 
variation among pi does not close a decade-scale gap. The placement of any individual system on Plane A, 
however, requires identification of the cascade bottleneck rather than a global p.  
The implication for adaptive soft matter is that any attempt to populate the gap on Plane A between Band 
1 and the biology band must either (i) raise the per-element energy scale or (ii) use a feedback 
architecture, in which the thermodynamic budget is shifted by the Sagawa–Ueda relation (eqn. 6). The 
second route is the motivation for developing intelligent rather than merely adaptive soft matter. 
10. A putative design space for adaptive and intelligent soft matter 
Between Band 1 (soft, ~1019 × Landauer) and Band 4 (biology, ~108 × Landauer) lies a region of ~1011 in 
Landauer ratio that is empty of synthetic systems. The condition for populating this design space requires 
more than chemical novelty alone: the analytical framework developed here shows that the Landauer


## Page 17


ratio of any soft-matter system using 1–10 kB T per element is set by molecular energetics, not by the 
specific chemistry. Populating the gap requires either (a) substantially higher per-element energies, 
transitioning the substrate toward Band 2, or (b) higher information rates per unit dissipation, achievable 
through feedback (intelligence) or through parallelisation of channels (multi-channel orthogonality). 
Three architectural elements could position soft matter within this design space: 
(i) A feedback path from output to channel. This is the definition of intelligent matter in eqn. (3). In soft 
matter the most realistic implementations are catalytic feedback (output product modulates input 
affinity), allosteric coupling (output state modulates internal state via cooperative binding), and reaction-
diffusion patterning (output spatial pattern modulates local reaction rate). The thermodynamic gain from 
feedback is set by eqn. (6) [6] For a few bits per cycle this is a few-kJ·mol−1 shift in the operating budget, 
which is comparable to the per-element responsive energy itself, and therefore non-trivial in the soft-
matter regime. 
(ii) Multi-channel orthogonality with controlled synergy. The data processing inequality [4] limits the 
information transferable through a single channel to its capacity; multi-channel architectures raise the 
ceiling by parallelising. The information-theoretic measure of channel orthogonality is the gap between 
the joint mutual information I(X1,…,Xn ; Y1,…,Yn) and the sum Σi I(Xi;Yi): zero gap is orthogonal, positive gap 
is synergistic, negative is redundant [21]. Designed redundancy is sometimes useful (for noise rejection) 
but the default failure mode of multi-stimulus soft matter is unintended cross-talk, which is redundancy 
without design. Measurements of TNF signalling [16] document this directly in biology, with ~1 bit total 
mutual information through cascades that include multiple parallel paths but lose information at every 
upstream bottleneck. 
(iii) Molecular memory consistent with feedback timescales. Eqn. (3) requires a representation of history 
Yt−1. In soft matter this can be implemented through metastable conformational states (shape memory), 
through covalent modifications that persist across cycles (catalytic imprinting), or through local 
concentration gradients that decay on a timescale slower than the response. The information stored in 
the memory is bounded by its configurational entropy I1 at finer coarse-graining, which is why the 
discriminative power of I1 at the operational coarse-graining is misleading: at per-chain or per-domain 
coarse-graining the same systems separate substantially. The choice of memory implementation is the 
architectural element that turns an adaptive material into an intelligent one. 
11. Summary and outlook 
The two-plane benchmarking framework places ten representative stimulus-coupled systems on a 
common (İ3/V, P) and (I1, I2) coordinate system with explicit uncertainty intervals. Four broadly distinct 
bands separate on the dynamic plane: soft matter and SMA at 10¹⁸–10²⁰ × Landauer; silicon digital at 10¹⁰–
10¹⁶; memristor neuromorphic at 10⁹–10¹⁰; biology at 10⁵–10⁸. The multi-decade separation that seems 
to differentiate synthetic soft matter and biology is an expression of the design problem that challenges 
the development of adaptive and intelligent soft matter systems.


## Page 18


The mechanistic origin of the gap is the per-element energy scale of soft matter (1–10 kB T) relative to 
silicon (104–105 kB T). This sets the optimal complexity N* of a soft adaptive channel at ~2–3 elements 
versus ~40–90 elements for hard substrates (eqn. 12), and it sets the volumetric information rate ceiling 
at ~104–105 bits·s−1·m−3 for purely thermal responsive soft matter. Populating the gap therefore cannot 
proceed by extending the same chemistry. The framework points to three architectural routes: feedback-
modified channels (intelligence proper), multi-channel orthogonality with designed synergy, and 
molecular memory consistent with feedback timescales.  
The framework is robust at the qualitative level: order-of-magnitude uncertainty in any single placement 
does not change the band structure or the existence of the gap. It is indicative at the quantitative level. 
The two principal recommendations for further work are (a) direct calorimetric measurement of the 
dissipated cycle fraction for two or three exemplar responsive polymers, separating reversible heat 
storage from irreversible dissipation; and (b) a principled accounting framework for the information rate 
of biological systems whose channels are not externally driven but internally regulated. With these 
achieved, the framework described in this paper would convert from a positioning diagram to a 
quantitative design rule.
