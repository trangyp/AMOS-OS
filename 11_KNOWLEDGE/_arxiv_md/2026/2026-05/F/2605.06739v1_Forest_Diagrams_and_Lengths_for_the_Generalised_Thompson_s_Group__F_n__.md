FOREST DIAGRAMS AND LENGTHS FOR THE GENERALISED
THOMPSON’S GROUP F(n)
MART´IN G´OMEZ REYNOLDS
Abstract. We extend the concept of two-way forest diagrams, introduced by Belk
and Brown in 2003, to represent elements of F(n) as a pair of infinite, bounded n-ary
forests together with an order-preserving bijection of the leaves. This representation
allows us to develop an alternative way to compute the length of an element of
F(n), distinct from the formula established by Fordham and Cleary in 2009. As an
application of our length formula, we re-prove the existence of dead end elements in
F(n) and show that their depth is always two, first proved by Wladis in 2009.
1. Introduction
Thompson’s group F is one of the most well-studied groups in geometric group
theory.
As an extension of the work done by Higman in [Hig74], Brown defined a
generalisation of F in [Bro87], called the generalised Thompson group F(n) or the
Brown-Thompson group F(n). This group shares many of the interesting properties
that F has. It can be defined by the following infinite presentation:
F(n) ∼= ⟨x0, x1, x2, · · · | xjxi = xixj+n−1 for i < j⟩,
Fordham’s method for computing lengths of elements in F with respect to the
{x0, x1} generating set, presented in [For03], enabled results such as the existence of
dead end elements in F and the fact that F is not almost convex, shown by Cleary and
Taback in [CT04] and [CT03], respectively. Later, Belk and Brown in [BB05a] devel-
oped an alternative length formula for F using forest diagrams, which can be viewed as
a simplification of Fordham’s work. Forest diagrams have subsequently proven useful
in the study of the Cayley graph of F [BB05b, Mat08, EFR10]. In 2009, Fordham and
Cleary extended Fordham’s original work to provide a length formula for the group
F(n) with respect to the {x0, x1, . . . , xn−1} generating set.
The main result of this paper consists in adapting Belk and Brown’s forest diagrams
to represent elements in F(n), thus providing an alternative to Fordham and Cleary’s
method to calculate lengths in F(n).

3. Classifying Carets and Spaces
Let us number the spaces of a forest diagram f for f ∈F(n) as follows: the space
immediately to the left of the top pointer will be numbered as 0. The spaces to the
left of 0 will be −1, −2, −3, . . . , moving further left; and the spaces to the right of 0
will be numbered as 1, 2, 3, . . . , moving further right.

4. The Length Formula
We are now ready to present the process of finding the length of any element
f ∈F(n).
This will consist of three steps: labelling the marked spaces of the for-
est diagram of f; assigning a weight to each of the marked spaces, according to the
combination of labels; and, finally, computing the length of the element with respect
to the calculated weights.
Let f ∈F(n) and let f be its reduced forest diagram. We label the marked spaces
of each forest of f as follows:
(1) Label a marked space L (for left) if it is exterior and to the left of a pointer.
(2) Label a marked space N (for necessary) if it is exterior and the rightmost
marked space to the left of a caret (and not already labelled L); or if it is
interior and to the left of a child of the caret that encloses that marked space.
(3) Label a marked space R (for right) if it is exterior and to the right of a pointer
(and not already labelled N).
(4) Label a marked space I if it is interior (and not already labelled N).

5. Proof of the Length Formula
Proving the generalised length formula from Theorem 4.1 basically follows the same
structure as the proof for the case of F found in [BB05a, Section 4.3]. Essentially, our
task is to show that the proposed length formula satisfies the conditions of Lemma 5.1
(from [FC09, Lemma 2.1]) and hence the result follows.
Lemma 5.1. Let G be a group with generating set S, and let ℓ: G →N be a function.
Then ℓis the length function for G with respect to S if and only if:
(1) ℓ(e) = 0, where e is the identity of G.
(2) |ℓ(sg) −ℓ(g)| ≤1 for all g ∈G and s ∈S.
(3) If g ∈G \ {e}, there exists an s ∈S ∪S−1 such that ℓ(sg) < ℓ(g).
For our purposes, let ℓdenote the function defined on F(n) by Theorem 4.1. It is
straightforward to see that ℓsatisfies the first condition. To show that ℓsatisfies the
other two conditions we only need to gather information on how left-multiplication by
generators affects the function ℓ.
