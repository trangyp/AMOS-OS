From branching quasiflats to flats in CAT(0) cube complexes
Shaked Bader, Oussama Bensaid, and Harry Petyt
Abstract
We study quasiisometric embeddings between finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube com-
plexes. More specifically, we introduce geometric branching conditions under which flats
in the domain, not necessarily of top rank, are mapped within finite Hausdorff distance of
flats. As a consequence, one obtains embeddings between natural graphs associated with
the Tits boundaries of those cube complexes.
These results form a key step in understanding quasiisometric embeddings between
right-angled Artin groups. In an appendix, we also explain how the same methods recover
previously established rigidity results for quasiisometric embeddings of symmetric spaces
and Euclidean buildings of the same spherical type.

Introduction
Quasiisometric rigidity is one of the central themes of geometric group theory and coarse
geometry. Broadly speaking, it asks to what extent groups or spaces can be determined by
their large-scale geometry. This guiding question has been very influential, and there is a rich
body of work showing that in many important settings, quasiisometric groups or spaces turn
out to be essentially the same.
As a natural extension of this, one can ask when one group or space can be quasiiso-
metrically embedded in another. This is especially relevant because in most cases, results
on quasiisometric rigidity rely in some way on understanding quasiisometrically embedded
flats. The goal of this paper is to provide geometric conditions under which a quasiisometric

1.1
History and motivation
The study of groups from the large-scale geometric perspective goes back at least as far as
work of Milnor and Wolf on growth in groups [Mil68a, Wol68, Mil68b], and really began to
come into focus with important developments such as Stallings’ theorem on ends of groups
[Sta68], Mostow’s rigidity theorem [Mos68, Mar74, Pra73], and Gromov’s polynomial growth
theorem [Gro81].
As a consequence of Gromov’s theorem and the Bass–Guivarc’h formula [Bas72, Gui73],
any group quasiisometric to a finitely generated abelian group is itself virtually abelian. Sub-
sequently, Gromov proposed the program of trying to classify finitely generated groups up to
quasiisometry [Gro93]. The body of work in this direction is too large for us to encompass
here, so let us give just a sample of some of it.
Generalising the case of Euclidean spaces, two symmetric spaces of non-positive curvature
are quasiisometric if and only if (after permuting and rescaling the irreducible de Rham
factors) they are isometric [KL97]; see also [Tuk88, Pan89, Cho96], and an analogous rigidity
statement also holds for higher-rank Euclidean buildings.
For mapping class groups, one
likewise has strong quasiisometric rigidity: except in some low-complexity cases, every self-
quasiisometry of a mapping class group is close to an isometry, and consequently any finitely
generated group quasiisometric to a mapping class group is commensurable with it [BKMM12].
Further examples include lattices in semisimple Lie groups other than Isom(H2), and solvable
Baumslag–Solitar groups BS(1, n) [Sch95, Esk98, FM99].
In the cubical setting, which is the setting of the present paper, Bestvina–Kleiner–Sageev
[BKS08] and Huang [Hua17a, Hua25] proved quasiisometric rigidity within natural classes of
right-angled Artin groups. For example, any two quasiisometric right-angled Artin groups
with finite outer automorphism group are isomorphic [Hua17a]. Even more, Huang found a
class of right-angled Artin groups that are strongly quasiisometrically rigid [Hua18]. It should
be noted, though, that there are right-angled Artin groups for which these results fail [BN12].
In some particularly nice situations, rigidity phenomena of this kind persist when one
replaces quasiisometries by quasiisometric embeddings.
In higher-rank symmetric spaces,
Fisher–Whyte proved that, under natural compatibility assumptions, quasiisometric embed-
dings remain rigid, forcing maximal flats to be mapped within finite Hausdorff distance of
maximal flats [FW18]. They also constructed exotic embeddings when these assumptions fail.
See also [Ngu21, BN25] for further developments, including Euclidean buildings. Analogously,
Bowditch proved that, for compact orientable surfaces of the same complexity at least 4, any
quasiisometric embedding between their mapping class groups is at bounded distance from an
isometry [Bow18b]. In particular, those surfaces are homeomorphic and the embedding is a
quasiisometry. As yet there has been no work in this direction for right-angled Artin groups.
A common feature of many of these rigidity theorems is that they rely, in one form or
another, on a higher-rank analogue of the Morse lemma. Recall that the Morse lemma states
that every quasigeodesic in a δ–hyperbolic metric space lies at finite Hausdorff distance from
a geodesic. This fails in higher-rank spaces: consider a log-spiral in the Euclidean plane,
for instance. Nevertheless, some form of rigidity can often be recovered in higher rank by

replacing quasigeodesics with top-rank quasiflats.
In symmetric spaces and Euclidean buildings, foundational results of Kleiner–Leeb [KL97]
and later Eskin–Farb [EF97] show that top-rank quasiflats are Hausdorff-close to finite unions
of Weyl sectors. In CAT(0) cube complexes, analogous theorems for top-dimensional quasi-
flats, with Weyl sectors replaced by cubical orthants, were obtained by Bestvina–Kleiner–
Sageev [BKS16] in the cocompact 2–dimensional case, and by Huang [Hua17b] in general. A
finer statement for top-rank biLipschitz flats in median metric spaces was also obtained by
Bowditch [Bow18b]. Similar quasiflat theorems with coarser objects in the conclusion were
later obtained by Behrstock–Hagen–Sisto and Bowditch in greater generality [BHS21, Bow19].
Our goal in this paper is to find geometric conditions under which we can upgrade these
quasiflats theorems for CAT(0) cube complexes. We wish to upgrade them in three ways. First
of all, we want to obtain a genuine flat in the codomain, not just a union of orthants. Secondly,
we would like to consider CAT(0) cube complexes whose dimension is strictly greater than
the maximal dimension of a flat, for improved stability properties under quasiisometries. And
finally, we want to obtain results for flats that are not necessarily top-rank. These refinements
form the basis for results on quasiisometric embeddings between right-angled Artin groups
in [BBP26b, BBP26a]. In particular, having control over lower-rank flats is important for
obtaining obstructions to the existence of quasiisometric embeddings.
In the setting of quasiisometries, the first of these refinements can often be deduced from
the existence of a quasiinverse. Indeed, Huang proved that if f : X →Y is a quasiisometry
between universal covers of compact weakly special cube complexes of dimension n, then
every n–flat in X is mapped within uniformly bounded Hausdorff distance of an n–flat in Y
[Hua17b, Thms 1.3, 5.4]. His argument, however, relies in an essential way on the existence
of a quasiinverse, and thus does not generalise to the setting of quasiisometric embeddings.
The necessity of additional assumptions for proving a quasiflats theorem for lower-dimensional
flats can already be seen in the log-spiral example.
Our main input is a collection of branching conditions on geodesics and flats, designed to
capture the presence of enough transverse flat structure to force rigidity. While our results
are formulated for general CAT(0) cube complexes, the guiding examples come from universal
covers of Salvetti complexes of right-angled Artin groups, where these branching conditions
can be read directly from the defining graph, making them especially concrete and useful in
practice.
1.2
Main results
As described above, our aim is to provide geometric branching conditions under which a quasi-
isometric embedding of CAT(0) cube complexes sends flats within finite Hausdorff distance of
flats. The following is a pared-down version of Theorem 10.1 that fulfils this brief. We shall
give more detailed statements below.
Theorem A. Let X and Y be finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes, and let f : X →Y
be a quasiisometric embedding. If X and Y have the same asymptotic rank, n, then f(F) lies
at finite Hausdorff distance from an n–flat of Y for every directionally branch-complemented
n–flat F ⊆X.
Asymptotic rank, introduced by Wenger [Wen11], is a large-scale notion of dimension
that is preserved by quasiisometries.
The asymptotic rank of a CAT(0) cube complex X
3

is the largest n such that one can find arbitrarily large n–dimensional boxes inside X; see
Proposition 2.19. For example, every hyperbolic CAT(0) cube complex has asymptotic rank
at most one, even though its dimension could be arbitrarily large. In this case, Theorem A is
just the Morse lemma, but it serves to illustrate the significant increase in generality compared
to taking X and Y to have dimension n.
The key notion in Theorem A is that of a directionally branch-complemented flat, which
we introduce for general CAT(0) spaces in Definition 9.5.
Definition 1.1. Let X be a CAT(0) space of asymptotic rank n. A flat H ⊆X is called
branching if it is an intersection of finitely many n–flats.
A geodesic γ ⊆X is branch-complemented if there is an n–flat F ⊃γ and an (n −1)–flat
H ⊆F transverse to γ such that F is coarsely covered by branching parallels of γ and also
coarsely covered by branching parallels of H.
A flat E ⊆X is directionally branch-complemented if it is spanned by geodesics such that
the branch-complemented parallels of each one coarsely cover E.
In the case of standard flats in universal covers of Salvetti complexes, these notions can be
read directly from the defining graph Γ. More precisely, a standard geodesic corresponding
to a vertex v ∈Γ is branching precisely when v is an intersection of top-dimensional cliques.
It is branch-complemented if moreover v belongs to a top-dimensional clique K such that the
codimension-one face K ∖{v} is also an intersection of top-dimensional cliques. A standard
flat is directionally branch-complemented precisely when each vertex of the corresponding
clique defines a branch-complemented standard geodesic. For instance, if Γ is triangle-free,
then the standard geodesic corresponding to v is branching as long as v is not a leaf, and it
is branch-complemented if v also has a non-leaf neighbour. The special case of right-angled
Artin groups is developed further in [BBP26b].
Figure 1: Examples in rank 3: the red vertices define branch-complemented standard
geodesics, and the red cliques define directionally branch-complemented standard flats.
Another basic example is given by products of 3–regular trees. In that case, every geodesic
contained in a factor is branch-complemented, and every k–flat contained in a product of k
factors is directionally branch-complemented.
In fact, this example has a much stronger
intersection pattern than the one required in Definition 1.1: every such directionally branch-
complemented flat is itself branching, as in the example on the right of Figure 1. By contrast,
the two other examples show that a 3–flat may be directionally branch-complemented even
though none of its 2–dimensional subflats is branching. Thus, our notion only imposes branch-
ing in dimension 1 and codimension 1, and not a full higher-dimensional intersection pattern.
The following is a combination of Theorems 10.1, 10.2, and 10.6.

Theorem B. Let X and Y be finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes of asymptotic rank
n. For each q there exists D = D(q, Y ) such that the following holds for every q–quasiisometric
embedding f : X →Y .
For each directionally branch-complemented n–flat F ⊆X, the image f(F) lies at Haus-
dorff distance at most D from an n–flat of Y . Moreover, if H is a branching subflat of F,
then f(H) is at finite Hausdorff distance from a semisingular flat H′ ⊆Y .
If dim X = dim Y = n, then H′ is singular and D depends only on q.
As well as giving more precise information about the image of F than Theorem A, this
seems to be the first general result giving control on the images of lower-rank flats. Also, the
control it gives is rather strong. Not only are the images close to genuine flats, those flats are
semisingular, or even singular. Let us define these terms; see also Section 3.
CAT(0) cube complexes arise naturally in various settings, and classically have two natural
metric structures: the CAT(0) metric and the median, or ℓ1 metric. In this paper we are
working with the CAT(0) metric, so the “flats” mentioned above are isometrically embedded
copies of (Rd, ℓ2) with respect to the CAT(0) metric. However, a particularly nice family
of flats are those that are also isometric embeddings of (Rd, ℓ1) with respect to the median
metric. These are precisely the singular flats.
In an n–dimensional CAT(0) cube complex X, an n–flat is automatically singular (Lemma 3.2),
but in the generic case where the dimension of X is greater than its asymptotic rank n, it
can happen that X has no singular n–flats, even when n = 1: see Example 3.4. We there-
fore introduce the more flexible notion of semisingularity: a flat is semisingular if it becomes
singular after passing to an asymptotic cone. See Definition 3.3.
Thus, Theorem B does not merely produce flats, it produces flats that match well with
the cubical structure on Y . We also highlight here that there is no properness assumption in
Theorem B. This contrasts with the general CAT(0) setting: Example 6.9 describes a complete
CAT(0) space that is quasiisometric to a line, has Tits boundary consisting of two points, and
yet contains no biinfinite geodesic. We rule out this type of degeneracy for finite-dimensional
CAT(0) cube complexes in Proposition 6.10.
All of the flats that can be understood using Theorem B are either top-rank or are inter-
sections of top-rank flats. Since the behaviour of lower-rank flats is in general much wilder
than that of top-rank flats, if one wants to control maximal flats that are not top-rank, then
one needs a stronger branching hypothesis. This leads to the following definition, requiring
branch-complemented geodesics to arise as intersections of directionally branch-complemented
top-rank flats; see Definition 9.9.
Definition 1.2. Let X be a CAT(0) space of asymptotic rank n. A geodesic γ ⊆X is strongly
branch-complemented if it is an intersection of directionally branch-complemented n–flats.
A flat H ⊆X is directionally strongly branch-complemented if it is spanned by geodesics
such that the strongly branch-complemented parallels of each one coarsely cover E.
As before, for standard geodesics in universal covers of Salvetti complexes these properties
can be read directly from the defining graph. It is worth noting that directionally strongly
branch-complemented flats of dimension greater than one need not be branching, and in fact
they need not even be contained in any n–flat. See, for example, the standard flats associated
to the three middle edges in Figure 2.

Theorem C. Let X and Y be n–dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes of asymptotic rank n,
and let f : X →Y be a q–quasiisometric embedding. If H ⊆X is a directionally strongly
branch-complemented flat, then there is a constant D, depending only on n, q, and the coarse
density in Definition 1.2, such that the following holds.
There is a singular flat of Y at Hausdorff distance at most D from f(H). Moreover, if
O ⊆H is a singular orthant, then f(O) lies at Hausdorff distance at most D from a singular
orthant.
Recall that an orthant is a subspace isometric to [0, ∞)k. It should be noted that it is not
automatic for a quasiisometry that sends flats to flats to also send orthants to orthants. See
Remark 7.2, for instance.

quasiisometric embedding induces an embedding between distinguished subsets of the Tits
boundaries of X and Y . The following is a rephrasing of part of Definition 10.11, together
with part of Definition 10.13. We say that a geodesic ray is strongly branch-complemented if
it is contained in a strongly branch-complemented geodesic.

A basic example is obtained when X is a product of n trees. Since the singular geodesics
are precisely those contained in a single factor, ∂singX is just the 1–skeleton of the join of the
boundaries of the factors: it is a complete n–partite graph. If each factor is a regular tree of

Another important example is for X the universal cover of the Salvetti complex of a right-

∂T X of standard geodesics are encoded by the extension graph Γext of Γ, introduced by Kim–

Interpreting Theorem C on the level of boundaries, we obtain the following corollary.
Corollary D. Let X and Y be n–dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes of asymptotic rank n.
Every quasiisometric embedding X →Y induces a graph embedding ∂sbcX →∂singY .
Again, a version of this also holds in more generality; see Corollary 10.15. In fact, in
this n–dimensional setting, we are able to establish a more general version of Corollary D

can fail in this greater generality, one still has enough control on certain singular 2–orthants

Since asymptotic rank is a quasiisometry invariant, the fact that some of the above results
can be stated without relying on the dimensions of the CAT(0) cube complexes gives them
additional stability properties. For example, one can extend them to allow X and Y to be
products of CAT(0) cube complexes and hyperbolic spaces of finite asymptotic dimension. In-
deed, hyperbolic spaces of finite asymptotic dimension are quasiisometric to finite-dimensional
CAT(0) cube complexes of asymptotic rank one [HW12, BS00, Pet21], and the various branch-
ing conditions are preserved by such a quasiisometry. If we consider only products of hyper-
bolic spaces, then our results can be compared with [Bow16a]. We refer to Remark 10.10 for
more discussion.

1.3
Strategy and main tools in the proofs
As is the case in [KL97], one of the main tools in the proofs of Theorems B and C is the
asymptotic cone, which is a way of looking at a space from infinity. The point is that a
quasiisometric embedding f : X →Y induces a biLipschitz embedding between asymptotic
cones fω : Xω →Yω. This removes coarseness and enables the use of topological and analytic
arguments.
The basic idea is to show that if X and Y are finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes
of asymptotic rank n, and if F ⊆X is a directionally branch-complemented n–flat, then fω
sends its ultralimit Fω ⊆Xω to a genuine n–flat of Yω, and then to bring this information
back to the original spaces. This is done in three steps.
Step 1. Since F is an n–flat, its ultralimit Fω ⊆Xω is again an n–flat. More importantly,
the fact that F is directionally branch-complemented transfers to Fω but instead of having
coarsely dense sets of parallels as in Definition 1.1, we have a property of all parallels. More
precisely, Fω is spanned by geodesics γ1, . . . , γn such that, for each i, the geodesic γi admits
a transverse (n −1)–flat Hi ⊆Fω, and moreover all parallels of γi and Hi inside Fω are
branching.
This first step is to prove that the fω-image of each γi is equal to a genuine
geodesic of Yω.
To do so, we use the fact that the asymptotic cone Yω carries the structure of a median
metric space. Since Y has asymptotic rank n, the median rank of Yω is at most n [Bow13]. A
key input is then a structure theorem of Bowditch [Bow18b] on top-dimensional biLipschitz
flats in median metric spaces; see Proposition 9.19. Applied to the restriction of fω to Fω, it
implies that outside a subset S ⊆Fω of codimension 2, every point of Fω has a neighbourhood
on which fω is flat, in the sense that its image is contained in a Euclidean n–cube. The same
theorem also implies that the images of all branching flats are cubulated, namely locally finite
unions of Euclidean cubes of the corresponding dimension, endowed with the ℓ1 metric.
Using a tool from geometric measure theory, see Theorem 9.17, one shows that almost
every parallel of each γi avoids S.
Fix such a parallel γ, and let x ∈γ.
Let H be the
transverse parallel of Hi passing through x.
Near x, the image of γ is a finite union of
“straight” segments, and the image of H is a finite union of (n −1)–dimensional cubes, while
both lie inside a single Euclidean n–cube because x avoids S. At this point, a local topological

argument enters: since H separates γ inside Fω, the same separation phenomenon must persist
in the image, which prevents fω(γ) from “turning” locally; see Section 8. It follows that fω(γ)
is locally a CAT(0) geodesic around every point, and hence is a global geodesic. By continuity
of fω, the same conclusion holds for all parallels of the γi.
Step 2. The second step is to show that Fω is mapped to a genuine n–flat. Since all parallels
of γ1 and γ2 are sent to geodesics, one can translate the image of γ1 along the image of γ2
to show that the 2–flat spanned by γ1 and γ2 is sent to a genuine 2–flat by fω. One then
translates this 2–flat along the image of γ3, and so on, continuing inductively. These first two
steps are concluded in Section 9.
Step 3.
Once one knows that fω(Fω) = (f(F))ω ⊆Yω is an n–flat, the final step is to
transfer this information back down to Y . This is carried out in Section 7. In this part of
the argument, things are considerably simpler in the case that Y is n–dimensional, so let us
outline that first.
When Y is n–dimensional, it follows from Huang’s quasiflats theorem, [Hua17b], that
f(F) ⊆Y lies at finite Hausdorff distance from a union of finitely many n–orthants, and
the fact that (f(F))ω ⊆Yω is a genuine flat then forces this union to consist of exactly 2n
orthants. One then passes to the “support set” (see Section 7) of the quasiflat f(F) and uses
a sharp volume-growth argument, again following [Hua17b], to conclude that this support set
is itself an n–flat. We thus conclude that f(F) is Hausdorff-close to an actual n–flat in Y .
This argument is Proposition 7.1.
In the more general setting where Y only has asymptotic rank n, the argument is more
involved, for two main reasons. Firstly, one no longer has support sets for n–quasiflats when
the ambient space is higher-dimensional. And secondly, in this setting the output of Huang’s
quasiflats theorem is no longer true.
The correct statement here is Bowditch’s quasiflats
theorem [Bow19], which instead of providing a genuine union of orthants in Y gives only a
coarse map Ω→Y , where Ωis a panel complex (see Definition 7.5). In this case, carrying
out Step 3 decomposes into three sub-steps.
Step 3a. First we analyse Bowditch’s construction of the panel complex Ω. Up to finite
Hausdorff distance, Ωis built as a union of orthants inside a larger n–dimensional CAT(0)
panel complex Ψ. By using the same strategy as in the n–dimensional case of Step 3, we show
that Ωcan actually be taken to be a flat subcomplex inside Ψ. Bowditch’s quasiflats theorem
now tells us that f(F) lies at finite Hausdorff distance from the image ϕ(Ω) of Ωunder a
quasiisometric embedding that is quasimedian on each orthant of Ω.
Step 3b.
So far we have replaced the quasiflat f(F) by another quasiflat ϕ(Ω) at finite
Hausdorff distance. But now we have extra median information on the orthants of Ωthat
we did not have for F. It suffices to find a flat at finite Hausdorff distance from ϕ(Ω), and
in fact, by an argument similar to that used in Step 2, for this it suffices to show that every
singular geodesic of Ωis mapped at finite Hausdorff distance from a geodesic in Y .
With that goal in mind, in this sub-step we upgrade the median information we have about
ϕ, by showing that it is globally quasimedian, and not merely quasimedian when restricted to
its orthants. To do this, we show that the ϕ-image of each orthant is coarsely median-convex,
and then iteratively apply a gluing result for the quasimedian property to build up larger and
larger subspaces of Ωon which ϕ is quasimedian. After a finite number of steps, we obtain
that ϕ is globally quasimedian.
Step 3c. Once ϕ is known to be quasimedian, we can show that if γ ⊆Ωis a singular

Concluding. The three steps above establish Theorem A. Let us describe how to complete
the proofs of Theorems B and C.
To complete the proof of Theorem B and show that the image of a branching subflat of F
lies within finite Hausdorff distance of a singular flat, one has to control coarse intersections
of finite unions of coarse orthants. Indeed, if H ⊆F is a branching k–dimensional subflat, we
show that its image f(H) is at finite Hausdorff distance from a union O of coarse k–orthants,
obtained as a coarse intersection of the coarse n–orthants provided by [Bow19]. Since f(F)
is at finite Hausdorff distance from an actual n–flat F ′ by Theorem A, the Tits boundary of
O forms a round (k −1)–sphere inside ∂T F ′. Hence f(H) is at finite Hausdorff distance from
a k–dimensional subflat of F ′. The details of these arguments are in Section 5.
For Theorem C, the stronger branching assumption allows the last step of the previous
argument to be strengthened: every branching subflat of F is mapped within uniformly
bounded Hausdorff distance of a singular flat. In particular, all singular geodesics of F are
mapped within uniformly bounded Hausdorff distance of singular geodesics. This uniformity
then allows us to apply an argument similar to the one in Step 2 to produce the desired
flat. Essentially the same argument also shows that orthants are mapped within uniformly
bounded Hausdorff distance of orthants. The details of this step are in Section 4.

2.3
CAT(0) cube complexes and median metric spaces
We refer the reader to [Wis21, Bow22, Gen23] for background on CAT(0) cube complexes.
One of the many equivalent ways to define them is as follows.
A simplicial graph is
median if for every triple v1, v2, v3 of vertices there is a unique vertex µ such that d(vi, vj) =
d(vi, µ) + d(µ, vj) for all i, j. A CAT(0) cube complex is the cell complex obtained from a
median graph by attaching, in the obvious way and with obvious identifications, a unit cube
[0, 1]n to every subgraph isometric to the Cartesian product of n edges, for each n ≥2.
The dimension of a CAT(0) cube complex is the supremal dimension among cubes involved
in its construction. If a CAT(0) cube complex is not finite-dimensional then it is not a complete
metric space.
If X is a CAT(0) cube complex, then the length metric d2 obtained by equipping each cube
with the ℓ2 metric makes X into a CAT(0) space. If one instead equips the cubes with the

ℓ1 metric, then one obtains a metric d1 on X that makes X into a median metric space. If X
is finite-dimensional then these metrics are both complete. See [Bow22] for an authoritative
account of median metric spaces and median algebras.
Definition 2.13 (Median metric space). Let (M, d) be a metric space. For a, b ∈M set
[a, b] = {x ∈M | d(a, b) = d(a, x) + d(x, b)}.
A median metric space is a metric space (M, d) such that for every triple a, b, c ∈M, the
intersection [a, b] ∩[b, c] ∩[c, a] consists of exactly one point. We denote this point µ(a, b, c)
and call it the median of the triple (a, b, c). The map µ: M3 →M is a ternary operation that
is 1–Lipschitz in each coordinate and makes M into a median algebra.
The median rank of M is the largest n ∈N ∪{∞} such that M contains a subset median-
isomorphic to {0, 1}n with the product median.
A median graph is precisely a graph whose edge metric makes the vertex set into a me-
dian metric space. If X is a CAT(0) cube complex, then (X, d1) and all of its asymptotic
cones are median metric spaces as well. If X is a finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex,
then corresponding asymptotic cones of (X, d2) and (X, d1) are biLipschitz equivalent. More
generally, a result of Bowditch shows that every complete, connected median metric space of
finite median rank admits a biLipschitz equivalent CAT(0) metric [Bow16b].

a point z from a point w if they do not lie in the same halfspace of h.
The metric d1 on the vertex set of a CAT(0) cube complex can be characterised with
hyperplanes: d1(x, y) is equal to the number of hyperplanes that separate x from y.

Definition 3.1 (Singular). Let X be a CAT(0) cube complex or an asymptotic cone of one,
and let A ⊆(X, d2) be a k–flat or k–orthant. We say that A is singular if it is the image of
an isometric embedding (Rk, d1) →(X, d1) or ([0, ∞)k, d1) →(X, d1), respectively.
When k = 1, we refer to these as singular geodesics and singular geodesic rays.
Equivalently, a flat or orthant A is singular if µ(a, b, x) ∈A for all a, b ∈A and all x ∈X.
Singular geodesics may be thought of as the “axis-parallel” ones.
For instance, in R2
(with its standard cubulation), the singular geodesics are precisely the horizontal and vertical
lines. They can be characterised in terms of hyperplanes: a geodesic γ is singular if no two
hyperplanes crossed by γ cross each other.
Lemma 3.2. Let X be an n–dimensional CAT(0) cube complex. If F ⊆X is an n–flat, then
F is singular.
Proof. If n = 1, then X is a tree, and every geodesic in a tree is singular. Now suppose that
we have proved the lemma in dimensions less than n. Let F ⊆X be an n–flat. Take an
arbitrary geodesic in F, and let h be a hyperplane of X that separates its endpoints. Recall
that h is itself a CAT(0) cube complex of dimension n −1. The intersection h ∩F is an
(n −1)–flat in an (n −1)–dimensional CAT(0) cube complex, so it is singular by induction.
Let γ ⊆F be a geodesic orthogonal to the subflat h ∩F. The parallel set P(h ∩F) splits
as P(h ∩F) = (h ∩F) × Y for some CAT(0) cube complex Y . Since X is n–dimensional, Y
must be 1–dimensional. Hence γ ⊆Y is singular. This shows that F is spanned by singular
geodesics, and hence it is singular.

Constructing flats and orthants
In this section, we study quasiisometric embeddings of flats with the standard cubulation
into CAT(0) cube complexes. We show that if singular geodesics are mapped uniformly close
to (semi)singular geodesics, then singular flats and orthants are mapped uniformly close to
(semi)singular flats and orthants (Proposition 4.3 and Lemma 4.5, respectively). This will
later be applied in situations where we have good control on singular geodesics, generally as
a consequence of control on top-dimensional flats, in order to gain control on singular flats of
intermediate dimension.
This is done by induction on dimension. For flats, we use parallel sets to build the target
flat from the images of singular geodesics. We then handle orthants using a coarse-separation
argument.
Afterwards, in Section 4.2, we strengthen the connection between (semi)singularity and
the Tits boundary. Then, in the n–dimensional case and under an additional assumption on
asymptotic cones, we extend Lemma 4.5 to singular 2–orthants where we do not have uniform
control on Hausdorff distances (Proposition 4.7).

5
Structures inherited by intersections of flats
In this section, we are interested in the images of intersections of flats in CAT(0) spaces
under quasiisometric embeddings.
The goal is to show that rigidity of flats passes down
to their intersections.
More precisely, Proposition 5.7 says that if some sets have images
Hausdorff-close to unions of singular orthants, then the image of their intersection is also
Hausdorff-close to a union of singular orthants. If, in addition, the intersection is a flat, then
Proposition 5.3, gives us that the boundary of the union of orthants is a sphere.
This will later be applied in situations where we have already established good control on
top-dimensional flats, by using Theorem 10.1, for example, and want to gain similar control
over their intersections.
The proof of Proposition 5.7 is a combination of the following two slogans: “the image
of the intersection is close to the intersection of the images” (Proposition 5.5), and “a coarse
intersection of orthants is essentially an orthant” (Lemma 5.6).
The bound on Hausdorff distance in Proposition 5.7 is finite but not uniform. A statement
with uniform bounds is given in Proposition 5.8: if some flats intersect in a flat and their
images are uniformly Hausdorff-close to flats, then the image of their intersection is also
uniformly Hausdorff-close to a flat.

In this section, we give three sets of conditions under which an n–quasiflat in a finite-
dimensional CAT(0) cube complex lies Hausdorff-close to a flat. All three are phrased in
terms of properties of the induced biLipschitz flat in the asymptotic cone.
The first two, Proposition 7.1 and Theorem 7.6, are variations of the following idea: for
a CAT(0) cube complex X, if a q–quasiflat is an n–flat in some asymptotic cone, then it is
uniformly Hausdorff-close to an n–flat. In Proposition 7.1 we assume that X is n–dimensional.
In Theorem 7.6 we allow X to only have asymptotic rank n, but we obtain constants that
depend on the specific space X.
These two results essentially reduce the problem of showing that a quasiflat is Hausdorff
close to a flat to the same problem for biLipschitz flats in the asymptotic cone. The latter
will be considered in Section 9.
The third main result of this section, Theorem 7.8, will not be used in this paper, but it
fits the theme and will be needed in [BBP26b]. It assumes the existence of a codimension-1
subflat whose image is an (n −1)–flat in the asymptotic cone.

9
Branching flats and biLipschitz embeddings
Any quasiisometric embedding between spaces induces a biLipschitz embedding between their
asymptotic cones. In this section we give conditions that allow us to control the images of
certain flats in the asymptotic cone under this biLipschitz embedding.
More precisely, in Section 9.1 we introduce the notion of a geodesic being branch-complemented.
In Section 9.2, we show that this property and related properties for flats are preserved by
passing to asymptotic cones. Our key result is then Proposition 9.20, which shows that the
images of such flats under biLipschitz embeddings are again flats.
We summarise this in
Corollary 9.21 for later use. In Section 10 we will use these results about asymptotic cones to
deduce similar statements about the original quasiisometry.
The argument is roughly as follows. In order to obtain some form of rigidity, we will
use the structure of top-dimensional biLipschitz flats in the asymptotic cone, as given by
Proposition 9.19. From the results in Section 5, we already know that we can pass some of
their structure down to intersections, and in fact this works more smoothly in asymptotic
cones because we are now dealing with fine properties rather than coarse ones. This leads to
the notion of branching flats. With more information about a branching geodesic, we can use
the methods of Section 8 to show that a biLipschitz embedding must send it to a singular
geodesic. This is formulated using the notion of branch-complemented geodesics. We can then
control the images of flats that are spanned by such geodesics. Again, the fine setting makes
this easier than in Section 4.

The branching theorems
Here we prove our main general results stated in the introduction about quasiisometric em-
beddings of directionally (strongly) branch-complemented flats into CAT(0) cube complexes.
Namely, in Theorem 10.1 we show that quasiisometric embeddings map each directionally
branch-complemented flat F of top rank Hausdorff-close to a flat. We then split the discussion
according to whether the cube complexes have the same dimension as F. Section 10.1 handles
the n–dimensional case, where the arguments need less machinery than in general.
The
results in Section 10.2 are more general, but the conclusions are slightly weaker. In either
case, we show that certain subflats of F are mapped at finite, but uncontrolled, distance
from (semi)singular flats (Theorems 10.2 and 10.6). We can also handle lower-rank flats that
do not lie in any n–flat provided that they are directionally strongly branch-complemented
(Theorems 10.3 and 10.7), provided that the domain is n–dimensional. When the codomain is
also n–dimensional, we even obtain controlled distance bounds. We conclude by interpreting
these results on the level of boundaries in Section 10.3.
