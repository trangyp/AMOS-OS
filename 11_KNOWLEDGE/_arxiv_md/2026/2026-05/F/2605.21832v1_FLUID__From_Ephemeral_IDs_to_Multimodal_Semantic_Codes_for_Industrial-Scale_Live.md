Abstract
Modern recommender systems rely heavily on ID-based collabo-
rative filtering: each item is represented by a unique ID embed-
ding that accumulates collaborative signals from user interactions.
Livestreaming recommendation, however, faces a unique challenge
in this paradigm: a live room typically broadcasts for only tens
of minutes, so its item ID remains poorly learned in a persistent
cold-start state and ID-centric ranking models fail to generalize.
We present FLUID, the first framework to fully retire the candidate-
side item ID from a production-scale livestreaming ranker. FLUID
couples a cross-domain multimodal encoder—jointly trained on
short videos and livestreams to produce discrete hierarchical codes
(LUCID)—with a late-fusion, ID-free design that injects slice- and
room-level LUCID as independent tokens, stabilized by a staged
warmup under online incremental training. Deployed on our indus-
trial livestreaming recommenders with a cross-platform combined
user base of over one billion globally, FLUID delivers significant
online gains of +0.55% Quality Watch Duration, +2.05% Cold-Start
Room Views, and +0.05% Active Hours.

Introduction
Livestreaming has emerged as one of the fastest-growing online
content ecosystems [18, 36, 44], where creators broadcast in real
time and users interact through viewing, commenting, and gifting.
Unlike video (typical item lifetime measured in days to months)
and e-commerce (months to years), livestreaming is dominated by
one intrinsic property absent from those domains: items are ex-
tremely short-lived. On our leading livestreaming platforms with a
combined user base of over one billion, a live room typically broad-
casts for only tens of minutes (∼45 min on average), after which
its item ID becomes obsolete. This poses a fundamental challenge
for recommender systems, which rely on accumulating collabora-
tive signals at the per-item level: as Figure 2 shows, the item ID
embedding remains poorly learned within this typical 45-minute
lifetime, leaving most live rooms in a persistent cold-start state.
Modern large recommendation models (LRMs) [9, 39, 41, 44] place
most of their capacity in ID-embedding tables. In livestreaming,
these tables can only memorize short-lived exposure signal, which
becomes useless once the room ends.
This short-lifetime vs. ID-memorization mismatch has motivated
extensive research on injecting content-derived signals into in-
dustrial ranking systems. One line of work attaches frozen dense
multimodal embeddings to items as auxiliary features [21, 29]. An-
other aligns or discretizes multimodal representations into compact

First, producing a high-quality semantic ID for livestreaming is
itself nontrivial. Live content is inherently multimodal and fast-
changing (visuals, speech, on-screen text, streamer metadata, and
audience signal all evolve within minutes [18, 36]), and the ID must
capture both transient dynamics and persistent room identity while
tolerating the high noise of live signals. Prior work on multimodal
encoders for recommendation is dominated by short-video or e-
commerce supervision [7, 21, 26, 29], which is far denser and less
noisy than live interactions; single-domain training on live data
alone struggles to match that quality.
Second, even when a high-quality semantic ID is available, simply
adding it alongside the item ID is insufficient. Multiple industrial
studies have reported that, when multimodal features coexist with
an ID-side signal, ranking models often default to the ID-side signal

To address these two difficulties, we propose FLUID (Framework

framework to fully retire the candidate-side item ID from a production-

FLUID’s two contributions mirror the two difficulties above. First,
a cross-domain multimodal encoder jointly trained on short videos
and livestreams transfers denser visual–textual supervision from
the short-video domain to the sparser and noisier live domain, pro-
ducing discrete multimodal codes we call LUCID (Live Universal
Content IDentifier) as the sole candidate-side content identifier.
Second, a late-fusion design injects slice- and room-level LUCID
codes into the ranker as independent tokens, with a staged warmup
that keeps the item-ID→LUCID transition stable in online incre-
mental training and prevents the backbone from collapsing back
onto the item ID.
In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:
• Problem framing: We argue that when the item ID is
ephemeral, the widely documented ID-dominance effect be-
comes a fundamental bottleneck rather than a tolerable nui-
sance, motivating full retirement of the candidate-side item
ID rather than further fusion tricks.
• Cross-domain multimodal encoder producing hierarchi-
cal LUCID codes as the sole candidate-side content identifier.
• Late-fusion ID-free ranker in which LUCID supplants the
item ID, with a staged warmup that makes the transition
safe under online incremental training.

Related Work
2.1
Multimodal Embedding
Multimodal embedding learning aligns heterogeneous data—such
as images, text, and videos—into a unified semantic space [12, 25],
enabling diverse downstream applications including industrial rec-
ommendation [18, 21]. Early work pioneered by CLIP [25] adopts a
dual-tower paradigm with independent unimodal encoders aligned
via contrastive learning, and has been extensively refined in spe-
cific aspects such as training objectives, architecture and applica-
tions [3, 7, 11, 17, 32, 33, 37, 40, 42]. More recent work repurpose
single-tower paradigms for deeper cross-modal fusion, leveraging
the rich world knowledge and reasoning capacity of pre-trained
(multimodal) large language models [4, 13, 18, 20, 22, 35, 43, 45].
Our multimodal encoder follows this single-tower line.
Prior multimodal encoders for recommendation are also typically
trained on data from a single domain, such as live streaming, short
videos, or e-commerce [7, 18, 21, 26, 29]. In contrast, our multimodal
encoder is trained on cross-domain data, enabling it to capture more
robust and transferable semantic signals across diverse scenarios.
2.2
ID-based Recommendation Models
The dominant paradigm in modern recommender systems repre-
sents users and items as ID embeddings that carry collaborative
signals. From early collaborative filtering [28] and matrix factoriza-
tion [8, 16] to Factorization Machines and their variants [14, 27],
ID embeddings have served as the primary carrier of collaborative
signal across heterogeneous sparse features.
Deep learning has further scaled this paradigm. Embedding-and-
MLP architectures [5, 6, 10, 34] replaced hand-crafted cross fea-
tures with learnable interactions; sequential models [15, 31, 49, 50]
brought attention and recurrence to user-behavior sequences; and
large recommendation models (LRMs) [9, 23, 39, 41, 44] pushed
model capacity onto massive ID-embedding tables. The bulk of
model capacity thus resides in ID-embedding tables, and perfor-
mance degrades sharply when the ID signal is insufficient. This
limitation is particularly acute in livestreaming, where item life-
times are extremely short.
2.3
Multimodal Information in Ranking and
Recommendation
While effective for long-lived items, the ID-centric paradigm de-
grades under cold-start or short-lived conditions, where insufficient
interactions prevent meaningful ID embeddings from forming—an
effect especially pronounced in livestreaming, where room identi-
fiers are ephemeral by nature. To address this, recent work has ex-
plored compact content-derived semantic codes such as YouTube’s
Semantic IDs [30] and TIGER [26], and, more broadly, has argued
that sufficiently powerful modality encoders can match or surpass
pure ID-based models in cold-start regimes [38]. These develop-
ments point toward a broader trend of combining the memorization
strength of IDs with the generalization capacity of content-derived
representations.
Efforts to inject multimodal signals into industrial ranking sys-
tems can be broadly grouped into two families, distinguished by
how tightly multimodal features are coupled with the ID embedding
space: (i) dense multimodal features, where frozen or lightly-trained
embeddings are attached to the item as auxiliary inputs; and (ii)
alignment and discretization, where multimodal representations
are explicitly aligned to, or compressed into, discrete codes more
compatible with downstream ID-based interaction.
Dense multimodal features. A common industrial recipe attaches
frozen multimodal embeddings to items as auxiliary dense inputs
to downstream rankers [21, 29]. The approach is simple but suf-
fers from two well-documented limitations [7, 21]: representation
mismatch with the user–item interaction signal, and representation
unlearning, as frozen features cannot adapt to drifting preferences
or business semantics.
Alignment and discretization. To close this gap, recent work
either aligns multimodal embeddings to the interaction space [7, 20,
47], or discretizes them into ID-like semantic codes via quantiza-
tion [18, 19, 21, 43, 48]; we adopt the prefix n-gram parameterization
of Zheng et al. [48] in our embedding lookup (Section 3.3). Despite

3
Method
Figure 1 gives an overview of FLUID. The pipeline has four stages:
(i) a cross-domain multimodal encoder (§3.1) maps each 2-minute
slice to a 128-d embedding 𝑧; (ii) RQ-KMeans (§3.2) discretizes 𝑧into
an 𝐿-level codeword tuple—LUCID; (iii) a prefix n-gram embedding
(§3.3) turns each LUCID tuple into a learnable embedding; (iv) a late-
fusion ID-free ranker (§3.4) injects LUCID as independent candidate-
side tokens, retires the item ID, and stabilizes the transition with a
staged warmup.
3.1
Multimodal Encoder
As argued in Section 2, live rooms are too short-lived for the ranker
to learn useful per-item ID embeddings, which motivates a content-
side multimodal encoder.
3.1.1
Cross-domain Training Data. Livestreams on their own, how-
ever, are also too sparse on the query-to-item side: short broadcasts
mean thin per-room user behavior signal. We therefore train a sin-
gle multimodal encoder jointly on livestreams and short videos with
a shared embedding space, where rich query-to-item pairs from
short videos facilitates the training of the multimodal encoder and
improves its generalizability.
3.1.2
Architecture. The multimodal encoder (Figure 1 top) consists
of three components: a vision module, a text module, and a single-
tower fusion module. The Vision Module, based on SigLIP2-base
(native-resolution ViT) [33] with a two-layer MLP projector, pro-
duces visual tokens 𝑇𝑣∈R𝑁𝑣×𝐷ℎ. The Text Module tokenizes
rich metadata—titles, OCR, ASR transcripts, author bios, audience
comments, and sticker tags—into 𝑇𝑡∈R𝑁𝑡×𝐷ℎ. The Single-Tower
Fusion Module, built on Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B [46], processes
the concatenated sequence 𝑇= [𝑇𝑣,𝑇𝑡, [EOS]]. Finally, the [EOS]
hidden state is linearly projected to a 128-d embedding𝑧. We adopt a
single-tower architecture so that vision and text interact across the
LLM’s full depth, rather than only meeting at the output layer as in
dual-tower designs. This single-tower advantage holds consistently
across retrieval and classification benchmarks (Section 4.3.1).
3.1.3
Training Recipe. The encoder is trained on a query-to-item
(Q2I) contrastive task with InfoNCE loss [24] and false-negative
masking. Queries are user search terms or MLLM-synthesized
keywords; items are 2-minute content slices drawn from both
livestreams and short videos, with positive pairs constructed from
user-behavior signals (likes, shares, and watch-through). To pre-
serve the pre-trained semantics in the LLM, we train in two stages:
(1) Alignment—only the MLP projector and output projection are
trained; (2) Joint fine-tuning—the full model is unfrozen and fine-
tuned end-to-end at a reduced learning rate. Within each batch,
we discard entire negative pairs whose query embeddings have
pairwise similarity above a predefined threshold to reduce spurious
negatives.
3.2
Discrete Representation via RQ-KMeans
Using the multimodal embedding 𝑧corresponding to the 2-min
content slice directly as a ranker input is suboptimal: embeddings
from a generic vision–language objective are misaligned with the
user–item interaction signal, and a shared MLP over frozen multi-
modal embeddings lacks the expresiveness of embedding lookup
tables of the ranker [21]. We therefore discretize 𝑧via Residual
Quantization K-Means (RQ-KMeans) [1, 21] into an 𝐿-level code-
word tuple we call LUCID (Live Universal Content IDentifier),
which would later enter the ranker through a learnable embedding
table co-trained with other sparse-ID features. We use RQ-KMeans
rather than RQ-VAE because RQ-VAE collapses codebook entries
under our online streaming retraining cadence, whereas K-means
gives stable partitions once fit. We set 𝐿=4 and 𝑁=64. The resulting
LUCID is a tuple [𝑐1, . . . ,𝑐4], e.g., [33, 42, 63, 3].
Slice-level LUCID captures 2-minute dynamics—a streamer briefly
switching from chatting to singing—but the ranker also needs a
stable room-level identity: user interactions accumulate at room
granularity, and an identifier that changes every two minutes would
force the embedding tables to chase a moving target rather than
accumulate room-specific signal across slices. We obtain such an
identifier by level-wise majority voting: at each quantization depth
𝑙, pick the most frequent codeword across all cumulative slices.
Because residual quantization already decouples coarse-to-fine se-
mantics, per-level voting distills dominant content without mixing
levels.

3.4
Late Fusion and Staged Warmup
The final step is to incorporate LUCID embeddings to the production
ranker. Our ranker backbone is a transformer-like architecture [44]
based on token-level feature interaction, where different groups
of feature embeddings are represented by different tokens. We
now provide details on the incorporation of LUCID embeddings as
separate feature tokens into our ranker.
3.4.1
Room-level and slice-level integration. Slice-level LUCID de-
scribes the current 2-minute content segment and captures short-
term changes in the livestream, such as a streamer switching from
chatting to singing. Room-level LUCID is obtained by the majority-
voting procedure in Section 3.2 and provides a more stable descrip-
tor of the live room. The two granularities are complementary to
each other: the slice token provides transient multimodal evidence,
while the room token provides persistent candidate identity.
Both embeddings use the prefix n-gram embedding scheme in
Eq. 1, but they are parameterized by separate embedding tables.
Sharing the same embedding tables leaves stable room semantics
and fast-changing slice semantics into the same parameter space,
which empirically weakens their expressiveness. We report the
shared-table ablation in Section 4.3.2.
3.4.2

existing item ID embedding of the candidate item, and let s and r
denote the prefix-n-gram embeddings of slice-level and room-level
LUCID, respectively. The fusion strategies between content-based
embeddings and ID-based embeddings can be broadly divided into
two categories: early fusion and late fusion.
Early fusion first maps the item ID and LUCID embeddings into
a single candidate representation h. For example:

4.3
Ablation Studies
We conduct ablation experiments to validate the key design deci-
sions in FLUID. Ablations on the multimodal encoder are reported
on retrieval and classification benchmarks (subsubsection 4.3.1);
ablations on ranking components use CTR AUC unless otherwise
stated, matching the ranking setup in subsection 4.2.
4.3.1
Multimodal Model. Our SigLip2 + Qwen3-Embedding single-
tower encoder substantially outperforms an internal dual-tower
CLIP-B/32 + Albert-V2 baseline across all retrieval and semantic
classification benchmarks, with the largest gains on cross-modal re-
trieval. Table 5 ablates the three design choices behind this encoder—
backbone (Qwen3-Embedding + SigLip2 over CLIP/Albert), fusion

training schedule (two-stage alignment + joint fine-tuning over
end-to-end)—and all three contribute consistent gains.

4.3.2
LUCID Embedding Design. We ablate two embedding-side
design choices that together shape how LUCID codes are looked
up on the candidate side: (i) the embedding scheme, namely Prefix
n-gram (subsection 3.3) vs. Level-wise decoding; and (ii) whether
slice- and room-level LUCID codes share a single embedding table
or use separate ones (subsubsection 3.4.1).
As summarized in Table 6, both prefix n-gram embedding (+0.11%
AUC over level-wise decoding) and independent slice/room tables
(+0.05% AUC over the shared variant) contribute positively, with
prefix n-gram providing the larger gain. This confirms that resolv-
ing residual-level codeword ambiguity is the dominant factor in
candidate-side LUCID embedding design, while disentangling slice
and room parameter spaces yields a smaller but consistent addi-
tional gain.
4.3.3
Candidate-side LUCID Integration: Fusion and Training Recipe.
Our deployment carries multimodal information only on the can-
didate side, which makes how slice LUCID is integrated with the
existing item ID the key design question for whether LUCID can
contribute incremental signal. This subsection ablates two dimen-
sions: (i) the fusion mechanism, i.e., whether slice LUCID and the
item ID are merged before the backbone (early fusion) or enter the
backbone as independent tokens (late fusion); and (ii) the training
recipe once late fusion is fixed, i.e., how to actually train LUCID
and ultimately retire the item ID. Table 7 organizes all experiments
along these two dimensions.
Fusion mechanism. As summarized in Table 7, none of the four
early-fusion variants—parameter-free combinations (Replace, Con-
cat) and learnable gates (LARM learnable, LARM feature) [18]—
improves meaningfully over the baseline (max +0.01%, min −0.13%).
The LARM learnable gate converges to predominantly relying on
the item ID (Figure 5), confirming that any pre-backbone fusion
collapses onto item-ID-only behavior. Moving LUCID into an in-
dependent token (late fusion) architecturally breaks this collapse
mode but, without a warmup recipe, still yields Δ AUC = +0.00%,
