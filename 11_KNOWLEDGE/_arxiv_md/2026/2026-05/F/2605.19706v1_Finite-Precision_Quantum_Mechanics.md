Abstract
Standard quantum mechanics is an idealisation based on infinite-precision objects:
point states, exact probabilities, and sharp measurements. Yet every real experiment
has finite resolution, and for macroscopic systems we never have access to the mi-
croscopic state; we only measure a few macroscopic observables such as total energy,
magnetisation, or particle number. Following Heisenberg’s call for a theory built only
on observable quantities, and von Neumann’s insight that a complete description of a
macroscopic system is neither possible nor necessary, we elevate the macroscopic state
to a fundamental concept.
We introduce Interval Quantum Mechanics (IQM), in which the state of a quantum
system is never a point but a quantum parcel – a basic weak* open set of density
matrices defined by finitely many open expectation intervals. Such a parcel is the exact
mathematical representation of the set of all microscopic states that are compatible
with the measured values of a finite set of macroscopic observables.
We show that unitary evolution lifts to a deterministic flow on parcels, and that
a finite-precision (fuzzy) measurement process is represented by a volume-contracting
update that refines the initial parcel into a more constrained open set, strictly increas-
ing the geometric information defined as the Hilbert-Schmidt volume of the parcel.
By introducing a second impossible set, we obtain a double-parcel whose information
increases monotonically – resolving the von Neumann entropy paradox.
The framework eliminates foundational puzzles without additional interpretational
assumptions: wave-particle duality becomes a smooth trade-off; Schrödinger’s cat is
never in a literal superposition; and the “spooky action at a distance” of entanglement
disappears, replaced by a purely epistemic geometric update. All empirical predictions
of standard quantum mechanics are recovered exactly in the infinite-precision limit,
which is never physically attained.
IQM provides a natural data type (hyper-rectangle parcels) for quantum computa-
tion, enabling resource-aware compilation and precision tracking for near-term devices.
The result is an observation-first, computationally practical reformulation of quantum
mechanics that takes macroscopic observables as primitive.

Introduction
Standard quantum mechanics is built on the idealisation of infinite precision: point-like
wave functions, exact real-number probabilities, and projective measurements that yield
sharp outcomes. Yet every real experiment has finite resolution, and fundamental physics –
from the Planck scale to the finite size of detectors – imposes hard bounds on the precision
we can ever achieve. The Planck length ℓP ≈1.616 × 10−35 m sets a fundamental limit on
spatial resolution; attempts to probe distances below this scale are physically meaningless.
Similarly, any measurement apparatus has a finite energy range, finite temporal resolution,
and finite sample size. Consequently, the notion of a point state (a single density matrix)
is an idealisation that can never be realised in the laboratory.
In his seminal 1925 paper, Heisenberg already called on physicists “to try to construct
a theory of quantum mechanics in which only relationships among observable quantities
occur.” [15] Von Neumann, in his foundational work [25], was acutely aware that infinite
precision is unattainable. He introduced the density matrix formalism precisely to describe
ensembles and partial knowledge, and he emphasised that a complete description of a macro-
scopic system is neither possible nor necessary. The work of Goldstein, Lebowitz, Tumulka,
and Zanghì [13] clarified and extended von Neumann’s original argument, introducing the
concept of normal typicality. Reimann [20] extended von Neumann’s approach to thermali-
sation, showing that for every initial state in an energy shell – not merely for typical states
– the expectation values of all bounded observables remain close to their microcanonical
values for the overwhelming majority of times. The key feature of Reimann’s result is that
the bound depends only on the effective dimension of the energy shell, not on the specific
initial state. This universality suggests that the correct object to describe a macroscopic
system at thermal equilibrium is not a single density matrix but the entire set of states con-
sistent with the macroscopic energy constraint – precisely what we call a quantum parcel.
A detailed IQM-native formulation of thermalisation, combining Reimann’s spectral bound
with the double parcel framework developed below, is deferred to a companion paper.
Following Heisenberg’s principle and von Neumann’s insight, we elevate the quantum
parcel—the open set of density matrices consistent with macroscopic resolution—to the role
of the fundamental physical state. Formally, a parcel is a basic weak* open set

point state – the point state is the idealisation. In the language of imprecise probability,
the parcel is the epistemic state: it represents what we can actually know.
For macroscopic systems – a cat, a measuring device, a thermal bath – we never have
access to the microscopic state. Instead, we measure only a few macroscopic observables.

The quantum parcel is the exact mathematical representation of the epistemic knowl-

1.1
Resolving the foundational paradoxes
The framework dissolves the traditional paradoxes without introducing interpretational
fuzziness:
• Wave-particle duality becomes a smooth trade-off controlled by the precision η
of a which-path measurement. As η increases, the possible set shrinks towards the
outcome subspace, and the interference pattern fades gradually – no abrupt switch
between two incompatible classical pictures.
• Schrödinger’s cat is never in a literal superposition of alive and dead. The parcel
contains both possibilities only because our macroscopic data cannot discriminate
between them. A fuzzy “which-cat” measurement refines the parcel, moving the ex-
cluded outcome into the impossible set. The cat is objectively alive or dead at all
times; the paradox vanishes.
• Entanglement loses its “spooky action at a distance”. When Alice measures her
half of an entangled pair, the joint parcel updates in a purely epistemic way – Bob’s

marginal changes instantaneously, but this is a geometric update, not a physical
signal. Relativistic causality is respected.
All empirical predictions of standard quantum mechanics are recovered exactly in the
infinite-precision limit, which is never physically reached. While the point-state formalism
serves as a powerful and essential calculational limit, it represents an operational singularity
that lies beyond the reach of finite-precision experimental observation.
From a computational perspective, IQM provides a natural data type for finite-precision
quantum states. Hyper-rectangle parcels, defined by intervals on an orthonormal set of
observables, require only O(m) storage and support polynomial-time operations (unitary
evolution, expectation intervals, Lipschitz-bounded measurement updates). This enables
resource-aware compilation and precision tracking for near-term quantum devices, bridging
the gap between high-level quantum algorithms and the noisy, finite-resolution reality of
NISQ hardware.
1.2
Related work
Geometric logic and domain theory.
The idea that the observable properties of a
system are the open sets of an appropriate topology (point-free topology) is the cornerstone
of geometric logic [18]. In this perspective, points are idealised limits of open sets, and the
logical propositions are the open sets themselves [24, 1]. Domain theory, as the study of
partial orders of information, is closely aligned with geometric logic [21, 22, 2]. A concrete
illustration of a domain is the collection of non-empty compact intervals of the real line
ordered with reverse inclusion. In this domain, a real number is approximated by a nested
sequence of rational intervals: the exact value π = 3.14159265 . . . is never known; we only
know it through intervals such as 3.14 < π < 3.15, then 3.141 < π < 3.142, and so on. In
the same way, a quantum state ρ is never known exactly; it is known only through a nested
sequence of basic open parcels defined by finitely many expectation intervals.
Epistemic interpretations.
Quantum Bayesianism (QBism) [11, 12] treats the quantum
state as a personal belief of an agent, updated via Bayes’ rule. While both QBism and
IQM assign an epistemic role to the state, IQM is objective: the parcel is determined by
the actual finite-precision data, not by an agent’s subjectivity. Moreover, IQM provides
a geometric measure of information (I = Vol(O2)/ Vol(O1)) that increases monotonically
under measurement, which has no analogue in QBism.
Operational probabilistic theories (GPTs).
GPTs [6, 19] provide a general frame-
work for probabilistic theories, but they typically assume exact states and measurements.
IQM is a specific GPT where the state space is convex and the measurement update is a
monotone operation on open sets. The novelty is the use of finite-precision knowledge as
a primitive, leading to a natural geometric information measure that resolves the measure-
ment problem.

Topos-theoretic approaches.
Butterfield and Isham [5], Döring and Isham [9], and
Heunen et al. [17] developed a topos-theoretic approach that replaces Hilbert space with
presheaves on contexts, providing a realist interpretation with intuitionistic logic, which is
related to geometric logic. Their focus is on the logical structure of propositions, not on
finite-precision measurements or macroscopic observables. Our parcels are concrete subsets
of the state space, not logical propositions.
Partial orders on quantum states.
Coecke and Martin [7] defined a partial order on
density matrices based on information content (spectral majorization), showing that quan-
tum states form a domain. While this orders individual states by purity, our partial order
compares sets of states (parcels) by information gain from finite-precision measurements.
Interval-valued probabilities.
Interval-valued probabilities appear in classical impre-
cise probability theory [26] and have been considered in quantum contexts [14]. However,
previous work treats intervals as arising from statistical ensembles or measurement im-
perfections, not as replacements for exact quantum states.
In IQM, the interval is the
fundamental object, not an approximation.
Resolving quantum paradoxes.
Standard interpretations address measurement dif-
ferently: Copenhagen [4, 16] invokes collapse; Many-Worlds [10, 23] eliminates it through
branching; QBism treats states as subjective beliefs. Our approach eliminates infinite-precision
idealisation, making the appearance of collapse a result of information gain about an ob-
jectively uncertain state. The double-parcel (O1, O2) allows us to track impossible states
explicitly, providing a monotonic information measure that resolves the von Neumann en-
tropy paradox.
In summary, IQM is distinguished by:
(i) Treating macroscopic observables as primitive, leading naturally to quantum parcels
with standard quantum mechanics recovered in the infinite-precision limit.
(ii) Introducing a double-parcel that tracks impossible states, yielding a monotonic geo-
metric information measure under Theorem 5.
(iii) Providing a unified resolution of wave-particle duality, Schrödinger’s cat, and entan-
glement without additional interpretative assumptions.
(iv) Offering a computationally practical data type (hyper-rectangle parcels) for finite-precision
quantum computation.

Extreme points
Let C ⊆T (H) be a convex set. A point ρ ∈C (the weak* closure
of C) is called an extreme point of C if it cannot be written as a convex combination
ρ = tσ1 + (1 −t)σ2 with 0 < t < 1 and σ1, σ2 ∈C, σ1 ̸= σ2.
For a weak* compact convex set (e.g., D(H) itself or the closure of a basic open parcel),
every point can be expressed as a convex combination of extreme points (Krein–Milman
theorem). In the case of a bounded polyhedron (such as the outer approximations used in
IQM), the extreme points coincide with its vertices. Linear functionals continuous in the
weak* topology attain their minima and maxima over C at extreme points of C.
Proposition 1. Let H be a Hilbert space of dimension ≥2, and let D(H) be the space of
density matrices endowed with the weak* topology. No non-empty weak* open set (quantum
parcel) O ⊂D(H) can consist exclusively of pure states.
3
Quantum Parcels
We now build a mathematical structure designed to represent states under finite-precision
knowledge. The state space is the set D(H) of density matrices equipped with the weak∗
topology. The fundamental objects are open subsets of this space, called quantum parcels,
because they arise naturally from coarse-grained measurements: any finite set of interval
constraints on expectation values defines a basic weak∗open set.
3.1
Single-parcels: possible states and information
A quantum parcel (first form) is simply a non-empty weak∗open subset O ⊆D(H) that is
convex with compact closure. In practice we work with basic open parcels of the form
O = {ρ ∈D(H) | aj < Tr(ρHj) < bj, j = 1, . . . , m},
(2)
where each Hj is a bounded Hermitian operator. These sets generate the weak∗topology
and are the ones that can be certified by a finite-precision experiment.
The physical and operational necessity of defining a quantum state as an open par-
cel, rather than a point or a closed subset, is fundamentally rooted in the topological
architecture of the state space under finite precision. This choice is rigorously justified by
Proposition 1, which establishes that no non-empty quantum parcel can consist exclusively
of pure states.
The operational meaning of this topological theorem is profound. In standard quantum
mechanics, a pure state ρ0 = |ψ⟩⟨ψ| represents an idealized system with infinite information,
where certain observables possess exact, sharp values. However, because a quantum parcel
is defined by a finite number of open macroscopic expectation intervals—representing the
realistic resolution limits of a physical apparatus—any open neighborhood surrounding a
pure state must necessarily leak into the interior of the state space, inevitably capturing a
dense continuum of mixed states.

are operational singularities—mathematical idealizations reachable only in the unphysical
limit of infinite precision. In the physical reality of macroscopic observation, we are topo-
logically confined to the open interior of D(H), reinforcing that the true operational state
of a quantum system is inherently an open parcel of mixed states.

never physically attained.

The Schrödinger Cat Paradox
In standard quantum mechanics, Schrödinger’s cat is described by a superposition
|Ψ⟩= α |undecayed⟩|alive⟩+ β |decayed⟩|dead⟩,
with arbitrary complex coefficients α, β satisfying |α|2 +|β|2 = 1. This leads to the paradox
that the cat is neither alive nor dead until observed. In Interval Quantum Mechanics (IQM),
the state is never a point; it is a basic weak* open set (a quantum parcel) representing the
set of density matrices consistent with finite-precision observations. We show that for any
such superposition, a basic open set containing the corresponding pure state behaves under
measurement in a way that resolves the paradox, and the treatment works uniformly for
a single qubit (the minimal model) and for the astronomically high-dimensional Hilbert
spaces of real cats.

balance between precision and computational cost. It is a proper data type for quantum
computation, enabling resource-aware compilation and verification of quantum programs
under finite-precision constraints.

Exact spectrahedra (defined by the same lin-

entanglement via CHSH intervals. The user can choose between the two representations
depending on the required accuracy and computational resources.

Conclusion and outlook
We have introduced Interval Quantum Mechanics (IQM) as a reformulation of quantum
theory that takes finite precision as a fundamental physical necessity rather than as an
approximation.
Motivated by Heisenberg’s call for a theory based solely on observable
quantities, we replace the idealised notion of a point state by a quantum parcel – a weak*
open set of density matrices defined by finitely many strict expectation intervals. Such a
33


## Page 34


parcel is the exact mathematical representation of the epistemic knowledge obtained from
a finite set of coarse-grained measurements.
The framework resolves several long-standing foundational puzzles without introducing
additional interpretations:
• Wave-particle duality becomes a smooth, information-theoretic trade-off: the pre-
cision η of a which-path measurement continuously interpolates between full interfer-
ence and complete particle-like behaviour.
• Schrödinger’s cat is never described by a literal superposition; the parcel contains
both possibilities only because our finite-precision data cannot discriminate between
them. A measurement merely refines the parcel, moving the excluded outcome into
the impossible set.
• Entanglement loses its “spooky action at a distance”. When Alice measures her
half of an entangled pair, the joint parcel updates in a purely epistemic way – Bob’s
marginal changes instantaneously, but this is a geometric measurement update, not
a physical signal. Relativistic causality is respected.
All empirical predictions of standard quantum mechanics are recovered exactly in the
infinite-precision limit, which is never physically attained.
The geometric information
I = Vol(O2)/ Vol(O1) (or I(O) = 1/ Vol(O) for a single-parcel) provides a rigorous, mono-
tonic measure of knowledge gain under measurement, resolving the von Neumann entropy
paradox.
From a computational perspective, IQM provides a natural data type for finite-precision
quantum states. Hyper-rectangle parcels, defined by intervals on an orthonormal set of
observables, require only O(m) storage and support polynomial-time operations (unitary
evolution, expectation intervals, Lipschitz-bounded measurement updates). This enables
resource-aware compilation and precision tracking for near-term quantum devices, bridging
the gap between high-level quantum algorithms and the noisy, finite-resolution reality of
NISQ hardware.
Future directions
Several avenues for further development are possible:
• Quantum programming languages: Extend existing systems (Qiskit, Cirq, Q#)
with interval types that explicitly track parcel width and separation certificates, en-
abling verified hybrid quantum-classical programs.
• Quantum Ergodic Theory, Thermalization, and Macroscopic Typicality:
While this work establishes the fundamental kinematics and measurement dynamics
of IQM, a major physical application—to be explored in a forthcoming companion

paper—is the resolution of the quantum thermalization problem via quantum ergodic
theory. Traditional approaches, such as the Eigenstate Thermalization Hypothesis
(ETH) and Reimann’s typicality frameworks, attempt to recover thermodynamic be-

the IQM framework allows for a native, robust formulation of ergodic properties di-
rectly in terms of quantum parcels. By utilizing ergodic theory to demonstrate how

We believe it provides a solid basis for the next generation of quantum information science,
from undergraduate teaching to the design of fault-tolerant quantum computers.
