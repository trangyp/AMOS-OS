First-Order Efficiency for Probabilistic Value

Ziqi Liu1, Kiljae Lee2, Yuan Zhang2, Weijing Tang1 *
1Department of Statistics & Data Science, Carnegie Mellon University.

Abstract
Probabilistic values, including Shapley values and semivalues, provide a model-
agnostic framework to attribute the behavior of a black-box model to data points or
features, with a wide range of applications including explainable artificial intelligence
and data valuation. However, their exact computation requires utility evaluations
over exponentially many coalitions, making Monte Carlo approximation essential
in modern machine learning applications. Existing estimators are often developed
through different identification strategies, including weighted averages, self-normalized
weighting, regression adjustment, and weighted least squares. Our key observation is
that these seemingly distinct constructions share a common first-order error structure,
in which the leading term is an augmented inverse-probability weighted influence term
determined by the sampling law and a working surrogate function. This first-order
representation yields an explicit expression for the leading mean squared error (MSE),
which characterizes how the sampling law and the surrogate jointly determine statistical
efficiency. Guided by this criterion, we propose an Efficiency-Aware Surrogate-adjusted
Estimator (EASE) that directly chooses the sampling law and surrogate to minimize
the first-order MSE. We demonstrate that EASE consistently outperforms state-of-
the-art estimators for various probabilistic values.
Keywords: Cooperative games; Shapley value; Monte Carlo sampling; Regression
adjustment.

Introduction
Shapley values [30], and the broader class of probabilistic values [10, 32], provide an
axiomatic framework for quantifying the contribution of individual players in a cooperative
game. This framework has become increasingly prominent in modern machine learning,
including model-agnostic data attribution, where the goal is to understand how individual
training examples affect model behavior, and explainable artificial intelligence, where the
goal is to assess how input features contribute to a black-box model output [15, 17, 23, 31].
In these applications, data points or features are treated as players in a cooperative game.
For a game with n players, the utility of a coalition is represented by a function u : 2[n] →R,
where [n] = {1, . . . , n}, and u(S) measures the performance of a coalition S ⊆[n], such as
the prediction accuracy of a model trained on the data points in S. Given u, a probabilistic
value assigns to each player i ∈[n] a weighted average of its marginal contributions over all
coalitions that do not contain i:

Exact computation therefore becomes impractical even for moderately large n.
The sampling-based approximation has therefore become the standard approach, and a
broad literature has developed a range of Monte Carlo estimators for probabilistic values;
see, for example, the surveys [4, 9]. These methods can be organized by how they identify
the target under a sampling distribution. One representative family uses weighted-average
identification, which directly represents the target as a weighted average of coalition
utilities. This family includes weighted sample means [6, 11], self-normalized weighted
sample means [16, 20, 31], and, more recently, regression-adjusted estimators [33]. Another
widely used family exploits weighted least-squares (WLS) identification, which obtains the
target through a projection of the utility function onto a structured feature space. This
family includes empirical WLS estimators for Shapley values [5, 12, 23, 26] and for general
semivalues [19].
Our key observation is that these seemingly different estimator constructions share a common
first-order error structure. For both weighted-average and least-squares estimators, the
first-order error term can be written as an augmented inverse-probability-weighted influence
term indexed by the sampling law and a working surrogate function. This surrogate may
be specified explicitly, as in regression-adjusted estimators, or induced implicitly, as in self-
normalized or WLS estimators. This representation therefore provides a unified first-order
characterization of many existing estimators and, more importantly, enables an explicit
expression for the leading mean squared error (MSE).
In particular, following the first-order expansion, if n is fixed and m →∞, the leading term
is the variance of the corresponding residual influence term divided by the Monte Carlo
sample size m. For probabilistic-value estimation, however, this is not the primary regime
of interest. The utility is defined on 2n coalitions, whereas feasible sampling budgets are
typically much smaller than 2n, often only polynomial in n. Thus, the relevant question is

whether the first-order term already dominates the remainder with the polynomial sample
sizes used in practice. To address this gap, we establish non-asymptotic remainder bounds
for representative weighted-average and WLS estimators. Our results show that the first-
order MSE approximation has a small relative error once m = Ω(n polylog(n)), aligning
with the regime studied in the literature [19, 20, 26]. This validation turns the first-order
MSE expression into a practical design criterion, which makes explicit how the sampling
law and the surrogate choice jointly determine statistical efficiency.
Motivated by this criterion, we propose the Efficiency-Aware Surrogate-adjusted Estimator
(EASE), which directly targets the first-order MSE. The optimization problem involves
two coupled design choices: fitting the surrogate and choosing the sampling law. Since
the objective depends on the unknown utility function, both components must be learned
from sampled utility evaluations. Moreover, without structural restrictions, choosing the
sampling law would require optimizing over all distributions on 2[n], which is computationally
infeasible for even moderate n. EASE makes this design problem tractable through a two-
stage procedure. The initialization stage uses pilot samples to fit an initial surrogate and
construct a residual-aware sampling law within a structured class. The estimation stage
then draws samples from this learned law and constructs a cross-fitted augmented inverse-
probability-weighted estimator, with the surrogate chosen to minimize the empirical first-
order MSE criterion. Numerical results demonstrate that EASE consistently outperforms
state-of-the-art estimators for a range of probabilistic-value targets.

4
Error Expansion and Design Principles
The regularity framework in the previous section connects many estimator constructions to
a common first-order expansion indexed by a sampling law q and a working surrogate h. In
this section, we study the leading term in the mean squared error implied by this expansion
and its dependence on q and h. This dependence provides a design criterion for choosing
sampling laws and surrogates that reduce first-order risk.
If m →∞with the number of players n fixed, the regular expansion in (7) suggests that
the leading MSE is the variance of ψh divided by m under some regularity conditions. In
probabilistic-value estimation, however, this is not the main regime of interest. The utility
14


## Page 15


function is defined on 2n coalitions, while feasible sampling budgets are usually much smaller
than 2n, often only polynomial in n [2, 15, 19, 20, 26]. Although the constants hidden in
the lower-order remainder are independent of m, they may depend on n. A leading-order
criterion is therefore informative only when this remainder is small relative to the leading
term at the sample sizes used in practice.
We proceed in two steps. First, we identify the leading MSE term associated with a regular
estimator, which captures the joint dependence on q and h. This reveals that the surrogate
and the sampling law used by existing estimators are not necessarily the one that minimizes
leading error. Second, we give non-asymptotic conditions under which the leading term
dominates the remainder for polynomial sampling budgets.

5
Efficiency-Aware Surrogate-Adjusted Estimation
So far, we have shown that many existing estimator designs are regular relative to working
surrogate classes induced by their estimation procedures. The regularity framework gives a
common first-order expansion, of which the leading MSE is given by V (A; q, h)/m. This
quantity depends on both the sampling law q and surrogate choice h. Our goal in this
section is to construct an estimator that targets this first-order MSE criterion directly.
The explicit surrogate-adjusted estimator, or AIPW estimator, in (9) provides a natural
construction. For any fixed (q, h), its MSE is exactly V (A; q, h)/m, matching the leading
MSE of a regular estimator with the same (q, h). Therefore, given a working surrogate
class H, we can target the first-order efficiency by choosing the sampling law q and the
surrogate h ∈H to minimize V (A; q, h).
This optimization problem involves two coupled design choices: how to fit the surrogate h,
and how to choose the sampling law q. Because the criterion depends on the unknown utility
function u, both choices must be learned from sampled utility evaluations. In addition,
choosing q without further structure would require optimizing over all distributions on
2[n], which is computationally infeasible for even moderate n. We first study the surrogate-
learning problem for a fixed sampling law q, and then turn to the remaining question of

how to choose q within a structured class using pilot data.

Numerical Results
This section evaluates the proposed EASE method for probabilistic value estimation with
two main goals. First, we compare EASE with representative state-of-the-art methods from
three estimator families: RegressionMSR [33] as a surrogate-adjusted method, OFA [20] as
a weighted-average method, and PolySHAP [12] as a WLS method. In each comparison, we
keep the working surrogate class fixed to the class used either explicitly or implicitly by

from the EASE design rather than from a richer surrogate class. Second, we benchmark
EASE against additional baselines across several probabilistic values, showing that the same
efficiency-aware design principle remains effective beyond the matched comparisons. Code
for reproducing the numerical experiments is available in the EASE GitHub repository.
