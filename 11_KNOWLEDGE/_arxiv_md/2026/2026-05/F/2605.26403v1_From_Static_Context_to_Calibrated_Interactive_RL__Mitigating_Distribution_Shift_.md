Abstract
A long-standing goal of the research community is to develop highly interactive
LLM-based dialogue agents. Recent research focuses on optimizing policies based
on fixed offline logs (Static Context RL) or using a prompt-based simulator (Interac-
tive RL). In this work, we theoretically show that both paradigms are fundamentally
limited by context distribution shift—a mismatch between dialogue histories ob-
served during training and those encountered in real conversations. This shift
compounds quadratically over turns and severely degrades dialogue quality. Specif-
ically, we attribute this shift to two distinct sources: (i) policy-induced shift, arising
from training on static histories rather than self-generated trajectories; and (ii)
simulator-induced shift, stemming from discrepancies between simulated and real
human behaviors. To address these challenges, we propose Calibrated Interactive
RL, a unified framework that couples interactive RL with simulator alignment. By
aligning the simulator with human interaction patterns, our approach reduces the
sim-to-real gap and mitigates compounding distribution shifts. Experiments across
multiple dialogue tasks confirm our theoretical analysis: (i) Interactive RL signif-
icantly outperforms the Static Context baseline by mitigating policy distribution
shift; and (ii) calibrating simulators with our alignment method further bridges the
sim-to-real gap, yielding state-of-the-art downstream performance.

Introduction
A long-standing goal in the research community is to develop highly interactive LLM-based dialogue
agents that can sustain coherent, safe, and goal-directed multi-turn conversations Li et al. [2025],
Laban et al. [2025]. To advance this objective, recent work has increasingly adopted reinforcement
learning (RL) to align the model’s behavior with human preferences Ouyang et al. [2022], Bai et al.
[2022a,b]. Broadly, existing approaches can be categorized into two primary paradigms: Static
Context RL Lee et al. [2023], Hong et al. [2023], Verma et al. [2022], Snell et al. [2022], Rafailov
et al. [2023] and Interactive RL Wu et al. [2025], Shani et al. [2024], Carta et al. [2023]. The former
optimizes policy models using fixed offline contexts (i.e., serialized conversation histories), without
the need to generate the preceding turns. In contrast, the latter addresses the inherently dynamic
nature of dialogue by employing a prompt-based user simulator, thereby enabling agents to engage in
multi-turn interactions within a closed-loop learning framework.
Despite their preliminary efficacy, these approaches face fundamental limitations as shown in Table 1.

In this work, we establish the theoretical foundations for addressing these challenges. We formally

real human behaviors.
To this end, we introduce Calibrated Interactive RL, a unified framework that couples interactive
RL with the aligned simulator. Specifically, we first align the user simulator using Supervised Fine-
Tuning (SFT) on real human multi-turn interactions, enabling it to capture realistic human behaviors
such as clarification-seeking, stubbornness, and strict goal-adherence. Building upon this calibrated
simulator, we leverage interactive RL to optimize the policy on self-generated trajectories, thereby
explicitly addressing the policy-induced distribution shift and allowing the agent to learn essential
error-recovery skills.
Empirically, our analysis reveals that standard prompt-based simulators exhibit severe behavioral arti-
facts, most notably sycophancy—a tendency to blindly accept incorrect agent outputs or prematurely
leak ground-truth information. This effectively trivializes the RL interaction loop and inevitably
leads to reward hacking, where the policy learns to exploit the simulator’s permissiveness rather than
solving the actual task. By treating the simulator as a trainable agent and performing behavioral
cloning on high-quality offline logs, our Simulator Alignment framework rigorously grounds the sim-
ulation in human reality. Our experiments demonstrate that Interactive RL consistently outperforms
the Static Context baseline and that training with our Aligned Simulators yields further significant
gains. These results highlight the need for both closing the interaction loop and explicitly grounding
simulations.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We establish the theoretical foundation for multi-turn dialogue RL, reveal the source of the con-
text distribution shift in existing RL methods, and mathematically demonstrate that these shifts
compound quadratically over turns.
• We propose Calibrated Interactive RL, a unified framework that couples interactive policy
optimization with simulator alignment. We introduce an SFT-based calibration method that grounds
the user simulator in real human behavioral distributions, eliminating simulation artifacts (e.g.,
sycophancy) and effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap.
• Extensive experiments demonstrate that Interactive RL consistently outperforms Static Context
baselines. Furthermore, explicitly calibrating the simulator yields significant additional gains,
highlighting the critical importance of preventing reward hacking by grounding simulations in
human reality.

2.2
Distribution Shift and Exposure Bias
The discrepancy between training on ground-truth history (i.e., teacher forcing) and generating on the
model’s own history (i.e., student forcing) is a fundamental challenge in sequence generation, known
as exposure bias Bengio et al. [2015], Ranzato et al. [2015]. In the context of decision-making, this
phenomenon parallels the distribution shift analyzed in imitation learning. Ross et al. Ross et al.
[2011] demonstrated that behavior cloning (i.e., offline training) results in errors that accumulate
quadratically (O(H2)) with the horizon H, whereas interactive expert guidance (i.e., DAGGER) can
reduce this to linear growth. While recent works have analyzed this shift in the context of single-turn
reasoning chains Wang et al. [2023], our work formally extends this analysis to the multi-turn dialogue
setting. We derive a rigorous bound illustrating how static context training fails to generalize to the
dynamic distribution induced by the policy’s own actions during prolonged interactions.
2.3
LLM-based User Simulators
Employing LLM-based simulators as surrogates for human users has emerged as a prominent
trend for scalable evaluation and training Shi et al. [2019], Tseng et al. [2021], Hu et al. [2023],
Gandhi et al. [2026]. Park et al. Park et al. [2023] demonstrated that LLM agents can simulate
believable social behaviors, and automated evaluation frameworks like MT-Bench Bai et al. [2024]
and AlpacaEval Dubois et al. [2023] utilize LLM judges to proxy human preferences. In the
domain of interactive training, recent studies have employed "Self-Play" or simulator-based RL to
improve task performance Shao et al. [2023], Chen et al. [2024], Silver et al. [2017]. However,
uncalibrated simulators often exhibit distinct artifacts, such as "sycophancy" (agreeing with the
model’s errors) Sharma et al. [2023] or unrealistic persona consistency Shanahan et al. [2023]. These
artifacts can be exploited by the policy agent during RL training, leading to reward hacking Wang
et al. [2025] where the agent optimizes for simulator approval rather than genuine task success.
Unlike previous works that use off-the-shelf LLMs as simulators, we propose a Simulator Alignment
framework, treating the simulator as a trainable agent optimized to minimize the sim-to-real gap,
thereby ensuring that interactive training yields robust real-world performance.
3
Distribution Shift in Multi-turn RL
In this section, we formally analyze the distribution shifts inherent in multi-turn dialogue training. We
derive two fundamental bounds that motivate our proposed Calibrated Interactive RL framework.
First, we show that standard offline training (i.e., Static Context RL) induces a biased objective due to
compounding policy deviations. Second, we demonstrate that while Interactive RL addresses this
by closing the interaction loop, it relies on simulator fidelity, as simulator errors also accumulate
quadratically. These insights underscore the necessity of our Simulator Alignment approach. Detailed
proofs are provided in Appendix A.

model trained purely via Static Context RL has only seen perfect, expert-guided trajectory histories.
When thrust into this self-generated, flawed context during deployment, the agent lacks the essential
error-recovery skills. Instead of correcting itself, it is prone to hallucinate further (yielding large
ε2, ε3, . . . ). Consequently, an initially negligible mistake cascades dynamically over the horizon H,
leading to catastrophic failure. Interactive RL mitigates this by explicitly exposing the agent to its
own mistakes during training, thereby teaching it how to navigate back to the correct path.

Experiments
We conduct extensive experiments to validate two core hypotheses: (1) Interactive RL significantly
outperforms Static Context RL by mitigating the distribution shift between offline training contexts
and on-policy generation; and (2) Simulator Alignment is critical for interactive training—optimizing
the simulator to mimic human intent and natural interaction patterns leads to superior downstream
policy performance.
5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets and Benchmarks.
To rigorously evaluate the dynamic interaction capabilities of our
framework, we employ two challenging multi-turn dialogue benchmarks inspired by recent collabora-
tive agent evaluations Wu et al. [2025]. These datasets are explicitly designed to test long-horizon
planning, ambiguity resolution, and error recovery:
• MediumDocEdit-Chat (Collaborative Editing): Derived from document editing corpora, this
task requires the agent to help a user iteratively refine a document. Unlike single-turn instruction
following, user intents in this dataset are frequently ambiguous or incomplete. The agent must
proactively ask clarifying questions, propose partial edits, and adapt to shifting user feedback over
multiple turns. The primary objective evaluation metric is BLEU, calculated between the final
model-refined document and the ground-truth target text.
• MATH-Chat (Conversational Reasoning): Adapted from the MATH dataset, this benchmark
evaluates the agent’s ability to act as an interactive tutor. Instead of directly outputting the
final answer, the agent must guide a simulated student (who may make logical errors or express
confusion) step-by-step toward the correct solution. This requires robust reasoning scaffolding and
the ability to correct trajectory deviations dynamically. The task success is measured by the final
answer Accuracy (Acc.).

In Phase I, the user simulator (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) is trained via SFT

8,192. We use a constant learning rate of 5 × 10−6 with Adam (β1=0.9, β2=0.98) and asymmetric
PPO-style clipping Schulman et al. [2017] (ϵ ∈[0.2, 0.28]). Full hyperparameter tables are provided
in Appendix D.

Following the evaluation protocol of COLLABLLM, we assess performance
along two primary dimensions. (1) Task Performance: Measured by BLEU for editing and Accuracy
for math reasoning. (2) Efficiency: We report the average generation length (#Toks, in thousands of
tokens), where lower values indicate more concise, natural conversational turns rather than monolithic
document dumps. We additionally report the Average Score (arithmetic mean of BLEU and ACC) as

Conclusion
In this work, we established the theoretical foundations for the limitations of Static Context RL,
formally proving that training on fixed offline logs induces a context distribution shift where errors
compound quadratically over turns. To address this fundamental bottleneck, we proposed Cali-
brated Interactive RL, a unified framework that couples interactive training with rigorous simulator
alignment. We demonstrated that simply closing the interaction loop is insufficient; the success
of interactive learning critically depends on minimizing the sim-to-real gap. By treating the user
simulator as a trainable agent and optimizing it to maximize a discriminator-derived realism reward,
we effectively aligned the simulation environment with human interaction patterns. Our extensive
experiments confirm that our framework not only mitigates policy-induced distribution shifts but
also fosters robust information-seeking behaviors, significantly outperforming baselines relying on
static contexts or uncalibrated simulators. This work highlights the necessity of high-fidelity, aligned
simulation as a prerequisite for the next generation of interactive dialogue agents.

• Statistics: The RL training set contains ∼38,118 editing prompts (Parquet format). Evaluation

In the second phase, the SFT-calibrated simulator is frozen, and we optimize the policy agent πθ via

E
Limitations and Future Work
While our work establishes a theoretical framework for mitigating distribution shift in interactive
dialogue and demonstrates strong empirical results, we acknowledge certain limitations that point
towards future research directions.
Computational Overhead. A primary limitation of the Calibrated Interactive RL framework is its
computational demand during the training phase. Unlike Static Context RL, which only requires
a single forward-backward pass over fixed offline datasets, our closed-loop paradigm necessitates
running generation steps for both the policy agent and the user simulator during the rollout phase.
This substantially increases the inference overhead and memory footprint, especially for long-horizon
conversations. Future work could address this bottleneck by distilling the aligned simulator into a
much smaller parameter-efficient model, or by exploring offline-to-online RL paradigms where the
22


## Page 23


agent is pre-trained heavily on static logs and only fine-tuned interactively for a few critical turns to
rectify specific distribution shifts.
Advanced Simulator Calibration. A second limitation lies in our current reliance on Supervised
Fine-Tuning for Phase I simulator calibration. While behavioral cloning is highly effective for
matching human distributions within the support of the training data, it may struggle to handle heavily
out-of-distribution dialogue states dynamically induced by a rapidly exploring policy agent. In future
work, we plan to extend simulator training beyond SFT by formulating it as a Reinforcement Learning
problem. By optimizing the simulator to maximize a discriminator-derived "Realism Reward," it can
proactively learn to maintain human-like stubbornness and constraints even in unobserved scenarios.
Furthermore, drawing inspiration from Generative Adversarial Networks and adversarial imitation
learning, a highly promising direction is to introduce an adversarial co-evolution framework. Instead
of relying on a fixed, rule-based or static reward model, we could dynamically co-train a discriminator
to distinguish between real human interaction logs and simulated trajectories. The simulator would
be trained to actively fool this continuously evolving discriminator, establishing an auto-curriculum
that mutually pushes both the dialogue policy and the simulator toward a higher level of behavioral
fidelity and robustness.
