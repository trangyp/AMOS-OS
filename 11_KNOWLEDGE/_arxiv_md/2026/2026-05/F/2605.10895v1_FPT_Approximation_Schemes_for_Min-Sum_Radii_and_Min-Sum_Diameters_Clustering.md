Abstract
In the classical Min-Sum Radii problem (MSR) we are given a set X of n points in a metric
space and a positive integer k ∈[n]. Our goal is to partition X into k subsets (the clusters) so
as to minimize the sum of the radii of these clusters. The Min-Sum Diameters problem (MSD)
is defined analogously, where instead of the radii of the clusters we consider their diameters.
For both problems we present FPT approximation schemes for the natural parameter k.
Specifically, given ε > 0, we show how to compute (1 + ε)-approximations for both MSD and
MSR in time (1/ε)knO(1) and (1/ε)O(k/ε log 1/ε)npoly(1/ε) respectively. The previous best FPT
approximation algorithms for these problems have approximation factors 4+ε and 2+ε, respec-
tively, and finding an FPT approximation scheme for both these problems had been outstanding
open problems.

Introduction
We consider two classical clustering objectives closely related to each other: min-sum radii and
min-sum diameters, which seek to partition the points of a given metric space into k clusters, such
that the sum of their radii or diameters is minimized. Both these problems have long been known
to be NP-hard, but their approximability has been a source of much interest, as we detail below.
In this work, we study these questions from the perspective of parameterized complexity, and ask:
what can we do in time f(k) poly(n), where k is the number of clusters and n the size of the metric
space?
Formally, both the Min-Sum Radii and Min-Sum Diameters problems are defined on a (sym-
metric) metric space M = (X, d) with X being a collection of n points. Given an integer k ∈[n]
denoting the target number of clusters, the goal in both problems is to partition the point set X
into k sets (also called clusters) S1, S2, . . . , Sk. The difference between the two problems is in their
objective functions:

Both these problems are NP-hard [DMR+00, GKK+08], and hence have been studied using the

algorithms [DMR+00, CP04, FJ22, BERW24], the approximation ratio for MSR has been improved
to 3 + ε; since the two objectives are separated by a factor of at most 2, this implies a (6 + ε)-
approximation for MSD as well, which is currently the best approximation factor known.
The
MSD problem does not admit a polynomial time algorithm with an approximation factor of better
than 2, unless P = NP [DMR+00]; however, MSR admits a quasi-polynomial time approximation
scheme (QPTAS) [GKK+08], and hence a polynomial time approximation scheme (PTAS) is likely
to exist.
MSR and MSD are also well-studied in terms of parameterized approximation algorithms, where
we allow the running time to depend super-polynomially in some parameter.
For the natural
parameter k, the best known results are a 2 + ε (resp., 4 + ε) approximation for MSR (resp., MSD)
in FPT time f(k, ε) · ng(ε) for some computable functions f, g [BLS23, CXXZ24]. In this work,
our focus is on FPT approximation schemes—i.e., (1 + ε)-approximation algorithms running in
time f(k, ε) · npoly(1/ε) for some computable function f. The following natural question remains
unresolved: do these two problems admit FPT Approximation Schemes?
1.1
Our Results
The main results of our work answer the above question in the affirmative. For the MSR problem,
which is known to be W[1]-hard [BBGH25], we substantially improve upon the mentioned (2 + ε)-
approximation in FPT time [BLS23, CXXZ24].
Theorem 1.1 (Min-Sum Radii). For any ε > 0, one can compute a (1+ε)-approximation for MSR
in deterministic time (1/ε)O(k/ε ln 1/ε)nO(1/ε3 ln 1/ε).
We then turn our attention to the MSD problem. It is remarkable that essentially all previous
approximation algorithms for this problem were obtained by losing a factor of 2 over the results
for MSR. In other words, the best approximation factors were 6 + ε and 4 + ε in polynomial and
FPT time respectively. We manage to break past this issue, and give a (1 + ε)-approximation in
FPT time:
Theorem 1.2 (Min-Sum Diameters). For any ε > 0, one can compute a 1 + ε approximation for
MSD in randomized time (O(1/ε))knO(1) and in deterministic time (O(k/ε))knO(1).
Note that such a result is not possible in polynomial time: an approximation of better than a
factor of 2 would imply that P = NP [DMR+00]. Our approach also gives an exact algorithm with
runtime nk+O(1) (see Appendix C), thereby improving the previous best runtime of n5k+O(1) given
by [BBGH25].
The same algorithm can also be utilized to give an FPT approximation scheme for the MSD
problem (and thus a FPT (2 + ε)-approximation algorithm for the MSR problem) with mergeable
constraints. Here, we are given an upper bound k on the number of clusters and each cluster is
required to satisfy some constraint. The constraint is such that, if two disjoint subsets of points

Our Techniques
1.2.1
The Algorithm for Min-Sum Diameters
Our FPT approximation scheme for MSD is surprisingly clean, once viewed from the right perspec-
tive. To explain the guiding intuition, let us present a slight variant of our algorithm. We claim that
the following algorithm has probability at least (ε/k)O(k) of returning a 1+O(ε) approximate solu-
tion; therefore, an FPT randomized approximation scheme can be obtained by repeating it an FPT
number of times. Our recursive algorithm gets in input a subset of points S ⊆X and a parameter
k′ ∈[k], and returns a clustering of S with at most k′ clusters. With probability 1/2, the algorithm
returns {S} itself as a clustering (this happens also when k′ = 1). Otherwise, let x, y ∈S be a
diameter witness pair, i.e., d(x, y) = diam(S). The algorithm chooses R ∈[0, diam(S)] uniformly
at random and k1 ∈[k′ −1] uniformly at random. Then it returns the union of the two solutions
obtained recursively for the subproblems (S1, k1) and (S \ S1, k′ −k1), where S1 = S ∩Ball(x, R)
and Ball(x, R) is a ball of radius R centered at x (containing all the points at distance at most R
from x). The desired clustering is obtained from the root call (X, k).

1.3
Related Work
Polynomial-time Approximate Algorithms. Both exact and approximation algorithms for
MSR and MSD problems have been extensively studied. (Any α-approximation for MSR immedi-
ately implies a 2α-approximation for MSD, so we do not explicitly mention all the implied results
for MSD henceforth.) For approximation algorithms running in polynomial time, a sequence of
improvements [DMR+00, CP04, FJ22] culminated in a (3 + ε)-approximation for MSR by Buchem
et al. [BERW24]; they also give a (3 + ε)-approximation for MSR with outliers, and a (3.5 + ε)-
approximation for MSR with outliers and generalized lower bounds, where there is a lower bound
for each point specifying the number of points that must be assigned to it, if it is made a cluster
center.
Exact Algorithms. A remarkable result of Gibson et al. [GKK+08] showed that the MSR problem
can be solved exactly in nO(log n log ∆) time, where ∆is the aspect ratio of the metric space; they
used this algorithm to also give a QPTAS. In another work [GKK+12], they showed that MSR can
be solved in polynomial time for Euclidean spaces of fixed dimension. The runtime of the exact
algorithm for MSR in general metric spaces was improved to nO(log n log log n+log ∆) by Banerjee et
al. [BBGH24]. While there is a trivial polynomial time exact algorithm for MSR when k is a constant
(one can choose the centers and the radii of all the clusters in nO(k) time), an exact polynomial
time algorithm for MSD for a constant k was first given by Behsaz and Salavatipour [BS15], with a
6


## Page 7


runtime of nO(k2). This was recently improved to n5k+O(1)by [BBGH25], which we further improve
to nk+O(1) in Appendix C.
Parameterized (Approximate) Algorithms. In the setting of parameterized algorithms, the
MSR problem parametrized by the number of clusters admits a (2 + ε)-approximation [BLS23,
CXXZ24]; to the best of our knowledge, this result (and the implied 4 + ε-approximation for
MSD) are the best previous results for general metric spaces. For metric spaces with bounded
doubling dimension, a (1 + ε)-approximation in FPT time is known [BBGH25]. For capacitated
MSR, the works of [IV20, BLS23, JKY24, FG25] culminated in a factor of (3 + ε) for uniform
capacities [JKY24] and 5.83 for non-uniform capacities [FG25]. For capacitated MSD, FPT ap-
proximations of (4 + ε) and (7 + ε) are achievable for uniform and non-uniform capacities, respec-
tively [FG25]. FPT approximation algorithms have also been designed for variants incorporating
fairness constraints [CXXZ24, CDH+24]. More generally, recent works have explored FPT approx-
imations for MSR under mergeable constraints, where the clusters are required to satisfy constraints
having the property that if two disjoint clusters satisfy the constraint, then so does their union.
Carta et al. [CDH+24] gave a (6+ε)-approximation algorithm for this problem, which was improved
to a (4 + ε)-approximation by Bandyapadhyay and Chen [BC25].
Other clustering objectives. FPT approximation algorithms have also been extensively studied
for other popular clustering objectives such as k-center, k-median and k-means. Here, we only
discuss the results in the unconstrained setting. Via the standard reduction from dominating set,
a better-than-2 factor approximation for the k-center problem is known to be W[2]-hard, and a
2-approximation in polynomial time is folklore. For the k-median and k-means problems, Cohen-
Addad et al. [CAGK+19] gave FPT algorithms with approximation ratios of 1+2/e+ε and 1+8/e+ε
respectively, and (almost) matching hardness results assuming gap-ETH.

Preliminaries and Notation
For a metric space M = (X, d), a k-clustering (or a k-partition) is a collection of subsets S1, . . . , Sk′,
where k′ ≤k, with X = ∪i∈[k′]Si. Observe that we allow a k-clustering to have fewer than k clusters.
For the objective functions that we consider, this is without loss of generality: one can transform
any clustering with k′ < k clusters into one having exactly k clusters and no larger cost, by simply
removing k −k′ points from some of the clusters and forming singleton clusters using these points.
Given c ∈X and a value r ≥0, we let Ball(c, r) denote the ball of radius r around the
center c: i.e., Ball(c, r) := {x ∈X | d(c, x) ≤r}.
Given a ball B, we use c(B) and r(B) to
denote its center and radius respectively. Recall that the diameter of set S ⊆X is defined as
diam(S) := maxp,q∈S d(p, q); any vertex pair p, q ∈S with d(p, q) = diam(S) is called a diameter
witness pair.
When considering the MSR problem, we define the cost for a set B of balls to be cost(B) :=
P

on the boundary have a negligible cost compared to the radius of E, and that a constant
number of optimal balls inside E account for a constant fraction of the radius of E. This is
done in Section 4.4.1.

Concluding Remarks
We consider the min-sum diameters and min-sum radii problems, where we partition a set of points
into k parts, to minimize the sum of the diameters or radii of the parts. For both problems, we
present the first FPT approximation schemes, with respect to the natural parameter k; the previous
best FPT approximation algorithms for these problems have approximation factors 4+ε and 2+ε,
respectively. Specifically, given ε > 0, we show how to compute (1 + ε)-approximations for both
MSD and MSR in time (1/ε)knO(1) and (1/ε)O(k/ε log 1/ε)npoly(1/ε) respectively.

Time complexity of recMSR2
In any call recMSR2(S, t, ℓ), we consider (1
ε)O(k) candidate clusterings in the set C. For each of
these clusterings, we iterate over O(2k) choices for the subset of good defensive balls, and form a
set of up to t temporary subproblems. Then we iterate over all (1/ε)O(k) possible expansions of the
chosen balls, and then make a call for each subproblem to guessAndSolveMSR2 at level ℓ+ 1. Each
invocation of guessAndSolveMSR2 makes O(n2M) calls to recMSR2 at level ℓ+ 1 corresponding to
the guessed optimal balls. Thus, any level ℓcall to recMSR2 makes (1/ε)O(k)·2k ·(1/ε)O(k)·t·n2M =
(1/ε)O(k)n2M calls to recMSR2 at level ℓ+ 1. Hence, the recursion tree of recMSR2 has a branching
factor of (1/ε)O(k)n2M and a depth of L as there are L levels.
