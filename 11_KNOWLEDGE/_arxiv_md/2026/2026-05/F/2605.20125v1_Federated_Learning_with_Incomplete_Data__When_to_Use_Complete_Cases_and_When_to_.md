Summary
Privacy constraints have driven the rise of federated learning (FL), which enables multi-site
analyses without sharing individual participant data. We develop a framework for FL with missing
data, identifying conditions under which the complete case (CC) estimator is preferred over the
inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator. For settings where the CC estimator fails, we
introduce a calibrated weight estimation approach that combines candidate weighting models
across sites and remains consistent if at least one is correctly specified. Consistency conditions
are stated at the site level, ensuring that the federated estimator inherits validity from local
properties. We derive a sandwich variance estimator that accounts for uncertainty in weight
estimation, and illustrate the framework by evaluating risk factors for 90-day mortality among
patients with pleural infections treated with intrapleural enzyme therapy.
Key words: Federated Learning; Inverse Probability Weighting; M-estimation; Missing Data; Multiply
robust; Site Heterogeneity

1. Introduction
Federated learning (FL) enables multi-site collaboration without pooling individual participant
data (IPD), addressing privacy regulations and institutional policies that increasingly prohibit
data sharing (Malpetti and others, 2025). A growing body of work develops FL methods for
regression estimation that address three key constraints: (i) privacy—only summary statistics may
leave a site (Xu and others, 2021), (ii) communication efficiency—one or few exchanges of data
or information between sites, and (iii) heterogeneity—allowing site-specific covariate distributions
(Jordan and others, 2019; Duan and others, 2021; Li and others, 2024). However, most methods
assume complete data at each site—an assumption rarely met in practice (Li and others, 2023).
Recent work has begun adapting missing data approaches to FL settings, primarily through
imputation-based approaches: e.g., the communication-efficient surrogate likelihood framework
(Jordan and others, 2019), distributed multiple imputation (Chang and others, 2020), and the
FedIMPUTE family (Li and others, 2025). These approaches typically assume data are missing at
random (MAR), with limited approaches for missing not at random (MNAR) settings (Galimard
and others, 2018). While flexible, imputation approaches are often computationally intensive,
with multiple imputation approaches at times requiring thousands of communication rounds
(Chang and others, 2020), which may limit practicality in real-word settings.
The inverse probability weighting (IPW) estimator offers an alternative by up-weighting com-
plete observations rather than imputing missing values, creating a pseudo-sample that approxi-
mates the full data-generating process (Robins and others, 1994). While the IPW estimator has
been extended to federated causal inference for confounding adjustment in fully observed data
(Yin and others, 2025), weighted approaches for missing data in FL remain underdeveloped. Key
challenges in implementing the IPW estimator in FL include: (i) how to leverage information
across the network to estimate the weights when IPD cannot be pooled, and (ii) how to properly
quantify uncertainty when the weighting model—a nuisance component, not of primary interest

In this paper, we develop a framework, for federated estimation with incomplete data. Our ap-
proach is organized around a decision-oriented question: given what is missing and what explains
the missingness at each site, what is the simplest valid estimator? We make three contribu-
tions. First, we characterize when the CC estimator is consistent in FL by establishing site-level
conditions that are sufficient for consistency of the federated estimator, explicitly enumerating
the combinations of missing variables and missingness mechanisms, thus extending the work of
Hughes and others (2019) and Little and others (2024). Second, for settings where the CC es-
timator introduces bias, we extend the IPW estimation framework for FL using two strategies:
site-specific weighting, in which each site estimates its weighting model independently, and cali-
brated weighting, in which sites share estimated parameters and combine candidate models via
calibration. We show that the calibrated approach guarantees consistency if at least one can-
didate model is correctly specified per site, enabling sites to borrow strength without sharing
IPD. Third, we derive sandwich variance estimators for both IPW estimator approaches that
correctly account for the missingness itself and uncertainty in the estimated weights—we show
that ignoring this uncertainty leads to invalid inference even when point estimates are unbiased.
These estimators decompose into site-specific contributions that can be aggregated without shar-
ing IPD. All proposed estimators are either one-shot (one round of communication) or few-shot
algorithms, making them practical under real-world governance constraints.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces notation and the
federated data structure. Section 3 develops the estimators: we begin with the CC estimator
and establish when it is valid, then present federated IPW under site-specific and calibrated
weight estimation, and provide the form of the sandwich variance estimator. Section 4 evaluates
performance through simulations. Section 5 applies the methods to a multi-site study of risk
factors for 90-day mortality among patients with pleural infections treated with intrapleural

3.2.3
Consistency conditions
Proposition 1(ii) characterizes when the IPW estimator is con-
sistent: when the oracle probability of a complete observation depends on Y , consistency requires
that the weighting model be correctly specified at those particular sites. The calibrated weight es-
timation framework facilitates correct specification by combining J candidate models across sites,
achieving robustness through multiplicity (Han, 2014). Beyond this, the calibration framework
allows sites with an insufficient sample size to leverage information from larger sites.
When the oracle probability does not depend on Y , correct specification is not required;
however, including Y in the weighting model may introduce bias in certain settings, such as
when X is missing. Empirically, Y may appear associated with missingness in X due to the
structure of the outcome model (2.1) rather than the true mechanism: when X is missing, Y
will often be significant in a fitted weighting model even when missingness does not depend
on Y (Bartlett and others, 2014; Vazquez and others, 2024). Distinguishing true dependence
from induced association requires subject-matter knowledge, often formalized using missing data
directed acyclic graphs (Hughes and others, 2019). The distinction matters because including Y
when the oracle probability does not depend on Y can introduce bias through a collider structure
(Supplementary Material, Section S.2.4); we illustrate this in the simulations of Section 4.

Estimator performance was evaluated in terms of percent bias, 95% confidence interval cov-

overestimation and below one indicate underestimation. Standard errors were computed with
and without the sandwich variance correction to assess the impact of ignoring uncertainty in the

4.0.1
Scenario 1: Homogeneous Missingness.
We first considered the case in which the miss-
ingness mechanism was the same across sites. The subject-level missingness in X was roughly
60%, and was introduced according to Pr(R = 1 | X, Z) = expit(−0.1 + 0.2X + 0.2Z1 + 0.2Z2)
under MNAR and Pr(R = 1 | Y, Z) = expit(−0.1 + 0.1Y + 0.2Z1 + 0.2Z2) under MAR. We
compared five weighting strategies: (i) Oracle (true weights), (ii) Pooled (weights from pooled
data), (iii) Site-specific (local estimation only), (iv) Calibrated (sharing bα from the largest site),
and (v) Uniform (drawn from the inverse of Uniform(0.1, 0.9) to evaluate sensitivity to weight
misspecification). To estimate the weights, we fit a logistic regression model for Pr(R = 1|Y, Z)
adjusting for (Y, Z1, Z2). This intentional choice induced misspecification for the MNAR sites.
For the calibrated weights, only the largest site was asked to share its weighting model estimates.
While neither the pooled estimator nor the oracle estimator is possible in the federated setting,
they serve as comparison benchmarks.

ability to correct for local misspecification, half the sites were assigned a MAR mechanism with

Pr(RX = 1|Y, Z) = expit(−0.2 + 0.1Y + 0.1Z1 + 0.1Z2) or expit(−0.2 + 0.1Y + 0.05Z1 + 0.05Z2 +
0.1Y Z1). A working model excluding the interaction was used for both the site-specific weighting
and pooled IPW estimators, leading to misspecification in half of the sites. For the calibrated
weighting estimation framework, coefficient estimates from the two largest sites—one from each

5. Application to Intrapleural Enzyme Therapy
Pleural infection (PI) leads to substantial morbidity and mortality despite advances in man-
agement. For patients who fail initial therapy, intrapleural enzyme therapy (IET) using tissue
plasminogen activator and DNase has been shown to improve outcomes, though heterogeneity in
patient response persists. Using a multicenter cohort of patients treated with IET, we evaluate
the measure of albumin at baseline as a clinical factor associated with 90-day mortality. Albumin
is a prognostic marker previously validated in patients with confirmed PI but not yet evaluated
specifically among IET recipients (White and others, 2015).
Data for this study were obtained from two sources: a retrospective multicenter cohort con-
ducted across 23 medical centers in the United States and the United Kingdom (2014–2020)
hosted by the University of North Carolina (UNC) at Chapel Hill, and a cohort from five Johns
Hopkins University (JHU) Health System hospitals (2018–2025). The analytic sample comprised
1,948 individuals (UNC: 1,494; JHU: 454), of whom approximately 88% (n = 1,710) had com-
plete data. Albumin was missing for approximately 14% of UNC individuals and approximately
2% of JHU individuals. We used logistic regression to estimate the association between 90-day
mortality and low albumin (<2.7 g/dL), adjusting for sex. The weighting model for albumin was
fit using logistic regression with fully observed baseline covariates: age, purulence, sex, and BUN.
The outcome was excluded from the weighting model since it was measured after baseline and
its inclusion could open a collider structure (Section S.2.5).

and 70.0%, respectively) and presenting with low albumin (56.9% and 49.9%). However, 90-day
mortality was slightly higher at JHU (18.3% vs. 14.4%), and JHU individuals were generally
older (aged 50 or older: 59% vs. 31%). Low albumin was associated with increased odds of 90-day

6. Discussion
Federated learning has largely developed under the assumption of complete data (Jordan and
others, 2019; Duan and others, 2021). Multiple imputation can require thousands of communi-
cation rounds to converge across sites (Chang and others, 2020), a cost that limits collaboration.
This work resolves that tension by situating federated estimation with incomplete data within
the M-estimation framework. Expressing the CC and IPW estimators as solutions to estimating
equations allows both the estimator and its sandwich variance estimator to be decomposed into
site-specific contributions, making explicit which summary quantities each site transmits and
bounding the communications required while adjusting for missing data.
The framing into an M-estimation framework pays off first for the CC estimator. It is con-
sistent whenever the probability of a complete observation does not depend on Y —which is


## Page 21


Federated learning with missing data
21
achievable under MCAR, many MAR, and certain MNAR mechanisms. This also clarifies a dis-
tinction important in applied practice, where MNAR is routinely equated with non-ignorability
(Heitjan and Rubin, 1991) and, by extension, with bias in analyses that involve only complete
cases. What determines validity is not the missingness label but dependence on the outcome
(Hughes and others, 2019; Little and others, 2024). The classical question of Heitjan and Rubin
(1991)—when can a coarsening mechanism be ignored?—warrants revisiting here, because in the
federated setting ignorability is a site-level property: mechanisms may differ across sites, and
the CC estimator remains consistent so long as each site satisfies the conditions enumerated in
Table 1. We additionally clarify when the coarsening must be ignored, rather than posing it as
an option to adjust for it or not. Forcing Y into a weighting model that is already unnecessary
may introduce bias through a collider structure (Bartlett and others, 2014; Vazquez and others,
2024), which our simulations show that it reduces coverage levels as the the number of sites grow.
When the CC estimator is biased, we provide two IPW estimator variants that differ in their
weighting approach. The site-specific approach estimates the weighting model locally, while the
calibrated approach combines candidate models across sites—allowing small sites to supplement
their local weighting models with models estimated elsewhere. By situating this problem within
an M-estimation framework, we deliver the stacked sandwich variance estimator to correct for
weighting-model estimation; our simulations show that ignoring nuisance parameter estimation
produces nominal coverage failures. Under a linear regression setting, both estimation and infer-
ence stay bounded at four rounds for the calibrated approach and three rounds for the CC and
site-specific IPW estimators. Under a GLM setting with finite outcome-covariate combinations,
the number of communication rounds for the site-specific and calibrated approaches stays the
same; however, the CC estimator reduces to one-shot.
The pleural infection study, a setting with a finite number of outcome-covariate combina-
tions, illustrates the site-level consistency conditions of Proposition 1(i). With mortality excluded

S.1.1
Sufficient information approach
Under a linear regression model with normally distributed errors, the CC and IPW estimators
can be computed via the sufficient information approach. Each site transmits (weighted) cross-
product matrices, and the coordinating site aggregates these to obtain bθ via ordinary least squares
(OLS) or weighted OLS. Variance estimation requires an additional round in which sites compute
local contributions to the sandwich estimator after receiving bθ. This yields a three-round algo-
rithm for CC (Algorithm S.1) and site-specific IPW (Algorithm S.2), and a four-round algorithm
for calibrated IPW (Algorithm S.3), which requires an additional round for sharing nuisance
parameter estimates across sites.

S.1.2
Count aggregation approach
When a GLM is of interest and the outcome-covariate space is discrete with a finite number
of unique combinations, the count aggregation approach enables a more communication-efficient
estimation. Under this approach, each site identifies the unique outcome-covariate combinations
uj = (yj, xj, zj) among complete cases and transmits these combinations along with their corre-
sponding (weighted) counts.
For the CC estimator, count aggregation yields a one-shot algorithm (Algorithm S.4): once
the coordinating site receives the combinations and counts, it can solve the estimating equation
and compute the variance estimate without additional communication rounds. This is because
the variance components bACC and bBCC depend only on the score function Sθ(uj; bθ) and its
derivatives, which can be evaluated from the transmitted counts. For the IPW estimators, count
aggregation reduces communication but does not achieve one-shot estimation (Algorithm S.5).
Weight estimation must occur before aggregation, and for calibrated weights, an additional round
is needed to share nuisance parameter estimates. Variance estimation for IPW requires the stacked
M-estimating equation components, which depend on both the outcome model and the nuisance
model; these cannot be fully reconstructed from the aggregated counts alone and require a sepa-
rate communication rounds.
We note that under count aggregation, combinations with fewer than T observations (typically
T = 6 or T = 11) must be suppressed or perturbed to comply with disclosure control policies
(Malpetti and others, 2025); we recommend readers to check with their institutions before sharing
any count data. This constraint may limit applicability when the covariate space is large relative
to site sample size(s). A worked example using pleural infection data is provided in Section S.6
showcasing the information transmitted to each of the sites.

To illustrate how the IPW estimator may introduce bias, we extend a missing data directed

scenario in which the probability of a complete case is modeled using (Y, Z) rather than (X, Z).
This opens a non-causal path from ϵ to RX through Y , inducing dependence between Y and RX

closed; however, the open backdoor path ϵ 99K RX remains, leading to biased estimation of θ.
This example illustrates that the IPW estimator can introduce, rather than mitigate, bias
when the weighting model opens a non-causal path through a collider structure. In such settings,
when missingness is not driven by Y , the CC estimator is the preferred estimator—a finding
explored by Bartlett and others (2014) and extended by Vazquez and others (2024) to other
forms of data coarsening. As these authors note, this setting arises when Y is measured during
follow-up while X and Z are recorded at study entry. By temporality, missingness in X cannot
depend on Y and is instead driven by (X, Z) or unmeasured baseline factors.

S.5.2
Regression Results
Table S.5 presents log odds ratio estimates for 90-day mortality using only JHU or UNC data, and
from their joint federated analysis, under the CC and IPW estimators. Under the CC estimator,
the estimated effect of low albumin was 0.20 (SE = 0.248) for JHU and 0.76 (SE = 0.163) for
UNC—the latter being larger and more efficient. The combined estimate fell between the two at
0.60 (SE = 0.136), pulled closer to the UNC estimate given its larger sample size. Sex was not
significantly associated with 90-day mortality in either sample or in the combined analysis.
For the JHU analysis, all three estimators yielded nearly identical results. This was expected
given the low percentage of missing data, which produced crude and weighted counts that were
highly similar. For the UNC analysis, the CC and IPW estimates were slightly more different,
reflecting the higher missingness rate in that sample. Among the IPW approaches, the log-odds
estimates were identical, though the standard errors for the intercept differed slightly between the
site-specific and calibrated approaches. Under the combined federated analysis, point estimates
differed slightly between the CC and IPW estimators, while the two IPW approaches agreed
closely, with only minor differences in standard errors.
Across all analyses, the agreement between CC and IPW is expected: because the outcome
was excluded from the missingness model on temporality grounds, both estimators are consis-
tent in this setting, and their close agreement provides empirical support for the validity of the
CC estimator. The near-identical results from the site-specific and calibrated IPW estimator are
similarly expected. With a binary outcome and two binary covariates, only eight unique combina-
tions exist, and the local data at each site may have been enough to estimate the weighting model
without borrowing strength across sites. The minor differences in standard errors between the
two IPW approaches reflect the adjustment for additional nuisance parameters under calibrated
weight estimation approach.
