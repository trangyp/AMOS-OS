Abstract—Advances in quantum computing introduce long-
term security challenges for widely deployed public-key cryp-
tographic systems used across blockchain platforms and de-
centralized applications. Although post-quantum cryptography
(PQC) standards are emerging, understanding quantum risk re-
mains fragmented across research, engineering, governance, and
investment communities. This demo presents Quantum Futures
Interactive, a live interdisciplinary demonstration platform com-
bining educational visualization, participatory interaction, and
cryptographic artifact generation to illustrate the transition from
classical to quantum-resilient blockchain systems. Participants
engage in a structured interaction flow including quantum threat
education, sentiment capture, technology prioritization, infras-
tructure tradeoff exploration, and generation of post-quantum
cryptographic outputs. The system integrates distributed trust
concepts, sustainability-aware infrastructure considerations, and
responsible innovation within an interactive decision frame-
work. The demonstration supports interdisciplinary dialogue
on blockchain resilience while aligning with United Nations
Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).
Index Terms—Post-Quantum Cryptography, Blockchain Se-
curity, Metaverse Infrastructure, Sustainable Computing, Inter-
active Demonstration, Blockchain Education, Distributed Trust,
Quantum Randomness, Digital Twins

mechanisms [4], [5]. Despite substantial progress in post-

We present Quantum Futures Interactive, a live demonstra-
tion system that allows participants to experience quantum risk
awareness education, post-quantum blockchain security exami-
nation, and infrastructure decision-making through interactive
visualization and simulation. The demonstration contributes
three implementation-oriented components. First, the system
architecture, illustrated in Fig. 1, presents an integrated server-
less design connecting user interaction, application services,
persistent storage, and quantum execution within a unified
framework. Second, the implementation is released as an open-
source platform supporting reproducibility and continued re-
search development, available on GitHub at https://github.com/
QuantBlockchain/qc-bc-interactive. Third, the demonstration
evaluates the impact of cryptographic transition across stake-
holder communities, as summarized in Table I, linking infras-
tructure decisions to interdisciplinary engagement and societal
objectives aligned with the United Nations Sustainable Devel-
opment Goals [12].
II. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE AND IMPLEMENTATION
Figure 1 illustrates the system architecture, implemented as
a serverless pipeline integrating user interaction, application
services, data persistence, and quantum execution within a
deployable research prototype. The architecture follows server-
less computing principles, where event-driven functions sup-
port scalability, reproducibility, and low operational overhead
for live demonstrations and audience interaction [13]. Client
requests are delivered through CloudFront and routed via API
Gateway to a Lambda-hosted Next.js application that manages
the interactive experience and API logic. Application state,
including sessions, sentiment input, voting results, and gen-
erated cryptographic artifacts, is stored in DynamoDB, while
optional artifacts are maintained in S3. Quantum execution is
delegated to a dedicated Lambda service invoking Amazon
Braket simulators or quantum processing units, reflecting
emerging quantum-computing-as-a-service architectures and
enabling reproducible interaction with real quantum hardware
within an interactive blockchain-focused demonstration envi-
ronment [14], [15].

The demonstration follows a seven-stage interaction flow
summarized in Table I. Pages 1–2 establish Context →Under-

and simulator-based execution environments [17]–[19] and

Scalability & Sustainability Issues. All stages contribute to
SDG 4, SDG 9, and SDG 16 through technical literacy,
resilient infrastructure awareness, and trustworthy digital sys-
tems; Pages 3–5 further emphasize SDG 17 through participa-
tory collaboration and consensus formation, while Pages 6–7
additionally address SDG 12 and SDG 13 through infrastruc-
ture sustainability and environmental impact considerations
informed by quantum computing life-cycle assessment studies
[20].

USER INTERFACE WORKFLOW AND TECHNICAL CONTEXT
This appendix provides a technical description of the seven-
page interaction workflow implemented in Quantum Futures
Interactive. The interface presents a structured progression
through the scientific foundations of quantum computation, the
implications of quantum algorithms for cryptographic systems,

the physical foundations of superconducting quantum computing.
and the infrastructural considerations associated with inte-
grating quantum computation and post-quantum cryptography
into distributed environments. The workflow culminates in the
generation of a verifiable cryptographic artifact derived from
quantum-generated randomness.
A. Page 1 — Scientific Context: Macroscopic Quantum Phe-
nomena and Computing Foundations
Page 1, shown in Fig. 2, introduces the physical principles
underlying contemporary quantum computing systems. Ex-
perimental demonstrations of macroscopic quantum tunneling
and energy quantization in superconducting circuits estab-
lished that engineered electrical systems can exhibit quantum-
mechanical behavior when operated at cryogenic temperatures.
These developments enabled the creation of artificial atoms
whose quantized energy levels can be controlled and measured,
forming the basis of superconducting qubits.
Superconducting qubits are implemented using Josephson
junction circuits that provide nonlinear inductance, allowing
isolation of two energy levels to form a quantum two-level
system. Microwave control pulses implement quantum gate
operations through unitary evolution, enabling programmable
quantum computation. The presentation establishes the rela-
tionship between advances in quantum hardware and compu-
tational models capable of affecting classical cryptographic
assumptions that underpin distributed trust systems.
B. Page 2 — Quantum Threat Model and Post-Quantum
Cryptographic Transition
Page 2 (Fig. 3) introduces the impact of quantum algorithms
on widely deployed cryptographic primitives through a visual
representation referred to as the Quantum Vulnerability Index.
The interface distinguishes between two categories of quantum
algorithmic effects. Shor’s algorithm enables efficient solu-
tions to integer factorization and discrete logarithm problems,
compromising cryptographic systems such as RSA, ECDSA,
and Diffie–Hellman. Grover’s algorithm provides a quadratic

The vulnerability indicators correspond to the following

• Diffie–Hellman key exchange loses forward secrecy in

• Cryptographic hash functions, such as SHA-256, ex-

quadratic speedups from quantum search algorithms.
• Advanced Encryption Standard (AES-256) retains ad-
equate security margins, as quantum attacks reduce effec-
tive key strength but do not compromise the underlying
construction.

E. Page 5 — Sentiment Aggregation and Technology Prioriti-
zation
Page 5 (Fig. 6) presents the aggregation of participant
responses collected in the previous stage and introduces a
structured voting mechanism over quantum-related technolo-
gies relevant to blockchain and distributed system security. The
visualization displays collective sentiment as a spatial aggrega-
tion of qualitative inputs, allowing participants to observe how
individual perceptions contribute to a shared representation of
community understanding.
The aggregation of sentiment serves two technical purposes.
First, it captures the perceived readiness and understanding of
emerging quantum technologies among participants, which is
a critical factor in large-scale cryptographic transitions. Migra-
tion toward post-quantum cryptography is not solely a tech-
nical process but also an organizational and socio-technical
one, requiring coordination across developers, infrastructure
operators, and users. Second, sentiment aggregation models
feedback mechanisms commonly present in decentralized gov-
ernance systems, where protocol evolution and infrastructure
adoption are influenced by stakeholder consensus rather than
centralized decision-making.
Following sentiment visualization, participants vote on a set
of candidate technologies representing distinct approaches to
achieving quantum resilience in distributed environments:
• Post-Quantum Signatures. These refer primarily to
lattice-based or hash-based digital signature schemes de-
signed to replace elliptic-curve signatures vulnerable to
Shor’s algorithm. In blockchain systems, digital signa-
tures are fundamental to transaction authorization, wallet
ownership, and identity verification. Transitioning signa-
ture schemes represents one of the most critical steps in
achieving long-term ledger security.

• Quantum Random Number Generation (QRNG).

for key generation, consensus leader selection, and pro-
tocol fairness. Quantum-generated entropy introduces a
hardware-derived randomness source whose statistical
properties differ from pseudo-random algorithms.
• Quantum-Safe Smart Contracts. This category refers
to modifications in smart contract design and execu-
tion environments to ensure compatibility with post-
quantum cryptographic primitives and future-proof ver-

signature verification methods, upgraded key formats, and
quantum-safe authentication flows.
• Zero-Knowledge Proofs (ZKPs). ZKPs enable verifi-

verification. Their relevance increases in quantum-aware
systems where privacy, scalability, and cryptographic
agility must coexist within distributed infrastructures.
The voting process models technology prioritization under
conditions of uncertainty, reflecting how distributed communi-

ity, infrastructure cost, and interoperability with existing sys-
tems. In blockchain ecosystems, protocol upgrades frequently
require broad consensus; therefore, understanding collective
perception and prioritization provides insight into potential
adoption pathways for post-quantum technologies.

By presenting environmental metrics alongside performance

3) Relevance to Blockchain Infrastructure: Quantum device
selection becomes directly relevant to blockchain-integrated
infrastructures when quantum computation contributes to cryp-
tographic processes or identity generation. Quantum-derived
randomness used for key generation, consensus mechanisms,
or identity construction depends on physical measurement
outcomes whose statistical properties vary across hardware
implementations. As a result, device calibration, noise char-
acteristics, and measurement fidelity become part of the trust
boundary when quantum-generated outputs are incorporated
into distributed ledgers.
Distributed systems require long-term verifiability and in-

in execution models influence reproducibility, auditability,
and provenance tracking when quantum-derived artifacts are
recorded on-chain. Execution metadata, including device se-

essary for maintaining transparency and verification across
decentralized environments.

remain verifiable over extended periods, infrastructure deci-
sions affect both security guarantees and governance mecha-
nisms. Hardware-dependent entropy sources, operational sus-
tainability, and execution provenance collectively determine
whether quantum-assisted services can be reliably integrated
into blockchain-based systems. Page 6 therefore establishes
