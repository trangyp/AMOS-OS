Quasiisometric embeddings between right-angled Artin groups:
rigidity
Shaked Bader, Oussama Bensaid, and Harry Petyt
Abstract
By introducing branching conditions on the defining graph, we prove a range of rigidity
results for quasiisometric embeddings between right-angled Artin groups. The starting
point for these is that, under mild conditions on the codomain, the branching conditions
imply that a quasiisometric embedding induces an embedding between the associated
extension graphs. Among other things, we:
• provide obstructions to the existence of quasiisometric embeddings into products of
trees;
• prove that if the direct product F n
2 × Am
C5 can be quasiisometrically embedded in a
RAAG of the same dimension, then this can be seen from its defining graph;
• classify all self–quasiisometric-embeddings of RAAGs defined on cycles;
• show that no n–dimensional RAAG is a universal receiver for quasiisometric embed-
dings of n–dimensional RAAGs.
We also establish a strong rigidity theorem for the quasiisometric images of 2–flats in
RAAGs defined by triangle-free graphs that are not stars, generalising a theorem of
Bestvina–Kleiner–Sageev.

Introduction
A group is a right-angled Artin group if it has a generating set where the only relations are
that some generators commute. If there are no such relations then the group is free, whereas
if every two generators commute then the group is free-abelian. Between these extremes there

is a large variety of examples, with perhaps the most obvious being direct products of free
groups, or free products of free-abelian groups. In fact, each right-angled Artin group can be
encoded by a simplicial graph, and the correspondence is bijective [Dro87b].
Because of the simplicity of the relations in right-angled Artin groups, they have some
very nice algebraic and combinatorial properties [HM95, Ser89, DJ00, HW99]. See [Cha07]
for a survey. However, as is perhaps to be expected from a class of groups that is naturally
indexed by the set of all simplicial graphs, their simple definition belies a very rich spectrum
of behaviours.
This is perhaps best illustrated by their subgroup structure. Indeed, the highly influential
class of compact special groups of Haglund–Wise can be characterised as the convex-cocompact
subgroups of right-angled Artin groups [HW08].
Such groups have a powerful associated
theory, particularly in the case where they are hyperbolic [Wis21]. Another famous example
is the result of Bestvina–Brady, who used subgroups of right-angled Artin groups to show the
inconsistency of the Whitehead and Eilenberg–Ganea conjectures [BB97].
In this article, we are interested in the large-scale geometry of right-angled Artin groups,
and in how such groups relate to one another.
Large-scale geometry of groups
The study of groups through their large-scale geometry is one of the central themes in geomet-
ric group theory, with the most ambitious goal being to classify groups based on their geome-
try. A prominent instance of this is Gromov’s program to classify groups up to quasiisometry
[Gro93], which he proposed in the wake of important results such as: his polynomial growth
theorem, which implies quasiisometric rigidity for free-abelian groups [Gro81, Bas72, Gui73];
Mostow rigidity and Tukia’s theorem for hyperbolic manifolds [Mos80, Mar74, Pra73, Tuk86,
Tuk88]; and the Stallings–Dunwoody theorem for virtually free groups [Sta68, Dun85].
There is by now a very large body of work on quasiisometric classification and rigidity,
which we mention just a few aspects of.
For symmetric spaces of non-compact type, quasiisometry determines the space up to a
rescaling of the irreducible factors [KL97]; see also [Tuk88, Pan89, Cho96] for the rank-one
cases. The same holds for higher-rank Euclidean buildings [KL97]. Mapping class groups en-
joy a similar rigidity phenomenon: apart from a few low-complexity exceptions, quasiisometry
determines the underlying surface [BKMM12]. Other examples include irreducible nonuni-
form lattices in semisimple Lie groups other than Isom(H2), some large-type Artin groups,
and solvable Baumslag–Solitar groups BS(1, n), for which quasiisometry within the family is
equivalent to commensurability [Sch95, Esk98, HO17, HOV24, FM99].
A natural variation of asking when two spaces are quasiisometric is the question of when
one can be quasiisometrically embedded in another. This is in general a much more difficult
question, in line with the difference between asking when two groups are isomorphic and asking
whether one is a subgroup of another. At the most basic level, one loses access to arguments
of the form “prove something for a quasiisometry, and then use that same property for its
quasiinverse”.
Nevertheless, in some settings with especially strong geometry one can still obtain some
analogous rigidity results. In higher-rank symmetric spaces, Fisher–Whyte [FW18] proved
that, under natural compatibility assumptions, quasiisometric embeddings remain rigid, in
the sense that maximal flats are mapped within finite Hausdorff distance of maximal flats.

For mapping class groups, Bowditch [Bow18] showed that, for compact orientable surfaces
of the same complexity at least 4, the existence of a quasiisometric embedding between the
corresponding mapping class groups forces the surfaces to be homeomorphic, and moreover
the embedding is at finite distance from an isometry. Related rigidity phenomena for quasi-
isometric embeddings were also established for nonuniform lattices in higher-rank semisimple
Lie groups [FN20] and for solvable Baumslag–Solitar groups [Nai23].
Our goal in the present paper is to obtain rigidity results for quasiisometric embeddings
between right-angled Artin groups.
Right-angled Artin groups
Quasiisometries between right-angled Artin groups are still not completely understood, though
there have been important contributions from a number of authors. As an early sign that
the picture is likely to be complicated, note that there is a marked difference between the
two extreme cases of free groups and free-abelian groups: any two nonabelian free groups are
quasiisometric, but no two free abelian groups are quasiisometric.
Moving away from the extreme cases, there seems to be a subtle line between these two
behaviours. On the “free” end, Behrstock–Neumann showed that if Γ and Λ are finite trees of
diameter at least three, then the associated right-angled Artin groups AΓ and AΛ are quasiiso-
metric [BN12]. They later found higher-dimensional examples, together with Januszkiewicz
[BJN10].
On the other side, Bestvina–Kleiner–Sageev introduced the family of atomic graphs, and
showed that if two right-angled Artin groups associated with atomic graphs are quasiisometric,
then they are isomorphic [BKS08]. This was later vastly generalised by Huang, who showed the
same result for all right-angled Artin groups with finite outer automorphism group [Hua17a]
and in various cases where infinite outer automorphism groups are permitted [Hua25]. Even
more remarkably, Huang, building on work with Kleiner [HK18], showed that for graphs Γ
that have no induced 4–cycles, are “star-rigid”, and such that Out(AΓ) is finite, the group
AΓ is absolutely quasiisometrically rigid, in the sense that any group quasiisometric to AΓ is
commensurable with it [Hua18].
At this point, we note that all of the rigidity results mentioned above rely in an essential
way on the existence of a quasiinverse. Indeed, each of them in some way involves using a
“double embedding” argument, where one shows a property of a quasiisometry, notes that it
holds for the quasiinverse, and then uses that the composition is coarsely the identity.
To our knowledge, the only general result about quasiisometric embeddings between right-
angled Artin groups is that of Rull [Rul08]. Elaborating on the “Alice’s diary” construction
introduced in [BDS07], she proved that if Γ is an n–colourable finite graph, then AΓ can be
quasiisometrically embedded in a product of n finite-rank free groups.
In particular, there do not seem to be any nontrivial general results obstructing the exis-
tence of quasiisometric embeddings. As in [FW18, Bow18], in order to have any control, we
must restrict our attention to pairs of right-angled Artin groups of the same dimension (note
that this is automatic when considering quasiisometries). If Γ is a graph, then the dimension
of the associated right-angled Artin group is equal to the clique number of Γ.
To get started with handling quasiisometric embeddings between more general right-angled
Artin groups and finding obstructions, we use the results from [BBP26a], which provide
geometric branching conditions that enable one to control the images of certain flats under

Branching conditions on flats
Let us briefly outline some of the definitions and consequences of [BBP26a] in the setting of
right-angled Artin groups. A more thorough discussion is given in Section 3.
Definition 1.1. Let Γ be a graph with clique number n ≥2.
• A clique of Γ is branching if it is the intersection of some n–cliques.
• A vertex v ∈Γ is branch-complemented if it lies in an n–clique K such that v and
K ∖{v} are both branching.
• Γ is directionally branch-complemented if all of its vertices are branch-complemented.
In the case when Γ has clique number is two, that is, when AΓ is two-dimensional, being
directionally branch-complemented is equivalent to having no leaves.
(See Section 10 for
discussion about leaves.) The property is also closed under taking joins.
We emphasise that being directionally branch-complemented does not impose any con-
ditions on the edges of Γ: it is carried by one-dimensional subspaces, namely the standard
geodesics, or equivalently the cosets of maximal cyclic subgroups corresponding to generators.
See Figure 1 for examples, and see Remark 3.4 for a comparison with the Laurence–Servatius
conditions on Γ that imply Out(AΓ) is finite [Lau95, Ser89].

In [KK13], Kim–Koberda studied the problem of when one right-angled Artin group can
be found as a subgroup of another - our interest in the present paper is the geometric analogue
of this. As part of their work, they defined an important combinatorial object known as the
extension graph of a graph, which encodes key algebraic and geometric information about
the associated right-angled Artin group. In many ways, it is analogous to the curve graph
of a surface [KK14], and just as how the curve graph is central to proofs of quasiisometric
rigidity for mapping class groups, the extension graph plays a fundamental role in Huang’s
work [Hua17a].
The extension graph Γext of a graph Γ can be viewed as a simplified Tits boundary for
AΓ. One way of defining it is as follows. It has a vertex for each parallelism class of standard
geodesics, and two vertices span an edge if there is a 2–flat spanned by representatives of
those classes. In particular, it contains Γ as an induced subgraph.
Another graph that still simplifies the Tits boundary but is more permissive than the
extension graph is the singular boundary graph ∂singAΓ [BBP26a, Def. 10.11]. It has additional
vertices compared to Γext, which correspond to singular geodesic rays; see Definition 2.5.

Theorem 1.2. Let Γ and Λ be finite graphs with clique number n. If Γ is directionally branch-

Main results
We start with some rather direct consequences of Theorem 1.2. To simplify the discussion,
all right-angled Artin groups in these statements will be assumed to be finitely generated,
although many of our results do not rely on that hypothesis.
Our first result concerns quasiisometric embeddings in products of trees. As mentioned
above, Rull proved that every right-angled Artin group with n–colourable defining graph can
be quasiisometrically embedded in a product of n trees of bounded degree. It is natural to
wonder about the converse: does one really need n trees? The simplest nontrivial example of
this is when Γ = Cn is an odd cycle of length at least five, which has clique number two but
chromatic number three. In [BBP26a], we observed that Theorem 1.2 implies that three is
the optimal number of trees for such right-angled Artin groups.
Our first result is a large generalisation of this. In it, we allow trees to be locally infinite,
and (F2)n denotes the direct product of n rank-two free groups.
Theorem A. Let Γ be a graph with clique number n, and assume that Γ is directionally
branch-complemented if n > 2. The following are equivalent.
1. Γ is n–colourable.
2. AΓ can be equivariantly isometrically embedded in a product of n trees.
3. AΓ can be quasiisometrically embedded in (F2)n.
4. AΓ can be quasiisometrically embedded in a product of n trees.
The forward directions of this are given by Rull’s theorem and an application of Sageev’s
construction; see Section 4. We note that the theorem in particular applies to all graphs with
clique number two, that is, all two-dimensional right-angled Artin groups.
Comparing the different items in Theorem A, one may ask when a right-angled Artin
group AΓ can be isometrically embedded in (F2)n. We show in Corollary 4.8 that this is only
possible when AΓ is itself a direct product of free groups.
A second consequence of Theorem 1.2 that follows quickly is that there is no universal
receiver for quasiisometric embeddings between n–dimensional right-angled Artin groups; see
Section 5.
Theorem B. For each n > 1, there is no n–dimensional right-angled Artin group into which
every n–dimensional right-angled Artin group quasiisometrically embeds.
This provides a geometric analogue of [KK13, Thm 1.16], which states that there is no
two-dimensional right-angled Artin group that contains all two-dimensional right-angled Artin

groups as subgroups. It is also a generalisation of that result, because whenever one two-

Theorem 1.2 is a special case of a more general result about quasiisometric embeddings
between CAT(0) cube complexes, where the strongest property one can ask from a geodesic
is to be singular. In the setting of right-angled Artin groups, we have the additional structure
of standard geodesics, and Theorem 1.2 is proved by showing that standard geodesics are
mapped at finite Hausdorff distance from singular geodesics.
Under an additional hypothesis on the codomain right-angled Artin group, we show that
something stronger is true: quasiisometric embeddings must map standard geodesics at finite
Hausdorff distance from standard geodesics (Theorem 6.4). More generally, we refer to quasi-
isometric embeddings with this property as stable, inspired by terminology from [Hua17a].
We show in Proposition 6.2 that stable quasiisometric embeddings induce maps of extension
graphs, which implies the following (see Corollary 6.6). We write Sn for the graph that is the
suspension of the disjoint union of a vertex and an (n −1)–clique.
Theorem C. Let Γ and Λ be graphs with clique number n. Suppose that Γ is directionally
branch-complemented and that Sn is not a subgraph of Λ. Every quasiisometric embedding
AΓ →AΛ induces a graph embedding Γext →Λext.
Observe that the condition on Λ is automatically satisfied if it has no 4–cycle subgraphs,
and this is precisely what the condition says when n = 2. We remark that, in contrast to
[KK13, Thm 1.3], the existence of a graph embedding Γext →Λext does not imply that AΓ
is a subgroup of AΛ. This even fails when Γ and Λ are cycle graphs - see [BBP26b], which
contains a complete classification of when ACm can be quasiisometrically embedded in ACn.
Theorem C forms the basis for showing more refined rigidity results for quasiisometric
embeddings between right-angled Artin groups. By combining with [KK13, Lem. 3.9], we
immediately get the following example of this.
Corollary D. If Γ is not a forest and has smaller girth than Λ, then AΓ cannot be quasiiso-
metrically embedded in AΛ.
Proof. If Γ has girth three, then AΓ has dimension greater than two, so cannot be quasi-
isometrically embedded in AΛ. Otherwise, the restriction of a quasiisometric embedding to
the subgroup of AΓ generated by a shortest cycle satisfies the conditions of Theorem C.
We therefore obtain cycles in Λext of length less than the girth of Λ, contradicting [KK13,
Lem. 3.9].
In fact, we can prove a much more precise unconcealability result. For subgroups, it was
shown by Kambites that if F2 × F2 = AC4 is a subgroup of a right-angled Artin group AΓ,
then C4 is an induced subgraph of Γ [Kam09]. Moreover, Kim–Koberda proved that if Γ is
triangle-free and ACn is a subgroup of AΓ for some n ≥5, then Cm is an induced subgraph
of Γ for some m ∈{5, . . . , n} [KK13, Cor. 8.1]. In particular, AC5 cannot be concealed by
subgroup embeddings in dimension two.
We prove a more general geometric analogue of these results; see Section 7. Let Kn×2
denote the complete n–partite graph whose parts have two vertices; the associated right-
angled Artin group is AKn×2 = (F2)n.

Returning to the setting of quasiisometric embeddings into direct products, we now con-
sider stable quasiisometric embeddings. When the codomain is a product of trees, we note
that, by the nature of the Alice’s diary construction, Rull’s embeddings are almost never
stable [Rul08], and in fact stability is a major restriction in such a case. The following is
Corollary 8.5.
Theorem H. If AΓ admits a stable quasiisometric embedding in a finite product of trees,
then AΓ is itself a product of finite-rank free groups.
This can be viewed as an instance of a more general phenomenon, where right-angled Artin
groups that are not “product-like” can only be quasiisometrically embedded in a product in
a degenerate way; see Corollary 8.4.
Theorem I. Let Γ be a connected graph with diameter at least four. If f : AΓ →AΛ is
a stable quasiisometric embedding, then f(AΓ) lies in a uniform neighbourhood of a single
irreducible factor of AΛ.
One noteworthy thing about these last two results is that they do not require the domain
and codomain to have the same dimension.
As described previously, a remarkable result of Huang shows that if Γ is a graph with no
induced 4–cycles that is star-rigid and has the property that Out(AΓ) is finite (which can be
read from Γ by [Lau95, Ser89]), then AΓ is absolutely quasiisometrically rigid [Hua18]. The
simplest examples of graphs satisfying this property are the cycle graphs, Γ = Cn for n > 4.
We also mentioned that [BBP26b] classifies when ACm can be quasiisometrically embedded
in ACn.

at least four, Bowditch proved that every self–quasiisometric-embedding is at finite distance
from a left translation [Bow18]. For right-angled Artin groups there are more possibilities,

Preliminaries
Let X be a metric space and A ⊆X. For r ≥0, the r–neighbourhood of A is A+r = {x ∈X :
d(x, A) ≤r}. The Hausdorff distance between subsets A, B ⊆X is
dHaus(A, B) = inf{r ≥0 : A ⊆B+r and B ⊆A+r}.
The clique number ωΓ of a graph Γ is the supremal cardinality of cliques of Γ. If ωΓ ≤2,
then we refer to Γ as being triangle-free. All graphs considered in this paper will be simplicial.
We write Kn×2 for the complete n–partite graph whose parts all have cardinality two.
2.1
CAT(0) spaces
We shall use only a limited amount of CAT(0) geometry in this paper. We refer the reader
to [Bal95] and [BH99] for background on the subject. The main class of CAT(0) spaces we
are concerned with in this paper are CAT(0) cube complexes (see Section 2.2) and more
specifically, Salvetti complexes.
Definition 2.1 (Flats). Let (X, d) be a CAT(0) space. A k–flat in X is the image of an
isometric embedding (Rk, ∥· ∥2) →(X, d).
Definition 2.2 (Parallels). Let X be a complete CAT(0) space, and let F, F ′ ⊆X be flats.
We say that F and F ′ are parallel if they are at finite Hausdorff distance. Equivalently, the
distance from F to F ′ and the distance from F ′ to F are constant.

## Page 10


The parallel set of a flat F is the union of all flats parallel to F, and is denoted by P(F).
It is a closed convex subset of X, and it admits a canonical splitting as a metric product
P(F) = F × Y for some complete CAT(0) space Y , see [KL97, Section 2.3.3].
The following simple lemma shows that a geodesic lying near a closed, convex subset has
a parallel inside that subset. We refer to [BBP26a, Lem. 4.2] for a proof.
Lemma 2.3. Let X be a complete CAT(0) space, let A ⊆X be a closed, convex subset, and
let γ ⊆X be a geodesic. If γ ⊆A+D, for some D ≥0, then there exists a parallel geodesic
γ′ ⊆A such that dHaus(γ, γ′) ≤D.
2.2
CAT(0) cube complexes
We refer the reader to [Wis21, Bow22, Gen23] for basic facts about CAT(0) cube complexes.
A cube complex is a cell complex obtained by gluing Euclidean unit cubes [0, 1]n along
isometric faces. A CAT(0) cube complex is a simply connected cube complex such that the
link of each vertex is a flag simplicial complex. The dimension of a CAT(0) cube complex is
the supremal dimension of the cubes involved in its construction.
A key combinatorial feature of CAT(0) cube complexes is their hyperplanes. For each edge
e of X, the hyperplane dual to e can be defined as an equivalence class of edges: set e1 ∼e2
whenever they are opposite edges in a square and extend transitively. Each hyperplane h
separates X into two halfspaces.
We say that h separates two points of X if they lie in
different halfspaces. Two hyperplanes cross if all four possible intersections of halfspaces are
nonempty.
If X is a CAT(0) cube complex, then the length metric d2 obtained by equipping each cube
with the ℓ2 metric makes X into a CAT(0) space. If one instead equips the cubes with the
ℓ1 metric, then one obtains a metric d1 on X that makes X into a median metric space. For
vertices x, y ∈X, the distance d1(x, y) is equal to the number of hyperplanes that separate x
from y. If X is finite-dimensional, then d1 and d2 are biLipschitz equivalent. In this paper,
we shall almost always consider CAT(0) cube complexes as being equipped with the metric
d1. The exception is when we are discussing flats.
Definition 2.4 (Singular). Let X be a CAT(0) cube complex. A k–flat F ⊆X is singular if it
is contained in the k–skeleton of X. A singular geodesic is a singular 1–flat. A singular geodesic

Right-angled Artin groups
A right-angled Artin group (RAAG) is a group A with a generating set S such that the only
relations are that some elements of S commute. This can be encoded by the (simplicial)
graph Γ that has vertex set S and an edge between vertices whose corresponding generators
commute. We write A = AΓ, and refer to S as the standard generating set of AΓ.
Remark 2.7. In the body of the paper, we do not require right-angled Artin groups to be
finitely generated unless specified otherwise.
2.8 Notation. If we are given a vertex v ∈Γ, then we shall sometimes write sv ∈S for
the corresponding standard generator. Similarly, given an element s ∈S, we shall sometimes
write vs for the corresponding vertex of Γ.
2.9 The space XΓ. The Cayley graph of AΓ with respect to the generating set S is a median
graph, which is the 1–skeleton of a CAT(0) cube complex XΓ. The quotient of XΓ by the
action of AΓ is known as the Salvetti complex of AΓ.
If XΓ is finite-dimensional, then it is quasiisometric to its 1–skeleton, AΓ.
The dimension of XΓ is equal to the clique number of Γ, so as long as Γ has finite clique
number, which it always will in this paper, XΓ is quasiisometric to AΓ.
Hence for most
purposes we will not distinguish the two, and just write AΓ. For instance, the dimension
of AΓ is the dimension of XΓ. But when thinking of the space as a CAT(0) object we will
consider XΓ. It is an easy fact that the asymptotic rank of XΓ is equal to its dimension.

Lemma 2.14. Let Γ be a simplicial graph.
1. If α, α′ ⊆XΓ are asymptotic singular geodesic rays, then α and α′ contain singular
subrays β ⊆α and β′ ⊆α′ with the same set of labels.
2. If α, α′ ⊆XΓ are parallel singular geodesics, then they have the same set of labels.
Proof. (1) By [Hua17b, Lem. 2.10], the distance between α and α′ is attained on singular
subrays β ⊆α and β′ ⊆α′. In particular, β and β′ are parallel, in the sense that the distance
from one to the other is constant. Since XΓ is a CAT(0) cube complex, and β is a singular
geodesic ray that is a subcomplex, its parallel set P(β) is well defined; see [Hua17a, Rem. 3.5].
It splits as P(β) = β × C, for some CAT(0) cube complex C. In this splitting, β corresponds
to β ×{v} for some vertex v ∈C, and β′ corresponds to β ×{v′} for some vertex v′ ∈C. Take
a combinatorial path v = v0, . . . , vk = v′ in C. For each i, set βi := β × {vi}. The singular
rays βi and βi+1 bound the flat strip subcomplex β × [vi, vi+1]. Hence every edge of βi is
opposite, in a square of this strip, to an edge of βi+1 with the same label. Thus βi and βi+1
have the same set of labels. By induction, β = β0 and β′ = βk have the same set of labels.
(2) The proof is the same, working directly with the parallel set P(α) instead of passing
to subrays.

Branching theorems for RAAGs
The main results of [BBP26a] concern quasiisometric embeddings between CAT(0) cube com-
plexes. They give geometric branching conditions on flats under which the images of those
flats are Hausdorff-close to flats in the codomain, and deduce the existence of induced maps
between appropriately defined boundaries. In this section we interpret some of those defini-
tions and results for right-angled Artin groups.
The following definitions are equivalent to specialising [BBP26a, Def.s 9.1, 9.3, 9.5] to
universal covers of Salvetti complexes. See also [BBP26a, Rem. 9.4].
Definition 3.1. Let Γ be a (simplicial) graph with clique number n ≥2.
1. A clique is said to be branching if it is the intersection of some n–cliques.
2. A vertex v is called branch-complemented if it is contained in an n–clique K such that
both {v} and K ∖{v} are branching.
3. A subgraph of Γ is directionally branch-complemented if each of its vertices is branch-
complemented.
If Γ is triangle-free, then a vertex is branching precisely when it is not a leaf, and branch-
complemented if and only if it is branching and has a branching neighbour. Thus Γ is direc-
tionally branch-complemented if and only if it has no leaves. See Figure 2 for examples of
directionally branch-complemented subgraphs when the clique number is 3.
Remark 3.2. It should be emphasized that being directionally branch-complemented imposes
no additional condition on the edges of a subgraph: this property only depends on its vertices.

Stable quasiisometric embeddings
As discussed in the introduction, Rull constructed quasiisometric embeddings of RAAGs with
n–colourable defining graph into (F2)n [Rul08, Thm 1.1]. The embeddings she constructed are
non-rigid, in the sense that they do not take standard geodesics Hausdorff-close to standard
geodesics. (We shall see in Section 8 that this is necessary in general.) In this section, we
consider quasiisometric embeddings between more general n–dimensional RAAGs, and show
that in many situations there cannot be any such non-rigid examples. We formalise this with
the following definition.
Definition 6.1. A quasiisometric embedding φ : AΓ →AΛ is said to be stable if there is
a constant D such that φ sends every standard geodesic of AΓ within Hausdorff distance at
most D of some standard geodesic of AΛ. It is weakly stable if φ sends every standard geodesic
of AΓ within finite Hausdorff distance of a standard geodesic of AΛ.
Stability allows us to pass from the coarse setting of quasiisometric embeddings to the com-
binatorics of extension graphs (see Definition 2.15). We later use this to obtain combinatorial
obstructions to the existence of such quasiisometries.

non-parallel standard geodesics in a RAAG diverge.

standard 2–flat F ⊆XΓ. We claim that f(v) and f(w) are adjacent in Λext.
By assumption, the quasiflat φ(F) lies at finite Hausdorff distance from a 2–flat F ′ ⊆XΛ.
Let βv and βw be standard geodesics in XΛ that lie at finite Hausdorff distance from f(γv)
and f(γw), respectively. According to Lemma 2.3, both βv and βw are parallel into F ′. Since

Beyond directionally branch-complemented
This section is a first step towards extending Theorem 3.9, which is about quasiisometric em-
beddings of directionally branch-complemented and directionally strongly branch-complemented
cliques, to cliques that are not directionally branch-complemented.
More precisely, we will give a weaker branching condition for top dimensional standard
flats, or equivalently, cliques in the defining graph. The condition is meant to capture leaf-like
cliques, and indeed it includes all edges containing leaves in connected, triangle-free graphs
that are not stars. We show in Theorem 10.4 that top-dimensional flats satisfying the condition
are mapped Hausdorff-close to flats. In the triangle-free connected case, when Γ is not a star,
we upgrade the result to all 2–flats in Theorem 10.5.
Definition 10.1 (Weak branching). Let Γ be a graph with clique number n ≥2, and let
K ⊆Γ be an n–clique. We say that K is weakly directionally branch-complemented if either
• there exists a directionally branch-complemented n–clique K′ ⊆Γ such that K ∩K′ is
an (n −1)–clique, or
• K contains a directionally strongly branch-complemented subclique of size n −1.
Weakly directionally branch-complemented cliques may to some extent be viewed as the
“leaf cliques”, see the blue cliques in Figure 8.
Note that the two conditions are not equivalent for n > 2. Indeed, in the first row of
Figure 8, all 3-dimensional blue cliques satisfy the first condition and not the second, as no
vertex is strongly branch-complemented. In the graph on the second row, the blue clique
on the left satisfies the second condition, but not the first, as all red vertices are strongly
branch-complemented. The blue clique on the right satisfies both conditions.

Recall that a graph is a star if it is connected and it has a vertex that lies in every edge.
Observation 10.2. Let Γ be a connected, triangle-free graph that is not a star.
• Every non-leaf vertex v ∈Γ is directionally branch-complemented. Indeed, otherwise
all neighbours of v would be leaves, so Γ would be a star.
• Every edge of Γ is either directionally branch-complemented or weakly directionally
branch-complemented. Indeed, let e be an edge of Γ. If e is not directionally branch-
complemented, then its two vertices cannot both have degree 1, since Γ is connected
and not a star. Hence one vertex v of e has degree at least 2. Again, the same argument
implies that one neighbour of v must also have degree at least 2. Therefore, e intersects
a directionally branch-complemented edge at v, so e is weakly directionally branch-
complemented.
