Existing direct-register, direct pixel-output quantum generative models for calorimeter shower
simulation tie the effective quantum output dimension to the image dimension, so the required
register size grows with the full image size.
More recent quantum-assisted approaches alleviate
this only by shifting part of the generative burden into hybrid latent-variable constructions. As
a result, current quantum demonstrations remain far below the detector geometries used in high-
energy physics experiments. We introduce the Quantum Feature Amplification Network (QFAN)
algorithm, which removes this bottleneck by decomposing an image into sequential blocks, each
produced by the same small parameterized quantum circuit whose input is a compressed summary
of all previously generated pixels. Because the circuit is reused rather than enlarged, the qubit
requirement is set by block size rather than by the full image dimension, while the per-step quantum
processing cost (the number of quantum circuits executed in one gradient step) is independent of
image size for the family of Pauli observables used here. We derive a conservative worst-case bound
on shot-noise propagation through the generation chain and identify an empirical decoder-capacity
heuristic that bounds the sequential depth reachable within the present architecture. A three-qubit
circuit with twelve shared quantum variational parameters, together with closed-form classical ridge
decoders and a post-hoc residual sampler, reproduces per-pixel intensity distributions, inter-pixel
correlations, and the total energy distribution of calorimeter showers on both a simulator and
IBM quantum hardware.
At this scale, the observed hardware–simulator gap is consistent with
optimization-budget limitations dominating over device noise, although the present experiments do
not causally separate these effects. These results establish a hardware-compatible proof of principle
and motivate, but do not validate, larger-scale extrapolations within the same circuit family.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Most particles entering a calorimeter initiate a stochas-
tic cascade of secondary interactions, depositing energy
across hundreds to thousands of detector cells. Simulat-
ing these showers from first principles with Geant4 [1]
is one of the largest consumers of computing in LHC ex-
periments, and projections for the High-Luminosity LHC
era place the annual budget at more than 1011 events [2],
a demand that cannot be met with current resources.
Classical surrogate models based on deep generative net-
works [3–6] have achieved 103–106× speedups, but they
rely on 106–107 trainable parameters and remain bound
to GPU inference.
Quantum generative models offer a qualitatively dif-
ferent design point in the form of compact circuits whose
correlation structure is mediated by entanglement rather
than by deep layered architectures. In principle, a few-
qubit parameterized circuit can encode nontrivial depen-
dencies with a parameter count far smaller than that
of modern classical generative networks.
In practice,
however, existing gate-model quantum calorimeter proof-
of-concepts and quantum-assisted calorimeter genera-
tors [7–10] remain either limited to strongly downscaled
image representations or rely on hybrid latent-variable

constructions rather than direct full-register pixel out-

for current quantum demonstrations. This is an architec-

formulation: the quantum register must scale with the
full image rather than with a compressed or blockwise
representation.
The insight behind the Quantum Feature Amplifica-
tion Network (QFAN) is that a quantum circuit does not
need to hold the whole image at once. If image genera-
tion is decomposed into autoregressive blocks, and each
block is produced by the same small circuit conditioned
on what came before, then the register size is set by
the block size B, not by the image dimension d. Con-
cretely, QFAN partitions a d-pixel shower into B contigu-
ous blocks and generates them sequentially with a shared
nq-qubit circuit (see Fig. 1). Between blocks, a stream-
ing count-sketch [11] compresses all previously generated
pixels into a fixed-length summary that conditions the
next circuit call. The circuit’s Pauli-expectation outputs
are decoded to pixel values by a ridge regressor with a
closed-form weight matrix, so the full pipeline is trainable
with SPSA [12] and requires no bilevel optimization.
Now, the register size is determined by the block size
b rather than by the image dimension d, and the number
of autoregressive steps grows as d/b, suggesting a gen-

II.
PROBLEM FORMULATION
A calorimeter shower image y ∈Rd records the en-
ergy deposited in d detector cells. The target distribu-
tion pdata combines two difficulties that make it a strin-
gent test for any generative model. Each cell marginal is
highly non-Gaussian, typically unimodal with a skewed
tail whose width and peak position vary across cells, and
the cells are strongly correlated across detector layers
through conservation laws and shower-shape constraints.
A faithful surrogate must reproduce both the marginals
and the correlation structure; matching one without the
other is insufficient for downstream physics analysis.
The architectural constraint that drives every design
decision in this work is nq ≪d.
At the demonstra-
tion scale this ratio is modest (nq=3, d=12, so d/nq=4),
whereas realistic calorimeter image dimensions are far
larger [13].
In a direct-register, direct pixel-output formulation,
the mapping from quantum register to image pixels im-
poses one of two costs. Born machines and amplitude-
encoded models use only ⌈log2 d⌉qubits, but they pro-
duce probability distributions rather than real-valued
pixel intensities, and generic state preparation requires
circuit depth that scales linearly in d. Angle-encoding
models that emit real-valued outputs require nq = Ω(d)
qubits. In either case, scaling to the full image dimen-
sion d imposes a resource cost, in register size, circuit
depth, or output representation, that grows with d. The
central question is therefore how to bridge this gap with-
out simply enlarging the quantum register.
Our approach is to exploit an autoregressive factoriza-
tion of the joint distribution. Partition the d-pixel image
into B contiguous blocks y1, . . . , yB, each containing up
to b pixels, and denote by y<β ≡(y1, . . . , yβ−1) the pix-
els generated before block β. The joint distribution then
factors exactly as

III.
ARCHITECTURE
Figure 2 summarizes the QFAN pipeline as a compres-
sion/expansion cycle within one autoregressive step. A
streaming sketch summarizes the previously generated
prefix and is projected into circuit angles. The shared pa-
rameterized quantum circuit produces a quantum state
from which Pauli features are extracted by measurement.
A ridge decoder maps these features to the next block
output, which is then fed back into the sketch before the
process repeats for the next block. The same circuit pa-
rameters θ are reused across all B blocks; only the sketch
input changes.
We now describe each stage, emphasizing the design
rationale: why each component takes the form it does,
not merely what it computes.
A.
Block decomposition and the block-size
trade-off
The shower image is partitioned into B=⌈d/b⌉con-

relative to targets (Sec. III E), while making them too
small lengthens the autoregressive chain and accumulates
sketch distortion across more steps (Sec. V). Remark 1
summarizes this trade-off through an empirical feature-
to-target threshold inferred from the present block-size
sweep, which sets the working maximum block size for a
given qubit count at the present scale.

F.
Gated residual sampler
The ridge decoder captures the linear relationship be-
tween Pauli features and pixel values, but calorimeter
distributions have a pronounced nonlinear structure that
a linear map cannot fully represent. The residuals Rβ
between the ridge prediction and the true pixel values
absorb this structure and are modeled with a small K-
means cluster bank (M=8 clusters). At generation time,
a logistic gate g: Rm→∆M−1 predicts cluster member-
ship from the sketch, and a residual is drawn from the
corresponding cluster. The choice M=2r is deliberate.
The cluster sampler is a natural drop-in replacement for
a Born sampler on r qubits, providing an upgrade path
when additional qubits become available.
In the present implementation, the residual bank is
fitted after the quantum encoder has been trained, using
residuals from the frozen ridge predictor on the train-
ing set. It therefore refines sampling at generation time
without entering the SPSA objective.
a.
Training-time conditioning versus generation-time
rollout.
During training, the sketch cache for block β
is constructed from ground-truth prefixes, so the block-
wise objective is evaluated under teacher-forced condi-
tioning.
During generation, by contrast, the sketch is
updated with model outputs, yielding a free-running au-
toregressive rollout. At the present demonstration scale
(B=2) this distinction is mild, but for long chains it can
induce exposure bias. Accordingly, the scaling analysis
developed below concerns architectural resource require-
ments and noise propagation under the stated condition-
ing mechanism; it does not by itself guarantee unchanged
sample fidelity at arbitrarily large block count.

Table XI places QFAN among classical surrogates,

XII.
DISCUSSION
QFAN demonstrates that the quantum register can
be decoupled from image dimension without abandon-
ing the circuit’s role in modeling inter-pixel correlations.
But demonstrating architectural viability at d=12 is not
the same as demonstrating benchmark-scale quantum
calorimeter generation, and the manuscript should be
explicit about that distinction rather than hoping the
reader will infer it charitably.
The architecture produces faithful marginals, corre-
lations, and total-energy distributions at d=12 with
3 qubits, 12 shared quantum variational parameters,
closed-form blockwise ridge decoding, and a post-hoc
residual sampler. For the chosen observable family, the
per-step QPU cost is independent of d, shot-noise prop-
agation is bounded in expectation under a conserva-
tive worst-case accumulation model, and the decoder-
capacity heuristic produces a concrete empirical extrap-
olation rule consistent with the optimal block size at the
demonstration scale. The hardware execution confirms
that the circuit logic transfers to a real device, with the

XIII.
LIMITATIONS AND NEXT STEPS
Several limitations remain. First, training uses teacher
forcing whereas generation runs free, so the present
results at B=2 and the initial extension to B=5 do
not resolve whether exposure bias will dominate at the
much longer autoregressive depths implied by the current
heuristic for larger geometries. The shot-noise analysis
of Theorem 2 controls measurement noise only and does
not bound model-bias accumulation under long rollouts.
Second, the observed feature-efficiency advantage over
classical random Fourier features is established only at
d=12 and only against a simple random-projection base-
line, not against an optimized classical encoder. Third,
the threshold ρmin ≈1.5 should be read as a practical
interpolation heuristic inferred from the present block-
size sweep, not as a universal constant.
Finally, al-
though the hardware and simulator paths show similar
residual structure, matched controls would be needed to
isolate hardware-specific effects more decisively. These
limitations define the main directions for future work:
intermediate-scale free-running validation, stronger clas-
sical benchmarks, and systematic characterization of ex-
posure bias as a function of autoregressive depth B.
XIV.
CONCLUSION
QFAN decouples the number of qubits from the image
size. Instead of requiring one qubit per pixel, a single
small circuit is reused across sequential blocks of the im-
age, conditioned each time on a compact summary of
all previously generated pixels. Adding more pixels in-
creases the number of blocks but not the circuit size.
Three observations organize the present resource pic-
ture. For the observable family used here, the per-step
circuit count is independent of the image size d. Under
bounded decoder gain and controlled sketch scale, main-
taining fixed expected sketch SNR admits a conservative
sufficient shot requirement of order O(d2pf).
Finally,
the empirical decoder-capacity heuristic suggests that,
at the present scale, the required sequential depth grows
approximately as d/n2

At d=12, the architecture reproduces shower distribu-
tions on both simulator and IBM hardware, providing a
hardware-compatible proof of principle.
An initial ex-

a longer chain, although this regime is still modest rela-
tive to the rollout depths that would arise at larger image
dimensions.

Appendix A: Appendix: QFAN Extension to
25-Pixel Images
We repeat the experiment at d=25 pixels using the
same nq=3 circuit architecture but now with 18 shared
variational parameters, on the noiseless simulator path.
This configuration is significant because now the autore-
gressive chain is more than 2× longer than the d=12 case,
the shared quantum parameters must encode the condi-
tional distribution across five detector regions, and the
finer spatial resolution makes marginal and correlation
targets more demanding.
1.
Per-pixel marginals
Figure 8 shows the marginal intensity distribution for
each of the 25 pixels, MC truth (blue) and simulator-path
QFAN (red). QFAN reproduces the MC shapes across all
25 pixels. The ratio panels scatter around unity with no
systematic drift toward the later blocks, providing direct
evidence that the sketch conditioning does not degrade
catastrophically.
2.
Correlation structure
Figure 9 shows the 25×25 correlation matrices for MC
truth, simulator-path QFAN, and the MC−SIM differ-
ence. The MC matrix reveals a block-diagonal structure
with inter-block anti-correlations reflecting energy con-
servation.
The simulator reproduces the overall struc-
ture.
Residuals are concentrated at block boundaries
and do not increase systematically from early to late
boundaries, suggesting that sketch-noise accumulation
has not reached a regime where later blocks are signif-
icantly worse.
