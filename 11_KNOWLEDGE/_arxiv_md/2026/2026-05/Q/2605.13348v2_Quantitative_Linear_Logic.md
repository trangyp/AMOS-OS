Real-valued logics have seen a renewed interest in verification for probabilistic and quantitative systems,
in particular machine learning models, where they can be used to directly integrate specifications in the
training objective. To do so effectively one has to strike a balance between the logical properties of the
connectives and their semantics. A major hurdle in this sense is to give “soft” (i.e. differentiable) semantics
to additive connectives—in linear and fuzzy logics, additives are necessarily “hard” lattice operations.
In this paper, we solve this problem by combining an accurate analysis of the properties of sum and
product on the reals with a significant revision of sequent calculus. We introduce ‘quantitative sequent
calculi’, which simultaneously generalize hypersequent calculi of fuzzy logics and deep inference, and in
which validity of a proof and provability of a sequent are real-valued quantities.
We present a family of calculi, 𝑝QLL, indexed by a hardness degree 𝑝, prove cut-elimination theorem for
them, and show completeness for enriched residuated ‘soft’ lattices. For 𝑝= ∞, 𝑝QLL reduces to MALL,
with provability in 𝑝QLL converging to provability in MALL when 𝑝→∞.

in a non-informative gradient.

less well-behaved, but Varnai and Dimarogonas proved in [54] a striking no-go theorem: a soft
operation cannot be simultaneously associative, idempotent, and smoothly monotonic.
Thus the softness requirement is destined to severely impact the proof-theoretic properties of
the connectives. The lack of associativity (as e.g. in STL [54]) could only lead to sequent calculi
without exchange—which would disagree with proof theoretic principles of linear logic [29],
traditionally taken as a ‘ground logic’ for fuzzy logics interpreted in residuated lattices [2, 40].

To solve the second, and much bigger problem of an adequately expressive proof calculus, we
turn to the second key insight of the paper, namely quantitative calculi.
Indeed, after Martin-Löf [38], we consider judgments the primary subjects of logic and thus we
believe that quantitative logic must have quantitative judgments. This distinguishes our approach
from the logics about quantity (like fuzzy logics, or the logics of the Lawvere quantale from [5, 6]),
and brings us in line with the vision of generalized logics of Lawvere [36].
Thus, while traditionally a sequent Γ ⊢Δ is ‘evaluated’ as true or false, we maintain that fitting
proof calculus for quantitative logic should interpret Γ ⊢Δ as a real number. Correspondingly,
while traditionally in a multipremise rule such as ∧R, the white space inbetween sequents tacitly
stands for a Boolean “and”, we replace such an anonymous connective by explicit operations on

which reflect some proof-theoretical notion of ‘efficiency’, with ‘inefficient’ proofs having validity

additives, including 𝜂-expansion, is quite low. We also show that, in the presence of prelinearity,
such efficiency is restored to degree 1, thus uncovering a new logical significance for prelinearity,
besides its semantical consequences. We do not manage to axiomatize prelinearity here, although
we show how to semantically account for it in Section 5.3.

QUANTITATIVE CALCULI
Formal logic studies the structures which formalize logical reasoning. In doing so, the mathemati-
cian juggles three different ‘levels’: an ambient level, where formalization happens, and which
is thus informal, i.e. not coded in mathematical structure; then a formal level in which both the
alethic (i.e. ‘metatheoretic’) and the object level are involved. The alethic level formalizes the
notion of ‘truth value’ under consideration—most commonly binary—whereas the object level
is the logical system being studied, stricto sensu.
Sequent calculi, more than other presentations of logical systems, exploit the interplay between
alethic and object level by describing object-level connectives as mere internalizations of the
alethic-level analogues. Thus a rule like ∧R above shows how ∧internalizes the alethic level
‘and’. However, this latter connective is itself distinct from the ambient level ‘and’, which does

The moral is that a sequent calculus for quantitative logic thrives in a quantitative alethic level,
i.e. that of [0, ∞]—a conclusion also drawn by Lawvere [36]. Indeed, we can consider the alethic
level as the enrichment base, familiar to the category theorist. Consequently, validity of proofs
and provability of sequents are notions valued in [0, ∞], and consequently proofs have a richer
combinatorial structure involving different kinds of branching, depending on the connectives
they involve. We stress that valuing validity and provability in the positive reals is not a form
of semantics, but still a description of syntax.
So let’s fix 0 < 𝑝≤∞, and thus [0, ∞]⊕𝑝,⊗as alethic metatheory. We now define precisely
which structure of [0, ∞]⊕𝑝,⊗we make use of in the calculus, and how.

Let Φ be a language, i.e. a set of formulae. A 𝑝-hard (quantitative) calculus pertaining Φ deals
with sequents Γ ⊢Δ where Γ, Δ are cedents Γ, Δ . . ., i.e. finite multisets7 of formulae, including
the empty one.
In a quantitative calculus, sequents are combined in structures, which are entities defined
as follows

6.2
Conversion rate interpretation
Linear logic has a resource interpretation whereby a sequent Γ ⊢𝐴asserts the convertibility of
the resources in Γ to the resource 𝐴. Quantitative linear logic, especially prelinear extensions of

to 𝐵is ≥1, then we are right to say 𝐴is convertible to 𝐵—this is our Theorem 4.7. In this light, 𝑎R
and 𝑎R quantify how much it costs to create or destroy a propositional variable.

term of (60) on the test set) to actual formal verification, which consistently reveals much lower
satisfaction rates than reported in the literature.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
We have formulated a new ‘quantitative linear logic’. Although many enlightening studies of
logics with quantitative semantics already exist [5, 7, 28, 40, 43], ours aimed to rethink the
very fundamentals—properties of additive connectives, interpretation of sequents and validity
of proofs—in a quantitative way. This exercise exposed 𝑝QLL as a natural generalisation (or
enrichment) of two established logic traditions: fuzzy and linear. By going quantitative, we built
new bridges between linear and fuzzy logic: first by showing addition on the reals presents a
previously-undiscovered additive nature, and then by exhibiting MALL as a limit of a family
of ‘quantitative fuzzy logics’.
In addition, thanks to its smooth monotonic additives, 𝑝QLL promises to fill the existing gaps
between fuzzy and differentiable logics, newly introduced by the machine learning verification
community. The latter have been reported to be problematic from the algebraic and proof-theoretic
points of view [1, 48, 54], and 𝑝QLL offers a long-awaited fix for the AI community. At the same
time, 𝑝QLL opens the way for exploiting new connections between linear logic and differentiable
logics, which goes in line with the recent efforts to connect linear logic and neuro-symbolic AI [55].
In future work, we would like to further explore the connection between the behaviour of
additives and prelinearity observed in Section 4.3. We expect this will enable to repurpose ar-
guments from categorical logic and/or algebraic semantics of fuzzy logic to prove completeness
of (some extension of) 𝑝QLL in [0, ∞]. Relatedly, a categorical exploration of the semantics of
the logic seems warranted, to better understand the behaviour of ‘additive universal properties’
we touched on in Section 5.
We also set to investigate proof nets for 𝑝QLL, adding to the traditional graph-based represen-
tation for MALL proofs a notion of flow of validity.

Finally, a first-order extension of these ideas is already in the workings, involving ‘soft’ quan-
