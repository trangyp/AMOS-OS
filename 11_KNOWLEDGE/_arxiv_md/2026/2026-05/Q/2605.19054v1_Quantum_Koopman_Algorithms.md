Quantum Koopman Algorithms
David Jennings, Kamil Korzekwa, Matteo Lostaglio,∗and Guoming Wang
PsiQuantum, 700 Hansen Way, Palo Alto, CA 94304, USA
We define an observable-space framework of Quantum Koopman Algorithms (QKAs) for sim-
ulating the dynamics of both linear quantum and nonlinear classical systems, based on approxi-
mately closed sets of observables and efficient coherent encodings of their Koopman-driven evolu-
tion. QKAs have two strands: Dynamic-QKA for the initial-value problem of observables dynamics,
and Spectral-QKA for the eigenvalue analysis of the Koopman operator. We demonstrate the scope
of the framework through several applications. First, for classes of N free fermions linearly coupled
to a bath, we construct quantum algorithms with gate cost O(polylog(N)), an exponential improve-
ment over classical methods, and use them to reconstruct heat flows and decay rates. Second, for
nonlinear classical dynamics, we introduce a novel nonlinear interaction-picture quantum algorithm
that enables perturbative expansions around solvable nonlinear reference flows, going beyond exist-
ing approaches that only apply to weakly nonlinear systems. Third, we develop spectral methods
for extracting eigen-frequencies of late-time nonlinear dynamics, introducing a windowed quantum
ODE-solver. Our results identify the Koopman-quantum interface as a natural setting in which
quantum algorithms can exploit observable-space structure to simulate both classical and quantum
dynamics.
Introduction.
Under the geometric view of dynamical
systems, one evolves states under equations of motion
˙x = F(x),
x(t) ∈Cd,
t ∈[0, T],
(1)
where F : Cd 7→Cd can be a linear or nonlinear function.
Observable properties are then treated as functionals over
the set of states. Most quantum algorithms for dynamical
systems follow this paradigm: they first output a coher-
ent encoding of the solution x(T) at final time t = T,
or a superposition of x(t) over a subset of discrete times
in [0, T], by solving Eq. (1); only then information ex-
traction is addressed, as a separate post-processing task.
This “compute first, ask questions later” paradigm un-
derlies much of the literature on quantum algorithms for
Hamiltonian simulation and ordinary differential equa-
tions (ODEs) [1–7].
In parallel, classical dynamical systems have long been
studied through Koopman theory. Although the mathe-
matical foundations go back to the 1930s [8], Koopman-
based methods have recently developed into practical
tools in engineering and industrial applications, including
aerospace, mechanics, fluid dynamics, and energy sys-
tems [9–12].
The central idea is to describe nonlinear
dynamics through distinguished observables whose evo-
lution is (approximately) closed under the flow. However,
this class of observables can quickly become classically
intractable if the dimension becomes exponentially large.
In this work, we delineate an observable-space frame-
work of Quantum Koopman Algorithms (QKAs), which
encode such large observable spaces in quantum ampli-
tudes and simulate their dynamics on a quantum com-
puter. This framework applies to nonlinear classical dy-
namics, but also provides alternative results in quan-
tum dynamics, e.g., for estimating dense observables [13]
or shadow Hamiltonian simulations and related algo-
rithms [14, 15]. We consider two strands of algorithms:
Dynamic-QKAs, aimed at initial-value problems for ob-
servables, and Spectral-QKAs, aimed at reconstructing
Koopman spectral data.
We apply Dynamic-QKAs to classes of open quantum
systems, achieving exponential improvements, and also
to nonlinear classical dynamics, tackling certain strong
nonlinearities by introducing a novel ‘nonlinear interac-
tion picture’ approach. Spectral-QKAs can be viewed as
a generalization of Quantum Phase Estimation (QPE),
as they extract spectral properties of the observables’ dy-
namics. We apply these to the extraction of decay rates
of an open quantum systems, but also to sample from the
oscillatory modes of a nonlinear dynamics. For the lat-
ter, we develop a windowed quantum ODE solver, which
may be of independent interest. See Table I.

QKAs & the nonlinear interaction picture.
Most
practically important classical dynamical systems, in-
cluding those in fluid and plasma dynamics, are governed
by nonlinear equations of motion. Since quantum com-
puters implement linear evolution, the problem must be
first embedded into a larger linear system of observables.
Controlling the dynamical closure error resulting from
such a finite-dimensional embedding is a central bottle-
neck [38–44]. In the absence of detailed knowledge, an
exponentially large space of observables may be needed.
One prominent closure scheme is the Carleman em-
bedding method, which tracks the monomial observables

We tackle this key constraint through the lens of the in-
teraction picture in quantum algorithms, where one splits
a Hamiltonian into one piece that is dominant, but can
be fast-forwarded, and another ‘perturbative’ one. In the
Koopman framework, an analogous separation yields

In the tra-

viewed as a nonlinear interaction picture, where A gives

nonlinearity of the perturbation δB(x), rather than by
the full nonlinearity of F: nonlinearity is measured rela-
tive to the solvable reference flow A(x). We illustrate this
formalism with a simple example in Appendix S4. With
this scheme, we can construct amplitude encodings of g
in quantum states and provide a QKA for observables A
of interest, for classes of strongly nonlinear systems.

Outlook.
We introduced Quantum Koopman Algo-
rithms (QKAs), a framework for simulating linear and


## Page 5


5
nonlinear dynamics on a quantum computer by solv-
ing the initial-value problem for a set of generalized ob-
servables (Dynamic-QKA), or performing modal analy-
sis of the Koopman operator (Spectral-QKA). Since the
seminal Applied Koopmanism work [9], Koopman-based
techniques have evolved from a promising conceptual di-
rection into practical tools for modeling, control, fore-
casting, and diagnosis, especially in settings with large
data [56, 57].
In contrast, the extension to the quan-
tum regime is a novel frontier, with exponential compu-
tational space but constrained access to raw data. Our
results provide a basis for such future work.
