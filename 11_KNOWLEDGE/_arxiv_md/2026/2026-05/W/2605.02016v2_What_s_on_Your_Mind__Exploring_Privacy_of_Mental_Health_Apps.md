Speak Freely & Never Mind the Pesky Trackers:
Privacy Analysis of Popular Therapy Apps
Chloe Georgiou1, Hans Lu1, Emiliano De Cristofaro2, Gene Tsudik1
1University of California, Irvine
2University of California, Riverside
Abstract
Therapy and life-coaching apps have been rapidly grow-
ing in number, flavors, and popularity.
However, their
users routinely share highly sensitive and personal in-
formation, such as, traumas, fantasies, desires, relation-
ship difficulties, and other mental health concerns. This
prompts the need for an empirical analysis of privacy prac-
tices in this ecosystem, and particularly the alignment be-
tween these apps’ privacy policies and their actual behav-
ior. In this paper, we present a comprehensive analysis of
25 popular Android mental health and life-coaching apps,
combining static analysis, dynamic network capture, and
LLM-assisted privacy policy extraction validated against
manual annotation.
Our findings highlight serious concerns and substantial
transparency gaps. First, every app embeds at least one
tracker SDK that its privacy policy does not name, and 68%
of apps fail to disclose at least half of the trackers detected
in their APKs; Talkie alone embeds 20 while naming none.
Second, we identify 16 permission-policy contradictions
across 13 apps, i.e., a dangerous permission is declared in
the manifest but omitted from the policy, including 6 apps
that request camera or microphone access without disclos-
ing photo, video, or audio collection. Third, 48% of apps
disclose third-party AI processing (e.g., via OpenAI, An-
thropic, Groq), with one app sending journal entries to all
three simultaneously, while 7 apps use only generic lan-
guage that leaves recipients unidentified. Taken together,
our findings demonstrate that current disclosure practices
fall short of the transparency required for meaningful in-
formed consent. We argue for a significantly updated reg-
ulatory framework governing therapy apps in the spirit of
the professional and ethical standards that bind licensed
human therapists.

Introduction
In recent years, the ecosystem of digital behavioral
health interventions [57] has transitioned from niche well-
ness tools to a more mainstream presence, with adoption
rates rivaling those of established consumer software [21].
Prominent examples include Headspace, a popular medita-
tion and mindfulness app with over 10 million downloads
and millions of paid subscribers [12], the AI-companion
app Replika with a reported user base of 40 million in
2025 [10], and 7Cups, an app connecting users with
peer supporters and licensed clinicians claiming to have
reached almost 80 million users [1].
Overall, digital mental health tools, including smart-
phone apps, virtual reality, and generative AI, can function
as self-help, coach-guided, or clinician-led interventions,
offering scalable care delivery beyond the constraints of
traditional telehealth [9]. Mental health apps promise to
substantially reduce financial barriers associated with con-
ventional psychotherapy, particularly among those with
limited insurance coverage [53]. They also afford con-
tinuous, on-demand availability, eliminating scheduling
constraints inherent to clinician-mediated care and pro-
viding support during off-hours when crises may be most
acute [7, 13]. Moreover, unlike human practitioners who
may ostensibly be subject to mood fluctuations, fatigue,
and biases that impair clinical judgment and therapeutic
alliance [50], mental health apps promise to offer consis-
tent therapeutic feedback, sustained attentional engage-
ment, and longitudinal documentation of patient histo-
ries — factors increasingly recognized as influencing treat-
ment outcomes [57].
However, these platforms occupy a uniquely sensitive
operational context, as users routinely disclose trauma his-
tories, suicidal ideation, interpersonal conflicts, and clini-
cal diagnoses. Many users would not disclose such sensi-
tive information even to their closest family members and
friends. Human therapists are bound by professional codes
of ethics and typically hold (country-dependent) creden-
tials and licenses that attest to formal training and com-
petency examinations [3, 4]. In contrast, mental health
apps, even those developed under expert clinical supervi-
sion, are not bound by the same rules or held to the same
credentialing standards [57]. While unauthorized disclo-
sure of patient information constitutes a serious ethical
and legal violation for licensed therapists, mental health
apps may transmit user (meta-)data to third-party entities,
e.g., advertising networks and social media platforms.

Additionally, apps in this space increasingly advertise a
variety of AI functions, relying on Large Language Mod-
els (LLMs) and conversational AI agents as their primary
interaction layer, which introduces additional opacity into
data-handling practices. Sensitive personal disclosures ac-
cumulated across longitudinal sessions may be retained
on users’ devices and/or stored in cloud-based infrastruc-
tures, a practice not fundamentally distinct from the elec-
tronic health records now ubiquitous among practitioners.
However, user devices remain vulnerable to a range of se-
curity threats, including unauthorized access, data leak-
age, or malicious modification of accumulated therapeu-
tic records. Moreover, while licensed therapists operate
within well-established procedural frameworks governing
patient data transfer, as well as data retention and dele-
tion protocols, there is currently no equivalent regulatory
infrastructure for the digital mental health app ecosystem.
The infamous 2020 data breach of the Finnish psy-
chotherapy service provider Vaastamo [62] demonstrated
the catastrophic consequences of failing to protect mental
health data. When a criminal hacked the patient database
and demanded a ransom, the provider refused, and ther-
apy session notes of over 33,000 patients were exposed.
In the wake of this release, many patients were extremely
traumatized and a few even committed suicide [38].
These concerns collectively underscore the need to care-
fully analyze privacy practices within the digital mental
health and life-coaching application ecosystem.
While
prior work has examined tracker disclosure in general-
purpose Android apps [33, 45] as well as security vulner-
abilities in older mental health apps [28], it remains an
open problem to systematically measure third-party ser-
vices involved in therapy applications, such as analytics,
advertising, or AI providers—in particular, against their
corresponding privacy policy disclosures. To address this
gap, this paper presents a multi-method measurement
study of 25 popular Android apps, offering an illustrative
snippet of the mental health and life-coaching apps. More
precisely, we combine static and dynamic analysis and se-
mantic privacy policy analysis to investigate whether the
policies accurately disclose their observable data practices,
and to characterize the nature and magnitude of any dis-
crepancies that emerge.
Technical Roadmap. On the behavioral side, we use two
complementary static analysis tools, MobSF [42] and An-
droguard [16], to extract dangerous permissions that apps
declare in their Android manifests, identify embedded
third-party tracker SDKs, and assess security configura-
tions. We also perform dynamic network traffic capture
using Wireshark during instrumented application walk-
throughs, uncovering third-party domains contacted at
runtime. On the policy side, we extract structured pri-
vacy practice claims through a two-stage pipeline: an ini-
tial keyword-matching pass followed by LLM-assisted ex-
traction using Gemini Pro 2.5, validated against manual
annotation and cross-checked with GPT-5.4.
We then compare behavioral evidence against policy
claims across three analytical dimensions: (1) embedded
tracker disclosure, (2) dangerous permission-policy con-
tradictions, and (3) third-party AI provider acknowledg-
ment. This allows us to systematically quantify disclosure
gaps at both the application and ecosystem level.
Overall, our analysis is structured across three axes:
1. Prevalence. To what extent do the observed data prac-
tices of mental health applications remain undisclosed
in their corresponding privacy policies?
2. Severity. Upon examining three analytical categories,
i.e., (1) embedded third-party trackers, (2) sensitive
permission usage, and (3) data transmission to third-
party AI providers, which exhibits the greatest discrep-
ancy between observed behavior and stated policy dis-
closures?
3. Validation. To what degree does LLM-assisted privacy
policy extraction mirror manual annotation, and do ex-
traction errors affect the measurement of disclosure
gaps?
Key Findings. Our results highlight several concerns:
• Significant disclosure gap: Static analysis with MobSF
detects at least one tracker SDK embedded in every app
that is not named in the corresponding privacy policy.
Also, 8 of the 19 apps (42%) that request camera or
microphone permissions fail to disclose at least one of
those permissions in their privacy policy.
• Dynamic analysis reveals runtime behaviors invisible to
static analysis: Each of the 20 apps we instrument at
runtime contacts at least one third-party tracker domain
not named in its policy. Talkie alone contacts five dis-
tinct advertising domains (Vungle, AppLovin, TikTok/-
Pangle, Moloco, Mintegral), while disclosing none. The
authentication method significantly affects tracker acti-
vation, with Replika triggering five tracker domains un-
der Google sign-in versus one under email sign-in.
• Two-thirds of the apps fail to disclose trackers: 17/25
apps (68%) fail to disclose at least half of their embed-
ded trackers. For instance, Talkie embeds 20 trackers
while disclosing none in its policy.
• Half of the apps use 3rd-party AI: 12/25 (48%) apps’
privacy policies state that user data is processed by
3rd-party AI providers.
Only 5 of these name spe-
cific providers (all name OpenAI), while Rosebuds pol-
icy names multiple providers (OpenAI, Anthropic, and
Groq). The remaining 7 apps use generic language such
as “AI services” or “large language models.”
Implications. While concerning, our findings should not
be interpreted as evidence of deliberate misconduct of
therapy app developers. Rather, they may be attributable
to systemic factors, e.g., the inheritance of third-party
SDKs without adequate privacy auditing, the reuse of boil-
erplate policy language, and organizational prioritization

That said, our measurement study raises serious con-
cerns for vulnerable populations. Therapy app users dis-

confidentiality, yet these apps lack the regulatory protec-
tions afforded to patients of licensed clinicians. Address-

for such regulatory development and serves as a call for
the broader security and privacy research community to
engage more systematically with the distinct risks posed
by AI-mediated mental health platforms.

Related Work
This section reviews prior work on: security and pri-
vacy analysis of Android apps, transparency and compli-
ance with privacy policies, and privacy risks in health and
AI companion apps.
Static Analysis. Kollnig et al. [33] perform static analysis
to study 3rd-party tracking across both iOS and Android
ecosystems using static analysis.
They find widespread
3rd-party tracking and unique user identifiers sharing, in-
cluding in apps for children. Razaghpanah et al. [45] use
static library detection combined with network analysis to
characterize tracker SDKs across thousands of apps glob-
ally, while Wessels et al. [61] focus on persistent cross-
context tracking using shared browser state. Specter et
al. [52] analyze fingerprinting SDKs embedded in mobile
apps, mapping the market of 3rd-party SDKs that enable
device fingerprinting beyond traditional tracking.
Dynamic Analysis. Along with static analysis, Reardon et
al. [46] monitor runtime behavior and traffic of 100, 000-
s of apps, uncovering side and covert channels used by
apps and 3rd-party SDKs to access sensitive data without
permission. Huckvale et al. [27] intercept traffic from 36
depression-focused and smoking cessation apps, finding
widespread data transmission to Google and Facebook ser-
vices, without adequate policy disclosure. Dong et al. [17]
find that tracking SDKs continue to evolve evasion tech-
niques, discovering covert 3rd-party identifiers stored in
external storage to bypass Android’s identifier restrictions.
To detect 3rd-party tracking within Android apps via em-
bedded WebViews, Liu et al. [37] introduce WTDetect
framework, showing that tracking occurs through native
SDKs and web content loaded within apps.
Sensitive Apps. Dong et al. [18] analyze privacy practices
of period-tracking apps following the Dobbs v. Jackson
decision [56], demonstrating the importance of domain-
specific privacy analysis for apps that handle sensitive
health data. Iwaya et al. [28] analyze 27 mental health
apps using static and dynamic analysis, as well as privacy
policy evaluation, finding that 20 of 27 apps pose critical
security risks due to insecure cryptographic implementa-
tions, leaked API keys and credentials in network requests,
and unnecessary permission usage. That work, published
in 2022, predates modern LLMs and focuses only on secu-
rity implementation flaws.
Privacy Policy Compliance. Prior work has also exam-
ined whether app privacy policies accurately reflect actual
data practices. Andow et al. [5] detect internal contra-
dictions within policies on Google Play and inconsistencies
between policy claims and actual data flows [6]. Zimmeck
et al. [65] combine machine-learning-based privacy policy
analysis with static code analysis of 17,991 Android apps,
demonstrating the viability of automated compliance de-
tection. A follow-up effort [64] also finds that many app
privacy policies are either absent or silent about the apps’
actual practices. Samarin et al. [49] use Wireshark to cap-
ture app traffic and compare it to privacy-policy claims,
evaluating Android apps’ compliance with the California
Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA). They find that many apps
fail to name the third parties they actually share data with.
We use the same approach but focus on therapy apps and
add 3rd-party AI/LLM providers, which Samarin et al. do
not cover.
Platform Transparency. Some prior work also focused on
apps’ transparency with respect to tracking and data col-
lection. Kollnig et al. [32] find that most Android apps en-
gage in 3rd-party tracking, while few obtain consent, indi-
cating potentially widespread violations of GDPR (the Eu-
ropean Union’s General Data Protection Regulation) and
CCPA (California Consumer Privacy Act).
Mohamed et
al. [43] investigate Apple’s App Tracking Transparency
framework, showing limited effectiveness at reducing real-
world tracking, while Reyes et al. [48] show that a large
number of children’s apps collect and share personal infor-
mation in ways that violate federal regulations protecting
minors, such as COPPA.
AI Companion Risk Analysis. In very recent work ex-
amining risks in AI companion applications, Brigham et
al. [11] provide a harm taxonomy for AI companion apps,
e.g., sensitive data collection, anthropomorphism, en-
gagement manipulation, and synthetic non-consensual im-
agery.
Brigham et al.
also conduct a qualitative walk-
through of 30 apps from a sample of 489, identifying
risks to user privacy, though without inspecting the apps’
APKs, manifest permissions, or runtime network traffic.
Kwesi et al. [35] examine users’ security and privacy atti-
tudes toward general-purpose LLM chatbots used for men-
tal health, finding that users simultaneously value the per-
ceived anonymity of AI-based support while expressing un-
certainty about data handling practices.
Summary. Overall, prior work established methodologies
for static tracker detection at scale [33, 52, 64], dynamic
network traffic verification [27, 46, 49], and privacy policy
consistency analysis [5, 6, 49, 65]. However, to the best of
our knowledge, this is the first measurement study of ther-
apy apps that systematically compares third-party services
observed in each app (covering analytics, advertising, so-

its privacy policy. We do so by combining static tracker de-
tection, dynamic network verification, and policy extrac-
tion to measure disclosure gaps across embedded trackers,

Preliminaries: Dataset
Our first step is to identify a meaningful dataset of
therapy and life coaching apps.
As common in prior
work [24, 33, 46], we focus on the Android ecosystem
mainly for two reasons: (1) it dominates the global mobile
OS market with a 68.24% share, as of February 2026 [54],
and (2) its open APK format facilitates static analysis.
Systematic App Discovery. We identify candidate apps
through Google Play searches using the following key-
words: “therapy,” “mental health,” “CBT” (Cognitive Be-
havioral Therapy), “counseling,” and “emotional well-
ness.” These terms span both user-facing language (how
people search for support) and clinically-grounded termi-
nology (established intervention types), mirroring the key-
word selection strategy of prior app studies [2, 25, 39]. We
retrieve the number of Google Play downloads and retain
apps with at least 10,000 downloads, a threshold consis-
tent with prior privacy studies of Android apps [8, 48].
This threshold yields a good balance between mean-
ingful user bases and scalability.
Incidentally, previous
work focusing on the mental health app ecosystem [29]
found strong rank correlations between downloads and
monthly active users, specifically a Kendall rank correla-
tion of 𝜏= 0.92 (𝑝< .001).
Unsurprisingly, our English-language keyword search
returns no apps that only support other languages and ex-
ceed 10k downloads. In other words, while there might be
non-English-only therapy apps, they would most likely ap-
pear via localized keywords outside our search terms. In
fact, Chinese-language apps are reportedly used for men-
tal health support [47] that are available from Chinese app
stores. Examples include KnowYourself, Jiandanxinli, and
JD Health’s AI therapeutic companion, as well as general-
purpose LLMs (e.g., DeepSeek). We leave a multilingual
analysis of therapy app privacy practices to future work.

Data Collection. In March 2026, for each app, we collect:
• Metadata, including: download count, country of de-

app is excluded due to missing or unreachable policies.

Methodology
We now describe our measurement pipeline, also shown
in Figure 2. It involves static and dynamic analysis tools
to extract app security/privacy behavior, LLM-supported
extraction of privacy policy claims, and the analysis of gaps
in disclosure between the two.

We treat a declared dangerous permission as a disclos-

Security Analysis. We rely on Mobile Security Framework
(MobSF) [42] for automated static analysis of Android
APKs, checking for manifest mis-configurations, insecure
cryptographic implementations, hard-coded secrets, and
known vulnerability patterns. MobSF tags each finding
with a Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) identi-
fier and assigns a severity level of High, Warning, or Se-
cure [40].
It then computes a composite App Security
Score in a [0 −100] range as a weighted count of those
severity labels, rather than from Common Vulnerability
Scoring System (CVSS) aggregates [41]. We use MobSF
primarily for tracker identification and manifest analysis.
We report its security scores as descriptive context rather
than as a validated security metric.
Overall, Androguard extracts the permissions data
needed for our analysis, while MobSF is broader, cover-
ing trackers and cryptographic and other security checks
in a single pipeline. We use both tools to cross-validate our
findings.
4.2
Dynamic Traffic Analysis
In addition to static analysis, we capture network traffic
with Wireshark during manual app walkthroughs. We use
a Google Pixel 6 Pro for all captures. After installing each
app, we connect it to a controlled Wi-Fi hotspot routed
through a monitoring Lenovo ThinkPad T14S laptop. We
capture network traffic to identify 3rd-party domains con-
tacted at runtime, corroborating and supplementing the
tracker evidence obtained through static analysis.
For
apps that support multiple sign-in methods (email, Google,
Facebook, and Apple), we run separate capture sessions for
each to observe whether each authentication method influ-
ences tracker activation. Note: two authors of this paper
independently validated captured traffic to ensure consis-
tency of domain classification.
While we capture network traffic for all 25 apps, since

1) camera or photo data collection, 2) microphone or au-

trackers and analytics services, and 5) advertising and
marketing data sharing. For category 3), we use two dis-

1. Named Providers:
“openai,” “chatgpt,” “gpt,” “an-

2. Generic AI Terminology:
“large language model,”
“llm,” “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “ai

6
Discussion
We now discuss our findings in the context of existing
privacy regulations, enforcement precedents, and broader
ethical concerns.
6.1
Regulatory Implications
United States.
The California Consumer Privacy Act
(CCPA) [55] requires businesses to disclose “the categories
of 3rd parties to whom the personal information was dis-
closed” (§1798.100(a)(5)). While CCPA permits categor-
ical disclosure, categories must be meaningful. Arguably,
“service providers” does not meaningfully describe Face-
book, AppsFlyer, Google AdMob, Amplitude, Mixpanel,
and 15 other entities, each with its own data practices.
The disclosure failures we document expose app devel-
opers to direct regulatory liability under U.S. consumer-
protection law. Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission
(FTC) Act (15 U.S.C. §45) prohibits “unfair or deceptive
acts or practices in or affecting commerce” [59] and the
FTC has used this authority to act against mental-health
services that share user data with advertisers while assur-
ing users of confidentiality.
The most relevant U.S. enforcement to apps in our study
is the 2023 Federal Trade Commission’s against the online
therapy provider BetterHelp (not in our corpus), which
was fined $7.8M for sharing sensitive mental-health data
with Facebook, Snapchat, and Pinterest after assuring
users their data would remain confidential [22]. Several
apps in our dataset make similar “private” or “confiden-
tial” assurances at the start of use (Section 5.1.1) while
embedding tracker SDKs which are not cited in their pri-
vacy policies.
GDPR. Under GDPR Article 13(1)(e), data controllers must
provide data subjects with “the recipients or categories of
recipients of the personal data, if any” at the time of col-
lection. Article 29 Working Party [19] clarifies that, where
an app chooses to disclose categories of recipients rather
than naming them, “the information should be as specific
as possible.” Again, generic terms like “3rd parties” or “ser-
vice providers” do not satisfy this criterion.
Alas, our findings reveal widespread non-compliance.
As discussed, more than two-thirds of the apps (17/25) fail
to disclose at least half of their embedded trackers, while
almost one-third (7/25) use exclusively generic language
while embedding 3 or more specific trackers. In particular,
the popular chatbot app Talkie embeds 20 named trackers
while naming zero in its privacy policy.
The Replika Precedent. In April 2025, the Italian data
protection authority issued a €5 million fine to Replika’s
developer for GDPR violations, finding privacy notices
and information practices inadequate and age-verification
mechanisms deficient [23]. Our analysis identifies sim-
ilar deficiencies across the therapy app ecosystem: Rep-
lika names only 1 of 7 trackers uncovered by our dynamic
analysis, and almost half (12/25) of the apps name none
of the detected trackers in their privacy policies. While
the Replika fine suggests that regulators are beginning to
scrutinize this domain, our analysis indicates widespread
exposure to similar enforcement risk.
6.2
Data Deletion
GDPR Article 17 and CCPA §1798.105 grant users the
right to request deletion of personal data. We examine the
deletion mechanism stated by each app (if any). We also
note the emails users can contact – to ease presentation,
we defer details to Appendix D.
Overall, across the 25 apps, 28% (7/25) offer in-app
deletion, 76% (19/25) require an email request, and 12%
(3/25) direct users to a web portal. Note that counts over-
lap, as several apps offer multiple ways to delete data. The
majority of apps (76%) require users to send an email re-
questing deletion, which itself forces users to disclose to
a stranger that they used a mental health app and want
their data removed. Two apps (CBThelp and Clarity) state
there is no deletion mechanism at all, which is a potential
violation of both GDPR and CCPA. Even when deletion is
possible, it may not be complete: data already sent to 3rd-
party AI providers may be embedded in model weights that
cannot be selectively erased [14].

6.3
AI Training
We also argue that sending data to 3rd-party AI
providers raises serious concerns. First, every additional
provider expands the attack surface for data breaches. For
instance, Rosebud’s use of three providers means a user’s
journal entries may potentially exist on four separate sys-
tems, each with its own security practices. Second, data
used to train AI models may never be fully removed. While
sending data to a third-party AI provider does not imply
training use, several apps in our dataset state in their poli-
cies that they do use user content for training.2
As discussed, e.g., by Cooper et al. [14], there is no
meaningful recourse for users to delete specific informa-
tion from a trained model. In fact, policies often confirm
the absence of opt-out mechanisms.3
This means that,
even if a user deletes their account, their mental health
disclosures may already be embedded in model weights or
training datasets that cannot be selectively erased. More-
over, 7 apps in our corpus reference AI providers with-
out naming them, making it impossible for users to know
where their data ends up or how to request its deletion.
6.4
Why Undisclosed Trackers Cause Harm
Our study reveals a non-negligible presence of undis-
closed trackers in therapy and life-coaching apps, yielding
two concrete concerns.
Mental health status inference through cross-app
tracking. Advertising SDKs (e.g., Facebook) transmit a
user’s Google Advertising ID (AAID) along with app-usage
events to their servers. Because the same AAID is shared
across every app on a device, a tracker embedded in both a
therapy app and a shopping app can link activities between
them. Kollnig et al. [32] show that, although most Android
apps engage in 3rd-party tracking, 3.5% obtain the con-
sent required by the ePrivacy Directive [20]. This means
this cross-app linking happens silently and at a massive
scale. In practice, an ad network that receives SDK calls
from Replika, Wysa, and BetterUp can determine that a de-
vice owner is seeking mental health support without ever
seeing a single conversation. Cuevas et al. [15] show that
Facebook assigns health-related ad-interest labels, such as
“anxiety disorder awareness” and “clinical depression,” to
users, based on inferred behavior, all without explicit con-
sent. In our dataset, Facebook’s SDK appears in 10 of 25
apps, while none of their privacy policies disclose it.
Implications for the data broker market. Data brokers
are already advertising the ability to sell sensitive mental
health information. In 2023, Kim [30] found 11 brokers in
the wild willing to sell lists of individuals sorted by men-
tal health condition (including depression, anxiety, bipo-

Most mental health apps fall outside HIPAA because they

Facebook, AppsFlyer, and Amplitude) feed into the same
advertising and data ecosystem that brokers draw from,
thus prompting serious concerns of a new stream of sensi-
tive data entering the data broker ecosystem.
6.5
Broader Ethical Concerns
Digital mental health apps promise a few compelling ad-
vantages – e.g., they scale beyond the constraints of con-
ventional therapy, reduce financial barriers, provide on-
demand support, offer consistent longitudinal feedback,
etc. [7, 9, 50, 53, 57]. On the other hand, these apps might
often lack rigorous clinical validation, and unlike licensed
practitioners, developers bear no professional liability for
harm [26, 36]. There is also a risk that app-based support
displaces rather than supplements professional care, with
users opting for convenient but unvalidated tools [58]. For
instance, experts fear that treating digital companions as
genuine solutions to loneliness may create an illusion of
connection without authentic reciprocity [51].
The sensitivity of mental health data further compounds
these risks, as monetization through advertising and data
brokerage may expose users to downstream consequences
in insurance or employment contexts [27, 30]. In partic-
ular, a potential mismatch between awareness of privacy
risks in using, e.g., LLM chatbots for mental health support
and their lack of technical knowledge to evaluate actual
data practices [35]. Our findings demonstrate that even
users who read privacy policies would encounter incom-
plete or misleading disclosures in 68% of analyzed apps.
In one case, transparency failure extends to language it-
self: Yana’s privacy policy is written entirely in Spanish,
despite serving English-speaking users.
7
Conclusion
This paper presented a measurement study of 25 pop-
ular Android therapy and life-coaching apps, comparing
privacy policy disclosures against static analysis and net-
work traffic observations. Our findings reveal systematic
transparency failures: 68% of apps fail to disclose at least
half their embedded trackers, and 48% send user data to
3rd-party AI providers. The stakes are not merely tech-
nical. Users who disclose suicidal ideation or trauma to
an app they believe is private cannot, arguably, make in-
formed decisions about data they do not know is being
collected. We argue that therapy apps warrant the same
standard of confidentiality obligations as human therapists
they increasingly replace.
Interpretation of Results.
Our findings should not be
read as accusations that any of these apps deliberately de-

ceive users. We have found no evidence of malicious intent.
Undisclosed trackers and permission-policy contradictions
are more likely the result of inherited SDKs, reused privacy
policies, and competing development priorities. However,
the same patterns across 25 apps point to a systemic prob-
lem: the therapy-app industry lacks a standard for han-
dling sensitive data. We see this as a motivation for a code
of ethics for app-based therapy, such as requiring apps to
audit their 3rd-party libraries, disclose their trackers, and
keep their privacy policies up to date. This could start as
voluntary industry guidance and evolve into regulations
over time.
Limitations & Future Work. Our work focuses only on
Android apps; iOS versions of the same apps may exhibit
different tracker configurations, permission models, and
SDK behavior. Also, our sample is determined by our key-
word search and popularity filtering (see Section 3), so we
may leave out apps specific to particular disorders, such as
bipolar disorder or eating disorders, as well as other ther-
apeutic practices like Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)
or Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT). Although
we believe our corpus captures a representative sample of
the broader ecosystem, we plan to extend our analysis to
a wider range of digital mental health intervention apps
in future work. Two apps in our dataset, Happify and
Youper, were removed from Google Play after our data col-
lection: Youper is available only on iOS as of March 2026,
and Happify has been discontinued and folded into the
broader Dario Mind ecosystem. Two more apps, Woebot
and Limbic, require healthcare provider invitation codes,
which limited our analysis to their pre-authentication be-
havior; thus, our observations likely underestimate the ac-
tual runtime tracker activity of these apps.
Static analysis via MobSF may over-approximate run-
time behavior, since a tracker SDK embedded in an APK
might not be activated in every session; conversely, it could
miss obfuscated SDKs not present in the code. However,
we cross-validated MobSF permission detection against
Androguard, which agreed on all 25 apps, and manually
verified tracker classifications to remove false positives.
Also, while our dynamic analysis captured trackers ac-
tive during app usage, it did not isolate the specific ac-
tions (e.g., sending a chatbot query or completing a mood
check-in) that may trigger them. Thus, future work could
study specific user interactions with specific trackers. Re-
latedly, our permission analysis measures declared rather
than exercised permissions. Declarations may originate
from SDK manifest fragments and may be unreachable at
runtime. As discussed in Section 4.1, we retain declaration
as the disclosure target, and leave separating first-party
from SDK-inherited declarations to future work.
Finally, our findings regarding AI providers are based
on privacy policy disclosures rather than network traffic
verification. We attempted runtime verification via packet
capture, but were unable to inspect API traffic as modern
Android apps use certificate pinning.
Ethical Considerations.
This work analyzed publicly
available Android apps and their public privacy policies.
It did not involve human participants, collecting user-
generated content, or interacting with real users. As such,
it is considered exempt by our Institutional Review Board
(IRB). All APKs were obtained through APKMirror and
APKPure; privacy policies were obtained from each app’s
publicly linked URL on Google Play; no credentials were
used. Privacy policy text was submitted to Gemini Pro 2.5
and GPT 5.4 for extraction with no personal user data in-
cluded in any prompt, and all outputs were manually re-
viewed and validated. To document data collected before
account creation or first use, we manually installed and
walked through each app using a dedicated research ac-
count with a pseudonymous identity; apps requiring pay-
ment information for free-trial access were enrolled using
a dedicated institutional research payment method, and
no real user data was entered beyond what was necessary
to access app functionality.

B
LLM Extraction Prompt
We used the prompt template reported in Figure 6 for the privacy policy analysis with Gemini Pro 2.5 and GPT 5.4.
Each policy was provided as an HTML attachment.
You are a knowledgeable, helpful, and honest assistant. You have deep expertise in current privacy regulations, including the
GDPR, CCPA, and other U.S. state privacy laws.
Task: I will provide an attachment of a therapy app’s privacy policy. Determine whether it includes information about: (1)
Collecting camera, photo, image, or video data; (2) Collecting microphone, audio, voice, or recording data; (3) Specific
named AI providers; (4) Specific named trackers; (5) Sharing data with advertisers.
Classification Rules:
Named AI Providers: Flag “1” only if the policy explicitly names a specific AI company or model (OpenAI, Anthropic, Claude,
ChatGPT, GPT-4, Google Gemini, Groq, Llama, Mistral, Cohere). Do NOT flag generic phrases (“artificial intelligence,”
“AI-powered,” “machine learning”). The app developer itself is NOT an AI provider.
Named Trackers: Flag “1” only if the policy names specific tracking/analytics services (Google Analytics, Firebase,
Mixpanel, AppsFlyer, Facebook Pixel, Amplitude, Segment, Hotjar, Braze, Adjust, Branch). Do NOT count social login, cloud
hosting, payment processors, or generic phrases (“cookies,” “web beacons,” “analytics services”) without company names.
Camera/Photo: Flag “1” if the policy mentions collecting photographs, images, video, camera access, profile pictures, facial
data, or biometric images. Check CCPA disclosure tables for “sensory data” or “audio/visual information.”
Microphone/Audio: Flag “1” if the policy mentions collecting audio, voice recordings, speech, microphone access, or call
recordings. Voice-to-text transcription should still be flagged since audio is collected, even if temporarily.
Advertising: Flag “1” if the policy mentions sharing data with advertisers, targeted/interest-based/ behavioral advertising,
ad networks, or marketing partners. Flag “0” only if they explicitly deny advertising AND there is no other indication.
Response Format: Camera/Photo: [flag, texts]
Microphone/Audio: [flag, texts]
Named AI Providers: [flag, texts]
Named Trackers: [flag, texts]
Advertising: [flag, texts]
Where flag = “1” (present) or “0” (absent), and texts = list of exact quotes or “None.”
