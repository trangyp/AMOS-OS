WHEN DO RIEMANN SOLUTIONS CONSIST OF
RAREFACTIONS, JUMPS, AND CONSTANTS?
BRADLEY J. PLOHR, STEPHEN SCHECTER, AND DAN MARCHESIN
Abstract. A solution of a Riemann problem for a strictly hyperbolic system of conserva-
tion laws is traditionally expected to consist of rarefaction waves, jump discontinuities, and
constant states. In this paper, we investigate whether a Riemann solution has this structure
when the solution is only assumed to be measurable and essentially bounded. To discrim-
inate continuous and discontinuous features in an L∞solution, we introduce one-sided
accumulation sets based on local essential images. Supposing that throughout a bounded
open interval a solution is continuous in the essential image (ess-im) sense, we prove that it
is a rarefaction wave if it is resonant (the characteristic speed equals x/t), and otherwise it is
constant. Although an ess-im discontinuity might not be a jump discontinuity, we show that
all ess-im accumulation states lie on a common Hugoniot locus and have the same speed.
Anomalies are possible if there are limit points of ess-im discontinuities, but if the set of
ess-im discontinuities is finite, then an L∞Riemann solution has bounded variation and is
composed of finitely many rarefaction waves, jump discontinuities, and constant states.
1. Introduction
Ever since Riemann’s seminal work [8] on gas dynamics, self-similar solutions of systems
of conservation laws, which we call Riemann solutions, have been constructed by assembling
rarefaction waves, jump discontinuities, and constant states. These features correspond to
mathematical properties of the solution profile.
In a rarefaction wave, the profile varies
continuously, fanning out along characteristic lines.
A jump discontinuity in the profile
propagates at a speed constrained by the Rankine–Hugoniot condition. In a constant state,
the solution is steady and homogeneous. If a Riemann solution is piecewise smooth, then
only these solution features occur. In this paper, we examine whether a Riemann solution
has this structure under the milder hypothesis that it is measurable and essentially bounded.
Dafermos [3] considered this question for strictly hyperbolic and genuinely nonlinear sys-
tems. He assumed that the solution v, as a function of the speed variable ξ := x/t, has
bounded variation (BV) and small oscillation. By leveraging an integral equation satisfied
by a solution, he showed that v is the sum of a jump function and a Lipschitz function. He
showed that the domain of v consists of three disjoint sets: the complement C of the support
of dv/dξ, where v is locally constant; the set S of jump discontinuities of v, which satisfy
the Rankine–Hugoniot condition; and the remaining set W, in which v varies continuously,
ξ equals a characteristic speed for v(ξ), and dv/dξ is a corresponding eigenvector almost
everywhere (a.e.).

are measurable and essentially bounded instead of BV. However, we find that extension to

The present paper concerns strictly hyperbolic systems that are not required to be gen-
uinely nonlinear. We treat a scale-invariant weak solution v ∈L∞without assuming that it
is a small oscillation solution. Our definitions of continuity and discontinuity for members
of L∞are based on the concept of essential image (abbreviated ess-im). Crucially, the fol-
lowing properties hold: if v is ess-im continuous at each point of an open interval, then v is
equal a.e. to a function v that is continuous within this interval in the usual sense; and at
an ess-im discontinuity point, v has distinct left and right ess-im accumulation sets.
Associated to v are ess-im variants of the sets C, S, and W. Following Dafermos, we show
that the integral equation satisfied by v implies that v is constant within each connected
component of C. We extend this result: within each bounded open interval contained in W,
v is a BV rarefaction wave (as generalized to accommodate loss of genuine nonlinearity).
Central to the proof is locally straightening the rarefaction integral curves of one of the
characteristic families.
We also show that an ess-im discontinuity point ξ ∈S has at least two distinct accumu-
lation states; this result entails that S ⊔W is closed and C is open. All accumulation states
for v at ξ ∈S are restricted to lie on a common Hugoniot locus and have the same speed.
Moreover, if ξ ∈S is isolated, then v has unique left and right accumulation states at ξ, i.e.,
v has an ess-im jump discontinuity.
Nonetheless, v can have anomalous features, such as limit points of S or infinitely many
connected components of C or W. For example [7], an infinite sequence of under-compressive
shock waves can accumulate at an ess-im discontinuity that is not a jump discontinuity.
However, if S is finite, a Riemann solution comprises finitely many rarefaction waves, jump
discontinuities, and constant states.
The system of conservation laws (2.1) often models a physical process that exhibits nearly
discontinuous features, such as shock waves, contact discontinuities, and under-compressive
waves, which are subject to physical principles beyond conservation. To serve as idealiza-
tions of these features, jump discontinuities must be constrained by admissibility conditions
that reflect these principles. However, as our purpose is only to explore the mathemati-
cal possibilities for self-similar solutions, we do not impose any admissibility conditions on
discontinuities.
For analysis involving L∞functions, we find it critical to employ ess-im accumulation sets,
which are local geometric objects that package measure-theoretic information and respect L∞
equivalence classes. While the essential image of a measurable function is a classical concept,
ess-im accumulation sets are rarely defined; they have received systematic development only
for real-valued functions [6]. In Sec. 4.1 and Appendix A, we explain the ess-im approach
and compare it to two alternatives that prove inadequate for L∞conservation law theory.
In outline, the paper is as follows. Section 2 recalls definitions from the theory of conser-
vation laws. In Sec. 3, we derive an integral equation satisfied by a self-similar solution; it
facilitates the proof of solution regularity and structure. In Sec. 4 and Appendix A we define
ess-im accumulation sets and continuity. As shown in Sec. 5, the integral equation implies
that a solution is constant in any interval in which it is ess-im continuous and non-resonant.
This argument is extended to characterize rarefaction waves in Sec. 6, the principal idea be-
ing to straighten the rarefaction integral curves of the relevant family locally. The integral
equation also constrains the accumulation states of an ess-im discontinuity point to lie on a

4.1. Continuity for weak solutions. Suppose that v is a self-similar weak solution of

9. Conclusion
As illustrated in Remark 8.3, a self-similar weak solution might contain a countably infinite
number of ess-im discontinuities. As S is contained in a compact set [ξL, ξR] by Prop. 7.3, S
is infinite if and only if S ′ is nonempty. Permitting infinitely many discontinuities, even ones
required to satisfy an admissibility condition, opens the door to anomalous phenomena.
For instance, in Ref. [7] we construct a self-similar weak solution v containing an ess-im
discontinuity at speed ξ⋆that is not a jump discontinuity. In this example:
• A−

• v also contains an infinite sequence of jump discontinuities with speeds {ξk}∞
k=0 that
increase to ξ⋆; and
• each jump discontinuity is admissible according to a traveling wave criterion, each
being an under-compressive shock wave, i.e., its profile is an orbit connecting two
saddle points.

many ess-im discontinuities, it consists of finitely many rarefaction waves, R-H jumps, and
constant states.
