Abstract
Large language models frequently generate
toxic, hateful, or harmful content, yet exist-
ing mitigation methods rely on costly retrain-
ing or output-level filtering with no mechanis-
tic insight into where toxicity originates inter-
nally. We introduce Meow2X and TRNE, two
complementary retraining-free frameworks that
localize toxicity to specific layers and neu-
rons by analyzing activation differentials be-
tween toxic and neutral prompts, then sup-
press them via inference-time scaling or min-
imal rank-one weight edits—without any gra-
dient descent. Evaluations across five LMs,
two benchmarks, and 90 configurations us-
ing dual safety evaluators demonstrate consis-
tent toxicity reduction while preserving lan-
guage modeling quality. Our analysis reveals
that toxicity is disproportionately encoded in
early MLP layers, varies across architectures,
and is systematically underestimated by single-
evaluator setups—underscoring the need for
multi-evaluator safety assessment. By bridg-
ing mechanistic interpretability with practical
detoxification, our framework offers a princi-
pled path toward safer, more transparent lan-
guage models. Code is available at this URL1.

methods also apply generic damping rather than
interventions tailored to the difference between
toxic and neutral inputs (Zhou et al., 2024; Villate-
Castillo et al., 2024; Pozzobon et al., 2024; Siu
et al., 2025). There is no simple, data-driven algo-

Motivation
Both methods share the insight that
only a small fraction of layers govern toxic behav-
ior (Lee et al., 2025). As shown in Figure 1, Meow2X
identifies these layers via mean activation differ-
ences between toxic and neutral inputs and sup-
presses them at inference time via scaling—leaving
weights unchanged and the intervention fully re-
versible (Lu et al., 2025; Shaik et al., 2025; Chen
et al., 2024). TRNE localizes the same layers via
contrastive gradient norms and commits a mini-
mal, norm-constrained rank-one weight edit that
encodes the toxic direction directly into the projec-
tion matrix—offering a persistent correction with-
out gradient descent. Together, they combine in-
terpretability (knowing where toxicity arises) with
effectiveness (measured by safety classifiers) (Ne-
plenbroek et al., 2024; Jain et al., 2024), grounded
in mechanistic interpretability findings that toxic
behaviors are mediated by localized neuron-level
circuits (Olah et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2022;
Geiger et al., 2021; Chen et al.).
Contributions
Our contributions are threefold:
• A principled toxic layer detection framework
using activation differentials (Meow2X) and
contrastive gradient norms (TRNE), revealing
where toxicity is encoded in language models.
• A controlled evaluation across 90 configura-
tions—five LLMs, two datasets, three com-
ponent scopes, three intervention strategies—
without any parameter updates for Meow2X and
with minimal rank-one edits for TRNE.
• A model-agnostic detoxification pipeline that
preserves language modeling capability via
reversible inference-time scaling (Meow2X) or
norm-constrained weight editing (TRNE).

Related Work
Detoxification of Language Models.
Prior work
on toxicity mitigation in language models has
largely focused on training-time interventions, in-
cluding supervised fine-tuning, preference opti-
mization, and reinforcement learning from human
feedback, as well as on output-level filtering and
rejection sampling (Ouyang et al., 2022; Beniwal
et al., 2025; Sap et al., 2019b). While effective at re-
ducing harmful generations, these approaches treat
toxicity as a surface-level behavior and provide lim-
ited insight into how such behaviors arise internally
(Dathathri et al., 2019). Moreover, they typically
require retraining or additional inference-time clas-
sifiers (Costa-jussà et al., 2024). In contrast, our
work operates directly on internal representations
and performs targeted inference-time interventions
without modifying model parameters.
Interpretability via Internal Representations.
Mechanistic interpretability research has shown
that neural behaviors can often be localized to spe-
cific layers, neurons, or substructures (Zhang and
Nanda, 2023). Methods such as Network Dissec-
tion (Bau et al., 2017) and representational simi-
larity analysis (Kornblith et al., 2019) demonstrate
that internal activations encode semantically mean-
ingful features and that behaviors can be traced
to localized components. These findings motivate
our approach, which explicitly compares mean ac-
tivations induced by toxic and neutral inputs to
identify layers and neurons that disproportionately

contribute to toxic behavior (Wang et al., 2024; Siu
et al., 2025).
Targeted and Causal Interventions.
Recent
studies have explored the causal manipulation of
internal components through neuron ablation, atten-
tion head suppression, or feature scaling, demon-
strating that selective intervention can reliably alter
model outputs (Li and Janson, 2024; Dai et al.,
2022; Yang et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024). How-
ever, many such approaches rely on manual selec-
tion or task-specific heuristics. Our method system-
atizes this process by using activation differentials
and contribution-based scoring to automatically
identify toxic layers and apply controlled scaling
through forward hooks, enabling reproducible and
architecture-agnostic intervention (Rai et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2024).
Evaluation and Positioning.
Unlike prior detox-
ification methods that rely on a single evaluator or
aggregate metrics, our framework employs multi-
ple independent safety classifiers and jointly mea-
sures toxicity reduction and language modeling
quality (Yang et al., 2025a; Dutta, 2024). Over-
all, our work bridges safety and interpretability
by framing toxicity as an internal representational
phenomenon (Suau et al., 2024). Rather than only
suppressing harmful outputs, we focus on revealing
where toxicity is encoded within the model, pro-
viding both practical mitigation and mechanistic
insight into models.
Positioning Against Baselines.
We position our
work relative to the closest prior methods: WHIS-
PERING EXPERTS (Suau et al., 2024) suppresses
toxic neurons but requires task-specific expert
identification and does not generalize across ar-
chitectures without per-model re-identification.
SAFEEDIT (Wang et al., 2024) applies knowl-
edge editing via gradient-based updates but re-
quires backpropagation at inference time. DEX-
PERTS (Liu et al., 2021) and PPLM (Dathathri
et al., 2020) are output-level steering methods that
cannot localize toxicity to specific model compo-
nents. Unlike all of the above, Meow2X operates
without any parameter updates, backpropagation,
or expert model, making it strictly lighter at in-
ference time. A full quantitative comparison is
left to future work as it requires standardizing gen-
eration hyperparameters, decoding strategies, and
evaluation prompts across codebases—a non-trivial
engineering challenge acknowledged by prior sur-
veys (Villate-Castillo et al., 2024).

Toxic Layer Detection
We identify toxic layers by analyzing systematic
differences in internal activations elicited by toxic
and neutral inputs. Given a pretrained language

Mitigation Strategies.
Following toxic layer
identification, we intervene directly at inference
time by modifying intermediate activations of se-
lected model components. Let L denote the set
of detected toxic layers. For each layer ℓ∈L,
we register forward hooks that rescale the output
activations of either the attention block, the MLP
block, or both, depending on the experimental set-
ting. These interventions are lightweight, do not
alter model parameters, and can be removed after
generation.
3.2.1
Deactivation
The deactivation strategy applies uniform suppres-
sion to all selected layers. For each ℓ∈L, the
layer output is multiplied by a constant scaling
factor α = 1 −s, where s ∈(0, 1] is the deacti-
vation strength. This strategy aggressively attenu-
ates toxic representations by uniformly reducing
their contribution to downstream layers. To avoid
destabilizing the forward pass, the scaling factor
is clipped to a minimum value αmin, ensuring that
information flow is preserved.
3.2.2
Dampening
The dampening strategy performs moderate sup-
pression by applying a reduced scaling factor rel-
ative to deactivation. Instead of fully applying
the specified strength, activations are scaled by
α = 1 −s
2, resulting in a smoother attenuation
of toxic signals. This strategy is particularly ef-
fective when toxicity is distributed across multiple
layers and excessive suppression may harm fluency
or coherence.
3.2.3

The adaptive strategy introduces contribution-
aware suppression by conditioning the scaling fac-
tor on each layer’s estimated toxicity contribution.
For a layer ℓ, the scaling factor is computed as

All mitigation strate-

original model for subsequent evaluations. The ap-
proach is fully model-agnostic, supports attention-
only, MLP-only, or combined interventions, and
integrates seamlessly with both top-k and dynamic
threshold–based layer selection.

5.4
Metrics
The effectiveness of detoxification is measured by
comparing aggregate toxicity rates before and af-
ter mitigation. Let Ubefore and Uafter denote the
fraction of unsafe generations; toxicity reduction
is defined as ∆U = Ubefore −Uafter. Model util-

(RQ1) Can toxicity be localized to specific layers
and neurons using activation differences?

Qwen-2.5 across layers, components, and neu-

heatmaps (Figure 2) show that toxicity-related acti-
vation differences are sparse and structured rather
than uniformly distributed (We observed different

for more details), with particularly strong signals
concentrated in the initial MLP layers (layers 1–
5).
This pattern is further corroborated by the
layer-wise toxicity scores and contribution plots
(Figure 3), where MLP components consistently
exhibit higher toxicity scores S = ∥∆∥1 than atten-
tion across both early and late layers, while atten-

tion contributions remain comparatively stable and
lower in magnitude. The box plots and scatter anal-
yses reveal a clear separation between MLP and
attention components, indicating that toxicity is dis-

mations. Finally, the neuron-level grids (Figure 4)
demonstrate that within high-scoring MLP layers,

Finding: Localizing toxicity is difficult — it varies

TRNE
Our experiments in Figures 17, 18, and 19
reveal that toxicity is not confined to a single com-
ponent type. In TinyLlama, both attention layers
[1, 2, 3, 4, 6] and MLP layers [0, 1, 2, 3, 5] exhibit
elevated contrastive scores, with toxicity concen-
trated in early layers. In Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct in
Figures 11, 12, and 13, toxicity shifts to late layers,
with attention [22, 21, 20, 23, 19] and MLP [21, 20,
22, 19, 23] showing similar distributions. This co-
localization suggests that toxic circuits span both
component types at consistent depths within each
architecture. Notably, MLP editing consistently
reduces toxicity (PolyGuard: −6.4pp for TinyL-
lama, −5.6pp for Llama-3.2-3B), while attention
editing alone sometimes increases detected toxicity
(+4.6pp and +3.6pp respectively). This asymme-
try indicates that MLP layers encode more direct
toxic associations, whereas attention layers may
contribute to contextual processing that, when dis-
rupted, triggers refusal patterns classified as toxic
by safety evaluators.
Finding: Toxicity is distributed across both atten-
tion and MLP components at similar layer depths,
not isolated to a single pathway.
(RQ2) Is layer deactivation an effective measure
to mitigate toxicity?
The experimental results using TRNE (Table 1)
across five models (Gemma, Llama3_2, Phi-2,
Qwen-2, Tiny-llama) and three edit components
(Attention, MLP, Combined) reveal clear patterns
in the effectiveness of different (Layers, Edit
Strength) configurations. From the comprehensive
data, configuration (5,2) demonstrates the most reli-
able toxicity reduction with minimal fluency degra-
dation—Gemma MLP achieves complete LG elim-
ination (0.40%→0.00%) and 3.4% PG reduction
while PPL increases by merely 0.74 points. The
(5,5) configuration proves effective for Llama3_2,
achieving the highest single-model PG improve-
ment (37.80%→32.20%) at this moderate setting,
though Phi-2 and Gemma exhibit PPL sensitivity.
Configuration (10,2) yields the most substantial PG
reduction overall, with Tiny-llama MLP dropping
from 44.60% to 29.80% (14.8 percentage points)
while maintaining acceptable PPL (+1.86). The
(10,5) pair shows inconsistent results—Qwen-2
achieves decent toxicity reduction but PPL nearly
doubles, indicating diminishing returns at higher
layer counts. Most critically, the aggressive (10,20)
configuration causes catastrophic model degrada-
tion across all architectures, with PPL values ex-
ploding to thousands (Llama3_2: 9297, Phi-2:
40060), rendering outputs incoherent despite ap-
parent toxicity metric changes. These findings sug-
gest that conservative edit strengths (2-5) targeting
fewer layers preserve model utility while achieving
meaningful safety improvements.
Finding: Toxicity is dispersed across components,
making fine-tuning-free removal inherently chal-
lenging.
(RQ3) Can model-agnostic, retraining-free
interventions mitigate toxicity while preserving
language quality?
Table 2 shows that inference-time, retraining-free
interventions consistently reduce toxicity across
models and datasets while largely preserving lan-
guage quality, as evidenced by small and stable
changes in perplexity. Despite variation across ar-
chitectures and components, toxicity mitigation is
achieved without parameter updates, indicating that
activation-level scaling is an effective and model-
agnostic strategy. Configurations employing full
deactivation on ParaDetox with Llama-3.2-3B ex-
hibit PPL spikes (12.96→135.37), consistent with
the observation that deactivation of structurally crit-
ical layers disrupts coherent generation; dampen-
ing and adaptive scaling reliably avoid this failure
mode (PPL changes of <10 across all other config-
urations).
Finding: Inference-time interventions reduce toxic-
ity with minimal impact on language quality.

LlamaGuard and PolyGuard produce dramatically
different toxicity assessments on identical outputs.

for toxicity research. Reporting results from a sin-

appears successful under one evaluator may fail
under another. We recommend using multiple eval-

Conclusion
This work demonstrates that toxic outputs in lan-
guage models can be effectively mitigated by de-
tecting and modifying a small set of specialized
neural pathways. Both deactivation and dampening
achieve mean toxicity reduction of 25% across di-
verse model scales. We find that larger models are
more amenable to targeted intervention, suggest-
ing that toxic knowledge is localized rather than
distributed. Selecting the top-5 toxic paths by acti-
vation ratio proves sufficient for substantial toxicity
reduction without comprehensive model retraining.
Future work should explore architecture-specific
optimization strategies and measure downstream
impacts on model helpfulness and factuality. This
approach offers a practical path toward safer lan-
guage models through interpretable neural interven-
tions. These findings highlight that effective detox-
ification requires component-aware editing, con-
servative hyperparameters, and evaluation across
multiple safety benchmarks.

Limitations
Our method operates in a strictly inference-only,
parameter-frozen setting, and therefore does not
capture toxicity mitigation achievable through fine-
tuning, LoRA, or RLHF. Toxicity localization is
derived from mean activation differences over a
fixed subset of prompts, which may underrepresent
rare, long-range, or highly contextual toxic behav-
iors. Although our experiments focus on decoder-
only models, the core methodology—activation
collection, component-wise scoring, and inference-
time scaling—is architecture-agnostic in principle.
Adapting the framework to other architectures (e.g.,
encoder–decoder models or mixture-of-experts)
would require identifying corresponding attention
and feed-forward modules and appropriately plac-
ing forward hooks, without altering the underlying
detection or intervention logic. Finally, our evalua-
tion relies on automated safety classifiers, such as
PolyGuard and LLaMA-Guard, which may intro-
duce bias or misclassification; we do not include
human judgments.
Ethics
This work focuses on analyzing and mitigating
toxic behavior in language models using inference-
time, non-invasive interventions that do not require
retraining or the deployment of new models. All
experiments are conducted on publicly available
datasets containing potentially harmful language,
which are used solely for research purposes and
never redistributed. While the proposed method
aims to reduce harmful outputs, automated toxicity
evaluators may reflect existing biases; therefore,
our results should not be interpreted as a complete
guarantee of safety. We emphasize that this work
is intended to support safer model behavior and
informed deployment, not to enable misuse or cen-
sorship. We acknolwedge the use of AI assistants
to help rewrite the content to improve readability
and writing.
