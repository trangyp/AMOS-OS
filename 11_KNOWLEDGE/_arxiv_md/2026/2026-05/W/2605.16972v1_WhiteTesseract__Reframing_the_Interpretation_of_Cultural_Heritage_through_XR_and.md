Cultural heritage exhibitions often struggle to sustain attention and support reflective engagement. While physical exhibitions
incorporate various interpretive aids, such as labels, audio guides, and tactile features, they often deliver fixed content that lacks
adaptability to individual backgrounds or curiosity. Their effectiveness is highly dependent on a visitor’s Personal Context, prior
knowledge, cultural literacy, and interpretive confidence. Meanwhile, digital exhibitions often prioritize convenience and accessibility
but risk weakening the Physical and Social Contexts that define embodied cultural experience.

distractions (via diminished reality) and engage in context-aware dialogue (via large language models). The goal is to preserve the
richness of the physical and social environment while providing a flexible space for personal reflection, enhancing Personal Context
without compromising physical authenticity.

INTRODUCTION
Over the past decade, digital technologies have significantly reshaped how cultural institutions present and interpret
cultural heritage materials. While virtual exhibitions and immersive environments have enabled access beyond physical
constraints, studies continue to emphasize the unique perceptual, spatial, and social affordances of physical exhibition
spaces in shaping visitor experiences [36, 62]. Among these, the “White Cube” gallery, a neutral, distraction-minimizing
spatial model, has remained a dominant format for encouraging focused and contemplative engagement with art [68],
especially in the presentation of visual art as cultural heritage. However, its strengths in visual isolation can also limit
multimodal interaction and individualized interpretive depth [18].
With the emergence of high-resolution extended reality (XR) devices [3, 12, 26], a new question arises: how
might such technologies augment, rather than substitute, the real-world cultural exhibition experience?
While physical exhibitions offer authenticity and embodied presence, they also present limitations. Visitors must
navigate visually dense environments, time constraints, and limited contextual information [16, 19], factors that can
shorten viewing durations and constrain interpretation to surface-level observations. In minimalist gallery formats
such as the White Cube, the reduction of visual noise supports focused looking yet often lacks mechanisms to adapt to
individual curiosity or facilitate a deeper understanding of the artwork’s context, meaning, or technique [15].
These gaps suggest two dimensions where technological augmentation may be useful: attentional focus, referring to
a visitor’s ability to control what is perceived within a complex sensory field; and cognitive engagement, referring to
the depth of thought, questioning, and interpretation that emerges during the viewing process. While most exhibition
environments offer limited support for either XR or AI technologies, they can be designed to intervene at both perceptual
and interpretive levels. In our system, the attentional focus is supported through diminished reality (DR) techniques
that allow users to reduce visual distractions by selectively masking surrounding elements and auditory distractions
through spatial noise cancellation [23, 64]. These combined interventions aim to create a more isolated perceptual
space that encourages sustained attention and concentration. Rather than adding visual or auditory load, the system
acts to simplify the sensory environment, helping visitors dwell longer and think more deeply, without disrupting the
aesthetic or curatorial integrity of the exhibition.
In this work, we present WhiteTesseract, a system that builds upon the White Cube paradigm by introducing
programmable spatial and cognitive extensions via XR and AI technologies. The system integrates two key features: (1)
a diminished reality interface that allows visitors to dynamically reduce both visual and auditory distractions around
individual artworks, creating what we term an adjustable attention space and (2) a language model-based dialogue

To make the study interpretable across readers, we implemented WhiteTesseract in a well-known Impressionist

practice, we developed and deployed WhiteTesseract in a controlled gallery setting using Apple Vision Pro and a curated
set of Claude Monet reproductions. We conducted a mixed-methods user study with 26 participants who experienced
both traditional White Cube and WhiteTesseract-based visits. Our data include motion tracking, questionnaire responses,
system interaction logs, and semi-structured interviews.
The system integrates two complementary mechanisms, an XR-based attention design that modulates visual focus
and an AI-powered dialogue that supports reflective interpretation. Rather than isolating these factors, the present
study examines their combined influence on visitors’ behavioral and interpretive engagement. Specifically, we ask:
• RQ1: How does WhiteTesseract system influence visitors’ viewing behavior, particularly in terms of duration
and movement patterns, compared to traditional exhibition formats?
• RQ2: How does the integration of AI-powered dialogue affect visitors’ interpretive engagement with artworks,
including the types of questions asked and the perceived depth of interaction?
Rather than proposing a radical transformation of gallery spaces, we aim to explore how emerging technologies
might enhance key dimensions of the physical cultural heritage experience—namely, attentional focus and interpretive
depth—without undermining its curatorial intent. This study contributes to ongoing discussions in HCI and museum
interaction design on the role of XR and AI in shaping future exhibition practices grounded in both technological
capability and experiential sensitivity.

RELATED WORK
In physical cultural heritage exhibitions, the viewer’s experience is shaped by the artworks themselves, while by spatial,
social, and personal contexts that influence perception and meaning-making [31]. Although minimalist gallery formats,
most notably the White Cube, aim to reduce distractions and promote contemplative viewing, they offer limited tools
for adapting to individual differences in perceptual focus or interpretive needs [68, 69]. At the same time, as digital
technologies become increasingly embedded in exhibition settings, new opportunities and challenges arise: how can
such tools enhance, rather than disrupt, the viewer’s ability to focus, reflect, and engage [90]?
Prior studies have explored various technologies designed to enhance the visitor experience, including augmented
reality overlays, immersive installations, and AI-powered guides [29, 73, 83]. However, these systems often address
either perceptual or cognitive layers in isolation [27]. Some prioritize immersion or information access, while others
seek to scaffold interpretation through text or audio, but without coordinating these functions within the real-time,
real-space dynamics of viewing. To better support sustained engagement with artworks, we argue for a combined
perspective: one that considers both attentional regulation (e.g., managing sensory input in complex settings) and
cognitive support (e.g., enabling personal meaning-making through dialogue or adaptive guidance).

In what follows, we examine three strands of related work that inform our system design: (1) prior applications of

2.1
XR and AI Technologies in Exhibition Contexts
XR technologies have been widely adopted in cultural institutions as tools for enhancing presence, accessibility, and
immersion [5] Foundational studies in XR emphasize the importance of presence, or the sense of “being there,” and
embodiment, which relates to how sensorimotor alignment affects engagement and memory [14, 79]. In museum settings,
AR and MR have been used to reconstruct historical scenes, embed digital overlays, or provide layered interpretation
through mobile guides [29, 81].
Yet, such implementations often emphasize content augmentation without adequately addressing perceptual load or
attentional coherence [61]. Research on DR has shown how selectively removing visual elements can reduce cognitive
interference and enhance focus [53]. Similarly, recent work on passthrough AR highlights how spatially integrated
digital content, when precisely aligned with real-world objects, can support seamless transitions between physical and
digital modes. These studies suggest that XR’s value lies not just in immersion but in the ability to control sensory
input—including soundscapes, which are often overlooked [48, 76]. For example, XR systems that dynamically manage
ambient sound via spatial audio or noise cancellation can improve task performance and subjective focus.
In parallel, museums have begun experimenting with AI systems to support curatorial tasks and enhance visitor
engagement [20, 38]. Applications range from chatbot-based museum guides to robotic tour companions to personalized
recommendation systems [44]. More recently, large language models (LLMs) have enabled flexible, open-ended interac-
tions [65]. For instance, the Smithsonian’s Open Access project integrates GPT-based models for artifact storytelling
[11], and Europe’s digital cultural library, museum, and archive has used conversational agents for archival document
exploration [60]. However, concerns persist about factual accuracy, hallucination risks in historical contexts, and ethical
alignment—especially when LLMs are used in educational or heritage settings [6, 37].
Unlike many prior systems that treat XR and AI as separate modalities, visual and textual, respectively, our work
explores their coordination for regulating sensory input and interpretive depth [13]. WhiteTesseract combines diminished
reality with spatial audio and an LLM-based companion that responds to recognized artworks in spatial context,
contributing to a hybrid attentional–cognitive environment.
2.2
Attention, Space, and the Legacy of the White Cube
The “White Cube” has shaped modern exhibition design for over a century, promoting an environment of sensory
minimalism and visual isolation [68]. As Brian O’Doherty articulated in his seminal critique, the White Cube enforces a
purified aesthetic that positions the artwork as autonomous, timeless, and abstracted from social and political contexts.
Subsequent critiques have examined this model through broader lenses. Boris Groys argues that the White Cube acts
as a frame for power and institutional neutrality [42]. At the same time, postcolonial perspectives critique its role in
reproducing Western epistemologies and excluding diverse forms of engagement [10, 58, 72].
From a perceptual perspective, the White Cube is often interpreted as a form of attention design—structuring the
viewer’s sensory field to reduce distractions and foster contemplation [9, 95]. However, cognitive psychology suggests
that selective attention is rarely stable or context-free. Classic models of attention demonstrate that perception is shaped

by salience, prior knowledge, and task framing [70, 84]. In museum contexts, researchers have found that divided

Moreover, recent work in embodied cognition suggests that understanding heritage objects is not purely visual or

Informed by these traditions, our work treats the White Cube as an architectural reference and a design constraint
and opportunity. WhiteTesseract extends this model into a programmable space of attentional modulation, allowing
visitors to suppress distractions while preserving the contemplative integrity of minimalist exhibition design [41, 86]. It
also invites inquiry into how spatial structures can be made more responsive to diverse cognitive styles and interaction
rhythms.
2.3
Conversational AI and Dialogic Engagement with Heritage Objects
Curatorial texts, guided tours, and audio commentaries often scaffold interpretive engagement in museums [35, 46].
However, each method presents distinct limitations. Curatorial texts offer useful yet fixed contextual information without
dynamically responding to individual visitors’ interests. Guided tours enable interactive flexibility, but personalized
interactions are practically constrained by factors such as the high cost of hiring private guides and limitations in
guides’ specific expertise. Audio commentaries, although widely accessible, typically follow predetermined sequences
and do not support spontaneous questioning or dialogue, limiting adaptability to visitors’ unique interpretive needs.
In contrast, dialogue-based learning has been shown to improve comprehension, retention, and reflective thinking
in educational psychology [24, 74, 87]. In cultural settings, research indicates that even rhetorical questioning can
encourage visitors to spend more time, formulate hypotheses, and form emotional connections with artworks [54].
The advent of conversational AI opens new opportunities for LLMs in art education platforms, enabling open-ended
interactions about techniques, styles, and historical movements [63]. For example, "ArtEMmis" uses vision-language
models to generate emotionally resonant descriptions of artworks [1]. At the same time, multimodal systems like
"PaLI" combine image input with textual output for captioning and answering questions [22]. These models enable
personalized and context-aware interpretation [49, 78], although challenges remain in ensuring factual grounding,
inclusivity, and explainability.
In museum contexts, however, the use of conversational AI is still in its early stages of development. Studies have
shown that visitors appreciate the ability to ask spontaneous questions but also feel frustration when responses are
generic, repetitive, or disconnected from the immediate context [45]. Our approach builds on this body of work by
integrating LLM-driven dialogue into a situated exhibition scenario, where the agent’s prompts are anchored to the
viewer’s gaze and the artwork being examined. Rather than simply answering questions, the system aims to act
as an interpretive scaffold or advance organizer [4], encouraging visitors to reflect, compare, and articulate their
interpretations through short conversational turns. This approach contributes to ongoing efforts in cultural heritage
computing by demonstrating how conversational AI can support personalized, situated interpretation without replacing
the embodied authenticity of physical exhibitions.

3
WHITETESSERACT: EXTENDING WHITE CUBE INTO EXTENDED REALITY
3.1
Conceptual Motivation: Beyond the Traditional White Cube
The WhiteTesseract concept emerged from a critical examination of how high-resolution XR technology could mean-
ingfully extend, rather than replace, the established principles of the White Cube gallery space. The White Cube, as
a curatorial paradigm, creates a visually neutral environment intended to foster focused contemplation. Yet, it offers
limited adaptability to individual viewers’ perceptual needs or interpretive preferences. WhiteTesseract reinterprets
this model by introducing dynamic, responsive digital elements that augment the physical exhibition space. Rather
than treating technology as an add-on, we explore how XR and AI can be integrated to reshape attention, perception,
and meaning-making within the gallery experience.
3.2
System Design: Coordinating Perception and Dialogue
WhiteTesseract integrates two interdependent design layers to support visitor engagement with artworks in real-world
gallery settings: one centered on attentional focus and the other on cognitive engagement. The attentional layer enables
visitors to dynamically reduce sensory distractions, while the cognitive layer facilitates lightweight, situated dialogue.
These layers are not isolated modules but are synchronized through spatial awareness and user control, creating a
flexible perceptual and interpretive environment. Figure 2 outlines the system’s conceptual framework, highlighting
how these components interact to form an adaptive exhibition experience.
To illustrate how these two components unfold in practice, Figure 3 presents a representative user scenario from
both first-person and third-person perspectives. The interaction proceeds through three coordinated phases: target
identification, perceptual transition, and dialogue initiation. In the first phase, the user naturally focuses on a
painting of interest in the physical gallery. The system detects this target using computer vision-based recognition,
which relies on subtle digital labels embedded in the scene. This recognition occurs passively and does not require
explicit user confirmation, allowing the gallery to remain visually minimal and uninterrupted. Once the target is
identified, the user activates the immersive mode using the headset’s Digital Crown, which gradually overlays a visual
mask that blends with the gallery’s background colors. This masking is coupled with spatial audio suppression via in-ear
filtering, reducing ambient distractions. Rather than instantly shifting the environment, these perceptual adjustments
unfold gradually, enabling the user to focus on the selected painting while minimizing external interference.

After the perceptual field has been adjusted, the system enters a state of visual and auditory isolation around the
selected artwork. At this stage, the LLM initiates the interaction by offering a context-sensitive prompt—such as a
reflective question or interpretive cue, without requiring the user to issue a command. The user can respond directly via
speech, which the system passively captures and interprets in real time. To support accessibility and comprehension, all
dialogue is visually represented in a lightweight on-screen chat box that users can reposition or resize as needed. This
interface is intentionally unobtrusive and designed to respect the artwork’s centrality; it appears only when active
and remains under user control. While the system avoids intrusive overlays, the optional visual feedback offers a
subtitle-like experience for those who prefer textual confirmation, supporting diverse interaction preferences without
compromising visual focus.
This coordinated design transforms how sensory and semantic attention are managed in exhibition contexts. Instead
of guiding the user through fixed pathways or scripted content, WhiteTesseract enables a responsive, visitor-driven
rhythm of seeing and thinking, consistent with the contemplative intent of the White Cube while expanding its
expressive potential.
3.3
Reframing the Exhibition Experience
The integration of attentional and cognitive modulation in WhiteTesseract alters how visitors interact with individual
artworks, while how they structure time, movement, and reflection within the exhibition space. Traditional gallery
design typically assumes a linear and uniform, uniform visitor journey, in which perceptual pacing and interpretive
content are space and time. By contrast, WhiteTesseract enables an adaptive exhibition model in which visitors can
regulate their sensory environment and dialogic engagement in real-time.

• Decoupling spatial layout from cognitive sequencing: WhiteTesseract enables visitors to freely choose

• Reframing peripheral attention through environmental modulation: By enabling visitors to focus or

curatorial reach without redesigning space.
• Transforming silence and solitude into configurable conditions of engagement: Through real-time
control of visual and auditory input, visitors can choose when and how to concentrate, turning perceptual quiet
into an active interpretive resource—rather than a passive default.
Together, these contributions position WhiteTesseract not simply as a technical enhancement of the gallery space, but
as a reframing of how exhibitions function as cognitive and attentional systems.

4.1
XR-Reducing Distractions
To effectively reduce distractions, our system utilizes XR technology to adjust the visibility of surrounding elements
dynamically. Utilizing spatial anchors and image recognition, the WhiteTesseract application identifies and selectively
occludes visual clutter that is unrelated to the artwork being viewed. The Apple Vision Pro was selected due to its
high resolution (3660 × 3200 pixels per eye) and minimal image distortion. The selective passthrough functionality
is implemented through VisionOS Tracking, which utilizes preregistered images via the 3D space API1. This allows
a mask to be spatially anchored and overlaid on the volume view, excluding the recognized artwork, enabling users
to selectively block visual clutter by rotating the Digital Crown. We also linked the Digital Crown with the active
noise cancellation earbuds (Inzone Buds WF-G700N) to reduce noise. When the user enters the "Immersive Space",
the earbuds automatically activate noise cancellation. These designs aim to provide an "isolated environment" that
enhances the user experience through a digital approach, blocking real-world interference.
4.2

developed the LLM conversation system combined with Apple Vision pro’s graphic interface with Google’s Cloud APIs.

LLM for providing more background knowledge of the artworks, and this didn’t require a complex reasoning function,
the API we used here is the "generate content" API that doesn’t store any history data. The user’s voice recorded by the

We designed the conversation system into a form of alternating questions and answers: The voice detection algorithm
will send the STT result combined with the prompt to Gemini 1.5 Flash if there is a 3 seconds silence after the user’s
speaking. After each triggered input, the LLM (Gemini 1.5 Flash) used its default generation parameters, which provided
a balanced level of response variability suitable for conversational tasks. Then the voice synthesis program will use the
TTS API to generate audio after the LLM has finished its response, which may have a delay of around 2 to 5 seconds,
according to the API’s condition. Through repeated testing, we ensured that the total response delay can be controlled
under 8 seconds. During the voice responding period, the speaking detection algorithm will keep pending. The user
cannot interrupt this process while the LLM is "speaking". But there may be situations where APIs don’t respond
within 8 seconds or just return error messages. In such cases, the program will immediately exit the current pending
conversation and start a new one. The user is notified of the current conversation state by the visual hint from Apple
Vision Pro. When the LLM API works normally, the response text is displayed in the conversation GUI; otherwise, the
interface indicates that the conversation is available.

5.2
Study Procedure
The study followed a within-subjects design comparing traditional and WhiteTesseract conditions. In the XR condition,
both the diminished reality feature and the conversational AI were active, allowing us to evaluate their combined
influence on art-viewing behavior. Each session lasted approximately 75 to 100 minutes, beginning with an orientation
and informed consent, followed by a pre-experience survey to gather demographic data and initial impressions.
Participants then engaged in two distinct viewing experiences: one using traditional gallery methods and the other
employing the WhiteTesseract XR system. In the traditional viewing condition, participants experienced the same 5
× 4.5 m layout and artwork sequence as in the WhiteTesseract system condition, but without any headset or audio
devices. Ten Monet reproductions were printed at identical sizes and mounted at eye level under uniform gallery
lighting. Participants received a brief written instruction, “Please view freely as in a normal exhibition,” and could move
naturally without time limits. Only physical wall labels were available (title, date, medium); no conversational AI or
audio suppression was provided. Following these viewing experiences, participants completed a post-experience survey

analysis. Based on cross-referencing with verified sources, every mark was classified into one of three categories:

represented figures or objects.

DISCUSSION
Our analysis integrates quantitative behavioral data with qualitative insights from semi-structured interviews conducted
with all 26 participants immediately following their experimental sessions. These interviews, lasting 15-20 minutes each,
employed open-ended questions to elicit participants’ subjective experiences, interpretive processes, and reflections on
both the perceptual modulation and AI dialogue components of WhiteTesseract. The interview protocol progressed from
general impressions to specific probes about attention management, question formulation strategies, and perceived
limitations of the system. This mixed-methods approach enabled us to move beyond surface-level behavioral metrics
to understand the cognitive and affective mechanisms underlying the observed changes in viewing patterns and
interpretive engagement. By triangulating motion tracking data, conversation logs, and participant narratives, we can
examine what changes occurred in museum viewing behavior, why participants adopted particular strategies, and how
they made sense of the novel exhibitionary format. The following sections synthesize these multiple data streams to
address our research questions concerning the WhiteTesseract system’s overall behavioral impact (RQ1) and the role of
AI-facilitated dialogue in shaping interpretive engagement (RQ2).
7.1
RQ1: Impact of WhiteTesseract System on Viewing Behavior
7.1.1
Extended Viewing Duration through Perceptual Modulation. The observed behavioral differences reflect the overall
influence of the WhiteTesseract experience, where visual attention modulation and AI dialogue jointly shape visitors’
engagement dynamics. Our findings demonstrate that WhiteTesseract design fundamentally transformed participants’
viewing behavior, most notably through a dramatic extension of viewing duration. The quantitative data revealed that
mean viewing time increased from 35.3 seconds in the conventional condition to 98.3 seconds with WhiteTesseract
across all artworks, representing a 175% increase. This effect was consistent across individual artworks, with some
areas showing even more pronounced differences, Area 1, for instance, increased from 33.72s to 113.28s (p < 0.001).
This substantial increase in viewing duration can be attributed to the system’s ability to create what participants
repeatedly described as a "personal attention bubble." Through visual masking and spatial sound isolation, visitors
experienced reduced environmental distractions, while conversational prompts encouraged sustained interpretive
reflection. As P3 noted, "Everything else turned white, so I felt gently compelled to concentrate on just one piece."


## Page 24


24
Li et al.
Several participants (P1, P15, P17, P18) also mentioned that being able to “talk” with the system deepened their focus by
giving them reasons to linger and think, turning passive looking into active interpretation.
The mechanism underlying this extended engagement aligns with established theories of attention design in HCI
and cognitive psychology. By reducing external cognitive load through environmental modulation, the system enabled
what Kahneman would term "effortless attention", a state in which cognitive resources are not depleted by filtering
distractions but can be fully devoted to interpretive processing [47, 94]. These findings extend prior results showing that
when perceptual and conversational elements co-exist, visitors sustain both perceptual focus and reflective reasoning
longer than in static environments [18].
7.1.2
Changes in Movement Patterns and Exhibition Navigation. Beyond temporal extension, WhiteTesseract altered the
spatial dynamics of exhibition navigation. The motion tracking data revealed that participants adopted fundamentally
different movement patterns when using the system. In conventional viewing, participants tended to follow a relatively
linear path through the gallery, spending comparable time at each artwork (ranging from 29.44s to 43.84s). With
WhiteTesseract, however, viewing became more selective and deliberate, with some artworks receiving substantially
more attention than others (ranging from 75.92s to 113.28s).
This shift from uniform to selective engagement suggests that the system allowed visitors to construct personalized
pathways through the exhibition based on their individual interests and curiosity. Several participants explicitly noted
this change in their interview responses. P23 described it as "choosing the pace and the depth—it felt more like my own
exhibition." The ability to isolate individual artworks perceptually appeared to liberate visitors from the implicit social
pressure to maintain steady progression through the gallery, allowing them to dwell with works that resonated more
deeply [34].
However, this personalization came with trade-offs. Some participants noted that the immersive mode made it
challenging to maintain an overall awareness of the exhibition. P10 and P11 mentioned that "in the immersive mode, it
would be difficult to see multiple artworks simultaneously," suggesting that while WhiteTesseract enhanced focused
engagement with individual works, it potentially fragmented the holistic experience of the exhibition as a curated
ensemble. This tension between depth and breadth represents a fundamental design challenge for future adaptive
exhibition systems.
7.2
RQ2: AI-Dialogue’s Effect on Interpretive Engagement
7.2.1
Diversification of Question Types and Interpretive Autonomy. While participants appreciated the dialogic nature of
the interaction, our findings reveal a complex relationship between perceived depth and actual interpretive substance.
Analysis of the 529 total questions revealed a diverse distribution that markedly differs from the information typically
provided by traditional museum labels. Where conventional wall texts primarily offer descriptive information (artwork
title, date, medium, dimensions) and basic contextual details (historical period, artistic movement), our participants’
questions showed a much broader interpretive scope [55]: Descriptive Information (40.8%), Analytical Understanding
(19.61%), Emotional and Personal Engagement (9.84%), Comparative and Contextual Inquiry (12.69%), Technical and
Functional Questions (6.7%), and Open-Ended Exploration (10.36%).
This distribution is particularly striking when we consider that traditional labels rarely address emotional responses,
personal connections, or comparative analysis across works [77]. The fact that nearly 60% of the questions went beyond
basic descriptive information—encompassing analytical, emotional, comparative, and exploratory dimensions-suggests
that the AI dialogue system enabled visitors to pursue interpretive paths that static labels cannot support. For instance,

questions like "Did the model of the child, his wife Camille, and son Johnny have to stand there the whole time while
painting?" or "Why was Monet fascinated by Japanese culture?" represent the kind of personal curiosity and cultural
inquiry that traditional labels, constrained by space and universal relevance, typically omit. Many participants reported
that this expanded scope encouraged them to think more deeply about the artworks—P5 noted that "AR made me focus
more on a single piece and think about its meaning," while P19 and P23 specifically mentioned that the LLM could
provide "deeper analysis of the questions." The ability to ask follow-up questions and receive immediate responses
created what P26 described as a more "interactive" experience that fundamentally transcended the one-way information
delivery of traditional interpretation.
The AI was widely perceived not as a teacher or expert but as a "gentle companion"—someone to talk to without fear
of making mistakes. This perception was deeply rooted in the system’s non-judgmental tone and anonymous interaction
model. As P1 noted, "It felt like I could ask anything without being embarrassed, even questions I would never ask
a human guide." Participants such as P4 and P11 emphasized the system’s ability to support dialogue "with anyone
at any time," reinforcing that conversational openness and emotional safety lowered the threshold for engagement.
This finding aligns with prior research on the value of non-evaluative social agents in educational contexts, where
agents designed as learning companions rather than authoritative tutors have been shown to increase willingness to
ask questions and foster continued engagement [50, 51].
7.2.2
Perceived Depth versus System Limitations. However, this comfort was not always paired with cognitive depth.
The word frequency analysis of LLM responses revealed repetitive use of terms like "light" and "color," suggesting
that while the system maintained conversational fluency, it often defaulted to generic artistic descriptors rather than
providing genuinely insightful analysis. Many participants found the answers to be repetitive, vague, or disconnected
from the artwork itself. As P5 reflected, "It responded, but not in a way that gave me more to think about." Others
expressed disappointment when the system failed to recognize the specificity of their questions, P13 and P14 noted
frustration when comparisons across artworks fell short, while P19 articulated, "I wanted the AI to go deeper... to
answer from multiple angles, not just repeat what’s on the label." In these cases, the AI’s role as a "companion" began to
feel superficial, warm in tone but lacking substance.
This gap between conversational presence and interpretive substance was not simply a matter of interaction design
but a limitation rooted in the LLM’s underlying capabilities [7]. Several participants (P5, P13, P14) noted that the AI
lacked the knowledge specificity needed to ground responses in historical, stylistic, or comparative art contexts. The
system struggled particularly with the 12.69% of questions seeking comparative and contextual inquiry, often providing
generic responses when participants sought nuanced connections between works or artistic movements. This reflects
a structural challenge in using general-purpose LLMs in domain-specific interpretive settings: rather than lacking
specialized art historical or curatorial knowledge, these models tend to be "lazy," often not actively retrieving relevant
details unless explicitly prompted. Such passivity limits their effectiveness in museum contexts, where divergent and
proactive exploration of information can significantly enhance visitors’ interpretive engagement with artworks.
Technical disruptions further exacerbated this tension between accessibility and depth. Participants P17 and P18
reported voice recognition issues, delays, or misinterpretations that interrupted the conversational flow and diminished
the system’s perceived intelligence. P3 and P13 specifically mentioned that "the reaction of the system was sometimes
slow and difficult to interrupt," while P7, P17, and P19 noted that "the system could not accurately understand questions
or give similar answers." These moments highlighted a critical fragility: trust in dialogic systems can quickly erode when
interactional continuity or semantic clarity breaks down [59]. The technical limitations were particularly problematic

for the 6.7% of questions that were technical or functional in nature, where users expected precise system responses but

Beyond stylistic repetition, a subtler limitation emerged in the factual grounding of the LLM’s responses. Our
post hoc verification (Section 6.1.3) revealed that, while most statements were accurate, occasional hallucinations
occurred—such as misattributing a painting’s location or interpreting artistic intent as factual detail. To minimize such
drift, each artwork was pre-labeled with its title and metadata before being sent to the LLM, effectively constraining
the model’s interpretive space and reducing spontaneous hallucinations common in open-ended dialogue. However,
this mechanism also created a trade-off between control and adaptability: once the input metadata was incorrect, as
in the case of The Seine at Lavacourt, mistakenly labeled as The Seine near Giverny, the entire chain of responses
became misdirected and the model could not self-correct. This design thus improved factual reliability under accurate
grounding but amplified systemic errors when contextual information was flawed, highlighting the need for future
iterations to integrate dynamic verification methods and domain-specific datasets capable of detecting and recovering
from such inconsistencies in real time.
Beyond system-level constraints related to factual grounding and error propagation, the current study also has
limitations at the level of evaluation scope. While an overall qualitative judgment by a domain expert (e.g., curators or
art historians) could provide valuable insight into interpretive quality, such assessments were beyond the scope of the
current study, which focuses on system feasibility and interaction patterns. We therefore adopt a conservative validation
strategy centered on factual correctness, treating it as a necessary but not sufficient condition for interpretive quality.
Future work will be required to incorporate expert-led qualitative evaluations to assess narrative appropriateness,
contextual sensitivity, and educational value in museum-specific settings.
Despite these limitations, the distribution of question types and participant feedback suggests that the AI dialogue
system succeeded in broadening the scope of visitor engagement beyond what traditional labels typically support. The
presence of emotional engagement questions (9.84%) and open-ended exploration (10.36%) indicates that participants
felt empowered to pursue personal and creative lines of inquiry. As P10 and P22 suggested in their vision for future
systems, there is potential for AI companions that can adapt their response depth and style based on visitor expertise
and interest, moving beyond current limitations to provide truly personalized interpretive experiences. The challenge for
future systems lies in balancing the approachability and non-judgmental interaction style that made WhiteTesseract’s
AI perceived as a "gentle companion," with the depth of knowledge and responsiveness that can sustain meaningful
intellectual engagement throughout the museum visit.

7.3.1

visitor interactions within exhibition contexts, particularly by integrating personal, spatial, and social dimensions

meaning in alignment with their prior knowledge, interests, and emotional states. It transforms the physical context
through diminished reality techniques that modulate spatial perception and visual salience, enabling deeper engagement
with individual artworks while minimizing environmental distractions. It also subtly reshapes the social context by
supporting private, low-pressure interactions—particularly valuable for visitors who may feel self-conscious or inhibited

The convergence of our findings across both research questions reveals fundamental insights about the future of
exhibition design in the era of high-resolution XR. WhiteTesseract demonstrates that the integration of perceptual

focused and more expansive than traditional formats—more focused in terms of sustained attention to individual works,

behavior is striking: mean viewing duration increased from 35.8 seconds to 98.6 seconds, with consistent effects across

visitors can control their sensory environment through diminished reality and engage in open-ended dialogue about
artworks, they transition from passive viewers following predetermined paths to active constructors of their own
exhibition experience [32]. As P23 articulated, it became "more like my own exhibition," suggesting that adaptive
technologies can fundamentally alter the power dynamics between visitors, artworks, and curatorial authority. This
shift toward personalization was echoed by P9, who noted that while current technology offers just ordinary viewing,
technological advances in the coming years will make these experiences truly transformative and wearable.

museum labels constrain interpretation to basic descriptive and contextual information, our participants’ 529 questions
spanned six distinct categories, with nearly 60% moving beyond factual information to explore analytical, emotional,
comparative, and speculative dimensions. This finding suggests that when given appropriate tools, visitors naturally
seek deeper, more personalized forms of meaning-making than static interpretation allows. The AI’s role as a "gentle
companion" lowered psychological barriers to inquiry, enabling visitors to ask questions that they might never voice in

connections. Similarly, AI interpretation could adapt dynamically, offering different depths of engagement ranging
from basic orientation for first-time visitors to scholarly discourse for experts.
7.3.2
Balancing Individual Agency and Collective Culture. The implications extend beyond individual system design
to institutional practices. Museums adopting such technologies must reconsider fundamental assumptions about
curatorial authority, visitor agency, and the social dynamics of gallery spaces [91]. If every visitor can create their own
perceptual bubble and engage in personalized dialogue, what becomes of the shared, collective experience that museums
traditionally foster? Several participants grappled with this question. P20 expressed desire to discover related works and
understand shared techniques across the exhibition space, suggesting that personalization should enhance rather than
replace curatorial connections. P17 similarly emphasized wanting easy navigation to find other works, indicating that
individual focus should not come at the cost of serendipitous discovery. Our findings suggest that the answer lies not in
choosing between individual and collective experience but in designing systems that support fluid transitions between
different modes of engagement. P7 captured this balance elegantly, noting that while pure digital experiences might
excel for educational purposes, mixed physical-digital exhibitions have broader public appeal. P13 articulated a vision
where visitors could move fluidly between analytical views (diagrams and technical details) and emotional engagement
with individual paintings and their natural contexts. WhiteTesseract points toward a future where visitors might begin
in social, exploratory mode, shift to isolated contemplation for works that particularly resonate, engage in AI-mediated
inquiry to deepen understanding, and then return to shared discussion enriched by their personalized exploration.
The social dimension of future exhibitions also emerged as a key consideration. P14 imagined exhibitions where
visitors could see virtual representations of other viewers and even engage in shared interpretation, while P21 envisioned
leaving digital traces or messages for future visitors. These suggestions point toward hybrid spaces that maintain
social presence while enabling individual customization. As P3 emphasized, the future lies not in a single model but in
supporting diverse choices and approaches to experiencing art.
Ultimately, WhiteTesseract demonstrates that high-resolution XR and AI can do more than augment traditional
exhibitions; they can reimagine the fundamental relationship between visitors and artworks. By giving visitors control

adaptive exhibition systems, the goal should not be to maximize individual customization at all costs but to create
flexible frameworks that honor both personal agency and collective culture. The future of exhibitions lies not in choosing
between physical and digital, individual and social, or focused and exploratory modes, but in creating adaptive systems
that allow visitors to move fluidly between these states based on their evolving needs and interests.
7.3.3
Practical Considerations for Real-World Deployment. While our controlled study demonstrates the system’s
conceptual effectiveness, its translation into real museum settings raises a number of operational and logistical
challenges. Each Apple Vision Pro costs approximately $3,500, making scalable deployment prohibitively expensive
for most institutions. Device management introduces operational challenges: our study required 5-10 minutes per
participant for calibration, which would create bottlenecks during peak hours. Battery life (2.5-3 hours) limits extended
visits, and hygiene protocols for shared headsets further reduce throughput. Beyond hardware, deployment also involves
software-related expenses, including maintaining the local server environment, ensuring compatibility with evolving
XR platforms, and refining dialogue behavior through iterative testing. As a result, large public museums may face
substantial financial and staffing barriers to adoption, whereas smaller galleries, university museums, and temporary
exhibitions could more feasibly pilot such systems under controlled conditions. Hybrid configurations, such as limited
“immersive viewing sessions” or optional booking-based experiences, may represent a practical middle ground between
innovation and scalability.
Furthermore, while the current system was implemented as a single-user experience, it still allows visitors to converse
and interact freely with their companions in the shared physical space. However, others cannot join or access the
same AI-mediated dialogue environment. Many museum visitors attend exhibitions in small groups, often with family
members, friends, or partners, and their interactions, including shared gaze, gesture, and conversation, play a crucial role
in meaning-making and collective engagement [25, 28, 33]. This design choice, therefore, prioritizes focused personal
reflection within the system but limits opportunities for shared interpretive exploration.
Our 5m × 4.5m controlled space with sequential sessions differs fundamentally from major institutions where
hundreds of visitors simultaneously navigate interconnected galleries. WhiteTesseract’s requirement for spatial tracking
and one-to-one device allocation makes it better suited for temporary exhibitions with timed entry (10-15 visitors per
session), small galleries, or private educational programs rather than large-scale public museums.
The most pragmatic path forward may position WhiteTesseract as optional enhancement rather than default
experience. Galleries could offer designated "immersive viewing sessions" at specific times, preserving collective
engagement while enabling deeper individual contemplation. As P7 noted, mixed physical-digital formats demonstrate
broader appeal than purely digital experiences. Future development should investigate lightweight AR glasses to reduce
cost and discomfort, asynchronous social features that maintain focus while enabling delayed interaction, and flexible
attention modes allowing rapid switching between isolated and social viewing. These implementation constraints
concern scalability and operational logistics, whereas broader methodological and contextual limitations are discussed
in the following section.

7.4
Limitations and Generalizability
Beyond these practical and logistical barriers, our controlled experimental design also introduced methodological
constraints that limit the generalizability of the findings. The gallery environment was deliberately simplified: consistent
lighting, minimal architecture, reproductions rather than originals, and absence of other visitors. Real museums present
complex sensory environments where natural light varies, architectural features shape movement, and the material
presence of authentic works generates engagement that reproductions cannot replicate [17]. Our finding that viewing
duration increased from 35.8s to 98.6s occurred within this simplified context. Although the study provides insights
into the integrated experience of WhiteTesseract, the current within-subjects design does not disentangle the specific
contributions of its visual and dialogic components. Future work will isolate these effects to further validate the attention
mechanism’s role. Moreover, because participants could freely adjust their level of perceptual isolation, minor variability
across sessions may have introduced uncontrolled differences in attentional focus. Future studies should employ a
standardized isolation setting to reduce potential confounding variables and strengthen the reliability of the findings.
The single-visitor protocol eliminated social dynamics that profoundly influence museum behavior. Participants
experienced the gallery alone, free from companions or crowding. Research shows that social cues, such as companion
presence, observations of others, and implicit pacing norms, significantly shape individual behavior [25, 28, 33]. The
extended viewing times may partly reflect absence of these regulatory factors rather than solely system effects. How
WhiteTesseract performs when visitors must negotiate shared space and companions’ interests remains unknown.
Our participant sample (N=27, ages 20-30, 92% engineering backgrounds, 100% prior XR experience) represents early
adopters rather than general museum audiences. This early adopter profile may have influenced multiple outcomes
reported in this study. First, participants with prior XR exposure may show lower initial cognitive load during onboarding
and greater tolerance to headset-related constraints, leading to longer voluntary engagement and extended dwell time
compared to first-time XR users [67]. Second, a technically literate population may be more inclined to probe system
behavior, ask “how/why” questions, and iterate on prompts, which could shift the observed distribution of question types
and increase the apparent diversity of dialogue [43]. Third, prior familiarity with interactive AI may shape participants’
expectations and evaluation criteria, potentially affecting subjective judgments of usefulness and interpretive support
[57]. We therefore interpret the observed effects primarily as evidence of feasibility and interaction patterns in an
early adopter setting; verifying the magnitude and form of these effects in heterogeneous museum audiences remains
an important next step. Older adults, children, and XR unfamiliar visitors may experience higher cognitive load,
longer adaptation, or different comfort levels [2, 92]. Additionally, our choice of familiar Monet reproductions may not
generalize to contemporary art, non-Western heritage, or less canonical works where baseline engagement differs. Some
observed mechanisms may nevertheless be less sensitive to technical expertise, for example, the benefit of a controlled
sensory environment for reducing external distractions, and the role of private dialogue in lowering social barriers to
asking questions in gallery. In contrast, measures closely tied to exploratory behavior (e.g., voluntary dialogue frequency,
prompt iteration, and the depth of analytical questioning) are more likely to vary with prior XR/AI familiarity [71]. We
therefore caution that the relative ranking of effects may generalize more readily than their absolute magnitudes.
We captured only short-term effects within 75-100 minute sessions, with no assessment of retention or long-term
impact. Whether WhiteTesseract’s effects persist beyond immediate experience or influence subsequent visits cannot
be determined. Technical constraints also shaped experience: the Vision Pro’s weight (600-650g) caused discomfort,
while visual quality issues—darker rendering, color distortion (P3, P10, P11, P13, P19, P20, P23), insufficient resolution
(P10, P26)—affected aesthetic appreciation.

Despite these limitations, our controlled approach provided baseline evidence that perceptual modulation and
conversational AI can enhance specific engagement dimensions: viewing duration, question diversity, and interpretive
autonomy. Future research should progressively introduce authentic artworks, multiple simultaneous visitors, natu-

magnitude of change in engagement-related measures may differ for XR-unfamiliar visitors. Similarly, our evalua-
tion prioritizes factual reliability over holistic interpretive quality, and should therefore be understood as a baseline

CONCLUSION
This work has introduced WhiteTesseract, a high-resolution XR exhibition system designed to reshape how visitors
engage with cultural heritage through adjustable sensory environments and conversational AI. Our study demonstrated
that subtle perceptual modulation can significantly increase viewing time and foster focused attention, offering an
alternative to spectacle-driven immersion in digital heritage applications. In parallel, our findings show that dialogic
interaction—despite current limitations in LLMs—supports more autonomous and personally meaningful interpretations
of visual heritage.
By integrating attentional design with interpretive flexibility, WhiteTesseract contributes to ongoing conversations
in HCI and cultural heritage computing about how emerging technologies can augment, rather than replace, situated
museum experiences. It presents a model for future systems that emphasize quiet adaptability, reflective engagement,
and co-construction of meaning. Rather than pursuing complete automation, we argue for systems that sensitively
mediate between technological possibilities and curatorial intentions, extending the interpretive potential of physical
exhibitions while respecting their material and historical specificity.

Semi-structured Interview Questions
To complement the quantitative findings, we conducted semi-structured interviews with all participants after their
interaction with the WhiteTesseract system. These interviews aimed to gain deeper insights into participants’ subjective
impressions, perceived impact of the XR features, and their preferences for future exhibition formats. Participants
were encouraged to reflect on their experiences, challenges, and design suggestions in their own words, allowing for
open-ended responses and the emergence of nuanced themes. The full set of interview prompts used in this study is
listed in Table 9.
