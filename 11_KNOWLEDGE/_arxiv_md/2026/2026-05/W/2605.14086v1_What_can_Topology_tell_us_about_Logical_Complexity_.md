What can Topology tell us about Logical Complexity?
Takayuki Kihara* and Ming Ng†
Nagoya University, Japan
kihara@i.nagoya-u.ac.jp
ngming.math@gmail.com
Abstract
In the 1980s, category theorists introduced the Lawvere-Tierney (≤LT) order in the Effective Topos, known to
effectively embed the Turing degrees. Understanding its structure is a longstanding open problem in the area. In
particular, there was an informal sense that the ≤LT-order reflects certain shifts in combinatorial complexity, but a
precise characterisation remained elusive for some time.
Recent work by the authors has substantially clarified the picture. In [KN26], the authors introduced a game-
theoretic (“gamified”) version of the Katˇetov order on filters over ω – essentially, this is the usual Katˇetov order
now closed under well-founded iterations of Fubini powers. The first major theorem of the paper was to show that
a computable variant of the gamified Katˇetov order is isomorphic to the original ≤LT-order. This was a surprising
discovery, and opens up many challenging questions regarding the interplay between combinatorial and computable
complexity, which informed the rest of the paper’s investigations.
This note gives an informal survey of some of these interactions explored in [KN26], and announces some forth-
coming results. The guiding perspective is that different notions of complexity arising in different areas of logic can
be seen to be controlled by the same mechanism – once placed in the right topological framework.
1
What is a generalised space?
Logicians are familiar with the key notion of a filter, either as a generalised point or as an abstract notion of largeness.
Topos theorists push this idea much further by asking: what is a generalised space?
Our main protagonist is the Lawvere-Tierney topology [hereafter: LT topology]. Informally, an LT topology gives
an abstract notion of localness. A space X might be said to satisfy some property P locally if X can be covered by a
family of opens {Ui}i∈I, each satisfying the property P. Abstracting this, one might require:
1. If “P implies Q”, then “locally-P implies locally-Q”
2. P implies locally-P
3. Locally-locally-P is equivalent to locally-P.
Such notions can be defined in any sufficiently well-behaved category (i.e. a topos), and admit several equivalent
formulations – see e.g. [MLM94, §V.1] or [Hyl82, §16]. Our focus is the Effective Topos Eff, a category-theoretic
framework for computability, where LT topologies take the following form.
Definition 1.1. An LT topology in Eff is an endomorphism
j : P(ω) ! P(ω)
subject to the following conditions:
1. ∀p, q ∈P(ω). (p ! q) ! (j(p) ! j(q))
2. ∀p ∈P(ω). (p ! j(p))
3. ∀p ∈P(ω). j ◦j(p) = j(p)

A key structural fact is that the Turing Degrees embed effectively into this ≤LT-order [Hyl82, §17], which indicates
that the ≤LT-order gives a measure of computable complexity. Returning to the informal picture we gave before, one
may ask: if LT topologies correspond to generalised spaces, what then are its (generalised) points? Moreover, how do
they interact with the ≤LT-order?
The situation is well-understood whenever the topos E is of topological origin, such as E = Sh(R), the category of
sheaves on R. In which case, the global points of Sh(R) correspond to completely prime filters in the lattice of open
sets in R, and the LT topologies in Sh(R) correspond to subspaces of R – see [Vic07, §2 and §7.2]. By contrast, in
more abstract settings such as E = Eff, the situation is much less well understood. In fact, progress on understanding
the ≤LT-order in Eff has been relatively slow. Early work by Hyland and Pitts [Hyl82, Pit81] established the existence
of a minimum and maximum class on the non-trivial LT topologies. Attention therefore turned to investigating what
lay in between the two extreme cases, a longstanding open problem in the area:

The crux move of our paper [KN26] was to regard the ≤LT-order as inducing a pre-order on subset families
A ⊆P(ω). In which case, item (ii) of the Summary Theorem has the following elegant translation: A belongs to
the ≡LT-minimum class iff T A ̸= ∅. This restatement will be suggestive to the set theorist because of the following
well-known fact:
Fact 2.2 (see e.g. [Gol22, Exercise 1.6.3]). The Rudin-Keisler order (≤RK) defines a preorder on ultrafilters over ω.
In particular, there exists a minimum class, where F ⊆P(ω) belongs to the ≡RK-minimum class iff T F ̸= ∅.
As it turns out, the Rudin-Keisler order is well-defined not just on ultrafilters, but also on upper sets over ω (i.e.
subset families closed upwards under ⊆). This sets up the first major theorem of [KN26]:
Theorem 2.3 ([KN26, Theorem A]). The Gamified Katˇetov order, written suggestively as
U ≤o

U, V ⊆upper sets ,
is a preorder on upper sets over ω. In particular:
(i) The Gamified Katˇetov order is equivalent to the Katˇetov order closed under well-founded iterations of Fubini
powers. In particular, it is strictly coarser than Rudin-Keisler and Katˇetov.
(ii) The Gamified Katˇetov order admits an explicit game-theoretic description, justifying its name.
(iii) The computable Gamified Katˇetov order is equivalent to the ≤LT-order on upper sets over ω.
Remark 2.4. In the same paper, we later extend the Gamified Katˇetov order from upper sets to upper sequences (i.e.
a countable sequence of upper sets), and show that the computable version of this extended Gamified Katˇetov order is
isomorphic to the original ≤LT-order [KN26, Theorem 6.5]. This move from upper sets to upper sequences mirrors
the move from basic topologies to recursive join of basic topologies, as in item (i) of the Summary Theorem.
Theorem 2.3 highlights several design features that distinguish the Gamified Katˇetov order from other preorders in
the set theory literature. Taken by themselves, these choices may seem puzzling to the reader, but in fact they reflect a
deep structural phenomenon, emerging in three different guises:
⋄as a natural variant of the Katˇetov order (set theory);
⋄as a concrete representation of abstract topologies, namely LT topologies (category theory);
⋄as a new hierarchy of computability notions, namely “computability by majority”1 (computability theory).
Seen in this light, the topos theory brings to the surface a series of deep structural connections we would otherwise
be hard-pressed to identify. With Theorem 2.3 in place, we now have the vocabulary to ask many interesting new
questions, laying the groundwork for a wide-ranging research programme.
3
“Coarse and Subtle”
One may view the ≤o
LT-order as simply isolating the combinatorial mechanism underlying the original ≤LT-order, but
it is also interesting in its own right. Theorem 2.3 states that the ≤o
LT-order is strictly coarser than the Rudin-Keisler
order, but the nature of its coarseness is subtle. Preliminary evidence indicates that the Gamified Katˇetov order detects
a kind of complexity quite different from that detected by the classical set-theoretic tools. For instance:
Theorem 3.1 ([KN26, Theorem B]). The Gamified Katˇetov and Tukey order are incomparable on filters over ω (in
ZFC).

It is well-known that the Tukey order (≤Tuk) is also strictly coarser than the Rudin-Keisler order. One may there-
fore wonder if these two orders are in fact responding to the same kind of structural properties of filters. Theorem 3.1
shows this is not the case: the Gamified Katˇetov and Tukey orders are in fact fundamentally misaligned. In particular,
the ≤o
LT-order does not measure the cofinality types of filters.
Moreover, the fact that Theorem 3.1 holds in ZFC is also noteworthy. A recent breakthrough result of [CMZ24]
shows that it is consistent with ZFC that all non-principal ultrafilters on ω are Tukey-equivalent. By contrast, part of
the proof of Theorem 3.1 shows that each ≡o
LT-class contains at most 2ℵ0 ultrafilters in ZFC. This rigidity was then
leveraged to deduce the existence of ultrafilters U, V such that U ≡Tuk V but U ̸≡o
LT V.
If the ≤o
LT-order does not measure the cofinality types of filters, what kind of combinatorial complexity does it
actually measure? One way to investigate this is to compare its structure with the classical Katˇetov order, and examine
what the ≤o
LT-order regards as significant vs. insiginficant differences between filters (dually, ideals) on ω.
As a warm-up, recall that an AD family (= Almost Disjoint) is a subset family A ⊆P(ω) such that all member sets
are infinite and have finite pairwise intersection. A MAD family (= Maximal Almost Disjoint) is an AD family A that
is maximal with respect to this property. Informally, MAD families are very flat – their member sets are spread out
across ω such that they only overlap in a small area.
Each MAD family generates an ideal on ω. From one point of view, these MAD families can be seen as mapping
out a wide range of complexity. Within the classical Katˇetov order, there exists at least c+-many equivalence classes
of MAD families as well as c-many pairwise incomparable ones [HGF03, §2]. By contrast, in the gamified setting,
all MAD families collapse to a single ≡o
LT-equivalence class [KN26, Conclusion 5.3]. On the other hand, despite
its apparent coarseness, there still remains a rich internal structure within ≤o
LT. A first indication of this is [KN26,
Theorem C], which establishes the existence of an infinite strictly ascending chain of classes within the Gamified
Katˇetov order. In a forthcoming paper, we significantly extend this picture:
Theorem 3.2 ([KN]). The Gamified Katˇetov order admits an embedding of P(ω)/Fin. In particular, it contains an
increasing chain of length b, as well as an antichain of size c.
Let us step back for a moment. Given what we now know about the Gamified Katˇetov order, how does this
illuminate the structure of the original ≤LT-order? What can we say about the interactions between the combinatorial
and computable within this framework?
These question guide the work of Sections 5-6 in [KN26]. When the terrain is still relatively uncharted, it is
difficult to make definitive statements on where the path may take us. Still, a couple of interesting takeaways have
emerged that are worth highlighting to the general logical audience.
First, a key theme in our recent investigations is that if we wish to separate equivalence classes within the ≤LT-
order, it is helpful to divide the analysis into two stages: start by performing the separation in the (non-computable)
setting of the Gamified Katˇetov order, before re-introducing the computability constraints to obtain the final separation
in ≤LT. In particular, since ≤o
LT is coarser than ≤LT (a consequence of Theorem 2.3), this means that H ̸≤o

4
Some Final Remarks
The Effective Topos Eff was introduced by Hyland in the early 80s [Hyl82], whereas the Rudin-Keisler order dates
back to the early 70s (if not earlier) [Rud71, Bla70]. Certainly the importance of both constructions were independently
well-appreciated, and have been extensively studied. Why, then, did it take forty-odd years after Hyland’s seminal
paper for the connection to finally be made?
One natural explanation lies in the historically limited interactions between category theory and set theory, but
there is more to be said here. The Gamified Katˇetov order emerged from re-examining previous results by Lee-van
Oosten [LvO13] (who showed the ≤LT-order reflects differences in intersection patterns of subset families) and by the
first author [Kih23] (who showed the ≤LT-order admits game-theoretic analysis). Looked at in this light, it seems that
certain structural results about the ≤LT-order needed to be in place before one might even begin formulating a result
like Theorem 2.3.
Moreover, recognising the possibility of a connection is only part of the story. As noted in the abstract of [KN26]:
“The proofs [of the paper] draw on ideas from general topology, descriptive set theory, and computability theory.” In
particular, none of the main arguments in [KN26] use any category theory — despite the category-theoretic origins
of the ≤LT-order. In this sense, establishing the connection required a genuine crossing of mathematical boundaries.
Perhaps then it is not so surprising that the link remained hidden for so long: there was little a priori reason to expect
that combinatorial set theory would provide an important key to understanding the ≤LT-order.
After we proved Theorem 2.3, many other results soon followed – surely there is still much more to be said.
We refer the interested reader to [KN26, §7], the open problems section of that paper. More broadly, our results
point to an emerging new understanding of category theory, particularly in regards to its relationship with logical
complexity. Beyond just providing an abstract framework for mathematics, category theory also implicitly determines
which distinctions should be regarded as structurally meaningful, and which should be treated as inessential. In
categorical logic, for instance, two first-order theories T0, T1 may fail to be bi-interpretable yet still be regarded as
equally expressive in the sense that Mod(T0) ≃Mod(T1), i.e. their categories of models are equivalent.
A parallel phenomenon arises in the present setting: filters (dually, ideals) on ω which are inequivalent in the
classical Katˇetov order may become equivalent in the Gamified Katˇetov order (e.g. MAD families). While category
theory is certainly not needed to show this, Theorem 2.3 places it within a broader structural context. The Gamified
Katˇetov order originates from the ≤LT-order, which is in order-reversing bijection with the lattice of subtoposes of
Eff [Joh02, Theorem A.4.4.8]. In this sense, the identifications made by the ≤o
LT-order reflect, albeit indirectly, the
category-theoretic equivalence used when defining the subtopos lattice of Eff. Clarifying the relationship between
these two notions of coarseness remains an interesting challenge, which we leave for future work.
