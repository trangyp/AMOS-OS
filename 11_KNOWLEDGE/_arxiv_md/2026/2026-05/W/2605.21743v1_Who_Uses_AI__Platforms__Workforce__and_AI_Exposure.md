Who Uses AI? Platforms, Workforce, and AI Exposure
Michelle Yin and Burhan Ogut∗
Abstract
A growing literature uses artificial intelligence platform conversation logs to measure occupation ex-
posure. We show that these scores partly measure platform user base rather than the workforce. Holding
outcome, sample, controls, and estimator fixed while varying only the platform input changes the post-
ChatGPT employment coeﬀicient by a factor of 1.9, and within-vendor consumer-versus-enterprise chan-
nels produce estimates that disagree in sign. Reweighting to Bureau of Labor Statistics workforce shares
attenuates estimates by 42 to 93 percent. We formalize the non-classical measurement error and derive
probability limits and partial-identification bounds for the employment elasticity. The bias understates
substitution more than augmentation.

Since the November 2022 release of ChatGPT, a growing empirical literature has studied how artificial
intelligence (AI) affects employment, wages, and the structure of work. The task-based framework of Au-
tor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) anchors this work, decomposing occupations into discrete tasks and asking
which tasks are susceptible to substitution. The framework’s empirical content depends on a per-occupation
exposure measure whose choice has shaped downstream estimates in the way that the choice of routine-task
index shaped the automation and job-polarization literature of the prior decade (Acemoglu and Autor 2011;
Goos, Manning, and Salomons 2014; Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020). Early measures of occupational AI
exposure operationalized this framework through inputs external to the occupations they describe, includ-
ing expert ratings (Frey and Osborne 2017), patent-text matching (Webb 2020), AI-capability benchmarks
(Brynjolfsson, Mitchell, and Rock 2018; Felten, Raj, and Seamans 2021), and a large language model rated
applicability rubric (Eloundou et al. 2024). The Eloundou et al. (2024) rubric has been adopted widely by
researchers and policy organizations (Acemoglu 2025; Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen 2025; Machovec,
Rieley, and Rolen 2025; Gmyrek, Berg, and Bescond 2023), and has become the dominant first-generation
measure in the downstream empirical literature.
A second generation of measures has moved from hypothetical capability toward observed behavior, map-
ping conversations on AI platforms to standardized task descriptions and reporting the share of conversations
associated with each occupation. Anthropic publishes such a mapping from millions of Claude conversations
(Handa et al. 2025; Appel et al. 2025), with parallel mappings from OpenAI ChatGPT (Chatterji et al. 2025)
and Microsoft Copilot (Tomlinson et al. 2025), alongside a national online survey of generative-AI use at
work from Bick, Blandin, and Deming (2026). Massenkoff and McCrory (2026) combine the two generations
by weighting the Eloundou et al. rubric by Anthropic conversation shares, reporting that the composite pre-
dicts Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) employment projections while the rubric alone does not. AI exposure
measures have entered downstream empirical work as fixed occupational regressors in studies of employ-
ment declines among young workers (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen 2025), wage effects (Chen, Kane,
Kozlowski, Kunievsky, and Evans 2025), and aggregate labor-demand reallocation (Hampole, Papanikolaou,
Schmidt, and Seegmiller 2025), each conditioning on a different measure.
The rapid adoption of platform-derived exposure reflects a measurement gap that traditional labor-market
instruments leave open. Worker surveys recover self-reported AI use at the extensive margin and have been
fielded at national scale by Bick, Blandin, and Deming (2026) and the U.S. Census Bureau’s Household
Trends and Outlook Pulse Survey (U.S. Census Bureau 2025), with parallel cross-national instruments from
the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) covering manufacturing and finance
workers (Lane, Williams, and Broecke 2023), but per-wave sample sizes constrain the occupational resolution

Unlike traditional labor-market variables derived from worker and employer surveys or administrative
records, the platform-derived exposure score depends on a self-selected user base shaped by pricing, product
launches, and competitive position. The share of conversations attributed to each occupation is therefore
a measure that mixes the platform’s commercial penetration of each occupation with the occupation’s un-
derlying task structure, and the two components are economically distinct. Computer and Mathematical
occupations generate 32 percent of consumer Claude conversations and 52 percent of enterprise conversa-
tions against 3.4 percent of U.S. employment, while Food Preparation workers are 8.8 percent of employment
but only 0.7 percent of conversations. Across the 22 Standard Occupational Classification major groups, the
ratio of platform conversation density to workforce employment density spans a factor of 72, and no platform-
derived measure correlates with BLS employment shares above a Spearman rank correlation of 0.33 (Figure
1). An OLS regression on this measure therefore confounds two distinct things, how exposed an occupa-
tion actually is to AI, and how heavily that occupation uses the particular platform supplying the data. The
coeﬀicient does not identify the underlying employment elasticity.
We test this implication by constructing the composite exposure score in the form proposed by Massenkoff
and McCrory (2026), in which the per-task applicability rubric of Eloundou et al. (2024) is weighted by the
per-occupation platform conversation share. We hold the regression specification, sample, outcome data, and
rubric fixed across ten composite variants drawn from the Anthropic Claude consumer and enterprise chan-
nels at multiple sampling waves and from Microsoft Copilot. Using a 2015 to 2024 American Community
Survey panel of 13.1 million person-year observations merged at the six-digit occupational classification level,
we estimate a difference-in-differences (DiD) specification of the form used widely in the AI-and-labor litera-
ture before and after the release of ChatGPT. We use this design as a diagnostic for platform-input sensitivity
rather than as a causal estimate of the underlying employment response, varying only the platform-derived
exposure across columns to isolate the contribution of the measurement input to the coeﬀicient. The rubric is
held fixed because its rating-model sensitivity is documented in Yin, Vu, and Persico (2026) and addressed
in Ogut and Yin (2026), and the conversation-share component is itself the input that the downstream ap-

The instability is structural, not statistical, and we address it in three steps. First, we decompose the
bias on the OLS coeﬀicient into two channels, a between-occupation channel that captures which occupa-
tions come to the platform, and a within-occupation channel that captures which tasks they perform once
there. Second, we show that reweighting platform conversation shares to BLS workforce shares removes
the between-occupation channel under a maintained ordering condition, that the two channels point in the
same direction. Third, we use the baseline and workforce-reweighted estimates as endpoints of a partial-
identification interval for the structural employment elasticity. We formalize the platform-selection problem
as non-classical measurement error (Bound, Brown, and Mathiowetz 2001; Meng 2018) while quantifying
its consequences for the AI-and-labor literature. A practical consequence is that two researchers running
an identical regression on the same labor-market data will recover different coeﬀicients if they draw their
exposure score from different platforms, because each platform’s user base produces a different bias. The
empirical analysis confirms the framework’s predictions about the temporal instability of the proxy as the
platform’s user base shifts and the magnitude of reweighting attenuation toward workforce shares. We bound
the structural employment elasticity through partial identification (Manski 2003), with the baseline OLS co-
eﬀicient as one endpoint and the coeﬀicient under reweighting to BLS workforce shares as the other, the
interval width quantifying the platform’s contribution to the published estimate. Reweighting attenuates the
composite coeﬀicient by 93 percent, leaving the structural elasticity statistically indistinguishable from zero,
while reweighting attenuates the Microsoft Copilot coeﬀicient by 42 percent, retaining significance because
Copilot’s user base lies closer to the workforce. The attenuation orders monotonically by the user-base-to-
workforce distance, exactly as the framework predicts.

weighted ranking does not flag as exposed, channeling resources away from the workers most affected by
substitution.
The implications extend into the divergent conclusions of the downstream empirical literature, where
recent studies report concentrated employment declines among young workers (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and
Chen 2025), null effects in Danish administrative records (Humlum and Vestergaard 2025), positive wage
effects (Chen, Kane, Kozlowski, Kunievsky, and Evans 2025), and muted aggregate effects with within-firm
reallocation (Hampole, Papanikolaou, Schmidt, and Seegmiller 2025). Each study conditions on a different
AI exposure measure and reaches a different conclusion. The platform-selection bias documented in this
paper is one source of measurement heterogeneity among several, and the partial-identification bounds and
workforce-representative reweighting developed here apply to any platform-derived measure used as a fixed
occupational regressor. The same identification gap will recur as platform-derived measures expand across
applied economics, where these methods are the minimum requirement for the field to recover the substantive
employment elasticity from the measurement input.

II. Data and Specifications
The empirical analysis combines ten platform-derived exposure measures with a 2015 to 2024 American
Community Survey (ACS) panel and benchmark workforce data from the BLS. This section describes each
data source and the DiD specification used to test the cross-platform dispersion implication of Section I.
A. Anthropic Economic Index
The Anthropic Economic Index (AEI; Handa et al. 2025; Appel et al. 2025) maps anonymized Claude
conversation transcripts to Occupational Information Network (O*NET) task descriptions using the Clio
classifier (Tamkin et al. 2024) and aggregates to per-occupation conversation shares at the six-digit Standard
Occupational Classification (SOC) level. The Anthropic Economic Index has five publicly released waves
spanning December 2024 to February 2026, with each wave reporting a Claude.ai consumer channel and an
enterprise first-party Application Programming Interface (API) channel.
The Clio classifier embeds conversation text and O*NET task descriptions into a shared vector space and
assigns each conversation to its nearest-neighbor task, with task-level conversation counts normalized to per-
occupation conversation shares. The five waves span model versions Claude 3.5 Sonnet (Wave 1, December
2024), 3.7 Sonnet (Wave 2, February 2025), Sonnet 4 (Wave 3, August 2025), Sonnet 4.5 (Wave 4, November
2025), and Opus 4.6 (Wave 5, February 2026), include two major product launches (Claude Code in March
2025 and a Claude Projects expansion in mid-2025), and span at least two pricing changes and a substantial
expansion of the enterprise customer base.

D. Subgroup Heterogeneity
Online Appendix Figure B.6 reports DiD coeﬀicients for fifteen demographic and occupation subgroups
under five platform-derived exposure measures, totaling 75 cells. The composite produces a statistically
significant negative coeﬀicient in nine of fifteen subgroups, the Claude.ai consumer channel produces eight
significant negatives, and the enterprise API channel produces three significant positives and four significant
negatives. Sign disagreement across measures is most pronounced for three subgroups, with the coeﬀicient
on workers with disabilities positive under Claude.ai consumer and negative under the composite, the coef-
ficient on workers under age 25 positive under the composite and negative under Microsoft Copilot, and the
coeﬀicient on female workers positive under the enterprise API and negative under all other measures.
The applied literature on AI exposure conditions on a single platform-derived measure, typically An-
thropic or the composite construction, without testing sensitivity to the platform choice. The 75-cell grid
suggests that published subgroup contrasts are partly driven by the platform from which the exposure was
constructed. A researcher claiming that workers with disabilities are less affected by AI than the population
average would draw that conclusion under the composite but the opposite conclusion under Claude.ai con-
sumer. The variation across measures is the same source of fragility documented in Sections IV.A and IV.B,

The subgroup pattern raises a second concern distinct from coeﬀicient dispersion. Platform conversation
data observes only workers who use the platform, and workers who do not use the platform are invisible to
the data, with the DiD coeﬀicient for a given subgroup depending on two objects, the true effect of AI on that
subgroup and the subgroup’s representation in the platform user base. Subgroups less visible to the platform
enter the regression through their workforce share rather than through their actual AI-use patterns, and their
estimated coeﬀicient inherits the user-base composition of the platform that supplied the exposure score.
The disagreement observed in the disability, age-under-25, and female rows is therefore consistent with two
mechanisms operating at once that the regression cannot separate, true heterogeneity in the AI-employment
relationship across subgroups and differential platform visibility across the same subgroups.
The distinction matters for policy interpretation because the platform-visible workforce overrepresents
workers with higher income, more education, urban residence, and non-Hispanic ethnicity, and underrepre-
sents the corresponding disadvantaged subpopulations (Online Appendix Figure B.2 plots occupations along
the joint dimensions of economic vulnerability and platform visibility). A subgroup that is both economically
disadvantaged and underrepresented on the platform sits in the quadrant of Figure B.2 where the DiD coef-
ficient is least informative about the true effect, because the platform’s view of that subgroup is constructed
from a small and likely non-representative sample of its members. Policy rankings that condition on a sin-
gle platform-derived exposure will therefore systematically underweight the populations whose AI-related
labor-market risks the rankings are intended to identify.

C. Partial-Identification Bounds
We construct a partial-identification interval (Manski 2003) that bounds the structural employment elasticity
under the maintained ordering condition stated in Section I and proved in Appendix A.5. The baseline com-
posite coeﬀicient and the workforce-reweighted version are two endpoints of this interval, with the baseline
estimate serving as the upper bound on the magnitude under the assumption that the user-base composi-
tion correlates positively with true exposure, and the reweighted estimate serving as the lower bound. The
between-occupation interval is [−70.09, −38.28] in 𝛽× 100 units per standard deviation, with a width of
31.81 that represents 45 percent of the baseline magnitude.
The width quantifies the user-base contribution to the published composite estimate (Massenkoff and
McCrory 2026). Approximately half the published magnitude is attributable to the between-occupation gap
between the platform user base and the workforce, with the remainder reflecting the rubric-and-task-time
content that survives reweighting. Reporting the baseline estimate alone identifies only the upper bound on
the magnitude, the lower bound is 45 percent smaller, and the interval spans the full range from one to the
other.
The cross-platform substitutes reported in Panels B and C span both signs and a wide range of magnitudes,
with coeﬀicients from −150.04 under Microsoft Copilot to +174.00 under OpenAI ChatGPT, a span of
324.04 in 𝛽×100 units per standard deviation. The substitutes are not directly comparable to the composite
because they enter the regression as raw conversation densities rather than as rubric-weighted task aggregates,
and the reweighted versions are degenerate under the operation defined in Section I. We therefore report the
substitution range as descriptive evidence on cross-platform variation in the between-occupation specification
rather than as a partial-identification interval.

bounded below in magnitude by the workforce-reweighted estimate in either specification, while the upper

The implication is that AI-employment coeﬀicients in the applied literature are not platform-independent

The partial-identification framing connects to the taxonomy of Tamer (2010). The bounds reported here

discussed in Section I becomes available.

The propagation result of Section I states that the user-base channel transmits to any occupation-level outcome

specification (Massenkoff and McCrory 2026). This section tests the propagation on a set of ten additional
occupation-level outcomes drawn from the ACS and the National Health Interview Survey, and develops the
distributional incidence of the resulting bias by decomposing platform visibility by worker demographics.

VII. Robustness
This section documents that the cross-platform coeﬀicient dispersion in Section IV is not a property of any
single modeling choice. We test seven dimensions of the DiD specification, with each test holding the analytic

relevant statistic is the cross-platform range of the post-2022 coeﬀicient across the ten variants, expressed as
the ratio of the largest absolute coeﬀicient to the smallest. The baseline ratio on the full-sample employment
specification is 1.9.

across platforms rather than differential post-treatment loading. For each of the ten variants we estimate

reference year. Six of the ten variants reject the zero-pre-trend null at the 5 percent level, and only the
workforce-reweighted composite passes. As discussed in Section IV.C, this asymmetric rejection pattern
is consistent with the user-base channel of Section I, because the platform-derived regressor is constructed
from the platform’s user base, and pre-period employment trends covary with occupational composition

VIII. Conclusion
The empirical study of technology and labor markets rests on measures of occupational exposure to the tech-
nology in question, and the choice of measure has historically shaped the downstream estimates the field
draws on. The task-based framework of Autor, Levy, and Murnane (2003) and Acemoglu and Autor (2011)
established that such measures should capture the technology’s potential to substitute for or complement the
tasks workers perform, and subsequent literatures on automation (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020) and on the
labor-market effects of information and communication technology (Atasoy 2013; Akerman, Gaarder, and
Mogstad 2015) have built on this foundation. The recent literature on generative AI faces a measurement
problem that earlier technologies did not, because the fraction of a task that AI in fact performs in produc-
tion cannot be directly observed at the worker-task level, and platform-derived AI-use measures (Handa et al.
2025; Chatterji et al. 2025; Tomlinson et al. 2025) have emerged as the closest available proxy for actual AI
use. A recent observed-exposure measure (Massenkoff and McCrory 2026) combines theoretical task capa-
29


## Page 30


bility from Eloundou et al. (2024), task-time weights from O*NET, and the share of each task in Anthropic
Economic Index conversation data, treating the resulting score as a proxy for the share of an occupation’s
task-time in which AI is being used. The conceptual move is reasonable, but the implementation inherits a
measurement problem this paper has characterized and bounded.
The share of conversations on an AI platform that map to a given O*NET task is a property of the plat-
form’s user base, not of the workforce (Anthropic overrepresents Computer and Mathematical occupations
by factors of nine on its consumer channel and fifteen on its enterprise channel), and the OLS coeﬀicient
on a measure built this way depends on the platform’s commercial reach as much as on the technology’s
substitution potential. This is a form of non-classical measurement error in the sense of Bound, Brown, and
Mathiowetz (2001), and Meng’s (2018) point about non-probability samples, that the selection mechanism
rather than the sample size governs the inferential distortion, applies as well.
The framework predicts the direction of the bias as well as its magnitude. Section I characterizes the
magnitude of the bias on the OLS coeﬀicient but does not distinguish between augmentation and substitu-
tion channels. When AI augments worker productivity, as documented in field experiments on writing tasks
(Noy and Zhang 2023), customer support (Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond 2023), software development (Cui
et al. 2024), and consulting work (Dell’Acqua et al. 2023), the workers visible in platform conversation data
are weighted toward those for whom augmentation is most productive, and the platform share is therefore
more representative of that subgroup than of the occupation as a whole. When AI substitutes for workers,
as documented for entry-level employment in exposed occupations (Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen 2025),
the workers being substituted for are underrepresented in platform conversation data because the platform
observes current users rather than workers who have been displaced or moved to other occupations. The plat-
form share therefore understates the intensity of substitution where it is most consequential. The asymmetry
has a direction the literature has not previously named. Usage-based exposure measures capture augmenta-
tion more fully than substitution, and the labor-displacement question motivating the field’s interest in AI
exposure is exactly the question these measures are least equipped to answer. The empirical results in Sec-
tion IV are consistent with this asymmetry. The largest reweighting attenuation falls on the occupations the
platform most overrepresents, which is where the augmentation experience is best captured. We do not sep-
arately identify the two channels, and the magnitude of the substitution-channel understatement remains an
open empirical question.
The distributional implications extend beyond any single coeﬀicient. The platform-visible workforce sys-
tematically overrepresents workers with higher income, more education, urban residence, and non-Hispanic
ethnicity, while underrepresenting the corresponding disadvantaged groups (Section VI.B). The retraining-
30


## Page 31


allocation exercise in Online Appendix C shows that a policy ranking based on platform-derived exposure
shares would redirect approximately two-fifths of a hypothetical 10-billion-dollar fund toward occupations
whose workers already have above-median wages and high Bachelor’s-degree shares. These are also the pop-
ulations for whom the substitution channel is most likely to be understated in usage-based exposure measures,
and the policy ranking and the measurement bias therefore compound rather than cancel. The workers least
visible on the platform are also those most affected by existing digital divides in broadband access and AI
literacy. A measure that treats their absence from the data as evidence of low AI exposure mistakes a barrier
to access for an absence of consequence, and a policy built on that measure directs resources away from the
workers most likely to need them.
Two questions remain open. The first concerns the intensive margin of AI use. Conversation-share data re-
veal whether a task appears in platform usage frequently enough to clear a threshold, but they do not record
how much of a worker’s task-time is in fact substituted by AI, the productivity of that substitution, or the
elasticity of demand for the worker’s remaining task bundle. The Bick, Blandin, and Deming (2026) RPS
micro release reports weekly time saved at work for the November 2024 wave, covering 3,724 employed
respondents of whom 899 report AI use at work in the last week. The weighted mean weekly time saved
per AI-using worker is 2.52 hours in Management occupations, 2.44 hours in Computer and Mathematical,
and 2.24 hours in Food Preparation. The RPS does not record within-task time share at the worker-task
level. Administrative-data evidence on this finer margin remains limited to single-country settings (Humlum
and Vestergaard 2025) and the small set of single-firm field experiments cited in Section I. A workforce-
representative measure capturing both margins would substantially narrow the partial-identification interval
developed here and, combined with exogenous variation in exposure, would identify the structural employ-
ment elasticity directly. The second open question concerns which outcomes AI affects and through what
economic channels. The applied literature has concentrated on within-occupation employment and wages. If
generative AI operates through demand-creation, search-cost, and matching channels like those documented
for prior information technologies (Atasoy 2013; Akerman et al. 2015), the relevant labor-market conse-
quences extend well beyond the within-occupation elasticities the recent literature has estimated.
The historical sequence of measurement problems in the technology-and-labor literature has typically
been resolved through the construction of measures aligned with the underlying labor-market concept rather
than with the technology that produced the data. Routine-task indices (Autor, Levy, and Murnane 2003;
Autor and Dorn 2013; Goos, Manning, and Salomons 2014) replaced ad-hoc automation rankings, and the
shift-share design for robot exposure (Acemoglu and Restrepo 2020) replaced firm-level adoption shares. The
platform-selection problem for AI exposure will require a similar move toward workforce-representative mea-
31


## Page 32


surement on the extensive and intensive margins jointly. The literature appears to require three measurement
layers each fit to a different question. The hypothetical-capability layer measures which tasks AI can perform
at equivalent quality, served by LLM-based rubric measurement at the task level (Eloundou et al. 2024) and
the multi-rater extensions that address its rating-model sensitivity (Yin, Vu, and Persico 2026; Ogut and Yin
2026). The adoption layer measures the worker-level rate at which AI is used in each occupation, observed
most directly through worker surveys (Bick, Blandin, and Deming 2026) whose unit of observation is the
worker. The behavioral layer measures conversation-level task intensity within each occupation, observed
through platform logs whose unit of observation is the conversation, of the kind analyzed in this paper. The
two layers are correlated empirically but neither substitutes for the other. A worker who never uses AI is in-
visible in the behavioral layer but visible in the adoption layer, and intensive per-user variation is invisible in
the adoption layer but visible in the behavioral layer. The structural employment elasticity is jointly identified
when all three layers are observed at suﬀicient task granularity, and the platform-selection problem this paper
documents prevents the behavioral layer from substituting for either of the other two. The methodological
response this paper develops bridges the interim period. We decompose the bias into a between-occupation
channel (which occupations come to the platform) and a within-occupation channel (which tasks they per-
form once there). Reweighting platform shares to BLS workforce shares removes the between-occupation
channel under a maintained ordering condition, and the baseline and workforce-reweighted estimates serve
as endpoints of a partial-identification interval (Manski 2003; Tamer 2010) for the structural employment
elasticity. The test in Section IV.F confirms the decomposition. Workforce reweighting absorbs roughly half
the cross-wave variation in the employment coeﬀicient, with the within-occupation residual concentrated at
product launches. Any subsequent applied study can perform the same operation on its exposure measure
and report both endpoints, with the interval’s width quantifying the platform’s contribution to the published
coeﬀicient. The question of who uses AI is therefore a prior question for the field’s empirical agenda, not a
corollary of it.

Online Appendix C. Implied Incidence of a Retraining Program (Policy Simulation)
To illustrate the policy implications of the platform-selection bias documented in the main text, we conduct a
hypothetical $10-billion AI retraining fund allocation exercise. Two rules are compared. Under the platform-
weighted rule, the allocation follows Anthropic Wave 5 Claude.ai conversation shares: occupations with the
highest platform exposure receive proportionally more retraining dollars. Under the workforce-reweighted
rule, the allocation follows BLS May 2024 OEWS employment shares: dollars track where workers are, not
where platform conversations are.
The platform-weighted rule redirects approximately $3.87 billion (39 percent of the fund) to occupations
with above-median wages and Bachelor’s-degree-or-higher shares above 60 percent, primarily Computer
and Mathematical, Business and Financial Operations, and Oﬀice and Administrative Support occupations.
The workforce-reweighted rule directs the same dollars toward middle-wage occupations with Bachelor’s-or-
higher shares closer to the workforce mean, primarily Sales, Oﬀice Support, Transportation, Production, and
Food Preparation.
Figure C.1 visualizes the incidence shift. The platform-weighted bars show a redistribution of dollars
toward high-skill occupations; the workforce-reweighted bars match the underlying workforce composition.
The $3.87-billion shift is the implied compositional bias when a policy maker uses platform-derived exposure
as an allocation key without workforce reweighting. The exercise treats the platform-weighted rule as a
benchmark for what would happen if the published Anthropic conversation shares were taken as a direct
measure of training need, holding total program dollars fixed.
The simulation is illustrative rather than prescriptive. The Bureau of Labor Statistics workforce distri-
bution is the relevant denominator if the policy aim is workforce-wide coverage, but a policy specifically
targeting workers most likely to be affected by AI productivity gains may have different weighting objec-
tives. The $3.87-billion gap quantifies the magnitude of the user-base channel in a concrete dollar amount
and isolates the compositional component from the substantive question of how AI exposure maps to retrain-
ing need.

Online Appendix D. Technical Details
This appendix consolidates specification, variable-construction, and replication-method details for the main
text. Items are ordered by appearance in the manuscript.
D.1 Analytic Sample
The canonical sample is constructed from the IPUMS American Community Survey 1-year extracts, 2015 to
2024. Sample restrictions are applied in order: year in {2015, …, 2024}; age 16 to 64; classwkr = 2 (civilian
wage-and-salary); valid six-digit SOC 2018 code; empstat in {1, 3}. The final sample is N = 13,104,072
person-years. The SOC crosswalk between the ACS occsoc field and the SOC 2018 six-digit codes retains
98.5 percent of the full ACS extract; the 147,281 dropped rows are concentrated in six SOC codes absent
from our exposure-variant tables and exhibit standardized mean differences below 0.01 from the retained
sample on all demographic and outcome variables.
The composite-merged subset (used in regressions that condition on a non-missing composite exposure
score) is N = 8,630,961 person-years and excludes 219 National Employment Matrix occupations not covered
by the Baseline Composite (Anthropic) table from Massenkoff and McCrory (2026).
ACS variable construction follows IPUMS conventions. Indicators are: female, married, bachelor-or-
higher, high-school-only, some-college, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, employed (emp-
stat = 1), labor-force participant (labforce = 2), and disability (union of the six ACS diﬀiculty items). The post
indicator equals one for 2023 and 2024. Annual wage-and-salary income (incwage) is cleaned per IPUMS
sentinels and log-transformed where positive. Person weights (perwt) are used throughout.
D.2 Cross-Platform Composition (Table 3)
Three of the six measurement columns in Table 3 are directly comparable: BLS workforce share (May 2024
OEWS), Claude.ai Wave 5 conversation share, and the first-party API enterprise Wave 5 conversation share.
Each column sums to 100 percent across the 22 SOC major groups.
The remaining three columns use different unit conventions and are not row-comparable to the share

The pre-treatment parallel-trends joint Wald F-test is reported per variant in Online Appendix Table B.9
Panel B. The asymmetric rejection pattern (Composite Reweighted alone passes; all unweighted AEI variants

clean parallel trends, consistent with the Section I.D skew-attenuation prediction. Within the Claude.ai wave
sequence, the F-statistic orders monotonically with how diffused the user-base occupation distribution is
