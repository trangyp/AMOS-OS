WEAK AND STRONG FIBRATIONS OF FUNCTORS
AND MOTION PLANNING
ISAAC CARCAC´IA-CAMPOS, ENRIQUE MAC´IAS-VIRG´OS,
AND DAVID MOSQUERA-LOIS
Abstract. We develop a homotopical framework for small cat-
egories that extends classical invariants of algebraic topology to
the categorical setting. Our approach is based on the construc-
tion of a genuine path category, obtained through a zig–zag and
localization procedure, which allows us to define strong and weak
fibrations for functors. We establish their basic properties, intro-
duce a fibrant replacement for functors, and extend homotopical
invariants such as the ˇSvarc genus and sectional category to small
categories. Finally, we apply this framework to motion planning in
small categories, providing categorical analogues of Farber’s topo-
logical complexity while removing finiteness constraints typical of
existing approaches.
Introduction
The study of homotopical invariants has played a central role in alge-
braic topology since its origins. Classical notions such as the Lusternik–
Schnirelmann category (LS-category) [7], topological complexity [3],
the ˇSvarc genus and sectional category [14], and the more recent no-
tion of homotopic distance [9], provide quantitative tools for measuring
the complexity of spaces and maps. These invariants are particularly
relevant in applications such as motion planning in robotics, where
their values for a configuration space determine the intrinsic difficulty
of designing a continuous motion planning algorithm [10].
In recent years, several authors have explored categorical analogues
of these invariants. Tanaka studied LS-category for small categories [12].
Subsequently, the two last authors of present paper introduced the no-
tions of topological complexity and homotopic distance for functors [8].

More recently, these ideas have been extended and refined in various
directions [2, 1].
All these works build on Lee’s approach to homotopies in cate-
gories [6] and on Grothendieck’s theory of fibrations [13]. Neverthe-
less, these approaches do not fully capture the homotopical behaviour
required in the categorical setting, which limits the study of motion
planning invariants for small categories.
The aim of this paper is to overcome these limitations by developing
a systematic homotopical framework for small categories, extending
classical invariants to this combinatorial setting. Our starting point
is Hoff’s approach to homotopy theory in categories [5], based on the
construction of a path category. However, Hoff’s construction proves
too rigid for our purposes. This motivates our definition of a novel
genuine path category associated with a small category, obtained via
a zig–zag construction followed by a localization procedure. This pro-
vides a flexible and well-behaved model for categorical paths, which in
turn allows us to introduce appropriate notions of fibration.
The main contributions of this work may be summarized as follows:
• We introduce a path category PC for any small category C,
together with an endpoint functor that plays the role of the
classical path fibration (Theorem 4.1).
• We define notions of strong and weak fibrations for functors,
inspired by the classical lifting properties in topology, and es-
tablish their first properties, including relations between them
(Theorem 2.8), and their behaviour under pullbacks and com-
position.
• We construct the fibrant replacement of a functor (Theorem 3.1),
showing that any functor factors as a weak homotopy equiva-
lence followed by a fibration.
• Using these tools, we extend classical invariants such as the
ˇSvarc genus, sectional category, LS-category, categorical com-
plexity, and homotopic distance to the setting of small cate-
gories, introducing both strong and weak versions.
• Finally, we apply this framework to motion planning in small
categories, obtaining categorical analogues of Farber’s topolog-
ical complexity and proving relations among the different in-
variants (Theorem 4.10).
Furthermore, by working in the setting of small categories we are able
to overcome the finiteness condition that is required for fibrations in the
simplicial context [4]. This yields a more flexible and general theory,

1. The right notion for a path category
1.1. Definition of the path category. For each natural number m
we define the zig-zag category of length m, denoted by Im, as the cate-
gory whose objects are the positive integers from 0 to m and morphisms
i →i + 1 if i is even and i ←i + 1 if i is odd.
Let C be a small category.
A path of length m in C is a functor
I : Im →C. For a path of length m I : Im →C we refer to the object
I(0) as the initial point or object of the path I and to I(m) as the end
point or object of the path. As an abuse of notation, we use the term
path to not specify the length. A category C is connected if for every
pair of objects C and D there is path such that the initial object is C
and the end point is D.
We define PC as the category whose objects are paths and a mor-
phism between two paths I : Im →C and J : In →C is a pair (F, α): I →
J where
• F : Im →In is a functor such that F(0) = 0 and F(m) = n
• and α is a natural transformation α: I =⇒J ◦F.

same constant value but different length are different, but from a ho-

a reparameterization of the other. We solve this difficulty by localizing.

2. Fibrations
2.1. Homotopy between functors. We introduce two notions of ho-
motopy between functors. We begin by recalling the notion of homo-
topy (we refer to it as strong homotopy) in small categories introduced
by Lee ([6]). Then we introduce a novel and weaker notion of homotopy
(we refer to it as weak homotopy) inspired on the one introduced by
Hoff [5] but with the difference that we work with a different category
of paths and explained in Example 1.2.

Definition 2.1. Let F, G: C →D be two functors between small cate-
gories. We say that F and G are strong homotopic, denoted by F ≃G,
if, for some m ≥0, there exists a functor H : C × Im →D, called a ho-
motopy (of length m), such that H(−, 0) = F and H(−, m) = G. We
will denote the fact that two functors F and G are strong homotopic
with F ≃G.
Strong homotopy between functors proves to be too rigid for develop-
ing motion planning in small categories since with this we do not have
the usual equivalence between homotopic sections to ∆: C →C×C and
sections to the path fibration π: PC →C. That is why we introduce
weak homotopies in the spirit of [5] and [4].
Definition 2.2. Let F, G: C →D be two functors between small cat-
egories. The functors F and G are weak homotopic, denoted F ≃w G,
(we also say there is a weak homotopy between them) if there exists a
functor H : C →PD such that π0 ◦H = F and π1 ◦H = G.
Observe that being strong or weak homotopic defines an equivalence
relation among functors.
Definition 2.3. Two small categories C and D are strong (respectively
weak) homotopy equivalent or strong (respectively weak) homotopic if
there are exist functors F : C →D and G: D →C such that G◦F ≃idC
and F ◦G ≃idD (respectively G ◦F ≃w idC and F ◦G ≃w idD). The
small category C is contractible (respectively weak contractible) if it is
strong homotopic (respectively weak homotopic) to the trivial category
• with only one object and one morphism.
