When Redistribution Becomes a State Variable:
Monetary-Fiscal Stabilization with Type-Specific
Sticky Wages
Kenji Miyazaki∗
2026-05-10
Many tractable TANK models treat redistribution as a contemporaneous wedge. I
show that this view is incomplete once wage contracts are type-specific. In a tractable
Two-Agent New Keynesian model, each household type adjusts its nominal wage
relative to its own previous wage. This own-lag contract makes the cross-type wage
gap a payoff-relevant distributional state variable. The wage gap follows a second-
order expectational law of motion and feeds back into aggregate demand through
consumption dispersion. Inflation stabilization or contemporaneous profit-wedge
neutralization therefore generally fails to restore the corresponding representative-
agent allocation. Under the maintained commitment benchmark, RANK-equivalent
stabilization from period t = 1 onward requires history-dependent transfers that
respond to inherited wage dispersion, not only current profits. In the benchmark
calibration, wage rigidity raises the peak output response to a transfer shock by a
factor of 3.27, from 2.49 × 10−4 to 8.14 × 10−4.
1 Introduction
Many tractable heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian models treat redistribution as a contempo-
raneous wedge. Distributional effects work through current profits, current transfers, or current
labor income, and policy can often neutralize those wedges period by period. I show that this
logic breaks down when wage contracts are type-specific. If each household type adjusts its
nominal wage relative to its own past wage, current redistribution leaves behind an inherited
wage gap. Redistribution then becomes a state variable.
This state variable creates a policy failure result. Stabilizing inflation or offsetting the current
profit wedge is not sufficient to restore the representative-agent allocation when inherited
wage dispersion is nonzero. Full stabilization requires transfers that respond to the history of
distributional distortions. In this model and under the commitment benchmark studied below,
monetary and fiscal stabilization must be history dependent once redistribution becomes a state
variable.

I study this mechanism in a tractable Two-Agent New Keynesian (TANK) model with sticky
prices, type-specific sticky wages, and balanced-budget transfers. The economy has hand-to-
mouth households, who consume current disposable income, and savers, who smooth consumption
and receive firm dividends. Movements in wages, profits, and transfers therefore change relative
spending power. The key question is whether those relative positions remain contemporaneous
wedges or become part of the economy’s state.
This question is difficult to isolate in the standard model classes. RANK models have no internal
distributional state. Standard analytical TANK models often contain profit and transfer wedges,
but these wedges are usually contemporaneous objects (Bilbiie 2008, 2020, 2024; Debortoli and
Galí 2024). Quantitative HANK models capture rich distributional dynamics, but usually do
not isolate this state-space mechanism analytically (McKay et al. 2016; Kaplan et al. 2018;
Kaplan and Violante 2018; Broer et al. 2020). Analytical TANK sits between these cases: it
retains enough heterogeneity to generate cross-type wage dispersion while preserving transparent
equilibrium relations. That tractability is useful here because the object of interest is not only
whether heterogeneity changes monetary transmission, but which state variable carries the
distributional effects of past shocks.1
Most analytical TANK results emphasize price stickiness as the main nominal friction. Once
wage rigidity is introduced, heterogeneity interacts with labor supply, wage setting, and profit
incidence at the same time. Existing sticky-wage TANK and sticky-wage HANK contributions
show that labor-market structure matters for fiscal multipliers, labor supply, aggregate dynamics,
and policy design (Galí et al. 2007; Furlanetto 2011; Colciago 2011; Ascari et al. 2017; Ida
and Okano 2024; Auclert et al. 2023; Gerke et al. 2024). Auclert, Bardoczy, and Rognlie
use sticky wages to address the joint behavior of marginal propensities to consume, marginal
propensities to earn, and fiscal multipliers; this paper uses type-specific wage adjustment to
identify a distributional state variable. Gerke et al. are especially close because they study
household labor supply in a sticky-wage HANK environment with union wage setting. This
paper is complementary: rather than asking how unions allocate hours in a quantitative HANK
economy, it asks how type-specific wage adjustment changes the state space of a tractable TANK
economy. The answer is a wage-dispersion state that makes redistribution history dependent.
This paper differs from the closest literatures in a simple way. Analytical TANK models emphasize
contemporaneous profit and transfer wedges; here, wage contracts make redistribution history
dependent. Quantitative HANK models add rich household heterogeneity; here, the goal is to
isolate which distributional object becomes a state variable. Sticky-wage heterogeneous-agent
models study labor-market structure and aggregate dynamics; here, the focus is the state-space
implication of type-specific own-lag wage adjustment.
The new result is not dual rigidity per se. A related paper, Miyazaki (2025), derives a closed-form
TANK model with price and wage rigidities and studies monetary-transmission amplification
and welfare under dual nominal frictions. Here, the model retains the forward-looking wage
term and defines type-specific wage inflation against each type’s own lagged wage. These two
features generate a second-order expectational law of motion for wage dispersion. Since own-lag
type-specific wage adjustment makes the distributional state predetermined, the stabilizing
transfer rule changes from contemporaneous profit-wedge neutralization to history-dependent
stabilization.

Second, it derives a policy failure result and its implementation benchmark. A transfer rule that
neutralizes only the contemporaneous profit wedge is generally insufficient. Under the timing
and commitment assumptions stated in Proposition 2, RANK-equivalent stabilization requires a
history-dependent transfer rule that responds to inherited wage dispersion. The rule eliminates

onward, and implements the corresponding Dual-Rigidity RANK allocation under the same
price and wage rigidities.
Third, it shows that the mechanism is quantitatively relevant. In the benchmark calibration,
wage rigidity amplifies transfer shocks and generates persistent wage and consumption gaps
after monetary shocks, even when aggregate output and inflation responses are close to the
representative-agent benchmark. The history-dependent transfer rule removes these gaps by
absorbing the inherited wage-dispersion state.
The benchmark magnitudes are modest in absolute terms but sizable in relative terms. The
peak output-gap response to a 1 percent transfer innovation is 8.14 × 10−4 in Passive TANK,
more than three times the 2.49 × 10−4 response in the sticky-price-only TANK economy. After a
monetary policy shock, aggregate output responses are close to the RANK benchmark: the peak
output gap is 1.47 × 10−2 in Passive TANK and 1.49 × 10−2 in Dual-Rigidity RANK. But wage
and consumption gaps remain persistent, with a maximum consumption gap of 5.04 × 10−3. The
history-dependent transfer rule eliminates these gaps by absorbing the inherited wage-dispersion
state.
The forward-looking wage-dispersion equation also gives a direct wage-setting channel for
redistributive forward guidance. Holding expected productivity and aggregate real wages fixed, a
higher expected future transfer lowers the expected future net distributional wedge and reduces
current wage dispersion. Expected future redistribution therefore enters current wage-setting
incentives through the distributional state.

2 Model and Canonical Representation
The model is a TANK economy with two household types, type-specific sticky wages, sticky prices,
and balanced-budget redistribution. Hand-to-mouth households consume current disposable
income. Savers trade bonds and receive firm dividends. Technology, monetary, and transfer
shocks move the economy, and own-lag type-specific wage adjustment makes the cross-type wage
gap the key state variable for the analysis.

Labor market and wage setting.
The labor market has differentiated labor services and nominal wage stickiness. Households
supply labor varieties to a representative labor packer, which aggregates them into a homogeneous
labor input. The elasticity of substitution across labor types is ψw > 1, and wage setting is
subject to Rotemberg adjustment costs with parameter ηw ≥0. Each household type adjusts its
nominal wage relative to its own previous nominal wage, rather than relative to an aggregate
wage index.
The own-lag specification has a concrete contract interpretation. If type j renegotiates its nominal
wage, the relevant reference point is type j’s previous contract. This captures segment-specific
wage bargaining, type-specific unions, occupation-specific contracts, firm-type wage ladders, and
contract renegotiation within labor-market segments whose past nominal wages are separately
recorded. In such environments, the object being renegotiated is the segment’s own nominal
wage, not the economy-wide average wage.2 A common-lag specification imposes the opposite
synchronization assumption, under which each type’s wage adjustment is measured against
the same aggregate lagged wage. That synchronization mechanically removes the inherited
relative-wage state studied in this paper. The own-lag case is therefore a transparent benchmark
for studying the state-space consequence of type-specific wage adjustment.

3 Analytical Policy Implications
Contemporaneous profit-wedge neutralization is not enough once wage dispersion becomes an
endogenous state variable. A stabilizing transfer rule must also respond to inherited wage
dispersion.
So far, zt has been an exogenous transfer disturbance.
I now reinterpret it as an active
stabilization instrument. The section first shows why sticky wages make wage dispersion stable
but history dependent. It then derives a history-dependent transfer rule that removes the
inherited distributional state and implements RANK-equivalent stabilization from period t = 1
onward. The final part isolates a direct anticipation effect: expected future transfers can affect
current wage dispersion before any transfer is paid.

4 Quantitative Analysis
The quantitative exercises ask whether the distributional state matters at economically meaningful
magnitudes. The answer is yes. Wage rigidity substantially amplifies the aggregate response
to transfer shocks. Monetary shocks can generate aggregate responses close to the RANK
benchmark while leaving persistent wage and consumption gaps. The history-dependent transfer
rule removes the inherited distributional state through a front-loaded fiscal response. The
inherited-state transition in Figure 4 is therefore central: it isolates why the lagged term in the
rule matters.

5 Conclusion
Type-specific sticky wages turn cross-type wage dispersion into a state variable. That state carries
the distributional legacy of past shocks and feeds into aggregate demand through consumption
dispersion. Stabilizing inflation or neutralizing only the contemporaneous profit wedge therefore
generally does not restore the representative-agent allocation.
The model’s implication is that stabilization and redistribution cannot be separated in this
reduced-form transfer benchmark once inequality is part of the state vector. Under commitment,
a benchmark history-dependent transfer rule offsets both the contemporaneous net wedge and the
inherited wage-dispersion state. It shuts down wage dispersion on impact and, from period t ≥1
onward, removes consumption dispersion and restores the corresponding RANK aggregate IS
relation under the same price and wage rigidities. In the quantitative exercises, passive transfers

At the baseline calibration, the passive-transfer full system is determinate. The numerical check

system used in the quantitative code and apply the Blanchard–Kahn root count. A point is
classified as determinate when the number of roots outside the unit circle equals the number of
forward-looking variables, indeterminate when that count is smaller, and as having no stable
solution when it is larger. To avoid notation ambiguity, the code variable phi denotes the
Taylor-rule inflation coefficient ϕ, whereas varphi denotes the inverse Frisch elasticity φ.
