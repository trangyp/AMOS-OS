WHEN IS THE ELLIS SEMIGROUP A COMPLETE
CONJUGACY INVARIANT?
JOHANNES KELLENDONK AND REEM YASSAWI
Abstract. The Ellis semigroup of a topological dynamical sys-
tem contains algebraic, topological and dynamical information. It
is invariant under conjugacy. Despite this wealth of structure, two
non-conjugate dynamical systems can have the same Ellis semi-
group. We identify a class of minimal dynamical systems inside
which this cannot happen, that is, for which the Ellis semigroup is
a complete conjugacy invariant.
1. Introduction
Let T be an abelian group acting on a compact Hausdorff space X
by a continuous action σ. The Ellis semigroup E(X) = E(X, σ, T)
is the compactification of the action with the topology of pointwise
convergence. It is a right-topological monoid with an action σE of T
given by left multiplication by σ. An Ellis morphism is a factor map
Φ : (E(X), σE, T) →(E(X′), σ′
E, T) which is a monoid morphism. Any
factor map ϕ : (X, σ, T) →(X′, σ′, T) between the dynamical systems
induces an Ellis morphism. This suggests the question: When is an
Ellis morphism induced by a factor map? We identify in Theorem 3.5
a class of topological dynamical systems for which this is always the
case.
Related to this question is the following: When is an algebraic iso-
morphism between two Ellis semigroups an Ellis morphism? Indeed,
this work was motivated by the fact that the Thue-Morse shift and the
Rudin-Shapiro shift have algebraically isomorphic Ellis semigroups, but
that the shifts are not conjugate. A direct check that the algebraic iso-
morphism is continuous seems out of reach, since the Ellis semigroups
are not tame and have very complicated topologies. As the class of dy-
namical systems for which the Ellis semigroup is a complete invariant
contains the two shifts, our results show that the algebraic isomorphism
between the Ellis semigroups of the Thue-Morse and the Rudin-Shapiro

2. Preliminaries
2.1. Dynamical systems. Throughout this work, T is an abelian
group. We look at T-actions (X, σ, T) on a compact Hausdorff space
X. If T is understood we write (X, σ) and if both σ and T are un-
derstood then we write X. If X is metrisable and d a metric on X
which generates its topology, a pair of points x, x′ ∈X are proxi-
mal if inft∈T d(σt(x), σt(x′)) = 0. This notion can also be formulated
for Hausdorff compact spaces by using uniformity structures [3]. An
action is distal if the proximality relation is trivial in the sense that
distinct points are never proximal. We make it a standing assumption
that our actions are not distal.
A dynamical system (X, σ) is called equicontinous if the family of
homeomorphisms {σt : t ∈T} is equicontinuous. If the action is tran-
sitive then this is the case if and only if, for any choice of x0 ∈X there
is an abelian group structure on X (denoted additively) such that x0 is
the identity element and σt(x) = x + σt(x0) −x0. This group structure
is topological.
An equicontinuous factor of (X, σ) is a factor π : (X, σ) →(Y, δ)
such that (Y, δ) is equicontinuous. An equicontinuous factor of (X, T)
is maximal if any other equicontinuous factor of (X, T) factors through
it. Maximal equicontinuous factors (MEFs) always exist, and in this
paper we will denote the maximal equicontinuous space by Xeq and the
factor map by πeq. Given ξ ∈Xeq we call π−1
eq (ξ) the fibre of ξ. It turns
out that two proximal points are mapped to the same point under πeq,
or stated differently, they belong to the same fibre, see, eg, [2].
A point ξ ∈Xeq is called singular if the fibre π−1

two proximal points, otherwise it is called regular. The assumption

2.2. Matrix semigroups. No semigroup discussed in this work has
a zero element. A semigroup is called completely simple if it does not
admit a proper bilateral ideal and contains an idempotent. This implies
that the semigroup is a disjoint union of isomorphic groups. The way
that multiplication of elements in different groups occurs is encoded in
a matrix. This is the result of the Rees-Suskevitch structure theorem
which characterises completely simple semigroups as those which are
isomorphic to matrix semigroups [16].
Matrix semigroups are defined as follows. Let G be a group, let I
and Λ be non-empty sets, and let A = (aλi)λ∈Λ,i∈I be a Λ × I matrix
whose entries lie in G. Then the matrix semigroup M[G; I, Λ; A] is the
set I × G × Λ together with the multiplication
(i, g, λ)(j, h, µ) = (i, gaλjh, µ).
The matrix A is called the sandwich matrix and the group G is called
the structure group. The little structure group of M[G; I, Λ; A] is the
subgroup Γ of G generated by the entries of the sandwich matrix A.

2.3. Ellis semigroup. A compact topological semigroup is a semi-
group endowed with a compact topology, and such that multiplication
(x, y) →xy is jointly continuous. A compact right-topological semi-
group is a semigroup endowed with a compact topology, and such that
right multiplication is continuous. Given a set X we let F(X) denote
the set of functions from X to itself with the topology of pointwise
convergence. Composition gives it the structure of a right-topological
semigroup. If X is compact then F(X) is a compact right-topological
semigroup.
It is a compact topological semigroup if and only if X
is finite. Given a dynamical system (X, σ, T), the family of homeo-
morphisms {σt|t ∈T} is a subsemigroup of F(X).
The closure of
{σt|t ∈T}, denoted E(X, σ, T), or simply E(X) if the rest is under-
stood, is still a semigroup, called the Ellis semigroup of the dynamical
system. Since X is compact E(X, σ, T) is a compact right-topological
semigroup, by construction. It is also a dynamical system, with the
action σE : E(X) →E(X) defined by σt
E(f) := σt ◦f. The system
(E(X), σ, T) is generally not minimal which is one of the reasons why
we will work with a minimal left ideal L of E(X).
The Ellis semigroup is closely related to the proximality relation [3,
Chapter 3, Proposition 8]. Two points x and y are proximal if and only
if there exists f ∈E(X) such that f(x) = f(y). In particular we see
that, given any idempotent p ∈E(X) and x ∈X, the points p(x) and
x are proximal.
Since the elements of E(X) are limits of generalised sequences of
powers of σ, the automorphism group, viewed as a subgroup of F(X),
lies in the commutant of E(X).

5. Examples
We provide examples to which our theory can be applied. This is to
say that inside the class of these examples we can say that two systems
are conjugate if and only if their Ellis semigroups are related by an
Ellis isomorphism. As Ellis semigroups are usually more complicated
than dynamical systems, the strength of our result does not so much
lie in the fact that we can prove that two systems are conjugate or not
by comparing their Ellis semigroups. But it is interesting to see that
there are non-conjugate dynamical systems which have algebraically
isomorphic Ellis semigroups. Our theory then tells us that the algebraic
isomorphism cannot be continuous.
Many of our examples are substitutional dynamical systems. For a
brief recap of these systems, see Section 6.2.
