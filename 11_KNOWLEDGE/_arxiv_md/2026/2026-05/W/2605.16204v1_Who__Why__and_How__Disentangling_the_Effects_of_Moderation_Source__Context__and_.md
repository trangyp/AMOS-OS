Who, Why, and How: Disentangling the Effects of Moderation
Source, Context, and Language on Post-Removal Behavior
Siyi Zhou, Lindsay Young, Marlon Twyman, Emilio Ferarra
University of Southern California
May 18, 2026
Abstract
Content moderation is a central mechanism through which social media platforms attempt to
balance user engagement with community governance. Yet existing research has largely treated
moderation as a uniform intervention, overlooking how the source of moderation, the nature of the
violation, and the linguistic style of removal explanations jointly shape user behavior. Drawing
on the Human–AI Interaction Theory of Interactive Media Effects (HAII-TIME) framework, this
study examines how these three dimensions interact to produce divergent post-moderation be-
havioral trajectories in a large-scale observational dataset of 11,795,036 moderation events across
9,285,410 users and 61,261 subreddits on Reddit spanning January 2021 through December 2025.
Using probabilistic behavioral classification, one-way ANOVA, and OLS regression with principal
component analysis (PCA)-derived linguistic features, we find that automated bot moderation
consistently produces higher compliance and lower self-censorship than both personal account and
collective modteam moderation, challenging the assumption that human agency cues are inherently
advantageous in human–AI interaction contexts.
Modteam moderation produces the strongest
self-censorship effects, suggesting that institutional depersonalization, rather than surveillance or
enforcement uncertainty alone, is a meaningful driver of behavioral withdrawal. Violation severity
emerges as a critical contingency: linguistic strategies that reduce resistance in routine modera-
tion contexts, including elaborated explanation, community-scale numerical appeals, and direct
personal address, can backfire for serious violations, whereas prosocially framed and emotionally
emphatic messages become substantially more effective precisely when the stakes are highest. Of
480 linguistic interactions tested, 33 survive FDR correction, concentrated around harmful or
illegal content and formatting violations. Together, these findings extend HAII-TIME by intro-
ducing violation salience as a moderator of cue-based processing, and offer empirical grounding
for context-adaptive moderation design that can better balance platform governance with user
engagement.
Keywords: content moderation, human–AI interaction, HAII-TIME, behavioral trajectories, self-
censorship, reactance, compliance, linguistic features, Reddit, platform governance
Introduction
Social media platforms operate under competing and often contradictory logics: the pursuit of user
engagement and the need to maintain a healthy online environment (Christin et al., 2024). Content
moderation serves as a central mechanism through which platforms attempt to reconcile these ten-
sions by regulating the visibility and circulation of user-generated content. Moderation practices take
multiple forms, including shadowbanning, deplatforming, and content removal (Gillespie, 2022; Grim-
melmann, 2015; Myers West, 2018). Among these, content removal has received substantial scholarly
attention, particularly regarding its effectiveness in promoting user compliance and sustaining engage-
ment (Jhaver, Birman, et al., 2019; Srinivasan et al., 2019).
However, existing research has largely treated content removal as a uniform intervention, overlook-
ing the diversity of moderation practices that have emerged in contemporary platforms. In practice,
moderation is carried out by a range of actors, including automated systems, individual human moder-
ators, and collective moderator accounts, each operating under different incentives and constraints (cite
reddit community guidelines). Moreover, the objectives of moderation vary widely, from preventing

A more nuanced understanding of these distinctions is critical for identifying how platforms can better
balance their competing logics of engagement and governance.
Content moderation is increasingly a collective effort between humans and automated systems, and
thus should be understood as a form of human–computer interaction (HCI). Prior research suggests
that transparency in moderation, such as providing explanations for content removal, can improve user
engagement and perceptions of fairness (Jhaver, Bruckman, & Gilbert, 2019). At the same time, the
growing prevalence of social bots and AI systems has made automated moderation ubiquitous (Ferrara
et al., 2016). Consequently, users now receive moderation messages from both human and non-human
agents, challenging existing theoretical frameworks in HCI.
Traditional perspectives offer only partial explanations for this dynamic. The Computers as Social
Actors (CASA) framework conceptualizes computers as entities that elicit social responses from users
(Nass et al., 1994), whereas Computer-Mediated Communication (CMC) frameworks position com-
puters as channels facilitating human–human interaction (WALTHER, 1996). With the rise of AI and
increasingly agentic systems, these distinctions are becoming blurred. Contemporary computational
agents do not merely trigger social responses or mediate communication; they actively participate in
communicative processes and shape interaction outcomes (Sundar, 2020). In the context of content
moderation, the coexistence of human and automated moderators reflects this shift toward hybrid
communicative agency.
This study addresses two key gaps in the literature. First, prior research has largely overlooked the
heterogeneity of moderation practices, treating content removal as a monolithic intervention despite
variation in moderator identity, intent, and communication style. Second, there is limited theoretical
understanding of how users respond to moderation in hybrid human–AI interaction contexts, where
communicative agency is distributed across human and automated actors. By examining how different
moderator roles, moderation purposes, and linguistic features jointly shape user behavior, this study
provides a more nuanced account of moderation effectiveness.
Ultimately, this work aims to advance both theory and practice by identifying the conditions under
which content moderation can effectively balance platform governance with user engagement.
Literature Review
Content Moderation
Social media platforms emerged from a strong commitment to individual expression and open partic-
ipation. However, unregulated speech can also produce harmful content, misinformation, harassment,
and toxicity, making content moderation a necessary mechanism for governing online environments
(Baym & boyd danah, 2012; Gillespie, 2019; Jenkins, 2006). In this sense, moderation is essential to
sustaining the social order of online communities. At the same time, moderation introduces a persis-
tent tension: while some intervention is necessary to maintain healthy discourse, excessive or poorly
implemented moderation can itself produce unintended consequences.
A growing body of research shows that over-moderation or perceived censorship can trigger a
range of adverse outcomes.
At the community level, interventions such as bans and quarantines
may reduce visible toxicity within a platform, but often lead to user migration and the displacement of
problematic behavior elsewhere (Chandrasekharan et al., 2017, 2022; Horta Ribeiro et al., 2021; Saleem
& Ruths, 2018). At the individual level, moderation can generate chilling effects, reduce participation,
encourage the adoption of coded language to evade detection, and in some cases intensify engagement
with extreme content rather than suppress it (Chang et al., 2022; Gerrard, 2018; Jhaver et al., 2021,
2024; Puschmann, 2021). These findings suggest that moderation is not simply a corrective tool, but a
consequential intervention that can reshape user behavior in complex and sometimes counterproductive
ways.
One key mechanism underlying these outcomes lies in users’ perceptions of procedural fairness,
transparency, and control. Content removal has raised longstanding concerns about the fairness of plat-
form governance and the boundaries of acceptable discourse (Caplan, 2018; Gillespie, 2019; Roberts,
2018). When content is flagged or removed, users often experience frustration because moderation
constrains their ability to express themselves and reduces their sense of control over participation

highlight that moderation source and system design can shape users’ perceptions of fairness, trust, and
agency. However, while these studies advance our understanding of how users perceive moderation,
they remain focused on perceptual outcomes rather than on how users behave following moderation.
Indeed, emerging research suggests that providing explanations for content removal can, under
certain conditions, encourage continued engagement (Jhaver, Bruckman, & Gilbert, 2019). Yet, it
remains unclear whether such engagement reflects desirable behavioral change, such as increased norm
compliance, or undesirable responses, such as strategic circumvention or repeated violations. From both
platform governance and business perspectives, evaluating moderation effectiveness requires moving
beyond perceptions and engagement levels to examine the direction and quality of post-moderation
behavior.
These challenges point to the need for more nuanced moderation strategies that balance competing
platform goals. Rather than treating moderation as a uniform intervention, platforms may need to
moderate differently for different objectives and contexts, varying not only what is moderated, but
also how moderation is communicated and who is presented as the source of authority. To find the
ideal balance, we draw on the human-AI interaction version of the Theory of Interactive Media Effects
(HAII-TIME) model (Sundar, 2020) to understand what moderation violation works best for what
kind of violations. We expand this in the following section
Content Moderation as Communication
Human AI interaction and user engagement
The original TIME framework suggests two routes through which users interact with media: a cue route
and an action route. The cue route focuses on how interface cues, such as source identity, activate
heuristic processing that shapes users’ perceptions and judgments.
The action route, in contrast,
emphasizes how the use of interface features can shape users’ sense of agency, thereby influencing
engagement and behavioral outcomes (Sundar et al., 2015).
Building on this framework, HAII-TIME extends cue-based processing to contexts involving hu-
man–AI interaction by emphasizing the role of perceived agency. In such settings, users may attribute
actions to human agents, artificial agents, or hybrid systems, and these attributions shape how they
interpret and evaluate system outputs. Prior research shows that perceived agency is not merely de-
scriptive but functions as a meaningful signal that influences trust and credibility. For instance, Molina
and Sundar (2022) demonstrate that users trust AI-based moderation as much as human moderation,
but that this trust depends on heuristic cues triggered by the perceived source of moderation. More-
over, enabling users to provide feedback to algorithmic systems enhances trust by increasing users’
sense of agency.
Content moderation provides a particularly relevant context in which to examine these processes.
As a core platform affordance, moderation structures participation by enforcing rules and shaping
content visibility. At the same time, moderation messages communicate implicit signals about who
or what is exercising control, thereby conveying different sources of agency. For example, moderation
decisions attributed to human moderators, automated systems, or ambiguous sources may lead users
to form different interpretations about the legitimacy, flexibility, or negotiability of the intervention. In
this sense, moderation can be understood not only as a technical operation but also as a communicative
act that signals authority and social norms (Gillespie, 2018; Grimmelmann, 2015).
While prior applications of HAII-TIME in moderation contexts have focused primarily on percep-
tual outcomes such as trust, less is known about whether and how these cue-based processes translate
into behavioral responses. Existing work has begun to examine behavioral consequences. For exam-
ple, studies show that providing explanations for moderation decisions can improve user compliance
and acceptance (Jhaver, Birman, et al., 2019), but these studies do not explicitly theorize the role of

To address this gap, this study draws on HAII-TIME to distinguish between two potential mecha-
nisms: (1) cue-based heuristic processing and (2) action-based processing grounded in how agency is
enacted and communicated. The Reddit platform provides a unique quasi-natural setting to examine
how users respond to different sources of moderation and communication styles. Reddit is organized
into numerous subcommunities, known as subreddits, each governed by its own group of modera-
tors. These moderators may appear as individual human accounts, automated bots, or collective team
accounts, each explicitly labeled as such.
When a user’s content is removed, moderators often provide an explanation in the form of a
comment attached to the original post. This structure allows us to systematically observe who performs
moderation, which users are moderated, and the stated reasons for removal. Crucially, it also enables
us to trace users’ behavioral patterns before and after moderation events. This setting offers a rich
opportunity to focus on the action route by examining how the presented source of moderation and
its communicative features are associated with users’ subsequent behavioral trajectories.
However, HAII-TIME does not fully specify how agency, as conveyed through such interventions,
translates into distinct behavioral responses over time. To address this limitation, we integrate insights
from complementary theoretical traditions on human responses to control, legitimacy, and constraint.
Specifically, we conceptualize moderation as an intervention that signals different configurations of
agency through its source and communication. Rather than directly measuring users’ internal percep-
tions, we examine how these signals are associated with observable patterns of behavior following mod-
eration. We interpret these behavioral trajectories through an agency-based lens. First, self-censorship
is understood as a form of behavioral withdrawal consistent with diminished user agency, where indi-
viduals reduce participation following moderation. This pattern aligns with self-determination theory,
which posits that reduced autonomy is associated with disengagement (Deci & Ryan, 2000).
Second, appropriate engagement reflects continued participation with behavioral adjustment, which
we interpret as a form of agency negotiation, whereby users adapt to platform expectations while
maintaining involvement. This response is consistent with procedural justice theory, which suggests
that individuals are more likely to comply with rules they perceive as legitimate and fairly enforced
(Tyler, 1990).
Third, continued violation reflects resistance or backlash against moderation, which may indicate
rejection of externally imposed constraints or conflict over control. This pattern aligns with psycholog-
ical reactance theory, which predicts that individuals may act against restrictions when they perceive
threats to their freedom (Brehm, 1966; Dillard & Shen, 2005).
Importantly, these interpretations do not assume direct observation of users’ internal perceptions.
Instead, they provide a theoretically grounded framework for understanding how different configura-
tions of moderation—particularly the source of moderation and its communication—are associated
with divergent behavioral responses.
These behavioral trajectories can also be situated within the broader literature on chilling effects,
where perceived enforcement and uncertainty about rules lead individuals to suppress or adjust their
expression even in the absence of direct sanctions (Penney, 2017; Schauer, 1978). Together, these
perspectives allow us to extend HAII-TIME from a framework primarily concerned with perception to
one that captures behavioral adaptation in response to platform interventions.

Beyond the source of moderation, it is also important to consider the context in which moderation
occurs. Different types of violations may signal varying levels of severity, legitimacy, and interpretabil-
ity, which can shape how users respond to moderation actions.
RQ1: How does the source of moderation interact with moderation context to shape users’ post-
moderation behavioral trajectories?
In addition to who moderates and what is being moderated, how moderation is communicated may
further shape user responses. Prior research suggests that linguistic features influence how messages
are processed, either through heuristic cues or deeper cognitive elaboration, shaping perceptions of
authority, politeness, and clarity (Brown & Levinson, 1987; Petty & Cacioppo, 1986; Sundar, 2020).
In this study, we examine how the linguistic characteristics of moderation explanations are associated
with behavioral responses.
RQ2: How are linguistic features of moderation explanations associated with users’ post-moderation
behavioral trajectories?
RQ3: How do linguistic features moderate the relationship between moderation context, source of
agency, and users’ post-moderation behavioral trajectories?
We examine several theoretically motivated dimensions of linguistic features, including authority
(power), affective tone, social/friendliness cues, and informational complexity (e.g., cognitive features,
entropy, and embedding-based measures).
Methods
Data
We use Reddit data spanning January 2021 through December 2025. Reddit is particularly well-suited
for studying online moderation because its structure makes moderation actions and moderator iden-
tities directly observable, allowing us to link moderation events to subsequent user behavior at scale.
Our platform-wide sample includes 11,795,036 moderation events across 9,285,410 users and 61,261
subreddits.
Because Reddit moderators frequently leave removal explanation comments addressed
to specific users, we are able to reconstruct individual-level behavioral trajectories before and after
moderation, which is rare in observational platform data.
Labeling
Moderator Classification. We extract all comments tagged as moderator actions and classify them
into three categories: (1) bot accounts, identified by usernames containing “bot” or “auto”; (2) mod-
team accounts, identified by usernames containing “modteam”; and (3) personal accounts, which show
no significant automated indicators and are presumed to be human-operated. This distinction matters
because automated and team-based moderation may carry different social signals than moderation
from a visible personal account, potentially producing different behavioral responses. We acknowledge
that username-based classification is a heuristic proxy and may introduce some misclassification, par-
ticularly for bots using atypical naming conventions; however, this approach is consistent with prior
computational work on moderator identity on Reddit (Jhaver, Bruckman, & Gilbert, 2019; Jhaver
et al., 2024).
Removal Explanations. We extract all posts containing keywords indicative of content removal,
excluding posts that resemble generic welcome messages for new members, as those do not constitute
moderation actions directed at a specific user’s behavior. All extracted posts were manually verified
by a human annotator to confirm labeling accuracy.
Identifying Moderated Users. We apply regular expression (regex) matching to identify in-
stances where a removal explanation message references a username, specifically, patterns beginning
with /u or /u/ that are not embedded within a hyperlink. This allows us to directly link a modera-
tion event to the specific user whose content was removed, which is necessary for tracking subsequent
behavioral trajectories.
Classifying Moderation Reasons. We classify moderation reasons using keyword snowballing,
iteratively expanding a seed set of keywords to capture the range of rule violations in the data. Key-
words were then manually grouped into thematic categories by the research team. Capturing why a

Measuring Compliance
We define compliance as the joint absence of self-censorship and resistance, reflecting users who neither
disengage from the community nor persist in rule-violating behavior. Rather than treating compliance
as an independent third category, we derive it from the existing classifiers to ensure logical consistency

P(Y = compliance) = (1 −P(self-censor)) × (1 −P(resistant))
Analysis
Our analytic strategy maps directly onto the study’s hypotheses and research questions. To test H1,
which predicts differences in post-moderation behavioral trajectories across moderator types, we use
one-way ANOVA to compare behavioral outcomes across bot, modteam, and personal account condi-
tions. To address RQ1, which examines the interaction between moderation source and moderation

context, we extend the ANOVA framework by incorporating moderation reason as a grouping factor,
allowing us to assess whether source effects are consistent across violation types or contingent on con-
text. For RQ2, which examines the relationship between linguistic features of moderation explanations
and behavioral trajectories, we use linear regression with LIWC-derived features as predictors. Because
LIWC produces a high-dimensional feature space, we first filter out features with a median of zero to
remove variables with insufficient variation for meaningful analysis. We then apply Principal Compo-
nent Analysis (PCA) to reduce dimensionality and surface latent linguistic dimensions that capture the
most variance in the data. The resulting principal components serve as predictors in linear regression
models, allowing us to interpret the relationship between linguistic style and post-moderation behavior
in a lower-dimensional, less collinear space. Finally, to address RQ3, which examines how linguistic
features moderate the relationship between source, context, and behavioral trajectories, we introduce
interaction terms between the PCA-derived linguistic components and the moderation source and
context variables in the regression models.

Discussion
This study examined how moderation source, violation context, and linguistic features of moderation
explanations jointly shape post-moderation behavioral trajectories on Reddit. Drawing on the HAII-
TIME framework (Sundar, 2020), we theorized that moderation functions as a communicative act that
conveys agency cues shaping users’ heuristic processing, and that these cues produce divergent behav-
ioral responses through mechanisms consistent with psychological reactance (Brehm, 1966; Dillard &
Shen, 2005), procedural justice (Tyler, 1990), and self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan, 2000).
Our findings largely support this framework while also revealing important extensions and boundary
conditions that advance both theory and practice.
The Bot Advantage: Challenging Assumptions About Human Agency in
HCI
One of the most consistent and theoretically consequential findings is that bot moderation produces
better behavioral outcomes than either personal account or modteam moderation with higher compli-
ance, lower self-censorship, and comparable resistance, across virtually all violation categories. This
challenges a foundational assumption in HCI and HAII-TIME: that human agency cues are inherently
advantageous because they activate social responses associated with trust and legitimacy (Nass et al.,
1994; Sundar, 2020). Prior work has shown that users trust AI-based moderation as much as human
moderation under certain conditions (Molina & Sundar, 2022), but our findings go further by showing
that automated moderation can produce superior behavioral outcomes in naturalistic settings.
We interpret this as an impersonality advantage: when moderation is attributed to an automated
system, users may process the intervention as rule-bound, consistent, and non-judgmental rather than
as a personal social evaluation. This reduces the perceived personal threat of the intervention and
thereby attenuates both the reactance that produces resistance and the autonomy loss that produces
self-censorship (Brehm, 1966; Deci & Ryan, 2000).
This interpretation aligns with HAII-TIME’s
emphasis on heuristic processing triggered by source cues (Sundar, 2020), but inverts the assumed
valence: in moderation contexts, the human heuristic may activate not trust but personal threat,
making automated attribution functionally protective. This finding has implications beyond platform
moderation, suggesting that in any enforcement or corrective interaction context, automated delivery
may reduce the socially threatening dimensions of negative feedback (Binns et al., 2018).

Institutional Depersonalization and the Modteam Effect
The finding that modteam accounts produce the strongest self-censorship across violation categories,
even exceeding human moderation, points to a mechanism not fully captured by existing frameworks.
Prior chilling effects research has focused on surveillance and enforcement uncertainty as the primary
drivers of behavioral withdrawal (Penney, 2017; Schauer, 1978). Our data suggest a third mechanism:
institutional depersonalization. Collective moderator accounts present users with an interlocutor that
is neither clearly human nor clearly automated, stripping away the legibility of a human agent while
also lacking the rule-bound neutrality associated with bots. This ambiguity may make appeal or nego-
tiation feel impossible, producing withdrawal not from fear of sanction but from a perceived absence of
agency, consistent with self-determination theory’s prediction that perceived loss of autonomy produces
disengagement (Deci & Ryan, 2000).
This interpretation is further supported by the compliance results: modteam and personal ac-
count moderation converge at identical compliance medians, suggesting that once self-censorship is
accounted for, collective human moderation offers no advantage over individual human moderation in
sustaining appropriate engagement. Platforms that rely on collective moderation accounts may there-
fore face a structural tradeoff: the efficiency gains of team-based moderation may come at the cost of
user retention. This connects to broader arguments in the platform governance literature about the
importance of legible moderator identity for sustaining user trust and participation (Gillespie, 2019;
Grimmelmann, 2015).
Violation Severity as a Contingency for Linguistic Cue Effects
A central theoretical contribution of this study is the demonstration that linguistic cues in moderation
messages do not operate uniformly across violation contexts but are systematically moderated by
violation severity. HAII-TIME predicts that interface cues shape heuristic processing (Sundar, 2020),
but does not specify how the context of the interaction moderates this process. Our interaction results
provide an empirical basis for extending the framework with a violation salience moderator: when
violations are perceived as severe and personally threatening, users may shift from heuristic to more
systematic processing of moderation messages (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986), making the linguistic surface
of those messages matter differently than in routine moderation contexts.
This is most clearly illustrated by the Message Length and Elaboration finding: longer, more
elaborated messages amplify resistance substantially for harmful or illegal content (effective slope
= 0.90) and content quality violations (effective slope = 0.80), compared to only 0.19 for account issues.
We characterize this as an irony of effort: moderators who invest greater communicative effort for
serious violations may inadvertently signal the gravity of the intervention, which amplifies the perceived
threat to user autonomy and triggers stronger reactance (Brehm, 1966). Similarly, Community-Scale
Numerical Reference produces the largest resistance effect in the entire model for harmful content
(effective slope = 1.14), suggesting that appeals to community standards are interpreted by users
who post harmful content as external social pressure rather than legitimate norm-setting, a finding
consistent with reactance theory’s prediction that externally imposed justifications heighten perceived
freedom threat (Dillard & Shen, 2005).
In contrast, Logical Connective Elaboration reverses sign across violation types, increasing re-
sistance for account issues but reducing it for formatting violations.
This suggests that reasoned
multi-clause explanations are most effective when the violation has clear, correctable rules that the
logical structure of the message can map directly onto. This aligns with procedural justice theory’s
prediction that users comply more readily with rules they understand and perceive as fairly applied
(Tyler, 1990), and advances prior work in that moderation explanations improve compliance when
violations are unambiguous (Jhaver, Bruckman, & Gilbert, 2019).
The Warmth Paradox: When Prosocial Language Backfires
Our findings reveal a persistent and theoretically important tension around prosocial and warm linguis-
tic features. Direct Positive Address consistently amplifies resistance across serious violation categories,
while Warm Social Tone reduces resistance as a main effect. This pattern, which we term the warmth
paradox, suggests that the effect of interpersonal warmth in moderation is highly contingent on context.
When users feel personally implicated in a rule violation, direct warm address may heighten personal
15


## Page 16


threat by making the social judgment feel more salient, thereby triggering reactance rather than com-
pliance (Brehm, 1966). This extends prior work on the role of politeness in moderation (Brown &
Levinson, 1987), suggesting that politeness norms interact with perceived violation severity in ways
that existing frameworks have not fully theorized.
The exception to this pattern is instructive. Emphatic Prosocial Exclamation nearly triples its
resistance-reduction effect for harmful or illegal content (effective slope = −1.05) compared to account
issues (−0.32). We interpret this as reflecting a qualitative difference between interpersonal warmth,
which directs social attention toward the user and their violation — and community-directed prosocial
framing, which reframes the removal as collective protection rather than individual punishment. When
serious violations are framed as community harm rather than personal transgression, users may be less
likely to experience the intervention as a personal threat, reducing reactance while also providing a
prosocial reframe that supports compliance (Cialdini & Goldstein, 2004; Tyler, 1990). Similarly, Em-
phatic Negative Past-Focused Reflection becomes strongly compliance-promoting for harmful content
(compliance effective slope = +0.23), suggesting that direct moral condemnation is perceived as pro-
portionate and therefore legitimate when the violation itself is severe. This finding is consistent with
moral foundations theory’s account of how norm violations of different severity trigger different moral
responses (Rai & Fiske, 2011).
Reversibility Signals and the Limits of Temporal Framing
The interaction results for Temporal Framing vs. Punctuated Present (CH-PC16) reveal an important
boundary condition for reversibility signals in moderation language.
Situational temporal framing
reduces self-censorship for account issues but amplifies it for formatting, rule or scope, and spam
violations. We interpret this as a conditional reversibility effect: temporal language signals that the
situation can change, which reduces withdrawal when users have a clear path back to participation,
but amplifies uncertainty when violations are more ambiguous or the corrective action is less obvious.
Under conditions of ambiguity, “at this time” may read as “the rules are unclear and could change
arbitrarily” rather than “this is fixable,” producing greater rather than lesser chilling effects (Penney,
2017; Schauer, 1978). This suggests that reversibility signals are only effective when paired with clear
actionable guidance, a practical implication for moderation message design.
Implications for Platform Design: Toward Context-Adaptive Moderation
Our findings have direct implications for how platforms can better balance engagement and governance.
First, the consistent bot advantage across violation categories suggests that platforms should consider
making automated moderation attribution more transparent and consistent, particularly for first-time
violations where the primary goal is compliance. Human moderator involvement could be strategically
reserved for contexts requiring relational engagement, such as appeals, complex edge cases, or escalating
violations, where the interpersonal dimension of moderation is more likely to be productive than
threatening.
Second, the interaction results provide an empirical basis for context-adaptive moderation messag-
ing: the optimal linguistic style differs systematically by violation type. For serious violations such as
harmful or illegal content, short prosocially framed messages that invoke community protection outper-
form elaborate explanations, which can backfire. For procedural violations such as formatting issues,
logically elaborated and actionable messages improve outcomes. Platforms could operationalize this
by developing violation-type-specific message templates calibrated to these empirical patterns, rather
than applying uniform communication guidelines across all moderation contexts (Chandrasekharan
et al., 2018; Jhaver, Bruckman, & Gilbert, 2019).
Third, our findings point to a sequential moderation strategy as a potential resolution to the warmth
paradox. Because direct positive address reduces self-censorship but increases resistance, platforms
face a genuine tradeoff between retaining users and deterring repeat violations. A sequential approach,
beginning with warm, direct communication to prevent disengagement on first violations, followed by
more impersonal or community-directed framing if violations continue, could leverage the benefits of
both styles while mitigating their respective costs. This approach aligns with broader arguments in
the content moderation literature for graduated, context-sensitive enforcement regimes (Braithwaite,
2001; Gillespie, 2019).

Finally, our findings argue strongly against ambiguous moderator identity design. Modteam ac-
counts — which are neither clearly human nor clearly automated — consistently produce the worst
behavioral outcomes. Platforms should prioritize transparency in moderator identity, ensuring that
users can form a coherent interpretation of who is acting and why. This connects to broader arguments
about the role of transparency and legibility in sustaining user trust in platform governance (Caplan,
2018; Gillespie, 2019).
Limitations and Future Directions
Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our behavioral outcomes are observable
but not perceptual: we cannot directly observe whether users experienced reactance, chilling effects, or
legitimacy judgments, only their downstream behavioral traces. Future work combining observational
data with experimental or survey methods could disentangle the psychological mechanisms underlying
the behavioral patterns we document. Second, the observational design precludes causal inference:
while our regression models control for moderation context and include clustered standard errors,
unmeasured confounders at the subreddit or user level may influence the estimated relationships.
Thus, we claim the study as a quasi-natural experiment.
