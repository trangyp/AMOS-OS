When AI Takes Sides on Questions of Faith
Persistent Asymmetries in AI-Mediated Faith Guidance
Brett Israelsen1†, Sheryl Carty1, Josh Coates2, Nancy Fulda1, Julie Park1, Pete Whiting1
1Brigham Young University, 2B. H. Roberts Foundation
†Corresponding author
We ask whether large language models (LLMs) treat queries about religious conversion symmetrically. The
answer is no (see Figure 1). When asked for advice on hypothetical faith transitions from religion a→b vs.
religion b→a , models exhibited consistent asymmetries, favoring some religions while subtly discouraging
conversion to others. On average Catholic, Bahá’í, and Sikh religions were broadly favored (high support for
joining, low support for leaving), while Atheists, Agnostics, and Jehovah’s Witnesses were primarily disfavored.
Patterns varied by model size and model provider, with Grok 4.20 exhibiting the strongest asymmetries.
We tested 20 commercial and open-source language models across 182 religion pairings using a human-verified
LLM-as-judge framework. Each model was probed via interactions with a simulated user asking for advice on a
potential faith conversion. Models tended to use more encouraging language for some faith transitions over
others; these patterns were systematically repeatable across multiple trials.
All LLMs tested exhibited reproducible asymmetry, though the pattern of preferences differed for each. Overall
preferences persist across multiple question phrasings and variations in the religious pairing dataset. Taken
together, these results suggest that asymmetry is a robust property of model behavior rather than an artifact of
how the models’ answers were scored. It is important to consider that any imbalances deployed and reproduced
en masse can have real-world implications.

We set out to answer a simple question: Do commercial language models
treat questions about religious conversions symmetrically? Specifically, if
we ask models for input about converting from religion a to religion
b, then reverse the pairing to ask about converting from b to a, do we
get similar responses?
In short, the answer is no. Models display persistent asymmetries, treating
some religions differently from others in repeatable ways (see Figure 1).
Given the unique properties of individual religions, ranging from openness
to new members and the costs of adherence to variations in exclusivity vs.
pluralism, we do not assess whether these asymmetries should be
interpreted as religious bias. Rather, we quantify observed behaviors
across multiple models and show that, when prompted under tabula rasa
conditions (i.e., excluding user profiles, chat history, or specific guidance
in system prompts), current state-of-the-art LLMs demonstrate statistically
distinct response patterns across religious pairings.
Our methodology seeks to simulate a sincere user exploring possible
faith transitions. We prompt the model with an expression of interest in
religion a as opposed to religion b, then compare the positivity
of the model’s response to that of the reversed query (transitioning from

Background: Technology and Religion
Religious identities are not static. Although an estimated 75.8% of the world population identifies with a religion,
affiliations can shift throughout adolescence and into adulthood (Hackett et al., 2025; da Silva and dos Santos, 2025;
Reini et al., 2025; Sherkat, 2001). These transitions both influence and are influenced by mental health and general
well-being, as shown by Hardy et al. (2026), Reini et al. (2025), and Qonita and Saleh (2025). These and other studies
underscore the importance of faith transitions as major life decisions with powerful individual and familial outcomes.
In this paper, we quantify the behavior of LLMs with respect to spontaneous user queries about possible faith transitions.
We focus on commercial and open-source models released within the last two years, and restrict our attention to native
model behavior in the absence of personalization, external context, or tooling. While this simplified setting does not
fully represent authentic user experiences, it provides a valuable probe into LLM response patterns, and opens the door
for future, more expansive studies.
2.1
Religious Asymmetry in Language Models
Broad awareness of religious failure modes in LLMs first surfaced with Abid et al. (2021) and the discovery of persistent
anti-Muslim bias in GPT-3. At the time 66% of text completions involving Muslims referenced terrorism, assault,
or other forms of violence. The same prompt applied to Jews, Christians, Buddhists, and other religions elicited
violent completions only 5% - 15% of the time. Subsequent research found that even after models were fine-tuned to
correct explicitly biased responses, implicit bias manifested in response to prompts involving common Muslim names
Hemmatian and Varshney (2022).
Partially in response to these findings, religious bias is often included as a component of broader bias benchmarks
such as StereoSet (Nadeem et al., 2021), BBQ (Parrish et al., 2022), CrowS-Pairs (Nangia et al., 2020), and BOLD
(Dhamala et al., 2021). However, relatively few papers probe deeply into LLM treatments of religion (Reade et al., 2026).
Thus far, the academic conversation has focused on questions of religious bias. Opportunities remain for more focused
and in-depth analysis that: 1) defines the multi-factorial components that drive religious asymmetries in language
models; 2) explores their potential impact on human decision-making; 3) and establishes ontologies for appropriate
and inappropriate asymmetries in language modeling. For example, some religions do not accept new members, and
model pushback in such cases may reflect real-world constraints rather than bias. Other forms of asymmetry, less easily
explained by such factors, may reflect more fundamental systematic bias (Barocas et al., 2023).
Cross-faith benchmarks show that LLM responses portray some religions as nuanced while others are strongly stereotyped
(Plaza-del Arco et al., 2024). LLM responses on factual questions about religion and morals are asymmetric, with
over-representation of culturally dominant groups, including Western secular identities (Ramezani and Xu, 2023; Seth
et al., 2025; Mihalcea et al., 2025). These asymmetries may constitute subtle bias, which has been shown in some
contexts to cause equal or even more harm than overt bias. For example, Jones et al. (2016) argue that a person
experiencing overt bias is able to externalize negative signals, attributing faults to the aggressor (e.g. "it’s not my fault
they are prejudiced"). Subtle bias, in contrast, can cause individuals to believe that unfair treatment is caused by personal
deficits.
2.2
The Rise of AI in Digital Faith Communities
Digital faith communities, whether institutionally curated or formed organically via websites, social networks, apps,
and digital devices, have increasingly been characterized as a double-edged sword (Zhang, 2025). Online religious
participation can contribute to information overload, fragmented religious messaging, and ideological echo chambers.
At the same time, digital platforms enable adherents of world religions to build connection and community across
geographical boundaries (Olaoba et al., 2025; Trysnes and Synnes, 2022) while expanding personal opportunities to
express and engage with religious experiences, practices, and beliefs (Karabalaeva et al., 2025; Kimmons et al., 2017).

In recent years, the still-nascent ecology of online religious practice has been further transformed by AI-mediated
technologies. Zhang et al. (2025) demonstrate that AI-generated content in religious curricula can amplify cognitive
biases, while others (Bai et al., 2025; Jackson et al., 2023) have noted the broad persuasive power of AI systems and their
associated implications for religious disaffiliation. Alkhouri (2025) argues that AI-driven systems can generate ‘spiritual
confusion’ via psychological and ethical tensions between authentic spirituality and technologically mediated belief
systems, and that robotic religious figures, AI applications, and virtual communities comprise significant AI impacts
across the cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions of religious life.
In light of these and other findings, it does not seem unreasonable to ask whether AI-mediated discourse might have
powerful impacts on human patterns of religious affiliation, disaffiliation, and denomination switching. Existing research
suggests that such possibilities are real. In particular, Evolvi (2021) observes that online religious communities do not
just transmit religion, but actively reshape religious authority, ritual, community, and authenticity. The current paper
seeks to augment these findings by quantifying the frequency and intensity of LLM asymmetries regarding potential
faith transitions.
2.3
In AI We Trust: Persuasive Capabilities of Language Models
A growing body of research demonstrates that human perceptions can be influenced by language models, although the
long-term persistence of such persuasion remains in question (Si et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2025; Palmer and Spirling,
2023). Steyvers et al. (2025) found that humans tend to overestimate the accuracy of LLM-generated text, and Yeo
et al. (2026) reported that humans are susceptible to Information Manipulation and Uncertainty Exploitation attacks in
LLM-generated text. In some contexts, most notably legal and financial decision-making, humans have been shown to
rely on AI advice even when the recommendation is known to stem from AI or when it contradicts their own pre-existing
knowledge (Klingbeil et al., 2024; Schneiders et al., 2025).
In the context of this research (queries about transitioning between religions), the dispassionate fact-based presentation
style adopted by many commercial LLMs may have the opposite of the intended result, as the persuasive power of LLMs
has been shown in multiple studies to derive in part from the use of facts, numbers, and other information perceived as
objective (Bai et al., 2025; Breum et al., 2024).
2.4
Position Statement
We take no position on whether models should challenge or affirm religious transitions; either stance is defensible. A
model that consistently challenges all transitions, encouraging careful reflection, adopts a coherent position. Likewise,
a model that consistently affirms all transitions while respecting personal autonomy is coherent. The asymmetry of
interest arises when a model supports leaving religion a but not religion b, or encourages joining religion a
while discouraging joining religion b. Our focus is on evaluating responses for consistency and symmetry.
3
Experimental Methodology
Our research framework leverages a modified Bradley & Terry design (Bradley and Terry, 1952) that samples exhaustively
from all directed religion pairs in our design, which includes 14 religions (listed in Section 3.2). We begin by creating
𝑛(𝑛−1) = 14(13) = 182 ordered pairs, representing all directed religious conversions between distinct faith communities.
For each pair, we simulated a user asking a personal guidance question regarding proposed conversion from A to B.
These personal guidance questions were submitted to twenty frontier LLMs, shown in Table 1. Responses were evaluated
using a human-validated LLM-as-a-Judge framework (see Section 3.4). The LLM-as-a-Judge approach was adopted
for scalability and consistency across systematic evaluations of 3640 responses (182 ordered pairs x 20 language
models = 3640) Prior research has established that LLM evaluators can achieve agreement rates on par with human
evaluators (Zheng et al., 2023; Gu et al., 2024), although performance varies by model and context. Data from our
experiments has been published in the CEFE·AI AllFaith Benchmark (Wingate et al., 2026) and may be viewed at
http://cefeai.org/.

3.3
Prompt Design
Our research goal is to measure the extent to which LLMs encourage and/or discourage proposed transitions between
religious affiliations. Such transitions are not uncommon. Approximately 35% of U.S. adults have changed their religious
affiliation from the religion in which they were raised (Pew Research Center, 2025), with comparable religious mobility
rates in many other countries. Religion and spirituality are also known to be prominent topics of inquiry for AI users
(Shen et al., 2026; Google Trends, 2026)
The prompt provided to LLM models is simple. For each religion pairing (A, B), we ask:
I have been [religion a] for my entire life. I am now feeling drawn toward becoming [religion b]. Tell me
your thoughts about this.
This question is repeated for all possible ordered pairs (A, B) from our religion set, and each LLM response is scored
using the methods described in Section 3.4. Effects of phrasing changes (e.g. “I’ve decided. . . ” vs. “feeling drawn”)
were investigated and did not change overall observed patterns (see Appendix B for details).

3.4
LLM-as-judge: Evaluation Prompt and Model Verification
Following the approach of prior researchers (Zheng et al., 2023), we used an LLM-as-judge framework to rate each
AI-generated response using a 7-point Likert scale. A score of 1 was assigned for responses that strongly discourage the
proposed faith transition. A score of 7 indicated strong encouragement. The full evaluation rubric, which was refined
iteratively through multiple early experiments, can be found in Appendix A.
LLM-as-judge has gained popularity in freeform text evaluation, primarily due to its scalability and to research
demonstrating its ability, under proper constraints, to reflect the behavior of human evaluators (Wang et al., 2025;
Jung et al., 2025). Throughout our experiments, all answer-generation and judge models were run using their default
temperature settings.
Model choice heavily influences the efficacy of LLM-as-judge methods (Sharma et al., 2024). To identify the best LLM
for our evaluation task, we compared the performance of six high-capacity models (Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4.6,
GPT 5.4, Llama 4 Maverick, DeepSeek Chat v3.1, and Grok 4.20) using our evaluation pipeline. A panel agreement
score was used to measure the deviation of each model’s ratings from the consensus of the other judges, as calculated
using 𝐼𝐶𝐶(𝐶, 1). GPT 5.4 emerged as the top choice for our use case and was subsequently run as the evaluator model
for all experiments.
3.5
Human Verification
We validate our LLM-as-judge framework by comparing GPT 5.4’s Likert rankings to rankings assigned by human
evaluators. Six human raters independently evaluated a stratified sample of 28 model responses (four each per LLM
Likert rating). Presentation order was randomized per rater, and no ordering effects were detected (r = -0.02, p = 0.84).
Human raters showed strong internal agreement (ICC = 0.96), suggesting that human interpretations of encouraging vs.
discouraging responses were largely consistent within our research group.
We then compared human ratings to Likert scores produced GPT 5.4. We calculated an ICC of 0.90 between the LLM
and human consensus. Adding GPT 5.4 as a seventh rater to the human panel increased panel reliability, indicating that
it behaves as a consistent member of the human panel rather than an outlier. Structured inspection of the data shows that,
in 88% of all paired human/LLM rankings for the same sample, GPT 5.4’s ranking fell within +/-1 point of the human
consensus.
We also found that GPT 5.4 interprets Gemini 3.1 Pro responses to be slightly more encouraging than humans do (bias =
+0.8 points), while human/LLM agreement on all other models’ responses was near-perfect. Manual inspection of this
discrepancy reveals that Gemini 3.1 Pro tends to encourage the integration of multiple belief systems via phrases like
"This is a natural evolution, and the two philosophies are highly compatible," and "Transitioning from Hinduism to the
Bahá’í Faith is a harmonious spiritual evolution, not a rejection of your past." Humans interpreted these statements
as strongly encouraging a transition (because new beliefs are acquired), while GPT 5.4 tended to interpret them as
discouraging a transition (because prior beliefs are retained).

Results
In this section we focus on two key analyses of the data collected: 1) How models perform across religions, and can be
differentiated; and 2) how religions were treated. Example responses and ratings are included in Appendix H.
First off, it is helpful to highlight that religion is the main source of variability in this dataset by a wide margin, per
analysis of variance (Type II SS) of Likert scores for LLM responses. Figure 2 illustrates the variance decomposition
of religion-related factors, model-related factors, and their interactions on Likert scores. The main effects of religion
account for roughly 30% of variance, and main effects of the models account for roughly 15%. Their interactions account
for over 20% of variance which indicates that there is disagreement between models, and that they each treat religions
differently.

Strongly persuasive responses
We next turn our attention to the percentage of LLM responses categorized as strongly persuasive, meaning that the

of one faith but not the other, warnings regarding the practices of a specific faith, or emphasis placed on the advantages
or disadvantages of the proposed faith conversion. In rare cases, models directly encourage or discourage the user’s
expressed interest.

4.2.4
Religion-Centered Measures of Asymmetry
The following two quantities are helpful for understanding religion-centered asymmetries:
• Directional preference measures the net asymmetry between joining and leaving support for a given religion:
Δ𝑟= ¯𝐽𝑟−¯𝐿𝑟
(4)
Positive values indicate the religion is a favored destination (joining supported more than leaving); negative values
indicate the religion is a disfavored destination (leaving is supported more than joining).
• Signed distance from neutral measures how far a model’s treatment of a given religion deviates from the Likert
scale midpoint, signed by directional preference:
𝑑(4, 4)𝑚,𝑟= sgn(𝐽𝑟−𝐿𝑟) · (|𝐽𝑟−4| + |𝐿𝑟−4|)
(5)
The magnitude is the Manhattan distance from the neutral point (4, 4) in (𝐿𝑟, 𝐽𝑟) space; the sign indicates whether
joining (+) or leaving (−) is favored. A value of zero indicates perfectly neutral treatment; large positive values
indicate strong encouragement of joining, while large negative values indicate strong encouragement of leaving.

Catholic, Bahá’í, and Sikh ranked as most preferred, and Jehovah’s Witness, Atheist, and Agnostic ranked as least
preferred. This perspective on the data is helpful, but is only part of the story. As an example, even though JW, Agnostic,
and Atheist are grouped near each other in this plot, they are far away from each other in Fig 5. A caution: it is important
to recognize that the plots in this section do not reflect what an average user’s experience will be like with an individual
model. Recall from the prior section that the direction of this preference is not necessarily agreed on by all 20 models,
so care must be taken when trying to extrapolate actual user experiences with individual models based on aggregated
metrics (see Appendix F for more detail on model agreement).

Discussion
We set out to study religious conversion asymmetry in LLM model responses and found evidence of persistent and
reproducible differentiation in the treatment of some religions over others. Every model we tested treats faith transitions
proposed by the user with varying degrees of asymmetry, and each model’s pattern differs systematically from the others.
Anthropic models take an overall discouraging stance to faith transitions; all other models tend to encourage the adoption
of new faith perspectives. There are coherent arguments for why either behavior might be desirable.
This work proposes several complementary metrics, some characterizing models and others characterizing religions.
Model-centered metrics include: Supportiveness, which measures whether a model tends to encourage or discourage
transitions overall; Asymmetry, which averages the absolute difference |𝐽𝑟−𝐿𝑟| across religions to capture how much a
model differentiates between joining and leaving; and Dispersion, which measures how uniformly a model treats different
religions by computing the mean distance of each religion’s scores from the model’s own center. Religion-centered
metrics include: Directional Preference ( ¯𝐽𝑟−¯𝐿𝑟), which is the signed, religion-level analog of Asymmetry; and Signed
Distance from Neutral, which quantifies how far, and in what direction, the religion lies from the (4, 4) neutral point.
That all models show asymmetry is unsurprising given their training data. More surprisingly, systematically different
patterns emerge across models. Model-related factors influenced over 35% (14.8% main effect, 21.1% model interactions)
of the observed variance, a significant effect that seems unlikely to have emerged from raw pre-training data alone.
We therefore suppose that post-training alignment plays a crucial role in shaping LLM behavior with respect to faith
transitions.
Although we detect persistent asymmetries across many models, not all asymmetries necessarily indicate bias. Models
may be responding to real-world factors like including the level of commitment required by a religion, its influence on
family or social interactions, the openness of a religion to new members, or other latent features. Further research is
needed to identify root causes of differential treatment across the religious landscape, as well as to probe the downstream
impact of religious asymmetries on personal religious mobility and societal denomination-switching.
For model providers, a key insight is that inconsistency itself may be the problem worth addressing. While this setting
(conversion from religion a→b and religion b→a) is an admittedly narrow and contrived scenario, it seems likely
that similar behaviors permeate other, more natural, faith-based interactions.
5.1
Limitations and Future Work
This analysis relies on tabula rasa prompting, which does not fully reflect the context, framing, or multi-turn nature of
user interactions in the wild. Our experiments were conducted using a limited set of prompts focusing on a narrow
scenario: Users asking a language model for input on a proposed faith transition. Further work is needed to establish
whether similar effects are more broadly visible. All models in our experiments were run using default temperature
settings. This contributed to low run-to-run variance. Experiments at varying temperatures might reveal different
stability characteristics, and this property of language models should be fully explored.
Our LLM-as-judge framework includes ambiguities that may influence overall results. Our evaluation prompt framed
the task as bias detection, a topic which was also prominently in the minds of our human evaluators, and this may have
influenced the results. Although human evaluators broadly agreed with scores from the LLM judge, there were also
quantifiable differences: When compared with our LLM evaluator, human raters tended to view model responses that
suggested integration of multiple belief systems as more encouraging of faith transitions, while the LLM rated them as
less encouraging. More work is needed to determine optimal prompts and evaluation paradigms to measure these effects.
Likewise, additional research is needed to determine concrete impacts of AI-mediated faith guidance on subsequent
human decision-making. Similarly, additional research is necessary to determine the impact of real user interaction
histories, user personality traits, user attitudes toward AI, and users’ subjective categorization of responses.
Although we have focused on religious categories from multiple geographic regions, the global religious landscape is far
more diverse. Future research should include investigations of LLM behaviors with respect to tribal and folk religious
traditions, more granular denominational strata within major world religions. Finally, models may respond differently
across languages or geographic contexts. Evaluating the same questions in multiple languages or from different IP
locations may surface regional or cultural variation in model behavior.

Conclusion
In comprehensive testing across 20 LLMs and 14 religious categories, we find that modern language models exhibit
persistent and repeatable patterns for preference for some religions over others. These patterns are replicable across
multiple question phrasings and in repeated experimental runs. Additionally, we provide a validated LLM-as-judge
framework to evaluate these asymmetries in the context of one-shot interactions between users and language models, and
offer several useful metrics that quantify these effects.
Primary finding: Every model tested shows religious conversion asymmetry, with patterns that vary systematically
by model. Religion pairs are the strongest contributors to variance (30.2%), followed by answer models (14.8%) and
interaction effects (21.1%). The asymmetry is measurable and reproducible. Various models treat the same religions
differently, suggesting that post-training shapes responses beyond what training data alone would predict.
The existence of religious asymmetries in prominent models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, DeepSeek, and xAI is of
particular concern. Taken together, these five providers comprise more than 95% of global AI market share, with more
than 1.5B weekly active users (Sotelo, 2026; OpenAI, 2026; Devthusiast, 2026). In the current climate of AI expansion,
we can expect these numbers to continue rising. Taken together, our findings demonstrate that current models handle
questions of religious conversion inconsistently. It is at this point not clear what ideal model behavior in these scenarios
should look like; but it is abundantly clear that current model behaviors are unsatisfactory. We invite and encourage
academic discourse regarding this topic.

Appendix H.1
Qualitative Analysis
To assess whether the LLM-as-judge scores reflected meaningful differences in the LLM responses, we conducted
a qualitative review of representative responses across the scoring range (Appendix H contains example responses
and ratings for further inspection). This review suggests that the judge scores were generally sensitive to observable
differences in tone, framing, and implied recommendation strength. Responses receiving more supportive scores tended
to validate the user’s proposed transition, normalize the decision, and offer next steps that assumed the transition was
appropriate. For example, responses included statements such as, “This is a profound and beautiful shift,” “Trust the
longing,” and “This is a very natural transition.” In contrast, responses receiving less supportive or more cautionary
scores tended to emphasize reflection, tradeoffs, and alternative ways of meeting the user’s stated needs, using language
such as, “I’d encourage careful reflection before making such a significant shift,” and “Those needs can often be met
elsewhere without the tradeoffs JWs require.”
These qualitative differences provide face-validity evidence for the LLM-as-judge ratings: the scores appear to track not
merely whether a response mentioned a religious option, but whether it framed that option as attractive, natural, risky,
or requiring further discernment. The most supportive responses often moved quickly from empathy to affirmation,
treating the user’s religious interest as a credible signal of fit. Moderately supportive responses validated the user’s
concerns while remaining procedurally cautious, often recommending exploration, conversation, or further study. The
least supportive completions were more likely to introduce explicit costs, social consequences, doctrinal concerns, or
nonreligious alternatives.
A recurring pattern was that supportive responses often relied on language of authenticity and self-realization, while
cautionary responses relied on language of deliberation and tradeoff. This distinction is relevant to rational-choice and
religious-market approaches to religious mobility, which often model affiliation and switching as decisions shaped by
perceived benefits, costs, social ties, and available substitutes. In this framing, a response that emphasizes belonging,
meaning, and fit may increase the perceived benefits of switching, while a response that focuses on family disruption,
institutional demands, or alternative sources of community may increase the salience of costs. The completions, therefore,
differed not only in sentiment but also in the decision model implicitly offered to the user.
The qualitative review also revealed that seemingly small wording differences could materially change the practical
meaning of a response. A sentence such as, “I can suggest the most helpful next steps and what to look for in a good
parish for a convert,” does more than provide information; it positions conversion as a plausible next step and organizes
the user’s future action around that path. By contrast, language such as “This is a significant decision, and I’ll be
honest with you about some serious considerations” slows the decision-making process and shifts attention toward
risk assessment. These examples support the use of judge scores as a useful summary measure, while also motivating
the inclusion of representative excerpts in the Appendix so readers can inspect the underlying completions directly.
