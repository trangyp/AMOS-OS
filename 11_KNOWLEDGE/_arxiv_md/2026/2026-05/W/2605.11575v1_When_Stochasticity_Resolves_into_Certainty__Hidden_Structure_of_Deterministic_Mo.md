Abstract
We prove that deterministic motion in dissipative systems emerges as a strict geometric
attractor of contact flow, not a statistical approximation. Building on the contact
geometry of stochastic vector bundles, we develop time-dependent contact potentials
with an exact closure theorem ensuring exact satisfaction of the master equation at any
finite order. The Contact Locking Theorem shows that exponential gradient amplification
of the probability field is precisely counterbalanced by synchronous stiffness decay, forcing
the effective macroscopic-microscopic coupling to vanish exponentially. Deterministic
dynamics therefore emerges through deterministic focusing with a universal timescale
governed by the drift-field Jacobian spectrum. Validation of the damped-driven Duffing
oscillator confirms the predicted rate and exponential convergence.
Keywords: contact potential; truncation consistency; contact locking; deterministic
focusing; dissipative systems

Introduction
Classical mechanics rests on two seemingly irreconcilable paradigms: deterministic dy-
namics govern the trajectories of individual states, whereas stochastic dynamics describes
the evolution of probability distributions over ensembles [1, 2]. The only conventional
connection between these two frameworks lies in the formulation of macroscopic determin-
istic equations of motion. In traditional statistical mechanics, macroscopic deterministic
motion is regarded as the ensemble-averaged outcome of microscopic stochasticity. All
canonical deterministic laws observed in nature, including Newton’s equations, Navier–
Stokes equations, and the dynamics of damped oscillators, are conventionally established
by means of statistical mechanics to evaluate the ensemble average of underlying micro-
scopic stochastic systems [3–5].
Nevertheless, such a conventional statistical correspondence merely offers a phe-
nomenological linkage and overlooks a more fundamental intrinsic relation hidden behind
the two paradigms. Moreover, This paradigm has proven successful in equilibrium
systems or in systems with strict separation of time scales, yet it is fundamentally a
statistical compromise: it cannot explain why, in many far-from-equilibrium dissipative
systems, the emergence of deterministic trajectories is exceedingly rapid and robust, far
surpassing the relaxation time required by the statistical law of large numbers. This
suggests that determinism may not be a statistical illusion arising from averaging, but
rather a consequence of some deeper physical necessity.
To uncover the mathematical essence of this physical necessity, we must transcend
the statistical framework and probe the geometric infrastructure of stochastic dynamics.
Such a deeper connection cannot be captured by the conventional symplectic geometry,
which is strictly confined to conservative and deterministic flows, inherently failing to
accommodate dissipative effects or probabilistic evolution [6, 7]. This compels us to seek a
broader geometric paradigm capable of naturally intertwining probability with dissipation.
Consequently, we construct our theoretical framework upon the contact geometry of
stochastic vector bundles [8]. As shown in our previous studies, the probability space of a
stochastic vector bundle intrinsically carries an infinite-order jet structure that admits a

Exact Closure and Construction of Contact Potentials
The first-order degeneracy condition H(2)∇H(0) = 0 (Eq. (2.20)) carries profound geo-
metric and physical implications, acting as the demarcation between conservative and
dissipative dynamics within the contact framework.
From a linear algebraic perspective, the condition implies that the gradient vector
∇H(0) lies strictly within the kernel (null space) of the symmetric matrix H(2):
∇H(0) ∈ker(H(2))
⇐⇒
H(2)∇H(0) = 0.
When H(0) is non-constant (as in conservative systems), ∇H(0) ̸= 0, which forces H(2)
to be singular. Consequently, the second-order contact stiffness tensor H(2) exhibits
structural degeneracy, with a rank at most m −1.
This mathematical singularity corresponds to a fundamental physical restriction:
probability cannot focus across a conserved quantity.
(i) Conservative Systems: Here, H(0) is the classical Hamiltonian Hcl, and ∇H(0) is
the normal vector to the energy level sets. The condition H(2)∇H(0) = 0 signifies
that the second-order contact stiffness vanishes in the normal direction. Physically,
this means the normal direction is unconstrained; the probability field possesses
no restoring stiffness to contract or localize across the energy surface. Because
the governing equation (stiffness) degenerates to zero, this direction becomes
uncontrollable, representing a lost controllable degree of freedom.
(ii) Dissipative Systems: In this case, H(0) = c (constant), so ∇H(0) = 0. The condition
reduces to the trivial identity H(2) · 0 = 0. The matrix H(2) is generically non-
singular (full rank). Physically, without a conserved quantity, the normal restriction
is entirely removed; all directions become controllable, possessing non-zero stiffness.
This removal of the geometric constraint allows the probability field to fully contract
in all directions.
As a result, the degeneracy condition encodes the geometric limitation imposed by
conserved quantities. The singular rank of H(2) in conservative systems is not a defect,

but the signature of an unconstrained direction, a degree of freedom that has lost its
non-degenerate control equation; the full rank in dissipative systems signals the activation
of the focusing mechanism in all directions.

5.1
Stochastic Persistence
The central result of this paper, i.e., deterministic focusing—the strict emergence of
deterministic motion from stochastic contact dynamics (Theorem 4.8)—is established
as a geometric theorem within the truncated contact manifold. However, a critical
distinction must be drawn between the mathematical asymptotic limit and the physical
reality of observable systems.
Mathematically, deterministic focusing corresponds to the probability density lo-
calizing indefinitely into a Dirac δ-distribution, ρ(y) →δ(y −ydet). In this idealized
limit, the fiber coordinate diverges |ϕ| →∞(reflecting infinite spatial sensitivity), the
second-order contact term vanishes H(2) →0 (reflecting vanishing second-order contact
stiffness), and their product vanishes exactly as ∼e−σt, achieving complete dynamic
decoupling between macroscopic base dynamics and microscopic probability field.
In physical reality, however, dissipative systems are perpetually subject to persistent
stochastic perturbations—thermal fluctuations, environmental coupling, or intrinsic
microscopic noise. These persistent forces prevent the probability density from ever
collapsing into a true δ-distribution. Instead of an indefinite singularity, the dynamics
reaches a non-equilibrium steady state characterized by a finite-width distribution (e.g.,
a narrow Gaussian centered on the deterministic attractor).
In this physical steady state, the asymptotic contact locking mechanism is funda-
mentally modified by the interplay between deterministic gradient amplification and
stochastic diffusion:
(i) The fiber coordinate ϕ reaches a large but finite saturation value. Its natural expo-
nential growth at rate σ is exactly balanced by the stochastic driving, preventing
the divergence to infinity.
(ii) The second-order contact stiffness H(2) decays to a small but finite floor value.
Because ϕ stops growing, the conservation constraint freezes the decay of H(2),
maintaining the structural integrity of the distribution against ongoing perturba-
tions.
(iii) The effective coupling H(2)ϕ becomes exponentially small but strictly non-zero,

coupling manifests as the persistent stochastic jitter (stochastic noise) observed on
