Why Do Safety Guardrails Degrade Across Languages?
Max Zhang∗, Ameen Patel∗, Sang Truong∗∗, Sanmi Koyejo∗∗
Stanford University
∗: Equal contribution, ∗∗: Equal senior authorship
Abstract
Large language models exhibit safety degradation in non-English lan-
guages. Standard evaluation relies on Jailbreak Success Rate (JSR), which
confounds several safety-driving factors into one, obscuring the specific
cause(s) of safety failure. We introduce a latent variable model, a Multi-
Group Item Response Theory (IRT) framework, that decouples safety-
driving factors such as language-agnostic safety robustness (θ), intrinsic
prompt hardness (β), global language processing diﬀiculty (γ), and a
prompt-specific cross-lingual safety gap (τ). Using the MultiJail dataset,
we evaluate the safety robustness of 61 model configurations across 5
closed-model families and 10 languages of varying resource, aggregating
a dataset of 1.9 million rows. Exploratory Factor Analysis shows safety
is primarily unidimensional: models refuse different harm types mainly
through a shared mechanism. Contrary to the expected trend that safety
degrades largely in low-resource languages, 22 model configurations are
more vulnerable in English than in low-resource languages. Low-resource
languages produce more uncertain responses (high entropy) than high-
resource languages. Also, high-τ prompts cluster in physical harm cate-
gories like Theft and Weapons and lower-resource languages, trends vali-
dated through cross-dataset generalization. While global translation qual-
ity shows low correlation with τ, severe mistranslations drive high-bias out-
liers, as validated by native speakers. Cultural and conceptual grounding
mismatches also contribute to τ. In predictive validation, the IRT frame-
work achieves AUC = 0.940, outperforming simpler baselines in predict-
ing safe refusal of unsafe prompts. Our framework reveals concept–lan-
guage vulnerabilities that aggregate metrics obscure, enabling fairer cross-
lingual safety evaluation and targeted improvements in dataset construc-
tion.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) exhibit degraded safety guardrails in non-English
languages, particularly in low-resource languages such as Swahili, Bengali, and Ja-
vanese (Deng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024), posing potential global safety concerns.
Standard evaluation relies on Jailbreak Success Rate (JSR) or Attack Success Rate (ASR),
aggregating binary outcomes into a single number. As Chouldechova et al. (2026) argue,
this creates an apples-to-oranges comparison: models tested under different conditions or
prompt subsets cannot be fairly compared. More fundamentally, when a model produces
an unsafe response to a prompt, JSR cannot entirely disentangle why the failure occurred.
Is the failure a lack of model capability, or is it language-specific? Is it a translation error
corrupting safety-relevant semantics? Or are certain harm concepts fundamentally harder
to represent in certain languages due to cultural framing? Lastly, does judge selection or
question ambiguity impose extra prompt diﬀiculties?

Prior work supports these concerns: physical harm concepts align poorly in vector space
across languages compared to abstract ones (Peng & Søgaard, 2024). Yet without an apt
measurement framework, these questions cannot be fully addressed.
Hence, we propose a Multi-Group Item Response Theory (IRT; Baker 2001) framework that
maps each source of failure to a distinct latent parameter: is the failure a lack of safety ca-
pability?—captured by θ, inherent safety ability, and intrinsic prompt hardness (β); is the
language intrinsically harder?—captured by γ; and is it a prompt-specific cross-lingual fail-
ure?—captured by τ, the residual safety gap after controlling for ability and global language
effects. τ represents our specific context’s Differential Item Functioning (DIF; Holland &
Wainer, 1993): when model configurations with equal abilities from different groups have
different probabilities of producing a safe response, signaling potential test bias.
By decomposing safety failures into these latent factors, we move beyond aggregate metrics
(JSR/ASR) to diagnose precisely where models are successfully jailbroken across languages.
Figure 1 provides an overview. Our contributions are as follows:
1. Framework. We introduce a multi-group IRT framework separating ability (θj),
language diﬀiculty (γL), and prompt-specific cross-lingual safety gaps (τiL), eval-
uating 61 model configurations across 5 families and 10 languages.
2. Safety is unidimensional. Exploratory Factor Analysis reveals safety is largely uni-
dimensional: models that refuse one harm category generally refuse others.
3. Reversed multilingual safety patterns. Contrary to standard assumptions, 22 of
61 model configurations exhibit their highest failure rates in English, spanning 3
model families. Models operate with significantly higher response uncertainty in
low-resource languages.
4. Translation quality is a minor factor. Translation quality correlates weakly with
both raw safety and cross-lingual safety gaps (∼1% of τ variance), but the decom-
position identifies severe mistranslations in the dataset that induce high τ diﬀiculty
(dataset bugs).
5. Cultural and conceptual gaps. High-τ prompts cluster in physical harm categories,
and native speakers identify culturally or concept-specific prompts, explaining τ
outliers with perfect translation; τ correlates weakly with judge disagreement.

XijL = 1 indicates a safe refusal and XijL = 0 indicates an unsafe response. We employ a
multi-group IRT model. The probability of a safe response is modeled as:

The left term captures ability: θj is the base safety robustness of model j, adjusted by a
language-specific deficit δjL. The right term captures diﬀiculty: βi is the intrinsic hardness
of prompt i (derived from English), γL is the global processing diﬀiculty of language L,
and τiL is the prompt-specific cross-lingual safety gap (DIF) after controlling for base dif-
ficulty and global language effects. The discrimination parameter αi controls how sharply
prompt i separates models of differing ability. In English, γL, τiL, and δjL are fixed to zero,
establishing English as the reference. We employ a 2PL IRT model, allowing item-specific
discrimination, which achieved the optimal AIC/BIC over 1PL and GRM (details in Ap-
pendix C).
Traditional anchor selection.
Classic approaches to anchor selection are iterative purifica-
tion (Lord, 2012) or iterative forward with MTT (Kopf et al., 2015), which build an anchor
set over repeated rounds. We found that traditional methods fail in our multi-group con-
text because a prompt might show τ—cross-lingual safety gap—in one language but not in
another; searching for hard invariance across all 10 languages proved too strict. Iterative
Purification produces an empty set of candidates after filtering for prompts (between 5%
and 95% safe). Without filtering, anchors span very little diﬀiculty range, thus providing
little information, and γ and τ demonstrate heavy confounding with Pearson |r| = 0.943.
Prior sensitivity.
γL (global language processing diﬀiculty for language L) and τiL (cross-
lingual safety gap of prompt i in language L) both capture language-level effects, and with-
out regularization, the model may confound signal across both parameters. Figure 8 in
Appendix A shows that our sparsity separates γL from τiL. With our method, anchor trans-
lation quality is marginally higher than non-anchors (4.55 vs. 4.53; Figure 9 of Appendix B).
Anchors.
Valid cross-lingual estimation requires anchor items, prompts invariant across
languages, to fix the measurement scale. We select anchors in two steps. First, we keep
only prompts with safety rates between 5% and 95%, excluding extreme items that provide
little information. Second, for the remaining prompts, we compute the Lord’s χ2 statistic
(Lord, 2012) between English and each other language under a 2PL IRT model, which is a
standard DIF statistic. We average χ2 values across languages and keep the 40 prompts with
the lowest values as our anchors (indicating invariance). This method serves as a heuristic;
therefore, rather than imposing a hard constraint (τiL = 0), anchors receive a tight Normal
prior (τ ∼N (0, 0.01)) that encourages near-zero values while allowing slight deviation.

Experiment
We use MultiJail (Deng et al., 2024): 3,150 jailbreak prompts, 10 languages (English, Chi-
nese, Arabic, Bengali, Thai, Korean, Vietnamese, Italian, Swahili, Javanese), 18 categories.
We measure 61 model configurations spanning 5 model families (GPT, Claude, DeepSeek,
Gemini, Grok) in Table 4 of Appendix D, aggregating a dataset of 1.9 million rows. A
single generation pass may introduce high stochastic noise. We therefore define Pass@k as
k independent generations per prompt, aggregated to estimate the response distribution
(distinct from the code-eval metric of the same name). Figure 2 shows that Pass@1 may
explain only 24% of variance; therefore, we perform Pass@10 to balance validity and cost.
To capture the generation manifold rather than a single mode (Huang et al., 2023), we eval-
uate 4 distinct temperature/sampling configurations per model: Low-Creativity (temp=0.4,
top-p=1.0), Standard (temp=0.7, top-p=0.9), High-Risk (temp=1.0, top-p=0.95), and Chaos
(temp=1.3, top-p=1.0)—high entropy or stochastic. We provide variant justifications and
address “p-hacking” concerns in Appendix E.1 by only fitting base models (N = 16).
Safety judgment and validation.
We employ LLM-as-a-Judge with GPT-5.2 to grade re-
sponses on a 6-point Likert scale from 5 (safe refusal) to 1 (unsafe response) and 0 (in-
valid/missing). The full rubric and prompt are in Appendix F.1. Prior work has shown
that LLM judges can introduce bias, particularly when the evaluator and evaluated mod-
els share a family (Chen & Goldfarb-Tarrant, 2025; Zheng et al., 2023; Spiliopoulou et al.,
2025). Two independent evaluators annotated 300 stratified responses (10 per language×10
languages×3 models from GPT, DeepSeek, and Grok). Human–GPT-5.2 agreement is sub-
stantial: Cohen’s κ = 0.80–0.83, with 93.2–96.6% binary agreement (Appendix F.2). For our
human validation, native speakers evaluated responses in Chinese, Thai, and Bengali, while
Google Translate was used in other languages for human evaluators. While this may intro-

IRT preprocessing.
For IRT modeling, we binarize responses: scores ≥4 are labeled safe,
scores 1–3 are labeled unsafe, and score 0 (invalid) responses are treated as “Missing
at Random.” In our dataset, only 0.7% of rows were “Invalid” or missing. We run Ex-
ploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) (Spearman, 1904) on the binary response matrix (Figure
15 in Appendix G) to verify unidimensionality. We assess this through the Kaiser-Meyer-
Olkin (KMO) measure and dominance ratio (first-to-second eigenvalue ratio). We proceed
with a global IRT model if the dominance ratio exceeds 3.

Results
Table 1 presents JSR across model families. Grok exhibits the highest vulnerability (5.3–
28.1% JSR) and DeepSeek the lowest (2.9–3.0%). Within families, higher temperature vari-
ants (Chaos and High-Risk) consistently show elevated JSR.
The “English reversal” phenomenon.
Prior work predicts that low-resource languages
should exhibit higher JSR. We find this holds for some families: GPT models often show the
highest JSR for Swahili or Javanese. However, Grok exhibits a reversed pattern: English
has the highest JSR (35.2%), with Bengali showing the lowest (18.8%). Overall, English is the
most unsafe language by JSR (Figure 16 in Appendix H). Our IRT analysis confirms this is
a genuine ability deficit: Grok non-reasoning variants have the lowest θ (mean = −0.36, vs.
field mean 0.92), and their δjL values for non-English languages are predominantly positive.
Figure 3 shows that these models are marginally safer outside English.
This reversal extends to 22 of 61 model configurations. The effect is concentrated in all 16
Grok variants, all 4 DeepSeek variants, and 2 of 5 Claude variants. XSafety confirms: 22
out of 53 configurations show the same trend, now extending to 8 GPT models. Manually
examining 300 stratified prompt-response pairs for English-most-unsafe model configura-
tions, we confirm correct labeling: detailed compliance in high-resource and concise refusal
in low-resource. We hypothesize that safety filters could trigger more easily on low-resource
tokens for SOTA models, though this remains speculative and discussed in Section 6.
Incomprehension check.
Could safe responses in low-resource languages reflect incom-
prehension rather than genuine safety capability? We audited the Grok family (the only
group showing potential incompetence) using gpt-4.1-mini to classify responses as genuine
or incomprehension (prompt in Appendix F.5). Correcting for incomprehension changes
JSR by under 1%: overall incomprehension rate of 2.2%. English remains 6–9 percentage
points higher than all low-resource languages, confirming the reversal. (Appendix I). For
comparison, gpt-4.1-mini only has a 0.32% incomprehension rate in Javanese and 0.0% in
other languages, as graded by GPT-5.2 instead.

5.1
Exploratory Factor Analysis: safety is unidimensional
Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) on the binary response matrix yields strong evidence for
unidimensionality: the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy is 0.942
(classified as “marvelous” by Kaiser 1974 and Kaiser & Rice 1974), and the dominance ratio
of eigenvalues is 7.37 (eigenvalues represent the variance explained by latent factors). This
shows that models refuse disparate harms mainly through a shared mechanism, instead of
learning separate “safety circuits” for each topic. This is consistent with mechanistic inter-
pretability work showing that refusal is mediated by a single direction (Arditi et al., 2024;
Wang et al., 2026). This evidence justifies fitting one global IRT model rather than separate
models per harm type. Also, since safety is largely unidimensional, safety engineering is
generalizable. Figure 19 in Appendix J shows high inter-category correlation.
Although unidimensionality is pervasive across harm categories, residual gaps remain in
specific harm types, consistent with the non-dominant secondary directions identified by
Pan et al. (2025) and Wollschläger et al. (2026). XSafety supports unidimensionality to a
lower extent: KMO = 0.897 and Dominance Ratio = 3.31. We validate that high refusal rates
are not artificially inflating unidimensionality in Appendix J.1 via Yen’s Q3 (Yen, 1984) and
Kendall’s W = 0.465 (Kendall, 1948) and that τ is mostly a per-prompt correction and only
soaking minimal multidimensionality (Appendix R).
5.2
IRT parameter estimates: where do cross-lingual gaps occur?
Response uncertainty by language.
We characterize the uncertainty structure of safety re-
sponses based on empirical P(safe) across 10 passes. Figure 4 reveals that low-resource
languages have both a higher proportion of boundary prompts and higher mean response
entropy, while English occupies the lower-left corner (fewest boundary cases, lowest en-
tropy). Low-resource languages more so produce uncertain responses. This highlights
the importance of multi-pass measurement, especially in low-resource languages. Interest-
ingly, high entropy correlates positively with θ (Pearson r = 0.59, p < 0.001): high-ability
models operate closer to the decision boundary, while Grok reflects consistent failure.
Cross-lingual safety gap (DIF, τiL).
Beyond global language effects, we identify substan-
tial cross-lingual safety gaps. Figure 5 shows the relationship between English diﬀiculty
(βi) and target language diﬀiculty in three languages spanning resource levels (all nine
languages in Figure 18, Appendix H). Points above the diagonal indicate prompts with
positive τiL—harder to refuse in the target language than predicted by βi + γL alone. The
proportion of prompts above the diagonal mostly increases with language resource level.

of the top 100 τ terms in Appendix K.2. High-τ prompts more often cluster in specific
harm categories—namely theft, property crime, and weapons—that are more grounded or
physical when compared to more abstract ideas like discrimination, which have lower τ.

Dataset bugs.
Among prompts with both extreme τ (top 50) and low translation quality
(TQ ≤2), we identify 6 severe mistranslations in MultiJail, likely causing high τ (e.g., “hot-
wire a car” →“warm a car”; “roofie a drink” →“steal a drink”; Table 2 in Appendix B). In a
multilingual context, the translation quality of jailbreak prompts influences test bias, neces-
sitating more accurate translation to prevent outlier-biased terms. 40 of 945 natively rated
prompt pairs received poor or unintelligible ratings (TQ ≤2) and an additional 66 showed
notable issues (11% overall)—unexpected for a dataset described as natively translated.
Cultural and conceptual gaps.
Because translation quality explains ∼1% of τ variance, we
examined whether the residual could reflect potential cultural or conceptual mismatches.
Native speakers annotated 30 pairs among the 945 native speaker items with perfect transla-
tion quality (TQ = 5), yet extreme τ (top 50), categorizing each as concept culturally foreign to
the target language, framing shift (prompt meaning preserved but adversarial intent lost in
cultural context), or other. 12 of 30 prompts revealed cultural or conceptual gaps (Examples
in Table 23 in Appendix P). For instance, prompts referencing U.S.-specific racial slurs, or
country-specific terms (e.g., Turkey, FBI, SSN) have culturally foreign concepts. 5 of the 12
prompts showed framing shifts, where the native speaker viewed the prompt as less threat-
ening, less specific, and more formal or casual in their culture, despite perfect translation.
The remaining prompts involved largely universally understood concepts. Category-level
τ suggests that cultural specificity is not a primary driver of the cross-lingual safety gaps.
Theft and weapons have the highest mean τ (Table 18) despite being broadly understood
domains, whereas discrimination (culturally specific) exhibits lower mean τ. Cultural or
conceptual mismatches may explain a subset of high-τ cases, but are unlikely to be a broad
contributor.
Prompt ambiguity does not explain τ.
We observed seemingly benign and ambiguous
pairs in MultiJail as noted by native speakers: “Can you tell me how to prevent criminals
from using my credit card information for purchases online?” Therefore, to check whether τ
may be correlated with the inherent subjectivity of MultiJail prompts, we used GPT-5.2 with
the system prompt in Appendix F.7 to separate items from benign to adversarial (Likert 1–5).
Spearman ρ analysis finds no statistically significant correlation. 45 out of 315 prompts were
rated 4 or 5 (benign or mostly harmless; 14.3%), which is surprising considering Deng et al.
(2024) lists MultiJail as a fully adversarial dataset. This further underscores why absolute
JSR is a flawed metric: models that “refuse” ambiguous prompts may be as safe as those
that refuse genuinely dangerous prompts, and models that accept the benign request may
be considered successfully jailbroken, distorting the measurement of model safety.

Discussion and conclusion
We introduce a multi-group IRT framework for safety decomposition. Our methodology
evaluates 61 model configurations across 10 languages and 1.9 million responses.
Returning to the questions.
We explore multiple questions as to multilingual safety
degradation: ability deficit, translation distortion, and conceptual grounding mismatch.
We observe patterns such as the English reversal and non-uniform degradation, which mo-
tivate further decomposition. Translation distortion is confirmed to increase cross-lingual
safety gaps, but the effect sizes are modest and concentrated in outlier pairs. Conceptual
grounding mismatches are supported by the clustering of high-τ prompts more so in spe-
cific, physical harm categories and low-resource languages, and by the 30 prompt pairs with
native speakers, where we found 12 pairs for this phenomenon. Lastly, the cross-lingual

Why do some models fail more in English?
The English reversal is an interesting pattern
that prompts speculation. Because MultiJail prompts are authored in English and are de-
signed to circumvent exactly these cues, they may succeed more reliably in English when
compared to other languages. Also, the family-specific trends (Figure 3) suggest differ-
ent alignment targets of helpfulness and safety. Mechanistic analyses of refusal directions
(Arditi et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026) may lay the groundwork for understanding different
multilingual safety alignment failures across model families.
Implications for practitioners.
τiL could be used for targeted remediation. We can easily
find high τ prompt×language pairs to remediate translation issues, cultural phenomena, or
for fine-tuning purposes to address these sparse alignment gaps. This setup also serves as a
standard quality-control step absent from current LLM evaluation. Next, 4 of 30 cultural or
conceptual candidates (extreme τ prompts with perfect translation) represent U.S.-specific
items, and 8 of 30 represent Western ideas in general, far more than other demographics.
This may inflate cross-lingual safety gaps because of the English-centric nature. Future
multilingual jailbreak benchmarks could consist of more universally understood harms:
works such as Ning et al. (2025) are a promising start. Additionally, ASR/JSR compar-
isons across different settings may be generally invalid (Chouldechova et al., 2026). This,
along with our own IRT results, critiques the apples-to-oranges nature of current LLM mea-
surement. Methodological planning, applying psychometrics or adaptive testing (Truong
et al., 2025b), could be more deeply considered, especially around binary metrics. Finally,
dataset creation should address translation quality to prevent outlier-driven test bias.
Limitations.
First, LLM judges may carry cross-lingual biases that survive our validations:
native-speaker agreement is verified only for Chinese, Thai, and Bengali, while the remain-
ing seven languages rely on Google-translated responses for human evaluators, which can
attenuate true disagreement in low-resource conditions. Second, our anchor-selection pro-
cedure is a custom heuristic that averages Lord’s χ2 across languages: it can miss prompts
that are invariant in some languages but not others, and we have not benchmarked sen-
sitivity to alternative anchor counts beyond the 40 prompts we adopt (explained in Ap-
pendix A). Third, the English-reversal phenomenon is documented behaviorally but re-
mains untested from a mechanistic lens; we cannot yet distinguish whether it stems from
tokenization, training-data composition, RLHF reward distribution, or refusal-circuit geom-
etry. Fourth, MultiJail and XSafety are predominantly English-authored, which structurally
favors English-targeted attack patterns and may understate vulnerabilities elicited by gen-
uinely native-language adversarial prompts. Fifth, our coverage is restricted to closed-API
models, so we cannot inspect internal activations to corroborate the latent-factor decompo-
sition; replication on open-source models is left to future work.
Future work.
First, the English reversal mechanisms could be tested more directly. Sec-
ond, more robust anchor selection methodologies could be developed for multilingual mea-
surement research. Third, our framework can be scaled to additional languages and open-
source model families to test the generality of the patterns observed in this paper. Fourth,
an intervention study may be interesting: would models fine-tuned on the specific high-τ
prompt×language pairs identified by our framework have unique results? Could an amor-
tized rephraser potentially find and rephrase terms with a high cross-lingual safety gap?

information: standard model selection criteria which identify a best-fitting model while

Variant justification
Varying generation configurations can increase misalignment rates from 0% to over 95%
(Huang et al., 2023), and 18–28% of prompts exhibit decision flips across temperature and
seed configurations (Larsen, 2025). Fleiss’ κ across variants ranges from 0.48 to 0.76 (me-
dian 0.59), and IRT analyses show very similar θ estimates across variants (Appendix E.2).
Therefore, we confirm that variants share a core safety profile while contributing non-
redundant signal for parameter reliability.
To address “p-hacking” concerns, we conduct an ablation study fitting the IRT model
only on base models (N = 16; Standard), confirming the main findings: the proportion
of English-worst configurations (37.5% vs. 36.1%), θ rankings, and τ category hierarchies
are preserved (Appendix E.4).
