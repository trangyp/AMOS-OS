WEAKLY NOETHERIAN LIE ALGEBRAS
AND THE SIERRA-WALTON CONJECTURE
OLIVIER MATHIEU
Abstract. Let K be a field of characteristic zero. Motivated by the
conjecture that an enveloping algebra U(g) is Noetherian only if g is
finite dimensional, we define the notion of weakly Noetherian Lie alge-
bras.
The main result, Theorem A, states that weakly Noetherian Lie alge-
bras have a very constrained structure. In the specific case of Zn graded
Lie algebras, it implies an explicit classification of the perfect strictly
weakly Noetherian Lie algebras, stated in Theorem B. The proofs of
both theorems are quite long, and uses concrete results due to Tits,
Formanek, Razmyslov, Grabowski and the author.
The first theorem provides some insight on the desired conjecture.
The second one implies the conjecture for all perfect Zn-graded Lie al-
gebras, improving a celebrated theorem of Sierra and Walton.

Introduction
0.1. General introduction. Over a field K of characteristic 0, any finite
dimensional Lie algebra g admits a Levi decomposition g ≃s ⋉r, where s is
semisimple and r is a solvable ideal. Motivated by the conjecture mentionned
in the abstract, we pose the very naive question:
Do infinite dimensional Lie algebras admit a structure
theory similar to the finite dimensional case?
In general the answer is obviously negative, but we can expect a positive
answer for some classes of infinite dimensional Lie algebras. For example,
Aldosray, Amayo and Stewart [1, 2, 3, 32] has proved some general results
for Lie algebras satisfying some Artinian-Noetherian conditions. Here we try
a different approach: we say that a Lie algera G is weakly Noetherian if
(a) for any subalgebra m ⊂G, the space m/[m, m] is finite dimensional.
If, in addition
(b) any chain of characteristic ideals in G stabilizes,
G is called Noetherian. Moreover G is called strictly weakly Noetherian if
G⊗L is weakly Noetherian, for any finite extension L of K. Typical examples
of Noetherian Lie algebras are the Krichever-Novikov Lie algebras VectX of
vector fields on a smooth algebraic curve X, as it is proved in Section 4.
At first glance, the notion of weakly Noetherian Lie algebras looks like
an abstract nonsense notion. In fact, we will see that it is connected with
interesting concrete algebraic theories, as explained in Subsection 0.3.
First we consider the case of Zn-graded Lie algebras, L = ⊕m∈Zn Lm,
where is is assumed that all homogenous components have finite dimensions.

In order to state the structure result in general we first need some addi-
tional definitions. Recall that the radical rad(G) of a Lie algebra G is the
intersection of all maximal ideals m of codimension > 1. Assume now that
G be a weakly Noetherian Lie algebra satisfying the following simplifying
hypothesis
(h1) G = [G, G], and
(h2) G does not admit finite dimensional simple quotients.
Then we define, by transfinite induction, a decreasing sequence, indexed by
ordinals α, of Lie subalgebras
G = G(0) ⊃G(1) ⊃. . . G(α) ⊃. . .
as follows:
(a) if the ordinal α is a successor, that is α = β + 1 for some β, then
G(α) = [rad(G(β)), rad(G(β))]
(b) If α > 0 is a limit ordinal, then
G(α) = [∩β<α rad(G(β)), ∩β<α rad(G(β))].
In its simplified form, the main result is as follows:
Theorem A. (simplified version) Let G be a weakly Noetherian Lie algebra
satisfying (h1) and (h2). Then, for any ordinal α:
(a) the Lie algebra G(α) is a perfect ideal,
(b) G(α)/G(α+1) is a central extension of G(α)/ rad G(α) by a finite di-
mensional center,
(c) any simple quotient of G(α) is infinite dimensional, and
(d) for α ≥1, no Krichever-Novikov algebras occur as a quotient of G(α).
Moreover if G is Noetherian, then G(α) = 0 for α big enough.
Therefore, Theorem A shows that Noetherian Lie algebras G have a very
constrained structure. Roughly speaking, G is a "tower" of central extensions
of infinite dimensional simple Lie algebras.
0.2. Relation with the Sierra-Walton conjecture. Our investigation is
motivated by the following:
Sierra-Walton Conjecture 1. [31] If g is infinite dimensional, then U(g)
is not Noetherian.
This question has been stated by several authors since the 70’s, among
them R. K. Amayo, I. Stewart, J. Dixmier, V. Latishev, K. A. Brown [3, 13]
A breakthrough result was obtained in 2013 by S. Sierra and Ch. Walton:
Theorem 2. [31] The enveloping algebra of the Witt algebra W(K) is not
Noetherian.

Consequently, S. Sierra and Ch. Walton stated this question as a conjec-
ture. From Sierra-Walton Theorem, one can derived the same result for all
Krichever-Novikov algebras [14]. The conjecture was confirmed recently for
some other classes of Lie algebras [7, 8] and [5]. The old work [35] is also
connected with these questions. As a consequence of Theorem B, we obtain
the following generalization of Sierra-Walton Theorem.
Corollary 65. Let L be a perfect Zn-graded Lie algebra. The algebra U(L)
is left Noetherian only if L has finite dimension.
A refined version of Theorem A is proved in section 8. As a consequence
we deduce:
Corollary 45. Any infinite dimensional Noetherian Lie algebra G contains
two characteristic ideals p ⊃q with
codim p < ∞
codim q = ∞
such that the Lie algebra p/q is just-infinite and either
(type A) p/q is simple, or
(type B) p/q is residually nilpotent, or
(type C) p/q is perfect, residually semi-simple and does not satisfies any poly-
nomial identity.
By [18], any Krichever-Novikov algebra VectX is simple, that is of type A.
Given any point P on the curve X, the Lie subalgebra Vect(2)
X of vector fields
vanishing twice at P is residually nilpotent, that is of type B. However, we
do not know examples of Noetherian Lie algebras of type C.
It follows from Corollary 45 that the Sierra-Walton conjecture can be
reduced to these three types of Lie algebras.
Hovewer Theorem A and some intuitive arguments, briefly outlined in
Subsection 9.2, suggest that the conjecture could be undecidable for some
simple Lie algebras S.
0.3. The ideas of the proofs. The proof of Theorem A is quite long.
Although its statement looks formal, its proof is based on concrete results.
The core of the proof consists of the following result:
Theorem 41. Let G be a weakly Noetherian Lie algebra.
Any maximal ideal of codimension > 1 is characteristic.
The difficult point in the proof of Theorem 41 concerns maximal ideals
of finite codimension. It is based on the theory of affine Lie algebras, as
initiated in [33, 34], and the next result:
Theorem 25. (simplified version) Let G be a weakly Noetherian Lie algebra
and let d > 1 be an integer.
Then G contains only finitely many maximal ideals m of codimension d.

Its proof combines the theory of central identities of Formanek [15] and
Razmyslov [29], together with the finitness of the dimension of centroids, i.e.
Proposition 20.
Obviously the proof of Theorem A runs by transfinite induction, and we
will illustrate the use of Theorem 41 to go to the second step. Using hy-
potheses (h1) and (h2), it is quite easy to prove that G(1) is perfect and that
G(0)/G(1) is a central extension of G(0)/ rad(G(0)). To continue by induc-
tion, we consider a maximal ideal m of G(1) and the corresponding simple
Lie algebra S = G(1)/m. Using Theorem 41, we show that the Lie algebra
Out(S) of outer derivations of S is nonzero. Thus by [12], S is infinite
dimensional, which allows to pursue the induction. Moreover by a theorem
of Grabowski [19], S is not a Krichever-Novikov Lie algebra either.
The proof of Theorem B starts with the following theorem, whose proof
is essentially based on author’s result [26, 27, 28, 5] for algebraically closed
fields. The descent argument from K to K is an easy consequence of Hilbert’s
90 Theorem.
Theorem 57. Let L be a simple infinite-dimensional Zn-graded Lie algebra
over K.
If L is strictly Noetherian, then L is isomorphic to Witt(L) or Vir(L) for
some finite extension L of K.
Given an arbitrary weakly Notherian Lie algebra G, the definition of the
filtration
G ⊃G(0) ⊃G(1) ⊃. . . G(α) ⊃. . .
is generalized in Section 8. However, when G does not satisfy (h1) or (h2),
we have G ̸= G(0). Nevertheless, G(0) satisfies the conclusion of Theorem A.
Now, let L be a Lie algebra satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem B. Theo-
rem A(d) and Theorem 57 easily imply that L(1) = 0. Moreover, we also
deduce that L(0)/ rad(L(1)) is a finite sum of copies of Witt and Virasoro Lie
algebras. Using an argument about representation theory of finite dimen-
sional Lie algebras, we also prove that L/L(0) is finite dimensional. Then
Grabowskiś theorem [19] is used to show that, up to isogeny, L is the direct
sum of L/L(0) and L(0), which concludes the proof of Theorem B.

1. Main Definitions and Notations
From now on, we denote by K a field of characteristic zero, and we write
K for its algebraic closure.
Depending upon the context, we will use symbols like G, g, L for Lie
algebras and m, r . . . for ideals of Lie algebras. Usually G denotes a Lie
algebra of arbitrary dimension, g a Lie algebra of finite dimension and L a
graded Lie algebra.
We collect now the main definitions, which mostly concern the infinite
dimensional Lie algebras.
1.1. Simple Lie algebras and radicals. As usual, a one-dimensional Lie
algebra is not considered as a simple Lie algebra. Accordingly, the radical of
a Lie algebra G, denoted by rad G, is the intersection of all its maximal ideals
of codimension > 1. Its finite radical, denoted as radf G, is the intersection
of all its maximal ideals m with
1 < dim m < ∞.
We define a semisimple Lie algebra as a finite dimensional Lie algebra
which is a direct sum of simple Lie algebras. With our restrictive convention,
neither a simple Lie algebra of infinite dimension nor an infinite sum of simple
finite dimensional Lie algebra is semisimple.
1.2. Derived and descending central series. Let G be a Lie algebra of
arbitrary dimension. Recall that G is called perfect if G = [G, G].
The descending central series of G is defined by induction by
C 1G := G,
C k+1G := [G, C kG] for k ≥1,
C ωG := ∩k≥1C kG
and the derived series by
D0G := G
Dk+1G := [DkG, DkG] for k ≥0,
DωG := ∩k≥1DkG.
With these conventions we have
[C kG, C ℓG] ⊂C k+ℓG and DkG ⊂C 2kG.

1.3. Residual and strict properties. Let P be a certain property of Lie
algebras. When a Lie algebra G satisfies P, we say that G is P. Given G a
Lie algebra, we say that
(a) G is residually P if, for any x ∈G, there is an ideal m ⊂G with
x /∈m such that G/m is P,
(b) G is strictly P if G ⊗L is P for any finite extension K of L.

residually semi-simple if radf g = 0. Equivalently, there is a collection {sα |

subalgebra of Q

The previous two properties are not exclusive each other. For example, the
Lie G = sl(2)⊗tK[t]. is simultaneously residually semi-simple and residually
nilpotent.

1.5. Structure theory of finite dimensional Lie algebra. The following
classical result is attributed to E. Cartan, cf. [12]§5.
Theorem 6. Let g be a finite dimensional Lie algebra.
(a) The radical rad g of g is the maximal solvable ideal.
(b) Set s = g/ rad g. Then g ≃s ⋉rad g.
(c) If g is perfect, then rad g is nilpotent.
(d) If g is solvable, then [g, g] is nilpotent.
(e) If g is semisimple, then Out(g) = 0.

3. Various Noetherianity properties for Lie algebras
3.1. Definition of Noetherianity for Lie algebras. We define three no-
tions of Noetherianity for Lie algebras:
(1) A Lie algebra G is called weakly Noetherian if
dim m/[m, m] < ∞
for all subalgebras m in G.

the ascending chain condition (ACC in brief) on the characteristic

(3) A Lie algebra G is called strongly Noetherian if all its subalgebras

We observe that the notion of Noetherianity for the associative algebras is

7.1. Affine Lie algebras. Let s be a finite dimensional simple Lie algebra.
A Lie algebra G is called affine if
(a) G contains s, and
(b) viewed as an s-module, G is a direct sum of adjoint modules.
This kind of Lie algebras first appeared in Tits work [33][34]. Over an
algebraically closed field, or, more generally when s is a split simple Lie
algebra, affine Lie algebras are special case of the Lie algebras graded by
a root system (LAGRS), which have been extensively investigated. We can
mention the early works of Koecher [25], Kantor [23] for rank one root system,
and, in the general case, there are a lot of papers, including those of Berman
and Moody [10] or Benckart and Zelmanov [9]. The main difference with the
axioms of [33][34] is the fact that affine Lie algebras do not contain trivial
representations of s. Also, at the difference of other precited works, we do
not assume that s is split, so there are no root decomposition in our setting.
Henceforth, it will be more easy to give direct proofs than extracting
results from previous works.
The first step consists in the computation of the space Homs(s ⊗s, s)
of all equivariant products on s. The Lie bracket [, ] is a skew symmetric
equivariant product. When s = sln, there is a also a symmetric product ·
defined by
x · y := 1/2(xy + yx) −1/nTr(xy)1n for any x, y ∈sln.
Here xy is the usual product of n × n matrices, 1n denotes the identity of
Matn(K), and the additional term 1/nTr(xy)1n insures that x · y is in sln.
For n ≥3, the product · is not zero.
The starting point is the following well-known observation:
Lemma 28. Assume that K = K is algebraically closed.
