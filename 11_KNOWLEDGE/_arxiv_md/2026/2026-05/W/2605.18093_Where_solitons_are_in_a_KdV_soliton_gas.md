Where solitons are in a KdV soliton gas
Benjamin Doyon
Department of Mathematics, King’s College London, Strand, London WC2R 2LS, U.K.
The Korteweg-De Vries (KdV) equation is a paradigmatic model of integrable classical fields,
admitting localised single-soliton profiles and multi-soliton solutions with elastic, factorised scat-
tering. When many solitons are near to each other, their shapes are modified, and it is not
manifest, from the KdV field, where they are. This is a key problem in the analysis of a soliton
gas, as its main object, the density of states, is a number of solitons per unit length. How to
define solitons’ positions at finite densities in the macroscopic limit? A sensible criterium is
that, projecting out solitons lying outside a mesoscopic region, the KdV field is unchanged in
this region, and the result is a multi-soliton field supported there. In the context of emergent
hydrodynamics, this is referred to as a fluid-cell projection. In this paper we solve this problem.
We define solitons’ positions and from this a fluid-cell projection, and show that it has these
properties, without introducing radiative corrections. We show that the weak limit of conserved
densities can be evaluated using the density of states. On large scales the solitons’ positions
satisfy the semi-classical Bethe equations introduced in the context Generalised Hydrodynamics,
that accounts for the two-body scattering shift and encodes factorised scattering. A non-rigorous
derivation from these reproduces the kinetic equation of the KdV soliton gas, first proposed by
Gennady El in 2003 using Witham modulation theory from finite-gap solutions. The results
hold under simple conditions on spectral parameters, and certain physically natural conditions
on impact parameters, as the number of solitons tend to infinity. No randomness is required.
Our proof is based on a novel tau function for the multi-soliton KdV field, which also allows us
to obtain new bounds on the growth of the multi-soliton support and on the supremum of the
field and its derivatives. We believe the methods are generalisable to other solitonic models.

Introduction
The Korteweg-de-Vries (KdV) equation
∂tu + 6u∂xu + ∂3
xu = 0
(1.1)
is a paradigmatic model of integrable PDEs [1, 2, 3]. One of its main properties is that it admits
solutions composed of many solitons. A soliton is a field configuration with exponentially localised
energy, that keeps its shape under the dynamics (1.1) and propagates with constant velocity, like
a particle. Solitonic models such as KdV have received a lot of attention recently in the context
of soliton gases, where one sees random fields u as distributions of solitons, with properties akin
to a gas or fluid. This was first proposed by Zakharov [4], and Gennady El and co-authors
[5, 6, 7, 8] first conjectured the soliton gas kinetic equation governing its large-scale dynamics,
see also [9]. This has been extended to generalised hydrodynamics (GHD), the hydrodynamic
theory for classical and quantum many-body integrable models [10, 11], see [12, 13] for reviews
on soliton gas, and [14, 15, 16, 17] for reviews on GHD.

Interestingly, it is conjectured that integrability gives more: the existence of stable quasi-
particles. Within the KdV context, this says that in a large family of configurations, there is
a meaningful notion of solitons’ positions at all times, up to a “small” imprecision ∆x ≪N

[23], and by Aggarwal [24, 25], which lend much credence to the notion of quasi-particles.

The above is, however, rather abstract. In practice, one may use a “time-of-flight” thought
experiment [14] to observe where solitons are, experimentally realised in the context of GHD
in cold atomic gases [26]. In this procedure, at some time Nt, we put to 0 the field outside a
mesoscopic interval I centred on the position Nx, of length 1 ≪L ≪N, and determine the
spectral parameters of solitons lying on I by letting the resulting field evolve, using the soliton
resolution conjecture [27].
The resulting spectral density divided by L gives the density of
states ρ(χ, x, t): the quantity dxdχ ρ(χ, x) is interpreted as the number of solitons with spectral
parameters within [χ, χ + dχ] and macroscopic positions within [x, x + dx] at macroscopic time
t. See Fig. 1. The soliton gas kinetic equation / GHD equation is a continuity equation for
ρ(χ, x, t). This indirect procedure to define ρ(χ, x, t) is very general, and does not even rely on
integrability. But it does not guarantee the factorisation (1.5) nor the existence of meaningful
solitons’ trajectories. In soliton gas theory the density of state ρ(χ, x, t) is in fact defined via a
Witham modulation procedure from multi-phase, finite-gap solutions (see recent results on how
N-soliton solutions arise from finite-gap solutions [28]), and in GHD it is defined indirectly as
a tool to represent the infinite set of conserved densities [10, 11]. However, the full connection
with a spectral density in N-soliton solutions is still missing.
The lack of a concrete and more precise notion of quasi-particles – of solitons’ positions – has
been up to now a major stumbling block in making sense of, and deriving rigorously, the kinetic
equation / GHD equation in many solitonic models.

Thus, we map, in the case of the multi-soliton solutions, the dynamics of the KdV field to that
of effective positions, defined algebraically in terms of spectral and impact parameters, which
are predictive for local observables of the KdV field. We believe that (1.9), (1.10) and (1.11) are

An important aspect of our work is that we establish precise assumptions for the above results
to hold: simple conditions on spectral parameters, and some density conditions. Our density
conditions are intricate, but physically sensible, and algebraically simple, as they are algebraically
expressed in terms of spectral and impact parameters. They do not require an analysis of the full
N-soliton field or the inverse scattering structures for the KdV equation. We show that all our
assumptions are satisfied in “ultra-dilute” soliton gases, and heuristically argue that they are in
finite-density soliton gases.
Our techniques also allow us to obtain new fundamental results on the KdV N-soliton field:
a bound on its support, and a bound on the field itself and its derivatives, as N gets large, under
weak conditions on spectral and impact parameters2.
Eq. (1.9) has a long history. The kinetic equation / GHD equation strongly suggests that
quasi-particles’ trajectories are obtained by accumulating two-body scattering shifts φij at soliton
crossings. This is the intuitive picture referred to as the collision rate ansatz [6, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35],
a local version of the asymptotic factorised scattering (1.3). It was proposed in [36] that solitons in
classical integrable models should be assigned positions x(t)
i ’s that solve (1.9), as this implements
the collision rate ansatz.
In fact, the special case φij = −a (negative constant) first arose
indirectly as the “contraction map” used to solve the hard-rod model, see e.g. [37]. Eq. (1.9)
also appears to be related to the canonical sine-Gordon soliton coordinates introduced in [21],
and is implemented by the flea-gas algorithm [31], used to solve the GHD equations. Eq. (1.9)
arose more explicitly in its general form for the first time from an analysis of the Euler-scale
limit of Bethe ansatz wave functions in the quantum Lieb-Liniger model [38], sometimes being
referred to as the “semi-classical Bethe equations”. Under the condition φij ≥0 (not satisfied
in KdV), it was shown [39], and further developed in [40], that (1.9) are trajectories of local
integrable Hamiltonian systems with elastic and factorised scattering as (1.3), arising from so-
called T ¯T-deformations. It is not too hard to show that the GHD equation arises for the weak
limit of the empirical density associated to (1.9), see e.g. [23, 36, 38]. It was in fact conjectured
in [36] that (1.9) should give a good description of many-body integrable models at all orders of
hydrodynamics, including their fluctuations, an idea developed numerically in [41, 42, 43].

results of Ferrari, Nguyen, Rolla and Wang [22], that Eq. (1.9) with for φij = −2 min{i, j} de-
scribes solitons’ positions arising from the “slot decomposition” of the box-ball cellular automaton
[44], leading to a proof of the GHD equation [23]. Further, it was shown by Aggarwal [24, 25]

3.3
Effective positions
In the previous Section 3.2, we introduced the magnifying-glass positions of solitons. The centred
form of the tau functions is their main justification. However, as explained, heuristically they
are not true soliton positions – they are affected by a non-trivial metric, a deformation that is
more important away from the observation point x∗, akin to the effect of a magnifying glass.
Our second notion of positions is that of the effective positions xi of solitons. These are
“universal” in that they are not defined with respect to any specific observation point. Essentially,
we would like to define xi as the solution to Xi(xi; χ, y) = xi; as when the magnifying-glass
position equals the observation point, it is a good soliton position. But there may be many
solutions, or none at all. Instead, we introduce a magnifying-glass local scale ∆X, an allowed

around x ≈xi ± ∆x, and the quasi-particle criterium (1.7) should be satisfied. It is indeed from

is taken on a mesoscopic length, Theorem 4.10 in Section 4.4.

5.1
Magnifying glass effect
Consider (3.26), (3.28). Their meaning is as follows: from the position Xi of a tracer soliton,
we subtract half of its scattering shifts with all other solitons j ̸= i which stand on the left of
the observation point x∗(at least a distance ε away), and add those for solitons j on the right,
in order to obtain a quantity, the impact parameter for soliton i, that evolves linearly in time.
The result is that whenever the position Xj of another soliton crosses the observation point, the
position Xi jumps by a scattering shift with that soliton. This jump is exactly what happens
over a full interaction event when soliton i interact with soliton j in a two-body collision. Over
long times and distances, the accumulation of these shifts represent the global effect of factorised
soliton scattering. This is very similar to the system (1.9) encoding the collision rate ansatz.
However, there is an important difference: here we account for solitons that cross the observation
point x∗, instead of those that actually cross the tracer soliton’s position itself as in (1.9). For
positions Xi that are near to x∗, this does not change very much from the viewpoint of large-scale
observations. But for solitons that are far, we introduce an “observation error”. It is as if we
were looking at solitons “through a lens”, with deformations away from the centre of the lens.
In the metric language of [54], the soliton displacements account for the metric change from the
impact-parameter space to real space around x∗, but not for that at positions far from it, and
therefore, soliton displacements that are large do not live in the real-space metric of the position
they represent. This is why Xi’s must depend on x∗, the point where we look.
Assuming that (1.9) give soliton positions that are accurate on large scales – that is, within
the correct metric –, we may heuristically describe the metric change induced by (3.28) away
from the observation point x∗. For, say, Xi < x∗, the difference is in accounting for positions
that lie within the interval (Xi, x∗) (disregarding the ε regularisation of the sign function): on
the right-hand side of (3.28), these give a sign function that is negative, while within (1.9) it is
positive. As φij < 0, for fixed yi, the value of Xi is therefore “too much to the right”. Thus,
the magnifying-glass effect, the deformation of the metric away from the observation point x∗, is
expected on large scales to bring accurate soliton positions, overall, towards x∗. We illustrate this
in Fig. 2. This is, of course, in a way that is not smooth on small scales, because of interaction
effects not accounted for by this simple arguments.
Theorem 4.4 applies to these deformed positions. The “error” can be estimated by noticing
that for a displacement d, the number of crossing missed is proportional to the “true” soliton
density times d. For a finite, bounded density, the true displacements are therefore proportional
to the soliton displacements with respect to the observation point.
The observation error can be eliminated by concentrating on the centre of the lens. This is

5.2
Meaning of the conditions on spectral and impact parameters
Consider Assumptions 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3, as well as the “mesoscopic conditions” (4.54), (4.56),
(4.65), (4.66). What do they mean in terms of the solitons involved?
The minimal value χ∗in conditions (4.6) is natural. Indeed, as χi decreases, the one-soliton
solution (1.2) becomes supported on a larger and larger domain, and is not “localisable” anymore
(cannot be given a meaningful position): it cannot be given a specific position in a local cell
of length L = Nγ. It is likely that the theorem can be generalised to account for a power law,
mini(χi) = N−ω, ω ≥0, we leave this for future works.
The minimal value of differences |χi −χj| in (4.6) is involved in the maximal size of the
scattering shift two solitons can be subjected to. By (3.6), the stretched exponential indicates
that the shift cannot be larger than a power law. Again, this guarantees that, even throughout
interaction events, solitons can be localised.
The maximal value of χi’s in (4.6) is also involved in the maximal size of the scattering shift
two solitons can be subjected to, however here it is simply a power law, this is a subleading effect
compared to the minimal value of differences |χi −χj|. For χi large, the one-soliton solution
(1.2) becomes very peaked, hence this is a regularity condition, and used to bound derivatives
of the KdV field.
The density requirement (4.8), and the bound on the number of solitons around an observation
point Eq. (4.7) are also natural. Indeed, for a density that is too high, or a too large number of
solitons within a region, there are too many scattering shifts for solitons to be localisable. As
discussed above, the density ρd(d) for a cell of radius d is with respect to soliton displacements,
where the lens deformation effect has occurred. Accounting for the additional soliton crossings
not counted in (3.26), the true density would be lower due to the increase in displacements (as
more interaction increases the expansion effect – see Fig. 2). If ˆρ(d) is the true density, a naïve

Finally, the mesoscopic conditions (4.54), (4.56), (4.65), (4.66) are quite natural, and require
that the effective imprecision be small enough, accounting for the fluid cell and for the fact that
the KdV field may grow; and that the fluid cell be large enough, accounting for the delocalisation
effects of spectral parameters, interactions and densities, as well as the fact that the KdV field
may grow.
The conditions (4.6) on χ are simple to verify in specific examples. Conditions (4.8), (4.7)
as well as (4.54), (4.56), (4.65), (4.66) are more difficult to verify in explicit examples as they
necessitate the evaluation of soliton displacements, via the system of nonlinear equations (3.26).
But independent of if the conditions are natural or not, the most important aspect is that
they are expected to be valid in the crucial application of soliton gases, as we now explain.
5.3
How the assumptions hold for the KdV soliton gas
Although the conditions of Assumptions 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3, as well as the mesoscopic conditions
(4.54), (4.56), (4.65), (4.66) are natural in terms of the physics of KdV solitons, it would be
good to verify that they are expected in the most interesting application of our results, that of
soliton gases. We do not have a full rigorous derivation, however we provide here arguments that
suggest that this is the case. These arguments are expressed for the homogeneous soliton gas, as
these are simplest to describe, however similar arguments should hold for inhomogeneous soliton
gases as well. Here, we only discuss Assumptions 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3, but similar arguments can be

Conclusion
In this paper we have proposed a precise meaning to the quasi-particle problem: the problem of
identifying meaningful solitons’ positions in a N-soliton field, in the limit where N is large. Quasi-
particles exist if there is a notion of effective positions of solitons and associated notion of fluid-cell
projection, which only keeps solitons within the fluid cell, and such that the projected field gives
an approximation of the original field on I that becomes exact as N →∞, and is supported on
the fluid cell. We have proposed the explicit, universal form of fluid-cell projections, Eqs. (1.10),
(1.11), in terms of solitons’ spectral and impact parameters, via effective positions which satisfy
the semiclassical Bethe equations (1.9) up to terms o(N). We have then shown how this indeed
holds for the KdV multi-soliton fields, under weak assumptions on spectral parameters, Eq. (4.6),
and natural assumptions concerning densities of solitons. Solving the quasiparticle problem is
an essential step in deriving the kinetic equation / GHD equation for soliton gases: without it,
78


## Page 79


the kinetic equation for the density of states – the spectral-spatial density of solitons – is not
predictive for local observables. Our conditions on densities of solitons are physically natural,
satisfied in ultra-dilute soliton gases, and easy to verify algorithmically, but they are somewhat
intricate. We leave for future works a mathematical analysis of these conditions, including how
they relate to soliton condensates [12, 13].
The correction terms o(N) to the semiclassical Bethe equations (1.9) are sub-leading in the
Euler scaling limit of large space and time at finite, non-zero densities. In this situation, the
semiclassical Bethe equations are known to give rise to the correct Euler-scale GHD equations,
see e.g. the derivations / proofs in [23, 36, 38], therefore our result could form the basis for a
proof of the soliton gas kinetic equation for the KdV model.
Eqs. (1.9) have been conjectured to give a good description of soliton positions in previous
works [36]. This was given credence recently by showing that they are a good approximation for
centres of Lax eigenvectors in thermal states of the Toda model, which are shown to be localised
by Anderson localisation [24, 25]. Our results further confirm this, in a different way, via our
formulation of the quasi-particle problem.
The effective positions, Eqs. (1.9), can be used to propose a general local-equilibrium grand-
canonical measure that admits all conserved quantities, following [39]:

Our techniques involve what we have referred to as “magnifying-glass positions”, Definition
3.3, where solitons are observed with approximate positions around an observation point x∗. Our
main technical theorem, Theorem 4.4, establishes how the KdV field around x∗is controlled by
solitons in a neighbourhood of x∗. Corrections terms are exponentially decaying, and controlled
by the slowest, widest soliton (the minimal impact parameter). This exponential is similar to the
fundamental result (see e.g. [30]) saying how a N-soliton field decays at large distances. Using
this result, which holds under weaker conditions, we have argued that the GHD equation is
reproduced not just for the density of states, but also for different objects: density of solitons at
fixed magnifying-glass radius, Eq. (5.18) with (5.19). This seems to preserve more information
about the distribution of solitons, and the equations we have obtained show how to time-evolve
this information. It would be interesting to understand better what these equations mean.
We have also established a new result about the support of a N-soliton field as N →∞,
showing that it must lie, under density conditions, within an interval controlled by the left-most
and right-most soliton positions, for which we give explicit formulas in terms of spectral and
impact parameters, Eq. (2.33).
The methods are based on the tau-function representation of the multi-soliton solution, and
in particular on the construction of new tau functions with a clear physical interpretation. The
techniques we present are novel, where a representation of the tau function is taken which
guarantees that far-solitons have negligible contributions. This suggests that in soliton theory,
the phenomenon of Anderson localisation of Lax eigenvectors is not necessary in order to be able
to locate solitons – a more in-depth analysis of our assumptions would be required. In particular,
it is a simple matter to construct explicit, non-random spectral and impact parameters satisfying
the assumptions of Theorem 4.4 for any given observation point x∗∈R: for χ this is done in
(2.62), which are regularly distributed, and then set d of finite density regularly distributed,
and y = Cχ(d), Eq. (3.26). Our methods also go beyond the application to thermal states or
generalised Gibbs ensembles: the fluid-cell projection can be applied to any multi-soliton KdV
field that satisfy certain conditions, and randomness or “genericity” appears not to be necessary.
An interesting observation is that the proof is based on the specific logarithmic form of the
KdV two-body scattering shift: the fact that log |χ−χ′| is related to the 2-dimensional Laplacian
operator, or has a strictly negative regular part of Fourier transform. This is a fact that was also
used in [49] in analysing the nonlinear dispersion relations of the soliton gases related to KdV. It
appears as though in classical soliton theory, the two-body scattering shift has special properties
that guarantee that the concepts of quasi-particle and soliton gas make sense. We believe our
proofs could be generalised to a large family of scattering shifts with similar property.
We believe this work opens many directions. Tau functions representations of solitonic solu-
tions are available in many other models, and therefore our techniques should be readily applica-
ble. In particular, they are available in higher-dimensional integrable field equations such as the
Kadomtsev–Petviashvili hierarchy, and it would be interesting to use the techniques developed
here to find locations of line solitons in this context, in view of understanding higher-dimensional
soliton gases [64].
Of course it would be extremely interesting to make the arguments that we have presented

the inclusion of radiative modes, which might be necessary in order to describe more general
measures on the KdV field that are not restricted to the set of multi-soliton fields.
