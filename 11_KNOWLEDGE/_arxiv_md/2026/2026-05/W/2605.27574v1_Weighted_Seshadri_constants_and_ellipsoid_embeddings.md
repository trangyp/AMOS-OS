Weighted Seshadri constants
and ellipsoid embeddings
Jonny Evans
May 28, 2026
Abstract
We explore Seshadri constants associated to weighted blow-ups of complex pro-
jective varieties and demonstrate how to use this notion to construct symplectic
embeddings of ellipsoids. We illustrate the utility of this point of view by providing
constructions of full fillings of CP2 by ellipsoids corresponding to all of the excep-
tional (post-Fibonacci) steps of the McDuff–Schlenk staircase and some non-obvious
embeddings of ellipsoids in ellipsoids.
1
Introduction
1.1
Seshadri constants and symplectic geometry
Recall that:
• the Gromov width of a symplectic manifold (Z, ω) is the quantity πr2 where r is the
supremum over all radii of symplectically embedded balls in (Z, ω);
• the Seshadri constant of a projective variety Z at a point p with respect to an
ample divisor D is defined to be the supremum sup{ε : g∗D −εC is ample}, where
g: Y →Z is the blow-up of Z at p and C = exc(g) is the exceptional divisor.
It has been known since the work of McDuff and Polterovich [19] that there is a close
connection between the Gromov width of a K¨ahler manifold and its Seshadri constants:
the Seshadri constant gives a lower bound on the Gromov width (see Lazarsfeld’s book
[16, Theorem 5.1.22]).
One can generalise this to the problem of embedding several
symplectic balls pairwise disjointly, which relates to the multi-point Seshadri constant.
Biran [3, 4] proved that one can fully fill any symplectic 4-manifold with balls of equal
size provided one has enough balls. In his ECM survey [5], Biran pointed out that one
should be able to use weighted blow-ups instead of ordinary blow-ups and thereby study
symplectic embeddings of ellipsoids.

equivalent to an associated ball packing problem and most subsequent research has taken
this as its basis rather than working directly with the weighted blow-up.
Similarly to Biran’s results, it follows that any symplectic 4-manifold admits a full filling

strongly on the slope of the ellipsoid: the set of possible pairs (a, b) ∈R2 for which there
exists an embedding of E(a, b) forms a staircase-like shape. This staircase has infinitely
many piecewise-linear steps; there is an infinite sequence of steps whose coordinates are
related to the Fibonacci numbers, and which accumulate when the slope approaches the
fourth power of the Golden Ratio. There are also nine “exceptional steps” beyond this
slope. For slopes outside these steps, CP2 can be fully filled (i.e. the packing ratio can
be made arbitrarily close to 1).
1.2
Inflation
Biran’s main tool for constructing multiple balls and McDuff’s main tool for construct-
ing ellipsoids is symplectic inflation, where one modifies a symplectic form along some
curve in a 4-manifold; see for example [18, Proof of Proposition 2.1, Step 2]. Inflation
along unicuspidal and sesquicuspidal plane curves is used by McDuff and Siegel in [21,
Theorem A, E] to construct the full ellipsoid embeddings in CP2 corresponding to the
Fibonacci portion of the staircase. Opshtein [24] showed that ellipsoids with slope p/q
could be constructed by inflating along a curve with “multi-cusp” singularities (points
where several irreducible branches each modelled analytically on yp = xq). In each case,
one needs to pay careful attention to the local model for the inflationary curve.
Already in his ECM survey [5, Section 2.1], Biran pointed out that inflation is a symplectic
analogue of using the Nakai–Moishezon criterion to find an ample divisor. The purpose
of the current paper is to bypass inflation and just use Nakai–Moishezon, giving a purely
algebro-geometric construction of symplectic ellipsoid embeddings.

1.4
Advantages
There are several advantages to working in the category of complex projective varieties.
First, the weighted blow-up is almost always singular, and the intersection theory of
curves on singular projective varieties is well-developed and convenient to use.
One
could further resolve and work with a smooth manifold, but this ends up clouding the
simplicity of computations with additional irrelevant terms. Second, one doesn’t need to
make any assumptions on what the inflationary divisors look like, or how they intersect
the exceptional divisor of the weighted blow-up: one simply applies the Nakai–Moishezon
criterion to find an ample divisor and hence a symplectic form.
Finally, we observe
that the original construction of the exceptional steps by McDuff and Schlenk was quite
involved: they had to enumerate all possible obstructive curves and find the strongest
obstructions in each given interval of slopes. By contrast, as soon as we find a curve D
to which we can apply our construction, we find an ellipsoid.
1.5
Disadvantages and difficulties
There are also several disadvantages. The main disadvantage is that the method is wholly
unsuitable for constructing ellipsoids whose slopes lie outside the steps of the McDuff–
Schlenk staircase, that is when there is a one-parameter family of slopes which yield full
fillings. To construct something arbitrarily close to the volume constraint one would need
to use a certain infinite sequence of divisors D and weighted blow-ups adapted to them, so
that the associated weighted Seshadri constant gets closer and closer to a limiting value
but never quite achieves it; see Example 7.4.2. It seems likely that this infinite sequence
of divisors is precisely what one needs to prove the Nagata conjecture.
When working with weighted blow-ups there are also subtleties: there is not a single
operation which, given a point and a slope, produces a canonical weighted blow-up.
Indeed, one can construct weighted blow-ups by iteratedly blowing up at infinitely-near
points and then contracting all but the last exceptional curve; the choices of which points
one blows up give moduli for the construction; we explore this subtlety in Section 6.2.
Rather than being a difficulty, this is really an advantage: we can make a “general” choice
of points to blow-up, which can be helpful for constructing the right divisor D.

Outline
After recapping some prerequisites and fixing conventions (Section 2), we define what

5.1
Review of Guillemin–Abreu theory
We will make extensive use of the Guillemin–Abreu theory of toric K¨ahler geometry. We
briefly recap what we need from this theory; the reader can learn more from the original
paper by Guillemin [14] or the subsequent work by Abreu [1, 2] which also treats the
orbifold setting. Recall that a K¨ahler structure on a manifold X comprises a complex
structure J and a symplectic form ω which are compatible in the sense that, at every
point p,
ω(Jv, Jw) = ω(v, w)
for all tangent vectors v, w ∈TpX and ω(v, Jv) > 0 for all nonzero tangent vectors v (this
second condition is called tameness: ω tames J). If X is an orbifold locally modelled on
a quotient Cn/Γ by the action of a finite group Γ ⊂GL(n, C) then we can still talk about
K¨ahler structures: they just come from Γ-invariant K¨ahler structures on Cn.
Let ℓ1, . . . , ℓm : Rn →R be linear maps and R1, . . . , Rm be real numbers. The linear
inequalities ℓi(µ) ≥Ri/2 cut out a polytope
P = {µ ∈Rn : ℓi(µ) ≥Ri/2 for i = 1, . . . , m} .
Given this polytope, we can construct:
• the normal toric variety X (a complex orbifold) associated with the inward normal
fan to P. This admits a holomorphic (C×)n-action which has a free Zariski-open
orbit.
We identify this Zariski-open torus with Cn/(2πZ)n via the exponential
map, writing complex coordinates λ + iθ with λ = (λ1, . . . , λn) ∈Rn and θ =
(θ1, . . . , θn) ∈Rn/(2πZ)n, so that the action of t = (t1, . . . , tn) ∈T n ⊆(C×)n is
given by λ + i(θ + t). We refer to these as complex coordinates. The notation λ is
intended to evoke “log-radius”.
• a toric symplectic orbifold (X, ω) together with a moment map µ: X →P. This
construction is due to Lerman and Tolman [17], following Delzant’s construction in
the case of manifolds [10]. This admits a Hamiltonian action of the torus T n which
is free over the interior P ◦⊆P of the moment polytope. The µ-preimage of P ◦is
symplectomorphic to P ◦×T n equipped with the symplectic form ω = Pn
j=1 dµi∧dθi
where (µ1, . . . , µn) are linear coordinates on Rn and (θ1, . . . , θn) ∈T n = Rn/(2πZ)n
are angle coordinates such that the circle action generated by µj rotates θj at unit
speed. We refer to these as symplectic (or action-angle) coordinates.
The orbifolds X and X are diffeomorphic, and the Guillemin–Abreu theory tells us how
to combine the symplectic and complex geometry to obtain a (torus-invariant) K¨ahler

The Farey tree
Even if Z is toric and p is a toric fixed point, not all weighted blow-ups at p are globally
toric: there is a lot of freedom in forming weighted blow-ups, and, in this section, we will
discuss the moduli space of weighted blow-ups at p.
Discussion 6.2.1. Since Ei1 is a −1-curve (Remark 6.1.2), we may contract it. Then one
of the neighbouring curves Ei2 must become a −1-curve. Contracting Ei2 and continuing
in this manner, we contract all the remaining curves in some order Ei3, . . . , Eim until
we get back to Z. Reversing this argument, we see that any weighted blow-up can be
obtained by iteratedly blowing up at points infinitely near to p: write F1 = Eim for the
first exceptional curve, then blow up a point on F1 with exceptional curve F2 = Eim−1,

Definition 6.2.2 (Farey tree). If we label the nodes of this decision tree with the weights
