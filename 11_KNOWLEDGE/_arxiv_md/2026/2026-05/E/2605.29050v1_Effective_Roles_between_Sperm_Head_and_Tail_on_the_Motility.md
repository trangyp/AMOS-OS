Effective Roles between Sperm Head and Tail on the Motility
Robert L. Scott,1 Shobitha Unnikrishnan,1 Ayomide Bolaji,1
Luke Achinger,2 Tomer Avidor-Reiss,2 and Chih-kuan Tung1
1Department of Physics, North Carolina A&T State University, Greensboro, NC, USA
2Department of Molecular, Cellular, and Developmental Biology, The University of Toledo, Toledo, OH, USA
(Dated: May 29, 2026)
In a low Reynolds number fluid environment that microswimmers encounter, back-and-forth mo-
tion cannot lead to net displacement. In mammalian sperm, the mechanical wave propagating along
their single flagellum breaks the cancellation between back-and-forth motion and, therefore, assumed
to define the movement direction. Here, we show experimentally that the movement direction de-
viates from the opposite of the wave propagation direction when sperm move at the interface of
a viscoelastic fluid and a solid substrate. In fact, the oscillation of the movement direction is out
of phase with the oscillation of the tail wave direction, in phase with the head, and the movement
has a larger amplitude than the wave direction. When we tried to reconstruct the movement di-
rection as a linear combination of the head orientation and the wave direction, we found that the
contributions from these two varied dynamically in time. Further, the last bend of the flagellum
does not move in the lab frame (as observed under the microscope). We characterized this as an
approximate semi-holonomic constraint on the speed of wave propagation, flagellar sliding, and cell
forward movement. Overall, our results highlight the appearance of head and tail taking up roles in
directing sperm motility.
INTRODUCTION
Across length scales, living organisms often need to
move in a fluid to perform physiological functions [1].
Due to their sizes, microorganisms, such as bacteria, al-
gae, and sperm, exist under the low Reynolds number
limit (Re ≪1), where transient terms of the Navier-
Stokes equation become negligible [2].
Therefore, the
fluid flow generated by a boundary movement reaches its
laminar steady state almost instantaneously, the reverse
motion of the boundary generates the exact opposite of
the flow, and the flow speed is exactly proportional to
the speed of the boundary movement [3]. As a result of
this linearity, back-and-forth motion leads to no net flow,
and no net displacement of the organism occurs. This is
known as the scallop theorem [2].
During their forward movement, for a microorganism
with one flagellum or multiple flagella that form a single
bundle, there is typically a mechanical wave sustained on
their flagellum or the bundle. In the classic case of Es-
cherichia coli, during their “run” phase, several flagella
form a bundle, and a helical wave (akin to a circularly po-
larized electromagnetic wave) propagates backward [4].
In the case of Vibrio alginolyticus, which has only one
flagellum, a similar helical wave was reported, and the
movement of the cell remarkably reverses when the rota-
tion of the helical flagellum reverses [5]. In mammalian
sperm, some helical beating patterns have been reported
in watery lab medium [6]. It has been known that, how-
ever, in an environment more similar to their natural
environment in the female reproductive tract, i.e.
at
the interface of viscoelastic mucus and solid tissue, their
beating is mostly planarized (akin to a linearly polarized
EM wave) [7, 8]. Nevertheless, a mechanical wave is still
propagating backward along the flagellum or flagella. In
all cases, this wave serves as the origin of locomotion, and
the cell is thought to be driven in the direction opposite
to the wave propagation [9–11].
For decades, it has been known that the generation of
the wave involves active bending of the flagellum along
the flagellum length in sperm [12].
Active bending of
the flagellum is produced by dynein motors that are dis-
tributed throughout the axoneme of the flagellum, by
coordinating their activities and moving different strains
of the microtubules relative to each other to generate
local curvature.
This curvature then couples with the
elasticity of the flagellum and other dynein activities to
propagate a mechanical wave from the proximal to the
distal end [13]. There is no known motors outside the
axoneme[14], and the sperm head is assumed to be a pas-
sive carriage pushed by the flagellum and takes limited
role in motility.
Within a low Reynolds number regime, direct solid-
solid interactions generate entropy, therefore a source
of irreversibility [15].
Recent investigations into mam-
malian sperm motility have revealed several notable in-
teractions between sperm and solid substrates, especially
when they move at the interface of viscoelastic fluid and
solid. Sperm have been shown to move preferentially to-
ward and along solid surfaces [16, 17].
Flocking kind
of long-range collective behaviors have been reported in
different mammalian species of sperm, including bovine
[18–20]. While self-propelled particles are known to be
able to generate flocks [21, 22], at the low Reynolds num-
ber limit, flocking was found to be unstable when the
momentum between the self-propelled particles and the
surrounding fluid needs to be conserved [23].
There-
fore, the observation of sperm flocks suggests direct mo-
mentum transfer between the sperm and the solid sub-
strate. Further, through direct imaging, our group re-
ported that sperm appeared to exploit kinetic friction
against solid substrates for locomotion, simultaneously

For microswimmers, the ability to control the random-
ization of the movement direction is an integral part of
their motility mechanism. In E. coli, when at least one
flagellum rotates in the opposite direction, the bundle
falls apart, and the whole cell stops and “tumbles”, ran-
domly reorienting at a moment [26, 27]. In V. alginolyti-
cus, a buckling at the base of the flagellum [28] results
in a “flick” motion [5], which also randomizes orientation
at a moment. Mammalian sperm, on the other hand, do
not have such single-event direction randomization. It
has been thought that sperm change movement direction
through changing the direction of flagellum [29]. We aim
to examine this experimentally.
EXTRACTION OF THE BEATING OF SPERM
FLAGELLUM
Sperm thawed from a frozen bovine semen sample was
seeded into a microfluidic device through a seeding port,
and the device was imaged on an inverted microscope
as shown in Fig. 1(a).
The channel within the device
was filled with 1% methylcellulose dissolved in standard
bovine sperm medium, which is known to planarize the
tail beating of bovine sperm [24]. Videos were recorded
at 200 frames per second with a 20X objective, with
the camera focused on cells moving at the upper sur-
face (PDMS) of the channel. To facilitate the analysis
of the movement of the head and flagellum of individual
sperm, we employed the machine learning tool SLEAP
[30].
A model was manually trained to automatically
track cells throughout the videos by placing a skeleton of
5 points representing the head and 32 points represent-
ing the flagellum for each time point t [Fig. 1(b)]. After
tracking, the exported tail points were used as nodes to fit
a second-order B-spline with 4000-5000 points [31], and
the resulting curve was resampled into fixed increments
of 0.1 µm in the arc length from the head s. The angle
spanned between the flagellum and the x axis, θ(s, t),
and the curvature κ(s, t) = ∂θ/∂s, were then computed,
as shown in Fig. 1(c).
MOVEMENT AND THE OPPOSITE OF THE
WAVE DIRECTIONS DIFFER
To determine the wave propagation direction, we ob-
served, in Fig.
1(c), that the wave propagated along s
at a constant speed v, with constant slope of the stripes
beyond the midpiece region of the flagellum (s ≥15 µm).
This implies κ(s, t) = κ(s −vt). Similar κ(s, t) or θ(s, t)

can also be determined by minimization of the change in

TAIL TO THE ⃗v DIRECTION

and a flagellum, with the latter containing all known
molecular motors. Therefore, θ⃗v is probably determined

number limit; (2) once the linear drift was removed, all
the time series were approximately sinusoidal oscillations,

STATIONARY LAST BEND OF THE
FLAGELLUM
As the sperm head moved forward, the curvature wave
propagated backward along the arc length s, and the
amplitude of κ increased as s increased, with most of
the wave on the flagellum seen as propagating backward.
Here, we report that the last bend of the flagellum (where
the largest curvature was found) did not move in the lab
frame (as observed under the microscope), while the tail
slid along the fixed contour of the last bend until the
distal end of the tail slid past the bend. This appearance
of “stalling” was observed on the interface of the polymer
solution and PDMS. This is clearly seen in Fig. 4(a).
We characterized this quantitatively as a kinematic,
semi-holonomic constraint on the speeds related to the
location of the last bend, and hope that this will lead to
a better understanding of the flagellum bending. Let us
focus on the point on the flagellum with the largest curva-
ture around the last bend, as indicated by the red arrows
in Fig. 4(a), which remained stationary. It was clearly ob-
served that, around the bend, the flagellum slid forward
along the contour at a speed. We could not measure this
speed independently, but by the assumption that the fluc-
tuation in length between this point and the distal end
of the flagellum was minimal, we took the speed of the
distal end (vtail end) as an approximation of this sliding

DISCUSSION
Being able to generate displacement within a low
Reynolds number regime is critical to the fitness and
function of microswimmers. This cannot be achieved by
back-and-forth motion. In the case of sperm, it has been
thought that the propagating wave along the flagellum
picks out the movement direction. While elegant, here
we report that the movement direction is different from
the opposite of the wave direction, from the larger ampli-
tudes of the movement oscillation than the tail wave to
the two oscillations being almost out of phase. In short,
the simple model is not supported by the experimental
data to actually describe sperm motility.
The origin of the discrepancies is curious. In the con-
dition of this study, when sperm moved along a flat up-
per surface of a PDMS microfluidic channel filled with
polymer solution, we previously showed that there was a
slithering kind of motion by the sperm flagellum [24]. In
the study of snake slithering, it has been shown that pe-
riodic redistribution of the weight of the body increases
the oscillation amplitude (although not larger than the
experimental measurements) than an evenly distributed
weight [25]. Clearly, to explain the discrepancies we ob-
served, a mechanism other than the third-dimensional
movement of the flagellum (perpendicular to the solid
surface) is needed.
Here, we present data indicating that sperm head ori-
entation can be seen as approximately steering the move-
ment of sperm. This is conceptually counterintuitive in
two ways. First, this description is against most current
fluid mechanics descriptions of sperm motility, that the
head is seen as a passive object pushed by the flagellum.
Second, there is no known motor protein or other active
element in moving the sperm head. The closest motors
are along the midpiece of the flagellum. On the hydrody-
namics front, we note that it is generally expected for the
settling elongated object to have the long axis parallel to

the settling direction [35–37], which supports our obser-
vation that the elongated sperm head can steer sperm
movement through hydrodynamics. On the motor front,
we clearly recognize that sperm head cannot take up ac-
tive steering role by itself, but it has been reported that
the base where the flagellum and head connect can un-
dergo kinking motion to allow the head a different ori-
entation [38]. Overall, we recognize that the sperm head
does not have active components for itself to perform the
steering function, but the activities of the axoneme some-
how effectively make the head orientation an indicator of
sperm movement, in some ways along the spirit of renor-
malization group treatment, that the microscopic interac-
tions between dynein motors and microtubule elasticity
lead to the appearance that the head steers the sperm
movement.
In single-flagellum bacteria, it has been recognized that
significant reorientation happens when the hook that
connects the flagellum to the head buckles [28]. When
they move forward or backward, the head and the tail
are mostly aligned. When the buckling occurs, the head
acquires a new direction, and then the tail moves into the
direction set forth by the head [5, 39]. Without the back-
ward movement of sperm, here we report that the oscil-
lation of the movement direction is mostly in phase with
the oscillation of the head orientation, and the head ori-
entation mostly has a positive contribution to the move-
ment direction through our fitting parameter β. In both
cases, when the head and the (opposite of the) tail direc-
tion diverge, the movement is seen as driven by the head.
In both cases of bacteria and sperm, there is no motor
actively turning the head. Whether or not the kinking
in sperm involves the buckling instability remains to be
verified, but we observe similarities here.
Another significance about this comparison is that
buckling in single-flagellum bacteria provides a reorienta-
tion mechanism, and this reorientation is seen as a cru-
cial component of their navigation mechanism such as
chemotaxis.
Although sperm do not undergo a single
“flick” kind of reorientation event, there are indications
that the deviation between the movement and head/tail
directions or the changes in sequential movement direc-
tions in sperm also serves as a reorientation mechanism.
This can be seen in Fig. 2(c), that the width of the move-
ment direction change in angle has a width of 0.08 (rad),
as opposed to 4 × 10−9 (rad) from rotational diffusion,
using the diffusion coefficient formula from Ref. [40] (Dif-
fusion coefficient from Ref. [41, 42] yield smaller values),
and the superdiffusion observed in Fig.
2(d) also indi-
cates diffusive randomization in short time scale. Here,
we provide several measures for future study in an envi-
ronment with a chemoattractant gradient. First, if the
frame-to-frame movement direction change does reflect
the reorientation, we expect to see the peak to narrow
under such a gradient. Second, since those are modu-
lated by the head and the tail, we expect the dynamic
modulation of α and β to have more correlated features,
as opposed to the lack of clear correlation we report here.
We lay the foundation for future studies to understand
how mammalian sperm chemotaxis is performed physio-
logically.
We also showed that the movement direction cannot
be fully recovered from the dynamics of the head and
wave alone, suggesting that additional mechanisms con-
tribute to the mechanistic determination of the move-
ment direction. One possibility is the third dimension
(perpendicular to the solid substrate) flagellar beating.
With the flagellum-solid and flagellum-fluid contributing
to the momentum transfer differently, we need to break
up the θs into multiple terms. Better simultaneous mea-
surement of the 3D flagellum beating will be needed for
this analysis.
Lastly, we report the observation that the last bend of
the flagellum does not move in the lab frame; therefore
does not provide locomotion, at least in the lab frame.
We note that there is a conflict with existing report on
human sperm [43]. It is important to note that the cur-
vature wave is still propagating backward along s here,
so depending on the frame of reference, there may or
may not be locomotion involved. Since the flagellum is
seen sliding forward along the fixed contour of this last
bend, and the substrate defines the lab frame, there is a
kinetic friction tracing this contour pointing backward,
making it a drag on the movement of sperm.
More-
over, since sperm flagellum is known to be locally bent
by the dynein motors [12, 13], we assume that the re-
ported kinematic, semi-holonomic constraint is a result
of the self-organization between the dynein motors. This
potentially provides us with clues on the generation of
flagellar bending and test models on bending regulation
[44]. It is important to note that this was observed on
a PDMS surface, with a Young’s modulus of ∼2 MPa
[45, 46]. It is possible that, getting close to the distal end
of the flagellum, whose thickness is tapering down, the
tail itself becomes easier to deform than the substrate,
so that it becomes energetically more costly to propagate
the wave further back. While this is possible, we suspect
that there is another biological function. Otherwise, pre-
sumably, evolution should have led to the shortening of
the flagellum to avoid this drag and additional expendi-
ture of energy, not to mention the cost associated with
the synthesis.
Stronger drag between the end of flagellum and the
solid substrate than between the head and the solid works
against sperm rheotaxis [47, 48], orienting the sperm into
(as opposed to against) the direction of the flow.
In
uterus, the fluid flow can go either toward the oviduct or
cervix due to muscle contraction, and this neutralization
of rheotaxis may or may not be beneficial. We hypothe-
size here that, maybe an “anchored” tail end, along with
high curvature on the tail, amplifies the effect of the head
orientation on movement direction through this “pivot”,
which we report through larger amplitude of the move-
ment oscillation and |β| > 1. Exact mechanistic details
remain to be worked out.

METHODS
A.
Microfluidic Device Construction
The design of the silicon mold used in this project is the
same as in previous works [24, 48]. The channels were 4
cm long, 2.47 mm wide, and 100 µm deep. The mold was
fabricated with a single layer of SU-8 negative photore-
sist using standard photolithography. SU-8 was selected
over etching to ensure clean, sharp channel edges. All
fabrication steps were completed using the instruments
and cleanroom facilities at North Carolina State Univer-
sity (NNF). Microfluidic devices were cast using PDMS
at a 10:1 ratio of base to curing agent; for each device,
13 g of base and 1.3 g of curing agent were combined.
The base and curing agent were mixed thoroughly and
degassed until all air bubbles were removed. The mixture
was poured onto the mold, degassed a second time, and
cured at 65 ◦C for 2 hours. After curing, devices were
cut from the mold, and sperm-seeding and fluid-input
ports were created using biopsy punches.
The PDMS
components were bonded to glass slides following oxygen
plasma treatment at high RF power for 60 seconds. After
bonding, the channel was flushed with ethanol to steril-
ize the interior and then rinsed thoroughly with deionized
water. For experiments, channels were filled with media
equilibrated at 38.5 ◦C under 5% CO2 in humidified air
for at least 2 hours, then returned to the incubator un-
til ready for microscopy. During imaging, devices were
placed in an environmentally controlled chamber main-
tained at 38.5 ◦C. Sperm were seeded into one end of
each device immediately before recording.
B.
Media Preparation
The viscoelastic fluid used in this experiment was 1%
MC dissolved in Tyrode’s Albumin Lactate Pyruvate
(TALP) by weight.
Methylcellulose was added to in-
crease viscosity and elasticity so that the fluid more
closely simulated conditions within the female reproduc-
tive tract [49].
TALP consisted of 99 mM NaCl, 3.1
mM KCl, 0.39 mM NaH2PO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM
HEPES free acid, 2 mM CaCl2, 1.1 mM MgCl2, 25.4 mM
sodium lactate, 1 mM/mL sodium pyruvate, 5 mg/mL
gentamicin, and 6 mg/mL bovine serum albumin (BSA).
The solution was titrated to pH 7.4 using 1 M NaOH.
C.
Sperm Sample Preparation
Frozen bovine semen stored in 500 µL plastic straws
was donated by Select Sires and kept in liquid nitrogen
until use. Straws were thawed by placing them in a 37 ◦C
water bath for 30 seconds. The sample was centrifuged
through two layers (40% and 80%) of Bovipure in Bovi-
Dilute solution at 300 × g for 10 minutes. Solutions were
equilibrated at 38.5 ◦C under 5% CO2 in humidified air
for at least 2 hours before centrifugation. Following cen-
trifugation, the supernatant was removed and the sperm
pellet was resuspended in 3 mL TALP. The suspension
was centrifuged again at 300 × g for 3 minutes.
The
supernatant was removed once more and the pellet was
resuspended in 300 µL TALP, then placed in an incu-
bator at 38.5 ◦C under 5% CO2 in humidified air until
use.
D.
Recording
Imaging was performed using a Nikon Eclipse inverted
phase contrast microscope with a 20× objective and a
Hamamatsu ORCA Flash 4.0 V3 camera. Approximately
10 µL of diluted sperm solution was introduced into a
microfluidic device filled with 1% MC and placed in a
temperature-controlled chamber on the microscope. Af-
ter allowing time for sperm to migrate away from the
entry port, recordings were acquired using NIS Elements
BR software. The camera was focused on the top surface
of the microfluidic device to maintain a clean background
and minimize optical obstruction. A wide field of view
was used to keep cells in frame for long trajectories. Each
video was recorded at approximately 200 frames per sec-
ond.
E.
Machine-Learning-Based Tracking
Sperm cells were tracked automatically using SLEAP,
a machine learning framework for multi-animal pose esti-
mation [30]. Recorded videos were converted from .nd2
to .avi format before being imported into SLEAP. A cus-
tom skeleton was created to mark anatomical features of
interest, including one node at the center of the sperm
head, nodes on the top, left, and right sides of the head,
three nodes along the midpiece region, and 30 nodes dis-
tributed along the rest of the flagellum (Fig. 1(b)). A
training dataset was generated by selecting frames at ran-
dom and manually adjusting the skeleton in each frame
to match the cell’s position and orientation.
The model was trained using the multi-animal top-
down approach, in which the software first identified a
centroid for each cell, cropped around it, and then pre-
dicted node positions using a centered-instance confi-
dence map.
The centroid was defined as the node at
the center of the head. Model performance was refined
through repeated cycles of training and correction. To
optimize accuracy, the crop size and input scaling were
tuned so that the entire cell remained visible while mini-
mizing unnecessary background. Rotation correction was
enabled to allow tracking of cells moving in any direc-
tion. For the centered-instance model, the batch size and
plateau patience were increased to extend training. Hard
keypoints were assigned to nodes near the distal end of
the flagellum, where the model initially performed poorly,
so that greater weight was placed on improving accuracy

in that region. After refinement, the model accurately
placed nodes along the entire cell. An inference step was
run to track all cells and assign each a unique track identi-
fier. After manually confirming node placement accuracy,
model output for each video was exported as a .csv file,
and a separate .txt file containing node positions was

F.
Flagellar Analysis
After exporting tracking data from SLEAP, custom
Python scripts were used to extract and analyze tra-
jectories. The first script read each .csv file, removed
accuracy scores and unused metadata, and organized the
remaining coordinates into continuous trajectories for in-
dividual cells. Tracks shorter than 300 frames were dis-
carded. For each cell and frame, a .txt file containing the
positions of all nodes was generated. The second script
reconstructed the flagellum waveform by fitting a smooth
B-spline to the (x, y) node positions for each frame, pro-
ducing a continuous curve parameterized by l ∈[0, 1].
The spline was evaluated at 1000 closely spaced values of
l to produce a dense curve from which the cumulative arc
length s was computed, giving the true physical distance
along the flagellum. Interpolation was then used to map
any value of s to the corresponding point on the spline,
and the curve was resampled at increments of 0.1 µm to
produce evenly spaced (x, y) points along the flagellum.
The third script performed all flagellar analyses, load-
ing the resampled coordinate files, computing the desired
metrics, and saving results to text files.
