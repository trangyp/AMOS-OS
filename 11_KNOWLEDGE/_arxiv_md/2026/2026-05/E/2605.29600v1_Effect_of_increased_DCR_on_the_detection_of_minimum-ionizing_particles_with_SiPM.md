Effect of increased DCR on the detection of
minimum-ionizing particles with SiPMs
K. Neumann 1 , M. Antonello, L. Brinkmann, E. Garutti and J. Schwandt
University of Hamburg, Institute for Experimental Physics
Luruper Chaussee 149, 22761 Hamburg, Germany
E-mail: katjana.neumann@uni-hamburg.de
Abstract: Radiation damage to a Silicon Photomultiplier (SiPM), as it occurs during the lifetime of
the planned CMS high-granularity calorimeter detector, increases the dark current and degrades the
signal-to-noise separation for minimum-ionizing particles (MIPs) and their detection efficiency. To
investigate these effects, a plastic scintillator tile air-coupled to a SiPM is used to detect MIPs from
a 90Sr source, in a single-channel design similar to the tiles of the CMS high-granularity calorimeter
upgrade. We compared the SiPM responses after actual radiation exposure with responses simulated
in the laboratory by increasing the dark-count rate (DCR) through optical illumination with an LED
light source. This optical method induces no structural damage or deep defects, thus isolating
the effect of increased dark-count rate. Our results show that both radiation-induced damage and
LED-induced dark-count rate increases lead to similar reductions in the MIP signal and the signal-
to-noise ratio. This indicates that the primary factor for the performance degradation is the elevated
dark-count rate itself, rather than additional defects in the silicon. The results demonstrate that
the key effects of radiation damage on SiPMs can be effectively replicated using controlled optical
illumination, providing a practical and easily accessible approach for evaluating and optimizing
SiPM performance under radiation-like conditions in the laboratory.
Keywords: Photon detectors for UV, visible and IR photons (solid-state), Radiation damage to
detector materials (solid state)

Introduction
Silicon Photomultipliers (SiPMs) are pixel arrays of avalanche photodiodes that operate above the
breakdown voltage and are widely used as photon detectors. They offer numerous advantages,
including the ability to detect single photons, robustness, compactness, high photon detection
efficiency, and insensitivity to magnetic fields. However, SiPMs also have drawbacks such as a
high dark-count rate (DCR) at room temperature, correlated noise from crosstalk and after-pulses,
and radiation sensitivity. Since SiPMs are often deployed in harsh radiation environments, such as
particle accelerators, it is essential to study the effects of radiation damage on their performance.
A relevant example is the CMS high-granularity calorimeter (HGCAL), a high-precision sampling
calorimeter composed of silicon and scintillator modules [1]. At the High-Luminosity LHC SiPMs
in the HGCAL will be exposed to radiation conditions with fluences up to Φeq ∼5 · 1013 cm−2 [2].
Parts of this calorimeter use the SiPM-on-tile technology pioneered by the CALICE collaboration
[3], with more than 200,000 SiPMs [4]. Here, the minimum-ionizing particle (MIP) response is used
as a standard candle for the calibration of the single channels, which requires a signal-to-noise (𝑆/𝑁)
separation above 2.5 during the whole lifetime of the detector at an operation overvoltage of Δ𝑉= 1-
1.5 V and at a temperature of 𝑇= −35 ◦C. Radiation damage affects the 𝑆/𝑁, which decreases with
irradiation fluence due to several effects. Most prominent is the SiPM’s DCR increase [5], due to
increasing leakage or dark current, which also increases the SiPMs self-heating [6]. Another effect
is the gain degradation with increasing fluence. For example, a reduction in gain of 20% can be
observed for a fluence of Φeq = 5·1013 cm−2 [7]. The reduction in gain and also in photon-detection
efficiency (𝑃𝐷𝐸) leads to a significant decrease of the signal, which can reach ≈40% for high
fluences as Φeq = 2 · 1014 cm−2 [8]. Further studies show that irradiation leads to an increase of the
breakdown voltage, 𝑉bd [8], and of the turn-off voltage, 𝑉off, by the same amount [9]. In this context,
the breakdown voltage refers to the voltage at which avalanche multiplication sets in, and the turn-off
voltage refers to the voltage at which avalanche multiplication stops. To operate the SiPM at a fixed
voltage above 𝑉bd can compensate this effect. This paper presents a method to compare the SiPM’s
MIP response under increased DCR caused either by true radiation damage or by DC triggered LED
illumination. While radiation damage induces structural changes, the LED method increases the
dark current exclusively through illumination, without inducing defects. The goal is to demonstrate

A readout board is positioned inside the box and directly connected to the SiPM, the SiPM’s

response measurements a 90Sr source with an activity of 2 MBq is positioned above the SiPM. A
DC triggered blue LED (NSPB500AS, 470 nm) is used to mimic the DCR increase induced by
irradiation of the SiPM. For this a DC current is provided by a Yokogawa Gs200 current source to
the LED.

The results indicate that the increase of 𝜎SiPM with increasing DCR is visible for both the
irradiated and the non-irradiated SiPMs, where the DCR is emulated by the random photons of the
DC illumination. Figure 4b shows the MIP response as a function of the dark-count rate. The MIP
response increases with voltage as expected due to higher gain and PDE. Furthermore, it decreases
with increasing dark current or fluence. To compare the data obtained from DC illumination used
to emulate irradiation and the truly irradiated samples, a spline is fitted through the former data
(see Figure 4a). This spline serves as a reference to quantify the differences in response between
irradiated SiPMs and those subjected to emulated irradiation (lower part Figure 4a). A deviation of
≲5% for the fluences of Φeq = 2 · 1012 cm−2 and 1 · 1013 cm−2 and a deviation of ∼20% for the
highest fluence of Φeq = 5 · 1013 cm−2 can be observed. From the work, described in [7], in which
a SiPM of the same pixel size and series was used, it is known that the gain reduces with increasing
fluence. A comparison of the deviation of the mpv of irradiated samples from the non-irradiated
sample with DC illumination replicating the same dark current as for the irradiated ones and the
reduction in gain for different fluences (data taken from Table 3, in [7]) is shown in Figure 4b.
The ratio of the irradiated and non-irradiated samples is plotted as a function of the overvoltage
Δ𝑉= 𝑉bias −𝑉off. A comparable reduction in gain and mpv is observed for the lowest and highest
fluence investigated. For the fluence of Φeq = 1 · 1013 cm−2, a reduction in the MIP response of
approximately 4-5% is observed, whereas the reduction in gain, according to [7], is 8-9%. This
leads to the conclusion that the difference between the data taken with irradiated samples and the
data taken with DC illumination can be exclusively explained by the reduction in gain due to the
irradiation.

4
Conclusion
The MIP response and 𝑆/𝑁ratio for a SiPM-on-tile system are characterized before and after
irradiation with reactor neutrons up to a 1 MeV neutron equivalent fluence of 5 · 1013 cm−2. For a
fluence of 1 · 1013 cm−2 the 𝑆/𝑁ratio for MIP detection is greater than 2.5 if operating the SiPM
slightly below Δ𝑉= 1 V at a temperature of −20 ◦C and an integration length of 𝑡gate = 25 ns.
Further cooling would enable the selection of an overvoltage above 1 V, where the response voltage
dependence is smaller. The presented method of emulating radiation damage through DC LED
illumination provides a straightforward approach to investigate the impact of increased dark-count
rate on the SiPM performance.
Our comparison between irradiated SiPMs and non-irradiated
samples demonstrates, that both methods yield consistent results when accounting for known
radiation damage induced gain reduction. The study confirms that the primary effect of radiation
damage on the MIP response and noise characteristics can be effectively reproduced using controlled
illumination, enabling more accessible laboratory testing and optimization studies on operating
conditions. With this method, we identify operational limits influenced by signal-to-noise ratio
considerations, highlighting the importance of optimizing temperature, voltage and integration
length conditions to maintain performance at higher fluences. Overall, this approach provides a
practical tool to evaluate the behavior of SiPMs under radiation-like conditions, reducing the need
for detailed irradiation studies.
