Engineering Quantum Criticality in the Integer Quantum Hall Regime through a
Screening Layer
C. T. Tai,∗P. T. Madathil,∗A. Gupta, L. N. Pfeiffer, K. W. Baldwin, and M. Shayegan
Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering,
Princeton University, Princeton, New Jersey 08544, USA
(Dated: May 29, 2026)
Disorder-induced localization of electrons and electron-electron interaction are among the most
fundamental problems in condensed matter physics. In two-dimensional electron systems, extensive
studies have led to the emergence of a scaling picture, characterized by a set of universal critical
exponents that govern the transitions between the integer quantum Hall plateaus. From the tem-
perature dependence of the plateau-to-plateau transitions, experiments primarily report κ ≃0.42,
implying a dynamic exponent z = 1, consistent with a theoretical picture where electrons have a
long-range (1/r) interaction. Theory also predicts that z = 2 for short-range electron interaction, but
an experimental verification has remained elusive. Here, we directly probe the influence of Coulomb
interaction on these transitions using a bilayer electron system confined to a GaAs double quantum
well device. The two layers are in close proximity, with an interlayer distance approximately equal
to the magnetic length at the relevant magnetic fields. By tuning the electron density in the top
layer, we access both insulating and metallic phases of the electrons in this layer as a function
of magnetic field, allowing in-situ control of the unscreened and screened interaction strengths in
the bottom layer as it goes through its plateau-to-plateau transitions. In the unscreened case, we
measure κ ≃0.42 consistent with the widely reported value. More importantly, when screening
is introduced, κ is reduced to ≃0.22, implying z = 2. Our results provide direct experimental
evidence for the role of electron-electron interaction in determining critical behavior in the quantum
Hall regime, and demonstrate screening as a powerful tuning parameter for engineering quantum
criticality.
The integer quantum Hall effect (IQHE) [1] stands
as a cornerstone of clean two-dimensional (2D) electron
systems, underpinning fundamental advances in physics
ranging from topology [2–4], precision metrology, [5–7]
to quantum phase transitions and criticality [8–33]. In
its simplest description, the IQHE manifests as a con-
sequence of Landau quantization of the energy levels
of non-interacting electrons in the presence of an exter-
nal magnetic field. This leads to a characteristic finger-
print in electrical transport measurements, namely, the
presence of quantized Hall resistance (Rxy) plateaus ac-
companied by vanishing longitudinal resistance (Rxx) at
integer filling factors (ν).
Between adjacent quantized
plateaus, the system undergoes a continuous quantum
phase transition where extended states emerge that per-
colate throughout the sample, leading to a finite con-
ductivity. These plateau-to-plateau transitions provide
an ideal platform for studying quantum critical phenom-
ena, particularly, the universality of non-interacting and
strongly-interacting systems, in the presence of disor-
der [8–33].
Two critical exponents emerge at quantum Hall
plateau transitions. The first is associated with the di-
vergence of the localization length (ξ). At zero tempera-
ture, ξ diverges at a single critical energy (Ec) according
to the power law ξ ∼|E −Ec|−γ with a universal crit-
ical exponent γ ≃2.4 [11, 18, 20, 22].
The second is
the dynamic exponent (z), which characterizes the di-
vergence of the temporal correlation length (ξτ), given
by ξτ ∼ξz [24].
Experimentally, these exponents can
be extracted from the temperature dependence of Rxy
and Rxx.
Specifically, the derivative of Rxy (with re-
spect to the magnetic field B) at the critical magnetic
field (Bc) and the inverse of the half-width of Rxx be-
tween two successive quantum Hall states both diverge
according to the power law T −κ [11].
κ is linked to
γ and z through the relation κ = 1/γz [14, 15, 19–
22, 24, 28, 29]. Experiments primarily report γ ≃2.4,
and more importantly, κ ≃0.42, implying z = 1 for
the IQH transitions [12, 14, 15, 17, 19–21, 25, 27–30].
Theoretically, z = 1 is expected if the electrons in the
IQHE regime are treated as interacting particles with
long-range (1/r) Coulomb interaction [20, 31, 33]. On
the other hand, in the non-interacting or screened (short-
range interaction) limit, theory predicts exponents z = 2
and κ ≃0.21 [20, 33]. An experimental confirmation of
these exponents, however, has remained elusive for over
three decades, until now.
Here, we demonstrate an experimental platform where,
by introducing a nearby screening layer, we can di-
rectly tune the strength of electron-electron interaction
at quantum criticality. While the use of a screening layer
to modify electron-electron interaction, and thus many-
body phenomena, has been reported in numerous ma-
terial systems [20, 33–38], our experimental study ap-
plies this technique to the problem of quantum criticality
and modification of critical exponents. Our platform is
a bilayer GaAs/AlAs/GaAs device, shown schematically
in Fig. 1(a), consisting of two electron layers in close prox-
imity. We leverage the fact that each electron layer un-

the top layer, we can realize the following two scenarios:
(i) The top layer is placed in a quantum Hall

(ii) The top layer is placed at a plateau-to-plateau
transition so that it is in a metallic (conducting) state
and therefore leads to a screened, short-range interac-
tion between electrons in the bottom layer. In this case
we find κ ≃0.22, consistent with z = 2.
Our results provide direct experimental evidence for
the role of interaction and its nature (long-range vs short-
range) in quantum criticality in the IQHE regime.
Our device consists of two 2D electron systems in close
proximity confined to two GaAs quantum wells (QWs)
of width 18 nm, separated by a 5-nm-wide AlAs barrier,
as shown in Fig. 1(a). The barrier is sufficiently wide
to strongly suppress interlayer tunneling [39]. This can
be seen from the calculated electron charge distribution
(Fig. 1(b)). The as-grown electron densities are ≃5.5
and 6.5 (in units of 1010 cm−2 which we use throughout
this manuscript) in the top layer and bottom layer, re-
spectively. The mobility in the bottom layer is ≃1.3×105
cm2V−1s−1. The device is an etched Hall bar of dimen-
sions 200 µm × 2700 µm (Fig. 1(c)), with alloyed InSn
contacts made to both electron layers. Four finger gates
are patterned on top of the Hall bar arms, three of which
(1, 2, and 3 in Fig. 1(c)) are energized to deplete the
top layer so we can independently measure transport in
the bottom layer [40]. Finger-gate 4 is electrically con-
nected to the drain contact, and together they serve as
the ground for the device. A global top gate (Vt) cov-
ers the active area of the Hall bar and is used to tune
the density of top layer and therefore the magnetic fields
at which its plateau-to-plateau transitions occur.
The
sample was cooled in a pumped 3He system and magne-
toresistance measurements were carried out using stan-
dard lock-in techniques. The value of κ was extracted
through the temperature dependence of the maximum of
the derivative of Rxy with respect to B, which should
follow the expression dRxy/dB|B=Bc ∝T −κ [11].

providing a natural explanation for our observation of
κ values which are very close to the universal value of
κ ≃0.21, the exponent predicted for this regime [20, 33].

non-universality of the measured κ.

unscreened cases of our experiments is consistent with
what has been reported in the literature.
Returning to Fig. 4(b), it is remarkable that the
screened (red) data points exhibit an exceptionally nar-
row distribution, and have an average value of 0.22±0.01.
These observations are both consistent with theoretical
expectations. The reduced spread in κ reflects the screen-
ing action of the top layer which makes the criticality less
sensitive to the exact nature of the disorder potential.
More importantly, the effective screening by the electrons
in the top layer leads to a short-range interaction of the
electrons in the bottom layer, which is essential for ob-
serving a universal value of κ = 0.21 (z = 2), as theoreti-
cally predicted [20, 33]. Our results therefore validate the
theoretical results and demonstrate how quantum criti-
cality can be tuned in-situ in a single device.
We close with two remarks.
First, while the IQHE
provides a unique platform to study the interplay be-
tween localization and interaction, the critical exponents
we report here are not specific to IQHE, but also per-
tain to a broader universality class. In particular, the
scaling behavior in the IQHE is widely understood in
terms of quantum percolation physics, as captured by the
Chalker–Coddington network model [10].
Within this
framework, the plateau transition arises from percola-
tion of equipotential contours combined with quantum
tunneling and interference at saddle points. Our results
therefore directly address the role of electron–electron in-
teractions at criticality in such quantum percolation net-
works. The observation of a reduced exponent κ ≃0.22
in our system, where interactions are effectively screened,
provides strong evidence that interactions can modify the
universality class of the transition. This makes our find-
ings broadly relevant, particularly in the context of un-
derstanding localization and interaction effects in low-
dimensional systems.
Second, our experimental platform can be naturally
extended to the strongly interacting fractional quantum
Hall effect (FQHE) regime. Superuniversality arguments
predict that the same scaling laws govern both IQHE and
FQHE transitions in the presence of long-range Coulomb
interaction [33, 49]. Consistent with this expectation, ex-
periments in the FQHE regime generally report κ ≃0.42
with a reasonable spread in the data points [42, 50, 51].
The fate of superuniversality in the screened (short-range
interaction) limit remains unresolved, even theoretically.
An extension of experiments such as ours to the FQHE
regime can offer a promising route to elucidate the nature
of quantum critical scaling in this unexplored regime.
Such studies present additional challenges. In particular,
achieving the regime d/lB ≲1, where interaction can be
strongly modified, typically requires access to lower car-
rier densities because of the low filling factors involved in
realizing FQHE. At such low densities, disorder-induced
localization and reduced many-body gaps can obscure the
emergence of robust FQHE states. Nonetheless, recent
advances in ultra-high-quality GaAs heterostructures at
very low carrier densities [52], as well as very high-quality,
atomically-thin 2D materials [53, 54] provide a promis-
ing route to explore these questions. These developments
open the possibility of testing interaction-driven modifi-
cations to superuniversality, in the FQHE regime.

In this section, we discuss the filtering procedure we use to obtain dRxy/dB values by
presenting, as an example, case A of the main text (Fig. 2(a)) at T = 0.30 K in Fig.

a window size of 0.1 T. Figure S1(a) shows the smoothed Rxy data (black) overlaid on the
raw trace (green) at T = 0.30 K in case A. For comparison, we show the derivative of
the raw Rxy [dRxy(raw)/dB, showed in green] together with the derivative of the smoothed
Rxy [dRxy(smoothed)/dB, showed in black] in Fig. S1(b). It is clear that dRxy(smoothed)/dB is
significantly cleaner and preserves the underlying features. Finally, the Savitzky–Golay filter
is applied again to the dRxy(smoothed)/dB trace. A comparison between dRxy(smoothed)/dB
and the smoothed dRxy(smoothed)/dB is presented in Fig. S1(c) as black and yellow curves,
respectively.
The filter order is 2 and window size is 0.02 T. The κ values reported in
our study are based on the smoothed dRxy(smoothed)/dB curves. As seen in Fig. S1, the
smoothed Rxy and dRxy/dB are in excellent agreement with the corresponding raw data,
and no artifacts are introduced in our smoothing procedure.

II.
DETERMINATION OF TOP AND BOTTOM QUANTUM WELL DENSI-
TIES
Figure S2 summarizes how we determine the top-layer density (nt) and the gating effi-
ciency in our sample. To tune nt to a desired value, we have to know the as-grown density
of the top layer and its gating efficiency. In Fig. S2(a), we extract the total electron density
from Hall measurements without energizing the finger gates; therefore, the measured density
corresponds to the total density (nt + nb). Because the interlayer separation (≃23 nm) is
only ≃3% of the distance from the surface to the center of the top layer (≃734 nm), we
assume that the difference in gating efficiency between the two layers is negligible. This as-
sumption is supported by the fact that there is no kink in Fig. S2(a) and the six data points
can be well fitted by a single straight line, yielding a gating efficiency of 2.06×108 cm−2/mV.
The as-grown density of the bottom layer is determined by following the field positions
of νb = 4 minima at various Vt values, as shown in Fig. S2(b). The field positions of νb = 4
minima, marked by yellow circles, remain fixed for −0.22 V ≤Vt ≤−0.18 V, indicating
that nt is still finite and screens the applied gate bias. A clear shift of the νb = 4 minimum
emerges at Vt = −0.23 V (red trace), signaling full depletion of the top layer. Starting
from this point, for more negative Vt, nb decreases in accordance with the gating efficiency
obtained in Fig. S2(a). Based on the νb = 4 position at Vt = −0.23 V, we deduce an as-
grown nb ≃6.5 × 1010 cm−2. Combining this with the total density measured at Vt = 0
(Figure S2(a)), we obtain an as-grown nt ≃5.5×1010 cm−2. Knowing the as-grown densities
and gating efficiency, we can then tune nt or nb to the desired values.
