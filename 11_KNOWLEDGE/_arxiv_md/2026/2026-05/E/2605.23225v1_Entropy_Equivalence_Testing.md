Abstract
We introduce the problem of entropy equivalence testing for probability distributions, a
relaxation of the well-studied closeness testing problem, where the distribution testing algorithm
is now only required to distinguish, given samples from two unknown distributions p, q and a
parameter ε ∈(0, 1/2], between p = q and |H(p) −H(q)| ⩾ε (where H denotes the Shannon
entropy). We provide a time- and sample-efficient algorithm for this task, showing that the
optimal sample complexity for this task can be significantly lower than that of closeness testing.
As an application, we leverage this result to provide the first non-trivial testing algorithm for
(standard) closeness of low-degree Bayesian networks, which significantly improves on either the
sample or time complexity of a baseline based on full learning.

Introduction
Distribution testing, a subfield of property testing [RS96; GGR98] concerned with efficient algorithms
for deciding statistical properties of data, has witnessed a significant interest since its systematic
introduction by Batu, Fortnow, Rubinfeld, Smith, and White [BFR+00], with a flurry of works
over the past two decades addressing a range of hypothesis testing tasks, under various models of
distribution testing (see, e.g., [Rub12; Can20b; Can22] and [Gol17, Chapter 11] for surveys and
expositions).
Two of the flagship and most fundamental tasks in distribution testing are identity testing
(and its special case, uniformity testing), and closeness testing (also known as equivalence testing):
in the first, an algorithm is given independent samples from an (unknown) discrete probability
distribution, and must decide whether the distribution is equal to some known reference distribution,
or significantly deviates from it in a meaningful notion of distance (typically total variation distance).
In the second, the algorithm has access to samples from two unknown distributions, and similarly
must decide whether they are equal, or significantly far from each other. Here, “significantly far”
is quantified by an input parameter ε > 0, and the algorithm is required be correct with high
probability 1−δ,1 while minimizing the sample complexity, that is, the total number of samples taken
from the distribution(s) in the worst case as a function of the domain size n, distance parameter ε,
and error probability δ.

amplify this by repetition to any target 1 −δ, at the cost of an O(log(1/δ)) factor in sample and time complexity.

distributions exhibit a low-degree Bayesian network structure, or are consistent with an Ising model.

Our work makes progress on both of these fronts. We first define a relaxation of closeness
testing, which we term entropy equivalence testing, where one is asked to detect if the two unknown
distributions have significantly different (Shannon) entropy:2
Entropy Equivalence Testing (EET): Given sample access to distributions p, q over a known
domain of size n and parameters ε ∈(0, 1/2] and δ ∈(0, 1], distinguish, with probability at least
1 −δ, between the following two cases: (1) p = q, and (2) |H(p) −H(q)| ≥ε.
We note that the “single-distribution” version of this task, when q is fully known to the
algorithm, was recently introduced [CY24], under the name Entropy Identity Testing (EIT). [CY24]
then leveraged their algorithm for EIT to obtain an improved identity testing algorithm for “standard”
identity testing (i.e., in total variation distance) of bounded-in-degree Bayes nets, establishing a
connection between the two aforementioned avenues of research. We build upon and deepen this
connection to obtain, from our algorithm for EET, the first non-trivial closeness testing algorithm
for Bayes nets.

Application to equivalence testing of Bayes nets
As laid out by Canonne and Yang [CY24], one can utilize testing entropy difference as a sub-

checking local (every subset of random variables of size at most d + 1) KL-divergences (see (5)).

Testing Bayesian networks.
A Bayesian network (Bayes net) is a high-dimensional distribution
over n random variables that factorizes into n conditional probabilities: p(X1, . . . , Xn) = Qn
i=1 p(Xi |
Πi), where Πi ∈[n] is called the parent set of Xi. This factorization is associated with a directed

(with Xj being one of Xi’s parents). A distribution p is called a Bayes net if its joint probability
density function factorizes according to G and p is called Markov with respect to G. Learning
Bayesian networks from samples is a fundamental problem in learning theory, and there is a plethora
of evidence that this problem is hard computationally [CHM04; Das99; BCGM25]. On the other
hand, time-efficient algorithms (polynomial in n, the number of random variables) do exist in some
special cases [CYBC24; BGG+25; BGP+23].

T yields an MI testing algorithm over [kA] × [kC] with sample complexity 3T and the same success
probability.
Proof. Map [kA] × [kC] with [n] via some fixed bijection. Using A, we construct a MI tester for PAC
over [kA] × [kC] as follows:
1. Draw 3T i.i.d. samples (a1, c1), . . . , (a3T , c3T ) from PAC.
2. Form T samples of P := PAC, denoted by (ai, ci) for i = 1, . . . , T.
3. Form T samples of Q := PA ⊗PC, denoted by (aT+2i−1, cT+2i) for i = 1, . . . , T. Since the
samples are i.i.d., the a- and c-coordinates of each pair are independent draws from PA and
PC respectively.
4. Run A on the T samples of P and the T samples of Q.
Observe that |H(P) −H(Q)| = |H(A, C) −H(A) −H(C)| = I(A : C). Then:
• If I(A : C) = 0, then PAC = PA ⊗PC, so P = Q and A returns accept.
