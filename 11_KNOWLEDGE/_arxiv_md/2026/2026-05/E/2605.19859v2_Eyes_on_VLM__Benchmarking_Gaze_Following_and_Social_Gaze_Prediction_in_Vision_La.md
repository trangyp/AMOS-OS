Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) have rapidly evolved into general-purpose multi-
modal reasoners with strong zero-shot generalization. In this context, VLMs could
greatly benefit the analysis of human gaze and attention, a central task in human be-
havior understanding that requires reasoning about the physical scene as well as the
activity, interactions, and social context. However, the extent to which VLMs can
reliably understand human gaze and related attentional behaviors remains largely
unexplored. In this work, we present EyeVLM, a systematic evaluation framework
for gaze understanding in VLMs across two complementary dimensions: tasks and
models. To assess gaze understanding capabilities, we focus on two core tasks. The
first, gaze following, i.e. predicting the 2D location where a person is looking at,
has a geometric and visual processing focus, requiring precise understanding of
the human face, attention direction, 3D scene structure and spatial grounding of
attended targets. The second, social gaze prediction, requires social and relational
reasoning over multi-person interactions (e.g., mutual gaze and shared attention),
and may benefit more from the LLM semantic reasoning capabilities within VLMs.
Regarding models, EyeVLM evaluates these tasks in two ways: a zero-shot setting
with a diverse set of state-of-the-art open- and closed-source VLMs, exploring
different prompting strategies; and a fine-tuning approach based on task-specific
QA pairs, studying the impact of model scale and data scale. As benchmarks, we
rely on existing gaze understanding datasets and perform a systematic comparison
with state-of-the-art purely visual models. Overall, our results show that current
VLMs lack precise gaze understanding capabilities. While standard training helps
reduce the gap with visual models, significant improvements are still needed.

Introduction
Understanding gaze is a key component of social cognition, as it provides direct cues about others’
attention and underlying intent [3, 4]. It plays a central role in non-verbal communication, supporting
the inference of intentions, the regulation of interactions, and the establishment of joint attention [5].
Accurate gaze understanding is therefore essential for modeling social behavior and reasoning about
interactions and social context.
In computer vision, human gaze understanding has been extensively studied through dedicated tasks
such as 3D gaze estimation, gaze following, and social gaze prediction. Gaze following aims to infer
a person’s attended target in the scene, typically represented as a 2D point or heatmap. Social gaze
prediction focuses on interaction-level phenomena, including mutual gaze (LAEO) [6], looking-at-
person behaviors (LAH) [7], and shared attention (SA) [8] in multi-person settings. Over the past
decade, the field has developed benchmarks and specialized models for these tasks, evolving from
early convolutional approaches [1, 2] to more recent transformer-based [4] and unified frameworks,
including multi-person [9] and temporal models [10], as well as methods incorporating 3D scene
representations for improved geometric reasoning.
Despite these advances, it remains unclear to what extent current models achieve true scene under-
standing. In particular, gaze reasoning requires situated perception, including understanding the
person’s activity, modeling interactions with objects or other individuals, and grounding predictions
in 3D space. Without such capabilities, generalization and further performance improvements may
be limited, and the gap to human-level understanding may persist.
In contrast, vision–language models (VLMs) have recently emerged as general-purpose multimodal
reasoners, demonstrating strong zero-shot and few-shot transfer across diverse visual understanding
tasks [11–13]. Powered by large-scale pretraining and instruction tuning, modern VLMs can parse
complex scenes, capture high-level semantics, and leverage linguistic knowledge about social interac-
tions and human behavior. This suggests that VLMs may be particularly well suited to address the
limitations of specialized gaze models, especially for tasks requiring social reasoning and contextual
understanding.
This shift raises a key question: to what extent do VLMs understand human gaze, a fundamental
social and perceptual signal underlying attention, communication, and interaction? More broadly, the
adoption of VLMs moves the paradigm from task-specific architectures to generalist models guided
by prompting and lightweight adaptation, raising the question of whether gaze understanding can
be addressed within this framework. However, despite the maturity of gaze-following and social
gaze prediction research, a systematic evaluation of modern VLMs on these tasks is still lacking. In
particular, we lack a unified study covering (i) diverse gaze tasks and datasets, (ii) a representative
set of open and closed-source models under consistent protocols, (iii) the impact of prompting and
adaptation strategies, and (iv) fair comparisons with state-of-the-art pure visual models. Existing
gaze literature focuses on specialized models, while VLM behavioral studies rarely isolate gaze as a
standalone capability, instead treating it implicitly within broader visual reasoning tasks [14, 15]. As
a result, it remains unclear what VLMs can achieve out of the box, when and why they fail, and how
they should be effectively prompted or adapted for gaze understanding.
To address this gap, we introduce EyeVLM, an evaluation framework designed to systematically
assess gaze understanding in VLMs. It is organized along two main dimensions: tasks and models.
First, at the task level, we study two representative problems: gaze following and social gaze
prediction, which exhibit different scene understanding requirements. Gaze following relies on gaze
direction perception, 3D scene structure, and geometric reasoning to localize attended targets within
a situated context (e.g., activity, gestures). In contrast, social gaze prediction emphasizes relational
reasoning over multi-person interactions, such as predicting gaze relationships between individuals.
EyeVLM standardizes task interfaces so they can be queried through language and enables direct
comparison with state-of-the-art visual models.
Second, at the model level, we conduct zero-shot evaluations of a diverse set of state-of-the-art
VLMs, including both open and closed-source models. We study multiple prompting strategies
(Chain-of-Thought, In-Context learning), including variations in instruction templates and reasoning
cues. Given the subtle visual and relational nature of gaze understanding, prompt design is expected
to significantly influence both task interpretation and output structure. Beyond zero-shot evaluation,
EyeVLM also probes adaptability. We construct task-specific QA pairs for supervised fine-tuning

• Analysis of zero-shot performance and prompting. We evaluate diverse VLMs and study the

• Study of fine-tuning. We construct QA pairs for adaptation and analyze how fine-tuning,
model scale, and data scale influence performance.

Related Work
Gaze Following Typical approaches to gaze following adopt a two-branch architecture: one branch
analyzes the broader scene to identify salient objects or regions, while the other analyzes the head
of the person of interest to estimate gaze direction. Information from the two branches is then
fused to produce the final prediction [1, 17, 2, 18–21]. Subsequent work has shown the benefits
of incorporating additional modalities, such as scene depth [17, 19, 21] and human pose [19].
Other studies further explored temporal modeling [2, 22] but have shown limited improvements.
Additionally, multi-person transformer based architectures have enabled single-pass prediction
for multiple individuals [9, 10]. Most recently, Gaze-LLE leveraged large-scale learned visual
encoders with lightweight decoding to reach strong performance, highlighting the value of foundation
representations for gaze [4]. However, these advances largely assume task-specific training and
outputs, motivating EyeVLM to systematically evaluate how general-purpose VLMs handle gaze
following under varying models, prompts, and adaptation settings.
Social Gaze Prediction Early work formulated mutual gaze detection in unconstrained videos
using head pose and geometry cues [23]. LAEO-Net and LAEO-Net++ introduced track-based
spatiotemporal deep models to improve robustness beyond frame-wise decisions [24, 6], and were
followed by other deep learning based models [25, 26]. Fan et al. [8] introduced a model for
in-the-wild shared attention estimation by combining gaze cones from individuals with object
region proposals in the scene. Attention Flow learns shared attention end-to-end, highlighting the
benefit of modeling collective focus rather than post-processing individual gaze [27]. More recent
unified formulations jointly predict gaze targets and social gaze labels [7], and temporal multi-person
frameworks further connect gaze following with social gaze prediction at scale [10]. However, existing
methods remain specialized and dataset-dependent, motivating EyeVLM to systematically evaluate
VLMs on social gaze prediction across model families, prompt strategies, and fine-tuning/data scaling.
Vision-Language Models Vision-language models (VLMs) have shown strong generalization across
visual reasoning tasks through large-scale image-text pretraining and instruction tuning [11–13].
However, gaze understanding remains a fine-grained and socially grounded capability that is rarely
isolated in standard VLM benchmarks. Recent studies have begun to explore the connection between
VLMs and gaze understanding. Gupta et al. [28] use VLMs as zero-shot contextual cue extractors
and inject the predicted cues into a specialized gaze following model. GazeLLM [29] employs
a pretrained LLM to perform spatial-semantic reasoning over structured object representations,
improving specialized vision gaze following architecture through a plug-and-play fusion module.
GazeVLM [30] develops a dedicated VLM for multi-task gaze understanding with RGB, depth,
and textual prompts. Despite these efforts, existing works mainly use VLMs as auxiliary modules,
construct gaze-specific VLMs, or improve specialized gaze models through language reasoning. A
recent exception is VL4Gaze [31], which formulates gaze following as a VQA-style problem and
constructs a multi-task benchmark covering gaze description, localization, and ambiguity recognition.
However, none of these works consider social gaze prediction. Furthermore, none of them do a
systematic comparison between current general-purpose VLMs with specialized gaze models under
both zero-shot and fine-tuning settings. As a result, it remains unclear where modern VLMs stand on
gaze understanding tasks, which tasks they can solve directly, and how much adaptation is needed
to close the gap. This positioning is a necessary foundation for further applying VLMs to gaze
understanding. Motivated by this gap, EyeVLM provides a unified evaluation of VLMs on gaze
following and social gaze prediction across tasks, models, prompts, and adaptation settings.

EyeVLM Framework
We present EyeVLM, a unified evaluation framework for systematically investigating and benchmark-
ing gaze understanding in VLMs, including comparisons with state-of-the-art vision-based models.
In Section 3.1, we introduce the task formulations. Section 3.2 describes and motivates the prompting
strategies we study, while Section 3.3 details our VLM fine-tuning approach.
3.1
Task Formulation
We formulate both our tasks into a unified VLM-compatible visual question answering setting. Each
sample consists of an image I and data B that specifies the subject(s) of interest. This information is
used to generate a prompt Prompt that depends on the task and prompting strategy Q, and further
passed through a VLM to generate a textual output J that included a structured JSON response,
enabling automatic parsing to generate the result of interest O and evaluation. Formally, we have:
Prompt = QT ask,P romptStrategy(I, B)
O = ParseT ask(J) with J = V LM(Prompt)
Gaze Following. In that case, the input B simply consists of the subject head bounding box
b = [xmin, ymin, xmax, ymax], while the output is O = (pio, (x, y)), which comprises the probability
pio ∈[0, 1] that the gaze target lies inside the visible image area, and (x, y) ∈[0, 1]2 denotes the
normalized gaze point. Note that for Qwen models, we report (x, y) ∈[0, 1000]2 as Qwen models
are trained to use such scale for coordinates representation
Social Gaze. Here the input B comprises the two head bounding boxes b1, b2 associated with the
social relation that is being tested: LAEO, looking at each other, SA, shared attention between the
two persons, LAH, is person b1 looking at person b2’s face. In the three cases, O only comprises
psg ∈[0, 1], which denotes the confidence that the queried social gaze behavior is true.
3.2
Zero-shot Prompt Protocols
Many studies have shown that LLMs may perform better with different prompting strategies, es-
pecially in domains requiring logical reasoning such as mathematics and code generation [32, 33].
However, their use in VLMs has shown mixed results: while some works demonstrate improved
accuracy [34, 35], others show that they can hurt performance, especially when perception is uncertain
[36], as it can be in our case. As our goal is not only to measure zero-shot accuracy, but also to
understand which aspects of the gaze tasks are already well captured by VLMs and which are not, we
design four prompting strategies with progressively increasing levels of guidance. These strategies
cover the two main prompting paradigms (Chain-of-Thought and in-context learning, also known
as few-shot prompting), and allow us to systematically analyze prompt sensitivity and impact on
performance measures. Below, we describe their main features, first focusing on gaze following,
before detailing how they are adapted to social gaze tasks. Detailed prompts are in Appendix J and K.
PRBASE. This prompt comprises three components: a role description, a task description, and an
input–output format description. The role description defines the model identity and provides the
general semantic space for task-relevant behaviors:
"You are a vision assistant specializing in human gaze analysis."
The task description specifies the semantic goal of the queried task. For gaze following, this consists
in asking where a person of interest is looking, including (i) the probability that the person’s gaze
target is within the frame, and (ii) the location of the gaze point within the scene. However, these two
outputs are inherently coupled: if the model predicts that the person is looking outside the frame, it
may trivially output a default gaze point (e.g., [0, 0]) rather than reasoning about the most probable
gaze location. To address this coupling, we design the prompt to ensure that VLMs predict a plausible
gaze point even when the person of interest is likely looking outside the frame. Finally, the format
description explicitly constrains the expected JSON output.
PRCOTBASE. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) is a prompting strategy that encourages VLMs to perform
step-by-step reasoning about a given task. While it is widely used to elicit intermediate reasoning, its
benefits can extend beyond simple logical decomposition. In gaze understanding, effective prediction
requires integrating multiple heterogeneous cues, including visual perception (e.g., head pose and eye
direction), scene semantics (e.g., objects and interactions), and 3D spatial reasoning (e.g., relative
positions and visibility). Such multi-factor reasoning is often implicit and not directly enforced by
standard prompts. CoT provides a mechanism to externalize and structure this process, guiding

PRCOTSTRUCT. This prompt provides a more structured reasoning scaffold. Its motivation comes
from the fact that while naive Chain-of-Thought prompting can be ineffective in VLMs, recent works
suggest structured or guided variants significantly improve reasoning, particularly for spatial and
visually grounded tasks [37–39] which share similarities with our task. We therefore guide the model
through a sequence of intermediate questions, including: (1) describing the scene, (2) identifying
the subject’s activity, (3) inferring gaze direction from head and eye cues, (4) enumerating plausible
gaze targets inside the field of view, (5) describing spatial relations between the subject and candidate
targets, and (6) synthesizing these cues into the final prediction. This prompt is intended to encourage
models to attend to information that is particularly relevant for gaze reasoning.
PRINCONTEXT-COT. Studies have shown that directly providing examples to VLMs can be more
effective than relying solely on explanation-only prompts, especially when these examples include
CoT-guided reasoning [37]. To this end, this prompting strategy relies on two carefully selected
in-context examples comprising an image, the question, the CoT process applied to that image, and
the corresponding structured output. We manually write the analysis process for each example,
considering all aspects mentioned in the PRCOTSTRUCT prompt. Samples are from the train sets.
This variant tests whether exemplars help the model internalize both the expected reasoning pattern.
Social Gaze Prompts. They follow the same patterns as the gaze following ones. Only the task is
adapted and defined as whether the queried pair of people exhibit a social gaze relation: looking-
at-each-other (LAEO), shared attention (SA), or looking-at-person (LAH). Chain-of-Thought and
In-Context prompts are adapted accordingly for each of the social task.
3.3
Supervised Fine-Tuning
While the previous section explores how far VLMs can be pushed through prompting alone, we
now investigate whether supervised fine-tuning can further specialize VLMs for gaze understanding.
Despite their strong general-purpose multimodal capabilities, VLMs are not explicitly trained to
capture gaze and attention cues, which are critical for our tasks. We therefore study whether task-
specific supervision can bridge this gap.
QA Pair Construction. Fine-tuning VLMs requires text-based QA pairs, while existing datasets
for gaze following and social gaze prediction do not provide such annotations. To address this,
we construct QA pairs for both gaze understanding tasks. As described in Section 3.1, each query
specifies the task and localizes the person(s) of interest via subject head bounding box(es), combining
a task-specific question template with the corresponding subject location For gaze following, we
further decompose the prediction into two separate queries: an inout query and a gaze_point query.
We adopt this decoupled design because jointly predicting both outputs may introduce undesirable
causal dependencies in the model responses, as discussed in Section 3.2. Querying these variables
separately alleviates this issue and leads to more stable predictions. Accordingly, the task-specific
question templates take the following form:
Gaze Following and Social Gaze Prediction
• In/Out Query: "Is the gaze target of <OnePerson> inside the frame?"
• Gaze Point Query: "Where is <OnePerson> looking at?"
• LAEO: "Are the <TwoPersons> in the image looking at each other?"
• LAH: "Is <OnePerson> looking at <OnePerson>?"
• SA: "Do <TwoPersons> appear to be sharing attention?"
<OnePerson>: the person whose head is within the bounding box [xmin, ymin, xmax, ymax]

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Protocol
We proposed an evaluation protocol enabling us to compare heterogeneous VLMs under a unified
interface, despite differences in architecture, training data, and native output behavior. This includes
comparisons with purely vision-basede models.
Datasets. We rely on three datasets, GazeFollow (GF), VideoAttentionTarget (VAT), and ChildPlay
(CP), for training and evaluation. These datasets cover complementary settings, including static
images, dynamic videos, and natural child–adult interactions, enabling a broad assessment of model
performance on both gaze following and social gaze prediction tasks. See Appendix B for more
details. In their original versions, these datasets provide annotations for gaze following, including
head bounding boxes, gaze target locations, and in-frame versus out-of-frame labels (for VAT and
ChildPlay). VSGaze [10] extends the annotations of several datasets [2, 21, 24, 8] to include social
gaze behaviors. In this work, we use the extended annotations on VAT and ChildPlay to construct the
social gaze prediction benchmark.
Benchmarks. For the gaze following task, we follow the standard train/test splits defined by each
dataset. For the social gaze prediction task, the original data exhibit substantial class imbalance
between positive and negative samples across the three categories, namely LAEO, LAH, and SA,
as well as significant redundancy due to dense temporal annotations, which can bias training and
evaluation. To address these issues, we re-sample the data and construct a balanced subset in which
positive and negative samples are evenly distributed across all three categories. Both training and
evaluation for social gaze prediction are conducted on this re-sampled dataset. Detailed sampling
procedures and the resulting data distributions are provided in the supplementary material (Supp. C).
Metrics. For gaze following, we follow the standard evaluation protocol and report metrics commonly
used in prior work: the average L2 distance between the predicted gaze point and the ground-truth
target (with locations expressed in the [0, 1] × [0, 1] normalized image space), and the average
precision APin/out for in/out prediction. On the GazeFollow dataset, we additionally report the
minimum distance between the predicted gaze point and multiple ground-truth targets. We further
report the 2D angular error between the predicted and reference gaze directions, which measures
how accurately the model infers head pose and gaze direction. For social gaze prediction, we report
precision, recall, and F1 score for the three categories: LAH, LAEO, and SA.
Output Parsing. Since the model outputs JSON text, we first parse the response and extract the
predicted variables for evaluation. In zero-shot evaluation, some models occasionally fail to follow
the required output format. In this case, we map the output to predefined default values to ensure that
all responses remain evaluable under a unified protocol. Concretely, we use a default value of 0.5
for inout, (0.5, 0.5) for gaze_point, and 0.5 for social gaze prediction. This fallback mechanism
avoids discarding malformed generations and penalizes format non-compliance in a consistent way.

on the gaze following task using the GF dataset, and then further fine-tune it on the target task and

4.2
Zero-Shot Prompt Evaluation
We evaluate different prompting strategies on gaze following and social gaze prediction using the
GazeFollow and VAT test sets, respectively, with Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct [16] as a representative
VLM. Results are reported in Table 1. Interestingly, we observe that prompting strategies exhibit
markedly different behaviors across the two tasks.
For gaze following, CoT-based prompts (PRCOTBASE, PRCOTSTRUCT, PRINCONTEXT-COT)
consistently outperform the non-CoT baseline PRBASE, indicating that the task requires structured
reasoning to integrate multiple visual cues. Moreover, PRCOTSTRUCT further improves performance
over PRCOTBASE by explicitly guiding the reasoning process. In particular, prompting the model to
analyze head pose, gaze direction, and spatial relations between the subject and candidate targets
leads to better localization accuracy and improved gaze direction prediction (lower angular errors).
This corroborates prior work showing that structured or guided CoT is more effective than naive
reasoning for visually grounded tasks [37, 39], and that CoT improves performance on tasks involving
spatial reasoning and multi-step inference [34, 35]. Finally, augmenting this guidance with in-context
examples (PRINCONTEXT-COT) provides additional gains, suggesting that exemplars help the model
internalize both the reasoning process and the expected output format.
In contrast, for social gaze prediction, a simple prompt without CoT already achieves strong perfor-
mance, while CoT-based variants bring limited or even negative gains. This suggests that the task
relies more on high-level semantic understanding of the scene and of the situation than on precise
geometric reasoning. In this context, by benefiting from large-scale language and vision-language
pretraining, VLMs are already good at capturing the global context and relational cues required for
social gaze prediction, making additional reasoning scaffolding less beneficial.
4.3
Gaze Following Evaluation
We evaluate three categories of methods: pure vision-based gaze models, zero-shot VLMs using the
PRINCONTEXT-COT prompt, and fine-tuned VLMs with different training strategies. Results on
GazeFollow and VAT are reported in Table 2, and results on ChildPlay (CP) in appendix (Table 6).
Specialized vision-based models remain the strongest for precise gaze target localization, while
zero-shot VLMs lag behind by a large margin across all datasets. Although larger zero-shot mod-
els improve localization accuracy, the gains remain limited. Fine-tuning substantially reduces this
gap: compared to zero-shot Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, its fine-tuned counterpart consistently lowers
localization errors on GF, VAT, and CP. However, even after fine-tuning, VLMs still underperform
the best specialized models in terms of L2 distance and angular error, particularly on VAT and CP.
Joint training on all datasets (AllD) or adding social gaze supervision has limited impact overall
(slightly worse on GF, similar on VAT, slightly better on CP). All this suggests that while VLMs
contain transferable visual representations for gaze understanding, their architectures and training
objectives remain suboptimal for fine-grained spatial prediction compared to dedicated gaze models.

to cluster around ambiguous candidate targets, and that averaging predictions consistently improves
performance as the number of samples increases. More details are provided in Appendix E.

5
Conclusion
We presented a systematic study of gaze understanding in vision-language models by evaluating gaze
following and social gaze prediction under a unified VQA formulation. Experiments on GazeFollow,
VAT, and ChildPlay show that zero-shot VLMs remain limited in precise gaze reasoning, while
task-specific fine-tuning substantially improves performance across datasets and tasks. Notably,
fine-tuned VLMs achieve competitive performance on social gaze prediction, approaching specialized
state-of-the-art models, suggesting that VLMs can effectively capture high-level human gaze relations
after supervision. However, their performance on gaze following remains clearly behind dedicated
gaze models, highlighting persistent limitations in precise spatial grounding and subject-conditioned
localization. Overall, these results indicate that generic vision-language pretraining alone is insuffi-
cient for reliable gaze target estimation, and that gaze understanding remains a challenging capability
for current VLMs. We hope that EyeVLM will serve as a foundation for better understanding these
limitations and for developing future gaze-aware VLMs.

A
Implementation Details
We fine-tune representative Qwen-based VLMs on the constructed QA pairs using LLaMA-
Factory [47]. Specifically, LoRA adapters are inserted into both the self-attention projection layers
(q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, and o_proj) and the gated feed-forward projection layers (gate_proj,
up_proj, and down_proj). The vision encoder and language modeling head are kept frozen. We set
the LoRA rank to 8, the batch size to 64, and the learning rate to 4.0 × 10−4.
Although Qwen3-VL models can be trained with native-resolution images, we resize all images
while preserving their aspect ratios and constrain the total number of pixels to 200,704 (448 × 448),
following the resolution commonly used in prior gaze works. The model is trained with the standard
next-token prediction objective on the target JSON text. Under this setup, gaze understanding is
learned through the conventional language modeling loss over structured textual outputs.
Training is conducted on a single NVIDIA H100 GPU for 2 epochs for the gaze following task. For
social gaze prediction, we observe overfitting during training and therefore select checkpoints based
on the validation sets of VAT and ChildPlay. For fine-tuning with FTspec, we report the results at 500
training steps. For fine-tuning with AllD/GFo+SG, we report the results at 5,000 training steps. To
improve model generalization, we randomly sample a question for each training instance at every
epoch. During inference, we uniformly use the vLLM [48] framework and disable sampling for
deterministic and reproducible predictions.
B
Datasets
We use three datasets, GazeFollow, VideoAttentionTarget (VAT), and ChildPlay, for training and
evaluation. These datasets cover complementary settings, as presented below.
GazeFollow [1] is a large-scale benchmark for gaze following in natural images. It is collected from
multiple public image sources, including SUN, MS COCO, ImageNet, and Places, and contains
diverse daily scenes and activities. The dataset includes 122,143 images and 130,339 annotated
person instances. Its annotations provide head location information together with the corresponding
2D gaze target, making it a standard benchmark for learning gaze target localization from head cues
and scene context.
VideoAttentionTarget (VAT) [2] extends gaze following from static images to videos and emphasizes
temporal modeling in dynamic scenes. It is constructed from 50 YouTube shows and contains 1,331
video clips, 71,666 frames, and 164,541 frame-level annotations. For each subject, VAT provides
dense head bounding boxes and gaze point annotations, and also distinguishes whether the gaze
target is inside or outside the image frame. Compared with GazeFollow, VAT contains less image
diversity but is more suitable for evaluating temporal reasoning (whenever a temporal model is used)
and in-frame versus out-of-frame gaze prediction.
ChildPlay [21] is a benchmark designed for understanding gaze behavior in natural child-centered
interactions. It contains 401 short videos collected from multiple online sources, comprising 120,549
frames and about 257,928 annotated instances. In addition to head bounding boxes and 2D gaze
points, ChildPlay provides fine-grained gaze labels and child/adult identity annotations. Compared
with conventional datasets that mainly focus on adults, ChildPlay presents more challenging inter-
action patterns and greater behavioral diversity, making it particularly valuable for evaluating gaze
understanding in child-adult social scenarios.

One of the key assumptions in this work is that VLMs are capable of understanding head localization
inputs. In this section, we verify this assumption by benchmarking the ability of VLMs to identify
a person’s head given a bounding box as input. The objective is to construct a balanced dataset of
positive bounding boxes containing a person’s head and negative bounding boxes containing none,
then evaluate prompting strategies for head presence detection.
Dataset. We manually selected a subset of 189 images from the VAT test set, drawn from different
videos and excluding images in which heads are unannotated (e.g., small heads in the background).
For each annotated head in each image, a corresponding negative bounding box is generated with
no overlap with any ground-truth head. Figure 3a illustrates both sets: ground-truth positive boxes
are shown in green and generated negative boxes in blue. The resulting dataset comprises 958
question-answering pairs, balanced with 50% positive and 50% negative examples.
Settings. We evaluate the Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct model on this task. All images are resized so that
the longest side is 448 pixels while preserving the original aspect ratio. We compare two bounding
box input representations: coordinates normalized to the range [0, 1000] and coordinates normalized
to the range [0, 1]. In both cases, the model is queried with the following prompt:
Look at the region defined by the bounding box coordinate <bounding box
coordinates>. Is there a person's head inside this bounding box? Answer
only `Yes' or `No'.

experiments in the main paper use only positive head bounding boxes as input.

Limitations
This work has a few limitations. First, due to the construction of VSGaze, the shared attention
annotations in VAT and ChildPlay are limited to attention shared toward human heads. As a result,
our benchmark does not yet evaluate shared attention toward objects or other scene entities. Extending
the annotation space to include object-level and more general scene-level shared attention would
provide a more comprehensive evaluation of social gaze understanding.
Second, although VLMs approach the performance of MTGS [10] on social gaze, it is important
to note that MTGS is trained on VSGaze, whose data distribution differs from the sampled social
gaze data used in this work. VSGaze incorporates additional datasets, including VideoCoAtt [8] and
UCO-LAEO [24], and contains substantially more negative pairs. The original paper finds that this
broader training setup introduces additional challenges and can lead to underperformance relative to
dataset-specific training, which enables models to learn dataset-specific priors.
Third, VLMs are substantially larger than specialized gaze models, which leads to higher hardware
requirements during both fine-tuning and inference, especially in terms of GPU memory. Their
autoregressive next-token prediction mechanism also makes inference slower than direct prediction
models, and this cost becomes more pronounced when using thinking or reasoning modes, where a
complete prediction may take several seconds. A promising direction is therefore to design more
efficient architectures that better exploit VLM features while reducing the computational overhead
for gaze-specific prediction.
Fourth, when fine-tuning VLMs for gaze following and social gaze prediction, the supervision is
applied through text-based language modeling losses rather than direct numerical losses on gaze
coordinates or social gaze labels. This makes the optimization objective less directly aligned with
the target evaluation metrics than in specialized gaze models. Whether language-modeling-based
optimization can provide gradients that are sufficiently consistent with task-specific spatial and
semantic objectives remains an important question for future research.
Finally, evaluating closed-source VLMs at scale is costly because inference requires paid API calls,
and the strongest models are often expensive to query. Therefore, we evaluate closed-source models
on 1k subsets for each task. Although this setting provides a practical comparison under realistic
budget constraints, larger-scale evaluation would yield more robust and comprehensive estimates of
their gaze understanding performance.

Broader Impact
Understanding human gaze behavior has several positive societal applications, including the develop-
ment of automated tools to support clinicians in tasks such as autism screening [49, 50], where gaze
patterns can provide important diagnostic cues. Because VLMs often offer easy-to-use interfaces, our
study can help practitioners better understand the capabilities and limitations of current models when
applied to gaze-related tasks.
At the same time, the increasing accessibility of such tools raises important ethical considerations.
Gaze data can reveal sensitive information about attention, preferences, health, and cognitive state,
creating risks related to privacy, surveillance, and misuse. These tools should therefore only be used
only in appropriate contexts, and with the prior informed consent of the individuals being observed.
