Emergence of a Flow-Assisted Casting Strategy for Olfactory Navigation via
Memory-Augmented Reinforcement Learning
Changxu Zhao,1 Dongxiao Zhao,1 Xin Bian,2 and Gaojin Li1, ∗
1State Key Laboratory of Ocean Engineering, School of Ocean and Civil Engineering,

(Dated: May 20, 2026)
In dynamic flow fields, various animals exhibit remarkable odor search capabilities despite relying
on stochastic detections. Interestingly, there exists an optimal time window for integrating these
detections that maximizes search efficiency. To understand the underlying mechanism, we investigate
the navigation performance of Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents in unsteady flows under varying
memory lengths and flow conditions. Without any predefined models, the agents develop a flow-
assisted casting strategy and adaptively adjust both the geometry of their search trajectories and
the concentration threshold for initiating casting to maximize the success rate. The agent’s average
speed toward the odor source exhibits a non-monotonic dependence on memory length, which can
be explained by the “sector-search” model.
INTRODUCTION
Understanding odor searching in animals within dy-
namic environments is important for deciphering their
survival-critical behaviors [1–3] and for advancing robotic
navigation in low-detectability environments across di-
verse applications, such as targeted therapy [4, 5], pollu-
tant monitoring [6, 7], and deep-sea exploration [8]. In
this challenging task, agents must detect fluctuating, in-
termittent odors against a low-concentration background
and resume the tracking of sparse, tortuous odor fila-
ments after a temporary loss of signal [9–11]. They often
also exploit ambient flow currents to facilitate naviga-
tion toward the target [12–14]. However, the underlying
physics and neural mechanisms for olfactory navigation
remain not fully understood.
Searching for a target of unknown location can be
modeled as a partially observable Markov decision pro-
cess (POMDP) [15], which requires solving a nonlinear
Bellman equation, yet finding its exact optimal solu-
tion is computationally intractable [16, 17]. Depending
on how the observation history shapes the planning or
learning policies, various approximate solvers have been
developed, including rule-driven approaches [18], finite
state controllers [19], infotaxis and its extensions [20–
22], sampling-based methods [23–25], and reinforcement
learning [26–30]. The length of the history, or the mem-
ory for the agent, plays a crucial role in determining
search efficiency. Chemotaxis and other single-step poli-
cies with zero memory can only handle simple scenarios.
Model-based methods, such as infotaxis and sampling-
based approaches, internally encode a perfect memory
to update the belief about possible source locations, of-
ten leading to near-optimal trajectories [20, 24–26]. For
RL agents, finite memories are sufficient for odor search
[27], and an optimal length exists that maximizes both
the success rate and average speed of reaching the tar-
get [22, 30, 31]. It was recently shown that the optimal
memory length matches the typical duration of no odor
detection [30] and later confirmed by measurements of
odor-evoked neuron activities in Drosophila [32]. How-
ever, previous studies typically neglect the kinetics of
agent in dynamic environments, and some rely on pre-
defined models, such as the free-energy based model [22]
or the sector-search model [27], to guide odor search. It
remains unclear how the performance of a search kinetics
of a model-free RL agent with finite memory compares
to these theoretical models.
In this work, we introduce a finite memory length to an
RL agent using a Bootstrapped Random Update (BRU)
algorithm and quantify its influence on odor search per-
formance across a large parameter space. By analyzing
the average behavior of search trajectories, we identify
the key features of the agent’s navigation strategy in a
dynamic flow field. The agents’ average speed is further
analyzed through a geometric analysis of the “sector-
search” casting model to explain the existence of an op-
timal memory length.

We use a Random-updated Recurrent Soft Actor-
Critic (RRSAC) algorithm [33] to embed sufficient com-
plexity into the agent model while adopting a relatively
simple flow configuration and including local fluid veloc-
ity in the detection information to reduce training diffi-
culty. Recent studies on navigation [13] and odor search
[33] have shown that the velocity information does not
qualitatively affect the agent’s performance.
At each
step, the agent’s reorientation angle ∆θ is sampled from
a Gaussian distribution whose mean and variance are de-
termined by a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) policy.
Through the BRU strategy, the RNN is continuously up-
dated using memory segments of finite length TM, each
comprising the agent’s observations, actions, and rewards
collected along its trajectory. In each learning episode,
the location of the odor source and the agent’s starting
position and time are randomly assigned to prevent the
agent from memorizing positional information. See Sup-
porting Information for details on the RL framework and
training settings [34].

In summary, we studied the dynamics of odor search
for a finite-memory-augmented RL agent in a highly un-
steady flow field. Relying solely on locally sensed concen-
tration and flow velocity information, the agent develops
a hybrid strategy that combines the well-known cast-and-
surge behavior [36, 38] with a tendency to move along
the cross-flow direction, which is commonly observed for
navigation in unsteady flows [39, 40]. Depending on the
complexity of the search task, the agent adaptively ad-
justs its scanning angle and the concentration threshold
for casting to optimally balance exploration and exploita-
tion. Furthermore, we showed that the non-monotonic
dependence of the agent’s speed on memory length can
be well described by the “sector-search” model.

S1.2
Curriculum Task Planning
To facilitate the acquisition of robust source-searching strategies in complex flows, we use a
curriculum learning approach [2,3] that progressively adjusts the difficulty of training tasks.
During the early training phase, the start regions are confined to relatively small regions
(dark cyan areas in Figure S1). For every ne episodes, the rectangular start area enlarges
proportionally to include more distant and complex flow zones (light cyan regions in Figure
S1). The curriculum level is defined as l = ⌊n/ne⌋, where ⌊·⌋denotes the floor function, and
n is the current learning episode. This level dynamically sets the size of the start region:
Width = 1.5D + 3.5Dl/ ⌊N/ne⌋and Height = 1.5D + Dl/ ⌊N/ne⌋, where the total number
of episodes N is set to 10,000 in our research.
S1.3
RNN-based Partial Observation Markov Decision Process
Odor searching is a typical Partial Observation Markov Decision Process (POMDP), as
the agent cannot directly perceive the true state (e.g. its relative position to the target).
Formally, a POMDP could be characterized by a 7-tuple (S, A, T , R, γ, O, Ω), where the
variables are as follows:
2


## Page 9


• S is the state space, which is the set containing all possible states of the system (agent
and environment). Ideally, a state s ∈S contains all information needed for optimal
sequential decision-making.
• A is the action space, which is the set of all possible actions the agent can perform.
• T : S × A × S →[0, 1] is the state transition probability, where T(s′|s, a) denotes the
probability of transitioning to the next state s′ after taking action a in state s.
• R : S × A →R is the reward function, where r = R(s, a) represents the immediate
scalar reward the agent receives after taking action a in state s.
• γ ∈[0, 1] is the discount factor, which balances immediate versus future rewards. A
smaller γ prioritizes short-term returns, while a larger γ encourages long-term planning.
• O is the observation space, which is the set of all possible observations the agent can
perceive.
• Ω: S × O →[0, 1] is the emission probability, where Ω(o | s) represents the probability
that the agent perceives observation o in state s.
At each timestep t, the agent interacts with the environment from an unknown state
st.
Under partial observability, it maintains a belief state b ∈B as an estimate of the
true state, where B denotes the belief space.
The agent selects an action at according
to its policy π : B × A →[0, 1], receives a scalar reward rt = R(st, at), and transitions
to a new state governed by a conditional probability T(st+∆t|st, at).
Notably, both the
reward function R and the state transition function T are intrinsic to the environment and
unknown to the agent. The agent’s goal is therefore to learn a policy π that maximizes the
expected discounted reward sum E [P
t γtrt], through trial-and-error interactions with the
environment.
In model-free RL, a recurrent neural network (RNN) is introduced to model the belief
state bt by encoding historical observations {o0, · · · , ot−∆t} into a hidden state ht ∈H [4–6].
The pair (ht, ot) captures the agent’s full history and current uncertainty, allowing it to
infer spatiotemporal dynamics and learn a source searching policy π(· | ot, ht) that selects a
heading angle adjustment ∆θt as action at each step.
S1.4
Random-updated Recurrent Soft Actor-Critic (RRSAC) Al-
gorithm
S1.4.1
Bootstrapped Random Update
To model the agent’s finite memory, a Bootstrapped Random Update (BRU) technique
is employed to update the odor search policy. This policy is parameterized by a neural
network πσ(·) comprising an RNN unit. Stepwise trajectory data are stored sequentially in
the trajectory database, with episode identifiers to separate different searching trails. During
the update phase, a batch Bm of exploration episodes is sampled with replacement. For each
sampled episode, a random starting point is chosen, and policy gradients are computed over

a fixed-length window TM, representing the agent’s memory length. As illustrated in Figure
S2(a), this BRU scheme ensures that the training samples are approximately independent
and identically distributed (i.i.d.), and allows efficient use of partial trajectories.

Each update samples a batch of fixed-length TM

observation, and action are stored as step data to form the trajectory database.
S1.4.2
RRSAC Scheme
The agent employs an RRSAC scheme for odor search, integrating recurrent mechanisms
into the policy of the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm. This policy is trained with the BRU
technique using trajectory data from agent-environment interactions, as shown in Figure
S2(b). At each time step t, the policy receives the previous hidden state ht and the current
observation ot. An internal RNN unit fR updates the hidden state via ht+∆t = fR(ot, ht), and
the policy outputs a stochastic distribution πσ(· | ot, ht), typically Gaussian. An action at is

objective σ∗= arg maxσ Eτ∼ρπσ [P

S1.4.3
Reward Setting
Our reward function rt combines an original component with a potential-based shap-
ing term.
The original reward consists of a chemotaxis bonus α(Xa(t + ∆t) −Xa(t)) ·
∇c/|Xa(t + ∆t) −Xa(t)||∇c|, a time penalty −β∆t, and corrections for boundary crossing,
successful arrival, and timeouts.
To accelerate learning, we incorporate a reward shap-
ing term derived from a potential function Φ(st) = −∥Xa(t) −Xtarget∥/Ua, formulated as
F(st, st+∆t) = γΦ(st+∆t)−Φ(st). This approach improves learning efficiency without altering
the original optimal policy, as documented in the literature [7,8]. An annealing mechanism,
µe−ζn/NF(st, st+∆t), is also applied to gradually diminish the shaping effect. The coefficients
{α, β, µ, ζ} balance these terms to optimize exploration and policy learning.
S1.5
Evaluation Environment Setting

with initial randomization. Notably, the initial point is chosen from the final start region of
larger size shown in Figure S1. During evaluation, the parameterized policy is fixed. Each
trial uses trained parameters for hidden state updates and stochastic action generation. The
