Entanglement-facilitated macroscopic cluster formation in
quantum many-body dynamics
Xiao Wang
,1 Alexander Yosifov,2, 1 Aditya Iyer,2 and Jinzhao Sun1, ∗
1School of Physical and Chemical Sciences, Queen Mary University of London, London, E1 4NS, United Kingdom
2Clarendon Laboratory, University of Oxford, Parks Road, Oxford, OX1 3PU, United Kingdom
(Dated: May 25, 2026)
The capacity of a quantum many-body system to preserve global information—encoded in the
non-local correlations—is a prerequisite for robust quantum computing. Unlike local degrees of free-
dom, large structures offer inherent resilience to noise, but their stability is often compromised by
dynamical fragmentation and local excitations. In this work, we investigate under what initial con-
ditions the quantum dynamics can sustain system-size cluster structures by examining false-vacuum
decay dynamics in a 2D quantum Ising model. We find that while product states rapidly fragment
into uncorrelated domains, initial-state entanglement suppresses the proliferation of true-vacuum
bubbles and stabilises macroscopic connected clusters. We find that this passive stabilisation is not
a mere consequence of entanglement entropy but rather depends on the specific pre-quench correla-
tions. Our results establish a connection between initial-state preparation and the preservation of
global structures, highlighting the role of entanglement for the passive protection of information in
2D quantum many-body simulators.
How to preserve global information in interacting
quantum many-body systems is a question of broad in-
terest in quantum information processing. While tradi-
tional approaches rely on active error correction, passive
protection—the process of information stabilisation by
the intrinsic dynamics—has gained considerable interest
[1–3]. Such mechanisms are often associated with non-
Markovian memory effects [4–6], where collective corre-
lations in the initial state influence the subsequent evolu-
tion and prevent information loss to the environment [7–
10]. Understanding how many-body dynamics can sup-
port system-size structures is thereby a key objective for
quantum simulation and quantum information.
Metastability—the long-lived persistence of an excited
configuration—provides a natural framework for non-
equilibrium dynamics in interacting many-body systems
[11–13], as it captures the competition between relax-
ation and the survival of global structure. A useful per-
spective is provided by the statistics of connected clus-
ters, widely studied in classical percolation theory [14],
where the presence of large components indicates macro-
scopic connectivity. In the quantum setting, such clus-
ters are useful proxies for extended correlations across
the system and thereby provide an operational notion of
global information [15, 16]. However, the conditions that
allow these structures to form and persist remain unclear.
Existing results point to two key ingredients. First, di-
mensionality plays a crucial role.
It is known that in
1D local defects fragment extended domains, whereas in
2D the presence of a nucleation barrier can support large
connected regions [17–19]. Second, most studies of quan-
tum quench dynamics on Rydberg analogue simulators or
quantum annealers rely on product states [7, 13, 20–24],
which are experimentally convenient but lack nonlocal

correlations and tend to generate local excitations that
drive rapid fragmentation [25–27]. Yet, the interplay of
the two has not been systematically studied partly due
to the difficulty of simulating higher-dimensional dynam-
ics by classical methods or preparing correlated states
in analogue quantum simulators.
As a result, the im-
portance of preparing correlated initial states in higher-
dimensional systems to control the formation and persis-
tence of system-size connected domains remains largely
underexplored.
In this work, we leverage metastability to enable in-
formation spreading. To that end, we consider false vac-
uum (FV) decay [13, 21–24, 28–32] in the 2D transverse-
field longitudinal Ising model (TFLIM) as a dynamical
testbed for information stability. Specifically, we study
the non-equilibrium dynamics following a sudden inver-
sion of the longitudinal field, which places the system in
a metastable configuration. Using tensor network (TN)
methods [33–37], we compare the evolution of product
states with that of correlated initial states. We show that
initial-state entanglement qualitatively alters the result-
ing cluster statistics: while product states lead to rapid
fragmentation, correlated states suppress the prolifera-
tion of small domains and instead drive the formation
of macroscopic, connected clusters with sizes comparable
to the system. This minimal model allows us to study
the interplay between nucleation dynamics and quantum
correlations.
Our results demonstrate that an entangled metastable
initial state can enable passive protection of global quan-
tum information in 2D many-body systems. In addition
to the false vacuum decay rates studied in prior works,
we focus on the dynamics of cluster distribution. The
cluster-number densities and largest-cluster distributions
from a metastable initial state indicate macroscopic clus-
ter formation. The statistics of system-size clusters also
provide an operational signature of this stability, showing
that entangled configurations sustain macroscopic clus-

Large clusters from metastable states.— While these
first-passage times quantify global stability, they do not
tell us about the underlying spatial structures that pro-

stability emerges, in Fig. 4 we examine the time evolu-

the |FV⟩state in Fig. 4(a), it is clear that the probabil-

the initial state’s structure, we compare these results to

This

Physically, these observations underscore the stabilis-
ing role of initial-state entanglement in FV decay. After
the quench, the product state |ψ0⟩excites uncorrelated
local modes, leading to the rapid formation and growth
of TV bubbles that destabilise the FV. In contrast, the
DMRG ground state encodes coherent superpositions of
domain-wall configurations that correlate local relaxation
processes and suppress dephasing, thereby extending the
lifetime of the initial order [43]. From a TN perspective,
the finite correlation length and structured entanglement
spectrum of the DMRG state constrain the post-quench
exploration of Hilbert space to more accessible timescales
and delay the thermalisation [44]. This manifests as an
effective separation of timescales: while the Hamiltonian
imposes a linear energy cost for large-domain flips, the
correlated initial state suppresses collective tunnelling
processes, resulting in a long-lived metastable plateau
that is only disrupted at late times via rare multi-spin
events. We note here that the intermediate states dif-
fer qualitatively, indicating distinct dynamical pathways
rather than mere delayed relaxation.

Discussion and perspectives.—Our work identifies a
mechanism by which global information can persist un-
der quantum many-body dynamics without active inter-
vention. Rather than being determined by the spectral
properties [45], we demonstrate how the stability of large
structures depends on both lattice dimensionality (Fig.
3) and the correlation structure of the initial state, see
Fig. 4(a,d). In 2D, the expected presence of a nucleation
barrier enables the formation of large connected domains
[as in Fig. 1(b)], but whether such domains survive the
dynamics is ultimately controlled by the initial entangle-
ment. While previous studies of FV dynamics have pre-
dominantly focused on product states, our work extends
this paradigm to correlated initial states, revealing how
initial quantum structures can alter the decay pathways.
From a quantum information perspective, this estab-
lishes a form of passive information protection, which
does not arise from encoding into a subspace or perform-
ing active measurements, but from the dynamical sup-
pression of the fragmentation into smaller regions. The
cluster statistics, including the cluster-number densities
and largest-cluster distributions (Fig. 4), show macro-
scopic cluster formation.
This finding indicates that
correlations remain distributed across the lattice rather
than being localised into local degrees of freedom. More
importantly, this mechanism is not captured by Pret(t)
alone, which depends on the energy distribution of the
state [46]. Instead, it is the spatial organisation of corre-
lations (encoded in the distribution of connected clusters)
that determines whether global information remains ac-
cessible. In this sense, entanglement can be interpreted
as a structural constraint on the dynamics, limiting the
pathways through which local fluctuations can nucleate.
These observations suggest a route toward engineer-
ing many-body systems that retain macroscopic infor-
mation over long times without active control. In pro-
grammable quantum simulators, such as Rydberg atom
arrays [21, 49], the ability to prepare correlated initial
states could be used to suppress the effective proliferation
of local errors by preventing their growth into system-size
decay processes. An interesting question is whether this
passive protection mechanism can be paired with active
control to enable real-time feedback protocols during the
dynamics to actively sustain or even grow the macro-
scopic cluster sizes. Another question concerns how the
connected-cluster statistics smax scale with lattice size L
and dimension d; for example, whether it scales poly-
nomially with respect to L, i.e., O(Lα) with α < d.
Addressing this is hard for classical computing as 7 × 7
simulations already approach the classical limits of MPS-
based methods for real-time dynamics. In the interme-
diate stage, methods based on tree TNs could extend to
much larger sizes [30, 31], although the associated sam-
pling procedure is less straightforward than using MPS
due to the branching network structure. This motivates
the use of digital quantum computers, where the distri-
bution of the largest connected clusters and their proba-
bilities can be accessed from projective measurements.
In this sense, the cluster statistics play a role analo-
gous to observables in quantum sampling problems, such
as Gaussian boson sampling, where the target distribu-
tions are generally believed to be classically hard to solve.
Probing the emergence and stability of large connected
domains, which may point out a physically motivated
task for quantum computers.

FIG. 6: Quench dynamics of the 2D TLFIM for system sizes (a) 4 × 4, (b) 5 × 5, (c) 6 × 6, and (d) 7 × 7, with J = −1.0,
g = 1.0, post-quench field hq = −0.2, initial symmetry-breaking field h0 = 0.1, and bond dimension χ = 256. In each panel,
the top plot shows the mean longitudinal magnetisation Eq. (3), and the bottom plot shows the return probability Eq. (4).
Blue line corresponds to the quench starting from the Z2-broken correlated DMRG ground state of H0, while the red dashed
line shows the quench starting from the product state |ψ0⟩.
Fig. 6 reveals a striking difference in Eqs. (3) and (4) between product state |ψ0⟩initialisation and the correlated
DMRG ground state of H0. For |ψ0⟩, we can see that Pret(t) decays sharply, indicating rapid dephasing across all
system sizes. In contrast, the correlated state retains finite Pret(t) for significantly longer, with more pronounced
oscillations for smaller lattices [see Fig. 6(a,b)] and delayed decay that signals the presence of a long-lived metastable
regime. We observe this behavior persists even for larger systems, Fig. 6(c,d). Although here finite-size effects are
more apparent, i.e., faster decay and suppressed oscillation amplitudes, the correlated state consistently shows slower
loss of Pret(t). In terms of Mz(t), both states begin from a FV polarization configuration. Here, for |ψ0⟩we observe
sharper initial growth that indicates swift decay via uncorrelated local spins that act as nucleation seeds for the
formation of TV bubbles. Notably, the magnetisation trajectories converge at late times only for the smallest 4 × 4
lattice, which confirms the expectation that initial correlations survive longer in larger systems.
To probe the role of correlations beyond product-state initialisation, in Fig. 7 we compare the quench dynamics
of the correlated DMRG ground state with that of the first excited state of H0 and different eigenstates, which can
help us determine whether intrinsic excitations in the FV act as nucleation seeds that accelerate the decay. Evidently,
the ground and first excited states exhibit distinct initial magnetisations, reflecting their different internal correlation
structures. However, their long-time magnetisation values nearly converge, indicating that the asymptotic state is
largely determined by Hq. The transient dynamics, in contrast, depend strongly on the initial-state structure. The
observed decay of Pret(t) demonstrates a dynamical stability hierarchy associated with the degree of initial-state
correlations. While the first excited state already contains nontrivial correlations relative to |ψ0⟩, it exhibits faster
loss of coherence than the DMRG ground state, indicating that low-energy excitations only weaken (but do not
eliminate) the stabilising effect of initial entanglement. These observations further support the view that metastable
behaviour is controlled not simply by the energy above the ground state of H0, but by the structure of correlations
in the initial state.
