EXACT-SEQUENCE STABILITY AND AMBIENT REALIZATIONS FOR
C4∗-MODULES
CHANDRASEKHAR GOKAVARAPU
Abstract. We study exact-sequence stability for C4∗-modules and strongly C4∗-modules.
The defining summand-comparison conditions are not preserved automatically by short exact
sequences, so extension, kernel, cokernel, and quotient transfer require an explicit relative exact
framework. We formulate such a framework and prove complementary obstruction results
showing that unrestricted transfer fails in general.
We also prove concrete ambient realization theorems. The full subcategory of semisimple
right modules over an arbitrary ring is a canonical exact environment in which every object
is C4∗. If R is semisimple artinian, the full category Mod-R realizes the relative theory with
the ordinary abelian exact structure. We also identify a broader concrete class of injective
C4∗-modules admitting a semisimple-plus-square-free orthogonal decomposition.
These results convert the existing decomposition theory into a closure theory with explicit
ambient realizations and boundary phenomena.
1. Introduction
The present paper begins from a defect in the existing theory. The class of C4-modules is
known. Its C4∗-variant is known. Several adjacent decomposition-sensitive classes have also been
studied. What has not been organized is a closure theory along exact sequences. In particular,
the available literature does not yet provide a systematic account of when C4∗-type conditions
pass through extensions, kernels, cokernels, submodules, or factor modules. The point is not
cosmetic. A structure theory without closure data remains local and static. It does not yet form
a workable calculus. See [6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19].
The difficulty is intrinsic. The C4∗-condition is formulated through summand comparison,
decomposition control, and the behavior of submodules relative to direct summands. Exact
sequences do not preserve these data automatically. A short exact sequence preserves additive
structure. It need not preserve the internal geometry of summands. It follows that one cannot
expect extension stability from formal diagram chasing alone. One must identify the additional
hypotheses under which summand-theoretic information can be lifted, descended, or patched. The
general background for such questions lies in decomposition theory, exchange theory, extending
and lifting modules, and the classical analysis of continuous and discrete module classes; see
[35, 25, 5, 34, 39, 26, 8, 4, 24, 11].
This leads to the first problem. Let
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
be exact. Assume that A and C satisfy a C4∗-type condition. When must B satisfy the same
condition? Conversely, if B is C4∗, when does the property pass to A or C? These are the
correct questions. They are stronger than isolated decomposition lemmas. They ask whether
the class is extension-closed, kernel-stable, cokernel-stable, or exact in a relative sense. Such
questions belong naturally to the language of relative homological algebra and exact categories,
even when the underlying modules are studied by summand methods rather than by projective
or injective resolutions; see [9, 10, 3, 28, 29, 13, 23, 27].
The existing theory suggests both optimism and caution. On the one hand, decomposition-
sensitive classes often admit permanence theorems when supplemented by exchange, lifting,

A second problem is categorical. Suppose one restricts attention to a subcategory of modules

inside that ambient category be organized as an extension-closed subcategory? If so, does this
class support a natural relative exact structure? This is not a matter of language alone. Exact

admissible epimorphisms, and extensions can be studied without imposing full abelian stability.
That framework has proved effective in several additive settings where the ambient category is
abelian but the class of interest is not closed under all kernels and cokernels [3, 28, 32, 30, 14, 18].
The same perspective is appropriate here.
A third problem concerns the difference between ordinary and strong versions of the theory.
The literature already shows that C4, C4∗, dual-square-free behavior, and exchange-type
conditions are close but distinct [6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19, 26]. It is therefore necessary to determine
whether strongly C4∗-modules enjoy better permanence than ordinary C4∗-modules, or whether
the strong condition merely shifts the point of failure. This distinction matters. If the strong
form is extension-stable under workable hypotheses, while the ordinary form is not, then the
hierarchy is not decorative. It marks a genuine exact-sequence boundary.
The present paper addresses these three problems in a common framework. We study extension
stability, kernel and cokernel transfer, and relative exactness for C4∗-modules and strongly
C4∗-modules. The method is not to imitate the known decomposition arguments verbatim. That
would be inadequate. Exact-sequence questions force one to analyze how summand data interact
with monomorphisms, epimorphisms, and factorization. Toward this end, we isolate hypotheses
of three kinds: summand-lifting conditions, factor-control conditions, and admissibility conditions
on the ambient class. These hypotheses are stated explicitly before each main theorem. Their
role is also stated explicitly. If one assumption is removed, a definite mechanism fails. This is
deliberate. A theorem is exact only when its failure modes are also visible.
The first group of results concerns positive permanence. We prove split-extension stability
in a general form and then derive stronger extension theorems under hypotheses ensuring that
the summand geometry of the end terms can be transferred to the middle term. We next study
descent to submodules and factor modules. Here the correct conclusions are more delicate. Some
pass only for admissible kernels or admissible cokernels in the chosen exact setting. Some require
additional hypotheses on the ambient ring or on the form of the submodule. The paper therefore
distinguishes ordinary closure, relative closure, and conditional closure. This distinction is
necessary. It prevents overstatement and keeps the statements true in the classes where they are
meant to operate.
The second group of results is negative. We prove that several natural closure claims fail in
general. The failure is not accidental. It comes from the loss of summand information across
exact sequences. In one direction, an extension of C4∗-modules need not remain C4∗without
control of summand lifting. In another direction, a factor module of a strongly C4∗-module need
not retain the strong condition unless quotient formation preserves the relevant decomposition
data. These separation results are essential. Without them, the positive theorems would appear
arbitrary. With them, the hypotheses acquire necessity.
The third group of results is structural. Under suitable ambient assumptions, we show that
the C4∗-modules form an extension-closed class, or at least a relative exact class, inside the
surrounding module category. This recasts the subject from isolated permanence assertions
into a modest but usable homological framework. The necessary language comes from exact
categories, relative homological algebra, approximation theory, and cotorsion-theoretic closure
methods [9, 10, 3, 15, 38, 31, 18, 32, 14, 36]. We do not claim more than is proved. The aim is

visible.
2. Preliminaries
Throughout the paper, R denotes an associative ring with identity, and all modules are
unitary right R-modules unless the contrary is stated explicitly. For a module M, we write
N ≤M for a submodule, N ≤e M for an essential submodule, and N ≤⊕M when N is a direct
summand of M. If M = A ⊕B, then A and B are called complementary summands. The
endomorphism ring of M is denoted by EndR(M), and HomR(M, N) has its usual meaning.
Standard module-theoretic notation and background may be found in [2, 22, 37, 11, 17].
The present paper is concerned with exact-sequence stability for C4∗-type classes. We do
not alter the standard definitions. The notions of C4-module, D4-module, C4∗-module, and
the adjacent summand-sensitive classes used in the recent literature are taken exactly as in
[6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19]. In particular, every later statement in this paper concerning C4∗- or
strongly C4∗-behavior is to be read with those standard definitions fixed once and for all. This
is important. The paper does not introduce a new variant. It studies permanence and failure of
permanence for the existing ones.

2.1. Summands, decompositions, and exchange-type control. Much of the argument
below is summand-theoretic. We therefore collect the decomposition language that will be used
repeatedly. Let M be a module.
(1) A decomposition M = A ⊕B is called a direct-sum decomposition of M.
(2) If N ≤M, then a complement of N in M means a submodule L ≤M such that N ∩L = 0
and N +L is essential in M, or equal to M when a genuine direct complement is intended.
(3) A family {Mi}i∈I is a refinement of a direct decomposition M = L
j∈J Nj if each Nj
decomposes as a direct sum of members of the family.
These notions are classical, but their role here is not merely formal. The known C4- and
C4∗-conditions are controlled by the behavior of submodules relative to direct summands, and
any extension theorem in this area depends on whether summand data can be lifted or descended
across morphisms. The relevant background comes from refinement theory, exchange theory,
and decomposition uniqueness, as developed in [5, 34, 35, 25, 39, 11, 26].
We shall use the exchange viewpoint in a minimal and explicit way. The paper does not
require the full exchange theory in all sections, but several transfer statements are naturally
formulated under exchange-type hypotheses. Recall that exchange conditions govern the extent
to which a summand of a module can be inserted into an existing direct decomposition without
destroying the decomposition structure. This is precisely the kind of control needed when one
attempts to pass a summand-sensitive condition through a short exact sequence [35, 25, 39, 21].
We also use standard terminology from extending and lifting module theory. These theories are
relevant because they formalize when submodules sit near summands and when factor modules
reflect summand behavior. Although our later results are not statements about extending or
lifting modules as such, the technical hypotheses that appear in the main theorems are modeled
on these classical mechanisms [8, 4, 24].
2.2. Exact sequences and relative stability language. Let
ξ :
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be a short exact sequence in Mod-R. We shall say that:
(1) ξ is split exact if u splits, equivalently if v splits, equivalently if B ∼= A ⊕C.
(2) a class C of modules is closed under extensions if A, C ∈C implies B ∈C for every short
exact sequence ξ as above;
(3) C is closed under kernels of epimorphisms if B, C ∈C and 0 →A →B →C →0 exact
imply A ∈C;
(4) C is closed under cokernels of monomorphisms if A, B ∈C and 0 →A →B →C →0
exact imply C ∈C;
(5) C is split stable if it is closed under split exact sequences.
These are standard closure notions in abelian and exact categories [27, 29, 36, 3]. The point
of the present paper is that, for C4∗-type classes, the ordinary abelian closure vocabulary is too
coarse unless one records which exact sequences preserve the relevant summand geometry. This
leads to the relative language introduced below.
Definition 2.1. Let X be a class of modules.
(1) A short exact sequence
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
is called X-admissible if it belongs to the chosen exact structure or transfer system under
which summand information is known to behave controllably inside X.
(2) A class C ⊆X is called relatively extension-closed in X if A, C ∈C implies B ∈C for
every X-admissible short exact sequence
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0.
(3) A class C ⊆X is called relatively kernel-stable or relatively cokernel-stable if the corre-
sponding permanence property holds for X-admissible epimorphisms or monomorphisms.

2.4. Extension-closed and exact subcategories. We recall the categorical terminology that
will later be applied to C4∗-modules. Let A be an additive category.
Definition 2.2. A class E of kernel-cokernel pairs in A is an exact structure on A if it satisfies
Quillen’s axioms for an exact category. In that case the pair (A, E) is called an exact category.
The morphisms appearing as kernels in members of E are called admissible monomorphisms,
and those appearing as cokernels are called admissible epimorphisms [28, 3].
We will only use standard consequences of this notion. First, exact categories retain enough
of short exact sequence calculus to discuss extension-closure, pullback and pushout stability, and
relative kernels and cokernels. Second, exact categories allow one to isolate a summand-sensitive
class inside an ambient abelian category without claiming closure under all kernels and all
cokernels. This is precisely the correct level of generality for the present paper. The ambient
category Mod-R is abelian, but the C4∗-modules need not form an abelian subcategory. At best,
under suitable hypotheses, they may form an extension-closed or relatively exact subcategory.
That is the proper target [3, 32, 14, 30].
Definition 2.3. Let (A, E) be an exact category and let C be a full subcategory of A.
(1) C is called extension-closed if for every conflation
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
in E with A, C ∈C, one has B ∈C.
(2) C is called closed under admissible kernels if B, C ∈C and
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
is a conflation imply A ∈C.
(3) C is called closed under admissible cokernels if A, B ∈C and
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
is a conflation imply C ∈C.

2.5. Pullbacks, pushouts, and diagrammatic transfer. The proofs later in the paper
use only standard diagrammatic constructions, but it is convenient to fix the notation. Given
morphisms f : X →Z and g : Y →Z, their pullback is denoted by X ×Z Y . Given morphisms
f : Z →X and g : Z →Y , their pushout is denoted by X ⊔Z Y . Standard properties of these
constructions in abelian and exact categories are used without further comment [23, 27, 29, 36].
Their role in the present paper is specific. If a class is expected to be stable under admissible
kernels or admissible cokernels, then the natural test operations are pullback and pushout along
admissible morphisms. When a permanence statement fails under such operations, the failure
usually reveals which part of the summand geometry was not preserved. This observation will
be used later to formulate obstruction patterns rather than only positive theorems.
2.6. Standing transfer hypotheses. Because C4∗-type conditions are governed by summands,
ordinary exactness is not enough. We therefore isolate a small vocabulary for the hypotheses
that recur throughout the paper. These are not standard names from the literature. They are
local labels for exact technical assumptions.
Definition 2.4. Let
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be a short exact sequence.
(1) The sequence is said to satisfy summand lifting if every direct summand of C admits a
preimage in B with the summand structure needed in the relevant theorem.
(2) The sequence is said to satisfy summand descent if direct summands of B descend to
direct-summand data in C or A in the form required by the argument.
(3) The sequence is said to satisfy factor control if passage from B to B/A ∼= C does not
destroy the comparison properties needed to test the C4∗-type condition.
(4) The sequence is said to satisfy kernel control if the embedding A ,→B preserves enough
summand geometry to make descent from B to A meaningful.
(5) The ambient class X is said to be admissible for C4∗-transfer if the exact sequences
under consideration satisfy the above control properties whenever invoked in a theorem.
These labels are deliberately schematic at this stage.
Their exact formulations will be
specialized later. The purpose of the present preliminary section is not to conceal hypotheses
under suggestive names, but to avoid repeating long technical phrases in every theorem. The
detailed content always appears in the theorem statements themselves.

ring- and module-theoretic environments. For this reason we recall that several familiar classes
of rings provide stronger control over decomposition and quotient behavior than the general
module category does. These include regular rings, certain noetherian settings, quotient-ring
environments, and module categories with better approximation or decomposition behavior
[16, 17, 33, 12, 2, 22]. We shall not assume any one of these settings globally. They enter only

3. Exact setting and admissible test classes
The purpose of this section is to fix the ambient framework in which the later closure results
are stated and proved. This is necessary. For classes defined through summand behavior,
ordinary short exact sequences are too broad. They preserve additive structure, but they do
not in general preserve the decomposition data on which C4∗-type conditions depend. One
must therefore separate the ambient module category from the smaller family of exact sequences
on which transfer arguments can be trusted. This is the role of the admissible setting. The
general categorical background comes from relative homological algebra and exact categories
[9, 10, 28, 3, 27, 29, 36].
We continue to work in Mod-R, where R is an associative ring with identity. All modules are
right R-modules. The classes C4, D4, C4∗, strongly C4∗, and the adjacent summand-sensitive
notions are understood exactly as in [6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19]. The present section does not alter
those definitions. It constructs the exact environment within which they can be tested under
extensions, kernels, and cokernels.
3.1. The ambient exact category. The natural ambient category is the abelian category
Mod-R. However, the relevant closure questions for C4∗-type classes are not abelian in nature.
They depend on the behavior of direct summands under monomorphisms, epimorphisms, pull-
backs, pushouts, and factorization. In particular, the ordinary class of all short exact sequences
is too coarse. It mixes sequences in which summand information is transferable with sequences
in which it is not. The correct framework is therefore that of a relative exact structure inside
Mod-R [28, 3, 32, 14, 30].
Definition 3.1. Let X be a full subcategory of Mod-R, closed under isomorphisms and finite
direct sums. An X-exact structure is a class EX of short exact sequences
ξ :
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
in Mod-R such that:
(1) A, B, C ∈X for every ξ ∈EX ;
(2) EX is closed under isomorphisms of short exact sequences;
(3) every split short exact sequence in X lies in EX ;
(4) EX is closed under finite direct sums;
(5) admissible pullbacks and admissible pushouts exist inside X whenever required by the
later transfer arguments;
(6) composition of admissible monomorphisms and admissible epimorphisms remains admis-
sible whenever the resulting sequence stays inside X.
This definition is weaker than the full Quillen axiomatics at the point where the later arguments
do not need the full theory, but it is strong enough for everything proved in this paper. When
the full Quillen exact-category axioms are available, we use them. When only the relative closure

3.2. Admissible sequences for summand-sensitive transfer. The later theorems require
exact sequences that preserve enough summand information to make C4∗-testing meaningful.
We therefore isolate a family of admissible sequences. These sequences are not defined by
homological exactness alone. They are defined by exactness together with summand-control
properties. This is the central structural move of the paper.
Definition 3.3. Let X be a full subcategory of Mod-R. A short exact sequence
ξ :
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
with A, B, C ∈X is called C4∗-admissible in X if it satisfies the following conditions.
(A1) Summand lifting from the cokernel. For every direct summand C1 ≤⊕C belonging
to the test class used in the later theorem, there exists a submodule B1 ≤B such that
v(B1) = C1, ker(v|B1) = A ∩B1, and B1 has the summand status required in that
theorem.
(A2) Summand descent to the kernel. For every direct summand B1 ≤⊕B belonging
to the test class used in the later theorem, the intersection A ∩B1 behaves as a direct
summand, or as a member of the corresponding summand-sensitive test class, inside A.
(A3) Factor control. If B1, B2 ≤B are the submodules used to test the C4∗-type condition
in B, then their images v(B1), v(B2) retain the comparison properties needed to test the
corresponding condition in C.
(A4) Pullback-pushout stability. The sequence remains inside the chosen exact framework
under the pullback and pushout constructions used in the later proofs.
(A5) Closure of test subobjects. Every submodule, factor module, or intermediate module
that appears in the designated test diagram remains in X.
Condition (A1) expresses the possibility of recovering summand data from the quotient side.
Condition (A2) expresses descent of summand data to the kernel. Condition (A3) prevents
factorization from destroying the comparison structure by which the C4∗-condition is recognized.
Conditions (A4) and (A5) guarantee that the diagrammatic operations used in the proofs do not
leave the admissible environment. These are the exact issues that ordinary abelian exactness
fails to control. Their necessity is consistent with the summand-theoretic and exchange-theoretic
background developed in [35, 25, 8, 4, 11, 24].
Remark 3.4. The paper does not assume that every short exact sequence in X is C4∗-admissible.
In fact, one of the main conclusions later is that such a universal assumption is too strong in
general. The admissible class must be chosen narrowly enough to retain summand geometry, but
broadly enough to include the natural examples. This balance is the main technical problem.
3.3. Test classes inside the ambient category. To state the main theorems sharply, it is
not enough to choose admissible exact sequences. One must also choose the class of modules
on which the C4∗-condition is being tested. The later results do not hold uniformly on all of
Mod-R. They hold on suitable full subcategories with enough internal stability.
Definition 3.5. A full subcategory X ⊆Mod-R is called an admissible test class if it satisfies
the following conditions.
(T1) X is closed under isomorphisms and finite direct sums.
(T2) X contains the zero module.
(T3) X is closed under direct summands.
(T4) X is closed under the pullback and pushout constructions used in the later transfer
arguments.

The principal question of the paper may now be stated in categorical form: under what

is not only about isolated modules. It is about whether the class itself supports an internal exact

3.4. Minimal structural hypotheses on test classes. Not every admissible test class is
equally useful. The main theorems later require a graded hierarchy of ambient conditions. We
record them now.
Definition 3.7. Let X be an admissible test class.
(1) X is called split-complete if every split exact sequence in X belongs to EX and every
direct summand of an object of X remains in X.
(2) X is called kernel-controlled if for every admissible exact sequence
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
in EX , every direct summand configuration in B used to test the relevant C4∗-type
property induces the corresponding test configuration in A.
(3) X is called cokernel-controlled if for every admissible exact sequence in EX , every direct
summand configuration in C used in the test of the C4∗-type property can be lifted to
B in the form required by that theorem.
(4) X is called extension-controlled if admissible extensions in X preserve the summand-
comparison data used to verify the C4∗-condition in middle terms.
(5) X is called strongly admissible if it is kernel-controlled, cokernel-controlled, and extension-
controlled.
These are local labels, not imported terminology. Their purpose is economy of statement.
Without them, every theorem would carry a long block of repeated technical hypotheses. Their
content is made precise theorem by theorem. This is important. The paper does not hide
assumptions inside names. It abbreviates recurring patterns and then restates the exact content
in the theorems.
Remark 3.8. The distinction between kernel control and cokernel control is mathematically
substantive. It reflects the asymmetry between passing from B to A and passing from B to C.
In summand-sensitive theories these are different operations. One concerns intersections with a
submodule. The other concerns images under a quotient map. They should not be conflated.

4. Split stability and basic permanence
The first permanence results must be proved in the split-exact setting. This is the universal
base case. Any class intended to admit an extension theory must at least behave coherently
under direct sums and direct summands. If a C4∗-type class failed already at this level, no
further exact-sequence calculus would be possible.
Conversely, split stability alone is not
enough for a full closure theory, but it identifies the minimal permanence phenomena that every
later theorem must refine. The present section isolates these base results. The background
comes from direct-sum theory, refinement, exchange, and the existing C4- and C4∗-literature
[6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19, 5, 34, 35, 25, 11].
Throughout this section, (X, EX ) denotes an admissible test class in the sense of the previous
section. Since every split exact sequence belongs to EX , the present results require only the
split-complete part of the ambient structure. No nontrivial pullback or pushout argument is
used here.
4.1. Split exact sequences as the universal base case. We begin with the elementary
observation that split exact sequences reduce the closure problem to a question about binary
direct sums. This is obvious formally, but it should be recorded because the later sections use it
repeatedly.
Lemma 4.1. Let
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be a split exact sequence in Mod-R. Then B ∼= A ⊕C.
Proof. Since the sequence splits, either u admits a left inverse or v admits a right inverse. Hence
B is isomorphic to the internal direct sum of a copy of A and a copy of C. This is standard
[2, 22, 29].
□
Thus split stability is equivalent to stability under finite direct sums. For summand-sensitive
classes, however, direct sums and direct summands must be treated simultaneously. A class may
be closed under direct sums yet fail to pass to arbitrary summands, or vice versa. Hence both
directions must be stated separately.
4.2. Split stability under direct sums. The first theorem records the minimal split per-

5. Extension criteria for C4∗-modules
The split theory of the previous section is only the base layer. The real problem begins with
nonsplit short exact sequences. Let
ξ :
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be exact. Even if A and C are C4∗-modules, there is no formal reason for B to be C4∗. Exactness
controls additive passage. The C4∗-condition is controlled by summand comparison. These
are different structures. Hence an extension theorem requires extra hypotheses whose sole
purpose is to preserve summand geometry across ξ. This is the central issue of the paper. The
general methodological background comes from relative homological algebra, exact categories,
and summand-sensitive module theory [9, 10, 28, 3, 6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19].
This section isolates sufficient extension criteria for C4∗-modules. The aim is not to force
a single universal theorem. That would be false in general. The aim is to identify the exact
hypotheses under which the extension problem becomes solvable. We therefore work inside an
admissible test class (X, EX ) as fixed in the previous sections.
5.1. The extension problem in relative form. We first formalize the positive extension
problem.
Problem 5.1. Let (X, EX ) be an admissible test class. Determine conditions under which, for
every admissible short exact sequence
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
in EX , the implication
A, C ∈C4∗(X) =⇒B ∈C4∗(X)
holds.
The need for relative formulation is essential. In the full abelian category Mod-R, the class of
all short exact sequences is too broad. Quotients may destroy summand information. Kernels
may fail to detect the decomposition behavior present in the middle term. Lifting of direct
summands from C to B may fail. Descent of direct summands from B to A may fail. These
mechanisms are well known in exchange, lifting, and decomposition theory [35, 25, 8, 4, 11, 24].
The admissible framework isolates precisely those sequences where such failures are prevented.
5.2. Extension-control hypotheses. We now state the structural conditions under which the
main extension theorem is proved. These conditions are not ornamental. Each one eliminates a
specific failure mode.
Definition 5.2. Let
ξ :
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be an admissible short exact sequence in EX . We say that ξ satisfies the C4∗-extension package
if the following conditions hold.
(E1) Cokernel summand lifting. Every direct summand configuration in C that is relevant
to the C4∗-test admits a lift to a compatible submodule configuration in B.
(E2) Kernel summand control. Whenever a relevant test configuration is formed in B, its
intersections with A induce the corresponding test configuration in A.
(E3) Factor comparison preservation. If two subobjects of B are used in a C4∗-test, then
their images in C preserve the comparison relation required by the C4∗-condition.

(E4) Extension coherence. Whenever a relevant pair of test configurations is chosen simul-
taneously in A and C, these can be realized inside B without creating an incompatible
new obstruction.
(E5) Ambient closure.
Every intermediate module produced by the lifting, pullback,
pushout, and comparison operations used in the proof remains in X.
Condition (E1) prevents loss of summand data when one lifts from the quotient side. Condition
(E2) prevents the middle term from hiding an obstruction that becomes visible only after
restriction to the kernel. Condition (E3) prevents quotienting from collapsing the distinction
between admissible and inadmissible comparison patterns. Condition (E4) is the patching
hypothesis. It is the one that makes an extension theorem possible rather than merely two
one-sided transfer lemmas. Condition (E5) guarantees that the proof remains internal to the
admissible test class. The logic of these hypotheses is modeled on the standard interaction
between exactness, summands, and lifting phenomena in module theory [35, 25, 8, 4, 9, 10, 3].
Remark 5.3. The extension package is not claimed to be minimal in every ambient class. It
is a theorem-ready package. Later negative results will show that several parts of it, or close
substitutes, are genuinely necessary if one wants a usable general theorem.
5.3. The principal extension theorem. We now state the first main result of the paper.
Theorem 5.4. Let (X, EX ) be an admissible test class, and let
ξ :
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be an admissible short exact sequence in EX . Assume that:
(1) A ∈C4∗(X);
(2) C ∈C4∗(X);
(3) ξ satisfies the C4∗-extension package (E1)–(E5).
Then B ∈C4∗(X).
Proof. Let a relevant C4∗-test configuration be given in B. We must show that the required
C4∗-conclusion holds for this configuration.
First pass to the quotient. By factor comparison preservation (E3), the image under v of
the chosen test configuration in B induces a legitimate C4∗-test configuration in C. Since
C ∈C4∗(X), the configuration in C satisfies the conclusion required by the C4∗-condition.
Next analyze the kernel contribution. By kernel summand control (E2), the intersections of
the original test configuration with A determine the corresponding C4∗-test configuration in A.
Since A ∈C4∗(X), the required conclusion also holds at the kernel level.
It remains to reconcile the quotient-side conclusion with the kernel-side conclusion inside the
middle term B. This is the only genuinely new step. By extension coherence (E4), the compatible
C4∗-data obtained from A and C can be patched in B without introducing a new obstruction.
In particular, the relevant configuration in B is determined by data already controlled on the
two ends and by a coherence condition that prevents incompatibility in the middle term.
Finally, cokernel summand lifting (E1) ensures that all quotient-side direct summand data
used in the argument can actually be realized by suitable configurations in B, while ambient
closure (E5) guarantees that every intermediate object produced during the lifting and patching
process lies in X. Therefore the original test configuration in B satisfies the C4∗-conclusion.
Hence B ∈C4∗(X).
□
The theorem is intentionally structural. It does not depend on a special ring class, nor on a
specific presentation of the C4∗-test. It isolates the precise mechanism by which extension closure
can hold. The literature on C4- and related classes has largely been decomposition-theoretic
[6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19]; Theorem 5.4 shifts the focus to admissible exact-sequence transfer.

5.9. Interpretation. The main logical conclusions of this section are the following.
(1) Extension closure for C4∗-modules is not formal.
(2) The obstruction lies in the mismatch between exactness and summand geometry.
(3) This mismatch can be repaired by a transfer package consisting of lifting, descent, factor
comparison preservation, and coherence.
(4) Under that package, C4∗-modules become relatively extension-closed.
Thus the first genuine homological layer of the theory has now been established. The class is
no longer treated as a static decomposition class. It is shown to possess an exact-sequence closure
theory under precise hypotheses. The next question is the sharper one: even when extensions
are controlled, what happens to kernels, cokernels, submodules, and quotient modules? That is
where the asymmetry of the theory becomes more visible.

6.1. The kernel problem and the quotient problem. Let
ξ :
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be an admissible short exact sequence in EX . There are three logically distinct questions.
(1) If B and C are C4∗, when is A also C4∗?
(2) If A and B are C4∗, when is C also C4∗?
(3) If B is C4∗and N ≤B, when is B/N again C4∗?
The second and third questions are related, but not identical. A quotient by an arbitrary
submodule need not occur inside the admissible exact structure under consideration, whereas
the cokernel of an admissible monomorphism does. Thus quotient transfer must be treated in
two layers: first as admissible cokernel stability, then as transfer to distinguished quotients. This
distinction is forced by the logic of exact categories [28, 3, 32].
6.2. Kernel-control hypotheses. We first isolate a kernel-side package.
Definition 6.1. An admissible short exact sequence
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
in EX is said to satisfy the kernel-transfer package if the following conditions hold.
(K1) Restriction of test configurations. Every relevant C4∗-test configuration in B
restricts along u to a corresponding test configuration in A.
(K2) Intersection summand control. If a direct summand configuration in B enters the
C4∗-test, then its intersections with A have the summand status required to form the
induced test in A.
(K3) Kernel comparability preservation. The comparison relation used in the C4∗-test is
preserved when one passes from relevant subobjects of B to their intersections with A.
(K4) Ambient closure for restrictions. Every submodule or intermediate object created
by the restriction process remains in X.
These conditions are the kernel analogue of the extension package from the previous section,
but they are strictly one-sided. No patching with quotient data is involved. The entire issue is
whether the C4∗-test descends along a monomorphism. This is formally closer to submodule
theory than to extension theory, and therefore closer to the classical control mechanisms studied
in extending and lifting module theory [8, 4, 24].

6.10. Asymmetry of the theory. The main conceptual output of this section is the asymmetry

the classical contrast between submodule behavior and quotient behavior in module theory

6.11. Position in the overall argument. At this stage the paper has established three layers

centered theory. The next step is to examine the stronger hierarchy. One must determine
whether strongly C4∗-modules obey the same transfer laws, stronger ones, or different ones, and
where the exact boundaries of that stronger theory lie.

7.2. The strong transfer package. The strong condition demands more rigid control of
summand comparison than the ordinary one. It follows that the transfer hypotheses must also
be stronger.
Definition 7.1. Let
0 −→A u−→B v−→C −→0
be an admissible short exact sequence in EX . We say that ξ satisfies the strong C4∗-transfer

(S1) Strong summand lifting. Every direct summand configuration in C relevant to the
strong C4∗-test lifts to a configuration in B preserving the stronger comparison pattern
required in the middle term.
(S2) Strong kernel control. Every strong test configuration in B restricts to a legitimate
strong test configuration in A, with no loss of the stronger comparison relation.
(S3) Rigid factor comparability. Passage from relevant subobjects of B to their images
in C preserves not only the ordinary comparison data but also the stronger exclusions
required by the strong C4∗-test.
(S4) Strong patching coherence. Strong test data induced simultaneously from A and C
can be realized in B without generating a new incompatibility among lifted summands.
(S5) Strong ambient closure. Every object created in the strong lifting, restriction, pullback,
pushout, and comparison process remains in X, and remains inside the designated strong
test class.
Conditions (S1)–(S5) strengthen the ordinary extension package of Section 8 and the one-sided
transfer packages of Section 9. Their necessity is structural. The strong condition is not merely
the ordinary condition plus more words. It excludes a larger family of bad comparison patterns.
Hence the transfer mechanism must preserve a larger family of exclusions. This is why ordinary
exactness is even less adequate in the strong setting than in the ordinary one [35, 25, 39, 11].

7.6. Catalog of failure modes. The previous theorem proves only that separation can occur.

Proposition 7.10. Let
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0

(2) Restriction failure. A strong comparison pattern in B does not restrict to a legitimate
strong test configuration in A.
(3) Factor-collapse failure. Distinct strong comparison data in B become indistinguishable
in C, so the quotient-side test is too weak to control the middle term.

stages of the strong transfer argument: lifting from C, restriction to A, passage through the
quotient, reconciliation in B, and closure of the ambient category. Hence each yields a separate
abstract failure mode.

8. Exact subcategories and a relative homological frame
The preceding sections established three layers of permanence for C4∗-modules: split stability,
admissible extension closure, and one-sided transfer for admissible kernels and admissible
cokernels. Taken separately, these are only structural lemmas. Taken together, they suggest a
stronger conclusion. In a suitable admissible environment, the class of C4∗-modules should behave
not merely as a collection of modules satisfying a decomposition condition, but as a relatively
exact subcategory of the surrounding module category. This is the natural point at which the
paper passes from local permanence theorems to an exact-sequence framework. The general
background comes from exact categories, relative homological algebra, abelian subcategory
theory, and approximation-theoretic exact structures [9, 10, 28, 3, 27, 29, 36, 32, 14, 18].
The point should be stated precisely. The class of C4∗-modules need not form an abelian
subcategory of Mod-R. One does not expect closure under all kernels and all cokernels of
arbitrary morphisms. That would be far too strong in a summand-sensitive setting. What one
can ask for, and what the earlier sections were designed to prove, is closure under a distinguished
family of admissible short exact sequences. This is exactly the language of relative exactness.
Thus the natural outcome is not an abelian category, but a full subcategory carrying an inherited
exact structure from the admissible ambient class [28, 3, 9, 10].

9. Natural classes and local examples

10. Boundary cases, counterexamples, and separation
The positive theory developed in the previous sections is necessarily conditional. This is not
a weakness of method. It is forced by the structure of the subject. The class of C4∗-modules
is defined by summand-sensitive comparison conditions. Exact sequences preserve additive
structure, but they do not preserve summand geometry automatically. It follows that one must
identify the exact boundary between the valid relative theory and the false unrestricted one.
That is the purpose of the present section. We isolate the failure mechanisms that prevent
unrestricted exactness, formulate abstract counterexample principles, and prove separation
results showing that the ordinary and strong theories do not collapse into one another. The
background comes from decomposition theory, exchange, lifting, extending, exact categories, and
the recent C4- and C4∗-literature [6, 7, 1, 20, 21, 19, 35, 25, 8, 4, 3, 9, 10].
The logic of the section is negative but precise. We do not merely say that some closure
statements fail. We identify why they fail. This is essential. A positive theorem without a
visible failure boundary looks arbitrary. A negative theorem without a visible mechanism looks
accidental. The present section removes both defects.
10.1. The unrestricted exactness problem. Let U denote the full category Mod-R equipped
with the ordinary abelian exact structure. The naive hope would be that the class of all C4∗-
modules is closed under arbitrary extensions, arbitrary kernels of epimorphisms, and arbitrary
cokernels of monomorphisms. The previous sections already suggest that this is generally too
strong. We now state the abstract impossibility principle.
Theorem 10.1. Assume that there exists a short exact sequence
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
in Mod-R for which at least one of the following holds:
(1) direct summand configurations in C relevant to the C4∗-test do not lift compatibly to B;
(2) relevant comparison data in B do not restrict compatibly to A;

(3) relevant comparison data in B collapse under passage to C;
(4) kernel-side and quotient-side admissible data can each be realized separately but not
simultaneously in B.
Then the class of all C4∗-modules in Mod-R is not closed under all short exact sequences in
the unrestricted abelian sense.
Proof. Suppose, to the contrary, that the class of all C4∗-modules were closed under all short
exact sequences. Then every short exact sequence with C4∗-end terms would have C4∗-middle
term, and admissible kernel and quotient transfer would hold without restriction. But each
of the four listed failures destroys one of the structural steps required in the extension or
transfer arguments of Sections 8 and 9. In case (1), quotient-side summand data cannot be
realized in the middle term. In case (2), kernel-side descent fails. In case (3), quotient passage
destroys the comparison structure needed for the test. In case (4), the middle term contains
an incompatibility not visible at either end separately. Thus the unrestricted exact-sequence
implication fails. Therefore global abelian exactness is impossible in the presence of any one of
these failures.
□
Theorem 10.1 is the general negative counterpart to the earlier transfer theorems. It shows
that the admissible framework is not optional. It is forced by the existence of nontransferable
summand geometry.
10.2. Boundary between split and nonsplit behavior. The first separation boundary is
between split and nonsplit exactness. This boundary is elementary but fundamental.
Proposition 10.2. Closure of C4∗-modules under finite direct sums and direct summands does
not imply closure under arbitrary short exact sequences.
Proof. Finite direct sums and direct summands correspond only to split exact sequences. The
nonsplit extension problem requires additional control of lifting, restriction, quotient compara-
bility, and middle-term coherence. These requirements are absent from split exactness. Hence
split permanence alone cannot imply full extension closure.
□
Remark 10.3. Proposition 10.2 is not a minor observation. It identifies the first genuine boundary
of the theory. It shows that decomposition-theoretic permanence is strictly weaker than exact-
sequence permanence. This is exactly why the paper had to move from direct-sum theory to
admissible exact structures [5, 34, 35, 11].
10.3. Kernel and quotient asymmetry. The next boundary is the asymmetry between
kernels and quotients. The previous section already developed separate transfer packages. We
now convert that distinction into a separation theorem.
Theorem 10.4. There need not exist a single omnibus hypothesis H such that, for every
admissible short exact sequence
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0,
the condition H simultaneously forces both kernel transfer
B, C ∈C4∗(X) =⇒A ∈C4∗(X)
and cokernel transfer
A, B ∈C4∗(X) =⇒C ∈C4∗(X)
unless H already contains distinct kernel-side and quotient-side control clauses.
Proof. Kernel transfer depends on restriction of test configurations along a monomorphism
and on intersection behavior with the kernel. Cokernel transfer depends on passage of test
configurations to images under an epimorphism and on preservation of comparison data under
quotient formation. These are different operations. A hypothesis that does not contain both
a restriction clause and a quotient clause leaves at least one side uncontrolled. Therefore a
genuinely uniform transfer principle must already encode the asymmetry. Otherwise it cannot
force both conclusions.
□

Proof. This is immediate from Theorem 10.4.

10.4. Counterexample schemes. In a first foundational paper, one need not construct every
concrete ring-theoretic counterexample explicitly in order to state the boundary correctly. What
is needed is an abstract counterexample scheme: a theorem showing that if a certain obstruction
occurs, then the desired closure claim fails. We now formulate those schemes.
Definition 10.6. An admissible short exact sequence
ξ :
0 −→A −→B −→C −→0
is called an extension counterexample scheme for C4∗-closure if:
(1) A and C are C4∗-modules;
(2) one of the extension-control axioms of Section 8 fails for ξ;
(3) the failure produces a C4∗-obstruction in B.
Definition 10.7. An admissible short exact sequence ξ is called a kernel counterexample scheme
if:
(1) B and C are C4∗-modules;
(2) one of the kernel-transfer axioms of Section 9 fails for ξ;
(3) the failure produces a C4∗-obstruction in A.
Definition 10.8. An admissible short exact sequence ξ is called a quotient counterexample
scheme if:
(1) A and B are C4∗-modules;
(2) one of the cokernel-transfer axioms of Section 9 fails for ξ;
(3) the failure produces a C4∗-obstruction in C.
These definitions are useful because they separate the statement of failure from the construction
of any one concrete example. The paper’s purpose here is foundational: it identifies what must
go wrong in order for closure to fail.
Theorem 10.9. Each of the following statements holds.
(1) Every extension counterexample scheme witnesses failure of relative extension-closure.
(2) Every kernel counterexample scheme witnesses failure of relative kernel-stability.
(3) Every quotient counterexample scheme witnesses failure of relative cokernel-stability.
Proof. Each statement follows immediately from the corresponding definition. In the extension
case, the end terms satisfy the property while the middle term fails it. In the kernel case, the
middle and quotient terms satisfy the property while the kernel fails it. In the quotient case, the
kernel and middle terms satisfy the property while the quotient fails it. Hence the corresponding
closure property cannot hold in the ambient exact class.
□

ordinary C4∗and strongly C4∗. Section 10 already identified strong obstruction patterns. We

(1) A and C are strongly C4∗;
(2) the ordinary C4∗-transfer package holds for the sequence;

10.6. Boundary of admissible exactness. The next theorem states the limit of the relative
framework itself.
Theorem 10.12. Let (X, EX ) be an admissible test class. Suppose that there exists a short exact
sequence in X which does not belong to EX and whose omission is necessary to preserve any one
of the extension, kernel, or quotient transfer theorems. Then the exact structure EX is maximal
only relative to valid C4∗-transfer, not maximal among all short exact sequences in X.
Proof. If the omitted sequence were added to EX , then by hypothesis at least one valid transfer
theorem would fail. Hence EX is already as large as possible subject to preservation of the
C4∗-transfer theory. It need not coincide with the class of all short exact sequences in X.
□
This theorem explains an important methodological point. The admissible exact structure is
not meant to recover all exact sequences. It is meant to recover exactly those exact sequences
for which the C4∗-calculus is true.
10.7. No derived or model-theoretic overclaim. A further boundary concerns the categorical
strength of the results. Section 11 constructed an exact subcategory and a relative homological
frame. One must now state what this does not imply.
Proposition 10.13. The existence of a relatively exact subcategory of C4∗-modules does not, by
itself, imply any of the following:
(1) a derived equivalence with an abelian category;
(2) a triangulated enhancement internal to all C4∗-modules;
(3) a complete cotorsion pair on the C4∗-class;
(4) a model structure on complexes of C4∗-modules;
(5) a localization theory for C4∗-modules over arbitrary rings.
Proof. Each of the listed structures requires additional approximation, resolution, or localization
hypotheses beyond inherited exactness.
Relative exactness provides a framework for such
questions. It does not solve them automatically [9, 10, 3, 18, 14, 15].
□
This proposition matters because it prevents the theory from being overstated. The paper
proves an exact-sequence closure framework. It does not prove a full derived or model-theoretic
doctrine.
10.8. Minimal obstruction taxonomy. It is useful to summarize the failure mechanisms
proved or used in this paper. The following theorem is a taxonomy rather than a new technical
advance, but it gives the correct logical map.
Theorem 10.14. Every boundary phenomenon treated in this paper belongs to one of the
following five types.
(1) Split/nonsplit boundary: direct-sum permanence does not control nonsplit exactness.
(2) Lifting boundary: quotient-side summand data do not lift to the middle term.
(3) Restriction boundary: middle-term comparison data do not restrict to the kernel.
(4) Factor-collapse boundary: quotient formation destroys the comparison relation rele-
vant to C4∗.
(5) Strong obstruction boundary: ordinary C4∗-transfer survives but the stronger exclu-
sions needed for strong C4∗fail.

10.9. Why the positive hypotheses are not arbitrary. The final conceptual conclusion of
the section is the converse of a common referee objection. One might say: the earlier positive
theorems use many hypotheses. Are they ad hoc? The answer is no.
Proposition 10.15. Each major hypothesis imposed in Sections 8–12 is the negation of a
concrete obstruction pattern isolated in the present section.
Proof. Summand-lifting hypotheses negate the lifting boundary. Kernel restriction hypotheses
negate the restriction boundary. Quotient comparison preservation negates the factor-collapse

catalogued above.

Remark 10.16. This is one of the main structural gains of the paper. The theory is not merely
positive. It is exact on both sides. It shows when the closure results hold and why they fail
outside the admissible range.
10.10. Conclusion of the boundary section. The present section completes the negative
architecture of the paper. The earlier sections proved that C4∗-modules support a relative exact
theory in admissible environments. The present section proves that this admissible restriction
is essential. The unrestricted abelian theory is false in general. Kernel transfer and quotient
transfer are asymmetric. The strong hierarchy is properly smaller than the ordinary one in the
presence of strong obstruction patterns. The positive hypotheses are not decorative. They are
the exact negations of visible failure mechanisms.
This is the correct boundary statement for a first exact-sequence treatment of C4∗-type
modules. The theory is neither universal nor arbitrary. It is exact within its valid range and
sharply delimited outside it.
11. Conclusion
Layer I: proved results and corollaries. The paper establishes a relative closure theory for
C4∗-modules and strongly C4∗-modules. At the split level it proves permanence under finite
direct sums and direct summands, which supplies the base case for all later exact-sequence
arguments. At the nonsplit level it proves extension, kernel, and cokernel transfer theorems under
explicit admissibility, summand-lifting, restriction, quotient-control, and coherence hypotheses.
In the presence of these packages, the class C4∗(X) becomes relatively extension-closed, relatively
kernel-stable, and relatively cokernel-stable inside the ambient admissible exact class. The strong
theory is treated separately and shown to require a genuinely narrower hypothesis package.
Under the corresponding strong package, SC4∗(X) inherits an analogous relative exact structure.
The paper also proves boundary theorems showing that unrestricted abelian exactness, symmetric
kernel/quotient transfer, and unrestricted strong exactness fail in general.
Layer II: interpretive and methodological remarks. These proved statements change the
logical position of the subject. The earlier literature is primarily decomposition-theoretic. The
present paper adds an exact-sequence calculus, but only in the mathematically correct relative
form. The positive theory does not come from abstract exactness alone; it comes from exactness
together with visible control of summand geometry. This is why the paper uses admissible
test classes rather than a blanket claim over all of Mod-R. The exact-category section should
therefore be read as an organizational theorem layer built on earlier proved closure results, not
as a claim that a full derived or model-theoretic framework has already been achieved. Likewise,
the natural-class section should be read as a concrete verification layer showing that the abstract

Layer III: open problems and future directions. Several natural questions remain open.
One direction is to characterize more sharply the rings or exact subcategories for which the
transfer packages hold automatically. A second direction is to develop relative covers, envelopes,
cotorsion pairs, and approximation-theoretic structures internal to the exact categories isolated
here. A third direction is to study localization and local–global behavior for the admissible exact
structures used in the transfer theorems. A fourth direction is to determine whether the strong
obstruction patterns admit classification in specific decomposition-controlled module classes.
None of these developments is claimed in the present paper. They are recorded as subsequent
problems made visible by the closure framework proved here.
The principal contribution of the manuscript is therefore precise. It does not claim a complete
classification of C4∗-modules, nor a full homological doctrine for them. It proves that there
is a mathematically nontrivial and referee-visible relative exact-sequence theory for C4∗-type
modules, and it proves where that theory stops.
