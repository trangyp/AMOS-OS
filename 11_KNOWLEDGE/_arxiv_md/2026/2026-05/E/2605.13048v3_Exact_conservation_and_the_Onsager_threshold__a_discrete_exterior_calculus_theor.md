Abstract
We develop a rigorous theory for a structure-preserving discretisation of the in-
compressible Euler and Navier–Stokes equations, based on discrete exterior calculus
on prismatic Delaunay–Voronoi meshes over closed Riemannian manifolds. The cen-
tral result is a selection principle: exact algebraic conservation at the discrete level is
not merely a fidelity property but rules out entire classes of weak solutions that other
discretisations reach unconditionally. We establish this in four regimes. Smooth solu-
tions: convergence at rate O(hmin(rrec, r⋆) | log h|βd), uniformly in viscosity ν ≥0, with
β3 = 0 and β2 = 1; first order on general meshes and second order on meshes with
centroid proximity and reconstruction symmetry. Leray–Hopf weak regime: subse-
quential L2 limits are weak solutions of the viscous system. Inviscid measure-valued
regime: limits are conservative measure-valued Euler solutions; their concentration
defect vanishes above the Onsager threshold α > 1/3 provided the discrete solutions
admit a uniform C0,α bound there. Dissipative regime: no subsequence converges
to an energy-dissipating Euler solution at any regularity, a structural exclusion that
follows from exact discrete energy conservation and distinguishes the scheme. The
gap 1/3 < α < 1, where energy conservation and defect-free convergence hold but
uniqueness remains open, isolates the central open problem of inviscid fluid dynamics.
Keywords.
Discrete exterior calculus; incompressible Navier–Stokes equations; incom-
pressible Euler equations; structure-preserving discretisation; Delaunay–Voronoi meshes;
Leray–Hopf weak solutions; measure-valued solutions; Onsager conjecture; energy conser-
vation; mimetic finite-volume methods.

Weak solutions and the regularity landscape.
The global existence of weak solu-
tions to the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations was established by Leray [39] and
Hopf [32], and uniqueness remains open in three dimensions. Caffarelli, Kohn, and Niren-
berg [11] proved that the singular set of a suitable weak solution—one satisfying a lo-
cal energy inequality as a structural hypothesis—has vanishing one-dimensional parabolic
Hausdorff measure; in two dimensions, smoothness and uniqueness are resolved for both
Euler [54] and Navier–Stokes [36]. For the inviscid Euler equations, the regularity land-
scape is shaped by the Onsager conjecture: weak solutions in the Hölder class C0,α conserve
energy if α > 1/3, while for α ≤1/3 energy-dissipating solutions exist. The positive di-
rection was established by Constantin, E, and Titi [14] (see also Duchon–Robert [21]); the
negative direction was completed through the convex-integration programme of De Lellis
and Székelyhidi [15, 16], building on earlier work of Scheffer [48], Shnirelman [49], and
culminating in Isett’s proof [33] and its refinement by Buckmaster, De Lellis, Székelyhidi,
and Vicol [10]. Below 1/3, weak solutions are maximally non-unique; above 1/3, energy is
conserved but uniqueness remains open. At Lipschitz regularity (α ≥1), Brenier, De Lellis,
and Székelyhidi [8] proved weak–strong uniqueness among conservative measure-valued so-
lutions. The gap 1/3 < α < 1—where energy is conserved but uniqueness is open—isolates
a central open problem of inviscid fluid dynamics.
Exact discrete conservation as a selection principle.
A numerical method that
aspires to simulate these equations faithfully must grapple simultaneously with two chal-
lenges: preserving the geometric and physical structure of the continuous equations, and
establishing a rigorous relation between discrete and continuous solutions. These are two
sides of the same coin. The thesis we develop is that exact algebraic conservation at the
discrete level is not merely a fidelity property but a selection principle on continuous limits:
it rules out entire classes of weak solutions that other discretisations reach unconditionally.
The analogy is with entropy inequalities in conservation laws, which select physical shocks
from among an infinite family of weak solutions allowed by the underlying PDE. Here the
role of the entropy inequality is played by the exact discrete energy identity, inherited from
antisymmetry of the discrete Lamb vector. The four regimes in which we analyse the DEC
scheme, smooth, Leray–Hopf weak, measure-valued inviscid, and dissipative, are not inde-
pendent results: each is a manifestation of the same selection principle acting at a different
level of regularity. In the dissipative regime, the principle produces a structural exclusion:
no subsequence can converge to an energy-dissipating Euler solution at any Hölder regular-
ity, regardless of mesh size, time step, or initial data. Classical Galerkin and finite-volume
schemes, whose discrete energy is conserved only approximately, admit no such exclusion.

Structure-preserving discretisation.
The principle that numerical schemes should
preserve geometric and physical structures of the continuous equations has a long history.
In the exterior calculus setting, the foundational framework is Finite Element Exterior
Calculus (FEEC) of Arnold, Falk, and Winther [1, 2], which constructs discrete de Rham
complexes on simplicial meshes and establishes optimal approximation properties for the
Hodge Laplacian. FEEC provides the topological infrastructure (exact sequences, com-
muting projections, discrete Poincaré inequalities) but does not address the nonlinear ad-
vection structure of the Navier–Stokes equations. The construction analysed here extends
the FEEC programme to incompressible fluid dynamics by equipping the discrete complex
with a nonlinear extrusion operator that preserves the energy identity.
DEC fluid mechanics was pioneered by Elcott et al. [22], who introduced discrete Lie
derivatives for fluids on surfaces. Mullen et al. [43] and Pavlov et al. [44] developed the
variational and conservation structure, establishing exact energy and Kelvin circulation
conservation on simplicial meshes; no error estimates were proved. We close that gap here.
Helicity, a topological invariant of ideal incompressible flows that constrains vortex tube
geometry [42], is not conserved by most schemes.
Several other structure-preserving approaches exist but differ in scope or mechanism.
Mimetic finite differences (Brezzi, Lipnikov, Shashkov [9]) discretise vector calculus opera-
tors to satisfy exact discrete analogues of Green’s identities on general polyhedral meshes,
but the convergence theory for nonlinear problems is limited. The EMAC (Energy, Mo-
mentum, and Angular-momentum Conserving) formulation of Charnyi et al. [12] conserves
energy, momentum, and angular momentum by blending the advective and rotational
forms of the nonlinearity in a standard FEM setting. Zhang et al. [55] achieve helicity
conservation via a mimetic dual-field discretisation that doubles the number of velocity
unknowns.

Contributions and proof strategy.
Antisymmetry and de Rham commutativity are
the structural inputs for the conservation laws (Thms. 3.4, 3.6 and 3.8). Both inviscid and
viscous discrete systems are globally well-posed for every fixed mesh (Thms. 4.6 and 4.9).
Smooth convergence (Thms. 5.7 and 5.13) follows a consistency–stability paradigm.
Consistency exploits that the Leray projection annihilates gradient contributions, leaving
only the extrusion error at rate rrec combined with the Hodge-star accuracy r⋆. Stability
rests on three ingredients:
(i) Lamb antisymmetry removes the cubic self-interaction;
(ii) a sharp pointwise-reconstruction bound on the projection residual (Lem. 5.10 (ii)),
obtained by exploiting DV orthogonality to identify the discrete Hodge Laplacian
with a cell-centred finite-volume Laplacian;
(iii) a polarised antisymmetry identity, combined with the cochain-complex relation ˜D1 ˜D0 =
0, eliminates the potentially rate-reducing bilinear Leray remainder.
In the absence of smoothness, uniform a priori estimates yield Aubin–Lions compact-
ness. The discrete energy inequality identifies the limit as a Leray–Hopf solution. For the
inviscid system, exact discrete energy conservation passes to the limit, yielding conservative
measure-valued solutions and excluding dissipative limits.
Pressure is not an evolution variable in the discrete state but is recovered post-hoc from
the Bernoulli function via a discrete Poisson problem; its convergence behaviour mirrors the
regime hierarchy. In the smooth regime (ν > 0), under a uniform L4–L4 Hölder bound on
the discrete Lamb form (Ass. 5.17, the natural discrete analog of continuous Hölder for ω×
u), the discrete pressure converges weakly to the continuous pressure in Lqd(0, T; L2(Ω)/R)
with qd := 8/(d + 4) (q2 = 4/3, q3 = 8/7), via an inf-sup-stable mixed reformulation of
the scheme on the cochain pair (C1(K∗), C0(K∗)/R) (Lem. 5.15, Cor. 5.19); a quantitative
rate would require a discrete Calderón–Zygmund estimate beyond the scope of this paper.
In the inviscid measure-valued regime, pressure-like contributions are absorbed into the
matrix-valued concentration defect σij (Thm. 5.28, Rem. 5.29).
The framework is intrinsically coordinate-free and operates on prismatic Delaunay–
Voronoi meshes, the meshes used by operational atmosphere–ocean models such as ICON [34]
and MPAS [47]; the results are therefore of direct practical relevance, not confined to ide-
alised triangulations. The operator framework originates in the unstructured-grid ocean
model of [34]; we extend it here to the non-hydrostatic incompressible Navier–Stokes sys-
tem and establish the analytical foundation that the computational paper anticipated.

Structure.
Section 2 develops the DEC framework: mesh geometry, exterior derivatives,

energy conservation restricts continuous limits to physically admissible classes at every
level of regularity.

2. Discrete Geometric Framework
The central principle of Discrete Exterior Calculus is a strict separation between topology
and metric. The exterior derivative is defined purely combinatorially — as the coboundary
7


## Page 8


operator of the cell complex — and is therefore exact: it commutes with the de Rham
interpolation without any approximation error. All approximation error in the scheme is
concentrated in the Hodge star, which must compare integrals of a k-form over a primal
cell with integrals of its dual over the complementary dual cell. The Delaunay–Voronoi
mesh is the natural setting for this construction: the orthogonality of every primal edge
to its dual face allows the off-diagonal Hodge star to be replaced by a diagonal one that
is a first-order (O(h)) approximation on general meshes and a second-order (O(h2)) ap-
proximation on centroidal Voronoi tessellations. This structure coincides with the spatial
discretisation used by operational atmosphere–ocean models (ICON [34], MPAS [47]), so
the mathematical theory developed here applies directly to those codes.
Why Delaunay–Voronoi prismatic meshes?
Three independent considerations con-
verge on this mesh class. (i) Hodge star diagonalisation. On a Delaunay–Voronoi mesh
every primal edge is orthogonal to its dual face, so the metric Hodge star reduces to a
diagonal, positive-definite operator with entries (Mk)ii = |σi|/|σ∗
i |. This simplification is
not available on general polyhedral meshes and is the structural reason why the approxi-
mation error in the scheme is concentrated in a single, simple operator rather than being
distributed across a dense mass matrix as in FEM. (ii) Centroid proximity and the jump
to second order. On centroidal Voronoi tessellations (CVTs) and on icosahedral or cubed-
sphere meshes widely used in geoscientific modelling, the circumcentre of each Delaunay cell
coincides—to higher order in h—with the centroid of its dual Voronoi cell. This centroid
proximity condition (Prop. 2.2) is the single geometric property that upgrades the Hodge
star accuracy from O(h) to O(h2) and with it the scheme’s convergence rate. (iii) Prismatic
structure matches physical anisotropy. Atmospheric and oceanic flows have anisotropic as-
pect ratios (∆z/∆x ∼10−3) and require vertical layer resolution independent of horizontal
mesh refinement. Prismatic extrusion of a two-dimensional Delaunay–Voronoi layer, rather
than a fully three-dimensional tetrahedralisation, preserves this anisotropy at the level of
the discrete framework and keeps the horizontal d = 2 DEC structure intact.
What is new in this paper relative to standard DEC.
We use standard DEC
objects where they suffice and introduce new constructions only where the standard toolkit
falls short.
Standard objects reused as-is: the primal/dual coboundary operators Dk,
˜Dk; the diagonal Hodge stars Mk on Delaunay–Voronoi meshes ([17, 30]); the de Rham

of antisymmetry and de Rham commutativity; the specific reconstruction controls the
convergence rate but not the conservation identities.

5. Continuous Limit in Various Regularity Regimes
Having established well-posedness and conservation for every fixed mesh, we now ask: do
discrete solutions converge to continuous solutions as h →0? The answer depends on the
regularity of the continuous solution: smooth references give explicit rates (Section 5.1),
while weaker regularity yields subsequential convergence in progressively weaker senses
(Section 5.3–5.4).
Assumption 5.1 (Regularity of reference solution). For ν ≥0 we assume the existence

5.4 Low Regularity Regime: Onsager Threshold
The central difficulty for the inviscid Euler equations is non-uniqueness. Wild weak solu-
tions constructed by convex integration [48, 49, 15, 16, 10] can violate energy conservation
and are manifestly non-unique below the Hölder threshold α = 1/3. Whether uniqueness
holds above 1/3 depends critically on the regularity class. At Lipschitz regularity (α ≥1),
Brenier–De Lellis–Székelyhidi [8] prove measure-valued weak–strong uniqueness (MVWSU)
for conservative measure-valued solutions (CMV); this is the strongest unconditional result
available. In the energy-conserving range 1/3 < α < 1, the Constantin–E–Titi theorem
[14] guarantees that the kinetic energy is conserved, but the MVWSU argument breaks
down: weak–strong uniqueness among C0,α Euler solutions in this range is a major open
problem. Below 1/3, both dissipative and energy-conservative weak solutions exist [33, 10],
and non-uniqueness is maximal.
A convergence theory for discrete schemes must navigate this landscape without know-
ing in advance which regularity regime the true solution lies in. The DEC scheme achieves
the following: discrete Euler solutions converge subsequentially to CMV solutions uncon-
ditionally (Thm. 5.28); the defect measure σ vanishes precisely at the Onsager threshold
α > 1/3 (Thm. 5.35), reducing the MVWSU hypothesis to ordinary weak–strong unique-
ness and yielding full-sequence convergence for Lipschitz solutions (Thm. 5.32); and no
subsequence can converge to a dissipative Euler solution at any regularity (Prop. 5.33).
The last exclusion follows because the DEC scheme inherits exact energy conservation
from the discrete level, so the limit cannot dissipate energy anomalously.
