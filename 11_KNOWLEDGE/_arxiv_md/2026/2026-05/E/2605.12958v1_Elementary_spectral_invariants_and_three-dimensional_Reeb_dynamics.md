Elementary spectral invariants and three-dimensional
Reeb dynamics
Michael Hutchings∗
Abstract
We survey various recent results on the existence and properties of periodic
orbits of Reeb vector fields in three dimensions. We give an introduction to
the “elementary spectral invariants” of contact three-manifolds, and we explain
how they can be used to prove some of these results. (The remaining results
can be proved using spectral invariants from embedded contact homology, of
which the elementary spectral invariants are a simplification.) We then review
the “alternative ECH capacities” of symplectic four-manifolds, and explain
how these can be modified to define the elementary spectral invariants. This
document is a set of lecture notes for a minicourse taught by the author at the
CIME school on Symplectic Dynamics and Topology in Cetraro in June 2025.

1
Reeb dynamics in three dimensions
We begin by recalling some recent results in three-dimensional Reeb dynamics that
have been proved using ECH spectral invariants and/or the elementary spectral
invariants of contact three-manifolds.
1.1
Reeb vector fields
Let Y be a (2n−1)-dimensional smooth manifold. A contact form on Y is a 1-form
λ on Y such that λ ∧(dλ)n−1 ̸= 0 everywhere. If λ is a contact form, then there is
a unique vector field R on Y , called the Reeb vector field, such that dλ(R, ·) = 0
and λ(R) = 1. A contact form λ also determines a rank 2n−2 subbundle ξ = Ker(λ)
of TY , which is a totally nonintegrable distribution. The restriction of dλ to each
fiber of ξ is a linear symplectic form. A rank 2n −2 subbundle of TY that can be
obtained as the kernel of a contact form is called1 a contact structure on Y .

structure given by the kernel of a globally defined contact form is sometimes called a co-oriented
contact structure. Sometimes a “contact structure” is defined to be a rank 2n−2 subbundle which
is only locally the kernel of a contact form.

The above examples provide some motivation for studying the dynamics of
Reeb vector fields, but there are many more examples. A theorem of Thurston-
Winkelnkemper [72] asserts that every closed orientable three-manifold admits a
contact structure, and today there are many more ways to construct contact struc-
tures in three dimensions. A more recent result of Borman-Eliashberg-Murphy [5]
implies that any odd-dimensional manifold admits a contact structure, as long as it
admits an “almost contact structure”, which is determined by algebraic topology.

manifold Y , and if f : Y →R is a smooth function, then efλ is also a contact
form on Y with the same associated contact structure. If f is a constant function,
then the Reeb vector field associated to efλ is a scaling of the Reeb vector field
associated to λ. If f is not constant, then the Reeb vector fields of λ and efλ are
not parallel and can have very different dynamics.
1.2
The Weinstein conjecture in three dimensions
Let λ be a contact form on Y . A basic aspect of the dynamics of the associated
Reeb vector field R is given by its periodic orbits, which we call “Reeb orbits” for
short. To be precise, a Reeb orbit is a map γ : R/TZ →Y , for some T > 0, such
that γ′(t) = R(γ(t)) for all t. We declare two such maps to be equivalent if they
differ by precomposition with a translation of the domain R/TZ. The Reeb orbit γ
is simple if the map γ is injective. A simple Reeb orbit is equivalent to a compact
connected one-dimensional submanifold of Y which is everywhere tangent to the
Reeb vector field4. For example, when Y is the unit cotangent bundle of a smooth
manifold N with a Riemannian metric, (simple) Reeb orbits correspond to (prime)
oriented closed geodesics5 in N.

With these definitions out of the way, the first question one might ask is: Do
Reeb orbits exist? The Weinstein conjecture, formulated by Weinstein [73] in
the 1970s, asserts that for every contact form on a closed manifold, there exists at
least one Reeb orbit.
The Weinstein conjecture is known for the two basic examples in §1.1, and these
were motivations for the conjecture. For the first example, a theorem of Rabinowitz
[66] asserts that every star-shaped hypersurface in R2n has at least one Reeb orbit.
We remark that a folk conjecture asserts that every star-shaped hypersurface in
R2n has at least n distinct simple Reeb orbits. This was recently proved by Cineli-
Ginzburg-Gurel [9] under the assumption that the star-shaped hypersurface Y is
dynamically convex6, which as in [38] includes the case when Y is strictly convex.
For the second example, the classical Lyusternik-Fet theorem asserts that every
closed Riemannian manifold has at least one closed geodesic. And there are many
results asserting the existence of more than one prime closed geodesic. For exam-
ple, any Riemannian metric on a closed surface has infinitely many prime closed
geodesics; in positive genus one can prove this by minimizing length in infinitely
many prime free homotopy classes, and Bangert [3] and Franks [33] proved this in
genus zero.
The three-dimensional case of the Weinstein conjecture was proved by Taubes
[70] in 2006. In fact, Taubes proved the following stronger statement:
Theorem 1.1 (Taubes [70]). Let Y be a closed three-manifold, let λ be a contact
form on Y with associated contact structure ξ, and let Γ ∈H1(Y ) be a class such
that c1(ξ) + 2 PD(Γ) ∈H2(Y ; Z) is torsion7. Then there exists a nonempty finite
set of Reeb orbits whose total homology class is Γ.
Here c1(ξ) ∈H2(Y ; Z) denotes the first Chern class of the contact structure,
regarded as a complex line bundle via the orientation given by dλ. Also PD(Γ) ∈
H2(Y ; Z) denotes the Poincar´e dual of Γ, defined using the orientation on Y given
by λ ∧dλ.

Prior to Taubes’s result, various special cases of the Weinstein conjecture were
proved, sometimes with stronger statements. For example, Hofer [37] proved that
if Y is a closed connected three-manifold, if λ is a contact form on Y , and if (i)
Y ≃S3 or (ii) π2(Y ) ̸= 0 or (iii) the contact structure ξ = Ker(λ) is overtwisted8,
then λ has a contractible Reeb orbit. Note that not all contact forms on closed three-
manifolds have contractible Reeb orbits; a counterexample is the canonical contact
form on the unit cotangent bundle of T2 = R2/Z2 with the standard metric. Abbas-
Cieliebak-Hofer [1] showed that if λ is a contact form on a closed three-manifold
such that the associated contact structure ξ = Ker(λ) is planar9, then there exists a
nonempty finite set of Reeb orbits with total homology class zero. It remains an open
question whether this is true without the hypothesis that ξ is planar. Colin-Honda
[12] showed that for a contact form λ on a closed three-manifold, if the associated
contact structure is supported by an open book decomposition whose monodromy
is pseudo-Anosov with sufficiently large fractional Dehn twist coefficient, then there
are infinitely many simple Reeb orbits, in distinct free homotopy classes.
The results mentioned in the previous paragraph were all proved using pseudo-
holomorphic curve techniques. On the other hand, Taubes’s proof of Theorem 1.1
uses Seiberg-Witten theory, and this is currently the only known proof of the We-
instein conjecture in all cases in three dimensions. See [42] for a longer survey. In
higher dimensions the Weinstein conjecture is largely an open question.

1.4
Contact three-manifolds with exactly two simple Reeb orbits
In fact, contact three-manifolds with exactly two simple Reeb orbits are quite rare,
and mostly agree with the above examples. To start, we have:
Theorem 1.4 ([17] plus [42]). Let λ be a contact form on a closed three-manifold
Y . Suppose that λ has exactly two simple Reeb orbits. Then Y is diffeomorphic to
S3 or a lens space, and the two simple Reeb orbits are the core circles of the solid
tori in a genus one Heegaard splitting of Y .
Remark 1.5. The conclusion of Theorem 1.4 was proved in [42] under the ad-
ditional hypothesis that the contact form λ is nondegenerate (see Definition 1.6
below).
Then [17] showed that this hypothesis is redundant: If a contact form
on a closed three-manifold has exactly two simple Reeb orbits, then it must be
nondegenerate!

1.5
Two or infinity
It is not known whether there exists a contact form on a closed connected three-
manifold with more than two but only finitely many simple Reeb orbits. In most
cases, we know that this is not possible:
Theorem 1.8 ([11] plus [18]). Let λ be a contact form on a closed connected three-
manifold Y . Suppose that at least one of the following holds:
(a) λ is nondegenerate.
(b) c1(ξ) ∈H2(Y ; Z) is torsion.
Then λ has either two or infinitely many simple Reeb orbits.
Remark 1.9. Hofer-Wysocki-Zehnder [39] showed that a star-shaped hypersurface
in R4 has either two or infinitely many simple Reeb orbits, assuming that the con-
tact form is nondegenerate and the stable and unstable manifolds of all hyperbolic
Reeb orbits intersect transversely.

genus zero Birkhoff section for the Reeb flow, thus reducing the problem to the

1.6
Closing lemmas
Irie [53] proved that for a generic contact form on a closed three-manifold, not only
are there infinitely many simple Reeb orbits, but the Reeb orbits are dense:
Theorem 1.10 (Irie [53]). Let Y be a closed three-manifold. For a C∞-generic
contact form on Y , the union of the (images of the) Reeb orbits is dense in Y .
The key to the proof is a “closing lemma”, asserting that given a contact form λ
on Y and given a nonempty set U ⊂Y , one can make a C∞-small perturbation of λ
to create a Reeb orbit passing through U. More precisely, Irie proved the following
“strong closing lemma”:
Theorem 1.11 (Irie [53], see proof in §2.2). Let Y be a closed three-manifold, let λ
be a contact form on Y , and let f : Y →R≥0 be a smooth function, not identically
zero. Then there exists τ ∈[0, 1] such that the contact form eτfλ has a Reeb orbit
intersecting supp(f).
This result should be compared with Pugh’s C1-closing lemma [65], which asserts
that given a nonwandering point x of a C1-diffeomorphism f of a closed manifold,
one can find a C1-small perturbation g of f so that x is a periodic point of g. The
proof requires great care in constructing the perturbation, and extending the result
to higher regularity seems very difficult. By contrast, Irie’s strong closing lemma
asserts that one can create a Reeb orbit through U by a C∞small perturbation of
the contact form via any nonnegative perturbation supported in U. As we will see
in §2.2, the proof does not even require that f is nonnegative, only that

1.7
Quantitative closing lemmas
Irie’s strong closing lemma asserts that given a contact form λ on a closed three-
manifold Y , one can create a Reeb orbit intersecting a nonempty open set U ⊂Y
by a C∞-small perturbation of λ supported in U. If λ does not already have a Reeb
orbit intersecting U, and if the perturbation is small, then one might expect the
Reeb orbit created to have a large period (because a short trajectory would not be
perturbed enough to close up). One can then ask for a quantitative refinement of
Irie’s strong closing lemma: Given L > 0, how much do we need to perturb the
contact form in order to create a Reeb orbit through U of period at most L? As we
will see, the answer is, roughly, O(L−1).

Remark 1.18. It follows from the definition that CloseL(Y, λ) is a nonincreasing
function of L.
Remark 1.19. If L is less than the minimum action of a Reeb orbit, then CloseL(Y, λ) =
+∞. The reason is that given δ > 0, one can consider the positive deformation given
by λτ = eaτλ where a > 0 is a constant. One can show that if a is sufficiently large,
then this positive deformation will have width greater than δ. Now the Reeb orbits
of eaτλ are the same as the Reeb orbits of λ, except that they are reparametrized
so that the action gets multiplied by eaτ. Thus this deformation does not create
any Reeb orbits of action ≤L.
Remark 1.20. It is also possible that CloseL(Y, λ) = 0.
This is equivalent to
the statement that every point in Y is on (the image of) a Reeb orbit of action
≤L. (This happens for example on ∂E(a, b) when a/b is rational and L is the least
common multiple of a and b. See [22, 32] for some characterizations of such contact
forms.) The direction (⇐) is immediate from the definition. To prove the reverse
direction, if CloseL(Y, λ) = 0, then for any nonempty open set U ⊂Y , one can
produce a Reeb orbit intersecting U of action ≤L by an arbitrarily small positive
deformation of λ supported in U. It then follows from the compactness of Y that
the original contact form λ has a Reeb orbit intersecting the closure U with action
≤L. Since U was arbitrary, it follows that Reeb orbits of action ≤L are dense in
Y . By another compactness argument, every point in Y is on a Reeb orbit of action
≤L.
Remark 1.21. If λ has no Reeb orbit of action ≤L intersecting U, and if {λτ} is
a positive deformation of λ supported in U with width greater than CloseL(Y, λ),
then for some τ ∈[0, 1], the contact form λτ has a Reeb orbit γ intersecting U with
action ≤L. One might expect that γ is a concatenation of Reeb trajectories of λτ
in U from ∂U to itself, together with Reeb trajectories of λ in Y \ U from ∂U to
itself. This may appear to be a contradiction if there is no Reeb trajectory of λ in
Y \ U from ∂U to itself taking time ≤L. The resolution of the paradox is that in
this case, the Reeb orbit γ for λτ is contained entirely within U. The idea is that
the large deformation “stirs up” the Reeb flow in U enough to create such an orbit.
Remark 1.22. The definition of CloseL also makes sense for higher dimensional
contact manifolds. This and more general notions are explored in [7].

In [43], embedded contact homology was used to define the ECH spectral invariants
of contact three-manifolds, which can be used to prove the dynamical theorems
stated in §1. The “elementary spectral invariants” of contact three-manifolds, de-
fined in [48], are a simplified version of the ECH spectral invariants, which behave
similarly to the original ECH spectral invariants, but which require much less tech-

in the spectral invariants under suitable deformations of the contact form, and to
then draw dynamical conclusions from this.
