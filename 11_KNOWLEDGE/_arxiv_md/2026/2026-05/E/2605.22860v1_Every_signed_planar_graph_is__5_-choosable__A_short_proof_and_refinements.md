Abstract
A signed graph is a pair (G, σ) in which G is a finite simple graph and σ : E(G) →
{+1, −1} is a signature. Following Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera and Jin–Kang–Steffen, a
proper coloring of (G, σ) is a map c : V (G) →Z with c(u) ̸= σ(uv) c(v) for every edge uv,
and (G, σ) is signed k-choosable if such a coloring exists from any list assignment L with
|L(v)| ≥k. In a celebrated two-page note, Thomassen proved that every planar graph is
5-choosable, and Jin, Kang, and Steffen subsequently extended this to signed planar graphs.
Our principal contribution is a short, self-contained, and signature-blind proof of the latter:
the inductive bookkeeping inserts one factor of σ(·) uniformly into every constraint, so
that with σ ≡+1 the argument reduces verbatim to Thomassen’s original.
From the
strengthened extension statement (Theorem 1.4) we deduce the main result (Theorem 1.3:
χs
ℓ(G, σ) ≤5 for every planar signed graph), the Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera signed Five-
Color Theorem in the symmetric palette N2 = {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2}, the Switching Invariance
Lemma, 3-choosability of outerplanar signed graphs, 1-defective signed 4-choosability of
planar signed graphs, a sandwich inequality relating χs
ℓto the unsigned and “positive” choice
numbers, and a polynomial-time list-coloring algorithm.

graph and Mirzakhani’s smaller variant show the bound 5 is best possible. We close with

Introduction
The chromatic number χ(G) of a graph G is the smallest k for which the vertices of G admit
a proper k-coloring. The Four Color Theorem of Appel and Haken [3, 33] states χ(G) ≤4 for
every planar graph; its weaker but elementary predecessor, the Five Color Theorem, was already
known to Heawood [17]. Standard references for this classical material are [5, 11, 19].
Building on this theory, one can ask what happens when colors are not freely chosen but must
come from prescribed lists. A list assignment for G is a map L associating to each v ∈V (G)
a finite set L(v) of admissible colors. The graph G is L-colorable if it admits a proper coloring

with |L(v)| ≥k. The smallest such k, denoted χℓ(G), is the list chromatic number (or choice
number). List coloring superficially resembles ordinary coloring, but χℓ(G) can be strictly larger
than χ(G), so analogues of classical theorems do not come for free.
The notion of choosability was introduced independently by Vizing [44] and by Erdős, Rubin
and Taylor [15]. Both papers asked whether every planar graph is 5-choosable. Voigt [45] showed
that planar graphs need not even be 4-choosable: she exhibited a planar graph on 238 vertices
and an associated list assignment of size 4 admitting no proper coloring. A smaller witness on
63 vertices was later found by Mirzakhani [29]. The matching upper bound was established
by Thomassen [41], who proved that every planar graph is 5-choosable. Thomassen’s proof,
occupying less than a page, is now standard fare in graph theory courses; it proceeds by induction
on a strengthened statement involving two adjacent precolored boundary vertices.
We turn now from ordinary graphs to signed graphs. A signed graph (G, σ) is a pair where G
is a graph and σ : E(G) →{+1, −1} is a signature; the notion was introduced by Harary [16] in
the context of social psychology and developed systematically by Zaslavsky [48, 50, 51]. Edges e
with σ(e) = +1 are positive and those with σ(e) = −1 are negative; the sign of a closed walk is
the product of the signs of its edges, and (G, σ) is balanced if every cycle is positive. Switching at
U ⊆V (G) negates the sign of every edge with exactly one endpoint in U, defining an equivalence
relation on signatures; by Zaslavsky’s theorem [48], σ is balanced iff it is switching-equivalent to
the all-positive signature. Beyond their original psychological motivation, signed graphs arise in
the geometry of root systems and arrangements [48, 50], in flow theory [6], and in the chromatic
theory developed by Zaslavsky, Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera, and others; for recent surveys
see [31, 36].
Several non-equivalent proposals for the chromatic theory of signed graphs appear in the
literature, and the choice of framework matters for a list-coloring statement. Zaslavsky’s origi-
nal definition [48, 49] uses “signed colors” from the set {0, ±1, . . . , ±k} and treats the zero color
asymmetrically. Máčajová, Raspaud, and Škoviera [28] reformulated the basic notion in a way
that more naturally generalizes the chromatic number and aligns with the homomorphism view-
point of Naserasr, Rollová, and Sopena [30]. We adopt the definition that is intrinsic to the
integer arithmetic of the signature, formalized as follows.

Preliminaries on signed graphs
We follow the terminology of Zaslavsky [48] for signed graphs and of Diestel [11] for ordinary
graph theory; all graphs are finite and simple unless otherwise noted. Throughout the paper G
denotes a finite simple graph with vertex set V (G) and edge set E(G), and a signed graph is a
pair (G, σ) with σ : E(G) →{+1, −1}. The underlying graph of (G, σ) is G, and we say (G, σ)
is planar (resp. outerplanar, k-connected) whenever G is.

To anchor these abstract notions, we record three concrete examples that will recur in the

Example 2.6 (All-positive and all-negative). The all-positive signed graph (G, +1) recovers
ordinary graph theory. The all-negative signed graph (G, −1) is balanced if and only if G is
bipartite, by Theorem 2.4: in a bipartition (A, B), switching at A turns every edge positive.
Example 2.7 (Signed K4). Let G = K4 with σ assigning −1 to a perfect matching and +1
to the remaining four edges. Each triangle of K4 contains exactly one negative edge, so every
triangle is negative, and (G, σ) is not balanced. Switching at any vertex changes the sign of
three edges, so we cannot reduce to σ ≡+1, consistent with Theorem 2.4.
Example 2.8 (Near-triangulations). A near-triangulation is a 2-connected plane graph in which
every bounded face is a triangle. These play a central role in [41] and in the proof of Theorem 1.4:
every planar graph can be augmented to a near-triangulation by adding edges, and adding edges
only increases coloring constraints.

Having established switching invariance, we record briefly how our coloring framework relates
to alternatives in the literature. The original proposal of Zaslavsky [48, 49] takes a coloring to
be a map c : V (G) →{0, ±1, . . . , ±k} with c(u) ̸= σ(uv)c(v), which is the same constraint as in
Theorem 1.1 but restricted to a finite symmetric palette; Máčajová, Raspaud, and Škoviera [28]
sharpened this by defining a proper (2k + 1)-coloring as a map c : V (G) →Nk = {−k, . . . , k}
satisfying Theorem 1.1, with χ(G, σ) the smallest 2k + 1 for which such a coloring exists, a
convention that is well-suited to the symmetric palette and specializes to χ(G) when σ ≡+1.
A different perspective comes from the homomorphism viewpoint introduced by Naserasr,
Rollová, and Sopena [30], in which the signed chromatic number is the smallest order of a
target signed graph (H, π) admitting a sign-preserving homomorphism (G, σ) →(H, π); this
perspective is particularly fruitful for minor-closed families of signed graphs. The list-coloring
framework of Theorem 1.2, employed in the present paper, is due to Jin, Kang, and Steffen [20]
and Kang and Steffen [22], and works directly with arbitrary finite subsets of Z. Beyond list
coloring lies the signed DP-coloring of Jin, Kang, and Steffen [21], which generalizes the DP-
coloring of Dvořák and Postle [13] to the signed setting; the corresponding constant for planar
signed graphs is open and is discussed in Theorem 8.5.
For each of these conventions, the
underlying coloring is invariant under switching in the appropriate sense, and reduces to the
classical notion when σ ≡+1.

Proof of the main theorem
We prove Theorem 1.4 by induction on |V (G)|. The argument mirrors that of Thomassen [41];
the only modification is the bookkeeping of edge signs, which is uniform throughout. We first
record the standard reduction to near-triangulations, which allows us to add edges freely without
changing the question.

Lemma 4.1 (Reduction to near-triangulations). Let G be a planar graph with |V (G)| ≥3.
There exists a planar near-triangulation eG with V (G) = V ( eG) and E(G) ⊆E( eG) such that the
outer face of eG is a triangle.
Proof. Embed G in the plane. While some bounded face is bounded by a walk of length > 3

and add the chord uv through that face; the resulting graph is still planar. Iterating gives a
near-triangulation. If the outer face is bounded by ℓ≥4 edges, add chords across it until the

Either way, hypothesis (ii) holds. Finally, every vertex of G′ that is interior to C′ was already
interior to C (no new interior vertices were created), so it retains a list of size at least 5, and
hypothesis (iii) holds. By induction, then, there is a proper L′-coloring c′ of (G, σ)|G′ extending
the precoloring at v1, v2.
It remains to extend c′ to a coloring c of G by choosing c(vp) ∈{x, y}. Such a choice is
admissible iff
c(vp) ̸= σ(v1vp) c1,
c(vp) ̸= σ(vp−1vp) c′(vp−1),
c(vp) ̸= σ(vpui) c′(ui) (1 ≤i ≤m).
We check each constraint in turn. First, by the very construction of Ω= L(vp) \ {σ(v1vp) c1},
both x and y are different from σ(v1vp) c1, so the constraint through v1 is automatic. Next,
the constraints from the interior neighbors ui are eliminated by the modified list assignment:
by construction c′(ui) ∈L′(ui), so c′(ui) ̸= σ(vpui) x and c′(ui) ̸= σ(vpui) y, and multiplying
through by σ(vpui) ∈{±1} (using σ(vpui)2 = 1) gives σ(vpui) c′(ui) ̸= x and σ(vpui) c′(ui) ̸= y,
so neither x nor y is forbidden by ui. The only constraint on c(vp) that may still rule out an
element of {x, y} is therefore the one through vp−1, namely c(vp) ̸= σ(vp−1vp) c′(vp−1), which
forbids a single value. Since x ̸= y, at least one of {x, y} avoids it, and setting c(vp) to be such
a value yields a proper L-coloring of (G, σ). This completes the induction.
With Theorem 1.4 in hand, the deduction of Theorem 1.3 is immediate.
Proof of Theorem 1.3. Let (G, σ) be a planar signed graph and L a list assignment with |L(v)| ≥
5 for every v. We may assume |V (G)| ≥3. By Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.2 we may augment
G to a planar near-triangulation eG extending σ arbitrarily to a signature eσ on the new edges,
choosing the outer face to be a triangle v1v2v3. Choose any c1 ∈L(v1) and any c2 ∈L(v2) \
{eσ(v1v2) c1}, which is possible since |L(v2)| ≥5 ≥2, so that c1 ̸= eσ(v1v2) c2. The list sizes
|L(v3)| ≥5 ≥3 at the third boundary vertex and |L(v)| ≥5 in the interior then verify the
hypotheses of Theorem 1.4 for ( eG, eσ), and the resulting proper L-coloring of eG restricts to one
of (G, σ).

Remark 4.3 (Why this proof is short). The key combinatorial fact, brought into focus by the
proof above, is that the substitution c′(ui) 7→σ(vpui) c′(ui) acts as an involution on Z, so that
“forbidding x at vp through ui” is equivalent to “forbidding σ(vpui) x at ui through its other-side
neighbor of vp”. The size-2 deletion in L′(ui) uses precisely this symmetry. The proof is therefore
signature-blind: every step is identical to Thomassen’s, with each color α replaced uniformly by
σ(·) α on the appropriate edge.

The strength of Theorem 1.4 as an extension lemma as opposed to a bare upper-bound statement
yields several refinements. The most direct allows the precolored pair to lie on any face of the
signed graph, not merely a chosen outer triangle.
Corollary 5.1 (Two-vertex precoloring extension). Let (G, σ) be a planar signed graph, and let

extends to an L-coloring of (G, σ).
Proof. Embed G in the plane so that uv lies on the outer face, then triangulate the interior and
exterior as in the proof of Theorem 1.3. Apply Theorem 1.4.

and Škoviera, recovering the natural signed analogue of Heawood’s classical Five Color Theorem.
Corollary 5.2 (Signed Five-Color Theorem). Every planar signed graph (G, σ) admits a proper
coloring in the symmetric palette N2 = {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2}. In the Máčajová–Raspaud–Škoviera
convention, χ(G, σ) ≤5.
Proof. Take L(v) := N2 for every v ∈V (G). Then |L(v)| = 5 and L uses colors in the symmetric

We close this section with an algorithmic consequence. The proof of Theorem 1.4 is con-

Proposition 5.10 (Polynomial-time list 5-coloring). There is an algorithm which, given a planar
signed graph (G, σ) with |V (G)| = n and a list assignment L satisfying |L(v)| ≥5 for every v,
computes a proper L-coloring in time O(n2).
Proof sketch. At each inductive step, either we split along a chord and recurse on two near-
triangulations whose total size is |V (G)|+2, or we eliminate a boundary vertex, reducing |V (G)|
by one. Maintaining a doubly-linked list representation of the boundary cycle and the planar
embedding, each boundary-vertex elimination takes O(deg(vp)) time, and each chord-splitting
takes O(n) time. The total cost is dominated by P
v deg(v) = O(n) per “layer” (since G is
planar) with at most n layers, yielding O(n2). A more careful analysis using the linear planarity
algorithm of Hopcroft–Tarjan [18], given the embedding, gives O(n).

Examples and Tightness
We turn now to examples. Three points need to be illustrated by concrete signed planar graphs:
that the bound 5 in Theorem 1.3 cannot be lowered, that the boundary list size 3 in Theorem 1.4
cannot be lowered either, and that negative edges genuinely refine the unsigned theory rather
than merely relabel it.
The canonical witness for sharpness of the bound 5 is provided by Voigt’s planar graph,
suitably re-interpreted as a signed graph with the all-positive signature.
Example 6.1 (Voigt’s planar graph as a signed graph). Voigt [45] constructed a planar graph
G0 on 238 vertices that is not 4-choosable, with explicit list assignment L of size 4 at every

A smaller witness on 63 vertices is provided by Mirzakhani’s construction [29].
The previous example shows that 5 is sharp through the all-positive signature, but it does
not exhibit any genuine signed phenomenon. The next example shows that signed list-chromatic

Comparison with the existing literature
The result that every signed planar graph is signed 5-choosable was first proved by Jin, Kang,
and Steffen [20]. Their proof, while structurally Thomassen-style, develops the inductive setup
with explicit reference to the parity of the negative edges and uses specific properties of the colors
0 and ±-pairs. The proof presented here is, to our knowledge, the first that is entirely signature-
blind during the induction: the bookkeeping multiplies by σ(·) uniformly on every constraint,
so that with σ ≡+1 the proof reduces verbatim to Thomassen’s original argument [41].
A natural companion question to Theorem 1.3 is the signed Four Color Theorem. Máčajová,
Raspaud, and Škoviera [28] conjectured the analogue of the Four Color Theorem in the symmetric
palette: every signed planar graph satisfies χ(G, σ) ≤4.
This conjecture was disproved by
Kardˇos and Narboni [23], who exhibited a signed planar graph with χ = 5. The list-coloring
counterpart of the conjecture is also false: every counterexample to 4-choosability of an unsigned
planar graph (e.g. Voigt [45]) gives, via the all-positive signature, a signed planar graph that
is not signed 4-choosable. The situation in the signed setting is therefore fully analogous to
the unsigned one: 4-choosability fails, but 5-choosability holds, and our Theorem 1.4 provides a
particularly transparent proof of the latter.
Beyond the planar case, work on higher-genus surfaces is also relevant.
Böhme–Mohar–
Stiebitz [4], Choi–Kierstead [9], and DeVos–Kawarabayashi–Mohar [10] established choosability
bounds for graphs embedded in surfaces of positive Euler genus, with explicit dependence on the
genus. The signed analogues are open, although our methods extend without serious difficulty
to signed graphs embedded in the projective plane, the torus, and the Klein bottle, yielding
5-choosability for sufficiently large face-widths. We do not pursue this here.
Several other lines of work in signed-graph chromatic theory deserve mention.
The ho-
momorphism viewpoint of Naserasr, Rollová, and Sopena [30] expresses the signed chromatic
number χ(G, σ) as the smallest order of a signed graph (H, π) admitting a sign-preserving ho-
momorphism (G, σ) →(H, π), and is particularly fruitful for minor-closed families. A different
direction was taken by Lu and Ma [27], who studied Zk-colorings of signed graphs with colors in
cyclic groups rather than in Z. A further refinement, the circular chromatic theory of Naserasr,
Wang, and Zhu [32], distributes colors on the unit circle and quantifies the “angular” chromatic
obstruction. Yet another direction is the signed DP-coloring of Jin, Kang, and Steffen [21],
12


## Page 13


generalizing the DP-coloring of Dvořák and Postle [13]. The list-chromatic counterparts of sev-
eral of these refinements are largely unexplored, and our techniques are likely to apply, with
appropriate modifications, in each case.

Open problems and concluding remarks
Theorem 1.4 suggests several natural further questions. We collect them here in order of imme-
diacy.
Problem 8.1 (High girth refinements). Determine the signed list chromatic number of signed
planar graphs of girth ≥5. Theorem 5.8 gives an upper bound of 4. Is the truth 3?
Conjecture 8.2. Every signed planar graph of girth at least 4 is signed 4-choosable.
Turning from girth restrictions to surface restrictions, one can ask for analogues of Theo-
rem 1.3 on surfaces of positive Euler genus.
Problem 8.3 (Higher-genus). Does every signed graph (G, σ) embeddable in the projective
plane satisfy χs
ℓ(G, σ) ≤6? In the torus, ≤7? The unsigned analogues are known [9, 10, 4, 39].
A different sort of refinement strengthens the algorithmic content of Theorem 1.3 from offline
to online list coloring.
Problem 8.4 (Online/paintability version). Develop an online (paintability) version of Theo-
rem 1.3. The online list chromatic number of an unsigned planar graph is 5 [34]; we conjecture
the same for signed planar graphs.
A still stronger refinement replaces lists by the more general correspondence assignments of
DP-coloring.
Conjecture 8.5 (Signed DP-5-choosability). Every planar signed graph admits a signed DP-
coloring with 5 admissible options at each vertex (in the sense of [21]).
Finally, in the spirit of fine-tuning the result by structural hypotheses on the signature itself,
we ask:
Problem 8.6 (Antibalanced refinements). Refine Theorem 1.3 for antibalanced signed graphs
(every even cycle positive, every odd cycle negative). Is there a clean structural condition under
which a signed planar graph is 4-choosable?

We end with some general comments on the proof method. The argument given in Section 4
preserves the conceptual elegance of Thomassen’s original argument [41] by absorbing the signa-
ture entirely into the bookkeeping: the involution α 7→σ(uv) α converts forbidden colors at one
endpoint of an edge into forbidden colors at the other, exactly as in the unsigned identity, and
switching invariance (Theorem 3.1) is then a clean consequence of the same involution applied
vertex-by-vertex. We view this as evidence that, for chromatic-extremal problems on signed
graphs, the right strategy is to combine Zaslavsky-style structural analysis (switching, balance)
with Thomassen-style precoloring-extension arguments. When this combination is set up cor-
rectly, the resulting proofs are typically as short as in the unsigned case, suggesting that the
signed setting may be more amenable to systematic adaptation than its abundance of competing
definitions might at first suggest.
