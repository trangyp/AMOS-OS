Global climate models are essential tools to simulate past and potential future pathways
of climate change, as well as associated climate impacts. Shared Socioeconomic Pathways
(SSPs) describe a range of future scenarios of global economic and demographic development.
These SSPs are intrinsically linked to changes in climate forcings—the external drivers, such
as greenhouse gas and aerosol emissions, which in turn lead to the human impact on Earth’s
energy balance over time. These forcings are fundamental boundary conditions in climate
models in order to gain insight into the potential climatic impacts of these changes described
by each SSP. Running a climate model, however, is extremely computationally expensive,
conflicting with the need for large ensembles of simulations for each model to give, e.g., more
robust estimates in the presence of internal variability (the inherent, chaotic fluctuations
within the climate system) and scenario uncertainty.
Recent research has demonstrated
the ability to capture climate model dynamics using machine learning when conditioned on
forcings from different climatic scenarios. We here train a Deep Learning (DL) model on
multiple SSPs and successfully generate scenarios unseen during training. Our emulator
is validated against MESMER-M, a statistical emulator of land surface temperature. Our
research demonstrates the capacity to generate such changing climate states in response
to a variety of simultaneous climate forcings (e.g., carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide,
sulphate aerosols, and ozone). In particular, our ablation studies underline a need to include
a range of different forcings to represent long-term atmospheric trends with a DL emulator.

Introduction
Humanity is facing the consequences of a changing climate, ranging from vanishing ecosystem
services (benefits that humans derive from the natural environment) to increased exposure to
extreme events, putting the lives of humans, flora, and fauna at risk [61]. To move forward,
we need to mitigate, adapt, and develop our future sustainably [1]. A cornerstone of climate
change adaptation is humanity’s capacity to project the future of our climate under different
scenarios. Policymakers need to understand how different emission plans might affect Earth’s
climate to make informed decisions on behalf of the groups they represent. For decades, sci-
entists have contributed to understanding Earth’s future climate by developing climate models
[13]. Climate models started by modeling only the atmosphere and ocean, but recently they
have developed into Earth System Models (ESMs), i.e.
they simulate different parts of the
Earth in tandem, using physical equations that formalize what we know about how the atmo-
sphere, ocean, cryosphere and biosphere operate and interact [49, 48]. ESMs are run for many
different “what-if” experiments, helping scientists better understand the Earth’s climate [13, 27].

One set of such experiments is called ScenarioMIP [60], which compares the climate outputs of
different ESMs under different future scenarios. These experiments essentially ask “how will
our Earth’s climate look like if humanity behaves in a certain way?”. Those future scenarios of
human behaviour are called Shared Socio-Economic Pathways (SSPs) [66]. They describe the
future in terms of economic, societal, and demographic changes over the coming decades and as
such also formulate assumptions about future emissions of greenhouse gases and aerosols. SSPs
have further implications for the secondary formation of other forcing agents through atmo-
spheric chemical reactions, in particular tropospheric and stratospheric concentrations of ozone
and concerning the atmospheric oxidizing capacity (relevant for the degradation of greenhouse
gases such as methane). Changes in anthropogenic greenhouse gases and aerosols expected from
each SSP are provided through Input4MIPs, a coordinated activity that provides standardized,
version-controlled datasets, providing time-dependent emission maps or atmospheric concentra-
tion fields that are then used as inputs for the ESMs participating in ScenarioMIP [54]. In
summary, we can, e.g., input a future emission scenario into an ESM to simulate its long-term
climate response for a given SSP.
The resulting projections have several types of uncertainty. In the near-term, i.e. 30 years,
model uncertainty (how unsure we are about the output of the model) is a major component
of the the total uncertainty, followed by the climate system’s internal variability (the natural,
stochastic fluctuations within the climate system), and a small amount of scenario uncertainty
(the range of potential future climate outcomes resulting from different plausible pathways of hu-
man activity). As the projection moves farther into the future, scenario uncertainty can start to
become the dominant uncertainty given a wide range of SSPs [47, 43]. Unlike model uncertainty,
which could be narrowed through, e.g., improved representations of model physical processes and
computing power, scenario uncertainty is irreducible because it pertains to future human agency.
Understanding and quantifying this uncertainty is critical for policymakers; it underpins the ex-
ploration of strategies that remain effective across various trajectories. By generating different
scenarios, researchers can better demonstrate how immediate policy interventions could directly
alter the long-term climate. ESMs are computationally expensive, restricting the generation of a
wide variety of future scenarios. This work aims to help climate models in exploring additional
scenarios by providing a robust method to quickly generate spatially and temporally explicit
future climate simulations that respond appropriately to a multitude of external forcings.
In this study, we investigate whether generative Deep Learning (DL) can generate unseen
future climate scenarios. We aim to investigate the need to accurately represent a combina-
tion of different forcings on the emulated climate scenarios. We use ArchesClimate as the DL
framework, an autoregressive probabilistic climate model emulator based on ArchesWeather
[16, 17] and here train ArchesClimate on a set of climate projections produced with the IPSL-
CM6A-LR ESM [8]. We extend the original ArchesClimate framework by conditioning on sets
of forcing agents that represent trajectories under different SSP scenarios. We will refer to the
extended model generally as ArchesClimate-SSP, with each specific forcing setup being indicated
as ACforcing_setup, where forcing_setup will be defined in subsection 4.2. We train our model
using monthly averages from 2015-2100 for 8 different future scenarios, in addition to historical
runs and control runs. We held out three SSP scenarios (SSP4-3.4, SSP5-3.4 and SSP5-8.5) for
testing.
We structure the paper as follows: we first provide a review of the current state of climate
model emulation. We then explain the methods used in our research. Following this, we show
that we can accurately emulate SSP scenarios unseen at training time. To do so, we first evaluate
if the generated scenarios represent temporal and spatial variability of the IPSL climate model,
and we compare our results to MESMER-Ms, a state-of-the-art spatial statistical emulator,
[57, 5]. Finally, we perform ablations of the model by removing different forcings. This shows how
different forcings inform the accuracy of ArchesClimate-SSP when generating future scenarios.
The contributions of this research are as follows:

Background
To contextualize our proposed method for accelerating SSP generation, it is necessary to examine
previous efforts to improve the computational efficiency of ESMs. We begin by outlining the
computational bottlenecks of traditional models, followed by a review of traditional non-DL
emulators.
We then explore the recent paradigm shift toward machine learning approaches,
concluding with the ongoing challenge of incorporating external forcings into these frameworks.
Historically, the primary alternative to ESMs has been the development of reduced-order or
intermediate complexity models. These tools trade the complexity of an ESM for computational
efficiency, allowing for the rapid simulation of diverse scenarios [42, 52, 55]. To generate spatially
explicit data at low computational costs, these emulators are often combined with pattern scaling
techniques [69, 71, 65] or functions based on historical temperature and precipitation relation-
ships [12]. More advanced statistical emulators, such as MESMER and its monthly extension
MESMER-M, successfully model internal variability and regional trends for temperature based
on global yearly values [5, 57]. The most direct method for improving the speed of ESMs is the
expansion of computational resources. However, ESMs are already computationally expensive,
and the associated engineering and operational costs remain prohibitively high [3]. While re-
searchers are increasingly leveraging automatic differentiation and GPU computing to optimize
the performance of modern physics-based climate simulations [31, 38, 41], further research is
needed to represent full ESMs, driving the need for alternative modeling approaches.
Deep learning (DL) has emerged as a means to decouple computational performance from
physical complexity. While DL applied to weather forecasting has already demonstrated the
ability to match or exceed state-of-the-art physics-based models for short-term predictions [4,
62, 17], DL applied to climate models translate these gains to decadal and centennial timescales.
Architectures used to tackle these problems are categorized into deterministic models, which
produce a single trajectory from a given initial condition [18, 32], and generative models, such as
diffusion or flow-matching networks, which generate an ensemble of trajectories for a single state
[10, 9]. Generative models create plausible samples that are particularly effective at capturing
the stochastic variability inherent in climate systems. While recent models have successfully
emulated both isolated ocean components and coupled ocean-atmosphere dynamics, these efforts
remain largely focused on stationary climate conditions [67, 26, 23].
Despite these advancements, capturing the non-stationarity of a long-term forced climate re-
sponse to external drivers like greenhouse gases and solar irradiation remains a significant hurdle.
While some models utilize solar irradiation or prescribed sea surface temperatures (SSTs) as in-
puts, this approach is often criticized as it avoids the fundamental task of simulating how the
atmosphere responds to greenhouse gases and aerosols, as the resulting climate trajectory is
already largely dictated by the input.
There is a notable scarcity of ML emulators capable
of prognostic modeling the Earth’s response to Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) inter-
nally rather than relying on pre-determined ocean conditions, which remains a critical gap for
contributing to meaningful policy-making. The core challenge lies in capturing shifting distribu-
tions. For example, recent work like ACE2-SOM has explored carbon dioxide (CO2) sensitivity
at fixed warming levels (e.g., 2°C or 4°C), but these models often exhibit extreme sensitivity
to distribution shifts [15, 39]. Similarly, AeroGP uses Gaussian processes to capture regional

attempt future state generation based on SSPs [36, 50], accurately modeling the spatiotemporal

Methods
This section introduces the dataset used in the project. We then detail the model architecture
and training procedures, concluding with an overview of the metrics and analytical methods
used for evaluation.
3.1
Forcings
Our model incorporates forcings from multiple sources, including greenhouse gases, aerosols,
solar irradiation and ozone. Here we describe each forcing in detail. Please see Table 1 for a
summary of the forcings included in ArchesClimate-SSP.
We incorporate concentrations for the major well-mixed greenhouse gases (GHGs)—carbon
dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O)—sourced from the input4MIPs repos-
itory [53, 54]. These three gases, measured in parts per billion (ppb) for CH4 and nitrous oxide
(N2O) and parts per million (ppm) for CO2, were selected as our primary forcing agents due
to their dominant roles in the historical anthropogenic greenhouse effect and under typical fu-
ture SSPs [11]. CO2 is the major anthropogenic greenhouse gas by historical radiative forcing
and also under future SSPs, driving long-term climate change due to its longevity and large,
persistent increases in emissions. Methane also poses a substantial climate forcing but has a
much shorter e-folding time (the time it takes for the concentration of a gas to decrease to
approximately 37% of its original amount) of around 10 years, resulting in potentially faster,
scenario-dependent fluctuations in its atmospheric concentrations. N2O has an estimated e-
folding time of more than 100 years and is the third most important anthropogenic greenhouse
gas directly emitted into the atmosphere, e.g. from agricultural activities. These gases are well
mixed, meaning they are relatively equally distributed throughout the atmosphere due to their
sufficiently long lifetimes for the atmospheric circulation to distribute them across the entire
atmosphere.
The GHG concentrations from input4MIPS are 0.5 degree latitudinal monthly
resolution, which we regrid to the resolution of the IPSL model ( 2.5x1.25 degrees) using first-
order conservative regridding. Conservative regridding is a numerical method for transferring
data between different grids that ensures the total integral of a quantity, like heat or moisture,
is perfectly preserved so that no energy or mass is artificially created or lost. Following Arch-
esClimate, we also include these forcings as non-spatial (spatially averaged means) conditionings
using Condition Layer Normalization [16]. Conditional Layer Normalization is a technique that
adapts the standard normalization process by making the gain and bias parameters dependent
on external information, allowing the model to dynamically shift its activations based on the
context [14].
Next to greenhouse gases, anthropogenic aerosol emissions pose another major human impact
on Earth’s energy budget, which we consider here.
Such aerosols are tiny solid particles or
liquid droplets suspended in the atmosphere.
We use the atmospheric load for four aerosol
types: Aerosol Soluble Nitrate (ASNO3M), Coarse Soluble Nitrate (CSNO3M), Coarse Insoluble
Nitrate (CINO3M) and Sulfate (SO4). In particular sulfate aerosols are known to reflect sunlight,
causing a counteracting cooling balance on the historical climate [58], including important non-
linear temperature effects[29, 50, 2]. We use data from INCA, a chemistry and aerosol model
developed at the Laboratoire des Sciences du Climat et de l’Environnement [45, 33] INCA is
ran beforehand to generate aerosols states that are then used downstream in the IPSL model.
We again conservatively regrid this data to a 144x144 resolution from 143x144. Here we simply
include the atmospheric load (kg m−2) of the aerosols as a rough proxy of Aerosol Optical Depth.

Solar Spectral Irradiance (SSI) is sunlight that reaches different parts of the atmosphere, de-
termined by its wavelength. We chose to include this forcing to give the model more information
to understand radiative forcing [11]. As it is the only consistent forcing across different SSP and
historical/control scenarios, we also expect it to act as a temporal signal. The solar irradiation
included is non-spatial at a monthly temporal resolution.
Finally, we differentiate ozone from the other greenhouse gases, because ozone is formed
through chemical reactions in the atmosphere rather than being directly emitted from the Earth’s
surface [63]. In addition, its concentrations differ by orders of magnitude between the tropo-
sphere and the stratosphere. This is down to very different ozone production and loss pathways
depending on the availability of sunlight, ozone-depleting substances, and (tropospheric) ozone-
precursors. For example, in the troposphere, primary ozone production pathways depend on the
availability of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) – including methane – and of nitrogen oxides
(NOx) from both biogenic and anthropogenic sources at Earth’s surface. In the stratosphere,
ozone production is primarily driven by the Chapman reactions in the presence of high-frequency
ultraviolet sunlight, in balance with a variety of major catalytic ozone loss reactions. The latter
also depend on anthropogenic emissions of N2O and, famously, on a variety of halogen-containing
ozone-depleting substances, which have led to the development of substantial Antarctic ozone
holes over the last decades [40]. In our framework, we use ozone mole fractions from input4mips
covering the troposphere, stratosphere, and even the mesosphere, and the data is conservatively
regridded from 96x144 (latxlon) to 144x144m [56]. There are 66 vertical layers ranging from
0.0001 hPa to 1000 hPa, but we only include the following layers: [1000, 850, 500, 200, 100, 50,
10, 1, 0.01, 0.0001]. We expect ozone to help inform both stratospheric and tropospheric cooling
and warming trends [59, 46, 64, 68].
We still only include a subset of the forcings provided to IPSL-CM6A-LR due to computa-
tional restraints and our focus on atmospheric dynamics [45]. For example, we assumed volcanic
activity would be implicit in the aerosol data provided, and that the climatic influence of ocean
biogeochemical data and land-use cover data were of lower magnitude and could be omitted as
a compromise with computational cost and model complexity, and left for future work.
3.2
Climate Model Data
We select the IPSL-CM6A-LR model [8] as our primary data source for several reasons. Its
grid resolution (1.25◦× 2.5◦) provides a computationally efficient yet physically dense dataset
ideal for training deep generative emulators. Furthermore, the model’s atmospheric component,
LMDZ6, incorporates advanced parameterizations for tropical convection and boundary layer
dynamics, providing a state-of-the-art target for our machine learning objectives [35]. We include
data from the IPSL-CM6A-LR for 8 SSP scenarios, the historical scenario and the piControl
scenario. Following ScenarioMIP conventions, the scenarios range from an approximate global
radiative forcing of 1.9 Wm−2 – a strong climate change mitigation scenario – to 8.5 Wm−2 by
2100. In general, we train our model on all ensemble members (which sample internal variability
uncertainty) of each scenario; however, we include only 10 of the 31 available members of the
historical run to keep the model focused on future scenarios. Please see Table 2 for the number
of members used for each scenario. A high-level description of the scenarios can be found in
Table 2. The variables included in ArchesClimate-SSP are summarized in Table 3.
For the evaluation of our model, we utilize SSP4-3.4 and abrupt-4xCO2 for the validation,
and SSP5-3.4 and SSP5-8.5 for the test set. We use historical, piControl, SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6,

3.3
Architecture
For our deep learning framework, we employ ArchesClimate-SSP [16, 17], an extension of the
ArchesWeather architecture. ArchesWeather is a Sliding Window (Swin) Vision Transformer
based on the Pangu-Weather model. It efficiently processes weather/climate states by partition-
ing spatial regions into tokens, which are then processed through successive attention blocks.
These blocks utilize a windowing mechanism that shifts in size and location to capture multi-
scale dependencies.
Following the methodology of both ArchesWeather and ArchesClimate,
the prediction task is split into two tasks. First, the model first learns a deterministic mean
for the subsequent timestep, which is followed by a generative residual to capture probabilistic
uncertainty [16, 17, 44, 7].
We incorporate external forcings through two distinct pathways based on their dimension-
ality. Spatially resolved forcings (ozone, aerosols, and spatial GHGs) are concatenated directly
to the input state. In contrast, non-spatial forcings (global GHG concentrations and SSI) are
integrated via conditional layer normalization, as implemented in ArchesClimate [14]. This tech-
nique modulates the normalization process by scaling and shifting the layer outputs using a mean
and bias derived from the forcing inputs. Our configuration utilizes an embedding dimension of

3.4
Training and Inference
While training and inference follow the ArchesClimate framework, we implement the following
modifications. To prevent overfitting on forcings, we mask the input forcing data with a proba-
bility of λ (as shown in Figure 1); this restricts the model to rely on the previous timestep and
internalize the climate response. To ensure long-term stability, we randomly sample a forecast
horizon h from the set of discrete lead times H = {1, 6, 12}, which is provided to the model
as an embedded value. subsection 5.2 describes how the values for these hyperparameters were
chosen. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss is then calculated over the specific interval ∆t = h.
This approach encourages the model to learn long-term dependencies, thereby enhancing sta-
bility (see subsection A.1 for related ablations of λ and ∆t). The randomized timestep is only
applied to the deterministic model. While the deterministic model utilizes a standard MSE loss,
the generative model employs a combination of MSE and spectral loss, detailed in the following
section. As in both [16, 17], we use an ensemble of 4 deterministic models to train the generative
model.

these generated time series as rollouts, with each rollout serving as a distinct ensemble member.
A detailed visualization of this pipeline is available in [16, 17].

4.1
Metrics
We use Latitude-Weighted Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Normalized RMSE (for compari-
son across variables), and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) to evaluate the accuracy of
ArchesClimate-SSP. To evaluate the variance of ArchesClimate-SSP, we use Interannual Variabil-

as a physical sanity check when comparing ablated models. In meteorology, hydrostatic balance
is expressed by combining the Hydrostatic Equation and the Ideal Gas Law [34]. Please see
subsection A.4 for precise definitions of the listed metrics.
4.2
Ablated Models
To determine which forcings are essential for successfully emulating a variety of SSP scenarios,
we conduct a systematic ablation study. Each generative model in this study is initialized and
trained following a deterministic training phase using the same forcing configuration. We do
not perform a separate hyperparameter search for the ablated deterministic models. While this
would allow for the most optimal performance for each model, it is computationally expensive
and therefore left for further work. It is important to note that when a forcing is removed in this
context, it is not physically absent from the underlying climate data; rather, the emulator is no
longer explicitly provided with that specific forcing. These forcings act as cues that emphasize
dynamics the model has already encountered during training, rather than introducing entirely
new information. We evaluate several model configurations to discern the impact of individual
forcings on simulated climate trends:
Full Model (ACfull): Our primary configuration, which includes all forcings. This model is
used for the scenario emulations discussed in subsection 5.3.
Ablated Models: Three variations where a forcing is withheld to test its necessity: AC¬O3
(no ozone), AC¬ghg (no greenhouse gases) and AC¬aero (no aerosols).
4.3
Checkpoint Selection
Selecting a point in a model’s training (i.e. a checkpoint) that will provide the best performance
presents a significant challenge, as long-term stability in climate rollouts can be elusive. As it
is too computationally expensive to generate full 85-year rollouts during training, we have to
verify the stability after training. To identify the most robust deterministic model for integration
into our generative framework, we execute full 85-year rollouts across several training epochs.
10


## Page 11


These epochs are evaluated based on their Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) across
all surface variables. All deterministic models are trained until convergence, which is assessed
by finding the lowest NRMSE of the final decade of a rollout. Once the superior checkpoint
is identified, it serves as the checkpoint for the generative model.
Following [17, 16], four
deterministic models are trained and served as an ensemble to best predict the mean state. If
the deterministic models were not fully converged, the Flow Matching (FM) model would have
to work harder to resolve the remaining residuals, resulting in slower convergence and requiring
more training epochs and possibly creating further bias.
For the generative phase, we evaluate different Flow Matching (FM) configurations using
NRMSE. We intentionally maintain constant hyperparameters across all forcing ablation exper-
iments. By holding the architecture and optimization settings static, we ensure that any observed
change in performance is attributable to the removal of the physical forcing, rather than shifts
in the model’s learning capacity or optimization efficiency. To assess the performance of each
ablated model, we calculated the Normalized Root Mean Square Error (NRMSE) for all ablation
experiments across checkpoints from training steps 16,000 to 40,000. This interval was selected
to capture checkpoints both prior to and following the inclusion of the spectral loss, with the
upper limit defined by the stabilization of the training loss over the final 5,000 steps. To opti-
mize computational efficiency, full validation was conducted post-hoc rather than during active
training. For each ablated generative model, as with the corresponding deterministic model, we
take the two model checkpoints that have the lowest NRMSE.
4.4
Baseline
We utilize MESMER-M [57, 5] as our primary baseline, a statistical climate model emulator
designed to produce spatially explicit monthly temperature fields. While the original MESMER
framework [6] focuses on annual temperature maps by accounting for both the long-term local
warming signal and grid-point-specific internal variability, MESMER-M extends this capability
to a monthly temporal resolution [57]. We selected MESMER-M as a benchmark because it is a
well-established, non-machine-learning method that decomposes the climate signal into distinct
mean and residual components.
The mean component of MESMER-M is modeled as a harmonic Fourier series that rep-
resents a smooth seasonal cycle. To account for non-stationarity, both the baseline and the
seasonal amplitude are fitted as linear combinations of the annual mean temperature. The re-
maining residual component, which represents internal variability, is modeled using a first-order
autoregressive process.
For our experimental setup, we calibrated MESMER-M using a single ensemble member
from five different scenarios: SSP1-1.9, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. Anomalies
were calculated relative to the 1850–1900 pre-industrial reference period. During the evaluation
phase, we provided MESMER-M with the target annual mean surface temperatures from the
IPSL model to predict the monthly temperature of two withheld scenarios, SSP4-3.4 and the
overshoot scenario SSP5-3.4. Further technical details regarding the MESMER-M architecture
can be found in [57].

5.2
Stability of Ablated Models
As described in subsection 4.3, we identify the most accurate checkpoints for final analysis,
requiring at least two stable checkpoints per model to ensure results were robust and to miti-
gate the potential for selecting a stochastic "lucky" iteration. Notably, the ozone-ablated model
produced only a single stable checkpoint and was consequently omitted from the comparative
analysis, as it failed to meet our stability criteria. This divergence in stability highlights the
varying importance of specific forcings within the emulator. The failure of the ozone-ablated
model underscores that vertically explicit ozone is a critical forcing for resolving the vertical
structure of the atmosphere over decadal timescales. As the primary driver of the thermal struc-
ture of the stratosphere, the input4MIPs ozone data provides the necessary physical constraints
for the model to learn vertical heat distribution. In its absence, we hypothesize that the emu-
lator lacks the information required to account for stratospheric heating and cooling dynamics,
which fundamentally govern tropospheric circulation. The rest of the paper omits analysis of
AC¬ozone.

Comparison to MESMER-M baseline
Our model demonstrates a high capacity to accurately emulate the IPSL outputs for several

The AC¬aero model achieves a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) comparable to MESMER−

veals a marked divergence in performance across the trained architectures; specifically, AC¬aero
significantly outperforms both ACfull and AC¬ghg. Furthermore, both ACfull and ACghg ex-

relative to MESMER −M and AC¬aero. We hypothesize that the inclusion of aerosol data in-
troduces substantial complexity into the predictive task. Conversely, the omission of greenhouse
gas concentrations appears to deprive the model of the primary forcing necessary to capture the
secular warming trend, leading to model divergence. These hypotheses are explored in greater
detail in the subsequent sections.
Interestingly, while all models exhibit similar or higher geographic variability than MESMER−
M, its IAV remains notably low. This suppressed IAV is likely a byproduct of the single-step
autoregression; without a broader temporal context, the model may fail to capture longer-term
oscillatory variations. Achieving an optimal balance between temporal variance and stability is
critical, as excessive variability can lead to long-term instability. Because MESMER −M is
conditioned on prescribed global temperatures, its inter-annual variations align closely with the
IPSL target. In contrast, generating unique, member-specific trends would require an impulse
response model to provide global mean temperatures, such as MAGICC [6]. Note that because
IPSL provides only a single member, no reference ensemble spread is available for comparison.

5.3.4
Spatio-Temporal Evolution of SST Anomalies
We have now seen that the model is able to adhere to regional trends over time at the surface,
but it is also important to see how the model evolves over time in the ocean. To qualitatively
compare whether the emulator effectively captures shifting climate dynamics in key sensitive
areas, we examine Sea Surface Temperature (SST) anomalies in Figure 5. This also gives us
the opportunity to assess the limited ocean representation in the model. SST anomalies are
calculated by removing the climatology derived from the decade 1994-2014. SST was selected
as the primary variable for this analysis because it is a fundamental indicator of oceanic heat
storage and atmospheric coupling [20]. For this comparison, five ensemble members are used for
all models and are compared to the single ensemble member of the IPSL. Given the presence
of significant internal variability, this assessment focuses qualitatively on the model’s ability
to represent large-scale temporal trends and spatial progressions rather than exact member-to-
member matching.
As illustrated in the Hovmöller diagrams (Figure 5), distinct spatiotemporal thermal trajecto-
ries emerge across both geographical regions and forcing scenarios. In the Northern Hemisphere,
the aerosol-masked experiment (AC¬aero) demonstrates an evolution most consistent with the
IPSL model baseline. Conversely, the greenhouse gas-masked ensemble (AC¬ghg) reveals pro-
nounced Arctic cooling, reinforcing the decadal anomalies previously identified in Figure 4.
While the magnitude varies by model, the simulated dynamics consistently capture the high-
latitude "overshoot" phenomenon, characterized by a distinct peak in thermal intensity followed
by a gradual decline. Furthermore, the inherent variance reduction in the ensemble yields a
significantly smoother signal compared to the stochasticity of the single-member IPSL baseline.

gions, suggesting a consistent overestimation of forced warming when aerosol cooling effects are
absent.

Atmospheric Performance
To assess the impact of forcings on vertical structure, we evaluate the vertical profiles of ablated
models across the Tropics (20°N–20°S), the Northern Region (20°N–90°N), and the Southern
Region (20°S–90°S) for the final decade of the century (2090–2100) for the SSP5-3.4 scenario.
This period was selected to evaluate model performance under the maximum forced response.
As illustrated in Figure 7 and Figure 12, we calculate the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)
for air temperature (ta) and geopotential height (zg) across all pressure levels. Given the high
fidelity of specific humidity (hus) predictions, its analysis is deferred to subsection A.5, while
zonal and meridional wind components (ua, va) are omitted due to poor baseline performance
across all configurations. Each ablation ensemble comprises 10 members, derived from the two
top-performing training epochs with five initializations each.
The resulting error distributions reveal several insights into model sensitivity.
Notably,
all models except AC¬ghg exhibit low uncertainty within the Tropics, consistent with the low
dynamical variability characteristic of this region. The AC¬aero model consistently yields lower
RMSE across nearly all regions and pressure levels, supporting the hypothesis that the inclusion
of aerosol predictors introduces confounding noise rather than predictive skill. In the Southern
Region, the performance of AC¬aero and ACfull is virtually indistinguishable; this suggests
that aerosols provide negligible information in the southern region, where anthropogenic sources
and landmass are limited.
Conversely, AC¬ghg exhibits the highest RMSE and the greatest
inter-member uncertainty, reinforcing the premise that greenhouse gas (GHG) concentrations
are essential for capturing secular trends. This degradation is most pronounced in the Tropics,
where the absence of a strong seasonal cycle forces the model to rely on GHG concentrations as
the primary anchor for long-term forced trends, which in AC¬ghg, is not explicitly provided.
Analysis of geopotential height (zg) mirrors the error patterns observed for ta. In the North-

with the temperature inaccuracies and supporting the hypothesis that a failure to resolve the
thermal structure precludes a correct representation of geopotential height. This hierarchical
dependency is consistent with the behaviour of flow-matching models, which typically resolve
low-frequency signals, such as temperature trends, prior to higher-frequency variables [28]. The
close correspondence between ta and zg errors further suggests that the models maintain phys-
ical consistency between thermal and barometric fields, even when the underlying forcing is
incorrectly specified.

6
Discussion and Conclusion
We present a deep-learning climate emulator that responds flexibly to forcings and can differ-
entiate between the forced response and climate response. We can properly reproduce different
SSP pathways for several variables with explicit spatial dynamics.
Our results demonstrate
that ArchesClimate-SSP can successfully capture shifting atmospheric dynamics across both the
troposphere and stratosphere when provided with sufficient forcings.
23


## Page 24


The competitive performance of ArchesClimate-SSP relative to MESMER-M is a promising
sign for climate modeling. While MESMER-M is valued for its transparent structure and com-
putational speed, ArchesClimate-SSP provides unique and important advantages. Its capacity
to produce diverse ensemble members is particularly useful because it enables thorough proba-
bilistic analysis for scenarios that originally only had one run. ArchesClimate-SSP also emulates
the entire atmospheric profile rather than just surface temperatures, which allows it to capture
a broader range of atmospheric behavior. By creating these ensembles from single instances
like those from IPSL, the model gives researchers a more flexible way to study the full range of
potential outcomes in various climate scenarios.
Each forcing integrated into our framework serves a distinct role in maintaining the phys-
ical consistency of the emulator.
The inclusion of greenhouse gas (GHG) concentrations is
essential for capturing long-term trends and ensuring stability, a result consistent with the well-
documented linear relationship between CO2 and global temperature response [51]. Further-
more, vertically explicit ozone is integral to the generation of stable and accurate emulations,
providing a coherent structural anchor across the atmospheric column.
In contrast, we find that incorporating the Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) from the INCA
model, which is characterized by high spatial and temporal variability, of multiple aerosol species
tends to degrade model performance. We hypothesize that while ozone provides a stable, spa-
tially coherent signal from which the model can effectively learn, aerosols introduce significant
stochasticity.
Because many aerosol processes, such as aerosol-cloud interactions, evolve on
timescales much shorter than a month, their representation in monthly-resolution data likely
presents a noisy signal. This high-frequency variability may confound the emulator’s learning
process, whereas the vertically explicit ozone profile provides the structural information neces-
sary to resolve the atmosphere’s thermal and dynamical state. These findings prompt further
investigation into whether simplified representations, such as vertically integrated ozone or more
explicitly constrained aerosol fields, could better guide model convergence and stability.
We posit that since the training data implicitly has these dynamics, these forcings act more
as attention-guiding mechanisms rather than new information. The selection of specific forcings,
therefore, can depend on the application and the desired level of complexity, as different inputs
emphasize distinct system behaviours. Including more diverse forcings could help the model
emulate more subtle dynamics, perhaps at the cost of overall accuracy. Prescribed land-use
change, volcanic activity, and biogeochemical information could all be used to improve different
aspects of the emulation, depending on the goal of the emulator. Future research should inves-
tigate the extent to which these forcings can be compressed without loss of fidelity to facilitate
easier integration into a climate model emulator. Furthermore, employing masked autoencoders
or adaptable embeddings could yield a more versatile framework for representing these forcings.
A comprehensive experimental suite, such as the one detailed in this research, is essential to
fully quantify the impact of these drivers. Further work could also include a more varied set of
idealized experiments to explore further the impact of these forcings. To further improve the
model’s ability to learn slower timescales, subsequent iterations could incorporate deep ocean
dynamics and land-use changes. Additionally, exploring multi-timestep training or leveraging
recent advances in generative video modeling could better resolve the temporal dependencies
inherent in climate evolution.
Despite these successes, the lack of inter-annual variability in ArchesClimate-SSP warrants
further investigation.
It remains to be determined whether this deficiency can be rectified
through alternative modeling strategies, such as introducing temporal attention or increasing
the temporal resolution of the training data. While monthly-averaged data is well-suited for
capturing centennial temperature trends, it may not be sufficient to resolve shorter-term oscilla-
tions. Currently, the model struggles to capture wind dynamics, which remain highly stochastic
at the monthly scale and are therefore prone to significant error. Refining the emulation of these
high-variance variables is a necessary next step for the development of a comprehensive climate
24


## Page 25


emulator. Without accurately resolving these dynamic fields, the model will likely remain unable
to capture climatic extremes and rare events that are driven by rapid atmospheric fluctuations.
A common challenge identified throughout this research is the systematic under-representation
of regions characterized by high gradients or abrupt transitions. This limitation is particularly
evident in surface precipitation and flux maps, at the tropopause, and along surface boundaries
in the geopotential height (zg) fields. To address these spatial discrepancies, future work could
leverage recent advancements in architectural design that move beyond standard convolutions.
For instance, recent research employing a hierarchical map of residuals could better capture
the multiscale interactions between global trends and localized gradients [70].
Furthermore,
transitioning to a HEALPix grid structure would likely mitigate the inherent spatial biases and
distortion issues common at the poles in traditional latitude-longitude projections.
While these datasets are rooted in physical processes, they can be effectively conceptualized
through the lens of classical machine learning theory. Our results mirror foundational principles
regarding feature selection and the bias-variance tradeoff. Specifically, incorporating features
with low signal-to-noise ratios (e.g. aerosols) can increase model variance without improving ac-
curacy. In this context, the model attempts to learn spurious correlations from stochastic noise
rather than capturing a robust physical signal. As we transition toward larger, high-capacity ar-
chitectures, determining the relevant set of forcings remains a critical bottleneck. Discerning the
predictive relevance of specific forcings will be integral to developing comprehensive climate em-
ulators that are both computationally efficient and physically grounded, ensuring that increased
model complexity translates into genuine predictive skill rather than mere overfitting.
In summary, this research demonstrates that atmospheric dynamics under a shifting climate
can be accurately captured by integrating specific forcings into a generative framework. We
show that our model can effectively emulate scenarios not seen during training, confirming that
GHG concentrations are a prerequisite for trend stability. Furthermore, we establish that a
correct set of forcings is essential for resolving the vertical thermal structure of the atmosphere.
These findings provide a vital roadmap for future climate modeling, offering insights into how
we can build more efficient, accurate, and physically grounded emulators for long-term climate
projections.
