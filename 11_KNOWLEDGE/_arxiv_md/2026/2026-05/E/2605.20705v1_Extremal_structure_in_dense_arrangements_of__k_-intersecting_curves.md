A reﬁned r-division theorem
A classical result due to Lipton and Tarjan [8] from 1979, known as the planar separator theorem,
states that every N-vertex planar graph contains a set of at most O(
√
N) vertices whose removal
leaves no connected component with more than 2N/3 of the vertices. It has played a central role
in the structural theory of planar graphs, enabling eﬃcient divide-and-conquer arguments. Over
the past several decades, numerous variants of the planar separator theorem have been established,
including a separator theorem for nonplanar graphs due to Alon, Seymour, and Thomas [1], as well
as the shallow-minor and excluded-minor separator results of Plotkin, Rao, and Smith [13]. We
will use the following simple-cycle version due to Miller. Throughout this paper, all simple cycles
C will be viewed as a simple closed curve in the plane. Moreover, all graphs are allowed to have
multiple edges.
Theorem 2.1 (Miller, [10]). Let G be a planar graph with N vertices embedded in the plane with
all faces of size 2 or 3, and let w : V (G) →R≥0 be a nonnegative weight function on the vertices of
G with total weight W. Then there exists a simple cycle C in G such that |C| = O(
√
N), the total
weight of the vertices strictly inside C is at most 2W/3, and the total weight of the vertices strictly
outside C is at most 2W/3.
In 1987, Frederickson [4] introduced the notion of an r-division of a planar graph, providing a
reﬁned separator-based decomposition into a structured partition. To deﬁne r-divisions precisely,
we ﬁrst recall several deﬁnitions. Let G = (V, E, F) be a planar graph embedded in the plane, where
V is the set of vertices, represented by distinct points in the plane, E is the set of edges, drawn as
simple curves connecting the corresponding pairs of vertices, and F is the set of faces determined
by this embedding. A subgraph R ⊆G is called a region of G if R is a connected edge-induced
subgraph embedded in the plane. A division of G is a collection of regions R = {R1, . . . , Rk} such
that each edge of G lies in at least one region Ri. Given a division R of G, a vertex v ∈V (G) is
called a boundary vertex if it lies in more than one region of R. For convenience, we denote by
b(R) ⊆V (R) the set of boundary vertices of a region R. We say that a division R is an r-division if
each region Ri has at most O(r) vertices, |R| = O(|V |/r), and each Ri has at most O(√r) boundary
vertices.
Theorem 2.2 ([4]). There is an absolute constant r0 such that the following holds. For r ≥r0,
every planar graph embedded in the plane admits an r-division.

in [7]. The proof is based on recursively applying planar cycle-separators. For our purposes, we

by at least two distinct leave regions of the recursion tree. This statement also holds for our reﬁned

in the unbalanced regimes |P| = Ω(|C|2) or |C| = Ω(|P|k+1).
Another interesting direction is to determine whether one can obtain a polynomial improve-
ment in Theorem 1.2. Since our argument relies on the hypergraph removal lemma, it yields only a
qualitative o(·)-improvement over the Pach–Sharir bound. Even in the special case of point-line ar-
rangements, proving a polynomial improvement beyond Solymosi’s result [17] remains a challenging
problem.
