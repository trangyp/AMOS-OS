Abstract
Everywhere-δ-dense graphs are defined as graphs on n vertices in which every vertex has
degree at least δn for some constant δ > 0. Approximation schemes are vital for handling
NP-hard optimization problems, but for many graph cut problems, existing PTAS algo-
rithms often suffer from running times of nf(1/ε). In this paper, we bring PTASs down to
EPTASs for several fundamental minimization problems on everywhere-Ω(1)-dense graphs.
Specifically, we present the first Eﬀicient Polynomial-Time Approximation Scheme (EP-
TAS), running in time f(1/ε)nO(1), for the ConstrainedMinCut problem under a global
constraint on vertex weights, a problem that captures BalancedSeparator and Small-
SetExpansion. Moreover, we give the first EPTASs for MinQuotientCut and Prod-
uctSparsestCut on everywhere-δ-dense graphs with integer-valued dense vertex weights;
these problems generalize the four well-known problems UniformSparsestCut, EdgeEx-
pansion, Conductance, and NormalizedCut.
Our main technical contribution is an EPTAS for ConstrainedMinCut, based on the
weak regularity lemma and sampling and estimation techniques. We then obtain EPTASs for
MinQuotientCut and ProductSparsestCut via a unified reduction that invokes this
algorithm as a subroutine. In contrast, previous works giving PTASs for these problems on
everywhere-δ-dense graphs typically rely on powerful tools such as the Lasserre hierarchy or
specific integer programming techniques [2, 15], which we avoid.

Introduction
1.1
Approximation Schemes
It is known that many optimization problems cannot be solved in polynomial time, under the
assumption that P ̸= NP. Approximation algorithms are among the most important approaches
for handling such hard problems. To be more precise, approximation algorithms are algorithms
that, in polynomial time, produce a solution that is guaranteed to be close to the optimal
solution within a particular factor.
In this paper, we are particularly interested in approximation schemes. Let f be a function
that depends only on ε. A polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) is a deterministic
algorithm that takes an instance of an optimization problem and a parameter ε > 0 and, in
nf(1/ε) time, produces a solution that is within a factor of 1 + ε of the optimal solution for
minimization problems (or within a factor of 1 −ε for maximization problems). A PTAS allows
us to obtain a solution arbitrarily close to optimal by choosing a suﬀiciently small ε, although the
running time may increase rapidly as ε decreases. To overcome this diﬀiculty, let us consider an
eﬀicient polynomial-time approximation scheme (EPTAS), which is a PTAS with running time
f(1/ε)nO(1), allowing us to obtain a solution arbitrarily close to optimal more eﬀiciently than a
PTAS. The randomized analogue of an EPTAS is called an EPRAS, i.e., an algorithm that runs
in time f(1/ε)nO(1) and that returns a (1 + ε)-approximate solution for minimization problems
(or a (1 −ε)-approximate solution for maximization problems) with constant probability.
Unfortunately, many NP-hard problems do not even admit a PTAS unless P = NP [3].
However, this result does not refute the existence of a PTAS for some restricted classes of
instances. For example, Arora, Karger, and Karpinski [2] presented the first PTAS in nO(1/ε2)
time for several NP-hard problems for dense graphs. Indeed, there has been extensive work on
PTASs for maximization problems for dense graphs [7, 10, 14, 16, 23, 28]. In addition, Goldreich,
Goldwasser, and Ron presented an EPRAS for MaxCut and additive-error approximation
algorithms for some cut problems by applying property testing techniques [14].
As stated
above, our focus is on NP-hard problems for dense graphs.
1.2
Minimization Problems for Dense Graphs
To the best of our knowledge, there are few known approximation results for NP-hard mini-
mization problems on dense graphs. Indeed, the density required for PTASs for minimization
problems is, in a sense, often stronger than that for maximization problems. One reason is that
maximization problems on dense graphs also have large optimal solution values, which allow
large additive error (i.e., small relative error), thereby yielding a PTAS or even an EPTAS. On
the other hand, since density alone in the minimization setting does not, in general, ensure a
large optimal solution value, even when the given graph instances are dense, it is diﬀicult to
apply techniques from maximization problems to minimization problems directly. This differ-
ence makes it substantially more challenging to design approximation algorithms that achieve
small relative error for minimization problems.
Nevertheless, some PTASs for minimization problems for dense graphs are known. For ex-
ample, several PTASs for CSPs on dense instances have been developed: Bazgan, Fernandez de
la Vega, and Karpinski gave PTASs for specific classes of minimum CSPs [6, 5], Karpinski and
Schudy gave EPRASs for minimum CSPs on specific (fragile or rigid, i.e., if we independently
round or guess the assignments of vertices, the objective value only shifts locally) classes of dense
instances [17], and Giotis and Guruswami gave a PTAS for correlation clustering with a fixed
number of clusters [13]. Guruswami and Sinop [15] presented a unified approximation scheme for
optimization problems that are formulated as specific quadratic integer programs with positive
semidefinite objectives and linear constraints. Specifically, they gave a

One of the most powerful tools for designing approximation algorithms for dense graphs is
the regularity lemma. Szemerédi’s regularity lemma is a fundamental result in graph theory that
states that every suﬀiciently large graph can be approximated by a union of a bounded number
of random-like bipartite graphs called regular pairs. Building on this perspective, Frieze and
Kannan [10] presented an algorithmic version of the regularity lemma and gave a constant-time
approximation scheme. Subsequently, many researchers have improved the error bounds of the
partition [25], and a deterministic version of the regularity lemma was presented [8].
In this paper, we focus on graph cut problems, including those with global constraints and
those minimizing cut ratios. We obtain an EPTAS for ConstrainedMinCut on the so-called
everywhere-δ-dense graphs by using the weak regularity lemma [10, 8] and sampling and esti-
mation techniques, while avoiding heavier tools such as the Lasserre hierarchy used in previous
PTAS results. Moreover, we obtain EPTASs for MinQuotientCut and ProductSparsest-
Cut through a unified reduction from the EPTAS for ConstrainedMinCut.
More details are given below.

Hardness:
Unlike problems such as MaxCut, for ConstrainedMinCut, the assumption
that the input graph is everywhere-δ-dense cannot be weakened to the condition |E| ≥Ω(n2),
often used to define so-called dense graphs. To see this, we first observe that by an argument
similar to that in [2], if there exists a PTAS for BalancedSeparator on dense graphs, then
there exists a PTAS for BalancedSeparator on general graphs, which clearly contradicts the
known hardness result.
Given any (not necessarily dense) instance of BalancedSeparator (G = (V, E), t(=
Θ(n)), ζ(= Θ(1))) with n vertices, we construct an instance G′ for BalancedSeparator
with |E(G′)| ≥Ω(|V (G′)|2) and show that we can recover a (1 + ε)-approximate solution to
the original BalancedSeparator instance from a nearly optimal solution to the constructed
instance.
We add a clique K with t(1+ζ)+n vertices to G, and let G′ denote the resulting graph, which
is dense. We show that if we run the PTAS on (G′, t, ζ), we can recover a (1 + ε)-approximate
solution to the original BalancedSeparator instance. Let S be a feasible solution to the
constructed instance, where X = S ∩V and Y = S ∩K.
Now, our goal is to show that we can recover a cut T from S such that |EG(T, T)| ≤
|EG′(S, S)| holds. The cut size of S is
|EG′(S, S)| = |EG(X, X)| + |Y |(|K| −|Y |).

Comparison with prior PTASs:
Our algorithm builds on the same sampling and estimation
paradigm as prior PTASs for dense graph problems. Most prior PTASs for dense CSPs [6, 5, 13]
treat unconstrained problems, and a few [17] handle hard local constraints, such as those in the
MultiwayCut problem. On the other hand, BalancedSeparator and SmallSetExpan-
sion require global interval cardinality or volume constraints, and hence these problems involve
non-fragile global constraints. Therefore, these previous works do not provide PTASs for prob-
lems that require non-fragile global constraints. We give more details in the next subsection.
The most relevant prior work is the PTAS for BalancedSeparator on dense graphs
due to Arora, Karger, and Karpinski [2], which runs in nO(1/ε3) time and is therefore not an
EPTAS. Our results improve upon this in two directions: we obtain an EPTAS running in
f(1/ε)nO(1) time, and we extend the framework to the more general ConstrainedMinCut
problem, which captures both BalancedSeparator and SmallSetExpansion.
A naive
sampling and estimation framework does not produce solutions satisfying the global constraint,
and we introduce an additional rebalancing step that adjusts the cut to satisfy the constraint
without significantly increasing the cut size.
1.5
Technical Contributions
As mentioned above, we leverage weak regularity and specialized sampling and rebalancing
procedures for cost-constrained problems. To this end, technically speaking, our main tool is to
generalize the EPRAS for the MinBisection problem in everywhere-δ-dense graphs with small
optimal value [2] to an EPRAS for the ConstrainedMinCut problem in the same setting.
For the MinBisection problem, there is a strong structural characterization of the optimal
solution, and this characterization is used to design the EPRAS.
Lemma 1.17 (Lemma 5.1 in [2]). Let (L, R) be an optimal cut for the MinBisection problem.
Then at least one of L and R has the property that every vertex on this side has more than half
of its neighbors on the same side.
Unfortunately, such a strong structural characterization does not extend to the Constrained-
MinCut problem because of the cost constraint. Indeed, this problem requires an interval cost
constraint (this is also the case for BalancedSeparator and SmallSetExpansion). If, for
example, we apply the independent sampling framework in [17] to the MinBisection problem,
we may miss the exact bisection threshold by O(εn) vertices. We may blindly try to “fix” the

the large-optimal-value case on everywhere-δ-dense graphs by using the algorithmic regularity
lemma. Combining the result in [2] for the small-optimal-value case, they obtained an EPRAS
for the BalancedSeparator problem for everywhere-δ-dense graphs.
In this section, we
present an EPRAS for the ConstrainedMinCut problem on everywhere-δ-dense graphs by
extending their technique. We denote the optimal cut by (LOPT, ROPT) and its size by OPT :=
|E(LOPT, ROPT)|. We divide the algorithm into two cases, based on the size of OPT.
Theorem 3.1. There exists an EPRAS for the ConstrainedMinCut problem on dense in-
stances, and its running time is nO(1) + 2 ˜O(1/(ζ2ε4)).
Theorem 3.2. There exists an EPTAS for the ConstrainedMinCut problem on dense in-

The difference in runtime between Theorem 3.1 and Theorem 3.2 is derived from the width

The most important difference from the BalancedSeparator problem is the existence of

3.1
Large OPT Case for ConstrainedMinCut Problem
If n ≤N(1/ε), the problem can be solved exactly by brute force in Oε(1) time. Thus, in what
follows, we assume that n is suﬀiciently large.
We apply the weak regularity lemma to handle the case in which the optimal cut size
OPT ≥αn2 for the ConstrainedMinCut problem (we refer to this as the “Large OPT”
case).
Our main result for this case is as follows:
Theorem 3.3. For dense instances of ConstrainedMinCut with optimal cut size OPT ≥
αn2, we can find a cut E(L, L) such that c(L) ∈[ρ(1 −ζ), ρ(1 + ζ)] and its cut size is at most
(1 + ε)OPT in time 2 ˜O(1/(αε)2) + nO(1) with probability 9/10.
Frieze and Kannan [10] applied the weak regularity lemma to obtain additive-error approxi-
mation algorithms for many graph problems, including MaxCut and UniformSparsestCut.
In this subsection, building on their framework, we impose a cost constraint to derive an algo-
rithm for the ConstrainedMinCut problem and prove Theorem 3.3.
The algorithm consists of the following four steps:
1. Compute a cut decomposition.
2. Check feasibility via an LP.
3. Round down the fractional solution.
4. Move vertices to satisfy the cost constraint.
Following the algorithm above, we first obtain a cut decomposition with constant width and
small error, according to the given precision parameter ε, by applying the weak regularity lemma
(Theorem 2.4) to the adjacency matrix of the given dense graph. Then, for each part of the
cut decomposition, we discretize its possible contribution to the cut size. For each assignment,
we check the feasibility of the assignment by solving a linear program. Finally, we round the
fractional solution of the LP to obtain a feasible cut satisfying the cost constraint. If we take the

Despite being everywhere-δ-dense, the graph admits an optimal cut of small size. Hence, in
the optimal cut (L, R), it is possible that only a few edges cross between L and R, and most
of the edges are internal to L or to R. In Algorithm 2, we first sample a set S with |S| =
Θ(log n) uniformly at random. Since the graph is everywhere-δ-dense, most of the vertices v

L or R by comparing the fraction of its neighbors in S that lie in either L or R. However, due
to the cost constraint, some vertices may be misclassified, and hence the resulting partition may
violate the cost constraint. Since the optimal cut size is small, the number of such vertices must
be small. Therefore, we can adjust the cut to satisfy the cost constraint without increasing the
cut size by much.
