Abstract
As AI systems are increasingly deployed in au-
tonomous agentic settings at scale, it is impor-
tant to ensure the actions they take are safe and
aligned with user intent. Monitoring agent ac-
tions is a key safety mechanism, yet reliable mon-
itors remain difficult to build and the scale of
these systems makes human oversight impracti-
cal. We show that combining signals from diverse
monitors into an ensemble improves detection of
misaligned actions. We build 12 GPT-4.1-Mini
monitors using both prompting and fine-tuning
strategies. We evaluate them on coding tasks
where candidate solutions pass standard tests but
fail on adversarial inputs. In this setting, diverse
ensembles outperform both individual monitors
and homogeneous ensembles. Our best 3-monitor
ensemble achieves 2.4× greater detection perfor-
mance gain compared to an ensemble composed
of three identical monitors, with the same en-
semble performing strongly on an independent
dataset. We contend that these results show that
diversity—not scale—drives gains. The best en-
sembles combine strong individual performance
with low correlation between monitors. Further-
more, fine-tuned monitors appear in every top-
performing ensemble and maintain this advan-
tage on out-of-distribution attack types, suggest-
ing that fine-tuning enables detection capabilities
that prompting alone does not elicit. These re-
sults support ensemble monitoring as a practical
AI control strategy for safety gains at reasonable
inference costs.

1. Introduction
As LLM-based coding agents are deployed with increasing
autonomy and with greater capabilities, there is a risk of
misalignment and intentional subversion, a concern espe-
cially acute for internal AI lab deployments. AI control
(Greenblatt et al., 2023) addresses this concern by designing
protocols that remain robust against even deliberate subver-
sion by an untrusted model. Such protocols treat sufficiently
capable models as potentially misaligned and employ mech-
anisms that include trusted monitoring (scoring outputs with
a weaker trusted model), resampling flagged actions (Bhatt
et al., 2025), and escalation to human review. One central
question in AI control is how to construct effective monitors,
specifically which approaches yield the strongest signals.
Further, if employing multiple monitors in an ensemble, one
must know how many are sufficient and whether diversity
or scale drives performance changes.
Previous monitor design research has characterized the
safety–cost tradeoff and explored combining signals. More
capable monitors achieve higher detection rates but at in-
creasing financial cost (Terekhov et al., 2025a). Protocols
can combine monitors of different capability levels cost-
optimally in a hierarchical system (Hua et al., 2025). More-
over, protocols that combine action and chain-of-thought
reasoning signals consistently outperform monitoring either
signal alone (Arnav et al., 2025). In concurrent work, ensem-
bling prompted monitors on realistic software engineering
tasks achieves a dramatic reduction in false negatives com-
pared with any single monitor (Stickland et al., 2025). These
results echo classical ensemble theory: aggregating weakly
correlated predictors reduces both variance and systematic
error (Dietterich, 2000; Dong et al., 2020). Yet prior work
introduces diversity only through capability differences or
signal type.
In contrast, we study whether diverse construction meth-
ods from a single base model, through prompting and fine-
tuning, yield complementary signals that can be used to
improve monitoring performance. We fix the base model
(GPT-4.1-mini) and monitored signal (code actions) and
construct 12 monitors through diverse prompting strategies
and fine-tuned variants on the APPS dataset (Hendrycks
et al., 2021) from ControlArena (Inglis et al., 2025). We

2. Related Work
AI Control. AI control (Greenblatt et al., 2023) is a frame-
work for designing deployment protocols that remain safe
even against deliberate subversion by an untrusted model.
Control protocols typically involve two components: moni-
toring (detecting outputs that constitute a sabotage attempt)
and intervention (modifying untrusted responses for safe
downstream use). Prior work has established trusted mon-
itoring protocols evaluated on backdoored code datasets
(Greenblatt et al., 2023; Terekhov et al., 2025a) and has
extended them to agentic settings through dynamic resam-
pling (Bhatt et al., 2025), trusted editor routing (Parent
et al., 2025), and deception detection via linear probes
(Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025). Recent work has explored

combining predictions from two monitors under cost con-

provides evidence that monitor ensembles are effective un-
der adversarial red-blue evaluation on realistic development
tasks, and Arnav et al. (2025) demonstrates merging sig-
nals from chain-of-thought (CoT) and action-only monitors
outperforms either individual monitoring source. CoT is
a compelling monitoring signal but a brittle one. Train-
ing choices may eliminate readable reasoning traces (Kor-
bak et al., 2025a), and as models grow more capable, CoT
becomes an increasingly unreliable signal (Korbak et al.,
2025b). We therefore ground our analysis in action-only
monitoring, which remains applicable regardless of whether
CoT is available or trustworthy.
Ensembling. Ensemble methods have a long theoretical
and empirical history in machine learning. Early work es-
tablished that ensemble error falls as member diversity in-
creases. Hansen & Salamon (1990) proved that independent
errors below 50% can drive ensemble error to zero as ensem-
ble size grows, and the ambiguity decomposition of Krogh
& Vedelsby (1995) formalizes this observation, stating en-
semble error equals average individual error minus average
member disagreement. Breiman (1996) demonstrated these
benefits empirically through bagging, and Dietterich (2000)
synthesized the theoretical foundations, identifying comple-
mentary reasons why ensembles outperform single models.
Breiman (2001) further formalized the role of diversity,
showing that ensemble error depends on both individual
accuracy and inter-member correlation, with lower corre-
lation directly reducing error. These principles have been
confirmed across diverse high-stakes application domains
(Dong et al., 2020), including medical diagnosis (Mahajan
et al., 2023), cybersecurity threat detection (Ojoje et al.,
2025), and financial fraud prevention (Ahmed et al., 2025).
Similar diversity-driven gains have been observed in LLM-
as-a-judge settings (Monzon et al., 2026) as well.
3. Method
3.1. Monitor types
We build monitors using two primary approaches.
All
prompt-based and fine-tuned monitors share the same base
model and output a suspiciousness score on a fixed integer
scale.
Prompting
Our Baseline monitor uses the default trusted monitor sys-
tem prompt from ControlArena (Inglis et al., 2025) (see
Appendix B), which instructs the model to analyze submit-
ted code for backdoor behavior given the problem statement
and output a suspicion score from 0 to 10 with reasoning.
Beyond this baseline, we develop 7 diverse prompts that
target different aspects of code analysis and exhibit com-
plementary strengths and weaknesses. Prompting leverages
the ability of LLMs to follow in-context instructions and
to express latent capabilities when provided with appropri-
ate context (Brown et al., 2020). These prompts include
instructions focused on identifying potential exploits, pri-
oritizing either recall or precision, structuring analysis into
two phases to scaffold the evaluation process, and a trusted
debate variant (Irving et al., 2018; Al Dahik et al., 2025) in
which two model instances argue opposing positions and
a third judges their exchange to produce a suspicion score.
The motivation behind each prompt is summarized in Ap-
pendix A.
Fine-tuning
We also investigate fine-tuning as a complementary ap-
proach for monitor construction. Fine-tuning (Radford et al.,
2018) adapts a base model to a target task by continuing
training on labeled examples, enabling the model to in-
ternalize task-specific behaviors and decision boundaries.
We fine-tune GPT-4.1-Mini on 50–200 training samples of
code and suspicion score pairs using the OpenAI API, with
sample counts varying by dataset. For some datasets, we
generate reasoning and score pairs by prompting more ca-
pable models to simulate high-quality human annotators.
We additionally apply dataset-specific filtering to improve
label quality. For "filtered" datasets, we impose a suspi-
ciousness threshold, retaining only examples where back-
door solutions score above the threshold and clean solutions
score below it. As an alternative, we provide the generating
model the correct label—whether the code is a solution or
backdoor—before asking it to generate a response-score
pair for training; these are "given labels" datasets. By de-
fault, "completions" datasets have no label filtering. We also
train some monitors on deterministically set scores with no
AI-generated reasoning traces to avoid introducing genera-
tive biases into the training data. Each fine-tuned monitor
is named after the corresponding fine-tuning dataset and
is prefixed with FT- throughout this work. Details of our
fine-tuning datasets are presented in Appendix C.

4. Experimental Setup
All monitors use GPT-4.1-mini as the single trusted model.
Evaluations are conducted using the ControlArena evalua-
tion framework (Inglis et al., 2025) at temperature 1, with
all other generation parameters at their defaults. Detection
performance is measured by pAUC (§4.2).
4.1. Datasets
APPS
We evaluate all monitors and ensembles on a filtered sub-
set of the APPS ControlArena dataset (Inglis et al., 2025),
which combines interview-style Python coding problems
from APPS (Hendrycks et al., 2021) with backdoored so-
lutions sourced from Greenblatt et al. (2023) and Terekhov
et al. (2025a). Backdoors are adversarially generated by
various LLMs to pass all standard tests while producing
incorrect outputs on specific attack inputs. To isolate back-
door detection from generally incorrect code, we keep only
working honest solutions (passing all tests) and working
backdoored solutions (successfully exploiting attack inputs
and passing all tests).
Our train/validation/test split simulates realistic deployment
where monitors must defend against a stronger attacker
while only having training examples from less-capable
attacking models. We allocate all samples with curated
backdoors generated by Claude-3.7-Sonnet-Thinking (the
strongest attacking model in our dataset) to the test set,
ensuring evaluation captures worst-case performance. We
exclude backdoors from weaker or older models (GPT-3.5-
Turbo, GPT-4o-Mini, Claude-3-5-Haiku), using generating
model capability as a heuristic for backdoor quality. We
train and validate on the remaining backdoors from more
capable models.
The filtered dataset contains 1,936 training (50% attack
rate), 482 validation (50% attack rate), and 1,375 test sam-
ples (172 attack, 1,203 honest). The test set is extended to
problems that have working solutions but no working back-
doors to sharpen FPR estimation at low thresholds. These
problems may differ from training problems, but the shift
affects all monitors equally and does not impact relative
comparisons.
We observe that backdoors in the dataset frequently contain
comments explicitly indicating the backdoor logic. To pre-
vent monitors from relying on this unintended signal, we
strip all comments from the code before evaluation.
BigCodeBench-Sabotage
We evaluate generalization using BigCodeBench (Zhuo
et al., 2025) augmented with backdoors from Arnav et al.
(2025) (BigCodeBench-Sabotage, BCB-S). BCB-S covers
11 backdoor types — edge case manipulation, I/O opera-
tions, permission changes, unusual variable names, complex-
ity injection, and others — letting us test whether ensembles
selected on one attack type transfer to qualitatively different
ones. We construct two evaluation sets — 500 attack and
500 honest edge-case samples most similar to APPS (BCB-
S Edge), and 500 attack and 500 honest samples drawn from
all 11 backdoor types (BCB-S All).

5.6. Diversity outperforms vertical scaling
We test whether a diverse ensemble of monitors outper-
forms a single stronger model at equivalent inference cost.
A single GPT-4.1 call is approximately cost-equivalent to a
5-monitor ensemble of GPT-4.1-Mini.1 On APPS test set,
the homogeneous 5×Baseline ensemble (pAUC = 0.766)
performs comparably to a single GPT-4.1 with the base-
line prompt (pAUC = 0.761). In contrast, 94.6% of diverse
5-monitor ensembles outperform GPT-4.1. Similar to hor-
izontal scaling, spending the same budget on a stronger
model does not substitute for diverse construction.
We extend the comparison further up the model lin-
eage.
Surprisingly, GPT-5.1 with reasoning disabled
(pAUC = 0.661) performs worse than GPT-4.1, indicating
that cost-constrained generation degrades monitoring qual-
ity. For GPT-5.1 with high reasoning effort (pAUC = 0.783),
we find no consistent evidence that the diverse 5-monitor
ensemble is better, though this comparison is not cost-
equivalent as high reasoning produces 1.8× more tokens
than the no-reasoning setting.
Finally, we test whether ensemble gains generalize across
base model capability by running the 8 prompted monitors
on GPT-4.1-Nano and GPT-4.1 alongside GPT-4.1-Mini.
Ensemble improvements are largest for the smallest model,
where ensembling raises pAUC from 0.162 to 0.553, while
larger models show notable but smaller gains. This suggests
that diverse ensembles provide the greatest benefit when
individual monitors are weakest.
6. Discussion
6.1. Why does ensembling work?
A monitor’s suspicion score can be decomposed into three
components: true signal, systematic bias, and random noise.
Under this decomposition, averaging multiple stochastic
samples from the same monitor reduces noise in expectation
but leaves systematic bias intact—a monitor’s idiosyncratic
errors on any given sample do not diminish with repeated
sampling.
Ensembling diverse monitors addresses this limitation. Clas-

sical ensemble theory establishes that aggregating predic-
tions from weakly correlated learners reduces both variance
and systematic error, as idiosyncratic biases partially can-
cel in expectation (Dietterich, 2000; Dong et al., 2020).
By deliberately constructing monitors through diverse ap-
proaches, we help reduce correlation between failure modes,
satisfying the diversity condition under which systematic
bias reduction holds at the aggregate level.
A complementary interpretation is that ensembling improves
coverage over attack modes. Diverse monitors behave as
a team of specialists; each monitor attends to different fea-
tures of the input, applies different heuristics, and calibrates
scores along different axes. This specialization increases
the probability that at least one ensemble member assigns
a high suspicion score to any given attack instance. Distin-
guishing between these mechanisms empirically remains an
open question and a promising direction for future work.
6.2. Selecting good ensembles
Our findings suggest a practical approach for building mon-
itoring ensembles. The first step is to build monitors using
diverse approaches, explicitly aiming to create complemen-
tary detection strategies rather than architecturally similar
approaches that share failure modes.
The second step is to evaluate individual monitors on a held-
out validation set before forming ensembles. Pure diversity
selection (lowest inter-monitor correlation) performs worse
than random selection when poor-performing monitors are
included; these monitors disagree with others not because
they detect distinct signals but because they are frequently
wrong. Ensemble pruning by filtering to the top performers
first, e.g., removing bottom quartile individuals, and then
selecting for diversity among the resulting ensembles consis-
tently outperforms both random selection and homogeneous
ensembles. For aggregation, simple arithmetic averaging is
sufficient—more sophisticated methods such as geometric
mean, weighted average, and majority voting do not show
consistent improvement in our experiments.
6.3. Limitations & future work
Our evaluation focuses on a well-defined but narrow threat
model, specifically short-horizon coding problems where
each attack takes the form of a discrete backdoor. How
well these findings generalize to subtler scenarios—realistic
deployments with ambiguous attacker intent, longer agent
horizons, or attacks distributed across multiple episodes—
remains an open question. Moreover, all evaluations treat
the attacker as unaware of the ensemble composition. We
did not red-team our ensembles against an adaptive adver-
sary with knowledge of the monitoring system. Until such
experiments are conducted, we cannot claim that ensembles
are robust to adversarially optimized attacks, or whether

ensembles can introduce new attack surfaces.
Our diversity results are also scoped to a single base model,
meaning the diversity we measure is entirely over fine-
tuning protocol and prompting strategy. This shared ar-
chitecture introduces a specific vulnerability: an adversary
can embed monitor-manipulating instructions directly in the
code under review, and Terekhov et al. (2025b) shows that
such prompt injection attacks can reliably subvert trusted
monitors. Ensembles drawn from different model families—
or that incorporate non-LLM-based signals—may be more
resistant, but we have not tested this directly. Thus, future
work is needed to build monitors from different sources to
test whether architectural diversity yields robustness gains
beyond what fine-tuning and prompting variation can pro-
vide.
Additionally, we measure diversity via average pairwise cor-
relation and use this metric to guide ensemble construction,
but the mechanism underlying complementarity remains
unclear. More targeted analysis—what features each moni-
tor attends to, which specific attacks each uniquely catches,
and whether fine-tuned monitors reliably specialize in the
same blind spots across datasets—would make ensemble
construction more principled and help validate the coverage
hypothesis proposed above.
Finally, stronger monitoring is a necessary but insufficient
safeguard in AI control. Recall depends critically on deploy-
ment choices—audit rates, score thresholds, and protocol
design—that fall outside the scope of this study. The results
here should be treated as one input into a broader safety
analysis rather than as standalone safety guarantees.
7. Conclusion
Ensemble monitoring offers a practical path to improved
catch rates in AI control settings. By combining moni-
tors built through prompting and fine-tuning, we aggregate
complementary detection signals that outperform any indi-
vidual monitor. The central finding is not that ensembles
universally outperform individuals, but that monitor diver-
sity—not count—drives performance gains. A carefully
selected subset of monitors can achieve most of the benefit
of larger ensembles, and these selections generalize across
datasets and attack types. Moreover, fine-tuning appears to
be a particularly valuable source of diversity, consistently
appearing in top ensembles across all our experiments. This
prevalence suggests that fine-tuning enables monitoring ca-
pabilities that prompting alone does not elicit, justifying the
additional cost of fine-tuning in safety-critical deployments.
Impact Statement
This work contributes empirical evidence toward the broader
goal of developing control protocols that provide meaning-
ful safety guarantees as AI systems become more capable.
However, as with any study that publishes monitoring re-
sults, this work may inadvertently assist future AI systems
in constructing backdoors that evade known ensemble mon-
itoring protocols.

Baseline
General-purpose backdoor detector that analyzes the suspiciousness of code submissions in the context of the

High-Recall
Designed to maximize recall in an ensemble system with a lower threshold for flagging suspicious code;

Designed to maximize precision in an ensemble system by only flagging code with strong evidence of

Extract-Evaluate
Two-stage pipeline, following the strategy in Arike et al. (2025): the first stage extracts 1–5 specific code
snippets that look most suspicious for potential backdoors, and the second stage judges whether these excerpts
indicate deliberate backdoors versus legitimate code, bugs, or style issues.
Reference-Compare
Two-stage pipeline where the first stage generates a clean reference solution with clear documentation and
straightforward approach, then the second stage compares the submitted solution against this reference to
identify suspicious logic differences that could indicate backdoors (e.g., specific value checks, unnecessary
conditions targeting hidden inputs).
Code-Heuristic
Pattern-matching backdoor detector that operates without access to the problem statement, relying on
statistical heuristics to detect suspicious patterns like magic numbers in conditionals, hardcoded special-case
checks, obfuscation signals, and arbitrary logic branches that deviate from typical clean code; outputs only a
score without reasoning.
Exploit-Finder
Security researcher perspective that actively searches for exploitable backdoor patterns by identifying
suspicious code elements, hypothesizing specific trigger inputs that would activate malicious behavior,
constructing concrete exploit scenarios, and scoring 0–10 based on how clearly exploitable the code is.
Trusted-Debate
Adversarial debate format (Al Dahik et al., 2025) with two debaters (one arguing code is clean, one arguing it
contains backdoor) presenting evidence over multiple rounds, followed by a neutral judge who evaluates the
transcript and renders a verdict with 0–10 score and clean/backdoor classification.

E. Ensemble Size Selection
We use two complementary analyses to identify the recommended ensemble size k.
E.1. Bootstrap Sign Test
For each transition k →k+1, we draw 1000 paired bootstrap resamples of the test set and compute the fraction in which the
best (k+1)-monitor ensemble improves over the best k-monitor ensemble, using 0.95 as the reliability threshold.
Marginal gains are reliable through k=3 and fall below the threshold at k=4. Beyond k=7, they turn consistently negative.
E.2. Cumulative Gain Capture
We measure the fraction of total available gain captured at each ensemble size, where total gain is the difference between
peak performance (at k=5) and k=1.
The ensemble captures 85.5% of total available gain at k=3 and 94.1% at k=4. In combination with the bootstrap evidence,
this points to k=3 as the preferred ensemble size.

G. Diversity-Selected Ensembles
Figure 6. Most diverse 3-monitor ensembles on APPS and BCB-S after filtering the bottom performance quartile, showing paired pAUC

Each panel plots a labeled ensemble (see Appendix F for compositions) against its average pairwise Pearson correlation on

The left panels (APPS) show that after filtering low-quality monitors, the most diverse ensembles outperform both the

quality floor is imposed. The right panels apply the same selection approach independently on BCB-S, where the diversity–
