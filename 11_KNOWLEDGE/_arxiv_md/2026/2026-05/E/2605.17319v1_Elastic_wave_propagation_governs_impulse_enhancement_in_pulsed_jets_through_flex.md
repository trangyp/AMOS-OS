Abstract
Inspired by cephalopod jet propulsion through compliant funnels, this study investigates elastic
wave propagation and energy exchange in passively deforming cylindrical nozzles through three-
dimensional, two-way fluid–structure interaction simulations. Flexible nozzles with varying stiffness
(𝐸ℎ= 75 −500 N m−1, where 𝐸and ℎare Young’s modulus and nozzle thickness, respectively)
are subjected to a pulsatile jet inflow at 𝑅𝑒∼4000. Increasing nozzle flexibility reduces the
deformation-wave speed in accordance with Moens–Korteweg scaling, thereby prolonging the nozzle
expansion phase. This delayed expansion enhances jet entrainment and elastic energy storage while
suppressing early shear-layer roll-up and vortex formation. During contraction, the stored elastic
energy is released, thereby enhancing jet acceleration and vortex formation. For the most flexible
nozzle, the primary vortex-ring circulation increases by 52.13%, the vortex convection distance by
9.00%, and the peak outlet kinetic energy flux by a factor of 4.62 compared with a rigid nozzle. These
effects collectively yield a 61.92% increase in total hydrodynamic impulse. These findings identify
passive wave-speed tuning via nozzle compliance as a mechanism to enhance pulsed-jet thrust for
bio-inspired underwater propulsion.
Keywords fluid-structure interaction · flexible nozzles · jet propulsion · elastic wave propagation.

The pulsatile flows ejected from nozzles are dominated by the formation and evolution of vortex rings, which provide
thrust through the combined effects of fluid momentum and pressure impulse at the nozzle or body exit (Krueger
& Gharib, 2003). In rigid nozzles, the generated impulse, composed of jet momentum and nozzle exit pressure
contribution, depends on the jet generator (e.g., piston and impeller) and the nozzle geometry. However, in a flexible
nozzle, coupled fluid–structure interaction (FSI) with the unsteady jet significantly affects the generated impulse and
propulsive efficiency. The efficiency of such pulsed-jet propulsion has been linked to the ability of the compliant
structure to interact dynamically with the surrounding fluid, a feature common in biological systems where flexibility
and resonant deformation augment vortex formation and energy recovery (Dabiri, 2009; Wang et al., 2022). Recently,
Mitchell & Morris (2025) experimentally demonstrated that flexible nozzles can generate multiple vortex rings via
nozzle oscillations, with the pulsed jet generator tuned to specific formation lengths.
When subjected to a pulsed jet, the flexible nozzle expands due to the positive pressure generated as the flow within it
accelerates, producing an expansion wave that propagates along the nozzle. The wave bounces back when it reaches
the nozzle exit, from which nozzle contraction begins. The time scale for these nozzle deformation phases (the time
required for the expansion and contraction waves to travel) is determined by the wave speed (correlated with nozzle
flexibility) and the nozzle length. Choi & Park (2022) suggested that for a continuous jet input, the jet impulse is
maximised when this time scale is tuned with the jet acceleration time, for maximising the jet velocity. In a subsequent
study, Choi & Park (2024) confirmed a similar effect with a single pulsed jet input condition. However, the relationship
among wave propagation, vortex dynamics, and the subsequent energy transfer mechanism remains to be established.
Building on the work of Choi & Park (2024), this study utilises numerical simulations to quantify the wave propagation
and reflection, study its influence on the nozzle vortex dynamics and quantify the energy transfer mechanism (from
nozzle elastic potential energy to jet kinetic energy) that leads to thrust enhancement from the flexible nozzles. To
study this energy transfer mechanism, this study uses a simplified model of a flexible cylindrical nozzle that undergoes
expansion and contraction due to jet acceleration and deceleration, respectively, thereby generating thrust. These
insights could then be potentially used to enhance the propulsive performance of underwater vehicles.

The influence of flexibility on impulse generation is quantified in figures 4(a, b, c). Figure 4(a) shows the momentum

The evolution of ˆ𝐼𝑚is consistent with the effect of wave propagation on vortex circulation. During the initial expansion

contracts, the entrained fluid is rapidly expelled, resulting in a substantial increase in the outlet momentum. This effect
becomes increasingly pronounced with decreasing stiffness, and for the 𝐸ℎ= 75 N m−1 nozzle, the peak momentum
