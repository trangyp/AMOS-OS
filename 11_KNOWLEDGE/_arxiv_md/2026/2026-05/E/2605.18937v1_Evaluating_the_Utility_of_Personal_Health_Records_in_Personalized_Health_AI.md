Access to Personal Health Records (PHR) promises to empower patients to better understand their health;
however the information in PHR is complex, hindering patients’ understanding and the derivation of useful
insights. In this study, we assess the potential of large language models (LLMs, Gemini 3.0 Flash in this
work) to provide helpful answers to user queries, when provided PHR context. A total of 2,255 user queries
were drawn from 3 different distributions: relatively short web search queries, longer questions derived from
templates of questions for chatbots, and questions patients asked to their healthcare team (‘patient calls’). The
queries were selected or synthesized based on relevance to a pool of 1,945 de-identified PHRs, and Gemini
responses were generated (1) without PHR context, (2) with a “basic” summary of demographics, conditions,
and medications, and (3) with “full”, extensive clinical notes. For evaluation, we leveraged the SHARP (Safety,
Helpfulness, Accuracy, Relevance, Personalization) framework, and developed a new framework to identify
specific potential error modes for interpreting PHRs. Evaluation was performed using autoraters for the full set,
and with clinician ratings for a subset (n=65), with both sets of raters knowing the full PHR context. Overall,
we see significant improvements in the helpfulness of answers to all question types when including PHR, with
more complex queries (chatbot templates and patient calls) deriving more benefit (p < 0.001, paired t-test). We
also observe potential gains in safety, accuracy, relevance and personalization of answers. Our PHR evaluation
framework further identifies gaps in LLM understanding of particular aspects of complex PHRs, such as temporal
disorientation, rare but meaningful confabulations, and considerations of healthcare access. Answers with full
PHRs as context tended to be better than those using basic abridged PHRs. These results suggest potential for
PHR to help people with a wide range of user needs; and provide a framework for monitoring for gaps in LLM
answers based on PHR context.

1. Introduction
10
Consumer access to personal health records (PHRs) has enabled people to review and track health data.
11
This technology promises to empower patients with their information, enabling them to obtain personalized
12
insights and better engage with their health. Many patient portals also facilitate secure messaging with
13
health care teams and services such as medication refills (Graetz et al., 2016). Although access to PHR has
14
accelerated (Ford et al., 2016), recent reviews suggest that PHR engagement has been lower than initially
15
expected (Abernethy et al., 2022; Alsyouf et al., 2023; Showell, 2017), possibly due to perceived low ease of
16
use and utility deriving from the complexity of the medical information in PHRs (Neves et al., 2021).
17
Meanwhile, use of large language models (LLMs) for answering health questions has increased sharply (Ayre
18
et al., 2025; Costa-Gomes et al., 2026; Montero et al., 2026; Pasquini et al., 2026a,b; Yun and Bickmore,
19
2025a,b). Recent surveys have indicated around one third of US-based adults have used LLMs to answer
20
their health questions in the past year, with 41% of these respondents also reporting that they have uploaded
21
personal medical information into an LLM chatbot (Montero et al., 2026). Users have reported that the
22
ability to engage in natural language conversation about health topics increases perceived ease of use and
23
trust (Yun and Bickmore, 2025a), although health information from LLMs is viewed as less personalized
24
compared to other sources (Pasquini et al., 2026a,b). Recent surveys indicate 22% of adults in the US

2.2. Additional gains on other SHARP metrics
97
In addition to overall helpfulness, answers with PHR were also more highly rated on several other axes from
98
the SHARP framework (Fig. 2). This framework defines 3 axes related to helpfulness: overall helpfulness,
99
actionability (“Do the model outputs provide information able to be followed, with clear guidance and
100
next steps?”) and motivation (“Does the model output encourage engagement, action, or a shift in user
101
perspective?”). For each of these axes, ratings from both clinicians and autoraters showed significant increases
102
for answers with PHR context. For clinician ratings, actionability increased from a mean score of 0.61 with
103
No PHR to 0.85 with Basic PHR (p < 0.001) and 0.84 with Full PHR (p = 0.001); while motivation increased
104
from 0.47 for No PHR to 0.62 for Basic PHR (p < 0.001) and 0.62 for Full PHR (p < 0.001). Autorater
105
ratings showed a similar increase, with actionability scores increasing from 0.26 No PHR to 0.97 Basic PHR
106
and 1.00 Full PHR on this dataset. Motivation increased from 0.58 No PHR to 0.88 Basic PHR and 0.95 Full
107
PHR (all p < 0.001).
108
Beyond helpfulness, the SHARP framework also specifies axes related to safety, accuracy, relevance, and
109
personalization. On these axes, we observed some benefits of PHR for answers, but the effects were not as
110
consistent. Clinician ratings were not significantly different between No PHR, Basic PHR, and Full PHR
111
answers on the clinician evaluation subset for these axes (Fig. 2a, Table 8). This is in part because for several
112
of these axes, baseline ratings are already high. For instance, for the medical / scientific consensus axis
113
(“For the information provided, how does it relate to the current consensus of the scientific and/or medical
114
community?”), clinicians rated a mean score of 0.90, meaning most responses were rated as being fully
115
supported by existing medical consensus. By comparison, values for Basic PHR were 0.94 and Full PHR were
116
0.91. However, autorater results on the full data set showed significant differences, even though the values
117
across conditions were similarly high (0.98 for No PHR vs. 1.00 for Basic PHR and Full PHR; p < 0.001),
118
due in part to the large sample size.

2.3. Quantitative analyses of use of PHR in answers
120
We applied our new PHR-specific evaluation framework to answers from the Basic PHR and Full PHR cases.
121
(Since the framework focuses on use of added medical context, it is inapplicable to No PHR answers.) Results
122
of our analyses are summarized in Fig. 3.
123
There was variability in the rate at which different PHR interpretation axes were found to apply to questions.
124
Both clinician raters and the autorater marked the conflict resolution, trend analysis, and social determinants
125
of health axes as being inapplicable to questions at a higher rate. Clinician raters tended to identify
126
abnormality detection as not being relevant to most questions (approximately 60%), whereas the autorater
127
only found it not relevant to 21%. Conversely, clinician raters only identified bias and stigma as not being
128
relevant to 7-10% of questions, compared to 40-46% by the autoraters.
129
Evaluation axes focused on robustness were marked as having issues at a higher rate compared to other
130
axes. Dealing with data gaps, or resolving apparent conflicts in PHR data, were most commonly flagged by
131
clinician raters; abnormality detection and conflict resolution were most commonly flagged by the autorater.
132
For instance, for a question “What are the common signs and symptoms of syphilis?”, the AI response did not
133
flag a coding of prior potential syphilis exposure in the PHR, which should have been used to contextualize
134
the response.
135
While groundedness ratings were overall high, the losses identified on this axis were of special interest, since
136
these may reflect confabulation or hallucination of information not in the PHR. A detailed analysis of this
137
loss pattern is described below under “Loss patterns”. By comparison, answers tended to score more highly
138

2.4. Qualitative feedback from clinical review
140
Thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006) of open-ended rater feedback identified several patterns in AI
141
answers depending on the medical context.
142
In the No PHR conditions, without access to the user’s medical history, clinician raters primarily focused on
143
the model’s inability to tailor advice and its reliance on generalized information.
144
• Lack of Personalization & Context: Raters frequently noted that the model failed to account for the
145
user’s specific medical history, medications, or allergies. They pointed out that the responses were
146
generic and did not (could not) reference any specific user data. For instance, for the question “How
147
can I improve my sleep while managing my insomnia?” from a user with existing medications in their
148
PHR, the response did not raise the possibility of discontinuing medications, which clinician raters
149
assessed as likely relevant for this case.
150
• Missing Clinical Nuance and Red Flags: Because the model lacked context, raters highlighted instances
151
where it missed crucial red flags or underlying risks. For example, it was unable to acknowledge a
152
history of sleep apnea, mention potential cardiac valve abnormalities requiring antibiotics, and or flag
153
significant signs like blood loss that should prompt immediate care.
154
• Generalized but Safe Advice: On the positive side, raters sometimes noted that while the information
155
was not highly tailored, it was purely educational, generalized, and unlikely to cause harm.
156
With information available in the Basic PHR condition, the model’s ability to incorporate past medical history
157
improved significantly, though raters still found areas requiring better clinical reasoning.
158
• Improved Use of User History: Raters frequently praised the model for successfully linking its advice to
159
the user’s specific medical record, such as mentioning past diagnoses (like Polycystic Ovary Syndrome
160
or dermoid cysts), previous vascular issues, and current medications.
161
• Stronger Actionability and Safety Netting: The inclusion of basic data allowed the model to provide
162
more practical advice and safety warnings. Raters noted the model correctly identified increased risks,
163
gave appropriate safety netting advice, and advised urgent medical reviews when necessary

2.5. PHR-specific loss patterns
192
Overall rates of losses (unfavorable or ‘No’ ratings) in our PHR-specific evaluation are presented for each
193
question source in Table 9. Although relatively uncommon, losses on the PHR axes may represent important
194
concerns in answer quality. As a result, we analyzed autorater reasoning traces to identify the most common
195
reasons for PHR-specific losses. Four major themes emerged from the negative ratings from the PHR specific
196
evaluation axes:
197
1. Chronological and Temporal Blindness
198
The largest volume of errors stemmed from the model’s struggle with the chronology of events presented in
199
the PHR. We observed a total of 69 (2.79%) Basic PHR responses, and 162 (5.74%) of Full PHR responses,
200
flagged by autoraters as having time awareness issues. Examples in this category include:
201
• Outdated Information: It treated old, discontinued medications as current and relied on historical
202
vitals or labs instead of the most recent entries.
203
• Timeline Logic Failures: The AI failed to appropriately make inferences when involving time, resulting
204
in errors like miscalculating gestational ages, or labeling events from years ago as "recent." For example,
205
when a user asked about a culture taken in November, the AI used an August abscess diagnosis and
206
Omnicef prescription as if they were the immediate reasons for the culture. The AI failed to acknowledge
207
the significant time gap and the fact that the August antibiotic course would have been completed
208
months before the November query.
209

The next biggest category of loss occurred when the model failed to anchor its responses to the provided
211
PHR.
212
• Groundedness losses could be classified as due to several issues (examples in Supplemental Results):
213
• Data Fabrication: The model might confabulate clinical data, including numerical values for blood
214
pressure and labs, physical symptoms, and entire diagnostic histories. Confabulation of the entire
215

216

217

218

219
• Misattribution: It could take real data but apply it incorrectly—mixing up encounter dates, or confusing
220

221
• Miscalculation: In one example (highlighted in Supplemental results), it correctly retrieved the user’s
222

223
multiplied them to result in a total dosage that was too high.
224
3. Inability to Identify PHR Data Issues
225
The model would sometimes take the provided text at face value, lacking the human intuition required to
226
recognize when the underlying data was incomplete, flawed, or nonsensical before rendering medical advice.
227
• Overlooking Internal Contradictions: It could fail to flag discrepancies within the notes, such as a
228
physical exam that contradicts the reported symptoms, conflicting diagnostic staging, or mathematical
229
errors in vitals (e.g., BMI vs. narrative descriptions of weight).
230
• Missing Data: It sometimes failed to notice when crucial pieces of the clinical situation were miss-
231
ing—such as vital recent lab results, clarifying symptoms, or a patient’s allergy history—before providing
232
recommendations.
233
• Accepting Flawed Data: It processed physiologically impossible data entry errors (e.g., height/weight
234
fluctuations) without questioning them.
235
4. Context Omission and Failure to Integrate Data
236
Though less common, the model sometimes provided generic, "boilerplate" medical advice because it failed
237
to synthesize or acknowledge the actual data present in the PHR.
238
• Demographic & Status Blind Spots: The AI ignored critical patient states, such as being in hospice,
239
pregnant, postpartum, or pediatric.
240
• Ignoring Medical History: It missed major surgeries, congenital defects, or active prescriptions, resulting
241
in clinically disconnected reasoning.
242
• Social Determinants of Health (SDOH): It failed to account for documented financial barriers, food
243
insecurity, or homelessness when making care or diet recommendations. For example, in one answer
244
the model recommended physical therapy as a primary next step to a patient who has explicitly stated
245
that she found it helpful but discontinued it because it ’got expensive’ without addressing this barrier
246
or acknowledging her switch to home therapy shows a lack of SDOH integration.

2.6. Remediation potential for losses
248
Although infrequent, losses like some of those described above could pose a significant safety risk, such as
249
from groundedness errors, including confabulation / hallucination. We implemented a simple self-critique
250
loop in which responses generated with PHR context (Basic PHR and Full PHR conditions) are fed to our
251

252
is re-evaluated. This simple intervention dramatically reduced the rate of nearly all PHR-related losses
253
(Table 10), with a 98% reduction in the largest category of losses, namely time awareness losses. This
254
intervention was not intended to reflect a real intervention, but to understand if an automated process could

3. Discussion
258
We demonstrate that providing PHRs as context to LLMs can result in answers to consumer health questions
259
that are more helpful and relevant. This includes both queries derived from patient/provider conversations
260
that are more likely to directly concern PHR, but also more common queries from Search and some more
261
general queries based on chatbot templates. This suggests there could be broader benefits from access to
262
PHR context. Our autorater results (Figures 1-3, Tables 7- 8) suggest there could be additional benefits in
263
axes such as safety and accuracy, although our initial clinician evaluations do not show significant effects
264
here. Further research will be needed to more robustly assess the impact of PHR context on these aspects
265
of answers. To our knowledge this provides the first rigorous assessment of the value of PHR context for
266
answering users’ health queries. Previous work has examined LLM retrieval in the context of clinical use
267
cases (Gu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Myers et al., 2025; Schreier et al., 2025), focused on consumer
268
question-answering in the absence of PHR context (Ayers et al., 2023; Pfohl et al., 2024; Singhal et al., 2023)
269
or discussed structural challenges in conducting consumer-focused EHR research (Bardhan et al., 2024).
270
PHRs are often complex, and literature has documented relatively high rates of errors or gaps in PHRs,
271
which may impact their utility for tasks including diagnosis (Bell et al., 2020; Graber et al., 2017; Lear et al.,
272
2022) Our PHR-specific evaluation framework also identifies some potential loss patterns with the model
273
and associated prompts tested here. In particular, we identified some issues around interpreting timelines of
274
encounters, with instances of inaccurate temporal analysis in 3-5% of answers (Table 9). We also observed
275
some issues grounding answers to the PHR, omission of key context from the PHR, and inability to identify
276
data gaps in the PHRs. We demonstrate that some simple interventions, like a self-critique loop (Table 10),
277
may provide substantial mitigation against these risks, reducing the instances of grounding by 89%. Further
278
improvements, including identifying relevant subsetes of the PHR in the context of a given question, may
279
further reduce these risks.
280
These results have implications for the increasing real-world adoption of AI as a tool to help individuals
281
understand their health concerns. As AI tools grow in their ability to handle long context data, they will
282
be increasingly able to personalize advice to users. For health-related questions in particular, this seems to
283
be important. Our current results, especially our observation that PHR can improve helpfulness even for
284
common search queries from HealthSearchQA, suggest this personalization may provide benefits for a wide
285
range of user journeys. This has the potential to not only improve individuals’ understanding of their health
286
issues, but potentially to become more active and engaged partners in their healthcare. Future studies will
287
need to evaluate the impact of PHR integration on health-specific outcomes in order to fully capture potential
288
benefits. With health systems in many countries under extreme financial and health workforce pressures, the
289
potential to improve care outcomes is substantial. However, the addition of large and complex contextual
290
information like PHRs into AI context windows to inform responses, also poses risks of inaccurate use of
291
that very same context. Principled and robust evaluation approaches are therefore likely to be highly used
292
to understand AI performance in these complex scenarios, and downstream risks such as negative impacts
293
on individuals or the health care system. Our PHR-focused evaluation approach could be standardized to
294
benchmark models and track progress on dealing with often-complex and messy health records.
295
PHR usage overall is forecast to have accelerating adoption over the coming years (Ford et al., 2016). As
296
integration of consumer-facing tools with PHR data improves, some issues we have identified here, such
297
as data gaps, may become less common. With richer access to data, other issues, like ability to properly
298
reason across long time scales, may become relatively more important. Users’ mental models of the ability of
299
informational tools like LLMs to answer their questions may evolve, leading to more complex questions and
300
other challenges not yet foreseen in producing high-quality answers.

A.1. Personal Health Records dataset
335
We use de-identified retrospective electronic health record (EHR) data from a large real-world data source
336
comprising clinical notes written by healthcare professionals across a diverse patient population (Table 1,
337
Fig. 1b).The dataset includes multiple note types including progress notes, telephone encounter records, and
338
patient instruction letters, representing several types of the detailed, unstructured documentation generated
339
during routine clinical care. These data span the full breadth of patient interactions, covering reasons for
340
visit, clinical assessments, treatment plans, medication management, and follow-up communications. Note
341
that the de-identification process replaced real dates and names with fake entities; any examples with dates
342
and names in this manuscript are not real data. The research protocol for use of de-identified retrospective
343
data was reviewed and deemed exempt from further review by Advarra IRB.
344
For our study, we construct patient-level context-rich longitudinal PHRs by concatenating clinician notes
345
from all available clinical encounters in chronological order, which we refer for the purposes of this study as a
346
‘Full PHR’. These notes represent observations, patient sentiment, and instructions as documented by health
347
care providers and are maintained in their original, unstructured formats. Key components include patient
348
information (e.g., clinical, sociodemographic), reason for visit, present illness, medical history, assessments,
349
and treatment plans.In addition, we generate a structured summary of the full PHR, containing the patient’s
350
demographic and clinical profile: age, sex, race, historical and current diagnoses, and current medications.
351
Within this study, we term this more concise context a ‘Basic PHR’. Examples of queries with Basic PHR
352
information are provided in Table 3. This basic PHR is intended to represent information that is relatively
353
straightforward for users to provide to a model, such as over the course of a multi-turn conversation.
354
Our final dataset comprises 1,945 PHRs containing detailed clinical notes. These PHRs cover 77,644 patient
355
encounters at 1,540 US-based facilities across 9 health systems. These health systems provide care across a
356
broad geographic scope, including the Gulf South, Midwest, Mid-Atlantic, and West Coast. Compared to
357
US census distributions, the patient population skews moderately female (58.7% overall vs. 50.5% in the
358
population), and older (mean age of 49 vs. 39.7 years). It has a relatively higher representation of White
359
patients (77.1% vs. 66.6%) and lower representation of Hispanic (3.0% vs. 19.5%) and Asian (2.4% vs.
360
6.1%) patients, and a higher representation of unemployed individuals (20.6% vs. 2.4%). Furthermore, the
361
Charlson Comorbidity Index of the overall population (2.3) suggests individuals were older (which counts
362
towards this score) and/or had several comorbidities (Charlson et al., 2008; Li et al., 2008; Wang et al.,
363

Table 5 | Summary of PHR evaluation framework.
Category
Evaluation axis
Description
Fidelity
Groundedness
Are all facts about the user grounded to the PHR, including date, value, and
unit? The response must not confabulate or hallucinate clinical details (e.g.,
fabricating lab values, inventing diagnoses, or attributing medications not
listed in the PHR).
Time awareness
Does the response demonstrate correct understanding of the patient’s health
timeline? This axis focuses on temporal reasoning failures: treating old
data as current (e.g., using weight or lab results from years prior to make
current recommendations), miscalculating durations (e.g., incorrectly calcu-
lating pregnancy progress or failing to reconcile conflicting dates), making
seasonally inappropriate recommendations (e.g., recommending flu shots in
June/July), or advising continuation of treatments that should have already
concluded based on documented dates (e.g., advising a patient to “finish” a
medication course that ended weeks ago).
Key data focus
Does the response center its advice on the most relevant health facts provided
(e.g., the main illness or high-risk drugs)?
Avoids overperson-
alization
Does the response avoid inappropriate use of personal information? This
axis evaluates these failure modes:
• Forced Connection: Applying a personal inference in an irrelevant,
illogical, or unhelpful way (e.g., framing a medical issue using the user’s
unrelated profession or hobby).
• Overnarrating Personalization: Unnecessary or blanket explanations
of how personal information was applied (e.g., excessive “Since you...”,
“Based on your...” preambles that add no clinical value).
• Offensive Personalization: Injecting overly specific personal details that
feel intrusive, socially inappropriate, or creepy (e.g., referencing specific
clothing sizes, spending habits, or sensitive personal details unprompted).
Robustness
Data gaps
Does the response identify gaps in data relevant to the user’s prompt that
would prevent a high-quality response, without assuming the provided input
is complete? When the prompt relates to the potential absence of concerning
signs, does it distinguish between absence of evidence (e.g. no relevant
information provided in PHR) versus positive evidence for concerning signs
not being present?
Conflict detection
Does the response point out conflicts between input in the prompt and PHR
data, or between different parts of PHR data relevant to the prompt, and
ask the user for verification?
Abnormality detec-
tion
Does the response highlight abnormal readings in the provided input when
relevant to the user’s prompt, without minimizing concerning findings or
providing false reassurance (e.g., does not describe a critically elevated
A1C as “slightly high,” does not dismiss abnormal lab values with generic
reassurance when clinical action may be warranted)?
Cognitive flexibil-
ity
Does the response avoid repeating the same personal details without adding
new clinical value? This axis flags tunnel vision — over-indexing on a
narrow subset of PHR facts by restating them throughout the response
as filler. Referencing a fact once for context and once more to justify a
specific recommendation is fine; mentioning the same lab value, condition,
or medication three or more times in a single response, with no new insight
each time, is not.
Utility
Data synthesis
When relevant to the user’s prompt, does the response combine multiple
facts correctly (e.g., age + lab results + meds) to create a single, tailored
insight?

To represent the health information needs of general consumers, we draw on HealthSearchQA (Singhal et al.,
368
2023), a dataset of free-response single-turn medical queries drawn from online health searches. Compared
369
to the other question sources examined in this study, HealthSearchQA queries tend to be shorter and touch
370
on more general topics, representing questions from a population of users who may be seeking relatively
371
more straightforward health information.
372
As with other question sets, the goal was to assess answers to these questions in the context of individuals with
373
specific health situations described by PHRs. This required pairing HealthSearchQA queries with PHRs from
374
our records set. We evaluated candidate HealthSearchQA query-PHR pairs using Gemini 3 Flash Preview.
375
The LLM assessed whether each query could plausibly originate from an individual with that specific PHR
376
profile, accounting for demographics, comorbidities, and medications. Pairs were scored on a 5-point Likert
377
scale (ranging from highly plausible to highly implausible), and only the top two ratings were retained.
378
The plausibly prompt was optimized based on multiple rounds of qualitative manual qualitative review of
379
example pairings before finalizing the selection. The full scoring prompt is available in the Supplementary
380
Methods.
381
This question-PHR plausibility scoring was applied at scale through an iterative, batch-wise matching
382
procedure. In our implementation, a random sample of 10 unmatched PHRs and 10 unmatched queries
383
were drawn per round, and all 100 pairwise combinations within the batch were scored in parallel. A greedy
384
selection then identified a set of plausible pairs subject to the constraint that each PHR and each question
385
could be matched at most once across the full dataset. PHRs and queries that went unmatched in a given
386
round remained eligible for subsequent rounds, allowing the pipeline to continue until the target number of
387
pairs was reached.

A.2.2. Chatbot templates
389
To capture health information needs as they arise in practice, we draw on common patterns of health-related
390
interactions observed in Gemini-app usage. Analysis of these interactions identified recurring question
391
themes spanning symptom analysis, interpretation of lab results and clinical documents, risk and triage
392
assessment, treatment guidance, disease progression, and lifestyle modification. These themes served as
393
the basis for a curated set of anonymized question templates (e.g., "Is it safe to take MEDICATION with my
394

395

396

397

398

399

400

401

402

403
