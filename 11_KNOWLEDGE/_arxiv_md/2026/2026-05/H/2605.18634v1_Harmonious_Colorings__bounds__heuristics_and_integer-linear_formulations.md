Abstract
A proper coloring c of a simple graph G is harmonious if, for every pair of dis-
tinct edges uv, xy ∈E(G), we have that {c(u), c(v)} ̸= {c(x), c(y)}. The harmonious
chromatic number of G, denoted by h(G), is the least positive integer k such that G
has a harmonious coloring with k colors. In this work, we extend an idea presented
in [Kolay, et al. Harmonious coloring: Parameterized algorithms and upper bounds.
Theor. Comp. Sci. 772 (2019), 132–142] to compare the harmonious chromatic num-
bers of two graphs G and H, with H being obtained from G by identifying vertices at
distance at least three. Furthermore, by fixing a proof presented in the same work, we
manage to improve one of its upper bounds. We also introduce and study the first, to
the best of our knowledge, integer-linear programming formulations for this problem
in the literature, along with some heuristics. We provide some preliminary tests on
random instances and instances from the second DIMACS Implementation Challenge.

Introduction
Given a simple graph G = (V, E), a k-coloring of G is a function c : V (G) →{1, . . . , k}.
A k-coloring c is proper if c(u) ̸= c(v) for every uv ∈E(G). The chromatic number of G,
denoted by χ(G), is the minimum positive integer k such that G admits a proper k-coloring.
It is well-known that computing χ(G) is an NP-hard problem [13].

A k-coloring c (not necessarily proper) is line-distinguishing if, for any pair of distinct
edges uv, xy ∈E(G), we have that {c(u), c(v)} ̸= {c(x), c(y)}.
The line-distinguishing
chromatic number, denoted by λ(G), is the least positive integer k for which G admits a
line-distinguishing k-coloring [11]. A harmonious k-coloring is a proper line-distinguishing
k-coloring.
The harmonious chromatic number of G, denoted by h(G), is the minimum positive
integer k such that G has a harmonious k-coloring [16]. It is worth mentioning that, in their
seminal paper, Hopcroft and Krishnamoorthy [11] used the term Harmonious Coloring to
simply mean line-distinguishing coloring; however, since the work of Lee and Mitchem [16],
the term is dedicated to the version of the problem that requires the coloring to be proper.
The distance between two vertices is the length of a shortest path between them (it is
+∞if no such a path exists), and the diameter of a graph is the maximum distance between
any two vertices. A harmonious coloring must assign different colors to vertices at distance
1 (since it is proper) or 2 (since it is line-distinguishing). So, complete graphs are not the
only n-vertex graphs having a harmonious chromatic number equal to n. Any graph with n
vertices and a diameter of at most two must receive n colors in a harmonious coloring [17].
Graph colorings have been used to model many different types of problems, from com-
puting derivatives [9] to testing printed circuits [8] and performing image segmentation [10].
However, harmonious graph coloring has been studied mainly from a theoretical point of view
for several years [6]. In the scarce literature on direct applications of harmonious coloring,
we can cite its use in modeling a radio navigation system [19].
In this work, we present some results on structural graph theory related to harmonious
coloring, alongside integer programming models and computational experiments for deter-
mining the harmonious chromatic number of a given graph.
Our contributions and related results.
In the next two sections, we study upper
bounds for h(G). Section 2 considers d-degenerate graphs.
We say that a graph G is d-degenerate if, for any subgraph H ⊆G, the minimum
degree δ(H) of H is at most d. The Harmonious Coloring of d-degenerate graphs has been
related to vertex cover [14]. Recall that S ⊆V (G) is a vertex cover of G if, for any edge
uv ∈E(G), we have u ∈S or v ∈S. Let vc(G) denote the cardinality of a minimum vertex
cover S ⊆V (G) of a graph G. In [14], the authors claim that if G is d-degenerate, then
h(G) ≤vc(G) + d(∆(G) −1) + ∆(G)(d −1), where ∆(G) is the maximum degree of G.
In Section 2, we argue that their proof is incorrect and show a counter-example for d = 1.
Besides, we prove that actually h(G) ≤vc(G) + d(∆(G) −1) + 1, thus providing a tighter
bound for d ≥2.
Section 3 considers arbitrary graphs. Using identifications on vertices at distance at least
three, we derive a sufficient condition for a graph to have a harmonious chromatic number
at most k. We remark that part of our argument is similar to the one used in [14] to identify
color classes in a harmonious coloring as part of a fixed-parameter tractable algorithm to
compute h(G), parameterized by vc(G).
Computing the harmonious chromatic number of a graph is a hard task. Given a graph

G and a positive integer k, determining whether h(G) ≤k is NP-complete, even if G is a
split graph [1], or an interval graph [1,2], or a tree of radius three, or a forest consisting of
three trees of radius two [7]. To address this computational hardness, Section 4 is devoted to

We introduce three integer linear programming models, two of them based on the repre-
sentatives formulation presented in [3]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work
to present a mathematical programming approach for harmonious coloring. By the way, the
first integer-linear programming formulations to compute the line-distinguishing chromatic
number were proposed only recently [18]. Of course, these formulations do not impose the
constraint that the coloring must be proper.
We emphasize that we do not only add this constraint to the formulation presented in [18].
We present a new set of O(n3) constraints to ensure a line-distinguishing coloring, which
improves the O(n4) constraints proposed in [18], where n stands for the number of vertices of
the input graph. We present computational experiments to test our formulations and heuris-
tics on random instances as well as on instances from the second DIMACS Implementation
Challenge [20].

Bound on the vertex cover and the maximum degree
In [14], the authors present the following bound:
Theorem 1 ( [14]). For any graph G with ∆(G) ≥2, ∆(G) + 1 ≤h(G) ≤vc(G) +
∆(G)(∆(G) −1).
And a refined version of it to d-degenerate graphs:
Theorem 2 ([14]). If G is a d-degenerate graph, then ∆(G)+1 ≤h(G) ≤vc(G)+d(∆(G)−
1) + ∆(G)(d −1).
The techniques to prove these theorems are similar.
The lower bound is simply the
constraint that vertices at distance two should have distinct colors. For the upper bounds,
in both theorems, the authors first present a harmonious coloring of the graph using exactly
the proposed upper bounds +1 color, and then a trick is applied to save such extra color. This
is how they obtain a harmonious coloring on the graph with exactly vc(G)+∆(G)(∆(G)−1)
colors at the end of Theorem 1 (resp vc(G)+d(∆(G)−1)+∆(G)(d−1) colors in Theorem 2).
However, since it has no relation to the graph being d-degenerate but only to the maxi-
mum degree of G, in Theorem 2, the trick for saving a color is misapplied, though it works
well for Theorem 1.
As a consequence of Theorem 2, the authors present the corollary:
Corollary 1 ([14]). If G is a forest with at least one edge, then ∆(G) + 1 ≤h(G) ≤
vc(G) + ∆(G) −1.

Corollary 1 is also incorrect. For a counter-example to both statements, let 2K2 be the
simple graph consisting of four vertices and two edges without a common endpoint. Note
that vc(2K2) = 2, ∆(2K2) = 1, and 2K2 is 1-degenerate, but h(2K2) = 3. Another possible
counter-example is a star Sk, for k ≥2, which is a tree on k + 1 vertices such that only
one is not a leaf. Note that vc(Sk) = 1, ∆(G) = k; again, it is a 1-degenerate graph, but
h(Sk) = k + 1.
When trying to fix Theorem 2, we were able to improve it.
Theorem 3. If G is a d-degenerate graph, then ∆(G)+1 ≤h(G) ≤vc(G)+d(∆(G)−1)+1.
Proof. The lower bound is the same bound shown by [14]. Let n = |V (G)|.
To prove the upper bound, we use induction on n to prove a stronger statement: if S is a
vertex cover of G, then there is a harmonious coloring c of G with at most |S|+d(∆(G)−1)+1
colors such that each vertex v of S has an individual color, i.e. no other vertex in V (G)\{v}
is colored with color c(v).
If G is trivial, i.e. n = 1, then G is 0-degenerate and ∆(G) = 0. So the result follows
since, regardless whether S is empty or not, h(G) = 1 ≤|S| + d(∆(G) −1) + 1.
Let G be a d-degenerate graph with n ≥2 vertices, and let S be a vertex cover of G.
Since G is d-degenerate, there exists an ordering σ = (v1, . . . , vn) over V (G) such that any
vertex vi has at most d neighbors with indices smaller than i, for every i ∈{1, . . . , n}.
Let H = G −vn.
Since S is a vertex cover of G, note that S′ = S \ {vn} is a vertex
cover of H. Moreover, H is d-degenerate. By inductive hypothesis, let c′ be a harmonious
(|S′| + d(∆(H) −1) + 1)-coloring of H such that the vertices of S′ have individual colors.
Now consider the two following cases:
1)
vn ∈S: In this case, |S| = |S′| + 1. So we can extend c′ to V (G) by using a new color
only in vn, creating a harmonious (|S′| + d(∆(H) −1) + 2)-coloring c of G. Note that:
|S′| + d(∆(H) −1) + 2 = |S| + d(∆(H) −1) + 1 ≤|S| + d(∆(G) −1) + 1.
So G can be harmoniously colored with |S| + d(∆(G) −1) + 1 colors, and in c each
vertex of S uses an individual color.
2)
vn /∈S: In this case, S = S′. Since S is a vertex cover of G, then V (G) \ S is an
independent set of G. By inductive hypothesis, each vertex of S = S′ is colored with an
individual color in c′. Note that if we extend c′ to a coloring c of V (G) by coloring vn
with a color that does not occur in S = S′, nor in the vertices at distance two from vn,
then c is a harmonious coloring of G. By the choice of vn, vn has at most d neighbors
in G, and thus at most d(∆(G) −1) vertices at distance two. This is one unit less than
the d(∆(G) −1) + 1 colors that do not occur in S = S′ in the coloring c′. Thus, we can
extend c′ to a harmonious (|S| + d(∆(G) −1) + 1)-coloring c of G such that the vertices
of S have individual colors.
The result follows by considering a minimum vertex cover S, i.e. with |S| = vc(G).

Identifications of vertices at distance three
Let G ◦uv denote the graph obtained from G by the identification of two non-adjacent
distinct vertices u, v ∈V (G), with the removal of multiple edges in case they appear. Recall
that such an operation removes u and v, adds a new vertex w, and adds an edge from w to
each vertex adjacent to u or v.
It is well-known that χ(G) = n(G) if, and only if, G is complete. Due to Zykov [21], it is
also known that, given u, v ∈V (G) and uv /∈E(G), the chromatic number of G satisfies
χ(G) = min{χ(G + uv), χ(G ◦uv)}.
(1)
With similar ideas, one can prove that:
Proposition 1. For any graph G, the following holds:
(a) if χ(G) = k then there exists a sequence of identifications of non-adjacent vertices to be
applied to G such that the remaining graph is Kk; and
(b) if Kk is obtained from G by a sequence of identifications of non-adjacent vertices, then
χ(G) ≤χ(Kk) = k.
In the sequel, we present similar observations to the case of Harmonious Colorings. Let
us first present a quite simple statement proved by [15]. Let diam(G) denote the diameter
of G, i.e. the maximum distance between two vertices.
Lemma 1 ([15]). For any graph G, h(G) = n(G) if, and only if, diam(G) ≤2.
Proof. Suppose first that diam(G) > 2. Then, there are u, v ∈V (G) such that distG(u, v) ≥
3. Consider that V (G) = {u, v, w1, w2, . . . , wn(G)−2}. Let us define a coloring c : V (G) →
{1, . . . , n(G)−1} as follows: c(u) = c(v) = 1 and c(wi) = i+1, for every i ∈{1, . . . , n(G)−2}.
Note that c is a harmonious coloring of G using n(G) −1 colors, and so h(G) < n. The
converse is trivial.
Concerning vertex identifications, let us prove a corresponding version of Proposition 1
to the harmonious chromatic number.
Lemma 2. Let G be an graph such that diam(G) ≥3. Then,
(a) there is a pair u, v ∈V (G) such that distG(u, v) ≥3 whose identification results in a
graph H with h(H) = h(G); and
(b) if H is obtained from G by the identification of u, v ∈V (G) such that distG(u, v) ≥3,
then h(G) ≤h(H).
Proof. Consider |V (G)| = n. Since diam(G) ≥3, by Lemma 1 we deduce that h(G) = k ≤
n(G) −1. Hence, in every harmonious k-coloring of G there is a pair of distinct vertices
in the same color class. Let c: V (G) →{1, . . . , k} be a harmonious k-coloring of G, and

Let c′ be a harmonious p-coloring of H, p = h(H). Define c: V (G) →{1, . . . , p} such
that c(wi) = c′(wi) for each 1 ≤i ≤n −2, and c(u) = c(v) = c(w). We will show that c
is a harmonious p-coloring of G. By the construction of H and knowing that uv /∈E(G),
certainly c is a proper coloring of G. Since c′ is a harmonious coloring, if there are two edges
e1, e2 ∈E(G) with the same colors in their endpoints, then certainly v is an endpoint of one
of these edges and u is an endpoint of the other edge. Without lost of generality, consider
e1 = uz, e2 = vy, c(u) = c(v) = 1 and c(z) = c(y) = 2. However, u and v are identified
to obtain H, so distH(z, y) ≤2. Thus, because c′ is a harmonious coloring, the equality
c(z) = c(y) = c′(z) = c′(y) is not possible.
One may naturally deduce what happens after applying Lemma 2 iteratively:
Theorem 4. For any graph G, the following hold:
(a) if h(G) = k, then there exists a sequence of identifications of vertices to be applied to G
such that, at each step, the identified vertices have distance at least 3 and such that the
obtained graph H in the end has k vertices and diameter at most 2; and
(b) if H is obtained from G by a sequence of identifications of vertices such that, at each
step, the identified vertices have distance at least 3, then h(G) ≤h(H).
Proof. To prove (a), suppose first that h(G) = k. If diam(G) ≤2, then h(G) = n(G) and
one may take an empty sequence. Suppose now that diam(G) ≥3. Thus, it is enough to
apply Lemma 2(a) exhaustively until the resulting graph has diameter at most 2, and then
we apply Lemma 1.
The second statement follows by successive applications of Lemma 2(b).
Theorem 4 is our source of inspiration for a heuristic we propose to obtain feasible
harmonious colorings in Section 5. We must also emphasize that Statement (a) of Theorem 4
is proved in [14], with the difference that they identify all vertices in the same color class of
an optimal harmonious coloring of G at once.
Another remark is that Zykov’s equation (1) to the chromatic number does not translate
easily as adding edges to G not only creates new constraints to obtain a proper coloring, but
also to obtain a harmonious coloring. A natural question is:
Problem 1. Can one find an equation similar to Zykov’s to the harmonious chromatic
number?

Integer Programming Formulations
Without loss of generality, we assume that the input graph G has no isolated vertices, as
such a vertex would not alter the harmonious chromatic number. This will simplify the
notation and the presentation of the formulations in this section.

Representatives Formulation

Greedy Heuristics
As computing the harmonious chromatic number is an NP-Hard problem, one has little to
no hope of solving large instances of the problem exactly. Hence, we propose heuristics to
handle such large instances. A simple type of heuristic method that commonly represents
the first attempt to tackle any problem is the greedy heuristic.
We present a simple greedy heuristic based on the iterative process of identification
of vertices discussed in Section 3.
We propose two different greedy choices for the pair
to be contracted: the minimum degree sum (Algorithm 1) and the maximum degree sum
(Algorithm 2). Both methods use the result presented in Theorem 4 to compute the correct
value of a harmonic coloring that colors the identified vertices with the same color.

Environment and Instances Description
All computational experiments were executed on a machine running Ubuntu 20.04 x86-64

The benchmark instances used in the computational experiments were divided into two
sets: random and dimacs. Both sets are also divided into small and large instances.
The set random is composed of randomly generated graphs, that respect a given prob-
ability of having an edge between any distinct pair of vertices. More precisely, for a given
natural n and a probability p, we generate a graph with n vertices and add an edge be-

estimation of the edge density of the graph. Besides, such a process is inherently random
and can produce different structures. Thus, for each pair composed of a vertex number

instances comprises those where n ≤50 and 0.20 ≤p. The set dimacs [12] comprises 31
graphs that are challenging for the classical vertex coloring problem. This set encompasses
diverse graph structures that are known to yield challenging instances for coloring problems.
We also select a part of these instances to form a set of small instances, but the criterion
adopted is only the number of vertices, due to the diversity of structures of the dimacs
graphs. More precisely, we select all instances that have 100 vertices or fewer.

6.2
Comparison of the Mathematical Models
The first group of experiments was designed to assess the effectiveness of the proposed
mathematical models.
Therefore, we used the small instances in such experiments.
We
limited the execution time of the solver to 1800 seconds in this first set of experiments, used
a single thread, and left the standard configuration of all the solver’s parameters.
All the presented formulations have a group of constraints to define stable sets (color
classes): (2c) in the standard formulation, (3c) in the representatives formulation and (4d)
in the asymmetric formulation. Although correct, this kind of constraint tends to enlarge
the model and is commonly replaced by a set of constraints related to a clique cover of the
edges of the graph. More precisely, assume that K1, K2, . . . , Kp is a set of maximal cliques
in the input graph square (G2) such that the endpoints of every edge belong to at least
one clique. Then, for each clique K, the |K|(|K| −1)/2 constraints in each formulation
forbidding both endpoints of every edge in K from receiving the same color can be replaced
by a single constraint ensuring that at most one vertex in K is assigned any given color. This
approach reduces the number of constraints in each model and leads to better computational
results. Thus, we employ such a strategy while keeping the same choice of cliques for the
three formulations to preserve the comparison between them.
The maximal clique cover we use in the experiments is built as follows. The first step
selects a vertex u with maximum degree in the graph square and creates a set K containing
only u. Then, we iteratively select a vertex of maximum degree in G2[N(K) \ K] and add
such a vertex to K. Eventually, K becomes a maximal clique in G2, and it is used in the

Concluding remarks
The harmonious coloring problem tends to be harder in sparse graphs, so it is not surprising
that the solution methods tend to reach the optimum faster in dense graphs, as observed in
the computational experiments. The higher efficiency of the representatives models is mainly
due to two facts: the number of variables in these models decreases with the edge density
of G2, and they are able to break some/all symmetries (especially the asymmetric version).
For d-degenerate graphs, we obtained a strong upper bound on the harmonious chromatic
number, but we believe it could be further improved if expressed in terms of parameters
other than the vertex cover and the maximum degree. The parameterized complexity of this
problem has not been well studied yet. So, this would be a good research avenue to explore.
