Abstract
The rapidly rotating B1 IV:nn star HD 3191 lies within the error box of a flaring Fermi γ-ray source. Although the counterpart
of the Fermi source is likely an active galaxy, HD 3191 has nevertheless been suggested to be a high-mass X-ray binary, possibly
hosting a black hole companion. The star displays roughly sinusoidal photometric variations with amplitudes of ∼12 mmag for
two frequencies ν1 = 0.1235 d−1 and ν2 = 1.6038 d−1. Half of the former frequency (ν1/2) had previously been interpreted as
the orbital frequency of a high-mass X-ray binary in which the B1 IV:nn primary undergoes ellipsoidal variations. We show that
this scenario fails to account for the lack of significant radial velocity variations and for the overall properties of the star. Our
spectroscopic observations instead unveil line profile variations, suggesting that the photometric and line profile variations arise
from multi-mode pulsations rather than from orbital effects. Whilst we cannot rule out the possibility that HD 3191 could be a
nascent binary, consisting of a B1 IV:nn with a low-mass pre-main sequence companion, the most likely scenario is a single star
displaying non-radial pulsations. The fast stellar rotation would thus be intrinsic to the B1 IV:nn star rather than being the result of
a spin-up during a past mass-transfer episode.
Keywords: stars: early-type, stars: individual (HD 3191), binaries: close, stars: variables: general, stars: oscillations (including
pulsations)
1. Introduction
Over the past two decades, the interest of the astrophysical
community in the evolution of massive stars and massive binary
systems enjoyed a strong boost. Observationally, it was found
that the majority of massive stars form and evolve in binary or
higher multiplicity systems (e.g. Sana et al., 2012; Offner et al.,
2023, and references therein). In the course of their evolution,
such systems can go through a phase where the most massive
component has already exploded as a supernova, leaving behind
a neutron star or a black hole orbiting the (initially less massive)
non-degenerate companion (e.g., Kruckow et al., 2018; Man-
del, 2024). Such a configuration frequently leads to situations
where the non-degenerate star transfers material and angular
momentum to its compact companion. Such systems appear as
high-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs) that emit copious amounts
of X-rays (Fornasini et al., 2023). In some HMXBs, such as
LS I +61 303, the high-energy emission extends into the γ-ray
domain (e.g., Albert et al., 2008).
Our target, HD 3191, has been cited as a candidate of such a
γ-ray binary. Pivato et al. (2016) reported the detection with the
Fermi satellite of a previously unknown transient γ-ray source
(Fermi J0035+6131) near the Galactic Plane. This source sub-
sequently entered the Fermi catalog as 4FGL J0035.8+6131.

2. Observations and data processing
2.1. Spectroscopic data
Given the uncertainties on the nature of HD 3191, we set up
a spectroscopic monitoring campaign to find out whether the
star undergoes orbital motion and to check whether the spec-
trum indeed exhibits He ii λ 4686 emission.
Thirty echelle
spectra of HD 3191 were collected between October 2021 and
September 2023 with the refurbished HEROS echelle spectro-
graph (Kaufer, 1998; Schmitt et al., 2014) on the robotic 1.2 m
TIGRE telescope (Schmitt et al., 2014; González-Pérez et al.,
2022) at La Luz Observatory near Guanajuato (Mexico). The
TIGRE/HEROS spectrograph has a resolving power of 20 000
over the wavelength range from 3760 – 8700 Å with a small
gap around 5600 Å. The data were processed with the HEROS
reduction pipeline (Mittag et al., 2011; Schmitt et al., 2014).
Telluric absorption lines in the spectral regions around the He i
λ 5876 and Hα lines were removed by means of the telluric
tool of the iraf software and using the atlas of telluric lines
of Hinkle et al. (2000). Further data analysis (continuum nor-
malization, RV measurements) was performed with the midas
software.
2.2. Photometry
Space-borne photometry of HD 3191 was obtained in six sec-
tors with the TESS satellite. TESS features four wide-field cam-
eras operating in the 6000 Å to 1 µm waveband. Each camera
covers a 24◦×24◦field with pixel sizes of 15 µm squared corre-
sponding to (21 arcsec)2 on the sky. TESS monitors sky sectors
of 24◦× 96◦for about 27 consecutive days. HD 3191 was ob-
served at a 2 min cadence during Sectors 17, 18 and 85, with a
30 min cadence during Sector 24, and every 200 s during Sec-
tors 58 and 78.
The 2 min cadence light curves, processed with the TESS
pipeline (Jenkins et al., 2016), were downloaded from the
Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST) portal2.
They provide simple background-corrected aperture photome-
try (SAP) as well as Pre-search Data Conditioned (PDC) pho-
tometry obtained after correcting trends correlated with sys-
tematic spacecraft or instrumental effects. In the present case,
both SAP and PDC light curves were found to be very similar.
However, since we found an apparently lower amplitude for the
8 day modulation in some of the pipeline-produced photometry,
we also analysed the full frame images (FFI) taken at a cadence
of 30 min (Sectors 17 and 18) or 200 s (Sector 85). The FFI data
were processed with the Lightkurve Python software package3.
Aperture photometry was extracted on 51 × 51 pixels image
cutouts. For the source mask, we adopted a flux threshold of
20 times the median absolute deviation over the median flux4.
The background was evaluated either from those pixels in the
cutout that were below the median flux, or by means of a prin-
cipal component analysis (pca) including five components. The
background-corrected fluxes were converted to magnitudes and
data points with errors larger than the mean error augmented by
three times the dispersion of the errors were removed. The same
procedure was applied to the FFI data taken during Sectors 24
(30 min cadence), 58 and 78 (both 200 s cadence). The results
of both background subtraction methods were generally in very
good agreement, with the exception of the data from Sector 78
which displayed small differences between the pca and median
fluxes method. We subsequently used the photometry obtained
with the pca method, except for Sector 78 for which the pca
lightcurve showed a weaker signature of the main modulation
and where we used the median fluxes instead.
Since the TESS photometry of a given source is extracted
over several pixels, the light curves of objects in crowded re-
gions can be contaminated by neighbouring sources. To check
whether this is the case for HD 3191, we queried the third Gaia
data release catalog (DR3, Gaia Collaboration et al., 2023). We
found a total of 70 additional objects within a 1 arcmin radius
of HD 3191.
However, the brightest neighbouring source is
4.3 mag fainter than HD 3191 in the Gaia GRP band implying
that the TESS photometry of HD 3191 should be free of signifi-
cant contamination. This conclusion is backed-up by the value
of the TESS CROWDSAP parameter which estimates the frac-
tion of (background corrected) flux in the photometric aperture
attributable to the target. This keyword amounts to 0.978 for
Sector 17, 0.968 for Sector 18, and 0.969 for Sector 85.

3. Results
3.1. Photometric variability
Figure 1 displays the TESS lightcurve during the six sectors.
One can clearly see two prominent modulations on timescales
of about 0.6 and 8 days as reported previously for Sectors 17,
18, and 24 by Martí et al. (2021). To further quantify these
results, we applied the Fourier method of Heck et al. (1985),
amended by Gosset et al. (2001), to compute the periodogram
of these time series. This method explicity accounts for the odd-
ities of the sampling of astronomical time series. Because of the
different cadences of the time series (30 min for Sector 17, 18,
and 24, 200 s for Sectors 58, 78, and 85), we first applied this
method to the data from each individual sector separately. The
results are displayed in Fig. 2. We computed the periodograms
with frequency steps of 10−3 d−1 up to the theoretical Nyquist
frequencies (25 d−1 for 30 min cadence and 216 d−1 for 200 s
cadence). However, no significant power is found in the peri-
odograms above about 3 d−1.

3.3. Radial velocities
Owing to the rapid rotation, the spectral lines of HD 3191
are heavily broadened. This situation directly impacts the mea-
surement of the star’s RVs. We performed such measurements
using different techniques outlined in Appendix A. The best es-
timates were obtained for RVmean,6, that is the mean RV of the
six strongest spectral lines (Hγ, He i λ 4471, Hβ, He i λ 5876,
Hα, and He i λ 6678).
Folding the values of RVmean,6 with the ephemerides of Martí
et al. (2021) or with our ν1 frequency, no obvious trend at-
tributable to an orbital motion appears. Instead, the RVs from
neighbouring phases scatter randomly (see Fig. 5). This result
contrasts with the conclusion of Martí et al. (2023) who re-
ported hints of a 16.09 d period in their RV data. These au-
thors collected 29 spectra of HD 3191 in the 6300 – 6730 Å
wavelength range at a resolving power of 12000. Their RVs
were measured by cross-correlation with a synthetic spectrum.
Applying a Phase Dispersion Minimisation method (PDM,
Stellingwerf, 1978), they reported a most likely period in the
class. Given the rather large uncertainties on the calibrations of Wegner (2006),
our value of the absolute magnitude is consistent with any of the luminosity
classes V, IV or III.

4.1. Summary of observational properties of HD 3191
The photometric time series revealed the presence of mul-
tiperiodic variations with two dominant frequencies ν1
=

as well as several additional frequencies producing modula-

changes in the amplitudes, the overall properties of the photo-
metric modulations seem rather stable over the timescale of the
TESS observations.
We reconstructed the variations with the ν1 frequency by

Our spectra did not reveal any He ii λ 4686 emission nor any
clear RV variations on the photometric frequencies. Instead, we
found strong line profile variability which hints at the presence
of non-radial pulsations. The X-ray data indicate an X-ray lu-
minosity of LX = (1.54 ± 0.20) × 1031 erg s−1, corresponding to

We first consider the possibility that ν1 corresponds to the
rotation frequency. Adopting the above inferred stellar radius
of (8.46 ± 0.76) R⊙then results in an equatorial velocity of

of the true equatorial velocity. Hence, rotation does not offer
a plausible explanation for the ν1 frequency. Rotation is also
unlikely to account for the ν2 frequency. Indeed, ν2 exceeds the
critical rotation frequency which is about 1.31 d−1.
The most obvious alternative to rotational modulation is
multi-mode pulsations. Based on the TESS light curves from
Sectors 17 and 18, and their periodograms, Balona and Ozu-
yar (2020) classified HD 3191 as an SPB pulsator. SPBs dis-
play non-radial gravity-mode (g-mode) pulsations of high ra-
dial order. These pulsations are driven by the κ mechanism
in the partial ionisation zone of iron-group elements (Miglio
et al., 2007a; Dziembowski, 2009, and references therein). Tra-
ditionally, SPB stars were considered to be lower-mass counter-
parts of β Cep type pulsators7 with spectral types in the range
B3 – B9 (Bowman, 2020). Theoretical calculations and space-
borne photometry revealed considerable overlap between the
instability regions of β Cep and SPB pulsators, with the lat-
ter extending towards earlier B stars (e.g., Miglio et al., 2007b;
Balona and Ozuyar, 2020). Table 2, which includes frequen-
cies above 2.5 d−1, qualifies HD 3191 as an SPB + β Cep hybrid
pulsator. Compared to typical SPB pulsators, the ν1 frequency
of HD 3191 appears rather low. Indeed, most SPB pulsators
have pulsation periods of less than 4 d (e.g., Shi et al., 2023).
An exception is the B0 II star HD 260623 (TIC 55079633) for
which Shi et al. (2023) found a pulsation period of 9.145 d. Like
HD 3191, this object also belongs to the hotter SPBs.
These results concur with the location of the star in the
Hertzsprung-Russell diagram. Indeed, adopting the parame-
ters inferred in Sect. 3.2 (Teff = 27 000 K, Lbol = 34040 L⊙),
HD 3191 is located right in the low-degree (ℓ≤3) β Cep insta-
bility strip for Z = 0.02 and Z = 0.1 as computed by Miglio
et al. (2007b), and lies near the blue boundary of the SPB insta-
bility strip computed by the same authors (see Fig. 11).
Non-linear resonant coupling between pulsation modes of
multiperiodic stars can lead to variability of the frequencies
and amplitudes (Buchler et al., 1997; Pigulski and Pojma´nski,
2008; Degroote et al., 2009). A similar process could be at
work in HD 3191, thereby explaining the differences found in
our Fourier analysis of the various TESS sectors.
From our stellar parameters, we find that Ωrot/Ωcrit ≥0.46.
The rapid rotation can affect the properties of the pulsations
(e.g., Townsend, 2004, 2014, and references therein).
Non-
radial pulsations with ℓ≤3 can be shifted towards lower fre-
quencies by the Coriolis force, which tends to counteract the
restoring force on displaced fluid elements (Townsend, 2014).
The Coriolis force further distorts the NRPs and traps g modes
into the equatorial region and generates so-called Rossby waves
(Townsend, 2004). The latter do not exist in the absence of
rotation and their restoring force is the conservation of vortic-
ity. All these effects probably impact the pulsation spectrum of
HD 3191.

5. Conclusion
Our study has shown that, with the currently available data
(optical photometry and spectroscopy, Gaia distance, X-ray
data), HD 3191 appears very unlikely to be a high-mass X-ray
binary. Whilst we cannot rule out the possibility of a nascent bi-
nary system, that is an early B-star paired with a low-mass pre-
main sequence star with an 8 d orbital period, the most straight-
forward explanation for the observational properties appears to
be a single star displaying NRPs. Whether the B1 IV:nn star
in HD 3191 is a genuine single star or is part of a nascent bi-
nary, its rapid rotation (v sin i = 256 km s−1) does not seem to
be the outcome of a spin-up during a past Roche lobe overflow
episode. Similar conclusions have been reached for nascent bi-
naries (Britavskiy et al., 2024) and for some rapidly rotating OB
stars in unevolved wide eccentric binaries (Putkuri et al., 2018).
Our study thus supports the idea that rapid rotation of massive
stars is not always a consequence of past binary interactions.

Appendix A. New RV data
Table A.4 lists the heliocentric dates at mid-exposure of our
TIGRE/HEROS spectroscopic observations. We measured the
RVs of twelve relatively strong absorption lines in our spectra
by fitting a single gaussian to the full line profile. Our mea-
surements include prominent H i (H9, Hζ, Hδ, Hγ, Hβ and Hα)
as well as He i (λλ 4026, 4387, 4471, 4921, 5876, 6678) lines.
The results are displayed in Fig. A.12 as a function of the num-
ber of the spectrum. We find that different lines exhibit different
amplitudes of variations and sometimes different trends (see for
instance the RVs of the He i λ 4471 line in Fig. A.12). Whilst
the weakest lines in our sample or those lines with lower S/N
ratios (H9, Hζ, He i λλ 4026, 4388, 4921) have dispersions of
their RVs up to 16.6 km s−1, the strongest lines (Hα, Hβ, He i
λλ 4471, 5876) exhibit much lower dispersions between 6.7 and
7.7 km s−1.
We computed a mean value RVmean,12 from the RVs of all
twelve lines. This quantity exhibits a dispersion of 5.5 km s−1.
If we restrict the calculation of RVmean,6 to the six strongest
lines (Hγ, He i λ 4471, Hβ, He i λ 5876, Hα, He i λ 6678), we
obtain a dispersion of 5.9 km s−1 (see Table A.4 and Fig. A.12)
which is very similar to the estimated errors on individual
RVmean,6 values (between 1.1 and 9.5 km s−1 with an average of
4.6 km s−1). These latter errors were estimated from the disper-
sion of the RVs inferred from the six lines. For comparison, the
dispersion of the RVs of the narrow (FWHM of 1.2 Å) diffuse
interstellar band (DIB) at 6613 Å was found to be of 1.6 km s−1,
that is much smaller than the errors on the stellar RVs. How-
ever, this DIB is much narrower than the stellar lines, allowing
a more precise RV determination.
To check the above results, we further cross-correlated the
blue part of the TIGRE/HEROS spectra (between 4200 and
5000 Å) with a synthetic TLUSTY spectrum taken from Lanz
and Hubeny (2007). The template spectrum had an effective
temperature 26 000 K, log g = 4.0 and was rotationally broad-
ened to v sin i = 260 km s−1. The results, corrected for the
mean RV of −31.8 km s−1, are shown by the turquoise symbols
in the upper panel of Fig. A.12. These RVs (named RVXcorrel,1)
have a dispersion around the mean of 6.4 km s−1. With a few
exceptions, they agree well with the RVmean,6 values. Finally,
since the absorption lines undergo profile variations, we also
performed a cross-correlation truncating all the strong lines at
a depth of 0.1 times the continuum to avoid their cores. The
resulting RVs, corrected for their mean value of −27.7 km s−1,
are displayed as RVXcorrel,2 in Fig. A.12. They have a higher
dispersion about the mean of 7.1 km s−1, but follow the general
trend of the other RV measurements.

Appendix C. Extinction towards HD 3191 and spectral en-
ergy distribution
In the compilation of Reed (2003), HD 3191 has mV = 8.58±
0.01 and (B −V) = 0.44 ± 0.01. Wegner (1994) quote the
intrinsic (B −V)0 of a B1 V star as −0.23 whilst it would be
−0.21 for a B1 III star. This implies a colour excess E(B −
V) = 0.66 ± 0.02. Assuming RV = AV/E(B −V) = 2.7 (see
hereafter), yields an extinction AV = 1.78 ± 0.06. Using instead
(B −V)0 = −0.278, as given by Pecaut and Mamajek (2013)
for B1 V stars, leads to E(B −V) = 0.72 ± 0.01 and AV =
1.94±0.03. Munari and Valisa (2016) used the equivalent width
of the interstellar K i λ 7696 absorption and the DIB at 6613 Å
to estimate a reddening of E(B −V) = 0.73, corresponding to
AV = 1.97 (again assuming RV = 2.7). Whilst there exists
a strong dispersion in the relationship between reddening and
DIB strength, their value agrees reasonably well with ours.
