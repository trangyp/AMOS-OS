High-fidelity EDSR in Si/SiGe Wiggle Wells
Hudaiba Soomro, Minyoung Kim, Avani Vivrekar, M. A. Eriksson, Benjamin D. Woods, and Mark Friesen
Department of Physics, University of Wisconsin-Madison, Madison, Wisconsin 53706, USA
(Dated: May 26, 2026)
Si/SiGe quantum wells that incorporate Ge concentration oscillations, known as long-period Wig-
gle Wells, have previously been shown to enhance the Dresselhaus spin-orbit coupling (SOC) of
conduction-band electrons. Such intrinsic SOC is desirable when performing spin-qubit gate oper-
ations based on electric dipole spin resonance (EDSR) because it eliminates the need for external
micromagnets. However, random-alloy disorder plays a key role in the valley physics of this mate-
rials system by spatially randomizing the valley phase ϕs,s, and has not been fully accounted for in
recent EDSR analyses. Here, we show that alloy disorder affects EDSR in two main ways. First,
the Rabi frequency Ωacquires a dependence on the valley phase, given by cos ϕs,s, which causes
spatial randomization of Ω. Despite this variability, we show that fast EDSR can be achieved at
most locations across a given sample. Second, a new Rabi driving mechanism emerges, enabled by
valley dipoles that result from disorder and the hybridization of ground and excited valley states in
response to an EDSR driving field. This mechanisms is dominant in regions of low valley splitting.
Alloy disorder can therefore strengthen EDSR, but it can also cause gradients in Ωthat lead to
dephasing in the rotating frame. We explore this problem by first identifying “sweet spots,” where
EDSR is relatively insensitive to electric-field fluctuations. We then show that high-fidelity Rabi
oscillations can be achieved in the presence of realistic charge noise. Our results demonstrate that
the Wiggle Well is a promising platform for high-quality, micromagnet-free gate operations.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Si-based spin qubits have emerged as a promising plat-
form for quantum computing due to their long coherence
times, high-fidelity operations, scalability, and compati-
bility with existing fabrication techniques [1, 2]. Single-
and two-qubit gate operations with fidelities above 99%
have been achieved [3–6], with some fidelities above
99.99% [7]. Electric-dipole spin resonance (EDSR) is a
common technique for implementing single-qubit gates,
especially in single-spin qubits, which requires some form
of spin-orbit coupling (SOC) [8]. For hole-spin qubits [9],
this is normally the intrinsic SOC [10, 11]. However, such
an approach is seldom used for Si conduction-band elec-
trons, because the SOC is weak [12].
In this case, it
is more common to employ a synthetic SOC based on
micromagnets [13]. While highly successful, on-chip mi-
cromagnets present challenges to scalability and expose
the qubits to charge-noise-induced dephasing [2]. Spin-
valley coupling also enables a version of EDSR that re-
lies upon broken translational symmetry at the silicon
interface to generate a valley-orbit coupling (e.g., at an
atomic step) [14–16]. However, this scheme is not well-
explored, and involves a higher-order process combining
valley-orbit coupling and SOC.
SiGe heterostructures with Ge concentration oscilla-
tions inside the quantum well, illustrated in Fig. 1(a),
were originally proposed to enhance the valley-state en-
ergy splitting (or “valley splitting,” Ev) between the two
low-energy valley states in quantum dots [17, 18]. More
recently, these so-called Wiggle Wells (WWs) were also
predicted to enhance the SOC by 1-2 orders of magni-
tude compared to conventional Si quantum wells [19].
The oscillation wavelength needed for SOC enhancement,
λGe ≈1.6 nm, has already been demonstrated experi-
mentally [17, 20], and could therefore enable fast EDSR
without micromagnets. However, Ge forms a disordered
random alloy in Si, which has not been fully accounted
for in the current SOC theory, although it is known to
play a prominent role in the physics of Si/SiGe quantum
dots [21–26]. Therefore, the effect of alloy disorder on
EDSR implementations still remains an important open
question.
In this work, we show that random-alloy disorder has
two main effects on EDSR in a WW. First, it causes
spatial variations of the valley phase ϕs,s, defined below,
which lead to spatial variations of the conventional EDSR
Rabi frequency, Ωorb. Second, alloy disorder brings a new
contribution to the Rabi frequency, Ωv, arising from SOC
and the presence of “valley dipoles,” which describe the
hybridization of the ground and excited valley states in
response to an EDSR driving field. It is important to
note that the center positions of these valley states are
shifted due to valley-orbit coupling [27], as illustrated
in Fig. 1(d); the valley-dipole ESDR driving mechanism
therefore cannot occur in the absence of alloy disorder.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, we de-
scribe our theoretical model for EDSR in the WW archi-
tecture. In Sec. III, we derive the effective EDSR theory
encompassing just the orbital and valley dipole terms.
We also derive statistical distribution functions for valley-
coupling matrix elements of importance for orbital and
valley EDSR mechanisms, with details given in the Ap-
pendices. In Sec. IV, we present results for the spatially
varying Rabi frequencies caused by alloy disorder. We
also present results for dephasing times, dephasing rates,

are minimized. In Sec. V, we account for the out-of-plane
component of electric-field noise and we demonstrate the
robustness of the sweet spots to this noise contribution.
Finally, in Sec. VI, we conclude.
II.
MODEL
The bulk band structure of Si is six-fold degenerate,
with conduction-band valleys located near the X, Y , and
Z points of the Brillouin zone [30]. Here, we consider
the Si/SiGe WW heterostructure shown schematically in
Fig. 1(a). The strain-relaxed SiGe barrier layers induce
tensile strain in the quantum well that partially lifts the
valley degeneracy, leaving only two low-energy, degener-
ate Z valleys at the bottom of the conduction band [31].
We label these valley basis states |+z⟩and |−z⟩.
We consider the Hamiltonian H = H0 + Hv, expressed
in terms of its intravalley (H0) and intervalley (Hv) parts.

suppressed.

VI.
CONCLUSION
In this work, we have assessed the feasibility of us-
ing long-period WWs as a platform for implementing
high-quality single-qubit gates. Due to the presence of
Ge throughout the structure, random-alloy disorder be-
comes a key feature that affects all aspects of EDSR op-
erations.
Here, we have highlighted two main effects.
First, the conventional Rabi driving term (Ωorb) is spa-
tially randomized by fluctuations of the valley phase, ow-
ing to Dresselhaus SOC being an intervalley process [see
Eq. (14)]. Second, we have identified an important new
Rabi driving mechanism (Ωv), enabled by the emergence
of valley dipoles, which is also randomized by random-
alloy disorder [see Eq. (15)].
Despite the prominence
of disorder in this system, we have demonstrated that
fast, high-quality Rabi oscillations can be achieved across
most of the heterostructure, without introducing micro-
magnets.
We have performed extensive simulations of the ef-
fects of random-alloy disorder on EDSR using the known
statistical properties of valley-coupling fluctuations [19,
21, 22], while extending those theories to include valley
dipoles.
In this way, we have mapped out the valley
splitting and valley phase, and the corresponding Rabi
frequency, across typical WW heterostructures. We have
also considered the effects of electric-field fluctuations,
both in-plane and out-of-plane, that can reduce EDSR
gate fidelities, due to the presence of spatial gradients of
the total Rabi frequency, Ω= Ωorb + Ωv. This allows
us to derive spatial maps of the coherence time T2,Rabi,
which is determined by charge-noise, and the correspond-
ing quality factor QRabi = ΩT2,Rabi.
These maps al-
low us to identify and analyze optimal working points or
sweet spots, where EDSR gates are relatively insensitive
to electric-field fluctuations, with any spatial orientation.
Our analytical models indicate that these sweet spots oc-
cur where Ωorb contributions to the Rabi frequency are
strong and the valley phase approaches an integer multi-
ple of nπ. We also identify locations that are unsuitable
for EDSR, due to their proximity to valley vortices, where
the valley splitting is very small.
Overall, we find that, although valley dipoles suppress

the very-highest quality factors, we still expect to ob-
serve fast EDSR gates and large QRabi factors over most
of the spatial landscape.
Higher-quality gates can be
achieved by identifying optimal working locations. The
first step to achieving high-fidelity gates is therefore to
map out the spatial structure of the intervalley coupling,
as demonstrated in recent qubit shuttling experiments

In the main text, we compare the effects of in-plane

electron.

A standard method for solving the electrostatics prob-
lem of charge screening, in the presence of metal and
dielectric layers, is the method of images. For the case of
multiple dielectric layers, in principle, an infinite number
of image charges is needed. Here, we adopt a particularly
efficient method for solving this problem, as described in
Ref. [49].
In this case, the image charge locations are
calculated recursively in each dielectric layer, to satisfy
a certain boundary condition. For our geometry, we find
that, although increasing the number of image charges
provides increasingly accurate answers, the qualitative
and quantitative answer remains similar to that from a
simple dipole, where an image charge of equal magni-
tude and separation is placed on the opposite side of the
screening gate, as is typically assumed in the literature
[38, 50].
