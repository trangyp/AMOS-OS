Abstract
We study the asymptotic behavior of widely used tests for evaluating and comparing
predictive accuracy when forecast errors exhibit heavy tails. In particular, when loss
differentials have infinite variance, the Diebold–Mariano test statistic converges to a
nonstandard limit involving non-Gaussian stable random variables. As a consequence,
conventional critical values can yield severely distorted inference: a nominal 5% test
may reject a true null as often as 70% of the time. To establish these results, we develop
a new stable limit theorem for strongly mixing, infinite-variance time series processes.
Building on this theory, we consider subsampling-based inference that remains valid
irrespective of tail-heaviness and requires no estimation of long-run variances or tail
indices. An application to risk forecasts for emerging-market exchange rates shows
that accounting for heavy tails can substantially alter conclusions about predictive
performance relative to standard procedures.
Keywords: Predictive ability, hypothesis testing, heavy tails, stable limit theory, subsam-
pling.

Introduction
The Diebold-Mariano (DM) test of Diebold and Mariano (1995) and the superior predic-
tive ability (SPA) test of Hansen (2005) are workhorse tools for evaluating and comparing
forecasts in economics and finance. They are routinely used to assess volatility and risk
forecasts, to compare macroeconomic forecasts across models and institutions, and to select
among asset-pricing and term-structure models. The asymptotic justification for both tests
rests on a central limit theorem (CLT) that requires loss differentials to have finite variance.
This justification is at odds with a basic stylized fact about the data.
Many of the
underlying variables – asset returns, exchange rate fluctuations, and measures of financial
volatility – have power-law (heavy) tails with index κ > 0, and the empirically relevant range
routinely includes κ < 2, corresponding to infinite (or undefined) variance; see, e.g., Gabaix
(2009) and Ibragimov et al. (2015, Section 1.2). If extreme tail realizations are not fully
captured by a forecast series, the corresponding forecast errors and loss differentials inherit
such tails.
The mismatch has substantial consequences for inference. In a simulation experiment,
we find that a standard DM test at the 5% nominal level can reject a true null as often
as 70% of the time, irrespective of the sample size, under empirically plausible heavy-tailed
processes. In our empirical application to risk forecasts for emerging-market foreign exchange
rates – where the loss differentials appear to have infinite variance – the standard DM
test would lead a researcher to conclude that a sophisticated GARCH-based risk model
statistically dominates each of nine competing forecasts.
A heavy-tail-robust alternative
reaches a substantively different conclusion: simple non-parametric rolling-window forecasts
cannot be rejected as inferior to any of the more sophisticated GARCH-based or score-driven
competitors.

the mean of a time series (Xt)t=1,...,n, where Xt is a forecast error, a forecast loss, a loss

(2023).
This paper makes three main contributions. First, we derive the limiting distribution
of Tn under H0 when (Xt)t=1,...,n is potentially heavy-tailed. When Xt has tail index κ <
2, Tn converges in distribution to a ratio given in terms of two dependent non-Gaussian
stable random variables whose joint distribution is fully characterized by κ and the extremal
dependence structure of (Xt)t=1,...,n. The resulting ratio may be skewed, and its quantiles
can differ substantially from those of the standard normal.
Second, to establish this result, we develop a novel stable limit theorem for strongly mixing
processes under infinite (or potentially undefined) variance, providing a natural extension of
the aforementioned CLT to heavy-tailed processes. Our theorem (Theorem 2.6) only imposes
regular variation, a mild anti-clustering condition on extremes, and weak rates on mixing
coefficients. These conditions hold for a range of well-known linear and non-linear processes
– including autoregressive, GARCH, and stochastic volatility models – and the theorem
should be of independent interest to researchers working with heavy-tailed time series more
broadly.
Third, we consider an inferential procedure that is valid under both finite- and infinite-
variance regimes.
We construct critical values by subsampling (Politis et al., 1999) and
establish their asymptotic validity using our new limit theory. The procedure is fully data-
driven: it does not require knowledge of κ, the convergence rate of the sample mean, or
consistent estimation of long-run variances. The same approach yields confidence intervals
for E[Xt], which may itself be of interest in forecast evaluation.
Beyond these three contributions, we develop two further results. We extend our analysis
to the SPA test of Hansen (2005), which addresses the more demanding problem of deciding
whether a benchmark forecast is dominated by any one of m competing forecasts. We provide
a multivariate version of our limit theory and use it to derive the limiting distribution of the
SPA test statistic under heavy tails. The result lays the groundwork for a heavy-tail-robust
SPA test based on subsampling. This complements the conditional SPA test of Li et al.
(2021), which, like the original, assumes finite higher-order moments.
Finally, we consider alternatives under which E[|Xt|] is infinite. Such alternatives have

Our work is most closely related to the working paper by Kim et al. (2021), who also
consider the DM test under heavy tails. Kim et al. (2021) obtain a non-Gaussian stable
limit for the DM statistic by direct application of Davis (1983) and Davis and Hsing (1995)
and propose subsampling for inference, invoking the validity result of Kokoszka and Wolf
(2004). They additionally analyze how the tail index of the loss differential depends on
the tail indices of the target variable, the forecasts, and the loss function, and illustrate
empirically the relevance of subsampling for realized volatility forecast comparisons. Our
analysis differs in three respects: (i) building upon Matsui et al. (2025b), we provide a new
stable limit theorem for strongly mixing processes under assumptions strictly more general
than those used in Kim et al. (2021), McElroy and Politis (2002), and Kokoszka and Wolf
(2004), and we verify these assumptions for a range of standard linear and nonlinear time
series processes; (ii) our analysis extends beyond the EPA setup to forecast encompassing,
optimality, and SPA testing, as well as alternatives under which the mean may fail to exist;
and (iii) we provide rigorous arguments for the asymptotic validity of the subsampling-based
tests.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the stable limit
theory and the resulting limiting distribution of Tn (Proposition 2.7). Section 3 presents
the subsampling algorithm and establishes its asymptotic validity. Section 4 contains the
extensions to alternatives with undefined mean and to the SPA test. Sections 5 and 6 report
the simulation experiments and the empirical illustration. The proof of Theorem 2.6 and its
multivariate extension is given in the Appendix; additional proofs and simulation results are
in the Supplemental Appendix.

2.1
Set-up and preview of main result
Given a random sample (Xt)t=1,...,n with a time-invariant mean, we consider testing the
hypothesis H0 in (1.1) against HA : E[Xt] ̸= 0 with E[|Xt|] < ∞.
Following much of
the existing literature on forecast evaluation and comparisons, we throughout take Xt as a
primitive. Depending on the construction of Xt, the hypothesis test resembles those often
encountered in the forecasting literature. We begin with three motivating examples.
Example 2.1 (Forecast Optimality) Let (ft)t=1,...,n denote a forecast series of a time
series (Yt)t=1,...,n, and let et = Yt −ft denote the forecast error. The forecast is said to be
unbiased, if E[et] = 0, and it is said to be efficient if E[etft] = 0; see, e.g., Odendahl et al.
(2023, p. 6). Hence, forecast unbiasedness or forecast efficiency corresponds to the hypothesis
H0 in (1.1) when Xt is given by et or etft, respectively. In both cases, Xt may be heavy-tailed
if the forecast errors are heavy-tailed.
♦
Example 2.2 (Forecast Encompassing) Let (f1,t)t=1,...,n and (f2,t)t=1,...,n denote two fore-

i = 1, 2. Following Harvey et al. (1998), forecast series 1 is said to encompass forecast se-

Xt = (e1,t −e2,t) e1,t, which may be heavy-tailed whenever at least one of the forecast errors
are.
♦

Conclusion
Many key variables in economics and finance exhibit heavy-tailed behavior, and the forecast
errors and loss differentials derived from them can inherit this feature. We show that stan-
dard tools for forecast evaluation, including the widely used Diebold–Mariano test, can be
severely unreliable in such settings: under empirically plausible heavy-tailed data-generating
processes, a test at the 5% nominal level can reject a true null of equal predictive ability as
often as 70% of the time. The root cause is that the standard normal critical values are jus-
tified by a finite-variance central limit theorem that breaks down when the loss differentials
have infinite variance.
To address this, we develop a novel stable limit theorem for strongly mixing time series
processes under infinite (or potentially undefined) variance, extending the classical central
limit theorem to the heavy-tailed setting. Building on this result, we derive the limiting
distribution of the test statistic, which is a ratio of dependent non-Gaussian random variables.
The quantiles of this ratio can differ markedly from those of a standard normal distribution.
We then consider a subsampling-based inferential procedure that is valid regardless of tail-
heaviness and requires no prior knowledge of the tail index, the convergence rate of the
sample mean, or estimation of long-run variances.
An empirical application to risk forecasts for emerging-market foreign exchange rates,
where the loss differentials appear to have infinite variance, illustrates the practical relevance:
the standard Diebold–Mariano test and the subsampling-based test yield substantively dif-
ferent conclusions about equal predictive ability. In particular, simple rolling-window risk
forecasts are not found to be inferior to forecasts based on more sophisticated GARCH
and score-driven models once the heavy-tailed nature of the loss differentials is properly
accounted for.

we have that there exists an almost surely unique, stationary, causal solution (log(σt))t∈Z to
(F.5). It follows that there exists an almost surely unique, stationary causal solution (Xt)t∈Z,
to (F.2). By Carrasco and Chen (2002, Proposition 2) it holds that (log(σt))t∈Z is strongly
mixing with geometric decay. Likewise, since σt is a measurable function of log(σt), (σt)t∈Z
is strongly mixing with the same decay. By arguments given by Mikosch and Wintenberger
(2024, pp. 325–326), the same mixing properties hold for (Xt)t∈Z.
Regular variation of
(Xt)t∈Z follows by Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024, Proposition 5.3.1). The properties in
(2.9)–(2.10) follow by arguments in Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024, Section 9.3.1).
Remark F.3 Condition 5. of Proposition F.2 requires that σt has a finite moment of some
order p exceeding the tail index κ > 0. For β ̸= 0 this moment condition does not necessarily
hold as log(σt) itself may be regularly varying; e.g., Mikosch and Wintenberger (2024, Theo-
rem 5.6.9). In the case where β = 0, log(σt) is a stationary AR(1) process (under condition
2.), and, for instance, with ut Gaussian, it holds that σt is log-normal with moments of any
order being finite.
