ABSTRACT
Hypergraphs provide a principled framework for modeling polyadic interactions, with applications in
recommendation systems, social networks, and molecular modeling. Hypergraph generation remains
challenging because incidence structures are discrete, sparse, and governed by heterogeneous higher-
order interactions. Existing generators often rely on implicit latent spaces or continuous incidence
decoders, which provide limited mechanistic interpretation of how node-hyperedge incidences
arise. To address these limitations, we propose HYVINT, an intensity-driven hypergraph generative
framework. Our key innovations are twofold: (i) we develop an intensity-driven incidence formation
mechanism for hypergraphs that links latent interaction strength to binary incidence, and (ii) we
derive a tractable lower-bound variational estimator for learning latent representations. We provide
generation error bounds with asymptotic convergence rates and empirically show that HYVINT
achieves strong fidelity while maintaining substantial novelty and diversity on synthetic and real-
world hypergraphs.

Introduction
Hypergraphs provide a powerful topological framework for modeling higher-order interactions across diverse domains,
from social network analysis to biological computing [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. However, in the study of hypergraphs,
generative modeling remains a frontier challenge [8]. Unlike ordinary graphs, hypergraphs are characterized by intrinsic
discreteness, extreme sparsity, and high-order structural heterogeneity [9, 10, 11], which make it difficult for traditional
generative models to faithfully capture their structures [12]. Furthermore, challenge lies in not only fitting structure but
also accounting for the mechanisms through which hypergraphs emerge [13]. Recently, AI and probabilistic modeling
approaches are increasingly being explored to tackle the challenges, and they can be grouped into three paradigms.
Adversarial and Optimization-based Approaches. Early deep generative models primarily framed hypergraph gener-
ation as an adversarial learning problem or a joint structure-predictor optimization task. Works such as HGGAN [14]
and MRL-AHF [15] adapt Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) to synthesize multimodal connectivity, utilizing
hyperedge-level inductive biases to align distributions. Parallel efforts in structure learning, like those by Cai et al. [16],
treat generation as a byproduct of optimizing topology for downstream robustness. While these methods established the
feasibility of deep generation, they rely heavily on implicit neural parameterization and minimax games, which are
notoriously difficult to stabilize and lack transparent generative mechanics.
Deep Probabilistic and Latent Space Models. To improve scalability and fidelity, the field has consolidated around
probabilistic frameworks that decompose the generation process or utilize diffusion processes. Autoregressive models
like DGMH [17] factorize the likelihood into sequential decisions, while projection-based methods like HyperPLR [18]
map hypergraphs to weighted graphs for reconstruction. More recently, diffusion-based generators have become
dominant. For instance, HYGENE [19] operates on bipartite representations, and DDE [20] performs denoising within a

LLM-driven Semantic Generation. The most recent paradigm shifts focus toward semantic control by integrating
Large Language Models (LLMs). Approaches such as LLMHG [21] and HyperLLM [22] leverage the reasoning
capabilities of LLMs to construct explainable hyperedges or simulate multi-agent formation processes. While these
methods introduce valuable semantic interpretability and zero-shot capabilities, they typically operate by prompting for
high-level patterns rather than strictly adhering to topological distributions. Consequently, they often struggle with
precise constraint satisfaction as scale increases, suggesting that semantic guidance alone cannot replace a rigorous
structural generative backbone.
However, most of the above methods remain subject to significant limitations. On the one hand, neural-based generators
often suffer from optimization instability and offer limited interpretability [23, 24]. On the other hand, probabilistic
modeling approaches, such as DDE [20], commonly model hypergraph incidence structures using linear generators
in Euclidean space [25]. More broadly, many latent-space formulations for hypergraphs are node-position-based or
directly score incidences from continuous embeddings, without explicitly separating node-side activity, hyperedge-side
activity, and their compatibility. Such modeling typically necessitates a continuous link function (e.g., a sigmoid)
to map Euclidean scores to valid incidence probabilities. While convenient, mapping Euclidean scores to incidence
probabilities via a link function typically induces sparsity and higher-order structure only indirectly through the link
choice and regularization, instead of specifying an explicit intensity-driven incidence formation mechanism [26, 27].
We reframe the task of hypergraph generation by focusing on the underlying mechanism rather than the static incidence
structure. Specifically, we employ latent variable modeling to induce the incidence structure. The intuition is drawn
from real-world scenarios, such as co-authorship hypergraphs [28], where nodes represent authors and hyperedges
represent papers. As Figure 1 shows, we consider, for example, an illustrative case with authors A, B, C, D and three
papers e1, e2, e3, where researcher D is an author of e2 and e3, but not e1. We view these incidences as induced from
latent interactions. From this perspective, D’s absence from e1 implies that the number of effective interactions (e.g.,
discussions, contributions) regarding e1 was zero. In contrast, for e2 and e3, D engaged in non-zero interactions, where
a higher interaction frequency naturally correlates with a higher probability of co-authorship.

To formalize this intuition, we consider an intensity-driven latent variable modeling approach that explicitly decouples
interaction intensity from binary incidence. Define the generative process as a chain z →λ →x →b. Specifically, a
prior z ∼p(z) encodes latent structural representations, a mechanism λviej ∼pθ(λviej | z) generates non-negative
interaction intensities, and latent interaction counts are sampled as xviej ∼Poisson(λviej). The observed incidence
is obtained by binarizing counts, i.e., bviej = I[xviej > 0], indicating whether node vi participates in hyperedge
ej. This formulation provides the following advantages. First, it specifies how binary incidences arise from latent
interaction strengths, rather than decoding them from unconstrained scores. Second, it provides a natural way to
model sparse incidences through small interaction intensities. Given these considerations, we introduce HYVINT, an
INTensity-driven HYpergraph generative model with structure-preserving Variational representations. Figure 1 provides
an overview of our framework. The key contributions are as follows:
• We develop an intensity-driven incidence formation mechanism that models hypergraph incidences through
latent interaction strengths and a Poisson-induced incidence link.
• We derive a tractable lower-bound variational estimator for learning structure-preserving node and hyperedge
representations, including node activity, hyperedge activity, and latent compatibility.
• We establish generation error bounds with asymptotic rates and empirically evaluate HYVINT in terms of
fidelity, novelty, and diversity on synthetic and real-world hypergraphs.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 details the HYVINT methodology. Section 3 presents
theoretical insights. Section 4 validates HYVINT empirically on synthetic and real-world datasets. Section 5 concludes
with a discussion, outlining future directions. Proofs and supplementary materials are deferred to the appendix.

Baselines.
We compare HYVINT with five representative baselines, covering both classical and recent state-of-the-art
hypergraph generators. Ber-Diff [29, 41] is a classical diffusion baseline for binary data, applying diffusion directly
to binary membership vectors via Bernoulli-type transitions. Gau-Diff [30] follows the canonical DDPM framework
and performs diffusion in a continuous Gaussian space. NMF-Diff [42] adopts nonnegative matrix factorization as
a representation learner and then couples it with diffusion to generate hypergraphs. DDE [20] is a recent diffusion-
based approach tailored to hypergraph generation, where diffusion is applied to Euclidean hyperedge embeddings and
hypergraphs are generated from the denoised embeddings. HYGENE [19] is a diffusion-based generator that operates
directly on the bipartite representation through a progressive local expansion scheme. We provide the details of these
five baselines in Appendix B.
Evaluation metrics.
In the simulation study, we use two evaluation metrics: RMSEmean and RMSEcov. Intuitively,
RMSEmean compares the average node co-occurrence patterns, while RMSEcov evaluates whether the model also
reproduces the variability and dependency structure of these co-occurrences across hyperedges. We provide their
definitions in Appendix C.
Simulation settings.
We consider latent dimensions K ∈{2, 4, 8} and vary the hypergraph size with n ∈
{200, 400, 800} nodes and m ∈{200, 400, 800} hyperedges. To model heterogeneity, we fix the node-side pa-
rameters αvi to be sampled from Unif[0, 1], while the hyperedge-side parameters ρej are independently sampled from
either Unif[0, 1] or Unif[1, 2]. Since ρej affects λviej monotonically through Eq. (3), sampling ρej ∼Unif[1, 2] results
in systematically larger intensities, leading to denser hypergraphs than ρej ∼Unif[0, 1]. For each setting, we repeat the
experiment 20 times with independent random seeds and report the mean and standard deviation of the results. We
provide more details such as hyperparameter configuration, fairness protocol and running time in Appendix D.
Main results.
Results are provided in Table 10–Table 14. Across the majority of simulation regimes, HYVINT
consistently attains the lowest RMSEmean and RMSEcov, indicating superior accuracy in recovering both first and
second-order statistics. Exceptions occur only in a few corner cases, where DDE yields slightly smaller errors, e.g., the
setting under K = 4 with ρej ∈[0, 1], m = n = 200, as well as the setting under K = 8 with ρej ∈[1, 2], m = 200
and n = 800. More details can be found in Appendix E.
Synthetic fairness.
To verify that HYVINT’s advantage is not an artifact of model-data alignment, we regenerate
synthetic hypergraphs under DDE’s sigmoid-based mechanism (see Appendix A) and re-evaluate HYVINT on all
simulation configurations. Tables 1–3 report the results under both data-generating schemes for M ∈{200, 400, 800}
respectively. Across all settings, HYVINT’s performance under the sigmoid-generated data remains close to that under
the Poisson-generated data, confirming that the observed gains are not driven by the alignment between the generator
and the evaluation data.

Real data
Datasets.
We adopt three representative open-source hypergraph datasets curated by Benson et al. [43]. These
datasets include email-Enron, contact-primary-school, and NDC-substances. They cover different application domains
and scales. The email-Enron dataset consists of 148 nodes and 10885 hyperedges. In this network, nodes represent
email addresses and a hyperedge connects the sender with all recipients. The contact-primary-school dataset involves
242 nodes representing individuals such as students and teachers, which contains 106879 hyperedges derived from
sensor-recorded group interactions. Finally, NDC-substances includes n = 5311 chemical substances and m = 112405
drugs, where each drug is modeled as a hyperedge connecting its ingredient set. We provide additional details in
Appendix F.
Experimental setup.
In all real-world experiments, we consider latent dimensions K ∈{2, 4, 8} and evaluate
hypergraph generation on three benchmarks, namely email-Enron, contact-primary-school, and NDC-substances. Since
real-world hypergraphs often exhibit highly complex and heterogeneous topology, we adopt a more comprehensive set
of evaluation metrics to assess generation quality from multiple perspectives based on Wasserstein-1 distance [19], i.e.,
LDeg, LSize, LSpec, LC
Cent, LH
Cent, LB
Cent. They are detailed in Appendix G. HYVINT and DDE have comparable parameter
counts: both require O((m + n)K) graph-specific parameters and share the same DDPM backbone (see Appendix D
for a detailed comparison). Following standard practice, we apply a simple post-processing filter to remove generated
hyperedges with fewer than two nodes before evaluation. In our experiments, fewer than 1% of sampled hyperedges are
filtered, and this step has negligible effect on all reported metrics (LSize and LSpec change by less than 0.001).
Main results.
As Tables 4–6 show, HYVINT consistently achieves the best overall generation quality across latent
dimensions. Specifically, by introducing a latent counting process and linking bviej to an underlying intensity λviej,
HYVINT better preserves higher-order structure reflected by the degree and centrality distributions, while remaining
competitive on hyperedge size statistics. Moreover, the diffusion module operates in a low-dimensional hyperedge
embedding space, which stabilizes learning and improves robustness to the choice of K, as evidenced by strong
performance from K = 2 to K = 8. Finally, HYVINT yields the best spectral fidelity at larger K, suggesting that it
captures global connectivity patterns of the clique expansion more accurately than baseline diffusion variants and matrix
factorization-based generators. We further analyze the effect of K in Appendix I: HYVINT improves monotonically
with K, while DDE degrades for K > 2 due to the difficulty of regularizing high-dimensional CMLE embeddings.

Ablation study.
We further conduct ablations along two axes to isolate the contribution of each design choice:
(a) the prior scale, by varying the Gamma prior hyperparameters over {0.1, 0.5, 1.0}, and (b) the treatment of the
intractable ELBO term (i), where we denote our proposed lower-bound-based estimator in Section 2 as HYVINT,
and a standard second-order Taylor approximation as HYVINTT. We conduct these ablations on email-Enron and
contact-primary-school with K ∈{2, 4, 8}. Table 23 reports results on email-Enron with K ∈{2, 4, 8}. Overall,
HYVINTT can be competitive on a subset of metrics under certain priors, but its behavior is less consistent across prior
settings. In contrast, HYVINT yields more stable performance patterns across metrics. Despite being less stable than
HYVINT across prior settings, HYVINTT can still achieve strong generation quality, since the two variants differ
only in how the intractable ELBO term is estimated, while the remaining components (ii)-(iv) are shared. Moreover, the
choice of prior scale has a noticeable impact on the generative behavior. More details can be found in Appendix K. We
further compare variational inference against point-estimate representations for the downstream diffusion model in
Appendix K, confirming that distributional latent representations improve generation quality. Additionally, we replace
the DDPM module with a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) and find that GMM achieves comparable performance for
K ≤8 (Appendix K), indicating that HYVINT’s gains are not specific to the diffusion architecture.

Conclusion
In this paper, we propose HYVINT, an intensity-driven framework for hypergraph generation. The central idea is to
model latent interaction intensities that induce binary node-hyperedge memberships through a Poisson link. This gives
an explicit intensity-driven incidence formation mechanism, while the proposed lower-bound variational estimator
makes the mechanism practical for learning node activity, hyperedge activity, and latent compatibility. On the theoretical
side, our analysis separates the generation error into node-side estimation, latent factor distribution, and diffusion
approximation components, and establishes a new bound for the first component. Through extensive evaluations, we
demonstrate that HYVINT achieves strong fidelity while maintaining substantial novelty and diversity on synthetic and
real-world hypergraphs.
There are several possible directions for future work. First, HYVINT does not exactly enforce hard cardinality
constraints, and constrained decoding may be useful when exact hyperedge sizes are required. Second, the present
incidence link may be less precise for role-based heterophily or intra-edge anti-correlation, where node inclusions
within the same hyperedge would interact more explicitly. Extending the framework with dependence-aware incidence
mechanisms is therefore a promising direction. Finally, adapting HYVINT to temporal and attributed hypergraphs may
further broaden its applicability in real-world systems.

HYGENE
HYGENE [19] is a diffusion-based hypergraph generator that operates on a multi-resolution representation
of the hypergraph via progressive coarsening and expansion. The core idea is to build a hierarchy of structurally
simplified hypergraphs, train a diffusion model at the coarsest level, and then progressively refine the structure back to
the original resolution.
Dual representation. A hypergraph H = (Vn, Em) is represented in two complementary forms. The first is the weighted
clique expansion GC = (Vn, EC, WC), where each hyperedge ej induces a clique among its constituent nodes, with
edge weights wuv = P
j:u,v∈ej 1/|ej|. The second is the star expansion (bipartite representation) GB = (VL, VR, EB),
where VL corresponds to the n original nodes, VR corresponds to the m hyperedges, and an edge (vL, vR) ∈EB
indicates vL ∈evR. A key spectral property underpinning the coarsening procedure is that the Bolla Laplacian
LH = DV −HD−1
E H⊤of the hypergraph coincides with the unnormalized Laplacian of the weighted clique expansion
GC, ensuring that coarsening GC preserves the spectral information of H.
Multi-resolution coarsening. Starting from the original hypergraph H(L) = H, HYGENE constructs a hierarchy
of coarsened hypergraphs H(L−1), . . . , H(0) by recursively merging nodes and hyperedges. At each level ℓ, a graph
coarsening algorithm is applied to G(ℓ)
C to select contraction sets—pairs or small groups of neighboring nodes that
are merged into a single super-node. Correspondingly, hyperedges incident to the merged nodes are combined: each
contraction set involves at most three hyperedges, which are merged into a single super-hyperedge. The coarsest
hypergraph H(0) consists of a single super-node and a small set of super-hyperedges, providing a compact summary of
the original structure. Both the clique expansion G(ℓ)
C and the bipartite representation G(ℓ)
B are maintained at each level.
Diffusion on the coarsest representation. At the coarsest level ℓ= 0, the hypergraph reduces to a small bipartite
graph G(0)
B whose structure is learned by a denoising diffusion probabilistic model. The denoising network is a Provably
Powerful Graph Network (PPGN), which operates directly on the bipartite topology and is more expressive than standard
message-passing GNNs. The forward diffusion process gradually corrupts the bipartite adjacency matrix with Gaussian
noise over T steps, while the reverse process is trained to iteratively denoise, predicting the node membership vector
vL ∈N|VL| (number of nodes to include per original node group) and the hyperedge membership vector vR ∈N|VR|
(number of hyperedges to retain).
Expansion and generation. Given a trained diffusion model at level 0, generation proceeds from coarse to fine.
Starting from random noise at the coarsest level, the reverse diffusion process produces a clean bipartite structure for
H(0). HYGENE then expands this structure level by level: at each step ℓ→ℓ+ 1, the expansion operator maps each
super-node back to its constituent nodes and each super-hyperedge back to its constituent hyperedges, using learned
expansion rules that predict the fine-grained connectivity. The process repeats until the original resolution ℓ= L is
recovered, yielding the generated hypergraph e
H. Compared to methods that generate hyperedges independently in a
single step, HYGENE’s hierarchical approach explicitly models the structural dependencies across resolutions, enabling
the generated hypergraph to better capture multi-scale topological patterns.

Performance Analysis.
Close examination of Table 10 through Table 14 reveals that HYVINT consistently minimizes
both RMSEmean and RMSEcov across the majority of configurations. This indicates that our model succeeds not
only in capturing the marginal probabilities of node participation but also in reconstructing the complex dependency
structures governing node co-occurrences. The gains are particularly pronounced in RMSEcov which is inherently
harder to minimize as it depends on the empirical covariance matrix rather than the simple mean matrix. The robustness
of HYVINT is evident even in the denser regime where it avoids the common pitfall of over-smoothing interactions.
This stability likely stems from our intensity-based formulation where the nonlinear mapping 1−exp(−λviej) naturally
interpolates between sparse and dense limits. Furthermore, HYVINT preserves hypergraph variability by explicitly
modeling heterogeneity via multiplicative factors and by training the diffusion process directly on the variational
parameters. This approach ensures that the distributional spread of the hyperedge factors is faithfully reproduced and
leads to superior second-order matching.
Robustness to Hyperedge Heterogeneity.
The two heterogeneity regimes essentially function as a stress test for
model calibration. In the sparser regime where intensities hover near zero, the link function operates in a near-linear
region. Conversely, the denser regime pushes the model towards saturation and makes covariance matching significantly
more challenging. While continuous diffusion methods relying on simple thresholding often struggle to adapt to these
shifts, HYVINT remains stable across both settings. We attribute this to the smooth saturation properties of the intensity
link which mitigates sensitivity to latent perturbations. While the precise performance advantage is modulated by the
interplay between the scaling of parameters and the embedding inner products, the results suggest a strong intrinsic
capacity for handling diverse structural densities.
Scaling with Hypergraph Size.
We observe a general convergence trend where both error metrics decline as m
increases for a fixed n. Since the metrics rely on empirical averages over hyperedges, a larger sample size m naturally
suppresses Monte Carlo variance in the reference statistics. Moreover, a larger pool of training hyperedges facilitates
more accurate estimation of the underlying latent factors and stabilizes the diffusion training. However, when n is scaled
up while keeping m fixed, the inference problem becomes increasingly under-determined as the dimensionality of the
membership vectors grows without a corresponding increase in observed samples. This is reflected in higher standard
deviations for settings with large n and small m. While HYVINT proves robust in most large-scale scenarios, it is not
entirely immune to these finite-sample effects and exhibits slightly higher variance when the number of observations is
disproportionately low relative to the node count.
Impact of Latent Dimension.
The latent dimension K dictates the expressivity of the generative model. While
a higher K allows for richer representations, it also exacerbates the ill-posed nature of the inverse problem given
finite data. Empirically, increasing K generally makes covariance matching more arduous as the variability in co-
occurrence patterns must be resolved across more latent directions. Despite this challenge, HYVINT retains competitive
performance at dimension 8. This suggests that our variational framework and diffusion components scale effectively
with model complexity. It is worth noting that at this dimension, the performance gap between methods occasionally
narrows. This phenomenon is consistent with the sample complexity limits inherent to high-dimensional latent variable
learning.

Analysis of Corner Cases.
We identify specific corner cases where DDE marginally outperforms HYVINT, notably
when the latent dimension is 4 with a 200 by 200 grid, and when the dimension is 8 with a 200 by 800 grid under
dense settings. These regimes are characterized by data scarcity relative to model complexity. In such sample-limited
scenarios, the inductive biases of different models play a decisive role. DDE employs constrained maximum likelihood
estimation with explicit identifiability constraints detailed in Appendix B. This effectively acts as a strong regularizer
that reduces variance when data is insufficient. In contrast, HYVINT relies on flexible variational inference. While this
yields richer distributional modeling in general, it can be more susceptible to estimation noise when m is small. This
observation highlights a potential avenue for future work such as integrating stronger priors or shrinkage mechanisms
into the variational objective for data-scarce regimes.
Structural Limitations.
HYVINT also has limitations in representing certain hard structural constraints. Because
incidences are generated through conditionally independent Bernoulli draws given the latent intensities, the model
does not exactly enforce hyperedge cardinality constraints. It may also be less precise when the target hypergraph is
governed by role-based heterophily or by intra-edge anti-correlation, where the inclusion of one node should explicitly
suppress or reshape the inclusion probability of another node within the same hyperedge. Capturing these effects would
require additional constrained decoding or dependence-aware incidence mechanisms beyond the present intensity link.
Computational Limitations.
Finally, we address a practical limitation concerning scalability in extremely dense
regimes. As the effective intensity and expected hyperedge size increase, the number of active node-hyperedge
incidences grows. This inflates the intermediate tensors and memory footprint during training. Consequently, for very
large m, HYVINT may face GPU out-of-memory constraints which is a challenge common to methods requiring the
evaluation of extensive incidence pairs [48]. However, this is an implementation bottleneck rather than a fundamental
flaw. Standard engineering solutions effectively mitigate these issues. Examples include reducing mini-batch sizes,
employing gradient accumulation, or utilizing mixed-precision training [49]. For extremely dense graphs where exact
likelihood evaluation is prohibitive, stochastic approximation via negative subsampling [50] offers a viable path forward.
These strategies ensure HYVINT remains applicable to large-scale data, and we leave a systematic optimization of
these trade-offs for future research.

Dataset
We evaluate hypergraph generation on three real-world benchmarks curated by Austin R. Benson at Cornell University.
The datasets are publicly available at https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~arb/data and were originally introduced
in [43]. These benchmarks are attractive for hypergraph generation because they represent genuine higher-order
interactions rather than pairwise links, and they span markedly different application domains, scales, and structural
regimes.
Specifically, we choose email-Enron, contact-primary-school, and NDC-substances for hypergraph generation. On the
one hand, they provide complementary graph sizes and hyperedge volumes, ranging from a small organizational network
to a large chemical knowledge base. On the other hand, they exhibit different hypergraph structural characteristics,
including variability in hyperedge sizes, heterogeneity in node participation frequencies, and different levels of sparsity.
These properties collectively make the datasets challenging and informative testbeds for assessing the fidelity and
robustness of hypergraph generators.
• The email-Enron dataset models internal email communications within the Enron corporation. Nodes corre-
spond to Enron email addresses. Each email forms one hyperedge that contains the sender and all recipients.
This representation naturally captures broadcast and group communication patterns within a topology. The
dataset contains n = 148 nodes and m = 10885 hyperedges. This type of hypergraph-structured data is
common in real life, and uncovering its topology is useful for anomaly detection, such as spotting abnormal
communication patterns including fraudulent collusion.
• The contact-primary-school dataset records face-to-face proximity contacts in a primary school using wearable
sensors. Nodes represent individuals, including students and teachers. Hyperedges represent group interactions
detected within a short sensing interval and are constructed from simultaneous proximity patterns. This
modeling choice captures higher-order contact events such as small group conversations, classroom interactions,
and playground gatherings. For the size, the dataset contains n = 242 nodes and m = 106879 hyperedges.
From a generative perspective, this dataset is difficult because it combines frequent repeated interactions with
rapidly changing group membership. It also tends to be relatively dense in observed interaction events, which
can amplify error accumulation for models that do not correctly capture the dependence structure among
co-occurring nodes.
• The NDC-substances dataset describes drug products and their constituent substances derived from the
National Drug Code directory. Nodes correspond to chemical substances. Each hyperedge corresponds to
a drug product containing all substances that constitute the drug. This hypergraph captures compositional
structure that is inherently higher-order because a drug is defined by a set of ingredients rather than by
pairwise associations. The dataset contains n = 5311 nodes and m = 112405 hyperedges. This dataset poses
substantial challenges for hypergraph generation due to its scale, its strong sparsity in the node space, and the
long-tailed participation pattern across substances. Despite its difficulties, generating this hypergraph dataset
will be conducive to discovering golden combinations of drug ingredients. It can also enable predicting new
potential drug formulations.
