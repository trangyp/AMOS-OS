Abstract. Density Functional Theory (DFT) is widely used for atom-
istic simulations. However, its reach stays limited due to several limita-
tions such as lack of accurate exchange-correlation functional, require-
ment of costly O(N 3) diagonalization etc. Although quantum computing
offers paths forward, including variational techniques, embedding strate-
gies, and quantum linear solvers, the discussion remains scattered. With-
out shared terms or structure, evaluating progress in hybrid quantum-
classical DFT efforts becomes challenging. To bring order, we introduce
a three-axis scheme based on where the method connects into DFT,
whether the quantum part boosts precision or cuts time, alongside in-
tended device type: current noisy machines or future error-corrected ones.
Sorting known approaches in this way shows why embedding frameworks
fit today’s tools better, while faster linear algebra waits for more ad-
vanced systems.

Introduction
Density functional theory (DFT) is known to balance accuracy and affordability
across numerous applications [14,17]. Yet this balance slips apart under certain
conditions already recognized in the field [4,8,18] etc. Hence, pursuit of modern
methods for improved treatment of problem resolution is active area of research.
Quantum algorithms efficiently represent many electon states which are diffi-
cult to capture classically and hence show promise for resolution of some of DFT’s
significant limitations [2, 16, 26, 28, 34]. Despite algorithmic progress, hardware
limitations and the need for DFT property prediction power push us to hybrid
work flows where DFT and quantum algorithms are used together. Studies link-
ing these two methods are plenty but with unclear methodological separation,
ranging from quantum assisted classical DFT (HQ-DFT)to quantum-native elec-
tronic structure methods where quantum algorithms fully replace DFT.
This paper aims to provide a three dimensional design space for HQ-DFT
approaches: the stage at which quantum computing integrates into the DFT

Motivation for HQ-DFT
The practical success of Density Functional Theory (DFT) is tied to the use of
approximations that enable computational efficiency but introduce systematic
limitations in electronically complex systems. These failures can be categorized
into intrinsic functional errors and computational scaling bottlenecks.
Intrinsic Limitations: The exact exchange-correlation (XC) functional is un-
known. All practical approximations, such as LDA, GGA, and hybrid function-
als, suffer from self-interaction and delocalization errors, which produce incor-
rect charge localization and underestimated band gaps [6, 25]. Furthermore, f-
electron compounds and transition-metal oxides, where Coulomb correlations are
strong, are poorly handled by standard functionals [7]. While Time-Dependent
DFT (TD-DFT) extends the theory to excited states, it fails to accurately cap-
ture charge-transfer excitations and long-time dynamics [21,31].
Computational Bottlenecks: The cost of solving the Kohn-Sham (KS) equa-
tions is dominated by the repeated diagonalization of the Hamiltonian matrix,
which scales as O(N 3) relative to the system size N [11]. Use of hybrid or double-
hybrid functionals, improve accuracy but at a cost of O(N 4) to O(N 5), rendering
them prohibitive for large-scale correlated materials.
For large, correlated systems, neither the functional approximation nor the
computational cost is satisfactory. We in what follows map some possible quan-
tum approaches which help overcome specific DFT limitations.
Exchange–correlation functional improvement: Quantum-generated cor-
relation datasets obtained from methods such as VQE and QPE have been ex-
plored for training machine learning-based exchange–correlation functionals [32].
These approaches remain limited to small molecular systems but may improve
functional accuracy in strongly correlated regimes.
Strong correlation treatment: Hybrid VQE-PDFT frameworks replace
classical multiconfigurational solvers with VQE-based active-space wavefunction
optimization while retaining classical density functional corrections for dynamic
correlation [5]. Applications have been demonstrated for biomolecular electron-
transfer systems.
Localized correlated subsystem treatment: Quantum embedding meth-
ods, including projection-based embedding and DMET-style frameworks, restrict
quantum computation to chemically active fragments embedded within larger
classical environments [29,30]. These methods improve tractability by localizing
expensive correlated calculations.

3.1
Intervention Level
The stage at which quantum resources are integrated into the classical workflow
determines the intercention level. As shown in Figure 2 if quantum resources
improve the Hamiltonian construction, it is Hamiltonian-level intervention; if
they are used only in the eigensolver, it is a solver-level intervention; if they
enter multiple stages of the SCF workflow, it is an embedding-level intervention.
Fig. 2. Intervention levels shown at the SCF workflow
Hamiltonian-level Intervention. Hamiltonian-level interventions are character-
ized by the modification of the core mathematical terms within the electronic
structure problem during or prior to the self-consistent field (SCF) loop. Rather
than treating the quantum processor as a post-processing solver, these methods
utilize quantum-derived correlation data to directly refine the classical Kohn-
Sham (KS) Hamiltonian.
A primary example is the work of Hatcher et al. [13], where a quantum
computer generates high-fidelity exchange-correlation (XC) potentials to ensure
formal exactness within the classical DFT cycle. Similarly, Sheridan et al. [32]
propose Quantum Enhanced DFT (QEDFT), which leverages quantum data to
optimize the underlying parameters of XC functionals for improved accuracy in
noisy regimes. This category also encompasses methodologies that utilize quan-
tum information to refine basis functions [19] or determine exact KS potentials
through epigraphical optimization [3]. Any strategy that employs quantum re-
sources to "correct" or parameterize the classical potential or its operator repre-
sentation belongs to this intervention level, ensuring that the classical mean-field
context is grounded in high-level quantum correlation.
Solver level. Solver-level interventions replace or augment the electronic struc-
ture solver within the SCF procedure. Variational approaches such as VQE are

circuits and hybrid classical optimization [23,26]. In contrast, QPE- and QSVT-
based solvers require deep coherent circuits to achieve high-precision eigenvalues,
putting them in the fault-tolerant regime [1,16].
Embedding-level Intervention. Embedding-level interventions partition the to-
tal electronic system into a localized, strongly correlated quantum region and
a surrounding classical mean-field environment. The theoretical foundation for
such partitioning is provided by Sun et al. [34], who established the frame-
work for Quantum Embedding. By restricting quantum resources to a numer-
ically demanding active subspace, embedding strategies significantly mitigate
qubit counts and circuit depth. Practical applications of this approach include
Density Matrix Embedding Theory (DMET) for pharmacological fragments as
demonstrated by Anurag et al. [36], and the project-based HF/DFT-embedding
schemes developed by Rossmannek et al. [29,30]. This hierarchy allows for sig-
nificant correlation energy recovery in complex systems while maintaining the
scalability of the classical DFT environment, making it the most viable strategy
for large-scale materials with localized strong correlation.
3.2
Functional Role
The quantum component when augmented in DFT pipeline is supposed to serve
some purpose and that purpose is defined by functional role, it may be reduc-
ing computational complexity which we call "Acceleration" or improving the
physical description of the system which we call "Accuracy".
Accuracy enhancement Accuracy-oriented HQ-DFT approaches focus on im-
proving the treatment of electron correlation beyond conventional mean-field
approximations. Variational solvers can capture localized strong-correlation ef-
fects more accurately than standard functionals, particularly in transition-metal
systems where static correlation is dominant [13,32].
Acceleration Acceleration-oriented interventions target numerical bottlenecks
within the classical SCF cycle, such as density matrix purification and the op-
timization of strongly correlated active spaces. While fault-tolerant algorithms
like QSVT provide asymptotic speedups for matrix operations [16], near-term
acceleration is achieved by using quantum solvers to provide high-fidelity ini-
tializations for the classical environment. By solving the localized "hard" corre-
lation problem on a quantum processor, embedding-based acceleration reduces
the number of classical iterations required to reach self-consistency in large-scale
heterogeneous systems [20].
Synergistic Coupling. Accuracy and acceleration are coupled through the nu-
merical stability of the electronic landscape. The balance of accuracy and ac-
celeration is naturally acceptable, but with a physically superior Hamiltonian

3.3
Hardware Regime
The hardware regime determines the algorithmic constraints, specifically the
trade-off between circuit depth and measurement noise.
NISQ Regime we are currently in the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum era
(NISQ). Quantum devices in this era are characterized by noisy gates that
quickly loose coherence. Near-term HQ-DFT studies hence favor shallow varia-
tional circuits where small quantum assistance aids DFT results. [15,26].
FTQC Regime Fault Tolerant Quantum Computers (FTQC) bundle many qubits
together for quantum error correction to achieve one perfect logical qubit. This
architecture will support the deep coherent circuits needed for running QPE
and QSVT which will enable us go beyond the "guess and check" variational
optimization to some real speed up possibility.

5.1
Hardware Landscape and Execution Fidelity
The transition from NISQ-simulation to hardware execution is characterized by
a high platform diversity. Recent implementations demonstrate the scalability
of the Embedding role across superconducting architectures, including 4–8 qubit
stochastic DMET on IQM (Google Rainbow) [36], 10-qubit surface reaction stud-
ies on IBMQ Guadalupe [12], and 30-qubit sample-based diagonalization on the
127-qubit IBM Sherbrooke (Eagle R3) [24]. Notably, the Hamiltonian level re-
mains viable for smaller registers, such as the 16-qubit VQE-DFT correction on
IQM Sirius [32]. However, circuit depth remains a critical constraint.
The above identified hardware executions implies that the near-term viability
of HQ-DFT is not solely a function of qubit count, but of the integration effi-
ciency between classical and quantum kernels. If the software interface cannot
handle high-frequency data interchange with minimal latency, the theoretical
gains of a quantum solver are lost to classical communication overhead. This
necessitates a transition from hardware-centric analysis to an evaluation of Soft-
ware Readiness, where the ’maturity’ of a hybrid framework is defined by its
ability to manage numerical conditioning and data-transfer bottlenecks in the
NISQ era."

6.1
Implementation Realities and Key Trade-offs
The transition from software selection to execution reveals fundamental trade-
offs. Literature studies and our empirical benchmarks in Section 5 confirm that
HQ-DFT is governed by the following constraints:
Accuracy vs. Circuit Depth: High-fidelity result needs and expressive
ansätze or larger active spaces, either of them increases gate depth. In the NISQ
regime (eg ≈10−3), addition of more gates for accuracy adds more noise which
negate any accuracy gain.
Functional-Induced perception of acceleration in Embedding [22]:
we find synergy between the exchange functional and solver latency. As demon-
strated by the reduction in accumulated VQE time for Pyridine (from 22,049s to
44s) due to sooner convergence. High-fidelity physical modeling aids secondary
acceleration demonstrated.
Measurement Tax vs. Wall-clock Time: Quantum solvers have to re-
peat measurements with number of shots in order to achieve chemical accuracy
(ϵ ≈1 mHa). This negates the theoretical speedup of quantum solvers for small
systems, shifting the near-term value proposition toward Secondary Accelera-
tion—reducing classical SCF iterations through superior initial guesses [9].
Fragment Size vs. Qubit Budget: In embedding-level approaches, frag-
ment size decides the accuracy gain mostly and scales linearly or quadratically
with qubit count needed. One has to balance accuracy via smaller fragment size
to fit within the available qubit budget [29,36].
7
Conclusion
Fully quantum-native approaches for electronic structure calculation are also
hybrid in a sense that classical DFT initiation does full handover to quantum
implementation. Apart from these, there are other methods which utilize quan-
tum computation selectively within classical DFT workflow. This work focuses
specially on such DFT-centered, quantum subroutine augmented, HQ-DFT ap-
proaches.

We propose a three dimensional framework for organizing HQ-DFT meth-
ods based on the level at which quantum intervenes the DFT workflow, the
functional role that quantum plays in the pipeline, and the quantum hardware
regime that is used in the augmentation. Synthesis of literature in this structured
framework reveals a preference to "accuracy" role use of quantum. Variational
methods via embedding level intervention via NISQ hardware show prominence
in recent literature in contrast, acceleration-oriented approaches are few and
mostly dependent on FTQC. Our results on performance of projection-based
embedding suggest that Range-Separated Hybrids (RSH) provide the necessary
physical conditioning to mitigate discontinuities (quantum-classical boundaries),
effectively reducing quantum solver latency.
Our proposed framework provides a structured basis for evaluating existing
and any new HQ-DFT approaches and identifying realistic directions for future
development as quantum hardware capabilities continue to mature.
