Abstract 
Clinical decision-making in emergency medicine demands rapid, accurate diagnoses under 
uncertainty. Despite benchmark progress, evidence for LLMs as interactive aids in live physician 
workflows remains sparse. MedSyn lets physicians iteratively query an LLM provided with the full 
clinical record while initially viewing only the chief complaint. Seven physicians (three seniors, four 
residents) completed baseline and AI-assisted sessions across 52 MIMIC-IV cases stratified by 
difficulty. Blinded evaluation showed residents' Hard-case correctness rose from 0.589 to 0.734; 
difficulty-standardised completely-correct rates confirmed a medium effect (Δ = 0.092; p = 0.071; d = 
0.47). Automated metrics corroborated these gains: standardised any-match accuracy improved by 
0.156 (p < 0.0001), and residents showed the largest F1 gain (Δ = 0.138; p < 0.0001). Dialogue 
analysis revealed expertise-dependent strategies (seniors asked targeted, hypothesis-driven 
questions; residents relied on broader queries) and cross-expertise concordance increased (Δ = 
0.145; p < 0.0001). Interactive LLM support meaningfully enhances diagnostic reasoning. 
Introduction 
Clinical decision-making in emergency care is a complex, high-stakes process that requires 
physicians to rapidly synthesize evolving, incomplete, and often ambiguous patient information under 
severe time pressure1. In this setting, clinicians must identify potentially life-threatening conditions 
while managing diagnostic uncertainty, cognitive overload, emotional stress, resource constraints, 
and multiple patients simultaneously2. These demands contribute to diagnostic error, a major source 
of patient harm for which emergency departments account for a disproportionate share of missed or 
delayed diagnoses3. The challenge is further amplified by the growing volume and complexity of 
electronic health record (EHR) data, which increases information overload and detracts from efficient 
diagnostic reasoning4. Such pressures may be particularly burdensome for less experienced 
clinicians, who often have less developed pattern recognition and clinical intuition5. Although


## Page 2


diagnostic decision support systems have been proposed to address these challenges, their adoption 
remains limited by poor EHR integration, lack of interoperability, and clinician distrust6. 
 
Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a promising new approach for supporting clinical 
reasoning7–10. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 83 studies found no significant 
difference in diagnostic accuracy between generative AI and non-expert physicians, whereas AI 
performed significantly worse than expert physicians7. Encouraging results have also been reported in 
specific clinical applications: retrieval-augmented LLM workflows improved emergency department 
triage, referral, and diagnostic performance on 2,000 MIMIC-IV cases8; a two-stage prompting 
framework that explicitly mimics verification in physician reasoning improved diagnostic accuracy by 
up to 5.2% and reduced uncertainty9; and an uncertainty-aware system improved expert recognition 
of diagnostic uncertainty when used collaboratively with clinicians10. However, important limitations 
constrain direct clinical deployment. In simulated consultations, AI chatbots have shown high rates of 
unnecessary test ordering and medication prescribing, as well as concerning sociodemographic 
disparities in care quality11. Performance in specialized domains such as stroke care may also fall 
below clinical competency thresholds, and hallucinated but plausible clinical outputs remain a major 
patient-safety concern12. Together, these findings suggest that the most appropriate role for LLMs in 
emergency medicine is not autonomous decision-making, but structured support for physician 
reasoning. A recent randomised controlled trial in cardiology13 provides complementary evidence: 
when nine cardiologists used an LLM assistant (AMIE) to manage 107 complex cases, diagnostic 
accuracy and management quality matched or exceeded unassisted specialist performance. 
However, that study evaluated a single-specialty, consultation-style interaction and did not examine 
how expertise level modulates the benefit of AI assistance. 
 
This perspective aligns with a growing body of work on physician-AI collaboration and multi-agent 
reasoning. Multi-agent conversational systems have shown that structured deliberation can 
outperform single-pass inference, including rare-disease diagnosis frameworks that surpassed 
standalone GPT-4 and human-in-the-loop systems that produced more comprehensive and traceable 
recommendations than clinician-written outputs14,15. At the same time, physician benefit appears to 
depend not only on model capability, but also on how clinicians interact with the system, how outputs 
are explained, and how AI is positioned within the clinical workflow. Prior work suggests that no single 
input strategy reliably improves reasoning outcomes16, whereas explanation design can substantially 
influence clinician uptake, confidence, and reliance on AI advice, albeit with marked inter-individual 
variability17,18. Adoption is further shaped by workflow integration, evolving perceptions of usefulness 
over time, and social stigma associated with using generative AI as a primary decision tool rather 
than as verification or support19,20. More broadly, although multi-LLM and multi-agent systems have 
been proposed to improve robustness and reasoning, evaluation has largely remained 
benchmark-centric and focused on closed-ended tasks21–28. Recent multi-turn benchmarks and 
collaborative evaluations show that interactive reasoning remains challenging because of retrieval 
failures, error propagation, limited adaptability, and instability, particularly in open-source systems29–35. 
Within healthcare, related work has examined clinical summarization, simulated patient–doctor 
dialogue, pathway-based evaluation, and multi-agent diagnostic systems, but much of this evidence 
remains simulation-driven or model-only, with persistent concerns regarding hallucinations, 
omissions, specialty coverage, and the limited clinical validity of standard automatic metrics such as


## Page 3


ROUGE and BLEU36–47. Consequently, a key gap remains in understanding whether LLMs can 
meaningfully improve clinical reasoning when used by physicians in realistic, iterative diagnostic 
workflows. Most prior studies have focused on standalone model performance or single-turn 
interactions, rather than on multi-turn physician-LLM collaboration that more closely reflects clinical 
decision-making in practice. Evidence is also limited regarding how such support affects physicians 
with different levels of expertise, despite the importance of this question for both training and 
workforce augmentation.  
 
This work focuses on three research questions: (1) Does the integration of LLMs into clinical 
workflows lead to improved diagnostic accuracy in emergency care? (2) How does LLM assistance 
influence the diagnostic accuracy and decision-making speed of physicians with varying levels of 
expertise? (3) To what extent can LLMs reduce the performance gap in diagnostic decision-making 
between expert and non-expert physicians? To address these questions, we developed MedSyn48 
(Fig. 1) as an experimental framework for studying interactive physician-LLM collaboration. In 
interactive sessions with MedSyn, the physician initially sees only the patient’s chief complaint, 
whereas the LLM assistant has access to the full clinical record excluding the ground-truth diagnosis. 
The physician can iteratively query the assistant for clarification, preliminary assessment, and 
diagnostic suggestions. This design approximates a clinically relevant pattern of decision support in 
which the model informs, but does not replace, physician reasoning. We conducted a controlled 
evaluation in which seven physicians (three senior physicians and four residents) completed baseline 
(unassisted) and interactive (AI-assisted) diagnostic sessions across 52 emergency medicine cases 
from MIMIC-IV49, stratified by difficulty (easy, medium, hard; see Methods: Sample Selection for 
stratification criteria). We assessed diagnostic accuracy using automated and clinician-judged 
measures, examined inter-user agreement, and analysed dialogue patterns to characterise how 
physicians at different expertise levels engaged with the LLM.

Results 
We assessed diagnostic performance using two complementary approaches. Automated 
fuzzy-matching metrics (any-match accuracy, exact-match accuracy, diagnosis-set F1) provide


## Page 4


reproducible, scalable measurement of string-level concordance with discharge diagnoses and 
support fine-grained stratification by difficulty, session, and expertise. Blinded expert evaluation, in 
which a senior clinician rated each of the 364 participant-case diagnoses on a three-point ordinal 
scale (wrong, partially correct, completely correct), provides the clinically definitive assessment of 
diagnostic quality. We report automated metrics first because they support the granular stratified 
analyses that require larger effective sample sizes, and then present the expert assessment as 
clinical validation. The agreement between the two approaches is analysed explicitly in a dedicated 
section. To enable direct comparison, we also applied the same difficulty-standardisation and 
bootstrap procedure to the manual evaluation, using the most stringent criterion, the 
completely-correct rate, as a binary endpoint. 
 
Diagnostic Accuracy 
We evaluated diagnostic accuracy across four sessions (two baseline and two interactive) involving 
seven physicians (three internal medicine experts and four residents), with each physician diagnosing 
13 cases per session. To account for differences in case difficulty across sessions, all reported 
metrics were difficulty-standardized using fixed weights for easy (3/13), medium (6/13), and hard 
(4/13) cases. The primary automated outcome measure, difficulty-standardized any-match accuracy, 
increased significantly from baseline to interactive conditions at the cohort level (median, 0.369 vs. 
0.577; mean improvement, 0.156; 95% bootstrap CI, 0.083–0.232; p < 0.0001; Table 1, Fig. 2). This 
improvement was observed in both groups: experts (mean improvement, 0.159; p < 0.0001) and 
residents (mean improvement, 0.153; p < 0.0001). Difficulty-standardized diagnosis-set F1 also 
improved significantly at the cohort level (median, 0.310 vs. 0.449; mean improvement, 0.111; 95% 
CI, 0.033–0.190; p = 0.0030). This effect was driven primarily by residents (mean improvement, 
0.138; p < 0.0001), whereas experts showed a smaller, non-significant gain (mean improvement, 
0.075; p = 0.2945). Difficulty-standardized exact-match accuracy improved among residents (mean 
improvement, 0.100; 95% CI, 0.010–0.185; p = 0.0074) but not among experts (mean change, -0.031; 
p = 0.5159), suggesting that LLM assistance may provide greater benefit to less experienced 
clinicians. Difficulty-standardized time per case did not differ significantly between conditions at the 
cohort level (mean change, -0.321 min; p = 0.3057). Residents showed a trend toward faster 
completion in the interactive sessions (mean change, -0.709 min; p = 0.1015), whereas experts 
showed a slight, non-significant increase (mean change, +0.196 min; p = 0.5156).

Difficulty-stratified analysis (Figure 3) reveals that the interactive benefit is concentrated on Medium 
and Hard cases. In Easy cases, performance either remained flat or declined; a striking pattern most 
visible in exact-match accuracy, which dropped from 0.619 to 0.167 for seniors and from 0.500 to 
0.125 for residents. This counter-intuitive result likely reflects a diagnostic-expansion effect: when the 
LLM is available, physicians tend to broaden their differential even for straightforward presentations, 
adding plausible but ultimately incorrect diagnoses that hurt exact-match while still including the 
correct one (any-match remains stable). In hard cases, by contrast, residents' any-match accuracy 
more than doubled (0.214 → 0.531) and their F1 tripled (0.167 → 0.500), demonstrating that LLM 
assistance is most impactful when diagnostic complexity exceeds the physician's baseline capacity. 
 
Figure 4 traces the session-by-session trajectory (S1 → S2 → S3 → S4) and reveals several patterns 
not visible in aggregated comparisons. First, the interactive boost is reproducible: both S2 and S4 
show a clear upward step relative to the preceding baseline, with comparable magnitude. Second, S3 
performance reverts to near-S1 levels for residents (any-match drops from ~0.50 in S2 back to ~0.32 
in S3), arguing against a learning-effect explanation; physicians did not retain knowledge from S2 into 
S3, which supports the interpretation that accuracy gains require real-time LLM access. Third, the 
group trajectories differ in shape: the senior curve is flatter (oscillating between ~0.45 and ~0.65 on 
any-match) while the resident curve shows wider swings (~0.28 to ~0.50), reflecting residents' greater 
responsiveness to LLM availability. Finally, the exact-match panel reveals that seniors' exact-match 
drops sharply in S4 despite stable any-match, consistent with the diagnostic-expansion effect

Figure 5 directly addresses the following research question: “To what extent can LLMs reduce the 
performance gap in diagnostic decision-making between expert and non-expert physicians?” by 
plotting the Senior-minus-Resident gap as a function of difficulty. Under baseline conditions, the 
expertise gap is wide for Easy cases (any-match: 0.24) and narrows toward Hard cases (0.12), 
reflecting the well-documented finding that difficult cases are challenging regardless of experience. 
With interactive support, the gap compresses across difficulty levels. On Hard cases, where equitable 
access to diagnostic reasoning support matters most, any-match gap shrinks from 0.12 to 0.09, and 
the F1 gap inverts entirely (residents slightly outperform seniors: 0.500 vs 0.507). This convergence 
suggests that LLM-mediated interaction compensates for experience-dependent knowledge gaps, 
effectively democratising access to clinical information. The blinded expert assessment corroborates 
this pattern: the Senior-Resident gap in mean ordinal correctness narrows from 0.316 (baseline) to 
0.162 (interactive) on Hard cases and from 0.198 to 0.104 on Medium cases, while on Easy cases 
both groups perform near ceiling and the gap remains negligible (0.107 vs 0.118).

Expert Clinical Assessment 
Automated fuzzy-matching metrics assess string-level accuracy and may underestimate clinical 
correctness when physicians use valid but differently worded diagnoses. To obtain a clinically- 
grounded evaluation, a senior clinician blinded to condition assignment rated each of the 364


## Page 8


participant-case diagnoses as completely correct, partially correct, or wrong. Binary correctness (the 
proportion of diagnoses rated as at least partially correct) was high across all four sessions (Table 2), 
ranging from 0.912 to 0.923. This ceiling effect limits the metric's sensitivity to detect condition 
differences and explains why binary agreement between Sessions 1 (baseline, 0.912) and 2 
(interactive, 0.923) is nearly identical. The more granular ordinal score (wrong=0, partial=0.5, 
complete=1) showed a modest increase from Session 1 to Session 2 (0.813→0.857) and was already 
high in Session 3 (0.863), possibly reflecting a practice effect in the blinded evaluator's calibration. 
The completely-correct rate (the most demanding criterion) improved from 0.714 (S1) to 0.791 (S2), a 
gain consistent with the automated F1 improvement observed for the same session pair.

Difficulty-stratified manual scores (Figure 6, Table 3) reveal the most clinically significant pattern in 
this study. Figure 6 shows boxplots of participant-level mean ordinal scores (each participant’s mean 
across cases of a given difficulty, averaged across sessions of the same condition), stratified by 
difficulty, expertise, and condition. For seniors (blue boxes), scores remain tightly clustered near 
ceiling across all difficulty levels and both conditions. For residents on Easy and Medium cases, 
baseline and interactive distributions largely overlap. The critical finding emerges on Hard cases: the 
Resident-Baseline box (light red) is wide and centered low (mean 0.589, median 0.571, IQR 
0.464–0.696), whereas the Resident-Interactive box (dark red) shifts upward (mean 0.734, median 
0.750, IQR 0.641–0.844), representing a mean gain of +0.145 (p = 0.175). Seniors, by contrast, 
scored 0.905 versus 0.896 on Hard cases (Δ = −0.009, p = 0.917), confirming that the LLM provides 
no additional benefit when baseline performance is already high. On Easy cases, both groups 
performed near ceiling (seniors: 1.000; residents: 0.893) and interactive support conferred no 
measurable improvement. On Medium cases, residents showed a modest gain (+0.052, p = 0.494) 
while seniors showed a slight decline (−0.042, p = 0.454). Although the per-cell p-values do not reach 
conventional significance (consistent with the limited sample size within each difficulty × expertise 
stratum), the effect sizes are consistent in both direction and magnitude with the automated metric 
findings, and the clinical interpretation is unambiguous: LLM-mediated interaction primarily benefits 
less experienced clinicians confronting diagnostically complex cases. 
 
To parallel the automated analysis (Table 1), we also computed difficulty-standardised completely- 
correct rates per participant (collapsing wrong and partially correct into a single category) and applied 
the same paired bootstrap procedure (Table 3b). Residents’ completely-correct rate improved from a 
median of 0.580 to 0.750 (mean Δ = +0.092; 95% CI, −0.021 to 0.228; p = 0.071; d = 0.47), 
approaching conventional significance and yielding a medium effect size consistent with the

Agreement Between Automated and Manual Evaluation 
Given the discrepancy between fuzzy matching and expert judgment, we quantified their alignment at 
the participant-case level (N = 364). Binary agreement between any-match and manual binary 
correctness was 0.547, corresponding to a Cohen’s kappa of 0.148, which indicates little agreement 
by conventional benchmarks (Table 4). The confusion matrix (Fig. 7a) shows that the disagreement 
was highly asymmetric: 165 of 364 cases (45.3%) were judged clinically correct by the expert 
reviewer but not credited by fuzzy matching, whereas no cases were credited by fuzzy matching and 
judged incorrect by the expert reviewer. This pattern suggests that the automated metrics were 
conservative, tending to under-credit clinically acceptable diagnoses rather than over-credit incorrect 
ones. Accordingly, the improvements observed with interactive support may be underestimated when 
assessed using any-match and diagnosis-set F1 alone.

Cross-expertise concordance (arguably the more clinically meaningful measure, as it captures 
whether senior and resident diagnoses converge) improved even more (Δ = 0.142; d = 1.48). This 
pattern suggests that the LLM acts as a shared information source that brings diagnostically diverse 
physicians toward a common, more complete differential. The effect is not simply that everyone 
copies the LLM's answers: if it were, we would expect near-perfect concordance, whereas the 
observed interactive medians (0.44 – 0.50) remain well below 1.0, indicating that physicians still 
exercise independent clinical judgement. Because concordance was computed using the same 
fuzzy-matching rule shown to have a 45% false-negative rate against expert judgment (Table 4), the 
absolute concordance values likely underestimate true inter-physician agreement. However, the 
relative improvement between baseline and interactive conditions is less affected, as the conservative 
measurement bias is approximately constant across conditions.

Discussion 
This study provides controlled evidence that interactive LLM assistance improves diagnostic accuracy 
in emergency medicine. Automated metrics showed a mean improvement in standardised any-match 
accuracy of 0.156 (p < 0.0001; d = 1.25), consistent across expertise levels and reproducible across 
two independent interactive sessions; blinded expert assessment confirmed the direction of these 
gains, with residents’ difficulty-standardised completely-correct rate improving by 0.092 (p = 0.071, d 
= 0.47). The benefit was most pronounced for residents, whose F1 improved by 0.138 (d = 0.94) 
compared to 0.075 (d = 0.32) for seniors, supporting the hypothesis that LLM assistance is most 
valuable when the physician's baseline knowledge is furthest from the diagnostic target. Critically, 
blinded manual assessment by a chief physician corroborated this finding: residents' mean ordinal 
correctness on Hard cases improved from 0.589 to 0.734 (+0.145), with the median shifting from 
0.571 to 0.750, while seniors remained near ceiling (0.905 vs 0.896). These expertise-dependent 
gains complement recent findings from O'Sullivan et al.13, who showed that LLM-assisted 
cardiologists matched or exceeded unassisted specialists in a single-specialty RCT; our results 
extend this evidence to emergency medicine and demonstrate that the magnitude of benefit depends 
on the physician's baseline expertise. This convergence of automated and clinician-judged metrics 
strengthens the conclusion that the observed improvements are clinically meaningful and not artifacts 
of string-matching sensitivity. 
 
The difficulty-stratified analysis adds nuance to this finding. On Easy cases, interactive support 
conferred no benefit and may even have broadened differentials unnecessarily, as evidenced by the 
sharp decline in exact-match accuracy. The manual evaluation confirms this pattern: both seniors and 
residents scored near ceiling on Easy cases regardless of condition (seniors: 1.000 vs 0.972; 
residents: 0.893 vs 0.854), leaving no room for improvement. On Hard cases, by contrast, residents' 
any-match more than doubled and the Senior–Resident gap nearly closed. The manual scores tell an 
even clearer story: residents' median Hard-case correctness shifted from 0.571 to 0.750 (IQR


## Page 17


narrowing from 0.464–0.696 to 0.641–0.844), indicating a consistent upward shift in diagnostic quality 
when LLM support was available. This difficulty-dependent effect has practical implications: in a 
deployed system, adaptive triggering (offering LLM assistance only when case complexity exceeds a 
threshold) could maximize benefit while avoiding the diagnostic-expansion cost on straightforward 
presentations. 
 
Dialogue analysis revealed that seniors and residents interact with the LLM differently: seniors 
employ a hypothesis-driven strategy (more information requests, higher context overlap) while 
residents ask broader questions. Despite these differing strategies, cross-expertise concordance 
increased significantly (Δ = 0.142; d = 1.48), suggesting that the LLM functions as a shared 
knowledge source that brings diagnostically diverse physicians toward a common, more complete 
differential without homogenising their reasoning. 
 
These findings carry several implications for the design and deployment of AI-assisted clinical 
workflows. First, the difficulty-dependent benefit profile suggests that adaptive deployment strategies, 
in which LLM assistance is offered selectively for cases exceeding a complexity threshold, could 
maximize diagnostic gains while avoiding the unnecessary broadening of differentials observed on 
straightforward presentations. Second, the expertise-stratified interaction patterns point to a need for 
differentiated interface design: senior physicians, who use the LLM primarily for targeted information 
retrieval, may benefit from concise, evidence-linked responses, whereas residents, who engage in 
more open-ended diagnostic reasoning, may benefit from structured scaffolding that guides them 
through differential diagnosis. Third, the finding that residents achieve the greatest gains in accuracy 
has implications for medical education: LLM-based tools could serve as a training resource that 
exposes trainees to structured clinical reasoning on complex cases, complementing traditional 
supervision. Finally, the significant increase in cross-expertise concordance suggests that 
LLM-mediated interaction may help standardize diagnostic quality across experience levels, a 
particularly relevant consideration for settings with heterogeneous staffing, such as emergency 
departments during off-peak hours. 
 
The modest agreement between automated and manual evaluation (κ = 0.148) is an important 
methodological finding. The false-negative rate of automated metrics (45% of cases) means that the 
improvements reported here are likely conservative. The difficulty-stratified manual scores (Table 3) 
provide a more clinically grounded view: the +0.145 improvement in residents' Hard-case ordinal 
scores and the shift from median 0.571 to 0.750 are arguably the most compelling evidence that 
interactive LLM support has tangible clinical value, particularly for less experienced physicians facing 
complex diagnostic challenges. Future work should develop clinically-validated matching algorithms 
(possibly using medical ontologies rather than string similarity) to narrow this gap. 
 
Several limitations qualify these results. The sample (7 physicians, 52 cases) limits generalisability; 
statistically significant effects should be interpreted as directional evidence. When the same 
difficulty-standardised bootstrap procedure used for automated endpoints (Table 1) is applied to the 
manual completely-correct rate, the Resident improvement approaches but does not reach 
conventional significance (Δ = +0.092, p = 0.071, d = 0.47). This reflects the near-ceiling baseline 
scores (manual baseline mean 0.83 vs. automated 0.37), the coarse three-point ordinal scale, and the


## Page 18


small sample (n = 7), which together limit the sensitivity of the manual metric. Three of seven 
participants showed slight manual score decreases despite automated metric improvements, 
indicating that string-level concordance and clinical correctness capture different facets of diagnostic 
quality. We therefore interpret the manual evaluation as a clinically grounded complement to the 
automated analysis: it confirms effect direction, localises the benefit to residents on Hard cases (Table 
3), and provides the most compelling evidence through difficulty-stratified effect sizes rather than 
aggregate significance tests. The within-subjects design introduces potential learning effects, 
although the S3 baseline reversion argues against persistent carryover. The use of retrospective 
MIMIC-IV notes rather than real-time encounters omits time pressure, interruptions, and evolving 
clinical information. Additionally, manual evaluation was performed by a single blinded rater; 
inter-rater reliability was not assessed, and individual calibration effects cannot be excluded. 
 
Future work should evaluate MedSyn in prospective clinical settings with larger cohorts. The 
framework is not limited to emergency medicine; adaptation to internal medicine, where 
documentation burden and diagnostic complexity are similarly high, is a natural extension. Priorities 
identified by participants include shorter responses, multilingual support, and explicit provenance 
linking answers to specific note sections. 
Methods 
Dataset 
We constructed our study dataset by combining MIMIC-IV2 and MIMIC-IV-Note3 records and selecting 
samples with ICD-10 diagnosis codes4. ICD-10 codes span multiple levels of granularity, from coarse 
"chapter-level" ranges (e.g., E00-E90) to fine-grained codes (e.g., E10.9, where E10 denotes the 
disease category and .9 specifies a subtype). The initial merged dataset contained 122,266 
hospitalization records and 5,802 unique diagnoses. Because our experiments rely on extracting 
clinically meaningful context from discharge documentation, we examined the discharge text field and 
found that while many notes follow a semi-standard structure, subsection presence and formatting 
vary substantially (e.g., "major surgical or invasive procedure" may be present or absent). Notes with 
missing headings or highly free-form text impeded reliable parsing and prevented enforcing a 
consistent input format across cases. After consultation with three physicians, we identified the most 
informative discharge note sections for diagnostic reasoning and retained only records whose 
discharge texts contained the following headings: chief complaint; history of present illness; social 
history; physical exam; pertinent results; major surgical or invasive procedure; brief hospital course; 
medications on admission; discharge medications; discharge diagnosis; discharge condition; and 
discharge instructions. We additionally excluded records where the patient status was "deceased" or 
"expired". This filtering yielded a final dataset of 74,850 records. From this filtered subset, we 
randomly sampled 1,000 records5 as the test set (seed = 13). The resulting test set contains 2,350 
unique diagnoses (13,384 total code occurrences), with an average of 5.61 ICD-10 codes per record.

Use cases 
We consider two use cases: (i) baseline (unassisted diagnosis), in which physicians review the 
complete clinical record and provide a diagnosis without LLM support; and (ii) interactive 
(AI-supported diagnosis), in which physicians initially view only the chief complaint and iteratively 
query an LLM (granted access to the full record) to retrieve additional information needed before 
finalizing their diagnosis. 
 
Choice of LLM for Experiments 
We evaluated MedSyn in two phases. Phase 1 consisted of a controlled, multi-agent simulation in 
which an LLM “chief physician” interacted with an LLM “assistant physician.” The chief physician was 
limited to the patient’s chief complaint, while the assistant had access to the full clinical note; the 
dialogue continued until the chief physician finalized a diagnosis-focused discharge output. To enable 
automated scoring under controlled conditions, we used ICD-10 codes as the diagnosis endpoint in 
this phase. Overall, Phase 1 was intended to stress-test multi-turn interaction dynamics, compare 
model behaviors, and characterize diagnostic overlap across models. 
 
In brief, these preliminary simulations suggested that role-separated dialogue can improve diagnostic 
completeness: complaint-constrained reasoning by a chief physician coupled with on-demand note 
extraction by an assistant can yield more clinically coherent discharge narratives and may help 
surface less common conditions. At the same time, performance varied substantially across models 
and was strongly influenced by instruction adherence and the ability to ask targeted follow-up 
questions. The simulation results also highlighted an important evaluation consideration: reference 
ICD-10 lists often contain chronic or historical comorbidities recorded through coding workflows, 
whereas MedSyn prompts emphasize the current presentation, making exact code-level comparisons 
potentially misleading. Coarser, set-based evaluation at the chapter/category level (e.g., 
precision/recall/F1/Jaccard) was therefore more interpretable, and under this lens models were 
generally better at identifying the broad disease domain than the specific ICD category. Full details of 
Phase 1 are reported in our workshop paper48. 
 
Phase 2 replaced the simulated chief physician with real physicians and evaluated MedSyn as an 
interactive decision-support workflow. Physicians initially saw only the chief complaint and iteratively 
queried the assistant (with access to the full record) before committing to a final diagnosis. In this 
phase, physicians reported diagnoses in free text, reflecting real clinical practice, and we compared 
their outputs against ground-truth textual discharge diagnoses. This paper focuses on the Phase 2 
study design, outcomes, and clinician-facing findings.

Stakeholders 
We evaluated our framework with seven physicians: three internal medicine experts (senior 
physicians) and four first-year residents (non-expert physicians). An additional senior physician, who 
did not participate in the diagnostic sessions, served as the blinded evaluator for manual assessment. 
All participants completed the required CITI training for access to MIMIC and provided written 
informed consent. 
 
Sample Selection 
The selection of clinical cases was performed through evaluation of the MIMIC database. Clinical 
records were reviewed sequentially according to the following exclusion criteria: 
●​ the presenting symptom directly corresponded to the final diagnosis (e.g., gunshot wound); 
●​ the medical diagnosis itself was recorded in the clinical chart as the presenting symptom (e.g., 
presenting symptom: pulmonary embolism); 
●​ elective hospital admission for a planned procedure or therapeutic intervention; 
●​ incomplete medical records in which instrumental and laboratory investigations, at the 
discretion of the evaluating physician, were insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis. 
Once selected, cases were stratified according to their level of difficulty (easy, intermediate, and 
difficult), as determined by the evaluating physician. In particular, the most challenging cases 
corresponded to diagnoses of rare or less common diseases. 
Sessions 
The study included four sessions. Each session comprised 13 cases. Because Session 3 differed 
slightly in case-difficulty composition from the other sessions (4 easy and 3 hard cases, versus 3 easy 
and 4 hard cases), all between-session comparisons were difficulty-standardized using fixed weights

Evaluation 
Our success criteria are twofold: (i) reducing the time and effort required for clinical decision-making, 
and (ii) improving the quality of the final decision. Within the MedSyn framework, we define the final 
decision as the diagnosis names rather than ICD codes, as the latter are primarily used for 
administrative purposes, such as assessing patient complexity or determining discharge costs, and 
lack the clinical granularity necessary for evaluating LLM-based diagnostic performance. Relying on 
diagnosis names, however, presents challenges for automated evaluation. Ground truth diagnoses 
often consist of comprehensive or detailed lists (e.g., "heart failure" and "congestive heart failure"), 
whereas clinician-generated outputs may include shorter, yet clinically equivalent, formulations. To 
address this discrepancy, we involve a senior clinician as a blinded evaluator in our user experiments. 
Acting as an independent referee, the clinician manually compares the generated diagnoses with the 
ground truth, assessing both accuracy and clinical concordance. Dialogue quality was additionally 
assessed using an LLM-as-judge approach (google/gemini-2.5-flash, chosen to avoid self-evaluation 
bias, as the assistant uses a GPT model) that scored each response for faithfulness and answer 
relevancy. 
 
Metrics  
For each participant-case pair, clinician-entered diagnoses were compared with the reference 
discharge diagnosis. Because both could contain multiple diagnoses, we treated each as a diagnosis 
set and evaluated concordance after text normalization and fuzzy string matching (RapidFuzz 
token-set ratio; a predicted diagnosis was considered a match if the similarity score reached or 
exceeded 80 on a 0-100 scale)  to account for minor phrasing differences. Predicted and reference 
diagnoses were matched one-to-one using the highest similarity scores, so that each predicted 
diagnosis could match at most one reference diagnosis and vice versa. Based on the number of 
matched diagnosis pairs, we calculated per-case precision, recall, and F1 score to quantify partial 
diagnostic concordance. Precision reflected the proportion of clinician-proposed diagnoses that 
matched the reference set, whereas recall reflected the proportion of reference diagnoses recovered 
by the clinician. F1 summarized the balance between these two quantities. We additionally computed 
any-match accuracy, defined as whether at least one diagnosis matched the reference set, and 
exact-match accuracy, defined as complete agreement between clinician and reference diagnosis 
sets under the same matching procedure. Time per case was measured in minutes. Because 
sessions differed slightly in their distribution of case difficulty, all summary metrics were 
difficulty-standardized to a fixed target composition of easy, medium, and hard cases (3/13, 6/13, and 
4/13, respectively). For each participant and session, metric values were first averaged within each 
difficulty level and then combined using these prespecified weights. This yielded standardized 
performance estimates that enabled fair comparison between baseline and interactive conditions.

Statistical Analysis. All between-condition comparisons used paired bootstrap resampling (20,000 
replicates, seed 42). For each bootstrap replicate, participants were resampled with replacement and 
the mean baseline–interactive difference was computed; 95% confidence intervals were derived from 
the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution, and two-sided p-values were computed 
as twice the smaller tail probability. Effect sizes are reported as Hedges’ g, a small-sample 
bias-corrected variant of Cohen’s d (correction factor J = 1 − 3/(4n − 5)). The primary automated 
outcome measure was difficulty-standardised any-match accuracy; all other automated metrics 
(exact-match, F1, precision, recall, time), manual evaluation scores, and concordance comparisons 
are reported as secondary or exploratory endpoints. No correction for multiple comparisons was

Pilot Studies & Training for Physicians 
In a pilot study conducted on December 11, 2025, participants completed two sequential sessions 
(baseline and interactive) in a single sitting. All cases were selected to have Medium difficulty. Two 
cases were assigned to each session: the baseline session used 10259153-DS-2 and 
12583650-DS-13, while the interactive session used 11880923-DS-18 and 18101466-DS-4. 
The pilot study was a 90-minute training block. During the first 30 minutes, we introduced the study 
task and guided participants through the MedSyn interface and workflow; the remaining training time 
was used to answer questions and ensure familiarity with the interaction format. Participants then 
completed the baseline session first (20 minutes), followed by a 10-minute Q&A/feedback period. 
Next, they completed the interactive session (20 minutes), again followed by a 10-minute 
Q&A/feedback period. This protocol was designed to (i) standardize participants’ understanding of the 
task and interface prior to data collection, (ii) support within-participant comparison between baseline 
and interactive conditions, and (iii) elicit qualitative feedback immediately after each session while the 
experience was fresh.

Ablation results for model selection in user experiments 
To better mirror real-world clinical practice, we evaluated the quality of discharge diagnosis predictions 
across both the baseline setting and the interactive MedSyn scenarios. To identify the model most 
suitable for collaborative use with physicians, we ran ablation experiments with five strong, recent 
LLMs: gemini-3-pro-preview, gpt-oss-120b, llama-4-scout, gpt-5.1-chat, and gpt-5.2-chat. Prompts used 
for ablation tests are provided in Supplementary Note 3. 
 
The goal was to evaluate how well each model predicts discharge diagnoses under: (i) Baseline 
scenario: model sees the full note and outputs diagnoses directly, and (ii) Interactive scenario: model 
outputs diagnoses after the interactive workflow. The key decision for deployment was: which model will 
perform best in real physician-facing interactive experiments. Each scenario consists of 52 cases. 
Since the ground truth diagnosis strings and the expected physician answers are not guaranteed to be 
identical (abbreviations, word order), we used soft matching to evaluate models’ answers: RapidFuzz11 
token set ratio on normalized strings. A prediction matches a label if similarity >= 62 (0–100 scale). 
When multiple predictions could match multiple labels, we perform one-to-one matching (each pred and 
label can be used at most once) by selecting the highest-similarity pairs first. 
 
We report results at the diagnosis level, with a focus on primary discharge diagnoses. For each model 
and scenario, we computed: (i) micro-precision (P): fraction of predicted diagnoses that match a 
ground-truth primary diagnosis, (ii) micro-recall (R): fraction of ground-truth primary diagnoses 
recovered by any prediction, (iii) micro-F1: harmonic mean of (P, R), (iv) Top-1 accuracy: whether the 
first predicted diagnosis matches any primary diagnosis, (v) Any-match accuracy: whether any 
predicted diagnosis matches any primary diagnosis for that case, and (vi) Average number of 
predicted diagnoses (avg_n_pred): proxy for verbosity / cognitive load.
