Abstract
Post-training has significantly enhanced the reasoning capability of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs),
especially with Reinforcement Learning (RL) like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). How-
ever, GRPO-style RL methods in multi-domain settings often fail to achieve consistent improvements
across all domains due to inherent interference in policy optimization. Prior studies on multi-domain
RL primarily focus on alleviating cross-domain interference, while often neglecting the pivotal
role of knowledge sharing, which we argue is the key to transforming cross-domain interactions
from harmful competition into beneficial transfer. To address this limitation, we propose Multi-
domain Contrastive Policy Optimization (MCPO), which analyzes the structural relationships among
rollouts and promotes cross-domain knowledge sharing and in-domain knowledge consolidation
in a contrastive manner. Specifically, for a given prompt, MCPO identifies transferable reasoning
trajectories from other domains as positive examples, while treating incorrect rollouts as negative
ones. It then encourages consistent representations for positive pairs and pushes negative pairs
apart, thereby facilitating knowledge transfer and reducing interference. Moreover, MCPO aligns
intra-domain correct rollouts to build a consolidated representation space. In this way, MCPO
contrastively learns a harmonious representation space that can accommodate diverse multi-domain
knowledge. Empirical results show that MCPO improves the reasoning capabilities of LRMs across
multiple domains and even outperforms single-domain training in some cases. Code is available at
https://github.com/Maricalce/MCPO.

1. Introduction
Recent post-training advances have substantially improved the reasoning capability of Large Reasoning
Models (LRMs), yielding strong performance in specialized domains such as mathematics [1–3],
coding [4, 5], and safety alignment [6, 7]. However, practical LRMs are expected to serve as general-
purpose reasoners [8, 9] that can accommodate diverse multi-domain knowledge within a single
model. This poses a fundamental challenge when jointly training one model across heterogeneous
domains. In particular, standard multi-domain post-training methods that optimize for average
performance often incur inter-domain interference [10–12]. Consequently, performance gains in
one domain often coincide with degradation in others, which hinders the compatible coexistence of
knowledge from multiple domains within a single model.

Current multi-domain post-training methods for LRMs usually follow reinforcement learning (RL)
frameworks, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) or its variants [11, 13, 14]. GRPO-
style methods estimate relative advantages from sampled rollouts for each prompt and provide
efficient policy optimization within each prompt [15, 16]. In multi-domain training, however, these
losses within each prompt are aggregated across prompts and domains under a single shared ob-
jective, which aligns optimization with average performance rather than consistent domain-wise
improvement [10, 17]. Prior studies on multi-domain RL therefore primarily focus on alleviating
cross-domain interference through sampling control [17, 18], reward normalization [3, 14], sample
reweighting [12], and gradient balancing [11]. While these methods can reduce harmful conflicts,
they often neglect the pivotal role of knowledge sharing, which we argue is the key to transforming
cross-domain interactions from harmful competition into beneficial transfer.
To share and transfer knowledge across domains, we argue that correct rollouts sampled by LRMs
across prompts and domains often preserve transferable reasoning structures, though their reasoning
chains are diverse. These rollouts can be viewed as mutually augmented examples, in analogy with
augmented images in computer vision [19, 20]. For example, two correct solutions to a math problem
may rely on algebraic and geometric reasoning, yet both reach the same answer through different paths.
This observation motivates a contrastive learning (CL) perspective, where informative representations
are learned by encouraging coherent representations for positive pairs and decreasing the similarity
of negative pairs [19–23]. At different granularities, correct rollouts provide within-prompt positive
examples, reveal reusable knowledge patterns for in-domain knowledge consolidation, and supply
transferable reasoning trajectories from other domains for cross-domain knowledge sharing. Incorrect
rollouts serve as negative ones, so all sampled rollouts form a structured contrastive pool that can
alleviate cross-domain interference while strengthening multi-domain learning.
Based on this motivation, we propose Multi-domain Contrastive Policy Optimization (MCPO), a
structured contrastive reinforcement learning framework for multi-domain post-training of LRMs. At
each optimization step, MCPO constructs a structured contrastive pool from the same mixed-domain
rollout batch and maps rollouts into a compact representation space through a lightweight contrastive
head. Inspired by the InfoNCE loss [21, 24], MCPO organizes structural rollout relationships through
two coupled objectives: (i) cross-domain knowledge sharing, which identifies transferable reasoning
trajectories from other domains as positive examples while treating incorrect rollouts as negative
examples; and (ii) in-domain knowledge consolidation, which aligns intra-domain correct rollouts
to build a consolidated representation space. The resulting contrastive loss is combined with the
within-prompt GRPO objective, so the policy update preserves stable optimization within each prompt
while adding explicit supervision for positive pairs and negative pairs across prompts and domains.
Compared with standard GRPO-style post-training, MCPO offers two advantages for multi-domain
LRMs. First, it aligns intra-domain correct rollouts to build a consolidated representation space,
allowing reusable solution structures within each domain to be retained during multi-domain training.
Second, it promotes cross-domain knowledge sharing by identifying transferable reasoning trajectories
from other domains as positive examples while separating incorrect rollouts as negative examples. In
this way, MCPO contrastively learns a harmonious representation space that can accommodate diverse
multi-domain knowledge. Empirical results show that MCPO improves the reasoning capabilities of
LRMs across multiple domains and even outperforms single-domain training in some cases.

efficient policy optimization within each prompt by estimating relative advantages in each rollout

Contrastive learning.
Contrastive learning learns informative representations by increasing sim-
ilarity between positive pairs and decreasing similarity between negative pairs, typically through
InfoNCE-style objectives [19–22, 24, 33]. This principle matches LRM reasoning because correct
rollouts can preserve transferable hierarchical reasoning structures despite diverse chains of reasoning,
allowing them to be treated as mutually augmented examples [34]. Recent LLM reasoning and RLVR
studies have introduced process rewards or auxiliary contrastive objectives to provide finer-grained
supervision beyond sparse outcome rewards [23, 35–37]. However, existing contrastive integrations
are mostly limited to each prompt: they can strengthen within-prompt supervision, but cannot identify
transferable reasoning trajectories from other domains. We extend contrastive learning to structural
rollout relationships in multi-domain RL, thereby learning a harmonious representation space for
cross-domain knowledge sharing and in-domain knowledge consolidation.

GPQA-Diamond [48] and SciBench [49] for Science, the held-out Toolpaca split for ToolUse, and 1k
held-out non-overlapping Beaver examples for Safety.
Evaluation Metrics.
Following standard RLVR evaluation practice [1, 13, 15, 23], we test each
benchmark 32 times under a zero-shot setting and report the average accuracy, denoted avg@32.
For domains with multiple benchmarks, the reported score is the average of their benchmark-level
avg@32 scores, and the overall score is the average over all benchmarks. All the results use the same
protocols of output and rules of verifiers, which are deferred to Appendix A.1.4.
Baseline Methods.
In our experiments, we compare MCPO against strong group-based RL methods
and recently proposed multi-domain RL methods, including:
• GRPO [1]: A standard group-based RL approach that estimates relative advantages within each
rollout group for a prompt, which provides no explicit relational signal across prompts.
• DAPO [15]: A GRPO variant that improves rollout filtering, clipping, and long-response handling.
It remains limited to each prompt, while ignoring in-domain or cross-domain rollout structure.
• CLIPO [23]: A GRPO variant that computes contrastive rewards within each rollout group for
a prompt. It can only strengthen optimization supervision within the same prompt, and cannot
handle cooperation across prompts or domains.
• MGS [11]: A multi-domain RL method that mitigates inter-domain interference via modular
gradient surgery. It directly targets destructive gradients, but the resulting conflict suppression
does not model knowledge sharing and can leave common structure across domains unused.
• MT-GRPO [12]: A multi-domain GRPO variant that emphasizes weaker tasks through adaptive
sampling and reweighting at the domain level. It improves task balance at the data and objective
level, but does not impose consolidation among rollout representations.
Implementation Details.
All methods are implemented in the VERL framework [50], and all
experiments are run on a single node with 8 H200 GPUs. Unless required by the original algorithms,
rollout count, batch size, sequence limits, and training schedule are aligned across methods. Full
hyperparameters, prompt templates, and reward rules are provided in Appendices A.1 and A.1.4.

6. Conclusion
We presented MCPO, a structured contrastive policy optimization framework for multi-domain RL
post-training of LRMs. MCPO addresses the limitation of GRPO-style post-training within each
prompt by modeling structural relationships among rollouts labeled by the verifier. Compatible cross-
domain correct rollouts support knowledge sharing, in-domain correct rollouts support knowledge
consolidation, and incorrect rollouts serve as negative ones for contrastive competition. This design
extends multi-domain RL from interference alleviation to learning a harmonious representation space
that can accommodate diverse domain knowledge. Experiments across Math, Code, Science, ToolUse,
and Safety show that MCPO consistently improves the corresponding GRPO and DAPO baselines and
validates the role of cross-domain knowledge sharing and in-domain knowledge consolidation.

A.1. Implementation Details
A.1.1. Baseline Implementation
GRPO.
GRPO [1] follows a standard PPO-style clipped surrogate objective with group-relative
advantages. In our implementation, GRPO uses the vanilla policy loss with token-level aggregation,
specified by loss_mode=vanilla and loss_agg_mode=token-mean. Unless otherwise noted, KL
reward shaping is disabled (use_kl_in_reward=False), and the rollout, batch, and optimization
settings are kept identical to the shared training protocol. This makes GRPO the most direct reference
baseline for evaluating the additional gains introduced by cross-rollout relational regularization.
DAPO.
DAPO [15] retains the same overall RL training pipeline as GRPO but adopts the DAPO
reward manager and its corresponding long-response handling strategy. Our optimization backbone
remains PPO-style, while the reward side is switched by setting reward_manager.name=dapo. All
other training factors, including batch size, rollout configuration, and sequence length, are kept
unchanged relative to the shared protocol. This isolates the effect of DAPO-style reward shaping from
the effect of MCPO-style contrastive regularization.
CLIPO.
CLIPO [23] augments GRPO-style RL training with a contrastive representation objective,
but it primarily focuses on alignment within each prompt or in-domain alignment rather than the full
multi-domain transfer-aware structure used in MCPO. In our comparison, CLIPO shares the same
backbone initialization, rollout configuration, and optimization settings as the other methods, while
introducing an auxiliary contrastive objective on top of the base policy update. This makes CLIPO a
strong representation-learning baseline for testing whether the gain comes merely from adding a
contrastive head, or from the specific cross-domain relational design of MCPO.
MGS.
MGS [11] keeps the same training data, rollout settings, and base GRPO optimization as
the vanilla baseline, and modifies only the gradient update rule before the optimizer step. In our
implementation, MGS is enabled by setting mgs_enable=True, with task-wise gradient grouping
controlled by mgs_task_key. For mixed-domain training, we use data_source as the grouping key
so that gradient surgery is performed across domains while all other optimization hyperparameters
remain unchanged. This setup allows us to compare MCPO against a strong optimization-side
multi-task baseline without changing the reward or sampling pipeline.
MT-GRPO.
MT-GRPO [12] introduces task-adaptive curriculum and sampling on top of GRPO-style
RL training. For fairness, we use a strict implementation that follows the original MT-GRPO code
path, while keeping the same dataset split, backbone scale, and output protocol as in our MCPO
experiments. Its key difference is that training examples are scheduled by a task-aware bandit-style
mechanism, together with the original rejection-sampling and FAMO-related components. This
comparison tests whether the gains of MCPO arise from better relational supervision or can already
be explained by adaptive task scheduling alone.

We implement MCPO on top of the public VERL framework [50]. To ensure a fair comparison,
we keep the base RL optimization setup identical to GRPO. We use algorithm_type=grpo and

Shared Training Configuration.
MCPO is trained on five domains jointly: Math, Code, Science,

Contrastive Head and MCPO-Specific Hyperparameters.
MCPO introduces a lightweight pro-

contrastive loss coefficient is set to 0.1, the in-domain balancing coefficient to 0.5, and the contrastive
temperature to 0.1. The projector output dimension and hidden dimension are both 256. We treat
responses with reward at least 0.5 as positive anchors. Contrastive learning is performed at the PPO
mini-batch scope. To keep the pool aligned with the current actor parameters, we do not reuse
rollout-cached representations; instead, the pool is built from the current actor forward pass. For
cross-domain structuring, the cross-positive attraction coefficient is 0.5. We further apply a con-
trastive warmup of 100 PPO mini-batches, during which the projector is trained while the backbone
is protected from early noisy contrastive gradients.

A.1.4. Domain-specific Reward Computation
Following prior reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) work, we use rule-based
verifiable rewards tailored to the answer format of each domain. Unless otherwise noted, all rewards
are binary: the reward is 1 for a correct output and 0 otherwise. Accordingly, since MCPO uses a
positive-reward threshold of 0.5, this multi-domain reward configuration does not affect the positive-
negative partition used by the contrastive module: all reward-1 responses are treated as positive
examples, and all reward-0 responses are treated as negative ones.
Math.
For mathematical reasoning, we follow the standard verifiable-reward protocol used in prior
work [1, 51]. We extract the final answer enclosed in \boxed{} from the model output and compare
it against the ground-truth solution using rule-based mathematical equivalence checking. The reward
is 1 if the extracted boxed answer is mathematically equivalent to the reference answer, and 0
otherwise.
Code.
For code generation, we use execution-based verifiable rewards following the Coder-R1
setup [40]. The generated Python program is executed against the reference test cases associated
with each problem. The reward is 1 if the program passes all test cases, and 0 otherwise.
