Languagemodel(LM)alignmentimprovesmodel outputs to reflect human preferences whilepreserving thecapabilitiesof
the base model. The most common alignment approaches are (i) reinforcement learning, which maximizes the expected
reward under a KL-divergence constraint, and (ii) best-of-N alignment, which selects the highest-reward output among
N independent samples. Despite their widespread use, the fundamental limits of reward improvement under a KL budget
remain poorly understood. We characterize the information-theoretic limits of KL-regularized alignment by deriving the
maximum achievable expected reward gain for a fixed KL-divergence budget. Our first result provides a closed-form
expression for the optimal reward improvement, governed by a Jeffreys divergence term rather than the
√
KL used in prior
analyses. We further reformulate this expression as a covariance under the base model, yielding a practical estimator that
predicts achievable alignment gains from base model samples alone. We extend our analysis to the proxy reward setting,
showing that the gap between ideal and proxy alignment (reward hacking) grows with the magnitude of reward error
and when the KL penalty factor decreases. We then prove that reward ensembling mitigates reward hacking, providing a
theoretical justification for this technique used in practice. Empirically, we compute the KL–reward Pareto frontier for two

in the alignment literature and suggest that algorithmic improvements are needed to achieve optimal alignment without

Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) perform complex tasks on behalf of users: such as solving mathematical
problems [44, 14], moderating online content [29, 32], writing code [11, 3], and shopping [53, 28]. While
these LLMs solve multiple problems without fine-tuning, their output may be toxic, deceiving, or otherwise
harmful [25, 35, 7]. To ensure that models perform in a safe manner and avoid producing harmful outputs,
they usually undergo an alignment step to ensure their outputs follow human preferences that ideally reflect
ethical values and social norms [33, 9, 10, 21].
Many approaches for aligning language models have been proposed including, (i) KL-regularized reinforcement
learning [4, 43], (ii) direct preference optimization [37], (iii) best-of-N finetuning [46], among others [31, 55,
20, 49]. At their core, these methods aim to solve a KL-regularized reinforcement learning (RL) problem
shown in (2.1). This approach begins by defining a reward function r which scores model outputs according
to how well they align with user preferences. A base LLM πbase is then fine-tuned to maximize the expected
reward while penalizing (proportionally to a parameter λ) the KL-divergence from the base, leading to the
aligned model πr,λ [12, 5, 4, 43].
Despite the rapid proliferation of alignment algorithms, their theoretical limits remain largely unaddressed.
Progress in alignment is typically measured by achieving higher expected reward for a fixed KL divergence
budget [19, 37, 31, 20], where the KL divergence acts as a proxy for how much of the base model’s general
capabilities we retain after alignment [34]. While alignment methods continuously push the empirical Pareto

Background
Language model.
A large language model (LLM) is a conditional probability distribution πbase over
sequences of text V. Given a user query x ∈V, the model generates a response y according to said distribution,
y ∼πbase(· | x). In practice, πbase is obtained through pretraining and supervised fine-tuning to follow user
instructions [36, 45]. This training procedure yields a highly capable model often referred to as a base model,
but model responses may not align with human values and preferences, motivating the need for an alignment
procedure to ensure safe model behavior [5, 35, 7, 4].
Reward models. Several alignment procedures rely on a reward model r : V × V →R that assigns a scalar
score r(x, y) measuring the quality of alignment of response y to query x. Ideally, the reward reflects user
preferences: if a user prefers yw over yl in response to x, then the associated reward should also be larger.
If a reward model perfectly reflects user preferences it is termed gold reward and we denote it by r∗. Reward
models are typically learned from preference data [33, 4, 37, 5]. However, due to annotation noise and the
stochastic nature of training a reward model, the resulting model is an imperfect proxy for the true user
preferences, often called a proxy reward [19], which we denote by ˜r. We analyze the difference in models
aligned with a gold and a proxy reward in Sec. 4.
KL-regularized alignment. At the heart of several alignment techniques [33, 37, 31] lies the KL-regularized
reinforcement learning problem (2.1). Specifically, to nudge the base model πbase toward user preferences,
these approaches seek an aligned policy over the vocabulary V that maximizes the expected reward while
penalizing deviation from the base model πbase. Formally, this amounts to finding the aligned model πr,λ
that optimizes the following objective:

Limits of Alignment & The Optimality of best-of-N. Prior work suggested the optimality of best-of-N
[52, 20, 40]. However, existing justifications rely on structural assumptions on language and reward models.
Yang et al. [52] showed that BoN converges to the optimal aligned policy as N →∞, but assumed linear
reward models and memoryless language models. Recently, Gui et al. [20] studied the win-rate–KL Pareto
frontier and found that best-of-N approaches it. However, their analysis focused on win-rate rather than
reward maximization which is the optimization objective used in practice, and they also assumed continuous
language and reward models. In contrast, we derive the exact reward–KL Pareto frontier for standard reward
maximization without structural assumptions on the language or reward model, giving a direct way to check
how close BoN follows the optimum at every value of N, and in our experiments BoN closely tracks the entire
frontier.
Reward Ensembling. Reward ensembling has emerged as a practical strategy for mitigating reward hacking.
Coste et al. [15] show that ensembles of reward models reduce reward hacking. Eisenstein et al. [17] compared
different ensembling strategies showing that some are more effective than others on reducing hacking. In
contrast, Zhang et al. [54] and Ahmed et al. [1] focused on improving the efficiency of ensembling by building
light-weight reward models for the ensemble. While these works show empirically that reward ensembling
mitigates reward hacking in practice, they do not provide a theoretical explanation for the observation, which
we establish in Thm. 3.

on proxy reward models ˜r trained from finite and noisy human preference data. Next, we connect our results
to proxy rewards used in practice.

Proxy Rewards and a Justification for Reward Ensembling

5
Experiments
In this section, we present experiments demonstrating that (i) the limit estimators proposed in Prop. 1 and
Prop. 2 quickly converge, (ii) best-of-N is close to the theoretical limit, i.e., the KL—Reward Pareto frontier,
and (iii) both GRPO and PPO are suboptimal in terms of the KL vs. Reward gain.
Dataset & Tasks.
We evaluate our theoretical limits on two alignment tasks: summarization and safety. For
summarization, we use the Reddit TL;DR dataset [42], which provides Reddit posts paired with two candidate
summaries and human preference labels; the task is to generate post summaries (TL;DRs) aligned with
annotator preferences — during RL we use the preprocessed variant of the dataset [47]. For safety, we use
BeaverTails [24], which consists of prompts designed to elicit harmful completions; the task is to align the
model to produce safe responses.
Base & Reward Models.
For summarization (Reddit TL;DR), we start from the base model Pythia-1B [8]
supervised fine-tuned on Reddit TL;DR [18], and use a Pythia-1B-based reward model trained on pairwise
TL;DR preferences [13], reproducing the setup of Huang et al. [22].
For the safety task (BeaverTails),
we start from Zephyr-7B [48], a base model obtained by supervised fine-tuning Mistral-7B [27] on the
UltraChat dataset [16], and train a Zephyr-7B-based reward model on pairwise safety preferences from
PKU-SafeRLHF [26] using the TRL reward trainer [50].
Alignment Algorithms.
We compare the theoretical limit against three alignment algorithms: GRPO [41] and
PPO [39], two KL-regularized RL algorithms, and best-of-N (BoN), an inference-time method that has proven
to be a strong baseline [31, 37, 52, 20]. We do not consider DPO [37], as it does not use an explicit reward
model. Since both GRPO and PPO are sensitive to hyperparameter choices, we performed an extensive sweep
(Sec. E) and report the best run, measured by the KL-reward trade-off, in the main paper. For best-of-N,
we vary N between 1 and 10_000 (10k) and compute its KL divergence using the estimator of proposed by
Beirami et al. [6].

6
Final Discussion
Conclusion. We characterized the information-theoretic limits of language model alignment in terms of KL
divergence vs. reward gain. First, we derived the optimal reward improvement after alignment showing that
the gain in expected reward is governed by the Jeffrey’s divergence λ JD(πr,λ∥πbase), improving upon the
previously conjectured
p
KL(πr,λ∥πbase) scaling which was empirically observed [4, 19] and proven to be an
upper bound for optimal alignment by Mroueh and Nitsure [30].
Second, we showed that the Jeffreys divergence term can be rewritten as a covariance under the base model,
yielding an exact characterization of the optimal reward gain that does not require access to the aligned model.
Building on this, we proposed convergent estimators that recover the full KL–reward Pareto frontier from
base model samples alone. This enables practitioners to forecast achievable reward gains and benchmark
alignment algorithms against an achievable limit.
Empirically, we found that best-of-N sampling closely
tracks the theoretical Pareto frontier, while PPO and GRPO are suboptimal, pointing to significant room
for algorithmic improvement in gradient-based alignment.
Third, we leveraged our covariance characterization to analyze the gap between optimally aligned LMs using
the gold reward and under proxy rewards. We proved that this gap is governed by the inverse KL penalty
λ−1 and the mean square error between proxy and gold rewards. The dependency on the KL penalty explains
why aggressive alignment with proxy rewards leads to reward hacking, which was previously observed in [19].
Building on the same characterization, we show that reward ensembling provably mitigates reward hacking,
with convergence to ideal-reward alignment at a rate of O(n−1/2), providing a theoretical guarantee for this
widely used practice.

Implications and Future Work. On the practical side, our estimators for the KL vs. reward gain Pareto
frontier open the door to principled hyperparameter selection and algorithm evaluation: practitioners can
rely on the Pareto frontier to identify the KL budget required for a target reward level, and to compare the
performance of alignment algorithms in absolute terms.
On the modeling side, our analysis on the use of proxy rewards exposes a structural limitation of the dominant
reward-modeling paradigm.
Currently, rewards are trained under the Bradley-Terry (BT) model.
This
approach captures only the relative preference of responses, but not how much one is preferred over another.
As a consequence, the preference residual N between learned proxy and gold reward can grow unboundedly,
even with a perfectly minimized BT loss — see Sec. C for a more detailed justification. However, Prop. 3
shows that the preference residual is precisely the culprit behind the degradation of alignment quality. These
findings suggest that a new reward training paradigm, capable of directly constraining the preference residual
(for instance, by incorporating preference magnitudes) could mitigate reward hacking at its source, rather
than relying on post-hoc corrections such as ensembling. We believe that designing such feedback protocols,
and understanding how they interact with the fundamental limits derived here, are important directions for
future work.
Limitations. Our work has limitations we hope future work will address. First, our results characterize
the optimal solution to the KL-regularized objective in Equation (2.1); extending the analysis to alternative
objectives (e.g., f-divergence regularization, win-rate maximization, per-token KL divergence) is an interest-
ing direction. Second, our proposed estimators converge slowly for smaller λ (large KL); future work could
focus on designing better estimators for this regime. Finally, our analysis of reward hacking (Prop. 3) and
ensembling (Thm. 3) assumes proxy rewards are unbiased estimates of the gold reward on average across
proxy reward samples; characterizing the impact of systematic biases in proxy reward design is left for future
work.

On the need for a new paradigm for reward modeling
Proxy reward models are predominantly trained using the Bradley-Terry model, which frames human feed-
back strictly as binary pairwise comparisons over relative rankings, and thus fails to impose restrictions on
preference residuals. Specifically, under the BT framework, the probability that a response yw is preferred
over a response yl given a prompt x is modeled as Pr(yw ≻yl | x) = σ(˜r(x, yw) −˜r(x, yl)), where σ is
the logistic function. However, because this objective captures only the relative ordering of preferences, it
provides no structural guarantee that the learned proxy reward ˜r is quantitatively close to the unobserved
gold reward r∗in terms of preference residual. Hence, a reward can perfectly minimize the BT loss with an
arbitrarily large preference residual N(x, y).
The result in Prop. 3 shows that preference residual —uncontrolled in the BT framework— causes an expo-
nential decrease in the gold reward, leading to reward hacking. Conversely, Prop. 4 provides justification that
if the preference residual is small, then reward hacking is mitigated, i.e., gains in performance are transferred
to the evaluation using the gold reward.
Together, these results highlight the necessity for a new reward modeling paradigm: we must incorporate
preference magnitude to directly anchor the proxy reward, allowing us to actively measure and minimize its
absolute ℓ2 distance to the gold reward. Incorporating the magnitude of a preference would anchor the scale
of the proxy, providing a direct mechanism to measure and minimize the preference residual.

When do clustered preference clusters arise?
This two-component structure naturally emerges in practice from
mixture-of-sources candidate generation paired with within-source preference labeling. The following are
examples of when this emerges:
1. Helpfulness and Harmlessness We can generate one batch of candidates using a “helpful” decoding
regime (cluster VA(x)) and a second batch using a “safe/refusal” regime (cluster VB(x)), as in HH-RLHF
[4], where it is useful to train a helpful and harmless reward.
2. Subtle Safety Risks To reduce annotation costs, alignment pipelines may use a selective mechanism
where an AI labels simpler candidate pairs while humans evaluate only the most challenging ones. This
inadvertently creates isolated data clusters.
3. Reasoning A model might generate one batch of candidates using Chain-of-Thought reasoning, and
another batch using direct answers. If human raters compare only CoT responses to other CoT responses
(to judge the logic) and direct answers to direct answers (to judge conciseness), we have two clusters.
4. Tool Use When training an LLM to use external tools (like web search), we may evaluate tool-use
trajectories against other tool-uses to improve tool usage. These are never compared against a model
simply answering from its own internal weights.
In these scenarios, the BT model learns accurate within-cluster rankings but leaves the relative reward
offset between the clusters weakly identified. The shift preserves all observed BT pairwise differences while
potentially inflating the absolute preference residual N.

D
Reward-KL Pareto Frontier
Fig. 4 validates the practicality of our proposed estimators for the Pareto frontier by showing the convergence
of the entire theoretical Reward-KL Pareto frontier as a function of the number of base model samples n.
Figure 4 illustrates the estimated frontiers using n ∈{10, 100, 1000, 10000} across both tasks (safety and
summarization). We observe that while small sample sizes (e.g., n = 10 or 100) systematically underestimate
the achievable reward and prematurely truncate in the high-KL regime, the frontier rapidly stabilizes as
n increases. This behavior directly reflects the mechanics of the estimators in Def. 1 and Def. 2: in the
high-KL regime (which corresponds to a small penalty λ), the exponential tilt er/λ amplifies the variance
of high-reward samples, thereby requiring a larger number of samples to achieve a tight estimate. Crucially,
the curves for n = 1000 and n = 10000 are nearly overlapping across most of the KL spectrum. This macro
view of the frontier’s convergence complements the pointwise convergence guarantees of Prop. 1 and Prop. 2,
as well as the fixed-λ empirical results in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3. Ultimately, Fig. 4 confirms that a few thousand
samples (around one thousand) are sufficient to reliably delineate the fundamental limits of alignment from
base model samples alone.
