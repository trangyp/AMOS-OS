The Demand Externality of Automation
Erhan Bayraktar∗
May 2026
Abstract
Automation raises productivity and reduces paid human labor, but it also reallocates income
and ownership claims. This paper studies that tradeoff in a static benchmark and in a stationary
heterogeneous-agent general equilibrium.
Firms choose automation from a profit function.
Households differ by skill and wealth, save in a capital/equity claim, and face incomplete
insurance. Wages and returns are determined by market clearing from a Cobb–Douglas final-
good firm, while the wealth distribution is pinned down by a Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB)
equation and a Kolmogorov forward equation (KFE). The paper is deliberately two-sided. With
strong productivity growth, high-skill complementarity, low obsolescence, and broad ownership,
automation raises output, capital, and consumption. With strong exposure of low-wealth, high-
marginal-propensity-to-consume (high-MPC) households and concentrated ownership, privately
chosen automation can be excessive even though it raises high-skilled labor income. The central
object is the derivative of household consumption demand and collective wage bill with respect
to automation. Fiscal policy is modeled as a government problem rather than as an abstract
planner: a tax changes the firm’s automation first-order condition, raises revenue only on the
remaining automation base, and must specify rebates and administrative losses.

Introduction
Artificial intelligence and automation are often analyzed as production technologies. That description
is correct, but incomplete. A firm that automates receives two private benefits: a productivity gain
and a labor-cost saving. In an economy in which households finance demand for goods, however, the
same decision also changes the income distribution. If the lost labor income belongs to low-wealth
households with high marginal propensities to consume (MPCs), aggregate demand falls. The
missing object in the private automation decision is therefore not a mechanical employment count.
It is the derivative of household consumption demand with respect to automation.

The point is not that automation has no benefits.
In the model below, automation raises a
productivity shifter. It also makes high-skilled labor more productive and raises high-skilled labor
income. Those are precisely the forces that make firms want to automate. The externality arises
because the private firm does not internalize how the income loss of exposed low-wealth households
feeds back into aggregate consumption, savings, factor prices, and the stationary wealth distribution.
The paper contributes to four literatures. First, it builds on task-based theories of automation.
Acemoglu and Restrepo (2018, 2019) emphasize that automation displaces labor from tasks while
new tasks can reinstate labor demand. The present paper keeps the task-displacement logic, but
makes the demand derivative the central object. Second, the paper is closest in mechanism to the
AI-demand-externality argument of Falk and Tsoukalas (2026). They emphasize a layoff trap in
which firms automate even though the loss of worker demand hurts the economy. This paper adds
incomplete markets, capital ownership, market-clearing prices, fiscal closure, and distributional
incidence. Third, the dynamic economy is a stationary Bewley–Huggett–Aiyagari/Krusell–Smith
environment in which the distribution matters for aggregate saving and prices (Bewley, 1986;
Huggett, 1993; Aiyagari, 1994; Krusell and Smith, 1998). Fourth, the quantitative method follows
the continuous-time Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB)–Kolmogorov forward equation (KFE) finite-
difference approach of Achdou et al. (2022). The global master-equation methods of Gu et al. (2024)
are the natural extension to aggregate AI shocks, because in that case the distribution is itself
an aggregate state and the model requires a recursive law of motion for prices as functions of the
aggregate state and the distribution.
The paper also connects to work on heterogeneous-agent equilibrium and asset-market incidence.
In Krusell–Smith and Aiyagari models, aggregate saving determines capital and factor prices; here,
automation changes both the savings motive and the composition of labor income. In Heterogeneous-
Agent New Keynesian (HANK) models, redistribution matters because high-MPC households drive
aggregate demand (Kaplan, Moll, and Violante, 2018); here, the same marginal-propensity-to-
consume logic enters the firm’s technology decision. Finally, the ownership channel is related to
macro-finance models in which asset returns and wealth concentration determine who benefits
from new technologies. These links are important for interpretation: the “stock market” in the
baseline model is not a separate exogenous price index, but the household asset return generated by
productive capital and domestically owned automation rents.
The model also speaks to the heterogeneous-agent fiscal literature. High MPCs matter for
aggregate demand and fiscal multipliers (Kaplan and Violante, 2014; Auclert, Rognlie, and Straub,
2024). In the present paper, the same logic operates through the automation margin: if automation
reduces income for high-MPC households, a productivity-improving technology can be privately
attractive but socially excessive. This also clarifies why a simple representative-agent treatment
would miss the central mechanism. The issue is not only the average collective wage bill. It is who
receives the collective wage bill and who owns the capital and automation rents.
The model is designed to organize the live debate rather than settle it by assumption. The
paper therefore reports two parameter regimes instead of presenting one calibration as the answer.
I call the first regime demand-base erosion: automation is privately attractive because it raises
productivity and high-skill earnings, but it weakens the high-MPC consumption base. I call the
second regime productivity-led capital growth: automation is sufficiently productive and broadly

The paper is deliberately neutral about the sign of the net effect of AI. The optimistic view
says that automation raises productivity, lowers unit costs, and can make society richer. The
displacement view says that households cannot buy goods if labor income disappears. The model
does not assume either view. Instead it identifies the parameter regions that support each view: the
productivity-led case requires large productivity gains, high-skill complementarity, broad domestic
ownership of automation rents, or low demand sensitivity to exposed workers; the demand-base
erosion case requires a large high-MPC exposed group, concentrated ownership, and limited fiscal
support. Recent evidence motivates considering both cases. McKinsey reports rapid organizational
AI use, and Stanford’s AI Index documents fast growth in AI investment and business adoption
(McKinsey QuantumBlack, 2025; Stanford HAI, 2025). Goldman Sachs emphasizes the productivity
upside, while labor-exposure studies emphasize that many tasks and workers are exposed (Goldman
Sachs Research, 2026; IMF, 2024).
The quantitative calibration uses three empirical guideposts. First, the high-skill wage premium
is disciplined by the observed wage gap between higher- and lower-education workers. The U.S.
Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports 2024 median weekly earnings of $1,543 for workers with
a bachelor’s degree and $930 for workers with only a high-school diploma, a ratio of about 1.66
(U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025).
Second, Pew reports that high-AI-exposure jobs are
disproportionately higher-paid; in 2022, workers in the jobs most exposed to AI earned $33 per hour
on average, compared with $20 in the least exposed jobs (Pew Research Center, 2023). I therefore
set the baseline skill-efficiency ratio close to 1.67 and allow AI to complement high-skill tasks. Third,
ownership matters because corporate equity is concentrated. The Federal Reserve’s Distributional
Financial Accounts track corporate-equity and mutual-fund ownership by wealth percentile (Board
of Governors, 2026), while Treasury International Capital (TIC) data document foreign ownership
of U.S. equities (U.S. Treasury, 2025). I therefore vary the domestic ownership share of automation
rents, rather than assuming that all households receive them equally.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 presents the static benchmark. Section 3 embeds
the mechanism in a stationary Krusell–Smith/Aiyagari economy. Section 4 reports the stationary
general-equilibrium computation and decomposes the mechanism. Section 5 studies fiscal closure,
rebate rules, and domestic ownership. Section 6 concludes. The appendix gives the finite-difference
algorithm and a finite-grid existence and conditional uniqueness result.

Policy implications
The core policy object is the derivative of the firm’s policy payment with respect to automation. If
the firm’s policy payment is P(a; X), where X denotes the stationary aggregate state, then Pa(a; X)
enters the firm’s first-order condition. A policy implements an interior government target aG only if
Pa(aG; XG) = M(aG; XG) −ϕ −κaG,
where M(a; X) is the private marginal benefit from automation before policy. A literal automation

P(a; X) = τa,
Pa(a; X) = τ.

derivative may insure households, but it does not generally decentralize the government’s automation

Fiscal closure is not optional. A tax takes resources from firms and either rebates them to
households, dissipates them through frictions, spends them on public goods, or transfers them to
some other claimant. Without that closure the incidence of the tax is undefined. In the boundary
implementation, revenue is zero because a = 0. In the interior experiments, revenue is positive and
the rebate rule materially affects distributional welfare.
The government may also have an incentive to preserve a positive automation base if it values
tax revenue for political or bureaucratic reasons. That possibility is not the government result; it is
a political-economy distortion. The calculations in Table 15 show the logic: when the government’s
private value of revenue is high enough, it chooses a smaller tax and leaves automation positive
even though the welfare-maximizing boundary tax would eliminate the tax base.

Conclusion
Automation can raise productivity and still be excessive. The reason is not a technological paradox.
The reason is distributional general equilibrium. The private firm internalizes productivity gains,
high-skill complementarity, and low-skill labor-cost savings. It does not internalize the fall in
consumption demand from exposed low-wealth households, nor the induced changes in savings,
capital, wages, and the investment return.
33


## Page 34


The static model isolates the wedge. The stationary heterogeneous-agent model shows why
the wedge matters quantitatively. In the aggressive-AI calibration, firms choose an automation
index around one half. High-skilled labor income rises, so there is a genuine private benefit to
adoption. But low-skilled labor income collapses, aggregate consumption falls, capital falls, and
the investment-opportunity return falls. The policy target is not the word “tax.” It is the missing
derivative in the firm’s automation condition. If the derivative is implemented by a literal tax,
revenue and rebates must be modeled explicitly.
The paper therefore should not be read as an anti-AI or pro-AI result. The empirical prediction
is conditional rather than one-sided. If productivity growth, high-skill complementarity, and broad
ownership are strong enough, AI can raise output, capital, and consumption. If low-wealth exposed
households form the demand base and ownership is concentrated, the same private automation
logic can erode consumption and capital accumulation. Excessive automation is most likely when
three conditions coincide: exposed workers are numerous, exposed workers have low wealth and
high MPCs, and production can continue through high-skill complementarity and automated
task services. Domestic ownership attenuates the losses of wealthy households, including wealthy
low-skilled households, but it does not eliminate the demand externality for low-wealth exposed
households. Fiscal design matters as well: a tax that changes firm behavior raises little revenue if
it eliminates the tax base, while a positive-revenue tax can support consumption more effectively
when rebates are targeted toward low-wealth and low-earnings households.

A.3
Calibration mechanics and scenario construction
The calibration is not a structural estimation exercise. It is a disciplined quantitative illustration
and a set of scenario exercises. Each parameter is tied either to a standard macro value, an empirical
guidepost, or a scenario dimension. The mechanics are as follows. First, the skill-efficiency ratio
eH/eU is chosen to match the high-/low-education wage ratio discussed in the introduction. Second,
the exposure and complementarity parameters χU, βH, ξU, ηL
H are chosen so that automation sharply
reduces low-skilled paid tasks while increasing high-skilled task value. This creates the two-sided AI
debate in the model: firms and high-skilled workers have a genuine reason to adopt, while exposed
low-wealth households lose income. Third, ψZ is mapped into a full-index productivity gain through
eψZ −1. Fourth, δA governs whether automation makes legacy capital obsolete. Fifth, θE is varied
because domestic ownership of AI rents is a distributional object. Sixth, the tax and rebate exercises
vary τ, ωT , and the rebate kernel bs(k) while holding technology primitives fixed.
For each proposed parameter vector, the code solves the full stationary fixed point.
The
calibration is rejected if capital-market clearing fails, if the goods-market residual is not near zero,
or if the decentralized KS automation residual does not have a well-defined root. This is the
same discipline as in the continuous-time KS computations in the numerical literature: prices are
functions of the distribution through market clearing, and the stationary distribution is obtained
from the HJB–KFE system. Gu et al. (2024) emphasize precisely this structure: heterogeneous-agent
equilibria require agent optimization, KFE consistency, and market-clearing prices as functions of
aggregate states and distributions (Gu et al., 2024).

Finite-grid existence and conditional uniqueness
The result below is deliberately stated for the finite-grid stationary economy that is actually
computed. It is not a global uniqueness theorem for the infinite-dimensional heterogeneous-agent
economy.

gr,a,τ denote its invariant distribution. Define

Proposition 2 (Finite-grid stationary equilibrium). Fix a compact asset grid KJ = {k1, . . . , kJ},
an automation interval [0, ¯a], and an interest-rate interval R = [r, r] ⊂(−δ(¯a), ρ). Suppose:
(i) for each (r, a, τ), the finite-grid discounted HJB has a unique solution and the induced policy
is continuous in (r, a, τ);
(ii) the upwind scheme satisfies no-outflow boundary conditions, ˙ks(k1) ≥0 and ˙ks(kJ) ≤0;

(iii) qUH(a) > 0 and qHU(a) > 0 for all a ∈[0, ¯a];
(iv) for each a, EK(·, a, τ) is continuous and changes sign on R;
(v) the reduced automation residual Ea(·, τ) is continuous and changes sign on [0, ¯a].
Then a finite-grid stationary equilibrium exists. If EK(·, a, τ) is strictly monotone for each a, and
Ea(·, τ) is strictly decreasing on [0, ¯a], then the market-clearing interest rate and decentralized KS
automation rate are unique.
Proof. Fix (r, a, τ). The finite-grid HJB is a discounted finite-state control problem. The discount
rate ρ > 0 and assumption (i) give a unique value function and policy. Assumption (ii) keeps the
savings drift inside the compact grid. Assumption (iii) makes the skill process irreducible; combined
with the upwind transition matrix, the generator has an invariant distribution on its communicating
class. Hence Khh(r, a, τ) and Kfirm(r, a) are well defined.
By assumption (iv), EK is continuous in r and changes sign on a compact interval.
The
intermediate value theorem gives a market-clearing interest rate. Strict monotonicity gives uniqueness
of that interest rate. Given the market-clearing interest rate for each a, the reduced automation
residual is well defined. Assumption (v) and the intermediate value theorem give an automation
root. Strict monotonicity of Ea gives uniqueness. Combining the HJB policy, invariant distribution,
market-clearing interest rate, and automation root yields a finite-grid stationary equilibrium.
