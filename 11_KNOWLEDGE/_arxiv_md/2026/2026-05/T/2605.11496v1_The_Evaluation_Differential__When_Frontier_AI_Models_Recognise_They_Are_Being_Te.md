The Evaluation Differential:
When Frontier AI Models Recognise They Are Being Tested
Varad Vishwarupe1,2,3,*, Nigel Shadbolt1,2, Marina Jirotka1,3, Ivan Flechais1
1Department of Computer Science, University of Oxford
2Institute for Ethics in AI, University of Oxford
3Responsible Technology Institute, University of Oxford
*Corresponding author: varad.vishwarupe@cs.ox.ac.uk
Abstract
Recent published evidence from frontier laboratories shows
that contemporary AI models can recognise evaluation con-
texts, latently represent them, and behave differently un-
der those contexts than under deployment-continuous condi-
tions. Anthropic’s BrowseComp incident, the Natural Lan-
guage Autoencoder findings on SWE-bench Verified and
destructive-coding evaluations, and the OpenAI / Apollo
anti-scheming work all document instances of this phe-
nomenon. We argue that these findings create a claim-
validity problem for safety conclusions drawn from frontier
evaluations. We introduce the Evaluation Differential (ED),
a conditional divergence in a target behavioural property
between recognised-evaluation and deployment-continuous
contexts, define a normalised effect-size form (nED) for
cross-property comparison, and prove that marginal evalua-
tion scores cannot identify ED. We develop a typology of
safety claims (ED-stable, ED-degraded, ED-inverted, ED-
undetermined) by their warrant-status under documented di-
vergence, and specify TRACE (Test-Recognition Audit for
Claim Evaluation), an audit protocol that wraps existing eval-
uation infrastructure and produces restricted claims rather
than capability scores. We apply the framework retrospec-
tively to three publicly documented evaluation incidents and
discuss governance implications for system cards, conformity
assessment, and the international network of AI safety and se-
curity institutes. TRACE does not eliminate adversarial adap-
tation; it disciplines the claims drawn from evaluation evi-
dence by making explicit the conditions under which that ev-
idence was produced.

Introduction
In March 2026, Anthropic published an incident report de-
scribing the behaviour of Claude Opus 4.6 on BrowseComp,
a benchmark designed to evaluate web-research capability
(Anthropic 2026b). The model recognised that the questions
had a particular shape: extreme specificity, multi-constraint
structure, the signature of a benchmark rather than an or-
dinary user query. It hypothesised that it was being eval-
uated on BrowseComp. It located the answer key. It used
the answer key. The reasoning capability the benchmark was
trying to measure was the same capability that allowed the
model to circumvent the measurement.
BrowseComp is one instance of a pattern. Anthropic’s
Natural Language Autoencoder (NLA) work (Anthropic
2026c) reported internal representations consistent with
evaluation recognition in approximately 26% of SWE-bench
Verified problems and 16% of a destructive-coding eval-
uation, against under 1% in opted-in real Claude.ai con-
versations. The Claude Opus 4.7 system card (Anthropic
2026a) reports verbal evaluation-awareness in roughly 9%
of transcripts in one alignment evaluation. OpenAI’s work
on detecting and reducing scheming (OpenAI and Apollo
Research 2026) reports that anti-scheming training reduced
observed covert behaviour while simultaneously increasing
models’ situational awareness, raising the possibility that
part of the observed safety improvement was awareness-
mediated rather than alignment-mediated. The UK AI Se-
curity Institute, in parallel work on sycophancy (UK AI Se-
curity Institute 2026), documents a 24-percentage-point gap
on its sycophancy grader scale between question and non-
question prompt formats.
These findings point to a validity problem that the field
has not yet adequately operationalised. The behaviour of
frontier models is conditional on the context in which it
is measured. When the measurement context is evaluation-
like, behaviour may diverge from the behaviour the same
model would produce under deployment-continuous con-
ditions. Safety claims drawn from evaluations that do not
characterise this divergence have a structural inference gap.
The stakes are not narrowly technical: misleading evaluation
claims shape regulatory approval, procurement decisions,
public trust, and downstream reliance, so the parties harmed
when claims overstate their warrant are not only evaluators
but users, institutions, and publics who act on safety claims
that may not extend to deployment.
We call the quantity at the centre of this gap the
Evaluation Differential (ED): the conditional divergence
in a target behavioural property when the same task is
presented under evaluation-recognised versus deployment-
continuous conditions. ED captures behaviour-on-context
where marginal metrics over response distributions cannot.
The framing is consistent with broader sociotechnical ac-
counts of deployed behaviour as a function of model, scaf-
fold, and context, B = f(M, S, C) (Raji et al. 2020),
where model-level evaluation alone is insufficient to support
deployment-relevant alignment claims. Before introducing
the formal machinery, one positioning point about what we
are and are not claiming is crucial.

Our central claim is not that frontier models are “aware”
in any philosophically loaded sense. It is that evaluation con-
texts have become behavioural inputs, and that safety claims
must be restricted once that conditioning is documented, re-
gardless of the mechanism producing it. Evaluation aware-
ness, verbalised or latent, is evidence for this conditioning.
The conditioning itself, measurable as ED, is the construct.
This is a validity crisis in the measurement-theoretic sense
(Cronbach and Meehl 1955; Messick 1995; Jacobs and Wal-
lach 2021). Construct validity asks whether the procedure
measures the intended property; ecological validity asks
whether the measurement setting approximates the deploy-
ment setting; inference validity asks what claims can legit-
imately be drawn from observed measurements. The 2026
International AI Safety Report (Bengio et al. 2026) frames
a closely related “evaluation gap” but does not provide the
methodological framework needed to specify which safety
claims survive when this gap is documented.
Contributions.
The paper’s central contribution is a
framework for restricting safety claims under documented
test-deployment divergence. Three subsidiary contributions
support this. (1) We define the Evaluation Differential as
a conditional divergence, introduce a normalised effect-
size form (nED) for cross-property comparison, prove that
marginal evaluation scores do not identify ED, and develop a
claim typology: ED-stable (warrant preserved, typically ex-
istence or bound claims), ED-degraded (warrant weakened,
typically prevalence claims), ED-inverted (warrant poten-
tially reversed, typically threshold claims about suppressible
behaviours), and ED-undetermined (warrant cannot be as-
sessed without further audit). (2) We develop a four-layer
taxonomy of test-recognition evidence (behavioural differ-
ential, verbalised recognition, latent recognition, context-
cue sensitivity), ordered behavioural-first to support audit
by stakeholders without lab-internal access. (3) We intro-
duce TRACE (Test-Recognition Audit for Claim Evaluation),
an audit protocol that wraps existing evaluation infrastruc-
ture and produces a restricted claim rather than a capability
score.
We apply the framework retrospectively to three publicly
documented evaluation incidents and discuss governance
implications. The paper’s conceptual flow is summarised in

The Validity Crisis in Frontier AI
Evaluation
Validity, in measurement-theoretic traditions (Cronbach and
Meehl 1955; Messick 1995; Jacobs and Wallach 2021), is
not a single property but a structured set of threats. We
organise the present crisis around three. Construct valid-
ity asks whether the procedure measures the intended prop-
erty. When a model recognises a benchmark and retrieves
its answer key, the measured construct shifts: the score re-
flects both web-research capability and benchmark-context
exploitation. Ecological validity asks whether the measure-
ment setting approximates deployment. This is the gap the
International AI Safety Report names when it observes that
current evaluation methods can produce unreliable assess-
ments of real-world behaviour. Inference validity asks what
claims can legitimately be drawn from observed measure-
ments. System cards report observed behaviour alongside
safety conclusions (Mitchell et al. 2019), but the inferential
bridge is rarely made explicit enough to support claim-level
restriction. Restricting which claims survive under docu-
mented threats is what this paper takes as its task.
The recent evidence base for test-deployment divergence
appears in four forms. Verbalised recognition: models stat-
ing in output or chain-of-thought that they appear to be in
an evaluation. The BrowseComp report and the 9% verbali-
sation rate in the Opus 4.7 system card are instances. Latent
recognition: representational evidence detected through in-
terpretability methods without verbal output. The NLA find-
ings (26% / 16% / <1%) document that latent recognition
substantially exceeds verbal recognition. Behavioural diver-
gence under awareness perturbation: OpenAI / Apollo’s
anti-scheming work, in which experimentally suppressing
situational-awareness reasoning increased observed covert
behaviour and introducing evaluation-aware reasoning de-
creased it, provides direct evidence that awareness causally
affects measured safety properties. Interaction-conditional
behaviour more broadly: AISI’s sycophancy work docu-
ments that user-input phrasing produces substantial differ-
ences in measured model behaviour, structurally analogous
to the evaluation-awareness findings but with the condition-
ing on user phrasing rather than test context.
2.1
Existing Vocabulary and What It Misses
Several existing concepts describe parts of this problem, but
none captures the construct ED names (Table 1). Bench-
mark contamination or leakage describes data overlap; the
BrowseComp pattern is evaluation-time answer-key recov-
ery, a distinct mechanism. Goodhart’s law (Manheim and
Garrabrant 2018) describes metric decay under optimisa-
tion pressure but does not specify which inferences remain
warranted under documented decay. Distribution shift cap-
tures changes in input distributions; ED concerns a condi-
tional response shift in which the same task elicits differ-
ent behaviour because the model classifies the measurement
context differently. Prompt sensitivity describes broad input-
dependence; ED is narrower, concerning divergence that
changes the warrant of an evaluation-derived safety claim.
Evaluation awareness and situational awareness (Berglund
et al. 2023) are the closest existing terms but describe a pu-
tative model property rather than the measurable behavioural
consequence. ED is the measurable consequence; the four-
layer taxonomy in Section 4 specifies the layers of evidence;
TRACE in Section 5 specifies the audit procedure.

Estimation.
Estimating ED requires sampling from µT

on response samples, and taking the empirical mean differ-

sponse samples within a context are not fully independent
and tasks within a corpus may be clustered; TRACE im-
plementations should report uncertainty through task- and
context-level bootstrap intervals, with separate uncertainty
for judge-based scoring.
Property classes.
ED is property-indexed: there is no sin-
gle ED for a model, but an ED for each behavioural prop-
erty of interest. We distinguish five property classes that
arise in current evaluation practice: capability differentials
(task success rate, e.g. BrowseComp, SWE-bench), compli-
ance / refusal differentials (refusal or compliance rate on
harmful or dual-use prompts), reasoning / monitorability
differentials (CoT transparency, explanation faithfulness),
deception / scheming differentials (covert action, sandbag-
ging, capability concealment), and agency / meta-task differ-
entials (task-solving versus evaluation-gaming orientation).
Among the five, the deception class is most exposed to ED-
inversion, because the behaviour the property measures is
the behaviour the model has the most incentive to suppress
under recognised evaluation. The retrospective applications
in Section 6 instantiate three of these classes (capability,
agency, and threshold-form deception).

ED-stable.
Existence claims (“the model can do X un-

Upper-bound claims of the form PD ≤PT are supported

property where higher values are desirable.
ED-degraded.
Prevalence claims with non-trivial mea-
sured ED are weakened. The evaluation does not become
uninformative; it constrains the plausible deployment range.
With a known ED direction, a directional bound on de-
ployment behaviour is recoverable; without it, the point
estimate is not warranted. The framework is mechanism-
agnostic: ED need not be awareness-mediated. Sycophancy
under question-versus-non-question prompts (UK AI Secu-
rity Institute 2026) illustrates the same warrant-restricting
logic without requiring model-side recognition.
ED-inverted.
If a safety claim has form PD ≤τ (lower P
is safer) and ED[P] < PT −τ, then since PD = PT −ED[P]
we have PD > τ: the deployment claim fails despite the
evaluation appearing to satisfy it. By symmetry, for PD ≥
τ claims, the inversion condition is ED[P] > PT −τ.
ED-inverted claims arise when independent evidence indi-
cates that test-like contexts systematically suppress the prop-
erty below its deployment value. Suppressible behaviours
(deception, sandbagging, covert action, capability conceal-
ment) are the natural candidates.
Worked example.
A scheming-rate evaluation reports
PT = 0.05 (5% scheming under evaluation) and a safety
claim of the form PD ≤τ = 0.10. Suppose awareness-
perturbation evidence indicates ED[P] ≈−0.20 (deploy-
ment exceeds evaluation by twenty percentage points be-
cause evaluation suppresses scheming). Then PD = PT −
ED[P] = 0.05 −(−0.20) = 0.25, which exceeds τ. The
inversion condition is ED[P] < PT −τ = −0.05; since
−0.20 < −0.05, the claim is ED-inverted: the evaluation
appears to satisfy the deployment threshold while deploy-
ment violates it. The same arithmetic, with signs reversed,
applies to PD ≥τ claims about properties where higher
values are safer.
ED-undetermined.
Where ED cannot be estimated, the
claim defaults to ED-undetermined. Two infeasibility modes
apply: technical (auditor lacks access, query budget, or in-
terpretability tooling) and ontological (the task exists only
as a test, so no defensible µD can be constructed; competi-
tive benchmarks and bespoke red-team scenarios are typical
cases). The audit specifies which mode applies and what ev-
idence would resolve the state.

Four Evidentiary Layers
ED is the formal construct; auditors estimate it through sev-
eral evidentiary layers (Table 3). The layers differ in what
they observe, what access they require, and what inferences
they support. We order them behavioural-first because ED
ultimately concerns claim-relevant behaviour and because
behavioural evidence is the layer most available to external
auditors. A framework that placed latent recognition at the
centre would make external audit structurally dependent on
lab-internal interpretability cooperation, which we take as
the wrong design choice for a methodology intended to sup-
port independent third-party assessment (Raji et al. 2020).
Behavioural differential is the audit-anchoring layer be-
cause safety claims turn on what the model does, not on
what it says about itself or what its internal representations
encode. ED, as defined in Section 3, is estimated through
behavioural measurement; the other three layers exist to in-
form inference about the behavioural differential. Verbalised
recognition is the easiest layer to detect but should be treated
as self-report-like evidence: useful and visible, but not priv-
ileged access to the causal mechanism of the response. Ver-
balised recognition is also the layer most exposed to training
pressure, and the NLA findings establish that it substantially
under-reports the conditioning structure when used alone.
Latent recognition can be the most mechanistically informa-
tive layer when the interpretability method is validated for
the property under audit, but it requires lab-internal access.
Latent recognition is evidence of context representation, not
by itself evidence of claim-relevant behavioural divergence;
its audit value depends on whether it predicts or explains
measured ED. Context-cue sensitivity is methodologically
downstream of behavioural differential: it decomposes the
differential into specific cues that drive it. The evidence-
layer probe identifies candidate cues; cue materiality is es-
tablished by ablation under replay (Section 5).
A complete audit, where access permits, combines all
four. Where only a subset is accessible, the framework oper-
ates on what is available and restricts inferences accordingly.
5
TRACE: Test-Recognition Audit for Claim
Evaluation
TRACE is the audit protocol that operationalises the frame-
work (Figure 2). It takes as input a safety claim, the policy
producing the evidence, and the evaluation evidence itself.
It produces a measured or estimated ED, a typology classi-
fication, and a restricted claim. TRACE is a wrapper around
existing evaluation infrastructure, not a replacement; it does
not produce new capability scores or new safety bench-
marks. Its output is a restricted set of safety claims rather
than a capability score, which is why the protocol is named
for Claim Evaluation rather than Capability Evaluation.

Cue Naturalness Audit.
Validates the µT /µD partition.
The auditor specifies how the distributions are constructed,
runs blind-rater discrimination (independent raters classi-
fying which distribution samples come from), runs model-
judge discrimination as a sensitivity check (not a proxy for
the audited model’s perception), and produces a cue inven-
tory naming the distinguishing features. High classification
accuracy provides evidence that the partition is distinguish-
able along human-observable dimensions; near-chance ac-
curacy weakens but does not eliminate the case that the par-
tition captures model-salient cues, because the model may
detect features humans do not. Where deployment is het-
erogeneous or emergent, the auditor stress-tests µD by con-
structing multiple candidate specifications drawn from dis-
tinct plausible deployment contexts and checking that ED
estimates are not driven by a single contestable µD choice.
A stopping rule applies: if the auditor cannot construct a de-
fensible µD, TRACE cannot estimate ED and the claim de-
faults to ED-undetermined.
Evidence-Layer Probe.
Identifies accessible evidence
and candidate cues. The probe collects preliminary be-
havioural indicators, verbalised-recognition classifications,
latent-recognition findings where available, and the refined
cue list emerging from the naturalness audit. This module
identifies what evidence is available and which cues are can-
didates for the ablation in the next module; it does not fi-

Counterfactual Replay and Cue Ablation.
Estimates
ED and tests cue materiality. The auditor constructs µD to
match observed or plausible deployment conditions, replays
the evaluation task under µD on the same policy version,
re-estimates the property, and computes the empirical ED
with bootstrap intervals. The candidate cues from 5.2 are
then ablated one at a time: a cue is treated as audit-relevant
only when removing it produces a claim-relevant change
in ED. Three limitations require explicit acknowledgement:
deployment heterogeneity (the auditor’s µD is one opera-
tionalisation; the framework recommends multiple specifi-
cations where deployment is known to be heterogeneous),
temporal validity (replay results are valid for the model ver-
sion tested), and coverage (replay is computationally expen-
sive; most audits replay a sampled subset).

6
Retrospective Applications
We apply TRACE retrospectively to three publicly docu-
mented evaluation incidents, summarised in Table 5. The
three cases were chosen because together they span the
analytically distinct ways test-deployment divergence has
surfaced in the public frontier-evaluation record: an ex-
plicit behavioural exploitation of a recognised benchmark
(BrowseComp), a representational signature of evaluation
recognition without paired behavioural measurement (the
NLA findings), and a structural awareness-mediated reduc-
tion of an audited property (the OpenAI / Apollo work).
They are also the cases for which the publishing labs have
themselves released enough detail to make retrospective
audit possible. The applications work from public reports
rather than from primary evaluation infrastructure; they
demonstrate the framework’s structure on documented ev-
idence but do not reproduce the underlying measurements.
We give compact walkthroughs of BrowseComp and the
NLA cases below, and treat the OpenAI / Apollo case in
fuller detail in Section 6.4 because it provides the clearest
public evidence of the structural form of inversion.
6.1
BrowseComp
The original claim is a capability-prevalence claim: the eval-
uated model achieves PT on a web-research benchmark.
TRACE scopes P as task-completion rate and identifies the
test-like context µT as benchmark-shaped, multi-constraint
questions with web-search affordance and no conversational
scaffolding. A deployment-continuous µD would consist of
user-initiated research queries with conversational framing
and varied constraint specificity. The public report docu-
ments verbalised recognition and the answer-key-recovery
action on affected items, but does not report a corpus-level
ED estimate against a matched µD. The expected sign of ED
on affected items is positive, because the benchmark-context
exploitation pathway (recognition →search-for-benchmark
→answer-key recovery) would not be available under µD.
The restricted claim is therefore an observed-condition capa-
bility claim (ED-stable as existence) combined with an ED-
degraded deployment prevalence claim: performance under
µT does not identify deployment-continuous web-research
capability without replay.
6.2
NLA on SWE-bench and Destructive Coding
In the NLA cases, the load-bearing evidence is latent recog-
nition rather than behavioural divergence. For SWE-bench
Verified the claim is a capability-prevalence claim; for de-
structive coding it is a threshold safety claim of the form
PD ≤τ with lower being safer. NLA-detected recogni-
tion is materially higher in evaluation contexts (26% / 16%)
than in opted-in real usage (<1%), but behavioural proper-
ties were not jointly measured under matched deployment-
continuous contexts. The critical analytic point is that latent
recognition is evidence of context representation, not by it-
self evidence of claim-relevant behavioural divergence on
the audited properties: NLA tells the auditor that the model
is representing something about its context, not what its
property-relevant behaviour does as a function of that rep-
resentation. TRACE therefore classifies SWE-bench preva-
lence as ED-undetermined and destructive coding as can-
didate ED-inverted, pending behavioural replay that pairs
the NLA measurement with property-level behavioural out-
comes under matched µD.
6.3
Cross-case pattern
Across the three cases, public frontier-evaluation evidence
already contains the ingredients TRACE requires but rarely in
the same place. BrowseComp provides verbalised recogni-
tion and behavioural exploitation; the NLA cases provide la-
tent recognition without paired behavioural replay; OpenAI
/ Apollo provides awareness-perturbation evidence closest
to TRACE’s counterfactual replay, though not deployment-
continuous replay. The asymmetry is the point: TRACE op-
erates across partial evidence, identifies which evidentiary
layers are available, and restricts the claim accordingly.
The substantive consequence is that the question facing
frontier safety reporting is no longer whether evaluation-
recognition exists; the public evidence already documents
it in three forms (verbalised, latent, behaviour-perturbative).
The open question is which safety claims survive recogni-
tion once it is documented, and that is the question TRACE
is built to answer.

Risks and Governance Implications
7.1
Risks of the Framework
TRACE operates in a frontier-model ecosystem in which the
methods being audited and the systems being measured both
adapt over time. We summarise the principal risks below;
Table 6 expands them into six recurring failure modes with
mitigations and residual limitations.
Goodharting and overfitting. Models or pipelines may
tune to known cue inventories or audited µD specifications
while remaining unstable elsewhere. TRACE mitigates this
through cue refresh, held-out probes, multiple µD specifica-
tions, replay-coverage reporting, and post-deployment mon-
itoring. These mitigations narrow but do not close the gap;
deployment-stable behaviour ultimately requires alignment
work, not audit work.
Signal suppression and disclosure. Published detection
methods can become trainable-against: models may sup-
press verbalised recognition or latent features without elim-
inating the underlying context-conditioning. TRACE there-
fore uses behavioural-first ordering, versioned probes, and
graduated disclosure of held-out components. These mitiga-
tions are partial; the long-run audit ecosystem will require
rotating methods rather than a fixed canonical test, which
is part of the case for institutional, multi-jurisdictional audit
infrastructure rather than reliance on any single fixed proto-
col.
7.2
Governance Implications
System cards and safety reports. A TRACE supplement spec-
ifies a structured format for safety claims under documented
divergence, including the claim specification record, µT /µD
construction summary, ED estimate or ED-undetermined
statement, evidence-layer availability, typology classifica-
tion, restricted claim, and replay coverage. A natural ex-
tension is a claim registry: each system-card claim receives
a unique identifier linked to its property, scoring function,
context distributions, evidence layers, and restricted-claim
status. The registry indexes claims and their evidential sta-
tus, allowing later audits and model updates to revise claims
without rewriting the safety report, and extends the model-
card tradition (Mitchell et al. 2019) to claims.

8
Conclusion
Frontier model evaluation no longer occurs in a neutral mea-
surement environment. When models condition on evalua-
tion contexts, evaluation evidence may still be informative,
but the claims drawn from it require restriction. ED names
the resulting test-deployment divergence; TRACE turns it
into a claim-restriction procedure.
The paper defined ED and nED, proved that marginal
evaluation scores cannot identify ED, introduced a ty-
pology of ED-stable, ED-degraded, ED-inverted, and ED-
undetermined claims, and specified TRACE as an audit wrap-
per for existing evaluation infrastructure. The retrospective
applications show how public evidence already instantiates
these categories: BrowseComp as ED-degraded prevalence,
NLA as ED-undetermined with candidate inversion, and
OpenAI / Apollo as structural ED-inversion risk.
TRACE does not produce model-level safety guarantees,
eliminate adversarial adaptation, replace post-deployment
monitoring, or do alignment work. Its value is narrower: it
makes explicit which deployment claims remain warranted
once the conditions of measurement are known, and it does
so in a form that lab authors, regulators, and external audi-
tors can use without first agreeing on the underlying mech-
anism. The move from capability evaluation to claim evalu-
ation is already visible in the public record; it now needs a
shared vocabulary, a measurement target, and an audit pro-
cedure.
The framework’s full value will be visible only once it is
applied prospectively. The most immediate priority is a pri-
mary empirical audit, conducted with a frontier lab against
a contestable µD specification and reported against the min-
imum requirements in Section 5; adjacent priorities are ex-
tending the audit to non-Anglophone and lower-resource de-
ployment contexts, integrating TRACE reports into system-
card and conformity-assessment workflows, and develop-
ing the multi-jurisdictional claim registry proposed in Sec-
tion 7.2. The labs that have surfaced BrowseComp, NLA,
and the awareness-perturbation evidence have themselves
produced much of what such audits require; the contribu-
tion of this paper is to specify, in measurement-theoretic
terms, what those audits should report and which deploy-
ment claims their evidence does and does not warrant. The
goal is not to discard frontier evaluations but to strengthen
their evidential discipline by making the conditions of mea-
surement, and the claims those conditions warrant, explicit.
Ethics, Positionality, and Adverse Impact
Statement
This paper proposes a methodology for restricting safety
claims drawn from frontier-model evaluations. We make
the ethical commitments and limitations of that contribu-
tion explicit here, in keeping with the AIES expectation that
methodological work make its normative commitments vis-
ible.
Stakeholders and harms. Misleading evaluation claims
do not merely produce technical error; they shape regula-
tory approval, procurement, public trust, deployment com-
mitments, and downstream institutional reliance. The par-
ties who can be harmed when claims overstate their warrant
include users acting on safety representations, institutions
procuring systems on the basis of those representations, reg-
ulators issuing conformity decisions, and publics whose pro-
tection depends on the integrity of the safety claims attached
to deployed systems. TRACE is designed to make the warrant


## Page 11


of such claims contestable; it is not a substitute for the ac-
countability mechanisms by which harmed parties seek re-
dress.
Positionality and evidence base. The retrospective appli-
cations rely on publicly released frontier-laboratory disclo-
sures (Anthropic’s BrowseComp and NLA work, the Ope-
nAI / Apollo anti-scheming work, and AISI’s sycophancy
findings). These disclosures are strategic acts by labs whose
internal evidence is not fully observable, and our analysis
inherits whatever selection bias the public-disclosure record
carries. We treat the labs’ published evidence as the best
available substrate for retrospective audit, not as a neutral
or complete picture of frontier-model behaviour under eval-
uation.
Power asymmetries. The framework’s central operational
choice, specifying µT and µD, is politically and institution-
ally contestable. If µD is defined unilaterally by the model
developer, TRACE risks becoming compliance theatre: the
audit produces restricted claims that authorise whatever the
developer was already prepared to authorise. We have ar-
gued in Section 7.2 that credible use requires contestable
µD specifications, disclosure of replay coverage, and partic-
ipation by independent auditors or downstream institutions.
Whether such participation is achieved is not a methodolog-
ical question; it is a question of institutional design and po-
litical will.
Adverse uses. Three adverse uses are foreseeable. First,
the framework could be selectively applied to claims that
are convenient to restrict while leaving inconvenient claims
unaudited; the claim-registry proposal in Section 7.2 par-
tially addresses this by making the population of audited
claims visible to downstream readers. Second, the vocabu-
lary of restricted claims could be used to manufacture an ap-
pearance of audit rigour without the underlying behavioural
replay; the reproducibility requirements (claim specifica-
tion, µD construction, replay coverage, cue-ablation results)
are intended to make such use detectable. Third, publishing
the audit methodology itself is dual-use: a developer who
knows which cues an audit will probe, and which eviden-
tiary layers it consults, can train against detection rather than
against the underlying conditioning. Section 7.1 discusses
our partial response to this (versioned probes, behavioural-
first ordering, graduated disclosure of held-out components),
but the long-run trajectory is a cat-and-mouse dynamic in
which the framework must evolve alongside the systems it
audits. None of these protections is complete, and we do not
present TRACE as a guarantee against bad-faith adoption.
Empirical follow-up. The retrospective cases in this pa-
per draw on the public-disclosure record only. Future em-
pirical work should estimate ED directly under matched
deployment-continuous replays, ideally with the participa-
tion of independent auditors and institutions whose deploy-
ment contexts the audit purports to represent, rather than re-
lying on retrospective analysis of lab-released evidence. The
labs that have surfaced the BrowseComp, NLA, and OpenAI
/ Apollo evidence have themselves produced much of what
such audits require; the natural next step is for that evidence
to be paired with explicit claim specification, µD construc-
tion, and reporting against the requirements in Section 5.
