Abstract
We introduce a mesoscopic partition function for classical many-body systems
based on a combined spatial and phase-space coarse-graining, replacing the canon-
ical phase-space integral with a discrete sum over occupation numbers. The con-
struction recovers the standard canonical partition function in the fine-graining
limit. Our main result shows that factorisation of the mesoscopic partition func-
tion across spatial cells is equivalent to extensivity of the coarse-grained free en-
ergy, with deviations governed by inter-cell correlations quantifiable via mutual
information. We derive a generalised Euler relation with a subextensive correction
encoding boundary and correlation effects. Together, these results provide a uni-
fied framework linking coarse-graining, factorisation, and extensivity in mesoscopic
thermodynamics.

Introduction
The relationship between microscopic dynamics and macroscopic thermodynamic be-
haviour has been a central problem since the foundational work of Boltzmann and
Gibbs [1, 2].
A major achievement in this direction is kinetic theory, in which the
Boltzmann equation provides a systematic route from particle dynamics to macroscopic
transport laws, recovering continuum descriptions such as the Navier–Stokes equations
in appropriate limits [3, 4].
More broadly, coarse-graining and scaling approaches—
developed notably by Kadanoff and Wilson—have clarified how macroscopic, fluid-like
behaviour emerges from microscopic interactions [5, 6]. Extensions to nonequilibrium
systems, particularly through the work of Onsager and Prigogine, further highlight the
interplay between microscopic reversibility and macroscopic irreversibility [7, 8].
Despite these advances, classical thermodynamics remains fundamentally a theory of
macroscopic systems. Its formulation relies on key assumptions, including extensivity
and weak coupling between a system and its environment, which ensure that bulk prop-
erties dominate over boundary effects and allow for a consistent definition of thermody-

the weak-coupling approximation [9, 11]. Hill’s nanothermodynamics [9] and its exten-

strong coupling. The complexity increases if magnetic fields are included: magnetically
induced interactions [14] change how macroscopic approximations emerge from micro-
scopic considerations. The situation is equally unclear when considering the fluid limit
of two interacting fluids [15, 16, 17].
Moreover, in nonequilibrium systems, thermodynamic behaviour is often only locally
valid: subsystems that admit an effective thermodynamic description may themselves be
small due to strong gradients or interactions [18]. This underscores the need for a frame-
work that systematically connects microscopic structure to mesoscopic and macroscopic
thermodynamic behaviour.

A Mathematical Formulation
In the classical Gibbs framework, coarse-graining is introduced as a partition of phase
space ΓN, leading to a piecewise constant approximation of the phase-space density and
an associated notion of entropy. This idea underlies early discussions of irreversibility
and the increase in entropy (see, e.g., [2, 19]).
Independently, spatial coarse-graining appears in the derivation of continuum theories,
where local densities and currents are defined by averaging microscopic observables over
finite spatial regions Λ. This procedure is fundamental in kinetic theory and hydrody-
namics, for instance, in the Irving–Kirkwood formulation of transport equations and in
the derivation of the Boltzmann equation from the BBGKY hierarchy (cf. [20, 21, 22]). In
such contexts, the assumption of local equilibrium implicitly combines spatial averaging
with a Gibbsian description at the level of each spatial cell.
More abstractly, projection operator techniques, such as the Zwanzig–Mori formalism [23,
24], provide a systematic framework for coarse-graining by projecting the microscopic
dynamics onto a reduced set of relevant variables. These approaches also encode, in an
implicit manner, both phase-space and spatial reduction of degrees of freedom. Despite
this extensive body of work, the combined action of phase-space and spatial coarse-
graining is typically not formulated as a single, explicit operator acting on the joint space
Λ × ΓN. Rather, the two procedures are introduced separately or are subsumed under
broader assumptions such as local equilibrium or scale separation.

5
Conclusion
This work introduces a combined coarse-graining of physical and phase space, yielding
a clear mesoscopic description in terms of cell occupation variables ϱi,α.
Within this
framework, extensivity is not assumed but emerges naturally: it holds precisely when
the mesoscopic variables factorise across spatial cells, and breaks down when correlations
persist.
This provides a unified way to understand extensivity as a form of statistical independence
at the coarse-grained level, while clarifying how and why it fails in systems with long-
range interactions. Although related concepts appear in the literature, the explicit link
between extensivity and factorisation under combined coarse-graining, derived from a
single cluster expansion argument, appears to be new.

The mesoscopic construction is consistent with the canonical ensemble in the fine-graining
limit (Theorem 2.1), making it a genuine extension of the Gibbs framework. The equiv-
alence between factorisation and extensivity (Theorems 3.1 and 3.5) is established by
logically distinct proofs: the forward direction is purely algebraic, while the converse uses
the cluster expansion to convert the extensivity hypothesis into a bound on the total inter-
cell interaction. The physical conditions under which factorisation holds are identified
in Lemma 3.4 via clustering of correlations and scale separation. Deviations from exten-
sivity are quantified by a generalised Euler relation, connecting entropy non-additivity,
inter-cell correlations, and partition-function structure in a single framework.
Overall, the framework establishes a direct bridge between microscopic dynamics, meso-
scopic structure, and macroscopic thermodynamics, with clear potential for applications
