Abstract
Circulant graphs are a widely studied family of graphs whose members
possess varying amounts of symmetry. Although considerable progress has
been made in finding the automorphism groups of circulant graphs under
certain restrictions, a complete classification is elusive. In general, the
structure of the automorphism group of a graph with twins can be sim-
plified by separating the effect of automorphisms that permute mutually
twin vertices and those that operate on the twin quotient graph. Further
simplification can be achieved in twin-free, vertex-transitive graphs that
have co-twins, which we define to be vertices whose neighborhoods are
complementary. In this paper, we demonstrate how the these simplifi-
cations can be used provide insight into the automorphism groups and
symmetry parameters of vertex-transitive graphs in general and circulant
graphs in particular.

Introduction
Let n ∈N and let A ⊆Zn \{0} that is inverse-closed in the sense that A = −A.
The circulant graph Cn(A) has vertex set Zn, with u, v ∈Zn being adjacent iff
u−v ∈A. The elements of A are called the generators of Cn(A). Each vertex in
Cn(A) has degree |A|; equivalently Cn(A) is a |A|-valent (regular) graph. With
their vertices drawn evenly spaced around a circle, circulant graphs certainly
have what we would intuitively call a symmetric appearance; see the three
examples in Figure 1. More technically, the symmetries of a graph G = (V, E)
are the bijections of V that preserve adjacency and non-adjacency. Symmetries
are also called graph automorphisms, and they form a group Aut(G) under
composition. The structure of Aut(G) provides different ways of measuring of
just how symmetric G is.
Any circulant graph Cn(A) is vertex-transitive, because for any u, v ∈Zn,
α(w) = w −u + v is a graph automorphism satisfying α(u) = v. However,
not all circulant graphs are edge-transitive. Note that τ−1(a) = −a is auto-
morphism of Cn(A), which implies that a circulant graph is edge-transitive iff

Another way to characterize the symmetry of a graph G is to quantify how
easy it is to block nontrivial automorphisms. For example, we could add the
requirement that graph automorphisms fix each vertex in a set D ⊂V ; if the
only automorphism that does so is the identity, then D is called a determining
(or fixing) set. The size of a smallest determining set, Det(G), is called the de-
termining number of G. As another example, we could assign any one of d colors
to each vertex of G and add the requirement that graph automorphisms must
preserve (setwise) the color classes. If the only automorphism to do so is the
identity, then the coloring is called d-distinguishing. The distinguishing number
of a graph, Dist(G), is the minimum number of colors required in a distinguish-
ing coloring. In [7], Cockburn and Loeb give the determining and distinguishing
number of all 3-valent and 4-valent circulant graphs by first determining their
automorphism groups.
However, finding the automorphism group of circulant graphs in general is
difficult. In 2007, Morris [22] provided an excellent survey of partial results,
including when Cn(A) is arc-transitive, or when n is prime, a prime power, or
square-free, and/or the elements of A are all divisors of n. More recent results
cover circulant graphs whose adjacency matrix has all integer eigenvalues or all
rational eigenvalues; see [2] and [18] respectively.
In this paper, we extend an approach used in [7] to investigate the automor-
phism group. Vertices are nonadajcent (respectively adjacent) twins if they have
the same open (respectively closed) neighborhood. In vertex-transitive graphs,
the presence of twin vertices completely decides the determining number and
reduces the task of computing the distinguishing number to computing that
of its twin quotient graph. In addition, we can separate the effect of automor-
phisms that permute mutually twin vertices from those that those permute twin
classes. In a few cases, iteratively looking at twin vertices in quotient graphs
completely determines the automorphism group. In all cases, we can reduce the
problem of finding automorphism groups of vertex-transitive graphs to finding
automorphism groups of those that are twin-free.
Vertex-transitive graphs that are twin-free can still have co-twin vertices, de-
fined in [7] to be vertices whose neighborhoods are complementary rather than
equal. In such graphs, distinct sets of co-twin vertices cannot be independently
permuted, and so their presence imposes strong constraints on the automor-
phism group. If a vertex-transitive, twin-free graph with nonadjacent co-twins
is also triangle-free, it must be a crown graph. If it has triangles, then its au-
tomorphism group, determining number and distinguishing number are related
to those of the subgraph induced by the open neighborhood of any vertex.

Circulant Graphs
Throughout this paper, n ∈N and A is an inverse-closed subset of Zn\{0}. The
corresponding circulant graph Cn(A) has vertex set Zn with u, v ∈Zn adjacent
iff u −v ∈A. It is immediate that the neighborhood of 0 is N(0) = A; more
generally, N(u) = u+A for all u ∈Zn. Two extreme cases are Cn(Zn\{0}) = Kn
and Cn(∅) = Nn; at the intersection of these two, there is the trivial circulant
graph C1(∅) = K1. For n = 2m, then Cn(A) = Kmm iff A = {1, 3, 5, . . . , n −1}.
The complement of Cn(A) is Cn(A), where A is the complement of A in
Zn \ {0}. As shown in [6], Cn(A) is connected iff gcd(n, A) = 1; more generally,
Cn(A) has gcd(n, A) components. From [12], a connected circulant graph Cn(A)
is bipartite iff n is even and every a ∈A is odd.
A graph automorphism is a permutation of the vertex set that respects adja-
cency and nonadjacency. For all w ∈Zn, αw : Zn →Zn given by αw(v) = w +v
is an automorphism of Cn(A). For fixed u, v ∈Zn, αu−v is the unique transla-
tion satisfying α(v) = u; thus Cn(A) is vertex-transitive. The only translation
that fixes any vertex is α0, the identity map. Thus Aut(Cn(A)) contains Zn as
a cyclic subgroup that acts regularly on the vertices.
For any unit t ∈U(n), it is easy to show that Cn(A) ∼= Cn(t · A). In [1],
Ádám conjectured that any isomorphism between two circulant graphs must be
of this form, but this was found to be false in general. Considerable work has
been done to find restrictions under which Ádám’s conjecture holds; see [23].
If u ∈U(n) satisfies u · A = A, then βu : Zn →Zn given by βu(v) = uv
is an automorphism of Cn(A). We denote the set of all such automorphisms
Stabn(A); it is easy to check that is it a subgroup of Aut(Cn(A)). For any
v ∈Zn, β−1α1(v) = −1 −v and α1β−1(v) = 1 −v, so Aut(Cn(A)) is nonabelian
for all 2 < n ∈N. As a special case of a result proved by Godsil in [10], if Zn is
a normal subgroup of Aut(Cn(A)), then Aut(Cn(A)) = Zn ⋊Stabn(A).

Twin Vertices
Definition. The open neighborhood of a vertex u in the graph G = (V, E) is
N(u) = {v ∈V : uv ∈E} and the closed neighborhood of u is N[u] = N(u)∪{u}.
Distinct vertices u, v are nonadjacent twins iff N(u) = N(v) and adjacent twins
iff N[u] = N[v].
Lemma 1. (a) A vertex cannot have both a nonadjacent and an adjacent twin.
(b) Two vertices in a graph G are nonadjacent twins in G iff they are adjacent
twins in the complement G.

Twin Quotient Graph
Define u ∼v iff N(u) = N(v) in a graph G. This is clearly an equivalence
relation on V (G); intuitively, the corresponding quotient graph eG is obtained
by collapsing the vertices in each equivalence class to a single vertex. More
precisely. the vertices of eG are the equivalence classes [u] = {v ∈V (G) | u ∼v},
with [u] and [z] adjacent in eG iff there exist v ∈[u], w ∈[z] such that v and w
are adjacent in G. By definition of ∼, all vertices in an equivalence class have

the same neighbors, so in this case, distinct vertices u and z are adjacent in G
iff [u] and [z] are adjacent in eG. We refer to eG as the nonadjacent twin quotient
graph and [u] as the nonadjacent twin class of u. It can be verified that eG has
no nonadjacent twins; see [4]. However it is possible that eG has adjacent twins;
for example, if G = K2,3 then eG = K2.
Similarly, we can define u∧v iff u and v are adjacent twin vertices in G, and
create a corresponding adjacent twin quotient graph, which we denote by bG. In
this case, distinct vertices u and z are adjacent in G iff either [u] = [z] or [u]
and [z] are adjacent in bG.
Lemma 4. Let G be a vertex-transitive graph. Then the adjacent twin quotient
graph of the complement G is the complement of the nonadjacent twin quotient
graph of G; equivalently, bG = eG.
Proof. If G has no nonadjacent twins, then by Lemma 1, G has no adjacent
twins, so bG = G = eG. If every vertex in G has nonadjacent twins, then by
Lemma 1, u and v are nonadjacent twins in G iff they are adjacent twins in
G. This implies that V ( eG) = V (bG). Hence we can use [u] to denote both the
equivalence of u in eG and the equivalence class of u in bG.
Next we must show that distinct [u] and [z] are adjacent in bG iff they are not
adjacent in eG. Equivalently, we can show that distinct [u] and [z] are adjacent
in eG iff they are not adjacent in bG. By definition, [u], [z] are adjacent in eG iff
u, z are adjacent in G iff u, z are not adjacent in G iff [u], [z] are not adjacent
in bG.
Since eG has no nonadjacent twins, by Lemma 1, eG = bG has no adjacent
twins. However, eG can have adjacent twins, so eG can have nonadjacent twins.

Corollary 7. Let G be a vertex-transitive graph with nonadjacent twins. Then
G is arc-transitive iff eG is arc-transitive.

Twins in Circulant Graphs
In this section, we provide an algebraic characterization of when Cn(A) has
nonadjacent twins in terms of the coset structure of the additive group Zn
modulo its proper cyclic subgroups. In particular, we use some of the algebra
of the action of groups on sets (see [9]).
The automorphism group of a graph acts on the vertex set of the graph,
so Aut(Cn(A)) acts on Zn. The dihedral group D2n ∼= Zn ⋊{±1} is always
a subgroup of Aut(Cn(A)), where the action of (w, t) ∈D2n ∼= Zn ⋊{±1} on
v ∈Zn is given by (w, t) · v = w + tv. Since A is inverse-closed, u −v ∈A iff
(w+tu)−(w+tv) ∈A, so (w, t) ∈Aut(Cn(A)). Geometrically, if the vertices of
Cn(A) are arranged in clockwise order in a circle, then (w, 1) is simply clockwise
rotation by w while (w, −1) is a flip across the diameter through vertex 0,
followed by clockwise rotation by w.
If group Γ acts on set X, then the stabilizer of Y ⊆X is the subgroup
Stab(Y ) = {γ ∈Γ | γ · Y = Y }. The next lemma characterizes the intersection
of the stabilizer of A in Aut(Cn(A)) with the dihedral subgroup of Aut(Cn(A)).
Lemma 8. In Aut(Cn(A)), Stab(A) ∩D2n = ⟨w⟩⋊{±1}, for some w ∈Zn.
Moreover, the nonadjacent twin class of 0 is ⟨w⟩.
Proof. Any subgroup of D2n is either cyclic or itself a dihedral group. Because
A is inverse-closed, (0, −1) ∈Stab(A) ∩D2n, so Stab(A) ∩D2n = ⟨w⟩⋊{±1},
for some w ∈Zn. By definition, 0 ̸= u ∈Zn is a nonadjacent twin of 0 iff
N(0) = N(u), or equivalently A = u + A. This holds iff (u, 1) ∈Stab(A) ∩D2n,
which in turn holds iff u ∈⟨w⟩.
An extreme case of Lemma 8 occurs if w = 0. Then Stab(A)∩D2n = {0, ±1}
and Cn(A) has no nonadjacent twins. Another extreme case occurs when w is
a unit in Zn. In this case, Stab(A) ∩D2n = D2n and every nonzero vertex is
a nonadjacent twin of 0, implying that Cn(A) is an empty graph (equivalently,
A = ∅).
Next, we want to detect when a nonempty circulant graph has nonadjacent
twins. We recall more terminology and facts concerning a group Γ acting on a
set X (see [9]). The orbit of an element x ∈X is Γx = {γ ·x | γ ∈Γ}; the orbits
under the action of Γ partition the set X. A subset W ⊆X is Γ-invariant iff
Γ·W = W; equivalently, the stabilizer of W is the whole group, or Stab(W) = Γ.
Any Γ-invariant subset must be a (disjoint) union of orbits.
Proposition 9. A nonempty circulant graph Cn(A) has nonadjacent twins iff
there exists 0 ̸= w ∈Zn \ U(n) such that A is a union of nontrivial cosets of
⟨w⟩. In this case, the nonadjacent twin classes are the cosets of ⟨w⟩where w is
the element of Zn of maximum order satisfying the preceding statement.
Proof. If Cn(A) is nonempty and has nonadjacent twin vertices, then by Lemma 8,
Stab(A) ∩D2n = ⟨w⟩⋊{±1} for some w ∈Zn that is neither 0 nor a unit.
To simplify notation, let T = Stab(A) ∩D2n; we consider the action of T on
V (Cn(A)) = Zn.

Moreover, the size of each twin class divides gcd(n, |A|).
