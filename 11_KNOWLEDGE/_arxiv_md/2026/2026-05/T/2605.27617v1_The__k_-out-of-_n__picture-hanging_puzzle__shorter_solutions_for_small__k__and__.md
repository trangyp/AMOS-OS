The k-out-of-n picture-hanging puzzle:
shorter solutions for small k and n −k
Tom Verhoeff∗
May 28, 2026
Abstract
The picture-hanging puzzle, popularized by Demaine et al. (2014), asks for a way to wrap a
wire around n nails such that the picture hangs as long as fewer than k nails are removed, but
falls as soon as any k are removed. Solutions correspond to words in the free group Fn. We give
explicit, deterministic, polynomial-length constructions for two regimes: 2-out-of-n with word
length at most 8
3nlog2 6 −4n2, and (n −2)-out-of-n with word length 6n log2(n/2), both for n
a power of two. These improve on Wästlund’s quasi-polynomial deterministic construction in
their respective regimes. We also report, via exhaustive computer search, the exact minimum
length of 16 for the 2-out-of-4 puzzle, attained by two structurally distinct solutions. As an
additional contribution, we observe that the natural workshop realization with carabiners on
a flat board introduces an over/under ambiguity at every wire crossing; a wrong choice can
produce a Whitehead link, which is topologically distinct from the intended commutator.

Introduction
The picture-hanging puzzle in its simplest form asks: can one wrap a wire around two nails such
that the picture falls if either nail is removed? The classical answer, popularized by Spivak [3] in
1997 and analyzed in detail by Demaine et al. [1], is yes: the commutator xyx−1y−1 in the free
group on two generators corresponds to such a wiring. In this paper we will write this commutator
in additive notation as 1 + 2 −1 −2 (the rationale is explained in Section 2). Demaine et al.
generalized this to arbitrary monotone Boolean functions, with particular attention to the k-
out-of-n family: the picture falls when at least k of the n nails are removed, and hangs otherwise.
Their construction for k-out-of-n uses AKS sorting networks and gives a word of polynomial length
O(n1 561 600) in the free group — polynomial, but with an astronomical exponent.
Wästlund [7] subsequently gave a simplified proof and a much better polynomial bound of
O(n7.004) via a probabilistic construction, together with an explicit deterministic divide-and-
conquer construction of quasi-polynomial length nO(log n). The explicit deterministic polynomial
regime remained unexplored for specific small values of k or n −k.
We address this gap. For fixed small k and fixed small n −k — two natural regimes where the
puzzle remains interesting — we give explicit deterministic constructions with low-degree poly-
nomial length, established by recurrences with closed-form solutions. Combined with exhaustive
computer search, we also pin down exact minimum lengths for the smallest open cases.
Origin
The present note arose from preparations for two public-outreach events: a workshop for elementary
and secondary school students during a preparation day (March 2026), organized by the Stichting
Vierkant voor Wiskunde for their summer mathematics camps, and a mini-lecture at the MathFest
in Utrecht (May 2026). For practical reasons we used a wooden board with carabiners rather than
nails: a wire cannot accidentally slip off a carabiner the way it can off a smooth nail, so participants
could handle a partially completed construction without the picture falling prematurely.

Notation and preliminaries
An additive algebra of variables
We work with formal expressions built from natural-number variables, written 1, 2, 3, . . . rather
than x1, x2, x3, . . .. The expressions use a binary operator + and a unary inverse −. Subject to
the rules below, this is the free group on the variables; we use the additive notation throughout
because we find it more accessible to a broader audience, including secondary-school students and
even upper-elementary pupils.
The expressions are governed by the following rules, which the reader should keep in mind,
especially the contrast with ordinary integer arithmetic:
• Associativity: (x + y) + z = x + (y + z).
• Non-commutativity: in general x + y ̸= y + x.
• Neutral element: we write 0 for the empty expression, and 0 + x = x + 0 = x.

• Inverse: x + (−x) = 0 = (−x) + x.
• Double negation: −(−x) = x.
• Inversion of a sum: −(x + y) = −y + (−x) = −y −x (note the order reversal).
We allow ourselves the shorthand x + x = 2x and similar, but the reader must keep in mind that,
due to non-commutativity, p(x + y) ̸= px + py in general.
The length of an expression is the number of variables-with-sign in its fully reduced form
(where reduction means repeatedly applying x + (−x) = 0 and absorbing the resulting 0).
A
nonzero expression has positive length; the only zero expression has length 0. The support of an
expression is the set of variables that occur in it; two expressions have disjoint supports when they
share no variable.
The picture-hanging puzzle
A solution w to the k-out-of-n picture-hanging puzzle (Demaine’s convention) is an expression in
variables 1, . . . , n such that:
(a) w is nonzero;
(b) setting any k of the variables 1, . . . , n to 0 reduces w to 0;
(c) for every set of fewer than k variables, setting those variables to 0 leaves w nonzero.
Condition (b) corresponds to the picture falling when any k nails are removed; condition (c)
corresponds to the picture still hanging when fewer than k are removed. We write hk,n for any
solution to the k-out-of-n puzzle, and Lk(n) for the length of a particular solution under discussion.
Specifications
It helps to name the object a puzzle asks us to realize. A specification on a set of nails V is a
monotone Boolean function
f : 2V →{hang, fall},
f(V ) = fall,

order hang < fall and read ∨and ∧as max and min, so that fall ∨x = fall and hang ∧x = hang;
monotone then means S ⊆S′ ⇒f(S) ≤f(S′), that is, removing more nails never restores the
picture. We say f falls at S when f(S) = fall, and hangs at S otherwise. An expression h on V
solves f if
h|S = 0 ⇐⇒f(S) = fall
for every S ⊆V,
where h|S denotes h with the nails in S removed (set to 0).
The condition f(V ) = fall is forced, not chosen: removing every nail reduces any expression
on V to 0, so no expression can solve a function that hangs at V . By monotonicity it excludes
a single function, the constant hang — which never falls, even when all nails are removed — the
lone monotone specification that no wire can realize. The opposite constant, fall, is solved by the
empty expression 0 (nothing is hung, so the picture has already fallen). Every other monotone
function with f(V ) = fall is realizable too: Demaine et al. [1] and Wästlund [7] each construct
an expression that solves it. (Wästlund excludes both constants as trivial; we keep fall, since the
empty expression 0 solves it.) Our concern is not realizability but the search for shorter solutions.

an expression already equal to 0 remains 0 when more variables are set to 0. Equivalently, if the
picture hangs with a set S′ of nails removed, it also hangs with any subset S ⊆S′ removed.
Monotonicity lets a correctness proof focus on the essential removals — those whose required
outcome does not already follow from another removal. A required fall is essential only when no
smaller removal already falls, and a required hang only when no larger removal already hangs. For
k-of-V the essential removals are therefore exactly the sets of k nails (which must fall) and the
sets of k −1 nails (which must hang): removing fewer than k −1 hangs by monotonicity from the
(k −1) case, and removing more than k falls by monotonicity from the k case. It thus suffices to
check that every removal of k nails makes the picture fall and every removal of k −1 nails leaves
it hanging.
For example, the expression 1 + 2 −1 −2 has length 4. It is nonzero, since no reduction rule
applies: no two adjacent terms are mutual inverses. Setting 1 to 0 reduces it to 2 −2 = 0; setting
2 to 0 reduces it to 1 −1 = 0. Hence it solves the 1-out-of-2 puzzle: it hangs as it stands, yet
removing either nail (variable) causes the picture (expression) to fall (vanish).
In group-theoretic terms, 1 + 2 −1 −2 is the commutator [x1, x2] in the free group on two
generators. We define the commutator of two expressions a and b as
[a, b] = (a + b) −(b + a) = a + b −a −b,
where the second equality uses the rule −(b + a) = −a −b.
The commutator thus measures
non-commutativity: it is the difference between the two possible orders of summing a and b.
Three key properties of the commutator follow immediately from this definition:
• Antisymmetry: −[a, b] = [b, a], because −(a + b −a −b) = b + a −b −a.
• Vanishing condition: [a, b] = 0 iff a + b = b + a, that is, iff a and b commute under addition.
In the free group, this happens exactly when a and b are both multiples of a common element
— in group theory, a fact about centralizers.
• Special cases that vanish: [0, b] = [a, 0] = [a, a] = [a, −a] = 0, and more generally [pa, qa] = 0
for any integers p, q.
We will use commutators recursively: the arguments a and b may themselves be expressions
built from variables, sums, and commutators. The vanishing condition then becomes a useful test,
which we phrase as follows. Say that two expressions solve the same problem when they vanish
under exactly the same removals. The inverse −h solves the same problem as h; so does any nonzero
multiple p h (that is, h repeated p times, p ̸= 0), since a nonzero multiple of a nonzero expression is
again nonzero (in group theory, the free group is torsion-free); and so does any conjugate g + h −g
(a copy of h wrapped in g and −g), since it too vanishes exactly when h does. Hence two multiples
of a common element solve the same problem. Reading the vanishing conditions through this lens,
for nonzero a and b: a + b = 0 forces b = −a, and [a, b] = 0 forces a and b to be common multiples
— in either case a and b solve the same problem. Contrapositively, expressions that solve different
problems satisfy a+b ̸= 0 and [a, b] ̸= 0; in particular this holds when they have disjoint, nonempty
supports. This gives only non-vanishing; that + and [, ] actually realize ∧and ∨needs more, as
we discuss in Section 3.

Three easy cases
Three cases of k-out-of-n admit short, optimal, and explicit solutions.
Proposition 1 (k = n). The minimum length of a solution to the n-out-of-n puzzle is exactly n,
attained by 1 + 2 + · · · + n.
Proof. This expression is 0 iff all variables are set to 0, and remains nonzero under any proper
subset of removals (some surviving variable is left, and the reduced expression contains it). Any
solution must mention every variable, so length ≥n.
Proposition 2 (k = n −1). The minimum length of a solution to the (n −1)-out-of-n puzzle is
exactly 2n, attained by
1 + 2 + · · · + n −1 −2 −· · · −n.
Proof. Let w = 1 + 2 + · · · + n −1 −2 −· · · −n.
Nonzero: w is freely reduced (no two adjacent terms are mutual inverses), so w ̸= 0.
Fall under n −1 removals: When n −1 variables are removed, exactly one variable j survives,
and the expression reduces to j −j = 0.
Hang under fewer removals: When exactly n−2 variables are removed, the two survivors a < b
give a + b −a −b = [a, b] ̸= 0 (since a ̸= b, they do not commute). By monotonicity, the picture
also hangs under any fewer removals.
Lower bound 2n: In any solution, fixing variable j and setting all other n −1 variables to 0
removes n−1 ≥k = n−1 variables, so the resulting expression must be 0. Hence the net exponent
of j — its number of +j occurrences minus its number of −j — is 0 in any solution, forcing j to
appear at least twice. Since this holds for every j, length ≥2n.
Proposition 3 (k = 1). The minimum length of a solution to the 1-out-of-n puzzle is Θ(n2). For
n a power of two, length n2 is attained by the balanced commutator tree, recursively defined by
h1,1 = 1,
h1,2n = [h1,n on {1, . . . , n}, h1,n on {n + 1, . . . , 2n}].
The lower bound Ω(n2) is due to Gartside and Greenwood [2]; the upper-bound construction goes
back at least to [4].
Sketch. The recursion L(2n) = 4L(n) with L(1) = 1 gives L(2i) = 4i = (2i)2.
Correctness
follows by induction: a commutator [A, B] with A, B on disjoint variable sets vanishes iff A = 0
or B = 0, and inductively each h1,n vanishes iff some variable in its support is removed. Hence
[h1,n-on-L, h1,n-on-R] vanishes iff some variable on either side is removed — i.e., iff any of the 2n
variables is removed.
The construction of Proposition 3 is, in fact, the simplest case of Demaine-perspective binary
splitting (Section 3): for k = 1, the disjunction at each split has just two terms (“some variable
removed in L” or “some variable removed in R”), giving a 2-leaf commutator that is the balanced
tree.
The next case in the co-rank family, (n −2)-out-of-n, is genuinely non-trivial; it is the subject
of Section 4.

Huffman placement.
A commutator tree over the disjuncts may take many shapes, all realizing
the same disjunction but with different total lengths. Since each commutator [A, B] = A+B−A−B
duplicates both of its arguments, a leaf placed at depth d contributes 2d times its own length to
the total. Minimizing the total length is therefore a Huffman problem on the leaf lengths |Dj|:
one repeatedly combines the two shortest current subexpressions into a commutator, which places
the longest leaves nearest the root. We use Huffman placement at every split.
Correctness and length.
Two points remain. First, that the construction actually solves the
k-out-of-n puzzle. Each conjunction here — a cross term Aj + Bk−j on the disjoint halves L, R
— is sound on its own by Lemma 4. The commutator tree is not: its leaves, the disjuncts, share
supports — for instance D0 and D1 both constrain R — so Lemma 4 does not apply to it, and
correctness of the overall disjunction needs the inductive argument of Section 3.3. Second, the
total length is governed by a recurrence determined by the Huffman placement of the leaves. We
solve this recurrence in closed form for the case k = 2 with balanced splits in the remainder of this
section; the general case is left open (Section 8).

The case k = 2

two shorter at depth 2 (a double doubling).

Proof. Each combiner has an automatic direction: if g1 ∧g2 falls at S then h1|S = h2|S = 0 and

The construction nests many commutators, not one. Lemma 5 is a sufficient test on specifi-
cations; for a whole tree the next theorem makes the criterion exact, and milder: a commutator
may harmlessly collapse at a removal where the target already falls, so only the removals where
the target hangs need care.
Theorem 6 (Commutator trees as disjunctions). Let T be a commutator tree whose leaves solve
specifications g1, . . . , gm, and let f = g1 ∨· · · ∨gm. Then T solves f if and only if no internal node
of T vanishes at an S where f hangs. In particular, T solves f if at every such S the specifications
solved by the two subtrees at each internal node separate above it.
Proof. The minterms of f are exactly those of the gi, each realized by a leaf. If f falls at S, some
leaf vanishes; a commutator with a vanishing argument vanishes, and this propagates to the root,
so T|S = 0 whatever the internal nodes do. Hence T solves f precisely when, in addition, T|S ̸= 0
wherever f hangs. A vanishing internal node propagates to the root, and a nonvanishing root is
itself the topmost internal node, so T|S ̸= 0 at an f-hang S iff no internal node vanishes there;
this is the equivalence. For the sufficient condition: at an f-hang S no leaf is covered, so every leaf
hangs and, inductively, the two subtrees at each node are nonzero; if their specifications separate
above S they cannot commute (Lemma 5), so the node is nonzero.
There is no counterpart of Theorem 6 for sums. A commutator tree falls as soon as any one
leaf is covered, so a collapse at a removal where the tree should fall does no harm; a sum falls

An exponential construction by one-step extension
The polynomial construction of Section 3 is asymptotically efficient, but at small n its constants
are loose: for n = 4 it gives length 32.
We now present an alternative construction that is
asymptotically exponential but produces shorter solutions for small n. It also illustrates a different
structural principle — overlapping variable supports with controlled cancellation — and was the
path by which the author first reached lengths below 24 for 2-out-of-4, motivating the exhaustive
search of Section 6. This approach was discovered after the Vierkant workshop while preparing for
the MathFest.

Fall, |S| = k. If nail n survives then ℓ= k, so A|S = B|S = 0 and hk,n|S = n −n = 0. If nail n

additive cost), so the length is Θ(2n) at worst, exponential in n.

Exhaustive search and exact minima
Parity
In any solution to the k-out-of-n puzzle with k < n, every variable has net exponent zero. Setting
all variables except j to 0 removes n −1 ≥k of them, so the resulting expression must vanish;
hence the net exponent of j in any solution is zero. Since each variable appears with net exponent
zero, it appears an even number of times, so the total length is even. Odd lengths are eliminated
without search.
Symmetry quotienting
The search canonicalizes each candidate word by four symmetry classes:
(S1) Nail relabeling (Sn): nails are renamed so that their labels appear in order of first occurrence
(1, 2, 3, . . .).
(S2) Per-nail sign flip ((Z2)n): each nail’s first occurrence is made positive.
(S3) Reversal: reading w backward gives an equivalent solution; together with the sign flips of (S2)
this is the wire inversion w 7→−w.
(S4) Cyclic rotation: any cyclic rotation of w gives an equivalent solution.
A word is canonical if it is the lexicographically smallest (S1 + S2)-normalized form among all its
cyclic rotations and the cyclic rotations of its reversal. Each equivalence class contains exactly one
canonical representative, substantially reducing the search space.

The following results are established by exhaustive search and verified by the unit test suite [6].

(confirming Proposition 2).

2 + 1 −2 −1 −3.
• 1-out-of-4: minimum length exactly 16; the unique canonical solution is [[1, 2], [3, 4]] (con-
firming Proposition 3 at n = 4).

With nails and gravity, the wire’s vertical hierarchy at each crossing is fixed by physics: a later
strand naturally falls in front of (or behind, depending on how the wire is paid out) an earlier one.
With carabiners on a flat board, the wire’s over/under choice at each crossing is a free parameter,
and incorrect choices produce topologically different links.
The simplest failure: a Whitehead link
Consider the standard 1-out-of-2 solution 1 + 2 −1 −2. If the second factor −2 is wired with
the wrong over/under choice relative to the preceding strands, the resulting wiring no longer
represents the commutator in F2 but instead realizes a Whitehead link with one of the carabiners
(see Figure 5). Removing the other nail then leaves the wire genuinely linked around the remaining
carabiner rather than free to fall.
Figure 5: Left: correct hanging 1 + 2 −1 −2, in which the strand for −2 passes consistently above
the previous strands. Middle: incorrect over/under choice for −2. Right: removing nail 1 produces
a Whitehead link, which does not fall.
This failure mode was observed during the workshop. Beyond its pedagogical value — partici-
pants quickly internalize that the algebra dictates the topology only if the geometric realization is
consistent — it is also a reminder that the standard picture-hanging analysis implicitly assumes a
particular planar projection or a gravitational hierarchy. In the absence of such a constraint, the
same word w ∈Fn can correspond to multiple non-equivalent wirings.

Open questions
Several questions are left open.
First, what is the exact length of the general Demaine-perspective binary-splitting construction
(Section 3) for fixed k ≥3?
(The construction is correct for every k by Theorem 8; only its
length is at issue.) For each fixed k, Huffman placement yields a divide-and-conquer recurrence
Lk(n) = ck Lk(n/2) + (lower-order terms). The recursive coefficient ck is governed not by the
number of disjuncts (k + 1) but only by the two disjuncts that are themselves k-out-of-(n/2)
subproblems, namely the j = 0 and j = k terms: Huffman placement keeps these two nearest the
root while the remaining, lower-order disjuncts are pushed deeper. Computation indicates that
ck = 6 for every k ≥2 (a balanced tree would instead give the larger coefficient ≈2(k + 1)), so
the polynomial exponent saturates at log2 6 rather than growing with k. The lower-order terms,
however, appear to carry polylogarithmic factors in n whose power grows with k; pinning down
this exact asymptotic is left open.
Second, what are the exact minima for the smallest open cases beyond 2-out-of-4? The exhaus-
tive search techniques of Section 6 reach lengths up to about 16 in pure Python; the next case,
3-out-of-5 (equivalently, (n −2)-out-of-n at n = 5, the first non-trivial case in the co-rank family
for odd n), should be within reach with a more efficient implementation.

Fourth, the relationship between the polynomial construction (Theorem 10, whose bound is
L2(4) = 32) and the exhaustive-search optimum (16 for 2-out-of-4, Section 6) is asymptotically
obscure. The optimum at n = 4 lies well below the construction, even after cancellations; whether
this gap persists, shrinks, or grows with n is unknown. Computational evidence at n = 5, 6, 7, 8
would clarify this, but pushing exhaustive search to those sizes is currently infeasible.
Fifth, the workshop-motivated question (Section 7): can the over/under choice at each crossing
be incorporated into the algebraic formalism, perhaps via braid groups, in a way that distinguishes
the intended commutator from the Whitehead link? This is more an exposition than a research
question, but a clean treatment would benefit pedagogy.
Sixth, is there a “puncturing” or “shortening” operation — by analogy with coding theory —
that lowers n at fixed k? Removing a nail is the easy move in the wrong direction: setting nail
n + 1 to 0 in a k-out-of-(n + 1) solution yields a (k −1)-out-of-n solution, lowering the threshold
along with the nail count. What is missing is an operation that removes a nail while preserving
the threshold k, turning a k-out-of-(n + 1) solution into a k-out-of-n one. Naively deleting a nail’s
letters does not preserve the puzzle property; a non-trivial reduction of this kind would supply a
recursion that decreases n by one at fixed k, complementing the halving recursion of Section 3 and
possibly narrowing the construction-versus-search gap above.
