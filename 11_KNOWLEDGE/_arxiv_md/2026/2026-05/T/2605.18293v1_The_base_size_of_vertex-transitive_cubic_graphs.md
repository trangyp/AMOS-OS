graph, then either |V Γ| ≤90, or Γ is a split Praeger–Xu graph, or there exist
two vertices α and β such that the identity is the only automorphism of Γ fixing

1. Introduction
1.1. Main result. For a permutation group on a finite domain Ω, a base is a
subset of Ωwhose pointwise stabiliser is trivial, and the base size is the minimum
cardinality among the bases. For instance, the base sizes of the symmetric group
and of the alternating group of degree n are n −1 and n −2 respectively, while the
base size of a general linear group coincides with the dimension of the underlying
vector space. Bounding the base size is a fundamental problem in permutation
group theory. On a theoretical level, an element of a permutation group is uniquely
determined by its action on a base, and on a computational level, this can be
leveraged to store and perform algorithms efficiently [Ser03].

It is natural and interesting to study the base size when the permutation group
arises as the automorphism group of some object, and this is the approach we
pursue in this paper. In particular, we will focus on finite graphs. Just as transitive
permutation groups form the natural setting for studying base sizes, connected
vertex-transitive graphs provide the corresponding setting for graphs. The first
genuinely nontrivial case is that of cubic graphs, that is, regular graphs of valency
3.

that can be considered low-level noise, and a certain natural infinite class of graphs,
the base size of the automorphism group of a connected vertex-transitive cubic

more satisfactory explanation of this phenomenon.

The distinguishing number of a permutation group is the minimum number of
colours needed to colour the domain so that only the identity preserves the colouring.
The distinguishing number of a connected vertex-transitive cubic graph is at most
2 unless Γ is either K4, K3,3, the cube graph or the Petersen graph [HIK+19,
Theorem 1.1]. The distinguishing cost is the minimum size of a colour class in a
symmetry-breaking colouring. We will prove that, among the 2-distinguishable
cubic vertex-transitive graphs, the only ones with large distinguishing cost are the
split Praeger–Xu graphs. This answers [ILTW22, Question 9.12].
Corollary 1.4. Let Γ be a finite connected vertex-transitive cubic graph with
distinguishing number 2. Then the distinguishing cost is at most 3 unless Γ is a
split Praeger–Xu graph.
The proof of Corollary 1.4 is slightly more involved, and we delay it to Section 2.7.
It is natural to ask whether a result in the spirit of Theorem 1.1 should hold for
graphs of higher valency. A major obstacle in this direction is the absence of a clear
analogue of the exceptional family appearing in the cubic case: in fact, it remains
a longstanding open problem in the theory of permutation groups acting on graphs
to identify a natural generalization of the Praeger–Xu graphs and their splits for
valency 5 and beyond. Nevertheless, we believe that a similar phenomenon should
hold at least for 4-valent vertex- and edge-transitive graphs, where we expect the
exceptional family to be given by the (nonsplit) Praeger–Xu graphs. In the course
of our proof we identify two specific points where our argument fails to extend to
this setting (see Remarks 3.2 and 8.6).
It would be highly interesting to have a conceptual reason for why large cubic
graphs have base size either at most 2 or unbounded. The structure and depth of
the proof of Theorem 1.1, which depends on the Classification of the Finite Simple
Groups, reflect this lack of a simple explanation.
1.3. Structure of the proof. The proof of Theorem 1.1 combines tools from
graph theory and permutation group theory, together with structural results on
p-groups and almost simple groups. For the convenience of the reader, we provide
a brief roadmap of the argument.

Let Γ be a connected vertex-transitive cubic graph, and let G ⩽Aut(Γ) act
transitively on V Γ with base size greater than 2. If G is transitive on the arcs

Conder, Lorimer, and Morton [CL89,Mor91], only finitely many graphs satisfy
this condition, so we can conclude by an explicit computation (Proposition 3.1).

a vertex Gα is a 2-group. We proceed by induction on |V Γ|, using the normal
quotient technique for graphs introduced in [Pra93]. A crucial ingredient is a
theorem of Djokovi´c [Djo80] on locally-D4 group amalgams. In particular, we
observe that Gα has nilpotency class at most 2 and exponent at most 4.

arc.

socle is the subgroup generated by the minimal normal subgroups. Two minimal

2.4. Split and merge. The operations of splitting and merging were introduced
in [PSV13, Constructions 7 and 11] to create a framework for translating results
from 3-valent graphs with a perfect matching invariant under the action of their
automorphism groups into results for 4-valent graphs with a 2-factor invariant
under the action of their automorphism groups, and vice versa. The fact that these
operators are the inverse of one another has been proved in [PSV13, Theorem 12]
and [BGS25, Theorem 2.9], up to a minor caveat.
This correspondence has a wide range of applications. It has been used in
[PSV13] to construct censuses of graphs, in [PS21] to study fixed-point ratios,
in [BGS25] to investigate the asymptotic behaviour of the order of semiregular
elements, and in [BZ25] to construct cubic graphs of arbitrary even girth. We refer
the reader to [BGS25, Section 2.4] and [PSV13, Section 4] for further details on
these operations.

2.5. Normal quotient method. The notion of normal quotient of a graph,
introduced in [Pra93, Section 4], is the group-theoretic special case of the usual
quotient construction in graph theory. We record a version for digraphs.
Definition 2.4. Let ⃗Γ be a connected digraph and let N ⩽Aut(⃗Γ). The normal
quotient digraph ⃗Γ/N is the digraph whose vertices are the N-orbits on V Γ, with

(G/N)αN = GαN/N.

In the proof of Theorem 1.1, we are left with G ⩽Aut(Γ) being a monolithic

Proposition 8.1. Let G be a monolithic group with nonabelian socle M, and let
