Abstract
Compositional score-based approaches to simulation-based inference (SBI) ap-
proximate the posterior over a shared parameter given n independent observations
by aggregating individually learned posterior scores: currently, there are two main
propositions of such methods (Geffner et al. [13], Linhart et al. [16]). As the result-
ing composite score does not correspond to the score of any distribution along the
forward diffusion path of the true multi-observation posterior, sampling from it via
a reverse SDE leads to an irreducible bias. Annealed Langevin dynamics provides
a principled alternative: it treats the composite score as the genuine score of a se-
quence of tractable bridging densities and samples from them in succession. When
properly tuned, it could lead to a controllable bias. However, its hyperparameters,
namely step sizes, the number of steps per level, and the number of annealing levels,
have so far been chosen empirically. We derive Wasserstein bounds for annealed
Langevin with approximate scores and translate them into explicit decision rules
for these hyperparameters that guarantee a prescribed sampling accuracy, while
highlighting different theoretical aspects of each composite score formulation. In
the Gaussian setting, we obtain closed-form expressions for all relevant quantities
and prove that the bridging densities of Linhart et al. [16] consistently admit larger
step sizes and require fewer total Langevin steps than those of Geffner et al. [13].
Furthermore, we show empirically that the tuning obtained in the Gaussian setting
generalizes to more complex problems, thus providing a well-understood and theo-
retically grounded starting point for practitioners using compositional score-based
approaches.

Introduction
In simulation-based inference (SBI), the goal is to approximate the posterior distribution p(θ | x) of
a stochastic model whose likelihood is intractable but which can be simulated [6, 8]. A recurrent
challenge arises when n independent observations x1, . . . , xn share a common unknown parameter

θ⋆: as n grows, the multi-observation posterior p(θ | x1:n) concentrates, and one wants inference to
sharpen accordingly. Pooling strategies train a single network on all n observations jointly [19, 20],
but their simulation budget scales with n and the network must be retrained for each new choice of n.
Compositional strategies [13, 16] instead combine individual posterior estimators trained once on
single-observation data, amortizing over n at no additional simulation cost: a key practical advantage
that motivates our focus on this paradigm.
Score-based diffusion models [24, 26, 22] provide a natural starting point: for a single observation,
one learns the individual posterior score ∇θ log pt(θ | xi) along a forward diffusion path and uses
it in a reverse SDE to draw samples from p(θ | xi). In the multi-observation setting, the tempting
shortcut is to aggregate these individual scores into a composite score and run the same reverse SDE
to target p(θ | x1:n). This shortcut is, however, theoretically unjustified. The reverse SDE must
be driven by the exact score of the joint forward diffusion of p(θ | x1:n), i.e. ∇θ log pt(θ | x1:n),
which satisfies the Fokker–Planck equation of that specific process. A composite of individual scores
does not satisfy this equation: it is the score of a different distribution πt ̸= pt(θ | x1:n) that does
not lie on the forward diffusion path of the target. Using such a score in a reverse SDE therefore
produces samples from the wrong distribution, and no amount of additional reverse steps can correct
this structural mismatch [9]. This also explains why some works [26, 14] attempt to correct this bias
by incorporating additional Langevin refinement steps into the standard reverse diffusion process.
However, this comes at the cost of sacrificing the efficiency of diffusion as a fast sampling method
while keeping the challenge of tuning the Langevin hyperparameters.

with the true forward diffusion, deliberately define their own tractable sequence of bridging densities
[13, 1, 16, 14]. In this paper, we focus on the following two main composite score formulations

remained an open problem; both Geffner et al. [13] and Linhart et al. [16] resort to empirical tuning.

hyperparameters, most relying on empirical validation, but leaving theoretical guarantee on the
sampling quality unaddressed. Our paper closes that gap.

Guidelines for fine-tuning annealed Langevin hyperparameters

Numerical applications
We consider the following four inference tasks, where d denotes the
dimension of the parameter space:
GMM prior (d = 2): The prior is a mixture of two Gaussians with tunable means, and the likelihood
is Gaussian. The resulting posterior is a mixture of Gaussians, whose modes become more separated
as the prior means move far apart. Note that as n grows, the posterior becomes approximately
Gaussian and unimodal. This task allows us to test the robustness of our decision rule for multimodal
target posteriors, where non-Gaussianity keeps mastered (see Figure 7 in Appendix B.3).
GMM likelihood (d = 10): The prior is a standard Gaussian, while the likelihood is a mixture of
two Gaussians, yielding a Gaussian mixture posterior. This task is inspired by Geffner et al. [13] and
deviates slightly more from Gaussianity than the first one.
SIR (d = 2): This epidemiological model tracks the evolution of susceptible (S), infected (I) and
recovered (R) populations. The prior is Log-Normal, the simulator is based on a set of differential
equations that outputs samples from a Binomial distribution. The resulting posterior is unimodal.
Lotka-Volterra (d = 4): This predator-prey model describes the evolution of the populations of two
interacting species. The prior is Log-Normal, the simulator is based on a set of differential equations
that outputs samples from a Log-Normal distribution. The resulting posterior is unimodal.
For the first two tasks, analytical individual posterior scores can be derived (see Appendix A.12). The
last two tasks, inspired from the SBI benchmark [17], allow us to test the robustness of our decision
rule when the individual posterior scores need to be learned and compositional error estimated
for different parameter dimensions d. The hyperparameters chosen according to our decision rule
allow us to run annealed Langevin without intermediate clipping steps for all tasks. Figure 8 (in
Appendix B.4) further shows that they yield a similar final Wasserstein error, chosen to be small,
for both choices of intermediate densities. Overall, Linhart’s densities use larger step sizes and
require fewer steps than Geffner’s counterparts across most inference tasks (see Figure 3): Linhart’s
composite score thus yields a more efficient sampler in terms of score function evaluations.
6
Conclusion
We propose the first theoretically grounded guidelines to fine-tune the hyperparameters of annealed
Langevin algorithm in compositional SBI, supported by Wasserstein error bounds under smoothness
and log-concavity assumptions. These guidelines remain valid under weaker smoothness assumptions
on individual posteriors, particularly for large n, thereby extending their applicability to broader
settings. We highlight the central role of intermediate densities that underlie the compositional scores
9


## Page 10


used in annealed Langevin, in the hyperparameter fine-tuning. We provide explicit hyperparameter
prescriptions in the Gaussian case and show that they can be effectively transferred to non-Gaussian
settings, thus avoiding the need for manual tuning while achieving a prescribed low Wasserstein error.
In addition, we show that Linhart’s compositional score yields a more efficient sampler than Geffner’s
one in both settings in terms of score function evaluations.
Our analysis could serve as a theoretically grounded starting point for practitioners using compo-
sitional score-based approaches for designing novel intermediate densities that improve annealed
Langevin sampling performance. Although our theoretical guarantees provide useful guidance for hy-
perparameter tuning, they still rely on smoothness and (approximate) log-concavity assumptions that
may not hold in highly multimodal regimes; extending these guarantees to more general distributions,
e.g. those satisfying a log-Sobolev inequality only, is a key direction for future work.

B.4
Technical details
Prior scores can be explicitly derived for all tasks. For GMM prior and GMM likelihood tasks,
individual analytical scores can also be derived (see Appendix A.12). For SIR and Lotka-Volterra,
we train an amortized neural score network sϕ(θ, x, t) using the sbi package (training loop, network
architecture and default training parameters) [8]. The network is a MLP composed of 3 hidden layers
of 256 hidden features and normalization layers. A Gaussian Fourier embedding is used for time.
For these two tasks, we train the network on log θ’s for more stability, then apply the reverse transform
during sampling. An additional embedding network (MLP with 2 hidden layers of 64 hidden features)
is used to encode observations x1, . . . , xn for the Lotka-Volterra task. We use a training set of 25000
pairs (θ, x) for each task with a validation split 90%−10%. We train the score network for maximum
1000 epochs with early stopping (implemented by default in the sbi package).
We sample from the true target posteriors using MCMC with numpyro [18] and jax [4], except for
GMM prior where analytical formula can be derived. We compute the Wasserstein error using the
POT Python package [11] using 3000 samples obtained from the annealed Langevin algorithm and the
true distribution. For Linhart’s composite score we use their GAUSS algorithm to estimate covariance
matrices using a small number (≈100) of diffusion steps.
We use γ = 0.5 as threshold for the Wasserstein error for all tasks and ω = 0.5 during hyperparameter
selection, except when the parameter dimension d = 10: in this case, we instead use ω = 0.8 to
reduce the number of steps while reaching a similar Wasserstein error. With respect to our training,
we use a maximal value of the compositional error ϵDSM = 0.1 for SIR and Lotka Volterra.
To run the experiments, we use a machine with an Intel CPU and 8 cores. We run the hyperparameter
tuning followed by annealed Langevin sampling for different values of n at once: in practice we
choose n IID observations x1, . . . , xn, then iteratively sample from the posteriors p(θ | x1:j) for
j ∈[2, . . . , n]. Finally, we consider T = 10 annealing levels (as in Song and Ermon [24]) uniformly
spaced on the interval [0, 1]: for computational stability, we set t0 = 1e −5 instead of 0 for sampling
and fine-tuning the hyperparameters.
Figure 8 shows that hyperparameters chosen according to our decision rule indeed allow one to
control the Wasserstein error of annealed Langevin algorithm for the different inference tasks.
