THE GEOMETRY OF WREATH AND SEMI-DIRECT PRODUCTS
CLAUDIO ALEXANDRE PIEDADE AND PHILIPPE TRANCHIDA
Abstract. Coset geometries are incidence geometries constructed from a group G and a
system of subgroups (Gi)i∈I of subgroups of G. For any algebraic group operation, it is then
natural to wonder whether it can be extended to the framework of coset geometries. This has
been achieved in the case of the halving ([1]) and in the case of free (amalgamated) products,
HNN-extensions, and semi-direct products ([2]).
In this article, we explore more deeply
two operations related to semi-direct products: the twisting and the wreath product. We
show that these operations extend to coset geometries in such a way that they preserve key
properties, such as flag-transitivity, residual-connectedness and being thin.
In particular,
we can apply twistings and wreath products to polytopes and hypertopes.
Doing so, we
show that there exists regular polytopes and hypertopes for almost-simple group with socle
a sporadic simple group.
1. Introduction
Coset incidence systems are incidence systems (see Section 2 for precise definitions) that
are constructed from a group G together with a system (Gi)i∈I of subgroups of G. These
structures, introduced by Jacques Tits [3, 4], are the basis for a variety of combinatorial
and geometric objects, such as abstract polytopes, vector and projective spaces, and Tits’
buildings [5, 6, 7]. In particular, the theory of buildings has had profound consequences across
mathematics, illustrating how interesting combinatorial structures can emerge from algebraic
objects [5, 8]. Regarding abstract polytopes, coset incidence systems, and in particular the
Tits’ algorithm [3], are the key to proving the one-to-one correspondence between abstract
regular polytopes and the class of groups known as string C-groups.
For any operation that can be performed on groups, it is natural to try to extend it in a
coherent way to systems (Gi)i∈I of subgroups of G so that the operation can then be applied
in the context of coset incidence systems. Whenever that is possible, it is also interesting
to try to give a geometrical interpretation to these algebraic constructions. Our goal is to
develop this concept, where algebraic operations are the inspiration for defining new geometric
operations.
This approach has already been implemented successfully in [1], where the group operation
under consideration is the halving (see [7, Chapter 7]). Indeed, the halving is classically defined
only for rank 3 polytopes and only as a group operation. In [1], we generalized this algebraic
operation in a natural way to a much larger class of objects, including the non-degenerate
leaf-hypertopes. Additionally, this operation was given a concrete geometrical construction,
based on previously described operations studied in [9].

this philosophy for a broader scope of operations on groups. There, we covered the cases
of free products, free-amalgamated products, HNN-extensions, semi-direct products and a

2. Background and Preliminary results
In this section, we regroup all the necessary definitions and well-established results that will
be used later on. We start by generalities on incidence geometries and coset geometries and
then give some context on (string) C-groups and permutation representation graphs. Notice
that all index sets I will be supposed to be finite, unless they are explicitly said to be infinite.
2.1. Incidence geometry and Coset Geometry. A 4-tuple Γ = (X, I, ∗, t) is called an
incidence system if
(a) X is a set whose elements are called the elements of Γ,
(b) I is the set of types of Γ,
(c) ∗is a symmetric and reflexive relation (called the incidence relation) on X, and
(d) t is a map from X to I, called the type map of Γ, such that distinct elements x, y ∈X
with x ∗y satisfy t(x) ̸= t(y).

3
The rank of Γ is the cardinality of the type set I. In an incidence system Γ, a flag is a
set of pairwise incident elements. The rank of a flag, rank(F), is the cardinality of F, that is
rank(F) = |F|. The type of a flag F, t(F), is the set of types of the elements of F. A chamber
is a flag of type I. An incidence system Γ is an incidence geometry if all its maximal flags are
chambers.
The incidence graph of Γ is a graph with vertex set X and where two elements x and y
are connected by an edge if and only if x ∗y. We say Γ is finite if the incidence graph of Γ is
finite.
Let F be a flag of Γ. An element x ∈X is incident to F if x ∗y for all y ∈F. The residue
of Γ with respect to F, denoted by ΓF , is the incidence system formed by all the elements of
Γ incident to F but not in F. Whenever Γ is a geometry, the rank of the residue ΓF is defined
to be rank(Γ) −|F|. If Γ is of rank n, then the corank of a flag F is the difference between
the rank of Γ and the rank of F.
An incidence geometry Γ is connected if its incidence graph is connected. It is residually
connected if all its residues of rank at least two are connected. Note that, by [18, Lemma
1.6.3], a residually connected geometry Γ of rank greater or equal to 2 is always connected.
On the other hand, rank one geometries are all trivially residually connected but are never
connected.
An incidence geometry is firm, thin or thick if all its residues of flags of corank one contain,
respectively, at least two elements, exactly two elements, more than two elements.
Let Γ = Γ(X, I, ∗, t) be an incidence geometry. A correlation of Γ is a bijection ϕ of X
respecting the incidence relation ∗and such that, for every x, y ∈X, if t(x) = t(y) then
t(ϕ(x)) = t(ϕ(y)). If, moreover, ϕ fixes the types of every element (i.e t(ϕ(x)) = t(x) for all
x ∈X), then ϕ is said to be an automorphism of Γ. The group of all correlations of Γ is
denoted by Cor(Γ) and the automorphism group of Γ is denoted by Aut(Γ). Remark that
Aut(Γ) is a normal subgroup of Cor(Γ) since it is the kernel of the action of Cor(Γ) on I.
If Aut(Γ) is transitive on the set of flags of Γ then we say that Aut(Γ) is flag-transitive on
Γ. We say Aut(Γ) is chamber-transitive on Γ if Aut(Γ) is transitive on the set of chambers
of Γ.
Note that, if Γ is a geometry, being chamber-transitive is equivalent to being flag-
transitive [19, Proposition 2.2]. If moreover, the stabilizer of a chamber in Aut(Γ) is reduced
to the identity, we say that Γ is simply transitive or regular. If G is any group together with
a homomorphism ψ: G →Aut(Γ), we also say that G is flag-transitive, simply transitive
or regular on Γ if that is true of ψ(G) ≤Aut(Γ). A regular hypertope is a thin, residually
connected and flag-transitive incidence geometry [19].
The main objects of interest in this paper are incidence geometries that are obtained from
a group G together with a set (Gi)i∈I of subgroups of G as described in [3]. A coset incidence
system Γ(G, (Gi)i∈I), denoted also as (G, (Gi)i∈I), is the incidence system over the type set
I where:
(a) The elements of type i ∈I are left cosets of the form gGi, g ∈G.
(b) The incidence relation is given by non-empty intersection. More precisely, the element
gGi is incident to the element kGj if and only if gGi ∩kGj ̸= ∅.
We usually talk about the subgroups Gi as the (standard) maximal parabolic subgroups. For
J ⊆I, we define (standard) parabolic subgroups as GJ = ∩i∈JGi, and define G∅= G. When
J = I \ {i}, we say that GJ = GI\{i} are the minimal parabolic subgroups, and we will denote
them as Gi. Finally, the subgroup GI = ∩i∈IGi is called the Borel subgroup of Γ.
A coset incidence system that is an incidence geometry is called a coset geometry. Flag-
transitive geometries can always be constructed as coset geometries. The following theorem

2.2. String C-groups and their permutation representation graphs. A C-group is a
pair (G, S) where G is a group and S = {ρi | i ∈I} is a finite set of involutions generating G
that satisfy the following property, called the intersection property.
∀M, N ⊆S, ⟨M⟩∩⟨N⟩= ⟨M ∩N⟩.
A C-group is said to be of rank r if |S| = r. It is common to use the index set I = {0, . . . , r−1}.
We say a C-group is string if S is an ordered set such that it satisfies the following property,
called the commuting property or string property.
∀i, j ∈{0, . . . , r −1}, |i −j| > 1 ⇒(ρiρj)2 = 1.
The dual of a string C-group is obtained by reversing the ordering of the generators.
The Coxeter diagram of a C-group (G, S), denoted D(G, S), is the graph whose nodes
corresponds to elements of S and where the edge between the generators ρi and ρj has label
pi,j := o(ρiρj), the order of ρiρj. By convention, edges with label equal to 2 are not drawn,
and, whenever an edge should be labelled with 3, its label is omitted. For string C-groups,
the commuting property forces the diagram to be linear. It must then be as in Figure 1. Note
that Coxeter groups are themselves C-group where the only relations imposed on the group
are those described by the diagram. In other words, every C-group is a quotient of a Coxeter

String C-groups are important groups in the study of regular polytopes; indeed, string
C-groups are in one-to-one correspondence with abstract regular polytopes. From a string
C-group (G, S), one can define its maximal parabolic subgroups as Gi := ⟨S \ {ρi}⟩, for every
i ∈I, and build a coset incidence system (G, (Gi)i∈I) that is a regular polytope, or in other
words, a regular hypertope with linear Buekenhout diagram (see [18, 19] for more details).
On the other hand, the automorphism group of a regular hypertope is always a C-group.
Hence, studying (string) C-groups give information regarding the geometrical structure these
encode.

5. Applications
In this section, we show a few applications of the tools developed earlier in the article.
5.1. High rank string C-groups represented by permutation representation graphs.
There is currently an active research on polytopes whose automorphism group G is a tran-
sitive proper subgroup of Sym(n). Particularly, there is a strong interest for groups of high
rank, meaning that the number r of generating involutions for G satisfies r ≥n/2. In this line
of research, one can obtain a classification of string groups generated by involutions (groups
generated by an ordered set of involutions such that any pair of non-consecutive involutions
commutes) with the desired properties. However, in order to obtain regular polytopes, one
must then show that these groups are actually string C-groups. In order to do so, the in-
tersection property given in Section 2.2 must be verified. This final step is usually difficult
(see [10, 20]).
In this section, we will show how the operations defined in this article and in [2] allow to
verify that the intersection property holds for many of these groups. In particular, we will
pick some cases from [10], for which the intersection property was not verified yet. We will
start with the easy case of a direct product, and move on to more complicated cases.

5.2. Twisting Correlations. Correlations are symmetries of an incidence system which
permute the types of the elements (see Section 2.1). Let α = (A, (Ai)i∈Iα) be a coset incidence
geometry and let B = Out(A) be the outer automorphism group of A. In this setting, the
group B naturally comes with an action φ on A. As long as B can naturally be considered
as an coset incidence system β such that α is (β, φ)-admissible, we can then construct the
twisting T (α, β).
This setting can for example be realized in the context of self-dual regular polytopes.
Theorem 5.2. Let α be a regular self-dual polytope of rank n ≥3 with automorphism group
A = ⟨ρ0, ρ1, · · · , ρn−1⟩. Then,
(a) There exists τ ∈Aut(A) such that τ(ρi) = ρn−i−1;

Moreover, if n > 3, the hypertope can be chosen such that its diagram is not linear;
(c) If n = 3, 4, 5, there exists a regular polytope of rank ⌈n/2⌉+1 with automorphism group
A ⋊⟨τ⟩;

A ⋊⟨τ⟩is a subgroup of Aut(A).

a duality φ of α that sends the chamber C to itself, while permuting the elements inside of it.

19], for example).
(b):
The hypertope in question is Γ = T (α, β) where β = (⟨τ⟩, {e}).

5.3. Lamplighter Group. We conclude this paper with an interesting application that is
slightly outside of the scope of the tools we developed. Indeed, we will apply the twisting of
coset incidence systems in the context of an infinite set Ω. This shows simultaneously that
the twisting and wreath product construction for coset incidence systems can be extended to
infinite index sets, but that one must be careful in doing so. Notice that when Ωis infinite,
the wreath product A ≀ΩB of two groups can be defined as (⊕ΩA) ⋊B or (Q
ΩA) ⋊B, and
those are different. The second one is usually called the unrestricted wreath product of A by
B.
The lamplighter group G is a classical example of wreath product. Its name comes from
its natural action on (⊕ZC2) that can be interpreted as an infinite street with lampposts that
can be turned on or off. As such, G is the (restricted) wreath product C2 ≀Z where Z acts
by shifts on (⊕ZC2). It is well know that G = ⟨a, t | a2, (atnat−n), ∀n ∈N⟩and that G is
not finitely presentable. Let α = (C2, ({e})) and β = (Z, ({e})) be the two rank one coset

sum ¯α = L
