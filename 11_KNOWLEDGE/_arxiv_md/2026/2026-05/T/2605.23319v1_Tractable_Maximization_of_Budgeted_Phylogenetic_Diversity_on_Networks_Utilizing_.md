Abstract
Identifying a subset of taxa that maximizes Phylogenetic Diversity (PD) is a cornerstone of quanti-
tative conservation planning. Traditionally, PD is defined over a phylogenetic tree in which leaves
resemble present-day taxa and the branch lengths capture the estimated evolutionary distinctiveness.
While PD maximization is computationally tractable on trees with unit costs, the problem becomes
NP-hard when transitioning to phylogenetic networks or to budgeted versions in which protecting
taxa incurs non-homogeneous costs. In this paper, we address these two challenges by providing
definitions and a comprehensive analysis of three distinct variants of budgeted PD on networks.
We conduct our study through the lens of a small structural parameter, node scanwidth (nsw),
which measures the “tree-likeness” of a phylogenetic network. We show that two of the considered
variants can be optimized in O∗(2nsw · B2) time, where B is the budget. For the computationally
harder, third variant, we provide an algorithm to compute PD scores in O∗(3nsw) time. We further
contribute the first exact algorithms to compute node scanwidth, recognizing that the utility of
algorithms based on nsw depends on the ability to compute nsw and its corresponding decomposi-
tion. Our approaches integrate data reduction rules, dynamic programming, and an Integer Linear
Programming formulation. We validate our theoretical results through extensive experiments on
highly reticulated, simulated networks containing several hundred taxa, using heterogeneous costs.
Our implementation computes PD scores and optimal nsw in fractions of a second, even on the most
challenging instances. Furthermore, our budgeted optimization algorithms significantly outperform
existing benchmarks for computing PD on networks, which were previously limited to unit-cost
scenarios. The software makes analyses even on networks with a thousand taxa tractable in practice.

Introduction
The biosphere is currently undergoing what scientists describe as the Sixth Mass Extinc-
tion [1, 6]. Unlike previous events driven by natural phenomena, the current decline in
biodiversity is largely anthropogenic, resulting from habitat destruction, climate change, and
over-exploitation. In this urgent context, conservation biologists face the daunting “agony of
choice”: Given limited resources, which species should be prioritized for protection? [34]
Functional diversity—the range of biological traits and roles within an ecosystem—is
often considered the gold standard for prioritization [24, 28]. However, collecting exhaustive
trait data for hundreds of thousands of species remains a major bottleneck [10]. Phylogenetic
diversity (PD) has thus emerged as a desired proxy, operating on the principle that the
evolutionary history captured in a phylogeny reflects the underlying feature diversity of
a clade. Classically, the basis of PD is a phylogenetic tree in which the leaves represent
present-day species and edge weights can model phylogenetic differences between species.
The PD score of a set of species A then is the total weight of the edges between the root of
the tree and any species in A [10].
Fortunately, PD can be computed with a greedy algorithm on trees [10, 26, 30], such that
even very large instances can be solved optimally within seconds [25]. Several extensions that
make the problem more realistic were introduced, but always at the cost of computational
complexity. One of the first generalizations introduced was adding integer costs to species [27],
which however also generalizes the weakly NP-hard Knapsack problem.
While PD on trees is well-understood, biological reality is rarely strictly bifurcating [16].
Evolutionary processes such as hybridization, horizontal gene transfer, and incomplete lineage
sorting necessitate the use of phylogenetic networks [16, 23]; see Figure 1. By moving beyond
trees, PD measures can account for the reticulate nature of evolution, capturing the unique
contributions of taxa with multiple ancestries. This ensures that conservation priorities reflect
the true genetic heritage and evolutionary potential stored within a complex ecosystem,
rather than an oversimplified approximation of a tree. On networks, however, even simple
variants of PD maximization become NP-hard [3, 31, 35]. While budgetary constraints (in its
most general form the Generalized Noah’s Ark Problem) have been studied on trees [21, 27],
their integration with network models remains unexplored.
Our Contribution
In this paper, we address this gap by considering budgeted PD on networks, where taxa
are assigned integer costs. As optimization of PD on trees with a budget is already NP-
hard [27] and many variants of PD on networks are W[2]-hard with respect to the solution
size [17, 32], we can not hope for tractable algorithm with respect to tree-likeness or the
budget alone. Instead, we propose a combined parameterization with respect to the budget
plus the tree-likeness of the network, captured by its node scanwidth [4] (formal definition in
the next section). Specifically, our research provides the following contributions to the study
of computational phylogenetic diversity.
New Budgeted PD Variants: We introduce budgeted variants of phylogenetic diversity
maximization on networks, in which species may have varying costs of protection.
Optimizing Budgeted PD: We show, in Section 4, how to solve three budgeted variants
of PD on networks efficiently with respect to the combined parameter of budget (B) and
node scanwidth (nswN). We provide two O∗(2nswN · min2(B, B)) time algorithms, where B
is the total cost of all species minus the budget. We further show that values for the third
variant can be computed in O∗(3nswN ) time.

Phylogenetic Diversity
For a phylogenetic tree T on X, the phylogenetic diversity of a set of taxa A ⊆X, denoted
by PDT (A), is the total weight of all edges in T with offspring in A, or equivalently, the total
weight of edges on paths from the root to taxa in A. We consider three different extensions
of phylogenetic diversity to networks. On trees, all three measures coincide with PDT (A).
A natural first extension to a phylogenetic network N on X considers all paths from the
root to taxa simultaneously. The all-paths phylogenetic diversity of A ⊆X in N, denoted
by PDmap
N
(A), is the total weight of all edges in N with offspring in A (see Figure 1).
A second family of PD on networks arises from switchings. Formally, letting R(N) ⊆V be
the set of reticulations of a phylogenetic network N = (V, E, ω), a switching σ : R(N) →E is
a function that maps each reticulation vertex to one of its incoming edges (called reticulation

diversity variant. For a phylogenetic network N on X and a set of taxa A ⊆X, we define

Computing Node Scanwidth
In this section, we show how to solve Node Scanwidth tree-extension, that is, we find
an optimal tree-extension together with the node scanwidth for a given DAG G. We do this
in two steps; first we apply reduction rules to identify and remove easy structures in N, and
then we show how to solve Node Scanwidth tree-extension on the remaining small
subgraphs with an algorithm based on [14], or with an Integer Linear Program.
Throughout this section, let G = (V, E) be a DAG—not necessarily a phylogenetic
network. As we operate with general graphs, reticulations in this section are defined to have
in-degree at least 2 and can have out-degree ≥2. This ensures that every non-leaf vertex is
either a reticulation, a tree-vertex, or a chain-vertex. We note that there might be several
roots, and leaves may be reticulations.
3.1
Preliminary Observations
The bag of any vertex contains all its parents. We conclude the following.
▶Observation 3.1. The node scanwidth of any graph is at least the maximum in-degree of
its vertices.
For a given graph G with tree-extension Γ, we observe that Γ is also a tree-extension
when removing edges from G. For each vertex v, identifying v with its Γ-parent creates a
tree-extension of G −v that does not have larger node scanwidth.

3.3
Dealing With Remaining Hard Instances
Since Node Scanwidth tree-extension is NP-hard [4], unless NP = P, reduction rules,
all taking polynomial time, won’t suffice to find an optimal tree-extension. We deal with
this issue by adapting a known algorithm for computing an optimal tree-extension for
scanwidth [14]. We deviate from this algorithm by considering not the reduction rules
presented there, as they are partly incorrect for node scanwidth. We further change the
computation of the bags GW (Lines 7, 9 and 12 in Algorithm 3 of [14]), as not counting the
edges, but the nodes—obviously. We leave the proper definition to [14], but for reasons of
self-containment, we want to give a sketch of the algorithm here.
The algorithm utilizes a dynamic programming table over a subset W of vertices where
in each computational step the (node) scanwidth of G[W] is computed by branching into
the different weakly connected components of G[W]. This creates a topological ordering of
the vertices, which later can be branched out into a tree-extension. If a threshold of (node)
scanwidth is exceeded, then the computation does not chase the current branch down even
further. For a proof of correctness we refer to [14].
We further define an Integer Linear Program instance to solve Node Scanwidth
tree-extension. This definition is not meant to be a fast algorithm, as Integer Linear

We use five kinds of variables to find an optimal tree-extension Γ. The variables are

Algorithms for Budgeted Phylogenetic Diversity on Networks
In this section, we show how to use node scanwidth as a strong tool for computing and
optimizing phylogenetic diversity. We assume that a phylogenetic network N and a node
tree-extension with node scanwidth nswN is given. We first show that b-MAP-PD and
b-Max-Tree-PD can be optimized in O(2nswN ·nswN 2·min2(B, B)·n2) time. Afterward, we
show that the value PDmin
N (A) can be computed in O(3nswN ·nswN 2 ·n2) time, for any A ⊆X.
We restrict ourselves to these three definitions of phylogenetic diversity on networks, as
optimizing the unweighted versions of Network-PD and Average-Switching-Tree-PD
is known to be NP-hard even for constant values of edge scanwidth and therefore node
scanwidth [32, 33].
In the three different algorithms for optimizing b-MAP-PD and b-Max-Tree-PD and
computing values of Min-Tree-PD, we use certain structures that we define upon using
them. Figure 4 gives a visual example of these structures.

Experiments
Simulated data.
To assess computational efficiency of our algorithms, we conducted a
simulation study on the effect of different network parameters on running time, using the
same benchmark networks as in [15]. The dataset consists of 6,400 phylogenetic networks
simulated under a birth-death-hybridization model using the R package SiPhyNetwork [19]. It
contains 100 level-ℓ-networks with n leaves for each combination of n ∈{20, 50, 100, 200} and
ℓ∈{0, . . . , 15}. Here, the (edge-)level is the most commonly used measure of tree-likeness in
the phylogenetics literature, defined as the maximum, over all bi-connected components, of the
number of reticulation edges minus the number of reticulation vertices. Since node scanwidth
is expected to be particularly advantageous over edge scanwidth for networks containing
vertices of larger outdegree, we modified the networks to be non-binary by contracting the
shortest 10% of edges, while preserving adherence to our network definition.
Experimental results.
Initial experiments confirmed that our dynamic programming
algorithm—combined with the reduction rules—computes the node scanwidth of all considered
instances with ease. Since it closely follows the edge scanwidth framework already evaluated

6
Discussion
In this work, we found a good trade-off between realistic models of phylogenetic diversity and
computational efficiency. By moving beyond tree models to explicit phylogenetic networks
that are not even necessarily binary, combined with non-uniform costs per taxa, we provide
a framework that better represents the reticulate nature of life on Earth.
Our O∗(2nswN · min2(B, B)) solvers have proven efficient when nswN is small—as is
common in many empirical biological datasets [14]—such that the addition of budgetary
constraints does not necessarily lead to an explosion in the algorithm’s running time.
Our practical implementations validate the theoretical results and establish a new base-
line for efficiency, also. By beating state-of-the-art algorithms [15] on unitary instances
while simultaneously handling arbitrary (non-binary) network topologies and non-uniform
conservation cost, we provide a tool that is ready for deployment in conservation planning.
From a theoretical perspective, we wonder whether b-Max-Tree-PD can be solved
in O∗(2B) time. Relatedly, we suspect that Min-Tree-PD can not be solved in O∗(cnswN ·
Bd) time for constants c and d. If true, it could be interesting to investigate whether
Min-Tree-PD can at least be solved in O∗(cℓ· Bd) time for constants c and d, where ℓ
is the vertex-level. This is immediate for binary networks but appears significantly more
difficult for arbitrary networks. Addressing these questions will be useful for further refining
the algorithmic tools available to protect our planet’s vanishing biodiversity.
