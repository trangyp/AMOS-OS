THE COMAXIMAL GRAPH OF A FINITE-DIMENSIONAL
LIE ALGEBRA
DAVID A. TOWERS, YESNERI ZULETA, AND ISMAEL GUTIERREZ
Abstract. In this paper, we introduce the comaximal graph Γ(L) of a
finite-dimensional Lie algebra L, whose vertices are the nontrivial proper
Lie subalgebras of L over a field F, and two vertices A and B are adjacent
if and only if ⟨A, B⟩= L. We establish general structural properties,
including a characterization of isolated vertices via the Frattini subalge-
bra and a criterion for completeness in terms of µ-algebras. We classify
Γ(L) for all Lie algebras of dimension at most three over a finite field
Fq, providing an explicit description in each case. The resulting graphs
exhibit a rich range of behaviors, depending on the structure of the de-
rived algebra and the action of ad x. For L ∼= sl2(Fq), we determine
several graph invariants, including the degree sequence, clique number,
chromatic number, domination number, diameter, and radius, and show
that Γ(L) is connected and non-planar.
The graph contains a large
clique formed by the nonsplit semisimple lines together with the Borel
subalgebras, while the nilpotent and split semisimple lines have a more
restricted adjacency structure governed by their containment in Borel
subalgebras.
1. Introduction
Associating graphs with algebraic structures has proven to be a fruit-
ful approach for encoding algebraic information into combinatorial form.
Well-known examples include commuting graphs, non-commuting graphs,
intersection graphs, and power graphs of groups and rings. These construc-
tions often reveal subtle structural features that are not immediately visible
from the algebraic definition alone.
In the context of vector spaces, A. Das [7] introduced the subspace sum
graph, whose vertices are the nonzero proper subspaces of a finite-dimensional
vector space V , and where two subspaces are adjacent if and only if their
sum equals V . This construction was later studied and extended by several
authors [8, 9]. The adjacency condition reflects a spanning property rather
than inclusion, leading to a highly symmetric and well-understood graph.

dimensional Lie algebras has been initiated in [13] and [14]. In [13], the

Let L be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field F. The comaximal
graph of L, denoted by Γ(L), is the simple graph whose vertex set consists
of all nontrivial proper Lie subalgebras of L, and where two distinct vertices
A and B are adjacent if and only if ⟨A, B⟩= L.
Unlike the subspace sum graph, the vertex set of Γ(L) is restricted to Lie
subalgebras rather than arbitrary subspaces. Consequently, the structure of
Γ(L) depends not only on the dimension of L but also on its internal Lie
structure. Even in low dimensions, the behavior of Γ(L) varies dramatically
among abelian, nilpotent, solvable, and simple Lie algebras.
Throughout this paper, we focus primarily on Lie algebras over finite fields
Fq. This setting is natural for a combinatorial study: over finite fields, the
number of subalgebras is finite and explicitly computable, the subalgebra
lattice has a concrete projective-geometric interpretation, and the resulting
graphs can be described with precise cardinalities and invariants.
Our aim is to initiate a systematic study of the comaximal graph of finite-
dimensional Lie algebras, with a primary focus on Lie algebras over finite
fields. We begin by establishing general structural properties of Γ(L), includ-
ing a characterization of isolated vertices in terms of the Frattini subalgebra
and a criterion for completeness.
The main part of the paper is devoted to a complete classification of Γ(L)
for Lie algebras of dimension at most three over a finite field Fq. When
dim L ≤2, the graph is easily determined. The three-dimensional case is
substantially richer and splits naturally according to the dimension of the
derived algebra L′.
In the simple case L = sl2(Fq), we compute the degree sequence of Γ(L)
explicitly and determine several graph invariants, including the clique num-
ber, chromatic number, domination number, diameter, and radius. We prove
that Γ(L) is connected and non-planar. The graph contains a large clique
formed by the nonsplit semisimple lines together with the Borel subalge-
bras; the Borel subalgebras alone form a complete subgraph Kq+1, while
the nilpotent and split semisimple lines have a more restricted adjacency
structure governed by their containment in Borel subalgebras.

2. Preliminaries
Basics on Graphs. A graph Γ = (V, E) consists of a set of vertices V and
a set of edges E ⊆{{u, v} | u, v ∈V, u ̸= v}. All graphs in this paper are
simple and undirected. We recall the following standard terminology; for a
comprehensive reference, see [4, 5, 6]. The order and size of Γ are |V | and
|E|, respectively. The degree deg(v) of a vertex v is the number of edges
incident to v. A graph is regular if all vertices have the same degree. The
degree sequence of Γ is the list of degrees of all vertices in non-increasing
order. A graph is complete if every pair of distinct vertices is adjacent; the
complete graph on n vertices is denoted by Kn.
A path of length k in Γ is a sequence of distinct vertices v0, v1, . . . , vk such
that {vi, vi+1} ∈E for all i. The distance d(u, v) between two vertices is
the length of a shortest path connecting them, or ∞if no such path exists.
The eccentricity ecc(v) of a vertex v is max{d(v, u) | u ∈V }. The diameter
diam(Γ) is the maximum eccentricity, the radius r(Γ) is the minimum ec-
centricity, and the center of Γ is the set of vertices achieving the minimum
eccentricity.
A graph Γ is connected if there is a path between every pair of vertices.
The girth of Γ is the length of a shortest cycle, or ∞if Γ has no cycles.
A clique is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices. The clique number ω(Γ) is
the size of a largest clique. An independent set is a set of pairwise non-
adjacent vertices.
A proper coloring of Γ is an assignment of colors to
vertices such that no two adjacent vertices share a color. The chromatic
number χ(Γ) is the minimum number of colors needed. A graph is called
perfect if χ(H) = ω(H) for every induced subgraph H of Γ.
A dominating set is a set S ⊆V such that every vertex not in S is
adjacent to some vertex in S. The domination number γ(Γ) is the size of a
smallest dominating set. A graph is planar if it can be drawn in the plane
without edge crossings. By Kuratowski’s theorem, Γ is planar if and only
if it contains no subdivision of K5 or K3,3 as a subgraph. A claw is the
complete bipartite graph K1,3. A graph is claw-free if it contains no induced
subgraph isomorphic to a claw.
Throughout the paper, we use Γ(L)∗to denote the subgraph obtained
from Γ(L) by removing the isolated vertices.
Basics on Lie Algebras. Let L be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a
field F. A Lie algebra is a vector space L over F, equipped with a bilinear
operation [·, ·] : L × L →L, called the Lie bracket, satisfying the following
axioms:
(1) Alternating property: [x, x] = 0 ∀x ∈L;
(2) Jacobi identity: [x, [y, z]] + [y, [z, x]] + [z, [x, y]] = 0 ∀x, y, z ∈L.
These imply anticommutativity: [x, y] = −[y, x] for all x, y ∈L.
A Lie algebra is said to be abelian if [x, y] = 0 for all x, y ∈L. Every
one-dimensional Lie algebra is abelian. The dimension of a Lie algebra is its

The derived series of L is defined recursively by L(0) = L, and L(k+1) =
[L(k), L(k)]. Further, the lower central series is given by L1 = L, and Li+1 =
[L, Li]. The derived algebra of L, usually denoted by L′ is L2 = [L, L]. It is

if L2 = L. The Lie algebra L is called solvable if L(r) = 0 for some r, and

the converse is not true.
The smallest nonabelian nilpotent Lie algebra is the Heisenberg algebra

central, and every ad x is nilpotent of index at most 2.

exhibit a more rigid and intricate subalgebra geometry.

3. The comaximal graph of a Lie algebra
Definition 3.1. Let L be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field F.
We define the comaximal graph of L as the undirected, simple graph Γ(L) =
(V, E) as follows: V is the set of all non-trivial proper subalgebras of L, and
for A, B ∈V, {A, B} ∈E if and only if ⟨A, B⟩= L.
Remark 3.1. The definition is inspired by the subspace-sum graph intro-
duced by A. Das [7] and subsequently extended by R. Venkatasalam and S.
Chelliah [8], where vertices are all nonzero proper subspaces of a vector space
V , and adjacency is defined by spanning the whole space. In our setting, the
vertices are Lie subalgebras rather than arbitrary subspaces, and the resulting
graph reflects the internal algebraic structure of L much more subtly.
Lemma 3.2. Let L be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field F. Then
a vertex of Γ(L) is isolated if and only if it is contained in the Frattini
subalgebra of L. In particular, if F(L) = 0, then the graph Γ(L) has no
isolated vertices.
Proof. Suppose that A is an isolated vertex of Γ(L), and let M be any
maximal subalgebra of L. If A ̸⊆M, then maximality of M implies ⟨A, M⟩=
L. This would mean that A and M are adjacent, a contradiction. Hence
A ⊆M for every maximal subalgebra M of L, and thus A ⊆F(L).
Conversely, assume that A ⊆F(L), and let B be any vertex of Γ(L). If
we choose a maximal subalgebra M of L containing B, we have ⟨A, B⟩⊆
M ̸= L. This shows that A is not adjacent to any vertex of Γ(L).
□
Following the same arguments as in [7, Theorem 3.1], we obtain the fol-
lowing result.
Theorem 3.3. Γ(L) is complete if and only if every proper subalgebra is
one-dimensional.
Proof. Suppose Γ(L) is complete.
If S is a proper subalgebra of L and
dim(S) > 1, pick any two linearly independent elements x, y ∈S. Then
⟨Fx, Fy⟩⊆S ̸= L, so A = Fx and B = Fy are not adjacent, contradicting
completeness of Γ(L).
Conversely, if every proper subalgebra of L is one-dimensional, the sub-
algebra generated by any two of them is equal to L, and so Γ(L) is com-
plete.
□

Before proceeding to study the comaximal graphs for low-dimensional Lie
algebras over a field F, we record two general adjacency facts that hold for
any three-dimensional Lie algebra over any field F, and that will be used
repeatedly throughout the next Section without further comment. The key
observation is that in dimension three, the condition ⟨A, B⟩= L depends
only on the dimensions of A and B, independently of the Lie bracket struc-
ture of L.
Lemma 3.7. Let L be a three-dimensional Lie algebra over a field F, and
let L denote the set of lines (one-dimensional Lie subalgebras) and P the set
of planes (two-dimensional Lie subalgebras) of L.
(1) If A, B ∈P with A ̸= B, then A ∼B in Γ(L). In particular, the induced
subgraph Γ(L)[P] is always a complete graph.
(2) If A ∈L and B ∈P, then A ∼B in Γ(L) if and only if A ̸⊆B.
Proof. (1) Since A and B are distinct two-dimensional subspaces of the
three-dimensional space L, we have dim(A + B) = 3, hence ⟨A, B⟩=
A + B = L, and therefore A ∼B.
(2) Let A ∈L and B ∈P. If A ⊆B, then ⟨A, B⟩= B ̸= L, so A and B are
not adjacent. Conversely, if A ̸⊆B, then ⟨A, B⟩= L, and A and B are
adjacent.
□
4. The comaximal graphs for low-dimensional Lie algebras
over finite fields
4.1. Comaximal graph of a 1-dimensional Lie algebra. Let L be a 1-
dimensional Lie algebra over the field Fq. Then the comaximal graph Γ(L)
is the empty graph.
4.2. Comaximal graph of a 2-dimensional Lie algebra. Let L be a
2-dimensional Lie algebra over the field Fq. Up to isomorphism, there are
exactly 2 Lie algebras of dimension 2 over Fq: the abelian one, and the non-
abelian one with bracket [x, y] = y (see [10], Chapter 1). In both cases, every
one-dimensional subspace of L is a Lie subalgebra. Then the comaximal
graph Γ(L) is the complete graph Kq+1. In fact, the set of vertices V has
q + 1 elements. If A, B ∈V, with A ̸= B, then ⟨A, B⟩= A + B = L. That
is, every pair of distinct vertices is adjacent in the comaximal graph, and so
Γ(L) is the complete graph Kq+1.
