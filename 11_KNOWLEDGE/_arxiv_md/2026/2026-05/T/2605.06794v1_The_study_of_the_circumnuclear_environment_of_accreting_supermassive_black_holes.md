ABSTRACT
X-ray spectral modeling is a powerful tool for studying the immediate environment of accreting
objects, including supermassive black holes. Several models, either phenomenological or physically
driven, have been developed over the past decade to study X-ray spectra, delivering important insights
into the properties of circumnuclear material of active galactic nuclei (AGN). Despite the fact that
these models are able to reproduce the data well, they often lack realistic geometries, and most of them
consist of simplified configurations such as a slab or a torus. We use the ray-tracing code RefleX to
generate new spectral models that cover a wide energy range in the X-ray band, adopting a realistic
configuration for the surrounding material.
We introduce two new table models that are publicly
available: 1) the RXToPo model, which features an X-ray source along with a dusty torus and a
polar hollow cone; 2) the RXagn1 model, which includes, besides the torus and polar cone, also the
accretion disk and the broad line region. Both models were applied to the X-ray spectrum of NGC
424, demonstrating their potential to study sources whose X-ray emission is dominated by reprocessed
radiation.

1. INTRODUCTION
Supermassive black holes (SMBHs) are known to re-
side at the center of most galaxies (e.g., Salpeter 1964;
Lynden-Bell 1969; Cavaliere & Padovani 1989; Marconi
et al. 2004; Kormendy & Ho 2013). These SMBHs are
often embedded in large amounts of gas and dust (e.g.,
Ramos Almeida & Ricci 2017), which serve as the nec-
essary reservoir of material that is eventually funneled
onto the SMBH during the accreting phase, when these
objects are observed as active galactic nuclei (AGN).
The accretion disk that is formed during this phase can
produce a significant fraction of the photons that come
out of these systems (e.g., Novikov & Thorne 1973;
Shakura & Sunyaev 1973).
The UV/optical photons
produced in the accretion disk are then up-scattered
to the X-rays through inverse Compton scattering by a
hot electron cloud located very close to the SMBH (e.g.,
Shapiro et al. 1976; Sunyaev & Titarchuk 1980; Light-
man & Zdziarski 1987; Haardt & Maraschi 1991, 1993;
Fabian et al. 2009). This hot electron cloud, known as
the X-ray corona, produces most of the X-ray radiation
in these systems.
Although we do not yet have a complete understand-
ing of the immediate environments of SMBHs, a widely
accepted theory suggests that a thick torus of dust and
gas obscures the central region. This paradigm, known
as the unification model, suggests that different types
of AGN arise when viewing this structure at different
angles (e.g., Antonucci 1993; Urry & Padovani 1995;
Netzer 2015). In most AGN (∼60 −70%) in the lo-
cal Universe the X-ray source is obscured (Ricci et al.
2015).
The dusty torus is expected to absorb a sig-
nificant amount of the disk emission and re-emit it at
infrared wavelengths (e.g., Rowan-Robinson 1995, 1998;
Stalevski et al. 2016). The accretion disk and the dusty
torus are expected to be connected through the broad
line region (BLR), where broad emission lines are pro-
duced by the high velocity of the gas (> 2000 km s−1;
e.g., Peterson et al. 1991; 1993; 2004, Kaspi et al. 2000;
Goad et al. 2012; GRAVITY Collaboration et al. 2020a,
2024).
Torus models have been shown to successfully re-
produce the IR emission of local AGN, which further
supports the unification model paradigm (e.g., Alonso-

these systems, and is expected to reside outside the dust
sublimation region, where dust is thought to exist as a
mix of carbon and olivine silicate grains.
When X-ray photons intercept the aforementioned
configurations of gas and dust, some of them are
scattered, absorbed and/or produce fluorescence (e.g.,
George & Fabian 1991a; Magdziarz & Zdziarski 1995a;
Lamer et al. 2000; Alexander et al. 2013; Del Moro et al.
2017; Gupta et al. 2021). One of the most important fea-
tures of reprocessed X-ray radiation are fluorescent lines,
such as the iron Kα line (Fe Kα) at 6.4 keV (e.g., Nan-
dra et al. 1997; Yaqoob & Serlemitsos 2000; Shu et al.
2010; Shu et al. 2011). Another important spectral fea-
ture is the ”Compton hump”, a broad component typ-
ically found at energies around 30 keV (e.g., George &
Fabian 1991b). Reprocessed X-ray radiation can there-
fore provide valuable information about the properties of
the material surrounding the SMBH, including its com-
position and geometry (e.g., Reynolds & Fabian 2008;
Brightman & Nandra 2011; Goad et al. 2012; Garc´ıa
et al. 2013; Saha et al. 2021; Diaz et al. 2023; Dong
et al. 2023).
To constrain the main physical properties of the cir-
cumnuclear material, numerous X-ray models have been
developed over the past decades. These models incor-
porate a variety of geometries and physical processes.
One of the most widely used is pexrav (Magdziarz &
Zdziarski 1995b), which considers a corona on top of
a slab, representing the accretion disk. More sophisti-
cated disk-reflection models, such as xillver (Garc´ıa
et al.
2010, 2013) and rellxil (Garc´ıa et al. 2014),
implemented relativistic effects in the accretion disk.
A significant amount of photons that are emitted from
the central region are expected to interact with the
gas and dust found beyond the accretion disk, and in
particular with the dusty torus.
As a result, several
torus models have been developed in the past 15 years.
MYtorus (Murphy & Yaqoob 2009) assumes an ax-
isymmetric toroidal geometry, implementing the clas-
sic orientation-dependent AGN paradigm, and consid-
ering a wide range of torus equatorial column densities
(NH eq = 1022 −1025 cm−2). The RXTorus model was
built using the radiative transfer code RefleX (Pal-
tani & Ricci 2017).
Similar to the MYtorus it fea-
tures a toroidal medium, although in this model, be-
sides the equatorial hydrogen column density (NH eq =
1022 −1025 cm−2), the covering factor (i.e. the fraction
of the sky of the AGN covered by gas and dust) is a
free parameter. The borus02 model (Balokovi´c et al.
2018) considers a spherical torus model, and it only in-
cludes reprocessed X-ray radiation. The two main free
parameters of this model are the torus column density,
in the log(NH/cm−2) = 22.0 −25.5 range, and the cov-
ering factor of the torus.
Over the past decade, sev-
eral models featuring clumpy torus geometries have been
developed. Liu & Li (2014) used the toolkit Geant4
(Agostinelli et al. 2003) to create a model in which the
torus is clumpy. The number of clouds (Nclouds), the
filling factor (ϕ) and the total line-of-sight column den-
sity (NH) are free parameters in this model. Tanimoto
et al. (2019) introduced the XCLUMPY torus model,
an X-ray adaptation of the CLUMPY infrared model
(Nenkova et al. 2008).
The UxClumpy model (Buch-
ner et al. 2019) is also based on the CLUMPY infrared
model, and assumes the same toroidal geometry. Be-
sides the X-ray spectral models that are available in the
literature with their given configuration, there are dif-
ferent Monte-Carlo codes that propagate photons into
various media. Some examples of such codes are: the
MONACO (MONte Carlo simulation for Astrophysics and
COsmology; Odaka et al. 2011), the python code XARS
(X-ray Monte-Carlo simulator; Buchner et al. 2019), and
SKIRT, which spans a large range of energies, from radio
to X-rays (Baes et al. 2003; Baes et al. 2011; Vander
Meulen et al. 2023).
The aforementioned models are widely utilized in the
literature. However, to accurately reproduce the repro-
cessed X-ray radiation observed in AGN, it is essential
to consider all the different structures present in AGN,
such as the accretion disk, broad line region, torus, and
polar dust. X-ray photons are expected to interact with
all these components; thus, simplified approaches, such
as separate modeling of the torus and the disk, could
suffer from strong degeneracies.
In this work, we use the ray-tracing code RefleX to
develop two new complex models to realistically repro-
duce the circumnuclear material around SMBHs. The
first model, RXToPo, includes a polar hollow cone
(representing the polar dusty gas) and a torus. The sec-
ond model, RXagn1 is an extension of RXToPo and

2. SIMULATION SETUP
2.1. RefleX
In order to construct a physically-motivated AGN
model, we conducted X-ray spectral simulations using
the RefleX1 ray-tracing code (Paltani & Ricci 2017).
RefleX models the propagation of X-ray photons with
energies ranging from 0.1 keV to ∼1 MeV, taking into
account all the main physical processes that occur at
these energies. It considers the full relativistic Klein-
Nishina formula for the cross-sections as well as the
proper corrections when the scattering involves bound
atoms.
Moreover, it includes the absorption energy
shells for 30 neutral elements along with their respective
emission lines.
Finally, the latest version implements
dust grains, where the interaction is governed by the
Mie theory (Mie 1908; Wiscombe 1980). The modular
design of RefleX allows the user to create X-ray spec-
tral models by incorporating multiple building blocks,
which can vary in both their geometrical form and their
physical properties. Through Monte Carlo simulations,
RefleX tracks each photon as it travels through the
material, marking all interactions that occur along the
way. The code generates images and spectra based on
the chosen geometrical setup; however, in this work, we
focus only on the spectral output to develop our models.
In § 2.2 and § 2.3 the geometries used are described in
detail. We utilized RefleX 3.0 (Ricci & Paltani 2023),
which also includes interactions between photons and
dust grains.

for the energy regions of the Fe Kα, Fe Kβ (including the

binning with a step size of 0.01. The cutoff energy of
the spectrum is fixed to 200 keV, corresponding to the
median value of nearby AGN (Ricci et al. 2018). The
photon index Γ varies from 1.6 to 2.4, covering the range
typically observed in AGN (e.g., Piconcelli et al. 2005;
Ricci et al. 2017b). In Table 1 we summarize the prop-

2.2.3. The polar cone
Infrared observations of local AGN suggest the pres-
ence of an extended region that can reach hundreds of
parsecs, composed of warm dust (e.g. Stalevski et al.
2017; Asmus 2019; Alonso-Herrero et al. 2021). In the
RXToPo model, we implement this dusty structure by
adopting a hollow cone geometry, similar to that which
has been shown to effectively reproduce the IR emission
of the Circinus galaxy (Stalevski et al. 2017, 2019). In
Fig. 1 we illustrate the cone component, while the col-
umn density profile of the RXToPo model is shown
in Fig. 2. The hollow cone has the angular width set to
10◦the findings of Stalevski et al. (2017) for the Circinus
galaxy. The opening angle of the cone, or in other words,
the angle at which the cone starts intercepting the X-
ray source, is controlled by the torus. In our model the
torus is assumed to collimate the polar cone, since this
structure is thought to be associated with material that
has been expelled from the dusty torus (e.g., Venanzi
et al. 2020). We use the opening angle of the torus (ϕ,
see for details § 2.2.2), and we set the cone to have a
maximum angle ξpol
max = ϕ + 1◦, to ensure that the two
components do not overlap. The opening angle of the

similar distances from the SMBH. Similarly to what was
done for the torus, the metal abundance adopted is lpgs

the sublimation radius since it is assumed to be dusty.
Therefore, we set the base of the geometrical object at

material the eventually settles in the polar cone origi-

natural to assume that there should be another ”com-
ponent” that lies between the innermost regions and the
polar cone. Yet, the main motivation for the polar cone
is to account for the extended emission observed in the
IR, and therefore we decided to include it in the model.

accomodates any additional neutral material

1.0 (see § 2.2.2 and Ricci & Paltani 2023), since it is ex-

most dense circumnuclear region, which can influence
the X-ray photon field that emerges from the corona.

Even though the two elements (BLR, Torus) are not
linked within our models we consider unphysical the
combination of a very small torus along with a bigger
BLR. Hence, the range of the covering factor of the torus
is CF tor = 0.2−0.9. In the case of the polar hollow cone
the column density across the inner surface is a free pa-
rameter as presented in §2.2.3. Finally, similar to the
RXToPo model the photon index and the observing
angle are free to vary (see §2.2 for details). The two
new elements included, BLR and accretion disk, have
their parameters fixed and are summarized in Table 2.
The RXagn1 model is illustrated in Fig. 3. To demon-
strate the influence of the BLR, Fig. 4 shows examples
of the line-of-sight column density for two different tori
configurations.
2.3.1. The accretion disk
The accretion disk (AD) is the structure that is ex-
pected to ”feed” the SMBH, and it is widely thought
to be responsible for the strong nuclear emission ob-
served in the optical and UV. To model the AD we


## Page 7


7
adopted the Shakura-Sunyaev (SS73) model (Shakura
& Sunyaev 1973), which considers a geometrically-thin,
optically-thick accretion disk. We set the disk to start
at 10 rg from the center and extend up to 1000 rg, to
be consistent with recent studies of optical variability
(e.g.
McHardy et al. 2023).
We set the metallicity
to unity, and used the lpgs abundance for consistency
with the other objects. Due to the high temperatures in
the disk, the hydrogen and helium are fully ionized and
the medium dust-free. Finally the volumetric density is
fixed to nH = 1012 cm−3 (e.g., Garc´ıa et al. 2013). The
current version of our model does not incorporate any
general relativity effect. In Table 2 we summarize the
properties of the disk.
2.3.2. The broad line region
The second additional object included in the RXagn1
model is the broad line region (BLR). The BLR is the re-
gion where broad emission lines are produced, which im-
plies the presence of gas that revolves at velocities that
can reach up to a few hundreds thousands of km/s. The
current version of RXagn1 model does not include any
velocity broadening associated with the motion of the
BLR clouds. It will be implemented in a future version
of RefleX. Yet, the BLR can provide additional obscu-
ration for high inclination angles (see Fig. 4). We assume
that the BLR extends from the end of the AD up to the
sublimation zone where the torus starts. To model the
BLR we used a flare disk model (e.g., Gravity Collabora-
tion et al. 2018, 2020a). To develop this model, we used
the annulus geometry provided by RefleX. A series of
30 consecutive annuli were generated. These annuli vary
in width (i.e. inner and outer radius from the center of
the simulation) and height. Using this method, we de-
velop a model for the BLR, where its center is associated
with the luminosity-weighted distance as described by
Kaspi et al. (2005). In our model the height of the BLR
annuli increases logarithmically.
The volumetric den-
sity spans from nH = 109 cm−3 to 106 cm−3, which is
consistent with a medium that connects the denser disk
with the lower density torus (e.g., Ferland et al. 1992;
Goad et al. 2012). In Fig. 4 the column density profile
of RXagn1 model is illustrated including the BLR. In
our simulations, the BLR is dust-free since it ends at the
sublimation zone. The gas is assumed to be neutral and
the molecular hydrogen fraction is set to 20%. We use
the lpgs metal abundance, and the metallicity is fixed
to unity. In Table 2 we summarize the properties of the
BLR.

4. SUMMARY

AGN often relies on phenomenological models or sim-
plified geometrical models. These models are frequently
limited in terms of flexibility and in the range of physical
processes they incorporate. In this work, we develop two
new models (RXToPo and RXagn1) using the ray-
tracing platform RefleX (Paltani & Ricci 2017). The
two models cover a wide range of energies, extending
from 0.3 to 300 keV and consider moderate energy reso-
lution (20 eV in the 0.3–10 keV range, and a logarithmic
binning of 0.01 above 10 keV). Both models, RXToPo
and RXagn1, include material that consists of neutral
gas and dust featuring all the major interactions between

the data being analyzed and/or for the physical scenario
they wish to adopt.

benchmark our models. We found that both models can

as illustrated in Fig. 7 and 8.
Our newly developed models have the potential to
constrain the geometry and properties of the circum-
nuclear material around SMBHs by considering very re-

can be used to take advantage of broadband X-ray spec-
troscopy and, in the near future, high-resolution X-ray
observations, such as those that will be carried out by
the newly launched XRISM observatory and future mis-
sions like Athena.

APPENDIX
A. THE ADDITION OF THE POLAR CONE COMPONENT
In this work, we add the hollow polar cone component perpendicular to the torus plane. The addition of such
a medium affects the reflection, and the emerging spectrum can vary from a simple toroidal configuration. Hence,
we make some comparisons between a simple torus spectrum and the RXToPo.
In both cases, we collect only
reprocessed photons (”RPRC” see Sec. 3.1) and we present their ratio in Fig. 9. The torus properties are fixed in all
cases (NH = 1024 cm−2 and covering factor 0.6). For the cone, we use three different column density options, and the
observing angle varies so that i) no material is intercepted (20◦), ii) the cone is intercepted (45◦) and iii) the torus
is intercepted (80◦). The results suggest that in low observing angles, where no material is in the line of sight, the
two spectra are similar, with a slight increase in the reflection due to the cone. In medium angles, there is stronger
absorption in low energies and a bump in mid energies (1 keV < energy < 10 keV) because of the enhanced reflection
in these energies. In high observing angles, the torus absorption dominates. This generates an increase in the lower
energies, which consists of photons that otherwise would have been lost and now are reflected towards the collector.
With this test, we illustrate that the polar dusty cone is indeed a useful component that can affect the observed
emerged spectrum.

B. LINE-OF-SIGHT COLUMN DENSITY
The best way to calculate the column density that the photons intercept along the line of sight is to use the REFLEXINO
tool. In this demo, we present how you can use this tool, based on the fit found in Sec. 3.2 for the RXToPo model.
We need to develop a parameter file (for example, my model.par) with the proper commands and parameters (for
more details, see the manual of RefleX5). Here is an example that can be used to calculate the line-of-sight column
density for the RXToPo best fit:
VERBOSE 0
NPHOTS 100000
LENGTH Centimeter
EMSPEC REFLEXINO
EMGEOM SPHERE 0.0 0.0 5.01534e12 3.0092e12 0.0 0.0 90
OBJECT WORLD 3e30
#OBJECT TORUS EXTERNAL
MATTER lpgs
DUST 1.0
TEMPERATURE 0
H2FRACTION 0.3
METALLICITY 1
DENSITY 2503429.94 #change it accordingly= nh*(2x INNER_RADIUS)
OBJECT TORUS TOR 0.0 0.0 0.0 9.1388e+17 6.0316e+17
#OBJECT HOLLOW CONE
MATTER lpgs
DUST 1.0
TEMPERATURE 0
H2FRACTION 0.3
METALLICITY 1
DENSITY 995.366 #for NH 1e23.19
OBJECT HCONE HCONE 0.0 0.0 0.0 3.1072e+17 1.2343e+20 3.4148e+17 1.3565+20 2.4015e+17 9.5396e+19
PERCENT ON
HISTOGRAM BINNING 20.0 25.5 0.001 LOG
HISTOGRAM NEW NH NHdeg79.txt
HISTOGRAM DIR_Z < 0.20791169081775945 #78deg
HISTOGRAM DIR_Z > 0.19080899537654492 #79deg
The output of this simulation is a text file, in this example NHdeg79.txt, which consists of three columns. The
first two columns are the left and right bins of the column density, and the third column gives the number of photons
that have been intercepted in that bin. Normally, the third column should be full of zeros except the bin in which
the line-of-sight column density is calculated. In some cases a few bins before and after can be populated with a few
photons, but this is not a problem. We consider the bin with the maximum number of photons to be the correct one.
In this case, the line-of-sight column density is Nlos
H = 2.89 × 1024 cm−2.
