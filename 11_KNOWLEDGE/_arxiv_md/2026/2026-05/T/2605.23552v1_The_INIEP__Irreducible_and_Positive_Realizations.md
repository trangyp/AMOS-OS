Abstract
Our focus is upon irreducible nonnegative n-by-n matrix realizations of nonnegatively realiz-
able spectra or, equivalently, characteristic polynomials. After giving some general background,
we make some useful new observations and show the existence of irreducible nonnegative real-
izations in some general cases. Then, we focus on n < 5, where the NIEP is solved. Finally,
we focus on the trace 0 case and, using graph theoretic methods, characterize nonnegative ir-
reducible realizability among realizable polynomials. The closely related problem of positive
realizations, for trace positive spectra, is also discussed.
AMS Classification: 15A29, 15A18, 05C20, 05C50, 15B48.
Keywords: Nonnegative inverse eigenvalue problem, Irreducible matrices, Positive matrices,
Strongly connected weighted digraphs.

Introduction
By an n-spectrum σ, we mean a list of n complex numbers, including possible allowed repeats. If
implicit, the n is omitted. We will think of these as sets (though repeats are allowed) and abuse
notation and terminology in natural ways, e.g., σ2 is the n-list of element-wise squares from σ.
In the Nonnegative Inverse Eigenvalue Problem (NIEP), σ is said to be realizable, R, if there is
an n-by-n entry-wise nonnegative matrix A, whose list of eigenvalues (with possible repeats) is σ.
There are well-known necessary conditions for realizability of σ:
Tr(σ), the sum of all elements of σ, must be nonnegative (trace condition)
σ must be self-conjugate (reality)
and
the largest absolute value of an element of σ, ρ(σ), the so-called Perron root, must occur in σ
(Perron condition).

There may be ties or repeats that may require additional structure in realizations. However,

Another approach to the NIEP is to view a nonnegative matrix as the adjacency matrix of a
weighted digraph and focus the attention on the coefficients of its characteristic polynomial P(x) =
xn + k1xn−1 + · · · + kn, see [21]. The polynomial P(x) is said to be realizable, R, if there is a
weighted digraph (equivalently an entry-wise nonnegative matrix) whose characteristic polynomial
is P(x).
The general NIEP is one of the most profound and difficult problems in all of mathematics. See
[6] for general background and [8] for a recent survey, as well as other references [13, 12, 19] for
further background. However, the difficulty, means that parts of, or variations upon the full NIEP,
can be very interesting or worthwhile, and there are many [16, 18, 3, 14].
Our interest here lay in identifying, among realizable spectra/polynomials, those that are re-
alizable by an irreducible matrix/a strongly connected weighted digraph. Recall that an n-by-n
matrix is irreducible if it is not similar by a permutation matrix to a nontrivially block triangular
matrix. A digraph is strongly connected if every two vertices are joined by a path. As R identifies
the realizable spectra/polynomials, we use IR to indicate the irreducibly realizable ones. Since the
IR spectra/polynomials are closely related to the spectra/polynomials realizable by an entry-wise
positive matrix, we denote the latter by R>0. Of course R>0 ⊆IR ⊆R, and each containment is
strict.
It is also well known [4], that if σ is the spectrum of an irreducible nonnegative matrix A, that
ρ(σ) has multiplicity 1 in σ and that ρ(σ) enjoys an entry-wise positive left and right eigenvector
associated with A. There could still be ties for the spectral radius ρ among other eigenvalues in σ.
The occurrence of such ties is significant, however. When none occur, we call σ spectrally simple
(SS, for short), i.e., ρ(σ) is the only eiegenvalue on the spectral circle. It will be convenient to use
some special notation: for t real we denote the spectrum, in which σ is modified only by adding t
to ρ(σ), by σt; t may be a positive or negative real number.
When σ is not SS (but enjoys the trace, reality and Perron conditions), we say that it has
Frobenius structure if there are h > 1 eigenvalues (in σ) on the circle of radius ρ(σ), and these are
evenly spaced about that circle. That means that they are multiples of the h-th roots of unity by
ρ(σ). Then any remaining nonzero eigenvalues must be inside this first circle. If we choose one
of the next largest absolute values say λ, with |λ| = a, there must exist h of them with absolute
value a equally spaced around the circle of radius a. If none of these is a, their conjugates must
also appear, by reality. Then continue looking inside the second circle for the next largest absolute
value, which may or may not also be a and continue as before. On each interior, nontrivial circle
there must be a self-conjugate set of eigenvalues of cardinality a multiple of h. Any zero eigenvalues
are “wild cards”, and there could be any number of them. The characteristic polynomial of σ with
ρ(σ) = ρ is of the form

After considerable thought and work, we have come to a general conjecture for the IR problem.
In some ways this seems natural, almost a converse to the classical structure, but far from trivial.
Conjecture: If σ (P(x)) is R and SS, then σ (P(x)) is IR.
We prove this conjecture for n-spectra with n < 4 and give considerable evidence for n = 4.

Background and Observations
A handy tool for adjusting an eigenvalue of a matrix, without putting it in any canonical form, was
observed by Brauer [1]. It is more general than we mention, but we give the case that we use and
that is most natural for nonnegative matrix analysis. Its use is often based on the orthogonality of
right and left eigenvectors associated with different eigenvalues, the “principle of biorthogonality”
[6].
Theorem 5. If A is a nonnegative matrix with spectrum σ, Perron root ρ and associated (nonneg-
ative) normalized right eigenvector x, and y is an arbitrary vector, then A + ϵ xyT has spectrum
{ρ + ϵ yT x} ∪(σ −{ρ}). If x is positive and y and ϵ are chosen positive, then A + ϵ xyT is positive
with Perron root ρ + ϵ yT x.
Corollary 6. If a nonnegative matrix A has a positive right (or left) eigenvector associated with its
Perron root, then there is a positive matrix arbitrarily close to A that has the same eigenvalues as
A, except that the Perron root has increased by an arbitrarily small amount.
It is well known [7] that an irreducible nonnegative matrix A of order n is similar to an irreducible
matrix B with constant row sums ρ(A): since A has a positive (right) eigenvector (x1, . . . , xn)
associated with its spectral radius, then B = D−1AD, where D = diag(x1, . . . , xn), is a nonnegative
matrix similar to A and with constant row sums ρ(A).

with constant row sums can be assured. In this case, the Frobenius Normal Form, arrived at by a

When Every Realization is Irreducible
The spectrum of a reducible matrix is the union of the spectra of its irreducible diagonal blocks.
If σ is R (realizable) and σ can be partitioned into two or more spectra, each of which is R, we
then call σ partitionable. Of course, no spectrum with a Perron root and all other eiegenvalues
either negative, or in complex conjugate pairs can be partitionable. Note that if σ is R and is
non-partitionable, then any realization of σ must be IR. This can happen in a variety of ways, but
the simplest is the following.
Theorem 10. If σ includes only one nonnegative member, then any realization of σ is irreducible,
i.e., R is equivalent to IR.

6
Proof. If σ were partitionable, then each part of the partition would have a Perron root. Since there
is only one possibility, σ cannot be partitionable.
(Of course, σ could be partitionable and still be IR.) This simple fact verifies IR in many
cases, especially in low dimensions. We suspect that “nonnegative” may be replaced by “positive”
in the theorem (discussion later), but this is subtle. There are spectra that are realizable only after
appending 0’s (see [7] for the first example). After appending the minimum number of 0’s, these
are also non-partitionable. We will see that if σ is IR, then appending any number of 0’s will result
in an IR spectrum, see Section 4.

In this section we study the IR spectra σ with trace zero using the characteristic polynomial rather

We say that a weighted digraph G = (V, E, w) with V = {1, . . . , n} is a simple EBL digraph if
w(i, i + 1) = 1, 1 ≤i ≤n −1, and the only possible cycles are formed by consecutive vertices and
include the first or the last vertex. i.e., they are 1, 2, . . . , r, 1 or n−(r−1), n−(r−2), . . . , n, n−(r−1)
for 1 ≤r ≤n. Thus, the weight of each cycle coincides with the weight of its closing arc: w(r, 1) =
ar1 for the initial r-cycles and w(n, n + 1 −r) = an,n+1−r for the final r-cycles. We denote these r-
cycles by Cr,1 and Cn,n+1−r, respectively. If r = 1 we have the initial and final loops (1, 1) and (n, n).
If r = n the initial and final n-cycles coincide in the basic n-cycle Cn,1. The adjacency matrices of
these digraphs are nonnegative lower Hessenberg matrices with ones in the superdiagonal and some
positive entries only in the first column and in the last row. Note that if these digraphs include the
basic n-cycle Cn,1 or two non-disjoint cycles Cp,1 and Cn,n+1−q, with p ≥n+1−q, then the digraph
is strongly connected, independently of the weights of the arcs. We distinguish in red these cycles
giving the strong connection. There can be other initial or final cycles in the digraph necessary to
obtain the corresponding characteristic polynomial, but irrelevant for the strong connectivity.
