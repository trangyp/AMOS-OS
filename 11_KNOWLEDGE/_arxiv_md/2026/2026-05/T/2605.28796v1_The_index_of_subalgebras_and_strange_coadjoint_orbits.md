THE INDEX OF SUBALGEBRAS AND STRANGE COADJOINT ORBITS
DMITRI I. PANYUSHEV
ABSTRACT. For an algebraic group Q with Lie Q = q, we develop a method for estimating
the index of a subalgebra h ⊂q via the use of coadjoint Q-orbits in q∗. Let qξ denote the
stabiliser of ξ ∈q∗. In the special case when qξ ⊕h = q, our estimate implies that ind h = 0.
Using our theory, we also answer a question of Duflo. An orbit Q·η ⊂q∗is said to be
strange, if qη ⊕h = q for some h. In the second part of the paper, we study strange orbits
for a semisimple algebra g. It is shown that an orbit O ⊂g ≃g∗is strange whenever the
complexity of O is at most 1. Furthermore, if S ⊂g is a sheet containing a strange nilpotent
orbit, then all orbits in S are strange. We also show that strange orbits in sln are not as
sparse, as one might expect, and discuss some conjectures on strange orbits.
1. INTRODUCTION
Let Q be an algebraic group with Lie Q = q. The index of q, denoted ind q, is an im-
portant numerical invariant related to the coadjoint representation of q. Let Bξ be the
Kirillov form associated with ξ ∈q∗. It is an alternate bilinear form on q that is defined
by Bξ(x, y) = ξ([x, y]). Let qξ be the kernel of Bξ. It is also the stabiliser of ξ w.r.t. the
coadjoint representation of q and ind q = minξ∈q∗dim qξ. The set of regular elements of q∗
is q∗
reg = {ξ | dim qξ = ind q}. If q is reductive, then ind q = rk q. But it is a non-trivial task
to compute the index of non-reductive Lie algebras; especially, because the behaviour of
index for a pair h ⊂q is unpredictable.
In Section 2, we provide a method for estimating the index of subalgebras of q using
coadjoint Q-orbits in q∗. Given ξ ∈q∗and a subalgebra h ⊂q, set a = codim(qξ + h)
and b = dim(qξ ∩h). Under natural mild assumptions, we prove that ind h ⩽a + b. In
particular, if a + b ⩽1, then ind h = a + b. The most interesting case occurs if a = b = 0,
i.e., qξ ⊕h = q. Then ind h = 0, dim h = dim Q·ξ and the group H has a dense orbit in Q·ξ.

Lie G = g and h = Lie H a subalgebra of g that does not contain proper ideals of g. Let
p : g ≃g∗→h∗be the natural projection. In Section 3, we prove that p(G·x) is dense
in h∗for any x ∈greg. Therefore, p(G·x) ∩h∗
reg ̸= ∅, which is the affirmative answer to

representation of G. In particular, we prove an auxiliary result, which is of interest in its

also prove that if a nilpotent orbit in a sheet of g is strange, then all orbits in this sheet are
strange (cf. Section 4).
The very name suggested by Ra¨ıs hints that “strange” orbits are rare and enigmatic.
But being truly mysterious, these orbits are not uncommon in sln. Recall that the nilpotent
orbits in sln are parametrised by the partitions of n. If λ is a partition, then O(λ) denotes
the corresponding orbit. In Sections 5 and 6, we provide several classes of strange orbits:
(1) if ˜e ∈sln is regular nilpotent, then the SLn-orbit of ˜ek is strange for any k ⩾1;
(2) if λ contains only two parts and O(λ) is strange, then either O(λ) = SLn·˜e2 or

(3) we point out three series of strange orbits such that λ has three parts. These are
O(m+2, m−1, m−1), O(m+3, m−1, m−1), and O(m+3, m, m−1).
An interesting feature of partitions given in (2),(3) is that the corresponding strange orbits
admit a parabolic complementary subalgebra. This also happens in many other cases, e.g.
O(3, 2, 1). These results show that all nilpotent orbits in sl5 are strange (hence all orbits
are strange), while O(5, 1) is the only non-strange nilpotent orbit in sl6. Having done a lot
of computations for sln (not all of them are presented in the text), I got an impression that
the presence of strange non-spherical orbits in sln is closely related to the fact that there
are many Frobenius parabolic subalgebras. On the other hand, if b is a Borel subalgebra
of a simple Lie algebra g and ind b = 0, then b is the only Frobenius parabolic subalgebra.
My conjecture is that in this case only spherical orbits are strange. We discuss this and
other related conjectures in Section 7.

Let Q be a connected algebraic group with q = Lie Q. For ξ ∈q∗, the Q-orbit of ξ with

q∗

q∗. By the Rosenlicht theorem [33, § 2.3], trdeg k(q∗)Q = dim q −maxξ∈q∗dim Q·ξ. Hence
one also has ind q = trdeg k(q∗)Q. Since the coadjoint orbits are even-dimensional, one has
dim q ≡ind q (mod 2).

Proof. (i) dim(qξ + h)⊥= dim q −dim(qξ + h) = dim q −dim qξ −dim h + dim(qξ ∩h).
(ii) We have dim p(q·ξ) = dim(q·ξ) −dim(q·ξ ∩h⊥). Since q·ξ ∩h⊥= (qξ + h)⊥, the assertion
follows from (i).

with b = 1.

4. STRANGE COADJOINT ORBITS
When I was visiting Universit´e de Poitiers at the beginning of 2000’s, M. Ra¨ıs told me
about a certain class of orbits in semisimple Lie algebras, which he suggested to call
“strange” (see below). His interest in these orbits was motivated by a connection with
the transverse Poisson structure of coadjoint orbits, see e.g. [7, 18]. Soon afterwards, I
explained to H. Sabourin (Poitiers) how to prove that all spherical G-orbits in a reductive
Lie algebra g are strange (via the use of my theory of doubled actions [22]). Then he took
on himself the task of publishing my proof in [27]. Below, I present some other results of
mine related to strange orbits.
Following Ra¨ıs, we give the following general definitions.
Definition 1. For an arbitrary Lie algebra q, the coadjoint Q-orbit O ⊂q∗is said to be
strange, if there is a subalgebra h ⊂q and ξ ∈O such that qξ ⊕h = q. Then we say that h
is a complementary subalgebra for qξ (or for O) and (O, h) is a strange pair.
For a strange pair, dim O = dim h and h is Frobenius by Corollary 2.4(iii). Hence h is
neither reductive nor nilpotent. In this case, H has a dense orbit in O and the action
(H : O) is locally free. It might be interesting to explore strange orbits for various classes
of Lie algebras; especially, in connection with Question 1.
In the rest of this section, we assume that Q = G is reductive, hence g∗≃g as G-
module. For any m ∈N, consider the locally closed subset g(m) = {x ∈g | dim G·x = m}.
The irreducible components of all these subsets are called sheets. The rank of a sheet S is
rk S = dim(¯S//G). For instance, if m = dim g −rk g, then g(m) = greg is the unique open
sheet in g and rk greg is the usual rank of g. Sheets have first been studied by Dixmier
for g = sln [8]. The basic general results are obtained in [2, 3]. In particular, each sheet

7. FURTHER PERSPECTIVES AND CONJECTURES
7.1. Towards a classification of strange orbits and Frobenius subalgebras. Results of
Sections 5 and 6 show that there are many strange non-spherical orbits in sln. More-
over, our computations demonstrate that, for sufficiently large nilpotent SLn-orbits, their
strangeness is often related to the fact that they possess complementary parabolic sub-
algebras. However, attempting to apply this idea to other simple Lie algebras, we face
a disappointing fact that if ind b = 0, then there are no other Frobenius parabolic subal-
gebras in g. This follows from the formula for the index of seaweeds in semisimple Lie
algebras, which is conjectured in [30, 4.7] and proved in [12, Sect. 8]. We call it the Tauvel–
Yu–Joseph (=TYJ) formula. By definition, s is a seaweed if there are parabolic subalgebras
p, ˜p ⊂g such that p+ ˜p = g and p∩˜p = s [23, Sect. 2]. The TYJ formula exploits the Kostant
cascade for Levi subalgebras of p and ˜p. If ˜p = g, then s = p, i.e., any parabolic subalgebra
is a seaweed. Let l be a Levi subalgebra of p. If ind b = 0 and K(l) is the Kostant cascade
for l, then the TYJ formula says that ind p = #K(l). Thus, if p ̸= b, then K(l) ̸= ∅and
ind p > 0. Hence non-solvable Frobenius parabolics may exist only if ind b > 0, i.e., for
Am (m⩾2), D2m+1 (m⩾2), E6.
For sln, the index of seaweeds can be computed via meander graphs of Dergachev–
Kirillov, which is quite easy if n is not too large. There is also an inductive formula that
always works for sln and sp2n [23]. For instance, a seaweed in sln (or gln) is determined
by two compositions of n, and the induction step reduces the problem to a seaweed in slm
with m < n, i.e., to a pair of compositions of m, see [23, Theorem 4.2].
Recall that ind b(sln) = ⌊(n−1)/2⌋, ind b(so4m+2) = 1, and ind b(E6) = 2. There are only
few standard Frobenius parabolics in D2m+1 and E6, and it is not hard to list all of them.
But we unable to detect any strange orbits in these two cases so far, not to mention strange
orbits with parabolic complements. For sln, the Frobenius parabolics are in abundance
and their explicit description is not known yet.

g, whereas degenerate Lie brackets correspond to the semi-direct products g(0) = ge ⋉h
and g(∞) = h ⋉ge, see [26, Section 3].

the Lie algebra gf. If the orbit O is strange, then this Poisson structure splits into the linear
and quadratic parts. This yields a pair of compatible Poisson brackets in S(gf). The study
of the corresponding Poisson-commutative subalgebras and integrable systems looks like
a rather promising enterprise.
