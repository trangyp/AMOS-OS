The omnidirectional trace in H1(Ω)
R. Eymard∗, T. Gallou¨et†, D. Maltese∗and L. Oger∗
May 19, 2026
Abstract
We ﬁrst prove that all the functions in L2 whose directional derivative is in L2 have a directional
trace on the boundary of any open bounded domain, without assumptions on its regularity. This
enables us to deﬁne the omnidirectional trace of the elements of the Sobolev space H1(Ω) for which
there exists a function on the boundary that is almost everywhere equal, with respect to the directional
measure, to the directional trace, regardless of the direction. The set of all these elements of H1(Ω),
denoted by H1
tr(Ω), is shown to be closed, and to always contain the closure in H1(Ω) of the set C0(Ω)∩
H1(Ω) (it is always equal to this set in the 1D case, and can be strictly greater in higher dimensions).
The omnidirectional trace always satisﬁes an integration-by-parts formula, which combines the values
of the trace on opposite points of the boundary. Examples show that this notion enables the resolution
of variational problems involving the values at the boundary of the domain.
Keywords: trace operator, Sobolev space, integration-by-parts, directional trace, integration for a family
of measures, Cantor sets.

Introduction
We consider, for an integer d ≥1, a bounded open domain Ω⊂Rd, denoting by ∂Ω= Ω\Ωits boundary.
For a given function u ∈H1(Ω) – the set of elements of L2(Ω) whose gradient in the distribution sense is
an element of L2(Ω)d – deﬁning a trace of u on the boundary of Ωis an important problem in the theory
of partial diﬀerential equations. Indeed, the elements of H1(Ω) may have no continuous representative
on Ωif d ≥2, and the use of a trace operator is indispensable for writing integration-by-parts formulas
that underpin the weak formulation of such equations.
The ﬁrst observation is that, if some part of the boundary of Ωis accessible by two sides (this is the
case of domains with cracks), deﬁning a single value for u ∈H1(Ω) on this part of the boundary is not
possible, unless u is continuous across it. This means that we cannot expect that, for a general open
bounded domain Ω⊂Rd, the domain of such a trace operator be the whole space H1(Ω). The goal is
therefore to deﬁne the trace operator on a subset of H1(Ω) that coincides with the whole space when
additional hypotheses are imposed on Ω.
From the trace on Lipschitz domains to the “approximative” trace.
Let us recall that the notion of trace is standardly introduced in the case of domains with Lipschitz
regularity. The existence of this trace is obtained through smooth local charts which transform the com-
putations at the boundary of the domain into computations on Rd−1 viewed as the half-space boundary
∂(R+×Rd−1) (see the seminal paper [13], and see [6, Theory of traces] for the introduction of local charts).
The trace of any function u ∈H1(Ω) can then be deﬁned as the limit, in L2(∂Ω, Hd−1), of the restric-
tions to ∂Ωof smooth functions converging to u in H1(Ω). Here Hd−1 denotes the (d −1)-dimensional
Hausdorﬀmeasure on Rd (see [11, Deﬁnition 2.1]).
For more general domains, this procedure was extended in [2, 3, 4, 20]. In these works, the set of the
so-called approximative traces of a given u ∈H1(Ω) is that of all ϕ ∈L2(∂Ω, Hd−1) such that there
exists a sequence (un)n≥0 in H1(Ω)∩C0(Ω) such that the sequence (un)n≥0 converges to u in H1(Ω) and

Let us observe that this deﬁnition opens a series of restrictions and questions. It requires the hypothesis
that Hd−1(∂Ω) < +∞, which excludes the case of domains with fractal boundaries. Denoting by eH1(Ω)

is discussed in [3] and is intensively studied in [20], considering functions on the boundary only locally
integrable for the measure Hd−1 (counterexamples for this uniqueness are given in [4, Example 4.3] or
[7, end of Section 3]). As noticed in these works, this uniqueness property is related to the fact that the
boundary of the domain may contain points that are not connected to Ω, and are therefore not supporting
the trace of the elements of H1(Ω). We can remark that similar observations are made in [21], in order
to deﬁne the trace of functions in the Sobolev space W 1,p(Ω) with p > d on the boundary of general
domains, introducing the notion of “α-accessible points”.
We also observe that the (d −1)-dimensional Hausdorﬀmeasure on Rd appears in the integration-by-
parts formula in the case of Lipschitz domains, and that generalisations can be obtained in the case
of some more general domains (see ﬁnite perimeter sets and Gauss-Green theorem [11, Section 5.8]).
Nevertheless, the loss of uniqueness of the approximative trace when passing to the limit of sequence of
continuous functions in the space L2(∂Ω, Hd−1) suggests that this measure becomes inappropriate for
deﬁning the topology on a set of functions on the boundary of more general domains, linked with an
integration-by-parts formula.
From the directional trace to the omnidirectional trace.
The approach we follow in this paper is entirely diﬀerent. We ﬁrst consider, for all directions θ ∈Sd−1,
where Sd−1 denotes the unit sphere in Rd, the space Wθ(Ω) (deﬁned in Section 2) of all functions of L2(Ω)
whose derivative in the direction θ, denoted by ∂θu, also belongs to L2(Ω). Then, for any open bounded
set Ω, any element u ∈Wθ(Ω) has a directional trace on ∂Ω, denoted in this paper γθu. The existence of
a boundary operator follows from the fact that the directional derivative is a skew-symmetric operator
(see Remark 2.11). The fact that it can be formulated as functions deﬁned a.e. for the measure µθ,
called the directional measure in the direction θ on the boundary (see Deﬁnition 2.1), is the extension to
Wθ(Ω) of results proved in [17, Theorems 1 and 2], or in [11, Theorem 2 p. 164]: any element of Wθ(Ω)
restricted to lines parallel to θ deﬁnes almost everywhere an element of H1(ω) for some open set ω ⊂R.
Then the directional trace γθu of u in the direction θ is an element of the space L2(∂Ω, µθ), where the
measure µθ is supported by the subset of ∂Ωcontaining the points which are accessible from Ωin the
direction θ (see Deﬁnition 2.1 and Appendix B for the properties of these measures, in particular the
link with the (d −1)-dimensional Lebesgue measure, denoted here by λd−1). We then remark that this
support is much smaller than ∂Ωin cases where this boundary is irregular (think about 1D domains whose
boundary is a Cantor set). It is worth noting that the following integration-by-parts formula always holds
for all elements u, v ∈Wθ(Ω):

The following inclusion relations
