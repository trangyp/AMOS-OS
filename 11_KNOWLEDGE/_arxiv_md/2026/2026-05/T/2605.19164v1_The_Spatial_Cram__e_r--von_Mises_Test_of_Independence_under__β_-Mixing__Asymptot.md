Abstract
We derive the asymptotic distribution of the spatial Cram´er–von Mises statistic
for testing bivariate independence in stationary random fields on R2 under poly-
nomial β-mixing dependence, and document the Python implementation that re-
produces all simulation results. The classical Cram´er–von Mises test assumes i.i.d.
observations; we extend it to spatially dependent data by combining three ingredi-
ents: (i) a Davydov-type covariance bound that yields integrability of the spatial
covariance kernel under the mixing condition θ > 2(2 + δ)/δ; (ii) a reformulation of
the inner-form test statistic as a degenerate U-statistic of order 2 with a product
kernel Q = G1 ⊗G2, following De Wet (1980); and (iii) an extension of Gregory’s
(1977) U-statistic limit theorem to β-mixing sequences via Yoshihara (1976). This
approach bypasses the functional central limit theorem entirely. The limit distri-
bution is a weighted sum of correlated χ2
1 variables whose eigenvalues factor as
products of marginal eigenvalues; in the small-bandwidth limit, the correlation van-
ishes and the limit reduces to a weighted sum of independent χ2
1 variables. Explicit
eigenvalue formulas are given for three common weight functions (uniform, optimal
normal, Anderson–Darling), producing computable critical values.
The software
generates Mat´ern random fields by circulant embedding, computes the test statis-
tic via the inner-form kernel decomposition, evaluates asymptotic critical values
by Monte Carlo on the limit distribution, and runs permutation-based alternatives.
Competing tests (Mantel, cross-K, distance covariance) are included for power com-
parisons. Simulation experiments show that the Anderson–Darling weight achieves
the best power across Gaussian and t-copula alternatives, while the Mantel and
cross-K tests have no power against cross-dependence in spatially correlated fields.
Keywords:
spatial statistics; goodness-of-fit; Cram´er–von Mises test; β-mixing; U-
statistics; random fields; simulation; Python

Introduction
The Cram´er–von Mises statistic measures the integrated squared difference between an
empirical and a hypothesised cumulative distribution function. For independent data the
classical theory is well understood: the limit distribution is a weighted sum of χ2
1 variables
whose weights are the eigenvalues of the covariance kernel [1, 9]. For spatially dependent
data—common in geostatistics, environmental monitoring, and spatial econometrics—the
dependence structure must be explicitly controlled.
This paper gives a derivation of the asymptotic distribution for a spatial Cram´er–von
Mises test of bivariate independence, together with a complete Python implementation
and simulation study. The null hypothesis H0 is that the marginal components Xs and Ys
of the bivariate random field Zs = (Xs, Ys) are independent, so that the joint CDF factors
as H(x, y) = F1(x)F2(y).
Under H0, the probability integral transform Us = F1(Xs)
and Vs = F2(Ys) are marginally uniform on [0, 1], reducing the spatial CvM statistic to
De Wet’s [8] U-statistic form (Section 11.4). We work in R2 with a stationary β-mixing
random field satisfying Assumption 11.6, a compactly supported kernel smoother, and a
sequence of spatial lattices whose mesh tends to zero. Our main result (Theorem 11.28)
states that under polynomial mixing decay, the centred and normalised test statistic
converges in distribution to a weighted sum of correlated χ2
1 variables whose eigenvalues
factor as products of marginal eigenvalues. In the small-bandwidth limit, the correlation
vanishes and the limit reduces to the classical i.i.d. form.
Two features distinguish our approach from previous work:
(i) U-statistic route. Rather than proving weak convergence in C([0, 1]) or ℓ∞(R)—
which requires tightness via Arzel`a–Ascoli and a functional central limit theorem for
mixing fields—we reformulate the spatial CvM statistic as a degenerate U-statistic of
order 2 [13, 17]. Gregory’s theorem [10] then yields the limit distribution from scalar
convergence alone, and Yoshihara’s [23] extension to β-mixing sequences handles the
spatial dependence. This bypasses the functional CLT entirely.
(ii) Eigenvalue factorization. The inner form of the CvM statistic (using empirical
marginals) yields a U-statistic kernel that factors as a product of marginal kernels:
Q = G1 ⊗G2. Consequently, the eigenvalues factor as products of marginal eigenval-
ues: γjk = λ(1)
j ·λ(2)
k
(De Wet [8]; Theorem 11.31). This reduces the two-dimensional
spectral problem to two one-dimensional problems. Explicit eigenvalue formulas for
three common weight functions (uniform, optimal normal, Anderson–Darling) give
computable critical values without numerical integration.
The accompanying Python implementation reproduces all simulation results and pro-
vides reusable components for researchers who wish to apply or extend the test. The soft-
ware covers: (i) computation of the spatial CvM test statistic via the inner-form kernel
decomposition; (ii) asymptotic critical values from the limit distribution; (iii) permutation-
based inference for finite-sample p-values; (iv) data generation under both the null hypoth-
esis (independent Mat´ern fields) and alternatives (Gaussian and t-copula dependence);
(v) competing tests (Mantel, cross-K, distance covariance) for power comparisons.
The paper is organised as follows. Sections 2–10 describe the software: package archi-
tecture, test statistic computation, asymptotic critical values, data generation, competing
tests, simulation experiments, design decisions, verification, and a practical usage guide.
Section 11 develops the full asymptotic distribution theory, including covariance bounds,

Competing Tests
Three competing tests of spatial independence are implemented for power comparisons.
6.1
Mantel test
The Mantel test [15] computes the Pearson correlation between the spatial distance matrix
DS = (∥si−sj∥) and the variable distance matrix DV = (∥vi−vj∥). Permutation inference
is used: V is permuted relative to U and the test statistic recomputed Bperm times. An
adaptive early-stopping rule exits after Bmin = 99 permutations if the running p-value
estimate is more than ϵ = 0.02 away from the nominal level α.
6.2
Cross-K function test
Following Ripley [16], the cross-K function K12(r) measures the expected number of type-
2 points within distance r of a type-1 point, adjusted for intensity. Points are classified

as type-1 or type-2 by whether Ui < median(U). The test statistic is maxr |K12(r) −πr2|.
Radius queries use scipy.spatial.KDTree for O(n log n) neighbour search.

the same adaptive early-stopping rule as the Mantel test.
6.4
Permutation test for CvM
The cvm permutation pvalue function computes a permutation-based p-value for the
CvM statistic. A key optimisation: the U-side kernel matrix ˆGU is precomputed once
and reused across all permutations. Only the V -side kernel ˆGV is recomputed for each
permuted V , reducing the per-permutation cost by approximately half. The p-value is
computed as (count + 1)/(Bperm + 1).

Design Decisions and Implementation Notes
8.1
Inner form vs. true-CDF form
The default use inner form=True computes the kernel using empirical marginals. Under
H0, the inner form and true-CDF form differ by Rn = oP(1) by the Glivenko–Cantelli
theorem (Proposition 11.16, Step 4).
However, only the inner form admits the exact
product-kernel factorisation ˆQ = ˆGU ⊗ˆGV that underpins the eigenvalue decomposition.
The true-CDF form is available via use inner form=False for comparison but should
not be used for inference.
8.2
Circulant embedding normalisation
After extracting the m × m subfield, the code normalises to exact variance σ2 via X ←
X · (σ/std(X)). This corrects for discretisation artifacts in the circulant embedding but
does not affect the correlation structure or the PIT transform (which depends only on the
marginal distribution).
8.3
Shared t-copula scaling
The t-copula draws a single S ∼χ2(νt)/νt shared across all spatial locations. This pre-
serves the Mat´ern spatial structure within the copula. An alternative implementation
drawing Si independently at each location would destroy the spatial dependence in the
copula.
8.4
Divergent centering constant for optimal normal weight
The centering constant µ = (P∞
j=1 1/j)2 diverges for the optimal normal weight. The
code handles this by computing µn = n−1 P

8.5
Permutation test optimisation
For cvm permutation pvalue, the U-side kernel ˆGU is precomputed and reused across
all permutations. Only the V -side kernel ˆGV needs recomputation for each permuted V ,
reducing the per-permutation cost by approximately half.

Adaptive early stopping
The Mantel and cross-K tests use adaptive early stopping: after Bmin = 99 permutations,
if the running p-value estimate is more than ϵ = 0.02 away from the nominal level α, the
permutation loop terminates early. This significantly reduces computation time in power
studies where most null cases are clearly non-significant.

Discussion
12.1
Relationship to the functional CLT approach
The U-statistic route taken in this paper yields the same limit distribution as the func-
tional CLT approach (Donsker-type theorem for the kernel-smoothed empirical process,
followed by the continuous mapping theorem). The advantage of the U-statistic route is
threefold:
(i) It requires only scalar convergence in distribution, not weak convergence in C([0, 1])
or ℓ∞(R) [2].
(ii) It avoids tightness arguments entirely—no Arzel`a–Ascoli, no bracketing entropy, no
Bernstein blocking.
(iii) It yields the eigenvalue factorization directly from the product structure of the
kernel, without requiring the Mercer decomposition on R.
The price is that the U-statistic approach does not, by itself, prove process-level con-
vergence. For applications requiring the full empirical process (e.g., confidence bands),
the functional CLT route remains necessary [19]. Appendix B provides complete proofs
of this alternative route.

12.2
Extensions
1. Higher dimensions.
The integrability condition generalises to Rd: the radial
integral converges for p > d, yielding θ > d(2 + δ)/δ as the mixing threshold. The
eigenvalue factorization extends to products of d marginal kernels.
2. Non-stationary fields. If the field has a slowly varying mean function µ(s), the
CvM statistic can be applied to the residuals Xs−ˆµ(s) provided the mean estimator
is sufficiently regular.
3. Other mixing conditions. Yoshihara’s theorem extends to ϕ-mixing with weaker
decay requirements. For long-range dependent settings where β-mixing fails, the
limit theory for U-statistics is different [5].
4. On the spatial extension and the small-bandwidth limit. The main theoret-
ical contribution of this paper is extending the CvM test from i.i.d. data to spatially
dependent β-mixing fields. However, the simulations use the inner-form statistic
without kernel smoothing (small-bandwidth limit h →0), which yields the same
limit distribution as the classical i.i.d. test. The spatial theory is therefore a robust-
ness result: it justifies using the i.i.d. critical values on spatially dependent data.
For fixed bandwidth h > 0, the limit distribution involves correlated chi-squared
variables (Remark 11.29) and requires a whitening transform or block bootstrap.
The reported power values are higher than those in Doukhan, Lang & Surgailis
[7]; this likely reflects methodological differences (inner-form vs. spatial-kernel form,
and the choice of PIT marginals).
12.3
Practical recommendations
1. Weight function choice. Despite its theoretical Bahadur efficiency, the optimal
normal weight suffers from a divergent centering constant that inflates critical values
and yields near-zero power at weak alternatives. We recommend the Anderson–
Darling weight as the default choice: it combines strong power across both Gaussian
and tail-dependent alternatives with reliable size calibration. The uniform weight
is a competitive alternative with slightly lower power but simpler critical values.
2. Bandwidth selection. The optimal bandwidth for d = 2 is h = O(n−1/6) [20]. As
shown in Table 8, the test size is robust to bandwidth choice and power is essentially
perfect for moderate to strong alternatives. Note that the current implementation
uses the inner-form statistic without spatial kernel smoothing (small-bandwidth
limit h →0; see Remark 11.12), so the bandwidth values in Table 8 refer to the
Mat´ern range parameter in data generation, not the test statistic’s kernel bandwidth.
When the spatial covariance Σ ̸= I (bandwidth h > 0), a block bootstrap [12] is
recommended for exact inference.
3. Comparison with spatial independence tests. The Mantel and Cross-K tests
conflate spatial autocorrelation with cross-dependence. Under β-mixing, they have
no power against cross-dependence alternatives (Table 9). The spatial CvM test
uses the probability integral transform to directly test for residual cross-dependence.
Distance covariance [18] provides a more natural comparison; the Anderson–Darling
CvM weight outperforms dCov at weak alternatives (Table 10).

B.1
Comparison of the two routes
The U-statistic route (Section 11.5) and the functional CLT route (this appendix) yield
the same limit distribution but differ in requirements and scope:
The U-statistic route is strictly weaker in its mixing requirement and avoids tightness
and Mercer entirely, making it the preferred route for establishing the limit distribution
of the test statistic. The functional CLT route provides process-level convergence, which
is needed for applications requiring simultaneous inference over thresholds.
