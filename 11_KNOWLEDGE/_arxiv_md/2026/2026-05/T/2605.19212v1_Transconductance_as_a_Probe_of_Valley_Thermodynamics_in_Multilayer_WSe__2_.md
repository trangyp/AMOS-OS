Transconductance as a Probe of Valley Thermodynamics in Multilayer WSe2
Katsunori Wakabayashi,1, 2 Souren Adhikary,1 and Tomoaki Kameda1
1Department of Nanotechnology for Sustainable Energy,
Kwansei Gakuin University, Sanda 669-1330, Japan
2National Institute for Materials Science (NIMS), Tsukuba 305-0044, Japan
(Dated: May 20, 2026)
Transconductance is a central figure of merit in field-effect transistors, typically governed by
charge accumulation and carrier mobility.
In multilayer WSe2 transistors, however, it is shown
to carry a nonlinear transport signature of inter-valley carrier redistribution between the K and Γ
valleys. This valley-crossover contribution suppresses transconductance in bilayer WSe2 and reverses
sign in trilayer, while remaining absent in single-valley systems. Unlike extrinsic mechanisms such
as trap-state filling or contact resistance, the anomaly leaves the subthreshold swing unchanged
and cannot be reproduced within conventional single-valley transport models.
Introducing the
valley susceptibility χv ≡∂fΓ/∂VGS, bounded by an intrinsic thermodynamic limit (4kBT)−1, we
quantify this response and show that it reaches ∼0.20 V−1 in bilayer WSe2 near threshold at room
temperature. The sign, magnitude, and temperature dependence of the anomaly provide directly
measurable fingerprints of valley thermodynamics, establishing transconductance as an electrical
probe of internal electronic degrees of freedom and revealing a previously hidden nonlinear response
in standard transistor measurements.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Transconductance gm = dID/dVGS is a central figure
of merit in field-effect transistors, quantifying the gate
response of channel current and governing amplifier gain
and switching speed. In conventional semiconductor de-
vices, gm is determined by charge accumulation and car-
rier mobility; its non-monotonic behavior is attributed
to extrinsic mechanisms such as trap-state filling [1–3],
interface-induced mobility degradation [4], or contact re-
sistance [5, 6].
The transconductance of multilayer WSe2 field-effect
transistors, however, carries a nonlinear transport signa-
ture of inter-valley carrier redistribution. When the K
and Γ valence-band maxima are separated by an energy
comparable to kBT, gate voltage not only accumulates
charge but also continuously redistributes holes between
valleys with markedly different effective masses [7, 8],
generating a valley-crossover contribution gm,v that sup-
presses gm in the bilayer, reverses sign in the trilayer,
and is absent in ideal single-valley systems.
Non-monotonic structure in gm is conventionally at-
tributed to the extrinsic mechanisms cited above, each of
which simultaneously degrades the subthreshold swing
SS. In contrast, the valley-crossover contribution identi-
fied here is qualitatively distinct: it leaves SS unchanged,
reverses sign between bilayer and trilayer, and cannot
be reproduced within conventional single-valley transport
models.
The physical origin is the gate-induced competition
between the light-hole K valley (m∗
K ≈0.40 m0) and
the heavy-hole Γ valley (m∗
Γ
≈
1.00 m0) in multi-
layer WSe2, whose relative population is controlled elec-
trically through gate-induced band alignment [9–14].
Shubnikov–de Haas spectroscopy on gated trilayer WSe2
has directly demonstrated this gate-controlled hole re-
distribution from Γ to K [15, 16] (Fig. 1); yet its direct
manifestation in the nonlinear transistor characteristics
has remained unexplored.
Previous
valleytronics
work
[17–22]
focused
on
symmetry-related K–K′ valleys of TMDCs [23–28], ex-
ploiting spin-valley locking [29, 30] and optical or mag-
netic control [31]. Extending the equilibrium two-valley
model of Ref. 7 to the energetically nondegenerate K–
Γ regime, we introduce the valley susceptibility χv ≡
∂fΓ/∂VGS and derive the decomposition gm = gm,0+gm,v
with gm,v = −∆µ (W/L)VDS qp χv, where ∆µ = µK −
µΓ > 0. The sign and magnitude of gm,v are governed
by the thermodynamic response of valley populations to
gate voltage.
These results establish transconductance as a direct
electrical probe of valley thermodynamics in multi-valley
semiconductors, providing experimentally accessible fin-
gerprints of internal electronic degrees of freedom within
standard transistor characterization.

V.
DISCUSSION
The transconductance decomposition [Eqs. (8)–(10)]
predicts three experimentally testable fingerprints: (i)
gm,v is absent in the monolayer but pronounced in the
bilayer and trilayer, with opposite signs; (ii) |gpeak
m,v | grows
as temperature decreases following (4kBT)−1 scaling;
(iii) the anomaly persists under interface-trap degra-
dation that suppresses SS, providing an unambiguous
valley-crossover criterion. For a W = L = 1 µm device
at 300 K, |gpeak
m,v | ≈1 µS, representing a 7% anomaly rel-
ative to gm,0, growing to ≈17% at 100 K. Lock-in mea-
surement of the numerically differentiated ID–VGS curve
provides sufficient sensitivity for detection.
The quasi-equilibrium treatment assumes that the in-
tervalley scattering time τiv is much shorter than the car-
rier transit time τtr = L/vd [60, 61]. When τiv ≫τtr,
the valley population is frozen at the source-contact
value and gm,v is suppressed; this provides an addi-
tional observable in the channel-length dependence of the
anomaly.
The framework extends beyond WSe2. The essential
requirement is that multiple valleys with distinct effective
masses are separated by ∆∼kBT. Analogous physics
is expected in other few-layer TMDCs [62–67], including

VI.
CONCLUSION
We have shown that gate-induced redistribution of
holes between the K and Γ valleys of WSe2 produces
a distinct contribution gm,v = −∆µ (W/L)VDS qp χv to
the transconductance.
This valley-crossover term sup-
presses gm in bilayer and enhances it in trilayer WSe2,
in both cases without affecting the subthreshold swing.
The valley susceptibility χv vanishes exactly in the sub-
threshold regime, peaks at threshold following the uni-
versal bound (4kBT)−1, and is suppressed by electro-
static screening by a factor of ∼50 in realistic GAA de-
vices. The condition ∆KΓ ≈kBT ln rDOS identifies bi-
layer WSe2 at room temperature as the optimal mate-
rial configuration. These results establish the transcon-
ductance as a direct probe of valley thermodynamics
in multi-valley two-dimensional transistors, providing an
electrical fingerprint of internal electronic degrees of free-
dom that is accessible through standard characterization
techniques.
