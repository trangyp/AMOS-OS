Abstract
When a graph G admits a vertex v that is contained in all its longest paths, we call v a Gallai vertex.
These are named after Gallai, who in 1966 asked the question if it is true that every connected graph
contains such a vertex. This was soon answered in the negative by Walther and Zamfirescu, who
presented a graph in which every vertex is omitted by some longest path of the graph.
In spite of its long history, the Gallai Vertex Problem, i.e. determining whether a graph has
a Gallai vertex, was until now neither known to be NP- nor co-NP-hard. In this work, we show
something much stronger, as we completely settle the computational complexity of determining
whether a graph has a Gallai vertex: we show that it is complete for the complexity class Θp
2 =
PNP[log n]. This class, also known as ‘parallel access to NP’, is a complexity class larger than NP
situated just below the class Σp
2 in Stockmeyer’s polynomial hierarchy.
In more generality, the longest path transversal number of a connected graph is the minimum
size of a set of vertices that intersects all its longest paths. I.e. if the graph has a Gallai vertex,
its longest path transversal number is 1. Thus, as a consequence of our theorem, the longest path
transversal number of a graph cannot be approximated in polynomial time by a factor better than 2,
unless P = NP. In fact, using related techniques, we show a strengthening of this result: For any
constant C, if there is a graph with longest path transversal number C, then there is no polynomial
time algorithm for approximating the longest path transversal number by a factor better than C,
unless P = NP. In particular, this excludes approximation by a factor below 3. Similar results hold
for the longest cycle transversal.

Introduction
It is a common exercise in an introductory graph theory course to show that every two
longest paths in a connected graph share a common vertex. An old question of Gallai [6] asks
a generic form of this, whether all longest paths in a connected graph share a common vertex.
Such a vertex is called a Gallai vertex. According to a counterexample due to Walther [37]
and Zamfirescu [39], there exists a graph (depicted in Figure 1) such that every vertex is
omitted by some longest path of the graph, providing a negative answer to Gallai’s question.
However, one could be modest and ask for a small subset S of vertices, instead of only one
vertex, such that every longest path in the graph has a vertex from the subset S. Such a

lct(G). Here, given a graph G, the question becomes to find a small set of vertices hitting
all the longest cycles of G. Very similar bounds as for lpt(G) were shown for lct(G) in
[28, 22, 18, 25]. Regarding the differences between longest path and longest cycle transversal,
we refer the reader to [33, 25].

Even though Gallai’s question is from 1966, little was known until now about the
computational complexity of Gallai Vertex. One possible explanation for this lack of
knowledge is that it seems very difficult to find graphs with lpt(G) > 1, and therefore seems
hard to reason about such graphs. Unlike many similar-looking computational problems,
Gallai Vertex was neither known to be NP-hard, nor was it easy to imagine any polynomial-
sized polynomial-time checkable certificate for the problem. In particular, any such certificate
would have to argue about all (potentially exponentially many) longest paths in the graph.
And indeed, as a consequence of our theorem, Gallai Vertex ̸∈NP unless common
complexity-theoretic assumptions fail.
Our contribution.
We are the first to show that Gallai Vertex is NP-hard. Moreover,
in this paper, we completely settle the complexity of the problem. We show that Gallai
Vertex is complete for the complexity class Θp
2 = PNP[log n]. This curious complexity class
was first studied by Papadimitriou and Zachos [27] and is defined as the class of problems that
can be solved with a polynomial time algorithm using O(log n) oracle calls to NP. There are
not many known Θp
2-complete problems, especially not outside of the research areas of logic
and computational social choice. Furthermore, a large portion of the known Θp
2-complete
problems are considered unnatural, a fact which has been acknowledged many times in the
literature [26, 12, 13, 29]. In this sense, another contribution in this paper is to present a
new and arguably very natural Θp
2-complete problem.
Inapproximability.
Our result implies that it is Θp
2-hard to distinguish between graphs with
lpt(G) = 1 and lpt(G) ≥2. Therefore, we obtain as an immediate consequence, that it is
Θp
2-hard (and therefore also NP-hard) to approximate lpt(G) to a factor better than 2.
We strengthen this result to the following: For any constant C, if there is a graph H
with lpt(H) = C, then it is Θp
2-hard to distinguish whether a given graph G has lpt(G) = 1
and lpt(G) ≥C. In particular, since there exists a graph of lpt(H) = 3 shown in [8, 39], this
shows polynomial time inapproximability by a factor of 3 −ε for any ε > 0.
If it should turn out that lpt(G) can take arbitrarily large values, then our paper in this
case proves that there is no constant-factor approximation algorithm. Our inapproximability
result holds even when restricted to planar graphs.
Longest cycle.
Finally, we show using similar ideas that it is Θp
2-hard to approximate the
longest cycle transversal. Furthermore, it is Θp
2-complete to decide if there exists a single
vertex that hits all longest cycles. This holds even in the 2-connected planar case.

The class Θp
2.
Papadimitriou and Zachos [27] introduced the class Θp
2 = PNP[log n] as the
class of problems that can be computed in polynomial time using O(log n) many oracle calls
to NP. The class Θp
2 falls in between the first two levels of Stockmeyer’s polynomial hierarchy
[34]: We have NP ⊆PNP[log n] = Θp
2 ⊆PNP ⊆NPNP. Complexity theorists have the strong
belief that all these inclusions are strict (the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse), but
the true status is unknown. Wagner [36] introduced the name Θp
2 and showed that the class
can be characterized in many equivalent ways. Kadin [16] has proven that if NP has a sparse
Turing-complete set then the polynomial hierarchy collapses to Θp
2.
There are not many known natural Θp
2-complete problems. Prominently, in the area of
computational social choice, the class Θp
2 plays a role. In 1876, Charles Lutwidge Dodgson,
nowadays better known under his pen name Lewis Caroll, proposed a voting rule for which
it may be computationally nontrivial to determine who is the winner of the election [4].
Hemaspaandra, Hemaspaandra, and Rothe [12] proved that determining the Winner under
Dodgon’s rule is Θp
2-complete. Similar results were soon after obtained for Young’s rule and
Kemeny’s rule [30, 13]. Further natural Θp
2-complete results are known in AI and logic [5].
We also mention [29, 24].
Hemachandra [11], and independently Köbler, Schöning, and Wagner [19] proved that
the class Θp
2 can be equivalently characterized as Θp
2 = PNP[log n] = PNP
∥. Here PNP
∥
denotes
the class of all problems that can be solved with so-called parallel access to an NP-oracle
(also sometimes called non-adaptive access). Formally, a decision problem Π is contained in
PNP
∥
if there exists a polynomial-time Turing machine, which, upon receiving an instance IΠ
of problem Π creates a table of polynomially many different membership queries to some
NP-complete problem. The Turing machine has to first write the entire table. It will then
receive the answers of all the queries at once (i.e. in parallel). It cannot make any additional
queries and has to make its final decision about instance IΠ based on the answers in the truth
table. As stated before, it is known that O(log n) many adaptive NP-queries is equivalent to
poly(n) many non-adaptive NP-queries.

Wagner’s technique can be explained as follows. Consider the problem SAT parity.
SAT Parity
Input: An even-length sequence (φ1, . . . , φ2n) of 3SAT formulas. We have the
promise that there exists an index s ∈{0, . . . , 2n} such that φ1, . . . , φs are satisfiable,
and φs+1, . . . , φ2n are not satisfiable.
Question: Is the index s an odd number?
We call the index s the split index. Wagner proved: For some decision problem Π (here,
Π = Gallai Vertex), if there is a polynomial-time computable function f that maps
arbitrary instances x of SAT Parity to instances f(x) of Π such that x is a yes-instance if
and only if f(x) is a yes-instance, then Π is Θp
2-hard. Informally, this can be understood as a
reduction from SAT Parity to Π. However, the reason this is not a reduction in the usual
sense is that instances of the problem SAT Parity contain a promise, but it cannot be
tested in polynomial time (unless P=NP) whether this promise holds. Hence SAT Parity
is not a computational decision problem in the usual sense. (See [35, Thm. 5.2], or [29] for a
formally very precise statement.)
In the context of our paper, this means that we need to start with an instance (φ1, . . . , φ2n)
of SAT parity, and transform it into a graph G, such G has a Gallai vertex if the split index
s is odd, and G does not have a Gallai vertex if the split index is even. How can we achieve
such a feat? The main idea is to introduce so-called odd/even-gadgets and show that they
behave differently depending on whether the split index s is even or odd. We remark that the
value of λ(Godd), λ(Geven) depends on s, but the truth of the equality ‘λ(Godd) = λ(Geven)?’
depends only on the parity of s.

Strong Inapproximability
In this section, we consider approximation algorithms to the longest path transversal problem.
We assume that such an approximation algorithm runs in polynomial time, and upon receiving
a graph G outputs some number A(G) ≥lpt(G). Its approximation ratio is the worst-case
value of the ratio A(G)/lpt(G) over all graphs G. We show that the longest path transversal
number is strongly inapproximable. We use the term strongly inapproximable, to denote the
following behavior.
▶Theorem 9. For all constants C ≥2, if there exists some graph H with lpt(H) = C,
then the computational problem to distinguish whether a given graph G has lpt(G) ≥C or
lpt(G) = 1 is Θp
2-hard (and in particular NP-hard).
Since Zamfirescu [39] found a graph G with lpt(G) = 3, this implies that lpt(G) cannot
be approximated better than a factor of 3. The theorem furthermore implies the following:
Should the answer to Question 1 be true, and there exists a (smallest) universal constant
U such that lpt(G) ≤U for all graphs, then the algorithm that always outputs A(G) = U
trivially has an approximation ratio of U. On the other hand, by our theorem this bound is
tight. Should the answer to Question 1 be false, then our theorem implies that there cannot
exist a constant-factor approximation algorithm (unless P = NP).
We are now ready to prove our theorem. We first give a short overview of the idea of the
proof: The main idea is somewhat analogous to our Θp
2-hardness proof of Lemma 8. Observe
that there, we took the Walther-Zamfirescu graph and attached gadgets to the three vertices
L, R, T. These three vertices are exactly the vertices of the Walther-Zamfirescu graph that
are endpoints of some longest path. Exactly one of the gadgets was a copy of Godd, and all
the remaining gadgets (in this case, two,) were copies of Geven. The new proof works in the
same basic way, except that we now swap the Walther-Zamfirescu graph for the graph H.
We do not have any information about the structure of H, except that lpt(H) = C, yet, this
suffices for our argument. We attach a copy of Geven to every vertex, except to one vertex,
which must be the endpoint of some longest path, where we attach a copy of Godd. This
way, we obtain a modified graph H′ from H. We show that for this new graph H′ it is hard
to distinguish lpt(H′) ≥C and lpt(H′) = 1.

Maximum Clique and Other Transversal Problems
In this section, we quickly discuss a general observation. We showed in the previous sections
that it is Θp

path, or the longest cycle in a graph. It is natural to ask whether an analogous result
holds for many other settings where a transversal of size 1 is sought after. In this section,
we answer the question positively for the maximum clique problem (and analogously the
maximum independent set problem). We conjecture that very similar results hold as well
for many other problems. In the following, we use the term maximum clique to denote any
clique that is cardinality-maximal among all cliques of a given graph. Consider the problem
