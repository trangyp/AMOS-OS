The fragility of reputation effects
Allen Vong∗
May 19, 2026
Abstract
I revisit the canonical reputation framework in which a long-lived player interacts
with a sequence of short-lived opponents and may be either strategic or a commitment
type who always plays the same, possibly mixed, action. I depart by allowing short-lived
players to be uncertain not only about the long-lived player’s type, but also about the
signal structure. I show that even vanishingly small misspecified skepticism of short-lived
players about commitment as an explanation of the observed signals can completely
eliminate reputation effects: a patient strategic long-lived player’s equilibrium payoff is
bounded above by the canonical complete-information benchmark.
Keywords: reputation effects, repeated games, misspecification.

Introduction
A central insight of the reputation literature, pioneered by Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts, and
Wilson (1982), Kreps and Wilson (1982), and Milgrom and Roberts (1982), is that even
a small amount of incomplete information about a player’s type can have large effects on
equilibrium outcomes in repeated games. Fudenberg and Levine (1989, 1992) cast this insight
in what is now a canonical framework, in which a long-lived player interacts with a sequence
of short-lived opponents. The long-lived player is either a commitment type, who plays a
fixed distribution of actions, or a normal type, who acts strategically. When sufficiently
patient, in every equilibrium, the normal type can secure a payoff arbitrarily close to that of
the commitment type. This payoff can strictly exceed any equilibrium payoff attainable under
complete information, even if the prior probability of the commitment type is arbitrarily
small. This is the celebrated “reputation effect.”
In the canonical framework, reputation effects arise from the short-lived players interpreting
public signals about the long-lived player’s behavior through the correct signal structure.
A growing body of evidence, however, suggests that in practice, agents often rationalize
observations by revising their subjective models of the signal-generating process; see, e.g., Frick,
Iijima, and Ishii (2020) and references therein. In doing so, they may misattribute informative
signals to unobserved shocks or structural features rather than to commitment. For example,
a central bank that consistently delivers low inflation may be viewed as benefiting from
favorable economic conditions rather than adhering to a strict rule; a firm that repeatedly
sets high prices may be seen as facing strong demand rather than committing to toughness;
and a worker who consistently performs well may be thought to face easier tasks rather than
exerting high effort.
Of course, economic models are approximations rather than literal descriptions. The
above examples nonetheless point to a common identification problem. When observers are
uncertain about how actions generate outcomes, even if they do not deny the possibility of
commitment, they may regard the observed outcomes as slightly better explained by narratives

such as favorable circumstances, measurement error, and omitted state variables other than
commitment. The canonical reputation argument selects the commitment narrative. This
raises a natural robustness question: do reputation effects remain a good approximation
when short-lived players’ worldviews are misspecified, even negligibly, in ways that support
narratives other than commitment?
In this paper I address this question. I extend the canonical framework by allowing
short-lived players to be uncertain about not only about the long-lived player’s type but
also about the signal structure, and also allowing their beliefs about the signal structure to
be misspecified in that they rule out the true signal structure. Monitoring of the long-lived
player’s actions is imperfect, so such misspecification affects how short-lived players interpret
the signals they observe.
My main result shows that reputation effects are not robust to misspecification. Consider
any sequence of frameworks converging to the canonical reputation framework, along which
short-lived players assign a small but positive probability to misspecified signal structures in
which the normal-type explanation fits the observed signals better than the commitment-type
explanation. I show that in the limit of every such sequence, the highest equilibrium payoff of
a patient normal-type long-lived player is no greater than his highest Nash equilibrium payoff
in the canonical complete-information game, where his type is commonly known. Thus, even
vanishingly small misspecification about the signal structure can fully eliminate, in every
equilibrium, the patient long-lived player’s payoff gain from reputation effects.
This limiting exercise holds fixed each misspecified framework while taking the patient-
player limit, and then lets the framework converge to the canonical one. This order of limits
mirrors the canonical reputation result: reputation effects are predictions about patient
long-lived players. My main result asks whether those patient-limit predictions are stable
under arbitrarily small perturbations of short-lived players’ subjective models.
The misspecified skepticism about commitment driving my main result is tight. I show
that reputation effects persist when short-lived players’ subjective models attribute their
signal observations to some commitment-type explanation, even if that explanation is mis-

specified. The correctly specified setting, including the canonical one, is a special case where
reputation effects emerge. Taken together, my results show that reputation effects are fragile:
although some misspecifications preserve them, arbitrarily small misspecifications that make
commitment a relatively worse explanation of the observed signals can eliminate them entirely.
The canonical reputation model has been hugely influential, guiding both theoretical
extensions and applications in various fields of economics; see Mailath and Samuelson (2006,
2015) for an overview. My results highlight a cautionary point about the interpretation of
reputation effects: such effects implicitly restrict how agents parse evidence. In particular,
when observations can be rationalized through not only the commitment narrative but also
others, reputation arguments become fragile. They require two forms of credibility: in
addition to short-lived players assigning positive prior probability to a commitment type,
as is now well understood, their subjective models must assign sufficient likelihood to the
observed signals under some commitment-type explanation.
Related literature.
Jehiel and Samuelson (2012) are the first to introduce misspecification
into the canonical reputation model. In their model, misspecification arises because short-lived
players interpret signals through fixed analogy-based models; reputation effects persist in
equilibrium. In contrast, in my model, short-lived players update their beliefs over both types
and signal structures in response to observed signals, and misspecification takes the form
of assigning zero probability to the correct signal structure. My main result concerns the
collapse of reputation effects.
Ely (2026) studies a repeated voting game with reputation concerns and misspecification.
The model departs from the canonical reputation setting: all agents are long-lived and there
is no commitment type. Misspecification arises because one player holds a fixed, incorrect
belief of her signal structure. Reputation concerns stem from other players’ beliefs about her
signal structure.
There is a large literature on misspecification that focuses on individual and social
learning; see Bohren and Hauser (2025) for a survey. An important theme in this literature is
3


## Page 5


robustness to misspecification. In individual-learning environments, Berk (1966) establishes
such robustness. In social-learning environments, by contrast, such robustness is present in
some settings (e.g., Bohren and Hauser, 2021) but not in others (e.g., Frick et al., 2020).
In correctly specified settings, recent work in the reputation literature extends the canonical
framework to settings in which a meaningful reputational payoff bound is harder to maintain
than in the canonical framework. Pei (2020) studies a setting in which, in addition to being
privately informed about his type, the long-lived player is privately informed about a state
that is payoff-relevant to the short-lived players, facing a tension between reputation building
and signaling about the state. Luo and Wolitzky (2025) study a setting in which the long-lived
player observes private signals before taking actions that are observable to short-lived players;
there, the long-lived player’s strategy is not identified by the observed marginal distribution
over his actions. My results show that reputation effects are fragile to misspecification in the
canonical framework, namely even when these additional difficulties for reputation building
are absent.
The correctly specified version of my framework generalizes the canonical reputation
model by allowing short-lived players to be uncertain about the signal structure, as in Deb
and Ishii (2025). My result that reputation effects persist in this setting contrasts with
their result that reputation effects are undermined. The distinction is that, in their model,
the same public signal may have different behavioral interpretations: it can indicate good
behavior toward the short-lived players in one state, but bad behavior in another. Thus, even
when short-lived players correctly predict the distribution of signals, they need not respond
favorably to those signals. This identification problem does not arise in my model.

exercise first evaluates the payoff implications of each nearby framework for a patient long-

the order of limits would answer a different question: if the framework is forced to become

canonical model.

7
Concluding remarks
My results have highlighted a caution in applying reputation arguments: they implicitly
discipline not only which long-lived player types short-lived players regard as possible, but
also how short-lived players interpret their signal observations. Reputation arguments require
these observations to be interpreted as evidence of commitment. Yet the short-lived players
may incorrectly attribute these observations to factors other than commitment, such as
favorable states, shocks, or measurement error. This misspecification can be destructive: even
a vanishingly small component of observers’ worldviews that incorrectly explains outcomes
without invoking commitment can fully mute the celebrated reputation effects.
