oscillating grids

Turbophoresis in inhomogeneous turbulent flows leads to the formation of large-scale nonuniform
particle number density distributions of inertial particles. This effect is associated with an effective
drift velocity directed toward regions of lower turbulence intensity and proportional to the particle
Stokes time and the spatial gradient of the turbulence intensity. In the present study, turbophoretic
transport is experimentally investigated in air flows generated by one-grid and two-grid oscillating
turbulence systems. The flow velocity field and particle spatial distribution are measured using
Particle Image Velocimetry. To isolate the effect of particle accumulation due to turbophoresis from
that associated with mean fluid flow, the measured particle number density of inertial particles is
normalized by the corresponding distribution obtained for noninertial tracer particles under identical
flow conditions. The measurements show preferential accumulation of inertial particles in regions of
lower turbulence intensity, consistent with the expected behavior of turbophoretic transport.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Transport of particles in turbulent flows has been stud-
ied extensively through theoretical analysis, laboratory
and field experiments, and numerical simulations; see,
e.g., Refs. [1–14].
Nevertheless, several important as-
pects of particle transport in turbulence remain unre-
solved, especially in geophysical and astrophysical flows,
where the governing parameters are difficult to reproduce
experimentally or numerically.
A central problem in turbulent transport of iner-
tial particles is the formation of large-scale inhomoge-
neous particle number density distributions at scales ex-
ceeding the integral turbulence scale. Two turbulence-
related mechanisms can lead to such particle accumula-
tion. The first mechanism is turbulent thermal diffusion
in temperature-stratified turbulence, which causes iner-
tial and noninertial particles to accumulate near regions
of minimum mean fluid temperature [15, 16]. The sec-
ond mechanism is turbophoresis, where inertial particles
migrate toward regions of lower turbulence intensity in
spatially inhomogeneous turbulent flows [17, 18].
Turbophoresis is due to the joint effect of particle iner-
tia and spatial inhomogeneity of turbulence and is com-
monly described in terms of an effective turbophoretic
velocity of inertial particles [17, 18]

laboratory configuration with relatively weak mean flow

turbophoresis. Simultaneous measurements of turbulent
velocity fields and particle number density distributions

of inertial particles and the turbulence intensity. To iso-
late the turbophoretic contribution, the measured parti-

noninertial particles under identical flow conditions.

III.
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND
MEASUREMENT TECHNIQUE
Experiments on turbophoresis of inertial particles were
performed in isothermal inhomogeneous turbulence gen-
erated by one and two oscillating grids in air flows.
The experiments were conducted in a rectangular trans-
parent chamber with dimensions Lx × Ly × Lz, where
Lx = Lz = 26 cm and Ly = 53 cm. The Z axis is verti-
cal, while the Y axis is perpendicular to the grid plane.
The oscillating grids, consisting of square arrays of
bars, are positioned parallel to the side walls of the cham-
ber in the XZ plane at a distance of 10 cm from the
chamber side walls. The grids oscillate along the Y di-
rection (Fig. 1). The oscillation frequency and amplitude

Rectangular pins of size 3 × 3 × 15 mm are mounted
on the upper and lower walls of the chamber (Fig. 1) to
reduce the mean flow velocity in the core region and to
enhance the spatial inhomogeneity of the mean flow.
The velocity field is measured using a Particle Image
Velocimetry (PIV) system [54–56] consisting of a Nd-
YAG laser (Continuum Surelite, 2×170 mJ) and a 12-bit
CCD camera with a spatial resolution of 1376×1040 pix-
els.
Incense smoke with solid spherical particles is used as
a tracer for the PIV measurements. The particles have
a mean diameter of 0.7 µm and a material density ρp ≈
103ρ, where ρ is the mean fluid density. The particles
are generated by high-temperature sublimation of solid
incense grains (see Ref. [42] for details).
The velocity fields are measured in a flow domain of
209.1 × 155.4 mm2 using images with a resolution of
1376 × 1024 pixels, corresponding to a spatial resolution
of 151 µm/pixel. The velocity field in the probed region
is analyzed using interrogation windows of 16×16 pixels.
Using the velocity measurements, we determine var-
ious mean-flow (Figs. 2–3) and turbulent (Figs. 4–11)
characteristics; see Sec. IV for details.
In particular,
the mean velocity field, mean velocity shear, root-mean-
square (r.m.s.) velocities, turbulent anisotropy parame-
ter, two-point velocity correlation functions and integral
turbulence length scales are obtained.
The mean and
r.m.s. velocity fields are determined at each spatial loca-
tion by averaging over 530 independent velocity maps.
To assess the statistical convergence of the velocity
measurements, we compare the first and last 20 velocity
maps in the recorded data set. The observed variability
is found to be small, indicating that 530 velocity maps
provide sufficient data for reliable statistical estimates.
Similar results are obtained when the number of velocity
maps is increased. In addition, the size of the probed
region does not affect the obtained results.

the first approach, the normalized two-point correlation

separation distance r up to the point where the correla-

particular, two inlets are located on the upper wall and
two on the lower wall of the chamber.
The spatial distributions of particles with diameters of
0.7 µm and 10 µm are obtained in separate experiments
using Mie light scattering measurements [57]. The mean
scattered-light intensity is determined using interroga-
tion windows of 32 × 32 pixels with 50% overlap. For
turbulence generated by one oscillating grid, the mea-
surements are performed on a grid of 85×64 interrogation
windows, while for turbulence generated by two oscillat-
ing grids the measurements are performed on a grid of
65 × 51 interrogation windows. This procedure enables
determination of the vertical distributions of scattered-
light intensity in vertical strips composed of interrogation
windows.
The scattered-light intensity produced by small parti-
cles is determined by Mie scattering [58]. The scattered
radiation energy flux Es depends on the particle diameter
dp, the wavelength λ, and the refractive index through
the scattering function Ψ(πdp/λ; a0, n).
For λ >∼πdp,
the scattering function follows the Rayleigh scattering
regime and depends on particle diameter, whereas for
λ <∼πdp the scattering function becomes weakly depen-
dent on both particle diameter and wavelength.
The
scattered radiation energy flux Es is proportional to the
local particle number density n. Therefore, the scattered-
light intensity recorded by the CCD camera can be used
to determine the spatial distributions of particle number
density.
The ratios of scattered-light intensities obtained at the
same spatial location in different measurements directly
represent the corresponding ratios of particle number
densities. To isolate turbophoretic particle accumulation
from other possible transport mechanisms, the scattered-
light intensity Ein measured for inertial particles is nor-
malized pointwise by the scattered-light intensity Enin
obtained in separate experiments with noninertial par-
ticles under identical flow conditions.
This normaliza-
tion enables characterization of the spatial distribution
of particle number density associated with turbophoretic
transport.

locity field is measured using the PIV system, and its
quality is evaluated using the standard Q-factor crite-
rion, defined as the ratio between the primary and sec-
ondary peaks of the cross-correlation function [54–56].

V.
CONCLUSIONS
In the present study, we investigate turbophoretic
transport of inertial particles in inhomogeneous non-
stratified turbulence generated by one and two oscillating
grids in airflow. Turbophoresis is caused by the combined
effect of particle inertia and turbulence inhomogeneity.
The competition between the effective turbophoretic ve-
locity and turbulent diffusion leads to the formation of
large-scale concentrations of inertial particles in regions
of minimum turbulence intensity.
The velocity field and spatial distributions of inertial
particles are measured using Particle Image Velocimetry.
To isolate the effect of turbophoresis,
the particle
number density measured for inertial particles is nor-
malized pointwise by the corresponding distribution
obtained for noninertial particles under identical flow
conditions. The experiments demonstrate that inertial
particles preferentially accumulate in regions of reduced
turbulence intensity, in agreement with the theoretical
estimate given by Eq. (17). The present results provide
experimental evidence of large-scale turbophoretic parti-
cle accumulation in strongly inhomogeneous turbulence
and may be relevant for geophysical and astrophysical
turbulent flows.
