I. INTRODUCTION
The Phillips curve, in its modern New Keynesian formula-
tion, links inflation to expected inflation, the output gap, and
marginal cost shocks [1]–[3], [10]. As AI services become
embedded in consumer-facing pricing engines, a new class
of marginal cost shocks arises: the inference cost of LLMs,
which is driven by GPU prices, electricity, and model scaling
laws [18]–[20], [30], [31]. At the same time, algorithmic
pricing by competing AI agents has been shown to sustain
supracompetitive prices even without explicit communication
[14]–[17].
This paper integrates these two strands into a uni-
fied macroeconomic framework. Our central object is the
Inference-Cost Phillips Curve (ICPC), a closed-form Phillips
relation in which the inference pass-through coefficient κinf
is an explicit function of the cross-sectional AI intensity
distribution and Calvo stickiness, and in which algorithmic
pricing penetration of intensity ϕρ enters as an attenuation of
the demand slope and an amplification of the inference pass-
through.

II. RELATED WORK AND EMPIRICAL BACKGROUND
The energy and economic costs of training and deploying
deep learning models are documented in [20], [21], [30], [31].
Scaling laws [18], [19] provide a structural basis for relating
model performance to compute expenditure. The industrial
organization of algorithmic pricing is established in [14]–[17].
The New Keynesian Phillips curve and its macroeconomic
foundations are developed in [1]–[8]. Welfare-theoretic policy
analysis and Taylor rules build on [9]–[11], and mechanism
design on [25]. Mean-field games and the propagation-of-
chaos approach are due to [24]. GMM identification and
inference rest on [12], [13], with attention to information
rigidity in inflation expectations [36] and to standard inflation
forecasting benchmarks [37].
U.S. data inputs are drawn from the Bureau of Economic
Analysis National Income and Product Accounts [26], the Fed-
eral Reserve Economic Data [27], the Congressional Budget
Office output gap series [28], and the International Energy
Agency electricity price reports [29]. Compute-cost inputs are
constructed from the Stanford AI Index 2025 [30] and the
Epoch AI compute database [31], both accessed on 2026-05-
18.

VIII. DISCUSSION AND POLICY IMPLICATIONS
The results suggest that as AI intensity diffuses across the
economy, central banks will face a Phillips curve whose slope
is increasingly shaped by infrastructure-side factors that are
partially endogenous to monetary policy through their effect
on capital costs [22], [23], [38]. The impossibility result
(Theorem 5) rationalizes a move toward mechanism-design-
free instruments such as a flat compute-price index in the
Taylor rule (Corollaries 1 and 2). Welfare gains from including
such an index are increasing in ¯λ and ϕρ under (6) and (12).
