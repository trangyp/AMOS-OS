TAMING WILD KNOTS WITH MOSAICS
MARY Y. DENG, ALLISON K. HENRICH, SEAN H. KAWANO, AND ANDREW R. TAWFEEK
Abstract. Wild knots—knots with inﬁnite knotting behavior—have resisted tradi-
tional methods of knot classiﬁcation, making them more of a curiosity in topology than
a subject of sustained investigation. In this paper, we present a new way to investigate
these objects. We extend Lomonaco and Kauﬀman’s knot mosaic theory to represent a
substantial subclass of wild knots that have isolated wild points. Our mosaics consist
of inﬁnite rooted trees with mosaics assigned to vertices and embedding functions gov-
erning connections. In developing this framework, we also introduce a notion of mosaic
tangles as well as mosaic rigid vertex spatial graphs of which mosaic singular knots are
a special case.

1. Introduction
Wild knots—knots with inﬁnite knotting behavior—are a curiosity in knot theory. The
vast majority of knot theorists are concerned with studying (tame) knots, i.e., those
knots that can be represented as ﬁnite closed polygonal chains. In this setting, knot
equivalence is described in terms of ﬁnite sequences of Reidemeister moves. (See e.g.,
[Ada04] or [Ada+20] for more on (tame) knot theory fundamentals.)
Though studied by few, wild knots exhibit fascinating behavior. Take, for example, the
wild slip knot, pictured in Figure 1. While this knot—referred to by Ralph Fox in 1949 as
a “remarkable simple closed curve” in [Fox49]—looks like it can be undone by pulling out
successive loops, it cannot be simpliﬁed. (According to Fox, it is only almost unknotted.)
One might wonder if any knot requiring an inﬁnite number of Reidemeister moves can be
simpliﬁed. In 2021, Forest Kobayashi [Kob21] justiﬁed the application of certain inﬁnite
collections of Reidemeister moves on wild knots.
Figure 1. A wild slip knot
Because wild knots are diﬃcult to classify, our aim is to represent a substantial subclass
of them using a more discrete, combinatorial structure: knot mosaic tiles. In this paper,
we develop and explore a new theory of wild mosaic knots.
1.1. History and fundamentals of wild knots. Since wild knots were deﬁned by Fox
and Artin in 1948 [FA48; Fox49], there have been some eﬀorts devoted to classifying
various subclasses of wild knots: from earlier work in the 1960s and ’70s [FH62; Lom64;
DH67; McP73] through more recent work in the 2000s [Fri03; JL09; Nan14; Kob21].
Also, higher dimensional wildly knotted spaces have been addressed [Fri05; Fri04], and
replacing S1 by the Cantor set, one obtains the related study of the so-called wild Cantor
sets whose classiﬁcation has also been of interest because of their importance in dynamical
systems [Gar+11].
From a descriptive set theory perspective [Hjo00], the classiﬁcation of tame knots is
trivial because there are only countably many of them (up to equivalence). In contrast,
the following was shown by Kulikov:
Theorem 1.1.1 ([Kul17]). The equivalence of wild knots is not classiﬁable by countable
structures.
Before we get too far, let us introduce some of the classical formal deﬁnitions we’ll need.
We’ll later adapt these topological notions to a more combinatorial framework.
Deﬁnition 1.1.2 ([Fox49; McP70; AB63]). We deﬁne knot, link, knot diagram, Reide-
meister moves, tame knot, wild knot, locally tame, wild point, and p-index as follows.

(1) A knot is a simple closed curve embedded in three-dimensional space (either R3
or S3).
(2) A link is a ﬁnite collection of non-intersecting knots.
(3) A knot diagram is a regular projection of a knot1 onto R2 where we retain the

(4) A tame knot is a knot that can be approximated by (is isotopic to) a ﬁnite
polygonal chain. Equivalently, we can deﬁne a tame knot as a piecewise-linear
curve in R3 or S3 consisting of a ﬁnite collection of line segments. (Most topologists
refer to tame knots simply as “knots.”)
(5) Two tame knots are equivalent if their diagrams are related by a ﬁnite sequence
of Reidemeister moves, shown in Fig. 2.
(6) A wild knot is any knot that is not tame.
(7) Let q be a point on a knot K in a space S (where S is either R3 or S3). Knot K
is locally tame at q if there exists a neighborhood, Nq, of q in S such that the
intersection of K with the closure of Nq (picture a—possibly smooshed—closed

(9) The penetration index or p-index of a point q in a knot K is the smallest

2.1. Tangles. A k-tangle is a proper embedding of a disjoint union of k arcs and m
circles into a 3-ball, B3, such that the endpoints of these arcs are glued along the ball’s
boundary at 2k marked points [Ada+20]. Knot theorists are particularly interested in
2-tangles since they can be closed to form knots and links in a straightforward way.
Traditionally, as deﬁned by Conway in [Con70], we picture a 2-tangle as a portion of a
knot diagram that lies within a circle, where the four points at which the strands cross
the circle occur in the four compass directions of NW, NE, SW, and SE.
Two tangles are equivalent if we can deform one into the other through a sequence of
Reidemeister moves and planar isotopies while keeping the endpoints of the arcs in the
tangle ﬁxed.
Figure 3. Examples of various tangles.
2.2. Rigid vertex spatial graphs. Next, we review the notions of a spatial graph and
rigid vertex spatial graph.
A spatial graph is a one-dimensional CW complex consisting of ﬁnitely many vertices
(zero-dimensional) and ﬁnitely many (one-dimensional) edges (including loops), tamely
embedded in R3 or S3. Each edge is homeomorphic to a closed interval whose boundary
is mapped to either two distinct vertices in the graph or a single vertex, in the case of a
loop.
In [Kau89], Kauﬀman presented spatial graphs as planar diagrams of vertices and edges
such that each vertex of the graph locally has a collection of rays emanating from it
(representing the edges that have that vertex as a boundary). When edges intersect in
the plane, their over/under crossing information is given, indicating how the graph is
embedded in space. By introducing new Reidemeister moves involving vertices, spatial
graphs can be studied from the perspective of knot theory.
Among the types of spatial graphs Kauﬀman considered, he specialized to 4-valent graphs
with rigid vertices, which we will denote as RV4 graphs. Kauﬀman describes rigid ver-
tices as ﬂat disks, each having (four) ﬂexible strings emanating from it, with string end-
points attached to the disk in a distinct cyclic order. These may, equivalently, be viewed
as singular knots, i.e. knots where a ﬁnite number of transversal self-intersections are
allowed.
Rigid vertex spatial graph equivalence, or (piecewise linear) ambient isotopy, is de-
scribed via diagrams using an extended set of Reidemeister moves that captures how
these rigid vertices can twist in space and interact with other edges. Kauﬀman proves
that these moves, shown in Figure 4 as moves I-IV and V*, capture the notion of ambient
isotopy in space for RV4 graphs.

2.3. RV2,4 graphs. Throughout this paper, we will work with objects that can be viewed
as rigid vertex spatial graphs having degree 4 rigid vertices, but we must also allow for
degree 2 vertices for reasons that will become clear.

or RV2,4 graphs. It is straightforward to expand RV4 graph ambient isotopy to describe
isotopies involving degree 2 vertices by introducing one additional Reidemeister move

Move VI, along with the moves I-IV and V* introduced in the previous section, generate
PL ambient isotopy of embedded RV2,4 graphs. The proof of the RV4 case was already

combinatorial isotopy.
Note: it was proven in [Gra50] that PL ambient isotopy and
combinatorial isotopy are equivalent for PL links in three-dimensional space, so this
suﬃces.
To show something is an elementary combinatorial isotopy amounts to showing that
we may embed a triangle into R3 piecewise linearly such that its interior is disjoint from
the graph (in our case, RV2,4) embedding, and the triangle shares either one or two edges
with the embedded graph. We then replace the shared edges of the triangle with the

Proposition 3.1.3 (Representability of Tame Tangles). Every tame m-tangle τ admits
a tangle mosaic representative L ∈L(n) for some n ∈N, with 2m connection points along
the boundary of L.
Proof. Since τ is tame, it admits a regular PL diagram in a disk whose 2m endpoints lie

We now proceed to assign RV2,4 mosaics to the vertices of a rooted tree with labeling
on the edges dictating the procedure of insertion. In particular, rather than limiting
ourselves to just ﬁnite trees (in which case we retain the theory of expressing tame knots,
cf. Proposition 4.2.8), we allow inﬁnitely many vertices in our tree.
4.2. Deﬁnition of a Tree Mosaic. A graph G consists of a set V(G) of vertices and
a family E(G) of unordered pairs of elements of V(G) called edges. If V(G) and E(G) are
both countably inﬁnite, then G is a countable graph. The degree of a vertex v ∈V(G)
is the cardinality of the set of edges incident to v, denoted by deg(v), and may be ﬁnite
or inﬁnite.
An inﬁnite graph is locally ﬁnite if each of its vertices has ﬁnite degree. For a locally
ﬁnite graph G and vertex v ∈V(G), we use N(v) ⊆V(G) to denote the neighbors of
the vertex v in G. Finally, if G is a tree, then we may distinguish a vertex v ∈V(G) as
being a root, and there exists a unique path from every vertex of G to v. Therefore if
G is rooted at a vertex v, there exists a unique orientation on all of its edges pointing
ultimately to v, hence we may consider it as an oriented graph. For two neighboring
vertices on an oriented tree, the vertex pointed to is called the parent while the other is
called the child. Notice, a vertex may have at most one parent and, if the tree is locally
ﬁnite, it may have ﬁnitely many children. In the mosaic data below, we write an edge as
(u, w) with the parent u listed ﬁrst and the child w listed second.

Deﬁnition 4.5.8 (Tree Mosaic Equivalence). Let M, P, P ′, N be tree knot mosaics. Sup-
pose P, P ′ are mosaic-level equivalent, and let P be a contraction of M, and P ′ a contraction
of N. Then, M and N are equivalent.
Corollary 4.5.9 (Tree Mosaic Equivalence Implies Topological Equivalence). If M and
N are equivalent tree knot mosaics, then |M| and |N| are ambient homeomorphic.
Proof. By deﬁnition, there exist tree knot mosaics P, P ′ such that P is a contraction of
M, P ′ is a contraction of N, and P, P ′ are mosaic-level equivalent. By Theorem 4.3.3,
contractions preserve the represented knot type, so |M| is ambient homeomorphic to |P|
and |N| is ambient homeomorphic to |P ′|.
By Lemma 4.5.7, |P| and |P ′| are ambient
homeomorphic. Therefore |M| and |N| are ambient homeomorphic.
