Abstract:
Holographic superfluids/superconductors are one of the most studied systems in the
AdS/CFT duality. In the low-energy, in the long-wavelength limit, they should be described by
a Ginzburg-Landau theory.
For critical dynamics, one expects that they belong to “model F”
universality class.
We consider a bulk 5-dimensional holographic superfluid/superconductor in
the probe limit. For the holographic superconductor, we impose the boundary Maxwell equation
to make the boundary Maxwell field dynamical. We identify the dual model F equations where
numerical coefficients are obtained exactly.

Introduction and Summary
Holographic superconductors and holographic superfluids are one of the most studied systems in the
AdS/CFT duality or the holographic duality [1–4].1 They describe superconductors and superfluids
[11–13]. On the other hand, from the macroscopic point of view, such a system should be described
by the Ginzburg-Landau (GL) theory near the critical point.
However, the exact form of the dual GL theory is little known. This is because a holographic
superfluid/superconductor is typically an Einstein-Maxwell-complex scalar system. Such a system
is hard to solve in general, and one needs a numerical computation. Even if one uses the probe
limit where the backreation of matter fields onto the geometry is ignored, one has to solve a
Maxwell-complex scalar system in an AdS black hole background, and one still needs a numerical
computation.
The exact form of the dual GL theory was identified only recently. In the bulk 5-dimensions,
one can construct analytic solutions near the critical point with a particular bulk scalar mass [14].
Using the solution and its generalizations, we identify the dual GL theory for the following systems:

• A “minimal” holographic superconductor [15, 16].
• A class of “nonminimal” holographic superconductors [17].
• A class of “nonminimal” holographic superconductors at finite ’t Hooft coupling (Gauss-
Bonnet holographic superconductor) [18, 19].
• A class of holographic Lifshitz superconductors [15]
Here, all numerical coefficients of the dual GL theories are obtained exactly.
Traditionally, most literature call both holographic superfluids/superconductors as “holographic
superconductors” collectively, but we make the clear distinction between these two systems. The
distinction lies in the difference whether the boundary Maxwell field is dynamical or not. If the
Maxwell field is dynamical, the system is a holographic superconductor. If the Maxwell field is
added just as an external source, the system is a holographic superfluid. Most literature actually
study holographic superfluids.
From the holographic point of view, one can make the boundary Maxwell field dynamical by
changing the boundary condition at the AdS boundary [16]. In this sense, we study both holographic
superconductor and holographic superfluid in this paper. As the result of the dynamical Maxwell
equation, the hallmark of the superconductor, the Meissner effect arises.
However, the dual GL theories mentioned above are the stationary case.
It is natural to
ask the time-dependence of a holographic superfluid/superconductor. A critical dynamics is clas-
sified by a dynamic universality class (Sec. 2.1).
The time-dependence of a holographic super-
fluid/superconductor is even harder to solve than the stationary case. However, it is commonly
believed that critical dynamics of a holographic superfluid is described by “model F” equations.
The model F describes a superfluid. There are some recent progress, most notably Refs. [20–22].
These studies rely on numerical computations to compare model F equations and holographic
computations. It is desirable to obtain an exact example. The purpose of this paper is to identify
the dual model F equations for a minimal holographic superfluid/superconductor.2

Preliminaries
2.1
Universality classes
2.1.1
Static universality class
As is well-known, a critical phenomenon is classified by a universality class.
In a critical phe-
nomenon, various quantities associated with the order parameter diverge at the critical point. The
divergences are parametrized by critical exponents. These exponents define a universality class.
For example, the minimal holographic superfluid/superconductor belongs to the |ψ|4 mean-
field theory universality class.
The very first paper of holographic superconductor pointed out
that the condensate takes the value of the mean-field exponent, and this is further confirmed by
computing all critical exponents [23]. In the holographic duality, one takes the large-Nc limit so
that fluctuations are suppressed, so one obtains mean-field theories.
Incidentally, when one says a mean-field theory, it implicitly assumes the |ψ|4 theory. But we
prefer to say the |ψ|4 mean-field theory. This is because one may obtain mean-field theories other
than the |ψ|4 theory. The N = 4 super-Yang-Mills (SYM) theory at a finite chemical potential is
an example.

2.1.2
Dynamic universality class
In dynamic critical phenomena, the relaxation time τ of the order parameter also diverges as τ ∝ξz,
which is known as the critical slowing down. The exponent z is the dynamic critical exponent. The
effect of slow dynamics is not limited to the order parameter. The other transport coefficients also
have singular behaviors in general due to couplings with the order parameter.
The details of the dynamic exponent depend on dynamic universality classes. The dynamic
universality classes were classified by Hohenberg and Halperin [24]: they are known as model A, B,
C, (D, E), F, G, H, and J.4 The classification is based on
1. whether the order parameter is conserved or not,
2. whether there exist the other conserved charges,
3. whether there exit couplings between these modes (mode-mode coupling).
Let us start with the simplest systems where only the order parameter matters near the critical
point. They are model A and B:
1. model A: the order parameter is not a conserved quantity (z = 2).
2. model B: the order parameter is a conserved quantity (z = 4).
In field theories, z can differ from the above values, but holographic results are the large-Nc limit
so that one obtains mean-field results. In order to obtain a nontrivial exponent, one needs to take
1/Nc corrections that are quantum gravity effects.
In the holographic duality, the study of dynamic universality class was initiated in Refs. [23, 25]
focusing on the high-temperature phase:
• Model A: A holographic superfluid/superconductor belongs to this class because the order
parameter is the “macroscopic wave function” ψ that is not conserved. The dynamic exponent
is z = 2 that is consistent with model A [23].
• Model B: The N = 4 SYM at a finite chemical potential belongs to this class because the
order parameter is a charge density [25]. The critical exponent z = 4 was shown explicitly in
Ref. [26]. The dynamic exponent is z = 4 that is consistent with model B.
For the other dynamic universality classes, the order parameter is coupled to the other modes.
As the result of the mode-mode coupling, a dynamic critical exponent is typically modified. For
example,
1. model F: a non-conserved order parameter is coupled to the other conserved modes.
2. model H: a conserved order parameter is coupled to the other conserved modes.
A superfluid belongs to model F. For a holographic superfluid/superconductor, ψ couples to the
conserved charge ρ in the low-temperature phase. Thus, one expects that a holographic super-
fluid/superconductor belongs to model F even in the probe limit.
In the context of gauge theories, the study of dynamic universality class was initiated in Ref. [27].
It became particularly popular after the work of Son and Stephanov [28]: they identified that QCD
belongs to model H universality class.
Similarly, the N = 4 SYM at a finite chemical potential should belong to model H universality
class [29]. The charge is coupled to the other conserved charge, energy-momentum tensor T µν,

1. As we saw, we impose the holographic semiclassical equation as the boundary condition for the
holographic superconductor, but it reduces to the Neumann boundary condition for the scalar
mode at the leading order in our expansion. Then, the result of the holographic superfluid
implicitly contains the result of the holographic superconductor. One would say that the
denominator P gives poles as the holographic superfluid whereas the numerator Q gives poles
as the holographic superconductor.
2. In this paper, we focus on a minimal holographic superfluid/superconductor, but there exist
other analytic solutions [14, 15, 18, 32], and it would be interesting to carry out a similar
analysis for those solutions.
