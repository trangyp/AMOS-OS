THE GAMIFIED KAT ˇETOV ORDER IS NOT LINEAR (IN FACT, VERY MUCH NOT SO)
TAKAYUKI KIHARA AND MING NG
ABSTRACT. Recently, the authors introduced the Gamified Katˇetov order on filters over ω. This was shown
to be strictly coarser than the classical Katˇetov order, and in fact collapses all MAD families to a single equiv-
alence class. In the opposite direction, the present paper shows that the Gamified Katˇetov order also embeds
P(ω)/Fin, and thus contains an antichain of size continuum. The analysis brings into focus some interesting
connections with Ramsey theory. As part of a broader programme investigating the interplay between combina-
torial and computable complexity, we then apply our construction to produce a large new family of non-modest
degrees in the extended Weihrauch hierarchy, which arise from associated effective subtoposes.
The Lawvere–Tierney order (≤LT) in the Effective Topos, introduced by Hyland [Hyl82], is a large-scale
classification programme for investigating differences in computable complexity. Early work by Hyland
and Pitts [Hyl82, Pit81] showed that the Turing degrees embed effectively into the ≤LT-order, and also
established the existence of a minimum and maximum class. Attention therefore turned to investigating
what lay in between the two extreme cases, a longstanding open problem in the area:
id <LT · · · · · · ? · · · · · · <LT ¬¬ .
Very recently, we made the surprising discovery [KN26] that this ≤LT-order is in fact tightly controlled
by the combinatorics of filters on ω. To make the connection precise, we introduced what we call the
Gamified Katˇetov order on filters over ω, before showing that a computable (and extended) variant of this
order is isomorphic to the original ≤LT-order. This marks an important shift in our understanding. Category
theorists have long attributed the difficulty of the ≤LT-order to the fact that the Effective Topos is not a
Grothendieck topos. Our result locates a subtler issue: the structure of the ≤LT-order reflects deep shifts in
set-theoretic complexity, now also interwined with constraints arising from computability.
Read in the above context, the Gamified Katˇetov order can be seen as isolating the combinatorial mecha-
nism underlying the ≤LT-order. Accordingly, we write ≤o
LT for this order, suggestively echoing the notation
≤LT with an added “o”. However, the ≤o
LT-order also exhibits many striking features that make it inter-
esting in its own right. Preliminary evidence indicates that it detects a form of complexity fundamentally
distinct from those captured by usual set-theoretic tools: the ≤o
LT-order is strictly coarser than the classical
Rudin-Keisler and Katˇetov orders [KN26, Theorem A], and is also incomparable with the Tukey order on
filters over ω [KN26, Theorem B].
The nature of its coarseness is also interesting. In the classical Katˇetov order, there exists a chain of
c+-many equivalence classes of MAD families as well as c-many pairwise incomparable ones [HGF03, §2].
By contrast, in the gamified context, all MAD families collapse to a single ≡o
LT-equivalence class [KN26,
Conclusion 5.3]. On the other hand, we also discovered an infinite strictly ascending chain of classes within
the ≤o
LT-order [KN26, Theorem C]. One reading of this result: despite its apparent coarseness, there still
exists a rich internal structure within the Gamified Katˇetov order.
The present paper extends this perspective. It would be consistent with previous results to conjecture that
the ≤o
LT-order defines a countable linear order on the equivalence classes of filters (dually, ideals) on ω.
However, we now show that this is emphatically not the case, answering [KN26, Problem 3]. Our first main
theorem is the following.
Theorem A (Theorem 2.6). The Gamified Katˇetov order admits an embedding of P(ω)/Fin. In particular,
it contains a chain of length b, as well as an antichain of size c.

1.2. The Gamified Katˇetov order. The Gamified Katˇetov order is a game-theoretic refinement of the
classical Katˇetov order, introduced by the authors in [KN26, §3]. We start by reviewing the usual definition
of Katˇetov reduction. Given any U, V ⊆P(ω) upper sets, we write
U ≤K V
just in case
∃h: ω ! ω such that A ∈U =⇒h−1[A] ∈V .
Informally, U, V each represent two abstract notions of largeness, and U ≤K V means that every U-large
set A can be uniformly witnessed to be V-large by a single fixed map h: ω ! ω. A useful reformulation,
exploiting the fact that U, V are upper sets, is that U ≤K V if and only if
∃h: ω ! ω such that A ∈U =⇒h[B] ⊆A for some B ∈V ;
see [KN26, Observation 3.7]. In plainer terms: instead of pulling A back along h, one may equivalently
push forward some V-large set B into A.
The Gamified Katˇetov order generalises this picture on two levels.
(1) It replaces the witnessing map h: ω ! ω with a partial map on finite strings
φ: ⊆<ωω ! ω.
Thinking of a finite string as a sequence of values revealed one at a time suggests a sequential,
in particular a game-like, interpretation of the construction (which we do not formalise here; for
details, see [KN26, §3.4]).
(2) The single set B ∈V is replaced by a tree whose branching is controlled by V. From the game-
theoretic perspective, the tree is a device that supplies a Bn ∈V at every stage n of the game.
In this way, a single global witness B is replaced by a family of stage-by-stage witnesses organised into a
tree, while the map φ assigns an output to each resulting sequence. This framework is formalised by the
following key definitions.
Definition 1.10. Let V ⊆P(ω) be an upper set, and T ⊆<ωω a tree. A node σ ∈T is called V-branching
if the set of immediate successors {n ∈ω | σ⌢n ∈T} is in V. A tree T is V-branching just in case:
(a) T is non-empty; and
(b) For any σ ∈T, σ is V-branching.
We emphasise Condition (b) includes the case when σ = ø, the root of T.

Equivalently, Φ(p) ∈A for any infinite path p through T.

As alluded to before, the original motivation behind the Gamified Katˇetov order came not from set theory,
but category theory. More precisely, it arises from the study of the Effective Topos Eff, introduced by Hyland
[Hyl82], which provides a category-theoretic framework for computability.
Every (elementary) topos carries a natural partial order on its Lawvere–Tierney topologies (the Lawvere-
Tierney order). In well-behaved cases, such as Grothendieck toposes, the ≤LT-order admits several useful
concrete descriptions, and topos theorists have developed a wide range of tools to analyse them. By contrast,
the ≤LT-order in Eff is far less understood. While we know it encodes computability-theoretic information
— for instance, it admits an effective embedding of the Turing degrees [Hyl82, Theorem 17.2] — a system-
atic analysis of its structure has proved difficult.
A key move in [KN26] is to identify the combinatorial mechanism underlying these category-theoretic
structures. Re-examining work of Lee-van Oosten [LvO13, §2], we observed that the ≤LT-order in Eff
induces a preorder on upper sets in P(ω). This set up the first major theorem of our paper [KN26]:
Theorem 1.14 ([KN26, Theorem A]). The Gamified Katˇetov order (≤o
LT) defines a preorder on upper sets
over ω. Moreover:
(i) The Gamified Katˇetov order is equivalent to the Katˇetov order closed under well-founded iterations
of Fubini powers. In particular, it is strictly coarser than the Katˇetov order.
(ii) The Gamified Katˇetov order admits an explicit game-theoretic description, justifying its name.
(iii) The computable Gamified Katˇetov order is equivalent to the ≤LT-order on upper sets over ω.
Discussion 1.15 (Coarseness). For further details, we refer the reader to [KN26]. The main takeaway here
is that Theorem 1.14 locates two distinct sources of coarseness within the Gamified Katˇetov order.
• By (i), the order is closed under well-founded Fubini iterations. In particular, U ≡o
LT U⊗U for every
upper set U – which is emphatically false for the classical Katˇetov order. Indeed, many separation
results for the Katˇetov order on ideals rely on the strict inequality Fin <K Fin ⊗Fin, and so this
collapse creates serious technical obstacles in the gamified setting; see Discussion 3.12.
• By (iii), the ≤LT-order is equivalent to the Gamified Katˇetov order once we restrict to computable
witnesses Φ. Hence, the ≤o

4. CONSEQUENCES FOR COMPUTABILITY THEORY
4.1. Computability-Theoretic Background. We now return to the setting of the Effective Topos Eff,
which originally motivated our study of the Gamified Katˇetov order. To explain the place of our work
within computability theory, we briefly review the major shifts in our understanding of LT topologies on Eff
over the past several decades:
(1) First stage (1980s–2000s). In his original paper [Hyl82], Hyland showed that the Turing degrees
embed effectively into the ≤LT-order on LT topologies in Eff. This suggested viewing the LT
topologies as a generalisation of Turing oracles (i.e. single-valued oracles), but the nature of this
generalisation remained unclear for many years. A few examples of non-Turing oracles were sub-
sequently discovered (most notably, the double negation topology, Pitts’ counterexample [Pit81],
and Lifshitz realisability [vO91]), but these were generally regarded as exceptional cases rather than
manifestations of a broader theory.
(2) Second stage (2010s). Lee-van Oosten [LvO13] provided concrete presentations of all LT topolo-
gies on Eff. They focused in particular on the basic LT topologies and found several new examples
that do not arise from single-valued oracles. However, the combinatorial mechanism separating the
basic topologies (or indeed LT topologies in general) was not yet identified.
(3) Third stage (2020s). Kihara [Kih23], building on Lee-van Oosten’s work, linked the ≤LT-order to
Weihrauch reducibility, a notion extensively studied in computable analysis [BGP21]. This marked
a shift in perspective on what should be regarded as the “paradigmatic” LT topology: in computable
analysis, multi-valued oracles (Weihrauch oracles) are the primary objects of study, with single-
valued oracles (Turing oracles) appearing as a special case.
(4) Fourth stage (2020s). In fact, Kihara [Kih23] linked the LT-topologies to the extended Weihrauch
degrees introduced by Bauer [Bau22]. The embedded copy of the Weihrauch degrees within this
new hierarchy is called the modest degrees, with the remaining non-modest degrees being viewed
as the exceptional case. Nevertheless, Kihara pointed out that each basic topology (in the sense of
Lee-van Oosten) yields a non-modest degree; several natural examples were analysed in the cited
paper, with the task of constructing further examples left as a future challenge [Kih23, Question 1].

(5) Fifth stage (Present work). Kihara and Ng [KN26] linked the basic topologies to the combinato-
rial complexity of filters (dually, ideals) on ω through the Gamified Katˇetov order. In particular, our
results highlight how these topologies encode rich interactions between computable and combina-
torial complexity. This discovery repositions the basic topologies, and thus the non-modest degrees,
from isolated curiosities to central objects of study.6
This fifth stage opens up a new research area previously overlooked in computability theory. Informally,
filters and ideals correspond to abstract notions of majority and minority. Consequently, the ≤LT-order
on basic topologies on Eff – equivalently, the computable Gamified Katˇetov order – measures the relative
strength of what we call computability by majority notions (for details, see [KN26, §7.2]). This section
extends the set-theoretic results established in the previous sections to illuminate several important structural
aspects of this relatively uncharted territory.
