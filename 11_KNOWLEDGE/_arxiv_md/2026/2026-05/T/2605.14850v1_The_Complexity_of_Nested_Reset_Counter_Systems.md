Abstract
Nested counter systems (NCS) are a generalization of counter systems to higher-order counters.
Here, a higher-order counter is allowed to have other (lower-order) counters as elements, instead
of just a number. Such systems can be viewed as working on trees, where the height of the tree
naturally corresponds to the highest order counter that the system is working with. It is known that
the coverability problem for NCS, which asks if a given final tree can be covered from a given initial
tree, is Fϵ0-complete. Here Fϵ0 is a class in the fast-growing hierarchy of complexity classes.
In this paper, we consider an extension of NCS called nested reset counter systems (NRCS) that
extends NCS with resets. We show that coverability for NRCS over order-k counters is FΩk-complete
where Ωk is the tower of height k of the ω ordinal. This gives the first natural hierarchy of complete
problems for all of these classes. Furthermore, to prove our upper bounds, we also develop length
function theorems for any fixed amount of applications of the multiset operation on finite sets.
As an application of our results, we improve existing upper bounds for various problems from
XML processing, graph transformation systems, π-calculus, logic and parameterized verification.
Furthermore, using our completeness results for k-NRCS, we also prove FΩk-completeness of the
considered problems from the realms of parameterized verification and logic, for all k.

Introduction
The last decade has seen the rise of a significant research program in theoretical computer
science, namely understanding the precise complexity of problems which are non-elementary,
i.e., problems whose running times cannot be bounded by any fixed tower of exponentials
of the input size. This has resulted in the development of a rich collection of mathematical
techniques in order to prove both upper and lower bounds for various decision problems.
Examples of such problems include reachability for Petri nets [38, 37, 10], coverability for lossy
counter machines and lossy channel systems [46, 19, 45], emptiness of alternating 1-register
and 1-clock automata [33, 14], coverability for unordered data nets, ν-Petri nets and various
other enriched nets [42, 36, 28], analysis problems for fragments of π-calculus [40, 4, 2],
broadcast networks [3, 13], bisimulation equivalence of pushdown automata [31], rewriting
systems [29, 23] and logics [5, 24, 25, 35, 34]. We refer the reader to the excellent survey
by Schmitz [44] for a wide collection of such problems from automata theory, logic, formal
languages and verification, which have been proven to be complete for various classes in the
fast-growing hierarchy of complexity classes. This hierarchy allows for a finer classification of
the complexity of problems lying beyond the elementary realm.
When such results are viewed from a tractability perspective, they are naturally negative.
However, what we get out of them are fundamental insights that allow us to transfer techniques

Completeness is such a fundamental notion of complexity classes, that once a complexity

complete problems were known only for the first 3 levels of this hierarchy. We generalize
these results and provide complete problems for all of these levels.
The specific hierarchy of problems that we prove to be complete are inspired by a model
of computation working over higher-order counters. Intuitively, an 1-order counter is a usual
counter over the natural numbers, which we can either increment or decrement by a constant.
A 2-order counter is a counter in which we can increment or decrement 1-order counters. A
3-order counter similarly then works over a 2-order counter and so on. Such systems can be
naturally viewed as labelled trees, where a tree of height 1 can model a collection of 1-order
counters (The root models the state of the machine and the number of children of the root
with label ℓdenotes the value of the counter ℓ). A tree of height 2 can similarly model a
collection of 2-order counters and so on. Such a model is called a nested counter system,
which has been considered in [12].
In this paper, we extend this model to also allow reset operations to the counters. For
instance, over 1-order counters, this would simply correspond to resetting the value of a
counter to 0, giving rise to the equivalent model of reset Petri nets [49]. The resulting systems
that we get out of adding resets to nested counter systems are called nested reset counter
systems (NRCS). The main result of this paper is to show that the coverability problem for
k-dimensional nested reset counter systems, is FΩk-complete for any k. This generalizes the
known result for k = 1 [44, Section 6.1.2].

multiset well-quasi-orders. Intuitively, a nested multiset well-quasi-order is obtained by
starting from the empty set and then closing it under disjoint sums as well as applications

contributions, we provide upper bounds on the so-called controlled bad sequences for such

Related work.
The closest work to ours is [12], where the authors proved that the coverability
problem for k-dimensional nested counter systems (without resets!) is FΩk−1-hard and in
FΩ2k. Our result significantly improves upon their upper bound by bringing it down to FΩk.
Length function theorems for various well-quasi-orders are known in the literature - for
instance for the product ordering over the natural numbers [19], for the subword ordering [46],
for powersets and multiset of natural numbers [42], for the priority subword ordering [27] and
for multisets of words [25]. However, none of those results except for the priority subword
ordering are applicable in our setting, because they can only be used on problems lying
in classes below FΩ4. The priority subword ordering is a general tool that can be used to
place a problem in Fϵ0 (roughly speaking, ϵ0 is the limit of Ω1, Ω2, · · · ); however, since tight
bounds are not known for it, we cannot use them in our setting here.

three levels FΩ1, FΩ2 and FΩ3 of the hierarchy and also for classes below FΩ1, as well as for
classes between FΩ1 and FΩ2. However, prior to our results, no natural complete problems
were known for FΩk for any k > 3. Curiously, however, we do know of complete problems for
Fϵ0 [27, 12, 4]. But a finer understanding of the classes in between FΩ3 and Fϵ0 was missing
prior to our work. As we demonstrate, this is not due to a lack of interesting problems - any
fixed problem in the hierarchy of problems mentioned above in parameterized verification,
logic and other fields fall between FΩ3 and Fϵ0.
Our techniques.
As mentioned above, there are no known natural complete problems
for FΩk for any k > 3. Hence, our lower bounds are from first principles, i.e., proven by
reductions from bounded counter machines. To this end, we show that k-NRCS can be used
to perform weak computations of Hardy functions and their inverses - this allows us to build
a very large but ultimately bounded counter which we can use to simulated bounded counter
machines.
Similar techniques have been used for proving lower bounds for other problems in the
literature; for instance, the ideas closest to ours are from [12] for proving hardness of k-
NCS (note the absence of resets). However, the hardness result was only for the class
FΩk−1. Furthermore, their construction seems to contain a subtle flaw: To perform (weak)
computations of Hardy functions, the underlying machines need to be able to represent and
manipulate ordinals. One important operation that would be necessary to implement such
computations would be to (lossily) identify the smallest term in the CNF of a represented
ordinal. The construction given in [12] seems to use the structural ordering on ordinals in
order to isolate the smallest term, i.e., when it compares two terms, it is only guaranteed to
return a term that is smaller than both in the structural ordering. This indicates that there

We first show how to construct copy gadgets, because we will also need them for our smallest-

need to start from a marked encoder T, where some child v of the root is labelled by mrkd

of just the node v (call it v′). Then, the idea is simply to perform a (lossy) depth-first search
(DFS) of the subtree rooted at v and copy branches of this subtree at v to branches of the
subtree at v′. More precisely, we start at v and systematically explore each of its branches in
a DFS manner, marking each branch as we go along. At any point, we non-deterministically
decide if either the current branch can be extended or we have hit a leaf. In the former case,
we recursively continue by selecting some child at the end of this branch. In the latter case,
we simply copy this branch as another branch of v′ and reset all the children of the last node
of this branch. (If we had guessed correctly, there would be no such children to reset; if we
had guessed wrongly, then we make a lossy error). Then we go back up one level along our
current branch and continue the DFS. In this way, we would have (lossily) copied the subtree
of v as the subtree of v′. When we end the DFS, we remove all the marked information from
all of the branches in v and label the root as endcopy and v′ as cpd. This proves Theorem 11.
(See Appendix E.1 for more details).

Upper Bound for Coverability of k-NRCS
In this section, we prove our upper bound for the coverability problem for k-NRCS. Namely
we shall show that for any k ≥1, coverability for k-NRCS is in FΩk. We first present a
global proof structure of how we prove this result. First, in subsections 4.1 and 4.2 we
show that k-NRCS can be viewed as a well-structured transition system (WSTS) [1, 20] by
means of the induced subgraph ordering on trees of height at most k, which is known to be a
(normed) well-quasi-ordering (nwqo). This allows us to reduce the problem of proving upper
bounds for coverability of k-NRCS to the so-called length function theorems for controlled
bad sequences over the induced subgraph nwqo. Then, in subsection 4.3, we show that this
nwqo can be interpreted in terms of another nwqo that we call nested multisets. In the next
three subsections, we then show how to prove length function theorems for controlled bad
sequences over nested multisets. We do this, by leveraging the framework of Schmitz and
Schnoebelen [47]: First, we compute the length function theorems by unraveling the descent
equation; next, we approximate each step of the unraveling by reflections; finally, we use the
machinery of order type and derivatives to bound the estimate of each reflection.
Having stated our proof strategy for the upper bound, we now proceed to subsection 4.1,
where we recall the notion of well-quasi-orders.
4.1
WQOs and Controlled Bad Sequences
Well-Quasi-Orders.
A quasi-order over a set X is a relation ≤X which is reflexive and
transitive. We write x <X y to mean that x ≤X y and y ≰X x. A well-quasi-order (wqo)
over a set X is a quasi-order ≤X such that in any infinite sequence x0, x1, x2, . . . , of elements
from X, there exists i < j such that xi ≤X xj. Finally, a norm function over X is a function
| · |X : X →N such that for any n ∈N, the set X≤n := {x ∈X : |x|X ≤n} is finite.
A normed well-quasi-order (nwqo) is a tuple (X, ≤X, | · |X) such that (X, ≤X) is a wqo
and | · |X is a norm. The set X will be called the domain of the nwqo. If (X, ≤X, | · |X) is a
nwqo and S ⊆X, then the nwqo induced by S is the nwqo (S, ≤S, | · |S) where ≤S and | · |S
are the restrictions of ≤X and | · |X to S respectively.
Whenever the underlying order ≤X and norm | · |X are clear from the context, we will
drop those and simply refer to the nwqo by the set X. Given any two nwqos X, Y we say
that X ≡Y when X and Y are isomorphic structures, i.e., there must be an isomorphism f
from X to Y such that x ≤X x′ iff f(x) ≤X f(x′) and |x|X = |f(x)|Y for any x, x′ ∈X.
▶Example 16. Let Γi denote the nwqo over any set {x1, . . . , xi} such that any two distinct
elements are unordered and |xj| = 0 for any j. Clearly, Γi is a nwqo. (Note that Γ0 has
empty domain and is hence called the empty nwqo). Similarly, the set N with the usual

▶Theorem 37. Coverability for k-depth broadcast networks over trees is in FΩk.

coverability of k-NRCS to both these problems. The hardness result is obtained by taking an
existing reduction from k-NCS, i.e., k-NRCS without resets to k-depth broadcast networks.
We utilise this reduction along with the fact that broadcasts can be used to simulate resets
of counters [17] to give an improved reduction from k-NRCS to k-depth broadcast networks.
(See Appendix H for more details).
▶Theorem 38. Coverability for k-depth broadcast networks and k-depth broadcast networks
over trees are FΩk-complete.
Previously the best known result was a Fϵ0-completeness result for these problems, when
k is part of the input [3, Theorem 16]. Our new result is a refined parameterized analogue of
this, giving a completeness result for each fixed k.

6
Conclusion
We have provided the first natural collection of complete problem for the hierarchy (FΩk)k∈N,
namely coverability for k-NRCS. Furthermore, to prove our upper bounds, we have also
developed length function theorems for nested applications of the multiset operator on nwqos.
Finally, we also presented various applications of our upper bound techniques and the newly
introduced model of NRCS to improve upper bounds for various other problems and identify
new complete problems for FΩk, for all k.
