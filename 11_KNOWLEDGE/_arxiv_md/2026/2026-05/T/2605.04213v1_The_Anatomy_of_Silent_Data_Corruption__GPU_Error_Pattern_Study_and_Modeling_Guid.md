The Anatomy of Silent Data Corruption:
GPU Error Pattern Study and Modeling Guidance
Chung-Hsuan Tung1,∗, Yanxiang Huang2, Nirmal Saxena2, Philip Shirvani2
Saurabh Hukerikar2, Twinkle Jain2, Abhishek Tyagi3,∗, Sanjay Gongalore2
1Duke University, 2NVIDIA, 3University of Rochester
Abstract—Silent data corruption (SDC) threatens the reliabil-
ity of large-scale GPU clusters used for training large language
models, yet its rarity and lack of explicit error signals make
accurate high-level modeling challenging. To address this gap,
we conducted a large-scale gate-level stuck-at fault injection
on a production-class data-center GPU, consuming over three
million simulator hours across 63 CUDA micro-benchmarks.
We extracted GPU SDC characteristics in terms of corruption
types, bit-flip behavior, and warp-aligned spatial correlation. Our
results show that NaN/±INF account for only 1.01% of SDC
outcomes, that single-bit flips constitute less than 40% of bit-flip
events, and that corruption addresses exhibit periodicity. These
statistics motivate distribution-aware high-level fault modeling
and realistic software-based fault injection for resilience evalua-
tion of production-class GPU architectures.
Index Terms—Silent Data Corruption (SDC), Graphics Pro-
cessing Unit (GPU), Fault Injection (FI), Hardware Reliability
I. INTRODUCTION
Silent data corruption (SDC) is a symptom of hardware
faults in which a processing unit produces incorrect results
without any hardware indication [1]–[3]. Open Compute
Project (OCP) [1] defines outcomes of hardware faults as
benign if they leave program output unaffected, corrected
if detected and repaired by protections (e.g., error correc-
tion code (ECC) in memories), detected unrecoverable errors
(DUEs) if flagged by error detectors (e.g., parity and instruc-
tion validation) but the output cannot be corrected, and SDCs.
SDCs are considered the most insidious outcomes, as they
escape internal detections when corrupting program output [1].
Modern machine learning (ML) algorithms, especially large
language models (LLMs), have shifted computation to massive
GPU clusters where even rare events become non-negligible.
Particularly, training is resource-intensive: GPT-3 with 175 B
parameters [4] required nearly 300 GPU-years on NVIDIA
V100s [5], and Llama 3 with 405 B parameters used up to
16 K NVIDIA H100 GPUs [6]. SDCs have been observed at
scale by Google [2], Meta [3], and Alibaba [7], causing 1.4%
of unexpected GPU interruptions in Llama 3 training [6], and
reported weekly/biweekly in Gemini training [8].
Despite robust hardware protection mechanisms, faults in
unprotected logic can still lead to SDC [2], [9], which becomes
especially harmful for modern LLMs that scale across many
GPUs. Insufficient understanding of SDC characteristics and
impacts leads to high handling costs [2], [3], i.e., detection

requires dual modular redundancy with comparison (2× over-
head), and correction needs triple modular redundancy (3×
overhead) [10]. Suspicion-based detections [2], [11] monitor-

the consequent loss spike [9], [12], also suffer from human-
centric debugging [3], and long-range, unreliable rollback [11]
due to delayed degradation of ML training accuracy [9].
Fault injection (FI) can evaluate SDC impact and guide
mitigation. While FI is standard in accelerator design [13]
and automotive functional safety [14], [15], high-fidelity FI
depends on the fault model, stimuli, and hardware details.
Prior work either uses software-level injectors with hy-
pothetical data corruption patterns [16]–[19], reports CPU-
cluster observations [2], [3], [7], or studies reduced-complexity
accelerators [9], [20]. Consequently, corruption patterns in
production-class data-center GPUs remain underexplored.
This paper addresses that gap by characterizing structural
SDC manifestations and spatial correlations via gate-level FI
on a production-class GPU architecture, with the goal of
informing higher-level modeling and mitigation strategies. Our
campaign yields the following contributions:
• A statistical characterization of GPU SDC manifestations,
providing distributions over corruption types, multi-bit
flip patterns, and warp-aligned spatial correlation.
• A large-scale (three million simulator-hours) gate-level FI
campaign on a production-class data-center GPU model.
• A CUDA micro-benchmark suite for systematic structural
fault characterization across GPU functional units.
Our campaign clarifies the structural nature of GPU SDC,
supporting realistic high-level fault modeling and injection.
II. FAULT INJECTION AND HIGH-LEVEL MODELING

test vectors, as shown at the top left of Fig. 1. We craft micro-
benchmarks (Sec. II-B) as test vectors to systematically exer-
cise critical GPU data paths. For each benchmark, injecting a
single fault yields one of four outcomes: hang, benign, DUE,
or SDC. For the SDC outcome, the simulation dumps the
corrupted memory, which is compared to the golden memory
from the fault-free simulation for pattern analysis.

physical defects, such as wear-out and process variation [3].

To enable large-scale FI, we utilize the single stuck-at
fault (SAF) model. While advanced models like bridging,
small delay [21], cell-aware [22], and physically-aware region
faults [23] offer higher accuracy, they are generally unsup-
ported by the simulation engines of commercial EDA tools.
Our reliance on the SAF model is justified by the fact that
the emergent error patterns, specifically bit-flip distributions
and spatial correlations, are largely a function of the logic
network’s internal topology. Most localized faults (including
bridging/delay defects) propagate through the same sensitized
paths to the primary outputs, yielding similar error signatures.
Cell-aware or physically-aware region faults may result in
more expansive error patterns due to wider internal fan-out,
but the core topological propagation remains consistent.
Simulation Outcomes.
Faults manifest as hang, benign
(masked), DUE, or SDC [17], [24]. hang means the runtime
exceeds twice the fault-free execution; DUE means a machine
check exception is observed. Otherwise, memory contents are
compared to the golden run: identical marks the fault as benign
and masked; a mismatch implies an SDC. Simulations in which
the injected fault causes an SDC are studied to understand the
spatial signature of the SDC in the program output (Sec. III).
B. Micro-benchmarks
A suite of 63 micro-benchmarks exercises critical GPU
pipelines through combinations of functional units, opera-
tions, data types, and input stimuli. Each benchmark pairs a
CUDA kernel (an operation with a specific data type executed
on the ALU or tensor core) with input sequences gener-
ated by Mersenne–Twister (MT) [25], universal test pattern
(UTP) [26]–[29], or linear feedback shift register (LFSR) [30],
[31]. Each operand uses a distinct sequence under the se-
lected method. Operations include addition, multiplication,
fused multiply-add (FMA), and general matrix multiplication
(GEMM, αAB + βC). We evaluate GEMM in addition (B =
I, where I is identity matrix), multiplication (β = 0), and
standard (α = β = 1) form. Data types span UINT8/UINT32
and FP16/FP32/BF16/TF32, with limited exploration of FP8.
III. MEMORY CORRUPTION PATTERN ANALYSIS METHOD
We analyze the locations (memory addresses) and values of
corruptions in dumped memory images. As software observes
the memory state, per-kernel statistics from the corresponding
micro-benchmark can be applied to higher-level modeling.
Construction of Error Profile. Fig. 1 shows the detailed FI
flow (top) with the error pattern extraction in the case of SDC
(bottom). The FI experiments will dump the raw memory im-
age, which stores the program outputs. We extract the bit-level
error pattern from each dumped image and its corresponding
golden memory using bitwise XOR for every bit-width, e.g.,
32-bit for FP32. We record the list of differing addresses
and values, compute per-benchmark corruption rates, bucket
values into error types, and accumulate bit flip probabilities.
Addresses are analyzed for spatial patterns for each error type.
Corruption Value Patterns.
We classify corruptions as
nullified (all bits zero) or bit-flipped. Nullification is common
when SAFs in control logic disable computations or select
data paths incorrectly. For bit flips, we track flip rates per bit
position and the distribution over flip counts in a bit-width.
For floating-point types, we separately record rates for special
values (NaN/±INF) that are often targeted by software checks.
Corruption Location Patterns.
Grouping corrupted ad-
dresses modulo the warp size (W=32) reveals periodic cor-
ruption patterns at the warp granularity. While the exact stride
may depend on the GPU model, the observed spatial correla-
tion suggests structured rather than random fault propagation.
IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
GPU Architecture.
We use a synthesized gate-level model
of a production-class data-center GPU in a reduced two-
SM configuration. Each SM integrates scalar/tensor pipelines,
warp scheduling, shared memory, and a private L1 cache,
and connects via an on-chip network to a shared L2 and
high-bandwidth memory. The model executes CUDA-derived
traces under a 32-thread SIMT execution model with an ECC-
protected cache and memory hierarchy. Product identifiers are
omitted; however, the modeled architecture reflects that of a
deployed data-center GPU used for ML workloads.
Fault Sampling. Using a commercial EDA tool, we extract
SAF sites with hierarchical net names to enable hardware-
unit attribution and uniformly sample them across the full
SM logic, while ensuring each failure mode is adequately
represented [15]. Stuck-at-0/1 are randomly assigned, resulting
in an approximately balanced distribution. Given the high
masked rates [2], [3], [24] and cost of gate-level FI, we
apply a testability check to ensure stimulus reachability. For
a representative micro-benchmark, this filtering reduces the
candidate sites from 6,925 to 1,507 (78.2%), with similar
reductions observed across other micro-benchmarks.

Across all benchmark–fault pairs, we observe 25k SDC
cases and 28k DUEs or hang outcomes. For each SDC case,
we analyze the output memory image (1.8 KB–64 MB), and
report aggregate statistics with key observations.
A. Corruption Outcome Distribution
Fig. 2 summarizes corruption rates regarding the memory
space in Fig. 2(a) and type breakdowns in Fig. 2(b).
Observation 1. Special values (NaN and ±INF) are rare.
Across 600 M observed corruptions, NaN/+INF/-INF ac-
count for 0.39%/0.49%/0.13%, respectively, i.e., 1.01% jointly.

typical values. The exponent logic is also more reliable due

C. Warp-Aligned Spatial Correlation
As GPU execution groups threads into warps, when batched
operations write to memory, a fault in one data path can man-
ifest as multiple corruptions in strided addresses. A common
warp size is 32 [32], and our evaluated GPU model follows
this design. Accordingly, we analyze spatial correlation by
grouping output addresses modulo the warp size (W = 32).
Observation 8. SDCs exhibit spatial periodicity across hard-
ware units, data types, and corruption categories.
Fig. 7 shows the corruption rate for each error type
across all combinations of data types and hardware units
(FP8 is omitted as discussed in Sec. V-B). The corrupted
addresses exhibit periodicity at multiple granularities, e.g.,
16 (TensorCore: FP32/FP16; L1$Miss: UINT32/FP32/FP16),
8 (L1$Miss: UINT32/FP32/FP16; CudaCoreIO: UINT32), 4
(ALU: FP32), and 2 (CudaCoreControl2: FP32/FP16).
The observed periodicity may also vary across corruption
categories, in addition to fault location and workload data
types; e.g., non-special corruptions repeat the pattern in a
period of 8, whereas nullifications in 16 for L1$Miss faults.
The periodicity may arise from GPU parallelism, which groups
data and threads whenever possible, leading to shared up-
streams across a set of program outputs.
VI. IMPLICATIONS FOR HIGH-LEVEL MODELING
This section summarizes the key insights from our gate-level
GPU FI and their implications for high-level SDC modeling.
A. Revising High-Level Fault Injection Assumptions
We distill three central insights regarding the structural
characteristics of SDCs in modern GPUs in Sec. V.
Insight 1. SDC outcomes are dominated by nullification and
non-special bit-flips, with fault-location dependence.
While prior DL accelerator studies qualitatively reported the
rarity of NaN/±INF [9], our work on a production-class GPU
quantifies their rate (1.01%) and further reveals fault-location
dependence, with control-path faults skewing toward nullifi-
cation and data-buffer faults toward non-special bit-flips. This
systematic skew suggests that high-level fault injection and
protection should reflect unit-specific corruption distributions
rather than assuming device-wide uniform behavior.
Insight 2. Bit-flips are multi-bit and position-dependent.
Less than 40% of bit-flip corruptions are single-bit events,
indicating that the single-bit corruption model underrepresents
the observed fault behavior. Multi-bit flip counts exhibit a
broad secondary mode beyond the single-bit peak, and flip
probabilities per bit position decrease from LSB to MSB, with
mantissa bits more frequently affected than exponents. These
characteristics suggest that software- and micro-architecture-
level FI using uniform single bit-flips [20], [33]–[35] and
high-level FI tools offering only single or two adjacent bit
flips [17]–[19], [36] can benefit from incorporating empirically
derived multi-bit and position-dependent distributions.
Insight 3. Corruptions show warp-aligned spatial periodicity.
Corruptions exhibit structured spatial periodicity rather than
random address dispersion. High-level models assuming inde-
pendent element-wise injection [20], [34] may therefore mis-
represent the structure of manifestations. The periodicity could
enable pattern-aware SDC predictors and fault localizers.

VII. RELATED WORK
Industrial Observations of SDC.
The presence of SDCs
in large-scale data centers has been widely reported by
Google [2], Meta [3], and Alibaba [7]. Several major vendors,
including Intel, AMD, and NVIDIA, have joined Google and
Meta in the Open Compute Project (OCP) to collectively
study and mitigate SDCs [37], highlighting broad industrial
concern. Recent disclosures from Amazon [12] and others
show continued investigation of SDC effects during LLM
training using faulty or aged hardware components.
SDC Characterization Efforts.
Intel, Auburn University,
the University of Athens [38] identify timing marginalities
as the major source of SDC in CPU. Alibaba [7] collects
the SDC patterns on their CPU cluster. FIdelity [20] and its
successor [9] from Google propose architectural-level SDC
modeling using open-source DNN accelerators (NVDLA). In-
tel [39] evaluates transient SDC propagation in ML workloads,
while NVIDIA [15] performs large-scale FI campaigns for
automotive functional safety. Note that earlier works examined
stuck-at faults propagating from flip-flops to memory [40]
and identified the mismatch between flip-flop-level and archi-
tectural fault injection [41]. More recent analyses find that
traditional FI models can miss fault scenarios leading to test
escapes and silent corruptions [23].
Software-level Fault Injection and Modeling. SASSIFI [36],
NVBitFI [17], and PyTorchFI [18] frameworks enable efficient
fault injection on commercial GPUs, supporting instruction-
or tensor-level analysis. These tools allow scalable evaluation
of ML model resilience but rely on synthetic error models
(e.g., random or single-bit flips) that lack empirical grounding.
Statistical models such as Error Model [24] target coverage
estimation rather than realistic corruption pattern prediction.
GPU Error Studies. While NCSA’s Delta Project [42] char-
acterizes DUEs and their impact in GPU clusters, studies on
GPU SDC remain scarce. Prior GPU FI works typically target
control or scheduler logic via instruction-level injectors [43],
[44], limited to specific kernel types or CNN workloads. As
a result, comprehensive gate-level SDC characterization on
modern data center GPUs remains underexplored.
VIII. CONCLUSION
To
characterize
silent
data
corruption
(SDC)
on
a
production-class GPU, we conducted a large-scale gate-level
fault injection campaign, consuming over three million simu-
lator hours across a comprehensive CUDA micro-benchmark
suite. Across hardware units, fault sites, and data types, we
quantify corruption-type ratios (NaN, ±INF, nullification, and
bit-flips) and characterize bit-flip distributions in which single-
bit flips constitute a minority. We further identify warp-aligned
spatial periodicity at multiple scales. These findings indicate
that GPU SDC behavior is structured rather than independent,
motivating higher-level models that capture bit-level and spa-
tial dependencies. This work establishes a quantitative basis
for future GPU reliability analysis and SDC mitigation.
