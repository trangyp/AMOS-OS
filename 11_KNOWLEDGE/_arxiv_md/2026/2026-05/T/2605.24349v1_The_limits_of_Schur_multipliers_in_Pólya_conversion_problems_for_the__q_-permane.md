Universal linear conversion is not possible for the standard permanent in dimension n ≥3.
However, introducing a deformation parameter q and considering Schur multipliers enables us
to reconsider this rigidity. This paper investigates whether q-permanents permit conversions
that are unavailable in the classical case (q = 1), or whether structural obstructions persist
in higher dimensions.
Organization and contributions. Section 2 establishes the impossibility of general linear
conversion for n ≥3 (Theorem 2.1) and provides a classification of linear converters, together
with an algebraic–geometric interpretation, for the case n = 2 (Theorem 2.2). We show
that the solution space is a six- or five-dimensional variety parameterized by the complex
orthogonal group O(4, C), and that it decomposes into two distinct families of solutions.
Section 3 studies the set of preserver exponents (Theorem 3.1), viewed as a (2n −2)-
dimensional subspace of sln(R) defined by a discrete Monge condition. We show, among
others, that this space is closed under the symmetric ternary product, giving it the structure
of a Jordan triple system, and describe its topology for q on the unit circle, where it is
a union of affine lattices. Beyond general matrices, Section 4 shows that, for the class of
lower Hessenberg matrices, the q-permanent admits an explicit determinantal representation,
yielding an O(n3) algorithm for its evaluation (Theorem 4.2). It also determines the exact
Schur-multiplier component as an affine space for |q| ̸= 1 and a union of affine spaces if |q| = 1.
Section 5 establishes a dimensional threshold for conversions involving matrix permutations
(Theorem 5.2). While such conversions exist for all τ ∈Sn when n ≤3, they are restricted
to the dihedral group Dn for n ≥4 due to trace constraints.
Section 6 addresses the
mixed conversion problem that expresses the q-permanent as a linear combination of the
determinant and the permanent. Here, the solution space is nonempty exactly for n ≤4,
while for n ≥5 the constraints are incompatible and there are no solutions (Theorem 6.1).
This section also describes the zero set of the q-permanent in low dimensions and relates it
to the generalized Pólya problem. Section 7 concludes the paper with a summary and a
discussion of possible directions for future research.

We now explain geometrically why there are so many solutions when n = 2 using an
interpretation of the classification.
Theorem 2.1 shows that for n ≥3 the hypersurfaces {Perq(X) = 0} and {det(X) = 0}
are not isomorphic. The case n = 2 is special: in this dimension, both functions arise from
nondegenerate bilinear forms Bq and B−1 on C4. In this setting, converting the q-permanent
into the determinant amounts to describing the linear isometries
φ : (C4, Bq) →(C4, B−1).

multiplication, and this action is transitive. It follows that Cq is a principal homogeneous
space (torsor) under O(4, C), hence a smooth complex variety of dimension six. From a
projective viewpoint, the associated isotropic cones define quadrics in P3, and over C these
quadrics are all equivalent, which explains the existence of solutions in this case.

7. Concluding Remarks
This paper shows a sharp transition between low-dimensional algebraic flexibility and high-
dimensional rigidity in generalized Pólya conversion problems for the q-permanent. In low
dimensions, the deformation parameter q yields nontrivial affine families of Schur-multiplier
converters and explicit mixed determinant–permanent representations. In higher dimensions,
the combinatorial growth of inversion constraints conflicts with the flat additive structure of
Schur exponents, producing universal obstructions.
The results obtained suggest several directions for further investigation.
(1) Mixed conversion identities beyond dimension 4. The lack of mixed converter spaces for
n ≥5 raises the question of whether this threshold is specific to Schur multipliers, and
whether other linear transformations might avoid this obstruction.
(2) Classification of sparse matrix patterns admitting exact conversion. Theorem 4.2 gives
an explicit determinant conversion for lower Hessenberg matrices, yielding an O(n3)
polynomial-time algorithm for the q-permanent—and thus the permanent—on this class.
An open problem is to classify matrix patterns (e.g., Toeplitz-type and interval-pattern
matrices) that admit similar polynomial-time linear converters, or to identify the exact
threshold where the conversion problem becomes #P-hard.
(3) Connections with algebraic graph theory. The use of permutation–entry incidence matri-
ces and compatibility lattices suggests deeper links with combinatorial optimization and
graph theory (cf. Section 3.1). In particular, the relation between the incidence matrix
rank and competition numbers merits further study within algebraic graph theory.
(4) Extensions to other immanants.
The weight σ 7→qℓ(σ) is not a class function for
generic q, and does not define an immanant in the classical sense.
Nevertheless, it
