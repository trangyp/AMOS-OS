Abstract
Score-driven models update time-varying parameters using conditional likelihood scores.
This paper gives a Bayesian interpretation based on Tweedie’s formula. In Gaussian signal
extraction, Tweedie’s formula expresses the posterior correction as a scaled score of the marginal
predictive density; in natural exponential families, the corresponding identity includes a base-
measure adjustment. For general conditional densities, we show that inverse-Fisher-scaled
conditional scores arise as local Gaussian posterior corrections based on Fisher scoring and
precision discounting. For conjugate natural exponential families, the classical discounted
Bayesian recursion has an exact score-driven representation: with steady-state precision
discounting and expectation-space inverse-Fisher scaling, the score-driven correction equals the
Bayesian posterior mean before transition dynamics are imposed. Tweedie’s variance-function
index further clarifies how conditional scores normalize forecast errors. The results link
empirical Bayes, approximate filtering, dynamic generalized linear models, and score-driven
models while distinguishing exact Bayesian updating from local score-based approximation.
Keywords: Tweedie’s formula, score-driven models, empirical Bayes, observation-driven models, exponential
dispersion models, filtering, variance functions.

Introduction
Score-driven models provide a parsimonious way to introduce time variation in the parameters of
conditional distributions. In the terminology of Cox (1981), they are observation-driven models:
the current observation directly updates the parameter governing the next conditional density. Their
defining feature is that the update is proportional to the score of the conditional log likelihood with
respect to the time-varying parameter. This framework was developed systematically by Creal et al.
(2013) and Harvey (2013); see also Artemova et al. (2022) for a recent overview. It also has roots
in earlier score-based and robust filtering ideas, including the approximate non-Gaussian filter of
Masreliez (1975).
The appeal of score-driven models is well established. They generate fully observation-driven
recursions, so the likelihood is available by prediction-error decomposition. They adapt automatically
to the conditional density, since the score reflects the local shape of the likelihood. They also provide
robust updating in heavy-tailed models, where unusual observations may have bounded or attenuated
influence; see, for example, Harvey and Luati (2014). In addition, Blasques et al. (2015) show that
score-driven updates have an information-theoretic justification: the score direction locally reduces
a Kullback-Leibler discrepancy between the model and the data-generating process. Gorgi et al.
(2024) further develop this optimality perspective.
This paper offers a complementary Bayesian interpretation. The starting point is Tweedie’s
formula.
In the Gaussian location model, Tweedie’s formula shows that the posterior mean
correction from a noisy observation to the latent signal is exactly proportional to the score of the
marginal predictive density. The identity was reported by Robbins (1956), who credited personal
correspondence with M. C. K. Tweedie, and was later emphasized by Efron (2011) in the context of
empirical Bayes and selection bias. Thus, in this setting, the score is not merely a local likelihood
direction; it is the exact Bayesian correction that moves the observation toward the latent quantity of
interest.
The same idea extends beyond the Gaussian location case. For natural exponential families,
the generalized Tweedie identity expresses the posterior mean of the natural parameter as the
score of the marginal density after correcting for the base measure. This shows that the Gaussian
formula is not an isolated coincidence, but an instance of a broader exponential-family relationship

between posterior learning and marginal scores. A distinct but related contribution of Tweedie is the
variance-function index, which classifies exponential dispersion models by their variance functions
(Tweedie, 1984; Jørgensen, 1997). In the present context, this index clarifies how conditional scores
normalize forecast errors across observation densities.
The connection to score-driven models requires distinguishing two different scores. Tweedie’s
formula involves the score of the marginal predictive density with respect to the observation. Score-
driven models typically use the score of the conditional density with respect to the time-varying
parameter. In a dynamic model, the exact Bayesian update generally requires integration over the
latent state and is unavailable in closed form. Score-driven models can therefore be viewed as
replacing the unavailable marginal-score correction by a tractable scaled conditional-score correction
evaluated at the current predictive parameter.
We make this connection precise in two steps. First, for general conditional densities, we show
that an inverse-Fisher-scaled conditional score arises from a local Bayesian-Fisher approximation
based on a Gaussian predictive approximation, Fisher scoring, and steady-state precision discounting.
This result gives score-driven updating a local Bayesian interpretation: the score provides the
correction direction, while Fisher information supplies the local curvature adjustment. Second, in
a conjugate natural-exponential-family setting, the connection becomes exact. With steady-state
precision discounting and expectation-space inverse-Fisher scaling, the score-driven correction
coincides with the Bayesian posterior mean before transition dynamics are imposed. This identifies
the conditions under which a score-driven update is not merely a plug-in analogue, but coincides
algebraically with the Bayesian posterior mean.
The examples illustrate the scope of the argument. In the Gaussian location model, Tweedie’s
formula reproduces the Kalman update. In Gaussian variance models, the relevant sufficient statistic
is the squared observation, and inverse-Fisher scaling converts the variance score into the innovation
in squared observations; embedded in an observation-driven transition equation, this yields GARCH-
type recursions. In Poisson and Gamma models, the variance function determines how the conditional
score normalizes raw forecast errors, while conjugate-prior updating in expectation space delivers
the exact posterior-mean recursion under steady-state precision discounting.
The contribution is therefore both interpretive and formal. The filtering interpretation of
Masreliez (1975), the score-driven model classes of Creal et al. (2013) and Harvey (2013), and the

replacing the conditional parameter score by a corrected predictive-marginal observation score,

point for the approximation and equivalence results below.

Tweedie’s Index and Variance-Function Scaling
The preceding section used Tweedie’s formula to explain why marginal scores arise in Bayesian
signal extraction. We now turn to a second, distinct contribution by Tweedie: the classification of
exponential dispersion models through their variance functions (Tweedie, 1984; Jørgensen, 1987).
Tweedie’s formula explains why scores appear as Bayesian posterior corrections. Tweedie’s index
helps explain how conditional scores normalize forecast errors in exponential dispersion models.

score itself often normalizes raw forecast errors by the variance structure of the observation density.

Fisher information. Thus, two operations should be kept conceptually separate: variance-function
normalization inside the likelihood score, and score-driven scaling applied to that score in the
recursion.

The conjugate Bayesian recursion itself is classical in dynamic generalized linear models; see

The base-measure correction in Lemma 2 is not a missing term in Proposition 2. It appears because

By contrast, Proposition 2 uses the conditional likelihood score with respect to the expectation

the base-measure correction to a score-driven recursion, but by combining conjugate updating with
inverse-Fisher scaling in expectation space.

a natural exponential family, the prior must be conjugate, the prior strength must be at its discounted

must be used as the score scaling. The steady-state condition 𝑛𝑡|𝑡−1 = 𝛿/(1 −𝛿) is imposed as the

this local interpretation collapses to an exact identity in expectation space. The Tweedie perspective
therefore complements, rather than replaces, the dynamic generalized linear model literature and the
approximate-filtering and optimization-based foundations of score-driven models.

7
Conclusion
This paper connects Tweedie’s formula with score-driven updating. The main point is that scores enter
Bayesian signal extraction in a precise way: in canonical empirical-Bayes problems, the posterior
correction is a marginal-score object. In natural exponential families, this correction requires the
base-measure adjustment in Lemma 2. Separately, Tweedie’s variance-function index explains how
conditional scores normalize forecast errors through the variance structure of exponential dispersion
models.
Score-driven models use a different score. They update time-varying parameters with conditional
likelihood scores evaluated at the current predictive parameter, rather than with the generally
20


## Page 21


unavailable marginal observation score appearing in Tweedie’s formula. This distinction is essential.
It explains why score-driven recursions are not, in general, exact Bayesian filters, but also why they
can be viewed as tractable plug-in analogues of Tweedie-style posterior correction.
The paper formalizes this interpretation in two steps. Proposition 1 shows that, for general
conditional densities, an inverse-Fisher-scaled conditional score arises from a local Bayesian-Fisher
approximation: a Gaussian predictive approximation, a Fisher-scoring quadratic approximation to the
likelihood, and steady-state precision discounting. Proposition 2 identifies the special case in which
the approximation becomes exact. For natural exponential-family observations with conjugate priors,
steady-state precision discounting, and expectation-space inverse-Fisher scaling, the score-driven
correction coincides algebraically with the Bayesian posterior mean before transition dynamics are
imposed.
This Bayesian interpretation is distinct from the Kullback-Leibler optimality results of Blasques
et al. (2015) and Gorgi et al. (2024). Those results justify the score direction through local or global
information-theoretic improvement of the conditional model. More broadly, this places score-driven
updating alongside the quasi-likelihood and forecast-loss literatures, where the likelihood or loss
function determines the pseudo-true target and the criterion by which forecasts are evaluated; see, for
example, White (1994) and Patton (2020). The present argument instead identifies settings in which
the score correction has a Bayesian signal-extraction interpretation: exactly as a marginal-score
posterior correction in Tweedie’s formula, locally through Proposition 1, and algebraically through
Proposition 2 in the conjugate expectation-space case.
The resulting interpretation is hierarchical. In Gaussian and conjugate natural-exponential-
family signal-extraction problems, the score correction can be exact in expectation space. For
general conditional densities, it has a local Bayesian-Fisher interpretation. For broader score-driven
specifications, including heavy-tailed, skewed, copula, and correlation models, the score remains
natural because of likelihood shape, robustness, and Kullback-Leibler optimality rather than because
of Tweedie’s identity itself. Thus, Tweedie’s formula and Tweedie’s index do not replace existing
foundations of score-driven models; they provide a Bayesian lens that clarifies when score-based
updating is exact, when it is approximate, and why its scaling and parameterization matter.
