Abstract
Put–call parity is a terminal-payoff identity, but its enforcement is capital-using. I
study the carry gap, the annualized wedge between option-implied and OIS discount
factors, in SPX and RUT index options. Quoted parity is tightly compressed, while
the synthetic–traded forward channel leaves a systematic wedge. I interpret this wedge
as an implementation premium under finite arbitrage capital. A drift-preserving GBM
term, rˆµτ, improves in-sample and leave-one-year-out fit, especially in SPX. The evi-
dence suggests that physical drift enters not option payoffs, but the process enforcing
risk-neutral parity.

Introduction
Put–call parity is one of the most basic no-arbitrage relations in derivative pricing. For
European calls and puts with the same strike and maturity, the call–put spread is pinned
down by the corresponding forward position and discount factor (Stoll, 1969; Black and
Scholes, 1973; Merton, 1973; Gould and Galai, 1974). Because this relation is a static identity
over terminal payoffs, the physical-measure expected return of the underlying has no direct
role in the standard risk-neutral pricing argument. In this sense, put–call parity is closed by
a Q-measure pricing relation, not by the P-measure drift of the underlying.
This paper studies what is left once that terminal relation is enforced in actual markets. In
a frictionless benchmark, the option cross-section, the traded futures market, and benchmark
money-market rates would be jointly aligned: quoted put–call parity would hold, the option-
implied synthetic-forward channel would match the traded-futures channel, and the option-
implied discount factor would coincide with the benchmark discount factor.
With finite
arbitrage capital, however, these equalities need not be jointly costless to enforce.
The data suggest exactly this more nuanced pattern. As documented in Shin (2026),
quoted put–call parity is tightly compressed in price space, but the alignment between the
traded-futures channel and the OIS-discounted synthetic-forward channel is not equally tight.
Figure 1.1, reproduced from Shin (2026), illustrates the distinction. When the traded futures
price is used as the forward input, and the OIS discount factor is used as the benchmark
discount factor, the put–call parity residual is close to zero. When the same OIS discount
factor is imposed and the option cross-section is instead used to recover the synthetic for-
ward from call–put spreads, the resulting OIS-discounted synthetic-forward channel leaves a
systematic wedge relative to the traded-futures channel.

literal violation of spot–futures parity. Index options and index futures differ in contract

implementation costs are taken into account.

I study this wedge through the carry gap, defined as the annualized wedge between the
discount factor implied by the option cross-section and the benchmark OIS discount factor.
The object is measured in carry space rather than price space because quoted parity residuals
can be small even when the capital required to enforce parity is not economically costless.
Related evidence in Shin (2026) shows that the carry gap in SPX and RUT options is
systematically related to a GBM-based path-risk term, trading frictions, and broad financial
conditions. The key component of that specification is an rσ√τ support-capital term, which
summarizes the path-dependent capital burden implied by zero-drift Brownian motion.
This paper asks whether the same implementation-risk logic changes once the physical
drift of the underlying is preserved. If the carry gap reflects the cost of maintaining parity
under finite arbitrage capital, the wedge should depend not only on volatility and funding
rates, but also on the direction of the physical price path. The reason is that put–call parity
can be enforced from two sides.
The +C −P −F and −C + P + F positions enforce
the same terminal parity relation, but their futures-leg exposures have opposite signs along
the pre-maturity path. Physical drift therefore creates asymmetric variation-margin and
capital-support burdens across the two enforcement directions.

The drift-preserving extension follows from the same support-capital logic. In the zero-
drift case, the implementation burden scales as rσ√τ. If drift is preserved, a first-order
approximation adds a directional margin-burden component proportional to rµτ. Because
the true physical drift µ is unobserved, I use ˆµ, a prior rolling-OLS slope of the log total-return
index, and implement the drift component as rˆµτ. This term is not an observed expected
return. It is a reduced-form physical-drift proxy designed to test whether medium-horizon
price direction aligns with the directional margin burden of parity enforcement.
The answer is yes, with stronger evidence in SPX than in RUT. The drift-preserving
GBM term improves the baseline GBM specification in both in-sample regressions and leave-
one-year-out out-of-sample validation.
The improvement is not concentrated at a single
lookback window; across rolling-drift horizons, the explanatory gain forms a stable plateau
around medium horizons. In SPX, the maturity-bin evidence is also consistent with the
rˆµτ structure, as the incremental fit is small at short maturities and becomes more visible
at longer maturities. In RUT, the drift coefficient remains positive and stable, but the fit
improvement is smaller and less uniform. These results do not imply that physical drift
prices option payoffs directly. They suggest instead that the margin and capital burden of
enforcing put–call parity can be exposed to physical-measure price dynamics.
The paper makes three contributions. First, it reframes the carry gap as an implemen-
tation wedge that can arise when tightly compressed put–call parity coexists with imperfect
alignment between option-implied and traded forward channels. Second, it derives and tests
a drift-preserving extension of the GBM implementation-risk term: rσ√τ captures diffusion-
driven path risk, while rˆµτ captures directional drift burden in reduced form. Third, it shows
that this drift term has incremental explanatory power for SPX and RUT European index
options after controlling for path risk, trading frictions, and financial conditions.
The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 reviews the related literature. Section 3 presents
the GBM path-risk term and the drift-preserving extension. Section 4 describes the data and
empirical specifications. Section 5 summarizes the baseline GBM regression. Section 6 re-
ports the drift-extended results. Section 7 and Section 8 discuss the economic interpretation
and conclude.

Related Literature
This paper connects to five strands of literature: put–call parity, limits to arbitrage, option-
implied discount factors, the relation between risk-neutral objects and physical-measure
information, and implementation costs in no-arbitrage relations. The organizing distinction
is that put–call parity is a terminal-payoff identity, whereas the trading strategy that enforces
5


## Page 6


it before maturity is path-dependent and capital-using. I study this distinction in carry space
by asking whether the capital burden of parity enforcement is related not only to diffusion
risk, but also to a physical-drift proxy.
Put–call parity is a foundational no-arbitrage relation for European options. Stoll (1969)
formalizes the relation between call and put prices, while Black and Scholes (1973) and Mer-
ton (1973) provide the standard risk-neutral foundation for option pricing. Empirical work,
including Gould and Galai (1974), Klemkosky and Resnick (1979), and Ackert and Tian
(2001), examines how closely parity holds in actual markets and how observed deviations
are related to transaction costs, execution frictions, and market efficiency. The common
implication of this literature is that observed deviations need not represent failures of no-
arbitrage logic.
They may instead reflect the costs and constraints of implementing the
arbitrage in real markets.
I retain this view, but shift the object of analysis from price-space deviations to a carry-
space wedge. Rather than asking whether the call–put price difference violates the terminal
payoff identity, I study the annualized wedge between the discount factor implied by the
option cross-section and the benchmark OIS discount factor. This object, which I call the
carry gap, measures the implementation wedge embedded in parity-implied discounting.
The focus is therefore not whether put–call parity holds as a terminal relation, but whether
enforcing that relation before maturity leaves a systematic state-dependent cost.
A second strand studies limits to arbitrage and funding liquidity.
Since Shleifer and
Vishny (1997), this literature has emphasized that arbitrage is not performed instantly by
unconstrained representative investors. Gromb and Vayanos (2002) analyze price distortions
created by financially constrained arbitrageurs, Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) show how
market liquidity and funding liquidity can reinforce each other, and Mitchell and Pulvino
(2012) document that arbitrage strategies can suffer sharp losses and capital withdrawals
in stress periods. These papers show that trades that appear riskless at the terminal-payoff
level can still expose arbitrageurs to mark-to-market losses, margin requirements, funding
constraints, and liquidation risk along the path.
Limits to arbitrage are also important in options markets. Ofek et al. (2004) link short-
sale constraints to put–call parity violations, showing that arbitrage constraints can affect
relative option prices. Muravyev et al. (2025) further argue that some apparent single-stock
option signals reflect measurement problems caused by omitted stock borrow fees.
The
setting of this paper is deliberately different. I study European-style index options on SPX
and RUT, rather than single-stock options, and focus on the wedge between option-implied
and OIS discount factors, rather than on single-stock implied-volatility spreads or borrow-
fee-driven measurement errors. In this setting, visible price-space parity residuals against
6

traded futures are already tightly compressed. The question is therefore not why an obvious
arbitrage violation remains open, but why a systematic carry-space wedge remains after the
visible parity residual has largely been closed.
This distinction is central to the paper. The limits-to-arbitrage literature provides the
economic motivation: arbitrage uses capital, requires margin, and is exposed to the pre-
maturity path. The contribution here is to apply this logic to a cleaner parity setting. Even
when the price-space residual is compressed, the enforcing position must still be maintained
through interim price movements, variation margin, and finite-capital constraints. Thus,
successful enforcement can close the visible parity residual without eliminating the economic
cost of carrying the enforcement position. The carry gap is interpreted as a reduced-form
implementation wedge rather than as a conventional unclosed mispricing.
A third strand estimates implied discount rates or funding conditions from option prices.
Brenner and Galai (1986) reverse-engineered implied interest rates from option prices and
compared them with actual short-term rates, showing that implied rates can vary system-
atically with maturity. More directly, Azzone and Baviera (2021) use European put–call
parity to estimate option-implied discount factors and compare them with OIS benchmarks
through a synthetic-forward identification procedure. Closely related work by Shin (2026)
applies this approach to SPX and RUT options and documents that the resulting carry gap is
related to a GBM path-risk term, trading frictions, and financial conditions. That evidence
motivates the baseline implementation-risk structure used here.
The present paper differs by preserving physical drift in the same path-risk logic. The
baseline implementation-risk term has the form rσ√τ, which summarizes the diffusion-driven
support-capital burden of maintaining a parity-enforcement position.
If physical drift is
preserved, the same approximation implies an additional directional component proportional
to rµτ. Because the true physical drift µ is not observed, I implement this term empirically
as rˆµτ, where ˆµ is a prior rolling-OLS drift proxy constructed from the underlying total-
return path. The empirical question is whether this drift proxy explains the carry gap after
controlling for diffusion path risk, trading frictions, and financial conditions.
The paper also relates to work connecting risk-neutral objects with physical-measure
information.
Bollerslev et al. (2009) show that the variance risk premium is related to
expected stock returns, Ross (2015) provides conditions under which physical probabilities
can be recovered from state prices, and Martin (2017) shows that risk-neutral variance
extracted from option prices contains information about expected market returns. These
studies generally move from option-implied Q-measure objects toward physical probabilities,
risk premia, or expected returns.
My question is complementary but different.
I do not recover physical probabilities

or expected returns from option prices. Nor do I argue that physical drift directly prices
option payoffs. Put–call parity remains a risk-neutral terminal-payoff relation. Instead, I
ask whether the carry-space residual associated with that Q-measure relation is exposed
to physical-measure price dynamics through the arbitrageur’s path-dependent margin and
capital burden. In this sense, the paper studies the P-measure implementation path behind
a Q-measure parity relation.
Finally, the paper connects to implementation costs in no-arbitrage relations. Du et al.
(2018) show that covered interest rate parity deviations persist after the global financial crisis
and are related to intermediary balance-sheet costs and funding constraints. The market and
measurement object are different, but the economic issue is similar. A no-arbitrage relation
may remain valid at the terminal-payoff level while its enforcement is affected by capital
constraints, margin requirements, funding conditions, and interim price paths.
In sum, this paper uses the carry-space wedge between option-implied and OIS discount
factors to test whether the path-dependent capital burden of put–call-parity enforcement is
related to a physical-drift proxy. Where prior work studies price-space deviations, implied
discount rates, or zero-drift implementation risk, I derive and test a drift-preserving GBM
approximation that adds an rµτ component. Empirically, this component is implemented as
rˆµτ, where ˆµ is a prior rolling-OLS drift proxy rather than an observed expected return. The
evidence should therefore be read not as a failure of risk-neutral parity, but as reduced-form
evidence that the capital-using mechanism enforcing a risk-neutral relation can be exposed
to physical-measure price dynamics.
3
Path-Risk Term and Drift-Extended Specification
This section develops the GBM-based empirical specification for the carry gap. The GBM
terms are not auxiliary controls; they summarize the path-dependent capital burden created
when put–call parity is enforced as a trading strategy. Although put–call parity is a termi-
nal payoff identity, the enforcing position must be maintained along the pre-maturity price
path and is therefore exposed to daily settlement, variation margin, interim losses, fund-
ing costs, and finite-capital constraints. Parity enforcement is therefore a path-dependent
implementation problem, not merely a static no-arbitrage condition.
The derivation begins with a zero-drift Brownian approximation to the interim profit-
and-loss process of a parity-enforcement position. In that case, required support capital
is driven by diffusion risk and scales with σ√τ, so the opportunity cost of tied-up capital
generates the standard rσ√τ path-risk term. Preserving physical drift in the same support-
capital calculation leaves the diffusion component in place but adds a first-order directional

the forms rσ√τ and rµτ.

I use ˆµ, a proxy obtained from the rolling OLS slope of past log total-return paths. The drift
term is not intended to identify the true expected return structurally. It is a reduced-form
regressor that tests whether an observable historical-drift proxy aligns with the directional
margin burden of parity enforcement.

3.1
A Motivating Example
Before deriving the GBM path-risk term, consider an arbitrageur, Bob, who observes a
put–call parity wedge and enters
+C −P −F.
At initiation, the position appears nearly self-contained. The call and put positions form
a synthetic forward, and the futures leg offsets much of the resulting directional exposure.
Aggregate Greek exposure, and in an idealized margin calculation the required net margin,
can therefore be small relative to the gross legs. Bob appears to have opened an almost
costless parity-enforcement trade.
The difficulty is that the terminal payoff is not the implementation path. The options
generate their main cash flow at maturity, but the futures leg is settled daily. If the index
rises and Bob’s short futures leg generates a variation-margin loss of 1,000 dollars, he must
post cash immediately by borrowing or drawing on internal liquidity. This cash carries a
funding cost, an opportunity cost, or both. If the index falls the next day and Bob receives
2,000 dollars through variation margin, he can redeploy the surplus in a money-market
account. The cash-flow direction has reversed, but the implementation problem remains:
until maturity, Bob must absorb negative margin flows, finance cash shortfalls, and redeploy
temporary surpluses.
The arbitrage payoff is therefore not evaluated only at maturity. Even when the terminal
payoff is deterministic, the daily settlement path of the futures leg creates interim funding
needs. The GBM path-risk term is a reduced-form way to summarize this support-capital
burden.

3.4
Drift Asymmetry and Carry-Gap–Index Comovement
The drift term helps interpret the comovement between cumulative carry-gap accrual and
the total-return index in Figure 1.2. Parity can be enforced in two directions:
+C −P −F,
−C + P + F.
These positions enforce the same parity relation from opposite sides, but the sign of the
futures leg is reversed. Their variation-margin burden under physical drift therefore moves
in opposite directions.
For example, when µ > 0, the +C −P −F direction, which includes a short futures leg,
is exposed to an adverse mark-to-market path, whereas the −C + P + F direction benefits
from the same drift environment. When µ < 0, the asymmetry reverses. Drift can therefore
act as a state variable determining which side of parity enforcement bears the larger path
risk and capital-support burden.
This asymmetry provides a natural mechanism for cumulative carry-gap accrual to co-
move with the total-return index. During persistent positive-drift regimes, the cost of enforc-
ing parity in one direction can rise systematically; during negative-drift regimes, the relevant
enforcement direction reverses. Figure 1.2 should therefore not be interpreted as evidence
that parity itself fails. It suggests that the drift-sensitive margin burden of two-sided parity
enforcement may accumulate asymmetrically.
This paper does not structurally identify the full mechanism. The drift-preserving GBM
derivation only shows which reduced-form term appears when true µ is not removed from
the support-capital calculation. Because true µ is unobserved, the resulting rˆµτ term is a
natural empirical candidate for explaining the comovement in Figure 1.2.

3.6
Economic Interpretation of the Regressors
The model decomposes the implementation burden of parity enforcement into diffusion path
risk and directional drift burden. The baseline GBM term,
GBM σ ∼rσ√τ,
captures the scale of random path risk determined by volatility, maturity, and the opportu-
nity cost of capital. The drift term,
GBM ˆµ ∼rˆµτ,
proxies for the accumulation of directional physical drift in the margin burden of the enforcing
position.
The coefficients are not fixed at one. Put–call parity can be enforced from both directions:
+C−P−F and −C+P+F enforce the same terminal relation, but their futures-leg exposures
have opposite signs. The observed carry gap can therefore be interpreted as the net result of
opposing arbitrage pressures after partial offsetting. The coefficients on GBM σ and GBM ˆµ
are reduced-form loadings measuring how diffusion path risk and directional drift burden
pass through into the observed carry gap.

coefficient on GBM ˆµ,OIS,1Y , however, should be interpreted as a reduced-form pass-through

4.3
Drift Horizon and Evaluation Method
The rolling drift proxy depends on the lookback horizon n. I estimate the drift-extended
specification over multiple values of n and examine how in-sample R2 and leave-one-year-
out out-of-sample R2 vary across horizons. The horizon scan is not used to select a single
pointwise optimum, but to assess whether the drift proxy delivers stable explanatory power
over a broad horizon band.
The main specification uses n = 504 trading days, roughly two trading years. This choice
is naturally interpreted as a medium-horizon physical-drift proxy rather than a short-term
return-noise measure. I apply the same horizon to SPX and RUT to restrict specification
flexibility and maintain a common empirical design across markets.
I compare the baseline and drift-extended specifications using three criteria: in-sample
R2, adjusted R2, RMSE, and MAE; leave-one-year-out out-of-sample R2, RMSE, MAE,
and fitted–actual correlation; and date-based HAC(Newey–West) coefficient inference with

generic return predictor, but as a reduced-form implementation-risk component measuring

7
Discussion
The results do not contradict the fact that put–call parity is a terminal payoff identity. The
point is the opposite. Put–call parity remains a strong no-arbitrage relation at maturity, but
the trade that enforces it in actual markets must be maintained along the pre-maturity price
path. Parity enforcement is therefore not only a static pricing equation. It is also a dynamic
implementation problem involving variation margin, interim profits and losses, funding, and
finite-capital constraints.
From this perspective, the carry gap is the carry-space location at which implementation
frictions can remain visible after quoted price-space parity residuals have been compressed.
32


## Page 33


The estimated rˆµτ term is a reduced-form proxy for one component of this implementation
layer: the directional margin burden generated by physical-measure drift.
7.1
Enforcement Costs and the P–Q Boundary
Standard no-arbitrage logic begins from the condition that two portfolios with the same
terminal payoff must have the same price. This logic is complete under the usual frictionless
assumptions: continuous trading, no transaction costs, unconstrained funding, no margin
constraints, and immediate arbitrage enforcement. In real markets, however, an arbitrageur
does not hold only the terminal payoff. The arbitrageur must also survive the path leading
to that payoff. Interim losses require variation-margin payments, and insufficient capital can
force position reduction before the theoretically certain terminal payoff is reached.
Identical terminal payoffs therefore do not imply identical implementation paths. This
distinction is especially relevant for put–call parity enforcement. The two enforcement di-
rections,
+C −P −F
and
−C + P + F,
enforce the same parity relation from opposite sides, but their futures-leg exposures have
opposite signs. When µ > 0, the direction containing a short futures leg faces a relatively
adverse margin path; when µ < 0, the burden can shift to the opposite direction. This
asymmetry disappears at the terminal payoff level, but remains relevant along the path over
which the position must be maintained.
The drift-preserving term is designed to capture this implementation layer in reduced
form. The diffusion path-risk term,
rσ√τ,
summarizes the support-capital burden generated by random path variation.

efficient on this term does not imply that physical drift prices option payoffs directly. It
suggests instead that the cost of enforcing a risk-neutral terminal relation can depend on
physical-measure price dynamics through variation margin, funding needs, and finite-capital
constraints.
In this sense, the “P behind Q” is not a claim that risk-neutral pricing collapses. Put–call
parity is a Q-measure terminal-payoff relation, and the carry gap is constructed from option-
implied and OIS benchmark discount factors. The claim is narrower: the capital-support

technology required to implement this relation is realized along the physical price path. The
carry gap therefore appears to reflect a Q-measure residual whose observed dynamics are
partly aligned with a P-measure drift proxy.
7.2
Joint Enforcement Across Parity Relations
The implementation-cost interpretation also helps explain why quoted put–call parity can
appear tightly closed while a related forward-channel wedge remains visible. In a frictionless
benchmark, put–call parity, synthetic-forward identification, the traded futures channel, and
the benchmark discounting curve are jointly pinned down by the same no-arbitrage logic.
If trading is costless, funding is unconstrained, and arbitrage capital is unlimited, the call–
put spread, the traded forward channel, and the benchmark discounting curve should all be
mutually aligned.
With implementation frictions, however, these equalities are not jointly costless to en-
force. The risk associated with enforcing one relation must be borne by some balance sheet,
margin account, funding line, or intermediary capital constraint. Arbitrage pressure may
therefore close the relation that is cheapest to enforce while leaving a wedge in the channel
that is more capital-intensive or institutionally costly to arbitrage.
This provides a natural interpretation of the empirical pattern motivating this paper.
Quoted put–call parity is the more direct option-market relation and can often be com-
pressed within a relatively tight option-market enforcement channel. By contrast, aligning
the OIS-discounted synthetic-forward channel with the traded-futures channel can require
broader balance-sheet capacity, including futures exposure, margin financing, dividend and
settlement risks, inventory constraints, and funding liquidity. Finite arbitrage capital may
therefore be allocated first to closing the visible put–call parity residual, while the remaining
implementation burden appears as a wedge in the synthetic–traded forward or discounting
channel.
Under this interpretation, the carry gap is not evidence that put–call parity itself fails.
Equivalently, the option-implied discount factor need not equal the OIS discount factor
once the cost of enforcing the full set of related parity relations is taken into account. The
difference can be interpreted as an implementation premium: compensation for funding
costs, path risk, trading frictions, and capital usage not captured by the frictionless OIS
benchmark.
This interpretation should not be read as a literal claim that spot–futures parity is
violated. For index products, contract design, settlement conventions, dividend exposure,
and timing differences make the strict textbook relation difficult to map one-for-one into the

data. The more conservative claim is that the option-implied synthetic-forward channel and
the traded-futures channel are not perfectly aligned after implementation costs. The results
suggest that part of this wedge is related to volatility, funding conditions, trading frictions,
and the drift-sensitive margin burden captured by rˆµτ.

7.4
Limitations of the Interpretation
The interpretation has several limitations. First, the rˆµτ term is a reduced-form regressor,
not a structurally identified primitive. Future physical drift is inherently unobserved, so ˆµ is
approximated using the rolling OLS slope of past log total-return paths. The coefficient on
GBM ˆµ,OIS,1Y is therefore not an estimate of the expected return perceived by arbitrageurs,
an equilibrium risk premium, or a structural drift parameter. It is a reduced-form loading
that measures how medium-horizon price direction comoves with the directional margin
burden of parity enforcement.
Second, the explanatory power of the drift term is not identical across markets.
In
SPX, the improvement in both in-sample and leave-one-year-out out-of-sample performance
is clear. In RUT, the sign and statistical significance of the coefficient are stable, but the
incremental explanatory power is smaller. This suggests that even if the drift channel is
common across markets, the intensity with which it is transmitted into the carry gap may
depend on liquidity, investor composition, maturity structure, and the nature of enforcement
capital.
Third, the paper does not propose a replacement for the Black–Scholes–Merton frame-
work or for standard no-arbitrage pricing. Its contribution is more limited. If the standard
model provides an idealized frictionless benchmark, this paper shows that the path-dependent
capital burden of implementing the benchmark relation is empirically visible. A full struc-
tural model of parity-enforcement pricing, with endogenous margin constraints, funding con-
straints, direction-specific capital burdens, and a clearer separation between true expected
returns and empirical drift proxies, is left for future research.
8
Conclusion
This paper studies put–call parity as an enforced no-arbitrage relation rather than only as a
terminal-payoff identity. In a frictionless benchmark, put–call parity, the synthetic-forward
channel, the traded futures channel, and the benchmark discounting curve are jointly aligned
36


## Page 37


by no-arbitrage. In actual markets, however, these relations are not jointly costless to enforce.
A parity-enforcement position must be carried through the pre-maturity path and is exposed
to variation margin, interim profits and losses, trading frictions, funding conditions, and
finite-capital constraints. The carry-space wedge between option-implied and OIS discount
factors can therefore reflect the implementation burden left after quoted put–call parity has
been compressed, rather than a failure of the terminal no-arbitrage identity.
The main contribution is to extend the diffusion-based GBM path-risk term by preserv-
ing physical drift in the support-capital approximation. Under zero-drift Brownian motion,
the implementation burden scales with rσ√τ. When drift is preserved, the first-order ap-
proximation adds a directional margin-burden component proportional to rµτ.
Because
true physical drift is unobservable, the empirical specification uses ˆµ, a prior-only rolling
OLS slope of past log total-return paths. Thus, GBM ˆµ,OIS,1Y is not an observed expected
return, but a reduced-form drift proxy designed to test whether medium-horizon physical
price direction aligns with the capital burden of parity enforcement.
The evidence supports this interpretation, especially in SPX. Adding the one-year-OIS-
scaled drift term improves the baseline carry-gap model after controlling for the OIS-based
diffusion path-risk block, trading frictions, and broad financial conditions. The improvement
appears both in sample and under leave-one-year-out out-of-sample validation. The horizon
scan shows that the result is not tied to a single knife-edge lookback window, but appears
over a stable medium-horizon region around two trading years. In SPX, the maturity-bin
evidence is also consistent with the τ-scaled structure of rˆµτ: the incremental fit is small at
short maturities and becomes more visible at longer maturities. In RUT, the drift coefficient
remains positive and stable, but the improvement in fit is smaller and less uniform across
maturities.
These findings do not imply that physical drift prices option payoffs directly, nor do they
challenge risk-neutral pricing. Put–call parity remains a terminal no-arbitrage identity. The
claim is instead about the empirical process that maintains this identity in real markets. A
parity trade may be deterministic at maturity, but the capital required to keep the trade alive
is committed along the physical price path. In this sense, the Q-measure relation closes the
terminal payoff, while the arbitrage mechanism that enforces it can depend on P-measure
dynamics through variation margin, funding needs, and finite-capital constraints.
The fitted carry gap also has a practical price-space interpretation.
Since the carry
gap is an annualized log discount-factor wedge, a fitted value can be mapped into a state-
dependent price-space hurdle by multiplying the unannualized wedge by the relevant forward
scale. A parity arbitrageur should therefore not compare a quoted price residual only with
zero, but with the implementation hurdle implied by funding costs, volatility, maturity,
37


## Page 38


trading frictions, financial conditions, and drift-sensitive margin burden. The model is not
a mechanical trading rule, but it provides a reduced-form screening heuristic for evaluating
whether a parity residual is large enough to compensate for the estimated cost of enforcement.
Future work should develop a structural model of parity-enforcement pricing that explic-
itly incorporates variation margin, funding constraints, direction-specific capital burdens,
arbitrage-capital pass-through, and the allocation of finite arbitrage capital across related
no-arbitrage relations. Such a model should also separate true conditional expected returns
from empirical drift proxies more explicitly. This paper provides a first step by documenting
reduced-form evidence that physical-measure drift enters not the terminal pricing relation
of put–call parity, but the capital-using process through which that relation is enforced.

A
Implementation Details of the Pipeline
This appendix summarizes the implementation procedure used to identify option-implied
discount factors and construct the carry gap. The main identification logic and regression
specifications are described in the text. Here I focus on data processing and sample con-
struction.
A.1
Data and Sample
I identify option-implied discount factors from SPX and RUT European-style index options
using the synthetic-forward procedure of Azzone and Baviera (2021). The option data are
minute-level NBBO quotes from ThetaData. The sample is restricted to the common period
in which both option quotes and the OIS curve are observed. Because SPX and RUT options
are European-style index options, early-exercise premia do not directly enter the put–call-
parity-based identification.
A.2
Identification of Option-Implied Discount Factors
For each date–maturity cell, I form call–put pairs with the same strike and maturity. I
then compute the synthetic forward as the difference between the call and put mid quotes.
The option-implied discount factor and forward value are jointly recovered by estimating the
linear relation between synthetic forwards and strikes across the strike cross-section.
This procedure has two advantages. First, it does not rely on a single ATM contract or
an arbitrary moneyness interval, but uses the cross-section of options at the same maturity.
Second, because the forward value is identified jointly from option prices, the procedure
does not require a separate dividend estimate as a direct external input. It also mitigates
nonsynchronicity problems that can arise when spot, futures, dividend, and interest-rate
data are combined from separate sources.
A.3
OIS Benchmark and Carry Gap
The benchmark discount factor is constructed from the daily OIS curve. I apply a standard
bootstrapping procedure to recover discount factors by maturity and then match the OIS
discount factor to each option maturity. As defined in the main text, the carry gap is the
annualized log wedge between the option-implied discount factor and the OIS discount factor.
The empirical analysis uses the basis-point-scaled version of this wedge.

A.4
Sample Filters and Daily Aggregation
The preprocessing focuses on minimal filters that improve the stability of cross-sectional
identification. I exclude observations without a valid call–put pair at the same strike and
maturity.
I also remove quotes with extremely low prices or excessive bid–ask spreads.
Date–maturity cells with too few valid strikes are excluded, as are dates on which OIS curve
construction fails.
The final sample is a market–date–maturity panel in which both the option-implied
discount factor and the matched OIS benchmark discount factor can be constructed reliably.
For figures and diagnostic exercises that require daily market-level series, I aggregate valid
maturity cells within each date by taking the median carry gap. Median aggregation reduces
the influence of outliers and transitory quote noise.
A.5
Drift Proxy and Regression Evaluation
The physical-drift proxy used in the drift-extended specification is constructed using the
prior-only rolling OLS slope procedure described in the main text. For date t, the drift
proxy is computed only from the log total-return path through t −1, which prevents look-
ahead bias. The main specification applies a common 504-trading-day lookback horizon to
both SPX and RUT.
Regression inference uses date-based HAC(Newey–West) standard errors. The main re-
sults use a maximum lag of 21 trading days. Out-of-sample performance is evaluated using
leave-one-year-out validation. For each calendar year, I exclude that year as the holdout
sample, estimate the regression coefficients on the remaining years, and compute out-of-
sample R2, RMSE, MAE, and fitted–actual correlation in the excluded year. This procedure
tests whether the improvement from the drift-preserving term is only an in-sample fit gain
or whether it is repeatedly preserved across year-by-year sample splits.
