The Incommensurability Principle in Biological Transport:
A No-Go Theorem, Metabolic Gauge Invariance,
and the Architectural Origin of the Minimax
Riccardo Marchesi
University of Pavia
May 6, 2026
Abstract
Biological vascular networks exhibit branching exponents (α∗≈2.72) conserved across
developmental stages and observed in multiple mammalian species [1, 2], despite vast
metabolic and anatomical variation. We prove this universality is a mathematical necessity
arising from the physical incommensurability of optimization constraints.
We establish three theorems. (1) No-Go Theorem: Local optimization combining extensive
metabolic costs with dimensionless wave-reflection penalties requires a coupling parameter
varying by 102–103 across the hierarchy, precluding universal exponents. (2) Metabolic Gauge
Invariance: The unique dimensionless cost functional consistent with scale invariance and
thermodynamic linearity is the fractional metabolic excess; alternative penalties (logarithmic
measures) fail empirical validation. (3) Architectural Invariance: The minimax duty cycle η∗
is an exact invariant of the allometric class A(G, p, αw), orthogonal to absolute metabolic
scales—explaining developmental stability.
The minimax emerges as the unique attractor for networks optimizing physically in-
commensurable costs, unifying previous single-mechanism results as degenerate boundary
cases.

Introduction
Papers I and II of this series established that the physiological branching exponent α∗≈2.72
of the porcine coronary tree emerges as the unique minimax saddle point of a network-level
Lagrangian that combines viscous transport costs and wave-reflection penalties [3, 4]. The
Lagrangian was constructed by normalising both cost functions to dimensionless fractional
excesses before optimising jointly over the branching exponent α and a duty cycle η weighting
pulsatile against steady-flow regimes. The result reproduced the Kassab [1] dataset without
free parameters. Paper III [5] extended the framework to derive Kleiber’s metabolic scaling law
as a corollary of the same branching geometry. These successes, however, left a foundational
question unanswered: why must the network-level Lagrangian take precisely this form? Is the
fractional-excess normalisation a modelling convenience, or the only admissible choice consistent
with the underlying physics?
The branching geometry of biological transport networks is governed by a competition
between physically incommensurable cost functions. In arterial trees, viscous dissipation and
wall-tissue metabolic maintenance enter as extensive quantities measured in watts, while wave-
reflection penalties enter as dimensionless reflection coefficients — a global network observable.
These two quantities cannot be added directly; they inhabit different physical spaces.

Previous work has addressed this tension by introducing phenomenological weighting
parameters [6, 7, 3, 4, 8] or alternative coupling indices [9, 10], but no study has asked whether
such a local combination is mathematically admissible in the first place. The present paper
answers this question negatively and constructively: we prove that local incommensurable
optimization is impossible (Theorem 1), identify the unique admissible network-level functional
(Theorem 2), and show that the resulting minimax saddle point produces an architectural
invariant that resolves a long-standing biological paradox (Theorem 3).
The logical chain is as follows. The No-Go Theorem (Section 2) establishes that any coupling
parameter µ required by dimensional consistency at a single junction must vary by several orders
of magnitude (∼102–103) across the vascular hierarchy, precluding a universal α∗. This impossi-
bility forces the optimization to the network level, where both costs become dimensionless. The
Metabolic Gauge Invariance Theorem (Section 3) proves that the normalization used in Paper II
is the only one consistent with scale invariance and thermodynamic linearity. The Architectural
Invariance Theorem (Section 4) then shows that the minimax duty cycle η∗emerging from this
framework is immune to metabolic fluctuations, providing a first-principles explanation for the
developmental stability of vascular geometry. Finally, the corollary (Section 5) demonstrates
that Papers I and II are the exact limiting cases of a single variational principle.

Discussion: The Minimax as a Universal Biological Attractor
The three theorems of this paper form a complete logical chain. Theorem 1 proves that local
optimization of physically incommensurable costs is ill-posed: any coupling parameter must
diverge across the hierarchy, destroying the universality of the branching exponent. This is not
a failure of a specific model but a structural mathematical impossibility.
Theorem 2 identifies the unique escape from this impossibility: lifting the optimization to
the network level, where both cost functions become dimensionless fractional excesses. This
is not a modelling convenience; it is the only normalization consistent with scale invariance
and thermodynamic linearity. The analogy with gauge theories in physics is precise: just as
electrodynamics is forced to be gauge-invariant by the requirement that physical observables
be independent of the choice of potential, biological transport optimization is forced to be
metabolically gauge-invariant by the requirement that architectural decisions be independent of
absolute physiological scale.
Theorem 3 reveals the deepest consequence: once both costs are gauge-invariant, their
minimax balance η∗becomes an architectural eigenvalue of the allometric class — immune
to the fluctuations of body mass, metabolic rate, and haemodynamic load that characterize
ontogenesis. This explains why α∗is conserved across developmental stages: not because the
organism actively maintains it, but because the minimax saddle point is topologically stable
under metabolic perturbations.
It bears emphasis that Axiom 3 of Theorem 2 is a statement about the physical cost of
entropy production, grounded in the stoichiometric linearity of oxidative phosphorylation,
not about the fitness landscape. The evolutionary interpretation of the minimax as a selected
optimum relies on the additional assumption that fitness penalties are monotonically increasing
in thermodynamic excess — a condition satisfied by any organism for which metabolic waste is
not advantageous.
Independent empirical support for the two-attractor structure comes from recent whole-
organism 3D vascular mapping: Forjaz, Kiemen & Wirtz [14] report branching exponents
converging to γ ≈3 in mature vasculature and γ ≈2 in immature developmental networks,
corresponding to the static (η →0) and wave-dominated (η →1) limits of the unified Lagrangian
predicted by the Corollary.

Within the class of hierarchical networks subject to a power-law transport cost ∆Φ ∝
Qnr−(n+m) and a dimensionless wave-reflection penalty, the minimax is the unique mathe-
matically consistent attractor. Whether this conclusion extends to other classes of incommen-
surable biological costs—such as information capacity versus structural investment in neural
networks—requires case-specific verification that the coupling parameter µ exhibits the requisite
scale-dependence across the relevant hierarchy, and constitutes an open problem beyond the
scope of this work.
Remark 7 (Incommensurability and channel distinguishability). We say two cost channels are
incommensurable if and only if they are distinguishable under the gauge group Λ: no rescaling
⃗θ →Λ⃗θ maps one cost function to the other while preserving the scale-independence of the
optimisation. This is exactly the condition that prevents µ from being absorbed into a redefinition
of either cost, and it is the content of Theorem 2 that each distinguishable channel contributes an
independently gauge-invariant fractional excess. Extending this correspondence to networks in
which the distinguishable cost dimensions are information capacity and structural investment
requires verifying that their coupling exhibits the scale-dependence of Eq. (7) across the relevant
hierarchy; this constitutes an open problem that the present framework renders sharply well-
posed [9, 10].
This perspective connects the present framework to the theory of robust optimization [12]
and to Prigogine’s theorem of minimum entropy production [15]: biological networks appear to
minimize not absolute dissipation (which would require a single incommensurable cost) but the
worst-case fractional excess across incommensurable cost dimensions. The minimax is, in this
sense, the thermodynamic signature of life.
7
Conclusion
We have proved that the network-level minimax framework of Papers I–II is not an elegant
choice but a mathematical necessity. Three theorems establish this progression:
1. No-Go Theorem: local incommensurable optimization requires a coupling parameter
varying by 102–103 across the vascular hierarchy, rendering a universal α∗impossible.
2. Metabolic Gauge Invariance: the fractional excess cost is the unique dimensionless
functional consistent with scale invariance and thermodynamic linearity.
3. Architectural Invariance: the minimax duty cycle η∗is an exact invariant of the allometric
class, explaining the developmental stability of vascular geometry from first principles.
The corollary recovers Papers I and II as the exact limiting cases η →0 and η →1, confirming
that the unified variational principle subsumes all previous single-mechanism results. The
companion study [5] extends the minimax framework to whole-organism allometric scaling,
recovering Kleiber’s 3/4-power law as a network-geometric consequence. Beyond vascular
biology, the incommensurability argument applies to any biological network optimizing across
physically distinct cost dimensions, suggesting that the minimax is the universal thermodynamic
signature of adaptive biological architecture.
