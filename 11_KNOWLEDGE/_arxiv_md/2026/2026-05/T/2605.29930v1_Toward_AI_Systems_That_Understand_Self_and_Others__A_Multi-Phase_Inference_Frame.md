Abstract
Mutual misunderstanding in contemporary society does not arise merely because people hold
different opinions or values. Even when subjects receive the same sequence of observations, they
may endogenously select different inferential targets, preserve different state representations,
evaluate different prediction errors as salient, and permit different update paths. This paper
formulates that phenomenon as a critique of the Single Intelligence Assumption (SIA): the ten-
dency to centralize intelligence around a single privileged mode of reasoning, to treat that mode
as normative, and to expect appropriately rational agents to converge on the same conclusion
from the same input. Under finite data, partial observability, representational constraints, and
heterogeneous action constraints, however, the same observations can support multiple coherent
world-model updates.
I formalize this position as the Multi-Phase Inference Assumption (MIA). This paper proposes
a multi-phase inference framework and defines its core internal mechanism as the Multi-Phase
Inference Mechanism (MIM). The central devices are: (i) a phase-formation space Zphase in
which reference targets and resolution modes are jointly organized; (ii) a foregrounding field
R, understood as a gradient-like directional field over this space, that biases how prediction
errors move processing toward particular inferential targets and state representations; and (iii)
an alignment map Φ that makes state representations formed in one subject’s world model
processable within another’s. The mechanism describes cognitive diversity not as error, irra-
tionality, or mere subjective preference, but as the constructive fact that different inferential
targets, state representations, prediction errors, error costs, and update paths can arise from
the same observations. I also introduce a dynamic loop from observation to target formation,
state-representation extraction, planning, action, re-observation, and feedback update, thereby
locating action, reflection, and coordination within a broader path toward generative modeling.
This reframes AI alignment not only as the problem of adapting AI systems to a single account
of human value, but also as the problem of making heterogeneous human world models mutually
processable. In this sense, understanding is not the sharing of an identical representation, but
the reconstruction of a state representation into a form that can be processed within another
world model. The framework also offers a way to restate long-standing disputes in epistemology,
semantics, ethics, social philosophy, cognitive typology, and social fragmentation in terms of
inferential targets, state representations, prediction errors, error costs, update paths, and align-
ment. The aim is not to settle philosophical disputes by choosing one side, but to formalize
some of the conditions under which such disputes are generated. The paper thereby connects
world-model research, the free-energy principle, active inference, AI alignment, philosophical

Introduction
1.1
Why do different worlds arise from the same observations?
The problem addressed in this paper is ordinary before it is technical. People can attend the same
meeting, read the same report, watch the same news segment, or encounter the same social-media
post and its reactions, yet what becomes salient for them may differ profoundly. One person may
see an operational bottleneck; another may see a violation of trust; another may see evidence of a
long historical pattern; another may feel that the dignity of a group has been harmed; and another
may find almost nothing of consequence in the same event. The input may be shared. The world
that is constructed from it is not shared by default.
This difference is often described as a difference of opinion, value, ideology, personality, or
background knowledge. These descriptions are sometimes useful, but they tend to begin too late. By
the time an opinion is stated, an inferential target has already been selected, a state representation
has already been formed, certain prediction errors have already been assigned cost, and some update
paths have already been treated as permissible while others have been suppressed.
A subject
does not merely attach a different judgment to a common representation.
In many cases, the
representation itself is different.
This distinction matters for AI. Large language models and other AI systems increasingly me-
diate meetings, documents, education, public discussion, interpersonal explanation, and political
disagreement. If such systems treat understanding as the recovery of a single intended proposition,
4


## Page 5


they will reproduce a familiar failure: they will make some representations legible while discarding
others as noise, emotion, irrationality, or irrelevant context. If, instead, they can model why dif-
ferent inferential targets and state representations arise, they may help subjects recognize what is
being preserved or lost across perspectives. The aim is not to make everyone agree. It is to make
disagreement processable.
This paper therefore asks why the same observation sequence can generate different inferential
worlds. The answer proposed here is computational and constructive: subjects differ in what they
estimate, how they represent it, which errors matter, how costly those errors are, and what forms of
action, reflection, suspension, or coordination are available for absorbing them. The problem of mu-
tual understanding is thus reframed as a problem of world-model operation and state-representation
alignment.
1.2
From SIA to MIA
Many institutions, educational settings, organizational discussions, and AI systems implicitly op-
erate with a weak version of what I call the Single Intelligence Assumption (SIA). SIA is not a
doctrine explicitly endorsed by all contemporary AI research. It is a recurring normative tendency:
intelligence is centered on a privileged form of abstract symbolic or logical reasoning; that form is
treated as the standard of proper understanding; and deviation from it is easily interpreted as error,
lack of knowledge, lack of effort, or moral failure.
SIA is not simply false. Shared abstraction, formal reasoning, symbolic representation, and insti-
tutional standards are indispensable achievements. They make science, law, education, engineering,
and coordination possible. The problem arises when these achievements are generalized into a the-
ory of intelligence itself. Under partial observability, finite data, limited representational resources,
heterogeneous embodiment, and different action constraints, the same observation sequence can
support multiple coherent world-model updates.
I therefore introduce the Multi-Phase Inference Assumption (MIA). MIA does not say that
all conclusions are equally valid. It says that intelligent systems need not perform inference by
a single homogeneous mechanism. They may foreground different targets, preserve different state
representations, assign different costs to prediction errors, and permit different update paths, even
when the input is shared. Cognitive diversity is not merely value pluralism. It is an operational
fact about world-model learning.
The framework developed in this paper specifies the internal mechanism by which such differ-
ences are generated, stabilized, transformed, and sometimes made mutually processable. I call this
mechanism the Multi-Phase Inference Mechanism (MIM). In this usage, the framework refers to
the broader theoretical proposal, while MIM denotes its core formal device.
1.3
Question and formal devices
The central question of this paper is:
How can we formalize the fact that the same observation sequence can give rise to dif-
ferent inferential targets, state representations, prediction errors, actions, and alignment
requirements across subjects?
The paper introduces three core devices.
First, the phase-formation space Zphase represents the space in which a subject’s processing
operates with respect to reference targets and resolution modes. It is not the latent state zt itself,

but the space of how observations are processed: what is being referred to, and at what grain, time
scale, or abstraction level.
Second, the foregrounding field Rα
t specifies how prediction errors move processing through
that phase-formation space for subject α. It is a directional field rather than a static weight: in
an abstract sense, it functions like a gradient-like field over the phase-formation space, without
requiring a literal differentiable potential. It describes how certain targets, representations, and
errors become candidates for consciousness, language, action, reflection, or suspension.
Third, the alignment map Φα→β maps a state representation formed within subject α’s world
model into a form that can be processed within subject β’s world model. It is not a universal
translation function. It is a local and context-sensitive condition of processability.
The first two devices describe how differences are generated within a subject. The third describes
how the resulting representations may become processable across subjects. In this sense, Zphase and
R form the minimal core of the intra-subjective generative mechanism, while Φ is the central device
for the social and AI-alignment scope of the paper.

1.4
Contributions and organization
The paper is intentionally written as a parent paper rather than as a narrow algorithmic con-
tribution. Its purpose is to fix a theoretical object: heterogeneous world-model operation under
multi-phase inference, and the alignment problem generated by that heterogeneity. Later papers
may specialize the model into simulations, empirical measurements, prompt-based alignment pro-
cedures, interface designs, or learning algorithms.
The contributions of the paper are as follows.
1. It formulates the Multi-Phase Inference Assumption as an alternative to the Single Intelligence
Assumption.
2. It defines inferential targets, state representations, and prediction errors as target-relative con-
structs rather than fixed objects.
3. It introduces a phase-formation space and a foregrounding field to model how different forms
of salience arise from the same observations.

4. It defines a profile state Ω= (θ, ζ, σ) that bundles operating tendencies, representation-
formation capacities, and directional maturity.
5. It introduces alignment maps Φ and distinguishes state-representation alignment, value align-
ment, and world-model alignment.
6. It extends the framework to philosophical traditions, cognitive typology, social fragmentation,
and AI alignment.
7. It sketches a research program for making the framework empirically and computationally
tractable.
In this paper, to understand another subject is not to share an identical representation.
It
is to reconstruct a state representation into a form that can be processed within the receiver’s
world model.
AI that understands self and others is not an AI that forces convergence to one
representation, but an AI that supports the processability of heterogeneous representations.
The guiding principle of the organization is simple: first define the mechanism, then show its
range. Sections 3–6 introduce the core formal vocabulary. Sections 7–8 show why that vocabu-
lary can reorganize older debates in philosophy and cognitive typology while keeping the formal
core independent of any one historical vocabulary. Sections 9–10 turn the same structure toward
alignment and social implementation. Section 11 specifies what remains to be turned into a fully
generative and empirically testable model. Section 12 outlines the research program opened by this
reformulation.
2
Existing Models and the Missing Problem
2.1
Relation to existing research areas
This section is not an exhaustive survey. Its purpose is to locate the gap that makes the formal
devices of this paper necessary. World-model research, the free-energy principle, theory of mind, AI
alignment, multi-agent systems, and cognitive typology have each accumulated important insights.
The present paper does not replace these traditions. It reorganizes some of their insights around
the problem of differences in inferential targets and state representations across subjects.
2.1.1
World-model research
World-model research studies how agents learn latent structures that support prediction, imagina-
tion, planning, and control [Schmidhuber 1991, Ha 2018, Hafner 2020, Hafner 2021, Brohan 2023,
LeCun 2022]. A world model can be written abstractly as a mapping from observations to latent
states and predictions:
Mϕ : o≤t 7→(zt, pϕ(ot+1 | zt, at)).
Such models are indispensable for understanding how agents can act without directly evaluating ev-
ery future possibility. However, standard world-model formulations often leave implicit the question
of which inferential target is being learned. They model how a latent state supports prediction, but
not why different subjects select different targets and sufficient statistics from the same observation
sequence.
MIM keeps the world-model insight but expands the object of analysis. The world model is not
only a latent predictive mechanism. It also includes the effective phase-formation space available
to the subject, the foregrounding field that determines the direction of processing, and the profile
state that controls how representations are formed and stabilized.

2.1.2
The free-energy principle and active inference
The free-energy principle, predictive processing, and active inference formulate perception, action,
and learning as processes of minimizing variational free energy or expected free energy [Friston 2010,
Clark 2013, Schwartenbeck 2015, Friston 2017, Veissiere 2020, Parr 2022]. These frameworks are
highly relevant because they already integrate prediction, action, uncertainty, and model update.
Active inference also provides a natural vocabulary for understanding action as a way of sampling
observations that reduce expected uncertainty.
The present paper does not attempt to replace this framework. Rather, it asks a complementary
question: when different subjects minimize errors, which errors are being treated as meaningful, and
relative to which inferential targets? MIM can be read as a target-selection and state-representation
layer that may be placed above, beside, or within active-inference formulations.
2.1.3
Theory of mind and other-agent modeling
Theory of mind studies how agents attribute beliefs, desires, intentions, and mental states to others
[Premack 1978, Rabinowitz 2018]. Such models are central to social cognition and multi-agent AI.
However, ToM models often presuppose that the relevant mental states to be attributed are already
of a known type. MIM shifts the focus to the formation of the state representations themselves.
The question is not only what another agent believes, but which target, representation, and error
structure the other agent is operating with.
2.1.4
AI alignment
AI alignment research has focused on preference learning, reinforcement learning from human feed-
back, direct preference optimization, and constitutional AI [Ouyang 2022, Rafailov 2023, Bai 2022].
These methods are crucial for aligning system outputs with human judgments, values, or norms.
Yet they often treat human feedback as if it were comparable across subjects once expressed as
rankings, rewards, or preference labels.
MIM suggests that alignment must also consider the state representations from which such
feedback arises. A disagreement in preference may reflect a disagreement in value, but it may also
reflect a difference in inferential target, representation, error cost, or allowed update path. Thus
value alignment is only one layer of alignment.
2.1.5
Multi-agent systems and distributed representations
Multi-agent systems study coordination, planning, communication, and strategic interaction among
agents [Gmytrasiewicz 2005]. Distributed representations and large language models show how com-
plex relational structures can be represented and transformed in high-dimensional spaces [Vaswani 2017,
Wei 2022, Yao 2023]. These lines of work provide important technical resources for implementing
alignment maps. MIM contributes a conceptual specification of what must be aligned: not only
actions or messages, but also target-relative state representations.
2.1.6
Cognitive typology
Cognitive typologies, including Jungian and Socionics traditions, have long attempted to describe
stable differences in how people attend, judge, and interpret [Jung 1921, Myers 1985, Kepinski 1972,
Pietrak 2018]. The empirical and psychometric status of such systems is contested. Nevertheless,
they preserve repeated observations about differences in attention, meaning, embodiment, relation,

and structure. This paper treats typological vocabularies as coarse maps whose observations may
be re-expressed in a continuous and testable formal space.
2.2
Position of this paper
The position of the present paper can be stated as follows. Existing approaches have developed
powerful accounts of latent state learning, variational inference, active control, preference optimiza-
tion, distributed representation, and other-agent modeling. MIM does not replace these accounts.
It asks a different question: before a subject predicts or controls a state, what has become a state
for that subject? Before a preference is optimized, what has become valuable enough to generate
prediction error? Before an utterance is translated, what state representation was the utterance
trying to preserve?
This question is especially important for AI systems that mediate human interaction. A system
that only optimizes for helpful, harmless, or preference-aligned responses may still fail to repre-
sent why two human subjects are speaking from different inferential targets. Conversely, a system
that can estimate the target-relative structure of disagreement may support communication with-
out pretending that disagreement has disappeared. The missing problem is therefore not merely
better modeling of human preferences, but modeling the formation and transformation of state
representations across heterogeneous world models.
This is why the paper treats world-model alignment as broader than value alignment. A value
judgment is not an isolated scalar. It is embedded in a representation of what is happening, to
whom, under which constraints, and at what temporal or normative scale. AI systems that mediate
human understanding therefore need a model of these embeddings. MIM provides a formal scaffold
for such a model.
The paper is therefore positioned at the intersection of world models, active inference, theory of
mind, AI alignment, social cognition, and philosophical epistemology. It asks what is missing when
models focus on prediction, action, or value alignment without explicitly modeling the endogenous
selection of inferential targets and state representations. MIM is proposed as a formal vocabulary
for that missing layer.

2.4
Common skeleton and limitation of existing models
This limitation is not a criticism of the achievements of the existing models. World models, ac-
tive inference, predictive coding, information bottleneck, reinforcement learning, theory of mind,
and alignment methods each formalize an indispensable part of intelligent behavior. The present
claim is narrower and more specific: their common skeleton usually presupposes, abstracts away,
or externally fixes the level at which the target of estimation is defined. MIM treats that level as
endogenous.
In this sense, MIM does not add another loss function to the existing list. It introduces a prior
layer at which the space of possible losses, representations, and update paths is itself structured by
the subject. The formal vocabulary developed below is intended to make this layer explicit.
The limitation is not that existing models are mistaken. It is that they often begin after a deci-
sive selection has already occurred. A reinforcement-learning formulation presupposes a reward or
value signal. A predictive-processing formulation presupposes the structure of the prediction being

representation, and error become available in the first place.
This point also clarifies the relation between MIM and information-theoretic approaches. The
question is not simply how much information is preserved under compression. It is also which target
variable makes a statistic sufficient. If different subjects define different effective target variables,
then different compressions become rational. Cognitive diversity can therefore be understood as
diversity in the target-relative sufficiency of representations.
Existing models commonly assume some mapping from observation to latent representation,
prediction, action, and update. MIM agrees with that skeleton. Its contribution is to make explicit
that the target, representation, error, and update path may differ across subjects even under shared
observations. This difference is the central object of the paper.

3.2
A minimal example: one observation, several inferential targets
Consider a short public statement after a controversial event: “The procedure was followed.” One

statement as evidence for a broader institutional pattern.

fourth. What must be modeled is not a fixed identity but the dynamic relation among observation,
target formation, representation extraction, prediction error, foregrounding, action, and feedback.
3.3
Multi-phase inference and inferential phases
An inferential phase is not a personality category. It is a local mode of world-model operation
in which a subject selects certain targets, extracts certain representations, and evaluates certain
errors. A subject may enter different phases in different contexts, and several candidate phases may
be weakly activated before one becomes foregrounded. The term “phase” is used because it points
to a configuration of reference, resolution, error evaluation, and update tendency, not merely to the
content of a belief.
The four terms used later in the paper—empirical, ideal, structural, and existential—are not
axioms of the model. They are coarse regions used to explain recurring patterns in human cognition
and intellectual history. The formal object is the higher-dimensional phase-formation space. The
coarse regions are maps drawn on that space.
MIM assumes that a subject’s inference can shift across such phases.
The same event may
be processed as a practical constraint, a violation of a principle, a structural inconsistency, or an
injury to relation and dignity. These are not merely different verbal descriptions. They correspond
to different target-relative prediction errors.

4.1
Definition
The phase-formation space should be distinguished from the latent state space of the generative
model. A latent state zt may encode the current hidden causes of observations. A point in the phase-
formation space encodes how the subject is organizing the act of inference itself: which reference
region is being used and at what resolution. In this sense, Zphase is a meta-representational space
of inferential organization.
The phase-formation space is defined as
Zphase = {(ψ, d) | ψ ∈Zref, d ∈Zres(ψ)}.
Here ψ is a position in a reference-target space and d is a position in a resolution space dependent

tutions, physical constraints, and observable events, and endophenomenal components, such as self,
other, relation, responsibility, injury, recognition, and trust:
Zref ⊃Zexo × Zendo.
The resolution space includes micro-operational directions and macro-ideational directions. Micro-
resolution emphasizes local feedback, concrete operation, bodily action, and short-term adjustment.
Macro-resolution emphasizes long-horizon meaning, principles, narrative, and reflective integration.
4.2
Abstract space and effective space
It is useful to distinguish the abstract phase-formation space from the effective phase-formation
space available to a subject at a given time. The abstract space Zphase is the theoretical space in
which reference directions and resolution directions can be defined. The effective space Zα
phase,t is
the region of that space that subject α can actually use at time t. It depends on learning history,
language, training, institutional scaffolding, bodily ability, social position, and repeated successes

The effective space may expand, contract, or differentiate.
It expands when new reference
targets become distinguishable. It contracts when repeated failure, social inhibition, or institutional
pressure makes certain targets unavailable. It differentiates when a previously coarse distinction
becomes representationally fine-grained. Thus, learning does not merely change parameters within
a fixed representational space; it may also change what counts as a usable region of the phase-
formation space.
This distinction prevents a misunderstanding. MIM does not assume that every subject has
immediate access to all inferential regions. Some regions may be weakly differentiated, over-costly,
socially inhibited, or unavailable as explicit representations. Conversely, education, practice, di-
alogue, therapy, institutional design, and AI support may expand the effective space. When this
happens, the subject does not merely change an opinion. The subject becomes able to form a target
or representation that was previously unavailable.

4.4
Resolution direction and error absorption
Resolution direction is crucial because not all prediction errors are absorbed at the same temporal
or representational scale. Some errors are best absorbed through immediate action: change the
procedure, move the body, repair the tool, clarify the next step. Other errors are absorbed through
reflection: reinterpret the event, place it within a longer history, revise an ideal, or suspend action
until a more coherent model becomes available. A subject’s preferred resolution direction influences
whether uncertainty is treated as a call to act, to think, to narrate, to measure, to withdraw, or to
coordinate.
Neither mode is intrinsically superior. They are different strategies for absorbing error under
different constraints. A model of cognitive diversity must therefore distinguish not only what error
is detected, but also at which resolution the subject can absorb it.

5.8
Four processing modes
The four processing modes should be understood as recurring ways in which the generative loop can
be continued. A subject may act, reflect, communicate, or suspend. Each can be rational relative
to a different error structure. Acting may reduce local uncertainty. Reflecting may preserve a long-
range representation. Communicating may attempt to build a cross-subjective map. Suspending
may prevent premature update when available representations are insufficient.
Four coarse processing modes are useful as explanatory vocabulary: empirical, ideal, structural,
and existential. They are not axioms. Empirical processing foregrounds bodily and environmental
feedback. Ideal processing foregrounds meaning, possibility, and long-horizon integration. Struc-
tural processing foregrounds formal relations, constraints, and reproducibility. Existential process-
ing foregrounds self, other, trust, responsibility, injury, and recognition.
5.9
Fixation of inference profiles
Fixation is not simply the strengthening of a successful strategy. It can also be the stabilization
of avoidance. If a subject repeatedly fails when attempting to externalize a relational injury, the
existential direction may become either suppressed or over-sensitized. If a subject repeatedly suc-
ceeds by structural compression, structural foregrounding may become dominant even where other

5.10
Sedimentation, crystallization, and bedrock
These metaphors are not decorative. They indicate different temporal depths and are compatible
with broader traditions that study how repeated activation stabilizes future patterns and how order
emerges in complex systems [Hebb 1949, Prigogine 1984]. Sedimentation describes repeated weak
traces that gradually bias future processing. Crystallization describes the formation of a stable
pattern that gives coherence to many events.
Bedrock describes a configuration that becomes
difficult to revise because it supports the subject’s broader world-model organization. A mature
theory of cognitive diversity must distinguish these levels; otherwise every stable difference is misread
as either a mere preference or an immutable essence.
It is useful to distinguish three levels of fixation. Sedimentation refers to weak traces left by
repeated foregrounding. Crystallization refers to more stable mixtures of target, representation,
error cost, and update path. Bedrock refers to deeply fixed patterns that are difficult to revise
without large-scale reorganization. The metaphor is not a theory of geology; it is a way of describing
how profiles can become stable without becoming immutable.

The Epistemic Shift Opened by MIM
This section draws out the epistemic consequence of the formalism. The shift is not merely from
one model to another. It is from asking which subject is right under a presumed common target
to asking how different targets, representations, and errors became salient. This does not dissolve
truth or responsibility. It changes the level at which disagreement is analyzed.
6.1
The question shifts before the answer
In many disputes, participants attempt to compare answers while asking different questions. One
asks what can be done now. Another asks what principle has been violated. Another asks what
relation has been damaged. Another asks what future becomes possible. When these questions
remain implicit, each answer appears evasive to the others. MIM gives a vocabulary for locating
the divergence before answer comparison begins.
Disagreement is often treated as a disagreement over answers. MIM shows that disagreement
may begin earlier, with the question itself. If subjects select different inferential targets, then they
may not even be evaluating the same prediction error. One subject asks what action is feasible;
another asks what principle is violated; another asks who is being made responsible; another asks
which structure can be reproduced.
6.2
From error to non-identifiability
Non-identifiability does not mean that every conclusion is equally valid. It means that the observa-
tion sequence alone does not determine a unique target-representation-error structure. Additional
constraints, histories, institutions, and communicative procedures are required. This allows the the-
ory to avoid both relativism and premature normativity. Some models are better for some targets,
but the target itself must be made explicit.
A divergent conclusion is not automatically an error. It may be the result of non-identifiability:
the same observations can support different targets and representations. The appropriate response
is not always correction, but clarification of which world-model operation produced the conclusion.

From disorder to inferential phases
Cognitive diversity is not formless.
It has structure.

6.4
Giving a map to high-dimensional difference
The role of coarse vocabularies such as empirical, ideal, structural, and existential is to provide a

6.5
Resolution setting and short-/long-term error absorption
Some subjects absorb error through immediate action and local feedback. Others absorb it through
long-horizon reflection or symbolic integration. Both are error-absorption strategies. Their conflict
often appears as impatience, abstraction, impracticality, or superficiality, but the deeper difference
lies in the resolution setting of the world model.
6.6
Supporting meta-recognition and processability
The practical aim is not to make subjects abandon their representations. It is to help them recognize
that other representations may be preserving errors that their own model does not foreground. Such
recognition is not agreement. It is a meta-cognitive condition for processability. AI systems may
support this by identifying likely target differences, proposing alignment maps, and preventing
premature reduction of one representation to another.
The aim is not to make all subjects converge on the same representation. It is to make the dif-
ference of representations visible and processable. Meta-recognition means recognizing that another
subject may be operating with a different target, state representation, error cost, and update path.

World-Model Rewriting of Philosophical Traditions
7.1
What have philosophical disputes preserved?
The guiding interpretation of this chapter is that philosophical traditions are not simply competing
answers to the same question. They often preserve different questions, different state represen-
tations, and different costly prediction errors.
Empiricism preserves the cost of departing from
observation. Idealism preserves the cost of losing form, norm, or meaning. Structuralism preserves
the cost of ignoring systems of relation. Existentialism preserves the cost of erasing the situated
subject. Phenomenology preserves the cost of overlooking how things appear before theory. Prag-
matism preserves the cost of separating truth from action. These costs are not interchangeable.
MIM therefore does not treat philosophy as a historical prelude to computational theory, nor
as a collection of primitive versions of machine-learning models. It treats philosophical history as a
distributed record of human attempts to protect different kinds of prediction error from premature
23


## Page 24


dismissal. The task is not to declare one tradition victorious, but to ask what inferential target,
state representation, error cost, and update path each tradition made visible.
Seen from MIM, the long persistence of philosophical disputes is not primarily evidence that
philosophy failed to converge. It is evidence that different traditions were preserving different state
representations from erasure. A tradition becomes durable when it makes visible an error that
another tradition tends to treat as noise. This is also why philosophical history matters for AI.
If AI systems are to mediate among heterogeneous human world models, they must not treat all
disagreement as failed information transmission. They must learn to ask which prediction error
each side is trying not to lose.
In the following subsections, the terms empirical, ideal, structural, and existential are used
as coarse explanatory regions in the phase-formation space. They are not postulated as discrete
bases of cognition. They name recurrent regions in which subjects often form targets and state
representations. Their role is to connect philosophical vocabulary to the formal devices introduced
above: Y , T, L, Zphase, R, Ω, and Φ.
7.2
Empiricism and idealism
From the standpoint of MIM, empiricism can be read as a discipline of anchoring the world model
in externally constrained observation. It foregrounds what can be checked, repeated, perceived,
measured, or corrected by contact with the world. Its danger is not that it lacks theory, but that
it may treat what cannot be easily externalized as less real. Idealism, by contrast, foregrounds
the form under which observations become intelligible: categories, norms, totalities, meanings, or
conditions of possibility. Its danger is not that it ignores experience, but that it may treat long-range
coherence as overriding local friction.
Goodman’s problem of projection and worldmaking also reminds us that observation and concep-
tual organization cannot be cleanly separated [Goodman 1955]. Both tendencies are indispensable.
A world model that cannot be corrected by observation becomes unconstrained. A world model
that cannot preserve form and meaning becomes scattered across local stimuli. MIM represents
this tension not as a dispute between two metaphysical camps, but as a difference in reference and
resolution: what is treated as the target of estimation, and at what temporal and conceptual scale
prediction error is evaluated.
Empiricism and idealism can be reread as preserving two different errors that intelligence must
not erase.
Empiricism insists that the world resists our concepts.
Observations, experiments,
and sensory constraints prevent inference from becoming a closed self-confirming system. Locke
and Hume preserve the fact that cognition is not intelligible without repeated contact with the
world and the correction of expectation by experience [Locke 1690, Hume 1748]. In MIM terms,
empiricism foregrounds exophenomenal reference, local feedback, and errors generated by external
resistance.
Idealism insists that observation is never simply given. Kant’s central move is not merely to
deny naive empiricism, but to show that experience is organized by forms and categories that
make objects available to cognition at all [Kant 1781]. Hegel further radicalizes this by treating
consciousness as a historical process in which forms of recognition and self-relation are transformed
[Hegel 1807]. In MIM terms, idealism foregrounds the contribution of ρ and τ: the formation of
inferential targets and the extraction of state representations are not passive receptions of the world,
but active conditions under which something can become an object of experience.
The conflict between empiricism and idealism can therefore be restated as a conflict over which
prediction error must not be erased.
A world model that ignores external resistance becomes
unconstrained.
A world model that ignores internal form treats observation as if it were self-
24


## Page 25


interpreting. MIM preserves both by distinguishing reference direction, resolution mode, target
formation, and representation extraction. It does not collapse empiricism into idealism or idealism
into empiricism. It asks how both types of error are routed, foregrounded, and updated inside broad
world-model states.
7.3
Structuralism and existentialism
Structuralism preserves the insight that meaning is not contained in isolated objects.
It arises
from differences, relations, positions, and systems. In MIM terms, structural thinking foregrounds
state representations that are stable under relational transformation: rules, institutions, codes,
categories, and formal differences. Existentialism preserves a different cost. It asks what happens
to the situated subject when relational systems are described without responsibility, vulnerability,
decision, anxiety, or finitude.
The difference is not merely between cold structure and warm subjectivity.
Structural rep-
resentations can protect subjects from arbitrary power by making relations explicit. Existential
representations can protect the irreducibility of lived position from being flattened into a system.
MIM therefore treats structural and existential regions as coarse descriptions of different state-
representation priorities, not as mutually exclusive doctrines.
Structuralism and existentialism preserve a second irreducible contrast. Structuralist approaches
foreground relational systems: signs, institutions, roles, oppositions, exchange relations, and repro-
ducible patterns. Saussure and Levi-Strauss show how meaning arises through differences within
a system rather than through isolated substances [Saussure 1916, L´evi-Strauss 1958]. Foucault ex-
tends this orientation by showing how epistemic orders make some objects of knowledge possible
and others difficult to formulate [Foucault 1966]. In MIM terms, structural processing foregrounds
relational constraints, formal order, reproducibility, and socially stabilized state representations.
Existential approaches preserve what cannot be reduced to structure: the fact that someone is
exposed, responsible, wounded, obligated, mortal, or called by another. Kierkegaard, Heidegger,
Sartre, Levinas, and Buber preserve different dimensions of this irreducibility [Kierkegaard 1849,
Heidegger 1927, Sartre 1943, Levinas 1961, Buber 1923]. In MIM terms, existential processing fore-
grounds endophenomenal reference and relational prediction errors: who was made responsible,
who was silenced, whose dignity was affected, which relation was damaged, and what kind of self-
understanding became impossible.
MIM does not treat existential concerns as emotional residue left over after structural explana-
tion. Such concerns are genuine inferential targets. At the same time, MIM does not treat structural
order as mere cold formalism. Institutions, rules, and symbolic systems preserve prediction errors
that cannot be reduced to immediate feeling or singular experience. A rule violation is not the same
error as humiliation; an obligation is not reducible to a formal constraint; but a society that cannot
stabilize rules also loses the very conditions under which many existential claims become shareable.
MIM attempts to preserve both errors without making one the foundation of the other.
7.4
Semiotics
Semiotics is central because meaning is not a simple property of an object. A sign becomes mean-
ingful by connecting an observation to an inferential target and a state representation within a world
model. Peirce’s account of signs and interpretants and Saussure’s account of differential relations
both point to the fact that meaning is relational, not merely physical [Peirce 1958, Saussure 1916].
A sign may be a procedural marker, a social insult, a legal category, a theological symbol, a tech-
nical instruction, or a private reminder.
What changes is not the sign alone, but the target–

representation–error structure into which it is routed.
MIM treats signs as devices for routing inference. A sign does not merely point to a referent; it
foregrounds a region of possible interpretation. Misunderstanding often occurs when subjects treat
the same sign as routing to different targets. For one subject, a phrase may activate institutional
procedure; for another, it may activate relational injury; for another, it may activate an ideal
that has been betrayed. This is not merely semantic ambiguity. It is a difference in world-model
operation.
This perspective also clarifies why symbolic systems are powerful. They compress and exter-

They allow subjects to coordinate around representations that no

But compression also hides what has been

State-representation alignment therefore requires not only sign matching, but reconstruction of the
inferential target and prediction error that made the sign meaningful.
7.5
Language, meaning, translation, and incommensurability
The problem of translation is one of the clearest precursors to state-representation alignment. Early
Wittgenstein sought the conditions under which language can picture the world; later Wittgenstein
emphasized language games and forms of life. Quine’s indeterminacy of translation and Kuhn’s
incommensurability both show that translation cannot be reduced to word substitution. A term
carries with it a field of use, expectation, error, and permissible inference.
MIM restates this as follows. An utterance is not merely a sequence of symbols. It is an output
generated from a target-relative state representation under an operating profile. Understanding the
utterance therefore requires reconstructing enough of the representation for it to become processable
in the receiver’s world model. This does not require agreement, but it does require a transformation
that avoids immediate rejection, flattening, or misclassification.
The shift from early Wittgenstein to later Wittgenstein can be read as a shift from a single log-
ical mapping relation to situated forms of use. The early Wittgenstein investigated how language
pictures the world through logical form [Wittgenstein 1922]. The later Wittgenstein emphasized
language games and forms of life [Wittgenstein 1953]. Quine’s indeterminacy of translation and
Kuhn’s incommensurability likewise show that expressions cannot always be mapped across sys-
tems by word replacement alone [Quine 1960, Kuhn 1962]. In MIM terms, translation is difficult
because words are embedded in different target-selection, state-representation, and error-evaluation
structures.
Grice’s theory of implicature shows that utterances rely on cooperative expectations that are
not contained in literal content [Grice 1975]. Lakoff’s account of categories and embodied meaning
suggests that concepts are structured by bodily and metaphorical organization rather than by purely
formal definitions [Lakoff 1987]. These insights point in the same direction: meaning depends on
the background profile that determines what kind of target is being formed and what kind of error
is being evaluated.
A single word may be processed as a rule violation in a structural region, as an insult in an
existential region, as a practical constraint in an empirical region, or as a betrayal of an ideal in
an ideal region. Alignment therefore requires more than lexical substitution. It requires a map Φ
that transforms a state representation into a form processable by another world model. This does
not guarantee agreement. It makes disagreement intelligible enough to become a possible object of
reasoning.

7.6
Linguistic relativity and social profile formation
Linguistic relativity can be incorporated without assuming that language rigidly determines thought.
Sapir and Whorf show that linguistic practices can shape perception, categorization, and attention
[Sapir 1929, Whorf 1956]. MIM does not need a strong deterministic claim. It only requires the
weaker and more testable claim that repeated linguistic distinctions scaffold profile formation. A
language, discourse community, or institutional vocabulary can stabilize certain inferential targets
and make others difficult to articulate.
Over time, such vocabularies influence Ω. They shape which errors are easy to name, which
distinctions are socially rewarded, and which representations can be shared without elaborate ex-
planation. In a professional community, certain structural errors may become immediately visible.
In a political community, certain existential or ideal errors may become central. In a technical field,
certain empirical and structural errors may dominate. These are not merely differences in opinion;
they are differences in trained salience.
This is especially important for social fragmentation. Groups may not merely disagree over
propositions. They may inhabit vocabularies that make different prediction errors visible. When
one group says that an issue is about procedure and another says that it is about dignity, the
disagreement is not always downstream of a shared representation. It may occur at the level of
which state representation is formed at all. Alignment then requires reconstruction of target and
representation structures, not only persuasion.
7.7
Phenomenology, epoche, and eidetic reduction
Phenomenology is especially important for MIM because it suspends the assumption that the object
is already given in the same way for all subjects. Husserl’s epoch´e and eidetic reduction do not merely
add introspective ornament to cognition; they show that what appears as an object depends on how
intentionality is suspended, redirected, and varied [Husserl 1913]. In MIM terms, phenomenological
reduction can be interpreted as a deliberate intervention on foregrounding. It changes which targets
are allowed to stabilize and which state representations become visible.
This anticipates a central problem of AI-mediated understanding. If an AI system simply ex-
tracts propositional content, it may miss how the content appears as threatening, relieving, humil-
iating, promising, meaningless, or obligatory. MIM gives a formal vocabulary for this difference:
the same utterance may connect to different inferential targets and error costs depending on the
subject’s phase-formation state.
Merleau-Ponty deepens this by showing that perception is embodied [Merleau-Ponty 1945]. The
body is not an external instrument used by a detached mind.
It is part of the field in which
affordances, orientations, and possibilities are formed.
This connects phenomenology to ecolog-
ical psychology, Uexkull’s notion of Umwelt, and Gibson’s account of affordances [Uexk¨ull 1934,
Gibson 1979].
What appears is not simply an image of the world.
It is a world structured by
possible action, bodily orientation, and environmental coupling.
For MIM, this means that the phase-formation space cannot be reduced to abstract propo-
sitions. Some inferential targets are bodily, situated, and pre-reflective. They become available
before explicit judgment. A theory of AI-mediated understanding must therefore avoid treating all
meaning as text-level content. It must account for the way state representations are formed through
embodied, situated, and action-oriented constraints.

7.8
Pragmatism and the intentional stance
Pragmatism preserves the testing of meaning through action. For Peirce, James, and Dewey, mean-
ing is not a static representation detached from practice; it is linked to habits, consequences, inquiry,
and reconstruction through experience [Peirce 1958, James 1907, Dewey 1925]. In MIM terms, prag-
matism highlights the loop from target formation to plan candidates, action, re-observation, and
feedback update. A representation is not only something held; it is something that guides possible
action and is corrected by what follows.
Dennett’s intentional stance provides another bridge [Dennett 1991]. To treat a system as an
intentional agent is to adopt a predictive strategy: assign beliefs, desires, and rationality in order to
explain and anticipate behavior. MIM can reinterpret such stances as profile-dependent modeling
choices. A subject may choose a structural stance, an existential stance, an empirical stance, or an
ideal stance toward another subject, depending on which errors are foregrounded and which forms
of explanation become salient.
This matters for AI systems that model humans. A model that treats another subject only as a
preference-maximizing agent may miss existential injury, institutional constraint, or symbolic mean-
ing. A model that treats another subject only as a bearer of values may miss practical constraints
and coordination costs. Pragmatism reminds us that understanding another world model requires
asking what actions, suspensions, and reconstructions become possible under that representation.
7.9
Action and reflection
One of the most important distinctions in MIM is whether prediction error is absorbed through
action or through reflection. Some subjects reduce error by acting in the near environment: testing,
repairing, negotiating, moving, building, or intervening.
Others reduce error by extending the
temporal horizon of representation: reinterpreting, reframing, narrating, theorizing, or suspending
action until a larger pattern becomes visible. Neither strategy is universally superior. They differ
in where the error is absorbed: in the environment, in the body, in social coordination, in narrative,
or in long-range abstraction.
MIM models this difference through the resolution direction and the action constraint. A micro-
resolution tendency may privilege local feedback and immediate intervention. A macro-resolution
tendency may preserve long-horizon coherence and meaning. Action constraints determine whether
either tendency can actually be executed. The same prediction error may therefore become a task,
a wound, a theory, a moral demand, or a reason for suspension.
This distinction explains why some disagreements become frustrating even when both sides are
trying to solve the problem. One subject may seek a concrete next step because error is represented
as actionable instability.
Another may resist immediate action because error is represented as
unresolved meaning or violated value. If each interprets the other through its own phase, the first
appears shallow or impatient, while the second appears evasive or impractical. MIM makes this
difference formal by connecting resolution direction, temporal horizon, and action constraints.
AI systems that mediate such situations should not simply summarize the discussion or propose
a compromise. They should identify whether subjects are attempting to absorb error through action,
reflection, suspension, reinterpretation, or coordination. This is one reason why Cact in this paper
includes not only bodily action, but also speech, reflection, suspension, and coordination attempts.
7.10
A bridge to cognitive science: Kahneman’s dual process
Kahneman’s distinction between fast and slow processes has become a widely used low-dimensional
map of cognition [Kahneman 2011]. It is useful precisely because it captures a recurrent experiential
28


## Page 29


difference: some processes are rapid, automatic, and associative; others are slow, deliberate, and
controlled. MIM does not reject this distinction. It treats it as a coarse projection of a richer
structure.
From the perspective of MIM, the fast/slow contrast does not specify which target is formed,
which state representation is preserved, or which prediction error is considered costly. A fast process
may be empirical, structural, existential, or ideal; a slow process may likewise operate in any of these
regions. The dual-process map therefore captures an important dimension of cognitive control, but
not the full geometry of phase formation.
This makes Kahneman’s framework a bridge rather than a rival. It shows how powerful a low-
dimensional cognitive distinction can be, while also revealing why additional axes are needed. MIM
extends the question from “fast or slow?” to “what target, what representation, what error, what
cost, what update path, and what alignment map?”
7.11
From philosophical history to state-representation alignment
The preceding traditions can now be read as different attempts to preserve classes of error. Empiri-
cism preserves external resistance. Idealism preserves formative conditions. Structuralism preserves
relational order. Existentialism preserves the stakes of self, other, responsibility, and injury. Semi-
otics and philosophy of language preserve the dependence of meaning on systems of interpretation.
Phenomenology preserves the transformation of appearance under altered attention. Pragmatism
preserves the testing of meaning through action. Cognitive science preserves low-dimensional maps
of process control.
MIM does not claim to replace these traditions. It offers a formal space in which their preserved
errors can become comparable. The aim is not to end philosophical disagreement. It is to make clear
which target, representation, error, and update path each disagreement is protecting. In this sense,
MIM gives a footing for comparing conflicts that have been expressed in different vocabularies.
For AI, this is not merely a matter of intellectual history. If AI systems are to support under-
standing between subjects, they must be able to recognize when a dispute is not about a shared
proposition but about different state representations. They must support transformations that pre-
serve what each representation is trying not to lose. This is the bridge from philosophical history
to state-representation alignment.

Computational Connection to Cognitive Typology
Cognitive typology is treated here as a demanding test case for MIM. Typological systems should
not be adopted as final scientific taxonomies. Their categories are often discrete, contested, and
coarse. At the same time, they have repeatedly named differences that many people recognize in
practice: what becomes salient, which explanations feel satisfying, which errors are hard to ignore,
and which forms of communication feel natural or effortful.
A rough map is not a precise survey. Yet if many travelers repeatedly give names to the same
passes and river lines, the terrain deserves attention. The aim of this section is not to defend an
old map, but to redraw the terrain in a more continuous, testable, and computational coordinate
system. In this sense, MIM attempts to preserve observations while updating the formalism.
8.1
Typology as a stress test of explanatory power
The value of typological vocabularies lies neither in treating them as final scientific entities nor in
dismissing them as mere folk psychology. Their historical persistence suggests that people repeatedly
29


## Page 30


encounter patterned differences in salience, attention, evaluation, and communication. A rough
map is not a precise survey. Yet if many travelers repeatedly name similar ridges, valleys, and
river crossings, the landscape should not be ignored. MIM takes this persistence as a stress test:
can a formal model preserve the observations while replacing rigid labels with continuous, testable
structures?
This stance also respects readers who are skeptical of typology. MIM does not ask them to
accept discrete personality types. It asks whether the recurring descriptive contrasts that typologies
attempted to capture can be reformulated as projections of a higher-dimensional operating structure.
In this sense, typology is best read as a demanding case of representational translation.
The relevance of typology to this paper is not historical authority. It is explanatory pressure.
A theory of human cognitive diversity that cannot explain why typological vocabularies have been
compelling to many users, while also explaining why they are scientifically insufficient, has not yet
fully addressed the phenomenon. MIM must be able to do both.
The first task is preservation.
Typological vocabularies often preserve recurrent differences
in salience, communication, preferred explanation, vulnerability, and developmental asymmetry.
The second task is correction. These differences should not be reified as discrete essences. They
should be represented as patterns in continuous phase formation, foregrounding, representation
formation, and directional maturity. The third task is testability. A typological claim should be
translatable into hypotheses about target selection, state-representation extraction, error sensitivity,
action constraints, and feedback history.
This gives the chapter its methodological role. Typology is a domain in which MIM’s explanatory
resolution can be evaluated. If MIM can reconstruct the useful observations of typology without
inheriting its rigid classifications, it gains evidence that its formal vocabulary captures something
important about human cognitive diversity.
8.2
Four coarse regions times two directions
The four coarse regions should not be read as a universal partition of the mind. They are explanatory
handles. The empirical region foregrounds observable events, bodily feedback, and local action.
The ideal region foregrounds meaning, possibility, long-range pattern, and narrative coherence.
The structural region foregrounds formal relations, systems, reproducibility, and constraints. The
existential region foregrounds self, other, responsibility, trust, injury, and recognition.
Each region can be explored or stabilized. Exploration expands alternatives, counterfactuals,
and possible transformations. Stabilization compresses them into reliable categories, habits, rules,
or identities. The familiar language of functions can be reconstructed as one way of naming such
region-direction combinations, but the underlying model remains continuous. Individuals are not
identical to labels. They are trajectories in a structured space.
The four coarse regions–empirical, ideal, structural, and existential–should be read as explana-
tory vocabulary. Each region can be explored or stabilized. This yields eight cognitive elements
as coarse projections of directional processing: empirical exploration and stabilization, ideal explo-
ration and stabilization, structural exploration and stabilization, and existential exploration and
stabilization. These are not modules or essences. They are useful ways of naming recurrent direc-
tions in the phase-formation space.
This reframing preserves what typological vocabularies often capture. Some subjects are repeat-
edly drawn to immediate sensory-action constraints, others to possibility and long-range meaning,
others to formal or institutional structure, and others to relational and existential stakes. It also
avoids treating these tendencies as fixed identities. A subject may operate in multiple regions, and
the profile state may change through learning, context, and feedback.

8.3
Connection to Jungian functions
Jung’s psychological types can be reread as attempts to name recurrent directions of attention and
valuation [Jung 1921]. In MIM, the eight elements are not taken as innate faculties. They are
interpreted as coarse projections of phase-region and direction. Sensing-like processes correspond
to empirical regions; intuition-like processes correspond to ideal regions; thinking-like processes
correspond to structural regions; feeling-like processes correspond to existential regions. Extraverted
and introverted orientations can be reread as exploratory and stabilizing directions, though this
mapping is only approximate.
The point of this reinterpretation is not to claim that Jungian typology was already computa-
tional theory. The point is to show how its observations can be translated. A claim such as “this
subject relies on structural stabilization” can be reformulated as a claim about which targets are
selected, which representations are compressed, which errors become costly, and which directions of
R are reinforced. Such a claim can then be tested, revised, or rejected.
This also changes how typological language should be used. It should not be used to freeze a
subject into a label. It can be used, more carefully, to generate hypotheses about phase accessibility,
foregrounding tendencies, vulnerabilities, and possible developmental paths. The value of typology
then lies not in final classification, but in making some differences visible enough to be investigated.
8.4
Connection to Model A and constraint sequences
Socionics’ Model A can be reinterpreted as a structured description of constraint sequences over
directional processing [Augustinaviciute 1980, Pietrak 2018].
Rather than treating functions as
discrete psychic organs, MIM can treat them as stabilized expectations about which regions are
easy to access, which are valued, which are vulnerable, and which remain backgrounded. This does
not validate every typological claim. It offers a way to translate some typological observations into
the vocabulary of foregrounding, representation formation, directional maturity, and constraint.
In this reinterpretation, a position in a typological model corresponds to a pattern over R and Ω.
A strong region may be one in which target formation is rapid, representation extraction is stable,
and errors are efficiently absorbed. A vulnerable region may be one in which errors are costly but
representations are unstable. A background region may be processed efficiently without becoming
a primary object of self-description. These are empirical hypotheses, not dogmas.
The methodological value is clear. If a typological description corresponds to a measurable pat-
tern of target selection, representation extraction, error sensitivity, action constraint, and feedback

8.5
Relation to Big Five and psychometrics
The Big Five and related trait models provide an important contrast [Goldberg 1990, Costa 1992,
Roberts 2000].
They aim at psychometric description and prediction across populations.
MIM
is not a replacement for such models.
It asks a different question: how are inferential targets,
state representations, and error costs generated in a subject’s world model, and how do they affect
communication, action, and alignment?
A trait measure may predict that a subject is generally open, conscientious, or neurotic. MIM
asks how, in a particular context, a subject selects a target, extracts a representation, and updates a
profile. The two approaches can complement one another. Trait models provide stable population-
level descriptors; MIM provides a process vocabulary for context-specific inferential divergence. A
mature research program could connect the two by testing whether trait dimensions correlate with
patterns in R, θ, ζ, σ, or Cact.
This distinction is important because MIM should not be judged by whether it reproduces
existing personality scales. Its primary contribution is not psychometric classification. It is a formal
vocabulary for the generation and alignment of heterogeneous state representations. Typology and
psychometrics become relevant insofar as they provide observations that can be translated into that
vocabulary.
8.6
From typological vocabulary to AI-readable structures
The ultimate purpose of this reconstruction is not to rescue typological terminology for its own
sake. It is to make the phenomena that motivated such terminology available to AI systems without
inheriting the rigidity of the terminology itself. An AI system need not classify a person as a type.
It can estimate which targets are being foregrounded, which representations are being preserved,
which errors are costly, and which update paths are blocked or available.
This is where typology becomes relevant to alignment. A system that recognizes only explicit
preferences may miss the operating structure from which those preferences arise. A system that
can model operating profiles and directional maturity may help translate between subjects who
are not merely disagreeing, but attending to different regions of the phase-formation space. Such
translation does not require treating typology as truth. It requires preserving the observation and
updating the form.
For AI systems, typological language is useful only if it can be transformed into operationally
meaningful structures. A model should not merely infer that a user is a certain type. It should
ask which inferential target is being formed, which representation is being protected, which error
is costly, and which update path is difficult. In this sense, MIM turns type language into a set of
questions rather than a set of labels.
This is especially important for alignment. If an AI system responds to a structural concern with
existential reassurance, it may fail. If it responds to existential injury with procedural clarification,
it may also fail. If it responds to empirical constraint with ideal language, it may appear detached.
Conversely, if it can identify the relevant region and direction, it can propose a transformation that
preserves more of what the subject is trying not to lose.
The purpose of this chapter is therefore not to adopt existing typologies as final classifications.
It is to reinterpret the differences they have observed as low-dimensional projections of Zphase, R,

Alignment Maps Φ and World-Model Alignment
9.1
Definition of Φ
An alignment map is not a guarantee of agreement. It is a condition of processability. A represen-
tation T α formed in subject α may be rejected by subject β as irrelevant, threatening, incoherent,
or meaningless if it cannot be mapped into a target and representation structure available within
β’s world model. Φα→β denotes a local mapping that allows such reconstruction.
World-model alignment therefore does not require agreement.
A subject may disagree with
another subject while still reconstructing the other’s state representation into a form that is pro-
cessable within their own world model. Conversely, apparent agreement may conceal a failure of
processability when the same words are attached to different inferential targets, error costs, or
prediction errors. The role of Φ is to support preservation under transformation, not to force con-
vergence. Appendix B.2 gives a more detailed discussion of the distinction between processability
and agreement.
An alignment map is written as
Φα→β(T α) = ˆT β.
It maps a state representation formed in subject α’s world model into a representation that can
be processed within subject β’s world model. Processability means that the representation is not
immediately discarded as noise, threat, or meaningless residue, but can be reconstructed as some
target, representation, or error within the receiver.
This mapping may be linguistic, institutional, affective, conceptual, or technical. A good ex-
planation, a ritual, a legal procedure, a scientific diagram, a user interface, or an AI-generated
reframing can all function as partial alignment maps. None is universal. Each works only under
constraints.
9.2
Alignment as preservation under transformation
The alignment map Φ should not be understood as a device for making two subjects identical. It is
better understood as a transformation that attempts to preserve what is relevant under a change of
world model. In mathematics, a useful transformation often preserves some structure while changing
the representation. In MIM, the preserved structure is not always formal equivalence. It may be
the prediction error that a subject is trying not to lose, the value-bearing representation that must
remain visible, or the action constraint that makes a proposed solution feasible or impossible.
This is why alignment is more difficult than summarization. A summary can compress content
while erasing the target-relative error. A good alignment transformation must ask what must survive
the transformation. In one case, it may be empirical constraint; in another, institutional order; in
another, dignity; in another, an ideal that organizes long-term action. The same sentence may
require different transformations depending on which structure is to be preserved.
This also clarifies why forced agreement is not the goal. Two subjects may remain in disagree-
ment after alignment, but their disagreement becomes better specified. They can know whether
they disagree about evidence, procedure, harm, value, feasibility, or future trajectory. This is a
weaker outcome than consensus, but often a more realistic and more valuable one.

9.3
Three layers of Φ
Three layers should be distinguished.
First, state-representation alignment ΦT concerns whether a representation formed by one sub-
ject can become processable for another. Second, value alignment ΦU concerns whether what one
subject treats as costly, worth preserving, or worth sacrificing can be represented within another
subject’s evaluation structure. Third, world-model alignment ΦW concerns whether the broader
configuration of targets, representations, foregrounding tendencies, profile states, errors, and up-
date paths can be compared, transformed, or coordinated.
Current AI alignment methods often operate primarily at the level of preferences, rewards,
instructions, or constitutional constraints. These are important, but they are downstream of state-
representation formation. If two subjects do not form the same target or representation, aligning
preferences over outputs will not explain why their disagreement persists. MIM therefore places
value alignment inside a broader world-model alignment problem.
Many alignment failures treated as value conflicts may begin as failures of state-representation
alignment. A value cannot be aligned if the state representation in which it is meaningful cannot be
processed. Conversely, a state representation can become processable without producing agreement.
This distinction is central to the paper’s account of understanding.
10
Social and Technical Implementation of World-Model Align-
ment
The preceding sections define how differences arise within subjects and how representations may be
transformed across subjects. This section turns to the social and technical consequences. The central
claim is that many conflicts are not failures to exchange information, but failures of processability
across world-model states.
10.1
Social fragmentation and processability
Social fragmentation is often described in terms of polarization, filter bubbles, misinformation, or
ideological extremity [Pariser 2011, Sunstein 2017, Bail 2021]. These descriptions are important,
but MIM adds a further layer. Fragmentation occurs not only when groups receive different infor-
mation, but also when the same information is routed into different targets, representations, and
error costs. A group may process a policy as a technical intervention, another as a symbolic insult,
another as evidence of institutional betrayal, and another as a necessary compromise.
This does not mean that all interpretations are equally valid. MIM does not remove questions
of truth, evidence, or harm.
It clarifies the stage at which conflict occurs.
If subjects are not
forming the same state representation, then argument at the level of propositions may fail before it
begins. A statement may be true relative to one representation and irrelevant or injurious relative
to another. The task is first to make the representations processable.
10.2
Society as an alignment network
Institutions can be interpreted as alignment networks. Law, education, journalism, science, ritual,
administration, and professional practice all create procedures for making representations shareable.
They do not eliminate world-model diversity. They stabilize transformations among representations
so that coordination remains possible despite diversity.
34


## Page 35


This perspective helps explain why institutional trust matters. When trust collapses, a rep-
resentation generated in one institution is no longer accepted as processable in another subject’s
world model. Scientific evidence becomes propaganda; legal procedure becomes domination; moral
testimony becomes manipulation. The issue is not only belief. It is the failure of alignment maps
that previously allowed one representation to travel across subjects.
AI systems increasingly enter this network. They summarize documents, mediate communica-
tion, recommend content, answer questions, and generate explanations. If they optimize only for
local satisfaction or average preference, they may unintentionally strengthen fragmentation by mak-
ing each world model more fluent in its own region. If they are designed for state-representation
alignment, they may instead help subjects identify which representations are missing from one
another’s view.
10.3
Repositioning AI alignment
This repositioning has a practical consequence. AI alignment should not be understood only as mak-
ing AI obey human values, nor only as aggregating human preferences. Human values themselves
are formed within heterogeneous world models. A system aligned to one representation of value
may therefore become misaligned with another subject’s state representation even if it is optimizing
a plausible preference signal.
The aim of world-model alignment is not to make humans the same. It is to provide mediating
structures by which different state representations can be compared without immediate erasure. An
AI system can contribute to this process by identifying likely inferential targets, making hidden error
costs explicit, proposing transformations between representations, and indicating which differences
are disagreements over content and which are differences in phase formation.
Current AI alignment research has often focused on aligning model behavior with human prefer-
ences, instructions, feedback, or constitutional constraints [Ouyang 2022, Bai 2022, Rafailov 2023].
These are crucial engineering achievements.
MIM does not replace them.
It repositions them
within a broader problem. A system aligned to one distribution of human preferences may still fail
to mediate among heterogeneous human world models.
MIM distinguishes three layers. Value alignment asks whether an AI system respects or optimizes
for values. State-representation alignment asks whether a representation formed in one subject can
be reconstructed in a processable form for another. World-model alignment asks whether broader
target-selection, error-cost, and update structures can be made mutually intelligible.
In many
human conflicts, value alignment alone is insufficient because the values themselves are embedded
in different state representations.
This shift matters for evaluation. An AI system may produce a polite summary of two sides while
failing to preserve the error that each side is trying not to lose. A stronger system would identify
the inferential target of each side, distinguish structural from existential or empirical errors, and
propose transformations that make each representation processable without forcing convergence.
10.4
AI that understands self and others
The title of this paper refers to AI that understands self and others. In MIM, understanding does
not mean that the AI shares the same inner experience as a human subject. Nor does it mean that
all subjects converge to a single representation. Understanding means that a state representation
can be reconstructed into a form processable by another world model.
An AI system that supports this kind of understanding would perform several functions. It
would infer which target is being formed, identify which representation is being protected, detect
35


## Page 36


which prediction error is costly, and ask which update path is acceptable.
It would also know
when not to collapse difference into consensus. In some situations, the appropriate output is not an
answer, but a representation of why several answers are being generated.
Such systems would be useful in deliberation, education, mediation, collaborative design, gov-
ernance, and human-AI interaction. Their purpose would not be to decide whose world model is
correct in all respects. It would be to help subjects see which parts of their world models are failing
to become processable to one another.
10.5
Meaning, value, justice, harm, culture, and civilization
The same formal vocabulary can be used to describe objects that are often treated as too humanistic,
too normative, or too historically embedded to be modeled. The purpose is not to replace moral
philosophy, cultural theory, or social analysis. It is to specify how meaning, value, justice, harm,
culture, and civilization become operational within world-model states.
Meaning is the connectivity by which an observation, sign, event, or utterance becomes at-
tached to possible inferential targets, state representations, and prediction errors. A statement does
not have the same meaning for all subjects simply because it has the same wording. It becomes
meaningful through the target it activates, the representation it preserves, and the error it makes
visible.
Value is the allocation of error costs and update priorities over states, representations, and
trajectories. A subject or community treats some representations as inexpensive to revise, others
as costly to lose, and still others as requiring protection even under pressure from external evidence
or coordination demands.
Justice is a social rule for deciding which meanings and values must be protected, transformed, or
sacrificed under conflict. It is not merely a preference aggregation problem. It concerns the public
organization of transformations among incompatible state representations and costly prediction
errors.
Harm is the construction of an object, action, policy, or utterance as destructive of state rep-
resentations that a subject or community treats as requiring protection. This definition does not
excuse all claims of harm. It makes explicit the world-model operation by which something becomes
intolerable, rather than merely disagreeable.
Culture can be understood as a historically stabilized environment of symbols, practices, in-
stitutions, skills, and narratives that scaffold meaning and value formation. Civilization can be
understood as a large-scale network of such scaffolds across time, including law, education, tech-
nique, ritual, science, art, and memory.
These working definitions do not remove normative disagreement. They make visible the world-
model operations through which disagreement becomes normative. A community protects some
representations, permits transformation of others, and rejects some transformations as unacceptable.
AI alignment is therefore not only the problem of fitting machines to human values. It is also the
problem of designing systems that can operate within, and sometimes mediate among, heterogeneous
human cultures of meaning and value. The goal is not to reduce this plurality to a single metric,
but to make the conditions of plurality more explicit and processable.

This paper is theoretical, but it is not meant to remain purely interpretive. The formal devices intro-

11.1
Measuring intra-personal operating profiles
The first empirical task is to measure patterns within subjects.
A subject’s profile state Ω=
(θ, ζ, σ) should be estimated not by asking for a fixed type label, but by observing how targets,
representations, and errors are formed across contexts. Tasks can be designed in which the same
observation is presented under different framing conditions. The researcher can then measure which
targets are selected, which representations are recalled, which errors are judged costly, and which
action or reflection plans are generated.
Perspective-shift and perceptual-style paradigms, including work by Anderson and Pichert,
Bruner and Postman, Witkin and colleagues, and Nisbett, suggest one route [Anderson 1978,
Bruner 1947, Witkin 1962, Nisbett 2003].
If changing a role or perspective changes what is re-
membered, then state representations are target-relative.
MIM generalizes this idea: the same
observation can be probed under empirical, ideal, structural, and existential framings, and the
resulting representations can be compared.
11.2
Measuring world-model alignment
The second task is to measure alignment across subjects. Given a representation T α, can another
subject β reconstruct a processable representation ˆT β? This can be tested by asking whether the
receiving subject can predict the sender’s concerns, reproduce the relevant error, identify protected
representations, or generate acceptable transformations. Alignment is not measured by agreement
alone. It is measured by whether the relevant state representation becomes processable.
This allows experimental designs in which AI systems generate alternative transformations.
Some transformations may preserve structural content but lose existential stakes.
Others may
preserve relational injury but lose empirical constraints. A good alignment map should preserve the
relevant error class while making it intelligible to another world model.
Appendix B.4 outlines a minimal empirical path: present the same observation to multiple
subjects, code their candidate targets, state representations, costly errors, and plan candidates, and
then test whether other subjects or AI systems can reconstruct those components without requiring
endorsement.
11.3
Evaluating AI-supported alignment
The third task is to evaluate AI-mediated alignment.
An AI system can be asked to mediate
between subjects or groups whose state representations differ. Evaluation should not be limited
to satisfaction or perceived neutrality. It should include whether each side judges that its relevant
representation was preserved, whether the other side can restate that representation, whether new
plan candidates become available, and whether prediction errors decrease without erasing important
differences.
This evaluation could be performed in deliberative experiments, collaborative writing, organi-
zational decision-making, educational tutoring, or online discussion settings. The key is to evaluate
not only output quality, but processability across world models.

MIM suggests several testable hypotheses:
• H1: The same observation sequence can produce systematically different inferential targets

Limitations of the present paper
The present paper does not provide a complete generative model, a full estimation procedure, or a

Prematurely specifying one algorithm

11.8
What a full generative implementation would require
The most direct implementation path would proceed in stages. First, construct annotated corpora
in which multiple subjects respond to the same observations and their inferred targets, state rep-
resentations, error costs, and plan candidates are coded. Second, train models to infer candidate
Y , T, and L structures from text, interaction traces, or multimodal data. Third, evaluate align-
ment maps by asking whether transformed representations improve processability without forcing
agreement. Fourth, integrate action constraints by modeling whether proposed interventions are
executable within bodily, temporal, skill, communicative, and cooperative limits.
Such a program would not validate MIM all at once. It would test whether the decomposition
into targets, representations, foregrounding fields, profile states, alignment maps, and feedback
updates explains variance that is not captured by opinion labels, sentiment, personality traits, or
preference models alone.
A full implementation would require at least five additional specifications. First, it would require
a candidate-generation model for targets and representations. Second, it would require a scoring
function for prediction errors and error costs. Third, it would require a model of foregrounding
dynamics over Zα
phase,t. Fourth, it would require a plan-generation and plan-evaluation mechanism
that includes Cact. Fifth, it would require feedback update rules for R, Ω, and possibly Zα
phase,t
itself.
These requirements can be satisfied in different ways. A probabilistic implementation may define
latent variables for targets and representations. A neural implementation may treat R as a learned
routing or attention field over the phase-formation space. A symbolic-neural implementation may
represent Φ as a set of transformation operators constrained by natural-language explanations. A
multi-agent simulation may define agents with different Ωand measure whether alignment maps im-
prove cooperation without requiring convergence. In an LLM-mediated implementation, Φ may be
operationalized as prompt- or architecture-level transformations that reconstruct another subject’s
state representation without collapsing it into the model’s own default frame.
These implementations should be evaluated by processability, not merely by agreement, user
satisfaction, or communication fluency.
A successful system should help subjects identify what
target is being formed, what representation is being protected, which prediction error is costly,
and what transformation would preserve the relevant information for another world model. In some
contexts the appropriate output may be an answer; in others it may be a map of why several answers
are being generated.
The present paper does not choose among these implementation paths. Its purpose is to specify
the object that such implementations must preserve. Without this specification, an implementation
may optimize agreement, politeness, summarization quality, or preference satisfaction while missing
state-representation processability. The theoretical contribution is therefore prior to, but intended
for, computational implementation.

close the theory, but to show the research program that becomes available once these devices are in

The following subsections specify several directions in which the framework can be developed.
They are intentionally heterogeneous. Some are mathematical, concerning the geometry of phase-
formation spaces, information-geometric descriptions of profile change, and the learnability of fore-
grounding fields [Amari 2016]. Some are empirical, concerning the measurement of target formation,
error cost, directional maturity, and processability. Some are philosophical and social, concerning
disagreement, justice, cultural transmission, and democratic conflict. Some are technical, concerning
AI systems that support alignment across heterogeneous world models.
12.1
Epistemology: incommensurability and constructive perspectives
Kuhn’s notion of incommensurability can be reconsidered in operational terms.
In the present
framework, different subjects or communities may not merely assign different beliefs to the same
proposition. They may form different inferential targets Y , preserve different state representations
T, and evaluate different prediction errors L as costly. Incommensurability can therefore be de-
scribed as the difficulty of constructing an alignment map Φ between world models that differ in
the targets, representations, costs, and permissible update paths they preserve.
This makes it possible to ask more precise questions. At which layer does incommensurability
occur? Does one community lack a representation corresponding to another’s target? Does it possess
the representation but assign low cost to the relevant prediction error?
Does the disagreement
concern the state representation itself, or the action plan that would follow from it? MIM does not
dissolve the epistemological problem, but it provides a vocabulary for locating it.
Perspectivism can also be given a constructive interpretation. A perspective is not merely a
subjective standpoint or a private opinion. It can be modeled as a configuration of target-formation
rules, representation-extraction rules, foregrounding tendencies, and directional maturity. In this
sense, a perspective may be described by components of W α
t , including Zα
phase,t, Rα
t , and Ωα
t . Such
a description does not reduce perspectives to arbitrary biases. It makes them objects of analysis:
what is selected, what is preserved, what is costly, and what cannot be updated without damaging
the structure of the world model.
The long tension between correspondence and coherence theories of truth can likewise be re-
stated. Correspondence theories preserve contact with external constraint; coherence theories pre-
serve the internal organization of state representations. MIM does not force one to choose between
them as exhaustive alternatives. It allows one to ask which coarse region and which target-relative
representation is being evaluated. A prediction error may concern contact with the world, coher-
ence among representations, violation of a norm, or damage to a relation. These are different error
structures, not merely different rhetorical styles.
12.2
Ethics and social philosophy: diversity as a constructive condition
In ethics and social philosophy, MIM suggests that value conflict often involves incompatible or
non-processable state representations, not merely competing preferences. What one subject treats
as a practical inconvenience may be experienced by another as a violation of dignity or trust. What
one institution treats as procedural fairness may be experienced by another group as the repetition
of a historically costly error. These conflicts cannot be adequately modeled as scalar preference
conflicts alone.
Meaning and value become ethical objects when certain state representations, prediction errors,
41


## Page 42


and update paths are treated as needing preservation. A value is not simply a preferred outcome.
It is a distribution of error costs and update priorities within a world model. Harm is not only
negative utility; it is the destruction or invalidation of state representations that a subject or
community treats as requiring protection. Justice can then be described as a social rule for which
representations, prediction errors, and transformations are protected across subjects.
This yields a constructive account of why cognitive diversity has ethical value. A society com-
posed of subjects with identical profile states would detect only a narrow range of possible errors.
Diversity is not only a moral ideal; it is also a functional condition for the generalization capacity of
social world models. Different subjects foreground different regions of error. Some preserve empiri-
cal friction; others preserve structural regularity; others preserve existential injury; others preserve
ideal coherence. A society that loses one of these sensitivities may become efficient in the short
term while blind to entire classes of error.
Respect for another subject can therefore be restated as a non-reductive operation: the goal is
not to assimilate the other subject into one’s own representation, but to construct processability
without erasing the other’s profile state. Levinas’s concern with the irreducibility of the Other and
Buber’s I–Thou relation can be reread as ethical resistance to representational collapse. In MIM
terms, the question is how Φ can make another subject’s state representation processable without
converting it into a mere instance of one’s own target structure.
12.3
Political philosophy: democratic disagreement as alignment under non-
identity
Democratic disagreement is often described as a failure of education, rationality, information sharing,
or good faith.
These factors matter, but they do not exhaust the problem.
Political conflict
frequently occurs among groups that appear to share words such as justice, freedom, dignity, security,
equality, or responsibility, while attaching those words to different state representations and error
costs.
MIM allows democratic disagreement to be modeled as interaction among heterogeneous world
models that nevertheless require shared procedures for processability. The aim of democratic insti-
tutions is not simply to force convergence on a single representation. It is to provide procedures
through which incompatible or partially incompatible representations can be made visible, con-
tested, transformed, and coordinated without requiring total assimilation.
This perspective connects deliberative democracy and pluralism in a new way. Deliberation
requires some degree of processability: participants must be able to reconstruct what another subject
is trying not to lose. Pluralism requires that this reconstruction not become forced identity. A
political order can therefore be analyzed by asking what kinds of Φ it institutionalizes: does it
translate existential injury into legal form without erasing it? Does it translate statistical evidence
into public meaning without distorting it? Does it allow structural constraints to become intelligible
to subjects oriented toward immediate experience, and vice versa?
12.4
Methodology of social science: qualitative and quantitative methods
The distinction between natural science and social science is often drawn in terms of observer-
dependence. MIM suggests another framing. Natural-scientific observation often foregrounds em-
pirical and structural coarse regions: measurable events, reproducible relations, and externally
auditable variables. Social-scientific observation often requires existential and ideal coarse regions:
meaning, interpretation, relation, identity, and normative horizon. But both kinds of inquiry pass
through target selection and state-representation extraction.
42


## Page 43


The methodological tension between qualitative and quantitative research can be reread accord-
ingly. Qualitative research often preserves state representations that would be lost under premature
operationalization: situated meaning, relational context, narrative significance, and lived injury.
Quantitative research preserves other state representations: comparability, reproducibility, struc-
tural relation, and population-level regularity. The question is not which is genuinely scientific in
the abstract. The question is what prediction errors each method preserves and what it tends to
discard.
MIM provides a vocabulary for designing mixed methods that are not merely additive.
A
qualitative phase may identify candidate targets and high-cost errors that a quantitative phase
can later operationalize. A quantitative phase may reveal structural regularities that qualitative
interpretation can make processable for subjects. A social-scientific method can thus be evaluated
by how well it preserves and transforms state representations across coarse regions.
12.5
AI ethics and design: supporting diversity without collapsing it
For AI ethics and design, the central question becomes whether AI systems can support human
diversity without either amplifying fragmentation or collapsing difference into a single normative
form.
Current recommender systems, dialogue agents, educational systems, and policy-support
tools often optimize for immediate preference signals, engagement, or short-term task completion.
These objectives can unintentionally privilege subjects who absorb prediction error through imme-
diate action and short feedback cycles. Subjects who require suspension, long-horizon reflection, or
symbolic integration may be structurally misread as slow, uncooperative, vague, or indecisive.
MIM suggests a different design orientation. An AI system should not merely ask what answer
a user prefers. It should ask what inferential target the user is forming, what state representation is
being protected, what prediction error is costly, and what update path the user can accept. In some
cases, the appropriate support is not to prompt immediate action, but to preserve ambiguity long
enough for a more adequate representation to form. In other cases, the appropriate support is to
translate a long-horizon ideal representation into an actionable plan without flattening its meaning.
This reframes value alignment. RLHF, DPO, and constitutional AI address important layers
of alignment, but they typically operate at the level of preferences, instructions, or normative
constraints. MIM places such approaches within a broader hierarchy: value alignment ΦU, state-
representation alignment ΦT , and world-model alignment ΦW . The higher layers ask whether an
AI system can preserve the structure of what a subject is trying to represent, not merely whether
it can satisfy a preference or avoid an objectionable output.
An AI system that implements multi-phase inference internally could also disclose its own fore-
grounding tendencies. It might indicate that a response is primarily structural, empirical, ideal, or
existential in its coarse orientation; that empirical evidence is thin; that existential cost is high; or
that a proposed action plan preserves one representation while damaging another. Such disclosure
would not make the AI conscious. It would make its world-model operation more processable for
users.
12.6
Collective scale: culture, tradition, and civilization
The preceding analysis can be extended from individuals to groups. A culture can be described,
in a first approximation, as a distribution of profile states, state representations, error costs, and
update practices across a group. Members of a culture may not share identical beliefs, but they
may share characteristic tendencies in what becomes meaningful, what is protected, what counts as
a serious error, and which transformations are acceptable.
43


## Page 44


Tradition can then be interpreted as intergenerational transmission of profile-state distributions.
Education, ritual, language, bodily skill, narrative, institutional design, and technical media func-
tion as mechanisms for preserving or modifying Ωacross time. A tradition is not only a set of
propositions; it is a way of making some targets and errors easier to form than others.
Civilization can be described at a still longer time scale as the stabilization of collective state
representations into institutions, technologies, archives, infrastructures, sciences, arts, and practices.
A civilization is a collective world model environmentalized across material and symbolic media.
From this perspective, historical transitions can be studied as changes in the distribution of coarse-
region foregrounding, representational maturity, and institutionalized alignment maps.
This proposal is not intended as a completed theory of culture or civilization. It marks a research
path. Multi-agent simulations, historical corpus analysis, ethnographic coding, and LLM-mediated
modeling can be used to ask how collective profile states emerge, stabilize, fracture, or transform.
The same formal devices used for individual cognition may therefore provide a bridge to cultural
dynamics and social fragmentation.
12.7
Implementation paths and technical research
The technical research program has at least four components. First, one can construct multi-agent
simulations in which agents differ in Ω, foregrounding field R, and effective phase-formation space.
These simulations can test when heterogeneous agents cooperate, polarize, ignore one another, or
develop alignment maps.
Second, one can train representation-learning models to infer candidate targets, state represen-
tations, and error costs from natural-language interaction. Such models would not merely classify
sentiment or stance. They would attempt to infer what a subject is estimating and what prediction
error the subject is trying not to lose.
Third, LLM-based systems can be designed to generate candidate Φ transformations. Given a
representation from subject α and a profile estimate for subject β, the system can propose several
transformations and evaluate whether β can process the representation while α judges that the
relevant error has been preserved.
Fourth, one can design user interfaces that make world-model differences visible without forcing
premature agreement. Such interfaces might display candidate targets, protected representations,
error costs, and possible transformation paths. In deliberative settings, this would shift AI support
from answer generation to processability construction.
12.8
Relation to neighboring fields
MIM also provides a bridge to cognitive linguistics, phenomenology, social cognition, cultural an-
thropology, and human-computer interaction. Lakoff’s account of embodied categories, Husserl’s
phenomenological reduction, Gibson’s affordances, Wittgenstein’s language games, and cultural-
anthropological descriptions of meaning can all be reread as observations about target formation,
state representation, and processability. This does not reduce those traditions to computation. It
makes their insights available for computational comparison.
More generally, epistemology, ethics, political philosophy, social-scientific methodology, and AI
design can share a formal vocabulary without losing their differences. That is the point of a phase-
formation framework. It provides a coordinate system in which heterogeneous questions can remain
heterogeneous while becoming comparable.

12.9
Scope and next horizon
The next horizon is not a universal theory of mind. It is a tractable program for measuring when
different subjects are estimating different things from the same observations, and for designing
transformations that make those differences processable. If this can be measured, then disagreement,
polarization, ethical conflict, and miscommunication can be reformulated at a more precise level.
This parent paper therefore serves as a theoretical timestamp. It fixes the vocabulary needed
for later work: phase-formation space, foregrounding field, profile state, alignment map, coarse
region, action constraint, and feedback update. Subsequent work can specialize this vocabulary
into algorithms, experiments, datasets, interfaces, simulations, and institutional designs without
losing the conceptual object that motivated it.

Conclusion
The question from which this paper began is simple: why do we so often encounter the same meeting,
document, report, news event, or public statement, and yet arrive at different thoughts, feelings, and
conclusions? Philosophy has asked this question for more than two millennia. Cognitive science has
observed its empirical forms. AI research now gives us a language for reformulating it in terms of
world models, state representations, prediction errors, and alignment. This paper has proposed one
structural answer: human intelligence is multi-phase. The same observations can activate different
inferential targets, different state representations, different costly prediction errors, and different
update paths.
The Multi-Phase Inference Mechanism develops this answer constructively. Its central devices
are the phase-formation space Zphase, the foregrounding field R, and the alignment map Φ. Together
they provide a formal basis for describing cognitive diversity not as a lack of rationality, information,
or value, but as a structural property of world-model operation. The profile state Ω= (θ, ζ, σ)
and the four coarse regions serve as supporting devices for describing how such diversity becomes
patterned, stabilized, and, in some cases, fixed.
This reframing also changes how philosophical traditions appear.
Empiricism and idealism,
structuralism and existentialism, early and late Wittgenstein, phenomenology and naturalism, lin-
guistic relativity, dual-process theory, and cognitive typology can be reread not as failed attempts
to converge on a single answer, but as traditions that preserved different sufficient statistics and dif-
ferent costly prediction errors. Philosophy is not a pile of discarded errors. It is a distributed record
of the routes by which human beings have discovered and refined different paths of world-model
update. The aim is not to replace philosophy with computation, but to give questions about recog-
nition, meaning, value, disagreement, and practice a constructive vocabulary that can be formalized,
tested, and mediated by AI systems.
AI alignment and social fragmentation also appear differently.
Alignment is not exhausted
by the task of making AI systems conform to a single human value vector. It becomes the task
of constructing processability among AI and humans, humans and humans, AI systems and AI
systems, and even among different phases within one subject. Social fragmentation is not merely
the failure to persuade people into the same conclusion. It is often the failure to preserve different
prediction errors while making them mutually processable. Value alignment is therefore only one
layer within a broader hierarchy: value-layer alignment ΦU, state-representation alignment ΦT , and
world-model alignment ΦW .
The ultimate object of alignment in this paper is not a state representation, nor even a world
model itself. It is the shared recognition that different world models coexist. Understanding is not
the sharing of an identical representation. It is the reconstruction of a state representation into a
45


## Page 46


form that can be processed within another world model. Cooperation is not the identity of subjects.
It is the construction of processability while preserving non-identical prediction errors.
People do not change easily. One reason is that past processing accumulates. Some patterns
remain flexible; others sediment; still others crystallize or become bedrock. Learning history de-
scends into the lower layers of a world model and constrains present processing. Yet not everything
is fixed. Some layers remain reconfigurable. The task of theory is not to dissolve all bedrock at
once. It is to identify where passages can still be made and where transformations can preserve
what each side is trying not to lose.
AI that understands self and others is therefore not a device for erasing differences among phases.
It is a device for making visible that different phases coexist, within persons and across society. The
work opened by this paper is to return, with this formal device, to philosophical traditions, social
fragmentation, cognitive typology, and AI design, and to ask what becomes newly visible when
differences are treated not as noise to be removed, but as structured world-model operations to be
made processable.

Appendix B: Remarks on the Formal Status of MIM
B.1 Descriptive formalization and executable implementation
The equations in this paper should be read at two levels. At the first level, they are descriptive
formalizations.
They specify the relations among observations, inferential targets, state repre-
sentations, prediction errors, foregrounding fields, profile states, action constraints, feedback, and
alignment maps. At this level, the purpose is to prevent conceptual collapse: a target is not a repre-
sentation, a representation is not a value, a prediction error is not yet an action, and an alignment
map is not a forced agreement.
At the second level, these equations identify components that can later be implemented. A
concrete implementation would need to instantiate candidate generation, scoring, routing, plan
evaluation, and update rules. The present paper does not choose one implementation because doing
so too early would narrow the theoretical object.
The same architecture may be implemented
probabilistically, neurally, symbolically, or through hybrid LLM-mediated procedures. What must
be preserved across implementations is the distinction between target formation, representation
extraction, error evaluation, foregrounding, action, feedback, and processability.
This distinction is important for evaluating the contribution of MIM. The paper should not be
read as claiming to provide a ready-made estimator. It provides a formal location for the estima-
tor. It also specifies what would be lost if a future implementation optimized only for preference
satisfaction, summarization quality, or agreement rate.
B.2 Processability versus agreement
A central claim of this paper is that understanding is not identical representation sharing. Two sub-
jects may understand one another better while continuing to disagree. Conversely, two subjects may
utter similar propositions while failing to process one another’s state representations. Processability
therefore names a weaker and more precise relation than agreement.
In experimental terms, processability can be operationalized by asking whether a receiving
subject can reconstruct the relevant target, state representation, and costly prediction error of
another subject. A subject need not endorse the other representation in order to process it. It is
47


## Page 48


sufficient that the representation becomes available as a candidate object of reasoning rather than
being rejected as noise, irrationality, or irrelevant emotion.
This also clarifies the role of AI. An AI system designed for processability should not merely
average positions or produce neutral summaries.
It should preserve the error that each side is
attempting not to lose.
In some cases, the most useful output may be a map of incompatible
representations rather than a recommendation. In other cases, it may be a transformation that
allows a structural representation to become intelligible to an existentially oriented subject, or an
existential representation to become actionable within a procedural context.
B.3 Why coarse regions are retained
The empirical, ideal, structural, and existential regions are retained because they are useful explana-
tory handles. They should not be understood as exhaustive ontological categories. They are coarse
regions in a higher-dimensional phase-formation space. Their purpose is to connect formal modeling
to vocabularies that have appeared repeatedly in philosophy, social theory, practical communication,
and cognitive typology.
Removing these terms entirely would make the model cleaner but less readable. Treating them
as fixed bases would make the model more intuitive but less general.
The present paper takes
an intermediate path. It uses the four terms as coarse explanatory regions while locating the real
formal object in Zphase, R, and Ω. In this way, the model can preserve familiar observations without
turning them into discrete essences.
B.4 Minimal empirical path
A minimal empirical program can begin without implementing the entire architecture. One can
present the same observation to multiple subjects and ask them to describe what the observation is
about, what state of the world it indicates, what error or risk it raises, and what action or reflection
it calls for. These responses can be coded as candidate targets, state representations, error costs, and
plan candidates. The degree to which another subject can reconstruct those components provides
an initial measure of processability.
A second step is to introduce AI-generated transformations. Given a representation from sub-
ject α, an AI system can generate several candidate transformations for subject β. These can be
evaluated by whether β can process the representation, whether α judges that the relevant error was
preserved, and whether new plan candidates become available. This procedure would not yet prove
MIM, but it would begin to test whether the formal distinctions introduced in the paper improve
measurement and mediation.
B.5 Relation to future technical work
Future technical work can proceed in at least three directions. First, simulation studies can de-
fine agents with different Ωand test how foregrounding and alignment affect cooperation. Sec-
ond, representation-learning studies can train models to infer targets and error costs from natural-
language interaction. Third, LLM-based systems can be designed to produce alignment maps that
preserve target-relative error structure across subjects.
The present preprint is intended to make these future projects referable. It fixes the conceptual
vocabulary before implementation choices narrow the space. That is why the theory is developed
at length before algorithmic details are specified. The goal of the first full theory version is not to
close the research program, but to make the program precise enough to begin.
