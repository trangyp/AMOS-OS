ABSTRACT
High-redshift active galactic nuclei (AGN) serve as powerful probes of early black-hole growth, galaxy formation, and the evolving
intergalactic medium (IGM). In this work, we use Lumina, a cosmological radiation-hydrodynamic simulation spanning the
epochs of hydrogen and helium reionization, which combines a large (500 cMpc)3 volume with 2 × 60003 resolution elements,
to explore high-redshift AGN. The simulation self-consistently follows hundreds of millions of galaxies and supermassive black
holes (SMBHs), together with their impact on the ionization and thermal state of the IGM. We exploit this uniquely large dynamic
range to predict multi-band AGN luminosity functions (LFs) at 𝑧≥3, from hard X-rays to the mid-infrared. These predictions
encompass both moderately luminous quasars and the faint “Little Red Dots” (LRDs) uncovered by JWST. We develop an
empirical model that maps simulated SMBHs onto observed AGN using bolometric and extinction/absorption corrections for
canonical AGN and LRDs, and in which SMBHs with 𝑀BH ≤10 𝑀seed ∼107 M⊙stay in the LRD phase with a duty cycle
of 30%. This simple framework reproduces the observed LFs and clustering of LRDs. Meanwhile, the pre-JWST quasar LF
constraints are recovered, although we find that a ∼0.3 dex log-normal scatter in bolometric luminosity is required to reproduce
the bright end. We place the simulated AGN population in the cosmological context by quantifying the redshift evolution of
AGN and LRD number densities, and their contributions to the integrated BH mass densities. The same AGN population is the
dominant driver for the He ii reionization modelled self-consistently in Lumina. This empirical AGN model paves the way for
general population-synthesis models of high-redshift AGN, including LRDs, in a unified cosmological framework.

Active galactic nuclei (AGN), and luminous quasars in particular,
are the observable manifestation of gas accretion onto supermassive
black holes (SMBHs) at the centres of most galaxies. The accreted
gas forms a hot, dissipative disc (e.g., Shakura & Sunyaev 1973; Rees

SMBH population, as most of the local SMBH mass density was built
up during the radiatively efficient accretion phases that we observe as
AGN (Soltan 1982). AGN are also among the most important sources
of high-energy radiation in the Universe, contributing significantly to

Beyond their role as energy sources, AGN are tightly coupled
to the evolution of their host galaxies. The masses of SMBHs are
correlated with the masses, luminosities, and velocity dispersions of
their host bulges (e.g., Magorrian et al. 1998; Ferrarese & Merritt
2000; Gebhardt et al. 2000; Gültekin et al. 2009). AGN feedback
is widely invoked to regulate or quench star-formation in massive
galaxies (e.g., Sanders & Mirabel 1996; Springel et al. 2005; Di
Matteo et al. 2005; Bower et al. 2006; Croton et al. 2006; Hopkins
et al. 2006; Sijacki et al. 2007; Somerville et al. 2008; Hopkins et al.
2008; Feruglio et al. 2010; Fabian 2012; Cicone et al. 2014) and to
resolve the classical “cooling-flow” problem (e.g., Cowie & Binney
1977; Fabian & Nulsen 1977; Fabian et al. 1984; Tabor & Binney
1993; Fabian 1994; Croton et al. 2006). Recent reviews summarize
the breadth of these connections (Alexander & Hickox 2012; Fabian
2012; Kormendy & Ho 2013; Heckman & Best 2014; Alexander
et al. 2025, and references therein).
Studying the cosmic evolution of the AGN population is therefore
central to both cosmology and galaxy formation. The measurement
of AGN abundance, i.e. luminosity functions (LFs), has a decades-
long history in the rest-frame optical/UV (e.g., Schmidt 1968; Boyle
et al. 1988; Warren et al. 1994; Fan et al. 2001, 2004; Richards et al.
2006a; Glikman et al. 2011; Ross et al. 2013; McGreer et al. 2013;
Kashikawa et al. 2015; Jiang et al. 2016; Niida et al. 2020), soft
X-ray (e.g., Maccacaro et al. 1991; Boyle et al. 1993; Jones et al.
1997; Page et al. 1997; Miyaji et al. 2000; Hasinger et al. 2005), hard
X-ray (e.g., Ueda et al. 2003; La Franca et al. 2005; Barger et al.
2005; Silverman et al. 2008; Ebrero et al. 2009; Yencho et al. 2009;
Aird et al. 2010; Ueda et al. 2014; Aird et al. 2015; Ananna et al.
2019), and IR (e.g., Brown et al. 2006; Matute et al. 2006; Assef et al.
2011; Lacy et al. 2015; Bulichi et al. 2026; Ling et al. 2026) bands.
These measurements have revealed strong redshift evolution of AGN
LFs (both in normalization and in shape; e.g., Cowie et al. 1996;
Barger et al. 2005; Hasinger et al. 2005; Aird et al. 2015; Kulkarni
et al. 2019), typical AGN spectral shape (e.g., Wilkes et al. 1994;
Green et al. 1995; Vignali et al. 2003; Strateva et al. 2005; Richards
et al. 2006b; Steffen et al. 2006; Just et al. 2007; Lusso et al. 2010;
Kashikawa et al. 2015; Lusso & Risaliti 2016), and the obscuring
column-density distribution (e.g., Hill et al. 1996; Simpson et al.
1999; Willott et al. 2000; Steffen et al. 2003; Ueda et al. 2003;
Grimes et al. 2004; Sazonov & Revnivtsev 2004; Barger et al. 2005;
Hao et al. 2005; Ueda et al. 2014; Ananna et al. 2019). Syntheses of
these multi-band measurements provided a comprehensive pre-JWST
view of the AGN population from 𝑧≃0 to 𝑧≃7 (Hopkins et al.
2007; Shen et al. 2020a).
Despite this rich, established picture, the rapid decline of the AGN
number density beyond 𝑧≃6 made it particularly difficult to con-
strain AGN LFs at the highest redshifts, which correspond to early
SMBH growth phases. Pre-JWST wide-field optical and near-IR sur-
veys identified ≳500 luminous quasars at 𝑧≳5.3 (e.g., Willott et al.
2010; Venemans et al. 2013, 2015; Jiang et al. 2016; Reed et al. 2017;
Mazzucchelli et al. 2017; Matsuoka et al. 2018; Ross & Cross 2020;
Fan et al. 2023) and pushed the redshift frontier to 𝑧≳7 (e.g., Mort-
lock et al. 2011; Bañados et al. 2018; Yang et al. 2020; Wang et al.
2021; Fujimoto et al. 2022). These observations provided key probes
of early SMBH growth and of the hydrogen reionization history
through their absorption spectra (e.g., Miralda-Escudé 1998; Madau
& Rees 2000; Fan et al. 2002, 2006). However, these samples were
dominated by the rare, hyperluminous tip of the AGN population,
and the demographics of moderate- and low-luminosity AGN at high
redshift, which likely dominate the integrated SMBH mass-growth
budget, remained essentially unconstrained.
JWST has dramatically transformed the landscape of AGN studies
at high redshifts. Deep JWST NIRSpec and NIRCam Grism surveys
have already uncovered hundreds of broad-line AGN (BLAGN) at
4 ≲𝑧≲11 (e.g., Harikane et al. 2023b; Maiolino et al. 2024; Greene
et al. 2024; Matthee et al. 2024; Kokorev et al. 2024; Akins et al.
2025b; Kocevski et al. 2025), with spectroscopically identified can-
didates extending to 𝑧> 10 (Bogdán et al. 2024; Napolitano et al.
2025; Maiolino et al. 2025). Meanwhile, deep JWST MIRI and X-
ray surveys are beginning to probe the obscured high-redshift AGN
population (e.g., Bulichi et al. 2026; Hviding et al. 2026; Ling et al.
2026). Faint AGN appear to be far more abundant in the early Uni-
verse than predicted by extrapolations of pre-JWST quasar LFs, with
potentially important implications for the high-redshift ionizing pho-
ton budget and build-up of the cosmic SMBH mass density (Madau
et al. 2024; Asthana et al. 2025; Inayoshi & Ho 2025).
Among the most striking JWST discoveries is the population of
“Little Red Dots” (LRDs), compact sources with characteristic “V-
shaped” spectral energy distributions (SEDs) that feature a blue UV
continuum, a steep red optical continuum, and a turnover near the
Balmer limit (e.g., Matthee et al. 2024; Kokorev et al. 2024; Akins
et al. 2025b; Kocevski et al. 2025; Setton et al. 2025b). Spectroscopic
follow-up has revealed broad Balmer lines in the majority of the ob-
served LRDs (e.g., Greene et al. 2024; Matthee et al. 2024; Maiolino
et al. 2024; Hviding et al. 2025), supporting an AGN origin for at
least the dominant red continuum component. Yet LRDs differ in
essentially every other respect from canonical Type-I/Type-II AGN:
they are systematically X-ray weak or undetected (e.g., Yue et al.
2024; Ananna et al. 2024; Maiolino et al. 2025), faint or absent in
the mid- and far-IR (e.g., Setton et al. 2025a; Casey et al. 2025), and
exhibit little or no continuum variability (at least in the majority; e.g.,
Hayes et al. 2024; Kokubo & Harikane 2025; Tee et al. 2025; Zhang
et al. 2025b; Liu et al. 2026, but see also Zhang et al. 2025a). Many
show prominent Balmer breaks and Balmer absorption features (e.g.,
Matthee et al. 2024; Naidu et al. 2025; de Graaff et al. 2025c; Setton
et al. 2025b; Ji et al. 2025) that are difficult to explain by simple
dust-reddened Type-I AGN. The same sources are often inferred to
host BHs that are over-massive relative to their host stellar masses
(e.g., Pacucci et al. 2023; Maiolino et al. 2024; Inayoshi & Ho 2025;
Durodola et al. 2025), although the interpretation depends sensitively
on the assumed line-broadening mechanism (e.g., Naidu et al. 2025;
Brazzini et al. 2025; Chang et al. 2026; Ji et al. 2026a; Rusakov
et al. 2026) and selection effects (e.g., Li et al. 2025). Whether LRDs
represent a transient, early-growth phase of more typical SMBHs,
a fundamentally distinct population, or a heterogeneous mixture of
both remains an open question.
Interpreting these new observational findings, however, is com-
plicated by the fact that observations in any single band are subject
to selection effects, host-galaxy contamination, and obscuration in a
wavelength-dependent manner. Although AGN are intrinsically very
luminous in the optical/UV, dust extinction along certain viewing
angles (e.g., Antonucci 1993; Urry & Padovani 1995) can render
them difficult to detect, and the optical/UV emission of heavily ob-

Mid-IR observations from the ground are limited by atmospheric

picture of AGN demographics over cosmic time.
Specifically for LRDs, the physical interpretation is evolving
rapidly. One emerging class of models invokes accreting BHs em-
bedded in extremely dense, optically thick gas envelopes that re-
process the emergent emission from the central engine into a “star-
like” (but not identical to stars) optical spectrum (e.g., Inayoshi &
Maiolino 2025; Naidu et al. 2025; de Graaff et al. 2025c; Kido et al.
2025; Rusakov et al. 2026), possibly forming a quasi-star config-
uration (e.g., Begelman et al. 2006, 2008; Volonteri & Begelman
2010; Begelman & Dexter 2026). A complementary scenario in-
vokes super-Eddington accretion onto modest-mass seed BHs as the
explanation for X-ray weakness, and the lack of variability in LRDs
(e.g., Lambrides et al. 2024; Pacucci & Narayan 2024; Madau &
Haardt 2024; Lupi et al. 2024; Inayoshi et al. 2025). Whether LRDs
trace heavy seeds (e.g., direct-collapse BHs), light seeds undergoing
efficient early growth, or some combination of both remains a major
theoretical question that connects directly to early SMBH-seeding
scenarios (e.g., Bromm & Loeb 2003; Begelman et al. 2006; Lodato
& Natarajan 2006; Devecchi & Volonteri 2009; Xiao et al. 2021;
Shen et al. 2025a; Jiang et al. 2026). A unified cosmological frame-
work that can simultaneously address the abundance and large-scale
environments of JWST-selected faint AGN together with luminous
quasars is therefore needed.
Theoretical efforts to interpret the high-redshift AGN population
have progressed in parallel with JWST observations. Large-volume
cosmological hydrodynamic simulations, such as Illustris (Vogels-
berger et al. 2013, 2014a,b), EAGLE (Schaye et al. 2015; Crain
et al. 2015), IllustrisTNG (Pillepich et al. 2018b,a; Springel et al.
2018), SIMBA (Davé et al. 2019), Horizon-AGN (Dubois et al. 2016;
Volonteri et al. 2016), BlueTides (Feng et al. 2016), ASTRID (Bird
et al. 2022; Ni et al. 2022), MillenniumTNG (Pakmor et al. 2023),
and FLAMINGO (Schaye et al. 2023), have established a coher-
ent framework for modelling the growth and feedback of SMBHs
alongside their co-evolution with host galaxies in a cosmological
context. Cross-comparison studies (e.g., Habouzit et al. 2021, 2022)
highlight that the predicted high-redshift AGN LFs and BH-host
scaling relations are sensitive to the choice of seeding prescription,
sub-resolution recipes for BH accretion and AGN feedback, motivat-
ing more theoretical experiments. Smaller-volume zoom and more
dedicated suites, such as BRAHMA (Bhowmick et al. 2024a,b),
NewHorizon (Dubois et al. 2021), and OBELISK (Trebitsch et al.
2021), systematically explored seed channels, accretion regimes, and
feedback prescriptions, while semi-analytic and empirical frame-
works, such as CAT (Trinca et al. 2022, 2024), DELPHI (Dayal et al.
2014, 2019), TRINITY (Zhang et al. 2023), L-Galaxies (Bonoli et al.
2025), and the Santa Cruz SAM (Yung et al. 2021), efficiently ex-
plored the high-redshift SMBH model parameter space.
In this paper, we use Lumina — a large-volume (500 cMpc)3
cosmological radiation-hydrodynamic simulation with an extreme
dynamical range of 2 × 60003 adaptive resolution elements that fol-
lows galaxies, AGN, and the intergalactic medium (IGM) through
hydrogen and helium reionization down to 𝑧= 3 — to make predic-
tions for multi-band AGN LFs at 𝑧≥3 across the electromagnetic
spectrum, from hard X-rays to the mid-IR. Lumina is designed to
play a complementary role to the theoretical efforts described above.
The large volume samples bright quasars together with a statistically
representative, well-resolved population of galaxies and faint AGN,
enabling joint predictions for AGN, host galaxies, and their distri-
butions on large scales coherently. In addition, Lumina is the first
simulation in this regime to include on-the-fly multi-frequency radia-
tive transfer through both hydrogen and He ii reionization, allowing
self-consistent modelling of the ionization and thermal state of the
IGM impacted by galaxies and AGN. This combination enables the
unified, multi-band view of high-redshift AGN in the cosmological
context that we develop in this paper. In Figure 1, we show a multi-
scale view of Lumina at 𝑧≃4 from the large-scale dark matter (DM)
distribution to a He iii bubble driven by a bright AGN to the smaller-
scale gas distribution and BHs, eventually to the host galaxy of the
AGN. In Figure 2, we give a more complete overview of the topology
of He ii reionization at 𝑧≃4, featuring large ∼10 −100 cMpc size
He iii bubbles created by AGN along with islands of He ii in voids.
Based on Lumina, we develop an empirical model to bridge BH
particles in the simulation with observed AGN, including the puz-
zling LRD population, and then use it to test whether the simulated
SMBH population can simultaneously reproduce several important
observables of high-redshift AGN, including LFs, large-scale clus-
tering measurements, and integrated cosmological constraints. The
paper is organized as follows. Section 2 summarizes the Lumina
simulation, including its galaxy formation and SMBH model, the
on-the-fly radiative transfer, and the structure-finding pipeline. Sec-
tion 3 describes the empirical model that maps simulated SMBHs
to observed AGN. Section 4 presents the predicted multi-band AGN
LFs at 𝑧≃3 −6, the large-scale clustering of LRDs, and the role of
AGN in the cosmological context. Section 5 discusses the underlying
assumptions and limitations of our framework, and compares it to
other theoretical models. Conclusions are presented in Section 6.
2 SIMULATION
Lumina is a cosmological radiation-hydrodynamic simulation that
evolves 2 × 60003 resolution elements in an 𝐿box = 500 cMpc box
from 𝑧init = 49 down to 𝑧= 3, spanning the epochs of hydrogen
and He ii reionization. This combination of volume and resolution
provides the dynamic range required to follow simultaneously the
galaxy population that drives hydrogen reionization and the rare,
bright quasars that drive He ii reionization. The simulation is run
with the moving-mesh code Arepo (Springel 2010; Pakmor et al.
2016; Weinberger et al. 2020), that solves the cosmological Euler
equations on an unstructured moving Voronoi mesh using a finite
volume Godunov method (Godunov & Bohachevsky 1959). Gravity
is solved with an adaptive Tree-PM scheme (Bagla 2002; Bagla &
Ray 2003). It is coupled to the GPU-accelerated radiative-transfer
module Arepo-rt (Kannan et al. 2019; Zier et al. 2024). Similar
to Mtng (Pakmor et al. 2023), magnetic fields are not evolved. We
adopt a flat ΛCDM cosmology consistent with Planck 2018 (Planck
Collaboration et al. 2020), which we summarize in Table 1. Initial

2.3 Radiative transfer
We solve the radiation-transport equations on the moving Voronoi

after hydrogen reionization is complete, the radiation field below

Faucher-Giguère et al. (2009), and only a single high-energy bin rel-

et al. (2026), AGN dominate the photon production in this remaining

to a non-equilibrium thermo-chemical network for primordial gas

2.4 Structure finding
DM haloes are identified using the FoF algorithm (Davis et al. 1985)
with a linking length of 0.2 times the mean inter-particle separa-
tion. Gas, stellar, and BH particles are associated with the FoF group
of their nearest DM particle if they lie within one linking length.
Each FoF group is then processed with the subfind-hbt algorithm
(Springel et al. 2001, 2021), which iteratively identifies gravitation-
ally bound substructures (subhaloes) and, unlike the original sub-
find, tracks subhalo membership across snapshots, improving the
identification of infalling systems through mergers (Han et al. 2012).
Following common practice, we identify subhaloes as galaxies, des-
ignating the most massive subhalo of each FoF group as the central
and the remainder as satellites. Because FoF masses can be inflated
by percolation via “particle bridges”, we report spherical-overdensity
quantities of DM haloes: 𝑅200c, defined as the radius enclosing an
average density of 200 times the critical density 𝜌c(𝑧), and the cor-
responding enclosed mass 𝑀200c. All BH particles in the simulation
enter the AGN observable predictions. The host galaxy of a BH is
identified as the subhalo to which it is gravitationally bound.
Lumina records the full set of properties for every BH particle
at high time cadence, enabling the construction of AGN light cones
alongside other quantities intended for sightline-resolved surveys
(Smith et al. 2026). Figure 3 shows a representative 3.6◦×3.6◦pencil-
beam realization from 𝑧≃3 to 𝑧≃6. Each point is placed according
to its sightline comoving distance and transverse position, with colour
indicating bolometric luminosity and size indicating BH mass. This
visualization highlights the wide dynamic range of the simulated
AGN population and the ability of Lumina to connect individual
SMBHs to their evolving large-scale environments. Future studies
will combine these light-cone catalogues with BH merger trees to
study the temporal variability of accretion, feedback, and clustering.
3 AN EMPIRICAL MODEL LINKING SMBHS TO AGN
The BH particles and their mass accretion rates tracked in Lumina
represent the time- and kernel-averaged growth of BHs (plus any un-
resolved gas or stellar distributions around them), whereas observed
AGN are characterized by their multi-band emission along a par-
ticular line of sight. Bridging these two descriptions requires a set
of choices about how the resolved mean accretion rate is related to
the instantaneous bolometric luminosity, how that luminosity is dis-
tributed across the electromagnetic spectrum, and how the emerging
radiation is post-processed by circumnuclear and host-galaxy mate-
rial. In this section, we present a compact post-processing framework
that makes these choices explicit, with three ingredients: (i) a log-
normal scatter applied to the instantaneous bolometric luminosity of
each BH particle to capture unresolved AGN variability; (ii) a pair
of empirical SED templates, one for canonical Type-I AGN and one
for LRDs, with the mapping between BH particles and SED tem-
plates determined by BH mass; and (iii) a gas absorption and dust
extinction prescription applied to the canonical AGN population. The
framework is deliberately constructed to be portable. Although cal-
ibrated and applied here to the SMBH population of Lumina, none
of its ingredients depend on the specifics of the simulation, and the
same recipe can be applied to other cosmological simulations, semi-
analytic models, or empirical halo–BH mapping schemes to generate
high-redshift AGN population predictions.
3.1 Stochasticity of SMBH accretion
We first model an intrinsic stochasticity (variability) of SMBH ac-
cretion at the sub-resolution level by convolving the raw bolometric
LF computed from binned estimations of simulated SMBHs with a
Gaussian kernel of dispersion 𝜎bol. We then reduce the bolometric lu-
minosities systematically by Δ log 𝐿bol = ln 10 𝜎2
bol/2 (the mean shift
of a log-normal distribution) to ensure conservation of total energy
output from AGN. Empirically, we find that a scatter of 𝜎bol = 0.3
dex brings the predicted AGN LFs in Lumina into good agreement
with observational constraints of quasars (Shen et al. 2020a). This
will be later shown in Section 4.1. We keep it as the fiducial choice
and explore variations in Section 5.3. We note that the BH accretion
rates are capped at the Eddington limit in Lumina, but the empiri-
cal scatter we add here can effectively boost the accretion rates to
moderately super-Eddington.
Similar phenomenological treatments have been adopted in other
large-volume cosmological hydrodynamic simulations to account for
unresolved AGN variability as well. For example, Rosas-Guevara
et al. (2016) explored similar log-normal scatters of 0.3 −0.5 dex
for the AGN LFs in the EAGLE simulation. Ding et al. (2026) intro-
duced a larger scatter of 0.75 dex for quasar LFs in the FLAMINGO
simulation (Schaye et al. 2023) to bracket the impact of unresolved
small-scale variability. Such a scatter is motivated by the observed
variability of AGN across a broad hierarchy of timescales, from
hours in the X-ray corona (e.g., Vaughan et al. 2003; González-
Martín & Vaughan 2012), through months to years in the opti-
cal/UV “damped-random-walk” (DRW) regime (e.g., Kelly et al.
2009; MacLeod et al. 2010), to the ∼0.1 −100 Myr timescales in-
ferred from e.g. broad Eddington-ratio distribution function (ERDF)
measured at fixed host-galaxy properties (e.g., Hickox et al. 2014;
Aird et al. 2018). Most large-volume cosmological simulations can-
not directly resolve the clumpy multiphase gas structures, angular
momentum transport, accretion-disc-state transitions, and other rele-
vant small-scale physical processes that underlie these observational
phenomena. Recent ultra-high-resolution zoom-in simulations reveal
that gas inflow towards SMBHs becomes increasingly intermittent on
progressively smaller scales, driven by nuclear gas structure, gravita-
tional torques, stellar/AGN feedback, magnetic fields, and disc-scale
instabilities (e.g., Hopkins & Quataert 2010, 2011; Anglés-Alcázar
et al. 2017a,b, 2021; Hopkins et al. 2024; Hopkins 2025). We defer
a more thorough discussion of this prescription to Section 5.

capture systematic variations of AGN spectra with the physical state
of the accretion flow. At low Eddington ratios, the thin disc transi-

with a weak UV “big blue bump”, a harder X-ray spectrum, reduced
obscuration, and, at the lowest accretion rates, a disappearing broad-
line region (e.g., Ho 1999, 2008; Elitzur & Ho 2009; She et al.
2018). Su et al. (2026) recently showed that an accretion-disc-based
SED model predicts substantially lower hard X-ray luminosities at

AGN we study in Lumina. At the opposite extreme, near or above the
Eddington limit, photon advection through a slim disc reduces the ra-
diative efficiency, saturates the UV/optical continuum, and radiation-
pressure drives outflows (e.g., Paczyńsky & Wiita 1980; Abramowicz
et al. 1988; Sądowski 2011; Kubota & Done 2019; Jiang et al. 2019).

& Maiolino 2026), for which we adopt a separate empirical SED in
the following section. The 𝜎corr absorbs intra-population variations
within the canonical regime but not the qualitatively distinct SEDs
at either 𝜆Edd extreme. A self-consistent 𝜆Edd-dependent treatment is
deferred to future work.
3.3 LRD SED model
Compared to the canonical AGN SED model described above, LRDs
are notably faint in X-rays given their optical luminosities and often
show weak mid-IR and far-IR emission, which is challenging to
explain by simple dust-reddening of a standard Type-I AGN (e.g.,
Yue et al. 2024; Ananna et al. 2024; Maiolino et al. 2025; Casey
et al. 2025). Recent spectroscopic observations have motivated a
picture in which many LRDs host accreting SMBHs embedded in
extremely dense gas cocoons (e.g., Naidu et al. 2025; de Graaff et al.
2025c; Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025; Ji et al. 2025; Rusakov et al. 2026),
which generate “star-like” (but not identical to stars) blackbody SEDs
in the optical featuring strong Balmer breaks and Balmer absorption
features. Many recent works (e.g., de Graaff et al. 2025a; Umeda et al.
2026; Sun et al. 2026) have revealed a surprising quasi-universality
of LRD SEDs at least in the optical-to-near-IR. In this section, we
will attempt to build an effective universal SED model for LRDs. We
note that our approach is deliberately agnostic about the underlying
physical configuration of LRDs, whether they are “star-like” objects,
dust-obscured AGN, or something else entirely. We restrict ourselves
to constructing an empirical SED template from current observations
and to bracketing its associated uncertainties.

(“xLRDs”), which is the AGN-dominated subset with comparatively
minor host-galaxy continuum contamination and interpreted as LRD
engines therein. Sun et al. (2026) present a complementary stacked

under the “BH-star” picture. In addition, we compile the spectra of

3.4 Gas absorption and dust extinction
The absorption and scattering of surrounding gas and dust further
modify the intrinsic emission of AGN. The photoelectric absorp-
tion of gas influences X-ray emission, while dust plays a key role in
the optical and UV. Here, we first introduce a model for the neutral
hydrogen column density (𝑁H) distribution, which determines the
absorption in the X-ray. 𝑁H is then converted to the column density
of dust, assuming a dust-to-gas ratio, which determines the dust ex-
tinction in the optical and UV. We note that the absorption/extinction
model described below is applied to the canonical AGN. For LRDs,
we do not apply any additional correction on top of the SED we
adopt, since we assume that it has already been reprocessed by the
gas (and potentially dust) around the central BH.
Large-volume cosmological simulations cannot resolve the cir-
cumnuclear gas distribution and underestimate the gas column den-
sity along the line of sight (see the measurement done in Ni
et al. 2022). Therefore, an observation-driven empirical model is
required. Following Shen et al. (2020a), we adopt the 𝑁H dis-
tribution from Ueda et al. (2014), based on measurements of
𝑁H and intrinsic hard X-ray luminosity for individual objects in
their sample. The model provides the probability distribution of
𝑁H as a function of intrinsic hard X-ray luminosity and redshift.

4 RESULTS
4.1 Bolometric LFs
We begin with the bolometric LF of AGN. This quantity is particu-
larly well suited for comparison with theoretical models because it is
directly tied to the BH mass accretion rates and total energy output
predicted by simulations (see Equation 7). In Figure 8, we show the
bolometric LFs of AGN in Lumina at 𝑧≃3 −6. For comparison,
we also show results from TNG100 and Mtng. At the luminous end,
Lumina and Mtng show good convergence across redshift. At the
faint end, Lumina is converged with TNG100 at 𝑧≲4, whereas a
mild resolution dependence persists at higher redshift, with TNG100
predicting a higher abundance of low-luminosity AGN. Given this
residual resolution dependence, we remain conservative and avoid
drawing overly precise quantitative conclusions at the faint end at
≲0.2 dex level. Another notable feature is the upturn at the very
faint end (𝐿bol ≲1044 erg s−1). A similar feature is also visible in the
SMBH mass functions shown in Figure 7. These features are likely
associated with the relatively large seed BH mass adopted in the sim-
ulations, since the Eddington luminosity of our seed BHs happens
to be 𝐿Edd ∼1044.2 erg s−1. Newly seeded BHs are inserted directly
at 𝑀seed, and the unresolved early growth phase of lower-mass BHs
is therefore not explicitly followed. It is one of the key reasons for
us to develop the empirical model for low-mass BHs to encapsulate
physics in the unresolved phase. The feature can be amplified by
the duty-cycle of LRDs in our model, which suppresses the LRD
contribution to the bolometric LFs at the faint end.
We compare the simulation predictions to the pre-JWST obser-
vational constraints compiled by Shen et al. (2020a), which include
measurements in the UV, optical, soft and hard X-ray, and mid-IR
bands, and the best-fit model therein. These multi-wavelength LFs
are mapped onto the bolometric plane using bolometric corrections
therein, while preserving the offset in log Φ between each observed
data point and the best-fit model LF. We choose the “Global fit A”
from Shen et al. (2020a), which has a relatively steep faint-end slope
driven mainly by the UV data in that compilation. We note that, in
preparing these observational data, we keep the original Shen et al.
(2020a) model without any modifications (e.g., the change in 𝑓CTK)
introduced above. With our fiducial choice of 𝜎bol = 0.3 dex, the
bolometric LF in Lumina agrees well with the observational con-
straints for moderately luminous quasars with 𝐿bol ≳1046 erg s−1.
At even higher luminosities, the finite simulation volume becomes
insufficient to sample the rarest bright quasars. In Section 5.3, we

are broadly consistent with the predictions from Lumina. Mean-

discrepancy is expected, since the faint-end LF in Shen et al. (2020a)

AGN are intrinsically X-ray weak or heavily obscured by gas (e.g.,
Yue et al. 2024; Maiolino et al. 2025). We revisit this issue in Sec-
tion 4.4, where we compare the X-ray LFs directly.

4.4 X-ray LFs
Another critical validation of our model comes from the X-ray LFs of
AGN. Hard X-ray emission in canonical AGN is expected to trace the
compact corona associated with accretion onto BHs and is relatively
less affected by contamination from host-galaxy starlight than the
UV or optical bands (e.g., Osterbrock & Ferland 2006; Brandt &
Alexander 2015; Hickox & Alexander 2018). At the same time, the
X-ray band provides an important consistency check on our LRD
interpretation. Most LRDs and JWST-selected faint AGN appear to
be X-ray weak or undetected relative to expectations from standard
AGN scaling relations (e.g. Yue et al. 2024; Ananna et al. 2024;
Maiolino et al. 2025, see 𝛼ox discussed in Section 3.2), although the
recently identified “X-ray dot” (Hviding et al. 2026) may represent an
important exception in a transitional phase. Nevertheless, we assume
that all our selected LRD-like objects are X-ray dark. The key test
of our model is whether the remaining canonical AGN population in
the simulation can reproduce the observed X-ray LFs.

4.5 Mid-IR LFs
The final band we consider is the mid-IR. For canonical AGN, the
near- to mid-IR emission is generally interpreted as accretion-disc
radiation absorbed and reprocessed by circumnuclear dust, often as-
sociated with the dusty torus or more extended dusty polar structures
(e.g., Antonucci 1993; Urry & Padovani 1995; Fritz et al. 2006;
Netzer 2015; Ramos Almeida & Ricci 2017; Hickox & Alexander
2018), while the far-IR can receive substantial contributions from
colder dust heated by star-formation in the host galaxy. As discussed
in Section 3.3, the mid- to far-IR SEDs of LRDs remain highly uncer-
tain, and even the physical origin of their IR emission is still debated.
Some studies find little evidence for hot or cold dust emission in in-
dividual or stacked LRD samples, challenging simple dust-reddened
AGN or dusty-starburst interpretations (e.g., Setton et al. 2025a;
Chen et al. 2025a; Xiao et al. 2025). However, evidence for warm
or hot dust emission in LRDs has been reported in stacking analyses
(Delvecchio et al. 2025) and in individual sources (Ronayne et al.
2025; Barro et al. 2026). Therefore, the IR LF provides a complemen-
tary consistency check, similar in spirit to the X-ray LF. If LRDs are
relatively faint in the mid-IR compared to canonical obscured AGN,
then the IR LF should mainly validate whether the canonical AGN
population in the simulation remains consistent with observations.
Observational constraints in this regime have recently become
possible with deep JWST/MIRI surveys. Early MIRI results showed
that mid-IR selection can reveal a population of heavily obscured
high-redshift AGN missed by X-ray surveys (e.g., Yang et al. 2023;
Pérez-González et al. 2024; Lyu et al. 2024). More recently, the
MEOW survey (Leung in prep., Bulichi et al. 2026) used deep MIRI
imaging, together with NIRCam, spectroscopic, and HST data, to
identify obscured AGN out to 𝑧∼6 and infer a rapidly increasing
obscured fraction towards high redshift. We explore two ways of con-
verting the MEOW constraints to 15𝜇m LF. The first is taking their
bolometric LFs and applying a typical mid-IR bolometric correction
of ∼10 (see Table 2). The second is taking their 3𝜇m LFs and con-
verting that to 15𝜇m with luminosity differences inferred from the
Krawczyk et al. (2013) template we used for canonical AGN SED.
They yield broadly consistent results.

not driven solely by the LRD-like population. In our “weak IR”
model (the lower limit of predicted IR LF), the faint-end mid-IR

IR comparison supports the interpretation that Lumina contains a
significant population of obscured but otherwise canonical AGN, in
addition to the empirical LRD component introduced above.

Miralda-Escudé 1998; Madau et al. 1999; Schirber & Bullock 2003;
Faucher-Giguère et al. 2008b, 2009; McQuinn et al. 2009; Haardt
& Madau 2012; Worseck et al. 2014, 2016; Basu et al. 2024). The
large abundance of LRDs discovered by JWST at 𝑧≳4 has revived
the long-standing question of whether AGN could also contribute
appreciably to hydrogen reionization (e.g., Madau & Haardt 2015;
Haardt & Salvaterra 2015; Madau et al. 2024; Asthana et al. 2025),
although models in which AGN drive both phase transitions remain
in tension with the observed Ly𝛼forest and IGM thermal history
(Asthana et al. 2025).
In Lumina, the joint cosmic radiation field of stars and AGN
is followed self-consistently with on-the-fly radiative transfer (Sec-
tion 2.3). As described in Zier et al. (2026), Lumina predicts a He ii
reionization history that begins around 𝑧≃6 and completes near
𝑧≃3, broadly consistent with existing constraints from the He ii
Ly𝛼forest (e.g., Worseck et al. 2014, 2016, 2019) and the IGM ther-
mal history (Lidz et al. 2010; Gaikwad et al. 2021). Here, we leverage
this self-consistent picture to dissect the AGN contribution to He ii
reionization in greater detail, asking how SMBHs of different masses
and evolutionary phases share the photon budget.

5 DISCUSSION
5.1 AGN variability is essential for early SMBH growth
As discussed in Section 3.1 above, one fundamental limitation of
any cosmological simulation is that many relevant physical processes
related to gas inflow and BH accretion operate many orders of mag-
nitude below the resolution limit. The Bondi–Hoyle prescription ef-
fectively delivers a “smooth” accretion rate set by the kpc-scale gas
properties, while real AGN are observed to vary over timescales of
hours to decades or longer. Quantitatively, in large optical quasar
samples, the asymptotic structure-function amplitudes (which quan-
tify the total light-curve variability) are typically ∼0.1 −0.4 mag
on rest-frame timescales of ∼102 −103 days (e.g., Vanden Berk
et al. 2004; Kelly et al. 2009; MacLeod et al. 2010; Caplar et al.
2017; Burke et al. 2021). In hard X-rays, where variability traces the
corona on much shorter timescales, fractional rms amplitudes of a
few tens of percent are commonly observed on timescales of hours to
days (e.g., Vaughan et al. 2003; González-Martín & Vaughan 2012;
Ponti et al. 2012), corresponding to ∼0.1 dex scatter in luminos-
ity. On the much longer timescales relevant to the lifetime of an
individual quasar (∼0.1 −100 Myr), the observed ERDF can span
several orders of magnitude in 𝜆Edd (e.g., Hopkins & Hernquist 2009;
Hickox et al. 2014; Aird et al. 2018; Sartori et al. 2018), with effec-
tive log-normal dispersions of ∼0.3−0.4 dex (Kollmeier et al. 2006;
Kauffmann & Heckman 2009; Kelly et al. 2010; Kelly & Shen 2013;
Lusso et al. 2012). Our adopted 𝜎bol = 0.3 dex therefore sits naturally
between the short-timescale optical/X-ray variability amplitude and
the long-timescale ERDF dispersion.
The 𝜎bol = 0.3 dex log-normal scatter applied in our empirical
model (Section 3.1) is an essential and physically motivated ingre-
dient. It captures unresolved accretion variability and translates the
simulated mean accretion rates into a realistic distribution of ob-
served AGN luminosities. However, we caution that this treatment
remains simplified in several aspects: (i) The scatter is applied to the
bolometric luminosity, so it does not just reflect intrinsic accretion-
rate variability but may also absorb fluctuations in the obscuration
properties. (ii) The assumed log-normal kernel is itself a simplifica-
tion, whereas the observed ERDFs of AGN are often better described
by a Schechter form, with a power-law tail towards low Eddington
ratios and an exponential cutoff near the Eddington limit (e.g., Hop-
kins & Hernquist 2009; Schulze & Wisotzki 2010; Aird et al. 2018).
(iii) We adopt a constant scatter across the full luminosity range,
whereas a luminosity- or mass-dependent kernel may provide a bet-
ter description (see Figure 19 for a hint).
Variability is also likely vital for early SMBH growth and quench-
ing galaxies. Recent zoom-in simulations have shown that allowing
bursty super-Eddington episodes, physically motivated by photon-
trapping in slim discs, can grow ∼104 M⊙seeds to ∼108−9 M⊙
within several hundred Myr, in regimes where Eddington-limited
Bondi–Hoyle accretion would fail by orders of magnitude (e.g., Lupi
et al. 2024; Pacucci & Narayan 2024; Huško et al. 2025). The duty

The amplification of bright-quasar abundances by intrinsic vari-

galaxy UV LF by bursty star-formation. JWST surveys at 𝑧≳10

is that the rest-UV luminosities of low-mass high-redshift galaxies
vary stochastically by 𝜎UV ≃1−2 mag driven by the short feedback-
modulated SFR cycles seen in zoom-in simulations (e.g., Mirocha &
Furlanetto 2023; Shen et al. 2023, 2024; Mason et al. 2023; Sun et al.
2023; Semenov et al. 2025a,b). Despite potentially very different rel-
evant astrophysical timescales, the conceptual lesson is the same: in
regimes where the underlying source population is steeply declining
at the bright end and where short-timescale fluctuations are large,
time variability is a central component of the observable LF.
5.2 Summary of explicit & implicit assumptions about LRDs
We have described above how Lumina, paired with an empirical
model connecting SMBHs to observable AGN, can reproduce sev-
eral key properties of high-redshift quasars and LRDs. Here, we pro-
vide a more direct summary of the explicit and implicit assumptions
entering this framework. The most important explicit assumptions
are:
(i) In Lumina, we place 8×105 ℎ−1 M⊙seed BH particles in every
halo that exceeds 5 × 1010 ℎ−1 M⊙and does not already contain a
BH. It provides the endpoint of the unresolved BH growth below the
seed mass.
(ii) For BH particles below 𝑀crit
BH = 𝜅𝑀seed = 1.18 × 107 M⊙
(𝜅= 10), a fraction 𝑓duty = 0.3 is assumed to appear observationally
as LRDs. This intuitively associates LRDs with the early growth
phase of SMBHs.
(iii) We assume Bondi–Hoyle accretion onto BH particles, with-
out an additional boost factor, limited by the Eddington rate, and
adopt a constant radiative efficiency 𝜖rad = 0.2 for all BHs.
(iv) We assume an intrinsic unresolved scatter in instantaneous
BH luminosities of 𝜎bol = 0.3 dex, which phenomenologically ac-
counts for accretion variability below the resolution scale of the
simulation.
The remaining ingredients are mainly observationally motivated, in-
cluding the SEDs and bolometric corrections of canonical AGN and
LRDs, and the column-density distribution of canonical AGN, which
determines absorption and dust extinction corrections. In this sense,
our model separates the problem into two parts: the simulation pro-
vides the abundance, growth, and environments of BH particles,
while the empirical model specifies how these BH particles appear
in different wavelength bands.
The physical interpretation of 𝑓duty is broader than its implemen-
tation suggests. For example, because Lumina seeds BHs in every
halo above the adopted threshold, 𝑓duty effectively absorbs the true
occupation fraction of BH seeds in early haloes. Alternatively, a
timescale-based interpretation of 𝑓duty emerges if we assume that
essentially every newly seeded BH passes through a single LRD-
like episode and is observable while in that phase. In that limit,
𝑓duty ∼𝑡LRD/𝑡low, where 𝑡LRD is the duration of the LRD-emitting
phase and 𝑡low is the total time a BH spends below 𝑀crit
BH. Both
time scales can be set from physical models. For example, in the
late-stage quasi-star scenario (Begelman et al. 2008; Begelman &
Dexter 2026), the LRD phase coincides when a central BH swallow-
ing a convectively powered envelope and lasts of order the Salpeter
time 𝑡Sal ≡𝜖rad 𝑀BH𝑐2/[(1 −𝜖rad)𝐿Edd] ≃112.5 Myr (assuming
𝜖rad = 0.2), modulo a factor that depends on the envelope-to-BH mass
ratio at envelope dispersal (Begelman 2010; Volonteri & Begelman
2010; Coughlin & Begelman 2024). The lifetime 𝑡low is set by the time
required for the BH to grow from 𝑀seed to 𝜅𝑀seed at its average accre-

bracketing our fiducial choice. Alternative scenarios, in which LRDs
correspond to recurrent super-Eddington accretion bursts each lasting
only a few Myr (Inayoshi 2025), are also consistent with 𝑓duty = 0.3
provided that the bursts repeat a few times during the LRD-eligibility
window, as those models in fact predict.
The BH particle in Lumina should be viewed as a sub-grid tracer
of an unresolved proto-nuclear ecosystem that may include the seed
BH itself, a surrounding gas reservoir, and possibly a compact nu-
clear star cluster. Several physical models have been proposed for
what such systems look like, including but not limited to the quasi-
star picture mentioned above, and an intermediate-mass BH growing
rapidly inside a dense nuclear star cluster (or a cluster of clusters) fed
by runaway stellar collisions and mergers (e.g., Devecchi & Volon-
teri 2009; Devecchi et al. 2012; Davies et al. 2011; Katz et al. 2015;
Shi et al. 2024; Dekel et al. 2025; Pacucci et al. 2025). Lumina
resolves the large-scale environment, halo assembly, and global gas
supply common to all of these scenarios, but does not resolve the in-
ternal radiative and dynamical processes that distinguish them. Our
empirical model for AGN is deliberately agnostic to which picture
applies. The broad consistency with the quasi-star timescale esti-
mates discussed above is encouraging in this context, but is not by
itself sufficient to discriminate between the underlying scenarios.

transition between the LRD-like and canonical AGN phases is treated
as a sharp mass cut, although in reality it likely depends on addi-

ASTRID (Bird et al. 2022; Ni et al. 2022) is a (250 ℎ−1 cMpc)3
smoothed-particle hydrodynamics simulation with 2 × 55003 resolu-
tion elements run with MP-Gadget (Feng et al. 2018). BHs are seeded
in haloes with 𝑀halo ≳5 × 109 ℎ−1 M⊙and 𝑀★≳2 × 106 ℎ−1 M⊙,
and the BH seed masses are drawn probabilistically from a power-

grow via Bondi–Hoyle accretion with an 𝛼= 100 boost factor to
account for the underestimation of the accretion rate due to the un-
resolved cold and hot phase of the sub-grid ISM nearby. Mildly
super-Eddington accretion is allowed, but the accretion rate is still
capped at two times the Eddington rate. AGN feedback is modelled
as an isotropic thermal feedback until 𝑧∼2.3 when the kinetic mode
feedback is turned on. The LFs shown in Figure 21 are taken from
the most recent ASTRID release (Ni et al. 2025), from which we take
the results assuming 𝜖rad = 0.2.
FLAMINGO (Schaye et al. 2023) is a suite of large-volume SPH
simulations run with swift (Schaller et al. 2024). The flagship run
covers a (2.8 Gpc)3 volume with 50403 baryonic particles and a
baryon mass resolution of 1.07×109 M⊙, with smaller boxes at higher
resolution. The sub-grid prescriptions for stellar and AGN feedback
are calibrated using machine learning to reproduce the low-redshift
galaxy stellar mass function and cluster gas fractions. BHs are seeded
with 𝑀seed = 105 M⊙in FoF haloes with 𝑀halo ≳2.757 × 1011 M⊙.
BHs accrete at a Bondi–Hoyle rate with a density-dependent boost
factor (Booth & Schaye 2009) and are capped at the Eddington rate.
In their fiducial model, AGN feedback is modelled thermally. They
assume 𝜖rad = 0.1. Ding et al. (2026) apply an additional log-normal
scatter of 0.75 dex in post-processing to the bolometric luminosities
before constructing the AGN LFs shown here, motivated by the
comparison to the Shen et al. (2020a) compilation at 𝑧≲3.
BRAHMA (Bhowmick et al. 2024a) is a suite of small-volume
cosmological simulations run with Arepo using a sub-grid galaxy
formation model close to that of IllustrisTNG. The recent addi-
tion (Bhowmick et al. 2024b) is designed specifically to system-
atically explore heavy-seed (direct-collapse-like) channels for the
formation of the first massive BHs. Heavy seeds with 𝑀seed ≃

“SM5_LW10” model for comparison. The small simulation volume
restricts the BRAHMA LFs to 𝐿bol ≲1045 erg s−1 in Figure 21.
The FIRE-2 high-redshift suite (Ma et al. 2018, 2019) consists
of zoom-in simulations of individual haloes at 𝑧≳5 using the
FIRE-2 model (Hopkins et al. 2018). Active BHs are not followed
self-consistently. Instead, in Marszewski et al. (2026), hypothetical
BHs are placed at the centres of resolved galaxies in post-processing
(“passive” BH model), and their accretion rates are computed from
the central gas distribution using either a gravitational-torque-driven
accretion prescription (Hopkins & Quataert 2011; Anglés-Alcázar
et al. 2017a,b) or a simple free-fall accretion model. Bolometric
luminosities are derived assuming 𝜖rad = 0.1. We take two illustrative
LFs from their gravitational-torque-driven accretion model: a “mass-
complete” LF that places a BH at the centre of every resolved host
galaxy above the simulation completeness threshold, and a “plausible
LRD” scenario that further restricts to BHs that are super-Eddington-
accreting, Eddington-luminosity-limited in host galaxies with 𝑀∗>
2 × 107 M⊙.
This paper based on Lumina differs from these efforts in the fol-
lowing aspects. First, Lumina strikes a favourable balance between
simulation volume and numerical resolution: it resolves low-mass
galaxies and faint AGN more reliably than the FLAMINGO flagship
run, while sampling rare luminous quasars more completely than
BRAHMA, FIRE-2 zoom-in volumes, and even ASTRID. Second,
the on-the-fly multi-frequency radiative transfer through H i and He ii
reionization couples the AGN ionizing emissivity to the IGM ther-
mal and ionization state self-consistently. The He ii reionization is
driven by the same AGN population studied here. Third, rather than
restricting the comparison to BH accretion rates and bolometric LFs,
we develop an empirical model that maps simulated SMBHs onto
multi-band AGN observables, and demonstrate that a coherent pic-
ture of the high-redshift AGN, including LRDs, can be established
within a single framework.

earlier sections, Lumina agrees well with the Shen et al. (2020a)
compilation at the bright end and with the JWST constraints at the

ficiency 𝜖rad = 0.2 rather than the 𝜖rad = 0.1 used in the original
simulation, which improves the bright-end agreement at the expense
of internal consistency. FLAMINGO underpredicts the bolometric
LF across the full luminosity range at 𝑧≃4, plausibly reflecting
the resolution limitations highlighted in our earlier Lumina–Mtng
comparison. At 𝑧≃6, FLAMINGO recovers the bright end but still
underpredicts at the faint-end. BRAHMA is restricted by its small
simulation volume and only gives predictions at the faint end. A
large abundance of low-luminosity AGN at both redshifts is found
in this simulation, which sits above the observational constraints
of LRDs at 𝐿bol ∼1044 erg s−1. Finally, the mass-complete FIRE-

similar to BRAHMA, which indicates a rapid fuelling of gas to
central BHs even in bursty and clumpy star-forming galaxies in
FIRE-2. Their subsample restricted to super-Eddington-accreting,
Eddington-luminosity-capped BHs in relatively massive hosts (the
“plausible LRD” sample) reproduces the JWST LRD LF at around
𝐿bol ∼1044 −1045 erg s−1.
6 CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we use the large-volume cosmological radiation-
hydrodynamic simulation Lumina to study the high-redshift AGN
population. The balance of large volume and high resolution offered
by Lumina allows us to simultaneously model some rare luminous
quasars, the more abundant moderate-luminosity AGN, and the low-
mass SMBH population that may be connected to the recently dis-
covered LRDs. We construct an empirical mapping between the sim-
ulated SMBH population and observed AGN across multiple wave-
length bands, and test whether the resulting AGN population is con-
sistent with the available observational constraints from LFs, large-
scale environments, and integrated properties of AGN. As described
in Section 3, this empirical model starts from the SMBH population
in Lumina and applies a modest unresolved log-normal scatter to the
instantaneous bolometric luminosity, with fiducial value 𝜎bol = 0.3
dex (Section 3.1). The simulated BHs are then split into two classes
by mass: those above a critical mass threshold 𝑀crit
BH = 𝜅𝑀seed are
assigned a canonical quasar SED with luminosity-dependent absorp-
tion/extinction corrections (Sections 3.2 and 3.4), while those below
the threshold may instead enter an LRD-like early-growth phase char-
acterized by an empirical universal LRD SED (Section 3.3) and a

𝜅= 10 and 𝑓duty = 0.3. Our main results are as follows.
(i) Bolometric LFs (Section 4.1). The bolometric AGN LFs pre-
dicted by Lumina agree well with pre-JWST multi-wavelength con-
straints at 𝑧≃3 −6 for moderately luminous quasars. The bright
end is broadly converged between Lumina and Mtng, while the
faint end remains mildly resolution-dependent when compared to
TNG100. The stochastic luminosity scatter is essential for matching
the observed abundance of luminous AGN and can be interpreted
as an effective treatment of unresolved accretion variability below
the resolution scale of the simulation. The faint-end bolometric LF
in Lumina matches more recent JWST constraints that include the
contribution from LRDs and MIRI-selected dust-obscured AGN.
(ii) Optical and UV LFs (Sections 4.2 and 4.3). Lumina repro-
duces the LFs of both the canonical AGN population and the LRDs
in the rest-frame optical and UV. In the optical, the model prediction
agrees with the large abundance of LRDs at 𝐿opt ∼1043−1044 erg s−1
while smoothly connecting to the pre-JWST constraints on canonical
AGN at the bright end. The comparison of optical LFs motivates
our fiducial choice of 𝜅and 𝑓duty. In the UV, the simulation predic-
tion again agrees with pre-JWST constraints at the bright end, while
the comparison to LRDs depends sensitively on the interpretation of
the physical origin of the UV emission. We explore one scenario in
which the LRD UV emission is significantly contributed by the host
galaxies. An empirical decomposition of AGN and host-galaxy light
in UV inferred from recent observations results in good agreement
between Lumina predictions and observed LRD UV LFs. However,
the simulated hosts of LRD-mass BHs are often too UV- and optically
luminous to satisfy observational LRD selection criteria, highlight-
ing an important limitation of theoretical models in reproducing the
AGN-host galaxy scaling relations at high redshifts.
(iii) X-ray and mid-IR LFs (Sections 4.4 and 4.5). The hard
X-ray LFs of canonical AGN (assuming no LRD contribution) in
Lumina broadly reproduce the observed LFs at 𝑧≃3 −6, including
the flattening towards the faint end. The model slightly overpredicts
faint X-ray AGN at 𝑧≲4, although this should be interpreted with
caution given that current X-ray surveys may still miss a larger pop-
ulation of heavily obscured or intrinsically X-ray weak AGN than
previously accounted for. In the mid-IR, Lumina appears to con-
tain a more complete sample of AGN compared to extrapolations
of pre-JWST LF constraints, in agreement with recent JWST/MIRI
constraints that capture even the X-ray weak and previously missed
sources.
(iv) Large-scale environments of LRDs (Section 4.6). The pro-
jected cross-correlation between LRDs and star-forming galaxies in
Lumina at 𝑧≃4 and 𝑧≃5 agrees well with recent JWST survey
results. The clustering amplitudes are consistent with characteristic
LRD host halo masses slightly above 1011 M⊙, similar to those in-
ferred observationally. Therefore, the LRD-like population appears to
be associated with relatively common, moderate-mass haloes rather
than the rare massive haloes hosting luminous quasars. This serves
as an important cross-check of our empirical LRD model, which is
largely calibrated based on optical LRD LFs. However, halo assembly
bias may complicate a direct mapping between clustering amplitude
and halo mass if LRD formation preferentially selects haloes with
unusual secondary properties.
(v) AGN in the cosmological context (Section 4.7). The pre-
dicted number density of observable LRDs is broadly consistent
with current constraints at 𝑧≃4 −6. The predicted LRD abundance
continues to rise towards lower redshifts while the observed num-
ber densities show an apparent decline at 𝑧≲4. Although we show
that observational bias and selection effects can play a role, this may
indicate limitations of our BH seeding or LRD model, which could
miss additional criteria uniquely associated with the high-redshift
environment. The total BHAD in Lumina is broadly consistent with
recent JWST/MIRI constraints at 𝑧≲5 and is higher than the value
inferred from previous X-ray and mid-IR surveys. The LRD contri-
bution is non-negligible at 𝑧≃4−6 but remains below the total AGN
contribution and is closer to estimates based on updated LRD bolo-
metric corrections than to upper limits derived from canonical quasar
corrections. The accumulated SMBH mass density at high redshifts
remains compatible with canonical local SMBH mass density con-
straints when we subtract the integrated mass gain from low-redshift
quasars (based on the Soltan argument). The full AGN population
in Lumina, including both canonical AGN and the LRD phase, is
therefore consistent with the global SMBH growth budget.
(vi) AGN-driven He ii reionization (Section 4.7). With multi-
frequency radiative transfer followed on the fly, Lumina predicts
a He ii reionization history that begins around 𝑧≃6 and com-
pletes by 𝑧≃3, driven essentially by AGN and broadly consis-
tent with observational constraints. The He ii-ionizing photon bud-
get during the bulk of this epoch is dominated by SMBHs with
𝑀BH ≃107 −109 M⊙. Crucially, BHs below our adopted LRD mass
threshold contribute negligibly to the He ii-ionizing emissivity over
this redshift range.
Taken together, these results show that Lumina provides a coher-
ent cosmological framework for interpreting the high-redshift AGN
population. The simulation can simultaneously reproduce the abun-
dance of classical quasars alongside the emerging population of faint
AGN revealed by JWST, the large-scale environments of AGN, the
integrated SMBH growth rates, and the reionization of He ii in the
IGM driven by the same SMBH population, all within a single self-
consistent framework. In this picture, LRDs are considered as a
possible early-growth phase of SMBHs. The observational evidence
collected for LRDs through LFs at multiple wavelengths and large-
scale clustering is mutually consistent, with minimal assumptions
motivated empirically from observations. Meanwhile, the remaining
canonical AGN component stays essential for matching the X-ray,
mid-IR, and bright-end AGN optical/UV LF constraints, as well as
supplying the bulk of the He ii-ionizing photon budget. Several un-
certainties remain, as discussed in detail in Section 5.2 and 5.3. The
few free parameters in the AGN empirical model and the BH seeding
and accretion prescriptions are simplified treatments of unresolved
physics with some level of degeneracies. The role of Lumina in this
work is to provide a cosmological backbone within which a physically
plausible, effective LRD phase can be tested against the abundance,
environments, and other observables of SMBHs simultaneously. Fu-
ture progress will require a larger and more complete observational
sample and more predictive theoretical models.
