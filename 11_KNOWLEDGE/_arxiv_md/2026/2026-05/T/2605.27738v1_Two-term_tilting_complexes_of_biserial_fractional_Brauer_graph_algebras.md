BOHAN XING
Abstract. Brauer graph algebras form a classical class of symmetric algebras with well-structured combi-
natorial properties and geometric models. Recently, they have been generalized to biserial fractional Brauer
graph algebras, which can be regarded as a self-injective version of the classical Brauer graph algebras. In
this paper, we show that the skew group algebras of biserial fractional Brauer graph algebras induced by the
Nakayama automorphism are in fact skew-Brauer graph algebras. We then study two-term tilting complexes
and Kauer moves for biserial fractional Brauer graph algebras. Moreover, we prove that a biserial fractional
Brauer graph algebra is tilting-discrete if and only if its reduced form (which is a Brauer graph algebra)
is tilting-discrete. Finally, we show that tilting-discrete biserial fractional Brauer graph algebras are closed
under derived equivalence.
1. Introduction
Brauer graph algebras (abbr. BGAs) originate from the modular representation theory of finite groups.
A subclass, Brauer tree algebras, first appear in the work of Janusz [28] and Dade [23]. The general
case, BGAs, is subsequently defined by Donovan and Freislich in [24]. In general, BGAs form a class of
symmetric algebras equipped with a combinatorial structure given by a pair (Γ, m), where Γ is a ribbon
graph (i.e., a graph embedded in an orientable surface) and m: V(Γ) →Z>0 is a multiplicity function.
They are precisely the symmetric special biserial algebras [44], and the representation-finite ones coincide
with Brauer tree algebras, which are derived equivalent to symmetric Nakayama algebras (see for example
[45]).
In order to study self-injective algebras via a similar combinatorial approach, Li and Liu [33] gener-
alized BGAs to fractional BGAs. In general, fractional BGAs are neither special biserial nor of tame
representation type [49]. Consequently, in [33, 35], Li and Liu studied fractional BGAs of type MS, which
are self-injective and special biserial. Here “MS” stands for “multiserial and self-injective” [27]. In this
article, we simply refer to such MS-type algebras as biserial fractional BGAs (abbr. biserial FBGAs),
since the biserial part of fractional BGAs is special biserial and coincides with fractional BGAs of type
MS. Same as BGAs, these algebras are defined from combinatorial data — a ribbon graph equipped with
a degree function — called a biserial fractional Brauer graph (abbr. biserial FBG), satisfying a certain
condition (see Definition 2.2). We illustrate these concepts with the following example.
Example 1.1. Consider the following ribbon graph Γ, consisting of two vertices v and w and three edges

ordering around each vertex is clockwise. We use i, i′ to denote the two distinct half-edges that correspond
to the edge ¯i.

Notation and Conventions
Throughout we assume that k is an algebraically closed field and all algebras considered are finite-
dimensional k-algebras. Unless stated otherwise, all modules will be finitely generated right modules.
Furthermore, we say that A is a bound quiver algebra, if A  kQ/I, where Q is a finite quiver and I is an
admissible ideal in the path algebra kQ. We denote by s(p) the source vertex of a path p and by t(p) its
terminus vertex. We will write paths from left to right, for example, p = α1α2 · · · αn is a path with starting
arrow α1 and ending arrow αn. A path is called a cycle if s(p) = t(p). By abuse of notation we sometimes
view an element in kQ as an element in the quotient kQ/I if no confusion can arise.
Recall that the derived category D(A) of an algebra A is obtained from the homotopy category K(A) by
inverting quasi-isomorphisms. Two algebras are said to be derived equivalent if their derived categories
are equivalent as triangulated categories. By Rickard’s theorem [41], this is equivalent to an equivalence
between their bounded homotopy categories Kb(proj A) of finitely generated projective modules.
A k-algebra A is called self-injective if AA is an injective A-module; and symmetric if AAA  DA as
A-A-bimodules. Indeed, symmetric algebras are naturally self-injective (see for example in [50]).
A k-algebra A is special biserial if it is isomorphic to an algebra of the form kQ/I where kQ is a path
algebra and I is an admissible ideal such that the following properties hold.
(1) At every vertex i in Q, there are at most two arrows starting at i and there are at most two arrows
ending at i.
(2) For every arrow α in Q, there exists at most one arrow β such that βα < I and there exists at most
one arrow γ such that αγ < I.

2.1. Ribbon graphs.
Ribbon graphs combinatorially encode the structure of orientable surfaces (see for example in [39,
Section 1.1]). A key feature of ribbon graphs is the cyclic ordering of (half-)edges at each vertex, which
captures the orientation data of the underlying surface. We begin this section by recalling their formal
definition.
Definition 2.1. A ribbon graph is a tuple Γ = (V, H, s, ι, ρ), where
(1) V (also denoted by V(Γ)) is a finite set whose elements are called vertices;
(2) H (also denoted by H(Γ)) is a finite set whose elements are called half-edges;
(3) s : H →V is a functions;
(4) ι : H →H is an involution without fixed points;
(5) ρ : H →H is a permutation whose cycles correspond to the sets Hv := s−1(v), v ∈V.

2.4. Reduced forms of biserial FBGAs.
Roughly speaking, the reduced form of a biserial FBG is essentially given by the graph of orbits Γ/⟨ν⟩.
However, since Γ/⟨ν⟩is in general not a ribbon graph, but an orbifold ribbon graph.
Definition 2.10. An orbifold ribbon graph is a tuple Γ = (V, H, s, ι, ρ) satisfying conditions (1)–(5) of a
ribbon graph in Definition 2.1, except that the involution ι is allowed to have fixed points. We denote by
H× ⊆H the set of fixed points of ι. The elements of H× are called orbifold edges. Each orbifold edge is
drawn as an edge connecting a vertex in V to a cross symbol ×, where the symbol × is not regarded as a
vertex of V.
Just as a ribbon graph naturally fattens to an orientable ribbon surface, an orbifold ribbon graph naturally
fattens to an orbifold ribbon surface [11, 18]. We will show that an orbifold ribbon graph can be obtained
as a quotient of a biserial FBG by its Nakayama automorphism.
For each biserial FBG (Γ = (V, H, s, ι, ρ), d), define
(Γ/⟨ν⟩, d) := ((V, H′, s′, ι′, ρ′), d),

(2) s′([h]) = s(h);

5.4. Applications.
Combining the results of Theorems 5.8 and 5.10, we obtain the following corollary.
Corollary 5.12. Let A be a tilting-discrete biserial FBGA with associated biserial FBG (Γ, d). Then any
basic algebra derived equivalent to A is also a (tilting-discrete) biserial FBGA.
Proof. Denote by Ared the BGA associated with the reduced form (Γred, d). By Theorems 5.8 and 5.10,
the graph Γred contains at most one odd cycle and no even cycles. As shown in [21, Lemma 5.13], for a
self-injective algebra A, tilting-discrete implies tilting-connected. Hence any tilting complex T of A can
be obtained from A by a finite sequence of irreducible tilting mutations. By Propositions A.1–??, the
algebra EndKb(proj A)(T) is again a biserial FBGA associated with some biserial FBG (Γ′, d), where Γ′
red has
the same numbers of odd and even cycles as Γred. Consequently, EndKb(proj A)(T) is also a tilting-discrete
biserial FBGA.
□
As noted in [17, 4], it is interesting to find algebras that are tilting-discrete but not silting-discrete. As
an application of our results, we obtain some examples of biserial FBGAs which are tilting-discrete but
not silting-discrete.
Example 5.13. Let A be a biserial FBGA with associated biserial FBG (Γ, d) such that m(v) ≥1 for every
vertex v of Γ. Let Ared be the BGA associated with the reduced form (Γred, d). Assume that Γred contains at
most one odd cycle and no even cycles, whereas Γ does not satisfy this condition. Then, by Proposition 4.7
and Theorems 5.8 and 5.10, the self-injective algebra A is tilting-discrete but not silting-discrete.
