TOPOLOGY AND CATEGORY FOR SINGULAR PRODUCT SPACES
YUSUKE HAYASHI AND TRISTAN VAN DER VLUGT
Abstract. For κ a regular uncountable cardinal, the higher Baire and Cantor spaces κκ and κ2
(endowed with the <κ-box topology) have been relatively well-studied, but less is known about the
case where κ is singular. We will consider several spaces of functions and box topologies that could
serve as higher Baire and Cantor spaces for singular cardinals. The ultimate focus of the article lies
in studying cardinal characteristics of the ideal of κ-meagre subsets of these spaces.
1. Introduction
The generalisation of the study of set theory of the reals, or rather of the (classical) Baire
space ωω, to the higher Baire space κκ, for κ some uncountable cardinal, has seen a significant
amount of interest in recent years. One early example, is the investigation of Cummings and Shelah
[CS95] into higher analogues to the dominating and unbounding numbers. Much more recently,
Brendle, Brooke-Taylor, Friedman, and Montoya [BBFM18] gave a comprehensive overview of the
generalisation of the cardinal characteristics of the Cicho´n diagram to the higher context, and with
it, a comprehensive study of the κ-meagre ideal on κκ for regular κ satisfying κ = 2<κ.
In many cases, the knowledge and intuition we have about set theory of the classical Baire space
transfers to the higher context. For instance, we could compare the middle part of the classical
Cicho´n diagram (see [BJ95, Chapter 2]) to the higher Cicho´n diagram from [BBFM18]:

Much less studied is the higher Baire space µµ where we assume µ is a singular cardinal. In this
article, we make a contribution to close this gap by providing a systematic study of the topology
of product spaces that involve a singular cardinal. It is a priori unclear what constitutes a higher
Baire space for a singular cardinal: even if we just restrict ourselves to spaces of functions between
two cardinals with some natural generalisation of the product topology (as we do in this article),
there are various choices to be made.

Apart from the topological goal of studying higher singular product spaces themselves, we will
study the cardinal charactersitics of the (higher) meagre ideal associated to each space. Indeed, the
choice of space is significant, and does consistently lead to different cardinal characteristics.
1.1. Overview of this paper. In Section 2 we will consider several spaces that consist of functions
on ordinals equipped with different generalisations of the product topology. We will give a full
overview of the conditions under which these spaces are homeomorphic to each other. In order to
do this, we compute some topological invariants of these spaces, such as their weight and character.
The remainder of this article will study certain cardinal characteristics on each of the spaces defined
in Section 2.
In Section 3 we recall the notion of meagreness, we generalise it to the higher context and treat
its additivity and covering numbers. In most of our spaces the additivity and covering numbers
equal κ+ (that is, the values are absolute). In Section 4 we look at the uniformity numbers. We
show that these are highly dependent on the choice of space. We give several consistency results
using the method of forcing to prove that several of the uniformity numbers are independent of each
other and of their respective lower and upper bounds. In Section 5 we briefly review the dominating
and unbounding numbers for singular cardinals. Finally, in Section 6 we discuss a lower bound
for the cofinality numbers in some of our spaces, modifying a result by Brendle [Bre22] to give a
connection between the cofinality numbers and certain dominating numbers. As a consequence we
obtain the consistency of 2µ being strictly smaller than some of the cofinality numbers.

2. Topological considerations
Before we can study the higher Baire and Cantor spaces for singular cardinals, we have to properly
define what these spaces are. When we generalise the classical context of ω to the higher context
of a singular cardinal µ, we first have to consider what property of ω we are trying to generalise:
should we replace ω by µ or by its cofinality κ? The choice depends on whether we want to interpret
ω as a cardinality or as a cofinality. Exhausting all such choices in the generalisation of ωω and ω2,
we end up with four sets of functions µµ, µκ, µ2 and κµ as potential underlying sets for the higher
Baire and Cantor spaces.
The ubiquitous choice for a topology on ωω (and ω2) is the product topology. Remember that a
subbase for the product topology is given by cylinder sets of the form Cn,m = {f ∈ωω | f(n) = m}.
To obtain a base, we close this subbase under finite intersections. The topologies we will consider in
the higher context will have bases given by intersections of cylinder sets as well, but we have to
choose how to generalise the notion of a finite intersection. The concept of “finiteness”—the natural
choice of smallness with respect to ω—can be understood in three ways: in terms of cardinality (finite
cardinalities are strictly smaller than ω), cofinality (finite sets in ω are bounded), or a combination
of both (the cardinality of a finite set is too small to be cofinal). For a regular cardinal, these three
interpretations coincide, but for a singular cardinal they do not, and hence we will consider three
topologies.

homeomorphic to the whole space).1

the conditions under which the existence of a homeomorphism between any pair of our twelve spaces
is provable. A schematic overview of these results is given in Figure 1. The reader who is purely

2.4. The <κ-box topology spaces. Let us now consider the spaces with the <κ-box topology. As
far as the regular higher Cantor and Baire spaces are concerned, it is well-known that (κκ, κ) and
(κ2, κ) are homeomorphic if and only if κ is not a weakly compact cardinal (cf. [HN73, Theorem 1]).
There are many equivalent ways to define weakly compact cardinals (see [CN74, Theorem 8.23]),
but the one we will use is topological in nature.
Definition 9. Let a topological space X be called <κ-compact if every open cover of X has a
subcover of cardinality <κ. We define an infinite cardinal number κ to be weakly compact if (κ2, κ)
is a <κ-compact space.
Although unconventional, for the sake of brevity we allow weakly compact cardinals to be
countable, and indeed ℵ0 satisfies the above conditions, since (ω2, ω) is a compact space. In our
context of spaces of functions with domain µ, we will show that (µ2, κ) and (µκ, κ) are homeomorphic
under the same assumption that the cofinality κ of µ is weakly compact. In the proof of Theorem 10
below, the direction (⇐) was told to us by Hiroshi Sakai, who granted permission to include it in
this article.

3. Category and its additivity and covering numbers
Let us first review the classical notion of Baire category. Let X be a topological space. A
subset N ⊆X is nowhere dense if every nonempty open U has a nonempty open V ⊆U such that
N ∩V = ∅, or equivalently if the interior of the closure of N is empty. In particular, each nowhere
dense is contained in a closed nowhere dense set, and the complement of a closed nowhere dense set
is open dense. A set is called meagre (or of first category) if it is the countable union of nowhere
dense sets, and otherwise it is called nonmeagre (or of second category). The complement of a
meagre set is called comeagre. The (classical) Baire category theorem states that, in any completely
metrisable space, the countable intersection of open dense sets is dense. In other words, the Baire
category theorem is equivalent to all comeagre sets being dense.
When the Baire category theorem (and with it the notion of category) are generalised to the
higher context, we may often prove a stronger theorem, in which countable intersections are replaced
by intersections of a larger family of open dense sets. For a cardinal λ, let us call a set λ-meagre if it
10


## Page 11


is the union of λ-many nowhere dense sets. Exactly, if we consider the higher Baire space λλ where
λ is regular, then the intersection of λ-many open dense subsets of λλ is dense, or equivalently every
λ-comeagre subset of λλ is dense. We will see that in the higher context for a singular cardinal µ,
the cofinality cf(µ) = κ plays the role of λ. We first establish that κ-meagre is the right notion to
obtain something strictly more general than nowhere denseness. The proof of the following fact is
elementary.
Fact 15. Let (X, τ) be a space considered in Remark 14. Then κ is the least cardinal such that
there exists a family of κ-many nowhere dense subsets of X whose union is dense.
□
One way to describe the statement of the above fact, is that the nowhere dense ideal of (X, τ) is
not κ-additive. This leads us to the definition of four cardinal characteristics associated with an
ideal I on a space X:
Definition 16. Let I ⊆P(X) be a proper ideal on X, then we define the following cardinal
characteristics.
(1) The additivity number add(I) is the least size of a set A ⊆I such that S A /∈I.
(2) The covering number cov(I) is the least size of a set C ⊆I such that S C = X,
(3) The uniformity number non(I) is the least size of a set N ⊆X with N /∈I,
(4) The cofinality number cof(I) is the least size of a set F ⊆I such that every I ∈I has some
J ∈F with I ⊆J.

We are primarily interested in domination on the sets of functions κκ, κµ, µκ and µµ. Let us
assume X is one of these four sets of functions and that δ is the domain of the functions in X, and
let f, g ∈X, then we define three orderings:
(1) f ≤bd g if the set {α ∈δ | g(α) < f(α)} is bounded in δ.
(2) f ≤ν g if |{α | g(α) < f(α)}| < ν, where ν is a cardinal.
(3) f ≤all g if f(α) ≤g(α) for all α ∈δ.
If ≤• is one of the orderings defined above, then we define the dominating number d(X,•) as the
least size of a set D ⊆X such that for each f ∈X there exists g ∈D with f ≤• g. Dually we define
the bounding number b(X,•) as the least size of a set B ⊆X such that no g ∈X exists for which all
g ∈B satisfy f ≤• g.8 We note that clearly the following implications hold for any f, g ∈X:
f ≤all g implies f ≤κ g,
f ≤κ g implies f ≤bd g,
f ≤bd g implies f ≤µ g.

functions in X with any set of the same cofinality and obtain the same cardinal characteristics,
e.g., these domination relations give the same cardinal characteristics on the spaces κµ and κκ; and
similarly between µµ and µκ. We will therefore purely consider the spaces κκ and µκ from now on.
