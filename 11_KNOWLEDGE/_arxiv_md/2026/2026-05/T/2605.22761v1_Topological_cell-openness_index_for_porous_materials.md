Abstract
We propose a method of estimating and parametrising the proportion of open and
closed cells in a porous material based on measuring Betti numbers on the structures.
Based on this method, we define a cell-openness index τ which can be used instead of
or complementary to the proportion of open-celled volume reported by gas pycnometry,
which is the current gold standard for pore type characterization. We discuss in what
types of structures mismatches between the two measures can occur and how such
mismatches convey additional information about the structure. We also demonstrate
initial examples of significant correlations between τ and measurable physical quantities
in numerically generated structures. We also discuss how Betti curves can be used to
estimate characteristic feature sizes in porous structures.
Keywords: Porous materials • Open pores • Closed pores • Gas pycnometry • Topological
data analysis • Persistent homology • Betti numbers • Microstructure characterization •
3D image analysis
1 Introduction
In this work, we propose an image-based method for estimating the open- and closed-pore
fractions of a porous material from a binary 2D or 3D voxelised microstructure- for instance,
one obtained by X-ray micro-CT, other imaging modality, or generated numerically as a
candidate structure. The method is based on tools developed within the field of topological
data analysis (TDA) [8, 4], specifically on persistent homology [6, 3]. We associate to every
binary image a single number- the cell-openness index
τ ∈[0, 1]
which varies from 0 (for systems with disconnected, closed pores) to 1 for completely open
and interconnected porous networks. In this respect, it corresponds in range and basic
interpretation to ϕ0- the fraction of open pore volume reported by gas pycnometry.
However, by construction, τ captures connectivity information that the existing standard
for openness measurements (gas pycnometry) cannot resolve, most notably the existence of
internally disconnected sub-networks within an otherwise “open” pore phase (see Sec. 3.3).

The output of a gas pycnometry experiment, which measures the fraction of the pores
penetrable to an externally delivered gas, is summarised by a single parameter, called the
"fraction of open pores" or "open porosity"[16, 20, 26]. Throughout this paper, we refer to
the volume fraction of pore voxels belonging to a connected component touching the sample
boundary as ϕ0, and use it as the benchmark against which τ is compared.
However, as any experimental method,gas pycnometry has its limitations. To find ϕ0, it
relies on the knowledge of the ground truth on the real density of the material [23]. Its
accuracy may also be limited when otherwise open pores are not connected to the surface
of the sample, when sample size is not much larger than the pore size, and when some of
the pores are blocked by contaminants such as condensating water microdroplets [18]. This
suggests the benefits of developing complementary methods of parametrization of the degree
of cell-openness in porous materials, which are enabled by the rapid growth in accessibility of
3D microimaging techniques [5, 14].
Topologically describing porous and granular materials is not a new idea in itself. Persistent
homology has been applied to qantify pore similarity in nanoporous candidate materials for
carbon capture or methane storage [12], uncover hidden hierarchical spatial structures in
amorphous solids [21] and to estimate characteristic percolation-related properties within
porous rocks [19].
A related use of persistent homology in materials science concerns the analysis of atomic
configurations rather than pore networks. In their influential work, Hiraoka et al. [10] used
persistence diagrams to characterize amorphous solids, where the absence of long-range
periodic order makes conventional structural descriptors, such as pair correlation functions,
insufficient for identifying medium-range organization. Starting from atomic coordinates,
they considered unions of balls around atoms and tracked the resulting topological features
across scales: one-dimensional persistence captures ring-like structures, while two-dimensional
persistence captures cavities. Importantly, the birth and death coordinates of persistence
points encode not only topology but also metric information about the size and geometry of
these features. In silica glass, this approach distinguished crystalline, liquid, and amorphous
configurations through qualitatively different persistence-diagram patterns, including islands,
diffuse clouds, and curve-like distributions. This work demonstrated that persistent homology
can uncover hidden geometric organization in disordered materials and relate it to physically
meaningful structural signatures.
Compared to these works, our main goal here is deliberately reductive: we ask whether a
single, interpretable number, directly comparable to the one number returned by a pycnometer-
can already capture the open/closed pore distinction in a way that complements, rather than
replaces, the classical method. A secondary goal of this work is to revisit the related question
of recovering characteristic feature sizes (pore radius, inter-pore distance, throat width) from
the same TDA signature, via simple geometric features of Betti curves, on our synthetically
generated datasets; related earlier work on recovering length scales from topological signatures
is further reviewed in Sec. 4.2.
This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the 2D and 3D microstructural
image datasets used in this study. Section 3 introduces the numerical analogue of gas

2 Materials and microstructural image datasets
All synthetic structures analysed in this study are voxelised binary microstructures stored in
the .npy format: in the 2D case on a 400 × 400 grid, in the 3D case on a 400 × 400 × 400
grid (with the exception of the 3D dataset used for heat-transfer analysis, which uses a 2003
grid). In all cases, pore voxels are labelled 0 and solid voxels are labelled 1. The data is
available together with the generating codes at [GitHub repo TBA].
Common construction of datasets generated for this work.
The four families of
synthetic structures generated for use in this work share the same basic features of the
generative procedure. Each structure is otherwise solid, with circular (in 2D) or spherical (in
3D) pores of mean radius r located close to nodes of a quadratic (in 2D) or cubic (in 3D) grid
of mean spacing a. Each pore radius and each precise pore location is drawn independently
from a normal distribution with standard deviations σr and σa, respectively. The unit of r, a
and of all other distance-related parameters is dimensionless and equal to one pixel/voxel;
the actual pores are obtained by performing a binary dilation operation around the pore
center. Where channels connecting neighbouring pores are present, they have mean width w
and standard deviation σw, and each pair of neighbouring pore centers is connected by such
a channel independently with probability p (and is left disconnected with probability 1 −p).
The parameters p, a, σa, r, σr, w, σw are separately drawn for every generated structure. To
avoid artificially disconnecting the structure from the grid boundary, one additional row and
column (or, in 3D, slab) of pore centers is temporarily created in the preparatory phase on
each side outside of the final structure’s boundaries and connected to the real pore centers
according to the same rules that govern how other pore centers are connected to each other.
The generation procedure is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1.
The four families differ only in (i) the connection probability p, (ii) whether the structure is
2D or 3D, and (iii) the sampling ranges of the remaining parameters. These differences are
summarised in the subsections below.

3 Methods
3.1 Numerical gas pycnometry / connected porosity
The numerical analogue of gas pycnometry that we use throughout this work, denoted ϕ0,
is defined as the volume fraction of pore voxels that belong to a connected component of
the pore phase touching at least one face of the cubical (or, in 2D, square) image domain.
Operationally, ϕ0 is computed by:
1. Labelling all connected components of the pore phase, using 26-connectivity in 3D (8-
connectivity in 2D);
2. Identifying which components contain at least one voxel/pixel adjacent to the domain
boundary;
3. Summing the voxel/pixel counts of those components and dividing by the total pore-
voxel/pore-pixel count.
This quantity is the digital counterpart of the open-porosity fraction reported by gas pyc-
nometry experiments.
3.2 Betti numbers and Betti curves
For a binary d-dimensional image X ⊆Zd, the Betti numbers β0(X), β1(X), β2(X) count,
respectively, the number of connected components, the number of independent 1-dimensional

loops, and (in 3D) the number of enclosed cavities. Throughout this work, we compute Betti
numbers on sub-level sets of the signed distance transform (SDT) of the binary structure:
pore voxels are assigned negative SDT values and solid voxels positive SDT values, and the
filtration parameter t ranges over integer pixel distances. The Betti curve βi(t) is then the
function t 7→βi({x : SDT(x) ≤t}); the SDT filtration and corresponding persistent-homology
output are illustrated in Fig. 2.
Because distances between pixels are discrete, Betti numbers are evaluated only at integer
values of t; gradients ∇βi(t) are computed using finite differences on this integer grid. In
the open-pore analyses, prior to gradient computation we restrict to persistent topological
features with persistence ≥1.5, as obtained from the persistence diagram associated with
the same filtration; this filters out small-scale noise arising from voxelisation artefacts.

4.2 Betti-curve based length-scale estimates
The idea that topological summaries of a porous structure can be used to recover its
characteristic length scales has a long history that predates the modern “persistent homology”
language. The classical approach is to track Minkowski functionals - volume, surface area,
integral of mean curvature, and the Euler-Poincaré characteristic- under successive erosions
or dilations of the binary structure, an idea introduced into statistical physics by Mecke [17]
and now established as a standard family of descriptors for porous media [2, 1], including soil
structures [25]. Scholz et. al [22] demonstrated that the Euler characteristic alone is sufficient
to predict the permeability of model porous materials. Robins et. al [19] investigated how the
persistence of topological signatures relates to percolation radia in porous materials, while
Lautensack et al. [11] used the evolution of the Euler number under iterative ball-erosion
of a ceramic-foam micro-CT image to extract characteristic minimal and maximal strut
thicknesses in open foams. Their construction is essentially a length-scale readout from a
single-curve topological signature.
The approach we take here can be seen as a further exploration of this programme. Instead
of tracking the alternating sum of Betti numbers (the Euler characteristic), which superposes
the signals from β0 and β1 and risks cancelling features against one another, we track
the individual Betti curves β0(t) and β1(t) over the signed-distance filtration, and read off
characteristic length scales from where each of them rises and falls. As we show below, this
separation allows several distinct length scales to be recovered in closed-cell foams from the
same filtration: not only the strutthickness, but also the pore radius and the inter-pore

With this background, we now turn to the dictionary of length-scale predictors that we will use
in this work. The idea is conceptually simple: as the filtration parameter t moves from negative
to positive values, the binary structure is progressively dilated, and topological features
(connected components, loops) appear and disappear at filtration levels that correspond
to natural length scales of the geometry. Tracking where β0 and β1 rise and fall therefore
provides a small dictionary of candidate predictors.

5 Discussion
Across the synthetic 2D and 3D systems considered here, the cell-openness index τ behaves
as a topological complement to the numerical open porosity ϕ0. The two indices are strongly
correlated through an approximate power law ϕ0 ∝τ 1/k, with k ≈2-3 in 2D and k ≈3-5 in
3D, but τ retains discriminatory power in regimes where ϕ0 saturates close to unity. The
mismatch between the two indices in that regime carries genuine structural meaning: it flags
pore networks in which most components individually reach the sample boundary but are
mutually disconnected from one another- a feature that pycnometry, by construction, cannot
resolve.
Betti curves computed on signed-distance filtrations carry predictive length-scale information
on the 2D dataset with closed pores. For this dataset, the points with extremum gradients of
β0 and β1 are the basis of effective estimators of the mean pore radius r, the halfwidth of
the inter-pore distance d/2, and the half-thickness of the solid cores h/2, with slopes close

to 1 and R2 between 0.67 and 0.95. In open-cell systems, the same family of estimators
is partially reinterpreted (with d/2 replaced by half the throat width w/2), and while the
procedure appears to work well for completely regular, periodic open porous structures, it
does not seem to be so successful for our 2D open-celled dataset with more realistic noise
in pore sizes and positions. In this case, the regressions are noticeably weaker, which might
stem from overlapping pore center-throat scales obscuring the natural Betti-curve signatures.
This observation somewhat resembles the conclusions of Robins et. al [19] about predicting
percolation radia based on topological persistence data; their study concluded that it was
much more difficult for highly heterogenous systems.
The preliminary physical-property analyses indicate that τ is not only a topological summary
but also carries information about transport properties. In a 2D dataset on percolation
properties of porous materials of Graczyk and Matyka [9], τ traces out a tight parabolic
relationship with log10(permeability) (R2 = 0.95) despite varying by less than 1% across the
dataset. In a 3D dataset we generate numerically, τ correlates positively (at R2 between 0.32
and 0.61, depending on sub-dataset) with the trace of the effective thermal-conductivity tensor
and is a consistently (if only slightly) better predictor than ϕ0 across all five sub-datasets.
More research is needed into both the type of structural information that our cell-openness
index τ can provide and what physical properties it can help predict. However, the work so
far suggests that τ is at least as informative as ϕ0- the fraction of open pores measured by gas
pycnometry- the reporting of which is an industry standard for commercially available porous
materials. We suggest that where plausible (specifically, where high-quality 3D imaging of
a porous structure is available), our cell-openness index should be reported alongside the
fraction of open pores measured by gas pycnometry.
