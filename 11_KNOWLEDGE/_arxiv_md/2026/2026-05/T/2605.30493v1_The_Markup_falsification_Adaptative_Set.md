THE MARKUP FALSIFICATION ADAPTATIVE SET
SANTIAGO ACERENZA, N´ESTOR GANDELMAN
Universidad ORT Uruguay
Abstract. In this paper we provide a constructive way for researchers to salvage the
classic De Loecker and Warzynski (2012) markup recovery procedure when falsified. To
do this, we consider continuous relaxations of the standard assumptions behind markup
estimation. By computing the values of the markup as a function of the relaxations across
the set of non-falsified models, we obtain an identified set for the markup which generalizes
the standard baseline markup estimand to account for possible falsification without the
need to impose additional assumptions. We illustrate our results using Chilean data from
Raval (2023).
Keywords: markup, falsification, sensitivity analysis, partial identification.
JEL subject classification: C18; C26; C51; E23; L0; L11.
1. Introduction
Markup estimation aims to recover the gap between prices and marginal costs at the
firm or product level, a key object for studying market power. Empirical work on firm-
level market power has developed around two primary methodological approaches. The
demand approach (Berry, 1994; Berry et al., 1993) relies on price and quantity data to infer
marginal costs under alternative assumptions about firms’ profit-maximizing behavior. In
contrast, the production approach does not require specifying demand systems or detailed
competitive interactions. This method, which traces back to Hall (1988), was formalized
and brought to prominence by De Loecker and Warzynski (2012).
Within the production approach, markups are inferred from firms’ cost minimization
conditions.
Under standard assumptions, the markup can be expressed as the ratio of
the output elasticity of a variable input to that input’s revenue share. This approach has

When more than one variable input is available, the assumptions required to recover
markups imply a set of testable restrictions on observable data. This feature renders the
model empirically falsifiable, as these restrictions can be directly assessed in the data. More
generally, model falsifiability refers to the property of a theoretical or structural model
whereby it generates testable restrictions on observable data that could, in principle, be
contradicted. A model is falsifiable if its assumptions imply relationships—such as moment
conditions, inequalities, or functional forms—that must hold in the data; failure of these
implications constitutes evidence against the model. In empirical economics, falsifiability
is crucial because it disciplines model selection: rather than fitting any pattern ex post, a
falsifiable model exposes itself to rejection through observable implications. When a model
is falsified, it indicates that at least one of its underlying assumptions is inconsistent with
the data, motivating either model revision, relaxation of assumptions, or a shift toward
frameworks that allow for partial identification.
With finite samples, researchers often use specification tests to check whether their base-
line model is false. This is the case for the production approach to markup estimation as
noted by Raval (2023). Abstracting from sampling uncertainty, the population versions
of such specification tests have a persistent problem: what should researchers do when
markups differ between variable inputs? Or put more abstractly, what should researchers
do when the baseline markup model is falsified?
Building on the framework of Masten and Poirier (2021), this paper provides a con-
structive framework for researchers to evaluate and recover markup estimates when the
underlying structural assumptions of the production approach are empirically falsified. Con-
currently, our approach yields an indirect specification test for the baseline model itself.
To achieve this, we introduce continuous relaxations of two fundamental baseline as-
sumptions: the assumption of cost minimization in the absence of adjustment costs and the
correct specification of output elasticities. By systematically weakening these restrictions,
an otherwise falsified baseline model can be rendered non-falsified. This allows us to define
the falsification frontier, which characterizes the minimal set of relaxations required to en-
sure the model remains compatible with the observed data. Under the assumption that the
true structural parameters lie on this boundary, we estimate the falsification adaptive set

When interpreting these results, two core tenets of the falsification literature must be
kept in mind. First, while a falsified model is logically impossible, a non-falsified model
is not inherently true; it is merely rationalizable by the data. Second, when the baseline
model is falsified, the rejection may stem from any combination of the underlying assump-

frontier: the frontier maps the magnitude of the required violations but cannot isolate the
precise causal mechanism behind the model’s structural failure.
Our work contributes to the recent markup-estimation literature by providing a falsification-
based framework for the production approach. While prior studies have noted that the iden-
tifying assumptions behind production-based markups can fail, the literature has largely
responded by imposing alternative structural assumptions. In contrast, we treat empirical
refutation as an input to identification. We characterize the minimal relaxations of the
baseline assumptions needed to rationalize the data (the falsification frontier) and, under
the assumption that the true model lies on this frontier, derive the falsification-adaptive
set (FAS) for markups. The FAS delivers an identified set for the true markup without
requiring researchers to commit to additional modeling choices (e.g., ad hoc modifications
of the production function), and it yields an operational diagnostic of the baseline model:
if the standard markup estimate lies outside the FAS, the baseline restrictions are rejected
by the data.
More broadly, our paper contributes to the falsification and sensitivity-analysis literature
initiated by Masten and Poirier (2021). That literature advocates reporting conclusions

and Weidner, 2022), and analyses of how alternative relaxations of refuted set-identified

the falsification-adaptive-set idea to linear IV settings with potentially invalid instruments

3. Why it may fail?
First, if there are adjustment costs or firms are not fully rational then the first order
condition 2.1 may not hold, and therefore the markup may be different than 2.2. These two
concerns have been extensively discussed in the literature.
Hamermesh and Pfann (1996) notes that labor demand requires adjustment due to
changes in the identity of the individuals filling a fixed number of jobs. In this sense, some
of the adjustment costs can be related to advertising new job post, screening candidates,
processing new employees, training (which induces disruptions to production as previously
trained workers’ time is devoted to on-the-job instruction) of new workers, severance pay and
the overhead cost of maintaining that part of the personnel function dealing with recruit-
ment and worker outflows. All of these can be substantial, as new workers must be hired and
trained to replace those who depart (whose possibly involuntary departure also generates
costs). Furthermore, changes in the capital stock or its rate of utilization can also generate

adjustment costs as an unchanged workforce’s routine is disrupted and tasks are reassigned
and restructured. For example, the delivery of newly purchased equipment and structures
takes time and this constrains production, as installing newly purchased equipment or struc-
tures shifts other inputs away from current production; as workers’ learning-by-doing with
the newly installed equipment takes time.
Similar considerations may apply to intermediate inputs. Although these inputs are often
modeled as flexible margins of adjustment, modifying their use may also entail non-negligible
costs. Adjusting input sourcing may require searching for and evaluating alternative suppli-
ers, renegotiating contractual arrangements, adapting logistics and inventory management,
and coping with delivery lags or minimum order requirements. In production processes
characterized by technological complementarities or quality-specific inputs, changing the
mix or intensity of intermediate inputs may also require modifications in routines, coordi-
nation across production stages, and learning about the performance of new materials or
suppliers. In addition, firms may face inventory adjustment costs, disruptions associated
with altering procurement schedules, or costs arising from breaking long-term supplier re-
lationships. To the extent that intermediate inputs are embedded in production networks
or customized to specific technologies, their adjustment may be subject to frictions similar
in spirit to those affecting labor and capital.
On the other hand, even if there are no adjustment costs, firm decision makers may
exhibit bounded rationality, a notion that goes back at least to Simon (1955), who empha-
sized that agents may satisfice rather than solve fully rational optimization problems. In
this spirit, evidence from Kelley (2014); Morales Burgos et al. (2020) suggests that firms
may depart from frictionless cost minimization because decision makers face cognitive and
computational limitations. As noted by Sterman et al. (2007), boundedly rational managers
may fail to anticipate market saturation in time to reduce capacity. Additionally, Conlisk
(1996) argues that under bounded rationality a firm may be unable to compute, costlessly
and exactly, its optimal output.
More recent work has formalized related departures from full optimization through ratio-
nal inattention, where firms face information-processing constraints and therefore allocate
limited attention imperfectly across margins of decision making (Sims (2003); Mackowiak
and Wiederholt (2009)). Under this view, observed input choices may reflect constrained
information use rather than frictionless optimization. Likewise, the Behavioral IO litera-
ture has emphasized that firms may make systematic mistakes, rely on heuristics, or use
imperfect decision rules, rather than behave as fully rational profit maximizers (e.g. Ellison

The second assumption presented in the previous section (correct specification of out-
put elasticities) may also fail. This issue is closely related to the estimation of production
functions, as reviewed by De Loecker and Syverson (2021). Their survey covers the most
widely used methods for estimating total factor productivity and recovering output elastic-
ities with respect to inputs. They distinguish between two broad approaches. The first is
the factor share approach, which recovers elasticities from the cost share of each input in
total revenue. This approach requires all inputs to be flexible and the production technol-
ogy to exhibit constant returns to scale. The second approach estimates output elasticities
by directly fitting regressions of output on production inputs. This latter approach faces
two well-known econometric challenges: simultaneity bias—arising because firms observe
their productivity when choosing inputs—and selection bias, driven by the endogenous sur-
vival of more productive firms. The literature has addressed these issues through two main
strategies: control function approaches and dynamic panel data methods. The control func-
tion approach builds on the seminal contribution of Olley and Pakes (1992), extended by
Levinsohn and Petrin (2003) and refined by Ackerberg et al. (2015).1
Recent work has highlighted important limitations in the identification of output elastic-
ities. Ackerberg et al. (2015) show that, under simple data-generating processes consistent
with the standard assumptions, the moment conditions underlying the first-stage estima-
tion of production function parameters may fail to identify the labor coefficient, leading to a
misspecification of elasticities. Doraszelski and Jaumandreu (2021) further argue that these
methods are not robust to unobserved heterogeneity in demand across firms or over time.
In a related contribution, Doraszelski and Jaumandreu (2021) show, first, that under imper-
fect competition, the estimation of input elasticities must account for markups themselves;
and second, that under commonly used production function specifications, cost minimiza-
tion conditions are often inconsistent with observed data, casting doubt on the baseline
relationship between markups, elasticities, and revenue shares. Additional evidence of lim-
itations of the procedures for correct output elasticities is provided by Casacuberta and
Gandelman (2025), who show that in the presence of labor market power, the cost-share
approach fails to identify the output elasticity of labor required to measure labor market

Utilizing the falsification frontier, we can construct the falsification adaptive set (FAS).
The FAS is defined as the identified set for the parameter of interest under the assumption
that the true structural model satisfies one of the restriction profiles located exactly on the
frontier. Notably, when the baseline model is not falsified by the data, the falsification adap-
tive set collapses down to the baseline identified set, which is a singleton. To understand the
mechanical behavior of the FAS, it is instructive to analyze its properties when the baseline
model is well-specified. By definition, the FAS is constructed as the union of all identified
sets evaluated along the falsification frontier. Each point on this frontier represents a min-
imal relaxation profile where the lower and upper bounds exactly coincide, rendering each
constituent set a singleton. Crucially, when the baseline model (δt = 0) is not falsified by
the data, the baseline lower bound is equal to its upper bound. By establishing assump-
tions 1-2, baseline parameters are point identified—collapsing the bounds to a single, true
average markup. Because the data can be fully rationalized without relaxation, the origin
(0) acts as the sharpest point on the falsification frontier. In this scenario, no relaxation is
required to rationalize the data, causing the origin 0 to serve as the sharpest point of the

Leveraging the replication package from Raval (2023), we use the Chilean dataset em-

which covers all Chilean manufacturing plants with at least 10 employees and contains
information on roughly 5,000 plants per year.
The final dataset contains establishment-year information on capital, labor, materials,
and sales. It also includes capital, materials, and output deflators, allowing for the con-

sample. In addition, the data are cleaned by removing observations in the bottom and top

workers, while labor expenditures include salaries and fringe benefits. Materials expendi-
tures include spending on raw materials, electricity, and fuels. The measure of capital is
constructed as capital stocks multiplied by their rental rates, plus any rental payments for
capital.

6. Conclusions
In this paper we provide a constructive way for researchers to salvage De Loecker and
Warzynski (2012) markup model when falsified. To do this, we consider continuous relax-
ations of the assumptions underlying the static cost minimization of variable inputs and the
accurate estimation of the input-output elasticity. By computing the values of the markup
as a function of the relaxations across the minimal set of non-falsified models, we obtain
an identified set for the markup which generalizes the standard baseline markup estimand
to account for possible falsification without the need to impose additional or new assump-
tions on the production function or firm behavior. We illustrate this using data from Raval
(2023) and conclude that there exists robustness in the result that markup is above 1 but
interpretations about the shape of the trend over time in markup should be treated with
caution since it is not a robust result.
