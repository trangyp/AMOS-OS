THE REAL JACOBIAN CONJECTURE FOR MAPS WITH ONE
COMPONENT HAVING DEGREE 6
FRANCISCO BRAUN∗, FILIPE FERNANDES†, JANUSZ GWO´ZDZIEWICZ‡, and BRUNA
OR´EFICE-OKAMOTO∗
Abstract. We show that if F = (p, q) : R2 →R2 is a polynomial map such
that the degree of p is 6 and whose Jacobian determinant is nowhere zero, then
F is injective. This together with previous works in the literature, guarantees
the validity of the real Jacobian conjecture in the plane provided that one of
the coordinate functions of the map has degree smaller than 7.
Introduction
Let F = (p, q) : R2 →R2 be a smooth map such that
(1)
JF(x, y) ̸= 0 ∀(x, y) ∈R2,
where JF(x, y) stands for the Jacobian determinant of F at (x, y). By the Inverse
Function Theorem, F is a local diﬀeomorphism but not necessarily a global one.
If we assume in addition that F is a polynomial map, the real Jacobian conjecture
states that (1) implies that F is a global diﬀeomorphism. This conjecture is not
true: in 1994 Pinchuk [21] constructed a class of non-injective polynomial maps
F = (p, q) satisfying (1). In this class p is a ﬁxed polynomial of degree 10 and q
varies, but the smallest degree it can have is 25, see [8] for more details.
On the other hand, if p has degree 1, then we can assume p(x, y) = x and
qy(x, y) ̸= 0, so that y 7→q(x, y) is injective for each x and then the real Jacobian
conjecture holds for F. Let k be the positive integer such that the real Jacobian
conjecture is true with the additional assumption that the degree of p is less than
k and such that there exists a counterexample with deg p = k. Then 2 ≤k ≤10.
Since the appearance of the Pinchuk counterexample, researchers have been try-
ing to determine the exact value of k. It is not diﬃcult to conclude, by extending
a little the above reasoning that k > 2.
In [15] it was proved that the real Jacobian conjecture holds if both deg p < 4
and deg q < 4. Subsequently, in [4, 5, 6], it was concluded that the conjecture holds
by assuming that deg p < 6, see also [12].
On the other hand, in [11] it was constructed a counterexample to the real
Jacobian conjecture with deg p = 10 and deg q = 15. Then in [2], a counterexample
with deg p = 9 and deg q = 15 appeared.
So far it follows then that 6 ≤k ≤9. In this work, we show that k ≥7, see
Theorem 5.1 and Corollary 5.2. Then it remains to decide whether k = 7, 8 or 9.

Our result was obtained by combining tools from algebraic geometry and dy-
namical systems. Indeed, in Section 1 we recall general key facts in the context
of the global injectivity, brieﬂy explaining our strategy. In Section 2 we recall the
tools from algebraic geometry we will employ in our paper. It is here that we will
introduce the function NS(c), which counts the number of branches at inﬁnity of
the curve p(x, y) = c associated with the outer edge S of the Newton polygon of
the polynomial p. When p is a submersion, the sum of the functions NS(c), con-
sidering all the outer edges, is N(c), which is precisely the number of connected
components of the ﬁber p−1(c). In this section, Lemma 2.4, with results on NS,
will be the main one. We ﬁnish the section by deﬁning the Euler integral of NS
and recalling S¸ekalski’s theorem relating the Euler integral with the index of the
gradient of p at inﬁnity.
In Section 3 we recall the Poincar´e compactiﬁcation of polynomial vector ﬁelds as
well as some key concepts on dynamical systems we will need. Then we particularize
to the Hamiltonian vector ﬁeld ∇p⊥associated with a polynomial function p. The
concept of hyperbolic sectors at inﬁnity of ∇p⊥will be recalled. It is the main

In Section 4 we put together the techniques of the previous sections to construct

1. Candidates for p
1.1. Connectedness of ﬁbers and bifurcation set. It is well known that a
polynomial map F satisfying (1) is injective if and only if the ﬁbers of p are con-
nected. Indeed, (1) ensures the monotonicity of q along the connected components
of p−1(c), what proves the “only if” part. The “if” part follows because injective
polynomial maps of Rn are automatically onto, see for instance [1], so the ﬁbers of
p are connected. Then for F satisfying (1), the injectivity of F is equivalent to p
being a locally trivial ﬁbration, that is, the bifurcation set of p, that we will denote
by B(p), is empty, see more details, for instance, in [6, 23].
1.2. Highest homogeneous part. For a polynomial function p, we will denote
by p+ its homogeneous part of highest degree. From [4, Lemma 4.2], as well as from
[9, Theorem 2.2] and [7, Proposition 4], it follows that if a polynomial submersion
has a disconnected ﬁber then p+ must have linear factors of multiplicity at least 2.
Therefore, since we want to prove the injectivity of maps F satisfying (1), we only
need to be concerned with polynomial submersions p such that
(2)
p+(x, y) = (ax + by)2r(x, y),
where a, b ∈R and r(x, y) is a homogeneous polynomial.
1.3. Jacobian mates. We say that a polynomial submersion p : R2 →R has a
Jacobian mate when there exists a polynomial submersion q : R2 →R such that
the map (p, q) satisﬁes (1). In this case, q is a Jacobian mate of p.
