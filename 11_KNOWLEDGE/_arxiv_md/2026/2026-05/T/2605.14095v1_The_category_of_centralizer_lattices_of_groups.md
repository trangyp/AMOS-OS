THE CATEGORY OF CENTRALIZER LATTICES OF GROUPS
WILLIAM COCKE, MARK L. LEWIS, AND RYAN MCCULLOCH
Abstract. We formalize the concept of a centralizer-respecting homomorphism, surjective
homomorphisms which are equivariant with respect to taking the centralizer of a subgroup.
There is a functor from the category of centralizer-respecting homomorphisms to the category
of centralizer lattices. Finally, we conclude with some theorems about centralizer-respecting
homomorphisms that show that the category of centralizer-respecting homomorphisms has
many interesting maps.
1. Introduction.
Within group theory, one encounters the concept of a centralizer, i.e., if G is a group and
X a subset of elements of G, then we can form the set
CG(X) = {g ∈G ∶∀x ∈X,xg = gx}.
In words, CG(X), called the centralizer of X in G, is the set of all elements in G that
commute with every element of X, and for any X ⊆G, the set CG(X) is a subgroup of G.
Of more interest, the centralizer operator acts on the subgroup lattice of G. As an operator,
the centralizer has enough entertaining properties to make it an amusing object for further
exploration. For example, the operator is antitone (short for anti-monotone), is an involution
when restricted to its image, and behaves well with respect to intersections.
(antitone) X ⊆Y ⇒CG(Y ) ≤CG(X).
(involution on its image) CG(CG(CG(⋅))) = CG(⋅).
(intersection of centralizers) CG(X) ∩CG(Y ) = CG(⟨X,Y ⟩).
Note that the notation ⟨X,Y ⟩means the subgroup generated by all elements in X and Y .
The poset of all centralizer of a group G forms the so-called centralizer lattice C(G) (see
Definition 2.3).
It is well-known that the centralizer behaves poorly with respect to homomorphisms, that
is, for groups G and H with a homomorphism ϕ ∶G →H, it is often the case that ϕ(CG(A)) ≠
CH(ϕ(A)) where A is a subgroup of G. For example, if G is nonabelian and H is abelian
then CH(ϕ(A)) = H for all subgroups A ≤G. Since the centralizer does not respect homomor-
phisms, it is difficult to study in a categorical setting. This paper reconciles the centralizer
operator and homomorphisms by formalizing the concept of centralizer-respecting homomor-
phisms. Moreover, we show that there is a pleasing categorical view of these homomorphisms
by exhibiting a functor from centralizer-respecting homomorphisms to centralizer lattices.
Definition 1.1 (centralizer-respecting homomorphisms). A surjective group homomorphism
ϕ ∶G →H is centralizer-respecting if for subgroups A ≤G we have ϕ(CG(A)) = CH(ϕ(A)).

We show that centralizer-respecting homomorphisms induce maps on the corresponding
centralizer lattices. For a centralizer-respecting homomorphism ϕ, we write Cϕ for the map
that takes a centralizer CG(X) to CH(ϕ(X)) = ϕ(CG(X)).
Theorem A. If ϕ is a centralizer-respecting homomorphism from G to H, then the map Cϕ
is a homomorphism of the centralizer lattices C(G) →C(H).
Moreover, more importantly, and more excitingly, the induced map is functorial. Once we
properly define the category Groupscrh of groups with centralizer-respecting homomorphisms
and the category CentLattices of centralizer lattices in Section 3, we show that the map C
given by C(G) = C(G) and C(ϕ) = Cϕ is a functor.
Theorem B. The map C where C(G) = C(G) and C(ϕ) = Cϕ is a functor from Groupscrh to
CentLattices.
After proving Theorem A and Theorem B we address the important question of “are there
interesting centralizer-respecting homomorphisms?”, equivalently “are there enough homo-
morphisms in the category Groupscrh to explore?”
In Section 4 we present a family of
centralizer-respecting homomorphisms and show how to construct new centralizer-respecting
homomorphisms from existing ones.
Theorem C. Let ϕ ∶G →K be a surjective homomorphism with a central kernel.
The
following are equivalent.
(1) The homomorphism ϕ is centralizer-respecting.
(2) The kernel of ϕ does not contain any nontrivial commutators, elements of the form
[x,y] ≠1.
Moreover, if such a homomorphism ϕ is centralizer-respecting, then the induced map Cϕ is an
isomorphism of centralizer lattices.
Theorem C provides a number of examples of centralizer-respecting homomorphisms. For
example, consider a group with exactly one non-commutator as identified by the recent work
of Hatem and Sinoira [4], or the family of such groups constructed by Skresanov [7]: the
homomorphism corresponding to taking the quotient by the subgroup generated by the non-
commutator is a centralizer-respecting homomorphism.
Theorem C also provides a most
entertaining corollary as an example of application of the functor C.
Corollary 1.2. Fix a positive integer n > 2. Let G and H be maximal class 2-groups of order
2n. Then the centralizer lattices of G and H are isomorphic.
Proof. As we show in Lemmata 4.3 and 4.4, any two nonisomorphic maximal class 2-groups
G and H of order 2n occur as quotients of a group J of order 2n+1 by central subgroups K
and I such that K and I do not contain any nontrivial elements of the form [x,y]. Hence
C(J) ≅C(G) and C(J) ≅C(H). We conclude that C(G) ≅C(H).
□
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. In Section 2 we provide some of the basics
of group theory and lattice theory. This lets us prove Theorem A. Next, we prove Theorem
B in Section 3. Finally, in Section 4 we explore some of the homomorphisms that are in
Groupscrh.

2. Groups and lattices.
Given a group, we can identify all of its subgroups into a poset. This poset is actually
a lattice, i.e., a partially ordered set with a top ⊺and bottom  satisfying the rules of the
following definition.
Definition 2.1 (lattice). A lattice L is a partially ordered set together with two distinguished
elements ⊺and  such that for all x ∈L we have x ≤⊺and for all x ∈L we have  ≤x. There
are two binary operations on L known as meet, written ∧, and join, written ∨; where
●x ∧y is the largest element z of L such that z ≤x and z ≤y.
●x ∨y is the smallest element z of L such that x ≤z and y ≤z.
Definition 2.2 (lattice homomorphism). Given lattices L,M, a map f ∶L →M is a lattice
homomorphism if f(x) ∧f(y) = f(x ∧y) and f(x) ∨f(y) = f(x ∨y).
For a group G, the set of all subgroups of G form a lattice where ≤is ⊆(inclusion as a
subset), ∧= ∩(intersection) and ∨= ⟨⋅⟩(subgroup generated by), and ⊺= G and  = 1. There
is a rich history of investigating groups via their subgroup lattices, for example, the book by
Schmidt [6] or the book by Suzuki [8]. More modern work on subgroup lattices tends to focus
on specific subgroup lattices, e.g., the so-called Chermak–Delgado lattice [9, 5] or other more
exotic subgroup lattices [3].
For a group G, the poset of all of its centralizers form a lattice where the meet is intersection
(as above) and the join is defined using the involution CG(⋅) as follows.
Definition 2.3. (centralizer lattice) For a group G, let the centralizer lattice of G, written
as C(G), consist of the poset of subgroups of G of the form CG(X) for some X ⊆G, together
with ∧C and ∨C, and an involution CG(⋅), where for all X,Y ≤G we have
CG(X) ∧C CG(Y ) = CG(⟨X,Y ⟩)
CG(X) ∨C CG(Y ) = CG(CG(CG(X)) ∧C CG(CG(Y )))
CG(CG(CG(X) = CG(X).
We use the notation ∧C,∨C to distinguish from the operations ∧and ∨on the subgroup lat-
tice of G. Antonov [1] has investigated groups where the operations coincide. For centralizers,
CG(X) ∨C CG(Y ) is also the smallest centralizer in G containing both CG(X) and CG(Y ).
While ∧C is just the restriction of ∧to inputs in C(G), it is not generally the case that
CG(X) ∨C CG(Y ) = ⟨CG(X),CG(Y )⟩= CG(X) ∨CG(Y ).
The centralizer lattice of a group is not a sublattice of the subgroup lattice because, as noted
in Definition 2.3, the join operation is not the same as the join operation for the group itself.
In addition, the centralizer lattice of a group G has additional structure, i.e., the involution
given by taking CG(⋅).
Definition 2.4 (centralizer lattice homomorphism). Given two centralizer lattices C(G) and
C(H) a centralizer lattice homomorphism is a lattice homomorphism f from C(G)
to C(H) (over ∧C and ∨C) that respects the centralizer involution, i.e., f(CG(CG(X)) =
CH(f(CG(X)) for all X ≤G.
We now prove Theorem A that for a centralizer-respecting homomorphism ϕ ∶G →H the
induced map Cϕ is a centralizer lattice homomorphism of C(G) →C(H).

3. The two categories and a functor between them.
We are now ready to show that groups with centralizer-respecting homomorphisms forms a
category. We use the definitions of categories and functors from Awodey [2].
Lemma 3.1. There is a category whose objects consist of groups and whose arrows are
centralizer-respecting homomorphisms. We write Groupscrh for this category.
Proof. Since Groups forms a category, we know that the composition of homomorphisms
is associative, and there are appropriate identity homomorphisms from each group to itself.

To show that Groupscrh forms a category, we need to show that the composition of two
centralizer-respecting homomorphisms is a centralizer-respecting homomorphism. Let both
ϕ ∶G →H and ψ ∶H →K be centralizer-respecting homomorphisms. Then
(ψ ○ϕ)(CG(A)) = ψ(CH(ϕ(A))) = CK(ψ(ϕ(A))).
Since the composition of surjective maps is surjective, we conclude that the composition of
centralizer-respecting homomorphisms is centralizer-respecting.
Moreover, for any group G, the identity isomorphism is the identity arrow 1G.
Hence Groupscrh forms a category.
□
Definition 3.2 (CentLattices). The category CentLattices has centralizer lattices of groups
as objects and centralizer lattice homomorphisms as arrows.
Definition 3.3 (functor). A functor F from A to B consists of
●a mapping of objects A ∈A to objects F(A) ∈B.
●a mapping of morphisms f ∈A to morphisms F(f) ∈B such that:
(1) (dom)(F(f)) = F(dom(f)).
(2) (cod)(F(f)) = F(cod(f)).
(3) F(1A) = 1F(A).
(4) F(g ○f) = F(g) ○F(f), for composable f and g in A.
We now prove Theorem B that the map C is a functor from Groupscrh to CentLattices.
Proof of Theorem B. We need to show that C satisfies the definition of a functor.
By construction C maps groups to centralizer lattices. Moreover for a centralizer-respecting
homomorphism ϕ from G to H the centralizer lattice homomorphism Cϕ has domain G and
H.
We note that for the identity homomorphism 1G from G to G, the induced map on centralizer
lattices is the identity map, i.e., C(1G) = 1C(G).
We now show that C behaves well with respect to composition of homomorphisms. Let
ϕ ∶G →H and ψ ∶H →K be centralizer-respecting homomorphisms. Consider the diagram
in Figure 2. We compute
(Cψ ○Cϕ)(CG(X)) = Cψ(ϕ(CG(X))) = ψ(ϕ(CG(X))) = (ψ ○ϕ)(CG(X)) = Cψ○ϕ(CG(X)).
Hence C is a functor from Groupscrh to CentLattices.
□

Proof. ⇒Suppose by way of contradiction that ψ ○ϕ is centralizer-respecting, but ψ is not
centralizer-respecting. Then there is some subgroup A ≤H such that ψ(CH(A)) ≠CK(ψ(A)).

The next lemma demonstrates that the functor C translates some properties of centralizer-
respecting homomorphisms to the induced map on lattices. The ability to ask such questions
motivates the categorical approach.
Lemma 4.2. Let ϕ be a centralizer-respecting homomorphism between two groups G and H.
Then the kernel of ϕ is central if and only if the induced map Cϕ on centralizer lattices is an
isomorphism.
Proof. ⇒Write K for the kernel of ϕ and assume that K is central. We show that Cϕ is an
isomorphishm. First we show it is surjective. Given a centralizer CH(B) in H, we note that
there is some A ≤G with ϕ(A) = B. Then
CH(B) = CH(ϕ(A)) = ϕ(CG(A)) = Cϕ(CG(A)).
For injectivity consider two subgroups A,C ≤G such that Cϕ(CG(A)) = Cϕ(CG(C)). Then
ϕ(CG(A) = ϕ(CG(C)) which implies that CG(A)K = CG(C)K. But, since K is central, it is
contained in CG(A) and CG(C) and thus we conclude that CG(A) = CG(C).
⇐Assume that Cϕ is an isomorphism of C(G) and C(H). Then in particular
H = CH(ϕ(K)) = ϕ(CG(K)).
Thus CG(K) = G and K is central.
□
