The Physical and Contextual Limits of Quantum Speedup
Karl Svozil
1
1Institute for Theoretical Physics, TU Wien, Wiedner Hauptstraße 8-10/136, 1040 Vienna, Austria∗
(Dated: May 20, 2026)
Quantum computation is frequently mischaracterized as the simultaneous execution of exponen-
tially many classical computations. This article offers a conceptual clarification of why this “branch-
wise parallelism” picture is misleading, demonstrating that the components of a quantum super-
position cannot be treated as independently readable classical branches. Quantum speedups arise
instead from reversible embeddings of algebraic structure made accessible through engineered inter-
ference patterns; more precisely, many speedups identify a class in a partition of possible instances
rather than reconstructing the full instance. We review this mechanism through several constraints:
unitary garbage erasure is impossible, copying and deletion are context-dependent, and contextu-
ality obstructs a single global classical history. We also distinguish circuit or unitary universality
from Turing universality: dense generation of unitaries is not the same as symbolic computation
over unbounded inputs with recursion, uniformity, and self-reference. In closed unitary dynamics
there is no nontrivial absorbing halting state of the classical many-to-one kind; operational termi-
nation requires clocks, flags, measurements, open-system records, or external control. Exponential
Hilbert-space dimension supplies a geometry for interference and high-dimensional embedding, not
unlimited classical readout.
I.
INTRODUCTION
A common description of quantum computation says
that a quantum computer evaluates a function on many
inputs “at once.”
The image is seductive: a register is
placed in a superposition, a function is applied to all
components, and a final measurement extracts the de-
sired answer.
This picture is often invoked when dis-
cussing Deutsch–Jozsa [1], Simon’s algorithm [2], Shor’s
algorithm [3], Grover search [4], and related oracle or
Fourier-sampling procedures [5, 6].
Taken literally, however, this picture is wrong. A quan-
tum computer does not return the list of all computed
values. The components of a superposition cannot be in-
dividually inspected, copied, deleted, or archived as clas-
sical alternatives. They are amplitudes in a Hilbert-space
vector, and only suitably arranged interference effects be-
come accessible in a final measurement context, where a
context is understood as a compatible set of observables,
or equivalently a choice of measurement basis in the non-
degenerate finite-dimensional case.
The thesis of this article is the following: Quantum
speedups are not many classical computations performed
in parallel. They arise when the relevant predicate of a
task, often a cell in a partition of possible problem in-
stances, can be embedded into a unitary evolution so that
it becomes accessible by interference in a chosen mea-
surement context. At the same time, copying and erasure
are physically constrained by no-cloning, no-deletion, and
Landauer irreversibility, while contextuality prevents the
intermediate process from being interpreted as a single
global classical history or as a noncontextual value as-
signment.

A related ambiguity concerns the word “universal.”
Circuit or unitary universality means that a finite gate set
can approximate a large class of unitary transformations.
Turing universality, by contrast, concerns the symbolic
simulation of arbitrary effective procedures on inputs of
unbounded length. These are not the same notion. A
family of quantum circuits may define an algorithm in
the complexity-theoretic sense only when a uniform clas-
sical or quantum rule generates the appropriate circuit
for each input size. A single finite circuit, or even a uni-
versal finite gate set, is not by itself equivalent to the
class of partial recursive functions.
This formulation is deliberately restrictive. It does not
deny quantum speedup. It denies that branchwise clas-
sical computation explains it, and it denies that circuit
universality should be conflated with Turing universality.
The claim is not meant to settle interpretive questions
about Everettian or many-worlds accounts of quantum
computation. Advocates of such accounts have argued
that quantum algorithms can be understood in terms of
computations distributed across branches or worlds [7–9].
The narrower point made here is operational and model-
theoretic: the components of a superposition are not in-
dependently readable classical records, and the computa-
tional advantage is realized only when the global unitary
evolution and final measurement context convert phase
relations into an accessible statistic.
The paper is therefore mainly clarificatory. Its tech-
nical contribution is the leakage construction in Ap-
pendix A, which gives a simple unitary interpolation be-
tween the Bernstein–Vazirani interference pattern and
ordinary value-query behavior by exporting a tunable
amount of which-path information to an inaccessible wit-
ness.

SUPERPOSITION IS NOT BRANCHWISE
READOUT
Let an n-qubit state be written in the computational
basis as

The dimension of the Hilbert space is 2n, and this expo-
nential dimension is often used to motivate quantum par-
allelism. But the amplitudes αx are not classical memory
cells. A measurement yields one outcome per run, with
probabilities determined by the Born rule. Recovering
the amplitudes requires many similarly prepared systems
and many measurement settings, that is, tomography.
Thus a superposition can be acted on linearly, but it
cannot be read branch by branch. Holevo’s theorem gives
one precise expression of this limitation. For an ensemble
of states on a Hilbert space H, the accessible classical
information is bounded above by the Holevo quantity χ,
and χ ≤log2 dim H. Hence, for an n-qubit transmission,
the accessible classical information per use is at most n
bits [6, 10].
The state vector may contain exponentially many am-
plitudes, but the accessible classical readout is sharply
constrained. This is the first reason why the naive par-
allelism metaphor is misleading. The superposed com-
ponents can interfere, but they are not simultaneously
available classical facts.

IV.
GARBAGE IS NOT ERASED BY
UNITARITY
A second misconception concerns garbage. Classical
irreversible computation often hides the physical cost of
deleting information. Quantum computation cannot do
so, because closed-system evolution is unitary:
|ψ⟩7→U |ψ⟩,
U †U = I.
A non-injective classical function cannot be implemented
by a unitary acting only on the input register. It must
be embedded reversibly, for example as
Uf : |x⟩|y⟩7→|x⟩|y ⊕f(x)⟩.
The input, or an equivalent record, remains present.
If a reversible computation produces
|x⟩|0⟩|0⟩7−→|x⟩|f(x)⟩|g(x)⟩,
the garbage register |g(x)⟩cannot simply be deleted. It
must be uncomputed, exported, measured, or thermody-
namically reset.
No isolated unitary erasure. Let |ϕ1⟩and |ϕ2⟩be two
normalized pure states on a Hilbert space H which are
not identical up to a global phase.
Then there is no
unitary U on H and no fixed normalized state |0⟩∈H
such that
U |ϕ1⟩= U |ϕ2⟩= |0⟩.
Indeed, unitaries preserve inner products. If both equal-
ities held, then
⟨ϕ1|ϕ2⟩= ⟨ϕ1| U †U |ϕ2⟩= ⟨0|0⟩= 1,
so |ϕ1⟩and |ϕ2⟩would coincide as rays, contradicting
the assumption.
This is the same elementary isomet-
ric obstruction that underlies the no-cloning and no-
deletion results: closed reversible dynamics preserves dis-
tinguishability and therefore cannot freely merge, dupli-
cate, or discard unknown alternatives.
Consequently, if |g(x)⟩B is a garbage register produced
by reversible evaluation of a non-constant function g on
input x, there is no unitary acting on B alone that re-
sets |g(x)⟩B to |0⟩B for all x. The garbage can be re-
moved only by (i) uncomputation, using the input reg-
ister A, which is reversible and redistributes rather than
destroys the information; (ii) coupling B to an environ-
ment, dissipating the information thermodynamically; or
(iii) measurement of B, creating a classical record whose
subsequent reset is subject to Landauer’s principle.
Bennett’s reversible-computation analysis shows how
intermediate garbage can be uncomputed [14–16]; it does
not make physical erasure free. Landauer’s principle lo-
cates the cost in reset, coarse graining, or environmental
dissipation [17]. Whether the Landauer–Bennett account
fully exorcises Maxwell’s demon remains a point of philo-
sophical dispute [18–20], but the operational lesson for
quantum computation is unambiguous: unitary dynam-
ics does not delete information.

VI.
CONTEXTUALITY AND THE ABSENCE
OF A GLOBAL CLASSICAL HISTORY
Quantum algorithms move between incompatible de-
scriptions: computational basis, phase basis, Fourier ba-
sis, stabilizer basis, and measurement basis. These basis
changes already express complementarity. A state sharp
in one basis is generally delocalized in another.
In Hilbert spaces of dimension at least three, the
Kochen–Specker theorem strengthens this point:
one
cannot assign pre-existing values to all observables in-
dependently of the commuting context in which they are
measured [25–27]. Operational versions extend contextu-
ality to preparations, transformations, and unsharp mea-
surements [28]. For single qubits the standard Kochen–
Specker obstruction for projective measurements is ab-
sent; simple hidden-variable representations of projective
qubit measurements can be constructed.
This qualifi-
cation is important: generalized measurements can ex-
hibit Kochen–Specker-type contextuality even for a sin-
gle qubit [29].
Nevertheless, complementarity already
prevents joint sharp values for noncommuting projective
observables and so blocks simultaneous classicalization of
all measurement bases.
This matters for quantum computation. The interme-
diate stages of an algorithm cannot generally be inter-
preted as a single classical trajectory through pre-existing
values. The algorithm is a sequence of context changes
implemented by unitary transformations and terminated
by a final measurement context. What becomes classi-
cal is not the whole history, but only the final record.
Between preparation and measurement there is no clas-
sically charted path, only coherent evolution whose in-
termediate description is basis-dependent—like Musil’s
bridge whose solid piers stand only at the beginning and
the end, while the passage through imaginary quantities
in between nevertheless leads from real input to real out-
put [30].
Resource-theoretic results support this perspective,
but with important qualifications. Stabilizer operations
admit efficient classical simulation by the Gottesman–
Knill theorem [31, 32]; hence universal quantum advan-
tage in the circuit model requires resources outside the
efficiently simulable stabilizer fragment. Depending on
the formalism and dimension, these resources may be de-
scribed operationally as magic states, phase-space the-
oretically as Wigner-function negativity, or foundation-
ally as contextuality [33–35]. These characterizations are
related but not identical.
In particular, the clean ne-
cessity results connecting contextuality with magic-state
quantum computation are most direct in odd-prime-
dimensional qudit settings, while the qubit case involves
additional subtleties.
The stable message is therefore
not that contextuality alone is the complete source of
speedup. Rather, quantum algorithms exploit transfor-
mations between incompatible contexts, and genuine ad-
vantage requires resources that prevent the whole com-
putation from being represented as a single efficiently
simulable classical stochastic process.
VII.
QUANTUM UNIVERSALITY VERSUS
TURING UNIVERSALITY
The word “universal” is used in two substantially differ-
ent senses in computation theory and quantum informa-
tion theory. Confusing them leads to conceptual errors.
In the quantum-circuit literature, a finite gate set is
called universal if circuits over that gate set can approxi-
mate arbitrary unitary transformations on n qubits, typ-
ically to arbitrary accuracy in operator norm.
In this
sense, universality is a statement about the dense gener-
ation of groups such as U(2n) or SU(2n) by finite collec-
tions of gates. It is a statement about controllability of
finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.
Turing universality is different. A Turing-universal de-
vice is not merely a generator of transformations on a
fixed finite-dimensional space.
It acts on symbolic in-
puts of arbitrary length, admits arbitrarily long compu-

tations, supports parsing and composition of programs,
and allows self-reference through coding of machines by
strings [36, 37]. This is the setting in which partial recur-
sive functions, undecidability, universal simulation, and
the halting problem arise.
Thus a finite quantum circuit, by itself, does not define
the same kind of object as a Turing machine. A circuit
on n qubits implements a unitary transformation
Un : C2n →C2n.
It is a finite-dimensional, norm-preserving map. It has
fixed width and depth, no unbounded tape, no open-
ended input parsing, and no intrinsic recursion. It also
has no autonomous mechanism for extending its own
computation to arbitrary symbolic length.
A family of circuits {Cn}n≥1 may compute a language
or function in the complexity-theoretic sense only when
there is a uniform rule generating the circuit Cn from
n. That uniformity condition is itself algorithmic and is
normally supplied classically, or by a specified higher-
level quantum model such as a quantum Turing ma-
chine [7, 38, 39]. In this sense, quantum circuit families
and quantum Turing machines are not identified merely
by the existence of a universal gate set; their equivalence
requires explicit uniformity and simulation assumptions.
This distinction is important for the phrase “quantum
computable function.” In the finite-circuit model, a cir-
cuit computes only a finite input-output relation, usually
probabilistically and after measurement. A uniform cir-
cuit family computes a sequence of such relations.
A
quantum Turing machine or another uniform quantum
model can be compared to classical Turing computation,
but then the usual Church–Turing issue concerns com-
putability, while the quantum advantage concerns com-
plexity. Standard quantum computation is not believed
to enlarge the class of computable classical functions be-
yond the partial recursive ones; rather, it may reduce the
resources needed for some computable tasks.
A related point concerns halting. Classical irreversible
computation may enter an absorbing terminal configura-
tion: many distinct pre-halting configurations can evolve
into one stable halt state.
Such many-to-one absorp-
tion is not a primitive of closed unitary dynamics.
If
U |h⟩= |h⟩and U |ψ⟩= |h⟩, then injectivity of U gives
|ψ⟩= |h⟩. Thus a closed unitary may coherently mark,
clock, or correlate halting information, but it cannot col-
lapse distinct computational histories into a single ab-
sorbing halt state without exporting information to ad-
ditional degrees of freedom.
This elementary observation is not a no-go theorem
against quantum Turing machines. Standard quantum
models implement operational termination through halt
flags, clocks, external control, or measurement proto-
cols [7, 38, 40, 41].
The point is only that the classi-
cal absorbing-state picture is not itself a closed-unitary
primitive.
This reinforces the main thesis. Quantum circuit uni-
versality is a powerful notion of finite-dimensional con-
trollability and approximation. Turing universality is a
notion of symbolic, unbounded, self-referential compu-
tation under an explicit uniformity condition. They in-
tersect in uniform models of quantum computation, but
they are not identical. Treating “universal quantum com-
putation” as if it automatically meant Turing universality
obscures the distinction between unitary dynamics, mea-
surement, and algorithmic control.
VIII.
CONCLUSION
Quantum computation is not classical computation
performed in exponentially many parallel branches whose
results can later be inspected.
Its power lies in re-
versible amplitude manipulation, phase-sensitive compu-
tation, and contextual measurement. Speedup appears
when the desired predicate—often a partition label rather
than a full classical description of the instance—can be
encoded into a unitary process and made visible by in-
terference in an appropriate final context.
Exponential Hilbert-space dimension should not be
mistaken for exponentially accessible classical storage.
Large dimension provides room for structured embed-
dings and interference, but readout is constrained by
measurement, Holevo bounds, and the chosen context. In
this sense high-dimensional quantum state space is a re-
source for controlled recombination, not for unrestricted
branchwise inspection.
The negative constraints are just as important as the
positive mechanism. Garbage cannot be erased by uni-
tary dynamics; it must be uncomputed or exported, and
physical reset is thermodynamic.
Unknown quantum
states cannot be freely copied, broadcast, or deleted. In-
compatible contexts prevent the assignment of a single
efficiently simulable classical history to the computation.
Nor should circuit or unitary universality be confused
with Turing universality. A finite universal gate set pro-
vides dense control over unitary transformations; it is
not, by itself, a symbolic machine with arbitrary parsing,
recursion, self-reference, and a classical halting seman-
tics. In closed unitary dynamics there is no nontrivial
absorbing halt state of the classical many-to-one kind.
Termination and output extraction require clocks, flags,
measurements, open-system records, or external control.
Thus quantum computation is neither magic paral-
lelism nor ordinary classical computation in disguise.
It is context-dependent, thermodynamically embedded,
reversible information processing whose useful answers
must be engineered as interference phenomena.

The two extremes make the physical point explicit. For
k = 0, no computational-basis path label is exported and
the ordinary one-query Bernstein–Vazirani interference
pattern is recovered. For k = n, the witness records the
entire computational-basis label x, all off-diagonal terms
in that basis are removed from the algorithm’s accessible
state, and the final Hadamard transform has no global
phase pattern left to recombine. The Boolean function
fa has still been evaluated coherently by a unitary per-
mutation on the enlarged system. What has been lost is
not evaluation but recombination.
This example shows that reversibility alone is not the
relevant criterion. The map W S
a is a unitary permuta-
tion on the enlarged Hilbert space. If the witness reg-
ister remains coherently accessible, one may uncompute
it and restore the full interference pattern. If, however,
the witness register has escaped into an inaccessible en-
vironment, then the reduced dynamics available to the
algorithm is no longer reversible.
The witness regis-
ter is precisely the ununcomputed garbage identified in
the main text as an obstruction to free unitary erasure,
here promoted to a tunable knob. The speedup is lost
not because the Boolean function fa has changed, nor
because the global dynamics has ceased to be unitary,
but because an inaccessible which-path record selects a
computational-basis context incompatible with the later
Fourier-basis recombination.
Pointedly stated, this demonstrates that a quantum
computer does not “compute all branches at once and let
us read them”—where the scare-quoted branch language
names precisely the classical picture being rejected. If
it did, leaking the computational-basis path labels to a
witness register would not matter: the function fa(x)
was still computed.
