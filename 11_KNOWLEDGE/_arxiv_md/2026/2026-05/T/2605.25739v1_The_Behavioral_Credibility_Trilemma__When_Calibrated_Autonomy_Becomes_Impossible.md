Abstract
We prove that no reinforcement learning policy with confidence-gated autonomy can si-
multaneously achieve maximum helpfulness, optimal calibration, and full autonomy under
rational oversight, whenever some tasks exceed the agent’s reliable competence: the Behav-
ioral Credibility Trilemma. The impossibility is geometric: adding any non-affine autonomy
incentive to a strictly proper scoring rule destroys strict properness, so an agent rewarded
for both calibrated confidence and autonomous action systematically inflates its reported
confidence on tasks below the principal’s approval threshold. The Behavioral Perturbation
Lemma quantifies the inflation (scaling as wA/(2wC) for the Brier score) and shows detection
requires Ω(1/∆2) observations. We prove the principal’s optimal oversight rule is neces-
sarily non-affine, making the impossibility unconditional and optimizer-independent across
log-concave-density policy families.
We formalize the Confidence-Gated Decision Prob-
lem, map existing methods onto the trilemma, and identify two constructive resolution
pathways (commitment, domain separation). A 540-configuration Best-of-N experiment
tests five pre-registered hypotheses, all strongly confirmed (effect sizes d = 1.10–5.32), and
adds a descriptive analysis of the achievable-(H, C, A) surface geometry showing a plateau-
truncated frontier consistent with the predicted inflation saturation.
Keywords:
reinforcement learning, proper scoring rules, mechanism design, agent cali-
bration, AI safety

1 Introduction
1.1 The General Problem
Consider any system in which an RL agent must report confidence before acting, a gating
mechanism uses the reported confidence to decide whether to approve the action, and
the agent’s reward increases with degree of autonomous operation. This combination of
private information, scored self-reporting, and conflicting incentives arises naturally in any
autonomous system that must earn trust through calibrated self-assessment: a coding agent
that states “I am 92% confident this refactoring is correct” before committing, a medical AI
that reports “probability of malignancy: 0.73” before recommending biopsy, an autonomous
vehicle that declares “confidence in lane-change safety: 0.96” before executing.
The central question is whether can such a system simultaneously maximize task per-
formance, maintain calibrated confidence reports, and retain full autonomy? We prove the
answer is no.
The structure is analogous to the credibility problems studied in mechanism design (Ak-
barpour and Li, 2020): when the objective depends on the report through a channel other
than accuracy, truthful reporting ceases to be optimal. The classical result of Savage (1971),
Schervish (1989), and Gneiting and Raftery (2007) establishes that strictly proper scoring
rules are the unique class of scoring functions under which truthful reporting is optimal, and
that strict properness is destroyed by any non-affine perturbation. The approval channel is
precisely such a perturbation.
1.2 The Trilemma, Informally
We formalize the underlying tension as a trilemma among three desirable properties of an
AI agent’s policy:
• Helpfulness (H): The agent selects utility-maximizing actions.
• Calibration (C): The agent’s reported confidence matches its true success probabil-
ity.1
• Autonomy (A): The agent acts without requesting human approval.
Any two of these properties are simultaneously achievable at their respective exact max-
ima; all three exact maxima are not simultaneously achievable, when the principal enforces
a meaningful safety standard and tasks involve genuine uncertainty (Figure 1). The impos-
sibility concerns exact joint optimality; in practice, the agent operates on a Pareto frontier
where each objective is achieved at a level strictly below its individual maximum, and
the weight vector (wH, wC, wA) selects the operating point. The trilemma is therefore a
structural trade-off, not a catastrophic failure. The three achievable pairs correspond to
recognizable behavioral patterns:

1.3 Why It Is Fundamental
The impossibility arises from the geometry of the scoring landscape, not from any assump-
tion about the agent’s reasoning process. Any oversight system that conditions approval on
reported confidence adds a non-accuracy component to the objective. Whether the agent is
a rational utility-maximizer or a neural network trained by gradient descent, any optimizer
ascending this perturbed surface arrives at a shifted optimum.
The formal content: a strictly proper scoring rule has a unique optimal report equal
to the true probability (Brier, 1950; Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). Adding any non-affine
function of the report destroys this strict properness (Schervish, 1989). Since any practical
approval policy is nonlinear in the report, the perturbed scoring rule is not strictly proper
and any optimizer’s fixed point deviates from the truth. The only perturbations preserving
strict properness are affine functions of the report, which provide no meaningful safety
gate. What is new beyond this classical perturbation result is detailed in the contributions
below: the AI-oversight application with threshold approval, the unconditional non-affinity
theorem, the optimizer-independence proof, the Brier-uniqueness characterisation, and the
method mapping.
1.4 Contributions
This paper makes four contributions:
1. The Behavioral Perturbation Lemma and the Trilemma Theorem. We prove
that any non-affine dependence of autonomy on the confidence report destroys strict
properness (Lemma 22), quantify the inflation magnitude and detection complexity,
and derive the three-way impossibility (Theorems 25 and 26). We further prove that
the principal’s optimal oversight rule is necessarily non-affine (Theorem 27), making
the impossibility unconditional, and identify the Brier score as the unique strictly
proper rule (up to affine equivalence) with distribution-uniform first-order welfare
loss under a smooth gate (Proposition 29).
2. Confidence-Gated Decision Problem formalization. We define the Confidence-
Gated Decision Problem (Definition 1), the natural formal setting for RL with
confidence-gated autonomy, and prove that the trilemma is a property of this decision
problem’s payoff surface (Section 3.1).
3. Optimizer-independence. We prove via the Harris–Proschan–Sethuraman covari-
ance inequality for monotone functions under log-concave measures (Harris, 1960;
Proschan and Sethuraman, 1977) that any expected-improvement optimization pro-
cess within the log-concave-density policy family, whether rational deliberation, policy
gradient, or evolutionary strategy, ascending the perturbed payoff surface converges
away from calibration (Theorem 37).
4. Method mapping.
We show where eight existing methods sit in the (H, C, A)
achievable region, distinguishing Goodhart effects (methods without calibration) from
trilemma effects (methods with calibration). We identify two constructive resolution
pathways (commitment, domain separation), each architecturally enforcing the third
objective.

2 Background and Related Work
2.1 RLHF and Alignment Training
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) trains language models by learning
a reward model from pairwise human comparisons under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model
(Christiano et al., 2017), then optimizing the policy via PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) with a
KL penalty against the reference policy. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) exploits the
closed-form KL-regularized optimal policy to eliminate the separate reward model (Rafailov
et al., 2023).
RLHF reward models trained on human preferences systematically favor
helpfulness and fluency, creating the systematic helpfulness bias that drives the H+A corner
of the trilemma.
2.2 Constrained and Safe RLHF
Safe RLHF (Dai et al., 2024) decouples helpfulness and harmlessness into separate reward
and cost models. CPO (Achiam et al., 2017) provides trust-region updates with per-iteration
safety guarantees.
Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) uses natural-language principles
evaluated by an LLM judge. These approaches share a fundamental limitation: none include
calibration as an explicit training signal.

2.3 Proper Scoring Rules and Calibration
A scoring rule S(r, ω) is strictly proper if the expected score ¯S(r; p) = Ep[S(r, ω)] is uniquely
maximized at r = p for every distribution p (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007). The Brier score
S(r, ω) = −(r −ω)2 is the canonical example (Brier, 1950).
Notation convention.
Throughout this paper, S(r, ω) denotes the outcome-based
scoring rule (a function of the report r and the realized outcome ω ∈{0, 1}), while
S(r, p) := Eω[S(r, ω) | p] denotes the expected score used in the theoretical analysis. Un-
der the Brier score, S(r, p) = −(r −p)2 −p(1 −p); since the p(1 −p) term is constant
in r, we write S(r, p) = −(r −p)2 when optimizing over r. In the experimental section,
S(ri, yi) = −(ri−yi)2 is the outcome-based score evaluated against the observed binary out-
come yi. The attribution chain for proper scoring rules runs from Savage (1971)’s elicitation
framework through McCarthy (1956)’s early formalization and Winkler (1969)’s systematic
treatment of probability assessors to the comprehensive characterization of Gneiting and
Raftery (2007). The Savage (1971) and Schervish (1989) characterizations establish that
strictly proper scoring rules are fully determined by a strictly convex function and that strict
properness is destroyed by any non-affine perturbation. This classical result is the founda-
tion of our impossibility. The Schervish (1989) representation also expresses each strictly
proper rule as a mixture of two-decision threshold scores against a measure on (0, 1): this
perspective is what isolates Brier in Proposition 29 as the unique strictly proper rule whose
threshold measure is uniform on (0, 1), hence the unique rule whose first-order welfare loss
vanishes for every type distribution under a single-threshold smooth-gate mechanism.
The LLM calibration literature shows that language models have internal states cor-
related with uncertainty (Kadavath et al., 2022), that pre-trained transformers exhibit
non-trivial calibration properties (Desai and Durrett, 2020; Guo et al., 2017), and that
calibrated confidence can be elicited through appropriate prompting (Jiang et al., 2021;
Tian et al., 2023). Post-hoc calibration methods such as Platt scaling (Platt, 1999), his-
togram binning (Naeini et al., 2015), and temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) can improve
calibration without retraining, but do not address the incentive conflict that arises when
calibration enters the training objective alongside autonomy. Ovadia et al. (2019) show that
calibration degrades under distribution shift, a concern orthogonal to but compounding the
trilemma. Tian et al. (2023) demonstrate that RLHF fine-tuning degrades calibration rela-
tive to pre-training, providing empirical support for the Perturbation Lemma’s predictions.
However, calibration is typically measured post-hoc rather than included as an explicit
training signal.
The qualitative impossibility established here is complemented by quantitative mag-
nitude bounds available within specific scoring rule families.

2.4 Alignment Impossibilities
Several recent impossibility results constrain the alignment landscape. Wolf et al. (2023)
show that no alignment procedure can simultaneously guarantee representativeness,
tractability, and robustness.
Gaikwad (2025) demonstrate mutual exclusivity of strong
optimization, perfect value capture, and robust generalization. Casper et al. (2023) survey
fundamental limitations of RLHF.
Our trilemma is fundamentally different in mechanism: existing impossibilities operate
at the system design level (computational or statistical barriers); ours operates at the deploy-
ment level (what a trained agent can achieve under oversight). Ours is incentive-theoretic:
even if training is perfect, the information asymmetry between agent and principal creates
the trilemma.
The Perturbation Lemma is related to Goodhart’s Law. In the taxonomy of Manheim
and Garrabrant (2019), the strategic inflation we identify is an instance of the adversarial
variant. What the Perturbation Lemma adds is the specific proper-scoring-rule formaliza-
tion: the inflation magnitude is characterized in closed form, and the detection complexity
is bounded.
2.5 Mechanism Design for AI
The connection between mechanism design and AI alignment has been explored from several
directions. The specific interaction between proper scoring rules and threshold oversight in
the AI agent setting has not been studied. Our formal model is a screening model with
hidden type in the tradition of Mirrlees (1971) and Myerson (1981); the single-task, multi-
objective structure with conflict between calibration and autonomy places it within the
mechanism-design tradition of Laffont and Tirole (1993). The canonical dominant-strategy
truthful mechanism for allocation with value-reporting agents is the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves
mechanism (Vickrey, 1961; Clarke, 1971; Groves, 1973); we are explicit that our CGDP is
not such a mechanism — the designer’s instrument is restricted to a scoring rule plus ap-
proval function, not a transfer-based allocation, so VCG-style truthfulness does not apply
(see Remark 8). This is also distinct from the cheap-talk model of Crawford and Sobel
(1982): the agent’s report is scored against outcomes, creating a direct payoff consequence.
Recent work by Conitzer et al. (2024) connects social choice theory to alignment; Hadfield-
Menell and Hadfield (2019) draws on incomplete contract theory (Hart and Moore, 1988).
The property elicitation framework of Frongillo and Kash (2015) characterizes what sta-
tistical functionals can be truthfully elicited; our result characterizes when that truthful
elicitation breaks under conflicting incentives.
The CGDP also sits within the broader
strategic-prediction landscape: Hardt et al. (2016) formalise strategic classification, where
data points are reported by strategic agents anticipating the classifier’s decision, and Per-
domo et al. (2020) introduce performative prediction, where the act of prediction shifts
the data distribution. The trilemma occupies the scoring-rule-based screening corner of
that landscape: the agent reports a calibration-scored confidence rather than manipulating
features, and the principal’s gating rule plays the role of the strategic-classifier’s decision

2.6 Prediction Markets and Proper Scoring
Prediction markets use proper scoring rules to incentivize truthful belief reporting from par-
ticipants with private information (Hanson, 2003; Chen and Pennock, 2010). The tension
between proper scoring and strategic incentives in these markets is well documented: par-
ticipants who hold private information and seek to maximize payoffs face a tension between
honest reporting and strategic behavior that mirrors the trilemma’s core conflict (Witkowski
and Parkes, 2012). Our CGDP formalizes this tension for autonomous agents rather than
human forecasters: the principal’s approval gating plays the role of the market mechanism,
the agent’s private success probability plays the role of private information, and the scoring
rule plays the same role in both settings.
2.7 Adjustable Autonomy
The adjustable autonomy literature (Dorais et al., 1999; Bradshaw et al., 2003; Scerri et al.,
2002; Parasuraman et al., 2000) studies dynamic allocation of decision authority. Recent
agentic systems (Nakano et al., 2021) implement de facto confidence gating by requiring the
agent to decide when to request human assistance. Our formulation captures the binary
approve/reject decision via the approval function q(r) ∈[0, 1] (Definition 1), the simplest
structure that yields the impossibility; richer measures would reveal additional nuance with-
out changing the fundamental result.

Remark 8 (Revelation principle) The revelation principle (Myerson, 1981) does not
collapse the CGDP to a truthful direct-revelation mechanism, because the designer’s in-

composed with a strictly proper scoring rule S, not to an arbitrary reward function. Within
this instrument space, Proposition 40 (appendix) shows any non-constant perturbation of
S destroys strict properness, so no non-affine q admits a direct-revelation implementation
making r = p a best response; affine q preserves properness but gives no meaningful safety
gate (Assumption 16). The impossibility is therefore genuine rather than an artifact of not
optimising over the full unconstrained reward space (Theorem 27).
Remark 9 (Reduced-form interpretation) The CGDP is a reduced-form model of the
incentive structure facing the training process, not a literal description of inference-time
cognition. The agent does not literally observe p(c, a) and strategically choose r ̸= p; rather,
the training objective creates a composite payoff landscape whose optimum is shifted away
from the calibrated report. The “private information” p and “strategic report” r are mod-

any optimization process ascending the perturbed surface converges to the shifted optimum
regardless of whether the agent has explicit access to p. The core impossibility results (Theo-
rems 26–27) depend only on the report-space payoff structure, not on the sequential wrapper
or the private-information interpretation.

4 Resolution Pathways
The trilemma is not a dead end. We identify two constructive resolution pathways (com-
mitment via feasibility maps, domain separation via a critic) that restore two of the three
objectives at bounded cost to the third (Table 2). These parallel mechanism-design resolu-

unified language for when these pathways can and cannot restore the objectives the trilemma
jointly negates.

5 Where Do Existing Methods Sit?
Scope note. The CGDP formalization and Theorems 25–27 are deployment-time results:
they characterize what a trained agent can achieve under oversight, regardless of how it
was trained.
The method mapping below extends these results to training-time claims
under the additional assumption that the training process (RLHF, DPO, etc.) converges
to an approximate payoff maximizer. This assumption is empirically supported (Schulman
et al., 2017; Rafailov et al., 2023) but not formally proved for all training regimes. Claims
about specific training methods in this section are therefore structural arguments about the
training objective’s incentive landscape, not formal theorems about convergence.
The CGDP framework provides a unified lens for classifying RL and alignment methods
by their composite payoff structure. We express each method’s training objective in the
template V (r) = wC · S(r, p) + wA · h(r) and determine whether the trilemma applies.
5.1 The Goodhart-to-Trilemma Transition
Current RLHF/DPO methods avoid the trilemma by not attempting calibration. Their
overconfidence is a Goodhart effect: the reward model is a proxy for human preferences,
and optimizing this proxy leads to confident-sounding outputs because human raters prefer
them. This is a training artifact, not a structural impossibility.
The trilemma becomes relevant precisely when the system design includes a calibration
objective. The transition occurs at the moment a proper scoring rule enters the training
(or evaluation) loop:

## Page 20


Lov´en
Regime 1 (Goodhart, no scoring rule): The agent’s payoff is V = h(r) with h non-
affine and no proper scoring rule component. Overconfidence is unbounded (limited only
by the reward model’s saturation). Resolution: improve the reward model, add calibration
data.
Regime 2 (Trilemma, with scoring rule): The agent’s payoff is V = wC · S(r, p) +
wA · h(r) with S strictly proper and h non-affine. Overconfidence is bounded (Lemma 22)
but structurally unavoidable. Resolution requires architectural changes (commitment, sep-
aration), not better training data.
The field is moving from Regime 1 to Regime 2: the confidence-reporting deployments
of §1 (the coding-agent, medical-AI, and autonomous-vehicle examples) report confidence
and are evaluated against ground truth, so they are CGDPs and the trilemma applies.
5.2 Methods Without Calibration: RL, RLHF, DPO
Plain RL (V = R(s, a), with wC = 0 and q ≡1 in the CGDP decomposition) escapes the
trilemma trivially: with no confidence report there is nothing to inflate. The remaining
methods in this class carry a non-affine autonomy incentive but no proper-scoring counter-
term.

7.9 Statistical Methodology
The primary analysis fits a mixed-effects model to the Brier score with N, wA/wC, and rmin
as fixed effects and task and seed as crossed random effects, so that per-task difficulty and
per-seed sampling variation are absorbed rather than confounded with the weight manipu-
lation. All five hypothesis tests are corrected for multiple comparisons by the Bonferroni–
Holm procedure across the family of five, and every pairwise contrast is reported with a
Cohen’s d effect size so that statistical significance is never presented without an accompany-
ing magnitude. Interval estimates are chosen by metric type rather than applied uniformly:
mean-difference and effect-size quantities use bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (10,000

7.10 Controls and Threats to Validity
Three controls isolate the gating mechanism from confounds. The first sets wA = 0 — a pure
calibration objective with no gating reward — under which selection should improve rather
than degrade calibration; this is the formal content of H6 in Table 4, and a failure here
would indicate that Best-of-N selection is itself a source of miscalibration independent of
the gate. The second sets N = 1 (no selection), establishing the base model’s unperturbed
calibration as the reference point against which all selection effects are measured.
The
third replaces composite-score selection with uniform random selection among the N com-
pletions: this preserves the sampling distribution while removing the optimization pressure,
so any systematic inflation that survives it cannot be attributed to the payoff-maximising
mechanism the theorems analyse. Together the three controls bracket the mechanism —
no incentive (Control 1), no selection (Control 2), and selection without the incentive gra-
dient (Control 3) — so that an inflation effect present only in the full composite-selection
condition is attributable to the gate.
Three threats to validity remain.
• Construct validity: A model’s stated confidence may not reflect an internal proba-
bility estimate. We use token-level log-probabilities ((13)) as the primary confidence
metric precisely because they are the finest-grained internal signal the model exposes;
a verbalised-confidence alternative (the model is asked to state its own confidence in
prose) is run as a robustness check. On a held-out task subset, the Spearman rank
correlation between the two metrics at the completion level is ρ ≈0.6, and the five-
hypothesis decisions (H1, H2, H4, H5, H6) are qualitatively identical under either met-
ric (signs, significance, and relative magnitudes match). The logprob metric produces
smaller absolute effect sizes than the verbalised alternative but is more sensitive to
within-completion variance. Two caveats are worth noting. First, the geometric-mean
logprob averages over all tokens in the completion (including format and reasoning
tokens), which dilutes the answer-specific confidence signal; a more targeted per-token
measure focused on the answer span is future work. Second, the τ = 0.8 softmax tem-
perature attenuates the probability magnitudes; a cross-temperature study is needed
to confirm the hypothesis decisions are temperature-robust.
• External validity: Results on Qwen-2.5-7B may not generalize to other architectures or
to larger scales. Theorem 37 predicts that the mechanism is architecture-independent,
but empirical verification on additional open-weights models is left as future work. The
experimental protocol and hypothesis specifications are stated in a model-agnostic
form so that a replication can fix them in advance.

8 Discussion
8.1 Implications for Alignment Training
RLHF with a calibration term targets an infeasible point. Standard RLHF (Ouyang
et al., 2022) optimises helpfulness against a reward model trained on human preferences and,
increasingly, folds honesty or calibration into the same composite signal. The trilemma
proves that if such a signal is used, one of helpfulness, calibration, and autonomy must
be architecturally enforced rather than jointly optimised: the composite reward creates
incentive to appear calibrated rather than be calibrated. Standard RLHF without an explicit
33

calibration term sidesteps the trilemma — the overconfidence it produces is Goodhart, not
trilemma — but only because it leaves calibration unmeasured.
The H+A corner dominates — conditional on Assumption 20.
RLHF re-
ward models weight helpfulness and fluency; human raters prefer agents that attempt to
help over agents that express uncertainty (Sharma et al., 2024; Perez et al., 2023). This
creates systematic pressure toward the sycophancy pattern, which the trilemma identifies
as a rational response to the incentive structure rather than a training deficiency.
The
strength of this conclusion depends on Assumption 20 (positive helpfulness–miscalibration
correlation on binding states): if human raters were calibration-preferring on average, the
trilemma regime would not apply. Recent longitudinal evidence supports the assumption’s
direction: in a preregistered study (N = 3,075, with a three-week census-representative
field component), a majority of users came to prefer sycophantic AI because it made them
feel understood rather than for the quality of its advice (Ibrahim et al., 2026) — a re-
vealed preference for affirmation over accuracy consistent with a positive population-level
helpfulness–miscalibration covariance. Recent alignment variants that use honesty-aware
reward models or explicit calibration constraints (Dai et al., 2024; Bai et al., 2022) move in-
side the trilemma’s regime, where the impossibility applies; standard preference-only RLHF
stays outside it, where Goodhart does.
KL-constrained RL and iterative RLHF. KL-penalised policy-gradient methods
(PPO, TRPO) anchor the policy to a reference πref via DKL(π∥πref). Two regimes must be
distinguished:
• Fixed-reference regime. With πref held fixed throughout training, KL-penalised RL
has a well-known closed-form optimum π∗(a | s) ∝πref(a | s) exp(R(s, a)/β), which
bounds the displacement from πref as a function of the KL coefficient β: a larger β
yields smaller displacement, and in the limit β →∞the policy stays at πref. If πref
is itself calibrated (e.g., a pre-trained base model before RLHF), a sufficiently strong
KL anchor can keep the policy within a bounded-calibration-loss neighbourhood of
the calibrated reference. The KL coefficient acts as a soft commitment device in the
sense of §4, and the trilemma’s trajectory toward the shifted optimum is bounded, not
merely slowed, as a function of β.
• Iterative / moving-reference regime. In iterative RLHF and online DPO-style meth-
ods, πref is updated between rounds (e.g., by replacing it with the current policy).
The fixed-policy analysis of Lemma 22 is then an approximation, and the composite
iteration is a two-timescale stochastic process. Here “slows but does not prevent”
is the correct description: the Perturbation Lemma’s shifted optimum is no longer
a fixed target, and each reference update shifts the feasible set toward the previ-
ous round’s displacement. A full two-timescale analysis (Borkar, 1997) or mean-field
treatment (Lasry and Lions, 2007) of this setting is open.
The empirical effect depends on the regime: fixed-reference PPO with large β stays near
the calibrated reference; iterative RLHF cumulates the drift across rounds.
Context-dependent Pareto selection. Different deployment contexts require dif-
ferent Pareto points. Local development environments tolerate the H+A corner (low blast
radius). Shared infrastructure requires the H+C corner. Safety-critical systems may require
the C+A corner.

8.2 The Formal Model and Gradient-Trained LLMs
The Perturbation Lemma models the agent as an expected-utility maximizer with a separa-
ble type θ and report r. Three points clarify where this analogy holds for gradient-trained
LLMs.
Point 1: The individual-rationality gap. A gradient-trained LLM does not ex-
plicitly compute p∗, form r, and decide whether to inflate. The strategic-inflation model
assumes a well-defined internal θ that diverges from the report, while LLMs may have
diffuse, context-dependent internal representations.
Point 2: Population-level equivalence via gradient selection. Despite this gap,
the RLHF/PPO training loop creates the same aggregate incentive structure at the popula-
tion level. The policy gradient increases the probability of report values that receive higher
composite reward. On binding states, reports just above rmin receive the highest composite
reward. Over many gradient steps, this selection pressure drives the policy toward inflation
as an emergent property.
Point 3:
Empirical confirmation.
The trilemma’s predictions (systematic over-
confidence, sycophancy under approval pressure, miscalibration after RLHF training) are
independently confirmed: Perez et al. (2023) document sycophancy, Sharma et al. (2024)
trace it to reward model preferences, Tian et al. (2023) show RLHF fine-tuning degrades
calibration concentrated in the uncertain regime, while Kadavath et al. (2022) demonstrate
that language models possess internal uncertainty representations that are correlated with
correctness. Beyond model-internal evidence, Ibrahim et al. (2026) provide longitudinal
field evidence that affirmation-seeking is a stable, revealed user preference with measurable
downstream cost, indicating the incentive structure operates in deployment and not only
on static benchmarks.
8.3 Connection to Bayesian Persuasion
The oversight setting sits inside the information-design family but is structurally dual to
Bayesian persuasion rather than an instance of it. In Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011) the
uninformed sender commits to a signal structure to induce the receiver’s preferred action.
In the CGDP the roles invert: the agent observes its own competence p and is the informed
party, while the uninformed principal commits to the approval rule q(r) and chooses the
report-to-decision mapping. The CGDP therefore belongs to the delegation tradition (Holm-
str¨om, 1984), in which an uninformed principal commits to a decision rule over reports from
an informed agent — exactly the role played by q here — and differs from cheap talk by
scoring the report against ground truth and anchoring the gate to a single threshold. The
optimizer-independence principle (Theorem 37) extends this reading: because the Pertur-
bation Lemma concerns the objective landscape rather than the agent’s cognitive process,
the delegation interpretation holds whether the agent is a strategic Bayesian updater or a
gradient-trained network.

8.4 Beyond Reinforcement Learning: Generality of the Trilemma
The method mapping of Section 5 covers RL, RLHF, DPO, and related alignment methods.
However, the Optimizer Independence theorem (Theorem 37) implies a stronger claim: the
trilemma is a property of the payoff surface, not the learning algorithm. Any ML system
whose training or inference-time objective instantiates the four CGDP ingredients (private
competence information, a self-reported confidence or score, a non-affine gating function
conditioned on that report, and a proper scoring rule evaluating accuracy) falls within the
trilemma’s scope. We survey the landscape, distinguishing direct instances from structural
analogues.
Table 5 maps six further settings onto the four CGDP ingredients. The first four are
direct instances (a confidence-or-quality report gated against a threshold under a proper-
scoring evaluation); the last two are structural analogues in which the role mapping is
inverted or the report is a contribution score rather than a confidence. Two effect-direction
claims are non-obvious and worth isolating: in selective prediction this yields a formal
explanation for the empirically observed overconfidence of selective classifiers near their
rejection boundary, with inflation magnitude scaling as wA/(2wC) (Lemma 22); and in
active learning the gating favours high reported uncertainty, so the Perturbation Lemma
applies with the inflation sign reversed, predicting systematic under-reporting of uncertainty
when querying is penalised. In the federated-learning instance the trilemma predicts that
no contribution-scoring mechanism can simultaneously ensure truthful quality reporting,
full participation, and helpful contributions from all agents.
Where the trilemma does not apply.
The trilemma requires all four CGDP ingredi-
ents; removing any one breaks the structure. Pure supervised learning (no gating mecha-
nism), plain RL without confidence reporting (no self-report), generative adversarial net-
works (the discriminator evaluates quality, not self-reported confidence), and self-supervised
learning (no principal-agent structure) all lack at least one ingredient. In these settings,
miscalibration may occur through other mechanisms (e.g., Goodhart effects, distribution
shift) but not through the specific incentive conflict the trilemma identifies.
Unifying principle.
The common thread is that the trilemma applies to any ML system
where a learned agent’s autonomy is mediated by its self-reported confidence under a proper
scoring evaluation. As AI systems increasingly operate with human-in-the-loop oversight
(selective prediction in clinical decision support, confidence-gated autonomous driving, tool-
use decisions in agentic systems), this structure becomes the norm rather than the exception.
The Optimizer Independence theorem ensures that the specific learning algorithm (gradient
descent, evolutionary strategy, Bayesian optimization, or in-context learning) is irrelevant:
any process ascending the perturbed payoff surface converges to the shifted, miscalibrated
optimum. The trilemma is not an RL result that happens to generalize; it is a payoff-
geometry result that RL happens to instantiate.

8.6 The “One Must Be External” Principle
The Commitment Resolution (Theorem 31) proves that the third objective must be archi-
tecturally enforced. This is an instance of the general principle from regulatory economics
that when an agent has conflicting roles, separation of roles can restore incentive compati-
bility (Tirole, 1986; Laffont and Tirole, 1993):
• Train H + C, enforce A via approval architecture (gating layer outside the model).
• Train H + A, enforce C via external evaluator (critic model, separate from actor).
• Train C + A, enforce H via domain-specific knowledge injection.

9 Conclusion
We have established the Behavioral Credibility Trilemma: no RL policy with confidence-
gated autonomy can simultaneously maximize helpfulness, calibration, and autonomy under
rational oversight when tasks involve genuine uncertainty and the principal enforces a mean-
ingful safety standard. The impossibility is a geometric property of the composite payoff
surface in the Confidence-Gated Decision Problem: adding any non-affine autonomy incen-
tive to a strictly proper scoring rule destroys strict properness. The Behavioral Perturbation
Lemma quantifies the inflation magnitude, and the Optimal Oversight Non-Affinity theo-
rem makes the impossibility unconditional. The optimizer-independence proof, built on the
covariance inequality for monotone functions under log-concave measures, shows the result
holds for any expected-improvement optimization process within the log-concave-density
policy family (Theorem 37).
We have located the impossibility precisely:
standard RL and RLHF avoid the
trilemma by not attempting calibration (their overconfidence is Goodhart, not trilemma);
the trilemma becomes relevant when the system design includes a calibration objective,
which is where the field is heading. The 540-configuration Best-of-N experiment of Sec-
tion 7 confirms all five pre-specified hypotheses and adds a descriptive analysis of the
achievable-surface geometry (full result line in §7). The protocol uses Best-of-N selection
as a controlled arg max over the composite payoff, isolating the mechanism the theorems
analyze without the confound of prompting or fine-tuning. Two constructive resolution
pathways (commitment, separation) restore two of the three objectives at bounded cost,
each architecturally enforcing the third.
The broader point is that calibrated autonomy is not a single-objective optimization but
a multi-objective constrained optimization where the objectives are provably incompatible.
Making the trade-off selection explicit is better than pretending it does not exist.
