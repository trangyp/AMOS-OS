Abstract
Test-time scaling (TTS) methods have proven highly effective for LLMs, yet their application
to vision-language models (VLMs) remains relatively underexplored. Existing VLM TTS methods
largely require open-weight model access or expensive repeated sampling, and are evaluated
primarily on multimodal mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks rather than general
visual understanding tasks. In this paper, we propose Test-Time Hinting, a method that improves
VLM performance via a single VLM call and requiring only black-box API access, which makes it
broadly applicable to frontier closed-weight models. Our method is motivated by the observation
that VLM errors tend to cluster around recurring failure patterns. We therefore train a lightweight
hint generator model to predict, for a given test input, which “hint” should be prepended to the
prompt, providing targeted contextual or procedural guidance that steers the VLM away from its
characteristic failure modes. We show that Test-Time Hinting improves the accuracy of multiple
closed-weight VLMs on natural-image VQA benchmarks and that these gains generalize to unseen
benchmarks and VLMs without retraining the hint generator.

Introduction
Test-time scaling (TTS) offers an alternative to the conventional strategy of scaling model size or
training data; it instead directs additional compute resources to improve the accuracy of a frozen
model at inference time. A wide range of TTS methods, from chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al.
(2023)) and self-consistency (Wang et al. (2023)) to process reward models (Lightman et al. (2023))
and optimal compute allocation (Snell et al. (2024)), have shown that performance continues to grow
(often significantly) with inference-time compute. However, TTS research has concentrated primarily
on LLMs; in this paper, we develop TTS methods tailored to vision-language models (VLMs).
Existing TTS approaches for VLMs have two limitations that restrict their practical application.
First, the most effective TTS strategies, such as reward-model-guided search, iterative sampling,
and process supervision, require open access to model weights or internal representations, which
excludes frontier closed-weight VLMs accessible only through APIs (Zhang et al. (2024b); Xu et al.
(2025)). Second, even methods that operate through the model interface rely on repeated sampling or
multi-pass inference through the same target model, which increases the per-query cost at inference
time (Madaan et al. (2023); Wang et al. (2023)). Additionally, the evaluation of these methods has
focused primarily on mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks, leaving more conventional
tasks such as general natural-image understanding comparatively unexplored.

In this paper, we develop a TTS method for VLMs that requires only black-box API access, a single
model call, and is applicable to general visual understanding tasks. The method applies to any open-
or closed-weight VLM, but is especially valuable in deployment settings where a frontier model is used
in a domain-specific application and further improvement using proprietary data or evaluations is
needed. Our method is motivated by an empirical finding that VLM errors cluster around a small set of
recurring failure modes that depend primarily on the input rather than the model, and that are
correlated across distinct VLMs. These regularities imply that the structure of VLM errors is learnable
and can be anticipated from the image and text inputs alone, before the model responds.
We call our method Test-Time Hinting (TTH). TTH trains a (lightweight) hint generator to
produce, for a given image–text pair, a short natural-language hint that is prepended to the target
VLM’s prompt before it responds; the hint provides contextual guidance that steers the model away
from the failure mode it is most likely to exhibit on that input. To construct training data, we search
for hints that, when prepended to the prompt, cause the target model to correct its previously incorrect
response. Because failure modes recur systematically, the generator learns to anticipate them from the
input alone. At inference time, the hint generator processes a new image–text pair, produces a hint,
and passes it along with the original input to the black-box VLM in a single call (Figure 1).
To our knowledge, TTH is the first single-call method for improving the performance of general-
purpose, black-box VLMs at inference time. Specifically, our contributions are:
1. We propose TTH, a preemptive single-call TTS method for black-box VLMs, in which a
lightweight hint generator prepends input-conditioned, failure-mode-anticipatory guidance to
the target VLM’s prompt before it answers.
2. We develop a two-stage training recipe for the hint generator that combines agentic-search
distillation with multi-target reinforcement learning, and show that it effectively improves the
accuracy of multiple frontier target VLMs.
3. We demonstrate that the efficacy of the hint generator transfers zero-shot, with no retraining,
to five unseen target VLMs and three unseen natural-image VQA benchmarks.
We begin with an analysis of VLM failure patterns in Section 2, which motivates our TTH
approach.

Dissecting Failure Modes in Vision-Language Models
One might expect that generating input-specific guidance toward the correct answer should be as hard
as answering the question itself. We show that this is not the case; frontier VLMs exhibit a small set of
recurring “failure modes” that are largely shared across models and predictable from the input alone, a
structure that a simpler VLM can learn to anticipate for new multimodal inputs.
To establish this, we evaluate three proprietary models from different families: GPT-5 Nano
(OpenAI), Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite (Google), and Claude 4.5 Haiku (Anthropic), on the A-OKVQA vi-
sual question answering benchmark (17,056 questions) (Schwenk et al. (2022)). Each model is queried
three times per question with temperature 0 and top-p=1; a question is classified as incorrect for a
given model only if all three trials produce the same incorrect answer. We ask two questions: do failure
modes overlap across models, and is the failure mode predictable from the input alone?
Cross-Model Failure Mode Overlap. We define a taxonomy of 12 failure modes in VLMs:
recognition, attribute binding, counting, spatial relation, optical character recognition, charts and
tables, math/quantitative, knowledge, logic/negation, hallucination, instruction format, and “other”
(full definitions in Section B.1). We use this taxonomy to categorize all error instances from various
VLMs in the A-OKVQA dataset; each incorrect response is labeled by an independent GPT-5 model
with access to the image, question, ground-truth answer with rationale, and the model’s incorrect
response along with its reasoning.
Conditioned on shared errors among pairs of VLMs (i.e., questions both models answer incorrectly),
we find that failure mode agreement is as high as 71–76%; that is, when a pair of VLMs both fail,
they tend to fail for the same reason (Figure 2). This observation suggests that a hint targeting the
anticipated failure mode for a given input could steer multiple models away from this common error.

projects the 3584-dim representation through two fully connected layers (3584 →256 →64),

failure mode of the VLM on a given input and prepends a targeted natural language hint to avert it.

Our Test-Time Hinting approach (TTH) involves three steps (Figure 3): (1) sampling base responses
from each target VLM, (2) eliciting per-question hints through an agentic loop, and (3) distilling these
hints into Gθ via supervised fine-tuning followed by a reinforcement-learning (RL) refinement stage.
3.1
Step 1: Base-Response Sampling
We run each target VLM on every training question with three independent passes at temperature 0
and top-p=1. Each question-model pair is classified as base-correct (correct on all three trials), base-
incorrect (incorrect on all three trials with a valid parse), or mixed (otherwise); only base-correct and
base-incorrect pairs are then used to train the hint generator in the following steps.

3.2
Step 2: Agentic Hint Generation and Optimization
Given an input (x, q) for which the VLM M produces an incorrect answer ˆa = M(x, q) ̸= a∗, we con-
struct a hint h such that prepending it to the same image-question pair yields the correct answer, i.e.,
M(x, [h; q]) = a∗. To generate such hints, we design an iterative agentic pipeline comprising three
roles, a Proposer, an Editor, and a Verifier, detailed below (pseudocode provided in the Appendix).
Our agentic process handles two hint types: repair hints for base-incorrect questions, where it seeks
a hint that flips the target to the correct answer, and reinforcement hints for base-correct questions,
where it seeks a hint that retains the correct answer while keeping attention on the relevant visual
evidence. A proposer P (GPT-5) with full privileged context (x, q, a∗, r∗, ˆa, ˆr) proposes a candidate
hint; an editor E (GPT-5) modifies and revises the hint to enforce certain criteria (no direct leakage
of a∗, at least one contrastive item, no certainty language, avoiding extraneous reasoning steps); and
the target VLM M then acts as the verifier by checking that the answer ˆa corresponding to (x, [h; q])
matches a∗. The loop runs for up to Rmax=3 rounds. For repair questions it terminates on the first
correct re-answer and otherwise retains the last editor-approved hint as an unsuccessful repair target,
which is excluded from downstream phases. For reinforcement hints, the system likewise terminates
on the first correct re-answer, and trials in which every round disrupts the originally correct answer
are discarded entirely. Full prompts for all three agents are provided in the Appendix.
3.3
Step 3: Hint Generator Training
In Step 3, we train a lightweight model Gθ to generate the hints h elicited in Step 2 conditioned on the
triplet (x, q, m) (i.e., the only information available at inference time). We initialize Gθ from Qwen3-
VL-4B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2025) and attach LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2021) to the language-model
backbone, the vision-language merger, and the last two blocks of the vision encoder; the full set of
modules and training hyperparameters for both stages, including LoRA rank, learning rates, and
optimization schedule, is provided in the Appendix. Training proceeds in two stages: supervised
fine-tuning using the hints generated in Step 2, followed by a reinforcement learning step that directly
optimizes the actual downstream behavior of the hint on the target VLMs (i.e., whether it flips
incorrect answers). We show in the Appendix how both stages empirically contribute to Gθ.
Stage 1: Supervised fine-tuning on agentic hints. Each training instance is a tuple (x, q, m, h∗),
where m is the natural-language target identifier and h∗is a hint produced in Step 2. We retain only
Step 2 hint generations that lead to correct answers: the hint either flipped a base error to a correct
answer (a successful repair) or preserved an already-correct answer (a successful reinforcement).
Trials in which the verifier never reached a correct answer are discarded; their unsuccessful-repair
hints would supervise the generator on guidance whose efficacy was never verified end-to-end, and
Stage 2 picks up these harder cases by directly observing downstream behavior. The remaining pool of
hints is balanced by target identifier via median-count resampling so that no single target dominates
updates, and we minimize next-token cross-entropy on the hint JSON.
Stage 2: Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Stage 1 supervises the generator to
imitate hints that worked in Step 2, but it does not directly optimize the generator’s own samples for
downstream effect on the target VLM. Stage 2 closes this gap. For each training prompt (x, q, m) the
policy samples a small group of candidate hints. Each candidate is sent to all three target VLMs, and
we compare each target’s hinted answer to its base (unhinted) answer, classifying the outcome as one of:
repair (a previous error becomes correct), no-op (a correct answer is preserved), harm (a correct answer
becomes wrong), or unrepaired (a previous error remains wrong). These outcomes are mapped to a

Related Work
Test-time scaling. Test-time scaling methods treat inference compute as a resource to optimize
rather than a fixed cost. For LLMs, gains have been achieved through chain-of-thought prompting
(Wei et al., 2023), self-consistency (Wang et al., 2023), process reward models (Lightman et al., 2023),
and best-of-N sampling with learned verifiers (Snell et al., 2024). Analogous efforts for VLMs include
iterative chain-of-thought reasoning (Zhang et al., 2024b; Xu et al., 2025) and reward-model-guided
search. Most VLM TTS methods, however, either require gradient or weight access to the model
being improved, which excludes frontier closed-weight models; other methods rely on repeated
sampling that multiplies per-query cost. Evaluation of these methods has focused on mathematical
and scientific reasoning, and less on general natural-image understanding. Our method addresses
these limitations as it requires only black-box prompt access with a single API call, and is evaluated
on natural-image VQA benchmarks spanning diverse image domains and question styles.
Post-hoc correction and critique. A prominent family of TTS methods improves accuracy through
repeated inference, i.e., generating a response, critiquing it, and optionally revising. The critique signal
varies across approaches, ranging from the target model itself (Madaan et al., 2023; Shinn et al., 2023)
to multi-agent debate (Du et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2024) or LLM-as-judge routing through a separate
model (Zheng et al., 2023), and to dedicated critique models trained for the task (Zhang et al., 2025;
Xie et al., 2025). Across all post-response methods, at least two inference passes through the target
are required, with external-judge approaches adding a third call, incurring meaningful cost at frontier
API pricing. Test-time hinting replaces these additional calls with a single pass through a compact
hint generator, thereby achieving comparable or superior accuracy improvement at single-pass cost.
Other prompting strategies. A complementary class of methods augments the prompt before the
model answers. Chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2023) with its visual extensions (Shao et al.,
2024a; Lu et al., 2022) and Tree-of-Thought (Yao et al., 2023) are task-generic and do not condition on
the failure mode a given input is likely to trigger. Prophet (Yu et al., 2025) moves toward input-
specific prompting by feeding candidate answers and confidence scores from a small VQA model to a
larger one, but its heuristics are not anticipatory of the target’s likely errors and provide candidate
answers regardless of whether the target is likely to fail. LEAP (Zhang et al., 2024a) mines mistakes
on few-shot examples to derive benchmark-level principles, but the resulting summaries are difficult to
apply to individual questions when benchmark identity is unknown. TTH differs in that its guidance is
conditioned on the specific image and question of each instance, with anticipation of the likely
failure mode emerging implicitly from the training signal. Importantly, CoT, Prophet, and TTH are
complementary: hints can be prepended to any prompting strategy without architectural changes.

5.1
Experimental Setup
Datasets. Our primary development and evaluation benchmark is A-OKVQA (Schwenk et al.,
2022), a knowledge-based VQA dataset with ground-truth rationales (Apache License 2.0). Because

its official test labels are not public, we use the validation split of 1,145 questions as our held-out
test set; all model development is performed on the 17,056-question training split, with 855 questions
reserved as an internal validation set for hyperparameter selection and early stopping. The test set is
used only for final model evaluation. To test whether Gθ learns general failure-anticipation strategies
rather than dataset-specific patterns, we additionally evaluate zero-shot transfer (no retraining or
domain adaptation) on three additional natural-image benchmarks: VCR (Zellers et al., 2019) (5,000
unique image-question pairs sampled from the validation set; AI2 custom research-only license),
Visual7W (Zhu et al., 2016) (5,000 sampled test pairs; MIT License), and RealWorldQA (xAI,
2024a,b) (628 four-way MCQ / yes-no / left-right / color questions retained from the original 768
for consistent MCQ-style evaluation; CC BY-ND 4.0 License).
Target models. Our primary target models are three proprietary frontier VLMs from different
families: Claude 4.5 Haiku (Anthropic), Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite (Google), and GPT-5 Nano (OpenAI).
The hint generator is trained using data from these three models and evaluated on them directly
(Section 5.2). To test cross-model generalization, we additionally evaluate TTH on five unseen target
models without retraining (Section 5.3). For same-family transfer, we apply hints generated for each
training model to a larger model from the same family: GPT-5 Nano hints are reused for GPT-5.2,
Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite hints for Gemini 2.5 Flash, and Claude 4.5 Haiku hints for Claude 4.5 Opus.
For different-family transfer (Grok 4 Fast Reasoning and Nova Pro), we apply the Gemini 2.5 Flash
Lite hints directly. All target models are evaluated using their default API hyperparameters; exact
prompts for all methods and target models are reproduced in the supplementary material.
Baselines. We compare TTH (1 target call) with: (i) Base (1 call): direct prompting with the ques-
tion and image; (ii) CoT (Wei et al., 2023) (1 call): the base prompt augmented with “Think step-by-
step. Then output ONLY the JSON object”; (iii) Self-refine (Madaan et al., 2023) (2 calls): the target
answers, then is shown its own response and asked to critique and optionally correct it; (iv) External
judge (Du et al., 2023) (2 target calls + 1 judge call): a more capable model from the same family cri-
tiques the target’s answer, and the target re-answers accordingly. Judges are GPT-5-Mini for GPT-5
Nano, Gemini 2.5 Flash for Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, and Claude 4.5 Sonnet for Claude 4.5 Haiku.
Metrics. We report three metrics. Overall accuracy is the fraction of test questions answered correctly.
The repair rate, Pr[ˆah =a∗| ˆa̸=a∗], restricts to the questions the base model answered incorrectly
and reports what fraction each method corrects. The harm rate, Pr[ˆah ̸=a∗| ˆa=a∗] (used in the
ablation in Section 5.5), restricts to base-correct questions and reports the fraction that flip to
incorrect after applying each method. Repair rate and harm rate are the two complementary axes
along which a hinting method’s deployment value is most directly measured: a useful method must
both correct previous errors and avoid disrupting answers the target already had right.

Zero-Shot Transfer to Unseen Target Models
A core design goal of TTH is that hints optimized for a set of training models should generalize to
unseen targets. Table 4 evaluates this on the A-OKVQA test set using the same checkpoint, without
any retraining or adaptation. TTH achieves both the best overall accuracy and the best repair rate

multiples of the next-best baseline. The same-family setting shows only modest absolute accuracy
gains because the larger family members already operate near ceiling at base, but the relative
repair-rate advantage over baselines is in fact larger than on the smaller targets seen during training.
The contrast suggests that what the hint generator has internalized are failure-anticipation strategies
tied to the visual-linguistic structure of inputs, rather than model-family-specific surface patterns.

6
Discussion
Deployment use case and inference cost. TTH is designed for settings where a downstream
consumer of frontier VLMs observes systematic in-domain failure patterns but cannot modify the
model, retrain it, or afford the latency and cost of multi-pass correction at scale. The total inference
overhead is one small hint generator call plus a single target call, the same budget as CoT, compared
with two target calls for self-refine and three (two target plus one judge) for the external-judge
baseline. For proprietary VLMs billed per token, this represents a meaningful cost reduction.
When post-hoc critique remains competitive and the role of judge capability. The one
situation where the external judge wins overall accuracy is GPT-5 Nano paired with GPT-5-Mini
(A-OKVQA, RealWorldQA, and Visual7W). Since self-refine provides no comparable advantage on
the same target, the gap reflects the capability asymmetry between GPT-5 Nano and the much
stronger judge, not the preemptive vs. post-hoc distinction. This advantage also comes at the highest
inference cost of any method we consider: the external-judge pipeline requires three API calls per
query (two target plus one judge), compared with one target call plus one small hint generator
call for TTH. The pattern reverses for GPT-5.2 (Table 4), whose judge (GPT-5.2-Pro) is closer in
capability: TTH outperforms all baselines including the external judge. TTH’s relative advantage
over post-hoc critique therefore grows as the target-judge capability gap narrows, a regime that
becomes increasingly common as frontier models converge.
Future directions. The aim of this paper is to introduce TTH as a single-call test-time scaling
concept and to evaluate it in a clean and unambiguous setting; we have therefore deliberately
confined the present study to multiple-choice VQA over three frontier API targets. Two natural
11


## Page 12


extensions follow from this scope. The error predictors of Section 2 suggest gating hint generation
by predicted failure risk, so that the hint pipeline only fires on inputs the target is likely to get
wrong without help, further lowering the average inference cost. A second direction is adapting the
framework beyond multiple choice to open-ended generative VQA, which will require rethinking both
the reward signal (which currently relies on exact-match correctness) and the no-leakage constraint
(which is straightforward in MCQ but subtler when answers are free-form text). Both extensions
preserve the central design of TTH, namely a small generator that produces input-conditioned hints
in a single call, and we leave them to future work.
7
Conclusion
We introduced Test-Time Hinting, a preemptive single-call test-time scaling method for black-box
VLMs. The method exploits an empirical regularity, namely that failure modes are recurring,
cross-model, and predictable from the input alone, to train a compact generator that prepends
failure-mode-aware guidance to the target’s prompt. A two-stage recipe combining distillation from
agentic search with reinforcement learning against the actual downstream behavior of each candidate
hint on multiple target VLMs yields gains on overall accuracy across our primary targets, and these
gains transfer zero-shot to unseen targets and unseen benchmarks.

Cross-Model Failure Structure: Full Results

B.1
Failure-Mode Taxonomy
The taxonomy F used throughout the paper consists of twelve failure modes, hand-picked by the
authors during a manual review of base-incorrect responses across the three primary target models.
The list is intended to cover the empirically frequent ways in which the targets fail on natural-image
VQA, and is used both for the cross-model analysis below and for the categorical-failure-mode-hint
baseline in Section 5.5.
• recognition. The target misidentifies the primary object, scene, or activity depicted in the image
(e.g., confusing a cat for a dog, a kitchen for a dining room).
• attribute binding. The target identifies the right entities but binds attributes (color, material,
state, role) to the wrong one.
15


## Page 16


• counting. The target’s numeric answer for the count of an object, person, or instance is incorrect.
• spatial relation. The target misjudges the spatial layout of entities (left/right, behind/in front,
above/below, near/far).
• OCR. The target misreads or fails to read text rendered inside the image (signs, captions,
packaging).
• chart/table. The target misinterprets the data structure of a chart, table, or schematic.
• math/quantitative. The target reaches an incorrect quantitative conclusion via arithmetic or
comparison error after correct perception.
• knowledge. The target’s perception is correct but it lacks or misapplies the world knowledge
required to answer.
• logic/negation. The target mishandles negation, conjunction, exclusion, or other propositional
structure in the question.
• hallucination. The target asserts the presence of entities, attributes, or events that are not in
the image.
• instruction format. The target’s reasoning is correct but its final answer fails to comply with
the requested output format.
• other. A residual category for failures that do not fit any of the above.
B.2
Single Model-Conditioned Generator vs. Per-Model Specialists
The cross-model overlap and failure-mode agreement reported above motivate a specific architectural
choice for the hint generator: rather than training one specialist generator per target VLM, we train
a single generator that takes the target identifier m as an input token. The motivation is twofold.
First, a substantial fraction of failures are shared across targets, both at the question level (Jaccard
overlap of 0.32 to 0.37) and at the failure-mode level (agreement of 71 to 76% on shared errors), so
a single model can learn the common failure-anticipation skill from data pooled across targets, with
target-specific adaptation handled through the conditioning token. Second, a shared-backbone risk
predictor (Table 1) performs comparably to per-target specialists, providing direct evidence that the
predictive structure underlying failure anticipation is largely shared. A single model-conditioned
generator also enables zero-shot transfer to unseen target identifiers at inference time, which is the
property exercised in Section 5.3.

C.5
Why Average the Reward Across Three Targets
A natural alternative to averaging the per-candidate reward across all three targets would be to score
each candidate against a single (e.g.,, the matching) target. We chose the multi-target averaged
reward for two reasons.
First, it acts as a variance-reduction mechanism for the advantage estimate. Within a GRPO
group, the advantage of each candidate is computed relative to the within-group mean reward.
Per-target rewards are noisy (a single target’s response to a hint depends on stochastic decoding
artifacts and on whether the hint happens to align with that target’s idiosyncratic style), and
averaging across three targets reduces this noise before the advantage is computed, yielding tighter
group-relative advantages and a more stable policy gradient.
Second, it acts as a regularizer that discourages the policy from chasing model-specific shortcuts.
A reward signal scored against a single target will, in the limit, encourage the policy to exploit any
prompt patterns that happen to flip that specific target without producing perceptual or procedural
guidance that would generalize. Averaging the reward across three architecturally distinct targets
penalizes such shortcuts directly: a candidate hint can only achieve a high reward if it shifts behavior
in the same direction across all three targets, which empirically requires guidance grounded in the
input itself rather than in target-specific quirks. This regularization is also what enables the zero-shot
transfer to unseen target VLMs reported in Section 5.3.
C.6
Why the Unrepaired Outcome Is Downweighted
The reward in Table 7 is asymmetric: the harm outcome (base correct, hinted wrong) receives −1,
while the unrepaired outcome (base wrong, hinted wrong) receives only −0.5. The natural-looking
choice would have been to keep the two penalties symmetric at −1, since both correspond to a wrong
hinted answer. We did not do this for the following reason.
A symmetric −1 penalty creates an over-commit pressure on the policy. On hard inputs where
every candidate hint is likely to leave the target wrong, a symmetric scheme treats all candidates
in a group as equally bad, and the policy receives a strong undifferentiated negative signal that,
propagated through the model, is most easily reduced by increasing the assertiveness of the hint,
since assertive hints are the ones that occasionally get a hard input correct. Empirically, this
assertiveness-inducing style does not stay localized to hard inputs: the same prompts and decoding
patterns spill over into nearby base-correct prompts and produce harm.
Downweighting the unrepaired outcome to −0.5 keeps the actual harm signal intact (the policy
is still penalized strongly when it disrupts a correct answer) while permitting cautious or no-op-style
hints when the input is genuinely ambiguous. We selected the −0.5 value via a small sweep on the
internal validation set; harm-rate behavior was consistent across nearby choices.
C.7
Mixing Base-Correct Prompts into the Stage 2 Pool
The Step 1 outputs are heavily skewed: roughly 71% of training prompts are answered correctly
by all three targets at base. Strictly excluding these would deprive the policy of exposure to the
no-op condition, which is exactly what the harm metric measures at evaluation. We therefore retain
a configurable share of all-base-correct prompts in the Stage 2 training pool. A small sweep over
shares of {30, 40, 50, 70}% identified 50% as the most balanced operating point: enough exposure to
