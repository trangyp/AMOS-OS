THE SYMMETRIC MAHLER INEQUALITY IN DIMENSION THREE
VIA ADMISSIBLE SHADOW SYSTEMS
SHIBING CHEN, YUANYUAN LI, DONGMENG XI, AND ZHE-FENG XU
Abstract. The three-dimensional symmetric Mahler inequality states that, for every
origin-symmetric convex body K = −K ⊂R3,
P(K) = |K| |K◦| ≥32
3 .
It was recently proved by Iriyeh–Shibata [8], and a shorter proof was later given by
Fradelizi–Hubard–Meyer–Roldán-Pensado–Zvavitch [5]. Both proofs combine ingenious
equipartition arguments of algebraic-topological origin with delicate geometric estimates
inspired by Meyer’s argument for unconditional bodies.
In this paper, we give a new proof of this inequality using a purely geometric approach,
based on what we call symmetric admissible shadow systems. This is a natural extension
of the new techniques developed in our proof of the three-dimensional non-symmetric
Mahler conjecture [4].
1. Introduction
The volume product is one of the basic affine invariants of a convex body. For a convex
body K ⊂Rn containing the origin in its interior, its polar body is
K◦= {y ∈Rn : x · y ≤1 for all x ∈K}.

Recently, the three-dimensional Mahler conjecture for non-symmetric convex bodies (3)

The shadow system method goes back to Rogers and Shephard [14]. For the volume
product, Campi and Gronchi [3] established a shadow-system theorem in the symmetric
setting, and Meyer and Reisner [12] proved a shadow-system theorem for the Santaló volume
product with a rigidity statement in the equality case.
In [4], the notions of admissible shadow systems and admissible speeds were introduced,
and several new tools were developed. The new admissible deformation allows us to pass
from one-point motions to coordinated shadow movements of vertices that preserve the face
lattice, and hence to treat general convex polytopes.
In the present paper, we further develop these ideas in the centrally symmetric setting and
apply them to the symmetric Mahler conjecture. In the symmetric version, the admissible
shadow system method must be rebuilt inside the class of centrally symmetric convex bodies:
admissible speeds are required to be symmetric and locally affine on the relevant faces, so
that the deformation preserves central symmetry, controls the face lattice, and keeps the
volume behavior rigid enough to force only trivial speeds at a minimizer. Finally, via a
symmetric admissible shadow system method, we give a new, purely geometric proof of the
three dimensional symmetric inequality (2):
Theorem 1.1 (New proof of the three-dimensional symmetric Mahler inequality). Every
origin-symmetric convex body K = −K ⊂R3 satisfies
|K| |K◦| ≥32

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 sets up the notations and recalls the basic
polarity, compactness, and shadow-system tools. Section 3 defines symmetric admissible
speeds and proves the local face-lattice stability and affine-volume property. Section 4
combines these deformations with the Meyer–Reisner theorem and with Santaló polarity
to show that a minimizer in a bounded symmetric vertex class cannot admit a non-trivial
admissible shadow system, nor can its polar body. Section 5 proves a three-dimensional

Definition 3.1 (Symmetric admissible speed). A symmetric speed vector α ∈RV is called
θ-admissible if the following condition holds for every facet F of P.
(1) If θ /∈lin(F −F), then there exists an affine function ℓF : aff(F) →R such that
ℓF (xi) = αi for every vertex xi of F.
(2) If θ ∈lin(F −F), no constraint is imposed on the values αi on the vertices of F.
In the parallel case, each moved vertex of F stays in the original facet plane because all
displacements are in the two-dimensional vector space lin(F −F); this is why no planarity
constraint is needed for such a facet. When the direction θ is fixed or clear from the context,
we simply say that α is symmetric admissible.

Definition 3.2 (Trivial speeds). A symmetric speed is called trivial if there exists w ∈R3

and evaluation on the vertex set is injective because a full-dimensional polytope has vertices
that affinely span R3.
Remark 3.3 (Non-trivial speeds). A non-trivial admissible speed means that it is not globally
affine. The Lemma 4.1 shows that, at an interior minimum of P along a volume-affine
origin-symmetric shadow system, admissibility forces the speed to be symmetric and globally
affine. Therefore, any admissible speed lying outside the three-dimensional globally affine
subspace gives a contradiction to local minimality.
