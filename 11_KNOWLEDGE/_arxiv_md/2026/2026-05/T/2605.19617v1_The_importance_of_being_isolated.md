THE IMPORTANCE OF BEING ISOLATED
SCOTT BALCHIN, JUAN OMAR G´OMEZ, AND GREG STEVENSON
Abstract. We give both a sufficient condition for and an obstruction to the derived
category of a commutative ring being generated by its residue fields. As an illustration,
we exhibit a ring for which Foxby’s small support classifies localizing subcategories
despite the failure of the local-to-global principle. We also conclude that the residue
fields do not generate for a polynomial ring in infinitely many variables. Finally, we give
a necessary and sufficient criterion for the derived category to satisfy the local-to-global
principle; it turns out to depend solely on the topology of the spectrum.
1. Introduction
A natural approach to understanding the unbounded derived category of a commutative
ring is through its various localizations. In the situations we understand well, for instance
in the noetherian setting, these derived localizations reflect the prime ideal structure of the
ring. However, the collection of all localizations remains mysterious beyond the noetherian
case, with only a few exceptions. In fact, we do not even know whether this class forms
a set or a proper class. This lack of understanding is perhaps not surprising given the
intricate nature of these localizations in the examples we have (cf. [8,13]).
An obvious starting point is to understand the boundary along which the methods from
the noetherian case break down. This leads us to the question: for which commutative
rings is the unbounded derived category generated by its residue fields? After all, this is
the crucial condition leading to Neeman’s classification in the noetherian case [7].
On the positive side, we give a sufficient condition for the residue fields to generate:
Theorem (2.1). Let A be a commutative ring. If Mod A has Gabriel dimension then D(A)
is generated by the residue fields. In particular, the lattice of localizing subcategories of
D(A) is given by the power set of Spec A.
Using this result, in Section 3 we exhibit a ring A such that the Hochster dual (Spec A)∨
of the spectrum does not satisfy the TD separation axiom (i.e. not all points are locally
closed), yet the residue fields still generate. In particular, this shows that Foxby’s small
support can classify localizing subcategories even in the absence of the local-to-global
principle. This also gives a negative answer to [1, Questions 12.2 and 12.5].
We do not know if having Gabriel dimension is a necessary condition for generation by
the residue fields. In fact, we are lacking general tools one could reach for to check that
the residue fields fail to generate for a given ring. To move towards remedying this, we
give an obstruction, in terms of the constructible topology on the spectrum, to generation
by the residue fields:
Theorem (5.3). Let A be a commutative ring. If (Spec A)con does not have Cantor-
Bendixson rank, then the residue fields do not generate D(A).

2. A sufficient condition
We begin by proving a sufficient condition for the residue fields of A to generate D(A).
We refer to [5] and [11, Chapter 5.5] for the relevant terminology and preliminaries on
Gabriel dimension (which is originally due to Gabriel [4]). We follow the convention that
(Mod A)0 consists of the semiartinian modules and let Tα : Mod A −→Mod A/(Mod A)α
denote the localization killing the objects at stage α.
Theorem 2.1. If Mod A has Gabriel dimension then D(A) is generated by the residue
fields. In particular, the lattice of localizing subcategories of D(A) is given by the power set
of Spec A.
The first step is to identify the residue fields as Gabriel simple modules, i.e. to check for
each p that there is a successor ordinal γ with Tγ−1k(p) simple and k(p) has no non-trivial
subobject in (Mod A)γ−1. We will say that a collection of modules M is a representative
collection of Gabriel simples if it consists of Gabriel simple modules and for each α every
simple object of Mod A/(Mod A)α is of the form TαM for some M ∈M.
Lemma 2.2. If A is a commutative ring such that Mod A has Gabriel dimension then the
residue fields k(p) for p ∈Spec A are a representative collection of Gabriel simples.
Proof. Suppose that E is an indecomposable injective A-module. By [11, Theorem 5.16]
E has an associated prime, say p, and hence we must have E ∼= E(A/p), the injective
envelope of A/p. It is a consequence of the work of Gordon and Robson [5, Section 3]
that the A/p are Gabriel simple. Then combining [4, Proposition IV.1.2] (see also the
discussion beforehand) with our observation about the injectives implies that the A/p are
a representative collection of Gabriel simples.
It then just remains to notice that k(p) is Gabriel simple. If Tγ−1A/p is simple then, by
virtue of k(p) being the filtered colimit along a sequence of endomorphisms of A/p, the

every object of (Mod A)0 has a filtration by simple modules, which are precisely the residue
fields at maximal ideals, and such a filtration witnesses membership in K.
The case of limit ordinals is also straightforward. If λ is a limit ordinal then (Mod A)λ
is the closure of ∪κ<λ(Mod A)κ under filtered colimits. So if M ∈(Mod A)λ then it is a
filtered colimit of modules lying in K (by the induction hypothesis) and hence in K by
closure under homotopy colimits.
It remains to treat the case of successor ordinals, so suppose that (Mod A)α ⊆K and
consider (Mod A)α+1 which sits in the localization sequence
(Mod A)α −→(Mod A)α+1
Tα
−→(Mod A)α+1/(Mod A)α
where the rightmost category is semiartinian. As used above, by [11, Theorem 5.16] every

every object in it. Thus, using the induction hypothesis, it is enough to show that the

Remark 2.3. We know the converse of Theorem 2.1 holds in at least one case: if the Krull
dimension of A is 0 then the residue fields generate D(A) if and only if A is semiartinian (i.e.
it has Gabriel dimension 0). Indeed, if the residue fields generate D(A) then every injective
A-module receives a non-zero map from a simple module and hence has non-zero socle.
Any module is an essential submodule of an injective module and so also has non-zero
socle and thus A is semiartinian (cf. Theorem 4.1).
3. An example
In this section we present an amusing example. Consider the commutative ring
A = k[x1, x2, . . .](x1,x2,...)
(xixj | i ̸= j)
that is, we take the local ring at the origin of union of the axes in infinite dimensional affine
space. This is a local ring and is isomorphic to the ring obtained by taking ⊕ik[xi](xi),
viewed as a non-unital ring, and freely making it a unital k-algebra.
Let us compute Spec A. If p is a prime ideal then the relations xixj = 0 mean that there
is at most one i with xi /∈p. Let us denote by pi the ideal (xj | j ̸= i). The corresponding
quotient is A/pi = k[xi](xi) and so pi is prime and it must be a minimal prime by the

Let us now show that Mod A has Gabriel dimension. Let (Mod A)0 denote the colimit
closed Serre subcategory of semiartinian modules and T0 : Mod A −→Mod A/(Mod A)0
the corresponding localization. The short exact sequence
0 −→m −→A −→k −→0
implies that T0m ∼= T0A. The maximal ideal decomposes as m = ⊕i(xi) and so to show
(Mod A)1 = Mod A it is enough to verify that the residue fields k(pi) ∼= k(xi) are simple in
Mod A/(Mod A)0 and T0k(pi) ∼= T0(xi).
We start with the latter. The short exact sequence
0 −→k[xi](xi) −→k(pi) −→k(pi)/k[xi](xi) −→0
shows that T0k[xi](xi) ∼= T0k(pi) as the quotient is semiartinian (in fact this is just a short
exact sequence of k[xi](xi)-modules and so one is doing the computation over a DVR). It is
obvious that T0k[xi](xi) ∼= T0(xi). From all of this we learn that killing (Mod A)0 inverts
the action of xi on T0k[xi](xi) and so it is simple (or one can again use that we are really
doing the computation over a DVR where it is standard).
Remark 3.1. The above makes explicit how the residue fields generate D(A).
Consequences 3.2. We see that A can have Gabriel dimension, and hence the lattice
of localizing subcategories of D(A) can be naturally lattice isomorphic to the power set
of Spec A, without (Spec A)∨being TD. In particular, Foxby’s small support can classify
localizing subcategories without the local-to-global principle holding. Indeed, in the given
example the punctured spectrum is not quasi-compact and so no idempotent cutting out
{m} can be constructed from finite localizations. Moreover, the situation does not improve
if one considers smashing localizations: one easily checks that T0A ∼= T0m is not compact
in D(A)/ loc(k) and so loc(k) cannot be smashing (in fact, the telescope conjecture holds
for D(A)).
4. Recollections on absolutely flat approximations
We denote by (Spec A)con the space obtained by equipping Spec A with the constructible
topology (see [3, Definition 1.3.11]). Let f : A −→Aabs be the absolutely flat approximation
of A. This is the initial map from A to a ring of weak global dimension 0. The map f
induces a continuous bijection Spec Aabs −→Spec A identifying Spec Aabs with (Spec A)con.
We regard this as an identification and use the usual symbols for points, e.g. x ∈Spec A, to
refer to points in both Spec A and Spec Aabs with the understanding that these correspond
to different prime ideals in different rings. This is somewhat harmless as the residue
fields of A and Aabs at the point x agree. An important original reference for material on
absolutely flat rings is [9] and some relevant material is collected in [13].
For an object X ∈D(Aabs) we set
suppAabs X = {x ∈Spec Aabs | X ⊗k(x) ̸= 0} = {x ∈Spec Aabs | Xx ̸= 0}

Theorem 4.1. Let A be a commutative absolutely flat ring. Then the residue fields

Proof. The theorems quoted above show that the residue fields generate A if and only if A

5. The Obstruction
As above let f : A −→Aabs be the absolutely flat approximation of A. We start by
computing enough about the f ∗k(x) for x ∈Spec A to reduce the problem of generation
to one in Aabs. Here, f ∗denotes the derived base change functor along f, and we write f∗
for its right adjoint, given by restriction of scalars.
Lemma 5.1. For a point x ∈Spec A we have suppAabs f ∗k(x) = {x}.
Proof. If y ̸= x is another point then, by the projection formula and agreement of the
residue fields for A and Aabs, we have
0 = k(x) ⊗k(y)
= k(x) ⊗f∗k(y)
= f∗(f ∗k(x) ⊗k(y)).
The functor f∗is conservative and so we deduce that f ∗k(x) ⊗k(y) vanishes for all y ̸= x
and so it has support contained in {x}. The support cannot be empty as
0 ̸= RHomA(k(x), k(x)) = RHomA(k(x), f∗k(x)) ∼= RHomAabs(f ∗k(x), k(x))
shows that f ∗k(x) ̸= 0 and the support on D(Aabs) detects vanishing.
□
Lemma 5.2. We have f ∗k(x) ∈locAabs(k(x)).
Proof. By Lemma 5.1 we know suppAabs f ∗k(x) = {x}. This is equivalent to the vanishing
of the stalks (f ∗k(x))y for y ̸= x. Consider then the localization triangle
ΓZ(x)Aabs −→Aabs −→k(x) −→
corresponding to the Thomason (i.e. open) subset Z(x) = Spec Aabs {x}. Here we identify
k(x) with LZ(x)Aabs; see [13, Lemma 4.2]. We know that
0 = ΓZ(x)Aabs ⊗k(x) ∼= (ΓZ(x)Aabs)x
and thus (f ∗k(x) ⊗ΓZ(x)Aabs)z ∼= (f ∗k(x))z ⊗(ΓZ(x)Aabs)z vanishes for all z ∈Spec Aabs.

so we have f ∗k(x) ∼= f ∗k(x) ⊗k(x). Thus f∗k(x) lies in locAabs(k(x)) as claimed.

Corollary 5.4. If the Hochster dual of Spec A is TD, so that the idempotents ΓxA exist for
all x ∈Spec A, then if (Spec A)con does not have Cantor-Bendixson rank the local-to-global

the local-to-global principle. We return to this in Section 7 after giving some examples.

7. Consequences for the local-to-global principle
As alluded to after Corollary 5.4, we are now in a position to completely characterize
when the local-to-global principle holds for the derived category. In order to get there we
need to discuss a little point-set topology.
Recall that a topological space X is scattered if every non-empty subset Y ⊆X contains
a point isolated in Y . It is a classical result that X being scattered is equivalent to X
having Cantor-Bendixson rank.
Lemma 7.1. Let X be a scattered space. Then X is TD.
Proof. Let x ∈X. Then {x} has an isolated point. Since open sets are generalization
closed, this isolated point must be x. That is, {x} = {x} ∩U with U ⊆X open. Hence
{x} is locally closed as required.
□
Lemma 7.2. Let X be a TD space. Then for all x ∈X we have (X \ {x}) ∪{x} is an
open subset of X.
Proof. Choose an open U ∋x such that {x} = U ∩{x}. Then
(X \ {x}) ∪{x} = (X \ {x}) ∪U.
□
Lemma 7.3. A space X is scattered if and only if every non-empty closed subset of X
has an isolated point.
Proof. The forward direction is immediate. For the converse, suppose that closed subsets
have isolated points, and let Y ⊆X. Then Y has an isolated point, y ∈Y , i.e. there exists
U open with U ∩Y = {y}. But then y ∈Y as required.
□
Lemma 7.4. Let X be a coherent space. If X is scattered then Xcon is scattered.
Proof. This is clear as the constructible topology is finer than the topology of X.
□
Lemma 7.5. Let X be a TD coherent space such that Xcon is scattered. Then X has an
isolated point.
Proof. As Xcon is scattered, the subset Xmin of generic points of X has an isolated point
x as a subspace of Xcon. By [3, Corollary 4.4.6], Xmin inherits the same topology from X
as it does from Xcon. As such x is isolated in Xmin as a subspace of X. That is, there
exists an open U ⊆X such that U ∩Xmin = {x}. This implies U ⊆{x} by generalization
closure of open subsets, and the assumption that x is a generic point.
As X is TD, we have that W := (X \{x})∪{x} is open by Lemma 7.2. Then U ∩W = {x}
is isolated as required.
□
Proposition 7.6. Let X be a TD coherent space. Then X is scattered if and only if Xcons
is.
Proof. The forward direction is Lemma 7.4. For the converse, let Y ⊆X be a closed
subset. Then Y is again coherent and TD, and Y con ⊆Xcon is scattered, so by Lemma 7.5
Y has an isolated point.
□
Theorem 7.7. Let A be a commutative ring. Then D(A) satisfies the local-to-global
principle if and only if (Spec A)∨is scattered.
