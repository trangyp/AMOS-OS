Abstract
Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) and its static sinusoidal subcarriers have un-
derpinned the 4G and 5G eras, delivering high spectral efficiency and resilience to multipath fading
through an efficient multicarrier architecture. However, as future systems move toward doubly dispersive
environments driven by high-mobility applications and migration to mmWave/sub-THz bands, the time-
invariance assumption underlying OFDM becomes increasingly difficult to maintain, and Doppler-
induced degradation becomes prominent. While enhancements such as multiple-input multiple-output
(MIMO), advanced coding, and scheduling provide incremental remedies, they introduce additional
overhead, because the sinusoidal subcarrier itself offers no inherent waveform-level robustness to Doppler
impairments. Accordingly, two time-frequency spreading philosophies have emerged to improve Doppler
resilience by distributing each symbol’s energy across both dimensions of the time-frequency plane:
(i) 2D isotropic spreading via the delay-Doppler (DD) domain, exemplified by the orthogonal time
frequency space (OTFS) family, and (ii) sheared spreading via parameterizable chirps, exemplified by the
affine frequency-division multiplexing (AFDM) family. In this article, we examine key considerations
for future waveform design across these paradigms and argue that transitioning from the sinusoidal
subcarriers of OFDM to the chirp-based subcarriers offers a viable design direction for improving
Doppler robustness while retaining much of the mature OFDM infrastructure. This perspective also
highlights the suitability of chirp-based waveforms for integrated sensing and communications (ISAC)
and their extensibility to emerging physical-layer techniques. Overall, we argue that the transition from
sinusoids to chirps is a technically motivated, compelling evolutionary direction for future wireless
physical layer design.

THE RATE-ROBUSTNESS PENDULUM OF WIRELESS EVOLUTION
The evolution of wireless cellular systems is often oversimplified as a monotonic trajectory
toward ever-higher data rates, with the gradual inclusion of applications and features, as illustrated
in Fig. 1a: from analog voice connectivity in the first generation (1G), to digital traffic in the
second generation (2G), mobile internet in the third generation (3G), broadband connectivity in
the fourth generation (4G), and the flexibility of fifth generation (5G), which supports massive
machine-type communication (mMTC), ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC), and
enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB) [1]. More recently, sixth generation (6G) systems have been
envisioned around AI-native operation and integrated sensing and communications (ISAC) [2].
A closer examination reveals that this evolution is more accurately represented not as a one-
dimensional progression along the rate axis over time, but as a three-dimensional trajectory in
the joint rate-robustness-time space, as depicted in Fig. 1b. In this view, the conventional rate-
centric narrative is recovered merely as a projection of the true richer trend in 3D – a projection
that obscures the oscillating design priority along the robustness axis.
Examining the rate-vs-robustness dimension ties these two threads together and exposes the
underlying pendulum-like engineering rhythm between the two design objectives on each axis.
2G pursued robustness against transmission errors through digitalization and coding, 3G pursued
robustness against interference through wideband spectrum spreading, 4G prioritized mobile
broadband rates through narrowband multiplexing, and 5G further enhanced rate and flexibility
through multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) beamforming and advanced signal processing.
As we approach 6G, however, the design priority is shifting once again toward robustness.
Driven by demands imposed by emerging 6G applications such as unmanned aerial vehicle
(UAV) and low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite networks, industrial Internet of Things (IIoT), and
high-speed vehicle-to-everything (V2X) [2], a renewed focus on robustness is required.
To motivate this architectural realignment, we cast the wireless evolution of the past decades
as a dialectic rhythm composed of the following stages:
• Thesis: The era of interference robustness through spectrum spreading.
• Antithesis: The era of rate and efficiency through multiplexing and MIMO.
• Crisis: The Doppler bottleneck arising from high-mobility and high-frequency bands.
• Synthesis: The era of Doppler robustness through the reintroduction of spectrum spreading.

THE DIALECTIC RHYTHM OF WIRELESS EVOLUTION
The Thesis (3G) – The Era of Interference Robustness
As the design objective shifted toward universal connectivity in the early 2000s, 3G addressed
the challenge of multiuser interference by anchoring its architecture in spread-spectrum princi-
ples. In this framework, each user’s narrowband information signal was multiplied in time by
a pseudo-noise (PN) chip sequence, flattening and widening the signal’s power spectral density
(PSD) across the available bandwidth, implementing spectrum spreading [3]. This spreading
served two purposes. First, the code-division multiple access (CDMA) scheme was introduced,
whereby assigning each user a unique semi-orthogonal PN sequence enabled simultaneous
transmission by multiple users over the same bandwidth. In turn, matched-filter despreading at
the receiver recovers the desired user’s signal efficiently while enabling multi-user interference
suppression via the so-called processing gain. However, the number of supportable users with
acceptable cross-correlation is fundamentally limited by the spreading-code design, as charac-
terized by the Welch bound.
From a waveform-centric perspective, another consequence of wideband occupancy is inherent
frequency diversity, because the spread signal naturally averages over many fading realizations.
This provided resilience to frequency-selective fading as an intrinsic waveform property, without
relying on external mechanisms. This philosophy also extended well beyond cellular systems: for
example, IEEE 802.11b Wi-Fi employed direct-sequence spread-spectrum (DSSS) techniques,
and ultra-wideband (UWB) systems pushed the spreading principle to its logical degree, spread-
ing signals across several hundred MHz up to a few GHz of bandwidth – enabling coexistence
with major narrowband systems while exploiting the channel diversity and robustness of spread
signals for efficient communications and sensing.
The Antithesis (4G/5G) – The Era of Rate and Efficiency
With the advent of smartphones and mobile Internet services, the design mandate shifted
toward achieving higher data rates, for broadband access in 4G [4] and for heterogeneous
services, including eMBB, URLLC, and mMTC, in 5G [1]. This motivated the search for
alternatives to CDMA, whose capacity was fundamentally limited by its spreading-code design
and therefore insufficient for the demands of the new era.
As a solution, orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) [5] emerged based on
the core principle of dividing the total bandwidth into a large number of narrow, orthogonal

subcarriers with distinct frequencies, rather than spreading a signal across the entire bandwidth.
These subcarriers of OFDM are static sinusoids, eigenfunctions of linear time-invariant (LTI)
channels, enabling elegant per-subcarrier equalization when the channel remains approximately
time-invariant over one symbol interval. This condition was generally satisfied in the low-
mobility, sub-6 GHz operating regimes of 4G/5G, and the subcarriers remain orthogonal and
exhibit resilience to multipath fading with the use of a simple cyclic prefix (CP). However,
they do not inherently provide frequency diversity, as each subcarrier is narrowband and may
experience deep fading.
To remedy this, various techniques have been developed on top of the OFDM paradigm: mas-
sive MIMO (mMIMO) beamforming providing channel hardening, intelligent resource schedul-
ing, and powerful channel coding across subcarriers, all made practically feasible by the compu-
tationally efficient fast Fourier transform (FFT)-based implementation of sinusoidal modulation.
Together, these enhancements allowed OFDM to mature into a spectrally and hardware-efficient
platform that has been the dominant wireless system design over the past two decades.
However, the nature of this robustness deserves a closer examination. Coded information can
be distributed across subcarriers and OFDM symbols, thereby providing frequency diversity when
the allocation is sufficiently wide. However, this type of diversity relies on the aforementioned
external mechanisms operating on top of narrowband subcarriers that do not themselves spread,
but rather map the coded information in a distributed fashion across non-spreading sinusoids.
Furthermore, while this mature technology and infrastructure continued to evolve, the time-
invariance assumption of OFDM was increasingly challenged by future application demands.
The Crisis (6G) – The Doppler Bottleneck
As we advance toward 6G, the time-invariance assumption underlying OFDM’s success be-
comes increasingly difficult to maintain in many of the new envisioned use cases. In high-
mobility scenarios such as UAV, V2X, and LEO satellite networks, the wireless channel becomes
doubly dispersive, varying rapidly in both time and frequency. This is further exacerbated
by the planned use of higher-frequency bands in 6G including upper mid-band, millimeter-
wave (mmWave), and sub-Terahertz (THz) bands, where even moderate-to-high velocities (e.g.,
120 km/h in V2X/UAV at 71 GHz) induce Doppler shifts corresponding to approximately 7 % of
a typical 120 kHz subcarrier spacing, thereby impairing orthogonality and causing detrimental
inter-carrier interference (ICI) in OFDM-based systems.

As mentioned, there exist mitigation strategies within the OFDM framework, but they require
additional overhead and complexity, or are suitable only for specific operating regimes. For
example, Doppler pre-compensation is effective for predictable, line-of-sight-dominated motion,
such as single-LEO orbital trajectories, but becomes challenging under unpredictable mobility
with rich multipath scattering. These approaches only fundamentally map information across
non-spreading subcarriers, rather than achieving intrinsic waveform-level spreading.
This distinction has several critical consequences. First, coded diversity via distributed re-
source mapping becomes less effective for narrowband transmissions — such as control signals,
synchronization channels, and most importantly the small data packets that dominate practical
traffic [6]. In such cases, allocations cannot span sufficiently distinct channel realizations, and the
waveform itself must carry the required robustness. Moreover, in a doubly dispersive channel,
Doppler-induced ICI does not create isolated fades but systematically degrades orthogonality
across the entire grid, producing correlated impairments that frequency-domain interleaving and
coding alone cannot efficiently resolve.
Furthermore, the emerging integrated sensing and communications (ISAC) paradigm reframes
the concept of Doppler entirely [7]: while communications has so far treated it as an impairment
to be mitigated or compensated, sensing relies on it as a primary observable quantity for estimat-
ing the velocity of environmental scatterers or targets. A Doppler-fragile waveform is therefore
less suitable for simultaneously supporting communication and sensing without resorting to slow-
time approaches across multiple time slots, and waveform-level Doppler resilience is essential
for the resource-efficient, multifunctional vision of 6G and beyond (6G+).
The Synthesis (6G+) – The Resurrection of Spectrum Spreading
A promising waveform direction is therefore to employ subcarriers that inherently occupy an
extended region of the time-frequency (TF) plane, thereby distributing each symbol’s energy
across both time and frequency as an intrinsic structural property.
Therefore, waveform design must shift its primary focus back to robustness, and a form
of spectrum spreading must return to the physical layer — but this time not for multi-user
interference suppression, as in 3G, nor for frequency diversity, which coded systems already
achieve, but specifically for Doppler resilience. However, at the same time, ever-increasing rate
demands still remain a key priority, precluding a naive return to CDMA-style frequency-domain

wideband spreading. Instead, the 6G+ paradigm calls for joint time-frequency spreading, while
preserving the multiplexing architecture, spectral efficiency, and maturity of the OFDM legacy.
Two fundamentally different philosophies have emerged to realize this joint TF spreading:
2D isotropic spreading via the delay-Doppler (DD) domain, exemplified by the orthogonal time
frequency space (OTFS) waveform family [8], and sheared spreading in the affine TF-domain
representation, where each symbol occupies a structured path across both time and frequency,
exemplified by the affine frequency-division multiplexing (AFDM) waveform family [9]. Both
approaches have been shown to effectively achieve spreading across the TF plane; however, they
differ fundamentally in their mathematical formulation, architectural realization, and practical
implications for the existing ecosystem and emerging 6G requirements.
THE RETURN OF SPREADING – BUT IN WHAT FORM?
Throughout the dialectic rhythm above, the fundamental design choice at each generation
has been the shape of the elementary per-symbol waveform. For multicarrier schemes such as
OFDM, this is straightforwardly the individual subcarriers (modulated sinusoids) – but the idea
also extends to others: in 3G, the elementary per-symbol waveform is the PN-multiplied wideband
pulse associated with each unique sequence; in 2G, it is the shaped time-domain pulse spanning
the full bandwidth. The same unit also serves as the basic resource for multiple access, giving
rise to time-division multiple access (TDMA), CDMA, OFDMA, and their 6G+ successors.
As illustrated in Fig. 2, the evolution of waveform spreading across wireless generations can
be intuitively understood by examining the footprint that a single data symbol leaves on the
TF plane, which reveals a distinct progression in resource allocation: TDMA maps a symbol to
a strictly time-localized pulse that spans the available bandwidth; CDMA spreads the symbol
across a wide frequency band, allowing overlapping transmissions in time through code-domain
orthogonality; OFDMA strongly localizes the symbol in frequency as a narrowband subcarrier
spanning a fixed symbol duration.
However, the emerging requirements for joint TF diversity necessitate a fundamental shift
from these strictly 1D-localized footprints toward extended, 2D-coupled resource allocation. To
ensure that each data symbol experiences the full doubly dispersive channel, two geometrically
distinct paradigms have emerged: the sheared approach illustrated as chirp-division multiple
access (ChDMA) – a term reflecting the geometric shearing effect of the sinusoidal lines across
the TF plane as illustrated in Fig. 2d – structurally couples the two dimensions by employing

high-mobility channels due to their ability to represent the full doubly dispersive channel, but
rather more in architectural realization and practicality. Ultimately, the two approaches represent
complementary yet competing design points along a common frontier [10].

The 2D Isotropic Spreading Paradigm: Strengths and Design Considerations
The 2D isotropic spreading approach was pioneered by the original OTFS proposal [8],
commonly referred to as multicarrier (MC)-OTFS to distinguish it from its descendants, and
has since matured into a rich family including Zak-OTFS (OTFS 2.0) [11] and orthogonal
delay-Doppler division multiplexing (ODDM) [12]. Information symbols are placed in the DD
domain and spread across the TF plane via a 2D transform, typically the inverse symplectic
finite Fourier transform (ISFFT) paired with a Heisenberg transform, or the inverse discrete Zak
transform (IDZT) for Zak-OTFS, yielding the 2D footprint shown in Fig. 2e. By construction, as
also illustrated, each DD-domain symbol is spread across the entire allocated TF grid, making
this the most literal realization of joint time-frequency spreading. Indeed, the paradigm is the
most mature representative of the spreading revival to date, supported by an active body of
theoretical works and experimental demonstrations since its first presentation in 2017 [8].
The DD-domain construction offers two key advantages. First, a doubly dispersive channel,
which is rapidly time-varying in the TF domain, maps to a nearly time-invariant and sparse
representation in the DD domain (see [10, Fig. 1]). To appreciate this sparsity, consider a V2X
scenario with a small number of dominant environment scatterers - a preceding vehicle, a roadside
building, and an overpass - each giving rise to a single resolvable path with a distinct delay and
Doppler shift determined by its range and relative velocity. In the TF domain, these paths combine
into a rapidly fluctuating, dense channel across the entire grid, whereas in the DD domain, the
same channel reduces to just a handful of isolated point-like taps, one per scatterer, whose
coordinates in the DD grid correspond to its path delay and Doppler shift, making estimation
and equalization structurally simple. Second, the representation is intrinsically sensing-friendly,
as DD coordinates translate directly into range and radial velocity, making OTFS a natural fit for
ISAC. Furthermore, the DD-domain input/output relation becomes predictable and nonfading,
and even extractable from a single pilot symbol.
However, these strengths come with some design considerations relative to OFDM systems.
The first concerns pulse shaping. By the Balian-Low theorem [13, Thm. 8.4.1], any pulse forming
a valid basis on the critically sampled TF Heisenberg lattice is poorly localized in time, frequency,
or both, making a jointly well-localized pulse physically unrealizable. Consequently, rectangular
pulses commonly assumed in MC-OTFS analyses, sacrifice frequency localization and produce
DD-domain spreading and leakage under fractional delay and Doppler.

Zak-OTFS [11] addresses this limitation through the crystallization condition, which guaran-
tees a predictable and non-fading DD-domain I/O relation when the delay and Doppler periods
exceed the effective channel spreads, restoring well-defined behavior under practical pulses.
However, achieving improved DD-domain localization in Zak-OTFS explicitly requires structural
machinery that is absent from an OFDM-like pipeline, such as factorizable DD-domain pulse
filters whose transmit and receive operations involve convolutions, along with the associated
time and bandwidth expansion overhead.
The second consideration concerns pilot overhead and resource granularity. Most OTFS vari-
ants use DD-grid-tailored pilots, which, in guard-protected embedded designs, incur higher
overhead than conventional OFDM pilot scattering, a gap that recent superimposed- and spread-
pilot schemes have partly addressed. More fundamentally, although OTFS can be implemented
as a pre-/post-coding layer over a conventional OFDM pipeline, such implementations often rely
on idealized pulse assumptions. In addition, the resource granularity of OTFS is the 2D DD
grid rather than the 1D sinusoidal subcarrier, introducing a nontrivial mapping between 3GPP
numerology and the DD grid for multiuser multiplexing and scheduling.
These considerations do not diminish the technical maturity or effectiveness of the 2D isotropic
paradigm; rather, they reflect the engineering depth invested in making DD-domain modulation
practical. They do motivate a natural question, however: can the structural benefits of joint TF
spreading – sparse effective channel, full TF diversity, and intrinsic Doppler resilience – be
obtained through a construction that stays closer to the OFDM legacy and intuition?
FROM SINUSOIDS TO CHIRPS: THE SHEARED SPREADING ALTERNATIVE
Motivated by the above question, we argue that the shearing approach offers a compelling
synthesis, exemplified by the AFDM family, which retains the structural benefits of joint TF
spreading of the 2D isotropic paradigm, while maintaining maximal backward compatibility
with the mature OFDM technology, and introduces a unique parameterizable degree of freedom.
Chirps as a Generalized Sinusoid
A chirp is any signal with a time-varying instantaneous frequency, with the general class in-
cluding linear, quadratic, hyperbolic, and exponential variants. In the wireless literature, however,
“chirp” is conventionally used to denote the linear chirp, i.e., a linear frequency-modulated signal
with instantaneous frequency f(t) = f0 + αt and corresponding quadratic phase, parameterized

by the chirp rate α. Under this definition, the sinusoid is recovered as the special case α = 0, as
illustrated in Fig. 3a. In other words, the relationship between the sinusoid and the linear chirp
is one of generalization rather than substitution: introducing a chirp rate adds a new degree of
freedom without discarding any property of the sinusoidal baseline.
The motivation for shifting from sinusoids to chirps is primarily channel-theoretic. Sinusoids
are the eigenfunctions of LTI channels, which is why FFT-based OFDM diagonalizes the channel
and achieves subcarrier orthogonality in static environments [5]. However, when the channel
becomes linear time-varying (LTV) – i.e., doubly dispersive – sinusoids lose this eigenfunction
property, and finding a universal orthonormal basis that exactly diagonalizes general LTV chan-
nels is non-trivial. Therefore, rather than seeking a strict eigenbasis, the practical strategy is to
project the signal onto a basis that induces a structured, sparse representation of the channel,
exploiting the compactness of the delay-Doppler spreading function. In this context, chirps serve
as a natural generalization of sinusoids: by matching the chirp parameters to the linear delay-
Doppler coupling of the propagation paths, the doubly dispersive channel is converted into a
sparse, quasi-diagonal effective channel matrix in the discrete affine Fourier transform (DAFT)
domain, making chirps a suitable basis when time invariance is lost [9].
Geometrically, the distinction is immediate on the TF plane (Fig. 3): a sinusoid occupies
a horizontal line at fixed frequency f0, whereas a chirp traces a diagonal line with slope α.
As illustrated in Fig. 3b and Fig. 3c, the two waveforms respond to delay and Doppler in
fundamentally different ways. For a sinusoid, delay and Doppler act on different signal properties:
a delay τ produces a phase rotation proportional to both the delay τ and the carrier frequency
f0 that leaves the waveform itself (sinusoid at frequency f0) unchanged, while a Doppler shift
ν translates the signal to a different sinusoid centered at frequency f0 + ν.
For a chirp, by contrast, both delay and Doppler act on the same property, namely the starting
frequency: delay produces a shift of −ατ Hz, Doppler produces a shift of +ν Hz, and the net
effect is a single displacement ν −ατ Hz in the starting frequency while the chirp rate α is
preserved. The chirp therefore retains its key functional parameter, becoming merely a shifted
version of itself in the TF plane – analogous to how a sinusoid is preserved under delay alone.
The structural significance of this is the collapse of the two-dimensional delay-Doppler effect
into a single scalar displacement ν −ατ on a preserved waveform family, with α acting as a
tunable coupling coefficient between delay and Doppler. It should be clarified that this does not
mean that shifted chirp subcarriers never overlap with other chirps in the TF plane, rather, it

On the other hand, the second chirp parameter, c2 does not affect subcarrier orthogonality or
diversity condition at all, and instead controls the effective precoder that rotates the phases of
the symbols before chirp-subcarrier modulation. Therefore, this flexibility of c2 can be exploited
for auxiliary design objectives, all without compromising the aforementioned diversity guarantee
of AFDM. We will later highlight how this unique design degree of freedom available through
such parameterized chirp subcarriers, has enabled a variety of multifunctional applications and

waveform design techniques, including ambiguity function (AF) shaping for sensing, spectral
shaping for peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR) and out-of-band emissions (OOBE) reduction,
index modulation (IM), physical-layer security (PLS), and more. In all, we highlight that through
AFDM, the system can achieve parametrizable sheared spreading of the subcarriers and symbols.
The Practical Case for Parametrizable Sheared Spreading
As established in the discussion of the 2D isotropic paradigm, both approaches achieve
the fundamental goal of joint TF spreading and offer analogous structural benefits in doubly
dispersive channels. Therefore, their differences lie more in architectural realization and practical
implications than in achievable performance.
The DAFT structure admits a simple transmitter decomposition that remains close to the
conventional OFDM pipeline. The inverse discrete affine Fourier transform (IDAFT) is equivalent
to a sample-wise multiplication with a quadratic-phase vector with chirp rate c2, followed by
a standard inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) and second sample-wise multiplication with
another quadratic-phase vector with chirp rate c1, as illustrated in Fig. 4. Therefore, any FFT-
based baseband designed for OFDM or DFT-s-OFDM can be extended to AFDM by inserting
two elementwise, unit-magnitude phase rotations around the existing core, adding an O(N)
computational overhead on top of the unchanged O(N log N) FFT operation.
It should be highlighted that this implementation uses exact elementwise multiplications for
chirps and does not require idealized assumptions in the DAFT definition or supplementary
compensation filtering for time-frequency pulse shaping. More importantly, the elementwise
phase-rotation mechanism underlying AFDM is already present in various forms across deployed
OFDM frameworks including Long-Term Evolution (LTE), 5G, and IEEE 802.11 standards, and
therefore, the two chirp multiplications required for AFDM represent a physically grounded
extension of a signal processing operation that existing OFDM hardware and firmware already
natively support.
At the receiver, AFDM requires a CPP, which is a CP with a deterministic phase rotation
per sample [10]. This preserves circular convolution and yields a structured effective channel
in the affine-frequency domain, enabling low-complexity detection algorithms, including band-
matrix minimum mean square error (MMSE) and message-passing variants. Channel estimation
can also be handled efficiently using a single-pilot scheme with one-dimensional padding in the
affine-frequency domain [9]. These components integrate directly into an OFDM-style transceiver

architecture by reframing the processing frame from 1D frequency to 1D affine frequency,
without requiring modifications to analog front-ends, radio frequency (RF) handling, or existing
numerology.
This ecosystem continuity is the primary practical advantage of realizing sheared spreading

core-based TF numerology remains intact for guarding, prefixing, and pilot allocation, and the

frequency-division multiple access (OFDMA) logic to carry over directly to affine frequency-

In all, the resulting combination of structural Doppler resilience on a direct-evolution OFDM

THE CHIRP-BASED RESEARCH LANDSCAPE:
DERIVATIVES, APPLICATIONS, AND THE ROAD AHEAD
Since AFDM’s formal introduction in 2021, a broad and still-expanding research landscape
has emerged across nearly every axis of physical-layer design, where the parameterizable chirp
structure has demonstrated applicability well beyond Doppler-resilient communications. As com-
prehensively illustrated in Fig. 5, this landscape spans mathematical foundations, transceiver
signal processing, MIMO and intelligent surface integration, multiple access, and ISAC spanning
both FMCW-equivalent radar processing and DAFT-domain joint sensing and communication
approaches. Waveform-domain extensions and optimization further exploit the chirp-parameter
degrees of freedom for various applications such as index modulation (IM), physical-layer
security (PLS), PAPR reduction, and hardware impairment robustness, while investigations within
the 6G application scenarios and channels including V2X, UAV, and non-terrestrial network
(NTN) networks consolidate the resilience advantage of the waveform. Across most of these
branches, new analytical results and novel techniques continue to emerge, indicating that research
on parameterizable chirps has not yet been exhausted.
In addition, some of the most important remaining challenges are integrative rather than
conceptual [15]. On the standardization side, per-resource block (RB) and per-bandwidth part
(BWP) chirp-parameter signaling must be incorporated into 6G numerology, with relevant dis-
cussions having recently begun to appear in 3GPP RAN1 feature-lead summaries. The small and
interpretable chirp-parameter space is also an unusually tractable target for AI-native waveform
adaptation, as the Agile-AFDM framework has already demonstrated through per-block data-
and channel-aware scalar parameter optimization, and is far more amenable to learning-based
approaches than end-to-end waveform design. For deployment, over-the-air testbeds and field
trials integrated with standardized numerology remain comparatively scarce relative to both
OFDM and OTFS, and their development will be essential to inform standardization decisions.
These directions build upon the parameterized chirp structure without structural reinvention:
the shift from sinusoids to chirps represents not a terminal design choice, but an entry point into
a unique flexible framework whose design space remains largely open for further investigation.
