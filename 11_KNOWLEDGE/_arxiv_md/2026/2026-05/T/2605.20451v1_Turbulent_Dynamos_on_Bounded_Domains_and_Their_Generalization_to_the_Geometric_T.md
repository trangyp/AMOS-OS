1. Introduction and main results
The aim of this work is threefold: (i) to construct a broad class of magnetic fields exhibiting
turbulent dynamo action in smooth bounded domains, (ii) to present a simple convex integration
scheme, inspired by [71, 6, 27], but based on explicit potentials rather than antiderivatives, and (iii)
to show its robustness by extending it to the GTE, which applies to both Maxwell and transport
systems, thereby recovering earlier results with the added feature of compact spatial support.

The kinematic dynamo model is primarily useful for studying the onset of dynamo action during
the early stages of a seed magnetic field, when the Lorentz force J × B is too weak to noticeably
affect the fluid motion [29, §1.2.4]. Beyond this regime, this model is replaced by the non-linear
dynamo equation, which extends (dynamo) to the full magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) system by
incorporating the Cauchy momentum equation with the Lorentz force. In general, we will omit the
word kinematic in this work and refer to it simply as the dynamo equation for brevity. We will also
refer to the case ϵ = 0 as the ideal dynamo equation.
The central question in dynamo theory is whether there exist velocity fields u whose associated
dynamics can generate a large magnetic field B from a given initial condition

The dynamo problem has attracted considerable attention, not only in the physics literature (see

of Moffatt–Dormy [72], or Childress and Gilbert [55, 29].
In recent years, there has been a revival in the rigorous mathematical construction of kinematic
dynamos, primarily in R3 and T3 (see the discussion below). However, the dynamo problem in
more general domains, which is of both physical and mathematical interest, remains largely open.
In this paper, we consider bounded domains and therefore introduce the appropriate boundary
conditions, the corresponding function spaces, and the weak formulation of (dynamo).

1.1.3. Turbulent dynamos on bounded domains. Next, we refine Theorem 1.3 to produce a genuine
turbulent dynamo. In the applied mathematics literature, so-called turbulent dynamos are fre-
quently discussed, and it is plausible that astrophysical dynamos are indeed turbulent. The precise
meaning of “turbulent” in this context requires clarification; nevertheless, following Tobias [82],
we may loosely define a turbulent dynamo as one in which the velocity field is highly irregular
and the dynamo action is “multiscale”, that is, present at both very large and very small scales.
Mathematically, this corresponds to rapid growth at both low and high frequencies as time evolves.
See [82, §4] for a detailed description of the various turbulent regimes in terms of the magnetic
Prandtl number (and, in nonlinear dynamo theory, the Reynolds number), as well as the potential
advantages of such flexibility for realizing dynamos in the laboratory.
See also [77, p. 4] for a
discussion of the mathematical challenges involved in constructing solutions whose energy modes
grow simultaneously at large and small scales, uniformly in the vanishing-diffusivity limit ϵ →0.
In the full space R3 or on the torus T3, scales are naturally expressed in terms of Fourier
frequencies. In a bounded domain, however, it is more natural to formulate this notion in terms of
the eigenfrequencies of Ωassociated with electromagnetic waves. For magnetic fields, the natural
frequencies are the square roots of the eigenvalues λk of the magnetostatic operator, which admits
an orthonormal basis of eigenfields Bk. We briefly review this theory in Appendix A.

1.2. Convex integration. Theorems 1.3 and 1.5 will be obtained as a corollary of an H-principle.
Since the pioneering work of De Lellis and Sz´ekelyhidi [36], convex integration has become a powerful

instabilities [22, 23, 24, 67, 52, 50], and the celebrated works on the Onsager conjecture and K41

In the context of MHD and plasma relaxation see

functions that depend on d−(k +1) variables only. When k = d−1 this would entail working with
functions that depend on 0 variables, i.e., constants, and this is too restrictive. The extension of
the results to the case k = d −1 is thus left for future investigations.
6. Conclusion
In this work, we have investigated the potential of convex integration for the (GTE), and in
particular for the (dynamo) equation. The outcomes of convex integration should be interpreted
in two complementary ways. On the one hand, they highlight the limits of well-posedness theory
for various partial differential equations, thereby complementing the results of [10, 11, 9] on the
well-posedness of the (GTE). On the other hand, the solutions we obtain often reproduce key
features of turbulent flows.

From this perspective, there is an ongoing debate about which convex integration solutions may
have a genuine physical interpretation. In this regard, our scheme is highly explicit, which raises the
intriguing possibility that such constructions could, at least in part, be replicated experimentally
in the laboratory to build an artificial dynamo. It is therefore natural to compare the numerical
simulations in [3, 13], see Figure 4, with the structure of the dynamo solutions presented here,
namely an intermittent superposition of Daneri–Sz´ekelyhidi Mikado flows [35].
From a mathematical standpoint, it would be of great interest to explore alternative convex
integration schemes, such as those conserving helicity [46, 42, 53], or to combine our approach with
other models of turbulent flows (e.g., [70, 16, 15]). Another promising direction is the combination
with homogenization, as in [2, 20, 21], which could lead to universal dynamos, namely velocity fields
that amplify all magnetic fields. At the same time, our scheme has certain limitations, notably the
lack of universality and the presence of “bad times”, which should be addressed in future work.
Overall, the theory of the kinematic dynamo appears to have reached a stage where it can be
fruitfully studied using modern mathematical tools developed over the past 15 years for the analysis
of turbulent flows.
