THE NORMAL SUBGROUP THEOREM FOR LATTICES
ON TWO-DIMENSIONAL EUCLIDEAN BUILDINGS
JEAN LÉCUREUX
AND STEFAN WITZEL
Abstract. We prove the normal subgroup property for every group
that acts properly and cocompactly on a two-dimensional Euclidean
building: every normal subgroup has finite index or is contained in the
finite kernel of the action. As a consequence, the non-residually finite
lattices constructed by Titz Mite and the second author are virtually
simple. They are the first known simple lattices on irreducible Euclidean
buildings.
Margulis’s celebrated Normal Subgroup Theorem [Mar91, Theorem 4] as-
serts that an irreducible lattice Γ in a higher rank, semisimple algebraic
group G over a local field has all normal subgroups either finite central or
of finite index. Each factor of G is either a Lie group acting on a symmetric
space of non-compact type (by Lie theory) or is an algebraic group over a
local field acting on a Euclidean building (by Bruhat–Tits theory). Thus
Γ acts as a lattice on a product of symmetric spaces and Euclidean build-
ings. Conversely, it follows from the classification of Euclidean buildings by
Tits and Weiss [Tit86, Wei09] together with Margulis arithmeticity [Mar91,
Theorem 1] that most lattices on such products are arithmetic, see [BCL19]
for a discussion. As an exception there are infinitely many two-dimensional
buildings that are not Bruhat–Tits, i.e. do not arise from algebraic groups,
but are exotic and also admit lattices. Such lattices cannot be arithmetic.
The Normal Subgroup Theorem has been extended in various directions (see
for example [BS06], [SZ94], [BH21] among many others). Our contribution
is to extended it to all uniform lattices on two-dimensional buildings:
Theorem A (Normal Subgroup Theorem). Let X be an irreducible two-
dimensional Euclidean building and let Γ be a group acting properly and
cocompactly on X. If N ◁Γ is a normal subgroup then either N is finite and
lies in the kernel of the action on X or it has finite index in Γ.
The case where the building X is of type ˜A2 was recently proven by Bader,
Furman, and the first author [BFL] and we will discuss the relationship
below. Extending the Normal Subgroup Theorem to exotic lattices can be
seen as a result unifying arithmetic and exotic lattices.

For buildings of type ˜A2 both theorems were proven in [BFL] making use

Roughly speaking, given a subalgebra A < L∞(∆) the plan is to show that
since it is Γ-invariant and closed, it is invariant under (almost all) projectivi-
ties. To do so, we first produce a sequence of elements of Γ that converges to
the needed projectivity in the compact-open topology (Theorem 8.1). This
crucially uses ergodicity of the singular Cartan flow (Theorem 7.5). Com-
bining convergence in the compact-open topology with a martingale con-
vergence result resembling Lebesgue differentiation (Theorem 9.1) we show
that the elements of Γ converge to the projectivity in the weak-*-topology
(Theorem 10.1). Since projectivities act highly transitively, this is enough
to deduce Theorem D.
The proof involves various measure spaces and showing that maps between
them are measure preserving or measure-class preserving. The best-known
among them are the harmonic measures on ∆which are indexed by vertices
of X and lie in a common measure class; they appear in [Par06] or more
recently [RT21]. Another important example is the measure on the regular
and the singular Cartan flow, that was the main object of study in type ˜A2
in [BCL19] (the regular Cartan flow is sometimes called Weyl chamber flow
in more algebraic settings). Let us also mention the Haar measure on the
group of projectivities and the measure on the space of pairs of opposite
chambers. In order to compare measures and to show disintegration results
prouniform measures were introduced in [BFL] as limit measures of finite
spaces.
One of our main contributions is to develop the theory of prouniform mea-
sures further and to exploit it systematically. Specifically, in Appendix A
we study how an inverse system of constant-to-one maps and a choice of a
counting measure on one object leads to a measure on the limit, and how
these measures can be compared and disintegrated. This method is used in
Section 6 to construct measures on spaces of embeddings of complexes. The
generality of the framework allows us to put constraints on the embeddings
we consider (technically implemented by appropriate categories). This al-
lows to subsume Haar measures on tdlc groups in the construction and make
the comparison of the singular Cartan flow with the Haar measure of the
projectivity group (Proposition 6.27) seamless, which it was not in [BCL19].
Another illustration of how useful the approach is is when constructing the
measure on the space of pairs of opposite chambers, whose existence is well-
known ([Par06, RT21]): we can use that the effects of a choice of basepoint
cancel out without having to explicitly compute these effects (Lemma 6.23).

1. Just infiniteness and residual finiteness
In this brief section we recall the relationship between the normal subgroup
property and residual finiteness. In particular, we show how Theorem A
reduces Conjecture B to showing that the lattices are not residually finite,
and how Corollary C follows.
We first recall the relevant definitions. The finite residual Γ(∞) of a group Γ
is the intersection of all its finite index subgroups (and one may equivalently
restrict to normal ones). It is the subgroup of all elements of Γ that map
trivially under any homomorphism to a finite group. The group Γ is residu-
ally finite if Γ(∞) = {1}. A group is just-infinite if every proper quotient is
finite or, equivalently, every non-trivial normal subgroup is of finite index.
It is hereditarily just-infinite if every finite index subgroup is just-infinite.
Note that another way to phrase the normal subgroup property in Theorem A
is to say that the image ¯Γ of Γ →Aut(X) (which has finite kernel) is just-
infinite. Note also that a finite index subgroup of a lattice on X is itself
a lattice on X hence Theorem A asserts that in fact ¯Γ is hereditarily just-
infinite.
There is the following dichotomy.
Proposition 1.1. If Γ is hereditarily just-infinite then Γ(∞) is either trivial
or simple of finite index.
In particular, Γ is either residually finite or virtually simple.
In the theory of just-infinite groups [Wil71], this is part of a trichotomy,
namely a just-infinite group that is not hereditarily just-infinite is a branch
group, see [Gri00, Theorem 3(a)].
Proof. If Γ(∞) ̸= {1} then it has finite index since Γ is just-infinite. Then
if {1} ̸= N ◁Γ(∞) is non-trivial normal, then N has to have finite index
in Γ(∞) because Γ(∞) is just-infinite being finite index in the hereditarily
just-infinite group Γ. Then N has finite index in Γ and it follows from the
definition of Γ(∞) that N = Γ(∞).
□
Combining with Theorem A we see:
Corollary 1.2. If Γ < Aut(X) is discrete and acts cocompactly on X then
it is either residually finite or virtually simple. More precisely if Γ = Γ(∞)
then it is simple.
In particular, Corollary C follows together with the Main Theorem of [MW25].

3.1. Amenable actions. The main part of the proof of Theorem A is to

There are many equivalent definitions of amenable groups. The one we will

6. Pro-uniform measures
Our goal in this section is to introduce pro-uniform measures.
The gen-
eral ideas for these constructions were developped in [BFL], but we will
need them in a much greater generality, which is why we develop a different
framework. The general idea of this construction is to define a notion of a
uniform measure on the set of embeddings of a given simplicial complex in
another. However, in our more general setup we will need to put additional
restrictions on these sets of embeddings.
To do so, it will be convenient to employ basic categorical language, espe-
cially (inverse) limits and (directed) colimits. The general constructions in
a categorical framework are developped in Appendix A below, to which we
will refer. Recall that a directed set is a partially ordered set I in which any
two elements i, j have a common upper bound k ≥i, j; it may be thought of
as a category with objects I and a single morphism i →j whenever i ≤j. A
directed (respectively inverse) system in a category C consists of an object
Zi for every i ∈I and a morphism Zi →Zj (respectively Zi ←Zj) for every
i ≤j such that the triangles associated to i ≤j ≤k commute; thus it is
a covariant respectively contravariant functor from I to C. A cocone of a
directed system in C consists of an object Z and morphisms Zi →Z such
that the triangles associated to i ≤j commute; similarly a cone of an inverse
system has morphisms Zi ←Z. A colimit of a directed system (Zi)i is a
cocone Z that is universal in the sense that if Z′ is any other cocone then
there is a unique morphism Z →Z′ through which every Zi →Z′ factors;
similarly a limit of an inverse system (Zi)i is a universal cone. Limits or
colimits may or may not exist but if they exist they are unique up to unique
isomorphism. This justifies to denote them by limi Zi respectively colimi Zi.
A basic fact [Rie16, Theorem 3.4.11] is that for an object X of C the functor
HomC(−, X) preserves colimits, that is, if (Zi)i is a directed system whose
colimit exists then
