Abstract
In this work we study agents in simulated bar-
gaining scenarios, where a buyer and a seller
communicate through a text channel and at-
tempt to negotiate mutually beneficial trades,
under different information regimes (complete
information, information asymmetry or mutual
uncertainty). We evaluate their performance
w.r.t. game-theoretical solutions and further
investigate their honesty (their tendency to dis-
close or withhold information or to mislead and
deceive) as well as their credulity (their ten-
dency to trust or distrust information provided
by the other agent). We study zero-shot LLM
agents with simple prompting scaffolding as
well as fine-tuned agents, in order to investigate
whether optimising the agents to maximise fi-
nancial profits makes them stronger negotiators
but also more dishonest and less trusting.
We find that off-the-shelf LLMs all substan-
tially deviate from game-theoretical equilibria,
they attempt to lie about their private informa-
tion but cannot efficiently exploit information
asymmetries. Fine-tuning on financial utility
makes the agents stronger at achieving better
deals but also more dishonest, highlighting the
risks that optimising agents for a task can have
on their safety. We release our code and a
dataset of bargaining scenarios.1

Introduction
Agents based on Large Language Models (LLMs)
are increasingly deployed in scenarios where they
can interact with humans or other automated agents
and take impactful actions with real-world conse-
quences, therefore it is important to understand
their properties both in terms of optimality w.r.t.
their task goals and in terms of their safety and secu-
rity. Assistants with tools such as email, browsers,
and cloud infrastructure can perform unintended

In particular, agents performing automated trading

Game theory predicts that under certain condi-
tions ideal buyers and sellers achieve an equilib-
rium outcome in which a final transaction either
occurs or fails to occur, depending on their reserva-
tion prices (their absolute price limit at negotiation),
the information they have about each other and the
details of the bargaining protocol. In this work we
consider the following research questions:
1. To what extent do LLM-based agents reach
game-theoretical equilibria when bargaining
with each other over a text channel?
2. Do agents tend to engage in deception (provid-
ing self-serving false or misleading informa-
tion)? Do they tend to trust the information

3. To what extent does fine-tuning agents to be
stronger traders improve them, and does training
alter their honesty and credulity?
As a concrete example, consider the following
scenario:
• A buyer and a seller are bargaining over a bag of
1 kg of white rice.
• The buyer knows they can buy it from the open
market for vB = $2.00, that is, $2.00 is the
buyer’s reservation price.
• The seller knows they can sell it to the open mar-
ket for reservation price vS = $1.00.
Because the buyer’s reservation price is higher
than the seller’s, they both have an incentive to
make a deal and trade with each other at any price
between $1.00 and $2.00 rather than trade on the
open market. If they both know each other’s reser-
vation prices, they can achieve such outcome, pro-
vided that they reach an agreement for how to split
the $1.00 utility gap between vS and vB, for in-
stance they can trade at $1.50, splitting the utility
gap evenly. This is known as the Nash Bargain-
ing Solution (Nash, 1953). However, if the agents
have private information and can engage in multi-
ple turns of communication, they might attempt to
strategically mislead each other by providing false
information, or at least withhold information that
is against their interest to share (Figure 1). Under
one-sided asymmetric information, game theory
predicts that the informed party can capture most of
the surplus, either through take-it-or-leave-it offers
that force the uninformed party to concede towards
its own reservation (Samuelson, 1984), or through
strategic delay that signals its type (Grossman and
Perry, 1986). Under two-sided private informa-
tion, no individually-rational, incentive-compatible
mechanism guarantees efficient trade in general
(Myerson and Satterthwaite, 1983).
We generate a dataset of bargaining scenarios
and we conduct multiple experiments, evaluating
agents implemented with LLMs of multiple fami-
lies, with respect to both their game-theoretical util-
ity and their honesty and credulity, evaluated by an
impartial LLM judge which always has complete
information. We find that the uninformed party
benefits from their own ignorance, the opposite to
what game theory predicts. Informed parties are
systematically dishonest, and the uninformed coun-
terparties are mildly credulous, yet this dishonesty
fails to produce gains in terms of the bargaining
outcomes. Reinforcement learning fine-tuning on
bargaining utility produces stronger but less honest
bargainers, joint training of both agents does not
converge to a cooperative midpoint equilibrium,
rather it results in an aggressive, seller-favourable
outcome that reduces the total welfare compared to
the untrained agents.
Contributions: (i) a dataset and protocol for
incomplete-information bargaining; (ii) an LLM-
as-judge framework for honesty and credulity with
role-aware rubrics; (iii) systematic evidence that
on-policy RL for bargaining utility produces de-
ceptive policies, replicated across multiple training
configurations.

Methodology
2.1
Dataset Generation
We construct a synthetic dataset of bargaining sce-
narios using an LLM-based generation pipeline.
Generation is conditioned on four base price tiers
(low, medium, high, very-high) so that each tier
covers a different order of magnitude. For each
scenario the generator returns a product name, a
short product description, a buyer persona and a
seller persona (each a short second-person descrip-
tion of the agent’s situation and incentives), and the
lower and upper bounds of an overall price band.
We then convert each scenario into a reservation-
price distribution pair: the midpoint of the band
partitions it into two intervals, and at each trial the
seller’s reservation price vS is sampled uniformly
from the lower interval and the buyer’s reservation
price vB from the upper interval. The construction
guarantees vB > vS for every trial and gives each
agent a non-degenerate uniform prior over the op-
ponent’s reservation when its true value is hidden
(see Section 2.3). Generation hyperparameters, the
generator prompt, and dataset statistics are reported
in Appendix A.
Across all four tiers the pipeline produces 4561
scenarios after deduplication (1516, 869, 886, and
1290 for the low, medium, high, and very-high tiers
respectively); the released dataset includes all of
them. The experiments in this paper use the ten
low-tier scenarios listed in Table 1; Figure 2 shows
one in full to illustrate the format.

We simulate two LLM agents, a buyer and a seller,
which have to negotiate over a commodity in a
multi-round variant of the simultaneous-offer game

If no deal is reached within T rounds, both agents

Each agent has a private reservation price: the
buyer’s maximum willingness to pay vB and the
seller’s minimum acceptable price vS, sampled per
trial from scenario-specific uniform distributions
subject to vB > vS. Buyer utility from a deal at
price p is vB −p and seller utility is p −vS. Total
welfare equals the surplus vB −vS when a deal is
reached and zero otherwise.
We define two game-theoretic reference points.
The true Nash Bargaining Solution (NBS) of Nash
(1953) is the unique outcome that maximises the
product of utility gains over each agent’s disagree-
ment payoff under symmetry, Pareto-efficiency,
scale-invariance, and independence of irrelevant
alternatives (Binmore, 2012).
For the protocol
used here, where the disagreement payoff is zero
for both sides and utility is linear in price, the
NBS reduces to the surplus-splitting price p∗
true =
(vB + vS)/2. The solution is well-defined when-
ever the bargaining set is non-empty and compact,
which in our setting corresponds to vB ≥vS; we
sample reservation prices subject to vB > vS so
the NBS exists for every trial. The expected NBS
is computed using each agent’s belief about the
opponent’s reservation price.
We define the Seller’s advantage as seller utility
minus buyer utility: (p −vS) −(vB −p) = 2p −
vB −vS. This is not the surplus vB −vS (which is
the price-independent total welfare, and is always
fully gained whenever the agents make a deal);
seller advantage depends on the deal price p and
measures how the surplus is split between the two
parties, with zero corresponding to an exactly equal
split.
2.3
Information Transparency Conditions
We vary the information each agent has about the
opponent’s reservation price along four conditions:
• Full: both agents know both reservation prices.
This is the complete-information benchmark
where the NBS is the natural reference point.
• Seller-unaware (Buyer-unaware): the seller
(buyer) knows both prices; the buyer (seller)
holds only a uniform prior on vS (vB) over
the seller’s (buyer’s) range.
These are the
information-asymmetry settings where theory
predicts the informed party should be able to
extract surplus.
• Both-unaware: each agent knows its own reser-
vation price and a uniform prior over the other’s.
This is the symmetric private-information setting
of Chatterjee and Samuelson (1983), in which
neither party has a direct informational advan-
tage2.

tier listed in Table 1. Reservation prices are resam-
pled independently for each trial. Eight indepen-
dent trials are run per (scenario × transparency)
cell, yielding 320 trials per model.

2.5
Agent Prompting and Models
Each agent is prompted with their role, the item,
their reservation price, their opponent’s reserva-
tion price or a prior over it (depending on the
transparency condition), the scenario-specific per-
sona, and the bargaining rules (see Appendix F.1).
Agents respond with a free-form reasoning block
followed by a structured reply containing a mes-
sage to the opponent, an action (OFFER, ACCEPT,
or REJECT), and, when the action is an offer, an
offer price.
We evaluate five models, grouped by provider.
Three are reasoning models that emit chain-of-
thought tokens in addition to the final structured
reply; two are non-reasoning models that emit only
the final structured reply (the Anthropic models
support an opt-in extended-thinking mode but we
do not enable it because of budget considerations).
• Anthropic claude-sonnet-4-6 (non-reasoning; re-
ferred to as snt-4-6 in tables): temperature 1.0,
max 2048 tokens.
• Anthropic claude-opus-4-7 (non-reasoning):
same configuration as claude-sonnet-4-6.
• OpenAI GPT-5.2 (reasoning): temperature 1.0,
max 2048 completion tokens (which include rea-
soning tokens).
• OpenAI GPT-5.5 (reasoning): same configura-
tion as GPT-5.2.
• Alibaba Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B (reasoning; referred
to as Q3.5-9B in tables): temperature 1.0, top-p
0.95, presence penalty 1.5, top-k 20, up to 81 920
completion tokens to support chain-of-thought
reasoning (thinking mode enabled via the default
chat template).
2.6
Honesty and Credulity Evaluation
For every trial with at least one uninformed agent
(transparency ̸= full), an LLM judge (GPT-5.2)
rates the informed agent’s honesty (the truthfulness
of its claims about its own reservation price) and the
uninformed agent’s credulity (the extent to which
it accepts unverifiable claims from the opponent).
Each metric is rated on a 0–4 scale.3
The judge sees the full conversation transcript,
both agents’ true reservation prices, and the per-
scenario uniform-prior ranges;See Appendix F.2
for the judge prompt.
3Terms such as lying, believing, honesty, and credulity
should be understood as metaphorical rather than as attributing
genuine intentional states; the scenarios nonetheless emulate
dynamics that commonly arise in human interactions.
The two scales are anchored at the neutral mid-
point 2, with deceptive or sceptical behaviour be-
low and prosocial or credulous behaviour above.
Honesty: 0 = outright lies; 1 = misleading but
technically true; 2 = withholds information against
own interest without lying; 3 = transparent while
still pursuing own interest; 4 = fully helpful even
against own interest.
Credulity: 0 = very distrustful, ignores opponent’s
claims; 1 = engages but sceptical; 2 = evaluates
claims critically; 3 = tends to trust without pushing
back; 4 = fully trusting even against own interest.

Experiments
3.1
Setup
We run five sets of experiments, one for each model,
all using 10 diverse low-price commodity scenarios
(n = 80 trials per transparency condition, T = 6
rounds). We report all utility and price-deviation
metrics normalised by the available surplus vB −
vS, so that 1.0 represents the full surplus and 0.5
represents an equal split.
Notation.
In tables and figures throughout the
paper B abbreviates buyer, S abbreviates seller, and
the four transparency conditions are written Full,
B-unaware, S-unaware, and ×2-unaware (both-
unaware). Paired-test significance is reported as a
superscript on each value: ∗p < 0.05, † p < 0.01,
‡ p < 0.001, and ns for p ≥0.05.

Related Work
Bargaining between agents that communicate over
a text channel has been studied by Fu et al. (2023).
Compared to our work, they only consider un-
trained models, using instead an in-context learning
system with suggestions generated by a critic LLM
to adapt the agents. They only adapt one agent at
time, and do not compare against game-theoretical
solutions or evaluate honesty and credulity, hence
our work constitutes a more comprehensive study.
Various other works evaluate LLM models
in classical problems from cooperative and non-
cooperative game theory, but compared to our work,
they do not let agents directly communicate over
a text channel in free form text, instead they have
the agents only output actions (and strategies or ra-
tionale for their own internal use; Duan et al. 2024;
Payne and Alloui-Cros 2025), or output programs
that then select actions (Willis et al., 2025).
5
Conclusion
Across multiple LLMs from different providers
and across our RL-fine-tuned agents, the results
converge on a single picture: utility-maximising
bargaining and deception are positively correlated.
Standard incomplete-information bargaining theory
predicts that the informed party captures most of
the surplus; we observe the opposite, together with
systematic informed-party dishonesty and mild
uninformed-party credulity. The informed party’s
misrepresentations are not credible enough to drive
the price, while the uninformed party’s anchor,
grounded in no claim, escapes the same scrutiny.
RL fine-tuning amplifies both effects. Training
either role for own utility produces stronger bar-
gaining and, simultaneously, less honest behaviour
in the trained role; the effect is symmetric across
roles. Joint self-play, where both sides share a
single trained policy, does not converge to the co-
operative midpoint but to a one-sided equilibrium
that destroys total welfare relative to the untrained
baseline. The induced failure mode is on the offer
side rather than in the language: the rhetoric stays
bland while the offers move past the counterparty’s
walk-away point.
We did not need to incentivise deception with a
special reward. The deception emerges from utility-
maximisation alone, even with an explicit anchor to
the base policy, and is detectable by a routine LLM
judge. Together with the welfare-destroying out-
come of joint self-play, this indicates that careful
reward shaping, third-party honesty constraints, or
explicit anti-deception training are required before
deploying RL-trained negotiators. This finding is
particularly relevant to the safety of the deployment
of bargaining agents, or more generally agents that
can perform impactful actions in the real world,
as it highlights their risk of deceptive behaviour,
especially when they are optimised (e.g. by fine-
tuning) to pursue goals that incentivise strategic
misleading.
Limitations
In the investigation of bargaining scenarios we
made multiple design choices about the bargaining
protocol and model prompt strategies, mainly moti-
vated by the need to achieve a consistent and mean-
ingful set of experiments while remaining within
our budget of API and compute credits.
• The games are always one-shot4.
The joint-
training fine-tuning experiments partially com-
pensate for this, but we do not study any true
iterated game scenarios. We performed some pre-
liminary experiments with in-context adaptation
similar to Fu et al. (2023), but we found it to be
too expensive for large-scale experiments.
4While each bargaining scenario consists of multiple
rounds, agents have no memories of previous negotiations
across different scenarios.

• Training is limited to a single base model with
CoT reasoning disabled.
• We only study a bargaining protocol with simul-

Ethical Considerations
We evaluated and fine-tuned LLM-based agents,
discovering potentially unethical behaviours such
as strategic deception. Our experiments were all
conducted in simulated environments, hence they
did not affect the general public. The fine-tuning
methods are well-known hence their publication
does not pose any additional risks, instead, we
shine light on the fact that such risk can exist in
the wild when agents are trained and deployed to
maximise financial utility objectives.

D.1
Training setup
Buyer-side training uses the seller-unaware
transparency condition with T=4 rounds and
simultaneous-offer play; the seller is held fixed
at base Qwen3-8B (temperature 0.1) and only the
buyer is updated. Seller-side training mirrors this
with the buyer-unaware condition, the buyer fixed
and the seller updated. Joint self-play trains a sin-
gle LoRA adapter (rank 32) that is shared between
buyer and seller; at each rollout the trained policy
plays one role and the other agent (also the current
policy, via the same adapter) plays the opposing
role, with the per-scenario draw of (transparency,
player role) uniform over the eight cells; both sides
are sampled at T = 0.7 during training. The joint
runs include all stability fixes from the seller-side
runs plus an importance-sampling correction on the
policy ratio.
Each training step samples 64 scenarios with
K=16 rollouts per scenario. The scalar reward of
rollout i is the trained agent’s normalised utility,
ri = (vB−p)/(vB−vS) (buyer) or (p−vS)/(vB−
vS) (seller) when a deal is reached at price p, and 0
otherwise. The within-group rewards are replaced

to best (ties resolved by averaging the ranks they
would otherwise occupy), and the advantage is then
Ai = (ρi −¯ρ)/(σρ + ε). This bounded-magnitude
advantage stabilises the gradient against single-
rollout outliers; for K=16 the per-group mean rank
is always 8.5 and the per-group standard deviation
lies in [0,

of the underlying reward distribution. Both vari-

(β = 10−3). Appendix E reports a rank-vs-no-rank

D.2
Paired evaluation
We use a paired evaluation to eliminate scenario-
composition variance, which otherwise dominates
between-run comparisons at modest sample sizes.
Each evaluated checkpoint (GRPO, CISPO) and
its untrained-base counterpart are run on the same
scenarios with the same random seed and the
same number of trials per scenario (ntrials = 40),
guaranteeing identical (vB, vS) draws. We verify
reservation-price correspondence across all 1280
paired rows before reporting any statistic; differ-
ences are evaluated with paired t-tests (n ≈320
per transparency condition). The small number
of trials in which the deal price exceeds 5 vB, at-
tributable to malformed-offer outliers, are dropped.
For each variant we report the checkpoint with
the highest mean validation reward on a held-out
validation split. Seller-side evaluation uses tem-
peratures matched to seller-side training (buyer
T = 0.1, seller T = 0.7); joint evaluation uses
T = 0.7 on both sides against a fresh base-vs-base
reference at the same temperatures.

Ablation: Effect of the Rank Reward
Transform
The bargaining and deception results in Section 3.5
are obtained with the within-group average-rank
transform applied to the scalar reward before the
GRPO advantage normalisation. To check how
much the rank transform contributes to the head-
line numbers, we re-train both variants with the
rank transform disabled (raw signed utility entering
the advantage computation directly), keeping every
other hyperparameter and the rest of the training
pipeline identical. We then report the same paired
comparisons against the same base buyer.
Table 12 reports the matched-cell paired dif-
ferences, comparing the rank and no-rank ver-
sions of each loss against the base.
Remov-
ing the rank transform reduces the matched-cell
price effect by roughly 0.05 to 0.09 in abso-
lute terms and the buyer-utility lift by a similar
amount.
The rank transform contributes most
strongly to CISPO’s matched-cell honesty drop:
with rank, ∆buyer_honesty = −0.356; without rank,
−0.175, almost exactly half. For GRPO the hon-
esty drop is largely preserved without rank (−0.301
vs −0.345). The buyer-credulity-in-both-unaware
effect (Section 3.5, Table 8) attenuates without rank
to +0.070 for GRPO and +0.073 for CISPO, both
still significant (p < 0.05) but roughly 35% smaller
than the rank versions.
The seller-control profile under the no-rank ab-
lation is informative. For GRPO, all four seller
honesty and credulity controls are null without
rank (p > 0.05 in every cell), strictly cleaner
than the GRPO+rank profile (which had one cell
at p = 0.012). For CISPO, two of the four seller
controls remain significant without rank (seller hon-
esty in the both-unaware cell, p = 0.006; seller
credulity in the seller-unaware cell, p = 0.004).
