Abstract
Monotonic semantic path orders and weighted path orders are powerful reduction orders for proving
termination of term rewrite systems. In this paper we present their simple unification as reduction
orders and reduction pairs. We also discuss the use of it as ground total reduction orders.

Introduction
We present a generalization of monotonic semantic path orders (MSPOs) [2] and weighted
path orders (WPOs) [8]. In the FroCoS 2023 paper [7] we showed that WPOs as reduction
orders are instances of MSPOs. In this note we adopt the opposite approach: First we
introduce a generalization of WPOs dubbed GWPOs, and then show that MSPOs are
instances of GWPOs. We see that desirable properties of WPO carry over to GWPOs. Full
details on these contributions are found in the first author’s dissertation [6].
2
Reduction Orders
This section considers reduction orders, which are well-founded orders on terms that are
closed under contexts and substitutions. It is known that a TRS R is terminating if and
only if there exists a reduction order > with R ⊆>.
The basic ingredient of our unified orders (GWPOs) is reduction triples, originally
introduced for MSPOs. Formally, a triple (⩾, ⊒, ⊐) is a reduction triple if ⩾is a rewrite
preorder on terms, (⊒, ⊐) is a stable order pair on terms with ⊐well-founded, and ⊒is
harmonious to ⩾. Here, rewrite preorder means preorder (or quasi-order) closed under
substitutions and contexts; the stability condition means that ⊒and ⊐are both closed under
substitutions; and the harmony condition means that ti ⩾u implies f(t1, . . . , ti, . . . , tn) ⊒
f(t1, . . . , u, . . . , tn) for all n-ary function symbols f, terms t1, . . . , tn, u and integers 1 ⩽i ⩽n.
For example, given a reduction pair (⩾, >) (which is a key notion in the dependency
pair method [1]), the triple (⩾, ⩾, >) is a reduction triple. Also, given a weakly monotone
and well-founded algebra A (say, a linear polynomial interpretation on natural numbers),
the triple (⩾A, ⩾A, >A) is a reduction triple. Moreover, given a precedence ⪰, the triple
(T × T , ⊒, ⊐) is a reduction triple. Here, T × T is the universal relation relating every pair
of terms, and ⊒and ⊐are defined as comparison of head symbols w.r.t. ⪰.
We are ready to define the unified order GWPO. Note that it generalizes GWPO in the
sense of our earlier paper [7].

Now, let (⩾A, ⊒A, ⊐A) and (⩾B, ⊒B, ⊐B) be triples of relations on terms. The monotonic
generalized weighted path order (MGWPO) >MG is the intersection ⩾A ∩⩾B ∩>G where
>G is induced by (⊒A, ⊐A) and (⊒B, ⊐B).
Technically, we take the intersection ⩾A ∩⩾B ∩>G because >G may not be closed under
contexts. This technique is due to Borralleras, Ferreira and Rubio [2].
To simplify the presentation, we globally assume that the signature is finite. A relation ⇝
on terms is said to have the subterm property if s ⇝t holds whenever t is a proper subterm
of s.
▶Theorem 2. For reduction triples (⩾A, ⊒A, ⊐A) and (⩾B, ⊒B, ⊐B) with ⊒A having the
subterm property, the induced MGWPO >MG is a reduction order.
▶Example 3. We show the termination of the TRS R = {p(s(x)) →x, f(s(x)) →f(p(s(x)))}.
Let A and B be the algebras on N with pA(x) = fA(x) = x, sA(x) = fB(x) = sB(x) = x + 1,
and pB(x) = max{0, x −1}. Then (⩾A, ⩾A, >A) and (⩾B, ⩾B, >B) are reduction triples
and ⩾A has the subterm property. We conclude that R is terminating because the induced
MGWPO satisfies R ⊆>MG.
A WPO is parameterized by an algebra A (assumed weakly simple), and a precedence ⪰.
The WPO is identical to the MGWPO whose reduction triple A is induced from A and B is
induced by ⪰. (So actually, the subterm property of ⊒A assumed in Theorem 2 is an analog
of the weak simplicity of A required by the WPO.)
The same goes for the MSPO: An MSPO is parameterized by a single reduction triple
(⩾, ⊒, ⊐). The MSPO is identical to the MGWPO whose reduction triple A is the trivial one
(T × T , T × T , ∅) and B is (⩾, ⊒, ⊐). Equivalently, the MSPO can be defined as follows.

▶Theorem 5 ([2]). Every MSPO is a reduction order.
Conversely, MGWPOs can be seen as instances of MSPOs.
▶Theorem 6. Let >MG be the MGWPO induced by reduction triples (⩾A, ⊒A, ⊐A) and
(⩾B, ⊒B, ⊐B) with the subterm property of ⊒A. Then >MG and the MSPO >MS are identical,
where >MS is induced by (⩾A ∩⩾B, ⊒AB, ⊐AB) and (⊒AB, ⊐AB) is the lexicographic com-
bination of (⊒A, ⊐A) and (⊒B, ⊐B).

This is an analog of a main result of the FroCoS paper stating that WPOs are MSPOs [7].
In fact, this result can be traced back to Geser’s observation ([4, Theorem 5]) that a version
of the Knuth–Bendix order can be simulated by the semantic path order (SPO) [5].
What is the point of having the unified order? One way of defending our GWPO concerns
ground totality, which is a crucial property for theorem proving methods like the superposition
calculus. On one hand, every WPO >W satisfying totality of the precedence and the ordered
algebra is ground total, meaning that either s >W t or t >W s holds for all terms s ̸= t. On
the other hand, this does not go naturally for the MSPO. Based on this observation, we can
construct a distinctive ground-total MGWPO. For instance, we can construct a ground-total
MGWPO >MG orienting the equations of the combinators S and K:
(x · z) · (y · z) >MG ((S · x) · y) · z
(K · x) · y >MG x
Moreover, such an MGWPO can be found by employing the standard techniques for auto-
mating path orders. To our knowledge, this is not possible by existing constructions. See [6,
Section 3.3] for the details of the construction.
Another point, again crucial for theorem proving, is that the MGWPO provides a way to
optimize comparison by an MSPO: Given an instance >G of the GWPO, one can conclude
s >G t immediately from a strict decrease s ⊐A t, without recursive checks. This is not
possible for the simulating SPO >S, compare Definitions 1 and 4. Although this does not
improve worst-case complexity, one can actually improve it by further imposing the subterm
property on the strict order ⊐A.
▶Proposition 7. Let (⊒A, ⊐A) and (⊒B, ⊐B) be order pairs and >G the GWPO induced by
them. Suppose that ⊐A and ⊒A have the subterm property. Then s >G t if and only if
1. s ⊐A t, or
2. s = f(s1, . . . , sm) ⊒A g(t1, . . . , tn) = t, and one of the following conditions holds:
i. s ⊐B t, or
ii. s ⊒B t and (s1, . . . , sm) >lex
G (t1, . . . , tn)
Proposition 7 provides a definition of the GWPO in a similar fashion to the Knuth–Bendix
order (KBO). The KBO runs in linear time while the lexicographic path order (LPO) runs
in quadratic time (even with the best known algorithm). Combining these, we obtain a
procedure for deciding s >MG t in linear complexity in |s| + |t|, where the measure is the
number of comparisons by the base relations of the reduction triples A and B. This is not
immediate from the definition of the MSPO which is similar to the LPO.

Reduction Pairs
A reduction pair (⩾, >) is an order pair defined on terms such that ⩾is a rewrite preorder and
> is a well-founded order closed under substitutions. In its simplest form, the dependency
pair method states that a TRS R is terminating if and only if there is a reduction pair (⩾, >)
such that R ⊆⩾and DP(R) ⊆>, where DP(R) is the set of dependency pairs, see [1, 3].
Let us consider a reduction pair version of the MGWPO incorporating partial statuses, as
is done for the original WPO [8]. A partial status π is a function that maps each n-ary function
symbol to a list [i1, . . . , im] with 1 ⩽i1 < · · · < im ⩽n. For an n-ary function symbol f and
terms t1, . . . , tn, we write π(f)(t1, . . . , tn) for the list [ti1, . . . , tim] where π(f) = [i1, . . . , im],
and π is called total if π(f) = [1, . . . , n] for all n-ary function symbols f.

▶Example 14. Suppose that the signature only contains a unary function symbol f. Let π
be the partial status with π(f) = [ ], let A be the algebra on N defined by fA(x) = x + 1, and
let (⊒B, ⊐B) be an arbitrary pair of relations (e.g., one induced by a precedence to consider
the original WPO). On one hand, the GWPO (⩾G, >G) induced by (⩾A, >A) and (⊒B, ⊐B)
satisfies f(x) ⩾G x and f(x) >G x by case 1. On the other hand, no SPO (⩾S, >S) (regardless
of the underlying order pair) satisfies neither f(x) ⩾S x nor f(x) >S x, because only possible
case 1 is blocked by π(f) = [ ].
All in all, in the setting of Theorem 13 in which ⊒A is simple with respect to π, the
GWPO is always preferred over the SPO. In contrast, if the simplicity condition is not
satisfied, the SPO provides a way to construct a reduction pair (Corollary 11), by combining
the reduction triples A and B lexicographically into the single one. In other words, the SPO
allows us to virtually bypass the simplicity condition of Theorem 9.
In order to evaluate the GWPO and SPO as reduction pairs, we implemented a prototype
termination tool based on the dependency pair method and the presented reduction pairs.
The problem set for the experiment consists of 1528 finite TRSs from version 11.5 of the
Termination Problem Database, and one-minute time limit is set for the benchmark. Overall,
one obtains more termination proofs by switching from the original WPO to our methods,
see [6, Section 4.5] for the details. However, compared to state-of-the-art termination provers,
every problem solved by our tool is also solved by AProVE or NaTT. So the authors do not
know if any new problem can be solved by our methods.
To grasp the evaluation results, we pick up the following three kinds of reduction pairs:
the WPO induced by a linear polynomial interpretation and a precedence;
the GWPO (Theorem 9) induced by reduction triples A and B; and
the SPO (Corollary 11) induced by the lex combination of reduction triples A and B.

Here, A is induced by a linear polynomial interpretation on natural numbers, and for linear
polynomial interpretations A (including those for the WPO) we demand a1, . . . , an ∈{0, 1}
for each interpretation fA(x1, . . . , xn) = a0 + a1x1 + · · · + anxn. Only for the WPO and
the GWPO, we additionally impose simplicity with respect to the partial status. The
other reduction triple B is induced by a max/plus interpretation B on natural numbers in
which b1, . . . , bn ∈{0, 1} and c0 ⩾0 are required for each interpretation fB(x1, . . . , xn) =
max{c0, b1x1 + c1, . . . , bnxn + cn}. For B, the integers c1, . . . , cn are allowed to be negative.
So interpretations like fB(x) = max{0, x −1} belong to this class. We construct A and B for
the SPO and B for the GWPO in the way described before Example 12, using fresh symbols
f !. Note that this construction cannot be used for A of the GWPO due to the simplicity
requirement.
In this setting, the WPO, the GWPO, and the SPO (each of which is used with the
dependency pair framework) prove termination of 486, 591, and 595 systems, respectively.
Actually, the three configurations solve different sets of problems. There are two reasons:
In theory, the GWPO subsumes the WPO as the max/plus interpretation generalizes
precedence. However, due to the one-minute time limit imposed in our experiment, the
GWPO misses 7 problems handled by the WPO.
Already in theory, the SPO considered here is incomparable with the WPO and the
GWPO. In fact, the SPO does not require A to be weakly simple with respect to the
partial status. So Theorem 13 does not apply; see also Example 14.
