Universal Cycles on Affine Lines
Ming-Hsuan Kang, Shin-Hsun Chou
April 2026
Abstract
A universal cycle is a cyclic sequence in which each object of a
combinatorial family appears exactly once as a contiguous window.
While such cycles are well understood for many discrete structures and
linear subspaces, the case of affine lines presents additional difficulties
arising from parallelism.
We prove that universal cycles exist for affine lines in AG(n, q)
for all n ≥2 and all prime powers q. Our construction embeds the
problem into PG(n, q), using points at infinity to encode directions, and
proceeds via a decomposition into pairwise and triple configurations
combined with a recursive lifting and gluing argument.
We further interpret the construction in the Grassmannian Gq(2, n+
1), where affine lines correspond to the outer shell of 2-subspaces,
thereby extending known constructions for Grassmannians. A Python
implementation is provided as supplementary material.

Introduction
Universal cycles, introduced by Chung, Diaconis, and Graham [CDG92],
provide compact cyclic encodings of large combinatorial families. A univer-
sal cycle for a family F of discrete objects is a cyclic sequence in which every
object of F appears exactly once as a contiguous subsegment (or “window”).
The classic example is the de Bruijn cycle, which efficiently encodes all q-ary
words of length n. Since their introduction, universal cycles have been con-
structed for many combinatorial families, including subsets, permutations,
partitions, and designs; see, for example, [HJZ09, Knu11, Joh09]. These
constructions are closely related to Gray codes and combinatorial genera-
tion problems, where one seeks cyclic listings with prescribed local transition
rules; see [Sav97] for a general overview.
A major development in the geometric setting was initiated by Jack-
son [Jac09], who proved the first general existence theorem for universal

“regular” subspaces, distinguished by whether they contain a fixed coor-

such as [CKH25] provides explicit cycles using the multiplicative structure

While most existing results focus on linear subspaces or combinatorial
objects with global symmetry, the case of affine lines introduces additional
challenges arising from parallelism and the absence of a canonical origin.
In particular, affine lines naturally decompose into parallel classes, and this
structure is not directly compatible with the standard universal-cycle frame-
works.
In this paper, we investigate universal cycles for the family of affine
lines in finite affine space. Let AG(n, q) ∼= F n

affine space over Fq. An affine line ℓ⊂AG(n, q) is uniquely determined by

cycle problem asks whether such a sequence can represent every affine line
of AG(n, q) exactly once.
1.1
The Affine Obstruction and the Projective Extension
A fundamental obstruction arises if one attempts to construct such a cycle
using only affine points. Consider AG(n, 2). Since each line contains exactly
two points, a universal cycle for lines would be a cyclic ordering of F n
2
in which every unordered pair of distinct points appear exactly once as
an adjacent pair. Equivalently, this corresponds to an Eulerian circuit in
the complete graph K2n. Because all vertices of K2n have odd degree, no
Eulerian circuit exists. Thus, purely affine universal cycles are impossible
for q = 2.
To overcome this obstruction, we extend the vertex set to the projective
completion PG(n, q) by adjoining the hyperplane at infinity H∞.
Every
affine line ℓhas a unique point at infinity [ℓ] ∈H∞representing its direction.
Allowing these points at infinity to appear in the cycle provides the flexibility
needed to capture the parallelism structure of affine lines.

1.2
Main Result
Our main theorem establishes the existence of universal cycles for affine lines
in all finite dimensions.
Theorem 1.1 (Main Theorem). Let n ≥2 and q be any prime power.
There exists a universal cycle for the affine lines of AG(n, q), that is, a
cyclic sequence
C = (pi)i∈Z/NZ ⊂PG(n, q)
such that:
1. each window (pi, pi+1) represents a well-defined affine line of AG(n, q);
2. every affine line of AG(n, q) appears exactly once as such a window.
The vertex set may include both affine points and points at infinity, as re-
quired by the construction.
Our construction also admits a Grassmannian interpretation. Via a stan-
dard homogenization map, affine lines in AG(n, q) correspond precisely to
the outer shell of the Grassmannian Gq(2, n+1). In this sense, our universal
cycles extend the recursive framework originating in Jackson’s work for full
Grassmannians.
Our main contribution is to establish the existence of universal cycles for
affine lines in all dimensions. This is achieved through a projective extension
framework, together with a structural decomposition into pairwise and triple
constructions, unified via a recursive lifting mechanism.
To facilitate verification and reproducibility, we provide a Python imple-
mentation of the universal cycle construction as supplementary material on
arXiv. Portions of the exposition and implementation were developed with
the assistance of the AI tool ChatGPT.

Preliminaries
2.1
Segments, Cycles, and Universal Cycles
A finite sequence of points
(p1, . . . , pm+1) ⊂PG(n, q)
is called a double-window segment if:
1. each window (pi, pi+1) determines an affine line, and
2. these windows represent pairwise distinct affine lines.
We require p1 ̸= pm+1 so that the segment has two distinct endpoints. If
instead pm+1 = p1, the sequence is a double-window cycle.
Throughout, all uses of “segment” and “cycle” refer to double-window
objects.
Transversality.
Two segments or cycles are transversal if the sets of affine
lines they represent are disjoint. Equivalently, all windows appearing in the
two objects represent distinct lines.
Universal cycle.
Given a set F of affine lines, a double-window cycle is
universal for F if its windows represent exactly the lines in F.
2.2
Gluing Lemma
We record the two basic operations for assembling cycles from smaller pieces.
Transversality is always assumed.
Gluing cycles. If several transversal cycles share a common vertex, they
may be spliced at that vertex to form a single larger cycle.
Gluing segments. Let S be a family of transversal segments, each with
two distinct endpoints. For a vertex v, let µ(v) be the number of segments
in S having v as an endpoint.
If µ(v) is even for every vertex, then by
reversing some segments if needed, all segments can be concatenated into a
single double-window cycle.
These facts follow immediately from the standard parity condition for
joining finite paths, and we omit the proof.

2.4
Strategy of Construction
With the parametrization of fibers in place, our construction proceeds in
four logical steps:
1. Pairwise Construction: We first show that any two direction fibers
admit a universal cycle. This serves as the primary building block for
the majority of directions.
2. Recursive Lifting: We establish a dimension-reduction tool (the
Recursive Lifting Lemma), proving that a universal cycle on a subspace
can be naturally extended to the full affine space.
3. Triple Construction: Utilizing the lifting lemma, we show that any
three coplanar fibers admit a universal cycle.
This construction is
crucial for handling the parity constraint when the total number of
directions is odd.
4. Assembly: We partition the set of all directions based on the parity
of [n]q. Using the Gluing Lemma, we assemble the pairwise and triple
cycles into a single universal cycle covering all affine lines in AG(n, q).

3.2
The Recursive Lifting Lemma
To simplify the construction for arbitrary dimensions, we introduce a method
to extend universal cycles from a subspace to the entire affine space. Before
presenting the formal proof, we provide the geometric intuition behind this
reduction.
Geometric Intuition: The Stack of Layers
We may visualize the affine space F n
q as a “stack of layers,” where each
layer corresponds to a coset of the subspace U. Since the set of directions
D is contained entirely within U, any affine line with a direction in D is
confined to a single layer; it never traverses between layers. Consequently,
the problem initially decouples into independent sub-problems on each layer.
The key to the construction is that while the affine points are distinct
across layers, the points at infinity (the directions) are identical for all layers
in the projective completion. These shared vertices at infinity act as “pivots”
or “hinges.” Our strategy is to construct a cycle on the base layer, replicate
it identically on all other layers, and then utilize these shared pivots to stitch
the disjoint layer-cycles into a single connected structure covering the entire
space.
