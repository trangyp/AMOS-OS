We investigate the computational potential and limitations of a passive linear optical reservoir
with a photodetector at the optical-to-electrical interface as the sole source of nonlinearity.
In
contrast to conventional nonlinear reservoirs, where transient dynamics and delay jointly enhance
complexity and distribute nonlinear responses, the proposed linear architecture isolates these con-
tributions, as intrinsic nonlinear spreading is absent. We thus provide a framework that enables the
independent and systematic analysis of key factors, including nonlinear transformations, transient
dynamics, and time-delay effects, as well as their interactions. By explicitly identifying the con-
tributing monomials for different tasks, we establish the relationship between task requirements and
the nonlinearity provided by the system. Incorporating transient coupling and delayed feedback is
shown to significantly enhance performance and attractor reconstruction capabilities by compensat-
ing for missing higher-order nonlinearities through access to multi-step integration schemes. This
improvement, however, comes at the cost of requiring a larger number of virtual nodes.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Gradient descent training of recurrent neural networks
is slow and prone to find local optima.
Even though
there usually exists a very optimized, small, fully trained
network for each task, it is generally hard and time con-
suming to attain and the entire resulting network is solely
optimized to this specific task. This is in contrast to the
tantalizing biological example of the human brain, where
a single network efficiently participates in a great variety
of tasks [1] - often even simultaneously.
This inspired
the idea of reservoir computing (RC): A machine learn-
ing approach that utilizes a nonlinear dynamical system
and only trains an output layer.
A reservoir might be a network of interacting nonlinear
elements such as oscillators, optical nodes or biological
cells, or it could be continuous media with wave prop-
agation such as fluids, cellular automata, or magnetic
materials using resonance, interference and synchroniza-
tion [2]. A reservoir will respond differently, depending
on the type and order of input signals, just as each object
thrown into a pond of water would induce different pat-
terns on the surface [3], maintaining an echo or trace of
the input history in its current state. The reservoir can
be connected to different output units which are trained
to extract the required characteristic patterns from the
high-dimensional states of the reservoir for the task of
interest.
Usually the output weights are obtained via
linear regression between the target signal and a number
of reservoir outputs [4]. The central property of keeping
the reservoir unchanged during training, only optimizing

the output unit, is the key difference and advantage of
reservoir computers over traditional recurrent neural net-
works, greatly reducing the computational costs, while
also counteracting overfitting.
Appletant et al. [5] introduced another type of reser-
voir consisting of a single nonlinear node with time-
delayed feedback, where time-multiplexing can be used
to create interacting virtual nodes. This is achieved by
multiplying each input point with a mask of shape N×D,
where D is the dimension of the input point and inject-
ing each resulting linear combination of the input point
into the reservoir for a certain amount of time, resulting
in N virtual neurons or nodes. These virtual nodes play
an analogous role to the network nodes in a traditional
reservoir of the network type. The time between different
virtual nodes is the node separation θ and the total time
per input point is the input clock-cycle T = N · θ, see
Fig. 1 (a). As a result, the time-multiplexed single-node
reservoir, processing information in parallel in space at
the cost of temporal extension could be considered as
a contrary approach to the spatially extended network
or continuous media reservoir, processing information in
parallel in time at the cost of spatial extension, basi-
cally using spatial multiplexing. In the time-multiplexed
single-node case, interactions are induced by the time-
delay as well as the transient dynamics of the node itself,
if the virtual node separation in time θ is not too high.
This particular scheme paved the way for a wide range
of experimental implementations of reservoir computing.
Indeed, in some fields it is simpler to realize a single
node at the cost of time-multiplexing than a large net-
work of e.g., oscillators, in particular, if the information
processing is very fast, e.g. in optical systems. For this
reason, delay-based RC has gained wide popularity in the
field of nonlinear optics and photonics due to compara-

output layer as we shall discuss in detail later in this
work. Further enhancements of the delay reservoir com-
puting scheme have been proposed. In particular, Chen
et al. [11] discussed the use of a second feedback loop,
while Ref. 19 demonstrated parallel task processing in
a single vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL)
by making use of polarization multiplexing providing an-
other demonstration of the potential of a flexible, non-
specialized, reservoir.
Despite many successful experi-
mental RC realizations, a systematic understanding of
the different hyperparameters and how they influence
performance in RC is still an active topic of research.
For example it was shown that the ratio between the
input clock cycle, i.e., the duration of a single masked
input point, and the delay time might degrade the mem-
ory capacity in case of a resonance [20, 21], while in-
cluding task specific timescales can significantly improve
performance [22].
Additionally, widely used quantities
such as the memory capacity or the more general infor-
mation processing capacity, measuring the system’s abil-
ity to memorize and utilize inputs for nonlinear trans-
formations, were shown to be poorly correlated to the
performance in many cases [23]. Ultimately, while cer-
tainly justified through their usefulness, many results re-
main hard to interpret. Gauthier et al. [24], based on
the equivalence of reservoir computing to nonlinear vec-
tor autoregression, replaced the complex random network
with vectors of the present and past inputs (linear part)
as well as their products (nonlinear part) that are linearly
combined into a polynomial in the output layer. The re-
sulting scheme has a rather intuitive interpretation as it
can be compared to the concept of multi-step integration
schemes, using past values of the solution to better ap-
proximate the derivative in the interval of integration at
high orders of accuracy.

While nonlinear reservoirs

absent in the case of a linear reservoir. This allows for a
clearer look on the sole influence of these two effects and
to interpret more easily our results.

To better compare the two dynamical systems, the Chua

To create the reservoir computing tasks, the Lorenz63
and Chua system were numerically integrated using a
Runge-Kutta (4) method. For the Lorenz63 system the
integration time-step is dt = 0.001 and two tasks, Lorenz-
0.01 and Lorenz-0.1, are created by down-sampling the
resulting time series by successive decimations. For the
Chua oscillator, an integration time-step of dt = 0.01 is
employed and the time series is used without any down-
sampling. In all three cases, all dynamical variables are
supplied as input to the reservoir and the reservoir is
trained to predict one step ahead in each of the input
time series.
In the prediction phase, we operated the
trained reservoirs in an autoregressive manner.
Numerical integration of any continuous dynamical
system yields a time-discrete approximation in the form
of an iterated map derived from the original differential
equations. Consequently, a network trained on this task
effectively learns this discrete map, which may deviate
from the true continuous dynamics, for instance when
the time step is too large or the integration scheme is
of low order. Our approach of integrating the Lorenz63
system with a high order numerical method and a very
small time-step and then sampling differently the result-
ing output, creates two tasks that converge well to the un-
derlying dynamical system but also towards each other.
The latter feature is good for comparison while still pro-
ducing greatly different requirements for any neural net-
work approaching these tasks. Even though the under-
lying dynamical system contains no monomials beyond
quadratic order, the integration scheme produces higher
order terms whose scale separation depends on the inte-
gration time-step, or equally on the number of integration
steps with dt = 0.001 in between two data points.
We evaluated the reservoir’s performance on short to
intermediate predictions using the valid prediction time
(VPT). The VPT measures the short-term prediction
quality by calculating the point where a predicted trajec-
tory first deviates from the true trajectory. It is defined
as in Ref. 33 with the same threshold of 0.4.

III.
INFLUENCE OF TRANSIENT DYNAMICS
The quadratic nonlinearity induced by the photodiode
only allows for a limited amount of monomials for learn-
ing the task at hand. Our reservoir is trained to construct
the specific linear combination necessary for the polyno-
mial map connecting the points of our trainings data, or
rather any map that is sufficiently converging. Increasing
the number N of virtual nodes increases the number of
degrees of freedom for building the trained map, however,
it does not increase the number of monomials available
for constructing the map. In this section we shall intro-
duce and discuss the potential uses of including transient
dynamics in our model.

COMPARING ATTRACTOR
RECONSTRUCTION CAPABILITIES
Note that VPT values can vary greatly depending on
the random seed or the initial position along the attrac-
tor where the closed-loop simulation has been started.
To account for this, we have been using a median VPT
of a number of trained reservoirs, ranging from the low
to mid hundreds, also including multiple different initial
positions along the attractor. Yet, a high VPT does not
capture the models long-time behavior, i.e.
the capa-
bility of reproducing the target attractor. The learned
attractor could be deformed with respect to the target
or the closed-loop trajectory might settle on some pe-
riodic orbit or fixed point after a transient of arbitrary
length. Hence, in the final section, we shall introduce two
measures capturing the long-term behavior of the system.
To that end Ref. 36 introduced multiple measures, e.g.,
Total Variation (TVar) and Attractor deviation (ADev).
The TVar is an empirical approximation of the total vari-

function π(r) produced by the Lorenz63 system and the
probability density distribution of data produced by the
trained reservoir q(r). Here, ⃗r in case of the Lorenz63
system is a point in R3 and the probability density π(r)
captures how frequent r is visited by trajectories as the
Lorenz system is solved with Runge-Kutta (4) scheme.

VI.
CONCLUSION
We analyzed the computational potential and short-
comings of a passive linear optical system, using a pho-
todetector and thus a quadratic nonlinearity at the
optical-to-electrical interface as the only source of nonlin-
earity. By explicitly calculating the contributing mono-
mials for different tasks, we demonstrated the task re-
quirements and necessary nonlinearity to learn various
tasks. The nonlinear monomials appearing in the under-
lying dynamical system are always dominant in the task
and act as a lower limit for the nonlinearity required for
the reservoir computer. Secondary nonlinear contribu-
tions, induced by the integration algorithm, are reduced
in their influence by choosing a smaller time-step. The
maximum number of virtual nodes necessary is shown to
equal the number of monomials provided by the nonlin-
earity. For a dynamical system such as Lorenz63, where
the present state fully determines the future, no delay
and transient interactions are strictly necessary. Hence,
a passive and linear reservoir with a photodetector act-
ing as quadratic output layer, can perform well on a task
derived from the Lorenz63 system, given the time-step
is sufficiently small. Here, transient coupling and time-
delayed feedback can greatly increase the performance,
substituting the missing higher order monomial terms
due to the access to higher order multi-step methods
without the need of higher order nonlinearities. To fully
utilize this benefit, however, a greater number of virtual
nodes is necessary. While transient coupling and time-
delayed feedback both perform well at long-term attrac-
tor reconstruction our findings suggest that time-delayed
feedback performs better at short-term predictions, i.e.
yields higher VPT and lower NRMSE values. Meanwhile,
a task derived from a system such as the Chua oscilla-
tor with cubic nonlinearity, cannot be represented by the
system, unless artificial electronic nonlinearities of higher
order are supplied, even in the presence of transient or
time-delayed feedback coupling.
