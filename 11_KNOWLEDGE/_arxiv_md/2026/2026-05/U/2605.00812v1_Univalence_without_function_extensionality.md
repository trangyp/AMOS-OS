Abstract
It is a well-known theorem of homotopy type theory, originally due to Voevodsky, that function extensionality holds inside
any univalent universe. We consider a weaker variant of the univalence axiom, asserting that the wild category formed by
the universe is univalent, which we call categorical univalence. We show that categorical univalence does not imply function
extensionality by an analysis of Von Glehn’s polynomial model construction, which produces models of Martin-L¨of type
theory that always refute function extensionality. We find in particular that when the base model has a univalent universe,
its polynomial model has a universe that is categorically univalent but lacks function extensionality.
Keywords:
univalence, function extensionality, homotopy type theory, type theory, polynomial functor

Introduction
In 2010, Voevodsky [47,48] discovered that any universe of intensional Martin-L¨of type theory (ITT)
satisfying his univalence axiom also satisfies function extensionality: (dependent) functions between types
in the universe are equal as soon as they are homotopic.
This result became a foundational pillar of
Homotopy Type Theory / Univalent Foundations. For constructivists, it was an additional motivation
to justify univalence constructively—noted for example by Bezem, Coquand, and Huber [7]—given the
historical difficulty of integrating function extensionality with constructive type theory.
At the same time, the connection between univalence and function extensionality has always seemed
contingent.
It is unclear whether univalence implies extensionality principles for other negative type
formers, such as coinductive [49] or modal [24, Conjecture 11.2.2] types, which suggests functions might
be privileged simply because they appear in the statement of univalence. Furthermore, minor variations
on the univalence axiom are not known to imply function extensionality.
In a post on MathOverflow in 2013 [18], Fran¸cois G. Dorais proposed 4 one such variation. To con-
textualize Dorais’ axiom, let us first review the standard definitions. For functions f, g: Q

Part of the appeal of weak foundations is that they allow us to tease apart the components of mathe-
matics. Each type former of Martin-L¨of’s type theory has a distinct, well-defined purpose. Univalence fits
in uneasily in this picture. While it has beautiful consequences, it also has many consequences, and—like
impredicativity or the law of the excluded middle—it may be hiding finer structure beneath its surface.
By scratching at that surface, we hope to understand what makes univalence tick. The polynomial
model offers some motivation and a testing ground for weaker forms of the axiom. We are left with more
questions than answers; unlike in the case with FE, where superficial variations on univalence usually turn
out to be equivalent, here we find subtly distinct axioms with no canonical choice among them. Still, we
hope our results can provoke further reflection on the foundations of homotopy type theory.

Decomposing univalence
Our basic theory ITT is Martin-L¨of type theory with Σ types, Π types, intensional identity types, binary
coproduct types, empty and unit types, and one universe U closed under all of these type formers. 5 We
use the term strict equality and symbol ˚= for equality on the judgmental level; we use = for identity types.
We use ˚∼= for strict isomorphisms: two functions in opposite directions composing strictly to the identity.
Note that A ˚∼= B is not a type, and e: A ˚∼= B is merely a shorthand for a meta-level assumption. Besides
strict β rules for all type formers, we include strict η rules for Σ types, Π types, and the unit type.
For basic results, we cite Rijke’s book [36], which does not introduce FE until Chapter 13; we only
use results from earlier chapters. Crucially, we have basic facts about contractible types and that Σ types
respect equivalences in both arguments. Note that the analogous fact does not hold for Π types absent FE.
In contrast to Rijke [36], we assume the strict η rule for Σ types, not only for Π types. This means, for
example, that the equivalence witnessing the distributivity of Π types over Σ types is a strict isomorphism.
2.1
Univalent wild categories
The universe of ITT has the structure of an (∞, 1)-category, with type of objects U0 := U and type of
morphisms U1(A, B) := (A →B). The first layer of such an (∞, 1)-categorical structure is captured by
the Capriotti and Kraus’ notion of wild category [11, Definition 4.1].
Definition 2.1 A wild category 6 C is a type C0 and family of types x, y: C0 ⊢C1(x, y) equipped with
(i) composites g ◦f : C1(x, z) for all g: C1(y, z), f : C1(x, y),
(ii) identities idx : C1(x, x) for all x: C0,
(iii) associators αh,g,f : h ◦(g ◦f) = (h ◦g) ◦f for all h: C1(z, w), g: C1(y, z), f : C1(x, y), and
(iv) unitors λf : idy ◦f = f and ρf : f ◦idx = f for all f : C1(x, y).
If clear from context, we omit the subscripts when referring to the type of objects or family of morphisms.
We write x →y for C1(x, y) when C is clear, and we sometimes write gf for g ◦f.
Example 2.2 As noted above, the universe U has a wild category structure with U(A, B) := (A →B),
composition and identities given by the usual composition of functions and identity functions, and reflexive
equalities for the associators and unitors. More generally, for every type I there is a wild category UI whose
objects are families A: I →U and whose morphisms are indexed functions, UI(A, B) := Q
i:I A(i) →B(i).
These wild categories are really strictly coherent (∞, 1)-categories: the associators and unitors are
strict equalities and satisfy all higher coherence laws (e.g., the pentagon) up to strict equality. All of the
concrete wild categories we encounter in this article are of this kind.
Importantly, wild-categorical structure suffices to define isomorphism.
Definition 2.3 Given s: x →y and r: y →x in a wild category C, we say that r is a retraction of

Univalence of a universe U (UAU, Definition 1.2) cannot be formulated on the level of an arbitrary wild
category, as it refers to homotopy of functions. As UAU implies FEU, however, it also implies that U is
a univalent wild category. Absent FEU, the converse may fail: as we will see, U can be a univalent wild

2.2
Categorical equivalences and function extensionality
In comparing A ∼=U B and A ≃B, it is natural to forget about universes entirely. Recall from Section 1
that a function f : A →B between (possibly large) types is a categorical equivalence if it admits a section
and retraction, that is, s, r: B →A with fs = id and rf = id. We write is-ceq(f) for the type of witnesses
that f is a categorical equivalence, and A ∼= B for the type of categorical equivalences from A to B.
In ITT, the only closed categorical equivalences are the strict isomorphisms, such as A × B ˚∼= B × A.
With CUAU (Definition 1.3), there are more; for example, any e: A ∼= B in U yields (a =A a′) ∼= (ea =B ea′)
for a, a′ : A. The map ceq-to-eq: (A ∼= B) →(A ≃B), which converts equalities in function types to
homotopies, becomes an equivalence under FE. In fact, it is an equivalence only if FE holds.

Von Glehn’s polynomial model construction
We use Von Glehn’s polynomial model construction [50,33] to separate UAU from CUAU.
This model
construction is related [50, §5.2] [32, §7.1] to G¨odel’s Dialectica interpretation [23], versions of which have
been used to interpret various logics; see for example de Paiva’s categorical models of linear logics [17].
For our purposes, the relevant feature of the polynomial models is that they always refute FE.
By a model of type theory, we mean a category with attributes [13], category with families [19], or
natural model [6]: a presheaf of types TyC : Cop →Set and presheaf of terms TmC : (∫C TyC)op →Set.
Here we depart from Von Glehn, who works with non-split models (categories with display maps), instead
following Kov´acs’ Agda formalization of a version of the construction [30]. 7

Remark 3.4 Strict η laws for coproducts are often omitted from the syntax of type theory due to issues
with strict equality checking. In the simply-typed λ-calculus, strict equality checking for coproducts with
the η law is decidable but difficult. Ghani [22] addresses the case of binary coproducts; Scherer [37] handles
the empty type. In ITT, the η law for the empty type makes strict equality undecidable: with this law,
deciding whether a: A ⊢in0(⋆) ˚= in1(⋆): 1 + 1 requires deciding whether A implies 0. To our knowledge,
it is an open problem whether strict equality is decidable for ITT with binary coproducts and their η law;
see, for example, discussion between Shulman, Kov´acs, and others on Proof Assistants StackExchange [40].

6
Variations
Once UAU was proposed by Voevodsky, it was quickly taken up as the canonical axiom for its intended
purpose. Inequivalent variations on UAU usually turn out to be significantly weaker, as in case of the “iso-
morphism reflection” that holds in Bauer and Winterhalter’s cardinal model [51, §8.3], or else inconsistent,
as in the case of “qinv-univalence” [44, Exercise 4.6].
Unfortunately, we do not see evidence for a canonical form of “FE-free univalence”. In this section, we
show that a few possible candidates are inequivalent; none stands out as the most natural. In Section 6.1,
we show that CUAU does not imply CUA•
U. In Section 6.2, we identify an axiom CCUAU that also satisfies
ITT + CCUAU ⊬FEU and ITT + CCUAU + FEU ⊢UAU but is not equivalent to CUAU or CUA•
U.
In Section 6.3, we recall a variant of univalence used by Van den Berg [46, Definition 2.13] which we
call approximate univalence or UA∼
U . It is an open question whether UA∼
U implies FEU; we do not resolve
the question, but we pose a related question that avoids mention of a universe.

7
Related work
To conclude, we comment on the status of weak forms of univalence in other known models of type theory
without function extensionality.
7.1
Realizability models
Realizability is a standard source of models of ITT that refute extensionality principles, including FE; see
Streicher [42, Theorem 2.9, §3.7]. However, most work combining features of realizability and homotopical
semantics, such as that of Frumin and Van den Berg [20] and Uemura [45], constructs models that do
satisfy FE. An exception is Speight’s groupoidal realizability [41]; his function types have neither FE nor
the η rule. Speight constructs an impredicative universe of modest fibrations, but we do not know if this
or any other universe in the model satisfies some kind of univalence.
7.2
