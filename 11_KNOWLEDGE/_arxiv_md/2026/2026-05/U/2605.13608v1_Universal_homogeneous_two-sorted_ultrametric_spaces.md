Abstract
We view ultrametric spaces as two-sorted structures consisting of a set
of points and of a linearly ordered set of distances. We call the appropriate
notion of embeddings distance-carrying (dc- for short). Those are obtained by
combining isometries and linear order embeddings. We show that the class of
all finite two-sorted ultrametric spaces with dc-embeddings is Fra¨ıss´e, and that
the limit is the countable rational Urysohn ultrametric space U. The space U
is dc-universal for all countable ultrametric spaces, and its Cauchy completion
U is dc-universal for all separable ultrametric spaces, which is in contrast with
the situation of classical ultrametric spaces and isometric embeddings, where
no such universal space can exist.
We study further properties of U, of its variants, and of its automorphism
group, which is richer than its group of isometries. In particular, we provide
two types of tree representations of the two-sorted ultrametric spaces, discuss
connections to valued fields, and characterize the automorphism group of U as
the semidirect product of a group of order preserving bijections and a group
of isometries. Furthermore, we show universality of Aut(U) and identify its
universal minimal flow.
Keywords:
ultrametric spaces, representations, universality, Fra¨ıss´e theory, auto-
morphism groups

Introduction
A metric space (X, d) is called an ultrametric space if for all x, y, z ∈X, the ultra-
metric triangle inequality holds: d(x, y) ≤max{d(x, z), d(y, z)}. Consequently, for
any x, y, z ∈X, either d(x, y) = d(x, z) = d(y, z) (the triangle is equilateral), or,
if we assume without loss of generality that d(x, y) is the minimum, it holds that
d(x, y) < d(x, z) = d(y, z).

The (open) ball of center x ∈X and radius r > 0 in an ultrametric space is
the set Br(x) = {y ∈X : d(y, x) < r}. Analogously, we define the closed ball as
¯Br(x) = {y ∈X : d(y, x) ≤r}. Note that for any two balls B and C, they are either
disjoint (B ∩C = ∅) or one is contained in the other (B ⊆C or C ⊆B). Moreover,
every point in a ball can serve as its center.
Example 1.1.
(1) The Baire space NN = {x: N →N} equipped with the metric
d(x, y) = max{2−n : x(n) ̸= y(n)}, for x ̸= y, is an ultrametric space.
(2) A valued field (with a countable value group) gives rise to an ultrametric space.
For example, consider the p-adic numbers Qp, where p is a prime. Any rational
number x ̸= 0 can be uniquely represented as pkr
s , where k, r, s ∈Z, s > 0,
and r, s are not divisible by p. We define the norm |x|p = p−k and the distance
d(x, y) = |x −y|p, letting Qp be the completion of the rationals with respect
to this metric d.
We are interested in universal ultrametric spaces. In the context of all metric
spaces, there exists a separable metric space into which every separable metric space
embeds isometrically. Standard examples include the space of continuous real-valued
functions C([0, 1]) equipped with the supremum norm or the Urysohn metric space,
the latter being also (ultra)homogeneous, i.e. every isometry between its finite sub-
spaces extends to an isometry of the whole space.
For ultrametric spaces, however, the situation is diametrically different: there is
no separable ultrametric space that is universal for all separable, or even just finite,
ultrametric spaces. In 1994, Vestfrid [33] constructed an ultrametric space universal
for all separable ultrametric spaces, but that space is not separable. It is defined as
U = {(xn) ∈(R≥0)N : xn+1 ≤xn for all n and (xn) converges to 0},
where for x ̸= y, we let d(x, y) = max{xk, yk}, with k being the least index such
that xk ̸= yk.
Furthermore, A. J. Lemin and V. A. Lemin [21] proved that there is no ultra-
metric space of weight < c into which every separable ultrametric space embeds
isometrically. The authors also provided an example of a space with weight c:
LN = {f : Q>0 →N: f is eventually 0}
equipped with the distance
d(f, g) = supR≥0{x ∈Q>0 : f(x) ̸= g(x)}
for f ̸= g. The space LN is complete and universal for ultrametric spaces of cardi-
nality < c. Let us just mention that replacing N with a cardinal number κ in the
definition above, Lemins obtained the space Lκ, which is universal for all ultrametric
spaces of weight ≤κ, and |Lκ| = w(Lκ) = κℵ0.

If we restrict ourselves to a countable set of distances 0 ∈E ⊆R≥0, a universal
space does exist. In that case,
UE = {(xn) ∈EN : xn+1 ≤xn for all n and (xn) converges to 0}
equipped with d(x, y) = max{xk, yk}, with k being the least index such that xk ̸= yk,
for x ̸= y, is a separable, complete E-valued ultrametric space into which all E-
valued ultrametric spaces embed isometrically, as shown in the work of Gao and
Shao [9]. This construction was inspired by that of Vestfrid. All these examples
admit a unified treatment, as sketched in Remark 3.25.
The reason why there is no universal separable ultrametric space X lies in too
strict treatment of distances: for every countable dense subspace D ⊆X and two
points x ̸= y ∈X there are points x′, y′ ∈D with d(x, x′), d(y, y′) < d(x, y). Hence,
by the ultrametric triangle inequality we have d(x, y) = d(x, y′) = d(x′, y′), and so
d(x, y) is one of the countably many distances attained by D. Therefore, X does
not contain even all two-point spaces, and if we wish to find a universal separable
ultrametric space, we must disregard exact numerical values for distances.
We work with ultrametric spaces viewed as two-sorted structures where the met-
ric takes values in a linearly ordered set of distances. We also consider appropriate
two-sorted embeddings, called dc-embeddings, preserving the orders of the distance
sets. Using Fra¨ıss´e theory, we will construct such a two-sorted ultrametric space.
The Fra¨ıss´e limit U will be countable and universal for all countable two-sorted ul-
trametric spaces. Furthermore, since a (uniformly continuous) dc-embedding from
one ultrametric space to another extends uniquely to a dc-embedding between their
Cauchy completions, the completion U will be universal for all separable two-sorted
ultrametric spaces. More broadly, in the paper we embark on a systematic study of
two-sorted ultrametric spaces, ways of representing them, methods of constructing
universal objects, and properties of their automorphism groups.
In a recent related work by Gheysens, Pavlica, Pech, Pech, and Schneider [10],
the authors consider structures with a quaternary relation expressing when points
in a pair are closer to each other than points in another pair, without explicitly
referring to any distances. They call such structures echeloned spaces, and among
other results prove that finite echeloned spaces form a Fra¨ıss´e class, that their order
expansions form a Ramsey class, and that the automorphism group of the Fra¨ıss´e
limit is universal (via a Katˇetov functor). Even though echeloned spaces are sub-
stantially different from ultrametric spaces, some of the results are parallel to ours.
We compare both structures in more detail in Remark 3.12.
In a follow-up paper [2] we prove the existence of a generic dc-automorphism
of U and the non-existence of a generic pair of dc-automorphisms. We achieve the
former result by showing cofinal amalgamation property of partial automorphisms
and characterizing amalgamation bases. Moreover, we develop a general strategy for
showing cofinal amalgamation property for a broad class of categories. Additionally,
we carry out a detailed analysis of possible structures of an orbit of a point under a
dc-automorphism.

1.1
Summary of the results
Initially motivated by the study of universal ultrametric spaces, this work provides a
construction of a generic universal ultrametric space and investigates its completions
and their automorphism groups. Parallel to these results, we develop several tools of
independent interest such as principles for transferring Fra¨ıss´e-theoretic properties
between categories, and representations of ultrametric spaces via certain categories
of trees and ball spaces and via spaces of functions. While this is partially present in
the mathematical folklore, we significantly extend the tools and provide a systematic
and unified treatment. We now summarize the variety of obtained results for the
reader’s convenience.
Section 2 establishes the general framework for studying two-sorted ultrametric
spaces, including two ways to represent them as trees, and for studying their auto-
morphism groups. In Section 3 we construct the universal homogeneous two-sorted
ultrametric space U and several related Fra¨ıss´e limits, and we investigate its comple-
tions. Section 4 compares the automorphism group Aut(U) with the isometry group
Iso(U) and discusses universality of Aut(U) via Katˇetov functors.
Preliminaries (Section 2). We introduce all key notions, present our setup, and
collect general observations for later use.
• Despite working with concrete possibly many-sorted structures, we use a basic
category-theoretic framework to systematically organize and compare classes
of structures we are interested in. This includes notation for induced categories
and of finite structures and of partial automorphisms.
• Ultrametric spaces are introduced as two-sorted structures, where the first
sort consists of points and the second of distances in a linear order. Maps,
called dc-embeddings, are not necessarily isometric embeddings, we only re-
quire that they preserve the order between distances. This does not always
imply (uniform) continuity. We provide a characterization (Lemma 2.13).
• The Cauchy completion X of a two-sorted ultrametric space X behaves as
expected, and in particular has the universal property for uniformly continuous
dc-embeddings (Observation 2.14).
• For any ultrametric space X, we endow Aut(X) with two topologies of point-
wise convergence depending on whether we view X as a discrete or metric
structure – the latter is denoted by Autm(X). If X is separable without iso-
lated points, then the adjacent balls functor B (Proposition 2.7) induces an
isomorphism of Polish groups Autm(X) →Aut(BX) (Proposition 2.19).
Generic ultrametric spaces (Section 3). We construct the universal homoge-
neous two-sorted ultrametric space U as a Fra¨ıss´e limit of the class of finite two-sorted
ultrametric spaces.

Preliminaries
2.1
General framework
As we shall discuss several different (but related) types of structures as well as
several types of structure-preserving maps, we shall adopt the language of category
theory to treat the matter in a systematic way. Recall that a category C consists
of a family of objects (sometimes called C -objects) and of a composable family of
morphisms (sometimes called arrows or C -maps), i.e. for every C -maps between
C -objects f : X →Y and g: Y →Z, we have the composition g ◦f : X →Z. The
composition is associative. We also have the identity morphism idX : X →X for
every C -object X that is neutral with respect to composition.
Recall that a subcategory D ⊆C is a category consisting of some C -objects and
some C -maps between them, with the same composition. A subcategory D ⊆C
is called full if we restrict only objects, i.e. if every C -map between D-objects is
a D-map, and a subcategory D ⊆C is called wide if it has the same objects as
C , i.e. if we are restricting only the morphisms. We will use some other standard
category-theoretic notions such as functors, natural transformations, equivalences of
categories, and colimits. See a standard reference such as [22] for details.
Our categories consist of model-theoretic structures in a fixed signature (some-
times many-sorted, but we use only finitely many sorts), and of all embeddings.
We call them categories of structures. For every category of structures C , the full
subcategory of all finite structures is denoted by Cfin. Also, there is a notion of a sub-
structure, i.e. of an embedding that is an inclusion on the level of sets. The notation
is X ≤Y , meaning that X is a substructure of Y and that Y is an extension of X.
Arguments are sometimes simplified by replacing embeddings with substructures.
This is without loss of generality because, up to isomorphism, every embedding is
an inclusion.
Recall that in general an isomorphism in a category C is a C -map f : X →Y
such that there is C -map f −1 : Y →X satisfying f −1 ◦f = idX and f ◦f −1 = idY .
In a category of structures, it is equivalently a C -map that is bijective (in every
sort) as C -maps are embeddings. Isomorphisms X →X are called automorphisms.
For every C -object X the automorphism group of X is denoted by Aut(X).
Remark 2.1. Some general results we will use, such as the characterization of ex-
istence of a generic automorphism due to Kerchis–Rosendal [17] or the Kechris–
Pestov–Todorˇcevi´c correspondence [16], are stated for classes of one-sorted structures
(with both functions and relations). This is not a problem for us as a many-sorted

structure can be turned into a one-sorted structure while preserving the notion of
embedding, and hence any category of structures is equivalent to a category of one-
sorted structures.
Let C be a category of finite structures. For every C -object A a partial auto-
morphism of A is a C -isomorphism p such that dom(p), rng(p) ≤A, i.e. an iso-
morphism between substructures of A. An embedding of partial automorphisms
f : ⟨A, p⟩→⟨B, q⟩is a C -map f : A →B such that f ◦p = q ◦f on dom(p),
from which it follows that f restricts to (unique) C -maps dom(p) →dom(q) and
rng(p) →rng(q). We denote the category of partial automorphisms in C and their
embeddings by C ∗. We can view C ∗as a category of structures. Indeed, if struc-
tures in C are one-sorted, we turn ⟨A, p⟩into a three-sorted structure with sorts
A, dom(p), rng(p), and we add the embeddings dom(p) →A and rng(p) →A as well
as both p and p−1 as operations. If C is many-sorted, we do this for every sort.
2.2
Two-sorted ultrametric spaces
By L we denote the category of all linear orders ⟨L, ≤L⟩and all embeddings, i.e.
maps f : L →L′ such that x ≤L y if and only if f(x) ≤L′ f(y). It is well-known
that Lfin is a Fra¨ıss´e class, whose limit is the linearly ordered set of rationals Q. (We
recall Fra¨ıss´e theory in Section 3.)
We shall consider ultrametric spaces with linear orders as sets of distances. First,
let L0 denote the category of linear orders ⟨L, ≤L, 0L⟩with the least element 0L and
of embeddings of linear orders preserving the least element. As usual, the triple
⟨L, ≤L, 0L⟩is often denoted just by L, and ≤L and 0L are often denoted just by ≤
and 0, respectively. Clearly, L0 is a category equivalent to L.
We propose to consider ultrametric spaces as two-sorted structures of the form
⟨X, dX, DX⟩, where X is a set of points, DX is a set of distances, i.e. an L0-object,
and dX : X × X →DX is an ultrametric. To be more specific, d = dX is symmetric,
d(x, x′) = 0 if and only if x = x′, and d satisfies the ultrametric triangle inequality
d(x, x′′) ≤max{d(x, x′), d(x′, x′′)}
for x, x′, x′′ ∈X.
Again, we shall denote dX by d and ⟨X, dX, DX⟩by ⟨X, DX⟩or just X whenever
convenient. We shall refer to the two sorts as the point sort and the distance sort,
respectively, when convenient.
A distance-carrying embedding f : ⟨X, dX, DX⟩→⟨Y, dY , DY ⟩(dc-embedding for
short) is a one-to-one map f : X →Y together with an L0-map Df : DX →DY such
that
dY (f(x), f(x′)) = Df(dX(x, x′))
for x, x′ ∈X.
Note that an isometric embedding corresponds to a dc-embedding f : X →Y such
that Df is the inclusion DX ≤DY , i.e. we have dY (f(x), f(x′)) = dX(x, x′).
Let U denote the category of ultrametric spaces and distance-carrying embed-
dings. Clearly the assignment (f : X →Y ) 7→(Df : DX →DY ) yields a functor

2.2.1
Tree representation of ultrametric spaces by precise balls
It is well known that ultrametric spaces correspond to trees of balls. We will make
it precise in our setup and we will introduce notation used later.
Let us fix an ultrametric space X. By a ball in X, we mean any subset B ⊆X
that is an open ball, i.e. a set of the form Br(a) = {x ∈X : d(x, a) < r} for some
a ∈X and r ∈DX \ {0}, or a closed ball, i.e. a set of the form ¯Br(a) = {x ∈
X : d(x, a) ≤r} for some a ∈X and r ∈DX. Note that neither the center nor
the radius is unique for a general ball (in fact, every point of a ball works as its
center in an ultrametric space). A precise ball is a closed ball of the form ¯B(a, b) =
¯Bd(a,b)(a) = {x ∈X : d(a, x) ≤d(a, b)}, or equivalently a ball B that attains its
9

diameter diam(B) = max{d(x, y) : x, y ∈B}. In particular, every singleton {a} is a
precise ball of diameter 0.
Note that every two balls B, B′ are either disjoint, or one is contained in the
other. In the former case, we put d(B, B′) = d(x, x′) for any x ∈B and x′ ∈B′, which
does not depend on the choice of the points. In the latter case, we put d(B, B′) = 0,
so in both cases we have d(B, B′) = min{d(x, x′) : x ∈B, x′ ∈B′}.
By a tree we mean a partially ordered set T such that for every t ∈T the down-
set (←, t] = {x : x ≤t} is linearly ordered. This differs from the classical definition
in set theory, where one requires down intervals to be well-ordered. T is a meet tree
if additionally for every t, t′ ∈T the meet t ∧t′ = inf{t, t′} exists. Let T denote the
category of meet trees and meet-preserving embeddings. For every t ∈T we have
the equivalence relation ≈t defined by s ≈t s′ if s ∧s′ > t that partitions the up-set
(t, →) = {x: x > t} into equivalence classes, which we call branching classes. To see
the transitivity of s ≈t s′ ≈t s′′ note that s∧s′′ ≥min{s∧s′, s′∧s′′} > t since (←, s′]
is linearly ordered. If T is finite, then branching classes have immediate successors
of t as smallest elements.
A leveled meet tree is a triple ⟨T, ℓT, Lev(T)⟩such that T is a meet tree, Lev(T)

We shall recall notions from Fra¨ıss´e theory and adapt them to our context. Classical
