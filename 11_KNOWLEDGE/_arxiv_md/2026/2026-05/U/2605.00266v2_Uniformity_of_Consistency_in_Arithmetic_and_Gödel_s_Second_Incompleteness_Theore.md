UNIFORMITY OF CONSISTENCY IN ARITHMETIC AND GÖDEL’S SECOND
INCOMPLETENESS THEOREM: EIN MÄRCHEN
HARALD GROBNER
Abstract. In much discussed work Artemov has recently shown that, for PA, the consistency schema admits
a form of uniform verification via selector proofs, despite the unprovability of the corresponding uniform
consistency sentence Con(PA). In this note, we recast that this phenomenon extends to all sufficiently strong
arithmetizable theories: For such theories T, there exists a primitive recursive selector producing proofs of
all instances of the associated consistency schema. This results – a soft version of a classical result of Pudlák
– yields a form of computational uniformity, despite the fact that it cannot be internalized as the uniform
consistency sentence of Gödel’s Second Incompleteness Theorem. Our main goal is to analyze this gap and
to locate selector proofs within the broader framework of provability and reflection.
Introduction
Gödel’s Second Incompleteness Theorem (2.UVS1), cf. [Göd31], is commonly taken to establish a definitive
limitation on formal theories: No sufficiently strong, recursively axiomatized, consistent theory T, based on a
system of axioms L, can prove its own consistency, provided that consistency is expressed in the standard
arithmetical form
Con(T) ≡∀x ¬ProofT (x, ⌜0 = 1⌝).
This formulation has played a central role in the interpretation of Hilbert’s program and in subsequent debates
about the epistemological significance of consistency proofs, cf. [Res74, Aue78, Aue89, Ste91]. The standard
conclusion, drawn from Gödel’s 2.UVS, is that a theory capable of formalizing a sufficiently rich fragment of
arithmetic cannot, by its own resources, certify the absence of a derivation of contradiction from its own axioms.
There is, however, a complementary perspective on this limitation, arising from the analysis of serial
properties and their formalization, leading to a form of uniform provability which is not captured by a single
universal sentence: For each standard natural number n, let
Conn(T) ≡¬ProofT (n, ⌜0 = 1⌝),
and consider the associated consistency schema
ConS(T) := {Conn(T) : n ∈N}.
It was shown in [Art19, Art25a] that, for L = PA, this schema is provable in the sense of “selector proofs”:
More precisely, there exists a primitive recursive procedure producing, for each n ∈N, a proof of Conn(PA).
Thus, while the universal sentence Con(PA) is unprovable, the corresponding series admits a form of uniform
computational witnessing. Otherwise put, PA proves, in the selector sense, its own associated consistency series.

for how close or how far this attempt must come to failure.
Indeed, in that spirit, and taken all together, the above results suggest a refinement of the usual (rather
apodictic) interpretation of Gödel’s 2.UVS: The relevant boundary is not simply between provability and
unprovability of consistency statements within a given theory, but between two distinct forms of uniformity:
On the one hand, there is a form of computational uniformity, embodied – implicitly by Pudlák’s results on
proof-complexity and explicitly by selector proofs – in which a single effective procedure produces proofs of
all instances of a given property (proofs, which are in this sense uniform). On the other hand, there is the
ordinary uniform consistency assertion, expressed by the single universal arithmetical sentence Con(T) or by
a reflection principle.
Selector proofs show that the former can exist even when the latter is provably unattainable. In this
sense, they isolate a genuine intermediate level of proof: stronger than mere and trivial instantwise verifica-
tion, yet strictly weaker than uniform reflection. In particular, the limitation identified by Gödel’s 2.UVS is
not a limitation on the existence of uniform computational witnesses, but on the passage from such witnesses
to the ordinary universal consistency sentence. From this perspective, the significance of schema-based
constructions clearly lies not in challenging Gödel’s 2.UVS, but in revealing a form of uniform computational
content that does not collapse into uniform reflection.

1. Standing assumptions and minor recaps
1.1. First-order theories. Unless otherwise stated, in this note, T denotes a recursively axiomatized theory,
formulated in first-order predicate logic (out of a system L of axioms, say), extending a weak arithmetic base
sufficient for elementary syntactic coding. By the latter we shall (at least) mean elementary arithmetic EA in
the sense of I∆0 + Exp, i.e. Robinson arithmetic together with induction restricted to ∆0-formulas and the
totality of exponentiation. However, for stronger metamathematical arguments, in particular those involving
derivability conditions and reflection principles, we work over IΣ1, i.e., arithmetic with induction restricted
to Σ1-formulas and we shall indicate this in the statement of our results.
1.2. Basics on provability and consistency. Given a system of axioms L with theory T as in Sect. 1.1,
we choose and fix a standard Gödel numbering and a standard proof predicate
ProofT (p, x),
where ProofT (p, x) expresses that p codes a T-derivation of the formula with Gödel number x. The very
details in its formalization as an expression of L are assumed to be fixed once and for all throughout the
paper.
Definition 1.1. The associated provability predicate is
ProvT (x) ≡∃p ProofT (p, x).

The standard consistency sentence is
Con(T) ≡¬ProvT (⌜0 = 1⌝),
i.e., Con(T) ≡∀p ¬ProofT (p, ⌜0 = 1⌝).
Lemma 1.2. For a standard arithmetization of syntax, ProofT (p, x) is elementary, indeed primitive recursive,
and hence representable in EA. In common codings it may be taken to be ∆0 after the usual coding of bounded
computations.
Proof. This is the standard representability theorem for primitive recursive relations. (Verification that a
finite sequence is a formal derivation is obtained by checking, for each line, whether it is an axiom or follows
from earlier lines by one of finitely many rules. Since the axiom set L of T is recursive, this verification is
primitive recursive relative to the chosen recursive axiom predicate, and in the usual elementary presentations
it is representable already in EA. See, for example, [Háj-Pud93, Chp. I].)
□
When T extends a sufficiently strong fragment of arithmetic, such as IΣ1 (and, for standard codings, already
EA in the usual presentations), the standard Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, underlying the
usual proof of Löb’s theorem and Gödel’s 2.UVS, hold for the standard provability predicate ProvT , assuming
a standard coding of syntax, see, e.g., [Hil-Ber39, Löb55], [Háj-Pud93, Ch. I–II], and [Smo85]. For weaker
base theories, such as Robinson arithmetic Q, these conditions may fail, in particular the third condition
(D3):
(D1) If T ⊢φ, then T ⊢ProvT (⌜φ⌝).
(D2) T ⊢ProvT (⌜φ →ψ⌝) →(ProvT (⌜φ⌝) →ProvT (⌜ψ⌝)).
(D3) T ⊢ProvT (⌜φ⌝) →ProvT (⌜ProvT (⌜φ⌝)⌝).
Remark 1.3 (Jeroslow’s trick). The third derivability condition (D3) is not strictly necessary for the
derivation of Gödel’s 2.UVS. Following Jeroslow, cf. [Jer71], one may replace the standard consistency
statement by a slightly modified formula for which the second incompleteness theorem can be established
using only (D1) and (D2). Thus, the essential content of the theorem does not depend on the full strength of
the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb conditions.
Definition 1.4. For each standard n ∈N, define the n-th consistency instance
Conn(T) ≡¬ProofT (n, ⌜0 = 1⌝).
The associated consistency schema is the external family
ConS(T) := {Conn(T) : n ∈N}.
It is important that ConS(T) is not a single formula of the object language. It is a meta-level family of
formulas indexed by the standard natural numbers.
Proposition 1.5. For each standard n ∈N, the statement ProofT (n, ⌜0 = 1⌝) is decidable by a finite
computation formalizable in EA. In particular, if T is consistent and extends EA, then
T ⊢Conn(T).
Proof. By Lem. 1.2, the predicate ProofT (p, x) is primitive recursive and hence decidable on each fixed
standard input. Thus, for each standard n, EA proves either ProofT (n, ⌜0 = 1⌝) or its negation. If T is
consistent, then no standard n codes a T-proof of 0 = 1, so the second case holds. Hence EA ⊢Conn(T), and
therefore T ⊢Conn(T) since T extends EA.
□
Quite obviously, the assertion “∀n T ⊢Conn(T)” is not by itself a meaningful consistency notion: If T is
inconsistent, then T proves every sentence, including instances Conn(T) which are false, when interpreted in
the standard model N. The relevant point is rather that, when T is consistent, each Conn(T) is true in N
and is moreover finitely verifiable, and hence provable in T.

Theorem 1.6. Let T be as in Sect. 1.1 and assume in addition that it is a consistent extension of IΣ1 with
the standard provability predicate. Then, the theory
T + ¬Con(T) + ConS(T)
is consistent. In other words, there is a model M |= T such that M |= Conn(T), for every standard n ∈N, but
M |= ¬Con(T), or, equivalently, M |= ∃x ProofT (x, ⌜0 = 1⌝). Any such witness is necessarily non-standard.
Proof. Since T ⊬Con(T) by Gödel’s 2.UVS, the theory T + ¬Con(T) is consistent. Moreover, each standard
instance Conn(T) is provable in T by Prop. 1.5. Therefore adjoining any finite subset of {Conn(T) : n ∈N}
to T + ¬Con(T) preserves consistency. By compactness, the full theory is consistent and (hence) has a model
M. In M, all standard instances hold by construction, while ¬Con(T) also holds. Therefore, if a ∈M
witnesses ProofT (a, ⌜0 = 1⌝), a cannot be the interpretation of any standard numeral n.
□
This theorem gives the semantic reason why the schema does not collapse to the universal sentence: The
schema says that each standard numeral fails to be a contradiction proof. The universal sentence says that
every element of every model fails to be such a proof code. Non-standard models separate these requirements:
Whereas the consistency schema controls standard proof codes only, the universal sentence also controls
non-standard objects in non-standard models.
2. Selector based proofs of consistency
2.1. Weak Syntactic Bases and Selector Proofs. The previous section treated ConS(T) as an external
family. Artemov’s formulation goes further, cf. [Art01]: A proof of a serial property is not merely the assertion
that each member is separately provable, but a selector together with a verification that the selector returns a
proof of the required instance. To this end, we adopt an arithmetized version of Artemov’s notion of selector
proof. More precisely, we formalize Artemov’s notion of selector proof in terms of the arithmetized proof
predicate ProofT in the following

Remark 2.4 (Pudlák’s finitistic consistency statements). Theorem 2.3 gives a genuine and self-contained
selector proof in the sense of Def. 2.1. Its proof-theoretic core, however, is closely related to, and in the cases
of interest translatable from Pudlák’s work on finitistic consistency statements [Pud86], Thm. 5.5. Pudlák
considers, for an axiomatization A, formulas of the form ConA(m), expressing that there is no A-proof of
contradiction of length at most m. Thus ConA(m) is a bounded-length consistency statement, whereas our
instance
Conn(T) ≡¬ProofT (n, ⌜0 = 1⌝)
is a code-wise statement about one particular alleged proof code.
For sequential theories and for suitable presentations of their axiomatizations, Pudlák obtains polyno-
mial upper bounds on the lengths of proofs of such finitistic consistency statements. In particular, rightly
interpreted, his results yield polynomially bounded proofs of the corresponding bounded consistency state-
ments of PA and ZF, see [Pud86], p. 192. It is therefore implicit, that for theories covered by Pudlák’s
hypotheses, his bounded-length result implies the existence of primitive recursive proof-producing functions
for the individual code-wise instances: Given a proof code n, choose a length bound m large enough to cover
the proof coded by n, use the proof of ConA(m), and then infer that n itself is not a proof of contradiction. In
this sense, Pudlák’s theorem contains the proof-complexity substance of the selector phenomenon considered
by Artemov, [Art19, Art25a], and indeed in a quantitative form.

3. Concluding remarks
3.1. A reflection on Reflection. Let us locate the schema/uniformity distinction in relation to reflection
hierarchies. Reflection principles and their iterated forms play a central role in the proof-theoretic analysis of
arithmetic. They provide a systematic way of measuring the strength of theories in terms of their internal
soundness properties. The resulting hierarchies of theories, obtained via iterated reflection or consistency
extensions, have been studied extensively, in particular in the work of Beklemishev [Bek95, Bek05, Bek10].
A closely related perspective is given by provability algebras and polymodal provability logics, where such
hierarchies are analysed in a modal framework, allowing for a fine calibration of reflection principles and
their iterations (see also [Vis01]). In particular, in Beklemishev’s GLP-framework, one considers a family of
modal operators, denoted by [n] (and their duals ⟨n⟩), each intended to formalize a provability predicate
of increasing strength. The corresponding iterated consistency statements, usually denoted ⟨n⟩⊤, serve as
canonical measures of proof-theoretic strength. For general background on arithmetized provability and
reflection we refer to [Háj-Pud93].
Slightly more precisely, let T be as in Sect. 1.1, and assume in addition that T ⊇IΣ1. Iterated reflection
progressions are commonly defined by
T0 := T,
Tα+1 := Tα + RFNΓ(Tα),
where RFNΓ(T) denotes the uniform reflection schema over Γ, with limit stages obtained by unions (or, more
generally, along ordinal notation systems, cf. [Bek05, Bek10]). In Beklemishev’s framework, such progressions
admit a natural interpretation in terms of the modal operators [n] of GLP, where successive reflection
principles correspond to increasing modal strength, and consistency extensions are represented by formulas of
the form ⟨n⟩⊤.
The consistency schema ConS(T), however, does not correspond to a single reflection principle, but rather to
a family of pointwise assertions. There are three distinct operations one might consider a priori:
(i) adjoining each standard instance Conn(T) as a separate axiom schema,
(ii) adjoining the universal closure ∀x ¬ProofT (x, ⌜0 = 1⌝), i.e. Con(T),
(iii) adding a rule permitting passage from finite verification of arbitrary numerals to the universal
sentence.
Operation (ii) is the usual consistency extension. Operation (iii) is a genuine uniformization principle and is
not formalizable in T in view of Gödel’s 2.UVS. Operation (i), by contrast, does not yield a genuine extension:
if T is consistent, then each instance Conn(T) is already provable in T (cf. Prop. 1.5).

3.2. Postlegomena. Theorem 2.3 gives a genuine selector proof in the sense of Def. 2.1. Thus, we are led to
conclude that the consistency schema ConS(T) admits a form of uniform provability at the level of proof
construction: There exists a single primitive recursive procedure producing proofs of all its instances, and
the correctness of this procedure is internally verified as a selector statement. The latter is just the very
assertion of (2.5).
However, this uniformity clearly does not collapse to the universal consistency sentence Con(T). In particular,
the existence of such a selector sT does not yield a proof of Con(T) within T itself. The gap between
selector-verifiable uniformity and the universal consistency sentence is exactly where Gödel’s 2.UVS applies.
Thus, selector proofs exhibit a form of computational uniformity which remains strictly below uniform
reflection.
In summary, our results naturally lead to a structural distinction between two notions of uniformity, to be
compared with [Art25b]:
(Unif1) Computational uniformity: the existence of a single algorithm (selector) producing proofs of all
instances,
(Unif2) Internal uniformity: the existence of a single arithmetical sentence expressing the corresponding
universal property.
Gödel’s 2.UVS can then be seen as asserting that, for sufficiently strong theories, selector-verifiable com-
putational uniformity does not entail the ordinary uniform consistency sentence: The selector exists, and
its correctness is internally verified as a selector statement, but this does not yield a single reflection-like
assertion such as Con(T).
From this perspective, the consistency schema ConS(T) even serves as a canonical example to witness
computational uniformity as a selector-provable property. Otherwise put, the significance of schema-based
approaches lies in exhibiting a general proof-theoretic phenomenon, namely the existence of exactly the above
mentioned uniform computational witnesses, which do not collapse into uniform reflection principles. Here it
is worth noting that selector proofs occupy a strictly intermediate position in a natural hierarchy: They are
stronger than mere pointwise, or, instance-wise verification of each sentence Conn(T), yet strictly weaker
than any form of uniform reflection. This intermediate status is not accidental, but reflects a structural
feature of formal theories capable of representing their own syntax.
Thus, we are led to an alternative and more refined reading of Gödel’s 2.UVS, namely the dictum that
the true boundary revealed by Gödel’s 2.UVS is not between provability and unprovability, but between
computational uniformity and its uniform internalization.
3.3. An orthogonal appraoch – different provability predicates. Another way of resisting the standard
interpretation of Gödel’s 2.UVS is to alter the provability predicate itself. This approach is conceptually
distinct from the schema-based strategy discussed above. Rather than weakening the form of the consistency
assertion, one replaces the standard provability predicate ProvT (x) by a predicate Prov∗
T (x) which may still
be extensionally related to theoremhood, but which fails to satisfy one or more of the structural conditions
required for the usual derivation of Gödel’s theorem, see, e.g., [Det79, Det01, Vis01].
Recall that under our standing assumptions, cf. Sect. 1.1, the standard provability predicate ProvT (x)
satisfies the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, and that these are essential for the classical proof
of Gödel’s 2.UVS. A predicate Prov∗
T (x) may therefore evade the theorem, if it violates a relevant portion of
these conditions. In this sense, one may obtain “consistency statements” which are provable in T, but only at
the cost of departing from the standard structural notion of provability à la Hilbert–Bernays–Löb.
A humble, non-standard example is given by
Prov∗
T (x) ≡ProvT (x) ∧x ̸= ⌜0 = 1⌝.

Then, Prov∗

predicate fails to satisfy the Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, as announced.
More sophisticated constructions, such as Rosser-style provability predicates, are considerably subtler,
see, for instance, [Smo85, Vis01]. Their philosophical significance has been extensively discussed in the
literature, most notably by Detlefsen [Det79, Det86, Det01]. The central question is whether the Hilbert–
Bernays–Löb conditions should be regarded as essential to the very notion of formal provability, or merely as
one convenient formalization among others.
From the perspective of the present paper, the key point is that this approach operates at a different
level from the schema-based distinction analysed above. The latter preserves the standard notion of provabil-
ity and weakens the form of the consistency assertion, whereas the present strategy modifies the notion of
provability itself. The two approaches are therefore orthogonal: One weakens the form of the consistency
assertion, the other the underlying notion of provability.
