Abstract
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) pose new chal-
lenges for ontology matching (OM). While OM systems built on
LLMs have shown remarkable capabilities in discovering more
matching candidates, traditional OM validation that relies on do-
main experts has become overwhelming. In this study, we explore
the use of crowdsourcing for OM validation and introduce a novel
crowdsourcing system. We propose three domain-specific mecha-
nisms, namely differential trustworthiness, coherence pre-filling,
and time-dependent opinion, to ensure the quality of crowdsourcing
for OM validation. We demonstrate that our crowdsourcing system
can be integrated with existing OM systems to enable human-in-
the-loop validation. The evaluation of the system also shows its
effectiveness in handling diverse user groups and different annota-
tion settings. We also discuss two real-world use cases and current
limitations for improvement.

Introduction
Ontology matching (OM) has been used in many real-world appli-
cations to enable semantic interoperability for knowledge graphs.
Matching validation is a crucial step to ensure that the system’s
output aligns with real-world facts. This procedure is typically con-
ducted by domain experts in surveys, interviews, and workshops.
With the popularity of large language models (LLMs), OM systems
have been widely incorporated with LLMs and LLM agents [2, 7,
11, 15, 20, 21, 23, 26]. While such systems have shown advanced
capabilities, rapid evaluation of LLMs poses several challenges for
OM validation. Several studies claim that LLM-based OM systems

can discover more matching candidates, but these mappings are
not present in the current ground-truth references. Miscounting
these disputed mappings has skewed the measurement of system
performance. To tackle this issue, one popular approach is to em-
ploy harness engineering (e.g., chain-of-thoughts [25] and agent
debate [5]) for LLMs, but LLM hallucinations are not easy to recover
and can remain unavoidable in some cases [8]. For these disputed
mappings that require additional human validation, it is not easy to
find domain experts and domain experts may also have unavoidable
human bias [24].
Crowdsourcing is particularly suitable for this scenario. Rather
than conducting the validation within a group of domain experts,
exposing this procedure to the large online community could signif-
icantly mitigate LLM hallucinations and reduce the negative effects
of human bias. However, crowdsourcing also has several limitations.
For example, the diversity of online users can include non-experts
for validation, but their decisions are treated the same as those of
experts. This can lead to a phenomenon in which non-experts may
dominate decision-making (i.e. Gresham’s Law [19]). Moreover,
crowdsourcing is labour-intensive and user errors increase signif-
icantly when tasks require extensive mental effort (i.e. Cognitive
Load Theory [22]).
In this study, we explore the use of crowdsourcing for OM valida-
tion and introduce a novel crowdsourcing system. The system can
be integrated with existing OM systems to enable crowdsourced
OM validation. To tackle known issues in crowdsourcing, we pro-
pose three domain-specific mechanisms to improve the quality of
crowdsourced OM validation. Our main contributions include:
• We introduce a crowdsourcing system for OM validation. The
system can be easily integrated with existing OM systems to enable
interactive human-in-the-loop matching validation for OM tasks.
• We propose using differential trustworthiness and coherence pre-
filling to improve the quality of crowdsourcing for OM validation.
The former uses a set of generated seed pairs to assess each user’s
trustworthiness and apply the results to the overall agreement
measure. This approach gives more weight to the expert’s decision
and reduces the bias from the non-experts. The latter can reduce
user errors by pre-filling annotation pairs when the decisions can
be inferred from the user’s existing answers. We also use a time-
dependent opinion to ensure the validation output is up to date.
• We implement our crowdsourcing system with quality-assurance
mechanisms on an online platform. We evaluate our proposed mech-
anisms in three controlled scenarios and demonstrate their effec-
tiveness in improving the quality of crowdsourced OM validation.

Related Work
OM with human-in-the-loop (a.k.a. interactive OM) aims to over-
come the bottleneck in automatic OM approaches that have reached
the performance upper bound. Involving domain experts in OM
validation has proven an effective way to utilise user feedback to
improve OM performance [4, 9, 14]. In the era of LLMs, human-
in-the-loop is integral. State-of-the-art LLM fine-tuning methods
(e.g. RLHF [13] and DPO [17]) leverage positive and negative user
feedback to adjust LLMs for domain-specific tasks. Foreseeing the
benefits of human-in-the-loop, the quality of user feedback becomes
externally important. If the user’s feedback does not provide suffi-
cient information, LLMs may have very limited knowledge to learn
or may even learn incorrect knowledge. Therefore, the user group
needs to be carefully selected, or the user feedback needs to be
carefully filtered and processed.
Crowdsourcing is one of the most popular techniques in human-
in-the-loop evaluation and is being used in a wide range of ontology
engineering tasks. In the context of OM, validation is traditionally
conducted by domain experts to ensure that the provided ground-
truth reference alignments are correct for benchmarking. This ap-
proach requires extensive expert labour and is no longer applicable
to large-scale OM tasks. CROWDMAP [18] is an early work that
proposes using a workflow model for crowdsourced OM. However,
this method is highly reliant on the CrowdFlow platform, and the
validation process provides limited support for resolving conflicting
or inconsistent cases. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the
first attempt to leverage crowdsourcing for quality OM validation
with considerations for reliability and feasibility.

System Design
Figure 1 shows the conceptual model of our crowdsourcing sys-
tem. The system consists of datasets from different domains and
matchers developed by different users. When a matcher produces a
system alignment, the result is compared with the corresponding
dataset to find disputed mappings. These mappings are formed into
an annotation task for users. Users need to make decisions on each
disputed mapping contained in the annotation task.
• Given the reference alignment 𝑅and the matcher alignment 𝐴,
our system considers non-disputed mappings to be the mappings
that appear both in 𝑅and 𝐴(i.e. 𝐴∩𝑅). The mappings that exist
only in 𝑅(i.e. 𝑅−𝐴) or only in 𝐴(i.e. 𝐴−𝑅) are disputed mappings
and require additional human validation.
• For each online user, they need to determine whether the two
entities in the disputed mappings are “Equivalent” or “Not Equiva-
lent”. They can view the class hierarchy, logic presentation, and text
description side by side to facilitate their decision on equivalence.
• The crowdsourcing system follows the majority voting rule. A
disputed mapping is considered equivalent if more than 50% of
users vote “Equivalence”. For some domains (e.g. biomedical) that
require high precision, the system allows the confidence threshold
to be adjusted from 50% to 100%.
Figure 1: Conceptual model of our crowdsourcing system.
Our crowdsourcing system operates as a two-sided marketplace,
similar to Airtasker [1]. A group of users can post their annota-
tion tasks, and another group of users can participate in them. We
initialise three different types of users within the system.
• Administrator: The administrator is the superuser of the system.
The administrator is responsible for uploading the original datasets
and controlling the open/close status. The administrator can also
view user details, such as the annotation tasks users participate in
and the decisions they made on each annotation pair.
• Developer: The developer is a specific type of system user. They
are allowed to upload the alignment produced by the matcher into
the system and specify the customised settings of their annotation
task. Developers are not allowed to participate in annotation tasks
produced by their own matchers.
• Annotator: The annotator is the main user of the system. They are
allowed to annotate the same datasets multiple times for different
tasks posted by different matchers.
We allow a single user to have multiple roles in the system. For
example, a developer can serve as an annotator by participating in
annotation tasks posted by other matchers. We consider a devel-
oper to be a user who has registered at least one matcher in the
system, whereas an annotator is a user who has started at least
one annotation task. Users must complete registration (including
reading the information sheet and signing the consent form) before
participating in the annotation task for data collection. To ensure
the quality of crowdsourced OM validation, we introduce three
novel quality-assurance mechanisms into the system.
3.1
Differential Trustworthiness
Crowdsourcing does not restrict non-experts from participating in
annotation tasks. While the number of non-experts is often greater

In such cases, the expert’s decisions are expected to carry more

online users (i.e. trustworthiness). These pairs have solid answers

• Positive seed pairs are generated from the non-disputed map-

• Negative seed pairs are generated using the following logic: (1)

fail to pass the ontology coherence check.
• To ensure that the difficulty of positive and negative seed pairs is
at the same level, we compute the total number and the distribution
of trivial and non-trivial rates for positive seed pairs and apply the
same number and a similar distribution to negative seed pairs.
3.2
Coherence Pre-Filling
Crowdsourcing expects online users’ attention and involvement to
be consistent over the annotation session. However, several studies
have shown that user engagement inevitably declines with long
online tasks. We aim to reduce the workload for online users.
We use the coherence check to pre-fill annotation pairs. The
user’s input creates an assertion rule, which can then infer other
supporting or conflicting rules. The coherence check can be cate-
gorised into two phases:
• Within the dataset. For example, if the user has already labelled
𝐴≡𝐵, then 𝐶. 𝐵can be pre-filled in a one-to-one mapping task.
• Between datasets within the same domain. For example, if the
user has already labelled 𝐴≡𝐵in one dataset and 𝐵≡𝐶in another
dataset, then a transitive equivalence 𝐴≡𝐶can be inferred and
pre-filled if it appears in the dataset under the same domain.
We construct an ontology network for the coherence check. The
ontology network contains users’ decisions (formed as assertions)
and ontologies. A reasoner is used to infer the explicit assertions
as pre-fills. For coherence check between datasets within the same
domain, we also combine multiple ontologies.
3.3
Time-Dependent Opinion
We believe that there is no persistent ground truth for OM tasks.
The reference alignments generated by the domain experts are
based on their own opinions. For example, “NCI” commonly refers
to “National Cancer Institute”, but within the context of Australia,
this abbreviation is more likely to refer to “National Computational
Infrastructure”. Therefore, our system allows annotators to mod-
ify their decisions over time and assign a timestamp label to the
validation output. When the user changes the decision, the overall
crowdsourced decision is expected to change accordingly.
• If the user modifies the decision on the real annotation pairs, this
directly affects the decision on each disputed mapping.
• If the user modifies the decision on the seed pairs, they may
change their trustworthiness score and further affect the decision
on each disputed mapping.
4
System Demonstration
We demonstrate how the crowdsourcing system can extend existing
OM systems to enable crowdsourced human-in-the-loop validation.
We select Agent-OM [15] as the matcher, and the matcher output
is obtained from the OAEI 2025 campaign [16].
• Administrator Actions. The system only allows the administra-
tor to upload the reference alignment 𝑅. The administrator needs
to classify the dataset under one of the registered domains. If the
domain does not exist, the administrator can register a new do-
main. Each dataset requires three files to upload: the two ontology
files and the reference alignment between them. In this case, the
administrator registers a domain called “conference” and adds two
datasets (“cmt-conference” and “cmt-conof”) under the “conference”
domain. The administrator can edit the settings for domains and
datasets. For example, set a confidence threshold and open or close
the domain for annotation. In this case, the administrator sets the
50% confidence threshold for the “conference” domain and opens
this domain for annotation.
• Developer Actions. The system only allows the developer to
upload the matcher alignment 𝐴. The developer needs to register
the matcher and provide a short description. By doing so, the user
automatically becomes the developer of the matcher. The developer
can also add other developers from the registered users. Once the
developer completes the upload, the backend computes the differ-
ence between the matcher alignment 𝐴and the reference alignment
𝑅to find disputed mappings and form an annotation task. The de-
veloper can edit the settings for the annotation task. For example,
the developer can enable or disable the differential trustworthiness
and coherence pre-filling mechanisms. (1) If differential trustwor-
thiness is being activated, the backend automatically generates a
set of positive and negative seed pairs. These seed pairs are labelled
as “seed” and are visible to developers but not to annotators. (2)
If coherence pre-filling is being activated, the backend performs a
coherence check based on the inputs from the annotator and pre-fill
mappings. Annotation pairs labelled as “pre-filling” are visible to
both developers and annotators, with a short explanation of the
description logic involved.
• Annotator Actions. The system allows the annotator to select
an annotation task produced by a single matcher for a specific
dataset. They can only annotate the tasks with the matcher that
does not belong to them, and the dataset under the domain is
currently open for annotation. There are three options available for
each annotation pair: “Equivalent”, “Not Equivalent”, and “N/A”. The
default selection is “N/A”. (1) The trustworthiness is computed when
the user completes the annotation (i.e. there is no “N/A” annotation
within the dataset). (2) The coherence pre-filling operates in the

new pre-fills. (3) The annotator can change their decision on each

System Evaluation
We select six OAEI tracks comprising 45 datasets for evaluation. The
matcher output is obtained from the Agent-OM results in the OAEI
2025 campaign. For each controlled environment, we simulate a
total of 100 annotators in the system. We use the default confidence
threshold of 50% and set the seed number to 42 for reproducibility.
• Differential Trustworthiness. We simulate 100 annotators
with a mixture of experts and non-experts. Experts are expected
to have a trustworthiness greater than 0.5, while non-experts are
expected to have a trustworthiness less than 0.5. The trustworthi-
ness reflects the accuracy of the user’s answers. For example, a
trustworthiness of 0.7 means that the annotator has a 70% prob-
ability of labelling the TP as “Equivalent” and a 30% probability
of labelling the TP as “Not Equivalent”. Here, we assume that the
reference alignments 𝑅provided by the OAEI track are complete.
Therefore, the mappings that exist only in 𝑅(i.e. 𝑅−𝐴) are true-
positive mappings (TPs), while the mappings only in 𝐴(i.e. 𝐴−𝑅)
are false-positive mappings (FPs).
Figure 2 compares the discovery rates of TPs and FPs as the
expert ratio varies from 0.0 to 1.0. Although there is no difference
when the expert radio is very low or very high, our differential
trustworthiness mechanism shows significant improvements when
the expert radio is in the range [0.1, 0.6], which is more common in
real-world scenarios. Figure 3 compares the discovery rates of TPs
and FPs as the expert knowledge lower bound varies from 0.5 to
1.0. We can see that our differential trustworthiness is particularly
useful when the expert knowledge lower bound is less than 0.85. In
reality, experts can also make mistakes and achieving the expert
knowledge lower bound above 0.85 is almost impossible.
• Coherence Pre-Filling. We simulate 100 annotators and each
annotator’s decision is randomly selected from “Equivalent” or
“Not Equivalent”. We do not specify whether annotators are experts
or non-experts. For the purpose of assessing the propagation be-
haviours of coherence pre-filling, we expect this function to work
for all users. Here, we define an “anchor” ontology for each domain
and call datasets containing this ontology “anchor” datasets. We
only simulate the annotator’s input on “anchor” datasets and leave
others for pre-filling candidates. We use “coverage” to describe the
fraction of annotated non-seed pairs in the “anchor” datasets.
Figure 4 compares the number of pre-fills by type as coverage
varies from 0.1 to 1.0. Only three types existing in the OAEI datasets
we evaluated: transitive equivalence, subsumption negativity, and
disjointedness. Overall, we can see that the coverage within [0.3,
0.8] yields more pre-fills. A low fraction of annotated pairs reduces
the number of assertions to discover, whereas a high fraction of
annotated pairs leaves little space for pre-filling to be functional.
Figure 5 compares the number of pre-fills by domain as coverage
varies from 0.1 to 1.0. We can see that the propagation behaviours
of coherence pre-filling vary across different domains. This may be
because the ontology design in each domain follows its own well-
agreed guidelines, thereby restricting the use of logic expressions.

Use Cases
Having evaluated the crowdsourcing system for improving the
quality of OM validation and the effectiveness of the proposed
mechanisms in enhancing the system’s robustness and reducing
user workload, we present two real-world use cases.
• Reference Evolution. OAEI is one of the largest communities
focused on OM. Over the years, the reference alignment used across
several tracks has been out of date. Since the introduction of Chat-
GPT [12] in 2022, new matchers incorporating LLMs have identified
more matching candidates that are not present in current references.
These mappings seem to be correct, but they lack human validation
to ensure their quality. While existing reference alignments are
curated by domain experts, they may exhibit confirmation bias, in
which their judgements are based on their preconceived notions.
Our crowdsourcing system can mitigate human bias in this case.
• Ontology Harmonisation. Ontology harmonisation in the build-
ing industry has received significant attention due to the emerg-
ing need to integrate building information systems from different
vendors. Despite the foundation established by the harmonisation

Limitations
It is recommended to have a diverse set of participants for anno-
tation tasks, but this is not controllable in crowdsourcing systems.
However, we consider the bias caused by homogeneity to be minor
when there is a considerable number of annotators. Moreover, real-
world annotations can be more complicated than those in controlled
simulations. For example, we assume the annotator’s performance
is consistent across seed and no-seed pairs, but it could be that the
annotator performs very well on seed pairs but poorly on the real
no-seed pairs. These edge cases need to be carefully identified and
preprocessed before fitting to the models for analysis.
Our crowdsourcing system currently focuses on the most com-
mon one-to-one equivalent matching, but it can be extended to
more complex matching tasks. For example, the logic for gener-
ating equivalent seed pairs can be extended for subsumption: (1)
Positive: If (𝑒1,𝑒2) is a non-disputed mapping and 𝑒3 is the subclass
of 𝑒1, then 𝑒3 should be a subclass of 𝑒2. (2) Negative: If (𝑒1,𝑒2) is a
non-disputed mapping and 𝑒3 is the superclass of 𝑒1, then 𝑒3 should
not be a subclass of 𝑒2. Coherence pre-filling can be modified to
support subsumption tasks in similar ways.
8
Conclusions
In this paper, we propose a novel crowdsourcing system for OM val-
idation with three quality-assurance mechanisms. We present the
system design and illustrate a system walk-through. The evaluation
shows that the proposed quality-assurance mechanisms improve
the system’s robustness and overcome the inherent challenges of
crowdsourcing for OM validation. In the future, we plan to adopt
our crowdsourcing systems for reference evolution and ontology
harmonisation and contribute to the creation of robust benchmarks
for OM tasks.
