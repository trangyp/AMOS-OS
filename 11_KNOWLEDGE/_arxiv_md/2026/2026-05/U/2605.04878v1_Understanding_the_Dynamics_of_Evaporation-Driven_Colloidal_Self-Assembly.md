SUMMARY
6
Complex colloidal cluster morphologies are desirable for the fabrication of advanced materials,
7
such as photonic crystals and meta-materials, and can be formed through evaporation-driven
8
packing. By coupling lattice Boltzmann and discrete element methods, here we elucidate the
9
rich interplay between fluid and particle dynamics during evaporation-driven self-assembly of
10
spherical colloidal particles. We construct a regime diagram for a wide range of evaporation
11
rates, interparticle friction coefficients, and particle numbers, identifying parameter regimes for
12
open, closed, and minimal moment of inertia cluster configurations. Analyzing the competition
13
between capillary, hydrodynamic, normal, and friction forces, we show that interparticle friction
14
can exert a disproportionately strong influence on the final packing outcome despite being con-
15
siderably smaller in magnitude than other forces at play. Our simulation results further highlight
16
the potential for tuning colloidal cluster configurations via their dynamic trajectories.

INTRODUCTION
21
The behavioral similarity between atoms and colloidal particles1,2 has inspired the application of
22
molecular principles to the design of colloidal systems, particularly for bottom-up material fab-
23
rication via self-assembly. In this context, colloidal particles and clusters serve as fundamental
24
building blocks for a wide range of functional materials3,4. Self-assembly refers to the sponta-
25
neous organization of colloidal particles, driven either by specific interparticle interactions5–7,
26
external field manipulation8,9, or mediated by environmental factors10,11. To enable effective and
27
controllable self-assembly, it is often desirable to employ anisotropic colloidal particles as build-
28
ing blocks12,13. A powerful strategy is to pre-assemble a finite number of colloidal spheres into
29
well-defined colloidal clusters14–16, which in turn are assembled into designed superstructures
30
through shape-complementary interactions17–19. Consequently, the rational design and fabrica-
31
tion of target colloidal clusters is a critical step towards the realization of functional assemblies.
32
A widely adopted method for fabricating well-defined colloidal clusters is the evaporation-
33
driven packing technique14,20,21. In this approach, a specific number N of colloidal particles are
34
encapsulated within emulsion droplets dispersed in surrounding fluids. As the emulsion evapo-
35
rates, the particles are drawn together by capillary forces, forming compact clusters. Hitherto,
36
both experimental and theoretical studies have focused on a regime of quasi-static, slow evap-
37
oration22,23 and frictionless particle dynamics24,25. In this limit, such processes typically yield a
38
structure for each N that is characterized by a minimal moment of inertia of the mass distribu-
39
tion14,22.

In practice, however, evaporation-driven self-assembly is a dynamic rather than a quasi-static
41
phenomenon. The final packing structure results from a complex competition between capillary
42
and hydrodynamic forces exerted by the liquid to the particles, as well as normal contact and
43
friction forces between the particles. In recent years, there is strong interest to accelerate the
44
droplet evaporation rate to speed up the self-assembly process. This often leads to poorly un-
45
derstood packing structures that deviate from those predicted by the minimal moment of inertia
46
theory26,27, and it motivates determining, as a function of evaporation rate, the yields of differ-
47
ent packing configurations. Moreover, frictional interactions between particles are intrinsically
48
complex and cannot be neglected28–31. Recent studies in related contexts underscore the cen-
49
tral role of particle friction. For example, particle friction can affect deposition patterns in drying
50
droplets32, self-cleaning on functional surfaces33, and jamming transitions in granular systems34.
51
Accordingly, particle friction can also influence the resulting cluster morphology, including the re-
52
quirement for cluster rigidity.
53
Taken together, we therefore identify two important, interrelated questions to address in this
54
work. First, what are the upper limits on droplet evaporation rate and particle friction coefficient
55
for which minimal moment packing remains achievable? Second, can we engineer other, more
56
diverse configurations by exploiting evaporation and friction dynamics? These require detailed
57
understanding of the complex interplay between droplet and particle dynamics.
58
While experiments provide valuable insights, numerical simulations offer a more effective
59
means of analyzing the forces and cluster rearrangements involved in self-assembly. Particle-
60
based models such as molecular dynamics35 and Monte Carlo simulations36 have been used
61
to study evaporation-driven packing. However, these approaches often ignore hydrodynamic
62
and frictional effects, which are central to our investigation. To overcome this limitation, we em-
63
ploy a recently developed hybrid simulation framework that integrates the lattice Boltzmann (LB)
64
method with the discrete element method (DEM)33,37. This framework enables accurate model-
65
ing of the emulsion-assisted packing process by fully capturing capillary, hydrodynamic, normal
66
contact, and frictional forces. Using this approach, we conduct a detailed analysis to reveal how
67
evaporation dynamics and friction jointly determine the resulting packing configurations in col-
68
loidal clusters. We believe these results are valuable for developing rational design principles for
69
controlled assembly of target colloidal structures.

2.1
Packing regime
95
A wide range of evaporation rates Ue and friction coefficients µ are considered to construct
96

97

102

We first discuss the effect of the evaporation rate Ue on the packing structure of colloidal
116
clusters, and for simplicity, let us initially consider smooth particles with negligible friction (µ <
117
0.05). With varying Ca, we identified three distinct categories of final packing configurations:
118
open, closed, and minimal moment packings, as illustrated in Figure 2a. The three regions in the
119
regime diagram in Figure 2b are defined based on the occurrence probabilities of open, closed,
120

121

122

123

124
For large evaporation rates (Ca > 0.05; indicated in green in Figure 2b), hydrodynamic forces
125
dominate, and capillary attraction is not sufficient to draw colloidal particles into close contact.
126
In this regime, the final structures are typically open packings with some colloids having fewer
127
than three contact points. These allow for loose aggregates with structural flexibility, such as
128
chains or planar assemblies (Figure 2a), which are more strongly influenced by the initial spatial
129
arrangement of particles than by packing dynamics.
130
For slow evaporation rates (Ca < 0.003, indicated in blue in Figure 2b), capillary forces domi-
131
nate and effectively drive particles into the minimal moment configuration with 100% probability,
132
regardless of the initial particle distribution. The minimal moment packing represents a special
133
subclass of closed configurations characterized by the minimization of the moment of inertia.

154

155

159

The boundary shift with increasing N saturates and eventually plateaus for N > 8 (Fig-

164

166

167

169

170

We have also explored the boundary between open and closed packings for different N. As

Force analysis
214
To elucidate the dynamics underlying the regime diagram, we analyze the forces acting on par-

216

The regime diagram in Figure 2b, together with the power-law boundary in Equation (1),
288
indicates that friction has a disproportionately influence on the final packing structure, largely
289
independent of the particle number N. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we perform a
290
particle-resolved force analysis throughout the packing process. Figure 5 shows the temporal
291
evolution of the force components acting on a representative particle (marked in red) for N = 8
292
under two contrasting conditions that share the same initial particle distribution: minimal moment
293
packing (Ca = 1.0 × 10−3, µ = 0.01) and closed packing (Ca = 1.0 × 10−3, µ = 1.0). The only
294
parameter that differs between these two cases is the particle friction coefficient µ.
295
During the initial packing stage, particles are freely distributed within the droplet and ex-
296
perience negligible forces. As evaporation shrinks the droplet, the system approaches critical
297
packing at t∗= 0.5. Up to this point, particle motions and force signatures are indistinguishable
298
under both conditions, yielding identical critical packing configurations (see the bottom panels of
299
Figure 5a and b). Differences arise only during the subsequent rearrangement stage.
300
In the minimal moment packing scenario (Figure 5a), the capillary force undergoes a rise-
301
and-fall process, reaching its peak around t∗= 0.75. Between t∗= 0.5 and 0.75, the shrinking
302
droplet radius leads to a gradual increase in capillary force. This capillary force acts as the driv-
303
ing force that promotes the reorganization of particles from the critical packing state toward the
304
minimal moment configuration. The normal contact force provides mutual constraints between
305
particles. This results in the formation of the minimal moment packing at t∗= 0.75. As evap-
306
oration continues, the droplet shrinks further, reducing the contact area between the particles
307
and the droplet surface, which in turn weakens the capillary force. Eventually, the droplet fully
308
evaporates.
309
In contrast, for rough particles (Figure 5b), the rearrangement follows a different pattern. Due
310
to interparticle friction, relative motion between particles is hindered. As a result, when the cap-
311
illary force reaches its peak at t∗= 0.8, the particles are not yet fully compacted, with the total
312
number of contacts still less than the rigidity threshold (Nc < 3N −6). The frictional force cre-
313
ates a self-locking effect among the particles, preventing further rearrangement into the minimal
314
moment configuration. Once the cluster becomes compact, relative motion between particles
315
ceases, and dynamic friction transitions to static friction. Therefore, although the magnitude
316
of the friction force is small compared to other forces, its effect becomes significant during the
317
rearrangement stage.

DISCUSSION
391
In this study, we systematically investigated the packing dynamics of colloidal clusters driven
392
by emulsion droplet evaporation, with a particular focus on the effects of evaporation rate and
393
interparticle friction for different number of colloidal particles. Numerical simulations demonstrate
394
that diverse packing configurations, including open, closed, and minimal moment structures, can
395
be accessed by tuning the Capillary number (Ca) and friction coefficient (µ).
396
A regime diagram was constructed to illustrate the relationship between these dynamic pa-
397
rameters and the resulting clusters. For smooth particles, the final packing configuration is deter-
398
mined by the competition between capillary and hydrodynamic forces. With increasing particle
399
number, the boundary between open and closed packings remains the same, but that between
400
closed and minimal moment packing shifts to lower Ca.
401
Introducing particle friction significantly broadens the range of conditions under which closed
402
packing can be achieved, reminiscent of previous experimental observations26. For rough parti-
403
cles with µ > 0.05, the transition between minimal energy and closed packing regimes follows a
404
power-law relationship, as given in Equation (1). This relationship is independent of the number
405
of particles.
406
Force analysis reveals that the packing dynamics consist of two distinct stages. In the initial
407
packing stage, the interplay between capillary and hydrodynamic forces dominates and deter-
408
mines the critical intermediate structure. In this stage, the initial distribution determines the final
409
packing when capillary force is not dominant. The larger initial second moment will lead to the
410
generation of non-minimal moment final packing structure. In the subsequent rearrangement
411
stage, frictional interactions play the leading role by dictating whether the system can further
412
evolve into its minimal moment configuration.
413
Overall, this work highlights the importance of dynamic parameters in evaporation-driven
414
colloidal packing. Tuning evaporation rate and interparticle friction provides a potential strategy
415
for selectively producing desired clusters while suppressing undesired ones. These insights
416
offer guidance for designing colloidal clusters with tailored structures and functions, which is
417
essential for the bottom-up fabrication of advanced materials. There are numerous avenues for
418
future work. For instance, it is interesting to study the packing dynamics of particle mixtures
419
with controlled size disparity to quantify the impact of system asymmetry and to investigate
420
particles with tunable wettability to broaden the applicability of this framework.
Another key
421
direction is to develop a predictive theoretical description of how Ca governs the force balance

423
