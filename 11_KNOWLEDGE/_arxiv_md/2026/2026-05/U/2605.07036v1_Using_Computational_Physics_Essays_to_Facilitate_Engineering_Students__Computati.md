Abstract
Background: As traditional coding tasks in education become increasingly vulnerable to the use
of Generative AI, there is a critical need for authentic, project-based assessments that evaluate
students’ scientific inquiry. To address this need, we adapted the existing Computational Essay
framework to create the Computational Physics Essay (CPE). Administered as a culminating
capstone project, the CPE required introductory engineering students to use Python within
Jupyter Notebooks to iteratively model real-world physics systems. We analyzed a random
sample of CPE submissions (N = 100) using a customized 20-item rubric based on Weintrop’s
computational thinking (CT) taxonomy.
Results: The project-based constraint successfully elicited a high variety of CT practices.
Students demonstrated high proficiency in Modeling and Systems Thinking, with 99% successfully
investigating complex systems as a whole. Furthermore, the use of CT practices strongly correlated
(ρ = 0.75) with expert ratings of the overall quality of the CPE. While some students showed
expected novice weaknesses in software modularity, the CPE successfully shifted their epistemic
frame toward physical sensemaking.
Conclusions: Situating computation within real-world capstone projects provides a robust
framework for assessing CT, bridging the gap between programming and scientific argumentation
in introductory engineering students.
Keywords: Computational Essay, Computational Thinking, Project-Based Learning, Engineering
Education

1 Introduction
Computation has become a fundamental domain of science, recognized by the American Association of
Physics Teachers (AAPT) as the “third pillar” of physics, alongside experiment and theory (American
Association of Physics Teachers, 2016). This paradigm shift has elevated the importance of fostering
Computational Thinking (CT) in STEM education. First popularized by Jeanette Wing in 2006 as
the thought processes involved in “solving problems, designing systems and understanding human
behavior, by drawing on the concepts fundamental to computer science”, the definition of CT has
since expanded to encompass distinct modeling practices specific to mathematics and science (Shute
& Sun, 2017; Weintrop et al., 2016; Wing, 2006). Computation is rapidly moving outside of the
classroom with the increase in available computational tools in the workplace (e.g., Python data
analysis, data visualization software, chatbots), creating an essential need to ensure students can
correctly apply these tools (S. Wang & Chao, 2021). This priority is reflected not only by AAPT
but also in broader STEM standards, such as the Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS) and
Accreditation Board for Engineering and Technology (ABET) (ABET Engineering Accreditation
Commission, 2019; National Research Council, 2013).
Despite the demand for more CT across physics education, a significant gap remains in how
CT is operationally measured. While many studies have focused on integrating CT practices into
the curriculum (Weintrop et al., 2015), few formalized assessments have been validated for higher
education. Existing instruments such as the Competent Computational Thinking Test (Hamamsy
et al., 2025), Computational Thinking Scales (Korkmaz, Cakir, & Ozden, 2017), and tools like Dr.
Scratch (Rich & Browning, 2022) are focused primarily on K-12 content or very general STEM
populations. There is a lack of validated instruments designed to authentically assess domain-specific
computational skills and modeling in undergraduate STEM; meanwhile, groups like ABET explicitly
mandate that engineering graduates demonstrate the abilities associated with CT.
As a result of this gap, the integration of computation in undergraduate physics often suffers
from students defaulting to unproductive problem-solving habits. In their work on mathematical
reasoning in physics, Tuminaro and Redish (Tuminaro & Redish, 2007) identified the “plug-and-chug”
epistemic frame. In this mindset, students treat problem-solving as a localized game: identify a
target variable, search for a corresponding formula, and insert numbers. This completely separates
the mathematical procedure from any conceptual understanding of the physical system. When
computation is introduced without careful pedagogical design, students naturally transfer this
mathematical frame to the computer. They treat programming as just a high-powered calculator
rather than a scientific sensemaking tool (Aiken et al., 2013). Consequently, students become content
with simply generating correct numerical outputs, without any of the authentic computational skills
they will need in professional practice (Kortemeyer, 2016).
This challenge has been exacerbated by the increase in the availability and adoption of Generative
AI. As demonstrated by recent studies, large language models (LLMs) can now generate functional
Python scripts instantly, allowing students to bypass the cognitive struggle of algorithmic design
entirely (Li, Li, Li, & Jin, 2023; Zion, Zarzecki, Glazer, & Finkelstein, 2024). This new tool at
students’ disposal renders common coding assignments (e.g., write a Python function to calculate a
mean or generate a plot) potentially ineffective in assessing CT. To verify learning in this age of AI,
we must require assessments that are fundamentally more AI-resilient. Such resilience is achieved by
demanding deep context, where computation is linked to a specific and multilayered physical scenario
rather than an isolated mathematical abstraction, and personalized modeling, which requires students
to make, evaluate, and iteratively defend design decisions about a physical system.
To address this challenge, we introduce the Computational Physics Essay (CPE). Adapted from
the Computational Essay framework established by Odden and Caballero (Odden, Lockwood, &
Caballero, 2019), the CPE is a multimodal digital document blending code, data visualization, and
narrative text into a scientific argument. Typically, it is implemented within a computational notebook,
also known as Jupyter Notebook, which allows interactive, web-based programming. Computational
Essays are distinguished from formulaic coding assignments by constraining the narrative to the
rigorous defense of a physical model. Students must first choose their own real-world problem, then
solve it in text and code using key physics principles (conservation of energy, momentum, and angular
momentum). This shifts the assessment focus from answer to argument, making it more difficult to
complete via automated code generation alone.
To evaluate the utility of the CPE as a scalable assessment for CT in undergraduate physics, we
distinguished between two levels of engagement. We defined authentic CT as the cohesive integration

2 Background & Framework
2.1 Constructionism and Project-Based Learning
Our pedagogical motivations regarding the hybrid-modality design of CPEs (computation + narrative
rhetoric) are grounded in constructionism. This framework shows that deep conceptual mastery is
not transmitted from instructor to student, but actively built on by the learner via public artifacts
(Papert & Harel, 1991). In this context, we define the ‘artifact’ as the CPE itself, rather than just
the output/visual simulation from the code. This integrated essay of code, narrative text, and data
visualization is contained in a Jupyter Notebook. We further base our decision to use CPE on the
theory of project-based learning (PBL). As described by Krajcik and Shin (Krajcik & Shin, 2014),
PBL environments promote scientific reasoning by centering learning around students’ extended
period of inquiry. The CPE serves as a capstone project for the end of the semester; Unlike weekly
laboratory and recitation exercises, where the procedure is prescriptive. This extended duration allows
students to engage in iterative improvement and gives them the opportunity to use the notebook as
a canvas to test their hypotheses.
Notably, this construction occurs in collaborative groups of 2-3 students, extending the framework
to social constructivism (Vygotsky, 1978). We hold that Computational Thinking can be found not
only in syntax, but also in the social negotiation required to generate it. Previous work has shown
that computational tasks create a “single keyboard bottleneck” that necessitates explicit verbalization
(Obsniuk, Irving, & Caballero, 2015). As such, the code acts more as a boundary object (Wenger,
1998), a shared object that forces students to articulate and agree on a physical model before it can
be turned into code.
2.2 Adapting the Computational Essay
To operationalize our approach of constructionism and PBL, we utilize the computational essay
framework established by Odden and Caballero (Odden et al., 2019). It allows students to present
code not as a black box, but as part of their narrative argument. It was shown to scaffold professional
scientific practices for undergraduate students as well as to foster their epistemic agency (Odden,
Silvia, & Malthe-Sørenssen, 2023). However, the student population in these studies consisted of
upper-level undergraduate STEM majors. In this study, we contributed to the development and
adoption of computational essays by considering the introductory STEM student population.
To adapt the computational essay for this specific population, we simplified the complexity
of computational methods that students used when solving the open-ended problem they chose.
Our main constraint was the conceptual synthesis of at least two fundamental physics principles
(e.g., conservation of energy and conservation of momentum) to help them model the system of
interest and solve their problem. This constraint shifts the assignment’s focus from broad creativity
to a targeted systems integration, in which they must solve a problem using physics principles
and computational tools. This ensures that the resulting artifact provides measurable evidence of
physics-specific computational skills.
2.3 Assessment Framework
To measure evidence of students’ engagement in CT in our essays, we required a consistent and
specific definition of computational thinking. While numerous frameworks exist, most notably the
comprehensive review by Shute et al. (Shute & Sun, 2017), which breaks down CT into general
categories like decomposition and debugging. We find these generalist models insufficient for our
3


## Page 4


investigation. Shute’s framework is fundamentally rooted in traditional computer science. It performs
well at evaluating how well a student constructs software, but it lacks the necessary granularity to
evaluate how well a student models real-world physics. As such, we adopt the taxonomy developed
by Weintrop et al. (Weintrop et al., 2016), which is explicitly designed for using computational
methods in mathematics and science classrooms. Unlike other frameworks (Brennan & Resnick, 2012;
Shute & Sun, 2017), this model elevates Data practices and Modeling & Simulation Practices to
primary domains. This helps us distinguish “Computer Science” CT from more “physics” CT (correct
real-world modeling over correct syntax).
However, as noted by Weller (Weller, Bott, Caballero, & Irving, 2022), the broad universality
of Weintrop’s taxonomy, which includes 22 distinct sub-practices, requires adaptation based on the
context in order to be properly measured. We redefine his framework by identifying 20 specific sub-
practices visible in the student’s final Jupyter notebook (CPE). We explicitly excluded Programming
and Troubleshooting and Debugging, from our list, both sub-practices of the Computational Problem
Solving Domain. These were removed as programming is inherent as part of what the CPE artifact is,
and Debugging would generally not be seen in their final product, though it should be noted to be an
important part of the process. The 20 specific sub-practices and the operationalized criteria used to
score them can be seen in Table 1.
2.3.1 Data Practices
In the context of undergraduate physics, data practices involve both the initial collection of real-
world data and the handling of information generated via algorithms and physics equations. Because
students were not collecting data experimentally via hardware, they researched theoretical conditions
(e.g., drag coefficients) from external sources. Notably, students who solely cited an LLM (e.g.,
ChatGPT) as their data source did not receive credit for Collecting Data in our analysis, as this
bypasses authentic information gathering. Additionally, students must have generated arrays of
data based on the information they collected. Students demonstrating strong data practices then
manipulated this generated data to create visualizations, such as plots, and used them to create
meaningful conclusions about the problem they were trying to solve.
2.3.2 Modeling & Simulation Practices
Where real-world experiments are constrained by hardware, modeling and simulation allow students to
explore complex scenarios, such as adding non-linear air resistance to a projectile. To evaluate students’
proficiency in this domain, we analyzed how they constructed and refined their computational models.
An example of this involves writing iterative update loops, such as the Euler-Cromer method (i.e., a
key technique for dealing with differential equations), to predict the future state of the system based
on its current state and physical principles. Iterative loops are much more powerful than merely
plugging numbers into an algebraic formula, allowing the model to handle varying forces and behave
more realistically, and thus, it can represent a higher-level, more advanced modeling approach.
2.3.3 Computational Problem Solving Practices
Here, students focus on developing the programming skills needed to convert physics concepts into
functional code. This domain measures a student’s ability to decompose complex physical problems
into logical steps. Key practices involve developing modular solutions and creating computational
abstractions. In practice, this included instances like creating reusable Python functions for each of
the forces used in their equations. For example, a student effectively utilizing abstractions defined
variables like mass (m) and change in time (dt) at the start of their notebook rather than hard-coding
numerical values into their equations, making the entire model easily adaptable.
2.3.4 Systems Thinking Practices
Physical systems often become difficult to understand when isolating properties, making a systems-
level perspective essential. For these practices, we look for evidence of students defining clear system
boundaries, such as having the Earth included when calculating the gravitational potential energy of a
falling object. This domain assesses how well students synthesize micro-level interactions, like friction,
into macro-level behavior, such as the total energy dissipated due to said friction. Furthermore, these
practices connect to a core principle of science: how the information is communicated. Measuring

how effectively students explain these intricate system interactions to the reader of the notebook is
crucial, as it further proves they are actively thinking about how to make their ideas clear.
3 Methods
3.1 Context and Participants
This study was conducted during the Spring 2025 semester of a calculus-based introductory mechanics
course at a large Midwestern research university. The course serves as a primary requirement for
engineering majors, with a total enrollment of N = 2086 students, of which the majority are first-year
students. The curriculum follows Matter and Interactions (Chabay & Sherwood, 2015), organizing
the mechanics into three main conservation principles: Momentum, Energy, and Angular Momentum.
This curricular structure is critical to the study as it emphasizes systems thinking and iterative
application of fundamental laws over formula retrieval.
Computational modeling was integrated throughout the semester. Students attended weekly
laboratory sections (14 total) where they utilized Python inside of Google Colab (Google, 2025)
to help visualize physical phenomena. Early labs focused on isolated skills (e.g., position update
using average velocity), while later labs focused on algorithmic integration methods such as the
Euler-Cromer method for solving equations. Each lab incorporated two or more of the practices in
order to begin fostering students’ computational thinking.
3.2 Task: Computational Physics Essay
The primary data source for this study, the CPE, was administered as a capstone project over the
final four weeks of the semester. They were given time at the beginning of the laboratories as well
as outside of class to work on the project. Students worked in collaborative groups of two or three
to write the CPE in Google Colaboratory (Google, 2025), a cloud-based environment for authoring
Jupyter notebooks (Project Jupyter Community, 2016). All data were collected following approval
from the institution’s ethical research review board.
Students were instructed:
Model a real-world system of your choice. Your model must require the synthesis of at least two of the three
fundamental principles (Momentum, Energy, or Angular Momentum) to fully resolve the system’s dynamics.
To scaffold the transition from “homework problem” to “computational modeling,” we provided
students a structured Jupyter Notebook template (available upon request). Unlike typical open-ended
coding assignments, this template guided students through a four-stage iterative process based on
the prior week’s work.
1. Problem Statement: Student defines real world problem and system, identifying necessary
physics principles.
2. First Iteration/Solution: Students construct an initial model to simulate the system.
3. Second Iteration/Solution: Students must critique or improve upon their first model and
implement refinement.
4. Conclusion: Students synthesize their findings into a scientific argument.
Common student topics included the projectile motion of objects such as rockets (energy +
momentum) and the rotational motion of objects such as a figure skater (energy + angular momentum).
This constraint served both to force students to define system boundaries more explicitly and also
to prevent the submission of more trivial scripts. An example of a group’s Problem Statement and
portion of their code can be seen in Fig.1. Students were assessed for their course grade using a
standard pedagogical rubric focused on scientific argumentation (McNeill & Krajcik, 2011), general
use of CT, and accurate physics. Notably, while this rubric prompted the four broad domains of CT,
it did not dictate the specific sub-practices measured in this study.

3.4 Rubric Development
To quantify the sub-practices above, we developed a binary (0/1) coding scheme based on an iterative
content validation process. Initial definitions were constructed using the framework provided by
(Weintrop et al., 2016). During the calibration phase, raters used an initial set of 10 Jupyter notebooks
to modify these definitions, adapting them to better fit the nature of the assessment. It was also
during this stage that it was decided to remove two categories from the framework for investigation,
Programming and Troubleshooting/Debugging, based on the assessment.
After refining the definitions, three expert raters independently coded a separate validation set of
10 different Jupyter notebooks across the 20 remaining sub-practices. The raters also assigned each
notebook a holistic score of 0, 1, or 2, corresponding to no, superficial, or rich use of CT. The initial
independent coding yielded a raw agreement of 81% (Fleiss’ Kappa, κ = 0.45) for the 200 binary
data entries. Agreement on the overall holistic quality score was 73% (Fleiss’ Kappa, κ = 0.43). Both
of these kappas are considered to be acceptable agreements for three raters (Fleiss, 1971).

While these moderate kappa values reflect the inherent subjectivity of evaluating complex, open-

A key outcome of this consensus meeting was the addition of strict negative constraints to the
rubric definitions. These constraints clearly distinguish between superficial computation and authentic
CT. For instance, a constraint was added specifying that students who simply stated they “imported
data from GPT” would not receive credit for Collecting Data. With these finalized constraints and a
shared understanding established through consensus, the raters proceeded to independently code
their own subsets of 100 different Jupyter notebooks for the primary analysis. The final definitions

Use of Large Language Models
During the preparation of this manuscript, the authors utilized Large Language Models (LLMs)
to assist with copyediting, structural formatting, and refining the clarity of the written prose. AI
tools were strictly limited to the communication and formatting of the work. No AI tools were used
to generate data, to human-code the Computational Physics Essays, or to formulate the study’s
conclusions.
4 Results & Discussion
4.1 Human Baseline
To ensure the validity of our assessment, we first evaluated the reliability of the human coding
process. During the initial calibration phase, the three raters independently coded a validation set of
10 Jupyter notebooks, achieving an average notebook score of 15.4 ± 1.2 out of 20. As for overall
quality, the average notebook score was 1.4 ± 0.1 out of 2.0. Following this calibration, we examined
the mean scores across the three independently coded subsets of the main dataset to check for rater
drift, a phenomenon where raters diverge when grading independently (Hoskens & Wilson, 2001).
Among the 100 Jupyter notebooks in the analysis set, the average scores for CT coverage were
16.5 ± 0.02 of 20. Similarly, for the “Overall CT Quality” score, the raters averaged 1.6 ± 0.1. These
consistent means, showing significantly less variance than the calibration set, provide a secondary
validation of our methodology. This indicates both that the random sampling was successful and that
the raters maintained strict adherence to rubric constraints without shifting excessively.

4.2 Student proficiency in Computational Thinking
To contextualize the scope of the analyzed data, we first examined the structural characteristics of the
submitted CPEs. Across the analysis set (N = 100), the final submissions were substantial artifacts.
Notably, students expanded beyond the provided template scaffolding; they added an average of 2.9
code and 1.4 text (markdown) cells to the existing 3 code input cells and 5 text input cells provided
in the template. This shows the students’ willingness to go beyond the template to express their
ideas and solve their problems. In terms of the amount of students’ input, the students wrote on
average 996 words and 78.3 lines of Python code per submission. This high volume of written text
and custom code supports the framing of the CPE as a comprehensive capstone project rather than
a brief, superficial coding assignment.
Having validated the internal consistency of the human-coded dataset, we aggregated the scores
to answer our primary research question regarding the computational proficiency of introductory
engineering students. Across the full analysis set, students demonstrated high levels of engagement
with the prompt. The results indicate that the CPE successfully elicited a strong breadth of practice,
with a mean score of 16.5 out of 20 possible sub-practices. As illustrated in the figure below (Fig. 3),

4.3 Quantity vs Quality in CT
To validate the binary rubric accurately reflects true proficiency, we compare the “breadth of practice”
(quantity) against the expert quality scores. A Spearman’s rank correlation revealed a significant
positive association between the use of the practices and the holistic quality score (ρ = 0.75, p < .001).
This confirms that the rubric does not merely reward the quantity of code, but actually captures the
sophistication of the modeling. The high scores Jupyter notebooks (mean = 18.0) weren’t simply
checking boxes but integrating a wide array of practices compared to the more superficial group
(mean = 14.3), see Fig. 5.

4.5 Areas of Weakness: Gap in Modularity
In contrast to their modeling success, students displayed slightly weaker performance in the aspect
of Computational Problem Solving. Interestingly, while the course grading rubric explicitly listed
’modularization’ as a desired component of Computational Problem Solving, only 50% of groups
actually engaged in Developing Modular Computational Solutions. While they effectively used the
template’s code cell for calculations for the physics problem, many students wrote linear scripts,
using Python to do algebraic equations to find a specific variable.
This finding highlights a critical trade-off in the current curriculum. The template prioritized
Computational physics (correct physical answer) over Computer Science (writing efficient code).
Because the template provided empty code cells for their calculations (though they were told they
could add more), students defaulted to linear scripting as they would with a traditional physics
problem. This suggests that without more explicit scaffolding or prior experience in computation,
students will not adopt these practices by default.

4.6 Implications: Assessing Physics-Specific Computational Thinking with
CPEs
A major affordance of the CPE format is its integrated notebook medium, which combines narrative
explanation, code, and visualizations within a workflow that naturally encourages and facilitates
iteration. This integration makes students’ decisions and reasoning visible and auditable by instructors
in higher education, where often, these intermediate steps are hidden, such as in traditional physics
problem sets or exams whose final answers are solely assessed (J. Docktor & Heller, 2009; Wilcox
et al., 2015). Importantly, in our study, we found that students’ CPEs often expanded beyond the
provided template. Our analysis showed that students engage in computational thinking practices
with high engagement for a sizable portion of the class. In other words, many students did not simply
complete the assignment. Students often elaborated beyond assignment expectations by adding text
and code cells to demonstrate CT practices across a wide range of defined sub-practices (Table 1).
These patterns imply that CPEs can support CT-in-use in ways that are more similar to authentic
modeling work (i.e., doing what is necessary for the argument, not just narrowly defined minimums),
rather than isolated coding skills.
In our study, we did not directly measure scientific reasoning with a dedicated instrument. The
prevalence of modeling-oriented CT practices in our data, however, suggests that CPEs can plausibly
scaffold scientific sensemaking through computation, as sensemaking in science is often realized
through building, evaluating, and revising models. CPEs create conditions in which several major
components of scientific inquiry are naturally utilized, from defining a system of interest to specifying
assumptions and boundaries, implementing an operational model, interpreting outputs, and evaluating
limitations (National Research Council, 2013). The most frequently observed CT subpractices in
our rubric, Modeling & Simulation (e.g., using computational models to understand a concept;
constructing, assessing, and designing these models) and Systems Thinking (e.g., investigating a
complex system as a whole, managing complexity, thinking in levels), mirror the epistemic work of
scientific modeling and the concept of “doing science” (Windschitl, Thompson, & Braaten, 2008).
Furthermore, the iterative CPE structure, where an initial model attempt is critiqued and revised, is
also a core characteristic of scientific practice: first models are provisional, and refinement is driven
by what the model reveals, where it fails, and what new questions emerge (Schwarz et al., 2009;
Windschitl et al., 2008). We are careful not to claim that CPEs measure scientific reasoning in a
comprehensive sense, but rather CPEs, as a format, can make model-based inquiry and evidence-based
justification more legible than conventional physics assessments, which often prioritize procedural
correctness over the logic of modeling choices (J.L. Docktor, Strand, Mestre, & Ross, 2015).
Further, in today’s learning environments where AI-assisted code generation via LLMs (e.g.,
ChatGPT) is readily accessible, CPEs may be particularly valuable because they shift what is
assessed toward what remains cognitively challenging and harder to outsource to AI. That is, CPEs
ask students to define a model, interrogate its outputs, justify the assumptions, and communicate
evidence-based conclusions. Recent evidence suggests that current LLMs often struggle with open-
ended, reasoning-heavy tasks compared to linear, clearly defined tasks (L´opez-Sim´o & Rezende, 2024;
K.D. Wang, Burkholder, Wieman, Salehi, & Haber, 2024). More traditional computational assessments
can run the risk of over-emphasizing code syntax and under-emphasizing model assessment, which
is precisely the dimension where conceptual mastery matters and where AI-generated code can be
syntactically correct while being conceptually wrong (Z. Wang et al., 2025). In comparison, the CPEs
in our study demand contextualization (“What is the problem?”), boundary setting (“What are the
assumptions?”), iteration (“How should your model be revised based on identified shortcomings?”),
and argumentation (“How do the outputs support a conclusion?”). Thus, LLMs can assist with pieces
of this workflow, but they cannot fully replace it, as students must evaluate and defend the modeling
decisions that are the result of what was learned and shifted at each step of the iteration. While we
did notice in our study that students sometimes cited generative AI tools as resources for data or
functions, within a CPE, students must still assess realism and interpretability, both of which need to
be explained and translated into narrative text that supports the student’s claims. Consistent with
this, we observed an association between broader participation in CT practice and expert-determined
quality, which suggests that higher-quality work is syntactic completeness combined with thoughtful
modeling and explanatory work throughout the artifact.
Our findings suggest that CPEs hold potential as a blueprint for types of scalable assessment
of computational modeling and systems thinking that are flexible and tunable to instructional
priorities. One design implication from our study is regarding the sequencing of intended skills and

competencies. Instructors may benefit from prioritizing modeling and systems sensemaking foremost,
before emphasizing good software practices, as the latter can at least be partially supported via
generative AI tools. Thus, the comparatively lower prevalence of modularity and code reuse (e.g.,
functions, abstraction, parameterization) should not be interpreted as a student deficiency, but rather,
it is consistent with certain norms in physics problem solving and a notebook-based medium, where
students may be likely to construct derivation-based narratives that allow for the demonstration
of reasoning over code reusability. Thus, if modularity is an intended outcome, it likely requires
explicit scaffolding and criteria. A practical approach could be to preserve the existing workflow,
then prompt a step in which students must re-express their solution modularly once the conceptual
model is stable. Alternatively, rubrics can include criteria that reward modularity rather than as a
programming norm. Lastly, instructors could communicate the emphasis of scientific evaluation in
CPEs by including templates or prompts for sanity checks (unit consistency, limit cases, validation
against expectations), which could encourage students to treat computation as an evaluation tool for
models and not a means to simply produce outputs.
5 Conclusions
This study demonstrates that the Computational Physics Essay (CPE) is a promising pedagogical
instrument for prompting authentic computational thinking in introductory engineering students.
By shifting the assessment’s focus from isolated coding tasks to an iterative, narrative investigation,
the CPE successfully prompted students to engage deeply with Modeling & Simulation and Systems
Thinking.
To address our research questions, regarding RQ1, the analysis reveals that the scaffolding
provided by the assessment’s format, having them first define a real-world problem, which they will
then solve and then solve it over multiple iterations, was successful. Regarding RQ2, the strong
correlation between breadth of computational practices and expert quality ratings (ρ = 0.75) further
confirms that the assessment distinguishes levels of engagement, as the number of modeling decisions
a student makes is a valid proxy for the quality of their scientific argument.
6 Future Work
Successfully automating the assessment of the CPE will accomplish more than just solving a grading
issue. It will allow large universities to fully realize the AAPT’s vision of computation as a fundamental
pillar of physics education (American Association of Physics Teachers, 2016). By combining the
authentic, project-based structure of the CPE with the analytical power of multimodal AI, we can
improve students’ growth in introductory physics courses. Rather than relying on formulaic coding
tasks that are easily bypassed by generative tools, we can give assessments that equip students with
the epistemic agency, critical evaluation skills, and complex systems thinking required to thrive in
the modern engineering workforce.
Our future implementation of the CPE aims to bridge the gap between computational and experi-
mental physics. While the current version relies on theoretical values, we plan to explore a hybrid
model where students use lab equipment to collect their own data, grounding their computational
models on observations. By having students create their CPE simulations based on physical exper-
iments, it will help promote deeper experiential learning (Kolb, 1984). This next step will push
students to connect their algorithms with real-world physics, improving both their computational
and experimental skill sets.
Additionally, though the human coding process provided a baseline for understanding students’
CT practices, it also highlighted a significant logistical challenge. Detailed qualitative analysis of 20
distinct sub-practices is labor-intensive for large enrollment courses (N > 2000). To make this level
of assessment feedback viable for instructors, we require automated methods capable of detecting
these practices.
Future work will focus on leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) to replicate this human
coding scheme. By using the reliable human-coded dataset established in this study as a ground
truth, we aim to investigate whether LLMs can pick up on students’ engagement with CT. This
would potentially allow for the development of real-time automated feedback that can guide students
toward richer computational modeling as well as provide insight for instructors.
