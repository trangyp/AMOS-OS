Abstract
The CH4rLiE (CH4 Livestock Emission) project investigates the techni-
cal feasibility of adapting gas recovery systems from high-energy physics to
mitigate methane (CH4) emissions in livestock housing. This work presents
a proof-of-principle based on the adaptation of CERN’s gas recuperation
systems for the capture of CH4 at low concentrations. A laboratory-scale

1. Introduction
The CH4 Livestock Emission (CH4rLiE) project aims to adapt gas re-
covery systems, originally designed for particle detectors, to methane (CH4)
capture, bridging the gap between high-energy physics technology and envi-
ronmental mitigation.
As the most abundant greenhouse gas (GHG) in the troposphere after
2


## Page 3


water vapor and carbon dioxide (CO2), CH4 concentration significantly in-
fluences the Earth’s radiative balance [1]. Since the 1700s, human activities
have more than doubled CH4 emissions, leading to atmospheric concentra-
tions that continue to increase steadily.
Because CH4 has a high global
warming potential (GWP) yet is a short-lived gas„ reducing its atmospheric
load offers an effective short-term strategy to mitigate global warming and
improve air quality.
A major challenge lies in anthropogenic activities where CH4 is emitted in
low concentrations over extended periods. A primary example is animal hus-
bandry: in a typical dairy barn, concentrations may remain below 100 ppm,
yet a single cow can emit approximately 110 kg of CH4 annually — equivalent
to 3080 kg of CO2. Globally, enteric fermentation and manure management
account for 15% of annual CH4 emissions [2].
The CH4rLiE project operates within this specific technical window, seek-
ing to validate whether recovery systems developed for gaseous detector mix-
tures at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) [3] can be effectively repurposed
for CH4 capture. While these technologies were developed to manage several
pollutant components, the starting point for this adaptation is the system
optimized for carbon tetrafluoride (CF4), an F-gas (fluorinated greenhouse
gas) used in Cathode Strip Chambers (CSC) with a GWP over a 100-year
horizon (GWP100) of 7390 [4]. Due to its high cost and environmental im-
pact — accounting for roughly 20% of CERN’s direct GHG emissions [5] —
CERN has developed a four-pillar strategy to manage CF4 based on:
1. gas recirculation systems;
2. gas recuperation systems;
3


## Page 4


3. research into novel, environmentally friendly gases for particle detec-
tors;
4. development of treatment plants to decompose GHGs into harmless
byproducts.
This study focuses exclusively on the first two areas. The recovery and
recirculation of gases in these systems rely fundamentally on the use of ad-
sorbent materials to selectively separate the target gas from the mixture [6].
While the original CERN systems were optimized for CF4, the same prin-
ciple can be applied to CH4 by selecting appropriate materials.
Numer-
ous types of adsorbents are widely used for CH4 capture from both high-
and low-concentration sources, including zeolites, activated carbon, metal-
organic frameworks, carbon molecular sieves (MS), and ionic liquids [7]. The
efficiency of these materials — particularly critical when isolating CH4 from
low-concentration streams — is determined by their physical characteristics
and pore structure, which encompasses morphology, pore size distribution,
connectivity, volume, and specific surface area [7, 8].
Based on these properties, various commercial materials were evaluated to
determine their suitability for methane adsorption, leading to the selection of
commercial zeolites for this study. Accordingly, the following sections discuss
the operational principles of this technology, the development of adsorbent
materials optimized for CH4, the results of laboratory tests performed on
a capture system prototype, and the final design of a system intended for
installation in livestock barns. A comprehensive discussion of the remaining
CERN strategies is available in Ref. [5].

2. LHC gas recirculation and recuperation systems
The active medium in gas detectors consists of gas mixtures enclosed
within a region where an electric field is generally present. As ionizing par-
ticles traverse the gas, they generate primary ionization, and the resulting
signals are collected by dedicated electrodes. To mitigate the accumulation of
space charge and impurities in high-rate applications — such as experiments
at the LHC — the gas is typically circulated through the detectors. The
effluent mixture, referred to as “exhausted”, primarily comprises the original
components along with potential impurities. Consequently, through appro-
priate filtration, these components can be reclaimed and integrated back into
the fresh mixture supplied to the detectors.
Gas recirculation systems are therefore designed to facilitate the reuse of
exhausted gases following its circulation through the detectors. As illustrated
in green in Fig. 1, the gas is collected upon exiting the active volume, pu-
rified, and re-injected into the detector system. In principle, a recirculation
fraction of up to 99% is achievable, depending upon the degree of mixture
contamination. A primary limiting factor is the accumulation of impurities
such as nitrogen (N2), which are not fully eliminated by standard filtration
processes.
At the LHC, most gas systems currently achieve recirculation
rates exceeding 90% [9]. However, the mandatory injection of the remaining
10% fresh gas mixture constitutes a persistent and non-negligible source of
GHG emissions.
In contrast, gas recuperation systems are designed to recover high-value
or environmentally detrimental gases from the exhaust stream. In red in
Fig. 1, the effluent gas from the detectors is routed to a dedicated separation

more components of the mixture while permitting the others to flow down-
stream [11].

2.1. The CF4 recuperation system
This system is employed for the recovery of the CSCs gas mixture and
comprises several components designed to efficiently isolate the target gas,
as shown in Fig. 2. The initial gas mixture consists of approximately 50%
CO2, 40% Ar, and 10% CF4. Upon exiting the detectors, the effluent gas —
now carrying contaminants such as O2 and N2 — is routed into the recovery
line. The objective is the sequential separation of all mixture components,
via the following specialized modules:
1. a membrane module for bulk CO2 separation;
2. a 4 Å MS for the removal of residual CO2;
3. a 13X MS for the selective absorption of CF4;
4. a CF4 compressor and corresponding storage tanks.
The membrane module is engineered to extract the bulk of the CO2 from
the mixture. However, due to imperfect selectivity, minor fractions of CF4
may be lost with the CO2 stream, while some CO2 remains in the primary
gas flow. To eliminate this residual CO2, the mixture is channeled through a
subsequent module containing 4 Å zeolites (pore diameter ∼0.4 nm). These
zeolites selectively adsorb CO2, permitting CF4 to bypass in the next phase.
This stage is crucial because the downstream 13X zeolite module (pore size
∼1 nm) is capable of adsorbing both CO2 and CF4. The main differences
between zeolite types are discussed in Sec. 4.2. Consequently, the upstream
removal of CO2 is necessary to maximize CF4 selectivity and recovery yield.
Ar and N2 exhibit negligible interaction with the adsorbents, occupying the

demonstrate high adsorption capacities for CH4.

3. Preliminary considerations on dedicated filter materials
As introduced in the previous section, the starting point of the analysis
of adsorbent materials dedicated to methane capture is the observation that
both CH4 and CF4 are tetrahedral molecules with similar molecular dimen-
sions (Fig. 3). For this reason, it is reasonable to begin the analysis of filter
materials with commercial zeolites, particularly Z5, which are widely used in
capture systems and which, as discussed in Section 4, will also be employed
in the prototype developed in this study.
Zeolite Socony Mobil-5 (ZSM-5 or simply Z5) was first patented by Mobil
Oil in 1975 and has since been extensively studied for gas adsorption. Z5
(Fig. 4) features a Mobil Five (MFI) framework topology, which is built by
connecting pentasil silicate chains. This structure forms a system of inter-
secting 10-membered ring channel: one sinusoidal channel with a pore size
of 5.1 × 5.5 Å along the [100] axis, and one straight channel with a pore size
of 5.3 × 5.6 Å along the [010] as described in Ref. [13]. The effective pore
opening size of Z5 (5 Å) exceeds the kinetic diameter of methane (3.8 Å), al-

4. Technology validation with laboratory scale prototype
The primary objectives of the technology validation phase are to assess
the feasibility of methane adsorption in a livestock housing environment and
to determine the adsorption capacity of CH4 under various operating con-
16


## Page 17


ditions. These aspects are critical for the design of the final prototype in-
tended for real-world validation.
To achieve this, a dedicated laboratory
setup was engineered to allow independent variation and precise control of
key parameters, enabling an accurate assessment of their individual impacts
on adsorption performance.
The initial phase of this study aimed to demonstrate the inherent capabil-
ity of zeolites to adsorb methane. Subsequently, the focus shifted to quanti-
fying the CH4 uptake across different commercial zeolite types to identify the
optimal candidate for practical deployment. Furthermore, the study evalu-
ated the regeneration of the adsorbent materials, specifically analyzing pro-
cess duration and efficiency to minimize both power consumption and system
downtime. Finally, environmental and operational parameters — namely hu-
midity, methane concentration, and system pressure — were systematically
varied to determine their influence on overall adsorption capacity.
4.1. Laboratory-scale prototype design
A custom laboratory-scale prototype was designed to conduct the adsorp-
tion experiments, as depicted in Fig. 10. The system can be conceptually
divided into three main sections: gas supply and conditioning, adsorption
stage, and analysis and regeneration line. The gas supply section comprises
two distinct lines: the primary carrier gas line utilizes compressed air to
modulate both the total flow rate and the relative humidity of the final mix-
ture. The secondary line acts as the methane source, supplying a calibrated
CH4-in-air mixture at a concentration of 4509 ppm. The flow rate of each line
is regulated by manual valves (HV-01, HV-02) and measured by dedicated
rotameters (FIV-01, FIV-02) with a measuring range of 3 L/h, allowing fine

deactivation of their adsorption capacity.

4.3. Zeolites regeneration
Subsequent testing focused on the regeneration of zeolites following methane
adsorption. For this process, Z5 and Z10 were evaluated, as they demon-
strated the most promising performance. The methods available for cartridge
regeneration were:
• Thermal Swing Adsorption (TSA);
• Vacuum Swing Adsorption (VSA).
The objective of these tests was to determine whether the adsorption capacity
of the zeolites could be fully restored using VSA, a method that offers lower
energy consumption compared to TSA. Therefore, during the experimental
procedure, the cartridges were saturated with methane and subsequently
evacuated using a vacuum pump at a pressure of −970 mbar.
This cycle
was repeated several times. During each iteration, the volume of methane
required to re-saturate the material was recorded.
Results are presented in Fig. 14: run 1 was performed after a TSA regen-
eration and it is taken as reference. The following runs, performed after VSA

4.8. Results summary
The tests conducted in the laboratory highlighted the following aspects:
1. It is possible to adsorb CH4 using commercial zeolites. Zeolites Z5 (5 Å)
and Z10 (13X) are the most effective commercially available zeolites for
capturing CH4.
2. Both regeneration processes, Thermal Swing Adsorption (TSA) and
32


## Page 33


Vacuum Swing Adsorption (VSA), are effective for regenerating zeolites
saturated with CH4.
VSA is more energy-efficient than TSA, thus
zeolite Z5 was ultimately selected as it is regenerated more effectively
with this method.
3. Scalability: the adsorption process is effective both on small volumes
(the test cartridges contain approximately 270 g of adsorbent material)
and on larger volumes, in the order of 2 kg.
4. Pressure effect: it has been demonstrated that the adsorption capacity
increases linearly with pressure (tested up to 5 bar). Physically, the
process is a balance between mechanical capture in the pores of the
zeolite and thermal agitation, which promotes the release of molecules.
Increasing the pressure shifts this balance in favor of capture.

6. Evaluation of the impact of the project
Assuming a baseline concentration of CH4 of approximately 100 ppm
within the livestock facility environment, roughly 100 kg of commercial Z5
zeolite adsorbent is required to capture 1 L of CH4. The current prototype
utilizes cartridges containing 2 kg of Z5, operating in an alternating sequence.
Consequently, at full saturation and an operating pressure of 1 bar, the sys-
tem achieves a maximum capture capacity of 40 mL of CH4. Increasing the
operating pressure to 3 bar yields a proportional increase, adsorbing approx-
imately 120 mL of CH4. Given that a single dairy cow emits approximately
164.9 kL (or 164.9 m3) of CH4 annually, averaging 450 L per day at 20 ◦C, the
present prototype does not yet have a substantial environmental or economic
impact. However, it successfully establishes a proof-of-concept for a highly
promising technology. System performance could be significantly enhanced
through the following modifications:
• Scaling the total mass of the Z5 zeolite adsorbent to 100 kg, distributed
across a battery of twenty 5 kg cartridges.
35


## Page 36


• Elevating the operating pressure up to 10 bar.
Assuming the linear correlation between adsorption capacity and gas pres-
sure extends beyond the currently tested experimental range, these modifi-
cations would enable the capture of up to 100 L of CH4 per operational cycle.
A more substantial optimization could be achieved by replacing the Z5 zeo-
lite with an advanced material possessing a markedly higher CH4 adsorption
capacity, ideally an order of magnitude higher. Such a material substitution
would theoretically allow the system to adsorb nearly the entirety of a single
animal annual CH4 emissions.
7. Conclusions
The CH4rLiE project has successfully demonstrated the technical feasi-
bility of adapting high-energy physics gas recuperation systems for reducing
GHG emissions in the agricultural sector. Laboratory validation of the pro-
totype shows that methane can be effectively captured and recovered even at
low concentrations through the integration of specific adsorbent materials.
Future work will focus on the field-testing of the full-scale system to evaluate
its long-term performance and stability in operational barn environments.
