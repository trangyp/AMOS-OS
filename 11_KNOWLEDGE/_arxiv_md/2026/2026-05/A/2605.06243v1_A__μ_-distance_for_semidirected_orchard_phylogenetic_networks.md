A µ-distance for semidirected orchard phylogenetic networks
Gerard Ribas1 Joan Carles Pons1 C´ecile An´e2,
Abstract—In evolutionary biology, phylogenetic networks are
now widely used to represent the historical relationships between
species and population, when this history includes reticulation
events such as hybridization, gene flow and admixture between
populations. Semidirected phylogenetic networks are appropriate
models when the direction of some edges and the root posi-
tion are not identifiable from data. Comparing semidirected
networks is important in many applications. For rooted and
directed networks, a µ-representation was originally introduced
to distinguish tree-child networks, and has since been extended in
two different directions: to the larger class of orchard directed
networks by adding an extra component that counts paths to
reticulations; and to semidirected networks, through an edge-
based variant. However, the latter does not provide a distance
between semidirected and orchard networks. We introduce here
a new edge-based µ-representation capable of distinguishing
distinct orchard binary semidirected networks. For this class,
we provide a reconstruction algorithm and therefore obtain a
true distance that is computable in polynomial time.

I. INTRODUCTION
P
HYLOGENETIC networks have become central to the
representation of biological processes such as hybridiza-
tion, gene flow, admixture, or horizontal gene transfer, by
which genetic material is transferred non-vertically. These
processes, generally referred to as ‘reticulations’, can be
represented in rooted phylogenetic networks, which generalize
rooted trees. In these networks, populations or individuals that
received genetic materials from multiple parents are repre-
sented as ‘hybrid’ nodes with multiple incoming edges. Many
inference methods are now available to estimate phylogenetic
networks from large genomic data [see e.g. 1, 2, 3, for
reviews]. Many of these methods infer a semidirected network,
which is the most that may be identifiable under many models,
instead of a rooted and fully directed network [e.g. 4, 5, 6, 7].
In semidirected networks, hybrid edges (that go into hybrid
nodes) remain directed. The other edges may be undirected.
The exact position of the root is constrained by the direction of
hybrid edges, but is generally unknown, and is not identifiable
under many models.
Multi-rooted networks have recently been considered [8,
9, 10, 11]. These networks may be useful to study groups
whose ancestral relationships of common ancestry may be too
difficulty to be recovered reliably, when primary interest lies
in the more recent events of gene transfer or introgression
between these groups.
Comparing phylogenetic networks is fundamental to many
endeavors. For example, measuring the dissimilarity between

estimated networks and the ground truth network is crucial in
simulation studies, to quantify the accuracy of an inference
method, or to compare the accuracy of different methods.
Comparing networks is also fundamental to quantify net-
work variability within posterior sample of networks from a
Bayesian analysis. Distances between networks in a sample
may be used, for example, to visualize the posterior dis-
tribution with distance-based dimension reduction or detect
clustering in this distribution.
Several dissimilarity measures have been proposed for
rooted networks, either of polynomial time complexity [e.g.
12, 13, 14, 15] or NP-hard [16, 17]. When choosing a
dissimilarity for some application, one has to choose between a
polynomial complexity or the power of discriminating between
any two non-isomorphic networks (with a non-zero dissim-
ilarity). Indeed, the isomorphism problem is not generally
solvable in polynomial time for rooted network [18, 19], and
therefore not for semidirected networks either (as semidirected
networks include rooted networks).
For semidirected networks with a single root (of unknown
placement) [20] proposed the cut-edge-transfer distance, but it
is NP-hard to compute. Only one polynomial-time dissimilar-
ity has been proposed so far for semidirected networks: [11]
considered general phylogenetic semidirected networks and
showed their dissimilarity to be a distance in the class of tree-
child (non-binary) semidirected networks. Their dissimilarity
is based on an encoding of the network, called an edge-based
µ-representation, in which each edge contributes an entry that
records the number of paths to each leaf starting from this
edge in either one or both directions. This encoding was first
introduced by [12] for rooted networks, with one entry per
node. Here we extend the edge-based encoding of [11] in two
ways. First, we include the number of paths to hybrid nodes
as in [21]. Second, the encoding stores two vectors for any
edge that may be subdivided to place the root, enriching the
approach by [11]. We prove that the dissimilarity obtained by
comparing our µ-representation of two networks provides a
distance, in the space of orchard semidirected binary networks.
Orchard networks received a lot of attention in the rooted
case [22, 23, 18, 24, 25], forming a large class that includes so-
called tree-child networks. They are computationally attractive
thanks to their recursive reducibility via tree cherries and retic-
ulate cherries, which are small local structures adjacent to two
leaves. Reducing these cherries decreases the complexity of
the network, which enables inductive arguments, and efficient
reconstruction and comparison algorithms.
In the semidirected context, Holtgrefe et al. [26] recently
defined orchard networks, with one or multiple roots. Com-
pared to their work, we mostly focus here on binary networks
but consider a large class of semidirected networks, in which
any edge may have a fixed direction, beyond hybrid edges

II. SEMIDIRECTED NETWORKS
We follow definitions from Maxfield et al. [11].
Definition 1. A semidirected graph N is a tuple (V, E), where
V is the set of vertices, and E = EU ⊔ED is the set of
edges, EU being the set of undirected edges and ED the set
of directed edges.
If an edge e ∈E is incident to nodes u and v in V , we
denote e as uv generally. To indicate directionality or lack
thereof, we denote e as uv if e ∈EU, otherwise as −→
uv if
e ∈ED is directed from u to v.
A directed graph is a semidirected graph with no undirected
edges, i.e. EU = ∅.
For v ∈V , degi(v, N) denotes the number of directed edges
with v as their child, dego(v, N), the number of directed edges
with v as their parent, degu(v, N) the number of undirected
edges in N incident to v, and deg(v) = degi(v) + dego(v) +
degu(v) the degree of v. We may omit N when no confusion
is likely. For an edge −→
uv, we say that u is its parent and v is
its child. Hence u is a parent of v and v is a child of u. Let
v be a node in N. Then,
• v is called a root if degi(v) = degu(v) = 0, i.e. if it only
has outgoing edges,
• v is called a leaf if dego(v) = degu(v) = 0,
• v is called a tree node if degi(v) ≤1,
• v is called a hybrid node or a reticulation node if
degi(v) > 1.
Furthermore, we shall say that N is binary if roots have
degree 0, 2 or 3, leaves have degree 0 or 1, and the remaining
nodes have degree 3.

Given a set of taxa L, an L-network N = (V, E, φ) is
an SDAG (V, E) whose leaves are tree nodes, and with an
injection φ from its leaves to L. We do not require φ to be a
bijection because we will consider reductions that prune a leaf.
Keeping L unchanged, including labels that may be absent
from N will simplify notations. Unless said otherwise, we will
assume that L = [n] = {1, . . . , n} for some positive integer
n, which corresponds to choosing a fixed ordering of the full
set of leaf labels.
We say N is a trivial forest on L0 ⊆L if N is the union
of isolated nodes labeled bijectively with L0.
As in [11], for nodes u, v ∈V we denote u ∼v if
there is a path connecting u and v that consists solely on
undirected edges. Note that ∼is an equivalence relation, and
we shall denote [u] the class of u under ∼. The contraction
of N, denoted Cont(N), is the directed graph obtained by
contracting every undirected edge in N, which can be seen as
the quotient graph of N under ∼, i.e. Cont(N) = N/∼, with
vertices {[u]|u ∈V }. Note that N/∼might not be a binary
L-network (nor a L-network) even if N is. We say that N is
complete if edges incident to leaves are directed (to the leaves)
and degi([u], N/∼) = 0 for every class [u] with more than
one node.

L-networks to be complete and binary, and will be typically

Lemma 1. Let u, x be nodes in an L-network N, which may
be non-binary. Let e be an edge incident to u which forms
a path from u to its other node v, that is, e = uv or −→
uv.
Then, M(u, x; e)⊔M(v, x; e) is in bijection with M(u, x). In
particular, m(u, x) = m(u, x; e) + m(v, x; e).
Proof. Let f : M(u, x; e)⊔M(v, x; e) −→M(u, x) defined as

A. Cherries in L-networks
Definition 2 (semidirected cherry). Let N be an L-network
and a ̸= b non-isolated leaves in N. Let pa and pb be the
parents of a and b in N. We call (a, b) a tree cherry in N
if pa = pb. We call (a, b) a reticulate cherry in N if pa is a
hybrid node and if pb is a parent of pa. We say that (a, b) is
a cherry in N, or reducible in N, if it is either a tree cherry
or a reticulate cherry.
If (a, b) is a reticulate cherry, let v be the node adjacent to pa
other than a and pb. We say that −−→
pbpa is the cherry’s internal
edge and −→
vpa is the cherry’s external edge.
Note that cherries in semidirected networks have already
been defined by Holtgrefe et al. [26]. In their work, semidi-
rected networks may also have multiple roots. They are defined
slightly less generally than here, but our definition of cherries
is equivalent. In [26], semidirected networks must come from
the ‘deorientation’ of a (multi-)rooted network, by which all
tree edges become undirected. Effectively, this means that
directed edges outgoing from root components must be hybrid
edges, whereas we allow them to be tree edges. In [26],
networks may not have parallel edges, whereas we do not
impose this restriction (as in N2 from Fig. 2 for example).
Also, in [26] the label set of a network may be strictly larger
than its leaf set, as roots may be labelled and have degree 1.
Their definition of ‘binary’ semidirected networks also differs
from ours, as roots must be of degree 1 or 2. However, this
difference is only technical, as they favor ‘nice’ rootings to
obtain rooted networks, in which roots are placed at existing
nodes. The exception is that a new root may subdivide a hybrid
edge whose parent is a labelled root, which then becomes a
labelled leaf. Our framework focuses on edges, with rootings
that subdivide edges as in the traditional tree framework, and
with sets of admissible edges (for rooting) that include directed
edges outgoing from a root component. However, [26] define
cherries accordingly, such that their definition and Definition 2
become equivalent.
We will often need to distinguish between different types
of cherries, illustrated in Fig. 3.
Definition 3 (cherry types). Let (a, b) be a cherry in an L-
network N, and pa, pb as in Def. 2. If pb is a resolved root,
we say that (a, b) is of type (r2). If pb is an unresolved root,
(a, b) is of type (r3). Otherwise, let e be the edge incident to
pb other than pbb and pba or pbpa. We say that (a, b) is of type
(d) if e is directed, and of type (u) if e is undirected. We also
prepend T or R for tree and reticulate cherries, respectively.
For example, (a, b) is of type T(r2) if it is a tree cherry of
type (r2).
Definition 4 (node suppression). A node v in a L-network N
is said suppressible if it is not a leaf and either
• v is of degree 2 and {v} is not a root component, or
• v is of degree 1, {v} is a root component, and its child
edge is a tree edge.
If v is suppressible, suppressing v from N consists in deleting
v and its incident edges, and in case v was of degree 2,
connecting its former neighbors u and w by a new edge uw.
If v was incident to at least one directed edge, say −→

Lemma 21 ([18, Lemma 8]). Let S = s1 . . . sk be a complete
reduction sequence for an orchard L-network N such that s2
is a cherry in N. Then, N s2 is orchard.
Proof. Note that s1 = (a, b) and s2 = (c, d) are cherries
in N by assumption. Let pa, pb, pc and pd be the parents of
a, b, c and d respectively (which must exist). We consider the
following cases:
• s1 = s2 (e.g. in N2 from Fig. 2 with parallel edges).
Then, N s1 = N s2 is orchard.
• s2 = (b, a). Then, both (a, b) and (b, a) are cherries in N,
and thus they must be tree cherries in N. Therefore, the
leaf a is not present in N s1, hence s2 is not a cherry in
N s1 and S is not a reduction sequence: this case cannot
occur.
• c = a and d ̸= a, b. Then, (a, b) and (a, d) are reticulate
cherries in N, pa is a hybrid node and its two hybrid
incident edges are pbpa and pdpa. Consequently, reducing
s1 and then s2 is the same as reducing s2 and then s1.
Therefore, as N s1s2 = N s2s1 is orchard, we have that
N s2 is orchard too.
• d = a and c ̸= a, b. As s2 = (c, a) is a cherry in N,
pa must be a tree node, and then s1 = (a, b) must be a
tree cherry. But then a is absent from N s1 and s2 cannot
be a cherry in N s1: a contradiction. Therefore this case
cannot occur.
• c = b and d ̸= a, b. As s1 = (a, b) is a cherry in N, pb
is a tree node. Then, as s2 = (b, d) is a also a cherry in
N, it must be a tree cherry and pb = pd. Therefore, pb
is a root of degree 3. By reducing (a, b) and then (b, d)
we obtain an isolated leaf labelled d. If instead we first
reduce s2 = (b, d), then pb is left unsuppressed in N s2,
so (a, d) is a cherry in N s2 of the same type as (a, b) in
N. Reducing it we obtain the same network as before:
N s2s1 = N s1s2. Therefore N s2 is orchard.
• d = b and c ̸= a, b. As s1 = (a, b) and s2 = (c, b) are
cherries in N, pb is adjacent to both cherries. Therefore,
pb is a root of degree 3 and, again, we obtain the same
result if we reduce first s1 and then s2 or first s2 and
then s1, so N s2 is orchard.
• c ̸= a, b and d ̸= a, b. Then, the reduction of one cherry
does not affect the other, so again we obtain N s1s2 =
N s2s1 and hence N s2 is orchard.
In all cases, we concluded that N s2 is orchard.

We are now ready to prove Proposition 5, the analog of
Proposition 1 from [18] for semidirected (binary) networks.
Proof of Proposition 5. Let (a, b) be a cherry in an orchard L-
network N. By Lemma 22, there exists a complete reduction
sequence S = s1 . . . sk for N such that one of the pairs of
S is either si = (a, b) or si = (b, a), and (a, b) is a cherry
in N s1...sj for every j ∈[i −1]. If i = 1, then s2 . . . sk
is a complete reduction sequence for N (a,b), hence N (a,b) is
orchard. Otherwise, we can assume that S is such that the
index i is the smallest possible, with the properties above.
If si = (a, b), then, we can apply Lemma 21 i times, to get
that N (a,b) is orchard.
If si = (b, a), then (a, b) and (b, a) are both cherries in
N s1...sj for every j ∈[i −1], so they must be tree cherries.
By Lemma 20, there exists a complete reduction sequence S′
for N starting with s1 . . . si−1(a, b). Then, changing S for S′
we can apply the previous case and thus N (a,b) is orchard,
