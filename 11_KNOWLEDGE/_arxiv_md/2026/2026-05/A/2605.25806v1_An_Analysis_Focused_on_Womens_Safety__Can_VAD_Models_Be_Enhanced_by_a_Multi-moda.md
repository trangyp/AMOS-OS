Abstract
Women’s safety and security are paramount for a modern society.
Crimes against women occur in daylight as well as in low-light con-
ditions. Often, such events are captured through real-world surveil-
lance cameras that operate at lower resolutions. Despite substantial
progress in CV-related research, video anomaly detection (VAD)
focused on women’s safety has not yet been adequately addressed.
Existing video anomaly datasets contain well-lit, high-resolution,
close-shot videos, and fail to represent women-centric anomalies
such as chain snatching, stalking, inappropriate touch, and other
subtle forms of crime against women. To address these problems,
we propose the ExtrAnom dataset, a new multi-modal benchmark
containing 1001 videos with textual descriptions, 500 normal and
501 anomalous, classified into 5 different types of women-centric
crimes. The dataset comprises low-light (8%), low-resolution videos
(13%), long-shot (15%), along with daylight (64%) anomalous videos.
And it covers anomalous events like stalking (3.9%), chain snatching
(17.6%), kidnapping (7.3%), assassinations (2.3%), harassment (18.9%),
and normal (50%). Each video is supplemented with 4 textual anno-
tations, including one human-generated and three LLM-generated
descriptions, enabling cross-modal and VLM-based validations. The
aim of creating a women-centric dataset is to accurately detect the
women-centric anomaly patterns, which are possible to observe
visually. The dataset supplements the VLMs to accurately generate
video-level descriptions. ExtrAnom has been benchmarked against
popular unimodal and multi-modal VAD datasets (e.g., XD-Violence,
UCF-Crime, and UCA) and SOTA methods. Experiments reveal that
the existing datasets are insufficient to train models for detecting
women-centric anomalies. This research directly addresses the sig-
nificant lack of resources for producing high-quality video descrip-
tions for these events. The dataset and supplementary documents
can be found in https://github.com/A24CS09005/ExtrAnom

Introduction
Video Anomaly Detection (VAD) is crucial for identifying unusual
behavior, patterns, and events for public safety systems. With the ad-
vent of AI and CV, it is now possible to find technological solutions
to tackle such events, patterns, and behaviour. As a precursor, re-
search has already made significant inroads to automatically under-
stand traffic anomalies [20, 24], industrial anomalies [7, 44], public
place violence [26, 32, 37, 38], etc. Recently, vision language mod-
els [41] have reshaped VAD by incorporating textual data, which
enable the detection of fine-grained anomalies, subtle differences,
and improves interpretability.
Women’s safety and security remain the biggest concern, as
crimes against women are rapidly increasing. According to the
UN Women and the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime
(UNODC) report published in November 2024 [17], around 85,000
women and girls lost their lives in 2023 due to abuse. According to
WHO’s report [34], one out of every three women (≈33%) experi-
ences violence in some form in their life. Nowadays, harassment,
chain snatching, stalking, and kidnapping happen frequently at
public places, and such events are captured in CCTV cameras. Out
of these, stalking is the most difficult pattern to identify as it is dif-
ficult to separate stalking from normal interactions. If VAD models
can identify stalking patterns early, it may lead to the prevention
of leading crimes. A handful of VAD datasets have become popular
over the last couple of decades. Some of these datasets focus on
generalized abnormal behaviors [26, 28, 35, 38] while others contain
event-specific anomalies [2, 10]. Datasets like UCSD-Peds1 [10] and
UCSD-Peds2 [10] present 5 anomalies, including bike, cart, subway

Related Work
Video Anomaly Detection Datasets: The VAD datasets are pub-
licly available for anomaly detection and evaluating the perfor-
mance of video anomaly detection models. Subway Entrance [2]
and Subway Exit [2] contain one large video, and anomaly types
are walking in wrong direction, no payment, jumping, or squeezing,
etc. UCSD-Ped1 [10] and UCSD-Ped2 [10] contain soft anomalies
such as bike or cart movements. CUHK Avenue [14] contains 37
types of anomalies, including run, throw, and new objects. The
street scene [23] dataset contains two-way urban street scenes,
containing 17 anomaly types, including a biker outside the street,
a person sitting on the beach, dogs on the sidewalk, etc. CCTV-
Fights [22] contains only fight videos, and ADOC [16] contains 25
anomaly types, including riding a bike, birds flying, and cat/dog
movements. UBnormal [1] contains 22 anomalies, including run-
ning, falling, fighting etc. All the above datasets contain medium-
scale and soft anomalies. UCF-crime [26], UCA [38], RareAnom [28],
and XD violence [35] are large-scale, widely used datasets in the
field of anomaly detection. These datasets contain frequently oc-
curring anomalies, including person falls, abuse, fighting etc. UCF-
crime[26] and UCA[38] are single-source datasets, captured using
CCTV. UCA[38] is an extended form of UCF-Crime[26], which con-
tains textual descriptions of events for VLMs. XD-violence [35] is
captured from various sources, including CCTV, Movies, anima-
tion, and performed by actors. RareAnom [28] is a multi-source,
multi-view dataset that contains 17 classes, including women abuse
and kidnapping. However, most of the datasets are collected under
a controlled environment, contain high-resolution, intense light-
ing, soft anomaly videos, and do not contain a detailed description
of the event to train multi-modal LLMs. Limiting its capability to
distinguish from extreme anomalies in real-world scenarios with a
textual description.
Video Anomaly Detection Methods: The VAD models are widely
used in the field of road safety, industrial safety, and to detect
any kind of anomaly. Vision-based models are classified into semi-
supervised [13, 25], weakly-supervised [6, 26, 30] and unsuper-
vised [28, 29, 32]. Weakly supervised models [6, 26, 30] use both
normal and anomalous videos with video-level annotation to train
a model that differentiates between abnormal behavior to nor-
mal behavior. Unsupervised models require only normal videos
to train the model and do not rely on annotated data. Recently,
multi-modality methods involving video and audio [18] or video
and language [4, 11, 40, 41] have also been introduced in the field of
anomaly detection. Holmes-VAU [41] performs well in generating
clip-level descriptions, but struggles in event-level and video-level
descriptions.

3.1
Importance of the Dataset
ExtrAnom dataset addresses a few critical gaps in VAD research. (i)
Existing VAD datasets [26, 28, 35] have only a few number of videos
of kidnapping (<20) and harassment (<50) involving women. (ii) No
dataset covers incidents like inappropriate touch, chain snatching,
or stalking. (iii) Datasets such as XD Violence largely contain high-
resolution, well-lit and staged videos. (iv) UCF dataset videos are
close-shot in nature and lack the discriminating features that are
necessary to understand distant anomalies related to women. (v)
Inappropriate touch, which is part of the harassment category usu-
ally happens at public and crowded places and no dataset addresses
this category. (vi) Multidimensional reference set is unavailable in
other datasets. Thus, we propose ExtrAnom with a wide variety
of interpretive viewpoints and descriptive emphasis to address the
aforementioned shortcomings.
3.2
Dataset Collection and Annotation
Video Collection: The ExtrAnom dataset has been created by
collecting videos from various sources, including News channels,
Twitter, YouTube, Instagram, and other social media platforms. The
dataset comprises only real-world video footage, collected from
CCTV cameras, mobile phones, and handheld cameras. It contains
high as well as low resolution videos, with day or poor lighting
conditions, and events recorded using long-shot views. By low-light
and low-resolution, we mean when the objects of interest are not
properly visible due to insufficient ambient light. This results in
dim and dark visuals containing poor contrast, color distortion, and
noise that hampers object detection, leading to degraded perfor-
mance in the downstream applications.
Textual Annotations: The main goal of textual annotation is to
make a detailed video-level description for VLMs. The ExtrAnom
dataset contains 4 textual descriptions (one human-generated and
3 LLM-generated) using keywords that include objects and actions
used in the event. Using these keywords and prompts, we have
generated LLM-based descriptions. We have used 3 LLMs, namely
ChatGPT [19], DeepSeek [5], and Mistral [8]. Seven impartial volun-
teers completed the annotation process and verified the descriptions
generated by LLMs. Example descriptions of an event are shown in
Figure 5.

4.1
How Multi-modal LLMs Perform on
ExtrAnom?
First, we generated video-level descriptions of ExtrAnom using
8 pretrained models [4, 11, 15, 27, 33, 40, 41, 43] trained on UCF-
Crime [26] and XD-Violence [35]. We then compare the similarity
of the video-level descriptions with ground truths. The inference
part of Table 3 shows the performance of SOTA models on the pro-
posed dataset. To evaluate the similarity and quality of video-level
descriptions generated by multi-modal LLMs with ground truths,
we use metrics including BLEU [21], BERT [42], CIDEr [31], ME-
TEOR [3], and ROUGE [12]. Video-LLaMA [40], Video-LLaVA [11],
and LLaVA-Next-Video [43] perform poorly on ExtrAnom; the rest
models perform below average. Next, we fine-tune the latest model
Holmes-VAU [41] using the training split and compare the simi-
larity scores between the test split and ground truths. Similarity
scores in green color of fine-tune section of Table 3 show that the
model performance has significantly improved. We also generate
video-level descriptions using the latest training-free VLM, i.e.,
LAVAD [39], and compare the descriptions with the ground truths.
The results show poor performance on ExtrAnom. Multi-modal
LLMs [4, 11, 15, 27, 33, 40, 41, 43] are unable to spot anomalies that
appear to be normal interactions.
We provide a descriptive analysis of different Multi-modal LLMs
and ground truths. Figure 6 shows a sample description of chain

e.g., Holmes-VAU [41], also suffers from gender and attribute mis-
classifications. In several cases, the model incorrectly classifies a
woman as a man and falsely detects a gun. Due to the erroneous
identification of a man holding a gun, the overall interpretation of
the event shifts from a chain snatching incident to a potential threat.
As a result, the conclusion of the model is significantly changed. Sim-
ilarly, the Video-ChatGPT [15] model incorrectly describes the man
and woman hitting each other with a stick, resulting in a change
in interpretation of the event from a chain snatching incident to a
physical fight. InternVL3-8B [4] detects the presence of a vehicle
and assumes that the person arrives using one of the vehicles. The
model further infers cold weather conditions and fails to identify
any unusual behaviour in the video. Moreover, it is unable to deter-
mine the gender of the individual involved or wrongly estimates the
number of participants in the event. As a result, InternVL3-8B [4]
classifies the video as normal, concluding that no anomalous activ-
ity occurred. This leads to an incorrect interpretation, highlighting
the limitations of a model in gender classification and event under-
standing. Similarly, QwenVL3-7B [33] is unable to identify whether
the person is a man or a woman and provides an incomplete descrip-
tion of the event, failing to capture the purpose of an individual.
Multi-modal LLMs [4, 11, 15, 27, 33, 40, 41, 43] identify most of the
events as typical interactions, normal traffic flow, accidents, or just
background descriptions due to a lack of women-centric training
data.

4.3
Why a women-centric dataset?
SOTA multi-modal LLMs and vision-based models trained on exist-
ing VAD datasets perform poorly on women-centric anomalies as
indicated in Table 3 and Table 4. These models are unable to distin-
guish between common interactions and women-centric anomaly-
specific activities. For example, stalking, inappropriate touch, or
chain snatching exhibit interactions similar to normal activities.
However, SOTA models perform noticeably better after training
using ExtrAnom.
5
Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we propose and release ExtrAnom, a women-centric,
low-light, and low-resolution video dataset for VAD tasks. The
dataset includes different categories of women-centric anomalies,
along with human and LLM-generated video-level descriptions. We
have benchmarked the dataset on recently proposed multi-modal
LLMs and fine-tuned the latest VLM. We have compared video-level
descriptions with ground truths using well-known metrics. Our
findings reveal that the multi-modal LLMs still lack in generating
accurate video-level descriptions of women-centric crimes. Next,
we have validated the dataset on SOTA vision-based models. The
results highlight the importance of a women-centric dataset. The
future works involve the development of focused VLMs that can
accurately generate video-level descriptions of such events. More-
over, it will be interesting to observe if the dataset performs equally
well on anomalies involving men.
