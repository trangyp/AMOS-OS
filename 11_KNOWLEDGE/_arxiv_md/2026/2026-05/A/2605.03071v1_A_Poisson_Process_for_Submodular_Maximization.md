Abstract
We study the problem of maximizing a monotone submodular function subject to a matroid inde-
pendence constraint. For more than a decade, a rich body of work has studied this problem. Initially, a
tight approximation of (1 −1/e) was given using the continuous greedy algorithm [Calinescu-Chekuri-
Pal-Vondrák STOC‘2008] and later non-oblivious local search techniques were able to match this tight
approximation guarantee [Filmus-Ward FOCS‘2012] and [Buchbinder-Feldman FOCS‘2024].
We propose a new and remarkably simple approach to this problem that is based on a stochastic
Poisson process. Our approach matches the tight (1 −1/e) approximation guarantee and it differs from
the known two techniques since it does not require discretization or rounding while performing very
few single element swaps. We also present applications of our approach and obtain fast algorithms for
submodular welfare maximization, and for the general and separable assignment problems.

Introduction
Submodular maximization has been a central topic in combinatorial optimization for several decades and
its study dates back to the late 70’s [NW78, NWF78, FNW78]. The appeal of submodular functions lies
in their diminishing returns property: a set function f : 2U →R+ over a universe U is submodular if it
satisfies that f(S + i) −f(S) ≥f(T + i) −f(T) for every S ⊆T ⊆U and i ∈U \ T.1 Submodular
functions naturally arise in many disciplines, including rank in linear algebra, graph cuts in combinatorics,
and entropy in probability theory. Moreover, submodular functions play a major role in many real world
applications such as sensor placement and data summarization [KLG+08, KSG08, LB10, LB11, MBK16],
influence maximization in social networks [KKT03, HMS08, MR10] and feature selection in machine learn-
ing [LWK+13, KED+17, BHZ22] (see also, e.g., a more comprehensive survey [Bac13])
In this work we consider the problem of maximizing a monotone2 submodular function f : 2U →R+
subject to a matroid M = (U, I) independence constraint (denote this problem by Sub-Mat). A notable
running example is that of a partition matroid, where the universe U is partitioned into (U1, . . . , Uk) and
the matroid independence constraint reduces to finding a set S ⊆U that contains at most a single element
from each Uj in the partition. This special case is already sufficiently rich in structure, as it captures well-
known problems such as the Generalized Assignment Problem [FGMS06, FV06, CCPV11] and Submodular
Welfare [DS06, Von08, CCPV11, FNS11, Von13].

The main source of progress for the Sub-Mat problem has been the introduction of continuous methods
via the multilinear extension [Von08, CCPV11]. The multilinear extension F : [0, 1]U →R+ is defined as:
F(x) ≜P
S⊆U f(S) · Πi∈Sxi · Πi/∈S(1 −xi). It carries an intuitive probabilistic interpretation of F(x) =
ER∼x[f(R)], where R ∼x denotes a random subset in which every element i ∈U is independently chosen
to R with a probability of xi. In these continuous methods, one approximately solves a fractional relaxation
of the problem whose objective is the multilinear extension, that itself is NP-hard, and then converts the
fractional solution to an integral one via rounding. Besides obtaining a tight (1 −1/e)-approximation for
the Sub-Mat problem [CCPV11, NW78], these continuous approaches have been successfully extended to
accommodate various constraints, including multiple knapsacks, multiple matroids, and even more complex
independence systems, e.g., [CVZ11, KST13, BV14, FKS21].
Unfortunately, the continuous approach described above has several drawbacks. First, the implementa-
tion of continuous methods as an algorithm involves discretizing a continuous time process. Thus, in order
to bound the error caused by this discretization one is required to perform sufficiently small steps, resulting
usually in a large number of iterations. Second, these methods require numerous evaluations of the gra-
dient of the multilinear extension F. Typically, every such gradient evaluation requires a large number of
evaluations of the original function f, which can be computationally expensive (especially for large-scale
problems). Third, and of no less importance, the continuous viewpoint (multilinear extension) and the dis-
crete viewpoint (rounding) are applied in sequence and in isolation. While this can be achieved with no loss
when considering the Sub-Mat problem, it can be time consuming.
Alongside greedy algorithms, local search methods have also been developed, as early as [FNW78], as
an alternative way to approach constrained submodular maximization. More recently, local search methods
have been able to achieve some of the results first obtained by the continuous greedy based approach, e.g., an
asymptotically tight approximation for Sub-Mat [FW14, BF24b]. The improved local search methods work
on a modified objective that approximates the true objective f. The main benefit of local search approaches
is that they typically work directly with the function f and therefore do not require gradient computation
and rounding. The challenge of these approaches is that the running times usually have large dependence
on 1/ε [FW14, BF24b] to obtain approximation guarantees that are within ε of the best possible.
Our Hybrid Approach.
We present a hybrid approach that aims to combine the strengths of both contin-
uous and discrete approaches. Our algorithm maintains a feasible base of the matroid at every iteration and
updates this solution through a sequence of elementary exchanges, replacing one element at a time while
preserving feasibility. What sets our approach apart from local search methods is the way it implements
these updates. The timing of each potential update is governed by a non-homogeneous Poisson process.
Meanwhile, the decision of which elements to exchange is informed by dynamically computed weights
based on the gradient of the multilinear extension evaluated at an appropriate time scaling of the current
base.
This approach gives a new tight algorithm for Sub-Mat as well as additional applications including fast
algorithms for the Generalized Assignment Problem and Submodular Welfare.
1.1
Our Results
We present a remarkably simple stochastic process based on a non-homogeneous Poisson process, which we
denote by Greedy Swap Poisson Process (GS-Poisson), that yields a tight approximation for the Sub-Mat
problem. The stochastic process is so simple that we can state it now. To simplify presentation, we denote
by ∇iF(x) the coordinate that corresponds to element i ∈U of the gradient of the multilinear extension at
point x ∈[0, 1]U and by 1A ∈{0, 1}U the indicator vector of A ⊆U. The internal state of GS-Poisson
consists of a single base A ∈B that is initialized to be some arbitrary base (B denotes the collection of all

bases of M). Starting from time t = ε, a non-homogeneous Poisson process with rate λ(t) = k/t, where k

1. A base Z(t) ∈B that maximizes P

The above simple steps are repeated every time an event occurs, stopping at time t = 1.
There are three key features that set GS-Poisson apart from prior approaches:
(i) No discretization: Time is not discretized since the Poisson process is simulated directly by sampling
the time of the next event.
(ii) No rounding: The algorithm operates directly on bases; no rounding step is required.
(iii) Few updates: The expected number of single element swaps is only k ln(1/ε).
We note that (i) and (ii) above set GS-Poisson apart from the continuous greedy approach, whereas (iii) sets
it apart from the local search approach.
The following theorem states the tight approximation guarantee of GS-Poisson.
Theorem 1.1. Given a matroid M = (U, I), a monotone submodular function f : 2U →R+, and a
starting time 0 < ε ≤1, GS-Poisson finds A ∈I satisfying E[f(A)] ≥(1 −ε)(1 −1/e)f(O). Here
O = arg max{f(S) : S ∈I} is an optimal base.
We make two important observations. The first observation is the close connection of GS-Poisson to
the continuous greedy algorithm, rather than local search methods such as [FW14, BF24b]. One approach
to modify the continuous greedy algorithm while maintaining only an integral solution is to round on the
fly: only maintain a (randomized) integral solution obtained by rounding the current fractional solution.
Unfortunately, this approach does not work directly. GS-Poisson is in fact rounding on the fly, but of
an appropriately chosen scaling of the fractional solution maintained by the continuous greedy algorithm.
Thus, GS-Poisson can also be interpreted as rounding on the fly of a time-scaled version of the continuous
greedy algorithm. From this point of view, the continuous greedy naturally becomes a Frank-Wolfe style
continuous local search algorithm. We discuss this connection in Section 1.2 in further detail.
The second observation is that GS-Poisson does not require the best base Z(t) as defined above in
step (1) in order to output a good approximation. Instead, a carefully chosen pair of elements i ∈A and
j ∈U to be swapped is enough. In particular, the construction of the whole base Z(t) can be avoided. We
refer to the procedure which finds the pair i and j as a swap procedure. This simple observation will be the
main driving force behind our additional applications.
1.1.1
Applications
Our applications include fast algorithms, based on GS-Poisson, for partition and generalized partition ma-
troids, as well as the Generalized Assignment Problem and Separable Assignment Problem. In what follows,
when given a submodular function, the running time of the algorithm is measured by the number of function
evaluations it performs, as this is the standard measure for these types of problems. We assume oracle access
to either f or F and consider results for both.

Partition Matroid.
When M = (U, I) is a partition matroid there exists a partition (U1, . . . , Uk) of U
and a set S ∈I if and only if |S ∩Uj| ≤1 for each 1 ≤j ≤k. Thus, a solution S ⊆U to Sub-Mat when
M is a partition matroid is feasible if and only if S contains at most a single element from each part Uj.
The case of a partition matroid is of special interest, as it captures the classic problem of Submodular
Welfare. In the Submodular Welfare problem, we are given k players, a set of m items I and a monotone
non-negative submodular utility function fj : 2I →R+ for each player 1 ≤j ≤k. The goal is to find
disjoint subsets S1, . . . , Sk ⊆I that maximize the sum of the utilities: Pk
j=1 fj(Sj). Here, Sj denotes the
items assigned to player j. A simple approximation preserving reduction (see, e.g., [CCPV11]) shows that
Submodular Welfare can be reduced to Sub-Mat with a partition matroid of rank m and a universe of size
mk.
The following two theorems summarize the case when M is a partition matroid, the first when the
algorithm can query the multilinear extension F and the second when the algorithm can query the original
set function f.
Theorem 1.2. There exists an algorithm that given a partition matroid M = (U, I), a monotone sub-
modular function f : 2U →R+, and 0 < ε ≤1, performs in expectation O(n ln (1/ε)) evaluations of F
and finds A ∈I satisfying: E[f(A)] ≥(1 −ε)(1 −1/e)f(O). Here n is the size of the universe U and
O = arg max{f(S): S ∈I} is an optimal base.
Theorem 1.3. There exists an algorithm that given a partition matroid M = (U, I), a monotone submod-
ular function f : 2U →R+, and 0 < ε < 1/4, performs in expectation O(n log2(1/ε)/ε2) evaluations of
f and finds A ∈I satisfying E[f(A)] ≥(1 −ε)(1 −1/e)f(O). Here n is the size of the universe U and
O = arg max{f(S) : S ∈I} is an optimal base.
Let us compare the results of the above theorems with previously known tight (1 −ε)(1 −1/e) approx-
imations. When querying F, the best known algorithm was given (implicitly) by [BV14] and it requires
O(n/ε) evaluations of F, while Theorem 1.2 requires only O(n ln (1/ε)) evaluations in expectation. When
considering f, the best known algorithm was given by [EN19b] and it requires O(n log2 (n/ε)/ε5) evalua-
tions of f (see also [KT24, BF24b] and the discussion in Section 1.3 for additional incomparable bounds),
where Theorem 1.3 requires only O(n log2(1/ε)/ε2) evaluations in expectation. It should be noted that
Theorem 1.3 provides the first linear-time algorithm for the problem.
It is worth noting that in order to prove Theorem 1.3 we establish an interesting connection with the
classic Multi-Arm Bandits problem [EMM06]. This enables us to obtain the first linear time algorithm for
the problem.

Second, consider taking the limit as the step size δ approaches 0. Rounding on the fly as described
above provides an approximation of (1 −1/e −O(δ · poly(n))), hence it also suffers from a loss introduced
by discretization of the continuous process. Previous works ([CCPV11]) as well as [BV14] and subsequent
follow up works) aim to take the step size δ as large as possible to reduce the number of iterations, while
bounding the loss δ incurs in the approximation. We adopt the opposite approach and consider the limit as
δ approaches 0.
In the limit as δ approaches 0, to the first order, applying the random variant of pipage rounding on
(t1A(t) + δ1Z(t))/(t + δ) boils down to one of the following two: (1) setting A(t + δ) to be A(t) w.p.
≈1 −δk/t; and (2) swapping a single uniform random element i ∈A(t) with h(i) w.p. ≈δk/t. The
crucial, yet trivial, observation is that if the former happens, i.e., A(t + δ) = A(t), then from an algorithmic
perspective nothing needs to be done! Since the random variant of pipage rounding is oblivious to the
objective, all that is needed is to understand the time of the next swap in this continuous process as δ
approaches 0. Calculating these limits, one can show that this is exactly given by a Poisson process with
rate λ(t) = k/t (as in GS-Poisson) that we analyze directly.
The Swap Procedure.
Following the above, we observe that we do not need to compute Z(t) when
executing GS-Poisson. It is enough, once an event in the Poisson process occurs, to find a suitable swap of
a single element in A(t). This simple yet important observation is the driving force behind our applications.
Refer to Section 3 for more details.
We establish an interesting connection between the swap procedure and the classic Multi-Arm Bandits
problem [EMM06]: given a collection of random variables accessible by samples alone, find the one whose
expectation is the highest. The error introduced by the algorithm of [EMM06] depends on the support of the
distribution, and in order to bound it we use a preprocessing algorithm that ensures that the effective support
is sufficiently small.
1.3
Related Work
Algorithms for submodular maximization have been studied as early as the late 70’s, with the seminal work
of [NWF78] who proved that greedy achieves an approximation of (1−1/e) for monotone submodular max-
imization subject to a cardinality constraint, i.e., a uniform matroid independence constraint. A matching
hardness of (1 −1/e) in the oracle model was given by [NW78], i.e., any algorithm that achieves an approx-
imation better than (1 −1/e) is required to perform exponentially many queries to f. Later, Feige [Fei98]
proved that assuming P ̸= NP the bound of (1 −1/e) is also tight for the special case of a coverage func-
tion. When considering a general matroid independence constraint, i.e., the Sub-Mat problem, it was shown
by [FNW78] that both the discrete local search and the greedy algorithms achieve an approximation of 1/2.
This problem was subsequently settled with the work of [CCPV11] who presented a tight approximation
of (1 −1/e) by introducing the continuous greedy algorithm and the use of the multilinear extension for
submodular maximization. Later algorithms that are based on non-oblivious local search [FW14, BF24b]
are able to match the above tight (1 −1/e) approximation for a general matroid. The continuous greedy ap-
proach has been successfully extended to accommodate various constraints, including multiple knapsacks,
multiple matroids, and even more complex independence systems, e.g., [CVZ11, KST13, BV14, FKS21].
When considering general submodular functions, which are not necessarily monotone, an approxima-
tion of 1/2 was given by [BFNS15] for the unconstrained case by introducing the double greedy algorithm.
A matching (1/2)-hardness for the unconstrained case was given by [FMV11]. When considering a gen-
eral matroid independence constraint, a long sequence of works [LMNS10, Von13, GV11, FNS11, EN16,
BF19, BF24a] presents ever improving guarantees, culminating in the current best-known approximation of
≈0.401 [BF24a]. All these works are based on continuous greedy and local search approaches. A hardness

The Greedy Swap Poisson Process
Recall that in Section 1.1 a description of Greedy Swap Poisson Process (GS-Poisson) was given. First,
the internal state is initialized to be an arbitrary base A ∈B. Second, a non-homogeneous Poisson process
with rate k/t is executed starting at time ε and terminating at time 1. Third, if the next event of the Poisson
process occurs at time t then: (1) a base Z(t) ∈B maximizing ⟨1Z(t), ∇F(t1A)⟩is found; (2) a bijection
h: A →Z(t) that satisfies A−i+h(i) ∈B, for every i ∈A, is computed; and (3) a uniform random i ∈A
is chosen and A ←A −i + h(i) (i is swapped with h(i)).

Poisson that uses a swap procedure. The swap procedure is a (possibly) randomized algorithm that is given
a base A ∈B and a time ε ≤t ≤1, and returns a pair (i, j) of elements i ∈A and j ∈U such that
A −i + j ∈B.
