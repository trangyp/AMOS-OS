Amicable lattice rhombuses are equable
Bohdan Biekietov
Iwan Praton
Weiran Zeng
Abstract
A polygon is equable if its area is equal to its perimeter. A pair of poly-
gons is an amicable pair if the area of the first is equal to the perimeter
of the second, and vice versa. A polygon is a lattice polygon if its vertices
lie on the integer lattice. We show that amicable lattice rhombuses are
actually equable.
1
Introduction
A polygon is equable if its area is equal to its perimeter. Two polygons form an
amicable pair if the area of one is equal to the perimeter of the other, and vice
versa. Slightly abusing terminology, we say that a polygon is amicable if it is one
of an amicable pair. Without further conditions, it is easy to find many equable
and amicable polygons, so we usually restrict the kind of polygons we consider.
In this paper we concentrate on lattice polygons, i.e., polygons whose vertices
lie on the integer lattice.
It has long been known that there are only five equable lattice triangles (see
the proof in the appendix of [1]). It turns out there is only one pair of amicable
lattice triangles [2]. There are two equable lattice rectangles (one of which is a
square), and there are five pairs of amicable lattice rectangles [3]. In this paper
we take a look at rhombuses. There are two equable rhombuses on the integer
lattice ([1], Corollary 1(a)); in this paper we investigate amicable rhombuses
instead.
We first need to be more explicit on what we mean by an amicable pair.
An equable polygon paired with itself forms an amicable pair, but it’s a trivial
pair. In this paper, we allow such trivial pairs since they arise from nontrivial
solutions to the equations we use below. Our results show that all amicable
lattice rhombuses are actually equable.

We first note some general properties of amicable parallelograms on the
integer lattice. Recall that the side lengths of an amicable polygon are inte-
gers (Lemma 2 in [3]). Suppose we have an amicable pair of parallelograms,
one with side lengths x and y, and the other with side lengths a and b, where
x, y,a,b are positive integers. These side lengths completely determine the two
parallelograms: if θ is an interior angle of the first parallelogram, then its area is
xy sinθ, which is equal to 2(a+b), so sinθ = 2(a+b)/(xy). The angle of the sec-
ond parallelogram is also determined by x, y,a,b. Thus it suffices to describe
an amicable pair by providing their side lengths.

curves. Take (∗) for example. It can be rewritten as α2β2 −32(α+β) = s2, where
