ACTION GRAPHS, SEMICONJUGACY, AND NON-EMBEDDING IN
THOMPSON’S GROUP V
JAMES HYDE, RACHEL SKIPPER, AND MATTHEW C. B. ZAREMSKY
Abstract. We prove a variety of results about subgroups of Thompson’s group V . First
we prove that every action graph of a finitely generated subgroup of V acting on an orbit in
Cantor space is quasi-isometric to a tree. Then we prove that for a broad class of groups of
homeomorphisms of the real line, for example Thompson’s group F, any action on the Cantor
space via an embedding into Thompson’s group V must be semiconjugate to the standard
action on the line. Finally, we use this to establish that many such groups cannot embed into
V ; in particular the Stein group F2,3 cannot embed in V , answering a question of the third
author.
Introduction
Thompson’s group V is the group of homeomorphisms h of Cantor space C = {0, 1}N that
are locally given by prefix replacements, i.e., for all κ ∈C there exist a finite prefix w of κ and
another finite string u such that (wκ′)h = uκ′ for all κ′ ∈C. (Throughout this paper we use
right actions, and correspondingly write functions to the right of their arguments.) This group,
together with its subgroup T, was the first known example of a finitely presented infinite
simple group, and a significant amount of research has been dedicated to understanding it.
Our first main result of this paper is that every action graph of a finitely generated subgroup
G ≤V acting on an orbit in C is a quasi-tree. More precisely:
Theorem A. Let G be a finitely generated subgroup of V , with S a finite symmetric generating
set for G. Let X be the G-orbit of some κ0 ∈C, and let Γ be the action graph for G acting on X,
that is, the Schreier graph with respect to the subgroup StabG(κ0). Then Γ is quasi-isometric
to a tree.
When G = V , this result is easy to see, but it is somewhat surprising that it holds for all
finitely generated G ≤V . Notably, it holds despite the inclusion G →V sometimes failing to
induce a quasi-isometric embedding of action graphs.

Remark. The term “semiconjugacy” is not entirely uniquely defined in the literature, so let
us emphasize what we mean. For us a semiconjugacy is a continuous surjective G-equivariant
map from one G-space to another. Sometimes in the literature semiconjugacies are only
considered from a G-space to itself. Additionally, continuity is sometimes not included in the
definition, with the term topological semiconjugacy used when the assumption of continuity is
included. Since our results are of the form, “these things are semiconjugate,” using a stronger
definition gives us stronger results.
The second main result of this paper is that, for F and groups similar to F, not only is
this “standard” copy of the group inside V semiconjugate to the standard copy acting on
S1 = R ∪{∞}, but every copy of the group inside V is semiconjugate to the standard copy
on S1.
Theorem B. Let E ≤Homeo+(R) be finitely generated, locally moving with abelian germs
(Definitions 2.1 and 2.4), and FG-filtered (Definition 3.6). Then for any abstract embedding of
E into V , the resulting action of E on C is semiconjugate to the standard action of E on S1.
The quintessential example of a group satisfying these hypotheses is Thompson’s group F,
so we conclude that for every copy of F in V , the action of this copy on C is semiconjugate
to the standard action of F. We remark that there do exist copies of F in V that are not
conjugate to the standard one (see Example 4.9).
As we said, it is convenient to work with the circle S1 = R ∪{∞}, so that there is a unique
global fixed point. Given the focus on S1, one might wonder for example whether Theorem B
holds for things like Thompson’s group T. However, since semiconjugacies send global fixed
points to global fixed points, this cannot hold if the given action of T fixes a point in C, which
is easy to achieve. It would be interesting to try and formulate an appropriate statement
about copies of T in V , but we will not pursue this here.
There is quite a bit of literature on situations where arbitrary actions on the line or circle
are semiconjugate to standard actions. For example, in [BMBRT21] the authors prove many
results involving semiconjugacies from the line to itself, typically for groups that are locally
moving, meaning the rigid stabilizer of each open interval has no global fixed points in the
open interval. For example [BMBRT21, Theorem 5.3.5] says that every action of Thompson’s
group F on R by C1 diffeomorphisms without discrete orbits is semiconjugate to the standard
action. Another example is [BMBRT21, Theorem 7.2.2], which provides semiconjugacy rigidity
for certain groups acting on the circle. We should also mention fundamental work of Rubin
in [Rub89, Rub96] on strong rigidity properties of locally dense actions; see also [BEM25].
Finally, see [HM23, Lod20] for related work on the interval and circle including non-embedding
results.
Our main application of Theorem B is to rule out certain groups (abstractly) embedding in
V . For example, we prove that the Stein group F2,3 cannot embed in V , answering a question
of the third author.

Theorem C. Let E ≤Homeo+(R) be robust (Definition 3.10), FG-filtered (Definition 3.6),
and tame at ∞(Definition 3.11). If E embeds in V , then every finitely generated subgroup of
the group of germs of E at ∞is cyclic.
Here the group of germs at ∞of E is the quotient of E by the normal subgroup of all
elements f such that f fixes some open ray (a, ∞). We will also use the group of germs at x
of a group G acting on a topological space X with x ∈X, which is the quotient of StabG(x)
by the normal subgroup of all elements g such that g fixes an open neighborhood of x. In
either case, the image of an element in the group of germs is the germ of the element.
Corollary D. The Stein group F2,3 does not embed in V .
The Stein group F2,3, first studied in depth by Stein in [Ste92] along with a broad family of
related groups, is the group of all piecewise-linear orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of
[0, 1] with breakpoints in Z[1/6] and slopes of the form 2m3n (m, n ∈Z). Identifying (0, 1)
with R, the group of germs of F2,3 at ∞is isomorphic to Z2, i.e., not cyclic. The Stein group
naturally contains F, and also the ternary relative F3 of F. In [Lod20], Lodha proved that
F2,3 cannot abstractly embed as a subgroup of F (see also [HM23] for another proof by the
first author and Moore), so Corollary D is a strengthening of this. The proofs in [Lod20] and
[HM23] do not seem to have easy analogs working with Cantor space, so instead we use the
semiconjugacies we construct in Theorem B to prove Theorem C and Corollary D.
One source of interest in the question of which groups embed in V is Lehnert’s conjecture,
which predicts that every co-context-free group embeds in V ; see [BMN16, Leh08, LS07]. We
do not know whether F2,3 is co-context-free, though we would expect it is not.

1. Action graphs
Throughout, we view connected graphs as metric spaces by giving each edge length 1 and
using the usual path metric. We will mostly focus on vertex sets of graphs, with the induced
metric coming from the path metric on the graph.
Definition 1.1 (Action graph). Let G be a group acting on a set X, and let S be a finite
symmetric subset of G. The action graph AGX(G, S) associated to these data is the directed
graph with vertex set V (AGX(G, S)) = X and a directed edge (x, x.s) for each x ∈X and
s ∈S.
For example if X = G with the usual translation action, then AGG(G, S) is the Cayley
graph Cay(G, S). More generally if X = H\G for some H ≤G then the action graph is the
Schreier graph. Note that an action graph is connected if and only if the subgroup of G
generated by S acts transitively on X. In this case, the action graph is canonically isomorphic
to the Schreier graph for the subgroup H = StabG(x) for any fixed x ∈X.
Now we specialize to action graphs of subgroups of Thompson’s group V .

edges (κ, 0κ) and (κ, 1κ) for each κ ∈C (allowing for loops). Note that the degree of each
vertex is 3, with one incoming and two outgoing edges. For a given κ ∈C, write QT κ for the

Now we can prove Theorem A, that Γ = AGX(G, S) is quasi-isometric to a tree.
Proof of Theorem A. The bulk of the work will be in proving that the first homology H1(Γ)
is uniformly generated, meaning there exists a constant L > 0 such that H1(Γ) is generated
by cycles represented by closed edge paths of length at most L. Let us assume this for the
moment, and see why the result follows. The map V (Γ) →V (QT ) is injective and Lipschitz,
and the graphs Γ and QT have bounded degree, so this map is regular in the sense of [HMT22,
Definition 1.2]. As explained in Subsection 1.3.3 of [HMT22], this implies that the asymptotic
dimension of Γ is bounded above by that of QT , which is 1 since QT is an unbounded
quasi-tree. By [FW07], using the Manning bottleneck trick [Man05], since H1(Γ) is uniformly
generated, Γ is therefore quasi-isometric to a tree.
It remains to prove that for any sufficiently long closed edge path C in Γ, there exist vertices
x, y ∈C and a path in Γ from x to y such that the new path is strictly shorter than either of
the reduced paths from x to y in C. Fix a constant N ∈N large enough that for all generators
s ∈S, the prefix replacements defining s all involve prefixes of length less than N. Since S is
finite, such an N exists. Note that the number of words in {0, 1}∗with length less than N is
2N −1, so the number 2N serves as a concise upper bound.
Now let C be a simple closed edge path in Γ with length (i.e., number of vertices) |C|.
Choose vertices p and q in C such that p has minimum fκ0 value and q has maximum fκ0
value, among vertices lying in C. Let f be the function obtained from fκ0 by translating
so that (p)f = 0, and set MC = (q)f ∈Z. In C we now have two paths from p to q in Γ;
designate one the “left” path and the other the “right” path, and note that the f values along
these paths never drop below 0 nor go above MC. Assume that |C| is greater than 2A22N+1,
where Ad is the constant from Lemma 1.5. In particular |C| > A22N+1, and so Lemma 1.5
says that C cannot be fully contained in a connected component of ([0, 22N + 1])f−1, which
since C is connected means MC > 22N + 1.

Lemma 2.2. Suppose E is locally moving. Let a < b in R. Then the group E′[a, b] has no
global fixed points in (a, b), and contains subgroups isomorphic to Zn for all n.
Proof. Let x ∈(a, b). Since E is locally moving we can choose f ∈E[a, b] with x.f > x, and
then also choose g ∈E[a, x.f] ≤E[a, b] with x.g < x. Now x.fg = x.f > x.gf, so x is moved
by fgf−1g−1 ∈E′[a, b].
For the second statement, choose a1, b1, a2, b2, . . . in (a, b) such that a1 < b1 < a2 < b2 < · · · .
Since each E′[ai, bi] acts non-trivially on (ai, bi) by the first statement, it is non-trivial, and
the E′[ai, bi] pairwise commute. Subgroups of Homeo+(R) are torsion free, so the result
follows.
□
Lemma 2.3. Suppose E ≤Homeo+(R) is locally moving. Let ℓ< m < r in R. Let a < b and
c < d with [a, b], [c, d] ⊆(ℓ, r) and m ∈(a, b). Then there exist f−∈E′[ℓ, m] and f+ ∈E′[m, r]
with [c, d] ⊆([a, b])f−f+.
Proof. Consider the E′[ℓ, m]-orbit O of a, and let i = inf(O), so ℓ≤i < m. We claim that
i = ℓ. Suppose i > ℓ, so by Lemma 2.2 we can choose f0 ∈E′[ℓ, m] with (i)f0 < i. Since f0 is
a homeomorphism there exists ε > 0 such that (i + ε)f0 < i. Since i = inf(O) there exists
f1 ∈E′[ℓ, m] with (a)f1 < i + ε. Now (a)f1f0 < i, contradicting that i = inf(O). We conclude
that i = ℓ, so in particular i < c, and so there exists f−∈E′[ℓ, m] with (a)f−< c. By a
parallel argument there exists f+ ∈E′[m, r] with d < (b)f+. Now f−f+ satisfies the desired

Remark 4.10. We have focused on piecewise-linear groups, and a natural question is what
can be said about piecewise-projective groups, as in [Mon13]. For example, regarding the
Lodha–Moore groups from [LM16], one could ask whether all copies of the F-like Lodha–Moore
group LMF inside the V -like one LMV are semiconjugate to the standard copy of LMF . One
could also define Stein-like versions of LMF , and ask whether they embed in LMV . We will
not pursue this here, but we should note that LMF does not embed in V [Zar16, Remark 2.9],
so the relevant questions should involve embedding piecewise-projective groups into other
piecewise-projective groups, not into piecewise-linear groups.
