Amplitude Noise Suppression in Frequency-Doubled Lasers:
A Lyapunov Mechanism for Intensity Stabilization in Coupled Oscillator Systems
Thomas M. Baer1
1Department of Applied Physics, Stanford University, Stanford, California 94305
(Dated: May 19, 2026)
Multimode intracavity frequency-doubled lasers can reach states of amplitude noise suppression
orders of magnitude beyond the predictions of independent-mode partition statistics.
We show
that the χ(2) coupled-wave dynamics in the doubling crystal admit a Lyapunov functional whose
monotone decrease under each crystal pass establishes a constant-intensity manifold as the per-pass
descent target of the mode dynamics. We confirm the mechanism in an intracavity frequency-doubled
Nd:YVO4/LBO laser, observing a 100× contrast between full and Fabry-Perot-filtered output noise
at fixed detector bandwidth, well beyond the
p
N/Nf statistical-averaging baseline. The mechanism
rests on the algebraic structure of the coupling — a coherent superposition of oscillators sharing a
quadratic dissipative channel — and is therefore a candidate for analogous noise-suppression effects
in other coupled oscillator systems with the same algebraic form.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Multimode intracavity frequency-doubled lasers admit
a Lyapunov functional whose monotone decrease under
the χ(2) coupled-wave dynamics in the doubling crystal
establishes a constant-intensity manifold as the per-pass
descent target of the mode dynamics. The full round-trip
dynamics, including gain saturation, spatial hole burn-
ing, noise injection, and the inter-pass mode coupling, ap-
proach this manifold across the parameter regime studied
here, as the simulations and experimental measurements
presented below demonstrate. The result is amplitude
noise suppression orders of magnitude beyond the predic-
tions of independent-mode partition statistics — the
√
N
scaling that holds when N independent modes sum inco-
herently with uncorrelated fluctuations. The mechanism
depends on the algebraic structure of the χ(2) coupling: a
coherent superposition of distinct-frequency modes shar-
ing a quadratic dissipative channel, with the dissipation
rate scaling as the square of the coherent total amplitude.
The same algebraic structure, when present in other cou-
pled oscillator systems, would imply analogous behavior;
we comment on this connection in Sec. VI.
Here we establish the mechanism analytically and
demonstrate it in a representative experimental realiza-
tion: the intracavity frequency-doubled multimode laser.
Hundreds of longitudinal modes share a common gain
reservoir and are coupled in both amplitude and phase
through a nonlinear crystal phase-matched for second-
harmonic generation. We show numerically and experi-
mentally that these couplings drive the laser into a state
in which the total output intensity is dramatically quieter
than independent-oscillator noise would predict.
The
full-vs-Fabry-Perot-filtered output contrast is of order
100× for a representative passband, far exceeding the
incoherent-averaging baseline.
We refer to this regime
as quiet multiaxial mode-doubling, following the laser-
engineering usage.
We identify the relevant Lyapunov
function, prove its monotone descent, and characterize
the manifold as a continuum of multimode configurations
of which the FM-laser Bessel-amplitude state is one mem-
ber.
Such lasers have been studied for nearly four decades
and display a range of dynamical behaviors depending
on mode count, pump rate, and crystal geometry: bista-
bility, deterministic chaos, and, in high-mode-count mul-
timode configurations, dramatic noise reduction of the
kind noted above. Prior theoretical treatments have ad-
dressed these regimes individually using rate equations
for mode intensities [1, 3, 4, 6, 7]. These treatments suc-
cessfully reproduced bistability and chaos but left the
noise-cancellation mechanism unexplained. Because they
track only intensities, they are incomplete. They cannot
distinguish the phase-dependent mechanisms involved.
Whether the low-noise state corresponds to the FM-laser
configuration, whether amplitude dynamics alone suffice
or coherent phase organization is required, and what role
back-conversion plays in that organization are questions
that can only be addressed with the full coupled-wave
dynamics.
Here we develop a numerical model that tracks both
the amplitudes and phases of all longitudinal modes,
based on an Ikeda-map strategy with a two-step Fourier
treatment of the coupled-wave χ(2) interaction in the
crystal.
We include spatial hole burning and the full
coupled-wave χ(2) dynamics, including back-conversion,
explicitly. The model agrees quantitatively with experi-
mental measurements across four attractor types selected
by cavity geometry: bistable mode-hopping, sequential
pulsing, quasi FM-quadrature locking, and quiet multi-
axial operation. It reproduces the regimes where prior
rate-equation treatments had been validated individu-
ally as well as the observed noise cancellation. A Lya-
punov functional for the coupled-wave dynamics estab-
lishes the mechanism. We present the numerical model,
the Lyapunov analysis, and new experimental measure-
ments that confirm the mechanism across mode-coupling
strengths from the bifurcation onset to the high-mode-
count regime typical of intracavity-doubled lasers in com-
mon laboratory use.

C.
Two channels: amplitude equalization and
phase organization
The χ(2) interaction in the mode basis decomposes into
two structurally distinct channels acting on every crystal
pass. When E(t)|E(t)|2 is expanded in modes, each mode
receives contributions from triplets of modes (j, k, l). A
diagonal triplet is one in which the same mode appears
as both raised and lowered index — concretely, terms of
the form Ej|Ek|2 that contribute to mode j. The remain-
ing contributions, in which three distinct modes combine
to drive a fourth, are the four-wave-mixing triplets. The
amplitude-equalization channel arises from the diagonal
triplets, producing intensity-quadratic depletion propor-
tional to each mode’s own amplitude. This channel co-
operates with spatial-hole-burning cross-saturation in the
gain medium and drives the mode-amplitude envelope to-
ward a smooth gain-shaped form. It does not by itself
organize phases.
The phase-organization channel arises from the four-
wave-mixing triplets, which are off-diagonal in the mode
basis and produce phase shifts on individual modes pro-
portional to the sine of four-mode phase combinations.
Each mode i receives a torque from every triplet (j, k, l)
satisfying the frequency-matching condition j +k −l = i,
with the torque vanishing when the phase combination
φi −φj −φk + φl is zero.
The mode-basis decom-
position therefore suggests a phase-organizing channel
through these off-diagonal four-wave-mixing terms; in
simulations, this channel drives the field toward phase
configurations with strongly suppressed coherent inten-
sity peaks, consistent with the M4 = 1 manifold of body
Sec. III. A formal analysis of the basin of attraction of
this phase-organizing channel is beyond the scope of the
present paper.
The structural form of this torque is opposite to that
of Kuramoto coupling [18], which uses an attractive sinu-
soidal force to align oscillators toward a mean-field phase.
The four-wave-mixing torque is repulsive. It drives mode
i away from configurations in which its phase aligns with
others, because alignment maximizes the intensity peaks
that the χ(2) depletion preferentially attacks. A natural
class of low-M4 configurations under this torque resem-
bles a splay-phase arrangement in which the modes are
phased so that constructive interference at any single in-
stant is suppressed [17]. The same algebraic structure
that produces synchrony under Kuramoto coupling pro-
duces this splay-like organization here, with the modes
phased to null the coherent sum rather than to align with
the mean field.
Both channels operate at first order in the coupling
strength ε and contribute to the descent. The leading-
order Cauchy-Schwarz proof of Sec. III.B uses only the
amplitude-equalization channel and is sufficient to estab-
lish the descent of M4 on its own. The all-orders Cheby-
shev proof of Appendix B treats the full coupled-wave
map, whose mode-basis projection contains both diago-
nal and off-diagonal contributions; it is a time-domain
proof of monotone M4 descent rather than a separate
proof of either channel’s mechanism. The simulation re-
sults presented in Sec. V are computed with the full χ(2)
dynamics, so both channels are active as they are in any
real intracavity-doubled laser.
D.
Round-trip dynamics and approach to the
manifold
Between crystal passes, the gain medium amplifies each
mode according to its gain and the spatial-hole-burning
coupling, which in general does not respect the Lyapunov
descent. The observed steady-state M4 is the round-trip
balance between the descent in the crystal and the drive
from the gain medium and noise sources. The magnitude
of the steady-state residual M4 −1 is set by this balance
and is determined numerically.
Two further structural features of the descent are es-
sential for what follows. First, the theorem establishes
that the per-pass change in M4 from the crystal is non-
positive in the operating regime.
Whether the system
actually reaches the manifold depends on whether the
inter-pass gain dynamics are dynamically subordinate to
the crystal’s descent. When subordinate, the system re-
laxes onto M4 = 1. When the gain medium pumps M4
back up enough to balance the crystal at non-stationary
M4, the round-trip composition produces a stable limit
cycle off the manifold. The four attractor types of Figs. 1
and 2 are organized by which side of this balance domi-
nates. Second, M4 is invariant under uniform scaling of
the intensity, so the descent acts selectively on partition
reorganizations rather than on total-power fluctuations.

IV.
EXPERIMENT
A.
The experimental laser platform
The experimental laser is a commercial intracavity-
doubled diode-pumped solid-state laser, the Spectra-
Physics Millennia Vs. The gain medium is Nd:YVO4 in
a standing-wave cavity. The doping density and crystal-
cut orientation are typical for this class of laser.
The
doubling crystal is intracavity LBO, phase-matched for
type I noncritical phase matching at the operating wave-
length. The pump is a multi-mode fiber-coupled diode
array. Total green output reaches 5 W at 532 nm. The
cavity supports approximately 200 longitudinal modes
across the gain bandwidth, spaced by the 358 MHz inter-
mode beat. Data was collected at the typical operating
point of approximately 4 W green output.
B.
Detection: full output and Fabry-Perot-filtered
output
Two detection paths are used. The first samples the
full green output at 532 nm with a fast photodiode and a
1 MHz analog detector bandwidth. The second passes the
green output through a free-standing Fabry-Perot etalon
used as a tunable spectral selector. The etalon mirrors
have 4 cm radius of curvature and are mounted on ad-
justable rails; the mirror spacing is set independently of
the laser cavity, allowing the etalon free spectral range
FSRFP to be varied while the finesse F ≈75 remains
fixed by the mirror reflectivities. The transmission peak
width is then ∆νFP = FSRFP/F, and the number of Mil-
lennia modes within each peak is Nf ≈∆νFP/358 MHz.
We use four configurations spanning Nf ≈3, 6, 15, 34
modes, selected by adjusting the mirror spacing. Both
detection paths feed identical detectors with identical
1 MHz analog bandwidths.
The contrast between full
and filtered outputs at the same detector bandwidth is
the primary observable.

The total IR power again swings several times its mean,
but with the irregular timing characteristic of chaos. The
corresponding green output in Fig. 2(b) runs at 35.0%
RMS.
Panel (c) is quasi FM-quadrature locking at N = 3 in

approximately 7 W with very small modulation. This is
a quasi-FM state on the manifold. The corresponding
green output in Fig. 2(c) runs at 2.24% RMS.
Panel (d) is the quiet multimode operation at N ≈
200 in the typical commercial intracavity-doubled cavity.
Multiple individual modes each fluctuate across order-
of-magnitude excursions on the timescale shown, while
the total IR power stays nearly flat at approximately
108 W. The corresponding green output in Fig. 2(d) runs
at 0.505% RMS. This is the regime that motivates the
paper.

D.
Robustness: invariance under round-trip phase
choice
The high-reflector phase δψ between the IR and the
back-traveling green field after the crystal exit and the
high-reflector reflection sets the relative phase at which
the back-pass χ(2) interaction resumes.
Commercial
intracavity-doubled lasers tune δψ to maximize green
output coupling, which corresponds to constructive in-
terference of forward-generated and back-reflected green
at the entrance of the back pass. The choice is a free
parameter of the crystal-mirror geometry.
To test whether the noise-cancellation mechanism de-
pends on δψ, we sweep δψ from 0 to 2π in steps of π/8
with all other simulation parameters held fixed. The IR-
side noise-cancellation metrics are flat across the sweep.
Steady-state ⟨M4⟩−1 = 3.4×10−5 to within seed-to-seed
scatter at every δψ. Total IR RMS is 0.074% across the
sweep. The active mode count is approximately 86 across
the sweep, defined as the number of modes whose time-
averaged intensity over the 5.6 ms record window exceeds
1% of the time-averaged peak. This subset accounts for
99.1% of the total mean IR power; the remaining modes
within the gain bandwidth carry residual leakage. The
roughly 200 modes referenced in Fig. 1 panel (d) and
elsewhere are the full set of modes that the simulation
evolves above quantization noise, of which the 86 are the
time-averaged dynamically participating subset.
The IR-side cancellation does not depend on δψ. The
Lyapunov mechanism that organizes the IR mode dy-
namics into the constant-intensity state operates entirely
on the IR field and is set by the χ(2) interaction within
the crystal, not by what happens to the green field at
the high-reflector. The green output coupling efficiency
does depend on δψ in the conventional way, with max-
imum extraction at the constructive-interference choice.
The IR-side intensity stabilization is invariant under the
choice.

VI.
DISCUSSION
The closest prior example of a multimode laser with
amplitude-stable output is the FM laser [2], in which
an intracavity phase modulator drives the field into a


## Page 12


12
Bessel-amplitude configuration with constant total inten-
sity. The FM laser and the multimode regime studied
here both produce M4 = 1 states but via distinct mech-
anisms. The FM laser uses external coherent forcing: an
intracavity modulator imposes the Bessel amplitudes and
phases in a ring laser cavity where SHB is absent. The
mechanism analyzed here is the internal Lyapunov de-
scent of the preceding section, operating in cooperation
with gain saturation and SHB cross-saturation.
In standing-wave cavities the Bessel ansatz is generally
precluded by the interplay of SHB with the Bessel am-
plitudes [9], outside of specific symmetry-protected gain-
medium placements treated in Appendix D; this applies
to both Nd:YAG and the Nd:YVO4 laser analyzed here
at the operating geometry of this work. The reason is
that SHB cross-saturation produces stronger gain deple-
tion at modes with higher intracavity intensity, which
selects an amplitude envelope that tracks the gain pro-
file rather than the externally-imposed Bessel envelope
of the FM laser.
The χ(2) interaction provides two coupling channels
with opposite relations to SHB. Amplitude equalization
through intensity-quadratic SHG loss cooperates with
SHB cross-saturation, both flattening the effective gain
seen by each mode and driving the amplitude envelope to-
ward the gain-shaped form. Phase organization through
coherent back-conversion can fight SHB. The phase re-
lations that minimize M4 are not in general those that
maximize the SHB-driven gain extraction at the oper-
ating point, where the laser is run to deplete the gain
efficiently. The residual M4 −1 floor is set by this bal-
ance. Which point on the manifold is selected depends
on where the gain-SHB constraint surface intersects it.
At the few-mode case of Fig. 2(c), symmetry suppresses
the tension and a quasi-FM Bessel-like state becomes ac-
cessible. At the high-mode-count case of Fig. 2(d) with
strong SHB, the system selects a configuration with a
gain-shaped amplitude envelope.
The geometric organization revealed by the principal-
component analysis of Sec. V E is robust to initial condi-
tions in a specific sense. Different cold-start phase con-
figurations lead to similar active mode sets, largely de-
termined by the gain-SHB cross-saturation, but to dif-
ferent specific eigenvector configurations of the mode-
fluctuation covariance.
Each cold-start configuration
finds its own arrangement of which weighted combina-
tions of mode amplitudes lie in the noisy versus quiet
subspaces.
All such configurations satisfy the geomet-
ric constraint that u lies in the quiet subspace, and
all produce comparable noise-cancellation performance.
The mechanism is basin-flexible: many distinct geomet-
ric configurations satisfy the descent constraint with u
properly aligned, and the specific configuration selected
is a property of the system’s history rather than a fine-
tuned hardware feature. This basin flexibility is one rea-
son the mechanism is robust to the perturbations that
real lasers experience without active stabilization.
An ε-sweep simulation confirms that the crystal serves
a dual role. It suppresses the SHB-driven mode compe-
tition that would otherwise limit free-running Nd:YVO4
lasers to ≈26 active modes, allowing the much larger ac-
tive mode populations seen with the nonlinear crystal in
place. At very high conversion strength the active mode
count is reduced again by the increased nonlinear loss,
but in every case the count exceeds the empty-cavity
result. The crystal simultaneously organizes the active
mode phases toward the M4 = 1 state. Both effects acti-
vate well below typical commercial operating parameters,
confirming that laboratory intracavity-doubled lasers op-
erate above the descent activation threshold.
The Lyapunov mechanism operates as a bandwidth-
limited active noise-cancellation channel.
The descent
timescale, defined operationally as the e-folding time over
which M4−1 decreases under the round-trip composition
starting from a random-phase cold-start initial condition,
is approximately 280 ns at typical operating parameters
based on cold-start simulations. Noise components slow
compared to this timescale are organized away from the
experimental observable with high efficiency on a per-
pass basis. Faster components have less time to be orga-
nized within each crystal pass and contribute proportion-
ally more to the residual variance of Ptot. This frequency
dependence is visible in Fig. 5: the slow drift of the noisy
projection in panel (a) is what the descent has routed
out of u, while the faster residual fluctuations visible in
panel (b) represent what survives.
The descent depends on the algebraic structure of the
coupling: a coherent superposition of oscillators at dis-
tinct frequencies, a dissipative channel acting on the to-
tal amplitude, and quadratic scaling of that dissipation
in the coherent sum.
Globally coupled oscillator ar-
rays studied previously include Josephson junctions [15]
and coupled laser arrays [16, 17]. These systems share
the multi-oscillator architecture and the existence of a
constant-total-amplitude manifold of equilibria, but re-
alize the loss channel in different forms.
Whether the
specific form required here, namely quadratic dissipa-
tion acting on the coherent superposition across distinct-
frequency modes, can be engineered into any of these
systems is an open question for further investigation in
those fields. The descent established here is therefore not
specific to intracavity-doubled lasers and may extend to
other coupled oscillator systems in which a quadratic dis-
sipative channel of this form can be realized.

intracavity-doubled lasers is discussed in Appendix C.
The relation to the FM laser is discussed in Appendix D.
VII.
CONCLUSION
We have shown that multimode intracavity frequency-
doubled lasers reach a state in which the total output
intensity is dramatically quieter than the mode-partition
noise of the constituent longitudinal modes would pre-
dict.
The mechanism is a monotone decrease of the


## Page 13


13
fourth-moment ratio M4 under the coupled-wave χ(2) dy-
namics in the crystal, which establishes the constant-
intensity state as the per-pass descent target of the
crystal dynamics. The full round-trip composition, in-
cluding gain saturation, SHB, and noise injection, ap-
proaches this manifold across the parameter regime stud-
ied here. A numerical model tracking both amplitudes
and phases of all longitudinal modes reproduces the ob-
served amplitude noise suppression quantitatively at typ-
ical operating parameters, and the same model struc-
ture reproduces historical observations in the bistabil-
ity and few-mode regimes with parameters appropriate
to each system. Experimental measurements confirm a
100× contrast between full and Fabry-Perot-filtered out-
puts of an intracavity frequency-doubled laser in com-
mon laboratory use, well beyond the
p
N/Nf statistical-
averaging baseline.
The χ(2) mode coupling that pro-
duced the green-problem instabilities in few-mode oper-
ation through rate equations [1] also produces the noise
suppression observed at high mode count, when the com-
plex mode amplitudes including phase are retained. A
similar progression, from few-mode bistability and chaos
to stable collective operation on a corresponding mani-
fold, may reward investigation in other coupled oscillator
systems that share the algebraic structure of a quadratic
dissipative channel acting on a coherent superposition of
distinct-frequency modes.

channel of the χ(2) interaction is therefore essential to the
all-orders result, not as a perturbative correction but as
the structural feature that closes the proof.
Relation to Boltzmann’s H-theorem.
The structural
form of the result is the same as Boltzmann’s H-theorem
in kinetic theory: a scalar functional of the dynamical
variables decreases monotonically under the dynamics
until it reaches its extremum on a manifold of equilibrium
states. Here M4 plays the role of H, the crystal plays the
role of the collision operator, and the M4 = 1 manifold
of constant-intensity multimode configurations plays the
role of the Maxwell-Boltzmann equilibrium. The Cheby-
shev inequality plays the role that Jensen’s inequality
plays in the original H-theorem.
Round-trip behavior.
Equation (B18) is a per-pass,
in-crystal result: M4 is non-increasing across each crystal
traversal. Global attraction of the M4 = 1 manifold un-
der the full round-trip dynamics — including gain, SHB
cross-saturation, noise sources, and inter-pass mode cou-
pling — is not proved by the Chebyshev result alone. It
is supported by the simulation evidence presented in the
body, in which the round-trip composition empirically
reaches the manifold from arbitrary initial conditions
across the parameter regime studied.
Between crystal
passes the gain medium and noise sources can drive M4
back up. The observed steady-state M4 is the round-trip
balance between the in-crystal descent and the inter-pass
drive (body Sec. III D).
Agnosticism to input phase configuration.
The proof
depends on the structure of the χ(2) amplitude deple-
tion and on monotonicity of I in I0 within the validity
range, but it makes no assumption about how the input
waveform I0(t) was produced. Whether the input mode
amplitudes and phases were generated by χ(2) phase cou-
pling on previous round trips, by other phase-affecting
mechanisms in the cavity (gain-medium dynamics, dis-
persion, thermal effects), or by random fluctuations, the
per-pass crystal traversal reduces M4 in the operating
regime. The steady-state M4 ≈1 observed experimen-
tally is therefore the round-trip balance between the in-
crystal descent and all other dynamics combined, includ-
ing any phase-affecting mechanisms beyond those explic-
itly modeled in this work. This robustness is a structural
feature of the proof, not a numerical observation.
Appendix C: Relation to prior treatments of
intracavity-doubled lasers
The intracavity-doubled multimode laser was first ana-
lyzed by one of us through rate equations [1], identifying
the χ(2) mode-coupling mechanism that produces bista-
bility, mode-hopping, and chaotic dynamics at N = 2
and N = 3.
The framework was developed by others
over four decades [3–8], remaining within rate-equation
variables tracking mode intensities and population inver-
sions. The original analysis was developed for and val-
idated against few-mode operation. The present treat-
ment includes complex mode amplitudes including phase,
identifies the Lyapunov descent of body Sec. III, and
addresses the high-mode-count, low-noise regime where

The FM laser and the Lyapunov descent reach this
manifold through different mechanisms. The FM laser
uses external coherent forcing in a ring laser cavity. The
system response is a driven equilibrium with no require-
ment for any internal stability mechanism, and the cavity
is typically a ring to eliminate spatial hole burning. The
χ(2) Lyapunov descent of the body drives the system onto
the manifold from arbitrary initial conditions with no ex-
ternal driving. The state reached is selected jointly by
the descent and the gain-SHB constraint surface. Under
standing-wave conditions with strong SHB, this selection
lies far from the Bessel point.
Gain-bandwidth truncation acts differently in the two
systems. In an idealized FM laser the Bessel distribu-
tion extends infinitely in mode index. In any real laser
the gain bandwidth limits the number of modes carry-
ing nonzero amplitude, producing a residual amplitude
modulation at integer multiples of Ωbecause the Bessel-
amplitude condition can no longer be satisfied exactly.
In the Lyapunov descent, the same bandwidth limit re-
stricts which φ(t) functions on the manifold are realiz-
able. The descent and the gain-SHB constraint surface
jointly select a φ(t) within the bandwidth-allowed subset,
and this selection is not restricted to the Bessel or FM
condition.
The mode-amplitude distributions observed
both in the simulation and in spectrally-resolved output
of the system studied here generally do not resemble an
FM-Bessel envelope and are not stationary. The system
explores points on the manifold rather than locking to
a single configuration. Numerically the resulting ampli-
tude residual is at the 10−5 level for typical operating
parameters at N ≈200.
