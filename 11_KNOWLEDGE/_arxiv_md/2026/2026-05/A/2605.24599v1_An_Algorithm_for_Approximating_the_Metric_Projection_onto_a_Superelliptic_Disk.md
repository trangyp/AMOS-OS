1.
Introduction
The topic of metric projections is wide and it has been extensively studied in various contexts.
In what follows, we briefly recall several results that are relevant to our approach.
Metric projections onto convex sets and convex bodies have been investigated from different
perspectives, including the existence, uniqueness, continuity, and approximation properties of
the metric projection operator. In particular, for convex bodies, various properties of the metric
projection operator have been studied (see for example [2] and the references therein).
In this article, we propose an algorithm for approximating the metric projection onto a su-
perelliptic disk of order p > 1, denoted by Dp, i.e., the convex hull of a superellipse of order p,
denoted by Cp, also known as Lam´e curve. For p ≥1, these sets are convex bodies. The proposed
method is based on approximating the superellipse, by a sequence of polygons that converge to it
in the Hausdorff–Pompeiu metric. The metric projection is then computed onto these polygonal
approximations, yielding approximations of the metric projection onto the superellipse.
Explicit formulas for metric projections onto polyhedral sets can be found for example in
Section 6.41 of [3], and in [7, 9].
Section 2 introduces the Convex Analysis framework of our algorithm, i.e. generalities about
the metric projection onto a subset C of a Hilbert space X, PC; why in our paper it behaves as
a single valued mapping when given additional constraints on C (Remark 2.5); and finally, by
using the Hausdorff–Pompeiu metric, we prove in Theorem 2.10 that, in our specific case, the
operator C 7→PC(x) is continuous, for every x ∈X.
In Section 3, we introduce our polygonal approximations of the superellipse: we take the
radial homeomorphism φp from the unit circle to the superellipse Cp (see Proposition 3.3),
through which we construct polygons Pp,k whose vertices lie on Cp (Paragraph 3.4). We continue
by describing the inverse images under the metric projection onto Qp,k (the convex hull of Pp,k)
corresponding to the relative interiors of the facets and to the vertices of Pp,k (Remark 3.7),
which we prove that they form a partition of R2 \ Qp,k (Proposition 3.8) and we conclude by
proving that the sequence of polygons (Pp,k)k≥3 converges, in the Hausdorff–Pompeiu metric,
to the superellipse Cp (Theorem 3.10).

Next, we recall some well known results of Convex Analysis (see [3]).
Proposition 2.2. Let C be a nonempty closed subset of X. Then every x ∈X has at least one
best approximation in C.
Proposition 2.3. Let C be a convex subset of X. Then every x ∈X has at most one best
approximation in C.
As an immediate consequence of Propositions 2.2 and 2.3 we have the following result.
Proposition 2.4. Every nonempty closed convex subset of X is a Chebyshev set.
Remark 2.5. Let C ⊆X be a nonempty closed convex set. According to Proposition 2.4, for
all x ∈X, PC(x) is a singleton. Therefore, in this case, PC is a single valued mapping.
Proposition 2.6. Let C be a convex subset of X, x∗∈X, and x0 ∈C. Then PC(x∗) = x0 if
and only if ⟨x −x0, x∗−x0⟩≤0, for all x ∈C.

Algorithm for the Metric Projection onto a Superellipse
4.1. This section uses the same notations as Section 3 and it focuses on creating and studying
the following algorithm.
Algorithm 4.2. Consider a fixed point x∗∈R2. If x∗∈Dp, then its metric projection onto
the superelliptic disk Dp is x∗. Otherwise, if x∗∈R2 \ Dp, choose an initial integer k ≥3 and
construct the polygon Pp,k.
Step 1 (Initial metric projection on Pp,k). Compute x1 = PPp,k(x∗). According to Propo-
sition 3.8 there are two possibilities:
(a) x1 ∈rint(Ft) for a unique edge Ft = [Pt, Pt+1].
(b) x1 = Pt for some vertex Pt.
Step 2 (Refinement step). After x1 is obtained, we consider the 2k-th roots of unity and
the polygon Pp,2k, with vertices denoted with Ql, where l ∈{0, 1, . . . , 2k−1}. Note that Pl = Q2l
for all l ∈{0, 1, . . . , k −1}. Again, by Proposition 3.8 there are two possibilities:
(a) If x1 ∈rint(Ft), we only need to compute the metric projection of x∗onto the edges
[Q2t, Q2t+1] and [Q2t+1, Q2t+2], and decide which one is at a minimum distance. Note
that indices are understood modulo 2k. The metric projection of x∗onto the new polygon
Pp,2k, coincides with the metric projection onto one of those edges.
(b) If x1 = Pt, then we compute the metric projection of x∗onto the adjacent edges of the
vertex Q2t of the polygon Pp,2k, namely [Q2t−1, Q2t] and [Q2t, Q2t+1]. Again, we decide
which one is at the minimum distance, and thus we obtain the metric projection of x∗
onto the new polygon.
We denote by x2 the metric projection of x∗onto the polygon Pp,2k.
Repeating the refinement step, each time doubling the number of vertices and testing only
the distances between x∗and two edges, we obtain a sequence (xn)n≥1 which converges to the
metric projection of x∗onto the superellipse Cp (see Theorem 4.3).
We start by proving that Algorithm 4.2 is convergent.
Theorem 4.3. Let x∗∈R2 be a fixed point. If x∗∈Dp, then its metric projection onto the
superelliptic disk Dp is x∗. Otherwise, if x∗∈R2 \Dp, then for any initial integer value of k ≥3,
the sequence (xn)n≥1, generated by Algorithm 4.2 converges to the metric projection of x∗onto
the superellipse Cp.
Proof. By Theorem 3.10 the sequence of polyhedrons (Qp,k)k≥3 converges in the Hausdorff–
Pompeiu metric to the superelliptic disk Dp. By Theorem 2.10 it follows that for every x∗∈R2
we have PQp,k(x∗) −→PDp(x∗) as k →∞.
Thus, for k sufficiently large, the projection of x∗∈R2 \ Qp,k onto the polyhedron Qp,k
provides an approximation of the metric projection of x∗onto the superelliptic disk Dp.
□
