Abstract
Building Information Modeling (BIM) is widely used in the Architecture, Engineer-
ing, and Construction (AEC) industry, but the complexity of Industry Foundation
Classes (IFC) limits accessibility for non-expert users. To address this, we intro-
duce IfcLLM, a hybrid framework for natural language interaction with IFC-based
BIM models. It transforms IFC models into complementary representations: a re-
lational representation for structured element properties and geometry, and a graph
representation for topological relationships.
These representations are integrated
through iterative retry-and-refine LLM reasoning. We implement the framework us-
ing an open-weight LLM (GPT OSS 120B), supporting reproducible and deployment-
oriented workflows. Evaluation on three IFC models with queries derived from 30
scenarios shows first-attempt accuracy of 93.3%–100%, with all failures recovered us-
ing a fallback LLM. The results show that combining complementary representations
with iterative reasoning enables more accessible natural language querying of IFC
data while supporting routine BIM analysis tasks.

1. Introduction
The Architecture, Engineering, and Construction (AEC) industry increasingly
relies on Building Information Modeling (BIM) [1] to manage complex data associ-
ated with built environments [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. BIM stores comprehensive information
about a building throughout its entire life cycle, from design and construction to
maintenance and demolition. It stores information about how buildings look, how
they are constructed and maintained, what materials are used, the properties of those
materials, and so on. As a result, BIM is a critical tool for design, construction, and
facility management processes [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. A widely used format for exchanging
BIM data is Industry Foundation Classes (IFC) [12], a standardized, software-neutral
schema that captures both semantic and geometric aspects of building elements. IFC
files encode properties (e.g., material, size, fire rating) and geometric information for
elements such as walls, doors, rooms, slabs, and stairs. Despite its richness, work-
ing with IFC data remains challenging. Effective use often requires familiarity with
complex schemas, domain-specific tools, and programming workflows [13, 14]. This
creates a barrier for many potential users, particularly those who are not deeply
technical but still need access to building information.
To address this, earlier research explored structured query interfaces, ontology-
driven approaches, and natural language interfaces tailored to BIM [15, 16, 17, 18].
While these methods improved accessibility to some extent, they were often limited in
handling complex spatial reasoning or still required users to understand aspects of the
underlying IFC schema. More recently, advances in large language models (LLMs)
[19, 20, 21] have made it possible to interact with structured data using natural

language.
However, directly applying LLMs to IFC data is not straightforward.
IFC files are typically large and structurally heterogeneous [22], which makes them
unsuitable for direct ingestion due to token limitations and context constraints. For
instance, the raw text of a simple model like FZK-Haus (shown in Figure 4) consumes
approximately 1.7 million tokens (using the GPT 4o tokenizer), which easily exceeds
the token context window of current state-of-the-art LLMs.
Recent work has begun to address this limitation by restructuring IFC data into
queryable structured representations and translating natural language queries into
executable database operations [23, 24, 25, 26]. In these approaches, the LLM acts
as an interface layer that generates queries over structured data sources, retrieves
relevant results, and produces a final response in natural language. This aligns with
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) paradigms [27], where the model does not
directly process raw IFC data but instead operates over curated representations.
Within this emerging direction, graph-based representations of IFC data have
been widely adopted [23, 24, 25, 26], as they provide an intuitive way to capture
relationships between building elements. These approaches have shown strong po-
tential for supporting structured querying and reasoning.
At the same time, some practical considerations remain open. First, it remains
an open question whether a single data representation can adequately support the
diverse range of query types encountered in BIM workflows, or whether combining
multiple representations may offer additional benefits. Second, in real-world IFC
exports, explicit relationships (e.g., IfcRelSpaceBoundary) may not always be present
or consistently defined across datasets [28].
In such cases, approaches that rely
primarily on such relationships can encounter challenges, particularly for adjacency,
inclusion, connectivity and navigation queries. This highlights the need to reduce
dependence on predefined IFC relationships by incorporating alternative sources or

In this work, we introduce IfcLLM, a hybrid framework for natural language

• We evaluate a geometry-derived graph representation for supporting connec-

• We implement the framework as an end-to-end reproducible and deployment-

2. Related Work
Ongoing efforts to make BIM data more accessible span several directions, in-
cluding IFC parsing, database representations, natural language interfaces (NLIs),
4


## Page 5


knowledge graphs, and the application of LLMs. This section reviews these efforts in
two parts. First, we examine how IFC data is transformed into structured, queryable
representations, including relational and graph-based approaches. Second, we discuss
how users interact with these representations through natural language interfaces and
recent LLM-based systems.
2.1. From IFC Parsing to Queryable Data Representations
Before IFC data can be used for querying, reasoning, or conversion into rela-
tional or graph representations, it must first be parsed from its native representa-
tion. This is a non-trivial task due to the complexity of the IFC schema, which has
a deeply nested relational structure and includes both semantic and geometric data.
Several tools have been developed to address this challenge. IfcOpenShell [29] is
one of the most widely adopted open-source libraries for parsing and manipulating
IFC data, which supports both semantic entity extraction and geometry process-
ing. Other widely used libraries include IFC++ [30], a performance-oriented C++
toolkit, and the xbim toolkit [31], which is tailored for .NET environments. More
recently, lightweight and high-performance tools have emerged, such as Ara 3D IFC
Toolkit [32] and Web-IFC [33].
Despite these availability, most IFC parsers remain limited in two key areas:
direct transformation to structured representations such as relational and graph
databases, and integration with reasoning engines or LLM frameworks. Although
tools such as Ifc2Sql [34] exist, they generate large, schema-driven relational databases
with many sparsely populated tables, resulting in representations that are difficult
for LLMs to traverse or reason over. This limitation highlights the need for more
systematic approaches to transforming complex IFC data into representations that
preserve both semantic and geometric information while remaining accessible for
5


## Page 6


querying.
To support scalable querying, a substantial body of work has explored map-
ping IFC data into database-backed representations. Early approaches focused on
translating the IFC EXPRESS schema into relational database schemas [35, 36],
enabling storage and querying through SQL. Subsequent work has proposed more
application-specific relational transformations. For example, Isikdag et al. [13] intro-
duced a BIM-derived relational schema tailored for indoor navigation, while Solihin
et al. [37] proposed a star-schema representation to support spatial queries. Other
domain-specific systems include BIM-based cost estimation [38] and facility manage-
ment frameworks integrating BIM with Big Data Analytics and NoSQL databases
[39]. Object-oriented database approaches [40], such as BIMserver [41], have also
been explored.
Beyond building-centric representations, studies have investigated integrating
IFC with other domain-specific schemas to support broader analysis [42, 43, 44]. In
the geospatial domain, IFC is often mapped to standards such as CityGML [45, 46]
via Application Domain Extensions (ADEs), typically implemented on top of spatial
database systems such as PostGIS or Oracle Spatial. Similarly, IFC spaces have
been mapped into cadastral and land administration schemas [3]. Collectively, these
transformations demonstrate that database-backed representations provide a struc-
tured backbone for scalable querying, offering a more practical alternative to raw
IFC files.
In addition to relational and object-oriented approaches, graph-based representa-
tions have gained increasing attention due to their ability to explicitly model relation-
ships between building elements. Knowledge Graphs (KGs) represent entities (e.g.,
IfcWall, IfcDoor) and their relationships (e.g., IfcRelContainedInSpatialStructure),
enabling semantic querying and relationship-based reasoning. IFC-GRAPH [18] is a
6


## Page 7


notable pipeline [47, 48] that transforms IFC data into property graphs, improving
interpretability and queryability. However, graph construction in such approaches
can become inefficient as the number of nodes and relationships increase. More im-
portantly, their ability to capture spatial relationships, such as adjacency between
rooms, relies heavily on predefined IFC relationships such as IfcRelSpaceBoundary.
When these relationships are incomplete or missing in the source IFC [2], adjacency
and containment cannot be reliably derived, limiting downstream reasoning tasks.
To address this, recent work has explored simplifying graph representations. For
example, Avgoren [49] proposed reducing IFC relationships to a smaller set of core
types (e.g., hasStorey, hasSpace, containsElements, hasBoundary, adjacentSpace) to
improve usability and reduce reasoning complexity. While this improves tractability,
the approach still depends on the availability of explicit relationships in the source
data.
2.2. Natural Language Querying of BIM Data
To improve accessibility, researchers have explored Natural Language Interfaces
(NLIs), which translate human queries into formal query languages. Early work in
this area focused on mapping natural language queries to structured database queries.
For example, Lin et al. [15] introduced a system that translates queries into MongoDB
operations using the buildingSMART IFD Dictionary [50]. However, this approach
lacks support for basic attribute constraints, such as numeric comparisons, which
are essential for practical querying. Other approaches have explored speech-based
and conversational interfaces. BIMASR [16] supports voice-based commands but is
limited to simple element retrieval and property modification. Similarly, dialogue-
based systems [51, 52, 53] focus primarily on attribute extraction rather than more
complex reasoning or spatial queries.
7


## Page 8


More advanced approaches have incorporated semantic web technologies and pre-
trained LLMs. Ontology-driven NLIs [17] translate natural language queries into
SPARQL [54] through semantic parsing, enabling multi-constraint querying. How-
ever, these systems often struggle with ambiguity, complex grammatical structures,
and spatial reasoning tasks. More recently, Liu and Chen [55] proposed a system that
translates natural language queries into structured BIM queries using fine-tuned and
fused pre-trained LLMs, reinforcing the importance of structured representations for
querying BIM data.
Ongoing developments in LLMs have led to a new generation of BIM applications.
LLMs have been applied to tasks such as filling missing IFC information and sup-
porting Automated Code Compliance (ACC) [56, 57], as well as controlling robotic
construction workflows through structured prompts and tool use [58]. Similarly, Guo
et al. [59] developed a framework that maps natural language queries to BIM-specific
code functions, while Du et al. [60] proposed a multi-agent system for generating IFC
models from natural language instructions.
More recently, several studies [23, 24, 25, 49, 26] have proposed natural lan-
uage querying systems for BIM data using retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
techniques [27, 61]. These approaches typically incorporate relevant contextual in-
formation, such as schema definitions, task instructions, and example queries, into
the prompt to guide LLM reasoning over BIM data. For example, Iranmanesh et al.
[23] transform IFC models into graph representations and guide the LLM to query
this graph by providing the schema, formatting constraints, and a small number of
examples. Similarly, BIMConverse [24] follows the similar graph-based RAG strat-
egy, where the LLM is supplied with structured instructions, including schema de-
tails, example queries, and task-specific guidance, alongside the user query. These
works indicate that the effectiveness of RAG-based BIM querying systems depends

strongly on how well the prompt is structured and how clearly the relevant context
is presented to the model.
2.3. Research Gap
The reviewed literature shows significant progress in making BIM data more
accessible through structured representations and, more recently, LLM-based nat-
ural language querying systems. In particular, recent approaches have shown that
coupling LLMs with database backends enables intuitive interaction with IFC data
without requiring users to understand complex schemas. Despite these advances,
several limitations remain.
First, there is a lack of systematic approaches for transforming IFC data into
lightweight, LLM-friendly representations. Existing tools primarily focus on pars-
ing or schema-driven conversions, often producing complex and sparsely structured
outputs that are difficult for LLMs to effectively traverse and reason over. This high-
lights the need for automated data pipelines that generate simplified relational and
graph representations while preserving both semantic and geometric information.
Second, current conversational querying systems for IFC data predominantly rely
on a single data representation, most commonly graph-based models constructed
from predefined IFC relationships [23, 24, 25, 49, 26]. While intuitive, this design
assumes that a single representation can support the full range of query types. In
practice, BIM queries span diverse requirements, including semantic filtering, hierar-
chical retrieval and topological reasoning, which may not be equally well supported
by a single data structure [23]. The potential benefits of combining complementary
representations within a unified querying framework remain largely unexplored.
Third, existing graph-based approaches depend heavily on explicit IFC relation-
ships to encode topology. However, as reported in prior studies [28], these relation-
9

ships can be incomplete or inconsistently defined in real-world IFC models. This
limits the reliability of such systems, particularly for connectivity and navigation
queries that require correct topological information as possible.
Taken together, these gaps motivate the need for an approach that (i) transforms
IFC data into LLM-friendly complementary representations within a single frame-
work, and (ii) reduces dependence on predefined IFC relationships by incorporating
alternative sources of topological information.
3. Proposed Framework
This section presents IfcLLM (Industry Foundation Classes Large Language
Model), a hybrid framework for natural language interaction with IFC-based BIM
models. The framework enables an LLM to query and reason over building data
without requiring direct interaction with the full native IFC schema. Rather than
treating an IFC file as a single source, IfcLLM restructures building information into
complementary representations that are efficient for LLMs to traverse.
The framework is motivated by three practical observations. First, direct query-
ing over raw IFC structures is difficult for LLMs because the schema is large, ver-
bose, and not naturally aligned with conversational reasoning. Second, many user
questions about buildings are not purely semantic, but also involve spatial and topo-
logical reasoning, such as connectivity, adjacency, inclusion and navigation. Third,
real-world IFC models may contain incomplete or unreliable explicit relationship en-
codings, which limits approaches that depend only on schema-defined relations. To
address these issues, IfcLLM combines a semantic representation, a graph represen-
tation, and an LLM-based agent within a unified architecture.

3.1. Architecture
IfcLLM consists of three conceptual layers: (i) a semantic layer, (ii) a graph layer,
and (iii) a conversational layer, as illustrated in Figure 1. These layers provide com-
plementary views of the same building model and work together to support natural
language querying. The semantic layer provides a structured representation of the
IFC model at the element level. It captures building elements, their attributes, hier-
archical information, and geometry. The graph layer complements this by represent-
ing topological relationships between elements, allowing the framework to support
questions about connectivity and navigation in the built environment. The conver-
sational layer integrates these structured representations with an LLM agent, which
interprets user questions, queries appropriate representations, and generates the final
response. Next, we discuss each layer in detail.
3.1.1. Semantic Layer
The semantic layer transforms a raw IFC model into a structured knowledge base
that is effecient for an LLM to query and interpret. Its main purpose is to decouple
natural language reasoning from the complexity of the native IFC schema. Instead
of requiring the LLM to understand deeply nested IFC entities and relationships
directly, the framework exposes a simplified and query-efficient representation of the
building model.
At this layer, the framework organizes building information around entities such
as storeys, spaces, walls, doors, windows, slabs, columns, beams, roofs, and other rele-
vant components. For each entity, the representation retains semantic attributes such
as names, descriptions, types, elevations, and properties, together with geometric in-
formation needed for downstream reasoning. The framework particularly preserves
the hierarchical organization of the model, i.e., building>storey>element{property}.

A key design choice in IfcLLM is to represent geometry at two complementary lev-
els. The first is a simplified geometric representation, consisting of primitives such as
centroids (points) and axis-aligned bounding boxes (rectangular polyhedra). These
representations support tasks such as distance estimation and graph construction.
The second level is a full geometric representation, which preserves richer geometric
detail for cases where simplified representations are not sufficient (e.g., visualization
tasks). This distinction allows the framework to remain computationally practical
while still retaining an option for more detailed geometric reasoning when needed.
The semantic layer therefore serves two roles. First, it supports direct retrieval of
semantic and quantitative information about building elements. Second, it provides
the geometric basis from which higher-level topological relationships can be derived.
In this sense, it is also the foundation for the graph layer. Another important aspect
of this layer is the derivation of absolute element placement. Because IFC elements
are often positioned through nested local placements, the framework resolves these

3.1.2. Graph Layer
While the semantic layer supports attribute-based retrieval, many building-related
questions depend on topological structure rather than only on stored attributes.
Examples include identifying connected rooms, determining whether a link exists
between two building elements, identifying rooms which contain specific building el-
ements or finding shortest routes. These tasks are more naturally expressed through
a graph representation. The graph layer addresses this need by encoding building
elements as nodes and their topological relationships as edges. In general, such rela-
tionships may include inclusion, containment, adjacency, and connectivity (as shown
in Figure 2).
Another key design decision in IfcLLM is that graph relationships are not re-
stricted to explicit IFC relationships. Instead, the framework allows the graph rep-
14


## Page 15


resentation to be constructed using the geometries of building elements. When IFC
models contain well-defined relationships, these can be used to infer connections and
build the graph. However, this work focuses on scenarios where such relationships are
not fully available or consistently defined. To address this, the framework supports
deriving topology independently of predefined IFC relationships, reducing reliance
on their availability and quality.
In this work, the focus is not on developing a novel geometry-based adjacency
detection method. Rather, we adopt a simple and practical strategy based on axis-
aligned bounding boxes to infer adjacency and connectivity. The framework evaluates
geometric interactions such as intersection, containment, and face-level proximity
within a tolerance to determine whether elements should be connected in the graph
(see Algorithm 1). This design choice reflects our aim to assess whether geometry-
derived topology, even when constructed using simple approximations, is sufficient
to support connectivity and navigation queries within the proposed framework. As
such, the emphasis is on integrating geometry-derived relationships into the querying
pipeline rather than on the complexity of the underlying geometric method.
Finally, the graph layer does not aim to represent the entire IFC model. Instead,
consistent with the overall design of IfcLLM, only relevant building elements are
included in the graph representation, with the selection adapted to the requirements
of the specific use case. This selective construction avoids unnecessarily large and
dense graphs, reduces preprocessing and query overhead, and keeps the graph focused
on topology-driven tasks such as connectivity and navigation. More broadly, the
graph layer acts as a complementary view to the semantic layer. While it is possible
to represent both topology and semantic properties within a single graph structure,
separating these representations allows each to be optimized for its primary purpose:
relational queries for attribute retrieval and aggregation, and graph traversal for

3.1.3. Conversational Layer
The conversational layer connects the LLM to the structured representations
provided by the semantic and graph layers. Its role is to coordinate an iterative
reasoning process in which the LLM interprets the question, retrieves relevant in-
formation, evaluates intermediate results, and constructs a final natural language
answer. The complete workflow is presented in Algorithm 2.
In IfcLLM, the LLM operates as an agent with access to multiple tools for struc-
tured data retrieval. Given a user query, it determines whether the answer can be
produced directly or requires database access. When retrieval is needed, it formulates
structured queries over the semantic and/or graph representations, receives the re-
sults, and uses them to guide subsequent steps. This process is iterative, as complex

4. Evaluation Setup
To evaluate the proposed IfcLLM framework, we implement an end-to-end sys-
tem that operationalizes the semantic, graph, and conversational layers described
in Section 3. This section outlines the key implementation choices, and evaluation
models, queries and criteria.
4.1. IFC Data Transformation
The building elements, their properties, and geometric information are extracted
from an IFC model and stored in a relational database. The database schema follows
the structure presented in Table 1. In our implementation, this resulted in 17 tables
corresponding to different IFC element types and supporting data, including: beam,
18


## Page 19


building, ceiling, column, door, property, railing, ramp, real_geometry, roof, room,
slab, stair, storey, transport, wall, and window. We use IfcOpenShell for parsing raw
IFC and SQLite1 as the relational database.
Simplified geometric representation such a centroid and bounding box are stored
as REAL values in the corresponding element tables, while full geometry is stored
separately in the real_geometry table as JSON arrays of vertices and faces2. To
establish hierarchical relationships, storey elevations are first extracted, after which
each element is assigned to its corresponding storey based on its computed place-
ment in the building’s coordinate system. This placement is determined by recur-
sively traversing the IFC object hierarchy and accumulating all relative placements.
For elements spanning multiple storeys, a single representative storey is assigned to
simplify querying. This design favors practical usability over full IFC expressiveness.

5.1. Ablation Study
We further evaluate the proposed framework through an ablation study that
isolates the impact of its core design choices. The study focuses on two aspects:
(i) the use of graph-based traversal for connectivity and navigation queries and (ii)
iterative LLM reasoning. Accordingly, we evaluate three system variants: (i) the
full system, combining relational and graph representations with iterative reasoning
(IfcLLM ); (ii) a relational-only variant with iterative reasoning (DB-Iterative); and
(iii) a relational-only variant using one-shot reasoning (DB-OneShot).
Existing BIM querying approaches that incorporate graph representations typ-
ically rely on predefined IFC relationships.
In contrast, our framework uses a
geometry-derived topology.
Rather than introducing a separate baseline system,
this design choice is evaluated through the ablation setup. The relational-only vari-
ants serve as reference configurations that reflect recent LLM-based BIM querying
approaches, which operate over single data representation and may employ either
one-shot [23, 24, 25, 26] or iterative reasoning [49]. The ablation study therefore
examines the impact of incorporating geometry-derived topology and iterative rea-
soning under consistent data and experimental conditions.
All variants are evaluated on the same IFC model (Building B1) and share an
identical preprocessing pipeline.
Adjacency between navigable building elements
(e.g., rooms, doors, stairs, and ramps) is derived from geometry, as per Algorithm 1.
This information is stored in two relational tables: spatial_nodes and spatial_edges.
The spatial_nodes table represents building elements with attributes such as a unique
identifier and element type, while spatial_edges encodes adjacency using from_node,
to_node, distance, and a binary is_vertical flag. These tables provide a consistent
representation of topology across all variants. When a graph database is used, the
same data are reused without modification to support graph traversal.

design in which only relevant building elements are selectively included in the graph
database, while all elements, properties, and geometries are stored in the relational

while the graph database contains only the building elements required for the use
case. In addition, graph databases are not always optimal for attribute-centric re-
trieval tasks, where relational representations remain efficient and straightforward.
The proposed hybrid design therefore reflects a practical trade-off: relational storage
supports efficient attribute-based querying, while the graph layer provides a special-
ized mechanism for topology-driven spatial reasoning.

5.3. Limitations and Future Directions
Although IfcLLM achieved strong performance on the evaluated query set, sev-
eral implementation-level challenges were identified. These findings highlight practi-
cal limitations and provide guidance for the design of more robust LLM-based IFC
querying systems.
Graph and Relational Backend Limitations. The current implementation’s
approach to generating the connectivity graph has inherent limitations. It can fail in
cases where building elements have curvilinear geometries or are irregularly shaped.
Such elements’ bounding boxes may overlap, leading to inaccurate adjacency compu-
tations. Such errors propagate through the pipeline, directly affecting LLM outputs;
a clear instance of the “garbage in, garbage out" problem. We purposefully used
Building B2 to illustrate this issue, where room A201 partially overlaps with the
bounding box of room A202. This overlap produces a false open-room connection
between the two spaces, as shown in their connectivity graph in Figure 5(c). Future
research should aim to develop graph generation methods grounded in full geometric
data [67], while integrating available predefined IFC relationships. Establishing a
more robust graph-building framework is critical for improving LLMs’ performance
on graph traversal tasks [68]. On the relational database side, the current imple-
mentation stores geometric primitives as individual scalar REAL columns in SQLite,
a deliberate design choice to maintain simplicity. Future work could adopt stan-
dardised spatial database extensions such as SpatiaLite or PostGIS, storing these
primitives as typed geometry columns following OGC standards. This would enable
native spatial SQL functions such as ST_Intersects and ST_Distance to replace the
38


## Page 39


current custom adjacency and distance computation logic. Since these standardised
type and function names are well-represented in LLM training data, such a schema
could further improve the reliability of LLM-generated spatial queries over IFC data.
Token Limit and Query Generation Errors. In the case of Building B2,
the primary LLM encountered a token-length issue due to an incorrectly generated
SQL query. When asked to retrieve wall properties, the model produced a query
that returned all rows from the walls table rather than listing only distinct property
names. This led to a substantial data volume being loaded into the LLM context,
thereby exceeding its token limit. The erroneous query generated by the model was:
SELECT w.name AS wall, p.property_name, p.property_value
FROM property p
JOIN wall w ON p.element_id = w.id
WHERE p.element_type = ‘IfcWall’;
This query returned every wall instance along with its associated properties and
values, resulting in an unnecessarily large context for reasoning. After rephrasing
the user prompt to: “What properties are available for walls?", the LLM produced
the following optimized query:
SELECT DISTINCT property_name
FROM property
WHERE element_type = ‘IfcWall’;
This corrected query retrieved only the unique property names for wall elements,
reducing the data volume and resolving the token-length issue. A similar problem was
observed with Building B3. When asked “Which doors are exit doors?", the model
retrieved all door data instead of filtering for exit-related doors. Rephrasing the
39


## Page 40


prompt to “Which doors can be used to exit the building?" produced the correct query
and response. Using the fallback LLM (Groq Compound) mitigated these issues.
This highlights the significance of having an LLM with strong reasoning capability
in the framework and, importantly, implementing an intermediate validation step
within automated LLM–SQL workflows [69]; specifically, a mechanism to verify query
results (or at least the context size) before performing full LLM inference. Such a step
can prevent unnecessary token consumption and improve computational efficiency.
A further observation concerns semantic interpretation errors within IFC datasets.
Although not observed in the case of Buildings B1 and B3, for Building B2, the LLM
initially calculated the total height of the building by adding the elevations of the
floors rather than calculating the difference between the maximum and minimum
elevation values. The LLM corrected this error in the second attempt. Such in-
consistencies highlight the importance of validating generated responses before pre-
senting reasoning results to users.
Future research could explore multi-model or
answer-validation frameworks to detect and correct such failures.
Context Management. Regarding the cost of LLM inference, the current im-
plementation includes all available properties for building elements (up to a maximum
of 100) in the system prompt. While this approach is manageable for smaller mod-
els, larger models often define hundreds of properties, which substantially increases
the token count. This challenge highlights the need for dynamic context manage-
ment when working with large language models. A more robust approach would
involve parsing the user’s query to identify and retrieve only the relevant properties
for inclusion in the system prompt. Restricting the prompt to contextually rele-
vant information [70] reduces the likelihood of exceeding context-length limits and
lowers the computational cost of inference. Future research should explore methods
for dynamically constructing system prompts [71], not only for managing property
40


## Page 41


data but also for representing database schemas. Prior studies demonstrate that
domain-adapted contextual embeddings provide more semantically meaningful rep-
resentations than general-purpose models, including in multilingual settings [72, 73].
Similar approaches could be applied to generate IFC schema–aware embeddings for
dynamic prompt generation.
Data Quality Issues in IFC Models. Another important consideration is that
complete geometric information for all building elements cannot always be guaran-
teed in IFC models [74]. Geometric issues can, in some cases, falsely represent build-
ing elements in the connectivity graph. As a result, the graph is incomplete or dis-
jointed. This limitation highlights the need for methods capable of handling partial
or incomplete geometric data when constructing connectivity graphs for LLM-based
reasoning. Furthermore, this research area currently lacks comprehensive benchmark
datasets that could serve as a reliable foundation for validating such framework im-
plementations in a fully automated manner.
Diverse IFC models, together with
carefully curated sets of queries and corresponding ground-truth answers covering
semantic, geometric, and spatial reasoning tasks, would benefit reproducible perfor-
mance assessment and the development of more robust LLM-based reasoning systems
for BIM data.
6. Conclusion
This paper introduced IfcLLM, a hybrid framework for natural language querying
of IFC data. The framework transforms IFC models into complementary represen-
tations: a relational for structured element properties and geometry, and a graph
for topological relationships. These representations abstract the complexity of the
native IFC schema and are integrated with LLM-based reasoning.

## Page 42


The evaluation across three IFC models shows that the framework can handle
different types of queries, including attribute retrieval, aggregation, and spatial rea-
soning, within the defined scope. The results highlight the importance of iterative
reasoning, especially when the initial query fails. The ablation study shows that re-
lational representations alone are enough for correctness, but graph-based traversal
provides a more direct and efficient way to express spatial queries, reducing query
complexity and token usage.
From an AEC perspective, results suggest that exposing such data representations
to an LLM can lower the technical barrier for querying and understanding BIM data
and reduce the need for manual query writing. Beyond simple information retrieval,
the framework shows potential to support routine BIM analysis tasks such as iden-
tifying disconnected spaces, detecting missing or incomplete data, extracting spatial
hierarchies, and computing navigation paths. At the same time, the study highlights
challenges related to geometry-based graph construction, context management, and
data quality in real-world IFC models. This shows that LLM-based querying de-
pends strongly on the quality of the underlying data. Overall, the results suggest
that combining complementary representations with iterative reasoning provides a
practical and extensible approach to conversational BIM querying.
There are several directions for future work.
The evaluation was based on a
curated set of queries drawn from 30 representative scenarios, designed to test func-
tionality rather than provide full coverage. Future work can expand this with more
diverse and challenging queries, compare against graph-only and other representa-
tion baselines, and study performance under incomplete or inconsistent IFC data.
Other directions include improving adjacency detection using full geometries, reduc-
ing database generation overhead, and extending the framework to support answer
validation, lower token use, and multi-building IFC scenarios.
