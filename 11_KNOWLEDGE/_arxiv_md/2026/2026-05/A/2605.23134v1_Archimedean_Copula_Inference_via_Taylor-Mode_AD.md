Abstract
No existing nested Archimedean copula tool handles all three of (a) arbitrary
per-variable (right-)censoring in survival analysis, (b) arbitrary nesting trees, and
(c) exact parameter gradients. Existing implementations handle only bivariate
problems, low dimensional (i.e., d ≤10) cases, two layers of nesting, or only hand-
derived copula nestings. We present ACOPULA, a JAX-native framework that, given
any Archimedean generator—classical or neural—evaluates exact nested-copula
likelihoods and parameter gradients under arbitrary censoring masks in polynomial
time. The mechanism is polynomial powering of Taylor-mode automatic differenti-
ation output, which replaces per-family hand-derived partial Bell polynomial tables
with a single differentiable computation that any user-defined generator can drive.
We conduct extensive simulations to verify the correctness of ACOPULA. We then
demonstrate (a) per-variable censoring on 85,229 MIMIC-IV ICU admissions in
high dimensions with d=53, fit by both classical Archimedean families and nested
neural Archimedean copulas; (b) an 11-sector hierarchical model on S&P 500
daily returns at d=98; (c) family-agnostic censored MLE across ten families, five
of them with no prior implementation, on a retinopathy study; and (d) a ∼650×
per-density speedup over R’s nacLL at d=35, scaling quadratically to d=8,000.

Introduction
Hierarchical multivariate data with partial observation is the default in modern survival, financial,
and clinical risk settings. Patients in a survival study with paired organs may never experience the
event during follow-up [Huster et al., 1989]. Stock returns cluster by industry sector and tail-co-move
during crashes. Intensive care unit (ICU) lab measurements group by clinical panel, and the analyst
right-censors each lab independently at hospital discharge [Johnson et al., 2023]. Each setting
demands a model that captures the hierarchy with interpretable parameters—tail dependence and
concordance—and accommodates an arbitrary censoring pattern per observation.
Archimedean copulas.
A copula C : [0, 1]d →[0, 1] is a multivariate distribution on the unit
cube with d uniform marginals; by Sklar’s theorem [Sklar, 1959], every joint distribution on u =
(u1, . . . , ud) ∈[0, 1]d factors uniquely into its one-dimensional marginals and a copula that captures
their dependence. This decomposition lets the analyst pair domain-specific marginal models—
e.g., Weibull [Weibull, 1951] for survival times, GARCH [Bollerslev, 1986] for returns—with an
interpretable dependence structure whose parameters directly encode tail behaviour and concordance.
Nested Archimedean copulas [McNeil, 2008, Hofert and Pham, 2013] extend copulas to hierarchical
dependence by composing generator functions in a tree: variables under the same internal node share
stronger dependence than variables across branches.
The inference barrier.
Fitting a nested copula requires its density—a mixed partial derivative of the
copula cumulative distribution function (CDF), one differentiation per observed variable—through a
tree of composed generators [Hofert and Pham, 2013]; three obstacles block existing tools.

• Per-variable censoring. We consider per-variable right-censoring under the survival setting. Each
subset of censored variables changes which mixed partial derivative of the copula CDF defines
the likelihood, so a d=53 ICU dataset with thousands of distinct censoring patterns requires the
framework to handle every per-observation mask δ ∈{0, 1}d uniformly, where δj = 1 marks
variable j as observed. Prior tools cover bivariate problems [Sun and Ding, 2020, Emura and Chen,
2018] or shared censoring [Liu et al., 2025b]; none reach d > 30 per-variable.
• Arbitrary nesting and any generator. R’s copula package [Hofert et al., 2012] hard-codes per-
family Stirling-number tables for five generators and supports only two nesting layers; adding a
sixth family—or any neural-based generator such as the Deep Archimedean Copula family [Ling
et al., 2020, Liu et al., 2025b]—demands fresh hand derivation.
• Exact automatic parameter gradients. Without automatic gradients, fitting a d=98 model relies on
poorly-scaling derivative-free or finite-difference methods, or Monte Carlo approximators [Hofert
et al., 2013, Ng et al., 2021] that are consistent but biased with finite samples.
Our approach.
We present ACOPULA, a JAX-native framework that resolves all three obstacles.
The user specifies at minimum a copula generator—classical or neural—and ACOPULA computes
the nested copula likelihood for any tree shape and any censoring mask, with exact parameter
gradients automatically. The mechanism is polynomial powering of Taylor coefficients: Taylor-
mode AD [Griewank and Walther, 2008, Radul et al., 2023] returns the higher-order derivatives of
each generator composition in one forward pass, and raising those coefficient vectors to successive
powers via convolution recovers the partial Bell polynomials that the density formula of Hofert and
Pham [2013] demands without per-family hand derivation, for any generator satisfying the standard
dc-monotonicity nesting condition [McNeil, 2008].
Contributions.
We make the following contributions:
1. A family-agnostic, gradient-based likelihood inference algorithm for nested Archimedean copulas:
given only a classical or neural generator (e.g., Deep Archimedean Copulas [Ling et al., 2020, Liu
et al., 2025b]), the framework derives densities and gradients automatically.
2. Per-variable censoring as a first-class operation: the likelihood algorithm handles arbitrary per-
observation masks δ ∈{0, 1}d directly through its core recurrence, lifting the bivariate-only or
single-shared-time restrictions of prior censoring-aware tools.
3. A complexity result: O(d3) worst-case for fixed-depth trees and O(d2) for

• Classical R-package ecosystem. The R copula [Hofert et al., 2012, 2013] and HAC [Okhrin and
Ristig, 2014] packages compute generator derivatives via hand-coded closed forms for five families,
restrict every edge to same-family pairs, and offer no analytic parameter gradients. The simulated
maximum likelihood estimator [Hofert et al., 2013] relies on Laplace-transform frailty sampling,
with Monte Carlo variance that grows with dimension; our exact-likelihood approach attains
∼100× speedup at matched accuracy (Appendix Q), and our deterministic-density comparison
against R’s nacLL reaches ∼650× at d=35 before R aborts (Section 5.2).
• Censoring-aware copula models. CopulaCenR [Sun and Ding, 2020] fits censored bivariate and
trivariate copulas, including interval-censoring via 2d-corner inclusion–exclusion of the survival
copula; Li et al. [2020, 2023] handle right-censoring using brute-force symbolic differentiation.
Both restrict to low ambient dimension. HACSurv [Liu et al., 2025b] fits hierarchical Archimedean
copulas for competing risks where each observation reduces to one event time and the likelihood is
a single first-order partial of the nested CDF. We handle the high-order mixed partials that arise
with per-variable right-censoring in the survival sense, and discuss interval-censoring in Section 6.
• Deep and probabilistic ML copulas. Recent neural-copula work [Foomani et al., 2023, Liu et al.,
2025a] learns flexible generators for bivariate dependent censoring and discrete-diffusion joint
sampling; we are complementary, targeting exact nested likelihoods at moderate-to-high d with
per-variable censoring. Vine copulas [Aas et al., 2009, Nagler and Vatter, 2023, Stöber et al., 2013]
and hierarchical Kendall copulas [Brechmann, 2014] are density-first: the censored likelihood
requires a per-observation multivariate integral over the censored block, exponential in its size.
METIC [Chen et al., 2025] handles this at small d via a stochastic-numerical scheme; no vine
implementation reaches per-variable censoring at d=53 (Appendices U and V).
• Higher-order AD and differentiable probabilistic systems. Our truncated-Taylor-series computation
uses JAX’s Taylor-mode AD primitive [Bettencourt et al., 2019], also formalized in the Weil-
algebraic framework of Betancourt [2018]: order-d derivatives in a single forward pass on a
truncated polynomial algebra (Section 3.2). Lin [2024] extends the JVP-rule protocol to functional
derivatives, complementary to the Taylor-mode AD we use here. NumPyro [Phan et al., 2019]
exposes first-order but not higher-order gradients required for nested copulas; Gen-AC [Ng et al.,
2021] learns Laplace transforms with Monte Carlo variance that grows with derivative order.
Roadmap.
Sections 2 to 4 formulate the problem, present the key algorithm, and describe the
JAX implementation. Section 5 validates correctness on simulation, characterises wall-clock scaling
against R’s nacLL with a ∼650× speedup at d=35 before R aborts, and runs four real-data exper-
iments: MIMIC-IV at d=53 with classical and neural Archimedean generators under per-variable
censoring; S&P 500 at d=98—a nested likelihood beyond R’s reach; diabetic retinopathy across ten
Archimedean families, five without any prior censored implementation; and end-to-end reproduction
of HACSurv’s Framingham competing-risks pipeline. Section 6 addresses validity under mixed
families and amortized JIT compile cost across observations.

Handling per-variable censoring
Censoring a leaf ℓsets α(ℓ) = [1] instead of [0, 1], which leaves
the Cauchy product unchanged on its level and reduces the effective dimension |δv| at every ancestor.

reduced JET order; per-node cost scales as O(|δv|3), so heavy censoring runs faster (Table 8).

6
Discussions
We address concerns deferred from the main sections.
Nesting validity. Equation (2) requires that each edge composition be a Bernstein function. Every
nested experiment in this paper uses a valid-by-construction parameterisation: the same-family
delta parameterisation of McNeil [2008] for Clayton/Clayton, Frank/Frank, and Gumbel/Gumbel;
Hofert [2010]’s closed-form shifted-softplus constraint for AMH/Clayton; and the Bernstein-
composition construction of Appendix G.1 for the Gen-AC and ACNet rows. In each case, the
nesting condition holds by construction without optimiser-side enforcement. The framework addi-
tionally returns a free sign-alternation check on the edge-composition Taylor coefficients per density
evaluation, and Appendix G outlines a soft-penalty method for cross-family or custom generators
that lack a closed-form valid-by-construction parameterisation.
Identifiability. Identifiability is a structural limitation every copula-based survival model inherits,
including HACSurv [Liu et al., 2025b] and DCSurvival [Foomani et al., 2023]. The practitioner
response is to encode identifying assumptions through the choice of copula family [Zheng and Klein,
1995, Foomani et al., 2023]; the family becomes an explicit modelling choice rather than an implicit
defect. Our family-agnostic inference makes that choice cheap: we enable the analyst to fit ten
families with identical code and report sensitivity directly (Section 5.6).
Censoring scope. This work does not consider interval-censored data. In principle, ACOPULA
supports this case by wrapping 2m calls to Algorithm 1, where m is the number of interval-censored
variables, for the inclusion–exclusion over rectangle corners of the CDF. The cost is exponential in
m, and we are not aware of any sub-exponential algorithm to compute this CDF exactly.
Stochastic density approximations. Two prior approaches replace exact copula density evaluation
with Monte Carlo: Simulated MLE (SMLE) samples frailties at the joint-likelihood level [Hofert
et al., 2013]; Gen-AC samples latents at the generator-derivative level [Ng et al., 2021]. Both yield
consistent but biased estimators, with bias O(1/N) from Jensen’s inequality on log composed with
the nonlinear density. Appendix Q empirically quantifies both: 1. SMLE at N=100 frailty samples
per evaluation biases the inner Clayton parameter estimate by 0.36; 2. Gen-AC-Fourier at N=32
frailty samples per evaluation biases the NLL by 0.09–0.12 nats per observation. Our work enables
exact density at high dimensions without finite-sample approximation bias.
Compile cost and memory. JAX compilation takes 5–30 s for an S&P-style d=98 nested likelihood;
MIMIC-IV hessian val+grad at d=53 takes 40–59 s across families, broken down in Table 27.
ACOPULA caches compiled programs to disk, so reruns avoid compilation overhead; peak memory
stays at 1.4 GB at d=98 and 700 MB at d=53.
7
Conclusion
ACOPULA unifies nested Archimedean copula inference under one differentiable pipeline. Where prior
tools offered only restricted slices—a few hand-coded families, two-level nesting, low-dimensional
mixed-partial derivatives—ACOPULA lifts all three to arbitrary families, arbitrary nesting depth, and
high-dimensional mixed-partial derivatives, in one open-source package, lowering the barrier to
nested Archimedean copula inference for survival, finance, and reliability practitioners. We hope
ACOPULA catalyzes expressive, interpretable censoring-aware dependence models with principled
likelihoods across survival, finance, and reliability practice.

Empirical scaling.
The O(d3) worst case assumes the per-sector leaf count K grows with d. In
practice, the application domain fixes the tree branching factor—K = 5 in our scaling benchmarks—
so the number of sectors M = d/K grows instead. The per-sector polynomial powering then costs
O(K3), constant in d, and the total across all sectors is M ·O(K3) = O(d·K2) = O(d). The Cauchy
product convolves M short kernels of length K with a d-length accumulator at O(d·K ·M) = O(d2).
Both terms carry small constants relative to the per-scan-iteration overhead in lax.scan, so the wall-
time transition where the quadratic term begins to dominate sits at d∗≈103 at fixed K. Appendix O
reports the empirical slopes that follow this prediction: sub-linear in the small-d regime, transient
super-quadratic during the L1/L2 cache transition, and the clean O(d2) asymptote at d ≥4,000 for

Mask-aware static-shape JIT.
ACOPULA pads each node’s α-vector to the mask-independent

Numerical Stability of Taylor-Mode Derivatives
A naive float64 implementation of the Bell pipeline underflows long before reaching the dimensions
our experiments target, because high-order generator derivatives and the geometric powers pj
1 that the
Bell transform multiplies in span hundreds of orders of magnitude. This appendix specifies the log-
domain machinery that keeps every intermediate quantity representable and reports the stress tests that
confirm its accuracy. Algorithm 3 states the log-domain version of Algorithm 1: per-index rescaling
to neutralize geometric decay in the polynomial powering, signed logsumexp for the Bell-transform
inner products and root assembly, and intermediate normalisation during the Cauchy product.
Generator derivative growth.
Each family’s derivative growth rate sets the precision ceiling that
the log-domain machinery must accommodate. For Clayton, ψ(t) = (1 + t)−1/θ, the Pochhammer
identity ψ(k)(t) = (−1)k (1/θ)k (1 + t)−1/θ−k gives |ψ(k)(t)|/k! = O(k1/θ−1) via Stirling, so
the jet-stored Taylor coefficients grow only polynomially in k for fixed θ and t. Joe and other
double-exponential generators grow faster because the chain rule nests an exponential inside another.
Empirically, every family matches Mathematica to relative error below 3 × 10−11 wherever D[]
completes (Table 10), and Table 9 confirms finite log-density evaluation up to d = 60 for nine of the
ten families and up to d = 4 for Nelsen9, whose d-completely monotone parameter range tightens
with d.
Edge composition safeguards.
The edge composition h = ψ−1
outer ◦ψinner that the nested density
requires admits three qualitatively different evaluation paths across our ten families (Appendix A.4),
and Frank in particular needs a closed-form rewrite to stay within float64. Clayton, AMH, Joe,
Gumbel, Nelsen9, and Nelsen12 provide closed-form inverses, so the composition introduces no
numerical root-finding error. InverseGaussian relies on Oryx’s symbolic inversion, which pattern-
matches individual primitives to their algebraic inverses. Two families also expose a numerical
hazard in the nested composition ψ−1
v (ψc(t)) even with closed-form inverses: Frank at d = 98
overflows because intermediate log-sum-exp terms exceed float64, and Nelsen9 underflows because
ψc(t) collapses to zero before ψ−1

can recover it. Both families ship a registered closed-form edge
composition (Appendix A.4) that algebraically cancels the offending exponentials, bypassing the
default implicit-differentiation solve of Algorithm 2.
Precision at large sector size.
At d=60 with sector size K=30, Appendix J reports a 3.5 × 10−5
discrepancy against R’s nacLL. Mathematica’s symbolic differentiation of the copula CDF confirms
that our framework matches the ground truth to machine epsilon at d=60; the discrepancy originates
in R’s nacLL, not in our polynomial powering.

Log-Space Taylor-Mode Differentiation
The log-domain machinery of Appendix B keeps the Bell pipeline’s polynomial coefficients finite
at high d, but it operates on outputs of jet_array.jet rather than inside the jet expansion itself.
For Frank and Joe generators on extreme-tail leaves, that boundary is exactly where the failure
now appears: the forward jet returns finite-but-denormal Taylor coefficients, and reverse-mode
AD then materialises 1/c for those denormal c, overflowing to ∞and producing ∞· 0 in the next
DOT_GENERAL step. We resolve the failure by representing every Taylor coefficient as a (sign, log |c|)
pair end-to-end; the log-space jet, opt-in via compile_model(..., log_jet=True).
C.1
Why naive float64 fails for Frank-type generators
For an Archimedean generator that decays as ψ(t) ≈c e−t at large t (Frank, Joe), the k-th Taylor
coefficient at the composed argument tr behaves like c e−tr/k! times a polynomial in k. Once
tr ≳500 — which for d=75 flat copies of u=10−3 gives tr = −P log uℓ≈518 — the high-order
coefficients drop into float64 denormal range (|ck| < 2.2 × 10−308) by k ≳60.
The forward computation tolerates the denormals. Backward does not: the upstream rules in
jet_array (notably _log_taylor and the body of _div_taylor_rule) recursively divide by c in
their Faà di Bruno recurrences, and the Jacobian-vector product of 1/c at c ∼10−310 is ∼10310,
beyond float64.max. The resulting ∞then meets a structural zero — for example, the constant
divisor’s higher Taylor terms in u/θ — and DOT_GENERAL returns NaN. The minimal reproducer is
d=75 flat-Frank with all leaves at u=10−3; raw forward log-density is 39.92, raw gradient is NaN.

Symbolic Inversion and Numeric Fallback
Two paths supply the inverse generator ψ−1 that the density and sampler require: a symbolic
derivation via Oryx for closed-form generators, and a numeric bisection fallback for everything
else. Oryx [Radul et al., 2023] derives ψ−1 by tracing the generator through JAX’s intermediate
representation and pattern-matching each primitive against a table of known algebraic inverses—for
example, exp ↔log, x 7→xa ↔x 7→x1/a, and x 7→x + c ↔x 7→x −c. The procedure succeeds
whenever the generator is a composition of individually invertible operations with no data-dependent
control flow. All ten built-in families satisfy this condition.
Oryx’s pattern matcher cannot invert functions that contain data-dependent branching—lax.cond
or lax.switch—because the branch the tracer follows at trace time may differ from the branch
the runtime selects at evaluation time. Functions that call numerical subroutines such as adaptive
quadrature or iterative solvers also fail, because those routines expand into opaque while_loop
primitives. The matcher raises an error at trace time in either case, never silently returning an incorrect
result.
The framework probes each generator at initialisation by evaluating the Oryx-derived inverse on a
concrete test value. When the probe succeeds, the symbolic inverse handles all subsequent calls.
When it fails, the framework switches transparently to a numeric path that solves ψ(s) = t for s
via optimistix.Bisection with tolerance 10−12. Because generators are strictly decreasing on
[0, ∞), the bisection bracket always exists. Gradients propagate through the root-finding step via the

Hofert [2008] identified the high-order generator-composition deriva-

derivatives in closed form for any user-supplied generator with the same complexity profile as the
density evaluation, so sampling and likelihood share a single code path.

Validity of Nested Constructions
A nested Archimedean copula defines a valid distribution only when every parent–child generator pair
satisfies a sufficient nesting condition. McNeil [2008] required the composition h = ψ−1
outer ◦ψinner
to be a Bernstein function (i.e., its derivative completely monotone): (−1)k−1h(k)(t) ≥0 for
all k ≥1 and t ≥0. McNeil and Nešlehová [2009] subsequently characterised d-dimensional
Archimedean copulas through the Williamson d-transform, weakening the complete-monotonicity
requirement on the generator itself to d-monotonicity and setting the foundation for the results below.
Rezapour [2015] showed that a weaker condition suffices: h need only be dc-monotone, that is,
(−1)m−1h(m)(t) ≥0 for m = 1, . . . , dc and all t ≥0, where dc counts uncensored leaves under the
child node. This finite condition matches the information our algorithm already computes: the JET
call at each edge returns exactly dc Taylor coefficients of h.
For the classical families, closed-form sufficient conditions exist: Clayton and Gumbel both require
θouter ≤θinner, Frank requires the same ordering, and Joe requires 1 ≤θouter ≤θinner. These
orderings guarantee complete monotonicity and therefore dc-monotonicity.
Our framework does not enforce these constraints automatically. For same-family pairs, a delta
parameterisation θinner = θouter +softplus(δ) enforces the ordering by construction; all same-family
experiments use this approach (Table 12).

Polynomial scaling in s.
Our Cauchy-product algorithm replaces R’s exponential partition enumer-
ation with polynomial multiplication, so cost in the number of sectors s grows polynomially rather
than exponentially. To quantify the difference, we time the JIT-compiled log-likelihood for nested
Clayton copulas while varying s from 2 to 50 at sector size K = 2, for which d = 2s. Execution time
grows from 0.4 ms at s=2 to 72 ms at s=50, matching approximately O(s1.7) scaling. Repeating
with K = 5 up to s=30, with d = 5s, yields a similar polynomial exponent. By contrast, R’s
partition count exceeds 1015 at s = 50, rendering exact enumeration infeasible. Beyond raw scaling,
the choice between exact and Monte Carlo ψ(k) evaluation involves further design tradeoffs that we
discuss next.
Extended Gen-AC comparison.
Our Taylor-mode computation of ψ(k) and the Monte Carlo
estimation in Gen-AC [Ng et al., 2021] occupy complementary points in the exactness–scalability
tradeoff. Taylor-mode AD computes each ψ(k) exactly in O(k) time per coefficient, yielding machine-
precision likelihoods that support gradient-based parameter estimation, nested copula structures,
and per-variable censoring without approximation error. Gen-AC, in contrast, sidesteps generator
differentiation by representing ψ(k) as a Monte Carlo average over learned latent variables; this
representation scales to very high k with bounded per-sample cost but introduces sampling variance
and restricts the generator to the learned Laplace class. For the moderate dimensions typical of
actuarial and survival-analysis applications, d ≤100, the exact approach dominates: it produces
unbiased gradients and handles arbitrary user-defined generators. Gen-AC suits regimes where only
samples—not likelihoods—matter, or where d is so large that even O(k) exact derivative cost grows
prohibitive.
Bivariate censored comparison with CopulaCenR.
ACOPULA reproduces CopulaCenR’s parame-

optimiser fails. We compare against CopulaCenR [Sun and Ding, 2020], an R package that fits

Exact MLE vs. Simulated Maximum Likelihood
Exact MLE through our Bell-polynomial likelihood dominates the simulated maximum likelihood
estimation (SMLE) approach that R nacopula [Hofert et al., 2013] relies on, in both parameter
accuracy and wall time, at every SMLE sample budget we test. The SMLE estimator draws N
frailty samples from the Laplace-transform hierarchy at each parameter evaluation, computes the
conditional density at each observation given those frailties, and averages over the N draws to
approximate the intractable marginal density. Because the outer copula parameter enters only
through the frailty distribution—not through the conditional density—the SMLE objective demands
fresh frailty samples at every evaluation, producing a noisy objective that forces derivative-free
optimisation. Our framework instead computes the exact density and exact gradients, opening the
door to L-BFGS-B with analytic gradients.

Hierarchical Kendall Copulas
Hierarchical Kendall copulas (HKCs) sit between vines and nested Archimedean copulas as a third
hierarchical construction, but they aggregate clusters through a scalar Kendall transform rather
than the full multivariate density—so they cannot supply the analytic gradients or per-variable
censoring that our framework provides on the same hierarchical structure. Brechmann [2014]
introduced hierarchical Kendall copulas as a construction intermediate between vines and nested
Archimedean copulas. HKCs aggregate each cluster of variables through the Kendall distribution
function KC(t) = Pr{C(U) ≤t}, building the joint distribution from cluster copulas linked by
univariate Kendall transforms rather than from generator nesting. Brechmann [2014] derives closed-
form HKC density expressions for the special case where every cluster copula is itself Archimedean,
drawing on the analytical Kendall function of each generator; for non-Archimedean cluster copulas the
density requires multivariate integration, which Brechmann [2014] approximates by sampling rather
than evaluates in closed form. Standard HKC implementations target sampling and pseudo-likelihood
estimation through L-BFGS without analytic parameter gradients, and they do not support censored
observations. Our ACOPULA framework therefore complements HKCs on the same hierarchical
structure with two extra properties: exact analytic gradients through the full multivariate density, and
per-variable censoring.
