Abstract

Introduction
1.1
The conditioning principle
Given the observed paid loss triangle D, the predictive distribution of the outstanding reserve
R is, in any well-specified probabilistic model, the conditional distribution p(R | D, ˆθ).
Sampling from the latter requires holding D fixed as conditioning information and simulating
only the future quantities whose distribution, conditional on D, is available from the model.
None of the existing standard bootstrap procedures for claims reserving do so. The ODP
bootstrap of England and Verrall (2002) resamples Pearson residuals from the observed
triangle and re-estimates Chain-Ladder development factors from synthetic triangles; the
Mack bootstrap likewise resamples residuals from the observed data. Both treat D as a
random variable to be resampled rather than as fixed conditioning information. As such,
the resulting coverage discrepancy does not vanish as the triangle grows, as formalized in
Lemma 7.2.
The Dirichlet–Gamma hierarchy for claim amounts admits a bootstrap that satisfies
this requirement exactly. Given observed cumulative payments Xobs

paid amount that has already been observed. As such, the observed data enter the bootstrap

Two consequences follow. The bootstrap is, first, model-agnostic: the development pro-
portions FI−i that parametrise the Beta can come from any method — Chain-Ladder,
Bornhuetter-Ferguson, Cape Cod, a GLM, or any other procedure that produces cumu-
lative proportions. The coverage deficit, second, is O(I−1/2) rather than O(1), since the only
estimation error is in the single parameter ˆc, whose asymptotic variance is derived explicitly
in Proposition 4.6.

1.2
Why the Dirichlet–Gamma hierarchy
The Dirichlet–Gamma hierarchy separates two sources of uncertainty with distinct actuar-
ial meaning: the ultimate paid amount Si ∼Gamma(αEi, β) captures the uncertainty on
how much will eventually be paid, while the allocation (Xi,·/Si) ∼Dirichlet(cπ) captures
the uncertainty on when it will be paid. Once Xobs
i
is observed, the former is partially re-
solved; the remaining uncertainty is purely allocational, governed by c and FI−i alone. The
latter separation is what renders the conditioning principle implementable: the predictive
distribution of (1 −Wi)/Wi conditional on Xobs
i
is available in closed form, requiring neither
approximation nor resampling.
Note that the credibility correspondence is a structural consequence of this hierarchy,
not an additional assumption. Chain-Ladder, Bornhuetter-Ferguson and Cape Cod emerge
as credibility estimators under diffuse, informative and pooling priors respectively, with
identical formulas for counts and amounts.
1.3
Contributions
The bootstrap of Section 7 provides uncertainty quantification that operates uniformly across
all major macro-level reserving methods. Existing bootstrap procedures (ODP and Mack)
operate only within the Chain-Ladder family.
Bornhuetter-Ferguson and Cape Cod, de-
spite being among the most widely used reserving methods, have no published bootstrap
for predictive interval construction; standard practice for these methods is to report point
estimates without uncertainty quantification. The multinomial bootstrap takes any vector
of cumulative development proportions ˆF as input — from Chain-Ladder, BF, Cape Cod,
or any other procedure — and returns calibrated predictive intervals. Section 9 illustrates
the latter on a Bornhuetter-Ferguson example.
The conditioning principle (Principle 7.1) is formalised as the requirement that a re-
serving bootstrap holds D fixed and samples only from p(R | D, ˆθ). The Dirichlet–Gamma
bootstrap satisfies the latter principle and the coverage deficit is O(I−1/2), independent of
J (Theorem 7.4).
Violating the principle, however, carries a permanent cost: the ODP
bootstrap commits a coverage discrepancy of Ω(1) regardless of triangle size, through two
mechanisms operating in opposite directions — re-estimation inflation and missing frailty
(Lemma 7.2). The cross-portfolio heterogeneity in miscalibration documented by Meyers
(2015) receives, as such, a structural explanation.

1.4
Related literature
Mack (1993) established distribution-free Chain-Ladder uncertainty; Renshaw and Verrall
(1998) and Verrall (2000) provided the GLM foundations for stochastic Chain-Ladder; Eng-
land and Verrall (2002) developed the ODP bootstrap; Wüthrich and Merz (2008) covers
stochastic reserving methods in detail. Note that several stochastic models reproduce the
Chain-Ladder point estimate (Mack, 1994; Mack and Venter, 2000; Renshaw and Verrall,
1998); the Dirichlet–Gamma hierarchy of the present paper adds to this list under a credibil-
ity interpretation. Tweedie GLMs (Tweedie, 1984; Jørgensen, 1987; Smyth and Jørgensen,
2002) are standard for severity modelling, with McCullagh and Nelder (1989) the canon-
ical reference on the GLM framework itself. The Dirichlet model for development has an
ancestor in Mack (1999), while Clark (2003) fitted smooth growth curves via maximum likeli-
hood. The systematic miscalibration of ODP and Mack across the Meyers (2015) Schedule P
database provides the empirical motivation for the present paper; Lemma 7.2 provides the
structural explanation through two mechanisms whose interaction varies across portfolios.
Note that the conditioning principle is, on a more foundational level, rooted in classical
bootstrap theory. Davison and Hinkley (1997) and Hall (1992) establish that the correct
bootstrap targets the conditional distribution of the statistic given the observed data. The
present paper applies the latter principle specifically to the predictive distribution in claims
reserving, hereby targeting p(R | D) rather than a sampling distribution.
A Dirichlet allocation model for loss reserving was previously introduced by Sriram and
Shi (2021), who use it to unify Chain-Ladder and Bornhuetter-Ferguson predictions un-
der a common statistical framework and discuss both frequentist and Bayesian inference on
Worker’s compensation triangles. The present paper takes the same Dirichlet allocation as
its starting point but pursues a different objective: rather than unifying Chain-Ladder and
Bornhuetter-Ferguson on the point-estimate level, we formalise the conditioning principle, de-
velop the model-agnostic bootstrap inheriting calibration from any development-proportion
method (Cape Cod included), derive the per-cell asymptotic variance of ˆc in explicit closed
form, and prove conservative bias under Tweedie misspecification. The two papers are, as
such, complementary: Sriram and Shi (2021) establishes the Dirichlet allocation as a unifier
of Chain-Ladder and Bornhuetter-Ferguson at the point-estimate level; the present paper
establishes its bootstrap as the calibrated uncertainty quantifier for that allocation and for
Cape Cod.
The Compound Negative Binomial-Gamma paper (Van Oirbeek, 2026a) establishes the
correctly specified parametric model for incremental paid amounts; the present paper estab-
lishes the model-agnostic bootstrap that the former cites.

bounded by the prior Ei·qBF and absorbs uncertainty only through Wi ∈(0, 1), while the CL
reserve scales with the heavy-tailed ratio (1 −Wi)/Wi. The choice of anchor is therefore not
only a point-estimate choice but a choice of uncertainty regime. The ODP bootstrap, which
resamples residuals from the observed triangle to re-estimate development factors, has no
analogue for BF—it cannot be applied without an observed-residual structure to resample.
10
Discussion
10.1
Summary
Existing bootstrap procedures for claims reserving violate the conditioning principle: they
resample functions of the observed triangle inside the predictive loop and, as such, commit
a coverage discrepancy that does not vanish as the triangle grows. The Dirichlet–Gamma
hierarchy, however, admits a bootstrap that respects it exactly: the observed cumulative
payments Xobs
i
are held fixed, while only the allocation proportion Wi is sampled. As such,
the bootstrap is model-agnostic and inherits its calibration from whatever development-
proportion method produced ˆFI−i.
The behaviour of the latter bootstrap is fully characterised. The coverage deficit under
correct specification is O(I−1/2), with the asymptotic variance of ˆc derived in explicit closed
form (Proposition 4.6).
The bias direction under compound Poisson misspecification is
conservative, with the overestimation factor 1/√FI−i derived analytically (Theorem 7.5).
The structural failure of the ODP bootstrap, finally, is proved rather than documented
(Lemma 7.2).
Note that the credibility correspondence holds identically for counts and
amounts, hereby unifying Chain-Ladder, Bornhuetter-Ferguson and Cape Cod under a single
probabilistic foundation.
10.2
The diagnostic role of c
When ˆc ≥30 and I ≥10, the bootstrap produces well-calibrated intervals. When ˆc < 30,
the diagnostic redirects the practitioner toward models with explicit accident-year frailty
(Van Oirbeek, 2026a).
10.3
Limitations
The baseline model assumes stationary development proportions. It does not model calendar-
year effects or accident-year dependence. The misspecification regimes where coverage de-
grades — non-stationary development beyond σδ = 0.05, and ˆc < 30 at I < 10 — are
mapped empirically in Sections 8–9.

A.4
Bayesian regularisation for small triangles
For I < 6, the moment estimator requires fewer than three rows per horizon and is unreliable.
As such, a Bayesian regularisation is required. Following the logic of Taylor (2015) for small
triangles, we place a log-normal prior log c ∼N(log 50, 1), with Metropolis-within-Gibbs
sampling delivering the posterior of c. The full Bayesian variant achieves 91% coverage at
I = 4 versus 89% for the plug-in bootstrap; for I ≥5 with ˆc ≥50 the frequentist bootstrap
suffices.
