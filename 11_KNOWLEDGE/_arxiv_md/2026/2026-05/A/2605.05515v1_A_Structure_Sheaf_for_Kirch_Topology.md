Abstract
Kirch topology on N goes back to 1969, and is remarkable for being Hausdorff,
connected, and locally connected. In this sense, it is analogous to the usual topology
on C, yet, to the author’s knowledge, there have been no Kirch topology analogs
of the sheaf of complex-analytic functions until very recently. In our paper [1] we
constructed such natural sheaf of rings, the sheaf of locally LIP functions. In this
paper we investigate some of its basic properties, primarily regarding zeroth and
first cohomology and ˇCech cohomology with respect to covers by basic open sets.
1. Introduction
In this paper N will mean the set of all positive integers and N0 the set of all non-
negative integers. By an arithmetic progression in N we mean an infinite arithmetic
progression: a set of the form a + dN0 for some natural a and d.
Kirch topology on N is defined as the topology with the basis of open sets formed
by all infinite arithmetic progressions a + dN0, where gcd(a, d) = 1 and d is square-
free. This topology is Hausdorff, connected, and locally connected (cf. [4], [7]).
There is also another version of this definition, where N is replaced by Z \ {0}. It
has the same topological properties and can be easily generalized to other domains
(cf. [3]). In this paper we will present our results in the classical setting over N,
but all lemmas and theorems have natural analogs for Z \ {0} and proofs are valid
with just minimal notational modifications.
To explain our results, we first introduce some terminology.
Definition 1. An arithmetic progression in N is called full if it is not contained
in a strictly bigger arithmetic progression with the same common difference. Al-
ternatively, full arithmetic progressions are intersections with N of doubly-infinite
arithmetic progressions in Z.
Definition 2. Suppose X ⊆N is a set. We will call the intersection of all full
arithmetic progressions in N that contain X the arithmetic convex closure (AC

Definition 5. Suppose U is an infinite subset of Z. Then f : U →Z is a locally

Lemma 2 ([1], Lemma 2). Suppose X = {x0, . . . , xd} ⊂Z. Suppose fX(x) ∈Q[x]

all x ∈X. Then fX(x) ∈Z[x].

collection of all LIP(U) is a presheaf, a sub-presheaf of the sheaf of all integer-
valued functions. Its sheafification gives the following important definition (cf. [1],
Definition 6).
Definition 6. Suppose U is a non-empty Kirch-open subset of N. Then a function
f : U →Z is called locally LIP if for every a ∈U there exists a Kirch-open Ua ⊆U

Lemma 7 (Proximity Principle). Suppose U is a Kirch-open set, n ∈N, and p is
a prime. Suppose there is some u ∈U such that u ≡n (mod p). Then for every
k ∈N there are infinitely many u ∈U such that u ≡n (mod pk).
Proof. Because U is open, we have some u + dN0 ⊆U, where d is square-free.
Suppose n = u + pim, where p does not divide m. We need to find x that solves
the congruence u + dx ≡u + pim (mod pk). Since i ≥1 and the order of p in d is
at most 1, this is always possible. And once we find one such x, any x + pky for
integer y will also work.

4. H1 and Higher Cohomology
Our intuition suggests that almost basic sets form a Leray cover for the arithmetic
structure sheaf, that is Hi(U, LIP) = 0 for all i > 0 for almost basic sets U
(cf. [8]). From [8], Theorem 7.8.5, this would imply that the sheaf cohomology
groups Hi(U, LIP) coincide with ˇCech cohomology using any cover by almost basic
sets. We cannot prove it yet, but we will prove it in this section for n = 1. We
assume some familiarity with sheaf cohomology and ˇCech cohomology. Since we are
primarily concerned with n = 1, we will avoid the spectral sequences formalism.
Remark 12 (on notation). When the desire for brevity of notation outweighs the
risk of being insufficiently pedantic, we will say that a function f : X →Z is LIP
on Y ⊆X whenever the restriction of f to Y is in LIP(Y ). In the same spirit
of suppressing the obviously necessary restriction maps, if f1 : X1 →Z and if
f2 : X2 →Z are functions, we will say that f1 −f2 is LIP on X1 ∩X2 whenever the
difference of the restrictions is LIP.
In order to work with H1(U, LIP), it is convenient to make some new definitions.
Definition 12. We denote by Z the sheaf of all Z-valued functions. Note that
LIP is a subsheaf of Z. The presheaf ZL is defined by ZL(U) = Z(U)/LIP(U).
Note that if LIP(U) = LIP(U), then ZL(U) = Z(U)/LIP(U). Finally, we define
the sheaf ZL to be the sheafification of the presheaf ZL.
Lemma 16. Suppose U is a Kirch open set. Then
1) ZL(U) naturally embeds into ZL(U).
2) The quotient of ZL(U) by the ZL(U) is isomorphic to H1(U, LIP).
3) For every k ≥2 we have Hk−1(U, ZL) ∼= Hk(U, LIP).
Proof. Be definition, we have a short exact sequence of sheaves
0 →LIP →Z →ZL →0.
Because Z is flasque, Hi(U, Z) = 0 for all U and all i > 0. By the long exact

0 →LIP(U) →Z(U) →ZL(U) →H1(U, LIP) →0;
0 →Hk−1(U, ZL) →Hk(U, LIP) →0,
for all k ≥2.
The result follows.

5. Conjectures and Comments
Based on the available results and our intuition, we propose the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1. For all almost basic sets U for all k > 0 Hk(U, LIP) = 0.
This conjecture would imply that ˇCech cohomology with respect to any cover by
almost basic sets calculates the correct sheaf cohomology of LIP (cf. [8], Theorem
7.8.5). In particular, by Theorem 27 the vanishing of higher cohomology should
generalize to almost star-like sets. One would also like to identify some natural
category of sheaves of modules over the LIP sheaf for which the same kind of
vanishing would hold.
Clearly, the obstruction in Theorem 26 can be generalized significantly. It would
be very interesting to figure out the exact structure of H1(Upq, LIP) for all odd
primes p and q, where Upq = N\pqN. There are reasons to believe that the Quadratic
18


## Page 19


Reciprocity Law for p and q can be reproved or reinterpreted using this non-zero
group or, perhaps, H1(U2pq, LIP) or H2(U2pq, LIP). Higher cohomology groups
may be related to more complicated constructions, like triple symbols.
In this paper we worked in the classical setting of Kirch topology on N. But the
parallel constructions for Z\{0} would work essentially the same way. Perhaps, the
main thing that will be lost is the discrete derivative identity from Remark 3. And
in the proofs of some theorems we would choose an ordering on Z\{0} to talk about
“first n elements” of its subsets. This clearly opens the door to generalizations to
other rings, in particular to Dedekind domains (cf. [3]).
Besides changing the input space, we can also change the target. In particular, it
makes sense to look at localizations of Z (cf. [6]). Another natural target is integers
in cyclotomic fields. Including them would allow to incorporate in our theory the
general Dirichlet characters.
As mentioned above, the first test of applicability for our theory may be to
reprove or reinterpret the Quadratic Reciprocity Law. Higher reciprocity laws and
Dirichlet Theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions are (perhaps, unreasonably
ambitious) next goals. For applications like this, one should try to get some natural
collection of LIP and locally LIP functions. One may also want to develop a version
of our theory that incorporates the “infinite prime”: include some conditions on the
growth of LIP functions. These conditions would have to be sufficiently lenient to
get a nontrivial theory (cf. [1]).
Number-theoretic applications and meaning of Kirch topology have not been
studied much. For example, Quadratic Reciprocity Law and Dirichlet’s Theorem
imply that the set of squares is closed in N. It is also connected and has a connected
complement. This is a particular case of several natural phenomena (cf. [2]).
One would like to explore any possible connections with Arithmetic Topology,
which is a much more sophisticated theory that claims analogy between Z and its
finite extensions and the topology of three-dimensional manifolds (cf. [5]).
There must also exist strong connections with the p-adic Analysis. After all,
product-sums converge with respect to the trivial norm on Z, so convergence with
respect to the p-adic norm would be its close relative. Incidentally, for an element a
of the p-adic integers Zp and an infinite subset V of Z the product-sum for any LIP
function f on V converges p-adically at a if and only if there are infinitely many
vi ∈V such that |vi −a|p < 1. Moreover, in this case the sum does not depend on
how we count elements of V (cf. [6]). The fact that we do not need a to be in the
p-adic closure of V , i.e. congruences modulo higher power of p do not come into
