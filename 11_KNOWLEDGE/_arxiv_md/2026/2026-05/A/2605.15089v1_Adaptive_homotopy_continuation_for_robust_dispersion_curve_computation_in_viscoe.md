Abstract
This paper presents the first systematic application of a material homotopy continuation
framework for efficient, automated computation of dispersion curves in viscoelastic waveg-
uides of arbitrary cross-section. A material homotopy continuously maps the original lossy
problem to an auxiliary lossless one via an attenuation parameter s in [0,1], addressing the
core challenges of the non-Hermitian eigenvalue problem. Grounded in analytic perturba-
tion theory, the method guarantees branch identity continuity—a one-to-one correspondence
between solutions at s=0 and s=1—provided the real-parameter path does not cross any ex-
ceptional points. Under a Type I exceptional point topology, physical mode labels established
at the elastic stage remain valid at the viscoelastic stage without post-processing, yielding
the characteristic real-part veering with imaginary-part crossing. The decoupling strategy
performs reliable mode tracking in the Hermitian regime via adaptive wavenumber refine-
ment, then propagates a sparse set of key solutions to the target viscoelastic state through
predictor-corrector homotopy continuation. Numerical examples across symmetric and un-
symmetric laminates validate the framework’s robustness and efficiency, with the majority of
cases verified at a loss factor of approximately 0.003 and a single symmetric laminate pro-
viding additional support at 0.02. For a challenging unsymmetric laminate at a loss factor
of 0.05, the method still produces numerically accurate solutions; two complementary diag-
nostic signatures—an extremely sharp imaginary-part crossing and a discernible discrepancy
between spectral group velocity and energy flux velocity—warn of potential label mismatch
and guide further analysis.
Keywords
Viscoelastic waveguides; Non-Hermitian eigenvalue problems; Guided wave dispersion; Homotopy
continuation; Exceptional points; Type I/II EP topology.

Introduction
Ultrasonic guided waves have emerged as a powerful tool for the non-destructive evaluation (NDE)
and structural health monitoring (SHM) of elongated engineering structures, owing to their ability to
propagate over long distances and their sensitivity to a variety of defects [1, 2]. The successful application
of guided wave-based techniques fundamentally relies on a thorough understanding of the dispersion
characteristics of the host waveguide, namely the relationship between frequency, phase velocity, group
velocity, and, in the case of lossy materials, attenuation [3]. For waveguides with simple geometries and
elastic material behavior, analytical or semi-analytical solutions are available [4].
However, modern engineering structures increasingly employ materials with inherent viscoelastic
properties, such as carbon fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) with lossy matrices, which introduces signif-
icant material attenuation [5]. Viscoelastic materials are widely used across aerospace, automotive, and
civil engineering sectors for their vibration damping and acoustic insulation capabilities [6]. Even when
the base material is elastic, adhesive layers or protective coatings often exhibit viscoelastic behavior that
modifies the wave propagation characteristics. Consequently, the analysis of guided waves in viscoelastic
waveguides of arbitrary cross-section has become essential for accurate NDE/SHM of a wide range of
components, including laminated composites, polymeric pipes, and structures with damping treatments.
Over the past decades, numerous methods have been developed for calculating dispersion curves in
waveguides. These can be broadly categorized into exact methods based on three-dimensional elasticity
theory, approximate methods, and semi-analytical approaches. Each method presents its own advantages
and limitations in terms of accuracy, computational efficiency, and applicability to complex material
systems.
Matrix methods based on partial wave decomposition represent one major family of approaches.
The Global Matrix Method (GMM) formulates the wave propagation problem by assembling matrices
that enforce continuity conditions at layer interfaces, providing a stable formulation for multilayered
systems [7]. The Transfer Matrix Method (TMM) offers a more computationally efficient alternative but
suffers from numerical instability at high frequency-thickness products [8]. To overcome this limitation,
the Stiffness Matrix Method (SMM) and its variants, including the Hybrid Compliance-Stiffness Matrix
Method (HCSMM), have been developed to maintain numerical stability while preserving computational
efficiency [9, 10]. These matrix methods have been implemented in widely used software packages such as
DISPERSE [11], and Dispersion Calculator (DC) [12, 13], which employs the GMM and SMM respectively
to handle multi-layered Cartesian and cylindrical systems for both leaky and non-leaky waves.
The Semi-Analytical Finite Element (SAFE) method has been established as one of the most versatile
and efficient techniques for modeling wave propagation in waveguides with arbitrary cross-section and
complex material behavior [14]. The SAFE method discretizes only the cross-section of the waveguide
using finite elements, while assuming a harmonic wave propagation along the axial direction.
This
approach reduces the three-dimensional problem to a two-dimensional one, yielding an eigenvalue problem
that can be solved for wavenumbers at discrete frequencies [15, 16]. Over the past two decades, the
SAFE method has been extensively developed and applied to a wide range of problems. Bartoli et al.
[17] presented a comprehensive SAFE formulation for damped waveguides of arbitrary cross-section,
demonstrating its capability to extract both phase velocity and attenuation dispersion curves. Marzani
et al. [18] further extended this formulation to model stress wave propagation in axisymmetric damped
waveguides.
Mu and Rose [19] applied SAFE methods to study guided waves in viscoelastic-coated
hollow cylinders, deriving orthogonality relations to facilitate mode sorting. Mazzotti et al. [20] coupled
SAFE with a 2.5D Boundary Element Method (BEM) to analyze leaky guided waves in waveguides
embedded in unbounded viscoelastic media. More recently, SAFE formulations have been adapted for
piezoelectric materials [21], prestressed viscoelastic waveguides [22], thin-walled orthotropic structures

Alternative discretization techniques based on global basis functions have also been proposed. Among
these, the spectral collocation method (SCM) has gained attention for its conceptual simplicity and spec-
tral accuracy [26]. Quintanilla et al. [27] developed a pseudospectral collocation method for generally
anisotropic viscoelastic media, employing both Kelvin–Voigt and hysteretic damping models. Unlike
SAFE, which discretizes the cross-section using finite elements with local support, SCM approximates
field variables using global polynomials (e.g., Chebyshev polynomials) and enforces the governing equa-
tions at collocation points. To handle complex wavenumbers in viscoelastic media, they extended the
SCM framework using the companion matrix method, which transforms the original polynomial eigen-
value problem into a larger linear eigenvalue problem that can be solved directly by standard eigensolvers
[27]. This approach yields the full complex spectrum at a given frequency, and the results were validated
against SAFE simulations. More recently, the SCM has been extended to incorporate acoustoelasticity
and viscoelasticity for soft materials, demonstrating its versatility for complex physical phenomena. The
Legendre Polynomial Method (LPM) [28] represents another global approach, expanding the field vari-
ables in terms of orthogonal polynomials and has been successfully applied to both elastic and viscoelastic
waveguides.
Recent comparative studies have evaluated the performance of various methods for viscoelastic waveg-
uides. Orta et al. [29] conducted a comprehensive comparison of six methods—including GMM, SMM,
HCSMM, SAFE, LPM, and fifth-order shear deformation theory (5-SDT)—for calculating complex
wavenumbers in viscoelastic orthotropic multi-layer laminates. Their study highlighted the accuracy
and computational efficiency of different approaches and resulted in the freely available toolbox “The
Dispersion Box”. However, their validation was primarily performed on symmetric laminates. Quiroga
et al. [30] further assessed five analytical and semi-analytical methods for viscoelastic plates, including
the Rayleigh–Lamb solution, GMM, SAFE, Scaled Boundary Finite Element Method (SBFEM), and
LPM. Their study demonstrated that the SAFE method exhibits a remarkable balance between com-
putational efficiency and physical accuracy, outperforming SBFEM at high frequencies. For epoxy and
high-performance polyethylene plates, the SAFE method and LPM significantly outperform GMM in
computational efficiency, with errors below 1% for fundamental modes across 0–100 kHz. Barazanchy
and Giurgiutiu [31] compared the unified analytic method, SAFE method, and DISPERSE for composite
materials, providing a concise overview of the advantages and limitations of each approach to assist end
users in selecting the appropriate tool.
Despite the valuable contributions of these studies and the diversity of available methods, a persis-
tent and fundamental gap remains for viscoelastic waveguides characterized by hysteretic (frequency-
independent) damping.
All existing methods share two intertwined difficulties: the solution of the
non-Hermitian eigenvalue problem and the subsequent tracing of modes across frequency to assemble
continuous dispersion curves.
The presence of material damping introduces complex arithmetic and destroys the Hermitian struc-
ture of the system matrices, leading to a polynomial eigenvalue problem in the complex wavenumber.
Whether one adopts SAFE (with linearization), SCM (with companion matrix method), or matrix meth-
ods (with complex root searching), the core numerical challenge remains—solving a non-Hermitian eigen-
value problem at discrete frequencies. For small to medium-scale models, a dense direct eigensolver (e.g.,
eig in MATLAB) can compute all eigenvalues at once, offering simplicity and completeness. However,
this approach scales poorly: computational cost grows as O(n3) and memory as O(n2), rendering it im-
practical for large-scale finite element models with thousands of degrees of freedom. Iterative eigensolvers
(e.g., shift-invert Arnoldi) mitigate the scalability issue but introduce heuristic parameter selection: shift
parameters must be chosen carefully to capture physical modes, and multiple shifts are often required to
explore the complex plane, risking omission of relevant solutions [32, 33]. Contour integral methods [34]

offer a more robust alternative for extracting all eigenvalues within a specified region, albeit at increased
computational cost. Complex root-searching methods [29, 30], while accurate for isolated modes, become
increasingly cumbersome in densely populated mode regions or when modes exhibit veering and crossing
behaviors.
Furthermore, the mode tracking problem—associating solutions at adjacent frequencies to form con-
tinuous modal branches—is equally challenging. In viscoelastic waveguides, the non-Hermitian nature of
the system introduces additional complexities to mode veering and degeneracy phenomena. In regions
where mode veering occurs, eigenvectors rapidly exchange characteristics over a narrow frequency in-
terval, making it exceedingly difficult to maintain correct mode identity even for sophisticated tracking
algorithms [35], let alone for conventional tools such as the Modal Assurance Criterion (MAC) [36]. Crit-
ically, errors in eigenvalue computation propagate irreversibly into mode tracking, and once misassigned,
modes cannot be corrected by post-processing alone.
Frequency continuation methods, which have been successfully used in solving nonlinear eigenvalue
problems [37, 38] and applied to trace modal branches in structural dynamics [39, 40], offer an efficient
alternative for tracing individual dispersion modes [41, 42].
These methods focus on tracing modal
branches in the frequency domain at a fixed material state (or along design parameters such as fiber
orientation). However, when applied to dispersion mode tracking in viscoelastic waveguides, their direct
application at the target damping state introduces fundamental difficulties. First, they require a high-
quality starting solution—a complex eigensolution that is itself challenging to obtain reliably in non-
Hermitian systems. Second, they are highly sensitive to mode veering and degeneracy: near exceptional
points, where eigenvalues coalesce and eigenvectors become parallel, the Jacobian of the extended system
becomes singular, causing continuation failure.
Unlike in Hermitian systems, where veering can be
resolved with sufficient frequency resolution, non-Hermitian systems offer no such guarantee, and mode
crossings are not necessarily symmetry-protected, making it impossible to ensure that a continuation
path remains on the intended modal branch.
As detailed in the preceding review (see also Appendix A for a comprehensive discussion), existing
methods share a common structural limitation: they fix the material state at the target viscoelastic con-
figuration (s = 1) and tackle the resulting non-Hermitian eigenvalue problem directly, thereby coupling
the challenges of eigenvalue solution and mode tracking in a regime where both are at their most difficult.
In doing so, they overlook a fundamental fact—the dispersion curves of a viscoelastic waveguide are con-
tinuously connected to those of the corresponding elastic waveguide through the material loss parameter.
This connection, governed by the analytic topology of the underlying Riemann surface, offers a natural
pathway to carry well-established modal identities from the Hermitian limit into the lossy regime. The
present work exploits this pathway by introducing an adaptive homotopy continuation (HC) framework
that decouples mode tracking from the non-Hermitian solution process.
To the best of our knowledge, no existing dispersion computation framework—whether based on
SAFE, GMM, SMM, or SCM—has systematically exploited homotopy continuation in the material pa-
rameter space. The present work fills this gap. Owing to its decoupled architecture, the HC framework
is capable of producing accurate viscoelastic dispersion solutions at s = 1 irrespective of the exceptional
point (EP) topology of the target system. The focus of the present work, however, is on the practically
important regime in which the target viscoelastic state possesses a Type I EP topology—i.e., the two
EPs governing a modal interaction remain on opposite sides of the real frequency axis. Under this con-
dition, no topological transition occurs along the real homotopy path s ∈[0, 1], and the physical mode
labels established at the elastic stage are automatically inherited by the viscoelastic solutions without
any post-processing. A robustness study under elevated damping, where the Type I assumption is likely
violated and the system enters a Type II regime, is also presented; the framework still returns numer-
ically accurate solutions in this regime, and two physically motivated post-hoc indicators are identified

The core insight of this work is twofold. First, the lossless (elastic) problem is Hermitian: eigenvalues
are real, eigenvectors are mutually orthogonal, and mode tracking via the Modal Assurance Criterion
(MAC) is reliable even through veering regions when adaptive refinement is employed.
Second, the
material loss can be introduced as a continuous analytic parameter s ∈[0, 1] , and the analytic dependence
of the system matrices on s provides a foundation for establishing that each eigenpair can be uniquely
and continuously tracked along the real homotopy path—provided no EP is intersected.
This decoupling is grounded in three theoretical pillars. Analytic perturbation theory [43] establishes
that eigenvalues of analytically dependent matrices form continuous Riemann surfaces away from EPs,
thereby guaranteeing a unique analytic continuation—the branch identity—along any real-parameter
path that avoids EPs. Crossing theory for non-Hermitian systems [44] and the physics of branch-point
projection [45–47] reveal that the observable veering and crossing patterns along the real frequency axis
are direct projections of the underlying EP configuration in the complex plane. Together, these theories
imply that if the material loss is introduced as a continuous analytic parameter, the modal identities
established in the lossless limit can, in principle, be propagated into the viscoelastic regime without
recourse to post-processing heuristics. Whether the physical labels remain valid at the target damping
state depends on the EP topology (Type I or Type II) in the observation space—a distinction that
governs the mode-interaction patterns and that will be fully developed in Section 2.2.4 and numerically
investigated in Section 4.3.
The contributions of this work are threefold:
1. Theoretical contribution. We synthesize analytic perturbation theory, EP physics, and non-
Hermitian crossing theory to establish that, provided the target viscoelastic state (s = 1) has
a Type I EP topology, the branch identity (one-to-one correspondence of solutions) is preserved
along the material homotopy path, and the physical mode labels established at the elastic stage
are automatically inherited at the viscoelastic stage without any post-processing.
2. Methodological contribution. We develop a complete computational framework that performs
accurate mode tracking in the Hermitian regime (s = 0), selects sparse key points, and propagates
them to s = 1 using a predictor-corrector homotopy continuation with adaptive step-size control.
The framework is inherently parallelizable and applies without modification to both frequency-
independent and frequency-dependent damping models.
3. Physical insights and diagnostic tools. We clarify how the same analytic structure governs
both the homotopy continuity in the coupling parameter space and the Type I crossing patterns
observed along the real frequency axis. Furthermore, through a robustness study at an elevated
loss factor where the Type I assumption is violated, we identify two physically grounded post-
hoc indicators of the Type II transition—an extremely sharp imaginary-wavenumber crossing and
a discernible discrepancy between the spectral group velocity and the energy flux velocity—that
enable the user to detect potential label mismatch and perform the necessary label exchange,
extending the practical utility of the framework beyond the strictly Type I regime.

material homotopy, describes its numerical implementation. It also discusses the theoretical foundations

crossing type in non-hermitian system. Section 3 presents numerical examples that validate the accuracy
and efficiency of the proposed method.
Section 4 discusses the computational efficiency, scalability,

2.2
Adaptive homotopy continuation for non-Hermitian dispersion analysis:
decoupled tracking with theoretical foundations
The proposed framework rests on a material homotopy: a continuous transformation parameterised by a
scalar attenuation factor s ∈[0, 1], which maps the target viscoelastic (lossy, non-Hermitian) problem at
s = 1 into an auxiliary elastic (lossless, Hermitian) problem at s = 0. Mode identification and tracking
are performed at s = 0, where the eigenvalue problem is well-conditioned, eigenvectors are mutually
orthogonal, and the Modal Assurance Criterion (MAC) provides a reliable metric for establishing modal
continuity. The resulting identities are then propagated to the target viscoelastic state along the ho-
motopy path. Provided this path does not intersect any EP, the eigen-solutions vary analytically with
respect to s [43], guaranteeing a one-to-one correspondence between the elastic and viscoelastic solutions.
Figure 1 illustrates the overall strategy. Hermitian mode tracking at s = 0 produces a complete
set of dispersion branches with unambiguous modal labels. These serve as starting points for predic-
tor–corrector continuation along the homotopy path s = 0 →s = 1, where the frequency ω is held
fixed and the wavenumber k and eigenvector q adapt to the evolving material properties. When the
target system exhibits a Type I EP topology (EPs on opposite sides of the real frequency axis, see Ap-
pendix B), the physical mode labels established at s = 0 remain valid at s = 1, and Type I dispersion
behaviour—real-part veering with imaginary-part crossing—emerges automatically without any post-
processing. The detailed construction of the homotopy and the associated predictor–corrector algorithm
are presented in Sections 2.2.1 to 2.2.3.

2.2.4
Theoretical foundations of branch and mode identity continuity along the homotopy
path
The proposed homotopy framework rests on two assumptions that together govern the continuity of
modal identities. The first is that the real-parameter path s ∈[0, 1] does not intersect any exceptional
point (EP). Under this condition, the Jacobian remains non-singular and each eigenpair can be uniquely
continued from s = 0 to s = 1, defining the branch identity [43]—a one-to-one correspondence between
the elastic and viscoelastic solutions that holds regardless of the EP topology at s = 1. The second
assumption is that the target viscoelastic state (s = 1) possesses a Type I EP topology (see Appendix B
for precise definitions), i.e., the two EPs governing a modal interaction lie on opposite sides of the real
frequency axis. In this case, the physical mode labels established at s = 0 are automatically inherited at
s = 1 without post-processing.
The practical robustness of the first assumption is supported by two considerations.
First, it is
essential to distinguish between the two parameter spaces in which EPs may reside. The homotopy
operates in the coupling parameter space (s) at fixed real frequency ω, where an EP is defined at a
point sEP in the complex s-plane. The observation space, in contrast, is the complex frequency plane
at s = 1, where EPs govern the Type I/II crossing behaviour along the real frequency axis. These two
spaces are independent: the migration of EPs in the ω-plane as damping increases—and the possible
crossing of the real frequency axis discussed in Section 4.3—does not imply anything about the location
of EPs in the s-plane along the homotopy path, and vice versa. In practice, the homotopy path s ∈[0, 1]
is a one-dimensional real interval in the complex s-plane; should an EP of the s-parameterised problem
happen to lie on or very close to this interval for a particular waveguide configuration, the numerical
safeguard described next will detect it.
Second, the predictor–corrector algorithm itself provides this safeguard: as the continuation path
approaches an EP, the Jacobian matrix becomes increasingly ill-conditioned, causing the Newton cor-
rector to diverge or the adaptive step-size control to reduce ∆s below the minimum threshold, at which
point the algorithm terminates without returning a solution at s = 1 (see Section 2.2.5 for algorithmic

## Page 12


details). Successful completion therefore constitutes numerical evidence that the path avoided all EPs.
Should the algorithm terminate prematurely—which has not occurred in any of the numerical examples
presented in Section 3—the user is alerted and can either adjust the homotopy path or verify whether
the target configuration indeed falls outside the intended Type I regime.
The theoretical basis for the second assumption—the preservation of physical mode labels—is pro-
vided by the interplay between analytic perturbation theory [43] and the projection principle of branch
points [47], as reviewed in Appendix B. In the elastic limit (s = 0), the EPs form a complex-conjugate
pair symmetric about the real frequency axis, corresponding to a Type I configuration. As the loss factor
of the viscoelastic material increases with s, the EPs migrate continuously in the complex frequency
plane. For weakly to moderately damped systems, the EPs remain on opposite sides of the real axis,
and the physical mode labels established at s = 0 are automatically inherited at s = 1 without post-
processing. When the loss factor exceeds a problem-dependent critical threshold, at least one EP may
cross the real frequency axis. In this Type II regime, branch identity continuity still holds—the homo-
topy continuation still returns numerically accurate dispersion solutions—but the automatic inheritance
of physical labels is lost. The two complementary post-hoc diagnostics introduced in Section 4.3—an
extremely sharp imaginary-part crossing and a discernible discrepancy between spectral group velocity
and energy flux velocity—serve as empirical indicators of such a transition and guide the necessary label
exchange.

Discussion and Analysis
The numerical examples presented in Section 3 demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed homotopy
continuation (HC) framework for robust dispersion curve computation across a diverse set of viscoelastic
waveguides. In this section, we discuss the method’s computational efficiency, analyse its scalability, reli-
ability, and robustness, investigate how it behaves when Type I assumptions are violated (high damping),
and compare it with existing approaches in the summary.
4.1
Computational efficiency
A primary innovation of the proposed HC framework is the decoupling of the viscoelastic (non-Hermitian,
s = 1) dispersion problem into two stages: (i) dispersion computation (including mode tracking) for the
elastic (Hermitian, s = 0) waveguide, and (ii) adaptive homotopy continuation from s = 0 to s = 1. This
strategy significantly reduces the overall computational cost compared to directly solving and tracking
modes in the non-Hermitian system. The efficiency gain arises from three aspects: the use of highly
efficient Hermitian eigensolvers in Stage 1, the adaptive sparse homotopy continuation in Stage 2, and
parallel processing employed in both stages.

4.4
Robustness and reliability
The numerical benchmarks in Section 3 reveal a systematic difference in failure modes between the HC
framework and the Dispersion Calculator (DC), rooted in their fundamentally different algorithmic ar-
chitectures. DC employs a mode-by-mode frequency-axis continuation strategy: it first detects modes at
a high phase velocity cutoff, classifies them into mode families (symmetric, antisymmetric, nonsymmetric
Lamb, SH, and Scholte waves), and then traces each mode individually along the frequency axis using
predictor–corrector steps at a prescribed frequency resolution. To prevent indefinite stalling when the
algorithm loses track of the intended branch—which occurs readily in non-Hermitian veering regions
where the Jacobian becomes ill-conditioned—DC employs an internal termination criterion that aborts
the current mode and proceeds to the next one.
This architecture creates two distinct failure pathways that are evident in the numerical examples.
First, if a mode is not detected during the initial high-phase-velocity sweep—due to a cutoff frequency
lying outside the search window or an atypical dispersion slope—it is never traced at all. This explains
the entirely missing mode 4 in UnSym1 (Fig. 6(a)) and the two undetected modes in Sym2 (Fig. 8(a),
black squares). Second, even when a mode is successfully initialized, the predictor–corrector may lose
the branch in a dense veering region and trigger premature termination. In UnSym1, mode 3 jumps
to mode 4 near normalized frequency ωa/cT = 1.4 and terminates; mode 7 jumps to mode 3 near 1.9;
mode 9 jumps to mode 7 near 2.8 (Fig. 6(a)). In UnSym2, mode 11 terminates at normalized frequency
ωa/cT = 3.43 after a significant branch jump, yielding only 30 solution points (Fig. 7(c)). These failures
are structurally irreversible: because DC traces modes sequentially and independently, an error at one
frequency propagates forward indefinitely; there is no mechanism to recover the lost branch or to verify
modal continuity against a reference state.
The HC framework avoids both failure pathways by construction. Mode identification is performed
once and for all in the Hermitian elastic regime (s = 0), where iterative eigensolvers capture the complete
spectrum at each wavenumber and the MAC—supported by adaptive refinement—establishes unambigu-
ous connectivity across the entire frequency band (Section 2.2.2). The subsequent homotopy continuation
propagates these pre-identified modes along independent paths in the material-parameter space (s), which
is decoupled from the complex modal interactions in the observation space (ω). Because the continua-
tion parameter is the material loss s rather than frequency ω, veering regions in the ω-plane—however
dense or closely spaced—do not cause path divergence in the s-plane. The adaptive step-size control
(Section 2.2.3) provides an additional safeguard: when local curvature increases as EPs approach the
real axis, the algorithm reduces ∆s automatically rather than jumping branches.
This structural robustness persists even in the Type II regime. As demonstrated in Section 4.3, HC
continues to produce numerically accurate wavenumbers for UnSym3 (η = 0.05) where DC suffers exacer-
bated mode loss and requires significantly longer computation time (1476 s versus 616 s for HC). Rather
than failing silently or producing unphysical crossings, the framework provides a continuous solution ac-
companied by diagnostic indicators—an extremely sharp imaginary-part crossing and a discernible vg−ve
discrepancy—that signal when label exchange is required. The combination of complete spectrum cap-
ture at the Hermitian stage, decoupled path tracking in the material space, and diagnostic transparency
at the target state distinguishes the HC framework from sequential mode-by-mode approaches that offer

4.5
Impact of damping-induced EP proximity on computational performance
While the theoretical guarantee of branch identity continuity holds for all EP-free homotopy paths, the
computational performance of the HC framework is governed by the curvature of the homotopy path in
the material-parameter space, which in turn reflects the strength of modal interactions in the observation
space.
Two distinct mechanisms contribute to path curvature, and their distinction is essential for
understanding the performance trends in Table 3.
First, the loss factor η controls the total path length: as η increases from 0.003 (Hernando lamina)
to 0.02 (Castaings) to 0.05 (UnSym3), the transformation from elastic to viscoelastic becomes more
substantial, requiring more continuation steps regardless of local spectral features. This is a global effect:
even if the ω-plane EP topology remains Type I with well-separated real parts, a larger η demands finer
discretization of the s-axis.
Second, and more critically, the proximity of ω-plane EPs to the real axis induces local path curvature
in the s-space through the following mechanism. When an EP approaches the real frequency axis, the
two interacting modes exhibit near-coalescence in k at the fixed real ω used for homotopy continuation.
This near-degeneracy renders the dynamic stiffness matrix D(k, s) ill-conditioned with respect to k-
perturbations, which in turn degrades the convergence of the Newton corrector in the predictor–corrector
algorithm. The adaptive step-size control responds by reducing ∆s, but the path remains traversable
because the s-plane Jacobian ∂G/∂y (Eq. (14)) retains full rank as long as no s-plane EP is encountered.
The elastic eigengap ∆λs=0 serves as a practical a priori indicator that correlates with both mech-
anisms. A large elastic eigengap implies that the ω-plane EPs are initially far from the real axis (in
the elastic limit, EPs form a symmetric pair whose imaginary separation is proportional to the eigen-

homotopy paths and allowing larger step sizes. This explains the performance contrast between Sym2

small eigengap anticipates stronger ω-plane modal interactions and thus higher path curvature, despite
both laminates operating within the Type I regime.
It is important to emphasize that this performance variation does not compromise robustness. Branch
identity continuity depends solely on the absence of s-plane EPs on [0, 1], not on ω-plane EP proximity.
The framework guarantees a one-to-one mapping regardless of path curvature; the curvature only affects
the cost of traversal (number of steps, Newton iterations), not the feasibility. For cases approaching the
Type II threshold (e.g., UnSym3 with η = 0.05 ), the vanishing ω-plane eigengap signals that physical
labels may require post-hoc exchange, but the numerical values remain accurate because the s-plane
path itself remains regular.

Conclusion and future work
This paper has demonstrated that the challenges of non-Hermitian dispersion analysis—eigenvalue solu-
tion and mode tracking—can be decoupled by a material homotopy that bridges the Hermitian elastic
limit and the target viscoelastic state. The theoretical foundation for this decoupling is a separation
of concerns: branch identity continuity along the homotopy path (guaranteed by analytic perturbation
theory [43] for all EP-free paths) is independent of physical label validity in the observation space (gov-
erned by the Type I/II EP topology). This separation clarifies why existing methods fail: by attacking
the non-Hermitian problem directly, they couple numerical error (eigenvalue omission) with topological

ambiguity (mode misassignment), making errors irreversible. The proposed framework eliminates this
coupling by confining mode tracking to the Hermitian regime, where the MAC is reliable and adaptive
refinement resolves veering without ambiguity.
Numerical benchmarks across symmetric laminates, unsymmetric laminates with pervasive veering,
and an L-shaped bar confirm that the framework produces physically consistent dispersion curves in
the Type I regime (validated for η ≈0.003), with automatic label inheritance. For the Type II regime
(η = 0.05), the framework continues to yield numerically accurate wavenumbers but requires post-
hoc label exchange, guided by two diagnostic signatures: extremely sharp imaginary-part crossings and
spectral–energetic velocity discrepancies. These diagnostics transform the framework from a ”black-box”
solver into a transparent tool that alerts users when assumptions are violated.
The main conclusions of this work are as follows:
• Separation of numerical and physical continuity.
Branch identity along the homotopy
path—a one-to-one mapping between s = 0 and s = 1—is guaranteed numerically for all EP-
free paths, independent of the target state’s EP topology. Physical label inheritance requires the
additional condition of Type I topology in the observation space. This separation is methodolog-
ically significant: even when Type I is violated, as in UnSym3 (η = 0.05), the numerical solution
remains valid and only requires a posteriori label exchange, not recomputation.
• Decoupling of robustness from computational cost.
In conventional methods, damping

concerns: branch identity is guaranteed regardless of path curvature, while the elastic eigengap

challenging cases such as the L-shaped bar (eigengap 4.29×10−4) without structural modifications.
• Practical applicability and extensibility. For weakly damped CFRP laminates (η ≈0.003,
Hernando lamina), numerical evidence confirms Type I topology across diverse configurations—symmetric,
unsymmetric, and arbitrary cross-section—yielding automatic label inheritance. The framework
is, in principle, extendable to frequency-dependent models (Kelvin–Voigt, Zener) under the same
analyticity condition, provided damping remains within the validated weakly damped regime.
• Type II regimes and diagnostic transparency. When the Type I assumption is violated,

signatures—extremely sharp imaginary-part crossings and spectral–energetic velocity discrepan-

transparent tool that alerts users to assumption violations.

further investigation.
First, the homotopy method assumes that no EP lies on the real s-axis.

A.3
Universal mode tracking challenges
Independent of the numerical strategy employed to obtain eigenvalues, the association of solutions at ad-
jacent frequencies into continuous modal branches—mode tracking—presents its own set of challenges. In
elastic waveguides modeled by the SAFE method, the system matrices are Hermitian, and modal behav-
ior is well-understood. Although eigenvectors are complex-valued due to the presence of the ikK2 term
and numerical error, the Hermitian structure imposes strong constraints: mode crossings are symmetry-
protected, and mode veering occurs in a predictable manner. The Modal Assurance Criterion (MAC)
has proven to be a reliable tool for linking modes across frequencies in such settings.
In viscoelastic waveguides, the loss of Hermiticity removes these structural constraints, making mode
tracking substantially more difficult. Mode veering becomes more complicated to analyze and track,
as the rapid exchange of eigenvector characteristics can occur over narrower frequency intervals. Mode
degeneracy in non-Hermitian systems can manifest as exceptional points, where eigenvalues coalesce and
eigenvectors become parallel—a phenomenon without analogue in Hermitian systems. These exceptional
points introduce topological complexity that cannot be resolved by simple frequency refinement alone.
Existing mode tracking strategies for non-Hermitian systems typically fall into two categories, each
with significant limitations.
The first relies on similarity measures such as the MAC. However, the
reliability of MAC degrades considerably in non-Hermitian systems due to the loss of orthogonality and
the presence of near-coalescing modes, where eigenvectors become nearly parallel and the inner product
loses its discriminative power. The second employs physical heuristics—for example, sorting modes by
the real part of the wavenumber or by group velocity—but these rules fail in regions of mode veering or
crossing, where modal identities can exchange without clear signatures in the sorted order. As a result,
mode tracking in the viscoelastic regime often demands substantial manual supervision, especially when
damping is significant or the frequency range includes complex modal interactions.
Crucially, mode tracking difficulties are compounded by the underlying eigenvalue solution challenges.
If the eigenvalue solver fails to capture all physical modes at a given frequency—a common risk in non-
Hermitian problems with poorly chosen shift parameters—then no subsequent tracking algorithm can
recover the missing branches. Errors in eigenvalue computation propagate irreversibly into mode tracking,
and once modes are misassigned, they cannot be corrected by post-processing alone.
A.4
Limitations of existing non-Hermitian dispersion methods
The preceding review reveals that existing methods, despite their utility in specific contexts, share a
common structural limitation: they inextricably couple the challenges of non-Hermitian eigenvalue solu-
tion with those of mode tracking. For small-scale models, the combination of a dense direct eigensolver
(e.g., eig) and the MAC can yield dispersion curves with reasonable effort, but this approach scales
poorly to large-scale finite element models and, more fundamentally, provides no theoretical guarantee
that modal identities are preserved from the lossless to the lossy regime. When damping is introduced
and the system becomes non-Hermitian, MAC reliability degrades and heuristic mode sorting rules often

fail, leaving no robust mechanism for preserving modal identity across the full viscoelastic frequency
band.
Each methodological class further suffers from its own intrinsic limitations. Iterative eigensolvers,
whether using shift-invert iterations or contour integrals, require heuristic exploration of the complex
plane, risking omission of relevant modes. Complex root-searching methods demand careful initializa-
tion and become unreliable in densely populated spectra with closely spaced branches. Frequency-axis
continuation methods are fragile near exceptional points, where the Jacobian becomes singular, and
may produce spurious mode jumps in veering regions. Across all of these approaches, the inability to
guarantee correct modal connectivity from the elastic to the viscoelastic regime remains an unresolved
difficulty.

Type I and Type II EP topology. The configuration of an EP pair relative to the real frequency axis
determines the observable crossing behaviour along the real frequency axis [44]. In a Type I configuration,
the two EPs lie on opposite sides of the real axis. Scanning the real frequency axis then yields real-part
veering (an avoided crossing with a positive gap) accompanied by imaginary-part crossing. The order
of the real parts does not change across the interaction region, and the physical mode shapes associated
with each branch remain consistent. In a Type II configuration, both EPs lie on the same side of the real
axis. The observable behaviour reverses: the real parts cross while the imaginary parts veer. To maintain
physical continuity in this case, the mode labels must be exchanged at the crossing point. In the elastic
limit (s = 0), EPs form a complex-conjugate pair symmetric about the real axis, corresponding to a Type
I topology. As material damping increases, the EPs migrate continuously in the complex plane; whether a
transition to Type II occurs depends on the damping magnitude relative to a problem-dependent critical
threshold. This classification is fundamental to the mode identity guarantee developed in Section 2.2.4.
Dispersion curves.
Dispersion curves are the restriction of the dispersion relation to real-valued
frequency (or wavenumber), typically expressed as k(ω) with ω ∈R. These curves are projections of the
underlying Riemann surface and do not uniquely encode branch identity.
Modes. A mode is defined as a solution pair (k(ω), q(ω)) satisfying D(k, ω)q = 0, together with the
corresponding left eigenvector qL satisfying q†

associated with a single branch. However, near exceptional points, eigenvalues and eigenvectors coalesce,
and this one-to-one correspondence breaks down.
Continuity of modes. A central concept is that a mode is not merely a pointwise solution, but a
continuous (analytic) trajectory on the Riemann surface:

Spectral–energetic distinction. In Hermitian systems, the spectral group velocity vg and the energy
flux velocity ve coincide, owing to the orthogonality of eigenmodes and the consistency of the associ-

regions—provides a physically grounded indicator that the system is approaching an EP-dominated
regime.
In summary, dispersion analysis in non-Hermitian SAFE systems relies critically on distinguishing
between analytic structures defined on Riemann surfaces and physically motivated mode tracking. A
mode is fundamentally a continuous trajectory on the Riemann surface, and mode tracking aims to
reconstruct such continuity from discrete solutions, upon which physical mode labels are assigned.
Accurate dispersion curves therefore require both reliable discrete eigenpair solutions and consistent
mode tracking. When these conditions are satisfied, the resulting curves are spectrally consistent, mean-
ing they faithfully represent the underlying analytic structure and correctly capture features such as
branch topology, modal interactions, and exceptional points. In contrast, inaccuracies in either eigen-
value computation or mode tracking may lead to incorrect branch connectivity, spurious crossings, or
misleading curvature, thereby obscuring the true physical spectral structure. Consequently, accurate
dispersion curves are essential for obtaining physically meaningful interpretations of wave propagation
in non-Hermitian systems.
