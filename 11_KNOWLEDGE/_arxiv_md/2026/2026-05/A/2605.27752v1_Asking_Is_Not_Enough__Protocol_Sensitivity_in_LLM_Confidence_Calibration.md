Asking Is Not Enough:
Protocol Sensitivity in LLM Confidence Calibration
Hankyeol Kim and Pilsung Kang*
Seoul National University
{hankyeol,pilsung_kang}@snu.ac.kr
*Corresponding author
Abstract
LLM confidence calibration is often evaluated
by comparing two signals: token-probability
scores and verbalized confidence. These sig-
nals are sometimes treated as direct readouts
of model uncertainty, but their comparison de-
pends on measurement choices that are rarely
made explicit. In the main analysis, we hold
the verbalized-confidence elicitation fixed: a
single prompt template, probability scale, and
output format. We then vary the measurement
axes that define the verbalized-vs-token com-
parison: which answer string receives the token-
probability score, how that score is read from
the answer tokens, and under which condition-
ing context it is measured. We evaluate this
design on four QA benchmarks across three
open 7–8B base/Instruct model families, with
larger Qwen2.5 variants as same-family robust-
ness checks. The resulting comparison is sen-
sitive to these choices: conditioning context
changes the sign or magnitude of the ECE gap
across settings, token readout produces smaller
but still sign-moving changes, and changing
the ECE estimator has little effect. Under the
default generated-answer, bare-context proto-
col, Instruct settings are close to parity rather
than showing a large calibration gain for ver-
balized confidence.
In a separate supplied-
answer analysis, surface-plausible wrong an-
swers receive nearly the same confidence as
supplied gold answers, suggesting that verbal-
ized confidence also reflects answer plausibility
and provenance rather than correctness alone.
We argue that both confidence signals should
be treated as protocol-dependent behavioral
measurements, and provide a reporting check-
list covering elicitation provenance, scored an-
swer, token-probability readout, and condition-
ing context.

Introduction
Calibration of language model confidence under-
pins safe deployment in high-stakes settings, from
medical question answering to coding agents. Re-
cent work therefore evaluates not only whether
a model answers correctly, but also whether its
stated confidence is meaningful. Two confidence
signals are especially common. One is read from
the model’s token probabilities; the other is verbal-
ized confidence, where the model is asked to report
a probability in text (Tian et al., 2023; Kadavath
et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024;
Dai, 2026). Verbalized confidence is attractive be-
cause it is easy to elicit from closed or chat-only
systems, but it also turns confidence estimation
into an interaction protocol: the answer may be
self-generated or supplied, the confidence may be
requested in different formats, and the token proba-
bility used for comparison must be read from some
context and some position.
Prior work has shown that verbalized confidence
is sensitive to how it is elicited (Xiong et al.,
2024; Yang et al., 2024; Dai, 2026). We study
a complementary source of sensitivity. Even when
the prompt template is held fixed, the compari-
son between a verbalized self-report and a token-
probability score can change with token-side and
answer-provenance choices that are usually left im-
plicit. Such choices matter because they define the
quantity being measured: whether the confidence
is attached to the model’s own answer or a supplied
answer, whether the token probability is read under
the bare query or after a verbalized prefix, and how
answer-token probabilities are read or aggregated.
If these choices are not reported, a scalar calibra-
tion improvement can be mistaken for a property
of the model or alignment procedure rather than a
property of the measurement protocol.
We examine this issue through a controlled
case study of the self-report/token-probability com-
parison. Whenever measuring verbalized confi-
dence, our main analysis uses the same continu-
ous 0.0–1.0 prompt template and evaluates three
open base/Instruct families (Llama-3-8B, Mistral-

The comparison is not stable under these choices.
Token readout and conditioning context each
change the sign or magnitude of the observed
gap across many model–dataset settings, with con-
text shifts comparable to changing the evaluation
dataset; by contrast, changing the ECE estimator
has little effect in our experiments. The results
also do not support a large, protocol-independent
calibration gain for verbalized confidence: under
the specified generated-answer, bare-context pro-
tocol, most Instruct settings sit near parity rather
than showing a large ECE reduction. Finally, when
the model is asked to assign confidence to a sup-
plied answer, the reported number distinguishes
clearly mismatched answers from plausible ones
but barely separates plausible wrong answers from
correct ones. This suggests that verbalized confi-
dence can track answer plausibility and provenance
rather than correctness alone.
These findings motivate a more conservative
interpretation of LLM confidence measurements.
Neither verbalized confidence nor token probability
should be treated as a direct readout of model un-
certainty; both are behavioral measurements whose
interpretation depends on how the answer and prob-
ability are elicited, conditioned, and scored. Claims
that alignment improves self-reported uncertainty,
or that verbalized confidence should be preferred to
token probabilities, therefore require the measure-
ment protocol to be specified first: which answer
the confidence attaches to, under which context
the token probability is measured, and how the
token-probability score is read from the answer.
Because different protocol choices define different
measured quantities, we interpret our comparisons
as sensitivity analyses rather than as a causal de-
composition.
The contributions of this paper are as follows:
• Protocol sensitivity with fixed verbalized-
confidence elicitation.
We use the same
prompt template, confidence scale, and out-
put format whenever measuring verbalized
confidence, and vary the answer and token-
probability choices:
answer provenance,
token-probability readout, conditioning con-
text, and estimator choice. Token readout and
conditioning context materially change the ob-
served ECE gap, whereas changing the ECE
estimator has little effect; the context effect is
comparable to changing the model family or
dataset (§4.1).
• Answer provenance and plausibility. We
compare verbalized confidence on self-
generated answers with confidence elicited on
supplied correct answers and supplied plau-
sible wrong answers. The reported number
barely separates a plausible wrong answer
from a correct one, even though it drops for
clearly mismatched answers, showing that ver-
balized confidence is not simply a correctness
estimate (§4.2).
• Reporting checklist and robustness. We
distill the results into a reporting checklist
for confidence-signal comparisons: elicitation
provenance, scored answer, token-probability
readout, and conditioning context (§4.3). The
conditioning-context sensitivity persists under
an integer 0–100 prompt scale, four non-ECE
metrics, and larger-model robustness checks
within the Qwen2.5 family, while the finding
that estimator choice has little effect is stable
to bin count (Appendices I, J, K, N, L).

Related Work
Verbalized confidence.
LLM confidence is com-
monly evaluated either from token probabilities
or from confidence values stated in text. Earlier
work studies natural-language uncertainty more
broadly, including calibrated uncertainty expres-
sions in words (Lin et al., 2022a) and linguistic cal-
ibration in dialogue (Mielke et al., 2022). Tian et al.
(2023) ask RLHF-tuned chat models to output con-
tinuous probabilities and compare them with token-
probability scores, while Kadavath et al. (2022)
elicit a “P(True)” judgment on a supplied candi-
date answer. Later work shows that verbalized con-
fidence is sensitive to how it is elicited: Xiong et al.
(2024) find systematic overconfidence and mixed
benefits from prompting or aggregation strategies,
Yang et al. (2024) compare many prompt variants
and find strong model and dataset dependence, and
Dai (2026) shows that the response scale can shape

the reported numbers. This literature establishes
verbalized confidence as an elicited behavior rather
than a context-free uncertainty readout. We ask
a complementary question: when the verbalized-
confidence measurement uses a common format,
probability scale, and output convention, is its com-
parison with token-probability scores stable un-
der choices about the answer being scored and the
token-probability readout?
Confidence signals and calibration metrics.
To-
ken probabilities are also not protocol-free: an
answer-level score requires choosing the condi-
tioning context, the token span or position, and
the correctness label.
Prior NLP work studies
whether pretrained transformer probabilities are
calibrated across tasks and distribution shifts (De-
sai and Durrett, 2020). Other work proposes alter-
native confidence or uncertainty signals, including
Rank-Calibration (Huang et al., 2024), sample con-
sistency (Lyu et al., 2024), and representation sta-
bility (Khanmohammadi et al., 2025). A separate
calibration-estimation literature shows that ECE it-
self depends on binning and estimator choices (Guo
et al., 2017; Nixon et al., 2019; Kumar et al., 2019;
Roelofs et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020; Błasiok and
Nakkiran, 2024; Gupta and Ramdas, 2021). This
motivates checking whether a confidence compar-
ison is robust to reasonable estimator or metric
choices, while keeping those choices distinct from
protocol choices about which token probability is
read.
Protocol specification.
Prompt-format sensitiv-
ity is a known issue in few-shot LM evaluation and
motivates contextual calibration (Zhao et al., 2021).
Surveys of LLM confidence estimation identify
prompt sensitivity, metric choice, and confidence-
signal choice as recurring limitations (Geng et al.,
2024), and empirical work shows that verbalized
confidence can change with elicitation and scale
design (Xiong et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Dai,
2026). We focus on protocol choices that define the
comparison itself: elicitation provenance, scored
answer, token position, and conditioning context.
A confidence value may refer to a self-generated
answer or to a supplied candidate answer; a token-
probability score may be read under the bare query
or after the prompt prefix used to elicit verbal-
ized confidence; and different token positions can
correspond to different measured quantities. Our
contribution is a sensitivity analysis and reporting
checklist for these choices, so that calibration dif-
ferences are interpreted as protocol-dependent mea-
surements rather than direct properties of a model
or alignment procedure.

Setup and Method
3.1
Models, Data, and Confidence Measures
We evaluate three open base/Instruct pairs: Llama-
3-8B (Meta AI, 2024), Mistral-7B-v0.3 (Jiang
et al., 2023), and Qwen2.5-7B (Qwen Team, 2024),
with their Instruct variants.
We use four QA
benchmarks: TriviaQA (Joshi et al., 2017) (1k
subset), SciQ (Welbl et al., 2017) (1k), Truth-
fulQA (Lin et al., 2022b) (817 full), and four
MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021) subjects (high-
school physics, philosophy, high-school govern-
ment and politics, and miscellaneous; 1,155 ex-
amples, per-subject sizes capped by availability).
The verbalized-confidence measurement uses one
continuous 0.0–1.0 probability template through-
out, applied through each Instruct model’s chat
template and as raw text for base models.
We vary answer-side and token-probability
choices to probe what the confidence measures
track, and to quantify how protocol choices affect
the verbalized-vs-token comparison:
• Answer provenance for verbalized confi-
dence.
Verbalized confidence is typically
elicited by asking the model to answer and
then report confidence in that answer. If this
measure were provenance-invariant, correct
self-generated answers and supplied gold an-
swers should receive similar confidence even
when they are not identical strings; exact
string matches are analyzed separately as a
matched-string control. Supplied wrong an-
swers should receive lower confidence. We
therefore compare the model’s own answer
with three prefilled-answer conditions, where
the answer string is inserted into the confi-
dence prompt and the model generates only
the confidence value: the gold answer, a same-
question plausible wrong answer generated
by another model, and an off-type wrong an-
swer taken from a different question. This
separates correctness sensitivity from answer
source and plausibility.
• Scored answer for token probability. The
same answer-identity issue also affects token-
probability scoring: the model-generated an-
swer and the dataset’s gold reference can be

3.2
Protocol Choices and Metrics
For each measurement configuration X for which
both confidence signals are defined, we report the
signed calibration gap
gX = ECE(cverb,X, y) −ECE(ctok,X, y),
where cverb,X is the parsed verbalized confidence
under configuration X, ctok,X is the corresponding
token-probability score, y is the binary correctness
label, and ECE is computed using the estimator
specified by X. In the main ECE contrasts, y is
held fixed to the correctness of the model-generated
answer unless stated otherwise. Negative values
mean verbalized confidence has lower ECE. For the
equal-width binned estimator, ECE averages the
absolute gap between mean confidence and empiri-
cal accuracy within confidence bins, weighted by
the number of examples in each bin. The main con-
figuration, gA, is the generated-answer, bare-query,
T1, 10-bin protocol.
The A–D notation is used for the context and
estimator variants: A, bare-query token probability
with 10-bin ECE; B, token probability measured
after the verbalized prompt prefix with 10-bin ECE;
C, bare-query token probability with the bin-free
estimator mean (KDE-ECE (Zhang et al., 2020),
KS-Cal (Gupta and Ramdas, 2021), and Smoothed
ECE (Błasiok and Nakkiran, 2024)); and D, both
changes applied. We report each gX with a 95%
bootstrap CI and report pairwise differences such
as gA −gB as descriptive changes in the observed
gap. Other axes in Table 1, including token readout,
scored answer, and supplied-answer provenance,
are reported separately as targeted contrasts rather
than as part of the A–D grid.
Configuration B measures token probability un-
der the same prefix used for verbalized-confidence
elicitation. This prefix asks the model to complete a
guess-and-probability format, so the resulting score
is an operationally different conditional probabil-
ity from the one read under the bare-query prompt
in A. Appendix C shows that this context shift is
not driven by the token-probability readout: the
shift has the same sign under both the answer-span
score T1 and the first-token score T2 on all Instruct
settings (12/12).
We include two auxiliary diagnostics to interpret
the main protocol effects. For the conditioning-
context change, we report first-step next-token KL,
final-layer CKA distance (Kornblith et al., 2019),
and answer-change rate between the bare and
verbalized-prefixed contexts (Appendix B). For ver-
balized outputs, we measure concentration near the
{0.0, 0.1, . . . , 1.0} grid to assess whether round-
number clustering could account for estimator-
dependent patterns.
All ECEs, gaps, and pairwise differences are
reported with non-parametric bootstrap 95% confi-
dence intervals using B = 1000 resamples. Cross-
setting aggregates use macro means with macro-
bootstrap intervals. Appendix diagnostic tables use
B = 200 resamples and are presented as descrip-
tive robustness checks.

4.1

results as macro means over twelve model-dataset
settings per variant group; the underlying per-
setting values are reported in Appendix A. We
begin with the default protocol: verbalized con-
fidence from the continuous probability template,

reductions reported by Tian et al. (2023) for RLHF-
tuned chat models, our open Instruct settings are
much closer to parity under the default protocol.

4.3
Reporting Implications
A verbalized-vs-token confidence comparison is
not fully interpretable without its measurement con-
figuration. Our results support reporting four mea-
surement axes:
C1 Elicitation provenance: whether verbalized
confidence is elicited for the model’s self-
generated answer or, in a supplied-answer
diagnostic, for an answer supplied in the
prompt.
C2 Scored answer: which answer string receives
the token-probability score, such as the model-
generated answer or a teacher-forced gold ref-
erence.
C3 Token-probability readout: for example, the
answer-span geometric mean (T1) versus the
first answer-token probability (T2).
C4 Conditioning context: bare query versus
the verbalized prompt-template prefix for the
token-probability measurement.
The ECE estimator should also be specified, but
in our experiments it is not a material source of
sensitivity: replacing 10-bin ECE with the bin-free
estimator mean leaves the main conclusions un-
changed. Separately from these measurement axes,
ordinary QA evaluation choices such as answer ex-
traction and correctness scoring should be reported
as well.
This conclusion is stable across the robustness
checks: the conditioning-context effect remains
larger than the estimator effect under an integer
0–100 prompt scale, non-ECE metrics, and larger
Qwen2.5 Instruct checks (Appendices I, L, J, K,
and N).
5
Conclusion
We show that two commonly used confidence mea-
sures, verbalized confidence and token-probability
confidence, depend on measurement configuration
and answer provenance. Rather than treating either
scalar as a fixed property of a model-question pair,
we evaluate how calibration comparisons change
when the scored answer, token-probability read-
out, conditioning context, estimator, and elicitation
provenance are varied. The sign and magnitude
of the calibration gap change with the scored an-
swer, token-probability readout, conditioning con-
text, and elicitation provenance. In our main analy-
sis, the conditioning-context choice shifts the gap
more than the evaluation dataset does and within
the same order as model family or base/Instruct
variant (§4.1), while estimator choice is small.
Taken together, the results give a cautionary read-
ing of scalar confidence claims. Verbalized con-
fidence and token-probability scores can both be
useful calibration signals, but neither should be
treated as a measure of model reliability or self-
knowledge independent of how it is elicited, scored,
and conditioned. Comparisons between the two are
therefore meaningful only when their measurement
configuration is made explicit.

Limitations
Our claims concern how confidence comparisons
vary with measurement configuration and answer
provenance, not a universal ordering of verbalized
confidence and token probability.
Model and scale coverage.
The main experi-
ments use three open 7–8B base/Instruct families.
This choice makes the conditioning-context and
token-probability analyses fully reproducible, but
it does not cover closed models or the full range of
larger chat models. Same-family Qwen2.5 checks
at 14B, 32B, and 72B show the same qualitative
pattern (Appendices J, K, N), but these are robust-
ness checks rather than a systematic scale study.
Descriptive protocol comparisons.
The proto-
col configurations differ in operational choices:
which answer receives the token-probability score,
how that score is read from the answer, which
conditioning context is used, and which calibra-
tion estimator is applied.
Pairwise differences
such as gA −gB are therefore sensitivity measure-
ments, not estimates of how much each choice
causally contributes to the observed gap. In partic-
ular, the verbalized-prefix configuration measures
token probability after the prompt prefix used to
elicit verbalized confidence and is best read as a
conditioning-context probe rather than as a norma-
tive calibration target.
Parseability.
Base models sometimes fail to fol-
low the verbalized-confidence format, most visi-
bly Llama-3-8B and Mistral-7B-v0.3 base, whose
parse rates are low in some settings (Appendix E).
In those cases, verbalized-side metrics are com-
puted on a smaller parseable subset. Our main
claims are therefore stated on the Instruct settings,
where parse rates are at least 0.966; base-model
results are stress tests of the measurement pipeline
rather than primary evidence about verbalized cali-
bration quality.
Prompt and provenance scope.
For the main
verbalized-confidence measurements, we use a con-
tinuous 0.0–1.0 probability format. An integer
0–100 check on the Instruct settings leaves the
qualitative protocol-sensitivity pattern unchanged
(Appendix I), but other elicitation formats and a
systematic prompt-scale study remain outside our
scope. The supplied-answer analysis is likewise
bounded: it uses the three 7–8B Instruct mod-
els with a Qwen2.5-14B robustness check, and
its wrong answers mix cross-model plausible er-
rors with off-type fallback answers when no cross-
model wrong answer is available.
Answer, decoding, and scoring conventions.
The default token-probability score is assigned to
the model-generated answer and read out as the
answer-span geometric mean (T1). We report gold-
reference and first-token (T2) checks separately
because they change the measured quantity. All
main generations use greedy decoding, and correct-
ness is assigned by one lenient string-matching rule
across datasets. Sampling-based decoding, alter-
native answer extraction, or stricter task-specific
scoring may produce different absolute calibration
values. Because all evaluations are QA-style tasks,
the results may not transfer directly to long-form
generation, multi-turn interaction, code generation,
or decision-making settings where the answer ob-
ject and correctness label are less discrete.
Ethical Considerations
This paper is a measurement study of confidence
signals in existing open language models on public
QA benchmarks. We do not train or deploy models,
collect user data, or run experiments with human
participants or crowdworkers. The experiments use
TriviaQA, SciQ, TruthfulQA, and MMLU only as
offline evaluation sets, and the models and datasets
are used through their released artifacts and asso-
ciated licenses or terms. Any released artifacts
should therefore be interpreted as research arti-
facts for reproducing aggregate measurements; they
should not be treated as a new benchmark of human
knowledge or as a source of deployable confidence
thresholds.
A central ethical risk is misinterpretation. Con-
fidence scores are often used to support absten-
tion, escalation, or other safety-relevant decisions.
Our results show that verbalized confidence and
token-probability confidence can change substan-
tially with answer provenance, scored answer, to-
ken readout, and conditioning context. Reporting
only a favorable scalar calibration number could
encourage over-reliance on a model in high-stakes
settings such as medical, legal, educational, or
safety-critical QA. We therefore present the work
as a protocol-sensitivity analysis and include a re-
porting checklist rather than recommending either
signal as a standalone reliability measure. Down-
stream use should validate the full measurement
protocol in the target domain and should not substi-

tute these confidence signals for expert review or
operational risk assessment.
The public benchmarks used here may contain
culturally specific coverage, named entities, out-
dated facts, and artifacts of their original collection
processes. We do not collect new user data, and we
do not release item-level personal information. Be-
cause public QA benchmarks and model-generated
wrong answers may contain named entities or po-
tentially sensitive or offensive content, we use them
only for controlled offline evaluation and report ag-

examples. Our evaluation is English QA-style and
does not assess whether calibration behavior dif-
fers across demographic groups, languages, or com-
munities. The supplied-answer analysis also con-
structs plausible wrong answers from other model
outputs; these outputs may include false statements
and should be used only for controlled evaluation.
We avoid making claims about fairness, truthful-
ness, or real-world safety beyond the tested setting.
The experiments require non-trivial inference
compute, including larger Qwen2.5 robustness
checks, although no model training or fine-tuning
is performed. We mitigate avoidable environmental
cost by reusing cached model outputs for most anal-
yses, reporting implementation details needed for
selective reproduction, and separating small-scale
robustness checks from claims that would require
extensive scaling studies.

Dataset-Specific Correctness Scoring
All four datasets are scored by the single lenient
normalized string-match rule of §3 (lowercase; non-
alphanumeric →space; whitespace collapsed; cor-

the other). Table 21 gives the split, example count,
answer format, gold object, and parse-failure han-
dling per dataset. SciQ and MMLU are multiple-
choice sources but are scored as free-form short
text against the correct option string, not as letter
options, so the same rule and the same answer-span
extraction apply throughout. The MMLU subset
is four subjects with per-subject counts capped by
dataset availability (high-school physics 151, phi-
losophy 311, high-school government and politics
193, miscellaneous 500; 1,155 total), not a uniform
500 per subject. Verbalized rows with no parseable
confidence are excluded from that cell’s verbalized-

valid sample (the bare/token side on the full sam-
ple, the verbalized side on its parseable subset, not
intersected by qid), which is immaterial for the
high-parse-rate Instruct cells and is checked for
base models by the qid-matched analysis of Ap-

Scorer-sensitivity check.
The rule above is le-
nient: it credits a normalized exact match or two-
way substring containment. Correctness scoring
is itself a protocol choice, so we re-derive ev-
ery correct label under the stricter normalized-
exact-match-only rule (dropping the substring
branch) and recompute the verbalized-vs-token
gaps from the stored predictions (Table 22; no
model reload). The stricter scorer changes abso-
lute magnitudes substantially, but the qualitative
ordering is unchanged: the conditioning-context
shift remains much larger than the estimator shift.
The headline numbers in the main text are reported
under the lenient rule; a systematic study of scorer
design is left to future work.
