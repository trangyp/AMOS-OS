A Polynomial Kernel for Vertex Deletion to the Scattered
Class of Proper Interval Graph and Trees
Ashwin Jacob∗1, Arpit Kumar†2, and Diptapriyo Majumdar‡2
1National Institute of Technology Calicut, India

Abstract
Vertex deletion to hereditary graph class is well-studied in parameterized complexity.
Vertex deletion to the scattered graph classes have gained attention in recent years. In
this paper, we consider (Proper-Interval, Tree)-Graph Vertex Deletion, the
input to which is an undirected graph G = (V, E) and an integer k. The goal is to
pick a set X ⊆V (G) of at most k vertices such that G −X is a simple graph and
every connected component of G −X is a proper interval graph or a tree.
When
parameterized by the solution size k, (Proper-Interval, Tree)-Graph Vertex
Deletion has been proved to be fixed-parameter tractable by Jacob et al. [JCSS-2023,
FCT-2021]. In this paper, we consider this problem from the perspective of polynomial
kernelization. We provide a first nontrivial polynomial kernel for (Proper-Interval,
Tree)-Graph Vertex Deletion, with O(k33) vertices.

Introduction
A graph class Π is hereditary if Π is closed under induced subgraphs. Graph modification
problems are among the central area in the field of algorithms. Graph modification problems
can be of various types. In the past 2-3 decades, vertex deletion problems have been exten-
sively studied from the perspective of parameterized complexity and kernelization. Such
problems include Vertex Cover, Feedback Vertex Set, Odd Cycle Transver-
sal, Chordal Vertex Deletion Set, Interval Vertex Deletion Set etc. Several
of these computational problems are NP-hard in general graphs but are polynomial-time
solvable in special graph classes. For instance, both Vertex Cover and Feedback Ver-
tex Set are NP-hard in general graphs but is polynomial-time solvable in chordal graphs
[12, 13, 15], AT-free graphs [23, 24], bounded treewidth graphs [3], bounded mim-width
graphs [2, 20] permutation graphs [26] etc. In addition, Vertex Cover is polynomial-
time solvable in bipartite graphs [8, 22, 28] as well. Consider the following situation when
the input graph G has multiple connected components and each such component belongs
to one of these special graph classes. Such graph classes are called scattered graph classes.
If a vertex deletion problem L is polynomial-time solvable in each such graph class, then
L becomes polynomial-time solvable in G. This makes the area vertex deletion problems to
scattered graph classes interesting to explore.

The study of scattered classes were initiated by Ganian et al. [14] from the perspective
of parameterized complexity. In their work, they explored backdoors to scattered classes
of CSP instances. Subsequently, Jacob et al. [16, 17] built on the works by Ganian et al.
[14] to initiate a study on vertex deletion to scattered graph classes from the perspective
of parameterized complexity. They considered (G1, . . . , Gd)-Vertex Deletion where the
input instance is (G, k) and the objective is to delete a set S of at most k vertices such
that each connected component of G −S is in Gi for some i ∈[d].
The solution size
k is a natural parameter choice. After that, (G1, G2)-Vertex Deletion gained special
attention where the graph class pair (G1, G2) satisfy some special properties. Jacob et al.
[17] provided single-exponential time FPT algorithms when (G1, G2) is (Clique, Trees),
(Split, Bipartite), (Clique, Cactus), (Claw-Free, Triangle-Free), (Interval,
Tree), (Proper-Interval, Tree) etc.
However, polynomial kernelization for vertex deletion to scattered graph classes re-
mained unexplored until recently. Jacob et al. [19] initiated this direction by giving the
first nontrivial kernel for the (Clique, Tree)-Vertex Deletion problem with O(k5)
vertices. This result was later improved to O(k4) by Tsur [30] and further to O(k2) by
Kumabe [25].
Our Problem and Result.
To the best of our knowledge, there have been very limited
exploration to vertex deletion to graph classes from the perspective of polynomial kerneliza-
tion that is one of the central subfields in parameterized complexity. Apart from (Clique,
Tree)-Vertex Deletion, the other polynomial kernelization results are only some folk-
lore results from [16, 17] and these folklore results are very restrictive and do not cover
any infinite families of graphs. Additionally, in the (Clique, Tree)-Vertex Deletion
problem, one of the graph classes (cluster graph case) has finite forbidden families. In this
paper, we consider (Proper-Interval, Tree)-Graph Vertex Deletion in which none
of the two graph classes can be characterized by finite forbidden families. For the simplicity
of presentation, if every connected component is a proper interval graph or a tree, we call
it a (prop-int, tree)-graph. The input to our problem is an undirected simple graph G and
an integer k, and we ask if there is a set X of at most k vertices such that G −X is a
simple (prop-int, tree)-graph. Nevertheless, our reduction rules can create some parallel
edges. Therefore, we consider the more general formulation of the problem as follows.

Preliminaries and Notations
Sets and Graph Theory.
We use standard graph theoretic terminologies from the book
of Diestel [6]. Throughout the paper, we refer to only undirected graphs. If every connected
component of a simple undirected graph G is a proper interval graph or a tree, then we
say that G is a (prop-int, tree)-graph. Throughout the paper, we use “a simple (prop-int,
tree)-graph” for the sake of simplicity most of the time. A path in a graph is a sequence of
distinct vertices (v1, v2, . . . , vℓ) such that for all 1 ≤i ≤ℓ−1, vivi+1 is an edge. A cycle in
a graph is a sequence of vertices (v1, v2, . . . , vℓ, v1) such that ℓ≥2, v1, . . . , vℓare distinct,
for every 1 ≤i ≤ℓ−1, vivi+1 is an edge and vℓv1 is an edge. A path P = (v1, v2, . . . , vℓ) is
a degree-2-path in a graph if every internal vertex has degree exactly 2. A degree-2-path is
a degree-2-tail in G if dG(v1) > 2 and dG(vℓ) = 1. A degree-2-path is a degree-2-overbridge
in G if dG(v1), dG(vℓ) > 2. Given an undirected graph G and v ∈V (G), we define attach
a degree-2-tail of length ℓat v as follows: create new vertices u1, u2, . . . , uℓand add edges
vu1 and uiui+1 for every 1 ≤i ≤ℓ−1.
Definition 1 (v-flower). For a graph G and a vertex v ∈V (G), a v-flower is a family of ℓ
cycles C1, C2, . . . , Cℓin G such that each cycle contains v, and any two distinct cycles Ci
and Cj intersect only at v. We refer to the Ci’s as the petals, and to v as the core. The
number of cycles ℓis the order of the v-flower.
We use the following proposition on the v-flower structure.
Proposition 1 ([4], Lemma 9.6). Given a graph G, a vertex v ∈V (G), and an integer k,
there is a polynomial-time algorithm that either finds a v-flower of order k + 1 or computes
a set Z ⊆V (G) \ {v} of size at most 2k that intersects every cycle of G passing through v.
We use v-flower only for cycles. For our kernelization algorithm, we also use a more
generic concept of sunflower: a family {S1, . . . , Sℓ} with core Y such that Si ∩Sj = Y for
all i ̸= j. The sets Si \ Y are petals, and we require none of them to be empty.
Proposition 2 (Lemma 3.2 of [11], Lemma 3.3 of [9]). Let F be a family of sets of cardinal-
ity at most d over a universe U and k be a positive integer. Then there is an O(|F|(k+|F|))
time algorithm that finds a non-empty set F′ ⊆F such that
(i) For every Z ⊆U of size at most k, Z is a minimal hitting set of F if and only if Z
is a minimal hitting set of F′; and
(ii) |F′| ≤d!(k + 1)d.

Definition 2 (q-expansion). Let q be a positive integer and G be a bipartite graph with
vertex bipartition (A, B). For bA ⊆A and bB ⊆B, a set M ⊆E(G) of edges is called a
q-expansion of bA into bB if
(i) every vertex of bA is incident to exactly q edges in M; and
(ii) exactly q| bA| vertices of B are incident to the edges in M.
The vertices of bA and bB that are the endpoints of the edges of M are saturated by M.
We use the concept of q-expansion, along with its algorithmic tools. It is important to
note that by definition of a q-expansion M of bA into bB, every vertex of bA is saturated by
M, and | bB| ≥q| bA|. However, a vertex of bB need not be saturated by M.
Proposition 3 (q-Expansion Lemma [4, 29]). Let q be a positive integer and G be a bipartite
graph with vertex bipartition (A, B) such that |B| ≥q|A|, and there is no isolated vertex in
B. Then there exist non-empty sets bA ⊆A and bB ⊆B such that
(i) There is a q-expansion M of bA into bB in G.
(ii) N( bB) ⊆bA.
Furthermore, the sets bA, bB and the q-expansion M can be computed in time O(mn1.5).
Recently, Fomin et al. [10] designed the following generalization of the q-Expansion
Lemma.
Proposition 4 (New q-Expansion Lemma [1, 10, 18]). Let q be a positive integer and G
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition (A, B). Then, there exist non-empty sets bA ⊆A
and bB ⊆B such that
(i) There is a q-expansion M of bA into bB in G.
(ii) N( bB) ⊆bA.
(iii) |B \ bB| ≤q|A \ bA|.
Furthermore, the sets bA, bB and the q-expansion M can be computed in polynomial-time.
Observe that Proposition 4 omits two conditions required by Proposition 3: B has no
isolated vertex and |B| ≥q|A|. In particular, if |B| > q|A|, then it must be that | bB| > q| bA|
and bB contains some vertex not saturated by the q-expansion M.
Forbidden Subgraph Characterization.
It is well-known due to [17] that a simple
undirected graph is (prop-int, tree)-graph if and only if it does not contain any net, tent,
and holes as induced subgraph, and it has no component that contains a claw and a triangle.
We call these graph structures obstructions for (prop-int, tree)-graph. If the vertices of a
claw J and the vertices of a triangle T has nonempty intersection, then we say that (J, T)
is a pairwise-intersecting (claw, triangle)-pair.

Proper Interval Graphs.
An interval graph is an intersection graph of intervals on the
real line. The set of intervals corresponding to the vertices of an interval graph is called
an interval representation or interval model of the graph which can be specified by a set of
intervals {Iv := [lpv, rpv] | v ∈V (G)} such that Iu∩Iv ̸= ∅if and only if uv ∈E(G). A proper
interval graph is an interval graph that has an interval representation in which no interval is
properly contained in another interval. A proper interval ordering [27] of a proper interval
graph G is a linear ordering of its vertices v1, v2, . . . , vn such that for every 1 ≤i < j ≤n,
lpvi < lpvj (and rpvi < rpvj by definition of proper interval graph). It is well-known that
given an undirected graph, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that either outputs
that the graph is not a proper interval graph or outputs a proper interval ordering in which
every interval is of unit length. Hence, we can assume that all the intervals representing a
proper interval graphs are closed intervals of unit length. The following proposition due to
Cao et al. [21] is well-known property of proper interval graphs.
Proposition 5 ([21]). Let v1, . . . , vn be a proper interval ordering of a proper interval graph
G. Then, for every 1 ≤i < j ≤n, if vivj ∈E(G), then vi, . . . , vj is a clique.
Construction of Clique Partition.
Let G be a proper interval graph, and we fix a
proper interval ordering V := {v1, v2, . . . , vn} of proper interval graph G. We partition the
vertex set of G into a sequence of cliques as follows. We start with the first vertex v1 ∈V and
put all the vertices adjacent to v1 (including v1) in the first clique K1, i.e., K1 = {v1, . . . , vi}
such that lpvi ∈Iv1 and lpvi+1 /∈Iv1 where vi+1 ∈V. Next, we remove all the vertices of
K1 from V and repeat the same procedure to form the next clique K2 from the remaining
vertices. We continue this process until all the vertices of G are assigned to some clique.
Let K := {K1, K2, . . . , Kt} be the sequence of cliques obtained from this process. Note that
the indices of the cliques in the sequence K correspond to their order not the number of
vertices in the cliques. We say that K is a clique partition of G. There are some important
properties of this sequence of cliques K as stated in the following propositions.
Proposition 6 ([21], Proposition 2.2). Let K = {K1, K2, . . . , Kt} be a clique partition of
a proper interval graph G obtained from a proper interval ordering V of G. Then, for each
1 < i < t, N(Ki) ⊂Ki−1 ∪Ki+1. Moreover, N(K1) ⊆K2 and N(Kt) ⊆Kt−1.
Now, we define one of the crucial techniques for our problem. Consider a clique partition
K = {K1, K2, . . . , Kt} of a proper interval graph. Suppose that there is an ℓsuch that
2 ≤ℓ≤t −1 and N(Kℓ) ∩Kℓ−1, N(Kℓ) ∩Kℓ+1 ̸= ∅. We borrow the concept of a graph
operation from [21] as follows. We say that we bypass Kℓif we remove Kℓfrom the graph,
and add edges between every pair of vertices u ∈N(Kℓ) ∩Kℓ−1 and v ∈N(Kℓ) ∩Kℓ+1. It
is well-known due to [21] that if a clique from a proper interval graph is bypassed, then the
resulting graph is also a proper interval graph.
The following two lemma statements ensure us that there are two graph operations
under which proper interval graphs are closed.

attach a degree-2-tail P = (v, u1, u2, . . . , uℓ) at v. We obtain bG by attaching P at v. Then
bG is a simple (prop-int, tree)-graph.

degree-2-tail at a pendant vertex v of G. It is sufficient to prove that bG is simple graph
every connected component of bG is a proper interval graph or a tree. First, observe that
this graph operation does not create any self-loop. Note that G is a simple graph, and the

Parameterized Complexity and Kernelization.
A parameterized problem L is a set
of instances (x, k) ∈Σ∗× N where Σ is a finite alphabet and k ∈N is the parameter. The
notion of ‘tractability’ for a parameterized decision problem is defined as follows:
6


## Page 7


Definition 3 (Fixed-Parameter Tractability). A parameterized problem L ⊆Σ∗×N is fixed-
parameter tractable (or FPT in short) if there is an algorithm A that given (x, k) ∈Σ∗×N,
correctly decides if (x, k) ∈L in f(k)|x|O(1)-time for some computable function f : N →N.
This algorithm A is called a fixed-parameter algorithm (or FPT algorithm in short) for the
problem L.
Observe that we allow combinatorial explosion in the parameter k while the algorithm
runs in polynomial-time with respect to |x|. We say that two instances (x, k) of L and (x′, k′)
of L are equivalent when (x, k) ∈L if and only if (x′, k′) ∈L. The notion of kernelization
(preprocessing) for in the area of parameterized complexity is defined as follows.
Definition 4 (Kernelization). A parameterized problem L ⊆Σ∗× N is said to admit a
kernelization if there exists an algorithm that, given an instance (x, k) of L, outputs in time
polynomial in |x| + k an equivalent instance (x′, k′) of L such that |x′| + k′ ≤g(k) for some
computable function g : N →N. The instance (x′, k′) is called a kernel for the instance
(x, k). If g(k) is kO(1), then we say that L admits a polynomial kernel.
A kernelization algorithm usually consists of a collection of reduction rules that have
to be applied exhaustively. A reduction rule is safe if given an instance (x, k) of L, one
application of the reduction rule outputs an equivalent instance (x′, k′) of L. It is well-
known due to [7, 5, 10] that a decidable parameterized problem is FPT if and only if it
admits a kernelization.

This section is devoted to the proof of our main result. Let (G, k) be the input instance.
We assume without loss of generality that G has no self-loop, as otherwise, we can always
remove them. Our first step is to invoke a polynomial-time algorithm by Jacob et al. [17]
that outputs a set bS such that G −bS is a (prop-int, tree)-graph and S is a 7-approximate
solution. Clearly, if |bS| > 7k, we can immediately output that (G, k) is a no-instance.
Hence, we can assume that |bS| ≤7k.
Now, with the help of this 7-approximate solution bS and Proposition 2, we first focus on
the small obstructions, i.e., nets, tents, and induced cycles Cℓ, 4 ≤ℓ≤6 to obtain a set S
such that G−S is a (prop-int, tree)-graph and bS ⊆S. It allows us to forget about the small
obstructions of size at most 6 and focus on the large obstructions like (claw, triangle)-pairs
and holes of size at least 7 in the next step. Let us define a new family F of obstruction
such that it contains net, tent, and holes C4, C5 and C6. Our next lemma prepares us a set
S such that bS ⊆S and ensures that it is sufficient to intersect C4, C5, C6, nets, and tents

3.1
Initial Preprocessing Rules
We have some preprocessing rules that are common for the entire graph that we describe
in this section. It is important to note that some of our subsequent reduction rules may
generate components in G −S that is a tree or a proper interval graph but has no neighbor
in S. Therefore, for the sake of completeness, we state the following reduction rule that
removes every connected component of G that is a proper interval graph or a tree. This
deletion is safe because every obstruction for PITVD is connected induced subgraph, and
intersect with S. Hence, no obstruction can be entirely contained within a component C
in G −S that has no neighbor in S.
Reduction Rule 1. If there exists a connected component C in G that is a simple proper
interval graph or a tree, then delete C from G. The new instance is (G −C, k).

Our next two reduction rules also apply to the (Clique, Tree)-Vertex Deletion
problem. One is the standard buss-kernelization rule for Vertex Cover whose safeness is
trivial. The other reduces edge multiplicity to at most 2 (standard for Feedback Vertex
Set) to obtain a simple graph after deleting at most k vertices. See [4, 7] for details.
Reduction Rule 2. If there exists an edge in G with multiplicity more than 2, then reduce
the multiplicity of that edge to exactly 2.
Reduction Rule 3. If there is a vertex v such that v is adjacent to k+1 vertices u1, u2, . . .,
uk+1 such that for every i ∈[k + 1], vui is a double-edge, then the new instance is (G −
v, k −1).

Now,
we bound the number of vertices in G[V1] and use it to prove our main result, restated
below.
Theorem 1. (Proper-Interval, Tree)-Graph Vertex Deletion admits a kernel
with O(k33) vertices.
Proof. Consider an instance (G, k) in which none of the reduction rules are applicable.
Our kernelization algorithm first invokes Lemma 3 that either outputs that (G, k) is a
no-instance, or computes a set S of size O(k6) such that G −S has no tent, no net, and
no C4, C5, C6 as induced subgraph. Additionally, any set X of at most k vertices from S
intersects all nets, all tents, and all induced C4, C5, C6 of G[S] if and only if X intersects
all nets, all tents, and all induced C4, C5, C6 of G. After that, we apply all our Reduction
Rules 1–14 exhaustively. It follows from Lemma 22 that G[V1] has O(k2|S|2) cliques which
is O(k14) cliques. Subsequently, due to our marking procedure Mark-Clique(K), we have
that every clique of K in G[V1] that intersects N(S) contains at most η(k) vertices which
is O(k19) vertices. Our procedure additionally also marks at most η(k) vertices for every
clique K such that K ∩N(S) = ∅. As |S| is O(k6), it follows that G[V1] is O(k33). It follows
from Lemma 16 that G[V2] is O(|S|2) which is O(k12). Hence, an irreducible instance (G, k)
has O(k33) vertices. Therefore, (Proper-Interval, Tree)-Graph Vertex Deletion
admits a kernel with O(k33) vertices.

Conclusion
We give the first nontrivial polynomial kernel for (Proper-Interval, Tree)-Graph Ver-
tex Deletion where both G1 and G2 are defined by infinite forbidden families. A natural
direction is improving the bound, e.g., to O∗(k6) vertices. We believe that it is possible
but requires a specific approach to handle obstructions like tent and net. Jacob et al. [17]
gave single-exponential FPT algorithms for (Proper-Interval, Tree)-Graph Vertex
Deletion running in O∗(7k); improving this to O∗(4k) or O∗(3k) is an interesting di-
rection. It would also be intriguing to explore if our ideas can be adopted to provide a
polynomial kernel when G1 is interval graphs (respectively, chordal graphs) and G2 is forest
(respectively, bipartite permutation graphs). Even more interesting is designing kerneliza-
tion algorithms for vertex deletion into three or more scattered hereditary graph classes
(G1, G2, G3, . . . , Gℓ) such that Gi’s-Deletion problems admits a polynomial kernel for each
i ∈[ℓ].
