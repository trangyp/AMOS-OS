Abstract
The 2 →𝑞norm of a matrix 𝑋∈ℝ𝑛×𝑑is defined as ∥𝑋∥2→𝑞= sup∥𝑣∥2=1∥𝑋𝑣∥𝑞. We give
polynomial-time multiplicative approximation algorithms for this norm when 𝑞> 2 (i.e. in
the hypercontractive setting). This problem either directly captures or is closely related to
long-standing open problems in combinatorial optimization and hardness of approximation
(e.g. Small Set Expansion), quantum information (e.g. Best Separable State), and algorithmic
statistics.
Very little is known about what approximation factors we can achieve for this problem in
polynomial time, even though such approximations have significant downstream consequences.
Barak, Brandão, Harrow, Kelner, Steurer, and Zhou showed that no polynomial-time algorithm
can achieve an approximation factor better than 2
√
log 𝑛, assuming the Exponential Time
Hypothesis [BBH+12]. On the other hand, a simple spectral algorithm gives a 𝑑1/4-approximation
as a baseline. We give, to the best of our knowledge, the first polynomial-time approximation
algorithm beating this baseline by polynomial factors. For the important special case of 𝑞= 4 it
achieves a 𝑑1/8-approximation. All previous algorithms required additional assumptions on 𝑋,
or only surpassed the baseline for small values of 𝑛.
Moreover, we construct sum-of-squares certificates for the 2 →𝑞norm. This directly im-
plies improved algorithms for robust mean and covariance estimation, robust regression, and
clustering, when the data only satisfies a bound on its 𝑞-th moment.

Introduction
We study approximation algorithms for the 2 →𝑞norm of matrices. For 𝑞⩾1, the 2 →𝑞norm of a
matrix 𝑋∈ℝ𝑛×𝑑is defined as
∥𝑋∥2→𝑞= sup
∥𝑣∥2=1
∥𝑋𝑣∥𝑞.
Special cases are the spectral norm (𝑞= 2) and the largest ℓ2-norm of the rows of 𝑋(𝑞= ∞). We
focus on the in-between setting of 2 < 𝑞< ∞, known as the hypercontractive setting. For such 𝑞, the
2 →𝑞norm captures how ‘spiky’ or ‘analytically sparse’ vectors generated by the columns of 𝑋are.
While the 2 →2 and 2 →∞norms can be computed efficiently, even computing the 2 →
𝑞norm approximately for general 2 < 𝑞< ∞appears to be significantly more challenging
[BBH+12, HNW19, BGG+23]. Inspired by [BBH+12], we say an algorithm achieves a 𝛾-approximation
if it can solve the following promise problem.1 Given 𝑋, decide whether it satisfies (YES case)
∥𝑋∥2→𝑞⩾𝛾or (NO case) ∥𝑋∥2→𝑞⩽1.
Such approximation algorithms have direct consequences for a number of important problems
such as Small Set Expansion and Best Separable State and key questions in algorithmic statistics
[BBH+12, HM13, HL19]. Despite its centrality, strikingly little is known about what approximation
factors we can achieve in polynomial time. There is a ‘naive’ approximation algorithm based on
a simple spectral certificate involving the rows of 𝑋achieving an approximation factor of 𝑑1/4.
Our main result is the first polynomial-time algorithm that improves on this ‘naive’ algorithm by
polynomial factors. In the paradigmatic case of 𝑞= 4, our algorithm gives a 𝑑1/8-approximation.
All previous algorithms required additional assumptions on 𝑋, or only surpassed the baseline for
small values of 𝑛.
Our algorithm is based on multiple spectral certificates for a carefully constructed family of
matrices. Further, it is also captured by the low-degree sum-of-squares proof system, which is
important for the applications to algorithmic statistics.
Small Set Expansion, Best Separable State, and Certifiable Moments.
The 2 →𝑞norm sits at
the heart of theoretical computer science, with close ties to graph expansion, quantum information,
and algorithmic statistics. Indeed, already approximating the 2 →4 norm captures major open
questions in several areas.
Graph expansion. First, it is closely tied to expansion of small sets in graphs: Taking 𝑋to
be an orthonormal basis of the eigenspace of eigenvalues larger than (say) 0.1 of the adjacency
matrix, [BBH+12, BKS14] showed that a graph has no small non-expanding sets if and only if 𝑋has
bounded 2 →𝑞norm, for even 𝑞⩾4. Specializing to 𝑞= 4, they show that a 𝛾-approximation to
the 2 →4 norm allows one to distinguish graphs in which there is a non-expanding set of measure
at most 1/𝛾4 from graphs in which all sets of at most small constant measure are well-expanding.2
In particular, a constant-factor approximation, with the appropriate run-time, would refute the
Small Set Expansion Hypothesis of Raghavendra and Steurer [RS10] under standard complexity

Algorithmic statistics. If 𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑛are the rows of 𝑋, the 2 →𝑞norm of 𝑋is equivalently
the maximum 𝑞-th moment of any one-dimensional projection of 𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑛, up to normalization.
Constant-factor approximation algorithms for this quantity are at the core of many recent algo-
rithms for challenging problems in algorithmic statistics, such as robust estimation of mean and
covariance, robust linear regression, and clustering [HL18, KSS18, KKM18, BP21, ST21, GKSV23].
Currently, such approximations are only known when 𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑛are independent draws from
a ‘nice’ probability distribution, e.g. Gaussian, sub-Gaussian, or satisfying a Poincaré Inequal-
ity [KSS18, HL18, DHPT25]. Whether this type of average-case assumption on 𝑥1, . . . , 𝑥𝑛can be
weakened or removed is thus also central open question in algorithmic statistics [HL19, DHPT25].
A positive answer would yield efficient algorithms for core problems in robust statistics and
clustering under almost information-theoretically minimal distributional assumptions. Similarly,
weaker yet non-trivial approximation factors directly lead to improved estimation guarantees under
significantly weaker distributional assumptions than current efficient algorithms require.
Quantum information theory. Lastly, computing the 2 →4 norm of a matrix is tightly
related to fundamental questions in quantum information theory. In particular, via a reduction
of [HM13, BBH+12], a constant-factor approximation algorithm for the 2 →4 norm implies an
algorithm for the Best Separable State problem of deciding whether a given measurement of a quantum
system is a good entanglement witness. If additionally this algorithm runs in quasi-polynomial-time
this implies that QMA(2) ⊆EXP [HM13], resolving a long-standing open problem in quantum
complexity about the power of two entangled provers.
Best approximation factor in polynomial time?
Despite significant investigations of various special
cases and variations [BV11, BBH+12, HNW19, BGG+23, GMU25, BGLR25], our understanding of
the computational complexity of approximating the 2 →𝑞norm is very limited. The only known
constant-factor approximation algorithm is based on brute-force search in the column span of 𝑋,
requiring time exp(𝑑). In the case when 𝑑is significantly smaller than 𝑛, as it is for the application
to Small Set Expansion, this gives a sub-exponential (in 𝑛) running time, recovering the sub-
exponential-time algorithm for Small Set Expansion of [ABS10]. On the other hand, [BBH+12]
show that approximating the 2 →4 norm to within a factor of 1 + 𝑛−𝑂(1) is NP-hard and that a
2𝑜(√
log 𝑛)-approximation requires time at least 2𝜔(log 𝑛) under the Exponential Time Hypothesis.3
Further, [HNW19] (unconditionally) showed that approximating the 2 →4 norm up to any constant
needs at least Ω(log 𝑑/poly(log log 𝑑)) rounds of the sum-of-squares hierarchy.
The main question we ask in this work is the following.

1.1
Corollaries for Algorithmic Statistics and General Hypercontractive Norms
We next describe corrollaries for algorithmic statistics and approximating general hypercontractive
norms. We also comment on the relation of Theorem 1.1 to the Small Set Expansion and Best
Separable State problems.
Algorithmic statistics.
A key aspect of the application of 2 →𝑞norm certificates to algorithmic
statistics is its universality: Existing algorithms for many problems rely on such certificates in a
black-box way [HL18, KSS18, KKM18, BP21, ST21, GKSV23]. Consequently, improvements on the
certification question directly lead to improved algorithms for all of these problems.
We pause to highlight one of them, robust mean estimation. Let 𝒟be a distribution over ℝ𝑑
with mean 𝜇and 𝑞-th moments bounded along every direction. Instead of directly receiving i.i.d.
samples from 𝒟as input, an adversary can first inspect this i.i.d. sample and arbitrarily change
an 𝜀-fraction of the points in it. We receive the corrupted set as input and our goal is to estimate
𝜇in ℓ2-norm. Statistically, this problem is well-understood: Given sufficiently many samples, we
can estimate 𝜇up to dimension-free error 𝑂(𝜀1−1/𝑞) and this is best-possible [KSS18]. However,
obtaining efficient estimators is significantly more challenging and only sub-optimal error guarantees
are known: Applying a one-dimensional estimator coordinate-wise leads to error 𝑂(𝜀1−1/𝑞√
𝑑) and
using exact certificates for bounded second moments (aka the 2 →2 norm) leads to error 𝑂(√𝜀)
[DKK+17, SCV18].
Over the past decade, significant research has been expended to understand the correct scaling
of the error as a function of both 𝜀and 𝑑in this and closely related settings [DKK+16, LRV16,
DKS17, HL18, KSS18, HL19, DKK+22a, DK23, DHPT25]. This has led to remarkable progress under
additional assumptions (such as 𝒟being sub-Gaussian). However, without such assumptions, it is
not known how to achieve error better than 𝑂(min{√𝜀, 𝜀1−1/𝑞√
𝑑}). A consequence of our results is
the first efficient algorithm to break this barrier for a non-trivial range of 𝜀.
