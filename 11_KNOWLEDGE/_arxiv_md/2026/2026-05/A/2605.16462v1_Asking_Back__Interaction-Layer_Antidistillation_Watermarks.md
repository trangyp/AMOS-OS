Abstract
This work is motivated in part by a parallel genome-model study (GENOTRACE on
GenomeOcean [44]; App. N), where a token-level distillation watermark suggested
a broader question: can a trace survive distillation when its carrier is not token
statistics, but interaction behavior? Detecting unauthorized knowledge distilla-
tion from a deployed language-model API is hard precisely because the defender
controls neither the attacker’s training pipeline nor, in the API setting, the next-
token logits. Existing defenses operate on the teacher’s output tokens, either by
biasing the next-token distribution (green-list watermarks, cryptographic schemes,
antidistillation sampling) or by rewriting outputs after generation (defensive out-
put rewriting). Recent work shows that an attacker who paraphrases harvested
responses before training can strip these signals without losing the underlying
knowledge—defeating the watermark while preserving the distillation. We pro-
pose interaction-layer antidistillation watermarks, which move the trace one layer
higher, into the teacher’s interaction behavior: the defender wraps the teacher
with a system prompt that intermittently induces a behavioral marker—an explicit
follow-up question, a low-frequency variant of it, or a declarative restatement.
An oblivious distiller inherits the behavior in their student, and the defender later
audits through black-box queries with a human-validated LLM-as-judge (Cohen’s
κ=0.84/0.78 on strong/style rubrics). Across 63 LoRA-distilled students under
a Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct teacher (35,343 judged samples), behavioral wa-
termarks transfer at 88.9% (Gemma) / 80.9% (OLMo) / 45.2% (Qwen) relative
fidelity (H1, H2); under non-adaptive DIPPER prompt paraphrasing, robustness
decomposes into a teacher-self ceiling (∼66.4%) and a student-relative retention
of 21–112%, with one family (OLMo) preserving the watermark above the teacher
itself (H3, F-Amp); low-density (∼20%) explicit and implicit declarative variants
both transfer reliably above per-family baseline (H4, F-Style). An N=20 in-lab
study under a pre-registered Latin-square protocol shows all marker variants within
0.22 Likert step of baseline; TOST, Friedman, and Bonferroni-Wilcoxon all support
H5. The interaction layer is, on present evidence, a viable design locus for antidistil-
lation watermarking, complementary to token-, model-, and reasoning-trace-layer
defenses.

Introduction
Frontier language models are increasingly served through paid black-box APIs, and unauthorized
knowledge distillation against those APIs is now a mature commercial threat that has motivated a
range of antidistillation1 defenses [29, 24, 19]. An adversary harvests prompt–response pairs from
the teacher, fine-tunes a smaller student, and ships the student without permission. The defender’s
central question is therefore not whether harvesting can be blocked—it cannot, in any deployable
sense—but whether the defender can leave an auditable trace that survives distillation and that can
later be verified through black-box queries to the suspected student.
Existing defenses are organized by the layer at which the trace is embedded. The token-distribution
layer family [15, 1, 4, 29] perturbs the teacher’s next-token distribution. The model layer family
[39, 19] edits the teacher itself so that the harvested text carries a model-side mark. The reasoning-
trace layer family [20, 7] reorders or rewrites chain-of-thought traces. All three share an attack
surface operating on the tokens the attacker harvests, and recent removal-attack work [24, 17, 27, 2]
shows that pre-distillation rewriting can detach a student from the inherited signal while preserving
the knowledge transfer. The deeper structural gap is that even when a surface-text signal survives, it
does not specify a distinctive, queryable behavior in the distilled student.
Our starting point was a parallel genome-model study, where a KGW-style logit-bias watermark [15]
was ported into the GenomeOcean [44] LoRA distillation pipeline and retained the radioactive-
data property of [28] in a non-text regime (GENOTRACE; App. N). For conversational LLMs, the
analogous carrier need not remain at the token layer: the defender also controls the interaction frame
between user and model. That observation led to the asking-back markers studied here. We return to
this connection in §6.1.
In this work, we propose a fourth, complementary locus—the interaction layer (Figure 1). The
defender wraps the teacher with a system prompt that intermittently induces a behavioral marker B:
asking-back (a follow-up question), or its implicit cousins (a low-density variant, or a declarative
restatement of the user’s request). An oblivious distiller who collects teacher outputs without access
to the hidden trigger policy trains a student on a corpus in which B appears at some defender-chosen
density ρ. Three properties make this useful: B requires no logit access (the trace lives in the
response text); it is distillation-transferable because B is part of the next tokens the student is asked
to imitate; and it operates at a semantic granularity partially insensitive to prompt-side paraphrasing
(response-side rewriting is left to future work, Appendix, section O.1).
We organize the experiments around five hypotheses any deployable interaction-layer watermark2
must satisfy: H1–Learnability: a student trained on watermarked teacher outputs exhibits B at a
rate substantially above its baseline-trained counterpart; H2–Cross-family generalization: the effect
holds across pretrained model families with different architectures, vocabularies, and post-training
intensities; H3–Paraphrase robustness: the watermark partially survives a non-adaptive prompt-side
paraphrase (DIPPER on the user prompt), decomposed into a teacher-self ceiling and a student-
relative component; H4–Density–detectability trade-off: even when B appears in only ∼20% of
training rows, the student-side detection rate is reliably above the per-family noise floor; H5–Stealth:
a ∼20%-density watermark is not perceived by users as a clear degradation of interaction quality.
Our evaluation has two phases: a within-family Qwen3.5-9B-Instruct →Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base
case study at N=452 paired prompts under a deterministic regex (judge-free) and a 3 × 7 ×
3 = 63-student matrix under a Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct [9] teacher and a structured-output
gpt-oss-120b [23] judge (35,343 judged samples). The three markers and the three student families
are also described in sections §3.2 and §4.
Our contributions are threefold. (i) Problem framing. We formalize the interaction layer watermark
(Definition 1) and three metrics: τ, τrel, R (Definitions 3–4, Appendix, section C). These are the
behavioral counterparts of green-list [15] and radioactive-data [26] metrics. (ii) Scale. To our
knowledge the broadest cross-family distillation watermarking study: 3×7×3 = 63 LoRA students,
35,343 judged samples, single shared rubric. (iii) Two-level paraphrase robustness. Paraphrase

Related Work
A growing body of work studies how to make unauthorized distillation auditable by embedding
detectable signals into the teacher’s outputs. These approaches differ primarily in where the signal is
introduced and how it survives downstream processing. Here, we organize prior antidistillation work
by the layer at which the defender’s signal is embedded.
Early work focuses on the token distribution. Green-list watermarks [15] bias generation toward a
hashed subset of tokens, while cryptographic schemes [1] use keyed pseudorandom mappings, and
undetectable schemes [4] aim for stronger formal guarantees. Antidistillation sampling [29] instead
modifies the distribution to degrade its usefulness for downstream learners. However, these methods
typically depend on logit access or surface-text statistics, making them vulnerable to paraphrasing
and rewriting attacks [17, 27]. Empirical work [24, 2] shows that such signals can weaken or be
removed entirely under realistic attacker pipelines.
A complementary line of work embeds signals at the model level. Instructional fingerprinting [39]
introduces model-side signatures through fine-tuning, while defensive output generation [19] modifies
the final layer of the teacher. Related approaches extend these ideas to distillation-aware fingerprint-
ing [40]. These methods generally assume that the defender has control over the teacher model and
can modify its parameters or output layer. Other approaches operate on reasoning traces. Some
have tried trace rewriting [20] or, for example, PART reorders sub-conclusions to preserve answer
information while disrupting inferential structure [7]. These approaches assume the teacher exposes
(and controls) a reasoning trace, which restricts them to specific deployment patterns.
More broadly, provenance and attribution have been studied through techniques such as membership
inference [30, 21], though these often require impractically large query budgets in API settings.
Radioactive data [26] is the closest classical analogue: a defender perturbs training data so that a
downstream learner’s parameters carry a detectable mark. Our approach adopts a similar philosophy
of embedding a signal in the data and detecting it through the trained model, but operates at the level

of behavior rather than parameters, avoiding the need for white-box access. Prior work also observes
that models can exhibit incidental stylistic fingerprints arising from training [31]; in contrast, our
watermarks are engineered signals deliberately induced by the defender via system prompts, so the
two are complementary (incidental fingerprints might be used to corroborate, but cannot substitute for,
an engineered watermark). The underlying clarifying-question behavior is itself a studied dialogue
capability [42], where our novelty here is the defensive use of such a behavior as a transferable,
auditable marker. Concurrent work [5] documents that distillation can transmit teacher behavioral
traits even through training data with no language-level content, a parameter-space channel that
operates only between teacher and student sharing initialization; we discuss the boundary between
this regime and ours in §6.
Taken together, these lines of work suggest that different classes of signals occupy distinct points in
the design space. Token-level approaches are vulnerable to response-level rewriting, while behavioral
signals can be disrupted by prompt-level transformations. Rather than viewing these approaches
as competing, we treat them as complementary: each provides an additional audit channel with a
different failure mode. Our work builds on this perspective by focusing on interaction-level behavior
as a distinct and practical locus for antidistillation watermarking.
3
Overview of Approach
3.1
Within-family case study
We begin with a controlled, within-family experiment to establish whether interaction-layer mark-
ers can survive distillation under the most favorable conditions. Concretely, we distill from a
Qwen3.5-9B-Instruct teacher into a Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base student using only the strong condi-
tion. The experiment uses a single seed and evaluates on 452 paired prompts. We adopt a strict proxy
for the behavioral marker: whether the response ends in a question mark, detected via a deterministic
regex applied to cleaned outputs (see Appendix, section D for regex specification). This measurement
is intentionally conservative, requiring a literal ? at the end of the response, to capture only the most
explicit form of the asking-back behavior, and it should be interpreted as a lower bound relative to
the broader semantic marker used later with the gpt-oss-120b judge.
We find that the student trained on watermarked data produces question-ending responses at a
rate of 99.56% (450/452, Wilson 95% CI [98.40, 99.88]), whereas the baseline-trained student
does so only 5.09% of the time (23/452, CI [3.41, 7.52]), yielding a gap of +94.47 percentage
points (CI [92.35, 96.59]) that excludes zero by a wide margin, showing the behavior is learned
almost deterministically. This provides strong evidence for H1 (learnability through knowledge
distillation, KD [11]) in the within-family setting, even under a deliberately strict proxy (see Table 2,
Appendix, section D, for more details). This design is limited in scope and does not address cross-
family generalization, robustness to paraphrasing, or behavior at lower marker densities, and it
relies on a proxy that captures only one surface form of the behavior. In the following, we adopt
(i) base/pretrained (PT) student checkpoints to avoid conflating inherited assistant priors with the
distilled marker; (ii) ID-aligned mixing for low-density variants, so that differences across conditions
are attributable to the marker rather than the prompts; (iii) a separate 70B-class teacher, decoupling
the results from within-family artifacts; and (iv) a structured-output LLM-as-judge, which allows us
to detect both explicit and implicit forms of the behavior. We turn to this broader evaluation next
(sections §3.2–§5).
3.2
Formalization
We formalize the watermark at the level of interaction. Rather than modifying token probabilities or
model parameters, the defender prepends a system prompt sB to a teacher T, producing a wrapped
teacher TsB whose responses can be tested for a behavioral marker B. A student trained on TsB’s
outputs inherits the marked behavior through standard distillation.

(§5.4), not a primary axis of the H3 paraphrase analysis; implicit-marker paraphrase robustness is
future work.

Experimental Setup
We evaluate the proposed watermark in a cross-family distillation setting designed to stress
generalization, robustness, and detectability. The study spans 3 base families × 7 conditions
× 3 seeds = 63 LoRA-distilled students.
All students are trained on outputs from a shared
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct teacher and evaluated using a structured-output gpt-oss-120b judge,
for a total of 35,343 judged samples. A full specification of the protocol is given in Table 3 (Appendix,
section G); we describe the non-mechanical design choices below.

The teacher [9] is served via vLLM with standard decoding settings (T=0.7, topp=0.9,
maxtok =1024, repetition penalty 1.0, seed 42) and wrapped with one of seven system prompts
corresponding to the different marker conditions, with no modification to the underlying model.
The seven conditions are baseline, strong, soft, style_control, and three DIPPER-paraphrased coun-
terparts (baseline_up, soft_up, strong_up); the full protocol is in Table 3 (Appendix, section G).
Students are drawn from three base/PT (non-Instruct) checkpoints from disjoint organizations—
Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base [25, 41], Gemma-3-1B-pt [8], and OLMo-2-0425-1B [22]. Using base mod-
els avoids a key confound: an instruction-tuned student may already encode assistant-style behaviors
that resemble the watermark, while cross-family pretraining ensures that any transferred behavior is
not tied to a shared training pipeline.
Teacher prompts are sampled from a 4,500-prompt mixture of OpenHermes-2.5 [33],
OpenMathInstruct-2 [34], Magicoder-OSS-Instruct [36], and an open chat slice. After seven-way ID
alignment across the four primary conditions and their three DIPPER-paraphrased counterparts, we
retain 3,009 prompts shared by all seven conditions; a fixed 582-id subset (19.34%, slightly under the
designed 20%) is the soft / style-control mix and is identical across soft, soft_up, and style_control.
Held-out evaluation begins from a separate 1,000-prompt pool (Alpaca [32], OpenAssistant [16],
math_train, MBPP [3], de-duplicated against the teacher corpus), and is filtered to 561 ID-aligned
prompts (next paragraph), giving 35,343 judged samples across the 63 students. Full alignment
and filtering details, including the 1,491-prompt drop and the teacher’s per-corpus rate caveat, are
provided in Appendix, section G.
We estimate ρ as the ratio of yes verdicts to (yes+no), with the third (abstain) verdict excluded
from the denominator. Achieved teacher rates (measured by the same downstream judge) are below
the design ceiling. Under the STRONG condition, the teacher exhibits the marker on 90.93% of
responses (measured on strong.jsonl); under SOFT and STYLE-CONTROL, the corresponding
rates are 91.45% and 83.15% on the 582-row low-density subset, compared to baseline rates of
0.20%/1.20% under the respective rubrics. We distinguish “designed 20%” from “achieved on the
mixed corpus” and report achieved rates throughout (Table 6, teacher row). Because teacher and
student are scored under one shared rubric, the relative quantities τrel and Rrel are calibration-invariant
(proof in Appendix, section C.2); judge–human κ validation is in Appendix, section J.

implicit chain-of-thought prior [6] that on long math/code prompts truncates the student’s response

agnostic sentence-ending criterion (analogous to Appendix, section D) to all 21 Qwen runs and
propagate the surviving 561-prompt ID-intersection to Gemma and OLMo. All 63 students are
therefore score on identical inputs at cost of 439 truncation-prone prompts excluded from the 1,000-

Students are fine-tuned with LoRA [13] on the seven projections (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down) at rank
r=16 and α=32 (learning rate η=2×10−4, one epoch, bf16, and max-seq-len 2048). Loss is masked

5.1
H1, H2: Behavioral watermarks transfer across families
Figure 2 reports the full 3 × 7 matrix (numerical mean ± SD in Table 6, Appendix, section I);
the qualitative pattern—large effects for strong, intermediate for soft, smaller but above noise for
style-control, near-zero baselines—is preserved across all three families.
All three student families learn the strong watermark well above their baseline noise floors (H1).
Against the teacher’s 90.93% strong rate, the relative transfer rates are 88.9% (Gemma), 80.9%
(OLMo), and 45.2% (Qwen), substantial in every case. The judge measurement is validated against
3 independent human annotators on a 200-item subset: Cohen’s κ=0.84 (95% CI [0.72, 0.94],
almost-perfect agreement) on STRONG_RUBRIC (Figure 6); the judge slightly under-detects on strong
(cell κ=0.62, 3 markers missed in 16 items; Appendix, section J), so the transfer rates above are
conservative lower bounds.
The preserved sign and ordering across three independent families (Gemma, OLMo, Qwen), trained by
disjoint organizations on disjoint corpora, supports H2. Family-specific susceptibility (45.2% Qwen
< 80.9% OLMo < 88.9% Gemma) is a bonus finding (F-Family) that we discuss mechanistically in
§6. Per-family judge κ is uniformly substantial-to-almost-perfect on STRONG (Gemma 0.87, OLMo
0.74, Qwen 0.94; Figure 9 in Appendix, section J), so the cross-family ordering is not a judge artifact.

of the marker survives, while one third does not. This sets a ceiling that any student inherits.

6
Discussion
The results above suggest a consistent pattern at one level: interaction-layer signals propagate through
distillation as behavioral tendencies rather than as surface-level patterns. Both explicit (STRONG,
SOFT) and implicit (STYLE-CONTROL) markers are detected above each family’s baseline, even
though the interrogative variants end with a question mark while the declarative variant does not.
The fact that two markers with no shared token- or syntax-level pattern both transfer across all three
families is hard to reconcile with a purely surface-imitation account. It reads more naturally as the
student learns to qualify its response with respect to the user’s intent, a discourse-level competence
the marker exercises, expressed across multiple surface realizations. This is consistent with prior
observations that distillation preferentially preserves structural regularities over token-level statistics
[35]. The family ordering, however, is not preserved across conditions. On STRONG, Gemma
(80.87%) > OLMo (73.56%) > Qwen (41.08%); on SOFT, OLMo (31.99%) far exceeds Gemma
(15.5%) and Qwen (16.0%); on STYLE-CONTROL, Gemma (11.71%) > Qwen (7.38%) > OLMo
(6.66%). What is consistent across conditions is that every (family, marker) cell sits above the
corresponding family baseline, so the qualitative conclusion that the interaction-layer signals transfer
does not depend on a specific model.
F-Family: Why families differ in magnitude. Three non-exclusive factors plausibly account
for the magnitude differences on STRONG. (i) Pretraining-mixture differences appear to produce
stronger assistant-style priors in some families: Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base shows the highest baseline
marker rate (1.67%) relative to Gemma (0.00%) and OLMo (0.65%), consistent with an instruction-
following prior that competes against marker acquisition under fixed-rank LoRA. (ii) Capacity
allocation: Qwen is 0.8B vs. 1B for the others, a ∼20% headroom non-trivial under fixed-rank LoRA.
(iii) Tokenization: different vocabularies split “Could you clarify...?”-style endings into different
token counts, reweighting the marker under response-only loss masking. No experiment decisively
discriminates the three. Importantly, none of these mechanisms explains why transfer succeeds at
all from a single Llama teacher across three disjoint pretraining pipelines; combined with F-Style,
the parsimonious reading is that the marker propagates at the interaction layer—a discourse-level
competence acquired during natural-language pretraining and reusable across backbones. Cloud et al.
[5] delineate a different channel: parameter-space transmission of behavioral traits between models
sharing initialization. The two regimes are complementary boundaries on what distillation can carry.
F-Amp: Two single-family anomalies in OLMo. OLMo exhibits two patterns that the other two
families do not: (a) on SOFT, its student exhibits the marker at 31.99% vs. the teacher’s 17.85%—a
179.2% relative transfer, well above the 87.0% (Gemma) and 89.5% (Qwen) observed on the same
condition; and (b) on STRONG under prompt-side paraphrase, its student preserves the watermark at
112% of the teacher’s own retention, where Gemma sits at 89% and Qwen at 65%. We treat these as
two distinct single-family observations rather than a generic property of 1B-class students.

The asking-back idea emerged during our broader exploration of watermarking techniques for
genome language models and of higher-level watermarks on genomic content.
The specific
token-layer experiment that surfaced the inspiration — a KGW [15] green-list applied to the
GenomeOcean-500M→100M [44] distillation pipeline on E. coli prompts (GENOTRACE; separate
manuscript in progress) — confirmed that the radioactivity property of [28] extends to a non-text

what produced the asking-back markers studied in the rest of this paper.
The detailed setup and the hypothesis-level summary of the genome experiment are gathered in
App. N; the dedicated GENOTRACE write-up is in preparation.
7
Conclusion
We propose interaction-layer antidistillation watermarks as a fourth, complementary locus alongside
token-, model-, and reasoning-trace-level defenses. Across a two-phase evaluation (within-family
and cross-family), we find consistent evidence for learnability, cross-family transfer, and low-density
detectability (H1, H2, H4), show that the signal generalizes beyond explicit interrogative forms (F-
Style), and characterize robustness as a two-level (teacher-self × student-relative) decomposition; H5
is supported in an N=20 in-lab study, indicating that low-density markers can be introduced without
degrading perceived interaction quality. Taken together, these results indicate that the interaction
layer is a viable design space for antidistillation watermarking, providing a complementary audit
channel at the level of response behavior. Open questions remain around adaptive robustness, broader
marker design, cross-lingual transfer, and the mechanisms underlying F-Amp. We discuss these
future directions and related limitations with further details in Appendix, section O.

The mechanism is dual-use. On the defender’s side it lowers the cost of auditing unauthorized

C.2
Calibration invariance of relative metrics
Throughout the paper we use the same gpt-oss-120b judge under identical rubrics on both teacher
and student rows. Suppose the judge bB deviates from the (unobservable) ground truth B by an
arbitrary multiplicative bias c > 0 that is shared across (teacher, student) and (clean, paraphrased)
columns: i.e. for any model M on any prompt set Q, Ep∼Q[ bB(p, M(p))] = c · Ep∼Q[B(p, M(p))].
Then both relative metrics from the main body are exactly invariant to c:
bτrel(S, B) = c · τ(S, B)
c · τ(T, B) = τrel(S, B).
For Rrel the argument is the same applied twice: each of RS and RT is already a ratio of two judge
rates that share the c factor (so bRS = RS and bRT = RT individually), hence bRrel = bRS/ bRT =
RS/RT = Rrel(B, π). A common multiplicative judge bias therefore enters numerator and denomi-
nator equally and drops out. The strict multiplicative assumption bB = c · B is exact only when the
judge’s False Positive Rate is zero; under a more general affine bias bB = c0 +c·B the relative metrics
are approximately invariant to the extent c0 ≪τ, which Table 6 (baseline rows: 0–1.78% across
families) and the per-rubric judge precision in App. J support empirically. The judge’s per-rubric
Cohen’s κ=0.84, 0.78 vs. the human majority (App. J) bounds the size of c as well; since bτ and bR
themselves can be biased, we also report absolute rates and treat them as conservative lower bounds
when the judge under-detects (e.g., the cell κ=0.62 on STRONG; §5.1).
C.3
Finite-sample estimators
We estimate every τ, τrel, RT , RS, and Rrel from the on-disk judge labels by plug-in (the sample mean
of the judge’s {0, 1}-coded verdicts replacing the expectation, and a ratio-of-means for the relative
quantities). The teacher reference τ(T, B) used as denominator in τrel and RT is a point estimate
on the training corpus (n=3,009 prompts judged on the teacher’s own outputs); since the held-out
distribution Pheld is a different mixture (Alpaca / OpenAssistant / math_train / MBPP), this empirical
denominator is a deployment-density reading of τ(T, B) rather than a strictly distribution-matched
one as written in Defs. 3, 4. Two empirical checks suggest the distribution shift this introduces
is small in practice: (i) the achieved marker density on the training corpus (582/3009=19.34% of

C.4
Interpretation of the Rrel regimes
Equation (1) cleanly separates a paraphrase attack’s two effects:
• RT < 1: the teacher itself loses some signal under prompt-side paraphrase. This is a ceiling
property of the watermark B and the paraphraser π that no distillation pipeline can recover
beyond.
• Rrel < 1: the student inherits an additional family-dependent loss on top of RT . This is the
regime where the student is more fragile under attack than the teacher’s own behavior would
suggest.
• Rrel = 1: the student preserves the watermark under paraphrase exactly to the extent the
teacher would. Most distilled students fall here or below.
• Rrel > 1: the student preserves the watermark above the teacher’s own ceiling. Mechanically
this can happen when distillation collapses the teacher’s stochastic condition-on-I20 rule
into a more deterministic marginal rule that paraphrase struggles to detach (the F-Amp
regime; §6, App. M).
The traditional one-number robustness reading of token-level watermarks [15, 24] collapses these
four regimes onto a single RS and correspondingly conflates “the watermark is brittle” with “the
student is brittle”; the two-level decomposition (Eq. 1) is what permits the reframing argued in the
introduction (Contribution iii).

Cleaning: pairwise sentence-ending filter.
The strong system prompt instructs the teacher to
append a follow-up question, so strong responses tend to fit within the 512-token budget; baseline
responses, lacking this constraint, more often exceed it on long math/code prompts. We measure
this asymmetry directly on the case study data: of the 1000 rows, 525 baseline responses and 222
strong responses do not terminate in sentence-final punctuation. To eliminate the resulting selection
bias we apply a pairwise filter: a prompt id is retained iff both its baseline and its strong response
terminate in sentence-final punctuation (ASCII period, exclamation, or question mark, plus their
CJK fullwidth counterparts at U+3002 / U+FF01 / U+FF1F, optionally followed by trailing quotes or
closing brackets). The sentence-ending criterion is identical to the one used in Phase 2’s truncation
filter (§4, “Why N=561”); the regeneration-tail cleaner is also shared with Phase 2 (§4, “Judge and
tail cleaning”). All scripts are part of the supplementary code archive. The pairwise filter retains
Npaired=452 prompts on which baseline and strong are compared on identical inputs.
Detection: deterministic regex.
We measure the rate of literal question-mark-terminating responses
with a multilingual-aware regex that matches a question mark (ASCII U+003F or CJK fullwidth
U+FF1F), optionally followed by trailing quotes or closing brackets, at the very end of the cleaned
response. The pattern is a deterministic operationalization of the strong system prompt’s literal
instruction (“end with a question mark”). It requires no LLM-as-judge—hence no Cohen’s κ and no
judge-portability question—and is fully reproducible from the cleaned files. By construction the regex
is strict: it counts only literal ?-endings and misses any implicit asking-back the gpt-oss-120b
judge in Phase 2 would catch (e.g., a declarative “Could you tell me your goal.”), so the case study
measurement is a lower bound on the broader behavioral marker measured later.

Truncation filter.
This paragraph expands the summary in §4 (“Why N=561”).
Our
Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base student inherits a known tendency of the Qwen3.5 architecture family toward
implicit chain-of-thought generation [6]: even with the enable_thinking=false chat-template flag
set on the sibling Instruct checkpoint, the family does not in practice fully suppress reasoning-style
generation, and the pre-trained Base weights inherit the same prior. Implicit reasoning therefore
consumes a non-trivial fraction of the maxtok =1024 generation budget on long math/code prompts,
producing mid-sentence truncation in the student’s response field. Because a truncated response
is a generation-pipeline artifact rather than a model-behavioral signal, passing it to the LLM judge
would inject a Qwen-only noise term into a metric we want to compare across families. To eliminate
this selection bias we apply a condition-agnostic sentence-ending criterion (analogous in spirit to
the case study’s pairwise filter, §D) to all 7 × 3 = 21 Qwen runs, take the intersection of surviving
prompt ids across those 21 runs, and propagate that common-id set as the held-out set for Gemma
and OLMo as well. The intersection contains 561 prompts shared by every (family, condition, seed)
cell, so all 63 students are scored on a strictly identical held-out set with strictly identical prompt ids.
The cost is the exclusion of approximately 439 truncation-prone prompts (heavily concentrated in the
math/code subset) from the original 1,000-prompt pool. We treat this Qwen-driven shrinkage as a
measurement-pipeline caveat rather than a finding: it narrows the prompt distribution slightly toward
shorter answers, which we acknowledge as a threat to external validity but accept in exchange for
tight cross-family ID alignment.

Protocol.
The pre-registered H5 user-study protocol uses a 5 × 5 Latin square that balances
the five conditions (baseline, strong-20, strong-50, style-20, style-50) across the five
ordinal session positions: each row of the Latin square is one participant; each condition appears
in each position exactly once across the five rows. Each session is a 10-turn free-form chat with a
backend serving Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (the same teacher used elsewhere in this work) and
per-turn Bernoulli mixing of SYSTEM_BASELINE with the marker prompt at the condition’s density
ρ ∈{0%, 20%, 50%}. Participants choose their own conversation topics, mirroring real chatbot
use; topic confound is controlled at the participant level by the within-subject design (each user
contributes one observation per condition relative to their own conversational style and preferences).
After each session, the participant rates satisfaction on a 5-point Likert scale and answers a free-text
direct-perception probe.

of the protocol: an onboarding screen with the participant ID input and study description; the chat

The pre-registered analyses—TOST equivalence at the within-subject paired

Audit-channel disjointness in detail
The disjoint-attack-surfaces argument summarized in §6 positions the interaction layer as a comple-
mentary audit channel rather than a competitor. Two practical consequences follow.
The literature already quantifies one half of this disjointness.
Token-level watermarks are known
to be vulnerable to paraphrase attacks: Krishna et al. [17] show that DIPPER alone substantially
reduces the detection z-score of KGW-style green-list watermarks; Sadasivan et al. [27] show that
adversarial paraphrasing can drive token-level detection below threshold entirely; and—closest to
our setting—Pan et al. [24] report that token-level watermarks survive distillation only at degraded
strength under realistic attacker pipelines. Our interaction-layer watermark is, by construction, not
a token-distribution statistic, so the same response-level paraphrase attacks that erode KGW-style
signals leave its hypothesis class intact. Conversely, our DIPPER experiments (§5.2) place its
student-relative retention in the 21–112% range. The two attack surfaces are therefore disjoint both
by construction and in measured effect; quantifying joint-deployment behavior on a single shared
distillation pipeline is left to follow-up work.
Auditor’s perspective.
From an auditor’s standpoint, behavioral watermarks have a further practical
advantage: the auditor reads the marker B from the defender’s own ledger of system prompts, queries
the suspected student, and runs a calibrated rate test on 561 samples; no white-box access, no
learned classifier, and a query budget that is small compared to MIA- or radioactive-data-style
detection [30, 26].
M
F-Amp contextualization in the small-LM literature
The F-Amp observation in §6 is consistent with two strands of prior work, neither of which we
claim to extend. First, capacity-bounded distillation in small students has been reported to yield
higher KL divergence and overgeneralization-like behavior on held-out inputs than the same recipe
applied to a larger student [10, 11]; this is the same abstract mechanism we sketch in the main-text
candidate-mechanism paragraph, just rediscovered in a different setting. Second, OLMo-2-0425-1B—
in contrast to its 7B / 13B / 32B siblings—uses a single-seed final checkpoint rather than the multi-run
averaging that the larger OLMo-2 sizes employ [22]; this matches both its lighter base-alignment
prior (the AI2 team report being initially surprised at how strong the post-trained 1B variant becomes
relative to its only-“mid” base benchmarks) and the higher seed variance we observe relative to
gemma-3-1b-pt and Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base. We therefore do not claim F-Amp as a novel mechanism
unique to our setting: the most parsimonious reading is that it is the asking-back-marker manifestation
of an already-documented small-model moldability, with an OLMo-specific component layered on
top. The capacity-bounded mechanism in §6 remains a candidate but is neither necessary nor unique.
Practical implication, contingent on follow-up.
If the F-Amp mechanism does generalize beyond
OLMo, defenders need not pay the human-perception cost of high-ρ watermarks to obtain high
student-side detection rates: a 20% teacher-side density already yields ∼32% on the OLMo student.

Limitations and future work
This appendix expands the brief future-work pointer in §7. We first lay out the limitations of the
present study (organized by hypothesis / finding), then the follow-up directions in priority order.
O.1
Limitations
H5 stealth at N=20.
The user-perception evidence in §5.4 is an in-lab study at N=20 (lab members
and invited participants only). All four marker variants sit within 0.22 Likert step of baseline, and the
three pre-registered inferential tests (TOST equivalence at ε=1.0 Likert step, Friedman, Bonferroni-
Wilcoxon) all support H5 (App. K). Replication on a larger crowdsourced sample, with a tighter
equivalence margin, remains future work.
F-Amp is a single-family observation.
The above-teacher amplification on OLMo (§6) is observed
on one family; Gemma and Qwen do not show it. The capacity-bounded mode-collapse mechanism
we sketch in the main body is a candidate, not a claim, and an OLMo-specific pretraining or tokenizer
effect is not ruled out (App. M). Until the three falsifiable predictions of the candidate mechanism
(capacity inverse, ρ inverse, training-length decreasing) are empirically tested, F-Amp should be read
as a single-family observation, not a general property of small distilled students.

Single paraphraser, prompt-side only, single threat model.
The paraphrase robustness numbers
in §5.2 apply DIPPER [17] (lex=60, order=60) to the user prompt before re-querying the teacher, not
to the harvested teacher response. Response-level rewriting (paraphrasing the teacher’s output before
it enters the student’s training set) is the canonical attack against token-level watermarks [17, 24]
and is the most pressing extension of our threat model; it is not evaluated here, and we commit to
running this experiment in the next phase of the project once additional GPU allocation becomes
available (the same 63-student matrix, restricted to STRONG for compute economy, with an LLM-
as-paraphraser applied to teacher responses before the student-training step). Stronger non-adaptive
attacks (LLM-as-paraphraser at scale, multi-stage rewriting) and adaptive attackers that target our
specific marker family (prompt-engineering removal, post-filter removal) are also not evaluated. The
21–112% student-relative retention range is therefore a prompt-side lower-bound estimate of the
threat surface, not a final security claim.
Implicit-marker paraphrase robustness (style_control_up).
We do not evaluate a DIPPER-
paraphrased counterpart of STYLE-CONTROL. STYLE-CONTROL was added as a supplementary
extension demonstrating that interaction-layer markers are learnable in an implicit, non-interrogative
form (F-Style, §5.4): an ∼20%-density declarative restatement transfers above per-family baseline
across all three student families, supporting the low-density learnability story (H4) for behavioral-
level interactions beyond the asking-back family. Our paper does not claim that all behavioral-level
markers enjoy uniformly good paraphrase robustness; the H3 analysis (§5.2) specifically establishes
that the asking-back markers (STRONG/SOFT) retain a non-trivial fraction of their signal under
prompt-side paraphrase, characterized by the two-level RT · Rrel decomposition. The absence of
style_control_up is therefore a deliberate scoping of our robustness claim to the asking-back family,
not a gap in marker coverage; extending the decomposition to implicit declarative markers is a natural
follow-up.
Marker-design coverage.
We test three markers (strong, soft, style-control) on a {density, syntax}
rectangle. F-Style (§5.4) shows the mechanism does not require a question-mark surface form, but
other behavioral axes—formatting choices, hedging patterns, structural openings, lexical biases—are
unexplored.
English-only, single tokenizer family per teacher.
All training and evaluation data are in En-
glish; multilingual carry-over and cross-tokenizer behavior are open. The teacher is a single
Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct checkpoint; we have no data on whether interaction-layer watermarks
transfer when the teacher is itself swapped for a different family.
Held-out distribution narrowed by the truncation filter.

Future directions, in priority order
(i) Crowdsourced replication of H5.
The current N=20 in-lab study (App. K) is conducted with
lab members and invited participants, and the three pre-registered inferential tests (TOST, Friedman,
Bonferroni-Wilcoxon) already support H5 at ε=1.0 Likert step. A larger crowdsourced replication
under the same 5×5 Latin-square protocol, with IRB approval and a tighter equivalence margin,
would provide independent verification.

(ii) Stronger and adaptive paraphrasers.
DIPPER is a textbook non-adaptive attacker. Three
strictly stronger threat models are immediate priorities: LLM-as-paraphraser at scale; two-stage
rewriting (paraphrase the prompt and the response); and an adaptive attacker that knows the marker
family and prompt-engineers / post-filters to remove it. We expect adaptive susceptibility to vary
sharply across our three markers: STRONG (a trailing question on every response) is most exposed—a
one-line system-prompt instruction or a regex post-filter plausibly strips it; SOFT shares the same
vulnerability at lower per-prompt density; STYLE-CONTROL (declarative restatement, no syntactic
anchor) is hardest to remove without semantic damage, since deleting the restatement collapses the
answer’s framing. Quantifying RT and Rrel under these attack models is the natural extension of
§5.2.
(iii) Watermark-type expansion.
The {density, syntax} rectangle is a small region of the marker
design space. F-Style (§5.4) suggests other behavioral axes (formatting choices, hedging patterns,
structural openings, lexical biases) are also likely to transfer; a systematic sweep across these would
map the boundary of the interaction-layer mechanism.
(iv) Cross-tokenizer and multilingual generalization.
All training and evaluation data are English.
Open questions: do interaction-layer markers transfer through a tokenizer boundary (e.g., a teacher in
one tokenizer family, a student in another)? Do they survive a multilingual teacher and a monolingual
student, or vice versa?
(v) Beyond LoRA r=16: full-parameter fine-tuning.
All students in this study are LoRA adapters
with rank r=16 on the seven projections, a deliberately capacity-bounded configuration. Full-
parameter fine-tuning (or higher-rank LoRA) admits strictly more degrees of freedom for the student
to track the teacher’s conditional behaviour, and we therefore expect the absolute transfer rates and
student-relative paraphrase retention Rrel reported in Table 6 and §5.2 to be conservative lower bounds
relative to a full-FT student. The ρ-density requirement for reliable detection should correspondingly
relax. Direct verification, including whether F-Amp is suppressed (capacity sufficient to track the
trigger predicate) or amplified (more deterministic mode collapse), is left to future work.
(vi) Beyond Base/PT students: Instruct-target distillation.
We use Base/PT student check-
points to isolate the marker signal from assistant-style priors that an Instruct checkpoint would
already carry (§4). Existing data partially probes the Instruct case: Qwen3.5-0.8B-Base—the
most heavily post-trained of our three students—shows the highest baseline (1.67% vs 0–0.65% for
Gemma/OLMo) and the lowest STRONG relative transfer (45.2%), consistent with an instruction-
resistance prior competing against marker acquisition (F-Family discussion in §6). Extrapolating, an
Instruct target should yield lower (but qualitatively similar) transfer rates; direct verification on, e.g.,
Qwen3.5-0.8B-Instruct or gemma-3-1b-it, is the natural follow-up.
(vii) Empirical grounding for F-Amp.
The capacity-bounded mode-collapse hypothesis we sketch
in §6 makes three falsifiable predictions: amplification should scale inversely with student capacity,
increase as ρ decreases, and decrease under longer training. None has been tested. Direct ablations
(capacity scans, ρ scans, multi-epoch runs) and a smaller-scale replication on a different 1B family
would let us either accept or rule out the candidate mechanism (an OLMo-specific pretraining or
tokenizer effect remains a competing explanation; App. M).
(viii) Theoretical framework.
An information-theoretic characterization of the stealth–detection–
robustness Pareto frontier (the trade-off synthesized in Fig. 5, right) would let practitioners pick ρ
analytically, rather than from a family-by-family tuning sweep, and would put the two-level robustness
decomposition (Eq. 1) on a firmer footing than the empirical reading we use here.
