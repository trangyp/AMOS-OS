Abstract
Binary endpoints are common in clinical trials and conditional odds ratios have tradi-
tionally been used to assess treatment effects. However, the interpretation of odds ratios is
difficult, they are non-collapsible and rely on strong assumptions in order to be a relevant
overall summary measure for the trial. As an alternative, risk differences have gained increas-
ing prominence as a more interpretable, clinically meaningful and assumption-lean measure
of treatment effects. This shift has also been motivated by new regulatory guidance, which
emphasizes the relevance of marginal estimands and encourages covariate adjustment. Yet,
covariate-adjusted inference for risk differences, particularly in smaller samples, has method-
ological subtlties and lacks well-established best practices. We conduct a simulation study
comparing methods for estimating and testing risk differences in small-sample (N ≤150)
randomized clinical trials with prognostic categorical baseline covariates, focusing on exact
unconditional tests, Mantel–Haenszel methods, and g-computation (standardization) ap-
proaches. We find that several g-computation approaches exhibit inflated Type-I error in
very small samples when standard Wald-type inference is applied, whereas robust or pe-
nalized variants improve error control at the expense of power. Classical methods such as
the Mantel–Haenszel and Suissa–Shuster tests remain robust but may forgo efficiency gains
from covariate adjustment. Overall, our results indicate that much of the observed Type I
error inflation reflects misalignment between estimand and variance estimation rather than
small sample size alone. Based on these results, we provide practical recommendations to
guide method selection that align the estimand, variance estimation, and inferential target.
Keywords: Risk differences, Covariate adjustment, Estimands, Small-sample inference, g-
computation, Mantel–Haenszel methods, Exact unconditional tests

Introduction
Binary endpoints are widely used in randomized clinical trials, and a common objective is to
compare event probabilities between treatment arms.
Examples include overall response in
oncology and clinical remission in ulcerative colitis trials.
For such comparisons, odds ratios, particularly conditional odds ratios, have long been a pre-
ferred summary measure. Their popularity stems in part from the fact that they can be obtained
straightforwardly from multivariable logistic regression models, in which regression coefficients
represent conditional log odds ratios. Despite their frequent use, odds ratios have well-recognized
limitations and have been subject to criticism with respect to interpretability, clinical mean-
ingfulness, and underlying assumptions.1–3 Odds ratios are difficult to interpret, because they
do not directly quantify differences or ratios of event probabilities and therefore obscure in-
formation about baseline risk. They are often seen as an approximation of the relative risks

of an event, which is only valid when the outcome is relatively uncommon, and they always
exaggerate the strength of the relative risk. Moreover, odds ratios are non-collapsible, i.e. even
when conditional odds ratios are constant across covariate strata, the corresponding marginal
odds ratio generally differs and is trending closer to the null in the presence of any prognostic
factor.4
Recent guidance from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) on adjustment for covari-
ates in randomized clinical trials highlighted the importance of distinguishing between condi-
tional and marginal treatment estimands.5–7 In this context, the terms marginal, unconditional,
and population-average refer to the same population-level treatment effect, defined by averag-
ing over the distribution of baseline covariates.
While marginal and conditional estimands
coincide in linear models without treatment-by-covariate interactions, this equivalence gener-
ally breaks down in nonlinear models, such as logistic regression for binary endpoints due to
non-collapsibility. Although conditional effects are, in principle, most relevant from an indi-
vidual patient perspective in a clinical setting, they depend on a correctly specified model and
can therefore generally not be estimated reliably in a clinical trial.
From a regulatory and
public-health perspective, marginal estimands align most directly with the objective of quan-
tifying average treatment benefit in the target population. It is also the estimand targeted
by an unadjusted analysis. The FDA guidance favors approaches that provide valid inference
under approximately the same minimal statistical assumptions as unadjusted analyses, which
themselves are considered acceptable. Conditional estimands, in the FDA guidance on covari-
ate adjustment, are framed primarily as supportive, exploratory, or subgroup-specific targets
rather than default choices for primary inference. The focus on population-level summaries is
consistent with the ICH E9(R1) estimand framework, which requires explicit prespecification
of the population-level summary measure aligned with the clinical question of interest before
choosing the estimator and estimation method.8
As a consequence of the limitations of (conditional) odds ratios and stimulated by the recent
FDA guidance, there has been increasing interest in assessing risk differences.
Unlike odds
ratios, they directly quantify absolute differences in event proportions and convey both base-
line risk and treatment impact on an absolute scale. Further, they are collapsible and avoid
interpretational ambiguities. Despite conceptual simplicity, estimation and inference for risk dif-
ferences are not necessarily straightforward in the presence of covariates. Statistical methods to
assess covariate-adjusted risk differences in clinical trials include Cochran-Mantel–Haenszel ap-
proaches (limited to categorical covariates),9–11 and various implementations of g-computation
(standardization),12,13 and inverse probability of treatment weighting.14,15 These methods may
differ with respect to targeted estimands, reliance on modeling assumptions and computational
complexity, and finite-sample behavior. As an alternative, one may forgo potential efficiency
gains from covariate adjustment and apply (unconditional) statistical tests.16,17
The challenges in assessing covariate-adjusted risk differences warrant closer scrutiny in small-
sample settings. Trials with total sample sizes of N ≤150 are common in phase II development,
and, more broadly, in rare diseases and pediatric indications, where feasibility constraints limit
enrollment. First, many applied inferential procedures are asymptotically valid but provide
no guarantees in finite samples.18 In small trials, adjusted risk difference estimators may ex-
hibit bias, non-normal sampling distributions, variance underestimation, miscalibrated p-values,
and confidence interval undercoverage.
For example, parametric variance estimators for g-
computation based on the delta method, as well as variance estimators for the Mantel–Haenszel
risk difference, are derived under maximum-likelihood theory assuming asymptotic normality,
which in turn relies on large-sample properties. Similarly, the Cochran-Mantel–Haenszel test
is based on a statistic that is asymptotically χ2-distributed and therefore requires a minimum
amount of information per stratum to perform as intended. When sample sizes are small, events
are sparse, or stratification leads to very small strata, these conditions may not be met. In such

settings, degrees-of-freedom penalties and numerical instability could offset potential gains from
stratification. Second, g-computation approaches for binary endpoints depend on a fitted mul-

non-convergence in small samples. Under these circumstances, the comparative advantage of
covariate-adjusted approaches over simpler unadjusted methods remains uncertain.
To address these concerns, we present a simulation-based comparative evaluation of a range of
methods for estimating and testing risk differences in randomized clinical trials, particularly
exact unconditional testing, Mantel–Haenszel methods, and g-computation (standardization)
approaches.
Our focus is on small-sample settings, where misalignment between the target
estimand, point estimator, and variance estimator is most likely to be revealed, and that have
been studied only to a limited extent. Our intent is to inform statistical practice by identifying
approaches that target the intended estimand, exhibit robust inferential performance in small
samples, and strike a balance between efficiency, robustness, and interpretability.

trial that serves as a running example and basis for the subsequent simulation study and we

interval coverage, across multiple scenarios spanning different sample sizes and effect sizes. In
Section 5, we report the results of the simulation study. Finally, in Section 6, we provide a
discussion of the results, offer concrete practical recommendations for statisticians, and outline
potential avenues for future research.

Hypothetical trial example and potential estimands
Consider a hypothetical two-arm randomized controlled trial with parallel groups, comparing
an investigational treatment (A = 1) with a control (A = 0). Let Y ∈{0, 1} denote a binary
primary endpoint indicating treatment response, where Y = 1 corresponds to response. The
parameter of primary interest is the average treatment effect on the risk scale, defined as the
risk difference
RD = Pr(Y = 1 | A = 1) −Pr(Y = 1 | A = 0).
The trial objective is to assess superiority of the investigational treatment with respect to this
estimand. This entails a two-sided hypothesis test

female}) and disease severity (X2 ∈{mild, severe}). These covariates may be used as stratifica-
tion factors in the randomization. Covariate adjustment for X is generally advisable to improve
statistical efficiency while targeting the same marginal estimand RD.
A critical step in selecting an appropriate statistical method for analysis is the clear definition
of the estimand. An estimand provides a precise and comprehensive specification of what the
trial aims to estimate, encompassing the treatment under investigation, the target population,
the endpoint, the handling of intercurrent events, and the summary measure used to quantify
the treatment effect.8,19,20 Each of these components is important; however, in this work we
focus on the choice of the summary measure and therefore use the term estimand in this broader
sense.

To formalize different estimands of interest, we introduce potential outcomes notation. Let Y (A)
denote the potential outcome for an individual under treatment assignment A, where A = 1
represents the investigational treatment and A = 0 represents control. Within the potential
outcomes framework,21 the treatment effect is defined as a contrast between the outcomes
that would be observed for the same individual under each treatment condition. In a two-
arm randomized controlled trial, only one of the two potential outcomes is observed for each
individual, while the counterfactual outcome remains unobserved.

focus on three estimands that are particularly relevant in the context of clinical trials, following
Magirr et al.22 We present these estimands in terms of risk differences. However, the same
estimands can equivalently be defined with respect to other contrasts of interest, such as risk

subtle. Magirr et al.22 argue that there is no universally correct choice between these esti-

estimator, confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests, which must be aligned with the chosen es-
timand. Rather than advocating for a single default estimand, we emphasize the importance of
acknowledging the diversity of clinically meaningful estimands, clearly communicating the cho-
sen definition, and ensuring alignment between the estimand, the estimator, and the inferential
procedure.

3.1
Suissa-Shuster test
The first approach we consider is the exact unconditional test for comparing two independent
binomial proportions proposed by Suissa and Shuster.17 Although it does not accommodate
covariate adjustment, it serves as an important benchmark for evaluating the finite-sample
properties of covariate-adjusted procedures, particularly with respect to Type I error control
and power.
The Suissa–Shuster test provides a principled alternative to two common approaches in this
setting: asymptotic tests (e.g., z- or χ2 tests) and conditional tests (e.g., Fisher’s exact test23).
Unlike asymptotic tests, it does not rely on large-sample approximations. Instead, inference
is based on the exact finite-sample distribution of the test statistic, deemed to maintain the
nominal Type I error rate even in small samples.17
Unlike Fisher’s exact test, it does not
condition on fixed marginal totals. We include the Suissa-Shuster test because it is an exact
unconditional procedure constructed directly on the risk-difference scale and therefore aligns
the hypothesis test with the estimand of interest. By contrast, other exact unconditional tests,
such as the Boschloo test, are based on an ordering induced by Fisher p-values and are only
indirectly connected to inference on the risk difference.16
To briefly outline the mathematical underpinnings and assumptions of the exact unconditional
test, recall that the statistical hypothesis of interest is on the risk difference, i.e., the difference
between two independent binomial probabilities. Let π1 = Pr(Y (1) = 1) and π0 = Pr(Y (0) =

3.2.1
Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test
Another method that we consider falls under the umbrella of Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel (CMH)
methods.9,11 CMH methods are explicitly mentioned in the FDA guidance on covariate adjust-

ment.5 They encompass a family of stratified tests assessing the association between row and
column variables across multiple 2 × 2 tables. However, it is in fact important to distinguish
between Cochran’s statistic and Mantel–Haenszel statistics (for a comprehensive overview of
the specific assumptions underlying the procedures, see Qiu et al.24).
The test we consider here is commonly referred to as CMH test, although it is more accurately
described as Mantel–Haenszel (MH) test.24 Originally proposed as a test of conditional inde-
pendence across a set of 2 × 2 tables, it is used in clinical trials to evaluate whether the null
hypothesis of no treatment effect holds uniformly in all of the strata. This null hypothesis can
equivalently be expressed in terms of a common conditional odds ratio, relative risk, or risk dif-
ference. Thus, in the present context, the test is formulated in terms of the strict null hypothesis
that the conditional risk difference in each stratum is zero, rather than the marginal risk differ-
ence across strata. Accordingly, the CMH/MH test is derived under a conditional framework
and tests the null hypothesis of no treatment effect within each stratum. For collapsible effect
measures such as the risk difference, this stratum-wise null coincides with the marginal null
hypothesis of no average treatment effect, even though the test is derived conditionally, whereas
for non-collapsible measures the test corresponds to inference on a conditional estimand. This
distinction is reflected in the FDA guidance, which endorses CMH methods when interest lies
in estimating a conditional treatment effect assumed to be constant across subgroups.5

The CMH/MH test is commonly used in clinical trials. It allows for covariate adjustment by

stratum-wise null hypothesis coincides with the marginal null, even though the test itself is not
a marginal procedure in the estimand-framework sense.
3.2.2
Mantel-Haenszel risk difference
We consider another member of the CMH family, namely the Mantel–Haenszel (MH) risk dif-
ference estimator. As the name suggests, in contrast to the MH test, this method is concerned
with estimation rather than hypothesis testing and, more importantly, can be shown to target
a different estimand. While the MH test evaluates the strict null hypothesis of no treatment
effect within any stratum—corresponding to a stratum-wise, conditional null—the MH risk dif-
ference estimator is often assumed to target the same estimand by averaging stratum-specific
risk differences under a homogeneity assumption (δs = δ for all strata). However, as recently
demonstrated, this strong homogeneity assumption is not required.24
Instead, the MH risk
difference estimator can be interpreted as targeting an average treatment effect across strata,
even in the presence of effect heterogeneity.25 Depending on whether the covariates are consid-

3.3
G-Computation
The g-computation (or standardization) approach is a general strategy for estimating causal
treatment effects by combining an outcome regression model with an explicit averaging step over
the covariate distribution.6,27,28 In a randomized trial, randomization identifies the marginal
mean potential outcomes E{Y (a)}, and standardization estimates these means by predicting
outcomes under each treatment level and averaging the predictions over the empirical distribu-
tion of baseline covariates in the study population.

While the above procedure describes the construction of the standardized point estimator of
the marginal treatment effect ˆδ, g-computation methods differ primarily in how they estimate
Var(ˆδ), construct tests and confidence intervals. A common approach is Wald-type inference
based on delta-method variance estimators that treat ˆδ as a smooth function of estimated re-
gression parameters and account for their estimation uncertainty via sandwich formulas. Early
implementations rely on model-based delta-method variances (e.g., Ge et al.33), whereas more
recent work derives model-robust sandwich estimators using stacked estimating equations or
influence-function representations, yielding valid inference under randomization even when the
outcome working model is misspecified.13,30,34 An alternative class of variance estimators is
based on efficient or augmented influence functions for the standardized estimand; these esti-
mators can offer improved robustness but may exhibit non-negligible finite-sample sensitivity,
particularly when the working model is poorly behaved.31 As a further alternative, nonpara-
metric bootstrap procedures are frequently used to approximate the sampling distribution of ˆδ,
and are straightforward to implement in practice. However, like the asymptotic variance esti-
mators mentioned above, standard bootstrap schemes are formally justified only under simple
randomization, with modifications required for covariate-adaptive or restricted randomization
designs.6,35 Small-sample performance can be sensitive to the inferential choice. Wald tests are
known to perform poorly when sample sizes are small or when estimated probabilities are near
the boundary, motivating alternatives such as robust score-test constructions for g-computation
estimators.31 Penalized working logistic models, e.g. using Firth’s method, can mitigate sep-
aration and small-sample instability; however at the cost of altering the estimation procedure
and potentially the targeted estimand.36,37

3.3.6
Firth
A final approach included in this work addresses a practical issue that may arise when sample
sizes are small, namely convergence difficulties of the logistic regression working model caused
by separation. In logistic regression models, separation occurs when a covariate or linear com-
bination of covariates perfectly predicts the outcome. Under complete or quasi-separation, the
maximum likelihood estimator does not exist in the usual sense and numerical optimization
fails to converge. Such situations may arise in small-sample situations, especially when event
rates are very low or when adjusting for multiple covariates.
To mitigate these convergence problems, we consider a penalized-likelihood method that com-
bines the bias-reduction procedure of Firth39 with the FLIC modification proposed by Puhr et
al.37 The resulting adjusted working model is a Firth-penalized logistic regression in which the
Jeffreys prior penalty is incorporated through the FLIC adjustment to ensure stable estima-
tion even when standard maximum likelihood fails. The regression coefficients are obtained by
maximizing this modified penalized likelihood, which corrects the infinite-estimate problem in-
troduced by separation while preserving the interpretability of the logistic working model.
Because the g-computation estimator depends only on the fitted linear predictor under each
treatment arm, the Firth adjusted logistic model can be substituted seamlessly for the standard
working model. The fitted values obtained from this penalized regression model then feed into
the usual g-computation procedure to obtain an estimator of the marginal risk difference. For
simplicity, we pair the Firth working model with the model-based conditional variance estimator
by Ge et al., which yields inference on the CPATE, although any valid variance estimator could
in principle be used.33

Simulation design
To evaluate the comparative performance of the ten statistical approaches described above, we
conducted an extensive simulation study.
4.1
Settings
The simulation design mirrors the hypothetical clinical trial introduced in section 2. We consid-
ered a binary endpoint Y and three independent predictor variables: the randomized treatment
indicator A and two binary baseline covariates X1 and X2.
Treatment allocation followed
simple randomization with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The covariates X1 and X2 were generated
from independent Bernoulli distributions with success probability 0.50. The binary response
Y was generated via a multivariable logistic regression model including treatment and both
covariates:
logit{P(Y = 1 | A, X1, X2)} = β0 + βAA + β1X1 + β2X2.
This model matches the working model used for the g-computation approaches and aligns
with the covariate structure used in the MH test and MH risk-difference estimator. Hence, all
methods are evaluated under ideal, correctly specified conditions. Since model misspecification
has already been studied extensively elsewhere (e.g., by Liu and Xi13), we did not incorporate
additional misspecification scenarios.
To control both the marginal response probability under control and the marginal risk difference,
we determined β0 and βA using the iterative bisection algorithm proposed by Austin.40 The
marginal response probability under control was fixed at 0.20, while the marginal risk difference
δ was varied across three scenarios: δ = 0 (no treatment effect), δ = 0.15 (moderate effect), and
δ = 0.30 (large effect). The prognostic strength of the covariates was varied by setting: β1 =
β2 ∈{log(1), log(1.5), log(3)}. The resulting conditional and marginal response probabilities
across all parameter settings are shown in Table 2.
To explore performance under small-sample conditions, the total sample size was varied across
five levels, with N ∈{30, 60, 90, 120, 150}.
In total, the simulation comprised 3 (treatment
effects) × 3 (covariate prognostic strengths) × 5 (sample sizes) = 45 scenarios. For each scenario,
50,000 trial datasets were generated, and all ten methods were applied to each dataset.
Within our simulation framework, the data-generating mechanism was constructed such that the
simulated true marginal risk difference corresponds to the MTE. Consequently, the performance
of all statistical methods is evaluated with respect to this estimand, even though some of the
methods are designed to target alternative estimands, as discussed above.
Given that the
MTE is often of primary interest in clinical trials, it is informative and practically relevant to
assess method performance with respect to this estimand. At the same time, it is important
to take differences in the targeted estimands into account when interpreting the results, as any
discrepancies in performance may reflect an estimand mismatch rather than shortcomings of
the methods themselves.
4.2
Operating characteristics
To compare the statistical methods across the simulation settings described above, we evaluated
a set of operating characteristics. Because our primary interest lies in statistical inference in
the form of hypothesis testing – assessing whether or not a treatment effect exists – the central
metric is the rejection rate. Under the null hypothesis (δ = 0), this corresponds to the Type I
error rate, whereas under alternative scenarios with δ ̸= 0 it denotes statistical power.
13


## Page 14


In addition we examined the quality of point and interval estimation for all methods, excluding
the MH test that does not afford an estimate of the risk difference.
Estimation bias was
quantified as the absolute deviation between the average estimated and the true risk difference,
and precision was assessed using the root mean squared error (RMSE). We further evaluated
the empirical coverage probabilities of nominal 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Together, these
metrics characterize the accuracy and reliability of the estimators beyond their hypothesis-
testing properties.
Further, we investigated potential computational challenges arising in the application of the g-
computation approaches. Logistic regression models fitted within g-computation are susceptible
to non-convergence and data-separation issues, particularly in small samples, when covariates
are highly prognostic, or when too many covariates relative to the sample size are included.
Computational issues like non-convergence impair feasibility, may lead to missingness, and
potentially distort conclusions about the inferential properties of the methods investigated in the
simulation. Yet, such issues are rarely reported or discussed in simulation studies.41 Therefore,
we quantified the frequency of computational issues across all simulation scenarios and examined
their downstream impact on the methods’ inferential characteristics. Thereby, we seek to ensure
that our performance assessment reflects not only the theoretical behavior of the approaches
under ideal conditions but also the practical limitations encountered when applying them in
small-sample trial settings.

Reproducibility

Simulations were con-

5.1
Computational issues
Before evaluating operating characteristics and estimation performance, we first examined po-
tential computational issues associated with the statistical procedures under consideration, with
particular focus on the logistic regression working models used in the g-computation approaches.
A well-known phenomenon in small-sample logistic regression is complete or quasi-complete
separation, which arises when a covariate or a combination of covariates perfectly predicts the
binary outcome. In such situations, parameter estimates may diverge toward infinity, often
accompanied by extremely large variance estimates, even when the optimization algorithm for-
mally converges. Although separation does not necessarily imply non-convergence, it results
in unstable parameter estimates and may compromise the reliability of downstream estimators
that depend on the fitted working model.
Interestingly, non-convergence was rare in our simulation despite small sample sizes considered.
Across all scenarios, only 0.0036% of the simulation runs resulted in a logistic regression model
that failed to converge, all for a total sample size of N = 30. In principle, these cases could be
addressed using penalized likelihood methods such as Firth’s correction or by replacing the g-
computation estimator with a simpler test such as Suissa-Shuster’s unconditional test as fallback
strategies. Given the very low frequency of non-convergence, however, these remedies seem of
14


## Page 15


limited practical relevance. We therefore decided to exclude all simulation runs that ended
in non-convergence. Because non-convergence was extremely rare and restricted to N = 30,
exclusion is likely to have negligible impact on operating characteristics.
In contrast, separation, occurred much more frequently, as shown in Figure 1. For small samples
(N = 30, 60) in the presence of strongly prognostic covariates, separation was observed in up to
more than 30% of simulations. As sample size increased, or when the data-generating treatment
effect was larger, the incidence of separation declined markedly. Nonetheless, in realistic trial
settings where sample sizes may be small and covariates may exhibit substantial prognostic
value, separation remains a non-negligible concern.
When separation is caused by inclusion of covariates in the working model, one possible miti-
gation strategy is to remove problematic predictors, as suggested for example by Liu and Xi.13
Importantly, however, our simulation results indicated that separation attributable to covari-
ates did not materially affect performance of the g-computation approaches. When separation
is driven by the treatment indicator, by contrast, it reflects an extremely strong observed treat-
ment effect and typically affects all methods, often resulting in inflated Type I error rates.
In such cases, excluding covariates does not alleviate the issue. For these reasons, we opted
to retain all converged simulation runs, regardless of whether separation was present, when
evaluating the performance of the g-computation estimators.

5.2
Type I error rate
Across all scenarios with a true risk difference of zero, the observed Type I error rates exhibit
a consistent pattern driven primarily by sample size and, to a lesser extent, by the prognostic
strength of the included covariates (Figure 2, top row).
At the smallest sample size (N = 30), several g-computation approaches show pronounced
Type I error inflation, particularly those relying on the (semi-)parametric variance estimators
proposed by Ge et al. and Ye et al. Importantly, this inflation reflects variance underestimation
rather than a failure of g-computation per se. The g-computation approaches based on the

non-parametric bootstrap and the score test exhibit slightly smaller, but still clearly inflated
Type I error rates relative to the nominal level. In contrast, the approaches proposed by Liu
and Xi and the Firth-based g-computation method behave conservatively, with Type I error
rates well below 0.05 in very small samples. As sample size increases, Type I error rates for
all g-computation methods steadily converge toward the nominal level, with most approaches
falling within an acceptable range around 0.05 by N ≥120.
For the Mantel–Haenszel (MH) risk-difference estimator, the variance estimators proposed by
Sato et al. and Qiu et al. display highly similar operating characteristics across all scenarios. At
small sample sizes, both variants exhibit clear Type I error inflation, reaching levels comparable
to those observed for the bootstrap-based g-computation approach. This inflation diminishes
steadily as sample size increases and approaches the nominal level by N ≥120. As for the
other methods, the rate of convergence toward nominal Type I error control depends on the
prognostic strength of the covariates, with accelerated convergence observed when covariates
are more strongly prognostic.
The MH test maintains Type I error control close to the nominal level across all sample sizes
and covariate settings, with only minor fluctuations around 0.05. The Suissa–Shuster exact
unconditional test is consistently the most conservative procedure, yielding Type I error rates
below 0.05 across all scenarios, although these gradually approach the nominal level as sample
size increases.
Taken together, these results suggest that (i) many g-computation approaches are liberal in very
small samples irrespective of covariate prognostic strength, (ii) robust or penalized variants, such
as those proposed by Liu and Xi or based on Firth’s correction, mitigate Type I error inflation
at the cost of conservatism, and (iii) the MH and Suissa–Shuster tests remain robust, with the
latter being characteristically conservative.

context of non-linear endpoints and both continuous and discrete covariates. Much of the ex-

such as the work by Liu and Xi,13 explicitly addressing also small-sample settings. For clinical
trials with limited sample sizes, this represents a conceptual and practical challenge. First, it is
unclear how well asymptotic approximations perform in the context of sparse data (see Magirr
et al.22) Second, g-computation relies on a logistic regression working model that may fail to
converge or whose parameter estimates may be unreliable due to complete or quasi-separation.
These concerns naturally raise the question of whether g-computation bears any practical advan-
tage over comparatively simpler methods such as unconditional tests or CMH-type procedures
in small-sample settings. From a design-based perspective, however, regression-based covariate
adjustment itself is justified by randomization and does not rely on correct specification of the
working outcome model; the main risks arise from inappropriate variance estimation rather
than from adjustment per se.42

To address this question, we conducted a comprehensive simulation study comparing ten sta-
tistical procedures within the context of two-arm randomized controlled trials. Our evaluation
focused on critical aspects of small-sample performance, including the frequency of computa-
tional problems, control of Type I error, statistical power, and the accuracy and precision of
treatment-effect estimation.
Across the investigated scenarios, non-convergence of logistic regression models was rare, even
under the smallest sample sizes. In contrast, separation leading to inflated variance estimates
occurred with appreciable frequency when sample sizes were extremely small.
Importantly,
though, the negative impact of separation on the performance of affected methods was limited
and even with total sample sizes as small as 60 patients, g-computation generally yielded reliable
results. Nevertheless, we believe that practitioners should remain aware of potential compu-
tational anomalies and consider robust alternatives such as a Firth logistic regression working
model or an assumption-lean procedure like the Suissa-Shuster test when necessary.
Regarding inferential properties, our results revealed considerable heterogeneity among meth-
ods. Several approaches exhibited marked inflation of Type I error rates in small samples, most
prominently g-computation with the variance estimators proposed by Ge et al. and Ye et al.,
consistent with recent observations in the literature.13 It is important to keep in mind, however,
that our simulation framework evaluated inferential properties with respect to the MTE, which
is not the targeted estimand for all of the methods considered. For example, g-computation
with the variance estimator of Ge et al. targets the CPATE rather than the MTE. Thus, the
observed inflation in Type I error for this approach may reflect the estimand mismatch rather
than a fundamental deficiency of the method in the context of small samples (see also Magirr
et al.22).
Score-test-based inference and the non-parametric bootstrap also showed elevated error rates at
the smallest sample sizes, although both converged more quickly toward nominal levels as sample
sizes increased. Performance of the MH risk-difference estimator may be located somewhere in
between, with moderate Type I error inflation that diminished with increasing sample size.
In contrast, both the Suissa-Shuster exact unconditional test and the MH test demonstrated
excellent to conservative Type I error control across all scenarios, though this robustness came
at the cost of relatively smaller power. This is true in particular for the Suissa-Shuster test
that does not incorporate any covariates and is thus insensitive to the availability of prognostic
baseline information.
Finally, the more conservative g-computation approaches – namely those using the robust vari-
ance estimator of Liu and Xi or a Firth logistic regression working model – exhibited conser-
vative Type I error behavior, especially in the context of very small samples, and accordingly
reduced power, but excellent coverage. The Firth-based approach did, however, introduce a
characteristic downward bias when the true risk difference was non-zero, a known consequence
of shrinkage in predicted response probabilities toward 0.50.37
Nevertheless, both methods
delivered the most reliable interval estimates under small-sample conditions, highlighting the
familiar trade-offs between Type I error and statistical power, as well as between estimation
bias and variance.
Our findings complement earlier work demonstrating the efficiency benefits of standardization-
based estimators under randomization.43 While these results establish consistency and asymp-
totic efficiency of marginal estimators, our simulations show that in small samples, inferential
validity hinges critically on aligning the variance estimator with the estimand. Our findings are
consistent with recent comparative work showing that, in randomized trials, covariate adjust-
ment can improve efficiency but that inferential performance, particularly variance estimation
and CI coverage, depends critically on modeling assumptions and is sensitive to misspecifica-
tion.44

6.2
Practical recommendations
A central aim of this work is to devise practical recommendations for the analysis of small-sample
clinical trials with binary endpoints. Our findings reveal an unsettling, yet not unexpected
insight: no single statistical method dominates across all relevant performance dimensions.
Instead, the choice of a method necessarily entails balancing competing considerations such
as computational stability, control of Type I error, statistical power, and the accuracy and
precision of treatment-effect estimation. These characteristics must be weighed carefully in light
of the specific trial objectives, particularly the estimand of interest and the role of prognostic
covariates.
When strict Type I error control for inference on the MTE is of paramount importance, our
results highlight several viable options.
The Suissa-Shuster exact unconditional test stands
out as a highly robust procedure that maintains nominal error control across all evaluated
settings, albeit at the cost of not leveraging prognostic covariates and, consequently, reduced
power. The appeal of such unconditional, design-based procedures lies in their transparency and
minimal reliance on modeling assumptions, a perspective emphasized in critiques of regression
adjustment in randomized experiments.45
Alternatively, to leverage prognostic information
from categorical covariates, the CMH test also demonstrates reliable Type I error control. Its
applicability may be limited, however, if sparse data result in too small cell counts or continuous
covariates should be included in the analysis.10 For these cases, robust g-computation combined
with the Liu and Xi variance estimator or a Firth logistic regression working model offers a
principled route to maintaining conservative error rates. These methods remain reliable even
under pronounced small-sample conditions at the cost of reduced statistical power, although
the advantage of covariate adjustment becomes evident once the sample size reaches moderate
levels (N ≥90).
For settings in which a balance between Type I error control and power is desired, our re-
sults suggest that g-computation combined with either the recently proposed score test or
a non-parametric bootstrap procedure provides an appealing compromise. Both approaches
exhibit moderate Type I error inflation for sample sizes ≥60 – less inflation than the MH
risk-difference estimator with either of the two considered variance estimators while achieving
substantially higher power. This performance is particularly notable given that our simula-
tion scenarios were limited to categorical covariates; when continuous prognostic covariates are
present, we anticipate that the efficiency gain of these g-computation methods will be even more
pronounced.
When the primary focus is on accuracy in estimation rather than hypothesis testing, consid-
erations shift toward bias and variance. Nearly all evaluated methods that produce a point
estimate yield unbiased estimators, with the exception of g-computation based on a Firth lo-
gistic regression working model. The bias introduced by this procedure is intentional through
the inclusion of a regularizing penalty term, but it is accompanied by reduced variance and
consistently lower RMSE across all scenarios, as well as excellent confidence-interval coverage.
Liu and Xi’s approach similarly provides very strong coverage performance without introducing
bias. Other methods, in contrast, show moderate to large undercoverage in small samples, with
g-computation approaches based on the score test or non-parametric bootstrap achieving the
closest approximation to nominal coverage levels in the considered sample-size range.
6.3
Consistency between estimation, testing, and confidence intervals
From a regulatory and scientific reporting perspective, it is desirable that a single analytical
framework yields a coherent trio consisting of (i) a point estimator, (ii) a hypothesis test, and
(iii) a confidence interval that are all aligned with the same estimand and based on compatible
inferential logic. Inconsistencies between these components, such as testing one estimand while

## Page 24


estimating another, or reporting confidence intervals that are not the inversion of the primary
test, can complicate interpretation, undermine transparency, and raise concerns in regulatory
review. Unadjusted and stratified design-based procedures illustrate these limitations clearly.
In contrast, g-computation provides a unifying framework in which estimation, testing, and
interval estimation are all derived from the same underlying standardized estimand. The stan-
dardized risk-difference estimator ˆδ = ˆπ1−ˆπ0 is well defined, interpretable on the marginal scale,
and applicable to arbitrary covariate structures. The remaining question is how inference is con-
structed around this estimator in finite samples. Among the explored approached, two stood
out as providing internally consistent inference within the g-computation framework: robust
variance-based Wald inference targeting the MTE, as in Liu and Xi,,13 and score-test–based
inference, as proposed by Zhang et al.31 The principal value of g-computation in small-sample
trials is not adjustment per se, but coherence. When paired with an inferential procedure that
respects the estimand and accounts for finite-sample uncertainty, g-computation offers a prin-
cipled and credible route to reporting risk differences that integrates estimation, testing, and
uncertainty quantification within a single framework.
6.4
Limitations and future directions
There are limitations of our study that merit consideration and point toward avenues for future
research.
First, our simulations considered only categorical baseline covariates. This choice ensured that
a controlled comparison across methods is possible, where differences in performance cannot be
attributed to differences in the set of considered covariates. Nevertheless, it necessarily restricts
the generalizability of our findings. In practical applications, continuous prognostic covariates
are common, and their inclusion may alter the relative performance of the approaches considered
herein. In particular, we expect the efficiency gains of g-computation relative to CMH-type
procedures to be more pronounced when continuous covariates can be fully exploited without
discretization (see Ge et al.).33)
Second, we restricted the simulated scenarios to correctly specified models in the sense that
all covariate-adjusted procedures included the same set of covariates used for data generation.
Because our primary aim was to compare methods rather than evaluate them on an absolute
scale, this design choice ensured a level playing field across approaches. Nevertheless, model
misspecification is an omnipresent concern in applied analyses.44 The relative robustness of
the methods – the g-computation variants in particular – to omitted covariates, incorrect link
functions, or interactions not specified in the working model thus remains to be addressed.
Third, a further methodological dimension concerns the randomization scheme. Our simula-
tions were based on simple randomization, which is arguably not the most prevalent choice
in modern clinical trials. Instead, stratified or covariate-adaptive randomization schemes that
are implemented to balance treatment assignment across key covariates are increasingly ap-
plied in practice. Variance estimators that appropriately account for stratified randomization
are available, e.g., Wang et al.,46 Bannick et al.,34 or Zhang et al.,31 and may further im-
prove efficiency of statistical inference. Future work should therefore evaluate how stratified
or covariate-adaptive randomization and appropriate strategies to adopt estimators accordingly
affect the comparative performance of different statistical methods.
Fourth, finite-sample corrections for Wald-type inference based on sandwich variance estimators,
such as the bias-adjusted variance and F-reference distributions proposed by Fay and Graubard,
could in principle be applied to g-computation estimators, but require nontrivial choices regard-
ing the effective number of independent contributions and parameter-specific degrees of freedom,
and are not routinely used.47 Closely related, Tsiatis et al. note that sandwich-based variance
estimators for covariate-adjusted estimators can be downward biased in small samples and pro-

## Page 25


pose multiplicative variance inflation factors that depend on sample size and the number of
fitted regression parameters.28 A systematic evaluation of such corrections for marginal risk
differences would constitute a separate investigation and was beyond the scope of the present
study, which instead focused on methods with established design-based or randomization-based
inferential guarantees.
Finally, several relevant approaches were not included in the present comparison. Most no-
tably, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and targeted maximum likelihood
estimation (TMLE) were not considered.15,29,48 Previous work has shown that robust standard
errors for IPTW were anti-conservative in small samples.44,49 A number of related methods,
such as overlap weighting, doubly robust estimating-equation approaches, and higher-order
influence-function estimators, were also omitted. Their implementation, tuning, and inferential
targets differ materially and would require separate, dedicated investigation. We further did not
consider the approach proposed by Klingenberg, which targets risk differences under specific
stratification and homogeneity assumptions and is closely related to MH-type constructions rep-
resented in our study.50 Extending the simulation to include these methods would substantially
broaden the scope and might have obscured a more focused comparison.
6.5
Conclusion
This study provides a large simulation-based evaluation of methods for covariate-adjusted in-
ference on risk differences in small-sample randomized clinical trials. Rather than identifying a
universally superior procedure, our results show that each has a distinct profile of advantages and
disadvantages with respect to computational stability, Type I error control, statistical power,
and estimation accuracy. Simple, highly robust procedures such as unconditional exact tests en-
sure strict error control but forego the efficiency gains available through covariate adjustment,
whereas g-computation methods can capitalize on prognostic information yet require careful
selection of variance estimators and working models to perform reliably in small samples. Our
findings underscore the importance of selecting statistical methods that are explicitly aligned
with the estimand of interest, the inferential objective, and operational constraints of the trial.
When this alignment is achieved, covariate-adjusted analyses can deliver coherent point esti-
mates, tests, and confidence intervals that are consistent with modern estimand-focused regula-
tory guidance. Overall, our results reinforce that method selection should be guided primarily
by the estimand of interest and the inferential framework, rather than by covariate adjustment
alone. By clarifying the trade-offs and providing empirically grounded properties and recom-
mendations, this work supports principled and transparent analysis choices for small-sample
clinical trials.

Appendix
A.1
Software implementation
This appendix summarizes practical aspects of software implementation for the statistical meth-
ods described and discussed in the main text.
The focus is on commonly used and well-
maintained R packages that were also employed in our simulation study, with brief reference to
alternative implementations. The listed packages and functions are not intended to provide an
exhaustive overview of available software.
A.1.1
Exact unconditional testing
The Suissa–Shuster exact unconditional test for comparing two independent binomial propor-
tions is implemented in R through the Exact package. The function exact2x2() provides direct
support for unconditional tests on the risk difference scale.
A.1.2
Mantel-Haenszel methods
Mantel–Haenszel tests and Mantel–Haenszel risk difference estimators are available in standard
statistical software.
In R, the CMH test can be carried out using the mantelhaen.test()
function from the stats package. Estimation of the Mantel–Haenszel risk difference and as-
sociated variance estimators can be implemented using custom code or via routines in SAS
(PROC FREQ), where both the classical and modified Greenland–Robins variance estimators are
available.
A.1.3
G-computation
Model-based variance estimation
For practical applications in which a conditional es-
timand such as the CPATE is appropriate, g-computation combined with the model-based
variance estimator of Ge et al. can be implemented straightforwardly using standard regression
output.33 The authors provide working examples in both SAS and R in the appendix of their
publication. In R, this approach can be implemented without custom code for variance esti-
mation using the margins package, which computes standardized (marginal) predictions from
generalized linear models together with delta method standard errors based on the model-based
covariance matrix of the fitted regression coefficients.51 This implementation treats the empir-
ical covariate distribution as fixed and yields inference conditional on the observed covariates.
The approach by Ge et al. is also implemented in the R package beeca.52
Robust variance estimation
Robust variance estimators for g-computation targeting the
marginal treatment effect are available through several R packages. The variance estimator pro-
posed by Liu and Xi can be implemented using a combination of the margins and sandwich
29


## Page 30


packages, replacing the model-based covariance matrix with a heteroskedasticity-consistent es-
timator and adding the covariate-distribution variability term.13,51,53 The authors provide R
code illustrating the implementation.
The variance estimator of Ye et al. is implemented in the RobinCar2 package and the beeca,
which supports covariate-adjusted estimation and inference for two-arm randomized trials un-
der simple randomization.30,52,54
The RobinCar2 package provides functions to compute
standardized estimators of marginal means and associated variance estimators derived from
influence-function representations.
Bootstrap-based
Non-parametric bootstrap inference for g-computation can be implemented
using base R functionality in conjunction with standard generalized linear model fitting routines.
Steingrimsson et al. provide reference implementations in both R and SAS in their supplemen-
tary materials.35 The bootstrap procedure requires repeated refitting of the working outcome
model and recomputation of standardized risk differences across bootstrap samples.
Score-test-based inference
The score-test-based procedure proposed by Zhang et al. can be
implemented using fitted values from a standard logistic regression working model and any valid
variance estimator for the standardized risk difference.31 The authors provide reference R code
in their supplementary materials. The implementation does not require numerical inversion of
likelihoods and relies only on closed-form expressions for the test statistic and corresponding
confidence intervals.
Penalized logistic regression
Firth-penalized logistic regression models are implemented
in R via the logistf package.55 Fitted values from the penalized model can be used directly
within the g-computation framework in place of maximum-likelihood-based predictions. For
inference, the penalized working model can be paired with either model-based or robust variance
estimators, depending on the estimand of interest.
