Asymptotic Rank Speedup Theorems, Revisited

Abstract
Motivated by fast matrix multiplication and recent connections between asymptotic tensor
rank and fine-grained complexity, we revisit classical tools from the matrix multiplication litera-
ture and develop a framework for obtaining improved asymptotic rank upper bounds for tensors
beyond matrix multiplication.
In the 1980s, Coppersmith–Winograd and Strassen discovered a series of speedup theorems
for asymptotic rank: in certain regimes, one can extract additional terms from a border rank
upper bound on a tensor T, and then use these terms to obtain an improved asymptotic rank of
T. We establish general speedup theorems that subsume these results and enable quantitative
improvements. Two representative applications are:
(1) The asymptotic rank of the small Coppersmith–Winograd tensor cwq is less than its border
rank. For instance, we prove R

degeneration data into explicit asymptotic rank bounds using Strassen’s theory of the asymptotic
spectrum.

Introduction
The exponent of matrix multiplication, ω, is defined as the smallest real number such that two nˆn
matrices can be multiplied using at most nω`op1q field operations. Because of the fundamental and
widespread applications of matrix multiplication algorithms, determining the value of ω has become
one of the most important open problems in computer science. Strassen’s famous algorithm [Str69]
showed the first nontrivial bound ω ď log2p7q ă 2.81. Since then, a long line of follow-up work has
culminated in the current best bound ω ă 2.371339 [ADW`25]. The only known lower bound is the
straightforward ω ě 2, since the input and output sizes are n2. In fact, it is frequently conjectured
that ω “ 2.
It has been known since Strassen’s work [Str73] that determining ω is equivalent to understanding
the asymptotic rank of the matrix multiplication tensor.

Our approach.
In this paper, we revisit the approach of bounding the asymptotic ranks of tensors
via speedup theorems. We establish a generalization of prior speedup theorems, and analyze them
using a method we call Strassen calculus which makes use of Strassen’s theory of the asymptotic
spectrum, in order to give new quantitative asymptotic rank bounds. We will discuss this in much
more detail in Section 2 below, but we first present some applications of our approach.

2.1
Strassen calculus: Bounds via asymptotic spectrum
Traditionally, techniques for bounding the matrix multiplication exponent ω proceed by constructing
a chain of border-rank upper bounds. This requires carefully tracking the orders of magnitude of
intermediate tensors, and typically culminates in a degeneration of the form xn, n, ny Ĳ xry, which
bounds ω via the basic inequality ω ď logn Rpxn, n, nyq. A major drawback is that these arguments
often rely on ad hoc derivation chains. A prominent example is the standard proof of the asymptotic

In Section 4, we introduce Strassen calculus—a systematic framework for deriving bounds from
a fixed collection of degenerations between direct sums of tensors—via Strassen’s theory of the
asymptotic spectrum [Str88, Str91, Str05].
Informally, the asymptotic spectrum provides a family of numerical “measures” for tensors.
Concretely, an element of the asymptotic spectrum X is a function
ϕ : tF-tensorsu Ñ Rě0
that behaves well with respect to the tensor operations. We refer to such a function ϕ as a spectral
point; see Section 4 for the precise definitions.
The key structural statement underlying the theory of the asymptotic spectrum is Strassen
duality (Theorem 4.1). Roughly speaking, it says that asymptotic degeneration relations are exactly
the relations that hold after applying all spectral points: if T asymptotically degenerates to S, then
ϕpTq ď ϕpSq for every ϕ, and conversely, if this inequality holds for all ϕ, then this implies the
existence of the asymptotic degeneration. In particular, this characterizes asymptotic rank via a
max formula,
R
r
pTq “ max
ϕPX ϕpTq.
Conceptually, this turns questions about asymptotic rank into optimization problems over the
asymptotic spectrum.
Of course, the space X of all spectral points is defined nonconstructively and may be infinite-
dimensional a priori, so constructing explicit spectral points is generally difficult, although there
has been significant progress in the past decade [CVZ23, CLZ23, SDW26]. Our approach is therefore
not to “parametrize” X directly. Instead, we start from a fixed collection of tensor degenerations,
translate them into numerical inequalities that must be satisfied by every spectral point, and then
use Strassen duality to turn the resulting constraints into explicit upper bounds on asymptotic
rank.
In favorable regimes one can solve the corresponding optimization problems analytically;

more generally, one can obtain sharp bounds via computer-aided evaluation. Moreover, Strassen
calculus is complete: for a fixed set of degenerations, optimizing over these constraints yields the
best bound obtainable from those relations. In Section A, we provide a rigorous treatment of the
notion of “optimal usage”, and show the completeness under this formalism.
We emphasize that although the theory of the asymptotic spectrum was originally motivated
by the study of tensor rank, its subsequent development has followed a curious trajectory. While
the framework has flourished in other areas such as (quantum) information theory, its application
to the asymptotic rank of tensors has remained largely conceptual. Prior to this work, the theory
occupied a somewhat dormant role in its own founding domain, as to the best of our knowledge, no
practical instances were identified to yield concrete, improved upper bounds.
The following examples illustrate the utility of the framework in other areas, and highlight the
gap between its success elsewhere and its (so far) limited impact on tensor rank:
• Strassen’s Foundation. In his seminal paper [Str88], Strassen introduced the asymptotic spec-
trum to provide a new proof of the asymptotic sum inequality, setting the stage for a theory
that, while foundational, remained difficult to apply to specific new bounds.
• Barriers to Fast Matrix Multiplication. Improvements to the exponent ω over the past thirty
years have largely relied on analyzing the big Coppersmith–Winograd tensor CWq. How-
ever, Christandl, Vrana, and Zuiddam [CVZ21] utilized spectral points to show that the laser
method—even in a highly generalized form—applied on big CW tensors, cannot yield an up-
per bound better than 2.16. Subsequent work [CLGLZ25] has established analogous barriers
for the rectangular case.
• Graph Theory.
Zuiddam [Zui19] applied the general theory of asymptotic spectrum (not
confined to tensors) to study the Shannon capacity ΘpGq of graphs. Wigderson and Zuiddam
[WZ26, Theorem 4.1] utilized the asymptotic spectrum to show a striking equivalence: for
graphs G and H, where G b H and G \ H denote the strong product and disjoint union
respectively, ΘpG b Hq ą ΘpGqΘpHq if and only if ΘpG \ Hq ą ΘpGq ` ΘpHq.1
• Circuit Complexity. Robere and Zuiddam [RZ21] developed an analogous theory to study
and generalize some results in the complexity of catalytic branching programs. Alman and Li
[AL25] applied asymptotic spectrum theory to derive a duality theorem for depth-2 circuits,
resolving the problem of optimally combining fixed constructions in that context.
These developments demonstrate that Strassen duality is a mature and productive framework
in other contexts. Our work aims to re-apply this machinery to its original setting by identifying
concrete tensor degenerations for which it provably yields new, improved upper bounds.

Speedup theorems
In Section 5, we prove a general speedup theorem that subsumes the results of Coppersmith–
Winograd [CW82] and Strassen [Str88, Theorem 3.14]. The Coppersmith–Winograd theorem fits
the following paradigm: starting from a border rank bound T Ĳ xry, one can (in certain regimes)
“speed up” the degeneration by taking a direct sum with a one-slice matrix multiplication tensor on
both sides, obtaining
T ‘ x1, t, 1y Ĳ xry ‘ x1, s, 1y.
Strassen later generalized this by showing that the right-hand side does not necessarily need to
be a diagonal tensor (i.e., a border rank bound). Specifically, a degeneration T Ĳ S can also be
accelerated to T ‘ x1, t, 1y Ĳ S ‘ x1, s, 1y. Our main theorem further generalizes this idea: under
suitable hypotheses, it allows one to add arbitrary tensors on both sides of a degeneration, producing
relations of the form T ‘ T 1 Ĳ S ‘ S1.
In Section 6, we present three concrete instantiations of the general speedup theorem. Each has
different strengths and ranges of applicability; together, they form the main toolkit we use to derive
explicit upper bounds on asymptotic rank.
• The first result relates a certain variant of the min-rank problem to one-slice speedups of the
form T ‘ x1, t, 1y Ĳ S ‘ x1, s, 1y, thereby recovering Strassen’s speedup theorem.
• The second result shows that, in a suitable regime, a degeneration of the form T ‘x1, t, 1y Ĳ S1
can be further accelerated to T ‘ x1, t1, 1y Ĳ S1 for some t1 ą t, while keeping the right-hand
side fixed.
In particular, if one takes an identity obtained from the first result and takes
a tensor power of both sides, this acceleration always applies; thus the first method can be
iterated. This generalizes a result of Coppersmith and Winograd [CW82, Theorem B].

5.4
Direct sum and compression of matrix multiplication
In the proof of Proposition 5.3, we used the following basic fact: for a one-slice matrix multiplication
tensor x1, r, 1y, restricting its first mode to a codimension-p subspace yields a tensor isomorphic to
x1, r ´ p, 1y. Although this observation looks elementary, it can be viewed as the simplest instance
of a much more general phenomenon.
Strassen proved the following result (see [Str88, Proposition 6.4])3: if T is a direct sum of matrix
multiplication tensors and A Ď U _ is a subspace of small codimension, then the partially restricted
tensor pA b idV b idW qT still contains, as a restriction, a “valuable” tensor T 1 that is again a direct
sum of matrix multiplication tensors. Informally, matrix multiplication is robust under losing a small
amount of information in one mode. This is often referred to as the compression property of matrix
multiplication tensors; see [WZ26, Part III] for an exposition and generalizations.
Algorithmic
versions have also been developed recently [HS25a, HS25b].
For our purposes, we only need a compression statement in the special case where all summands
are one-slice tensors. This case is simple enough that we include a short proof for the reader’s
convenience.
We also note that the concrete applications in this paper do not require the full
generality of this theorem: the tensors and linear maps we use enjoy additional structure that
simplifies the analysis. Nonetheless, we expect that the compression statement in this general form
may be useful for more complicated degenerations in the future, for instance when the relevant
linear maps are found via computer search.
