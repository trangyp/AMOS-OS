Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increas-
ingly used in decision-making tasks where
they can amplify or suppress perspectives,
raising concerns in high-stakes settings af-
fecting autistic communities.
While previ-
ous research has identified disability-related
biases in LLMs, it remains unclear how they
conceptualize ableism or detect it in text.
We introduce a bias-aware evaluation frame-
work targeting anti-autistic ableist language
with a psychometrically-weighted, community-
proximate ground truth anchored in annota-
tor positionality. This framework constitutes
a stricter standard than conventional majority-
vote aggregation which significantly and con-
sistently underweights autistic and autism-
accepting perspectives. We find that LLMs
frequently produce harmful outputs, mislabel
community-reclaimed language as ableist, and
express more negative attitudes toward autis-
tic people when assessment instruments are
masked. Our error analysis reveals that models
rely on surface-level keyword matching rather
than contextual factors such as speaker iden-
tity, and whether the language fosters in-group
solidarity or inflicts out-group harm.
Trigger warning: this paper contains ableist lan-
guage including explicit slurs and references to
violence.

Introduction
Despite the growth in using large language mod-
els (LLMs) for content moderation and other
decision-making pipelines where they can suppress
or amplify community perspectives, prior work
shows that LLMs reproduce societal biases, in-
cluding those related to disability and autism, in
downstream applications such as resume screening
(Schramowski et al., 2022; Glazko et al., 2024).
However, their ability to recognize and reason
about ableism remains underexplored.
Social biases such as ableism shape how individ-
uals and communities are represented, described,
and evaluated in text (Campbell, 2009; Wolbring,
2008). Like other forms of social bias, ableist lan-
guage ranges from overt slurs to subtle pathologiz-
ing framings that may not be recognized as harmful
without community-grounded knowledge (Cepol-
laro et al., 2026; Bottema-Beutel et al., 2021; Kapp,
2019). This creates challenges for both human
annotators and LLMs, with implications for unin-
tended censorship and misclassification (Fortuna
and Nunes, 2018; Waseem and Hovy, 2016). De-
tecting anti-autistic ableist speech is particularly
challenging as it requires awareness of (1) the spe-
cific historical and ongoing social biases against
autistic individuals, including dehumanization and
persistent deficit-based narratives in contemporary
discourse (Kapp, 2019; Rizvi et al., 2024); and
(2) AI research framing autism as a problem to
be diagnosed, cured, or used as an analogy for
model behavior (Cho et al., 2023; Attanasio et al.,
2024; Ciobanu et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024). Fur-
ther, the harmfulness of autism-related language is
context-dependent in a distinctive way: the same
term (e.g., “autie,” “aspie”) can be ableist when
used by an outsider and self-affirming when re-
claimed within autistic communities (Osorio, 2020;
Cepollaro et al., 2026). This insider/outsider dis-
tinction creates systematic misclassification risks
for both human annotators and automated systems,
with consequences for unintended censorship of
community speech (Waseem and Hovy, 2016; For-
tuna and Nunes, 2018).
Detecting anti-autistic ableist speech requires:(1)
a ground truth that up-weights community-
proximate annotators (higher autistic-trait scores,
lower implicit bias) over majority-vote aggrega-
tion, and (2) an analysis of reasoning to un-
derstand whether models attend to contextual
factors (speaker identity, community member-
ship, reclaimed language) or surface-level cues.

Psychometric-informed annotations allow us to
weigh annotator labels according to their measured
bias levels and autistic trait scores, treating anno-
tator identity as a meaningful variable rather than
noise to be averaged away (Aroyo and Welty, 2015;
Uma et al., 2021; Sap et al., 2021; Davani et al.,
2022). The reasoning gap we investigate helps un-
cover whether LLMs justify their classifications by
attending to surface-level lexical cues or to contex-
tual factors such as speaker positionality and prob-
able community impact, as community-proximate
human annotators do (Waseem and Hovy, 2016;
Rizvi et al., 2025b,a).
To address this gap, we propose a bias-aware
evaluation framework for anti-autistic ableist lan-
guage detection. Our approach combines a psy-
chometrically weighted ground truth derived from
implicit and explicit bias measures and autism as-
sessment instruments (Dickter et al., 2020; Flood
et al., 2013; Baron-Cohen et al., 2001) with con-
trolled evaluations of LLM prompting strategies
(zero-shot, in-context learning, chain-of-thought,
and personas).
We investigate three questions:
(RQ1:) How does psychometric-informed anno-
tation weighting affect estimates of human-LLM
agreement? (RQ2:) How do LLM and human rea-
soning differ in detecting anti-autistic ableist con-
tent, and can prompting reduce this gap? (RQ3:)
How does masking autism-related cues in psycho-
metric evaluations impact LLM self-assessment?
We introduce a composite annotator reliabil-
ity score (Ri) integrating IAT (Dickter et al.,
2020), SATA (Flood et al., 2013), and AQ (Baron-
Cohen et al., 2001) measures, demonstrating that
majority-vote aggregation systematically underrep-
resents autistic and autism-accepting perspectives.
Evaluating eight LLMs across seven prompting
strategies against this stricter baseline reveals that
surface-form keyword matching dominates reason-
ing failures, regardless of prompt design.
Fur-
thermore, our 12-model psychometric evaluation
shows recognition-controlled instruments expose
latent anti-autistic biases hidden by standard self-
reports. Together, these contributions provide a
replicable template for community-grounded, bias-
aware evaluation in harmful language detection.
2
Related Work
2.1
Bias and Ableism in Large Language
Models
LLMs inherit and reflect social biases present in
their training data, including those related to dis-
abilities (Venkit et al., 2025). Prior research has
examined how LLMs adopt a “default persona” that
tends to favor dominant groups over marginalized
populations (Tan and Lee, 2025). This persona of-
ten aligns with able-bodied and neurotypical norms,
which may contribute to the generation of ableist
content (Tan and Lee, 2025). While ableist biases
are beginning to receive more attention in NLP
research, anti-autistic ableism, and methods for
evaluating it, remain largely understudied. An ex-
ception is the AUTALIC dataset, which we use in
this work to study anti-autistic ableist language in
context (Rizvi et al., 2025b).
2.2
Annotator Disagreements and
Positionality
Several LLM benchmarks overlook the interplay
between sociodemographic cues and problem-
solving behavior, and the implicit biases driving
these shifts can only be surfaced through evaluation
methods that account for the values and perspec-
tives of the target group (Yin and Huang, 2025).
This is particularly salient for ableist speech, where
human annotators’ own identities and biases shape
their judgments, making annotator positionality a
variable to be measured, not controlled away (Sap
et al., 2021; Rizvi et al., 2025b). Prior work on
annotation disagreement has established that col-
lapsing diverse annotator perspectives into a ma-
jority vote discards meaningful signal (Aroyo and
Welty, 2015; Uma et al., 2021; Davani et al., 2022).
Approaches that treat disagreement as informa-
tive, such as soft labels, per-annotator modeling,
or identity-stratified evaluation, better capture the
subjective nature of harmful language judgments
(Davani et al., 2022). In-context learning (ICL)
allows LLMs to learn tasks given only a few exam-
ples (Dong et al., 2024). Although ICL and per-
sona prompting are common techniques for steer-
ing LLM behavior in sensitive contexts, assigned
personas can skew problem-solving, and implicit
biases may persist or emerge even under seemingly
neutral framings (Yin and Huang, 2025; Tan and
Lee, 2025; Hua et al., 2025).

2.3
Context-Sensitive Harmful Language
Detection
Prior work in abusive language detection has shown
that surface lexical features are insufficient for reli-
able classification, and that contextual factors such
as speaker identity, community membership, and
pragmatic framing are necessary for accurate la-
beling (Hoeken et al., 2024). Reclaimed language,
or terms that carry pejorative force in out-group
use that may signal solidarity or self-identification
in in-group use, are systematically mislabeled by
classifiers (Suvarna et al., 2025). LLMs have been
shown to replicate these surface-pattern failures
at scale (Gallegos et al., 2023), and the challenge
of community-grounded evaluation is increasingly
recognised as a first-order methodological problem
(Homayounirad et al., 2025; Suvarna et al., 2025).
Our work situates anti-autistic ableism within
this broader context, contributing a domain where
insider/outsider distinctions are especially conse-
quential and especially understudied.
3
Methods
3.1
Psychometric Evaluations of Humans
We selected 2,120 sentences labeled as either 1
(ableist) or 0 (not ableist) toward autistic people by
human annotators in the AUTALIC dataset (Rizvi
et al., 2025b).
To characterize participants’ at-
titudes and traits relevant to autism perception,
we administered established psychometric instru-
ments. Annotators completed the Societal Atti-
tudes Toward Autism (SATA) scale (Flood et al.,
2013) to measure explicit acceptance of autistic in-
dividuals and the Autism-Spectrum Quotient (AQ)
(Baron-Cohen et al., 2001) to quantify autistic traits.
Both instruments use Likert-scale responses cover-
ing personality, behavior, and attitudes related to
autism. In addition, participants completed an Im-
plicit Association Test (IAT) (Dickter et al., 2020),
adapted to measure implicit biases toward autism.
The IAT is a reaction-time-based categorization
task that assesses the strength of positive or nega-
tive implicit associations with autism. Examples of
these tests are provided in Appendix B.4.
3.2
Using Psychometrics to Segment Human
Annotations
We curated a set of 283 sentences from AUTALIC
with perfect within-group annotator agreement that
were separate from the 2,120 classification tar-
gets. These sentences provide the labeled exam-
ples for ICL conditions. Annotators are character-
ized across four psychometric groups: (1) autis-
tic (AQ ≥32, clinical range); (2) non-autistic
(AQ < 26, below clinical threshold); (3) high-trust
(Ri > 0.69; the three annotators with highest com-
bined trust scores); and (4) lower-trust (Ri < 0.66;
the three annotators with lowest combined trust
scores). Labels from groups 1 and 2 are used in the
ICL example sets ICL-A and ICL-B respectively
while groups 3 and 4 shape the reliability weighting
scheme described below. We focus on psychomet-
ric extremes to maximize diagnostic contrast with
LLM outputs.
We then min–max normalized each score and av-
eraged them into a raw trust score Ri = 1
3(ˆxAQ,i +
ˆxSATA,i + ˆxIAT,i). The IAT scores were inverted
so that higher values indicate lower implicit bias
while the SATA was retained in its original direc-
tion so that higher values indicate greater accep-
tance. The trust scores in our annotator pool range
from Ri = 0.610 to Ri = 0.819. We then normal-
ized each Ri relative to its annotation team mean
to obtain a final weight Wi (range [0.881, 1.168])
to ensure the mean weight within each team is
exactly 1.0. The ground-truth label ˆy for each in-
stance is computed as the weighted mean of an-
notator labels. Full derivation is provided in Ap-
pendix C.
This composite weighting is motivated by a pat-
tern in our annotator pool. Autistic annotators
(n = 3) show substantially greater dispersion in im-
plicit bias (IAT SD = 0.344) than non-autistic an-
notators (n = 6, IAT SD = 0.120), while explicit
acceptance scores remain comparably consistent
across both groups (SATA SD = 0.024 vs. 0.038).
We treat this as a descriptive, within-sample ob-
servation given the small annotator pool, but its
methodological implication is concrete. Two anno-
tators can share high AQ scores and similar explicit
attitudes yet diverge substantially in implicit bias.
This gap cannot be resolved by single-instrument
selection but our composite Ri metric is designed
to capture it. This is confirmed empirically in Ap-
pendix C.1, where AQ-only weighting fails to pro-
duce a ground truth significantly stricter than ma-
jority vote (p = .651, d = 0.096), whereas the full
composite does (p < .001, d = 0.869). The com-
posite is therefore not an arbitrary design choice
but an instrument-level response to a demonstrated
limitation of single-measure annotator characteri-
sation.

3.3
Psychometric Evaluations of LLMs
Since the SATA and AQ are published instruments,
they may appear in LLM training data and elicit
recognition-based responding rather than genuine
self-assessment, as prior work shows awareness of
an instrument’s purpose biases responses toward
socially desirable profiles (Orne, 1962; Podsakoff
et al., 2003). To mitigate this, each LLM completed
both the original instruments and a purpose-built
rewrite combining SATA and AQ items with other
questions. Following established procedural reme-
dies for demand characteristics (Podsakoff et al.,
2003), the rewrite applied: (1) item randomiza-
tion to disrupt order effects; (2) phrasing varia-
tion (synonyms, passive/active reordering, reverse
logic) to reduce lexical match with training data;
and (3) filler items from the International Personal-
ity Item Pool (Goldberg et al., 2006) interspersed
with 52 validity (dummy) items (e.g., “I am a dish-
washer”) to obscure the questionnaire’s purpose
and detect indiscriminate responding. Full details
are in Appendix B. Responses to both instruments
were scored by mapping each item back to its cor-
responding subscale and applying the scoring pro-
cedures from the original validation studies (Flood
et al., 2013; Baron-Cohen et al., 2001). Following
prior work, we treat psychometric responses as be-
havioral proxies for learned social representations
rather than genuine self-report (Argyle et al., 2023;
Salecha et al., 2024) as we acknowledge that LLMs
cannot straightforwardly self-report autistic traits.

Error Analysis
We did not provide ground-truth
labels or human justifications to the LLMs during
the primary classification task. To analyze quali-
tative misalignments in LLM reasoning, we con-
ducted a triangulated error analysis on a propor-
tionally sampled set of 92 LLM-generated classi-
fication outputs with accompanying explanations,
drawn across all model–condition combinations
in proportion to their representation in the corpus.
Three analysts independently examined the full
set without reconciliation, each applying a distinct
analytical lens: (1) thematic content and error
type, identifying recurring patterns such as lexi-
cal reliance, stereotype invocation, and reasoning
focus; (2) disability-studies linguistic features,
analyzing model-generated explanations as autism
discourse, including identity-first versus person-
first language use, medicalized framing, implicit
assumptions, and intersectional cues; and (3) rea-
soning structure and quality, characterizing the
logical form and completeness of justifications in-
dependent of content. As these three lenses apply
distinct analysis methods, traditional inter-rater re-
liability statistics are not appropriate. Instead, we
use quantitative convergence between the thematic
and linguistic lenses on the same pattern as our
cross-lens reliability criterion, and we report con-
vergence explicitly for each finding. Pre-defining
distinct lenses mitigates the risk that a single analyt-
ical frame suppresses alternative interpretations.

Findings
We address three questions: whether psychome-
tric weighting constitutes a stricter ground truth
than majority voting (RQ1; §4.1); whether LLMs
align with community-proximate human reason-
ing and whether prompting closes this gap (RQ2;
§§4.2–4.3); and whether LLM psychometric atti-
tudes persist when instrument-recognition cues are
masked (RQ3; §4.4).
4.1
Ground Truth Construction is Not
Neutral
Human–human κ within each psychometric group
confirms that the task is genuinely difficult.
The high-AQ (autistic-proximate) group achieves
κhuman-A = 0.208 (73.9% raw agreement), while
the high-bias group reaches only κhuman-B =
0.016 (41.6% raw agreement). This chance-level
agreement justifies down-weighting biased annota-
tors. In fact, a single high-bias annotator assigned
non-neutral labels to 99.3% of items (vs. 28–44%
for all others), with near-zero pairwise κ against
every other rater, shifting the group baseline by
δκ ≈0.04.
Across all 56 model–condition combinations,
psychometrically weighted κ was lower than un-
weighted κ (mean δκ = −0.012; t(55) = −6.50,
p < .001, d = 0.869), with the negative direction
in 84% of combinations. The effect size reflects di-
rectional consistency rather than magnitude as the
mean shift is small (0.012 κ units). Model rankings
are stable across all five weighting schemes (r ≥
0.94), confirming that the weighting choice affects
ground-truth stringency without altering conclu-
sions about which models perform better or worse.

Classification Performance
Overall performance is low across all 56 model–
condition combinations:
mean weighted κ =
0.118, mean F1 = 0.237. We identify and discuss
three structural patterns below.
Precision–recall imbalance.
Recall substan-
tially exceeds precision in 51 of 56 combinations
(mean recall = 0.531; mean precision = 0.173), re-
flecting systematic over-classification of sentences
as ableist. However, given AUTALIC’s class imbal-
ance (≈13% ableist), accuracy (mean = 0.661) is
misleading. F1 is used as the primary secondary
metric throughout.
Two performance tiers.
Safety-oriented classi-
fiers substantially outperform all other models re-
gardless of parameter count. LLaMA Guard3 (mean
κ = 0.236) and GPT-OSS (mean κ = 0.175) both
exceed all instruction-tuned and general-purpose
models (κ range 0.013–0.145).
This suggests
task-specific safety training encodes sensitivity to
disability-related harm signals that general instruc-
tion tuning does not provide and that prompting
alone cannot recover.
Condition effects.
ZS and CoT produce the high-
est mean κ (0.155 and 0.161) and F1 (both 0.273).
CoT benefits are model-specific as they are largest
for GPT-OSS (∆κ = +0.050) and StarlingLM
(∆κ = +0.025), with no benefit or a slight decline
for DeepSeek-R1, LLaMA Guard3, and Gemma 3.
ICL performs below ZS for most models (ICL-A

Conclusion
Psychometric weighting produces a stricter ground
truth than majority-vote aggregation, which sys-
tematically underrepresents autistic and autism-
accepting perspectives without altering model rank-
ings. Structured scaffolding of prompting condi-
tions proved insufficient to overcome lexical re-
liance, and masking instrument-recognition cues
revealed latent anti-autistic bias is concealed by
standard self-reports. Practitioners applying this
framework should (1) screen annotators with a
community-membership and implicit bias measure;
(2) compute reliability weights using Equations 1–5
in Appendix C; (3) derive a weighted ground truth
via Equation 6; and (4) administer recognition-
controlled instruments per Appendix B. Mean F1
= 0.237 indicates near-chance performance on a
task used in consequential moderation decisions
affecting autistic communities. Task-specific fine-
tuning on community-labeled data is therefore a
precondition for deployment-grade performance,
not an optional enhancement.

Limitations
The standardized instruments used in our study
have known some psychometric limitations. Devel-
opers of these tools note that results are generally
more reliable when tests are administered multiple
times. In our study, however, participants com-
pleted each test only once. Nonetheless, to the best
of our knowledge, this is the first evaluation that di-
rectly compares LLM outputs to human annotators
who also identify as autistic.
Our error analysis is designed for pattern iden-
tification rather than frequency estimation. The
proportionally sampled set of 92 excerpts yields
approximately 1–2 excerpts per model–condition
combination, which is sufficient to characterize
qualitatively distinct failure modes. Findings from
this analysis should be interpreted as illustrating
patterns that exist and are reproducible across mod-
els, not as estimates of how frequently those pat-
terns occur at the corpus level. Additionally, the
three analysts used different coding frameworks
rather than a shared one, so standard inter-rater
reliability measures do not apply. Instead, when
two analysts independently reach the same find-
ing through different approaches, that agreement
is taken as evidence the pattern is robust. We note
that the second analyst (reasoning-structure lens)
applied an open-ended qualitative characterization
of reasoning form rather than a predefined categor-
ical scheme, meaning findings from that lens are
best treated as illustrative rather than exhaustive.
The segmentation κ analysis compares LLM
agreement against five human reference groups
(n = 24 matched model–condition pairs). Al-
though we apply paired t-tests and Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests throughout, the comparisons in-
volving the autistic-proximate reference group are
based on n = 3 annotators, and high-bias and
unreliable groups are identical by construction. Re-
ported differences, while statistically significant for
the key comparisons, should be interpreted in light
of the small annotator pool and the exploratory
nature of the within-sample variance observations
that motivate the composite weighting scheme.
Finally, while we made efforts to recruit a di-
verse participant group in terms of race, gender,
and cultural background, the majority were college
students in computing-related programs within a
Western context. As such, their perspectives may
not be fully representative of broader or global pop-
ulations.

B
Psychometric Rewrite: Implementation
Details
To reduce the risk that LLM responses to the SATA
and AQ reflect memorized answer patterns rather
than genuine self-assessment, we administered a
purpose-built rewrite of both instruments alongside
the originals. The rewrite combined all SATA and
AQ items into a single questionnaire and applied
the following survey design techniques:
• Item randomization. Question order was ran-
domized across administrations to disrupt any
order-effect patterns present in the originals.
• Phrasing variation. Item wording was varied
(e.g., synonyms, passive/active reordering) to re-
duce the likelihood of lexical match with training
data.
• Filler items. Items drawn from the International
Personality Item Pool (Goldberg et al., 2006)
were interspersed to obscure the questionnaire’s
purpose and prevent targeted responding.
• Validity (dummy) items. 52 items designed to
be straightforwardly false (e.g., “I am a dish-
washer”) were embedded throughout to detect
indiscriminate or acquiescent responding. Re-
sponses to these items were used as a validity
check when scoring.
Responses to both the original and rewritten in-
struments were scored by mapping each item back
to its corresponding SATA or AQ subscale and ap-
plying the scoring procedures specified in the orig-
inal validation studies (Flood et al., 2013; Baron-
Cohen et al., 2001).
B.1
SATA
The Societal Attitudes Toward Autism (SATA)
scale is a 26-item instrument designed to measure
societal attitudes towards autistic people. It has
been shown to have good internal consistency and
construct validity (Flood et al., 2013). Example
items from the SATA scale include:
• People with autism should not engage in ro-
mantic relationships.
• People with autism should have the opportu-
nity to go to university.
• People with autism should not have children.

B.3
IAT
The Implicit Association Test (IAT) is used to probe
automatic associations between cognitive concepts
and attributes. In the context of autism research,
an IAT can be adapted to examine unconscious as-
sociations between autism diagnostic labels (e.g.,
"Autistic," "Neurotypical" or "Typically Develop-
ing," "Autism Spectrum") and personal attributes
(e.g., "Pleasant" words like "Friendly," or "Unpleas-
ant" words like "Awkward") (Dickter et al., 2020).
The task typically involves a multi-block design
where participants categorize words presented on
screen. For example:
• Block 1 (Concept Categorization): Partic-
ipants categorize terms related to diagnostic
concepts (e.g., pressing ’e’ for "Typically De-
veloping" and ’i’ for "Autism Spectrum").
• Block 2 (Attribute Categorization): Partici-
pants categorize words based on personal at-
tributes (e.g., "Pleasant" or "Unpleasant").
• Block 3 (Combined - Prejudice Consistent):
Concept and attribute categories are paired
in a prejudice-consistent manner (e.g., "Typ-
ically Developing or Pleasant" vs. "Autism
Spectrum or Unpleasant").
• Block 4 (Reversed Concept Categorization):
Similar to Block 1, but key assignments for
concepts are reversed.
• Block 5 (Combined - Prejudice Inconsis-
tent): Concept and attribute categories are
paired in a prejudice-inconsistent manner
(e.g., "Autism Spectrum or Pleasant" vs. "Typ-
ically Developing or Unpleasant").
The IAT measures reaction times to infer implicit
biases.

C.1
Weighting Scheme Robustness
To assess whether the direction of the psychometric
weighting effect is an artifact of the specific com-
bined scheme (AQ + SATA + IAT, equal weights),
we computed δκ = κweighted −κunweighted for four
alternative weighting approaches on the subset of
23 model–condition combinations for which single-
test weights are available: IAT-only, SATA-only,
AQ-only, and variance-weighted (where annotators
with greater score variance receive higher weight).
The combined scheme, IAT-only, and variance-
weighted approaches all produce significantly neg-
ative δκ (all p ≲.001, d range 0.830–0.922), con-
firming that down-weighting implicitly biased an-
notators makes the ground truth stricter. SATA-
only weighting produces a significantly positive
δκ (mean = +0.006, t(22) = 2.70, p = .013,
d = 0.563): explicitly accepting annotators, up-
weighted by SATA, tend to label fewer sentences
as ableist, shifting the ground truth in a more per-
missive direction. AQ-only weighting is not sig-
nificantly different from unweighted (p = .651,
d = 0.096), suggesting AQ scores alone are insuf-
ficient to differentiate annotator reliability on this
task.
Despite these directional differences, all five
weighting schemes correlate with each other at r ≥
0.94 (minimum pairwise r across all scheme pairs),
confirming that the relative ranking of model–
condition pairs is stable regardless of which scheme
is applied. The maximum pairwise spread between
any two schemes for a single model–condition pair
is 0.059 κ units (mean 0.030), which is large rel-
ative to the claimed δκ = 0.012 effect but small
relative to the 0.32-unit range of human–human
kappa in this study. The weighting choice therefore
affects the stringency of the ground-truth threshold
but does not alter conclusions about which models
perform better or worse.
