ANNULAR CHAOS FOR NON-WANDERING HOMEOMORPHISMS
ALEJANDRO PASSEGGI AND FAVIO PIR´AN
Abstract. We study topological conditions ensuring the presence of rotational chaos
for non-wandering or area-preserving annular homeomorphisms. Compared to previous
criteria, our main result provides a simpler alternative that avoids the need to locate
periodic points, requiring only knowledge of the behavior of certain open sets.
This
feature is crucial for enabling concrete applications to Poincar´e return maps arising in
Hamiltonian systems with two degrees of freedom.
The resulting topological criterion admits straightforward numerical implementation:
a computer can verify all the required conditions using a simple algorithm that relies
solely on basic data from the map. We illustrate this approach with the so-called driven
pendulum.
1. Introduction
The study of chaos is one of the central concerns in dynamical systems: how to define
it, establish its existence, and describe the structure underlying this phenomenon. The
theory of surface dynamics has a strong and historical connection with this goal, dating
back to Poincar´e’s study of the three-body problem and the Van der Pol equation, where
surfaces naturally appear as return sections of the associated flow1. As in certain restricted
versions of the three-body problem and other renowned problems in mechanics, we focus
on the study of conservative dynamics on the annulus.
Classical techniques for proving chaos have proven difficult to implement in concrete
systems. As a consequence, many physically motivated examples still lack a rigorous proof
of chaotic behavior—despite a vast literature in which numerical simulations suggest, or
even non-rigorously claim, its presence. This issue goes beyond the matter of definition:
it reveals how limited our understanding remains of the dynamics in key situations that
originally inspired the field. Indeed, if we cannot even rigorously establish the existence
of chaos in such systems, we are even further from identifying a geometric structure that
supports it. See the detailed discussion in the introduction of [20].
Recently, the aforementioned article established results linking the existence of a rota-
tional horseshoe with finitary conditions based on simple properties of the given maps.
This development brings the state of the art in surface dynamics closer to practical appli-
cations. Furthermore, building upon these criteria, [3] presents a list of computer-assisted
proofs of chaos for well-known families of maps, across large regions in parameter space,
demonstrating the effectiveness of the new method. As emphasized in these works, a key
motivation is to find applications of the largely developed topological theory of surface
dynamics to concrete systems.
Nevertheless, a key limitation arises in many relevant cases in these previous results:
the need to locate two fixed points of the map with different rotation numbers. While
this requirement poses no difficulty for maps defined by explicit formulas, such as those
considered in [3], it becomes a major obstacle when dealing with Poincar´e return maps. To
illustrate this, one may consider one of the simplest physical systems suspected to exhibit

1.1. Main theorem. The main goal of this paper is to generalize Theorem A in [20], in
order to guarantee chaos without requiring the localization of fixed points. As we will
see in the examples, this generalization cannot be achieved through purely perturbative
arguments. In the aforementioned result, the key concept is that of a disjoint pair of
neighborhoods U0, U1 of fixed points x0, x1 with nonzero rotational difference. Once such
a suitable pair is identified, the existence of a rotational horseshoe follows, provided that
the sets visit each other. As mentioned, our goal is to remove the requirement of locating
fixed points, thus making the result applicable to a broader and more natural class of
examples.

Theorem. Let f ∈Homeo 0,nw(A) and assume that for some disjoint pair of disks U0, U1
one has ρ(U0, U1) ≥3 and that U0 visits U1. Then f exhibits rotational chaos.
Moreover, a rotational horseshoe determines an instability region I intersecting both U0
and U1, and this region satisfies that its rotation set, for some lift F, contains the interval
[1/3, 2/3]. In particular, every rational point in this interval is realized by some periodic
orbit contained in the instability region.
Remark 1.1. An inspection of the vast literature rigorously addressing the concept of
chaos reveals that, for the first time, we arrive at a criterion whose application does not
require the prior localization of periodic orbits. This marks a significant departure from
classical approaches, where chaotic dynamics are typically inferred through the detection
of homoclinic orbits or braid configurations involving periodic orbits.
Remark 1.2. At first glance, one might attempt to extend Theorem A from [20] to obtain
our main result by constructing a suitable perturbation g of the given map f, supported
inside the sets U0 ∪f(U0) and U1 ∪f(U1). Unfortunately, this strategy does not work, as
such a perturbation could itself create the desired rotational horseshoe. We will return to
this point later in the paper.
Remark 1.3. As we will show in the example of a periodically forced pendulum, the
existence of the pair of sets U0, U1 can be reliably verified through numerical methods.
Remark 1.4. Under the hypotheses of the main theorem, a rotational horseshoe produces
an instability region, which can be characterized as an open subannulus with no invariant
circloid contained in it. In the theorem, when the existence of a horseshoe is guaranteed
by the conditions imposed on the open sets U0 and U1, it is shown that the resulting
instability region intersects both of these sets. This information was not available in the
previous result of [20].
Remark 1.5. The statement provides a version of the classical Poincar´e–Birkhoff theorem
in which it is not necessary to first identify a pair of periodic orbits with different rotation
numbers. The classical twist condition is replaced by the existence of a disjoint pair of
disks with nontrivial rotational difference. Following the ideas of [8], the hypotheses on
the disks U0 and U1 allow us to choose suitable lifts of the map so as to obtain both
positively and negatively returning disks. This guarantees the existence of fixed points in
the universal cover for different lifts of the map.
Remark 1.6. As in the driven pendulum case, one sometimes encounters dynamical
systems preserving an area form that, although invariant, is not finite; hence the dynamics
need not be non-wandering. Fortunately, this issue has been addressed in the theory of
surface dynamics, and alternative hypotheses have been developed for settings that fall
outside the non-wandering framework (see [2]).
One such setting concerns an annular
homeomorphism f that fixes the ends, where the ends are said to be Birkhoff-related. This
means that, given arbitrary neighborhoods of the two ends, there exist positive orbits from
one neighborhood to the other. Below, we state the previous result also for this type of
map.

topological dynamics, and numerical methods might a priori appear to be unrelated fields.
Nevertheless, the results presented here reveal a nontrivial and meaningful intersection

Let us now outline the proof of the main result, Theorem A.
1.4. Sketch of the proof of Theorem A. The conclusion of the theorem can be divided
into two parts:
(i) the existence of a rotational horseshoe;
(ii) the proof that there must exist an instability region intersecting both
U0 and U1.
In the proof of the first part we argue by contradiction and assume that no such rota-
tional horseshoe exists. For simplicity, consider here that the rotational difference is ρ = 3.
A direct and important consequence of Theorem A in [2], applied to non-wandering an-
nulus homeomorphisms with nontrivial rotation set, is that if the map does not support
a rotational horseshoe, then the annulus admits a decomposition into f-invariant essen-
tial annular continua, each having trivial rotation set. Moreover, there exists a rotation
function ρ: A →R which is continuous and well defined with respect to a lift F of f.
By performing small perturbations to create fixed points inside U0 and U1, it is possible
to choose two periodic chains of disks for two suitable lifts of f, implying the existence of
periodic points with different rotation numbers. The new points found, because of their
rotation values, cannot be contained in the support of the perturbation and therefore
must be periodic points of the original map f, showing that the rotation set of f is
nontrivial. Hence, by applying the aforementioned theorem, we obtain a decomposition of
the annulus. The focus now shifts to studying the relationship between the disks U0, U1
and the continua in the decomposition.
Since U0 and U1 are Birkhoff-related, there can be no invariant essential continuum
separating them. The desired contradiction will arise from proving the opposite statement,
namely, that such a separating continuum must exist. To this end, we analyze whether
the intersection of one of the disks—say, U0—with a continuum C0 from the decomposition
imposes rotational constraints on C0. The answer is affirmative, and this observation is
a key ingredient of the argument. The non-wandering or area-preserving hypothesis is
crucial here.
Indeed, assume that F is a lift of f such that, for every lift ˜U0 of U0, we have
F( ˜U0) ∩˜U0 ̸= ∅,
and that the union of U0 with its next three iterates remains inessential. Then we prove
that the rotation number of C0 with respect to F must belong to the interval [−1, 1].
Analogously, if some continuum C1 in the decomposition intersects U1, then its rotation
number for the same lift F must lie in the interval [2, 4].
It is important to remark that constraints of this type arise only in a conservative
setting, that is, when f is area-preserving or non-wandering (see Section 3 for further
details and a counterexample in the absence of this hypothesis).
Let C0 and C1 be two such continua, which must be distinct, and consider the subannulus
between them. By continuity of the rotation function ρ, there must exist a continuum B
within this subannulus such that ρB(F) = 3/2. Since B cannot intersect either U0 or U1,
it necessarily separates them, yielding the desired contradiction.

2.4. Instability regions. We use the term Birkhoff instability region, or simply instability
region, to refer to an open subannulus A ⊆A that is f-invariant, has a nontrivial rotation
set, and does not admit any f-invariant circloid in its interior. By considering, when they
exist, the upper and lower circloids delimiting the instability region, or by filling the region
with disks accumulating on the ends of the annulus in the unbounded case, one can always
associate a regular annulus defined by these circloids, which is also an instability region.
We refer to this as a regular instability region.
Rotational horseshoes and instability regions are strongly related. In the C1+α and
area-preserving setting, each local unstable set Xθ−of the horseshoe can be considered as
a simple arc and determines an unstable manifold W u(Xθ−). In this case, it is well known
that the closure of every unstable manifold of this kind accumulates on the same set, the
so-called chaotic sea. By adding to the chaotic sea every disk in its complement, we get
an instability region whose rotation set is at least as large as that of the horseshoe.
