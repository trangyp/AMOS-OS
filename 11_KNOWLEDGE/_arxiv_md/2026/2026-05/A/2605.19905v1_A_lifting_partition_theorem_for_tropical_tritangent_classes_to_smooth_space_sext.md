A LIFTING PARTITION THEOREM FOR TROPICAL TRITANGENT
CLASSES TO SMOOTH SPACE SEXTIC CURVES
MAR´IA ANG´ELICA CUETO, HANNAH MARKWIG AND YUE REN
Abstract. The set of tritangent planes to smooth tropical space sextic curves has 15 connected
components, recording continuous displacements of planes preserving the tritangency condition.
These 15 tritangent classes are polyhedral complexes in R3, and each of them contains the trop-
icalization of precisely eight tritangent planes to any smooth space sextic curve with the given
tropicalization.
Prior joint work of the authors with Len confirms that each tropical tritangent plane has 0,
1, 2, 4 or 8 lifts to classical tritangent planes defined over the algebraic closure of the field over
which the original algebraic curve is defined. Our main theorem states that when the input classical
curve is generic, then only six out of the ten possible partitions of 8 into powers of 2 arise from
lifting multiplicities of tritangent classes. Furthermore, we show that these partitions are completely
determined by the dimension of a suitable connected subcomplex of the class and the existence of
a member with a tropical tangency of a predetermined combinatorial type.
1. Introduction
The classical question of counting the number of tritangent planes to a smooth, space sextic com-
plex curve C dates back to work of Clebsch and Coble [1, 2]. By working over a non-Archimedean,
non-trivially valued algebraically closed field K with residue field of characteristic not 2 or 3 (such
as C{{t}}) these questions can be addressed using tropical geometry methods [3, 6].
Even though the tropical count of tritangent planes to these curves is infinite, the collection of
all tropical tritangent planes to a given smooth tropical space sextic curve Γ can be grouped into
15 equivalence classes, corresponding to continuous translations of these planes within this collec-
tion [6]. Jensen and Len’s work [8] confirms that each tritangent class contain the tropicalization
of exactly eight classical tritangents, which recovers the classical count.
In [3], the present authors and Len revisited this question from a computational perspective
with three goals in mind: determine which tritangent planes to Γ lift to classical tritangent planes,
decide their lifting multiplicities and provide an algorithm to compute these lifts. In particular, [3,
Theorem 1.2] shows that these individual lifting multiplicities are either 0, 1, 2, 4, or 8. In turn,
these numbers can be determined by the local structure of Γ at each tropical tangency point.
Since each tritangent class has eight lifts in total, it is natural to ask which combinations of
non-zero lifting multiplicities can occur within a tritangent class to a fixed Γ. This information,
which we refer to as the lifting partition of the input class, is recorded as a quadruple, indicating
the number of members with lifting multiplicities 1, 2, 4 and 8, respectively.
Our first main result gives a precise answer to this question, under mild genericity assumptions.
Theorem 1.1. Assume that the input classical curve is general relative to its tropicalization Γ.
Then, there are six possible values for the lifting partition of a given tritangent class to Γ, namely
(8, 0, 0, 0), (4, 2, 0, 0), (0, 2, 1, 0), (0, 4, 0, 0), (0, 0, 2, 0) or (0, 0, 0, 1).

The next definition appeared in [3, Section 2] and it is inspired by the classical notion of tangency
between two plane curves. Note that set-intersections of tropical curves must be replaced by their
stable analogs (see, e.g., [7] and references therein).
Definition 2.3. Let Γ and Λ be two tropical curves in TP1 × TP1 of bidegrees (3, 3) and (1, 1),
respectively. Assume that Γ is smooth. Then, we say that Λ is tritangent to Γ if any of the following
three conditions hold:
(i) Λ ∩Γ has three components, each with total stable intersection multiplicity 2, or
(ii) Λ ∩Γ has two components, of total stable intersection multiplicities 2 and 4, respectively, or
(iii) Λ ∩Γ is connected, and its stable intersection multiplicity is 6.
To simplify the construction of curves Λ tritangent to Γ it is convenient to predetermine the
possible tangencies that can occur on Γ. The following statement, which originally appeared in [3,
Theorem 3.1], provides a complete classification of such local tangencies.
Theorem 2.4. Up to D4-symmetry, there are 38 possible local tangencies between a (1, 1)-tropical
curve and a smooth (3, 3)-tropical curve in TP1 × TP1. They are depicted in Figure 2.

As we mentioned in Section 1, the existence of infinitely many tritangent planes to a given Γ
is a common phenomenon. Continuous displacement of the vertices of these planes preserving the
tritangent condition produce equivalence classes of tritangent planes. Under the tropical Segre
map, these movements correspond to continuous compatible translations of the vertices v0 and v1
of each Λ, or equivalently, continuous displacements of v0 followed by piece-wise linear maps on the
signed length component with slopes in {0, ±1}. The next definition summarizes these facts:
Definition 2.6. A tropical tritangent class of Γ is a connected component of the subset of R3
consisting of all points (v0, l) encoding all smooth tropical (1, 1)-curves that are tritangent to Γ.
We label these sets by Ω.
The following result from [6, Theorem 5.2] describes how the classical count of tritangent planes
to V (f) can be recovered from the tritangent classes of Γ:
Theorem 2.7. Each Γ has exactly 15 tritangent classes. Furthermore, each of them contains the
tropicalization of exactly eight tritangent curves V (ℓ) to V (f) in P1 × P1.
These numbers are deduced from the classical proof of Coble-Clebsch formula and the computation
of tropical theta characteristics on metric graphs via Zharkov’s Algorithm [12].
By analogy with prior work of the first two authors on bitangent classes to smooth tropical
plane quartics [4], it is natural to ask if the location and lifting multiplicity of the liftable members
of Ωcan be detected solely from f and the combinatorial type of Γ. The proof of Theorem 1.1
yields an affirmative answer to this question. Most notably, we can do so without providing a full
classification of all tropical tritangent classes to Γ. Note that obtaining such a list would not be
a simple task, given the high number of local tangency types provided in Theorem 2.4 and the
existence of 5 941 possible combinatorial types for Γ (up to D4-symmetry).
Prior work of Len and the second author [9] shows how tropical tangencies between two curves
can often be used to compute lifting multiplicities, by solving local versions of the classical system
ℓ= f = det(∇ℓ, ∇f) = 0 determining tangencies between the input curves, with respect to the
tangency points.

3. Local moves and combinatorial structures on tritangent classes
In order to prove the lifting partition theorem for tritangent classes to a given smooth (3, 3)-curve
Γ in TP1 × TP1, we must first understand their structure. This will be achieved by describing their
local behavior. We remark that all results in this section are purely combinatorial: they rely solely
on the duality between Γ and unimodular triangulations of the standard square of side length three.
The metric structure on Γ and the genericity conditions from Definition 2.8 play no role.
Throughout, we let Λ be a tropical (1, 1)-curve in TP1 × TP1 tangent to Γ. We let P be the
corresponding tangency, i.e., the tuple of tangency points on the component of Λ ∩Γ with even
stable intersection multiplicity corresponding to the tangency condition. Any move on Λ will shift
the point P. In what follows we discuss how to to perturb the point (v0, l) in R3 corresponding to
Λ while mantaining the tangency condition at the translation of P.
We restrict such movements to a bounded neighborhood of the input point in R3, and let Ξ be the
region of R3 recording such local moves. As we will see throughout the various results presented
