Abstract
Audio super-resolution (SR), also referred to as bandwidth extension (BWE), aims to re-
construct high-fidelity signals from low-resolution (LR) or band-limited (BL) observations,
an inherently ill-posed task due to the ambiguity of missing high-frequency (HF) content.
This survey provides a comprehensive overview of the field, with a particular focus on the
paradigm shift from discriminative mapping to modern generative modeling. We first review
early discriminative deep neural network (DNN) models, which formulate BWE/SR as a de-
terministic mapping problem and are prone to regression-to-the-mean effects and spectral
over-smoothing. We then systematically review generative approaches, including autore-
gressive (AR) models, variational autoencoders (VAEs), generative adversarial networks
(GANs), diffusion and score-based models, flow-based methods, and Schrödinger bridges.
Across these approaches, we examine key design aspects, including representation domain,
architecture, conditioning mechanisms, and trade-offs among reconstruction fidelity, percep-
tual quality, robustness, and computational efficiency. Furthermore, we discuss emerging
directions involving large language models (LLMs) and multimodal foundation models, and
highlight open challenges in perceptual evaluation, phase modeling, and real-world general-
ization. By providing a structured taxonomy and unified perspective, this survey establishes
a comprehensive foundation and offers a practical roadmap for advancing BWE/SR from
deterministic point estimation toward distribution-aware generative modeling.

Introduction
Audio super-resolution (SR) (Kuleshov et al., 2017) and bandwidth extension (BWE) (Abel & Fingscheidt,
2017) are essential tasks in artificial intelligence and audio research communities. They are widely used in
applications such as telecommunications (Li & Lee, 2015), hearing aids (Van Eeckhoutte et al., 2020), and
legacy recording restoration (Moliner et al., 2024), and can benefit downstream tasks including speech and
speaker recognition (Li et al., 2019; Yamamoto et al., 2019). Although these terms are defined from different
perspectives and arise from different historical contexts, they share the common objective of restoring high-
fidelity audio from bandwidth-constrained observations.
From a learning perspective, BWE/SR can be
formulated as ill-posed reconstruction problems in which missing high-frequency (HF) content must be
inferred from band-limited (BL) or low-resolution (LR) observations. Because the missing HF content is
not uniquely determined by the input, a single degraded waveform may correspond to multiple perceptually
plausible broadband (BB) or high-resolution (HR) reconstructions. This inherent one-to-many ambiguity
makes BWE/SR a useful testbed for conditional distribution modeling beyond deterministic point estimation.
Traditional techniques, including source-filter models (Makhoul & Berouti, 1979), codebook mapping (Unno
& McCree, 2005), Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) (Ohtani et al., 2014), and hidden Markov mod-
els (HMMs) (Chen & Parsa, 2004), relied on structured priors and simplified acoustic assumptions, often
producing over-smoothed spectra and audible artifacts due to limited modeling capacity. The advent of deep
learning (DL) shifted BWE/SR toward data-driven modeling of complex spectral relationships. Early deep
neural network (DNN) architectures, such as multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) (Li et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2015;
Wang et al., 2015), recurrent neural networks (RNNs) (Schmidt & Edler, 2018; Birnbaum et al., 2019; Hou
et al., 2020; Edraki et al., 2024), and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) (Kuleshov et al., 2017; Lim et al.,
2018; Wang & Wang, 2020; Lin et al., 2021; Tamiti et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026), demonstrated superior perfor-
mance over statistical models by directly learning non-linear mappings from degraded observations to clean
targets. Subsequent research introduced more sophisticated structures, including U-Net variants (Kuleshov
et al., 2017), Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017), and Mambas (Shams et al., 2024), to better capture fine-
grained spectral details and model long-range temporal dependencies. However, despite their success, these
discriminative models frequently suffer from regression-to-the-mean behavior when trained with standard
distance-based losses, resulting in spectral over-smoothing and a lack of HF richness. These limitations are
especially pronounced in BWE/SR, where the missing HF content is inherently ambiguous and may admit
multiple plausible reconstructions.
Recent advances in generative modeling have provided new paths to address this ambiguity by modeling the
conditional distribution of plausible HF content rather than predicting a single deterministic target. Earlier
generative frameworks—including autoregressive (AR) models (Gupta et al., 2019), variational autoencoders
(VAEs) (Bachhav et al., 2020), and generative adversarial networks (GANs) (Hauret et al., 2023)—introduced
non-deterministic reconstruction. Yet these methods face distinct trade-offs: AR models provide fine-grained
temporal modeling at the cost of high inference latency, while VAEs offer structured latent spaces but
often lack HF fidelity. Although GANs sharpen spectral details via adversarial training, they are frequently
hindered by training instability and mode collapse. Conversely, more recent research leverages likelihood- and
score-based paradigms, such as diffusion probabilistic models (Ho et al., 2020; Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021), flow-
based methods (Lipman et al., 2022), and Schrödinger bridges (De Bortoli et al., 2021), provide alternative
ways to model conditional audio distributions through iterative denoising, continuous-time dynamics, or
stochastic bridges between degraded and clean signals. These methods have broadened the design space for
BWE/SR, introducing new trade-offs among sample quality, diversity, controllability, computational cost,
and inference latency. To contextualize this modeling shift from discriminative formulations to generative
designs, Fig. 1 summarizes representative BWE/SR studies over recent years.
Despite the rapid advancements in BWE/SR, the field currently lacks a systematic work that articulates the
significant paradigm shift from discriminative mapping to modern generative modeling frameworks. Existing
surveys (Prasad & Kumar, 2016) remain fragmented and increasingly outdated: they primarily emphasize
classical signal-processing approaches and provide limited coverage of early DNN-based models. Moreover,
the definitional relationship between SR and BWE remains underexplored, leaving their distinctions and
common objectives ambiguous across communities. To the best of our knowledge, this survey addresses this

Background
2.1
Problem Formulation
The tasks of BWE, SR, and speech super-resolution (SSR) can each be formulated as learning a function
f : x 7→y,
(1)
where x = [x[1], . . . , x[N1]]⊤and y = [y[1], . . . , y[N2]]⊤are one-dimensional (1-D) discrete audio sequences
with N1 and N2 samples, respectively, and x[n] and y[n] denote the corresponding scalar-valued samples of
x and y. For BWE, the core objective is to restore missing HF spectral components, where the input x and
target y correspond to BL and BB signals, respectively. In simulation, x is typically obtained by applying a
low-pass filter to y to remove spectral content above a specific cutoff frequency. Some studies (Wang et al.,
2015; Liu et al., 2016; Abel et al., 2018; Li et al., 2021; Hauret et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023a) perform BWE by
maintaining identical sampling rates for inputs and targets, resulting in equal sequence lengths (N1 = N2). In
this configuration, the BL signal is represented on the same temporal grid as the target BB signal. Conversely,
others (Liu et al., 2015; Li & Lee, 2015; Gu et al., 2016; Dong et al., 2020; Bachhav et al., 2020; Su et al.,
2021) use a lower sampling rate for the input, resulting in N1 < N2. This requires the model to resolve
temporal resolution mismatches in addition to spectral recovery, thereby aligning the formulation closely with
SR. Conventional BWE assumes a known, fixed degradation process (e.g., a predefined band-limiting filter),
whereas blind BWE (Moliner et al., 2024) relaxes this assumption and must infer unknown degradations from
the observed signal. Although BWE has historically focused on speech signals to improve intelligibility in
telecommunications, recent advancements have extended these techniques to musical applications (Moliner
& Välimäki, 2022; Moliner et al., 2024; Hernandez-Olivan et al., 2026).

higher rate R′ > R, with the upsampling ratio defined as

Consequently, SR is explicitly framed as a temporal interpolation problem where the model must increase

Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem (Jerri, 1977) guarantees that the maximum representable bandwidth is
R/2 and R′/2, respectively. Therefore, SR inherently requires the restoration of HF content, in contrast to
a standard upsampling operation that only increases the sampling rate. Without additional HF recovery,
simply performing BL interpolation would provide no new information above the original Nyquist limit R/2.
Additionally, SSR is defined similarly to SR but is restricted to speech signals, whereas SR targets general
audio, including speech, music, and sound effects.

2.2
Survey Scope
To maintain a focused survey of BWE and SR, we establish specific scope constraints regarding the input
signal and restoration task. When the input x is both BL and corrupted by additive noise, the task is
defined as noise-robust BWE (Lin et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025a). Fur-
thermore, universal speech enhancement (SE), also referred to as speech restoration, has recently emerged
as a prominent research direction, as promoted by the URGENT challenge (Zhang et al., 2024). Such ap-
proaches (Serrà et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2022b; Andreev et al., 2023; Lemercier et al., 2023; Scheibler et al.,
2024; Ku et al., 2025; Welker et al., 2025; Kwak et al., 2026; Gao et al., 2026a; Lee & Choi, 2026; Hsieh
& Braun, 2026) are designed to handle diverse distortions, including additive noise, reverberation, clipping,
and bandwidth limitation. We include these works as they remain within the single-observation inverse
problem framework. Conversely, we exclude methods that leverage multiple input modalities, such as the
fusion of bone-conducted and air-conducted speech (Yu et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). We categorize these
approaches as multi-modal SE rather than pure BWE/SR, as their performance relies on sensor fusion rather
than the data-driven spectral modeling considered in this work.

Learning Framework
3.1
Input Representations
Early DNN-based methods have primarily relied on spectral-domain features that explicitly characterize
the short-time spectral shape and excitation information of speech signals. Typical examples include the
log-power spectrum (LPS) (Li & Lee, 2015; Li et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2015; Gu et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016;
Abel et al., 2018; Schmidt & Edler, 2018; Eskimez & Koishida, 2019; Bachhav et al., 2020), line spectral
frequency (LSF) (Liu et al., 2015; 2016; Li et al., 2018), mel-frequency cepstral coefficient (MFCC) (Liu et al.,
2015; 2016), and band-pass voicing coefficient (BPVC) (Liu et al., 2015; 2016). Beyond these frequency-
domain descriptors, other works (Abel et al., 2016; Abel & Fingscheidt, 2017) explore time-domain and
modulation-domain features such as the autocorrelation function, zero-crossing rate, gradient index, and
normalized relative frame energy. In addition to these handcrafted features, some approaches instead learn
compact spectral-envelope representations directly from data, using methods such as the Gaussian-Bernoulli
restricted Boltzmann machine (Wang et al., 2015), STRAIGHT vocoder (Gu & Ling, 2015), and a specially
structured DNN (Gu et al., 2016).
The advent of DNN architectures has motivated a shift toward learning directly from time-domain and
frequency-domain audio representations.
In the literature, numerous works operate directly on the raw
waveform (Kuleshov et al., 2017; Gu & Ling, 2017; Lim et al., 2018; Ling et al., 2018; Birnbaum et al., 2019;

are computed by mapping STFT magnitudes through perceptually spaced mel filterbanks, with or without
an additional logarithmic transformation, respectively. However, both STFT magnitude spectrograms and
mel-spectrograms lack the phase information and therefore require additional algorithms for phase recovery.
To address this limitation, several studies instead use STFT complex spectrograms (Mandel et al., 2023;
Soltanmohammadi et al., 2023; Edraki et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024a;b; Yu et al., 2024; Tamiti et al., 2025;

log-magnitude and phase spectra—to enable more accurate waveform reconstruction and phase-aware mod-
eling. Moreover, recent studies (Liu et al., 2021; Shuai et al., 2023) employ the modified discrete cosine
transform (MDCT) and leverage the real-valued spectrogram representation. This technique supports phase-
aware reconstruction by encoding phase information into the sign of the MDCT coefficients. MDCT removes
inherent redundancy in the complex spectrogram and operates with fewer coefficients for the same tem-
poral resolution, providing a compact and computationally efficient solution. Finally, recent trends (Fang
et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026) employ discrete latent tokens extracted from a neural audio
codec (NAC) as features, producing a compact and semantically rich representation that enables high-fidelity
and perceptually consistent audio reconstruction. Overall, the evolution of input representations reflects a
trajectory from handcrafted spectral features toward richer, phase-aware, and semantically grounded repre-
sentations, driven by both architectural advances and increasing demands of high-fidelity reconstruction.
3.2
Training Targets and Objective Functions
Early BWE methods have relied on spectral-domain regression, which predicts the HF or fullband LPS (Li
& Lee, 2015; Li et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2015; Gu et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2016; Eskimez & Koishida, 2019),
HF cepstral vector (Abel & Fingscheidt, 2017; Abel et al., 2018), HF posterior probabilities modeled by
HMM (Abel et al., 2016), or other spectral-envelope representations (Wang et al., 2015; Gu & Ling, 2015;
Schmidt & Edler, 2018; Li et al., 2018; Bachhav et al., 2020). These approaches are typically trained using
the minimum mean-square error (MMSE) criterion.
The missing phase information is reconstructed by
mirroring the low-frequency (LF) phase, followed by inverse STFT (iSTFT) and overlap-add synthesis. Such
spectral-domain training targets provide a smooth and meaningful representation that is easier to model
and leads to stable training. However, they discard phase information and cannot capture the stochastic
structure of HF components, often resulting in over-smoothed spectra and limited perceptual realism.
Given these limitations, BWE/SR research has shifted toward direct waveform prediction (Kuleshov et al.,
2017; Gu & Ling, 2017; Wang et al., 2018; Lim et al., 2018; Ling et al., 2018; Birnbaum et al., 2019; Gupta
et al., 2019; Li et al., 2019; Wang & Wang, 2020; Hou et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2020; Hao et al., 2020; Wang
& Wang, 2021; Li et al., 2021; Su et al., 2021; Rakotonirina, 2021; Nguyen et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023a;
Andreev et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024a; Zhao et al., 2025; Lee & Kim, 2025), where the model implicitly
learns both amplitude and phase and can capture fine-grained HF details without relying on explicit spectral
synthesis. Typical time-domain loss functions include the mean squared error (MSE) loss (Kuleshov et al.,
2017; Lim et al., 2018; Birnbaum et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2020; Rakotonirina, 2021; Kim et al., 2024a) and
mean absolute error (MAE) loss (Li et al., 2019; Wang & Wang, 2020; Dong et al., 2020; Wang & Wang,
2021; Su et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023a) applied directly to the waveform, scale-invariant signal-to-distortion
ratio (SI-SDR) loss (Hou et al., 2020), and cross-entropy (CE) loss (Gu & Ling, 2017; Wang et al., 2018;
Ling et al., 2018; Gupta et al., 2019). In addition, several studies incorporate frequency-domain losses for
waveform targets, including MSE loss on MFCC (Li et al., 2019), MAE loss on STFT spectrograms (Wang

and multi-scale STFT loss (Nguyen et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024a; Lee & Kim, 2025). Later research has

enforce both sample-level waveform accuracy and perceptually important spectral structure.
Parallel to waveform prediction, another major class of approaches uses the STFT complex spectro-

Ruan, 2025). Moreover, AP-BWE (Lu et al., 2024a) and its subsequent extension (Lu et al., 2024b) introduce
a combination of log-amplitude MSE loss, phase anti-wrapping loss, and short-time complex spectral MSE
loss to jointly model amplitude and phase. Additionally, several approaches (Liu et al., 2022a;b; 2024a; Kim
et al., 2024b; Im & Nam, 2025; Yun et al., 2025) adopt a two-stage pipeline where the model first predicts a
mel-spectrogram as an intermediate representation and then applies a pretrained vocoder to synthesize the
waveform. In the first stage, the MAE loss is typically used to achieve accurate mel-spectrogram prediction.
Regarding diffusion and score-based models (Lee & Han, 2021; Han & Lee, 2022; Yu et al., 2023; Lin et al.,
2024; Liu et al., 2024a; Im & Nam, 2025; Fang et al., 2025), the training targets are typically the additive noise
or an equivalent denoising objective at each diffusion step. In this case, the model predicts either the noise
or velocity vector in the waveform domain, spectrogram domain, or a learned latent space. These models
are optimized using MAE or MSE losses applied in the corresponding domain. For GAN-based approaches,
the training target can be either waveforms or spectrograms, and a combination of adversarial loss and
reconstruction loss is employed to enhance perceptual quality. In flow-based approaches (Yun et al., 2025; Ku
et al., 2025), training is performed by regressing an analytically defined velocity field that transports a simple
base distribution to the target BB/HR signal distribution using the MSE loss. In bridge models (Li et al.,
2025a;b), training aims to estimate a drift field that bridges a source and a target distribution by minimizing
the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the induced path measure and a reference stochastic process
under boundary constraints, either directly in signal space or within a learned latent representation space.
Finally, other methods use alternative training targets such as PQMF-decomposed subband signals (Hauret
et al., 2023) or latent token sequences (Fang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2026). Taken
together, the evolution of training targets mirrors the shift in model paradigms: early spectral-regression
objectives gave way to direct waveform prediction, which in turn has been complemented by phase-aware
spectral targets and the objective functions of modern generative frameworks.
3.3
Spectral Mapping Paradigms
Existing BWE/SR frameworks can be categorized by their spectral mapping paradigm, which describes
how the input cutoff frequency is configured during training—whether fixed at a single value, varied across
a discrete set, or treated as a continuous free parameter. Three paradigms can be distinguished: fixed-
constraint, multi-scenario, and bandwidth-agnostic. The fixed-constraint paradigm restricts the experimental
protocol to a unique spectral mapping, in which a single cutoff frequency is used for both training and
evaluation. This setting is typical of specialized systems such as legacy telephony restoration, where the
speech bandwidth is extended from narrowband (NB) (e.g., 300 Hz–3.4 kHz or 0–4 kHz) to wideband (WB)
(e.g., 0–8 kHz). The multi-scenario paradigm trains and evaluates separate models for multiple discrete input
cutoff frequencies, enabling improved robustness across heterogeneous bandwidth conditions compared to a
single fixed-cutoff setting. For instance, AudioUNet (Kuleshov et al., 2017) employs distinct models for
upscaling ratios of 2×, 4×, and 6× at a target sampling rate of 16 kHz, each optimized specifically for its
corresponding input bandwidth. The bandwidth-agnostic paradigm employs a single universal model trained
to handle a wide range of input cutoff frequencies, enabling many-to-one spectral bandwidth mapping within
a unified framework. In this regime, input cutoff frequencies are either sampled from a continuous stochastic

4
Evaluations
4.1
Audio Datasets
Most existing BWE/SR approaches are primarily designed for speech signals and are commonly evalu-
ated using standard speech corpora. In particular, the TIMIT Acoustic-Phonetic Continuous Speech Cor-
pus (Garofolo et al., 1988) and the CSTR Voice Cloning Toolkit (VCTK) (Yamagishi, 2012) are the most
frequently adopted benchmarks due to their high-quality recordings and phonetic diversity. The TIMIT
database contains approximately 5.4 hours of English speech from 630 speakers recorded at a sampling
rate of 16 kHz with detailed phonetic and word-level annotations. The VCTK corpus consists of roughly
44 hours of English speech from 109 speakers with diverse accents recorded at a sampling rate of 48 kHz.
Other studies employ a wider range of speech datasets, including LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015), Wall
Street Journal (WSJ0) corpus (Corpus, 1992), LibriTTS (Zen et al., 2019), HiFi-TTS (Bakhturina et al.,
2021), VoiceBank-DEMAND (Botinhao et al., 2016), and LJSpeech (Ito & Johnson, 2017). The BL/LR
signals are typically synthesized from these ground-truth BB/HR sources by applying low-pass filtering or
downsampling to simulate bandwidth-limited acquisition.
Among SSR-focused works, VCTK at 48 kHz has emerged as the de facto benchmark, used both for training
and evaluation by systems such as NU-Wave (Lee & Han, 2021), AudioSR (Liu et al., 2024a), and FlashSR (Im
& Nam, 2025), making it the most consistently adopted corpus for SSR despite its original design as a multi-
speaker text-to-speech resource. Conversely, several bone-conducted BWE approaches incorporate real-world
sensor data. Edraki et al. (2024) recorded a proprietary corpus of own-voice signals using paired air and in-
ear microphones, while Li et al. (2023a;b) use the Elevoc Simultaneously-recorded Microphone/Bone-sensor
(ESMB) speech corpus1.
Beyond speech, BWE/SR has expanded to music and general sounds, requiring datasets that capture greater
spectral complexity. Several studies (Kuleshov et al., 2017; Lim et al., 2018; Rakotonirina, 2021; Mandel
et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024a; Moliner et al., 2024; Im & Nam, 2025; Li et al., 2025b) incorporate diverse
music datasets to evaluate performance on signals with richer spectral density and polyphonic structure
than speech. Representative datasets include MUSDB18-HQ (Rafii et al., 2019), MoisesDB (Pereira et al.,
2023), MedleyDB (Bittner et al., 2014), SDS (Manco et al., 2023), MAESTRO (Hawthorne et al., 2018),
COCOChorales (Wu et al., 2022), and FMA-small (Defferrard et al., 2016). These datasets span a wide
range of musical content, from solo recordings to multi-instrument mixtures, enabling rigorous evaluation
of HF reconstruction and polyphonic detail. A subset of studies (Tian et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024a; Im &
Nam, 2025; Li et al., 2025b) further considers datasets of general sounds, such as ESC-50 (Piczak, 2015),
FreeSound (Mei et al., 2024), and ShipsEar (Santos-Domínguez et al., 2016). These datasets cover diverse
non-stationary and stochastic acoustic conditions—from urban noise to underwater signals—often lacking
the harmonic regularity found in speech or music.

channels (e.g., coding artifacts, packet loss) rather than HF reconstruction, and can therefore be misleading
for evaluating BWE/SR systems.
Short-Time Objective Intelligibility (STOI): STOI (Taal et al., 2010) is an objective metric that esti-

under noisy and reverberant conditions. STOI/ESTOI are most relevant for NB speech BWE, where intel-

where perceptual quality is more critical.
4.2.2
Subjective Evaluations
Mean Opinion Score (MOS): MOS (Wester et al., 2015) is a subjective evaluation method standardized
by the ITU-T for assessing perceived speech and audio quality. It is computed by averaging listener ratings,
typically collected on a five-point Absolute Category Rating scale, where 1 denotes bad quality and 5 denotes
excellent quality. As a direct measure of human perception, MOS serves as a reliable benchmark and is
widely used to complement objective metrics in speech and audio research. In BWE/SR, MOS evaluations
are increasingly conducted via crowdsourcing platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, while automatic
MOS predictors, such as MOSNet (Lo et al., 2019), DNSMOS (Reddy et al., 2021), NISQA (Mittag et al.,
2021), and WV-MOS (Andreev et al., 2022), have emerged as scalable alternatives to full listening tests.
Preference Tests: Preference-based listening tests are subjective evaluations in which listeners compare
audio samples produced by different systems and indicate their preference. In A/B tests, two audio samples
are directly compared in terms of perceived quality or intelligibility, while ABX tests require listeners to
identify which of two candidates is closer to a reference signal.
These methods enable direct pairwise
comparisons and are often used alongside MOS evaluations, providing higher sensitivity to subtle differences
between high-performing BWE/SR systems.
Multiple Stimuli with Hidden Reference and Anchor (MUSHRA): The MUSHRA test3 is a stan-
dardized subjective evaluation methodology designed to assess intermediate-quality audio systems. In this
test, listeners are presented with multiple stimuli, including a hidden reference and one or more anchor
signals, and are asked to rate the perceived audio quality on a continuous scale from 0 to 100. Compared
with MOS-based evaluations, MUSHRA provides finer resolution and improved sensitivity for distinguishing
perceptual differences among systems. In BWE/SR studies, the BL/LR input signal is typically used as the
low-quality anchor, grounding the rating scale in the degradation being addressed.
In practice, LSD and MOS are the most consistently co-reported metrics across the BWE/SR literature—LSD

5
Discriminative Models
Discriminative models for BWE and SR learn direct mappings from BL/LR representations to their BB/HR
counterparts. This section categorizes existing approaches into MLP, RNN, CNN, Transformer, and Mamba-
based frameworks, reflecting the progression from foundational to more advanced architectures. Table 2
summarizes representative methods across these paradigms.
5.1
Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)
Early studies (Li et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2015; Gu & Ling, 2015; Li & Lee, 2015) formulate
BWE as a high-dimensional nonlinear mapping problem and employ MLPs to model the complex relationship
between lower-band (LB) spectral envelopes and upper-band (UB) components. A common strategy is to
first perform unsupervised pre-training using restricted Boltzmann machines (RBMs) (Ackley et al., 1985)
to initialize the DNN weights and then discriminative fine-tuning with the MMSE criterion to estimate UB
spectral parameters. The UB phase is typically recovered via inverse mirroring of the phase from the BL
signal. Abel et al. (Abel et al., 2016; Abel & Fingscheidt, 2017; Abel et al., 2018) compare HMM-GMM,
DNN-HMM hybrid, and MLP-only approaches for BWE, finding that direct MLP-based cepstral prediction
with LB phase-copying achieves more accurate UB energy estimation and improved perceptual quality at
low computational cost. The MLP-based approaches have outperformed conventional GMM-based methods
across both objective and subjective evaluations.
5.2
Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)
RNNs constitute an important model family for audio signal processing because they are designed to capture
both short-range and long-range temporal dependencies. Gu et al. (2016) enhance BWE by incorporating
linguistic information through bottleneck features extracted from an MLP-based HMM state classifier, pro-
viding a compact representation of both acoustic and phonetic cues. To improve the reduction of disconti-
nuities from frame-independent mapping, this method employs a deep RNN with stacked long short-term
memory (LSTM) layers, outperforming both GMM-based and MLP-based approaches. Liu et al. (2016)
propose a two-stage BWE approach that uses a bidirectional LSTM (BLSTM) to estimate the UB LPS

combining with LB components.
Subsequent works (Schmidt & Edler, 2018; Birnbaum et al., 2019; Hou et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2021; Edraki
et al., 2024), further explore hybrid CNN-RNN architectures that combine convolutional feature extraction
with recurrent temporal modeling to improve BWE/SR performance. Schmidt & Edler (2018) present a
BWE system that estimates UB spectral envelope from LB power spectra using a regressive CNN-LSTM
architecture, which improves speech quality without additional algorithmic delay and can be integrated
into modern speech and audio codecs. Birnbaum et al. (2019) introduce Temporal Feature-Wise Linear
Modulation (TFiLM), a mechanism that captures long-range dependencies by modulating convolutional
activations with contextual information extracted by an RNN. This hybrid design effectively expands the
receptive field of convolutional sequence models with minimal computational overhead, achieving superior SR
performance over the MLP-based model. Hou et al. (2020) combine acoustic features with a low-dimensional
feature vector that summarizes speaker-specific characteristics and phonetic posteriorgrams (PPGs) and
propose a residual dual-path network (RDPN) for BWE. The RDPN segments the input sequence into
overlapping chunks and applies BLSTM layers along intra-chunk and inter-chunk dimensions to capture
utterance-level temporal dependencies while mitigating gradient-vanishing issues. Lin et al. (2021) introduce
a two-stage BWE (2S-BWE) framework that leverages the complementary strengths of frequency-domain
and time-domain modeling to reconstruct high-fidelity speech. The approach employs a spectrogram-based
network—utilizing either a convolutional recurrent network (CRN) (Tan & Wang, 2018) or a temporal
convolutional network (TCN) (Bai et al., 2018)—to estimate the HF spectral envelope, followed by a Wave-
U-Net-based (Stoller et al., 2018) refinement stage trained with a multi-resolution STFT loss (Defossez
et al., 2020) to improve phase consistency and enhance temporal details. Edraki et al. (2024) investigate
BWE for bone-conducted speech using a deep complex convolutional-recurrent network (DCCRN) (Hu et al.,
2020), which comprises a pair of complex two-dimensional (2-D) convolutional encoder-decoder and stacked
LSTM layers. To improve generalization in this highly speaker-dependent setting, the authors introduce a
bottleneck module that disentangles speaker-specific characteristics from speech content, therefore enabling
the architecture to adapt more effectively to unseen speakers.
5.3
Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)
CNN-based BWE/SR models can be broadly grouped into three categories:
plain feed-forward
CNNs (Schmidt & Edler, 2018), autoencoder-based CNNs (Dong et al., 2020; Wang & Wang, 2020; Hou
et al., 2020; Wang & Wang, 2021; Edraki et al., 2024), and U-Net-based CNNs (Kuleshov et al., 2017; Lim
et al., 2018; Birnbaum et al., 2019; Lin et al., 2021; Rakotonirina, 2021; Nguyen et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023;
Li et al., 2023a; Kim et al., 2024a; Tamiti et al., 2025). Earlier research primarily adopted plain feed-forward
CNNs that process features at a single scale via sequential convolutions or autoencoder-based CNNs that
employ an encoder-decoder structure to compress features into a bottleneck representation before reconstruc-
tion. Dong et al. (2020) propose a BWE method based on a time-frequency network whose dual branches
are constructed from an autoencoder architecture enhanced with channel-attention and non-local modules
to achieve richer channel-wise and spatial feature modeling. By jointly predicting waveform-level phase in
the time branch and spectral-magnitude information in the frequency branch, the model enables concurrent
phase-magnitude modeling and avoids invalid STFT reconstruction. Wang & Wang (2020); Wang & Wang
(2021) employ an autoencoder architecture called AECNN (Pandey & Wang, 2019) and introduce cross-
domain training objectives for SSR, combining time-domain waveform losses with frequency-domain STFT
losses to achieve balanced reconstruction of temporal and spectral structures. In addition, the transposed-
convolution upsampling layers in AECNN are replaced with subpixel layers to mitigate artifacts and improve
computational efficiency. As discussed in the previous section, Hou et al. (2020); Edraki et al. (2024) inte-
grate LSTM modules into an autoencoder-based BWE framework. Although the above methods are effective,
plain feed-forward CNNs are limited by single-scale receptive fields, while autoencoder-based models may
suffer from information loss due to heavy bottleneck compression.

5.4
Transformer
Transformers have increasingly been adopted for BWE/SR due to their ability to capture long-range tempo-
ral and spectral dependencies through a global self-attention mechanism. Unlike convolutional models, which
are constrained by local receptive fields, Transformers can model interactions between distant time-frequency
regions, which are crucial for restoring HF harmonics and capturing phoneme-level context. Transformer-
based discriminative models (Rakotonirina, 2021; Nguyen et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2024a; Tamiti et al., 2025;
Liu et al., 2025a) are commonly embedded within U-Net structures, where self-attention modules replace
or augment convolutional blocks at the bottleneck or intermediate feature levels, enabling global context
modeling while retaining the multi-scale benefits of U-Net feature fusion. AFiLM (Rakotonirina, 2021) is
among the first to introduce Transformers into SR, replacing RNN-based conditioning with a self-attention
mechanism through an Attention-based Feature-Wise Linear Modulation layer. AFiLM enables the model
to capture long-range temporal dependencies beyond the limits of local convolutions, which leads to faster
training and improved reconstruction accuracy. Subsequently, Nguyen et al. (2022) propose Transformer-

5.5
Mamba
Mamba (Gu & Dao, 2024) is a sequence modeling architecture based on selective state-space models (SSMs)
that enables efficient modeling of long-range dependencies with linear computational complexity in sequence
length. Compared with CNNs, which rely on finite receptive fields or deep stacking to capture long-term
context, Mamba naturally models global temporal dependencies through its state-space formulation. In con-
trast to Transformer-based models, whose self-attention incurs quadratic complexity with respect to sequence
length, Mamba achieves scalable and memory-efficient sequence modeling while maintaining strong expressive
power. TRAMBA (Sui et al., 2024) presents a hybrid Transformer-Mamba SSR framework specifically for
deployment on mobile and wearable platforms. By pretraining on large-scale speech datasets and fine-tuning
with only a small amount of user-specific bone-conduction data, TRAMBA effectively addresses data scarcity
and reduces the burden of modality-specific data collection. Extensive evaluations show that TRAMBA
achieves substantial improvements in speech quality and intelligibility while maintaining a compact model
size and enabling highly efficient inference. Furthermore, real-system integration and user studies demon-
strate that TRAMBA delivers robust SSR performance in noisy environments and significantly improves
wearable battery life by reducing sensing and data transmission requirements. Beyond purely discriminative
settings, Mamba-based designs have also been adopted within generative frameworks: Wave-U-Mamba (Lee
& Kim, 2025), VM-ASR (Zhang & Ruan, 2025), EDNet (Kwak et al., 2026), HCGAN (Gao et al., 2026b),
and SEMamba++ (Lee & Choi, 2026) integrate Mamba blocks into GAN generators (discussed in Section 6),
and VQ-Diffusion (Fang et al., 2025) employs a Mamba-2 backbone within a discrete diffusion model for
token-level BWE. This cross-paradigm adoption underscores the versatility of state-space sequence modeling
as an efficient alternative to self-attention across the full spectrum of BWE/SR architectures.
Across the discriminative paradigm, the progression from MLPs to RNNs, CNNs, Transformers, and Mam-
bas reflects a consistent drive toward richer temporal context, greater spectral detail, and more efficient
computation. While these models achieve strong objective performance, they share a fundamental limita-
tion: trained with distance-based losses, they tend to produce over-smoothed reconstructions that lack the
fine stochastic HF structure of natural audio. This motivates the shift to generative modeling frameworks,
surveyed in the following section.

6.3
Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)
GANs constitute one of the most prominent families of generative models, demonstrating substantial success
across image domains, including text-to-image synthesis (Reed et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2019; Kang et al., 2023),
image SR (Ledig et al., 2017; Bulat et al., 2018; Mahapatra et al., 2019), and image style transfer (Azadi
et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2019a). Motivated by these advances, GANs have been increasingly
adopted in audio processing applications, including SE (Donahue et al., 2018a; Soni et al., 2018; Cao et al.,
2022), audio synthesis (Donahue et al., 2018b; Engel et al., 2019; Morrison et al., 2021), and BWE/SR (Kim &
Sathe, 2019; Su et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022a; Hauret et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024a; Lee & Kim, 2025). Within
the BWE/SR paradigm, a generator network G reconstructs the BB/HR feature representation or waveform
from the corresponding BL/LR input x, while a discriminator network D is trained to distinguish between
the generated output ˆy and BB/HR target y, thereby establishing a min-max adversarial optimization
framework. This training mechanism encourages the generator to produce BB/HR signals that preserve
natural harmonic structures and exhibit spectral characteristics consistent with authentic audio.
GAN-based BWE/SR approaches can be systematically categorized along three principal design dimensions:
(i) generator specification, (ii) discriminator configuration, and (iii) training objective formulation.
The
generator specification addresses whether the generator operates as a standalone model or as a submodule
within a composite framework, as well as the processing domain (e.g., waveform-based or spectrogram-based)
and the neural architecture (e.g., MLP, RNN, CNN, Transformer, or Mamba). Table 4 provides a taxonomy
of GAN-based BWE/SR frameworks according to the generator’s processing domain and architectures. The
discriminator configuration specifies whether a single discriminator or multiple discriminators are employed;
the former evaluates audio realism independently, whereas the latter operate in parallel to jointly assess
perceptual fidelity across multiple representations. Finally, the training objective formulation specifies the
loss function design. Collectively, these design factors govern the perceptual quality and spectral fidelity of
the reconstructed signals, as well as the system robustness.

networks which operate directly on raw waveforms and spectrogram-based networks which operate on time-

while VM-ASR (Zhang & Ruan, 2025) applies VMamba-style Visual State Space blocks (Liu et al., 2024b)

and improve harmonic reconstruction. EDNet (Kwak et al., 2026) adopts a dual-stream architecture where
Gating Mamba modules integrate magnitude masking and mapping to guide phase reconstruction through
long-range temporal–spectral modeling. HCGAN (Gao et al., 2026b) employs a dual-branch generator con-

two-stage SSR pipeline, in which a ResUNet-based analysis network (Kong et al., 2021a) first predicts the

in which the first stage employs a U-Net-based generator with adversarial training to perform BWE on

et al., 2021b) is trained separately to reconstruct high-fidelity waveforms from the extended spectral features.

Discriminator
Early GANs (Li et al., 2018; Eskimez & Koishida, 2019; Li et al., 2019; Sautter et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2020)
typically employ a single discriminator operating on either spectral features or raw waveforms to evaluate
signal realism and perceptual fidelity. While MLP-based discriminators (Li et al., 2018) operate on spectral
parameters such as LSF and energy, CNN-based architectures leverage a variety of input representations,
including full-band LPS (Eskimez & Koishida, 2019), MFCC (Li et al., 2019), mel-spectrograms (Haws & Cui,
2019), and raw waveforms (Kim & Sathe, 2019; Hao et al., 2020). Subsequent works adopt multi-discriminator
architectures to provide complementary adversarial guidance from multiple perceptual perspectives, enabling
joint modeling of global structure and fine-grained temporal details. The majority of works (Li et al., 2021;
Su et al., 2021; Moliner & Välimäki, 2022; Andreev et al., 2023; Gómez et al., 2023; Hauret et al., 2023;
Mandel et al., 2023; Shuai et al., 2023; Soltanmohammadi et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024;
Kim et al., 2024b; Lu et al., 2024a;b; Lee & Kim, 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Zhang &
Ruan, 2025; Gao et al., 2026a; Lee & Choi, 2026) employ either the multi-scale discriminator (MSD) from
MelGAN (Kumar et al., 2019a) or the multi-period discriminator (MPD) from HiFi-GAN (Kong et al.,
2020). MSD evaluates waveforms at multiple temporal resolutions (full, 2×, and 4× downsampled), allowing
lower-resolution branches to capture LF structures such as coarse spectral envelopes and prosodic patterns,
while the full-resolution branch preserves HF details and transient characteristics. In contrast, MPD analyzes
periodically sampled waveform segments via 2-D convolutions, explicitly modeling pitch-dependent harmonic
regularity and periodic structure that conventional time-domain discriminators often fail to capture.
Alternative designs (Su et al., 2021; Yoneyama et al., 2023; Hauret et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2024a;b; Zhao
et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025; Tamiti & Barua, 2025; Lee & Choi, 2026) further diversify discriminator
architectures.
Su et al. (2021) introduce a spectral-domain discriminator operating on log-mel spectro-
grams to enhance HF reconstruction. Yoneyama et al. (2023) combine waveform-level (Yamamoto et al.,
2020) and multi-band spectral discriminators (Kumar et al., 2020) for independent frequency-band analysis.
EBEN (Hauret et al., 2023) employs a multi-scale ensemble discriminator on PQMF-decomposed subbands
with a full-scale MSD sub-discriminator to enforce cross-band coherence. Lu et al. (2024a;b) introduce a
multi-resolution amplitude discriminator (MRAD) and a multi-resolution phase discriminator (MRPD), in-
spired by the multi-resolution discriminator (MRD) (Jang et al., 2021), operating across multiple STFT
resolutions to alleviate spectral over-smoothing and phase distortion artifacts. HiFi-SR (Zhao et al., 2025)
addresses the limited phase sensitivity of MRD via a multi-band, multi-scale time-frequency discrimina-
tor (MBD) that jointly analyzes complex STFT components across time scales and frequency subbands.
Yuan et al. (2025) design a high-band multi-band discriminator (HB-MBD) that focuses on reconstructing
HF content in the MDCT domain. Tamiti & Barua (2025) introduce a multi-scale recurrence discriminator
(MSRD) and a multi-resolution Lyapunov discriminator (MRLD), which impose nonlinear dynamical-system
constraints to enable lightweight, depthwise-separable discriminator designs. In addition, SEMamba++ (Lee
& Choi, 2026) employs a multi-scale sub-band constant-Q transform (MS-SB-CQT) discriminator (Gu et al.,
2024) to better capture periodic and harmonic structures.

7
Challenges and Overlook
7.1
Discussion
Discriminative DNN-based models have driven major progress in BWE/SR, yet their core limitation is
structural: they implement a deterministic mapping from a BL/LR observation to a single BB/HR estimate.
Since HF recovery is an inherently one-to-many problem, point-estimation training—often dominated by
sample-level or spectral-point losses—tends to collapse conditional uncertainty, leading to a regression-to-
the-mean effect.
Empirically, the reconstructed audio signals often exhibit spectral over-smoothing and
reduced harmonic, transient, and aperiodic micro-structure. Consequently, such models may preserve LF
fidelity while under-delivering perceptual brightness, detail, and naturalness.
In contrast, generative modeling—including AR models, VAEs, GANs, diffusion and score-based models,
flow-based models, and bridge models—reframes BWE/SR as learning a conditional distribution rather than
a deterministic mapping. This probabilistic perspective better reflects the inherent ambiguity of the task: the
missing HF band is not uniquely determined by the observed LF content. A principled model should therefore
represent conditional uncertainty and support multiple plausible reconstructions. Consequently, generative
approaches can synthesize HF details that are consistent with semantic content, timbral structure, and event
characteristics across speech, music, and general sounds, rather than regressing toward an average spectrum.
This explains the shift toward conditional generative modeling, where conditioning enforces fidelity to the
observed band while distribution modeling enhances perceptual realism.
Within the generative family, GANs were early attempts to improve perceptual sharpness through adver-
sarial learning. However, they have become less dominant in recent BWE/SR research because adversarial
objectives can be unstable and prone to generating HF details that sound sharp but are weakly constrained
by the observed LF content. These trade-offs have motivated increasing interest in generative paradigms
with more explicit sampling dynamics and better-controlled conditional generation.
Diffusion, flow, and bridge-based models address this limitation in different ways.
Diffusion and score-
based models capture complex conditional distributions through iterative denoising, often achieving high
perceptual quality and strong mode coverage, but at the cost of multi-step sampling latency (Han & Lee, 2022;
Liu et al., 2024a). Flow-based models instead learn continuous transformations between simple and data
distributions, offering a more direct path toward efficient generation and few-step sampling for low-latency
inference (Welker et al., 2025; Hsieh & Braun, 2026). Bridge models further generalize this view by learning
stochastic paths between degraded and clean signals, which is conceptually well aligned with restoration
tasks, but they often introduce additional modeling and sampling complexity (Li et al., 2025a;b). In this
landscape, flow-based approaches are particularly favorable for BWE/SR as shown in Fig. 1, because they
offer a practical balance between generative flexibility and inference efficiency: they retain the distributional
modeling benefits needed for plausible HF synthesis while enabling faster sampling than standard diffusion
models, making them attractive for real-time audio reconstruction.
7.2
Challenges and Open Problems
Despite the growing success of diffusion, bridge, and flow-based models for BWE/SR, several challenges
remain open. First, high perceptual quality often comes with nontrivial inference cost, especially for iterative
27


## Page 28


diffusion and bridge models, making low-latency or real-time deployment difficult; although flow-based and
flow-matching methods improve efficiency, their quality-speed trade-offs remain task- and design-dependent.
Second, evaluation remains a major bottleneck, as common objective metrics may not reliably capture
perceptual naturalness, artifact suppression, or the realism of hallucinated HF details; this limitation is
especially pronounced for music and general audio, where perceived quality depends on timbre, texture, and
event realism rather than waveform fidelity alone. Third, robustness to real-world degradations remains
limited, since many systems are trained on simplified low-pass or downsampling operators and may degrade
under codec artifacts, noise, device coloration, unknown bandwidth constraints, and broader domain shifts.
Finally, the design space of diffusion schedules, bridge endpoints, flow paths, conditioning mechanisms, and
neural representations remains underexplored, leaving open questions about how to balance fidelity, diversity,
controllability, and efficiency in next-generation generative BWE/SR systems.
7.3
Future Outlook
Recent significant advances in LLMs (Touvron et al., 2023; DeepSeek-AI, 2024; Nie et al., 2025; Li et al.,
2025d; Wang et al., 2025b; Wu et al., 2025), large audio-language models (LALMs) (Chen et al., 2025; Chu
et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2025; 2026; Wu et al., 2025; Rong et al., 2026; Gong et al., 2026), and multimodal
pretraining (Xu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025a; Kong et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b) suggest several
promising directions for BWE/SR. First, large Transformer-based foundation models trained on audio-text
or broader multimodal corpora can provide semantically structured priors over speech, music, general sounds,
and complex acoustic scenes. Conditioning BWE/SR models on such high-level representations may help
constrain HF synthesis, reducing implausible hallucination while preserving perceptually meaningful detail.
Second, retrieval-augmented paradigms offer a possible route toward more controllable BWE/SR. Instead of
relying solely on parametric model memory, future systems could retrieve high-quality exemplars matched
by speaker identity, instrument family, acoustic scene, timbral profile, or caption-level semantics, and use
these references to guide HF reconstruction conditioned on the degraded input. Such retrieval mechanisms
may be especially useful when the missing bandwidth depends strongly on source-specific structure, but they
also raise open questions about retrieval quality, reference mismatch, and computational overhead.
Additionally, self-supervised and multimodal representation learning may improve robustness and evaluation.
Objectives such as contrastive learning, masked audio modeling, and audio-language alignment can produce
representations that transfer across restoration tasks and degradation conditions, potentially improving gen-
eralization to noise, codec artifacts, device coloration, and unseen bandwidth constraints. In parallel, learned
perceptual metrics derived from audio-language or audio-visual models may better capture human judgments
of naturalness, timbre, texture, and event realism than conventional signal-based measures, making them
useful for evaluation, perceptual fine-tuning, and guided generation.
Overall, future progress in BWE/SR will likely depend not only on stronger generative backbones, but
also on improved conditioning, retrieval, perceptual evaluation, and deployment-aware optimization. Joint
advances across these directions will be essential for developing robust, efficient, and perceptually faithful
systems across speech, music, and general audio.
8
Conclusion
This survey presented a unified review of BWE and SR, treating both as ill-posed HF reconstruction problems
while clarifying their relationship and distinctions. We traced the field’s evolution from discriminative DNN-
based models to modern generative paradigms, including AR models, VAEs, GANs, diffusion and score-
based models, flow-based models, and Schrödinger bridges. From this perspective, we highlighted a central
transition in BWE/SR: from point estimation, which often leads to regression-to-the-mean behavior and
spectral over-smoothing, to distribution-aware generation, which better captures the one-to-many nature of
missing HF content. We further summarized key trade-offs among existing methods in reconstruction fidelity,
perceptual quality, robustness, controllability, and computational efficiency, and identified open challenges
in scalable deployment, perceptual evaluation, and real-world generalization. We hope this survey provides
28


## Page 29


a clear taxonomy and practical roadmap for advancing robust, efficient, and perceptually faithful BWE/SR
systems across speech, music, and general audio.
