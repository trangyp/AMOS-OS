Abstract. Distributed protocols are the linchpin of the modern inter-

a massive body of research ensuring the security, reliability, and per-
formance of distributed protocols. In these works, a wide-ranging as-

controlled channels, where messages can be arbitrarily inserted, dropped,
replayed, or reordered. Formal verification work targeting distributed
protocols typically defines its own notion of faulty or malicious channels,

Introduction
Distributed protocols are the linchpin of the modern internet, representing the
fundamental communication and coordination backbone of all modern services
built on top of it. The vast importance of distributed protocols has motivated
ample research in ensuring their security, reliability, and performance.
In practice, the communication channels between distributed protocol endpoints
are imperfect: messages can be dropped due to network congestion, replayed by
adversaries, or reordered due to multi-path routing. These channel-level faults
are a source of subtle bugs, denial-of-service vulnerabilities, and safety violations
in distributed systems. Despite this, the landscape of tools for systematically
analyzing channel faults remains fragmented and largely manual.
On the practical side, several tools exist for testing network protocol implemen-
tations under adverse channels. Scapy [2] is a Python-based packet manipula-
tion library enabling users to forge, send, and capture arbitrary network pack-
ets, Linux netem [10] provides kernel-level network emulation, PacketDrill [5]
supports testing of TCP/UDP/IP implementations through injecting precisely-
timed packet sequences, and TCP-Fuzz supports randomized testing and fuzzing
approaches.

These tools are invaluable in practice but share a fundamental limitation: they
provide no completeness guarantees. That is, when these tools terminate without
finding a bug, the user cannot conclude the absence of bugs.
On the formal methods side, there has been significant work verifying distributed
protocols under faulty communication channels, with the caveat that the capa-
bilities of the attacker must be manually defined. von Hippel et al. presented
an automated attack synthesis framework targeting communication channels for
distributed systems, supporting an attacker that may only arbitrarily insert mes-
sages onto the victim channel [22]. This attack synthesis framework was later
used to analyze the Stream Control Transmission Protocol, where Ginesin et
al. modeled different types of attackers by specifying exact message types the
different attackers may insert onto the channel [8]. Raft has been verified us-
ing mechanized proof assistants such as Rocq via the Verdi framework [24], and
Paxos has been verified using IronFleet [9]; in both scenarios, ad-hoc presenta-
tions of channel faults are verified against. Cryptographic protocol verifiers such
as Tamarin [15] and ProVerif [3] provide automated analysis of cryptographic
properties when processes communicate over an untrusted channel, but do not
target channel-level faults. Model checkers such as Spin [11] and TLA+ [13] have
been applied to distributed protocols, but there exists very little systematic sup-
port for reasoning directly about channel faults.
As it stands, the current formal methods-driven approaches for protocol vali-
dation lack support for automated channel fault analysis, while testing-driven
approaches remain fundamentally incomplete. To fill in this gap in the literature,
we propose a general methodology for the automated discovery of attack traces,
counterexamples, and formal guarantees across various channel configurations.
To realize our methodology we introduce Tofu, a highly generalizable tool for
synthesizing attacks on the faulty communication channels of distributed pro-
tocols that supports attacker models capable of arbitrarily dropping, replaying,
reordering messages. Tofu targets the communication channels between pro-
tocol endpoints and synthesizes attacks to violate general linear temporal logic
(LTL) specifications. Tofu is designed to either synthesize an attack or prove
the absence of such via an exhaustive state-space search. Tofu is sound and
complete: if there exists an attack, Tofu will find it, and Tofu will never pro-
duce false positives. Tofu is also designed to be easy to use once the protocol
model is constructed: all a user must do is select the victim channels, select the
desired attacker models, and invoke Tofu.
We summarize our contributions:
– We present generalizable gadgets representing attackers capable of dropping,
replaying, and reordering messages on communication channels of distributed
protocols, and we formally describe their construction.
– We present Tofu, a tool for synthesizing attacks against distributed commu-
nication protocols. Tofu supports three general attacker models: an attacker
that can drop, replay, or reorder messages on a channel.

Preliminaries
In this section we introduce the mathematical foundations underpinning Tofu.
We define linear temporal logic and Büchi Automata, formalize distributed pro-
tocols as compositions of communicating processes, and state the attack synthe-
sis problem.
2.1
Linear Temporal Logic and the Büchi Automata
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) [18] is a modal logic for specifying properties
of infinite sequences of program states. LTL extends propositional logic with
temporal operators that express how propositions evolve over time along an
execution.
Definition 1 (LTL Syntax). Given a finite set of atomic propositions AP,
LTL formulas are defined inductively:
ϕ ::= ⊤| p | ¬ϕ | ϕ1 ∧ϕ2 | X ϕ | ϕ1 U ϕ2
where p ∈AP, X (next) asserts ϕ holds in the next state, and U (until)
asserts ϕ1 holds continuously until ϕ2 holds.
The standard LTL derived operators are defined as: F ϕ ≡⊤U ϕ (eventually,
asserting ϕ holds at some future state), G ϕ ≡¬F ¬ϕ (always, asserting ϕ
holds at every future state), and ϕ1 ⇒ϕ2 ≡¬ϕ1 ∨ϕ2 (implies).
LTL properties are commonly classified into safety and liveness. Safety proper-
ties assert that “a bad thing never happens” and can be violated by finite prefixes,
while liveness properties assert that “a good thing eventually happens” and re-
quire infinite traces to be violated. This distinction is important for Tofu’s
gadget design, as we discuss in Section 3.
We say a program P models a property ϕ, written P |= ϕ, if ϕ holds over
every infinite execution trace of P. LTL model checking, the problem of deciding
whether P |= ϕ for a finite-state P, is decidable, and model checkers will always
terminate given sufficient resources.
LTL is fundamentally equivalent to the Büchi Automaton, which expresses lan-
guages over infinite words. Büchi Automata are defined as follows.

Definition 2 (Büchi Automaton). A Büchi automaton (BA) is a tuple
B = (Q, Σ, δ, Q0, F) where:
– Q is a finite set of states,
– Σ is a finite alphabet,
– δ ⊆Q × Σ × Q is a transition relation,
– Q0 ⊆Q is a set of initial states,
– F ⊆Q is a set of accepting states.
A run of B on an infinite word w = a0a1a2 · · · ∈Σω is an infinite sequence
of states q0, q1, q2, . . . such that q0 ∈Q0 and (qi, ai, qi+1) ∈δ for all i ≥0.
The run is accepting if it visits states in F infinitely often. The language of
B, denoted L(B), is the set of all infinite words for which B has an accepting
run.
Model checkers such as Spin are fundamentally reliant on the equivalence of
LTL and Büchi Automata to implement LTL model checking [11]. In LTL model
checking, a program is modeled as a Büchi Automata, and the LTL property of
interest is negated then converted to another Büchi Automata – finally, both
automata are intersected and exhaustively searched for accepting runs [21].
The execution semantics, as well as the formal equivalence relation between LTL
and Büchi Automata, are elaborated upon in Appendix Section 8.1.
2.2
Processes and Channels
We formalize distributed protocol components as processes — labeled transition
systems augmented with input/output distinctions on transitions, following the
distributed protocol synthesis framework of Alur and Tripakis [1].
Definition 3 (Process). A process is a tuple P = ⟨AP, I, O, S, s0, T, L⟩
where AP is a finite set of atomic propositions; I and O are finite, disjoint
sets of input and output actions, respectively; S is a finite set of states with
initial state s0 ∈S; T ⊆S × (I ∪O ∪{τ}) × S is the transition relation,
where τ /∈I ∪O denotes a silent internal action; and L : S →2AP is a
labeling function mapping each state to the set of atomic propositions true
in that state. A transition (s, x, s′) ∈T is an input transition if x ∈I, an
output transition if x ∈O, and an internal transition if x = τ.
Communication between processes occurs over channels. Channels mediate all
inter-process communication by providing buffered, typed message conduits.
Definition 4 (Channel Process). A channel is a tuple c = (M, k) where
M is a finite message domain (the set of messages that can be transmitted
over c) and k ∈N is the buffer capacity. The state of a channel at any point
in an execution is a sequence b ∈M ≤k (i.e., a sequence over M of length at
most k), representing the contents of the channel’s FIFO buffer.

2.4
Attack Synthesis
LTL attack synthesis [22] is the dual of LTL program synthesis. In program
synthesis, given an LTL specification ϕ, the goal is to derive a program P such
that P |= ϕ. In attack synthesis, the problem is inverted: given a system P and
a property ϕ such that P |= ϕ, we ask whether there exists some process A, the
attacker process, such that (P ∥A) ̸|= ϕ.
Definition 6 (Threat Model). A threat model is a tuple (P, (Qi)m
i=0, ϕ)
where:
– P, Q0, . . . , Qm are processes,
– each Qi has AP = ∅,
– ϕ is an LTL formula such that P ∥Q0 ∥· · · ∥Qm |= ϕ,
– the composite system has at least one infinite run (non-triviality).
The attack synthesis problem (R-∃ASP) asks: given a threat model (P, (Qi)m
i=0, ϕ),
does there exist an attacker A whose I/O interface is a subset of that of the Qi
such that P ∥Daisy(Q0) ∥· · · ∥Daisy(Qm) ̸|= ϕ? Here, Daisy(Qi) is a gadget
that nondeterministically exercises the full I/O interface of Qi, as defined by von
Hippel et al. [22].
In the general attack synthesis framework, the Qi are arbitrary environment
processes; however, in Tofu, the Qi are instantiated as the channel processes
mediating communication between the protocol endpoints in P. That is, each Qi
models a communication channel (Definition 4), and Tofu synthesizes attackers
that replace one or more Qi with fault gadgets exercising the same I/O interface.
Given a user-selected subset of victim channels {Qi1, . . . , Qij} ⊆{Q0, . . . , Qm},
and letting Gadget(Qj) denote the fault gadget (Section 3) synthesized by Tofu
for channel Qj, Tofu then checks whether:
P ∥Gadget(Q0) ∥· · · ∥Gadget(Qj) ̸|= ϕ

Properties of the Gadget Constructions
We state four properties of the gadget constructions that are essential for the
correctness of Tofu.
Theorem 3 (Finiteness). For any finite message domain M and fault limit ℓ,
the state sets of Dℓ(c), Rℓ(c), and Oℓ(c) are finite.
Proof. The state set of each gadget is a product of a finite set of control locations,
a bounded counter in {0, . . . , ℓ}, and a buffer drawn from Bufℓ(M), which has
Pℓ
i=0 |M|i elements. All components are finite.

4
Tofu Architecture
In this section we discuss the design and implementation of Tofu.
4.1
High-Level Design
Tofu is designed to synthesize attacks with respect to imperfect channels. That
is, Tofu synthesizes attacks that involve replaying, dropping, or reordering mes-
sages on one or more communication channels relied upon by the victim protocol.
Tofu targets user-specified communication channels in state machine-based for-
mal models of distributed protocols. To use Tofu, the user provides: (1) a formal
model of a distributed protocol in the Promela language, (2) the communica-
tion channel(s) to attack, (3) the desired attacker model (drop, replay, reorder),
and (4) a formalized correctness property in LTL. The protocol model should
satisfy the correctness property in the absence of any attacker.
Once invoked, Tofu parses the Promela model, identifies the target chan-
nels and their message types, automatically synthesizes the appropriate attacker
gadgets, and injects them into the model. The modified model is then passed
to Spin, which performs an exhaustive state-space search. If an attack exists,
Spin returns a counterexample trace; otherwise, Spin certifies the absence of an
attack via complete state-space exploration.
4.2
The Spin Model Checker
We implement Tofu on top of Spin, a model checker for reasoning about dis-
tributed and concurrent systems [11]. Models in Spin are written in Promela, a
modeling language for communicating state machines. Promela processes com-
municate via built-in channel structures, which can be either synchronous (buffer
size 0) or asynchronous (buffer size > 0). Tofu generates attacker gadgets with
respect to these channels.
Internally, Spin reduces verification problems to deciding Büchi Automata inter-
section emptiness via the LTL–BA correspondence (Theorem 5). Given n Büchi
Automata P1, . . . , Pn representing the protocol processes and the negation of the

of {int, int, int}) and indexed multi-channels (e.g., chan c[N] = [8] of
{int}). The parsed channel types determine the shape of the variables and
buffers used in the synthesized gadgets.
Drop gadget implementation. The drop gadget is synthesized as a Promela
proctype that monitors the target channel using the polling operator ?[...].
When a message is detected and the drop counter has not been exhausted, the
gadget atomically consumes the message via ? and decrements the counter. The
skip(c) action is implemented by tracking len(c) and blocking until the channel
length changes, as described in Section 3.4.
Replay gadget implementation. The replay gadget is synthesized with a local
Promela channel gadget_mem serving as the internal FIFO buffer Bufℓ(M). In
the Consume phase, messages are copied from the target channel using the non-
destructive receive operator ?<...> (corresponding to c ? ⟨m⟩) and stored into
gadget_mem. In the Replay phase, messages are nondeterministically selected
from gadget_mem using a rotation idiom: Spin’s select statement generates a
random index, and messages are rotated through the FIFO until the selected
message is at the head, at which point it is dequeued and sent onto the target
channel. Messages may also be silently discarded from the buffer to model partial
replay.

Reorder gadget implementation. The reorder gadget is the most complex. It
is synthesized with Promela’s priority mechanism set to the maximum value
(priority 99 in the implementation), ensuring the gadget always takes precedence
over other processes when consuming messages from the target channel. This is
essential: without priority, other processes may consume messages from c before
the reorder gadget intercepts them, breaking the reordering semantics. The Init
phase uses the skip(c) implementation to nondeterministically skip an arbitrary
prefix of messages. The Consume phase uses the destructive receive ? to remove
messages from the channel into the local buffer. The Replay phase uses the same
rotation-based selection as the replay gadget.
Gadget injection. Once generated, the gadget Promela code is injected into
the user-provided model by appending the generated proctype declarations. The
modified model is then passed to Spin for verification. Multiple gadgets targeting
different channels can be composed in a single model.

Case Study: TCP
The Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) is a transport-layer protocol designed
to establish reliable, ordered communication between two peers. TCP is ubiqui-
tous in today’s internet and has therefore seen ample formal verification efforts,
including using Promela and Spin [6,17].
We constructed a Promela model of TCP referencing the set of TCP RFCs
[19,7]. Our model follows the state-level control flow for connection establish-
ment and teardown, the base-level packet transmission structure, and timeouts.
The model consists of two symmetric TCP processes communicating over two
asynchronous channels, AtoB and BtoA, each with buffer capacity 2. Messages
are drawn from the domain {SYN, FIN, ACK}.
We derive six LTL properties from the RFC specifying the correctness of our
TCP model, and verify that our model satisfies all six under the assumption of
perfect, fault-free channels:
– ϕ1: No half-open connections (safety).
– ϕ2: No infinite stalls or deadlocks (liveness).
– ϕ3: SYN_RECEIVED eventually resolves to ESTABLISHED, FIN_WAIT_1, or CLOSED
(liveness).
– ϕ4: Simultaneous close eventually resolves (liveness).
– ϕ5: Active close eventually terminates (liveness).
– ϕ6: The three-way handshake cannot be bypassed (safety).
Crucially, we purposely do not model implementation-level resilience features
such as retransmission timeouts, duplicate ACK detection, sequence numbers,
acknowledgement numbers, or receive buffers — features designed precisely to
mitigate channel faults in practice. We thoroughly assess our limited TCP model
against Tofu’s drop, replay, and reorder attackers to evaluate their capabilities
in the scenario where the TCP implementation lacks these resilience features.

Related Work
Formal Analysis of Secure Protocols. Several formal methods tools rea-
son about protocol security, primarily in the cryptographic setting, including
Tamarin [15], ProVerif [3], and CryptoVerif [4]. Model checker-based approaches
using Spin [11] or TLA+ [13] have been applied to distributed protocols but have
historically focused on functional correctness rather than channel-level faults.
There is also a long history of using formal methods ad-hoc to reason about on-
channel attackers, particularly in the context of Byzantine protocols, as well as
message tampering, delays, and congestion control [14]. The automated attacker
synthesis framework as presented by von Hippel et al. [22] was also extended to
support stochastic systems by Oakley et al. [16]; however, this extension was de-
signed primarily to target statistical models rather than insecure communication
channels as Tofu does.

Network Testing Tools. On the implementation side, Scapy [2] provides pro-
grammatic packet crafting and injection, netem [10] provides kernel-level fault
injection (loss, delay, reordering), and packetdrill [5] enables scripted network
stack testing. These tools target implementations rather than models and pro-
vide no completeness guarantees. Tofu complements them: it operates on formal
models with sound, complete analysis, while implementation-level tools remain
invaluable for confirming that discovered attacks manifest in practice.
7
Conclusion
We have presented Tofu, a tool for the automated, sound, and complete synthe-
sis of channel fault attacks on distributed protocols. Tofu fills a gap in the formal
verification landscape by providing systematic support for reasoning about drop,
replay, and reorder attackers on communication channels, operations that were
previously modeled only in an ad-hoc fashion. By leveraging Spin, Tofu either
synthesizes concrete attack traces or certifies the absence of attacks via exhaus-
tive state-space search. We have demonstrated Tofu’s applicability through a
case study on TCP, and we release Tofu and all models as open source.

8.2
Partial Order Reduction
Spin employs partial order reduction (POR) to mitigate state-space explosion
by pruning interleavings that lead to equivalent states. However, POR can be
unsound in the presence of certain nondeterministic constructs. We compile all
Tofu models with the -DNOREDUCE flag, disabling POR to ensure the exhaustive
search remains sound and complete. While this increases verification time, it is
necessary to preserve the completeness guarantees of Tofu.
