Hardy spaces and real-variable methods, we refer to the monographs [44, 49, 51, 52, 66, 81, 84].
Motivated by the classical real-variable theory of Hardy spaces, Bu et al. [7] recently initiated the
study of matrix-weighted Hardy spaces with the matrix Ap weight, which reduces to the scalar
Muckenhoupt A1 weight when p ∈(0, 1] and m = 1.
On the other hand, the theory of variable Muckenhoupt Ap(·) weights was initiated by Cruz-
Uribe et al. [30]. Subsequently, Cruz-Uribe and Penrod [37] introduced variable matrix Ap(·)

to [31, 32, 40, 79] for more studies on variable Lebesgue spaces and their corresponding (matrix)
weights, and to [1, 41, 89, 91] for more studies on variable exponent function spaces. Variable
Hardy spaces were introduced by Nakai and Sawano [68] and Cruz-Uribe and Wang [38], and
weighted variable Hardy spaces were later studied by Ho [56]. We refer to [33, 34, 36, 57, 59] for
further developments on variable Hardy spaces and related operator estimates.

This generalization needs an improved approach for the atomic decomposition, which is essen-
tially different from the one used in [7]. Indeed, the key of the approach used in [7] is the level-set
construction. A level set is determined by single or finite scalar quantities related to vector-valued
functions under consideration and therefore records only their sizes, but not directions. However,
in the matrix-weighted setting, the direction of vector-valued functions under consideration mat-
ters under the action of the matrix weight. Thus, the level-set construction has to involve the whole
matrix-weighted object such as W ⃗f, which in turn leads to a stronger assumption of the matrix
weight under consideration, namely W ∈Ap in [7]. We escape this by introducing the convex-
body reducing operator, which represents the local average of convex body valued functions under
consideration via a positive definite matrix. This average serves as a vector-valued substitute for
the scalar level in the classical level-set method. Thus, instead of comparing a pointwise quantity
with a number such as 2j, in this article we introduce the level set EQ in (4.48) with Q being a
dyadic cube via comparing the pointwise convex-valued function with its local average on a given
cube. This, combined with the stopping-time construction appearing in the proof of [29, (2-12)]
(namely a refined Whitney decomposition, which is formulated as Lemma 4.10), yields the atomic
characterization of Hp(·)
W , which allows us to weaken the assumption on matrix weight from Ap in
[7] to Ap(·),∞in the present article. This approach to establish atomic characterization is different
from all previous ones.
In addition, as mentioned above we introduce several convex body valued maximal functions,
which provide a convenient way to separate the matrix action from the construction of the matrix-
weighted maximal functions. This also plays a crucial role in establishing the atomic character-
ization and the mutual equivalences among various maximal function characterizations of Hp(·)
W .
Moreover, Lemmas 4.6 and 4.7 serve as substitutes of the Fefferman–Stein type vector-valued in-
equality, which is commonly used in the variable Hardy space theory. These two estimates are
frequently used in the proofs of the atomic characterization and the boundedness of Calder´on–
Zygmund operators.
