ABSTRACT
We present results from very-long-baseline-interferometry (VLBI) observations of the
10
nucleus in the lobe-dominated quasar 3C207. These observations were completed at 8.4
11
or 10.7 GHz (X-band) from 1981 to 2010, spanning 29 years. The nucleus of 3C207 is
12
the strongest and most variable in the 3CR complete sample of LDQs, which is under
13
study to test relativistic jet models over a wide range of jet orientation angles. Images
14
have typical resolutions of ∼0.5-1.0 milliarcseconds (mas) and sensitivities of ∼0.1-0.2
15
mJy beam−1. The VLBI core region has flux density outbursts at mean intervals of
16
∼7 yr; two of these are multiple outbursts from a stationary “true” core that feeds a
17
“swinging component” ∼0.5 mas to the east. The position angle (PA) of the swinging
18
component shows a long-term increase of ∼40◦, with a short-term reversal of ∼10◦.
19
A one-sided, curved VLBI jet extends ∼25 mas eastward, with components spanning

1. INTRODUCTION
31
This work is part of a larger, long-term very-long-baseline-inteferometry (VLBI) survey to test
32
models of relativistic jets in active galactic nuclei (AGN) and AGN unification (Hough & Readhead
33
1989). In contrast to the various highly productive surveys of bright, compact AGN (e.g., Readhead
34
et al. 1978; Lister et al. 2009), we have selected a complete sample of 25 lobe-dominated quasars
35
(LDQs) from the revised 3CR catalog (Laing et al. 1983). Unlike the bright, compact AGN, the
36
LDQs have been selected by their extended, steep-spectrum radio emission on kpc scales, which
37
minimizes orientation bias due to highly-beamed structures and permits tests of jet models over a
38
wide range of orientations. To define the complete sample, Hough & Readhead (1989) considered
39
all quasars in the 3CR catalog, used a strict flux density cutoff for the extended emission (10 Jy
40
at 178 MHz), and examined source structure and spectra to select all the “double-lobed quasars”
41
(DLQ) with two steep-spectrum lobes straddling a nucleus coincident with the optical identification.
42
We also used the definition of the quantity R to be the ratio of arcsecond nucleus flux density to
43
extended flux density at 5 GHz, following Orr & Browne (1982). Hough et al. (2002) then adopted
44
the term “lobe-dominated quasar” and defined an LDQ to have R < 1. All 25 DLQs were found to
45

53

54

55

56

57

58

61

62

63

67

68

69

70

3. RESULTS
120
3.1. Flux Density Variability in the Milliarcsecond Core Region
121
As the best means of tracking variability, we plot the X-band VLBI flux densities of the milliarc-
122
second core region as a function of time as determined by modelfitting (Figure 2). Super-resolved
123
imaging and modelfitting both show that the core region consists of a close double: the “true” core C
124
(the base of the jet) to the west and a “swinging” component S to the east that appears to maintain
125
a nearly constant radial distance from the core while undergoing significant PA changes; these are
126
discussed further in Section 3.2. The only exception is epoch 2002.03, for which a single elliptical
127
Gaussian component was fitted to the core region. Five “outbursts” are evident, and while the time
128
sampling is not sufficient to define them all precisely, we can characterize the outbursts in terms
129
of their typical spacing, rise and decay times, and rise and decay amplitudes. We identify these
130
outbursts by their highest observed flux densities at 1983.47, 1989.27, 1995.67, 2003.45, and the last
131
epoch 2010.81, where the flux density was still rising. This is suggestive of quasi-periodic events
132


## Page 9

9
with a mean spacing of 6.9 ± 0.9 yr. The rise/decay times and amplitudes are simply defined by
133
adjacent local minima and maxima. With only one observation prior to the 1983.47 maximum, we
134
take the minimum rise time for this event to be 1.7 yr. Including the large pre-outburst gaps, the
135
maximum rise times for the 1989.27 and 1995.67 events are 4.7 and 4.5 yr, respectively. The rise
136
time for the 2003.45 event is 3.8 yr, and the minimum rise time for the 2010.81 event is 2.0 yr. These
137
data are consistent with a typical rise time of ∼3-4 yr. By similar methods, the typical decay time
138
is also ∼3-4 yr. The rise and decay amplitudes range over a factor of ∼10, at ∼100 mJy for 1983.47,
139
∼400 mJy for 1989.27, ∼200 mJy for 1995.67, and ∼1000 mJy for 2003.45; the rise amplitude is
140
at least ∼300 mJy for 2010.81. This indicates that the outbursts may alternate regularly between
141
weaker and stronger amplitudes.

The well-sampled 2003.45 outburst provides the best evidence for nearly symmetrical rise and decay

144

145

146
these events to be at 2001.5, 2003.7, and 2005.7; the first two peaks are between each event’s two
147

148

149
mirrors the first two sub-outbursts at later times; a presumed third S event would have occurred after
150

151
occur at 2002.21 and 2002.96, on either side of a 0.75 yr gap, so that a reasonable estimate of the

153

154

155
S peak. Therefore the two S peaks are ∼2.0 yr apart, and lag the first two C peaks by ∼1.0 yr.
156
Although the peaks for S were not directly observed, the S profiles suggest that these peaks may
157
have approached the ∼900 mJy level of the C peaks. The maxima of S roughly overlap the minima
158
of C, which leads to the flux density of S exceeding that of C when C is near its minima.
159

160

161

162

163

4. DISCUSSION
584
4.1. Variability
585
The quasi-periodic outbursts in the core region of 3C207 have a mean spacing in the co-moving
586
frame of 4.1±0.5 yr, with rise and decay times of ∼2 yr. Despite nearly three decades of observations,
587
the coarse sampling of most of the outbursts does not allow us to make any strong claims regarding
588
periodicity. We do note that periods of order ten years in flux density monitoring data, when well-
589
determined, have been interpreted as evidence for binary black holes in, e.g., OJ287 (Valtonen et al.
590
2006).
The minima between outbursts suggest a quiescent level of ∼400 mJy.
If we define the
591
fractional variability amplitude fv = (Smax −Smin)/Smin, the outbursts range from fv ∼0.25 for
592
1983.47 to fv ∼2.5 for 2003.45. Thus the outbursts range over an order of magnitude in strength.
593
This range could of course be intrinsic. If it is due to redirection of an inner relativistic jet component
594
in the core region, we can estimate the range of angles θ to the line of sight using the Doppler factor
595
δ = [γ(1 −βcosθ)]−1, where γ is the Lorentz factor and β is the speed parameter (assumed constant
596
for this calculation). If we treat the core region as a single flat-spectrum component, then a δ3 ∼10
597
flux density range only requires δ to range over a factor of ∼2. For γ = 10, this is easily accomplished
598
if θ varies from, e.g., 15◦to 10◦.
599
The details of shorter-term variations in flux density and component diameters are of particular
600
interest during the 2003.45 outburst. As we have already seen, there are actually three sub-outbursts
601
of C only ∼1.3 yr apart in the co-moving frame. For the first two sub-outbursts, the component
602


## Page 37

37
S mirrors these with a lag of ∼0.6 yr. The diameter of C is remarkably stable during the entire
603
2003.45 outburst. The diameter of S begins at less than ∼0.2 mas. It first has a distinctly non-zero
604
diameter of 0.16 mas at 2002.2 just prior to the peak of the first S sub-outburst, rises to a maximum
605
of 0.37 mas at 2005.0 just past the peak of the second S sub-outburst, and then eventually drops to
606
0.13 mas.
607
These behaviors for C and S support an interpretation in which three closely-spaced sub-outbursts
608
in C each create a new component of small diameter (less than ∼0.2 mas) that continually expands
609
as it moves out. As the first component passes through the S region and blends with it, the “S
610
blend” increases in diameter. As the second component later does the same, the diameter of the S
611
blend reaches a larger maximum value. This could be because the first new component also remains
612
at least partly blended with S, having not yet completely cleared the S region, at least as can be
613
determined given the lower quality of the 2005.02 data noted previously. By 2005.22, the first new
614
component is seen to be separately resolved from S, and the diameter of S decreases. We note that
615
the 2002.03 epoch – at which the core region could only be modeled with a single elliptical Gaussian
616
component – is halfway between the first maxima for C and S. If the first new component was about
617
midway between C and S at this time, it is plausible that the resulting quasi-continuous line of flux
618
required an elliptical Gaussian representation at our resolution.
619
We identify the newly-resolved component at 2005.22 as J4. The emergence of the second new
620
component is not observed during the 2005 observations, but for 2008 through 2010, J4 has to be
621
represented as a double (sub-components J4a and J4b) or triple (third sub-component Jc). This is
622
fairly convincing evidence that the triple structure of J4 has its origins in the triple sub-outbursts
623
in the core C. It also leads us to the reasonable speculation that the 1989.27 outburst consisted of
624
two sub-outbursts, responsible for the wide diameter of J2 at its first VLBA epoch in 1995.67 and
625
its double nature (J2a and J2b) at all succeeding epochs through 2001.63. It is not clear why J2
626
thereafter presented as a single component from 2001.86 through 2010.81. Component J3 has always
627
appeared as a single component; this raises the possibility that the 1995.67 outburst was a single
628
event producing only one component. Component J1 has only been observed as a single component
629

at large distances from the core, and we have no detailed observations of the 1983.47 outburst (and
630
no observations at all of any earlier events) that may have produced it, so we can say nothing about
631
the nature of its associated outburst.
632
4.2. Component Motions
633
For the swinging component S, the unshifted positions are consistent with no radial motion, but
634
the proper motion using the shifted positions (0.008 ± 0.003 mas yr−1) corresponds to a subluminal
635
apparent transverse velocity (in units of c) βapp = 0.3 ± 0.1. Thus at most, S is undergoing mildly
636
relativistic outward motion. Its angular motions, however, are quite significant. The largest angular
637
motion of ∼5◦yr−1, at the mean d = 0.44 mas, yields a modestly superluminal βapp ∼1.5. This
638
feature must therefore be associated with changes in direction of the mechanism that is ejecting new
639
jet components, and likely represents – in the radial direction – a (nearly-)stationary shock in a
640
(quasi-)continuous flow.
641
The linear fits to core separation d as a function of time for the four jet components yield the
642
following apparent superluminal speeds: βappJ1 = 7.7 ± 1.8, βappJ2 = 9.9 ± 0.5, βappJ3 = 9.7 ± 0.3,
643
and βappJ4 = 3.0 ± 0.7. As discussed in Section 3.4.3, J2 is better modeled with a two-component
644
linear fit that yields βappJ2 = 6.8 ± 0.4 before 2004.5 and βappJ2 = 14.2 ± 0.9 afterwards. Likewise,
645
J3 is better modeled with a two-component linear fit that yields βapp = 12.8 ± 1.2 before 2004.5 and
646
βapp = 9.0±0.3 afterwards. The slower speed for component J4 is consistent with our earlier 15 GHz
647
results (Danielson et al. 2007; Polito & Hough 2010) and the 15 GHz results of Lister et al. (2013).
648
This may be due to both blending with the swinging component S at small radial separations and a
649
lower apparent transverse velocity for J4. We note that despite mistaking S for other components,
650
Hough et al. (2002) did find reasonable speed estimates for J1 (βapp ∼10) and J2 (βapp ∼7), because
651
these components had been recently created at the times of misidentification and were then essentially
652
still coincident with S.

4.3. Model for Jet Cone Opening Angle with Recollimation Zone
654
The time gaps between the outbursts that spawned J2 and J3, and then between those that gave
655
rise to J3 and J4, are roughly equal, and all three outbursts are quite clear in the VLBI flux density
656
record (see Figure 2). However, it is possible that the gap between the outbursts that formed J1 and
657
J2 is considerably longer. Our identification of an outburst peaking at 1983.47 is only tentative; in
658
fact, within the uncertainties, the core region flux density could have been constant between 1981.76
659
and 1984.60. Moreover, the time gap between J1 and J2 is clearly the largest one on the core distance
660
plot in Figure 9.
661
This large time gap is important in attempting to model the projected trajectories of the four jet
662
components in terms of an orderly progression of ejection angle, as in, e.g., a precessing jet. We
663
are compelled to do so by the orderly progression of position angles for the successive components
664
J2, J3, and J4. The trajectories in Figure 15 could possibly be explained by precession– e.g., from
665
a binary black hole or jet/disk misalignment– if the time gap between ejection of J1 and J2 is long
666
enough. For example, J1 may have been ejected when the launching mechanism was directed along
667
the back side of the precession cone with PA ∼90◦. The ejection of J2 may have then have occurred
668
when the launch direction was at or near its smallest (i.e., most northerly) position angle. Then J3
669
and J4 may have followed, closer in time and at successively larger position angles, as the precessing
670
jet axis moved back toward PA ∼90◦along the front side. Beyond ∼2.5 mas, the trajectory of J2
671
strongly suggests a recollimation zone where the opening angle of the jet precession cone is reduced
672
from its value in the inner jet. Finally, we see that individual jet components expand until reaching
673
the recollimation zone (Figure 17).
674
We now have several pieces of evidence that allow us to develop a working model that might explain
675
much of the rich VLBI phenomenology in 3C207. The model requires a quasi-regular motion (perhaps
676
precession) of the swinging component S around the axis of a cone. Deprojection of the observed
677
PA range for S based on the superluminal jet speeds suggests a cone with true half-angle of ∼5-6◦
678
or less. The jet components follow straight paths along this cone, so there is no evidence for helical
679
motion related to magnetic fields. It is interesting that J4, with the slowest motion, is associated
680


## Page 40


40
with the most powerful – and therefore perhaps most highy beamed – outburst in 2003. This could
681
be explained by motion along the cone at a very small angle to our line-of-sight. Beyond 2-3 mas,
682
there is a narrowing of the opening angle in a recollimation zone, which accounts for the redirection
683
of J2 and J3, and seems to be associated with their apparent acceleration and possible deceleration,
684
respectively. While bending of the jet must play some role for both components, it is difficult to
685
produce an increase in J2 from βapp ≈7 to βapp ≈14 without intrinsic acceleration.
686
5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
687
After thirty years, we have a working model for 3C207 in which jet components are ejected at
688
different angles around a cone, with the flow recollimating at 2-3 mas downstream, of order ∼100 pc
689
deprojected distance. But the jet in 3C207 is most likely aimed close to our line-of-sight. So the
690
question remains: How well does our model apply to the other LDQs, taking larger orientation angles,
691
beaming, and projection effects into account? Other sources have evidence of swinging innermost
692
components, but projection effects – and thus observed PA variations – are smaller. Good determi-
693
nations of the amplitude of PA variations in several objects would help to constrain the intrinsic cone
694
angle. Other sources show evidence of acceleration, but we still need to confirm trends observed thus
695
far. It is still unclear the circumstances in which apparent jet acceleration/deceleration occurs, and
696
whether it is explained best by jet bending or intrinsic acceleration. Other sources show bending or
697
misaligned jet features, but it is not clear yet when this may be associated with a recollimation zone
698
as it is for 3C207.
699
Future VLBI monitoring of 3C207 and other LDQs can help to resolve these questions. We note
700
that two additional epochs of VLBA observations of 3C207 were obtained by D.H.H. in 2014.45 and
701
2018.64 for this project but were not modeled. It would be interesting to know if jet components
702
J2-J4 were detected in these epochs, and if new components appeared on the expected time interval
703
of ∼7 yr. However it might be challenging to match components because of the 4-year gaps between
704
these and prior observations.
705
Other VLBI observations of 3C207 exist as well. Using 15 GHz VLBA observations between 1994
706
and 2016, Lister et al. (2019) identify seven moving features in the core of 3C207, with median and
707

708

709

710

Our 3C207 analysis methods can be applied to the five other LDQs in our sample: 3C208, 3C212,
