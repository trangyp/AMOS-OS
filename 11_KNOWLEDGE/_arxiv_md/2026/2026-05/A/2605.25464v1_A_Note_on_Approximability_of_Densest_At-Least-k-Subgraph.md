Abstract
We study the Densest At-Least-k-Subgraph (DALkS) problem, in which we are given
an undirected graph G and an integer k, and the goal is to find a subgraph of G with at
least k vertices with maximum density. The best-known algorithm, independently discovered
by Khuller and Saha [KS09] and by Andersen [And07], yields a 2-approximation for DALkS in
polynomial time.
In this note, we provide a (simple) reduction from Densest k-Subgraph (DkS) to Dens-
est At-Least-k-Subgraph, which shows that, if DkS is hard to approximate to within any
constant factor, then DALkS is hard to approximate to within (3/2 −ε) factor for every ε > 0.
This holds in both the normal (non-parameterized) and the parameterized (by k) settings.
We then generalize the reduction to provide a tight (2 −ε) factor hardness of approximating
Densest At-Least-k-Subgraph, albeit under a stronger hypothesis which roughly states that
Densest k-Subgraph is hard to approximate to within k1−δ factor for any constant δ > 0.
Once again, this extends naturally to the parameterized setting.
Previously, (2 −ε) factor
inapproximability for DALkS was only known under the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis [Ber13,
Man18], which does not apply to the parameterized version of the problem.
Furthermore, we show that the exact version of DALkS is W[1]-hard (parameterized by k).

Introduction
We study the Densest At-Least-k-Subgraph (DALkS) problem, where we are given an undi-
rected graph G and an integer k, and the goal is to find a subgraph of G containing at least
k vertices with maximum density. Here the density of a graph is defined as the ratio between
the number of edges and the number of vertices. This problem was introduced by Andersen and
Chellapilla who presented a 3-approximation algorithm for the problem [AC09].
Later, an im-
proved 2-approximation algorithm was independently discovered by Khuller and Saha [KS09] and
Andersen [And07]; the former authors also proved that solving the problem exactly is NP-hard.
Interestingly, it was then shown independently by Bergner [Ber13] and Manurangsi [Man18] that,
assuming the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis (SSEH) [RS10], the problem is hard to approximate
to within a factor of (2−ε) for any ε > 0, meaning that the aforementioned algorithm is essentially

NP-hardness of approximation of the problem is known, even for a factor of 1.001.
This lack of NP-hardness of approximation for DALkS is, in fact, not a coincidence. Specifically,
this is also the case for a closely related problem, known as Densest k-Subgraph (DkS), which
is the same as DALkS, except that the subgraph is asked to be made of exactly k vertices instead
of at least k vertices. Densest k-Subgraph has been studied long before the introduction of
Densest At-Least-k-Subgraph (see [FKP01]) and, despite efforts in establishing hardness of
approximation for DkS [Fei02, Kho06, AAM+11, BCV+12, BKRW17, Man17, CDGT23], no NP-
hardness of approximation for the problem is known, even for a factor of 1.001. Observe that a
ρ-approximation algorithm for DkS immediately implies a ρ-approximation algorithm for DALkS;
putting it differently, any hardness of approximation for DALkS immediately transfers to a similar
hardness of approximation for DkS. As a result, establishing NP-hardness of approximating DALkS
is at least as hard as doing so for DkS, which, as stated earlier, is a challenging open question.
Besides its approximability, another interesting complexity aspect of DALkS that has so far been
unexplored is its parameterized complexity. A natural parameter for the problem is k, the size
requirement. Recall that an algorithm is said to be fixed parameter tractable (FPT)1 if it runs in
time f(k) · nO(1) for some function f : N →N. Prior to this work, no hardness (or algorithmic)
result has been shown in this setting. Specifically, it could be that there exists an FPT algorithm
for the problem, or that there exists an FPT algorithm that significantly beats the 2-approximation
given by the polynomial time algorithm. The latter premise is particularly plausible, in light of the
fact that quite a few problems, which are known to be hard under SSEH (in the non-parameterized
setting) via “similarly looking” reductions, turn out to admit better approximations in FPT time.
Examples of such problems include Minimum k-Vertex Cover2 and Minimum k-Cut3; similar
to DALkS, both are SSE-hard to approximate to within factor of (2 −ε) for any ε > 0 [GK15,
Man18] but both admit FPT approximation schemes [Mar08, GLL18, Man19, LSS24]. We refer
interested readers to the survey [FKLM20], which discusses more results on FPT approximation in
detail.
1.1
Our Results
Our first result is a “reverse reduction” from DkS to DALkS, which shows that, if there is no
polynomial time algorithm that approximates DkS to within any constant factor, then there is no
polynomial time algorithm that approximates DALkS to within (3/2 −ε) factor for any ε > 0.
The reduction also works in the FPT regime; that is, if we assume further that there is no FPT
(parameterized by k) algorithm that approximates DkS to within any constant factor, then there
is no FPT algorithm that approximates DALkS to within (3/2 −ε) factor for any ε > 0.
To be more formal, let us introduce a few notations. For any undirected graph G, we use VG and EG
to denote its vertex set and edge set, respectively. For S ⊆VG, we use G[S] to denote the induced
subgraph of G on S. Let max-edge=k(G) := maxS⊆VG,|S|=k |EG[S]|, i.e., the maximum number of
edges in any k-vertex subgraphs of G; max-edge⩽k(G), max-edge⩾k(G) are defined similarly. We
use den(G) to denote the density of G, i.e., den(G) := |EG|/|VG|. For convenience, we use den=k(G)
to denote maxS⊆VG,|S|=k den(G[S]); den⩽k(G) and den⩾k(G) are defined in similar manners.

The reductions in the paper will be formalized through gap problems. The starting point of our
first result is the gap version of the Densest k-Subgraph problem, as defined below.
Definition 1 For λ ⩾1, the GapDkS(λ) problem is: given a graph G and k, ℓ∈N, distinguish
between (Completeness) max-edge=k(G) ⩾ℓ, and, (Soundness) max-edge=k(G) < ℓ/λ.
Our first result can then be stated as follows.
Theorem 2 Suppose that, for any λ ⩾1, no polynomial time algorithm solves GapDkS(λ). Then,
for any ε > 0, no polynomial time algorithm approximates DALkS to within a factor of (3/2 −ε).
Moreover, if we assume further that, for any λ ⩾1 and f : N →N, no f(k) · nO(1) time algorithm
solves GapDkS(λ), then, for any ε > 0, no f(k) · nO(1) time algorithm approximates DALkS to
within a factor of (3/2 −ε).
Although, as stated earlier, no NP-hardness of approximation of DkS is known, hardness of ap-
proximation under stronger assumptions is known. In particular, DkS is hard to approximate to
within any constant factor assuming the Random 3-SAT (R3SAT) Hypothesis4, the Planted Clique
Hypothesis5 [AAM+11], or the Exponential time Hypothesis (ETH)6 [Man17]; in the parameter-
ized setting, DkS is known to be hard to approximate to within any constant factor assuming
Gap-ETH7 [CCK+17]. As a result, the following is an immediate consequence of Theorem 2:
Corollary 3 Assuming Planted Clique Hypothesis, R3SAT Hypothesis, or ETH, there is no poly-
nomial time (3/2 −ε)-approximation algorithm for DALkS for any ε > 0. Moreover, assuming
Gap-ETH, for any function f : N →N, there is no f(k) · nO(1) time (3/2 −ε)-approximation
algorithm for DALkS for any ε > 0.
Note that the above result is incomparable to the known SSEH-based inapproximability results [Ber13,
Man18], as the assumptions above are not known to follow from SSEH and vice versa.
While the above hardness provides evidence that there is unlikely to be an FPT approximation
scheme for the problem, the obvious remaining question is whether it is possible to (significantly)
beat the 2-approximation in FPT time. Although we do not know how to rule out such hardness
under the previous assumption that DkS is hard to approximate to within any constant factor, we
can show such a result under a stronger assumption which is stated formally below.
Definition 4 (PolyGapDkS(δ, t)) For any δ ∈(0, 1] and t ∈N, the PolyGapDkS(δ, t) problem
is the following: given a graph G and k ∈N, distinguish between
• (Completeness) there exists S ⊆VG of size k s.t. G[S] contains at least kt−δ copies of t-
cliques, and,
• (Soundness) den⩽k(G) < kδ.
Theorem 5 Suppose that, for any δ > 0 and any t ∈N, no polynomial time algorithm solves
PolyGapDkS(δ, t). Then, for any ε > 0, no polynomial time algorithm approximates DALkS to
within a factor of (2 −ε).

Moreover, if we assume further that, for any δ > 0, t ∈N and f : N →N, no f(k) · nO(1)

We remark that the completeness in PolyGapDkS(δ, t) is indeed stronger than just requiring that
G[S] is dense. In particular, there are dense graphs that contain few copies of t-clique; for example,
a complete bipartite graph does not contain t-clique for any t ≥3. Similarly, the soundness is also
stronger since it requires the density bound even for subgraphs of size less than k. Nevertheless,
it turns out that some hardness results in the literature do satisfy these stronger conditions. In
particular, Manurangsi et al. [MRS21] proved, under the Strongish Planted Clique Hypothesis8, a
o(k)-hardness for DkS that holds even with “perfect completeness” where there exists S ⊆VG such
that G[S] is a k-clique. This obviously implies that G[S] contains kt−o(1) copies of t-clique. Their
soundness also holds for all subgraphs of size at most k (rather than just equal to k). Moreover,
their hardness result applies even against FPT algorithms. Thus, Theorem 5 yields the following
corollary.
Corollary 6 Assuming Strongish Planted Clique Hypothesis, for any function f : N →N, there is
no f(k) · nO(1) time (2 −ε)-approximation algorithm for DALkS for any ε > 0.
Finally, we also adapt our reduction slightly to show that Densest At-Least-k-Subgraph pa-
rameterized by k is W[1]-hard to solve exactly.
Theorem 7 Densest At-Least-k-Subgraph is W[1]-hard when parameterized by k.
We remark here that Densest At-Least-k-Subgraph is in XP9. Note that it is not a priori clear
that this is the case; unlike in Densest k-Subgraph, the solution(s) to Densest At-Least-k-
Subgraph can have large size and hence we may need to enumerate all 2n subsets of vertices to
find the optimal solution. Fortunately, Saha et al. [SHK+10] showed that, given a subset S ⊆VG,
it is possible to efficiently find the densest subgraph containing S. As a result, we can simply
enumerate all subsets S’s of size k, use Saha et al.’s algorithm and output the densest subgraph
among these subgraphs; this yields an nk+O(1) time algorithm for DALkS.

A Strong Hardness for Densest k-Subhypergraph
As an intermediate problem, we will be reducing to a variant of the Densest k-Subhypergraph
(DkSH). The standard version of DkSH on t-uniform hypergraphs can be stated as follows: Given
a universe U and a collection S of t-element subsets of U, find T ⊆U of size k that entirely
contains as many subsets S ∈S as possible. The variant we work with – which is formalized below
– is a stronger version, where the completeness remains the same but, in the soundness, only a
few S ∈S even satisfy |S ∩T| > 1. (In contrast, the standard gap version would only consider
|S ∩T| = t.)
Definition 12 (t-uniform StrongGapDkSH(λ, γ)) For any λ, γ ⩾1 and t ∈N, the t-uniform
StrongGapDkSH(λ, γ) problem is: given a universe U, a collection S of t-element subsets of U
and k, ℓ∈N, distinguish between
• (Completeness) There exists T ⊆U of size k such that |{S ∈S | S ⊆T}| ⩾ℓ.
• (Soundness) For any T ⊆U of size at most γ · k, |{S ∈S | |S ∩T| > 1}| < ℓ/λ
It is simple to see that GapDkS(λ, γ) can be cast as 2-uniform StrongGapDkSH(λ, γ).
Lemma 13 For any λ, γ ⩾1, there exists an FPT polynomial time reduction from GapDkS(λ, γ)
to 2-uniform StrongGapDkSH(λ, γ).

completeness and soundness of the reduction are trivial from definitions.

Concluding Remarks
In this work, we provide a reduction from (variants of) DkS to DALkS, which establishes hard-
ness of approximation for the latter under several assumptions. An interesting open question is
whether we can strengthen our second reduction Theorem 5, which currently requires a rather
strong assumption that PolyGapDkS is hard. A concrete direction here is to attempt to relax
the assumption to, e.g., that GapDkS is hard.
