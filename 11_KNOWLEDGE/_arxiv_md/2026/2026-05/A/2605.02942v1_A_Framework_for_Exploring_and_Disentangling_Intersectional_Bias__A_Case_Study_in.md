A Framework for Exploring and Disentangling
Intersectional Bias: A Case Study in Fetal
Ultrasound
Aya Elgebaly1, Joris Fournel1, Benjamin Laine Jønch Jurgensen1, Kamil
Mikolaj1, Anders Christensen1, Martin Tolsgaard2, Claes Ladefoged1, and
Aasa Feragen1
1 Technical University of Denmark, Denmark
2 CAMES Rigshospitalet, Denmark
aafel@dtu.dk, afhar@dtu.dk
Abstract. Bias in medical AI is often framed as a problem of repre-
sentation. However, in image-based tasks such as fetal ultrasound, per-
formance disparities can arise even when representation is adequate, be-
cause predictive accuracy depends strongly on image quality. Image qual-
ity is shaped by acquisition conditions and operator expertise, as well as
patient-dependent factors such as maternal body mass index (BMI), all of
which may correlate with sensitive demographic features. Consequently,
observed disparities may reflect the combined influence of demographic,
clinical, and acquisition-related factors rather than data imbalance alone,
and may obscure underlying interaction or confounding effects. We pro-
pose a structured framework to explore and detect intersectional bias,
combining unsupervised slice discovery, systematic factor-wise analysis,
and targeted intersectional evaluation. In a case study of over 94,000
ultrasound images for fetal weight estimation, we analyze bias in a state-
of-the-art deep learning (DL) model and the clinical standard Hadlock,
a regression formula using biometric measurements. Pixel spacing (PS)
– a parameter considered suboptimal in current acquisition protocols –
emerged as a consistent driver of performance differences, with higher
PS associated with improvements of up to 24% in selected subgroups for
both models. Because PS is often adapted in cases of high BMI or low
gestational age (GA), this effect carries a substantial risk of confounding.
Our intersectional analysis revealed that part of the PS-associated signal
is explained by GA, while PS-related improvements persist across BMI
strata, highlighting the importance of acquisition-aware and interaction-
aware evaluation in medical AI fairness research.
Keywords: Medical AI fairness · Intersectional bias · Fetal Ultrasound
imaging · Fetal weight estimation · Image Acquisition · Pixel spacing

Fetal ultrasound provides a particularly illustrative case. Although it is in-

and workflow contribute to systematic variability in image quality [1,24]. In ad-

acquisition-related factors are demographically structured, performance dispar-
ities in fetal ultrasound may arise independently of subgroup representation,
and mitigation strategies based solely on dataset balancing may therefore be
insufficient.

attributes compound disparate impact [12,23,25], is particularly relevant when
apparent demographic disparities may mask other sources of model error, such
as shortcut learning from chest drains in chest X-ray images [9,18,19]. Beyond
demographics, clinical and acquisition-related factors can structure model per-
formance and interact with demographic attributes, yet remain comparatively
underexplored in fairness analyses. Recent work in cervical ultrasound has shown
that acquisition parameters such as pixel spacing can introduce bias in preterm
birth prediction [5]. To identify systematic errors without a priori knowledge
of underserved groups, slice discovery methods (SDM) automatically surface
underperforming subgroups within a dataset. Early slice discovery methods in-
clude [4,10], which leverage embeddings to identify coherent data slices. Recent
medical imaging studies have applied similar approaches to characterize latent
subgroups with distinct error profiles [19].
In this paper, we contribute a framework (illustrated in Fig. 1) for discov-
ering and disentangling intersectional performance disparities. The framework
integrates three components: (1) unsupervised slice discovery applied to model
embeddings to identify latent subgroups with distinct error profiles, enabling hy-
pothesis generation without predefined groups; (2) structured stratified perfor-
mance analysis to quantify variation across clinically and technically meaningful
factors; and (3) intersectional analysis to examine how clinical and acquisition-
related variables jointly influence model behavior.
We demonstrate this framework in a large-scale case study of fetal weight
estimation from ultrasound. Fetal weight estimation is traditionally performed
using biometric measurements combined with the Hadlock formula [7]. Recent
deep learning (DL) models have shown substantial improvements in accuracy
[14], but such gains do not guarantee equitable performance across subgroups.
We therefore evaluate both a DL model and the clinical standard Hadlock for-
mula within our bias-discovery framework.
Our analysis identifies pixel spacing (PS)—an acquisition parameter re-
flecting field-of-view—as a consistent driver of performance variation. Because
PS is correlated with maternal BMI and gestational age (GA) [8], both con-
founding and interaction effects are plausible. Through intersectional analysis
with BMI and GA, we show that PS-related improvements persist across BMI
strata but vary across GA groups, indicating an independent contribution of ac-
quisition resolution with gestational-age–dependent effects. Our case study thus
demonstrates that joint intersectional analysis improves our insight into the main
drivers of performance disparities and their confounders – and that fairness and
robustness can, in this case, be improved through protocol optimization, not
only algorithmic refinement.

Methods: Intersectional bias exploration and analysis
We describe below our framework for exploring and disentangling intersectional
bias through three steps: (1) Unsupervised slice discovery, (2) structured strat-

ified performance analysis, and (3) pairwise intersectional analysis. Across all
steps, we examined performance variation with respect to a comprehensive set
of factors, including clinical factors (gestational age (GA), parity, and concep-
tion type), acquisition factors (ultrasound device, PS per anatomical plane, and
averaged PS), and demographic factors (ethnicity, BMI, and maternal age). We
demonstrate our bias-detection framework in a state-of-the-art deep learning
(DL) model [14] for fetal weight estimation as well as the clinical standard Had-
lock formula [7], which takes biometric measurements from ultrasound as input.
Both methods are based on three standard biometric planes (head, abdomen, fe-
mur) and output estimated fetal weight at scan time. The reference fetal weight
at scan time was derived from birth weight using the Marsal growth curve [13].
2.1
Unsupervised Slice Discovery
Each test scan was represented by a 388-dimensional feature vector extracted
from the penultimate layer of the DL model using center-cropped grayscale in-
puts (224×224). Dimensionality was reduced to 128 using principal component
analysis (99% variance retained). Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs) were fitted
with k = 5–20 clusters. Model selection using the Bayesian Information Crite-
rion and the silhouette score identified k = 10. Hard assignments were obtained
via maximum posterior probability. Clusters were ranked by Mean Relative Er-
ror (MRE). For exploratory characterization, clinical factors, acquisition factors,
and demographic factors were summarized within clusters.
2.2
Structured Stratified Performance Analysis
We performed a stratified evaluation across predefined clinical, acquisition, and
demographic-related factors. At the full-dataset level, a global radar plot sum-
marized performance variability across all factors. For each factor, the MRE gap
between best- and worst-performing subgroups was computed for both mod-
els. Each factor was then analyzed independently. Continuous factors were par-
titioned using quantile-based binning; categorical factors were analyzed using
natural categories. Radar plots visualized within-factor subgroup performance.
Finally, stratified analyses were repeated within the best- and worst-performing
clusters identified by slice discovery to assess factor-specific variability within la-
tent slices. Differences between strata were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney
U test.
2.3
Intersectional Bias Analysis
Intersectional evaluation was conducted using two-dimensional subgroup parti-
tions constructed from quantile-based stratification of PS, GA, and BMI. These
factors were selected based on the unsupervised slice discovery results, as their
subgroup distributions differed most markedly between the best- and worst-
performing latent slices. Mean Relative Error (MRE) was computed within each
joint subgroup for both DL and Hadlock models under identical partitions.

Experimental Validation and Results
3.1
Data
Analysis was conducted on 94,161 third-trimester (GA > 28 weeks) ultrasound
images from 17 xxx hospitals (2008–2018). All analyses were performed on a
held-out test set that was not used during the dl model training.
3.2
Unsupervised Slice Discovery
Clustering identified 10 latent slices with MRE ranging from 6.25% to 7.57%,
representing a 21% relative variation in performance. Comparison of the best-
performing (Slice 5) and worst-performing (Slice 9) slices revealed systematic
differences in both acquisition and clinical characteristics (Fig. 2 left). Higher PS
and GA values were significantly enriched in the best-performing slice, whereas
lower PS and GA ranges were more prevalent in the worst-performing slice.
Intra-cluster stratified analysis further confirmed these trends; lower GA and PS
subgroups exhibited higher errors even when analyzed within individual slices
(Fig. 2 right).

3.3
Structured Stratified Analysis
Global stratified evaluation revealed significant performance variability across
clinical and acquisition factors (Fig. 3). While the DL model outperformed the
Hadlock formula (6.74% vs. 7.7% MRE), both were sensitive to specific data
characteristics. Primary Performance Drivers: Both pixel spacing (PS) and
gestational age (GA) exhibited consistent monotonic relationships with error
(p < 0.001). Lower PS (wider-field-of-view) and lower GA (earlier gestation)
were significantly associated with higher MRE across all fetal planes (head, ab-
domen, and femur)(Fig. 4). Slice-restricted radar analysis confirmed that these
patterns persisted even within the best- and worst-performing latent subgroups
(Fig. 2 right). Secondary Factor Variability: Maternal BMI showed increased
error in high-BMI cases, though the impact was less than PS or GA. While ap-
parent performance differences were observed across device types and Ethnicity
factors, the findings are constrained by small subgroup sizes; notably, the best
performance was not observed in the majority (device: V830, Ethnicity: Cau-
casian) groups.

Discussion
We have demonstrated how our 3-step analysis of intersectional bias helps un-
cover and confirm important drivers of bias and their relationships with potential
clinical confounders. First, unsupervised slice discovery revealed structured per-
formance heterogeneity beyond demographic imbalance, with up to 21% relative
error variation across latent slices. These disparities aligned with systematic
clinical and acquisition differences – we highlight GA and PS in particular.
Across global and slice-restricted analyses, PS emerged as a consistent driver
of performance differences, with higher PS associated with lower error for both
the DL model and Hadlock. Intersectional BMI–PS analysis showed that PS-
related improvements persist across high-, normal-, and low-BMI strata, arguing
against BMI as a confounder. In contrast, GA–PS analysis revealed that part of
the PS signal is explained by GA, with earlier gestational ages exhibiting higher

The DL model showed stronger sensitivity to PS than Hadlock, suggesting
that representation learning amplifies resolution-dependent effects, whereas the
biometric formula is less influenced by acquisition resolution. This difference
likely reflects that current acquisition protocols are historically optimized for
Hadlock-based workflows rather than for end-to-end deep learning models.
Overall, performance disparities in fetal ultrasound appear to be shaped more
strongly by interacting clinical and acquisition parameters than by standalone
demographic imbalance. Because PS is modifiable, protocol optimization may
complement algorithmic refinement; however, more extensive analyses are re-
quired to determine whether the apparent PS-related improvements in later
gestation reflect true effects or sampling variability. This retrospective obser-
vational study limits causal inference. Residual correlation between GA, BMI,
and acquisition parameters may remain, and subgroup imbalance was present in
some strata. External validation across institutions and protocols is required.
