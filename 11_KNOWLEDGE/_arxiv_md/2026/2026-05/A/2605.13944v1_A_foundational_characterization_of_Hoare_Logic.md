A foundational characterization of Hoare Logic
Daniel Leivant
Computer Science Department, Indiana University Bloomington, USA
Abstract
We show that a partial-correctness assertion about an iterative program is provable in Hoare Logic iff
it is provable in standard second-order logic with comprehension restricted to first-order predicates.
This equivalence was claimed twice in the past, both with faulty proofs, and seems to be the first
foundational characterization of Hoare’s Logic.

1
Introduction
Hoare-style formalisms are arguably the predominant paradigm of imperative program verifi-
cation. Their building blocks are partial-correctness assertions (pca’s) i.e. phrases φ[α] ψ
with α a program and φ, ψ first-order formulas (ff’s). This asserts that α ’s output satisfies
ψ provided its input satisfies φ. A pca is satisfied vacuously when the input yields no output,
so nothing is being said about program termination (“totality”), hence the qualifier “partial.”
Since the set of pca’s that are Hoare-provable is RE, whereas the set of pca’s valid in all
structures is not [5], Hoare Logic is not semantically complete, unlike first-order logic (FL).
Are then foundational properties that do characterize the provable pca’s?
A foundational characterization of Hoare Logic, proposed in [10], is to match Hoare Logic
with an innocuous extension FL+ of first-order logic, which admits relational quantification
but restricts the Comprehension Rule to first-order definable predicates. FL+ combines the
expressive power of relational quantification with the ontological and deductive clarity of
first-order logic. Unfortunately, the proof sketched in [10] relies on a faulty formal definition
of program semantics.
We show here that HL for iterative programs is indeed characterized by provability in
second-order logic with first-order comprehension. It is a proof-theoretic matching two forms
of provability, thereby solidifying a long-held tenet that Hoare Logic is syntactic sugar for
first-order reasoning about pca’s. The characterization holds also for the constructive (i.e.
intuitionistic) readings of the two formalisms.
2
Related works
Note from the outset that our characterization is unrelated to the extensive literature on
second-order methods in program verification. In fact, it is the proof-theoretic weakness of
FL+ that makes it relevant here.
An obvious question is whether Cook’s relative completeness [3] is related. Relative
Completeness epitomize the “one structure at a time” approach, and as such fails to
characterize HL: if π is any pca that is valid but unprovable in HL then extending HL
with π yields a proper extension of HL that is also sound and relatively complete. Such π
must exist since HL is RE while the set of valid pca’s is not.
Blass and Gurevich observed that expressiveness is guaranteed if existential fixpoints are
permissible in pre- and post-conditions [2]. This trivializes the expressiveness condition of
relative completeness at the cost of a second-order extension of the underlying language.

3.1
Basic conventions
We refer to the usual notion of a vocabulary, consisting of function- and relation-identifiers,
each assigned an arity ≥0. We posit a set V = {v1, v2 . . .} of (first-order) variables. The
Σ -terms are generated from variables and function identifiers as usual. Thus every nullary
function-identifier (a “constant”) is a term. The atomic-formulas are the formal equations
between terms, and the phrases Q(t1, . . . , tk) where Q is a k-ary relational identifier in
the vocabulary. The first-order formulas (ff’s) are generated from atomic-formulas using
connectives and quantifiers, as usual. If E is a term or a formula then η is adequate for E
if it is defined for all variables free in E. For such η we write ηE for the restriction of η to
variables free in E. For distinct variables x1 . . . xk and elements a1 . . . ak ∈|S| , we write
[⃗a/⃗x] for the valuation that maps xi to vi ∈|S| (i = 1 . . . k). η · η′ is the valuation that
overrides η by η′ : (η · η′)(x) = η′(x) if defined, and = η(x) if not. We also use brackets
for syntactic substitution: If E is a term or a formula with free variables x1 . . . xk then
E[t1, . . . , tk / x1, . . . , xk] is the result of simultaneously substituting in E the terms ti for
free occurrences of xi (i = 1 . . . k). When ⃗x is implicit we write E[t1, . . . , tk] .
A Σ-structure S consists of a set (S ’s universe, denoted |S| ); an interpretation of each
k -ary function-identifier f as a k-ary function fS : |S|k ⇀|S|; and an interpretation of
each k -ary relation-identifier Q as a k-ary relation QS ⊆|S|k. A valuation (in S) is a
function η : V ⇀|S|. We write ValS for the set of such valuations. An interpretation is a
pair (S, η) where η is a valuation into S. tη denotes the value of the Σ-term t under that
interpretation (we leave S implicit). S, η |= φ states that η is adequate for φ and φ is
true in (S, η) .

4.3
Henkin models
A naive definition of truth for second-order formulas is analogous to that for first-order
formulas. The truth of first-order formulas in a structure S is defined modulo valuations
η of free-variables as structure elements. Likewise, the truth of second-order formulas in
S is defined modulo a valuation as above, as well as a valuation H of relational-variables
as relations over S. A formula ∀2 Rk φ is then true in S modulo η, H iff φ is true in S
modulo η, H′ for every extension H′ of H that encompasses R.
A challenge appears as soon as we attempt to axiomatize second-order logic by a deductive
system L2. Of course, we’ll promise to only prove formulas that are valid for the “standard
interpretation” above. Since the set of valid second-order formulas is not definable in second-
order arithmetic, let alone RE, any such proof-system would only yield some, but not all,
valid second-order formulas. The semantics that L2 would capture must therefore allow
“structures” that falsify formulas that are true in the standard interpretation above, but not
provable in L2. Henkin’s insight [8] was to consider a non-standard notion of modeling, in
which relational variables range over a prescribed family of relations which is part of the
model. The coherence of this construction requires that those families of relations satisfy
certain closure conditions. The interested reader is advised to consult [8] for detail.

4.5
Proofs of FL+
The huge deductive power of the calculus above for second-order logic resides in the scope
of relational quantifiers. Recall that the definition of S, η, H |= ∀Rk φ requires S, η, H ·
[A/R] |= φ for all A ⊂|S|k. But the inference rules might remain correct even if A
ranges over a sub-collection C of P(|S|k), a possibility envisioned by Henkin [8]. The
Comprehension principle requires that C include all the relations definable by second-order
formulas, and the relational quantifiers in such definitions are themselves interpreted with
respect to C . In short, C must be closed under definitions by second-order formulas. This
circular situation may be unsettling, but it disappears once we weaken Comprehension to
relations that are first-order definable.
We thus focus on a variant FL+ of FL, in which all predicates in Comprehension are
defined by first-order formulas.
A sequential calculus for FL+ is thus simply the calculus for SOL with the stipulation
that the eigen-formula of the ∀2 Antecedent Rule be a first-order Σ-formula.
The main take-away here is that FL+ has no ontological or epistemic premises beyond the
admission of first-order definitions of relations. FL+ is analogous to the subsystem ACA0
of second-order Arithmetic [14], but it is not the same! FL+ is an uninterpreted logic, with
a generic notion of semantics, whereas ACA0 is a second-order theory for the particular
structure of natural numbers with a collection of basic operations. We allow free relational-
variables (i.e. parameters) in eigen-predicates, as does ACA0. This is innocuous for both FL+
and ACA0, but the theory ACA0 includes, in addition, a template of Induction, and letting
induction-formulas have free relational variables does make a difference. Evidently, we need
not worry about this here.

Characterization theorems
7.1
FL+ characterizes HL
▶Theorem 18. (Deductive characterization of HL) A pca π is provable in Hoare Logic iff
its explicit rendition ˆπ is provable in FL+.
The same equivalence holds for the constructive (i.e. intuitionistic) versions of HL and
FL+.
Proof. The first equivalence combines Theorems 9 and 10.
To see that the equivalence holds for the constructive versions of HL and FL+ observe
that the proofs of Theorems 9 and 10 remain correct once the Consequence Rule of HL is
stated for provability in intuitionistic first-order logic, and the rules of FL+ are restricted to
sequents with at most one formula in the succedent (i.e. the sequents’ right-hand side).
◀
The result for constructive logics illustrates one advantage of proof-theoretic methods over
model-theoretic methods, which are far more complex to adapt to constructive formalisms.
Theorem 18 identifies HL as capturing first-order reasoning about regular programs, since
FL+ does not go beyond an acceptance of first-order definitions, which already underlies
first-order logic itself.
Note that the essence of the theorem is not tied to the specific second-order definition
we adopt here for program semantics. That second-order definition is correct for standard
semantics no matter our focus on FL+.

7.3
Semantic completeness for Henkin’s models
Consider Henkin’s Σ-models for SOL as defined in [8], with simple adaptation to our context.
The basic idea is that relations over objects are treated as structure elements of separate
sorts, one for each arity, whereas the basic objects fall under their own sort. The sorts are
tied together by a binary relation ε that holds between k-ary tuples of “object”-elements
and elements that are “k -ary relations”. This is akin to treating second-order logic as a
multi-sorted first-order theory.
We obtain then from Theorem 18
▶Theorem 21. (Henkin-model characterization of HL) A pca π is provable in Hoare Logic
iff it is true in every Henkin model of FL+.
