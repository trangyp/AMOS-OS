An ℓ2 Obstruction for Elementary Embeddings of
Hyperbolic Groups
Connor MacMahon
Abstract
The first ℓ2 Betti number of a group is non-decreasing under various embeddings arising from
first order logic. Strict inequality is proved for elementary embeddings of non-abelian proper sub-
groups within torsion free hyperbolic groups using Perin’s classification of such inclusions. The
monotonicity is further demonstrated for existential embeddings of arbitrary finitely generated
groups.
1
Introduction
In groundbreaking work of Sela, it was demonstrated that each of the natural inclusions along the
tower:
F2 ,→F3 ,→F4 ,→. . .
consisting of free groups is elementary ([1]). This strongly resolved the Tarski problem, demonstrat-
ing that all free groups Fn for n ≥2 have the same first order theory. Since this breakthrough, the
structure of elementarily embedded subgroups of torsion free hyperbolic groups has been completely
elucidated [1] in terms of extended towers. The purpose of this note is to use this characterization
to prove that ℓ2 Betti numbers may arise as an obstruction to elementary embeddings.
First defined in the seminal work of Atiyah [2] in the context of index Theorems, the first ℓ2 Betti
number of a countable discrete group has become an invariant of great interest at the intersection
of algebra, geometry, and functional analysis (see [3] and [4]). In view of the myriad interpretations
that the first ℓ2 Betti number β(2)
1 (G) of a group G has, it is desirable to investigate how β(2)
1 (G) is
related to the first order theory of G.
The main result is as follows.

subgroup which is elementarily embedded in G. Then β(2)

Logic Preliminaries
Here, the necessary first order logic is introduced following [13]. Within the language of groups, the
terms are words in the variables denoting group elements, their inverses, and the identity element.
An atomic formula consists of two terms separated by "=".
To describe first order formulae, one combines atomic formulae, logical connectors (∨, ∧, ¬),
and quantifiers (∀, ∃). Where no free variables are present (i.e. each variable is quantified over),
a formula is referred to as a sentence. For example, the following is a sentence in the first order
language of groups:
∀g1∃g2(g1g2g−1
1 g−1
2 = 1) ∧¬(g2 = 1).
The elementary theory of a group G is the collection of first order sentences which hold true in G.
An embedding H ,→G of groups is called elementary if each first order sentence ϕ in the language
of groups with constants in H satisfies G |= ϕ if and only if H |= ϕ [1].
Observe that if H is elementarily embedded within G, then H and G have the same elementary
theory. Indeed, a first order sentence ϕ in the language of groups is also a first order sentence in
the language of groups with constants in H. If G and H have the same elementary theory, then they
are called elementarily equivalent. More precisely, G and H are elementarily equivalent provided
that for each first order sentence ϕ in the language of groups, G |= ϕ if and only if H |= ϕ.
It should be noted that H being elementarily embedded within G is strictly stronger than H being
elementarily equivalent to G (for instance, 2Z ⊂Z is not an elementary embedding [1] 3.1.1).
Below, it will be desirable to also consider existential embeddings. An existential sentence is a
first order sentence ϕ in the language of groups which takes the following form:
∃x1∃x2 . . . ∃xnψ(x1, . . . , xn),
where ψ is a quantifier free formula. The existential theory of a group G consists of the collection of
existential sentences which hold true in G. As with elementary embeddings, an embedding H ,→G
of groups is called existential provided each existential sentence ϕ in the language of groups with
constants in H satisfies G |= ϕ if and only if H |= ϕ.

The Result
Before beginning, it is convenient to recall that in the present context, a graph of groups consists of
a nonempty connected graph Γ, groups Gv corresponding to each vertex in Γ, and groups Ge corre-
sponding to each edge of Γ. In general, self loops at vertices are allowed, though they will not arise
in the construction below. Moreover, for each edge group Ge, the graph of groups carries the addi-
tional data of two injective homomorphisms from Ge to each of the vertex groups Gv corresponding
to vertices v incident to the edge e.

To build the fundamental group π1(Γ) of the graph of groups Γ, fix first a maximal tree T in Γ.
Enumerate the edges in T by {e1, e2, . . . , eq} such that each subgraph of T on the edges e1, e2, . . . , ei
for i < q forms a tree. Begin with a vertex v incident to e1. If v′ is the other vertex incident to e1,
form the amalgamated free product Gv ∗Ge1 Gv′ using the data of the injective edge homomorphisms
Ge1 →Gv and Ge1 →Gv′. In this way, inductively form amalgamated free products over all the edges
e1, e2, . . . , eq in the maximal tree. Call the resulting group G0.
Contracting Γ along T, one is left with a graph of groups with one vertex v0 with vertex group
G0. The remaining edges (those not in T) now form loops connecting v0 to itself. Inductively perform
HNN extensions corresponding to each of these loops, once again using the data of the injective edge
homomorphisms. The resulting group G = π1(Γ) is the fundamental group of the graph Γ of groups.
For more details, including a presentation of the graph of groups and its independence from the
choice of maximal tree, see Serre ([14]).
Now, a definition which arises in Perin’s characterization of elementarily embedded subgroups
within torsion free hyperbolic groups.
Definition ([1]): Fix a group G. A non-exceptional centered splitting of G is a graph of groups
decomposition G = π1(Γ) such that the vertices of Γ are v, v1, . . . , vr with r ≥1, where v carries the
fundamental group π1(Σ) of a compact surface Σ and each edge joins v to some vi.
The edge groups are isomorphic to Z, and they correspond to the maximal boundary subgroups
of π1(Σ). The boundary components of Σ are in bijective correspondence with edges incident to v.
The vertices vi are the bottom vertices, and one denotes the groups they carry by Bi. The vertex v is
called the central vertex. The abstract free product B1 ∗B2 ∗· · · ∗Br is referred to as the base of the
splitting. Additionally, χ(Σ) < 0 and Σ is not among the following list of exceptional surfaces:
• S0,3 the thrice-punctured sphere,
• N1,2 the twice punctured projective plane,
• N2,1 the once punctured Klein bottle,
• and N3 the closed non-orientable surface of genus 3.
The closed non-orientable surface of genus 3 is already disallowed by the fact that r ≥1. However,
it is still included in the list of exceptional surfaces in order to stay true to Perin’s definition.

Discussion and Outlook
First, some discussion of Theorem 1. Consider its contrapositive. This states that if H is a non-
abelian proper subgroup of a torsion free hyperbolic group G, and β(2)
1 (H) + 1
2 > β(2)
1 (G), then H is not
elementarily embedded within G.
More explicitly, there exists a first order sentence ϕ with constants in H such that ϕ holds true
in G but not in H (if instead there were a sentence which held true in H but not in G, its negation
would do). In the special case in which β(2)
1 (G) = 0, one obtains the following Corollary.
Corollary 1: Suppose G is a torsion free hyperbolic group with β(2)
1 (G) = 0. Then G admits no proper
elementarily embedded subgroup H which is non-abelian.
Some torsion free hyperbolic groups G with β(2)
1 (G) = 0 are those with property (T) [12] and the
fundamental groups of closed, hyperbolic 3-manifolds ([17]). The first ℓ2 Betti number of G also
vanishes if G has a finitely generated infinite normal subgroup of infinite index [9].
Furthermore, examining the proof of Theorem 1, note that each step in the extended tower of
Perin adds at least 1/2 to the first ℓ2 Betti number. A straightforward induction yields the following
Corollary.

is elementarily embedded within G. Then G is an extended tower over H of length at most
