Abstract
We consider the Linnik–Goldbach problem of writing all large even integers as the sum
of two primes and a fixed number of powers of 2. We show that, under the generalised
Riemann hypothesis, one can use 6 powers of two. In addition, we update the best
known bounds on Romanov’s constant, showing unconditionally that more than 25%
of odd numbers can be written as the sum of a prime and a power of 2.

Introduction
We refine the numerical bounds on two well-studied problems in number theory — the
Goldbach–Linnik problem and Romanov’s problem. The main source of improvement arises
by incorporating new bounds on Goldbach representations due to Lichtman [17]. However,
there are other minor optimisations we employ.
We remark that p, possibly with a subscript (e.g. p1, p2, p3), is assumed to be prime
throughout. Moreover, φ(·) denotes the Euler totient function, τ(·) denotes the divisor
function, and (a, b) stands for gcd(a, b).

1.1
The Goldbach–Linnik problem
Our first result is related to Linnik’s approximation to Goldbach’s conjecture. This
problem asks for the smallest value of K ≥0 such that every large even integer can be
expressed as the sum of two primes and K powers of 2:
n = p1 + p2 + 2ν1 + · · · + 2νK.
(1)
Linnik [19] was the first to show that K exists; the first values of K were very large (see e.g.
[20]). However, after a series of improvements, Heath-Brown and Schlage-Puchta [14] were
able to obtain K = 7 assuming the generalised Riemann hypothesis (GRH). Unconditionally,
they were able to use some zero-density results by Heath-Brown [12] to show that K = 13
is admissible. Pintz and Ruzsa [27, 28] independently obtained the same result under GRH
and K = 8 unconditionally. We show it is now possible to obtain K = 6 under GRH.
Theorem 1. Assuming GRH, every sufficiently large even integer can be expressed as the
sum of two primes and K = 6 powers of 2.
Later in Table 1, we display the parameters required to achieve further improvements,
both conditionally and unconditionally. For example, we see that K = 4 is possible upon
assuming the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture (see Conjecture 1 and Theorem 4). Moreover,
we fall just short of obtaining K = 7 unconditionally with our setup.

Statement of sieve upper bound results
In this section, we state a series of upper bound results, which are variations on
Proposition 1. Here, it is useful for the reader to have a basic understanding of sieve-theoretic
arguments, which are detailed in texts including [9, 8, 7].
For the Linnik–Goldbach and Romanov problems, we require upper bounds for
R(N, h, k, ℓ) := #{p1, p2 ≤N : p2 ≡k (mod l), p1 −p2 = h},
(9)
where N is even, h < N, ℓ≥1 is fixed, and k is coprime to ℓ. Of particular importance is
the case ℓ= 1, in which we write
R(N, h) := R(N, h, 1, 1) = #{p1, p2 ≤N : p1 −p2 = h}.
In the following subsections, we first outline the standard argument to obtain bounds for

we discuss how one can slightly modify Lichtman’s proof as to achieve an analogous bound
for R(N, h, k, ℓ). Here, we emphasise that the modifications to Lichtman’s argument are
minor and routine. In particular we have opted to reduce the length of this paper by only
mentioning the modifications required, rather than producing a detailed proof of each result.
