Before we explain the strategy involved in proving Theorem 1.2, we briefly recall prior
works and relevant literature in the study of random lemniscates and related areas.
Random polynomials with i.i.d. roots were first considered by Pemantle and Rivin [38]
to study a pairing phenomena between roots and critical points. This phenomenon asserts
that for such polynomials, each root has a nearby critical point. Hence if we have an un-
derstanding of the typical distance between a root and its nearest critical point, one can
in principle use Taylor’s theorem to estimate the corresponding critical value. In this way,
Lemma 2.1 below acts as a bridge connecting the pairing phenomenon to the study of lem-
niscate components. Pemantle and Rivin [38] conjectured (and proved it for some special
cases) that if roots are sampled as i.i.d. random variables, then the empirical distribution of
the critical points of such polynomials converges weakly to the distribution of an individ-
ual zero. This was later proved by Kabluchko in full generality [19]. A more quantitative
version of this pairing phenomena was proven in [20]: the distance between a critical point
and the root nearest to it is of order O(n−α) for 1/2 < α < 1 while the typical spacing be-
tween i.i.d. roots is bigger at O(n−1
2 ). Subsequent works established related results for the

The number of connected components of random rational lemniscates for rational functions
p/q, where p and q are sampled independently from the complex Kostlan ensemble was
studied by Lerario and Lundberg [26]. They showed that the expected number of connected
components grows linearly with the degree n. Later, Kabluchko and Wigman [21] obtained
the exact asymptotics for nodal components in terms of the Nazarov–Sodin constant cNS
[34]. For a sequence of Kac polynomials pn (with i.i.d complex Gaussian coefficients), it was
shown by Lundberg and Ramachandran in [28] that n−1E(Cpn) →1. Hence, in this model,
the average number of components is asymptotically the maximal it can be. Another related
work is [13] where the first two named authors (S.G and K.R) used probabilistic methods
to answer some (deterministic) questions of Erd¨os on the maximal number of components
when the zeros of the polynomial are constrained to lie on a given compact set. The study
of certain other geometrical aspects of random polynomial lemniscates was investigated in
[25]. We now move on to give a rough sketch of the proof of Theorem 1.2.
1.1. Sketch of the proof of Theorem 1.2 and organization of the paper. It is a well known
fact that the number of connected components of Λn is one more than the number of critical
points of Pn which lie outside Λn, see Lemma 2.1. By the Gauss-Lucas theorem, all the
critical points of Pn lie in D. Hence, we have to count the number of critical points in
D \ Λn: denote this number as Ln. We assert that E[Ln] ≈nE[Area(D \ Λn)]. A heuristic
argument to support this claim can be given as follows. By the pairing phenomena alluded to
before, we can treat a critical point of Pn as uniformly distributed on D. Hence, pretending
as if critical points and Λn are independent of each other, this suggests that the probability
that a critical point lies in D \ Λn is roughly 1
π Area(D \ Λn). Since there are n −1 critical
points, this suggests that E[Ln] has the same asymptotic behaviour as n × E[Area(D \ Λn)].
Taking this assertion granted for now, we next claim that E[Area(D \ Λn)] ∼C/√n, which
in turn implies Theorem 1.2. We verify this claim in two steps. Firstly, we obtain an a
priori inradius bound of Λn stating that Λn contains a large ball with high probability,
see Figure 1 and Proposition 3.1. This a priori estimate gives a O(
p
log n/n) bound on
E[Area(D\Λn)]. Secondly, to get rid of the √log n factor and enhance this to the asymptotic
E[Area(D\Λn)] ∼C/√n, we write E[Area(D\Λn)] in the form of an integral representation
and invoke the Edgeworth expansion theorem (c.f. Theorem 2.2 below).
The main difficulty that arises is to rigorously prove that E[Ln] ≈nE[Area(D\Λn). We were
unable to proceed along the lines of the heuristic stated above. Instead we use a Kac-Rice
method to obtain an explicit formula for E[Ln], see Proposition 6.1 and Proposition 6.3. This
yields E[Ln] = nMn + Rn, where the term Mn is approximately equal to E[Area(D \ Λn)],
and the remainder term Rn is negligible, see Section 6.3 for the details. The analysis of Rn
is quite delicate. Our key input, Proposition 5.1, combines a large deviation estimate in the
heavy tail regime and an anticoncentration/small ball inequality to handle Rn. This is done
in Section 5 and Section 9.
