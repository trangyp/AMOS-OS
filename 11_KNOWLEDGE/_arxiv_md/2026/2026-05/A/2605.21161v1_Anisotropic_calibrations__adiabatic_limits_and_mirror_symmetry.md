ANISOTROPIC CALIBRATIONS, ADIABATIC LIMITS
AND MIRROR SYMMETRY
KOTARO KAWAI AND TOMMASO PACINI
Abstract. Let (M, g) be a Riemannian manifold.
Choose a pair (α, H) where α is a
calibration and H is a calibrated distribution. Using this data we define a 1-parameter
family of forms αε and study its adiabatic limit as ε →0. We show that (i) the limit is a
calibration in a generalized sense, (ii) under the usual closedness assumptions, the adiabatic
calibrated submanifolds are anisotropic minimal in the classical sense defined in the calculus
of variations/PDE theory.
We apply this construction to G2-manifolds. In this case the adiabatic calibrated condi-
tion is equivalent to a Fueter-type equation. We provide explicit examples and prove local
analytic existence theorems for the adiabatic calibrated submanifolds. Applying mirror sym-
metry as described by the real Fourier–Mukai transform, the general picture is as follows:
adiabatic limits correspond to large radius limits, α-calibrated (associative) submanifolds
correspond to deformed Donaldson–Thomas connections, adiabatic calibrated submanifolds
correspond to G2-instantons.

1. Introduction
One of the main intuitions that string theory has contributed to geometry is the concept of
mirror symmetry. According to [34], [11], the fundamental structures underlying this theory
are (i) manifolds with special holonomy, (ii) calibrated fibrations.
Generally speaking, the motivation behind this paper is to take a step back with respect
to both structures, developing a geometry based on (i’) Riemannian manifolds with a cali-
bration, (ii’) calibrated distributions. A number of features come to light, which previously
seemed to have been either unnoticed or studied only in very specific settings.
The paper is split into two parts: Sections 2-6 cover the general theory, Sections 7-10
concern its application to a specific class of manifolds related to special holonomy: G2-
manifolds.
Summary of Part 1. It may be useful to briefly review two keywords.
Adiabatic limit. In differential geometry the term “adiabatic limit” goes back at least to
the 1980s, in work by Witten, Bismut-Cheeger, Bismut-Freed, Mazzeo-Melrose et al. The
underlying idea is to study objects depending on a parameter ε which, in different directions,
scale at different speeds. This construction is generally implemented in the context of fiber
bundles M →B, with the idea of distinguishing horizontal and vertical directions and,
intuitively, of collapsing the vertical fibers. In certain situations the “formal limit”, defined
by letting ε →0, is a new, interesting, object in its own right, defined on the base space B.
Calibration. Let (M, g) be a Riemannian manifold. Assume we want to prove that a given
submanifold minimizes the volume functional. A classical technique is to look for an ap-
propriate “calibration”, i.e. a differential form α satisfying certain conditions. The role of
α is to select, within the Grassmannian of planes, a certain subset of “calibrated” planes: if
the tangent spaces of the given submanifold are calibrated, the properties of α will imply
that the submanifold is a minimizer. Calibrated geometry, as theorized in [12], concerns the
reverse problem: given a calibration, find its calibrated submanifolds. Any such submanifold
is minimizing, thus “minimal”, i.e. it is a critical point of the volume functional.
The starting point for our paper is the following, very simple, idea.
Let us choose a
Riemannian manifold (M, g) and (i) a calibration α, (ii) a distribution H whose planes are
calibrated. Let V denote the orthogonal distribution. We may then apply the construction
of adiabatic limits to the object α: inserting the parameter will scale these two directions dif-
ferently, producing a form αε. We shall study its geometry for ε →0. As usual in calibrated
geometry, the main geometric objects of interest to us are the immersed submanifolds.
Let us introduce another keyword.

Anisotropic minimal submanifold. The standard volume functional can be generalized via a
choice of weighting function that assigns different weights to different directions in M. In this
(also very classical) context, critical points are known as “anisotropic minimal” submanifolds.
We can now state the main goals of Part 1.
(i) Introduce the notion of anisotropic calibrations, parallel to the classical notion but
whose corresponding submanifolds minimize a generalized volume functional, thus are anisotropic
minimal (rather than minimal). This is discussed in Section 2. See, in particular, Proposition
2.8 and Remark 2.9.
(ii) Show that, in the very general setting (M, g, α, H) discussed above, the adiabatic
limit of the forms αε generated by α is an anisotropic calibration on M. This is contained
in Section 5. See, in particular, Theorem 5.2.
(iii) Study the geometric features of the adiabatic calibrated submanifolds, both from the
variational and the mirror-symmetric viewpoint. See, in particular, Corollary 5.3, Corollary
5.7 and Section 6.
If M has the structure of a fibration M →B, it is very natural to let V be the (integrable)
distribution defined by the fibres and H be the orthogonal distribution. It is also natural
to concentrate on the special class of immersions defined as sections B →M. In general,
however, neither of our distributions will be integrable. This implies that there does not
exist a “base manifold” B defined by projection along fibers.
The absence of B has two consequences: (i) Contrary to other studies of adiabatic limits,
all limiting objects must be defined within M itself. (ii) There is no notion of sections. We
will replace sections with a notion of “projectable” immersions Σ →M, defined with respect
to the distribution H and using the abstract manifold Σ to fix the topological type of the
submanifolds. The intermediate sections 3, 4 discuss distributions, projectable immersions
and the generalized volume functionals of interest in our paper.
Summary of Part 2. Here, we apply the general theory of Part 1 to the class of “G2-
manifolds”. These manifolds are automatically endowed with a metric and a distinguished
calibration, usually denoted φ. They are of special interest within the theory of special
holonomy. We now need one more keyword.
Fueter equation. Recall the Cauchy-Riemann equation fx +ify = 0 for functions f : C →C.
Strictly speaking, the Fueter equation fx + ify + jfz + kfw = 0 for functions f : H →H is
its quaternionic analogue. It can be generalized in many ways, e.g. [14], [35], [37].

Additional comments. The above summary of our results glosses over an important is-
sue: whether the initial calibration α is a closed form, and whether this holds also for its
adiabatic limit. Interest in the G2 class of examples is in part based on the fact that (i)
the condition dφ = 0 is geometrically extremely natural, (ii) this condition also allows us to
bypass the analogous condition on its adiabatic limit, still obtaining similar results: compare
the assumptions in Corollaries 8.3, 8.4.
Comparisons with the literature. We are not aware of results in the literature similar
to Part 1. Regarding Part 2, the idea of studying adiabatic limits in the context of manifolds
with special holonomy is certainly not new: in particular, there is some overlap with work
by Donaldson [9] and by Li [25] (see also [15], [37]). Their studies assume, however, the
existence of a very strong fibration structure: M →B is a Riemannian submersion, and the
fibers are K3 surfaces. The following remarks might provide some guidance in comparing
our results with these.
1. One advantage of our more general framework is that, by simplifying the background
geometry, it sheds light on some very general properties of the adiabatic process that, up

to now, seem to have escaped attention: in particular, the fact that adiabatic calibrated
submanifolds are anisotropic minimal.
2. [25] uses a different concept of mirror symmetry, based on Mukai K3 duality. This
sometimes leads to results different from ours.
3. Our generalized volume functionals, applied to an immersion Σ →M, require a metric
on the image submanifolds. We obtain this from the metric on M using the projectability
assumption, rather than via the usual restriction process. On the other hand, in the special
case where M →B is a Riemannian submersion, Σ = B is endowed with a canonical metric.
If we restrict our attention to immersions determined by sections B →M, as in [25], this
metric coincides with the pull-back of our metric. It follows that our results in Section 8
include and extend those of [25], Section 2.3.
4. Sections have a very special feature: they cannot be reparametrized. In the more
general setting of manifolds with a distribution and immersions Σ →M, our results would
not be possible if we fixed a metric a priori on Σ because the functional would then depend
on the specific parametrization, leading to a theory similar to that of the energy functional
and harmonic maps. The volume functional, calibrated geometry and all functionals in our
paper are instead parametrization-independent. We will return to this point in Section 4.1.
5. Since our general setting does not include a base manifold B, the adiabatic construction
takes on a different flavour compared, for example, to [9]. This is true in at least two ways.
(i) Since both the initial and the limiting objects belong to the same ambient M, the
limits can be defined in the usual, topological, sense: there is no need to invoke any idea of
“formal limit”. As often in the literature, however, it remains true that we make no claims
concerning how solutions of an original equation may persist and converge to solutions of
the limiting equation, as ε →0.
(ii) We will find ourselves applying the adiabatic construction to several objects (tensors or
equations) simultaneously; the main role of this construction is then to highlight which parts
of each object scale at the same speed, suggesting relationships between them. Corollary
5.6, showing how α2 is related to χ1, is a concrete instance of this.
Outlook. The first notion discussed in this paper is that of anisotropic calibrations. As
explained above, we have chosen to focus on its relationship to the theory of pairs (α, H)
but its scope is wider: Sections 2.1, 2.2 provide examples of how it relates to other research
in the literature. It is natural to expect that it has a useful role to play within the theory of
anisotropic minimal submanifolds.
The class of G2-manifolds is just one in Berger’s list of manifolds with special holonomy
[16, Section 3]. In work in progress, the authors have applied the general theory of Part
1 to several other classes in this list. In each case, the corresponding adiabatic calibrated
submanifolds have properties similar to those seen in Section 8, confirming that the general

2. Anisotropic calibrations
Let M be a smooth manifold. Recall that a Grassmannian geometry on M is de-
termined by a subset G of the oriented Grassmannian of k-planes G(k, M). Let Σ be an
abstract oriented k-manifold. We say that an immersion ι : Σ →M is a G-submanifold if,
using the orientation on Σ and for each σ ∈Σ, ι∗(TσΣ) ∈G.
An important source of Grassmannian geometries arises as follows. Let (M, g) be a Rie-
mannian manifold. Given any π ∈G(k, M), let volπ denote the volume form on π induced
by the orientation and by g. We will say that a k-form α on M is a calibration if it has
the following properties: (i) for each π ∈G(k, M), α|π ≤volπ, (ii) dα = 0.
Condition (i) means the following: if we write α|π = λvolπ, then λ ≤1. This can be
equivalently written as follows: for each π ∈G(k, M) and each positive orthonormal k-frame
e1, . . . , ek of π, α(e1, . . . , ek) ≤1.
Remark 2.1. Given any π ∈G(k, M), let −π denote the same k-plane endowed with the
opposite orientation. Since condition (i) must apply also to −π, it can be rephrased as follows:
for each non-oriented k-plane π and orthonormal k-frame e1, . . . , ek of π, α(e1, . . . , ek) ≤1.
Changing the sign of one of the vectors leads to |α(e1, . . . , ek)| ≤1.
This formulation downplays the role of orientations, emphasizing that α is a calibration if
and only if −α is a calibration.
We obtain a Grassmannian geometry by setting
G(α) := {π ∈G(k, M) : α|π = volπ}.
The elements of G(α) are known as calibrated planes. The corresponding G(α)-submanifolds
are known as calibrated submanifolds. In this case ι∗α coincides with the volume form
induced by the given orientation on Σ and by the metric ι∗g.

Remark 2.3. As already mentioned, α is a calibration if and only if −α is a calibration.

We will say that a s-dimensional submanifold Σ ⊆M is integral if the forms in I vanish
after restriction to Σ.
Notice that W is integral with respect to I if and only if it is integral with respect to the
ideal generated by I.
On the other hand, a submanifold Σ is integral with respect to I if and only if it is integral
with respect to the ideal I generated by the forms in I and by their differentials. Such I is
then a differential ideal, in the sense that it is closed with respect to the exterior derivative
d.
We will usually be concerned with the case where I is a finite set of differential forms of
the same degree k. In this case:
1. If s < k, any W, Σ are automatically integral. The integrality condition is thus relevant
only when s ≥k.
2. I is generated by the k-forms in I and by the (k+1)-forms defined by their differentials.
It is thus a homogeneous ideal, in the sense that I = ⊕Ij, where Ij denotes the subspace
of forms in I of degree j. One can check that W, Σ are integral with respect to I if and
only if they are integral with respect to Is.
The pair (M, I), where I is a homogeneous differential ideal, is known as an exterior
differential system (EDS). Cartan-Kähler theory studies such systems in the analytic
category. It requires the following notions.
Definition 10.2. Let I be a homogeneous differential ideal.
Let W ⊆TxM be an integral s-plane with respect to I. Choose a basis {v1, . . . , vs}. Its
polar space is the subspace
E(W) = {X ∈TxM : ω(v1, . . . , vs, X) = 0, ∀ω ∈I}
= {X ∈TxM : ω(v1, . . . , vs, X) = 0, ∀ω ∈Is+1}.
We say that W is regular if the dimension of E(W) is locally constant, with respect to small
integral perturbations of W and of x.

By definition, W ⊆E(W). The quotient contains the possible directions in which W can
be extended so as to remain integral. Roughly speaking, regularity can be rephrased by
saying: the dimension of E(W)/W is locally constant, i.e. nearby points/subspaces have the
same number of integral extensions.
Definition 10.3. Let I be a homogeneous differential ideal.
Let W ⊆TxM be an integral s-plane with respect to I. We say that W contains a regular
flag if there exists a flag
W0 = {0} ⊆W1 ⊆· · · ⊆Ws−1 ⊆W,
where Wj has dimension j and each W0, . . . , Ws−1 is regular.
Notice that W itself is not required to be regular. The notion of regular flag will be used
in the proof of Theorem 10.5.
Remark 10.4. Notice the difference between Definition 10.1, which refers to an arbitrary set
of forms I, and Definitions 10.2, 10.3, which are formulated for a homogeneous differential
ideal I. When I consists of k-forms and s = k, the notion of an integral s-plane depends
only on the k-forms in I (since (k + 1)-forms vanish automatically on s-planes). However,
the polar space E(W) (hence regularity and the regular flag condition) is defined using the
(s + 1)-forms in I, and these include the forms coming from dI. It follows that regularity
must be checked with respect to I rather than the generating set I alone.
One version of the Cartan-Kähler theorem is as follows.
Theorem 10.5 (Cartan-Kähler, I). Let M be an analytic manifold and let I be an analytic
EDS. Choose an integral s-plane W ⊆TxM. If W contains a regular flag, then there exists
a local analytic s-dimensional integral submanifold whose tangent plane at x is W.
The proof is inductive. Starting from x (whose tangent space is W0), it iteratively builds
integral (j + 1)-dimensional extensions of j-dimensional integral submanifolds, with tangent
space Wj+1 at x. Each new extension requires the regularity of the given Wj.
This statement is very simple, but it gives no indication of the number of possible integral
submanifolds.
Theorem 10.6 (Cartan-Kähler, II). Let M be an analytic manifold and let I be an analytic
EDS. Choose an integral (s−1)-plane W ⊆TxM. Assume (i) W is regular, (ii) W contains a
regular flag, (iii) dim(E(W)) = s. Then any (s−1)-dimensional analytic integral submanifold
whose tangent space at x is W admits a unique analytic s-dimensional integral extension
whose tangent space at x is E(W).

10.2. Existence of associative submanifolds. The facts in this subsection are well-
known, going back to [12]. We include them here for completeness.
Let M be an analytic manifold and let φ be an analytic G2-structure. Locally χ can be
viewed, via its components, as a collection of 7 3-forms. Let I be the set of these 3-forms
and let I denote the corresponding analytic EDS. By definition a 3-plane W is associative
if and only if it is integral with respect to I, but any 4-form vanishes automatically on W.
It follows that W is associative if and only if it is integral with respect to I. We can thus
study the local existence of associative submanifolds in M via the Cartan-Kähler theorems,
applied to the EDS I.
Let us check the regularity assumptions:
1. Since I is generated by forms of degree 3 and 4, any subspace W of dimension s = 0, 1
is automatically integral with respect to I. Its polar space has dimension 7: this is constant
with respect to perturbations.
2. For the same reason, any subspace W of dimension s = 2 is automatically integral with
respect to I. Applying the cross product shows that W is contained in a unique associative
3-plane, so its polar space has dimension 3: this is constant with respect to perturbations.
This shows that, for s = 0, 1, 2, any s-plane is regular. Theorems 10.5, 10.6 thus lead to
the following existence results.
Corollary 10.8. Assume M and the G2-structure φ are analytic.
Choose x ∈M, an
associative 3-plane W at x and any flag {Wi} in W. Then
(1) There exists an analytic associative submanifold tangent to W at x.
(2) Any analytic 2-dimensional submanifold tangent to W2 admits a unique analytic as-
sociative extension tangent to W at x.
The initial 2-dimensional submanifolds can be written as follows. Choose local coordinates
(x1, . . . , x7) on M such that x ∼= 0 and W2 is generated by ∂x1, ∂x2. The initial submanifold
can then be locally projected onto the 2-plane generated by ∂x1, ∂x2, so it can be written as
the graph of some analytic function f(x1, x2) with 5 components such that f(0, 0) = 0 and
∇f(0, 0) = 0. Any such submanifold admits a unique extension to an associative submanifold
tangent to W at x. Since there are infinitely many such functions, there are infinitely many
associative submanifolds tangent to W.
