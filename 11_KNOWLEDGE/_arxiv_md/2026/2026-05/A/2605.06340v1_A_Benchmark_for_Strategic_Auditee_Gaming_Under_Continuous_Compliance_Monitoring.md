Abstract
Continuous post-deployment compliance audits, mandated by emerging regulations
such as the EU AI Act and Digital Services Act, create a class of strategic gaming
distinct from the one-shot input/output gaming studied in prior work. Regulated
systems can delay outcome reporting, drift their reports within plausible noise
envelopes, exploit longitudinal sample attrition, and cherry-pick among ambigu-
ous metric definitions.
We formalize continuous auditing as a 𝑇-round Stackel-
berg game between an auditor that commits to a temporal policy and an adap-
tive auditee, and identify a structural feature of any noise-aware static-auditor
design: a cover regime in which coverage gaps and granularity gaps cannot be
closed simultaneously. We make this formal as Observation 1 and show that two
minimal extension policies, each derived from the observation, close the regime
along orthogonal axes: a sample-size-aware static rule (Periodic-with-floor) closes
the granularity-failure case, while a history-conditioned suspicion-escalation policy
closes the coverage-failure case for the naive Drift strategy — and neither closes
both, exactly as the observation predicts; an audit-aware OffAuditDrift strategy
that exploits Stackelberg commitment defeats both. To support empirical study
we contribute a non-additive harm decomposition (welfare loss 𝑊, coverage loss 𝐶)
that exposes how attrition shifts harm from the regulator-accountable surface to
a regulator-invisible one; an initial library of five auditee strategies (Delay, Drift,
Cherry-pick, Attrition, OffAuditDrift) and five auditor policies, calibrated to sum-
mary statistics from published audits of the DSA Transparency Database; and a
reproducible simulator with a small, extensible Python interface. The artifact is
pip-installable, runs the full headline grid in under a minute on a laptop, and is
designed for community-contributed strategies and policies.

Introduction
The European Union’s AI Act mandates post-market monitoring for high-risk AI systems
(Article 72): providers must continuously document and analyze their systems’ real-world
behavior throughout the system’s lifetime, not merely at the point of release. The Digital
Services Act (DSA) imposes related governance obligations on large online platforms
(VLOPs/VLOSEs), including transparency reports on content moderation (Article 15),
statements of reasons for moderation actions (Article 24), six-month publication cycles
for VLOPs (Article 42), systemic-risk assessment (Article 34) and mitigation (Article 35),
and annual independent audits (Article 37). These regimes share a common structural
feature: the audit relationship is longitudinal. A regulated firm reports outcome metrics
across many rounds, an auditor decides which rounds to scrutinize, and the firm can
adapt over time.
This longitudinal structure creates a class of strategic auditee behavior that prior work
on AI auditing has largely sidestepped. The strategic classification literature (Hardt et al.,
2016; Dong et al., 2018), performative prediction (Perdomo et al., 2020), and audit games
(Blocki et al., 2015) all model essentially one-shot interactions: a single classifier deployed
against strategic agents, or a single audit allocation across one population. Recent work
on AI audit ecosystems and access requirements (Casper et al., 2024; Raji et al., 2022)
has clarified what auditors need but assumed an honest-but-curious auditee. The most
directly comparable empirical study—a deployed multi-party computation protocol for
fairness monitoring (He et al., 2026)—explicitly assumes honest-but-curious parties. None
of this work asks how an adaptive auditee should be expected to behave when audited
continuously, with discretion over the timing, definition, and population coverage of its
reports.
Empirical work on continuous compliance reveals that this gap is not merely theo-
retical. Audits of the DSA Transparency Database have documented inconsistencies in
moderation reporting that survive across cycles (Drolsbach and Pröllochs, 2023; Trujillo et
al., 2025), and recent work documents systematic platform-side restriction of API access
that creates “audit blind-spots” preventing independent verification across X/Twitter,
Reddit, TikTok, and Meta (Burnat and Davidson, 2025). Continuous-auditing research
in accounting has long acknowledged adaptive auditees (Power, 2019; Heald, 2018) but
has not been formalized into game-theoretic primitives that the AI auditing community
can build on.

the formal study of agents who modify features to game a classifier; Dong et al. (2018)
extended this to revealed-preference settings. Perdomo et al. (2020) introduced perfor-

essentially one-shot interaction: a single classifier deployed against strategic agents, with

no notion of an audit cadence chosen by an auditor. We are interested in the dual set-

a temporal policy.
Audit games.
Blocki et al. (2015) introduced multi-resource audit games as a formal
framework for allocating audit effort across multiple targets under budget constraint. We
borrow the bilevel-with-commitment template but specialize to the longitudinal compli-
ance setting where a single regulator-firm pair plays out over 𝑇reporting cycles, and
where the strategic move is the auditee’s choice of when and how much to drift, not the
allocation of defender resources.
Privacy-preserving fairness monitoring.
He et al. (2026) provide the most directly
comparable applied work: an MPC-based fairness monitoring protocol for algorithmic
hiring, validated in a real industrial deployment. Their framework assumes honest-but-
curious parties and uses a single-round audit interface. Recent game-theoretic treatments
of privacy-preserving algorithmic accountability (e.g., Yang et al., 2025) also remain
single-round. Our benchmark is the temporal/strategic complement: it asks what hap-
pens when the auditee adapts across rounds and the auditor must trade off detection
latency against welfare loss.
AI audit institutions.
Raji et al. (2022) map the third-party AI audit ecosystem;
Casper et al. (2024) argue that black-box access is insuﬀicient for rigorous AI audits;
Burnat and Davidson (2025) document how platform API restrictions operationalize
that access-insuﬀiciency in practice across X/Twitter, Reddit, TikTok, and Meta. These
contributions clarify what regulators need from access and information rights but stop
short of formal models of adaptive auditees. Empirical work on the DSA Transparency
Database (Drolsbach and Pröllochs, 2023; Trujillo et al., 2025) documents inconsisten-
cies in self-reported moderation actions but provides no game-theoretic framework. Our
benchmark fills this gap: we provide a runnable environment in which the analyst can
pose hypotheses about auditor and auditee behavior and obtain headline numbers under
standardized metrics.

3.1
Audit Game Setup
We model continuous compliance audits as a 𝑇-round Stackelberg game between an au-
ditor (regulator) and an auditee (regulated firm operating an AI system).
At round
𝑡∈{0, 1, … , 𝑇−1}:
1. The latent ground-truth fairness metric 𝑚𝑡∈[0, 1] evolves according to a clipped
baseline drift, 𝑚𝑡= clip(𝑚𝑡−1 + 𝜂𝑡, 0, 1) with 𝜂𝑡∼𝒩(0, 𝜎2) (𝜎= 0.02 in our
experiments) representing natural population-level variation.
2. The auditee chooses a reported metric
̂𝑚𝑡∈[0, 1] and a sample size 𝑛𝑡∈{𝑛min, 𝑛max},
possibly conditioned on its own prior actions and audit-detection history.
3. The auditor decides whether to audit round 𝑡. We restrict attention here to static
policies, where the audit set 𝒜𝜋⊆{0, 1, … , 𝑇−1} is committed at 𝑡= 0 and the
audit decision conditions only on the round index 𝑡, not on observed reports.
Information structure.
The auditee observes the auditor’s committed policy 𝜋(in-
cluding any pseudo-random seed determining the audit schedule) at 𝑡= 0, but does not
modify its own committed strategy mid-game. Detection events are observable to the au-
ditee but do not trigger strategy changes within a single play of the game; multi-game rep-
utation effects are deliberately bracketed (§6). This corresponds to the full-information
Stackelberg setting in which the auditor moves first by committing. Strategies in §3 are
canonical policy probes, not equilibrium best-responses (§6).
Three information regimes.
To make the dependence of our results on this assump-
tion explicit, we distinguish three regimes the same simulator can instantiate: (R1) public
deterministic schedule — the audit set 𝒜𝜋is fixed at 𝑡= 0 and known to the auditee;

4
The Cover Regime: A Structural Observation
The simulator runs are organized by a single structural observation about noise-aware
static auditors. Observation 4.1 below is conditional, not universal: it identifies a quan-
tifiable interval (the cover regime) in which the auditor design space exposes a binding
trade-off, and it identifies the analytical exit from that interval. We state it as an obser-
vation rather than a theorem because the proof is immediate from the Wald-CI threshold
formula; the contribution is the framing and the exit, not the proof.
We restrict attention to static temporal-coverage policies: policies 𝜋that specify an
audit set 𝒜𝜋⊆{0, … , 𝑇−1} at 𝑡= 0, condition only on the round index, and apply the
noise-aware Wald rule (3.1) as their sole acceptance criterion. This class stylizes current
reporting and audit regimes (DSA Articles 15/37 transparency reports and audits; AI
Act Article 72 post-market monitoring) in which cadence is often specified more explicitly
than verified-sample construction. A precision floor is regulatorily available but has not
been prescribed in practice for reasons — false-positive inflation on legitimately small
operating populations, legal contestability of any specific 𝑛floor value, and methodological
discretion left by existing DSA / AI Act obligations — that we discuss in Appendix D.
The Min-sample floor baseline of §3 demonstrates the analytical exit from this class while
making these adoption barriers empirically visible.

Auditor / Auditee protocol
Both interfaces are deliberately minimal so that user-supplied strategies and policies plug

• Auditee.choose_report(t, true_metric, audit_history) -> (reported, n_t):

round 𝑡.
Static policies ignore history entirely; the parameter is provided for
adaptive extensions.
A.4
Detection rule
The implementation of equation (3.1):
def detection_threshold(reported_p, n, epsilon=0.0, z=1.96):
p = max(0.0, min(1.0, reported_p))
se = math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / max(n, 1))
return epsilon + z * se
At 𝑝= 0.5 and 𝑧= 1.96, this gives ∼0.031 for 𝑛= 1000, ∼0.098 for 𝑛= 100. Detection
fires when | ̂𝑚𝑡−𝑚𝑡| exceeds this threshold on an audited round.

B.4
Compute requirements
A full 30-seed sweep across the 5×5 gaming-strategy-by-policy matrix on each config (50
gaming cells across both configs, plus 10 Honest baseline rows; 60 cells total) completes
in well under a minute on a 2024 MacBook (Python 3.11, NumPy 1.26, no GPU); a
single-seed cell runs in under a second. The benchmark is intentionally lightweight; the
analytical interest is in policy and strategy design, not in training neural surrogates.
Future extensions with neural auditee policies will require GPU but are out of scope for
the current submission.

D
Why precision floors are regulatorily available but
rarely prescribed
The static-temporal-coverage class scoped in §4 is a stylization of current reporting and
audit regimes (DSA Articles 15/37 transparency reports and audits; AI Act Article 72
post-market monitoring) in which audit cadence is often specified more explicitly than
verified-sample construction. A precision floor is regulatorily available but has not been
prescribed in practice for at least three reasons immediate from the sensitivity analysis
(§5, Figure 3):
• (i) FPR on legitimately small populations. A precision floor inflates false-
positive rates on operating populations that are legitimately small — low-traﬀic
content categories, platforms in early growth phases, or product lines serving narrow
user segments — creating administrative friction the regulator must adjudicate
round-by-round.
• (ii) Legal contestability of the threshold.
Any specific 𝑛floor value is con-
testable as arbitrary in legal proceedings without a calibration to the noise-aware
threshold, and the noise-aware threshold itself depends on the very 𝑛𝑡the floor is
meant to constrain.
• (iii) Methodological discretion in existing obligations. DSA and AI Act obli-
gations leave substantial methodological discretion over how reported metrics are
operationalized, including which populations are included in a report, presumably
to accommodate heterogeneous platform sizes.
The MinSampleFloor baseline of §3 demonstrates the analytical exit from the static-
temporal-coverage class while making these adoption barriers empirically visible: Fig-
ure 3(a) shows the binary trade-off in 𝑛𝑡that any level-conditioned floor exposes, moti-
vating richer adaptive frontiers conditioning on 𝑛𝑡trajectories rather than levels.
