A C∗-ALGEBRAIC HOFFMAN–WIELANDT THEOREM
BHISHAN JACELON
Abstract. We observe that the 2-norm distance dU,2 between the unitary orbits of normal
elements in a II1 factor M is equal to the 2-Wasserstein distance between the spectral measures
induced by the trace τM. Using classification and optimal transport theory, we deduce an
analogous 2-norm equation for normal operators x and y in simple, separable, unital, nuclear,
Z-stable C∗-algebras that are either monotracial, or real rank zero with finitely many extremal
traces, provided that σ(x) = σ(y) is convex. Consequently, dU,2 equips the set of approximate
unitary equivalence classes of contractive normal elements of M with the structure of a compact
length space. The same is true of the set of equivalence classes of embeddings into the Jiang–Su
algebra Z of classifiable tracial 2-Wasserstein spaces over compact, convex planar domains.
Introduction
This article is devoted to measure-theoretic computations of distances between unitary
orbits of normal elements in C∗-algebras. The origin of this research programme is Weyl’s
theorem [38, Satz 1], which implies that for self-adjoint matrices x, y ∈Mn, the distance

permutation once the eigenvalues of x and y are listed in increasing order α1 ≤· · · ≤αn and
β1 ≤· · · ≤βn. Lacking such a canonical ordering for more general (multi)sets of complex
numbers, it is not a priori clear whether the equality dU(x, y) = δ(x, y) should hold for other
classes of normal matrices. Since the inequality dU(x, y) ≤δ(x, y) is an easy consequence
of diagonalisation, what is at issue is the reverse inequality δ(x, y) ≤dU(x, y). That is the
substance of Weyl’s theorem for self-adjoint matrices, and Bhatia and Davis’s later theorem
for unitaries [1]. The inequality is in fact known not to hold in general, even in M3 [17],
although there is a universal constant c such that δ(x, y) ≤c · dU(x, y) for normal elements
x, y in any Mn [2] and indeed in any semifinite von Neumann algebra [9, 15].
For tracial von Neumann or C∗-algebras, one can interpret the optimal matching distance
δ as the ∞-Wasserstein distance (1.2) between spectral measures, and then look for classes of
normal elements for which dU = W∞(see, for example, [15, 18, 24, 25]). In [21, 23], a case
is made for broadening the scope of this analysis to include the full family of Wasserstein
metrics (Wp)p∈[1,∞] (1.1). Motivated by recent developments in the theory of unitary orbits in
II1 factors (see [11, 27, 19], or Theorem 2.1 for a summary), our priority here is p = 2. The goal

distance dU,2(x, y) (1.3) is equal to the 2-Wasserstein distance W2(x, y) (1.4).

There are a few available strategies for facilitating the move from von Neumann algebras
to tracial C∗-algebras. For self-adjoint operators in C∗-algebras of real rank zero (meaning
that the invertible self-adjoint elements are dense among all self-adjoint elements), one can
compute W∞as the distance between eigenvalue functions and proceed as in [33] or [32,
Theorem 5.1]. For more general classes of normal elements, there have been two different
approaches. The first approach (more fundamental, and closer in spirit to the work of Hiai
and Nakamura) uses Riesz interpolation in conjunction with weak unperforation, which are
properties of the K0-group that in particular hold when A has real rank zero, stable rank one
and tensorially absorbs the Jiang–Su algebra Z (see [39, 14, 30]). This method establishes
an analogue of the p = ∞version of Theorem 2.6 (one half of the proof of Theorem A)
without restrictions on spectra. See especially [18] (in particular, [18, Theorem 3.6]), as well
as [12, 36, 37].
The second approach, developed in [25], combines classification and optimal transport
theory. In Section 3, we revisit, clarify and simplify some of this theory (see in particular
Theorem 3.6 and Theorem 3.11), and we adapt it to p < ∞. Here, our focus shifts from
normal elements to embeddings φ: B →A for certain commutative (or noncommutative
[23]) domains B, and certain simple, separable, unital, nuclear, Z-stable codomains A. The
idea is to solve an optimal transport problem in B (see Theorem 3.7), and then use the
classification of embeddings [8] to convert the optimal transport automorphism of B into an
optimal conjugating unitary in A (see Theorem 3.9). The approach of [18], in which B is the
C∗-algebra of continuous functions on the spectrum of a normal operator, is to directly solve
a noncommutative transport problem in the C∗-algebra A using the structural properties
discussed above. Our technique needs additional assumptions on A and B. Namely, we
require either that A has a unique trace and that K1(B) = 0, or that A has real rank zero
and finitely many extremal tracial states. In both cases, the classifying invariant KTu of [8]
reduces to tracial data on the K-theoretically localised components (3.5) of the set of unital
embeddings of B into A (see Theorem 3.2). We can then extract an inequality of the form
dU,p ≤k·Wp for a suitable ‘transport constant ’ k that depends on the geometry of the domain
(see Definition 3.3). An application of Hoffman–Wielandt (Theorem 3.11) gives us the reverse
inequality and our main result for tracial C∗-algebras (see Corollary 3.13).
Theorem B. Let A be a simple, separable, unital, nuclear, Z-stable C∗-algebra that is either monotra-
cial, or real rank zero with finitely many extremal tracial states. If x, y ∈A are normal elements such
that σ(x) = σ(y) is convex, then dU,2(x, y) = W2(x, y).
The utility of these theorems is that they provide insight into the geometry of the set of
approximate unitary equivalence classes of normal elements (in the II1 factor setting) or of
embeddings (in the setting of classifiable tracial C∗-algebras). In similar spirit to ongoing
work of Toms [34, 35], we are especially interested in the existence and characterisation of
length-minimising geodesics between classes. These geodesics are very well understood in
classical Wasserstein space: they are ‘displacement interpolations’ corresponding to optimal
dynamical transference plans that describe how mass is transported over time. Theorem A
affords us an operator-theoretic interpretation of this metric structure (see Theorem 2.9).
