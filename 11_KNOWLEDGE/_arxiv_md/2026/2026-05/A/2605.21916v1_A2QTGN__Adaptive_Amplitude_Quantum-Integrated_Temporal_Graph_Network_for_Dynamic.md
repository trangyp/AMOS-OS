Abstract—Dynamic link prediction is important for model-
ing evolving interactions in complex systems, including social,
communication, financial, and transportation networks. Classical
temporal graph models capture sequential dependencies, but they
may struggle to represent concurrent and rapidly changing node-
edge interactions in large dynamic graphs. We propose A2QTGN
(Adaptive Amplitude Quantum-Integrated Temporal Graph Net-
work), a hybrid quantum-classical framework that combines
adaptive amplitude encoding with a Temporal Graph Network
backbone. The proposed mechanism represents node interaction
features as quantum states and selectively refreshes amplitude
embeddings based on temporal activity, preserving stable node
states while emphasizing meaningful structural changes. This
design reduces unnecessary quantum re-encoding and improves
temporal representation for link prediction. Experiments on
five Temporal Graph Benchmark datasets show that A2QTGN
achieves strong predictive and ranking performance across diverse
dynamic graphs. Ablation studies confirm the importance of
both the quantum embedding module and the adaptive update
strategy, while hardware-aware inference using a noisy backend
and limited real-device execution supports the feasibility of near-
term quantum-assisted temporal graph learning.
Index Terms—Quantum Machine Learning, Temporal Graph
Networks, Amplitude Encoding, Dynamic Graphs, Link Prediction
I. INTRODUCTION
Dynamic graphs, which represent systems whose structure
changes over time, capture sequences of graph snapshots where
both nodes and edges may appear, disappear, or update their
attributes [1]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, such representations
naturally arise in social platforms, recommender systems,
financial transaction ecosystems, and large-scale transportation
networks. A central computational challenge in these domains is
dynamic link prediction [2], the task of forecasting future edge
formation or dissolution. Accurate prediction enables critical
downstream applications, including friend recommendation,
fraud detection, anomaly monitoring, and biological network
analysis. Conventional approaches, including static Graph
Neural Network (GNN) [3], temporal GNNs, autoregressive
sequence models, and heuristic predictors, often struggle to
capture long-range temporal dependencies, structural drift, and
the non-stationary behaviors characteristic of evolving relational
Fig. 1: Dynamic graphs
systems [4], [5]. These limitations are further amplified by
fairness and bias concerns, as undesirable patterns can accu-
mulate or propagate across successive graph states, ultimately
constraining reliable deployment in real-world settings.
Recent progress in quantum embedding techniques provides
a promising avenue for addressing these gaps [6], [7]. Instead of
relying on claims of exponential advantage, quantum-inspired
feature maps offer alternative geometric representations by
transforming classical inputs into structured quantum states
whose inner-product relationships may reveal patterns not easily
captured by standard encoders. These embeddings modify the
underlying decision boundary in feature space, potentially
enabling models to distinguish subtle structural variations in
evolving graphs.
Prior hybrid studies have demonstrated that augmenting
classical architectures with parametrized quantum circuits can
improve representation quality, introduce non-linear feature
transformations, and stabilize optimization dynamics [8]–[12].
These observations motivate deeper exploration into quantum-
enhanced temporal graph models.
However, most existing quantum–graph approaches remain
limited to static scenarios and do not incorporate mechanisms
that adapt quantum representations as the graph evolves. This
gap highlights the need for quantum embeddings that are
both expressive and temporally responsive, enabling effective
modeling of continuously changing relational structures.
To address these challenges, we propose the A2QTGN,
an Adaptive Amplitude Quantum-Integrated Temporal Graph
Network, that integrates adaptive quantum feature encoding

II. RELATED WORK
Prior studies on link prediction can be systematically
categorized into three major methodological strands: those that
capture time-evolving relationships through temporal graph
networks, those employing established techniques such as
classical link predictors and meta-learning approaches, and
those exploring the potential of quantum computation with
quantum link prediction methods.
A. Temporal Graph Networks
The evolution of temporal graph networks began with models
like JODIE [13], which used recurrent neural networks to
model continuous-time dynamics, but its sequential dependency
incurred high computational cost and limited scalability. This
led to the foundational TGN [14], which introduced an
efficient, event-driven message passing mechanism coupled
with a node memory module for inductive learning on
streaming graphs. However, TGN’s design suffered from
two primary shortcomings which were limited expressive
power due to simple aggregation, and poor generalization
across diverse networks. Post-TGN advancements addressed
these issues through architectural diversification: TGAT [15]
utilized self-attention for better aggregation but faced quadratic
complexity; PINT [16] enforced provably injective message
passing to enhance expressivity; and lightweight models like
GraphMixer [17] favored efficiency using MLPs but often
sacrificed performance on complex dynamics. The latest trend,
exemplified by research into TGS [18], aims to overcome
the generalization barrier by using large-scale pre-training to
establish transferable knowledge and neural scaling laws for
dynamic graphs.
B. Classical Link Predictors, Embeddings, and Meta-Learning
Link prediction in static and dynamic graphs remains a
challenge, as extensively studied by Ghasemian et al. [19].
Their evaluation across hundreds of real-world networks
demonstrated that no single algorithm dominates, leading
to the consensus that near-optimal performance relies on
meta-learning ensembles combining multiple structural and
embedding-based methods. While models that integrate node
attributes, such as AGEE [20], successfully push performance
yielding a 3% improvement over conventional embedding
methods, the struggle for efficiency and expressivity persists in
increasingly complex scenarios. For instance, methods designed
for highly expressive knowledge graph link prediction, such
as ComplEx [21] or RotatE [22], achieved high scores but
are fundamentally constrained by their transductive nature and
high computational demands, struggling to scale to the billions
of edges found in massive industrial graphs. Similarly, while
deep GNN architectures like GraIL [23] enable inductive link
prediction, they require expensive local sub-graph extraction
for every link prediction query, drastically increasing the
inference latency and posing a clear, quantitative trade-off
between structural expressivity and the necessity of real-time
computational efficiency. This ongoing friction necessitates
continued research into scalable yet highly expressive models.
C. Quantum Link Prediction and Embeddings
Recent efforts have explored quantum models for link
prediction and representation learning. Fundamental to this
area are quantum encoding schemes, including basic strategies
such as angle encoding [24] and Amplitude encoding [24],
as well as more complex circuit-based methods, including
Instantaneous Quantum Polynomial (IQP) circuits and Quan-
tum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)–inspired
circuits, all of which map classical features into expressive
quantum states. Building upon these foundational encoding
techniques, specialized quantum models have been developed
for relational tasks. PQKELP [25], for instance, introduced
a projected quantum kernel embedding approach tailored
for dynamic networks, demonstrating that quantum feature
mappings can significantly improve relational reasoning capa-
bilities. Concurrently, Moutinho et al. [26] proposed a quantum-
walk-based link prediction model, effectively showing how
quantum interference effects can be harnessed to uncover latent
connectivity patterns.
Despite these critical advances, existing quantum approaches
predominantly operate on static graphs. They fundamentally
lack dedicated mechanisms for temporal memory or continuous-

III. METHODOLOGY
The proposed framework, A2QTGN, integrates quantum
feature encoding within a temporal graph learning architecture
to enable dynamic link prediction on evolving graphs. The
model combines two complementary modules: (1) the AAE,
which encodes temporal variations of node features into quan-
tum states, and (2) a memory-based TGN, which propagates
temporal dependencies across events for link prediction.
An overview of the workflow is shown in Fig. 2. Temporal
interaction data are processed sequentially: node features are
quantum-encoded using the AAE, integrated into temporal
embeddings through the TGN memory mechanism, and finally
used for link prediction. The following subsections describe
each component in detail.
A. Discrete Temporal Graph Formulation
To support the proposed methodology, the temporal learning
pipeline first requires a structured representation of how the
graph evolves over time. In this work, we adopt a discrete
temporal graph formulation that aligns with the event-driven
processing of A2QTGN.
A discrete temporal graph is modeled as a sequence of graph
snapshots indexed by discrete time steps:

A2QTGN.
B. Adaptive Amplitude Encoding
The AAE module represents the core novelty of our approach,
addressing a fundamental efficiency challenge in temporal
quantum graph processing. This module efficiently translates
changes in node features over time into the quantum amplitude
space. Rather than re-encoding every node at every timestep,
AAE measures the magnitude of feature change and adjusts the
encoding accordingly. Nodes that exhibit meaningful temporal
variation receive fresh amplitude embeddings, while nodes with
minimal change simply retain their previous quantum state. This
adaptive mechanism allows the model to focus computational
effort where the graph is evolving most, significantly improving
efficiency while preserving the expressiveness of the learned
representations.

C. Temporal Memory Update
The temporal memory update module is responsible for
tracking historical dynamics and maintaining a long-term
profile for each node (users and items). It builds on the
core principles of the TGN architecture. Specifically, TGN
introduces a continuous-time memory mechanism in which
each node maintains a dedicated memory vector that is updated
only when new interaction events occur. This event-driven
paradigm enables efficient processing of large-scale temporal
graphs without recomputing entire node histories at every time
step.
In our framework, this principle is adopted by assigning
every user and item a persistent memory state mi together
with an associated timestamp tlast. When a new interaction
involving node i is observed at time t, its memory is retrieved
and combined with the freshly generated quantum-temporal
embedding zi,t to produce an updated node representation.
The resulting high-dimensional embedding hi,t is then written
back into the node’s memory, mirroring TGN’s message-based
memory update rule. By decoupling the persistent memory
(long-term state) from the transient embedding (task-specific
state), the module preserves the temporal evolution of each
node while enabling efficient real-time adaptation, consistent
with the design principles of TGN.
a) Memory Storage: Each node i maintains a dedicated
memory vector mi ∈RDmem, and a record of the last time it was
updated, tlast. These are managed by the MemoryModule (i.e.,
self.user_mem and self.item_mem): The memory
state mi encapsulates the node’s long-term history and evolving
behavioral patterns.
b) Temporal Integration: When a new interaction occurs
at time t, the node’s current memory mi,tprev is retrieved. This
memory is then used, along with the newly computed quantum-
encoded feature representation zi,t (derived from the AAE), to
calculate a new, high-dimensional temporal embedding hi,t:
hi,t = EmbeddingModule(mi,tprev ∥zi,t).
(3)
After hi,t is used for the link prediction task, it is immedi-
ately used to update the memory. The new memory state mi,t
is set to the current temporal embedding (detached from the
computation graph to prevent gradients from flowing backward
through time), and the update timestamp is set to t:
mi,t ←hi,t,
tlast ←t.
This process ensures that the memory is incrementally updated
in real-time with the most recent, relevant temporal information,
allowing the model to adapt its predictions dynamically.
D. Node Representation and Link Prediction:
The formation of the node-level embedding and its subse-
quent use for link prediction is a two-step process, combining
adaptive quantum encoding with a classical neural network for
final representation and scoring.
a) Adaptive Quantum Encoding (x →z): The AAE is
used to create an intermediate quantum-derived feature zi,t for a
node i based on the change in its features between interactions.
1) Change Detection: The temporal feature change ∆xi,t
is computed: ∆xi,t = Xcurr −Xprev.
2) Activity Factor: An activity factor α is calculated using a
sigmoid function σ applied to the ℓ2-norm of the change,
weighted by a hyperparameter β: α = σ(β∥∆xi,t∥).

a threshold τ. If the change is significant (α > τ), a
new feature vector is calculated as Xprev + α∆xi,t and
normalized to form x′. If the change is insignificant, the
previous state Xprev is retained. This ensures only salient
temporal changes are propagated, reducing the encoding
of noise.

C. Algorithmic Convergence Performance
The objective of this experiment is to evaluate the conver-
gence speed and stability of A2QTGN during training across
graphs of varying scales. As shown in Fig. 3, A2QTGN exhibits
stable convergence behavior across the evaluated datasets.
The model converges rapidly on tgbl-wiki and maintains
smooth learning trends on larger graphs such as tgbl-coin.
The training and validation curves remain relatively close
throughout training, suggesting that the model does not show
severe overfitting under the considered settings.
These convergence patterns are consistent with the design
of the adaptive update mechanism. By updating the quantum
embedding selectively rather than at every time step, A2QTGN
can focus quantum encoding updates on more informative
temporal changes while avoiding unnecessary recomputation.
This selective update strategy helps maintain stable optimiza-
tion behavior while reducing redundant quantum embedding
evaluations.
D. Ablation Study
The ablation study evaluates the contribution of the two main
components of A2QTGN: the quantum embedding module
and the adaptive quantum embedding update mechanism. We
consider three update strategies. First, the proposed adaptive
update strategy recomputes the quantum embedding only
when the adaptive decision rule is activated. Second, the
always-update variant removes the adaptive decision rule and
recomputes the quantum embedding at every time step. Third,
the no-update baseline disables quantum embedding updates
entirely and reuses a fixed embedding throughout the temporal
sequence. This design allows us to separate the benefit of

G. Discussion
The results provide three main insights into the behavior
of A2QTGN. First, the model shows strong predictive and
ranking performance across datasets with different temporal
structures, but the source of difficulty differs by dataset. For
example, tgbl-flight achieves the highest accuracy and
AUC, suggesting that its temporal interaction patterns are
highly learnable despite its large edge volume. In contrast,
tgbl-review presents a more difficult generalization setting,
with lower accuracy, precision, AUC, and MRR. This indicates
that dataset difficulty is not determined only by graph size,
but also by temporal regularity, interaction variability, and the
predictability of future links.
Second, the ablation results confirm that both the quantum
embedding and the adaptive update rule are important to
the final performance. Removing the quantum embedding
substantially weakens the representation, while replacing the
adaptive rule with always-update or no-update strategies re-
duces generalization. This suggests that the benefit of A2QTGN
does not come only from adding a quantum layer to TGN, but
from controlling when quantum representations are refreshed.
Selective updating appears to preserve useful temporal infor-
mation while reducing unnecessary recomputation and limiting
unstable representation changes.
Third, the hardware-aware inference results show that the
learned quantum representations remain usable under noisy
quantum execution. The FakeTorino backend results in-
dicate that A2QTGN can preserve strong ranking behavior
under finite-shot noisy simulation, especially on tgbl-wiki,
tgbl-coin, and tgbl-flight. The limited real-hardware
run further supports executable feasibility, although its results
should be interpreted cautiously because of the restricted
100-shot budget. These findings suggest that the proposed
architecture is compatible with near-term quantum execution,
while also showing that performance remains sensitive to shot
budget, backend noise, and dataset-specific separability.
Despite these strengths, several limitations remain. The adap-
tive threshold introduces a trade-off between responsiveness
and computational savings: conservative thresholds may miss
meaningful temporal changes, while aggressive thresholds may
trigger excessive quantum updates. In addition, the quantum
embedding capacity may become limiting for high-dimensional
or highly heterogeneous temporal graphs unless paired with
more effective feature compression or more efficient circuit
designs. Future work should therefore study threshold selection,
circuit scaling, and hardware-aware optimization strategies
to improve robustness on larger and more irregular dynamic
graphs.

V. CONCLUSION
This work presented A2QTGN, a hybrid quantum–classical
framework for temporal link prediction that combines adaptive
amplitude encoding with a Temporal Graph Network backbone.
The proposed design enables quantum embeddings to be
updated selectively based on temporal feature changes, reducing
redundant re-encoding while preserving informative node-state
evolution.
Evaluation on five TGBL datasets shows that A2QTGN
achieves strong predictive and ranking performance across
diverse dynamic graphs. The results also show that the adaptive
update mechanism improves generalization compared with
always-update and no-update alternatives, while hardware-
aware inference supports the feasibility of executing the model
under noisy quantum settings.
Future work will focus on improving scalability for larger
and more heterogeneous temporal graphs, refining the adaptive
threshold selection, and developing more hardware-efficient
quantum circuits for dynamic network analysis.
