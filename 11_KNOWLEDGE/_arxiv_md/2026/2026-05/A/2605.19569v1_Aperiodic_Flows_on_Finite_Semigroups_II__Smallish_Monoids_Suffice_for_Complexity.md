Aperiodic Flows on Finite Semigroups II: Smallish Monoids Suffice
for Complexity 1
Stuart Margolis and John Rhodes
May 20, 2026∗
Abstract
A smallish monoid M is a monoid that has a unique 0-minimal ideal I(M) that is a 0-simple
subsemigroup and such that its regular J -classes are the group of units and the two in I(M). We
show constructively how to embed an arbitrary finite semigroup S into the evaluation semigroup
of a smallish monoid SEv. We use the theory of flows to show that a group mapping semigroup
S admits an aperiodic flow if and only if SEv admits one. This reduces the computation of
Krohn-Rhodes complexity 1 to the class of smallish monoids.

Introduction
This paper is a continuation of the paper [6]. We will use all the notation and results from that
paper. We summarize some of that information and other background material in the Appendices
of this paper.
The main result of this paper shows how to embed any semigroup into the evaluation semigroup
(See Section 4 of [4] or Appendix B) of a GM semigroup with a very restricted structure. More
precisely, a smallish monoid S is a monoid that has a unique 0-minimal ideal I(S) and whose regular
J -classes are its group of units U(S), I(S) −{0} and {0}. Regular monoids with this property are
called small monoids and have appeared in many places in semigroup theory and its applications.
The proof is constructive. To every GM semigroup S we construct a smallish GM monoid SEv
such that S embeds into the evaluation semigroup Eval(SEv). Furthermore, we use the theory of
flows to show that S has an aperiodic flow if and only if SEv has an aperiodic flow. In particular,
if it is known by induction that RLM(S)c ≤1, then Sc = 1 if and only if SEvc = 1. This reduces
computing complexity 1 to smallish monoids.
Although complexity has been proved decidable for all finite semigroups [8, 9], the construction
of SEv is of independent interest. Furthermore, it has been known for more than 50 years that
complexity of small monoids is decidable [13]. Thus our main result shows that adding non-regular
J -classes between the group of units and the unique 0-minimal ideal to small monoids defines a
class general enough to compute complexity 1 for all semigroups.

Preliminaries
All semigroups are finite in this paper. Complexity means Krohn-Rhodes complexity. The complexity
Sc of a finite semigroup S is the least integer n such that S divides a wreath product
S ≺A0 ≀G1 . . . An−1 ≀Gn ≀An
where all the Ai are aperiodic semigroups and all the Gj are groups. The Krohn-Rhodes Theorem
[11] guarantees that every finite semigroup S has a well-defined complexity Sc. For background in
complexity, see [11, Chapter 4] and [5]. For all undefined notation see [11], [5], [3]. For background
on the theory of flows, see [4, 6] or Appendix A.
Examples are given in the form of a Rees matrix semigroup M0(G, A, B, C) and additional generators.
For historic reasons, instead of C as above we use the transpose CT : A × B →G0. Generators
are given (usually not a minimal set) of the form (a, g, b) ∈M0(G, A, B, C) that generate all of
M0(G, A, B, C), and a collection of elements of the monoid RMB(G) of |B| × |B| row-monomial
matrices with entries in G0. We write an element X of RMB(G) as a G-labeled partial function.
The edge i →gj, i, j ∈B, g ∈G means that the (i, j) entry of X is g. We identify X with the set of
such edges, one for each non-zero row of X.
We have the dual representation for the monoid of |A| × |A| column-monomial matrices CMA(G)
with entries in G.
We assume that each generator X ∈RMB(G) is linked with at least one
Y ∈CMA(G). This means that XC = CY . The collection of all such pairs (X, Y ) is called the
translational hull of M0(G, A, B, C) [11, Chapter 5]. We do not need any facts about translational
hulls except that it guarantees that our semigroups have M0(G, A, B, C) as their unique 0-minimal
ideals. We do not give a Y such that (X, Y ) is in the translational hull of M0(G, A, B, C). The
reader can compute that directly.
A generalized group-mapping (written GGM) semigroup S is a semigroup that has a unique
0-minimal ideal I(S) that is also a 0-simple subsemigroup I(S) = M0(G, A, B, C) such that S acts
faithfully on both the left and the right of I(S) by multiplication. S is a group-mapping semigroup
(written GM) if it is a GGM semigroup such that the maximal subgroup of I(S) is non-trivial. An
important property of GM semigroups is that every finite semigroup S has a GM image that has
the same complexity as S.
With the notation above, we have a transformation semigroup (G × B, S). If s ∈S is given as above
by a G-labeled partial function, we have (g, b)s = (gh, b′) if b →hb′ is an edge of s and undefined if
there is no edge beginning with b in s.

Embedding arbitrary semigroups into Evaluation Semigroups
Proposition 5.16 of [4] proves that any GM semigroup S embeds into its evaluation semigroup
Eval(S). See [4] or Appendix B of this paper for background on Eval(S). Since Z2 × PTn is a
GM semigroup, where PTn is the monoid of all partial functions on an n-set, it follows that every
semigroup embeds into the evaluation semigroup of some GM semigroup. The purpose of this
section is to improve this result.

Recall that a small monoid is a monoid M of the form U(M) ∪I(M) where U(M) is the group
of units of M and I(M) = M0(G, A, B, C) is its unique 0-minimal ideal that is also a 0-simple
semigroup. In other words, small monoids are precisely the regular monoids with exactly 2 non-zero
J -classes. We generalize this definition.
Definition 3.1. A monoid M is called a smallish monoid if and only if the ideal M −U(M) of
non-units of M is a nilpotent ideal extension of a 0-simple semigroup. That is, if I is the ideal of
non-units M −U(M) of M, then there is a k > 0 such that Ik is the unique 0-minimal ideal of M
and is a 0-simple semigroup.
The following Lemma justifies the name smallish monoids for this class.
Lemma 3.2. A monoid M is a smallish monoid if and only if M has a unique 0-minimal ideal
I(M) that is a regular subsemigroup of M and has exactly 2 non-zero regular J -classes, namely
I(M) −{0} and its group of units U(M).
Proof. Let M be a smallish monoid and let I = M −U(M). Then there is a k > 0 such that
Ik = I(M) where I(M) is an ideal that is also a 0-simple semigroup.
It follows that every
idempotent of I is in I(M) and this implies that the only regular J -classes are U(M), I(M) −{0}
and 0. Therefore, M has 2 non-zero regular J -classes and I(M) is the unique 0-minimal ideal of M.
Conversely assume that M is a monoid with a unique 0-minimal ideal I(M) that is a 0-simple
subsemigroup of M and exactly 2 non-zero regular J -classes. Clearly the regular non-zero J -classes
are the group of units U(M) and I(M)−{0}. Let I = M −U(M). It follows that all idempotents in
I are in I(M). Since it is well known that the unique idempotent power Ik of I satisfies Ik = IE(I)I
where E(I) is the set of idempotents in I, it follows that I is a nilpotent ideal extension of the
0-simple semigroup I(M).

Discussion 3.8. Before continuing we discuss the intuition behind the construction of SEv. We
have replaced the partial functions in S acting on G × B by the collection of partial functions
{hx | x ∈S} acting on G × BEv. Each hx has domain (as a monomial function) contained in the
“diagonal” set B0 = {(i, i) | i = 1 . . . n} and image contained in R0 = {(i, 0) | i = 1 . . . n}. Clearly all
of these functions compose to the empty function. We have remembered the formula for each x ∈S
and stored the values in G × R0 but forgotten the composition in S. t acts as a (mod n + 1) clock,
mapping Ri to Ri+1. Note then that Ri, i > 0 intersects the diagonal in precisely one point (i, i). It
follows that any element of SEv that as a composition of generators uses at least 2 of the hx has
rank 1 as a monomial function. This will imply that SEv is a smallish monoid. We then will see
that the operator ω + ∗in the evaluation semigroup Eval(SEv) applied to t allows us to “recover”
the formula and composition rules for elements of S. We give the details below.
We see that the main idea of our construction is to replace a collection X of partial functions
from a set Q to itself by a collection hX of partial functions between disjoint sets DQ to RQ in
bijection with Q. If f : Q →Q ∈X then we define hf : DQ →RQ ∈hX by dqhf = rqf for
q ∈Dom(f).
The idea of separating the domain and range of functions occurs in a number of fields of
mathematics. In graph theory this leads to the bipartite double of a graph. In category theory it is
a special case of the cograph (or collage) of a profunctor. The reader can search the literature for
these and other cases of this construction.
As mentioned above, for us, this allows us to store the values of the elements of S in G × R0
and then use t to ”mutate” these values in order to build a smallish monoid.

A.1
The Type II Subsemigroup and the Tilson Congruence
In this subsection we review the type II subsemigroup SII of a semigroup. It plays an important
role in finite semigroup theory and a central role in flow theory. It was first defined in [12]. In that
paper it was proved that it is decidable if a regular element of a semigroup S belongs to SII. In
particular, it followed that if S is a regular semigroup, then membership in SII is decidable. Later
Ash [1] proved that membership in SII is decidable for all finite semigroups S.
The type II subsemigroup SII of S is the smallest subsemigroup of S containing all idempotents
and closed under weak conjugation: if xyx = x for x, y ∈S, then xSIIy ∪ySIIx ⊆SII. Membership
in SII is clearly decidable from this definition. Its importance stems from the following Theorem,
where we give its original, not a priori decidable definition.
Theorem A.1. Let S be a finite semigroup. Then SII is the intersection of all subsemigroups of
the form 1ϕ−1 where ϕ : S →G is a relational morphism from S to a group G.
The aforementioned decidability results in [12, 1] prove that the two definitions we give define
the same subsemigroup of a semigroup S. Here are some important properties of the type II
subsemigroup. See [11] for proofs.
Theorem A.2.
1. Let f : S →T be a morphism between semigroups S and T. If s ∈SII, then
sf ∈TII. Therefore the restriction of f to SII defines a morphism fII : SII →TII. This
defines a functor on the category of finite semigroups.
2. If S divides T, then SII divides TII.
3. Assume that a semigroup S divides a semidirect product T ∗G where T is a semigroup and G
is a group. Then SII divides T.

Let L be a lattice and let L2 = L × L. Let f be a closure operator on L2. By definition this
means that f is an order preserving, extensive (that is, for all (l1, l2) ∈L2, (l1, l2) ⩽(l1, l2)f),
idempotent function on L2. A stable pair for f is a closed element of f. Thus a stable pair (l, l′) ∈L2
is an element such that (l, l′)f = (l, l′). The stable pairs of f are a meet closed subset of L × L.
Conversely, each meet closed subset of L × L is the set of stable pairs for a unique closure operator

on L × L. We will identify f as a binary relation on L whose pairs are precisely the stable pairs.
Let B(L) be the monoid of binary relations on the set L. As is well known, B(L) is isomorphic to
the monoid Mn(B) of n × n matrices over the 2-element Boolean algebra B, where n = |L|. The
Boolean matrix associated to f is of dimension |L| × |L|. It has a 1 in position (l1, l2) if (l1, l2) is a
stable pair and a 0 otherwise.
With this identification, the collection C(L2) of all closure operators on L2 is a submonoid of

Definition B.3. Let X be an alphabet. We define a well-formed formula inductively as follows.
