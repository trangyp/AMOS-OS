Abstract. Using nonstandard analysis, an intuitive and very short proof of
the Radon-Nikodym theorem is provided.
1. Introduction
Although the Radon-Nikodym theorem is one of the most fundamental theorems
in measure theory, proofs in standard mathematics are long and not straightforward
(e.g. Bruckner [1]).
On the other hand, Luxemburg [3] offered a nonstandard proof of the Radon-
Nikodym theorem using a result of Riesz, while Ross [5] gave another nonstandard
proof of the theorem through a conditional expectation argument. Recently, Ma
[4] presented a Loeb space version of the theorem.
Here we provide an intuitive and very short proof of the Radon-Nikodym theorem
using nonstandard analysis.

5. Appendix: a quick introduction to nonstandard analysis
In this appendix, we assume that the reader is fimiliar with basics of (naive) set
theory and first order logic.
Nonstandard analysis is a theory founded by Abraham Robinson in the 1960s,
motivated largely by the revival of Leibnizian infinitesimals. In essence, he con-
structed a nonstandard extension ∗U (Theorem 5.9) of a universe (Definition 5.1)
containing R. ∗U contains a proper extension of R denoted by ∗R, which is logically
similar to R but includes ideal elements such as infinitesimals and infinite numbers.
We need several definitions and lemmas to prove Thoerem 5.9.
Definition 5.1. (Universe) A universe U is a set satisfying the following conditions:
(1) u ∈v and v ∈U imply u ∈U,
(2) u ∈U and v ∈U imply {u, v} ∈U,
(3) u ∈U implies S u ∈U,
(4) u ∈U implies P(u) ∈U, where P(u) is the power set of u.
Definition 5.2. (Formula without Constants) A formula without constants is de-
fined by the folloing rules only:
(1) For the variables x and y, x = y and x ∈y are (atomic) formulae without
constants,
(2) If ϕ is a formula without constants, ¬ϕ, ∃xϕ, and ∀xϕ are formulae without
constants,
(3) If ϕ and ψ are formulae without constants, ϕ∧ψ, ϕ∨ψ, and ϕ →ψ ≡¬ϕ∨ψ
are formlae without constants,
A bounded variable x in ϕ is a variable that appears in the form of ∃xϕ or ∀xϕ.
Other variables in ϕ are free variables.
The free variables in a formula ϕ is often indicated explicitly by ϕ(x1, x2, · · · , xn).
For a universe U, a formula without constants ϕ(x1, x2, · · · , xn), and the constants
c1, c2, · · · , cn ∈U, we can verify whether ϕ(c1, c2, · · · cn) holds or not in U by letting
the bounded variables in ϕ range over U.
As usual, ∃x ∈a[ϕ] ≡∃x[x ∈a ∧ϕ], ∀x ∈a[ϕ] ≡∀x[x ∈a →ϕ], and ∃!x[ϕ(x)] ≡
∃x[ϕ(x) ∧∀y[ϕ(y) →y = x]].
Definition 5.3. (Internal Formula without Constants) For a formula without con-
stants ϕ, the internal formula without constants ∗ϕ is given by replacing all the ∈’s
in ϕ with the binary relation ∗∈on ∗U (defined in Theorem 5.9). As in the previous
definition, for the constants c1, c2, · · · , cn ∈∗U, ∗ϕ holds or not in ∗U.
Example 5.4. (Universe containing R) Let V0 = R and define Vn+1 by Vn+1 =
S Vn inductively. Set U0 = S Vn. Define Un+1 by Un+1 = Un ∪P(Un) inductively.
Set U = S Un. It is straightforward to verify that U is a universe.
Note that any universe containing R has various mathematical objects for anal-
ysis. Usually we adopt a universe U that has all relevant mathematical objects.

Definition 5.8. (Ultrapower) Let V be an infinite set and I be an infinite index
set. Let denote the set of all maps from I to V by V I. Let < a(i) >, < b(i) >∈V I.
Define the equivalence relation on V I by {i ∈I|a(i) = b(i)} ∈F (this is well-defined

set of equivalence classes of V I.
Theorem 5.9. (Nonstandard Extension) For a given universe U, there exsits a
nonstandard extension ∗U and a binary relation ∗∈on ∗U that satisfy the following
two conditions for any formula without constants ϕ:
(1) the Transfer Principle: There exists an injective map ∗: U →∗U such that
ϕ(c1, c2, · · · , cn) holds in U if only if (iff) ∗ϕ(∗c1,∗c2, · · · ,∗cn) holds in ∗U,
where c1, c2, · · · , cn ∈U and ∗ci’s are the images of ci’s under the map ∗.
(2) the Concurrence Principle: If ϕ(x, y, c1, c2, · · · cn) (c1, c2, · · · cn ∈U) is
”concurrent” with respect to X(⊆U), then there exists an element y ∈∗U
such that ∗ϕ( ∗x, y,∗c1,∗c2, · · · ,∗cn) holds in ∗U for all x ∈X. Here ”conc-
curent” with respect to X means that for any finite collection xi ∈X, there
exists an element y ∈U such that ϕ(xi, y, c1, c2, · · · , cn) holds in U for all
xi.
Proof. The construction of ∗U proceeds as follows. Consider the index set I of all
finite subsets of U. For each i ∈I, let µ(i) = {j ∈I | i ⊆j}. Since µ(i1) ∩µ(i2) ∩
· · ·∩µ(in) = µ(i1 ∪i2 ∪· · ·∪in), this family is a filter basis on I so that there exists
an ultrafilter F on I containing all the µ(i)’s by Lemma 5.6. Denote the ultrapower
of U over F by ∗U and the equivalence classes by [a(i)], [b(i)]. The binary relation
[a(i)] ∗∈[b(i)] is also well-defined by {i ∈I | a(i) ∈b(i)} ∈F. The map * is defined
by a 7→[a(i) = a]. This map is injective by definition.
The Transefer Principle is the special case of the next lemma. For the Con-
currence Principle, by assumption, there exists an element y(i) ∈U such that
ϕ(x, y(i), c1, c2, · · · , cn) holds for all x ∈i ∩X so that we obtain the desired result
again using the next lemma.
□

Example 5.12. (Transfer Principle I: Embedding, *-Omission, *-Pair, Exstension)
(1) If A ∈U and a ∈A, then a ∈U by the transitivity of U. Thus ∗a is defined
and a ∈A iff ∗a ∗∈∗A by the Transfer Principle so that we usually leave
out ∗from ∗∈for simplicity. Since the map ∗: U 7→∗U is injective, A is
embedded into ∗A and we often assume A ⊆∗A.
(2) Since ∀x, y∃!z∀u[u ∈z →u = x ∨u = y] holds in U, ∀x, y∃!z∀u[u ∈z →

the *-pair denoted by ∗{x, y}. By defitition z = {x, y} iff ∗z = ∗{∗x, ∗y}.
Similarly we can also define the *-ordered pair ∗<x, y > so that z =< x, y >
iff ∗z = ∗<∗x, ∗y >. Thus < x, y >∈f iff ∗<∗x, ∗y >∈∗f.
(3) For a, b ∈R a < b iff ∗a ∗< ∗b by (2). If we assume R ⊆∗R as in (1), ∗< is
regarded as an extension of < so that ∗is often omitted,
(4) For A, B ∈U and f : A 7→B, b = f(a) iff ∗b = ∗f(∗a) by (2). If we assume
A ⊆∗A and B ⊆∗B as in (1), ∗f is again an extension of f so that ∗is
often omitted.
