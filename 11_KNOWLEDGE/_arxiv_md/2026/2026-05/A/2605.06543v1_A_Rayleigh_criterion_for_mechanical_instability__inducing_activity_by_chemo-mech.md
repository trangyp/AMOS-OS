A Rayleigh criterion for mechanical instability:
inducing activity by chemo–mechanical coupling
Aaron Beyen
,1 Francesco Casini
,1 and Christian Maes
1
1Department of Physics and Astronomy, KU Leuven
(Dated: May 8, 2026)
Instabilities in thermodynamic systems are often undesirable, as they can lead to loss of
control or even catastrophic behavior. Yet, the same mechanisms can also generate rich
nonequilibrium behavior and may play a constructive role in living systems. We introduce
a theoretical framework, inspired by Rayleigh’s analysis of thermoacoustic instabilities, to
study the emergence of mechanical activity. In particular, we derive Rayleigh-like criteria
governing the onset of activity and the generation of rotational motion in a slow Newtonian
probe coupled to driven chemical processes, described by Markov jump processes. These
criteria are expressed in terms of the phase relation between entropic and frenetic contri-
butions, providing a transparent condition for when chemical driving results in sustained
rotational or active mechanical motion.
Keywords: Negative friction, Rotational force, Activity, Rayleigh-instability
I.
Introduction
In classical physics, instabilities often mark the onset of motion. A paradigmatic example is the
thermoacoustic instability analyzed by Rayleigh, where heat input, when appropriately phased
with pressure oscillations, drives self-sustained sound, [1, 2]. This mechanism illustrates a general
principle: energy supplied out of equilibrium can generate coherent mechanical motion when the
coupling obeys specific phase relations [3].
A closely related question arises in biophysical systems. There, mechanical activity is not imposed
externally but emerges from underlying chemical processes that continuously dissipate energy.
Molecules consume fuel, reactions proceed out of equilibrium, and yet the conditions under which
this chemical driving produces persistent motion remain only partially understood. What plays
the role of Rayleigh’s phase criterion in such chemo-mechanical systems?
In this work, we address that question at a general level. Rather than modeling specific biological
details, we develop a thermodynamically consistent framework that captures how nonequilibrium
chemical driving couples to mechanical degrees of freedom. By combining local detailed balance

with semi-reciprocity, we derive Rayleigh-like criteria for the emergence of activity, expressed in
terms of the phase relation between entropic and frenetic contributions. This provides a physically
transparent condition for when driven chemistry generates sustained mechanical motion, the basic
example of bio-chemo-mechanics, [4, 5].
Semi-reciprocity guides the feedback between two sets (x, σ) of degrees of freedom, possibly of a
very different nature. It refers to the situation where the mutual forces that actually couple the
two systems originate from the same interaction energy U(x, σ), but not necessarily of the form
U(x, σ) = u(x −σ) (full reciprocity) since x and σ might live in different spaces.
Local detailed balance, on the other hand, prescribes how to enter the thermodynamic forces
that bias microscopic transitions, and how entropy and energy changes in the environment (at
uniform temperature) enter the system trajectories, [6–9].
Fundamentally, credible models of
chemo–mechanical activity must respect those two principles; see [10–15] for issues and discus-
sions.
Otherwise, one may transfer or generate rotational motion, even activity, but not in a
way that can be cleanly and consistently interpreted mechanically and thermodynamically. Re-
constructing these ways is part of ongoing and major research initiatives in experimental and
theoretical mechanobiology, also related to pattern formation and mechanically induced biological
functioning, [16–22].
To keep the discussion sharp and simple, we work in the deliberately restricted setup of a probe
(point-particle) moving on a circle, coupled to a collection of fast, independent but driven jump
processes, abbreviated as “jumpers”. This minimal setup allows us to focus entirely on the transfer
of nonequilibrium features and on the mechanism of activity generation in particular. The jumpers
act as an internal chemical reservoir, driven away from equilibrium, to which the probe is coupled.
Going beyond the result in [23] where only the (leading) induced streaming term was discussed,
we study here the emergence of friction and noise (at higher order) from such a coupling, and
how the reduced probe dynamics becomes active, by a simple yet consistent (thermo-)dynamical
coupling to driven chemistry.
In these terms, our central result is that the chemo–mechanical
coupling can produce a regime of (partially) negative linear friction over a range of parameters,
i.e. an effective anti-damping where the velocity amplitude grows; see also [24–26] for a similar
phenomenon in different settings.
It is precisely the time-symmetric dynamical activity of the
chemical section, often referred to as frenesy [27–30] and to be compared with the antisymmetric,
dissipative entropy production part of the response, that allows chemical driving to feed energy
into mechanical motion, at least under the appropriate phase condition. That specification is new

and original even though the phenomenon itself has been described at least since Rayleigh’s work
on the thermo-acoustic (linear) instability, [1, 2], where e.g. an amplification results if, on average,

Plan of the paper: We start with Section II, where the theoretical setup of a Newtonian probe
dynamics coupled to Markov jump processes is introduced. Under the assumption of time-scale
separation, we obtain and discuss in Section III the reduced dynamics of the probe when integrating
out the bath degrees of freedom. The effective dynamics contains an induced mean force, a friction
coefficient, and a noise term, which are computed explicitly under weak coupling.
We discuss
the Rayleigh-like criteria for the linear instability. In a way, the probe may start to behave as a
Rayleigh oscillator, which stands for persistent mechanical behavior. The saturation and nonlinear
regime (beyond time-scale separation) are analyzed in Section IV. It includes the active and the
rotational regimes, which are summarized in a number of numerical simulation results. The final
Section V adds some additional remarks on the large driving limit, the emergence of nonreciprocal
forces, and the possible generalization of a confined probe not moving on a circle. The appendix
contains various clarifications and explicit computations, together with a guide for the numerical
implementation. In particular, in Appendix A 1, we discuss some examples to make our point
about thermodynamic consistency.

VI.
Conclusion
The transfer of a low-entropy condition and associated nonequilibrium features from one set of
degrees of freedom to another is a central question of nonequilibrium statistical mechanics with
important relevance to biology. In the present paper, we have focused on theoretical modeling of


## Page 21


21
chemo-mechanical transfer in a hybrid dynamical setup, and we found the emergence of activity
and rotational motion. More specifically, we have shown that sustained mechanical activity can
be generated for an underdamped probe by coupling it to driven Markov jump processes, while
maintaining semi-reciprocity and local detailed balance with a single thermal bath. By activity
for the probe on the ring, we mean here the occurrence of two sharp peaks in the otherwise
symmetric velocity distribution. The construction makes explicit that activity does not require ad
hoc nonconservative forces at the mechanical level. Instead, it emerges from a thermodynamically
consistent chemo–mechanical coupling in which the internal jump dynamics is driven by a nonzero
chemical affinity.
By keeping the setup simple yet structurally faithful to local detailed balance, we sharpen the
conceptual understanding of how nonequilibrium chemistry can generate mechanical motion, and
under what precise conditions it does so. More generally, that is an instance of the importance
of the time-symmetric fluctuation sector, as summarized by the frenesy [27, 28], only possible
thanks to the presence of net dissipation. One finds that the friction has an entropic and frenetic
component, with the latter enabling negative friction; see also [24, 25]. The origin of activity is
thus traced to the interplay between entropy production and dynamical activity (frenesy), rather
than to explicit violation of mechanical laws. Interestingly, the criteria for the occurrence of rota-
tional forces and for the emergence of negative linear friction are very similar to those derived by
Rayleigh for the combustion instability. Also, the velocity and phase space distribution show the
characteristic of a Rayleigh oscillator.

A.
Coupling diffusion to reaction
There exists a multitude of ways by which coupling to jumpers produces motion, [67]. A recurrent
mechanism in small-scale systems is to couple a diffusive degree of freedom (e.g. representing a me-
chanical position x(t)) to an internal jump process, describing chemical transitions, conformational
changes, or binding–unbinding events. In this section, we review representative constructions of
models in which the mechanical motion is overdamped diffusion x(t), as is typical for biological
motion, and the chemical dynamics is described by a finite-state Markov process σ(t). Naturally,
time-scale separation between mechanics and chemistry, and weak vs strong coupling influence the
possible outcomes for the reduced probe dynamics as well.
1.
Semi-reciprocity and local detailed balance
The naive statement of Newton’s third law (“equal and opposite forces”) is not generally invariant
under arbitrary coordinate transformations.
More to the point is that interactions should not
create or destroy total momentum, i.e. the total stress-energy tensor should be divergence-free.
Yet, in the overdamped limit, you partially lose the clean variational (Hamiltonian) structure, and
reciprocity has to be reformulated again. For a particle coupled to a chemical process, we are
satisfied with the presence of a joint (free) energy U(x, σ) where x is the mechanical and σ is the
chemical coordinate. If the reversible part of the joint dynamics is generated by that energy, we
say that the setup is satisfying semi-reciprocity. If there is no extra driving, we would then have
detailed balance and equilibrium distributions with respect to that energy. We also exclude here
the case where the ‘mechanical’ and ‘chemical’ temperatures are different. The only driving we
allow is via chemical affinities.
That brings us to the second pillar of our modelling and thermodynamic formulation, known as
local detailed balance, [6–9]. A priori, the mechanics is slow and undriven, but is open to receive
energy from the reaction part.
The latter is about chemistry, which is fast and driven, with
dissipation in a thermal environment at fixed temperature T. For that purpose, we model it as a
collection of Markov jump processes with transition rates kx(σ, σ′) for the jump σ →σ′ which may
depend on the position x of the probe. This is the basic structure underlying flashing ratchets,
molecular motors, and chemically driven active particles. Then, under local detailed balance, the

2.
No feedback as thermal disequilibrium
When the probe dynamics for x(t) is externally driven by switching potentials U(x, σ), as specified
by the state σ(t) of the jumper, and the jump rates k(σ, σ′) for σ(t) are x-independent, then there

is no feedback from mechanics to chemistry. That implies there is no reciprocity, except when
interpreting the chemistry to take place at infinite temperature. The nonequilibrium condition
(violation of detailed balance) can then be ascribed to the presence of different thermal baths at
very high (in fact, infinite) temperatures. That is the scenario of the classical flashing ratchet
model [68, 69], where the particle alternates between two periodic potentials, one asymmetric
and one flat. The switching is externally imposed, often modeled as a two-state Markov chain
with fixed rates. Each potential separately satisfies detailed balance for the diffusion, but global
detailed balance is violated because the time-dependent switching is at a different (often infinite)
temperature. Transport results from the interplay of spatial asymmetry and temporal modulation.
The mechanism can be interpreted as rectification of diffusion. There is no emerging activity here.
In rocking ratchets [70], a time-dependent force replaces switching potentials. The chemical or
driving sector acts as a prescribed protocol.
Again, these models are conceptually simple but
thermodynamically incomplete, as the energy cost of switching is not explicitly modeled, invisible
behind infinite temperature.
Moreover, the linear instability discussed in the main text is only present if the bath feels a
backreaction to the oscillator chain. That follows from the formula (11) since without backreaction
the Born-Oppenheimer distribution ρx(σ) would not depend on x such that ν(x) vanishes.
