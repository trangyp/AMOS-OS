A computationally-tractable measure of global sensitivity for
sampling-based Bayesian inference

1Department of Statistical Science, University College London

Abstract
Bayesian inference can often be sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters of the prior or like-
lihood, yet defining and quantifying this sensitivity in a principled and computationally feasible
way remains challenging in practice. Unfortunately, existing sensitivity methods are rarely appli-
cable in modern Bayesian workflows due to their high computational cost and poor performance
in moderate to high dimensions. To address these limitations, we introduce a new approach to
global sensitivity analysis based on the Fisher divergence. Our method only requires a set of
samples from a reference posterior and the ability to evaluate score functions, making it broadly
computationally tractable. Under mild regularity conditions, it controls changes in the whole
posterior, and provides a bound on the impact of perturbations on the first two moments. We
demonstrate these strengths on challenging Bayesian inference problems which are practically
out of reach of existing approaches, including generalised Bayesian inference for unnormalised
models, inference in Bayesian models of time series, and neural simulation-based inference.

INTRODUCTION
Bayesian posterior distributions can be highly sensitive to modelling choices, particularly prior and
likelihood hyperparameters. This sensitivity may substantially affect uncertainty quantification and
predictive performance (Berger et al., 1994; Ruggeri et al., 2005; Kleijn and Van Der Vaart, 2006;
Owhadi et al., 2015), and may arise even when modelling choices vary within a plausible range.
This challenge is further amplified in generalisations (Bissiri et al., 2016; Knoblauch et al., 2022) or
approximations (Cranmer et al., 2020) of Bayesian inference, which frequently introduce additional
hyperparameters whose influence is hard to assess.
Consequently, evaluating sensitivity is a key
component of the Bayesian workflow (Gelman et al., 2020; Depaoli et al., 2020).
This task falls within the scope of Bayesian sensitivity analysis (Berger, 1990; Ruggeri et al.,
2005), with existing methods generally categorised into local and global approaches, both of which
analyse sensitivity relative to a reference prior/likelihood pair. The local approach (Gustafson et al.,
1996; Gustafson, 2000; Roos et al., 2015; Al-Labadi et al., 2021; Giordano et al., 2023; Kallioinen
et al., 2024; Di Noia et al., 2025) employs differential techniques to assess the effect of infinitesimal
perturbations around this reference. It is well studied and often computationally tractable, but its
interpretation can be less intuitive and its relevance limited to small perturbations.
In contrast,
global sensitivity analysis (Berger and Berliner, 1986; Moreno, 2000; Kurtek and Bharath, 2015;
Ghaderinezhad et al., 2022; Ho, 2023), the main focus of this paper, aligns more closely with practical
concerns: it evaluates the maximum change in the quantity of interest within a neighbourhood of
the reference choice. Practitioners often have a sense of a plausible range of alternatives, and this
approach directly quantifies how much their inferences could change within that realistic range.
Existing global sensitivity approaches can be categorised according to three main design choices:
a measure used to quantify sensitivity, such as a posterior functional or a statistical divergence
to a reference posterior, a neighbourhood in which sensitivity is measured, and a computational
tool through which the method is implemented, typically combining an estimator for the sensitivity

measure and a numerical optimiser. These algorithmic components interact, leading to significant
trade-offs between three key desiderata: (i) the strength of the sensitivity measure, i.e. whether the
measure controls changes in the entire posterior or only some functionals such as the mean, (ii) the
interpretability of the measure, and (iii) the computational tractability of the method.
To date, no existing method has successfully balanced these desiderata in a way that is practically
relevant for most modern Bayesian modelling problems, where the dimensionality of the posterior can
be large, and posterior computation is expensive. The biggest limitation has been computational,
with many existing methods either being limited to low dimensions (Moreno and Cano, 1991; Moreno,
2000), requiring a conjugate posterior (Berger et al., 2000), requiring a large number of runs of a
sampling method (Kurtek and Bharath, 2015; Ghaderinezhad et al., 2022), or requiring the solution
of challenging optimisation problems (Wasserman et al., 1993; Lavine et al., 2000). Consequently,
global sensitivity analysis is rarely applied in practice. Instead, many practitioners adopt an informal
approach: they evaluate a few variants around a reference model and informally assess their impact
on the posterior. While straightforward, this approach can easily overlook other plausible modelling
choices that may lead to substantially different inferences.
Our goal in this paper is to address the computational challenges that have limited the practical
use of global sensitivity analysis. To this end, we propose a new sensitivity measure based on the
Fisher divergence (FD) (Hyv¨arinen, 2005), a statistical divergence which controls changes in the entire
posterior. This measure is both strong and interpretable: under regularity conditions, it provides a
bound on the difference in the first two moments and on changes in total variation, Kullback-Leibler
and Wasserstein distance. The key advantage of the FD, however, is its computational tractability.
It can be estimated using only a single set of samples from the reference posterior, without requiring
repeated posterior sampling for each perturbed model in the sensitivity neighbourhood.
This is
achieved through evaluations of the score of these perturbed posteriors, a key quantity used for
many methods in Bayesian computation, including algorithms based on Langevin dynamics and
Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Hoffman and Gelman, 2014; Barp et al., 2018; Fearnhead et al., 2024).
Furthermore, the FD has computational complexity which is linear in the number of posterior samples
and in the dimensionality of the posterior, and can be approximated at a rate depending on the
square root of the number of samples, which is dimension-free. This makes it much better suited for
measuring sensitivity in high-dimensional posteriors. Armed with this sensitivity measure, we consider
two settings. The first, most general, consists of performing global optimisation over the space of
hyperparameters, allowing us to consider a broad range of problems of practical relevance. The second,
more limited, case considers neighbourhoods constructed through hypercubes of hyperparameters in
exponential family priors. The latter setting does however allow us to recast the optimisation problem
in a tractable form which can be solved exactly in a finite number of evaluations of the FD.
We demonstrate the widespread applicability of this approach through extensive experiments
on problems which are out of range of all existing methods. This includes Bayesian inference for
heavy-tailed time-series, amortised neural simulation based inference for simulators of time-series in
telecommunication engineering, and generalisations of Bayesian inference, which we consider in the
context of challenging doubly-intractable models including the Ising model.

• Firstly, for a given candidate posterior ˜Πλ with fixed λ ∈Λ, we typically need to estimate
ρ(˜Πλ) from samples, which can be computationally challenging. For instance, when ρ is defined
through a statistical divergence, its estimation typically requires sampling from the reference
˜Πref and the candidate ˜Πλ. If the estimator converges slowly in the number of samples or is
computationally expensive, this will quickly become prohibitive. For example, (Ghaderinezhad
et al., 2022; Mingo et al., 2026) quantify sensitivity through the Wasserstein distance and
therefore suffer from the curse of dimensionality, with the most common estimator incurring a
cost of O(m3) and converging at a rate of O(m−1/dΘ) in the number of samples m when dΘ ≥2
(Fournier and Guillin, 2015). Similarly, Kurtek and Bharath (2015) use the Fisher-Rao distance,
for which common sample-based estimators would also suffer from a large computational cost
and curse of dimensionality due to the need to perform (nonparametric) density estimation. For
this reason, most Bayesian global sensitivity methods rely on simpler posterior functionals such
as posterior moments (Moreno, 2000; Ho, 2023), which have a cost of O(m) and convergence
rate of O(m−1/2), but unfortunately do not necessarily capture changes in the entire posterior.
• Secondly, beyond the difficulty of estimating ρ once, many existing methods require estimating it
repeatedly for different candidates ˜Πλ1, ˜Πλ2, . . . for λ1, λ2, . . . ∈Λ throughout the optimisation
procedure. This often leads to the use of computationally expensive sampling schemes, with
most existing methods requiring sampling from every candidate considered (Zhu et al., 2011;
Kurtek and Bharath, 2015; Giacomini et al., 2025; Ghaderinezhad et al., 2022). One alternative
is to avoid this through importance sampling using samples from ˜Πref, or to use a few iterations
of sequential Monte Carlo; see e.g. Ho (2023). However, such approaches can lead to high-
variance estimators of ρ(˜Πλ), especially for high-dimensional posteriors and when the reference
has lighter tails than the candidates.
• Thirdly, solving the optimisation problems in (4) can be very challenging, particularly when
the optimisation objective is non-convex.
Several approaches have been proposed to tackle
this issue; see for example the linearisation algorithms of (Lavine, 1991; Lavine et al., 2000)
which turn sensitivity measures constructed through posterior expectations into the solution
to a sequence of linear optimisation problems. However, most papers bypass the issue entirely
by taking a neighbourhood with a finite number of elements, allowing for a direct solution to
the supremum and infimum in Equation (4) (Kurtek and Bharath, 2015; Ghaderinezhad et al.,
2022; Ho, 2023). This severely limits the applicability of these methods, and makes them much
closer to the informal approach to sensitivity analysis.
Put together, these computational challenges mean that existing global sensitivity methods are not
well-suited for modern Bayesian inference problems.

4
STRENGTH AND INTERPRETABILITY
We have now proposed a measure of sensitivity which is highly tractable in the sense that it can
be estimated without suffering from a curse of dimensionality, and where the optimisation problem
can be solved efficiently in a broad range of settings relevant for Bayesian and generalised Bayesian
inference. The remaining questions are whether the measure of sensitivity is meaningful and whether
it is interpretable to the user.
4.1
Strength of the Measure of Sensitivity
We have already seen in Proposition 1 that our measure is based on a statistical divergence, and
therefore controls changes in the entire posterior.
This makes it a much stronger measure than
one based only on moments, or any other quantity which measures differences based on summaries.
Below, we now show that it is in fact possible to relate our sensitivity measure to sensitivity measures
based on alternative statistical divergences which are widely used. However, this is only possible with
stronger assumptions on the neighbourhood.

4.2
Interpretability of the Measure of Sensitivity
Before concluding, we also consider the interpretability of our measure of sensitivity.
As for all
divergence-based Bayesian global sensitivity measures, it is hard to interpret the raw value of SFD(Γ).
Several approaches have been taken to mitigate this issue in the literature.
For instance, when
measuring sensitivity to the prior, one approach is to normalise the values by e.g. dividing by the
value of the divergence evaluated at the worst-case prior in the neighbourhood (Mingo et al., 2026).
This would be inconvenient in our case as the approach is limited to prior sensitivity and would
require additional sampling from Πref.

two neighbourhoods Γ1, Γ2 ⊂Λ, we can use SFD(Γ1) −SFD(Γ2) to measure the relative sensitivity to

various decompositions which can shed more light on what is being measured, including the relative
sensitivity to the prior and loss function, or the relative sensitivity to different hyperparameters.

Comparison with alternative sensitivity measures.
Having illustrated our method and dis-
cussed the importance of the strength of a sensitivity measure, we move on to computational con-
siderations. In the following example, we further extend our analysis to multivariate Gaussians with
dΘ > 2 and study the impact of the dimension of the posterior dΘ and the number of samples m on
the estimation error and computational cost of different methods. We compare against three other
measures of sensitivity: one based on the posterior mean, and two based on statistical divergences:
the Wasserstein-2 distance and the KL divergence. Our results are given in Figure 3.
We first compare the FD and mean measures.
These both have similar estimation errors of
O(m−1/2), and relatively small computational cost of O(md2
Θ) and O(mdΘ) respectively. Although
the FD will have a larger cost for large dΘ, this has to be balanced with the fact that it controls changes
in the entire posterior. In contrast, the two other divergence-based measures are both able to control
changes in the entire posterior, but as discussed below, have estimation errors and computational
costs which are orders of magnitude larger than that of the FD.
Firstly, the KL divergence is one of many statistical divergences used for sensitivity analysis, such
as the Fisher-Rao (Kurtek and Bharath, 2015) and Jensen-Shannon divergences (Kallioinen et al.,
2024), which require access to densities. This is typically not feasible for posterior distributions,
where densities are only known up to normalisation constants. In those cases, it is first needed to
obtain samples from both the reference and candidate posteriors, then to perform density estimation
separately for the reference and candidate posteriors, and finally to plug-in these estimated densities
to estimate the divergence through a Monte Carlo estimator. In Figure 3, this is achieved through
kernel density estimation, which leads to a computational cost O(m2dΘ) and whose sample complexity
is well known to suffer from the curse of dimensionality. This directly shows in the corresponding top
row plot, where we do not observe convergence for dΘ = 25 and dΘ = 100 for m < 5000.
Secondly, we consider the Wasserstein-2 distance, as studied by Ghaderinezhad et al. (2022);
Mingo et al. (2026). Here, we must sample from both the reference and candidate posterior, then
estimate the divergence by computing the Wasserstein distance through optimal transport between the
corresponding empirical measures. The computational complexity of this procedure is O(m3 +m2dΘ)
whenever dΘ > 1, which is significantly larger than all other alternative methods, and the sample
complexity is O(m−1/dΘ) for dΘ > 2 (Fournier and Guillin, 2015), once again exhibiting a curse of
dimensionality. We note that similar results would also hold for the Wasserstein-1 distance.
Before concluding, we note that our study has considered the computational problem of estimating
the measures of sensitivity for a fixed candidate, but in practice these will be used within numerical
optimisation routines as in Equation (4). This will be an issue for all three of the mean, KL and
Wasserstein-2 sensitivity measures since they will each require re-sampling from each candidate dis-
tributions. This explains why existing work in global sensitivity analysis tends to almost exclusively
consider discrete and finite neighbourhoods. In contrast, the FD only requires samples from ˜Πref, and
only uses the score of the candidates, which can typically be obtained relatively cheaply.

CONCLUSION
This paper introduces a new computationally tractable method for global sensitivity analysis in
Bayesian inference based on the Fisher divergence. By requiring only a single set of posterior samples
and operating through score functions, our method directly addresses the key computational limita-
tions that have hindered the practical use of Bayesian global sensitivity analysis. In many cases of
practical relevance, we showed that the Fisher divergence is a convex quadratic form, which allows the
associated optimisation problems to be solved efficiently. In addition, because the Fisher divergence
considers discrepancies in the entire posterior, rather than merely summary statistics, it provides a
strong measure of sensitivity which has high potential for challenging Bayesian inference problems.
We believe there are many possible and exciting extensions for the use of score-based divergences
for Bayesian sensitivity analysis. The method could be readily applied across a wide range of Bayesian
inference settings, including hierarchical models, as well as multi-task (Guo et al., 2011), federated
(Yurochkin et al., 2019; Cao et al., 2023), and transfer learning (Suder et al., 2025) settings. These
settings are particularly relevant because model parameters are organised across levels (e.g., global
and task-specific), and the FD objective can therefore be simplified under a per-level decomposition.
This could enable sensitivity analysis of different levels of the model, allowing the practitioner to
19


## Page 20


identify, for instance, whether posterior instability is driven by shared hyperparameters or task-
specific components, or whether heterogeneity across tasks amplifies sensitivity.
Beyond this, several methodological extensions could be pursued in future work. A first direction
would be to address settings involving multimodal posteriors with well-separated modes, a scenario
for which score-based divergences are known to perform poorly (Wenliang and Kanagawa, 2020;
Zhang et al., 2022). One potential remedy is to construct the divergence using a tempered version of
the reference posterior, thereby reducing the blindness to disjoint modes. A second direction would
be to extend our measure beyond hyperparameter sensitivity.
For instance, Berger and Berliner
(1986); Moreno and Pericchi (1992); Kurtek and Bharath (2015); Ho (2023) study robustness under
neighbourhoods defined by probability balls centred at a reference prior or likelihood; adapting our
approach to these types of neighbourhoods could yield valuable insights. Finally, a third promising
avenue would be to generalise the method beyond Euclidean parameter spaces to encompass posteriors
defined over discrete domains, for which score functions are not naturally defined. In this case, the
divergence proposed by Laplante et al. (2025) offers a principled alternative and also leads to a
convex quadratic form. Such an extension would be particularly relevant for Bayesian model choice
and variable selection problems involving discrete indices, as well as for hidden Markov models with
discrete latent states.
