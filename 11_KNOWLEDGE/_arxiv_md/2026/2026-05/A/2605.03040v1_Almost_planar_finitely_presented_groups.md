ALMOST PLANAR FINITELY PRESENTED GROUPS
JOHN M. MACKAY, JOSEPH P. MACMANUS, AND DAVIDE SPRIANO
Abstract. We show that finitely presented groups which admit k-
planar Cayley graphs contain finite-index subgroups with planar Cay-
ley graphs. More generally, we answer a question of Georgakopoulos
and Papasoglu in the special case of coarsely simply connected graphs:
a k-planar, coarsely simply connected, connected, locally finite, quasi-
transitive graph is quasi-isometric to a planar graph.
1. Introduction
The study and classification of planar groups—that is, finitely gener-
ated groups which admit planar Cayley graphs—is among the most classical
topics in (geometric) group theory, beginning with Maschke’s classification
of the finite planar groups in 1896 [Mas96].
Infinite planar groups were
classified much later, first by work of Wilkie [Wil66] and Zieschang–Vogt–
Coldewey [ZVC70] in the one-ended case. The multi-ended planar groups
are more delicate, first studied by Droms [Dro06] and later enumerated by
Georgakopoulos–Hamann [GH19,GH23], completing their classification.
As is typical in geometric group theory, it is often productive to consider
properties ‘up to finite-index’. A finitely generated group is called virtually
planar if it contains a finite-index planar subgroup. While planar groups are
tricky to classify on-the-nose, the class of virtually planar groups admits an
easy and well-known description, as precisely those groups which are virtually
free products of free and surface groups. Here, the interesting problem is not
to classify these groups but to characterise them via their geometry. More
generally, one can ask the same about locally finite, quasi-transitive graphs
which are quasi-isometric to planar graphs; note that every such graph is
quasi-isometric to a planar group [Mac24], and so morally we should expect
such graphs to have analogous characterisations.
In this paper we seek such a characterisation by studying locally finite,
quasi-transitive graphs which satisfy a weaker planarity hypothesis. Recall
that a graph Γ is said to be k-planar if Γ can be drawn in the plane so that
each edge crosses at most k other edges. The study of such graphs has its
roots in work of Ringel [Rin65], where attention was restricted to 1-planar
graphs. The topic of k-planarity for k > 1 received attention from graph
theorists much later, for the first time in work of Pach and Töth in 1997
[PT97]. The class of k-planar graphs is famously quite fickle. For example,
the problem of deciding whether a given finite graph is even 1-planar is NP-
complete [GB07]. This is in stark contrast to deciding planarity, which is
solvable in linear time [HT74].

We will call a graph almost planar if it is k-planar for some k ≥0. In
the context of groups, almost planarity was first considered by Benjamini
and Schramm in 1996 [BS96]. Among other things, they show that almost
planarity is a quasi-isometry invariant amongst bounded-degree graphs, and
so it makes sense to speak of an almost planar group, meaning a finitely
generated group such that some/every Cayley graph of this group is an
almost planar graph. The study of almost planarity in graphs and groups
has appeared implicitly in the literature in many forms for quite some time.
We give a brief survey of this below in Section 1.1.
The following conjecture, which has, on occasion, been referred to as the
k-planar conjecture, is of interest.
It was posed by Georgakopoulos and
Papasoglu [GP25, Prob. 9.4]; see also [EG24, Conj. 6.1].
Conjecture 1.1 (The k-planar conjecture). Let Γ be a connected, locally
finite, quasi-transitive graph. Then Γ is almost planar if and only if it is
quasi-isometric to some planar graph.
The ‘if’ direction of Conjecture 1.1 follows from the fact that almost pla-
narity is a quasi-isometry invariant amongst bounded degree graphs. The
converse poses an interesting challenge. The purpose of this article is to set-
tle this conjecture for a large class of quasi-transitive graphs, namely those
graphs which are coarsely simply connected. See Definition 2.1 for a precise
definition of this property.
Theorem A. Let Γ be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive, coarsely
simply connected graph. Then Γ is almost planar if and only if it is quasi-
isometric to some planar graph.
Most notably, the class of connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive, coarsely
simply connected graphs includes Cayley graphs of finitely presented groups.
In particular, we deduce the following corollary.
Corollary B. A finitely presented group is almost planar if and only if it is
virtually planar.
The jump from ‘quasi-isometric to a planar graph’ to ‘virtually planar’ in
the above follows from results in [Mac23], though it is also possible to prove
this more directly using our intermediate results in this paper. We give such
a direct proof in Section 4.3 below.
Another source of examples comes from hyperbolic graphs, which are al-
ways coarsely simply connected [BH13, Prop. Γ.3.23].
Corollary C. Every almost planar, hyperbolic, connected, locally finite,
quasi-transitive graph is quasi-isometric to a planar graph.
Our results add to the ever-growing list of characterisations of virtually
planar groups, which is known to be a particularly rigid class of groups. Most
famously, it is a deep theorem, originating in the work of Mess [Mes88] and
completed with the convergence group theorem of Tukia, Gabai, and Casson–
Jungreis [Tuk88,Gab92,CJ94], that a finitely generated group which is quasi-
isometric to a complete Riemannian plane is virtually planar.
This was
recently extended to the statement that any finitely generated group which
is quasi-isometric to a planar graph (indeed, to a ‘string graph’ [Dav25]) is

Theorem D. Let Γ be a one-ended, connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
graph. If Γ is almost planar, then one of the following must hold:
(1) Γ is quasi-isometric to the Euclidean plane.
(2) Γ admits a regular map into the hyperbolic plane.
A regular map is coarsely Lipschitz and bounded-to-one in a suitable sense,
see Definitions 2.2 and 2.3. Note that a complete solution to Conjecture 1.1
for one-ended graphs would require upgrading the regular map f : Γ →H2 in

Section 1.2 below for discussion.
We remark that Theorem D has the immediate consequence that a con-
nected, locally finite, one-ended, almost planar, quasi-transitive graph is
either quasi-isometric to the Euclidean plane or has logarithmic separation
profile, in the sense of Benjamini–Schramm–Timár [BST12]. To the best
of our knowledge, it is an open problem to exhibit a connected, locally fi-
nite, quasi-transitive graph with logarithmic separation profile which is not
hyperbolic.
We pose the existence of such a graph as a question below
(Question 1.3).
1.1. Known obstructions to almost planarity. We now walk through
existing obstructions to almost planarity which appear (sometimes in dis-
guise) in the literature to motivate our approach to this problem. We will
not define most of the invariants mentioned and instead defer to references,
unless they are directly relevant to this paper.
One of the most straight-forward ways to obstruct almost planarity is
through asymptotic dimension. It is known that the asymptotic dimension
of any planar graph (in fact, any graph excluding some finite minor) is at
most two [BBE+23] (see also [FP21,JL22]). Since asymptotic dimension is
monotone with respect to regular maps [BST12], we deduce that bounded-
degree almost planar graphs have asymptotic dimension at most two.
Another powerful coarse invariant applicable here is the separation profile
sepΓ(n) of a graph, introduced by Benjamini–Schramm–Timár in [BST12].
It is a consequence of the famous planar separator theorem of Lipton–Tarjan
[LT79] that a planar graph Γ satisfies sepΓ(n) = O(√n). If f : Γ →Λ is
a regular map between bounded-degree graphs, it is known that sepΓ(n) =
O(sepΛ(n)). In particular, if the separation profile of a bounded-degree graph
Γ asymptotically dominates √n, then it cannot be almost planar. The sep-
aration profile has been computed for many classes of groups. This, in turn,
settles Conjecture 1.1 in many specific cases. For example, almost planar
solvable groups must be virtually Z2 or virtually cyclic [HMT20,GLC23] (in

1.2. Outline of the proof of Theorem A. We now describe the key steps
in the proof of our main result.
The first step is to prove Theorem D. To do so, we begin with a regular
map Γ →Λ where Λ is a planar, one-ended, bounded degree graph, and Γ is
a one-ended, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph; using one-ended-ness and
a theorem of Richter–Thomassen [RT02], we may assume that the drawing
of Λ in the plane is VAP-free.1 We then fill in discs and half-planes in the
complement of Λ with disks and half-planes from the hyperbolic plane to
get a planar complex P. If P is not hyperbolic, this will be seen by bi-
Lipschitz embedded loops in P which—having limited interaction with the
hyperbolic balls and half-planes—can be pulled back to Γ to find subsets
whose isoperimetric properties imply (by the Varopoulos inequality) that Γ
has at most quadratic growth. In particular, this implies that either Γ is
quasi-isometric to the Euclidean plane, or P is Gromov-hyperbolic. We go
on to show that our plane P is in fact quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane
H2 (see Theorem 3.2), which implies Theorem D.
We then proceed with the proof of Theorem A. Applying results of Hamann
on accessible graphs [Ham18,Ham25], which are graph-theoretic generalisa-
tions of theorems of Dunwoody [Dun85] and Papasoglu–Whyte [PW02], we
immediately reduce to the case that Γ is one-ended.
Using Theorem D,
we obtain that each such graph is either quasi-isometric to the Euclidean
plane, or it admits a regular map into the hyperbolic plane. Without loss
of generality, we may assume we are in the latter case.
To prove Theo-
rem A, we need to promote this regular map into the hyperbolic plane to be
a quasi-isometry. Assuming coarse simply connectedness allows us to replace
the graph Γ with a 2-dimensional, simply connected, cocompact simplicial
complex X admitting a continuous regular map into H2.
The core intuitive idea is that we want to map X into H2 ‘avoiding folds’,
essentially meaning that we want to avoid something like the absolute value
map R →R≥0. Morally, such folds are what makes general regular maps far

1.3. Questions. We conclude this introduction by stating two existential
questions. A negative answer to both of these questions would settle Con-
jecture 1.1 in its entirety.
Firstly, as was remarked above, Theorem D implies that any almost pla-
nar, one-ended, connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph Γ is either
quasi-isometric to the Euclidean plane (in which case, we are very happy),
or it has logarithmic separation profile. Currently, all known examples of
finitely generated groups with logarithmic separation profile are hyperbolic.
Even then, the structure of hyperbolic groups with such a separation profile

Question 1.3. Does there exist a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
graph with logarithmic separation profile which is not hyperbolic?
Of course, a positive answer would not be entirely surprising, as the world
of non-finitely presented groups is full of paradoxical constructions. For ex-
ample, it is known that there exists examples of finitely generated groups—in
particular, examples of so-called lacunary hyperbolic groups—whose sepa-
ration profile grows arbitrarily slowly on an infinite subset of its domain
(though may explode outside of this subset); see [HM20, Thm. 1.4].
There is also the issue of accessibility to contend with. There are inacces-
sible groups which do not contain any one-ended subgroups (for example, see
some of the groups constructed in [Dun93]). Directly answering the following
question is therefore imperative to settling Conjecture 1.1.
Question 1.4. Does there exist an inaccessible, connected, locally finite,
quasi-transitive graph which is almost planar?
The fact that an inaccessible transitive graph cannot be quasi-isometric
to a planar graph was the main technical result of [Mac23], and it is likely
that resolution to Question 1.4 would involve reducing to this case. It is also
known—a fact which is due to Esperet, Giocanti, and Legrand-Duchesne—
that they cannot be minor-excluded [EGLD24]. Outside of these results, our
current understanding of the geometry of inaccessible transitive graphs is
quite limited.

2. Preliminaries
In this section we establish our notational and terminological conventions.
2.1. Coarse geometry. In this paper we will often consider several metric
spaces at the same time, to avoid confusion we will use subscripts to denote
the metric space we are referring to. For instance, dX refers to the distance in
the metric space X. The (closed) neighbourhood of a set A is the set BX(A, r)
of points in X at distance r or less from A. We may sometimes suppress the
decorative subscripts from our notation if the space under consideration is
clear from context.

A metric space X is said to have bounded geometry if for all r ≥0, there
exists n > 0 such that every closed ball of radius r in X can be covered by n
balls of radius 1. Note that if X is a graph, this is equivalent to asking that
X have bounded degree.
The following definition will play a central role.
Definition 2.1 (Coarsely simply connected). Let Γ be a graph and m ≥3
an integer. The m-filling of Γ, denoted Xm(Γ), is the polygonal 2-complex
obtained by attaching 2-cells to all cycles of length at most m. We say that Γ
is coarsely simply connected if there exists m ≥3 such that Xm(Γ) is simply
connected.
The motivating examples include Cayley graphs of finitely presented groups,
as well as hyperbolic graphs.
2.2. Regular maps. We now define the notion of a regular map. We will
give two definitions, one for graphs and the other for generic metric spaces.
Definition 2.2 (Regular maps between graphs [BST12]). Let Γ, Λ be graphs,
and κ ≥1 a constant. Then, a map f : V (Γ) →V (Λ) is said to be κ-regular
if the following hold:
(1) The map f is (κ, κ)-coarsely Lipschitz.
(2) For all y ∈V (Λ), we have that |f−1(y)| ≤κ.
We will often abuse notation and write f : Γ →Λ, meaning f : V (Γ) →
V (Λ).
Definition 2.3 (Regular maps between metric spaces [BS96, Def. 1.3]). Let
X, Y be metric spaces. Then, a map f : X →Y is said to be κ-regular if
the following hold:
(1) The map f is (κ, κ)-coarsely Lipschitz.
(2) For all y ∈Y , we have that f−1(BY (y, 1)) can be covered by κ balls
of radius 1.
Note that we do not require f to be continuous in the above definition.
However, continuity will often be desirable, and so we may speak of contin-
uous regular maps.
Regular maps arise naturally from subgraph inclusion, and from coarse
embeddings (e.g. quasi-isometric embeddings) of graphs. For example, if G
and H are finitely generated groups and H includes into G as a subgroup,
then this inclusion induces a regular map between the Cayley graphs of G
and H.

2.3. Almost planar graphs and groups. Recall that a graph is said to
be k-planar if it can be drawn in the plane such that every edge crosses
at most k other edges. For example, 0-planar graphs coincide with planar
graphs. We say that a graph is almost planar if it is k-planar for some finite
k ≥0. The minimal k such that a graph is k-planar is sometimes called the
local crossing number of the graph.
The following is due to Benjamini and Schramm [BS96] (in fact, it is their
original definition of almost planarity), and plays a central role throughout
this paper.
Theorem 2.4 ([BS96, Thm. 1.8]). Let Γ be a bounded-degree, connected
graph. Then Γ is almost planar if and only if there exists a planar, bounded-
degree, connected graph Λ and a regular map ρ : Γ →Λ.
This has the following immediate consequence.
Corollary 2.5. Let Γ, Λ be connected, bounded degree graphs. If f : Γ →Λ
is a regular map and Λ is almost planar, then Γ is almost planar.
In particular, almost planarity is a quasi-isometry invariant among con-
nected graphs of bounded degree. This allows us to meaningfully describe
a finitely generated group as an almost planar group, meaning some/all of
its Cayley graphs are almost planar graphs. Then, another consequence of
Theorem 2.4 is the following.
Corollary 2.6. Let G be a finitely generated group and H ≤G a finitely
generated subgroup. If G is almost planar, then so is H.

ended, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph Γ is almost planar, then either Γ
is quasi-isometric to R2, or maps regularly into H2. We prove this in several
steps.

3.1. Gromov-hyperbolic planes. We begin by observing that the hyper-

We now prove the following.
Theorem 3.2. Let P be a Gromov-hyperbolic, visual, complete Riemannian
plane of bounded geometry. Then P is quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic plane
H2.
Proof. Let ∂∞P be the boundary at infinity of P with some choice of visual
metric ρ.
We now assemble some results from the literature, omitting definitions
which we do not work with directly. By Bonk–Schramm [BS00, Thms. 7.4, 8.2],
P is quasi-isometric to H2 if and only if ∂∞P and ∂∞H2 = S1 are “power
quasisymmetric”. For connected metric spaces, being power quasisymmet-
ric is equivalent to being quasisymmetric [TV80, Cor. 3.12] (the “λ-HD”
definition there is weaker than being connected).
And again by Tukia–
Väisälä [TV80, Thm. 4.9], a topological circle Z is quasisymmetric to S1
if and only if it is “HTB” and “BT”.
The “HTB” definition is equivalent to being “doubling”, which holds for
∂∞P by the bounded geometry assumption [BS00, Thm. 9.2].
The “BT” or “bounded turning” condition is also known as being “linearly
connected”: a metric space Z is linearly connected if there exists L ≥1 so
that for any a, b ∈Z there exists a connected set J containing a, b such that

Proposition 4.5. Suppose f is such that width(f) is minimal amongst all
simplicial regular maps X →P. Then f is width-visible.
Proof. Let fx,R = f|BX(x;R). Then, slightly abusing notation, there must
exist some R > 0 such that for all x ∈X, we have that
width(fx,R) = width(f).
If not, we may use the fact that X and P have cocompact automorphism
groups and apply the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem to find another simplicial regu-
lar map f′ : X →P satisfying width(f′) < width(f), which contradicts the
minimality of f. The proposition follows.
□

4.2. Stability. We now move onto the second property on our manifesto.
This will pertain to our map being ‘stable’ in a particular sense. This will
be a bit more technical. The ideas in this section take inspiration from the
methods of Bonk and Kleiner used in [BK02].
In order to give the precise definition for what we mean when we say that
f is ‘stable’, we will need to introduce the notion of a ‘local modification’.
Definition 4.7 (Local modification). Let ε > 0, x0 ∈X.
Then an ε-
local modification of f at x0 is a continuous (not necessarily simplicial) map
f′ : X →P such that f|U = f′|U, where U = X \ BX(x0, ε).
Intuitively, the first condition just says that f ′ agrees with f everywhere
except for on a controlled ball. We now define stability as follows.
Definition 4.8 (Stability). Let ε > 0, x0 ∈X. We say that f is ε-stable at
x0 if for all ε-local modifications f ′ of f at x0, we have that f(x0) ∈f′(X).
If f is ε-stable at x0 for all ε > 0, then f is said to be very stable at x0.

Theorem A. Let Γ be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive, coarsely
simply connected graph. Then Γ is almost planar if and only if it is quasi-
isometric to some planar graph.
Proof. Let Γ be an almost planar, coarsely simply connected, connected,
locally finite, quasi-transitive graph.
By [Ham18], we have that Γ is an
accessible graph, as such it can be factorised into a ‘tree-amalgam’ of a
finite collection {H1, . . . , Hn} of connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive
graphs with at most one end (see [Ham25] for definitions). It is easy to see
that these graphs are also almost planar and coarsely simply connected. By
Theorems D and 4.1, each of the Hi is either finite or quasi-isometric to
either the Euclidean or hyperbolic plane. By [Ham25], we conclude that Γ is
quasi-isometric to a free product of free and surface groups, and in particular
is quasi-isometric to a planar graph.
□
We will finish this section by providing a direct proof of Corollary B which
does not use the machinery of [Mac23].
Corollary B. A finitely presented group is almost planar if and only if it is
virtually planar.
Proof. Let G be an almost planar, finitely presented group. By Dunwoody’s
accessibility theorem [Dun85], G splits as a graph of groups with finite edge
groups and finitely presented vertex groups Hv with at most one end. By
Theorems D and 4.1, each infinite Hv is quasi-isometric to either the Eu-
clidean plane or the hyperbolic plane. This implies that the vertex groups
are all finite or virtual surface groups. Note that virtual surface groups are
residually finite and virtually torsion-free. It is a classical fact that a group
which splits as a graph of groups with finite edge groups and residually fi-
nite, virtually torsion-free edge groups is itself residually finite and virtually
torsion free. In particular, G has a finite-index subgroup H which splits as
a free product of free and surface groups, and so H admits a planar Cayley
graph [AC04, Thm. 3].
□
