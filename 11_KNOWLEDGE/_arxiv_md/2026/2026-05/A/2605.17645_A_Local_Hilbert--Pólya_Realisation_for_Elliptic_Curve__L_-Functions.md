A LOCAL HILBERT–P´OLYA REALISATION FOR ELLIPTIC CURVE
L-FUNCTIONS
KEJUN LIU
Abstract. We introduce a class of J-self-adjoint causal operator pencils whose spectral de-
terminants exactly encode the local Euler factors of L-functions. Driven by a fractional causal
kernel z−1/2, these operators manifest a rigid arithmetic encoding hierarchy governed by the
geometric genus g of their spectral curves.
For g = 0, a unique pencil recovers the Euler factors of the Riemann ζ(s). For g = 1, we
prove a universal Euler matching theorem: every 2 × 2 causal pencil canonically encodes the
local factors of an elliptic curve E/Q, with the operator invariants mapping dominantly onto
the elliptic moduli space. We resolve the arithmetic obstructions of quadratic twists and inert
primes via the topological reality of the operator basepoints. For g = ∞, discrete encoding
capacity provably collapses into continuous transcendental spectral measures.
As applications, we provide new operator-theoretic proofs of the CM Sato–Tate distribution
and establish an unconditional interpolation obstruction, proving that global L-functions are
structurally inaccessible to any single local operator. Finally, we assemble the global restricted
tensor product of these local Krein spaces. We demonstrate that the Hilbert–P´olya realisation
for ζ(s) reduces strictly to a single explicit convergence hypothesis on the global resolvent trace,
which we state as a precise conjecture.
1. Introduction
1.1. The question. The Hilbert–P´olya conjecture asks whether the zeros of ζ(s) are the eigen-
values of a self-adjoint operator. After a century, no such operator has been found for ζ(s) itself.
The Langlands programme generalizes the question: for any automorphic L-function, is there a
spectral theory whose eigenvalues are its zeros?
This paper asks a narrower version of that question, one whose answer turns out to be
affirmative and constructive:
For the L-function of an elliptic curve over Q, does there exist a finite-dimensional
operator, defined entry-by-entry, whose spectral determinant reproduces the
Hasse–Weil Euler factor 1 −app−s + p1−2s at every good prime?
The answer occupies the rest of the paper. The object that supplies the operator is a J-self-
adjoint analytic pencil on a Riemann surface, with a fractional kernel dictated by causality.
1.2. The causal chain. The operator class has four ingredients:
(1) A finite-dimensional Krein space (CN, J) with fundamental symmetry J = J∗= J−1;
(2) A J-self-adjoint system operator LS;
(3) A J-self-adjoint coupling matrix V with entries (a, b, −b, d);
(4) A fractional kernel z−1/2, the Laplace transform of the Abel memory kernel t−1/2, the
canonical causal response of fractional calculus.

1.4. Comparison with previous work. The Hilbert–P´olya programme has a rich history.
Berry and Keating (1999) proposed that ζ zeros correspond to eigenvalues of xp operators.
Connes (1999) developed a spectral interpretation of the explicit formula using non-commutative
geometry. The present work differs from these approaches in three key respects: (i) the operator
is finite-dimensional; (ii) the target is the degree-2 L-function of an elliptic curve; and (iii) the
construction is explicit down to the last matrix entry.
The elliptic Euler factor 1−app−s+p1−2s has been related to spectral determinants in random
matrix theory (Katz–Sarnak, 1999), but the connection is statistical rather than constructive.
This paper provides the explicit operator for every prime individually.
The closest precursor within the present framework is the single-curve realization at (τ, δ, ∆) =
(2, 0, 2) on the curve 256b2 (§4); the present paper subsumes that case as one point in a four-
parameter family and exhibits the universal probe that covers all reachable elliptic curves.

4.9.5. Scope. Theorems 4.9 and 4.11 establish that the canonical two-mode operator class of
Section 2 realises a specific elliptic curve over Q — namely E0 = LMFDB 256b2 — and con-
sequently has, attached to it, a specific degree-two L-function L(E0, s). We close this chapter
with a precise statement of what this identification does, and does not, claim.
What is claimed. The construction supplies a closed-form local Euler-factor correspondence at
the level of L(E0, s): for every good prime p, the characteristic polynomial of the 2×2 resolvent
at an explicit algebraic basepoint reproduces the local factor Lp(E0, s)−1 identically in s (Sec-
tion 4.10). The construction of Section 4 together with the identification of this chapter supplies
a Pontryagin-space pencil whose spectral curve is E0 and whose resolvent local data is shown
in Section 4.10 to match the local Euler factors of L(E0, s) at every good prime. This is a sub-
stantive arithmetic-geometric statement: the η-non-degenerate operator pencil of Theorem 2.2
sits naturally in the L-function geometry of a specific CM curve.
What is not claimed. The L-function L(E0, s) is not the Riemann zeta function ζ(s). Indeed,
L(E0, s) has degree two as an Euler product (local factors Lp(E0, s)−1 are degree-two polynomi-
als in p−s; see Equation (62)), whereas ζ(s) = Q
p(1 −p−s)−1 has degree one (local factors are
degree-one polynomials). The path from elliptic L-functions (degree two) to ζ(s) (degree one) is
governed by the Langlands degree ladder: it would require a descent from a GL2 automorphic
L-function to a GL1 Dirichlet L-function, which is not a free operation. In the operator-pencil
framework of this manuscript, that descent is structurally barred — the obstructions are cat-
alogued in Section 10 as five closed-form obstruction lemmas, each pinning down an explicit
reason why a candidate descent path fails.
What this chapter does provide. The identification eC ∼= E0 over Q together with the modu-
larity / Hecke-Gr¨oßencharakter data of Theorem 4.14 make L(E0, s) available for the detailed
off-shell Euler matching of Section 4.10. The headline of the manuscript is therefore: the op-
erator pencil supplies a closed-form local Euler-factor correspondence for L(E0, s), with every
local Euler factor reproduced exactly by the resolvent characteristic polynomial at an explicit
algebraic basepoint, and with the Hasse bound for E0 implying the existence of real basepoints
(Section 4.10).
This is, to our knowledge, the first construction with this property for any
specific CM elliptic L-function; the comparison with prior partial precedents (Berry–Keating,
Bender–Brody–M¨uller, Bellissard, Moxley) is deferred to Section 9.

5. Genus ∞: The Continuum Limit and Its Collapse
The Dirac pencil (§3, §3.6) took the J-self-adjoint structure into infinite dimension with
L∞= sgn(n). At rank 2, it produced the same j = 1728 spectral curve as RSCO, but with
a complex basepoint that resolves the inert prime obstruction. What happens when the rank
grows further?
The finite-rank construction occupies the genus values g = 0 and g = 1. At g = 0, the pencil
is uniquely rigid. At g = 1, it is flexible enough to encode one Euler factor per prime. The
natural progression is to let the rank grow.
5.1. The three pillars fail together. The genus formula g(N) = N −1 (§2) implies that as N
increases, the spectral curve genus grows. At N →∞, the secular equation becomes transcen-
dental. Three structural pillars that make finite-rank encoding possible collapse simultaneously:
(1) Analyticity. In finite rank, the resolvent has isolated poles on the secondary sheet of
the Riemann surface, and those poles are the spectral data. In the continuum, the poles
merge into a branch cut [−1, 1] on the real axis. The Fredholm determinant becomes an
entire function of infinite order — no longer a polynomial, hence no longer factorable by
the arguments of its zeros.
(2) Trace-class property. The resolvent trace tr(R) in the continuum involves the har-
monic series P |n|−1, which diverges. The Krein regularization would require an infinite
subtraction. The determinant formula det(I −τR) = 1 −τtr(R) + O(τ2) that gave the
Euler factor in the genus-1 case no longer has a finite coefficient for the τ term.
(3) Polynomial factorization. If det(I −τR) is a polynomial, then R must have finite
rank (Surprise Registry S-004). Infinite rank forces a transcendental spectral function.
The Euler factor 1 −apτ + pτ 2 is a polynomial of degree 2, and no infinite-rank operator
can produce it.
The dimensional inversion principle (S-001) captures this: finite rank is not a limitation
that larger rank improves upon. Finite rank is the load-bearing feature that makes arithmetic
encoding possible. The continuum limit is not a regime to be regularized toward — it is the
boundary beyond which encoding is impossible.

9. Outlook: From Local to Global
The paper has established a complete local theory: for every good prime of each reachable
elliptic curve, an explicit finite-dimensional operator reproduces the exact Hasse–Weil Euler
factor. What remains is the step from local to global — assembling the prime-indexed operators
into a single operator whose spectral determinant is the full L-function.
9.1. The encoding-capacity hierarchy. Before turning to the global assembly, we summarise
the stratification of the local theory by encoding capacity, as it furnishes the natural way to read
the entire framework as a chain of forced consequences.
At one extreme, the Zeta Causal Operator has a single free parameter (the scale E), genus-0
spectral curve, and encodes the degree-1 Euler factor of ζ(s) at every prime through a single
prime-independent basepoint. At the other extreme, the rank-∞continuum pencil has con-
tinuous parameter space, transcendental spectral measure, and encodes no arithmetic at all
(Theorem 5.1). Between these poles sits the genus-1 stratum: a four-dimensional moduli of
operator invariants (τ, δ, ∆, µ) whose j-image is Zariski-dense in the moduli space of elliptic
curves (Proposition 4.4), splitting further by basepoint reality into the RSCO branch (real,
high-conductor twist) and the CD branch (complex, primitive twist) per Theorem 7.1.
The stratification can be read as a chain of forced consequences. The number of free operator
parameters fixes the genus of the spectral curve (Theorem 2.13). The genus fixes the maximum
degree of an encodable Euler factor: degree 1 at genus 0, degree 2 at genus 1, and no polyno-
mial encoding beyond (Theorem 7.3). The encodable Euler degree fixes which L-functions are
reachable: ζ(s) at degree 1, Hasse–Weil L-functions of elliptic curves at degree 2, and nothing
at the transcendental endpoint. The choice point at each level is discrete: the operator either
has the structural property required to descend one level, or it does not. The continuum limit’s
