Abstract
The algorithmic differentiation (AD) of mathematical functions can be interpreted as a
sequence of vertex eliminations in an underlying directed acyclic graph. The problem of deter-
mining a minimum-cost elimination ordering, which we call Optimal Vertex Elimination,
is NP-complete. Consequently, much effort has been devoted to the design of heuristics. Many
of these heuristics are widely believed to perform well in practice, but this hypothesis has so far
been difficult to test due to the lack of scalable exact methods.
We design and engineer new integer programming formulations for Optimal Vertex Elim-
ination and for a related objective we call Minimum Edge Count. Our implementations scale
to graphs one-to-two orders of magnitude larger than existing techniques, enabling the assembly
of a corpus of medium-sized graphs for which optimal solutions are known. This corpus facili-
tates a study of existing heuristics, confirming that on real data popular methods achieve high
quality solutions.
We also make several theoretical contributions.
We give a tight analysis of the forward
and reverse modes of AD, and extend our techniques to provide a simple algorithm for Opti-
mal Vertex Elimination with approximation ratio parameterized by the size of a minimum
source-sink separator. On the complexity side, we give the first approximation lower bounds for
both problems.

Introduction
For over fifty years, the (linearized) computational graph model has been used to guide the algo-
rithmic differentiation (AD) of functions [4, 18]. In a directed acyclic graph (DAG) D = (V =
S ⊎I ⊎T, E), source vertices S and sink vertices T represent independent and dependent variables,
respectively. The internal vertices I represent intermediate values, while directed edges model data
dependencies. By associating with each edge (u, v) a local partial derivative ∂v/∂u, we obtain
a chain-rule-based interpretation of the Jacobian: the derivative of a dependent variable t with
respect to an independent variable s is equal to the sum over all st-paths of the product of the edge
weights (partial derivatives) along the path. Many popular procedures for computing derivatives
can be interpreted as a sequence of graph edits that preserve correctness of this chain-rule-based
interpretation.

directly a non-zero entry in the Jacobian. The so-called reverse mode of AD eliminates vertices in

the internal vertices in any order, obtaining an exact (up to round-off error) computation of the
Jacobian. However, not all elimination sequences require the same computational effort.
The cost of eliminating a vertex v is the Markowitz degree µ(v) := |N−

D(v)|, reflecting
the number of multiplications required to maintain correctness of the chain-rule based interpreta-
tion of D. The cost of a sequence of eliminations is the sum of the costs of the involved eliminations.
While forward and reverse modes both eliminate every internal vertex (i.e., they produce total elimi-
nation sequences), the costs of these sequences may differ, as the elimination of a vertex may affect
the Markowitz degrees of its not-yet-eliminated neighbors. Since the number of multiplications
greatly affects the computational effort associated with derivative evaluations, a central problem
in AD is to find low-cost elimination sequences; see the standard textbook [13], or a recent open
problems paper [1].
Input:
A DAG D = (S ⊎I ⊎T, E).
Task:
Find a total elimination sequence of minimum cost.
Optimal Vertex Elimination
The original formulation of this problem is due to Griewank and Reese [12], who also proposed
a greedy heuristic: at each step, the internal vertex with minimum Markowitz degree is eliminated.
Many other greedy heuristics have been proposed; see Section 4.
Additionally, a variety of randomized, search-based algorithms have been developed, including
algorithms based on simulated annealing [22, 23] and Monte Carlo tree search [20]. Due to their
running times, these methods are best used in situations where the search cost can be amortized
over many derivative evaluations.
Another randomized algorithm, based on Markov chain Monte Carlo, is described in [21]. This
algorithm targets a different objective, introduced in [11] as scarcity, and formalized in [1] as the
Minimum Edge Count problem. In this problem, the elimination sequence σ need not be total,
i.e., not all internal vertices need to be eliminated, and the objective changes from the cost of σ to
the number of edges in Dσ. We refer to the original paper [11] for a detailed motivation of scarcity.
Input:
A DAG D = (S ⊎I ⊎T, E).
Task:
Find a (not necessarily total) elimination sequence σ minimizing |E(Dσ)|.
Minimum Edge Count
Ideally, one would evaluate heuristics by comparing them to an optimal elimination sequence
or (tight) lower bounds. Unfortunately, the decision problems underlying both Optimal Vertex
Elimination and Minimum Edge Count are NP-hard [5], and finding good lower bounds has
remained elusive except in very restricted structural classes. Nonetheless, in an effort to provide a

2.3
Integrality Gaps
By relaxing constraints (10) or (22) to allow variable values in the range [0, 1], we obtain efficiently
solvable linear programming (LP) relaxations for Optimal Vertex Elimination and Minimum
Edge Count, respectively. It is natural to wonder whether rounding fractional solutions to these
LPs may lead to high-quality approximations. It turns out that this is unlikely to be a fruitful
direction without additional assumptions.
Proposition 6. The fractional LP relaxation of the ILP defined by objective (4) and constraints
(5)-(14) has an integrality gap of Ω(n1/4).
Proposition 7. The fractional LP relaxation of the ILP defined by objective (18) and constraints
(19)-(22) has an integrality gap of Ω(n).
Both of our constructions (see Section A.1) have vertices with degree linear in n, so it may still
be interesting to explore rounding schemes in the bounded-degree setting. Bounded in-degree, in
particular, may be practically relevant for computational graphs.

which eliminate internal vertices in forward (or reverse, respectively) topological order. We now
give a straightforward upper bound on the cost of these algorithms. Proofs of statements in this

Summary of Evaluated Heuristics
In addition to Fw, Rev, and separator-based methods, numerous other heuristics for Optimal
Vertex Elimination have been proposed, which we summarize here. A key is presented below.

The original Greedy Markowitz (Mar) rule of Griewank and Reese [12] greedily minimizes the cost of
the current elimination. Several variations have subsequently appeared. For a vertex v ∈I, let Sv ⊆
S be the subset of sources from which v is reachable, and let Tv be defined similarly. Naumann [22]
proposed to modify Mar by considering the cost of eliminating v last: Relative Markowitz (rMar)
greedily minimizes µ(v) −|Sv| · |Tv|. Two more variations, both proposed in [2], consider the effect
of eliminating v on the Markowitz degrees of the remaining vertices. Let M(D) := P
v∈I µ(v)
denote the total Markowitz degree of D. Maximum Total Markowitz Reduction (MR) greedily selects
v minimizing M(D/v). This idea can be refined as follows. Define the damage d(v) of eliminating
v to be d(v) := M(D/v) −M(D) + µ(v). Least Markowitz Minimal Damage (M2D) greedily selects v
minimizing µ(v)+d(v). Finally, we introduce a new variant, which considers the Markowitz degree
of v in comparison to its minimum possible elimination cost µ∗
D(v) := cut(S, v)·cut(v, T), as stated
in Observation 1. Difference to Minimum Cost (DMC) greedily selects v minimizing µ(v) −µ∗
D(v).
Another natural strategy arises from an upper bound on the optimal objective value, shown
in [13]. Let ||S →T||D denote the sum of the lengths of all maximal paths in D. This quantity
is computable via a standard dynamic program, leading to Maximum Pathlength Reduction (PL),
which greedily selects v minimizing ||S →T||D/v [13, 22]. Similarly, writing num-paths(v) for the
number of paths on which v appears, Naumann [22] proposed Pathcount (PC), which greedily selects
v minimizing the ratio µ(v)/num-paths(v).
Finally, Naumann [22] explored the intuition that reducing the number of edges should lead to
low cost. Greedy Edge Reduction (ER) greedily selects v minimizing |E(D/v)|, while a computation-
ally simplified variant (MD) greedily selects v minimizing µ(v) −deg(v).

Evolution Graphs.
We note that the performance of Mar on evolution graphs is somewhat
deceptive, because we can only compute optimal solutions on small evolutions. It is known that the
performance of Mar on these graphs degrades as the graph increases in size. For example, though we
cannot compute a minimum-cost elimination sequence on a two-dimensional 20 × 20 × 20 evolution
(8800 vertices), we can observe that the solution Mar computes is 2.69 times as expensive as that
computed by Fw. Similarly, for a planar evolution derived from a 9-point stencil (see [13, Chapter
10]), Mar produces a solution 2.27 times as expensive as that produced by Fw. On the latter graph,
rMar also fares poorly (2.14 times worse than Fw), and we acknowledge that the performance of
rMar on the evolution graphs in this paper is buoyed by use of the 5-point stencil as a template;
rMar is known to perform poorly for other stencil choices [13]. It has been observed previously that
Fw and Rev perform very well on evolutions (of multiple stencils) [13]; we have found no evidence
to the contrary, and suggest that it would be interesting to pursue a theoretical justification.

Approximation Lower Bounds
The decision problems underlying Optimal Vertex Elimination and Minimum Edge Count
have only recently been shown to be NP-complete [5], and little else is known about their complex-
ity. We now establish the first approximation lower bounds for Optimal Vertex Elimination,
Minimum Edge Count, and Maximum Edge Reduction. For all three problems, we exclude
a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) unless P = NP.
In each case, the hardness
holds even in bounded-degree DAGs; for Minimum Edge Count, NP-hardness was not previously
known in bounded-degree DAGs. Finally, we exclude a sub-polynomial approximation for Maxi-
mum Edge Reduction, unless NP ⊆BPP. Complete proofs can be found in Section A.3.

7
Conclusion
We have introduced and implemented novel ILP formulations for Optimal Vertex Elimina-
tion and Minimum Edge Count, enabling the assembly of a corpus of graphs for which optimal
solutions are known. We used this corpus to evaluate a wide range of heuristics, presenting mea-
sured approximation ratios. On the theoretical side, we have given a tight analysis of the forward
and reverse modes of AD, and have extended this analysis to provide a simple parameterized ap-
proximation guarantee. Also, we have established the first approximation lower bounds for vertex
elimination problems arising in AD. Several interesting questions remain open, including the design
of rounding schemes for our ILPs (in restricted settings), the tightness of the bound in Theorem 11,
and an analysis of the forward and reverse modes of AD on evolution graphs. Indeed, we find it
surprising that these highly structured graphs are not better understood. Finally, while our results
exclude polynomial-time approximation schemes, the (non) existence of constant-factor approxi-
mations remains open.
