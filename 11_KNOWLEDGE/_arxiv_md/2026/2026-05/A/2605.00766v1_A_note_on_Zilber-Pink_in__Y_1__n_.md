Georgios Papas

Abstract.
Building on [DOP25, DP26], we prove two Zilber–Pink-type statements in Y (1)n,
assuming a weak form of the Lang–Trotter conjecture for pairs of elliptic curves.

Introduction
Following work of P. Habegger and J. Pila, the Zilber-Pink conjecture for curves in Y (1)n has
been reduced to counting points of intersection of a curve S ⊂Y (1)n with special subvarieties
cut out by at least two modular polynomials. It is a particular case of such intersections that
we study here.

From Zilber-Pink to height bounds. As mentioned above, following [HP12], the

2.3
Families of elliptic curves and G-functions
Here we fix a family of elliptic curves f : E →S over a curve S defined over a number field K.
We also assume that we are given a point s0 ∈S(K) and, for simplicity, that S is smooth and
geometrically irreducible.
The nature of the theorems we are trying to establish, i.e. height bounds on certain points in
S, allows us a certain degree of freedom. In particular, we may base change the above picture,
i.e. the pair (f : E →S, s0), with either a finite cover of S or some finite extension of K. See,
for example, the discussion in §2.2 of [Pap24] for more on this.
Within this context, we may make a series of assumptions on the pair (E →S, s0). Firstly,
we may assume structural properties about the fiber Es0, such as having everywhere semi-stable
reduction, see Lemma 2.4 in [Pap24] for some references. Secondly, we may make assumptions
about the curve S. The most crucial of these is the existence of a “local parameter” x ∈K(S)
at s0. For more on this, see Lemma 5.1 of [DO25].
The construction of Daw-Orr in §5 of [DO25] associates to the triple (E →S, s0, x) a family
of tuples of elliptic curves E1 ×S . . .×S EΛ →S by considering twists of the original by automor-
phisms of the curve. This process introduces technical details to the proofs of the height bounds
that are fairly well understood and documented in the literature, see for example [DO25] as well
as the subsequent [Pap25a, DOP25]. For that reason, in the sequel we assume that we are in a
simplified setting where such a local parameter x exists and has a simple zero only at s0.
Under the above assumptions, we may associate to the pair (E →S, s0), not uniquely, a
matrix YE ∈M2(K[[x]]) whose entries are G-functions that are “centered at s0”. This process
may be carried out so that YE also satisfies:
1. YE ∈SL2(K[[x]]), and
2. for any s ∈S(¯Q) and any v ∈ΣK(s),f, we have |x(s)|v < rv(YE) := min{1, Rv(YE)} if and
only if Es and Es0 have the same reduction modulo v.
For more on this, we refer the reader to [And89], where the connection between G-functions
and 1-parameter families of abelian varieties first appears. The additional properties above are
ensured upon choosing, with particular care, a basis of sections of H1
dR(E/S). For more details
on these choices, we refer the reader to §3 of [Pap25a].
Given a tuple E1 ×S . . . ×S En →S, in the above sense, we may associate a matrix Yj := YEj
to each of the Ej. The collection of G-functions we will be working with in the sequel will be
YG := {Y1, . . . , Yn}. With the above formalism in mind, in the sequel we say that s ∈S(¯Q) is
v-adically close to s0 if it satisfies the above condition, i.e. if |x(s)|v < rv(YG).

3.4
Lang-Trotter phenomena and unlikely intersections
Let us fix X to be either Y (1)n or Ag, the moduli space of principally polarized g-dimensional
abelian varieties. We also fix S ⊂X a curve defined over ¯Q. Here we briefly discuss what
seems to be a reasonable strategy to establish Zilber-Pink-type statements for the intersection
of S with families of special subvarieties of X that correspond to abelian varieties with extra
endomorphisms, e.g. Shimura curves in A2 corresponding to QM abelian surfaces.
The most direct approach to Zilber-Pink statements, from the perspective of the G-functions
method, is to assume that S contains at least one such point s0 and then try to bound the height
of all other points s ∈S(¯Q) that correspond to an intersection of S with a special subvariety of
“the same type”. For example, assume that in X = A2 our curve S has a QM point s0 and then

try to bound the height of all other QM points. This is much in line with the starting point of
our Theorems 1.2 and 1.4.
Let us write YG for a family of G-functions associated to S and centered at s0, as highlighted
for example in Section 2.3.
The above line of inquiry was studied for A2 by the author in
[Pap25a, Pap25c] and for Y (1)3 in joint work with C. Daw and M. Orr [DOP25].
It is in
these works that the first ingredient that we make crucial use of here appears. Namely, the
independence from the place v of the “local factor” Rs,v of the global relation Rs, in the spirit of
the proof of Theorem 2.1, provided that v is a place of ordinary reduction of the “central fiber”
over s0.
This phenomenon essentially reduces the height bound we are trying to achieve, via Theo-
rem 2.5, to bounding the possible contribution to the global non-trivial relation Rs, among the
entries of YG at s, from the local factors corresponding to places of proximity of s and s0 modulo
which the “central fiber” over s0 has supersingular reduction. In other words, what we think of
as “places of supersingular proximity of s to s0”, in the language of Section 3.2.
It is natural, in light of the results in [DP26, DOP25, Pap25a, Pap25b, Pap25c], to expect
that a version of the above phenomena holds in greater generality. We briefly discuss these
phenomena, beginning with the
3.4.1
Independence of local factors at non-supersingular places. The independence
of local factors for ordinary places in all known cases highlighted above hinges on properties
of the action of Frobenius in crystalline cohomology groups. In short, we may build bases in
crystalline cohomology groups that consist of eigenvectors of Frobenius. In the case of ordinary
reduction, Frobenius has eigenvalues with distinct valuations. This allows us to significantly
reduce the number of algebraic numbers in period relations that depend on the place v.
In general, reduction modulo v is no longer simply either supersingular or ordinary. In con-
trast, the types of reduction that appear may be encoded into finitely many possible types of
Newton polygons, whose number and slopes depend on the dimension g of the abelian vari-
eties under consideration, i.e. which Ag we are studying. It seems reasonable to expect that
non-supersingular Newton polygons of a fixed given type contribute a single “local factor” in-
dependent of the place v, possibly depending on the codimension of the special subvariety with
which S is intersected.
The second phenomenon, that is more closely studied here and in [DP26], is what we refer
to as the
3.4.2
Sparsity of supersingular places of proximity. In short, in all constructions of
global relations mentioned above in the literature, the problematic part is the analogue of the
factor

to bound the degree of this factor polynomially in [K(s) : Q] and sub-polynomially in h(s).
In [DOP25, Pap25a, Pap25c] the problem of bounding the number of such places was first
raised. In these works we study intersections of a curve S, either in A2 or Y (1)3, with spe-
cial subvarieties that are of the smallest possible codimension relevant to Zilber-Pink in these
settings, i.e. codimension 2 special subvarieties.
Considering intersections with special subvarieties of higher codimension, as is done here
and in [DP26], allows us to reduce the bounds we want to a weakened form of the Lang-
Trotter conjecture for pairs of elliptic curves defined over a number field K. As noted in the
introduction, this conjecture for pairs has been studied in the past, see [AP19, FM95], at least
over Q. The form of the conjecture presented here, namely Conjecture 1.5, is weaker than the
bounds predicted by Sato-Tate-type heuristics. For more on this we point the interested reader
to [DP26].
From the perspective of the G-functions method, the sparsity of supersingular places of
proximity emerges as the natural number-theoretic obstruction underlying Zilber–Pink. Based
on Sato-Tate-type heuristics, it seems natural, at least in the settings considered here and in
[DP26], to expect that places of supersingular reduction of the central fiber over s0 are sparse
under the assumption that s0 is a “sufficiently generic” point in the ambient moduli space. This

fiber over s0, in the case where s0 is no longer “generic”. For example, for CM elliptic curves
Deuring’s criterion ensures, roughly speaking, that for any given x half of the primes p ≤x will
be supersingular for the curve in question.
