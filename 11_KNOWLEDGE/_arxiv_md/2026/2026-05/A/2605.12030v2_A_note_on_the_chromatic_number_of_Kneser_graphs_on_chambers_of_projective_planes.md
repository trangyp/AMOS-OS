A note on the chromatic number of Kneser graphs on
chambers of projective planes and incidence-free sets
Philipp Heering∗, Klaus Metsch†, Vladislav Taranchuk‡
2026
Abstract
Let D = (P, B) be a symmetric (v, k, λ)-design and let (X, Y ) be an equinumerous
incidence-free pair, with X ⊆P and Y ⊆B. In this note, we give an elementary
proof which shows the existence of a perfect matching between P \ X and B \ Y in
the incidence graph of D. This recovers a result of Spiro, Adriaensen and Mattheus,
who already showed this using different arguments for k ≥36.
We use this to
connect some dots in the literature and prove that finding the chromatic number
of the Kneser graph on chambers of a projective plane is equivalent to finding the
incidence-free number of the incidence graph of the plane.

Introduction
The Kneser graph on flags of a projective space has the flags as vertices, with
two vertices adjacent when the corresponding flags are opposite. Determining its
maximal cocliques is therefore an Erdős-Ko-Rado-type problem. For several choices
of dimension and flag type, the largest cocliques are known, and geometric proofs
often yield stability results. In some cases, these stability results can be leveraged
to determine the chromatic number, see [2, 3, 5].
We write Γq for the graph whose vertices are the chambers (or point-line flags) (P, ℓ)
of a (not necessarily Desarguesian) projective plane of order q. Two vertices (P1, ℓ1)
and (P2, ℓ2) are adjacent if and only if P1 /∈ℓ2 and P2 /∈ℓ1. It was already observed
in [6] that every non-empty, maximal coclique F of Γq is one of the following:
• There is a chamber (P, ℓ) in the plane, such that all chambers of F have ℓas
their line, or P as their point. Such a coclique F has size 2q + 1 and is called
a chamber coclique.

Thus a stability result is available, but determining the chromatic number χ(Γq)
remains non-trivial. It was shown in [6] that
(q + 1)(q + 1 −√q) ≤χ(Γq) ≤q2 + 1,
with equality on the left when a √q-arc exists. Such arcs are known to exist if q is
an even power of two.
The point-line incidence structure of a projective plane is a symmetric (q2 + q +
1, q + 1, 1)-design. We use the language of designs to state the main result of this
note.
Theorem 1.1. Let D = (P, B) be a symmetric (v, k, λ)-design. If (X, Y ) is an
equinumerous incidence-free pair, with X ⊆P and Y ⊆B, then there is a perfect
matching between P \ X and B \ Y in the incidence graph of D.
This generalizes a result due to S.A.M. [Proposition 5.1 in [7]], as we have no con-
dition on k. Using Theorem 1.1 instead of Proposition 5.1 in [7] also gives a gen-
eralization of their main result [Theorem 1.3 in [7]] as we have no condition on
k.
Corollary 1.2. Let D = (P, B) be a symmetric (v, k, λ)-design. Then the edge
domination number of this design is v −α, where α is the maximum size of an
equinumerous incidence-free pair (X, Y ).
Our proof of Theorem 1.1 is quite elementary. Furthermore, we can use it to prove
the following.
Theorem 1.3. Finding a t-coloring of the Kneser graph on chambers of a projec-
tive plane of order q that uses only chamber cocliques is equivalent to finding an
equinumerous, incidence-free set (X, Y ) of points and lines of size q2 + q + 1 −t.
We apply the following lemma from the Thesis of Mussche.
Lemma 1.4 ([6]). For every t-coloring of Γq that contains a triangular coclique,
there exists a coloring with at most t colors that contains no triangular coclique.
This leads to the following corollary.
Corollary 1.5. Finding an optimal coloring of the Kneser graph on chambers of a
projective plane is equivalent to finding a maximum, equinumerous, incidence-free
set (X, Y ) of points and lines in the plane.
The size of |X| in a largest possible, equinumerous, incidence-free set (X, Y ) as in the
theorem above is also called the incidence-free number of the incidence graph of the
projective plane. Inspired by the notation of [4], we denote this number as αq. In [1]
a similar (but more general) number was studied. In fact, the authors of [1] reproved
a result from Haemers, namely √q(q −√q + 1) ≥αq with equality if and only q is

Proof of Theorem 1.1
Consider a bipartite graph with bipartite sets A and B. An A-perfect matching is
a matching of disjoint edges that covers every vertex in A. Assume that W ⊆A
and denote by N(W) the neighborhood of W in B, i.e. the set of all vertices in B
that are adjacent to a vertex in W. If we have |W| ≤|N(W)| for any such W, then
Hall’s marriage theorem implies the existence of an A-perfect matching.
Hall’s marriage theorem implies in particular that any regular bipartite graph has
a perfect matching.
The idea of our proof is as follows: First we fix a bijection Φ between the removed
points X and the removed blocks Y . For every pair (x, Φ(x)) consider their neigh-
bours. In this neighbourhood, we can find a (local) perfect matching. These (local)
perfect matchings can be used to construct a weight function for the edges, such that
the assigned weights give a fractional perfect matching. This implies the existence
of a perfect matching.

Proof of Theorem 1.3
Proof of Theorem 1.3. First, we show how we find an incidence-free, equinumerous
pair (X, Y ) of points and lines, starting from a coloring that uses only chamber
cocliques.
Assume that we have such a t-coloring. Then, this coloring can be described as
follows: There are chambers (P1, ℓ1), . . . , (Pt, ℓt) in the projective plane, such that
for every chamber (P, ℓ) there is an i such that P = Pi or ℓ= ℓi.
Denote by P the set of points in the plane and let X := P \ {P1, . . . , Pt}. Similarly
denote by L the set of lines in the plane and let Y := L \ {ℓ1, . . . , ℓt}. Now (X, Y )
is an equinumerous pair with |X| = |Y | = q2 + q + 1 −t. Assume that (X, Y ) is not
incidence-free. Then there is a point P /∈{P1, . . . , Pt} and a line ℓ/∈{ℓ1, . . . , ℓt},
such that (P, ℓ) is a chamber. Then there is no i, such that P = Pi or ℓ= ℓi, which
is a contradiction.
Now, we show how to find a coloring, starting from an incidence-free, equinumerous
pair (X, Y ) of points and lines.
Consider the incidence graph G of the projective plane that we obtain if we remove
X ∪Y . By Theorem 1.1, we know that we can find a perfect matching of this graph.
Every edge of the perfect matching connects a point to an incident line. So, we can
understand the matching as a list of chambers (P1, ℓ1), . . . , (Pt, ℓt). We claim that
for every chamber (P, ℓ) there exists an i, such that P = Pi or ℓ= ℓi. If there is
no Pi with Pi = P, then we must have P ∈X. But since ℓis incident with P and
(X, Y ) is incidence-free, then ℓ/∈Y . Hence, ℓis a vertex in G, so there is an i with
ℓ= ℓi.
Now, every vertex of Γq is in at least one of the cocliques determined by the chambers
(P1, ℓ1), . . . , (Pt, ℓt). This gives a coloring with t colors.
Finally, we geometrically describe an incidence-free pair (X, Y ) of points and lines
with |X| = |Y | = 7 for q = 5, which is best possible via [4]. Our construction
resembles a grid.
Fix a point P and consider the lines ℓ1, . . . , ℓ6 incident with P. Next, fix a line g
that is not incident with P. Denote by Q the point g ∩ℓ1. Let g1, g2 be distinct
lines that are incident with Q, but not P, such that g ̸= g1, g2. Let g3 be the line
that is incident with g1 ∩ℓ2 and g2 ∩ℓ3. We define Y := {ℓ4, ℓ5, ℓ6, g, g1, g2, g3}.
Let X be the set of points on ℓ1, ℓ2, ℓ3 that are not incident with any of the lines in
Y . The lines ℓ4, ℓ5, ℓ6 intersect ℓ1, ℓ2, ℓ3 only in P. Furthermore, the line g intersects
ℓ1, ℓ2, ℓ3 in three distinct points. So, there are q −1 = 4 points left on the three
lines.
The lines g1, g2 intersect ℓ1 in the same point as g. However, g3 intersects ℓ1 in a
different point. Hence, there are 3 points left on ℓ1.
The lines g1, g2 intersect ℓ1, ℓ2 in points not on g. However, g3 intersects ℓ1, ℓ2 in
g1 ∩ℓ2 and g2 ∩ℓ3, so it removes no additional points. Hence, there are 2 points left
on ℓ2, ℓ3. In total, we have |X| = 3 + 2 + 2 = 7.
