Asymptotic Limits of Entanglement Distribution
Piotr Masajada, Aby Philip, and Alexander Streltsov∗
Institute of Fundamental Technological Research, Polish Academy of Sciences, Pawi´nskiego 5B, 02-106 Warsaw, Poland
Reliable distribution of quantum entanglement over long distances is a central challenge in quantum infor-
mation science, fundamentally limited by decoherence in noisy communication channels. In this work, we
investigate the asymptotic limits of entanglement distribution across quantum networks utilizing intermediate
repeater stations and local operations and classical communication (LOCC). We establish a strict dichotomy:
the asymptotic preservation of entanglement over arbitrarily long distances is possible if and only if the under-
lying quantum channel admits a correctable subspace. For channels lacking such a subspace, we prove that the
transmitted state converges exponentially fast to the set of separable states, rendering standard LOCC filtering
insufficient. To counteract this exponential degradation, we analyze networks employing parallel channel uses
per link. We derive a fundamental lower bound on the physical resource requirements, proving that for channels
without a correctable subspace, the number of parallel channels per link must scale at least logarithmically with
the number of intermediate stations to sustain a non-zero amount of entanglement. This theoretical limit serves
as a stringent benchmark for quantum repeater architectures and underscores the necessity of advanced quantum
error-correcting codes, such as qLDPC codes, which show promise in saturating this optimal resource scaling.
Introduction. Quantum entanglement [1] is a fundamental
resource for a wide array of quantum information processing
tasks, including quantum key distribution [2], quantum tele-
portation [3], and distributed quantum computing [4, 5]. A
central challenge in realizing these technologies is the reliable
distribution of entanglement between distant parties. In re-
alistic scenarios, quantum states must be transmitted through
noisy quantum channels, which inevitably interact with the
environment and induce decoherence. This continuous degra-
dation strictly limits the amount of entanglement that can be
shared and maintained between remote parties.
In the standard entanglement distribution scenario [6–9]
two distant parties, Alice and Bob, have access to a noisy
quantum channel Λ. Alice first prepares a bipartite quantum
state ρ in her laboratory, keeping one subsystem and using the
channel to transmit the other subsystem to Bob. Because the
transmission is imperfect, the channel acts nontrivially on the
particle sent to Bob, while the particle retained by Alice re-
mains unchanged. As a result, the initially prepared state ρ
is transformed into the final bipartite state Λ ⊗11[ρ]. Any en-
tanglement initially present in ρ is generally degraded by the
action of the channel, and the properties of Λ determine how
much entanglement can ultimately be shared between the two
parties.
If the noisy channel Λ is used sequentially, the resulting
state shared by Alice and Bob is given by (Λ ◦· · · ◦Λ) ⊗11[ρ].
This setting is relevant, for example, in long-distance quan-
tum communication through a chain of noisy transmission
segments, where the quantum system effectively undergoes
several consecutive applications of the same channel before
reaching Bob. In general, the detrimental effects of noise ac-
cumulate with the number of channel uses, leading to a pro-
gressive degradation of the shared entanglement [10]. In many
physically relevant situations, this entanglement eventually
disappears altogether in the limit of infinitely many channel
applications [11].
To counteract these effects, parties can employ entangle-
ment distillation [12–15] and filtering protocols [16]. Using
local operations and classical communication (LOCC) [17],
they can extract high-quality entangled states from a larger
ensemble of degraded ones. Additionally, the use of interme-
diate repeater stations can significantly enhance the feasibility
of establishing entanglement over long distances [18].
In this work, we investigate the fundamental and asymp-
totic limits of long-distance entanglement distribution in the
presence of repeater stations. The central question we address
is whether there exists any LOCC-based strategy capable of
preserving a nonzero amount of entanglement in the asymp-
totic limit, that is, over arbitrarily long distances.
In this scenario, after each application of the noisy chan-
nel Λ, the parties—or the stations assisting the transmission—
may apply an LOCC protocol Fi to the shared state before the
next channel use. These operations can depend on previous
measurement outcomes and therefore need not be identical at
every step; see also Fig. 1. Such intermediate processing sta-
tions can be viewed as local checkpoints that attempt to coun-
teract, delay, or partially compensate for the progressive accu-
mulation of noise. In a multipartite transmission architecture
involving many channels operating in parallel, these interme-
diate nodes play the role of quantum repeater stations [18, 19].

Discussion. In this work, we have established fundamental
asymptotic limits for long-distance entanglement distribution
across noisy quantum networks. We demonstrated a strict di-
chotomy dictating the survival of entanglement: the asymp-
totic preservation of entanglement over arbitrarily long dis-
tances is possible if and only if the underlying quantum chan-
nel possesses a correctable subspace. For channels lacking
such a subspace, we proved that the shared state inevitably
converges to the set of fully separable states exponentially fast
with respect to the number of intermediate nodes, regardless
of any intermediate local operations and classical communi-
cation filtering protocols applied.
To overcome this inherent exponential degradation, we ex-
tended our analysis to scenarios employing parallel channel
uses between repeater links.
Our central result establishes
a strict theoretical lower bound on the required physical re-
sources: to successfully distribute a non-zero amount of en-
tanglement across a network of length n using depolarizing
channel, the number of parallel channels per link must scale
at least logarithmically with n. Since many full-rank quantum
channels can be expressed as convex combinations involving
the depolarizing channel, it is natural to expect that related
scaling constraints may extend beyond the purely depolariz-
ing case.
This fundamental lower bound provides a crucial bench-
mark for the evaluation and design of quantum repeater archi-
tectures and quantum error correction protocols. It is known

that by employing quantum error correction codes, the num-
ber of physical resources needed to establish long-distance en-
tanglement scales as polylog(n), where n is the number of re-
peater stations [29, 30]. Specifically, using surface codes, it
is possible to achieve a scaling of O(log2(n)) [31]. It is shown
by using property of surface codes stating that probability of
error decreases exponentially with code distance [32], while
code distance increases as √m, where m is the number of
physical qubits [32]. A similar result has been obtained for
quantum Low-Density Parity-Check (qLDPC) codes: it was
shown in [33] that there exist qLDPC codes for which the er-
ror probability decreases exponentially with the code distance.
More recent results established the existence of asymptoti-
cally good qLDPC codes with linear minimum distance [34],
together with efficient decoding algorithms capable of correct-
ing a constant fraction of errors [35]. Combined with the fun-
damental limits derived in this work, these advances strongly
suggest that the optimal resource overhead for long-distance
entanglement distribution scales as O(log n). Although a rig-
orous proof of the achievability of this scaling is still lack-
ing, establishing such a result remains an important and com-
pelling direction for future research.
