ABSTRACT
The time-consuming formation process critically impacts the longevity of sodium-ion coin cells and
End Of Life (EOL) performance. This study aims to optimize formation protocols for duration effi-
ciency, targeting high-performance outcomes while minimizing the number of experiments to reduce
resource consumption and accelerate discovery. Specifically, we consider two potentially competing
objectives: minimizing formation time and maximizing EOL performance. Beyond this application
focus, we also present a methodological contribution: a framework designed to enable interoperability
between the Fast-INtention Agnostic LEarning Server (FINALES) and Kadi Research Data Manage-
ment (RDM) ecosystems, which we employ to tackle our optimization problem. In this setup, the
FINALES framework orchestrates experiment planning and execution on the Post-Lithium Storage
Cluster of Excellence (POLiS) Materials Acceleration Platform (MAP), while an active-learning agent
implemented within Kadi4Mat guides experiment selection, using multi-objective batched Bayesian

1
Introduction
Accelerating battery development has become increasingly important as demand for cost-efficient, sustainable energy
storage technologies grows. The battery production chain involves several key steps: material preparation, electrode
production, cell manufacturing, and cell conditioning [1]. High costs and extended cell conditioning times significantly
slow innovation and consume substantial resources. Notably, the longest steps that occur during the conditioning phase
are formation and aging process, which can last up to 24 hours [2] and three weeks [3, 4] respectively. Formation
is considered one of the key steps determining battery quality, whereas during aging, quality and safety parameters
are monitored. The associated costs of the formation and aging processes account for approximately 14 % [5, 6, 7]
to 30 % [1, 8] of the total cell production expenses. Part of these costs is the necessary equipment, which accounts
for a significant amount of floor space, up to 25 % of the entire production facility [4]. Formation procedures are
highly material-dependent and rarely directly transferable. Furthermore, scaling from small lab cells (e.g., coin cells) to
large industrial formats is challenging due to the emergence of new behaviors [9]. For these reasons, the formation
process, as a critical bottleneck in battery manufacturing, is a closely guarded secret and has received particular attention
in optimization research. Promising current approaches for optimization and accelerated discovery aim to combine
automated workflows and experiments of Materials Acceleration Platforms (MAPs) [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15] guided
by machine learning models [16, 17, 18]. MAPs with focus on optimizing material properties or reaction pathways
only rarely account for processes at the device level, such as formation. The Fast-INtention Agnostic LEarning Server
(FINALES) framework combines both and was demonstrated to identify an optimized electrolyte formulation regarding
conductivity and End Of Life (EOL)[11, 10].
The EOL is a cell quality indicator for the maximum application duration commonly described by the number of cycles,
after which the capacity of the cell falls below 70 % – 80 % of its initial capacity [19, 20, 21, 22]. While high-throughput
experimentation enabled by automation implicitly assumes that all relevant process steps can be automated. In practice,
however, certain process steps remain difficult or undesirable to automate [23, 24], for example, due to safety constraints
[25]. Recent publications are targeting the formation and its acceleration using machine learning models and data-driven
approaches, mostly for known lithium-ion-based systems [26, 27], leaving upcoming chemistries such as sodium-ion
batteries unexplored.
Given the constraints on battery development, data-driven materials process design, integrated with human expertise,
offers a promising approach for efficient parameter optimization. Machine learning methods are increasingly used to
support such optimization tasks, particularly in settings where data acquisition is cost-intensive. Active Learning is a
subset of machine learning that focuses on sequentially selecting the most informative experiment configuration, aiming
to improve sample efficiency while reducing overall experimental cost. As an active-learning query strategy, Bayesian
optimization provides a model-driven, uncertainty-aware approach for identifying promising regions of a design space
using relatively few evaluations compared with traditional methods such as grid or random search. This makes it
a powerful tool for navigating complex, high-dimensional material landscapes. Previous work [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]
demonstrated that such an approach significantly outperforms classic search methods across diverse materials systems,
achieving superior results with fewer experiments. Specifically to battery research, [33] developed a machine learning
framework to optimize the synthesis of Lithium Aluminum Titanium Phosphate, and [34] efficiently identified optimal
model parameters for simulating the aging of nickel-gadolinium-doped ceria anodes. Interactive human-in-the-loop
workflows extend this paradigm by allowing domain experts to interpret and execute suggested parameter configurations
as physical experiments, feed the resulting measurements back into the active-learning cycle, and, when necessary,
impose domain knowledge or feasibility and steer the learning process. In this work, we build on these concepts
to develop a model that iteratively learns from cycling experiments on sodium-ion coin cells to balance formation
efficiency and long-term degradation. Specifically, we formulate an optimization problem with two potentially
competing objectives: minimizing formation time and maximizing EOL performance.

In parallel with the optimization objective, this work addresses a methodological challenge in distributed experimental
environments related to Research Data Management (RDM). Modern battery research increasingly relies on heteroge-
neous RDM systems to store, process, and exchange experimental data. However, limited interoperability between such
systems often hinders efficient data reuse, coordination, and automation across platforms. To address this limitation,
we present a framework that enables interoperability between two complementary RDM ecosystems: Kadi and the
FINALES-based Post-Lithium Storage Cluster of Excellence (POLiS) MAP. The Kadi ecosystem consists of multiple
instances, among which Kadi4Mat serves as the virtual research environment that manages data, metadata, and workflow
automation. It combines repository functionality for data management with electronic lab notebook capabilities and a
plugin system, all of which enable the documentation and automation of research processes. The FINALES framework
orchestrates experiment planning and execution on the POLiS MAP via so-called tenants, which combine automated
capabilities with tasks performed by human researchers, and implement the MAP concept for process optimization at
the device level. To bridge these systems, we establish interoperable links via dedicated plugins and tenants (see Fig. 1).
The resulting interactive optimization framework enables machine-learning-guided studies that deploy automated
laboratory experimentation coupled with human-in-the-loop process steps.
2
Results and Discussion
The main results of this work consist of two complementary components: methodological developments and application-
oriented findings. The first component, presented in Section 2.1, introduces our interactive optimization framework. We
consider this framework a result in itself, as it demonstrates, through the Kadi-FINALES coupling, how interoperability
among RDM systems can enhance data exchange, promote reuse and reproducibility, improve operational efficiency,
and ultimately accelerate research processes. The second component, presented in Section 2.2, reports the outcomes
of the optimization study conducted with this framework, and the identified trade-off between formation time and
end-of-life performance.
2.1
Methodological results: the human-in-the-loop optimization framework
In this section, we present the workflow implemented for the active-learning agent and laboratory operations, and
describe the data flow between these processes, from the operating user to the MAP and back. The developed framework
combines complementary functionalities of Kadi4Mat and FINALES, enabling synchronized operations that support
automated, human-in-the-loop optimization.
Figure 1 shows the workflow sequence at a conceptual level. The data flow begins within the Kadi ecosystem, where
parameter optimization and FAIR data management are performed. In Kadi4Mat, the user defines the optimization
problem via a user-friendly template, which generates a central (“umbrella”) record linking all study components. The
KadiAIgent plugin responds to umbrella record activation and interfaces with Computational Intelligence and Data
Science framework (CIDS), enabling our active-learning Bayesian optimization framework. The optimizer follows a
goal-aware, information-driven learning strategy, iteratively selecting experiments that are the most informative with
respect to the target objectives defined for the study. Using a multiphase generation strategy, the KadiAIgent identifies
the most informative parameter configurations within the defined search space for the next batch of experiments. The
resulting information is then submitted to the FINALES server via the FINALES plugin, which translates it from the
Kadi representation into a FINALES-compatible request, thereby configuring the corresponding experiments. The Kadi
ecosystem now waits for FINALES to deliver the experimental results so Kadi can score and complete the current batch
of trials. Further details on the active-learning method and the Kadi components of the framework are provided in
Section 4.1.
In the second part of the loop, FINALES receives the workflow request from the FINALES plugin, and passes it to
the workflow management tenant OVERLooking ORchestrating Tenant (OVERLORT). The OVERLORT coordinates
the execution of the workflow by orchestrating the individual services within the automated laboratory. This includes
fully automated tasks, such as cell assembly, as well as manually performed tasks, such as formulating and transporting
electrolyte or cells to the next device. The full workflow is described in Section 4.3. A higher degree of automation
is possible, but the executing scientists provide quality control and monitoring. Once all results are available, the
OVERLORT posts a request to upload data, which is handled by the Kadi tenant. The results are automatically uploaded
to Kadi4Mat in a predefined data structure and incorporated into the dataset to improve subsequent steps in the search.
Further details on the FINALES components of the framework are provided in Section 4.2.
Since data in both FINALES and Kadi4Mat are represented as JSON structures, they are inherently human- and machine-
readable. Augmented by the Kadi4Mat web interface, the data representation becomes more intuitive for human users,
thereby enhancing the user-friendliness of the data generated in the POLiS MAP. Moreover, the connection to Kadi4Mat
not only provides tools for data visualization but also enables researchers to add observations that automated procedures

2.2
Application results: multi-objective optimization of Sodium ion coin-cell formation time and cycle life
The multi-objective optimization study was designed to identify formation protocols that simultaneously maximize cell
lifetime while minimizing formation time. The search space is defined by three parameters: the charge and discharge
rates during formation, and the number of formation cycle repetitions. The considered ranges for each parameter are
shown in Table 1. Two competing objectives were considered: maximizing the EOL cycle number and minimizing the
total formation time. To improve efficiency, the optimization was performed in a batch setting. Up to three batches
were evaluated in parallel at each iteration of the optimization loop. Furthermore, to reduce errors and minimize the
impact of potential outliers, each batch comprised four cells with identical configuration settings. The reported metrics
correspond to the mean values obtained across these four cells. Details on the experimental setups can be found in
Section 4.3.

3
Conclusion
The presented active-learning optimization framework integrates the functionalities of Kadi4Mat and FINALES. Both
systems were jointly extended to enable efficient data exchange and closed-loop interaction between experimental
control, data management, and decision-making. The framework’s utility and efficiency are showcased through this
study, where we employed it to optimize formation time and end-of-life performance of sodium-ion coin cells. Shorter
formation protocols, although reducing experimental time, may alter interphase formation and consequently affect
long-term performance. Therefore, the objectives of this study do not scale proportionally and require joint optimization
to identify a solution that balances formation efficiency and cell longevity. Our multi-objective optimization framework
successfully handled this tension by identifying a Pareto front. This identifies the set of solutions in which formation
time cannot be reduced further without decreasing EOL cycle life, and EOL cycle life cannot be increased further
without increasing formation time. Our work showcases a unified learning framework in which human expertise and

Methods
4.1
The KadiAIgent active-learning framework in the Kadi ecosystem
The proposed active-learning framework operates within the Kadi ecosystem, which is built around Kadi instances that
host the core repository and web frontend [37]. Through its modular components and plugin architecture, the Kadi
ecosystem enables users to create data records and to initiate events and automated workflows [38]. The constituent
modules of the Kadi ecosystem communicate through well-defined interfaces, enabling seamless coordination of events
and information flow. This design explicitly supports scientists and laboratory technicians as the primary operators. For
instance, advanced visualization and dashboard generation features make it possible to follow the optimization process
in real time. A further support for researchers’ daily tasks within the Kadi ecosystem is KadiStudio [38], a standalone
desktop application that enables visual design and execution of scientific workflows through an interactive, graphical
editor.
To start the optimization process, the operating user accesses the Kadi instance through the web interface, where data
and associated metadata are organized as records (basic structured data entry of Kadi4Mat). By default, records require
only a minimal set of generic metadata (e.g., title and identifier), which is automatically enriched with system-generated
information such as timestamps of the most recent changes. Through templates, the metadata structure of records,
including keys (labels, names), identifiers, validation, and pre-filled values, can be predefined for different applications
and research domains. The active-learning tool provides an umbrella template for arbitrary optimization and design
studies. From that template, users can create umbrella records whose metadata fully define their own design study,
including design parameters, optimization objectives (or metrics), constraints on the output, and optimization settings.
The latter supports a multiphase generation strategy in which each phase is user-configurable, including the number of
initial random (or Sobol) trials and the selection of the kernel and acquisition function for the surrogate model. A single
optimized parameter rarely captures the full structural or functional diversity of a material. Therefore, multi-objective
optimization is often necessary to balance competing or interdependent properties and identify robust design solutions.
In the present study, we address these limitations by extending our active-learning framework to experimental data,
enabling both multi-objective and constrained optimization. Specifically, we developed a data-driven active-learning
model that guides parameter tuning to minimize formation duration while maximizing the EOL of coin cells.
Kadi instances provide a plugin system that uses hooks to listen for events and trigger actions. The framework presented
here uses two plugins: the KadiAIgent active-learning plugin for sequential decision-making within the optimization
loop, and the FINALES plugin for communication with FINALES. Both plugins automatically manage access and
permissions for the involved records, ensuring that only authorized users can trigger events.
KadiAIgent is an advanced active-learning agent that integrates the Ax adaptive experimentation platform [39] with
CIDS [40] as part of KadiAI, the Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence interface of Kadi4Mat. CIDS provides
modular, flexible AI infrastructure specifically designed to handle diverse scientific applications in engineering, materials
science, and the natural sciences. The KadiAIgent active-learning plugin integrates Bayesian optimization into the Kadi
ecosystem to guide the adaptive exploration of design parameter spaces, while automating the experimental optimization
process. Based on an event marking the start of an optimization loop iteration, the active-learning plugin creates
trial records for the optimization problem defined in the umbrella record. Specifically, the triggering event is either
adding the !kadiaigent-al-umbrella-active tag to an umbrella record, indicating that the study configuration is
complete, or adding the !kadiaigent-al-trial-completed tag to a trial record associated with an active umbrella
record, indicating successful trial completion. Both these events trigger a new iteration, creating a new trial record
that encodes the metadata for a specific configuration of each design parameter. The new trial is then automatically
linked to the umbrella record via a link labeled !kadiaigent-al-trial-for. The decision on which configuration
to try next is made using a multi-objective Bayesian optimization method. Bayesian optimization builds a probabilistic
surrogate model of the experiment’s objective function, allowing it to predict promising parameter settings together
with their uncertainties. Initial data points are required for the model to start constructing a probabilistic map of the
search space. In the absence of such data, the model lacks knowledge of the objective functions, leading to uniformly
high uncertainty across the entire domain. For the present study, instead of using Sobol-sampled initial points, we
leveraged 11 previously available data points from a previous study. The selected samples align with current practice for

The FINALES plugin in Kadi provides the interface that allows the KadiAIgent active-learning plugin to communicate

the extraction of the trial configuration as key-value pairs from the trial record. By tracing the knowledge graph, i.e.,
following the links from the trial to the umbrella and on to the base workflow record, the FINALES plugin extracts
the blueprint for the experimental workflow to be executed through FINALES. After filling in the key-value pairs,
a FINALES workflow record is created to store the experimental workflow instructions for FINALES as metadata.
Finally, the workflow is submitted to the FINALES server via the HTTP-based REST API, and the workflow record
receives the state tag !finales-request-running.
4.2
Experimental workflow
Upon successful submission via the HTTP-based REST API, the workflow is received and processed by the FINALES
system, which orchestrates the experiment’s execution. To perform closed-loop optimization, we adapted the previously
reported FINALES system [10, 11] and implemented a tenant that automatically uploads the generated data to the
Kadi4Mat platform upon completion. Within FINALES, individual tenants (i.e., software or hardware clients providing
specific capabilities to the system) were registered with the following functionality: Autonomous Battery Assembly
System (AutoBASS) tenant (assembling coin cells), Cycler tenant (battery cycling), Transportation tenant (transportation
of physical samples), OVERLORT (workflow management), KadiAIgent (active-learning optimizer), and Kadi Tenant
(upload results to Kadi4Mat in a structured way).
Each iteration in the laboratory starts with a workflow execution request, posted to FINALES via the FINALES plugin
in Kadi, which directs the assembly and cycling of coin cells according to a specified C-rate for formation charge and
discharge, as well as a defined number of formation cycle repetitions. Figure 5 shows the sequence of events performed
at the CELEST Green Energy Lab Ulm.
After pulling the workflow request from FINALES, the OVERLORT posts the first sub-request and waits for the
corresponding result before posting the next one. To do so, the OVERLORT keeps track of active workflows and their
respective most recent requests in an internal queue, which is regularly updated as shown in Fig. 6. As a first step in
the workflow, the OVERLORT requests the reservation of cycling channels and the required number of channels is
flagged as reserved at the battery cycler. Subsequently, the electrolyte formulation is requested by the OVERLORT.
The electrolyte was manually formulated by a human researcher, who also entered the corresponding batch data into a
JSON structure posted to FINALES. Likewise, the electrolyte was manually transferred to the AutoBASS and loaded
into a vial. The automatic cell assembly used the provided electrolyte and cell components, including the disk-shaped
electrodes and separator. In the next step, the cells were transported to their reserved channels at the battery cycler
outside the glovebox. After completing the manual transfer, a human researcher confirmed the transport using the
Transportation tenant. This confirmation closed the transport request, and the OVERLORT submitted a request to cycle
the cells, which prompted the cycler tenant to create the test protocol and start the tests at the respective channels. Upon
finishing the test, the automatic export of the data, after which the OVERLORT posts the final result related to the
workflow request, requests the upload of the data to Kadi4Mat, and removes the workflow from its internal queue. On
Kadi4Mat, the optimizer accesses the data, processes it, and based on the newly acquired knowledge, sends the next
workflow request.
To avoid an excessive number of experiments and to enable our information-driven strategy to make the most of each
new data point, only three batches of cells were allowed to run in parallel. Each batch comprised four cells assembled
with the same materials and parameters.

4.4
Closing the loop with the human researcher
In Kadi, the FINALES plugin finds the result records uploaded by the FINALES Kadi tenant. Two approaches are
possible: On the one hand, a metadatum in the workflow record stores the request UUID returned by the FINALES
instance, which can be used to search for uploaded result records that also contain this UUID, even across different
Kadi instances. On the other hand, the FINALES plugin can detect record links added to the workflow record that
includes the request, thereby identifying results belonging to that workflow record. In the present study, we used the
first option because development was performed on a dedicated test Kadi instance, whereas FINALES communicated
results from a production Kadi instance.
Based on the result records, the FINALES plugin extracts and evaluates cycling data and parameters. A Python script
analyzes the cycling curves for each assembled cell per batch by extracting characteristic points and computing the mean
and SEM for both objectives. In addition, because design parameters may deviate from the original workflow request
due to intrinsic device uncertainty, errors, or human overrides based on intuition and experience, the data points are
updated with the actual measured design parameters. Finally, the FINALES plugin saves the updated design parameters
and objective metrics in the workflow and trial records. It changes their state tags to !finales-request-completed
and !kadiaigent-al-trial-completed, respectively.
The optimization loop can be closed either manually or by the KadiAIgent active-learning plugin, which detects new
completed trials. Based on that event trigger, the active-learning plugin accumulates data from all completed trials
linked to the umbrella. The measured parameter configurations and corresponding objective metrics and variances are
used to fit a Gaussian process model for Bayesian inference (Section 4.6). The inferred most-informative parameter
values serve as trial configurations for the subsequent loop iterations of the design study.
To visualize the progress of the optimization study, umbrella records can be enhanced with dashboards (Fig. 7). The
dashboards provide interactive plots that visualize trade-offs and the optimization process’s progress, along with contour
plots of the objectives. Each Bayesian inference run updates the interactive plots. Internally, the dashboards are
implemented in Python through the dash and plotly libraries, which export interactive plots as JSON objects. These
JSON objects can be embedded directly in the Kadi instance’s web interface under the corresponding record.
4.5
Data structure and knowledge graph
In FINALES, all services, whether physical setups in a laboratory or computations on a server or a computing cluster,
are registered as so-called tenants. Upon registration, each tenant obtains a unique identifier (UUID), referred to as the
tenant-UUID, from FINALES, which uniquely identifies the tenant. Prior to registering a tenant, the tenant developer
and the FINALES administrator define the input and output data structures for the tenant. Requests and results posted to
FINALES are validated against these data structures and rejected if they are not compliant. Each tenant provides one or
more capabilities to the MAP by querying FINALES and posting requests or results depending on its task. A capability
in this context is defined as a combination of a certain quantity and a method that yields a value for this quantity. In the
scope of FINALES, a quantity is not limited to a measurable, physical quantity but can also be defined as a label for the
output of a service (e.g., transportation).

All requests and results that are posted to FINALES are stored in an SQL database. FINALES assigns a unique identifier
(UUID) to each request and result to enable traceability and link results to their corresponding requests. It also adds
a timestamp to the request to document the time when it was received by FINALES. Finally, FINALES assigns the
status pending to the request. This is crucial for tenants to pick up tasks, as they usually query FINALES regularly for
pending requests that align with their capabilities. Apart from this metadata that is automatically added by FINALES, a
request object contains the parameters that specify the requested result. This means it includes all relevant information
about the parameters or inputs needed to execute the method that generates the requested data, and, if applicable, also a
specification of the sample under investigation.
A tenant that picked up the request performs its tasks using these inputs and generates a result, or it processes the results
available through FINALES and posts a new request. The results posted to FINALES also obey to a structure similar to
the requests. The body of the result contains the data obtained by the tenant in the output format that was defined by the
FINALES administrator and the tenant developer. Analogous to the requests, metadata such as a UUID, a timestamp,
and a status are automatically assigned by FINALES once the result gets posted. An important difference between
requests and results is that the latter include the UUID of the request they reply to, establishing a link. Moreover,
the result not only reports the parameters actually used when performing its tasks but also the parameters and inputs
provided in the request. This allows for the comparison of targeted and actual parameter values, which may, for example,
be used to identify severe discrepancies.
In this study, seven tenants with varying capabilities interact with FINALES. These tenants are:
• KadiAIgent – The tenant related to the agent processing all the data available via FINALES to generate new
requests.
• OVERLORT – The workflow manager used in the self-driving laboratory to ensure the correct sequence of
events.
• Cycler – The tenant connected to the automated battery cycler.
• Electrolyte – The tenant responsible for the human researcher who prepared the electrolyte solutions.
• Transportation – The tenant coordinating the transport of physical components in the laboratory.
• AutoBASS – The tenant connected to the Automated Battery Assembly System (AutoBASS).
• Kadi4Mat – The tenant used for the automated upload of the data to the Kadi4Mat data management platform.
Each tenant has its own data structures for inputs and outputs, since the applicable structures depend on each tenant’s
capabilities. FINALES provides an endpoint for querying data structures registered for a specific capability. Based on
the definitions of data structures in JSON schemas, both human users and programs interfacing with FINALES can read
and process them.
Within the SQL database of FINALES, links between requests, results, tenants, and other objects are traceable using the
UUIDs assigned to each element in the database. Although this enables traceability, the lack of visualization makes it
challenging for a human researcher to quickly grasp the relations and get an overview. Also, it was decided not to save
data files in a binary format in the SQL database, even though this is technically possible.
Kadi4Mat enables human researchers to get an overview more quickly. Therefore, the Kadi4Mat tenant not only uploads
data for storage but also defines a structured hierarchy of collections, records, and links. In this study, the structure
created in Kadi4Mat is designed around a campaign and a study. The campaign used in this study is called Auto-POLiS.
A campaign can relate to a high-level structure, such as a research project or a research group. Before starting a new
campaign, the administrator can create a collection and a record for it. A campaign can include several studies that are
closely linked to it, e.g., through funding. A study is also represented by a collection and a record in Kadi4Mat. The
study presented here is labeled Human-Lab-AI, and the corresponding record and collection in Kadi4Mat are shown at
the center of Fig. 9. A reduced view of the knowledge graph, focusing on the records related to the KadiAIgent is shown

interface. This umbrella record is linked to each and every individual trial records, which contain all data and metadata
for specific branch cells. In turn, each trial record is linked to a FINALES request record, used to communicate with
the FINALES instance and trigger a FINALES workflow. The user can control the request record to start, stop, and
evaluate the workflow using tags. Collections enable the grouping of records, helping human researchers more easily
find data related to a study. With the additional use of a record, it is possible to create relations in the form of links
between the records related to a study.

in Kadi4Mat and linked to their corresponding workflow record. This includes results for the reservation of cycling

(e.g., if one objective is noisy while another is smooth), we chose a single-task, independent-GP framework, with
each GP modeling only one objective. These m independent surrogate models are subsequently combined into the
acquisition function, thereby integrating information from all objectives.
