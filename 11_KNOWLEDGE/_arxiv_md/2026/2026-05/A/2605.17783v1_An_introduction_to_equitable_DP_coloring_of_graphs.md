AN INTRODUCTION TO EQUITABLE DP COLORING OF GRAPHS
H. A. KIERSTEAD, ALEXANDR KOSTOCHKA, AND ZIMU XIANG
Abstract. A proper k-coloring of vertices of an n-vertex graph is equitable if the size of
every color class is ⌊n/k⌋or ⌈n/k⌉. An extension of it to list coloring requires only that
the size of every color class is at most ⌈n/k⌉. Such colorings have interesting applications
and have been actively studied recently. In this paper, we extend the notion of equitable
coloring to the more general notion of equitable DP coloring and study properties of the
new parameter.
1. Introduction
Let G = (V, E) be a graph.
We let |G| = |V | and ∥G∥= |E|.
The neighborhood,
N(x) = NG(x), of a vertex x in G is the set of neighbors of x in G, the degree of x in G
is d(x) = dG(x) := |NG(x)|. Further, ∆(G) is the maximum degree of G, and δ(G) is the
minimum degree of G. For a positive integer n, set [n] := {1, . . . , n}.
For d ≥1, an ordering σ = (v1, . . . , vn) of the vertices of an n-vertex graph G is d-
degenerate, if for each 1 ≤i ≤n, |N(vi) ∩{v1, . . . , vi−1}| ≤d. We call a graph d-degenerate
if it has a d-degenerate ordering.
The goals of this paper are (a) to introduce the notions of equitable DP-coloring and
strongly equitable DP-coloring, (b) to show examples when such colorings behave differently
from equitable list coloring and strongly equitable list coloring, (c) to prove the basic results
on such colorings, and (d) to state questions/problems on such colorings that seem interesting
to us. We start from definitions and basic results on various versions of equitable coloring.
1.1. Ordinary and equitable colorability. A (proper) k-coloring of a graph G = (V, E)
is a mapping f : V →[k] such that for each i ∈[k] the color class f −1(i) is an independent
set in G.
An equitable k-coloring of an n-vertex graph G = (V, E) is a k-coloring f of G such that
each color class f −1(i) has ⌊n/k⌋or ⌈n/k⌉vertices.
Equitable coloring and similar notions for graphs have applications in construction of
timetables, mutual exclusion scheduling problem, and round-a-clock scheduling; see, e.g. [3,
10, 11, 17, 18, 24, 25]. This concept is also useful in studying extremal combinatorial and
probabilistic problems, see e.g. [1,2,12,22,23].
One of the central results on equitable coloring of graphs is the Hajnal–Szemerédi Theorem
below.

1.2.
List, equitable list, and strongly equitable list colorability. Let Γ be a set of
colors and G = (V, E) be a graph. A list assignment for G (from Γ) is a function L : V →2Γ.
An L-coloring f is a proper coloring of G such that f(v) ∈L(v) for all v ∈V . If L(v) = Γ for
all v ∈V then L is plain, and all ordinary colorings of G with colors from Γ are L-colorings.
If |L(v)| = k for each v ∈V (G), then L is a k-list assignment.
Kostochka, Pelsmajer and West [16] proposed the following list version of equitable col-
orability. A coloring f of G is m-bounded if |f−1(α)| ≤m for all α ∈Γ. Given G and k
write m := m(G, k) := ⌈|G|
k ⌉for the size of a largest color class in an equitable k-coloring
of G, and call such a color class full. Given a k-list assignment L for G, Kostochka et al.
defined G to be equitably L-colorable if it has an m-bounded L-coloring, and they defined G
to be equitably list k-colorable (EL k-colorable) if it is equitably L-colorable for every k-list
assignment L.
In contrast to ordinary equitable coloring, in the list setting it is possible that the sizes of
two color classes must differ significantly, as it could be that some color appears only in the
lists of few vertices. On the other hand, the theory of equitable list colorability reflects the
theory of equitable colorability. This justifies the similar terminology. The following analog
of Theorem 1 was conjectured in [16].
Conjecture 1 (Kostochka, Pelsmajer and West, Conjecture 1.1 [16], 2003). Every graph G
is EL k-colorable for each k > ∆(G).
Partial cases of the conjecture were proved in [13,16,20,21,26–28], but the conjecture is
still wide open.
An anomaly of the definition of EL colorability is that for a plain k-list L, an L-coloring
of a graph G may be m-bounded, but not equitable. For example, if L is a plain 3-list
assignment then an ordinary 2-coloring of C4 is not an equitable coloring (one color class

be represented as a DP-problem by letting H be plain and normal.
Many results on list coloring remain true for DP coloring. For example, every planar graph
is DP 5-colorable, every d-degenerate graph is DP (d+1)-colorable, and a version of Brooks’
Theorem holds for DP coloring.
A graph G is DP degree-colorable if G has an H-coloring whenever H is a cover of G with
|H(u)| ≥dG(u) for all u ∈V (G). A GDP-forest is a graph in which every block is either a
complete graph or a cycle. A GDP-tree is a connected GDP-forest.
A classical theorem of Gallai [8] on degree-colorable graphs extends to DP coloring as
follows.
Theorem 4 (Dvořák and Postle [7]). Suppose that G is a connected graph. Then G is not
DP degree-colorable if and only if G is a GDP-tree.
As we already have the definitions of equitable and strongly equitable list colorability, it
is natural to define equitable and strongly equitable DP colorability analogously. Given a
k-cover H of G, call G equitably H-colorable if G has an m-bounded H-coloring, and call G
equitably DP k-colorable (EDP k-colorable) if it is equitably H-colorable for all k-covers H of
G. Call G strongly equitably H-colorable (SE H-colorable) if there is a strongly m-bounded
H-coloring of G, and call G strongly equitably DP k-colorable (SEDP k-colorable) if G is SE
H-colorable for every k-cover graph H of G.
1.4. Terminology. One issue remains. What should we call graph colorings that witnesses
that a graph is equitably L-colorable, strongly equitably H-colorable, etc? Kostochka et
al. used the term m-bounded L-coloring. But they also informally used the undefined term
equitable L-coloring.
Since then the latter formulation has been used by many authors
including us. It is not really correct; grammatically, it should refer to an L-coloring that
is equitable in the ordinary sense. This issue becomes more noticable as we explore new
versions of equitable colorability.
Here we will revert to m-bounded L-coloring and use
strongly m-bounded H-coloring, etc.
1.5. Organization. While EDP coloring is similar to EL coloring, the situation with bounds
is very different. For example, K2 is not EDP 2-colorable, a graph on n vertices may not
be EDP n-colorable, and so on. In this paper we present some introductory results on EDP
and SEDP coloring.
In the next section, we present a number of examples of graphs G such that are SEL
k-colorable, but not SEDP k-colorable. In Section 3 we prove that if G is not SEDP (n+d)-
colorable then |G| > n or G is not d-degenerate, and that there are infinitely many d-
degenerate graphs on n vertices that are not EDP (n + d −1)-colorable. In Section 4 we
consider SEDP colorability of forests with given maximum degree. In Section 5 we show
that if G is not EDP k-colorable then k < 3∆2(G) −2. We think that this bound is far from
optimal. In Section 6 we conclude with some comments and questions.

6. Concluding remarks
1. It seems that EDP coloring and SEDP coloring are natural extensions of EL coloring
and SEL coloring with interesting properties.
2. Let g(D) (respectively, gse(D)) be the minimum k0 such that for every k ≥k0 every
graph with maximum degree at most D is EDP (respectively, SEDP) k-colorable. It would
be interesting to determine whether g(D) and/or gse(D) are O(D). Our Theorem 13 gives
only a quadratic upper bound on gse(D). We even do not know g(3). Examples 4 and 5
show that g(3) ≥7 and Theorem 13 shows that g(3) ≤27.
3. It looks that the differences between the bounds for EDP coloring and EL coloring
are more significant when the graphs are dense. In particular, we think that every n-vertex
graph with maximum degree less than n/2 is EDP n-colorable, but cannot prove it.

4. As observed by Dvořak and Postle [7, Observation 3], in DP k-coloring it is enough to
consider plain k-covers. In all cases that we have come across, if a graph G with a k-cover H
is not SE H-colorable, then also there is a plain k-cover H′ of G such that G is not SE H′-
colorable. It would be interesting to determine whether this always holds for SEDP colorings.
This is not the case for SEL coloring, see [15, Example 22] (Also arXiv:2504.14711).
