Abstract
We investigate the cosmological implications of generalized mass-to-horizon entropy, a two-parameter extension
of the standard Bekenstein entropy based on the mass-to-horizon relation. Assuming the entropy balance relation,
we derive the change in the generalized mass-to-horizon entropy, which entirely accounts for the heat exchange
across the horizon as measured by an observer near the apparent horizon. We have then derived the Friedmann
equation, using the Clausius relation, and also using modified law of thermodynamics.
The thermodynamic
consistency of the entropy, is examined through entropy evolution and entropy maximization conditions, where
the generalized entropy and its higher-order derivatives indicate that the universe evolves toward a stable maximum
entropy configuration consistent with the generalized second law of thermodynamics. In addition, fluctuations in
horizon energy are investigated to probe the thermal stability and thermodynamic behavior of the cosmic horizon.
The fluctuation analysis reveals finite and physically stable behavior throughout cosmic evolution, supporting the
thermodynamic viability of the proposed model. The present work therefore establishes the generalized mass-to-
horizon entropy as a viable thermodynamic framework for describing modified cosmological dynamics and also
the accelerated expansion of the universe as well.

Introduction
Current research in gravity has shown that horizons are thermodynamic systems [1]. As a result, they can have
both entropy and temperature[2]. In a seminal paper by Bekenstein, it was proved that the entropy of the horizon
is proportional to their surface area[3].
Meantime Hawking has shown that the temperature of the horizon is
proportional to their surface gravity[4, 5].
Using these ideas, Jacobson[6] have derived the Einstein equation of
gravity by projecting the thermodynamic Clausius relation to a local Rindler horizon by assuming the Bekenstein
relation for the entropy and Hawking’s relation for the temperature of the horizon.
This, in turn, implies that
the underlying theory of gravity is Einstein’s gravity, provided one assumes the Bekenstein relation for the horizon
entropy. But the problem with the Bekenstein relation for horizon entropy is that it is proportional to the area,
which is contrary to the conventional idea in thermodynamics, where entropy is proportional to the volume of
the system[7]. Nevertheless, there exist several different approaches defining the horizon entropy, which arise by
neglecting the standard Bekenstein considerations.

Despite the considerable progress in entropy-based cosmological models, most proposed horizon entropy formu-
lations remain predominantly geometric in nature, since they are generally constructed as functions of the horizon
area[3, 5]. Consequently, such approaches do not explicitly incorporate the energy or information content enclosed
within the horizon, and the resulting dark energy is essentially geometric rather than arising from the vacuum energy
or quantum effects contained within the horizon volume[6, 17]. This may partly explain why such models are not fully
successful in addressing the cosmological constant problem. Interestingly, consistency between Hawking temperature
and Clausius relation requires the introduction of a generalized mass-to-horizon relation[20, 21]. In this framework,
the horizon entropy becomes directly connected to the enclosed energy content, thereby providing a more physically
meaningful thermodynamic description of dark energy and gravitational thermodynamics in cosmology.
In the literature, several studies have explored cosmological models based on generalized mass-to-horizon entropy

Overview of Generalized Mass-to-Horizon entropy
The standard Bekenstein entropy provides a purely geometric characterization of the horizon entropy, since it is
proportional to only the area of the horizon. However, the clausius relation implicitly involves an exchange of energy
across the boundary, which consequently affects the content of the system enclosed by the horizon. This motivates
the construction of an entropy that incorporates the physical content within the horizon. As we mentioned in the
Introduction, the mass associated with the horizon can be interpreted as a measure of the total energy enclosed
within it. Although the detailed internal structure of the system cannot be uniquely determined, the mass encodes
contributions of all energy present within it. Therefore, constructing a generalized entropy relation based on the mass-
horizon connection provides an extension of the standard geometric approach to define the horizon entropy[17, 23].

3
Thermodynamic approaches to the Friedmann Equations in Cosmol-
ogy
The dynamical evolution of the Universe is conventionally described by the Friedmann equations, which were origi-
nally obtained from Einstein’s field equations under the assumptions of homogeneity and isotropy of the Universe, de-
scribed by the Friedmann–Robertson–Walker (FRW) metric[25, 26]. However, developments in gravitational physics
have revealed a profound connection between gravity and thermodynamics. This connection suggests that the dy-
namical equations governing the evolution of the Universe can be derived from the thermodynamics of the horizon
by assuming appropriate relations for the horizon temperature and entropy.
In this section, we derive the Friedmann equation using two distinct thermodynamic approaches, namely the
Clausius relation and the first law of thermodynamics, by applying them to the apparent horizon of the expanding
universe. It should be noted that the exact form of the Friedmann equations depends strongly on the form of the
entropy relation one assumes for the horizon. In the current context, it is the generalized mass-to-horizon entropy.
In the literature, the Friedmann equations corresponding to this entropy are derived in [6] from both the Clausius
relation and the modified first law of thermodynamics. In such derivations, the total change in entropy was taken
to be proportional to δQ, the heat exchanged across the horizon, weighted by the horizon temperature. That is true
only if the change in entropy is fully due to the change in the area of the horizon[27]. However, in more general forms
of entropy, which have additional dependencies on the curvature or other equivalent quantities, the total change in
the entropy has a thermal content proportional to δQ and a non-thermal part, which arises due to the variation
of additional curvature dependence of the horizon entropy. In contrast to earlier works in the literature, we have
extracted the thermal part from the total variation of the generalized mass-to-horizon entropy, which is then used to
obtain the Friedmann equations from the Clausius relation and the modified first law of thermodynamics.

6
Conclusion
The recently proposed generalized mass-to-horizon entropy relation provides a unique connection between the total
gravitating mass enclosed within a cosmic horizon and the entropy associated with that horizon. This construction
establishes a direct link between the matter–energy content of the Universe and the thermodynamic or informational
capacity of spacetime, thereby offering a unified framework for studying cosmological dynamics.
We first derived the modified Friedmann equation from the first law of thermodynamics. By identifying the
thermal part of the horizon entropy through the entropy balance relation and applying the Clausius relation at
the horizon, we obtained a modified Friedmann equation.
The coefficients of this equation differ slightly from
those reported in earlier works, where the total entropy variation, including the additional non-equilibrium entropy
produced by the mass-to-horizon entropy structure, was also taken into account in formulating the Friedmann
equations using the equilibrium thermodynamics. In such approaches, the usual conservation law may no longer
remain valid, because of the inclussion of the non-equilibrium part in the generated entropy. In contrast, we consider
only the equilibrium part of the entropy and neglect the additional non-equilibrium contribution. In this case, the
entropy change is entirely proportional to the change in the horizon area and corresponds only to the heat flow across
the horizon.
We then proceed to analyze the evolution of the horizon entropy. We found that the entropy is non-decreasing,
satisfying ˙S ≥0 throughout the evolution of the universe, in agreement with the generalized second law of thermo-
dynamics. In the regime dominated by in the cosmological constants in late-time, condition ¨S < 0 holds, indicating
that the entropy approaches a maximum value. This confirms that the Universe asymptotically evolves toward ther-
modynamic equilibrium, consistent with the de Sitter phase. This behavior is also consistent with the well-known
Bekenstein entropy relation for horizons.
In order to examine the microscopic thermodynamic structure underlying this evolution, we analyzed the fluctu-

entropy, the relative fluctuations reduce to a constant, which is independent of cosmic expansion, thereby highlighting
a clear distinction between the standard Bekenstein-Hawking entropy and the generalized mass-to-horizon entropy
descriptions. When we look at relative fluctuations, we found that, it decrease as the number of horizon degrees
of freedom increases. Since the degrees of freedom scale with entropy as Nsur = 4SH, this means larger entropy
leads to smaller fluctuations. As a result, both the energy and entropy of the horizon show only tiny fluctuations at

behavior shows how the Universe approaches a state of maximum entropy.
In fact, the relative fluctuation in horizon energy is extremely small at the end epoch, given by ⟨∆E⟩/EH ∼

with General Relativity. The parameter γ similarly does not alter the qualitative behavior of the fluctuations.
Overall, the generalized mass-to-horizon entropy framework preserves the standard gravitational limit while ex-
tending it to a thermodynamic description of cosmic expansion. The suppression of fluctuations and their holographic
scaling indicate that horizon thermodynamics plays a fundamental role in the emergence of large-scale cosmological
behavior.
