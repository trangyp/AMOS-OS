ABSTRACT
Complex operational workflows coordinating personnel, tools, and
information are central to enterprise operations, yet end-to-end
automation remains challenging due to extensive requirements
for human inputs and the inability to adapt over time. We present
GraphMind, an end-to-end system that constructs, executes, and
evolves action-centric workflow graphs without human effort. The
system operates in three phases. First, a scalable offline pipeline
extracts structured workflow graphs from large volumes of human
resolution traces, capturing problems, actions, and their causal rela-
tionships. Second, an online multi-agent traversal engine navigates
the graph to dynamically construct and execute workflows, com-
bining graph-guided retrieval with LLM-driven reasoning at each
step. Third, Adaptive Traversal Reinforcement (ATR) reinforces suc-
cessful traversal paths and decays stale elements. This closed-loop
mechanism enables the graph to self-optimize and adapt to shifting
operational conditions. GraphMind has been deployed across four
production cloud database services for incident investigation. Eval-
uated on production data, the system substantially outperforms a
Trace-RAG baseline in mitigation reach, groundedness, and diag-
nostic throughput, scoring 4.95/5 in blind expert review. The ATR
layer provides further gains across all metrics, demonstrating that
workflow graphs can learn and improve from execution-derived
feedback.

Although LLM-based agents [5, 23, 26, 35, 39] and agent skills

duction systems evolve while artifacts become stale; (iii) ineffi-

(iv) unable to capture the long tail of edge cases that experts handle
through improvisation but never document. Meanwhile, opera-
tional traces—historical records of how experts actually carried
out complex workflows—are abundant yet remain underutilized as
structured, reusable knowledge.

from operational traces, offer a far more efficient and agent-friendly
format than unstructured playbooks. Moreover, these graphs can
learn from their own execution: each traversal generates signal
about which paths succeeded, which were dead ends, and which
novel sequences the agent discovered. We draw inspiration from

Introduction to GraphMind. We present GraphMind, a system
realizing this vision through three core pillars (see Figure 1).
Pillar 1: Offline graph construction (warm start). As LLM context
windows expand and agentic retrieval matures [3, 28], the central
challenge has shifted from maximizing retrieval recall to navigat-
ing a three-way trade-off among cost, latency, and accuracy.
GraphMind occupies a pragmatic middle ground between naive
long-context ingestion of raw traces, which inflates online cost and
latency, and fully resolved entity-centric knowledge graphs [14, 17],
which incur heavy offline construction cost. Our offline pipeline
extracts actions as the central unit of a lightweight workflow
graph with a fixed, minimal schema, reducing online retrieval to a
bounded graph traversal (Section 3).
Pillar 2: Online multi-agent traversal. Rather than single-step
retrieval, the online phase performs iterative graph traversal: at each
step the agent retrieves a relevant subgraph, reasons over context
and execution history, and selects the most promising action to
exploit; if no suitable action exists, it pivots to a different graph
region to explore. Each traversal generates a complete trajectory
that serves as the raw material for adaptive traversal reinforcement
(Section 4).
Pillar 3: Adaptive traversal reinforcement. Inspired by Ant Colony
Optimization (ACO), we treat accumulated trajectories as reinforce-
ment signals in a closed-loop feedback framework. Reinforcement
deposition strengthens edges and nodes along successful paths,
while temporal decay fades stale elements. Over time, the graph
self-organizes: frequently successful paths become highways guid-
ing future agents, while ineffective paths fade—encoding collective
decision-making experience without explicit knowledge engineer-
ing (Section 5).

PILLAR 1: OFFLINE GRAPH
CONSTRUCTION (WARM START)
This section describes the offline pipeline that transforms raw oper-
ational traces into a structured, queryable workflow graph at scale.
In the ACO analogy, this phase corresponds to constructing the ini-
tial search space before any traversals have occurred. In production,
traces grow continuously in large volume (e.g., as new incidents are
resolved manually), producing an ever-expanding source of expert
decision-making data. The pipeline therefore supports incremen-
tal construction, integrating newly extracted knowledge without
rebuilding the graph. As illustrated in Figure 2, construction pro-
ceeds in four steps. (1) Workflow Extraction: each incoming batch
of traces is processed by an LLM-based extractor that produces one
workflow extract per trace (a subgraph), capturing the problems
encountered, actions taken, and their causal ordering (Section 3.2).
(2) De-clustering and Merging: the current version of the workflow
graph is de-clustered, reverting clustered nodes back to their raw
workflow extracts, which are then merged with the newly extracted
ones. (3) Clustering: semantically similar or identical nodes across
all workflow extracts are identified and merged, deduplicating the
graph while preserving distinct nodes (Section 3.3). (4) Graph and
Index Update: the updated graph is synchronized to the production
database and the vector index is updated to enable efficient search
and retrieval during online traversal (Section 3.4).
3.1
Graph Schema
We define a typed graph schema with three entity types and four
edge relations. Domain (D): a bounded operational area sharing
common systems, tools, and ownership (e.g., payments operations,
infrastructure reliability). Problem (P): an operational issue re-
quiring investigation or resolution within a domain (e.g., degraded
performance, authorization failures). Action (A): an executable
step, observational or interventive, taken to gather evidence or
resolve a problem.
Four edge types capture semantic connections: CAUSES (𝑝𝑖→𝑝𝑗,
problem causation), RESOLVES (𝑎→𝑝, action addresses prob-
lem), LEADS_TO (𝑎𝑖→𝑎𝑗, sequential workflow dependency), and
BELONGS_TO (𝑝or 𝑎→𝑑, domain classification). This yields a work-
flow graph G = (V, E) where V = D ∪P ∪A and E comprises
the typed edges, supporting structured traversal from root causes
to resolution strategies.
3.2
Workflow Extractor
The system employs a multi-agent extraction pipeline that pre-
serves causal and temporal relationships. It ingests heterogeneous
sources including issue-tracking databases and historical resolu-
tion traces, proceeding in three stages. Stage 1: Data Preprocess-
ing normalizes formats via document parsing, converts embedded
images/tables to text via vision-language models, and segments
documents using domain-aware heuristics. Stage 2: Workflow Con-
struction extracts problem statements, filters for actionable content,
identifies discrete action steps with preconditions, and assembles
reasoning chains conforming to the graph schema. Stage 3: Pruning
and Alignment removes redundant nodes and verifies type consis-
tency.
3.3
Clustering
As workflows accumulate, the graph acquires semantically similar
nodes describing the same problem or action in different terms.
After de-clustering and merging with existing workflows (see Sec-
tion 3.4), we apply a lightweight clustering procedure per node type
using off-the-shelf embedding models for easy maintenance and
incremental updates. While a more sophisticated approach could
leverage LLMs to perform semantic deduplication with richer con-
textual understanding, such methods do not scale to production-
sized graphs containing tens of thousands of nodes due to pro-
hibitive cost and latency. We therefore opt for the simplest effec-
tive approach: embedding-based agglomerative clustering, which
achieves comparable quality at a fraction of the cost (Section 6.1).
The clustering pipeline proceeds as follows: (1) Input canonical-
ization: node text is canonicalized via regex masking to remove
trace-specific tokens (GUIDs, timestamps, IPs). (2) Embedding:
canonicalized descriptions are embedded using a pre-trained model
(e.g., text-embedding-3-large). (3) Agglomerative clustering:
nodes are grouped using agglomerative clustering [18] with cosine
distance threshold 𝜏tuned per node type. (4) Representative node
generation: for each cluster, three members (longest, shortest, me-

De-Clustering, Graph Update and
Maintenance
When new traces arrive, the existing cluster representative nodes
are removed and all original members are re-enabled (de-clustering).
The raw workflows are then merged with the newly extracted ones,
and clustering is re-applied to produce the updated graph. This
approach maintains a clean, deduplicated graph while supporting
incremental updates without full reconstruction.

PILLAR 2: ONLINE MULTI-AGENT GRAPH
TRAVERSAL
Each online traversal corresponds to a single autonomous execution:
a team of agents iteratively navigates the workflow graph, selecting
edges and nodes based on a combination of reinforcement weights
(encoding historical reward signals) and LLM-driven reasoning,
executing the chosen actions, and producing a trajectory that serves
as input to the ATR layer (Section 5). In our deployment, action
nodes include diagnostic actions (e.g., KQL telemetry queries) and
mitigation actions (e.g., restart or escalation recommendations). For
safety, GraphMind autonomously executes diagnostic queries while
high-impact mitigations are surfaced as recommendations unless
explicitly approved by operators.
4.1
Orchestration
We leverage an existing enterprise orchestration framework [42]
as the online agentic execution environment; the orchestration
layer is modular and replaceable by any Model Context Protocol
(MCP)-compatible client [1] (e.g., GitHub Copilot CLI [16], Claude
Code [2]). The framework employs a nested control flow: an
outer loop over agents and an inner loop over skills. Each outer
iteration maintains a global context 𝐶𝑡(long-term memory); each
skill writes to a temporary context 𝑐𝑡,𝑗(short-term memory).
The Coordination Agent drives reasoning via Graph Loader
and Action Planner skills, while Execution Agents (e.g., Kusto
Agent) carry out actions and return results for iterative refinement.
4.2

and edges, each edge carrying a reinforcement weight 𝜙(𝑒) that
reflects accumulated traversal reward (initialized uniformly during
warm start and updated by ATR in Section 5). Given this scale, the
online execution engine requires a traversal algorithm that is effi-
cient, robust, and accurate, so it can identify the most relevant
next actions based on principled reasoning, reinforcement-weighted
edge priorities, and on the outcomes of previously executed actions.

mains, problems, actions) connected by causal and procedural edges,

PILLAR 3: ADAPTIVE TRAVERSAL
REINFORCEMENT
While the offline pipeline (Section 3) provides a warm-start graph
and the online engine (Section 4) traverses it, neither alone enables

5.5
Extensibility and Open Directions
A key advantage of the ATR framework is its simplicity: the rein-
forcement and decay rules are lightweight to implement yet open
a rich design space for future refinement. We highlight three direc-
tions.
Learning from low-quality trajectories. The current design
only deposits reinforcement for trajectories exceeding the qual-
ity threshold 𝛿𝑞, discarding signals from lower-quality traversals.
Applying negative reinforcement to suppress edges along failed tra-
jectories could accelerate convergence, though careful design is
needed to avoid permanently eliminating viable paths that failed
due to transient conditions.
Scaling retrieval beyond exploitation. The current probability-
based edge selection (Eq. 11) favors exploitation of proven paths.
As graphs grow to larger scale, the retrieval mechanism will need
more sophisticated dynamic pruning strategies that trim low-value
nodes without eliminating infrequently used but genuinely useful
actions.
Domain-differentiated reinforcement. Inspired by how ants
from different colonies carry distinct chemical signatures for nest-
mate recognition [9, 34], a natural extension is domain-differentiated
reinforcement: maintaining separate weight channels 𝑤𝑑(𝑒) per
problem domain 𝑑, so that traversals from capacity incidents re-
inforce different edges than those from authentication failures,
reducing cross-domain noise and enabling faster convergence of
domain-specific diagnostic strategies.

To evaluate the accuracy of the offline graph construction pipeline,

Extraction Quality. Table 2 summarize the results against human
annotations. The pipeline achieves node-level F1=0.89 (93% preci-
sion, 85% recall) and edge-level F1=0.92 (91% precision, 92% recall).
Problem nodes reach 98% precision, reflecting accurate root-cause
identification. Among edge types, CAUSES achieves the highest accu-

Error Analysis. We manually analyzed extraction errors and iden-
tified four dominant failure patterns. Implicit actions account for
38% of errors, where actions described indirectly (e.g., “after check-
ing the firewall rules, the issue was resolved”) are not extracted
as explicit action nodes. Domain jargon causes 26% of errors, as
highly specialized terminology or abbreviations unknown to the
base LLM occasionally lead to misclassification. Cross-reference
resolution contributes 21%, where references to external docu-
ments or previous incidents are not always resolved correctly. The
remaining 15% stem from multimodal fusion failures, where
critical information split across text and images is not properly
integrated.

6.2
Online Traversal Evaluation
We evaluate GraphMind’s online agentic traversal using the full
workflow graph constructed from one year of human resolution
traces spanning approximately 1,300 production incidents, with
roughly 12,000 active nodes at clustering threshold 𝜏=0.01. Each
test incident is investigated by GraphMind autonomously without
human intervention: given only the incident description, the system
traverses the workflow graph, executes Kusto Query Language
(KQL) diagnostic queries indicated in the action nodes to gather
telemetry data, and attempts to reach a mitigation. Throughout the
evaluation, a traversal denotes one autonomous investigation run
for an incident; a trajectory is the logged sequence of graph nodes,
edges, actions, and observations generated during that traversal;
and a conversation refers to a real user interaction in the production
deployment (Section 7). We organize metrics in a hierarchy of
decreasing importance:
• Mitigation Reach: Whether the system reaches a mitigation
action (binary).
• Groundedness: Whether conclusions are supported by exe-
cuted diagnostic queries (binary).
• Usefulness: LLM-judged score (1–5) comparing the sys-
tem’s recommended actions against human resolution logs.
• Avg. KQL Succ.: Number of successfully executed KQL
queries per incident.
• KQL Tbl. Recall: Percentage of Kusto tables in human-
logged queries also covered by agent-generated queries.
• Action Recall: Percentage of human-logged actions also
performed by the system.
Note that Action Recall and KQL Table Recall are computed against
incident human resolutions, that do not necessarily represent opti-
mal diagnostic paths. We define hallucination rate as 1−groundedness,
where groundedness indicates whether conclusions are supported
by executed diagnostic evidence.
In-Sample Generalization. Before comparing against baselines, we
verify that the graph generalizes beyond memorization. By tracking
action provenance across 118 in-sample incidents, only 28% of cases
retrieve actions originating from the same incident; the remain-
ing 72% draw entirely from other incidents. Even without same-
incident actions, performance remains strong: mitigation reach
98.8% vs. 97.0%, groundedness 92.9% vs. 87.9%, and usefulness 3.46
vs. 3.24, confirming that the workflow graph captures transferable
diagnostic knowledge rather than incident-specific traces.
GraphMind vs. Trace-RAG. We compare GraphMind against a
Trace-RAG baseline that retrieves LLM-condensed summaries
of past incident traces via similarity search and uses them to guide
an LLM agent, similar to RCACopilot [6]. Both systems benefit
from LLM-based preprocessing; the key difference is knowledge
representation: Trace-RAG retrieves per-incident summaries as
flat documents, whereas GraphMind retrieves from a deduplicated
workflow graph with explicit causal and procedural edges. The
comparison therefore primarily tests graph-structured traversal
vs. flat retrieval, though GraphMind’s graph also provides cross-
incident deduplication and reinforcement-weighted prioritization
that per-incident summaries lack.
Two domain experts conducted blind reviews of 19 matched
incidents (the maximum feasible given expert availability). Graph-

perfect). While the sample is small, the effect size is large (Cohen’s
𝑑>2) and consistent: experts noted that Trace-RAG frequently
produced incorrect query predicates and incomplete investigations.

DISCUSSION
From static knowledge to living infrastructure. The ATR layer trans-
forms the workflow graph from a static artifact into a self-improving
ecosystem. Each traversal contributes signal that shapes the graph’s
structure; over hundreds of traversals, emergent properties (re-
inforced highways, dormant dead ends) arise that no individual
engineer could design manually, representing a form of collective
intelligence.
Edge reinforcement as interpretability. Reinforcement values provide
a human-interpretable summary of learned diagnostic strategies.
Domain experts can inspect highest-reinforcement paths for vali-
dation and knowledge discovery; anomalously high reinforcement
on unexpected edges can flag emergent failure patterns.
Connections to reinforcement learning. ATR can be viewed as tabular
reinforcement learning where edge/node weights correspond to
Q-values. The deposition rule (Eq. 9) is analogous to temporal-
difference updates and decay (Eq. 10) provides a discount factor,
suggesting extensions such as learned value functions or MCTS-
based planning over the graph.
Limitations. The current reinforcement model assumes edge inde-
pendence; extending to path-level reinforcement with contextual
credit assignment is an important future direction. The decay rate
𝜌is fixed; adaptive decay based on detected distribution shifts
could improve responsiveness. The evaluation uses a single base-
line (Trace-RAG over LLM-condensed incident summaries); while
this tests graph-structured traversal vs. flat retrieval with compara-
ble preprocessing, both systems share LLM-based trace processing,
making it a meaningful comparison of knowledge representation
rather than extraction quality. Finally, the system is evaluated on a
single domain (cloud database incident management); generaliza-
tion to other operational domains remains to be validated.

CONCLUSIONS
We presented GraphMind, a system that transforms static workflow
graphs into living, self-improving operational assets, drawing on
Ant Colony Optimization as foundational inspiration. The system
operates through three pillars: (1) an offline pipeline that warm-
starts the graph from historical traces (node F1=0.89, edge F1=0.92
at $0.22/ticket); (2) an online multi-agent traversal engine achiev-
ing 98.5% mitigation reach, 4× higher successful KQL execution
throughput, and 4.95/5 expert scores; and (3) Adaptive Traversal
Reinforcement (ATR) that deposits reinforcement on successful
paths, improving groundedness by 2.7 percentage points and KQL
throughput by 14% over uniform traversal across 8 independent
runs, with directionally consistent gains across all metrics. In a
35-day production field study across four database services, 97% of
conversations produce actionable output, with failure cases primar-
ily attributable to coverage gaps and telemetry expiration rather
than system design flaws.
Several directions remain open. Additional ablations isolating
individual graph components (e.g., edge structure, deduplication,
reinforcement) would further clarify their contributions. The edge-
independent reinforcement model could be extended to path-level
reinforcement. Adaptive decay rates and domain-differentiated re-
inforcement channels could improve responsiveness. Finally, the
swarm-inspired paradigm may generalize beyond incident manage-
ment to software debugging, clinical decision support, and supply
chain optimization.
