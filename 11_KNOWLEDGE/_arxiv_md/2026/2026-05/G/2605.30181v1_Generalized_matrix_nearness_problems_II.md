GENERALIZED MATRIX NEARNESS PROBLEMS II
RONGBIAO THOMAS WANG, CHI-KWONG LI, AND LEK-HENG LIM
Abstract. Given a matrix A, a matrix nearness problem seeks an X that most closely approxi-
mates A in the sense of minimizing ∥A −X∥under a variety of constraints on X. A generalized
matrix nearness problem seeks the same but with three given matrices A, B, C and ∥A −BXC∥
in place of ∥A −X∥. We extend previous studies of the latter problem in three directions: incor-
porating an affine term, replacing matrix product by Kronecker product in various manners, and
generalizing Frobenius norm to any orthogonally invariant norm. We will solve several of these in
closed form. For the rest, we develop an iterative algorithm that works for any Schatten norm,
proving that it converges to a global minimizer regardless of the initial point. In addition, the
algorithm relies purely on numerical linear algebra, and notably does not compute any explicit
gradients or subgradients. Along the way, we will also show that there is no Mirsky-type theorem
for rank constrained generalized matrix nearness problems.
1. Introduction
This article is intended to be a sequel to [13], in which the authors addressed the generalized
matrix nearness problem:
min
X∈S ∥A −BXC∥F,
(1.1)
for given A ∈Rm×n, B ∈Rm×p, C ∈Rq×n, and ∥· ∥F the Frobenius norm. The problem was solved
in [13] for a variety of common feasible sets S, defined by:
➀rank constraint: rank(X) ≤r for a given integer r > 0;
➁norm constraint: ∥X∥F ≤ρ for a given ρ > 0;
➂product constraint: FXG = H for given matrices F, G, H;
➃spectral constraints: X has a prescribed eigenvalue or eigenvector;
➄symmetry constraints: X is symmetric or skew-symmetric;
➅structure constraints: X is Toeplitz, Hankel, or circulant;
➆positivity constraints: X is positive semidefinite, correlation, nonnegative, stochastic, or
doubly stochastic.
The original motivation was ➀, which first appeared in [21], was rediscovered in [9], and finally
given an essentially one-line solution in [13].
While one may simply treat (1.1) as a constrained optimization problem and seek a solution
by applying general-purpose optimization algorithms, the methods in [13] are notable in that they
solve an ostensible optimization problem (1.1) without computing a single gradient.
Solutions
are either given as closed-form expressions or as the results of a specialized iterative algorithm
that is “zeroth order” in optimization parlance, i.e., no derivatives involved. This algorithm [13,
Algorithm 5] is provably convergent (i.e., yields the global minimizer X∗), globally convergent (i.e.,
regardless of the choice of initial point X0), and linearly convergent (i.e., error ∥Xk−X∗∥F decreases
exponentially from one step to the next).
In this article, we extend the problem (1.1) in three directions:
affine: adding an affine term bxTC or BxcT where B, C are matrices and b, c are vectors;
Kronecker: replacing matrix product by Kronecker product ⊗, for example, BXC by B⊗X⊗C,
matrix rank by Kronecker rank, eigenpairs Xw = λw by partial trace trP(X(W ⊗I)) = λW;
Schatten: generalizing Frobenius norm ∥· ∥F to an arbitrary Schatten norm.

The solution methods in this article are of the same nature as those in [13] — either a closed-form
solution or an iterative solution from a provably and globally convergent algorithm. Regretfully,
we are unable to maintain linear convergence rate for these more complex variants of (1.1); our
proposed iterative algorithm could only be guaranteed to have sublinear convergence in most cases.
The best Kronecker rank-r approximation problem deserves some historical commentary. Let
m = m1m2, n = n1n2. The Kronecker rank of X ∈Rm×n is defined by
rank⊗(X) = min{r : X = Y1 ⊗Z1 + · · · + Yr ⊗Zr},
where the minimum is taken over Yi ∈Rm1×n1, Zi ∈Rm2×n2, i = 1, . . . , r.
This reduces to
standard matrix rank when m1 = m, n2 = n (thus n1 = 1 = m2); and so the best Kronecker rank-
r approximation problem minrank⊗(X)≤r∥A −X∥F contains the usual best rank-r approximation
problem as a special case. What is a bit surprising is that the Eckart–Young solution of the latter
[8] via singular value decomposition extends nearly verbatim to the former. This fact has been
rediscovered several times, for example, in [2, 11, 22] — the authors did not appear to be aware of
earlier efforts. We will extend their works to (1.1), providing closed form solutions when BXC is
replaced by B ⊗X ⊗C or (B1 ⊗B2)X(C1 ⊗C2); beyond these cases, our iterative algorithm would
yield the required result to machine precision.
We will elaborate on these and other new contributions below.
1.1. Closed-form solutions. New closed-form solutions for any matrix approximation problems
are hard to come by, given that the topic has been thoroughly explored over the past ninety years
since the appearance of [8]. Nevertheless we found four in the course of this work. In Section 3, we
will present these four variants of (1.1) that admit closed-form solutions.
(i) Affine generalized matrix nearness:
min
X∈S, x∈Rn∥A −BXC + dxTE∥F
or
min
X∈S, x∈Rm∥A −BXC + DxeT∥F,
for any A ∈Rm×n, B ∈Rm×p, C ∈Rp×n, nonsingular D ∈Rm×m, nonsingular E ∈Rn×n,
d ∈Rm, e ∈Rn, and any S ⊆Rp×p for which (1.1) has a closed-form solution.
(ii) Separable Kronecker product constraint:
min
X∈S ∥A −B ⊗X ⊗C∥F,
for any A ∈Rm1pm2×n1qn2, B ∈Rm1×n1, C ∈Rm2×n2, and any S ⊆Rp×q for which (1.1)
has a closed-form solution.
(iii) Generalized Kronecker rank constraint:
min
rank⊗(X)≤r∥A −(B1 ⊗B2)X(C1 ⊗C2)∥F,
for any A ∈Rm1m2×n1n2, B1 ∈Rm1×p1, B2 ∈Rm2×p2, C1 ∈Rq1×n1, C2 ∈Rq2×n2.
(iv) Prescribed partial trace Kronecker product constraint:
min
trP(X(W⊗I))=λW∥A −(B1 ⊗B2)X(C1 ⊗C2)∥F,
for any A ∈Rm1m2×n1n2, B1 ∈Rm1×p, B2 ∈Rm2×p, C1 ∈Rp×n1, C2 ∈Rp×n2, and λ ∈R.
This an analog of the prescribed eigenvalue problem in ➃, where W plays the role of a
λ-eigenvector. The definition of partial trace will be given in Section 3.4.
1.2. Nonexistence of Mirsky-type result. The closed-form solutions in [21, 9, 13] to ➀require
the Frobenius norm. One might wonder if they extend to other orthogonally invariant norms ∥· ∥,
i.e., ∥UXV ∥= ∥X∥whenever U and V are orthogonal matrices. The underlying motivation is
Mirsky’s celebrated result [16], i.e., the Eckart–Young solution [8] for the best rank-r approximation
problem minrank(X)≤r∥A −X∥holds universally across all orthogonally invariant norms ∥· ∥. We

min

(a) f is a subdifferentiable function that attains a minimum on {A −BXC : X ∈S};
(b) f is a convex function;
(c) f is an orthogonally invariant convex function;
(d) f is an orthogonally invariant norm ∥· ∥;
(e) f is a Schatten norm ∥· ∥σ,p for some p ∈[1, ∞];
(f) f is the Frobenius norm ∥· ∥F = ∥· ∥σ,2.
Indeed, our convergence result — that the iterates of Algorithm 2 converge from any initial point
to a global minimizer of (1.2) at a sublinear rate — applies to the most general case (a) and thus
to all other cases.
Nevertheless, the imposition of progressively stricter assumptions (b), (c), (d) does not allow us
to say more until we get to (e), i.e., when f is a Schatten norm as defined in (2.3). In this case,
we are able to obtain explicit closed-form solutions to the two main update steps in Algorithm 2,
simplifying it to Algorithm 3. We stress that even though we do not have separate results and
algorithms for cases (b), (c), (d), these play a role in our derivation of Algorithm 3 from Algorithm 2.
In addition, the base case (f), which is just (1.1), is important for a different reason: the first
update step in Algorithm 2 essentially reduces the problem (1.2) to (1.1). So Algorithm 2 effectively
draws on everything developed earlier in [13].
These algorithms and their convergence proofs will be given in Section 5. We will also present
a range of numerical experiments arising from real-world applications in Section 6 to illustrate
Algorithm 3.

5. Iterative solutions
Given that we do not have closed form solutions for norms other than the Frobenius norm, we
now turn to iterative solutions. As mentioned in Section 1.3, our proposed iterative algorithm is
very versatile: it works with any of the constraints ➀–➆listed on p. 1 (originally in [13]); it works
with the Kronecker rank constraint in Section 3.3 and partial trace constraint in Section 3.4 — new
constraints not considered in [13]; it also works with a combination of any of these constraints; and
in all cases it works with the more general affine objective function in Section 3.1.
The iterative algorithm in question is Algorithm 3, and it has convergence guarantees: (a) to the
global minimizer, (b) from any starting point, (c) at sublinear rate. In fact these hold true more
generally for any subdifferentiable f : Rm×n →R that attains the minimum
min
X∈S f(A −BXC).

Since treating this more general case requires no extra effort, we will establish our convergence

reduces to Algorithm 3 when f = ∥· ∥σ,p.
A word about the sublinear convergence in case it gives the reader pause: Such is the reality of
working with norms that are not strongly convex. Note that the Schatten norm ∥· ∥σ,p is convex but
not strictly convex for p = 1 and ∞; it is strictly but not strongly convex for all p ∈(1, 2) ∪(2, ∞);
and it is strongly convex for the unique value p = 2, the Frobenius norm. Strong convexity is the
key ingredient required for linear convergence in [13, Theorem 4.4] and we lack that here. And we
are not alone: sublinear convergence is typical of works involving ∥· ∥σ,1, the nuclear norm [3, 4];
without additional assumptions like the Kurdyka– Lojasiewicz property (which does not hold in our
case), a sublinear rate is the best one can do [1]. Our silver lining is that, in practice, the numerical
experiments in Section 6 indicate near linear convergence in Algorithm 3.

7. Conclusion
We expanded the study of generalized matrix nearness problems, deriving exact closed-form
solutions in four new cases: affine objective, separable Kronecker product constraint, Kronecker
rank constraint, and prescribed partial trace constraint. We showed that there is no result analogous
to Mirsky Theorem for such problems. In part to address this, we proposed an iterative algorithm
for generalized matrix nearness problems in any Schatten norm; the algorithm converges to a global
minimizer from any starting point, and never computes a gradient or a subgradient explicitly.
As the numerical experiments show, our algorithm handles objectives like the Schatten 3
2-norm,
which are convex and yet lie outside any standard convex solver’s reach. For generalized nearness
problems that do fall within the domain of such solvers, our algorithm is both faster and more
accurate.
This reaffirms a core premise alluded to in [13, Section 7]: Despite the popularity
of general purpose convex optimization solvers, an old-school numerical linear algebra approach
allows one to exploit structures far richer than mere convexity. This yields superior computational
performance, and in several cases also extends to nonconvex problems.
