GEOMETRISATION OF 3-MANIFOLDS
BRUNO MARTELLI
Abstract. The geometrisation theorem of 3-manifolds was conjectured by
Thurston the 1980s and proved by Perelman in the 2000s. This is an overview
on the subject. We explain the content of the theorem and describe its effects
in various situations.
Introduction
The geometrisation theorem of 3-manifolds was conjectured by Thurston in a
famous paper [Thu82] published by the Bulletin of the AMS in 1982, and then
proved by Perelman in a sequence of three papers [Per02, Per03a, Per03b] posted
on the arXiv in 2002 and 2003.
The theorem may be stated essentially in two
versions. The most complete one is
Every 3-manifold decomposes canonically into geometric pieces.
A more focused version says
Every 3-manifold is hyperbolic unless there are some obvious obstructions.
Each sentence contains a few terms that need to be carefully defined. The aim of
this paper is to explain the content of both versions, and to describe geometrisation
in action in various situations. We will not say a single word on Perelmann’s proof,
that is admittedly far outside of the author’s expertise.
Acknowlegments. Some figures are taken from Wikipedia Commons: Figures
19 and 18 were made by Cyp and Figure 22 was made by RyBu.
1. The theorem
The most complete version of the geometrisation theorem for 3-manifolds says
Every 3-manifold decomposes canonically into geometric pieces.
We explain the terminology.
Every 3-manifold here is tacitly assumed to be
compact, connected, orientable, smooth, and possibly with boundary consisting of
a disjoint union of tori. A decomposition is a two steps operation, where we first
cut the manifold along a canonical family of spheres, and then we cut it again along
a canonical family of tori, see Figure 1. We will explain these two steps carefully.
A geometric piece is a 3-manifold whose interior may be equipped with
a complete locally homogeneous Riemannian metric.

1.1. Three-manifolds and their submanifolds. As already said above, every
3-manifold M considered in this paper is tacitly assumed to be smooth, compact,
orientable, and with (possibly empty) boundary that consists of a finite union of
tori. The reason for admitting tori and not other kinds of surfaces is that tori fit
very well in the geometrisation perspective. Note that we have χ(M) = 0.
Every submanifold N ⊂M in a 3-manifold M is tacitly assumed to be compact,
without boundary, and contained in the interior of M. If N is orientable, its tubular
neighbourhood νN is always trivial! If N is a 1-manifold we get νN ∼= N×R2, and if
N is an orientable surface we get νN ∼= N ×R. Life is easier in dimension three, and
much more complicated in dimension four, where orientable surfaces in orientable
4-manifolds may have (and often have) non-trivial tubular neighbourhoods: for
instance, the tubular neighbourhood of a line in CP2 is not trivial.
1.2. Knots and links. A 1-dimensional submanifold L ⊂M of a 3-manifold M
is called a link. It is diffeomorphic to a finite union of circles, and if it consists of
a single circle it is called a knot. Mathematicians have started to classify knots in
S3 or R3 up to isotopy since the XIX century, most notably when Tait started to
produce tables like the one shown in Figure 2. Considering knots in S3 or R3 is
pretty much the same, and we prefer S3 because it is compact.
By what said above, the tubular neighbourhood of any knot K ⊂M is diffeo-
morphic to K × R2 ∼= S1 × R2. It is sometimes preferable to consider the smaller
closed tubular neighborhood S1 × D2 ⊂S1 × R2, that is compact and diffeomorphic
to the solid torus S1 × D2. Here Dk ⊂Rk denotes the unit disc. The boundary of
a solid torus is the torus S1 × S1. See some examples in Figure 3.

The following is the first important theorem about 3-manifolds. Note that the
inclusion S ,→M induces a homomorphism π1(S) →π1(M) of fundamental groups.
Lemma 1 (Dehn’s Lemma). An orientable connected surface S ⊂M of genus
g ≥1 is incompressible if and only if the map π1(S) →π1(M) is injective.
The Lemma was famously stated and “proved” by Dehn [Deh10] in 1910. The
proof however contained a serious gap that was then solved by Papakyriakopoulos
[Pap57] in 1957.
Dehn’s lemma is in fact more subtle than it might seem!
If
we replace inclusion S ⊂M with any map, it transforms into a well-known old
conjecture, that has been verified only for some classes of 3-manifolds:
Conjecture 2 (Simple Loop Conjecture). Let f : S →M be a map from an ori-
entable surface to a 3-manifold. If f∗: π1(S) →π1(M) is not injective, there is an
element in ker f∗that is represented by a simple (that is, embedded) loop.
1.4. Essential surfaces. A 3-manifold M contains plenty of surfaces, but only
few of them are really interesting for us. An orientable connected surface S ⊂M
is essential if the following holds:
(1) If S is a sphere, it does not bound a three-dimensional disc D3 in M;
(2) If S has genus g ≥1, it is incompressible;
(3) If S is a torus, it is not isotopic to a component of ∂M.

1.6. Prime decomposition. There is a well-established notion of connected sum
on 3-manifolds that is similar to the one on knots defined above. Let M1 and M2
be two connected oriented 3-manifolds. The connected sum M1#M2 of M1 and M2
is defined as follows:

Since the diffeomorphism is orientation-reversing, the new manifold M1#M2 is
oriented; we have ∂(M1#M2) = ∂M1 ⊔∂M2. By a theorem of Palais [Pal60] the
discs D1, D2 are in fact unique up to ambient isotopy, and by a theorem of Smale

that the resulting manifold M1#M2 is unique up to diffeomorphism. It is worth
noting that Palais’ theorem holds in every dimension n, while Smale’s does not!
For this reason, to define the connected sum in higher dimension we must choose
the gluing diffeomorphism with more care.
The manifold M1#M2 contains a sphere S, that is the result of glueing S1 with
S2. We can invert the move by cutting M1#M2 along S, and then capping off the
two new resulting sphere boundary components with two discs D1 and D2. This is
what we call cutting a manifold along a sphere: we first cut along a sphere, thus
producing two new spherical boundary components, and then attach two discs.
We observe that the resulting sphere S is essential, that is it does not bound a
disc, if and only if both M1 and M2 are not diffeomorphic to S3.
Oriented 3-manifolds form a commutative monoid with the connected sum op-
eration, with S3 playing the role of the neutral element. A connected sum M =
M1#M2 is non-trivial, that is M1, M2 ̸= S3, if and only if the resulting sphere S is
essential. The presence of an essential sphere S in a 3-manifold M that separates
M into two components thus certifies that M decomposes non-trivially as some
connected sum M = M1#M2.
Like with knots, we say that an oriented 3-manifold different from S3 is prime
if it cannot be obtained as the connected sum of two 3-manifolds, both different
from S3. By what just said this is equivalent to saying that M contains no essential
sphere S that decomposes M into two pieces. After Dehn’s lemma and Alexander’s
theorem, the third important result in three-dimensional topology is the following,
where existence was proved by Kneser [Kne29] and uniqueness by Milnor [Mil62].

iterate. But how can we be sure that this process ends?

1.7. JSJ decomposition. Having cut canonically every 3-manifold along spheres,
we now turn to tori. This procedure is quite different from the previous one, and
is usually called the JSJ decomposition, following the names of Jaco, Shalen, and
Johannson who discovered it independently [JS79, Joh79] in 1979, precisely 50 years
after Kneser’s contribution. A different approach to the theory is given by Neumann
and Swarup [NS97].
Let M be a prime 3-manifold. We say that an essential torus T ⊂M is canonical
if every other torus T ′ ⊂M can be isotoped to be disjoint from T. The following
example shows that this is a serious requirement.
Example 5. Let S be a surface of genus g ≥1 as in Figure 16. An essential curve
in S is a simple closed curve γ ⊂S that does not bound a disc. If γ ⊂S is an
essential curve as in the figure, then T = γ ×S1 ⊂S ×S1 is an essential torus in the
3-manifold M = S × S1. However, this essential torus is not canonical: it is always
possible to find another essential curve η ⊂S that intersects γ in an essential way,
so that there is no way to isotope T ′ = η × S1 away from T. See Figure 16.

tori. This collection exists and is unique up to isotopy.

1.9. Geometric pieces. To understand the geometrisation theorem, we are left
with the definition of a geometric piece. The definition is very simple to state.
Definition 7. A 3-manifold M is geometric if its interior admits a complete locally
homogeneous Riemannian metric.

H3, R3, S3, S2 × R, H2 × R, g

1.10. Geometrisation. We can finally state the geometrisation theorem.
Theorem 8 (Geometrisation). Let M be a prime 3-manifold. Every piece arising
from the JSJ decomposition of M is geometric.
If the JSJ decomposition is empty, the manifold M is itself geometric: this
holds for instance if M contains no essential tori. The theorem was conjectured
by Thurston [Thu82] and proved by him in many cases, which include all prime
3-manifolds M that contain at least one incompressible surface, and all prime 3-
manifolds M with boundary. Perelmann then proved the theorem [Per02, Per03a,

3. Geometrisation in action
We show the effect of geometrisation in a few different contexts.
3.1. Knots. By Alexander’s theorem, the exterior M = S3 \ νK of a knot K ⊂S3
is a prime 3-manifold. However, it may have a non-trivial JSJ decomposition: this
holds precisely when the knot K is a satellite, that is it is contained non-trivially
in the closed tubular neighbourhood νK′ of another non-trival knot K′ (contained
non-trivially means that K is neither isotopic to K′ nor contained in a ball), see
Figure 22. In this case the boundary torus ∂νK′ is essential. Every non-prime knot
is a particular kind of satellite knot.
By geometrisation there are three types of non-trivial knots in S3:
• the hyperbolic knots, whose exterior is hyperbolic;
• the torus knots, whose exterior has geometry g
SL2 or H2 × R;
• the satellite knots, whose exterior has a non-trivial JSJ decomposition.
A torus knot is a knot contained in a standard torus: such knots are easily
parametrized by pairs of coprime integers (p, q).
The exterior of a torus knot
admits two different geometries because f
SL2 and H2 × R coexist in manifolds with
non-empty boundary. This trichotomy appeared for the first time in [Thu82].
Hyperbolisation can be written succinctly as follows:
Every knot that is neither a torus knot nor a satellite knot is hyperbolic.
3.2. Surface bundles. Let a 3-manifold M fibre over the circle. The fibre Σ is
a connected orientable surface with (possibly empty) boundary, and by fixing a
horizontal vector field and taking its flow one finds a monodromy φ: Σ →Σ that
is well-defined only up to isotopy. We can recover M from φ by taking Σ × [0, 1]
and glueing its two boundary components via φ. We suppose that χ(Σ) < 0 since
the finitely many cases χ(Σ) ≥0 are simpler to deal with.

Hyperbolisation in this context is easy to state:
If φ does not fix any finite set of essential curves up to isotopy, M is hyperbolic.
This formulation says as usual that M is hyperbolic unless there is some obvious
obstruction. A simple closed curve in Σ is essential if it does not bound a disc and
it is not parallel to a boundary component of Σ. If φ fixes a finite set of isotopy
classes of essential curves, by sliding them along the fibration we would construct
some (immersed) essential tori, and this would prevent M from being hyperbolic.
Geometrisation can be formulated in a more structured way following Thurston’s
characterisation of the homeomorphisms of surfaces [Thu88]. Thurston proved that
every self-homeomorphism φ: Σ →Σ can be chosed after an isotopy to be one of
the following types:
(1) finite order, that is φk = id for some k;
(2) reducible, that is φ fixes an essential curve;
(3) pseudo-Anosov.
The types (1) and (2) are the only two that may hold simultaneously. A pseudo-
Anosov map is a marvelous package that consists of:
• A cone flat structure on the surface ¯Σ obtained by shrinking the boundary
components of Σ to ideal points, where some points have cone angle kπ for
some integer k ̸= 2, and only the ideal points are allowed to have k = 1;
• Two orthogonal geodesic foliations in ¯Σ called stable and unstable, that
look like Figure 23 near the singular points;
• A homeomorphism φ: ¯Σ →¯Σ that preserves the singular points and each
foliation, and looks like an affine map away of the singular points that
stretches the unstable leaves by some λ > 1 and contracts the stable ones
by 1/λ (in particular it preserves areas). The number λ > 1 is called the
stretching factor of φ.

Let M be a 3-manifold fibering over the circle with
monodromy φ. The geometrisation type for M depends elegantly on the type (1),
(2), or (3) of φ stated above, as follows. The manifold M is always prime, and
correspondingly to the type of φ we have
(1) M has geometry H2 × R;
(2) M contains an essential torus;
(3) M is hyperbolic.
Coherently with what said above only (1) and (2) may coexist. In case (2) we
know that φ fixes an essential curve γ, and to recover the full geometric decompo-
sition of M one should cut Σ along γ and proceed inductively.
The correspondence between the three types of φ and the three possible geomet-
ric outcomes for M is among the most beautiful manifestations of geometrisation of
3-manifolds, and was proved by Thurston [Thu98] before Perelman’s more general
accomplishment. A standard complete reference is Otal [Ota96]. The strong part
in this correspondence is
M hyperbolic ⇐⇒φ pseudo-Anosov.
It is worth noting that neither of the two implications is easy to prove: we cannot
jump directly from the pseudo-Anosov map φ to the hyperbolic structure on M,
nor conversely from the hyperbolic structure of M to the pseudo-anosov map φ.
One may wonder how general this construction is, since a priori there could be
only very few 3-manifolds that fibre over the circle. A recent celebrated break-
through of Agol [Ago13], obtained with fundamental contribution of Wise [Wis21],
shows that there are plenty of them, since (answering a famous question of Thurston
[Thu82]) every hyperbolic 3-manifold has a finite cover that fibres over the circle.

3.5. Dehn surgery. Let L ⊂S3 be a link. A closed regular neighbourhood of L
consists of a disjoint union of solid tori, one for each component of L as in Figure
1. A Dehn surgery on L is the operation that consists of removing each solid torus,
thus creating the exterior M = S3 \ νL, and gluing it back along a different map.
The result is a new manifold N without boundary.
The isotopy class of every regluing map is uniquely determined by a pair (p, q) of
coprime integers that encode the isotopy class of the image of the meridian D×{pt}
of the solid torus D × S1 in the boundary of the regular neighbourhood of the
component of L. We have here a combinatorial way of describing many manifolds
N, out of a link L and some rational numbers p/q attached to its components.
Lickorish [Lic62] and Wallace [Wal60] proved independently that in fact every 3-
manifold N without boundary may be constructed in this way. For instance, if we
attach the number 0 to the three components of the Borromean rings in Figure
26, we get the 3-torus N = S1 × S1 × S1, and if we attach the number 3 to the
figure-eight knot, we get the Hantsche – Wendt flat 3-manifold.
This combinatorial presentation for N can be given to the extremely efficient
and easy to use program SnapPy [CDGW], that tries to find a hyperbolic structure
for N, and in the frequent cases where it succeeds, it calculates many interesting
geometric invariants of N.
In contrast with the previous contexts (knot complements, polyhedra, and double
branched coverings), here there is no nice theorem that, given a link L and some
rational numbers attached to its components, says whether the resulting manifold
N is hyperbolic or not. Thurston’s hyperbolic Dehn filling theorem [Thu79] says
If L has hyperbolic exterior and the rational numbers
are not contained in some finite set S ⊂Q, then N is hyperbolic.
The finite set S depends on L. For instance, the figure eight knot has hyperbolic
exterior, and if we pick any number p/q ̸∈S = {−4, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, ∞}, the
manifold N is hyperbolic: this famous example was described by Thurston [Thu79].
If we assign a number p/q ∈S, we get a manifold of some other geometries, or with
a non-trivial decomposition.
