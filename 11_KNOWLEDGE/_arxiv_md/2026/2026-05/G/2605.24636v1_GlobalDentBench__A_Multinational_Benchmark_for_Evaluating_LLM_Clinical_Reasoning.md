Abstract
While large language models (LLMs) hold transformative potential for medicine, their rea-
soning robustness and safety in real-world clinical scenarios remain critically underexplored,
particularly in dentistry.
Here we introduce GlobalDentBench, the first multinational den-
tal benchmark, featuring a taxonomy that encompasses 14 dental specialties across 88 countries
and regions spanning six continents. The benchmark comprises 8,978 expert-validated questions
across three formats (multiple-choice, short-answer, and case-based questions) and assesses three
progressive reasoning levels: knowledge recall (L1), routine reasoning (L2), and individualized
reasoning (L3). To ensure data quality, the automated construction framework was calibrated
by six senior dentists, achieving expert agreement rates of 99.98% for multiple-choice and short-
answer questions and 96.78% for the more complex case-based questions.
Evaluation of 12
frontier LLMs on GlobalDentBench revealed a sharp, stepwise performance degradation with
increasing reasoning complexity. Specifically, accuracy plummeted from 81.34% on multiple-
choice to 64.53% on short-answer and 22.34% on case-based questions, while declining markedly
from 74.01% at L1 to 55.64% at L2 and 35.71% at L3. More critically, risk analysis of real-world
dental cases demonstrated an alarming overall unsafe rate of 31.01% in LLM-generated clinical
recommendations, with 4.51% posing risks of irreversible patient harm and risks particularly
pronounced in specialties such as orthodontics. These findings expose fundamental limitations
in the medical reasoning and safety of current LLMs. Consequently, GlobalDentBench provides
a scalable foundation for trustworthy clinical AI evaluation, underscoring the urgent need for
rigorous validation before the safe deployment of these models in healthcare.

Introduction
The rise of large language models (LLMs) has initiated a paradigm shift for artificial intelligence
within healthcare, offering sophisticated capabilities for the understanding of natural language, the
synthesis of knowledge, and the support of clinical decisions [1, 2, 3]. Through extensive training
upon vast corpora of medical literature and clinical data, frontier models have demonstrated a high
level of proficiency in standardized examinations for medical licensing, frequently matching the
performance of human experts within controlled settings [4, 5]. Systematic evaluations across var-
ious branches of medicine indicate that these models excel in communication with patients, health
education, and scenarios involving simulated diagnosis, thereby offering the potential to alleviate
the cognitive burden on practitioners while supporting practice rooted in evidence [6, 7]. Never-
theless, a persistent gap remains between the performance observed on benchmarks modeled after
closed examinations and the rigorous requirements of clinical environments in the real world. These
authentic settings necessitate the management of diagnostic ambiguity, the processing of incom-
plete information, and the execution of iterative reasoning across multiple steps under conditions
of uncertainty [8, 9, 10].
This disparity is particularly acute within the field of dentistry, a discipline requiring the inte-
gration of spatial analysis, procedural expertise, and treatment planning over longitudinal periods,
all tailored to the context of individual patients [11, 12]. Although LLMs have been utilized for
dental queries and educational tasks, existing assessments are largely restricted to examinations for
local licensing or narrow subfields. These evaluations rely predominantly on multiple-choice ques-
tions(MCQs) that measure the recall of facts rather than the inferential reasoning based on specific
cases that remains essential to routine clinical practice [13, 14]. Studies focused on benchmarking
consistently demonstrate that the performance of models declines sharply when tasks transition
from the retrieval of canonical knowledge to open reasoning that incorporates clinical ambiguity
[15, 16, 17].
Recent progress in multimodal models, such as DentalGPT [18], ToothXpert [19], OralGPT [20]
and DentVLM [21] represents the current technological frontier for artificial intelligence specialized

for dentistry. These systems have achieved notable milestones in integrating visual and linguistic
data for the purpose of complex reasoning. However, such advances also illuminate the limitations
of current evaluation frameworks, which remain fragmented and insufficiently aligned with the full
spectrum of dental practice across diverse geographies. This underscores an urgent requirement
for a more rigorous and globally representative benchmark capable of systematically measuring the
robustness of reasoning under authentic clinical conditions [22, 23].
Foundational benchmarks for medical models, while useful for the establishment of initial ca-
pabilities, possess inherent constraints that limit their applicability in a clinical setting [24, 25]. In
domains where the stakes are high, models exhibit documented weaknesses, including an overconfi-
dence in incorrect outputs, sensitivity to variations in prompts, and the occurrence of hallucinations
during reasoning across multiple steps [26, 27]. Such limitations carry direct consequences for clin-
ical deployment; evaluations of collaboration between physicians and AI show that models may
introduce cognitive biases if close supervision is absent [28, 29]. In dentistry, where errors in di-
agnosis can produce consequences that are irreversible, the lack of a framework for evaluation
spanning multiple levels of cognition has restricted the reliable assessment of whether these models
are ready for clinical use [30, 31].
Here we introduce GlobalDentBench, the first multinational benchmark designed to evaluate
the robustness of reasoning within dentistry. Spanning 88 countries or regions and 14 dental spe-
cialties, GlobalDentBench comprises 8,978 questions validated by experts.
These questions are
stratified across three clinically anchored reasoning levels: L1 (Knowledge Recall), L2 (Routine
Reasoning), and L3 (Individualized Reasoning). Source materials are drawn exclusively from qual-
ification examinations of a gold standard, textbooks subjected to peer review, and case reports of
high impact, ensuring direct alignment with authentic practice [32, 33]. We developed a novel agent
pipeline utilizing automated agents combined with a process of calibration by experts, achieving
a high concordance rate with senior dentists during the human-in-the-loop validation phase. Fur-
thermore, our automated judging architecture, led by Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, demonstrated over
98% agreement with expert grading, thereby establishing a framework for evaluation that is both
scalable and trustworthy [34, 35].
GlobalDentBench addresses fundamental gaps in the methodology of current AI evaluation.
First, it extends assessment beyond the recall of factual knowledge to encompass formats such as
short-answer questions (SAQs) and case-based questions (CBQs) that more closely mirror the work-
flow of a clinic. Second, the framework for construction offers a template that can be transferred
to other medical specialties [36]. Finally, by anchoring every item in official sources and employ-
ing automated judging based on clinical rubrics, the benchmark guarantees meaningful results for
clinicians rather than superficial metrics of statistics [37, 38].
Benchmarking of twelve frontier LLMs on GlobalDentBench reveals a sharp, stepwise perfor-
mance degradation with increasing reasoning complexity. Specifically, accuracy plummeted from
81.34% on MCQs to 64.53% on short-answer and 22.34% on case-based questions, while declining
markedly from 74.01% at L1 to 55.64% at L2 and 35.71% at L3, with no model exceeding 50% on
individualized reasoning. More critically, risk analysis of real-world dental cases demonstrated an
alarming overall unsafe rate of 31.01% in LLM-generated clinical recommendations, with 4.51% pos-
ing risks of irreversible patient harm, and risks particularly pronounced in orthodontics at 44.30%.
These findings expose fundamental limitations in the medical reasoning and safety of current LLMs,
indicating that they function primarily as supervised aids for knowledge retrieval rather than au-
tonomous clinical agents. Consequently, GlobalDentBench establishes a scalable infrastructure for
the rigorous evaluation of medical AI, underscoring the urgent necessity for strict validation and
human-in-the-loop oversight prior to the safe deployment of these models in healthcare settings
where the stakes are high [39].

Results
2.1
Benchmark Overview
To systematically evaluate large language models, we developed GlobalDentBench (illustrated in
Fig. 1), a scalable and trustworthy multinational dental benchmark comprising 8,978 questions.
GlobalDentBench is designed along four aspects:
• Geographic coverage (Fig. 1A): The dataset spans 88 countries and regions across six
continents, reflecting a diverse range of global educational and clinical standards.
• Reasoning level (Fig. 1B): To represent question requirements in a clinically interpretable
manner, each item was assigned to one of three levels: L1 (knowledge recall), assessing recall
of established foundational dental knowledge without meaningful case-based reasoning; L2
(routine reasoning), assessing reasoning based primarily on the core clinical presentation and
typical diagnostic or therapeutic logic; and L3 (individualized reasoning), assessing reasoning
that requires incorporation of patient-specific information, individualized constraints, or non-
standard clinical considerations to reach the final judgement.
• Dental Disciplines (Fig. 1C): Each item was mapped to one of 14 dental disciplines, such
as Anesthesia and Medical Emergencies (AME), Basic Sciences and Preventive Dentistry
(BSPD), Caries, Tooth Defects and Trauma (CTDT), Conventional Prosthodontics (CP),
Dentoalveolar Surgery (DS), Maxillofacial Diseases and Surgery (MFDS), Oral and Maxillo-
facial Radiology (OMR), Oral Implantology (OI), Oral Mucosal Diseases (OMD), Orthodon-
tics (Ortho), Pediatric Dentistry (PD), Pulp and Periapical Diseases (PPD), Periodontal and
Peri-implant Diseases (PP), and Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety (SHPS).
• Question types (Fig. 1D): The benchmark comprises three distinct formats: 3,679 Multiple-
Choice Questions (MCQs) sourced from official qualification examinations; 3,709 Short-Answer
Questions (SAQs) derived from officially recognized dental books; and 1,590 Case-Based Ques-
tions (CBQs) extracted from peer-reviewed clinical case reports for scenario-driven evaluation.
To support both scalability and trustworthiness, GlobalDentBench combined automated con-
struction with expert validation.
The benchmark was generated through an automated agent
pipeline and subsequently reviewed by six senior dentists, who verified question validity and answer
correctness. Under a unified evaluation protocol, we assessed 12 frontier LLMs, including six propri-
etary models (Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, GPT-5.4, Grok-4, Claude-Sonnet-
4.6, and Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro) and six open-source models (DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-K2.5, GLM-5,
Qwen3.5-Flash, Qwen3.5-Plus, and MiniMax-M2.7). MCQ items were scored by exact-match accu-
racy, whereas SAQ and CBQ responses were evaluated using Gemini-3-Flash-Preview as the judge
model.
2.2
Performance Disparities Across Question Type and Reasoning Complexity
Evaluation across the three question types revealed a clear stepwise decline in model performance
along an increasing gradient of clinical complexity, from standardized MCQs to open-ended SAQs
and real-world CBQs (Fig. 2A). The aggregate mean score across all evaluated models was highest
on MCQs (81.34%), decreased on SAQs (64.53%), and declined further on CBQs (22.34%). This
gradient aligned with the distinct provenance and difficulty structure of the three datasets, rather

than with response format alone. Whereas MCQs and SAQs primarily assessed standardized exam-
ination knowledge and textbook-based responses, respectively, CBQs were derived from real-world
clinical case reports and required models to reason over nuanced and sometimes conflicting clinical
information.
To further resolve this performance gradient, we stratified the benchmark into three hierarchical
reasoning levels (L1–L3) based on the demands of the underlying clinical tasks (Fig. 2B). To reduce
the influence of uneven question-type distributions, we report the Macro Average Score across
question types. The results show a clear decline in performance as reasoning complexity increases:
74.01% on knowledge recall tasks (L1), 55.64% on routine clinical reasoning tasks (L2), and 35.71%
on individualized reasoning tasks (L3), which require integration of patient-specific information,
individualized constraints, or non-standard clinical considerations to reach the final judgement.
2.3
Comparative Analysis of Frontier Large Language Models
We evaluated 12 frontier LLMs spanning both proprietary and open-source systems: Gemini-3-
Flash-Preview, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview, GPT-5.4, Grok-4, Claude-Sonnet-4.6, Doubao-Seed-2.0-
Pro, DeepSeek-R1, Kimi-K2.5, GLM-5, Qwen3.5-Flash, Qwen3.5-Plus, and MiniMax-M2.7. Overall
proficiency was quantified as an unweighted macro-average across the three question types (Fig. 2C),
thereby reducing bias from the imbalanced question distribution. Under this metric, Gemini-3.1-
Pro-Preview achieved the highest overall proficiency (63.27%). Among the open-source models,
GLM-5 showed the strongest overall performance (56.43%).
When stratified by reasoning level, performance declined consistently from L1 to L3 across all
12 models (Fig. 2D). Scores remained relatively high on L1 and L2, with most models clustering
at 63.77–82.64% and 45.67–63.75%, respectively, but dropped sharply on L3, where all models
scored from 23.30–46.75%. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview led on L1 and L2, whereas Claude-Sonnet-4.6
achieved the highest score on L3. Importantly, all models remained below 50% on L3, indicating
that individualized reasoning posed a shared challenge even for the strongest models.
Overall,
the results suggest a stepwise degradation in performance as reasoning demands increased, with
substantial declines from knowledge recall to routine clinical reasoning and again from routine
clinical reasoning to patient-specific individualized reasoning.
To assess the cost–performance profile of each model, we estimated model-specific inference
costs by applying official pricing schedules to the observed input and output token counts across
all inference records, and expressed them as the mean cost per 1,000 queries. In the resulting
cost–performance distribution (Fig. 2E), variation in cost was substantially larger than variation
in macro-average score, indicating that performance gains were modest relative to differences in
inference cost. Among proprietary models, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview provided the most favorable
trade-off, with a macro-average score of 61.59% at $3.72 per 1,000 queries. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview
achieved the highest macro-average score overall (63.27%) but at a substantially higher cost ($18.45
per 1,000 queries), while Claude-Sonnet-4.6 also ranked near the top (59.71%) at $19.59 per 1,000
queries. Among open-source models, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5 showed the strongest trade-offs: GLM-
5 reached 56.43% at $5.48 per 1,000 queries, whereas Kimi-K2.5 reached 55.49% at only $1.37 per
1,000 queries.
By contrast, Grok-4 incurred the highest cost ($38.14 per 1,000 queries) while
achieving a macro-average score of 57.77%.
Overall, higher inference cost did not consistently
translate into better overall performance.

Performance Disparities Across Dental Disciplines
We summarized discipline-level performance across the 14 dental disciplines as a heat map in Fig. 3,
with darker shading indicating higher performance. For each discipline, the score was calculated as
the macro-average of model performance across the three question types (MCQ, SAQ and CBQ).
Across all 12 models, Oral Mucosal Diseases achieved the highest mean score (OMD, 63.66%), fol-
lowed by Pulp and Periapical Diseases (PPD, 59.62%) and Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology (OMR,
59.44%). By contrast, the lowest mean scores were observed in Orthodontics (Ortho, 50.59%), Pe-
diatric Dentistry (PD, 51.08%) and Conventional Prosthodontics (CP, 51.92%). The gap between
the highest- and lowest-performing disciplines was 13.07 percentage points. This discipline-level
pattern was broadly consistent across models: Oral Mucosal Diseases (OMD) was the top-scoring
discipline for all models, whereas the lowest-scoring discipline varied across models but was most
frequently Orthodontics (Ortho). These results indicate that model performance differed system-
atically across dental specialties, with stronger performance in mucosal and endodontic-related
domains and weaker performance in orthodontic, pediatric and prosthodontic contexts.
2.5
Risk Analysis of LLM Answers in Case-Based Questions
To evaluate the clinical safety of model-generated answers in real-world dental scenarios, we per-
formed a risk analysis of 19,080 responses from 12 frontier LLMs across 1,590 case-based questions.
Responses were assigned to one of three safety-risk categories: S0 denoted clinically safe re-
sponses, S1 denoted unsafe responses with potential for reversible patient harm, and
S2 denoted unsafe responses with potential for irreversible patient harm. Overall, 68.99%
of responses were classified as S0, whereas 31.01% were classified as unsafe (Fig. 4A). Most unsafe
responses belonged to S1 (26.50%), while S2 responses accounted for a smaller but clinically impor-
tant fraction (4.51%). Thus, nearly one-third of LLM-generated answers in real-world dental cases
contained potentially harmful clinical content, underscoring the need for expert validation before
clinical use.
Model-wise risk profiles showed substantial heterogeneity (Fig. 4B,C). For model-level analysis,
we used the total number of responses generated by each model as the denominator. The overall
unsafe rate used the number of S1 and S2 responses as the numerator, whereas the S1 and S2 rates
used the corresponding category-specific counts as numerators. Based on this calculation, overall
unsafe rates ranged from 15.97% to 45.85% across models.
GPT-5.4, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview
and Gemini-3-Flash-Preview showed the lowest overall unsafe rates, whereas Grok-4, MiniMax-
M2.7 and Qwen3.5-Flash showed the highest rates. This model-level variation was driven mainly
by differences in S1 responses, whose rates increased substantially from the lowest-risk to the
highest-risk models (Fig. 4B). By contrast, S2 responses occurred at lower absolute rates but were
still observed across all models (Fig. 4C). MiniMax-M2.7 showed the highest S2 rate, followed by
Qwen3.5-Flash and Grok-4, whereas Claude-Sonnet-4.6, GPT-5.4 and Qwen3.5-Plus had the lowest
S2 rates. The model ranking for S2 risk therefore differed from that for S1 risk, indicating that a
lower overall unsafe rate did not necessarily imply better control of severe-risk clinical errors.
The discipline-level heatmaps revealed distinct specialty-specific risk structures (Fig. 4D,E).
For discipline-level analysis, responses were grouped by dental specialty and model, with the total
number of responses in each model–discipline pair used as the denominator. The overall unsafe rate
used the number of S1 and S2 responses as the numerator, whereas the S1 and S2 rates used the
corresponding category-specific counts as numerators. Discipline-level mean rates were obtained by
averaging these model–discipline rates across models. Based on this calculation, when S1 and S2
responses were combined, the highest overall unsafe rates were observed in Orthodontics (Ortho,

2.6
Reliability of Benchmark Construction and Evaluation
To ensure the structural integrity and clinical validity of the benchmark, we rigorously validated
both the automated dataset generation pipeline and the evaluation framework. The extraction
of MCQs and SAQs was strictly constrained to standardized medical examinations and canonical
dental textbooks, thereby guaranteeing high fidelity to established clinical ground truths. Con-
versely, the generation of CBQs from unstructured, real-world clinical case reports may introduce
induced hallucinations of LLMs. To mitigate this, we implemented a stringent human-in-the-loop
validation phase within our data labeling pipeline. A panel of six senior dentists manually audited a
substantial stratum of 523 CBQs out of the total 1,590 generated items (representing 32.89% of the
CBQ corpus). Manual validation by the expert panel demonstrated a 96.78% accuracy rate, estab-
lishing the pipeline’s capacity to distill complex clinical narratives while systematically suppressing
generative hallucinations.
Beyond dataset validity, we audited the reliability of the automated grading architecture. Five
candidate judge models were selected from models with an average inference cost below $10 per
1,000 queries and the highest overall performance: Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro,
GPT-5.4, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5. Two dentists reviewed randomly sampled SAQ and CBQ grad-
ing records, comprising 300 SAQ judgements and 1,500 CBQ key-point judgements, with a 5%
overlapping subset used to estimate inter-rater consistency. Candidate judge models showed high
agreement with dentist review: 98.15% for Gemini-3-Flash-Preview, 97.88% for Kimi-K2.5, 97.49%
for GPT-5.4, 96.96% for GLM-5 and 94.05% for Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro. Dentist inter-rater agree-
ment was 96.67% under the same criterion. These findings support rubric-based LLM judging as a
scalable approximation of expert grading for benchmark evaluation, with Gemini-3-Flash-Preview
selected as the preferred proprietary judge model and Kimi-K2.5 as a strong open-source alterna-

Case Study
To illustrate the CBQ evaluation workflow, we present an example L3 case involving a 66-year-old
male patient with recurrent abscesses and dull pain associated with tooth #26 (Fig. 5). The case
required individualized reasoning across endodontic, periodontal and prosthetic factors, including
pulpal non-vitality, apical and mesial radiolucency, Grade II mobility, a deep periodontal pocket,
occlusal interference and an ill-designed partial denture. Gemini-3-Flash-Preview identified the con-
dition as a true combined endodontic–periodontal lesion and proposed a staged multidisciplinary
plan, including occlusal adjustment, prosthetic modification, root canal treatment, post-RCT ob-
servation and subsequent periodontal regenerative surgery.
The judge model then compared the response against five reference key points. Four key points
were fully aligned: the model recognized the endodontic component, described guided tissue regen-
eration for the 14-mm mesial defect, recommended an observation period after root canal treat-
ment, and addressed removal of occlusal and prosthetic predisposing factors. One key point was
not aligned because the model suggested ruling out vertical root fracture during exploratory flap
surgery, whereas the reference indicated that vertical root fracture was ruled out after successful
response to endodontic treatment. This yielded a key point alignment score of 80/100. Despite
this deviation, the response was classified as S0 because it did not introduce clinically harmful
recommendations: the treatment sequence followed the endodontic-first principle, included an ap-
propriate observation interval before regenerative surgery, and addressed occlusal and prosthetic
contributors to recurrence. This case demonstrates how GlobalDentBench evaluates not only final-
answer correctness, but also the alignment of intermediate clinical reasoning and safety risk in
case-based dental decision-making.

Discussion
The integration of large language models (LLMs) into clinical workflows necessitates rigorous, clin-
ically aligned evaluation frameworks. In this study, we developed GlobalDentBench, a scalable and
trustworthy dataset comprising 8,978 expert-validated questions spanning 88 countries or regions
and 14 distinct dental disciplines. By evaluating 12 frontier LLMs, a consistent pattern emerged:
performance remained comparatively strong on standardized, exam-like tasks, but deteriorated
sharply as tasks moved toward open-ended and case-based reasoning. This decline was evident
both across question formats (MCQ, 81.34%; SAQ, 64.53%; CBQ, 22.34%) and across reasoning
levels (L1, 74.01%; L2, 55.63%; L3, 35.71%). Together, these findings indicate that current LLMs
in dentistry remain substantially more reliable at retrieving and reproducing structured knowledge
than at resolving non-standard clinical problems under ambiguity in real-world.
GlobalDentBench makes three main contributions. First, GlobalDentBench fills a major gap in
dental LLM evaluation. Existing dental LLM evaluations have commonly relied on licensing ex-
aminations, board-style questions, or other closed-form assessment formats, which are informative
for measuring factual knowledge but less able to capture the inferential and case-based demands
of clinical practice [40, 41]. By integrating MCQs from gold-standard qualification examinations,
SAQs from officially recognized dental textbooks, and CBQs from peer-reviewed case reports pub-
lished in leading dental journals, GlobalDentBench extends evaluation from canonical knowledge
to multi-step clinical reasoning. The benchmark further introduces a comprehensive 14-discipline
taxonomy, informed by established dental discipline classifications, and a clinically grounded three-
level cognitive framework adapted from hierarchical models of cognitive complexity in assessment.

This design allows model performance to be interpreted in clinically meaningful terms rather than
by question format alone.
Second, GlobalDentBench was built for scalable benchmark construction. Its source materials
are heterogeneous in origin, file format and content structure, spanning qualification examinations,
officially recognized textbooks and clinical case reports. Manual standardization of such materials
would be difficult to sustain at scale. We therefore developed an automated agent pipeline that
normalizes documents, constructs samples according to question type, applies unified tagging and
performs final verification. This type-aware architecture allows heterogeneous clinical and educa-
tional materials to be converted into a consistent benchmark format, while preserving a unified
evaluation protocol. This design also makes the framework transferable to other medical specialties
where high-quality source materials are abundant but difficult to standardize manually.
Third, GlobalDentBench was designed as a trustworthy benchmark, with reliability controls
embedded throughout both construction and evaluation. MCQs and SAQs were extracted from
standardized examinations and canonical dental textbooks, anchoring these items to established
clinical knowledge sources.
CBQs required additional safeguards because they were generated
from unstructured clinical case reports.
To address this risk, six senior dentists manually au-
dited 523 of 1,590 CBQs, representing 32.89% of the CBQ corpus, and confirmed a validation
accuracy of 96.78%. The evaluation protocol was similarly constrained by question type: MCQs
were scored by direct matching, while SAQs and CBQs were assessed using a rubric-based judge
model selected through expert calibration.
Among the five candidate judge models, Gemini-3-
Flash-Preview achieved the highest dentist acceptance rate, at 98.15%, across a stratified audit
of 300 SAQ judgements and 1,500 CBQ key-point judgements, demonstrating strong alignment
with dental experts and supporting its use as a reliable automated evaluator. Together with 297
person-hours of senior-dentist oversight, these procedures establish GlobalDentBench as a clinically
grounded and methodologically reliable benchmark for dental LLM evaluation.
The clearest signal from GlobalDentBench is not simply that performance declined with task
difficulty, but where this decline occurred. Models performed strongly on MCQs (81.34%) and
retained moderate performance on SAQs (64.53%), but deteriorated sharply on CBQs (22.34%).
A similar gradient was observed across reasoning levels, with performance decreasing from L1
(74.01%) to L2 (55.64%) and further to L3 (35.71%). This pattern is consistent with recent dental
assessment studies showing that LLMs can perform well on structured examination-style questions,
yet remain sensitive to assessment format and task demands [7]. More broadly, it echoes medical
LLM studies showing that strong performance on benchmark or licensing-style questions does not
necessarily imply readiness for real-world clinical decision-making, where models must integrate
incomplete information, contextual constraints and competing clinical evidence [3, 42, 43]. Taken
together, our results suggest that current frontier LLMs can retrieve and organize canonical dental
knowledge, and can often handle relatively standardized forms of clinical reasoning, but remain
unreliable when reasoning must be individualized to complex patient cases. Because these higher-
complexity settings more closely approximate real clinical practice, current models should not be
regarded as suitable for autonomous dental decision-making. Their near-term role is better framed
as assistive: supporting knowledge access, preliminary interpretation and structured reasoning
under expert supervision, rather than substituting for clinical judgment.
Model-level comparisons suggest that current frontier LLMs remain limited by a shared bot-
tleneck in advanced clinical reasoning. Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview achieved the highest overall pro-
ficiency, whereas GLM-5 was the strongest open-source model, indicating that both proprietary
and open-source systems can perform reasonably well on lower-complexity dental tasks. This com-
petence, however, did not extend uniformly to more demanding forms of reasoning. Performance
declined stepwise from L1 to L3 across all 12 models, and no model exceeded 50% on L3. Al-

though Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview led on L1 and L2 and Claude-Sonnet-4.6 achieved the highest L3
score, the uniformly low L3 performance indicates that patient-specific individualized reasoning re-
mains a common limitation. This finding is clinically important because L3 tasks require models to
integrate contextual information, weigh competing clinical factors and generate case-specific judge-
ments, rather than retrieve knowledge or apply routine rules. The cost–performance analysis further
qualifies the deployment value of these systems. Higher inference cost did not consistently trans-
late into better overall proficiency. Among proprietary models, Gemini-3-Flash-Preview showed the
most favorable cost–performance profile, achieving a macro-average score close to the leading model
at a much lower cost. By contrast, Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview achieved the highest macro-average
score overall, but required substantially higher inference cost, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6 showed a
similar high-cost profile. Among open-source models, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5 offered the strongest
trade-offs: GLM-5 achieved the best open-source performance, whereas Kimi-K2.5 delivered com-
parable utility at the lowest cost. These results suggest that selected lower-cost models may be
suitable for supervised educational or assistive applications, but that current LLMs should not be
used autonomously for complex dental decision-making.
Performance heterogeneity across dental disciplines provides a specialty-level dimension. Oral
Mucosal Diseases (OMD) achieved the highest mean score and was the top-scoring discipline for all
12 models, whereas Orthodontics (Ortho), Pediatric Dentistry (PD) and Conventional Prosthodon-
tics (CP) formed the lowest-performing group. This pattern is consistent with emerging dental LLM
studies showing that performance varies across dental disciplines, rather than reflecting a uniform
capability in dentistry [41]. One plausible interpretation is that domains such as OMD, PPD and
OMR are more readily expressed through standardized diagnostic terminology, canonical disease
patterns and well-codified textual descriptions [44, 45, 46]. By contrast, orthodontic, pediatric and
prosthodontic decision-making often depends on spatial relationships, developmental stage, longi-
tudinal treatment planning, behavioural context, procedural design and patient-specific feasibility
constraints [47, 48, 49]. Therefore, the observed specialty-level disparities should not be interpreted
simply as differences in specialty difficulty. They suggest that LLM performance is partly shaped
by how well each clinical domain can be reduced to language-mediated reasoning. This reinforces
the need for specialty-aware evaluation, because aggregate scores can mask clinically important
pockets of weakness and may overstate readiness in domains where multimodal or longitudinal
reasoning is central.
The safety analysis further shifts the interpretation from model accuracy to clinical risk. Nearly
one-third of LLM-generated answers in case-based questions were classified as potentially unsafe,
echoing broader evidence that medical LLMs require evaluation not only for factuality and rea-
soning, but also for possible harm, hallucination, bias and clinical appropriateness [42, 50, 51, 52].
Importantly, unsafe responses were not distributed uniformly. S1 errors accounted for most un-
safe outputs and largely explained model-wise variation, whereas S2 errors were less frequent but
concentrated in clinically sensitive specialties such as Systemic Health, Pharmacology and Safety
(SHPS), Applied Medical and Basic Sciences (AME) and Pediatric Dentistry (PD). This distinc-
tion matters because a model may reduce its aggregate unsafe rate while still producing occasional
high-severity recommendations, and such errors are precisely those that carry the greatest clinical
consequence. Similar concerns have been raised in recent studies showing that medical LLMs re-
main vulnerable to hallucination, misinformation, adversarial perturbation and workflow-specific
failure modes despite strong benchmark performance [3, 11, 53]. These findings also align with re-
cent evidence that human–LLM collaboration in clinical medicine remains context-dependent and
should prioritize safety and error metrics rather than accuracy alone [54]. Taken together, the risk
results reinforce the central message of GlobalDentBench: current LLMs may support supervised
knowledge retrieval, but they should not be used as autonomous dental decision-makers. Clinical

deployment should require expert validation, specialty-specific guardrails and risk-aware evalua-
tion protocols that explicitly measure both the frequency and severity of unsafe LLM-generated
recommendations.
Several limitations remain in this study.
First, while the automated pipeline significantly
enhances scalability, LLM-induced hallucinations during dataset generation preclude fully au-
tonomous construction, necessitating indispensable human-in-the-loop verification.
Second, the
expert validation panel was sourced from a single clinical institution, which may inadvertently in-
troduce localized subjective bias into the consensus protocols. Third, the benchmark is currently
restricted to dental disciplines, somewhat limiting the immediate validation of this evaluation frame-
work’s universality across the full spectrum of medical specialties. Finally, GlobalDentBench eval-
uates models in a static, single-turn inference setting, whereas authentic clinical decision-making
relies on an iterative, multi-turn dialogue between practitioner and patient to resolve diagnostic
ambiguity.
Future work should advance this framework in four directions. First, automated quality control
should be further strengthened to reduce expert burden while preserving benchmark reliability.
Second, expert validation should be expanded across institutions and regions to mitigate localized
consensus bias. Third, and most importantly, this framework should evolve from a dentistry-specific
benchmark into a broader, community-driven evaluation ecosystem for medical AI. This will require
extending benchmark construction to additional clinical specialties, geographically diversifying ex-
pert participation across medical centers, and establishing multi-center consensus protocols for
case validation and adjudication. It should also create mechanisms for experienced clinicians to
contribute atypical, complex, and high-stakes real-world cases, thereby enriching the benchmark
with more challenging scenarios that better probe the capability boundaries of emerging models.
In this sense, the long-term goal is not simply to enlarge a dataset, but to build a scalable and
continuously updated infrastructure for rigorous evaluation across the broader medical spectrum.
Fourth, to better reflect clinical reality, evaluation should move beyond static single-turn inference
toward multi-turn settings that capture iterative clarification, incomplete information, and evolv-
ing patient context. Together, these directions would shift medical LLM assessment from narrow
benchmark performance toward a more realistic, generalizable, and clinically grounded evaluation
paradigm.
In conclusion, GlobalDentBench establishes a rigorous, globally representative evaluation frame-
work comprising 8,978 expert-validated questions to systematically assess LLM capabilities in den-
tistry, which has filled the critical void of multi-regional, full-spectrum assessment tools in dentistry
and established a standardized, reproducible methodological paradigm for evaluating large language
model capabilities in authentic clinical scenarios. Leveraging an automated construction pipeline
with high validation fidelity, our benchmarking of 12 frontier models reveals a stark capability
boundary. While frontier models achieve strong proficiency in standardized knowledge recall (L1
accuracy of 74.01%), they remain highly constrained in advanced clinical reasoning (L3 accuracy
of 35.71%). Consequently, the clinical integration of current LLMs must be strictly confined to
their role as human-supervised knowledge retrieval systems instead of independent clinical agents,
as their fundamental deficits in multi-step advanced reasoning currently preclude autonomous di-
agnostic deployment.
Looking ahead, the hybrid evaluation architecture introduced here provides a foundation for clin-
ically grounded evaluation of medical artificial intelligence. By aligning benchmark standards with
authentic clinical complexity, this framework can support more reliable model assessment, respon-
sible development and sustainable deployment of large language models across global healthcare
settings.

multiple rounds of periodontal curettage and antibiotics. Clinical findings include pulpal non-vitality, grade II mobility, a 14mm mesial probing depth, and radiographic
radiolucency at both the mesial and apical root surfaces, complicated by occlusal interference and a poorly designed partial denture. What is the definitive diagnosis
for this tooth, and what is the specific sequence of multi-disciplinary interventions required to ensure successful regeneration and long-term retention?
Reference Key Points for Evaluation:
1. The tooth was non-responsive to pulp vitality testing and exhibited radiographic radiolucency at the root apex, confirming an endodontic component to the lesion.
2. A 14mm infrabony defect was identified on the mesial aspect of tooth #26, necessitating a guided tissue regeneration (GTR) procedure using bone grafts and a
barrier membrane.
3. Vertical root fracture (VRF) was a primary differential diagnosis due to isolated deep pockets and recurrent abscesses, but it was ruled out after the tooth responded
successfully to endodontic treatment.
4. The treatment protocol required root canal therapy (RCT) to be completed and monitored for two months before initiating periodontal regenerative surgery.
5. Long-term success required the elimination of predisposing factors, including the removal of occlusal interferences and the replacement of a single-clasp denture
with a periodontal-friendly design.

• Taxonomy: Periodontal & Peri-implant Diseases.

Methods
GlobalDentBench is an auto-constructed and expert-validated benchmark for evaluating dental
knowledge and clinical reasoning in LLMs.
All source materials were collected from licensed
databases or publicly accessible resources recommended by senior dentists, including professionally
recognized licensed examinations, educational materials, and peer-reviewed case reports. Bench-
mark construction was conducted through an automated agent pipeline, with six senior dentists
participating in iterative pipeline refinement and final data validation. The involvement of these
senior dentists in the process spanned from February 5 to March 25, 2026, with a total contribution
of 297 person-hours.
4.1
Data Curation
GlobalDentBench was designed to assess the potential of LLMs in dentistry through the evaluation
of dental knowledge and reasoning capabilities. To support this goal, the benchmark was con-
structed from diverse source materials collected from multiple reliable channels, providing broad
coverage of different forms of dental knowledge and problem-solving tasks.
The source materials for GlobalDentBench were curated from reliable and authorized channels
to ensure clinical relevance and professional authority. These materials were selected to support
the construction of three distinct question types, providing broad coverage of dental knowledge
and reasoning tasks within a unified framework. The selection process was guided by senior dental
experts to prioritize high-quality, evidence-based resources.
• Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQ): Source materials for MCQs were derived from of-
ficially recognized national dental qualification and licensure examinations. These resources
represent the gold standard for foundational dental knowledge and clinical competency. To
ensure geographic and educational diversity, the corpus includes publicly accessible exam-
ination materials from multiple jurisdictions, specifically Australia, New Zealand, Canada,
India, the United Kingdom, and the United States.
• Short-Answer Questions (SAQ): Materials were drawn from authoritative dental text-
books identified through a multi-dimensional selection process. Core textbooks proposed by
senior dentists were filtered based on scholarly impact and external evaluations from academic
and commercial platforms. This process prioritized highly-rated publications such as Diagno-
sis and Treatment Planning in Dentistry and Dental Radiography Principles and Techniques,
covering foundational and specialized dental education.
• Case-Based Questions (CBQ): Source materials were obtained through a systematic
search of peer-reviewed journals published between 2020 and 2025 via the Scopus database.
The journal list was curated by senior dentists to reflect publications frequently consulted in
professional practice. The search targeted high-impact journals across major specialties, such
as The Journal of the American Dental Association and International Journal of Oral and
Maxillofacial Surgery. A substantial corpus of non-duplicate, peer-reviewed case reports was
collected to form the basis for complex clinical reasoning tasks.
To characterize the geographic diversity of the benchmark, all collected materials were fur-
ther assigned to countries or regions according to their source origin. When the origin could not
be directly determined, the assignment was based on the primary associated source, such as the
affiliations of the textbook author team or the case report author team. Country and region as-
signments were standardized according to the United Nations M49 classification, under which the

final corpus was verified to cover 88 countries and regions. Most source materials were obtained
in document-based formats, primarily PDF and Word files, and were subsequently passed to the
following pipeline stages for automated normalization and benchmark construction.
4.2
Agent Pipeline for Benchmark Construction
The source materials in this study were large in volume and diverse in origin, file format, and content
structure. As a result, manual processing by dental experts would be prohibitively labor-intensive.
In addition, existing automated pipelines are typically designed for only a single input structure,
which limits their applicability to such heterogeneous materials. To address this issue, the study
developed an automated agent pipeline (illustrated in Fig. 6A,B,C) to transform heterogeneous
raw materials into standardized benchmark samples. The pipeline supports multiple input sources
and file formats, and can automatically switch processing strategies according to document type.
This automated design substantially improves the scalability of benchmark construction and offers a
reusable framework that may be transferred to other specialized domains with limited modification.
The agent pipeline consists of three main stages, namely document normalization, type-aware
benchmark construction, and unified tagging with final verification. Together, these stages convert
heterogeneous raw materials into standardized benchmark samples with structured attributes and
built-in quality control. Details of each stage are described below.
Stage I: Document Normalization
(Fig. 6A) In the first stage, the pipeline employs a Re-
format Agent to normalize heterogeneous source materials before benchmark construction. As a
tool-using component, the Reformat Agent autonomously invokes multiple format-specific conver-
sion tools to process inputs from different sources. It accepts files in diverse formats, including
PDFs, images, office documents, text-based files, and web or markup files, and routes them to the
appropriate parser according to the input type. For example, XML files are parsed with Beautiful-
Soup to preserve their structural organization, whereas visually formatted materials such as PDFs
and images are processed with DeepSeek-OCR2 [55] to recover textual content. Most text-based
files are instead normalized using Pandoc, which supports robust conversion across heterogeneous
document formats. The extracted content is then uniformly transformed into markdown format
and stored in a buffer for downstream processing. At the same time, the Reformat Agent supple-
ments document-level metadata, such as title, source, author information, and other source-related
attributes. Through this process, the Reformat Agent establishes a unified intermediate represen-
tation and reduces the sensitivity of downstream benchmark construction to differences in source
formats.
Stage II: Type-aware Benchmark Construction
(Fig. 6B) Once buffered content becomes
available, the pipeline proceeds to the second stage, in which an Extract Agent constructs bench-
mark samples according to the predefined question type. To prevent truncation caused by context
window limitations, the buffered content is first divided into manageable chunks before extraction.
The Extract Agent then applies task-specific construction strategies. For MCQ materials, it re-
structures the source content into standardized multiple-choice items, including the question stem,
options, and reference answer. For SAQ materials, it extracts aligned question–answer pairs; when
the document structure is implicit, an additional structure extraction step is used to recover the
underlying organization before final extraction. For CBQ materials, the Extract Agent generates
clinically meaningful case-based questions from case narratives while simultaneously extracting five
key points for downstream evaluation. Through this type-aware construction process, heteroge-
neous source materials can be systematically transformed into a unified benchmark format.

## Page 19


To improve sample quality, all generated samples in this stage are processed through a Self-
Correction Loop. After each candidate sample is produced, it is verified against the source text
and the corresponding construction criteria.
Samples that fail verification are regenerated and
re-evaluated. Each sample is allowed up to three rounds of self-correction. Only samples that
pass verification are retained, whereas those that still fail after three attempts are discarded. For
CBQ materials, the verification process is more stringent, with particular emphasis on whether
the generated question and key points remain faithful to the source document, require clinical
reasoning, and are answerable without ambiguity.
Stage III: Unified Tagging and Final Verification
(Fig. 6C) After sample construction, all
MCQ, SAQ, and CBQ samples enter the third stage, in which a Tag Agent assigns structured labels
and coordinates final verification before inclusion in the benchmark. The Tag Agent annotates each
sample along two dimensions, namely dental discipline taxonomy and reasoning level. To improve
label stability, it adopts a multi-round consistency-based strategy. Specifically, two independent
rounds of tag prediction are first performed. If the two rounds agree, the labels are directly retained.
Otherwise, a third round of prediction is triggered, and the final labels are determined by majority
voting. The tagged samples then enter a final verification process with a Self-Correction Loop, in
which the pipeline checks whether the generated content remains faithful to the source material,
whether the assigned labels are logically consistent with the sample content, and whether the sample
satisfies the benchmark construction criteria. Samples that fail verification are regenerated and
rechecked for up to three rounds, and those that still fail are discarded. In parallel, senior dentists
participate in expert verification to further assess sample quality, label correctness, and clinical
appropriateness, serving as the final quality assurance step before the samples are incorporated
into GlobalDentBench.
4.3
Evaluation Protocol
As GlobalDentBench comprises three question types, namely MCQ, SAQ, and CBQ, which differ
substantially in answer format and required capabilities, question type-specific evaluation strategies
(illustrated in Fig. 6D) were adopted to ensure accuracy, consistency, and scalability. In general,
explicit-answer questions were evaluated by direct matching, whereas open-ended questions were
assessed using an automatic judge model. Detailed evaluation procedures for each question type
are described below.
Question Type-specific Evaluation
For MCQ questions, models were required to output only
the final answer option in order to evaluate their direct decision-making ability. For non-reasoning
models, the output was constrained to a single option. For reasoning models, intermediate reason-
ing was allowed, but only one final explicit option was retained as the answer. Model predictions
were then directly matched against the reference answers, and accuracy was reported as the evalua-
tion metric for this question type. For SAQ questions, models generated free-form natural language
responses. As the reference answers for these questions are often open-ended, simple string match-
ing is insufficient to accurately assess response quality. An automatic judge model was therefore
employed to compare model responses against the reference answers and determine whether each
response was correct or incorrect, based on which accuracy was computed. For CBQ questions, the
same judge model was used for automatic evaluation, but the scoring scheme was further refined into
a key-point-based scoring framework. Specifically, each question was associated with five predefined
key points, with each key point contributing 20 points. The judge model determined whether each
key point was correctly covered, rather than assigning continuous scores, thereby ensuring clear
19


## Page 20


and interpretable scoring criteria. Each question therefore had a maximum score of 100, and the
average score across all CBQs was reported as the final metric for this question type. In addition
to correctness evaluation, CBQ responses were further assessed through a safety-risk analysis to
characterize the potential clinical consequences of model-generated recommendations. Using the
dentist-calibrated automatic judging framework, each CBQ response was assigned to one of three
safety-risk categories: S0 indicated a clinically safe response, S1 indicated an unsafe response with
potential for reversible patient harm, and S2 indicated an unsafe response with potential for irre-
versible patient harm. This procedure enabled both performance-based and risk-aware evaluation
of LLM behaviour in case-based dental decision-making.
Evaluation Settings
All tested models were evaluated using a unified input template and evalua-
tion protocol, without model-specific prompt tuning or decoding adjustments. All models were eval-
uated using temperature=0.1, which is a common setting in LLM-as-a-Judge evaluations [9, 56].
Gemini-3-Flash-Preview was selected as the judge model mainly because of its stable performance
in answer consistency judgment and key-point coverage evaluation, as well as its favorable cost
efficiency, which makes it suitable for large-scale automatic benchmarking.
Evaluated LLMs
To ensure broad and representative coverage, 12 frontier large language mod-
els were evaluated, spanning both proprietary commercial systems and open-source models. The
proprietary commercial models included Gemini-3-Flash-Preview (google/gemini-3-flash-pre
view), Gemini-3.1-Pro-Preview (google/gemini-3.1-pro-preview), GPT-5.4 (openai/gpt-5.4
-2026-03-05), Grok-4 (x-ai/grok-4-07-09), Claude-Sonnet-4.6 (anthropic/claude-sonnet-4
.6), and Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro (doubao/doubao-seed-2-0-pro-260215), while the open-source
group comprised DeepSeek-R1 (deepseek/deepseek-r1-0528), Kimi-K2.5 (moonshotai/kim
i-k2.5), GLM-5 (z-ai/glm-5), Qwen3.5-Flash (qwen/qwen3.5-flash-02-23), Qwen3.5-Plus
(qwen/qwen3.5-plus-02-15), and MiniMax-M2.7 (minimax/minimax-m2.7). Such coverage
allows GlobalDentBench to evaluate performance differences across both licensing paradigms and
model capabilities, offering a more comprehensive view of dental knowledge understanding and
clinical reasoning in modern LLMs.
4.4
Expert-in-the-Loop Framework
To ensure clinical validity and professional rigor, GlobalDentBench was developed under a Dentist-
in-the-Loop framework. This governing structure integrated an expert team of six senior dentists
to provide oversight throughout the entire benchmark development process. These dentists are
all board-certified practitioners with an average clinical tenure of 6.8 years, possessing extensive
clinical backgrounds and specialized expertise. The participation spanned both the agent pipeline
and the evaluation framework, providing iterative expert supervision, validation, and refinement.
Rather than merely verifying model outputs, these experts contributed to systemic improvements by
identifying potential failure modes, assessing clinical appropriateness, and guiding multiple rounds
of workflow optimization.
Authentication of Data Sources
During the initial phase of development, the expert team
focused on the authentication of data sources and the formulation of a structured tagging tax-
onomy. This process involved the verification of licensed databases, educational materials, and
peer-reviewed journals to ensure that all source materials were authoritative and educationally
meaningful. By curating the data across diverse countries and regions, the experts ensured a global
representation of dental knowledge. Furthermore, a unified taxonomy system was established to

## Page 21


categorize samples by dental specialty and reasoning level, providing a clinically relevant framework
for subsequent performance analysis.
Optimization of Agent Pipeline
During benchmark construction, experts were deeply involved
in the validation and refinement of the agent pipeline. Iterative feedback from the expert team
was utilized to refine the systemic architecture, specifically targeting the logic of self-correction
loops and the underlying processing workflows. Accuracy and acceptance metrics were determined
through binary expert verification, where a sample was classified as correct only if the generated
content and underlying reasoning were in full alignment with professional medical logic. While
the automated model demonstrated strong capabilities in question extraction, all MCQ and SAQ
samples produced by the pipeline were subjected to full expert review. This validation confirmed
that most extraction errors were successfully filtered out during the self-correction process, resulting
in a final accuracy of 99.98%. For CBQ questions, experts addressed instances where the model
generated questions that were overly case-dependent. A 30% subset of the final CBQ dataset was
manually audited, achieving a clinical acceptance rate of 96.78%. This represented a significant
improvement over the initial 89.10% acceptance rate recorded prior to expert-guided architectural
adjustments, indicating that the systemic refinements successfully aligned the benchmark with
high-level clinical expectations.
Verification of Evaluation Accuracy
In the evaluation phase, expert participation focused
on supervising and calibrating the automated assessment framework. To support reproducibility
and future judge-model selection, we evaluated five candidate judge models with strong overall
performance and average inference costs below $10 per 1,000 queries (Fig. 2E), including Gemini-
3-Flash-Preview, Doubao-Seed-2.0-Pro, GPT-5.4, GLM-5 and Kimi-K2.5. Two dentists indepen-
dently reviewed randomly sampled grading records comprising 300 SAQ judgements and 1,500 CBQ
key-point judgements, with an additional 5% overlapping subset used to estimate inter-rater consis-
tency. Dentist acceptance rates for the judge models reached 98.15% for Gemini-3-Flash-Preview,
97.88% for Kimi-K2.5, 97.49% for GPT-5.4, 96.96% for GLM-5 and 94.05% for Doubao-Seed-2.0-
Pro, while dentist cross-validation agreement reached 96.67%. Among the evaluated candidates,
Gemini-3-Flash-Preview achieved the highest expert acceptance while maintaining strong cost-
effectiveness and top-tier overall capability, and was therefore selected as the default judge model
for all experiments. Kimi-K2.5 also demonstrated high expert acceptance and is recommended as
a strong alternative judge model for reproducible future studies.

The professional oversight and validation process represented
a significant, strategically deployed investment in quality assurance. This effort was conducted
over two distinct phases of the study.
From February 5 to March 8, 2026, the senior dentists
focused on the validation and refinement of the agent pipeline for benchmark construction, including
source authentication and taxonomy development. From March 8 to March 25, 2026, the focus
shifted to the refinement and verification of the automatic evaluation framework. Over the course
of 33 working days, an average daily contribution of 1.5 hours per expert was recorded.
This
collective human-in-the-loop involvement totaled 297 person-hours, ensuring that GlobalDentBench
is a professionally vetted instrument meeting the rigorous standards of the medical and dental
research communities.
