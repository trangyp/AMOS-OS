ABSTRACT
The neutral hydrogen (Hi) 21-cm line serves as a powerful tracer of the neutral interstellar medium (ISM). Thermal stability analysis
suggests that the neutral ISM is bistable in nature, consisting of the cold neutral medium (CNM) embedded within the warm neutral
medium (WNM), both in approximate thermal pressure equilibrium. When turbulence is incorporated into the numerical simulations,
a third thermally unstable medium (UNM) emerges between the CNM and the WNM. Although observational studies support the
existence of this intermediate phase, a clear empirical correlation between the fraction of the UNM gas and the strength of the tur-
bulence remains elusive. In this study, we investigate the various phases of neutral ISM using Hi 21-cm emission-absorption spectra
from the publicly available GWA and LAB surveys and compare it with TIGRESS-NCR and TIGRESS-CLASSIC numerical sim-
ulations. From our observational modeling, we find that 19.8% of the gas reside in the CNM phase, 32.5% in the UNM phase, and
47.8% in the WNM phase, assuming phase boundaries defined by spin temperature: Ts < 250 K for the CNM, 250 K < Ts < 5000 K
for the UNM, and Ts > 5000 K for the WNM. These results are entirely in agreement with the TIGRESS-NCR numerical simulation.
We further expect that deep, sensitive absorption studies with the Square Kilometre Array (SKA) or the Next Generation Very Large
Array (ngVLA) or existing Upgraded Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (uGMRT) capable of robustly detecting WNM clouds in
absorption will place more tighter observational constraints on the fraction of the gas in three different phases of the neutral ISM.

1. Introduction
Thermal stability analysis indicates that the neutral interstellar medium (ISM) is bistable in nature and consists of two major phases:
the clumpy cold neutral medium (CNM) and the more diffuse, widespread warm neutral medium (WNM) (Field 1965; Field et al.
1969; Wolfire et al. 2003). However, both observational and numerical studies provide evidence for an intermediate phase between
these two, referred to as the thermally unstable medium (UNM) (Heiles & Troland 2003b; Roy et al. 2013b; Stanimirovi´c et al.
2014). Although these three phases are primarily distinguished by their temperatures, the precise temperature ranges remain un-
certain. Recent observational studies suggest that the spin temperature (Ts) of the CNM is up to 250 K, followed by the UNM
phase extending up to approximately 4000−5000 K, and the WNM phase above this (McClure-Griffiths et al. 2023, and references
therein). The UNM, which lies between the CNM and the WNM, is thought to arise due to turbulent effects; where turbulence pre-
vents the complete condensation of WNM gas into the CNM phase, leaving a substantial fraction of gas in this intermediate regime.
This implies that the fraction of thermally unstable gas is strongly correlated with the level of turbulence (Audit & Hennebelle 2005;
Ho et al. 2024). To study gas clouds across these three phases (CNM, UNM, and WNM), the most commonly used tracer is neutral
hydrogen (Hi). The standard approach involves comparing Hi emission and absorption spectra observed along nearly the same lines
of sight. The absorption spectra are obtained toward background quasars, while the emission spectra are taken from nearby direc-
tions. Through decomposition of these spectra, one can derive various physical and kinematical parameters of the gas clouds, such
as column density (N[Hi]), spin temperature (Ts), upper limit of kinetic temperature (Tk,max), peak optical depth (τpeak), line width
(∆VFWHM), and central velocity (vc). While this technique has its limitations, it is currently considered as the standard method for
probing the kinematic and physical properties of gas clouds in the neutral ISM and has been widely used for several decades (Heiles
& Troland 2003a,b; Stanimirovi´c et al. 2014; Murray et al. 2018; Nguyen et al. 2019).
However, it may not always be possible to successfully decompose the emission and absorption spectra into multiple Gaussian
components. Several complications may arise that hinder the reliable identification of components, particularly those that are narrow
and associated with small spatial scales (Koley 2023). Cloud properties may also change between the emission and absorption lines
of sight, even when the spectra are observed toward nearby lines of sight. Moreover, joint decomposition of the emission-absorption
spectral line does not directly provide the kinetic temperature of the gas cloud; instead, it yields the spin temperature, which in

(Non-equilibrium Cooling and Radiation) numerical simulation performed by Kim et al. (2023) to assess whether the observational

2. Methodology
This iterative method critically relies on the thermal pressure profile, the proper turbulent scaling law, and the relation between spin
and kinetic temperatures in neutral ISM. We first outline how these quantities are adopted from previous studies before describing
the iterative method.
2.1. Thermal pressure
The value of the thermal pressure is obtained from the work of Jenkins & Tripp (2011). They studied the ionization balance of [Ci]
and [Cii] (spectra taken towards 89 bright stars) using the rotational temperature (Trot) obtained from the H2 molecule, and obtained
the log-normal thermal pressure (Pth) profile with a median value of ∼3800 K cm−3. In some cases where no direct measurements
of [Cii] were available, alternative tracers such as [Oi] and [Siii] were used. Since the detection rate of the H2 molecule is biased
towards the CNM clouds, this pressure profile is biased towards the CNM cloud. Although the thermal pressure ranges from ∼102.5
to ∼104.5 K cm−3, the lower and upper extremes were excluded at the end due to the theoretical lower limit for the CNM and the
exceptionally high pressures near the Hii regions (Wolfire et al. 2003). Excluding these regions reduces the lognormal tail and the
deviation from the Gaussian profile. It is evident from the works of Heiles & Troland (2003b) and Murray et al. (2018) that most of
the components that are observed both in emission and absorption lie below 500 K, implying that the majority of the components
belong to CNM gas clouds. The reason for this is that because of the low optical depth (τ) of the line, diffuse WNM gas clouds are
hardly detected in absorption. Consequently, it is appropriate to consider the value of Pth derived from the aforementioned work.
2.2. Turbulent scaling relation
The scaling relation of turbulence is equally critical for this iterative process. Isotropic hydrodynamic (HD) turbulence follows the
Kolmogorov law of turbulence, based on which the non-thermal velocity dispersion (σnth) varies with the length scale (L) with
a power law index (α) of 0.33 (Kolmogorov 1941; Frisch 1995). However, ISM, particularly the CNM phase, is compressible as
well as magnetized, which means that the power law may be different from the Kolmogorov law of turbulence. Theoretical and
simulation studies predict that, in a compressible magnetohydrodynamic system (in neutral ISM mainly in CNM phase), turbulence
perpendicular to the magnetic field—where energy is carried by Alfven waves—follows a power-law spectrum (α) similar to the
Kolmogorov turbulence (0.33). In addition, along the magnetic field, shock compression associated with slow modes leads to a
steeper power-law spectrum, closer to that of Burger law of turbulence (0.50) (Burgers 1948; Kritsuk et al. 2007; Kowal & Lazarian
2007; Federrath et al. 2010). When researchers study observationally the turbulent velocity dispersion along the line of sight, con-
tributions from both parallel and perpendicular components are combined. In addition, the observed spectra are density-weighted.
As a result of these effects the line of sight mixes the contributions from different turbulence modes.

Observational studies of the compressible interstellar medium particularly in the cold neutral medium and molecular clouds,
generally found a power-law index close to 0.5, consistent with Burger-like turbulence (Solomon et al. 1987; Heyer & Brunt 2004;
Koley 2023; Koley et al. 2025). For example, Koley (2023) using a simple analytical method (after taking the observed Hi emission-
absorption gas components of Hi from various large-scale surveys) showed that the intensity (A) and the power-law index (α) in
the CNM phase are 1.14 and 0.55, respectively. Likewise, Kalberla (2025) fitted the velocity dispersion of high-latitude CNM gas
clouds with the values of A = 1.2 and α = 0.37 and 0.50 obtained from Wolfire et al. (2003) and noticed that for α = 0.50, the data
set is fitted more accurately than α = 0.37. Based on all these results, it is likely that in the CNM phase, α is close to 0.50. For this
reason, we have decided to use the values of A and α from the work of Koley (2023) for this iterative method. This is because current
absorption studies primarily detect the CNM, as well as the lower-temperature portion of the UNM. We note that in Koley (2023), a
clear turbulence correlation is found above 0.4 pc, whereas no significant correlation is observed below this scale. In Appendix A,
we discuss the plausible reasons for this behavior. In our analysis, we adopt a single turbulence scaling relation for the entire data
set where the value of A is 1.14 km s−1 and α is 0.55.
2.3. Relation between spin and kinetic temperatures
Along with the thermal pressure and the turbulent scaling law, it is also essential to establish the relationship between the spin
temperature and the kinetic temperature of the gas clouds. The spin temperature Ts determines the population ratio between the
two hyperfine levels of the ground state of the Hi atom. Several mechanisms influence the population of these levels, including the
impact of Lyman-α radiation, collisions between Hi atoms, and collisions with electrons and ions. Using empirical formulations for
these processes, Liszt (2001) derived the correlation between Ts and Tk of Hi gas under typical interstellar medium (ISM) condi-
tions. It is important to note that these correlations depend on the thermal pressure (Pth) of the neutral ISM and the strength of the
Lyman-α radiation field (the Wouthuysen–Field effect, hereafter WF effect). In the CNM phase, collisions are sufficiently frequent
that Ts ≈Tk, and the effect of Lyman-α radiation is minimal. In contrast, within the WNM phase, Ts is significantly lower than Tk
in the absence of this effect, but strong Lyman-α radiation can raise Ts to values comparable to Tk (Field 1958; Seon & Kim 2020).
Consequently, Liszt (2001) computed the Ts–Tk relationship for different scenarios based on varying strengths of the Lyman-α field.
However, in most Hi absorption surveys, the majority of gas clouds belong to the CNM or UNM phase, where the Lyman-α radiation
has little influence (Heiles & Troland 2003b; Chengalur et al. 2013; Murray et al. 2018). Therefore, by combining the appropriate
thermal pressure, the turbulent scaling law, and the conversion factor between Ts and Tk, it is possible to determine the physical
properties of Hi gas clouds solely from the absorption spectra. In the following, we describe the outline of this iterative method.

3. Data Acquisition
All the fitted components of the Hi absorption spectra were obtained from the works of Roy et al. (2013b) and Patra & Roy (2024),
which we have listed in Table B in Appendix B. Observations were conducted with the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope (GMRT),
the Westerbork Synthesis Radio Telescope (WSRT), and the Australia Telescope Compact Array (ATCA) toward 37 radio-loud
quasars. In the following, this survey is called the GWA survey. Of these, 30 lines of sight were observed by Roy et al. (2013b,
hereafter NR13), and 7 by Patra & Roy (2024, hereafter NN24). For the GMRT observations, a total bandwidth of 0.5 MHz was
divided into 256 spectral channels, corresponding to a velocity coverage of approximately 105 km s−1 and a velocity resolution of
approximately 0.40 km s−1. The WSRT observations used a bandwidth of 2.5 MHz divided into 2048 channels, providing a velocity
resolution of 0.26 km s−1 for most sources; for two sources, 1024 channels were used instead, producing a velocity resolution of

4. Results from the Iterative Method
We applied the iterative method to the ongoing GWA survey, which contains a total of 256 decomposed gas cloud components along
37 lines of sight. Using this method, we derived all the relevant physical parameters for each component, including spin temperature,
kinetic temperature, column density, and characteristic length scale. After deriving the column density for each component from
the absorption spectra, we compared these values with the emission-derived column densities for each line of sight, specifically
quantifying the differences between the emission and absorption measurements. We performed this analysis for two cases: when
Ts < Tk (WF effect minimal) and Ts = Tk (WF effect maximum), derived from the theoretical model of Liszt (2001) A summary
of these results is presented in Table 1. We find that on average about 50% of the total column density is not detected in absorption
(in both cases when Ts < Tk and Ts = Tk). This missing fraction is mainly attributed to the Warm Neutral Medium (WNM) or the
higher-temperature components of the Unstable Neutral Medium (UNM), whose optical depths are relatively low and remain below
the detection limit of the GWA survey (Roy et al. 2013a; Patra & Roy 2024). We present a comparison of the emission and absorption
spectra for 37 different lines of sight in Fig. 3 for both cases. We note that, we divided the lines of sight based on the Galactic latitude
(b) to verify that the gas content is higher toward the Galactic plane and gradually decreases with increasing latitude. In a few cases,
particularly at high Galactic latitudes, the absorption spectra are significantly weaker than the emission spectra. The primary reason
is that noise in the absorption spectra makes it difficult to detect all gas components compared to the emission spectra (Roy et al.
2013a; Patra & Roy 2024). From this comparison, we find that most WNM components are not detected in the absorption survey.
We also compared this missing column density fraction with that in the SPONGE survey (Murray et al. 2018), where we find that
nearly 60% of the gas is not detected in absorption. This value is slightly higher than the missing column density derived from the
GWA survey. Below, we investigate the evidence for the non-detection of WNM in the GWA survey using two independent approaches.

averaging both cases, we obtain 19.75% of the gas in the CNM, and about 32.50% and 47.75% in the UNM and WNM phases,
respectively, in the neutral ISM. We note that the precise phase boundaries among the three phases are not well constrained by ob-
servations. Several studies suggest that, due to the effect of strong Lyman-α photons, the spin temperature of the UNM components
can reach 4000−5000 K, followed by the WNM component where the spin temperature can reach up to 8000 K (Field 1958; Seon
& Kim 2020; McClure-Griffiths et al. 2023, and references therein). In this study, we thus assume that the spin temperature of the
UNM component can reach up to 5000 K, followed by the WNM component. Bhattacharjee et al. (2024) summarized the gas phase
distribution for the SPONGE and Millennium surveys using both emission and absorption spectra. According to their investigation,
adopting phase boundaries of spin temperature as 250 K for CNM, 250−1000 K for UNM, and above 1000 K for WNM, they found
that in the SPONGE survey approximately 28% of the gas is in the CNM, 20% in the UNM, and 51% in the WNM phase. In contrast,
for the Millennium survey, about 40% is in the CNM, 24% in the UNM, and 36% in the WNM phase. Similarly to the SPONGE
survey, we also find that nearly half of the gas fraction resides in the WNM phase, which remains largely undetected in current
absorption studies.

5. Discussion
The concept of a two-phase neutral interstellar medium originated from the pioneering work of Field (1965). Their thermal stability
analysis and later Wolfire et al. (2003) demonstrated that two stable phases—the cold neutral medium (CNM) and the warm neu-
tral medium (WNM)—can coexist in an approximate thermal pressure equilibrium. Between these two phases lies an intermediate
regime known as the thermally unstable neutral medium (UNM). Gas in the UNM is unstable to small thermal perturbations: slight
compressions or rarefactions cause the gas to undergo runaway cooling or heating, driving it to condense into the CNM or expand
into the WNM.
The thermal equilibrium curve is obtained when radiative heating balances radiative cooling. The heating rate per unit volume
is proportional to the gas number density (n), while the cooling rate per unit volume scales with the square of the number density.
Thermal equilibrium is reached when the net cooling rate—given by the difference between the cooling and heating terms, L =
n2Λ(T) −cn is equal to 0. Λ(T) is the cooling function, which is only a function of temperature T and the constant c is the heating
rate per particle. The cooling function is constructed by combining the contributions from various cooling agents. The resulting
curve exhibits a characteristic shape: it rises steeply at low temperatures, increases more gradually over an intermediate temperature
range, and then rises sharply again at higher temperatures. This behavior is reflected inversely in the density–temperature equilibrium
curve and, consequently, in the pressure–density relation, where it gives rise to thermally stable and unstable regimes. The thermally
unstable medium satisfies the Field criterion (Clarke & Carswell 2014):

This condition implies that a small temperature decrease leads to enhanced net cooling (or reduced heating), causing the per-

the net cooling rate, which further lowers the temperature, leading to runaway condensation and causing the gas to transition into
the cold neutral medium (CNM). Conversely, a small increase in temperature reduces the net cooling efficiency (or allows heating
to dominate), which further raises the temperature, resulting in runaway expansion and driving the gas toward the warm neutral
medium (WNM). In more realistic simulations, the injection of turbulence generates velocity fluctuations that simultaneously in-
duce fluctuations in density and temperature. In regions of compression, the density increases, enhancing the net cooling rate; this
further lowers the temperature and, in the absence of other effects, would lead to runaway collapse into the cold phase. However,
turbulent mixing plays a crucial role in modifying this behavior. The eddy turnover timescale, which is approximately equal to the
turbulent mixing time, decreases from large to small spatial scales. When the turbulent mixing time scale becomes comparable to
or shorter than the local cooling timescale, the gas is continuously perturbed before it can cool and condense into the cold neutral
medium. A similar situation arises in rarefied regions, where expansion would otherwise drive the gas toward the warm phase.
As a result, a significant fraction of the gas can be maintained for a long time in the thermally unstable regime, despite classical
thermal instability predictions. In the thermally unstable medium, the adiabatic index (n

6. CONCLUSIONS
We investigate the gas phase distribution in the neutral interstellar medium using the publicly available GWA survey, which is pri-
marily based on Hi absorption observations obtained with the GMRT, WSRT, and ATCA telescopes and compare with publically
available Hi emission observations obtained from LAB survey. Using an iterative method, we derive the gas column densities from
the absorption spectra and compare them with the corresponding emission spectra. From this analysis, we find that 19.8% of the

their analysis, they found that below ∼0.4 pc, no significant scaling relation is observed. There are multiple reasons that can lead to
the breakdown of the power law. In the following, we outline the plausible reasons for this.
a) These observations are made mainly using the Arecibo single-dish telescope, which has a beam size of ∼3 arcminutes. This
corresponds to a physical scale of approximately 0.44 pc at a distance of 0.50 kpc or 0.87 pc at a distance of 1.0 kpc. Consequently,
tiny clouds that reside at a distance of ∼0.50 kpc with sizes below 0.44 pc or ∼1.0 kpc with sizes below 0.87 pc are not resolved.
Consequently, the beam fill factor f for these small components of the gas cloud is less than one, which reduces the brightness
temperature, while leaving the line width unchanged:
TB,obs = f TB,true.
(A.1)
For example, if f = 0.2 and TB,true = 23 K, then TB,obs = 4.6 K. From the work of Koley (2023), the median value of TB,obs is ∼23
K for the CNM clouds for which Llos > 0.4 pc and TB,obs ∼4 K for the CNM clouds for which Llos < 0.4 pc. Thus, the beam filling
factor can be a plausible reason for the decrease in brightness temperature. In the following, we provide a proof of this.
For a source whose size is less than the beam size, we obtain the integrated flux as follows:
S int(Jy) = S peak,obs(Jy beam−1) × Ωbeam,
(A.2)
where, S peak,obs is the peak flux density and Ωbeam is the beam size of the telescope. This integrated flux can also be expressed as
S int(Jy) = S peak,true(Jy beam−1) × Ωsource.
(A.3)
where, S peak,true is the true flux density if the source size was greater than the beam size and Ωsource is the source size. By comparing
the two expressions, we obtain the following:

Ωbeam

(A.4)

(A.5)
Here, f is the beam filling factor which is equal to Ωsource/Ωbeam.
