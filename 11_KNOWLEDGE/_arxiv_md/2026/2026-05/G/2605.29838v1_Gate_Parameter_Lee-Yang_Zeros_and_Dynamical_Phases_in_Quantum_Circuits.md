Gate Parameter Lee-Yang Zeros and Dynamical Phases in Quantum Circuits
Chang Liua, Yu Wub, Yunfeng Jianga,c,∗and Yang Zhangb,c,d†
aSchool of Physics & Shing-Tung Yau Center, Southeast University, Nanjing 211189, P. R. China

We propose gate-parameter Lee-Yang zeros of Loschmidt amplitudes as probes of dynamical
phases in finite quantum circuits.
We illustrate this approach using a brickwork model, where
the time evolution is generated by repeated application of a Floquet operator.
The Loschmidt
amplitude can be expressed as a rational function of the gate parameters. At fixed system size and
large circuit depth, its zeros in one complexified gate parameter, with the other parameter held fixed,
condense onto limiting curves. We show that these curves comprise a universal component governed
by equimodular Floquet eigenvalues, as described by the Beraha–Kahane–Weiss theorem, together
with state-dependent contributions controlled by the overlap of eigenstate of the Floquet operator
with the initial state. As one of the parameters is varied, the set of zeros reorganizes abruptly,
providing a finite-qubit diagnostic of a dynamical phase transition. This mechanism does not rely
on integrability: while integrability enables an exact calculation of the Loschmidt amplitude, the
condensation of zeros follows from spectral competition and local unitarity alone.
Introduction. Quantum circuit models provide a natu-
ral language for quantum computation [1] and for digital
simulation of quantum many-body dynamics [2, 3]. They
are now directly realized in programmable quantum pro-
cessors [4], where finite-size circuits have been used to
explore nonequilibrium many-body phenomena such as
robust bound states [5] and measurement-induced dy-
namics [6].
In parallel, circuit models have become a
theoretical laboratory for entanglement growth [7], oper-
ator spreading [8], monitored dynamics [9–11], and dy-
namical phases beyond equilibrium [12]. Exactly solvable
circuit families, including dual-unitary circuits [13, 14]
and Yang-Baxter-integrable Trotterizations [15–17], pro-
vide complementary settings in which analytic structure
can be exposed sharply.
In a typical circuit experiment, one controls a finite
number of qubits, prepares an initial state, evolves it by
a sequence of gates, and measures a transition amplitude
after a prescribed number of periods. This setting differs
from conventional condensed-matter systems, where one
usually starts from a Hamiltonian and takes the ther-
modynamic limit. For finite circuits with discrete time-
translation symmetry, transition amplitudes are natural
observables. A key question is whether such amplitudes
can diagnose sharp dynamical regimes without requiring
a thermodynamic limit.
We answer this question by proposing gate-parameter
Lee-Yang zeros of transition amplitudes as probes of dis-
tinct dynamical phases. Interpreting a transition ampli-
tude as a cylinder partition function, its zeros are analogs
of the Lee-Yang zeros of a statistical partition function
[18–20]. This viewpoint is related to, but distinct from,
the usual Loschmidt-zero formulation of dynamical quan-
tum phase transitions.
In that setting one fixes the
Hamiltonian or Floquet operator after a quench and ana-
lytically continues time. Fisher-zero lines in the complex-
time plane diagnose nonanalyticities of real-time rate
functions in the thermodynamic limit [21–26]. Here the
circuit depth is kept as the discrete parameter, while one
gate parameter is fixed and the other is complexified. We
therefore study zeros in a complex gate-parameter plane,
rather than zeros in complex time. At fixed system size
and in the large circuit-depth limit, these zeros condense
onto limiting curves with two distinct origins. One part
is state dependent and comes from overlap coefficients.
The other is universal: it is fixed by the Floquet spec-
trum and arises from equimodular eigenvalue branches,
as described by the Beraha-Kahane-Weiss (BKW) the-
orem [27]. As the circuit parameter is varied through a
critical value, this universal zero set reorganizes abruptly,
providing a finite-size diagnostic of a dynamical phase
transition.
We illustrate these features in the brickwork model
[15], which is an exactly solvable quantum circuit. Bethe
ansatz, together with algebraic-geometric methods for
summing over Bethe solutions [28–31], allows us to com-
pute the Loschmidt amplitude as a rational function of
the gate parameters.
The universal mechanism, how-
ever, relies on local unitarity and spectral equimodularity
rather than on integrability itself. Integrability is used
here as an exact computational tool, not as the physical
reason for the transition.

Gate-parameter Lee-Yang zeros.
The Loschmidt
amplitude is a rational function of q = eη and x = eξ/2
(see SM for explicit examples). It can be written as

of PΨ,n are zeros of the Loschmidt amplitude. The de-
nominator does not define additional Loschmidt zeros. In
the examples considered here it factorizes into an initial-

study the zeros of PΨ,n(q0, x) as a polynomial in the com-

Lee-Yang (GPLY) zeros of the Loschmidt amplitude.
We introduce the anisotropy parameter ∆= (q +
q−1)/2. Following the terminology of the XXZ spin chain,
we call |∆| < 1 and |∆| > 1 the massless and mas-
sive regimes, respectively.
We approach the transition
at ∆= 1 from q = eiγ on the massless side and from
real q > 1 on the massive side. Visible patterns already
appear for even relatively small n ∼L. For large n ≫1,
the zeros condense onto curves, which we call limiting
curves.

Discussions. We have shown that gate-parameter Lee-
Yang zeros of Loschmidt amplitudes provide a sharp
probe of dynamical phases in finite quantum circuits. In
general, there are two types of limiting zero curves: uni-
versal curves fixed by the Floquet spectrum and state-
dependent curves fixed by overlaps with the initial state.
For the brickwork model, the universal curves reorganize
at ∆= 1, producing a finite-size dynamical phase tran-
sition in the large circuit-depth limit.
The origin of the universal curves is spectral rather
than state specific. By the BKW theorem, equimodu-
lar Floquet eigenvalues generate limiting zeros indepen-
dently of the detailed overlap coefficients, provided the
relevant overlaps do not vanish identically.
Local uni-
tarity gives a simple geometric characterization of these
equimodular loci, explaining the unit circle in the massive
regime and the coordinate axes in the massless regime.
This mechanism is not specific to the simple brickwork
model and can be analyzed similarly to other models. In
SM, we perform a similar analysis for another integrable
quantum circuit. In fact, the mechanism does not even
rely on integrability. Integrability is used here to com-
pute the Loschmidt amplitude exactly and to expose the
structure cleanly; the same spectral mechanism should be
testable in weakly nonintegrable or fully chaotic circuits.
It is useful to contrast this diagnostic with the standard
DQPT use of Loschmidt zeros. Conventional DQPTs fix
the dynamics and study zeros in complex time, with real-
time nonanalyticities emerging after a thermodynamic
limit. Our construction fixes one circuit parameter and
studies zeros in the other gate parameter, with the sharp
structure emerging in the large-depth limit at fixed fi-
nite L. The two approaches are complementary slices of
the same broader analytic object, the Loschmidt ampli-
tude as a function of time/depth and circuit parameters.
Both are governed by competition between spectral con-
tributions, but they diagnose different projections of that
competition.
The diagnostic is naturally suited to present-day quan-
tum processors. Loschmidt amplitudes can be accessed
through interferometric protocols or related echo mea-
surements, and Lee-Yang zeros have already been in-
ferred experimentally from quantum coherence and full
counting statistics [46–48]. Partition-function zeros have
also been proposed as a route to many-body thermody-
namics on quantum computers [49]. Since the transition
studied here is formulated at fixed L and n, it does not
require extrapolation to a thermodynamic limit.
Several questions remain open. First, the finite-time
scaling of the zero density near ∆= 1 should be devel-
oped into a quantitative critical diagnostic, it would be
interesting to see whether the exponents are universal
and can be determined analytically. Second, in this pa-
