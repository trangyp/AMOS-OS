Ground state correlations in the one-dimensional Fermi one-component plasma
Massimo Boninsegni1, 2
1Department of Physics, University of Alberta, Edmonton, Alberta, Canada T6G 2H5
2Phenikaa Institute for Advanced Study, Phenikaa University,
Nguyen Trac Street, Duong Noi Ward, Hanoi, Vietnam
(Dated: May 27, 2026)
Structural and dynamic correlations in the ground state of the one-dimensional Fermi one-
component plasma are studied by Quantum Monte Carlo simulations. Results are presented for
the pair correlation function, static structure factor, the one-particle density matrix and the mo-
mentum distribution, for the cases of full, partial and no polarization. Evidence is reported of density
correlations slowly decaying with distance, with the concurrent emergence of quasi-crystalline order
even in the weakly correlated regime. Effects of quantum statistics in the momentum distribution
are discussed.
I.
INTRODUCTION
A system of spin-1/2 charged particles moving in the
presence of a uniform neutralizing background of op-
posite charge is possibly the most extensively studied
model in theoretical condensed matter physics. Known
as jellium, electron gas, or, as we shall refer to it here,
one-component plasma (OCP), this model is regarded as
foundational in the understanding of electronic proper-
ties of metals, or the effects of strong electron correla-
tions, collective phenomena such as plasmons, screening
and Friedel oscillations. As well, it is the basic build-
ing block of just about any computational framework
aimed at investigating real materials, such as Density
Functional Theory [1].
Interest in the one-dimensional Fermi OCP dates back
decades [2]; while in the past the study of many-body sys-
tems confined to one spatial dimension might have been
regarded as of merely conceptual, academic relevance,
there now exist experimental systems in which electron
motion is confined to one dimension, for practical pur-
poses [3–8].
Thus, a meaningful comparison of theory
and experiment is not out of the question.
From the theoretical standpoint, one spatial dimen-
sion occupies a special place within the many-body prob-
lem, as accepted theoretical frameworks that broadly de-
scribe general behavior in higher dimensions (e.g., Lan-
dau Fermi Liquid Theory, LFLT) no longer hold.
For
example, in one dimension effects of quantum statistics
are almost entirely suppressed by repulsive interactions
among particles [9]; for Fermi systems, this causes the
disappearance of a sharp Fermi surface, hence of the con-
ventional particle-hole excitations that are a cornerstone
of the LFLT.
A single unified description of one-dimensional (1D)
Fermi and Bose systems is provided by the Tomonaga-
Luttinger Liquid theory (TLLT), which affords a num-
ber of remarkably precise predictions [10–12]. In order
to test such predictions, a significant effort over the past
two decades has been devoted to the identification and
realization in the laboratory of (quasi) 1D many-body
systems.
The 1D Fermi OCP is particularly interesting because
it does not conform to the standard TLLT, due to the
long-ranged nature of the interaction, which results in the
absence of conventional low-lying elementary excitations
with a linear dependence of the energy on the wave vector
q, in the long-wavelength limit. Rather, the dispersion
curve takes the form ωq ∼q

ln(1/q) [13], i.e., there
is no well-defined speed c associated with the elemen-
tary excitations and therefore no characteristic parame-
ter K = c/vF (where vF is the Fermi velocity), which
governs the physics of conventional Luttinger liquids.
The 1D OCP has been predicted to display distinct
behavior, e.g., an enhanced tendency to develop quasi-
crystalline order even in the weakly interacting (high
density) limit [14]. It seems that these predictions could
be tested by Quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) simulations,
which have played an important role in shaping our cur-
rent understanding of the physics of the Fermi OCP, in
three [15–22] and two [23–25] dimensions. In particular,
deviations of numerical data from the most important
scaling predictions of the TLLT may be observed.
Somewhat surprisingly, however, relatively little has
been published so far for the 1D case, despite the fact
that in one dimension no sign problem arises, and vir-
tually exact numerical estimates can be obtained for all
the physically relevant quantities, including the momen-
tum distribution, embodying all detectable signatures of
quantum statistics. This is in stark contrast with two
and three dimensions, where QMC is affected by the well-
known “sign” problem, which limits its applicability and
generally requires some workarounds, typically involving
uncontrolled approximations (e.g., the fixed-node one).
The few QMC studies of the 1D Fermi OCP have been
based on variational [26, 27] and Diffusion Monte Carlo
[28] simulations. Although they have provided consider-
able insight, these calculations are inherently biased by
the specific choice of trial wave function utilized. In DMC
some of the bias can be removed; for the ground state en-
ergy, in principle an exact result can be obtained, as long
as a careful (and often costly) extrapolation is performed
with respect to the number of random walkers and pro-
jection time [29–31]. On the other hand, for other ob-
servables the bias arising from the trial wave function is

separate, independent QMC study of the ground state of

We illustrate in this paper the results of a theoreti-
cal investigation of the 1D Fermi OCP based on QMC
simulations. We make use of a finite temperature tech-
nique, and attempt to extract ground state (i.e., zero-
temperature) structural and dynamic correlations in the
thermodynamic limit by collecting results at sufficiently
low temperature. The standard procedure to achieve that
goal is that of collapsing data for varying temperature T
and systems size L, taking advantage of an exact scaling
property derivable within the TLLT [32–34], according
to which, in the limit L →∞and T →0, all physical
averages should depend only on the product LT.
Be-
cause the standard TLLT is not applicable, deviations
from this behavior are in principle expected. However,
at least to our knowledge it is not clear how quantita-
tively significant and/or readily observable they are in
practice, and, conversely, whether the standard TLLT
theory may still yield useful indications. We therefore
cautiously adopt the product LT to guide our analysis
of our numerical data. Our main purpose is to obtain
possibly new insight by providing estimates for quanti-
ties for which none are yet available; for example, while
the study of Ref. [28] is restricted to the fully polarized
case, here we also consider the partially polarized and
unpolarized cases, in order to expose how the momen-
tum distribution reflects the state of polarization of the
system.
This paper is organized as follows: in section II we in-
troduce the model and describe our methodology, present
our results in sec. III and outline our conclusions in Sec.
IV.
II.
METHODOLOGY
We consider an assembly of N identical spin-1/2
charged particles moving in a one-dimensional box of
length L with periodic boundary conditions. Let ζ be
the net system polarization, i.e., a fraction (1 + ζ)/2 of
the N particles have positive spin projection. Particles
interact via the standard pairwise, repulsive Coulomb
interaction, ∝1/r; moreover, a uniform background of
charge of sign opposite to that of the particles is in-
cluded, so as to ensure overall electric neutrality. We use
Hartree atomic units and characterize the density of the
system through the average interparticle distance, con-
ventionally expressed by the (dimensionless) parameter
rs ≡(L/2Na), a being the standard length unit (Bohr
radius).

We computed thermodynamic properties of the sys-
tem described by Eq. 1 by means of QMC simulations
at finite temperature (T), based on the canonical variant
[36, 37] of the continuous-space Worm Algorithm [38, 39].
We obtained results in the density range 1 ≤rs ≤20..
Details of the simulation are standard; we used a short-
time propagator which is accurate to fourth order in the
time step τ [40, 41]. We carried out numerical extrapo-
lation of the estimates to the τ →0 limit, and generally
observed convergence of the thermal averages for a value
of τ ≈10−3 rs2 Ha−1 for all quantities of interest here.
These include the energy per particle, the pair correla-
tion function and the one-body density matrix. Unlike
in DMC, these quantities can be computed using unbi-
ased estimators, not affected by a specific choice of trial
ground state wave function.
As mentioned at the outset, a QMC simulation of this
system is not affected by the infamous “sign” problem
that plagues studies of Fermi systems in two and three
dimensions. There is generally no sign problem in one
dimension, regardless of the interaction; in the specific
case of interest here, the repulsive core of the Coulomb
potential at short distance inhibits the crossing of world
lines, i.e., only the identity permutation contributes to
the partition function in the thermodynamic limit. This
de facto eliminates quantum statistics, and with that the
sign problem. It also implies that thermal averages of
observables such as the ground state energy as well as
structural properties such as the static structure factor
are independent of the polarization ζ. An important ex-
ception are off-diagonal properties such as the one-body
density matrix, and the related momentum distribution,
which display distinct signatures of quantum statistics
[9].

III.
RESULTS
We begin by discussing the energetics, not because it
is is of particular interest in and of itself but because
it allows one to make a quantitative, direct comparison
with other calculations, in order to check for consistency
and validate the methodology and the results presented.
As mentioned above, we aim at extracting ground state
(T = 0) results in the thermodynamic (L →∞) limit.
As mentioned above, the TLLT establishes that in the
limit L →∞, T →0 the values of all physical quantities
must depend only on the product LT (obviously, since
L is the system size and we work at constant density,
doubling L implies doubling N) [32, 33]. However, the
system of interest here ought not to conform to that, as
the standard TLLT does not apply, i.e., corrections to
such scaling must emerge in the low temperature limit,
though they may not be easily observable in a numerical
simulation.

The next quantity that we consider is the static struc-
ture factor S(q). This can in principle be computed from
the g(x) by carrying out a Fourier transform, on extend-
ing the g(x) outside the simulation cell, not to incur loss
of accuracy. This procedure becomes in our view prob-
lematic and ambiguous for rs even moderately large (i.e.,
≳2), as the g(x) features oscillations that die off quite
slowly with x. Thus, in this study we opted to compute
the static structure factor directly.

in higher dimensions interactions cause crystallization at

densities, a fact of which the momentum distribution (at
last computed as illustrated here) gives no hint.

IV.
CONCLUSIONS
We have carried out an extensive study, based on
computer simulation, of the ground state properties of
the one-dimensional electron gas (Fermi one-component
plasma), in the range of mean interparticle separation
1 ≤rs ≤20. This system is known not to conform to
the standard TLL theory [13], and it seems worthwhile
to investigate its low temperature properties based on
an unbiased numerical QMC technique which in one di-
mension does not suffer from any sign instability. To our
knowledge, there is only one previous comparable study,
based on a different (ground state) method [28], which
however only considered the fully polarized case.
Within the precision of our calculation we could not de-
tect any significant deviation of our results from the main
scaling prediction of the TLL theory; specifically, we gen-
erally observed collapse of the data in the L →∞, T →0
limit. The results quoted herein as “ground state” reflect
this observed behavior; they are generally in excellent
agreement with those of Ref. [28], as far as energetic and
structural properties are concerned.
Our results are consistent with Wigner crystallization
for any value of rs, as shown by a visible peak in the
static structure factor, whose height is a monotonically
increasing function of system size. Interestingly, distinct
signatures of quantum statistics remain, as shown by the
momentum distribution computed as a function of sys-
tem polarization. In other words, quantum-mechanical
exchanges which are known to play a crucial role in shap-
ing the phase diagram of many-body systems in higher
dimensions [42] but are suppressed in one dimension by
the repulsive nature at short distance of the Coulomb
interaction, nevertheless remain important, underscoring
how differences between phases are blurred in one dimen-
sion.
