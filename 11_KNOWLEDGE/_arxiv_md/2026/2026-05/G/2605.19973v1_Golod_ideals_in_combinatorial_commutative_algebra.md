GOLOD IDEALS IN COMBINATORIAL COMMUTATIVE ALGEBRA
BENJAMIN BRIGGS, TRUNG CHAU, AND ALESSANDRO DE STEFANI
Abstract. In this article we study the Golod property of standard graded algebras. We
show that determinantal ideals, binomial edge ideals, and permanental ideals are Golod if
and only if they have a linear resolution. Next, we give a characterization of when cover
ideals define Golod rings, exploiting some considerations on multidegrees of Koszul cycles
and Massey products.
Finally, we show that squarefree strongly Golod ideals (and, more
generally, lcm-strongly Golod ideals) are Golod, and not just weakly Golod.

then R is Golod. Moreover, if R is Gorenstein, then it is Golod only in this case, i.e., only if I

[28]. Our first main result shows that having a linear resolution is also a necessary condition
for several classes of well-studied rings. We remark that the equivalence between being Golod
and having a linear resolution was previously known for some classes of rings, e.g., Koszul
rings ([9, Remark 3.1]) or 2 × 2 sub-determinantal rings [34]. We remark that part (ii) of the
theorem below is a special case of [34, Theorem 1.1], which was obtained independently using a
different technique.

Theorem A. Let I be a homogeneous ideal in a polynomial ring over a field given by one of
the following:
(i) the determinantal ideal of a generic, generic symmetric, or generic Hankel matrix;
(ii) the binomial edge ideal of a finite simple graph;
(iii) the 2 × 2 permanental ideal of a generic matrix.
Then R = Q/I is Golod if and only if I has a linear resolution.
Another well studied class of ideals that we will give a full characterization for Golodness
is cover ideals of graphs (we refer to Section 3 for the exact definition). Cover ideals emerged
naturally as the Alexander dual of the better known edge ideals of graphs.
This led to a
systematic investigation of their homological invariants, ordinary powers, symbolic powers, and
asymptotic behavior [10, 14, 17, 18, 26, 27]. Interestingly, cover ideals have been established to
be counterexamples to conjectures due to their erratic behaviors [24, 35]. Somewhat surprisingly,
despite the large body of literature on cover ideals, a complete characterization of componentwise
linear cover ideals remains an interesting open problem (we refer to [18, 47] for partial answers).
What we prove is that, most of the time, cover ideals define Golod rings, despite not always being
componentwise linear. This marks an important difference with Theorem A, and contributes to
the literature yet another ground where the Golod property is ubiquitous.
Theorem B. For the cover ideal J(G) of a finite simple graph G, the following conditions are
equivalent:
(i) J(G) is not Golod;
(ii) J(G) is a complete intersection of two monomials of degree at least two;
(iii) J = Km,n is a complete bipartite graph with min{m, n} ≥2.
With the exception of special classes such as the ones listed above, establishing whether a
ring is Golod or not can be a very challenging task. In [32], Herzog and Huneke introduced a
notion for a homogeneous ideal I in a standard graded polynomial ring Q = k[x1, . . . , xn] over
a field k of characteristic zero: I is called strongly Golod if ∂xi(f)∂xj(g) ∈I for all f, g ∈I.
Exploiting an explicit technique to lift homology classes of TorQ
• (Q/I, k) to KQ/I, described in
[29], they showed that strongly Golod ideals have trivial product on the Koszul complex, and
hence they define Golod rings. A big advantage of considering the strongly Golod notion is that
it is a much easier condition to test than the one of being Golod. For instance, it allows to
deduce that, in the context of its definition, powers, symbolic powers and certain saturations
and closures of ideals are always Golod (see [32, Theorem 2.3, Theorem 2.11 and Proposition
2.13]). The notion of strongly Golod ideals was later revisited by Herzog and Maleki to treat
the case when k has positive characteristic [31]; however, for the sake of exposition, in this
introduction we will assume that k has characteristic zero. Note that being strongly Golod is
far from being a necessary condition for Golodness. For instance, squarefree monomial ideals are
essentially never strongly Golod. For this reason, Herzog and Huneke in the same paper defined
the notion of squarefree strongly Golod ideal, which applies to squarefree monomial ideals. The
difference with the original definition is that, if f, g are monimials in I, one has to test whether
∂xi(f)∂xj(g) ∈I only if such a product is squarefree. In [32, Theorem 3.5], they claimed that
squarefree strongly Golod ideals define Golod rings; however, their proof relied on an incorrect
result of Berglund and J¨ollenbeck (see [5, Theorem 3] and [37]). What can be deduced from their
proof, however, is that squarefree strongly Golod monomial ideals define weakly Golod quotients.

of being squarefree strongly Golod, and proved that lcm-strongly Golod ideals also define weakly
Golod quotients (see [12, Theorem 4.6]). In this paper, we slightly modify the notion of lcm-

2. First technique: Restriction and algebra retracts
Throughout this section, let k be an arbitrary field. Given two k-algebras R ⊆S we say
that R is a retract of S if there exists a k-algebra homomorphism φ : S →R such that φ(r) = r
for all r ∈R. When R and S are non-negatively graded with R0 = S0 = k, we further tacitly
assume that φ is homogeneous, necessarily of degree zero.
Lemma 2.1 ([41, Corollary 2.6]). Let R ⊆S be an algebra retract of graded k-algebras. If S is
Golod, then so is R.
Algebra retracts are well-studied objects in the literature (e.g. [16, 23, 41]). We will now
describe a well-known way to build algebra retracts in a polynomial ring Q = k[x1, . . . , xn].
For a set of variables S = {xi1, . . . , xik} we write Q|S := k[xi1, . . . , xik]. Let I be a homoge-
neous ideal of Q. Setting xj = 0 for any xj /∈S yields an algebra map Q →Q|S and therefore
an ideal IQ|S. Likewise there is an inclusion Q|S ⊆Q yielding an ideal I ∩Q|S. When these
ideals are equal we write
I|S := IQ|S = I ∩Q|S,
and we say that I|S is a restriction of I (with respect to S).
Said in words: an ideal is a
restriction of I if it is both generated by elements of I and it is obtained from I by setting some
variables equal to zero.
Lemma 2.2. If I|S is a restriction of I, then Q|S/I|S is an algebra retract of Q/I. In particular,
if Q/I is Golod then so is Q|S/I|S.
Proof. This follows from the observation that the composition
Q|S/I ∩Q|S →Q/I →Q|S/IQ|S
is the identity map if I ∩Q|S = IQ|S. The last statement is due to Lemma 2.1.

2.1. Determinantal ideals. As an application of the restriction process, we can describe all
Golod determinantal ideals of generic, or generic symmetric, or generic Hankel matrices. We
recall some terminology. Let k be a field; an m × n matrix X is called
• generic if its entries are algebraically independent indeterminates over k;
• generic symmetric if X is symmetric (in particular, this means m = n) and the entries
{xij}1≤i≤j≤n are algebraically independent indeterminates over k;
• generic Hankel if xij = xi+l,j−l for any i, j, l, and the entries {xij}i=1 or j=n are alge-
braically independent indeterminates over k.
A determinantal ideal of X is an ideal of k[X] that is generated by all t × t minors of X
for a given t. The following is well-known among experts, but we will provide a proof here for
completeness.
Lemma 2.3. Let k be a field, and X be a generic matrix (respectively, generic symmetric
matrix). If Y is a submatrix (respectively, symmetric submatrix) of X, then for any positive
integer t, the natural map k[Y ]/It(Y ) →k[X]/It(X) is an algebra retract.
Proof. First assume that X and Y are generic. It suffices to prove the claim when Y is X without
its last column. Then for any fixed positive integer t, any t × t minor of X, after specializing
the variables in the last column of X to 0, becomes either 0 (if the submatrix corresponding to
the minor has 0 as the last column) or a t × t minor of Y . In other words, It(Y ) is a restriction
of It(X), so the result the follows from Lemma 2.2. The case where X and Y are symmetric
follows by a similar argument, in which Y can be assumed to be X without its last row and its
last column.
□
We can now give a characterization of which determinantal ideals are Golod.
Theorem 2.4. Let X be an m × n generic, or generic symmetric (in which case m = n), or
generic Hankel matrix, and let t be an integer. Then the following holds.
(1) If X is generic, then k[X]/It(X) is Golod if and only if t = min{m, n} or t = 1.
(2) If X is generic symmetric (in which case m = n), then k[X]/It(X) is Golod if and only
if t ∈{1, n −1, n}.
(3) If X is generic Hankel, then k[X]/It(X) is Golod.
Proof. If t = 1, then k[X]/It(X) = k is a Golod ring. For the rest of the proof, we assume that
t ≥2, and m ≤n.
(1) Assume that X is generic. If t = m = min{m, n}, then k[X]/It(X) has a linear res-
olution, which can be seen from its minimal Eagon-Northcott resolution (see also [7,
Theorem 8.7.13]). Thus k[X]/It(X) is Golod by [28, Theorem 4], as desired. Now as-
sume that t < m. Let Y be the submatrix of X formed by its first m columns. Since
Y is square, k[Y ]/It(Y ) is Gorenstein, and since 2 ≤t < m, it is not a hypersurface.
Therefore, k[Y ]/It(Y ) is not Golod, which implies that k[X]/It(X) is not Golod by
Lemmas 2.2 and 2.3.
(2) Assume that X is generic symmetric (in which case m = n). If t ∈{n −1, n}, then
k[X]/It(X) has a linear resolution ([13, Theorem 1]), and thus is Golod by [28, Theorem
4]. Now assume that 2 ≤t < n −1. If n −t is even, then k[X]/It(X) is Gorenstein,
and since it is not a hypersurface, it is not Golod either, as desired. Now assume that
n −t is odd. Let Y be the square submatrix of X formed by its first n −1 rows and
n −1 columns. Then k[Y ]/It(Y ) is Gorenstein, and thus is not Golod since it is not
a hypersurface. This implies that k[X]/It(X) is not Golod by Lemmas 2.2 and 2.3, as
desired.

An explicit description of all hypergraphs whose edge ideal is Golod seems out-of-reach at
this moment. A description of all hypergraphs whose edge ideal is lcm-strongly Golod in terms
of forbidden induced sub-hypergraphs can be theoretically obtained from Lemma 5.1, a task that
is easier with more imposed conditions. Theorem 5.5 (3) is a complete list of forbidden induced
sub-hypergraphs when the hypergraph in question is assumed to be 2-uniform, i.e., every edge
is of cardinality two. Taking path ideals or connected ideals of a graph is a generalization of
taking edge ideals, and each such action corresponds to a special hypergraph constructed from
a finite simple graph. In this direction, one may ask whether Theorem 5.5 can be generalized
accordingly for such hypergraphs, leading to the following:
Question 5.7. For each integer t ≥3, what are all finite simple graphs that admit a lcm-strongly
Golod t-path ideal/t-connected ideal?
It is important to note that, under suitable conditions (e.g., finite simple graphs), the full
family of forbidden induced sub-hypergraphs for the property of Golodness is an infinite one,
while that for the property of lcm-strong Golodness is finite.

Lemma 5.1. This phenomenon carries over to the cases of path ideals and connected ideals,
