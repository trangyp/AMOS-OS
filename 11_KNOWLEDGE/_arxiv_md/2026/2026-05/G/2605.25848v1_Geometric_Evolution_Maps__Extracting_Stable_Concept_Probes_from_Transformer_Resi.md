Abstract
Concept probes extracted from transformer residual streams are only as reliable as the layer from
which they are extracted. The common practice of probing at a fixed late layer or at the peak of a
separation score function ignores a fundamental structural feature: concept representations undergo
substantial directional rotation during their assembly phase, and do not settle into a stable direction
until a characteristic handoff layer after the primary Concept Allocation Zone (CAZ). We introduce
Geometric Evolution Maps (GEMs), which track the full directional trajectory of a concept through
residual stream activations, identify the handoff layer where rotation ceases, and extract the settled
probe direction from that layer. Across 23 architectures spanning 70M to 14B parameters and 17
concept types, the entry-to-exit cosine similarity within CAZs has a mean of 0.233, showing that
probe direction at CAZ entry does not reliably predict probe direction at exit. Ablation experiments
across 391 concept × model pairs (23 models × 17 concepts) show that GEM-extracted probes
are at least as precise as peak-layer probes in 268/391 trials (68.5%), and strictly outperform in
259/391 (66.2%; §9.3). The architecture split is pronounced: MHA models favour the handoff in
173/221 trials (78.3%); GQA models favour the handoff in only 56/119 trials (47.1%). A Wilcoxon
signed-rank test on per-model handoff-preference proportions (N=23 models) gives W=214, p=0.010
(one-sided; excluding gpt2, N=22: p=0.0036) — significant at the model level. An adaptive ablation
width rule (w=1 when HL/N > 0.85) targets the 79/391 near-final-layer cases (20.2%): it improves
probe quality in 60/79 triggered cases (75.9%), with a mean gain of +7.44pp for those cases (overall
mean across all 391 pairs: +0.93pp, dominated by the 312 non-triggered pairs where the rule is
inactive); a depth-corrected variant (N ≥20 required) excluding gpt2 (N=12) and pythia-1b (N=16)
retains 53/62 triggered-case improvements at mean delta +6.80pp. A direction-specificity control
across 111 base-model pairs and 16 models confirms that the ablation effect is carried by the concept
direction specifically: concept-direction ablation suppresses separation at a median of 377× the
rate of random-direction ablation (mean 45.6% vs. 0.24%; 99.1% of concept directions beat all
10 random seeds). A depth-matched control experiment across 374 concept × model pairs (29
models) confirms that the GEM settling criterion contributes +32.5pp mean additional suppression
beyond a post-CAZ layer at equal relative depth but without the settling criterion (88.0% of pairs;
§5.5), ruling out depth-of-extraction as a confound. One structured failure is documented: gpt2
(ablation at the handoff layer pathologically increases concept separation in 13/17 pairs, due to
depth proximity to unembedding computations) — an architecture-specific pattern, not a GEM
flaw. OPT-6.7b, initially identified as a failure mode in a smaller corpus (7/17 = 41%), shows
13/17 = 76% at N=250 — its earlier below-chance rate did not replicate at full corpus size (§7).
GEM provides the extraction methodology underlying the CAZ Validation and PRH papers in this
program.

1. Introduction
A linear probe of a transformer’s residual stream is only meaningful at a layer where the concept
has been fully assembled and stably encoded. The central question this paper addresses is: which
layer is that?
Three naive answers appear in the literature. The first is the final layer, on the grounds that
information must be maximally processed there. The second is the peak of a separation score
function — the layer where a contrastive probe scores highest. The third is a fixed middle-to-late
depth selected by hyperparameter search or convention.
Each of these approaches misses the same structural reality: concept representations in transformer
residual streams are not static between layers. They emerge gradually, rotate substantially during
their allocation zone, and then — if the concept is well-formed in that model — settle into a direction
that remains stable through the remaining depth. We call the transition point the handoff layer:
the layer at which the concept direction finishes rotating and locks in.
This paper introduces Geometric Evolution Maps (GEMs), a method for tracking the directional
trajectory of a concept through the residual stream, identifying the handoff layer, and extracting
the settled concept direction as the probe. The core empirical finding is that probing at the handoff
layer more often outperforms probing at the separation peak, with the advantage concentrated in
larger models, because the settled direction after the handoff is the direction that has stabilized for
downstream use.

The mean EEC across our dataset is 0.233 (median 0.216). 93.9% of pairs have EEC < 0.5,

construction, since the CAZ is defined to include it), extracts a direction that has not yet settled.
The validation in §5 therefore compares a post-assembly probe (GEM at LH) against an in-assembly
probe (peak at Lpeak) — not against the best-possible layer, but against the standard alternative.

1.3 Scope and Relationship to Other Papers
GEM addresses the probe extraction step. It presupposes that CAZs — the zones where concept
directions are allocated — have been identified. The CAZ Framework paper (Henry, 2026a) provides
that detection methodology. GEM is introduced in §4.4 of the CAZ Framework paper as the
extraction answer; this paper provides the full treatment.
The CAZ Validation paper [Henry, 2026c] uses GEM’s ablation protocol — the handoff layer LH as
the ablation target (§3.2) — for its causal experiments; concept directions in that paper are centroid
differences at each layer, not GEM settled-direction probes stored and reused. The PRH paper
[Henry, 2026d] uses peak-layer DOM vectors (the normalized centroid difference at the argmax
of the separation curve S(l)) as the concept directions aligned across architectures via Procrustes
rotation — not GEM settled-direction probes, which require the full handoff-tracking pipeline.

2. Background
2.1 Probing Transformer Representations
Linear probing in transformer representations is well-established (Conneau et al., 2018; Tenney et
al., 2019; Belinkov, 2022). The typical approach trains a linear classifier on frozen layer activations
and interprets high accuracy as evidence that the concept is encoded at that layer.
Two methodological concerns motivate our work. First, layer selection in probing is often ad hoc.
Many studies probe the final layer, cite the token representations “most relevant” to the task, or
sweep a range and report the best. This conflates where a concept is stored with where a probe can
best recover it — the latter may simply reflect where the contrastive pairs most differ in surface
form.
Second, even studies that probe multiple layers typically extract a single direction (the probe weight
vector) and treat it as the concept representation. If the concept direction changes substantially
across layers, a single probe captures only a slice of that trajectory.
GEM addresses both concerns by computing the trajectory of the concept direction and selecting
the extraction layer principled on when that trajectory settles.
2.2 Concept Allocation Zones
The CAZ Framework [Henry, 2026a] characterizes the layer range within which a concept undergoes
its primary separation event — the interval from first significant above-noise separation through the
separation peak and back to a floor. CAZs are detected via Fisher-normalized separation score S(l),
with region boundaries defined by velocity v(l) = dS/dl zero-crossings, and scored by a composite
function (prominence × coherence boost ×
√
width). A CAZ is not the concept itself — it is a
depth-localized region of the residual stream in which the model organizes its geometry to serve a
concept [Henry, 2026a].
A critical property of CAZs relevant here: they describe where a concept is allocated, but not the
direction of the resulting representation. The peak separation layer within a CAZ is not necessarily
the layer at which the settled representation is available — peak separation may occur while the
concept is still actively rotating between attention sub-structures, before the direction has converged
to its final orientation.
2.3 Concept Directions and Rotation
The dominant direction of a concept at layer l is the first principal component of the matrix of
activation differences between positive and negative pairs at that layer. As l increases from the
shallow to deep layers, this direction is not constant. In models that exhibit CAZ dynamics, the
direction typically:
1. Is undefined or noisy before the CAZ onset (near-chance separation)
4

2. Begins to crystallize within the CAZ, showing increasing separation and initial directional
coherence
3. Rotates substantially during the CAZ — successive layers show high angular velocity
4. Reaches a stable, high-coherence direction at the handoff layer
5. Maintains that direction through the remaining depth with slow drift
Steps 3–4 are the core phenomenon GEM is designed to detect. Our analysis shows this rotation is
not subtle: the mean entry-exit cosine across our corpus is 0.233, with maximum per-layer rotation
of 0.31.

3.2 Handoff Layer Detection
The handoff layer LH is operationally defined as:
LH = min(LCAZ_end + 1, N −1)
where LCAZ_end is the final layer of the detected CAZ region — operationally, the last consecutive
layer at which angular velocity ω(l) = 1 −|u(l) · u(l−1)| > ε with ε = 0.05 (the threshold mentioned
in §9.3 is therefore the CAZ-end criterion, not a separate handoff criterion; LH is derived from
LCAZ_end, not detected independently). Conceptually, this is the first post-assembly layer: the CAZ
end criterion detects the point where angular velocity drops below ε, which is the same event as the
concept direction completing its primary rotation. The layer immediately after is where the settled
direction first becomes available.
The cosine similarity between u(LH) and u(N−1) — the handoff cosine — has a mean of 0.942 and
median of 1.000 across the GEM corpus (88% of pairs exceed 0.85; range 0.31–1.00), confirming
that the settled direction is stable through the remaining depth for most pairs. The high median
(1.000) reflects the N=250 finding that handoff layers cluster near the final layers of most models,
where little further drift occurs. Low-handoff-cosine outliers (below 0.5) arise predominantly in
concepts with atypical post-handoff processing.
3.3 Adaptive Ablation Width
When evaluating probe quality via ablation (§5), we ablate w consecutive layers starting at LH.
The default is w = 3. For near-final-layer handoffs (where LH/N > 0.85), ablating 3 layers can
extend into the final-layer unembedding-preparation zone, diluting the concept signal and producing
spurious results. For these cases, we apply the near-final rule: when LH/N > 0.85, set w = 1.
The empirical effect of this rule is characterized in §5.2.
3.4 The Settled Direction as Probe
The settled direction u(LH) is the GEM-extracted concept probe. It is a unit vector in Rd representing
the direction in residual stream space that most discriminates positive from negative pairs at the
layer where that discrimination has stabilized.
To use this probe for downstream tasks (concept detection, steering, PRH alignment), it is extracted
from the settled-layer activations and is not retrained on new data. Its validity rests on the claim
that the concept has converged at LH — it represents the model’s internal encoding of the concept
after assembly is complete.

3.5 Contrastive Pair Construction
Pairs are constructed using the multi-model consensus protocol described in Appendix C. Each
positive sentence expresses the target concept in context; each negative is drawn from a pool
of semantically proximate but concept-absent sentences, with pairs retained only when multiple
independent models agreed on the concept label (the cross-model consistency filter serves as the
ambiguity exclusion criterion).
For the 17 concepts in this paper’s corpus (agency, authorization, causation, certainty, credibility,
deception, exfiltration, formality, moral_valence, negation, plurality, sarcasm, sentiment, specificity,
temporal_order, threat_severity, urgency), pairs were drawn from the Rosetta Concept Pairs
dataset (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.20059650). Pair counts range from 183 to 200 per concept (mean
197); variation is per-concept, reflecting available corpus size in the dataset, not per-model filtering.

5. Validation
Corpus design. Both §5.1 and §5.2 use the 23-model, 391-pair primary corpus (Appendix
A). Results are directly comparable.
5.1 Handoff Layer vs. Peak Layer: Ablation Experiment
Setup. For each concept × model pair, we extract two probes: (1) the GEM-extracted settled
direction u(LH) and (2) the peak-layer direction u(Lpeak), where Lpeak is the layer of maximum
separation score. We then apply directional ablation at each respective layer and measure the
fractional reduction in concept separation (the retained percentage after ablation, lower = better
ablation = stronger probe).
The ablation operator removes the concept direction from all residual stream activations at the
probe layer: for activation h ∈Rd, the ablated activation is h′ = h −(h · u) u, which zeroes the
component along u while preserving all orthogonal components.
Formally, for probe direction u extracted from layer l∗, an orthogonal projection Pu is applied
to all residual stream vectors at l∗, and separation is re-measured at that layer.
A handoff
improvement is recorded when the handoff probe achieves lower retained percentage (greater
separation suppression) than the peak probe. Note that each probe is evaluated at the layer from
which it was extracted; the baseline separation at LH and Lpeak may differ, so retained percentages
are normalized within-layer and are comparable as relative measures but not as absolute suppression

## Page 10


magnitudes across extraction layers. Depth confound: LH is always ≥Lpeak (the handoff layer is
post-CAZ; the peak is inside the CAZ). The handoff probe improvement rate could partly reflect
a depth-of-extraction advantage — later layers carry more processed information — rather than
directional settling per se. The direction-specificity control (§5.3) provides partial evidence against
this: random-direction ablation at the same handoff layers produces mean suppression of 0.24%,
while concept-direction ablation at those same layers produces mean 45.6% — indicating that depth
alone is not driving the suppression. If depth were the primary factor, random directions at that
depth would also suppress concept separation; they do not. A depth-matched control (comparing LH
against a post-CAZ layer at the same relative depth, without the GEM settling criterion) directly
isolates the settling contribution from any residual depth effect; that experiment is reported in §5.5,
where it confirms the settling criterion’s contribution (+32.5pp mean advantage, 88.0% of 374 clean
pairs across 29 models).

5.4 Multi-Node Relay Structure
Motivation. The preceding validation treats each concept’s GEM as a single node — the handoff
layer identified by the angular velocity threshold. In practice, many concepts have multiple GEM
nodes: the CAZ Framework analysis (Henry, 2026a) finds a mean of 3.4 CAZ peaks per concept per
model, and each peak can produce a detectable handoff. A multi-node concept raises a structural
question: are the nodes independent encodings of the same concept (redundant), or is each node
causally upstream of the next (relay)? The answer determines whether GEM is documenting a
distributed relay or selecting among redundant alternatives.
Setup. We ran a full permutation ablation on pythia-6.9b (32 layers, 17 concepts): for every
concept with multiple GEM nodes, we tested every non-empty subset of nodes (2ˆN −1 subsets per
concept). For each subset we applied directional ablation simultaneously at all nodes in the subset
and measured separation reduction at every node’s handoff layer and at the final layer. This yields
three derived quantities per concept: (1) which single node drives final-layer separation most; (2)
whether ablating all nodes together exceeds the best single node (synergy); and (3) whether ablating
only the shallow nodes reduces separation at the deepest node’s handoff layer (cross-disruption),
which would establish causal relay rather than independence. N_PAIRS = 50, width = 1 at each
handoff layer.
Node count distribution across 17 concepts: 2 nodes in 12 concepts, 3 nodes in 3 concepts (causation,
exfiltration, negation), 1 node in 2 concepts.
Results.
Deepest node dominates (15/15): In every multi-node concept, the single ablation that produces the
greatest final-layer separation reduction targets the deepest GEM node. The empirical selection
rate is 100%, providing post-hoc confirmation that GEM’s handoff layer — which, in pythia-6.9b,
is consistently the deepest detected node — is the most causally concentrated single intervention

point.
Cross-disruption (9/15 concepts > 0.05): Ablating only the shallow nodes reduces separation at
the deepest node’s own handoff layer in 9 of 15 multi-node concepts, with a mean cross-disruption
of 0.11 among those cases. The shallow nodes are causally upstream: disrupting them partially
disassembles the input the deep node receives. This is relay structure, not independence.
Apparent anti-synergy (mean −0.058, 0/17 positive): Ablating all nodes simultaneously produces
less suppression than the best single node alone. This result cannot distinguish relay structure
from redundancy without the adaptive-direction protocol (§9.4). The ablation patches
use settled directions computed from unablated activations. When an upstream patch is applied,
the representation flowing into downstream layers shifts — the downstream patch then targets a
direction calibrated to the unperturbed model, which no longer accurately describes the shifted
representation. This patch-interference artifact artificially deflates multi-node ablation relative
to single-node, producing apparent anti-synergy. The correct reading, consistent with the cross-
disruption finding, is a causal relay in which upstream disruption weakens but does not misdirect
downstream assembly. Resolving true synergy versus redundancy requires re-estimating the settled
direction at each downstream node after upstream patches are applied (adaptive-direction protocol);
that experiment is deferred pending compute availability.
Single-node ceiling: Even at the deepest, most causally concentrated node, single-node ablation
does not approach complete suppression. Consistent with the broader ablation results (§5.1, mean
retained percentage 32.8% for improved cases), the multi-node permutation confirms no single
intervention reaches a 1.0 separation reduction in any concept. This is consistent with a relay model:
the deepest node carries the most concentrated signal, but not all of it.
Cross-architecture comparison. A parallel dependency analysis across 999 CAZ pairs in 25 base
models (Henry, 2026c §6.2) finds that 42% of CAZ pairs are forward-dependent (shallow-to-deep
causal direction; 0% backward-dependent). In MHA architectures, upstream ablation cascades
to 100% of dependent downstream nodes; in GQA architectures, cascade rate is 43%. We ran
the full permutation protocol on two additional models to characterize whether relay topology is
architecture-dependent: GPT-2-XL (MHA, 48 layers, 1.5B parameters; 12 multi-node concepts)
and Qwen2.5-7B (GQA, 28 layers, 7B parameters; 14 multi-node concepts). GPT-2-XL replicates
the pythia-6.9b MHA pattern exactly: cross-disruption in 10/12 concepts, mean 0.122; apparent
anti-synergy mean −0.040, 0/12 positive. Qwen2.5-7B (GQA) shows the opposite profile: cross-
disruption in 13/14 concepts, mean 0.324 (≈3× MHA); genuine positive synergy mean +0.047, 10/14
positive. The GQA result is opposite to the prediction from P3 cascade rates — despite GQA having
fewer forward-dependent CAZ pairs overall (43% cascade rate), GQA relay disruption is stronger
per relay and synergy is positive. The reconciliation: GQA grouped key-value sharing concentrates
concept routing through fewer attention paths; when a relay exists, there are fewer redundant routes
through which downstream layers can absorb upstream disruption, so cross-disruption propagates
fully and nodes cooperate rather than substitute. In MHA, broader routing redundancy distributes
disruption across more paths, weakening per-relay cross-disruption and masking cooperative synergy
behind patch-interference artifacts. Relay topology is architecture-dependent in both its quantitative
profile and its sign.
Interpretation. GEM does not find the end of a relay and call it the concept. It maps the relay as
a whole: each GEM node is a handoff event in a causal sequence, and the permutation ablation
characterizes which events are necessary, which are upstream of which, and where the signal can or
cannot be reconstituted after disruption. The deepest node is the most causally loaded point in the

node in a structure, not the structure itself.
5.5 Depth-Matched Control
Motivation. The §5.1 comparison (handoff vs. peak) cannot fully isolate the GEM settling criterion
from a depth-of-extraction advantage: by construction, LH ≥Lpeak, so any benefit from probing at a
later, more-processed layer favours the handoff regardless of whether directional settling contributes.
The direction-specificity control (§5.3) limits this concern — random directions at the handoff layer
suppress separation at only 0.24%, confirming the effect is carried by the specific settled direction
— but does not directly compare GEM against a non-settling alternative at the same depth. The
depth-matched control provides that comparison: for each (model, concept) pair, we select a control
layer at the same relative depth as LH but chosen without the GEM settling criterion, and compare
GEM handoff suppression against control-layer suppression. If depth explains the handoff advantage,
a depth-matched non-settling layer should achieve comparable suppression; if the settling criterion
adds value beyond depth, GEM should outperform.
Setup. For each concept × model pair with existing ablation_gem data (the stored handoff ablation
result), we: (1) enumerate all post-CAZ candidate layers in [LCAZ_end + 1, N −1], excluding LH

post-CAZ candidate exists are skipped; (3) extract the concept direction at the control layer as the
L2-normalized centroid difference (the same DOM vector used by GEM); (4) ablate the concept
direction at the control layer and measure final-layer retained percentage, comparing to the stored
GEM handoff result. Control directions are computed from the same N=250 contrastive pairs as
the GEM probe.

6. Scale Analysis
6.1 GEM Structure Across Scale
GEM structure — the presence of a detectable handoff layer — is present at all scales tested,
including pythia-70m (70M parameters). The rotation phenomenon (§4.1) is not a large-model
emergent property: even the smallest models show mean EEC < 0.35.
However, probe quality (as measured by ablation depth) improves with scale. The improvement is
not monotonic within architectures but is consistent across the scale ladders we have data for.
Within the Pythia scale ladder (70M →160M →410M →1B →2.8B →6.9B →12B), mean
ablation depth follows an uneven but increasing trend. The most notable jump occurs between 1B
and 2.8B, where the near-final rule becomes relevant: pythia-2.8b shows 5 concepts with HL/N >
0.85 (compared to 6 in pythia-1b), and the corresponding settled probes are substantially cleaner.
pythia-12b extends this pattern: 7 near-final concepts, all showing strong settled-direction probes.
Within the Qwen2.5 scale ladder (0.5B →1.5B →3B →7B →14B), the EEC pattern is
non-monotonic: Qwen2.5-1.5B (0.392) and Qwen2.5-3B (0.365) show notably elevated EEC relative
to the 7B (0.257) and 14B (0.232) endpoints, suggesting that more rotation is occurring before
settling at the 1.5B–3B range, not less. This is counterintuitive and warrants investigation: higher
EEC may indicate a different assembly pattern (faster settling from a less-differentiated initial
direction) rather than weaker GEM structure.
6.2 The 410M–1B Emergence Window
From the adaptive width data, a consistent pattern emerges around 410M–1B: this is the scale at
which multiple concepts first begin to show near-final-layer handoffs (HL/N > 0.85) that respond to
the adaptive width rule. Below this range, handoffs tend to occur earlier in relative depth, where
the near-final rule does not apply and the concept direction is already stable at width 3.
This aligns with the scale threshold observed in the CAZ Framework paper’s ablation analysis:
above approximately 1B parameters, single-layer ablation at the concept peak is insufficient to
suppress concept separation, consistent with concepts having distributed, multi-layer representations
rather than single-layer peaks.

7. OPT-6.7b: From Failure Mode to Recovery
7.1 Initial Finding and Reversal
In the initial corpus (N < 250 contrastive pairs per concept), OPT-6.7b showed the lowest handoff
improvement rate: 7/17 improvements (41%), at or below the null expectation of 50% and below the
corpus mean. This was interpreted as a structured failure mode related to low directional rotation
during assembly — several OPT concepts showed high entry-exit cosines (temporal_order: 0.627,
sarcasm: 0.807, sentiment: 0.436, plurality: 0.369), suggesting concepts converged to their final
direction before the handoff and left no improvement for the GEM probe to capture.
At N=250, this finding does not replicate. OPT-6.7b achieves 13/17 = 76% improvement
at full corpus size — above the corpus mean (66.2%) and comparable to the Pythia scale ladder.
Among comparably-sized models at N=250: Mistral-7B-v0.3 achieves 65% (11/17) and Qwen2.5-7B
achieves 47% (8/17); OPT’s 76% is the highest of the three 7B models. The original below-chance
rate was corpus-size-dependent and should not be generalized.
7.2 Structural Profile Remains Distinctive
OPT’s high-EEC profile remains a structural observation: several concepts genuinely rotate less
during assembly than in Pythia or Llama-family models. This is a real property of OPT’s architecture
and training. However, the high-EEC observation evidently does not prevent GEM from identifying
productive handoff layers at N=250 — either the higher statistical power at N=250 resolves marginal
improvement cases, or OPT’s behavior shifts when concept signals are estimated more precisely.
This result is methodologically informative: the N=250 rerun reversed a corpus-dependent artifact
while leaving the EEC structural profile intact. GEM’s sensitivity to OPT’s behavior at N=200
vs N=250 should be treated as a calibration note, not as evidence that OPT is architecturally
anomalous. N-stability was not systematically verified across the corpus: the EEC distribution,
scale-wise improvement rates, and architecture cohort split were each measured once at N=250,
which is the definitive corpus. The OPT reversal is the only case where a smaller-N finding was
directly compared to N=250 data.
7.3 gpt2 Remains a Genuine Failure Mode
gpt2’s ablation failure is architecturally distinct from the OPT case and replicates across corpus
sizes. In 13/17 pairs, ablation at the handoff layer pathologically increases concept separation rather
than suppressing it. The root cause is depth: gpt2’s 12-layer architecture places the handoff in the
penultimate layer (HL/N ≈0.92), where ablation engages unembedding-preparation computations
rather than concept directions. GEM correctly detects this as a near-final-width case; the near-final
rule (§5.2) is designed to flag models with N < 20 layers for this reason. gpt2 is the genuine
structured failure; OPT-6.7b is not.
8. Relationship to Existing Probe Methods
8.1 vs. Fixed-Layer Probing
Fixed-layer probing (typically the final layer) ignores the concept’s assembly dynamics entirely. The
final layer is not the handoff layer: GEM handoffs occur at mean relative depth 0.78 (median 0.94)
with N=250 pairs; specificity, plurality, and negation hand off substantially earlier (mean 61.2%,
17


## Page 18


71.7%, and 69.6% depth respectively), while most other concepts settle in the final 10–20% of model
depth.
That GEM handoff cosines to the final layer average 0.942 (median 1.000; §3.2) does not diminish
the method — it documents that for most concepts in most models, the direction stabilises before
the final layer and remains stable through the end. GEM’s primary deliverable is the trajectory
map: the rotation profile, handoff depth, and relay structure (§5.4) are architectural findings that
no single-layer extraction can supply. The method characterises the full depth of the residual stream
and identifies where assembly occurs, not merely where to sample. The MHA vs. GQA cohort
split — 11/13 MHA models preferring the handoff over the CAZ peak versus 2/7 GQA models
— is a product of trajectory analysis and would be invisible to any single-layer comparison. For
early-handoff concepts, GEM provides a probe the final layer cannot; for late-settling concepts,
GEM confirms stability and characterises how that stability was reached.
A direct ablation comparison of GEM vs. final-layer probes is not included in this paper’s validation
experiments — GEM’s claim is about extracting the settled direction and characterising the
trajectory, and the framework’s use case (concept steering, cross-architecture alignment) requires an
unsupervised directional extraction rather than a trained classifier. A final-layer vs. handoff-layer
ablation comparison is a natural future benchmark. Similarly, supervised linear probes trained
on labeled activations are a different method class — they require held-out labels and cannot be
used in the PRH alignment or steering contexts that motivate GEM; the relevant comparisons are
therefore other unsupervised directional methods (peak-layer, delta PCA, windowed PCA), which
§8.2–8.3 address.
8.2 vs. Peak-Layer Probing
Peak-layer probing extracts the direction at the layer of maximum separation score. This is better
than fixed-layer probing, but the separation peak typically falls inside the CAZ — at a layer where
the concept direction is still rotating. The GEM probe, extracted at the handoff layer (typically
shortly after the CAZ peak), is more stable and achieves deeper ablation.
8.3 vs. Delta PCA / Windowed PCA
Methods that compute PCA on activation differences over a window are computing something similar
to GEM but without the trajectory analysis. The key distinction is that windowed PCA selects
the window by maximizing variance explained, not by detecting rotational settling. In practice this
tends to select windows centered on the separation peak rather than on the post-handoff stable
region.
8.4 vs. Sparse Autoencoder Features
SAE-extracted features represent individual circuits rather than concept-level abstractions. In
the companion validation paper [Henry, 2026c], we show that SAE features accumulate signal at
layers identified by CAZ detection (Gemma Scope cross-validation, 17 concepts: best positive cosine
agreement 0.51–0.84 across all concepts at CAZ peak layers; four cazellar layers — L11, L15, L17,
L19 — confirmed by both methods, each with 6 concepts and 25 polysemantic features). GEM and
SAE methods are complementary: GEM extracts the aggregate concept direction, SAE decomposes
it into circuit-level features. The settled direction from GEM should be expressible as a weighted
sum of SAE features active at the handoff layer.

9. Discussion
9.1 Why Handoff Layers?
The handoff phenomenon has a natural architectural interpretation. In transformer models, the
residual stream accumulates information from attention heads and MLP sublayers as residual
additions. One untested hypothesis, consistent with transformer architecture but not examined
here, is that a concept assembling via multiple attention heads requires several layers for their
contributions to integrate — each head adds a partial update, and the concept direction converges
once all relevant heads have contributed. GEM detects the geometric settling event at the residual
stream level without decomposing it into attention vs. MLP contributions; that decomposition
remains an open question (§9.4).
A handoff_cosine of 1.0 (seen in many pythia-2.8b concepts) is consistent with an abrupt settling
event, but equally consistent with a plateau that began before LH; the mechanism is not resolved
by this data.
The relay structure characterised in §5.4 offers a complementary framing. GEM identifies the
handoff layers where concept assembly produces a detectable settling event — but these events are
embedded in a continuous process. Every layer of a transformer makes some contribution to every
concept representation; what GEM finds are the layers where that contribution is large enough to be
geometrically organised, directionally stable, and causally concentrated enough to be isolated. The
handoff layer is not the location where the concept lives; it is the location where the accumulated
relay signal is most accessible to interrogation and intervention. This distinction matters for any
downstream use of GEM probes: a steering or ablation intervention at the handoff layer reaches the
most causally loaded event in the relay, but it does not reach everything.
9.2 Safety-Relevant Concepts Handoff Deep
Among the deepest-handing-off concept types in this corpus — threat_severity (0.860), certainty
(0.865), deception (0.874), and temporal_order (0.878) — threat_severity and certainty have
plausible safety and epistemic relevance. Their deep handoff layers are consistent with requiring
more model depth to assemble, though the difference from the mid-band is smaller than in pilot
data. Behavioral ablation experiments would be needed to evaluate whether late handoff correlates
with functional importance.
A notable structural observation: Figure 1 shows that certainty and threat_severity rotate least
across the corpus (high EEC, low within-CAZ rotation), yet they also hand off latest.
This
demonstrates that EEC and handoff depth are independent dimensions. A concept can assemble
without dramatic directional rotation and still require late-layer processing — the CAZ occurs deep,
the rotation within it is modest, but the settled direction is only available after that deep CAZ.
EEC measures how much a concept rotates during assembly; handoff depth measures when the
assembly occurs; the two are not redundant.
These findings are thematically adjacent to the gentle CAZ hypothesis, which proposes that low-score
allocation zones are nonetheless causally active; a direct connection would require comparing GEM
handoff depths to CAZ assembly scores for the same concept × model pairs. In this three-concept
sample, deep handoffs correlate with safety-relevant concept labels; whether this pattern holds
across the broader concept space is not established here.

9.3 Limitations
Corpus scope. The current corpus covers 23 base models up to 14B parameters, 17 concept types,
and 250 contrastive pairs per concept. The corpus was expanded from an initial 16-model set (prior
preprint) to include pythia-1.4b, gpt2-large, gpt2-xl, opt-1.3b, gemma-2-2b, Llama-3.1-8B, and phi-2.
Models above 14B, instruct-tuned variants, and mixture-of-experts architectures are not included in
the primary corpus.
Ablation methodology. The ablation experiments measure separation suppression at the probe
layer, not downstream behavioral change. We are measuring the quality of the direction in the
residual stream at the handoff layer, not whether blocking that direction changes model outputs.
Behavioral ablation experiments (the behavioral ablation script) are designed but not yet run at
scale. A structural confound exists: since LH > Lpeak by construction, benefits from probing a
deeper, more-processed layer are not fully separable from benefits due to directional settling. The
direction-specificity control (§5.3) limits this concern — random directions at the handoff layer
return ≈0 suppression, confirming that the effect is carried by the specific settled direction rather
than by depth per se — but a depth-matched control comparing LH against a post-CAZ layer at
the same relative depth (without the settling criterion) provides a cleaner isolation; that experiment
is reported in §5.5, where it confirms the settling contribution (+32.5pp mean advantage, 88.0% of
374 clean pairs across 29 models).
Near-final rule. The gpt2 failure mode (§5.2) reveals that the simple HL/N > 0.85 threshold
is insufficient for very shallow models. A depth-corrected rule (HL/N > 0.85 AND N ≥20) is
recommended in practice but was not the rule evaluated in this paper’s ablation statistics.
Handoff detection threshold. The angular velocity threshold ϵ = 0.05 was set empirically
by inspecting EEC distributions on a small development set (pythia-70m and gpt2); no formal
sensitivity analysis was performed, and “stable across a range of reasonable values” is a qualitative
impression rather than a tested claim. Readers should treat ϵ as a fixed implementation detail
rather than an optimized parameter.
OPT-6.7b. In the initial corpus, OPT showed below-chance improvement (7/17 = 41%); at N=250,
this did not replicate — OPT achieves 13/17 = 76% (§7). The high-EEC structural profile remains a
real observation; whether smaller OPT variants (OPT-125M through OPT-2.7B) share it is untested.
Scope of ablation claims. The 45.6% mean separation reduction in §5.3 is not the fraction
of concept information residing at the handoff layer. It is the fraction accessible from a single
surgical intervention at the most causally concentrated location GEM can identify. The remainder
is distributed across the residual stream at layers that contribute continuously and at levels below
the detection threshold of allocation-event methodology. We would not be surprised if every layer
makes some contribution to every concept — what CAZ and GEM identify are the layers where
that contribution is large enough to be isolated, directionally organised, and causally testable.
Any single-layer intervention, of any type, operates on a slice of a continuous process. That the
deepest GEM node achieves mean 45.6% suppression from one slice — and that shallow-node cross-
disruption reaches the deepest node in 9/15 multi-node concepts — is what makes the identified
events scientifically useful. It is not a complete account of concept encoding, and we do not claim it
to be.
Statistical independence. The 391 trials in §5.1 are not exchangeable — 17 concepts per model
produce correlated outcomes, as the per-model improvement rates demonstrate directly (Llama-
3.1-8B: 17/17 = 100%; Pythia-1.4b: 17/17 = 100%; Qwen2.5-14B: 4/17 = 24%). A Wilcoxon

9.4 Open Questions
• Does the near-final rule reflect a genuine model-boundary phenomenon or a measurement
artifact of the unembedding layer’s influence on residual streams?
• What is the contribution of attention vs. MLP sublayers to the handoff event? GEM detects
it at the residual stream level but does not decompose it.
• Do instruct-tuned models show different handoff depths or rotation profiles for the same
concept? (RLHF Geometry paper question)
• Is the elevated EEC in Gemma-2 models (gemma-2-2b: 0.372; gemma-2-9b: 0.283) architec-
turally explained by the alternating attention paradigm?
• Adaptive-direction rerun (§5.4): Re-estimating the settled direction at each downstream
GEM node after applying upstream patches would resolve whether the apparent anti-synergy
is entirely a patch-interference artifact or whether some genuine redundancy exists. This is
the key experiment for characterising the relay’s true topology.
• Cross-architecture relay topology: Does GQA’s lower cascade rate (43% vs. 100% MHA
in the P3 dependency analysis) manifest as systematically lower cross-disruption in the
permutation ablation? If so, the relay topology is architecture-dependent in a quantifiable
way, and GEM provides the tool to characterise it.
• Relay completeness: What fraction of total concept signal lies outside detectable GEM
nodes? A lower bound could be estimated by comparing the separation suppression achieved
at the deepest GEM node against the theoretical maximum from full-layer projection onto the
concept subspace.
10. Conclusion
Geometric Evolution Maps provide a principled, tractable method for extracting stable concept
probes from transformer residual streams. The central finding — that concept directions rotate
substantially during assembly (mean entry-exit cosine 0.233) and settle into a stable representation
at a characteristic handoff layer (mean handoff cosine 0.942) — motivates why probing at the
separation peak is suboptimal, and is consistent with the hypothesis that probing at a fixed final
layer also misses the settled direction — though no ablation comparison to final-layer probing is
included in this paper’s validation experiments (§8.1).
GEM-extracted probes are at least as precise as peak-layer probes in 268/391 cases (68.5%): 259 strict
outperformances (66.2%) and 9 exact ties; model-level Wilcoxon W=214, N=23, p=0.010 (significant;
excluding gpt2, N=22: p=0.0036); see §9.3. The architecture-level split is now statistically clear:

11/13 MHA models prefer handoff versus 2/7 GQA models (Fisher p=0.022). The scale breakdown
(<500M: 47%, 500M-3B: 71%, >3B: 70%) shows a genuine <500M underperformance beyond
the gpt2 artifact — excluding gpt2, <500M is 61% (31/51) versus 71%/70% for larger models.
Improvement is particularly pronounced when the handoff occurs in the final 15% of model depth.
The adaptive width rule improves probe quality in 60/79 triggered cases (75.9%), with a mean
improvement of +7.44pp for cases showing improvement and an overall mean of +0.93pp across all
391 primary corpus pairs (triggered + non-triggered; see §5.2, Table 4).
gpt2 demonstrates the one replicating structured failure mode: ablation pathology from unembedding-
proximity at 12 layers. OPT-6.7b’s initially below-chance rate (7/17 = 41% in a smaller corpus)
reversed to 13/17 = 76% at N=250, showing the earlier finding was corpus-size-dependent rather
than an architectural invariant. A result that would falsify GEM on rotation-present architectures
would be systematic failure even where EEC is low — that pattern is not observed in our corpus.
The permutation ablation (§5.4) establishes that GEM nodes participate in a causal relay: the
deepest node is the most causally concentrated single intervention point (15/15), but shallow nodes
are causally upstream of it (cross-disruption in 9/15 multi-node concepts), and no single intervention
approaches complete suppression. GEM is a framework for finding and characterising the allocation
events in that relay — the layers where concept assembly produces a geometrically organised,
directionally stable signal large enough to be isolated and tested. It is not, and does not claim to
be, a complete account of how concepts are encoded. Concept information is distributed across
every layer of the network; the relay events GEM identifies are the peaks of that distribution, not
its entirety. Any intervention — ablation, steering, or otherwise — that acts at a single layer
reaches one event in a continuous process. That the identified events are causally real, architecture-
systematically distributed, and scale-sensitive is what GEM establishes. What lies between them
and below detection threshold remains open.

Appendix C: Contrastive Pair Construction
Pairs are drawn from the Rosetta Concept Pairs dataset (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.20059650). Each
pair consists of a positive sentence (strongly expressing the target concept) and a negative sentence
(same topic, concept absent), constructed via a multi-model consensus protocol: for each concept
and topic, multiple large language models from the Claude, GPT, Gemini, and Mistral families
independently produced candidate pairs, and pairs were retained only when all contributing models
agreed on the concept label (unanimous agreement across the generator models that produced the
pair — typically 3–5 models per topic, drawn from 14 generator models spanning four AI labs
(Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, Mistral); the exact model set varies by concept and topic as recorded in
the Rosetta Concept Pairs dataset metadata). This cross-model consistency filter is the operational
ambiguity exclusion criterion — pairs where any model assigned a different label were discarded.
Passages are paragraph-length (approximately 150–350 tokens). Each pair shares the same topic
and surface register, differing specifically along the target concept dimension. The maximum pair
count per concept is 200, clamped to however many consensus-validated pairs are available; the 17
concepts in this paper yield 183–200 pairs each (mean 197), with per-concept variation reflecting
corpus size rather than per-model filtering (the same pair set is used for all models).
Activations are extracted as the last-token representation from the post-MLP residual stream output
of each transformer block (the full block output, after both attention and feed-forward sub-layers),
using bfloat16 forward passes and float32 metric computation, via the shared extraction tooling
for this research program (Henry, 2026). Models are set to inference mode (no dropout). Passages
are tokenized using each model’s default tokenizer with default BOS token handling; no system
prompt, instruction prefix, or task framing is prepended. For models distributed across multiple
GPUs under device_map="auto", cross-device tensor operations are handled explicitly.
