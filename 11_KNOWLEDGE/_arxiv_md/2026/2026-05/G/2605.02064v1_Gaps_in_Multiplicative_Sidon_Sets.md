Abstract. For a positive integer n, let g(n) denote the infimum of all real numbers L such that
there exists a multiplicative Sidon set A ⊆{1, 2, . . . , n} that intersects every interval [x, x+L] ⊆[1, n].

every n ∈N. We first show that this estimate does indeed hold, with a proof that was autonomously

1. Introduction
With N the set of positive integers, we say that a set A ⊆N is a multiplicative Sidon set if all
products aa′ with a, a′ ∈A and a ≤a′ are distinct. Naturally, the set of prime numbers is such a set,
from which we deduce that there exist multiplicative Sidon sets in [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n} with at least
π(n) integers. Erdős [4, 5] proved that this is the right asymptotic and determined the correct order
of growth of the error term. More precisely, he showed that the maximum size of a multiplicative
Sidon set contained in [n] is

On the other hand, much less is known about gap sizes in
multiplicative Sidon sets. In this regard, Sárközy [8, Problem 34] asked the following questions in
his 2001 list of unsolved problems.
Problem 1.1 (Sárközy [8]). How small can one make the maximal gap between the consecutive
elements of a multiplicative Sidon set selected from {1, 2, . . . , n}? Does there exist for all n a
multiplicative Sidon set A ⊆{1, 2, . . . , n} so that A∩[m, m+√n] ̸= ∅for all 1 ≤m < m+√n ≤n?
As Sárközy [8] points out, if one could prove that the sequence of prime numbers p1, p2, . . . satisfies
the estimate pi+1 −pi ≤p1/2

, then an affirmative answer to the second question would follow.
However, even under the assumption of the Riemann Hypothesis such an estimate is not known.
To quantify the gap size question, let us introduce the following function.
Definition 1.2. For a positive integer n, define g(n) as the infimum of all real numbers L ≥0 for
which there exists a multiplicative Sidon set A ⊆[n] satisfying A ∩[x, x + L] ̸= ∅for every x ∈R
with [x, x + L] ⊆[1, n].
By combining the prime number theorem, Erdős’ estimate (1.1), and the pigeonhole principle, the
lower bound g(n) ≥(1 + o(1)) log n quickly follows.
In the other direction, the best known upper bound on g(n) obtained purely from prime gaps is
g(n) ≤n21/40 for all large enough n, by a theorem of Baker, Harman and Pintz [2].
In this paper we manage to lower the exponent and reach the square-root barrier, answering
Sárközy’s second question affirmatively.

3. Preliminaries
3.1. A multiplicative Sidon criterion. The fact that the set of primes is a multiplicative Sidon
set can be seen as a special case of the following more general criterion.
Lemma 3.1. Let J ≥1, and let A be a set of integers such that every ai ∈A can be written in the
form ai = mipi, where pi is a prime, 1 ≤mi ≤J < pi, and the primes pi are pairwise distinct as ai
ranges over A. Then A is a multiplicative Sidon set.
Proof. Assume a1a2 = a3a4, and suppose by contradiction that a1 /∈{a3, a4}. Write ai = mipi
with 1 ≤mi ≤J < pi and p1 different from p3 and p4. Since p1 > J ≥m3, m4, the prime
p1 cannot divide m3m4. Hence p1 must divide p3p4, so p1 = p3 or p1 = p4, contradicting the
assumption that these primes are distinct. Hence, a1 ∈{a3, a4} and cancelling the common factor
gives {a1, a2} = {a3, a4}.
□

The proof of Theorem 1.3 was autonomously discovered and formally verified in Lean by Aristotle,
a formal reasoning agent developed by Harmonic [1] and publicly available for free at aristotle.
harmonic.fun.

produced a proof of Theorem 1.3, together with a Lean formalization. After that, the human authors
studied the first subquestion in Problem 1.1 more carefully and managed to prove the stronger
conclusion stated in Theorem 1.4. We then asked Aristotle to formalize the latter proof, under the
assumption of the results by Baker–Harman–Pintz and Laishram–Murty that we used. Aristotle
successfully completed this second (conditional) formalization as well.
