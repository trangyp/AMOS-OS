1. INTRODUCTION
Given a finitely presented group Γ, the character variety Char(Γ) is an affine scheme
parametrizing finite-dimensional semisimple representations of Γ. The first systematic
treatment of character varieties is given by Lubotzky–Magid [LM85], but they already
appeared in the work of Weil [Wei60a, Wei60b], Artin [Art69] and Procesi [Pro75]. A
modern treatment can be found in the work of Sikora [Sik12]. They have been widely
studied in the literature and present a rich geometry. A central example is the case
Γ = π1(X, x), where X is a topological space with finitely presented fundamental group.
For instance, in the case of X an (open) Riemann surfaces, character varieties give
examples of (Poisson, respectively) symplectic varieties [Gol84].
The so-called monodromy equivalence allows one to interpret Loc(X)♡:= Char(π1(X, x))
as an algebraic object parametrizing locally constant sheaves on X. Locally constant
sheaves and their moduli arise in many areas of geometry and physics. Moreover, locally

In order to present our main result, we need to take a more intrinsic point of view on
character varieties. For any X as above, we can construct the stack of locally constant
sheaves, or character stack Loc(X)♡. The character stack carries more information than
the character variety: indeed, while the character variety parametrizes semisimple lo-
cally constant sheaves, the stack Loc(X)♡parametrizes all locally constant sheaves or,
equivalently, all representations of the fundamental group, and its underlying topologi-
cal space parametrizes conjugacy classes [ρ] of representations of the fundamental group
of X. Notice that each point has a unique closed point in its closure and such closed
point corresponds to the conjugacy class [ρss] of its semisimplification, i.e. the associated
graded to any Jordan–Holder filtration of ρ. The class [ρss] can be also seen as a point of
the character variety Loc(X) and we get a well defined morphism
q: Loc(X)♡→Loc(X)♡
[ρ] 7→[ρss] .
The morphism q defined above is an example of good moduli space, which is the most
natural generalization of good quotients in the language of algebraic stacks (see Section 2
for a more detailed discussion).
The stack Loc(X)♡introduced above is an open substack of a bigger stack Loc(X),
where the latter parametrizes complexes of sheaves on X with locally constant coho-
mologies. Notice that complexes of sheaves with locally constant cohomologies are a
particular example of complexes of sheaves with constructible cohomology and, indeed,
the theory generalizes in the constructible setting. If P is a stratification of X verifying
some mild hypothesis, Haine-Porta-Teyssier in [HPT26] constructed a (derived) stack
ConsP(X) parametrizing complexes of sheaves with P-constructible cohomology. Inside
this stack, one can find open substacks parametrizing perverse sheaves pPervP(X), de-
pending on a perversity function p: P →Z. We will refer to these as perverse character
stacks.
An axiomatic background for perverse sheaves has been introduced in the influential
paper [BBDG18]. The most fundamental and studied examples of perverse sheaves are
the one associated to the zero perversity (where one recovers the abelian category of
constructible sheaves) and the middle perversity (related to the theory D-modules via a
far-reaching generalization of the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence mentioned above
[Kas84, Meb84]). In both cases, generalizing the aforementioned results for classical
character stacks, perverse character stacks admit shifted Poisson structures [CL26b] and
good moduli spaces p PervP(X) [Lam25a]. We will refer to these good moduli spaces as
perverse character varieties. Its points are in bijection with semisimple perverse sheaves.
In this paper we are concerned with another feature of perverse character varieties, that
is, their affineness. This leads to the main result of this paper:

global sections of the structural sheaf OpPervP(X). Informally, to any loop γ contained in

proof in classical algebraic geometry.
In what remains, we explain informally the construction of these global sections using
Hochschild homology. The stack ConsP(X) is an example of moduli of objects MC in
a stable compactly generated ∞-category C introduced by Toën-Vaquié [TV07], and
revisited by Antieau-Gepner [AG14]. More precisely, for k a commutative ring and
C = ConsP(X; Modk), there is an equivalence of derived stacks over k
ConsP(X) ≃MC .
For any stable compactly generated ∞-category C, one can consider its Hochschild
homology HH(C). Hochschild homology can be used to construct k-forms on MC,
k ∈N: see for example [BD21], where a relation between Calabi–Yau structures on C
and shifted symplectic forms on MC is established. In the special case of n = 0, there is
a canonically defined morphism
HOC : HH(C) →RΓ(MC, OMC) .
Hence taking H0 on both sides and setting C = ConsP(X; Modk), we have that Hochschild
homology HH(ConsP(X; Modk)) gives rise to global sections of the derived stack ConsP(X).
Following [CL26a], this map can be explicitely described: in Lemma 4.3.1 we describe
HH(ConsP(X; Modk)) and in Corollary 4.3.2 we describe the morphism HOC is this
setting. Restricting to the open substack pPervP(X), this gives a map to global sections
of the perverse character stack. Since by definition the morphism to the good moduli
space q: pPervP(X) →p PervP(X) induces an equivalence Op PervP(X) ≃q∗OpPervP(X), we
have that global sections of the structural sheaf of pPervP(X) agree with global sections
of the structural sheaf of p PervP(X).

2. GOOD MODULI SPACES
In this section, we recall the definition of good moduli spaces and their main properties.
Good moduli spaces for algebraic stacks were introduced by Alper in [Alp13] and have
played a prominent role in moduli theory as generalizations of Mumford’s good GIT
quotients [MFK94] and Abramovich–Olsson–Vistoli’s tame stacks [AOV08]. If a good
moduli space q: X →X exists, then it is unique, as q is universal for maps from
X to algebraic spaces ([Alp13, Theorem 6.6]). Similarly to the Keel–Mori Theorem
[KM97], a fundamental result of Alper–Halpern-Leistner–Heinloth [AHLH23, Theorem
A] provides an intrinsic way to show that an algebraic stack admits a good moduli
space. The existence of a good moduli space for an algebraic stack X has many pleasant
consequences. It allows for example to give a local presentation of X by quotient stacks
and prove the compact generation of QCoh(X ) ([AHR25]). Also, the existence of good
moduli spaces can be exploited to construct BPS Lie algebras and unlock the study of
the cohomology of X via cohomological Hall algebras, see e.g. [DHM22, Dav24, Hen24,
BDN+25, HK25].
Definition 2.0.1 ([Alp13, Definition 4.1]). Let q: X →X be a qcqs morphism over an
algebraic space S with X an algebraic stack and X an algebraic space. We say that
q: X →X is a good moduli space if the following properties are satisfied:
(1) the functor q∗: QCoh(X ) →QCoh(X) is t-exact;
(2) the canonical morphism OX →q∗OX is an equivalence.
For completeness, we recall some of the main properties of good moduli spaces:
Theorem 2.0.2 ([Alp13, Proposition 4.5 & Theorem 4.16 & Theorem 6.6]). Let q: X →X
be a good moduli space. Then
(1) The functor q∗: QCoh(X)♡→QCoh(X )♡is fully faithful;
(2) the map q is universally closed and surjective;
(3) if Z1, Z2 are closed substacks of X , then
Im(Z1) ∩Im(Z2) = Im(Z1 ∩Z2)
where the intersections and images are scheme-theoretic.
(4) for κ an algebraically closed field over k, the κ-points of X are κ-points of X up to closure
equivalence (see [Alp13, Theorem 4.16-(iv)] for a precise statement);

check intrinsic properties of X .
Definition 2.0.4. Let π : X →X be a moduli space morphism with X an algebraic space
and let x ∈X be a point. We say that x is polystable if x is closed in π−1(π(x)).
Remark 2.0.5. The previous definition is inspired by the definition of polystable vector
bundles (and more recently K-polystable Fano varieties). Indeed, if X is locally of finite
type over some algebraic space S and admits a good moduli space, a point x of X is
polystable if and only if there are no non-trivial Θ-degenerations of x, namely morphisms
Θ →X which maps the generic point of Θ to x. This follows from [AHLH23, Lemma
3.24].
If X does not admit a good moduli space, the more general definition of N-closed
point can be given using the notion of N-specializations. This will appear in a future
work of David Rydh on topological moduli spaces.
Finally, let us recall that a notion of derived good moduli spaces has been introduced
in [AHPS23]. The following is the main result of that paper:
Theorem 2.0.6 ([AHPS23, Theorem 2.12]). Let X be a geometric derived stack.
(1) If t0X admits a good moduli space t0q: t0X →t0X, then X admits a derived good
moduli space q′ : X →X such that t0q′ ≃t0q.
(2) If X admits a derived good moduli space q: X →X, then t0q is a good moduli space for
t0X.
Remark 2.0.7. When applying Theorem 2.0.6 one should take some care in general, as
the notion of good moduli space in [AHPS23] is stronger then the one of the one in
[Alp13, AHLH23]. However, the two notions agree under mild conditions ([AHPS23,
Proposition 2.5-(iii)]) which will always be verified in the cases of interest for us.
2.1. Quasi-affineness of good moduli spaces. By definition, a good moduli space is
only an algebraic space. In this paragraph we provide a simple criterion (Corollary 2.1.6)
for a good moduli space to be a quasi-affine scheme.
Definition 2.1.1 ([Sta25, Tag 01P6] & [Lur18, Definition 2.4.1.1]). A (derived) scheme X
is called quasi-affine if it is quasi-compact and isomorphic to an open subscheme of a
(derived) affine scheme.
For a derived scheme X, the property of being quasi-affine can be checked on it
classical truncation. Indeed as a special case of [Lur18, Proposition 2.4.2.3] we have the

Definition 2.1.3. Let Y be an algebraic stack and y1, y2 ∈Y. We say that a line bundle

Lemma 2.1.4. Let Y be a separated algebraic space locally of finite type over a scheme S. Assume
that the structural sheaf OY separates points. Then
f: Y →Spec(H0RΓ(Y, OY))
is locally quasi-finite.
Proof. Given y1, y2 ∈Y and a global section s ∈H0RΓ(Y, OY) be a section separating y1
from y2, we have that the pullback of X and Spec(H0RΓ(Y, OY)s) along Spec(H0RΓ(Y, OY))
contains y1 but not y2. Hence the fibers f are finite and the result follows by [Sta25, Tag

□
Lemma 2.1.5. Let Y be a derived quasi-compact separated algebraic space locally of finite type
over k. The following are equivalent:
(1) Y is a quasi-affine;
(2) the canonical morphism f: Y →Spec(τ≥0RΓ(Y, OY)) is representable by a quasi-
compact open immersion;
(3) Y is quasi-compact and the structural sheaf OY separate points of Y.
Proof. The equivalence between (1) and (2) is [Lur18, Proposition 2.4.1.3] and the fact
that Y is connective (see also [AHPS23, Lemma 1.5]). Clearly (2) implies (3). We now
show that (3) implies (2).
Since Y →Spec(k) is of locally of finite type, then f is locally of finite type. Since
being locally quasi-finite is a property defined on the classical truncation of Y ([Lur18,
Definition 3.3.1.1]), if (3) holds then f is locally quasi-finite by Lemma 2.1.4. Moreover
since X is quasi-compact and separated and Spec(τ≥0RΓ(Y, OY)) is affine, we have that
f is quasi-compact and separated. The result then follows by Zariski’s Main Theorem
[Lur18, Theorem 3.3.0.2].
□
We will use the following criterion for quasi-affineness of a good moduli space.
Corollary 2.1.6. Let q: X →X be a good moduli space over k with X separated and of finite
type over k. Then X is quasi-affine if and only if OX separates polystable points.
Proof. The points of X are in bijection with the polystable points of X by Theorem 2.0.2-
(4). Since q∗OX ≃OX by definition of q, it follows that OX separates points if and only
if OX separates polystable points. The claim then follows by Lemma 2.1.5.
□

4. MODULI OF CONSTRUCTIBLE AND PERVERSE SHEAVES
In this paragraph we introduce constructible and perverse sheaves, as well as their
moduli. The role of perverse sheaves is central in the study of singularities and they
naturally generalize local systems. Moreover they are linked to the study of differential
equation with regular singularities (i.e., simple poles) via the Riemann–Hilbert corre-
spondence ([Kas84, Meb84]). Perverse sheaves originated form the theory of D-modules
via the Riemann–Hilbert correspondence as the shifted solution complex and the shifted
de Rham complex of a holonomic D-module on a complex manifold ([Kas84, KS90]). An
axiomatic background has been introduced in the influential paper [BBDG18] We claim
no originality here, possibly except Section 4.3. Every statement claimed in this para-
graph holds true in much wider generality, for which we refer to [PT22, HPT24, HPT26].
We choose to work under more restrictive hypothesis so that we can avoid introducing
many technical notions, and in any case this is enough for our scope.

(local) categorical compactness for a stratified space (X, P) from [PT22, Definition 2.2.1]).
Corollary 5.2.7. Let (X, P) be a categorically compact, locally categorically compact conically
stratified space with locally weakly contractible strata and P finite. Let k be a noetherian ring
containing Q. The constructible character variety ConsP(X)♡, that is, the good moduli space of
ConsP(X)[0,0] is a disjoint union of quasi-affine schemes over k.
Proof. By [HPT26, Proposition 4.1.12], we have that ConsP(X)[0,0] is representable by
an algebraic stack. By [Lam25b, Theorem 3.2.16], its truncation admits a good moduli
space ConsP(X)♡. That Proposition 4.1.14-(3) holds for (X, P) and k is a special case of
[Lam25a, Proposition 4.2.5-(3)] whose polystable points are in bijection with semisimple
constructible sheaves. Hence the assumptions of Theorem 5.2.5 are satisfied. It follows
directly from the definition of good moduli space, by Theorem 2.0.2-(4) that Ot0PervP(X)
separates points. The result then follows by applying Corollary 2.1.6 to each connected
components of ConsP(X)♡, which is possible by [Lam25b, Proposition 3.1.10 & Notation
3.2.15].
□
Example 5.2.8. Corollary 5.2.7 applies for example to complex Whitney stratified spaces.
It also applies to a wide variety of conical algebraic and subanalytic stratifications. See
[HPT24, HPT26].
