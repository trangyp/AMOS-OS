ABSTRACT
Galaxy formation scenarios can be interpreted through morphology and the level of rotational versus
pressure support, quantified through the ratio of a galaxy’s rotation speed to its velocity dispersion:
V/σ. Observational studies of dwarf galaxies find that V/σ does not strongly depend on environment,
and may weakly depend on galaxy mass, which could shift our understanding of how dwarf galaxies
form. We utilize the Marvelous Massive Dwarfs suite to examine whether V/σ depends on mass in
simulations, and understand how this varies for different baryonic components of the galaxy: H i gas,
young stars (<1 Gyr) and old stars (>1 Gyr).
We use a simulation sample of 67 isolated dwarf
galaxies with M⋆= 106 −109M⊙and produce line-of-sight kinematic maps for different viewing angles
per galaxy. We find that V/σ increases with mass, and that H i gas and young stars are more rotation-
supported (V/σ ≈1−13) while old stars are more dispersion-supported (V/σ ≈0.2−5). This result is
consistent with the scenario where young stars are born from cold gas in the interstellar medium and
undergo dynamical heating over time. We quantify the effects of spatial resolution on V/σ and find
that observations using old stars may underestimate the intrinsic V/σ. We find a correlation between
V/σHI,global and H i profile shape that is qualitatively similar to previous simulation results, but we
find higher V/σHI,global compared to prior work which mostly found values ≲2 in this mass range.
Our results motivate future work to examine V/σ and dwarf galaxy formation with different kinematic
tracers of the galaxy.

1. INTRODUCTION
We have yet to fully understand what influences angu-
lar momentum (‘rotation’) versus pressure (‘dispersion’)
support in galaxies.
The Milky Way exhibits struc-
ture that is kinematically and morphologically disky,

while dwarf galaxies (M⋆< 109 M⊙) seem to be more
dispersion-dominated or irregular. One way to quantify
the level of support is through kinematics with the ra-
tio V/σ, where V is the galaxy’s rotation velocity and
σ is its velocity dispersion.
The Milky Way exhibits
V/σ ∼10 when using measurements from either lo-
cal H i observations or measurements of thin disk stars,
though other galaxies of comparable mass tend to have a
stellar V/σ ∼5 (I. Pessa et al. 2023; F. McCluskey et al.

2025). Meanwhile, dwarf galaxies exhibit V/σ ≲2 when
measured with stars (C. Wheeler et al. 2017; M. A. C. de
los Reyes et al. 2023). These observations may suggest
that there is a dichotomy of rotation-supported (high
V/σ) versus dispersion-supported (low V/σ) galaxies,
but they may also imply there should be a trend of in-
creasing V/σ with galaxy mass.
Either way, we have
yet to fully understand how V/σ varies as galaxy mass
increases, and whether there is a sharp transition or a
more gradual increase.
Meanwhile, to discern trends in V/σ with galaxy mass,
we must also disentangle the role of environment, partic-
ularly since many of the dwarfs with V/σ data are within
the gravitational influence of larger galaxies.
Earlier
theoretical work predicted that disky dwarf galaxies are
accreted and transformed into dwarf spheroidals (e.g.,
L. Mayer et al. 2001a,b). In this picture, ram pressure
strips the infalling galaxy’s gas, while repeated tidal per-
turbations alter its morphology, transforming dwarf ir-
regulars (dIrrs) into gas-poor dwarf spheroidals (dSphs).
In this scenario, an evolution in V/σ is expected, as
rotation-dominated dIrrs are transformed into dSphs
dominated by stellar dispersion (σ⋆) and V/σ⋆< 1 (e.g.,
J. Klimentowski et al. 2009; L. Mayer 2010; E. L.  Lokas
et al. 2012; M. Tomozeiu et al. 2016; S. Kazantzidis et al.
2017; H. Rathore et al. 2025).
However, observations of stars in dwarf galaxies sug-
gest that V/σ⋆is not a function of environment, which
has major implications for dwarf galaxy evolution. C.
Wheeler et al. (2017) examined 40 dwarf galaxies from
the Local Volume, while M. A. C. de los Reyes et al.
(2023) examined 30 more distant dwarf galaxies in the
DIVE (“Dwarfs in Void Environments”) Survey. Neither
study found clear trends between V/σ⋆and distance to
the nearest L⋆galaxy (a proxy for environment). The
lack of trend with environment suggests that dwarfs are
born with lower V/σ⋆than had been hypothesized, and
that V/σ⋆does not evolve significantly if dwarf galaxies
indeed evolve from dIrrs to dSphs.
Instead of depending on environment, observations
find that V/σ⋆might be a function of galaxy mass,
though larger samples are needed to determine whether
this trend is discrete or more gradual. By including the
data from C. Wheeler et al. (2017), M. A. C. de los
Reyes et al. (2023) found a hint of increasing V/σ⋆over
a mass range of M⋆∼104 −109 M⊙. Using a linear
fit for V/σ⋆versus log(M⋆/M⊙), they found a slope of
0.24 ± 0.04 and an intercept of −0.98 ± 0.35.
It is also likely that V/σ varies based on the baryonic
component used to measure σ, since stars are born from
kinematically cold gas and expected to increase in dis-
persion with age (L. Spitzer & M. Schwarzschild 1951;
R. Wielen 1977; R. Leaman et al. 2017). The σ measure-
ments of all of the above studies in dwarf galaxies were
dominated by older stellar populations. To our knowl-
edge, V/σ has not been explicitly studied as a function
of mass using the H i gas of dwarf galaxies in observa-
tions. However, this quantity can be determined with
existing survey data by using the rotation speed (either
Vmax from resolved rotation curves or W20/2 from unre-
solved H i observations, which is the width at 20% of the
peak H i emission divided by 2) and dividing by a global
σHI. Hence, observational constraints for V/σHI of dwarf
galaxies can come from the kinematic analysis of galax-
ies from THINGS (“The H i Nearby Galaxy Survey”; F.
Walter et al. 2008), LITTLE THINGS (“Local Irregu-
lars That Trace Luminosity Extremes, The H i Nearby
Galaxy Survey”; D. A. Hunter et al. 2012; S.-H. Oh et al.
2015) and VLA-ANGST (“Very Large Array survey of
Advanced Camera for Surveys Nearby Galaxy Survey
Treasury galaxies”; J. Ott et al. 2012). In particular,
global σHI values have been determined for galaxies in
these surveys by A. M. Stilp et al. (2013) and K. A.
Oman et al. (2019). ‘Beam smearing’ is a phenomenon
where unresolved regions have artificially flatter veloc-
ity gradients, hence less rotation is inferred and this re-
sults in artificially higher velocity dispersions.
High-
resolution observations, like in the surveys listed above,
generally minimize the effects of beam smearing (as dis-
cussed in Section 6 of S.-H. Oh et al. 2015).
Additionally, state-of-the-art HI surveys have begun
taking data of dwarf galaxies, such as Apertif (W. A.
van Cappellen et al. 2022; E. A. K. Adams et al.
2022); FEASTS (FAST Extended Atlas of Selected
Targets Survey, J. Wang et al. 2025); MHONGOOSE
(MeerKAT H i Observations of Nearby Galactic Objects:
Observing Southern Emitters, W. J. G. de Blok et al.
2024); MIGHTEE-H i (MeerKAT International Giga-
Hertz Tiered Extragalactice Exploration survey - H i,
N. Maddox et al. 2021); and WALLABY (Widefield
ASKAP L-band Legacy All-sky Blind surveY, B. S. Ko-
ribalski et al. 2020; K. Spekkens et al. 2020; N. Deg et al.
2024). These surveys are sensitive to galaxies with ro-
tation speeds as low as ∼30 −40 km s−1. These sur-
veys can make spatially resolved maps of diffuse H i gas
down to lower surface densities than ever before, and
provide new opportunities to assess the unique physics
of star formation in more low-mass galaxies. With more
maps of rotation velocity and velocity dispersion, we
can further our understanding of baryonic feedback and
galaxy evolution for dwarf galaxies across a wider range
of masses and morphologies.
Simulations provide a way to help us interpret veloc-
ity dispersions, both as a function of time and bary-

onic component (e.g., young stars versus old stars, or
H i gas). Using the FIRE-2 simulations, F. McCluskey
et al. (2024) found that σ⋆depends on what component
of the galaxy is being measured (specifically all stars or
young stars), since stellar dispersions increase with age.
The age-velocity dispersion relation of stars might be
set by the initial dispersion of star-forming gas, which
itself may change with time (T. Kaufmann et al. 2007;
K. El-Badry et al. 2018a; J. C. Bird et al. 2013, 2021).
Stars may also be dynamically heated over time due to
mergers (A. Helmi et al. 2012) or scattering with spiral
arms or giant molecular clouds (J. M. D. Kruijssen 2015;
R. Leaman et al. 2017), among other non-axisymmetric
instabilities like bars (V. P. Debattista et al. 2006). The
existing literature on dynamical heating mostly focuses
on Milky Way-mass galaxies, but these mechanisms of
dynamical heating could be mass-dependent.
In the
dwarf regime, we are still in the early stages of under-
standing whether these age-kinematic trends hold. For
their sample of simulated dwarf galaxies, J. A. Benavides
et al. (2025) compared the fraction of rotational to to-
tal kinetic energy (κrot) between baryonic components
and found that old stars are indeed more dispersion-
supported, while cold gas and young stars exhibit more
rotation support at similar levels.
Simulations find that the level of rotation support
increases with galaxy mass (e.g., K. El-Badry et al.
2018a,b; A. A. Dutton et al. 2019; J. A. Benavides et al.
2025; B. M. Celiz et al. 2025).
However, most simu-
lations have had difficulty reproducing some observed
aspects of dwarf galaxy morphology. For example, real
dwarf galaxies exhibit an empirical size–mass relation
(A. van der Wel et al. 2011; F. Lelli et al. 2016; S. G.
Carlsten et al. 2021; Y. Asali et al. 2025), but many
simulations fail to make more compact galaxies, possi-
bly due to strong feedback (F. Jiang et al. 2019; L. V.
Sales et al. 2022).
In addition, disk-dominated dwarf
galaxies have been found in observations (e.g., J. Ott
et al. 2012; S.-H. Oh et al. 2015; F. Lelli et al. 2016), but
stronger feedback may prevent the formation of disks in
simulated dwarf galaxies. Using the FIRE (“Feedback in
Realistic Environments”) simulations, K. El-Badry et al.
(2018a,b) found that galaxies with stellar masses < 108
M⊙do not form H i gas disks and are more dispersion-
supported with V/σHI ≲2. Similarly, J. A. Benavides
et al. (2025) used FIREbox, a uniform volume simu-
lation run with FIRE-2 physics, and did not find any
disks (gas or stellar) for galaxies with M⋆< 109 M⊙.
They highlighted that this transition mass is inconsis-
tent with observations from LITTLE THINGS (S.-H.
Oh et al. 2015), in which dwarf galaxies with M⋆< 108
M⊙can exhibit V/σHI > 3.
In another study using
the Illustris TNG50 (“The Next Generation Illustris”)
simulations, B. M. Celiz et al. (2025) found this transi-
tion from spheroid to disky galaxies at a slightly lower
mass, between M⋆∼108 −109 M⊙. However, they also
cautioned that these simulated dwarf galaxies could be
artificially more dispersion-supported due to limited res-
olution of their star formation physics, which preserves
baryonic clumps around 1 kpc in size that prevent disk
formation in the surrounding region.
The high-resolution Marvelous Massive Dwarfs simu-
lations (“Massive Dwarfs”, A. Cruz et al. 2025) success-
fully reproduce characteristics of observed dwarf galax-
ies with stellar masses from ∼106−109 M⊙, and provide
an opportunity to study V/σ versus mass across bary-
onic components. A. Cruz et al. (2025) found that the
Massive Dwarfs reproduce observed size–mass relations
(A. van der Wel et al. 2011; F. Lelli et al. 2016; S. G.
Carlsten et al. 2021; Y. Asali et al. 2025), along with
diversity of rotation curve shapes (K. A. Oman et al.
2015). In addition to this, the Massive Dwarfs exhibit
disky morphologies down to at least M⋆∼108 M⊙(B.
Keith et al. 2025; R. Geda et al. 2025). The Massive
Dwarfs are gas-rich, with gas-to-stellar mass fractions
(1.4MHI/M⋆) ∼1 −10, and reproduce observed H i ve-
locities and sizes (D. Ruan et al. 2025). The simulated
dwarfs also match the observed mass–metallicity rela-
tion and specific star formation rates of dwarfs, and
they reasonably reproduce the column densities of low-
ionization metals in the circumgalactic medium of low-
redshift dwarfs (D. R. Piacitelli et al. 2025). Given this,
the Massive Dwarfs allow us to study V/σ for H i gas
and stars, across different dwarf galaxy morphologies.
In this work, we quantify the trend between V/σ ver-
sus galaxy mass using different baryonic components
(H i gas, young stars, and old stars) for a sample of 67
galaxies from the Massive Dwarfs simulations. In Sec-
tion 2, we describe the simulations and methods used to
derive global V and σ values. In Section 3, we present
our results for V/σglobal using H i gas and stars, both
young and old. In both cases we compare to observa-
tions. We also compare V/σglobal between baryonic trac-
ers and compare our total sample versus subsamples of
our galaxies that are disky or oblate. In Section 4, we
assess how V/σglobal changes with spatial resolution. In
Section 5, we examine the relation between H i profile
shape and V/σHI,global.
In Section 6, we discuss how
the implementation of baryonic feedback in simulations
could influence our results, along with comparisons to
other simulations. We discuss what our results imply
for the theory that field dwarfs transform into dwarf
spheroidal satellites. Finally, we summarize our work in
Section 7.

2. SIMULATIONS & METHODS
We utilize the high resolution “zoom-in” Marvelous
Massive Dwarfs (A. Cruz et al. 2025) simulations for
this work. Zoom-ins re-simulate high-density regions of
a halo and its surroundings from a larger-volume simu-
lation. The region of the halo is simulated with higher-
resolution particles and baryons, while the rest of the
simulation box is kept at low resolution, dark matter-
only. Large-scale structure is needed to deliver angu-
lar momentum to the zoom-in regions as described by
tidal torque theory (P. J. E. Peebles 1969; J. Barnes &
G. Efstathiou 1987). The Massive Dwarfs are higher-
resolution runs of halos from the Romulus25 simulation
(M. Tremmel et al. 2017). 40 zoom-in halos were se-
lected based on two criteria: (i) being in the stellar mass
range of M⋆∼108−9 M⊙(where rotation curve diversity
is maximized), and (ii) are at least 1.5 Mpc away from
another galaxy with M⋆> 1010 M⊙at z = 0.1. An-
other 15 zoom-in halos were selected to extend the mass
range down to M⋆∼106 M⊙, with the same isolation
criteria. The Massive Dwarfs have three times higher
force resolution and 512 times higher mass resolution
than Romulus25. Each Massive Dwarfs simulation has
a volume of (25 Mpc)3, and uses 87 pc for spline gravi-
tational force resolution, 994 M⊙for initial star particle
mass, 3300 M⊙for gas particle mass, and 18000 M⊙for
dark matter particle mass. Massive Dwarfs and Romu-
lus25 assume ΛCDM and parameters from Planck 2015
(e.g., h = 0.677, Ωm = 0.307, Ωbh2 = 0.02226, Planck
Collaboration et al. 2016).
Massive Dwarfs were run using ChaNGa (H. Menon
et al. 2015)13 for collisionless N-body and smoothed
particle hydrodynamics (SPH) processes.
The hydro-
dynamics for ChaNGa have been imported from the
tree+SPH code GASOLINE2 (J. W. Wadsley et al.
2004). ChaNGa is implemented in Charm++ (L. Kale
& S. Krishnan 1993), a parallel programming system,
and handles dynamic load balancing to strongly scale
with clustered data up to ∼105 cores.
ChaNGa calculates gas cooling and non-equilibrium
ion abundances by using collisional ionization rates (T.
Abel et al. 1997), radiative recombination (J. H. Black
1981; D. A. Verner & G. J. Ferland 1996), photo-
ionization, free-free emission, and cooling from hydro-
gen and helium (R. Cen 1992). A redshift-dependent,
uniform UV background (F. Haardt & P. Madau 2012)
is included in the heating and cooling rates. We also
simulate the non-equilibrium formation, shielding, cool-
ing, and destruction of H2, according to C. Christensen

Throughout each timestep, ChaNGa calculates the
H i mass fraction for each gas particle. The gas frac-
tion is based on the particle’s temperature and den-
sity, heating from the cosmic UV background radiation
and young stars, H2 self-shielding, and dust shielding
in H i and H2. The total H i mass of a galaxy (MHI)
is calculated by summing over the product of the mass
and H i fraction for each gas particle. The total stellar
mass (M⋆) and halo mass (Mhalo) are calculated by sum-
ming over particles within the virial radius. F. Munshi
et al. (2013) estimated that photometric observational
methods yield stellar masses that are ∼60% of the di-
rect sum of simulated star particles within a halo, so
we include this factor for a more direct comparison to
observations. Throughout this study, any stellar mass
will be 60% of the simulated halo total. We also de-
fine the total baryonic mass as Mbary =M⋆+1.4MHI, in
which the 1.4 factor accounts for the mass of helium and
metals in the gas phase (D. Arnett 1996).
ChaNGa follows the return of energy, mass, and met-
als from stellar winds and Type Ia and Type II super-
novae (SNe). Massive Dwarfs uses the superbubble feed-
back model from B. W. Keller et al. (2014) for Type II
SNe, and deposits energy at a rate of 1051 erg per SN.
Superbubble prevents numeric overcooling by placing
feedback-heated particles into a temporary two-phase
state, where each phase has a separate temperature and
density. These two states correspond roughly to the cold
swept-up ISM and the hot interior that has been heated
by SNe and are kept in pressure equilibrium. Mass flows

from the cold phase to the hot via thermal evaporation

We model the formation and evolution of black holes
with a model designed to mimic the direct collapse of gas
into massive black hole seeds (J. Bellovary et al. 2011).
Black holes are formed via gas particles in regions of
high density (n > 1.5 × 104 cm−3), low temperature
(T < 5×103 K), low metallicity (Z < 10−5), low molec-
ular hydrogen fraction (XH2 < 2×10−3), and high Jeans
mass (MJ > 105 M⊙). These choices reproduce the con-
ditions required to prevent fragmentation of gas. Black
holes are seeded with a mass of 105 M⊙. The simula-
tions include a modified Bondi accretion, following M.
Tremmel et al. (2017) and motivated by C. M. Booth
& J. Schaye (2009), with a boost density threshold of
100 cm−3. The accretion energy around a black hole
is transferred to surrounding gas using the superbubble
feedback model from B. W. Keller et al. (2014). In order
to understand the effects of black holes, we have a sub-
set of zoom-ins which were run with the same fiducial
physics but no black holes. In the 16 galaxies compared
with and without black holes, the global velocity dis-
persions can vary up to 20% but with no preferential
shift. We conclude that the inclusion of black holes is
not impacting our dispersion results.
The simulation data are analyzed with Pynbody14
(A. Pontzen et al. 2013), a python package for SPH +
N-body simulations. We identify halos with Amiga’s
Halo Finder (AHF) (S. R. Knollmann & A. Knebe
2009), and use the virial definition of 200 times that of
the critical density at a given redshift.
2.1. Galaxy Selection
Our sample consists of 67 dwarf galaxies with stel-
lar masses ranging from log(M⋆/M⊙) = 5.97 −9.09,
H i masses from log(MHI/M⊙) = 6.05 −9.43, and halo
masses from log(M200/M⊙) = 9.50 −10.96. This sam-
ple is selected to have MHI > 106 M⊙, which corre-
sponds to the detection limit of lower-mass galaxy sur-
veys like SHIELD (“Survey of H i in Extremely Low-
Mass Dwarfs”, J. M. Cannon et al. 2011) and other lo-
cal surveys (I. D. Karachentsev et al. 2011). In the left
panel of Figure 1, we show the H i versus stellar masses
for our simulated sample (red markers). The black line

shows a one-to-one relation and demonstrates how, on
average, H i mass is larger than stellar mass. Our masses
are consistent with observed dwarf galaxies from J. D.
Bradford et al. (2015) (black points) and K. B. W. Mc-
Quinn et al. (2022) (black ×’s).
As previously mentioned, there are 55 zoom-in runs,
and we can analyze additional galaxies within the re-
solved Lagrangian region. We use a naming convention
of “r431-1” to denote that the galaxy is from zoom-in
run “r431”, with a halo ID of 1 (which would be the
most massive galaxy in the zoom region).
We aim to select isolated dwarfs whose kinematics are
not strongly impacted by environment. We select iso-
lated field dwarfs based on the requirement that there
are no other galaxies more massive than itself within a
virial radius (R200). We also removed two galaxies from
our sample that undergo major mergers (mass ratio of
1:2) since z ≈0.1. Our entire sample has not had any
mergers with a mass ratio less than 1:10 since z ≈0.1.
Our sample is selected by mass and exhibits a range of
disky and non-disky morphologies. Following the defini-
tion described in Section 2.6.2 of R. Geda et al. (2025),
we classify these galaxies based on the normalized spe-
cific angular momentum of stars (jz,⋆/jtot) when viewed
face-on. We consider the galaxy ‘disky’ if the median of
jz,⋆/jtot for all star particles is > 0.5. Based on this def-
inition, 41 out of 67 of our simulated galaxies are disky.
2.2. Masking by Radius
We mask our data by radius to ensure satellites are
not included in our kinematic maps.
This radial cut
also imposes a limit to study dwarf galaxy kinematics
at similar sensitivities to observations.
For maps of H i gas, we limit our kinematic analy-
sis out to the size at an H i surface density limit of 1
M⊙pc−2 (RHI). In D. Ruan et al. (2025) we demon-
strated that measuring out to a limit of 0.08 M⊙pc−2
is necessary for H i to accurately trace the halo’s max-
imum rotation speed and hence the turndown in the
baryonic Tully-Fisher relation. However, we choose to
measure out to 1 M⊙pc−2 in this study since it is the
fiducial limit used in many radio surveys.
For kinematic maps of stars we consider data out to
R28, which is the size at a B-band surface brightness
of 28 mag arcsec−2. This surface brightness limit was
chosen as it measures out to 1% of the mean central
surface brightness for our sample (∼23 mag arcsec −2).
We calculate R28 with the profile class in pynbody.
This method assumes stellar population models from P.
Marigo et al. (2008) and L. Girardi et al. (2010).

2.3. Line-of-Sight Maps
First, we read in the particle data with Pynbody and
center on the galaxy in position and velocity using the
shrinking sphere method (C. Power et al. 2003) for a re-
gion that is 1 kpc in size. We align each galaxy according
to its midplane, which Pynbody determines from the
total angular momentum of gas particles within 10 kpc
from the halo’s center.
Then, we align the galaxy according to different incli-
nation (i) and azimuthal (ϕ) angles. For each galaxy, we
generate a random value of cos i from a uniform distribu-
tion ranging from 0 to 1, convert i to degrees, and use a
random ϕ. The right panel of Figure 1 shows the distri-
bution of inclination angles used throughout this work,
resulting from our random draw in cos i. In order to un-
derstand how much our kinematic quantities vary with
viewing angle, we also generate maps with inclinations
of i = 0◦, 30◦, 45◦, 60◦, 75◦, 90◦in combination with az-
imuthal angles of ϕ = 0◦, 72◦, 144◦, 216◦, 288◦, resulting
in a total of 31 viewing angles per galaxy. These maps
are used for the errors in our global line-of-sight (LOS)
kinematic quantities.
After centering and aligning the galaxy, we gener-
ate moment maps of column density (cm−2); line-of-
sight velocity (VLOS); and line-of-sight velocity disper-
sion (σLOS). We bin particles in a uniform grid with
spatial pixels (spaxels) that are 0.175 kpc in size. The
spaxel length on each side is ∼2ϵsoft (where ϵsoft is the
spline gravitational force resolution, 0.087 kpc). For col-
umn density maps, we sum over particles for the total
mass per spaxel, divide by the spaxel area, and then con-
vert to cm−2. For the first and second moment maps,
we perform a mass-weighted average for VLOS and σLOS
per spaxel. We construct maps for each baryonic com-
ponent: H i (neutral hydrogen with T < 104 K), young
stars (< 1 Gyr in age), and old stars (> 1 Gyr in age).
We show examples of these velocity and dispersion
maps in Figure 2. The upper two rows show maps for
r431-1, an extended disky galaxy, at an inclination angle
of i = 45◦. In terms of the spatial extent for each com-
ponent, old stars and H i gas extend further out than
young stars.15
As demonstrated in the σLOS row for
r431-1 in Figure 2, older stars tend to have higher ve-
locity dispersions than young stars or H i gas.
Older
stars can become more dispersion-dominated due to dy-
namical heating (see Appendix A). In the lower two rows
of Figure 2, we show maps of LOS velocity and disper-
sion for r488-1, which is an irregular galaxy. The H i gas
of r488-1 exhibits rotation and disturbed regions with
high velocity dispersion. The old stars and young stars
of r488-1 do not exhibit rotation as strongly, and the cor-
responding dispersion fields are more smooth compared
to the H i.

dwarf galaxies. First, we compare the V/σglobal values

amine whether morphologically disky galaxies are corre-
lated with higher values of V/σglobal. The data for LOS
rotation speed, LOS velocity dispersion, and V/σglobal

kinematic contributions.

3.2. Young and Old Stars
Using a similar approach, we compare V/σ for stars
in our simulated galaxies versus observations. We com-
pare to observational constraints from C. Wheeler et al.
(2017) and Barba et al., in prep., which is a follow-up
paper to M. A. C. de los Reyes et al. (2023). We se-
lect isolated galaxies from C. Wheeler et al. (2017) with
M⋆≥106 M⊙, which provides more constraints on the
lower-mass end. Barba et al., in prep. has a sample of
isolated dwarf galaxies in field and void environments
with M⋆= 107 −109 M⊙, which overlaps well with our
simulated sample.
Different methodologies are adopted by C. Wheeler
et al. (2017) and Barba et al., in prep. in order to get
LOS rotation speeds and LOS velocity dispersions. C.
Wheeler et al. (2017) determine the rotation speed and
velocity dispersion of their galaxies through Bayesian
analysis with rotating and non-rotating models to fit
their individual stellar spectra. Barba et al., in prep. de-
termine stellar velocities and dispersions by fitting their
galaxy spectra with stellar templates and construct-
ing IFU maps, then determining global values from the
maps. Absorption features in the stellar continuum are
dominated by older stars. Therefore, a more direct com-
parison between simulated and observed V/σ should use
old stars, and we use an age cut of > 1 Gyr. In this sec-
tion, we also show results for young stars (< 1 Gyr) to
compare with the kinematics of old stars.
The left panel of Figure 4 shows the stellar LOS ro-
tation speed as a function of M⋆. Similar to Figure 3,
the markers are values measured at a random inclination
angles, drawn from a uniform distribution in cos i, while
the error bars show the range of values over all 31 view-
ing angles. Our simulated values for old stars are shown
with blue diamonds, and young stars are shown with
orange diamonds. Data from Barba et al., in prep. are
shown with maroon circles (open markers for galaxies
in voids, and filled markers for field galaxies), and data
from C. Wheeler et al. (2017) are the red open squares.
The observed rotation speeds are not corrected for in-
clination, and therefore we also do not correct for incli-
nation in Figure 4.
The right panel of Figure 4 shows the stellar LOS ve-
locity dispersion as a function of M⋆. For our simulated
galaxies, old stars exhibit systematically higher disper-
sions that are, on average, more than 2× that of young
stars. The simulated values are mostly consistent with
the observed stellar velocity dispersions within the range
between young and old stars. Ten of the 32 real galaxies
(marked with lower-opacity markers) exhibit dispersions
that are higher by at least 10 km s−1 (up to a factor of
2.3) than our simulated values at a given mass.
The bottom panel of Figure 4 shows V/σ⋆,global with
stellar mass.
Following the upper panels, old stars
have higher velocity dispersions and similar rotation
speeds as young stars, and therefore this results in lower
V/σ⋆,global values. The old stars in our simulated dwarf
galaxies exhibit V/σ⋆,global ≈0.2 −5. As shown with
the horizontal black line for V/σ = 1, more of the old
stars in our simulated dwarf galaxies become dispersion-
dominated below M⋆∼108 M⊙.
Observed galaxies
seem to be more dispersion-supported compared to our
simulated V/σ values for old stars. 19 out of the 32 real
galaxies have V/σ⋆,global < 1, whereas 16 out of the 67
simulated galaxies have V/σ⋆,global < 1.
Spatial resolution and sample selection may explain
differences between the simulations and observations. In
Section 4, we explore how lower (uniform) spatial bin-
ning and Voronoi binning may influence V⋆,global and
σ⋆,global. We find that lower uniform spatial resolution
means worse sampling of the velocity field, and hence
lower rotation speeds and higher velocity dispersions.
Voronoi binning results in larger spatial bins in the outer
regions of the galaxy, and hence lower rotation speeds
and lower dispersions.
It is likely that differences in
stellar V/σ⋆,global between simulations and observations
stem from methodology. However, higher stellar disper-
sions for some galaxies in Barba et al., in prep. cannot
be fully explained by Voronoi binning. In Section 6.1,
we discuss what aspects might differ in galaxy popula-
tions between the simulations and observations to re-
sult in comparatively higher stellar velocity dispersions
for some of the observed galaxies in the right panel of
Figure 4.

5. H i PROFILES AS A PROXY FOR V/σ
Current and upcoming surveys will provide H i emis-
sion profiles for a large sample of galaxies, and the
shape of a given H i profile can provide an indication of
whether the galaxy is more dispersion or rotation sup-
ported. Double-horned H i profiles are the characteristic
shape of a more rotation-supported galaxy, as the gas
is moving both away and towards us along the line of
sight to produce this distinct shape. Gaussian H i pro-
files are typically associated with a galaxy that is more
dispersion-supported, and this shape is often found in
dwarf galaxies.
In Figure 10, we show the H i emission spectra for
three galaxies in our sample, each at a randomly-drawn
inclination angle. We have generated idealized H i emis-
sion profiles for our simulated dwarf galaxies using the
martini python code (K. Oman 2024), which accounts
for thermal broadening. We use a channel width of 6.4
km s−1 (similar to FASHI, C.-P. Zhang et al. 2024), with
more idealized conditions such as Gaussian noise with an
RMS of 3×10−8 Jy beam−1, and a Gaussian beam that
is 12 arcsec in size. For the mock H i profiles, we place
each galaxy at a distance of 3 Mpc.
As seen in Figure 10, lower-mass galaxies like r1223-1
tend to have a more Gaussian H i profile, while higher-
mass galaxies like r613-1 exhibit a double-horned profile.
However, not all higher-mass galaxies exhibit a double-
horned profile, such as r489-1. We can use the relative
linewidth difference ∆W = (W20 −W50)/W50 to quan-
tify the shape of the H i profile, where W20 and W50
are the linewidths at 20% and 50% of the peak H i flux.
r613-1 has a double-horned profile and a lower value of
∆W due to its weaker wings and hence more similar
values of W20 and W50. r1223-1 has a Gaussian H i pro-
file with most of its gas in the central region moving at
lower velocities, and therefore has a higher value of ∆W.

6. DISCUSSION
We demonstrate that for a given dwarf galaxy, V/σ
measured by H i is systematically higher than that of
young stars or old stars, and we compare our results
to other simulations and observations. Here, we discuss
physical reasons beyond spatial binning (Section 4) to
reconcile any discrepancies. In Section 6.1, we consider
how differences in methodology and sample selection
may explain higher stellar dispersions for some observed
galaxies relative to the simulations. In Section 6.2, we
discuss how different choices in simulation physics can
influence velocity dispersion and hence, galaxy rotation
and morphology (Section 6.3). In Section 6.4, we dis-
cuss the implications of our results with respect to dwarf
galaxy evolution.
6.1. Stellar Dispersions in Simulations versus
Observations
We discuss potential sources of the discrepancy be-
tween simulated and observed stellar velocity disper-
sions (in Figure 4). In particular, ten out of 32 observed
galaxies from Barba et al., in prep. exhibit velocity dis-
persions which are higher than the constraints of old
stars in the simulated dwarf galaxies at a given stellar
mass.
The mismatch could be from different stellar popula-
tions probed in observations versus simulations. We use
a cutoff of 1 Gyr in age for the simulated galaxies to
distinguish ‘old’ versus ‘young’ stars, assuming stellar
populations from P. Marigo et al. (2008) and L. Girardi
et al. (2010). Meanwhile, the spectra in Barba et al.,


## Page 19


19
in prep. are dominated by the CaII H and K lines from
older stars (lifetimes ∼10 Gyr), though many of their
galaxies also exhibit strong hydrogen Balmer lines (in-
dicative of recent star formation, < 1 Gyr). If the CaII
H and K lines are dominant such that the stellar pop-
ulation is on average older than 1 Gyr in the observed
galaxies, this could result in higher velocity dispersions
relative to the simulated values.
Differences in sample selection may also influence ve-
locity dispersion. We select galaxies with M⋆= 106−109
M⊙and MHI > 106 M⊙. The simulated sample over-
laps with that of Barba et al., in prep., which has M⋆=
107 −109 M⊙. However, DIVE galaxies were selected to
have high [O iii]λ4363 fluxes. Higher [O iii]λ4363 fluxes
likely mean the DIVE Survey galaxy selection is biased
toward low-metallicity systems.
As discussed in Sec-
tion 4.3.1 of M. A. C. de los Reyes et al. (2023), gas-
phase metallicities are inversely correlated with specific
star formation rate (e.g., R. Maiolino & F. Mannucci
2019), and star formation rate is correlated with ve-
locity dispersion (e.g., X. Yu et al. 2019; Y. Luo et al.
2025). Therefore, the selection of galaxies with bright
[O iii]λ4363 fluxes could lead to an observed galaxy sam-
ple with higher dispersions than the simulated sample.
Differences in stellar velocity dispersions between ob-
servations and the simulated results may be explained
through the effect of selection functions and stellar pop-
ulations, or other systematic uncertainties in the data
that are not yet understood.
Choices in simulation
physics can also influence velocity dispersion.

next section, we elaborate on what aspects of stellar
feedback physics could affect velocity dispersion.
6.2. The Impact of Simulation Physics
We discuss how choices related to star formation and
feedback in simulations can influence σ. First, we dis-
cuss what influences the initial velocity dispersion of
stars at birth, σbirth. Then, we discuss how the choice of
supernova feedback model, the choice of subgrid physics,
and the implementation of early stellar feedback from
young, massive stars may influence σ.
Previous simulation studies have found that σbirth de-
pends on the assumed star formation model and resolu-
tion of the cold gas. In particular, using better spatial
resolution and a higher star formation threshold can re-
sult in lower vertical dispersions (E. L. House et al. 2011;
J. Kumamoto et al. 2017) and higher radial dispersions
(J. Kumamoto et al. 2017). By resolving the clumpy,
multiphase ISM, including the cold phase, simulations
can produce galaxies with relatively thinner disks that
exhibit more non-circular motion. As discussed in Sec-
tion 2, the star formation model adopted in the Mas-
sive Dwarfs simulation suite requires the presence of H2,
generally ensuring that stars form at densities n > 100
cm−3. Our star formation physics, in tandem with a
force resolution of 87 pc, ensures that stars can only
form from dynamically cold gas (see, e.g., J. C. Bird
et al. 2021, and discussion therein).
This dispersion floor will influence the dispersion of
young stars that we have measured here. Stars can be
dynamically heated quickly after birth (see Appendix
A), so that the dispersions are already higher for a given
population even after 1 Gyr. Hence, the young stellar
dispersions we measure here will be influenced by σbirth,
but higher than σbirth due to heating in the first Gyr
after formation. Likewise, σbirth is not the same as the
H i dispersions we have presented here, because stars
form from gas with T < 1000 K, while H i is dominated
by gas closer to T ∼104 K.
Thermal injection from supernovae will impact σ.
Models like blastwave supernova feedback (G. Stinson
et al. 2006) delay cooling in surrounding gas particles
by numerically ‘turning off’ radiative cooling until the
end of the snowplow phase.
The Massive Dwarfs use
superbubble feedback, which accounts for energy input
from clustered supernovae and thermal evaporation of
the ISM from the cold phase to the hot phase (B. W.
Keller et al. 2014).
Superbubble feedback can have
higher mass loading in its outflows, stronger star for-
mation regulation, and more retention of high-angular-
momentum gas than blastwave feedback (B. W. Keller
et al. 2014). In a comparison of two simulated dwarf
galaxies with superbubble and blastwave feedback, M.
Mina et al. (2021) found that superbubble feedback re-
sulted in comparable V/σ values for stars, and higher
V/σ values for H i.
Specifically, they found that the
superbubble runs had higher H i rotation speeds, and
lower H i velocity dispersions. Overall, the superbubble
feedback model seems largely responsible for retaining
high-angular momentum material in H i, and therefore
higher V/σ.
SNe may be a dominant source of turbulence at low
redshifts (e.g., M. R. Krumholz et al. 2018), and the
choice of momentum injection may also influence σ. Su-
perbubble feedback considers the energy from super-
novae to be only in a thermal component, and this ther-
mal energy is transformed into kinetic energy (momen-
tum) through the N-body + SPH code. Other simula-
tions include momentum injection from supernovae, in
addition to thermal energy, particularly if the radius im-
pacted by supernova feedback is unresolved (e.g., C.-G.
Kim & E. C. Ostriker 2015).
In addition to supernovae, a number of simulations im-
plement subgrid physics to capture stellar feedback from


## Page 20


20
young, massive stars with local photoionization, momen-
tum injection from stellar winds, and radiation pressure
(e.g., J. H. Wise et al. 2012; M. Aumer et al. 2013; G. S.
Stinson et al. 2013; P. F. Hopkins et al. 2014; D. Cev-
erino et al. 2014; O. Agertz & A. V. Kravtsov 2015;
P. F. Hopkins et al. 2018). The Massive Dwarfs cur-
rently exclude this feedback, and include only feedback
from supernovae. These subgrid feedback implementa-
tions can add together in a non-linear way such that it
is non-trivial to determine how much energy is coupled
to the ISM, and not all simulations will yield similar re-
sults, particularly in terms of velocity dispersion (e.g.,
R. Roˇskar et al. 2014; O. Agertz & A. V. Kravtsov 2015).
However, some have found that inclusion of young stellar
feedback could lower the central gas velocity dispersions,
at least in Milky Way-sized disks (O. Agertz et al. 2013).
In dwarfs, early stellar feedback can also influence the
burstiness of star formation when a stochastic sampling
of the initial mass function is adopted (E. Applebaum
et al. 2020; M. C. Smith 2021).
More recently, some
simulators have been able to follow radiative hydrody-
namics in the formation of cosmological dwarf galaxies
(e.g., O. Agertz et al. 2020; B. Baumschlager et al. 2025;
M. P. Rey et al. 2025). In general, these studies have
found that the inclusion of radiative transfer leads to
less bursty star formation in dwarf galaxies, and M. P.
Rey et al. (2025) found that it leads to more H i rich
dwarfs. However, the implications for σ have yet to be
explored in detail.
Finally, we note that all of these different physics im-
plementation choices can lead to different impacts on po-
tential tracers of σ. For example, because of the subgrid
model for early stellar feedback used in FIRE, Y. Luo
et al. (2025) recently used the ionized gas near young
star particles to directly compare dispersions to observed
H II regions in dwarf galaxies. Such a comparison is not
possible in simulations that lack early stellar feedback.
For example, S. A. Kassin et al. (2014) attempted to
study the evolution of gas velocity dispersions in Milky
Way-mass simulations that did not include early feed-
back. They were limited to using cold, potentially star
forming gas, or dense gas that had been heated by su-
pernovae. Neither were a good match to the ionized gas
in H II regions of galaxies.
Since the Massive Dwarfs
do not include early stellar feedback, we do not con-
sider velocity dispersions from ionized gas in this work.
Velocity dispersions measured in observations from the
emission lines of ionized gas in dwarf galaxies (e.g., Y.
Xu et al. 2024; Y. Luo et al. 2025, Barba et al., in prep)
can be up to ∼50 km s−1, which is higher than our
values for H i, young stars, and old stars in this work.
We also note that our definition of young stars (ages
< 1 Gyr) includes stars that would not be traced by
emission lines (such as Hα, which traces stars ≲10 Myr
in age). Given our results, we expect different velocity
dispersions based on the component used; therefore, it
makes sense that other studies using ionized gas disper-
sions may measure higher values than what we measure
for H i gas or stars.
6.3. Comparing Morphologies in Simulations
Given the choices in simulation physics discussed
above, there are many reasons why simulations might
disagree on σ. Pinpointing the source of difference in σ
between simulations is likely to require detailed tests
that isolate various aspects of feedback implementa-
tions, as well as resolution. We showed in Figure 6 that
V/σglobal is tied to morphology. In this section, we dis-
cuss the effect of feedback choices in terms of different
V/σ and morphologies between simulations.
We use H i kinematics as a common basis to compare
between simulations. In our mass range of dwarf galax-
ies, we find σHI,global ≈3 −20 km s−1 (Figure 3), which
is consistent with the range from A. A. Dutton et al.
(2019) (5 −20 km s−1) using the NIHAO simulations
(L. Wang et al. 2015). Other work using the APOSTLE
simulations (K. A. Oman et al. 2019) and the FIRE-
box simulations (J. A. Benavides et al. 2025) generally
find H i dispersions around 10 −20 km s−1. Differences
in velocity dispersion can manifest in the overall ratio
V/σ.
For galaxies with Mbary = 108−9 M⊙, we find
V/σHI,global ∼4 −10. Our range is consistent with that
of NIHAO galaxies (around 1 −10 for Vmax ∼50 −100
km s−1, Figure 11 of A. A. Dutton et al. 2019) and
APOSTLE galaxies (around 4 −7 for Mbary ∼109 M⊙
K. A. Oman et al. 2019). These values are in contrast
with the FIRE (K. El-Badry et al. 2018b) and FIRE-
box (J. A. Benavides et al. 2025) galaxies which exhibit
V/σHI,global ≲2 (except for one galaxy in FIRE, m11b,
which has V/σHI,global ∼4). The Massive Dwarfs ex-
hibit global H i dispersions and V/σ values that are more
similar to that of the NIHAO and APOSTLE simula-
tions rather than the FIRE simulations. However, we
note that there are other known differences between the
Massive Dwarfs, NIHAO, and APOSTLE due to star
formation and feedback choices (e.g., NIHAO sizes are
larger and APOSTLE simulations do not form dark mat-
ter cores; A. Cruz et al. 2025).
Simulation physics impact V/σ, and similarly the for-
mation of disks. We demonstrate in Figures 6 and 11
that the Massive Dwarfs exhibit disky galaxies down to
M⋆∼3 × 107 M⊙(consistent with B. Keith et al. 2025;
R. Geda et al. 2025, from the same simulation suite), and
that these disky galaxies exhibit systematically higher


## Page 21


21
V/σglobal than non-disky galaxies at a given mass. Feed-
back that influences radial motion may influence the for-
mation of disks. K. El-Badry et al. (2016) found that
stars in FIRE could form in gas with strong radial flows,
both inward and outward. The feedback driving these
flows can drive ‘breathing modes’ in the FIRE simula-
tions (K. El-Badry et al. 2016), in which the size of the
galaxy oscillates with time, and young stars can migrate
∼1 kpc within 100 Myr. This feedback either removes
angular momentum-rich gas in dwarfs or prevents its ac-
cretion (V. Pandya et al. 2020), and thus prevents the
formation of disks (K. El-Badry et al. 2018a). As previ-
ously mentioned, J. A. Benavides et al. (2025) used the
FIREBox simulations (R. Feldmann et al. 2023) and did
not find disks for galaxies with M⋆< 109 M⊙. In con-
trast, the Massive Dwarfs and other simulations with
similar physics (such as the Marvel/DC Justice League
dwarf galaxies F. Munshi et al. 2021) do not exhibit
signs of breathing modes.
For instance, C. L. Riggs
et al. (2024) found net radial velocities closer to 0 for
young stars (see their Figure 3), and R. Geda et al.
(2025) found no evidence for large size and specific SFR
fluctuations. As mentioned in Section 6.2, superbubble
feedback helps retain high angular momentum gas in our
simulated dwarf galaxies, and hence higher V/σ and the
formation of disks.
6.4. Dwarf Galaxy Scenarios
The fact that our dwarfs are diskier and more rotation-
ally supported than some other simulated and observed
dwarfs has implications for the theoretical understand-
ing of whether disky field dwarfs can transform into
dispersion-dominated dwarf spheroidal satellites.
As
discussed in the Introduction (Section 1), initial studies
that proposed a transition assumed that infalling dwarfs
start with V/σglobal in the range 2 −5. This idea was
called into question when both cosmologically simulated
dwarfs (C. Wheeler et al. 2017; J. Frings et al. 2017) and
observed field dwarfs (C. Wheeler et al. 2017; M. A. C.
de los Reyes et al. 2023) demonstrated V/σ⋆,global < 2.
However, our analysis found several potential biases that
could lead to lower V/σ⋆,global in observations.
Our work demonstrates that different baryonic trac-
ers bias the inferred V/σglobal.
Old stars are always
the most dispersion-dominated tracer, as highlighted in
Figure 5. C. Wheeler et al. (2017) examined low-mass
galaxies in the Local Group using stellar velocity dis-
persions exclusively of red giant branch and horizontal
branch stars, i.e., stars that would fall into our ‘stars
> 1 Gyr’ category.
Likewise, M. A. C. de los Reyes
et al. (2023) and Barba et al., in prep. measured stel-
lar kinematics from continuum galaxy spectra which
are also dominated by older stars. Understanding how
V/σglobal depends on environment using other baryonic
tracers could help inform whether dwarf galaxies are in-
deed born puffy. The relative difference in V/σglobal be-
tween baryonic tracers may also be informative of the
timescales needed to undergo transformation via tidal
stirring, if any.
We found that V/σglobal depends on galaxy mass, and
this has implications for interpreting previous studies.
C. Wheeler et al. (2017) exclusively examined dwarfs
with M⋆< 107 M⊙. In that mass range, we also find
V/σ⋆,global < 2, and generally V/σ⋆,global < 1, meaning
that our results are consistent with C. Wheeler et al.
(2017) at these low masses. Meanwhile, M. A. C. de los
Reyes et al. (2023) and Barba et al., in prep. examined
field dwarfs up to M⋆∼109 M⊙.
A majority of the
observed galaxies in this mass range (M⋆≳108 M⊙)
are dispersion-dominated with V/σ⋆,global < 1. This is
seemingly at odds with the old stars in simulated galax-
ies that are mostly rotation-dominated. However, this
is resolved if we take into account biases due to the res-
olution imposed on observational studies.
In Section 4, we found that spatial resolution also
plays a major role in measuring V/σglobal. Lower spaxel
resolution (i.e., larger spaxel size), especially in the outer
regions of a galaxy, will artificially lower the Vglobal mea-
surement. Voronoi binning, which is used to measure
stellar dispersions, will also result in lower σglobal. The
systematic bias due to Voronoi binning is larger for ro-
tation speed than for velocity dispersion. The bottom
panel of Figure 8 suggests that, for old stars, rotation
speeds can be significantly underestimated, particularly
for the higher-mass dwarfs, by ∼50 −90%. This may
explain why Vglobal is as low as 10 km s−1 for some of
the higher-mass observed dwarfs in Figure 4.
Overall, our results suggest that V/σglobal is likely to
be underestimated by the current observations due to
bias from spatial binning in IFU data and the spectra
being dominated by stellar populations older than 1 Gyr.
The effects due to spatial binning and older stellar pop-
ulations must be further characterized when consider-
ing whether dwarf galaxies indeed form as dispersion-
dominated objects.
7. CONCLUSIONS
Velocity dispersion and the ratio V/σglobal provide
metrics to understand the morphology of a galaxy and
its level of rotation versus pressure support. Given the
observational difficulty in measuring V/σglobal, this pa-
rameter has yet to be fully constrained for dwarf galax-
ies. The Marvelous Massive Dwarfs simulations can re-
produce disky dwarf galaxies and rotation curve diver-


## Page 22


22
sity (A. Cruz et al. 2025; R. Geda et al. 2025; B. Keith
et al. 2025) and the H i content of dwarfs (D. Ruan et al.
2025), and with these successes we examine V/σglobal as
a function of mass. We calculate V/σglobal for 67 galax-
ies with M⋆= 106 −109 M⊙from the Massive Dwarfs
and compare mass trends for different baryonic compo-
nents. We produce line-of-sight maps for rotation speed
and velocity dispersion and determine global quantities
over 31 viewing angles. We compare rotation speed, ve-
locity dispersion, and V/σglobal as a function of galaxy
mass for our simulated galaxies and constraints from ob-
servations. The results for H i kinematics are shown in
Figure 3 and the results for stellar kinematics are shown
in Figure 4. Our key results are:
• We find that V/σ increases with galaxy mass (Fig-
ure 5). H i gas and young stars (ages < 1 Gyr) are
more rotation supported in this mass regime, with
V/σglobal ≈1 −13.
Old stars (ages > 1 Gyr)
are more dispersion supported with V/σglobal ≈
0.2 −5. This is consistent with the idea that stars
become more dispersion supported over time due
to dynamical heating.
In summary, the level of
rotation support measured for a given galaxy de-
pends on its mass and which baryonic component
is being used to trace the kinematics.
• In terms of the stellar kinematics, some observed
galaxies exhibit higher dispersions and lower ro-
tation speeds than our simulated sample. Higher
stellar dispersions may be due to differences in the
selection functions and stellar populations (Sec-
tion 6.1). Lower rotation speeds in observations
are likely due to lower spatial resolution or Voronoi
binning (Section 4, Figure 8). The effect on rota-
tion speed due to Voronoi binning has more of an
impact to lower V/σglobal than dispersion (Figure
9).
• We find that disky and oblate galaxies exhibit sys-
tematically higher V/σglobal than non-disky galax-
ies at a given baryonic mass (Figure 6). The rela-
tive difference in V/σ between disky and non-disky
galaxies is more apparent in H i gas and young
stars.
• Upcoming surveys will measure H i emission pro-
files for many more galaxies, offering the po-
tential opportunity to quantify rotation support
for large samples.
The H i profile shape can be
quantified through the relative linewidth differ-
ence ∆W = (W20 −W50)/W50, and this metric
serves as a proxy for V/σglobal.
Similar to K.
El-Badry et al. (2018b), we find that ∆W and
V/σHI,global are inversely proportional, but we find
higher V/σHI,global values (up to 14, Figure 11),
and a wider spread in ∆W.
• Related, diverse morphologies for the simulated
dwarf galaxies manifest in terms of scatter in ∆W
(right panel of Figure 11). At a fixed galaxy mass,
morphology and ∆W can vary widely. We recover
a range of double-horned and Gaussian H i profile
shapes with ∆W = 0.25 −0.87.
Previous observations suggest that dSphs may be born
dispersion-dominated due to low V/σglobal and a lack
of trend with environment.
Our results suggest that
V/σglobal < 1 may be the case for dwarf galaxies and
their old stars, but the same galaxy may exhibit strong
rotation support in H i gas or young stars.
As stars
are born from cold gas and undergo dynamical heating
over time, understanding V/σglobal as a function of en-
vironment and for different baryonic tracers will help us
understand how dwarf galaxies evolve.
We have found that the measurements of V/σglobal us-
ing older stellar populations, as well as binning of IFU
spaxels to achieve sufficient signal-to-noise, might have
biased previous observational studies to lower determi-
nations of V/σglobal.
We predict that measurements
of young stellar populations and H i will yield higher
V/σglobal values.
Given our trend of V/σglobal with
mass, our results are not in contradiction with previous
results that low-mass field dwarfs are born dispersion-
dominated. At M⋆≲107 M⊙, we generally find that
V/σ⋆,global < 1.
The Massive Dwarfs successfully produce a mix of
morphologies, including disks, down to stellar masses of
107 M⊙. We determine disks are correlated with higher
V/σglobal. We find higher V/σglobal values than previ-
ous simulations and stellar observations. Understanding
star formation and feedback through observable signa-
tures like velocity dispersion remain essential to probe
galaxy formation, constrain galaxy formation models,
and make predictions for the large surveys to come.
