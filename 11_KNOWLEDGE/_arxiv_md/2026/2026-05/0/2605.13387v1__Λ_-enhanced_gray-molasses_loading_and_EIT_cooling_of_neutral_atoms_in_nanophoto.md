Λ-enhanced gray-molasses loading and EIT cooling of neutral atoms in nanophotonic
traps
Lucas Pache,1, ∗Antoine Glicenstein,1, † Philipp Schneeweiss,1
J¨urgen Volz,1 Arno Rauschenbeutel,1, ‡ and Riccardo Pennetta1
1Department of Physics, Humboldt-Universit¨at zu of Berlin, 10099 Berlin, Germany
Nanophotonic traps for cold atoms typically have trap volumes that are orders of magnitude
smaller than, e.g., free-space optical tweezers. This makes efficient loading of these traps challeng-
ing, thereby limiting the total number of atoms coupled to the nanophotonic waveguide.
Here,
we demonstrate that Λ-enhanced gray-molasses (ΛGM) can substantially increase the number of
trapped atoms in a nanofiber-based cold-atom setup. Specifically, we observe a six-fold increase in
the number of loaded atoms compared to conventional red-detuned polarization gradient cooling.
Despite the unusually small depth of our optical trap of only 24 µK, we load about 4000 individual
Cesium atoms, achieving optical depths exceeding 140 and reaching the collisional blockade regime
over a length of approximately 1 mm. After loading, we perform efficient EIT-assisted cooling that
is found to increase the trap storage time to 400(9) ms. This is a 5-fold improvement over the passive
storage time. Remarkably, EIT-cooling also works with two co-propagating nanofiber-guided light
fields and requiries only about a few hundred picowatt of optical power. Our results provide an
efficient method to boost both the number of loaded atoms and the storage time of nanophotonic
atom traps.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Interfacing trapped neutral atoms with nanophotonic
waveguides enables the experimental observation of sta-
ble and efficient interactions between guided photons and
ensembles of up to thousands of atoms [1–4]. In the last
decade, this capability has enabled significant advances
in, e.g., quantum optics [5–11], quantum information pro-
cessing [12–19], and studies of collective radiative phe-
nomena [20–24].
These achievements have been made
possible by the development a wide range of nanopho-
tonic platforms for cold atom experiments, including
optical nanofibers [25, 26], slow-light waveguides [27],
whispering-gallery-mode resonators [15, 18, 28, 29], and
photonic crystal cavities [30, 31].
In all these different platforms, atoms are typically
trapped either by optical potentials formed by guided
modes [25] or by external optical tweezers, which are par-
tially reflected by the waveguide [30]. Both approaches
share a defining feature: the trapping potentials occupy
much smaller volumes compared to conventional atom
traps in free space.
Specifically, typical volumes for
nanophotonic traps are a few orders of magnitude smaller
than λ3 [1].
This severely renders the loading from a
thermal cloud of laser-cooled atoms challenging.
As a
consequence, efficient and reliable controlled atom load-
ing remains a central challenge in this field.
Recently,
Λ-enhanced gray molasses (ΛGM) cool-
ing [32–36] has emerged as a highly effective technique

In this work, we experimentally demonstrate that
ΛGM and the closely related technique of electromag-
netically induced transparency (EIT) cooling [40–44] can
address several of the challenges posed by nanophotonic
interfaces for laser-cooled atoms. Specifically, we show
that ΛGM can substantially enhances the loading of
nanofiber-based traps for cold atoms.
In our experi-
ment, we observe a six-fold increase in the number of
trapped atoms compared to red-detuned polarization-
gradient cooling (RPGC). This enables the trapping of
about 4000 individual atoms and reaching maximum ocu-
pation in the the collisional blockade regime [45]. Using
the same optical beams employed for ΛGM, we imple-
ment EIT–assisted cooling of the atom trapped near the
nanofiber. This is particularly noteworthy because our
experiment uses very shallow traps with a trap depth
of only 24 µK, for which standard RPGC was found to
be ineffective.
This approach not only yields a 5-fold
increase in the atom storage time, but also allows us
to preserve the comparatively high atom–photon inter-
action strength observed immediately after loading over
very long storage times. Finally, we demonstrate that
EIT-cooling can also be implemented by exclusively us-
ing guided beams with a total power of just a few hundred
pW.

II.
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
The experimental setup is shown schematically in
Fig. 1a).
We interface an ensemble of laser-cooled
Cesium atoms with the evanescent field of light propa-
gating in an optical nanofiber with a nominal radius of
225 nm. The latter is realized as the waist of a tapered
optical fiber. The atoms are trapped in the evanescent
field of the nanofiber using a two-color dipole trap [25].
In our experiment, the red- and blue-detuned trapping
light fields operate at wavelengths of λred = 935 nm and
λblue = 685 nm, which is close to magic wavelengths
of the D2-transition [26, 46].
For all results in this
paper, the red-detuned trapping field is set to a power
of Pred = 55 µW and is launched from both ends of the
nanofiber to create a standing-wave dipole trap.
The
blue-detuned beam has a power of Pblue = 7.75 mW.
This configuration results in traps with a calculated
depth of 24 µK with a minimum located about 270 nm
from the nanofiber surface [47]. Due to their small trap-
ping volumes of about 1×10−16 cm3, the nanofiber traps
have an occupancy of at most one atom per trapping site
in the collisional blockade regime [45, 48]. The experi-
ment begins by preparing a cloud of laser-cooled atoms
in a magneto-optical trap.
We then proceed with an
initial stage of RPGC on the D2 transition (see Fig. 1b),
yielding an atomic temperature of 5.5 ± 0.2 µK, that
was measured via time-of-flight imaging. Subsequently,
we implement ΛGM cooling [33, 34]. In this stage, two
phase-coherent lasers in a Λ configuration are applied
on the D1 transition, coupling the two hyperfine ground
states to the 6P1/2, F ′ = 4 excited state. The stronger
of the two fields is blue-detuned by ∆1P = 4.5 ΓD1
from the F = 4 →F ′ = 4 transition. The two-photon
detuning is defined as ∆2P = ∆1P,P −∆1P,C (see Fig. 1
b)). The frequency of the phase coherent second field is
used to control the two-photon detuning, ∆2P, of the Λ
scheme (see Fig. 1b). After 150 ms of ΛGM cooling, the
atomic temperature was found to be 1.9±0.1 µK (see the
Supplementary Material for more details of experimental
sequence and the ΛGM cooling scheme). We estimate
the optical depth (OD) of the nanofiber-trapped ensem-
ble by performing transmission spectroscopy through
the nanofiber on the F = 4 →F ′ = 5, D2 transition.
In addition, we estimate the total number of trapped
atoms, N, using the technique described in Ref. [49].
This method consists of measuring the optical energy
required to pump an ensemble initially prepared in the
F = 4 ground state into the F = 3 ground state, using a
laser resonant with the F = 4 →F ′ = 4, D2 transition.
With these two independent measurements, we can
estimate the atom-photon coupling strength β, defined
as the probability for an excited atom to decay by
emitting a photon into the waveguide, as β = OD/(4N).
Immediately after loading, we measure β = 1.08±0.03%.
III.
LOADING OF NANOFIBER-BASED TRAP
We start by characterizing the loading dynamics of the
nanofiber trap during the RPGC and ΛGM phases. Fig-

we attribute to the atoms being still too hot for being
loaded into our comparatively shallow traps when ramp-

measured OD increases, saturating at OD = 20.4 ± 1.2.
The measured data are well described by a saturating ex-
ponential with a time constant of τRPGC = 9.4 ± 1.6 ms.
After 45.0 ms, we switch off the RPGC beams and start
the ΛGM phase.

V.
FIBER-GUIDED EIT-COOLING
In our nanofiber-based trap, atom loss is ultimately
caused by heating in the radial direction. Interestingly,
the radially decaying intensity profile of the evanescent
guided light field enables the detection of the radial mo-
tional sidebands of nanofiber-trapped atoms [54]. This
suggests that direct cooling of the radial motional de-
gree of freedom using exclusively nanofiber-guided light
should be possible. This is in contrast to conventional
cooling schemes based on paraxial light fields, where laser
cooling along directions orthogonal to the propagation
direction is typically prohibitively inefficient.
To test cooling with guided light fields, we establish a
Λ-system on the D2 transition, as depicted in Fig. 1 b), by
introducing two additional phase-coherent guided light
fields that co-propagate through the nanofiber. Figure 5
a) shows the measured storage time with and without the
presence of the EIT-cooling via guided beams for a one-
photon detuning of the strong coupling laser, ∆1P,EIT,C
= 2.5ΓD2. Again, two effects are visible: first, the storage
time of the atoms in the trap is extended by a factor
2.6 and, second, a slight increase in the measured OD is
observed when the cooling beam are switched on. This
suggests that EIT-cooling via guided beams is also able
to recover the initial value of the coupling strength, β,
by cooling the atoms within the traps.
To better understand the cooling mechanism in this
case, we show in Fig. 5 b), a measurement of the OD after
a cooling time of 20 ms for different two-photon detunings

VI.
CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, our results establish ΛGM and related
cooling techniques as important tools for nanophotonic
interfaces with laser-cooled atoms. We demonstrate that
these methods can substantially enhance the loading of
nanophotonic traps, reaching filling factors that are cur-
rently limited by collisional blockade. Importantly, since
ΛGM operates with blue-detuned light, it could in princi-
ple be used to achieve filling factors beyond the collisional
blockade limit via tuned light-assisted collisions [35, 38].
For this reason, implementing ΛGM in nanophotonic
cold-atom interfaces could prove crucial for investigating
collective radiative phenomena that require high filling
factors, such as selective radiance [50, 55]. This regime,
however, was not explored in the present work. In fact,
this would require the trap depth U ≳ℏΓD1, which is not
the case in our experiment [38]. All of our results have
been obtained in comparatively shallow trapping poten-
tials requiring minimal optical power. This an important
advantage for integrated nanophotonic platforms, where
optical absorption and heat dissipation impose critical

A.
Experimental sequence
The experimental sequence used to performed the mea-
surements is shown in Fig. 6. We begin by preparing a
cloud of cold Cesium atoms in a magneto-optical trap
(MOT) for a duration of 1.5 s. During this stage, the
atoms are cooled using a laser, red-detuned by 2ΓD2 from
the F = 4 →F ′ = 5 D2 cycling transition, hereafter re-
ferred to as the D2-cooler. Here, ΓD2 denotes the natural
linewidth of this transition. An additional D2-repump
laser, resonant with the F = 3 →F ′ = 4 transition,
is employed to prevent atoms from accumulating in the
F = 3 hyperfine ground state.
The pairs of counter-
propagating light fields are σ+ and σ−polarized.
After loading the MOT, in a 19 ms long transition
phase, we ramp the magnetic field gradient down to zero,
gradually reduce the intensity of the D2 cooling beam
and increase its detuning to 11ΓD2. We then perform
a first stage of red-detuned polarization-gradient cooling
(RPGC) lasting 45 ms.
At the end of this phase, the
atomic molasses reaches a temperature of approximately
5.5 µK, as measured via time-of-flight.
Next, we implement Λ-enhanced gray molasses (ΛGM)
cooling.
For this step, the D2 cooling and repumping
lasers are switched off and two light fields driving a Λ
system are applied on the D1 transition between the
two hyperfine ground states and the 6P1/2, F ′ = 4 ex-
cited state as depicted in Fig. 1b). Again, the counter-
propagating light fields of ΛGM are σ+ and σ−polar-
ized. The first field, referred to as the D1-cooler, is blue-
detuned by ∆1P = 4.5ΓD1 from the F = 4 →F ′ = 4
transition.
We produce a phase-coherent second light
field by using an electro-optic modulator (EOM QUBIG
PM-Cs 9.2) which creates sidebands around the cooling
laser at a frequency corresponding to the the hyperfine
ground state splitting of Cesium, with a sideband to car-
rier ratio of 1 to 4. The parameters for loading the atoms
using ΛGM (one-photon detuning ∆1P, two-photon de-
tuning ∆2P and sideband-to-carrier ratio) were optimized
to yield the highest optical depth (OD). We use an atomic
clock (Safran mRO-50) to precisely define the modulation
frequency sent to the EOM. The ΛGM stage lasts up to
200 ms.
For measurements involving cooling of the already
trapped atoms, all molasses beams are switched off for
20 ms after loading, allowing the cold atomic cloud to
fall below the nanofiber. Afterwards, we apply the ΛGM
as described above.
For the measurements that demonstrate EIT cooling
using exclusively guided field, we launch in the nanofiber
a laser beam that is 2.5ΓD2 blue-detuned with respect to
the F = 4 →F ′ = 4 hyperfine transition of the Cesium
D2-line with a power of about 500 pW. In this case, the
sidebands are generated using a fiber-based phase modu-
lator (Exail NIR-MPX800-LN-10) and the carrier to side-
band ratio is 13 to 1. The transverse polarization of the
