Abstract
Concept Activation Vectors (CAVs) are a fundamental tool for concept-based
explainability in deep learning, yet their practical utility is limited by statistical
instability. We analyze the stochastic nature of CAVs and the Testing with CAVs
(TCAV) method, deriving the distributions of major CAV classes including Pattern-
CAV, FastCAV, and ridge regression-based CAVs. We then identify a fundamental
flaw in the standard TCAV score: its reliance on a discontinuous indicator function
induces non-decaying variance in critical regimes. To address this, we introduce
α-TCAV, a generalized framework that replaces the indicator with a parameterized
smooth function, yielding a unified probabilistic formulation that subsumes both
TCAV and Multi-TCAV. We characterize the induced distributions of sensitiv-
ity scores and different TCAV variants, showing that established state-of-the-art
choices lack theoretical justification. We provide principled guidance on tuning
the parameter in α-TCAV — either to imitate Multi-TCAV at substantially lower
computational cost, or to obtain a calibrated Bayes-optimal probabilistic measure
of a concept’s influence. Finally, our analysis yields practical recommendations
that challenge established routines: most notably, allocating the full sampling
budget to a single CAV rather than splitting it across several.

Introduction
Explainable AI: from Feature Attribution to Concept-based Explanations.
As deep neural
networks have achieved impressive performance across diverse domains, understanding their decision-
making has become critical, particularly in high-stakes applications such as medical diagnosis or
autonomous driving; explanations of AI systems are also increasingly required by law. Traditional
explainability methods focus on low-level feature attribution (heatmaps) via techniques such as

Saliency Maps [Simonyan et al., 2013], Grad-CAM [Selvaraju et al., 2017], and Integrated Gradients
[Sundararajan et al., 2017], highlighting key input regions for specific predictions. Yet such pixel-level
maps diverge from human reasoning: they show where the model looks, but not what concepts it has
detected, while humans naturally reason in terms of patterns, shapes, and colors (in a vision context).
This gap motivates concept-based explanation methods, which decode internal representations in
terms of human-understandable concepts. Among these, Concept Activation Vectors (CAVs) [Kim
et al., 2018] have emerged as one of the most popular and influential techniques, and are the
topic of this article. Concept-based XAI has been highly active in recent years: [Ghorbani et al.,
2019] introduced Automatic Concept Extraction for unsupervised concept discovery, while Concept
Relevance Propagation (CRP) [Achtibat et al., 2023] generalizes the feature-based Layer-wise
Relevance Propagation (LRP) [Bach et al., 2015]; [Aysel et al., 2025] establish a benchmarking
framework with novel metrics quantifying alignment between model explanations and human-
annotated ground truth. We refer the reader to the survey [Poeta et al., 2023] and references therein.
Concept Activation Vectors.
[Kim et al., 2018] introduced CAVs and Testing with CAVs (TCAV),
representing human-understandable concepts as directions in latent space. Using linear classifiers
(e.g., SVMs) and directional derivatives, TCAV quantifies concept importance (e.g., stripes for
zebras). Various CAV computation methods exist [Dreyer et al., 2024], including PatternCAV [Pahde
et al., 2022, Haufe et al., 2014, Martin and Weller, 2019], and FastCAV [Schmalwasser et al., 2025]
for improved efficiency. Originally framed within computer vision, CAVs have proven useful in
other domains, e.g. for analyzing large policy-value networks in deep reinforcement learning such
as chess [Pálsson and Björnsson, 2023] and AlphaZero [McGrath et al., 2022, Schut et al., 2023].
Several related extensions have appeared: Concept Bottleneck Models (CBMs) [Koh et al., 2020]
enforce human-defined concepts as intermediate representations, enabling test-time interventions;
[Yuksekgonul et al., 2022] extend this to post-hoc CBMs without retraining; Visual-TCAV [De Santis
et al., 2024] bridges local saliency and global concept-based explanations via a generalization of
Integrated Gradients; and Concept Activation Regions (CARs) [Crabbé and van der Schaar, 2022]
represent concepts as regions rather than single vectors. For large language models, recent work
focuses on steering and control: [Zhao et al., 2026] use sparse autoencoders to denoise concept
vectors, [Zhang et al., 2025] steer behavior controlling toxicity (GCAV), [Sun et al., 2024] route
activations through concept bottlenecks for text generation, and [Postmus and Abreu, 2024] employ
Conceptors (ellipsoids). [Fel et al., 2023] provide a unified pipeline bridging automatic concept
extraction and TCAV-based importance estimation, and CoCoX [Akula et al., 2020] uses TCAV to
identify “fault-lines” (minimal concept-importance changes flipping a prediction) for conceptual
and counterfactual explanations. A critical challenge in XAI is stability—producing consistent
explanations for the same model and input across multiple applications [Sundararajan et al., 2017,
Alvarez Melis and Jaakkola, 2018]. Inconsistent results erode user trust [Krishna et al., 2022], a
problem especially prevalent in TCAV due to its stochastic nature.
Related Work.
Despite intensive research efforts on CAVs over the last years, the theoretical
and particularly the statistical properties of CAVs have only recently begun to receive attention.
Most notably, [Wenkmann and Garreau, 2025, Schnoor et al., 2026] have initiated a more rigorous
investigation of the stochastic nature of CAVs, aiming to describe their distributions and variability.
Our paper continues this line of work, and we will make extensive references to those two articles
throughout. A key idea in our paper is the smooth approximation of the indicator function in an
expression of the type E[1X>0] which can be traced back to fundamental results in probability theory,
most prominently perhaps Lindeberg’s classical proof of the central limit theorem [Lindeberg, 1922]
and its non-asymptotic variante, the Berry-Esseen theorem [Vershynin, 2026], or in applications of
Steins method [Stein, 1972]. Surprisingly, to our best knowledge this classical technique has been
missed out so far in the context of TCAV. On a higher level, our paper is similar to other efforts aiming
for a theoretical analysis of XAI methods such as [Garreau and Luxburg, 2020, Garreau and von
Luxburg, 2020] for Local Interpretable Model Agnostic Explanation (LIME) [Ribeiro et al., 2016], or
[Chen et al., 2025] for Shapley Values [Shapley et al., 1953] and their adaption to XAI [Lundberg
and Lee, 2017]. Despite the theoretical nature of our work, we also provide concrete practical
recommendations, partly proposing fundamental changes, while partly we complementing previous
advice for working with CAVs (multi-layer analysis, concept dependencies and entanglement)
[Nicolson et al.].

Summary of Contributions.
This paper provides a unified theoretical treatment of CAVs, signifi-
cantly extending on previous recent work [Wenkmann and Garreau, 2025, Schnoor et al., 2026].
• We derive the distributions of PatternCAV, FastCAV and Ridge Regression, enabling to investigate
their classification accuracy and induced distribution of sensitivity scores. We consider both
classical (d ≪n) and modern (d ≍n) regimes, for dimension d and sample size n; see Sec. 2
and Sec. B.
• We introduce α-TCAV in the end of Sec. 3.2, a parameterized generalization of the TCAV score.
We show that α-TCAV is a unified framework that recovers existing approaches while offering
computational and statistical advantages that we investigate in detail in Sec. 4.
• In particular, we show analytically and empirically that α-TCAV can reduce variance by roughly
50% compared to Multi-TCAV in Sec. 3 (especially Sec. 4.2) Crucially, it achieves this stability
at a fraction of the computational cost, effectively breaking the traditional trade-off between
compute and reliability.
• In Sec. 4.2, we discuss the question of fine-tuning α, and argue that TCAV is overconfident, and
the state-of-the-art method of Multi-TCAV is underconficent. We propose a Bayes-optimal choice
of α, a calibrated probability that the concept contributes to the model’s prediction.
• We challenge the established practical recommendation of averaging multiple TCAV scores as it
is statistically sub-optimal. We propose a drastically different single-run strategy allocating the
entire sample budget into computing a single CAV. This is discussed in Sec. 4.4.
On a higher level, this paper helps to more rigorously address the following two questions, both of
which can be answered by studying (random) inner products ⟨z, wCAV⟩for CAVs (random vectors)
wCAV, where the meaning of z differs (firstly, latent activations of (non-)concept data, and secondly,
gradients of the predictions). Both are crucial questions in concept-based XAI, and underline the
importance of a rigorous analysis of the distributional properties of CAVs studied in detail Sec. 2.
1. Is a concept encoded in the neural network? This can be answered by the classification accuracy
of a CAV-based classifier separating the latent concept and non-concept distributions; see Sec. 5.
2. Is a concept being used for the model’s prediction? This is answered by sensitivity scores (inner
products of the type ⟨z, wCAV⟩; see (14)) and the TCAV method, adressed in detail in Sec. 3.1.
The main part of this paper is structured as follows. Sec. 2 introduces the theoretical background and
presents several methods for computing CAVs, together with characterizations of their distributions;
these in turn make the variability of CAVs—a measure of their stochastic nature—straightforward
to study. Sec. 3 recalls sensitivity scores and the (Multi-)TCAV technique, and reinterprets them
probabilistically as expectations of random variables. We identify shortcomings of the current
approach and introduce α-TCAV as a remedy. Building on our findings, we develop a mathematical
model associating each TCAV variant with a classical probability distribution, enabling a rigorous
comparison of their properties. Building on these findings, Sec. 4 introduces a mathematical model
that characterizes the various TCAV approaches in terms of corresponding classical probability
distributions, from which their means and variances can be derived. While the most direct application
of this model concerns the sensitivity scores, the framework enables a qualitative comparison of
TCAV variants in the general case as well; we discuss the relations between the different approaches
in detail and provide numerical simulations that show excellent agreement with our theoretical
predictions. Sec. 4.2 then addresses the practical question of how to choose the sharpness parameter
α together with the corresponding normalization of the sensitivity scores. In Sec. 4.3 we provide
experiments on real data and conclude conclude with practical recommendations in Sec. 4.4. Finally,
while the main paper focuses on the TCAV technique — asking whether a concept is used for the
model’s prediction — and in particular on our novel α-TCAV approach, Sec. 5 additionally provides
a detailed treatment of the classification accuracy, i.e. whether a concept is encoded in the network
at all. This is possible because both questions can be phrased in terms of (different) inner products
of the form ⟨z, wCAV⟩that recur throughout the paper: interpreting wCAV as a random vector and
studying its distribution (cf. Sec. 2 and Sec. B) lets us address both topic. More technical details are
provided in the appendix.

Remark 3.1 (Interpretation and Limitations of TCAV). TCAV quantifies the influence of concept
C on the kth class by measuring the prevalence of positive directional derivatives. Values near 1
indicate a strong positive contribution, 0.5 suggests a neutral role (uncorrelated), and values near
0 imply the concept contradicts the prediction. Despite its intuitive appeal, the standard definition
in (15) suffers from a significant flaw: only the sign matters and it is insensitive to magnitudes and
exhibits problematic behavior at the decision threshold. Specifically, the metric fails to distinguish
between a truly negative contribution (SC,k,l(x) < 0 for all x) and a neutral, zero-gradient case
(SC,k,l(x) = 0 for all x) - in both scenarios, TCAVQC,k,l collapses to 0. This discontinuity is more
than a theoretical curiosity, but the reason why TCAV’s variance may sometimes be non-decaying
[Wenkmann and Garreau, 2025, Sec. 4.4].
Remark 3.2 (Sources of Randomness). Note that the sensitivity score SC,k,l(x) in (14) is a random
variable with several sources of randomness. While the model itself (specifically the functions hl,k
and fl) is assumed to be deterministic — thereby neglecting potential dependencies on the training
data — the distribution of SC,k,l(x) originates from, firstly, x being a random data vector, drawn
from the input data distribution (mixture of several classes), and secondly, the CAV vl
C being a
random vector, whose distribution is induced by both the (lth layer) concept and the non-concept
distribution.

Before we lay out the technical assumptions, let us introduce some notation required specifically
for this section. We write 0d for the zero vector in Rd, and similar 1d for the all-ones vector in
Rd. We denote the diagonal matrix of v as Dv. Norms are denoted by ∥· ∥p for ℓp-norms, ∥· ∥2→2
for spectral, and ∥· ∥F for Frobenius. Vertical concatenation is written as [v1, v2]⊤∈R2d, while
[v1|v2] ∈Rd×2 denotes horizontal concatenation. Next, we make more specific assumptions on
the data to be concentrated, meaning that any Lipchitz-continuous scalar observations are tightly
concentrated around their mean. We will first state the precise definition of the notion of concentrated
random vectors just below in Definition B.2, together with additional comments and motivation.
Definition B.2 (q-exponential concentration; observable diameter). Let (X, ∥· ∥X ) be a normed
vector space and q > 0. A random vector x ∈X is said to be q-exponentially concentrated if for any
1-Lipschitz continuous (with respect to ∥· ∥X ) real-valued function φ : X →R there exists C ≥0
independent of dim(X) and σ > 0 such that, for all t ≥0,
P (|φ(x) −Eφ(x)| ≥t) ≤Ce−(t/σ)q.
(A.19)
This is denoted as x ∝Eq(σ | X, ∥· ∥X ), where σ is called the observable diameter. If σ does not
depend on dim(X), we write x ∝Eq(1 | X, ∥· ∥X ).
The most fundamental example for a random vector with the concentration property from B.2
is the isotropic Gaussian random vector x ∼N(0, I) in Rd, for which x ∝E2(1 | Rd, ∥· ∥2)
by the classical Gaussian concentration inequality [Ledoux and Talagrand, 2011, Ledoux, 2001].
While similar notions of the concentration property described in B.2 exist, such as linear and
convex concentration, Lipschitz concentration turned out to be particularly useful by being both a
mathematically convenient assumption, and a model for realistic data such as natural images. This is
due to a fundamental property of its stability under Lipschitz transforms: if f : Z →X (for (Z, ∥. ∥Z)
being another normed space) is a K-Lipschitz function and x ∈X with x ∝Eq(σ | X, ∥· ∥X ), then
f(x) ∝Eq(K · σ | Z, ∥· ∥Z), where K might depend on dim(X). As neural networks represent
Lipschitz continuous mappings, generative adversarial networks are Lipschitz continuous transforms
of (isotropic) Gaussian random vectors and can therefore be modeled using concentration properties
as provided in Definition B.2, thus making them a model for realistic data [Seddik et al., 2020].
Lipschitzness of neural networks has been studied in a variety of works; [Seddik et al., 2020] also
provides a short overview providing Lipschitz constants of various parts of neural networks (fully
connected and convolutional layers, pooling layers and activation functions, residual connections,
batch normalization layers etc.). Due to the nested structure of Lipschitz-continuous functions, any
of their concatenations is Lipschitz-continuous, notably also parts of network outputting hidden-layer
activations, making this approach highly suitable regarding concept activation vectors. We make the
following assumptions on the data concentration, as well as large-dimensional asymptotics.

Limitations and Gaussian Universality
While our theoretical framework effectively leverages the
Gaussian universality of the decision score g(x)—providing a robust approximation that aligns well
with our empirical observations (see Section C for accurate prediction of both the distribution of the
decision score and the corresponding classification accuracy), we acknowledge that this property may
not be exact in all high-dimensional regimes. Recent studies have characterized specific conditions
under which this universality breaks down, suggesting that the score distribution may instead be better
described as a convolution between a Gaussian and an auxiliary distribution. In particular, [Yaakoubi
et al., 2026, Xiaoyi and Liao, 2025] highlight these nuances in the context of high-dimensional
empirical risk minimization and linear factor models. Although our current Gaussian-based results
provide a highly satisfactory description of the classification accuracy for the synthetic and real-world
data tested, incorporating these more precise distributional results remains a valuable direction for
future work to further refine the alignment between theory and experiment in edge-case scenarios.
