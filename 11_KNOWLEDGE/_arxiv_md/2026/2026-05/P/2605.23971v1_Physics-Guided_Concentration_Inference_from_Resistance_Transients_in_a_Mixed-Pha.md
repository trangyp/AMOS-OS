Physics-Guided Concentration Inference from
Resistance Transients in a Mixed-Phase
SnO-SnO2 Carbon Monoxide Sensor with p-n
Switching
Sani Biswas, Preetam Singh, and Amit Kumar Gangwar
Abstract— This work presents a physics-guided machine-learning framework for carbon monoxide concentration infer-
ence from experimentally measured resistance transients of a mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 martials gas sensor exhibiting
temperature-dependent p-n switching behavior. Cycle-level transient responses are represented through physically
interpretable descriptors and complemented by compact fast Fourier transform (FFT)- and discrete wavelet transform
(DWT)-based summaries. Using leakage-aware grouped cross-validation, we study both multi-class concentration clas-
sification and continuous concentration regression for the p-type and n-type sensing regimes separately. Across both
regimes, fused features provide the strongest overall performance, while the physics-guided descriptor block remains
highly competitive, indicating that the dominant concentration information is already encoded in physically meaningful
transient dynamics. The p-type branch shows the best concentration-class discrimination, with the fused Random Forest
classifier reaching approximately 96.5% accuracy, whereas the n-type branch yields the best quantitative concentration
estimation, with the fused Random Forest regressor achieving MAE ≈1.48 ppm and R2 ≈0.992. These results reveal a
clear dual-regime behavior: p-type sensing is particularly favorable for classification, whereas n-type sensing is more
favorable for high-fidelity regression. More broadly, the study demonstrates that leakage-aware, cycle-level, physics-
guided machine learning can extend conventional gas-sensing analysis beyond single-response metrics while preserving
physical interpretability.
Index Terms— carbon monoxide sensing, concentration classification, concentration regression, grouped cross-
validation, mixed-phase SnO-SnO2, n-type sensing, p-type sensing, physics-guided machine learning, resistance tran-
sients.

Conventional gas-sensing analysis often compresses each
sensing event into one or a few scalar quantities, such as
sensor response, response time, recovery time, or maximum
resistance change. These quantities are physically meaningful
and experimentally standard, but they do not fully exploit the
temporal structure of the resistance transient. Two sensing
cycles may exhibit similar peak responses while still differing
substantially in onset sharpness, rise rate, plateau formation,
local curvature, and recovery behavior. From the perspective of
concentration inference, such transient-shape differences may
carry information that is not captured by a single response
ratio alone [3].
This observation motivates the central question of the
present paper: can experimentally measured cycle-level resis-
tance transients from mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 sensors be used
not only to report conventional sensing observables, but also to
perform accurate concentration classification and quantitative
concentration estimation in a physically interpretable manner?
The present work answers this question through a physics-
guided machine-learning framework built on experimentally
meaningful sensing cycles rather than on synthetic data or
purely black-box waveform modeling.
Physical interpretability is central to this framework. In gas-
sensing applications, it is often insufficient for a machine-
learning model merely to produce a numerical prediction;
the representation should remain tied, whenever possible, to
experimentally meaningful sensing quantities. For this reason,
the proposed framework is centered on physics-guided tran-
sient descriptors derived from standard sensing observables,
including baseline resistance, gas-state resistance, response
magnitude, relative response, characteristic time scales, slope-
based kinetic summaries, duration, and area-based descriptors.
Compact fast Fourier transform (FFT) and discrete wavelet
transform (DWT) summaries are also included, but only as
auxiliary descriptors that complement rather than replace the
core physics-guided representation.
A second key issue is statistical credibility. In experimental
gas-sensing datasets, multiple sensing cycles often originate
from the same parent acquisition family or measurement
session. Such cycles are correlated and should not be treated
as fully independent during validation. Random splitting can
therefore produce overly optimistic performance estimates by
allowing family-specific information to leak from training
to testing. To address this, the present study uses grouped
cross-validation throughout, ensuring that cycles from the
same parent acquisition family remain in the same fold. This
leakage-aware design is essential for scientifically defensible
performance assessment.
The present paper is positioned at the intersection of sensing
physics, experimental materials research, and interpretable
machine learning. It does not attempt to replace the under-
lying experimental analysis; rather, it extends that analysis
by showing that transient resistance curves contain a richer
concentration signature than conventional scalar summaries
alone. The framework is applied separately to the p-type
and n-type sensing regimes of the same mixed-phase SnO-
SnO2 platform. This regime-wise treatment is methodologi-
cally preferable because the two branches differ in response
polarity, kinetic behavior, and potentially in the most infor-
mative feature structures. Their comparison then reveals how
the same material platform supports two distinct but related
concentration-inference regimes.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
(i) formulation of an experimentally grounded cycle-level
learning problem for CO sensing using mixed-phase
SnO-SnO2 resistance transients;
(ii) development of a physics-guided feature representation
based on interpretable sensing observables and transient-
kinetic descriptors;
(iii) incorporation of compact FFT and DWT summaries as
auxiliary representations of transient morphology;
(iv) implementation of grouped classification and regression
analysis to control correlation-induced data leakage;
(v) separate quantitative assessment of the p-type and n-type
sensing regimes, followed by a comparative interpreta-
tion of their concentration-inference behavior.

II. PHYSICAL BACKGROUND AND EXPERIMENTAL BASIS
Before introducing the data representation and machine-
learning methodology, it is useful to summarize the physical
and experimental basis of the sensing platform underlying the
present study [6]. The computational framework is not in-
tended as a stand-alone black-box prediction exercise; rather, it
is a physics-guided extension of an experimentally established
mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 CO sensor exhibiting temperature-
dependent p-n switching. This section therefore summarizes
the material platform, the sensing mechanism, the role of
the mixed-phase heterojunction, and the physical rationale for
treating the p-type and n-type branches as two distinct but
related learning regimes.
A. Mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 platform and
temperature-dependent p-n switching
The experimental foundation of the present study is the
mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 thin-film CO sensing platform re-
ported by [6]. In that work, thin films containing coexisting
SnO and SnO2 phases were deposited on Si substrates by reac-
tive magnetron sputtering and characterized using structural,
spectroscopic, and electrical measurements. The coexistence
of the two tin-oxide phases is central to the sensing behavior,
since SnO is typically associated with p-type semiconducting
behavior, whereas SnO2 is classically n-type. The resulting
material therefore forms a mixed-phase heterostructured sys-
tem in which interfacial charge transfer and p-n junction
effects can strongly influence the gas response.
The reported experimental characterization provides direct
evidence for this mixed-phase nature. XRD [12] and XPS [9]
analyses confirmed the coexistence of SnO and SnO2 compo-
nents, while Hall-effect measurements at room temperature
indicated an overall p-type electronic character of the as-
deposited film [7]. In particular, the positive Hall coefficient
reported at room temperature supports the dominance of the
p-type branch under ambient conditions. Thus, although the
material contains both phases, the low-temperature electronic
behavior is not a simple average of two independent oxides;
rather, it reflects the effective conductive state of an interacting
mixed-phase system [10].
A key finding of the underlying materials study is the
observation of temperature-dependent p-n switching in the CO
sensing response. The sensor exhibits a p-type response branch
at lower operating temperatures and an n-type response branch
at higher temperatures. The p-type branch is optimized near
150 ◦C, while the n-type branch is optimized near 325 ◦C, with
the transition between the two regimes occurring near 225 ◦C.
This temperature-dependent reversal of response polarity is
the defining physical reason that the present paper treats the
p-type and n-type datasets as two distinct but related sensing
regimes within a common material platform.
From the viewpoint of the present machine-learning study,
this dual-regime structure is especially valuable. The same
physical device platform supports two concentration-inference
settings with different response direction, potentially differ-
ent kinetics, and possibly different feature relevance. Conse-
quently, the p-type and n-type branches are analyzed separately
and compared only after independent modeling and evaluation.
B. Gas sensing mechanism and resistance-transient
interpretation
The sensing mechanism of metal-oxide-semiconductor gas
sensors is commonly understood in terms of oxygen adsorp-
tion, surface ionization, and target-gas-induced charge transfer.
When the sensor surface is exposed to ambient air, oxygen
molecules are adsorbed and converted into chemisorbed ionic
species such as O−
2 , O−, and O2−, depending on the operating
temperature and surface conditions. These adsorbed oxygen
species withdraw electrons from the near-surface region of
the oxide, thereby modifying the local carrier concentration
and forming a surface space-charge layer. As a result, the
baseline resistance in air is itself a physically meaningful
state variable that depends on both the material type and the
operating temperature [4].
Upon exposure to a reducing gas such as CO, the
chemisorbed oxygen species react with the gas molecules and
release electrons back to the oxide surface. In the underlying
experimental study, this process is described through reactions
of the form [5]:
CO(gas) + O−
(ads) −→CO2(gas) + e−,
2 CO(gas) + O2−
(ads) −→2 CO2(gas) + e−.
These released electrons alter the near-surface carrier balance
and therefore change the measured resistance. The direction
of the resistance change depends on the dominant conduction
type of the operative sensing regime. In an n-type branch, the
returned electrons increase the effective electron concentration
and typically reduce the resistance. In a p-type branch, the
same electron-return process effectively compensates holes
and tends to increase the resistance. Thus, the same CO ex-
posure mechanism can produce opposite resistance directions
depending on whether the operative branch is p-type or n-type.
This polarity reversal is precisely what is observed in the
mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 system. At lower operating temper-
atures, where the SnO-associated p-type contribution domi-
nates, the sensor exhibits p-type dynamic behavior and the re-
sistance increases upon CO exposure. At higher temperatures,
the SnO2-associated n-type contribution becomes dominant,
and the response reverses direction so that the resistance
decreases. The experimentally observed switching near 225 ◦C
is therefore consistent with a change in the effective dominant
conduction pathway across the mixed-phase system.
Beyond response polarity alone, the time-dependent resis-
tance trajectory carries rich kinetic information. A typical
sensing cycle contains at least three physically distinct stages:
(i) a pre-exposure baseline regime in air,

(ii) a gas-exposure regime characterized by adsorption, re-
action, and approach toward a transiently stabilized gas-
state resistance,
(iii) a recovery regime after gas removal, during which
desorption and re-equilibration drive the resistance back
toward the air baseline.
These stages motivate the use of the full resistance transient,
rather than only a single response percentage, as the funda-
mental object of analysis. In particular, the baseline level, peak
or trough response, rise and fall rates, time-to-response, time-
to-recovery, plateau formation, and integrated deviation from
baseline are all physically interpretable quantities that may
depend systematically on concentration.
C. Role of the p-n heterojunction and physical
justification for regime-wise modeling
The mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 system should not be inter-
preted as a trivial linear combination of an isolated p-type
sensor and an isolated n-type sensor. Because the two phases
coexist within the same thin film, their interface forms local
p-n heterojunctions. Owing to the difference in the electronic
structure of SnO and SnO2, charge transfer occurs across
the interface, leading to band bending and the formation of
a depletion region. This interfacial region modifies the local
carrier density and the transport barrier experienced by charge
carriers. Consequently, the observed resistance response is
governed not only by surface adsorption chemistry, but also
by junction-mediated transport.
This heterojunction picture is also important for the design
of the present data-analysis framework. If the p-type and
n-type branches were pooled into a single undifferentiated
learning problem, the model would be forced to learn across
two distinct response polarities, two distinct kinetic regimes,
and potentially different dominant physical mechanisms. Such
a unified treatment may be possible in principle, but it would
obscure physical interpretation at the present stage and reduce
methodological clarity. A regime-wise treatment is therefore
scientifically preferable: the p-type branch and the n-type
branch are first modeled separately, each with its own physi-
cally coherent transient interpretation, and are compared only
after independent analysis.
D. Scope of the present computational extension
It is important to state clearly what the present paper
does and does not attempt to do. The experimental study
of [6] established the materials synthesis, structural charac-
terization, p-n switching behavior, selectivity, stability, and
conventional gas-sensing performance metrics of the mixed-
phase SnO-SnO2 platform. The present work does not replace
that experimental analysis, nor does it claim to reinterpret the
fundamental materials physics independently of the original
measurements.
Instead, the present paper adds a new computational anal-
ysis layer on top of the experimentally measured resistance
transients. Its central objective is to determine whether the
full cycle-level transient contains sufficiently rich information
to support:
(i) multi-class concentration classification, i.e., discrimina-
tion among discrete CO concentration levels;
(ii) continuous concentration regression, i.e., quantitative
estimation of the CO concentration from transient-
response data.
This extension is designed to remain physically interpretable.
For that reason, the later feature representation is grounded pri-
marily in transient descriptors corresponding to standard gas-
sensing observables and kinetic summaries, with transform-
domain descriptors included only as auxiliary supplements.
III. EXPERIMENTAL DATA REPRESENTATION AND
DATASET CONSTRUCTION
Having established the physical and experimental basis of
the mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 sensing platform, we next describe
how the experimentally measured resistance transients are
converted into machine-learning-ready samples. The central
methodological choice is to treat each experimentally mean-
ingful sensing cycle as the fundamental supervised-learning
unit, rather than relying on a single scalar response metric or
on long concatenated multi-cycle traces. This section therefore
defines the data source, the cycle-level sample representation,
the concentration-label structure, and the leakage-aware group-
ing strategy used in the classification and regression analyses.
A. Data source and computational scope
The data analyzed in this work are not synthetically gen-
erated signals and are not derived from arbitrary numeri-
cal simulation. Instead, the machine-learning inputs are con-
structed from experimentally measured resistance transients
of the mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 thin-film CO sensing platform
described in Section II. The underlying measurements orig-
inate from the experimental study of [6], which established
temperature-dependent p-n switching in the same material
system and identified physically distinct p-type and n-type
operating regimes. Accordingly, the present computational
study should be interpreted as a data-analytic extension of ex-
perimentally validated gas-sensing measurements rather than
as a stand-alone black-box prediction exercise.
For machine learning, the experimentally acquired resis-
tance signals are represented at the cycle level. Thus, the
fundamental supervised-learning object is neither a long multi-
cycle trace nor a single scalar response value, but one experi-
mentally meaningful sensing event associated with a known
CO concentration. This choice is central to the methodol-
ogy because it preserves repeated sensing-event variability
while maintaining a direct link to the underlying adsorption–
reaction–recovery process.
Two regime-specific datasets are constructed from the same
mixed-phase SnO-SnO2 platform:
(i) a p-type dataset, corresponding to the lower-temperature
operating branch centered around the experimentally
optimized p-type regime near 150 ◦C;

D. Grouped experimental families and leakage-aware
validation design
A central statistical issue in experimental gas-sensing data is
that multiple cycle-level samples may originate from the same
parent acquisition family, measurement session, or closely
related experimental batch. Such samples are correlated. If
these related cycles are split indiscriminately across training
and testing folds, a model may partially exploit family-specific
structure present in both sets, producing artificially optimistic
performance estimates.
To prevent this form of information leakage, each cycle-
level sample Γi is assigned a group label gi, where samples
sharing the same gi are treated as belonging to the same parent
experimental family. The dataset is therefore represented as

practical terms, cycles originating from the same experimental

advantage of cycle-level learning while avoiding the statistical

IV. PHYSICS-GUIDED PREPROCESSING AND FEATURE
CONSTRUCTION
Having defined the cycle-wise dataset and the leakage-
aware grouped validation protocol in Section III, we now
describe how each measured resistance transient is converted
into a structured representation for supervised learning. The
guiding principle is to preserve the physical meaning of the
sensing response while maintaining numerical stability and
comparative informativeness. Accordingly, the feature pipeline
is built around physics-guided transient descriptors derived
from standard gas-sensing observables, with compact FFT- and
DWT-based summaries included only as auxiliary signal-shape
descriptors.
A. Overview of the representation pipeline
The experimentally measured resistance transients are not
used directly in raw form for final supervised learning. In-
stead, each cycle-level sample is converted into a structured
feature representation through a preprocessing and descriptor-
construction pipeline designed to remain close to the under-
lying sensing physics while ensuring numerical consistency
across cycles.
Starting from one experimentally meaningful sensing cycle
Γi = {(ti,1, Ri,1), . . . , (ti,mi, Ri,mi)},
we first apply light preprocessing to stabilize the extraction
of transient descriptors. This preprocessing is intentionally
conservative: it is not intended to alter the experimental signal,
but only to suppress small-amplitude fluctuations, estimate a
stable baseline, and place cycles on a common representational
footing. Each transient is then mapped to three complementary
feature blocks:
(i) a physics-guided transient descriptor block consisting of
interpretable sensing features;

V. SUPERVISED LEARNING FRAMEWORK, GROUPED
VALIDATION, AND EVALUATION METRICS
Having constructed the cycle-level feature representations
in Section IV, we now specify the downstream supervised-
learning framework used for concentration inference. The
objective is not only to quantify predictive performance, but
also to compare the scientific utility of the four feature configu-
rations under a leakage-aware validation protocol appropriate
for experimentally correlated gas-sensing data. Accordingly,
this section defines the learning tasks, model classes, grouped
cross-validation strategy, and evaluation metrics used through-
out the comparative analysis.
A. Overview of the downstream learning stage
After the representation stage described in Section IV,
each experimentally measured cycle Γi is mapped into one
or more fixed-dimensional feature vectors. The downstream
supervised-learning study is then formulated separately for two
tasks:
(i) multi-class concentration classification;
(ii) continuous concentration regression.
The same evaluation framework is applied independently to
each of the four feature configurations:
ϕphys,
ϕFFT,
ϕDWT,
ϕfused.
B. Feature standardization and model input
Before model fitting, all numerical feature vectors are
standardized in a fold-wise manner. For each training fold,
the scaling transformation is fitted exclusively on the training
subset and then applied to both the training and validation
subsets within that fold. This prevents distributional leakage
and ensures that the validation protocol remains statistically
sound.
C. Classification models
For concentration classification, three supervised-learning
models are compared:
(i) Random Forest (RF),
(ii) Support Vector Machine (SVM) with radial-basis-
function kernel,
(iii) Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
These models were selected to represent complementary
inductive biases for moderate-sized structured feature spaces.
RF provides a nonlinear ensemble-tree model that is often
robust on tabular data, SVM provides a margin-based non-
linear classifier with kernel-induced feature geometry, and
MLP provides a compact feedforward neural model capable
of learning nonlinear feature interactions.
D. Regression models
For continuous concentration estimation, the principal re-
gression model is Random Forest regression, evaluated in two
target-space variants:
(i) linear-target regression;

E. Grouped cross-validation
A defining feature of the present methodology is the use of
grouped cross-validation throughout. As emphasized in Sec-
tion III, multiple cycle-level samples may originate from the
same parent experimental family and are therefore statistically
correlated. Any protocol that splits such related cycles arbitrar-
ily across training and validation sets risks information leakage
and may substantially overestimate predictive performance.
To avoid this problem, all classification and regression
experiments are carried out using grouped K-fold cross-
validation. If gi denotes the group label of the i-th sample,
then all samples sharing the same group label are kept in the
same fold, so that no parent experimental family is split across
training and validation partitions.

G. Model-comparison logic
The benchmarking framework is not intended merely to
identify the numerically strongest predictor. Rather, it is de-
signed to address three scientific questions:
(i) Are the physics-guided transient descriptors alone suffi-
cient for strong concentration inference?
(ii) Do the auxiliary FFT and DWT summaries contribute
additional predictive information?
(iii) Does the fused representation provide a systematic im-
provement over the individual feature blocks?
Accordingly, all candidate models are evaluated under the
same group-preserving cross-validation protocol so that com-
parisons remain fair and leakage-controlled across feature con-
figurations. For each fold, all preprocessing and feature-scaling
operations are fitted exclusively on the training groups and
then applied to the corresponding held-out groups. Fold-wise
predictions are aggregated across the validation partitions, and
the resulting grouped metrics are used for model comparison.
For classification, this procedure identifies the strongest
grouped classifier within each sensing regime, which is then
carried forward for the detailed confusion-matrix analysis
reported in Section VI. For regression, the same grouped
comparison is used to identify the most stable and accurate
continuous concentration estimator, including the comparison
between linear-target and log-target formulations where appro-
priate.

This work presented a physics-guided machine-learning
framework for carbon monoxide concentration inference from
experimentally measured resistance transients of a mixed-

as a computational extension of an experimentally established
sensing platform, combining cycle-level transient analysis,
physically interpretable feature construction, and leakage-
aware grouped cross-validation.

resistance transients carry rich and reliably exploitable con-
