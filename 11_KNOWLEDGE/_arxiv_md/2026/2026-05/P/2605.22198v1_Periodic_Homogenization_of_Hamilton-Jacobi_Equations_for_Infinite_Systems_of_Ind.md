EQUATIONS FOR INFINITE SYSTEMS OF
INDISTINGUISHABLE PARTICLES
SEHO PARK
Abstract. We study the homogenization of first-order Hamilton–Jacobi equa-
tions on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, motivated by systems of infinitely
many indistinguishable particles on the torus.
A central difficulty is that the
analysis takes place in an infinite-dimensional setting, where the compactness
arguments available in finite dimensions break down.
The problem is further
complicated by the possible nonconvexity of the Hamiltonian, which prevents the
direct use of variational methods. Under suitable assumptions on the Hamiltonian
and the initial data, we characterize the effective Hamiltonian through an associ-
ated cell problem and prove that the solutions converge to those of the limiting
equation at rate O(ε1/3). This yields a qualitative and quantitative homogeniza-
tion result for a class of possibly nonconvex Hamilton–Jacobi equations in infinite
dimensions.

The problem is motivated both by the analytical challenge of homogenization in
infinite dimensions and by models of infinite particle systems exhibiting periodicity
and invariance structures. In our setting, the Hamiltonian is assumed to satisfy
periodicity with respect to lattice-valued translations together with invariance under
measure-preserving bijections.
In finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces, the theory of viscosity solutions provides
a robust framework for first-order Hamilton–Jacobi equations [5, 6, 7]. In infinite
dimensions, however, the lack of local compactness makes the basic comparison,
stability, and existence arguments substantially more delicate. The foundational
work of Crandall and Lions established an infinite-dimensional viscosity framework
for Hamilton–Jacobi equations, closely connected to ideas from differential games
[8, 9]. In that setting, Banach spaces with the Radon–Nikodym property play an im-
portant role because smooth variational perturbation principles are available there
[10], and these are central to the test-function arguments underlying the theory.
Additionally, under coercivity assumptions, Ishii’s Perron-type method yields exis-
tence results for the Cauchy and Dirichlet problems [11]. Beyond these foundational
results, a broad literature has studied infinite-dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi equa-
tions from the viewpoint of optimal control and related problems [12, 13]. These
developments provide the starting point for our study of homogenization in an
infinite-dimensional Hilbert space.
For homogenization problems, formal asymptotic expansions suggest that the
limiting dynamics should be governed by an effective equation, while the micro-
scopic oscillations are captured through an associated corrector problem or cell
problem. In finite dimensions, this program was developed systematically by Li-
ons, Papanicolaou, and Varadhan [14], and further advanced by Evans through the
perturbed test function method [15]. Since then, both qualitative and quantitative
aspects of homogenization have been extensively developed [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21].
In infinite-dimensional settings, by contrast, the literature remains comparatively
limited. Existing results on cell problems and effective dynamics are often tied to
convexity, variational structure, or methods from weak KAM theory [22]. In par-
ticular, Gomes and Nurbekyan study the cell problem in an infinite-dimensional
framework [1]. This work is closely related to their variational work on minimiz-
ers in Hilbert spaces [23], and builds on earlier work of Gangbo and Tudorascu on
infinite-dimensional weak KAM theory and action-minimizing trajectories in La-
grangian systems [24, 2].

indistinguishable particles on the d-dimensional torus, but we allow general, possi-
bly nonconvex Hamiltonians for which the connection to an underlying Lagrangian
or weak KAM structure is not directly available [18, 25, 20]. We also note the re-

2. Perron’s method and the convergence
In this section, we establish the basic well-posedness and compactness properties
needed for the homogenization of (CP)ε. The arguments are inspired by the finite-
dimensional treatment of Perron’s method and Lipschitz estimates in Chapter 1 of
[7], together with Ishii’s infinite-dimensional Perron framework for Hamilton-Jacobi
equations on Banach spaces with Radon–Nikodym property [11].
Before turning to Perron’s method, we recall the role of the Radon–Nikodym
property in the viscosity theory of Hamilton–Jacobi equations in infinite dimensions.
A Banach space V is said to have the Radon–Nikodym property if, for every bounded
lower semicontinuous function φ on a closed ball B ⊂V and every ε > 0, there exists
p ∈V ∗with ∥p∥≤ε such that φ + p attains its minimum on B. This variational
perturbation principle, due to Stegall [10], plays a fundamental role in infinite-
dimensional viscosity theory, since closed bounded sets are no longer compact and
extrema need not exist. In the work of Crandall and Lions [8, 9], the property
provides the geometric framework underlying the comparison principle and hence
uniqueness, and it also enters the existence theory through Perron-type arguments
by Ishii [11]. In our setting, V = L2(I; Rd) is a Hilbert space, hence reflexive, and
therefore automatically has the Radon–Nikodym property.

3. The cell problem and the effective equation
In this section, we identify the effective Hamiltonian through an associated cell
problem and then derive the effective equation satisfied by the locally uniform limit
of uε. The overall strategy is analogous to the periodic homogenization theory in
finite dimensions: one first solves the cell problem for each macroscopic momentum,
then defines the effective Hamiltonian from the corresponding ergodic constant,
and finally applies a perturbed test function argument in the spirit of Evans [15].
The main additional difficulty in the present infinite-dimensional setting is that the
microscopic variable lives in V , where local compactness is no longer available. In
particular, when carrying out the perturbed test function argument, one cannot rely
on the usual finite-dimensional maximization procedure. Instead, we use the Radon–
Nikodym property through Stegall’s theorem to produce approximate maximizers.
At the same time, to recover compactness on the microscopic side, we pass from V
to the compact quotient space SSd. Thus, both the Radon–Nikodym property and
the compactness of SSd play essential roles in the analysis below.

Theorem 1.2 completes the homogenization analysis by strengthening the qualita-
tive convergence of Theorem 1.1 to a quantitative estimate. The rate O(ε1/3) is the
same rate obtained in the finite-dimensional nonconvex periodic setting, but the
present argument requires additional structural ingredients: the reduction to the
mean configuration, the compact quotient SSd, and the use of the Radon–Nikodym
property to compensate for the lack of local compactness in V . Thus, although the
limiting equation is finite-dimensional, the proof of convergence is genuinely infinite-
dimensional because the cell problem and the oscillatory correctors are posed on the
full configuration space modulo the symmetry structure.
