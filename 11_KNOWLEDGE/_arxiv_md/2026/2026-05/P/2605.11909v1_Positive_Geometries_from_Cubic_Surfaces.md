Positive Geometries from Cubic Surfaces
Bernd Sturmfels and Simon Telen
Abstract
We present a study of cubic surfaces from the novel perspective of positive geometry.
Our positive geometries have dimension two (the surface minus its 27 lines), dimension
three (its complement in 3-space), and dimension four (the moduli space). In each case
we explore the positive arrangement, its combinatorial rank, and the canonical forms.
1
Introduction
The cubic surface with its 27 lines is one of the most beautiful vistas in 19th century math-
ematics. The subject has inspired many generations of algebraic geometers. In this article,
we show how the rich classical theory of cubic surfaces lends itself naturally to the current
perspective of positive geometry [18, 19, 23, 30], a subject at the interface of mathematics
and theoretical physics [2, 4]. The paper is partly expository, building on earlier work on del
Pezzo surfaces [11, 12, 25, 27] while incorporating a range of recent developments in positive
geometry [7, 9, 14, 17]. Along the way, we establish several new definitions and new results.
Our exposition will be guided by the following result. We shall derive a detailed proof.
Theorem 1.1. Let X be a general real cubic surface in P3 whose 27 lines are real, and let Y be
the union of these lines. The complement (X\Y )R consists of 130 curvy polygons, namely 10
triangles, 90 quadrilaterals and 30 pentagons. Each of these is a positive geometry for (X, Y ).
Their 130 canonical differential forms span the space Ω2
log(X\Y ) which has dimension 109.
Here P3 denotes the projective space of dimension 3, with homogeneous coordinates
y = (y0, y1, y2, y3). A cubic surface X is the solution set in P3 of a homogeneous polynomial
equation of degree three. The following cubic equation in y will serve as our running example:

What is a Positive Geometry?
Positive Geometry is an emerging field of mathematics with origins in theoretical physics
[4, 18, 23]. It studies the geometry and combinatorics underlying physical observables such
as scattering amplitudes and cosmological correlators. Here “geometry” is meant in a broad
sense, including complex, real and tropical algebraic geometry. The discovery of the ampli-
tuhedron by Arkani-Hamed and Trnka was an early milestone [5]. This was followed by nu-
merous other geometries. The physical principles underlying these objects prescribe several
common features. This motivates the development of a unifying mathematical framework.
The first step was taken in [4], which gives an axiomatic definition of a positive geometry.
The Hodge-theoretic approach in [7] suggests an alternative definition, first formulated
in [30, Definition 1.13]. The two definitions are not quite equivalent, but many relevant
examples align with both, as shown in the lecture notes [30]. Our Definition 5.2 imposes
reality constraints on the objects of [30, Definition 1.13]. While the debate about the question
in the section title is still ongoing, we here propose the following workflow for practitioners.
We use the term domain for an open semialgebraic subset of some Rd that is orientable.
Workflow 5.1. When a domain S is suspected to be a positive geometry, proceed as follows:
1. Find an irreducible projective variety X and a divisor Y ⊂X such that S is embedded
in XR as (the closure of) a union of regions in XR\YR. We view S as a relative cycle.
2. Show that (X, Y ) is a positive arrangement.
3. Count the regions of (X, Y ) and determine the combinatorial rank.
4. Examine whether the combinatorial rank equals the real combinatorial rank.
5. Identify the canonical form ω(S) by computing residues and proceeding recursively.
6. Your positive geometry is the pair (S, ω(S)). Repeat step 5 for other regions of interest.
We now turn to the formal set-up and define the italicized objects in Workflow 5.1.
Consider a d-dimensional irreducible complex projective variety X and a divisor Y ⊊X
such that X\Y is smooth. A relative d-cycle of the pair (X, Y ) is a d-dimensional chain
in X whose boundary is contained in Y . The formal Q-linear combinations of the relative
d-cycles constitute the Q-vector space Zd(X, Y ). Taking the quotient of Zd(X, Y ) by the
group of boundaries gives the relative homology group Hd(X, Y ). The pair (X, Y ) is said to
have genus zero if the mixed Hodge numbers h−p,0 of Hd(X, Y ) for p > 0 are all equal to zero
[7, Section 3]. This technical condition can be checked using the tools in [7, Section 3.2].
Definition 5.2. A positive arrangement is a pair (X, Y ) of an irreducible complex projective
variety X and a divisor Y on X, which satisfies the following four conditions:
1. The variety X is defined over R, and X has a smooth real point.
2. Each irreducible component of Y is defined over R and has a smooth real point.
3. The complement X\Y of Y in X is contained in Xreg. Equivalently, Sing(X) ⊆Y .
4. The pair (X, Y ) has genus zero.
Condition 1 ensures that the real points of X are Zariski dense in X. Condition 2 does the
same for each irreducible component of Y . Our running example is a positive arrangement.

their unions; the union of two regions corresponds to the sum of their cycles in Zd(X, Y ).
