Perpetual Fully Online Horizon-Free Approximate Fairness
IDO KAHANA, Ariel University, Israel
EREL SEGAL-HALEVI, Ariel University, Israel
NOAM HAZON, Ariel University, Israel
Many decision processes run for a long and unknown duration: in each round new requests arrive, an
irrevocable choice must be made immediately, and the system is judged by ongoing fairness requirements.
Examples include food banks allocating donated items as they arrive, computing systems repeatedly scheduling
scarce resources across users, and institutions making repeated public decisions (e.g., which proposals or cases
to prioritize) while remaining fair over time.
A key challenge in such settings is that fairness requirements are often naturally scale-dependent. For
example, in fair item allocation, it is common to require that the unfairness is bounded by the highest values
of items seen so far. Thus, the scale of fairness changes over time.
We propose a general approach to online fairness based on deficits, which measure each requirement’s
current shortfall relative to a time-varying benchmark.
Within this framework, we analyze a simple fully online rule that, in each round, chooses the action that
best improves the next-round deficit profile. We prove anytime (prefix-wise) guarantees: after every round,
all tracked requirements remain satisfied up to a slack that grows only on the order of √𝑡(up to logarithmic
factors), and we show this growth is unavoidable in general. We instantiate the framework for online allocation
of indivisible goods (yielding natural relaxations of proportionality and envy-freeness) and for online public
decision-making. In contrast to previous works on online fair allocation, our rule does not need to know the
horizon (the total number of rounds), nor any other information on the future (e.g. the maximum item value).
Moreover, our guarantees hold perpetually, at each individual time step.
Additional Key Words and Phrases: online algorithms, online fair division, indivisible goods, approximate
proportionality, envy, potential functions, public decision-making

Introduction
Many real-world systems make decisions repeatedly and under uncertainty. At each time step, new
information arrives, an action must be chosen immediately, and the choice is irrevocable—once
taken, it becomes part of the history and cannot be changed later. Meanwhile, the system is expected
to satisfy various fairness requirements perpetually — over a long and unknown duration.
1.1
Motivating application: approximately-fair allocation of indivisible items
Consider a food-bank that receives short shelf-life food donations, and must allocate them imme-
diately among several organizations, which may have different preferences over the donations.
Because storage is limited (or forbidden by health regulations), the allocation must be made upon
arrival [Aleksandrov et al., 2015, Aleksandrov and Walsh, 2020]. The cumulative allocation at each
time step should be fair. As another example, consider a cloud-computing controller that repeatedly
assigns CPU/GPU time, memory, or bandwidth to jobs as they arrive. These assignments must be
made at high frequency, and still satisfy some pre-specified fairness requirements. In such systems
a “round” can correspond to a millisecond-scale scheduling decision, so it is natural to evaluate
fairness across thousands (or millions) of rounds. [Verma et al., 2015]
Common fairness measures are approximations of proportionality (each agent receives a subjec-
tive value of at least 1/𝑛the total value, where 𝑛is the number of agents) and envy-freeness (each
agent values its value at least as much as the value of any other agent). As a proportional allocation
may not exist even for a single item, it is common to pursue an additive approximation called
PROP𝑐, which requires that each agent gets at least her proportional share minus the 𝑐highest
values of items allocated to others [Conitzer et al., 2017, Segal-Halevi and Suksompong, 2019].
Analogously, envy-freeness is relaxed to EF𝑐, which requires that each agent does not envy any
other agent after removing 𝑐highest valued items allocated to the other agent.
In the offline setting (when all items arrive at the same time), it is easy to attain PROP1 and EF1,
for example by allocating the items in a round-robin fashion. However, the online setting is much
more challenging. As an illustration, we show that some natural algorithms might yield very bad
approximations to proportionality, even for two agents.
Consider first the round-robin algorithm. Suppose every item that comes in an odd round is
worth 1 to both agents, and every item that comes in an even round is worth 𝜖≪1 to both agents.
Then, for any 𝑐, the allocation violates PROP𝑐after 4𝑐/(1 −𝜖) ∈Θ(𝑐) rounds. Second, consider a
greedy algorithm, that allocates each item so that the smallest utility of an agent is maximized.

worth 1 to agent 1 and 𝜖≪1 to agent 2. Then the next ⌈1/𝜖⌉items will be allocated to agent 2. For
any 𝑐such that 2𝑐+ 1 < 1/𝜖, the allocation violates PROP𝑐by round 2𝑐+ 3.

Our goal is to develop a fully online algorithm, that is, guarantee fairness perpetually, after each
individual step, without any knowledge of the total number of rounds or the maximum item value.
In Section B we consider several natural ways to extend the algorithm of Benadè et al. [2018] to a
fully online algorithm, and explain why they do not work.
In this work, we present a general framework that achieves perpetual fully online fairness, not
only for item allocation but also to more general online decision-making problems. As an example
of such more general problem, consider a supreme court that repeatedly decides which petitions
to hear (grant certiorari) from a large pool of filings each term. Only a small fraction of petitions
receive full review and oral argument, and denials are effectively final. Even so, the long-run
sequence of agenda choices is expected to remain fair over time across constituencies (e.g., regions,
litigant types, or issue areas) [Supreme Court of the United States, 2019].

A general framework: deficits (Section 3). We consider a decision-maker who, in each round,
chooses an action from a feasible set, determined by the current input. The outcome is evaluated by
a family of quality variables. For each quality variable, we track a deficit value. The goal is to ensure
that all deficits remain below a time-dependent threshold. Formally, given a threshold 𝑐𝑡> 0, call a
quality variable 𝑐𝑡-disappointed if its deficit exceeds 𝑐𝑡. An outcome is 𝑐𝑡-fair if no quality variable
is 𝑐𝑡-disappointed. The decision-maker wants to ensure that the outcome is 𝑐𝑡-fair at every 𝑡≥0.
Why perpetual guarantees are hard. In offline problems, or in online problems where the horizon
𝑇is known, algorithms can sometimes be tuned to𝑇and analyzed only at the final time. In perpetual
systems this is not enough: we want a single process that runs forever and maintains guarantees at
every prefix. Moreover, greedy “fix the worst quality variable right now” instincts can behave poorly
over long durations: even if each individual step looks reasonable, small systematic imbalances can
accumulate, and the slack needed to keep all quality variables satisfied may grow linearly with
time (see Section 1.1). This leads to a basic question that applies across domains: How fast must the
threshold 𝑐𝑡grow with time?
A generic perpetual online decision rule (Section 4). We present a simple greedy rule that chooses
an action at every round. The rule is fully online; it does not need to know the horizon or any other

Related Work
2.1
Online fair item allocation
Most works on fair division of indivisible items study the offline setting; see [Amanatidis et al.,
2023] for a recent survey. In recent years, however, there has been a growing interest in online or
dynamic fair division; see the survey by Aleksandrov and Walsh [2020].
Specifically, Aleksandrov et al. [2015] study online allocation of indivisible items in a restricted
setting in which agents report 0/1-type values (“like” / “dislike”) for each arriving item, motivated
by applications such as food banks. They analyze simple online mechanisms and their fairness
properties over time. These works are domain-specific, but they highlight the same basic difficulty
that motivates our paper: decisions are irrevocable, inputs arrive over a long and unknown duration,
and fairness must be monitored repeatedly rather than only at a single terminal time.

Several works consider stochastic rather than adversarial input models. Jiang et al. [2019] study
online fair division for two agents under stochastic arrivals. Zeng and Psomas [2020] also consider
stochastic adversaries and give high-probability fairness/efficiency guarantees. Gao et al. [2021]
study online market equilibrium under stochastic valuations. Our work focuses on the harder
setting of a deterministic, adaptive adversary.
A related direction allows recourse—retroactive modifications of past allocations—to improve
fairness ex post; see He et al. [2019]. In contrast, our model is irrevocable: once an action is taken it
is never changed later, and our guarantees are evaluated on the realized prefix without corrections.
A closely related but different line of work is temporal fair division [Cookson et al., 2025, Elkind
et al., 2025]. Similar to our setting, the goal is to guarantee fairness repeatedly over time. However,
these models typically assume substantially more information about the future (e.g., access to the
full sequence upfront or planning within windows), whereas we require fully online, irrevocable
decisions with worst-case inputs.
There is also growing work on repeated allocation, where similar or identical resources recur
and fairness is measured across repetitions; examples include repeated fair allocation of indivisible
items [Igarashi et al., 2024], repeated matching [Caragiannis and Narang, 2023], and repeated
house allocation [Micheel and Wilczyński, 2024]. These frameworks differ from ours in that they
usually optimize fairness over cycles or repetitions (often with structural assumptions on the
repetition pattern), rather than providing worst-case, anytime guarantees for an arbitrarily long
and adversarial sequence.
2.2
Online fair public decision-making
Another related model is public decision-making. In this model, each round selects a single alternative,
and the chosen alternative may give positive utility to many agents (so it generalizes private-good
allocation). Freeman et al. [2017] study the online version of public decision-making and analyze
greedy mechanisms with welfare objectives. Kahana and Hazon [2023] study fairness notions
and impossibility results for online public decision-making. These settings fit naturally into our
framework viewpoint, and we include public decision-making as one of our concrete instantiations.
Related long-run collective decision models include perpetual voting and proportionality notions
over an unbounded horizon; see, e.g., Lackner [2020], Lackner and Maly [2023]. Public decision-
making is also closely related to online allocation of public goods; for example, Banerjee et al.
[2023] study proportionally fair online allocation of public goods with predictions.
2.3
Online vector balancing
A closely related mathematical line studies online discrepancy and online vector balancing, where an
algorithm makes irrevocable online assignments in order to keep many coordinates simultaneously
balanced over time. This line is closely connected to online envy minimization, especially when
one seeks guarantees that hold at every prefix. For example, Bansal et al. [2020] and Bansal et al.
[2021] develop online discrepancy/vector-balancing techniques under stochastic/oblivious arrival
models, and Kulkarni et al. [2024] give optimal bounds for online discrepancy minimization in
their setting. More recently, Halpern et al. [2025] make the relationship between online envy
minimization (for 𝑛= 2 agents) and multicolor discrepancy explicit and study equivalences and
separations across adversary models. These works typically focus on a single global balancing

• Constants and parameter dependence. The square-root dependence on 𝑡is tight in the worst case,

• Extensions to other domains. Because the analysis is phrased in terms of the deficits {𝑧𝑡

A useful next step is to find additional applications that can be modeled by our framework.
• Stronger fairness objectives. We focused on pointwise 𝑐𝑡-fairness (no disappointed variables).
In applications one may want guarantees that are group-aware, weighted, or prioritized (e.g.,
different 𝑞carry different importance). This might possibly be incorporated by modifying
the potential (or by duplicating quality variables with weights) while preserving the same
moment-based proof strategy.
• Beyond worst-case adversaries. Our guarantees and lower bounds are proved against a fully

• Limited recourse. While our main model is fully irrevocable, many practical systems allow small
