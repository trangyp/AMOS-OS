Permutation-symmetric quantum trajectories
Elliot W. Lloyd,1 Aleksandra A. Ziolkowska,1 and Jonathan Keeling1
1SUPA, School of Physics and Astronomy, University of St Andrews, St Andrews, KY16 9SS, United Kingdom
(Dated: May 2026)
We show how one may perform a stochastic unraveling which respects weak permutation symmetry
for models of N emitters coupled to a common system (e.g. a cavity mode). For problems involving
2-level emitters, such an unravelling reduces the computational cost from O(N 5) to O(N 2), and with
additional refinements, allows reduction to O(N). This significantly increases the range of system
sizes for which one can model exact quantum dynamics of such systems. We further show how the
method can also be applied to d-level systems, with computational effort scaling as O(N d(d−1)/2),
and we show it allows large-N simulations for d = 3.
Many paradigmatic models of cavity QED involve N
emitters coupled to a common cavity mode. Such mod-
els arise in contexts including simple models of las-
ing [1, 2], superradiant dynamics [3], superradiant phase
transitions of the Dicke model [4, 5], and single-mode
models of photon condensation [6]. Recently there has
been increasing interest in versions of such models where
the emitters are more complex, with multiple electronic
states, or dressed by vibrational modes [7–9]. In the most
general case, the cost of simulating such models scales
exponentially with N, however, when the emitters are
identical this cost reduces significantly. If all terms in the
model (the Hamiltonian and all dissipators) involve only
sums of single emitter terms then one has strong permu-
tation symmetry and may rewrite the model in terms of
collective operators. For two-level emitters, these are to-
tal spin operators, and the the simulation cost reduces to
O(N 2N 2
c ) (where Nc is the truncation of cavity occupa-
tion). If there are also individual dissipation terms for the
two-level emitters, the model still has weak [10, 11] per-
mutation symmetry. This prevents using collective spins,
but one can use permutation symmetric density matrix
approaches with a cost O(N 3N 2
c ). Such approaches have
been applied to many of the models listed above [12–17].
While simulating the density matrix evolution can pro-
vide most properties of interest for a Markovian open
quantum system—i.e. operator expectations and corre-
lations including multi-time correlations—there has re-
cently been much interest in the properties that can
only be found from the dynamics of individual quantum
trajectories rather than their ensemble average [18–22].
Quantum trajectories [23–28] can be thought of as simu-
lating individual experimental runs, and thus the state of
the system conditioned on a measurement record. Dif-
ferent forms of measurement (e.g. photon counting vs
homodyne measurement) lead to different forms of quan-
tum trajectory, such as the quantum jump formalism vs
the quantum state diffusion equations [27, 28]. Differ-
ent measurement apparatus correspond to different forms
of “stochastic unravelling”, and thus different forms of
entanglement within the trajectories, even though the
ensemble-averaged density matrix may be the same [29].
Quantum trajectories also provide a route to cheaper nu-
merical simulations, as one simulates a wavefunction with
a size given by the Hilbert space dimension, dH, rather
than the density matrix size d2
H.
For problems with weak permutation symmetry—i.e.
involving individual dissipation terms—direct applica-
tions of stochastic unravelling causes a problem. Indi-
vidual dissipation of the emitters means that measure-
ments corresponding to that dissipation make the emitter
states inequivalent: a naive unravelling would require tra-
jectories that break the permutation symmetry. Rather
than reducing the computational time, such unravelling
increases it back to exponential in N.
In this paper we show that when formulated in terms
of collective spin representations, one may perform a
stochastic unravelling of the master equation that holds
within the space of permutation symmetric density ma-
trices. Considering emitters as two-level systems (2LS),
this approach leads to a computational cost that grows
only linearly with system size, O(NNc), the same scal-
ing as would occur in the absence of individual dissipa-
tion. We further show that the factor Nc (which typically
needs to be of order Nc ∝N) can be eliminated. This
means a reduction from O(N 5) for density matrix sim-
ulations to O(N) for the approaches we describe here.
This enables massively larger simulations than were pre-
viously possible with individual dissipation. We further
show how the method can be extended beyond 2LS, to
d-level systems, allowing large quantum simulations of
more complex models.

next discuss two direct extensions that allow an even
greater improvement in computational cost, by reducing
the factor of Nc to constant in the scaling noted above.

While the above method allows linear scaling for prob-
lems with weak U(1) symmetry, no such symmetry exists
for the Dicke model. However, an alternate approach,
building on an idea discussed in Refs. [2, 35, 36], can be
applied. The idea is to consider stochastic unravellings
where the phase symmetry of the photon field is broken,
such that ⟨ˆa†ˆa⟩≃|⟨ˆa⟩|2. In such cases, one can signifi-
cantly reduce the state space needed for the photon by
making a unitary transform to displace the photon field
ˆa →ˆa+α such that the displaced field is centered around
the vacuum state, allowing a smaller truncation Nc.

not allow for the improvement described above. Quan-

count photons, rather than measuring the phase of the
cavity field, and thus ⟨ˆa⟩remains zero throughout the
quantum jump evolution. To have a non-zero expectation
of ˆa one should instead consider the quantum state dif-

Our discussion so far has been restricted to 2LS. How-
ever, inspired by the recent work Bastin and Martin [37],
our results can be directly extended to d-level systems,
as discussed in the supplement [31]. Such an extension
comes at the cost of more involved notation in terms of
Young diagrams (labeled ν) and standard Weyl tableaux
(labeled Wν) [38]. The structure of equations for the d-
level systems is analogous to that for the 2LS, with ν
playing the role of J and Wν playing the role of M. The
matrix elements can be found through explicit formu-
lae [37] in terms of Gelfand-Tsetlin patterns [39], see [31]
for details. As such, the computational cost of modeling
the d-level system is given by the number of distinct Weyl
tableau of a fixed shape ν, and at large N this asymp-
totically approaches N d(d−1)/2. The same simplifications
around cavity truncation can still be applied, eliminating
Nc from this scaling. This compares to N d2−1N 2
c for the
permutation symmetric density matrix approach. For a
three-level system (with Nc ∝N) this means reduction
from N 10 to N 3 scaling, allowing significantly larger sys-
tem sizes to be reached. To illustrate this, Fig. 3 shows
results for a model of inversionless lasing with three-level
systems introduced in Ref. [40]; the model is summa-
rized in [31]. The increased system sizes accessible via
trajectories confirm the behavior anticipated in Ref. [40]
of mean-field results being recovered at large N.
The scaling of run-time with N still remains pro-
hibitive for large d, but simulations with d = 4, 5 and
mesoscopic N ∼30, 10 respectively should be possible,
in contrast to density matrix methods where d ≤3.
In summary we have shown that one can perform
stochastic unravelling in a way that respects permuta-
tion symmetry, leading to a significant increase in the
size of systems for which exact quantum simulations can
be performed. The fact that these speedups require a
specific prescription for unravelling—treating the indi-
vidual dissipation through mapping to effective operators
ˆL ˆ
X,τ and then using a quantum jump unravelling—can
be seen as an example of the observation that different
forms of unravelling can lead to different computational
complexity [29]. The methods described here can be ap-
plied across the range of cavity-QED models describing

One important context is lattice problems with on-site

For such models, one could formulate a quantum
jump approach where momentum is conserved between
jumps, allowing more efficient trajectory simulations of
lattice models [28].
Another direction for future work is to explore how the
ideas here could be extended to non-Markovian stochas-
tic unravellings. The Hierarchy Of Pure States (HOPS)
method [36, 41] can be considered as a non-Markovian ex-
tension of the quantum state diffusion equation. The key
role of quantum jumps in the results above, however, sug-
gests there could be substantial computational benefits
in developing non-Markovian quantum jump formalisms,
which might allow for efficient exact simulations of non-
Markovian individual dissipation processes in systems of
N emitters. Such exact methods could be complemen-
tary to recent approaches using 1/N expansions via the
BBGKY hierarchy [42].

GENERALIZATION TO d-LEVEL SYSTEMS
In this section we summarize how the results in the
main paper can be generalized to d-level systems. We
make use of the approach and notation of Bastin and
Martin [37]. As discussed there (as well as in textbooks
e.g. Ref. [38]), for the general d-level case it is conve-
nient to replace the labels J, M by Young diagrams and
standard Weyl tableaux. To make our description self-
contained, we briefly describe these standard results in
the following sections before showing how this leads to
the effective jump operators.
Young diagram and Weyl tableau notation
We will use the Greek letters ν, µ, λ to indicate a Young
diagram representing N systems with d levels. Such a
diagram consists of N boxes arranged in at most d rows
such that the length of each successive row is not in-
creasing.
The diagram represents the pattern of sym-
metrization and antisymmetrization that construct the
overall state: boxes in the same row are to be mutually
symmetrized, while boxes in the same column are to be
mutually antisymmetrized.
Note that the object ν contains information equivalent
to the combination of integers J and N. For 2LS, the
index J corresponds to half of the difference in length
between the first and second row, so that maximum J
means all boxes are in the first row, and J = 0 (possible
only for even N) means equal numbers of boxes in each
row.
The analog of M for the d-level system is the standard
Weyl tableau. This object, denoted Wν, consists of plac-
ing species labels 1 . . . d in the Young diagram ν such
that (a) along each row the labels are not decreasing,
and (b) down each column the labels are strictly increas-
ing. For 2LS, the two labels 1, 2 are normally replaced
by ↓, ↑. The index M counts half the difference between
the numbers of ↑and ↓boxes. The fact that the second
row can only contain ↑, and that the sites above it must
be ↓encodes the standard constraint |M| ≤J.
As with the two-level case, a further index, in addition
to Wν is needed to uniquely specify a quantum state.
This index (which we denoted as T for the two-level case
discussed in the main text) now becomes Tν, which is
a standard Young tableau.
This consists of a way of
putting each of the N labels 1 . . . N into the boxes of
the Young diagram ν, such that labels in each row and
each column are always increasing and each label appears
only once. The tableau Tν specifies a particular way to
associate the N different d-level systems to the patterns
of symmetrization and antisymmetrization.
