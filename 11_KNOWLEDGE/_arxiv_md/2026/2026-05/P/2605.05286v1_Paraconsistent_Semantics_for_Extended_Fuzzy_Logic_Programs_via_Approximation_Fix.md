Abstract In logic programming, negation can be interpreted in various
ways. Probably best known is the concept of “negation as failure”, where
“not p” is true if we have no evidence for p. On the other hand, strong neg-
ation requires that we have evidence for p being false. Defining semantics
for logic programs containing both kinds of negation is a challenging
task, and this becomes even more challenging when combining this with
other extensions of logic programming, e.g. fuzziness. In this work, we
use the framework of approximating fixpoint theory to formulate well-
behaved semantics for fuzzy logic programs containing both “by-failure”
and strong negation. We show that this framework generalizes several
existing semantics as well as giving rise to a host of new semantics.
1
Introduction
Logic programs (LPs) are among the oldest rule-based formalisms within the field
of artificial intelligence [26]. While originally used for declarative programming,
their modern incarnation in answer set programming [7,16] enjoys widespread
practical success for declarative problem-solving, while using the same basic
constructs at the core of the language. Researchers noted early on that the
inherent restriction to two truth values limits applicability, and proposed gener-
alizations from crisp (two-valued) to fuzzy (many-valued) logics – for resolution
in general [27], and for (definite) logic programs in particular [37,39,20].
Naturally, “weak” negation (indicating absence of provability and thus also
known as negation by failure [8]) has been combined with fuzziness, leading to nor-
mal fuzzy logic programs offering various semantics, including stable models [42,9]
and well-founded semantics [28]. On the other hand, crisp normal LPs have also
been extended with “strong” negation (indicating established falsity [17]), lead-
ing to extended logic programs with both kinds of negation. While the original
proposal of semantics for extended LPs required answer sets (stable models) of
programs to be consistent, that is, disallowed the presence of an atom and its
classical negation in the model, it did not take long until paraconsistent semantics

were proposed for extended LPs – again both well-founded semantics [34] and
stable model semantics [35]. The idea of paraconsistent reasoning is that incon-
sistency is “allowed” to occur in a model, but is “locally contained” and thus does
not lead to the usual ex falsum quodlibet issues of classical logic [5], i.e. it avoids
trivializing logical reasoning. And while extended fuzzy LPs with stable model
semantics do exist, they either do not offer paraconsistent reasoning at all [33,22],
or do not offer it in full generality [2,12,1] – they base their semantics on the
coherence principle, which requires them to make some restricting assumptions
on the negation functions that can possibly be employed.
The common theme in all of these works on various extensions of normal
LPs is their piecemeal nature, the high amount of manual labour involved, and
the sheer repetitiveness with which authors took the same ideas, such as the
monotone one-step consequence operator of van Emden and Kowalski [14] and
the reduct of Gelfond and Lifschitz [17], and manually adapted them to cover
the next extension. The result is a scattered landscape with no uniform syntax,
semantics with the same name but formally unclear relationships, and varying
assumptions on possible sets of truth values and logical connectives.
In this paper, we unify a considerable portion of the existing work in this area.
First, we employ a syntax that covers major recurring features of the literature
on extended fuzzy logic programming. Second, and more importantly, we define
an operator that captures a considerable number of existing semantics. This is
possible because we employ the powerful framework of approximation fixpoint
theory (AFT) [13]. AFT reduces the amount of manual labour required to a
minimum, as it already incorporates – within its standard definitions – ideas
that previous authors had to rework explicitly in their respective settings, such
as the program reduct of Gelfond and Lifschitz [17]. More concretely, we show
how our single operator reconstructs the semantics of Sakama [34], Sakama
and Inoue [35], Saad [33], and Cornejo et al. [10], while also giving rise to new
semantics, in particular the first stable model and well-founded semantics offering
paraconsistent reasoning for extended fuzzy LPs that allows for non-involutive
negations (where double negation is not always identity, e.g. Gödel negation).
The rest of the paper proceeds as follows. In the next section, we give the
necessary background. Section 3 introduces the syntax of the language we use,
while Section 4 defines our main operator. The following two sections reconstruct
existing semantics of extended (fuzzy) logic programs, with Section 7 providing
additional results beyond mere reconstruction. We finally discuss avenues for
future work.

2.1
Approximation Fixpoint Theory (AFT)
A particular way of defining semantics for programming languages is to associate
a program P with a transformation operator TP that transforms a given input-
output relation R into an updated relation TP (R). In this view, the semantics of

program P is given by the least fixpoint of operator TP . Van Emden and Kowal-
ski [14] applied this way of defining semantics to logic programming, developing
the first one-step logical consequence operator and employing its monotonicity in
conjunction with Tarski’s fixpoint theorem [38] to define the semantics of definite
logic programs with potentially recursive predicate definitions.
More formally, an operator is a (total) function O: V →V on a partially
ordered set (V, ⩽). Here, we even require (V, ⩽) to be a complete lattice, that is,
we require that every subset S ⊆V has a least upper bound (lub) WS ∈V and
greatest lower bound (glb) VS ∈V ; this entails that (V, ⩽) has both a least element
0 = VV = W∅and a greatest element 1 = WV = V∅. An operator O: V →V
is monotone iff x ⩽y implies O(x) ⩽O(y), and antimonotone iff x ⩽y implies
O(y) ⩽O(x). An element x ∈V with O(x) = x is a fixpoint of O; if O(x) ⩽x
then x is a pre-fixpoint of O; if x ⩽O(x) then x is a post-fixpoint of O.
A fundamental result in lattice theory, Tarski’s fixpoint theorem [38], states
that whenever O is a monotone operator on a complete lattice (V, ⩽), the
set {x ∈V | O(x) = x} of its fixpoints forms a complete lattice itself, and so
has a least element lfp(O) and a greatest element gfp(O). While this result is
immensely useful, it is not constructive; however, constructive versions exist and
tell us how to actually construct least fixpoints [30,11]: To this end, one defines,
for any x ∈V , the iterated (transfinite) application of O to x via O0(x) := x,
Oα+1(x) := O(Oα(x)) for successor ordinals, and Oγ(x) := W{Oα(x) | α < γ}
for limit ordinals. It is then known that (for monotone O) there exists an ordinal
α such that Oα(0) = lfp(O) [6]; by duality, we have gfp(O) = Oκ(1) for some κ.
In order to apply Tarski’s fixpoint theorem [38] (or its constructive versions),
it is thus necessary to have an operator that is monotone, a condition that is no
longer fulfilled when moving from definite to normal logic programs, i.e., when
allowing weak negation in rule bodies. To still have a useful fixpoint theory in the
case of non-monotone operators, Denecker, Marek, and Truszczyński (DMT) [13]
fundamentally generalized the theory underlying the fixpoint-based approach to
semantics, thereby founding what is now known as approximation fixpoint theory.
Its underlying idea is that when an operator of interest does not have properties
that guarantee the existence of fixpoints, one can approximate this operator in a
more fine-grained algebraic structure where fixpoint existence can be guaranteed.
Formally – following ideas of Belnap [5], Ginsberg [18], and Fitting [15] –
DMT [13] moved from a complete lattice (V, ⩽) to its associated bilattice on
the set V 2 := V × V ; where elements of V correspond to interpretations, the
pairs contained in V 2 correspond to approximations of such interpretations.
More technically, a pair (x, y) ∈V 2 approximates all z ∈V with x ⩽z ⩽y.
A pair (x, y) ∈V 2 is called ordered iff x ⩽y, in other words, iff the interval
[x, y] := {z ∈V | x ⩽z ⩽y} is non-empty;5 a pair (x, x) is exact.

the (truth) lattice ordering ⩽, (x1, x2) ≤t (y1, y2) :⇐⇒x1 ⩽y1 and x2 ⩽y2, and
(2) (x1, x2) ≤p (y1, y2) :⇐⇒x1 ⩽y1 and y2 ⩽x2, the precision ordering compar-

Just as pairs of V 2 approximate elements of V , a foundational idea of DMT [13]
was that operators O: V →V can be approximated by operators on V 2: An
approximator is an operator A: V 2 →V 2 that is (a) ≤p-monotone and (b) maps
exact pairs to exact pairs. By condition (b), we say that A approximates the
operator O: V →V with O(x) = A(x, x)1 (where (x, y)1 = x and (x, y)2 = y); by
condition (a), A is guaranteed to possess (≤p-least) fixpoints. An approximator A
is symmetric iff for all (x, y) ∈V 2, we have A(x, y)2 = A(y, x)1; thus symmetry
allows to specify A by giving only A(·, ·)1.
The main contribution of DMT [13] was the association of the stable approx-
imator Ast to an approximator A: for a complete lattice (V, ⩽) and an approx-
imator A: V 2 →V 2, define the stable approximator for A as Ast : V 2 →V 2 by
Ast(x, y) := (lfp(A(·, y)1), lfp(A(x, ·)2)), where A(·, y)1 (resp. A(x, ·)2) denotes
the operator that maps any z ∈V to A(z, y)1 (resp. to A(x, z)2). Among other
results, DMT [13] also showed that this construction is well-defined because
both relevant operators A(·, y)1 : V →V and A(x, ·)2 : V →V are ⩽-monotone
(as A is an approximator) and thus possess least fixpoints. Furthermore, any
approximator maps ordered pairs to ordered pairs, and the stable approximator
Ast maps ordered post-fixpoints of A to ordered pairs. Consequently, not only is
lfp(A) ordered (and called the Kripke-Kleene fixpoint of A), but so is lfp(Ast),
the well-founded fixpoint of A. Moreover, a fixpoint (x, y) of Ast, called a stable
fixpoint of A, is always a ⩽-minimal fixpoint of O (when A approximates O).
The term “stable” is not coincidental: When using (the normal logic program
version of) the transformation operator TP [14] as O and defining a suitable
approximator A (see Section 4 for a generalization of such an approximator), then
the exact fixpoints of Ast correspond one-to-one to the stable models of P [13].

2.3
Normal Fuzzy Logic Programs
Syntactically, normal FLPs (nFLPs) extend (1) with the case ∼p where ∼is
“negation as failure”. The remaining notions for positive formulas, rules, and
programs carry over to the normal case as expected; in particular, the evaluation
of a normal fuzzy formula φ under K ∈K has the additional inductive case
[K](∼φ) := ∼·[K](φ). This directly extends the one-step consequence operator
(3) to the case of normal FLPs, with models of P and pre-fixpoints of TP still
being in one-to-one correspondence. Alas, as in the crisp case, the occurrence of
negation ∼leads to TP no longer being necessarily monotone, whence several
authors generalized (crisp) stable-model and well-founded semantics to the fuzzy
case [29,22,9], by hand-crafting the definitions.
Recently, Kettmann et al. [24] have presented an approximator AP of TP for
the case of nFLPs, and showed that this one approximator (via the standard
definitions of approximation fixpoint theory) uniformly reconstructs several hand-
crafted semantics: the well-founded fixpoint of AP coincides with the fuzzy
well-founded semantics defined by Loyer and Straccia [29]; the exact stable
fixpoints of AP coincide with the fuzzy stable models defined by Cornejo et al. [9].
The operator we will present in Section 4 is a generalization of AP .
Kettmann et al. [24] have not explicitly discussed the work of Janssen et
al. [22]; however, as the latter base their semantics on the definitions of Medina
et al [31], it follows that the semantics used in [22] is also covered by AP .

Extended Fuzzy Logic Programs
In this paper, we are interested in extended fuzzy logic programs. Firstly, “ex-
tended” means that we allow two kinds of negation – weak (default) negation ∼,
which captures the absence of a proof, and strong (classical) negation ¬, which
expresses explicit falsity. Secondly, we abstract away from concrete sets V of
truth values, e.g. V = {0, 1} in the Boolean case, or V = [0, 1] in the case of
fuzzy LPs [43], but merely require the set V of truth values to be equipped with
a (possibly partial) truth ordering ⩽such that (V, ⩽) forms a complete lattice.
Syntactically, the move from normal to extended FLPs sees us replacing atoms
with literals across definitions. Based on a set Π of propositional atoms, we define
the set of literals as Π± := Π ∪Π−, where Π is the set of positive literals and
Π−:= {¬p | p ∈Π} the set of negative literals.

8
Discussion
We already discussed relations with a host of related work in the reconstructive
sections (Section 5 and 6). Here, we survey other related work. One line of
work on extended logic programs that we did not reconstruct is that done by
Alferes, Damásio, Pereira, and Alcântara [2,12,1]. They introduce the well-founded
semantics for extended logic programs. Their semantics is a straightforward
adaption of the well-founded semantics for normal logic programming [40], but is
based on what they call the principle of coherence: “believing ¬p entails believing
∼p”. By double negation, this also means that believing p entails believing ∼¬p.
In the presence of contradictions, the AFT-perspective we take (and, as shown
by the reconstructions, also underlies other approaches) is incompatible with the
principle of coherence. Indeed, as we only consider pairs (L, U) s.t. L ≤p U, any
time we believe p and ¬p (i.e. p, ¬p ∈L), we also believe in the non-falsity of p and
¬p (i.e. p, ¬p ∈U), which precludes us from believing ∼¬p respectively ∼p. On
the other hand, Alcântara et al. [1] need some additional, restricting assumptions
on their negation functions to make full use of the coherence principle: both
classical and default negations must be involutions (self-inverse bijections), and
furthermore they must commute. In our framework, we need no such assumptions
and can thus also accommodate Gödel negation (which is not involutive).
Another related work is that of Killen and You [25], who use tetralattices
to propose an alternative definition of stable revision. Essentially, they use

tetralattices to obtain pairs (T, F, U, P), where T gives necessary truth, F well-
established falsity, U possible falsity and P possible truth. On the other hand, we
consider pairs of pairs ((L1, L2), (U1, U2)) where U1 and U2 [L1 and L2] represent
the possible [necessary] truth of p respectively ¬p.
We also provide a proof-of-concept implementation in ASP, using the meta-
programming-capabilities of clingo [23]. We restricted our implementation to
fixpoint and Kripke-Kleene semantics for crisp eFLPs. In meta-programming we
encode a grounded logic program as a set of facts called a reification. The semantics
is encoded in a logic program called a meta-program, and applied by feeding both
the reification and meta-program into clingo for grounding and solving. Since
clingo’s grounder gringo performs some simplifications and defines away strong
negation to a constraint, we had to introduce a predicate snot which encodes
strong negation (instead of using clingo’s strong negation -). More details can be
found in the online repository at https://github.com/JeroenSpaans/sningo.
Janssen et al. [22] showed that several common extensions of normal fuzzy
logic programs, such as constraints and extended aggregators, can be simulated by
normal rules while preserving the fuzzy answer set semantics. For the extension
allowing strong negation, however, the semantics is based on a slightly different
notion of modelhood, using the approach of Van Nieuwenborgh et al. [42]. In this
setting, two special aggregators are required to determine the extent to which a
fuzzy interpretation I : Π± →V is (1) consistent with respect to the consistency
values induced by an associated consistency function, and (2) accumulates the
truth values of all rules of a program P to obtain the degree to which I is a
model of P. We aim to generalize this approach in future work. Other future
work foreseen includes the generalization of this work to extended or fuzzy logic
programs with disjunction, using non-deterministic AFT [19]. Furthermore, we
see several potential applications of extended fuzzy logic programs, e.g. in law,
where conflicting obligations have to be combined with fuzzy concepts [3], or in
neuro-symbolic AI, where fuzzy logic is often used to integrate logical rules in a
loss function [36], and where hallucinations might cause inconsistencies.
