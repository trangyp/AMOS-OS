POLYTOPES AND POSETS ASSOCIATED TO PREORDERS
CHRISTOS A. ATHANASIADIS AND FR´ED´ERIC CHAPOTON
Abstract. Preorder polytopes, defined from preorders on finite sets, are introduced and stud-
ied from a lattice point enumeration point of view. They naturally generalize arbor polytopes,
recently introduced and studied by the second named author. Preorder polytopes are shown
to be lattice polytopes which satisfy a certain duality relating their Ehrhart polynomials with
the zeta polynomials of their posets of lattice points. A combinatorial interpretation of the
normalized volume of a preorder polytope is proven, together with formulas for the Ehrhart
polynomial and the h∗-polynomial, and a combinatorial interpretation of the latter is conjec-
tured. Several conjectures and results on the lattice point enumeration of arbor polytopes are
generalized to preorder polytopes, new conjectures are proposed and new interesting examples
of preorder polytopes are studied.

It is not obvious that preorder polytopes are lattice polytopes and no longer true that they are
always simple. Our first main result shows that preorder polytopes are indeed lattice polytopes
which form a subclass of the class of generalized permutohedra [Pos09].
Theorem 1.4. Let τ be a preorder on an n-element set. Then, Qτ is an n-dimensional lattice
polytope which is unimodularly equivalent to a generalized permutohedron.

is that the new setting allows for more interesting examples. We denote by τ ∗the dual of the
preorder τ and define the poset Pτ of lattice points of Qτ as we did for arbors. The following

2.1. Notation. Let E be a nonempty finite set. We will denote by (ee)e∈E the standard basis
of the space RE. We will use boldface letters to denote vectors x = (xe)e∈E of RE, or collections
of real numbers. We will denote by |x| the sum of the coordinates of a point x ∈RE and by |S|
or #S the cardinality of a finite set S.
2.2. Preorders. Let E be a nonempty finite set. A preorder on E is defined as a reflexive and
transitive relation ≤on E. Declaring two elements a, b ∈E to be equivalent if a ≤b and b ≤a,
one gets an equivalence relation on E. The induced order on the set of equivalence classes, which
we call vertices, is a partial order denoted by ⪯. Thus, a preorder ≤on the ground set E can
also be defined as a partial order ⪯on the set of blocks of a partition of E. We will depict ≤by
the Hasse diagram [Sta11, Section 3.1] of the associated partial order ⪯(where, as usual, the
order increases from bottom to top). In the language of species [BLL98], we are working with
the composition of the species of partial orders with that of nonempty finite sets. Preorders on
a finite set E are in one-to-one correspondence with topologies on E [Sta11, Exercise 3.3]. We
will usually denote preordered finite sets by small greek letters. We will refer to the cardinality
of the ground set E as the size of a preorder on E and to the cardinality of a vertex as the size
of this vertex. Figure 1 shows the Hasse diagram of a preorder of size 5; there is one vertex of
size two and three single element vertices.
Basic constructions for posets [Sta11, Sections 3.1-3.2], such as disjoint unions, ordinal sums,
duality, order ideals and filters and principal order ideals and filters, extend naturally to pre-
orders. For example, an order ideal of a preorder on the ground set E is defined as any subset
I ⊆E such that for every b ∈I we have a ∈I for every a ∈E with a ≤b. Thus, order ideals
are unions of vertices which are ideals of the associated partial order ⪯. Similarly, an order filter
(or dual order ideal) is defined as any subset J ⊆E such that for every a ∈J we have b ∈J
for every b ∈E with a ≤b. We will denote by D(e) (respectively, U (e)) the principal order
ideal (respectively, principal order filter) generated by e ∈E. We will sometimes abuse notation
and write D(v) and U (v) for a vertex v.
A preorder ≤on E is called a chain if a ≤b or b ≤a for all a, b ∈E (equivalently, the induced
partial order ⪯is a total order). Chains are preorders associated to linear arbors [Cha25b,
Section 4]; the corresponding arbor polytopes were studied in [Ath25] and called composition
polytopes.
2.3. Poset combinatorics. Let (P, ⪯) be a finite poset and let d be the length (one less than
the cardinality) of the longest chain in P. The zeta polynomial [Sta11, Section 3.12] of P is
the unique polynomial Z(P, t) with the property that Z(P, m + 1) is equal to the number of
m-element multichains in P, meaning sequences (p1, p2, . . . , pm) of elements of P such that
p1 ⪯p2 ⪯· · · ⪯pm, for every m ∈N. The degree of Z(P, t) is equal to d and the leading
coefficient is equal to the number of (necessarily maximal) chains of P of length d, divided by
d!. We say that P is graded if all maximal chains in P have length d.
