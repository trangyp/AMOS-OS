POISSON FIELDS OF TWO VARIABLES
KEN GOODEARL AND JAMES J. ZHANG
Abstract. We study invariants and structures of Poisson fields of rational functions
in two variables. For four particular families, we classify the members, establish cri-
teria for isomorphisms and, with the exception of the Weyl Poisson field, describe
the automorphism groups. Embeddings are also investigated, along with an analog of
the Dixmier Conjecture: For which Poisson fields is every Poisson endomorphism an
automorphism? The answer is negative for the first family, but positive answers are
obtained for several subclasses of the other families. Finally, we exhibit a Poisson field
which is not isomorphic to any Poisson field k(x, y) for which {x, y} is a polynomial
in k[x, y].
Introduction
Poisson algebras have been used extensively in many areas, and several important
questions, such as automorphism and isomorphism problems, for Poisson algebras or
fields, have recently been studied by several authors [1, 10, 7, 8]. The subject of Poisson
algebras is closely connected to Poisson geometry, deformation quantization, and non-
commutative algebraic geometry. For example, Poisson fields are viewed as an analogue
of skew fields that are essential in the study of noncommutative birational geometry.
There are two important Poisson fields that are commonly studied and have close
connections to other research topics. The first one is the Weyl Poisson field
KWeyl := k(x, y | {x, y} = 1)
whose underlying commutative field is the field of rational functions of two variables
k(x, y) and whose Poisson structure is determined by the equation {x, y} = 1. Here we
fix a base field k that is of characteristic zero. Throughout this introduction, we assume
that k is algebraically closed.
The second well known instance is the q-skew Poisson field, for a scalar q ∈k, namely
Kq := k(x, y | {x, y} = qxy)
whose underlying commutative field is k(x, y) and whose Poisson structure is determined
by the equation {x, y} = qxy. Note that the Weyl and q-skew Poisson fields can be
viewed as semiclassical limits of the Weyl skew field Fract k⟨x, y | xy −yx = 1⟩and the
skew field Fract k⟨x, y | xy = [(p −1)q + 1]yx⟩.
More generally, for every element f ∈k(x, y) one can define a Poisson algebra struc-
ture on k(x, y) by using the equation

{x, y} = f.

It is well-known that Kq is not isomorphic to KWeyl (e.g., [5, Corollary 5.3]). In fact,
Kq cannot be embedded in KWeyl [8, Corollary 5.9(2)]. On the other hand, given two
general elements f1, f2 in k(x, y), it is difficult to tell whether K{f1} is isomorphic to
K{f2} or not. So we formally ask the following questions:
Question 0.1 (Classification Problem). Can we classify all Poisson fields of the form
K{f} up to Poisson isomorphism?
This problem is extremely difficult and the moduli of Poisson fields K{f} is very
complicated. We introduce some invariants that can help or control the classification.
Definition 0.2. Let K be a Poisson field of the form K{f} as defined above. The flag
height of K is defined to be
(E0.2.1)
fht(K) := min{deg g | K ∼= K{g}, g ∈k[x, y]}
if there exist g ∈k[x, y] such that K ∼= K{g}. Otherwise, we define fht(K) := ∞.
For every non-negative integer h, let Poih denote the moduli space of Poisson fields
K{f} with fht h. Trivially, Poi0 consists of one Poisson field, which is KWeyl. (This
assumes that we view the zero polynomial as having degree less than 0.) It is easy
to check that Poi1 is empty (cf. Proposition 1.1(1)(2)). Dumas [3, Proposition, p.12]
proved that every Poisson field K{f} with fht 2 is isomorphic to Kq (∼= K−q) for some
q ∈k×. This also gives a classification of the Poisson fields in Poi2. In [6] we work
out a description of Poi3 (as well as some partial classifications of Poih when h = 4, 5).
Suppose f ∈k[x, y] has degree 3. If f is reducible (or more generally if the curve f = 0 is
singular), then K{f} is isomorphic to either KWeyl or Kq. If the curve f = 0 is smooth,
then K{f} is completely determined by the (elliptic) curve f = 0. So the moduli of Poi3
are related to the moduli of elliptic curves [6]. For h = 4 or 5, the big picture of Poih
is mysterious. When f is irreducible of degree 4, we have some conjectures [6]. When f
is reducible of degree 4, it is also very complicated to understand K{f}.
For higher h, it is impossible to completely solve the classification problem of Poih
using currently available tools. The approach in this paper is to consider several families
of reducible f and the corresponding Poisson fields. Here is a list of the Poisson fields
that we will study in detail.

Motivated by the Embedding Problem, we introduce the following concepts.
Definition 0.6. Let K and K′ be Poisson fields of the form K{f}.
(1) We say K is height cohereditary if fht(K) ≤fht(K′) for arbitrary K′ ⊇K.
(2) We say K is height hereditary if fht(K′) ≤fht(K) for arbitrary K′ ⊆K.
Question 0.7. Is there a Poisson field K{f} that is not height (co)hereditary?
By definition, KWeyl is height cohereditary. It follows from [8, Corollary 5.9(2)] that
Kq is height cohereditary. In this paper we will show that several families of Poisson
fields are height cohereditary, see Lemmas 6.2 and 7.2. But we cannot verify any example
that is height hereditary; in particular, we do not know if KWeyl (resp., Kq for q ̸= 0) is
height hereditary.
There is another notion of “height”, called 1-valuation height and denoted by vht1,
see Definition 3.13. The 1-valuation height has a few advantages:
(1) vht1 is defined for all Poisson fields with nontrivial Poisson bracket;
(2) vht1 has the cohereditary property [Lemma 3.14(2)];
(3) vht1 is bounded above by fht when applied to K{f} [Lemma 3.14(4)].
In general, vht1(K) ̸= fht(K), see Lemma 4.4(3). We compute both vht1 and fht for
Poisson fields in the four families mentioned above.
The automorphism problem is always an interesting question.
Question 0.8 (Automorphism Problem). Let f be a nonzero element in k(x, y). Are
there any effective ways of computing the Poisson algebra automorphism group of K{f}?
Since the algebra automorphism group of k(x, y) is called the Cremona group of rank
2 and denoted by Cr2(k), we call the Poisson algebra automorphism group of K{f}
the Poisson Cremona group associated to the flag f and denote it by Cr2(k, f). Note
that Cr2(C, qxy) (= AutPoi(Kq)) was computed by Blanc in [1, Theorem 1] (solving
a conjecture of Usnich [13]).
However, it is an open problem to describe Cr2(k, 1)
(= AutPoi(KWeyl)). The group Cr2(k, 1) does contain the automorphism group of the
Weyl Poisson algebra (i.e., k[x, y] with {x, y} = 1), which was determined in the famous
paper of Jung [9], but Cr2(k, 1) is much larger.
In this paper we will compute Cr2(k, f) for several classes of flags f, see Proposition
4.8, Corollary 5.7, and Theorems 6.6, 7.5.
In the families (1) and (2), Cr2(k, f) is
infinite, while it could be finite in families (3), (4), and may even be trivial (Corollary
7.7).

Definition 3.1. Recall that a discrete valuation on an algebra A is a map

determined by {x, y}, and that every polynomial in k[x, y] occurs as {x, y} for a (unique)
Poisson bracket {−, −} (e.g., [11, p.235, last paragraph of §9.1.1]). Here we note the
corresponding facts for Poisson brackets on k(x, y).
Lemma 9.2. Let K = k(x, y) with a Poisson bracket {−, −}, and set f := {x, y}.
Then {g, h} = {g, h}wf for all g, h ∈K.

Corollary 9.4. Let K = k(x, y) and f ∈K. Then there is a unique Poisson bracket
{−, −} on K such that {x, y} = f.

{−, −}wf. We apply Lemma 9.3 with {−, −} and {−, −}′ replaced by {−, −}w and

Example 9.5. There exists a Poisson field K with an element f ∈K such that the
biderivation {−, −}f on K is not a Poisson bracket.
Take K = k(x1, x2, y1, y2) to be the rank 2 Weyl Poisson field, where
{xi, xj} = {yi, yj} = 0
and
{xi, yj} = δij
for i, j = 1, 2. Observe that
