Persistent-Homology-Guided Topology Scanning of Qualitative
Indicators for Acoustic Inverse Scattering
Xiaomei Yang∗
Jiaying Jia†

Abstract
Qualitative methods such as the linear sampling method and the factorization method
reconstruct acoustic scatterers through sampling indicators. In practice, these indicators are
gray-scale fields on a prescribed sampling window and a binary obstacle shape is obtained only
after thresholding. The choice of threshold is usually empirical and may be unstable when
the indicator contains noise-induced artifacts or when the scatterer has nontrivial topology,
such as multiple components or holes. This paper proposes a topology-aware postprocessing
framework based on persistent homology. Given any normalized qualitative indicator, we scan
the persistent homology of its superlevel sets and use the resulting zero- and one-dimensional
persistent features to estimate or impose the topology of the unknown scatterer. A topology-
guided threshold is then selected by minimizing a Betti-number discrepancy together with mild
geometric penalties. The method is indicator-agnostic: it can be applied to the linear sampling
indicator, the factorization-method indicator, or a normalized fusion of indicators. The main
formulation is single-frequency and therefore remains close to the classical qualitative inverse
scattering setting. We present the mathematical construction, an automatic topology detection
rule based on persistence lifetimes and lifetime gaps, and a detailed algorithmic protocol for
numerical implementation. Numerical tests verify that the proposed method is effective.
Keywords: acoustic inverse scattering; linear sampling method; factorization method; persistent
homology; Betti numbers; topology-preserving reconstruction; qualitative indicators.

Introduction
The inverse scattering problem seeks to recover information about an unknown object from measure-
ments of the field scattered by incident waves. Such problems arise widely in various applications,
including biomedical imaging, non-destructive evaluation or geophysics, remote sensing, sonar
detection, and so on (see e.g. [2, 10, 13, 19, 21]). The acoustic case is particularly fundamental in
this field, providing the basic ideas for more complex scenarios. In the acoustic setting, the measured
data are often consist of far-field patterns generated by plane waves incident from multiple directions.
A major class of methods for such problems is the class of qualitative reconstruction methods,
which includes the linear sampling method (LSM) [4] and the factorization method (FM) [12, 14].
These methods produce an indicator function defined on a sampling region: the indicator value is
expected to be large inside the scatterer and small outside it. Qualitative and sampling-type methods
have continued to develop beyond the classical full-aperture setting. For example, direct sampling

methods provide fast indicator-based reconstructions from far-field data [15]. Extended sampling
methods have also been developed for more restricted measurement configurations, including one-

wave models, such as inverse elastic scattering problems [16]. These developments illustrate that
qualitative methods are increasingly used as flexible mechanisms for generating imaging indicators

In practical implementation, qualitative sampling methods return gray-scale indicators rather
than binary shapes. To obtain a reconstructed scatterer, one must convert the indicator into a
candidate region, usually by thresholding. This post-processing step can have a substantial effect on
the final topology of the reconstruction: small artifacts may be promoted to separate components,
while genuine connected structures or interior holes may be lost. Such threshold sensitivity is
particularly problematic for scatterers with multiple components or nontrivial interior topology.
We address this issue by incorporating topological information into the thresholding process.
The key idea is to regard the indicator not only as a point wise measure of scattering evidence, but
also as a scalar field whose topological structure changes as the reconstruction level varies. Persistent
homology provides a natural tool for tracking this evolution: it records connected components
and holes that persist over a range of levels, while suppressing short-lived features that are more
likely to be caused by noise or artifacts [7, 8, 9, 11, 18, 22]. In the present two-dimensional setting,
persistent H0 features correspond to stable scatterer components, whereas persistent H1 features
correspond to stable holes or annular structures. These topological features are then used to guide
the conversion of the gray-scale indicator into a topology-consistent binary reconstruction.
Persistent homology is one of the central tools in topological data analysis (TDA). Since the
early work on topological persistence [9] and the algebraic formulation of persistent homology [22],
it has developed into a standard method for extracting multiscale topological information from
data. A key reason for its usefulness is stability: small perturbations of the input function lead to
small changes in the persistence diagram [3]. This makes persistent homology particularly suitable
for noisy data and for level-set analysis of scalar fields. In recent years, persistent homology has
also been combined with statistical learning and scientific computing, for example through stable
vectorizations such as persistence images [1] and PH-based machine learning frameworks [20]. For
Bayesian inverse problems, Deng et al. [6] proposed a persistent-homology-based Bayesian prior for
reconstructing a potential coefficient in an elliptic PDE, where the persistent homology encodes
topological information in the prior distribution.

Qualitative indicators
This section reviews the qualitative indicators used as inputs to the PH scan. The proposed topology
module applies to any normalized indicator I, so the formulas below should be understood as
representative choices rather than mandatory components.
For computational convenience, we assume that the scatterer D is embedded in a bounded
sampling window Ωinv ⊂R2, i.e., D ⊂Ωinv. This window defines the region where sampling points
z are tested and indicator functions are evaluated; it is not the physical scattering domain. In
practice, one may simply take Ωinv = [−R, R]2 with R sufficiently large. For instance, if the true
scatterer consists of a disk near (−2, −2) and an annular component near (2, 2), choosing R = 5
(i.e., Ωinv = [−5, 5]2) is large enough to enclose the unknown support.

8
Conclusion
We have proposed a persistent-homology-guided topology scan for qualitative acoustic inverse
scattering indicators. The method scans the sublevel sets of the distance-type indicator ρ = 1 −I,
extracts persistent H0 and H1 features, estimates or enforces the topology of the scatterer, and
selects a topology-consistent reconstruction threshold. The framework is independent of the specific
indicator and can be applied to LSM, FM, or a fused indicator. Its main advantage is that it turns
qualitative gray-scale indicators into topology-aware binary reconstructions.

A.1
Cubical complexes
Let Ωinv ⊂R2 be the rectangular sampling window and let Ωinv,h be a uniform Cartesian grid on it.
A binary image or a grid function on Ωinv,h naturally defines a cubical complex. In two dimensions,
the basic cells are
vertices,
edges,
square pixels.
A cubical complex K is a finite collection of such cells that is closed under taking faces. Thus, if a
square belongs to K, then its four edges and four vertices also belong to K; if an edge belongs to
K, then its two endpoint vertices also belong to K.
For a binary set D ⊂Ωinv,h, the associated cubical complex is obtained by taking all pixels
contained in D, together with their edges and vertices. This cubical complex is the topological
object whose connected components and holes are counted.
A.2
Cubical chains and boundary operators
We now recall the algebraic definition of homology for a finite cubical complex K. Fix a coefficient
field F. In computations one often takes F = F2, so that orientations and signs do not need to be
tracked explicitly.
For q = 0, 1, 2, let Cq(K) be the vector space over F generated by the q-dimensional cells of K.
Thus:
C0(K) : generated by vertices,
C1(K) : generated by edges,
C2(K) : generated by square pixels.
Elements of Cq(K) are called q-chains.
The boundary operator
∂q : Cq(K) →Cq−1(K)
maps a q-dimensional cell to the formal sum of its (q −1)-dimensional faces. For example, the
boundary of an edge is the sum of its two endpoints, and the boundary of a square is the sum of its
four edges. The boundary operators satisfy
∂q ◦∂q+1 = 0.
Consequently,
im ∂q+1 ⊆ker ∂q.
The sequence
· · · −→Cq+1(K)
∂q+1
−−−→Cq(K)
∂q
−→Cq−1(K) −→· · ·
is called the cubical chain complex of K.
A.3
Homology groups and Betti numbers
The q-th homology group of K is defined by
Hq(K) = ker ∂q/ im ∂q+1.
The elements of ker ∂q are q-cycles, and the elements of im ∂q+1 are q-boundaries. Therefore, Hq(K)
measures q-cycles that are not merely boundaries of higher-dimensional chains.

The q-th Betti number is the rank of the q-th homology group:
βq(K) = rank Hq(K).
In the two-dimensional setting of this paper, the most relevant Betti numbers are
β0(K) : number of connected components,
and
β1(K) : number of holes.
For example, a binary set consisting of two disjoint solid components has (β0, β1) = (2, 0), while a
set consisting of one solid disk and one annular component has (β0, β1) = (2, 1).
A.4
Distance-type indicator and sublevel sets
Let
I : Ωinv,h →[0, 1]
be a normalized qualitative indicator. Large values of I(z) indicate that the sampling point z is
more likely to belong to the scatterer. To use the standard sublevel-set filtration, we introduce the
distance-type indicator
ρ(z) = 1 −I(z).
Then small values of ρ correspond to strong scattering evidence.
For s ∈[0, 1], define
Ds(ρ) = {z ∈Ωinv,h : ρ(z) ≤s}.
As s increases, the sets Ds(ρ) are nested:
Ds1(ρ) ⊆Ds2(ρ),
0 ≤s1 ≤s2 ≤1.
Equivalently,
Ds(ρ) = {z ∈Ωinv,h : I(z) ≥1 −s}.
Thus the sublevel sets of ρ are precisely the high-indicator regions of I. This reformulation avoids
switching between superlevel filtrations of I and sublevel filtrations of −I.
Each set Ds(ρ) defines a cubical complex Ks. Hence the nested sublevel sets induce a cubical
filtration
Ks1 ⊆Ks2 ⊆· · · ⊆Ksm,
0 ≤s1 < s2 < · · · < sm ≤1.
A.5
Filtration values on cubical cells
If the function ρ is defined on grid vertices, the lower-star convention assigns to a cell σ the filtration
value
ρ(σ) = max
v⪯σ ρ(v),
where v ⪯σ means that v is a vertex of σ. Then
Ks = {σ ∈K : ρ(σ) ≤s}
is a cubical subcomplex.
If ρ is stored as a pixel-valued image, cubical persistent-homology software often assigns filtration
values directly to top-dimensional cells and then extends them to lower-dimensional faces in a way
that preserves the filtration property. In either convention, the output is a nested family of cubical
complexes indexed by the level s.

A.6
Persistent homology
For every level s, the cubical complex Ks has homology groups
H0(Ks),
H1(Ks).
As s increases, new connected components may appear, components may merge, holes may be
created, and holes may be filled. Persistent homology tracks these events across the entire filtration.
A topological feature is born at level b if it first appears in the filtration at Kb. It dies at level d
if it disappears at Kd, for example because a connected component merges into an older component
or because a hole becomes filled. The pair
(b, d)
is called a persistence pair, and its lifetime is
ℓ= d −b.
A long lifetime indicates that the feature persists over a large range of levels and is therefore regarded
as a robust topological feature of the indicator. Short lifetimes are more likely to correspond to
noise, discretization effects, or small artifacts.
The persistence diagrams in dimensions 0 and 1 are denoted by
D0(ρ),
D1(ρ).
The diagram D0(ρ) records the evolution of connected components, while D1(ρ) records the evolution
of holes.
A.7
Interpretation for scattering reconstruction
In the proposed reconstruction method, persistent homology is used to infer the dominant topology
of the scatterer from the indicator field.
The H0 persistence diagram is used to infer the number of dominant connected components. In
a sublevel-set filtration, one connected component may persist as an essential class, while additional
significant connected components are often represented by long finite H0 bars. Thus, the estimated
number of connected components is taken as
bβ0 = 1 + #{dominant finite H0 bars}.
The H1 persistence diagram is used to infer the number of holes:
bβ1 = #{dominant H1 bars}.
After the topology
(bβ0, bβ1)
has been estimated, the final reconstruction is obtained by selecting a level s∗such that the sublevel
set
bD = Ds∗(ρ)
has Betti numbers matching the estimated or prescribed topology.
The persistence diagram therefore provides topological information, while the selected set Ds∗(ρ)
provides spatial localization. The connected components of bD localize the scatterer components.
The holes are localized as the bounded connected components of
Ωinv,h \ bD.
