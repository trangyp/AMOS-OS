Polynomial-time isomorphism test for groups
with abelian Sylow subgroups
Saveliy V. Skresanov
Abstract
The group isomorphism problem in computational complexity asks
whether two finite groups given by their Cayley tables are isomorphic or
not. Although polynomial-time isomorphism tests exist for many specific
types of groups, no general polynomial-time algorithm is known, classes
of solvable and nilpotent groups being the main obstacles. In 2012 Babai
and Qiao gave a polynomial-time isomorphism test for the class of solvable
groups admitting normal series with abelian Sylow factors. We generalize
their result and give a polynomial-time isomorphism test for A-groups,
i.e. groups with abelian Sylow subgroups. The algorithm heavily relies
both on the computational methods developed by Babai and Qiao, and
structural properties of A-groups.

Introduction
The group isomorphism problem in computational complexity is a problem of
testing whether two finite groups given by their Cayley tables are isomorphic or
not. Although computations with groups given by Cayley tables are impractical
for most orders, the Cayley table model serves as a useful theoretical tool.
From the one hand, many group-theoretical tasks like computing centralizers,
normalizers, Sylow subgroups etc. are performed trivially in polynomial time
for groups given by Cayley tables. On the other hand, the group isomorphism
problem is polynomial-time reducible to the graph isomorphism problem and
hence serves as a lower bound on the complexity of the latter.
It is well-known [20] that isomorphism of groups of order n can be tested in
time nlog n+O(1), since such a group has at most log n generators (all logarithms
are base 2 in this work). Since Babai’s algorithm [1] for testing isomorphism of
graphs on n vertices in time nO((log n)c), c > 1, the group isomorphism problem
became one of the main obstacles to further reducing the time complexity of
graph isomorphism.
There has been a lot of progress in the group isomorphism problem for
specific classes of groups, see the introduction of [11] for an overview.
We
note here that, perhaps unsurprisingly, isomorphism of abelian groups can be
tested in polynomial time and even in linear time [17]. On the other end of
the spectrum, one can test in polynomial time isomorphism of groups with no

solvable radical [2]. Partly because of the latter result, it is widely believed
that solvable groups or even nilpotent groups are the hardest cases of the group
isomorphism problem, see [3, Subsection 1.1] and [11, Subsection 1.2].
Sylow subgroups of a finite group are nilpotent, and although there is no
known reduction of the group isomorphism problem to the isomorphism problem
for Sylow subgroups of a group, it is natural to consider some group classes with
restrictions on the Sylow structure. One such class of groups was considered by
Babai and Qiao in [3]. A finite group G has an abelian Sylow tower if there is a
normal series G = N1 ⊵N2 ⊵· · · ⊵Nk = 1, such that for every i = 1, . . . , k −1
the group Ni/Ni+1 is abelian and isomorphic to a Sylow subgroup of G. A
group with an abelian Sylow tower is solvable and every its nilpotent subgroup
is abelian. The main result of [3] gives a polynomial-time isomorphism test for
groups with abelian Sylow towers.
Proposition 1.1 ([3, Theorem 1.1]). Let G and G0 be groups given by their
Cayley tables, and assume that G and G0 have abelian Sylow towers. In poly-
nomial time one can check if G and G0 are isomorphic and find the coset of
isomorphisms, if they are.
We note that the set of isomorphisms between G and G0 is either empty,
or a coset of Aut(G) in the symmetric group Sym(G ⊔G0), where G ⊔G0 is
the disjoint union of G and G0. We can compute this coset if we can compute
permutation generators of Aut(G) and a coset representative.
Groups with abelian Sylow towers are a particular case of A-groups, that
is, groups with abelian Sylow subgroups. The class of A-groups is closed with
respect to taking subgroups, quotients and direct products, and contains non-
solvable groups, such as the alternating group Alt(5). As in the case of groups
with abelian Sylow towers, every nilpotent subgroup of an A-group is abelian.
The main result of this paper is a polynomial-time isomorphism test for
A-groups given by Cayley tables.
Theorem 1.2. Let G and G0 be groups given by their Cayley tables, and assume
that all Sylow subgroups of G and G0 are abelian. In polynomial time one can
check if G and G0 are isomorphic and find the coset of isomorphisms, if they
are.
It is mentioned in [3, Subsection 1.1] that there are at most nO(log n) iso-
morphism classes of solvable A-groups of order at most n, and at least nΩ(log n)
of those are groups with abelian Sylow towers. Notice that a solvable A-group
does not always have an abelian Sylow tower, the group Sym(3) × Alt(4) being
an example, so Theorem 1.2 is an improvement to Proposition 1.1 even in the
solvable case.
Our proof of Theorem 1.2 follows the general strategy from [3], so we explain
the latter first. If the group G has an abelian Sylow tower, then one can de-
compose G as a semidirect product G = A ⋊H, where A is an abelian p-group,
and |H| is not divisible by p. By [3, Theorem 1.2], the isomorphism problem

Sylow tower, until we are left with the isomorphism problem for abelian groups.
The reduction itself is nontrivial, and relies on polynomial-time solutions to two
problems about representations of H on A: the “representation-transporting
automorphisms problem” [3, Problem 2] and the “intertwining automorphisms
problem” [3, Problem 3] (we postpone the formal treatment of these problems
to Sections 3 and 4). Polynomial-time algorithms for these tasks utilize repre-
sentation theory of finite groups and heavily depend on the fact that |A| and
|H| are coprime.
In order to prove Theorem 1.2, we first show that the isomorphism problem
for A-groups can be reduced to the problem of computing the full group of
automorphisms. The argument here follows [25], but works inside the class of
A-groups. Unfortunately the main result of [25] was formulated for the class of
all finite groups, so in Section 6 we reprove that result for an arbitrary class of
groups closed with respect to direct products and direct factors.
Now, when G is an arbitrary A-group, we no longer have a decomposition
G = A⋊H, where A is an abelian p-group and |H| is not divisible by p. Instead,
as follows from the results of Broshi [4], if G has a nontrivial solvable radical
then there exists a characteristic abelian p-subgroup A of G such that G = A⋊H
for some subgroup H of G, and H is in a precise sense unique up to conjugation
in G. Since a Sylow p-subgroup of G is abelian, all p-elements of H act trivially
on A, and hence the subgroup K of H generated by all p-elements lies in the
kernel of the action of H on A. So the questions about representations of H
on A reduce to the coprime case of representations of H/K on A, where the
representation-transporting and intertwining automorphisms problems can be
solved in polynomial time. Hence to compute Aut(G) it suffices to compute
Aut(H), so we proceed inductively until G has the trivial solvable radical and
we can apply the main result of [2] to find Aut(G).
We note that the results of Babai and Qiao [3], which are instrumental to our
work, were published in a conference proceedings and their proofs were often
sketched or postponed to the full version. As far as the author is aware, the
full version has never appeared in print, so in order to make this paper as self-
contained as possible, we provide complete proofs of all results from [3] that we
require. In many cases we either generalize the results to better suit our needs,
or provide an alternative proof. A notable example is Proposition 4.3 which
resolves the intertwining automorphisms problem without any assumptions on
coprimality or the groups being A-groups.
The structure of the paper is as follows.
In Section 2 we give group-
theoretical and computational preliminaries, Sections 3 and 4 are devoted to
the representation-transporting automorphisms problem and the intertwining
automorphisms problem, respectively. In Section 5 we prove the main result,
and Section 6 gives polynomial-time reductions between various natural prob-
lems on groups, including the reduction from the group isomorphism problem
to the group automorphism problem.

Preliminaries
We consider only finite groups in this work. If G is a group and S ⊆G then
⟨S⟩denotes the subgroup of G generated by S. Given groups G and H, let
Hom(G, H) denote the set of all homomorphisms from G to H. Let End(G)
denote the monoid of all endomorphisms of G, and let Aut(G) ≤End(G) denote
the full group of automorphisms of G. Given a set Ω, the symmetric group on
Ωis denoted by Sym(Ω).
We compose maps from the left to right, so for f : G →H and g : H →K
we have f ◦g : G →K. We consider right group actions only unless stated
otherwise, in particular, if ϕ ∈Aut(G) and g ∈G then we write gϕ = ϕ(g) for
the image of g under ϕ. Similarly, if π ∈Sym(Ω) and x ∈Ω, then xπ = π(x) is
the image of the point x under the permutation π. If g, h ∈G then gh = h−1gh
denotes the conjugation action of G on itself.
For a prime p, we say that G is a p-group if its order |G| is a power of p.
The group G is a p′-group, if |G| is not divisible by p. A subgroup H ≤G is a
Hall subgroup, if |H| is coprime with the index |G : H|. An abelian p-group is
called homocyclic if it is a direct product of cyclic groups of the same order.
Let H be a group and let A be an abelian p-group. A representation of H
on A is a homomorphism α : H →Aut(A). Note that α gives A the structure
of a right H-module. If a group G can be decomposed into a semidirect product
G = A ⋊H, then the conjugation action of H on A defines a representation. In
the other direction, if we are given a representation α : H →Aut(A), then we
can construct a semidirect product A ⋊H where the conjugation action of H
on A coincides with α.
The group Aut(A) acts on the set of all representations of H on A by the
following rule: for ψ ∈Aut(A) and α : H →Aut(A) define αψ : H →Aut(A)
by αψ(h) = ψ−1 · α(h) · ψ for h ∈H. For α, β : H →Aut(A) we will write
α ∼β if there exists ψ ∈Aut(A) such that αψ = β. Clearly ∼is an equivalence
relation.
When A is an elementary abelian p-group, it can be viewed as a finite vector
space over the prime field Z/pZ, so our notion of a representation coincides
with the classical notion from representation theory. In particular, in this case
the relation ∼is the usual equivalence of representations. We refer the reader
to [10, Chapter 3] for basic facts about representations. If p does not divide
|H|, then by Maschke’s theorem [10, Theorem 3.3.1] every representation of H
on A can be decomposed into a direct sum of irreducible representations. Every
representation is determined up to equivalence by its irreducible constituents
and their multiplicities, moreover, there are at most |H| equivalence types of
irreducible representations of H, see [10, Theorem 3.6.14].
Note that Aut(H) also acts on the set of all representations of H on A.
Indeed, for ϕ ∈Aut(H) and α : H →Aut(A) define αϕ : H →Aut(A) by
αϕ(h) = α(hϕ−1) for h ∈H. This action commutes with the action of Aut(A)
on representations, so there is an action of Aut(A) × Aut(H) on the set of
representations of H on A.

Representation-transporting automorphisms
Let H and A be finite groups given by their Cayley tables, and let A be an
abelian p-group for p not dividing |H|. Suppose we are given representations
α, β : H →Aut(A) and the generators of P ≤Aut(H) viewed as a permutation
group on H. In this section we are interested in computing the following set:
Pα→β = {ϕ ∈P | αϕ = β}.
Note that Pα→β is either empty, or is a coset of Pα→α in P, so it can be specified
by the generators of Pα→α and a representative.
In the case when P = Aut(H), the problem of computing Pα→β was called
the “representation-transporting automorphisms problem” in [3, Problem 2].
Our situation is a bit more general as P is an arbitrary subgroup of Aut(H),
but the overall idea of the algorithm follows [3, Sections 5 and 6]. First we
resolve the case when A is elementary abelian (cf. [3, Section 5]), and then
we follow the proof outline in [3, Section 6] to reduce the general case to the
elementary abelian one.

Let H and A be finite groups given by their Cayley tables, and let A be an
abelian p-group for p not dividing |H|. Assume we are given representations
α, β : H →Aut(A). The task of computing the set
Aut(A, α ∼β) = {ψ ∈Aut(A) | αψ = β}
was called the “intertwining automorphisms problem” in [3, Problem 3]. Es-
sentially this is the problem of computing all isomorphisms between two H-
modules, where A is given one H-module structure via α, and another H-module
structure via β.
Note that Aut(A, α ∼β) is either empty or a coset of Aut(A, α ∼α) in
Aut(A). Since A is given by its Cayley table, we can view Aut(A) as a permu-
tation group on A.
In [3, Subsection 5.2] this problem is solved in the case when A is elemen-
tary abelian, and in [3, Section 6] it is noted without proof that the work of
Ranum [21] allows one to reduce the general case to the elementary abelian one.

phisms problem can be solved in polynomial time even when |H| and |A| are
not coprime. The key tools that we need are polynomial-time algorithms for
the module isomorphism problem and for the computation of the unit group of
a finite ring.

GrpISOX(G, H) : determine if two groups G and H are isomorphic or not.
GrpIMAPX(G, H) : find an isomorphism between G and H if it exists.

GrpACOUNTX(G) : count the number of automorphisms of G.
GrpAGENX(G) : find generators of the full automorphism group of G.
GrpAPARTX(G) : find the automorphic partition of G.
For two computational problems P1 and P2 we write P1 ∝P2 if P1 is
polynomial-time reducible to P2.
Problems P1 and P2 are polynomial-time
equivalent if P1 ∝P2 and P2 ∝P1.
Theorem 6.1. Let X be a class of finite groups which is closed with respect to
taking direct factors and direct products. The following holds:
(i) GrpISOX ∝GrpACOUNTX ∝GrpAGENX,
(ii) GrpISOX ∝GrpAPARTX ∝GrpAGENX,
(iii) GrpIMAPX ∝GrpAGENX,
(iv) GrpICOUNTX and GrpACOUNTX are polynomial-time equivalent.
The proof of Theorem 6.1 essentially repeats the proof in [25] with the main
difference that we note that our intermediate constructions lie in the class X.
We provide the full proof here for the reader’s convenience.
We need two preliminary results from [25].
Lemma 6.2 ([25, Lemma 3.1]). Let A and B be groups given by their Cayley
tables, and assume that B is abelian. Then one can compute | Hom(A, B)| in
polynomial time.
Lemma 6.3 ([25, Lemma 3.2]). Let G and H be finite directly indecomposable
nonabelian groups, and let S = G × Z(H). Then S ≤G × H is invariant under
Aut(G × H) if and only if G is not isomorphic to H.
Proof of Theorem 6.1.
The reductions GrpACOUNTX ∝GrpAGENX and
GrpAPARTX ∝GrpAGENX follow from standard permutation group algo-
rithms for finding the order and orbits of a permutation group.
Note that we can assume that input groups G, H in problems GrpISOX and
GrpIMAPX are directly indecomposable. Indeed, by [18] in polynomial time we
can decompose our groups as G ≃G1 ×· · ·×Gk and H ≃H1 ×· · ·×Hm, where
Gi, i = 1, . . . , k, and Hj, j = 1, . . . , m, are directly indecomposable. By the
definition of the class X, we have Gi, Hj ∈X. By the Krull–Schmidt theorem,
these decompositions of G and H are unique up to reordering of the factors, so
it suffices to check isomorphism (or find an explicit isomorphism in the case of
GrpIMAPX) for k · m ≤log |G| · log |H| pairs of directly indecomposable groups
