Abstract
Learning-augmented algorithms have received
significant attention in recent years, particularly in
the context of online optimization. Motivated by
the high computational cost of generating predic-
tions, a growing line of work studies the tradeoff
between performance guarantees and the number
of predictions used in learning-augmented algo-
rithms for problems such as caching and metrical
task systems. In this paper, we extend this line of
research to online metric matching by developing
parsimonious learning-augmented algorithms and
establishing lower bounds on their performance.
Our approach extends the Follow-the-Prediction
framework to the parsimonious setting by filling
in a virtual prediction in the absence of an actual
prediction, using an online metric matching algo-
rithm that maintains good intermediate matchings
throughout its execution. We complement our
theoretical results with an empirical evaluation,
demonstrating the practical effectiveness of our
approach.
1. Introduction
Online metric matching is a fundamental online optimiza-
tion problem that naturally arises in applications such as
ride-hailing and resource allocation (Kalyanasundaram &
Pruhs, 1993; Khuller et al., 1994). The problem is defined
as follows. We are given a metric containing n servers and n
requests. The locations of the servers are known in advance,
while the requests arrive one at a time step in an online
fashion. Upon the arrival of a request, the algorithm must
irrevocably match it to an available server at the moment;
once matched, a server cannot be used again, and a request
cannot be reassigned to another server at a later time step.
At termination, the algorithm produces a perfect matching
between servers and requests. The objective is to minimize
the cost of the perfect matching, measured as the sum of

Due to a dedicated line of work (Kalyanasundaram & Pruhs,

algorithmic side, there exists a deterministic algorithm that
outputs a perfect matching whose cost is at most a factor

a factor of O(log3 n) to the optimal cost with hindsight;
this factor was further improved to O(log2 n) by Bansal
et al. (2014). It is also known that these guarantees are
(nearly) best-possible. In particular, no deterministic online
algorithm can achieve a factor better than 2n −1 (Kalyana-
sundaram & Pruhs, 1993; Khuller et al., 1994), matching
the above algorithmic result. For randomized algorithms, an
Ω(log n) lower bound is known (Meyerson et al., 2006).
A major source of these strong lower bounds is the uncer-
tainty about the future input. However, recent advances in
AI and machine learning suggest that reasonable predictions
about the future input may be available in many practical
settings. Motivated by this observation, learning-augmented
algorithms, also known as algorithms with predictions, have
received significant attention in recent years, especially in
online optimization since the seminal work of Lykouris &
Vassilvitskii (2021). These algorithms are provided with
predictions as part of the input and are designed to leverage
them to solve the problem. The performance is typically
measured by the following three factors (Kumar et al., 2018):
consistency, which measures performance when predictions
are accurate; robustness, which guarantees performance
regardless of prediction quality; and smoothness, which cap-
tures how gracefully performance degrades as prediction
error increases.
Online metric matching has also been studied in this
learning-augmented framework. Antoniadis et al. (2023b)
introduced the action prediction model and presented a de-
terministic algorithm that uses the prediction provided at
every time and outputs a perfect matching with cost at most

try to select additional servers from those unmatched in P

an auxiliary sub-instance in which the servers are those
unmatched in P and the requests are those arriving between

matching throughout its execution, which is measured by
adherence and strong competitiveness.

synthesized or derived from real-world data. Our experi-
mental results are broadly consistent with our expectations,
demonstrating the practical effectiveness of our approach.

2. Preliminaries
Online Metric Matching
In the online metric matching
problem, we are given n servers S and n requests R con-
tained in a metric (V, d), i.e., for any u, v, w ∈V , d(u, u) =
0, d(u, v) = d(v, u), and d(u, v) ≤d(u, w)+d(w, v). Two
distinct servers or requests can be placed at the same loca-
tion in the metric; one can regard S and R as multisets

of V . The servers are known from the beginning whilst
the requests are given one at a round in an online manner.
When a request r ∈R arrives, we need to assign r to a cur-
rently unmatched server s ∈S; this decision is irrevocable,
i.e., s is not allowed to be matched with any of subsequent
requests, and r cannot be reassigned to any other server
in later rounds. The objective is to find a perfect match-
ing M between S and R at minimum total cost denoted by
cost(M) := P
(s,r)∈M d(s, r). For an online metric match-
ing algorithm A, we also write cost(A) to denote the cost
of matching output by A.
Definition 2.1. For some ρ : N →R+, we say an algo-
rithm A is ρ-competitive if cost(A) ≤ρ(n)·cost(OPT) for
any instance, where OPT denotes a minimum-cost perfect
matching between S and R in the metric with hindsight.
Throughout this paper, we assume cost(OPT) ≥1. This
assumption is without loss of generality by scaling the met-
ric with an appropriate factor; if cost(OPT) = 0, one can
easily construct an optimal perfect matching even in the
online setting.
Learning Augmentation with Action Predictions
For
the learning-augmented setting of online metric matching,
Antoniadis et al. (2023b) introduce the action prediction
defined as a server set Pt ⊆S of size |Pt| = t for every
round t ∈{1, . . . , n}.
The definition of prediction error requires a notion of dis-
tance between two “configurations” which is formally de-
fined as follows: for any multisets A and B of V with
|A| = |B|, we denote by dist(A, B) the minimum cost of
a perfect matching between A and B in the metric (V, d).

Parsimonious Learning Augmentation
For the parsimo-
nious learning-augmented setting (Im et al., 2022; Sadek
& Eli´as, 2024), it is assumed that there exists an oracle
providing a prediction to the algorithm in any round on re-
quest from the algorithm. We consider the following two
parsimonious regimes studied by Sadek & Eli´as (2024):
• Well-separated queries to the oracle: The algorithm
receives a prediction once every k time steps, for some
separation parameter k. Without loss of generality,
the predictions are available to the algorithm in rounds
ik for every i ∈{1, . . . , ⌊n/k⌋}.
• Bounded number of predictions: The algorithm is al-
lowed to make a query to the oracle at any time under
one hard constraint that the total number of queries
must not exceed some budget B ∈N.
Note that the first regime is a special case of the second
regime with budget B = ⌈n/k⌉−1.
The error of the oracle is defined to be the sum of errors
of queried predictions, i.e., η(Q) := P
t∈Q ηt, where Q ⊆
{1, . . . , n} denotes the rounds in which the algorithm makes
queries to the oracle.
Combination Algorithm
In what follows, we focus on
presenting a learning-augmented algorithm that performs
well when the prediction is good, but is not robust when
the prediction is bad, because there is a way to make any
algorithm robust at a constant factor in cost (Fiat et al., 1994;
Antoniadis et al., 2023b). For the sake of completeness, we
provide the formal proof in Section B.
Theorem 2.3. For two (possibly randomized) algorithms A
and B for online metric matching, there exists a combination
algorithm whose output incurs in expectation at most 9 ·
min{E[cost(A)], E[cost(B)]}.

3. Algorithms
In this section, we present our parsimonious learning-
augmented algorithms for online metric matching. They
are built upon a meta-algorithm which is essentially the
Follow-the-Prediction (FtP) algorithm of Antoniadis et al.
(2023b) that queries the prediction to the oracle in every k
round for some k ∈N while generating virtual predictions
for the other rounds using an adherent, strongly competitive
algorithm. The main technical theorem about the meta-
algorithm is as follows:
Theorem 3.1. Given a γ-adherent, strongly ρ-competitive
algorithm and k ∈N, there is an algorithm that outputs a
perfect matching of (expected) cost at most
(1 + γ + ρ(k −1)) cost(OPT) + (2 + γ + ρ(k −1)) η(Q),
where Q = {k, 2k, . . . , ⌊n/k⌋· k} denotes the rounds in
which the algorithm makes queries to the oracle.
Note that the meta-algorithm works for the regime of well-
separated queries to the oracle with separation parameter
k, and hence, it also works for the more general regime of
bounded number of predictions with budget B = ⌊n/k⌋.
We begin by reviewing the FtP algorithm in Section 3.1, and
then describe the construction of (virtual) predictions and
prove Theorem 3.1 in Section 3.2. Finally, in Section 3.3,
we leverage existing algorithms within our meta-algorithm.

5.1. Experimental Setup
Algorithms
We evaluate the following five algorithms in
our experiments:
• Ours, the algorithm described in Theorem 3.6;
• Comp, the deterministic (2n −1)-competitive algo-
rithm due to Kalyanasundaram & Pruhs (1993) and
Khuller et al. (1994);
• Comb-Comp, the combination of Ours and Comp as
formalized in Theorem 1.1;
• Greedy, the simple greedy algorithm that matches
every request to its nearest available server;
• Comb-Greedy, the combination of Ours and Greedy.
We remark that, when setting k = 1, Ours is equivalent
with the Follow-the-Prediction algorithm of Antoniadis et al.
(2023b).
Instance Generation
Each instance is defined on a metric
space with 200 vertices, consisting of 100 servers and 100
requests. We consider three classes of instances—Line,
Plane, and Taxi—which are constructed as follows.
To generate a Line instance, we independently sample 200
numbers uniformly at random from the interval [0, 1], each
representing the location of a vertex on the line. The dis-
tance between two vertices is defined as the absolute differ-
ence between their corresponding numbers. Among these
200 vertices, we sample 100 uniformly at random without
replacement as servers, and sample another 100 uniformly
at random with replacement as requests.
An instance of Plane is constructed similarly to Line. The
only difference is that each vertex corresponds to a point
sampled uniformly at random from the two-dimensional
unit square [0, 1]2, and distances are measured using the
Euclidean metric, with a modest rounding applied for nu-
merical stability.
Inspired by Feng et al. (2024) and Jin & Ma (2022), Taxi
denotes a class of instances constructed from the Chicago
Taxi Trips (2013–2023) dataset (City of Chicago, 2024).
Specifically, for a randomly sampled time point, the most
recent 100 data entries preceding this time are treated as
servers, while the earliest 100 entries following it are treated
as requests arriving sequentially. Distances are measured us-
ing the Manhattan metric. In the interest of space, we defer
the full details of Taxi instance construction to Section F.

6. Conclusion
In this paper, we extend the study of parsimonious prediction
models in learning-augmented online optimization (Im et al.,
2022; Sadek & Eli´as, 2024) to the setting of online metric
matching with action predictions (Antoniadis et al., 2023b).
Several directions remain open for future work. An im-
mediate direction is to develop a more refined analysis for
randomized algorithms in the parsimonious setting. In par-
ticular, it would be interesting to determine whether one
can achieve performance guarantees that depend only on the
separation parameter k, rather than the O(log n log k) factor
in Theorem 3.10, as well as lower bounds for algorithms
that make randomized queries, rather than Theorem 4.3.
The combination algorithm is essential for robustifying our
algorithmic framework. However, its current version incurs
a multiplicative factor of 9 in the competitive ratio. More-

A. Further Related Work
Online Metric Matching
Online metric matching has been studied under a variety of metric spaces. Gupta & Lewi
(2012) presented a randomized O(log n)-competitive algorithm for line and doubling metrics. For deterministic algorithms,
Antoniadis et al. (2019) gave the first sublinear-competitive algorithm for the line metric. Subsequently, Raghvendra
(2018) developed a deterministic O(log n)-competitive algorithm for line metrics, building on an instance-dependent
O(µM(S) log2 n)-competitive algorithm due to Nayyar & Raghvendra (2017), where µM(S) denotes the maximum ratio
between the traveling salesman tour and the diameter over all subsets of the server set S. On the lower-bound side, Peserico
& Scquizzato (2023) recently showed that any algorithm, including randomized ones, must have a competitive ratio of
at least Ω(√log n) on the line metric, improving upon the previous lower bound of 9.001 for deterministic algorithms
established by Fuchs et al. (2005).
The problem has also been studied under other arrival models than the adversarial model considered in this paper. Raghven-
dra (2016) considered the random arrival model and obtained a best-possible O(log n)-competitive algorithm, together with
a matching lower bound. In the known i.i.d. model—where each request is drawn independently from a known distribu-
tion—Gupta et al. (2019) presented an O((log log log n)2)-competitive algorithm for general metrics, which achieves a
constant competitive ratio of 9 for line and tree metrics.
Parsimonious Usage of Predictions
Besides Im et al. (2022) and Sadek & Eli´as (2024), a growing body of work has
investigated how to reduce the amount of predictive information used in learning-augmented algorithms. Drygala et al.
(2023) studied the ski rental and Bahncard problems in settings where using predictions incurs an additional cost. Benomar
& Perchet (2023) examined the tradeoff between algorithmic performance and a bounded number of predictions for problems
such as ski rental, secretary, and non-clairvoyant scheduling, under a model in which predictions are accurate with known
probabilities. Bampis et al. (2024) showed that a logarithmic number of predictions suffices to improve the running time of
PTASs for dense instances of several NP-hard problems. Antoniadis et al. (2023a) introduced single-bit prediction models
for paging and developed learning-augmented algorithms for both models, together with matching lower bounds. Finally,
we note that the parsimonious use of predictions in learning-augmented algorithms is closely related to the literature on
online optimization with advice, which studies the bit complexity of accurate advice required to achieve given performance
guarantees; we refer interested readers to the survey by Boyar et al. (2017).
Learning-Augmented Online Matching and Beyond
Online bipartite matching has also been extensively studied in
the learning-augmented setting. Mahdian et al. (2012) initiated this line of work by developing a learning-augmented
algorithm for the AdWords problem under the small-bids assumption; their result was later improved by Choo et al. (2025).
Spaeh & Ene (2023) extended this framework to the Display Ads and generalized assignment problems. Jin & Ma (2022)
investigated a two-stage model of unweighted/vertex-weighted/edge-weighted online bipartite matching. Related work
has also considered the random arrival model (Antoniadis et al., 2023c; Choo et al., 2024) and the stochastic graph setting
(Aamand et al., 2022). Beyond online bipartite matching, a substantial body of research has explored learning-augmented
algorithms more broadly; interested readers are referred to the survey by Mitzenmacher & Vassilvitskii (2022) and the
website (Lindermayr & Megow), which provides a comprehensive collection of publications in the area.

C.2. Randomized Algorithm
As shown in Meyerson et al. (2006) and Bansal et al. (2014), it is without loss of generality to assume that the vertices of the
given metric spaces are precisely the servers (i.e., V = S) because one can easily modify any general metric space into such
one at a constant factor in competitive ratios. Bansal et al. (2014) presented an algorithm on 2-HSTs.
Definition C.4 (HST). Given α ≥1, an α-hierarchical well-separated tree (α-HST) is a rooted tree T = (U, E) along with
a length ℓ: E →R+ satisfying
• for each internal node u ∈U, the length between u and any child of u is identical, i.e., ℓ(u, c1) = ℓ(u, c2) for any two
children c1 and c2 of u;
• for any internal node u ∈U, the length between u and its parent p is exactly α times the length between u and its child
c, i.e., ℓ(u, p) = α · ℓ(u, c).
It is well-known that any metric space can be embedded into an HST with a logarithmic expected distortion:
Theorem C.5 ((Fakcharoenphol et al., 2004)). Given any metric (V, d) with n vertices, there exists a randomized algorithm
producing 2-HST (T, ℓ) such that
• the leaf nodes of T are corresponding to V ;
• for any u, v ∈V , the distance between u and v in (T, ℓ) is at least d(u, v);
• for any u, v ∈V , the expected distance between u and v in (T, ℓ) is bounded from above by O(log n) · d(u, v).
The factor of d(u, v) in the last property is called the (expected) distortion of (T, ℓ).
We now describe the algorithm of Bansal et al. (2014). Given a 2-HST (T = (U, E), ℓ) with n leaves, for every leaf node
u ∈U and height h ≥0, let T(u, h) denote the set of leaf nodes that belong to the subtree of height h containing u, and let
N(u, h) := T(u, h) \ T(u, h −1) (and N(u, 0) := T(u, 0) for h = 0). The algorithm maintains two matchings M on and
M off such that
• no reassignment happen in M on, but it happens in M off;
• the matched servers are equivalent between M on and M off.
Throughout the execution, the algorithm maintains the level of every server s ∈S, denoted by L(s), defined as the minimum
height of a subtree containing both s and its matched request in M off (and L(s) := ∞if unmatched). Accordingly, it also
maintains the level of every request r that has arrived so far, defined as the level of its matched server in M off.
Upon arrival of a request rt ∈R, the algorithm modifies M off by the following recursive procedure: Let r be the request to
be (re)assigned, initially r := rt and L(r) := 0. It finds the lowest height h ≥L(r) where there is a server s ∈N(r, h)
whose current level is higher than h, i.e., L(s) > h. It then chooses a server s′ uniformly at random among the servers s
in N(r, h) with L(s) > h, and (re)assign s′ to r in M off with setting L(s′) = L(r) = h. If s′ was matched with another
request r′ in M off, the algorithm recursively reassigns r′ using the procedure. Otherwise, if s′ was unmatched, it then
matches rt with s′ in M on and proceeds to the next round.
Its exact adherence is immediate from the proofs of Lemma 4.1 and 4.3 in Bansal et al. (2014), so we omit its proof here.
