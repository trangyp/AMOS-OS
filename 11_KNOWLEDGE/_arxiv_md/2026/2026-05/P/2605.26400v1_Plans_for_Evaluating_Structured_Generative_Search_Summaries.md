Abstract
We propose a framework for evaluating structured generative search
summaries that are placed atop organic web search results. A struc-
tured summary, generated by a large language model, typically
consists of an overview, several sections with section titles, and a
list of source documents that are cited within the summary. We then
describe our plans for implementing and evaluating the framework.
Keywords
evaluation measures, large language models, summaries, web search
1
Introduction
The advance of Large Language Models (LLMs) is rapidly chang-
ing the experience of web search engine users. To try to quickly
satisfy the user’s information need without making them read re-
trieved web pages, search engine companies have started to include
a generated output in the Search Engine Result Page (SERP) for
some queries, atop traditional “organic” web search results. Fig-
ure 1 shows a typical structure of a generated output that comprises
a structured summary and a doclist (i.e., a list of cited documents).
Unlike plain-text search engine outputs that have been considered
in earlier studies (e.g., [43]), the structured summary starts with a
short textual overview that is designed to address the user’s query di-
rectly, followed by one or more textual sections, where each section
consists of a section heading that briefly describes what the section
is about, and a section body that contains one or more statements.
In general, the overview and each statement are accompanied by
zero or more citation numbers. Each citation number points to one
of the documents in the doclist shown beneath the structured sum-
mary. Thus the doclist is a short list of cited web pages, typically
representing a subset of the traditional organic web search results
that are placed below it.
For over two decades, search engine companies have used nDCG
and nDCG-like measures to evaluate and optimise document lists:
see, for example, Burges et al. [4]. However, as generated outputs
in SERPs like the one illustrated in Figure 1 are becoming more
common than ever, we need a sound framework to evaluate such
outputs. In light of this situation, the present study addresses the
question of evaluating the structured summary part of a SERP, i.e.,
the red parts shown in Figure 1, by treating the doclist as a given.
Put another way, our concern is: Given this doclist, how good is this
structured summary? Which parts of it are good/bad and why? How
can we improve it? This evaluation problem is particularly important

Related Work
2.1
Summarisation and Complex Question
Answering Evaluation
Historically, text summarisation and question answering tasks had
a few aspects in common regarding how the systems’ textual re-
sponses were evaluated. Firstly, it was assumed that the ground truth
data (i.e., manually-composed reference summaries or manually-
identified correct answers) were available. Second, the comparison
of the system response and the ground truth were performed by
decomposing both into smaller units such as N-grams [25], se-
mantic content units [29], or nuggets [11, 28, 34, 56]. Among the
nugget-based approaches, our work is inspired by the S-measure/U-
measure frameworks [42, 43], which are unique in that they take
into account the position of each nugget within the summary for
the evaluation.
The modern challenge in evaluating system-generated texts,
however, is that we often do not have access to the ground truth;
it may not even exist. This is also the case in our study, where
the task is to evaluate generated summaries for any web search
query entered by the users. Thus we need a reference-free [13, 19]
evaluation approach.
2.2
RAG and Conversational Search Evaluation
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) combines the strengths of
IR and the generation capability of LLMs [23, 31]. As LLMs can now
extract nuggets from text with reasonable accuracy, Pradeep et al.
[32] “refactored” the nugget-based evaluation method from the
TREC 2003 question answering track [25] for the TREC 2024 RAG
track; see also Alaofi et al. [2], Łajewska and Balog [21]. Nugget-
based and similar RAG evaluation methods were also adopted at the
TREC iKAT 2024 track [1] and the TREC 2024 NeuCLIR track [50].
Other nugget-based RAG evaluation efforts include the ongoing
NTCIR-19 R2C2 task [47].
Conversational search [33] (or Conversational Information Ac-
cess [19]) generally involves multi-turn system responses. (See also
pre-LLM-era efforts in interactive question answering [11].) The
present study focusses on single-turn responses, i.e., a single gen-
erated summary for a given query. However, our work builds on
a user model proposed in the context of conversational search,
namely, that described in Sakai et al. [48]. In their conversational
search user model, a population of users with the same information
need examines a ranked list of nuggets, which represent parts of the
system responses (which can be multi-turn), from top to bottom.
Some of the users abandon the list after seeing a particular relevant
nugget; others continue on.2 Instead of using nuggets as the basic
evaluation unit, we leverage the structured nature of our generated
summaries, and use “line numbers” in the summary as the basic
unit, as we shall describe in Section 3.1. Inspired by the aforemen-
tioned S-measure/U-measure approach, we use line numbers as the
positions of relevant pieces of information within the summary.
2The user model is similar to that of the Sakai/Robertson Normalised Cumulative Utility
(NCU) measures [46]; the main difference is that the NCU user model is for ranked list
of documents such as web pages.
2.3
Evaluation Axes
The SWAN (Schematised Weighted Average Nugget) framework [40]
lists 21 evaluation criteria for conversational systems, including
groundedness (i.e., Is the claim supported by a piece of evidence?) [27],
sufficiency (i.e., Does the system’s turn satisfy the requests in the
previous user turn?) modesty (i.e., Is the system’s answer confidence
score appropriate?) [41, 47], fair exposure (i.e., Does the system men-
tion entities from different groups fairly in its responses?) [48], and
harmlessness (i.e., “no stereotypes, no microaggressions, no threats, no
sexual aggression, no insults, no hate or harassment” [18]). See also
the HHH (helpful, honest, and harmless) criteria of Askell et al. [3].
Joko and Hasibi [19] take an approach related to SWAN for
evaluating conversational information access, where not only turn-
level axes such as interestingness (i.e., engagingness of SWAN) but
also dialogue-level axes such as task completion and overall impres-
sion are considered. The Dialogue Quality subtasks of the NTCIR
DialEval tasks [53, 59] also used related criteria, including task ac-
complishment and customer satisfaction, in the context of evaluating
helpdesk-customer (human-human) dialogues.
In terms of evaluation criteria, our framework concerns relevance
to the user’s turn, faithfulness [5, 13, 16, 22, 24, 26, 35, 55, 60] with
respect to the cited documents (which we equate with grounded-
ness),3 and comprehensiveness which is similar to sufficiency [40]
and coverage [17]. While it is relatively easy to discuss the rele-
vance and faithfulness of a given summary, that is not the case
with comprehensiveness, since we generally lack the ground truth
and therefore there is no notion of recall base. Hence, to define
comprehensiveness, we require multiple summaries per user query,
so that a concept akin to relative recall [8] can be leveraged. We
shall define comprehensiveness in Section 3.4.
2.4
Reading Models
The SIGIR 2024 perspectives paper by Gienapp et al. [17] has a movi-
tation similar to ours except that they do not discuss the structured
nature of generated summaries for a given query. They propose
a user reading model which is a “monotonically decreasing weight
function over statements that discounts the contribution of later state-
ments in a response” to reward “putting the most important pieces
of information first.” This is exactly what the aforementioned S-
measure from 2011 [43] featured. In this paper, we also employ a
user reading model, but ours is based on the assumption that there
is a population of users reading a structured summary, as mentioned
in Section 2.2.
2.5
LLMs Evaluating LLMs
There is a controversy regarding the evaluation of LLMs using
LLMs [6, 14, 30, 45, 51, 54]. In the context of web search, clearly our
goal is to satisfy the user, not an LLM. However, our view is that
LLMs can be leveraged effectively when used within a divide and
conquer evaluation approach: “if LLMs’ behaviours are not altogether
predictable, then we should try to reduce the number of confounding
uncertainty factors, by giving them simple and controllable subtasks

Proposed Evaluation Framework
Unlike existing studies that explore the evaluation of “flat” text
with citations (e.g. [17]), our evaluation framework evaluates the
structured summary part of a SERP in the context of a given query
and a doclist, as illustrated in Figure 1. The structured summary
generally consists of an overview 𝑜followed by 𝐼(≥0) sections,
where each section sec𝑖(𝑖= 1, . . . , 𝐼) contains a section heading
ℎ𝑖and 𝐽𝑖(> 0) statements 𝑠𝑖𝑗(𝑗= 1, . . . , 𝐽𝑖). The overview and the
statements are accompanied by zero or more citation numbers. (In-
stead of an overview at the beginning of the structured summary,
a short “summary” may be placed at the end: our evaluation frame-
work accommodates both. Hereafter, we shall focus on structured
summaries with an initial overview.)
Sections 3.1-3.3 describe our Expected User Experience (XUX)
measure, which evaluates a given structured summary from a user
perspective. However, as XUX cannot quantify neither “relevant
information that is missing in the text” nor “balanced coverage of
multiple aspects relevant to the information need,” Section 3.4 de-
scribes our Comprehensiveness (Comp) measure which is designed
to address these questions by “pooling” sections from multiple struc-
tured summaries; this is similar in spirit to estimating the recall of
multiple “10-blue-links” search engines [8] and to the traditional
document pooling for constructing IR test collections [39, 52, 57]. Fi-
nally, Section 3.5 defines our quick summary measure, SGSS (Struc-
tured Generative Search Summary) score, as a combination of XUX
and Comp.

3.4
Comprehensiveness
In 1973, Cooper, in the context of discussing the choice of IR eval-
uation measures, remarked: “Why should the status of documents
which the system user has not examined have any influence on a
user-oriented performance rating?” [9, p.95]. (See also Zobel et al.
[61] from 2009.) The XUX measure discussed above aligns with
the above “precision-oriented” perspective since it only evaluates a
given structured summary, i.e., what the user actually sees. How-
ever, from a system developer (e.g., search engine company) point
of view, we would also like to ask “What is missing in the struc-
tured summary? What else could we have offered to the user?”: a
“recall-oriented” perspective.
As we have discussed in Section 2.3, we are dealing with reference-
free evaluation and therefore we have to establish a notion similar
to relative recall. In order to do this, we require that we have multi-
ple summaries for a given query so that we can “pool” information
contributed by each of them. More specifically, as we are evaluating
structured summaries, we pool sections from multiple structured
summaries to compare them in terms of comprehensiveness as de-
scribed below. As each section can be regarded as a representation

4
Plans
We are currently preparing data for implementing and evaluating
the SGSS evaluation framework described above. Below, we describe
our ongoing work and near-future plans.
4.1
Dataset Construction
We are collecting generated output data (See Figure 1) for real
search engine queries. As comprehensiveness evaluation requires
multiple structured summaries per query, we will collect at least
two (each with one or more sections) structured summaries per
query by using multiple generative summarisers. Moreover, from
these structured summaries, we will automatically create degraded
versions of the summaries using strategies such as the following:
NoHeadings Remove all section headings from the original
summary. Comparing the original summary with a NoHead-
ings version in terms of SGSS should be able to tell us the
importance of the section headings. Note that NoHeadings
summaries will receive zero HR scores (Eq. 6).
NoSection1 Remove the first section from the original sum-
mary. As the first section of a structured summary is gener-
ally expected to be at least as important as the other sections,
if we compare the original summary with its NoSection1
version, we expect SGSS to generally prefer the original
over the NoSection1 version (unless the quality of the first
section is low).
We will construct summary pair data for a query set, where
the two structured summaries represent two different generative
summarisers. To have human labellers compare the two summaries
side by side, we will develop an annotation interface similar to that
for comparing traditional ten-blue-links (e.g. [49, Figure 1]). We
will assign 𝐻(> 2) human labellers to each summary pair, and each
human labeller will provide the following labels.
• OS (Overview Sufficiency), OF (Overview Faithfulness), and
OR (Overview Representativeness) labels for the overviews
of the two summaries (3-point scale);
• An HR (Heading Representativeness) label for each heading
of the two summaries (3-point scale);
• SRel (Statement Relevance) and SF (Statement Faithfulness)
labels for each statement of the two summaries (3-point
scale);
• An absolute Comprehensiveness label for each summary
(3-point scale);
• A preference label which declares which of the two sum-
maries is preferred (LEFT or RIGHT).
In addition, we will have LLMs provide the same set of labels. We can
then average the labels over the (human or combined) labellers. In
addition, we can derive similar data for the degraded summaries: we
assume that an original summary is preferred over its NoHeadings
or NoSection1 version (if the removed section contained at least
one relevant statement); the other absolute labels for the original
summary mentioned above can simply be inherited to its degraded
version.
The dataset thus constructed will be split into a training part
and a test part: the former will be utilised for tuning purposes, i.e.,
for setting the weights, and the latter will be used for evaluating
and validating the SGSS framework.
Besides the above dataset, we will utilise a commercial search
engine query log to explore methods for estimating the maximum
time the user spends on a structured summary, to determine 𝐿max
for computing XUX (See Section 3.1).
4.2
Implementing SGSS
It can be observed from the formulas presented in Section 3 that
SGSS is a linear combination of several measures, with weights
𝑤OS,𝑤OF,𝑤OR,𝑤HR,𝑤SRel,𝑤SF,𝑤Comp. Hence, if the SGSS scores of
a given pair of summaries from the above dataset are denoted
by SGSS(𝑆LEFT) and SGSS(𝑆RIGHT), the following is also a linear
combination of the same measures with the above weights:
ΔSGSS = SGSS(𝑆LEFT) −SGSS(𝑆RIGHT) .
(13)
Since we have a preference label for each summary pair as well as
the absolute labels for the overviews, headings, and statements in
the summaries, we can apply (say) logistic regression to the training
portion of the dataset to determine the weights, where the target
variable is the probability that 𝑆LEFT is better than 𝑆RIGHT. This will
give us our first instantiation of SGSS.
We will also consider model selection: can we simplify the defi-
nition of SGSS by removing one or more evaluation criteria?
4.3
Evaluating SGSS
We would like SGSS to agree with human summary preferences. To
check this, we can use the test portion of our dataset (which was
not used for tuning the weights), and compute the agreement rate
for each summary pair:
AR = #human preference labels that agree with ΔSGSS

test data, we will have to check whether relying only on LLM-based
labels suffices, because we would like to be able to automatically
estimate whether a given new summary is better than another. (We
will first have to examine the reliability of LLM-based labels by
comparing them with the human labels.) We will then take the
mean of AR over the summary pairs in the test set to obtain the
Mean AR (MAR) [49]. We want the MAR of SGSS to be as high as

Summary
We proposed a framework for evaluating structured generative
search summaries that are placed atop organic web search results,
and described our plans for implementing and evaluating the frame-
work. The outcomes of our implementation and evaluation will be
reported elsewhere.
