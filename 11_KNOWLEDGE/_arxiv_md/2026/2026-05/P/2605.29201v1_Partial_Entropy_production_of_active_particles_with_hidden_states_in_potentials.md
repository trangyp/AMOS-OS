Partial Entropy production of active particles with hidden states in potentials
Jacob Knight1, ∗and Gunnar Pruessner1, †
1Department of Mathematics, Imperial College London, South Kensington, London SW7 2BZ, UK
(Dated: 26 May 2026)
Partially observed stochastic systems can appear (almost) time-reversal symmetric while in fact
operating far from equilibrium. The present work extends the perturbative framework introduced
in [Phys. Rev. Lett. 136, 198302 (2026)] to calculate in a generic confining potential the partial
entropy production, which quantifies the time-reversal asymmetry of a generic active particle with
hidden self-propulsion. Focusing on the harmonic case, we apply our framework to reproduce an
exact result for the partial entropy production rate of an active Ornstein-Uhlenbeck particle and to
derive the partial entropy production rate of a run-and-tumble particle.
Broken time-reversal symmetry (TRS) always implies
that a system is out of equilibrium. However, the con-
verse is not true: a system which is out-of-equilibrium
may appear fully or almost fully time-reversible if it can-
not be observed with complete resolution, in particular
when (some of) its internal degrees of freedom remain
hidden. This presents a problem to experimentalists aim-
ing to answer the key question of whether a system oper-
ates at equilibrium or is subject to some non-equilibrium
driving. This is of particular relevance when this becomes
a question of observation, i.e. which observables are en-
quired to determine its “non-equilibriumness”.
Since
many experiments involve observing complex systems at
a mesoscopic level, particularly in biology, this problem
is ubiquitous across a range of fields [1, 2].
Stochastic processes, both physical and theoretical,
can appear identical, similar or very different upon time-
reversal. When a stochastic process is observed in full mi-
croscopic detail, its time-irreversibility as quantified by
the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the probability
densities of the forward and time-reversed trajectories,
Eq. (2) below, is proportional to its physical entropy pro-
duction rate (EPR). This allows thermodynamic bounds
to be placed on the speed and accuracy with which such
processes can occur [3–5]. When such a process is instead
observed at a mesoscopic level (i.e. partially hidden), the
Kullback-Leibler divergence of distributions of the visi-
ble variables represents only a lower bound to the entropy
production rate [6, 7]. Adopting terminology introduced
by us in [8], this lower bound is subsequently referred to
as the partial EPR of the process.
Hiding a single degree of freedom can completely
restore TRS to the visible dynamics of an out-of-
equilibrium system, such as a free run-and-tumble (RnT)
particle [8] with its internal self-propulsion state hidden.
Despite the fact that the underlying system is away from
equilibrium, the particle dynamics will therefore yield a
partial EPR of zero and yet may still have work extracted

In the present work we derive the partial EPR of a
class of canonical stochastic processes, namely confined
active particles whose position, but not self-propulsion,
can be observed. We thereby extend the framework of
[8] to include the presence of a confining potential. Our
approach is to start from a complete description of the
fully observable system, represented by joint path proba-
bility distributions of the position and self-propulsion of
the active particle. We then marginalise over the hid-
den self-propulsion, representing the partially observable
system through the path probability distribution of the
particle position only. Although marginalising over the

III.
DISCUSSION AND OUTLOOK
The key result of this paper is the expansion Eq. (15), a
series representation of the partial EPR of an active par-
ticle. For active particles with no net self-propulsion, i.e.
⟨w(t)⟩= 0, the first term in the expansion vanishes, re-
sulting in a partial EPR of order n = 2 or higher. These
processes can be considered zero-mean non-Markovian
fluctuations.
However, even for active particles with
⟨w⟩̸= 0, the leading order is n > 1 in a confining
potential as in steady state ˙x = 0 to all orders in ν.
As such, Eq. (15) demonstrates that in such current-free
processes, the leading order of the partial EPR is beyond
that of the full EPR, which for most active particles is of
order ν2/D [16, 29].
In general, compared to their free counterpart, intro-
ducing a potential increases the time-reversal asymme-
try of purely spatial trajectories of particles, i.e. their
partial EPR, since it provides a preferred direction of
travel at each point in space.
This can be seen from
Eq. (16), which suggests that the leading order contribu-
tion due to the potential is generally positive. Intuition
is provided by the run-and-tumble (RnT) dynamics illus-
trated in Fig. 1. In the absence of a confining potential,
forwards and reversed trajectories can be distinguished
only when both their position and their self-propulsion is
visible. When the self-propulsion is hidden, forwards and
reversed trajectories become indistinguishable, Fig. 1a.ii.
This is not true for a harmonically confined particle. Spa-
tial trajectories alone break time-reversal symmetry in
this case, since the particle moves quickly down the po-
tential and slowly up the other side.
In reversed tra-
jectories, particles move slowly down the potential and
climb quickly upwards.
However, the extent to which
time-reversal symmetry is broken decreases when the self-
propulsion is hidden, as quantified by the partial EPR,
as Eq. (30) is ∝ν8/D4 (n = 4), while the full EPR is
∝ν2/D.
The presence of a potential in Eq. (1) means that
symmetries of w(t) do not fully determine the leading-
order partial EPR. Since the expansion with a potential,
Eq. (15), contains all the terms of the expansion without
a potential in addition to new terms, the partial EPR of
a particle in a potential is of an order n in the expansion
that is generally lower or equal to that of a free parti-
cle. For instance, the asymmetric telegraph process with
⟨w⟩= 0 described in Ref. [18, Suppl. SIII A] has leading-
order partial EPR n = 2 in the expansion Eq. (15), un-


## Page 8


8
like the corresponding free particle whose leading-order
partial EPR appears at n = 3. In the special case of a
harmonic potential, the leading-order EPR does enter at
n = 3 (cf. Eq. (18)) but contains an extra term compared
to the free case.
An AOUP in a harmonic potential represents the
unique nontrivial choice of w(t) and V (x) that produces
zero partial EPR [26, 27] in the steady state: No distri-
bution other than a Gaussian contains a finite number of
non-zero cumulants [30], making the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck
process a unique choice of w(t) that gives rise to a finite
number of terms in Eq. (15). The single term that arises,
given in Eq. (21), comes from the second cumulant of
w(t) and is non-zero for any confining potential except
for V (x) ∝x2. Any other choice of self-propulsion pro-
cess represents a non-Gaussian noise in the dynamics of
x(t) [9–11]. The non-vanishing persistence of the noise
combined with the presence of a potential results in x(t)
violating TRS.
While order n implies a pre-factor (ν/D)n in the ex-
pansion Eq. (15), the results above suggest, but do not
show in general, a pre-factor (ν2/D)n, with an addi-
tional factor of νn stemming from the leading order of
( ˙x(t1) + V ′ (x1)) ... ( ˙x(tn) + V ′ (xn)). The symmetry ar-
guments in Sec. II A determine the leading-order term
in Eq. (15) for a harmonically-confined RnT particle.
This does not explain why evaluating this leading-order
partial EPR yields an expression proportional to ν8/D4
rather than ν4/D4 [31] or ν6/D4 (with additional fac-
tors containing D, α and k for dimensional consistency).
Equivalently, we observe that x(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4) con-
tains terms proportional to ν0D2, ν2D and ν4 while
Ldx(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4) contains only terms proportional
to ν4. We expect Ldx(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4) not to contain
terms proportional to ν0 since such terms describe the
behaviour of a purely diffusive particle in a harmonic po-
tential (i.e. OU dynamics): the self-propulsion in Eq. (1)
has a prefactor of ν.
Correlation functions of x(t) to
order ν0 can be written as sums of pairwise moments,
x(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4)|0 = f(t4 −t3)f(t2 −t1) + . . ., where
•|0 here denotes terms in • proportional to ν0, i.e. the
terms at ν = 0. Since the derivative operator can be ex-
pressed as Ld = ∂t1∂t2(∂t3 +∂t4)+∂t3∂t4(∂t1 +∂t2), or as
Ld = ∂t1∂t3(∂t2+∂t4)+∂t2∂t4(∂t1+∂t3) etc., Eqs. (S23) in
[19], we have Ldx(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4)|0 = 0 by the same
mechanism that produced Eq. (18).
However, such an
argument does not explain why Ldx(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4)
does not contain terms at order ν2D.
It is striking that the leading-order partial EPR of an
RnT particle in a harmonic potential Eq. (30) appears
at order (ν2/D)4, while the full EPR is proportional to
ν2/D. However, such high-order partial EPRs have al-
ready been identified in [8], in particular for active par-
ticles with parity-symmetric, time-asymmetric driving,
such as allowing w(t) to undergo diffusion with stochas-
tic resetting.
Calculating the correlators needed to evaluate leading-
order partial EPRs in the present framework is challeng-
ing. Doi-Peliti field theory [28, 29, 32–35], used for the
case of a harmonically confined RnT particle, represents
a powerful tool for such calculations. Conventional prob-
abilistic methods based on Fokker-Planck equations can
also be used if more convenient [24, 36]. Correlation func-
tions can also be determined through Monte Carlo simu-
lations and partial EPR calculated through numerical in-
tegration [18, SIV]. Expressing time-irreversibility, which
is challenging to measure numerically, in terms of corre-
lation functions is an appealing feature of the present
framework.
As discussed in [8], a thermodynamic interpretation
of the partial EPR would be an interesting subject of
future research [6]. In particular, this framework could
be extended to time-varying potentials to allow for the
investigation of the entropy production associated with
work extraction protocols [37, 38] or to potentials which
depend on both the observable and the hidden variable
[39].

according to Eq. (30) is n = 4, giving rise to a term ∝ν8/D4. As the correlator x(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4) contains terms
proportional only to powers m ≤2 of D2(ν2/D)m, it will not give rise to further corrections.
In this section, we verify that the next term in the expansion Eq. (15), n = 6, does not contribute to the same or
lower order in ν than n = 4, i.e. that terms of order ν0 and ν2 in x(t1)x(t2)x(t3)x(t4)x(t5)x(t6) do not contribute
to the partial EPR, as they would otherwise give rise to terms ∝(ν/D)6D3 or ∝(ν/D)6D2ν2 overall. The leading
order contribution from the next order in n is therefore of the same order as the next order contribution at the
leading order in n, was it not for the constraint 2m ≤n. That the n = 8 term contributes without transmutation,
contributing overall (ν/D)8D4, can be ruled out as this is a term due to an equilibrium process. Mathematically,
as this is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, the correlation functions can be written as a product of pair-correlation
functions, depending on pairwise time-differences, which vanish under the action of time-differentiation operators like
Eq. (S23).

A.
Generation of diagrams
The algorithm is used to generate all Doi-Peliti diagrams which contribute to the correlator x(t1) ... x(t6). Diagrams
are generated order by order in ν. The logic for generating all diagrams with n legs of order νz is laid out in the
following.
We label the legs that make up a diagram by i = 1, 2, . . . , n from right to left. Each such leg contains si ≥0
field transmutation vertices ∝ν, e.g. s1 = 1 in the two diagrams shown in Eqs. (S18), producing the constraint
Pn
i=1 si = z. Each field transmutation increases a leg’s index (or Hermite-mode) by one [5]. Each junction between
legs increases or decreases the index by one, Eq. (S16), giving rise to a factor that needs to be tracked. Consistent
with that factor, the index cannot become negative. We will further denote by pi ∈{−1, 1} the difference between
the right index of the leg i + 1 to the left of a junction and the left index of leg i to the right. Every diagram begins
on the right with index 0 and terminates on the left with index 1, so that Pn
i=1(si + pi) = 1.
In a “brute-force” algorithm, we generate all {s1, ... , sn} ∈{0, 1, ... , z}n and all {p1, ... , pn} ∈{−1, 1}n, in total
(2(z + 1))n configurations and retain those if
• the diagram has the desired order, Pn
i=1 si = z.
• the index increases overall by unity, Pn
i=1(si + pi) = 1.
• the index never becomes negative, Pj
i=1(si + pi) ≥0 for j = 1, 2, ... , n −1.
For n = 4, this algorithm reproduces the diagrams shown in Eq. (S14) for z = 0 and in Tab. I for z = 2 and z = 4.
