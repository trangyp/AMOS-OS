Introduction
The Helmholtz free energy F is the fundamental object of classical equilibrium statistical
mechanics: all thermodynamic quantities follow from it by differentiation. Computing
F from first principles for a realistic interacting system requires either an exact solution
(rare) or a controlled approximation. Perturbation theory, originating with Zwanzig [3]
and Barker–Henderson [4], expands F in powers of a coupling parameter λ about a soluble
reference F0, yielding a cumulant series whose coefficients are equilibrium averages with
respect to the reference Gibbs state.
Independently, [2] we have shown that the intensive–extensive classification of thermo-
dynamic variables is itself emergent: it arises precisely when a combined coarse-graining
operator C = Cx ◦Cp, acting jointly on physical space and momentum space, produces
mesoscopic variables that factorise across spatial cells. The coarse-grained entropy is
additive if and only if inter-cell mutual information vanishes—a condition equivalent to
exponential decay of correlations, which holds under the stability and temperedness of the
pair potential.
In this paper, we unite these two lines of work. The central object is the meso-
scopic partition function Zmeso(λ), constructed directly from the combined coarse-graining
framework of [2]: given the product-cell partition {Ci,α} of single-particle phase space
and the cell-averaged Hamiltonians ¯εi,α and inter-cell interactions ¯v(i,α)(j,β), one sums
the Boltzmann weight e−βHmeso over all occupation-number configurations weighted by
the phase-space volume W. The full perturbation theory of F is then derived at the
mesoscopic level: each ingredient—the cumulant expansion, Gibbs–Bogoliubov bound, and
coupling-parameter integration—has a precise mesoscopic counterpart formulated in terms
of {π(0)
i,α} and {¯v(i,α)(j,β)}.
This paper present the following main new results are:
(i) A rigorous definition of the mesoscopic partition function Zmeso(λ) from the combined
coarse-graining operator of [2], and its exact factorisation at λ = 0 into Z(0)
meso =
(Z(0)
1 )N.
(ii) A mesoscopic cumulant expansion of Fmeso(λ) in terms of occupation-number cumu-
lants of Vmeso with respect to the reference multinomial distribution.
(iii) A mesoscopic Gibbs–Bogoliubov inequality and coupling-parameter integration for-
mula, with explicit convergence to the standard continuum results as cells shrink.
(iv) A connection formula F(λ) = Fmeso(λ)−kBT P
i<j I(i, j; λ)+O(|Λ| ℓ−de−2ℓ/ξ), show-
ing that the mutual information from the extensivity analysis is precisely the correc-
tions between the mesoscopic and full partition functions.
(v) For systems with long-range interactions, where factorisation fails, a systematic
correction to the mesoscopic free energy expressed in terms of non-vanishing inter-
cell mutual information.

Discussion
The mesoscopic partition function as a bridge.
The mesoscopic partition function
Zmeso(λ) defined in theorem 3.5 is the exact bridge between the coarse-graining framework
of [2] and standard perturbation theory. Its reference part (15) factorises into (Z(0)
1 )N
because the combined coarse-graining produces statistically independent cells—this is
precisely the factorisation condition that [2] identifies with extensivity. The perturbed part
introduces inter-cell interactions that break factorisation, and the degree of this breaking
is measured by the mutual informations I(i, j; λ), which are the same objects that control
entropy additivity in [2].
Extensivity as a precondition for perturbation theory.
The connection formula (31)
shows that perturbation theory at the mesoscopic level is trustworthy precisely when
the system is extensive: F(λ) ≈Fmeso(λ) iff I(i, j; λ) ≈0 iff SCG ≈P
i Si (extensivity).
Thus the conditions under which the standard perturbation theory is valid—stability,
temperedness, short-range interactions—are exactly those that guarantee extensivity in
the sense of [2]. The two theories are not merely analogous; they are logically equivalent
characterisations of the same microscopic condition.
Non-extensive systems.
For gravitational or other long-range systems, I(i, j; λ) ̸→0
and the mesoscopic free energy Fmeso(λ) overestimates F(λ) by the total mutual information
kBT P
i<j I(i, j). Equation (31) provides a systematic correction: one can include the pair
mutual information term explicitly, using the Ursell-function bound I(i, j) ≤C|xi −xj|−s
for potentials decaying as r−s, to obtain a controlled non-extensive free energy perturbation
theory.
Cell-size dependence and the continuum limit.
The mesoscopic theory depends
on the choice of cell partition {Ci,α}. As ℓ→0, theorems 6.1 and 7.2 show that the
mesoscopic cumulants converge to their continuum counterparts and Fmeso →F. For finite
ℓ, the mesoscopic theory is an approximation whose accuracy improves monotonically as
ℓ→0 (assuming ℓ≫ξ is maintained). The optimal cell size balances computational
tractability (few cells for large ℓ) against accuracy (small ℓfor convergence to the contin-
uum); theorem 11.1 shows that larger cells enlarge the convergence radius of the cumulant
series.
