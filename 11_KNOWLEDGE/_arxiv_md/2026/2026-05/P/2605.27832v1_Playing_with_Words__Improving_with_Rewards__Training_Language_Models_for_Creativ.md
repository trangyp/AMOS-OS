Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are being ap-
plied to increasingly difficult problems and use
cases. To navigate their vast solution spaces
effectively, LLMs need to be creative. Yet the
subjective nature of creativity and the limits
of human judgment make training LLMs for
creativity especially challenging. As a solu-
tion, we train LLMs on Codenames, a word-
association game that exercises the two cen-
tral axes of creativity, divergent and conver-
gent thinking, while yielding objectively ver-
ifiable outcomes.
This verifiability lets us
bypass human judgment and train with Rein-
forcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards
(RLVR). We train Qwen3-1.7B, 4B, and 8B
models and evaluate them on ten creativity and
four reasoning benchmarks. We find that the
precision-diversity trade-off is scale-dependent:
the 8B model prioritizes creativity over preci-
sion, while the 1.7B and 4B models gain rea-
soning precision at the cost of creativity. Con-
cretely, the 8B model shows modest but consis-
tent creativity gains (8 of 10 benchmarks) with
only minor reasoning degradation, whereas the
smaller models achieve substantial gains on rea-
soning tasks. Our study presents a scalable and
effective solution to train LLMs for creativity.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) have advanced at
a remarkable pace, expanding from fluent text gen-
eration to tackling challenging tasks such as solv-
ing complex mathematical problems to generating
novel content (Bubeck et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2024;
Wu et al., 2026). Performance on these hard prob-
lems hinges on creativity, the capacity to navigate
vast problem spaces and arrive at unconventional
yet effective solutions (Yang et al., 2022; Beaty and
Kenett, 2023; Tian et al., 2024). This exposes a
central but unresolved question in contemporary

AI research: how can we explicitly train LLMs to
be genuinely creative, rather than merely hoping
creativity emerges as a byproduct of scale?
A natural starting point comes from cognitive
science, where human creativity has long been
operationalized as an associative process over se-
mantic memory (Beaty and Kenett, 2023; Med-
nick, 1962; Luchini et al., 2025).
Novel ideas
arise when distantly related concepts are combined
into new, functional wholes. Computational and
neurocognitive studies converge on a striking find-
ing that highly creative individuals possess more
densely connected semantic networks with shorter
path distances between concepts, enabling the rapid
linkage of remote ideas that underlie creative as-
sociation (Kenett and Faust, 2019; Kenett et al.,
2014). This architecture aligns with the principle
of combinatorial creativity, where novelty emerges
from the rearrangement and integration of existing
knowledge components rather than from creation
ex nihilo (Boden, 1998; Yang et al., 2022). LLMs,
despite their architectural differences, also learn
predominantly through association (Mikolov et al.,
2013; Johnson et al., 2025; Barenholtz, 2026). This
parallel motivates a concrete research question: can
we systematically train LLMs to integrate the ex-
isting knowledge to improve not just creativity but
complex problem-solving more broadly?
The central obstacle to training creativity directly
is that creativity is inherently subjective (Vessel
et al., 2018; Forthmann et al., 2017). Humans do
not always agree on what constitutes creative work
(Vessel et al., 2018; Cortes and Lawrence, 2021).
We sidestep this difficulty (Casper et al., 2023) by
turning to a structured environment where creative
decision-making is inherent, but the outcomes of
the decisions are objectively measurable: the word-
association game Codenames. Codenames exter-
nalizes the two canonical stages of creative cogni-
tion — divergence (generating remote associations
that span multiple concepts) and convergence (se-

lecting the single best association that uniquely
identifies the targets) — and grades each turn with
an unambiguous outcome (Cropley, 2006). This
makes it an ideal testbed for putting an LLM’s asso-
ciative capacity under measurable pressure without
the noise of subjective preference judgments.
In Codenames, a board of unrelated words is
laid out, and a small subset is secretly designated
as target words; the remaining words on the board,
including some that must be actively avoided, are
non-targets. One player, the spymaster, sees which
words are targets and must produce a single clue
word that points to as many targets as possible
while steering teammates away from every non-
target. The teammates, who see only the board,
then guess which words the clue refers to.
A
good clue is semantically remote enough from the
non-targets to disambiguate them, yet semantically
close enough to several targets to link them through
a shared latent dimension — a chair, an altar, and a
stage might all be connected through the abstract
concept of platform. Strong play, therefore, de-
mands exactly the divergent-then-convergent asso-
ciative leap that cognitive scientists identify as the
core mechanism of creativity.
Casting creativity as a game with verifiable
outcomes lets us bypass expensive human pref-
erence annotation entirely. Instead, we train with
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards
(RLVR) (Guo et al., 2025), the paradigm that has re-
cently driven step-change improvements in LLMs.
Through RLVR training on Codenames, we fine-
tune three Qwen3 models (1.7B, 4B, and 8B) to
reorganize existing knowledge and discover unseen
associations. We summarize our key contributions
and findings below:
• Creativity Training. We cast creativity as a
verifiable-reward game, train LLMs via RLVR
without human annotation, and evaluate on ten
creativity tasks and four reasoning benchmarks.
• Precision-Diversity Trade-off. We show this
known trade-off is scale-dependent: smaller
models gain reasoning precision at the cost of
creativity, while the 8B model gains creativity
with a slight precision loss.
• Creativity Gains. The 8B model shows modest
but consistent creativity gains.
• Reasoning Gains. The 1.7B and 4B models
show substantial gains on reasoning tasks.

Related Work
Improving LLM Creativity.
Prior work has ex-
plored several routes toward more creative LLMs.
A first line uses prompting and decoding strategies
(Tian et al., 2024; Mehrotra et al., 2024; Nair et al.,
2024; Summers-Stay et al., 2023; Franceschelli and
Musolesi, 2024; Meister et al., 2023), including
inference-time analogical reasoning that searches
for cross-domain solutions (Shen et al., 2026). A
second line uses multi-agent role-playing, such as
the three-phase divergent-to-convergent ideation
framework of Lu et al. (2024). A third line turns
to learning: Fukuda et al. (2025) fine-tune on the
Divergent Association Task and find gains only
under specific conditions, while Ismayilzada et al.
(2025) proposes CrPO, a preference-based align-
ment method optimizing novelty, diversity, and sur-
prise on human creativity judgments. While some
studies focus on data selection strategies to improve
various dimensions of creativity (Lanchantin et al.,
2025; Deshpande et al., 2025). Across these ef-
forts, RLVR (Guo et al., 2025), the method most
responsible for recent step-change gains and the
most scalable since it needs no human preference
annotation, remains unexplored as a route to cre-
ativity.
Games and Self-Improvement with RLVR.
A
core limitation of RLHF (Ouyang et al., 2022) is
its dependence on human-curated data, which con-
strains scalability (Liu et al., 2025; Wang et al.,
2025). Training LLMs on games through self-play
with verifiable rewards (RLVR) holds the potential
to address this bottleneck to a great extent (Schaul,
2024). A growing body of work has applied this
paradigm across diverse environments, e.g., adver-
sarial word games (Cheng et al., 2024), logic puz-
zles (Xie et al., 2025a; Wong et al., 2025), strate-
gic board games (Hwang et al., 2025; Yuan et al.,
2025), arcade-style visual games (Xie et al., 2025b;
Tong et al., 2025), and zero-sum multi-turn games
(Liu et al., 2025)—and a striking commonality is
the consistent report of out-of-distribution (OOD)
gains on reasoning benchmarks unrelated to the
training games.

Method
3.1
Training Data
Game Environment
We adopt Codenames, a
word association game, as the training environment.
The original board game places four sets of words


## Page 3


on the board and involves two competing teams.
Of these four sets, two are target sets, one assigned
to each team. The remaining words form a neutral
set and a single assassin word that ends the game.
We simplify this setup by removing the neutral and
assassin words. This allows us to focus on a clean
signal of word association, free from higher-level
rules. Each team consists of one clue generator (the
Spymaster) and one or more guess generators (the
Operatives). Only the clue generator can see which
target words belong to which team. Its goal is to
find a common link, the clue, that connects multiple
words in its team’s target set. The guess generators,
by contrast, cannot see how the separation between
the two target sets. They have access to all words
on the board along with the clue, and their goal
is to deduce which words the clue refers to. Each
team aims to correctly guess all of its target words
before the opposing team.
In our training setup, we train a single LLM that
plays the role of one team in the game environ-
ment. This is equivalent to having the LLM switch
between both teams, intending to complete its as-
signed target set as quickly as possible while avoid-
ing the words in the non-target set. In its natural
form, Codenames for a single team is a multi-turn
discussion. The clue generator has access to all
context, while the guess generators cannot see the
separation between the two target sets. These dis-
cussions can become extremely long. Thinking
traces alone can easily reach several thousand to-
kens for a single turn. This increased length raises
training cost and makes training instabilities more
likely. To address this, we break the multi-turn
discussions into multiple single-turn conversations.
Specifically, we model clue generation and guess
generation as single-turn tasks, and we strategically
sample board positions to simulate every stage of
the full end-to-end game.
More precisely, we model this training environ-
ment as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). Let
w be any whitespace-separated English word that
can be represented by one or more tokens in the
LLM’s vocabulary V. Let T and N denote the tar-
get and non-target word sets, and let B denote the
combined and shuffled set T ∪N. Let wclue be a
single whitespace-separable clue word, let G be the
set of guesses, and gmax be the maximum number
of allowed guesses in one chance. We represent the
state set of the MDP as
S = {T , N, B, wclue, gmax}.
(1)
During the clue generation task, B, wclue, gmax are
null. During the guess generation task, T and N
are empty. We define the action set as
A = {wclue, Tselected, G}.
(2)
During the clue generation task, the LLM generates
wclue for a subset of words in T i.e., Tselected (so
G = ∅). During the guess generation task, it gener-
ates only G (so wclue = Tselected = ∅). Note that,
Tselected ⊂T and 1 ≤|Tselected| = gmax. We
assume that the transition function P is not accessi-
ble for this MDP. In the following subsections, we
present details on how we sample our state set and
subsequently discuss details on reward functions.
Sampling State Set
We loosely refer to both B
and {T , N} as board positions. The two differ
in one important way: {T , N} encodes the sep-
aration between T and N, whereas B does not.
Accordingly, {T , N} is available only during clue
generation, and B only during guess generation. A
complete state also includes the clue word wclue,
which is required during guess generation. In a nat-
ural game, this clue would come from a preceding
clue-generation turn. Because we train each turn as
an independent single-turn task, no such preceding
turn exists, so we must supply wclue in advance.
We therefore generate clue words automatically
through a systematic three-step process.
• Generate a Pool of Diverse Topics: We begin
by manually creating a seed list of 50 diverse
topics, such as ocean, food, hobbies etc. With
the help of Claude Opus, we then extend this
list to roughly 1,000 unique topics. Each topic
is a single whitespace-separable word that later
serves as the clue in guess generation training
examples.
• Find Words Associated with Topics: For each
topic, we use Claude Opus to generate a list of
directly associated words, which we stratify by
frequency into common and rare words. For ex-
ample, the topic ocean yields common words
such as tide, wave, and salt, and rare words
such as pelagic, benthic, and halocline.
We
store the top 10 common and 10 rare words
per topic in a repository R.
For any topic
t, R[t] = [W (t,c), W (t,r)], where W (t,c) and
W (t,r) are the common and rare words associ-
ated with t, and |W (t,c)| = |W (t,r)| = 10.
• Sample State Sets: To create a board position, we
sample two topics, one for the words in T and
one for the words in N. This lets us maintain a

coherent theme and a clear separation between
the words on the board, which keeps the task
tractable for LLMs, especially smaller ones. We
then draw the size of each set independently from
[2, 6] (inclusive) and sample that many words
from the corresponding topic. Varying the set
sizes lets us simulate the early and late game
states encountered in a long single-turn progres-
sion. Finally, the topic word for T serves as the
clue for the words in T . Following this process,
we sample the state sets (see Equation (1)) used
to train the LLMs.
Difficulty of States
When sampling the state sets,
we apply four rules of increasing difficulty. Each
rule changes only how the words in T are sampled,
while the rule for N stays fixed. Let tT and tN de-
note the topics sampled for the target and non-target
sets, and let nT ∼U(2, 6) and nN ∼U(2, 6) de-
note the number of words sampled for each set.
The nN non-target words are always drawn from
the common pool W (tN ,c). The four rules control
the target set as follows.
• Simple: all nT words are drawn from the com-
mon pool W (tT ,c).
• Moderate: ⌊nT /2⌋words are drawn from the
common pool W (tT ,c), and the rest from the
rare pool W (tT ,r).
• Advanced: all nT words are drawn from the
rare pool W (tT ,r).
• Expert: ⌊nT /2⌋words are drawn from the
rare pool W (tT ,r), and the rest from the com-
mon pool of the non-target topic, W (tN ,c).
We order the training data from the Simple rule to
the Expert rule, forming a natural curriculum for
the LLM. Intuitively, difficulty rises as target words
shift from common to rare, and the Expert rule adds
distractors that overlap with the non-target topic.
Moreover, in all but the last rule, Tselected = T and
gmax = |T |. In the case of the last rule, Tselected ⊂
T and gmax = ⌊nT /2⌋.

Reward Function
Clue Generation Task Reward
Let π be the
actor policy we train, which maps a state to an ac-
tion over the spaces defined in Equation (1) and
2 (π : S →A). To reward a generated clue, we
run the full clue-then-guess loop and score the re-
sulting guesses, which proceeds in three stages.
First, in the clue generation state S = {T , N},
the actor samples a clue for Tselected words condi-
tioned on the separated target and non-target sets,
wclue, Tselected ∼π(T , N). Second, we form the
shuffled board B = T ∪N from the same two
sets, pair it with the clue, and a bound for guesses
gmax = |Tselected| to obtain the guess generation

frozen judge policy ϕ that returns a set of guesses,
G ∼ϕ(B, wclue, gmax). Third, we compare the
guesses against the ground-truth sets T and N to
compute a scalar reward r = f(T , N, G).

Experimental Setup
4.1
Training
Models.
We train three models from the Qwen3
family: Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen3-8B
(Yang et al., 2025). For every model, we perform
full-parameter fine-tuning in full bf16 precision,
without parameter-efficient adapters or quantiza-
tion. All training is implemented with the VeRL
library (Sheng et al., 2025). For the judge model
ϕ(·), we use Google Gemini Flash-2.5, suitable for
large inference volume.
Training Algorithm and Hyperparameters.
We optimize the DAPO objective (Equation (6))
with the GRPO advantage estimator. For each
prompt, we sample a group of G = 32 responses,
and each optimization step uses a batch of 32
prompts, giving 32 × 32 = 1024 rollouts per step.
We train for 2 epochs over the 3,680 training ex-
amples, yielding 230 parameter update steps. We
use a constant learning rate of 1 × 10−6 with no
warmup or decay. Following DAPO, we decouple
clipping bounds with εl = 0.2 and εh = 0.28 (clip-
higher), aggregate the policy loss at the token level
(token-mean), and remove the KL term entirely,
using neither KL loss nor KL reward shaping.
Dynamic Sampling and Length Control.
We
adopt DAPO’s dynamic sampling: we oversample
and discard prompt groups whose responses all re-
ceive the same reward, since such groups yield zero
advantage and no learning signal, and we allow up
to 10 oversampling batches per step to refill the
effective batch. We cap the prompt length at 2, 048
tokens and the response length at 16, 384 tokens.
We also apply DAPO’s soft overlong reward shap-
ing with a 4, 096-token buffer. Responses longer
than 12,288 tokens incur a penalty that grows lin-
early with the overage and reaches its maximum
at the 16384-token cap. This discourages runaway
generations while avoiding hard truncation of oth-
erwise useful reasoning traces.
Infrastructure.
We run experiments on a hetero-
geneous pool of GPUs based on availability on the
Vast-AI platform. We train and evaluate Qwen3-
1.7B on a single A100, train Qwen3-4B on 4×RTX
5090 and 4×RTX Pro 6000 GPUs, and train and
evaluate Qwen3-8B on 4×RTX Pro 6000 GPUs.
Approximately, the training runs for 4B and 8B
models took 40-50 hours on 4× RTX Pro 6000
GPUs, and the 1.7B model took ≈25 hours on 4×
RTX 5090. In total, we spent approximately 3, 000
USD for training and evaluation experiments.
4.2
Evaluation
Creativity Evaluation
We evaluate on a broad
suite of creativity tasks. The suite spans divergent
and convergent thinking, figurative language and
humor understanding, creative problem solving,
the generation of long and coherent narratives, and
conceptual understanding. We group the tasks be-
low by the construct that each one targets, and for
each task, we describe its goal, a representative
instance, and the metric we report.
• Divergent thinking: AUT (Alternate Uses Test)


## Page 6


(Guilford, 1956) and DAT (Divergent Associa-
tion Test) (Olson et al., 2021) probe the ability to
produce many distinct ideas. In AUT, we prompt
the model to generate 5 creative and novel uses
for an everyday object, such as a belt or a brick.
We score the five uses for creativity and coher-
ence with an LLM judge, where higher is better.
In DAT, we prompt the model to list 10 words
that are as semantically dissimilar from one an-
other as possible, and we sample 100 such lists.
For each list, we compute Divergent Semantic In-
tegration (DSI) (Johnson et al., 2023) and report
the average over all 100 lists. We additionally
report vocabulary size, the fraction of unique
words across the full pool of 10 × 100 words.
DSI captures semantic diversity, and vocabulary
size captures lexical diversity; higher values are
better for both.
• Convergent thinking: RAT (Remote Association
Test) (Bowden and Jung-Beeman, 2003) evalu-
ates the complementary ability to converge on a
single associative link between distant concepts.
Each instance presents three remote cue words,
and the model must produce one word that asso-
ciates with all three. We report accuracy against
the reference answers.
• Figurative language and humor understanding:
MUNCH (Metaphor Understanding Challenge)
(Tong et al., 2024) evaluates whether a model
can interpret metaphorical language. It com-
prises three multiple-choice variants and one
open-ended generation variant. We report ac-
curacy for the multiple-choice variants and re-
call for the generation variant. The New Yorker
Caption Contest (Hessel et al., 2023) evaluates
humor understanding. Each instance provides a
description of a cartoon together with a caption.
In the explanation task, the model must explain
why the caption is funny, which we score with
an LLM judge. In the matching task, the model
selects the most suitable caption from several
options, which we score with accuracy.
• Creative problem solving: MacGyver (Tian et al.,
2024) presents an everyday problem together
with a limited set of available tools, and the
model must devise a creative solution that re-
spects the tool constraints. Following Tian et al.
(2024), we score solutions for feasibility and
efficiency with an LLM judge. NYT Connec-
tions (Lopez et al., 2025) requires partitioning
16 words into four themed groups of four. We
report group-level accuracy and the exact-match
rate over all four groups.
• Narrative generation: CWT (Creative Writing
Test) (Prabhakaran et al., 2014) and EQ-Bench
(Emotional Quotient Benchmark) (Paech, 2023)
target short- and long-form story writing, respec-
tively. In CWT, we give the model three words
and ask for a 5-sentence story that uses all three,
and we score the story for creativity and coher-
ence with an LLM judge. In addition, we report
DSI (semantic diversity) and MTLD (Measure
of Textual Lexical Diversity) for the generated
stories. In EQ-Bench, the model receives a topic
and a narrative outline that specifies required
details, for example “Describe the gladiator’s
weapon of choice and its significance to them”,
and must produce a 1,000-word story. We use
LLM-Judge to measure creativity and report the
final score on a 0-100 scale, where higher is bet-
ter.
• Conceptual grounding: Because grounded con-
cept representations underpin both near and far
associations, we test whether associative train-
ing strengthens them using the LLM-Grounding
dataset of Xu et al. (2025). Each instance asks
the model to rate the association between a word
and a conceptual axis on an integer scale. To
bound the evaluation cost, we subsample 1,000
examples from the original dataset, balanced
across concepts and human ratings. We com-
pute Spearman’s correlation between the model’s
integer ratings and the mean human ratings,
where higher values indicate stronger conceptual
grounding.
Reasoning Evaluation
To anchor our creativity
findings against well-established ability, we eval-
uate the same checkpoints on a standard reason-
ing suite covering arithmetic, competition mathe-
matics, graduate-level science, and multi-step nar-
rative reasoning. All tasks are run through the
lighteval (Habib et al., 2023) library with the
original task formulations and metrics, and are
evaluated 0-shot. GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021)
comprises grade-school word problems requiring
multi-step arithmetic, scored by extractive-match
accuracy. AIME 2024 and AIME 2025 (Patel et al.,
2024) are olympiad mathematics problems with
a single integer answer, also scored by extractive
match. GPQA-Diamond (Rein et al., 2023) is a
four-way multiple-choice benchmark of Google-
proof graduate-level questions in physics, chem-
istry, and biology, scored by accuracy.

5.2
Reasoning Evaluation
The reasoning results in Table 2 mirror the creativ-
ity picture in reverse: the gains concentrate in the
smaller models. Qwen3-1.7B improves on all four,
raising AIME24 accuracy from 0.43 to 0.57 and
GPQA-Diamond by 5.6 points (0.414 →0.470).

decrement on GSM8K (0.9431 →0.9416). The
8B model moves in the opposite direction: it
holds steady on AIME25 and GSM8K but drops
by roughly 2 points on GPQA and 13 points on
AIME24 (0.80 →0.67).
5.3
Synthesis: Associative Learning Meets
RLVR
Taken together, the creativity and reasoning results
reveal a coherent pattern shaped by the interaction
of associative learning (Codenames) with RLVR
(DAPO). RLVR is known to carry a diversity-
precision trade-off (Wu et al., 2025), and we ob-
serve exactly this, except its direction flips with
scale. The 8B model shifts toward diversity, gain-
ing creativity at a slight cost to math and science
precision, while the smaller models shift the other
way, gaining robustly on precision-bound reason-
ing without consistently gaining creativity. We
speculate that associative learning sets this split
(Johnson et al., 2025): easy problems are solvable
by association alone, so smaller models earn reward
by precisely solving the problems now within reach,
while larger models, already solving these, pursue
diversity for the harder ones. A length test on the
1.7B responses to the reasoning dataset supports
this. Its traces lengthen overall (ratio 1.42) but
stay shorter on problems it newly solves (1.13) and
fall below the base model on AIME (< 1.0), con-
verging faster on likely easy problems. Together,
these results point to a method that pays off at ev-
ery scale. One associative-RLVR recipe, with no
task-specific tuning, delivers substantial reasoning
gains in smaller models and consistent creativity
gains in larger ones. The scale-dependent trade-off
is a key property that we highlight with our results:
each model gains where it can.
6
Conclusion
We set out to train creativity without human judg-
ment, and found that a single game, Codenames,
reshapes what a model improves at depending on
its size. The same associative reward sharpens rea-
soning in smaller models and broadens creativity in
the largest, exposing a scale-dependent precision-
diversity trade-off rather than a uniform win. Con-
cretely, fine-tuning improved the 8B model across
the large majority of creativity metrics. The 1.7B
and 4B models instead showed strong improve-
ments in math and science reasoning. A single,

verifiable word game thus provides a practical and
scalable lever for strengthening LLMs.
Limitations
Our work has several limitations. First, we sim-
plify Codenames by removing the neutral and as-
sassin words and by decomposing multi-turn play
into single-turn tasks, which may omit dynamics
present in the full game.
Training with fewer
constraints to satisfy may also explain the con-
straint failures we observed on NYT Connections,
where the tuned model violated task rules it was
never pressured to respect. Second, our training
combines a chain-of-thought scaffold with generic
RLVR, and, primarily due to experimental cost,
we do not run an ablation that isolates the associa-
tive component; part of the observed effect may
therefore stem from RLVR or the reasoning format
alone. Third, we include no representational probe,
which could clarify how distant concepts interact
internally and how that interaction shapes the fi-
nal generations. Fourth, many creativity metrics
rely on an LLM judge, and the clue-generation re-
ward itself depends on a frozen judge model, so
judge bias may influence both training and eval-
uation. Finally, several reasoning results rest on
small test sets, notably AIME with its 30 problems,
where a shift of only a few items changes accu-
racy substantially; these deltas should be read with
corresponding caution.
Ethics Statement
Our work focuses on training language models
for creative association, improving the diversity
of outputs in open-ended tasks. As our results
show, creative diversity can trade off against pre-
cision, so we caution against using our models
or data where factual accuracy is critical, such as
medical advice, legal reasoning, or scientific fact-
checking. Our training data consists of topics and
word lists generated synthetically with a large lan-
guage model and contains no personal, sensitive,
or human-subjects data, and all evaluation bench-
marks are publicly available and used in accor-
dance with their licenses. Because several rewards
and creativity metrics rely on LLM judges, which
can encode cultural and linguistic biases, and be-
cause our tasks are English-centric, our results may
not generalize across languages or contexts. A key
motivation behind our approach is accessibility: by
relying on verifiable rewards rather than human
preference annotation, using a small training set,
and showing gains on models as small as 1.7B,
we aim to lower the resource barrier for creativ-
ity research. Finally, while we used proprietary
language models (Claude Opus) to assist in experi-
mental setup, data curation, and manuscript editing,
no portion was generated entirely by an LLM; all
content was written, reviewed, and edited by the
authors.
