Methods that rely on proxies, without imposing strong parametric struc-
ture, are increasingly used to deal with unobserved variables in causal infer-
ence. One influential line of this work reconstructs latent distributions used to
identify the target functional by exploiting eigenvalue-eigenvector structure.
Within this framework, we first establish identification of the full-data law in
the presence of hidden outcomes, and then develop influence function–based
estimators for causal effects. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first
work to develop influence function–based estimators in this setting without
relying on unbiased proxy measurements or partial observation, while achiev-
ing multiple robustness and desirable efficiency properties. We demonstrate
the performance of our approach through simulation studies.
1. Introduction.
Hidden variables remain a central challenge in causal inference, often
preventing identification and estimation of causal targets of interest. Proxy-based approaches
have recently emerged as a way to circumvent such hidden structure without relying on strong
parametric assumptions [1, 9, 17, 18, 28]. A prominent line of this work achieves identifi-
cation by reconstructing latent distributions from observed data under relatively mild condi-
tions, exploiting eigenvalue–eigenvector structure [1, 17, 28].
These methods are often described as “nonparametric,” in the sense that their assumptions
are sufficiently weak; under relative support size/dimensionality conditions on the variables,
distributions that violate them can be approximated arbitrarily closely by distributions that
satisfy them [2, 4]. Conceptually, this line of work builds on ideas dating back to Kruskal’s
uniqueness theorem for the Candecomp/Parafac (CP) decomposition [16], which character-
izes when a three-way array admits a unique representation as a sum of rank-one compo-
nents. This uniqueness result allows recovery of the joint distribution of hidden and observed
variables by ensuring that the observed joint distribution (represented as a three-way array)
admits a unique factorization.
Using this framework, we establish identification of the full-data law of hidden and ob-
served variables in the presence of proxies for hidden outcomes and develop an influence
function-based approach for estimating causal effects. Our estimators do not require unbi-
ased proxy measurements, are √n-consistent under standard regularity conditions, and ex-
hibit multiple robustness, in the sense that consistency of estimation is preserved when certain
nuisance functions are correctly specified, even if others are misspecified.
Substantial literature studies causal identification under missing outcomes or bounded
measurement error [3, 6, 7, 12, 21, 27], and recent work has considered proxy-based meth-
ods for hidden outcomes under the presence of at least one unbiased proxy [8]. To the best of
our knowledge, our work is the first to establish semiparametric estimation results for hidden
outcomes that do not rely on unbiased proxies or partial observations of the true outcome.
We assess the performance of our proposed estimators through simulation studies on discrete
and mixed data generating processes.

2. Background.
2.1. Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs).
We use a causal directed acyclic graph (DAG) as
a convenient representation of a setting consistent with our assumptions (Figure 1). DAGs
encode a statistical model; nodes correspond to random variables, and edges encode condi-
tional independence relations, with all implied independences determined via d-separation.
Additionally, causal DAGs provide a transparent way to encode assumptions about the direc-
tion of causal relationships and the conditions under which causal effects can be identified,
i.e., expressed as a function of the observed-data law. [19, 20, 23, 25].

2.3. Related Work.
Our work builds on classic results such as Kruskal’s uniqueness the-
orem for the Candecomp/Parafac (CP) decomposition [16] and subsequent developments
[1, 17], which represent the joint distribution of observed variables as a multi-way array. Un-
der conditional independence of the observed variables given latent variable(s), together with
appropriate rank conditions, this array admits a unique factorization that recovers the full-data
law. The underlying argument hinges on unique eigenspaces [22]; obtaining the full-data law
from the observed-data law can often be achieved via a sequence of eigendecomposition steps
[1, 17], or alternatively through iterative search procedures (e.g., alternating least squares,
gradient descent) [15, 26].
Continuous analogues replace these finite-dimensional decompositions with operator-
based formulations, relying on completeness conditions characterizing the variability of
conditional distributions. This perspective was developed by Hu and Schennach [11] and
extended to causal inference settings with observed variables associated with (“proxies”
for) hidden confounders in Deaner [5], as well as to settings with similar proxies for hid-
den treatments in Zhou and Tchetgen Tchetgen [28]. Obtaining the full-data law from the
observed-data law can be achieved through sieve-based or expectation-maximization ap-
proaches [11, 28].
We build on the ideas from Hu and Schennach [11], Deaner [5], and Zhou and Tchetgen
Tchetgen [28] by adapting such identifying assumptions to settings with analogous proxies
for hidden outcomes. In addition, we develop influence function–based estimators for the
resulting causal effects, yielding estimators that are multiply robust and √n-consistent under
standard regularity conditions.

6. Conclusion.
In this work, we study causal inference in the presence of hidden vari-
ables through a proxy-based lens. Leveraging mild structural assumptions, we show that it is
possible to recover the full joint law over latent and observed variables from observed data
alone. Building on this identification strategy, we develop an influence function–based ap-
proach for estimating causal effects, yielding estimators that are √n-consistent and exhibit
multiple robustness under standard regularity conditions. Notably, our framework avoids re-
liance on unbiased proxies or strong parametric restrictions.
A number of key direction remain to be explored. While our framework assumes three
proxies, many applications feature multiple candidate proxies. Understanding how to op-
timally combine information across these proxies for efficient estimation remains an open
problem. Second, our estimation strategy relies on achieving product bias convergence rates
for nuisance functions; developing principled modeling strategies and learning procedures
that reliably attain these rates in practice is an important challenge, particularly in high-
dimensional or nonparametric settings. Finally, our results hint at a more general theory
for influence functions under proxy-based full law identification, including systematic con-
struction of influence function-based estimators across a broad class of target functionals
