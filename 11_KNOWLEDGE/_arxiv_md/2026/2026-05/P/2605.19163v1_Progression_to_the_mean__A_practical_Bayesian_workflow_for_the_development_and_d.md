Progression to the mean:
A practical Bayesian workflow for the development and deployment
of clinical prediction models
Mohsen Sadatsafavi, Richard D. Riley
2026-05-17
Abstract
Clinical prediction models provide a prediction (e.g., estimated risk) for each individual, typically
expressed as a point estimate derived from a deterministic function such as a logistic regression equation.
Such ‘plug-in’ predictions hide inherent uncertainty.
In contrast, Bayesian methods offer a coherent
mechanism for uncertainty quantification based on an individual-specific posterior distribution of risk.
However, Bayesian prediction models are underutilised, due to perceived subjectivity, computational
cost, and implementation complexity. To address this, we propose a pragmatic Bayesian pipeline for
producing and deploying prediction models. The main components are (i) shrinkage priors leading to
posterior distributions of regression coeﬀicients based on a Laplace/normal approximation, which avoids
Monte Carlo sampling; and (ii) using an individual’s posterior mean for decision-making, justified by
an expected utility perspective.
For (i), we suggest priors with complementary features (simplicity,
user input, automatic shrinkage). For (ii), we suggest exact and approximate methods for computing
the posterior mean, including quadrature, MacKay’s approximation, and an adaptation of projection-
predictive mapping that creates a simple logistic equation approximating the mean. Using examples
and simulations, we demonstrate the Bayesian workflow often matches or exceeds predictive performance
compared with plug-in predictions, while enabling uncertainty quantification with suitable coverage. In
the majority of simulations, using the posterior mean predictions resulted in higher clinical utility, at times
substantial, compared with plug-in predictions. In summary, a Bayesian approach to clinical prediction
modelling and deployment is both pragmatic and clinically advantageous, so is highly recommended.
Keywords: Bayesian inference; Clinical prediction models; Uncertainty quantification; Prognosis; Predictive
value of tests

Background
The prevailing practice in predictive analytics for healthcare is to derive and present a prediction model as
a deterministic function, resulting in a single predicted value for an individual. An example is a prediction
equation (algorithm) corresponding to a fitted regression model, such as a logistic regression equation that
provides a single point estimate of an individual’s outcome probability (risk) conditional on a function of
multiple predictors. These point estimates are often based on the maximum likelihood estimates (MLE) of
regression coeﬀicients, perhaps after applying some form of shrinkage, such as a logistic regression equation
estimated using (penalised) MLE. Focusing solely on such point estimates obscures the reality that model-
based predictions are inherently uncertain, as models are developed using finite samples[1, 2]. Beyond such
epistemic uncertainty, additional sources of uncertainty may arise when models are deployed, especially due
to differences between development and target populations, as well as variations in predictor measurement
and outcome ascertainment[3, 4].
There has been a call for reporting uncertainty around predicted values at the time of deploying a clinical
prediction model[5]. It is argued that model developers have an ethical responsibility towards quantifying
strength of evidence behind a model’s predictions, including uncertainty quantification at point of care, which
can in turn help with critical appraisal of a model, contribute towards assessments of model fairness, and
facilitate shared decision-making. Reporting and communicating prediction uncertainty, however, requires
changes in current practices. For example, when the model is based on multivariable regression, the uncer-
tainty around regression parameter estimates (e.g., the covariance matrix) could be deployed alongside the
prediction equation, allowing point estimate predictions and additional computations to quantify prediction
uncertainty for an individual.
Such a change in practice opens a unique window of opportunity for adopting Bayesian thinking in pre-
diction modelling. One advantage of such Bayesianism is in risk communication[6]. When communicating
uncertainty, Bayesian credible intervals (CrI) are better aligned with how individuals naturally interpret
uncertainty. Unlike confidence intervals (CIs) which describe the outcomes of long-run hypothetical exper-
iments, CrIs directly address the patient’s central question: “What is the outcome risk in people like me,
and how certain is the model about this risk?”. Evidence indicates that both lay people and clinicians often
misinterpret frequentist CIs as such direct probability statements[7, 8].
Even beyond interpretability advantages, a Bayesian framework arguably provides the most principled foun-
dation for risk prediction modelling, which leads to a posterior distribution for an individual’s prediction
(risk). We perceive a lack of debate about the merits of, and approaches for, a systematic Bayesian thinking
in the context of clinical prediction modelling. Some of the inertia about Bayesian prediction modelling
in healthcare might be due to the impression that Bayesian inference involves subjective priors, requires
complex Monte Carlo sampling techniques, demands specialised skills in model specification and diagnostics,
and poses diﬀiculties in model deployment, where a “model” is a large set of posterior draws. These are
understandable trepidations for a field that values objectivity, reproducibility, transparency, and usability.
The aim of this article is to propose a pragmatic Bayesian workflow for clinical prediction modelling. We do
not claim novelty in the application of Bayesian methods in clinical prediction[9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14], nor do
we intend to rehash the extensive literature on the Bayesian predictive inference[15]. Instead, our proposal is
a widely applicable general framework that encompasses the main principles of Bayesianism while remaining
pragmatic in terms of the required machinery, making it more immediately applicable to statisticians, data
scientists, and other healthcare researchers developing and deploying prediction models.
The outline of this paper is as follows. We begin with a motivating example, which runs throughout the
article to demonstrate the key concepts. Then, we briefly review key frequentist and Bayesian approaches for
predictive inference. We recap the central role of posterior mean of outcome probability from an expected
utility perspective. We then discuss a pragmatic Bayesian workflow which involves assuming multivariate
normality of the posterior density of model parameters, specifying shrinkage priors that enable Laplace
approximation of the posterior, and various approaches for deployment-time computation of the posterior
mean. We run simulation studies that show this approach is feasible, and offers performance at least compa-
rable with conventional MLE-based point predictions, while enabling coherent uncertainty propagation. We
conclude by highlighting some open questions and areas for future research.

The frequentist framework
In conventional practice, prediction models are presented using a single set of coeﬀicients ̂𝛽. Because most

intercept and predictor effects):

for individuals.
We refer to these predictions as plug-in estimates.

regression model, an inverse-logit equation provides the deterministic prediction function for an individual’s

Such overfitting is typically manifested as a calibration slope (𝑏in the model logit(𝑃(𝑌= 1)) = 𝑎+𝑏logit(𝜋)))

Because of such overfitting concerns, in certain situations, some form of penalisation of the likelihood is
recommended. These penalisations introduce an intentional shrinkage bias in the choice of

to reduce overfitting. Simple, tuning-free penalisations include the Firth correction (discussed below). More
commonly, hierarchical shrinkage methods such as ridge or LASSO are considered, where a global tuning
parameter determines the strength of penalisation. This parameter is commonly estimated from the same
development data via cross-validation. This makes standard covariance-based inference on the coeﬀicients
invalid, because the tuning parameter is considered fixed when it is itself a function of the data. Commonly,
in prediction models based on ridge or LASSO, uncertainty is characterised via bootstrapping.
This is
computationally intensive, introduces Monte Carlo noise, and might result in convergence issues with smaller
datasets. For LASSO, some coeﬀicients might shrink to zero in some iterations, which causes challenges as
to how to summarize the zero-inflated empirical distribution of 𝛽s. Recent developments have specialised
post-shrinkage inference techniques[24]. But these approaches target coverage-correct confidence intervals on
regression coeﬀicients, not on individual-level predicted probabilities, which are what clinical decision-making
requires.
Regardless of whether unpenalised or penalised estimation is used, the frequentist approach is typically used
to make point estimate predictions, determined based on the linear combination of MLEs of the parameter
estimates. For example, in Table 2, using the MLE estimates, and applying it to our patient Sam, we will
compute the logit-probability to be -3.0095 which evaluates to a predicted probability of 0.0470.
2.3
The Bayesian framework
In the Bayesian approach, regression coeﬀicients are treated as random variables, and the posterior distri-
bution given the data is obtained via Bayes’ rule: we place a prior on 𝛽, 𝑝(𝛽), that is combined with the
likelihood to estimate the posterior probability 𝑝(𝛽|𝐷):
𝑝(𝛽|𝐷) ∝𝑝(𝛽)𝑝(𝐷|𝛽).
Therefore, in a regression framework, one extra step in the Bayesian thinking is the specification of a prior
on regression coeﬀicients such as intercepts and predictor effects.
Indeed, placing an improper flat prior (𝑝(𝛽) ∝1) on coeﬀicients provides a direct Bayesian interpretation
of the frequentist estimation of the likelihood. In this case, the posterior is proportional to the likelihood,
and the multivariate normal approximation of the likelihood can be seen as a Laplace approximation of
the posterior distribution[15, section 4.1]. This means the MLE estimates of regression coeﬀicients become
maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimates under the Bayesian framework, and Wald-type CIs can be interpreted
as approximate CrIs.
For the more general case, if the prior and the likelihood are conjugate, the posterior distribution can be
expressed in closed form and inference can be analytically tractable. In many instances, conjugacy is not
available. General-purpose Bayesian inference platforms that offer flexibility in the choice of the prior often
perform inference using MCMC methods, resulting in draws from 𝑝(𝛽|𝐷). Alternatively, one can place an
improper prior on the data-generating mechanism itself, giving rise to the Bayesian bootstrap[25]. In this
approach, we obtain a posterior distribution over the population from which the sample was drawn, which
then propagates to the parameters (e.g., regression coeﬀicients).

From a given posterior distribution of regression coeﬀicients, two distinct predictions can be derived. The
plug-in estimate, 𝜋(x; ̂𝛽), uses only the MAP value for each parameter and ignores uncertainty; this coincides
conceptually with the prediction one would obtain in a frequentist framework. We note that under a Laplace
approximation,
̂𝛽is the posterior mean, median, and mode of 𝑝(𝛽|𝐷). However, due to the monotonic but non-

𝔼[𝜋(x; 𝛽)], integrates over the full posterior of 𝛽, incorporating parameter uncertainty into the prediction.
For the remainder of this paper, we refer to these two types of predictions as plug-in point estimates (PE),
based on the deterministic function of

posterior distribution of predicted probabilities. Under standard regularity conditions, different summaries
of the posterior distribution (e.g., mean, median, and mode) converge to the same estimand asymptotically.
However, their finite-sample behaviour can differ.

Posterior mean as the central quantity for clinical decision-making
In Bayesian inference, the most common measure of centrality is the posterior mean:

connected to statistical inference. Likelihood-based methods, whether frequentist or Bayesian, can be inter-
preted through the lens of minimising information loss (in terms of Kullback–Leibler divergence) between
the candidate model and the true data-generating process[26]. Such information-theoretic concepts become
relevant when we approximate the posterior mean, and investigate the coverage of the posterior distribution.

3
A pragmatic Bayesian pipeline to enable posterior mean predic-
tions and uncertainty quantification
Building on the foundations detailed in the previous sections, we now propose a pragmatic Bayesian pipeline
that emphasises objectivity, reproducibility, and ease of implementation. This framework consists of two
core components. First, we utilise priors that enable Laplace approximation of the posterior distribution
without the need for Monte Carlo approaches (MCMC sampling or bootstrapping). Second, we encourage
the use of posterior mean as the measure of centrality for informing individual decision-making, and provide
a suite of tools ranging from precise and approximate methods for its computation.
3.1
‘Practical’ priors that enable Laplace approximation of the posterior distri-
bution
In the Bayesian approach, priors can carry external information about coeﬀicients (e.g., from previous
studies), and the transfer of such information is a major strength of the Bayesian framework. While the
Bayesian analysis should be used to transfer predictive information across populations, here we focus on
learning entirely from a single population.
In line with our pragmatic pipeline, we restrict attention to
priors that enable a Laplace / normal approximation of the posterior.
This enables packaging a model
for deployment as a vector of point estimate and a covariance matrix that specify a multivariate normal
distribution for regression coeﬀicients. Without claiming exhaustiveness, we consider four priors that span
a range of shrinkage strategies: an improper flat prior (no shrinkage), the Jeffreys prior (fixed, tuning-free
shrinkage), the log-F prior (user-specified shrinkage), and a hierarchical Gaussian prior (Bayesian ridge;
data-driven shrinkage).
3.1.1
Flat prior and the inherent shrinkage properties of the posterior mean
An improper flat prior on coeﬀicients enables a direct interpretation of the unpenalised regression in a
Bayesian way.
In large sample sizes, where the posterior is dominated by the likelihood, this approach
provides a convenient Bayesian interpretation of unpenalised regression. While the flat prior does not intro-
duce any shrinkage, predictions based on the posterior mean would often show some shrinkage behaviour
compared to using the plug-in values of regression coeﬀicients. Here we apply the word shrinkage loosely to
invoke the notion that the posterior mean generally results in lower variation of predicted probabilities. This
arises from the fact that the posterior mean is the average of predictions across the ‘model space’, defined
by 𝑝(𝛽|𝐷). Optimistic predictions are moderated by contributions from regions of the parameter space with
poorer fit to the data. For the logit link function, this can be more formally approached as an application of
Jensen’s inequality: the non-linear link function means 𝔼[𝜋(x, 𝛽)] ≥𝜋(x, 𝔼[𝛽]) for an individual with 𝜂< 0,
and 𝔼[𝜋(x, 𝛽)] ≤𝜋(x, 𝔼[𝛽]) for an individual with 𝜂> 0. Thus, compared to the plug-in prediction, the
posterior mean is shifted towards 𝑝= 0.5 (𝜂= 0), with more extreme values shifted more aggressively.
The plug-in prediction, due to the score equation for the intercept in unpenalised logistic regression, guar-
antees that O/E=1 in the sample. This property is extended to the frequentist ridge and LASSO as, by
default, these methods do not shrink the intercept. Compared with the plug-in prediction, the posterior
mean strikes a balance between two calibration properties. It trades exact O/E preservation for improved
individual-level calibration, a trade-off we view as appropriate when the accuracy of individual predictions
is concerned. These will be systematically investigated in our simulation studies in Section 4.

3.3
Further considerations for summarising the posterior distribution
A key appeal of the Bayesian framework is the intuitive presentation and interpretation of prediction un-
certainty. Posterior distributions are generally accessible to clinicians and patients with minimal training,
and the entire distribution can be visualised to communicate uncertainty[41]. In our framework, this would
involve a direct visualisation of the probability density of the linear predictor transformed via the inverse
link function (e.g., the logit-normal distribution for logistic regression). More commonly, credible intervals
are communicated, which can be intuitively interpreted as the patient-specific range containing the outcome
probability with a certain coverage (often 95%). We recommend the standard approach for summarising
the posterior distribution: the posterior mean as a measure of centrality, coupled with tail-based intervals
(e.g., the 2.5th to 97.5th percentiles for 95% coverage). Given the monotonic link function, the percentiles of
predictions correspond directly to the percentiles of 𝜂, with the latter being derived from the tail probabilities
of the normal distribution.
This approach is practical and aligned with typical Bayesian reporting (including the typical summarisation of
draws from posterior distributions in MCMC-based sampling); however, a few alternatives deserve mention.
One could construct intervals based on the highest density regions of the posterior distribution, which
generally yield the shortest possible width. Further, while the posterior mean can theoretically fall outside
tail-based boundaries, this should be very rare unless the SD of 𝜂is implausibly large. Regardless, if so
desired, one could create an interval geometrically centered on the posterior mean. However, both highest-
density and mean-centered intervals require deployment-time numerical methods and lack the standard status
of tail-based intervals. Therefore, we do not prioritise them in our pipeline and do not investigate them in
this study.

Simulation studies
To examine the proposed Bayesian pipeline outlined in Section 3, we now conduct proof-of-concept simulation
studies to examine various aspects of the proposed pipeline. The purpose of these simulations is not to
prove the merits of Bayesian predictions in general, which are well established and extensively studied.
These simulations are meant to test the key steps of the proposed workflow: the regularising effect of
the proposed priors, normal approximation of the posterior distribution, various deployment-time posterior
mean computations, and the coverage of the ensuing posterior distributions. The simulation design generally
follows the ADEMP framework [42].
We compared the predictions based on plug-in estimates versus the posterior mean using flat, Jeffreys,
log-F(2,2), and Gaussian priors.
Although the frequentist ridge and LASSO do not enable uncertainty
quantification for predicted probabilities and thus are not eligible approaches, we included them for a general
comparison given their popularity in conventional workflows. The point estimates from both were based on
the tuning parameter determined via cross-validation, minimising deviance (preliminary studies indicated
that alternative approaches, including minimising prediction error or choosing the tuning parameter based
on 1SE rule resulted in worse performance).
Two sets of simulations were conducted: (i) using the full GUSTO dataset, and (ii) using fully synthetic
data. The former is a ‘real-world’ example where we need not make any assumptions on the data-generation
mechanism, while the latter provides opportunities for changing major features of the development sample
such as outcome prevalence, as well as studying the coverage of the posterior distribution (as we know the
true outcome probabilities). We compared the performance of competing approaches in terms of prediction
error, discrimination, calibration, and standardised incremental NB (sNB, equal to the difference between
the NB of the model and the best default strategy, divided by outcome prevalence in the sample). Results
are summarised as mean ± SD for all metrics, except for calibration metrics (O/E ratio and calibration
slope), where median with IQR was reported - as these metrics can take large values and their distribution
might not have an expected value.
4.1
Simulations using GUSTO data
Within each simulation, we split the full GUSTO data (n=40,830) into the training and test sets. We fitted
models using various methods and compared their performance in the test sets. This data-splitting exercise
gives us the ability to examine model performance without knowing the true response-generating mechanism.
We chose three sample sizes: 500, 1000, and 5000. The expected number of events are 34.9, 69.8, and 349.1.
The sNBs in this simulation were assessed at 2%, 5%, and 10% thresholds. Each simulation was run for
2,500 iterations. This value ensures that the Monte Carlo SE for any probability estimate is ≤0.01, and
generally provided trivial Monte Carlo SE against variability across scenarios.
Results based on different priors (and exact posterior mean computation) are provided in Figure 2. An

predictions, as well as frequentist ridge and LASSO resulted in O/E~1 (as expected). The plug-in predictions
based on flat prior had a calibration slope far away from the ideal value of 1. The posterior mean nudges

In terms of clinical utility, the posterior mean outperformed the plug-in predictions across all scenarios. At

Evaluation of the coverage of the posterior distribution of outcome probability

model predictions. As our simulations cover both misspecified and correctly specified candidate models,
the true value (estimand) for each individual’s predicted probability should be carefully chosen.
Under
correct specification, 𝛽s converge to their true values, and the posterior distribution of outcome probability

candidate predictors using the entire simulated population.

Discussion
We proposed a Bayesian pipeline for developing and deploying prediction models that results in coherent
uncertainty propagation from the development sample to the point of care, whilst also focusing on the benefit
18


## Page 19


of using the posterior mean for predictions, rather than the current approach of making point predictions
based on plug-in (MLE) estimates of coeﬀicients. We placed a large emphasis on pragmatism, proposing
an approach that requires only minor modifications of commonly used analytical tools, while remaining
reproducible and computationally tractable. This pragmatism rests on two main features: the use of priors
that enable Laplace approximation of the posterior, resulting in a model being packaged as a vector of point
estimates and a covariance matrix for regression coeﬀicients; and, the proposal of algorithms for deployment-
time computation of posterior mean as the principled measure of centrality. As part of the latter, we proposed
a two-step regression approach based on KL divergence minimisation that results in a simple logistic equation
approximating the posterior mean.
One could classify the first component of our proposal - the use of shrinkage priors - as a form of likelihood
penalisation, without invoking Bayes. This characterisation, while technically correct, misses three essential
features of Bayesian thinking. First, whereas uncertainty under the frequentist view refers to the properties
of repeated experiments, a Bayesian view provides direct probabilistic statements about the unknown truth
- the information the patient and care provider seek.
Second, while our focus was on single-population
learning, Bayesian thinking provides a solid bedrock for incorporating external knowledge. For example, the
posterior distribution from previous development studies naturally becomes the prior for the new analysis,
ensuring optimal learning and coherent uncertainty propagation. Finally, the expected utility framework
provides a principled justification for the posterior mean (a Bayesian quantity) as the optimal metric for
decision-making. It is not incidental that in our simulations, posterior mean mostly outperformed the plug-in
estimator in terms of NB (with a degree of gain depending on where the threshold is placed), as NB builds
directly on an expected utility framework.
Of course, the optimality of the posterior mean is guaranteed only under correct model specification. When
the model is misspecified, alternative summaries may ‘happen to’ mitigate the bias due to misspecification.
However, we do not know the direction of misspecification error in advance (otherwise we would have updated
the model specification accordingly), so a blind move away from the mean in the hope of landing somewhere
better is not a grounded strategy. A view towards maximising clinical utility would thus commit to an earnest
effort in correct model specification, followed by the use of posterior mean for clinical decision-making. For
Sam Doe, in some scenarios (e.g., with Jeffreys and log-F priors) the plug-in and posterior mean predictions
straddled the plausible 5% threshold. Even occasional changes in the decision easily offset the trivial costs
of computing the posterior mean. Indeed, in several simulated scenarios, such changes gave rise to gains
in clinical utility that were non-trivial fractions of the gain expected by doubling the development sample
size. Aligning the deployed model predictions with decision theory can be an inexpensive way of improving
eﬀiciency!
We presented the core developments around binary outcomes to keep this paper focused, but this framework
is broadly applicable to other outcome types.
One can package the output of any GLM as a vector of
means, a covariance matrix, and a link function. Similar principles apply to other modelling approaches
such as survival models. For proportional hazards (Cox) models, a technicality arises in that the partial
likelihood does not include the baseline hazard. However, one may augment the parameter space to include
the cumulative baseline hazard at the time point of interest. Jeffreys prior is implementable for any model
with a computable Fisher information matrix, the log-F prior can be implemented via data augmentation
for categorical and survival models[35], and the mgcv implementation of the Bayesian ridge applies to all
GLMs as well as to proportional hazards models[45, 36]. In all these approaches, Gauss-Hermite quadrature
applies under the approximate normality of the linear predictor. Our proposed two-step regression is generic
and can approximate the posterior mean from any of these modelling strategies.
Several methodological extensions warrant further investigation. The choice of priors and deployment-time
computation methods for various outcome types needs to be investigated. The Laplace approximation can
be further refined, via approaches such as the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA)[46]. We
explored INLA using R-INLA[47], but this implementation reverted to the ordinary Laplace approxima-
tion when a multivariate normal approximation of the posterior was requested. Variational inference is an
information-theoretic alternative that approximates the posterior with a parametric family by minimising
KL divergence[48]. This is conceptually attractive as it is more aligned with the information-theoretic un-
derpinnings of statistical inference, though accessible implementations remain limited. Model simplification
19


## Page 20


and variable selection in a pragmatic Bayesian framework also warrant investigation.
In the frequentist
paradigm, shrinkage methods such as LASSO provide a data-driven means for variable selection. Variable
selection within a Bayesian framework can be performed by projecting the reference model onto a smaller
predictor space[49]. Projection predictive mapping has primarily been developed in the context of MCMC-
based approaches, and its extension to a fully parametric pipeline should be pursued. Our framework could
in principle extend to black-box models such as neural networks, provided their predictive uncertainty can
be represented through an appropriate parametric distribution. Uncertainty quantification without loss of
predictive power for such models, however, might demand alternative approaches beyond the scope of this
work[50]. Further, a Bayesian view enables the extension of decision-theoretic approaches to uncertainty
assessment, referred to as value-of-information analysis[51], to the point of care, for example to decide for
which patients prediction uncertainty is large enough to demand a more definitive test. Crucially, we view
model transportability as a particularly promising area for Bayesian approaches, with potential to improve
the eﬀiciency and coherence of current practices.
We have summarised our specific recommendations in Table 4. Our last recommendation might deserve
further reflection. Adopting a Bayesian framework requires rethinking what counts as ‘good’ performance.
Frequentist estimation has long privileged unbiasedness of model performance estimates, such as calibration
and discrimination. An example is the O/E ratio, which is guaranteed to be equal to 1 within the development
sample when fitting a logistic regression using (penalised or unpenalised) MLE. However, this might come
at the cost of overfitting and extreme predictions, higher prediction error, worse KL divergence, and lower
clinical utility. In contrast, Bayesian decision theory constructs estimators targeting explicit loss functions.
It is a well-known result that with a proper prior distribution, the posterior mean is generally biased in
finite samples[52; 53, p244; 15, p94]; yet it emerges as the optimal metric with respect to expected quadratic
prediction error, KL divergence, and, critically for risk prediction, clinical utility. This line of reasoning
indicates that under a Bayesian paradigm, model assessment should prioritise eﬀicient learning under relevant
objective functions, rather than the finite-sample bias of a given summary statistic.
Clinical prediction models are, by their nature, about the transfer of knowledge - from a development sample
to the bedside, and from one population to another. At the bedside, two important questions arise: how
uncertain evidence should be communicated, and how it should inform medical decisions. The Bayesian
approach provides coherent answers to both. While adopting this view implies a paradigm shift in how
we conceptualise, present, and deploy prediction models, the corresponding analytical updates - specifying
pragmatic proper prior distributions and using the posterior mean for decision making - need not be a
revolution. We have shown how the core benefits are available with minor tweaks to the current machinery
of risk prediction.
