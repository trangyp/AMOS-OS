Abstract
The classic concept of “calibrated forecasts” and its more recent refinement,
“calibeating,” are defined with respect to the standard quadratic scoring rule. We
extend these notions to the class of proper scoring rules (for which the best forecast
is the true distribution) and define proper-calibration and proper-calibeating by re-
quiring the errors to converge to zero uniformly over all bounded proper scoring
rules. We first establish that calibration always implies proper-calibration, whereas
calibeating need not imply proper-calibeating. Second, we show how to guaran-
tee proper-calibeating and proper-multicalibeating. Finally, we demonstrate the
equivalence between proper-calibration and universal no regret when best replying
to forecasts in decision-making under uncertainty.

Introduction
Forecasting the probability of future events is a foundational problem. Forecasters issue
probabilistic predictions that are then compared with the outcomes that materialize.
How should one evaluate such a forecaster? A natural approach is to use a scoring rule:
a penalty function that assigns a loss to each forecast–outcome pair. A scoring rule is
deemed proper if the loss is minimized when the forecast equals the underlying probability
distribution of the outcome. The standard and most widely used proper scoring rule is
the quadratic rule (Brier 1950; see Savage 1971, Schervish 1989, and the comprehensive
treatment in Gneiting and Raftery 2007).
2


## Page 3


A classic result (Sanders 1963; Murphy 1972; DeGroot and Fienberg 1983) is that the
Brier quadratic score B decomposes as
B = K + R,
where K is the calibration score—measuring how close the forecasts are to the realized
frequencies—and R is the refinement score—measuring how informative the partitioning
of the outcomes into bins is, where the partitioning is determined by the announced
forecasts (see Section 2.2 for the precise setup and definitions). A surprising result of
Foster and Vohra (1998) is that one can generate forecasts that are guaranteed to be
calibrated (i.e., K converges to zero), no matter what the outcomes turn out to be (for
the ensuing literature, see the survey of Olszewski 20151).
In Foster and Hart (2023), we introduced the notion of “calibeating”: beating a
reference forecaster by achieving a Brier score no worse than its refinement score—thus
gaining calibration without losing expertise, which, as we argued there, is reflected in
the induced partitioning into bins and the resulting refinement score. We showed that
calibeating can be achieved in several ways: by a simple deterministic online procedure;
by a stochastic procedure that is itself calibrated; and by a deterministic continuously
calibrated procedure. All these results, however, were stated exclusively for the quadratic
score.2
However, there is an extensive class of proper scoring rules (in particular, every
decision-making setting under uncertainty generates one; see below and Section 6). A
fundamental concern is thus whether these guarantees are robust or merely artifacts of
the quadratic functional form. If a forecaster’s performance evaluation is sensitive to the
specific choice of a proper loss function, its theoretical and practical relevance may well
be questionable. This raises the natural question:
If a procedure is calibrated (or calibeats) under the quadratic score, do these
guarantees persist under every other proper scoring rule?
This paper answers that question for proper scoring rules that are bounded.3 The
answer turns out to be positive for calibration, and negative for calibeating.
We will call a procedure proper-calibrated (respectively, proper-calibeating) if the corre-
sponding guarantee holds simultaneously for all bounded proper scoring rules (see Section
3 for the precise definitions).

Calibration entails proper-calibration. We show, first, that calibration transfers
for free: every calibrated procedure is automatically proper-calibrated. The key obser-
vation is that the calibration score under any bounded proper scoring rule is bounded
by a multiple of the square root of the quadratic calibration score. Consequently, all
established calibrated forecasting procedures—the stochastic procedures, as well as the
deterministic ones that are continuously calibrated4—are already proper-calibrated, with
no modification needed.
Calibeating does not entail proper-calibeating. Our second result is perhaps
a surprise: unlike calibration, calibeating does not transfer to proper-calibeating. We
exhibit a concrete example (Section 5.1) in which a forecasting sequence c calibeats a
reference sequence b under the quadratic scoring rule, yet fails to calibeat b under, for
instance, the 2-spherical proper scoring rule (which is bounded, and even Lipschitz).
Both sequences b and c are perfectly calibrated; the failure is purely in the refinement
comparison.
The reason for the asymmetry between calibration and calibeating is instructive.
Calibration scores shrink whenever the quadratic calibration score shrinks, regardless
of which proper scoring rule is used; but refinement scores under different proper rules
need not move in tandem.
Achieving proper-calibeating. We next provide three positive results on proper-
calibeating (i.e., calibeating simultaneously under all bounded proper scoring rules).
First, we show that a stochastic procedure that calibeats a finer binning—one that
cross-classifies forecasts by the bins of both the reference forecaster and the procedure
itself—is proper-calibeating as well as proper-calibrated. A key insight is that it is not
enough to calibeat the reference forecaster and to be calibrated separately; what matters
is calibeating the joint binning, which keeps track of how the two sets of bins interact
(Appendix A.2 elaborates further on this).
Second, the very simple deterministic procedure of Theorem 3 of Foster and Hart
(2023)—which forecasts the running average action in the reference forecaster’s bin—is
shown to be proper-calibeating for every Lipschitz proper scoring rule (which we term
“proper-Li-calibeating”). We further show that this result does not hold for all bounded
proper scoring rules.
Third, we establish the existence of a deterministic procedure that is simultaneously
proper-Li-calibeating and continuously proper-calibrated; this is obtained via an approx-
imate decomposition of the score (that generalizes from the quadratic case; see Appendix
A.7 in Foster and Hart 2026).
All three results extend to the simultaneous proper-calibeating of multiple reference
forecasters, by cross-classifying across all of their bins.

Decision-making under uncertainty. Forecasts are commonly used in decision-
making under uncertainty, where the probabilities of the various states of nature are
unknown (see Section 6). The forecast is utilized to make a decision that optimizes the
decision-maker’s expected utility. This process induces a scoring rule, where the loss is
taken as the realized disutility of the decision that is optimal with respect to the fore-
cast. The scoring rule is proper: when the forecast is “correct,” i.e., equal to the true
distribution, expected utility is maximized and expected loss is minimized. We show
that, in a sequential decision-making setting, the calibration score of the sequence of
forecasts, computed according to the induced scoring rule, is exactly the “regret” in-
curred by best-replying to these forecasts. Consequently, proper-calibration is equivalent
to achieving “universal” no-regret, simultaneously across all bounded utility functions.
Similarly, proper-calibeating yields appropriate gain guarantees across all utilities.
1.1
Related Work
The question of how forecasting guarantees extend across different scoring rules has at-
tracted attention from several directions.
Closest to our work, Chen, Huang, Jordan, and Luo (2026) prove calibeating rates for
general proper losses with a reduction to regret minimization. Lee, Noarov, Pai, and Roth
(2022) study multicalibeating through online multiobjective optimization. Universality
across losses is also central to the “U-calibration” framework of Kleinberg, Leme, Schnei-
der, and Teng (2023) and the “omniprediction” framework of Gopalan, Kalai, Reingold,
Sharan, and Wieder (2022). On the statistical side, Dimitriadis, Gneiting, and Jordan
(2021) and Popordanoska, Gruber, Tiulpin, Buettner, and Blaschko (2023) analyze the
calibration–refinement decomposition for general proper rules. Additional connections
between proper losses and calibration appear in Blasiok, Gopalan, Hu, and Nakkiran
(2023) and Luo, Senapati, and Sharan (2025).
1.2
Outline of the paper
Section 2 sets up the framework: scoring rules, divergences, and the Brier, calibration,
and refinement scores. Section 3 introduces the “proper” concepts (proper-calibration
and proper-calibeating). Section 4 proves that calibration automatically yields proper-
calibration. Section 5 presents the counterexample showing that calibeating does not
imply proper-calibeating, establishes the three routes to proper-calibeating and proper-

In the Appendix we collect background material on scoring rules and a provide a further
analysis of the joint-binning condition.

A.2
On the Proof of Proper-Calibeating by a Calibrated Pro-
cedure
Our proof of Theorem 9, which establishes proper-calibeating by a proper-calibrated
procedure, is based on the seemingly stronger construct of calibeating the joint binning
(see Section 5.2). As noted in the remark at the end of the section (cf. the example in
Section 5.1), calibeating together with calibration does not guarantee proper-calibeating.
In this appendix we provide evidence of the need to calibeat the joint binning. We do so
by keeping the action averages in all joint bins fixed, and varying the relative frequencies
of these bins. Under this setting, we show that calibeating the joint is the only way to
guarantee that proper-calibeating always follows from calibeating by a calibrated forecast.
Our main tool is Theorem 29 in Section A.2.1 below, a general result that may be of
independent interest.
Thus, fix the finite set of bins B of the sequence b, the finite set of bins D of our
forecasting sequence c, and the action average ¯a(b, d) of each joint (b, d)-bin, and allow
the (relative) frequencies λ(b, d) of the bins to vary.42 For instance, repeating each period
of a certain (b, d)-bin twice amounts to doubling this λ(b, d) while keeping all the rest
unchanged.43 For clarity, we assume that all calibration and calibeating errors are exactly
zero. Given bin frequencies λ, we take our sequence c ≡cλ to be (perfectly) calibrated,
and so c = ¯a(·, d) = P
b λ(b, d)¯a(b, d) for each bin44 d.
Theorem 27 Assume that the matrix (¯a(b, d))b∈B,d∈D of action averages has more than
two distinct entries. Then the following statements are equivalent:
(i) For every bin-frequency matrix λ, if the calibrated forecasting sequence c ≡cλ calibeats
b then it proper-calibeats b.
(ii) For every bin-frequency matrix λ, if the calibrated forecasting sequence c ≡cλ cal-
ibeats b then it calibeats b × c.
One way to interpret this result is as follows: if there are bin frequencies λ where the
calibrated sequence cλ is calibeating b but it does not calibeat the joint b × c (and so
(ii) does not hold), then there are other bin frequencies λ′ such that the calibrated cλ′
calibeats b but does not proper-calibeat b (i.e., (i) does not hold). In short, for calibeating
to entail proper-calibeating no matter what the bin frequencies are, one needs calibeating
to entail calibeating the joint.
