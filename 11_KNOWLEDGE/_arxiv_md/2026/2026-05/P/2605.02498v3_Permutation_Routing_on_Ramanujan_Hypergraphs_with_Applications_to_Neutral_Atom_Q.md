Introduction
Reconfigurable neutral atom arrays have emerged
as a leading platform for scalable quantum com-
puting [1–7]. In these architectures, qubits are
encoded in individual atoms trapped by optical
tweezers or acousto-optic deflectors (AODs), and
entangling gates are performed by bringing atom
pairs within the Rydberg blockade radius [4, 5].
Between gate layers, atoms must be rearranged
(routed) to bring the next set of interacting pairs
into proximity. Routing depth becomes a domi-
nant contributor to overall circuit depth, compu-
tational time, and by extension, gate fidelity.
Given N atoms and a target permutation π ∈
SN (the symmetric group on the N atoms), we
ask how many parallel rearrangement steps are
needed?
The answer depends on connectivity
topology (which atom pairs can interact) and
move model (which simultaneous rearrangements
are physically allowed).

Proof technique and novelty
The proof of Theorem 3.1 combines Valiant’s two-
phase randomized routing [12], negative associa-
tion of random permutations [13, 14], and the
Leighton–Maggs–Rao packet scheduling theorem
[15, 16].
The contribution lies in the applica-
tion, identifying clique expansions of Ramanu-
jan hypergraphs as a natural class of expanders
for qubit routing and finding applicable regimes
relating mathematical bounds to quantum hard-
ware. The six extensions in Sections 5–9 develop
practical consequences for architecture design.

2.2
Clique expansion and spectral identity
The clique expansion Gcl(H) is the weighted
graph on vertex set V with adjacency matrix
A(H) (so the weight on {u, v} is the number of
hyperedges containing both vertices). When ev-
ery pair of vertices shares at most one hyperedge
(holding for projective planes, generalized quad-
rangles, and random regular hypergraphs used
here), the weighted clique expansion coincides
with the simple graph in which {u, v} is an edge
if and only if u and v share a hyperedge.
Proposition 2.2 (Spectral identity). The adja-
cency matrix of Gcl(H) equals A(H). In partic-
ular, Gcl(H) is d(r −1)-regular with eigenvalues
λ1 ≥λ2 ≥· · · ≥λN of A(H).
We write d′ = d(r −1) for the degree and
β = λ∗/d′ for the spectral ratio, where λ∗=
max(λ2, |λN|).
2.3
Routing number
Definition 2.3 (Routing number). A routing
step on Gcl(H) selects a matching M and simul-
taneously swaps the pebbles at the endpoints of
each edge in M. The routing number rt(H) is the
minimum T such that every permutation π ∈SN
can be realized by a sequence of T routing steps.
Routing matchings are taken on the under-
lying simple graph.
Spectral parameters λ∗, β
in Lemma 2.5 refer to the weighted adjacency
A(H). For multiplicity-free hypergraphs the two
coincide and the matching argument transfers di-
rectly.
Remark 2.4 (Packing model). An alternative
model selects vertex-disjoint packings of hyper-
edges and applies arbitrary τ ∈Sr (the symmet-
ric group on the r elements of a single hyper-
edge) within each. This packing routing number
rtH(H) can differ substantially when the match-
ing number ν(H) is small, being the maximum
number of pairwise vertex-disjoint hyperedges in
H.

• Virtual Ramanujan overlay (§5). Use AOL se-

• Algebraic constructions on the grid (§6).

• Constructive Ramanujan families (§7). SFM

The remainder of §11 returns to the hardware
setting with concrete near-term predictions and
architectural recommendations once these escape

Proposition 5.3 (Capacity independence). A

12.3
Limitations
Explicit constants (Table 2) are large for small
(d, r). Grid hypergraphs are not Ramanujan, so
the bounds apply only by the virtual-overlay the-
orem (§5). Further, numerical validations are lim-
ited to small grids with N ≤4096 (simulation)
and N ≤65,536 (theoretical scaling).
Asymp-

104 scale relevant to next-generation hardware.
The matching-model framing (Remark 11.1) is a
common denominator across reconfigurable plat-
forms (AOD, AOL, ion shuttling, optical lat-
tices), so bounds transfer to every such platform
up to constant factors.
