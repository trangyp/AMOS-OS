Abstract
In this article, we define a locally finite graph X as η-polynomially hyperbolic if there
exists a Lipschitz map φ : X →Z to some hyperbolic space Z satisfying the following
condition: there exists C ≥0 such that
|B(p, R1) ∩φ−1(B(q, R2))| ≤(CR1)η(CR2) for all p, q ∈X, R1, R2 ≥0.
The picture to keep in mind is that coarse fibres of φ have polynomial growth with a
degree coarsely controlled by η as the thickness of the fibres grows. The map η quantifies
how brutal we have to be in order to turn X into a hyperbolic space. Our main result is
that, among cocompact special groups, being lin-polynomially hyperbolic amounts not to
contain F2 × F2 as a subgroup. Consequently, containing F2 × F2 as a subgroup turns out
to be quasi-isometric invariant for cocompact special groups.

Introduction
In geometric group theory, given a geodesic metric space X, it is often desirable to
construct a hyperbolic model that extracts the negative curvature of X.
Typically,
the strategy is to identify the obstruction of X from being hyperbolic and to cone-off
X in order to collapse this obstruction, producing a(n equivariant) coarsely surjective
Lipschitz map from X to a hyperbolic space. Of course, the goal is to cone-off subspaces
of X that are as small as possible, in order to keep as much information about the
geometry of X as possible. Loosely speaking, the less we need to collapse, the more
hyperbolic our group is. In this article, we propose to quantify this idea through the
following definition:
Definition 1.1. Let X be a locally finite graph and Y a metric space. Given a function
F : [0, +∞)2 →[0, +∞), a map φ : X →Y is F-gentle if there exists a constant C ≥0
such that
|B(p, R1) ∩φ−1(B(q, R2))| ≤CF(CR1, CR2)
for all R1, R2 ≥0, p ∈X, and q ∈Y .
The idea to keep in mind is that the first coordinate of the map F(·, ·) controls the
growth of coarse fibres and the second coordinate of F(·, ·) controls how this growth
evolves as the thickness of the fibres increases. Roughly speaking, F quantifies how
brutal we have to be in order to turn our metric space into a hyperbolic space. See
Section 2 for examples. In this article, we mainly focus on functions F(x, y) = xη(y).
Then, coarse fibres have polynomial growth and η quantifies how the degree coarsely
evolves as the thickness of the fibres increases.
Definition 1.2. A locally finite graph X is η-polynomially hyperbolic if there exists a
Lipschitz map X →Y to a hyperbolic space that is xη(y)-gentle.
For instance, every graph X of polynomial growth is bnd-polynomially hyperbolic since
X ↠pt is xn-gentle for some n ≥1. Similarly, given a graph X of polynomial growth
and a hyperbolic graph Y of bounded degree, X ×Y is lin-polynomially hyperbolic since
the projection map X × Y ↠Y is xy-gentle. It is worth noticing that, because every
graph embeds in a hyperbolic horoball (see Example 2.4), it turns out that every locally
finite graph is exp-polynomially hyperbolic.
Thus, every locally finite graph is η-polynomially hyperbolic for some η between bounded
and exponential. The main result of our article is that the lamplighter graph Lam(Z),
or equivalently the lamplighter group Z2 ≀Z, is not η-polynomially hyperbolic for some
polynomial η, which motivates the idea that Lamp(Z) is very far from being hyperbolic.
Since Lamp(Z) quasi-isometrically embeds into the product of free groups F2 × F2, the
same conclusion holds for F2 × F2.
Theorem 1.3. The lamplighter Lamp(Z), and consequently the product of free groups
F2 × F2, is not pol-polynomially hyperbolic.
It turns out that, in several cases of interest, there is a gap in the behaviour of polynomial
hyperbolicity for finitely generated groups: either our group is lin-polynomially hyper-
bolic or it cannot be pol-polynomially hyperbolic since it contains a quasi-isometrically
embedded copy of F2 × F2. As a consequence, polynomial hyperbolicity allows us to
characterise geometrically when some finitely generated groups contain F2 × F2 as a
subgroup.
We illustrate this phenomenon with two families of groups. The first groups we con-
sider are cocompact special groups, as introduced in [HW08]. Our main result in this
direction is:

Theorem 1.4. Let G be a cocompact special group. The following are equivalent:
• G contains F2 × F2 as a subgroup;
• G contains F2 × F2 as an undistorted subgroup;
• F2 × F2 quasi-isometrically embeds into G;
• Lamp(Z) quasi-isometrically embeds into G;
• G is not pol-polynomially hyperbolic;
• G is not lin-polynomially hyperbolic.
As an immediate consequence, it follows that:
Corollary 1.5. Given two quasi-isometric cocompact special groups G and H, F2 × F2
is a subgroup of G if and only if F2 × F2 is a subgroup of H.
Corollary 1.5 completes the facts that, among cocompact special groups, containing Z2
or F2 ×Z is also invariant under quasi-isometries (characterising hyperbolicity and toral
relative hyperbolicity respectively; see[CH09, Gen21a]). (See Question 1.10.) However,
it is worth mentioning that our corollary is no longer true among cubulable groups, since
there exist uniform lattices in products of two trees that are commutative transitive
[Rat05], proving that F2 × F2 is quasi-isometric to a cubulable group that does not
contain F2 × F2 (or even F2 × Z) as a subgroup.
Theorem 1.4 can be applied to explicit examples of groups. For instance, it provides the
following characterisation for right-angled Artin groups. Recall that, given a graph Γ,
the right-angled Artin group A(Γ) is defined by the presentation
⟨V (Γ) | [u, v] = 1 for every edge {u, v} ∈E(Γ)⟩.

We conclude this introduction with the following question, which we leave open:
Question 1.10. Let n, m ≥0 be two integers. Among cocompact special group, is
containing Fn
2 × Zm as a subgroup a quasi-isometric invariant?
As already said, the answer is positive for (n, m) equal to (0, 2) according to [CH09]
(characterising hyperbolicity), to (1, 2) according to [Gen21a] (characterising toral rela-
tive hyperbolicity), and finally to (2, 0) according to Corollary 1.5 (characterising poly-
nomial hyperbolicity). A positive answer is also obtained in [BBP26] for m = 0 and n
arbitrary but in the more restricted family of two-dimensional right-angled Artin groups.
Polynomial hyperbolicity does not allow us to distinguish F2 × F2 and F2 × F2 × F2.
However, the more general idea that Fn
2 can be mapped to a hyperbolic space only with
large coarse fibres could apply. We expect polynomial hyperbolicity to be a first step in
a hierarchy of properties that would allow us to distinguish cocompact special groups
containing F2 × Zm for various values of n and m.

Gentle maps
Intuitively, what we want to do is to quantify how brutal we have to be in order to turn
a metric space into a hyperbolic space. For this purpose, we introduce the following
notion:
Definition 2.1. Let X be a locally finite graph and Y a metric space. Given a function
F : [0, +∞)2 →[0, +∞), a map φ : X →Y is F-gentle if there exists a constant C ≥0
such that
|B(p, R1) ∩φ−1(B(q, R2))| ≤CF(CR1, CR2)

for all R1, R2 ≥0, p ∈X, and q ∈Y .
The idea to keep in mind is that the first coordinate of the map F(·, ·) controls the
growth of coarse fibres and the second coordinate of F(·, ·) controls how this growth
evolves as the thickness of the fibres grows.
Typically, given a locally finite graph, the goal will be to find a function F as small
as possible such that there exists a Lipschitz F-gentle map from our graph to some
hyperbolic space.
Remark 2.2. Definition 2.1 naturally extends to arbitrary metric measure spaces. In
view of the applications we are interested in, namely finitely generated groups, we restrict
ourselves to locally finite graphs for simplicity.
First examples.
In order to illustrate the notion of gentle maps, we record below a
few natural examples.
Example 2.3. Given two graphs of bounded degree X and Y , clearly every map X →Y
is F-gentle for F(x, y) = growthX(x) where growthX : R 7→maxp∈V (X) |B(p, R)|. Thus,
every graph X of bounded degree can be mapped F-gently to the hyperbolic space {pt}
with F(x, y) = growthX(x), and a fortiori with F(x, y) = exp(x).
Example 2.4. Recall that, given a graph X, the horoball H(X) is the graph whose
vertex-set is V (X) × N and whose edges connect (x, n) to (y, m) if either y = x and
m = n + 1 or m = n and dX(x, y) ≤2−n. Horoballs are always hyperbolic [GM08,
Theorem 3.8]. As an easy consequence of [GM08, Lemma 3.10], if X is locally finite, then
the canonical embedding φ : X →H(X) is F-gentle for F(x, y) = growthX(min(x, 2y)).
Example 2.5. Given two locally finite graphs X and Y , the projection map X ×Y →Y
is F-gentle with F(x, y) = growthX(x)growthY (min(x, y)). For instance, F2 × Zn →F2
is 2y min(x, y)n-gentle.
Example 2.6. Let F2 = ⟨a, b |⟩be the free group of rank two. The canonical map
Cayl(F2, {a, b}) →Cayl(F2, ⟨a⟩∪⟨b⟩) is xy-gentle. This can be checked by direct com-
putation, or as a consequence of Lemma 2.14 below.
Polynomial hyperbolicity.
We will mainly focus on F-gentle maps with F(x, y) =
xη(y) for some functions η : [0, +∞) →[0, +∞). In this case, the intuition to keep in
mind is that coarse fibres have polynomial growth and that the map η controls how the
degree evolves when the thickness of the fibre increases.
Definition 2.7. A locally finite graph X is η-polynomially hyperbolic if there exists a
Lipschitz map X →Y to a hyperbolic space that is xη(y)-gentle.
As justified by our next lemma, polynomial hyperbolicity is clearly preserved by quasi-
isometries. Consequently, it makes sense to refer to a finitely generated group as being
(η-)polynomially hyperbolic whenever its Cayley graphs (constructed from finite gener-
ating sets) are (η-)polynomially hyperbolic.
Lemma 2.8. Let X, Y be two graphs of bounded degree and ρ : X →Y a quasi-isometric
embedding. If φ : Y →Z is an F-gentle map to some metric space Z, then φ ◦ρ is also
F-gentle. Consequently, if Y is η-polynomially hyperbolic for some η, then X must be
η-polynomially hyperbolic as well.

An osbtruction to polynomial hyperbolicity
In this section, our goal is to prove a criterion, namely Theorem 3.3 below, that allows
us to show that some graphs cannot be η-polynomially hyperbolic for small functions η.
Loosely speaking, our criterion applies to graphs containing quasi-isometrically embed-
ded trees in which any two vertices can be connected by many distinct quasi-geodesics.
In order to be more precise, we need to introduce a couple of definitions.
Definition 3.1. Let X be a graph. Two vertices x, y ∈V (X) are exponentially (a, L)-
connected if, for every L ≤R < d(x, y)/2, we can find N ≥aR paths α1, . . . , αN of
lengths ≤Ld(x, y) connecting x and y such that the αi\{x, y}+R are pairwise disjoint.
In our next definition, we denote by (T2, o) the 2-regular tree rooted at o ∈V (T2).

Preliminaries on median geometry
We start by recalling basic definitions and properties related to median graphs.
Definition 5.2. A connected graph X is median if, for all vertices x1, x2, x3 ∈V (X),
there exists a unique vertex m ∈V (X) satisfying
d(xi, xj) = d(xi, m) + d(m, xj) for all i ̸= j.
Hyperplanes, which we now define, provide a fundamental tool in order to understand
the geometry of median graphs.
Definition 5.3. Let X be a median graph. A(n oriented) hyperplane is an equivalence
class of (oriented) edges with respect to the reflexive-transitive closure of the relation
that identifies two (oriented) edges whenever they are opposite in some induced 4-cycle.
Two hyperplanes are transverse (resp. tangent) whenever they contain two edges with a
common endpoint that span (resp. that do not span) an induced 4-cycle.
See Figure 2. Roughly speaking, the geometry of a median graph is encoded in the
combinatorics of its hyperplanes. This idea is partly justified by the following statement:
Theorem 5.4. Let X be a median graph.
• For every hyperplane J, the graph X\\J obtained from X by removing the edges
in J has exactly two connected components, referred to as halfspaces.
• Halfspaces are convex.
• A path is a geodesic if and only if it crosses every hyperplane at most once.

every hyperplane separating x and y separates {a, x} and {b, y}.

every hyperplane separating x and a must be transverse to every hyperplane separating
x and y.

Preliminaries
In this section, we record some basic definitions and properties related to quasi-median
graphs, and we describe their connection with graph products as highlighted in [Gen17].
Quasi-median graphs admit several different but equivalent definitions, see [BMW94].
The definition we give here echoes the characterisation of median graphs as retracts of
hypercubes. (Recall that, given a graph X, a subgraph Y ⊂X is a retract if there exists
a graph morphism X →Y that restricts to the identity on Y .) Roughly speaking, we
can think of quasi-median graphs as defining the smallest reasonable class of graphs that
encompasses median graphs and products of complete graphs.
Definition 6.2. A connected graph is quasi-median if it is retract of a Hamming graph
(i.e. a product of complete graphs).
This definition is not the most useful in practice, but it highlights the tight connection
between median and quasi-median graphs. Similarly to median graphs, the key objects
to understand in order to describe the geometry of quasi-median graphs are hyperplanes.
Definition 6.3. In a quasi-median graph, a hyperplane is an equivalence class of edges
with respect to the reflexive-transitive closure of the relation that identifies two edges
whenever they belong to a common 3-cycle or they are opposite in some induced 4-cycle.
Two hyperplanes are transverse if they contain two edges with a common endpoint that
span an induced 4-cycle.

the combinatorics of its hyperplanes. Our next statement justifies this leitmotiv.
