Abstract
The bulk motion of E-region radar aurora provides a sparsely distributed, direct mea-
surement of the ionospheric electric field in intermittent bursts. We present a tracking
procedure for icebear VHF measurements of Farley-Buneman waves. Each cluster is
represented as an α-shape; frame-to-frame association is a Hungarian linear-assignment
problem with a cost combining centroid distance and shape Intersection-over-Union; kine-
matic prediction amounts to a degenerate Kalman filter. Births, deaths, splits, and merg-
ers are monitored; each tracked trajectory is reduced to per-segment velocities by piecewise-
linear regression. We validate against a Defense Meteorological Satellite Program (DMSP)
conjunction on 20 May 2021 and Swarm ion-drift statistics. During the G5 storm of 10
May 2024, on closed dayside field lines, the method recovers a five-second cluster at
11,240±660 m/s, implying ≈560 mV/m, a value that exceeds documented sub-auroral
thermal emission speeds and the most extreme reported sub-auroral drifts. The detec-
tion is consistent with extreme E-field structures appearing as short-lived bursts.
Plain Language Summary
Earth’s aurorae, or northern and southern lights, mark the location of electric currents
that flow in the upper atmosphere when the solar wind pushes hard against Earth’s ex-
tended magnetic field. The strongest of these electrical currents — the auroral electro-
jets, at roughly 105 km above Earth’s surface — are driven by electric fields that are dif-
ficult to measure: satellites pass through them too quickly to map their structure, and
ground-based radars typically return difficult-to-interpret plasma motions. We present
a machine-learning method that automatically tracks compact clusters of radar echoes,
clusters that correspond directly to plasma turbulence excited by the strong electric fields.
This is made possible by the advanced scientific imaging radar icebear, located in Saskatchewan,
Canada. Each tracked radar echo cluster is automatically followed across consecutive frames;
its motion is then converted into a local electric field measurement, validated against four
years of polar-orbiting satellite measurements (2020–2024). During the May 2024 geo-
magnetic superstorm — the most powerful storm in two decades — the method detected
a transient (fast-appearing and fast-disappearing) event in which an electric structure
travelled at over 11,000 meters per second, faster than any value previously documented
in this region of space.
1 Introduction
In Earth’s upper atmosphere, at the lower edge of the partially ionized layers of the iono-
sphere, intense, small-scale plasma turbulence is observed during geomagnetic storms,
in the form of crashing waves that appear inside the electrical currents belonging to the
auroral electrojets. Compared to the large scales of the aurorae and the magnetosphere-
ionosphere (MI) coupling, these turbulent waves, a few meters in size, are microscopic,
and the highly structured, turbulent, plasma that is created around aurorae subsequently
disturbs radio propagation through the ionosphere (Bahcivan et al., 2006).
Electrojet turbulence, called Farley-Buneman (FB, Farley, 1963; Buneman, 1963) waves,
is excited by the externally applied electric field, which produces a relative velocity be-
tween ions and electrons at altitudes between 90 km and 120 km. Here, the ions expe-
rience collisions and their motion is restricted, while the electrons are unaffected owing
to their much smaller sizes. The resulting polarization causes instability (St.-Maurice
and Hamza, 2001).
Of some importance, in the nightside auroral region, the crashing of these waves occur
fast and numerous, and the sum total of these crashes outline, in time and space, the
driving electric field imposed by the magnetosphere (Ivarsen et al., 2025a). This short-
lived, or ephemeral, quality, means that the occurrence of FB turbulence around auro-
rae act as tracers, tracing the shape and trajectory of moving electric field structures. As

2 Methods
The suite we have implemented is a three-stage chain operating on ICEBEAR Level-2
echo data (Huyghebaert et al., 2019, 2021). Recently, Ivarsen et al. (2024a) implemented
an algorithm to automatically track clutters, or clusters, of radar backscatter targets through
time and space. The present paper improves on that procedure in four respects.
(i) The point-cloud footprints are represented as α-shapes (Edelsbrunner and M¨ucke, 1994),
which support shape-overlap arithmetic via the Intersection-over-Union (IoU).
(ii) Frame-to-frame association is formulated as a linear assignment problem on a cost
matrix combining centroid-to-prediction distance with the IoU between each previous-
frame α-shape, translated by its kinematic prediction, and each current-frame α-shape
— following the IoU-association lineage of SORT and DeepSORT (Bewley et al., 2016;
Wojke et al., 2017) — and solved by the Hungarian/Munkres algorithm (Kuhn, 1955),
with the gating radius tightening once a track has a velocity estimate.
(iii) The births, deaths, splits, and mergers of tracked clusters are monitored and book-
kept explicitly, allowing the evolution of the complex auroral-electrojet field structure
to be reconstructed without imposing one-to-one correspondences at every frame; the
motion model is a degenerate Kalman filter that maintains a one-step finite-difference
velocity per track without covariance propagation (cf. Yang and Baum, 2017, for the explicit-
noise form).
(iv) Forward-greedy sliding-window piecewise-linear regression (Keogh et al., 2001) con-
verts each tracked centroid trajectory into per-segment velocities.
2.1 Frame-to-frame association
After a new cluster has been identified, the algorithm looks at where each cluster has
just been, then predicting where that cluster should appear next, and then matching pre-
dictions to the new frame’s proposals under a cost that combines centroid distance and
shape overlap. The labels assigned by the associator are the object identities that the
system ultimately reports, whose evolution is measured. Of some importance, polygons
called α-shapes (Edelsbrunner and M¨ucke, 1994) are drawn around the cluster footprints.
The problem has Ni clusters at frame t and Nj clusters at the most recent non-empty
frame within a nine-frame lookback window (so the tracker tolerates brief detection gaps),
and we cast association as a linear assignment problem on an Ni×Nj cost matrix. For
each previous-frame cluster j this computes a predicted position at frame t by linear ex-
trapolation using that cluster’s instantaneous velocity. The gating radius is the maximum
allowed centroid-to-prediction distance, in km, for a current cluster to be considered as
a possible match for j, which starts out at a value between Dgate =10–40 km, but which
is tightened to a value between Dpredict =5–15 km during tracking. Pairs separated
by more than the gating radius are excluded from the assignment problem entirely (in-
finite cost).

2.2 Births, deaths, mergers, and splits
Once the foregoing assignment problem is solved, the method maps out the four ways
a cluster can fail to participate cleanly in a 1-to-1 match (that is, cease to be tracked),
yielding the lineages of the observed shapes. For each new shape, if the intersection be-
tween it and a previous shape covers more than 5% of the current cluster’s area, the cur-
rent cluster is interpreted as a split product of the matched parent, giving the next, fresh,
target an existing velocity estimation and gating. This allows velocities to be monitored
over time and space, as in a flow, consistent with the long tail of field strength events
appearing and quickly vanishing stochastic ensembles. This bookkeeping is borrowed from
extended-target tracking (Granstrom et al., 2017, and references therein), where a sin-
gle label cannot represent a one-to-many event and parent/child provenance must be car-
ried explicitly.
For each cluster, after its full evolution from birth to death has been documented, the
trajectory of the cluster-centroid (median location, or echo centre-of-mass) is treated as
two scalar time series in displacement (geomagnetic) east and displacement (geomagnetic)
north (each in km, referenced to the track’s first point), each modelled as piecewise lin-
ear in time. The algorithm splits the trajectory into intervals where a constant-velocity
model fits: a sliding window is extended forward one sample at a time until, for each ac-
tive displacement component (i.e., one whose range exceeds a minimum displacement thresh-
old), the linear correlation with time drops below a fixed threshold (akin to online piecewise-
linear approximations, Keogh et al., 2001). The method’s central output, the speed of

3 Data
The methods of Section 2 turn each radar-aurora cluster into a labeled trajectory and,
via the magnetic-field projection of Section 2.2, into a local electric-field estimate (Ivarsen
et al., 2024b). Section 3 brings two conjunctions to bear on this interpretation. Figure 3
(20 May 2021) anchors the tracked motions to in-situ ion drifts measured by DMSP F16,
confirming the proxy relation under standard auroral driving. Figure 4 (10 May 2024)
applies the same proxy during the recent G5 superstorm, with observations made on closed
field-lines equatorward of the cusp (the conjunction was first reported in Ivarsen et al.,
2025b), where the tracker recovers a 5-second event with a fitted speed of 11,240±660 m/s.
Between 11:59:48 and 12:15:44 UT on 20 May 2021, icebear resolved 94 clusters of suf-
ficient duration to enter the segmented-velocity analysis (Figure 3f). The weighted-median
segmented speed was 770 m/s; the speed-segment distribution is well described by a Gaus-
sian of σ = 965 m/s. The DMSP F16 horizontal ion-drift moments collected over the
same interval averaged 851 m/s with a 1σ scatter of 209 m/s. The two distributions agree
on the mode, but the icebear distribution is considerably broader, owing to a large num-
ber of in-cluster velocity segments (whose distribution are represented by the solid red
line in Figure 3f), which are averaged out in the quantified tracks — witness that only
five tracks are faster than 1400 m/s and the rest of the tracks exhibit speeds that are
wholly enclosed by green histogram (the F-region ion drifts). The long tail of segment
speeds in Figure 3f) represent small-scale variability, captured by icebear’s exceedingly
high spatio-temporal resolution.
The agreement between the green distribution and the black circles in Figure 3f) extends
the validation of Ivarsen et al. (2024a,b, 2025c), who favourably compared tracked mo-
tions to ion drifts inferred from coincident Swarm, DMSP, and Superdarn.
Figure 4 details the metop-1 conjunction, which took place at ∼18:00 UT on 10 May
2024, during the main phase of the G5 superstorm of that date, at or near the equator-
ward edge of the dayside cusp. metop-1 is a weather, or climate, satellite operated by
EUMETSAT, with an instrumentation seemingly identical to the United States NOAA
satellites, capable of measuring precipitating particles through a number of telescopes.
icebear resolved 59 clusters in the 12-minute interval centred on the conjunction. The
cluster-speed distribution (Figure 4e) had a weighted median of 1388 m/s and a segment-
fit σ = 2056 m/s, both larger by a factor of approximately two, compared to the val-
ues measured during the 2021 conjunction in Figure 3. The MEP precipitating-particle
channels in Figure 4b, c) show a precipitation signature in both the 50 eV–1 keV elec-
tron and ion fluxes, co-located in mlat with the bulk of the icebear echo distribution;
the TED channel in panel d) shows substantial trapped electron flux at 40, 130, and 287 keV
across the same mlat band. This combination places the observed radar aurora on closed
field-lines just inside the open-closed boundary of the dayside auroral oval, consistent
with the configuration documented for this event by (Ivarsen et al., 2025b), and with the
similar conditions observed during the 23 April 2023 storm (Ivarsen et al., 2025c).

magnetic north) over a tracked duration of 5 seconds. The centroid translated ∆x =
52.6 km eastward and ∆y = 16.6 km northward over that interval. Converting via |E| =

in space, rather than persistent features of any bulk drift.
The four-year icebear cluster-speed distribution is shown in Figure 5 against the cor-
responding distribution of Swarm A’s electric field instrument (EFI, Knudsen et al., 2017)

4 Discussion & Summary
We have improved a recent 3D radar tracking algorithm, implementing a Hungarian-method
association scheme to identify and track clusters of radar echoes through time and space.
The core of the scheme is a cost matrix based on intersection-over-union (IoU) between
α-shapes, with kinematic predictions between each frame via a degenerate Kalman fil-
ter. The resulting trajectories, or tracks, accurately reflect the motion of electric field
structures, since the moving structures excite quickly-decaying Farley-Buneman waves
in their path.
We applied this method during two fortuitous space-ground conjunctions between ice-
bear and Superdarn, and the spacecrafts metop-1 and DMSP F16, as well as to sta-
tistically aggregated radar aurorae. The resulting tracked radar speeds matched with in-
situ observations of the electric field in the F-region, during conjunctions (Figure 3), and
in statistical aggregates (Figure 5). A conjunction with the EU climate satellite metop-

The intermittent and localized bursts of energy in icebear’s field-of-view are consistent
with a largely turbulent state of the magnetosphere-ionosphere coupling, in which heavy-
tailed electric-field distributions are expected to intensify with the imposed magnetospheric
driver (Wiltberger et al., 2017). Extreme (> 500 mV/m) spikes in this electric field
have been measured in-situ (Marklund, 1997; Marklund et al., 1998; Johansson et al.,
2004; LaBelle et al., 2010), but never before with radar.
The implied Joule heating density in such spikes can make a large impact. At Pedersen
conductance values ΣP ∼5–30 S, Cluster 3624’s ∼560 mV/m field gives Q = ΣP |E|2 ≈
1.5–10 W/m2, at the upper end of point measurements reported during major storm in-
tervals (e.g., Codrescu et al., 1995; Deng and Ridley, 2007; Knipp et al., 2004). Such ex-
treme electric field spikes may therefore contribute to the storm-time ‘missing heat’ (Rosen-
qvist et al., 2006), through Joule heating or wave-particle interactions (St-Maurice and
Goodwin, 2021).
The context in which the specific observation was made, detailed in Figure 4, reflects re-
cent research into transient, eastward radar motions equatorward of the ionospheric cusp
(Ivarsen et al., 2025b,c). In those papers, coincident observations of high-energy parti-
cle precipitation equatorward of the cusp proper were established as the ultimate cause
of the observed spikes, verified by coincident observations of whistler-wave particle in-
teractions in the radiation belts (see Figure 6 in Ivarsen et al., 2025b). The May 2024
conjunction between icebear and metop-1 likewise occurred on closed magnetic field-
lines (Figure 4d), and particle precipitation was widespread (Themens et al., 2024). In
addition, the incidence, absorption, or reflection of Alfv´en waves is likely or possible to
have occurred on the same field-lines. We therefore attribute particle precipitation closed
field-lines, perhaps in combination with ongoing Alfv´en wave interactions with the medium,
as the direct cause of the extreme electric field spikes observed in the present paper.
icebear’s ability to circumvent the limiting effects of the ion sound speed, which oth-
erwise keep fast VHF radars from inferring electric fields from Doppler measurements,
itself enabled by the multiple interferometry links combined with fast software-defined
radio, gives the prospect of using radar tracking in routine space weather monitoring an
appealing aspect.
