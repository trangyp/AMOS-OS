ALEXANDER CAMPBELL
Rosso Street octogenario magna cum admiratione
Abstract. We fix the notion of parity complex by a judicious selection from among
the axioms originally considered by Street. We show that parity complexes so defined,
together with the morphisms of parity complexes defined by Verity, form a category
equivalent to the category of strong Steiner complexes (nés augmented directed complexes
with strongly loop-free unital bases). To this end, we isolate the purely combinatorial
structure possessed by the bases of free augmented directed complexes. This analysis
reveals the essential advantage of Steiner’s formalism to be that the role of subsets in
Street’s formalism is played instead by multisets.

1. Introduction

freely generates a combinatorially defined ω-category. Parity complexes arose within a
research programme in higher category theory initiated by Roberts’s insight [Rob79, §2]

The orientals are a family of combinatorially defined ω-categories constructed by Street
in [Str87] for the purpose of defining a simplicial nerve functor for ω-categories. The Gray
tensor product of ω-categories is part of a biclosed monoidal structure on the category of
ω-categories (first studied by Al-Agl and Steiner in [AAS93]) which ultimately depends
for its construction on another family of combinatorially defined ω-categories (see [Str04,
§9]), namely the free n-categories on the parity n-cubes (themselves first constructed by
Aitchison and Street, cf. [Ait10, Str91, Str95]).
Street introduced the theory of parity complexes as a general framework for managing
the combinatorics of such families of ω-categories, abstracting the previous treatments
of the orientals and cubes. He was especially interested in the Gray tensor products of
orientals with globes (where the n-globe is the free n-category containing an n-cell), which
featured in his original definition of cohomology ω-categories. For this reason he wanted
parity complexes to be closed under a suitable notion of product; in his own words [Str91,
p. 316]: “The abstraction arose purely from the desire to deal with products of examples
already understood.”4
And yet the notion of parity complex defined in [Str91, §1] is not closed under products.
For Street chose in that paper to defer to the penultimate section [Str91, §5] the treatment
of products of parity complexes and the introduction of the strong loop-freeness axiom –
antisymmetry of the “solid triangle order” ◀– that ensures that parity complexes are
closed under products. It is nevertheless evident, both from the Introduction of [Str91]
and from remarks made in [Str04, pp. 539, 561], that Street intended parity complexes
to be closed under products and strong loop-freeness to be one of the axioms for parity
complexes.5
This expository choice was rendered problematic by the subsequent discovery of counter-
examples to certain statements in [Str91, §§3–4], revealing inadequacies in the initial set
of axioms given in [Str91, §1]. First, Verity found a simple counterexample to [Str91,
Lemma 3.2(a)],6 which fault Street was able to repair in [Str94] by the introduction of a
new “tightness” hypothesis, which he showed to follow from the globularity and strong
loop-freeness axioms. A subtler and more fatal counterexample was later discovered by
Forest, who produced a parity complex (see [For22, pp. 13f.]) which satisfies both the
axioms of [Str91, §1] and the tightness hypothesis of [Str94], but for which the “freeness”
conclusion of Street’s main theorem on parity complexes [Str91, Theorem 4.2] does not
hold. Crucially, Forest’s parity complex does not satisfy the strong loop-freeness axiom; as
we shall see in the final section of this paper, Street’s main theorem does hold for parity
complexes satisfying the strong loop-freeness axiom, from which we may conclude that
Street’s intended notion of parity complex is fit for purpose.
Our goal in this paper is to clear these weeds from the theory of parity complexes by
fixing the notion of parity complex and placing it in its modern mathematical context,
which for us means Steiner’s theory of augmented directed complexes [Ste04]. For more
than a decade now Steiner’s theory has played the role in higher category theory originally
envisioned for parity complexes (see e.g. [AM20] and [GH25]), and rightly so: we regard
Steiner’s theory as an amelioration of the theory of parity complexes; indeed, Steiner

2. Preliminaries on Steiner theory
We preface our study of parity complexes with a somewhat idiosyncratic recollection of
the basics of Steiner’s theory of augmented directed complexes [Ste04]. We begin with
some notions common to both Steiner theory and directed algebraic topology (cf. [Gra09,
§2.1.1]) and conclude by observing (in Propositions 2.20 and 2.23) that a pair of results
of Steiner theory due to Forest [For22] hold under a weaker hypothesis than previously
stated in the literature.
2.1. Preordered abelian groups. A preordered abelian group is an abelian group A
equipped with a preorder relation ≤compatible with addition, in the sense that a ≤b
implies a+c ≤b+c for all a, b, c ∈A. Such a preorder relation on A is uniquely determined
by its positive cone A∗⊆A, the submonoid of positive elements (a ∈A such that a ≥0),
since a ≤b if and only if b−a ∈A∗; moreover, every submonoid of an abelian group arises
in this way. A group homomorphism between preordered abelian groups is monotone if it
respects the preorder relations, or equivalently if it preserves positive elements; these are
the morphisms in the category of preordered abelian groups.
A basis of a preordered abelian group A is a subset B ⊆A∗which is a basis for both
the commutative monoid A∗and the abelian group A. Note that a basis of a preordered
abelian group is unique if it exists, since the same is true of commutative monoids; the
basis elements may be characterised intrinsically as the minimal strictly positive elements.
We call a preordered abelian group free if it admits a basis. The functor that sends a
preordered abelian group to its set of positive elements admits a left adjoint which sends
a set B to a free preordered abelian group with basis isomorphic to B.
Let A be a free preordered abelian group with basis B. Then A is canonically isomorphic
to the free abelian group ZB of functions B −→Z of finite support under pointwise
addition, equipped with the pointwise natural order. It follows that A is moreover a
lattice-ordered abelian group, whose join ∨and meet ∧operations correspond under the
canonical isomorphism to the pointwise operations of max and min in ZB. In particular,
each element x ∈A can be expressed uniquely as the difference of two disjoint positive
elements, i.e. as x = x+ −x−, with x−, x+ ∈A∗and x−∧x+ = 0; here x+ = x ∨0 and
x−= (−x) ∨0; we call x−and x+ the negative and positive parts of x respectively.
2.2. Directed chain complexes. A directed chain complex is a positively-graded chain
complex of abelian groups K for which each chain group Kn (n ≥0) is a preordered
abelian group. Note that the boundary maps of a directed chain complex are not assumed

A basis of an augmented directed complex is simply a basis of its underlying directed
chain complex (cf. [Ste04, Definition 3.1]). We call an augmented directed complex free if
it admits a basis. We say that a basis of an augmented directed complex K is normal if
it is normal as a basis of the underlying directed chain complex and if the augmentation
ε: K0 −→Z sends each basis element of K0 to 1. The following proposition tells us that,
when working with augmented directed complexes with normal bases, we may effectively
ignore the augmentations.
2.7. Proposition. The category of augmented directed complexes with normal bases is
isomorphic to the category of directed chain complexes with normal bases and normal
morphisms between them.
Proof. By definition, if an augmented directed complex has a normal basis, so too has its
underlying directed chain complex. Let K and L be augmented directed complexes with
normal bases and let f be a morphism between their underlying directed chain complexes.
Since the basis elements of K0 and L0 are precisely the elements with augmentation 1,
the homomorphism f0 : K0 −→L0 preserves the augmentations of K and L if and only if
it preserves basis elements. Hence the forgetful functor ADC −→dCh restricts to a fully
faithful functor from the category of augmented directed complexes with normal bases to
the category of directed chain complexes with normal bases and normal morphisms.
This functor is bijective on objects because any directed chain complex K with normal
basis admits a unique augmentation ε: K0 −→Z sending each basis element to 1; this
indeed defines an augmentation since, for each basis element x of K1, we have
ε(∂x) = ε(∂+x −∂−x) = ε(∂+x) −ε(∂−x) = 1 −1 = 0,
using that ∂−x and ∂+x are basis elements of K0 by normality of the basis of K.
□

3. Additive parity complexes
We begin our study of parity complexes by analysing the structure of free augmented
directed complexes, which leads us to the purely combinatorial notion of additive parity
complex. The basic strategy of our analysis is to eliminate all reference to negative
elements, phrasing everything in terms of positive elements. We realise these positive
elements as multisets, which allows us to imitate the combinatorial language of Street’s
theory of parity complexes.


## Page 9

3.1. Multisets. A free commutative monoid with basis B is canonically isomorphic to the
monoid of functions B −→N of finite support under pointwise addition. Such functions
admit a combinatorial interpretation as the characteristic functions of finite multisets of
B. A multiset of a set B is a collection of elements of B in which elements may occur with
finite multiplicity; in short, a multiset is a “subset with repetition”. Note that subsets of
B are precisely the multisets of B in which each element occurs with multiplicity at most
one.
We henceforth take the set Mf(B) of finite multisets of a set B as our preferred model
for the free commutative monoid on B, and hence also for the positive cone of the free
preordered abelian group on B. The monoid operation on Mf(B) is disjoint union ⊔of
multisets and the zero element is the empty subset ∅; as a free commutative monoid, its
basis elements are the singleton subsets of B and its radical elements are the subsets of
B. We make frequent use of the “multiset difference” operation S \ T, defined to be the
positive part of the element S −T, i.e. S \ T := (S −T) ∨0; this generalises the familiar
set difference operation on subsets. The crucial advantage of the algebra of multisets
over the algebra of subsets is that the operation of disjoint union of multisets is defined
universally, not only for disjoint pairs of subsets.
3.2. Additive parity structures. Let K be a free directed chain complex with basis
B. (We shall consider augmentations at the end of this section.) Observe that, for each
n ≥0, the preordered abelian group Kn can be recovered up to isomorphism from its
basis Bn, and the boundary homomorphism ∂: Kn+1 −→Kn can be recovered from its
values ∂x ∈Kn on basis elements x ∈Bn+1, which values can be recovered from their
disjoint negative and positive parts ∂−x and ∂+x in K∗

3.3. Definition. An additive parity structure is a graded set B = (Bn)n≥0 together with,
for each element x ∈Bn+1 (n ≥0), a disjoint pair of finite multisets ∂−x and ∂+x of Bn.

5. Parity complexes
It now remains only to state our final definition and to prove our final theorem.
In accordance with §2.9, we say that a parity structure C is strongly loop-free if there
exists a partial order ◀on C such that, for x, y ∈C, x ◀y whenever x ∈y−or y ∈x+.
Note that any strongly loop-free parity structure is weakly loop-free. It is immediate from
the definitions that a weak Steiner complex is strongly loop-free, i.e. is a strong Steiner
complex, if and only if its underlying weak parity complex is strongly loop-free. This
motivates the following definition.
5.1. Definition. A parity complex is a parity structure which is globular, unital, and
strongly loop-free.

Note that any parity complex is a weak parity complex. We define the category of

5.2. Theorem. The category of parity complexes is equivalent to the category of strong

Combining this theorem with Steiner’s Theorem 2.11, we conclude that parity complexes

5.3. Corollary. For every parity complex C, the ω-category OC is freely generated by its
