Abstract
We introduce a technology to formally verify that a software system
satisfies a temporal specification of functional correctness, with-
out revealing the system itself. Our method combines a deductive
approach to model checking to obtain a formal certificate of cor-
rectness for the system, with zero-knowledge proofs to convince
an external verifier that the system—kept secret—complies with
its specification of correctness—made public. We consider proof
certificates represented as ranking functions, and introduce both
an explicit-state and a symbolic scheme for model checking in zero
knowledge. Our explicit-state scheme assumes systems represented
as transition graphs. We use polynomial commitments to convince
the verifier that the public proof certificates correspond to the secret
transition relation. Our symbolic scheme assumes systems speci-
fied as linear guarded commands and uses piecewise-linear ranking
functions. We apply Farkas’ lemma to obtain a witness for the va-
lidity of the ranking function with public and secret components,
and employ sigma protocols for matrix multiplication and range
proofs to convince the verifier of the witness’s existence. We built a
prototype to demonstrate the practical efficacy of our two schemes
on linear temporal logic verification examples. Our technology en-
ables formal verification in domains where both the safety and the
confidentiality of the system under analysis are critical.

Applied Cryptography, Linear Temporal Logic, Model Checking,

Introduction
Model checking is the problem of determining whether a software,
hardware, or cyber-physical system satisfies a formal specifica-
tion of correctness. Unlike traditional testing, which is inherently
non-exhaustive, model checking provides formal proof that the sys-
tem behaves as intended [6, 10, 18]. It is an established standard in
electronic design automation, protocol verification, and control soft-
ware development in domains where safety and security are highly
critical. Moreover, it is increasingly viewed as a promising avenue
for the safety assurance of AI systems. Formal proofs of correctness
guarantee the absolute (or probabilistic) absence of violations with
respect to a given specification, thereby enabling verifiable compli-
ance with safety standards and fostering trust between technology
providers, regulators, and users of such systems.
Scalability has been a central focus of the formal methods com-
munity for decades, yet the industrial adoption of model checking
faces another pressing challenge: confidentiality. The traditional
formal verification algorithms require disclosure of the system
description in order to convince an external verifier of the sys-
tem’s compliance with its specification. In a setting where formal
verification is a requirement potentially involving multiple juris-
dictions, standardisation bodies, or collaborating companies, the
obligation to disclose a system description is likely to create tension
between regulators’ or customers’ need to ensure compliance with
safety requirements and providers’ desire to protect their intellec-
tual property. To date, no technology exists that can provide an
external verifier with a formal proof of safety and correctness in
zero knowledge, without disclosing the system itself.
Zero-knowledge proofs provide a cryptographic mechanism by
which a prover can convince a verifier that a given statement is
true without revealing any information beyond the validity of the
statement itself. They are widely used in areas such as crypto-
graphic protocol design, blockchain and cryptocurrency systems,
privacy-preserving authentication and identity management, se-
cure electronic voting, and confidential data access control. In the
context of system verification and certification, zero-knowledge
proofs offer a the opportunity to demonstrate compliance with for-
mal specifications while preserving confidentiality, as correctness
claims can be validated without disclosing system internals, propri-
etary designs, or sensitive customer data, eliminating reliance on
trust between parties. Despite their maturity and broad adoption
in multiple domains, the application of zero-knowledge proofs to
formal verification remains rare [53, 55, 56].
We introduce the first approach to model checking in zero knowl-
edge: Zero-Knowledge Model Checking (ZKMC). Our method com-
bines proof-theoretic model checking with zero-knowledge proofs
to construct a proof certificate that the system satisfies the spec-
ification and then convince an external verifier that our public
artefacts—the specification and the proof certificate—are valid with
respect to our secret artefact—the system under analysis—without
revealing any information about the system itself.
Our setting considers the model checking of reactive systems
with discrete state spaces against 𝜔-regular specifications, sub-
suming linear temporal logic (LTL) [61]. We formulate the model
checking problem as establishing that the Büchi automaton for
the complementary specification never accepts an execution of the
system, that is, that the language of the corresponding synchronous
composition is empty. To prove this, we require a ranking function

Accordingly, our proof certificate consists precisely of this rank-

two complementary algorithms: an explicit-state and a symbolic

Our explicit-state algorithm assumes systems represented as
finite-state transition graphs whose states are labelled with atomic
propositions, i.e., as Kripke structures (Section 4). We associate the
system with a ranking function over the state space of the sys-
tem alongside an appropriate automaton. The task is to convince
an external verifier of the validity of the proof certificate without
revealing the system’s initial states or its transition relation. Our
scheme employs KZG polynomial commitments with batch evalua-
tion to ensure that any hypothetical state or transition that would
violate the publicly disclosed proof certificate cannot correspond
to an actual initial state or transition of the secret system. Upon
successful verification, which is guaranteed by the standard cryp-
tographic properties of polynomial commitments [47], the proof
of correctness follows by contraposition. Our proof is two group
elements regardless of state-space size; prover work is quadratic in
the number of state of the system.
Our symbolic algorithm assumes reactive systems represented
by integer variables and guarded commands defined through lin-
ear constraints with integer coefficients, potentially yielding very
large or infinite state spaces. We require state propositions to be
represented as Boolean combinations of linear inequalities, and
ranking functions to be expressed as piecewise-defined affine func-
tions; we derive equivalent proof obligations in a uniform form,
each having a secret component corresponding to the coefficients
of the original reactive system. The task is to convince an exter-
nal verifier that each obligation holds without revealing the secret
component. Our scheme computes a Farkas witness for each obliga-
tion and then, to establish the validity, employs specialised sigma
protocols for zero-knowledge matrix multiplication and equality
sub-protocols over two-tier Pedersen commitments [21], and we
give a new zero-knowledge range proof construction (zkrp). Upon
successful verification, we obtain a zero-knowledge proof of correct-
ness for the reactive system under analysis. Prover and verifier cost
are independent of the state space and polynomial in the number
of guarded commands and automaton transitions.
We developed a prototype implementation and evaluated our
two algorithms on a range of liveness verification tasks. Our bench-
marks include a 3-way handshake protocol with exponential back-
off, a model of DHCP, and a round-robin scheduling algorithm
(Section 6). Our explicit-state algorithm can verify systems with
up to approximately 29 states within a 2 h time limit. Under the
same time constraint, our symbolic algorithm handles significantly
larger tasks, scaling to approximately 221 states. We emphasise that
the explicit-state algorithm makes no assumptions about the struc-
ture of the system, whereas the symbolic algorithm requires the
system to be expressed as linear guarded commands. These results
therefore highlight a trade-off between expressivity and perfor-
mance, with the explicit-state and symbolic approaches addressing
different use cases.
In summary, our contribution is threefold. First, we introduce
the first framework for model checking in zero knowledge, com-
bining cryptographic principles with proof techniques for formal
verification. Second, we present two schemes that realise this frame-
work: an explicit-state algorithm for finite-state transition graphs
and a symbolic algorithm for guarded command languages. Third,
we implement both schemes in a prototype and demonstrate the
practical efficacy of our framework on LTL model checking tasks
applied to standard communication and coordination protocols.

Zero-Knowledge Model Checking
We address the problem of convincing an external verifier that
a state transition system M satisfies a temporal specification 𝜙,
denoted M |= 𝜙, without revealing any information about the
initialisation or the transition structure of M.
Our framework combines three complementary technologies,
corresponding to the three phases of our approach: (1) formal certi-
fication, (2) cryptographic commitments, and (3) zero-knowledge
proofs. First, we require a proof certificate witnessing M |= 𝜙and
that can be verified in polynomial time. Second, we employ a com-
mitment scheme that binds to M while keeping it hidden, ensuring
that the system is uniquely identifiable through its cryptographic
commitment. Third, we use a zero-knowledge proof scheme to con-
vince an external verifier that (1) the proof certificate is valid and (2)
that it correctly corresponds to M and the specification 𝜙, without
revealing any information about the system M.

which they rest, in Sections 4 and 5.
Remark 2.2 (Specification- and Certification-Induced Leak-
age). Our framework ensures that neither the commitment 𝑐M nor
the proof 𝜋reveals any information about M. However, selecting a
secure specification 𝜙and certificate 𝑣𝜙remains the user’s respon-
sibility. In the extreme case where the public specification 𝜙or the
certificate 𝑣𝜙uniquely determine the secret system M, any party
capable of verifying the proof could, in principle, reconstruct the sys-
tem regardless of the protocol’s cryptographic guarantees. Developing
systematic specification and certification schemes that mitigate this
risk is beyond the scope of this work and constitutes an open problem.
We further emphasise that side channels arising from the prover’s
computation, denial-of-service attacks, or attacks that combine our
protocol with auxiliary channels (such as the timing of certificate
generation, which may depend on M) are addressed by standard
countermeasures in the literature.
Summarising, we require the design of three components: a cer-
tification scheme for model checking, a commitment scheme for
the system, and a zero-knowledge proof scheme that jointly cap-
tures certification and commitment. We instantiate our framework
for LTL model checking in Section 3, and present two alternative
commitment and zero-knowledge schemes in Sections 4 and 5.

Remark 5.1 (Soundness and Completeness of Farkas Wit-
nesses). Farkas’ lemma characterises the unsatisfiability of systems
of linear inequalities over the rationals, whereas our witnesses (𝜆s, 𝜇s)
are integer and restricted to the range [−𝑀, 𝑀]. This approach is
sound for the obligations in Eqs. (22) to (24), since every integer solu-
tion is also a rational one. However, the converse fails on two accounts.
First, the integer restriction of the dual system may be unsatisfiable
even when its rational counterpart is satisfiable, in which case no
witness can be found. Second, a rational witness may exist but require
coefficients outside [−𝑀, 𝑀], making it unrepresentable in our encod-
ing. Thus, our witnesses are sufficient but not necessary, and a prover
may fail to produce one even when the obligation holds.
Each obligation of the form of Eq. (28) comprises three classes
of operations: (i) matrix–vector multiplications and additions, (ii)
range membership proofs, and (iii) equality proofs. This decompo-
sition suggests a modular approach: we design a specialized zero-
knowledge sub-protocol for each class, prove each independently
and then compose them into a single zero-knowledge proof.

Experimental Evaluation
We implemented an end-to-end prototype2 of both the explicit-
state and symbolic algorithms and use it to investigate three re-
search questions. (RQ1) Can either scheme verify realistic protocols
within practical time and memory budgets? (RQ2) How do the two
schemes compare in scalability on these protocols (explicit-state in
terms of state-space size, symbolic in terms of the number of proof
obligations)? (RQ3) In which settings is each scheme preferable?
Our main finding is that the two approaches are complementary.
The explicit-state scheme performs well on small systems with
negligible setup cost but degrades quadratically in |𝑆|. By contrast,
the symbolic scheme scales to systems with up to 220.9 states and
212.2 proof obligations within a two-hour budget, albeit with a
higher constant overhead.

Related Work
Our work lies at the intersection of zero-knowledge proofs and
automated reasoning, a new research direction for the cybersecu-
rity and formal methods communities. Prior work has proposed
zero-knowledge protocols for theorem proving with different logi-
cal backends, including zkUNSAT for propositional unsatisfiabil-
ity [56], zkSMT for first-order logic unsatisfiability in decidable
theories [55], and zkPi for interactive theorem proving [25, 53].
Although model checking can be broadly viewed as a special case
of theorem proving, our setting differs fundamentally from prior
work from both formal methods and security and privacy perspec-
tives. We target the automated verification of software systems
and operate at a different level of abstraction than the existing
approaches, which focus on pure validity questions over logical
statements. Crucially, prior work assumes a public theorem and a
secret proof witness; this assumption does not directly carry over
to our setting, where the theorems—in this paper, the obligations
of Theorem 3.2—contain both public and secret components. Even
more importantly, our approach mandates commitments to the
system as a core component, a requirement considered tangentially
in prior work. Commitments are essential to prevent attacks in
which a malicious prover replaces the system under analysis with
a different one, whether during, before, or after the proof.
Privacy-preserving verification is a broader, emerging area at
the intersection of computer-aided verification and applied cryp-
tography. Prior work has explored this space through the lens
of multi-party computation (MPC), with solutions for CTL model
checking and runtime verification [42, 46]. To the best of our knowl-
edge, our work is the first to implement a privacy-preserving formal
verification algorithm using zero-knowledge proofs.
Proof certificates are rooted in program logics and the axiomatic
semantics of software systems [33]. The proof rule of Theorem 3.2
builds on results on fair termination in nondeterministic Büchi
automata [40, 72]. Our scheme extend to alternative proof rules
for generalised Büchi automata [50] and to other temporal specifi-
cations [22, 23], assuming systems and proof obligations are rep-
resented either explicitly or as linear guarded commands, as in
Sections 4 and 5. In principle, our approach is also amenable to
proof rules for probabilistic model checking [3–5, 28, 43, 51] and
to the verification of cyber-physical systems [64, 65]. However, ex-
tending ZKMC to reasoning over continuous domains, which is
needed for probabilistic reasoning and for systems with continuous
state spaces, requires a generalisation of our cryptographic routines
and remains future work.
An important application domain for ZKMC is AI systems, where
we identify two main directions: verification of robustness proper-
ties of neural networks, both in isolation and under feedback con-
trol [9, 45, 66, 68–70, 74], and neural certificates for software [36–38]
and for cyber-physical systems [1, 2, 16, 54, 58, 75, 76]. Although
this setting is, in principle, a special case of our approach, in prac-
tice it is likely to yield an intractable number of proof obligations
and will therefore require the development of specialised symbolic
techniques for neural networks, which we leave to future work.
Our symbolic algorithm combines Farkas witnesses with zero-
knowledge proofs for linear algebra in a novel way. The duality of
linear programming, of which Farkas’ lemma is a special case, is
a standard technique in the synthesis and verification of ranking
functions [19, 62], while zero-knowledge proofs for linear-algebraic
relations are now well studied [14, 21]. To the best of our knowledge,
our approach is the first to use linear programming duality as the
structural backbone of a zero-knowledge proof.
A natural alternative is to encode a model-checking proof as an
arithmetic circuit and verify its execution using a general-purpose
zkSNARK or zkVM [34, 39, 53, 67]. We deliberately avoid this ap-
proach for two reasons. First, the arithmetisation cost of explicit-
state search scales with the size of the state space and quickly be-
comes prohibitive. Even for our medium-sized DHCP benchmarks
(approximately 10 5 states), the resulting circuit would contain hun-
dreds of millions of constraints. Second, and more fundamentally, a
proof-theoretic perspective decouples the certificate of correctness
from the procedure that produced it. The prover may use any model-
checking algorithm, whether explicit-state, symbolic, abstraction-
based, or even manual, provided that the resulting ranking function
satisfies the public obligations of Theorem 3.2. Our schemes verify
these obligations directly, with costs determined by the size of the
certificate rather than by the effort required to construct it.
8
Conclusion
We have presented the first approach to model checking in zero
knowledge, enabling formal verification in scenarios where the
confidentiality of the system is crucial. Our implementation has
demonstrated the practical viability of our new technology. Our
work raises many scientific questions, from the security implica-
tions related to commitment before and after a model checking
proof, as well as the information a public specification may reveal
about a secret system.
