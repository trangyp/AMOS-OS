VISUALIZING CONJUGATION IN AFFINE COXETER GROUPS
AMY HERRON AND ANNE THOMAS
Abstract. Affine Coxeter groups are fundamental objects in mathematics and in crystallogra-
phy. If two group elements are conjugate, then they have very similar algebraic and geometric
properties. Using recent structural results of Mili´cevi´c, Schwer and the second author, we de-
velop an app to visualize conjugation in affine Coxeter groups in dimensions 2 and 3.
The
resulting pictures exhibit beautiful geometric symmetries.
1. Introduction
We describe an app we have developed to visualize important structures in affine Coxeter
groups. These collections of symmetries are also known as affine Weyl groups and Euclidean
reflection groups. Affine Coxeter groups have long been studied in algebra and geometry, and
they are a key family in crystallography: in dimensions 2 and 3, every crystallographic group
has finite index in some affine Coxeter group (see, for example, [MST25a, Appendix A]).
An affine Coxeter group W is, by definition, a discrete group of Euclidean isometries generated
by reflections in the faces of a convex polytope P in Euclidean space. The action of W induces
a tiling of Euclidean space by copies of P, and the elements of W can then be identified with
the tiles. A first example is W of type ˜A2, which induces the tiling of the Euclidean plane by
equilateral triangles, as depicted in Figure 1.

Two elements x and y of W are conjugate if there is some z ∈W such that zxz−1 = y, and
the conjugacy class of x ∈W is the set of all conjugates of x:
[x] = {zxz−1 | z ∈W}.
Elements of the same conjugacy class have closely related properties. Algebraically, if φ : W →A
is a homomorphism from W to an abelian group A, then for every y ∈[x] we have φ(y) = φ(x).
Geometrically, every conjugate of a reflection (respectively, translation) in W is another reflection
(respectively, translation). The set of elements which conjugate x to itself is called the centralizer
of x:
C(x) = {z ∈W | zxz−1 = x}.
More generally, if x and y are conjugate then the corresponding coconjugation set is given by
C(x, y) = {z ∈W | zxz−1 = y}.
In this paper we describe an app AffineCoxeterExplorer.java [Her26] that we have devel-
oped to visualize conjugacy classes and coconjugation sets in affine Coxeter groups in dimensions
2 and 3. Using the identification of the elements of W with the tiles in the induced tesselation,
we shade all tiles which are in the desired conjugacy class or coconjugation set (and which lie
inside a bounding box of size chosen by the user). For example, in type ˜A2, Figure 1 shows
the output from our app for a conjugacy class [x] and a coconjugation set C(x, y). (All figures
appearing in this paper were generated using version 1.0.1 of our app.)
Our first goal with this visualization is to illustrate how both conjugacy classes and coconju-
gation sets lie along certain classically-defined subspaces. For example, in both parts of Figure 1,
the shaded tiles lie along certain lines. To make this more precise, write E for the Euclidean
space tesselated by the action of W. Then the conjugacy class [x] lies along certain images of
an affine subspace of E called the move-set:
Mov(x) = {q ∈E | q = xp −p for some p ∈E} = range(x −I) = (x −I)E,
where I is the identity map on E. Now any y ∈W can be decomposed uniquely as the product of
a translation tλ and an element w ∈W which fixes the origin. Given y = tλw, the coconjugation
set C(x, y) then lies along certain images of a linear subspace of E called the fix-set:
Fix(w) = {p ∈E | wp = p} = ker(w −I).

2. Mathematical background
In this section we give some additional, more precise background. Section 2.1 recalls some
of the general theory of affine Coxeter groups, while Section 2.2 recalls the relevant results
from [MST25a, MST25b].
2.1. Affine Coxeter groups. We recall some key notions concerning affine Coxeter groups. A
concise reference for this material is [Ron09, Chapter 2], and the standard reference is [Hum90].
As in [MST25a, MST25b], we follow the conventions of [Bou02].
A Coxeter group is a group W with presentation of the form
W = ⟨S | (st)mst for all s, t ∈S⟩
where mss = 1 for all s ∈S, and mst = mts ∈{2, 3, 4, . . . } ∪∞if s, t ∈S are distinct.
Here, mst = ∞means that the product st has infinite order. Given x ∈W, an expression
x = si1 . . . sik where si1, . . . , sik ∈S is called a word for x, and is a reduced word if no shorter
product of elements of S equals x. We often abbreviate a product si1 . . . sik to si1...ik, as in
Figures 1–3. We write e for the identity element of W, which is represented by the empty word.
For W and S as in the previous paragraph, the pair (W, S) is called a Coxeter system. A
Coxeter system (W, S) is irreducible if there is no nontrivial partition of S into two disjoint
commuting subsets, spherical if W is finite, and affine if S is the set of reflections in the faces
of a compact polytope P in some Euclidean space E. For convenience, if dim(E) = n, we define
the corresponding affine Coxeter system (W, S) to be n-dimensional (this terminology is not
standardized).
The irreducible affine Coxeter systems were classified by Coxeter in the 1930s, and correspond
to the case that P is a simplex. Hence if (W, S) is n-dimensional and irreducible affine, the
generating set S has n + 1 elements; these are denoted s0, s1, . . . , sn. For all affine Coxeter
systems, the action of W induces a tesselation of E by copies of the polytope P.
We call
these copies of P the alcoves, and identify the elements of W with the alcoves in the induced
tesselation.

2.2. The geometry of conjugacy classes and coconjugation sets. We now briefly recall
the relevant results of Mili´cevi´c, Schwer and the second author [MST25a, MST25b]. See the
introduction for the definitions and our notation for conjugacy classes and coconjugation sets,
together with move-sets, fix-sets, and mod-sets.
Let (W, S) be an n-dimensional affine Coxeter system, with coroot lattice L and finite Weyl
group W0.
We first recall some relationships between the mod-set Mod(w), the move-set
Mov(w), and the coroot lattice L, for elements w ∈W0.
It is immediate from definitions
that Mod(w) is contained in Mov(w) and in L. Hence Mod(w) ⊆Mov(w) ∩L. Moreover, the
lattice L can be viewed as a free abelian Z-module (of rank n), and then both Mod(w) and the
intersection Mov(w)∩L are submodules of L. The next statement is a special case of Corollary
1.9(a) of [MST25a].
Theorem 2.5. For any w ∈W0, the mod-set Mod(w) has finite index in Mov(w) ∩L.

the user’s understanding of the underlying mathematics. We describe how to install and run

3.1. Application. In this section we explain how to install and run our app.
The application AffineCoxeterExplorer.java [Her26] is written using Java and SageMath.
Both need to be installed locally before running the app. Java 11+ can be downloaded from
https://adoptium.net and SageMath can be downloaded from https://www.sagemath.org
(both are free). Note that installing SageMath locally can sometimes be difficult. We found
that artificial intelligence can help resolve individual computer issues.
Once Java and SageMath are installed, the user should download the following files from
https://github.com/herron-amy/AffineCoxeterExplorer:
(1) AffineCoxeterExplorer.java
(2) compute helper.sage
(3) README.txt
(4) one of the following, as appropriate:
(a) Launch (Linux).sh
(b) Launch (macOS).command
(c) Launch (Windows).bat
The user can then run the app by either double clicking on the appropriate Launch option or
by inputting
java --source 11 AffineCoxeterExplorer.java
in a terminal window, after changing the directory to the one containing the app. The app will
open and the user then chooses the following:
• The type of the affine Coxeter system (W, S) they want to visualize. All 2-dimensional
and all irreducible 3-dimensional types are available (see Examples 2.1 and 2.2, respec-
tively).
• Whether they want to visualize a conjugacy class or a coconjugation set.
– If “Conjugacy Class” is selected, the user then inputs one element of W.
– If “Coconjugation Set” is selected, the user then inputs two elements of W.
The methods for inputting elements of W are described below.
• Size of bounding box, in the range 1–15. The larger the bounding box, the more alcoves
are shown, at the cost of increased run time. When the user selects the type of (W, S),
we suggest a bounding box size which gives a reasonable trade-off, but this size can be
modified by the user.
Instead of these selections, the user can instead choose one of 15 quick examples. These illustrate
both conjugacy classes and coconjugation sets.

correspond to two useful algebraic structures on W. The first method corresponds to the pre-

3.2. Output and visual features. In this section we describe the output of our app, which
comprises both text and graphics. The visual features of the app have been chosen carefully, as
we explain.
The text output, which appears in the right-hand column of the app, is the SageMath output.
It provides coordinates for the simple coroots, and then a list of all alcoves in the conjugacy class
(respectively, coconjugation set) which lie inside the selected bounding box. These alcoves are
listed using both their reduced word which is first in lexicographic order, and their translation
part (with respect to the basis of simple coroots) and spherical direction. Thus once a conjugacy
class [x] is computed, we have easy access to a list of elements to which x is conjugate. Any of
these elements can then be used in addition to x to visualize a nonempty coconjugation set.
The graphical output shows the tesselation of the Euclidean plane by alcoves in the 2-
dimensional cases, and by wireframe alcoves (the 1-skeleton of the induced tessselation) in the
3-dimensional cases. In dimension 2 this output is displayed in the center of the app, while in
dimension 3 a browser window will open, and the user can then use the mouse to zoom in or out
and rotate the wireframe around, in order to better understand the image. In dimension 2, the
coroot lattice vertices are indicated by heavy black dots, and the coroot basis vectors α∨
1 and
α∨
2 are drawn in bold red and bold blue, respectively (see Figures 1 and 3). We decided not to
display this coroot lattice information in dimension 3, so as to avoid cluttering the image.
In the graphical output, certain alcoves are decorated to indicate mathematical information.
The alcoves in the computed conjugacy class (respectively, coconjugation set) which lie within
the chosen bounding box are shaded, with each spherical direction assigned a different color as
explained further in Section 3.3. This choice of shading helps the user to see that conjugacy
classes lie along images of move-sets, and that coconjugation sets lie along images of fix-sets. We
decided not to depict the move-set or fix-set itself, because the pictures became too cluttered,
especially in the 3-dimensional cases, and by Theorem 2.5 the move-set can be inferred from the
mod-set.
To help locate the computed conjugacy class (respectively, coconjugation set), in both di-
mensions 2 and 3, the identity alcove is shaded and labeled e. If e is an element of the given
conjugacy class (respectively, coconjugation set), then e is also striped. This can be seen in
Figure 4, which depicts the conjugacy class [e] = {e} in the 3-dimensional cases. In dimension
2, we additionally shade and label all elements of the finite Weyl group W0, and stripe them
if they are in the computed conjugacy class (respectively, coconjugation set). In dimension 3,
since the finite Weyl group W0 has either 24 or 48 elements (see Example 2.4), such shading
and labeling of the entire group W0 is not feasible. Instead, we indicate the origin in dimension
3 by a red dot.

3.3. Color choices. As explained in Section 3.2, certain alcoves are shaded in our graphical
output, according to their spherical direction. In this section, we explain these color choices in
more detail.
The 2-dimensional affine Coxeter groups of types ˜A1 × ˜A1, ˜A2, ˜B2, ˜C2, and ˜G2 have finite
Weyl groups of orders 4, 6, 8, 8, and 12, respectively (see Example 2.3). For these finite Weyl
groups, we simply assign colors chosen for maximum contrast to each element of W0.
In dimension 3, the finite Weyl groups corresponding to types ˜A3, ˜B3, and ˜C3 contain 24, 48,
and 48 elements, respectively (see Example 2.4). This makes it infeasible to assign a visually
distinct color to each finite Weyl group element. To manage this, we observe that if x = tλw
and x′ = tλ′w′ are conjugate elements of W, with λ, λ′ ∈L and w, w′ ∈W0, then w and w′
must be conjugate in W0. Thus it is enough to assign visually distinct colors to the elements
of each conjugacy class in W0. Now it is well known that two permutations are conjugate in
a symmetric group if and only if they have the same cycle type. Thus in order to determine
conjugacy classes in W0 in dimension 3, it makes sense to find an injective homomorphism from
W0 to a symmetric group.
For all n ≥1, the finite Coxeter group of type An is isomorphic to the symmetric group
Sym(n + 1).
Thus in particular, if (W, S) is of type ˜A3, then the finite Weyl group W0 is
isomorphic to Sym(4). For W0 of type B3 or C3, let t1, . . . , t5 be the Coxeter generators for
the finite Weyl group of type A5, which is isomorphic to Sym(6). Then there is an injective
homomorphism ϕ : W0 →Sym(6) induced by s1 7→t1t5, s2 7→t2t4, and s3 7→t3. We use these
identifications of the finite Weyl groups in types ˜A3, ˜B3, and ˜C3 with a subgroup of a symmetric
group to assign visually distinct colors to elements of W0 that have the same cycle type.
3.4. Outline of code. We conclude by giving an overview of the code for our app.
The code for the 2-dimensional affine Coxeter groups differs substantially from that for the
3-dimensional cases. This is in part because built-in graphics are available for the 2-dimensional
tesselations, but not the 3-dimensional ones.
Also, we display coroot lattice information in
dimension 2 but not in dimension 3, and our method of assigning colors to spherical directions
differs between these dimensions, as explained in Section 3.3. We are, however, able to obtain
reduced words and the translation parts and spherical directions for elements of conjugacy classes
and coconjugation sets using existing SageMath code for extended affine Weyl groups, in both
dimensions 2 and 3. In both dimensions, we build labels for alcoves by hand, in order to modify
the label font depending on the size of the individual alcoves.
For the 2-dimensional cases, we first find Euclidean coordinates for the simple coroots α∨

In 3 dimensions, we need to build the tesselations for our graphical output by hand. For this,

W0 on this alcove to obtain all alcoves containing the origin. We now take these finite Weyl

balance of the logic remains the same for the 3-dimensional cases as it does for the 2-dimensional
