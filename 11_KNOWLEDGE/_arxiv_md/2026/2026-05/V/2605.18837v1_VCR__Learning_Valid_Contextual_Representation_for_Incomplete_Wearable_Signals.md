Abstract
Wearable devices enable continuous health monitoring from multimodal signals,
but real-world deployment is hindered by limited labeled data and pervasive sensor
incompleteness. While large-scale self-supervised pretraining reduces label depen-
dence, most existing methods assume full modality availability. Current approaches
for handling modality missingness often reconstruct entire absent signals, which
can encourage hallucinating modality-specific details that are not inferable from
the observed sensor signals and degrade robustness. We propose VCR, a self-
supervised framework that learns to extract valid representations robust to modality
missingness. VCR employs an orthogonal tokenizer to enforce strict orthogonal
disentanglement by rectifying latent manifolds and applying a geometric projection,
separating each modality into shared semantics and modality-specific residuals.
This design preserves complete information integrity while serving as a structural
foundation for robust learning under modality missingness. The resulting tokens are
processed by a missing-aware mixture-of-experts backbone that adapts to varying
patterns of modality availability. By constraining the objective to reconstruct only
the inferable shared components of missing modalities, VCR effectively mitigates
hallucinations of non-inferable modality-specific details. Across multiple health
monitoring tasks, VCR consistently improves performance and robustness under
full, single-missing, and multiple-missing modality settings compared with strong
supervised and self-supervised baselines.

Introduction
Wearable devices enable continuous and non-invasive monitoring by capturing synchronized motion
and physiological signals such as photoplethysmography (PPG), electrodermal activity (EDA),
accelerometer (ACC), and skin temperature (TEMP). Beyond basic signal processing (e.g., estimating
heart rate from PPG), these modalities support a broad range of downstream health applications,
including emotion recognition [1, 2], sleep staging [3, 4], and activity recognition [5, 6]. However,
reliable generalization in real-world wearable deployments remains challenging due to two coupled
factors: (i) label scarcity across diverse tasks [7], and (ii) pervasive modality missingness 1 caused by
hardware constraints and user-dependent effects [8] (e.g., some wearables omit EDA/TEMP entirely,
users disabling PPG to save power, and motion/firmware issues can cause ACC streams to drop out).
Recent progress in self-supervised learning and foundation models [9, 10, 11] has made label-efficient
wearable intelligence increasingly feasible: models can be pretrained on large-scale unlabeled data
streams and transferred to downstream tasks with limited labeled data [8, 7]. However, most
existing models implicitly assume full modality availability during pretraining, thereby limiting
their real-world utility under heterogeneous modality missingness. While contrastive learning ap-
proaches [12, 13, 14] can handle missing modalities by aligning shared representations, their emphasis
on cross-modal commonality may suppress modality-specific cues. Conversely, missingness-aware

• Non-identifiability of Missing Modality-Specific Information. When a modality is absent, its

• Availability-Dependent Cross-Modal Dependencies. Different missing-modality patterns de-

architecture to model all modality combinations without interference.
• Information Loss Caused by Imbalanced Learning. Wearable modalities differ substantially in
sampling rate, variance, and semantic content. As a result, masked reconstruction can be dominated
by high-variance modalities and underutilize other modalities, while contrastive alignment can
overemphasize shared semantics and underrepresent modality-specific cues.
To address these obstacles, we propose VCR, a missingness-robust general-purpose framework
for multimodal wearable sensor data, pre-trained on raw PPG, EDA, ACC, TEMP signals. VCR
consists of two stages: an Orthogonal Tokenizer that separates each modality into shared and specific
components, and a Missing-Aware MoE Backbone that learns contextual representations under diverse
modality-availability patterns. Together, these components enable VCR to preserve information under
full observations while avoiding unsupported reconstruction when modalities are missing.
Our key contributions are as follows:
• Validity-Aware Learning: To address the non-identifiability of missing modality-specific informa-
tion, we propose an orthogonal tokenizer that rectifies encoder representations, applies whitening,
and uses a learned orthogonal projection to decompose each modality into modality-shared seman-
tics and modality-specific residuals. During structural missingness, our validity-aware objective
reconstructs only inferable shared components of absent modalities, thereby avoiding unsupported
modality-specific reconstruction and mitigating hallucination-like completions.
• Missing-Aware MoE Backbone: To handle availability-dependent cross-modal dependencies,
we introduce a sparse MoE backbone with learnable partition embeddings. This preserves modal-
ity/component identity and enables experts to specialize under different observed modality sets.
• Two-Stage Information Preservation: To mitigate imbalanced and conflicting SSL objectives, we
first train the tokenizer to preserve shared semantics via cross-modal contrast and specific details
via intra-modal reconstruction. We then train the backbone with masked latent reconstruction,
reducing domination by high-frequency or high-variance raw signals.
We evaluate VCR on a comprehensive benchmark spanning three classification tasks (i.e., emotion
detection, activity recognition, sleep staging), and one regression task (i.e., VO2 prediction). Across
these tasks as shown in Figure 1(a), VCR achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance under

Method
This section introduces VCR. We first formalize the problem and motivate disentanglement (Sec. 2.1),
then describe the overall framework (Sec. 2.2), the orthogonal tokenizer (Sec. 2.3), and the missing-
aware MoE backbone with training objectives (Sec. 2.4).
2.1
Preliminary
Problem Formalization. We consider multimodal wearable time-series data collected from M sens-
ing modalities, including PPG, EDA, ACC, and TEMP. After synchronization and non-overlapping
windowing, each sample is denoted as X = {xm}M
m . Here, xm ∈RTm×Cm denotes the sequence for
modality m, where Tm is the number of time steps (the product of window duration and sampling rate)
and Cm is the number of channels. In actual deployments, not all modalities are always available due
to heterogeneous device configurations, sensor failures, or user non-compliance. In this paper, a miss-
ing modality refers to the complete absence of an entire modality throughout the current window. We
formalize this by defining the observed and missing modality sets as Mobs and Mmiss, respectively,
where Mobs, Mmiss ⊆M = {1, . . . , M}. Accordingly, we denote the set of observed modalities as
Xobs = {xk|k ∈Mobs} and the set of missing modalities as Xmiss = {xk|k ∈Mmiss}. When
handling missingness, the model receives only Xobs from the current window.
Information Disentanglement Hypothesis. We assume the latent information of each modality
xm can be structurally disentangled into two components [18, 19]: a modal-shared component
sm that captures semantics correlated with other modalities (e.g., heart-rate-related patterns in PPG
may correlate with ACC micro-vibrations), and a modal-specific component pm that is unique
to m (e.g., skin impedance drift in EDA). By definition, pm contains the information that is not
reliably identifiable from other modalities. Accordingly, our framework encourages the disentangled
components to be statistically independent, i.e., I(sm; pm) = 0.
Hallucination under structural modality missingness. A reconstruction objective is well-posed
only when the target information is identifiable from the available context. Under our decompo-
sition, a modality signal is represented by (sm, pm). Standard masked autoencoding approaches
implicitly maximize the likelihood of reconstructing the full modality signal: log p(xm | Xobs) =
log p(sm, pm | Xobs). When modality m is structurally missing, the modal-specific component pm
is not identifiable from the observed modalities. Thus, reconstructing the full signal forces the model
to generate plausible but ungrounded modality-specific patterns, which we term hallucination.
2.2
Overview of the Framework
Figure 2 illustrates the proposed two-stage VCR framework for learning robust multimodal represen-
tations from wearable time-series under heterogeneous modality missingness. Given a windowed
sample with an availability pattern, VCR consists of two components: (i) an Orthogonal Tokenizer
that is pretrained on fully observed windows and decomposes each modality into modality-shared and
modality-specific factors, and (ii) a Missing-Aware MoE Backbone that receives only the tokens from
observed modalities and adapts computation to different observed modality sets. During backbone
pretraining, we use both random patch masking and structural masking, where 1–3 entire modalities
are removed at the sample level.
2.3
Orthogonal Tokenizer
Disentangling modal-shared and modal-specific features is a prerequisite for explicitly preserving
information integrity [18] and enabling selective reconstruction. Existing disentanglement methods
typically impose soft constraints (e.g., orthogonality penalties in loss functions) between the shared
and specific components [20]. Such an approach can lead to information leakage between the two
components, causing s to attempt to reconstruct p-related information.

this zero covariance implies statistical independence, i.e., I(s; p) ≈0. This guarantees that s contains
no information about p, effectively eliminating feature redundancy by construction.

populate s and p with semantic correctness and align the shared spaces across modalities. A single

force the model to generate specific details using other modalities. Conversely, applying contrastive
learning to p is detrimental, as standard data augmentations (e.g., scaling ACC data or adding noise
to TEMP) can destroy the physical semantics of motion and physiological signals. Therefore, we
adopt a hybrid strategy with three specific objectives.

2.4
Missing-Aware MoE Backbone
Architecture. Wearable sensors exhibit intrinsic physical and semantic entanglements. For example,
interpreting EDA activation often requires temperature signals to distinguish between physical activity
and stress. However, when the model learns to handle modality missingness, it tends to weaken
cross-modal dependencies. In traditional dense architectures, using a shared set of parameters to
learn both objectives leads to modality conflict, preventing the model from effectively capturing
cross-modal entanglements. To address this, we incorporate a MoE [23] block at the early layer
of the backbone. Each expert autonomously learns specialized combinations of modalities. When
certain modalities are missing, unaffected experts naturally gain greater influence, ensuring globally
optimal representations under the current conditions without compromising for the missing modality
patterns. Meanwhile, conventional approaches to modality missingness often rely on zero-padding or
special tokens, which introduce noise and degrade model performance. Instead, we discard missing
modalities to avoid such interference. To ensure the router is aware of each token’s modality origin,
we inject learnable partition embeddings that explicitly identify the semantic role (i.e., modality
identity and component type s vs. p) of each token. By inheriting the structural inductive bias from
the Orthogonal Tokenizer, this design simplifies the decision boundary for the router, significantly
reducing optimization difficulty and allowing the backbone to focus on robust inference.
Optimization Objective. The backbone is tasked with mining deep contextual semantics from the
tokenizer outputs and handling diverse missingness patterns. Given the difficulty of augmenting
human physiological signals, we employ masked reconstruction for self-supervised learning. However,
reconstructing the raw signals of missing modalities forces the model to predict modal-specific details
that are statistically unpredictable from the remaining context. To avoid this and mitigate modality
competition arising from variations in frequency and complexity, we perform masked reconstruction
on the latent embeddings [24, 25] (s and p) with two complementary masking strategies as the
optimization objectives.

available modalities. The model is required to reconstruct both the shared (s) and specific (p)

3
Experiments
3.1
Experiment Setup
Model Configuration. VCR uses a two-stage architecture (detailed in Appendix H) with 50.7M
parameters at inference: 16.94M in the orthogonal tokenizer and 33.74M in the missing-aware MoE
backbone. All datasets are collected using the widely deployed Empatica E4 wristband, which records
PPG at 64 Hz, EDA at 4 Hz, ACC at 32 Hz (3 axes), and TEMP at 4 Hz. We segment each stream
into non-overlapping 10 s windows and use a 1 s patch size.
Datasets and Tasks. We use publicly available datasets for pretraining and downstream evaluation,
with no subject or dataset overlap between the two stages. Pretraining uses three daily-life datasets:
BIG IDEAs [27], UE4W [28], and the non-cannabis-user subset of CAN-STRESS [29], totaling
3,565 hours from 56 subjects. Downstream evaluation includes WESAD [1] for 3-class emotion
classification (15 subjects), DaLiA [30] for 8-class activity classification (15 subjects), AAUWSS [31]
for 5-class sleep-stage classification (13 subjects), and WEEE [32] for VO2 prediction (17 subjects).
We adopted a subject-wise 5-fold cross-validation. Specifically, neither the baselines nor VCR
have been exposed to the modality missingness in the downstream benchmark. Supervised training,
fine-tuning, and linear probing are all conducted under the full-modal setting. During evaluation for a
specific missing modality pattern, the designated modalities are removed from all test samples.
Baselines and Metrics. We compare VCR with 3 supervised baselines and 7 pretrained baselines,
each configured with ∼50M parameters for capacity-matched comparison. The supervised baselines
include ResNet [33], ViT [34], and FlexMoE [16]. The pretrained baselines include SimCLR [22],
MSN [13], CMC [12], MF-CLR [35], and FOCAL [20], which are based on contrastive learning,
as well as LSM [8] and LSM-2 [15], which are masking-based methods specifically designed for
wearable signals. Among them, FlexMoE and LSM-2 are designed to handle modality-missing
scenarios. For evaluation, we employ Macro-F1, Balanced Accuracy (BAcc), and Cohen’s Kappa (κ)
for classification, and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) alongside R2 for regression. We report results
under two standard transfer settings. FT denotes supervised end-to-end fine-tuning of the pretrained
model on the downstream task. LP denotes linear probing, where the pretrained encoder is frozen,
and only a linear classifier/regressor is trained on top.

Experimental Results
Results on Full Modalities. Table 1 compares VCR with existing baselines when all modalities are
available. VCR consistently outperforms both supervised and pretrained baselines. For classification,
VCR improves macro-F1 by at least 0.097 on WESAD, 0.038 on AAUWSS, and 0.024 on DaLiA
over the best supervised baselines, and by at least 0.060, 0.006, and 0.002 on the respective datasets
over the best pretrained baselines. For regression, VCR reduces MAE by at least 0.406 and 1.105,
while improving R2 by 0.198 and 0.282 over the best supervised and pretrained baselines on WEEE,
respectively. These results indicate that VCR learns transferable representations across diverse
health monitoring tasks. Importantly, this full-modality performance indicates that robustness to
missingness does not come at the cost of representation quality when all sensors are available; instead,
preserving both modality-shared semantics and modality-specific residuals benefits downstream
performance even when all sensors are available.
Results under Single Modality Missing. Table 2 presents the model performance when each
modality is individually missing at test time across the four downstream tasks. Among the 32 primary
metric comparisons (i.e., Macro-F1 for classification and MAE for regression × 4 missing-modality
scenarios × 2 baseline types), VCR outperforms the baselines in 23 cases. Across all 88 total reported
metrics, VCR achieves the best score in 65 cases and the second-best in 20 cases. We highlight a
representative challenging setting where a critical sensor is missing. On DaLiA, removing ACC
substantially degrades activity recognition for competing methods, whereas VCR maintains F1 scores
of 0.416 (FT) and 0.428 (LP). Moreover, when ACC is missing, VCR ’s margin over supervised
baselines increases from 0.024 to 0.060, while its margin over pretrained baselines rises from 0.002
to 0.048, indicating that VCR degrades more gracefully when a key sensor is unavailable.
Results under Multiple Modality Missing. To evaluate robustness under severe sensor absence,
we test all cases where 2 or 3 modalities are simultaneously missing, as detailed in Appendix C.
Across 3 classification tasks (10 missing patterns and 30 evaluation settings), VCR ranks Top-1 in
20/30 and Top-2 in 25/30 against pretrained baselines, and Top-1 in 24/30 and Top-2 in 28/30 against

Ablation Studies.
We conduct three groups of ablations to assess the contribution of each design
choice. 1) replacing the structural disentanglement in the orthogonal tokenizer with a FOCAL-type
soft disentanglement based on orthogonal loss; 2) removing the missing-aware MoE backbone or
replacing the learnable partition embedding with a fixed sinusoidal encoding; and 3) modifying the
objective for structurally missing modalities from reconstructing only shared components to recon-
structing both or reconstructing neither of the shared and modality-specific components. Figure 5
reports the Macro-F1 drop of each ablated variant relative to the full VCR pipeline. Each ablation
causes a clear degradation across all three tasks and all four missing-modality settings (with only 1
exception among 60 metrics). Overall, the results suggest that soft disentanglement allows leakage
between shared and modality-specific factors, weakening information separation; the missing-aware
MoE backbone is necessary to adapt computation to heterogeneous modality availability, whereas
fixed encodings are insufficient to capture modality-dependent semantic relations (e.g., the semantic
affinity between EDA and TEMP may differ from that between EDA and ACC); and reconstructing
modality-specific components for missing modalities incentivizes the model to generate unobserved
content, increasing the risk of hallucination. Despite s being partially predictable from the ob-
served modalities, explicitly reconstructing s improves robustness relative to skipping reconstruction,
indicating a benefit from enforcing representation consistency.
Discussion and Limitations.
A limitation of this work is that our evaluation focuses on datasets
from a single wearable device (i.e., Empatica E4), which allows us to isolate the core challenge of
modality missingness but does not capture cross-device variability. While the proposed framework is
modality-agnostic and does not rely on specific sensors (e.g., PPG/EDA/ACC/TEMP), validating
cross-device and broader generalization remains an important direction for future work.
4
Conclusion
We presented VCR, a missingness-robust pretraining framework for learning valid representations
for multimodal wearable signals. VCR utilizes a geometric orthogonal tokenizer to structurally
decompose multimodal inputs into shared semantics and modality-specific residuals, which preserves
information integrity while providing a rigorous inductive bias. Together with a missing-aware MoE
backbone, VCR enables experts to adapt to diverse modality combinations without being forced to
hallucinate unobservable specific details. Overall, VCR demonstrates significant advantages and
strong generalization across multiple health monitoring tasks in both full-modality and modality-
missing scenarios. VCR paves the way for general-purpose pretraining under missing modalities
and highlights the importance of prioritizing representation validity, which is often overlooked when
learning from incomplete observations.

A
Related Works
Wearable / Physiological Foundation Models and Self-Supervised Pretraining. Self-supervised
pretraining on large-scale wearable signals has enabled general-purpose representations across
heterogeneous sensors [36, 8, 37]. Beyond wearable-specific pretraining, generic time-series SSL
frameworks [38, 35, 39, 40, 41] have demonstrated strong label efficiency and transferability across
diverse time-series domains, motivating the pursuit of general-purpose sensing representations. While
such models demonstrate broad transfer [42, 43, 44] (e.g., imputation and downstream health/activity
prediction), many formulations still train under fully observed inputs. While recent years have seen
notable progress in incomplete multimodal learning, many existing works are tailored for specific
tasks (e.g., recommendation systems [45] or sleep staging [46]), designed for particular modality
combinations with a dominant junction modality [47], or focus primarily on handling modality
missingness during training [48]. These approaches fall short of the goal of a modality-agnostic
representation learning framework that can be fine-tuned for various health-related downstream tasks
and effectively handle train-on-full, test-on-missing scenarios, especially in limited data settings.
LSM-2 [15] with Adaptive and Inherited Masking (AIM) explicitly trains on naturally incomplete
streams without pre-imputation, improving robustness to sensor dropouts. However, during training,
for a missing modality that is entirely dropped, LSM-2 learns by reconstructing the raw data, which
forces the model to generate unpredictable modality-specific details.
Supervised Methods for Missing Modalities. Recent routing/MoE architectures Flex-MoE [16] and
FuseMoE [17] improve subset-robust inference by conditioning computation on observed modalities.
MAESTRO [49] targets multimodal dynamic time series with arbitrary missing observations via
symbolic tokenization, sparse cross-modal attention, and sparse MoE routing. These methods are
difficult to transfer to new tasks, especially those with very limited labels [50, 51].
Shared-Specific Disentanglement, Orthogonality. Shared-specific factorization aims to sepa-
rate cross-domain semantics from domain-specific variation [52], with early formulations such as
DSN [18] combining task supervision, reconstruction, and soft separation constraints. In multimodal
SSL for sensing, FOCAL [20] factorizes each modality into shared and private embeddings and
encourages orthogonality while optimizing contrastive objectives. These methods rely on soft con-
straints, which fail to strictly disentangle the modalities and thus cannot prevent the model from
generating inherently unpredictable modality-specific information.

Hyperparameter Settings
For the loss weights tuning, we fix λalign = 1 and λrand = 1, so the main coefficients are: λrecon,
λwhite, and λstruct. For Tokenizer, λrecon / λalign controls the trade-off between reconstructability
and cross-modal shared semantics. If λrecon is too large, the model can offload part of the shared
information into the specific branch p, because reconstructing from p is easier to optimize than
aligning the shared branch s across modalities. This weakens the semantic content of s. This behavior
is consistent with our ablation in Appendix Table 8, where: λrecon / λalign = 0.1 performs best
across all three downstream tasks. We keep λwhite small (0.05) because it serves as a regularizer
for approximate whitening/independence rather than a primary task objective, and thus should not
dominate semantic learning. For Backbone, Lrand and Lstruct are both latent-space reconstruction
losses with matched form, so their scales are much more comparable than the tokenizer objectives.
They play complementary roles: Lrand learns general contextual semantics Lstruct explicitly trains
robustness to modality-level missingness Since both are essential in our setting, we use the balanced
default: λrand : λstruct = 1:1.

Compute Resources
All experiments, including pretraining and downstream evaluations, are conducted on a NVIDIA
RTX 5090 GPU (32GB VRAM), an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8470Q CPU (25 vCPUs), and 90GB
of system memory. The complete pretraining pipeline of our VCR framework requires approximately
11.67 GPU-hours (including both the orthogonal tokenizer and the missing-aware MoE backbone).
Taking into account the evaluation of baselines, cross-validation on multiple downstream tasks,
and failed experiments, the total computational budget of this research project is estimated to be
approximately 2400 GPU hours.
J
Broader Impacts
Positive Societal Impacts: The primary positive impact of VCR lies in advancing continuous,
non-invasive health monitoring. By significantly improving model robustness against real-world
modality missingness (e.g., sensor failure, user non-compliance, or hardware limitations), VCR
makes intelligent wearable health applications more reliable and accessible. This can potentially
lower the barrier to entry for digital health technologies, allowing users with cheaper or partial sensor
configurations to still benefit from high-quality physiological tracking. Furthermore, by relying
on self-supervised learning, VCR reduces the reliance on large-scale expert annotations, which are
notoriously expensive and time-consuming to obtain in the medical and healthcare domains.
Potential Negative Impacts and Risks: Wearable signals can infer highly sensitive personal states.
If misused, such robust tracking systems could be deployed for continuous surveillance by employers
or insurance companies without the user’s explicit consent, potentially leading to privacy violations.
