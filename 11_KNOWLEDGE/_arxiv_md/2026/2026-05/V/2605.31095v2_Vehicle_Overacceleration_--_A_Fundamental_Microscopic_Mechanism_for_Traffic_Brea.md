Vehicle Overacceleration –
A Fundamental Microscopic Mechanism for Traﬃc Breakdown Control Using
Automated Vehicles and AI
Boris S. Kerner
1
1 Physics of Transport and Traﬃc, University of Duisburg-Essen, 47048 Duisburg, Germany
This review article addresses a fundamental controversial question in traﬃc theory: Is the nucle-
ation character of traﬃc breakdown at a bottleneck governed by vehicle overdeceleration (overbrak-
ing) or by discontinuous vehicle acceleration, referred to as vehicle overacceleration.
A widely accepted view in traﬃc science attributes traﬃc breakdown to traﬃc instability induced
by vehicle braking, in which the speed of a braking vehicle becomes lower than that of its preced-
ing vehicle. Within standard traﬃc theories, such overdeceleration is assumed to generate traﬃc
instability, which in turn is regarded as the primary mechanism of traﬃc breakdown. Based on this
assumption, various traﬃc-management approaches, including jam absorption driving, have been
proposed to suppress traﬃc instability and thereby prevent congestion. In contrast, three-phase
traﬃc theory proposes a fundamentally diﬀerent mechanism. It suggests that traﬃc breakdown is
not caused by braking-induced overdeceleration leading to traﬃc instability, but rather by vehicle
overacceleration. Because the prevailing view has long associated traﬃc breakdown with braking-
induced instability, the hypothesis that overacceleration governs the nucleation of traﬃc breakdown
remains controversial. For this reason, in this review we analyze whether the empirical nucleation
nature of traﬃc breakdown at a bottleneck is caused by vehicle overdeceleration leading to traﬃc
instability or by vehicle overacceleration. This question is of particular importance in the context
of automated vehicles and AI, whose individual dynamic behavior can enable reliable strategies for
traﬃc breakdown control in the future. We show that, in both human-driven and automated traﬃc
ﬂow, traﬃc breakdown is governed by vehicle overacceleration rather than vehicle overdeceleration.
With this objective, in microscopic modeling we separate traﬃc breakdown caused by overacceler-
ation from traﬃc instabilities caused by overdeceleration due to braking behavior, while following
recent papers [Phys. Rev. E 108, 014302 (2023); 108, 064305 (2023); 112, 034309 (2025)].

I.
INTRODUCTION
A.
Importance of Traﬃc Breakdown Control
Traﬃc breakdown is a transition from free ﬂow to con-
gested traﬃc. Traﬃc breakdown occurs mostly at a bot-
tleneck. During traﬃc breakdown the average speed de-
creases and vehicle density increases. Travel time and en-
ergy consumption in congested traﬃc increase, whereas
traﬃc safety decreases.
For these reasons, there have
been developed diﬀerent theories for the explanation of
the nature of traﬃc breakdown as well as a huge num-
ber of various macroscopic and microscopic modeling ap-
proaches to traﬃc breakdown simulation and control at
the bottleneck (see for reviews [1–44]).
Examples of
macroscopic approaches to traﬃc breakdown control are
on-ramp metering (see, e.g., references in [45–48]) and
speed limit control (see, e.g., [48–55]). Microscopic ap-
proaches to traﬃc breakdown control are based on fea-
tures of microscopic vehicle characteristics that control
can prevent traﬃc breakdown.
In the age of automated vehicles and artiﬁcial intelli-
gence (AI), the importance of investigating which micro-
scopic vehicle features can be eﬀectively used for traﬃc
breakdown control has been increased considerably. It is
assumed that through an appropriate change in micro-
scopic behavior of automated vehicles, traﬃc breakdown
could be avoided.
Conversely, it is also assumed that
AI can be used to determine when and how this change
in the microscopic behavior of automated vehicles should
be applied to achieve the greatest possible eﬀect in main-
taining the free ﬂow of traﬃc at the bottleneck. For this
reason, in this review we limit the consideration on mi-
croscopic vehicle characteristics that control through au-
tomated vehicles and AI can prevent traﬃc breakdown
at the bottleneck.
B.
Purpose of the Review
This review article addresses a fundamental point of
contention in traﬃc theory:
– Is the nucleation character of traﬃc breakdown at
a bottleneck governed by vehicle overdeceleration
(overbraking) or by discontinuous vehicle accelera-
tion – termed “vehicle overacceleration”?
C.
Vehicle Overdeceleration (Overbraking), Traﬃc
Instabilities, and Jam Absorption Driving
Traﬃc instability was discovered in microscopic traf-
ﬁc simulations by Herman, Gazis, Montroll, Potts, and
Rothery [56–58] as well as Kometani and Sasaki [59–62]


## Page 3


3
at the end of 1950s.
Traﬃc instability occurs due to
driver’s delays, in particular, a driver reaction time that
can cause vehicle overdeceleration. Vehicle overdeceler-
ation is as follows:
The speed of the braking vehicle
becomes lower that the speed of the preceding vehicle.
If overdeceleration is realized for the following vehicles,
traﬃc instability, i.e., a growing speed wave of a local
speed decrease propagating upstream occurs in traﬃc
ﬂow.
Note that vehicle overdeceleration is also called
vehicle overbraking.
The idea to traﬃc instability that should explain traf-
ﬁc breakdown is used in the most microscopic traﬃc
ﬂow models of the standard traﬃc theory, in particu-
lar, well-known and widely used models by Newell [63],
Gipps [64, 65], Wiedemann [66], macroscopic models of
Payne [67, 68] and Aw-Rascle [69], cellular automation
(CA) model of Nagel and Schreckenberg [70], optimal
velocity model of Bando et al. [71–74], lattice traﬃc
ﬂow model of Nagatani [75, 76], intelligent driver model
(IDM) of Treiber [77], stochastic microscopic model of
Krauß [78, 79], and full velocity diﬀerence model of Jiang
et al. [80].
There are a huge number of other micro-
scopic and macroscopic traﬃc ﬂow models in which traf-
ﬁc breakdown is simulated through the classical traﬃc
instability caused by vehicle overdeceleration (see, e.g.,
papers [81–84] and reviews [6, 7, 13–15, 21, 25–28, 31–
33, 35–37]). In 1993, it was found [85] that the develop-
ment of classical traﬃc instability leads to the formation
of a moving jam (J) within the initially free ﬂow (F),
which is referred to as the F→J transition. The mov-
ing jam is an upstream propagating congestion pattern
spatially bounded by two jam fronts. Within the moving
jam, vehicle density is high, while speed, and thus the
ﬂow rate, can drop to zero. Sequences of moving jams
(called also stop-and-go traﬃc, or jammed ﬂow, or jams
or else traﬃc oscillations) have been observed in real traf-
ﬁc almost since the beginning of traﬃc research (see clas-
sical papers by Koshi, Edie, Treiterer et al. [86–92]) (see
empirical examples of sequences of moving jams, e.g., in
Figs. 2.13 and 2.14 of Sec. 2.6 of [48]). In simulations of
standard traﬃc models, where traﬃc breakdown at the
bottleneck is explained by traﬃc instability, the forma-
tion of moving jams during and after traﬃc breakdown
at the bottleneck exhibits complex spatiotemporal traﬃc
ﬂow dynamics [20, 21, 27, 33, 73, 93–97].
It is assumed that future vehicular traﬃc is mixed traf-
ﬁc that consists of human-driving and automated vehi-
cles (e.g., [98–146]). Traﬃc instability caused by vehicle
overdeceleration can also occur in traﬃc of automated
vehicles. This traﬃc instability called string instability
can occur in automated vehicle platoons even when a re-
action time of the automated vehicle can be considered
negligible (e.g., [98–104, 113, 115–117]). In this case, the
choice of dynamic coeﬃcients in the control of the au-
tomated vehicle determines whether the vehicle overde-
celerates and, therefore, string instability occurs or not.
It should be emphasized that for simulations of mixed
traﬃc usually standard traﬃc models for human-driving
vehicles are used (e.g., [118–146]).
Based on the above-mentioned classical works to the
theory of traﬃc instabilities leading to moving jam for-
mation, microscopic approaches to jam absorption driv-
ing (also referred to as stop-and-go wave dissipation,
stop-and-go wave suppression, mitigation of traﬃc os-
cillations, or shock wave damping) have been developed
(see, e.g., papers [147–158]); a history of the jam absorp-
tion driving can be found in a recent review of He et
al. [159]. It is assumed in these studies that the cause
of traﬃc breakdown is traﬃc instability resulting from
vehicle overdeceleration.
D.
Why Cannot Traﬃc Instability Be The Cause
of Empirical Traﬃc Breakdown?
Contrary to theoretical predictions of standard traﬃc
models of Sec. I C [6, 7, 13–15, 21, 25–28, 31–33, 35–
37, 73, 93, 95–97, 147–159], as shown in books [44, 48,
160, 161], the empirical traﬃc breakdown observed in
real ﬁeld traﬃc data is a transition from free ﬂow (F)
to synchronized ﬂow (F) (F→S transition), not moving
jam emergence. Synchronized ﬂow is a new traﬃc phase
of congested traﬃc introduced in the three-phase traﬃc
theory [44, 48, 160, 161]. Traﬃc breakdown (F→S tran-
sition) at the bottleneck exhibits the nucleation nature.
But what is the empirical nucleation nature of traf-
ﬁc breakdown (F→S transition) at the bottleneck? One
empirical example of the empirical nucleation nature of
traﬃc breakdown is presented in Fig. 1(a): A moving syn-
chronized ﬂow pattern (MSP) occurs at a downstream
oﬀ-ramp bottleneck B-down and it propagates further
upstream. When the MSP reaches the upstream on-ramp
bottleneck B, the MSP induces traﬃc breakdown (F→S
transition) at bottleneck B (Fig. 1(b), x = 17.0 km). Due
to the breakdown a localized synchronized ﬂow pattern
(LSP) is realized at bottleneck B (Fig. 1(b), x = 17.0 and
16.2 km).
Contrary to a moving jam, where the ﬂow rate is
signiﬁcantly lower than outside the jam, the ﬂow rate
within empirical synchronized ﬂow is nearly identical to
the ﬂow rate in free ﬂow outside the MSP and LSP. In
other words: No moving jams emerge within the MSP
(Fig. 1(b), x = 17.9 km) and no moving jams occur
within the LSP (Fig. 1(b), x = 16.2 km).
Therefore,
empirical traﬃc breakdown at a bottleneck is indeed the
F→S transition that exhibits the empirical nucleation na-
ture (Fig. 1(a))1.

of [48]), none of the standard traﬃc theories (see, e.g.,
papers [56–85, 93, 95–97, 172] and reviews [1–43]) can

How Does Three-Phase Traﬃc Theory Explain
The Cause of Empirical Traﬃc Breakdown?
The cause for the introduction of three-phase traﬃc
theory [165–170] was the empirical nucleation nature of
traﬃc breakdown (F→S transition).
The three-phase
traﬃc theory is the framework for the description of em-
pirical vehicular traﬃc in the three traﬃc phases 3,4:
1. Free ﬂow (F).
2. Synchronized ﬂow (S).
3. Wide moving jam (J).

Three-phase traﬃc theory follows exclusively from the
analysis of empirical spatiotemporal traﬃc data, not
from simulations of mathematical traﬃc models.
Through the analysis of real traﬃc data collected over
many years in various countries, the three-phase traﬃc
theory has established that there are common empiri-
cal spatiotemporal traﬃc phenomena that do not require
conﬁrmation through mathematical traﬃc models. One
of the most important of these empirical spatiotemporal
traﬃc phenomena is the following (see Chap. 5 of [44]
and Fig. 1(a)): Empirical traﬃc breakdown at a bottle-
neck is the F→S transition that exhibits the empirical
nucleation nature.
The three-phase traﬃc theory explains the common
empirical spatiotemporal traﬃc phenomena through a se-
ries of theoretical assumptions (hypotheses) that should
be conﬁrmed by mathematical traﬃc models [165–170].
One of the most important of the theoretical assumptions
of the three-phase traﬃc theory is the following:
– The empirical nucleation of traﬃc breakdown
(F→S transition) at the bottleneck is caused by a
discontinuous character of vehicle acceleration be-
haviors called by vehicle overacceleration.
F.
Overdeceleration versus Overacceleration –
The Fundamental Contradiction between Standard
and Three-Phase Traﬃc Theories
There is a widespread belief among traﬃc scientists
that traﬃc breakdown should be explained primarily by
features of vehicle braking leading to vehicle overdecel-
eration and, therefore, to traﬃc instability (Sec. I D). In
contrast to this conclusion of the standard traﬃc theories
(e.g., [3, 4, 6–8, 10–12, 14–17, 20–37, 41, 43]), three-phase
traﬃc theory proposes a fundamentally diﬀerent mecha-
nism [44, 48, 160, 161, 164]:
– Three-phase traﬃc theory assumes that traﬃc
breakdown is caused not by braking behavior lead-
ing to vehicle overdeceleration, but by a discontinu-
ous character of vehicle acceleration: The probabil-
ity of vehicle acceleration drops when due to traf-
ﬁc breakdown free ﬂow transforms to synchronized
ﬂow. In three-phase traﬃc theory, this type of ve-
hicle acceleration behavior is referred to as overac-
celeration.
Because of the long-standing belief that traﬃc breakdown
is driven by traﬃc instability due to overdeceleration, the
idea that overacceleration could be the underlying cause
for real traﬃc breakdown is diﬃcult for many researchers
to accept.
The concept of “discontinous overacceleration,” in-
tended to explain the empirical origins of traﬃc break-
down (F→S transition), was ﬁrst introduced in 1999
within the framework of three-phase traﬃc theory [165–
167]. At that time, instead of the term “probability of

diﬃcult to show that the empirical nucleation nature of
traﬃc breakdown can be explained exclusively by discon-
tinuous overacceleration, i.e., without any eﬀect of vehicle
overdeceleration.
G.
Methodology and Central Question to Be
Answered
Contrary to reviews [44, 48, 160, 161, 164], we use in
this review a diﬀerent methodology: We consider deter-
ministic three-phase traﬃc ﬂow models for both human-
driving and automated vehicles [200–202].
We choose
model parameters in the deterministic three-phase traﬃc
ﬂow models [200–202] at which no vehicle overdecelera-
tion is realized. This means that no classical traﬃc insta-
bility and no string instability is realized in the models.
The central question that we would like to answer in this
review is as follows:
– Can mathematical three-phase traﬃc models, in
which neither vehicle overdeceleration nor traﬃc in-
stability occur, nevertheless simulate the empirical
nucleation of traﬃc breakdown (F→S transition)
at the bottleneck exclusively through vehicle accel-
eration behaviors?
The absence of vehicle overdeceleration and, as a result,
the absence of traﬃc instabilities in the models allows us
to explain that and why overacceleration is a fundamen-
tal microscopic mechanism in traﬃc breakdown control
both for human-driving and automated vehicles.
The main contribution of this review is that with the
use of microscopic deterministic models of Ref. [200–
202] we show that the control of vehicle overacceleration,

The choice of the term “overacceleration” can be jus-

1. Discontinuous
overacceleration can

underlies the S→F instability [203], whose devel-

tually complementary to overdeceleration, while
representing the opposite eﬀect in terms of local
speed change.
Speciﬁcally, overdeceleration pro-
duces a growing wave of local speed decrease in
synchronized ﬂow, i.e., the S→J instability that
results in the emergence of moving jams.
Thus,
the term “overacceleration” should emphasize its
opposite eﬀect on synchronized ﬂow to the term
“overdeceleration”.
2. Among the many possible forms of vehicle accelera-
tion behavior, only those that exhibit a discontinu-
ity at the transition from free ﬂow to synchronized
ﬂow qualify as overacceleration. Thus, overaccel-
eration serves to distinguish acceleration behaviors
with such discontinuities from those that remain
continuous.
Taken literally, the term overacceleration suggests
“excessive acceleration,” which can lead to ambiguity.
As explained in Sec. II B, vehicle overacceleration is de-
ﬁned as that vehicle acceleration behavior that causes the
metastability of free ﬂow with respect to the F→S tran-
sition at the bottleneck. As we will show in this review,
there can be at least several diﬀerent vehicle acceleration
behaviors that can cause the metastability of free ﬂow
with respect to the F→S transition at the bottleneck.
D.
Behavioral Origin of Vehicle Overacceleration
– The behavioral origin of vehicle overacceleration is
related to the vehicle’s desire to move in free ﬂow.
Due to a bottleneck, a local speed decrease occurs
in free ﬂow at the bottleneck.
First assume that the
minimum speed within this local speed decrease is only
slightly lower than the free ﬂow speed upstream and
downstream of the bottleneck.
Consequently, drivers
moving upstream of the bottleneck who are approach-
ing such a minor local speed reduction are motivated to
decelerate as little as possible within this local speed re-
duction. In this case, the local speed decrease remains
to be localized at the bottleneck, i.e., no upstream prop-
agation of congested traﬃc is realized at the bottleneck.
The same behavior can be assumed for the dynamic be-
havior of automated vehicles approaching the local speed
decrease at the bottleneck.
Contrarily, when the minimum speed within the local
speed decrease at the bottleneck is low enough, drivers
see that they must brake sharply to avoid collisions
within the local speed reduction. In this case, the prob-
ability of vehicle acceleration within the local speed de-
crease is low enough. For this reason, upstream propaga-
tion of synchronized ﬂow (congested traﬃc) is realized at
the bottleneck, i.e., traﬃc breakdown occurs. The same
case can be assumed for the dynamic behavior of auto-
mated vehicles.
Empirical spatiotemporal traﬃc data collected at var-
ious bottlenecks suggest that the local speed decrease in
free ﬂow at the bottleneck, within which the minimum
speed is low enough to initiate traﬃc breakdown, can
be regarded as a “nucleus” for synchronized ﬂow at the
bottleneck. Indeed, when the minimum speed within the
nucleus is higher than some critical speed, the probabil-
ity of vehicle acceleration within the local speed decrease
is high enough. As a result, no traﬃc breakdown occurs
at the bottleneck. In the opposite case, when the mini-
mum speed within the nucleus is lower than the critical
speed, the probability of vehicle acceleration within the
local speed decrease is low. In this case, the growth of
the nucleus results in traﬃc breakdown (F→S transition)
at the bottleneck.
These assumptions about the behavior of vehicles mov-
ing through the local speed decrease at the bottleneck
explains the hypothesis about the discontinuous proba-

IV.
OVERACCELERATION THROUGH
SAFETY ACCELERATION IN THREE-PHASE
ACC (TPACC)
Dynamics rules of motion of an automated vehicle can
be chosen totally diﬀerent in comparison with dynam-
ics rules of motion of human-driving vehicles. However,
when the dynamic motion of the automated vehicle be-
comes unpredictable for human-driving vehicles, the au-
tomated vehicle could be perceived as an obstacle for
human-driving vehicles in mixed traﬃc. This can cause
the decrease in both traﬃc safety and highway capacity.
In particular, empirical traﬃc data show that, con-
trary to classical ACC-model (10), rather than control
a ﬁxed time headway, human-driving vehicles do not
control time headway within the indiﬀerent zone of car-
following (1) (Figs. 2 and 3). To make the motion of au-
tomated vehicles compatible with human-driving vehicles
in mixed traﬃc, the author has introduced a Three-Phase
ACC (TPACC) whose basic feature is the existence of the
indiﬀerent zone of car-following (1) in the rule of TPACC-
vehicles [205, 206]: Rather than a ﬁxed time-headway
of standard ACC-models, acceleration/deceleration of
TPACC-vehicle a does not depend on time-headway to
the preceding vehicle within the time-headway range (1).
As shown and explained in [206, 207], there are at least
the following advantages of TPACC-vehicles in compari-
son with ACC-vehicles described by Helly’s formula (10):
– No string instability:
Contrary to ACC-vehicles
with a ﬁxed time-headway (10), there is no string
instability in platoons of TPACC-vehicles. This is
due to the existence of the indiﬀerent zone for car-
following (Figs. 2 and 3).
– Reduction of the local speed decrease at the bot-
tleneck: The indiﬀerent zone for car-following in
TPACC-vehicles causes the reducing of the local
speed decrease at the bottleneck in mixed traﬃc.
– Reduction of the breakdown probability:
The in-
diﬀerent zone for car-following in TPACC-vehicles
causes the reducing of the breakdown probability
in mixed traﬃc.
The analysis of these advantages has been already done
in [206, 207].

The mechanism for overacceleration through vehicle
lane-changing on multi-lane roads, which was predicted
already in ﬁrst publications of three-phase traﬃc the-

of Sec. IV. Through simulations of automated vehicles
moving on two-lane road with the bottleneck we show

overacceleration through lane-changing with the mecha-
nism of overacceleration through safety acceleration stud-
ied for single-lane road in Sec. III.

choose parameters of automated vehicles at which no ve-

Controversial Views on the Origin of Traﬃc
Breakdown
However, the origin of traﬃc breakdown (Fig. 26(c)) is
understood qualitatively diﬀerent in standard traﬃc ﬂow
theory (see, e.g., for review [3, 4, 6–12, 14–17, 20, 21, 25–
37]) and three-phase traﬃc theory [44, 48, 160, 161]:
– Standard traﬃc ﬂow theory assumes that the origin
of traﬃc breakdown in the free ﬂow at the bottle-
neck lies in the traﬃc instability caused by vehicle
overdeceleration (for a review, see, e.g., [27]). The
development of traﬃc instability, in turn, leads to
the formation of moving traﬃc jams within the GP.
– In contrast, three-phase traﬃc theory assumes that
the origin of traﬃc breakdown at the bottleneck lies
in the F→S transition, which is caused by the inter-
play between speed adaptation and discontinuous
overacceleration, rather than by traﬃc instability.
The moving jams arise later in synchronized ﬂow
resulting from traﬃc breakdown, i.e., due to the
sequence of F→S→J transitions [44, 48, 160, 161].
In three-phase traﬃc theory [44, 48, 160, 161], the ex-
planation of the GP shown in Fig. 26(c) is as follows:
There is almost no time-delay between the time instant of
traﬃc breakdown t = T (B) and the beginning of moving
jam emergence. This means that the time-delay between
the F→S transition and S→J transition within the se-
quence of the F→S→J transitions presented in Fig. 26(c)
is not possible to distinguish: At the chosen model pa-
rameters of three-phase traﬃc model (21)–(23), (28),
(D1), and (D2), moving jams occurs due to overdecelera-
tion already in the emergent synchronized ﬂow. However,
as shown and explained in Sec. VIII C, the emergence of
this synchronized ﬂow caused by the F→S transition does
not depend on whether there is vehicle overdeceleration
or not.
F.
Resolution of Controversial Views Regarding
the Origin of Traﬃc Breakdown
The results of the consideration of traﬃc breakdown
leading to the emergence of moving jams made in
Secs. VIII B–VIII E are theoretical results derived from
simulations of traﬃc ﬂow models. The resolution of con-
troversial theoretical views on the origin of GP emergence
(Sec. VIII E) can be made only from analysis of empirical
spatiotemporal traﬃc data measured in real traﬃc.
Studies of empirical spatiotemporal data collected on
highways presented in the books [44, 48, 160, 161] has
shown that traﬃc breakdown is the F→S transition lead-
ing to the emergence of synchronized ﬂow. Moving jams
can emerge within this synchronized ﬂow, i.e., due to a
sequence of the F→S→J transitions: The empirical mov-
ing jams do not emerge spontaneously in empirical free
ﬂow at the bottleneck.
– Thus, the empirical origin of the traﬃc breakdown
– whose subsequent evolution leads to the emer-
gence of moving jams – is the F→S transition, and
not traﬃc instability due to vehicle overdecelera-
tion.
Moreover, there are a diverse variety of empirical spa-
tiotemporal traﬃc data measured in real traﬃc in which
no moving jam emergence is observed in synchronized
ﬂow resulting from the F→S transition. One of the ex-
amples of these data is presented in Fig. 1(a). None of the
standard traﬃc theories (see, e.g., books and reviews [1–
43]) can explain the empirical nucleation nature of the
F→S transition shown in Fig. 1(a).
– The common empirical feature of vehicular traﬃc
is the empirical nucleation nature of the F→S tran-
sition at the bottleneck, which cannot be explained
by the standard traﬃc theories.
Thus, the empirical nucleation nature the F→S tran-
sition cannot be explained by traﬃc instability caused
by vehicle overdeceleration. For this reason, approaches
for preventing traﬃc instability such as “jam absorption
driving” (see for review [159]) are not capable of prevent-
ing real traﬃc breakdown in free ﬂow. Contrary to traﬃc
instability, as we have shown in this review, the empirical
nucleation nature of traﬃc breakdown (F→S transition)
is explained by a competition of discontinuous overaccel-
eration with speed adaptation in free ﬂow:
– Vehicle overacceleration is the fundamental micro-
scopic mechanism for traﬃc breakdown control.

A.
Microscopic Overacceleration Management
Through Automated Vehicles and AI
As shown in this review, for the control of traﬃc break-
down at the bottleneck, automated vehicles should con-

acceleration and speed adaptation at the bottleneck, not
traﬃc instability. We deﬁne “microscopic overaccelera-

– Microscopic overacceleration management is the
control of the competition between discontinu-
ous overacceleration and speed adaptation together
with the control of speed disturbances at the bot-
tleneck.
– The objective of this microscopic overacceleration
management depends on the current traﬃc state at
the bottleneck:
(i) If free ﬂow still exists at the bottleneck, then
traﬃc breakdown (F→S transition) should be
prevented.
(ii) If synchronized ﬂow exists at the bottle-

can
be

Potential Cooperation of Overacceleration
Management and Jam Absorption Driving to
Restore Free Flow
As explained above, rather than traﬃc instability, the
cause of traﬃc breakdown is associated with a compe-
tition between discontinuous overacceleration with speed
adaptation. For this reason, approaches such as “jam ab-
sorption driving” (also referred to as dissipation of stop-
and-go waves, stop-and-go wave suppression, or shock-
wave damping), which try to prevent traﬃc instability,
are not capable for controlling traﬃc breakdown.
Nevertheless, we can assume that overacceleration
management in cooperation with jam absorption driv-
ing could be useful for the prevention of GP formation
(Fig. 25(b)) as follows. We assume that the use of over-
acceleration management has failed to prevent traﬃc
breakdown (F→S transition) at the bottleneck. Then,
as explained in Sec. VIII B, moving jams emerge in syn-
chronized ﬂow, i.e., the GP appears (Fig. 25(b)). Now,
through the application of jam absorption driving of au-
tomated vehicles, we try to dissolve moving jams in the
synchronized ﬂow.
In this case, the GP in Fig. 25(b)
transforms into the WSP shown in Fig. 25(a).
Later,
overacceleration management can be applied with the
aim of the initiating of the S→F instability at the bottle-
neck. Such a cooperation of jam absorption driving with
the following overacceleration management can be used
to initiate a return S→F transition at the bottleneck.

could represent very interesting tasks for future traﬃc
research.

E.
Why Do Empirical Microscopic Validations of
Standard Models Fail to Uncover Overacceleration
Mechanisms?
In this review, we have argued that vehicle overacceler-
ation is a fundamental microscopic characteristic govern-
ing traﬃc breakdown. A widely used methodology for
validating assumptions about driver behavior relies on
empirical vehicle trajectory data (see, e.g., [27, 214, 215]).
However, when such assumptions are implemented in
standard traﬃc ﬂow models, they fail to reproduce the
empirically observed nucleation nature of traﬃc break-
down (F→S transition) at bottlenecks (Fig. 1(a)).
One possible explanation is that discontinuous vehi-
cle overacceleration, as introduced in three-phase traﬃc
theory, makes no sense within the framework of stan-
dard traﬃc theories and models. This raises a fundamen-
tal question: which aspects of real traﬃc dynamics are
not captured by the prevailing methodology of validating
driver behavior solely through empirical trajectory data?
A qualitative explanation can be proposed as fol-
lows. Traﬃc is inherently a spatiotemporal phenomenon.
Therefore, prior to validating a traﬃc model using mi-
croscopic trajectory data, it is essential to verify whether
the model can reproduce empirical spatiotemporal traf-
ﬁc patterns. In particular, the model should be capable
of simulating the nucleation nature of traﬃc breakdown
(F→S transition) at bottlenecks. Only after such macro-
scopic and spatiotemporal consistency is established does
it become meaningful to use microscopic trajectory data
for parameter calibration. A comprehensive investigation
of this issue lies beyond the scope of this review but rep-
resents an important direction for future traﬃc research.
X.
CONCLUSIONS
1. The essential contribution of this review article is
that, by employing microscopic deterministic three-phase
traﬃc ﬂow models, we are able to demonstrate that the
key element in controlling traﬃc breakdown lies in the
control of vehicle overacceleration – and not, as assumed
in standard traﬃc theories and models, in the control of
traﬃc instability. The control of vehicle overacceleration
can be realized through the individual control of vehicle
motion. For this reason, vehicle overacceleration consti-
tutes a fundamental microscopic feature for the control
of traﬃc breakdown with the use of automated vehicles
and AI.
2. We have shown that the empirical nucleation nature
of traﬃc breakdown is indeed caused by the competition
of discontinous overacceleration with speed adaptation,
not by vehicle overdeceleration, i.e., not by traﬃc insta-
bility.
3. To conﬁrm this statement, we have considered sev-
eral microscopic three-phase traﬃc ﬂow models for auto-
mated and human-driving vehicles, in which traﬃc break-
down (F→S transition) at the bottleneck is realized ex-
clusively through vehicle overacceleration: In the models,
neither classical traﬃc instability nor string instability
can occur.
4. The nucleation nature of traﬃc breakdown (F→S
transition) at the bottleneck is qualitatively the same in
traﬃc with human-driving vehicles or in traﬃc with au-
tomated vehicles: The nucleation nature of traﬃc break-
down is caused by the interplay of discontinuous vehicle
overacceleration and vehicle speed adaptation.
5. Simulations of traﬃc consisting of automated vehi-
cles can reproduce empirical data of traﬃc with human-
driving vehicles, in which the MSP while propagating
upstream has induced the F→S transition at the bottle-
neck with the LSP emergence: As found in the empirical
data (Fig. 1(a)), in simulations of automated vehicles no
moving jams have been observed during the MSP prop-
agation as well as during and after the traﬃc breakdown
(Figs. 23 and 24).
