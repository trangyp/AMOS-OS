empirical flexibility. Classical mechanistic models (Stro-
gatz, 2015) provide identifiable rate constants but depend on
assumptions — well-mixed populations, linear functional
responses, absence of unmodeled dynamics — that are rou-

Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018) parameterize the instan-

well, but on held-out initial conditions it produces artificial
spirals where the true dynamics admit conservative closed

This paper investigates two complementary remedies and
their combination. Soft physics-informed regularization
(Raissi et al., 2017; 2019) steers the learned vector field
toward physically plausible behavior at sampled collocation
points. Multiple-Initial-Condition multiple-shooting (Bock
& Plitt, 1984; Turan & J¨aschke, 2022) broadens phase-space
coverage and enforces continuity of the learned flow across
segments. A central methodological observation is that a
single scalar metric — such as validation MSE — does
not reveal whether a learned model has recovered the true
dynamics or merely fit observed trajectories. We evaluate
every method on three complementary axes:
1. Out-of-sample (OOS) prediction error — accuracy
on held-out initial conditions in the training distribu-

2. Long-horizon stability — accumulated error over mul-

3. Drift in the conserved Hamiltonian H(x, y) — a
trajectory-independent measure of whether the learned
dynamics respect the geometric structure of phase
space.

2. Related Work
Neural ODEs and variants. Neural ODEs (Chen et al.,
2018) parameterize a continuous-time vector field with a
neural network. Latent ODEs (Rubanova et al., 2019), Aug-
mented Neural ODEs (Dupont et al., 2019), and Neural Con-
trolled Differential Equations (Kidger et al., 2020) address
architectural limitations (partial observability, expressivity,
exogenous control) but not global consistency across initial
conditions, which is our target.
Physics-informed losses. Physics-Informed Neural Net-
works (Raissi et al., 2017; 2019) enforce soft constraints
through collocation sampling but can require extensive
sampling and struggle with long-horizon prediction (Kr-
ishnapriyan et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2021). We integrate
the PINN idea into the Neural ODE framework, reducing
collocation requirements by sampling along the integrated
trajectory rather than over a phase-space grid.
Multiple shooting. The direct multiple-shooting method
originated in numerical optimal control (Bock & Plitt,
1984) to overcome the phase-mismatch pathology of single-
shooting on long-horizon oscillatory systems. Turan &
J¨aschke (2022) first applied this idea to neural differential
equations, showing it can rescue the “flattened-out” trajecto-
ries single-shooting produces on oscillatory time series. Our
MIC mechanism is a multiple-shooting curriculum in that
lineage, augmented with a freshly-sampled initial-condition
distribution each epoch and combined with a PINN-style
residual that these works do not address.
Structure-preserving networks. Hamiltonian Neural Net-
works (Greydanus et al., 2019), related Hamiltonian-system
identification (Bertalan et al., 2019), and symplectic net-
works (Jin et al., 2020) build conservation laws into the
architecture itself; this approach is orthogonal to and poten-
tially combinable with ours.
Mechanistic-neural hybrids. Universal Differential Equa-
tions (Rackauckas et al., 2020) embed a mechanistic model

Data generation. Ground-truth trajectories are integrated

3.2. Phase-1 Setup: Strongest Neural-ODE
Configuration
Three implementation choices — network architecture, tem-
poral sampling, and positivity wrapper — are decided up
front by a sequential ablation against a composite metric
(log-space mean of in-sample, OOS, and long-horizon MSE)
using only the baseline Neural ODE variant. This decouples
them from any physics-loss or MIC effect. The empirical
winners are summarized in Table 1; per-variant diagnostics
are in Sections B to D.
The three choices jointly define what we call the strongest
Neural-ODE configuration we identified for this benchmark.
Every method in this paper — LV NN, LV PINN, LV MIC,
MPINeuralODE, and LV Structured — adopts these choices.
This is deliberate: by fixing the implementation details to the
best Neural-ODE setup we found, any observed difference
between methods reflects only the algorithmic ingredient
under test (physics loss, MIC curriculum, or both), not a
confounding hyperparameter choice.
Temporal sampling.
We compared the full-window
scheme (each sampled trajectory integrated over [0, ttrain]),
an expanding-window curriculum (Bengio et al., 2009) that
grows the horizon over the first 75% of training, and slid-
ing windows of fixed length 6.0 at random offsets. The
full-window scheme achieves the lowest composite met-
ric: paying the long-horizon cost from epoch one finds a
vector field consistent over the full horizon throughout train-
ing, whereas the curriculum variants leave the long-horizon
regime under-trained when they finally expose it.
Positivity wrapper.
LV populations satisfy x, y > 0, but
the learned vector field has no built-in reason to respect this.
We evaluated four wrappers on the raw output r of the vector-
field network: none (identity), tanh-bound (18 tanh(r/18),
a smooth saturating function), squared (r |r|), and clamp
(clip(r, −20, 20)). Clamp and none tie on the composite
metric to ten digits (Section D): at width 128 the raw out-
put rarely approaches the clamp bound, so clamp acts as
the identity on most forward passes. We adopt clamp for
principled safety — identical expected behavior, with a de-
terministic safety net against the rare blow-up that none
cannot prevent. The tanh-bound and squared wrappers un-
derperform either by saturating useful signal (tanh-bound)
or distorting the derivative scale near zero (squared, where
∂(r|r|)/∂r = 2|r| vanishes at the origin).

3.4. Intermediate Extensions: PINN and MIC
We evaluate each ingredient of MPINeuralODE in isolation
under the Phase-1 configuration of Section 3.2, so observed
differences isolate the algorithmic contribution.
LV PINN.
Augments the data loss with a soft physics
term that penalizes deviation of the learned vector field from
the known fLV, evaluated symmetrically on states the model
visits and corresponding ground-truth states. Because the
residual is only applied where samples exist, its effect is con-
centrated in whatever corridor of phase space the integrated
rollouts actually traverse during training. Mechanistically,
the residual directly shapes fθ(z) to approximate the true
fLV(z) pointwise, so orbits inherit the physical structure of
the true system — closed orbits, correct period, conserva-
tion of H — wherever the trajectory visits. Outside that
visited corridor, fθ remains free to drift.
LV MIC.
Draws a larger batch of m > n initial condi-
tions per epoch from the mixed sampler of Section 3.1, then
splits each trajectory into K = 4 segments and applies the
direct multiple-shooting method (Bock & Plitt, 1984) along
the time axis. Segment k > 0 starts from the detached
predicted endpoint of segment k −1; a continuity penalty
pulls the predicted endpoint of segment k −1 toward the
true state at the next segment’s boundary, which is the soft-
penalty form of the multiple-shooting continuity constraint
advocated by Turan & J¨aschke (2022) for neural differential
equations. Mechanistically, the broader IC sampler exposes
the model to many orbit families and the continuity penalty
enforces that learned trajectories behave like an autonomous
flow whose segments concatenate at ground truth. This is
what keeps trajectories inside the positive orthant (the IC
sampler covers it densely) and preserves the closed-orbit
topology. What MIC alone does not provide is an inductive
bias on the absolute magnitude of fθ: orbits can still rotate
at the wrong rate, producing the mild spiraling visible in

3.5. Universal Differential Equation Oracle
We implement a Universal Differential Equation (Rack-
auckas et al., 2020) as an oracle reference. In its general
form the UDE combines a mechanistic backbone with a
neural residual, dz/dt = fphys(z) + fNN(z). In our setting
fphys is taken to be exactly the Lotka–Volterra functional
form, with only the four scalar rate constants (α, β, γ, δ)
to be learned; the neural residual is dropped because with
the correct functional form supplied it is identically zero.
The remaining four-parameter problem is well-posed un-
der a collocation loss (matching fθ(z) ≈fLV(z) pointwise
on ground-truth states) and recovers the true parameters to
∼10−6 relative error.
Parameter-count asymmetry. LV Structured therefore has
only 4 learnable parameters, against ∼67k for the neural
methods. This is not a fair-comparison issue: the structured
model contains the true LV mechanism (the underlying dif-
ferential equations) a priori, so it carries a substantial learn-
ing advantage over methods that must learn the entire vector
field from data. The purpose of including LV Structured is
to provide a ceiling on what is achievable when accurate
mechanistic knowledge is fully available, not a competing
data-driven method. A real practitioner does not know the

The in-sample inversion is a regularization tradeoff.
LV NN attains in-sample MSE 1.31 on the typical-regime
test ICs, against 2.68–3.20 for the regularized variants. This
inversion reflects capacity allocation: LV NN trains on
32 ICs from the typical regime alone and concentrates
its representational capacity there, while LV MIC and
MPINeuralODE spread 128 ICs across the much broader
log-uniform sampler that also covers the edge regime, low-
ering per-IC concentration in exchange for substantially
better OOS coverage (see Section F). Similarly, the physics
constraint pulls fθ toward fLV pointwise, trading a small
amount of trajectory MSE for a vector field closer to truth.
A model that minimized in-sample MSE alone would neces-
sarily lose this trade.
The structured ceiling is qualitative. LV Structured sits
six orders of magnitude below the neural methods on tra-
jectory error and three orders below on Hamiltonian drift.
The qualitative gap that MPINeuralODE closes is not the
trajectory-error gap (which the oracle’s mechanistic prior
makes essentially unbridgeable for purely data-driven meth-
ods) but the phase-space-structure gap: MPINeuralODE
recovers closed-orbit topology and bounded Hamiltonian
drift, while LV NN spirals and dissipates.

Figure 5 shows validation-MSE training curves for the five
methods on the same axis. Two patterns are worth flagging.
First, the four neural methods plateau at qualitatively the
same single-trajectory level (∼101 on this axis), which
is the visual counterpart to the small in-sample MSE dif-

separates these methods. Second, LV Structured descends
through several orders of magnitude over ∼2500 epochs
because its collocation loss directly minimizes the residual
against the true fLV, an objective the other methods cannot
reach because they must also fit a 67k-parameter network
from data. The plateau gap is therefore the visual diagnos-
tic for “oracle vs. data-driven” rather than “good vs. bad
data-driven”.

4.4. Long-Horizon Stability and Hamiltonian Drift
Figure 7 consolidates the long-horizon and Hamiltonian
axes. LV NN accumulates error rapidly within the first
oscillation period; LV PINN delays this growth but still
drifts (the physics residual is enforced only on the narrow

inherits the local-accuracy benefit of the physics residual
on top of the broader support, and is the most stable of
the four neural methods over the full horizon. The relative
Hamiltonian drift mirrors this pattern: LV NN’s H oscilla-

LV MIC offers a smaller improvement by visiting more

4.5. Summary: Why Three Axes Matter
Putting the three axes together:
• Physics regularization (LV NN →LV PINN) reduces
OOS MSE by ∼19% and Hamiltonian drift by ∼13%.
It is the largest single-ingredient contribution.
• MIC multiple-shooting (LV NN →LV MIC) reduces
OOS MSE by ∼22%, slightly better than PINN-only
on this axis, but matches PINN on Hamiltonian drift
only approximately. It contributes to the breadth of
phase-space coverage rather than direct constraint on
fθ.
• MPINeuralODE achieves OOS MSE 5.3% better than
MIC-only and 8.7% better than PINN-only, while es-
sentially tying PINN on Hamiltonian drift. No axis is
worse than either ingredient alone.
The methodological takeaway is that validation MSE alone
understates the value of combining these ingredients: the
largest qualitative gains — closed-orbit topology, stable
long-horizon integration, bounded conservation drift — are
visible only when all three axes are reported together. For
applications in which the learned model must extrapolate
to new initial conditions, integrate over many cycles, or
preserve known invariants, the case for combining physics-
informed regularization with multiple-IC training is much
stronger than any single metric admits.
4.6. Discussion: When to Use Which Method
The three-axis report enables concrete advice on when each
method in our comparison is the right choice. LV NN
(vanilla Neural ODE) wins only on the narrow in-sample
trajectory-error axis, and only because it concentrates a
fixed capacity on a single typical-regime distribution. Use it
when the deployment distribution is identical to the training
distribution and short-horizon prediction is the only objec-
tive — e.g. smoothing observed trajectories in a controlled
experimental setting. Do not use it when the model will be
asked to extrapolate to new initial conditions or integrate
over many oscillation cycles: the spirals in Figure 1(b) are
not a freak training failure but a generic consequence of
having no inductive bias outside the visited corridor.
LV PINN is the right choice when the dominant evaluation
metric is conservation-of-invariants and the deployment dis-
tribution sits near the training corridor. Its physics residual
is the single largest contributor to Hamiltonian fidelity on
our benchmark, and at 0.940 relative drift it is statistically in-
distinguishable from the combined MPINeuralODE model.
The qualification is that “near the training corridor” is a
strong condition: the residual is a Monte-Carlo estimator
and its support is the same narrow set of states the integrated
rollouts visit, so the conservation guarantee does not extend
to far-out-of-distribution initial conditions.
LV MIC is the right choice when the dominant evaluation
metric is broad phase-space coverage and partial physics
knowledge is unavailable. Its multiple-shooting curriculum
is what gives the learned vector field the breadth that the
physics residual then sharpens; with λphys = 0 the method
still recovers the closed-orbit topology of Figure 2(b), trad-
ing some Hamiltonian fidelity for the ability to operate with-
out an assumed fphys.

5. Conclusion
We introduced MPINeuralODE, a Neural ODE frame-
work that combines a soft physics-informed residual with
a Multiple-Initial-Condition multiple-shooting curriculum.
The case for combining the two is structural rather than
additive: each ingredient’s blind spot is exactly the regime
the other constrains. On Lotka–Volterra, MPINeuralODE
achieves the lowest OOS and long-horizon error among
purely data-driven methods while essentially matching the
PINN ablation on Hamiltonian drift, and is the most consis-
tently strong method across the three axes simultaneously.
In the typical practitioner regime — partial mechanistic
knowledge, limited data coverage, and a desire for long-
horizon extrapolation — this makes MPINeuralODE the
most practical surrogate among the methods we evaluated.
Generality beyond Lotka–Volterra. The construction is
system-agnostic: any dynamical system for which an ap-
proximate fphys can be written fits into the same training
and evaluation pipeline, with a conserved quantity required
only for the Hamiltonian drift axis.
The framework applies to systems satisfying four conditions:
(i) autonomous or weakly time-driven, so the Neural-ODE
adjoint is well-posed; (ii) continuous in state, so the adap-
tive integrator does not encounter switching events; (iii)
smooth enough for adaptive Runge–Kutta integration, so
stiff regions do not exhaust the time budget; and (iv) at least
partial mechanistic knowledge expressible as fphys. When
no partial physics is available the method still applies with

Impact Statement
This paper advances scientific machine learning by improv-
ing the ability of Neural ODE models to learn dynamical sys-
tems that generalize across initial conditions, remain stable
over long prediction horizons, and respect known conserva-
tion laws. Direct applications include ecological forecasting,
chemical kinetics modeling, epidemic spread modeling, and
any setting where partial physical knowledge is combined
with observational time-series data. The method encodes
physical structure as a soft constraint that the learner can
override when data contradicts the assumed physics; this is
a feature for scientific exploration but a risk in safety-critical
applications, where mis-specified physics could silently bias
forecasts. Practitioners deploying MPINeuralODE in such
settings should report the three evaluation axes proposed
here — out-of-sample accuracy, long-horizon stability, and
conservation-law fidelity — rather than a single trajectory-
error scalar, so that silent geometric failures of the learned
vector field are visible to auditors. There are many other
potential societal consequences of work in this area, none
of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

F. In-Sample Performance and the Regularization Tradeoff
Table 2 in the main text reports that LV NN has the lowest in-sample MSE while MPINeuralODE has the highest among the
four neural methods. At first glance this is counterintuitive: MPINeuralODE is the most accurate method on every other
axis. The pattern reflects a deliberate regularization tradeoff and is not a sign of a failed training run.
Capacity allocation. LV NN trains on 32 initial conditions per epoch, drawn uniformly from the typical regime [0.1, 10.0]2.
The model concentrates its capacity on this narrow distribution. LV MIC and MPINeuralODE train on 128 ICs per epoch
with the mixed sampler that also covers the log-uniform edge regime [10−3, 10.0]2, spreading the same capacity across a
broader phase-space support.
Physics constraint. LV PINN and MPINeuralODE are pulled by λphys Lphys toward the true fLV pointwise. When the bare
data fit and the physics constraint disagree on a particular trajectory, the model trades a small amount of trajectory MSE for
a vector field closer to truth. This is the regularization-vs-fit tradeoff, visible as the in-sample MSE gap between LV NN
(1.31) and LV PINN (2.68).
Long-horizon integration of in-sample ICs. The “in-sample” metric integrates each held-out IC over the full t ∈[0, 30]
horizon (∼5.85 oscillation cycles). Any small vector-field error compounds over five cycles, so an in-sample MSE of 3
corresponds to an RMSE of ∼1.7 in population space. All four neural methods produce visually plausible trajectories on
the in-sample plots; the differences are most visible in the late-time phase relative to ground truth.
A reader interested in raw in-sample fit on a narrow training distribution should use LV NN. A reader who wants a model
that generalizes, stays stable over long horizons, and respects known conservation laws should use MPINeuralODE. The
three-axis report exposes this choice transparently in a way that a single trajectory-MSE scalar would not.
