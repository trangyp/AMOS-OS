Abstract
The 3GPP Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X) resource allocation framework defines two entity classes — the base
station and the vehicle User Equipment (UE) — and four modes across Long-Term Evolution (LTE) and New
Radio (NR) generations. We demonstrate that this binary taxonomy is structurally incomplete. Base station-led
scheduling saturates at high-density traffic nodes, producing latency-tail failures that persist even when mean
packet delivery ratios approach the service-class target. UE autonomy is categorically incapable of pre-emergence
warning for occluded traffic participants and insufficient for large-scope cascading environmental hazards. We
propose Mode 0, a new 3GPP V2X category whose defining entity is the Roadside Computing Unit (RCU) —
an infrastructure ensemble integrating elevated sensing (Seeing), sidelink communication (Speaking), and local
computational evaluation (Thinking), owned by traffic management authorities. Mode 0 defines a subfamily spectrum
from Mode 0a (all-passive UEs, the guaranteed minimum) through Mode 0c (all-active UEs, the optimal target).
Convergent deployment evidence from Chinese national standards (DB11/T 2329.1-2024, T/ITS 0224.1-2025), China
Unicom RS-MEC infrastructure, and European and US C-V2X programs confirms that both institutional sides are
converging on the roadside traffic node without a coordination standard. A fifteen-run Multi-Agent Proximal Policy
Optimization (MAPPO) simulation validates the architectural family: Mode 0a in shared-pool baseline sits at the
analytical symmetric-Nash coordination floor; Mode 0c with demand separation achieves strict Pareto improvement
for both traffic classes (M0 PDR 0.999, M1 PDR 0.998 at ρpool ≤1) and lifts the worst-Transmission Time Interval
(TTI) delivery ratio from near-zero to 0.601 — the only configuration satisfying the latency safety requirement
structurally. We call for a 3GPP study item on Mode 0 within the NR-V2X sidelink enhancement work programme.

II. LIMITATIONS OF THE EXISTING 3GPP V2X RESOURCE ALLOCATION FRAMEWORK
A. The Existing Mode Taxonomy
The 3GPP V2X framework has produced four modes across two generations [2]. Mode 1 (LTE) and Mode 3
(NR) assign resource scheduling authority to the eNB/gNB respectively. Mode 2 (LTE) and Mode 4 (NR) enable
vehicle UEs to select resources autonomously from pre-configured pools, with Mode 4 incorporating an enhanced
sensing-based mechanism. Despite the evolution from LTE to NR and the introduction of Mode 2 subfamilies (2a–
2d), across a growing range of V2X safety and traffic efficiency applications [6], the structural logic is invariant:
every defined mode reduces to centralized base station authority or distributed UE autonomy. Both patterns fail
under the conditions that define the most demanding V2X safety scenarios.
B. Resource Contention and the Structural Failure of Base Station-Led Scheduling
European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) Intelligent Transportation System (ITS) and Society of
Automotive Engineers (SAE) specifications require cooperative awareness transmission at 1–10 Hz per vehicle [7],
[8], with the worst-case hazard warning latency bound of 20 ms derived from 3GPP TR 22.885 [1]. At a signalized
urban intersection, vehicle density within a 300-metre radius routinely exceeds 100 vehicles. When 100 vehicles
simultaneously require scheduling grants for 10 Hz Cooperative Awareness Message (CAM) transmission, the gNB
faces 1,000 grant cycles per second from vehicular traffic alone — before accounting for pedestrian and general
cellular traffic in the same coverage zone. The 5QI priority framework provides QoS differentiation, but priority
scheduling cannot create capacity that does not physically exist. Real-world ITS-G5 (ETSI ITS G5 radio standard)
channel load simulations at deployed Cooperative ITS (C-ITS) sites confirm that as V2X penetration increases, the
channel busy ratio approaches saturation in ways that degrade safety message delivery [9].
It is worth distinguishing two fundamentally different failure modes that resource expansion alone cannot resolve.
The first is capacity saturation — insufficient aggregate bandwidth for the offered load — which 3GPP’s ongoing
resource evolution directly addresses: flexible numerology (NR µ = 0–3, slot durations 1 ms down to 0.125 ms),
mini-slots, Massive MIMO, and carrier aggregation all raise the per-unit-time resource ceiling. The second is
coordination failure: in specific Transmission Time Intervals (TTIs), safety-critical transmissions across all vehicles
may simultaneously select conflicting channel resources because the peer-equal allocation mechanism cannot break
intra-class symmetry, regardless of how many channel resources are available. The Phase B simulation finding —
that the worst-TTI delivery tail remains near zero across a 3.3× supply range (M ∈{3, 5, 7, 10} at N = 4, ρ from
1.33 to 0.40) — is precisely this second failure mode. The tail is invariant to supply because it is not caused by
insufficient resources but by the allocation mechanism’s structural inability to guarantee distinct channel resource
assignments to all safety-critical vehicles in every TTI. 3GPP’s resource evolution across Releases 15 through 19
raises the capacity ceiling. Mode 0 establishes a delivery floor.
The deeper problem is structural coupling: M0 safety-critical traffic and M1 entertainment and non-safety traffic
— including navigation updates, over-the-air (OTA) software downloads, and general cellular data — share the
same physical resources under the same scheduler on the same hardware. This coupling cannot be severed by QoS
policies, only moderated. The gNB optimizes for aggregate performance across all subscribers; safety message
scheduling is a minority use case in this optimization context.
C. UE Autonomy, Global Blindness, and the Categorical Gap
Mode 2/4 avoids base station contention by removing infrastructure from the scheduling loop. Under moderate
density conditions it performs well. Its fundamental limitation is categorical, not algorithmic: the Mode 2 family is
built on peer equality. R1-1812399 states explicitly that “the group leader UE is by no means a different class or
category of UEs, as all UEs are expected to have the capability to be a group leader” [10]. This characterization
applies even to Mode 2d, the most behaviorally specialized variant of the Mode 2 subfamily: enhanced scheduling

This peer equality becomes a safety failure in occluded scenarios. In the ghost peeking scenario — a leading

The RCU addresses this through a three-layer information architecture structurally inaccessible to peer UEs.
Its elevated sensors perform two distinct broadcast functions. First, primary proactive broadcast: elevated sensors
independently detect occluded traffic participants before any UE report exists, enabling hazard warnings that the UE
population cannot generate at all. Second, secondary coverage broadcast: when a UE-originated warning has been
transmitted via sidelink, the RCU assesses coverage adequacy across the zone and re-broadcasts at infrastructure-
grade power where gaps exist. This secondary coverage function is partial — as vehicle density increases, indirect
occlusion zones for elevated sensors also grow — but extends safety margins substantially beyond what peer-to-peer
transmission achieves. Its aggregation of all zone Basic Safety Messages (BSMs) builds a complete kinematic picture
no individual UE can construct. This categorical distinction — not a performance improvement but a structural
capability — is the core argument for a new entity class.
III. THE CASE FOR MODE 0: WHY A NEW CATEGORY IS NECESSARY
A. The Compute Sinking Argument and Traffic Risk Classification
The failure modes of Section II share a common structural origin: scheduling decision authority is too far —
architecturally, institutionally, and physically — from the safety-critical events it must serve. The operator edge cloud
reduces geographic distance to the base station but does not eliminate the multi-hop path: vehicle sensing event →
uplink →base station →edge cloud →scheduling decision →downlink →vehicle transmission. Worst-case latency
is dominated by hop-count variability, not average latency. The roadside boundary eliminates all intermediate hops:
the RCU is co-located with the sensors, the vehicles, and the communication resources. Its scheduling advisory is
a single local hop with deterministic latency bounds.
The institutional dimension is equally decisive. The operator edge cloud is owned and optimized by the network
operator, whose mandate is aggregate network performance across all subscribers. The RCU is owned and optimized
by the traffic management authority, whose sole mandate is traffic safety and flow. Placing safety-critical scheduling
within operator infrastructure means safety optimization always competes with throughput objectives. The RCU
resolves this by institutional design.
A deeper examination of the traffic safety scenarios that Mode 0 must serve reveals an additional argument
organized around a three-class risk taxonomy.
Category A — Small-scope bilateral contact risk (vehicle-vehicle, vehicle-pedestrian, ghost peeking): both
parties are active traffic participants. The RCU’s elevated Seeing detects the risk before any road-level sensor can;
its advisory architecture is sufficient for prevention and, if prevention fails, for post-incident documentation and
emergency coordination.
Category B — Small-scope unilateral contact risk (road debris, stalled vehicle not blocking the full lane): one
party is a passive environmental object. The RCU’s advisory architecture is again sufficient — elevated sensing
detects the hazard, warnings are broadcast to approaching vehicles.
Category C — Large-scope cascading contact risk is categorically different. Environmental hazards — road
surface damage in a blind approach corridor, sudden dense fog at an unmonitored section, flash freezing on an
elevated interchange — create conditions where each moment without halting approaching traffic produces a new
secondary incident. Neither the eNB/gNB (lacking zone-specific hazard awareness and traffic authority mandate) nor
any collection of vehicle UEs (lacking global zone awareness and coverage verification) can halt the cascade. The
RCU possesses all four necessary capabilities simultaneously: independent elevated sensing, global zone awareness,
zone-wide communication authority, and an institutional position within the traffic safety mandate.

B. The M0/M1 Information Classification and Demand Separation
The resource contention argument of Section II-B understates the structural severity: the two traffic classes have
fundamentally asymmetric demand growth trajectories, ensuring that any architecture coupling their resource pools
produces systematically worsening M0 service quality over time.
M0 — Safety-critical traffic comprises all information whose delayed delivery has direct safety consequences:
CAMs at 1–10 Hz [7], BSMs [8], hazard warnings with ≤20 ms latency [1], and RCU zone advisories. The
M0/M1 classification is consistent with C-V2X service-level-requirement taxonomies [12]. M0 demand is physically
bounded — no vehicle can generate safety-relevant updates faster than its kinematic state changes — and grows
approximately linearly with vehicle density.
M1 — Non-safety traffic comprises infotainment, navigation updates, OTA downloads, and streaming. M1 demand
is driven by consumer trends and grows continuously [13]: a vehicle’s M0 stream consumes 3–8 Kbps while its
M1 stream consumes 2–20 Mbps — two to three orders of magnitude larger (Fig. 2) [12]. The multidimensional
possibilities of multiple passengers per vehicle and multiple terminals per passenger could further widen this order-
of-magnitude gap, an effect already observable in the Vehicle-to-Pedestrian (V2P) context discussed in Section VII.
Critically, M0 and M1 demand are not independent: population density drives both vehicle counts (M0 demand) and
cellular subscriber density (M1 demand, compounded by in-vehicle occupant devices), meaning peak M0 safety-
critical periods — rush hour at high-density urban intersections — coincide with peak M1 throughput demand at
exactly the locations and moments where coordination failure is most dangerous. This demand correlation makes
the shared-pool architecture doubly problematic: it fails hardest precisely when it matters most.

C. Existing Deployments as Empirical Validation
Multiple independent regulatory ecosystems have deployed entities matching the RCU specification without a
3GPP mode definition to inhabit. DB11/T 2329.1-2024 [3], effective March 2025, defines the Roadside Computing
Unit (l`uc`e j`ısu`an d¯anyu´an, in Chinese) as a computing facility deployed along roads that performs traffic information
aggregation, processing, and decision-making — explicitly distinguishing it from the Roadside Unit (RSU) as a
separate entity within the roadside infrastructure layer. T/ITS 0224.1-2025 [4], effective July 2025, provides the
national functional architecture for this entity class, confirming fusion computation as a core service function.
Simultaneously, China Unicom’s RS-MEC [5] — operator-owned roadside compute supporting 5G/V2X dual-
mode communication — is being deployed at the same intersections as traffic-authority RCUs. Two entities
from opposite institutional domains are physically co-located without a standardized coordination mechanism.
European C-ITS programs [15], [16] and US C-V2X deployment plans [17] show the same convergence. Mode 0
standardization does not propose that roadside computing infrastructure should exist. It already exists, from both
sides of the institutional boundary. Mode 0 is the interoperability framework.
IV. FORMAL DEFINITION OF MODE 0 AND THE RCU ENTITY CLASS
A. Entity Classification
The RCU is not a base station: it serves entities participating in traffic behaviors within a geographically
bounded zone, not a general subscriber population; its optimization objective is traffic safety and flow, not network
performance; it is owned by a traffic management authority under transportation law, not a network operator under
telecommunications law. It is not a UE: it is fixed infrastructure; it possesses elevated sensor installations unavailable
to any mobile terminal; it is not a peer of the vehicles it serves. It cannot be classified as a Mode 2 variant — the
Mode 2d group leader “is by no means a different class or category of UEs” [10]; adding the RCU as Mode 2e
would eliminate the institutional distinction and the mandatory escalation capability simultaneously.
The RCU is more precisely characterized as an infrastructure ensemble of three functional layers that map onto
three named capabilities. The observation layer (Seeing): elevated sensing hardware — cameras, millimeter-wave
radar, Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), and other exploitable sensor types — positioned at intersection height,
detecting traffic participants and occluded hazards inaccessible to road-level sensors, continuously refining the zone
kinematic model, and auditing traffic behaviors for compliance assessment. The communication layer (Speaking):
an RSU communicating with vehicle UEs within the traffic node over PC5 sidelink to broadcast advisories, aggregate
V2X inputs, and relay warnings; and via backhaul to cloud management platforms and network operator interfaces
for resource coordination. The evaluation layer (Thinking): a local Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) facility fusing
sensor and V2X data into the global zone model and executing scheduling advisory logic that exceeds what any
individual vehicle’s bounded on-board computation can produce.
The eNB/gNB possesses unbounded Thinking and Speaking without Seeing — an abstract network function with
no fixed presence at the traffic node. The UE possesses all three capabilities but with bounded Thinking and road-
level Seeing. The RCU possesses elevated Seeing that neither the eNB/gNB nor any UE can replicate; its Thinking
derives authority from this elevated perspective. The RSU-MEC compound — found in prior literature as edge RSU
or roadside MEC [18] — specifies only the communication and evaluation layers; omitting the observation layer
eliminates the pre-emergence hazard detection, zone kinematic model construction, and traffic behavior auditing
capabilities that define the RCU’s categorical advantage. Table I summarizes the systematic contrast across all
defining dimensions.

B. Mode 0 Operational Specification
Mode 0 Subfamily Classification: The RCU’s role is fixed; the coordination quality vehicle UEs contribute
depends on their on-board compute capability.
• Mode 0a — All-Passive UEs: No vehicle can operate as an independent per-vehicle Multi-Agent Reinforce-
ment Learning (MARL) agent. Shared per-class actor policies serve as the guaranteed minimum — Mode 0a
must function correctly for fully legacy fleets.
• Mode 0b — Hybrid UEs: A mixed fleet of passive and active vehicles; the expected transitional state during
fleet modernization.
• Mode 0c — All-Active UEs: Each vehicle runs its own independent policy network. Mode 0c breaks the
symmetric-Nash coordination constraint that Mode 0a’s shared-actor architecture is structurally subject to,
enabling both subchannel coordination and per-agent power control.
Zone definition: The RCU defines a coverage zone corresponding to a traffic node and its approach corridors,
determined by traffic geometry. Within the zone, the RCU maintains a persistent real-time model of all traffic
participants.
Resource partitioning: A subset Mm0 of the available PC5 sidelink subchannels is designated as the M0 resource
pool; the remainder constitutes the M1 pool for base station scheduling. A subchannel is a set of consecutive Physical
Resource Blocks (PRBs) within the sidelink resource pool sized to accommodate a complete M0 safety message
payload (formally defined in Section V-B). The critical deployment sizing rule is Mm0 ≥peak expected concurrent
M0-class subchannel demand (reusable across kinematically non-conflicting sub-zones, as detailed in the Advisory
scheduling component below) — ensuring the deterministic coordination regime is maintained and the anti-helpful
undersizing failure mode is avoided.
Advisory scheduling: The RCU divides the zone into spatial sub-zones — the minimum sub-zone being a
single vehicle UE, when kinematic precision warrants it — and broadcasts a non-mandatory M0 subchannel
recommendation to each sub-zone over PC5. The purpose of this division is to minimise M0 subchannel conflicts
among vehicles that are kinematically likely to interact: vehicles on converging trajectories or sharing a road
segment are assigned distinct subchannel recommendations to eliminate co-channel interference at the moments
of closest proximity. Crucially, two or more sub-zones that are kinematically unlikely to intersect — separated
by physical barriers, on diverging trajectories, or sufficiently far apart that vehicle paths cannot plausibly meet
within the current planning horizon — may be assigned the same M0 subchannel resources without conflict risk.
This spatial subchannel reuse within the zone is the mechanism by which Mode 0 serves more vehicles than the
raw M0 subchannel count Mm0 might suggest: the effective reuse factor depends on zone geometry and traffic
flow topology, both of which are continuously available to the RCU’s evaluation layer and inaccessible to any
individual vehicle UE. Formally, the sub-zone assignment problem is a graph coloring problem: sub-zones are
nodes, kinematic conflict relationships are edges, and subchannels are colors. For planar conflict graphs — the

Advisory latency structure: Unlike Mode 1/3’s multi-hop scheduling cycle — Scheduling Request / Buffer Status

grant overhead and no sensing window latency. This single-hop structure is the architectural basis for Mode 0’s
compatibility with the 20 ms worst-case latency bound as a structural property rather than a statistical outcome:
the advisory delivery latency is bounded by the RCU’s processing cycle and one PC5 propagation delay, not by
any multi-party protocol negotiation.
Warning validation and re-broadcast: When a UE-originated safety warning has insufficient coverage, the RCU
re-broadcasts at infrastructure-grade power within the M0 resource pool, labeled as an RCU relay.
Emergency escalation protocol — two pathways: The advisory default is appropriate for Category A and B
traffic risk scenarios. Category C scenarios — large-scope environmental hazards producing cascading secondary
incident risk — require mandatory escalation authority.
Pathway 1 — Autonomous escalation (in-coverage): Three conditions must be simultaneously satisfied: (1) the
RCU’s sensor fusion independently verifies a Category C hazard; (2) approaching vehicle density within the stop-
distance horizon exceeds a configurable threshold; (3) the RCU holds regulatory pre-authorization from its traffic
management authority for the verified hazard category. The mandatory stop advisory specifies the affected segment,
minimum stopping horizon, and duration; it is time-bounded, logged in the tamper-resistant episode record, and
cancelled by broadcast when the hazard resolves.
Pathway 2 — Commanded escalation (out-of-coverage): When the Category C event occurs outside sensor
coverage, Condition 1 cannot be satisfied autonomously. Traffic management authorities possess situational
awareness through monitoring cameras, survivor reports, and emergency services. A qualified representative issues
a real-time override command to the nearest capable RCU, replacing Condition 1 with human judgment while
Conditions 2 and 3 remain active. The authorizing representative’s credentials are logged alongside the episode
record. This extends Mode 0’s mandatory broadcast capability beyond the RCU’s own sensor range, with human
authority as the verification mechanism. Neither pathway grants autonomous authority without institutional backing;
neither requires physical-layer modifications to vehicle UEs; neither conflicts with Modes 1–4 operations.
The mandatory escalation capability — in both pathways — cannot be replicated by any UE-side behavior: the
Mode 2 peer equality principle forecloses mandatory stop commands between UEs, and no UE can receive a real-
time traffic authority override command and execute it as a zone-wide mandatory broadcast. This is the strongest
form of the categorical gap argument.
C. Relationship to Existing Modes and Generational Independence
Mode 0 is complementary to, not competitive with, the existing mode framework. A vehicle within RCU zone
coverage may simultaneously operate in Mode 3 for uplink cellular services, Mode 4 for autonomous Vehicle-to-
Vehicle (V2V) safety messaging within the M0 resource pool, and receive Mode 0 sub-zone advisories from the
RCU that inform its Mode 4 resource selection. Mode 3 governs the vehicle’s relationship with the cellular network
for M1-class services. Mode 4 governs the vehicle’s autonomous V2V communication decisions. Mode 0 provides
the advisory layer that improves the interference geometry of those Mode 4 decisions by supplying global zone
information that Mode 4’s local sensing cannot access.
Mode 0’s generational independence is a defining property. Mode 0 is defined at the architectural level rather
than the radio access technology level. The RCU’s advisory function — zone partitioning based on kinematic data,

D. Data Locality and Privacy Properties
An architectural property of Mode 0 that deserves explicit treatment is the data locality guarantee it produces —
superior to both traditional cloud-dependent MEC architectures and federated learning approaches, without requiring
any additional privacy mechanism.
In a cloud-dependent MEC architecture, vehicle kinematic data is aggregated at a remote server for scheduling
computation. Even when the server is an edge cloud rather than a centralized cloud, the data necessarily travels
beyond the traffic node boundary, crossing operator infrastructure and potentially multiple administrative domains.
Trajectory data is deeply personal, enabling inference of home locations, daily routines, and behavioral patterns.
Transmitting it to operator infrastructure subjects it to operator data governance policies rather than traffic authority
governance policies.
Federated learning offers a partial remedy by keeping raw data on each device and sharing only model gradients.
However, gradient inversion attacks can reconstruct training data from shared gradients, and the federated approach
still requires communication of model parameters across the network at each training round.
The Mode 0 RCU architecture avoids both problems through geographic data containment. The RCU’s evaluation
layer processes only the data of vehicles currently within its bounded traffic zone, and that data circulates only
within the RCU’s local computational environment. A vehicle’s position and kinematic state are visible to the RCU
only for the duration of its zone traversal — typically seconds to minutes — and are not transmitted beyond the
zone boundary except in aggregated, anonymized, or event-triggered form as specified by the traffic authority’s data
governance policy. The zone boundary is not merely a computational scope — it is a data governance boundary
enforced by the physical architecture.
This data locality property is a direct consequence of the institutional boundary argument in Section III-A: because
the RCU is owned by the traffic authority rather than the network operator, its data governance is determined by
transportation law rather than telecommunications law. In jurisdictions with strong data protection requirements for
personal location data — including the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) framework
and China’s Personal Information Protection Law — the Mode 0 architecture’s zone-local data containment provides
a structurally compliant privacy posture that cloud-dependent alternatives cannot match without additional technical
controls.
E. The Priority Argument: Why Mode 0 and Not Mode 5 or Mode 2e
The naming of a new mode category within 3GPP is not a purely terminological question. Mode numbering
carries semantic content that reflects architectural relationships.
The first alternative — sequential extension as Mode 5 — would imply that Mode 0 is a peer addition to the
existing set, a fifth option at the same taxonomic level. This is architecturally incorrect. Mode 0 is not a fifth option
at the same taxonomic level as Modes 1 through 4. It is a meta-layer that defines the resource domain within which
Modes 1 through 4 operate in safety-critical contexts. Mode 0 and Mode 4 are not alternatives between which a
vehicle chooses — they are different layers of the same operational scenario.
The second alternative — subfamilial extension as Mode 2e — would be the most technically damaging. Adding
Mode 2e for the RCU would imply that the RCU is also not a different class — that it is simply another UE with an
additional scheduling advisory behavior. This misclassification would foreclose the RCU’s institutional distinction
from the UE, eliminate the basis for traffic-authority ownership, and reduce the M0/M1 resource separation to a
behavioral convention among peers rather than an architectural guarantee enforced by infrastructure-level resource
partitioning. It would also eliminate the Category C mandatory escalation capability entirely, because that capability
depends on the RCU’s categorical distinction from UEs.
Mode 0 is proposed as numerically prior to the existing modes to reflect a specific architectural claim: within
the M0 safety-critical traffic domain, the RCU’s advisory layer takes precedence over the scheduling decisions of
Modes 1 through 4 in terms of informational authority. The RCU’s zone-level global awareness is informationally

The university analogy makes this layered relationship intuitive, and can be stated in its complete form. Mode 2/4

The analogy has a further dimension that clarifies Mode 0’s generational independence. Modes 1 and 2, both
defined within LTE-V2X in 3GPP Release 14, belong to what we may call the 4G School: Mode 1 is general
education within that generation’s institutional framework, and Mode 2 is self-directed study within the same
generation. Modes 3 and 4, defined within NR-V2X in Release 16, belong to the 5G School: Mode 3 is general
education within the NR framework, and Mode 4 is self-directed study within NR. Each generational school offers
the same two course types — centralized scheduling and autonomous selection — instantiated in the specific radio
access technology of that generation. Future generations — a 6G School, a 7G School — will predictably offer
the same two course types again.
Mode 0 is the major-specific core course that exists across all schools. It is neither offered by the 4G School
nor by the 5G School — it is offered by the discipline of traffic safety itself, and every school must accommodate
it regardless of its own institutional framework. When the 6G School opens, the same core course remains — its
content defined by traffic safety requirements that do not change with radio access technology generations. This is
the precise sense in which Mode 0 is generationally independent: it is defined at the disciplinary level, not at the
institutional level.
The analogy is completed at the economic layer in Section VII: the RCU as faculty awards contribution scores
— scholarships — to vehicle UEs as students, creating population-level incentives for cooperative engagement with
the Mode 0 curriculum.
V. SYSTEM MODEL AND PROOF-OF-CONCEPT SIMULATION DESIGN
A. CTDE–Mode 0 Correspondence
The Mode 0 architecture maps precisely onto the CTDE paradigm in MARL [19], [20]. The centralized critic is the
computational model of the RCU: it accesses the global state — positions, velocities, subchannel assignments, and
per-vehicle Exponential Moving Average (EMA) Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR) estimates for all N
vehicles — during training, using this global information to guide decentralized actor policies. At execution time, the
critic is absent; each actor operates on its own local observation. The distributed actors are the computational models
of vehicle UEs. The simulation programme validates two Mode 0 subfamily configurations: Mode 0a (per-class
shared actors, one shared network per traffic class) models the guaranteed-minimum deployment for passive UE
fleets; Mode 0c (per-vehicle distinct actors) models the optimal deployment for fully active fleets. The weakest-link
(barrel) effect applies to mixed deployments: a zone’s safety coordination quality is bounded by its lowest-capability
participants, making Mode 0a the essential minimum that the entire traffic class must satisfy regardless of how
capable the majority of the fleet is. The centralized RSU critic is identical in both configurations, isolating actor
architecture as the sole variable.

(Dec-POMDP) (N, S, {Ai}, {Oi}, T, R, γ) with γ = 0.99. The global state s ∈S is the RCU’s information:
positions, velocities, headings, subchannel assignments {chi}, and per-vehicle EMA SINR across M subchannels;
dimension N ·obs dim+M. The local observation oi has dimension obs dim = 3+M +3, where the three trailing
features are a normalized agent-identity index vehicle id norm ∈[0, 1], a class identity bit (M0 vs M1), and a

E. Discussion
The four results validate distinct claims. Result 1 confirms that Mode 0a sits at the analytical symmetric-Nash
floor — the structural constraint motivating the entire Mode 0 proposal. Result 2 confirms supply expansion is
probabilistic relief: the floor structure is invariant to supply across a 3.3× sweep. Result 3 confirms the three-
regime structure with the Mm0 ≥peak m0-count sizing rule as the operational output. Result 4 establishes Mode 0c’s
two-capability mechanism and validates that global information — the centralized critic — is a functional necessity:
demand separation alone (C1 vs D1) accounts for only +0.017 of the total +0.223 PDR lift; per-vehicle architecture
accounts for the remainder.
Two simulation limitations merit acknowledgment. Ghost peeking pre-emergence warning and the Category C
emergency escalation protocol are validated conceptually but not in the co-simulation environment, which lacks
sensor occlusion geometry. V2P scenarios with heterogeneous device types are not represented. Both are identified
as priorities for future simulation work in companion research.

VII. STANDARDIZATION PATHWAY AND OPEN ISSUES
3GPP integration. Mode 0 does not require a new standards track. The architectural components it relies upon —
PC5 sidelink, subchannel resource pools, UE resource selection, and infrastructure-assisted resource configuration
— are all specified within the NR-V2X framework of Release 16 and its successors. A study item within RAN1
and RAN2 would address five standardization questions: the formal RCU entity class definition including the
Seeing/Speaking/Thinking functional decomposition; the Mode 0 subfamily taxonomy (Mode 0a as minimum
compliance, Mode 0b as hybrid transitional, Mode 0c as advanced) with UE capability declaration mechanisms;
the Mode 0 advisory message format; the two-pathway emergency escalation protocol and trigger condition
specification; and the M0 resource pool partitioning and Mm0 sizing interface between traffic management authorities
and network operators. Mode 0 is complementary to Release 17 and 18 NR-V2X enhancement work items, adding
an advisory layer above existing sidelink operation without replacing it. A detailed message format specification
and full treatment of the commanded escalation pathway for out-of-coverage Category C scenarios are developed
in companion work.
Cross-domain interface. The most significant standardization challenge Mode 0 introduces is institutional rather
than technical. The traffic management authority owns and configures the RCU’s M0 resource pool; the network
operator must adjust its resource allocation accordingly. The co-deployment of traffic-authority RCUs and operator-
owned RS-MEC units [5] at the same intersections — documented in Section III-C — makes this interface challenge
concrete rather than hypothetical. Because Mode 0’s default advisory authority is non-mandatory, the cross-domain
interface requires configuration-level coordination only, preserving each domain’s operational autonomy. The Mm0
sizing interface — ensuring Mm0 ≥peak expected concurrent M0-class subchannel demand (reusable) — is the
critical parameter that the standardization must formally specify.
Compliance and generational roadmap. Mode 0’s baseline compliance model rests on individual rationality:
advisory compliance is locally optimal because the RCU’s sub-zone assignments derive from global zone information
that no individual vehicle can replicate. As Level 4/5 autonomous vehicle penetration increases, this compliance
operates at the application / On-Board Unit (OBU) layer independently of the Mode 0 subfamily tier: an
autonomous vehicle can be configured to treat Mode 0 sub-zone advisories as high-confidence inputs and follow
them deterministically, achieving near-certain Mode 0a-compliant coordination without requiring per-vehicle actor
capability (Mode 0c). The compliance architecture extends to the economic layer: consistent with the university
analogy of Section IV-E, the RCU functions as faculty awarding contribution scores — scholarships — to vehicle
UEs based on observed cooperative behavior within the zone. A detailed specification of this scoring mechanism,
including distributed ledger implementation and tangible benefit channels, is developed in companion work.
Mode 0’s generational independence — established in Section IV-E — ensures that as 3GPP progresses toward
6G, the RCU entity class definition and advisory architecture require no re-specification; only the air-interface
instantiation adapts at each generational transition.
Open issues. The V2P extension introduces a dual-domain membership problem [24]: a pedestrian’s personal
device is simultaneously a traffic zone participant (RCU domain) and a cellular subscriber (eNB/gNB domain). Three
research directions are identified: vehicle OBUs declare associated Uu subscriber counts to the RCU as an in-vehicle
occupant proxy, with coverage responsibility delegated to Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) (near-term);
a lightweight high-priority RCU-to-pedestrian broadcast channel over Uu with pre-committed resource allocation
(medium-term); and RCU sensor detection algorithms that reliably distinguish on-road participants from vehicle
occupants (longer-term). Mode 0d — all-active UEs with partial voluntary downgrade for personalized driving
preferences — is a named future subfamily tier pending resolution of safety-critical questions around voluntary
capability downgrade. Mobile deployment of the RCU evaluation layer, exploiting compute-storage disaggregation
to serve geographically shifting traffic demand while preserving node-bound model privacy guarantees, is analyzed
in companion work.
VIII. CONCLUSION
The 3GPP V2X resource allocation framework rests on a binary entity taxonomy — the base station and the
vehicle UE — that is structurally incomplete. Neither entity class can meet the safety-critical scheduling requirements
of high-density traffic nodes, provide pre-emergence warning for occluded hazards, or halt cascading Category C

environmental emergencies through mandatory zone-wide advisory authority. The failure is categorical: the entity
capable of resolving it does not exist in the current framework.
This entity — the Roadside Computing Unit — is defined in this paper as Mode 0, integrating an elevated
observation layer (Seeing), a sidelink communication layer (Speaking), and a local computational evaluation layer
(Thinking) as a unified ensemble under traffic management authority ownership. Mode 0 defines a subfamily
spectrum from Mode 0a (all-passive UEs, the guaranteed minimum) through Mode 0c (all-active UEs, the optimal
target), enabling adoption across the full capability range of current and future vehicle fleets.
Three independent lines of evidence support the proposal. Theoretically, base station-led scheduling saturates un-
der resource contention at traffic node densities; Mode 2/4 UE autonomy is categorically incapable of pre-emergence
warning for occluded hazards; and the traffic risk taxonomy identifies Category C cascading environmental hazards
as the scenario class where advisory architecture fails and Mode 0’s two-pathway mandatory escalation capability
is uniquely necessary. Empirically, Chinese national standards (DB11/T 2329.1-2024, T/ITS 0224.1-2025) and
operator infrastructure (China Unicom RS-MEC) confirm that both sides of the traffic-authority/operator institutional
boundary are independently converging on the roadside traffic node without a standardized coordination mechanism.
Computationally, the fifteen-run MAPPO simulation programme establishes four mechanistic results: Mode 0a in
shared-pool baseline sits at the analytical symmetric-Nash coordination floor provably unbreakable by shared-actor
architecture [20]; supply expansion provides probabilistic relief but cannot break the floor structure; Mode 0c plus
demand separation with Mm0 ≥peak m0-count achieves strict Pareto improvement for both traffic classes across
both density regimes tested; and Mode 0c lifts the worst-TTI packet delivery ratio from near-zero to 0.601 — the
only configuration satisfying the latency safety requirement as a structural property.
3GPP should initiate a study item on Mode 0 within the NR-V2X sidelink enhancement work programme.
The scope covers RCU entity class definition, Mode 0 subfamily specification with UE capability declaration
mechanisms, advisory and two-pathway emergency escalation message format specification, and the Mm0 cross-
domain sizing interface. The standardization effort is bounded and tractable. The need it addresses grows with every
vehicle that adds an infotainment screen, every intersection approaching peak density, every Category C emergency
without a standards-compliant mandatory broadcast capability, and every RS-MEC and RCU deployment proceeding
without an interoperability framework. Mode 0 provides that framework.
