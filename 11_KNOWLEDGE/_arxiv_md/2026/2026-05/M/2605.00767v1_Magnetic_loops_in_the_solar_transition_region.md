Abstract
Transition region (TR) loops are arcade-like features in the solar transition
region, with temperatures roughly between 2×104 K and 6×105 K. They are a
fundamental building block of TR, which are results of the coupling between the
magnetic field and the TR plasma. Their dynamics is closely related to the trans-
port of energy and mass through the TR. Studies on this class of loops since the
launch of the Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph (IRIS) have revealed that
they are distinct from coronal loops. Observations have revealed that they are
associated with many small-scale dynamic phenomena in the TR, from which one
can infer the physics behind the energy and mass transfer in a magnetically con-
fined environment at TR temperature. This review summarises the observational
results of TR loops, showing their morphology, dynamics, plasma parameters,
their relationship with flux emergence, their heating properties, and their impli-
cation in the heating of the solar atmosphere. This class of magnetic loops is
much less well understood than their coronal counterparts. This review also con-
cludes with several critical questions that need to be answered in the coming
era with more advanced observational techniques and more precise and realistic
simulations.
Keywords: The sun, solar atmosphere, solar transition region, magnetic field, solar
plasma, coronal heating

The solar atmosphere is the outer layers of the sun, where the photons can escape into
outer space in the form of sunlight. The bottom boundary of the solar atmosphere,

the solar atmosphere cannot be precisely defined. It extends into interplanetary space
in the form of the solar wind, which fills in the entire heliosphere and sometimes is
considered as part of the solar atmosphere.
In the classic plane-parallel models (Vernazza et al. 1981; Cranmer and Winebarger
2019), the stratified solar atmosphere includes three major layers, photosphere, chro-
mosphere, and corona (Fig. 1). The photosphere is the innermost layer and the visible
surface of the sun, with a thickness of a few hundred kilometers, yet it emits most of the
sunlight. At the bottom of the photosphere, the temperatures are about 6 000–7 000 K,
and at its top, the lowest temperature in the solar atmosphere is about 4 500 K. The
chromosphere lies above the photosphere, which has a thickness of about a thousand
kilometers. The temperatures in the chromosphere gradually rise to 10 000–20 000 K at
its top. The corona is the outermost layer that extends to several solar radii. It is the
hottest part of the solar atmosphere, with temperatures around a million Kelvin. This
abrupt temperature increase to a million degrees constitutes the so-called “coronal
heating problem”, which is one of the longstanding questions in solar physics.
Between the chromosphere and corona, the temperature rapidly increases from
20 000 K to about 1 000 000 K within just a few tens of kilometres. This defines a criti-
cal interface in the solar atmosphere, namely the transition region (TR). Through this
region, most elements of the sun, including the two most abundant elements (hydro-
gen and helium), become fully ionised (see Table 1). As a result, the radiation from
the TR is dominated by emission lines from various ions. Collisions (via thermal) are
the major ionisation process in the TR (Nussbaumer and Storey 1975), and ionisation
has a profound effect on the thermal equilibrium and the coupling between the mag-
netic field and the plasma (Bradshaw and Testa 2019). In other words, a major part
of the energy that pours into the TR is used for ionisation. Because radiation takes
only a small fraction of the energy, also the region has a short cooling time scale, any
brightenings, as observed by the remote sensing facilities, cannot sustain as long as
those in the corona. This is also a reason why the observed TR is highly dynamic.
Observations obtained from many generations of instruments, such as the High
Resolution Telescope and Spectrograph (HRTS, Dere 1990) of Naval Research Lab-
oratory (NRL) rocket missions, the Solar Ultraviolet Measurements of Emitted
Radiation (SUMER, Wilhelm et al. 1995) and the Coronal Diagnostic Spectrome-
ter (CDS, Harrison et al. 1995) on board the SOHO mission (Domingo et al. 1995),
the Transition Region and Coronal Explorer (TRACE, Handy et al. 1999), the EUV
Imaging Spectrometer (EIS, Culhane et al. 2007) for Hinode (Kosugi et al. 2007), the
Interface Region Imaging Spectrograph (IRIS, De Pontieu et al. 2014) and the Extreme
Ultraviolet Imager (EUI, Rochus et al. 2020) aboard Solar Orbiter (SolO, Müller et al.
2020) have revealed that the TR has an extremely dynamic nature. Figure 2 shows a
full disk mosaic image of the sun emitting at a temperature of about 8.0×104 K, from
which we can clearly see that the TR is highly structured. The coupling between the
ionised plasma and the solar magnetic field gives rise to many magnificent phenom-
ena in the TR. In this region, many small scale transients are ubiquitous, including
small-scale reconnection events (e.g. Dere et al. 1989; Innes et al. 1997; Huang et al.

In this review, I focus on loop structures in the TR, namely TR loops, whose full
lengths have temperatures roughly between 2 × 104 K and 6 × 105 K. Comparing to
their widely-studied coronal counterparts, coronal loops, TR loops have significantly
different physical characteristics and dynamic evolutions as revealed by high-resolution
observations achieved by several successful solar missions in the past decade. In what
follows, a general introduction to loop in the solar atmosphere is given in Section 2;
observations to TR loops are summarised in Section 3; a summary of existed modelling
results of TR loops is provided in Section 4; and a summary to this review with a few
open questions is given in Section 5.

2 Loops in the solar atmosphere
When the plasma in the solar atmosphere begins to be ionised, its coupling with the
solar magnetic field becomes strong, and it starts to distribute along the magnetic
field lines. The plasma-β is often used to characterise the interaction between the
plasma and magnetic field, which is defined as the ratio of the thermal pressure to
the magnetic pressure, and in cgs units, it expresses
β = 8πnkBT/B2,

upper chromosphere, TR, and the corona (see Fig. 3), the magnetic field dominates,
and the solar atmosphere is structured by the magnetic field.
A notable structure in the upper solar atmosphere, representative of the coupling
between the solar magnetic field and the ionised gas, is the so-called loop, which
connects two magnetic elements on the solar surface with opposite polarities. They
are arch-like, bright structures that stand out from the ambient solar atmosphere.

Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA, Lemen et al. 2012) on board the Solar Dynamics
Observatory (SDO, Pesnell et al. 2012), from which we can see that loops are one of
the fundamental building blocks of the solar atmosphere.

The lengths of loops vary from a few to hundreds of mega-meters (Mm) (see review
by Reale 2014). Short loops with sizes from a few Mm to about 30 Mm connecting
small-scale opposite polarities usually locate in quiet sun regions and form localised
brightenings, such as coronal bright points (e.g. Habbal and Withbroe 1981; Huang
et al. 2012; Mou et al. 2016; Madjarska et al. 2023) and even smaller brightenings
revealed by SolO/EUI (Berghmans et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2021; Mandal et al. 2021).
They are also present in the cores of active regions (Huang 2018; Huang et al. 2019).
Longer loops with sizes up to hundreds of Mm normally shape the outer boundaries
of an active region and connect its two major opposite polarities, or connect opposite
polarities in a pair of active regions (Wiegelmann et al. 2014) that sometimes even
locate in opposite hemispheres (Svestka et al. 1977; Tsuneta 1996; Pevtsov 2000).
Because loops are easy to trace in the solar atmosphere and connect different layers
and locations, they are ideal objects for studying mass and energy transfer in the solar
atmosphere. Many mysteries of the solar atmosphere have been uncovered by stud-
ies of the morphology and dynamics of loops. For example, the heating mechanism of
coronal loops can be inferred from the relations among their temperatures, pressures,
and lengths (Rosner et al. 1978; Klimchuk and Porter 1995; Xie et al. 2017); oscilla-
tions in loops are a good proxy to probe the magnetic field in the corona (see reviews
by Nakariakov and Verwichte 2005; De Moortel and Nakariakov 2012; Nakariakov and
Kolotkov 2020); and small-scale dynamics in loops reveals braiding reconnection pro-
cesses in the corona (Cirtain et al. 2013; Huang et al. 2018; Zuo et al. 2025; Chen et al.
2025). Therefore, loops have been studied intensively since the 1960s, and they have

3 Observations of TR loops
Loops have also been thought to be a basic structures of the TR since the
1980s (Feldman 1983; Antiochos and Noci 1986; Feldman 1987, 1998; Feldman et al.
2001; Dowdy et al. 1986; Dowdy 1993; Sasso et al. 2012), but solid observational
evidence only becomes available after the launch of the Solar and Heliospheric Obser-
vatory (SOHO), which are ascribed to raster scans provided by two spectrographs
(SUMER and CDS) with spectral lines formed at the TR temperatures (Brekke et al.
1997; Brekke 1999; Landi et al. 2000; Chae et al. 2000). After the launch of IRIS
in 2013, the dynamical evolution of TR loops can be followed thanks to the special
7


## Page 8


design of the instrument that allows both imaging and spectral data to be acquired
simultaneously at high spatial resolution and temporal cadence (Hansteen et al. 2014).
In this section, the observational aspects of TR loops are summarised. TR loops
rooted in active regions and quiet-sun regions are distinct from each other likely due
to the difference in the magnetic dynamics (i.e. local dynamos), and their observa-
tional results are summarised separately in Subsections 3.1 and 3.2. While small-scaled
activities associated with loops are crucial for understanding how the magnetic field
acts in heating and reshaping the solar atmosphere, this aspect related to TR loops
is given in Subsection 3.3.
3.1 TR loops in active regions
In active regions, loops with various temperatures are usually found. A study (Fludra
et al. 1997) based on CDS observations of active regions identifies several classes
of loops, including multi-thermal loops seen in all temperatures from 2 × 105 K to
2×106 K, warm loops seen at 106 K but not reaching 1.6×106 K, cooler loops seen at
2–4×105 K and occasionally reaching 6×105 K but not exceeding 106 K. Observations
from Hi-C rocket mission (Kobayashi et al. 2014) also reveal loops with temperatures
of 2.5×105 K connecting two moss regions (Winebarger et al. 2013). This demonstrates
that loops in TR temperatures are a distinct group of loops in active regions, which
are much more dynamic than their coronal counterparts (Fludra et al. 1997; Brekke
et al. 1997; Di Giorgio et al. 2003). As shown in Figure 5, the loop structures seen
in SJ 1400 Å (TR loops) are significantly distinguished from those hotter ones (e.g.
in AIA 193 Å, XRT Al_poly). TR loops at lower temperature of about 7.9 × 104 K
have been discovered by IRIS observations (Huang et al. 2015), as shown in Figure 6.
Please note that the legs of coronal loops or fan loops often have a response at TR
temperature (Del Zanna and Mason 2003; Young et al. 2007; Ugarte-Urra et al. 2009).
“TR loops” described here refer to those loops that can be discerned in full length
at TR temperatures. Some TR loops might also be temporarily heated to coronal
temperatures while strong heatings take place (Yan et al. 2018), but that can only
sustain for a short period of time.
Strong flows are one of the dynamic phenomena frequently present in TR loops.
The CDS observations of a loop at about 2 × 105 K has revealed a flow of about
50 km s−1 along the loop axis (Brekke et al. 1997), which is also confirmed by SUMER
data (Chae et al. 2000) and Hinode/EIS observations (Ugarte-Urra et al. 2009). Flows
at the footpoints of TR loops might show inversion between upflows and downflows
at a temperature of about 2 × 104 K (Rao et al. 2019). Clear evidence of siphon flows
with speeds of about 10 km s−1 in TR loops rooted in active region near the solar
disk center has been revealed by IRIS observations in Si iv (7.9 × 104 K) (Huang et al.
2015), as shown in Figure 6 (c). Such a siphon flow is clear evidence of an imbalance
of heating sources in the footpoints of the TR loops, and enthalpy flows are crucial in
distributing heat along the entire loop (Doyle et al. 2006).
As loops are a good proxy for tracking magnetic geometry in the solar atmosphere,
understanding how they form, along with flux emergence, is critical for understanding
the formation of the multi-layered solar atmosphere. Such studies have been carried
out by using observations of IRIS, AIA, EIS, XRT, and HMI (Huang 2018; Huang et al.

The sizes of TR loops might vary from one individual to another. A study of 15 TR
loops in steady status based on the IRIS spectral data in Si iv 1402.8 Å found that their
apparent lengths vary from 7 Mm to 30 Mm (Huang et al. 2015). Their cross-sections
are about 500 km, but one should keep in mind that this is close to the instrument’s
resolution limit (Huang et al. 2015). A statistics with 25 samples, considering the
extensions of TR loops to magnetic polarities, gives a range of 8.03–29.64 Mm for their
lengths (Liang et al. 2024).
The electron density of a TR loop also varies from case to case and depends
on its temperature. A case study of loop observed in Mg vii (T ∼6.3 × 105 K) by
Hinode/EIS gives densities of 3 × 109 cm−3 at its footpoint and 1.5 × 109 cm−3 at a
projection height of 20 Mm (Young et al. 2007). In an emerging TR loop at 8 × 104 K,
the electron density is found to be about 5.0×1010 cm−3 (Huang 2018). In a transient
loop at about 1.4 × 105 K, the density is measured as 6.3 × 1010 cm−3 (Gupta and
Nayak 2022). A systematic study of 25 TR loops based on IRIS O iv (T∼1.4×105 K)
observations (Liang et al. 2024) shows that their electron densities range from 8.9 ×
109 cm−3 to 3.5 × 1011 cm−3.
Based on spectral data, effective temperatures of TR loops can be derived from
the broadening of spectral lines, which are representative of the thermal or thermal-
like velocities of ions at the emission source. The analysis of 25 TR loops shows that
the effective temperatures of the same TR loop observed in Si iv (T∼8 × 104 K) is
normally greater than that in O iv (T ∼1.4 × 105 K) (Liang et al. 2024). Specifically,
those TR loops have effective temperatures in a range of 1.9 × 105 K–1.3 × 106 K in
O iv, by contrast to 2.2 × 105 K–2.3 × 106 K in Si iv (Liang et al. 2024). Those results
imply that the heating energy effect on ions at 8 × 104 K is stronger than that at
1.4 × 105 K.
The relations among parameters of loops are critical to infer the heating mecha-
nisms therein (Rosner et al. 1978; van Ballegooijen et al. 2011; Bradshaw et al. 2019).
The first study on these relations of TR loops (Liang et al. 2024) has revealed their
extremely diverse natures, and the distribution may not be converged to a relation
(see Figure 7). One can also see that the goodness of the fitting obtained in Figure 7 is
modest. This suggests that TR loops tend to have a great deal of variety. Neverthe-
less, based on regression analysis, a reliable relation between electron densities (ne)
and loop length (L) is given as
log10(ne) ∝−0.78log10(L).
This is much different from those found in coronal loops (see e.g. Aschwanden et al.
1999; Bourdin et al. 2014). For example, a scaling law of log10(ne) ∝−0.41log10(L)
has been reported for isothermal coronal loops (Aschwanden et al. 1999). Owing to
this relation, together with their diverse nature as revealed in Figure 7, TR loops must
be heated in a very different way than their coronal counterparts.
Regarding the heating profiles of active region TR loops, they are well believed
to be heated impulsively (e.g. Huang et al. 2015; Srivastava et al. 2020; Bahauddin
et al. 2021, etc.). With high resolution observations from SolO/EUI, a statistics of 42
brightenings in TR loops (Zuo et al. 2026) has been carried out. The statistics shows
that the heatings are impulsive and mostly located near the footpoints. The impulsive

phase of the brightenings has a time scale of 118.4 ± 12.0 s, and the decaying phase

loops could be explained by magnetic-reconnection-mediated impulsive heating at
braiding sites of multiple loop strands (Bahauddin et al. 2021).
3.2 TR loops in quiet sun regions
Magnetic loops in quiet sun regions are not rare, and a typical phenomenon is a coronal
bright point (CBP). A CBP has a size less than 50 Mm that consists of multiple
loops with various temperatures (Madjarska 2019). Although some loops in coronal
bright points have cool legs with TR temperatures (Habbal and Withbroe 1981; Tian
et al. 2008; Kwon et al. 2012), such a feature with TR temperature along its full
length (i.e., TR loop) has hardly reported. Based on spectral data from SUMER in
O vi (T ∼2.8 × 105 K), a loop-like feature along with supersonic flows in quiet sun
region with a size of about 15 Mm in length was reported (Teriaca et al. 2004), but
convincing evidence of TR loops in quiet-sun regions is only available until that IRIS
is in orbit (Hansteen et al. 2014), and some examples are shown in Figure 8. Based on
IRIS observations, TR loops in the quiet sun region have a full length of 4 to 12 Mm
and a maximum height of 1 to 4.5 Mm (Hansteen et al. 2014; Bura et al. 2025).
Small-scale loops (or loop-like features) in quiet sun region has also been observed
by EUI aboard SolO in 174 Å passband with unprecedentedly-high resolutions, which
have a similar size as the TR loops seen by IRIS (Berghmans et al. 2021; Mandal et al.
2021; Narang et al. 2025). Because the response function of EUI 174 Å passband has
a large contribution from TR temperatures, some of these small-scale loops observed
by EUI are likely TR loops (Dolliou et al. 2024, 2026). The interaction between such
a loop and ambient field may lead to component reconnections and thus could be a
good candidate for coronal heating mechanism in the quiet sun (Chen et al. 2021).
The TR loops in quiet sun region should be associated with weak magnetic field
in the photosphere at granular scales (Sánchez Almeida et al. 2007). The connection
between TR loops and granular activities remains unclear. A comparison between
IRIS observations and 3D models shows that these TR loops in quiet sun is also
episodically heated, and the eﬀicient radiative cooling due to high density prevents
the occurrence of coronal temperatures in these structures (Hansteen et al. 2014).
However, both observational and theoretical studies on this group of loops are still
very sparse. Further investigations based on higher-resolution observations and/or
more advanced numerical simulations are critical to better understand their natures
and their roles in linking different layers of the solar atmosphere.
3.3 Small-scale activities associated with TR loops
As described above, TR loops could be nascent magnetic structures in the solar atmo-
sphere. Therefore, activities in TR loops provide a good window into how the solar
atmosphere is formed and shaped. Multiple types of small-scale dynamics associated
with TR loops have been discovered in the past decade since the launch of IRIS.

Explosive events, which describe TR features emitting non-Gaussian line profiles
with enhanced wings at Doppler velocities of 50–150 km s−1, are possible signatures
of magnetic reconnections in the TR (Dere et al. 1989; Innes et al. 1997). This phe-
nomenon has been observed at the joint footpoints of multiple TR loops, with the
interaction of opposite polarities underneath (Huang et al. 2015), suggesting mag-
netic reconnection between TR loops. They are also found in the middle part of TR
loop brightenings (see Figure 10) or associated with propagating bright blobs in TR
loops (Huang et al. 2017), which might be due to splitting motions of or braiding
reconnections among TR loop threads (Bahauddin et al. 2021).

4 Modelings of TR loops
A three-dimensional modeling based on the MURaM code (Rempel 2017) has shown
that TR loops as seen in Si iv could be consequences of localised recurrent heating
events driven by convective motions together with the complexity of magnetic features
at the footpoints (Skan et al. 2023). The magnetic reconnection, which is responsi-
ble for the heating events, might not require any flux emergence (Skan et al. 2023).
Regarding heating profiles in TR loops, a recent numerical experiment based on the
hydrodynamics and radiative emission code (HYDRAD, Bradshaw and Klimchuk
2011) shows that TR loops are heated impulsively with a complex heating profile that
consists of multiple heating scales (Bahauddin and Bradshaw 2024). From simulations
based on Bifrost code (Carlsson et al. 2016) suggest that cold loops such as TR loops
are a natural result of impulsive magnetic energy release by reconnection of mag-
netic braidings at low heights in the solar atmosphere (Winebarger et al. 2013). The
impulsive heating events in TR loops can then drive plasma flows therein (Srivastava
et al. 2020). These numerical experiments also emphasize that the non-equilibrium
ionization in the TR is critical for the energy distribution during the heating of TR
loops.
For TR loops in the quiet sun, 3D simulations using Bifrost suggest that those short
TR loops are consequences of low-lying magnetic structures coupling with plasma that
is episodically heated (Hansteen et al. 2014). A study comparing SolO/EUI observa-
tions and simulations results from 1D hydrodynamics (HD) code HYDRAD again
suggests that TR brightenings are results of TR loops in the quiet sun subjected to
impulsive heatings (Dolliou et al. 2025).

Although modelling studies specified on TR loops are sparse, many simulations
based on Bifrost have shown a great potential in resolving the TR features includ-
ing loops (e.g. Hansteen et al. 2014; Martínez-Sykora et al. 2017, etc.). Numerical
experiments based on advanced modern codes like Bifrost and MURaM merit further
investigation on the aspect of TR loops in the future.
5 Summary and outlooks
TR loops are arcade-like features in the solar atmosphere observed at transition-
region temperatures, representing the coupling between the under-heated, partially
ionized plasma and the magnetic field in the TR. They provide an important win-
dow for studying how energy and mass are transferred from the chromosphere to
the corona through the TR. They might form directly alongside flux emergence and
thus help understand how the emerging magnetic flux reshapes the solar atmosphere.
Observations have also revealed that TR loops are very different than their coronal
counterparts. They are transient features in the solar atmosphere that are heated
impulsively and are associated with many small-scale energetic events.
Despite the growing focus on TR loops following the IRIS mission, studies on this
subject, both observational and theoretical, are still very insuﬀicient. Some critical
questions remain to be answered:
– What is the upper limit of the sizes of TR loops? The upper limit of their sizes is
crucial in understanding the coherent range of physical processes in the TR. TR loops
are better identified and measured with a spectrograph rather than imager because
of the large temperature gradient in the region. However, the operation of a normal
spectrograph takes a long time to raster a large region and thus impossible to capture
a large TR loop evolving rapidly. Such a study will require spectral observations with
larger field-of-view acquired in a very short time, which might be full-filled by the
forthcoming Multi-slit Solar Explorer (MUSE, De Pontieu et al. 2020).
– What are the properties of TR loops in the quiet sun? This group of TR loops are
still less known. Their populations, sizes, heights, plasma flows, magnetic properties,
and their connections to the lower and higher atmosphere are not fully understood.
To carry out such an investigation, higher resolution data from imagers, spectro-
graphs, and magnetographs are required.
– How do TR loops link to many other dynamic phenomena in the TR? Since TR
loops are representative of closed magnetic field lines in the region, how they link to
the other dynamic phenomena is important to understand how the magnetic field
governs the plasma evolution of the region.
– How do heating events take place in TR loops? A wide variety of dynamics in TR
loops have been attributed to bald patch topology of emergence flux (Toriumi et al.
2017), twisting and braiding topology of the emerging flux (Chen et al. 2025), or inter-
actions among different loops or ambient field (Zuo et al. 2026; Wei et al. 2022). How
they are really driven, and their modulated mechanisms are not yet clear, and further
investigation with high-resolution data and advanced simulations is warranted.
– How does the energy released in TR loops transfer to the ambient atmosphere? TR
loops are very dynamic, but how their dynamics connect to the ambient atmosphere

## Page 19


remains unknown. This is crucial for determining the role of the TR in the entire
chain of energy and mass transport in the solar atmosphere.
– Whether and how are TR loops evolved to coronal loops? Heating events in TR
loops are frequently observed, but so far, there is no observational evidence showing
that they are heated to the corona or that they are cooled down. This is due to the
lack of observations with suﬀicient spatiotemporal resolutions and/or continuous
representative temperatures, especially the latter one. High-resolution data from the
Daniel K. Inouye Solar Telescope (DKIST, Rimmele et al. 2020) and the forthcoming
2.5-meter Wide-field and High-resolution Solar Telescope (WeHoST) combined with
those observed in more spectral lines from the forthcoming MUSE and Extreme
Ultraviolet High-Throughput Spectroscopic Telescope (EUVST) should shed more
light on the subject how TR loops are connected to the cooler and hotter layers of
the solar atmosphere.
