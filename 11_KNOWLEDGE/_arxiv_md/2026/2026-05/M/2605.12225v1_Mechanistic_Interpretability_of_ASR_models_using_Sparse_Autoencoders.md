Abstract
Understanding the internal machinations of deep Transformer-based NLP
models is more crucial than ever as these models see widespread use in
various domains that affect the public at large, such as industry, academia,
finance, health. While these models have advanced rapidly, their internal
mechanisms remain largely a mystery. Techniques such as Sparse Autoen-
coders (SAE) have emerged to understand these mechanisms by projecting
dense representations into a sparse vector. While existing research has
demonstrated the viability of the SAE in interpreting text-based Large
Language Models (LLMs), there are no equivalent studies that demon-
strate the application of a SAE to audio processing models like Automatic
Speech Recognizers (ASRs). In this work, a SAE is applied to Whisper, a
Transformer-based ASR, training a high-dimensional sparse latent space on
frame-level embeddings extracted from the Whisper encoder. Our work un-
covers diverse monosemantic features across linguistic and non-linguistic
boundaries, and demonstrates cross-lingual feature steering. This work
establishes the viability of a SAE model and demonstrates that Whisper
encodes a rich amount of linguistic information.
1
Introduction
In the last few years, advances in the Transformer architecture have led to models with
the ability to produce text, audio, image and video. These impressive capabilities come
with an increased need to understand their internal representations. While there have been
many studies focused on understanding how text is generated by an LLM, for example by
monitoring the chain-of-thought reasoning tokens, it has remained challenging to interpret
the internal representations of these models.
Despite this difficulty, there have been some approaches that have seen success. For example,
one form of mechanistic interpretability that has shown promise is the Sparse Autoencoder
(SAE), which uses a sparse latent space to examine semantically meaningful information
(Bricken et al., 2023b). SAE models have been successful in analyzing the type of information
present in densely encoded data produced by intermediate layers of neural network models.
Recent work has focused largely on large language models, however the application of this
technique can be used on a wide variety of different types of models to extract interpretable
information from previously uninterpretable machine output.
While SAEs have been successfully applied to text-based LLMs, it is unclear whether they
can successfully be applied to interpret audio-based models, such as Whisper. Whisper,
an automatic speech recognition model (ASR), is capable of transcribing and translating
spoken word in a wide variety of languages (Radford et al., 2022). While it would be
expected that only linguistic information is encoded by Whisper, there has been evidence
that other information such as noise is used to condition the decoding of the transcript
(Gong et al., 2023). Whisper possibly encodes a wide variety of information that is not

In this work we explore the use of a SAE model to probe the internal data representations of

distinct representational patterns, providing evidence for the diversity of representations
encoded by Whisper. The key contributions of this work are threefold:
1. To the best of our knowledge, we believe this is the first work that applies a SAE-

understanding features across linguistic and non-linguistic boundaries, and enable
feature steering.
2. Our results show that Whisper learns a rich and diverse set of representations from
audio input, including phonetic, semantic, morphological, positional, and lexical
features.
3. Our findings demonstrate the utility of SAE as an effective interpretability tool for
probing the internal representations of Whisper.
2
Related Work
There has been a substantial body of research attempting to understand the internal repre-
sentations of large language models (Tenney et al., 2019; Haley, 2020; Li & Wisniewski, 2021;
Lasri et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2025; Misra & Mahowald, 2024; Houghton, 2025; Pan & Bergen,
2025; Houghton et al., 2025). While these results have generated valuable insights into the
internal representations of large language models, they have often relied on examining the
probability distribution of output tokens in carefully designed studies.
Recently, however, researchers have found success with mechanistic interpretation methods
for inspecting the internal workings of models. One such approach is the use of sparse
autoencoders (SAEs) to decompose the densely encoded information of a model into sparse
monosemantic features. Much of this work has focused on interpretability in text-based
Large Language Models (LLMs) as a means to better understand what information is being
encoded in these large and complex models (Bricken et al., 2023a; Huben et al., 2024; Tem-
pleton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025). For example, Bricken et al. (2023a) demonstrated the
effectiveness of training a Sparse Autoencoder on the internal activations of a 1-layer trans-
former model. They found that a Sparse Autoencoder trained this way learns monosemantic
features such as text written in Arabic. Further, Huben et al. (2024) built upon this and
demonstrated the effectiveness of Sparse Autoencoders on even more complex language
models. Specifically, they demonstrated that a Sparse Autoencoder trained on the internal
activations of Pythia (Biderman et al., 2023) learns human-interpretable features, such as
a feature for court cases and a feature for last names. Sun et al. (2025) found that a SAE
is capable of learning intrinsically dense features of an LLM, while Chanin et al. (2025)
examined SAE feature splitting and absorptions.
While these studies demonstrate that SAEs are a useful tool for interpreting the internal
representations of text-based LLMs, the evidence beyond this is mixed. It remains unclear,
for instance, whether this approach extends to other modalities, such as end-to-end ASR
models that take audio rather than text as their input. Some prior work has probed the
internal representations of Whisper by building a classifier from each layer of the encoder
(Gong et al., 2023), and others have applied the Sparse Autoencoder to a speaker recognition
model (Pluth et al., 2025). Neither of these, however, investigated linguistic representations
within an audio processing model. There is therefore no convincing demonstration to date
that the SAE is an effective tool for interpreting ASR models. Our work addresses this gap.

Method
In order to examine the internal representations of Whisper, a SAE was applied immediately
after the final layer of the encoder. Since the final layer of the encoder is directly processed
by the decoder, it potentially contains the most important information in order for the
decoder to accurately transcribe the audio.1 Figure 1 shows the Whisper architecture with
the SAE inserted. This analysis focuses entirely on the latent space of the SAE, with the
Whisper decoder only being utilized to examine the effects on the transcript after modifying
the latent values.

The SAE training framework used was developed based on the OpenAI sparse autoencoder2
which implements a k-Sparse Autoencoder (Makhzani & Frey, 2014; Gao et al., 2025). The
SAE model encoder is a simple linear layer with a topK activation. The SAE decoder is
another linear layer which reconstructs the Whisper embedding space. The SAE effectively
maps the 512 width Whisper base encoding to a large hidden layer of 16000 elements and
then back to the 512 elements. In order to constrain sparsity, the model was trained using
a TopK activation to constrain the number of latents which can be activated, in this work
K=45. This creates a total of 16.4 million trainable parameters3. The SAE is trained using
mean squared error (MSE) as the loss function between the input and output of the SAE.4
Since Whisper takes an input of 30 seconds of audio, all audio files are padded or trimmed to
30 seconds by default. To avoid training the SAE on padded frames, they are identified and
removed during SAE training. The final input data to the SAE is specifically the individual
frame-level Whisper embedding cut to the audio length. A given audio will generate up to
1500 frames (30 seconds worth) of training data.

3.2
Steering
Steering is a causal analysis that demonstrates that modifying a given latent (or latents) in
the latent space has a specific and predictable effect on downstream tasks. For example,
in Figure 1, the sparse latent vector would be modified then passed to the SAE decoder to
produce ϵ′. This modified encoding can then be taken as an input to the Whisper decoder
to produce a manipulated transcript. This is a powerful technique to demonstrate that the
latent index is a representation of the hypothesis feature, since the causal relationship is
clearly visible through differences between the transcript from before steering the latent and
after steering the latent.
In order to identify features, the latent space was explored for correlations between latents
and various linguistic and non-linguistic features, including language features, phonetic
features, lexical features, syntactic features, morphological features, and semantic features.
A latent can be considered to correspond to a given feature if its activation is predictive
of that feature. For each hypothesized feature, precision and recall values were computed
to quantify what proportion of activated files contain the hypothesized feature. For some
features, causality was confirmed through feature steering, which demonstrates that a latent
was not simply correlated with a given feature, but directly encode that feature.

3.2.2
Automated Labeling
To supplement the manual feature analysis, all latents were labeled using GPT-OSS 120B.
For each latent, the 100 files closest to the median activation strength were selected as
representative examples, and up to 20 were presented to the model with activated character
spans marked by asterisks (e.g., grad*ual*ly). The model was prompted to assign one of
seven categories: phonetic, orthographic, morphological, lexical, semantic, syntactic, or
diffuse for the examples. The model subsequently returns a structured JSON response
containing a label, explanation, confidence level, and category. Only high-confidence labels
were retained; lower-confidence responses were assigned the diffuse label with the leading
hypothesis preserved. A random subset of 100 of these latents was manually evaluated to
determine the quality of the automated labeling. The automatically labeled latents yielded
an accuracy of 76%. These results are reported in Appendix B.
4
Whisper SAE Results
The results of the feature analyses are presented in this section. The SAE reveals that Whis-
per encodes a surprisingly rich hierarchy of representations, spanning low-level acoustic
properties, positional information, and abstract linguistic features up to the level of se-
mantics. It should be noted however that latent indexes exhibit complex behavior with
both splitting and absorption. A latent associated with a feature may not be activated for
all samples containing that feature. There may be other latents with more narrow feature
characteristics. See (Chanin et al., 2025) for more detail description of feature absorption
and splitting.
We present our results organized by feature type: language features, phonetic features,
lexical features, syntactic and morphological features, and finally semantic features. The
focus is entirely on linguistic information, however there is evidence that non-linguistic
information is contained within the latent space, see Appendix C.
4.1
Language Feature
The effect of language was examined by looking at the differences in the latent spaces
between Spanish and English samples in the dataset. A simple analysis was performed to
evaluate whether or not the presence of single activation of a latent index in any frame of an
utterance could discriminate between English and non-English utterances. This approach
lead to the discovery of latent index 5106, which allows the classification of non-English
samples with a precision of 74.7% with a recall of 91.2%. Limited further analysis reveals
that many of the false positives appear to be non-native English speakers.
4.2
Phonetic Features
The Whisper encoder also encodes finer-grained linguistic representations. While it seems
necessary for Whisper to represent phonetic information in some manner, it is unclear how
exactly it does so. For example, it is unclear whether individual phone-level representations
are sufficient or whether richer, context-specific representations are required (see Ambridge,
2020, for an in-depth discussion).
This is further complicated by co-articulation – the phenomenon in which the acoustics
of phones can change depending on their surrounding phones (Daniloff & Hammarberg,
1973). This suggests that Whisper may represent the acoustic linguistic stimuli in terms of
larger-level representations such as diphones or triphones, rather than individual phones.
Indeed, after examining the latent space, it is clear that the model learns even more complex
representations. The SAE has encoded both phones, diphones, and potentially triphones.
Figure 2 demonstrates an example of a latent representing the diphone R UW1 (precision
value of 88.7%, recall value of 64.9%). Many further examples can be found in Appendix B.

4.3
Lexical Features
The existence of phonetic features is intuitive given that the system’s goal is to transcribe
speech. However, lexical features also seem possible especially in English where there is
not a 1-to-1 mapping between orthographic representations and phonetic representations.
For example, the letter “a” in (American) English can represent several different vowels,
such as /æ/ (as in cat), /A/ (as in father), /eI/ (as in cake), /E/ (as in many), and @ (as in about).
As a result, it may be advantageous for Whisper to encode lexical information in addition
to phonetic information. Indeed, this seems to be the case, as we see features that do seem
to be convincingly lexical such as latent index 28, which represents the word his and has a
precision value of 99.3% and a recall value of 81.4%.
4.4
Syntactic and Morphological Features
Linguistic features such as representations of morphological elements are not strictly neces-
sary to transcribe audio, however, given that the sparse autoencoder seems to encode indi-
vidual words, the model may also encode other abstract representations. An examination of
the SAE confirms that the model does seem to be learning morphological representations.
Figure 3 demonstrates the words which have the largest ratio of activated frames for latent
index 29. This latent index correlates with the suffix ‘ly’, which in English serves as an
adverbial marker (precision value of 87.2%, recall value of 17.8%). While the recall value
is somewhat low for this latent, this is likely due to feature splitting: there are multiple
different ‘ly’ adverbial latents, such as latent 9779 (See Appendix for an example B).

Semantic Features
Despite not being strictly necessary for transcription, semantic representations may help
resolve ambiguities such as homophones. Even for words without homophones, excessive
noise or other interference could result in poor transcription which can sometimes be

4.5.2
Profanity
Curse words are another semantic group which can be used in a wide variety of ways and
as special intensifiers. Similarly, the set of 331 utterances containing profanity was curated
from Mozilla Common Voice English. Through examining the encodings of these audio
samples, it appeared that latent index 3584 was an effective identifier of the presence of swear
words, with a recall of 35.4%. Further examination revealed that a significant number of
utterances containing the word ’damn’ were better represented using a separate latent, latent
index 104. Table 3 shows a sample of utterances which have activations of latent index 3584
and latent index 104. Together these latents account for a recall of 89.7% with a precision of
6.6%. It should be noted that profanity is quite rare in this dataset, occurring in 0.11% of the
entire corpus, based on a word-level count. The false positive rate is only 1.45%. It was also
noticed that many of the false positives occur in utterances which contain profanity, again
suggesting that the activation of these semantic latents may not be strongly temporally
constrained, with some utterances having activations through many frames of the encoding.
It is also possible that these latents may more generally represent anger or intensity, often
co-occuring with swearing. For example, one utterance with the transcript ”With clenched
fist, he hit the table.” has an activation of one of these latents, despite an absence of swear
words.

4.5.3
Cross-Lingual Activation
The results of Templeton et al. (2024) suggest that many latent indices may have semantic
activations that are independent of language. Since Whisper is trained to be a multilingual
ASR, it is possible to examine the same effect by curating audios with curse words in Spanish
and French using Mozilla Common Voice. Table 5 demonstrates that latent index 3584 is
similarly capable of steering in all three languages, here shown avoiding swear words in
the resultant transcript from the steered latent vector. This cross-lingual generalization
is particularly notable for French, which was entirely absent from the SAE training data,
suggesting that latent index 3584 encodes language-independent semantic information
inherent to the Whisper encoder.
5
Discussions
The present results demonstrate the effectiveness of the SAE as a tool to explore the rep-
resentations of the Whisper ASR model. More specifically, the present study reveals that
the Whisper encoder represents a surprisingly rich hierarchy of information — spanning
language, phonetics, lexical items, morphology, and semantics. The emergence of such
abstract linguistic structure in a model trained purely on transcription suggests that rich
internal representations are a natural consequence of learning to map speech to text at scale.
A broad examination of the latent space reflects this; Appendix B shows a subset of the
features discovered.5
In principle, Whisper only needs phonetic information to accurately transcribe the speech,
but there are several advantages to encoding additional information. Many different sen-
tences can have near identical phonetics. For example, the sky is cool and this guy is cool are
quite similar phonetically which can provide a challenge for transcription. However, if the
context of the previous sentence was about the warmth of the sun, then it is clear what the
correct interpretation is. In other words, semantic information provides an additional tool
to disambiguate sentences with multiple potential interpretations.
The encoding of multiple information sources may also reflect the inherent noisiness of
speech. Conversations are frequently interrupted by environmental noise, competing
speakers, or degraded signal quality, yet both humans and ASR systems must remain robust
to such interference. Noisy-channel models of human language processing propose that
listeners overcome this by actively combining multiple cues (phonetic, lexical, semantic, and
contextual) to recover the most likely intended message from a degraded signal (Gibson

6
Conclusion and Future Work
This study demonstrates the ability of Sparse Autoencoders as tools to interrogate the inner
workings of Whisper, an end-to-end ASR model. Basic building block features such as
phonetic and positional information were identified. However, increasingly complex and
abstract concepts like morphemes, words and even semantic information were discovered
to be present.
Future work may explore whether these same features are present among other end-to-end
ASR models or even between different sizes of the Whisper architecture. Additionally,
explorations of different latent dimension sizes may hint at the atomic concepts which these
models encode. Finally, training SAEs at different layers within ASR models may also reveal
how different types of information are propagated throughout ASR models. For example, it
seems likely that the type of information represented at each layer of the encoder may vary.
SAEs provide an avenue to explore how information develops and evolves throughout the
model.
6.1
Limitations
This work has three main limitations, mostly based around resources. First, the work has
only focused on a single sparse autoencoder. Future work will focus more on examining
SAEs built with differing latent dimensions and understanding how the features of the
latent space evolve. Second, only the Whisper base model was investigated, which is one
of the smallest Whisper models. It would be interesting to understand if larger Whisper
models encode information differently, or in fact if other end-to-end ASR models contain
the same depth of semantic information. Finally, although some non-English data was used,
English was the primary focus of the study.

A
Domain Space
Let E : X →Z denote a fixed Whisper encoder, mapping inputs from a high-dimensional
space X to a latent space Z. The encoder’s weights define an image manifold M = {E(x) :
x ∈X } ⊂Z, a structured subset of Z whose geometry is entirely determined by E. A sparse
autoencoder trained on the outputs of E operates exclusively in Z-space and therefore has
access only to sampled points on M. Critically, M is invariant to any choice of SAE training
data: an ablation that removes a semantic subset of X modifies the sampling measure over
M but cannot alter M itself. The SAE cannot recover structure that E does not encode, nor
can it fail to recover structure that E does encode due to ablation alone, provided coverage
of the manifold is sufficient.
An analogous invariance holds for SAE hyperparameters. The latent space dimensionality
and sparsity constraint parameterize a family of approximations φθ(M) to the manifold, but
varying θ does not alter M. Increasing latent dimensionality improves the granularity of the
decomposition; relaxing sparsity allows greater feature entanglement per activation. Both
affect approximation fidelity, not the underlying representational geometry. Consequently,
ablation studies over training data composition and hyperparameter searches are properly
understood as studies of approximation error under a fixed target M, rather than as
explorations of what structure is in principle recoverable from E. The object of interest, the
geometry of M as determined by the encoder (representing the discovered features), is not
a variable in either type of experiment.

C.1
Noise
As previously mentioned, noise information has been shown to be present in the Whisper
encoded data, however it was not clear how this information would be encoded within
the SAE latent space as an activation. Noisy samples were sourced from ESC-50 which
contains 50 distinct types of non-speech noise (Piczak, 2015). By comparing voiced and
non-voiced samples, the activation of latent index 15019 clearly discriminated the two classes.

C.2
Positional Features
The Whisper encoder uses a positional encoding which is added to the input features,
therefore some positional information likely exists in the encoding. However, since the
SAE encodes all information as discrete latent indexes, it is not clear how the positional
information would manifest. Figure 5 shows three examples of positional latent indexes, all
of which are tightly located temporally. Latent index 12816 has an mean activation timestamp
of 8.44 seconds and a standard deviation of 0.19. Latent index 15039 is at 10.09 seconds on
average with an SD of 0.04, and latent index 3604 activates at 14.04 seconds with an SD of
0.37. This positional representation is potentially useful for the decoder to determine word
order since the encoding is passed to the decoder with cross-attention.
