Abstract
Let P be a set of n points in the plane, where each element of P is assigned a weight ω(p),
positive or negative. In this paper, we present an algorithm that runs in O(n4 log n) time and
O(n) space to find two possibly overlapping axis-aligned rectangles A and B so as to maximize
the total weight of the points contained in the symmetric difference of A and B. The same
optimization framework can easily be adapted to solve related problems such as to maximize
the total weight in the symmetric difference of k ≥3 boxes and/or in the union of k ≥2 boxes.
Keywords: Computational Geometry, Plane Points Covering, Axis-aligned Rectangles, MCS-Tree.
1
Introduction
Covering weighted point sets with geometric objects is a classical problem in computational geom-
etry. Given a finite set P of points in the plane and a weight function ω : P →R, the goal is to
place one or more geometric shapes to optimize an objective function defined over the weights of
the points covered. For examples of the problem with different geometric objects, see [2, 9, 10, 12].
Depending on the shape and the objective, these types of problems arise in data analysis, clustering,
discrepancy theory, and machine learning [14, 17].
A particularly well-studied case is the maximum weighted box problem, where the shape is an
axis-aligned rectangle. Cort´es et al. [13] showed that in two dimensions this problem can be solved

Related problems have also been studied from different geometric and combinatorial perspectives.
In the maximum weight island problem, the goal is to select a subset of points whose convex (or
rectilinear convex) hull maximizes the total enclosed weight, leading to algorithms based on dynamic
programming and geometric transformations [5, 19]. Another closely related line of work considers
the case in which the weight function is restricted to values in {−1, 1}, and studies how well a
set of points can be separated using simple geometric regions such as strips, boxes, and guillotine
partitions [7, 15, 16, 18].
These problems share with ours the central challenge of optimizing
weighted point subsets induced by axis-aligned rectangular regions, and they further motivate the
study of efficient algorithms for multiple-box configurations.
In this paper, we study a natural extension of this problem in which two axis-aligned boxes are
allowed. We seek two (possibly overlapping) axis-aligned rectangles A and B that maximize the
sum of the weight of the points according to one of the following objective function: The symmetric
difference of A and B. Although problems involving two boxes have been studied under different
optimization criteria, such as minimizing area or balancing box [1, 6, 8], no previous work addressed
the maximization of weighted coverage under these overlap treatments.
This paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we introduce the Maximum-Weight Two Box
Symmetric Difference problem and describe the underlying optimization framework, including
the generalized MCS-tree data structure. In Section 3, we present the algorithmic results, discuss
extensions to k boxes, analyze the computational complexity, and establish hardness results when
the number of boxes is part of the input and other variants of the problem, for example, considering
the union of two boxes. The paper ends with a section of conclusions.
1.1
The MCS-tree
The MCS-tree [13] is a balanced binary tree data structure that allows efficient computation of a
maximum-weight consecutive subsequence of a given weighted sequence, and can also be efficiently
updated when the weights change.
Given a sequence X = (x1, . . . , xn) of n elements, we say that a block of X is a subsequence of
consecutive elements of X, where an empty block of zero length is allowed. A block X1 is contiguous
to a block X2 if the concatenation X1X2 is also a block of X. For a block Xi,j = (xi, xi+1, . . . , xj) of
X and any function f : {x1, . . . , xn} →R, we write f(Xi,j) = Pj
k=i f(xk) to simplify the notation.
For a sequence X = (x1, . . . , xn) and a weight function ω : {x1, . . . , xn} →R, the nodes of the
MCS-tree are the canonical blocks of X with X itself at the root. For any internal node U, its
children U1 and U2 are consecutive blocks with U = U1U2. Attached to each node U a set of values
A(U) is stored; one of these values is:
1. M(U) = maxV ω(V ), where V is a block of U.

The key point of the construction is that for any nonempty block U of X composed of two sub-

M(U) = max{M(U1), R(U1) + L(U2), M(U2)},

For single-element blocks U = (x), we have S(U) = ω(x) and L(U) = M(U) = R(U) = ω(x) if

In the previous construction, to simplify the presentation, only the actual values of A(U) were
recorded at each node; if the indices of the maximizing subsequence were desired, the argmax
indices should be recorded alongside the values. Note that the initial construction of the MSC-tree
takes O(n) time; and if the weight of a single element ω(xi) were to be changed, it could be updated
in O(log n) to account for the change. See [13].

Maximum-Weight Two Boxes Symmetric Difference

or horizontally aligned and that there is a given weight function ω : P →R that assigns positive or

sum of weights of points that can be covered by the symmetric difference of two axis aligned
rectangles (boxes). We propose a generalization of the MCS-tree data structure that enables an
efficient algorithms for this Maximum-Weight Two Boxes Symmetric Difference problem.

3.1
Maximum-Weight k Boxes Symmetric Difference
When k is fixed, the brute-force algorithm that analyzes all possible placements of the boxes takes
O(n4k) time and O(1) space. The natural extension of our framework would consider 2k horizontal
lines separating 2k−1 horizontal-strips as in equation (2), and 2k−1 vertical-strip adding functions
like equation (3). The algorithm that sweeps one line aided by the generalized MSC-tree for each
possible placement of the other lines will now require O(n2k log n) time.
The required extension of the generalized MCS-tree will now solve the one dimensional problem of
finding 2k −1 consecutive blocks in the abscise coordinate sorted sequence X, so the number A(U)
of values that need to be stored at each node are one M value, 2k −1 L’s values, 2k −1 R’s values,

the number k is considered as an input, the problem is NP-hard as the reduction in the next
subsection shows.
3.2
Hardness of the k-Boxes Problem
Given a finite set P = R ∪B of red and blue points in the plane, a (monochromatic) matching
of P with rectangles is a set of axis-aligned rectangles (boxes for short) such that each box covers
exactly two points of P of the same color, and also the boxes are pairwise disjoint. The boxes of a
matching are assumed to be minimal in the sense that the two points matched are vertices of the
box. Caraballo et al. [11] proved that the problem of deciding the existence of a perfect matching
with boxes is NP-complete, even in the following context: |R| and |B| are even numbers; there is
a set S1 of |R|/2 pairwise disjoint boxes matching all |R|; if two blue points can be matched with
a box, then such a box is disjoint from all the boxes of S1; and any box containing more than two
blue points necessarily contains a red point (this last condition can be derived from the reduction
in the NP-completeness proof of Caraballo et al. [11], when the colored point set is transformed to
be in general position).
Let P = R ∪B be a restricted instance, as above, of the problem of deciding the existence of a
perfect matching with boxes. If we assign weight −∞to each element of R, and weight +1 to each
element of B, then finding k = |B|/2 axis-aligned rectangles A1, A2, . . . , Ak that maximize the total
weight W of the points in the symmetric difference of A1, A2, . . . , Ak, can be used to decide the
existence of a perfect matching with rectangles as follows: There is a perfect matching if and only
if W = |B|. That is, when each Ai covers exactly two different elements of B. Hence, our problem
is NP-hard when the number k of boxes is part of the input.
3.3
Other variants
By just replacing by 1’s the zeros corresponding to the intersection segment of the two boxes in
each sector activation matrix, we solve the analogous Maximum-Weight Two Boxes Union
problem in exactly the same manner as before.
Corollary 1. The Maximum-Weight Two Boxes Union problem can be solved in O(n4 log n)
time and O(n) space.
When the union of k ≥3 boxes is considered, the number of cases is upper-bounded by the number
of cases required by the symmetric difference, but in that case, the combinatorial problem of actually

4
Conclusions
We studied the Maximum-Weight Two Boxes Symmetric Difference problem, extend-
ing the classical maximum weighted box problem to two possibly overlapping boxes under the
symmetric difference. Our main contribution is a unified optimization framework based on a gen-
eralized MCS-tree, which reduces the two-dimensional problem to a sequence of one-dimensional
optimization subproblems.
Various variants of related problems can be considered, including cases where coverage is determined
by the union of the boxes or the symmetric difference. It is worth identifying special cases induced
by the relative positions of the boxes, as certain configurations may admit more efficient solution
techniques than those established in the general framework proposed in this work.
