MCSHANE–RIVIN NORM BALLS AND SIMPLE-LENGTH
MULTIPLICITIES
NHAT MINH DOAN, XIAOBIN LI, AND VAN NGUYEN
Abstract. We use normal-turn estimates for McShane–Rivin norm balls to prove that, for
every complete finite-area hyperbolic once-punctured torus X, the number of simple closed
geodesics of length exactly L ≥2 is at most CX(log L)2. For the modular torus, this gives
#λ−1
M (m) ≤C(log log(3m))2
for every Markoff number m, improving the previous logarithmic Markoff-fiber bounds. These
estimates also give new quantitative information on the local geometry of McShane–Rivin norm
balls, including obstructions to infinite-order flatness at certain irrational directions.

There are two related counting problems. The cumulative problem counts simple closed
geodesics up to a length bound:
SX(L) = #{γ : γ is an unoriented simple closed geodesic and ℓX(γ) ≤L}.
The McShane–Rivin norm makes the cumulative counting problem on a once-punctured torus
especially concrete: SX(L) counts primitive lattice points in the dilated convex ball LBX, modulo
sign. McShane and Rivin used this viewpoint to study the growth of simple closed geodesics
on hyperbolic tori [MR95a, MR95b]. For general finite-type hyperbolic surfaces, Mirzakhani
proved that the number of simple closed geodesics of length at most L grows like a constant
times L6g−6+2n, using measured laminations, mapping class group dynamics, and Weil–Petersson
volume computations [Mir08]. Related counting results and the geodesic-current approach were
developed by Erlandsson–Souto [ES16, ES22].
This paper focuses on the corresponding boundary problem. Instead of counting lattice points
inside the dilated ball LBX, we count lattice points on its boundary:
#(L∂BX ∩Z2).
Geometrically, after restricting to primitive lattice points and quotienting by sign, this boundary
count gives the multiplicity function
mX(L) = #{γ : γ is an unoriented simple closed geodesic and ℓX(γ) = L}.
For context, multiplicity questions for simple closed geodesics on once-punctured tori are closely
tied to the Markoff theory of the modular torus, going back in particular to Cohn [Coh55].
Schmutz Schaller [Sch96b] conjectured that the multiplicity of the simple length spectrum of any
once-punctured torus is bounded above by 6; this can be viewed as a geometric generalization
of the Frobenius unicity conjecture in the modular case recalled below. McShane and Parlier
[MP08] studied the equal-length loci E(α, β) for pairs of distinct simple closed geodesics as
hypersurfaces in Teichmüller space, proved that the simple length spectrum is Baire-generically
simple, and showed that a naive one-holed-torus analogue of the Schmutz–Schaller bound fails
by producing one-holed tori with at least 12 simple closed geodesics of the same length.
Main results. We first state the global counting result. From the lattice-point viewpoint, the
general comparison is Jarník’s classical theorem [Jar26]: for every fixed bounded strictly convex
plane curve γ, #(Lγ ∩Z2) = Oγ(L2/3). The exponent 2/3 is optimal for general convex curves.
Gaster asked whether McShane–Rivin norm spheres satisfy a better bound [Gas22]. Our first
main result answers this question in a strong form.
Theorem 1.1. For every complete finite-area hyperbolic once-punctured torus X, there is a
constant CX > 0 such that
#(L∂BX ∩Z2) ≤CX(log L)2
for every L ≥2. Consequently,
mX(L) ≤CX(log L)2
for every L ≥2.

The conjectural benchmark for Corollary 1.2 is the Frobenius unicity conjecture [Fro13, Aig13].
It concerns positive integer solutions of the Markoff equation

The conjecture asserts that a Markoff triple (a, b, c) with a ≤b ≤c is determined by its largest
entry c. By the modular-torus correspondence recalled above, this is equivalent to the expected
uniform bound
#λ−1
M (m) ≤6
in our geometric convention. Many arithmetic partial results toward this conjecture are known;
see, for instance, [Bar96, But98, But01, Sch96a, LT07, Zha07, CC13].
Our result gives a growing upper bound, still far from the conjectural uniform bound.
Nevertheless, it improves the previously known general estimates. As noted by Gaster [Gas22,
Remark 1.13], Zagier’s analysis of Markoff triples [Zag82] gives a logarithmic bound
#λ−1
M (m) = O(log m).
Another logarithmic bound follows from Lee–Li–Rabideau–Schiffler [LLRS23, Corollary 1.5(b)].
The error term in the McShane–Rivin cumulative counting theorem [MR95b, Gas22] gives the
weaker super-logarithmic estimate
#λ−1
M (m) = O(log m log log m).
Our result gives the much smaller estimate
#λ−1
M (m) ≤C(log log(m))2.
In particular, the Markoff-fiber size is subpolynomial in log m: for every ϵ > 0,
#λ−1
M (m) = Oϵ((log m)ϵ).
We now return to the geometric source of these estimates: the McShane–Rivin norm ball.
McShane and Rivin described a sharp local picture: rational directions give corners with
exponentially small exterior angles, whereas irrational directions are expected to be flat to
infinite order [MR95b, Theorem 2.1]. Their paper gives a sketch of the argument, but, as
noted in [Gas22], a complete proof of this full local statement does not seem to have appeared.
Sorrentino–Veselov related this picture to Mather’s β-function and proved the corresponding
differentiability at irrational directions and non-differentiability at rational directions [SV19].
The boundary geometry of the McShane–Rivin norm ball is also adjacent to the Markov-
ordering problem. For context, Rabideau–Schiffler proved Aigner’s fixed-numerator ordering
conjecture using continued fractions and continuant polynomials, while Lee–Li–Rabideau–Schiffler
used cluster-algebra methods to prove the full set of Aigner ordering conjectures and stronger
slope-monotonicity results [RS20, LLRS23]. McShane gave a hyperbolic-geometric proof of
Aigner’s conjectures using convexity of the norm ball of the modular torus [McS21]. Following
this viewpoint, Gaster used one-sided slopes at the corners of the norm ball to characterize
sharper monotonicity ranges and to confirm conjectures of Lee–Li–Rabideau–Schiffler that refine
Aigner’s conjectures [Gas22].
Our first use of this boundary geometry is global. After separating the rational boundary
points of height at most H, we prove that the remaining boundary has exponentially small
normal turn. This is the mechanism behind the improved lattice count: on a small-turn arc,
consecutive lattice point chords have primitive directions in a narrow angular sector, and a
localized Jarník argument [Jar26] gives many fewer lattice points. The only nonlocal Farey-tree
input is a root-selection step: Bowditch’s sink orientation [Bow96, Bow98] shows that, for every
high Farey gap, the gap side carries the larger Fricke root. The endpoint estimates then make
the one-sided supporting lines of the gap arc exponentially close.

As another application, the same normal-turn tail estimate gives a quantitative version of
the local picture stated by McShane–Rivin [MR95b, Theorem 2.1]. Rational directions give

supporting line. The remaining question is the order of flatness at an irrational direction; it is
governed exactly by an exponential rational approximation rate. As a consequence, almost every
irrational direction is flat to infinite order, but every open interval also contains many irrational
directions with forced non-flatness.

Organization of the paper. Section 2 collects the preliminary material used throughout
the paper. It fixes the McShane–Rivin norm, the height convention, the Farey-tree notation,
the Fricke trace relation, Bowditch’s sink theorem, the height-gap decomposition, and the
convex-geometric language of supporting functionals and normal turn. Section 3 proves the main
geometric input, namely the exponential tail estimate for the normal turn away from low-height
rational directions. Section 4 combines this tail estimate with a localized Jarník-type lattice
count and proves the global counting theorem. Section 5 proves the local boundary theorem.
The last section is about some further generalizations and directions.

Finally, we introduce the convex-geometric notation for supporting functionals, one-sided support

6. Further directions and generalizations
We have focused on once-punctured tori to keep the normal-turn argument transparent, but
the method should apply more broadly. In particular, our results extend to hyperbolic one-holed
tori. If the boundary length is b, the trace surface becomes
x2 + y2 + z2 −xyz = 2 −2 cosh(b/2),
while the Vieta flip xu+v + xu−v = xuxv is unchanged. After replacing the endpoint-support
estimate by its shifted version, the exponential tail-turn estimate, the polylogarithmic multiplicity
bound, and the local flatness criterion all remain valid.
A parallel extension is to generalized Markoff systems. Gyoda–Matsushita [GM23] studied
x2 + y2 + z2 + k1yz + k2zx + k3xy = (3 + k1 + k2 + k3)xyz,
which contains the symmetric k-Markoff equation when k1 = k2 = k3 = k. Related generalized
Markoff numbers and orderings were studied by Banaian–Sen and Banaian [BS24, Ban25]. For
these generalized Markoff systems, the McShane–Rivin picture has a colored shifted analogue,
and the normal-turn method gives the same polylogarithmic fiber bounds and local flatness
picture.
A deeper direction is to move beyond the torus. For higher-complexity hyperbolic surfaces,
simple closed curves are not parametrized by a two-dimensional lattice. The natural replacement
is the length ball
{λ ∈ML(S) : ℓX(λ) ≤1}
in measured-lamination space, together with the integral multicurve lattice and Thurston
measure. This is the setting of Mirzakhani’s counting theorem and the geodesic-current approach
of Erlandsson–Souto [Mir08, ES16, ES22]. An analogue of the normal-turn decomposition in
this higher-dimensional setting could lead to multiplicity bounds, but new ideas are needed
because the Farey-tree structure used here is special to punctured and one-holed tori.
