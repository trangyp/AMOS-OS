Abstract—Quantum computing has demonstrated its signifi-
cant advantage over supercomputing for specific applications and
shown promising prospect, such as machine learning, cryptogra-
phy, finance, etc.. Quantum oracles are very common in many
quantum algorithms and oracle resource consumption directly
affects algorithm performance. However, existing oracle designs
often exhibit high resource overhead and limited compatibility.
Moreover, structured description tools and complexity analysis
methods are lacked. In this work, we introduces a Hierarchical
Recursive Synthesis-Evaluation (HRSE) model, enabling formal
description and precise quantum gate complexity analysis of
oracles. Based on this model, we propose an Adaptive Space-
Depth Trade-off (ASDT) algorithm for generating oracle struc-
tures under a fixed qubit constraint. We provide a theoretical
proof showing that the ASDT algorithm achieves the optimal
gate count for a given number of qubits. Experimental results
show that the ASDT algorithm reduces the average quantum
circuit depth by 53.99% compared with the W-cycle approach,
with the number of variables being 10, 15, and 20, respectively.
Index Terms—Quantum computing, HRSE model, ASDT al-
gorithm, space-depth trade-off, oracle circuit synthesis
I. INTRODUCTION
In the past decades, many quantum algorithms have been
proposed in solving different problems, such as machine
learning [1], [2], [3], cryptography [4], [5], [6], and finance [7]
[8]. The oracle is a key component of many algorithms, such
as Shor’s algorithm [9], Grover’s algorithm [10], and Simon’s
algorithm [11]. Oracle construction methods have a significant
impact on algorithm performance.
There is a significant body of research on oracle, which
has been extensively studied as an independent and modular
component in quantum algorithm design. Oracle techniques
have been applied to Boolean equations [12], [13], SAT
problems [14], [15], [16], graph coloring [17], [18] and AES
cryptanalysis [19], [20]. However, these approaches mainly
focus on the encoding of specific functions for particular ap-
plications. Other studies consider oracles with a single function
[21], [22], [23], but they face challenges when handling the
multiple combinatorial functions common in practice. One
line of research addresses this problem by optimizing the
management of intermediate computation results. Early work
by Bennett [24] introduced pebbling techniques to analyze

the scheduling of intermediate results to achieve flexible trade-

In general, oracle construction involves two related but
distinct issues: the encoding of individual functions and the
composition of multiple function evaluation results. The qubit
multiplexing technique employs multiple qubits to evaluate
different functions and then merges the results to satisfy
them simultaneously. Such multiplexing-based composition
has been widely adopted in oracle constructions across prior
works [12], [13], [22], [27]. Among existing studies, Li et
al. [13] provide a systematic treatments of multiplexing-based
oracle structures. Li et al. [13] described this structure as
a W-cycle, which reduces resource overhead by balancing
qubit count and circuit depth. Although this approach pro-
vides several preset construction schemes, it mainly relies
on discrete structural choices and may lead to suboptimal
results when the problem parameters do not match these
predefined configurations. Therefore, a systematic framework
for modeling the recursive structure of oracle computation and
analyzing its quantum circuit complexity is essential.
To address these issues, this paper introduces a Hierarchical
Recursive Synthesis-Evaluation (HRSE) model. The HRSE
model provides the first unified and formalized abstraction
of multi-function composition in quantum oracles. It enables
quantum gate complexity analysis and systematic comparison
of different construction methods within a common theo-
retical framework, and serves as a general analytical tool
for future studies. Based on this model, we further propose
an Adaptive Space-Depth Trade-off (ASDT) algorithm. The
ASDT algorithm dynamically balances the trade-off between
qubit count and circuit depth under given resource constraints.
We theoretically prove that, for a given number of available
qubits, the ASDT algorithm minimizes the number of function
computations. Compared with the state-of-the-art (W-cycle)
under the same problem scale and qubit count, the ASDT
algorithm achieves a significant reduction an average reduction
of 53.99% in circuit depth.

The quantum oracles studied in this work implement
Boolean functions of the form f : {0, 1}n →{0, 1}. Such
oracles are commonly represented in two forms: shift oracles
and phase oracles. Without loss of generality, we use the
XOR oracle as the primary representation. The XOR oracle
is a special case of the shift oracle, which simplifies the
presentation and avoids unnecessary technical details. The
modeling framework and algorithms proposed in this work
are equally applicable to general shift oracles.
As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the XOR oracle and the phase or-
acle can be converted into each other using multi-controlled X
(MCX) and multi-controlled Z (MCZ) gates. More generally,
shift oracles and phase oracles can be transformed into each
other using the quantum Fourier transform [28], [29], which
further allows the proposed methods to be applied in the phase
oracles.
Figure 1(b) shows an example in which three functions
(R = 3) are evaluated using qubit multiplexing. Importantly,
oracles constructed in this manner can themselves serve as
modular building blocks. These blocks can be recursively
combined to form higher-level oracles, yielding a hierarchical
structure that substantially reduces the number of required
auxiliary qubits, as illustrated in Figure 1(c). For clarity, an or-
acle that computes a single function is called a single-function
oracle, while an oracle that computes multiple functions using
qubit multiplexing is called a multi-function oracle.
In this paper, we use an abstract representation of func-
tions to develop a general methodology. Our focus is not
on how to encode a specific problem function, but on how
the computations of multiple functions should be organized
and composed once their quantum circuit implementations are
given. In practice, user’s only need to provide the quantum
circuit implementation of each function, which can then be
incorporated into the proposed framework to construct and
analyze the corresponding oracle structure.
III. HIERARCHICAL RECURSIVE SYNTHESIS-EVALUATION
MODEL
Constructing an optimal oracle structure requires tools for
quantum gate complexity analysis. The recursion depth of an
oracle is a key factor affecting its complexity. To address this,
we introduce a formal model for oracle structures, termed
the Hierarchical Recursive Synthesis and Evaluation (HRSE)
model.
To provide an intuitive understanding, we briefly outline
the modeling philosophy of the HRSE model. The HRSE
model aims to provide a structured analytical framework for
the complex architecture of multi-function oracles. The key
observation is that the composition of functions within a
multi-function oracle typically exhibits a recursive structure,
which can be naturally represented using a recursive tree.
Specifically, each function evaluation unit is modeled as a
node in the tree, while the containment relationships between
computation units are represented as edges. To enable com-
plexity analysis and optimization, each node is associated
with several attributes, such as its depth and the number of
child nodes. Under this modeling approach, even if an oracle
appears highly complex at the quantum circuit level, it can be
represented by progressively constructing the corresponding
HRSE tree: each function computation unit forms a node, and
edges are introduced according to the relationships among
these computations. By traversing and analyzing this tree
structure, the quantum gate complexity of the oracle can
be systematically evaluated and its structure can be further
optimized.
In Section III-A, the HRSE model and its validity con-
straints are defined. Section III-B then demonstrates how
the model can be used to analyze the quantum gate count
of an oracle. The HRSE model thus provides a systematic
foundation, identifies the key factors affecting gate complexity,
and establishes a theoretical basis for further optimization.

tation unit in the oracle; E is the set of edges, representing
the hierarchical containment relationships among computation
units; and A is the set of attributes, which assigns to each
node a quintuple of properties:

B. Complexity Analysis
Let δ denote the constant circuit overhead of a single-
function oracle, a value that depends on the chosen encoding
method. The cost of decomposing the MCX gate at a non-
leaf node vi into elementary gates is denoted as Γ(vi). This
term remains symbolic in subsequent calculations because the
proposed model and algorithm are designed to accommodate
multiple encoding methods. The value of Γ(vi) depends on
the number of control qubits in the MCX gate at node vi,
the number of auxiliary qubits available for its decomposition,
and the specific gate synthesis algorithm employed. Numerous
techniques for MCX gate decomposition have been proposed.
Without auxiliary qubits, an MCX gate can be synthesized
with O(n2) complexity using generalized Peres gates [30].
With a single auxiliary qubit, phase modulation enables a
linear resource cost O(n) implementation [31]. Ji Chu et al.
[32] achieved linear complexity for MCZ gates; by adding X
gates on the target qubit for phase conversion, an equivalent
linear-complexity MCX implementation can be derived. Nie
et al. [33] further demonstrated that with one auxiliary qubit,
an optimal decomposition achieving circuit depth and width
of O(n) is possible.

c(vi) = δ. According to the construction rules of the HRSE

can be recursively calculated from the complexities of all its

IV. ADAPTIVE SPACE-DEPTH TRADE-OFF ALGORITHM
Building upon the HRSE model and its complexity frame-
work, we introduce the Adaptive Space-Depth Trade-off
(ASDT) algorithm, which incrementally generates HRSE tree
structures to minimize root-node complexity under a given
qubit count constraint. Section IV-A details the ASDT algo-
rithm’s procedure, and Section IV-B provides a proof of its
optimality in terms of quantum gate count.
A. Algorithm Process
In the ASDT algorithm, the HRSE model tree Tk(m) is
constructed incrementally by progressively adding function
nodes. To efficiently select the node for expansion, the al-
gorithm employs two prioritization strategies. Strategy 1 (the
minimum-depth strategy) selects the node with the small-
est depth, thereby controlling the growth scale of the tree.

When multiple nodes share the same depth, Strategy 2 (the
maximum-size strategy) is applied, selecting the node with
the largest size to maximize its capacity for accommodating
child nodes. The rationale for these strategies stems from the
complexity formula in Equation 5. Strategy 1 minimizes node
depth. Because expanding a leaf node (i.e., splitting it) incurs

total cost. The combined use of these strategies guarantees the
optimality of the generated structure Tk(m); a rigorous proof
is provided in Section IV-B. Upon completion of the tree, a
single post-order traversal computes the complexity c(vi) and
the number of leaf nodes ℓ(vi) for each node vi.

C. Complexity Analysis for Non-Leaf Node Cost
This section provides a supplementary complexity analysis
of the non-leaf-node cost term P
κ(u)̸=0 2d(u) · Γ(u) to better
understand the overall complexity behavior. While we do not
provide a formal optimality proof for the non-leaf-node term,
this term is still reduced to some extent under the ASDT
algorithm.
The
ASDT
algorithm
minimizes
the
leaf-node
term
P
κ(v)=0 2d(v) · δ, i.e., the exponential sum over leaf depths.
This optimization also benefits the non-leaf-node term. Since
every non-leaf node lies on a path to a leaf, its depth cannot
exceed that of its descendant leaf nodes. Therefore, reducing
leaf-node depths directly constrains the exponential factor
2d(u) in the non-leaf-node cost.
For a non-leaf node u, Γ(u) denotes the decomposition
cost of the corresponding MCX gate, which depends on the
number of control qubits κ(u) (equivalently, the number of
child nodes) and grows polynomially, i.e., Γ(u) = O(κ(u)p)
for p ∈{1, 2} [30], [31], [32], [33]. Since the total number
of functions is fixed to m, the number of functions that can
be assigned to any non-leaf node is inherently limited. At the
same time, under a fixed qubit budget, the number of qubits
that can be allocated to a non-leaf node is constrained by the
available qubits k. Hence, the range of κ(u) is jointly bounded
by m and k. Moreover Γ(u) grows only polynomially with
κ(u), its variation is much smaller than that of the exponential
factor 2d(u). As a result, the non-leaf-node term is governed
primarily by node depth. Thus, minimizing the leaf node
cost P
κ(v)=0 2d(v) · δ also constrains the non-leaf node cost
P
κ(u)̸=0 2d(u) · Γ(u).

V. RESULTS
This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the
ASDT algorithm, with a focus on its quantum circuit depth and
the space-depth trade-off. We first compare the circuit depth of
oracles constructed using ASDT against those generated by the
W-cycle approach proposed by Li et al. [13] under identical
problem conditions. Subsequently, we investigate the impact
of auxiliary qubit allocation on circuit depth, demonstrating
ASDT’s efficacy in achieving an effective balance between
qubit count and circuit depth.
A. Comparison of Quantum Circuit Depth for the ASDT
Algorithm
This section presents a comparative analysis between the
proposed ASDT algorithm and the W-cycle approach intro-
duced by Li et al. [13]. The W-cycle approach was proposed
for solving systems of Boolean equations using Grover’s algo-
rithm. Its main objective is to combine the evaluation results of
multiple functions in a hierarchical manner, thereby reducing
the number of required auxiliary qubits. In our experiments,
the Boolean equation systems are encoded into phase oracles
using the ASDT algorithm and the W-cycle approach, respec-
tively. The oracle takes the form |x⟩|0⟩= (−1)f(x)|x⟩|0⟩,
where f(x) indicates whether the assignment x satisfies all
equations. The resulting oracle can be directly used in the
amplitude amplification step of Grover iterations.
In our evaluation, the oracle structures generated by the
ASDT algorithm are compared against the fixed W-cycle
structures, demonstrating a significant reduction in quantum
circuit depth. A key limitation of the preset W-cycle scheme
is its susceptibility to discretization effects, often resulting
in suboptimal gate counts. In contrast, as proven in Section
IV-B, the ASDT algorithm achieves theoretical optimality in
gate count. To ensure a fair comparison with the W-cycle
approach, we adopt the same problem sizes (n = 15, 20, 25)
and construct systems of Boolean equations from the test sets
available in its public code repository. For each problem size,
15 uniquely solvable equation instances are used. The circuit
depth is evaluated using a two-tier averaging method. First,
the average depth per Grover iteration is computed for each
individual equation instance. Subsequently, these averages are
aggregated across all 15 instances to yield the final depth
metric for each problem size. The circuit depth is measured
after decomposing the circuits into single-qubit gates and
multi-qubit gates such as CNOT and CCNOT, with MCZ gates
further decomposed into H and MCX gates.
As summarized in Table I, the performance of the W-
cycle approach is sensitive to its level parameter, due to the
tight coupling between the effective recursion depth and the
predefined level configuration in its framework. In this rigid
structure, the level setting dictates the average node depth,
and suboptimal level assignments can introduce significant
quantum circuit depth overhead. A detailed analysis of the
results reveals specific scenarios where the advantages of
ASDT become apparent. For example, at level 1, the W-cycle
fails to execute (denoted by ”–”) in several configurations,
while ASDT functions successfully, yielding low quantum
depth and minimal node depth. This indicates that ASDT
provides superior stability and lower resource requirements
in scenarios with shallow recursion. As the level increases,

the W-cycle approach is prone to rapid depth accumulation.
B. Space-depth Trade-off of ASDT Algorithm
The performance of the ASDT algorithm is governed by a
key parameter: the number of auxiliary qubits k, which is in a
direct trade-off relationship with the resulting quantum circuit
depth. Our experimental analysis, summarized in Figure 5,
systematically explores this space-depth trade-off by varying
k while holding the number of variables constant. The results
reveals a distinct two-phase behavior. Initially, increasing k
leads to a significant reduction in circuit depth, as evidenced
by the sharp decline observed from k = 5 to k = 9 for all
variable sizes (e.g., for n = 16, the depths drops from 23,975
to 11,816). This reduction occurs because additional qubits
reduce the need for costly qubit reuse and the serialization
of operations, thereby enabling a higher degree of parallel
execution and resulting in shallower circuits.
However, this improvement plateaus once k exceeds the
number of functions m required for the problem instance.
Beyond this critical point, further increases in k yield dimin-
ishing returns, and the circuit depth stabilizes. For example,
for n = 9, the depth reaches its minimum of 1448 at k = 9 and
remains unchanged for k = 10. This saturation indicates that
the circuit has achieved its maximum parallelism under the
given functions; supplying additional qubits beyond what is
functionally necessary does not further reduce the depth. This
observed threshold, therefore, identifies the minimal resource
allocation required to achieve the optimal circuit depth for a
given problem size.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper introduces the Hierarchical Recursive Synthesis-
Evaluation (HRSE) model, specifying its structural definition,
attribute correspondences, and the constraints required for
model validity. The framework provides a comprehensive
representation of XOR-oracle recursive structures, enabling an
intuitive characterization of complex recursive relationships.
Building on the HRSE model, we propose a quantum gate
complexity analysis approach that quantifies the number of
quantum gates in the oracle circuit by aggregating the costs
associated with nodes and edges in the model. This approach
reveals the primary sources of quantum gate complexity and
provides an analytical foundation for subsequent optimization
algorithms. The HRSE model is applicable not only to the
ASDT algorithm but also serves as a theoretical foundation
for future studies on oracle optimization.

Several open issues and directions for future work remain.
The current HRSE model and ASDT algorithm do not ac-
count for specific function encoding methods or decomposition
strategies for multi-controlled X (MCX) gates. The depth
of the circuit after MCX gate decomposition is significantly
influenced by the number of auxiliary qubits available for the
decomposition process. Future work will explore the joint op-
timization of auxiliary qubit allocation between the constraint
function computation and MCX gate decomposition stages to
achieve further reductions in circuit depth. Another important
direction is to investigate the asymptotic behavior of the
proposed model and algorithm under large-scale parameters,
and to establish more general analytical results for complexity
characterization. The proposed framework is highly general
and can be applied to a broader class of constraint satisfaction
problems (CSPs). In particular, evaluating the HRSE model
and ASDT algorithm on more complex problems, such as AES
cryptanalysis, may further demonstrate the effectiveness of the
proposed framework and optimization strategy. In addition,
