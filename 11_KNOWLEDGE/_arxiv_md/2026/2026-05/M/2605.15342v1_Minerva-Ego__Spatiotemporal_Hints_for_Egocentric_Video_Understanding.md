Abstract
Video reasoning models are a core component of egocen-
tric and embodied agents. However, standard benchmarks
for assessing models provide only evaluation of the output
(e.g. the answer to a question), without evaluation of inter-
mediate reasoning steps, and most provide answers only
in the text domain. We introduce Minerva-Ego, a bench-
mark for evaluating complex egocentric visual reasoning.
We extend recent high-quality video data sources recorded
from egocentric / embodied settings with a set of challenging,
multi-step multimodal questions and spatiotemporally-dense
human-annotated reasoning traces. Benchmarking experi-
ments show that state-of-the-art models still have a large gap
to human performance. To investigate this gap in detail, we
annotate each reasoning trace in the dataset with the objects
of interest required to solve the question, as spatio-temporal
mask annotations. Through extensive evaluations, we iden-
tify that prompting frontier models with hints of ‘where’
and ‘when’ to look yields substantial improvements in per-
formance. Minerva-Ego can be downloaded at https:
//github.com/google-deepmind/neptune
1. Introduction
Understanding and reasoning over events in the visual world,
both fine-grained and over long temporal spans, is a core
capability of humans. Reasoning about videos from an ego-
centric perspective is foundational to enabling effective and
broad deployment of assistive and embodied agents [14, 25].
Broadly, there has been remarkable progress towards the
development of video reasoning methods, evolving from sim-
ple action recognition on short clips [16, 22, 49] to more ad-
vanced capabilities over longer time horizons (LVBench [43],
VideoMME [15], Neptune [29], and others). The develop-
ment of the latter has been accelerated by the rise of multi-
modal LLMs [1, 6, 38], which can perform complex video

tasks on hour long videos [43], and are steadily approaching
human performance on these recent benchmarks [15, 29, 30].
However, despite this progress, a critical gap remains
in evaluating complex reasoning for long-form, egocentric
video. Existing egocentric benchmarks often focus solely
on the final answer and overlook the underlying reasoning
process [27]. While recent work such as EgoTempo has
begun to address temporal localization [35], it fails to assess
reasoning over extended contexts.
Attempts to improve the reasoning process of models
have led to a paradigm shift where models spend more time
thinking [2] i.e. producing intermediate thoughts towards
a final answer, often via additional compute at inference
time [21, 41]. The idea has been introduced in the video do-
main as well, where benchmarks such as MINERVA [30] as-
sess the types of errors made specifically for video reasoning.
These works, however are primarily exocentric. Unlike ex-
ocentric videos, which often feature detached observations,
egocentric data centers on close-up, frequent interactions
with objects, producing a continuous flow of activities that
demand a model’s ability to identify and focus on key objects
of interest over time.
To address these limitations, we introduce a new bench-
mark for long-form egocentric video understanding, called
Minerva-Ego. Our benchmark, built on top of HD-EPIC [34],
features 1,160 complex, hand-crafted questions that require
reasoning over long temporal contexts.
Crucially, each
question includes five answer choices annotated with dense
spatio-temporal reasoning traces, which link timestamps and
segmentation masks to relevant objects of interest (see Fig. 1
for examples).
We find that state-of-the-art video understanding models
(Gemini [42], GPT [32] and Claude [4]) perform poorly
on our dataset, with a large gap to human performance.
Analysing the errors in more detail, we find that models
make reasoning mistakes in two key areas—perceptual iden-
tification and temporal grounding of objects—which is es-
pecially significant since object manipulation is central to
egocentric video. To remedy this, we perform experiments

2. Related Work
2.1. Recent VideoQA Datasets
Many valuable video datasets have been proposed recently.
TemporalBench [9] and PerceptionTest [33] include a va-
riety of tasks, such as video QA, captioning, and ground-
ing, but use relatively short videos (a couple of minutes).
Similarly, CinePile [37] and VideoVista [24] focus on short-
form content (avg ∼160s). VideoVista [24] is notable for its
broad coverage of 19 understanding and 8 reasoning tasks.
These benchmarks, along with InfiniBench [5] (max 52 min
videos), and Neptune [29], rely on semi-automatic pipelines
using LLMs for annotation. In contrast, our dataset is en-
tirely manually annotated focusing on quality. TVBench [12]
specifically focuses on temporally challenging questions
which require visual information. E.T. bench [26] focuses on
multi-event and time-sensitive understanding, using human-
annotated videos with a maximum length of 795s. Video-
MME [15] bridges the gap between short and long-form
content, and datasets such as LVBench [43] (avg 68 mins)
extend to even longer videos. In contrast to these existing
benchmarks, our work provides human-annotated reason-
ing traces with spatio-temporal grounding, which enables
evaluating the model’s reasoning ability on top of reporting
accuracy.
2.2. Egocentric Datasets
A few recent datasets focus specifically on Egocentric VQA.
EgoSchema [27] is a long-form video question-answering
dataset containing over 5,000 human curated multiple choice
question answer pairs from Ego4D [17] with five choices.
EgoTempo [35], a dataset specifically designed to evaluate
temporal understanding in the egocentric domain, includes
500 question-answer pairs on egocentric videos with an aver-
age duration of 45s. HourVideo [10] targets very long videos
spanning durations of 20-120 mins and features 12,976 five-
way multiple-choice questions from 500 manually curated
egocentric videos selected from Ego4D [17]. HD-EPIC [34]
is a kitchen-based egocentric dataset collected in-the-wild
but with detailed annotations matching those in controlled
lab environments. Similarly, EgoVQA [13] contains 600
question-answer pairs and covers queries of actions, posi-
tions, objects and counting which are relatively broad (e.g.
“What am I doing?”) and does not capture fine-grained tem-

2.3. Reasoning Traces for Video
As discussed in the previous section, few datasets provide
auxiliary data beyond final answers. ScienceQA [39] con-
sists of science questions about images, and provides the ac-
companying lectures, which when used for training results in
increased performance with fewer data points. Some recent
works have aimed to develop automatic labeling pipelines
to instead generate noisy auxiliary information at scale. Vi-
sual CoT [40] provides bounding box pseudolabels gener-
ated by off-the-shelf models for image VQA, while VideoE-
spresso [18] does something similar for video, constructing
a pipeline connecting different frozen models together to
label bounding boxes for sparse key frames. VideoCoT [44]
is perhaps the closest to our work, aiming to generate text-
based chain of thought for videos; however, we note key
differences. The primary goal of VideoCoT is to present a
semiautomatic pipeline for training purposes. In contrast we
present a fully manually annotated, high-quality dataset for
evaluation. The videos in VideoCoT are all taken from Ki-
netics700 [22], making them shorter and limited in domain.
Finally, their automated rationales tend to contain substantial
information about the video that is unrelated to the particular
query, rather than providing specific reasoning for the given
question.
2.4. Spatio-temporal Highlighting
Recent advances in VLMs have spurred interest in vi-
sual prompting techniques like Set-of-Mark (SoM) [47],
which demonstrate that overlaying semantic marks (numbers,
masks, or bounding boxes) significantly unlocks ground-
ing capabilities in static images. However, applying these
concepts to egocentric video introduces unique challenges,
namely reasoning over dynamic, long-form content where
objects frequently enter and exit the field of view, undergo
occlusion, or change state. We extend visual highlighting
to the spatiotemporal domain. Distinct from SoM, as well
as [7, 45], which operate on single frames, we introduce
spatiotemporal hints that guide the model not only where
to look (via spatial highlighting) but also when to look (via
temporal frame selection).
Unlike “Look, Remember, and Reason” [8], which im-
plicitly learns grounding by training a specialized encoder on
surrogate tasks, our approach uses explicit spatio-temporal
highlighting directly on the pixels. This avoids the need
for architectural changes or retraining, enabling us to probe
perceptual failures in fixed, frontier models like Gemini and
GPT.
3. Minerva-Ego
3.1. Statistics
Minerva-Ego consists of 1,160 questions covering 156
unique egocentric videos. Questions are designed to require
multi-step reasoning to solve and come with 5 multiple-
choice options, with one being the right answer. Addition-
ally, we provide a detailed reasoning trace for each question
that explains how to arrive at the correct answer. The reason-
ing traces contain references to timestamps in the video and
references to objects with ground-truth segmentation masks.
All annotations are entirely hand-written by highly skilled
raters trained for this task. Fig. 1 shows example questions.
The videos are sourced from the HD-EPIC [34] dataset,
a high-quality egocentric video dataset. We re-use their

ground-truth object segmentation masks. While HD-EPIC
also has QA annotations, they are generated using standard-
ized templates automatically and do not focus on multi-step
reasoning. To maintain manageable inference costs for large
models, we limit the dataset to approximately 1,000 ques-
tions, given that each requires multi-step reasoning and con-
tains detailed traces.
Detailed statistics are provided in Fig. 2. Videos cover
a diverse range of lengths, from 10 seconds to 75 minutes.
Reasoning traces are long and detailed, averaging 122 words
each, and refer to both timestamps and objects of interest.
The average number of objects referenced in a reasoning
trace is 2.8, but some refer up to 20 objects. A more-detailed
breakdown appears in Section 6.1 of the supplementary.
3.2. Dataset Creation Pipeline
Our pipeline consists of the following 3 steps:
1. Selection: We start with all videos from the HD-EPIC [34]
dataset, and overlay the existing ground-truth segmentation
masks for all objects labeled on to frames of the video.
2. Annotation and Verification: We use highly qualified
raters to annotate questions, answers, and reasoning traces.
For reasoning traces, raters are asked to reference the key
objects of interest needed to answer the question, after being
provided all the segmentation masks for the video. We then
perform a quality review process, where we ask a separate
set of annotators to try and answer the questions, and for the
ones that they got wrong, we ask a third set of annotators to
look at both sets of annotation and reconcile them, changing
the questions, options or reasoning traces if necessary. Note
that our entire annotation and quality review process is done
manually to ensure high quality and avoid model bias.
3. Post-processing: Finally, we post-process the dataset by
identifying and filtering modality bias (e.g. questions answer-
able with only the text question and answers) following best
practices outlined in prior work [19, 23, 30, 43], with more
details provided in the supplementary materials, Sec. 6.2.

4.1.2. Reasoning Analysis
We evaluated the reasoning traces using an LLM according
to the MINERVA rubric (MiRA) introduced in [30]. The
MINERVA rubric (repeated in full in suppl. Fig. 9) evaluates
across four dimensions, namely Perceptual correctness, Tem-
poral grounding, Logical reasoning and Completeness, and
scores answers on a 3-point Likert scale. Fig. 3 (left) shows
the MiRA scores for four different models. Overall, all four
models score well on Logical Reasoning and Completeness
while Perceptual correctness is the weakest dimension. On
all four dimensions, the Gemini models seem to outperform
the others. To check for scorer bias, we re-evaluated using
GPT-5; the relative model ordering is preserved (supplemen-
tal Section 9.1).
We also evaluated the recall of relevant objects, extracted
from the ground truth reasoning, in the model reasoning
traces. Fig. 3 (middle) reports both object-level recall, where
we use the complete description of an object (e.g. ‘left half of
an apple’), and noun-level recall where we use only the main
noun in the object description (e.g. ‘apple’). All four models
are only able to reference 20-50% of the relevant objects
mentioned in the ground truth reasoning traces which could
explain the low scores for Perceptual correctness. This also
provides a clear indication that models may not be focusing
on the correct objects in the videos, motivating our method
in Sec.4.2, which highlights the key objects in space and
time and feeds them to the model at test time.
4.1.3. Frame Ablations.
Tab. 2 analyzes the influence of how many video frames
are sent with the question. We limit this ablation to Gemini
due to costs. As can be seen, both Gemini models con-
tinue to benefit from an increased number of frames. This
demonstrates that our dataset requires fine-grained temporal
information to solve, and that Gemini can effectively uti-
lize large contexts. Note existing reasoning datasets such as
MINERVA [30] tend to plateau at around 256 frames. On
the other side of the spectrum, given only the question and
answer choices and no visual data (i.e. frames), performance
is barely above random, with single-frame performance not
much better showing that temporal information is necessary.

Oracle experiment. The original HD-EPIC [34] dataset
comes with some ground-truth segmentation annotations:
each time an object is moved, the object is segmented in
the first and last frame of this movement. Since this is quite
sparse, we use our own re-implementation of SAM2 [36]
to fill in the segments of the frames in between. Hence we
have decent segmented tracks of all objects when they are
moved. For each object mentioned in the reasoning traces,
if it was annotated, we linked to the corresponding object
ID. This link exists for 82% of the objects in our reasoning
traces. Using this, we can highlight for each question the
(majority of) relevant objects. We highlight the object by
drawing a mask contour in red, or surrounding the object
by a box or ellipsoid. Additionally, we draw the class name
in the frames. Fig. 5 visualizes what these annotations look
like. Furthermore, since Gemini natively predicts class labels
and boxes, instead of highlighting the objects we interleave
frames with objects and box coordinates using the Gemini
box prediction format (non-json).

5. Conclusion
We introduce Minerva-Ego, a benchmark for evaluat-
ing complex egocentric visual reasoning consisting of a
set of challenging, multi-step multimodal questions and
spatiotemporally-dense human-annotated reasoning traces.
Through extensive evaluations, we identify surprising suc-
cesses and key areas for improvement for frontier models in
video reasoning. We leverage our fine-grained annotations
to better categorize reasoning errors at a more nuanced level,
and show the value of our spatio-temporal highlighting for
improving performance.

6.2. Dataset Post-processing details
As described in Sec. 3.2, our pipeline consists of selection,
annotation + verification, and post-processing. We elaborate
here on the verification and post-processing stages. We fol-
low best practices and guidelines that have been established
in prior work [19, 23, 30, 43], which note that benchmarks
often have issues regarding modality biases (e.g. questions
that are answerable with only text), and propose solutions
to help mitigate these. The verification stage is described in
the main paper, and was conducted through manual human
studies, separated reviewer pools, and several rounds of inter-
vention and refinement. In this context, the post-processing
stage is a further step of refinement on top of our initial
(model-free) process. Here, we use the same model ensem-
ble of open and closed-source text-only language models
in [30] (e.g. Deepseek, GPT-4o, Gemini Flash, and Qwen)
and take their consensus agreement as a set of candidate
questions that merit further review. As described in prior
work, this technique is designed in order to avoid discarding
difficult questions that models may have answered correctly
by chance. Notably however, rather than completely dis-
carding all questions in the consensus set like in prior work,
this set was subject to one more round of review – if the
question could be “corrected” (e.g. the text bias was readily
clear and it was clear how a change in the question or an-
swer choices could remove this bias) then this measure was
taken (we found that this was the case in the majority of the
questions, where “too much” was given away in the question
itself). If the question required more than a simple change,
the question was removed.
7. Rater Guidelines
All textual data in Minerva-Ego was manually annotated

7.2. Human Study
We used a disjoint pool of 12 raters for the human study,
by asking the rater leads to ensure that the same rater who
proposed the question (or even saw the video before) is not
the same as the one who performs the human study. Hence no
rater performing the humans study has seen the video before.
All raters had access to the full videos. Rater guidelines are
below:
Goal: You will be given a video, a question, and 5 answer
choices. Please watch the video and pick the correct answer
(A, B, C, D, E). (Like a multiple choice exam). You can

rewatch various parts of the video. Please take as much time
