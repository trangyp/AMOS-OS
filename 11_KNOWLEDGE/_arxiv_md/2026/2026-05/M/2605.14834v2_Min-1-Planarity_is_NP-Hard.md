Abstract
In this paper, we show that it is NP-hard to determine whether a given graph admits a min-1-planar
drawing.
A drawing of a graph is min-k-planar if, for every crossing in the drawing, at least
one of the two crossing edges involves at most k crossings. This notion of min-k-planarity was
introduced by Binucci, Büngener, Di Battista, Didimo, Dujmović, Hong, Kaufmann, Liotta, Morin,
and Tappini [GD 2023; JGAA, 2024] as a generalization of k-planarity.

Introduction
A drawing of a graph is called a min-k-planar drawing if, for every crossing between two
edges, one of the two edges involves at most k crossings. This notion of min-k-planarity was
introduced in 2023 by Binucci, Büngener, Di Battista, Didimo, Dujmović, Hong, Kaufmann,
Liotta, Morin, and Tappini [5, 6], as a generalization of k-planarity, where every edge can
involve at most k crossings. Hliněný and Ködmön [13] later continued the study of min-k-
planar graphs by investigating the relationship between simple and non-simple min-k-planar
drawings, showing that for every fixed k ≥2 there is a graph that admits a (non-simple) min-
2-planar drawing but admits no simple min-k-planar drawing. We mention that, although
the terminology is recently introduced, Wood and Telle [18, 19] implicitly studied (circular)
min-k-planar drawings earlier.
In recent years, the study of beyond-planar graphs has been prominent in the graph
drawing community, aiming to explore ways of drawing non-planar graphs. Many beyond-
planar graph classes are defined by a set of forbidden crossing configurations or patterns in a
drawing, including the classes of k-planar graphs, fan-planar graphs, k-quasi-planar graphs,
and min-k-planar graphs [15]. However, the recognition problems for such classes are typically
NP-hard. Indeed, recognition is known to be NP-hard for the following classes: k-planar
graphs, for every fixed k ≥1 [12, 17]; fan-planar graphs [7], even if the rotation system
is specified [3, 4]; and a variant of quasi-planar graphs [2]. The complexity of recognizing
quasi-planar graphs remains open to the best of our knowledge.
Our result.
This paper adds another example to the above list of beyond-planar graph
classes, namely the problem of recognizing min-1-planar graphs.
▶Theorem 1. Min-1-Planarity is NP-complete.
The high-level idea of the reduction follows that of Grigoriev and Bodlaender [12], which
was used to show the NP-hardness of testing 1-planarity. However, in contrast to 1-planar
drawings, a min-1-planar drawing allows the existence of an edge with an unbounded
number of crossings, which makes it difficult to build gadgets for showing NP-hardness. Our

Related results.
For every fixed k, testing whether a given graph admits a min-k-planar
drawing with at most c crossings is fixed-parameter tractable with respect to c [15]. It is also
known that the class of optimal min-1-planar graphs, namely n-vertex min-1-planar graphs
with the maximum possible number 4n −8 of edges, coincides with the class of optimal
1-planar graphs [5, 6]. However, the NP-hardness of recognizing 1-planar graphs does not
imply that of min-1-planar graphs by this relationship, as optimal 1-planar graphs can in
fact be recognized in polynomial time [8, 9].

Preliminaries
We follow the standard notation in graph theory (see, for example, the textbook by Dies-
tel [10]). In this paper we only consider simple graphs, namely, graphs without a self-loop
and parallel edges. The complete graph on t vertices is denoted by Kt. The length of a path
is defined as the number of edges on the path.
Let G be a graph and let Γ be a drawing of G in the plane. A drawing Γ is simple if (1)
two edges cross at most once, (2) two adjacent edges do not cross, and (3) three edges do
not cross at a single point. For an edge e ∈E(G), let crΓ(e) be the number of edge crossings
that e involves. For an integer k, the drawing Γ is a min-k-planar drawing if, for every edge
crossing in Γ between two edges e and f, either crΓ(e) ≤k or crΓ(f) ≤k holds. A graph G
is min-k-planar if it admits a min-k-planar drawing.
The problem Min-1-Planarity then asks, given a graph G, to determine whether G is
a min-1-planar graph. By the following fact, this is in fact equivalent to asking whether G
admits a simple min-1-planar drawing.
▶Lemma 2 (Proposition 2.2, [13]). Every min-1-planar graph admits a simple min-1-planar
drawing.
Two drawings are isomorphic if there is a homeomorphism of the sphere that maps one
drawing to the other. Two simple drawings are weakly isomorphic if they have the same set
of pairs of crossing edges.

Uncrossable Edge
Throughout this paper, by an uncrossable edge between u and v we mean the subgraph
depicted in Figure 1. Formally, for two distinct vertices u and v of a graph, an uncrossable
edge between them can be added in the following manner:
1. add three copies of K4 to the graph;
2. add an edge between u and each of the vertices added in step 1;
3. connect u and each of the vertices added in step 1 with three paths of length 2;
4. add an edge between v and each of the vertices added in step 1;
5. connect v and each of the vertices added in step 1 with three paths of length 2;
6. connect u and v with ten paths of length 2.
This section is devoted to showing the following lemma, which will be used in Section 4
to obtain a min-1-planar drawing where each uncrossable edge is essentially crossing-free,
namely, not crossed by any other part of the graph.

5
Conclusions
In this paper, we showed the NP-hardness of Min-1-Planarity. A natural next open
question is the complexity of testing min-k-planarity for fixed k ≥2. We expect all of them to
be NP-hard and some ideas in this paper could be used to show that. However, for k ≥2 it is
known that there is a graph that admits a min-k-planar drawing but no simple min-k-planar
drawing [13], while our proof heavily uses the fact that every min-1-planar graph admits a
simple min-1-planar drawing. Hence, one may have to come up with a new idea to handle
non-simple min-k-planar drawings.
