Abstract
We study the problem of Hamiltonian sparsification: given a parameter ε ∈(0, 1) and an
n-qubit Hamiltonian H which is the sum of r-local positive semi-definite (PSD) terms H1, . . . Hm,
our goal is to compute a sparse set L ⊆[m], along with weights w : L →R⩾0 such that for every
state |ψ⟩∈C2n,

When the set L is significantly smaller than m, this reduces the number of terms in the
underlying system, while still ensuring that the behavior of the system is essentially unchanged.
Perhaps surprisingly, despite the strength of this approximation condition, we show that many
Hamiltonians indeed are sparsifiable to a number of terms much smaller than nr, including: (a)
Hamiltonians where each term is an r-local Pauli string, (b) Hamiltonians where each term is an
r-local random operator of rank R, for R ⩾2r−1 + 1, and (c) Hamiltonians where each term is an
arbitrary r-local operator of rank ⩾2r −1 (a.k.a. Quantum SAT).
Taken together, our results show that the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians is a robust phe-
nomenon, contrary to prevailing belief (see for instance, Aharonov-Zhou ITCS 2019, QIP 2019).
Our results find applications, for instance, to better (semi-)streaming algorithms for quantum
Max-Cut, answering a question left open by Kallaugher and Parekh (FOCS 2022). In fact,
our results even codify that quantum systems are often easier to sparsify than their classical
counterparts.
On a technical level, our results rely on an extensive analysis of a quantity that we call the
“non-redundancy” of a Hamiltonian. Roughly speaking, this measures the largest set of terms
where each can be “uniquely satisfied”: i.e., such that for each term Hi in the set, there is a state
|ψ⟩which gives term Hi non-zero energy, and all other terms H̸=i zero energy. This quantity is
inherently related to the sparsifiability of the Hamiltonian, but is often easier to work with, as it
is governed entirely by the manner in which the kernels of the Hamiltonian terms interact.

Hamiltonian sparsification is not a new direction of study; indeed, inspired by the success
of analogous sparsification methods in the classical world (see for instance, works on graph
sparsification [BK96, SS11, ST11, BSS14]), several works have sought to understand whether this
task of Hamiltonian sparsification is feasible, as it would have wide-ranging impacts in both
algorithm design and complexity theory. Perhaps most notably, motivated by potential applications
of sparsification to the Quantum PCP conjecture [AAV13], the work of Aharonov and Zhou
[AZ19] provided a negative result in this direction. Indeed, even for the weaker goal of so-called
“gap simulation,” which intuitively only requires preserving the energy of ground states of the
Hamiltonian along with the spectral gap, [AZ19] shows that there exist Hamiltonians H for which
sparsification is not possible. In the time since this work [AZ19], this result has often been cited
as a “no-go” theorem, showing that Hamiltonian sparsification has inherent limitations (see, i.e.,
[GY19, KPBC20, KP22]).
However, a recent and separate line of work has instead studied the ability to sparsify constraint
satisfaction problems (CSPs), the natural classical analog of Hamiltonians. In this setting, a CSP C is
given by a collection of n variables x1, . . . xn ∈{0, 1}, along with m constraints of arity r, where each

Hamiltonian sparsification seeks to preserve the energy on every state |ψ⟩, CSP sparsification
instead seeks to preserve, for every assignment to the variables x1, . . . xn, the number of satisfied
constraints.
Despite the tremendous success of sparsification in graphs [BK96, SS11, ST11, BSS14], recent work
[FK17, BŽ20, CKN20, KPS24, KPS25, BG25, BGJ+25] has shown that the sparsification landscape in
CSPs is significantly more intricate; indeed, just as shown in Aharonov and Zhou’s work [AZ19],
there exist classical CSPs where sparsification is not possible. However, the results are not entirely
negative: the works of [KK15, FK17, BŽ20, KPS24, KPS25, BG25] show that there are huge classes
of CSPs (including many natural classes like XOR, 3-SAT, and r-SAT), for which significant
sparsification is still possible. Intuitively, these works show that the sparsifiability of CSPs is not
absolute, but rather depends inherently on the types of interactions (predicates) that are allowed in
the CSP.
This more general understanding of CSP sparsification motivates a natural question about
Hamiltonian sparsification:
Is Hamiltonian sparsification always impossible? Or are there natural classes of Hamiltonians
for which significant sparsification is possible?
In what follows, we give a strongly affirmative answer to the second question.
1.1
Our Work
As our first result, we study the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians specified by Pauli strings (we call
these Pauli Hamiltonians). In this setting, we consider a Hamiltonian H where each local term is of
the form P1 ⊗· · · ⊗Pn, where Pj ∈{Id, X, Y, Z} acts on the jth qubit. When we say that these terms
are r-local, this is equivalent to requiring that #{j : Pj ̸= Id} ⩽r. Note that arbitrary Pauli strings
define operators which have eigenvalues that are ±1, thus, in our setting we consider Hamiltonians
where each local term is a Pauli string shifted by the identity matrix (to ensure that each term is
PSD), i.e., Hi = P1 ⊗· · · ⊗Pn + Id.2 Such Hamiltonians naturally arise in the analysis of quantum
stabilizer codes, with each Pauli string corresponding to a stabilizer term of the code [ABN23]. For
this class of Hamiltonians, we show the following:

In this setting, we show that even for these very rank-deficient Hamiltonians, significant
sparsification is still possible:
Theorem 1.2 (Generic Hamiltonian Sparsification, Informal). Let H be a Hamiltonian with m terms
H1, . . . Hm, where each term Hi = (Mi)|Ti ⊗IdTi, for Mi a random PSD matrix of rank R, where R is a
fixed constant ⩾2r−1 + 1. Then, with high probability over the random matrices Mi, for any ε > 0, there is a
randomized (classical) algorithm running in time poly(n, m) which, with probability 1 −2−Ω(n), computes
an ε-sparsifier L ⊆[m], w : L →R⩾0 of H such that |L| = eOr(ε−2n).
In the above, we use Or to hide large constant factors that depend on the parameter r, and eO to
hide poly-logarithmic factors in n.
Theorem 1.2 shows that for arbitrarily large constants r, it is still the case that Hamiltonians
with random r-local terms of rank 2r−1 + 1 are sparsifiable to size eO(ε−2n). For comparison,
in the classical setting of CSP sparsification, the analog of a random r-local operator of rank
2r−1 + 1 would be a random predicate P : {0, 1}r →{0, 1} with 2r−1 + 1 satisfying assignments
(i.e., |P −1(1)| = 2r−1 + 1). In Section 4.3, we show that in general these random-predicate CSPs
require sparsifiers of size Ω(n⌈log2(r)−3⌉) (where the exponent grows, even for constant r). Together
with Theorem 1.2, this shows that, contrary to prevailing belief [AZ19], random Hamiltonians are
significantly more sparsifiable than their classical random CSP counterparts.

the space allotted to the algorithm grows from o(n) to eO(n).

Underlying our improved sparsifier constructions is an extensive study of a quantity that we
call the non-redundancy of Hamiltonians, which generalizes the classical theory of CSP non-
redundancy [BCH+13, BCK20, Car22, BG25, BGJ+25, LGE26, BGP26]. More formally, given a

ground-states of the sparsifier is strictly larger than the ground-space of the original Hamiltonian H.
In general, when a Hamiltonian H is redundant (i.e., not non-redundant), we define its
non-redundancy to be the size of the largest subset of terms which is non-redundant.
Given the ample success non-redundancy has had in characterizing the sparsifiability of various
classical CSPs (in particular, [BG25]), we believe that a strong theory of Hamiltonian non-redundancy
is necessary toward the further construction of Hamiltonian sparsifiers.
To understand the non-redundancy of a Hamiltonian, one must reason about the manner in
which the the ground states of the various terms of H interact. For example, as we elaborate upon
in Lemma 1.8, the crux of proving Theorem 1.2 is to study the interactions between the kernels of
two random r-local Hamiltonians which share a single qubit. More precisely, we show that if the
rank of these individual Hamiltonian terms is sufficiently large, then the ground states of these two
local Hamiltonians are entirely disjoint! This disjointness condition is critical for us getting lower
bounds on the spectrum of our Hamiltonian, leading to a near-optimal sparsifier–this is discussed
in much more detail in Section 1.4.2. Similar observations are used in the proof of Theorem 1.3.
However, our contributions to the study of non-redundancy extend far beyond the technical
concerns of these sparsifier constructions. In particular, one of our primary contributions is a more
systematic understanding of non-redundancy in translation invariant Hamiltonian systems where
each r-local Hamiltonian is the same PSD predicate matrix M ∈C2r×2r. We let NRD(M, n) denote
the size of the largest non-redundant Hamiltonian H all of whose terms are copies of M applied to
different r-qubit subsets (i.e., each term is of the form M|T ⊗Id[n]−T ).
This is the natural Hamiltonian analogue to the study of the non-redundancy (or sparsifiability) of
classical CSPs. In fact, we show in Proposition 2.9 that the study of non-redundancy in Hamiltonians
is strictly more general than in the CSP world, as one can represent any classical predicate as a
diagonal matrix M ∈C2r×2r with {0, 1} entries. Thus, any classical result on the sparsifiability or
non-redundancy of constraint satisfaction problems [KK15, FK17, JP19, BŽ20, CJP20, LW20, CKN20,
Car22, KPS24, KPS25, BG25, BGJ+25] immediately translates to understanding Hamiltonians with
diagonal operators M.

Obviously, the class of r-local Hamiltonians is much richer than the space of diagonal matrices.
As such, we require novel tools for studying the non-redundancy of r-local Hamiltonians.
As our first contribution, we give a criterion for which sparsification is impossible. In particular,
if our predicate matrix M can be expressed as the tensor product of rank 1 matrices, then we can
construct r-local non-redundant Hamiltonians with Ωr(nr) terms. This result can be viewed as a
quantum analogue of known lower bounds for the ANDr predicate [FK17, BŽ20, Car22, KPS25] as
well as a vast generalization of “Example A” in [AZ19]. See Section 7 for full details.
Theorem 1.5 (Tensor Lower Bound). Let M = M1 ⊗M2 ⊗· · · ⊗Mr be a predicate matrix where
M1, . . . , Mr ∈C2×2 are non-zero rank 1 PSD matrices. Then NRD(M, n) ⩾(⌊n/r⌋)r ⩾Ωr(nr).
As mentioned above, Theorem 1.5 implies immediate, stronger lower bounds for Hamiltonian
sparsification, both in the sense of preserving the energy on every state |ψ⟩, and also for the weaker
task of only preserving the spectral gap.
Our second contribution is a systematic understanding of predicate matrices M acting on
2-qubits (analogous to the arity-2 predicate classifications of [FK17, BŽ20, BCK20]). In particular,
we show that the setting of Theorem 1.5 is the only situation in which NRD(M, n) is superlinear!
Theorem 1.6. Let M ∈C4×4 be a nonzero predicate matrix. Then NRD(M, n) = Θ(n) unless M =
M1⊗M2 where unary predicate matrices M1, M2 ∈C2×2 have rank one, in which case NRD(M, n) = Θ(n2).
Given a Hamiltonian H whose terms are each of the form M|T ⊗Id[n]−T , the crucial idea behind
Theorem 1.6 is to study the automorphism group of the ground state of H. In particular, we isolate
any two terms Ha, Hb in our Hamiltonian H which share a single qubit. Say the first term acts on
the first two qubits {1, 2} and the second term acts on the first and third qubits {1, 3}. Roughly
speaking, we show that for any state in the common ground of both of these two terms, the state
must be invariant under swapping the second and third qubits (see Lemma 8.3). Crucially, the
automorphisms of a quantum state can be composed, so from just a handful of these automorphisms
we can deduce a large family of additional ones. As such, we can deduce that whenever the
underlying interaction graph of Hamiltonians forms certain (bipartite) cycles, the Hamiltonian itself
must be redundant (see Lemma 8.4)! From here, a simple linear bound on the non-redundancy
quickly follows. See Section 8 for further details.
Our final contribution to the study of NRD is a further understanding of the non-redundancy
of generic r-local Hamiltonians. In particular, while Theorem 1.2 applies to M ∈C2r×2r of rank
at least 2r−1 + 1, we can adapt the techniques of Theorem 1.6 to improve our understanding of
translation-invariant Hamiltonians of slightly lower rank.
Theorem 1.7. Let M ∈C2r×2r be a random predicate matrix of rank R ⩾2r−1 −1. Then, with probability
1 over M, NRD(M, n) ⩽eO(n).

qubit again induce an automorphism in the ground state. However the automorphism which is
created is now a composition of r −1 transpositions rather than a single one (see Lemma 9.2). This

automorphism increases the size of the overall automorphism group by a factor of at least 2, and
(2) the total size of the automorphism group is at most |Sn| = n!. Thus, there can be at most
2 log2(n!) = eO(n) non-redundant constraints.
1.3
Broader Context and Comparison to Related Work.
We do remark that our setting differs slightly from that of [AZ19]. In their work, Aharonov and
Zhou primarily study the weaker problem of gap-simulation mentioned above, which requires
preserving the ground states along with the spectral gap of the Hamiltonian (and places no stronger
requirements on the higher end of the spectrum). [AZ19] studies this task through the lens of
degree-reduction (reducing the maximum number of terms that any qubit participates in) and dilution
(reducing the total number of terms). Our setting aligns with this goal of dilution.
Of particular note however, is the fact that the negative results in [AZ19] rely on an inability
to increase the interaction strength between any two qubits, which is often important for building
physical quantum systems. In our stronger setting which asks for full-spectrum simulation, reducing
the interaction strength between qubits is not possible, as this inherently reduces the energy on
the upper end of the spectrum. This setting where increased interaction strength is permitted is
also studied in [AZ19], where it is shown that degree reduction is possible, albeit, by introducing
poly(n) extra auxiliary qubits (a commonly allowed operation, see [CM16, ZA21]). However, for
the task we study in this paper of dilution (reducing the number of terms in the Hamiltonian),
[AZ19] prove no analogous results, instead showing only impossibility results for certain classes
of Hamiltonians. Our results hold without introducing extra qubits; the only modification to the
underlying Hamiltonian is the removal of certain terms, and the re-weighting of others. Indeed,
even the underlying operators in the individual terms are unchanged (up to scaling).
Nevertheless, the problem we study of Hamiltonian sparsification (or sometimes called “full
spectrum simulation”) is still natural in many contexts. Indeed, as discussed above, many problems
like quantum MAX-CUT (i.e., see [PM17, GP19, AGM20, PT21, KP22, HNP+23, Pid25]) or in general,
the maximization of the energy of a stoquastic Hamiltonian (see [JGL10]) require sparsifiers that
do preserve the upper end of the Hamiltonian’s spectrum, while others require the preservation
of the lower end of the spectrum [ŞS21, ZS24]. We thus view our full-spectrum sparsification
requirement as a natural algorithmic question, as it serves as an important preprocessing step for
downstream quantum algorithms. Indeed, by reducing the number of distinct terms (i.e, performing
sparsification), downstream algorithms can operate more efficiently on Hamiltonians with smaller
descriptive complexity and fewer terms.
At a broader level, our results point to an ability to perform general size reduction in certain
classes of Hamiltonians. Such procedures have been widely studied in the context of the Quantum
PCP conjecture previously; see, for instance, [Ara11, Has21, AAV13, AZ19]. However, our results
are lacking in two regards: first, as mentioned above, the sparsification can increase the interaction
strengths of the remaining terms, and second, our sparsification result does not hold for every
Hamiltonian. Nevertheless, we view it as a promising open question whether our techniques can
be repurposed for this setting. In particular, our primary technical insight of studying Hamiltonian
7


## Page 10


non-redundancy deals with this same fundamental objective of trying to preserve the ground state of
a Hamiltonian while reducing the number of terms (and thus degree) as much as possible.
Finally, we note that the study of quantum non-redundancy is closely related to the recent
study of conditions for the sparsifiability of general families of PSD matrices. Indeed, while the
study of CSP sparsification (as described above) using the notion of non-redundancy has been very
fruitful, the techniques used to prove these results are highly combinatorial, thus preventing their
use in more general contexts. However, [BKLM26] generalizes the notion of non-redundancy in
a more quantitative manner to define the quantity known as connectivity threshold, which allows
them to prove sparsification results about families of PSD matrices even when there might not
be any underlying combinatorial structure. [BKLM26] analyzes a simple “importance sampling
sparsification” algorithm (á la [SS11]) and shows that just like non-redundancy is the appropriate
notion of sparsification in CSP sparsification, connectivity thresholds are the appropriate notion
of sparsification in PSD sparsification. Our results can be interpreted as developing a framework
towards rigorously understanding what these “connectivity thresholds” look like in Hamiltonians.

To give the reader a sample of the technical methods used in this paper, we give proof sketches of
two of our main results: Theorem 1.1 on sparsifying Pauli Strings and Theorem 1.2 on sparsifying

1.4.1
Pauli String Sparsification
We now give a brief overview of the proof of Theorem 1.1. In this setting, we are given some
local Pauli strings (each term shifted by identity so that the minimum eigenvalue becomes 0).
Before we describe how we prove Theorem 1.1, we invite the reader to appreciate why Hamiltonian
sparsification is in general a harder problem than classical CSP sparsification. When we frame
classical CSP sparsification in terms of Hamiltonians, the corresponding Hamiltonians are diagonal
(in the computational basis {|0⟩, |1⟩}⊗n), and in particular it suffices to reason about the energies of
the basis vectors alone, which gives the problem a very combinatorial flavor.
However, given two local Hamiltonians MT = M|T ⊗IdT and MT ′, where T, T ′ intersect,
MT , MT ′ no longer commute, and the usual combinatorial tools fail.
Nevertheless, for the case of Pauli strings, we can restore commutativity by partitioning the
underlying hypergraph into partite pieces. More formally, suppose the non-identity terms in our
Pauli string are P1, . . . , Pr. Further suppose the hypergraph H underlying our Hamiltonian was
r-partite, i.e. the qubits can be partitioned into sets V1, . . . , Vr such that H ⊆×

terminates in O(log n) ⩽eO(1) many rounds. This proof sketch can be generalized to hypergraphs,

Sparsifying Random Hamiltonians
In this section, we will study the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians using random predicate matrices
M ∈C2r×2r.
Remark 4.1 (Distribution assumptions). Throughout this section, we will consider matrices M
which have rank R ⩾2r−1 +1, and whose span can be written as span(M) = span(v1, . . . vR), where
the vectors vi ∈C2r are chosen at random from a distribution with absolutely continuous joint
support, and uniform boundedness (see Definition 2.19, Claim 2.18). Furthermore, we suppose that
this distribution is fixed only as a function of r. We then let the number n of qubits grow independently
of this distribution over predicate matrices.
With this notation in place, our main theorem is the following:
Theorem 4.2. Let M ∈C2r×2r be a random predicate matrix of rank R ⩾2r−1+1 drawn from a distribution
in accordance with Remark 4.1 and let H = {(Ti, M) : i ∈[m]} be an arbitrary Hamiltonian over n variables
and m terms. Then, with probabilty 1 −or(1) over the choice of M, for any ε > 0, there is a randomized
algorithm running in time poly(n, m) which computes a weight function w : H →R⩾0 such that w is an
ε-sparsifier of H and the size of w is bounded by eOr(ε−2n) with probability 1 −2−Ω(n).
24


## Page 27


Remark 4.3. Note that we use Or(·) to denote that the leading constant of the sparsifier size has
a constant which depends on the arity r (in particular, on the exact non-zero eigenvalues of the
predicate matrix M). Importantly, the arity r and the predicate matrix M are fixed independently of
the number of qubits n, and so as the number of qubits n increases, the size of the sparsifier scales
only linearly in n (and the error parameter ε).
We can also prove a version of Theorem 4.2 which holds when the individual predicate matrices
are different. Formally, we show:
Theorem 4.4. Let H = {(Ti, Mi) : i ∈[m]} be an arbitrary Hamiltonian over n variables and m terms,
where each predicate matrix Mi ∈C2r×2r is random of rank R ⩾2r−1 + 1, independently drawn from a
distribution in accordance with Remark 4.1.
Then, for any ε > 0, there is a randomized algorithm running in time poly(n, m) which computes a
weight function w : H →R⩾0 such that w is an ε-sparsifier of H and the size of w is bounded by eOr(ε−2n)
with probability 1 −2−Ω(n) over the randomness of the Mi’s and the algorithm.
Remark 4.5. Going forward, when we describe matrices as being “random”, we mean exactly that
they are drawn from a distribution as described in Remark 4.1. For brevity of the statements, we
omit this qualifier.
Additionally, going forward, we will use Proposition 2.3 to assume that the underlying
Hamiltonian is r-partite WLOG, which leads to extra logarithmic factors in the sparsifier size.

Conclusion
In this paper, we systematically studied the sparsifiability of Hamiltonians composed of PSD local
terms. In comparison to Hamiltonians with negative eigenvalues [AZ19], a very rich family of
Hamiltonians admit near-linear sparsifiers. A crucial tool we used for constructing these sparsifiers
was a quantum analogue of the concept of non-redundancy which captures the interactions between
the ground states of various terms in the Hamiltonian. In addition to constructing a few families
of sparsifiers, we also systematically studied the non-redundancy of a few types of Hamiltonians,
showing the potential for the further construction of Hamiltonian sparsifiers.
Overall, we find that the study of hamiltonian sparsification has been largely unexplored, despite
the promise it holds. We hope that others will continue to study questions in this direction, and so
we conclude the paper with a few crucial open questions:

Question 10.1. For an arbitrary predicate matrix M ∈C2r×2r, what is the relationship between
SPR(M, n, ε) and NRD(M, n)? In particular, when is SPR(M, n, ε) = eOM(NRD(M, n))?
By a result of Brakensiek and Guruswami [BG25] in combination with Proposition 2.9, we know
that the answer to Question 10.1 is yes whenever M is a classical predicate matrix. A natural
quantum extension of [BG25] would be to first study Question 10.1 for projection matrices M, i.e.
matrices M all of whose eigenvalues are 0 or 1.
We also highlight a particularly well-studied special case of Question 10.1: Quantum Cut.
Question 10.2. What is the sparsifiability of Quantum Cut: MQC = (|01⟩−|10⟩)(⟨01| −⟨10|)?
Although MQC has rank 1, MQC cannot be decomposed as the product of two rank-1 tensors.
Thus, by Theorem 8.1, we know that the non-redundancy of M is linear, suggesting the the
sparsifiability of M could also be near-linear.
