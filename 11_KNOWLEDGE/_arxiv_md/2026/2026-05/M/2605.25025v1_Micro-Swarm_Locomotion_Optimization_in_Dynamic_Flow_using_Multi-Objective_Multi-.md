Micro-Swarm Locomotion Optimization in Dynamic Flow using
Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
Josef Berman and Oren Gal
Abstract—Coordinating micro-robotic swarms in physiologi-
cally realistic, time-dependent fluid environments remains an
unsolved challenge for biomedical and environmental applica-
tions. We present a hybrid Computational Fluid Dynamics–Multi-
Objective
Multi-Agent
Reinforcement
Learning
(CFD-MO-
MARL) framework that directly couples a high-fidelity in-
compressible Navier-Stokes solver with decentralized proximal
policy optimization to learn physically consistent swarm control
strategies in oscillatory flow. Sixteen magnetically actuated micro-
robots navigate a pulsatile arterial waveform (peak systolic
velocity 400 mm/s) within a 2 mm channel, simultaneously op-
timizing upstream progression, energy conservation, and motion
smoothness. Competing policy gradients across these objectives
are reconciled using Projected Conflicting Gradient (PCGrad)
surgery, which projects conflicting gradient components into
orthogonal subspaces to prevent destructive inter-objective in-
terference. Without PCGrad, energy efficiency and smoothness
rewards collapse to near zero within 10,000 training steps
while progress exhibits persistent large-amplitude oscillations,
confirming that gradient conflict resolution is a structural re-
quirement rather than an optional refinement in this domain.
The converged policy achieves a progress reward of 6.5-7.0,
a sustained energy efficiency of 0.63–0.65, and near-maximum
smoothness (0.97–0.99), representing improvements exceeding 8
reward units over brute-force baselines on the primary objective
while both baselines yield negative energy efficiency throughout.
Training reveals three emergent behavioral phases not encoded
in the reward function: a collective two-layer hydrodynamic
throttling formation that suppresses peak channel velocities from
700 mm/s to approximately 400 mm/s during forward flow, a
cycle-synchronized ratchet mechanism that exploits flow reversals
for upstream repositioning, and an individualized final approach
as agents near the success boundary. These results establish that
time-dependent fluid-agent interactions can be captured directly
within multi-objective reinforcement learning loops, offering a
scalable and physically grounded paradigm for micro-swarm
control in biomedical navigation, environmental monitoring, and
industrial microfluidics.
Index Terms—Multi-Robot Systems, Model-Learning for Con-
trol, Learning and Adaptive Systems, Swarms.

A. Micro-swimmer Propulsion Mechanisms
At the microscale, where viscous forces dominate inertial
effects, propulsion must circumvent the constraints of the
“scallop theorem”, which prohibits net motion via reciprocal
strokes in a Newtonian fluid. Seminal theoretical investigations
by Hancock and Purcell demonstrated that non-reciprocal,
traveling-wave undulations of slender filaments generate a net
thrust by continuously breaking time-reversal symmetry [1],
[5]. These insights established the fundamental role of helical
and flexural deformation modes in producing locomotion at
vanishing Reynolds numbers and underscored the necessity of
cyclic shape changes or external field interactions to achieve
propulsion.
Based on these principles, a variety of bio-mimetic micro-
swimmers have been engineered to emulate the solutions
of nature. Magnetic helical swimmers employ rigid ferro-
magnetic helices actuated by rotating magnetic fields; by
tuning field frequency and amplitude, they achieve precise
three-dimensional steering, though deployment often requires
bulky coils or permanent magnets proximate to the swimmer
[6], [7]. In contrast, acoustic streaming motors exploit ultra-
sonic transducers to establish steady streaming flows around
asymmetric micro-structures. These contactless actuators offer
high actuation rates and localized control but can generate
undesirable heating and cavitation effects in surrounding fluids
[8].
A second class of autonomous swimmers leverages chem-
ical energy: Janus particles with asymmetric surface coatings
catalyze reactions (e.g., peroxide decomposition) to produce
self-generated concentration gradients, driving phoretic motion
without external fields. While inherently autonomous, these
swimmers contend with limited directional persistence and
fuel depletion over time [9]. Similarly, photophoretic micro-
swimmers absorb focused light to create localized thermal gra-
dients, which induce thermophoretic forces; these approaches

Despite the rich diversity of propulsion strategies, the

of these microscale propellers under oscillatory or pulsatile

Understanding how unsteady background flows interact with
swimmer kinematics is critical for translating micro-swimmer
technology into practical biomedical and environmental appli-
cations.
B. Swarm Coordination in Steady Flows
Swarm robotic systems draw inspiration from collective behav-
iors in biological populations, such as fish schooling and bac-
terial clustering, where simple, local interaction rules give rise
to resilient, emergent patterns [11]. In microfluidic Poiseuille
flows, magnetic micro-robotic swarms exploit rotating or
oscillating external fields to trigger self-assembly into one-
dimensional chains or compact clusters. By synchronizing field
frequency and orientation with the background flow speed,
these swarms can navigate upstream against the pressure-
driven profile or drift downstream in a controlled manner [12],
[13].
At the heart of these maneuvers lies the delicate balance
between magnetic dipole interactions, which draw neighboring
particles into aligned formations, and viscous drag forces that
tend to disperse them and oppose motion. Researchers have
demonstrated that by tuning the field strength and rotation rate,
one can modulate the dipole-dipole attraction to overcome
Brownian dispersion, preserving coherent formations over
distances comparable to channel lengths. Such field-modulated
navigation strategies have been validated in straight micro-
channels and simple bifurcating networks, enabling swarms
to reverse direction or traverse junctions without distributed
sensing or inter-agent communication.
Despite these successes, existing coordination methods rest
on the assumption of a time-invariant flow field. In many
real-world settings, blood vessels, respiratory pathways, and
environmental conduits, pressure and velocity profiles oscillate
or pulsate. The influence of such unsteady flow conditions on
swarm coherence, steering accuracy, and collective stability
remains largely uncharacterized, presenting a critical gap in the
translation of micro-robotic swarms to dynamic, physiological,
and industrial environments.
C. Challenges in Oscillatory Fluid Environments
Pulsatile flow in blood vessels features phase-lagged velocity
profiles and evolving boundary layers that fluctuate throughout
the cardiac cycle, creating time-varying shear stresses on
suspended particles [3], [14]. Similarly, engineered pipelines
subject to cyclic pumping generate oscillatory currents that
impose rapidly changing pressure gradients and recirculation
zones [2], [15]. Under these conditions, micro-swarms may
experience trapping in transient vortices during deceleration
phases or be driven into vessel walls when flow direction
reverses. The unsteady shear environment also modulates lift
forces on individual agents, potentially disrupting previously
stable formations. Crucially, the spatiotemporal interplay of
wakes shed by neighboring swimmers can either reinforce col-
lective motion or lead to intermittent clustering and dispersion,
depending on the synchronization of their actuation cycles [4].

E. Reinforcement Learning in Fluid Locomotion
Reinforcement learning (RL) frames control as a Markov
decision process where agents learn policies by maximizing
cumulative rewards through interaction [24]. Model-based RL
improves sample efficiency using learned dynamics surrogates
[25], [26], but constructing accurate models for oscillatory
fluid environments is challenging. High-fidelity CFD exhibits
strongly nonlinear, high-dimensional dynamics where small
approximation errors accumulate, producing physically in-
consistent predictions [27]. For N-agent swarms, modeling
pairwise hydrodynamic coupling requires O(N 2)-dimensional
state spaces, making surrogate learning intractable [28].
Model-free algorithms like PPO, SAC, and TRPO by-
pass explicit dynamics modeling, directly optimizing policies
through sampled interactions [29], [30]. Despite higher per-
sample costs from expensive fluid simulations, they eliminate
surrogate-modeling bottlenecks and learn from physically con-
sistent simulations, yielding adaptive, decentralized controllers
[31]. Recent work has demonstrated model-free RL for single-
agent swimming in steady flows, achieving efficient gaits [9],
[32].
F. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning and Multi-Objective
Optimization
1) Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL): Micro-
swarm coordination requires multi-agent reinforcement learn-
ing (MARL), which extends RL to multiple interacting
decision-makers [33], [34]. MARL faces challenges beyond
single-agent settings: partial observability, non-stationary envi-
ronments as policies evolve, and ambiguous credit assignment

for joint actions [35], [36]. Fully decentralized approaches
suffer from instabilities, while centralized methods are im-
practical for micro-swarms with limited communication [37].
Centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE)
has emerged as the dominant paradigm, leveraging global
information during training while executing local policies
[38]. Key algorithms include MADDPG with centralized
critics [35], QMIX using value factorization [39], COMA
with counterfactual advantages [36], and MAPPO extending
PPO to multi-agent settings [40]. Communication mechanisms
like CommNet and graph neural networks further enhance
coordination [41], [42].
2) Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL):
Multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) balances
competing goals by treating rewards as vectors rather than
scalars, seeking Pareto-optimal policies [43], [44]. Simple
scalarization via weighted sums requires a priori preferences
and may miss non-convex Pareto regions [45]. Alternative
methods like Pareto Q-learning maintain non-dominated Q-
vector sets [46], while deep MORL uses multi-head architec-
tures with gradient conflict resolution [47].
When objectives conflict, naively summing gradients under-
mines individual progress [48]. Projected Conflicting Gradient
(PCGrad) resolves this by projected conflicting gradients onto
each other’s normal planes, removing destructive interference
while preserving complementary components [49]. This en-
sures updates for one objective don’t degrade others, acceler-
ating convergence. PCGrad has been successfully applied to
multi-task learning and recently extended to RL domains [50].
3) Multi-Objective Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning
(MO-MARL): MO-MARL combines multi-agent coordination
with multi-objective optimization, where agents must balance
competing goals while cooperating [51], [52]. This introduces
compounded complexity: non-stationary environments, gradi-
ent conflicts, and coordinated trade-offs across objectives. Ap-
plications include robotics (speed, energy, safety), autonomous
driving (efficiency, comfort, safety), and resource management
(fairness, efficiency) [44].
Algorithmic frameworks include MOMAPPO with multi-
objective critics [53], PAC-MARL maintaining per-agent
Pareto sets [54], and MO-QMIX extending value factoriza-
tion to vector returns [55]. Recent integration of PCGrad
into MARL shows improved stability in cooperative multi-
objective tasks [50]. Despite advances, MO-MARL remains
unexplored in physically grounded domains like fluid-structure
interaction where objective coupling depends on continuous-
time PDE dynamics.
G. Reinforcement Learning in Computational Fluid Dynamics
Integrating RL with CFD enables learning control strategies
that respect physical constraints (incompressibility, momentum
conservation) while discovering non-intuitive solutions [56],
[57]. However, high-fidelity simulations are computationally
expensive, and RL training requires millions of steps [58].
Recent advances in differentiable physics engines and GPU-
accelerated CFD enable gradient backpropagation through
solvers and massively parallel simulation [59], [60].
Early RL-CFD applications focused on active flow control
for drag reduction and mixing enhancement. [61] pioneered
deep RL for cylinder flow control using rotating cylinders to
suppress vortex shedding, achieving drag reductions exceeding
30% [62]. Subsequent work applied PPO to airfoil separa-
tion control [63], multi-agent RL for wake stabilization [64],
and LES-based turbulent channel control [65]. These studies
established that RL can discover non-trivial periodic control
strategies exploiting flow physics [66].
[32] pioneered RL for optimizing undulatory locomotion
using immersed boundary methods. [31] introduced ”Smar-
ties,” applying PPO with DNS to learn fish-like swimming
with realistic C-start maneuvers and rheotactic behaviors. [67]
demonstrated navigation in turbulent flows, while [68] showed
schools could exploit wake interactions to reduce collective
drag. [9] discovered non-reciprocal strokes satisfying Purcell’s
scallop theorem at low Reynolds numbers.
1) CFD-MARL for Swarm Systems: Extending RL-CFD
coupling to multi-agent swarms remains nascent due to com-
putational costs: N agents require O(N 2) interaction evalu-
ations, and training demands millions of steps [58]. Early
work used simplified potential flow or Stokesian dynamics,
sacrificing accuracy for speed [69]. These models fail to
capture vortex shedding, wake interactions, and transient flow
reversals critical for realistic swarm behavior.
Recent advances in differentiable physics engines enable
gradient backpropagation through Navier-Stokes solvers with
full hydrodynamic fidelity [59], [60]. Initial studies explored
coordinated particle manipulation in microfluidic flows [70]
and multi-robot coordination in viscous fluids, but remain
constrained to quasi-steady or low-Reynolds regimes with sim-
plified dynamics. None have addressed multi-objective swarm
control balancing speed, energy conservation, and smoothness
simultaneously.
2) Gaps in CFD-MO-MARL: No prior work has inte-
grated multi-objective multi-agent RL with high-fidelity, time-
dependent CFD for swarm locomotion in oscillatory flows.
Existing CFD-RL studies address single-objective tasks with
single agents or homogeneous swarms sharing scalar rewards
[58]. Multi-objective formulations in CFD use offline op-
timization (evolutionary algorithms, Bayesian optimization)
rather than online RL [71]. Pulsatile flows in physiological sys-
tems and industrial applications remain unexplored for micro-
robotic swarms, despite being ubiquitous [72]. The challenge
involves multi-scale temporal dynamics where agents must
exploit flow phases, synchronize with neighbors, and maintain
formation over millisecond-to-second timescales while balanc-
ing competing objectives decentrally.
This work addresses this gap by coupling a Navier-Stokes
solver with decentralized multi-objective policies learned via
PPO and reconciled through PCGrad. We demonstrate that
time-dependent fluid-agent interactions can be captured di-
rectly within RL loops, enabling physically grounded swarm
control balancing upstream progression, energy conservation,
and motion smoothness. Each micro-robot optimizes three
concurrent objectives whose competing gradients are recon-
ciled using PCGrad to prevent destructive interference and
accelerate convergence toward Pareto-optimal policies. This

II. METHODS
A. Physical Setup
In this study, all computational fluid-dynamics simulations
were carried out using the open-source PhiFlow frame-
work [73]. PhiFlow’s built-in incompressible Navier–Stokes
solver combines a semi-Lagrangian advection scheme with a
projection-based pressure correction to enforce mass conser-
vation on a uniform Cartesian grid. The flow domain was
discretized at a fixed resolution, and time integration was
performed using an explicit, second-order scheme with a
constant time step chosen to ensure numerical stability. No-
slip boundary conditions were imposed on solid surfaces,
while zero-gradient boundaries were applied in the transverse
directions. Fluid properties, including dynamic viscosity and
density, were specified to match the physical system, and
velocity and pressure fields were recorded at regular intervals
for downstream analysis.

D. Numerical Discretization and Solver Pipeline
The domain is discretized on a uniform Cartesian grid, with
spatial steps ∆x, ∆y, both equal to 0.1 mm, chosen to resolve
boundary layers and vortex structures, and a temporal multi-
scaling: time step ∆t, equal to 5 × 10−3 s for updating the
forces acting on and by the swarm, divided by 20 substeps
(∆tsub equal to 2.5 × 10−4 s) for updating the forces acting
on and by the fluid, set by Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL)
stability constraints to give a Courant number of 1. Each
time step advances the velocity field through three stages:
Diffusion via an explicit viscosity update ∆u = ν∇2u∆t;
Semi-Lagrangian advection; Incompressibility by solving the
Poisson equation for pressure ∆p = −ρ∇· (u · ∇u).
This solver pipeline ensures second-order spatial accuracy
in smooth regions under low Reynolds conditions and stable
evolution under high Reynolds conditions.
E. Control Problem and Optimization Framework
To optimize micro-robotic locomotion in time-dependent flow,
we cast the task as a multi-agent multi-objective Markov
Decision Process (MA-MOMDP) and employ model-free deep
reinforcement learning (PPO) with a Centralized Training, De-
centralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm. The following sum-
marizes the scientific conceptualization and technical specifi-
cations of this framework.
1) MDP Specification and Transition Dynamics: The state
space S is defined at each discrete timestep by the concate-
nation of decentralized, per-agent observation vectors. Each
individual agent’s local feature space consists of 8 dimensions:
its Cartesian coordinates (x, y), current velocity components
(vx, vy), and four pressure samples sampled at equispaced
points around its circumference to capture local hydrodynamic
gradients. While execution is decentralized, training is central-
ized; the joint state s ∈S is provided to the critic to alleviate
the non-stationarity inherent in multi-agent environments. The
action space A allows each agent i to exert a continuous 2D
propulsive force Fi,internal = (ai,x, ai,y), which is normalized
within [−Fmax, Fmax] based on physical magnetic actuation
limits. Transition dynamics P are governed by the coupled
fluid-structure solver, where the Navier-Stokes propagator
updates flow fields and agent trajectories are determined by
linear accelerations integrating hydrodynamic, drag, internal,
and contact forces.
2) Multi-Objective Reward Framework: The reward func-
tion R is formulated as a per-agent reward matrix of shape
(N, 3), supporting a three-head critic architecture. Our frame-
work utilizes local environmental feedback for each swarm
member i:
• Progress Reward (rprogress,i): Measures the displacement
of each agent, normalized by a reference length. The
reward is biased positively towards up-flow displacement.
The reward is capped by a constant success reward and a
failure penalty, and penalized by a constant to eliminate
idle behavior (Eq. 8).
• Energy Efficiency (renergy,i): Rewarding mechanically
productive force application (Fi) by computing the ratio

F. Reproducibility and Data Management
All physical parameters (geometry, fluid properties, inflow
waveform), swarm configurations (agent spacing, initial lat-
tice), and RL hyperparameters are specified in version-
controlled configuration files [77]. During each run, pressure
and velocity fields, agent trajectories, and reward histories are
logged at every time step. Post-processing generates quantita-
tive metrics (e.g., mean upstream velocity, success rate) and
visualizations (scalar field maps, trajectory overlays, reward
curves) to support systematic comparison across parameter
studies.
III. RESULTS
A. Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning Convergence and
Policy Stabilization
Fig. 6 presents the per-episode learning curves for the three
reward objectives – progress, energy efficiency, and smooth-
ness – normalized by episode length, across approximately
110,000 global environment steps. All three objectives are
evaluated against two fixed brute-force baselines: a strategy
that applies maximal thrust directly upstream, and one that
drives the swarm members toward the channel walls while
moving upstream.
The progress reward, measuring the swarm’s ability to
navigate against the fluid flow, exhibits the clearest learning
dynamic. From an initial value near zero – comparable to
the brute-force baselines, which remain stagnant at approx-
imately -2.0 and -2.5 throughout training – the learned policy
improves rapidly between approximately 5,000 and 25,000
global steps, converging to a sustained mean of roughly 6.5-
7.0. This represents an improvement of more than 8 reward
units over either baseline, demonstrating that the learned pol-
icy acquires non-trivial flow exploitation strategies that fixed
control schemes cannot achieve. A notable period of elevated
inter-environment variance is observed between approximately
30,000 and 70,000 steps, reflected in the widened confidence
band, suggesting that the policy undergoes iterative refinement
and temporary destabilization as it explores higher-reward be-
havioral modes before restabilizing. By approximately 75,000
steps, the mean progress reward recovers and the variance
narrows, indicating policy consolidation.
The energy efficiency reward exhibits a smoother conver-
gence profile. Starting at approximately 0.30 in early training,
it rises monotonically to a plateau near 0.63-0.65 by 20,000
steps and remains broadly stable for the remainder of training,
with only moderate variance. Critically, both brute-force base-
lines yield negative energy efficiency (approximately -0.15 and
-0.27, respectively), indicating that fixed-force strategies ex-
pend thrust in directions misaligned with actual displacement
– a consequence of fighting the flow rather than exploiting it.


## Page 8


8
The learned policy, by contrast, consistently achieves positive
energy efficiency, confirming that it develops force application
patterns that are directionally coherent with member motion.
The smoothness reward displays the fastest convergence
among all three objectives. After an initial transient near 0.87
in the first few thousand steps, the learned policy rapidly
stabilizes in the range of 0.97-0.99 by approximately 5,000
global steps and sustains this level for the remainder of training
with very low variance. Notably, both brute-force baselines
achieve a smoothness of 1.00, the theoretical maximum, as
their fixed action directions produce perfectly consistent, zero-
variance action sequences by construction. The learned policy
falls marginally short of this ceiling, reflecting the inherent
cost of adaptive, state-dependent control: a policy that mod-
ulates its actions in response to local flow conditions will
necessarily incur some degree of temporal variation that a
static rule does not. The gap between the learned policy and
the brute-force baselines on this objective is nonetheless small
in absolute terms (below 0.03 across the entire stable training
regime), and must be interpreted in the context of the far larger
improvements the learned policy achieves on the progress and
energy efficiency objectives, where both baselines fail severely.
This trade-off confirms that the MOMAPPO agent learns to
balance the competing demands of navigational effectiveness
and action consistency, accepting a modest smoothness cost in
exchange for the substantially superior upstream progress and
energy performance that define the primary task.
Taken together, the convergence profiles across all three
objectives demonstrate that the MOMAPPO framework suc-
cessfully learns a Pareto-improving policy relative to both
brute-force baselines simultaneously – without sacrificing any
individual objective – and that the learned policy generalizes
stably across parallel environment instances, as evidenced by
the narrow confidence bands in the steady-state regime.
B. Emergent Behavior
The trained policy gives rise to a repertoire of collective
behaviors that were not explicitly encoded in the reward
function, but emerged organically from the interplay between
the multi-objective signal, the triphasic fluid dynamics, and the
decentralized control architecture. Comparing the untrained
baseline (Fig. 7) with the trained agent (Fig. 8) reveals a
qualitative shift in swarm strategy that can be decomposed into
three temporally distinct phases, each exploiting a different
feature of the pulsatile flow cycle.
1) Phase 1: Collective Flow Throttling During Peak For-
ward Flow: The most striking and counterintuitive emergent
behavior is the formation of a deliberate two-layer hydro-
dynamic obstruction during the peak forward-flow phase of
the triphasic waveform. Under the untrained policy, all swarm
members cluster against the upper wall (y ≈2 mm). Rather
than sheltering the members from the bulk flow, this config-
uration creates a severe constriction between the swarm and
the opposite wall that locally accelerates the fluid to velocities
exceeding 700 mm/s. The resulting drag force is sufficient
to drive the entire swarm past the episode-failure boundary at
x = 80 mm within a few time steps (Fig. 7, panels 1–4).

integrity of the two-layer formation and keeps the center of
mass well within the safe operating corridor.

through successive reverse-flow pulses – a goal that each
member can pursue independently. The progress reward is
sufficient to drive individual members to the success boundary
without requiring the inter-agent spatial coordination that was
essential when the swarm was concentrated in mid-channel
and vulnerable to being swept downstream as a unit.
C. Influence of Gradient Surgery on Learned Policy
To assess the contribution of PCGrad to training stability and
multi-objective performance, we compare the full MOMAPPO
policy against an ablated variant in which the standard
summed-gradient update replaces the PCGrad merging step,
leaving all other hyperparameters and architectural choices
identical. The results are presented in Figure 9.

in the energy efficiency and smoothness objectives, where
the effect of conflicting gradients manifests with particular
severity. Without PCGrad, the energy efficiency reward rises
transiently during the first few thousand steps, reaching values
comparable to the PCGrad variant, before collapsing abruptly
to near zero by approximately 10,000 steps, where it remains
flat for the remainder of the observed training window. The
PCGrad variant, by contrast, sustains a stable plateau near
0.60–0.65 throughout training. The smoothness reward ex-
hibits an even more dramatic collapse: after an early peak

critical component of the MOMAPPO framework as applied to
this domain. The energy efficiency and smoothness objectives,
which carry smaller gradient magnitudes than the progress

naively, reducing the multi-objective problem to a de facto
single-objective one with degraded and unstable optimization
dynamics. Gradient surgery preserves the learning signal for
all three objectives simultaneously, enabling the stable and
Pareto-improving convergence reported in the previous sec-

over the complete training horizon will be reported in the final
version of this work.
IV. DISCUSSION
This work demonstrates that coupling a high-fidelity Navier-
Stokes solver with a multi-objective multi-agent reinforce-
ment learning framework is both computationally tractable
and scientifically productive for micro-swarm locomotion in
physiologically realistic flow conditions. The results estab-
lish three principal contributions: a training paradigm that
achieves stable Pareto-improving convergence across compet-
ing objectives, a repertoire of non-obvious emergent collective
behaviors arising from the interplay of fluid physics and
decentralized reward signals, and a quantitative demonstration
that gradient conflict resolution is functionally necessary in
this domain.
The most consequential finding is not the convergence of the
reward curves per se, but what the converged policy actually
does. Rather than applying maximum thrust upstream – the
strategy both brute-force baselines approximate, and which
fails catastrophically – the trained swarm discovers a multi-
phase ratchet mechanism in which the forward-flow phase is
