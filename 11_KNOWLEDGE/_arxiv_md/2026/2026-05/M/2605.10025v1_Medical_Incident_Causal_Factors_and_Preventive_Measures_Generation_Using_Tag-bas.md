Abstract—In high-stakes domains such as healthcare, the reli-
ability of Large Language Models (LLMs) is critical, particularly
when generating clinical insights from incident reports. This
study proposes a tag-based few-shot example selection method
for prompting LLMs to generate background/causal factors and
preventive measures from details of the medical incidents. For
our experiments, we use the Japanese Medical Incident Dataset
(JMID), a structured dataset of 3,884 real-world medical accident
and near-miss reports. These reports are variably annotated with
a wide range of tags—some include descriptive information (e.g.,
“medications,” “blood transfusion therapy”). We compare
three few-shot example selection strategies—random sampling,
cosine similarity-based selection, and our proposed tag-based
method—using GPT-4o and LLaMA 3.3. Results show that the
tag-based approach achieves the highest precision and most stable
generation behavior, while similarity-based selection often leads
to unintended outputs and safety filter activation. These findings
suggest that selecting examples based on human-interpretable
dataset tags can improve generation precision and stability in
clinical LLM applications.
Index Terms—LLM, Patient Safety, Few-shot Learning
I. INTRODUCTION
A. Motivation and Purpose
Large Language Models (LLMs), which possess the capa-
bility to comprehensively understand and integrate a broad
spectrum of medical knowledge, are increasingly being rec-
ognized for their potential to enhance operational efficiency
and support clinical decision-making in healthcare settings,
due to their advanced natural language processing abilities.
According to the findings in [1], clinical summaries generated
by LLMs were frequently preferred over those written by clin-
icians themselves. This implies that LLMs may offer a viable
solution to support clinical documentation tasks, potentially
improving workflow efficiency and reducing the administrative
burden on healthcare providers. Moreover, a lack of clarity in
patient reports has been linked to a decline in the quality of
patient care [2], highlighting the clear importance of effective
communication between patients and healthcare providers.
Given their ability to simplify complex text and their extensive
medical knowledge, LLMs have been suggested as a potential
means to improve the quality of such communication [3].
However, LLMs often produce inaccuracies or ”hallucina-
tions,” which pose a significant barrier to their practical use
in clinical settings—environments where errors by healthcare
professionals can mean the difference between life and death
for patients [4]. In this study, we focus on medical safety
within the healthcare domain and propose a tag-based example
selection method for few-shot learning in order to improve pre-
cision in the task of generating the background/causal factors
and preventive measures from details of the medical incidents
descriptions. In clinical practice, when a medical incident
occurs, it is critically important to promptly and accurately
identify its causes and implement preventive measures to avoid
recurrence. To support this, Japan’s Ministry of Health, Labor
and Welfare has been implementing the ”Project to Collect
Medical Adverse Event Information”1 since 2004. Through
this initiative, harmful events such as medical accidents are
continuously collected, and reported cases—including near-
miss incidents—are analyzed and made publicly available to
medical institutions and the general public to provide useful
information for improving patient safety. Despite the fact that
patient safety and medical incidents are critical issues within
the medical domain, unfortunately, there has been limited
research conducted on this subject. Building on this context,

B. Our approach
To achieve this aim, we first prepared a dataset called
Japanese Medical Incident Dataset (JMID), based on reports
provided by the Japan Council for Quality Health Care
(JQ)2 for the evaluation. This dataset includes ”Details of
the Incident”, ”Background/Causal Factors”, and ”Preventive
Measures” of each medical incident as shown in Table II. In
addition, ”descriptive information” tag as shown in Table I is
assigned to certain incidents.
We then propose a novel example selection method called
”tag-based example selection” in few-shot learning setting
using LLMs. Our approach selects few-shot learning examples
within the same category based on the ”descriptive informa-
tion” tag that can be available on the dataset (Table I).
In contrast to the random selection approach, our method
is expected to prevent the generation of unrelated content. In
addition, in contrast to the selection method based on cosine
similarity between the input and incident descriptions, the
examples selected by our method are expected to be more
diverse; therefore, the outputs generated using our approach
are expected to be more flexible.
C. Contribution
The main contributions of this study are as follows:
• Proposal of a tag-based few-shot example selection
method: We introduce a method that selects relevant
examples based on descriptive tags (e.g., medication,
blood transfusion, patient misidentification) from medical
incident reports, achieving higher precision than random
or similarity-based approaches.
• Challenge of patient safety and medical incidents: there
has been limited prior work explicitly focusing on au-
tomatically generating ”Background/Causal Factors” and
”Preventive Measures” from medical incident reports.
This should be a significant challenge in the medical
domain.
II. RELATED WORK
The output performance of LLMs is widely known to vary
significantly depending on prompt design, and the technique of
prompt engineering has recently emerged as an active area of
research [5]. Among the various prompting techniques, few-
shot learning has gained attention as a method that enables
2https://jcqhc.or.jp/,https://jcqhc.or.jp/en/
high-quality output using only a small number of examples,
without the need for additional training or fine-tuning, and
without requiring thousands or tens of thousands of training
examples. In few-shot learning, the model is presented with
several input-output pairs in the prompt to help it understand
the task context. In this setting, the selection of which exam-
ples to include is of critical importance, as it has been shown
to significantly affect model performance [6]. While early ap-
proaches often relied on random selection of examples, it has
been reported that such a strategy can still yield competitive
results in certain cases [7]. More recent work has explored
example selection based on similarity metrics, such as cosine
similarity, to identify semantically relevant examples [8]. In
contrast to surface-level similarity, Zhang et al. [9] proposed a
skill-based selection method, which prioritizes examples based
on the underlying reasoning skills required by the task. This
approach has demonstrated superior performance compared to
traditional similarity-based methods. Furthermore, recognizing
the limitations of individual strategies, Pecher et al. intro-
duced ACSESS [10], which automatically combines multiple
selection strategies and dynamically selects the most effective
one per task, yielding consistently higher performance. In the
medical field, numerous tasks have been challenged [11] [12]
[13]. In these researches, LLMs are utilized in order to solve
the medical Question & Answering tasks and knowledge and
reasoning tasks [14] [15] [16]. In Japanese, similar efforts
have been made, including IgakuQA [17], a QA task based
on past questions from Japan’s national medical licensing
examinations from 2018 to 2022. In addition, several medical
tasks including information extraction task and QA task related
to medicine and disease have been challenged using Large
Language Models in JMED-LLM project3.
However, to the best of our knowledge, there is still limited
research specifically focused on the generation task of medical
incidents and patient safety. In this study, we focus on the
medical domain—specifically the field of patient safety, where
mistakes can have critical consequences—to examine how
differences in few-shot example selection impact the precision
of model outputs. We propose a novel selection method that
uses the JMID dataset and leverages the ”description tags”
annotated within each case as the basis for selecting examples.
We argue that such an approach is particularly meaningful in
the medical domain, where the availability of training data is
limited due to privacy constraints. Moreover, since our method
is based on pre-existing categorical tags, it offers practical
advantages: it incurs minimal computational cost and can be
easily integrated with existing databases.

employing both metrics, we are able to capture lexical overlap
and semantic similarity, thereby enabling a more balanced and
comprehensive evaluation.

B. Discussion
This section discusses the precision-related findings.
1) Task Misinterpretation with LLaMA 3.3: With simi-
larity setting, LLaMA 3.3 produced unexpected outputs in
207 cases (approximately 10%). These unexpected outputs
exhibited two distinct patterns. First, some outputs regenerated
background/causal factors and preventive measures for all
five example cases already provided in the prompt, treating
them as new inputs rather than reference examples. Second,
other outputs summarized the five example cases into a single
aggregated incident and generated only preventive measures
for this artificial combined case, completely ignoring the actual
input case requiring analysis (Table VI).
2) Violence Filter Activation with GPT-4o: With GPT-4o,
19 cases were blocked by the violence filter despite being
standard medical reports. We hypothesize that extreme sim-
ilarity amplified certain semantic features (e.g., patient harm
terms), sharpening meanings and triggering the violence filter
inappropriately.
3) Stability and Advantages of Tag-Based Example Se-
lection: In contrast, in both the random and tag-based
settings, GPT-4o and LLaMA 3.3 generated outputs for all
cases. Unlike the similarity setting, GPT-4o showed stable
generation without content-filter blocking, while maintaining
high BERTScore and ROUGE-L precision in the tag-based
setting. This supports its suitability for high-stakes domains
such as healthcare, where high precision is critical. Moreover,
prior work [9] suggests that selecting examples containing
task-relevant skills—rather than purely high cosine similarity
—can improve performance. Consistent with this, our tag-
based, which uses shared dataset tags, increased BERTScore
and ROUGE-L precision. This implies that tags function as
human-interpretable proxies for skill-based subset selection,
making them both effective and practical criteria for real-world
deployment.

VI. FUTURE WORK
Although the tag-based setting outperformed the random
and similarity settings in terms of precision, two key directions
warrant further investigation.
First, dynamic example selection within tag-based con-
straints should be explored. We used fixed example sets for
reproducibility, but dynamically selecting examples for each
input might yield even higher BERTScore and ROUGE-L
precision, given content variation within categories.
Second, clinical validation with healthcare professionals
is essential. As shown in Table VII, the model sometimes
generates additional terms that are absent from the Reference
Cause, which lowers the BERTScore and ROUGE-L precision
score under automatic evaluation. However, such additions are
not necessarily errors, since medical incident analysis often
admits multiple valid interpretations. Therefore, evaluation
by healthcare professionals would offer clinically grounded
insights and is crucial before real-world deployment.
VII. CONCLUSION
In order to develop the method of automatically generat-
ing the background/causal factors and preventive measures
of each medical incident, in this paper, we proposed a
novel example selection method called ”tag-based exam-
ple selection”. Experimental evaluation using a real textual
dataset demonstrates that our approach outperforms the other
baselines in BERTScore and ROUGE-L precision score in
a few-shot learning setting with GPT-4o and LLaMA 3.3.
While BERTScore and ROUGE-L precision were the primary
focus, experimental results also revealed that this approach
contributed to more stable generation behavior, highlighting
an additional benefit of the method. Future work should fur-
ther explore hybrid selection strategies that balance semantic
relevance with task-specific diversity, aiming to enhance the
robustness and trustworthiness of LLMs in medical environ-
ments.
