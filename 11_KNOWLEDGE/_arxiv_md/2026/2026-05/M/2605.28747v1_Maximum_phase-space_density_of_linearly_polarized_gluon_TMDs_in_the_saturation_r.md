I.
INTRODUCTION
At high energy, the gluon density of a large hadron or
nucleus grows rapidly toward small Bjorken x. Nonlin-
ear recombination effects eventually become important
and generate the saturation scale Qs(Y ), where Y de-
notes the rapidity interval. The resulting weak-coupling
but high-occupancy state is described by the Color Glass
Condensate (CGC) effective theory [1–7]. In this picture
the fast partons act as stochastic color sources, while
the small-x gluons are classical fields whose correlations
evolve with rapidity according to JIMWLK or, in the
large-Nc mean-field limit, BK evolution. A standard es-
timate states that gluon occupation numbers in the sat-
uration region are of order 1/αs, which justifies the use
of classical color fields. A more refined and less trivial
question is whether occupancies at transverse momenta
below Qs can become parametrically larger than 1/αs.
The Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) is especially well
suited to address this question. In electron–nucleus scat-
tering the virtual photon provides a clean and tunable
hard probe, while the nuclear target enhances Qs and
extends the lever arm in x. Measurements of back-to-
back dijets, diffractive dijets, heavy flavor, and more dif-
ferential multi-particle correlations can directly test the
interplay between saturation, transverse momentum de-
pendent factorization, and Sudakov resummation [8–12].
Recent EIC-oriented studies of electron–dijet and lepton–
jet angular correlations, heavy-quark and quarkonium
production, rescattering, and precision decorrelation ob-
servables have sharpened this program by showing how
saturation, Sudakov radiation, and harmonic angular co-
efficients compete in realistic kinematics [13–18]. For lin-
early polarized gluons, the EIC is not only a discovery
machine for a new TMD; it also provides a way to deter-

The distribution h⊥g

perimental issue is that a large intrinsic polarization at
the saturation scale is not by itself enough: the measured
asymmetry also depends on hard factors, phase-space
cuts, and TMD evolution between Qs and the measured
hard scale.
At small x, linearly polarized gluon TMDs come in
two standard gauge-link structures, Weizsäcker-Williams
(WW) and dipole (DP). The WW distribution has the
genuine gluon number-density interpretation in light-
cone gauge and is the natural object in DIS dijet pro-

The motivation for repeating Mueller’s calculation

small-x color field, not a scalar density multiplied by an
after-the-fact analyzing ratio. The relevant question is

and whether the Sudakov factor associated with vetoing
additional radiation cuts it off in the same way. As we
show below, the answer is different for the dipole and
WW gauge links.
The use of Sudakov evolution in a saturated target also
requires some care.
In the strict dense regime generic
TMD factorization can be spoiled by process-dependent
gauge links and by multiple color exchanges, and the re-
sulting color entanglement is a genuine obstruction rather
than a matter of notation [33]. In the correlation limit,
however, where a hard scale P⊥or Q is much larger
than the momentum imbalance k⊥, the CGC calcula-
tion can be expanded into small-x gluon TMDs multi-
plied by a process-dependent hard factor and a Sudakov
form factor [34–36].
This is the kinematic region as-
sumed throughout the numerical part of this work. The
point is not that saturation invalidates Collins-Soper evo-
lution, but that the matching must be performed in a
regime where the hard recoil, the small imbalance, and
the gauge-link structure are all kept explicit. The ques-
tion addressed here is therefore not whether the Collins-
Soper kernel changes for a polarized gluon; it does not.
Rather, the issue is whether the J2 Bessel weight in
the tensor TMD definition and the gauge-link-dependent
CGC input allow the tensor coefficient to realize the same
Sudakov-limited saddle as the scalar occupancy.
The calculation below is the polarized analogue of the
occupancy analysis in Ref. [19], supplemented by the
CGC expressions for the two small-x gluon TMDs. One
cannot obtain it by attaching a polarization fraction to
the final scalar result.
The tensor coefficient has to
be inserted in the same phase-space normalization be-
fore the maximization is performed, and one must then
check whether the resulting expression still has the form
L exp(−aL2). This is analogous to other applications of
small-x factorization, where the dense-target dynamics
becomes predictive only after the process-dependent ten-
sor and gauge-link structures have been specified.
