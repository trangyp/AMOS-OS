Abstract
Understanding the molecular mechanisms by which denaturants modulate protein
structure remains a central challenge in protein biophysics. In this work, molecular
dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to investigate the effects of urea on the
structural stability of bovine serum albumin (BSA) in its F isoform at pH 3.7, over
a broad range of urea concentrations (0 M to a fully urea-solvated system).

mechanism within the protein hydration shell. At low urea concentrations, a marked
reduction ( 40%) in protein–water hydrogen bonds is observed, accompanied by a corre-
sponding increase in protein–urea interactions, consistent with a competitive solvation
process. At higher concentrations, urea–urea self-association becomes significant, lim-

surface. Despite these solvent rearrangements, the secondary structure of BSA remains
largely preserved, whereas local and tertiary structural features, particularly in Domain
III, exhibit increased solvent exposure and conformational flexibility. These findings
support a dynamic compensation mechanism in which urea partially replaces water
in the solvation shell without fully disrupting the hydrogen-bonding network. Over-
all, this study provides molecular-level insight into the interplay between preferential
interactions, solvation dynamics, and protein stability under denaturing conditions.
Introduction
Over the past few decades, the role of urea and other denaturing agents in aqueous solutions
containing proteins or micelles has sparked intense debate, leading to considerable progress in
our understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Although various mechanisms have been
proposed to explain denaturation, and despite the extensive body of literature on the topic,
direct and indirect mechanisms have traditionally been the most frequently cited—some
proposals even combine both perspectives.1–15
More recently, a growing number of studies have questioned whether a common de-
naturation mechanism can be generalized across different systems.16–21 Key questions have
emerged: is the denaturation mechanism analogous when using guanidine chloride versus
urea?,22–24 and do factors such as pH and thermodynamic parameters25—including pressure
and temperature—significantly influence the process?
These questions, together with more recent studies,21,26 suggest that the possibility of

generalizing a denaturation mechanism for urea and other denaturing agents remains unclear
and requires further investigation.
In this work, we use molecular dynamics simulations to examine how varying urea con-
centrations affect the structure of bovine serum albumin (BSA). Given that BSA undergoes
reversible conformational changes in response to pH variations,27 we selected the N-isoform
state of BSA at pH 7 as our initial reference for simulations. This state represents a folded,
functional conformation. Our results show strong agreement with experimental findings and
with previous simulation results reported in the literature, thereby validating the robustness
of our approach.
Materials and Methods
Systems and Simulations
Urea-induced effects on the structural stability of bovine serum albumin (BSA) were ex-
amined in aqueous environments over a range of urea concentrations. Urea molecules were
represented using the Boek model,28 while all nonbonded interactions and covalent parame-
ters were adopted from the GROMOS 54A7 force field.29
Water molecules were described using the Simple Point Charge Extended (SPC/E)
model.30 The starting atomic configuration of bovine serum albumin (BSA) was taken from
its crystallographic structure available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 4F5S).31 The
protein topology corresponding to physiological conditions at pH 3.7 was adopted from prior
studies and validated by Small-Angle X-ray Scattering (SAXS) measurements in combination
with molecular dynamics simulations.27 Under these conditions, BSA carries a net charge of
-99, which was compensated by the addition of sodium (Cl−) counterions to ensure overall
charge neutrality.
Intermolecular interactions were explicitly accounted for using the GROMACS 2023.4
molecular dynamics simulation package.32 As a reference, a control system devoid of urea

The control system was equilibrated using a two-step protocol. First, a 500 ps NVT
simulation was conducted with harmonic positional restraints applied to the protein Cα
atoms (force constant: 1000 kJ mol−1 nm−2 in each Cartesian direction). Subsequently, the
restraints were removed, and the system was equilibrated for an additional 500 ps in the
NpT ensemble to achieve density and pressure equilibration.
Following equilibration of the control system (Control W), the resulting structure was
used as the starting configuration for all urea-containing simulations. Urea molecules were
randomly introduced into this configuration (Figure ??), generating seven systems with
concentrations spanning 1–7 M, along with a urea-only system (Control U) (Table I). All
configurations listed in Table I were equilibrated using the same two-step protocol applied
to the control, ensuring methodological consistency. Production simulations of 500 ns were
subsequently performed. For enhanced sampling, ten configurations were randomly selected
from the final 10 ns of each trajectory and used as starting points for independent 10 ns
simulations, each initialized with different velocity sets. This strategy enabled a more com-
prehensive characterization of long-timescale dynamics and intermolecular interactions in
the protein–urea–water systems.

All equilibration and production simulations for both the control system and BSA in
water–urea mixtures were performed in a cubic simulation box under periodic boundary
conditions at 300.15 K and 1 bar.
Temperature and pressure were controlled using the
velocity-rescaling thermostat33 and the Berendsen barostat,34 with coupling constants of
0.1 ps and 1.0 ps, respectively. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the
particle mesh Ewald (PME) method,35 with a cutoff distance of 1.0 nm applied to both van
der Waals and Coulomb interactions. Covalent bonds within the solute were constrained
using the LINCS algorithm.36 A simulation time step of 2 fs was used in all cases.
Hydrogen Bonds.
Hydrogen-bond counts per molecule were determined using the
GROMACS tool gmx hbond. Hydrogen bonds were identified based on geometric criteria,
with a donor–acceptor distance ≤0.35 nm and an acceptor–donor–hydrogen (A–D–H) angle
≤300, consistent with standard definitions implemented in GROMACS.
Results and discussion
All analyses are based on the average of ten independent simulations, each 10 ns in dura-
tion, performed for every system investigated (the control system without urea, urea–water
mixtures ranging from 1 to 5 M and 7 M, and the system composed exclusively of urea in
the absence of water). Before data collection commenced, all systems underwent 500 ns of
hydration/solvation to ensure adequate equilibration.

Radial distribution function (RDF)
To further support our initial results and broaden the discussion, we analyzed the radial
distribution functions (RDFs) across several groups within the system. In Figure 5, we show
the RDFs calculated between the protein’s center of mass (CM) and the water molecules’
CM, and separately between the protein’s CM and the urea molecules’ CM.
In Figure 5, the radial distribution function (RDF) between the center of mass (CM)
of the protein and that of the water molecules shows that, in the presence of urea, water
molecules appear to be favored to approach the protein CM, even at distances shorter than
0.20 nm. This suggests that local hydration near the solvent-accessible regions of the protein
core is maintained across all urea concentrations and may even be enhanced at high urea
concentrations. A secondary peak is observed around 0.28–0.30 nm, corresponding to the
outer hydration layer. This feature seems to exhibit a monotonically decreasing behavior
with increasing number of urea molecules, being lower in all cases than that observed for the
Control W system. This change in behavior at the molecular level is not easily correlated
directly with the total average number of hydrogen bonds between the protein and water
molecules, but can be understood by normalizing the number of hydrogen bonds in terms of
the number of water molecules in each system.

In this case, a main peak is also observed at xx–yy nm,
which decreases almost monotonically with increasing number of urea molecules. At 1 M, a
prominent peak is observed compared to the others, followed by the Control U system, which
exhibits the second-highest peak intensity. We attribute this to the fact that the complete
absence of water forces chloride ions to interact with urea molecules, thereby increasing this
value and breaking the monotonic concentration-dependent behavior. In Figure 7d-e-f, we
complete the role of chloride ions by examining their interaction with the protein through
the RDF between the protein’s center of mass and the ions. The presence of urea appears to
favor the interaction of the protein with chloride ions in the case of the Control U system,
which exhibits several peaks of higher intensity compared to the other concentrations. For
the remaining urea concentrations, including the Control W system, no monotonic behavior
is observed; the behavior of the systems is very similar, with a slightly higher intensity for the
main peak at 4 M. However, we do not believe this indicates anything remarkable regarding
the behavior of the other systems.
These results do not suggest that electrostatic interactions, whether urea–chloride ions
or water–chloride ions, play a central role in the stability of the protein structure in the
presence of urea, as observed by Ferrara and co-authors.37
The radial distribution function between the carbon atoms of urea was analyzed to gain
a better understanding of the interaction of urea with the protein (Fig. 8). At low urea
concentrations (2–4 M), an increase in the intensity of the first and second coordination

behavior at concentrations above 2 M can be attributed to specific geometric arrangements
adopted by urea molecules during aggregation, consistent with the model proposed by Susan
and co-workers.38

these conditions, the protein is already partially unfolded, potentially limiting the extent of
additional conformational changes induced by urea.

CONCLUSION
This study employed Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to examine the effects of urea on
the structural properties of bovine serum albumin (BSA) at pH 3.7. Under these conditions,
BSA adopts a partially unfolded conformation characterized by increased solvent exposure
of its domains, commonly referred to as the F isoform. The simulated systems were con-
structed with urea concentrations ranging from 0 M (water control, Control W) to a system
containing urea as the sole solvent component (urea control, Control U). Within this range
of urea concentrations, our results indicate that urea does not significantly alter the overall
secondary structure of BSA. In particular, the number of residues participating in secondary
structural elements remains largely preserved, with α-helical content showing minimal vari-
ation across all systems.

A more detailed analysis of local conformational features, including S-bends, hydrogen-
bonded turns, and helical conformations, reveals subtle effects of urea on protein structure.
Specifically, the presence of urea increases in locally disordered configurations, suggesting
enhanced conformational flexibility. However, these local perturbations do not translate into
significant changes in the protein’s secondary structure. This behavior is consistent with
the notion that urea weakens intramolecular interactions while stabilizing solvent-exposed
regions, without substantially altering the secondary structural framework. The observed
structural resistance of BSA under denaturing conditions is consistent with prior experi-
mental findings at different pH values, which indicate that the F isoform retains secondary
structure despite partial unfolding. These results underscore the intrinsic stability of the F
isoform and suggest that, within the studied concentration range, urea primarily modulates
local structural dynamics rather than inducing significant loss of secondary structure.
Analyses based on the radius of gyration (Rg), root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), and
solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) support the proposed interpretation. Specifically, no
significant global structural changes are observed in the protein, and the presence of urea
appears to stabilize the protein in its F isoform. Notably, Domain III is the most affected
region, exhibiting increased solvent exposure in the presence of urea.
A key aspect emerging from the analysis is the progressive dehydration of the protein
surface as urea concentration rises, driven by the replacement of protein–water hydrogen
bonds with protein–urea interactions. This exchange mechanism, which maintains or slightly
increases the total number of hydrogen bonds involving the protein, suggests the presence of a
dynamic solvation equilibrium. A comparison between the two control systems indicates that,
although urea can partially compensate for the loss of protein–water interactions, it does not
fully restore the original hydrogen-bonding network in this case. This may account for the
observed alterations in the local structural stability and integrity of the protein. A domain-
23


## Page 24


level analysis further corroborates the previously described behavior, confirming the presence
of a compensation mechanism whereby the total number of hydrogen bonds among the
protein, water, and urea remains nearly constant. At the level of individual domains, Domain
III exhibits particularly stable hydrogen-bonding patterns at low urea concentrations. This
behavior can be rationalized within the framework of preferential interaction theory: in the
F isoform, Domain III presents greater exposure of hydrophobic regions, which promotes
preferential hydration at low urea concentrations, thereby stabilizing its local hydrogen-
bonding network. As urea concentration increases, this preferential hydration is progressively
reduced due to the accumulation of urea in the solvation shell, leading to a redistribution
of intermolecular interactions without a substantial disruption of the overall hydrogen-bond
network.
The presence of urea primarily affects hydrogen-bond (HB) formation between the protein
backbone and water, leading to a marked reduction of approximately 40% in their number
across all concentrations. Concurrently, the formation of HBs between urea and the protein
backbone suggests a preferential interaction with urea, indicating that the attenuation of
backbone–water hydrogen bonds is consistent with a competitive solvation mechanism. In
this framework, urea molecules progressively replace water at critical hydrogen-bonding sites,
a process that may facilitate urea-induced conformational destabilization.
Analysis of hydrogen-bond formation between protein side chains and either water or
urea further supports the presence of a compensatory mechanism, whereby urea partially
substitutes for water at accessible polar and charged residues, thereby preserving the local
interaction network despite changes in the solvent environment.
Analysis of the radial distribution functions (RDFs) for the different groups present
in the system indicates the presence of local hydration near solvent-accessible regions of
the protein core.
This hydration is maintained across all urea concentrations and may
even be enhanced at higher concentrations. Consistently, the analysis of the normalized
average number of hydrogen bonds between the protein and water confirms the presence of

a rehydration phenomenon at the protein surface. Increasing urea concentration appears to
favor interactions between water molecules and chloride ions, whereas interactions of these
ions with urea or with the protein remain comparatively limited, except under water-depleted
conditions. The propensity for urea–urea interactions increases with urea concentration, with
self-association becoming dominant at higher concentrations.
