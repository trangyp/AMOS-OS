MOMENTS FOR GENERALIZATIONS OF A COIN FLIP GAME
JIA HUANG
Abstract. We derive a recursive formula for the moments of the number of flips using a possibly biased
coin to produce a prescribed finite binary string S when S is either a run of heads or a run of heads followed
by a tails. Our recursive formula involve certain sums, which we simplify by using a one-parameter extension
of the well-studied Eulerian number, which belongs to the two-parameter family of numbers introduced by
Graham, Knuth, and Patashnik.
We also use the Goulden–Jackson cluster method and Fa`a di Bruno’s
formula to establish a closed formula for the moments in a more general situation where a die having an
arbitrary number of faces with possibly different probabilities is rolled repeatedly until a prescribed finite
word occurs.
1. Introduction
An X-post by Litt [15] in 2024 asked whether HH (two heads in a row) or HT (a heads followed by a tails)
will occur more often when one flips a coin 100 times. It attracted a lot of attentions, including some follow-
up work from various perspectives by Basdevant–H´enard–Maurel-S´egala–Singh [1], Cheplyaka [4], Ekhad–
Zeilberger [6], Grimmett [11], Janson–Nica–Segert [14], and Segert [21], all in the same year. A somewhat
similar game concerning how many flips it will take to see the first occurrence of HH (or HT instead) was
mentioned back in 2022 by Simonson [23, Ch. 2]. In recent work [13] we derived some summation identities
involving higher order Fibonacci numbers and certain variations of them from a more general coin flip game
in which one keeps flipping a coin until a prescribed string S of heads and tails occurs.
Our work [13] on the coin flip game partially overlaps with work of Benjamin, Neer, Otero, and Sellers [3]
on some weighted Fibonacci sums obtained from higher moments of the expected coin flips to produce
S = HH. Motivated by this connection, we now study a common generalization, i.e., the expected value
E(Y n) of the nth power of the random variable Y for the number of coin flips to obtain a prescribed string
S of finitely many heads and tails. We also allow the coin to be biased, so the probability for heads and tails
may be unequal. Note that even using a fair coin, the starting side is slightly favored (with a probability of
about .51) according to Diaconis, Holmes, and Montgomery [5].
We determine the moment E(Y n) recursively when S = Hk (a run of k heads) or S = HkT (a run of k
heads followed by a tails). For small values of k our result for E(Y n) coincides with some integer sequences
on OEIS [24]. When k is large we can simplify certain finite sums involved in our recursive formula by using
an extension (depending on k) of the well-known Eulerian number, which is interesting by itself as it satisfies
a closed formula and some other identities that reduce to the alternating sum formula, the Carlitz identity,
and the Worpitzky identity for the classical Eulerian number. This can also be viewed as special case of a
sequence defined by a two-parameter recurrence, first introduced by Graham, Knuth, and Patashnik [10] and
later studied by others from various perspectives, such as Mansour–Shattuck [17], Neuwirth [19], Spivey [25],
and Wilf [26].
Nonetheless, it seems tedious to derive a closed formula for the moment E(Y n) from our recursive formula
when n gets big. It is also natural ask what happens when S is an arbitrary finite string of heads and tails.
Furthermore, instead of a coin with only two sides, one can use a die with m faces marked 1, 2, . . . , m and
allow the probabilities of the faces to be distinct.
It turns out that a very different approach becomes
effective in this much more general situation: the Goulden–Jackson cluster method [8, 9] (see also Guibas–
Odlyzko [12] and Noonan–Zeilberger [20]) gives the generating function for words avoiding S as a factor (i.e.,
consecutive subword), and we connect these words (non-bijectively) to words with S occurring exactly once

This paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we define a one-parameter extension of the Eulerian
number and establish some summation identities related to it. In section 3 we study the moment E(Y n)
when a (possibly biased) coin is tossed repeatedly until S occurs and use the results from Section 2 to help
us determine E(Y n) recursively when S = Hk or S = HkT. In Section 4 we use the Goulden–Jackson cluster
method and Fa`a di Bruno’s formula to obtain a closed formula for the moments E(Y n) in the more general
situation when a die with m faces is rolled repeatedly until a prescribed finite word S on the alphabet [m]
occurs. Finally, in Section 5 we give some questions for future study.

One can generalize the die rolling game studied in this paper by allowing it to terminate upon the first
appearance of any element of a finite set S of prescribed words. Let Y = Y (S) be the random variable for
the number of turns to end this more general game. It might be possible to determine E(Y n) by extending
our proof of Theorem 4.1 since the generating function for finite words in [m] avoiding every element of S as
a factor can be derived from the Goulden–Jackson Cluster Method [8, 9], although the result may become
complicated when different words in S overlap with each other [20, examples on p. 9].
The game studied in this paper is similar to another game played by two people as mentioned in Section 1:
Two players respectively pick two words of the same lengths on the alphabet [m] and take turns to roll a
die with m faces marked 1, 2, . . . , m for n times, the player seeing their word appearing more frequently
being the winner. Ekhad–Zeilberger [6], Grimmett [11], and Segert [21] provided asymptotic formulae (as
n →∞) for the probabilities of HH outnumbering HT or the other way around in the unbiased coin flip
case. Janson, Nica, and Segert [14] generalized the formulae to the unbiased die rolling situation with two
arbitrary words of the same length picked by the two players, and the result depends only on the number of
overlaps of each of the two words. In particular, the winner’s word must have a smaller number of overlaps.
This can be naturally explained by our work in this paper since Theorem 4.1 implies that the average number
of occurrences of a word S after rolling the die n times is n/E(S) = n
 P
R∈ov(S) P(R)−1, which becomes
larger when ov(S) gets smaller. It would be nice to use the results in this paper to recover the asymptotic
formulae mentioned above and derive more consequences in that direction.
