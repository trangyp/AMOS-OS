Introduction
The classical theory of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) is built over the field R
(or C): the domain is an interval [a, b] ⊆R, and the unknown takes values in a Banach
space. While this framework encompasses the vast majority of applications, it does not
account for the algebraic structure of the domain. Replacing R by a finite-dimensional
k-algebra Λ raises the question of how the representation theory of Λ governs the structure
of differential equations and their solutions.

Categorical or algebraic approaches to calculus have developed along several lines, in-
cluding integration [6, 7, 14, 24], differentiations [1, 12, 15–17], equations [5, 11, 13]. Note
that the Rota-Baxter algebra [3,21,22] provides another algebraic description of integra-
tion, but it is different from the categorification of integration given by the categories
provided in [14, 19]. Several important works deserve to be mentioned in this context.
For example, Ehrhard and Regnier [8] introduced differential operations in linear logic;
Blute, Cockett and Seely [4] developed the theory of differential categories; Cockett, Crut-
twell and Lemay [5] defined differential equations in tangent categories via curve objects;
and Lemay [16] studied exponential functions in Cartesian differential categories. These
frameworks operate at a high level of abstraction and have not been instantiated on finite-
dimensional algebras. We note that the algebra Λ in our setting is finite-dimensional, and
its module category mod(Λ) possesses rich representation-theoretic structure, which dis-
tinguishes the present work from D-module theory [11,13] where the differential operator
ring is infinite-dimensional. On the other hand, Leinster [14] obtained a categorical deriva-
tion of the Lebesgue integral as the unique morphism from the initial object of a category
A p. Building on Leinster’s work, the authors and collaborators extended the framework
to finite-dimensional algebras. In [19], the normed module category N orp and the integral
Banach module category A p of a finite-dimensional k-algebra Λ were defined, the Lebesgue
integral was realized as the unique morphism from the initial object, and the differential
operator D was identified as a morphism in A 1 between two distinct objects, revealing
a categorical asymmetry between integration and differentiation. Subsequently, [10] es-
tablished transformation formulas between integrals on different algebras, and [18] defined
integrals with variable upper limits via integral partial ordered sets and proved a categorical
fundamental theorem of calculus.
