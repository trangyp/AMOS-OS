Abstract
While Local Differential Privacy (LDP) serves as a foundational primitive for
distributed data collection, its stringent noise injection requirement often leads to
severe degradation in data utility. This degradation stems from the task-agnostic
nature of conventional LDP mechanisms, which inject noise uniformly across all
dimensions regardless of their relative importance to the downstream objective. To
address this issue, we propose a novel approach that mitigates noise in task-relevant
subspaces of the data representation. Our method identifies task-critical subspaces
via the Jacobian matrix of the public downstream model, selectively attenuates
noise along those dimensions, and reshapes the isotropic noise of standard LDP
into an anisotropic distribution. This method preserves the uniform per-dimension
privacy budget while heterogeneously modulating noise impact across dimensions,
thereby substantially enhancing data utility. Furthermore, our approach general-
izes to both linear and non-linear models and integrates seamlessly with existing
mechanisms. Extensive experiments on CIFAR-10-C (Brightness corruption at the
highest severity level 5) demonstrate that integrating our approach improves the
utility of PrivUnit2 and PrivUnitG by approximately 20% at ϵ = 7.5. The source
code is available at https://github.com/ymha/jacobian-anr-ldp.

Introduction
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) [Kasiviswanathan et al., 2011] has become a foundational primitive
for privacy-preserving distributed data collection. Unlike centralized approaches [Dwork et al., 2006],
LDP eliminates the need for a “trusted data curator” by enabling each data owner to locally randomize
their data prior to sharing. Owing to this decentralized trust model, it has seen widespread adoption
in practice. Prominent examples include Google’s RAPPOR for collecting statistics from Chrome
clients [Erlingsson et al., 2014], Apple’s deployment in iOS for collecting new word suggestions,
emoji usage frequencies, and health data statistics [Apple Differential Privacy Team, 2017], and
Microsoft’s integration into Windows telemetry for application usage tracking [Ding et al., 2017].
A well-known drawback of LDP is the severe degradation of data utility due to its heavy randomiza-
tion [Duchi et al., 2013, Kairouz et al., 2016]. To provide rigorous privacy guarantees, standard LDP
mechanisms enforce a pessimistic randomization process. Specifically, since the privacy guarantee
must hold for any arbitrary points over the entire private data space, the magnitude of randomness is
governed by the worst-case sensitivity. Although this prevents inferring any individual’s true input, it
significantly lowers the signal-to-noise ratio and distorts the original characteristics of the data. As a

To mitigate this privacy-utility trade-off, a substantial body of literature has explored numerous

Hay et al., 2010, Wang et al., 2017, Jia and Gong, 2019, Cormode et al., 2019, Wang et al., 2020,
Sajadmanesh and Gatica-Perez, 2021, Fang et al., 2023] as well as the incorporation of public data,
models, and knowledge [Kurakin et al., 2022, Nasr et al., 2023, Hou et al., 2024] to further enhance
downstream utility while preserving privacy.
However, enhancing utility through the analysis of downstream models remains underexplored.
Although Cheng et al. [Cheng et al., 2022] investigated this direction, their methodology is predomi-
nantly centered around linear models and requires the direct use of private data for offline training.
Furthermore, it does not fully utilize geometric principles. For example, the data representation space
can be partitioned into a task-relevant row space and a task-irrelevant null space. These subspaces,
which are distinct from the subspace reflecting the primary data correlations [Aumüller et al., 2024],
dictate the directions in which the model is sensitive or robust to perturbations. While identifying and
exploiting these subspaces can enhance utility, the existing LDP literature has largely overlooked
these structural properties.
In this paper, we propose a novel method to enhance utility under LDP by reducing the noise injected
into task-critical subspaces defined by downstream models. Our approach is motivated by the geo-
metric insight that Jacobian-based analysis can identify task-sensitive and task-insensitive subspaces,
thereby enabling fine-grained control over the noise injected into each subspace. Furthermore, we
exploit the observation that, under an isotropic privacy budget allocation, sensitivity modulation
can effectively reduce the noise scale applied to a specific subspace. Building on these principles,
we introduce a method that reshapes standard isotropic noise into an anisotropic distribution. The
anisotropic noise reshaping does not incur any additional privacy leakage because it is a bijective
post-processing procedure.
To the best of our knowledge, our work pioneers the enhancement of utility under LDP through
Jacobian-based row space identification and anisotropic noise reshaping. A key advantage of our
approach is its applicability to both linear and non-linear downstream models without incurring addi-
tional privacy leakage. Moreover, it can be seamlessly integrated with other advanced mechanisms.

Preliminaries
2.1
Row Space and Null Space
Suppose that we have an m-dimensional data representation z ∈Rm and a linear transformation
matrix W ∈Rl×m, where m, l ∈Z+. The row space and null space of W are two subspaces that
together form an orthogonal decomposition of Rm. The row space, Row(W ), is spanned by the row
vectors of W and captures the directions along which the transformation has a non-trivial effect. In
contrast, the null space, Null(W ) = {z ∈Rm | W z = 0}, consists of all vectors annihilated by the
transformation. These two subspaces characterize which components of a representation are task-
relevant (e.g., for element-wise summation, linear regression and classification) and task-irrelevant,
respectively.
Consider a linear task function T : Rm →Rl, which applies a weight matrix W to a representation
z, i.e., T (z) = W z. Any representation z can be decomposed into zr ∈Rm belonging to
Row(W ), and zn ∈Rm belonging to Null(W ), i.e., z = zr + zn. The linear operation then
unfolds as W z = W (zr + zn) = W zr, implying that zn has no effect on the task output, while
zr determines the results.

For a linear task function, a basis for Row(W ), ur, is derived from the Jacobian, JT (z) =

characterizes the transformation does not exist. Instead, we can analyze the local behavior of T around
a specific representation z0 using a first-order Taylor expansion: T (z0+∆z) ≈T (z0)+JT (z0)∆z,
where ∆z is a sufficiently small perturbation. It is worth noting that this local linear approximation is
well-suited for modern deep learning architectures. For instance, since neural networks composed of
parallel ReLU-equipped units function as piecewise linear approximators, even complex non-linear
decision boundaries can be decomposed into a collection of locally linear maps. Locally, the Jacobian
JT (z0) acts analogously to the linear weight matrix W , allowing us to define a local row space
and a local null space specific to the point z0. If the perturbation ∆z lies within the local null
space of JT (z0), then JT (z0)∆z = 0, and consequently T (z0 + ∆z) ≈T (z0), meaning that the
non-linear transformation remains locally invariant to perturbations along the null space directions. In
the case of learning-based downstream models, the Jacobian can be computed using backpropagation
without parameter updates.
2.2
Local Differential Privacy
Local Differential Privacy (LDP) [Kasiviswanathan et al., 2011] operates under a decentralized trust
model in which each data owner randomizes their data locally prior to disclosure to any untrusted
party. This contrasts with Central Differential Privacy (CDP) [Dwork et al., 2006], which assumes a
trusted curator to collect raw data and randomize aggregate statistics computed over the dataset.
In this work, we consider an LDP setting that applies a randomization mechanism directly to
each data representation z ∈Z, within the representation space Z ⊂Rm. Let ϵ > 0 denote
the privacy budget and δ ≥0 the failure probability, where δ = 0 corresponds to pure ϵ-LDP
and δ > 0 to (ϵ, δ)-LDP. A mechanism M : Rm →Rm satisfies (ϵ, δ)-LDP if, for any pair of
representations z, z′ ∈Z and any measurable subset S ⊆Range(M), it holds that Pr[M(z) ∈
S] ≤eϵ Pr[M(z′) ∈S] + δ. A canonical class of mechanisms satisfying these guarantees is that of
additive noise mechanisms [Dwork and Roth, 2014], which produce a noisy representation of the
form
˜z = M(z) = z + ξ.
(1)
For (ϵ, 0)-LDP (i.e., pure ϵ-LDP), the Laplace mechanism ML is a natural choise, where the noise
scale b is directly proportional to the ℓ1-sensitivity, defined as ∆1 = maxz,z′∈Z ∥z −z′∥1, and
each element of ξ is sampled i.i.d. from Lap(0, ∆1/ϵ) [Dwork and Roth, 2014]. For (ϵ, δ)-LDP
with δ > 0, the Gaussian mechanism MG is a natural choice, where ξ ∼N(0, σ2I) and the noise
scale σ is proportional to the ℓ2-sensitivity, ∆2 = maxz,z′∈Z ∥z −z′∥2, calibrated via the Analytic
Gaussian Mechanism [Balle and Wang, 2018].
As a local instantiation of DP, LDP inherits its fundamental strengths, including worst-case bounds on
privacy leakage and immunity to post-processing [Dwork and Roth, 2014]. These theoretical bounds
hold even against a strong adversary with full knowledge of the randomization mechanism M and its
parameters, including the privacy budget, sensitivity, and noise distribution settings. Furthermore, the
privacy guarantees of LDP are preserved under arbitrary post-processing independent of (private)
data representation. Once ˜z is produced by a mechanism satisfying (ϵ, δ)-LDP, any measurable
downstream computation or transformation g(˜z) independent of the private data maintains the same
privacy guarantee without consuming additional privacy budget.

space and null space of the classifier, respectively. Crucially, perturbing a representation z ∈R2
along ur can alter the classification outcome, whereas perturbations of any arbitrary magnitude along
un leave the classification result unchanged. Based on this observation, our approach anisotropically
reshapes the isotropic noise ξ to allocate less noise along the task-sensitive direction ur and more
along the task-invariant direction un.

mechanism. Finally, through the linear bijective rotation and scaling applied during post-processing,
noise satisfying ϵ-LDP is automatically distributed across all coordinates.

Covariance Matrix for Rotation and Rescaling
To exploit post-processing immunity, our approach relies on a covariance matrix, Σ, constructed
from public knowledge. In typical LDP settings, although downstream models cannot access the
(private) representation z, they are generally expected to perform interpolation or be trained on data
following a similar distribution. We therefore assume access to widely known downstream models or
those pre-trained on public datasets, and estimate Σ by analyzing the row and null spaces of these
public models. Moreover, we assume that the dimension m of the representation space exceeds the
rank r of the model’s row space, inspired by [Bengio et al., 2013, Li et al., 2018, Aghajanyan et al.,
2021]. Recall that Rm can be orthogonally decomposed into a row space and a null space, where the
dimension m is the sum of the rank r and the nullity n.
The construction of Σ consists of two components: the rotation and the scaling, as mentioned in
Section 4.1. We first determine the rotation matrix U, which aligns the axes of ξa with the basis
vectors of the row and null spaces. Then, we determine the scaling matrix Λ = diag(λ1, · · · , λm),
which assigns noise scales to each coordinate to enhance data utility.

4.3
Sensitivity Bounding
We employ a bounding strategy to effectively reduce the noise injected into task-critical subspaces.
Using (6), we assign a ratio less than or equal to 1 to important coordinates and a ratio greater
than or equal to 1 to less important coordinates. Applying L−1 during the pre-processing step thus
stretches the row space and shrinks the null space. The stretching of the row space amplifies the
overall sensitivity in Rm (see Appendix A.3), requiring a large amount of noise to be injected to
guarantee ϵ-LDP. Consequently, when L is applied during the post-processing step, compared to
directly injecting isotropic noise into the original space Z, the row space receives the same amount
of noise, whereas the null space suffers from excessive noise amplification.
However, by regulating the sensitivity with a bounding function in the transformed space L−1Z, we
can effectively reduce the amount of noise injected into the row space while satisfying ϵ-LDP. The
null space also remains protected by noise satisfying ϵ-LDP, although this noise is often excessive. A
more detailed explanation, along with an illustration, is provided in Appendix A.2.
Candidate bounding functions range from ℓp-norm-based clipping to threshold-based filtering, for
p ∈{1, 2, . . . , ∞}. The choice of this function depends on the mechanism and the application. The
ℓ1-norm is often used when δ = 0. Importantly, regardless of the chosen bounding function, the
threshold parameter has to be determined using public information or prior domain knowledge to
prevent any unintended privacy leakage; see Appendix A.4.

Related Work
In this section, we briefly review closely related work. Further discussions on relevance-aware and
feature-wise approaches, alongside post-processing, public knowledge, and DP assisted by public
data and models, are provided in Appendix A.1.
Task-Aware LDP. The work by [Cheng et al., 2022] is most closely related to ours: they propose
an encoding-decoding framework that exploits downstream model information to apply LDP to
high-dimensional data, injecting noise between the encoding and decoding steps. The most significant
difference emerges when dealing with non-linear downstream models. For these models, they propose
training the encoder and decoder using unprotected data. Specifically, the encoder and decoder are
trained by minimizing the discrepancy between the downstream model’s outputs when fed with
protected versus unprotected private data. In contrast, we utilize the public Jacobian to identify the
local row and null spaces. Consequently, unlike their private-data-dependent encoder and decoder,
our method avoids any risk of privacy leakage. See Appendix A.1 for more differences.
Coordinate-Wise DP. Our work is also closely aligned with [Muthukrishnan and Kalyani, 2025],
which investigates the determination of non-identical noise scales for independent coordinates with
varying sensitivities under DP. Their primary objective is to minimize MSE between the original and
privatized data. In contrast, our approach prioritizes downstream utility over the data MSE (DMSE)
minimization. These two objectives are often misaligned. For example, injecting substantial noise
into the null space may significantly degrade the DMSE while leaving downstream performance
unaffected. Furthermore, while they directly inject anisotropic noise into the private data, we opt to
inject isotropic noise, which is then distributed anisotropically through reshaping.
Variance-Aware DP. In terms of utilizing prior information, the approximate method proposed by
[Aumüller et al., 2024] shares similarities with our work. By assuming a known covariance and
data sampled from distributions with bounded coordinate-wise standard deviations, [Aumüller et al.,
2024] calibrates noise by disproportionately allocating the privacy budget to coordinates exhibiting
higher variance, guided by the data geometry inherent in the covariance matrix. In contrast, our
approach determines noise scales based on the task-specific importance of subspaces utilized by the
downstream model, rather than relying on data correlations.
6
Evaluation
We evaluate our method across downstream inference tasks (one regression and two image classifica-
tion) under varying representation dimensions (m ∈{16, 32, 64}) and privacy budgets (ϵ ∈[0.5, 10]).
Datasets. We apply user-level LDP to time-series regression using London household smart meter
(LHSM) data [UK Power Networks], which records the half-hourly electricity consumption (kWh/half-
hour) of 5,547 London households over approximately 27 months. Additionally, we apply item-
level LDP to image classification on the CIFAR-10-C, CIFAR-10 datasets [Krizhevsky, 2009], and
MNIST [LeCun et al., 1998].
We split the LHSM dataset into public and private sets based on both household and chronological
criteria. The public dataset comprises the initial 14 months of data from 3,000 randomly sampled
households, used for training the LR model. The private dataset consists of the remaining 13 months
from the other households, where it is independent and out-of-sample for the pre-trained model.
The training sets from CIFAR-10 and MNIST are treated as public. These are partitioned into training
and validation sets with an 80:20 split to pre-train the public feature extractors (VAE [Kingma
and Welling, 2013] and ResNet-20 [He et al., 2016]) and classifiers. To assess the utility of the
mechanisms, the test sets of CIFAR-10-C [Hendrycks and Dietterich, 2019] (e.g., brightness, fog,
defocus blur, and Gaussian noise), CIFAR-10, and MNIST are treated as private. Note that the
CIFAR-10-C test set exhibits a distribution shift compared to the CIFAR-10 training set.
Downstream Tasks. Both the regression and classification models are trained on the public dataset
and subsequently evaluated by feeding them the randomized private dataset to measure accuracy
and agreement. Specifically, the regression task involves predicting the next day’s energy_mean
using the previous 16 days (m = 16) of energy_mean values as input. For the classification task, an
m-dimensional feature representation (m ∈{32, 64}) is first extracted via a public feature extractor.

Non-Linear Classification. The non-linear classification experiments were conducted on the CIFAR-
10-C (Brightness corruption at the highest severity level 5) test dataset using a ResNet-20-based
non-linear MLP classifier with ReLU activations, where m = 64 dimensional feature vectors serve
as input. Utility is evaluated by classification accuracy, with the no-randomization baseline achieving

budgets. Laplace (ℓ1) yielded accuracies near 0.1, equivalent to random guessing on a 10-class task,
while PrivUnit2 and PrivUnitG achieved results of 0.2933 and 0.2858 at ϵ = 7.5, respectively.
The task-aware approach by [Cheng et al., 2022] is focused on MNIST and does not offer guidance
for more complex datasets such as CIFAR-10(-C). Crucially, their method relies on direct access
to private data during the training phase. Despite avoiding such direct access to private data, our
approach demonstrates superior results. As detailed in Appendix A.8.6, integrating PA with PrivUnit2
and PrivUnitG significantly outperforms their method under its optimal configuration, while the
Laplace+PA mechanism yields comparable or superior performance.
Incorporating PA consistently and significantly improved classification accuracy across all mecha-
nisms and ϵ values. Laplace+PA improved over its base counterpart at every operating point, reaching
0.1989 at ϵ = 7.5 compared to 0.1177 without PA. More notably, PrivUnit2+PA and PrivUnitG+PA
delivered substantially stronger performance, achieving accuracies of 0.4978 and 0.4860 at ϵ = 7.5
and 0.3754 and 0.3688 at ϵ = 5.0, respectively. This represents an improvement of approximately
20 and 15 percentage points for each respective budget compared to their non-PA counterparts,
underscoring the effectiveness of PA in recovering a significant portion of the utility lost to privacy
noise. CW+PA , however, underperformed relative to other PA-augmented mechanisms in this setting,
recording only 0.1581 at ϵ = 7.5 and 0.1366 at ϵ = 5.0. This performance was even lower than that
of Laplace+PA. The distinction between these two approaches lies in the use of sensitivity bounding
versus a DMSE-optimized noise scale. These results suggest that our clipping-based approach
constitutes a more effective under conditions where DMSE optimization is suboptimal.

Ablation, Distribution Shift, and Additional Baseline
We provide results of our ablation studies (on clipping thresholds and pre-/post-processing) A.8.7;
distribution shift analysis (across 20 corruption-severity combinations) A.8.9; comparisons with

Several findings from these evaluations are noteworthy: First, the noise scale, rather than the distortion
induced by clipping, constitutes the primary bottleneck under LDP constraints. Second, both pre-
processing and post-processing components are indispensable for achieving optimal utility. Third, PA-
integrated approaches demonstrate robust performance across both the type and severity of practical
distribution shifts. Fourth, PA-integrated approaches outperform approximate DP mechanisms,
without the relaxation provided by a non-zero failure probability δ. Finally, PA remains effective for
non-linear classifiers that employ various activation functions within hidden layers.
7
Conclusion and Limitations
We proposed a principled approach to improve the privacy-utility tradeoff under LDP by reshaping
isotropic noise into an anisotropic distribution via Jacobian-based subspace analysis. Extensive
experiments across various linear and non-linear tasks demonstrate consistent utility gains without
compromising privacy. A formal theoretical analysis of the utility loss induced by clipping in complex
tasks remains an open challenge, as this tradeoff is contingent on the underlying data distribution.
Establishing a generalized analytical methodology represents a compelling direction for future
research.

A.1
Related Work
Task-Aware. The work by [Cheng et al., 2022] is most closely related to ours. They propose
an encoding-decoding methodology that exploits downstream model information to apply LDP to
high-dimensional data. In their approach, noise is injected after the encoding step and prior to
decoding.
For linear downstream models, our methodology shares several structural similarities with theirs.
However, the key distinctions lie in the details. First, their analytical approach is not based on
Jacobian computations and is limited to linear models. Second, they focus solely on minimizing
the Mean Squared Error (MSE), rendering their approach sub-optimal for categorical predictions
that rely on argmax operations. Finally, when allocating noise scales or ratios across coordinates,
they adopt a constraint that fixes the total noise energy from an MSE perspective. In contrast, from a
privacy perspective, we enforce a constraint that maintains either the sum of the inverse noise scales
or the sum of the inverse variances.
The most significant difference emerges when dealing with non-linear downstream models. For these
models, they propose training the encoder and decoder using unprotected data. Specifically, the
encoder and decoder are trained by minimizing the discrepancy between the downstream model’s
outputs when fed with protected versus unprotected private data. In contrast, we utilize the public
Jacobian to identify the local row and null spaces. Consequently, unlike their private-data-dependent
encoder and decoder, our method avoids any risk of privacy leakage.
Relevance-Aware. Another line of related work focuses on dynamically allocating privacy budgets
based on feature importance. For instance, [Phan et al., 2017] proposed the Adaptive Laplace
Mechanism (AdLM), which adjusts the amount of noise injected into each input dimension based
on its contribution to the final model output. To quantify this importance, they employ Layer-
wise Relevance Propagation (LRP), a set of formulas that mathematically redistributes the output
prediction score backward to the individual input features. Based on these calculated LRP scores,
AdLM allocates a proportionally larger privacy budget to highly relevant features, thereby reducing
the noise added to them while heavily perturbing less relevant features.
While both AdLM and our methodology share the high-level intuition of task-aware, they fundamen-
tally differ in their mathematical foundations. Regarding the measurement of importance, AdLM
relies on LRP to compute the proportional attribution of each feature (i.e., how much a specific
pixel contributed to the total output score). In contrast, our approach utilizes the Jacobian matrix to
evaluate the sensitivity (i.e., the gradient or rate of change) of the output with respect to the input.
This mathematically grounded approach allows us to analytically decompose the input space into
geometrically meaningful structures, specifically, the local row and null spaces, rather than simply
assigning scalar scores to individual pixels.
Coordinate-Wise, Correlation-Aware, and Feature-Wise DP. Our work is also closely aligned
with [Muthukrishnan and Kalyani, 2025], which investigates the determination of non-identical
noise scales for independent coordinates with varying sensitivities under (ϵ, δ)-DP. Their primary
objective is to minimize MSE between the original and privatized data. In contrast, our approach
prioritizes downstream utility over the data MSE (DMSE) minimization. These two objectives are
often misaligned. For example, injecting substantial noise into the null space may significantly
degrade the DMSE while leaving downstream performance unaffected. Furthermore, while they
directly inject anisotropic noise into the private data, we opt to inject isotropic noise, which is then
distributed anisotropically through reshaping.
[Alaggan et al., 2016] introduced the concept of Heterogeneous Differential Privacy (HDP), which
captures the non-uniformity of privacy expectations both among different users and across various
data items belonging to the same individual. The contribution of HDP lies in its stretching mecha-
nism, a modification of the Laplace mechanism that manipulates function sensitivity through linear
transformations on the input domain. This approach enables item-grained privacy, allowing for the
adaptive calibration of noise levels based on the specific sensitivity of each piece of information.
Their empirical evaluation demonstrates that HDP effectively accommodates heterogeneous privacy
requirements while sustaining high utility, particularly in semantic clustering tasks, by avoiding
unnecessary noise injection into less sensitive data.

The primary distinction between our approach and HDP lies in the targeted noise reduction based
on dimensional importance for the downstream model. We allocate a smaller privacy budget to
less critical dimensions while preserving the budget for more important dimensions to minimize
noise injection. Ultimately, our goal is to reduce the noise injected into the key dimensions while
maintaining the privacy budget to enhance utility.
The Pufferfish [Kifer and Machanavajjhala, 2014] was introduced to address the vulnerabilities of
traditional Differential Privacy (DP) in scenarios where the assumption of record independence is
violated. While DP defines privacy based on the syntactic structure of neighboring databases, it can
lead to significant privacy leakage in real-world datasets characterized by complex correlations. The
core contribution is the introduction of data evolution scenarios, which are probabilistic models that
mathematically define the data-generating process. By incorporating the joint probability distribution
of records directly into the privacy constraint, Pufferfish accounts for data dependencies within the
privacy definition itself.
Similarly, assuming a partially known covariance and data sampled from distributions with bounded
coordinate-wise standard deviations, [Aumüller et al., 2024] calibrate noise by disproportionately
allocating the privacy budget to coordinates exhibiting higher variance, guided by the data geometry
inherent in the covariance matrix. Building on this, [Dagan et al., 2024] extend this methodology
to settings with unknown covariance by employing FriendlyCore during pre-processing, thereby
effectively relaxing the aforementioned coordinate-wise assumptions.
The works in [Kifer and Machanavajjhala, 2014, Aumüller et al., 2024, Dagan et al., 2024] explore
anisotropic noise injection by accounting for data correlations. In contrast, our approach controls
noise levels based on the importance of subspaces within the data space utilized by the downstream
model, rather than relying on data correlations.
Post-Processing & Public Knowledge. There have been numerous efforts to enhance utility under
LDP by designing post-processing functions based on public knowledge, incorporating methodologies
such as data aggregation, filtering, denoising, deconvolution, and statistical inference.
In LDP, data aggregation traces its roots back to Warner’s Randomized Response [Warner, 1965].
Fundamentally, it involves estimating the true underlying distribution by removing statistical bias
from data that clients have intentionally perturbed prior to transmission. Under Warner’s original
design, a respondent answers a sensitive question with probability p, and a complementary question
with probability 1 −p. This ensures plausible deniability, making it impossible for the surveyor to
determine which specific question the individual answered. To correct the induced bias, the server
applies a linear inversion estimator based on the observed frequencies.
Google’s RAPPOR [Erlingsson et al., 2014] is a pioneering LDP mechanism deployed in Chrome
that combines Bloom filters with a two-stage randomized response. It first maps input values into
a Bloom filter using hash functions, then perturbs the filter via Permanent Randomized Response
(PRR) to ensure longitudinal privacy. Each transmission is further randomized through Instantaneous
Randomized Response (IRR). On the server side, the collected bitstrings are decoded using Lasso
regression and Bonferroni correction to accurately estimate category frequencies.
[Apple Differential Privacy Team, 2017] has deployed a LDP pipeline based on Count Mean Sketch
(CMS) and Hadamard Count Mean Sketch (HCMS) algorithms to securely collect user data—such as
emoji usage frequencies and newly coined keyboard words, across its iOS and macOS environments.
Specifically, CMS is employed to aggregate emoji frequencies, whereas HCMS is utilized to detect
high-memory and high-energy consuming web domains in Safari. The client-side HCMS mechanism
sequentially performs hash encoding, Hadamard transformation, single-bit sampling, and LDP-
compliant bit-flipping. To reconstruct the population statistics, the server aggregates millions of these
transmitted 1-bit vectors and applies an inverse Hadamard transform.
For collecting counter-type telemetry data, such as app usage duration on Windows OS, [Ding
et al., 2017] employed a 1-bit mechanism known as dBitFlip in conjunction with histogram-based
aggregation. The dBitFlip mechanism applies bit-flipping probabilities that are conditioned on the
user’s actual value. To estimate the distribution, counter values are discretized into predefined bins,
and the client transmits a randomized 1-bit response for each corresponding bin.
[Hay et al., 2010] introduced a post-processing method that uses hierarchical consistency to refine
noisy histogram counts. Histograms collected under LDP often suffer from hierarchical inconsisten-

top-down phase that adjusts these values to satisfy the parent-child sum constraint. This dual-stage
approach not only ensures consistency but also significantly reduces variance, thereby enhancing the
overall accuracy of the private histogram.
[Wang et al., 2017] formalized the perturbation process of the pure LDP through two key parameters:
p, the probability of reporting the true value, and q, the probability of reporting a perturbed value.
Based on the privacy-utility relationship defined by these probabilities (i.e., p/q = eϵ), they derived
a generalized unbiased estimator to reconstruct original frequencies from noisy observations. A
contribution of their work is the mathematical derivation of optimal p and q values that minimize
the estimation variance under a fixed privacy budget ϵ. This theoretical foundation led to the
development of Optimized Unary Encoding (OUE) and Optimized Local Hashing (OLH), which
improve estimation accuracy compared to traditional randomized response mechanisms.
[Jia and Gong, 2019] introduced a calibration to enhance the aggregation accuracy of LDP-collected
data. Their approach uses statistical inference, under the assumption that the underlying noise
mechanism and certain characteristics of the true distribution are publicly available, to refine the
results of frequency estimation and heavy hitter identification. By applying an iterative calibration
process, the proposed method effectively mitigates the estimation errors of standard LDP protocols,
providing more reliable data for downstream analytical tasks.
To address the inconsistency between parent and child nodes in tree-based range queries under LDP,
[Cormode et al., 2019] propose a post-processing method based on Least Squares (LS) estimation.
The primary contribution of this work is the theoretical proof that their approach achieves the
Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE). By using this consistency-based refinement, the method
minimizes the weighted variance across the hierarchical structure, ensuring that the sum of child
nodes aligns perfectly with their parent.
To further refine LDP frequency estimation, [Wang et al., 2020] propose a post-processing framework
that uses prior knowledge of the probability simplex constraint that individual frequencies must
be non-negative and sum to unity. By projecting noisy estimates onto a valid probability simplex,
their approach ensures that the reconstructed distribution is statistically consistent. This method
significantly enhances utility without requiring any modifications to the underlying LDP protocols,
demonstrating the power of constraint-based post-processing.
To enable effective learning on GNN under LDP, [Sajadmanesh and Gatica-Perez, 2021] designed
KProp, a specialized propagation mechanism. KProp aggregates and normalizes feature information
from neighbors within K hops to mitigate the impact of local noise. By assuming the server has
access to the graph topology, the method denoises private node features through neighborhood
averaging. This approach leverages the structural properties of the graph as a post-processing step,
boosting model performance despite the presence of stringent LDP noise.
[Fang et al., 2023] introduced a frequency estimation method by demonstrating that the encoding and
perturbation processes in LDP can be mathematically formulated as circular convolution. Relying
on the fact that the perturbation mechanism is publicly known, the authors proposed a systematic
denoising approach using deconvolution and Wiener filters. This signal processing-based perspective
provides a more rigorous and effective aggregation framework compared to traditional linear inversion
methods, enhancing the signal-to-noise ratio in reconstructed distributions.
Among these studies, [Sajadmanesh and Gatica-Perez, 2021] is closely related to our work in that
it addresses LDP for high-dimensional data. However, while our research focuses on the inference
phase under LDP, their work explores learning of GNNs. Furthermore, their primary contribution is
centered on aggregation methods, distinguishing it from our focus on reducing the noise injected into
the task-sensitive subspace.
Public Data & Model Assisted DP. Various studies have leveraged public data or pre-trained models
to alleviate the utility loss inherent in Differential Privacy (DP). For instance, [Kurakin et al., 2022]
investigated the use of ResNet-18 models pre-trained on the Places365 dataset, demonstrating that
such priors are vital for navigating the loss landscape under noise injection. Their findings underscore
that leveraging feature representations learned from public data is a key factor in improving both
convergence speed and final classification accuracy. Similarly, DOPE-SGD [Nasr et al., 2023]

A.5
Compatibility
Our approach can be seamlessly integrated with mechanisms that achieve randomization by sampling
noise from Laplace, Gaussian, or other distributions and adding it to the representation. Furthermore,
it is compatible with mechanisms based on probabilistic sampling designed to preserve directional
information [Bhowmick et al., 2018, Asi et al., 2022]. This is achieved simply by incorporating an
additional step to transform ¯z into the required input format of the target mechanism, followed by an
inverse transformation prior to applying the post-processing function.
A.6
Aggregation
Because of the heavy randomization in LDP, practical applications frequently adopt an aggregation
step to average out the injected noise. In our proposed approach, this aggregation takes place between
the randomization and post-processing steps. Since we inject zero-mean, isotropic noise during the
randomization, the aggregation step acts as an unbiased estimator, canceling out the noise as the
number of samples increases. Our post-processing function is applied to this aggregated output,
ensuring that the resulting representations perform effectively in the downstream model.

A.8.1
Baseline
We compare against representative LDP mean estimation mechanisms:
• Laplace [Dwork and Roth, 2014]: This mechanism clips the input vector to an ℓ1-ball of radius ρ
and adds Laplace noise calibrated to the corresponding sensitivity. It serves as a fundamental pure
ϵ-LDP baseline with no structural assumptions.
• AGM [Balle and Wang, 2018]: This method clips the input to an ℓ2-ball and adds isotropic Gaussian
noise calibrated via the analytical Gaussian mechanism. It satisfies (ϵ, δ)-LDP and is widely used
as a strong reference for approximate LDP.
• PrivUnit2 [Bhowmick et al., 2018] and PrivUnitG [Asi et al., 2022]: We select these mechanisms
as our primary baselines because they represent strong theoretical references for minimizing
estimation error, allowing us to benchmark the effectiveness of our proposed method against
near-optimal utility limits. These spherical mechanisms project inputs onto the unit sphere Sm−1
and apply a step-function randomizer. PrivUnit2 is minimax optimal for ℓ2 mean estimation on the
sphere under pure LDP, while PrivUnitG extends this to a Gaussian ambient-space variant with
improved empirical performance in high dimensions.
• Instance Optimal [Huang et al., 2021]: This mechanism applies a randomized Hadamard rotation,
shifts each coordinate by its empirical median, clips the result, and adds AGM noise. It is
specifically designed to be instance-optimal for mean estimation under approximate LDP.
• PLAN [Aumüller et al., 2024]: This is originally proposed as a variance-aware zCDP (Concentrated
Differential Privacy) mechanism. We adapt it to the (ϵ, δ)-LDP setting. PLAN scales each client’s
report by the inverse of its per-dimension variance prior to clipping, where the variance estimates
are obtained from a public training dataset. This scaling balances noise across dimensions, reducing
error in both low- and high-variance directions compared to uniform noise addition.
• TASK [Cheng et al., 2022]: This baseline is the most closely related work to ours, as it also utilizes
task-relevant functional information for linear models.
• CW [Muthukrishnan and Kalyani, 2025]: This baseline employs a coordinate-wise independent
but non-identically distributed (i.n.i.d.) budget allocation strategy. It non-uniformly distributes the
privacy budget across coordinates with varying sensitivities to minimize the Mean Squared Error
(MSE). Since our proposed approach induces coordinate-wise disparities through its singular-value
weighting λi, we integrate this i.n.i.d. allocation strategy into our framework. Consequently, it is
evaluated as an adapted variant of our approach, rather than as a strict reproduction of the original
method.
All clipping thresholds ρ are set to the 90th percentile of the corresponding norms computed over the
public training set.
Our proposed approach is mechanism-agnostic. It exploits task-relevant subspaces without altering
the internal noise-generation procedures of the underlying LDP mechanisms, and can be seamlessly
integrated with existing approaches. To demonstrate this broad applicability and orthogonal benefit,
our evaluation presents comprehensive results combining our method with a diverse set of representa-
tive baselines. Specifically, we show that our method consistently enhances the utility of Laplace,
PrivUnit2, PrivUnitG, and CW, proving its effectiveness as a versatile, standalone enhancement.

are then uniformly sampled at random without replacement from the round’s assigned subset. For
instance, with N = 20, each round is allocated 127 households, yielding a maximum of K = 127 per
round. Furthermore, evaluation dates are spaced at least m days apart, ensuring that no household’s
measurement from a single day appears in more than one evaluation window. Together, these
conditions guarantee that each household contributes to at most one aggregation across the entire
evaluation, ensuring that the privacy cost incurred is ϵ per household regardless of N.
The evaluation is conducted over N rounds. For instance, setting m = 16 corresponds to performing
N = 20 regression rounds over a span of 320 days.
We evaluate utility by measuring the Mean Squared Error (MSE) against ground-truth values.
Composition. In our evaluation, the sequential composition of privacy budgets is circumvented. As
detailed above, the partitioning of the household pool into disjoint subsets and the m-day spacing
between evaluation dates ensure that the data from any given household is queried at most once
across all N rounds. According to the parallel composition theorem of differential privacy, evaluating
queries on disjoint subsets of data does not accumulate the privacy cost. Therefore, the total privacy
guarantee for any individual household remains bounded by the single-query budget ϵ, without
requiring advanced composition mechanisms or budget degradation.

A.8.5
Feature Extraction
For the image classification tasks, we employ two distinct models for feature extraction: a standard
Variational Autoencoder (VAE) and a ResNet-20 architecture.
The VAE is applied to the MNIST dataset to facilitate experiments across various latent dimensions,
enabling us to evaluate the impact of dimensionality on LDP utility. Specifically, we investigate latent
representation vectors of size m = 32.
Conversely, we utilize ResNet-20 for the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C datasets. Unlike the simple
grayscale digits in MNIST, CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C consist of complex color images, necessi-
tating a feature extractor capable of processing multi-channel inputs. ResNet-20 was chosen for its
optimal balance of efficiency and performance; it is a relatively lightweight model that minimizes
computational overhead while achieving approximately 90% accuracy on CIFAR-10. To construct
the base representations for our evaluations, we utilize the pre-trained ResNet-20 to extract a fixed
64-dimensional feature vector, which serves as the input to the downstream linear classifier.
VAE. For the MNIST dataset, we pre-train a standard Variational Autoencoder (VAE) composed of
multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) using the public dataset. The encoder network takes the flattened
28 × 28 grayscale images as input and processes them through two shared hidden layers, each
containing 512 units with ReLU activations. This is followed by two parallel linear layers that
output the mean and log-variance of the m-dimensional latent space. The decoder mirrors this
architecture, mapping the latent vector back to the original image space through two 512-unit
hidden layers, culminating in a sigmoid output layer. The VAE is trained to minimize the standard

objective function, combining binary cross-entropy for reconstruction loss and Kullback-Leibler (KL)
divergence. We utilize the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−3 and a batch size of 512. To
prevent overfitting, training incorporates early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs, monitoring the
validation loss on the 20% split of the public training data.
Following this unsupervised pre-training, the encoder is jointly fine-tuned end-to-end with the
downstream classifiers using cross-entropy loss. This joint optimization ensures that the encoder
generates task-specific representations optimized for classification accuracy rather than solely for
image reconstruction. Finally, for the downstream LDP evaluations, we deterministically extract the
mean vector directly from this fine-tuned encoder to serve as the base representation vector, bypassing
the reparameterization trick to ensure stable evaluation.
ResNet-20. For the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-10-C datasets, we implement a standard ResNet-20
architecture as the feature extractor. The network processes the 3-channel color images through
an initial convolutional layer (16 channels), followed by three sequential stages of residual blocks.
Each stage consists of three basic blocks, progressively increasing the feature channels from 16 to 32
and finally to 64, with spatial downsampling applied via strided convolutions. Network weights are
initialized using Kaiming normal initialization. To extract the representation vector z, the final feature
map is processed through an adaptive average pooling layer and flattened, yielding a 64-dimensional
deterministic feature vector.
Similar to the pre-trained classifiers, the feature extractors are pre-trained exclusively on public
datasets, specifically the training sets of MNIST and CIFAR-10. During evaluation, these extractors
process inputs from private datasets (the test sets of MNIST, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-10-C) to produce
an m-dimensional vector z as output.

Clipping Threshold. The performance of the proposed approach (PA) is evaluated on the CIFAR-10-
C dataset under maximum brightness corruption (Level 5). We specifically investigate the effect of
different clipping thresholds ρ on ReLU-based non-linear MLP classifiers.
PA employs ℓ1-norm clipping within the transformed space L−1(z−µ), restricting the representations
to the bounded space ¯Z. Notably, ℓ1-norm clipping only modifies the magnitude of the representations
while preserving their angular information. The clipping threshold ρ is determined based on a specific
percentile of the public data (e.g., the original CIFAR-10 training set).
Tables 4 presents the results of integrating the PA with the Laplace [Dwork and Roth, 2014],
PrivUnit2 [Bhowmick et al., 2018] and PrivUnitG [Asi et al., 2022] mechanisms, across clipping
thresholds ρ ranging from 10 to 100.
Overall, the mechanisms integrated with the PA achieve higher accuracy when a lower ρ is applied.
Across all three mechanisms, tighter clipping thresholds (10th percentile) consistently yielded superior
accuracy. Although omitted here for brevity, our approach reached peak performance at the 30th
percentile of ρ for the ReLU-based MLP classifier operating on VAE-encoded MNIST features.
Despite the increased distortion of individual representations due to clipping, the substantial reduction
in injected noise scale dominates. This confirms that noise scale is the primary bottleneck under LDP
constraints, rather than the distortion induced by clipping.
The interaction between privacy budget ϵ and clipping threshold ρ reveals that the marginal benefit of
tight clipping grows with larger ϵ, implying that clipping strategy becomes increasingly critical as the
privacy constraint is relaxed.

The ablation results demonstrate that both pre-processing and post-processing components are
indispensable. Removing either component causes all three mechanisms to collapse to near-random
performance (10%), regardless of the privacy budget. This confirms that the two components are not
merely complementary enhancements but jointly necessary for our approach to function.
A notable instability is observed when post-processing is ablated: the standard deviation of accuracy
grows substantially with increasing ϵ, reaching up to 0.026 for PrivUnit2+PA and 0.025 for PrivU-
nitG+PA at ϵ = 10. This suggests that without post-processing, the representations become erratic as
the privacy budget relaxes, undermining the reliability of downstream classification.
Compared to the ℓ1-clipping baseline, the full PA integration achieves up to 90% relative improvement
(Laplace, ϵ = 10), demonstrating that the utility gains stem not from clipping alone, but from the
coordinated interaction of the pre- and post-processing pipeline introduced by PA.
To disentangle the internal mechanics of the post-processing phase, we evaluate a "w/o Reshaping"
variant, which retains the noise rotation but omits the scaling procedure. Across Laplace, PrivUnit2,
and PrivUnitG, the performance of this variant falls between the fully ablated post-processing baseline
and the full PA integration. For instance, with PrivUnit2 at ϵ = 10, "w/o Reshaping" variant recovers
substantial utility (0.5117) compared to the complete absence of post-processing (0.0875), yet still
falls short of the full PA (0.5829). This result implies that while directional alignment (rotation) alone
successfully mitigates a portion of the noise impact, structural scaling (reshaping) is necessary to
fully utilize the proposed approach.
A further distinction emerges when comparing the "w/o Reshaping" variant against the direct ℓ1-
clipping baseline applied in the original space Z. Although both configurations omit anisotropic
noise scaling, they differ in one fundamental respect. The "w/o Reshaping" variant applies ℓ1-
clipping within the transformed space L−1(z −µ), where the axes are not only aligned with the
task-critical subspaces but also inversely scaled according to their respective importance. In contrast,
the direct ℓ1-clipping baseline operates in the raw space Z without any such alignment. The consistent
performance gap between these two variants across all three mechanisms demonstrates that clipping
in a task-aligned space is inherently beneficial even without rescaling. This highlights that the utility
of PA stems from the interaction of three key components: space alignment, sensitivity bounding in
the transformed space, and anisotropic rescaling.

A central assumption of our approach is that the Jacobian computed on public data captures the
task-critical subspaces of the private data. To stress-test this assumption, we evaluate PA across four
corruption types (Brightness, Fog, Defocus Blur, and Gaussian Noise) and five severity levels on
CIFAR-10-C, yielding 20 distinct distribution shift scenarios. These corruptions vary not only in
severity but also in nature: Brightness and Fog primarily alter global statistics, Defocus Blur degrades
spatial frequency, and Gaussian Noise directly corrupts pixel-level information, offering a diverse
probe of how different types of public-private mismatch affect the utility of Jacobian-based subspace
identification.
A direct comparison of absolute accuracy across severities is confounded by the fact that the no-
randomization baseline, which serves as a natural upper bound on achievable utility, itself degrades as
corruption severity increases. For instance, under Gaussian Noise at severity 5, the no-randomization
accuracy drops to 0.2621, leaving far less room for any mechanism to demonstrate improvement.
To account for this, we measure the performance of PA in terms of the fraction of recoverable
accuracy it restores. Formally, for each corruption type and severity, we define the recoverable gap as
the difference between the no-randomization accuracy and the base mechanism accuracy, and the
performance gain as the improvement brought by integrating PA:
Recovery Ratio =
Mean Acc(Baseline+PA) −Mean Acc(Baseline)
Mean Acc(No Randomization) −Mean Acc(Baseline)
A higher recovery ratio indicates that PA recovers a larger fraction of the utility lost to LDP noise,
independent of the absolute difficulty imposed by the corruption. This metric cleanly isolates PA’s
contribution from the confounding effect of corruption-induced accuracy degradation.
Tables 6 through 10 present the accuracy of PrivUnit2+PA and PrivUnitG+PA across all 20 distribution
shift scenarios, while Figure 7 summarizes the corresponding recovery ratios at representative privacy
budgets ϵ ∈{1.0, 3.0, 5.0, 7.5}.
In summary, the results demonstrate that PA is robust to both the type and severity of practical
distribution shifts.
These results show several consistent patterns. First, PA consistently enhances utility across all 20
scenarios. The recovery ratio remains positive for every combination of corruption type and severity.
This confirms that the Jacobian computed on clean public data remains a viable proxy for identifying
task-critical subspaces even when private data undergoes substantial distributional shift. Such findings
provide direct empirical support for our central hypothesis.
Second, the recovery ratio scales positively with the privacy budget. Across all corruption types and
severities, the ratio grows monotonically with ϵ. This reflects the fact that PA becomes increasingly
effective as the noise scale decreases, a trend observed without exception across all 20 scenarios.
However, interpretations of absolute accuracy necessitate careful consideration.
The no-
randomization accuracy, which serves as the upper bound on recoverable utility, itself degrades
as corruption severity increases. This is particularly evident under Gaussian Noise, where the no-
randomization accuracy drops to 0.2621 at severity 5 and drastically narrows the recoverable gap.
Consequently, a lower recovery ratio for a specific mechanism, such as Laplace+PA, may not stem
from a failure of PA to improve utility. Instead, it may result from a disproportionately large gap
created by the baseline mechanism’s own instability. The recovery ratio metric effectively decouples
these distinct sources of variation.
Furthermore, the type of corruption modulates the stability of subspace identification. Brightness
corruption yields a nearly constant recovery ratio across severities, suggesting that luminance shifts
leave the downstream model’s gradient directions largely intact. In contrast, Fog and Defocus Blur
show a modest decline in recovery ratio as severity increases. This might be due to the progressive
degradation of spatial frequency content, which creates a mismatch between public and private feature
distributions. Gaussian Noise, conversely, exhibits an apparent increase in the recovery ratio at higher
severities. This is not indicative of enhanced PA effectiveness. Rather, it reflects the rapid contraction
of the recoverable gap as no-randomization accuracy collapses, which mechanically inflates the ratio
even when absolute gains remain stable.
