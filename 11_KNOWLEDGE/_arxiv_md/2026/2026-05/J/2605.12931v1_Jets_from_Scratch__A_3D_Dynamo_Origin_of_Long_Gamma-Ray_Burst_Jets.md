ABSTRACT
The origin of the large-scale poloidal magnetic field required to power relativistic jets in collapsars
remains uncertain. While such a field may be inherited during PNS collapse, the efficiency of this
process is unclear, motivating an in situ mechanism to generate poloidal fields out of the predominantly
toroidal fields produced by stellar differential rotation. We present the first 3D general-relativistic
magnetohydrodynamic collapsar simulations initialized with toroidal magnetic field profiles that closely
follows those of pre-collapse stellar models. As the toroidal field in the disk becomes dynamically
important, it seeds the dynamo, producing coherent poloidal magnetic loops that appear at ∼O(100)
gravitational radii and are then advected inward along paths that may deviate from the disk midplane.
The resulting poloidal fields thread the black hole (BH) and launch highly variable, wobbling relativistic
jets on timescales of order seconds, with the onset depending on the initial magnetic field and the
plasma circularization radius. Although the jets are highly variable and misaligned with the BH spin
axis, they sustain ≳1050 erg s−1, comparable to that inferred for long gamma-ray bursts (LGRB). We
identify magnetic-flux inversions driven by the stochastic dynamo, leading to the formation of striped
jets that could be imprinted in LGRB light curves. These results demonstrate that the accretion-disk
dynamo provides a robust pathway for jet production in collapsars across a broad range of progenitors.

1. INTRODUCTION
The collapse of rapidly rotating massive stars (collap-
sars) is a widely accepted model for the production of
long gamma-ray bursts (LGRBs; S. E. Woosley 1993;
A. I. MacFadyen & S. E. Woosley 1999) — the most
luminous electromagnetic transients in the Universe. In
collapsars, the iron core of a massive star first under-
goes gravitational collapse to form a proto-neutron star
(PNS). If the PNS subsequently accretes sufficient mass
from the infalling stellar gas, it collapses further to form
a black hole (BH). The BH may launch highly colli-

1992; J. E. Rhoads 1999) via the Blandford–Znajek (BZ)
mechanism (R. D. Blandford & R. L. Znajek 1977).
Once these jets break out from the star, they generate
the observed LGRB emission.
The BZ mechanism requires a coherent, large-scale,
dynamically important poloidal magnetic field thread-
ing a spinning BH. In the presence of such fields, mag-
netic field lines tap into the rotational energy of the BH
and extract it in the form of outward-directed Poynt-
ing flux (R. Narayan et al. 2003; J. C. McKinney 2006;
A. Tchekhovskoy et al. 2011). Despite its central im-
portance, the origin of the large-scale poloidal magnetic
field threading the BH remains largely unknown.

Early studies often assumed that the large-scale
poloidal magnetic field originates from the progenitor
star (e.g., A. Burrows et al. 2007; S. S. Komissarov &
M. V. Barkov 2009; P. M¨osta et al. 2014; M. Ober-

A. Janiuk et al. 2023; M. Shibata et al. 2024; D. Issa
et al. 2025; G. Urrutia et al. 2025), which is amplified
during stellar collapse via flux-freezing (J. M. LeBlanc
& J. R. Wilson 1970). However, stellar evolution mod-
els show that the progenitor star primarily generates
toroidal magnetic fields through the Tayler-Spruit dy-
namo (TSD,
R. J. Tayler 1973; H. C. Spruit 2002; J.
Fuller & L. Ma 2019a). The associated poloidal compo-
nent is small-scale and randomly oriented, leading to a
substantial cancellation of the field upon collapse. Con-
sequently, the net poloidal flux available to thread the
newly formed BH is orders of magnitude lower than that
required to power an LGRB jet (O. Gottlieb et al. 2024).
This disparity suggests that another mechanism must be
at work to generate a large-scale poloidal magnetic field.
O. Gottlieb et al. (2024) proposed that the BH ac-
quires the poloidal magnetic flux required to launch an
LGRB jet from the PNS — prior to the PNS collapse,
dynamo processes in the PNS amplify the magnetic field
to proto-magnetar strengths (R. Raynaud et al. 2020; P.
Barr`ere et al. 2023; E. R. Most 2023; P. Barr`ere et al.
2025; K. Kiuchi et al. 2024; A. Igoshev et al. 2025),
generating the large-scale poloidal field that will thread
the newly formed BH. During the PNS phase, the high-
angular-momentum infalling gas forms a centrifugally
supported disk. Upon the PNS collapse, the plasma cur-
rents in this disk anchor the magnetic field lines and pre-
vent the field from rapidly decaying. Whether the BH
accumulates magnetic flux faster than it is lost through
electromagnetic decay depends on whether the disk re-
mains magnetically connected during the PNS collapse;
this requires the disk to viscously extend inward through
the magnetospheric radius set by the strong PNS field
before the magnetic flux is lost through reconnection at
the horizon (A. Bransgrove et al. 2021).
The model by O. Gottlieb et al. (2024), however, is
subject to several uncertainties.
First, the amplifica-
tion timescale of the PNS dynamo may be comparable
to the PNS collapse timescale, raising the question of
whether sufficient poloidal magnetic flux can be gener-
ated prior to collapse. Second, it remains unclear how
much of the amplified field survives the transition from
a PNS to a BH, as magnetic reconnection and dynami-
cal reconfiguration during the PNS collapse may reduce
the net flux that would be inherited by the BH (e.g.,
A. Bransgrove et al. 2021; S. Selvi et al. 2024). Most
critically, the rotation profiles of LGRB progenitors re-
main poorly constrained, leaving open the question of
whether a centrifugally supported disk forms during the
PNS phase (see, e.g., O. Gottlieb et al. 2024; M. Shibata
et al. 2024; C. L. Fryer et al. 2025). If no disk exists prior
to the PNS collapse, the external currents needed to an-
chor the magnetic field lines vanish, preventing the BH
from retaining the PNS magnetic flux.
An alternative to the PNS inheritance scenario is that
the large-scale poloidal magnetic field is generated in
situ within the collapsar disk through some turbulent
dynamo processes (J. F. Hawley et al. 1996). In partic-
ular, the magnetorotational instability (MRI) dynamo
(A. Brandenburg et al. 1995; F. Rincon et al. 2007, 2008;
A. Riols et al. 2013, 2017; O. Gressel & M. E. Pessah
2015; G. Mamatsashvili et al. 2020; J. Guilet et al. 2022;
L. E. Held et al. 2024; J. Jacquemin-Ide et al. 2024b)
has been shown to self-consistently amplify and gener-
ate the large-scale poloidal field required for jet launch-
ing in a variety of systems, including steady-state ac-
creting tori (I. M. Christie et al. 2019; M. Liska et al.
2020; P. E. Rodman & C. S. Reynolds 2024), post-binary
neutron-star merger disks (C. Palenzuela et al. 2022;
O. Gottlieb et al. 2023b; K. Hayashi et al. 2023; M. R.
Izquierdo et al. 2025; E. M. Guti´errez et al. 2026), and
the accretion-induced collapse of (magnetized) white
dwarfs (L. Combi et al. 2025).

2. NUMERICAL SIMULATIONS
We initialize the simulations with a spherically sym-
metric star surrounding a newborn BH of mass M =
3 M⊙, and set its natal dimensionless spin to be close to
the maximum equilibrium PNS spin, a = +0.5 (e.g.,
O. Gottlieb et al. 2024).
We evolve the plasma dy-
namics by solving the ideal GRMHD equations with
the GPU-accelerated code H-AMR (M. T. P. Liska et al.
2022), and provide the full set of equations in Ap-
pendix A. Because H-AMR employs a static metric, the
BH mass and spin remain fixed throughout the simu-
lation. We model the accreting plasma as an ideal gas
with an adiabatic index of γ = 4/3, representative of
radiation-dominated gas.
The radial profiles of the rest-mass density, internal
energy, and magnetic field are taken from MESA models
computed with the same setup as in O. Gottlieb et al.
(2024). In those models, angular-momentum transport
in radiative regions is provided by magnetic torques via
the TSD (H. C. Spruit 2002), while angular momentum
is transported diffusively in convective layers. Mass loss
via winds is modeled as line-driven (J. S. Vink et al.
2000, 2001), and the model adopts the empirical Wolf-
Rayet wind prescription of T. Nugis & H. J. G. L. M.
Lamers (2000) when the surface helium mass fraction
falls below 0.4.
The progenitor models span zero-age
main-sequence masses of (30 −96) M⊙and initial rota-
tion rates of (0.5 −0.99) times the critical angular ve-
locity. We define core collapse as the moment when the
infall velocity in the Fe core reaches 300 km s−1, yield-
ing minimum and maximum stellar masses of 19.5 M⊙
and 45.7 M⊙upon collapse, with corresponding radii of
1.48 R⊙and 1.60 R⊙, respectively.
To determine representative MESA profiles, we compute
an ensemble average across all 120 models and perform
a semi-analytical fit to the aforementioned quantities.
Since the simulation does not include self-gravity to hold
the gas together, we set the initial gas internal energy
profile to be ug(r) = 10−6 GMρ(r)/r.

3.2. Variability and Morphology
Most of our models exhibit flipping of the magnetic
coherence parameter, suggesting that the jet switches

5. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS
The production of Poynting-flux-dominated jets in
collapsars
requires
strong
poloidal
magnetic
fields
threading the BH. Such fields may be inherited from the
PNS at the time of collapse (O. Gottlieb et al. 2024), al-
though the efficiency of this process and the amount of
flux ultimately retained by the BH remain uncertain.
Meanwhile, differential rotation in massive stars pre-
dominantly generates toroidal magnetic fields. There-
fore, in the absence of efficient flux inheritance, an MHD
dynamo is required to convert toroidal fields into the
large-scale poloidal fields needed to power the jet. In this
letter, we present the first three-dimensional GRMHD
study, with initial conditions that closely follow those of
pre-collapse stellar models computed by MESA, demon-
strating that an MHD dynamo can generate coherent,
large-scale poloidal fields of sufficient strength to thread
the BH and launch steady jets with a comparable power
to what is inferred from LGRBs.
5.1. Comparison with Previous Work
M. Shibata et al. (2025) presented the only prior nu-
merical study of collapsar BZ jet formation via an MHD
dynamo. However, their simulations are axisymmetric,
and the mean-field dynamo is imposed through a param-
eterized source term in the evolution equations for the
electromagnetic fields (N. Bucciantini & L. Del Zanna
2013; M. Bugli et al. 2014; M. Shibata et al. 2021a,b).
As such, the dynamo is not captured self-consistently.
Our study differs in several key respects11: (i) the
onset of jet launching in their simulations occurs at
O(10) s after disk formation, whereas we find jet launch-
ing within the first few seconds; (ii) although their
horizon-threading flux can reach ∼1028 G cm2, it fre-
quently changes sign, with the typical timescale for the
flux to remain at the same sign being less than a second.
In contrast, our models reach saturated MAD states

while maintaining a coherent magnetic field threading
the BH horizon, unless a magnetic-flux inversion occurs;
(iii) Whereas their Poynting luminosity rarely reaches
1050 erg s−1 and fluctuates over several orders of mag-
nitude, our jetted models have jet powers of at least
1050 erg s−1 and remain steady unless a magnetic-flux
inversion occurs; (iv) they assume a rapidly spinning BH
(a ≥+0.7), while we use a moderately spinning BH, as
required to produce an LGRB power with ΦMAD ≳10
(O. Gottlieb et al. 2023a). These comparisons indicate
that the dynamo we observe is more efficient at gen-
erating strong, large-scale, and coherent poloidal fields,
and launches more powerful jets. These differences high-
light the importance of fully three-dimensional, self-
consistent dynamo modeling in accurately capturing jet
formation in collapsars.
5.2. Implications for LGRBs
This work, together with that of O. Gottlieb et al.
(2024), addresses one of the longstanding unresolved
problems in collapsar physics: the origin of the mag-
netic field that powers the jet. The accretion-disk dy-
namo model may offer a robust alternative to the fossil
PNS magnetic-field scenario. The fossil-field model re-
lies on a specific ordering of multiple timescales, and
even if the PNS possesses sufficiently strong magnetic
flux, it remains unclear how much of that flux is re-
tained by the BH. In contrast, as we showed in this
study, the accretion-disk dynamo model is more robust
to variations in the initial rotation rate and magnetic-
flux distribution. We emphasize that the accretion-disk
dynamo and fossil-field scenarios are not mutually ex-
clusive; even if the fossil-field mechanism operates, the
accretion-disk dynamo will proceed.
This
result
has
two
important
implications
for
LGRBs.
First, nearly any progenitor that forms an
accretion disk may be capable of launching a jet, sug-

and the circularization radius of the accreting plasma.
Stronger magnetic fields and moderate circularization

Nevertheless, our results suggest that jet launching typ-
ically occurs within the first few seconds after collapse.
5.3. Uncertainties
There are still several unanswered questions arising
from our simulations.
One is how the initial toroidal
field is amplified to the point of dynamical importance.
We argued above that the early growth is largely gov-
erned by toroidal-flux-freezing (see also H. Sawai et al.


## Page 15


A 3D Dynamo Origin of Long Gamma-Ray Burst Jets
15
2005).
It may seem surprising that model rc 36 B w,
which begins with a weaker magnetic field, attains a
toroidal-field strength and plasma-beta evolution com-
parable to the stronger-field models. However, we find
that the weak-field model possesses only a factor of
two less total toroidal flux compared to the strong-field
model, further supporting the conclusion that the ini-
tial amplification is set primarily by flux-freezing, i.e.,
the collapsing gas is compressing a similar amount of
toroidal flux down to ∼100 GM/c2.
At first glance, this might suggest that the initial to-
tal toroidal flux available at large radii alone determines
whether the collapsar dynamo can generate large-scale
poloidal loops capable of launching jets. In this picture,
progenitors with insufficient toroidal flux (i.e., those
near the lower bound of Figure 1 (d) or rc 36 B vw)
could fail to produce an LGRB. However, It remains
uncertain whether the ability of the MRI dynamo to
generate large-scale poloidal loops depends primarily on
the initial βˆϕ or on numerical resolution. Although P. E.
Rodman & C. S. Reynolds (2024) showed in their weak-
field (b200) model that the dynamo’s efficacy is largely
independent of resolution, their high-resolution weak-
field run only achieved a toroidal quality factor of ∼50.
In contrast, M. Liska et al. (2020) demonstrated that the
dynamo’s ability to produce large-scale poloidal fields
disappears when the resolution is reduced.
To verify
this, one should perform higher-resolution simulations
of the weak-field case (e.g., model rc 36 B vw).
Moreover, differential rotation inherited from the
angular-momentum profile can twist the poloidal mag-
netic field (which we neglect here) into a dynamically
important toroidal component during stellar core col-
lapse (T. Takiwaki et al. 2004; S. Yamada & H. Sawai
2004; H. Sawai et al. 2005; M. Obergaulinger et al. 2006;
S. G. Moiseenko et al. 2006). The poloidal field can also
be further amplified through turbulence (S. Sur et al.
2012; M. Obergaulinger et al. 2014; S. Higashi et al.
2024; A. Brandenburg & E. Ntormousi 2025) induced
by the standing shocks (E. Endeve et al. 2010, 2012;
M. Obergaulinger et al. 2014). However, an uncertainty
arises because the progenitor’s poloidal field is shown to
be small-scale (O. Gottlieb et al. 2024). Thus, whether
the poloidal field is coherent on scales comparable to
the MRI wavelength, and therefore capable of generat-
ing toroidal fields of similar scale required for MRI ac-
tivation (P. S. Santhiya et al. 2025), remains uncertain.
If the poloidal field loops in the progenitor star are suf-
ficiently strong, they may prevent differential rotation
from winding up and amplifying the toroidal field. In
that case, the initial magnetic-field topology is better
described as mixed toroidal-poloidal or purely poloidal.
But note that a poloidal field strong enough to inhibit ef-
ficient TSD must also transport angular momentum out-
ward, which would likely suppress disk formation. Ad-
ditionally, the description of angular momentum trans-
port in the progenitor star is not well understood, and
several alternatives (J. Fuller & L. Ma 2019b; J. Fuller
et al. 2019; V. A. Skoutnev & A. M. Beloborodov 2025)
exist that could impact the resulting magnetic field.
Two additional points are worth noting: (i) radia-
tive cooling changes the generation of poloidal magnetic
fields required for jet launching via an MHD dynamo (J.
Jacquemin-Ide 2025). In collapsars, neutrinos are the
primary cooling channel, which we neglect here, leav-
ing it uncertain whether successful jet launching would
occur in a more realistic collapsar environment, and (ii)
model rc 36 B w exhibits only a brief MAD state, which
is subsequently destroyed by the inward advection of a
strong magnetic-field loop of opposite polarity. Whether
such behavior is imprinted in the LGRB light curve re-
mains an open question. We will address these questions
in future work.

Consistent with J. D. Hogg
& C. S. Reynolds (2018) and J. Jacquemin-Ide (2025),
the collapsar disk, being geometrically thick due to the
absence of cooling, produces a highly chaotic butter-
fly pattern. A dominant sign of Bˆr and Bˆϕ emerges at
∼1 s, coinciding with the moment when a poloidal mag-
netic loop emerges and is subsequently advected onto the
BH, driving the system into the MAD state. After this
point, Bˆr and Bˆϕ maintain the same sign along direc-
tions away from the midplane, although we still observe
oscillatory behavior near the midplane, albeit without
a well-organized structure. Note that we find Bˆθ to be
weaker than the other two components, as expected for
a MAD disk dominated by vertical and toroidal fields
(the latter induced by the BZ process).
