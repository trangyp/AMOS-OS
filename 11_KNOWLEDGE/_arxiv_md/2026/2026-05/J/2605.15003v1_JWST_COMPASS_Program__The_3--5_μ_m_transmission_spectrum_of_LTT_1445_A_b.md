ABSTRACT
The search for an atmosphere on the closest rocky M dwarf planet, LTT 1445A b, has been the subject
of intense investigation from both the ground and space. Here, we present the first JWST transmission
spectrum of LTT 1445A b using a single visit spanning 3-5 µm using NIRSpec/G395H. We conduct
two independent reductions of the data using both the Eureka! and ExoTiC-JEDI pipelines. Overall,
we measure the NRS1 transit depths to a median precision of ∼23 ppm in 41 spectroscopic channels
with uniform widths of 30 pixels (∼0.02 µm), and the NRS2 transit depths to ∼36 ppm precision in 65
spectroscopic channels, also with uniform widths of 30 pixels. We rule out any statistically significant
spectral features at this precision and place limits on atmospheric metallicity using a grid of chemical
equilibrium models with grey opaque clouds. Using NIRSpec/G395H alone, we can place limits on the
atmospheric metallicity of ≳350 × Solar when the opaque pressure level is greater than 0.01 bars. We
also conduct a combined analysis of JWST/NIRSpec and HST/WFC3 transmission data and find our
atmospheric limits can be extended ≳500 × Solar when considering both datasets. Future analyses
both in transit and emission will uncover whether there are detectable atmospheric features.
1. INTRODUCTION
Rocky planets, defined here as ≲1.8 R⊕, which orbit
M dwarfs have been a key area of focus for atmospheric
reconnaissance with both the Hubble Space Telescope
(HST) and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).
M dwarfs are approximately three times more common
in our stellar neighborhood than solar-type stars (Henry
& Jao 2024), and their smaller size allows us to push at-
mospheric observations towards smaller and cooler rocky
worlds, some of which could reside in the habitable zone

of their host star (Kopparapu 2013). Both JWST and
HST have devoted several hundred hours of observa-
tional time targeting these worlds. However, thus far
it has been difficult to determine whether or not rocky
planets orbiting M dwarfs have observable atmospheres.
With HST, ten planets across four M dwarf systems
have been targeted with WFC3/G141 (1.1-1.65 µm)
transmission spectroscopy: TRAPPIST-1 b, c, d, e, f,
g, and h, (de Wit et al. 2016; Moran et al. 2018; Wake-
ford et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2018; Garcia et al. 2022;
Gressier et al. 2022) L98-59 b (Damiano et al. 2022; Bar-

and most recently, LTT 1445A b (Bennett et al. 2025a).

TOI-1685 b (Luque et al. 2025; Fisher et al. 2026); TOI-

Transmission spectroscopy has not been the only av-
enue for exploration with JWST. Other small planets
that orbit M stars have also been explored with emis-
sion spectroscopy and/or photometry. Though not an
exhaustive list, some have been: GJ 367 b (Zhang et al.
2024), TRAPPIST-1 b and c (Zieba et al. 2023; Greene
et al. 2023; Gillon et al. 2025), LHS 1140 b and c (For-
tune et al. 2025; Damiano et al. 2024), and GJ 486 b
(Weiner Mansfield et al. 2024).
So far these investi-
gations involving rocky planets around M dwarfs show
emission spectra that are broadly consistent with a bare
rock.
The LTT 1445 stellar system, a hierarchical triple of
mid-to-late M dwarfs (Teff=3562 K for LTT 1445 A),
is of particular interest because it is only 6.9 pc away,
making it the nearest known M dwarf system with tran-
siting rocky planets.
LTT 1445A b , first reported in
Winters et al. (2019), has a radius of 1.304±0.063 R⊕, a
mass of 2.87±0.25 M⊕, and an equilibrium temperature
T(Ab=0)=424 K. A few years later, its transiting sib-
ling LTT 1445A c, (R⊕(min) = 1.15, M⊕=1.54±0.20,
T(Ab=0)=508 K) was discovered by Winters et al.
(2022). Planet parameters for this system have also been
refined in Pass et al. (2023). The combination of LTT
1445A b’s escape velocity, bolometric stellar flux, and
host star luminosity makes it amenable to retaining an
atmosphere based on the purported “cosmic shoreline”
(Zahnle & Catling 2017; Berta-Thompson et al. 2025),
the theoretical line delineating planets that could versus
could not have atmospheres.
LTT 1445A b was previously observed via low-
resolution 0.6–1 µm transmission spectroscopy with
Magellan II/LDSS3C from the ground (Diamond-Lowe
et al. 2023) and from space with HST (Bennett
et al. 2025a). These observations confidently rule out
≤100×solar metallicity atmospheres. LTT 1445A b has
also been studied in emission with MIRI/LRS spec-
troscopy (5–12µm); Wachiraphan et al. (2025) inferred
from those observations that although the planet likely
lacks a thick CO2 atmosphere, it could potentially have
a thin atmosphere similar to Mars, Titan or Earth. Sec-
ondary eclipse photometry at 15µm using MIRI will be
taken via the Rocky Worlds Directors Discretionary Pro-
gram, which deemed LTT 1445A b a high community in-
terest target. Taken as a whole, LTT 1445A b is one of
the most well-studied rocky planets orbiting an M-dwarf
to date and offers us a unique opportunity to address
overarching questions surrounding the ability of rocky
M-dwarf planets to retain atmospheres. However, it is
clear from these previous investigations that to fully un-
derstand the nature of rocky M dwarf worlds, multiple
follow-up observations are needed to refine atmospheric
spectral precision, expand wavelength coverage, and as-
sess multiple planetary viewing geometries across both
transmission and emission. This is especially true for
targets that are of high community interest, such as LTT
1445A b.
To that end, here we present the first transmission
spectrum of LTT 1445A b taken with JWST via the
COMPASS (Compositions of Mini-Planet Atmospheres
for Statistical Study) Program (PID # 2512, Batalha
et al. 2021). Broadly speaking, COMPASS has an over-
arching goal of obtaining NIRSpec/G395H transmission
spectra of eleven 1-3 R⊕planets. The main driver of
the program is to kickstart observations of small planets
and build a link between atmospheric characterization
and planetary demographics of super-Earths and sub-
Neptunes.
We have taken both an individual, “deep-
dive” approach for each planet (Wallack et al. 2024;
Alderson et al. 2024; Scarsdale et al. 2024; Alam et al.
2025; Adams Redai et al. 2025; Alderson et al. 2025,
Teske, Batalha et al. 2025, Meech et al. 2026; Wallack
et al. 2026, Gagnebin et al.
submitted), as well as a
broader population-level look at our sample (for the first
seven, Gordon et al. 2026) and how it compares to other
JWST-observed small planets. The investigation pub-
lished here on LTT 1445A b will round out the sample

2. OBSERVATIONS & DATA REDUCTION
2.1. NIRSpec/G395H
We observed a single transit of LTT 1445A b with
JWST/NIRSpec using the high-resolution (R ∼2700)
G395H grating, which provides spectroscopy between
2.87–5.14 µm across the NRS1 and NRS2 detectors (with
a ∼0.1 µm detector gap between 3.72–3.82 µm). The ob-
servations were taken with the NIRSpec Bright Object
Time Series (BOTS) mode using the SUB2048 subarray,
the F290LP filter, the S1600A1 slit, and the NRSRAPID
readout pattern. The 4.5 hr exposure (beginning at Sep
1, 2025 22:37:46 UT and ending at Sep 2, 2025 04:43:42
UT) consisted of 4468 integrations with 3 groups per in-
tegration, and was designed to be centered on the transit
event (0.5042 hrs) with sufficient out-of-transit baseline.
2.1.1. Eureka!
We reduce the observation first with the Eureka!
(Bell et al. 2022) pipeline in a similar manner as we
have done in previous COMPASS papers (e.g., Teske,
Batalha et al. 2025). Eureka! is an end-to-end pipeline
for the reduction of JWST and HST data, acting as
a wrapper for the jwst pipeline and employing addi-
tional reduction steps. Overall, we utilize Eureka! ver-
sion 0.13 and jwst version 1.18.0 with context map
jwst 1364.pmap. For Stages 1 and 2 we primarily use
the default Eureka! reduction steps (with the exception
of a 15σ jump-detection threshold), which in addition
to the default jwst steps includes a group-level back-
ground subtraction to account for the 1/f noise noise
in the observation by removing the median of each col-
umn after masking the trace. In Stage 3, we optimize
the extraction aperture, background aperture, polyno-
mial order for the background subtraction, and sigma
threshold for the outlier rejection of the optimal extrac-
tion, selecting the version of the reduction that mini-
mizes the median absolute deviation of each white light
curve (generated by summing over 2.863–3.714 microns
for NRS1 and 3.820–5.082 microns for NRS2). We con-
sider extraction aperture half-widths of 4-8 pixels, back-
ground aperture half-widths of 8-11 pixels, either a full
frame or additional column-by-column background sub-
traction, and a sigma threshold of 10 or 60 for the outlier
rejection during the optimal extraction to allow for an
approximate standard extraction. The result of this op-
timization is as follows: extraction aperture half-widths
of 5 pixels and 8 pixel backgrounds (as measured from
the center of the trace to the edge of the detector) are
preferred for both NRS1 and NRS2 respectively, while
an additional full frame background subtraction and a
sigma threshold of 10 is preferred for NRS1, and an ad-
ditional column-by-column background subtraction and
a sigma threshold of 60 is preferred for NRS2. We then
generate 30 pixel spectroscopic binned light curves from
these optimal white light curves.
We then implement a custom light curve fitting code
to fit both the white light curves and spectroscopic light
curves from Eureka! as we have done in previous COM-
PASS papers. We first iteratively trim 3σ outliers from
a 50 point rolling median three times and trim the ini-
tial 200 points (∼12 minutes) from the light curves to
remove any initial ramps in the observation. We then
fit the white light curve with a combination of an as-
trophysical model using batman (Kreidberg 2015) with
transit depth (Rp/R∗), time of transit (T0), inclination
(i), and ratio of the semi-major axis to the stellar ra-
dius (a/R∗) as free parameters, and a systematic model
of the form
S = p1 + p2 × T + p3 × X + p4 × Y,
(1)
where pN are free parameters, T is the array of times,
and X and Y are arrays of the positions of the trace
to be consistent with the simplest noise model that we
have utilized in previous COMPASS papers that have
used Eureka! (e.g., Wallack et al. 2026). We also fit for
an additional per-point error added in quadrature to the
measurement errors. We fix the quadratic limb darken-
ing to the theoretical values from Set One of the MPS-
ATLAS models using the stellar parameters from Win-
ters et al. (2019) (Teff= 3337 K, [Fe/H]= -0.340, log(g)=
4.967) with ExoTiC-LD (Grant & Wakeford 2024) and
assume a circular orbit.
Next, we first fit our white
light curves using a Levenberg-Marquardt minimization
where we minimize the log likelihood. To these best-
fit parameters we then initialize 3× the number of free
parameters as walkers for a Markov chain Monte Carlo
(MCMC) fit using the package emcee (Foreman-Mackey
et al. 2013). We run 100,000 steps, discarding the first
50,000 steps as burn-in. We show our best fit parame-
ters from these white light curves in Table 1, the white

2.1.2. ExoTiC-JEDI
We use the ExoTiC-JEDI pipeline (Alderson et al.
2022) to carry out a second reduction following the
same setup as described in Gordon et al. (2026). We
use the standard jwst pipeline (version 1.18.1, context
map 1364; Bushouse et al. 2025) for the first stage with
the addition of the custom bias subtraction routine im-
plemented in Alderson et al. (2023) and a group-level
column-by-column background subtraction routine. We
then flag pixels with data quality bit values indicating
“do not use”, “saturated”, “dead”, “hot”, “low quan-
tum efficiency”, and “no gain value” and replace them
with the median of the neighboring four pixels.
Pix-
els exceeding 10σ in the spatial dimension and 6σ in the
time dimension are flagged as outliers and replaced with
the median of the nearest 10 pixels. The spectrum is ex-
tracted using the intrapixel aperture extraction routine
implemented in ExoTiC-JEDI with an aperture of 7 pixel
FWHM, which is the value that minimizes the median
absolute deviation of the resulting white lightcurve.
After extracting the spectral timeseries, the white
lightcurves for each detector were independently fitted
with a custom light curve fitting code. First, we apply a
Gaussian filter with a standard deviation of 50 points to
the white lightcurve. We then trim flux measurements
that deviate more than 4σ from the filtered light curves,
which excludes 9 data points in NRS1 and 12 in NRS2.
Finally, we trim the first 200 integrations 12.1 minutes of
observation to avoid any effects of instrument settling.
We model the light curve as the product of a transit
model computed using batman (Kreidberg 2015) and a
systematics model of the form
S = p1 + p2 × T
(2)
where p1 and p2 are free parameters and T is the array
of times. Note that we omit the x- and y-positions of
the trace used in the systematics model for the Eureka!
reduction, as they did not appear to correlate strongly
with the red noise in the light curves based on a visual
inspection.
The transit parameters we fit for are the
planet-star radius ratio (Rp/R∗), the semimajor axis in
units of stellar radii (a/R∗), the time of transit (T0),
the inclination (i). As for the Eureka! reduction, we
assume a circular orbit. We use ExoTiC-LD (Grant &
Wakeford 2022) to compute priors for the quadratic
limb-darkening parameters using the values from Set
One of the MPS-ATLAS models with the stellar param-
eters from Winters et al. (2019). Finally, we include an
additional error term that is added in quadrature to the
errors output by the ExoTiC-JEDI pipeline.
To infer the parameters of our model we initialize 22
walkers (twice the number of model parameters) and
run them for 100,000 steps each using emcee(Foreman-
Mackey et al. 2013), discarding the first 50,000 steps as
burn-in. The best fit parameters from this white light
curve fitting procedure are given in Table 1, with the
white light curves themselves and best fit models in Fig-
ure 1 and the RMS vs. bin size plots for the light curves
minus the model shown in Figure 2.
Following white light curve fitting we bin the spec-
tral timeseries into 30 pixel spectroscopic bins with the
same binning scheme as was used for the Eureka! re-
duction. We fit these binned light curves with the same
systematics and transit models as for the white light
curves. We fix all transit parameters except the limb-
darkening parameters and Rp/R∗to the best-fit values
from the white light curves for each respective detec-
tor.
For the limb-darkening parameters we again use
ExoTiC-LD with the same stellar parameters and MPS-
ATLAS models to compute priors for each wavelength
bin. During the fitting procedure for each spectral light
curve the two parameters of the systematics model are
allowed to vary, as is the additional noise term. We ini-
tialize 12 MCMC chains for each light curve and run
10,000 steps of MCMC, discarding the first 5,000 steps
as burn-in. Figure 3 shows the resulting transmission
spectrum.

3. INTERPRETATION OF PLANET’S
TRANSMISSION SPECTRUM
Our strategy to interpret the planet’s transmission
spectrum is two-fold and based on the other published
works in COMPASS. First, we apply a purely paramet-
ric, non-physical approach to assess the statistical signif-
icance of any structure in our spectrum. By structure,
we mean slopes, offsets, or any Gaussian-like features
that could resemble astrophysical signals. These tests
are separately applied to both data reductions, allowing
us to determine any differences between them. Second,
we apply a more physically informed set of models as-


## Page 7


7
suming either chemical equilibrium or a simple three-gas
component (e.g., H2/He background with CO2). These
physically informed models allow us to test the bounds
of atmospheric metallicities and mean molecular weights
that can be statistically ruled out by the data. More de-
tails on our methods as well as the results are outlined
below.
3.1. Spectral Feature Detection
Our spectral feature detection technique is described
in many other COMPASS works (Wallack et al. e.g.,
2024; Alderson et al. e.g., 2024).
Briefly, we use the
MultiNest fitting code, Ultranest (Buchner 2021) to fit
a set of five model types, ranging in parameter complex-
ity: 1) a zero-slope line (1 parameter), 2) a step function
to represent two zero-sloped lines with an offset between
NRS1 and NRS2 (2 parameter), 3) a sloped-line (2 pa-
rameter), 4) a Gaussian-feature constrained to be within
the NRS1 wavelength range, plus an NRS1/NRS2 offset
(5 parameter), and 5) same as #4 but in NRS2.
The results of this analysis are shown in Table 2 and
corresponding Figure 5. Neither data reduction prefers
a zero sloped (flat) line. Eureka! strongly prefers (∆lnZ
= 27) an offset between NRS1 and NRS2. The Eureka!
data reduction results in an offset of −43±5 ppm (NRS2
baseline below NRS1), and the ExoTiC-JEDI data reduc-
tion results in an offset of −15 ± 6 ppm. The Eureka!
NRS1 and NRS2 baselines are 2016.8 ± 3.5 ppm and
1973 ± 4 ppm, respectively, and the ExoTiC-JEDI NRS1
and NRS2 baselines are 1969 ± 4 ppm and 1954 ±
4 ppm, respectively.
None of the other models we tested were a bet-
ter match to the data.
However, both Eureka! and
ExoTiC-JEDI Free Gaussian (NRS1) model tests in-
dicate some structure (weak preference ∆ln ≥1) at
∼3.1 µm.
We mention this because weak structure
around 3 µm has been seen in other COMPASS datasets;
it was most prominently discussed in TOI-260 b (Meech
et al. 2026) but also appeared in TOI-836 c (Wallack
et al. 2024). However, Gordon et al. (2026) found that
there was no common spectral structure (being of either
astrophysical or systematic in nature) between the first
seven COMPASS datasets.
3.2. Physical Atmospheric Modeling Methodology
We take two different physical modeling approaches
to analyzing the LTT 1445A b spectra.
The first is
based on pure chemical equilibrium, in which we pa-

the assumption of chemical equilibrium. Below, we fur-

results of each.

4. DISCUSSION
4.1. Are aerosols likely present at pressures less than
10−2 bar on LTT 1445A b?
Our reported metallicity limits of > 500 × Solar
become dependent on the opaque pressure level when
this level becomes lower than 0.01 bar (see Figure 6b).
Therefore, it is worth considering whether an opaque
pressure level < 0.01 bar is plausible. Full scale cloud
modeling of this system is beyond the scope of this anal-
ysis but we can hypothesize potential aerosols that could
exist on LTT 1445A b given its equilibrium temperature.
Our parametrized pressure-temperature profile for LTT
1445A b gives it a temperature of 365 K in the upper
atmosphere (p < 0.01 bar), and a temperature of 700 K
at depth (p=200 bar). These values give us an approxi-
mate temperature range in which to hypothesize possi-
ble aerosol species.
The most straightforward species to consider are con-
densible species, which we determine based on the open
source atmospheric cloud code Virga (Batalha et al.
2026). Of the canonical condensibles species available
in Virga, the two that have condensation temperatures
closest to that of LTT 1445A b’s atmosphere are H2O
and KCl. Condensation temperatures are generally de-
pendent on metallicity and mean molecular weight, with
higher metallicities raising the point of condensation.
For example, in a 1 × Solar metallicity atmosphere, the
condensation temperature of H2O is 210 K at 0.01 bar.
In a 500 × Solar metallicity atmosphere, the condensa-
tion temperature of H2O rises to 270 K at 0.01 bar.
However, this is still well-below the temperature ex-
pected at that pressure for LTT 1445A b (Batalha et al.
2026). Therefore H2O is unlikely to be in a condensed
form. The condensation temperature of KCl at 1 × So-
lar metallicity is 889 K at 0.01 bar. In order for KCl to
condense on LTT 1445A b it would need temperatures
to cross this threshold.
However, the temperature of
LTT 1445A b’s atmosphere at these pressures is likely
too cold and would not cross the threshold for condensa-
tion of KCl to occur either. Other more exotic aerosols
could also be considered as viable aerosols.
Photochemically produced hydrocarbon organic hazes
could also be an avenue to create an effective opaque
pressure level at lower pressures. The most likely path-
way to create hydrocarbon haze requires CH4 (Trainer
et al. 2006). However, abundant H-bearing species like
CH4 are unlikely to be stable over geologic time on warm
and small rocky planets like LTT 1445A b because of
rampant hydrogen escape to space (e.g., Adams Redai
et al. 2025). Self-consistent simulations of rocky plan-
ets orbiting M stars (e.g., Krissansen-Totton et al. 2024)
predict that billions of years of escape should most of-
ten yield hydrogen poor atmospheres rich in CO2 or O2.
On such oxidizing atmospheres, significant CH4 would
not be thermodynamically stable, and thus hydrocar-
bon hazes from methane photochemistry seem improba-
ble. Additionally, hazes derived from CO or CO2 photo-
chemistry would be much less abundant and unlikely to
build up to opaque layers high in the atmosphere (H¨orst
et al. 2018; Moran et al. 2022).

0.01 would be super-saturated and would condense to
form clouds (Dai et al. 2022).
Determining whether
such concentrations are achievable on LTT 1445A b
would require photochemical simulations that are be-
yond the scope of this study.
If the atmosphere was
instead relatively reducing in its bulk content, S8 vapor
would be more likely than H2SO4 (e.g., H2-rich; Gao
et al. 2017). S8 saturation vapor pressure is very similar
to H2SO4 (Lyons 2008), so a similar ∼1% concentra-
tion at 0.01 bar would be necessary for S8 to condense.
Again, whether such a concentration is attainable on
LTT 1445A b could be investigated by future studies
that employ photochemical models.
Overall, there are several plausible pathways that
could lead to aerosol formation in LTT 1445A b’s at-
mosphere at lower pressures.
However, each presents
a unique challenge.
Increasing the signal-to-noise of
LTT 1445A b observations combined with more de-
tailed cloud and photochemical modeling would enable a
clearer determination of whether or not these processes
are in fact viable.
4.2. Combining HST and JWST Data
We use our chemical equilibrium grid fitting method
from §3.2 to understand how and if adding the HST
data from Bennett et al. (2025a) aids in our derived
metallicity constraints. Introducing the HST data adds
another free parameter as there is an inherent offset be-
tween HST, NRS1, and NRS2. We show how the HST
data compare to the Eureka! reduction presented here
in Figure 9a. Overall there is an offset between the HST
data and the NRS1/NRS2 data and the HST data has
generally lower SNR compared to the data presented
here.
In §3.2 we show that offset method #2 (freely fitting
each individual spectrum section to the data rather than
pre-correcting the data for all offsets) is more robust to
differing data baselines. Therefore we opt to use that
method for this joint HST and JWST analysis. When
applying this method #2 and fitting each section of the
spectra individually, we can get an estimate for how the
addition of HST improves our atmospheric constraints,
shown in Figure 9b. With HST only we report a 3σ limit
of 120×Solar (nearly identical to the 100×Solar limit re-
ported in Bennett et al. (2025a)), a reassuring check that
our modeling framework is reproducible. When we add
the Eureka! reduction we increase our 3σ JWST-only
limit from 380×Solar to 520×Solar. Overall, the broader
wavelength coverage from HST data tightens the over-
all limits we can place on LTT 1445A b’s atmosphere,
assuming chemical equilibrium.

4.3. Combining transmission with emission data from
JWST
Here, we show how our transmission-based constraints
on LTT 1445A b’s atmosphere compare to the emission
spectrum of the planet published by Wachiraphan et al.
(2025).
As described previously (Section 3), we interpret our
featureless transmission spectrum of LTT 1445A b by
ruling out certain atmospheric metallicities (as a proxy
for mean molecular weight) based on fully chemical
equilibrium atmospheres and opaque pressure level (as
a proxy for muting by aerosols, though it could also
be in reference to a surface pressure).
Therefore, to
compare our transmission-based results to the emission
spectrum, we analyze the emission observations using
the same metallicity-based parameterization for atmo-
spheric composition. We note that previous studies of
rocky planet emission spectra have taken a different ap-
proach: typically, eclipse data are compared to simu-
lated atmospheres with ranging mixes of simple compo-
sitions (e.g. 100% CO2, O2 or H2O, or some combina-
tion thereof) at various surface pressures (e.g., Wachi-
raphan et al. 2025). Some exoplanet analyses with both
emission and transmission data have also attempted to
place each other in context. For example, the JWST
transmission spectrum analysis of GJ 1132 b from Ben-
nett et al. (2025b) compared 2σ-limits on atmospheric
pressure and mean molecular weight obtained from the
emission analysis (Xue et al. 2024) to their own 2σ-limits
on the same parameters.
They also considered if the
constraints on atmospheric pressure in a pure H2O at-
mosphere from transmission were physically plausible
considering the nightside temperature derived from the
emission results. Such approaches are valid, and per-
haps even preferable in some cases, but we do not adopt
them here as we aim to maintain a consistent, directly
comparable framework between our emission and trans-
mission analyses.
To interpret the JWST emission spectrum of LTT
1445A b we use the climate model and equilibrium chem-
istry solver in the Photochem software package (Wogan
et al. 2025) to simulate atmospheres at radiative-
convective and chemical equilibrium for various metal-
licities and surface pressures. We use the Koll (2022)
parameterization to capture the effects of day-to-night
heat redistribution. We use this code to compute a grid
of atmospheres for surface albedos of 0 and 0.2, metal-
licities between 100 and 1000× Solar, and pressure from
100 to 10−4 bar.
For each calculation, we perform a
χ2-test between the simulated emission spectrum and
the 16-bin MIRI LRS spectrum from Wachiraphan et al.
(2025), following the grid fitting procedure described in
Section 3.2. Note this is not meant to be an exhaustive
modeling analysis of the emission data.
Figure 10 shows results for a surface albedo of 0 (cen-
ter panel) and 0.2 (right panel). For a surface albedo
of 0, the MIRI LRS data is consistent with atmospheres
at ≳200 × Solar metallicity and ≲10−4 bar or atmo-
spheres between 100 and 200 × Solar metallicity and
≲10−2 bar. For a surface albedo of 0.2, the LRS ob-
servations permit any modeled metallicity for surface
pressures ≲10−2 bar. For comparison, we found in Sec-
tion 4.2 that our combined JWST+HST transmission
spectrum is compatible with atmospheres ≳500 × So-
lar metallicity for opaque pressure level ≲10−2 bar.
It is important to note that this constraint on surface
pressure is only valid for atmospheres based on metallic-
ity at chemical equilibrium. For example, a pure 0.1 bar
O2 atmosphere (zero surface albedo) is consistent with
the emission observation to within 3σ (Figure 10, left).
Further modeling work must be done to consider best
strategies to extract information from combined emis-
sion and transmission spectra as the community builds
a set of rocky planets with both (e.g., LTT 1445A b, GJ
1132 b, GJ 486b).

5. SUMMARY & CONCLUSIONS
Here we presented the first JWST transmission spec-
trum of the rocky planet, LTT 1445A b. It is of par-
ticular interest because: 1) it belongs to the nearest
known M-dwarf system with rocky transiting planets,
and 2) its approximate atmospheric escape velocity,
bolometric stellar flux, and host star luminosity make
it amenable to retaining an atmosphere, when consider-
ing the purported “cosmic shoreline” (Berta-Thompson
et al. 2025). LTT 1445A b has already been the subject
of both ground- and space-based campaigns and has a
long list of future JWST observations that will continue
to try and characterize its atmosphere.
For our JWST transmission spectrum we utilize NIR-
Spec/G395H, which is dispersed across the NRS1 and
NRS2 detectors.
In NRS1, using uniform widths of
30 pixels that produce 41 spectroscopic channels, we
achieve a spectral precision of 22.3 ppm and 23.6 ppm
for our two independent Eureka! and ExoTiC-JEDI re-
ductions. We achieve 36.5 and 36.7 ppm precision, re-
spectively, for 65 similar width spectroscopic channels in
NRS2. For comparison, PandExo’s median precision for
the equivalent wavelength bins is 20 ppm and 32 ppm
for NRS1 and NRS2, respectively. This indicates that
our data closely match expectations (see Figure 4).
Our reduced data show no statistically significant fea-
tures besides a detector offset between NRS1 and NRS2.
Specifically, we test and rule out any sloped structure or
Gaussian-like features that could be indicative of molec-
ular absorption, aerosol opacity, or even stellar activity.
Both the Eureka! and ExoTiC-JEDI reductions produce
offsets between NRS1 and NRS2.
Given our featureless spectra, we conduct a series of
atmospheric modeling investigations to determine what
scenarios can be statistically ruled out. Specifically, we:
1) create a grid of chemical equilibrium-based models
as a function of metallicity and opaque pressure level to


## Page 16


16
understand what limits can be placed on these param-
eters, and 2) run retrievals on simple three-gas compo-
nent models (e.g., h2/He background with either H2O,
CH4, or CO2 to place approximate limits on the mean
molecular weights that could be ruled out. Our chem-
ical equilibrium models assume a 5-parameter parame-
terized pressure-temperature profile, grey optical thick
clouds. Our three-gas component models make the same
assumptions and also use constant-with-altitude abun-
dances. Overall we find that we can rule out atmospheric
metallicities greater than 350 × Solar, when the opaque
pressure level is at pressures higher than 0.01 bar. This
number varies slightly depending on the data reduction
method used (360 × Solar for ExoTiC-JEDI and 380
× Solar for Eureka!). Our results are not sensitive to
carbon-to-oxygen ratio but do vary strongly when con-
sidering lower opaque pressure levels. We also find sen-
sitivity to atmospheric metallicity limits when we con-
sider different methods for removing the effect of the
NRS1/NRS2 offset.
Next, we use the simple three-gas mixture model com-
posed of either H2O, CO2, or CH4, each with back-
grounds of H2+He, to determine approximate ranges in
mean molecular weight limits. We find that we can rule
out approximately < 10% abundance levels of H2O and
CO2. For H2O < 10% can only be ruled out for opaque
pressure levels > 10−2. For CH4, we can rule out up
to 100% abundance levels except for cases where the
opaque pressure level is below 10−4 bar. Considering
these pressure limits as well this corresponds to mean
molecular weight limits of about <7, <4, and <16 g/mol
for CO2, H2O, and CH4, respectively.
Lastly, we look at how our data fits into the context of
current and future data sets. When using the Eureka!
data in combination with HST data from Bennett et al.
(2025a), we find that we are able to rule out metallici-
ties greater than 520×Solar, which is an increase from
380×Solar ( Eureka! reduction alone).
With the ad-
dition of the JWST emission spectrum, we find that
when considering pure chemical equilibrium scenarios,
surface pressures greater than 10−2 bar can also be ruled
out for metallicities greater than 500×Solar. However,
this analysis must be expanded to include broader at-
mospheric scenarios in order to truly understand the
limits we can place on atmospheric pressure. Lastly, we
also consider a simulated spectrum of all the allocated
JWST transits that will be available for LTT 1445A b.
In total this includes 2 visits of NIRSS/SOSS, 2 visits
of NIRSpec/G395H, 4 visits of NIRCam/F322W2, and 4
visits of NIRCam/F444W from JWST Programs #2512,
#7073, and #7251. We find that CO2 dominant atmo-
spheres nearing 100% will be the most challenging case
to detect and could still result in non-detections. How-
ever, many other scenarios could be detectable including
pure H2O atmospheres and metal-rich atmospheres up
to 1000 × Solar. We look forward to future observations
of LTT 1445A b that could provide clues to the nature
of this closest, M-dwarf hosted rocky world.

metallicity limits can all be found in the Zenodo posting:
Batalha et al. (2026).
