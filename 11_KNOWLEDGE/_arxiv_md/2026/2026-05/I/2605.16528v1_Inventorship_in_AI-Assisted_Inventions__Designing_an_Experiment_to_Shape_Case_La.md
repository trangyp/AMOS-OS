Inventorship in AI-Assisted Inventions: Designing an Experiment to Shape Case Law 
 
Yevhenii Shchetynin [Corresponding Author], University of Turin, Turin, Italy, evgeniy.schetinin@gmail.com 
Duygu Usta, University of Turin, Turin, Italy, av.duyguusta@gmail.com 
Bryan Khan, University of Turin, Turin, Italy, bkhan.mail@gmail.com 
Abstract 
The latest improvements in artificial intelligence (AI) raise new challenges for intellectual property laws, 
particularly concerning the inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions — that is, those in which AI is used in the 
inventive process. While most jurisdictions allow only a natural person to be considered the inventor, the question of how 
to deal with AI-assisted inventions remains relevant. Namely, what is the nature and contribution of AI tools in an AI-
assisted invention that would prevent a human from being recognized as its inventor? The main challenge in addressing 
this question is the lack of case law on the issue. 
It is reasonable to assume that with the development of AI and the growing interest in its use in the inventive 
process, new cases will naturally arise, which in turn will harmonize and address the inventorship issue in AI-assisted 
inventions to some extent. However, this process will take significant time and may not keep pace with the rapid 
development of AI, nor fully address the new problems that arise alongside AI advancements. 
This research proposes the conditions of an experiment to create relevant case law. This experiment could be 
initiated by society, involving stakeholders specializing in AI. The article also proposes a methodology for conducting 
the experiment and selecting cases that best reflect the current state of AI use in the inventive process. Conducting such 
an approach will help identify the most effective methods for measuring human contribution to AI-assisted inventions 
when determining inventorship. 
Keywords 
AI-assisted inventions, artificial intelligence, human contribution, inventive step, inventorship, patentability.

1. 
Introduction 
Most artificial Intelligence (AI) systems use machine learning (ML) methods, such as artificial neural networks, 
k-means method, genetic algorithms, and generative AI, including large language models (LLMs). The main tasks AI 
aims to solve include classification, clustering, regression, anomaly detection, and others. Fundamental AI models have 
been under development since the 1940s. However, due to limitations in computing power and data storage, the accuracy 
of such models only began to approach human-level performance after the 2010s (Kiela, et al., 2021). From the early 
2020s, generative AI, including LLMs, started to develop significantly. These technologies enable the production of new 
content such as text, images, sounds, and more. Recent studies also identified that machine invention systems based on 
AI are even capable of assisting in creating inventions (Vasilescu & Filzmoser, 2021). More recent studies (Pipada, et al., 
2026) has shown rapid transformation and acceleration of drug discovery and development using AI technologies. In 
response to the growing use of AI, the U.S. Food & Drug Administration (FDA) has published guidelines addressing the 
use of AI and promoting good AI practices in drug development (FDA, 2025), (FDA, 2026). 
Therefore, these innovations and improvements in AI raise new challenges for intellectual property (IP) laws, 
particularly concerning the issue of inventorship in inventions where AI (or any other computer tool) is used in the 
inventive (or creative/invention) process (hereinafter referred to as AI-assisted inventions). Thus, the inventorship issue 
in AI-assisted inventions can be formulated as follows: under what conditions can AI be assumed to have contributed to 
the invention, and if so, what is the nature (and contribution) of AI tools in an AI-assisted invention such that a human 
cannot be considered as its inventor? Or, in other words, what level of human contribution to an AI-assisted invention is 
considered necessary and sufficient to be recognized as its inventor? 
The relevance of this issue arises from numerous initiatives from patent offices in different countries aimed at 
developing guidance to address it. For example, the Japan Patent Office (JPO) recently pointed out AI is expected to play 
a broader role in the inventive processes, see (METI, 2024). Similar issues also arose in academic literature. Recently 
The AI Scientist (The AI Scientist, 2025a) system based on LLM models introduced probably the first fully AI-generated 
scientific paper that has passed the peer-review process on ICLR 2025 Workshop (The AI Scientist, 2025b). 
The inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions was also recently addressed by the United States Patent and 
Trademark Office (USPTO) in their “Inventorship Guidance for AI-Assisted Inventions” (USPTO, 2024a). The USPTO 
introduced five basic principles based on the Pannu factors (Pannu v. Iolab Corp., 1998) and provided two artificial 
examples (one is related to the use of a LLM model and another with the use in silico methods in drug discovery), see 
(USPTO, 2024b). These examples illustrate how the principles can be applied to measure human contribution. At the 
same time, they admitted that there is “no bright-line test” to determine human contribution to AI-assisted inventions, 
indicating that the question requires further development due to the lack of case law and practical application of the 
proposed principles. 
Although, in November 2025, the USPTO published revised inventorship guidance (USPTO, 2025a) rescinding 
previous guidance, they still highlight that only natural persons can be named as inventors, while AI is referred as a tool 
in hands of humans. The previous approach based on Pannu factors to AI-assisted inventions was withdrawn with 
arguments that those factors only apply for joint inventorship between natural persons and are inapplicable to AI. 
Regarding AI-assisted inventions USPTO referred to the case law (Shatterproof Glass Corp. v. Libby-Owens Ford Co., 
1985) and which (Hess v. Advanced Cardiovascular Sys., 1997) which establishes that inventors may “use the services, 
ideas, and aid of others” with conclusion that the same principle should apply to AI systems. 
However, such strong interest from patent offices together with rapid changes in legislation, as well as the pace of 
AI development, which may lead to another revision of laws or guidelines, only underscore the relevance of the topic 
arises in this study: under what conditions can AI be assumed to have contributed to an invention, and if so, what level of 
human contribution to an AI-assisted invention is considered necessary and sufficient to be recognized as its inventor, 
and how might examination procedures be adapted? 
Since most jurisdictions currently allow only natural persons to be considered inventors, answering the question 
posed will help applicants determine in advance which inventions are worth patenting and which might be better protected 
by other methods (such as trade secrets, publication etc.), at the same time reducing the workload of the patent offices. 
On the other hand, if AI were to be recognized as an inventor in the possible future, as proposed by some authors 
(Mohamed, Rahma, & Hemairy, 2025), addressing this question would help us adapt more quickly to these new realities. 
One of the problems with addressing this question is the lack of case law on the issue, see (Nemec & Rann, 2023). 
Some of the well-known cases are the so-called DABUS cases. These involve a food container created by DABUS, an 
AI tool developed by Stephen L. Thaler (as part of his “Imagination Engines Incorporated” project (Thaler S. , 2023)), 
who listed DABUS as the sole inventor and applied for patents in several jurisdictions. Most jurisdictions rejected the 
patent applications on the grounds that only a natural person can be indicated as an inventor (e.g., in the United States of 
America and China). It should be noted that most courts did not address the question of joint inventorship (also called co-
inventorship) between humans and AI tools, nor whether an invention should be granted if Thaler were to amend the 
application to list himself as the inventor.

Moreover, there already exist AI-systems which help patent attorneys to autonomously answer for office actions

Therefore, this research discusses the conditions under which a bold new experiment could be implemented to

(which are not technically “cases”)). Additionally, a methodology is proposed for conducting this experiment and 
selecting cases that best reflect the current state of AI use in the inventive process. This experiment could be initiated by

for assessing human contribution to AI-assisted inventions in determining inventorship. Engaging diverse participants 
would also make it possible to compare whether the perceptions of law and patent offices align with those of industry and 
other stakeholders. 
2. 
State of the art 
2.1. 
Traditional Notion of Inventorship in Patents 
Inventorship refers to the individual or individuals who have made significant and original contribution to the 
invention that is the subject of the patent. This may be a contribution to the conception of the idea or the subject matter 
of at least one claim included in the patent application. Inventorship provides the starting point for determining ownership 
of patent rights (Chisum, 1978). 
Despite ongoing discussions regarding the notion of inventorship, particularly with the rise of AI, the traditional 
patent system recognizes only natural persons as inventors, since inventorship requires a mental act of creativity exclusive 
to humans. This principle aligns with the historical foundations of intellectual property, which naturally emphasize the 
necessity of human element (Saw & Chan, 2023). Intellectual property is grounded in three main theories: the labour 
theory, which asserts that individuals should benefit from their productive labour (Hull, 2009); the personality theory, 
which views person’s creations as an expression, i.e. an extensions of an individual’s identity (Radin, 1982); and the 
utilitarian theory, which justifies rewards for contributions that benefit society (Mill, 2004). In accordance with these 
theories that are the basis of IP, mandating that inventorship be exclusively assigned to natural persons guarantees that 
the rights associated with patents are directly linked to the people whose creative efforts contribute to the invention. 
In most jurisdictions, there is no legal obligation for patent applicants to disclose the process by which their 
invention was created. Consequently, determining the true contributors to an invention can pose significant challenges. 
This lack of transparency in the patent application process not only challenges attributing inventorship but also may raise 
concerns regarding the integrity and fairness of the patent system. 
In most jurisdictions, the designation of inventors is declaratory in nature — the inventors are the individuals 
named in the patent application, unless proven otherwise. If an individual believes they deserve recognition as a co-
inventor but are not listed on the patent application, they may seek legal recourse through litigation or administrative 
review processes. This mechanism is intended to incentivize applicants to disclose all legitimate co-inventors correctly. 
A similar situation arises when the list of inventors includes individuals who have not made a creative contribution and 
should be removed from this list. An already issued patent may even be invalidated if the list of inventors turns out to be 
incorrect. However, this logic breaks down in the case of AI, which cannot assert legal rights or initiate claims, thereby 
undermining the effectiveness of administrative oversight. 
But at the same time, this gap in Patent Law creates legal uncertainties about inventorship issue for applicants. If 
applicants will not designate all human inventors, or if they designate human inventors who haven’t made any inventive 
contribution in an invention fully made by AI, in the future such an invention could be invalidated in court if someone (e.g. 
competitor) finds this discrepancy in the list of inventors.

2.2. 
Criteria for identifying and challenging inventorship 
The criteria for identifying and challenging inventorship vary significantly across jurisdictions, reflecting diverse 
legal traditions and policy considerations. This section examines the criteria and processes in three members of the IP51 
— USPTO, the European Patent Office (EPO, representing Europe), and the JPO — each with advanced patent systems, 
strong economies, and representing both common law and civil law traditions. These jurisdictions have already begun 
addressing inventorship issues arising from AI-assisted inventions — with varying degrees of progress — making them 
particularly relevant case studies. While most other countries operate under broadly similar patent law principles, the IP5 
examples provide a clear and influential benchmark for global practice. 
The United States of America (USA) 
Under 35 U.S.C. § 116 (USPTO, 2022), the United States Patent and Trademark Office sets forth the requirements 
for inventorship. According to this guidance, an inventor is defined as an individual or a group of individuals who 
contribute to the conception of the claimed invention. This means that inventorship is determined based on the patent 
claims rather than the specification or drawings. Each inventor must have made a contribution to at least one claim of the 
patent, and this contribution must be included in the final set of claims as granted. 
Challenges to inventorship in the U.S. can arise if it is discovered that an inventor listed did not contribute to the 
conception of the claimed invention or if an unlisted individual did. Such disputes often require legal proceedings to 
resolve, with substantial evidence needed to prove or disprove the contributions in question. The USPTO may require 
affidavits or other documentation to support claims of inventorship or to contest them. 
Europe 
The European Patent Office mandates that every patent application must designate the inventor, who must be a 
natural person. The EPO guidelines (EPO, 2024) specify that the EPO does not verify the accuracy of the information 
provided in the designation of the inventor but ensures that the designated inventor is a natural person, as stipulated in 
case law (J 0008/20 (Designation of inventor/DABUS), 2021). 
If the designation of the inventor is not filed, or if it cannot be considered valid due to deficiencies (such as missing 
name, country, place of residence, or applicant’s signature), the EPO will inform the applicant that the European patent 
application will be refused if the deficiency is not remedied within the prescribed period under Rule 60(1). This strict 
adherence to formal requirements ensures that the inventor’s designation is complete and accurate, preventing potential 
legal issues related to inventorship. 
Challenging inventorship at the EPO typically involves addressing these formal requirements. If there is a dispute 
over who should be named as an inventor, the challenge must be resolved through legal or administrative proceedings, 
with the EPO ensuring compliance with procedural rules rather than evaluating the substantive contribution of each 
inventor. 
Japan 
According to the guidelines provided by the Japan Patent Office, see (JPO, 2015), the criteria for recognizing 
inventorship are based on the contributions made to the invention as described in the specification and drawings. Unlike 
some other jurisdictions including the USA, Japan does not determine inventorship solely based on patent claims. This 
means that any individual who has contributed to the inventive concepts detailed in the specification and drawings must 
be listed as an inventor. This approach ensures that all contributors to the inventive process are duly acknowledged. 
Challenging inventorship in Japan can involve proving that the listed inventors did not actually contribute to the 
invention as described or, conversely, that someone not listed made a significant contribution. The JPO’s emphasis on 
the specification and drawings as the basis for inventorship highlights the importance of comprehensive and detailed 
documentation during the invention process. 
In conclusion, IP5 jurisdictions have different criteria for identifying and challenging inventorship, reflecting their 
distinct legal traditions and policy concerns. In the USA, the USPTO considers a person an inventor if they have 
contributed to at least one patent claim, with disputes often requiring legal action and substantial evidence. Europe, 
through the EPO, enforces strong formal standards for inventor designation, focusing on procedural compliance rather 
than substantive evaluation, and mandates that inventors must be natural persons. In contrast, the JPO in Japan bases 
inventorship on contributions detailed in the specification and drawings, ensuring all contributors are acknowledged.

2.3. 
Joint inventorship 
Joint inventorship occurs when two or more individuals collaboratively contribute to the conception of an 
invention. Unlike sole inventorship, where a single individual is responsible for the invention, joint inventorship 
recognizes the collaborative nature of the inventive process. This collaboration can involve a diverse array of 
contributions, each of which is essential to the final invention. 
In the United States, the criteria for joint inventorship are guided by Section 35 U.S.C. § 116, (USPTO, 2022) 
which stipulates that there is no need for inventors to work together physically or at the same time, nor do they need to 
contribute equally to the invention. Instead, each inventor must make a significant contribution to the conception of the 
invention as articulated in the patent claims. 
The determination of joint inventorship in the U.S. is further clarified by the (Pannu v. Iolab Corp., 1998) case, 
which established several key factors known as the Pannu factors, which ensures that joint inventorship is not merely a 
formality but a recognition of genuine collaborative effort that results in the creation of an invention. 
Issues related to joint inventorship are particularly pertinent in the context of AI-human contributions to inventions. 
Complex collaborations can arise in both scenarios, where it’s hard to pinpoint the precise contributions of each party. 
For instance, determining the contributions of an AI system versus a human inventor raises questions is similar to 
determining the contributions of each inventor in joint inventorship cases: 
Attribution of Contributions: It can be challenging to clearly attribute specific contributions to human or AI, much 
like distinguishing individual contributions in joint inventorship. 
Inventive Step: Assessing whether the AI’s output constitutes an inventive step and how it integrates with the 
human inventor’s input parallels the evaluation of significant contributions among co-inventors. 
Both situations demand a nuanced understanding of the inventive process and careful documentation to ensure that 
all contributions are accurately captured and credited. Therefore, managing joint inventorship and joint ownership 
requires careful legal and contractual planning to mitigate potential disputes and ensure that the rights and contributions 
of all parties are fairly recognized and protected. 
Before exploring the complexities of AI-assisted inventorship, it is crucial to understand the traditional definition 
of inventorship in patents. Inventorship refers to individuals who have made significant and original contributions to the 
conception of an invention, a concept rooted in the belief that only natural persons can perform the necessary creative or 
inventive mental acts. This principle, based on theories of labor, personality, and utilitarianism, ensures that patent rights 
are connected to human creators. However, there are notable regional differences in the criteria for determining and 
challenging inventorship. As discussed above, in the United States, inventorship is based on contributions to patent claims, 
often resolved through legal processes. Europe, through the EPO, enforces strict formal standards focused on procedural 
compliance. In Japan, the JPO determines inventorship based on contributions detailed in specifications and drawings. 
Furthermore, as U.S. case law has established, joint inventorship recognizes collaborative contributions from 
several parties, requiring a substantial amount of work from each contributor even in the absence of simultaneous or 
physical collaboration. To ensure fair recognition and protection of each party’s rights, meticulous documentation is 
essential because the complex nature of AI-human inventorship complicates the attribution of contributions and the 
evaluation of inventive step. 
2.4. 
Inventorship in AI-assisted Inventions 
The notion of inventorship has started to be questioned, particularly in patents where the inventive process involves 
AI assistance. This is because in many legal jurisdictions, applicants are not required to disclose the process by which 
their invention was created. Consequently, challenges to inventorship typically arise during opposition procedures or 
subsequent legal proceedings. This explains the limited number of cases concerning inventorship, particularly in the 
context of AI-assisted inventions. This situation underscores the relevance of the proposed approach outlined in the 
current research, wherein it is advocated for distributing the responsibility of resolving inventorship issues in AI-assisted 
inventions to both patent examiners and judges. However, before proposing possible solutions, it is essential to analyze 
the inventions involved. 
Depending on the different proportions of human and AI contributions/involvements (hereinafter human-AI 
contributions/involvements) in the inventive process of inventions there are three main categories: 
1. 
Inventions fully made by human(s) without using AI tools (simply — inventions/traditional 
inventions/human inventions). 
2. 
Inventions made with minor AI-assistance (AI-assisted inventions). 
3. 
Inventions made with significant AI-assistance (so-called AI-generated inventions). 
Traditional inventions (1) are solely created by humans and the inventorship is credited to the individual or 
individuals involved in the patent’s creation. Under this framework, there is clear certainty regarding inventorship, with 
potential discussions focusing on the level of contribution from each person, especially in cases of joint inventorship.

Nonetheless, the significance of this distinction is minimal, as even a single claim may be enough to determine someone 
as an inventor. 
AI-assisted inventions (2) will be discussed in the following sections in more detail. This type of invention is of 
the greatest interest, because it is in relation to them that this paper proposes an approach to determine human-AI 
contributions. 
So-called AI-generated inventions (3) refer to situations where AI contributes to the invention process but don’t 
independently conceptualize the inventive idea. AI acts as a tool or aid to human inventors. These include human-made 
inventions using AI for outcome verification and inventions where a human identifies a problem and employs AI to find 
a solution. It also includes the (largely hypothetical) scenario where inventions are made purely “autonomously” by AI 
systems. At the moment, patent protection is not provided for this type of invention in most jurisdictions. This was 
resolved in DABUS cases that will be discussed in more detail below. Although in certain situations a dependent claim 
that recites a minor contribution made entirely by AI may still be included in an application, so long as the underlying 
independent claim has a valid human inventor who made a significant contribution to the conception of the overall 
inventive concept, see (Crawford & Bockman, 2024). It is also worth noting that, despite the fact that AI-generated 
inventions are not protectable in general at the moment, the approach proposed in our work can be used in the future if 
protection is granted to AI-generated inventions (either in terms of inventorship, ownership, or sui generis rights). 
2.5. 
Significant AI-assistance in inventions (AI-generated inventions) 
AI systems can provide substantial assistance in various stages of the inventive process, including data analysis, 
simulation and modeling, idea generation and optimization. However, in the context of AI-assisted inventions, defining 
what constitutes a “significant” contribution is crucial for determining inventorship. 
For this purpose, case laws will be reviewed to determine which instances constitute a “significant” contribution 
by AI. Additionally, the examination will cover the countries where AI makes a substantial contribution but is not 
recognized as an inventor due to the minimal involvement of human contribution. 
The United State of America 
The United States has established a new approach to determining inventorship in AI-assisted inventions. The 
guidance (USPTO, 2024a), as per the USPTO, focus on the contributions of natural persons involved in the inventorship 
process. Key principles include: 
1. 
The natural person can be listed as an inventor if his contribution to AI-assisted invention was significant. 
2. 
Merely identifying a problem, general goal or research plan isn’t sufficient for the natural person to be 
listed as an inventor. At the same time “a significant contribution could be shown by the way the person 
constructs the prompt in view of a specific problem to elicit a particular solution from the AI system”. 
3. 
Mere recognition and appreciation of the output of an AI system as an invention is not sufficient. 
4. 
A natural person who develops an essential building block from which the claimed invention is derived 
may be considered to have provided a significant contribution. 
5. 
A person simply owning or overseeing an AI system that is used in the creation of an invention, without 
providing a significant contribution to the conception of the invention, does not make that person an 
inventor. 
Thaler v. Vidal 
In the landmark Thaler case (Thaler v. Vidal, 2022), the Federal Circuit reaffirmed that inventors must be natural 
persons. Thaler had listed his AI system, DABUS, as the inventor for two patents. The court held that the Patent Act 
requires inventors to be human, thus rejecting the AI’s designation as an inventor. The USPTO’s decisions explained that 
under current U.S. patent laws, inventorship is limited to a natural person(s). “The position expressed by the Federal 
Court in Thaler, that an inventor must be a natural person, and by extension, any joint inventor must be a natural person. 
As such, patent applications that name a machine on an application data sheet (37 CFR 1.76), an inventor’s oath or 
declaration (37 CFR 1.63), or a substitute statement (37 CFR 1.64) as either an inventor or joint inventor will be 
considered by the USPTO to have improper inventorship” (USPTO, 2024a). 
Additionally, applications and patents must not list any entity that is not a natural person as an inventor or joint 
inventor, even if an AI system may have been instrumental in the creation of the claimed invention (USPTO, 2024a). 
Pannu v. Iolab Corporation 
This case provides a critical precedent for understanding the requirements of significant contribution. The court 
emphasized that an inventor must be involved in the conception of the invention, reinforcing the notion that mere 
supervision or ownership is inadequate for inventorship as explained in Chapter 1. 
Although the Pannu factors are generally applied to two or more people who create an invention (i.e., joint 
inventors), it follows that a single person who uses an AI system to create an invention is also required to make a

significant contribution to the invention, according to the Pannu factors, to be considered a proper inventor (USPTO, 
2024a). Moreover, a named inventor doesn’t need to contribute to every claim in a patent application; contributing to just 
one claim is enough. However, each claim must be invented by at least one named inventor in contrast with Japan. In 
essence, a natural person must significantly contribute to each claim in a patent application or patent. 
Inventorship is improper in any patent or patent application that includes a claim in which at least one natural 
person did not significantly contribute to the claimed invention, even if the application or patent includes other claims 
invented by at least one natural person. Therefore, a rejection under 35 U.S.C. 101 and 115 should be made for each claim 
(USPTO, 2024a). 
Europe 
The EPO has similar guidelines to the USPTO but with its unique rules. From an inventorship standpoint, AI 
inventions can be categorized into three types: 
1. 
Human-made inventions use AI for outcome verification. 
2. 
Inventions where a human identifies a problem and employs AI to find a solution. 
3. 
AI-made inventions, where AI identifies a problem and proposes a solution without human intervention. 
In the first two categories, AI is used as a tool for human inventors, augmenting their capabilities and it’s a minor 
contribution explained in the previous chapter. In the third category (AI-made inventions), scientists seem to agree that 
AI which could invent independently of human direction, instruction and oversight is a matter of undefined future and 
thus science fiction (EPO, 2023). 
There is a consensus that an inventor is a human being who creates an invention through their own creative efforts. 
This view is supported by an academic study on AI inventorship commissioned by the EPO and discussions with EPC 
contracting states (Shemtov, 2019). Moreover, the EPO mandates that the designated inventor in a patent application must 
be a human being, not a machine. This requirement has legal implications, ensuring that the designated inventor is 
legitimate and can benefit from the rights associated with this status. To exercise these rights, the inventor must possess 
legal personality, which AI systems or machines lack. 
The legal requirement that an inventor must be a human was challenged when two applications named an AI 
system (DABUS) as the inventor were filed with various patent offices worldwide. In 2019, the EPO rejected these 
applications (see (EP Patent No. 18275163.6 (patent application), 2018), (EP Patent No. 18275174.3 (patent application), 
2018)) because the EPC requires inventors to be natural persons. Appeals were filed by the applicant but were dismissed 
by the EPO Legal Board of Appeal during oral proceedings on December 21, 2021 (see (J 0008/20 (Designation of 
inventor/DABUS), 2021) and (J 0009/20 (Designation of inventor/DABUS II), 2021)). The Legal Board affirmed that 
under the EPC, the inventor must have legal capacity, and a statement indicating the origin of the right to the European 
patent must specify the inventor’s successor in title. On December 20, 2021, the applicant filed divisional application (EP 
Patent No. 21216024.6 (patent application), 2018), which is currently under examination. 
Germany 
German Patent Law, while referencing “inventorship” in Sections 6-8, 37, and 63, lacks a clear definition of what 
constitutes an “invention”. German case law stipulates two conditions for being considered an inventor: (1) the person 
must make a substantial, creative contribution to solving the problem addressed by the invention, although this 
contribution need not be inventive; and (2) the contribution must be intellectual — i.e. it must originate from the person, 
and not from following instructions by others. This means the contribution must be significant to the invention’s success 
and result from intellectual effort (Shemtov, 2019). 
In the recent case (Thaler v. German Patent and Trade Mark Office, 2024), the Federal Court of Justice of Germany 
ruled that AI-generated inventions are patentable and that a natural person must be designated as the inventor, even if AI 
played a role in developing the technical teaching. The decision states that providing a sufficiently clear designation of 
the inventor, such as indicating that the inventor directed a specific artificial intelligence to generate the invention, is 
legally irrelevant and does not justify the rejection of the application. 
The criteria established by the Court for obtaining a patent on an AI-generated invention are as follows: 
 
A natural person can be designated as the inventor even if AI was used to develop the claimed technical 
teaching. 
 
A human contribution that significantly influenced the success of the invention is sufficient for the person 
to be considered the inventor, even if AI assisted in the discovery. 
 
The specific type or intensity of the human contribution needed for inventorship is debated, but not crucial 
to the determination. 
 
It is still possible to identify human contributions when using AI, and legal assessment can establish 
inventorship based on this, as no AI system operates entirely without human input.

 
Even if AI made a substantial contribution, there should be at least one natural person recognized as the 
inventor based on their contribution, making it reasonable to name a human inventor. 
In (Kim, 2025) author argues that the German Federal Court of Justice’s decision in the DABUS case adopted a 
surface-level, formalistic approach to inventorship in AI applications, effectively reducing inventor designation to a legal 
fiction by accepting “any human” as sufficient while sidestepping the substantive question of meaningful human 
contribution. 
Thus, even though it is possible to obtain a patent in Germany in the case of DABUS if a natural person is 
designated as an inventor, the formality of such a decision leaves the legal uncertainty regarding human contribution in 
AI-assisted inventions unresolved. 
The United Kingdom (UK) 
The Intellectual Property Office of the United Kingdom (UKIPO) aligns with the EPO and USPTO, requiring that 
inventors be natural persons. 
Section 7(3) of the UK Patent Act 1977 (UKIPO, 2021) defines the inventor as “the actual deviser of the 
invention”. The Court of Appeal clarifies that “invention” in this context refers to the information in the specification, 
not just the claims, since entitlement issues can arise even before claims exist. The inventive concept, which must be 
identified, refers to the core idea or “heart of the invention” disclosed in the specification. The “deviser” is someone who 
contributes intellectually to the inventive concept, beyond financial, abstract, or administrative input. This contribution 
must involve aspects not disclosed in prior art and potentially rendering the invention patentable (“contribution must be 
to the formulation of the inventive concept” — see (Yeda Research and Development Co Ltd v Rhone-Poulenc Rorer 
International Holdings Inc House, 2007)). 
The “deviser” typically means a human actor, as AI lacks the thought process required for devising an invention. 
AI can be crucial in developing an invention but is seen as a tool rather than an inventor. The human who utilizes AI to 
contribute to the inventive concept is considered the inventor. 
In view of DABUS, UKIPO made a consultation with society (UKIPO, 2021a) regarding how patent system should 
deal with AI technology and particularly about protection of AI-devised (AI-assisted) inventions. UKIPO received 48 
responses (see (UKIPO, 2021b) and (UKIPO, 2022)) about the view of patents and AI inventorship and concluded that 
UKIPO will not change current patent laws, as existing rules are seen as sufficient for protecting AI-assisted inventions. 
The UK government highlighted that they would pursue international discussions on AI inventorship to support UK 
innovation and competitiveness, while continuing to assess future IP needs for AI-assisted inventions. Also, from the 
report it can be concluded that they generally include generated AI-generated inventions in the set of AI-assisted 
inventions and thus AI-generated inventions might receive patent protection if application satisfies the legal requirements, 
including novelty and non-obviousness and human-inventorship. Thus still, in certain cases AI-generated inventions 
might not satisfy non-obviousness criteria if human contribution wasn’t inventive. 
South Africa 
In South Africa, the AI system DABUS was acknowledged as an inventor for a patent. This marked the first time 
an AI system was granted patent rights as an inventor, signaling a notable shift in the field. 
However, unlike some other patent offices, South Africa’s patent office does not conduct substantive examinations 
of patent applications. Instead, it focuses on ensuring that applications meet the necessary formal requirements. This 
means that no substantive decision was made regarding the invention’s merit or the suitability of an AI system as an 
inventor within this process. The patent office did not formally assess the legitimacy of the AI as an inventor but primarily 
ensured the application forms were complete and correct, see (Inventa, 2023). 
Canada 
The recent decision (Thaler S. L., 2025) of the Commissioner of the Canadian Patent Office and the Patent Appeal 
Board in the DABUS case refused the application for the food container. In this decision, the Commissioner and the 
Board reviewed the patent application, oral hearing arguments, and cited articles submitted by Thaler. 
The main conclusions regarding inventorship were as follows: 
 
The term “inventor” is limited to a natural person or persons. 
 
The filed application cannot be patented because DABUS is an AI system. Dabus cannot be recognised as 
an inventor and, furthermore, the application cannot be filed by “the inventor or the inventor’s legal 
representative,” as required by the Canadian Patent Act. 
 
A valid inventor cannot be identified. Therefore, there is no “inventor’s legal representative” who could 
file the application, even though it was submitted by Thaler and all required fees were paid.

2.6. 
Challenges in Determining Human Contribution to AI-assisted Inventions 
Although there have been very recent developments on this issue, see (USPTO, 2024a), the USPTO acknowledges 
that “…in determining whether a natural person’s contribution in AI-assisted inventions…there is no bright-line test”. 
Instead, the process remains detailed and involves evaluating the specific circumstances of each case. 
Kathi Vidal, Under Secretary of Commerce for Intellectual Property and Director of the USPTO emphasized that 
the patent system is designed to incentivize and protect human ingenuity, as well as the investments required to transform 
ideas into marketable products and solutions. It also incentivizes the sharing of ideas to enable further innovation. Recent 
guidance seeks to balance granting patent protection for AI-assisted inventions while ensuring that innovation remains 
accessible for future advancements. This approach acknowledges AI’s role in innovation while maintaining a focus on 
human contribution, see (USPTO, 2024a). 
In Europe in testing patentable subject matter in case law such as (Aerotel Ltd v Telco Holdings Ltd, 2006) in the 
UK, led also to the development of the fourth-step test. The second step of this test introduced the concept of “technical 
contribution”, which notes: “What has the inventor really added to human knowledge perhaps best sums up the exercise”. 
3. 
Method 
In previous sections, existing cases related to inventorship issues in AI-assisted inventions were discussed. Due to 
the fast development of AI technologies and legal uncertainty, relevant cases currently include only so-called DABUS 
cases. But these are corner cases, where AI tool DABUS was used with such a contribution that notion of inventorship 
was not granted to Thaler, a developer of DABUS system. Moreover, the provisions of the IP laws have just begun to 
adapt to new realities of emerging AI technologies, and so far only in the form of guidance for examinators or comments


## Page 10


10 
on the existing provisions of the IP laws. Therefore, the problem of a lack of case law and legal provisions of how to deal 
with inventorship issue in this emerging area of AI-assisted inventions has arisen. 
Thus, this section proposes for discussion a new approach of how inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions can 
be resolved. Specifically, it addresses the question of how to measure the amount of AI contribution (and vice versa — 
human contribution) to an AI-assisted invention that will be considered necessary and sufficient for a human inventor to 
be recognized as its inventor. 
3.1. 
Creation of case law by applicants 
One of the objectives of this paper is to propose a mechanism to create precedents that define the test or describe 
the principle of how to measure human-AI contributions to AI-assisted invention in determining inventorship. The current 
study proposes artificially create such a case law (or presidential rulings/determinations at the administrative level of the 
national patent office), by creating case studies by some initiative group of stakeholders. This can be initiated by 
stakeholders specialized in AI — companies using or developing AI, academic institutions, government entities as well 
as individuals. This group will apply for patents for different inventions in different jurisdictions in order to incentivize 
patent offices and IP authorities of countries to create case laws in a more fast and methodological manner. Below, the 
rationale for the economic benefits for such an initiative group in using the proposed approach will be presented. However, 
for simplicity, it should be assumed that such an initiative group has been found and is ready to invest funds in the 
proposed experiment. 
This incentive can be based on a methodology that includes the following steps: 
1. 
Creating a set of inventions (i.e., cases) with varying degrees/levels of human and AI involvement. 
For the purposes of the experiment, it is assumed that all created inventions satisfy the patentability 
requirements. Namely, the following optional actions may also be undertaken: 
a. 
Manually assessing the created inventions against the patentability criteria — novelty, inventive 
step, and industrial applicability. 
b. 
Selecting, from each level of human-AI contributions, the invention most likely to meet the 
patentability requirements. 
c. 
Manually improving certain inventions based on the patentability assessment. If such improvements 
increase human contribution, the invention would be reassigned to a higher human-involvement 
level. 
2. 
Filing patent applications by stakeholders (applicants) for selected inventions in designated jurisdictions. 
Each application should include a description detailing how the invention was created and specifying the 
respective contributions of humans and AI. This will require patent examiners to evaluate compliance with 
human inventorship criteria for each involvement level. 
3. 
Pursuing appeals in case of rejection, as Court of Appeal or Supreme Courts often establish influential case 
law in this area. 
4. 
Deriving long-term insights — in the coming years, society is expected to gain a clearer understanding of 
the threshold level between human and AI contributions to the inventive process in AI-assisted inventions 
that is sufficient to recognize a person as an inventor and grant patent protection.

development in the shortest possible time. 
This proposal will speed up the process of creating case law. There is also an economic feasibility in this. 
Companies specializing in AI, as well as those utilizing AI in their R&D, may face legal uncertainty regarding the 
patentability of inventions involving AI tools. This legal uncertainty could lead them to prefer to have trade secrets, 
resulting in increased deadweight loss, undermines the function of the patent system in incentivizing socially beneficial 
disclosure, and slowing down technical progress. 
It should be noted that step 2 of the proposed approach can also be implemented in different ways. Besides filing 
patent applications by stakeholders for chosen inventions in designated jurisdictions, inventions can be considered by 
courts, specialized third-party entity specially created to develop criteria of how to address inventorship issue in AI-
assisted inventions and provide developed recommendations to patent offices. The entity which will proceed with step 2 
is also a question for further research and discussion, as the main goal of current study is to provide a general 
methodological approach of how to resolve inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions. 
It is also worth noting that creating a set of inventions (i.e., test cases) in step 1 of the proposed approach can be 
done in different ways: 
1. 
Strict Approach. 
In this approach, inventions (i.e. cases) are selected or created according to predefined levels that reflect 
varying proportions of human contribution and AI involvement (human-AI contributions) to the inventive 
process. Step 1 is then implemented by creating several inventions for each level using a robust 
methodology — for example, by organizing them within selected technology domain which can be selected 
using IPC (International Patent Classification) categories. 
2. 
Broad Approach. 
In this approach, inventions are selected without regard to, or at a fixed value of, the human-AI 
contributions ratio. Instead, selection is based on (a) the type of inventive process in which AI tools were 
involved, and (b) the technology domain in which the patentable subject matter resides. This allows for 
variation in process type and technical field while controlling or disregarding contribution ratio. 
A hybrid implementation of step 1 can also be employes, combining the structured classification of the strict 
approach with the flexibility and scope of the broad approach. However, it is worth noting that the present research only 
introduces a novel approach for creating case laws, thus work provides only a few possible ways of implementing it, 
while other methods may be developed through further research and discussion, surveys and experiments. 
Mentioned implementations of the proposed approach are discussed in more detail below in the relevant 
subsections.

3.3. 
Broad approach in creating test cases 
In contrast or even in addition to the mentioned strict approach in creation test cases, current study also proposes 
broad approach in creation test cases, where cases are chosen both in different areas of technology and in different types 
of inventive process in which AI tools were involved. This approach may provide case law as an outcome according to 
different types of inventive process at the same time in different types of inventive process compared to strict approach, 
but perhaps with less measurement accuracy of human-AI contributions. 
In a broad approach, the selection of case studies for conducting the proposed experiments may vary. It is necessary 
to consider a range of parameters that reflect the current practices in the creation of AI-assisted inventions. These 
parameters include the diversity of inventive processes, the number of individuals involved in the inventive process, their 
respective roles (such as programming, brainstorming, management, experimentation, etc.), the specific AI tools 
employed and their modes of application, and the domain of the final invention (such as software, medicine, mechanics, 
etc.).

3.4. 
Advantages and limitations of proposed approaches 
As a result of conducting the strict approach, society will develop more certain criteria of how to measure human-
AI contributions in the inventive process at least for chosen and neighboring technological areas. A limitation of the strict 
approach is the need to conduct a separate experiment for each area of technology. 
Conducting the broad approach will lead to obtaining fewer sharp criteria compared to the strict approach, but at 
the same time these criteria will be obtained for a wide range of areas of technology and different types of inventive 
process involved. 
Thus, the most comprehensive approach will be a hybrid approach, when conducting both strict and broad 
approaches. Moreover, it will allow us to compare results of several experiments, and if the obtained results of several 
approaches are similar, it will confirm scientific grounds and robustness of proposed approach and chosen cases, while if 
results are different, it will give feedback on how to improve conducted approaches. It will also allow us to compare 
developed criteria for a different field of technology and inventive process, thus confirming the robustness of the proposed 
approach. 
4. 
Experiments 
To confirm the scientific grounds in the first approximation of the proposed methodology, we conducted a pilot 
survey between August 19 and 31, 2024 among young professionals in IP, professors in IP, and AI experts. Participants 
were asked to provide their opinions on whether a human in each of several hypothetical AI-assisted inventions (cases) 
we created met the legal criteria to be named as the inventor or patent protection should be refused. The pilot survey 
included twenty-three respondents, offering a diversity of perspectives across relevant expertise areas. While this pilot 
survey may not lend itself to broad statistical generalization, it serves as proof of concept and demonstrates the 
applicability of the methodology described in this paper. 
The need to conduct this pilot survey arose to test our methodology in a short time. This could be the first stage of 
a “pilot test” for our methodology in the survey, which can be scaled up. After that, it can be used by applying patent 
applications to the patent offices or to the specialized third-party entity specially created to develop criteria of how to 
address inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions. This entity may then submit the recommendations it develops to the 
legislature or the patent office. In fact, if step 2 of the proposed approach involves having inventions reviewed by a 
specialized third-party entity (rather than the patent office itself), the process essentially functions as a large-scale survey. 
The necessity of testing our methodology through a pilot survey is further supported by other studies in the field 
of AI and Law, which have likewise employed survey-based approaches. In (Harasta, Novotná, & Savelka, 2024) authors 
discussed topic of legal documents automatically generated using AI tools. This study used a survey between professionals 
and law students to explore the perception legal documents based on their assumed origin (human vs. AI-generated), 
revealing a clear preference for human-crafted texts despite expectations of widespread future use of generative AI. At 
the same time, their findings underscore a prospective trend toward the automatic generation of legal documents. 
Further support for the need for a pilot survey comes from a consultation with society (UKIPO, 2021a) regarding 
how patent system should deal with AI-devised (AI-assisted) inventions that were discussed earlier. 
Thus, these two mechanisms — pilot survey (or on a larger scale — reviewing inventions by special third-party 
entity) and testing a proposed approach by applying patent applications can provide further policy context by testing how 
actual patent examination results contrast with the perceptions of those in the industry in terms of what constitutes human 
contribution. At the same time, it is not just about “human contribution” in the abstract sense, but how human contribution 
maps onto the legal interpretation of the specific conditions outlines in the USPTO/EPO or Patent Laws in other 
jurisdictions.

Even based on the results of the pilot survey conducted, it is already possible to draw some conclusions and 
recommendations for patent offices. The greatest progress in this matter has been achieved by the approach of the USPTO 
in their initial “Inventorship Guidance for AI-Assisted Inventions” (USPTO, 2024a). They proposed the consideration of 
two artificial examples of AI-assisted inventions (one is related to the use of a LLM model and another with the use in 
silico methods in drug discovery) and how patent examiners should consider them in the question of human inventorship 
depending on human contribution, see (USPTO, 2024b). If the approach reflected in the initial guidance (USPTO, 2024a) 
is taken as a basis, our pilot survey and the cases proposed in it indicate potential value in further expanding the guidance 
through additional examples involving different categories of AI tools (i.e., not only LLM and in silico). Such examples 
may include AI-assisted inventions created with the use of AI tools to simulate work processes to increase efficiency or 
other characteristics of existing inventions (like in our proposed example 2). In general, the more examples future 
guidance will include such that are considered from different areas of technology, with different uses of AI-tools (and 
creative techniques), and with a variety of human contributions, the better for a more complete understanding of how to

Given the current lack of case law on inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions, the results of the pilot survey

challenges posed by AI-assisted inventions. 
During the creation of test cases and the analysis of the pilot survey results, it is also become evident that a new 
challenge is emerging: in the near future, there could be a significant increase in AI-assisted patent applications that 
represent incremental improvements on existing patents. AI tools may also be employed to improve existing patents while 
avoiding infringement. Consequently, patent law will need to address these challenges in the patent granting process and 
provide guidance for examiners to maintain a balance between social welfare, technological progress, and the rights of 
patentees. Stakeholders should also consider these issues in their business processes, for instance, by developing internal 
guidelines for the use of AI tools in the inventive process, as well as in patent enforcement and portfolio management. 
After conducting our pilot survey (August 19-31, 2024) and finalizing the main analysis, we identified an article 
(Henry H. Perritt, 2025), in which the author describes filing a patent application for an AI-assisted invention: on 
November 13, 2024, the author asked ChatGPT to design “a generative AI system to search for and identify products and 
services that potentially infringe on a patent”. Perritt drafted the entire specification with AI assistance and filed the 
patent application with the USPTO together with a declaration describing how AI was used. In the declaration, he also 
stated that he believes that the initially AI-generated application exceeds USPTO’s Example 1, Scenario 3 (USPTO, 
2024b), according to which additional prompting of the AI system and other steps such as conducting experiments and 
changes to the invention’s elements are sufficient to recognize a human as an inventor. The final rejection issued on 
March 17, 2025, did not include any objection related to inventorship. However, the application was rejected based on 35 
U.S.C. § 101 subject matter eligibility and 35 U.S.C. § 103 obviousness grounds.  
One of the possible explanations for the absence of an objection or any feedback related to inventorship in Perritt’s 
case is the possible revision of USPTO guidance (USPTO, 2025a) at the time of the final rejection of his invention, 
following U.S. policy changes introduced on January 23, 2025 (USPTO, 2025b), which directed agencies to review and 
revise policies in order to reduce barriers to AI innovation. Another explanation is that Perritt’s contribution to the AI-
assisted invention was considered sufficient under the initial USPTO guidance, highlighting the importance of such 
guidelines, which provide inventors with a clearer understanding of what constitutes sufficient human contribution and 
what does not. 
Such strong interest from academia and legislators, together with the rapid pace of AI development, may lead to 
another revision of laws and guidelines, further reinforcing the relevance of the inventorship issue addressed in this study. 
The results of our pilot survey, as well as the Perritt case as an illustrative example, provide preliminary support for the 
proposed methodology and indicate the potential value of scaling the experiment to further examine legal uncertainty in 
AI-assisted inventions. 
However, it is stressed that the analysis in this paper focusses on the interpretation of human inventorship as a 
criterion for patentability. The widespread deployment of AI-technology will also affect other substantive patent criteria, 
such as novelty and most notably inventive-step/non-obviousness (Abbott, 2019). Determining the minimum threshold 
for human inventorship contribution is independent of an application’s evaluation in relations to these other substantive 
criteria. However, when implementing a pilot survey in accordance with the methodology outlined in this paper, it needs 
to be clear to respondents that the focus is on the issue of machine/human inventorship contribution, which should not be 
conflated with the concept of inventive step and/or obviousness. 
5. 
Conclusions and Discussion 
This study addressed an emerging topic at the intersection of areas of AI and IP: the nature and contribution of 
using AI tools in AI-assisted inventions and whether such contributions mean a human cannot be considered the inventor. 
The research summarized the current legal landscape and case laws regarding inventorship and its application to AI-
assisted inventions in most jurisdictions. The current work also identified an inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions, 
particularly the lack of case law addressing this issue and the absence of developed criteria for measuring human-AI 
contributions in defining inventorship.


## Page 17


17 
As a novel approach, this research proposes discussing a condition under which a new and bold experiment can 
be implemented to create case law and resolve the legal uncertainty regarding inventorship in AI-assisted inventions. This 
experiment could be initiated by society, involving stakeholders specializing in AI. The proposed approach essentially 
includes steps such as creating inventions (cases) with varying levels of human-AI contributions or types of AI 
involvement in the inventive process, applying for a patent with an explanation of the inventive process and AI 
involvement, and receiving either a patent grant or refusal. The results of such decisions may be used in the future in 
determining criteria of which amount of human-AI contributions is enough to obtain a patent. The research proposed a 
methodology for conducting this experiment and selecting cases that best reflect the current state of AI use in the inventive 
process. Utilizing this approach will help identify the best methods for measuring human contributions to AI-assisted 
inventions and determining inventorship. The proposed approach can be also used in other fields of IP law, where legal 
uncertainties exist, especially in relation to new emerging technologies in Patent Law and Copyright Law. 
To validate the scientific basis of the proposed methodology, we conducted a pilot survey among young 
professionals in IP, professors, and AI experts, asking them to provide their opinions on whether inventorship should be 
granted or patent protection refused for each of six proposed cases. The survey results indicated that incorporating an 
additional step of human involvement in the inventive process leads to a greater consensus that the human researcher 
meets the legal criteria for being named as the inventor. In addition, the use of different inventive processes is evaluated 
differently. For example, using simulation AI tools versus generative AI tools is more often considered in favor of 
recognizing a human as the inventor of the AI-assisted invention. 
Based on pilot survey results we propose several recommendations for different patent offices of how guidance 
for examinations can already be improved in considering inventorship issue in AI-assisted inventions. The main 
recommendation is to release or improve guidance for examination with a large and varied number of examples of AI-
assisted inventions and how to address inventorship issue in each of them, where these AI-assisted inventions should 
cover different areas of technology, different uses of AI-tools, and with a variety of human contributions. 
Our methodology and pilot survey provide a basis for developing test patent applications that explore varying 
levels of AI involvement and human contribution. These cases could stimulate legal discourse and judicial consideration 
and help shape case law on AI-assisted inventions. Our results can also inform policy by comparing actual patent 
examination outcomes with industry perceptions of what constitutes human contribution. With this proof of concept, this 
mechanism based on a survey methodology can be scaled up to generate robust results that can inform patent examination 
practices.
