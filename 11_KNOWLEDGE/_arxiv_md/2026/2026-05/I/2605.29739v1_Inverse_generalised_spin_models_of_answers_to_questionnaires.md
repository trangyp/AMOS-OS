Network psychometrics conceptualises psychological constructs as emergent properties of systems
of interacting variables.
Energy-based probabilistic models have gained popularity as models of
these interactions, but their psychometric application has so far been limited, since most implemen-
tations assume binary or ternary responses and rely on limiting inference assumptions. We infer and
analyse three generalised spin models of ordinal questionnaire data: the generalised Ising, Blume-
Capel (BC), and Blume-Emery-Griffiths (BEG) models. These are maximum-entropy models that
accommodate ordinal responses on symmetric Likert-type scales with an arbitrary number of op-
tions, allowing for single-site anisotropy (BC, BEG) and bi-quadratic item interactions (BEG). We
prove the identifiability and the concavity of the maximum likelihood estimation of the parameters,
as well as the gauge invariance of the Ising and BC models. Afterwards, we propose an inference
protocol of approximated maximum likelihood, based on the estimation of the likelihood gradients
through Monte Carlo sampling. We apply this procedure to eleven psychometric and sociological
questionnaire datasets, comparing the inferred spin models against the multivariate Gaussian and
three further benchmark probabilistic models. We then assess whether the inferred models reproduce
the empirical features of the data in terms of different observables, including principal-component
histograms, and histograms of Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances to the mean answer. At the
item level, the multi-modality observed in the histograms of principal components is partially cap-
tured by the spin models. These traits of polarisation can be understood, in the light of mean-field
theory, as coexistence of stable and metastable (finite-size) phases of the spin models. At the subject
level, the BEG model systematically outperforms the other models in capturing the distribution of
distances to the mean, while all models underestimate the heavy tails of the Mahalanobis distance.
Overall, the analysis witnesses the predictive power of the BEG model, able to account better than
others for the abundance of outliers and mean responders, and reveals several highly non-linear
features of questionnaire data that both Gaussian and spin models fail to account for.
I.
INTRODUCTION
In conventional psychometric frameworks, observed indicators (e.g., questionnaire items such as “I work hard to
accomplish my goals” and “I strive for excellence in everything I do”) are treated as effects of an underlying latent
variable (e.g., a personality trait such as conscientiousness), consistent with a cause-and-effect interpretation [1].
Although this approach has dominated psychometrics for much of the past century, alternative perspectives have
emerged in recent years. In particular, approaches rooted in network theory conceptualise psychological phenomena
as emergent properties of complex systems [2–5]. From this perspective, a construct is not assumed to be the common
cause of its indicators but is instead understood as arising from interactions among them, reflecting a mereological
(i.e., part–whole) relation [6]. Within this framework, a range of psychological constructs can be conceptualised as

systems of interacting components. For example, personality may be viewed as a network of affective, cognitive, and
behavioural processes [7, 8]; intelligence as emerging from mutualistic interactions among cognitive abilities during
development [9, 10]; psychopathology as arising from interdependencies among symptoms [11–13]; and attitudes
as reflecting interactions among beliefs, feelings, and behaviours toward an object [14, 15]. This perspective has
motivated the development of formal models that differ from those traditionally used in latent variable approaches,
with network models providing one such class of tools [16]. Importantly, conceptual differences between network
and latent variable frameworks do not imply a strict separation at the statistical level. A growing body of work
has shown that several network models are equivalent to latent variable models—such as item response theory [17]
and factor analysis [18, 19]—under specific conditions. Moreover, network models can recover meaningful structure
even when the underlying data-generating process follows a common-cause model [17, 20]. Thus, although network
theory entails specific assumptions about the nature of psychological constructs, the associated statistical models are
not tied to a single data-generating mechanism. To represent interactions in network models, researchers commonly
employ Pairwise Markov Random Fields, in which nodes represent variables (e.g., questionnaire items) and edges
represent conditional dependencies [16, 21]. Estimating such networks is a central and nontrivial task [22, 23], as
it involves determining both the presence and the strength of conditional relationships between variables.
This
step is critical because commonly used network indices—such as measures of global structure, node centrality, and
community organisation—depend directly on the estimated edge strengths [16]. Accordingly, a range of methods
has been developed to estimate network structures from psychometric data (see, for instance, [24–31], with ongoing
refinements driven by critical evaluations in the literature [32–34]).
Among these methods, the Ising model has
received increasing attention in the psychometric literature. Originally developed in statistical physics to describe
ferromagnetism, it represents a system of discrete elements that can take one of two states, whose configuration
is determined by local interactions and external influences [35]. Within this framework, the probability of a given
system state is defined by an energy function, and its behaviour can, under certain conditions, be approximated using
methods such as mean-field models. In psychometrics, the Ising model has been adopted as a general framework
for modelling interactions among dichotomous variables [24]. For example, it can represent binary responses—such
as endorsement versus rejection of an item (e.g., pro vs. contra attitudes, coded as +1 and −1)—and examine how
these responses interact with one another and with external variables, such as involvement [15] or attention [36]. This
approach has been applied to the study of attitudes [14], political beliefs [37], polarisation [38], and choice behaviour
[39]. Similarly, in the network approach to psychopathology [12], the Ising framework is used to model interactions
among symptoms, which can be either absent (coded as 0) or present (coded as 1), as well as perturbations induced
by external variables such as stress [11]. This framework has been used to account for phenomena such as recovery,
chronicity, and resilience [40]. Despite its usefulness, the standard Ising model is limited by its assumption of binary
variables. Most psychological data, however, are collected on ordinal scales, and practices such as dichotomisation can
result in information loss and potential bias [30, 41]. Furthermore, intermediate response options—such as neutral
or undecided categories (e.g., “neither agree nor disagree”)—often carry substantive meaning that is not adequately
captured by binary representations [31]. One way to address this limitation is to consider extensions of the Ising
model developed in the physics literature (see [42] for an accessible overview), such as general-spin formulations that
allow for more than two discrete states [43]. These models have only recently been introduced into psychometrics
as a framework for modelling responses on visual analogue scales, with accompanying work examining their mean-
field behaviour and dynamical properties, including pitchfork bifurcations and hysteresis [44]. Another extension is
the ternary spin Blume–Capel model, originally developed to describe magnetic systems with vacancies [45, 46]. In
addition to the interaction and external field terms of the standard Ising model, the Blume–Capel formulation includes
a single-site anisotropy term that modulates the tendency of spins to occupy the zero state, thereby regulating the
balance between active (±1) and inactive (0) states. In a psychometric context, this property directly addresses a key
limitation of the standard Ising model by allowing nodes to occupy a neutral or inactive state, providing a natural
representation of neutrality or uncertainty in item responses [31]. It has also been suggested that such models can
offer a unified framework for studying different forms of psychopathology, including bipolar and major depressive
disorders [31]. In parallel, dedicated software for analysing psychometric data within the Blume–Capel framework is
currently under development [47]. A relatively underexplored extension of the Ising model in network psychometrics
is the Blume–Emery–Griffiths (BEG) model [48]. This model builds on the Blume–Capel formulation by retaining the
single-site term and introducing an additional biquadratic coupling term that captures intensity associations—that is,
the tendency of two nodes to exhibit strong activation simultaneously, irrespective of their sign. Consequently, when
applied to psychometric data, the BEG model can account for neutrality and uncertainty while also enabling the
modelling of tendencies toward extreme responding [49]. A final aspect concerns the notions of alignment and energy
minimisation in Ising models. In general, the energy of the system is minimised when nodes (i.e., questionnaire items)
adopt the same state (e.g., +1 or -1) and are aligned both with one another (i.e., responses tend to be consistent across
items) and with external influences acting on the system [42]. This reasoning entails the constraint that interactions
between nodes, when present, are predominantly positive, a consideration particularly relevant for network structure

estimation, a core aspect of network psychometrics. In practice, this implies that items can be coded consistently with
respect to the emergent property of the system. For example, if the item “Artificial Intelligence (AI) will make this
world a better place” is coded positively, then “I prefer technologies that do not feature AI” should be reverse-coded
to maintain consistency and reduce attitudinal energy [15], thereby facilitating the emergence of a coherent positive
attitude toward AI [50]. Although these assumptions may be reasonable in some domains, such as attitude research,
they represent a substantial simplification for more complex systems and are likely to be violated in multi-construct
settings, where variables may differ in meaning and direction. Allowing for both positive and negative interactions
introduces structural frustration, in which not all pairwise relations can be simultaneously satisfied, resulting in more
complex system behaviour but also greater modelling flexibility [42, 51].
In light of these considerations, we consider the generalised Ising, BC and BEG models as a framework for describing
cross-sectional questionnaire data. This approach is designed to accommodate graded responses on an ordinal scale
of arbitrary length, as well as tendencies toward neutral and extreme response categories. By relaxing the constraint
of predominantly positive pairwise associations between items, the generalised Ising, BC and BEG models are also
suited to multi-construct settings, such as personality research. We infer the parameters of such models from different
empirical cross-sectional questionnaire datasets spanning diverse psychological constructs, including both unipolar
and bipolar scales. We compare the inferred generative models against simpler probabilistic benchmarks and examine
their predictive properties, including the statistics of distances to the mean and the emergence of multi-stability.
In particular, we first define the models and prove their identifiability and the concavity of their maximum likelihood
inference, we also prove the gauge invariance for the generalised Ising and BC models. Afterwards, we propose a
protocol of approximated full likelihood maximisation, based on the estimation of the stochastic gradients of the
likelihood by means of Markov Chain Monte Carlo Gibbs sampling, combined with Adaptive Moment Estimation
(ADAM) and Persistent Contrastive Divergence (PCD). The description of the models and their properties, the
numerical methods, the observables in terms of which we evaluate the inferred distributions (histograms of principal
components and of distances to the mean), and the benchmark probabilistic models, is done in Sec. II. We then
perform an assessment of the convergence of the spin models, an out-of-sample analysis of their relative accuracy, and
an analysis of their adherence to the empirical data in Sec. III. We draw our conclusions in Sec. IV.

B.
Probabilistic generative models with which we infer the data
Given a dataset, we divide the subject indices into two sets of length N, Nte, with which we define the training- and
the test-set matrices, X, Xte, of dimension N ×M and Nte×M respectively. We perform unsupervised inference of the
training-set data X. This requires a likelihood probability distribution PM(·|θM) : S⊗M →[0, 1], corresponding to
the model M, whose parameters θM are fixed according to certain criteria, to be defined in Sec. II C. We will consider
two kinds of probabilistic models. First, the spin models, which are energy-based models, with discrete support in
S⊗M, corresponding to three different Hamiltonian functions. Second, four simple probabilistic models: the Gaussian
model, the categorical-independent model, the copulas, and the discretised-Gaussian model. We now describe both the
spin and the simple models.

F.
Considered datasets
We briefly describe the considered datasets in Table I. Initially, we considered 14 datasets, spanning a heterogeneous
range of psychometric instruments: clinical and diagnostic scales (dass), measures of opinions and beliefs (gcbs, pwe,
rwas, cfcs), and personality-trait inventories, which include both broad multi-scale instruments (big5, hexaco, msscq)
and narrower constructs such as narcissism (hsns), Machiavellianism (mach), the Dark Triad (sd3), and three empathy-
related scales (iri, ei, acme). The datasets also differ markedly in size: the number of items per questionnaire M ranges
from 12 (cfcs) to 240 (hexaco), and the number of subjects N from a few hundreds to 105. Most datasets are taken
from the Open-Source Psychometrics Project website [84]; three empathy inventories (iri [85], ei [86], acme [87]) were
collected in-house and are described in the following. We therefore excluded those datasets for which the number of
parameters of the BEG model nBEG = M(M + 1) exceeds the number of sample points, nBEG > N; these are also
the questionnaires (acme, hexaco, msscq) for which the pseudo-likelihood maximisation algorithm (that we use as an
initial condition for the PCD algorithm) turns out not to converge. We consequently consider, for the analysis of the
present article, the remaining 11 questionnaires.
The inversion of the BEG model analysed here can be, in principle, also performed for such under-sampled datasets,
for which the number of parameters to infer is of the same order of the number of data samples. First, one could
split the questionnaire into two or more sets of questions exhibiting particularly low inter-set correlation, learn the
parameter of such sets independently, and use the resulting inferred set of couplings (with zero inter-set couplings)
as an initial condition. Second, and more simply, one could apply a regularisation (like L2 or Lasso), to reduce
the effective number of inferred parameters. Our publicly available repository [88] actually allows one to apply an
L2 regularisation, which, however, in the present study is set to zero since, as we explain in Sec. III, a systematic
accuracy/complexity trade-off analysis of the inference problem at hand is out of our present scope, and left for future
studies.

III.
RESULTS
The results are organised in three groups. Section III A concerns the validity of the inference and the out-of-sample
comparison between the spin models. Section III B analyses the emergence of non-Gaussian structure at the level of
item statistics, and the multi-stability in the inferred Gibbs measure. Section III C examines how well the models
reproduce the distributions of distances from the mean.
A.
Inference validity and out-of-sample model comparison.
a.
Moment matching.
The maximum-likelihood spin models fitted with PCD approximately reproduce, up to
the attained value of the losses Lh, LJ, LK after the training, the corresponding sufficient statistics. This is illustrated
for the mach dataset in Figs. 7 and 9 of Appendix D. See the equivalent figures for the rest of the datasets in Figs.
14,15 of Appendix J).
b.
The BEG and BC models present higher out-of-sample pseudo-likelihood than Ising.
The BEG model presents
higher out-of-sample scores (higher test-pseudo-likelihood and lower test-completion-error, see Appendix I) than the
Ising and BC models. This suggests that the BEG model is a better model also accounting, in the sense of the
accuracy-complexity trade-off, for its higher complexity. This, however, may depend on the quantity in terms of
which one performs such a model selection analysis –considering that, as we discuss in Appendix I, the likelihood
function is not computationally accessible in this case. The main scope of this article is, in any case, not to perform
an exhaustive model comparison between these models but, rather, to examine whether the spin models, and which
of them, reproduce the observables defined in Sec. II D and evaluated in the training-set. We assess the statistical
significance of the histograms evaluated in the training-set reporting, as error-bars in the histogram figures, the Wilson
score confidence interval with a p-value p = 0.05 (see Appendix I for further details).
Beyond their sufficient statistics, the maximum-likelihood, maximum-entropy models approximately reproduce as
well other quantities that they are not required to reproduce by construction.
At the item-level, these are the
histogram of item responses and the histogram of principal components. At the subject level, these are the histograms
of Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances to the mean answer.

IV.
CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES
We have studied the generalised Ising, Blume-Capel (BC) and Blume-Emery-Griffiths (BEG) spin models as inverse
probabilistic models of questionnaire data, fitting them to eleven psychometric and sociological questionnaires and
comparing their ability to reproduce a range of observables beyond their training constraints. The main findings of
this work can be summarised as follows.
First, we have inverted the spin models with an algorithm of approximated full likelihood maximisation, based
on a stochastic Markov Chain Monte Carlo estimation of the gradients, and with no need of regularisation (that is,
however, probably needed to fit questionnaires with too low number of subjects per item, subject to the curse-of-
dimensionality). The full likelihood maximisation is necessary in order to achieve moment-matching (Appendix D),
since Pseudo-likelihood maximisation yields parameters whose theoretical moments do not match the empirical ones
(Appendix E). Methodologically, and to the best of our knowledge, this represents an advancement in the literature of
inverse spin models of real questionnaire data, from the points of view of the algorithm of full likelihood maximisation
[30, 47, 91], of the inference of generalised spin models of general value of the spin [30], and of the study of the BEG
model, which has received less attention in the literature [42].
These improvements also translate into a theoretical gain in terms of adherence of the model to the data. The BEG
model outperforms Ising and BC in out-of-sample predictive accuracy (Appendix I, Tables III–IV). This suggests that
the biquadratic couplings Kij, penalising or favouring couples of response intensities both of which are very small
or very large, actually capture genuine structure in questionnaire data beyond the description capacity of bilinear
interactions. Interestingly, thanks to this extra information, the BEG model faithfully reproduce the histogram of
Euclidean distances to the empirical mean hdx, better than the Ising, BC, or simple models, for all considered datasets.
In particular, it accounts for the empirical over-representation of subjects close to and far from the mean.
Nonetheless, there are features of the empirical data that remain elusive not only to the spin models, but to the
Gaussian, categorical-independent, Gauss-discrete and copulas models. The empirical data exhibit heavier tails in
the Mahalanobis sense than any of the considered discrete-support models can predict, suggesting that questionnaire
responses could encode a higher-order structure of correlations beyond the reach of the present models.
Summing up, we conclude that questionnaire data present non-Gaussian properties at multiple levels, and features
that elude an accurate description not only by Gaussian models, but also by discrete-support spin models with bilinear
and biquadratic interactions. Our results open several directions for future investigation, which we now outline.
We have shown that the double peak in the first principal component distribution of at least one questionnaire can be
understood as the coexistence of stable and metastable phases of the (BEG) model inferred from such data. However,
in some cases, the coexistence of stable/metastable states, typical of the low-temperature phase of such models, may
be an artefactual feature, absent in the data. In order for inverse spin models to be better representations of survey
answers, it may be necessary to avoid the discontinuous, first-order phase transition, for example, by fitting the Ising
model with zero external fields from de-meaned data, since this system does not exhibit a first-order phase transition.
This constitutes a possible direction for future developments.
A second natural completion is a systematic assessment of the validity of mean-field theory for the inferred models
in Appendix F, and a comparison of its predictions for histograms, phase transitions and critical temperatures against
MCMC sampling. In particular, the extension of the mean-field argument to the BEG model, needed to account for
the three-maxima structure observed in hsns, mach and rwas, will be presented elsewhere.
The multi-stability identified in Sec. III raises the question of the nature of the low-temperature phases of the
inferred finite-size models.
An in-depth analysis –via overlap histograms and spectral properties of the overlap
matrix– would make possible an analysis beyond the simple metastability picture emerging from the weak-disorder
approximation of Appendix F. If the Gibbs measure of the (finite-size) distributions P(·|θ) exhibited more than

learning rate η provide rapid, coarse progress toward the maximum likelihood manifold. In the later blocks, nc is
increased to reduce the variance of the stochastic gradient estimates and η is decreased to allow the optimiser to settle
into the minimum. The BEG model requires a more conservative initial block (small nc and ti) because of its larger
parameter space and higher correlation times (see Appendix C).

Appendix E: Lack of consistency of maximum pseudo-likelihood learning
Since the L-BFGS-B algorithm for the pseudo-likelihood maximisation converges for all the datasets not leading to
an underdetermined problem, the conditions in Eqs. (4) are satisfied. This is illustrated, for the big5 data, in Fig. 10.

pseudo-likelihood maximisation. In fact, when learning the data with the pseudo-likelihood maximisation algorithm,

parameters do not match the empirical moments. Such a lack of consistency is shown in Fig. 10 for the big5 data. The
inconsistency persists when the theoretical expectation value is sampled from the pseudo-likelihood ⟨o(x)⟩Ppl(x|θ∗)
(with parameters θ∗inferred from the pseudo-likelihood maximisation), as visible in Figure 10.
Nonetheless, a
systematic study of the validity of the pseudo-likelihood approximation, is not shown, and left for future studies.
It is worth noticing that, despite such a lack of consistency, the main results of this study, in particular the fact
that the BEG model captures better (than the other spin models) the histograms of Euclidean distance to the mean,
and that the Mahalanobis distance to the mean is not reproduced by the spin models, remain qualitatively valid
also for the approximated maximum entropy likelihoods P(x|θ∗), Ppl(x|θ∗) whose parameters θ∗have been learned
by pseudo-likelihood maximisation.

Appendix G: Identifiability, gauge invariance and strict concavity of the log-likelihood
This appendix establishes three interrelated mathematical properties of the spin models used in this paper: gauge
invariance, identifiability and strict convexity of the log-likelihood.
We first introduce gauge transformations (induced by translations of the spin values) and their effect on the
parameters (§G 1), we define gauge invariance as invariance under these transformations, then recall the notion of
model identifiability and its connection to the strict concavity of the log-likelihood (§G 2–G 3). Finally, we verify
explicitly that these three properties - gauge invariance, identifiability and strict concavity of the log-likelihood- hold
for the Ising, BC and BEG models (§G 4–G 5). More precisely, gauge invariance for the BEG model requires including
symmetric mixed cubic terms, while identifiability holds for all three models whenever R ≥3 (and already for R = 2
in the Ising case).
Notation. A statistical model M is specified by a parameter space Θ and a likelihood function PM(·|θ), θ ∈Θ, on
a finite state space X. For the spin models considered here, Θ = Rk where k is the number of maximum entropy
constraints, and X = SM if N = 1, while X = SN×M for a sample of N subjects, where S = {v1, . . . , vR} is the
common set of R spin values for each item. We also write x = (x1, . . . , xM) ∈SM for the response vector of a single
subject, while X = (x1, . . . , xN) will be the matrix corresponding to a sample of N subjects, with xi denoting response
vectors of subjects in the sample. There will be cases when we want to talk about the case of one or more subjects
simultaneously; we will then just write x for a state in X, whether it be the response vector of a single subject or the
matrix corresponding to a sample of subjects.
1.
Gauge transformations and gauge invariance
Given a shift vector a ∈RM, let X′ = X + a denote the translated state space. We say that the pair ξ = (a, f)
defines a gauge transformation from M′ (on X′) to M (on X) if f : Θ′ →Θ is invertible and
PX+a(x + a | θ′) = PX(x | f(θ′))
∀x ∈X, θ′ ∈Θ′.

3.
Strict concavity of the log-likelihood
As shown in [78, pp. 62–63], a maximum-entropy model is identifiable in the sense of the definition in subsection G 2
if and only if the Hessian of the log-likelihood is negative definite for all states and parameters (the term that is used
in [78, pp. 40,62–63] for an identifiable model is a model having a minimal representation). Identifiability therefore
implies that the log-likelihood is strictly concave with at most one local (hence global) maximum. As a consequence,
any gradient-ascent algorithm that maintains an ascent direction which is a sufficiently good approximation of the
gradient of the log-likelihood is guaranteed to converge to the unique maximum-likelihood estimator, and to always
strictly increase the log-likelihood at each step, for sufficiently small learning rates. We note that the discussion in
the previous two subsections will allow us to establish for our maximum entropy models identifiability - and so, strict
concavity of the log-likelihood - both in the case of N = 1 and of N > 1 subjects.
