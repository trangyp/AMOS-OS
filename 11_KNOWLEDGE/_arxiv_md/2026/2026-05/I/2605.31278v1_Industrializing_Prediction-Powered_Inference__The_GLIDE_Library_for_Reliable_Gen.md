Abstract
Reliable evaluation of agentic systems requires
unbiased estimates with valid uncertainty, but
standard practice navigates between costly hu-
man annotation and biased LLM-as-judge prox-
ies. Prediction-powered inference (PPI) combines
both into debiased estimates with valid confidence
intervals, yet its various methods remain scattered
across papers under partial implementations. We
introduce GLIDE, an open-source Python library
that unifies state-of-the-art PPI estimators (PPI++,
Stratified PPI, Predict-Then-Debias and its strat-
ified variants, Active Statistical Inference) and
samplers (uniform, stratified, active, cost-optimal)
under a scipy-style API specialized to mean esti-
mation. GLIDE ships with a reproducible Monte
Carlo validation suite, an empirically grounded
decision tree for method selection, and an agen-
tic evaluation case study showing substantial an-
notation savings at equivalent precision.

1. Introduction
Consider a clinical decision-support agent that retrieves
guidelines, reasons over a patient record, and proposes a
treatment. Measuring whether such an agent behaves cor-
rectly requires expert clinician review of multi-step trajecto-
ries, a process where today’s two dominant practices both
fail at scale. Full human annotation is reliable but slow and
costly; LLM-as-judge is fast and cheap but biased, espe-
cially for knowledge-intensive, domain-specific tasks.
Rather than choosing between human reliability and proxy
scale, prediction-powered inference (PPI) (Angelopoulos
et al., 2023a;b) combines them to obtain the strengths of

3. Why Agentic Evaluation Needs PPI
Section 2 fixed the abstract setting; we now argue that agen-
tic systems exhibit four properties, simultaneously and to
an unusual degree, under which PPI is most valuable. Each
property maps directly to a method in the recent literature,
and to a corresponding GLIDE component.
1. Extreme cost asymmetry.
Evaluating an agentic tra-
jectory is qualitatively more expensive than evaluating a
single LLM output. A trajectory may involve retrieval, tool
calls, intermediate reasoning steps, and a final response, all
of which a domain expert must inspect to certify correct-
ness. Annotation costs of several dollars per trajectory are
common in healthcare, legal, and financial settings, while
an LLM-as-judge call costs cents. This two-to-three-orders-
of-magnitude gap is precisely the regime where cost-aware
sampling (Angelopoulos et al., 2025) is relevant, setting
the labeling rate as a function of the proxy/annotation cost
ratio, and the available signal.
2. Natural stratification.
Agentic pipelines partition ob-
servations along several axes that are typically known at
evaluation time: the tools invoked, the type of query han-
dled, the sub-agents involved, and the retrieval source con-
sulted. Proxy quality varies across these strata in ways that

reasoning may struggle with code-execution traces or with

et al., 2024; Fogliato et al., 2024) runs PPI within each

3. Available proxy uncertainty.
Modern judges can pro-
duce per-sample uncertainty signals: verbalized confidence,
log-probabilities, multiple generation consistency, or ensem-
bles of judges. These signals identify the trajectories on
which the proxy is least reliable, and therefore the trajec-
tories where human annotation is most informative. Ac-
tive Statistical Inference (Zrnic & Cand`es, 2024; Gligori´c
et al., 2025) exploits this by sampling labeling probabilities
proportional to proxy uncertainty, concentrating expensive
expert effort where it carries the most statistical value.
4. Consequential deployment regimes.
Criticial systems
(e.g., clinical decision support, financial advisory, regu-
lated automation, safety-critical autonomy) are also those
where biased point estimates without valid uncertainty have
direct consequences. Distribution-free coverage guaran-
tees are a deployment requirement in these settings. PPI’s
unconditional-on-the-proxy validity makes it well suited to
agentic pipelines that must be auditable.
4. The GLIDE Framework
4.1. Three-step decomposition
GLIDE organizes evaluation around three sequential steps
inherited from classical survey theory: sampling, annota-
tion, and estimation. A small budget of observations (or
trajectories) is selected from a large pool of proxy-labeled
ones (sampling); domain experts produce ground-truth la-
bels on the selected subset (annotation); the labeled subset
and the proxy predictions are combined into a debiased
point estimate with a confidence interval (estimation). Sam-
pling and estimation are statistical and algorithmic; while
annotation is irreducibly task-specific and out of scope for
GLIDE. The library requires only that experts return labels
in a machine-readable form (categories or numbers), whose
business meaning is context-dependent but whose statistical
treatment is not.
This factorization is more than a presentation choice. It
allows samplers and estimators to be developed, tested, and
contributed independently: a contributor proposing a new
sampler need not touch any estimator, and conversely. It
also lets practitioners mix and match across the families
of methods recalled in Section 2, choosing the sampler
that matches their evaluation setup and the estimator that
matches their statistical regime.
4.2. Samplers
GLIDE implements four samplers covering the regimes
identified in Section 3.
UniformSampler draws n observations uniformly without
replacement from the pool. It is the appropriate baseline
when no auxiliary signal is available.
StratifiedSampler allocates n observations across user-
defined strata. Two allocation rules are supported: propor-
tional (nh ∝Nh), and Neyman (nh ∝Nhσh), where σh is
the stratum standard deviation of the proxy labels (Fogliato
et al., 2024). Both allocations produce non-integer val-
ues that GLIDE rounds via Hamilton’s largest-remainder
method to ensure P
h nh = n exactly.
ActiveSampler draws each observation independently with
a Bernoulli probability proportional to a user-supplied un-
certainty score on the proxy . The proportionality constant
is calibrated so that the expected sample size equals the
budget (Zrnic & Cand`es, 2024; Gligori´c et al., 2025). Be-
cause the draws are independent Bernoullis, the realized
sample size matches the budget only in expectation; this is
the standard cost of admitting per-sample probabilities.
CostOptimalSampler comes in two variants.
The
constant-rate variant (CostOptimalRandomSampler)
computes a single sampling probability from a small burn-in
dataset and known proxy/annotation costs, minimizing total
cost (Angelopoulos et al., 2025). The feature-dependent
variant (CostOptimalSampler) additionally consumes
a per-sample uncertainty score, computing observation-level
optimal probabilities π(x). The feature-dependent variant
strictly dominates the constant-rate one when the uncer-
tainty score is informative, but requires an extra signal that
is not always available.

4.5. API
GLIDE follows the conventions of the scientific Python
ecosystem (scipy, scikit-learn).
Estimators are stateful
objects with an estimate method. Samplers expose a
sample method returning a pair (π, ξ): π ∈[0, 1]N holds
the per-observation sampling probabilities, and ξ ∈{0, 1}N
holds the binary inclusion indicators drawn as Bernoulli
realizations of π. Results are dataclasses carrying the point
estimate, the confidence interval at user-specified level 1−α,
the effective sample size neff, and the metric label. A com-
plete end-to-end workflow (sampling, annotation, estima-
tion) fits in fewer than six lines of Python; we provide a
minimal example in Appendix A. New samplers and estima-
tors can be contributed as single-file modules implementing
the sample or estimate interface, lowering the barrier
for the research community to deposit reference implemen-
tations.
5. Empirical Validation
Each estimator implemented in GLIDE is accompanied by a
Monte Carlo validation notebook that establishes three prop-
erties: (i) the empirical coverage of its confidence intervals
matches the nominal level across a range of proxy quality
and confidence levels, (ii) the interval is no wider than the
classical labeled-only baseline at any correlation, and (iii)
the effective sample size neff grows with the proxy/ground-
truth correlation. We describe the protocol and report rep-
resentative results for the PTD estimator; other notebooks
follow similar protocols and yielding consistent conclusions.
The full per-estimator notebooks are released alongside the
library and documentation in the “deep dive” section.

5.3. Interval width and effective sample size
Coverage validity is necessary but not sufficient: we also
want the intervals to be useful. Figures 3 and 4 report in-
terval width and effective sample size as functions of ρ.
The PTD interval width decreases monotonically with ρ,
from ≈0.072 at ρ = 0.1 (essentially matching the labeled-
only baseline of ≈0.073) to ≈0.049 at ρ = 0.9 (a 33%
reduction). Equivalently, the effective sample size grows
from ≈500 at ρ = 0.1 to ≈1100 at ρ = 0.9: 500 human
annotations supplemented with 1000 proxy-labeled obser-
vations are statistically equivalent to roughly 1100 purely
human-labeled observations, a 2.2× effective gain at no
cost to validity. This is the practical return on investment
of Predict-Then-Debias, and it is governed by proxy qual-
ity: the better the LLM-as-judge, the larger the equivalent
annotation budget.

Prediction-powered inference.
The PPI framework was
introduced by Angelopoulos et al. (2023a) and extended
with power tuning in PPI++ (Angelopoulos et al., 2023b).
Subsequent work has covered stratified populations (Fisch
et al., 2024; Fogliato et al., 2024), small-sample boot-
strap procedures (Kluger et al., 2025), active sampling
under proxy uncertainty (Zrnic & Cand`es, 2024; Gligori´c
et al., 2025), cost-optimal annotation budgets (Angelopou-
los et al., 2025), and unified theoretical treatments (Song
et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2025). Related approaches address the
broader problem of using imperfect labels for downstream
inference: design-based supervised learning in social sci-
ence (Egami et al., 2023), AutoEval for synthetic-data evalu-
ation (Boyeau et al., 2024), and prediction-powered e-values
for sequential testing (Csillag et al., 2025). Multi-proxy ag-
gregation has been studied recently in (Cowen-Breen et al.,
2026; Shan et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025); we discuss these
as roadmap targets in Section 8.
Software for PPI and LLM evaluation.
The ppi py
library (Angelopoulos et al., 2023a) established a founda-
tional implementation covering means, GLM coefficients,
and M-estimators across the PPI family. GLIDE comple-
ments it by specializing to mean estimation (the regime cov-
ering most performance and risk metrics in deployed evalu-
ation), integrating recent methods (Stratified PPI, PTD, ASI,
cost-optimal samplers), and adding a reproducible cross-
method validation suite. The GLIDE layer is orthogonal to
LLM evaluation orchestration frameworks (HELM, lm-eval-
harness, DeepEval, RAGAS), which solve the upstream
problem of running evaluations: GLIDE consumes their
outputs (proxy labels and selected human annotations) and
provides the downstream statistical layer that converts them
into debiased estimates with valid confidence intervals.
8. Limitations and Roadmap
Limitations.
GLIDE specializes in mean estimation. This
is a deliberate scope choice: the vast majority of perfor-
mance and risk-control metrics in deployed settings (accu-
racy, relevance rate, hallucination rate, toxicity rate, preci-
sion, recall) are means or proportions, and restricting to this
regime simplifies the codebase, since several methods that
diverge in the general M-estimation setting collapse to the
same estimator when the target is a mean. Other estimands
of practical interest, in particular quantiles, and GLM co-
efficients, are out of scope; ppi py (Angelopoulos et al.,
2023a) remains the appropriate reference for the broader
family. The CLT-based estimators in GLIDE (PPI++, Strat-
ified PPI++, ASI) require, as a rule of thumb, at least fifty
labeled samples per stratum for the asymptotic intervals to
be reliable; below that threshold, the bootstrap-based PTD
variants are the documented fallback. The library currently
assumes a single proxy per observation and i.i.d. labeled
and unlabeled sets drawn from the same population: it does
not provide anytime-valid constructions, does not aggre-
gate predictions from multiple proxies, and does not yet
handle covariate or label shift between the labeled and un-
labeled sets. Finally, the annotation step is intentionally
unmanaged; practitioners must integrate their own labeling
pipeline, which in domain-specific deployments is often the
most labor-intensive component of the workflow.
Roadmap.
Four directions are immediate. Multi-proxy
aggregation (Cowen-Breen et al., 2026; Shan et al., 2025;
Chen et al., 2025; De Bartolomeis et al., 2025) addresses
the common case where several judges (rule-based filters,
small LLMs, large LLMs) are available simultaneously, and
is particularly relevant to multi-judge agentic eval. Anytime-
valid PPI based on prediction-powered e-values (Csillag
et al., 2025) would enable continuous monitoring and prin-
cipled stopping for evaluation pipelines that report metrics
over time. Non-deterministic evaluation extends the frame-
work to the one-input-many-outputs regime characteristic of
stochastic agents, where the open question is how to allocate
budget between input coverage and output replication. Last
but not least, Multi-annotator ground truth addresses the
common situation where several human experts disagree
on the same trajectory, with inter-annotator disagreement
(different experts give different labels) and intra-annotator

9. Conclusion
We presented GLIDE, an open-source Python library for
prediction-powered evaluation of GenAI and agentic sys-
tems. GLIDE specializes in mean estimation, and integrates
modern estimators (PPI++, Stratified PPI++, PTD, Strat-
ified PTD, ASI) and samplers (uniform, stratified, active,
cost-optimal) under a single scipy-style API. A reproducible
Monte Carlo validation suite establishes nominal coverage
across estimators and characterizes their efficiency gains as
a function of proxy quality.
The library makes a practical point alongside its statistical
one: investing in a higher-quality LLM-as-judge translates
directly into a larger effective annotation budget, with the
gain growing monotonically in the proxy/true correlation.
Better proxies are not a substitute for human annotation;
they are a multiplier on it.

Impact Statement
GLIDE provides statistical tools that can inform consequen-
tial decisions about AI deployment, suspension, or audit-
ing. Used responsibly, it contributes to more transparent
and accountable AI evaluation. However, valid confidence
intervals on a poorly chosen or biased metric remain unin-
formative about underlying harms: when applied to strongly
biased evaluation data, GLIDE’s debiasing machinery ad-
dresses proxy bias, not the structural or societal biases the
AI system itself may exhibit. Users should bear this dis-
tinction in mind, particularly in high-stakes settings where
evaluation outputs carry direct operational consequences.

field, while agent messages carry thought (the agent’s internal reasoning) and action (the operation performed).

SYSTEM: {profile}

USER: {content}
AGENT:
[Thought] {thought}
[Action] {action}
...
