Abstract
Canary deployment routes a fraction of traffic to a new software
version, monitors metrics, and rolls back on regression. Mainstream
controllers (Argo Rollouts, Spinnaker, Flagger) change the deployed
system’s cryptographic identity during the canary window. The
drift is harmless for stateless microservices but breaks the claim
that “the agent you certified is still the agent you have” for safety-
critical embodied agents, forcing re-certification per canary. We
present ICAN-Deploy (Identity-stable CANary Deployment), a
middleware construction whose state machine holds the identity
hash invariant across the canary window by separating capability
names (frozen, hashed) from capability versions (mutable runtime
state). We implement ICAN-Deploy inside a runtime governance
layer for LLM-driven robots and verify invariance by closed-form
proof, AST lint, and TLA+ model-checking, then corroborate over
𝑁=100 real canary cycles on a Franka Panda arm in MuJoCo (zero
drift; entry latency 95% BCa CI [1.52, 2.01] ms). A feature-flagged
strawman that folds versions into the manifest falsifies on the same
workload. A system certified once at identity-creation time can then
ship arbitrary capability evolution under that same certification,
within the version-and-name envelope.

Introduction
Canary deployment is the standard cloud middleware pattern for
safely rolling out a new software version. The deployment con-
troller routes a fraction 𝑓∈(0, 1) of traffic to the new version for a
soak window, watches metrics, and either promotes (𝑓→1) or rolls
back (𝑓→0) [Humble and Farley 2010; Sato 2014]. Industry imple-

For stateless cloud microservices the identity drift is harm-

straightforward. For safety-critical embodied agents (robots whose
certification, operator trust, and audit chain depend on a stable
cryptographic identity), the same drift breaks a load-bearing claim:
“the agent you certified is still the agent you have” [International
Electrotechnical Commission 2010; International Organization for
Standardization 2011, 2015]. Re-certification per canary becomes
the safest practice and is prohibitive for regulated deployments.
For LLM-driven robots [Ahn et al. 2022; Driess et al. 2023; Liang
et al. 2023] the drift compounds further: the planner reasons about
“what the robot can do” [Anthropic 2025; OpenAI 2024], and canary
changes break that mental model in subtle, often undiagnosable,
ways. Re-training the planner does not apply at canary speed. The
drift is not exotic. It is the design intent of every existing canary
middleware. The middleware question this paper asks is whether
canary deployment can be reconstructed so that identity is invariant
by construction.
We present a construction whose identity is invariant across
canary transitions even while behaviour evolves. It relies on a
type-level separation between capability names (frozen and hashed
into the identity manifest at agent creation time) and capability
versions (mutable runtime state that no hash sees). A canary that
changes only versions cannot change identity. A canary that wants
to change identity is no longer a canary but an agent re-keying
event, surfaced separately at the API boundary. We integrate the
construction into AEROS, an existing runtime governance layer for

Background
Cluster-management middleware. The canary pattern is one of
several deployment-time controls that middleware platforms expose
to operators of distributed systems. Beneath them is a lineage of
cluster-management systems (Borg [Verma et al. 2015], its successor
design Omega, and Kubernetes [Burns et al. 2016], with Facebook’s
Twine [Tang et al. 2020] as a recent representative) that share a

common property: the deployed identity of a job or pod is the
digest of its container image and configuration, and any rollout is
by definition an identity change. Canary controllers built on top
(Argo Rollouts [Argo Project Authors 2024], Spinnaker [Spinnaker
Community 2023], Flagger [Flagger Authors 2024]) inherit this
assumption. The assumption is correct for stateless workloads. It is

Canary deployment. A canary routes a fraction 𝑓∈(0, 1) of
traffic to a new version for a soak window of duration 𝑡, monitors
metrics, and either promotes (𝑓→1) or rolls back (𝑓→0) [Hum-

attestations [Newman et al. 2022; Torres-Arias et al. 2019]) during
the soak window: before the canary, image v1.0.0; during, both;
after, v1.1.0.
AEROS: runtime governance for embodied agents. We position
the construction relative to AEROS, an existing runtime gover-
nance layer for embodied LLM agents. AEROS partitions agent

ployed and updated independently, and ships a governed-evolution
pipeline [Xue et al. 2026a] that handles ECM updates through a
synchronous validator / sandbox / shadow / rollback sequence.
What AEROS does not address, and what motivates this paper,
is identity invariance during the canary window: the version-map
mutations its evolution pipeline performs change the cryptographic
identity hash by construction. Our construction is orthogonal to
AEROS’ governance machinery and could in principle bolt onto
any runtime layer that exposes a similar capability registry; we use
AEROS as the deployment target because its evolution pipeline is
the closest existing baseline for embodied-agent capability rollout.

Why naive canary breaks identity. The simplest canary construc-
tion puts the active version map V into the manifest hashed by
identity_hash, mirroring container deployments where the running
image digest is part of identity. Every canary then toggles V, which
changes identity_hash, which breaks the invariant in equation (1).

nary refer to different identity hashes, so audit chains require per-
event identity resolution. Re-certification per canary becomes the
safest practice, and prohibitive for regulated deployments. The con-
struction in §3 resolves this tension by separating canary behaviour
from canary identity drift.
3
Design
3.1
Name-Set vs. Version-Map
Our construction relies on a type-level separation between three
data structures defined on the bridge process, each fixed as either
mutable or immutable:
Definition 1 (Capability name set). N = {𝑛1,𝑛2, . . . ,𝑛𝑘}, lexico-
graphically sorted strings. Frozen at agent construction time and
stored in the identity manifest. Hashed into ℎregistry.
Definition 2 (Active version map). V : N →semver. Mutable
runtime state on the bridge.
Not hashed into identity_hash.
Definition 3 (Provisional version map). V′ : N →semver, partial.
Set on entry to canary_running for one capability; cleared on
entry to any terminal state. Not hashed into identity_hash.
The invariant we want: for any deployment-pipeline transition
𝑇, ℎregistry is unchanged. Capability names enter and leave N only
through explicit register / deregister operations, which are not
canary-pipeline operations.
3.2
State Machine
Figure 1 gives the eight-state pipeline. Every canary upgrade tra-
verses the same machine, exercising at most seven transitions per
run; the * →rolled_back edge denotes a family of transitions from
any of the canary states (canary_running, canary_promoted)
on metric violation, not a single edge. The seven transitions come
in three flavours:
• Pure bookkeeping: advances the job phase only, and does not
touch V, V′, or any identity input. E.g., pending →validating.
• Provisional: writes to V′ but not to V or any identity input.
E.g., shadow_passed →canary_running.
• Atomic flip: writes both V and V′ in a single critical section
guarded by an asyncio.Lock, and does not touch any identity
input. E.g., canary_promoted →promoted.
The "identity input touched?" column of Table 1 spells out, for
each transition, exactly which fields the transition writes. By in-
spection of the relevant source (evolution_pipeline.py; pointers
in §4), every transition either modifies V, V′, the audit-chain log,
or the in-memory EvolutionJobStore, and never modifies any of
the inputs to identity_hash in equation (1).

Worked Example

v1.0.0 that operators wish to canary-upgrade to v1.1.0. The
agent’s manifest contains N={grasp, place} and active versions
V={grasp↦→v1.0.0, place↦→v1.0.0}. We trace the canary cycle

The identity-hash prefix is the same eight bytes at every row,
which is the byte-level reading of Property 1. The two struc-

which writes V′ but not V and not the manifest, and ca-

under asyncio.Lock. The supply-chain attestation pointing at the
agent identity hash continues to point at the same digest after the
upgrade as before; the operator’s certificate remains valid; the audit
chain does not require per-event identity reconciliation.
3.7
Rollback Semantics
The construction is symmetric with respect to failure: when a
canary rolls back at the end of the soak window, the bridge clears
V′, leaves V at its pre-canary value, records the rollback in the audit
log, and (by Property 1) identity remains invariant. The challenging
case is a crash during the canary: the metrics provider raises an
exception, a bug in a policy hook fires, or a network partition severs
the bridge from the executor. A naive implementation would mark
the job failed with V′ still set, leaving the provisional version
active even though the canary has terminated.
Our integration adds a try/except around the canary body
that calls the rollback closure before re-raising; the path is shown
explicitly as Algorithm 1, lines 14–16, and exercised by the
test_crash_rolls_back integration test (one of 35 canary-related
tests; results in §5.4).
3.8
Why Not Re-Key on Every Canary?
A natural alternative to identity-stable canary is to admit that
canary changes identity, and to re-key the agent (and re-issue all
its supply-chain attestations) on every canary cycle. We refer to
this design as rotation-on-update, in contrast to ICAN-Deploy’s
invariance-under-update.
Rotation-on-update is the design adopted by Uptane for auto-
motive over-the-air updates [Kuppusamy et al. 2016], and implicit
in supply-chain identity tools such as sigstore [Newman et al. 2022]
and in-toto [Torres-Arias et al. 2019] that bind identity at artefact-
build time and treat every new artefact as a new identity. The
advantage is conceptual simplicity: the deployed identity is always
the digest of the running bill of materials, with no separate notion
of “canary state”.
The disadvantage is that every downstream system that depends
on agent identity must be told about the rotation and must update
its local view. For an autonomous-driving fleet of ∼106 agents each
undergoing ∼1 canary upgrade per week, that is ∼106 identity
rotations per week, each of which must propagate through the
operator’s certification database, the insurer’s audit chain, the reg-
ulator’s filing record, and the fleet’s own internal trust graph. The
propagation is slow (certification revalidation is typically hours to
days) and the window between rotation and propagation is exactly
the window in which the agent’s identity is, from a downstream
perspective, ambiguous. Uptane’s design accepts this cost because
automotive ECUs upgrade infrequently and the certification regime
is built around rotation. Embodied agents driven by VLA poli-
cies [Brohan et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2024] upgrade frequently (every
retraining, every demo collection, every fine-tune iteration), which
makes the rotation cost prohibitive.
ICAN-Deploy inverts the trade-off. Identity is held invariant
across capability-version canaries; the propagation cost is paid
only when the name set, persona, or policy files are touched (§3.5),
and the operator schedules each such event. Within the version
envelope the agent is the same agent, so downstream systems can
take its identity as given.
4
Integration with the AEROS Bridge
Bridge architecture. The AEROS bridge (§2) sits between an
LLM planner and a robot executor (Figure 2). It is a FastAPI
application with an asyncio event loop that runs admission,
policy checking, contract verification, and runtime safety, and
writes results to an executor backend (simulator or hardware).
Our identity-stable canary construction lives inside the evolution
pipeline of this existing bridge, not beside it: when an opera-
tor issues POST /api/evolution/upgrade, the bridge builds an
EvolutionPipeline closure parameterised by bridge state, dis-
patches it to the asyncio loop, and returns HTTP 202 with a polling
URL. The state machine of §3 drives the closure to one of three
terminal states.
Pipeline driver and job store. EvolutionPipeline is a ∼400-
line asyncio class with each transition of Table 1 as a private
coroutine method, parameterised at request time by four closures:
validator() (persona-driven semver gating plus contract compat-
ibility [Xue et al. 2026a]), promote_fn (writes V′[𝑛]), rollback_fn
(clears V′[𝑛], reverts V[𝑛] if necessary), and metrics_provider
(returns a structured CanaryMetrics). Job lifecycle state lives in
an in-memory asyncio.Lock-guarded dictionary; we explicitly
chose not to make canary jobs durable, since restart-and-resume
across a bridge restart admits identity-unsafe semantics and the
soak window is short enough to make restart during a canary a
tolerable exception.

Integration with cloud canary controllers. The bridge exposes
a small HTTP surface that lets existing canary controllers drive
the construction without source modification. The relevant end-

job.status field which advances through Table 1 states), and POST

the rollout pipeline: the existing canary controller continues to man-

policy), and the AEROS bridge (now extended with our identity-
stable state machine) takes care of the identity-invariant write set
on the agent side. The bridge does not duplicate rollout-strategy
logic; it guarantees the structural property “the agent identity hash
does not drift” regardless of what strategy the controller chooses.

We inject a ZeroDivisionError into the metrics provider at

Audit-chain ambiguity. Episodic memory entries written during

hashes was current at write time, so post-cycle audit reconstruction
must per-event resolve which identity owned which entry. Under
ICAN-Deploy, all entries written during the cycle refer to the same
hash, and audit reconstruction is a single-identity query.
6
Discussion and Limitations
Identity invariance is not behavioural invariance. A capability at
v1.42.0 may behave arbitrarily differently from v1.0.0 even though
identity is unchanged: ICAN-Deploy transfers trust from bill of
materials to name set, so a grasp v1.42.0 with intact ECM Contract
but a more aggressive torque profile is invisible to identity yet
real to physics. Name-level trust must be supplied by the persona
validator (§3.5) and the AEROS contract layer that gates capability
invocation beyond identity-level checks.
Deployment middleware design choices. ICAN-Deploy’s construc-
tion occupies one corner of a small design space. The two orthog-
onal axes are: what is hashed into identity (build artefact alone,
build plus version map, build plus registry plus version map) and


## Page 9

when identity is permitted to change (every rollout, only at re-keying
events, never). Cluster-management middleware historically picks
“build artefact alone, every rollout” [Burns et al. 2016; Verma et al.
2015]. Sigstore and in-toto operate at “build artefact, every artefact
issuance” [Newman et al. 2022; Torres-Arias et al. 2019]. Uptane
adds “every targets-metadata rotation” [Kuppusamy et al. 2016].
ICAN-Deploy selects “registry plus persona, only at explicit re-
keying”, which is the unique corner where canary deployment is
identity-stable by construction. The choice is appropriate when the
deployment target has a load-bearing identity claim downstream of
deployment (audit chain, operator certification, regulatory filing).
For workloads without such a claim the trade-off inverts and the
cluster-manager default is correct.
Fleet-scale integration. The construction’s per-canary cost is a
constant number of dictionary writes per agent, independent of
fleet size. For a fleet of 𝐾agents undergoing simultaneous canary
upgrades of the same capability name, the orchestrator issues 𝐾
independent calls to POST /api/evolution/upgrade; each agent’s
bridge runs its own state machine to completion in parallel, with
no cross-agent coordination required by Property 1. The identity-
stability guarantee is per-agent because each agent’s manifest is
per-agent, and the fleet-level guarantee is the quantification of
the per-agent property over agents. Federated re-keying for name-
set additions does require cross-agent coordination and is a sep-
arate operation handled outside the identity-stable canary path;
for capability-version canaries (the most frequent operation) the
construction remains 𝑂(1) per agent.
6.1
Future Work
The construction has been validated in MuJoCo simulation with
single-arm Franka Panda workloads. Four extensions are natural
follow-up directions, listed in the order in which we expect them
to ship.
Physical-hardware rollouts. The construction is platform-
agnostic in principle because it operates on the agent identity
manifest rather than on robot kinematics or perception models.
We expect the canary-entry latency reported in Table 3 to remain
∼ms-scale on physical Franka and similar arms, because the
identity-affecting code path is in-process Python with no network
or hardware-bus latency. The full-pipeline latency is dominated
by the configurable soak window and is therefore insensitive to
platform. The first hardware rollout would target a single physical
Franka and replicate the 𝑁=100 canary cycle protocol against real
metrics; the comparison metric we expect to remain stable is the
qualitative “1 vs 3 distinct hashes per cycle” contrast (Figure 4a,
Figure 4c), which is a structural property of the construction
independent of the physical platform.
Cross-platform validation. Beyond Franka, we expect the con-
struction to apply to any embodied-agent runtime that exposes
a stable identity manifest: ROS 2-based stacks [Macenski et al.
2022; Open Robotics 2018] via the manifest abstraction, AUTOSAR-
compliant ECUs [AUTOSAR Consortium 2023] via a similar map-
ping. Validation on ROS 2 in particular is appealing because the
lifecycle node abstraction maps cleanly to our state machine. Cross-
platform validation is a question of porting the bridge implementa-
tion (§4), not of revisiting the construction.
Production webhook integration with Argo Rollouts. The bridge
already exposes the HTTP endpoints that an Argo Rollouts hook
would call (§4). What remains is to ship a worked example of a
rollouts-controller-driven ICAN-Deploy canary against a public
benchmark workload, and to report on the operational ergonomics:
how operators perceive the additional constraint that “identity is
invariant”, whether the constraint catches real-world misconfigura-
tion, and how the audit log integrates with the operator’s existing
observability stack.
Federated re-keying protocol. Name-set additions and persona
edits trigger a certifier re-key (§3.5). For a fleet of 𝐾agents, the
re-key must be either fleet-wide-synchronous (all agents transition
at once, briefly creating a fleet-wide window of dual identity), or
agent-by-agent-asynchronous (each agent transitions on its own
schedule, briefly creating a heterogeneous fleet identity). Neither
is obviously preferable; the choice depends on the operator’s au-
dit chain and the regulator’s filing cadence. A future paper will
work out a federated re-keying protocol with explicit liveness and
consistency guarantees over the fleet.
6.2
Threats to Validity
We use the four-quadrant scheme of empirical software engineering
to enumerate the risks to the contribution.
Internal validity. The most direct risk is that Property 1 is tauto-
logically satisfied because we chose which fields enter the manifest.
The static lint and the TLA+ check both check exactly that property;
both pass; the proof is also structural over the same field set. Three
independent corroborations of a tautology are still a tautology. We
mitigate this in two ways. First, the strawman (§5.4) is the same
code with a single feature flag flipped, and on the same workload
it loses the property; so the property is not vacuous, it is a real
constraint that is broken by a small perturbation of the field set.
Second, the canary-emitting transitions in Table 1 are exhaustive
of methods on EvolutionPipeline that write the job-status field:
a separate AST lint asserts no other method does so, so the seven-
transition cover is closed under the implementation rather than
chosen for convenience.
External validity. We evaluate on a single physical platform
(Franka Panda 7-DOF arm in MuJoCo simulation [Franka Robotics
2024; Todorov et al. 2012]) with a single planner family (LLM-
driven). The construction is agnostic to the platform because it
operates on the agent identity manifest, not on robot kinematics
or perception models; we expect generalisation to other VLA poli-
cies [Brohan et al. 2023; Kim et al. 2024] and to non-LLM planners.
The construction does not generalise to deployment targets without
a load-bearing identity claim downstream of deployment; for those
the cluster-manager default is correct (§6, “Deployment middleware
design choices”).
Construct validity. We measure distinct identity hashes per cycle
(Figure 4a, 4c) as a proxy for the structural property “identity is
invariant”. The two are equivalent under our hash construction

Related Work
Cluster-management middleware. The middleware design space
within which canary deployment sits has a deep lineage.
Borg pioneered large-scale declarative cluster management at
Google [Verma et al. 2015], the Borg/Omega/Kubernetes retrospec-
tive [Burns et al. 2016] explains the design evolution toward declara-
tive orchestration, and Twine [Tang et al. 2020] reports comparable
ground at Facebook. All three treat the running container’s image
digest as the canonical deployment identity; rollouts are deliberately
identity-changing operations. ICAN-Deploy is orthogonal: it does
not propose a new cluster manager but a new canary state machine
that sits above any of these orchestrators. The novelty is in what is
hashed into identity, not in how the deployment is scheduled. The
construction’s per-canary cost is a constant number of dictionary
writes per agent, independent of fleet size [Bondi 2000], so identity
stability composes with whatever placement and scheduling policy
the underlying cluster manager implements.
Canary deployment and progressive delivery. The canary pattern
descends from a long line of progressive-delivery work [Humble
and Farley 2010; Sato 2014], implemented in industry at the load
balancer [Flagger Authors 2024], the deployment controller [Argo
Project Authors 2024; Spinnaker Community 2023], or as feature
flags. None of these tools preserves a cryptographic identity across
the canary window; all treat “the deployed bill of materials has
changed” as the canary’s defining property.
Robot deployment. ROS 2 [Macenski et al. 2022; Open Robotics
2018] and AUTOSAR [AUTOSAR Consortium 2023] cover the
lifecycle and safety-bound deployment vocabulary respectively;
neither preserves a cryptographic identity. Xue et al. [2026a] is the
closest predecessor (governed evolution with validator, sandbox,
shadow, rollback); ICAN-Deploy isolates the canary state from the
identity manifest entirely.
Cryptographic identity, supply chain, and LLM agents. Repro-
ducible builds [Reproducible Builds Project 2024], sigstore [New-
man et al. 2022], in-toto [Torres-Arias et al. 2019], package-manager
attacks [Cappos et al. 2008], and Uptane [Kuppusamy et al. 2016],
the closest analogue, establish identity at artefact-build time via
Merkle digests. Uptane rotates identity by re-signing targets meta-
data on every update; ICAN-Deploy holds the manifest invariant
across updates instead, so the agent’s attestation does not need to
be re-issued each canary cycle. The LLM-agent literature [Ahn et al.
2022; Anthropic 2025; Driess et al. 2023; Liang et al. 2023; OpenAI
2024; Wang et al. 2024] exposes tool-use surfaces and runtime skill
libraries but does not treat agent identity as a hashable, regulator-
facing artefact; the construction of Xue et al. [2026b] provides the
cryptographic identity foundation this paper builds on.
Runtime assurance and Simplex. The closest conceptual an-
cestor is Simplex [Seto et al. 1998; Sha 2001], generalised to
aerospace [Schierman et al. 2020] and safe RL [Brunke et al. 2022].
Simplex retains behavioural authority through redundancy; ICAN-
Deploy retains identity-level authority through the manifest hash.
The two are complementary deliverables on the same deployment
safety case.
State machines, fault injection, governance. The provisional-then-
commit pattern is a classical database-transactions device [Gray
1981; Papadimitriou 1979]; TLA+ [Lamport 1994] provides the
formal-methods framing; the spec excerpt in §3.4 mechanically
checks Property 1 over a finite cover of the seven-transition state
machine. The crash experiment in §5.4 is in the chaos-engineering
tradition [Alvaro et al. 2015; Basiri et al. 2016; Bennett and Tseitlin
2014]. Functional-safety standards [International Electrotechnical
Commission 2010; International Organization for Standardization
2011, 2015] motivate the regulatory continuity argument in §1.
Table 4 positions ICAN-Deploy against the most adjacent
deployment-middleware and supply-chain-identity tools on five
dimensions that matter for safety-critical embodied agents. The
salient pattern is that no existing tool combines identity-stable
canary with structural verification of the property; ICAN-Deploy
is the unique corner that does.
The three cluster-side canary controllers (Argo Rollouts, Spin-
naker, Flagger) all support rich rollout strategies (traffic weighting,
metric-driven decision, multi-cluster orchestration) and are mature
production systems. They are not designed to preserve identity
across the rollout. Their rollback semantics are controller-level: a
rollback is a controller decision that re-points traffic at the prior
version, but the deployed bill of materials and its identity hash
remain ambiguous during the canary window. ICAN-Deploy is
complementary rather than competitive: an operator running Argo
Rollouts or Flagger can call ICAN-Deploy from a webhook step
(§4) to add the identity invariance guarantee without giving up the
controller’s rollout strategy.
The two supply-chain identity tools (sigstore, in-toto) operate at
artefact-build time, not at deployment time, and so are orthogonal
to canary deployment in the sense that they neither support nor
obstruct it. They are useful as inputs to ICAN-Deploy: an agent’s
build attestation, signed by sigstore at build time and linked through
an in-toto attestation graph, becomes a stable input to the identity
manifest that ICAN-Deploy holds invariant across canaries. The
invariance argument depends on the manifest input being well-
formed; sigstore and in-toto are how an operator gets a well-formed
input in the first place.
Uptane is the closest direct analogue and the only entry in
Table 4 that addresses identity at deployment time. Its design is

rotation-on-update (§3.8): every update rotates the targets-metadata

ICAN-Deploy inverts this trade-off and holds the manifest invariant
under capability-version updates, paying the rotation cost only at

The two designs are appropriate for different update frequencies:

Conclusion
We presented identity-stable canary deployment: hashing capability
names but not versions holds the agent’s cryptographic identity in-
variant across the canary window. The construction is corroborated
by proof, TLA+, AST lint, a 104-seed fuzzer, 𝑁=100 real MuJoCo
cycles (canary entry 1.7 ms, BCa CI [1.52, 2.01]), and a matched
strawman-flag falsifier.
