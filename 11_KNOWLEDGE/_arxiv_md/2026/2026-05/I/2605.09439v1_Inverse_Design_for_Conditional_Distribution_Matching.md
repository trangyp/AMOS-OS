Abstract
Generative models are powerful tools for sampling from a learned distribution
P(Y | X), and inverse-design methods invert this map to find an input x that
produces a desired point output y∗. However, many design goals are naturally
distributional rather than pointwise, incorporating the inherent uncertainty of Y
and targeting a specific form for it, a task not addressed by standard inverse design.
To address this issue we introduce Conditional Distribution Matching (CDM)1,
a new inverse-design problem class in generative modeling: given a joint distri-
bution P(X, Y ) and a target distribution G(Y ), find an input x∗whose induced
conditional distribution P(Y | X = x∗) matches G. We formally define two
variants: Conditional Distribution Matching Sampling (CDMS) and Conditional
Distribution Matching Optimization (CDMO). To solve these problems, we propose
MLGD-F (Matching-Loss Guided Diffusion with a Fast inner sampler), a plug-
and-play inference-time algorithm that combines a pretrained score-based diffusion
model with a pretrained fast conditional sampler, requiring no additional training
or fine-tuning. By leveraging single-step conditional sampling, MLGD-F enables
tractable gradient computation, making the estimation of P(Y | X) both memory-
efficient and computationally lightweight. We validate MLGD-F on synthetic
benchmarks, structured image transformations, and generative editing optimization,
demonstrating reliable recovery of inputs whose conditional distributions match
diverse user-specified targets, including discrete mixtures and continuous low-rank
supports.

Introduction
Generative models have emerged as a cornerstone of modern machine learning, achieving remarkable
results in image synthesis, voice generation, and time-series modeling [19, 39, 43]. The paradigm
has evolved from unconditional generation, learning to sample from an unknown data distribu-
tion P(X) [17, 33, 34], toward increasingly controlled generation. Conditional generation allows
sampling x0 ∼P(X | Y = y) for a user-specified label y [9, 16], loss-guided methods [6, 35]
generate samples that minimize a given objective L : X →R while remaining faithful to the data
distribution. A natural instantiation of loss-guided generation is inverse design: find an input x such
that a forward learned model f(x) is close to a desired point output y∗[31], successfully applied
in protein engineering [8] and drug discovery [13]. However, this paradigm is inherently limited to
point targets: it cannot express goals where the desired outcome is a distribution over outputs rather
than a single value.
In many modern settings the mapping from input to output is mediated by a complex generative
process P(Y | X) that we cannot modify: a pretrained diffusion model with billions of parameters,
a physical process such as protein folding, or a black-box simulator. The only lever the user

Problem Formulation
We consider a joint distribution P(X, Y ) over paired random variables, where X denotes a variable
of interest and Y an associated variable. Let ∥· ∥denote a distance metric between probability
distributions (e.g., the Maximum Mean Discrepancy, MMD, or the Sliced Wasserstein Distance,
SWD).

Given a target distribution G(Y ), the ideal target is the tilted distribution

Background and Related Work
Diffusion models.
Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) [17, 33] define a forward pro-
cess that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data x0 over T steps, producing a sequence x0, x1, . . . , xT ,
and learn a reverse process that denoises xT ∼N(0, I) back to a sample x0 from the data distri-
bution. Throughout we use this notation: xt is the noisy state at timestep t; the forward marginal
has the closed form q(xt | x0) = √¯αt x0 + √1 −¯αt ϵ with ϵ ∼N(0, I) and noise schedule

Few-step diffusion models.
Standard diffusion models require 30–1000 sequential denoising
steps at inference time [17, 34], motivating research into faster inference strategies. One direction
is consistency models [36, 38], which enforce self-consistency along the probability-flow ODE
trajectory without requiring a pretrained teacher. Another direction is diffusion distillation, which
compresses a pretrained diffusion model into a student that generates samples in one or very few
steps [30, 32]. We exploit the single-step property common to both families to make inner-loop
conditional sampling tractable at every denoising step of the outer diffusion process.
Loss-guided sampling.
Diffusion Posterior Sampling (DPS) [6] and Loss-Guided Diffusion
(LGD) [35] modify the reverse diffusion process by incorporating gradient information from a
differentiable loss function L(x0). At each denoising step, the score is augmented by ∇xtL(ˆx0),
where ˆx0 is an estimate of the clean sample obtained via Tweedie’s formula. LGD further stabilises
the gradient by averaging over multiple Monte Carlo Tweedie estimates. This framework enables
flexible, zero-shot guidance for a wide range of objectives without retraining the generative prior.
Distribution matching.
A large family of generative methods trains a parametric generator so that
its output distribution matches a target G, using losses based on Maximum Mean Discrepancy [21, 22],
Optimal Transport / Sinkhorn divergences [12], These approaches require differentiating through the
generator and updating its weights, making them expensive and target-specific: a new G requires
retraining. Our setting is orthogonal: the generative pipeline (both the diffusion prior and the
conditional model fϕ) is frozen, and we instead optimise a single input x∗such that P(Y | X = x∗)
matches G. No generator weights are updated, no target-specific training is required, and the method
operates entirely at inference time.
Terminological note.
The phrase "conditional distribution matching" has prior unrelated uses.
In the domain-adaptation literature [41] it refers to aligning the source and target conditionals
Psrc(X | Y ) and Ptgt(X | Y ) across two datasets so that a classifier learned on one transfers to the
other. In concurrent work on discrete-diffusion distillation [11] it refers to a training objective in
which a student model is trained to match a pretrained teacher’s reverse conditional transitions. Our
use is unrelated to either: we study the inverse-design problem of finding an input x∗whose induced
conditional P(Y | X = x∗) matches a user-specified target G(Y ), with a single fixed generative
process. The three problems share only a phrase, not a setup or method.
4
Methodology
MLGD (Matching-Loss Guided Diffusion) specialises Loss-Guided Diffusion [35] to the conditional
distribution matching setting (Problems 1–2) by combining the pretrained score network sθ for
P(X) with a conditional sampler fϕ for P(Y | X). When fϕ is a few-step sampler, we refer to the
instantiation as MLGD-F; MLGD is the broader framework that also covers slower choices of fϕ.
The method has two components: an outer loop that performs loss-guided reverse diffusion with a
black-box loss L(x) (the LGD skeleton), and an inner estimator that evaluates and differentiates the
distribution-matching loss L(x) = ∥P(Y | X = x) −G(Y )∥at each outer step. The inner estimator
is the paper’s algorithmic contribution.
The two components at a glance.
Algorithm 1 is the standard LGD skeleton with an abstract black-
box loss L(x): at each reverse-diffusion step we form Tweedie’s estimate ˆx0, query the loss, and add
its gradient (scaled by step size ζt) to the denoising update. Algorithm 2 specifies what we plug into
that black box (the paper’s algorithmic contribution) via a fast differentiable conditional sampler fϕ.
For any query x we draw ncond samples from fϕ(x, ·), compare them to a fixed set of target samples
SG ∼G using a distributional distance (e.g. MMD or SWD), and propagate the gradient through
fϕ. The full detailed version, including Monte-Carlo perturbations around ˆx0 used to stabilise the
gradient estimate, is given as Algorithm 3 in Appendix D. We write DDIM_STEP(xt, sθ, t) for one
deterministic reverse-DDIM update [34]; the explicit form is given in Algorithm 3.

Why a fast sampler.
The choice of a fast model for fϕ is deliberate. Because the inner estimator
is invoked at every one of the T outer denoising steps, a standard K⋆-step diffusion model would
multiply the sampling cost by T · K⋆· ncond and, more critically, would require backpropagating
through a K⋆-step unrolled chain at each outer step, rendering gradient computation memory-
intensive and effectively intractable. A single-step fϕ (Ks=1) keeps both cost and activation memory
lightweight; we measure the resulting memory gap in Section 5.4.

5
Experiments
Our experiments address two core properties of MLGD-F: its capacity to optimize over high-
dimensional input manifolds X, and its scalability to semantically complex output spaces Y . We
evaluate across three settings of increasing scale: synthetic Mixture of Gaussians (Section 5.1), where
low-dimensional X and Y allow verification against known ground-truth optima; an MNIST rotation
task (Section 5.2), which scales X to image space (R784) while keeping Y small; and a Stable
Diffusion image optimization (Section 5.3), which scales both X and Y to high-dimensional spaces.
5.1
Synthetic Simulations
We compare MLGD-F against MLGD with a slow diffusion sampler on Mixture of Gaussians across
multiple dimensionalities. We run 25 optimisation instances and select the top-10 by final loss
(Problem 2). Reporting top-k reflects how multistart optimisation is used in practice: a user running
R restarts returns the best result by the same objective, so top-k captures achievable quality under
finite restart budgets. We also report all-25 statistics to characterise variance across seeds. Full
models and training details are provided in Appendix A.
As shown in Figure 1, MLGD-F recovers near-optimal solutions with an 11× speedup over MLGD
(4.03s vs. 45.58s for the representative run shown; full per-dimensionality timing across all 25 runs
is reported in Appendix A). Notably, ECDF analysis (Figure 4) reveals that MLGD-F’s relative
performance improves with dimensionality. We attribute this to the trade-off between sampler
fidelity and gradient variance: while MLGD with a slow sampler uses a more accurate conditional
model, backpropagating through its unrolled K⋆-step chain accumulates variance that scales poorly
with dimension. MLGD-F’s single-step model provides a cleaner gradient signal, which becomes
the decisive factor in high-dimensional settings (Appendix A.8). The β-sweep in Figure 1 (bottom)
empirically illustrates the sampling-to-optimisation interpolation of Problem 1: as β increases from 0
to large values, MLGD-F transitions from sampling the prior P to concentrating near arg minx L(x).
5.2
MNIST: Rotated Digits
We apply MLGD-F to an image-space problem using the MNIST dataset [20], where X ∈R784 is a
digit image and Y ∈R2 is its rotation angle encoded as (cos θ, sin θ); a standard diffusion model
serves as P(X) and a consistency model as fϕ for P(Y | X). The goal is to find x∗such that
P(Y | X = x∗) ≈G for various user-specified target distributions G.
Since the unconditional model can generate any digit at any orientation, the optimization searches
over the full joint space of digit identity and rotation. We present the top-5 results by final loss across
15 runs (Figure 2, and Table 1); full qualitative results are provided in Appendix B.6. These runs are
approximate samples from the β-tempered posterior Qβ(x) ∝P(x)e−βL(x) (Problem 1), and the
digit-class distribution in Table 1 is the empirical realisation of this posterior. While the objective
is distributional and agnostic to digit identity, MLGD-F recovers semantically coherent digits that
satisfy the orientation constraint G: symmetric digits dominate under the uniform target (digit 0) and
bimodal target (narrow 0, 1, 8, 6, 9), while oriented digits (2, 3, 7) emerge under the unimodal target.

Architectural Parity and Scaling.
To isolate the impact of the denoising step count, we observe
that our setup utilizes an identical architectural backbone across both configurations: the UNet
(≈2.6B parameters), ControlNet [44], and CLIP text encoders [29] remain constant. Notably, SDXL-
Turbo is obtained by distilling SDXL-Base with the architecture unchanged - the two models differ
only in their weights, not their structure [32]. Consequently, the computational cost of a single
forward pass is equivalent across both, and all observed differences in memory and speed are a direct
consequence of the denoising step count alone.
The observed differences in memory and speed are thus a direct consequence of the computation
graph depth. Since SDXL Base requires K⋆= 30 steps for high-quality reconstruction compared
to Ks = 2 for SDXL Turbo, the resulting gradient graph is fundamentally 15× deeper. To manage
memory, one can employ gradient checkpointing; however, while this technique avoids storing
intermediate activations, the overhead of maintaining the computation graph nodes for each of the K
denoising steps still scales linearly with the number of iterations.

Conclusion
We introduced Conditional Distribution Matching (CDM), a new problem class that generalizes
inverse design to the distributional setting. Rather than seeking an input x that produces a specific
output y∗, CDM seeks an input x∗whose entire induced conditional distribution P(Y | X = x∗)
matches a user-specified target G(Y ). To our knowledge, this problem class has not been explicitly
studied before. We formalized two variants, CDMS and CDMO, and introduced MLGD-F to solve
them at inference time without any training or fine-tuning.
A central advantage of the plug-and-play design is that MLGD-F directly inherits quality gains
from improvements in the underlying pretrained models: as stronger diffusion priors and few-step
conditional samplers become available, optimization quality improves automatically. The few-step
model is essential for tractability: as demonstrated in Section 5.4, replacing a single-step sampler
with a standard multi-step diffusion model increases peak memory by nearly an order of magnitude
and runtime from hours to days, rendering the method infeasible at scale.
Empirically, we validated MLGD-F across settings of increasing scale and complexity, from synthetic
Mixture of Gaussians, through an MNIST rotation task, to a large-scale Stable Diffusion image editing
pipeline, demonstrating reliable recovery of inputs whose induced conditional distributions match
diverse user-specified targets, including discrete mixtures and continuous low-rank submanifolds.
Limitations.
Optimization quality is bounded by the fidelity of the pretrained distilled model —
if the conditional sampler fϕ poorly approximates P(Y | X), the guidance gradient will be biased
regardless of the number of runs. The stochastic nature of the inner-loop loss estimation means
multiple independent runs are often needed to find a good solution, which multiplies the compute
cost. Runtime at large scale may be prohibitive for latency-sensitive applications. Finally, the method
currently requires the conditional model to be differentiable end-to-end with respect to the input x;
non-differentiable or black-box conditional models would require gradient-free extensions.
Future Work.
CDM opens a research direction of optimizing over induced conditional distributions,
one we hope will inspire dedicated solvers, tighter theoretical guarantees, and new application
domains. This work focuses on continuous input spaces; an important direction is extending CDM
to discrete spaces. A complementary direction is replacing the LGD-style approximate sampler
(Remark 2) by an asymptotically exact corrector for the outer loop, twisted SMC, MCMC, or
Feynman–Kac reweighting, to obtain Qβ samples with quantifiable bias at the cost of extra compute.
Finally, an intriguing question is whether few-step samplers offer advantages beyond speed: their
single-step structure may yield cleaner gradient signals compared to unrolled multi-step chains, as
suggested by Appendix A.8.
Broader Impact.
CDM opens both beneficial and potentially harmful applications. Distributional
control can produce diverse and targeted outputs on complex models: for instance, the Stable
Diffusion experiment (Section 5.3) demonstrates how MLGD-F can enforce demographic balance
in generated portraits, a direct application to bias reduction. However, since G is defined with great
freedom, the same mechanism could be misused to deliberately steer frozen pipelines toward harmful
distributions. Since MLGD-F operates on frozen models, existing safety mechanisms remain fully
active.

edge on L2 GMM, and MLGD-F dominates in 10D. We conjecture this reflects two opposing error
sources that scale differently with dimensionality, both identified formally in Appendix E. The

still matches or outperforms LGD in 10D, underscoring that gradient quality rather than sampler

low-to-intermediate dimensions the distillation error dominates, as seen in 5D, while in 10D the
gradient variance term takes over and MLGD-F’s cleaner gradient graph becomes the decisive factor.
The 5D setting is the approximate crossover between these two regimes.

B.2
Dataset Construction
We construct an augmented MNIST dataset that captures rotational ambiguity across digit classes.
B.2.1
Conditional Model
For the conditional training the pipeline proceeds in two stages.
Stage 1: Logical-angle entries.
We start from the standard MNIST training split (60,000 images).
For each base image, we always retain a copy at logical angle 0. We additionally include rotated
copies at 90, 180, and 270 if a pretrained classifier assigns the rotated image to a digit class with
confidence ≥0.9999. This threshold ensures that only genuinely rotationally ambiguous digits
(e.g., “0”, “1”, “8”, “6”/“9”) contribute additional logical-angle entries. The classifier used is the
dataset-construction CNN described below, trained on the standard MNIST training split.
Stage 2: Uniform random rotation.
Each logical-angle entry is then rotated by a uniform random
angle θ ∼Uniform[0, 360). The stored label angle is (logical_angle + θ) mod 360. This produces
a dataset of 82,483 samples with continuous, uniformly distributed rotation angles within each
rotationally ambiguous class.
B.2.2
Unconditional Model
The unconditional diffusion model is trained on a separate dataset consisting of all 70,000 MNIST
images (train + test splits), each independently rotated by a uniform random angle at every epoch
access, with no classifier filtering.

C.8
What Does MLGD-F Actually Change in the Scribble?
Where MLGD-F edits the scribble.
Figure 8 contrasts the source scribble with the MLGD-F-
optimized scribble, along with their pixel-wise | · | difference. The edits are spatially concentrated
rather than diffuse: the largest modifications lie along the eye and eyebrow region and the lower
hair outline. Most of the scribble is essentially unchanged; a minority of anatomically-meaningful
contours account for the bulk of the edit magnitude.
Yet even with the pixel-difference heatmap in hand, it remains unclear why these subtle edits suffice
to shift the generated distribution toward a balanced male–female output. From a human perceptual
standpoint, the changes are too small and too localized to constitute a legible gender signal. We
hypothesize that MLGD-F has identified a structural sensitivity in the ControlNet–SDXL pipeline: a
region of the conditioning space where small perturbations disproportionately influence the generative
process, not because they are semantically meaningful to a human observer, but because the diffusion
model’s internal representations are unexpectedly responsive to them. This points to a potential
vulnerability in the underlying generative model — one that MLGD-F successfully exploits to
achieve distributional control, but that also raises questions about the robustness and interpretability
of ControlNet-based conditioning more broadly.
Why those regions: gradient saliency.

rather than incidental, we ask which scribble pixels the CLIP gender signal is gradient-sensitive

portion of the edit concentration is explained by the fact that stroke pixels produce non-zero latent
activations while background pixels do not — edits are suppressed in the background simply because
there is little latent signal to perturb there. This does not invalidate the saliency finding, but it does
suggest that the spatial concentration of MLGD-F edits is at least partly a consequence of the latent
geometry: the optimiser can only meaningfully move in directions where the latent is already active.
Takeaway.
The two analyses together resolve the apparent paradox of small pixel edits producing
a large distributional shift. Among the strokes of the source scribble, MLGD-F does not drift
diffusely: it injects an aligned gradient signal that preferentially modifies the subset of strokes the
downstream CLIP score is structurally sensitive to. This validates the inner estimator of Algorithm 2
as a mechanism rather than a black box: despite the long differentiable chain from scribble through
ControlNet, VAE, and CLIP, the pulled-back gradient at each denoising step retains enough semantic
structure to localise onto the anatomical features actually predictive of the target attribute. The regions
identified — the eye, eyebrow, and hairline — are anatomically intuitive as gender-discriminative
features. Yet from a human perceptual standpoint, the magnitude of the edits is strikingly small:
changes that are nearly imperceptible to a human observer are sufficient to produce a substantial shift
in the generated gender distribution. This disproportion between edit magnitude and distributional
effect suggests a sensitivity in the underlying generative model that goes beyond what human
perception would predict, and raises broader questions about the robustness of ControlNet-based

Analysis of the MLGD-F Estimator
This appendix analyses the plug-in loss estimator and the gradient estimator used inside the MLGD-F
inner estimator (Algorithm 2; detailed form in Algorithm 3). The goal is to quantify how far the
practical estimator is from the population quantity we would ideally optimize, and in particular
to make explicit the two error sources introduced by the distilled conditional sampler fϕ: (i) the
sample-level error εs with which fϕ reproduces the true conditional, and (ii) the gradient error εg
with which the Jacobian of fϕ in the conditioning input x matches the true reparameterising map
(Proposition 2); a direct student-vs-teacher analogue is the subject of Proposition 3, which ties the
bounds to the memory argument of Section 5.4: a Ks-step student replacing a K⋆-step teacher cuts
reverse-mode graph depth by the ratio K⋆/Ks at the cost of an additional student-teacher gradient
discrepancy controlled by εg,dist, εdist.

Output gap εs.
The output gap εs of Assumption 2 is an idealised fidelity assumption. Directly-

idealisation, since the teacher does not exactly match ftrue.

Open questions.
Several questions remain open. First, what prompt-level properties predict
Jacobian instability: 16 outliers across 100 prompts show no obvious syntactic or semantic pattern,
though one plausible explanation is mode mismatch — prompts that elicit visually different outputs
from teacher and student may induce larger Jacobian discrepancies, since the two models effectively
compute gradients around different local modes of the image distribution. Second, whether the strong
alignment observed for photo/cinematic reflects an inductive bias of SDXL-Lightning towards
photorealistic outputs, which would imply that the choice of distillation target distribution matters for
downstream gradient-based use of the student. We leave these to future work.

• The distillation error enters both the loss and its gradient linearly in εs, via the Lipschitz

• The memory advantage of the distilled sampler comes at an analytically quantifiable student-

The bound is qualitative, not numerical.
Empirically, the Jacobian gap ˆrg exceeds the output
gap ˆrs across all style groups (Table 18). Without outliers (Panel B), ˆrg/ˆrs ranges from 2.39×
(photo) to 4.05× (cartoon), with an overall ratio of 4.34×; outlier prompts can push this to 33.31×
(Panel A). Proposition 2’s bound is therefore qualitatively informative — it identifies the right two
error sources and shows the gradient bias decomposes cleanly into them — but not quantitatively
tight at current distillation quality. We view the propositions as a framework for measurement and

More broadly, this points to a gap in the theory of model distillation itself. Standard distillation
analyses, and the training objectives that flow from them, control output fidelity: L2 or Wasserstein
closeness between student and teacher. This is the right bound when the distilled model is used
as a function evaluator. MLGD-F, by contrast, uses fϕ as an autograd oracle: its Jacobian is
consumed, not just its values. Whenever a distilled or compressed model is deployed in a downstream
optimisation rather than a downstream evaluation (MLGD-F, bi-level optimisation with learned
inner solvers, differentiable simulators, gradient-guided inverse design in general), output-fidelity
distillation bounds are insufficient, and an εg-type Jacobian-fidelity guarantee is required. Current
consistency-model training provides the former but not the latter.
