Abstract
Odrzywo lek defined a system Exp-Minus-Log (EML) [4] that reduces
all elementary functions over complex numbers down to a constant ‘1’, and
a single two place function E(α, β) = exp(α) −log(β). This paper shows
that in this system, equivalent to Chow’s EL numbers [2], every EML-
expressible number is computable. We go on to prove that the canonical
example of a non-computable real, Chaitin’s ΩU, is inexpressible in EML.
This gives a formal inexpressibility theorem for this system.

Introduction
The original paper due to Odrzywo lek [4] presents a way to express all ele-
mentary functions, constants, and operations through a single binary operator
E(α, β) = exp(α) −log(β) with the constant ‘1’. This is at once surprising and
aesthetically pleasing. Indeed, such closed-form number systems are useful in
symbolic computation [2], CAS systems [5], exact arithmetic [7], etc.
Earlier work due to Chow [2] created the EL numbers that are expressible
through the use of exp and log. Chow introduced the EL numbers to formalize
the intuitive notion of “numbers expressible in closed form” [2]. We show an
equivalence with EML numbers.
The content of [4] is mostly constructive and existential, and leaves open the
question about any formal inexpressibility theorem. This paper fills that gap by
showing the computability of the EML numbers, and thereby using Chaitin’s ΩU
as the “canonical” non-computable witness - left-computably enumerable but
not computable - thereby being a witness to inexpressibility in EML. Moreover,
we prove this inclusion:
EMLR = EL ∩R ⊆computable reals ⊊R
The paper is structured as follows: §2 defines EML and ΩU, and §3 proves
EMLR ⊆CompR and derives the main inexpressibility result.

Preliminaries
2.1
EML Numbers
Let us first define Exp-Minus-Log (EML) numbers, and state some basic facts
about them, all taken from [4].
Definition 1 (Syntax of EML). Let Σ = {‘1’, ‘E’, ‘(’, ‘)’, ‘, ’}. The set E of
closed EML expressions be the smallest set of strings over Σ such that ‘ 1’ ∈E,
and if α, β ∈E then ‘E(α, β)’ ∈E
Definition 2 (Semantics). Define [[·]] : E →C recursively:
[[1]] = 1
[[E(α, β)]] = exp([[α]]) −log([[β]])
Here exp is the exponential function, and log is the principal-branch complex
natural logarithm defined on C\{0} with imaginary part in (−π, π]. Given this,
[[φ]] is defined wherever [[β]] ̸= 0 This ensures that [[φ]] ∈C. Let
EML = {[[φ]] : φ ∈E, [[φ]] defined} ⊆C
and let the EML-constructable reals be EMLR = EML ∩R
As shown in [4], the following elementary constants and functions are ex-
pressible in EML:
1. The integers and rationals,
2. The constants e, π, i, −1, with e = E(1, 1), etc.
3. Arithmetic operations +, −, ×, /, xy, √x, along with the standard tran-
scendental functions exp, ln, sin, cos, tan, as well as the inverse trigono-
metric and hyperbolic functions.
The set of EML-Expressible numbers equals the set of complex numbers
obtainable by any finite combination of 1, exp, log (as defined above), and
subtraction. Equivalently, as +, −, ×, and / are derivable, the EML-expressible
numbers are the smallest subset of C containing 1 closed under those operations
with exp and log. We now show latter is exactly Chow’s EL-numbers in [2].
2.2
Chow’s EL Numbers
Chow defines the EL numbers in [2] as follows:
Definition 3. A subfield F of C is closed under exp and log if; exp(x) ∈F for
all x ∈F, and log(x) ∈F for all nonzero x ∈F where log() is the branch of
the natural logarithm function such that for all x, −π < ℑ(log x) ≤π. The field
E of EL numbers is the intersection of all subfields of C that are closed under
exp and log.
Proposition 1.
EML = E

Proof. EML ⊆E is immediate from E in Definition 2 - any number expressible
in EML is formed of exp, log, and subtraction, and so naturally also in E.
E ⊆EML follows as every number expressible in exp and log in Definition
3, by [4] may be rewritten with the following substitutions:
exp(x) = E(x, 1),
log(x) = E(1, E(E(1, x), 1))
along with the following substitutions for field operations, with 1 already given:
0 = log(1) = E(1, E(E(1, 1), 1))
x × y = E(E(1, E(E(E(1, E(E(1, E(1, x)), 1)), y), 1)), 1)
x + y = E(1, E(E(E(1, E(E(1, E(1, E(x, 1))), 1)), E(y, 1)), 1))
−x = E(E(1, E(E(1, E(1, E(x, 1))), 1)), E(E(1, 1), 1))
x−1 = E(E(E(1, E(E(1, E(1, x)), 1)), E(E(1, 1), 1)), 1)
As shown in [4], every elementary function may also be derived in EML, and
the conditions on log are the same. As such, we can build the full subfield of C
with the substitutions given above from the field operations 0, 1, +, ×, and the
inverses −x and x−1. So each z ∈E is expressible as some z′ ∈EML.

EML Expressibility
Now we build our reasoning towards inexpressibility within EML. To give a brief
overview; first, we note the countability of E, and then utilise facts from com-
putable analysis [7] and effective lower bounds (Lemma 3) to show in Theorem
2 that all EML expressible values are computable, and then in Theorem 3 that
Chaitin’s ΩU /∈E. This section is implicit in [2] and is itself derivative of [6, §9].

Lemma 1 (Countability of Syntax). E is countably infinite.
Proof. Each φ ∈E has finite length over a finite alphabet. Thus, the set of
strings of arbitrary length is countable. The presence of ‘1’ indicates that E ̸= ∅,
and iterating ‘E’ in line with Def. 2 produces infinitely many distinct expres-
sions.
Corollary 1. The set of non-EML-expressible reals has full Lebesgue measure
on R, given that it is R minus a countable set.
Lemma 2 (Computability of exp and log on computable complex numbers). If
z is a computable complex number, then exp(z) is a computable complex number.
Similarly, if z is a computable complex number with z ̸= 0 and given a
positive computable lower bound δ > 0 with |z| ≥δ, then log(z) is a computable
complex number.
Proof. These are standard results in computable analysis. By [7, Thm. 6.5.2],
a complex analytic function with a computable power series at a computable
centre is computable on any effectively given closed subset of its disc of conver-
gence. Applied to exp(z) = P
n≥0 zn/n! at the origin, with infinite radius of
convergence, [7, Thm. 6.5.2] gives that exp is computable at every computable
complex input.
The computability of log for computable input z is established in Tent–Ziegler
[6, §3–4], where the principal branch logarithm is shown to be lower-elementary
computable on the slit plane C \ (−∞, 0] via an integral representation, and
lower-elementary computable functions are computable. Subtraction of com-
putable complex numbers is computable by [7, Thm. 4.3.9].
Lemma 3 (Bounds on log-arguments). Let φ ∈E be a closed EML expression
with [[φ]] defined. Then for every sub-expression of φ of the form E(α, β), either
[[β]] ∈(−∞, 0), or there exists a rational εβ > 0 with
dist([[β]], (−∞, 0]) ≥εβ
Proof. The expression φ is finite, so it has finitely many sub-expressions of the
form E(α, β). Fix one. Since [[φ]] is defined, [[β]] ̸= 0 (Def. 2). If [[β]] ∈(−∞, 0)
we are done. Otherwise [[β]] ∈C\(−∞, 0], which is open, so dist([[β]] , (−∞, 0]) >
0, and density of Q supplies a rational εβ ∈(0, dist([[β]] , (−∞, 0])).
Let CompR denote the computable reals, CompC the computable complex num-
bers, and recall EMLR = EML ∩R.
Theorem 2 (Computability of EML values). For every φ ∈E with [[φ]] defined,
[[φ]] is a computable complex number. In particular, EMLR ⊆CompR.
Proof. By induction on φ.
Base: [[1]] = 1 ∈Q ⊂CompC.
Step: Let φ = E(α, β) with [[φ]] defined. Then [[α]] and [[β]] are defined and,
by induction, computable. By Lemma 2, exp([[α]]) is computable. By Lemma 3,
either [[β]] ∈(−∞, 0) or there is a rational εβ > 0 such that dist([[β]] , (−∞, 0]) ≥
εβ. We then have two cases:

1. If [[β]] ∈(−∞, 0), (i.e. on the principal branch cut) then
log([[β]]) = log | [[β]] | + iπ
Since | [[β]] | is a positive computable real, log | [[β]] | is computable by [7,
Thm. 6.5.2] applied to log on R>0, and adding iπ preserves computability.
2. Otherwise, by Lemma 2 we have that log([[β]]) is computable.
Subtraction is computable, so [[φ]] = exp([[α]]) −log([[β]]) is computable.
It should be noted that Theorem 2 in no way claims to be uniformly com-
putable - in fact, the central disjunction is not computable (not even r.e.), i.e.
there is not a singular Turing machine that will perform this computation. This
is fine for the pointwise arguments here, but needs to be noted as a limitation.
Theorem 3.
ΩU /∈EMLR
Proof. By Theorem 2, EMLR ⊆CompR, and by Theorem 1, ΩU ∈R \ CompR.
Hence ΩU /∈EMLR. Similarly, for any non-computable real r, r /∈CompR ⊇
EMLR, therefore ∄φ ∈E such that [[φ]] = r.
Corollary 2. The set of non-computable reals, which is of full Lebesgue mea-
sure, is entirely disjoint from EML. In addition to the above, this includes nat-
ural sub-classes such as:
• The binary expansion of the halting set {0.χHALT (1)χHALT (2)χHALT (3) . . .}
• The limits of Specker sequences (see [7])
• Any random real in the sense of Martin-L¨of (see [3]).
