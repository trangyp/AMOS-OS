IMPROVED BOUNDS FOR THE DOUBLE CAP CONJECTURE
DOMONKOS CZIFRA, ´AKOS D´UCZ, M´AT´E MATOLCSI, D´ANIEL VARGA, P´AL ZS´AMBOKI
Abstract. In 1974, Witsenhausen asked for the maximum possible density αn of a measurable subset A
of the unit sphere Sn−1 ⊂Rn such that A contains no pair of orthogonal vectors. For n = 3, the best
known lower bound is 1 −1/
√
2 = 0.29289 . . . , obtained from the natural “double cap” construction of two
opposite spherical caps. This construction was conjectured by Gil Kalai [9] to be optimal for all n. In this
paper, we use a novel approach to establish the upper bound α3 ≤0.2953, improving on the previous best
known bound of 0.2977 due to Bekker et al. [2]. Our approach combines arguments from harmonic analysis
with the concept of the geometric fractional chromatic number of finite graphs, introduced recently by
Ambrus et al. [1]. Within this framework, any finite subset of the sphere yields an upper bound for αn;
we obtain our bound by identifying a suitable 33-element point set through a large-scale computer search.
The same method can also be used in higher dimensions to yield potential improvements of the best
known bounds.
1. Introduction
A measurable subset A of the unit sphere Sn−1 = {x ∈Rn :
∥x∥= 1} is said to avoid orthogonality
if ⟨a, a′⟩̸= 0 for all a, a′ ∈A. Witsenhausen [16] posed the problem of finding the maximum possible
density of such a set A ⊂Sn−1. That is, we aim to determine the value of
(1.1)
αn = sup{ω(A)/ωn : A ⊆Sn−1 is measurable and avoids orthogonality},
where ω is the surface measure of the sphere, and ωn is the total measure of the sphere.
The best known lower bound for αn comes from the natural construction of two open antipodal spherical
caps of spherical radius π/4. This “double cap” construction is conjectured to be optimal for all n by Gil
Kalai [9]. The conjecture is open for all n ≥3. If true, it would imply that αn = (
√
2 + o(1))−n.
As for upper bounds, Witsenhausen [16] observed the trivial bound αn ≤1/n by considering an or-
thonormal system of n unit vectors, and an averaging argument over the sphere. The first exponentially
decreasing bound, αn ≤(1 + o(1))1.13−n was given by Frankl and Wilson [7], which was later improved
to αn ≤(1 + o(1))1.225−n by Raigorodskii [12].
Besides progress on the asymptotics of αn, there have been several improvements on upper bounds in low
dimensions. For n = 3, the first improvement on the trivial bound α3 ≤1/3 was obtained by a harmonic

2. The geometric fractional chromatic number
The notion of the geometric fractional chromatic number, introduced in [1], is a very effective tool in
several problems with underlying symmetries. In this section, we describe how this concept fits into the
framework of Witsenhausen’s problem.
We first make a technical remark, and introduce some terminology. If A ⊂Sn−1 avoids orthogonality, then
so does the symmetrized set A ∪−A, of possibly larger measure. As such, we will assume without loss of
generality, throughout the paper, that A is symmetric, A = −A. Similarly, in the forthcoming arguments,
whenever we consider a finite subset X ⊂Sn−1, we always assume that X is symmetric, X = −X. As
such, X can be written as a union of antipodal pairs, X = ∪N
i=1{xi, −xi}. (That is, X is essentially a
subset of the projective plane, but still, we would rather think of it as a collection of antipodal pairs on
the surface of the sphere.) To such a set X, we will associate a corresponding orthogonality graph GX in
the following way: any vertex vi of GX is a pair {xi, −xi}, and we join two vertices vi and vj by an edge if
and only if ⟨xi, xj⟩= 0. For an orthogonal transformation T ∈O(n) we say that T(vi) = vj if T(xi) = xj
or T(xi) = −xj. We will call two sets of vertices Y = {vi1, . . . , vik} and Z = {vj1, . . . , vjk} geometrically
congruent, in notation Y ∼= Z, if there exists an orthogonal transformation T of Rn such that T(Y ) = Z.

5. Appendix
5.1. Computer search. Below we give a high level overview of our graph search.
The symmetric witness set X, for which the value ∆(GX) provides the upper bound for α3 as described
in Section 2, is obtained by a heuristic search algorithm. Throughout the search we restrict our attention
to symmetric sets X, and consider any antipodal pair {xi, −xi} as a single vertex vi in the orthogonality
graph GX, as explained in Section 2.
We start from a promising configuration GX0 of small cardinality, and iteratively add and remove vertices
until we obtain a sufficiently low value of ∆(GX). Below, we refer to candidate sets X as nodes, as in
nodes of a search tree, not to be confused with vertices, which term is reserved for the elements of GX.
Computing ∆(GX) is challenging: writing up the linear program requires finding all the independent
sets of GX and all the isometries among them. We work in the space of antipodal pairs, and adapt
the isometry checks accordingly. The size of the LP seems to grow exponentially in |GX|, so computing
∆(GX) for large graphs is not feasible. We employed the GUROBI solver [8] on a computer with 1TB of
RAM for our graph search, which limited us to circa |GX| ≤33.
The design of the search algorithm was guided by the following considerations:
• monotonicity: ∆(GX) is monotone w.r.t. set inclusion.
• evaluation time: evaluating ∆(GX) is roughly exponential in |GX|.
5.1.1. Beam-constrained Monte Carlo Tree Search. In standard MCTS setups typical in game-tree search
[3, 10, 14], inner nodes have similar evaluation times rather than depending heavily on tree depth. Hence,
we modify the standard MCTS algorithm with a beam search aspect: we extend a node only in the case
when its value is at least the k-th best among nodes of the same size. This trade-off may exclude some
promising nodes, but significantly reduces computational demands.
The search starts from a carefully chosen 6-vertex graph GX0 (corresponding to 6 antipodal pairs), see
Table 2. We only add vertices that are orthogonal to at least two vertices already present. Consequently,
there are O(n2) possible expansions, which can be computed efficiently by taking cross products of vertex
pairs. Adding new vertices can make others redundant. Therefore, node expansion involves removing
vertices as well as adding them. These reduced nodes are inserted into the selection path: they become
the parent of the expanded node and the (virtual) child of its grandparent.
Node selection is performed according to the UCB bound [10]. As our target is a simple maximization
task and not the standard 2-player min/max-setup, we are looking for the best descendant of the root.

observed among them. We empirically normalize and rescale target values to the range [0, 1].
In the search that found X, we use exploration rate 0.1 and beam size 5. The total computational budget
