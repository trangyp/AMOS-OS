Abstract:
Speckle is an intrinsic pattern in optical coherence tomography (OCT) that
obscures fine image features and degrades effective resolution. In this study, we propose a
numerical speckle reduction method based on the dispersed scatterer model and the imaging
formulation of OCT. Utilizing the shifted-complex-conjugate-product, the proposed method
digitally modulates speckle patterns by shifting the complex en face OCT signal and averaging
the resulting real-part images. This approach allows for effective speckle suppression using a
single volumetric acquisition without additional hardware modifications. OCT point spread
function phantom measurement demonstrated lateral resolution preservation of the proposed
method. We validated the method using a custom-built full-field swept-source OCT system on
human breast adenocarcinoma spheroids and a zebrafish eye. Quantitative evaluations using the
contrast-to-noise ratio and equivalent number of looks demonstrated that the proposed method
significantly outperforms conventional frame-averaging techniques. The speckle-reduced images
revealed microstructures previously obscured by speckle, such as necrotic regions in spheroids,
while preserving the original image sharpness and resolution.
1.
Introduction
Recent improvements in cell culture techniques have enabled three-dimensional (3D) cell culture
models such as organoids and spheroids [1,2], which have been widely adopted in biomedical
and life science research. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) [3] is expected to be suitable to
non-invasively investigate such thick 3D cell cultures. However, the lateral resolution of OCT is
conventionally configured to be relatively low, such as around 5 to ten few micrometers, due to
the due to the trade-off between lateral resolution and depth of focus (DOF).
Full-field swept-source OCT (FF-SSOCT) [4–6] is a variant of OCT that uses a wavelength-
sweeping light source and an area camera. Since FF-SSOCT does not have confocal pinhole, it is
free from confocal signal decay [7]. In addition, computational (i.e., holographic) refocusing
enables diffraction limit resolution over the depth range much wider than the DOF [6]. The
absence of confocal pinhole and the computational refocusing resolve the resolution-DOF trade-
off, and hence, FF-SSOCT can achieve micrometer-scale lateral resolution while maintaining
millimeter-scale imaging depth [6,7].
FF-SSOCT enables fast volumetric imaging with high lateral resolution, and hence it well-suites
for 3D imaging of thick in vitro samples, such as tumor spheroids. However, the coherent
nature of OCT inevitably causes the speckle [8,9]. Speckle is a granular pattern that obscures
fine sample structure and hence reduces the effective resolution. Therefore, effective speckle
reduction is essential for microscopic OCT imaging.
There are several speckle reduction methods, but each of them has its own limitations. For
example, the multi-frame averaging method [10–12] is a widely used and very effective. However,

it requires a large number of OCT images acquired at the same position, and hence, it requires a
long acquisition time. The angle-compounding method [13,14] is based on averaging of multiple
images acquired from several directions. Although it works well, it requires a major hardware
modification. The wavelength-compounding method [15,16] is based on the averaging of OCT
images acquired at different wavelength bands. Typically, the multi-band OCT images were
generated by windowed Fourier transform of a spectral interference signal, and hence it causes
the reduction of axial resolution. The polarization diversity method [17], which is based on
averaging of OCT images obtained with different polarization states, is free from this resolution
reduction. However, there are only two independent states of polarization in principle, it causes
the only two fully de-correlated speckle patterns at most, and it limits the maximum efficacy of
speckle reduction.
While the above-mentioned methods are based on modified acquisition protocol or hard-ware
extensions (so we call it as “hardware-based methods”), there are some methods which solely
based on image processing (i.e, software-based methods). Although the software-based methods
are successful, there are still some limitations. For example, digital filter-based methods [18–20]
can be suffered from resolution degradation, expensive computation, or structural distortion. The
non-local-mean method effectively suppresses speckle as keeping the perceptually important
image features, but is computationally expensive [21,22]. Neural network-based methods [23–27]
require large datasets for training. In addition, both of the hardware- and software-based methods
were designed without a clear analytical model (i.e., the mathematical definition) of speckle.
Recently, Tomita et al. proposed an analytic signal-formation model of OCT [28]. In this
formulation, an OCT image is represented as the summation of two mathematically defined
components: an “incoherent OCT image” and “speckle.” The incoherent OCT image is expressed
as a convolution between a real-and-positive point spread function (PSF) and the sample structure,
and is an ideal speckle-free image. In contrast, the speckle arises from the interactions among
scattered light from different scatterers and is described as an integral term in this formulation.
Although this speckle term is complicated, it provides a clear mathematical definition of OCT
speckle and offers a theoretical basis for manipulating speckle independently from the incoherent
image.
In this paper, we exploit this formulation and derive a fully numerical speckle reduction method
that does not require a specific hardware extension. This method is based on a model-based
numerical complex-signal processing which can modulate the speckle patterns after image
acquisition while keeps the incoherence image component unchanged. We generate multiple
OCT images with different speckle realizations, and these images are averaged to suppress the
speckle. The imaging model suggests that this operation does not sacrifice the OCT resolution.
Since this method uses only one OCT image, its image-acquisition time is short. In addition,
since this method uses only simple and definitive numerical computation, it requires only a
short computation time. We implement this method on FF-SSOCT. Since FF-SSOCT does not
require mechanical lateral scanning, it has high transversal phase stability and is suitable for our
complex-signal processing-based speckle reduction. The resolution preservation nature of this
method is experimentally demonstrated by measuring an established scatterer phantom. This
method is also applied to biological samples including tumor-spheroid and zebrafish OCT, and
its performance is demonstrated subjectively by image observation and also objectively using
quantitative image-based metrics.

Implementation and study protocol
3.1.
FF-SSOCT system
A custom-built FF-SSOCT [6] was used to validate our speckle reduction method. The light
source is a wavelength-sweeping light source with a center wavelength of 840 nm and a sweeping
range of 75 nm (BS-840-1-HP, Superlum, Ireland). The probe beam illuminates the sample as a
collimated plane wave where the beam diameter and the power on the sample is 2.34 mm and
5.23 mW, respectively. The sample is images on a high-speed area camera (FASTCAM Mini
AX100, Photron, Japan) through a microscope objective (NA = 0.3, effective focal length = 18
mm, RMS10X-PF, Olympus, Japan) and a tube lens ( 𝑓= 750 𝑚𝑚). The back scattered probe
beam is overlapped with a reference beam and forms an interference signal on the camera.
The in-focus lateral resolution at the full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) intensity is 1.4 μm
(in air), and the axial resolution is 6.5 μm (FWHM intensity in air). The camera pixel size is
20 μm, which corresponds to 0.48 μm on the sample and is almost 1/3 of the in-focus lateral
resolution. The DOF is 16.0 μm. The sensor resolution is 1024 × 1024 pixels, which results
in the maximum lateral field of view (FOV) is 0.49 mm × 0.49 mm. The camera operates at
at 4,000 frames/s and a single OCT volume is reconstructed from 1,000 spectral interference
signals. And hence, the volumetric measurement speed is 4 volumes/s.

3.2.
Implementation of speckle reduction method
As described in the theory section (Section 2.2), the speckle reduction method generates multiple
real SCCP images with several shifts (i.e, several shift amounts and directions) and average them.
These shifts are defined as follows.
Figure 2(a) illustrates one of the digital shift operation. Here the complex en face image 𝑠of
Eqs. (4)-(6) is digitally shifted for Δ𝒙/2 and 𝑠∗is shifted for - Δ𝒙/2. The mutual displacement of
these two images are Δ𝒙.
In our speckle reduction method, multiple real SCCP images are generated with several shifts
are generated and averaged. The set of the shifts are exemplified in Fig. 2(b), where each circle
indicate one of the shift of the 𝑠image, i.e., Δ𝒙/2. The shifts are selected to form a rectangle
homogeneous grid as shown in this diagram. The number of shift patterns along 𝑥′ and 𝑦′
directions are identical and denoted as 𝑁. In this example, 𝑁= 5 and there are 25 (𝑁2) shift
patterns in total. It should be noted that when the complex image 𝑠shifts for Δ𝒙, the other image
𝑠∗shifts for −Δ𝒙. Namely, when the shift of 𝑠is the blue circle, the shift of 𝑠∗is the purple circle.
We define a parameter “maximum shift amount” (MSA) as the maximum of |Δ𝑥|, which is
identical to the maximum of |Δ𝑦| in our configuration, as depicted in Fig. 2(b).
Each shift (the circle in Fig. 2(b)) corresponds to a real SCCP image, and all of the real SCCP
images are averaged to reduce the speckle. It should be noted that some shifts give an identical
real SCCP image. For example, the SCCP obtained with the shift of blue circle in Fig. 2(b) is the
complex conjugate of the SCCP of the purple circle. And hence, the real-part images (i.e., real
SCCP images) of these SCCPs are identical. This suggests that “the number of unique images”
(NUI) to be averaged is not 𝑁2 but (𝑁2 + 1)/2.
In some shift configurations, the digital shift amount is not always the integer multiples of the
pixel size. For the shift with sub-pixel accuracy, the complex en face OCT image is up-sampled
by Fourier-space zero-padding method.

3.3.
Protocol for optimization and performance evaluation
3.3.1.
Samples
To quantitatively evaluate the impact of our speckle reduction method on the lateral resolution, a
sparse-scatterer phantom (OCT point spread function phantom, OCTPSF01, Edmund Optics, NJ)
was measured. The phantom consists of a solid medium with a refractive index of approximately
1.5 and a thickness of 10 mm, in which FeO nano-particles, which are smaller than 1 μm, are
sparsely suspended. The randomly distributed scatterers generate isolated bright spots in OCT
images, which enables quantitative assessment of the lateral resolution.
To validate the speckle-reduction effect of the present method in biomedical samples, in vitro
cancer spheroids and an fixed adult zebrafish were measured. The spheroids were formed by
seeding 1,000 MCF-7 (human breast adenocarcinoma cell line) cells. To include variety of
samples, we used three spheroids with different cultivation protocols. Namely, two of them were
cultured without anti-cancer drug administration, where one of them was cultured for 8 days
and the other was cultured for 10 days. The other one was cultured for 8 days with 10 μM of

5.
Discussion
5.1.
Image processing speed
We measured the image processing time of the proposed speckle reduction method.
The
computational cost of both the speckle reduction algorithm and the overall post-processing
pipeline was evaluated using a 1024 × 1024 × 512-pixel OCT volume.
For the proposed speckle reduction process, as in processing Step-(ii) to Step-(vii) in Fig. 1,
approximately 0.55 s is required to process a single en face image. When applied to a single en
face OCT image, the processing time is approximately 0.55 seconds per frame.
The complete imaging and post-processing workflow consists of several sequential steps. First,
the OCT interference signal is acquired by the camera, including spectral detection and data
storage. With our FF-SSOCT system, the acquisition of a single OCT volume takes approximately
0.25 seconds, while saving the acquired data requires about 16 seconds.
After acquisition, the OCT signal is reconstructed through spectral rescaling followed by
a Fourier transform, with a computation time of approximately 94.5 seconds per volume.
High-frequency noise is then removed using a binary spatial frequency filter, which requires
approximately 104.7 seconds per volume. Subsequently, computational refocusing is applied to
correct defocus, which constitutes the most time-consuming step in the post-processing pipeline.
The refocusing process requires approximately 1151.9 seconds per volume, excluding manual
parameter selection.
It should be noted that all post-processing procedures were implemented in a Jupyter-based
Python environment; therefore, the reported computation times are not optimized. Despite its
high computational cost, refocusing is necessary in FF-SSOCT imaging because the depth of
field is limited to approximately 16 μm.


## Page 15


In the study to determine the optimal parameters in Section 4.2.1, up-sampling was applied to
achieve sub-pixel shifts of OCT images. However, based on the experimental results, the optimal
despeckling protocol requires a maximum lateral shift of 8 pixels with a step size of 1 pixel,
indicating that up-sampling is not required for practical applications.
Overall, the total post-processing time per OCT volume is approximately 1632.7 seconds.
As shown in Table 2, the proposed speckle reduction method uses approximately 17.3% of the
overall post-processing time and increases the total post-processing time by only about 20.8%.
Considering its effective speckle suppression performance and the absence of additional hardware
requirements, the proposed method introduces a modest computational overhead and is practical
for FF-SSOCT image quality enhancement using post-acquisition signal processing.

Comparison with other methods
As discussed in Section 1, most of hardware-based speckle reduction methods rely on averaging
multiple uncorrelated speckle patterns. In this section, we compare our SCCP-based speckle
reduction method with representative speckle reduction techniques in terms of acquisition strategy
and the required number of frames.
Frame averaging is one of the most widely used speckle reduction methods in clinical OCT
imaging. Here, we first compared the performance of our SCCP-based speckle reduction method
with the frame-averaging approach using the en face image of the same spheroid sample as in
Fig. 6 but at a different depth. In this study, due to camera memory limitations, only 32 OCT
volumes can be captured in one acquisition of our FF-SSOCT. We apply the frame-averaging
approach using only 32 frames, which is fewer than the 50 or 60 frames reported in the literature.
We generated the SCCP-based speckle-reduced image with optimal parameters. Fig. 8 presents a
visual comparison among the original image, the 32-frame averaged speckle-reduced image, and
the SCCP-based speckle-reduced image. Quantitative evaluations using the CNR and ENL are
summarized in Table 3.

features compared with conventional frame averaging, and the appearance of speckle is more
effectively suppressed. In contrast, although frame averaging reduces speckle to some extent,
speckle remains evident.
The quantitative results in Table 3 further support these observations. The SCCP-based method
achieves a higher CNR than both the original image and the 32-frame averaged image, indicating
enhanced image contrast. In addition, the ENL values in ROI 1 and ROI 2 are improved using

In addition to the speckle reduction performance, the processing time of 32-frame averaging

more frames to average. Götzinger et al. [10] presented a frame averaging method that achieves
effective speckle suppression using 60 OCT frames to average, while Alonso-Caneiro [11] used
50 frames. It should also be noted that the frame averaging method is primarily designed for
B-scan imaging and is not well-suited for FF-SSOCT. In FF-SSOCT, a large volumetric dataset
is acquired in each frame, which leads to excessive data size and increased demands on camera
memory when conventional frame averaging is applied.
In addition, frame averaging is mainly designed for in vivo samples. On the other hand, in
vitro samples are generally more stable than in vivo samples, such as the retina, so the correlation
between the speckle patterns of different frames is relatively high. This causes the effectiveness
of the frame averaging method to be low. In comparison, our SCCP-based method achieves
superior speckle reduction performance.
In conclusion, the results demonstrate that the proposed SCCP-based speckle reduction method
outperforms conventional frame averaging in FF-SSOCT, achieving better speckle suppression
and contrast enhancement in significantly less time.

Additionally, several other hardware-based speckle reduction techniques have also been
published. Desjardins et al. [14] demonstrated an angular compounding method requiring a single
acquisition with approximately 400 frames. Zhao et al. [13] presented a multi-angle compounding
approach using 11 illumination angles and 20 B-scans per angle. Polarization diversity methods
can achieve speckle reduction within a single acquisition, while Pircher et al. [15] proposed
a wavelength-compounding technique using two light sources. Despite their effectiveness,
these methods require specialized optical hardware and system modifications, making them
incompatible with most OCT systems, including our FF-SSOCT system. Furthermore, angle
compounding methods acquire a large amount of data, and polarization diversity methods
theoretically improve speckle contrast by only a factor of
√

methods, such as the method proposed by Zhao et al. [16], achieve speckle reduction at the
cost of reduced axial resolution, which limits its applicability in high-resolution OCT imaging.
In contrast, our SCCP-based method does not require additional hardware modifications, only
requires one acquisition, and preserves axial resolution, making it particularly suitable for the
FF-SSOCT system.
6.
Conclusion
In this paper, an imaging-model-based numerical speckle reduction method for FF-SSOCT
is presented. PSF phantom experiments confirm that the proposed method preserves lateral
resolution. Experiments conducted on spheroids and a zebrafish eye demonstrate improvements in
image contrast and smoothness, as quantitatively evaluated using the CNR and the ENL. In addition,
subjective visual observations confirm effective speckle suppression, with microstructures clearly
revealed.
Owing to its simple implementation, fast processing speed, resolution preservation, and
remarkable speckle reduction performance, our proposed speckle reduction method is both
practical and effective for improving the image quality of FF-SSOCT using only post-acquisition
signal processing.
