Statistical Learning
Statistical learning—ofen considered a subﬁeld of, or synonymous with, machine learn-
ing—is concerned with training probabilistic models from data. Tese models are then
usedforprediction, sample generation, andthediscoveryof compact, meaningfulrepresen-
tations of the data. Learning and information theory intersect in both model training and
the characterization of fundamental performance limits. Tis chapter focuses on model
training, while performance limits are addressed in the next chapter.
Te central problem in statistical learning is simple to state (Figure .): Given a
dataset sampled from an unknown distribution pdata and a family of probability distri-
butions (a model) P = {pθ; θ ∈Θ}, identify a distribution pθ∗∈P that is as close as
possible (or most similar) to pdata.
Te ﬁrst step in formulating a learning problem is therefore to specify a probability
model together with an appropriate measure of divergence between probability distri-
butions. Among the latter, relative entropy (or cross-entropy) is the most widely used,
largely because its minimization is equivalent to maximizing the log-likelihood of the ob-
served data. In some settings, however, directly maximizing the log-likelihood is compu-
tationally intractable; one instead optimizes the evidence lower bound (ELBO). In other
scenarios, alternative measures, such as f-divergences and Fisher divergence, are used.
We ﬁrst discuss supervised learning, illustrating it with classical linear and logistic re-
gression. Tese models serve as a prelude to neural networks—the most powerful and
widely used models in statistical learning. We then consider generative models, whose
primary goal is to generate samples from a trained model that resemble those in the
dataset. For example, in natural language processing, theaim is to generate text that mim-
ics natural English; in music composition, to create pieces aligned with a speciﬁc genre;

Statistical learning and inference are both rooted in probability theory and share many
basic techniques. However, they diﬀer in their setups and aims. Statistical learning is con-
ceptually related to the parameter estimation problem discussed in Chapter , where we
assumed that pdata ∈P—that is, there exists a true parameter θ∗∈Θ such that pθ∗=
pdata—and the goal is to estimate θ∗from the data. Te statistical learning problem de-
parts from this classical paradigm in a crucial way: we do not assume that pdata ∈P.
In fact, the true data-generating distribution may lie outside P. Instead, the choice of
the model P reﬂects our prior knowledge, modeling assumptions, or inductive biases
about the data-generating distribution. Moreover, the parameter set (also referred to as
the model class) Θ here may be considerably more complex than in classic parameter es-
timation, potentially encompassing families of neural network architectures with varying
weights.
Another key distinction between learning and inference lies in their objectives. Learn-
ing emphasizes prediction and sample generation, rather than drawing conclusions about
a population. Tis gives rise to a central tension: a model must balance ﬁtting the train-
ing data against its ability to generalize to new inputs. An overﬁtted model may perform
exceptionally on the training dataset but fail to generalize to unseen data. To evaluate
generalization, the dataset is typically split into a training set and a testing set, with model
performance measured by its accuracy on the testing set. We will investigate theoretical
limits on this fundamental aspect of statistical learning in the next chapter.

eﬃcient latent representations of the data. Source coding, as discussed in earlier chapters,

Te chapter focused on models trained using supervised and unsupervised learning.
Another very important, but conceptually distinct paradigm, is reinforcement learning. In
this setting, learning takes place through sequential interaction with an environment. At
each time step, an agent selects an action based on its current state, receives a reward, and
transitions to a new state according to an unknown probabilistic law. Te objective is to
learn a policy for choosing actions so as to maximize a long-term cumulative reward. Un-
like supervised learning, there is no explicit target output for each input; instead, feedback
is indirect, delayed, and typically noisy. Connections between reinforcement learning and
information theory can be found, for example, in (Lu, Roy, Dwaracherla, Ibrahimi, Os-
band, and Wen ).
With the availability of vast datasets and computation power, statistical learning has
had a profound impact on many applications in several domains. Large language mod-
