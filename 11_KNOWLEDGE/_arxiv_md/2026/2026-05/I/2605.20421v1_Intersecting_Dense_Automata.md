Abstract
We observe that the classical Cartesian product construction for the intersection of (languages of)
nondeterministic finite automata (NFA) is non-optimal in the worst case, if the automata have many
transitions. For a fixed alphabet, the product of two NFA may have Θ(m2) transitions if these NFA
have at most n states and m transitions each.
We describe alternative constructions with O(mn) transitions: or O(mnk−1) for the intersection
of k NFA (for fixed k ≥2 and alphabet Σ). This gives a faster algorithm for deciding NFA intersection
emptiness. The new algorithm is optimal, unless there exists a breakthrough combinatorial algorithm
for detecting (k + 1)-cliques in undirected graphs. This also leads to a more eﬀicient certification
scheme for NFA intersection emptiness.

Introduction
The direct product construction for finite automata is a cornerstone of formal language and automata
theory [24]. Given automata A and B with sets of states P and Q, respectively, the product automaton
A × B has as set of states the Cartesian product P × Q. For each letter a in the input alphabet Σ, it
contains a transition (p, q) →(p′, q′) iff A contains a transition p →p′ and B contains a transition q →q′,
both labelled by a. Thus, A × B simulates A and B simultaneously. This construction shows that the
family of regular languages is closed under intersection: L(A × B) = L(A) ∩L(B) for an appropriate
choice of initial and final states.
The direct product of two deterministic finite automata (DFA) is deterministic, but the construction
applies to nondeterministic finite automata (NFA) too. In fact, direct product constructions for more
sophisticated forms of automata are based on the same idea.
The number of transitions in the product automaton is what determines the worst-case complexity of
algorithms for NFA intersection emptiness, that is, those deciding whether two given NFA accept a word
in common. In the model checking of finite-state systems, deciding NFA intersection emptiness is a core
sub-procedure in the verification of regular safety properties [30].
It is well-known that the number of states in the automaton A × B cannot, in general, be reduced:
that is, there exist finite automata A and B, with n states each, such that every DFA (and even NFA) for
the language L(A)∩L(B) has at least n2 states (see, e.g., [21]). In this paper, we show that an analogous
statement for the number of transitions is false. In other words, the direct product construction (for
NFA) does not always produce automata with the smallest possible number of transitions.
Let NFA A and B have n states each. If they contain, for some a ∈Σ, many transitions labelled
by a (are dense), then the number of transitions with this label in A × B may be as high as n4. We
prove that, for any fixed input alphabet, O(n3) transitions always suﬀice for a suitable NFA to recognise
L(A) ∩L(B), with the number of states remaining O(n2) as in the direct product A × B.
The contributions of this paper are as follows. We consider the intersection of languages recognised
by k ≥2 NFA A0, . . . , Ak−1, each with at most n states and m transitions.1
1. We give several constructions of NFA for the language L(A0)∩. . .∩L(Ak−1) that are sparser than
the classical direct product NFA. If k ≥2 and the alphabet Σ are fixed, our NFA have O(nk) states and
O(mnk−1) = O(nk+1) transitions. (We also give more precise estimates, tracking the dependence on k
and |Σ| without any assumptions about these parameters.) This is an improvement in the number of
transitions when compared with the direct product, for which the tight upper bound is mk = O(n2k).
The improvement is achieved at the cost of a constant-factor increase in the number of states. The idea

relies on ε-transitions and has at most k|Σ| · nk states and k · mnk−1 transitions. The NFA intersection
emptiness algorithm is a search in its transition graph. If we require an NFA (not an ε-NFA) for the
intersection language, our constructions (catch-up product and leapfrog product) suffer a factor of |Σ|k
(or |Σ|k−1; in any case constant if k and Σ are fixed) growth in the number of states and transitions
compared to the nodding product. The resulting NFA is still smaller than the direct product as long as
|Σ| = o(n).
Related work
Constructions of sparser NFA; faster algorithm.
The main ingredient of our first two contribu-
tions is well-known: the interleaving of equal-length words where letters alternate [24, Exercise 4.2.7] has
been studied under several names, ‘perfect shuffle’ and ‘literal shuffle’ among them [6, 5, 22, 23].
Nevertheless, we have been unable to find these results in the literature. The closest is the work of
Boigelot and Wolper [7], who interleave transitions (rather than fire them simultaneously) for automata
that encode sets of satisfying assignments to formulas of linear arithmetic.
This reduces the size of
description of the product automaton in this application. However, both the constructions of sparser
NFA in general and the faster algorithm for NFA intersection emptiness appear to be new. For example,
a recent textbook by Esparza and Blondin gives the direct product–based algorithm for the intersection of
two NFA, with running time bounded by the product of the number of transitions in them [17, Sec. 3.2.3].
Baier and Katoen’s monograph on model checking [3, Chap. 4] also reduces the NFA intersection
problem to a reachability check in the direct product. For the verification of regular safety properties,
Baier and Katoen (following Kupferman and Vardi [30]) describe how to apply the direct product con-
struction to a finite labelled transition system and an NFA that recognises the language of ‘bad’ prefixes
for the property. When transitions of the LTS are re-labelled with subsets of atomic propositions, rather
than with actions, the usual product construction on NFA can be applied; see Vardi and Wolper [35]. The
complexity of verification algorithm is bounded from above by the product of the number of transitions
in the system and the NFA [3, Thm. 4.22]. Our new algorithm would in this case traverse a graph with
fewer transitions than this pessimistic estimate, reducing the complexity.

NFA intersection
Definition 1 (NFA). A nondeterministic finite automaton A is a tuple (Q, Σ, ∆, s, F) consisting of a
finite nonempty set of states Q, a finite input alphabet Σ, an initial state s ∈Q, a set of final states
F ⊆Q, and a transition relation ∆⊆Q × Σ × Q.
A deterministic finite automaton (DFA) is an NFA whose transition relation is in fact a partial function
δ: Q × Σ →Q.
Every NFA is associated with a labelled graph called its transition graph. The vertices of the transition

Certifying (non-)emptiness of NFA intersection
In this section, we show that the nodding product construction does not just give a faster algorithm for
NFA k-IE, but also leads to a faster algorithm for verifying whether k NFA have an empty intersection or
not, using an appropriate certificate. A certificate is an object that acts as a proof of the correctness of
the algorithm’s output. It can obtained as a byproduct of the NFA k-IE decision procedure. We describe
what these certificates look like, and algorithms for verifying the output of an instance of NFA k-IE given
a certificate. For more on certification and certifying algorithms, see [31].
We show that verifying suitable certificates for NFA k-IE is faster than the decision procedure for
NFA k-IE. More specifically, NFA k-IE has certificates that can be verified in O(kℓ· nk+ω−2) time,
which is strictly faster than our deterministic O(k · mnk−1)-time algorithm whenever ℓnω−1 = o(m). For
example, when k = 2, m = O(n2) and ℓ= O(1), i.e., for two dense NFA over a constant alphabet, given
a purported certificate, the verification algorithm runs in time O(kℓn2+ω−2) = O(nω) time while the

A tight SETH lower bound for NFA k-IE is unlikely
We already mentioned in Section 1 that an O(nk−ε)-time algorithm for DFA (and thus NFA) k-IE would
refute SETH, the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis, by a result of Wehar [38, Cor. 7.19]. Our lower
bound of mnk−1 (Theorem 5) is based on the Combinatorial k-Clique Hypothesis [1].
SETH is, in
comparison, more widely used. One can ask whether it is also possible to establish hardness results
similar to Theorem 5 but based on SETH.
We show that, in fact, a tight SETH-based lower bound for NFA k-IE is unlikely. This is a consequence
of our previous result, that our certificate checkers for NFA k-IE (Corollary 2 and Corollary 3) run faster
than the deterministic algorithm for NFA k-IE (Theorem 4).
We need a notion of reduction that distinguishes between the running times O(nk) and O(nk+1).
Let pairs (L, T) correspond to assertions that (membership in) a language L can be decided in T time.

Hypothesis 2 (SETH = Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis [26]; see also [39, 8]). For every ε > 0,

breakthrough in proof complexity. This would also imply new circuit lower bounds [10].

Transition complexity.
We have constructed NFA (and ε-NFA) for languages of the form ∩
i∈[k] L(Ai),
where A0, . . . , Ak−1 are NFA with at most n states in each. The advantage over the standard direct
product construction is that our product automata are sparser, that is, have fewer transitions.
An
interesting open question is whether even sparser constructions exist. Proving lower bounds on the number
of transitions in nondeterministic automata is a delicate task; we refer the reader to publications [20, 25]
and to Section 2.1.2 in the book chapter [21] for an introduction to the state of the art.
Let an NFA recognising a language L be called transition-minimal if, among all the NFA recognising
L, it has the fewest number of transitions. The transition complexity of a language L is the number of
transitions in a transition-minimal NFA that recognises L. Given two regular languages, the transition
complexity of their intersection does not exceed the product of their individual transition complexities [14].
Our Theorem 1 shows that this bound is not optimal when the two languages have dense transition-
minimal NFA.
Comparing Theorem 1 with the standard direct product also suggests the following interpretation:
to obtain an automaton for the intersection of two languages, if one wants to reduce the number of
transitions, one pays a factor of |Σ| in the number of states. This is reminiscent of a conjecture due to
Domaratzki and Salomaa [15]: Given a regular language L over an alphabet Σ, let the minimum number
of states an NFA needs to recognise the language be n. Then, the NFA recognising L that has the least
number of transitions, must have at most |Σ| · n states.
The automata delivered by Theorem 1 have ε-transitions, but our other two product constructions,
the catch-up and leapfrog product, due to being ε-free NFA, are much larger than the nodding product
when the alphabet of interest grows. Still, the catch-up and leapfrog products can be smaller than the
direct product in the worst case, even when the alphabet grows. For an alphabet of size ℓ, over n states,
the (dense) worst case for the upper bounds of Theorems 2 and 3 is when m = ℓn2 and m≤k = n2. Let
k be fixed. The size (number of states and transitions) of the direct product is then O(ℓn2k), while the
size of both the catch-up and the leapfrog automata is bounded above by O(ℓknk−1m≤k) = O(ℓknk+1).
If ℓ= o(n), then ℓknk+1 = o(ℓn2k). Hence, whenever the number of letters in Σ grows slower than the
bound n on the number of states, the worst case bounds for all of our products improve upon those of
the direct product.
Fine-grained complexity.
Fix k ≥2. For the intersection emptiness problem for k DFA, Wehar [38]
proves that, for every real ε > 0, an nk−ε-time deterministic algorithm would falsify SETH. At the
same time, best existing algorithms for this problem run in time O(ℓ· nk), where ℓ= |Σ|; which is
the same as O(mnk−1) if the DFA are complete. If ℓmay grow, there is a gap between the lower and
upper bound. One of our contributions is to close this gap, strengthening the lower bound. Theorem 5
shows that an (mnk−1)1−ε-time algorithm (already for the problem on DFA, not NFA) would falsify the
Combinatorial k-Clique Hypothesis. Our hard instances have automata with |Σ| = Θ(n). Thus, it is
the density of the state-transition diagrams (in particular, m may be as high as n2) that our reduction
exploits. Previously known reductions do not give tight bounds for the case |Q| = o(|∆|), that is, with
the dense state-transition diagram.
In the case |Σ| = O(1), existing lower bounds for the intersection emptiness problem for k DFA are
tight. For NFA, there was previously a gap between Ω(nk) and O(mk). Our algorithmic result improves
the upper bound to O(mnk−1) but the bounds are still a factor of m/n apart. If k = 2, the recent NFA
acceptance hypothesis [9] is that the running time of O(mn) is necessary even if one of the two NFA
accepts exactly one word.
Relation satisfaction problems.
The starting point of our constructions in Section 3 is Observation 1.
The k-stuttering of the intersection of k regular languages3 is the same as the interleaving of the languages
intersected with (ak + bk)∗. This is because the k-tape automaton that realises the equality relation
between regular languages is very similar to an NFA that recognises (ak + bk)∗. By ‘k-tape automaton’
we refer to a nondeterministic automaton with a finite set of control states and k one-way read-only
tapes, where each transition also specifies one of the k tapes, from which an input letter is read.
Observation 1 can be generalised to check whether k regular languages ‘satisfy’ a rational relation [12].
Rational relations are exactly the relations recognised by one-way k-tape automata (for more details see
Chapter 4, section 1.6 of [34]).
3‘2-stuttering’ means that each letter is duplicated in all words; with k, we replace each a ∈Σ with ak; see the end of
Sec. 3.1.

We define the k-Relation Satisfaction problem (k-RS) as follows. Fix k. Given k NFA A1, . . . , Ak as
input, with n states and m transitions, over an alphabet of size ℓ, and a k-tape automaton C with nC states
and mC transitions. NFA A1, . . . , Ak are said to satisfy C if there exist words w1 ∈L(A1), . . . , wk ∈L(Ak)

NFA k-IE is a special case of the more general NFA k-RS problem, but in fact, when the alphabet is
fixed, NFA k-RS is inter-reducible with NFA (k + 1)-IE in a ‘fine-grained’ sense; see Section D.
Further directions.
Our constructions of sparser product automata may be applied to automata other
than NFA. For verification, Büchi automata as well as other automata on infinite words are a natural
target, and the idea of interleaving several runs should apply. Automata with data structures, such as
pushdown automata, can be explored, but we have not identified fine-grained algorithmic improvements
so far.
We also leave it for future work to explore applications in graph databases. Queries to graph databases
can often be interpreted in automata-theoretic terms. In the recent years, fine-grained lower bounds for
regular path query evaluation were obtained by Casel and Schmid [11]; and for Boolean conjunctive
queries by Fan, Koutris, and Zhao [18]. (Casel and Schmid also discuss the ‘product graph approach’,
but when a query arises from a regular expression, the corresponding automaton is sparse.) We refer the
reader to Durand’s survey [16] for a broader overview. A popular type of query related to the intersection
of regular languages is conjunctive regular path queries; see, e.g., Barcelo et al. [4].

Controlled Shuffling
In Section 3, we start by using the idea that the k-stutter of the intersection of k regular languages is
the same as the interleaving of the languages intersected with (ak + bk)∗. This is because the k-tape
automaton that realises the equality relation between regular languages is very similar to an NFA that
recognises (ak + bk)∗. In the nodding product, we ensure that the component automata move one at
a time, to avoid pairing up transitions. In order to ensure they all read the same word, the nodding
product mimics the structure of the equality k-tape automaton, intuitively letting it control which letters
are read by each component.
In this section, we generalise this observation to allow checking whether k regular languages satisfy
any rational relation.
D.1
Fine-grained complexity of NFA k-RS
Rational relations and k-tape automata
We begin by defining the automaton model we call k-
tape automata, as the Rabin and Scott model for one-way k-tape automata; see [34, Chap. 4, sect. 1.6]
and [33].
Definition 8. A k-tape automaton C is a tuple (Q, Σ, ∆, s, F) consisting of a finite set of states Q, a finite
alphabet Σ, an initial state s ∈Q, a set of final states F ⊆Q and a transition relation ∆⊆Q×(Σ×[k])×Q.
Each transition (q, (σ, i), q′) ∈Q × (Σ × [k]) × Q selects the ith of the k read-only tapes, consumes
one symbol of input from the selected tape, and updates the current state from q to q′. At a given cell,
if the ith tape is chosen, the automaton reads the letter written in the current cell and moves one step
forwards on that tape, remaining stationary on all other tapes.
We say the initial tape configuration of C is (w0, w1, . . . , wk−1) if the contents of the tapes, for
all i ∈[k], consists of wi on the ith tape, followed by an endmarker.
We say a k-tuple of words
(w0, w1, . . . , wk−1) is accepted by a k-tape automaton C if C has a run that starts at the initial state,
with initial tape configuration (w0, w1, . . . , wk−1), and reaches a final state, with all k reading heads
ending at the endmarkers of their respective tapes.
The set of k-tuples a k-tape automaton recognises is denoted by L(C). This is also known as the
relation C recognises. The class of relations k-tape automata recognise is known as the k-ary rational
relations [12]. The special case of 2-tape automata are usually referred to as transducers, the interpre-
tation being that symbols on the first tape are inputs being read, and symbols on the second tape are
outputs produced by the transducer.
Definition 9. The k-Relation Satisfaction problem (k-RS) takes as input k NFA A0, . . . , Ak−1 with n
states and m transitions each, over an alphabet of size ℓ, and a k-tape automaton C with nC states and mC
transitions. NFA A0, . . . , Ak−1 are said to satisfy C if there exist words w0 ∈L(A0), . . . , wk−1 ∈L(Ak−1)
such that (w0, w1, . . . , wk−1) ∈L(C).
With this definition, clearly NFA k-IE is a special case of the more general NFA k-RS problem.
Lemma 2. Given k NFA with at most n states and m transitions each over an alphabet of size ℓ, NFA
k-IE reduces to NFA k-RS with the same k NFA and an O(kℓ)-sized k-tape automaton that recognises
the equality relation. This reduction takes O(k(n + m + ℓ)) time.
Moreover, we can reduce NFA (k + 1)-IE to NFA k-RS.
Theorem 9. Given k + 1 NFA with at most n states and m transitions each, over an alphabet of size
ℓ, NFA (k + 1)-IE reduces to NFA k-RS for k NFA with at most n states and m transitions over an
O(ℓ)-sized alphabet, as well as a k-tape automaton at most kℓ· n states and m + (k −1)ℓ· n transitions.
This reduction takes O(m + kℓ· n) time.
It turns out NFA k-RS also reduces to NFA (k + 1)-IE, by modifying the k input NFA and the k-tape
automaton C into appropriate NFA.
Theorem 10. Given k NFA with at most n states and m transitions each and a k-tape automaton with
nC states and mC transitions, over an alphabet of size ℓ, NFA k-RS reduces to NFA (k + 1)-IE for k NFA
with at most O(n) states and O(m) transitions, as well as a new NFA with O(nC) states and O(mC)
transitions, over an O(kℓ)-sized alphabet. This reduction takes O(k2(n + m + l)) time.
Hence, many of our results on NFA k-IE transfer to the NFA k-RS problem immediately.
