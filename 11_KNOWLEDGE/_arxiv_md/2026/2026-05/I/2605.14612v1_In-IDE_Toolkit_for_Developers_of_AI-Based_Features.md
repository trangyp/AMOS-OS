Abstract
AI-enabled features built on LLMs and agentic workflows are diffi-
cult to test, debug, and reproduce, especially for product-focused
software engineers without a machine learning background. We
present the AI Toolkit1 plugin for JetBrains IDEs, which brings
tracing and evaluation directly into the Run/Debug loop. A mixed
methods study with practitioners presents three consistent needs:
(1) make evaluation regular and repeatable, (2) expose traces at the
moment of execution, and (3) minimize setup and context switching.
Guided by these needs, the AI Toolkit introduces an IDE-native
workflow: run-triggered trace capture; immediate, hierarchical in-
spection; one-click “Add to Dataset” from traces; and unit-test–like
evaluations with pluggable metrics. The first release in PyCharm
shows promising early signals – strong conversion when promoted
at Run, sustained usage among those who capture traces, and low
churn – suggesting that IDE-native observability lowers activa-
tion energy and helps developers adopt disciplined practices. We
detail the design and implementation of the AI Agents Debugger
and AI Evaluation, report initial adoption telemetry, and outline
next steps to broaden framework coverage and scale evaluations.
Together, these results indicate that integrating AI observability
and evaluation into everyday IDE workflows can make modern
AI development accessible to non-ML specialists while preserving
software-engineering practices.

Introduction
AI-enabled features are increasingly built on top of large language
models (LLMs) and agentic workflows that orchestrate prompt-
ing, retrieval, tools, and memory. While these systems accelerate
development, they are often experienced as black boxes: behavior
depends on data, context, model versions, and non-deterministic
sampling, making failures hard to localize and successes hard to
reproduce. As teams scale AI features, they need tools for observ-
ing agent behavior, reasoning about changes, and verifying that
improvements transfer from local tests to real projects.
A growing ecosystem addresses parts of this need, including
LangSmith [17], Weights & Biases [3, 31], MLflow [34], and Lang-
Fuse [25]. Collectively, these tools provide (i) tracing to capture
prompts, tool calls, and intermediate states; (ii) evaluation to com-
pare variants with human or automated metrics; and (iii) experiment
tracking to record configurations and outcomes over time. How-
ever, these capabilities are typically offered as separate services
or dashboards, optimized for ML practitioners who already know
which levers to pull and how to stitch them together.
We observe an emerging group of AI-based feature developers:
product-focused software engineers who add LLM/agent capabil-
ities to existing applications without a background in ML. This
cohort is growing rapidly – Gartner projects that by 2027, 55% of
software engineering teams will build LLM-based features [10]. To
solve day-to-day problems, these developers often adapt familiar
monitoring tools rather than adopt new AI-native solutions [26].
There is a clear gap between AI-native tooling and AI-based fea-
ture developers. In our understanding there are several reasons for
that: first, these developers lack the intuition that the new develop-
ment approach is needed for AI systems, including (a) systematic
evaluation beyond ad hoc testing, (b) structured experiments rather
than one-off tweaks, and (c) observability of the behavior of non-
deterministic AI systems. Second, the existing AI-native tooling
is optimized for ML developers and is not intuitive for Software
Engineers. Even when powerful tools are available, the user journey
is unclear: developers are dropped into rich dashboards without

2.2
Interview Insight
We conducted follow-up semi-structured interviews with eight
engineers who build AI-powered applications. In each session, par-
ticipants walked us through how they constructed their systems
and how they determined when an agent’s output is “good enough,”
with a focus on pre-release evaluation practices.
The core insight is that, without an ML background, pre-release
evaluation is often absent: practitioners ship prototypes after man-
ual self-checks and then depend on production metrics and user
feedback to assess quality. Elaborating on this, LLM-based develop-
ment decouples evaluation from training, so teams frequently skip
systematic evaluation entirely. Lacking ML intuitions (e.g., datasets,

2.3
Design Requirements
Combining our mixed-methods findings, the survey highlights dif-
ficulty testing non-deterministic behavior (Table 1), strong demand
for tracing/observability and evaluation (Table 2), and a reliance on
manual testing or production feedback over automated pipelines
(Table 3). Interviews further show that non-ML engineers often
skip pre-release evaluation altogether. We therefore articulate the
following design requirements:
DR1 Prioritize tracing and evaluation. In the IDE-native toolset
we are building, tracing/observability and evaluation must
be high-priority capabilities.
DR2 Datasetless start. Evaluation should naturally incorporate
data from manual testing and production signals; it must not
require a pre-existing dataset.
DR3 Education. We should educate through evaluation (e.g.,
through lightweight guidance or read-to-use templates)
3
Design and Implementation
We present the AI Toolkit plugin1 for PyCharm [14], consisting
of two main components: AI Agents Debugger and AI Evaluation,
aligning with DR1. The former auto-captures and visualizes traces
of LLM/agent launches at Run/Debug; the latter turns examples into
datasets and runs repeatable evaluations with pluggable metrics.
Our approach is to surface AI observability and evaluation di-
rectly at the developer’s Run moment within the IDE, turning an
ad hoc cycle (“try a prompt, look at the output”) into a tight loop of
trace →inspect →curate to dataset →evaluate – with near-zero
setup and without switching to external dashboards.
In order to achieve high adoption, we need to make it an “easy
journey” for the users. Thus, we optimize for three requirements:
UX1 Precise target action: Propose value at the exact moment
when a user needs it and has time for it.
UX2 Clarity of value: Show immediate, visible results.
UX3 Low activation energy: Require minimum configuration,
avoid code edits and external accounts for a useful baseline.
3.1
Tracing and Observability in the Run Loop
Modern tracing tools (e.g., LangSmith [17], LangFuse [25], and
Weights & Biases [3, 31]) capture prompts, tool calls, and intermedi-
ate states, but they typically assume outside-the-IDE workflows. We
instead bind tracing to the IDE’s Run action, so that observability
appears precisely when code executes. To cover the broader agen-
tic Python ecosystem, we support the major agentic framework,
LangChain [6], including LangGraph [16].
Our tracer uses a client–server architecture. At runtime, a light-
weight client, embedded through a Python wrapper, intercepts
framework events via callbacks, serializes them, and streams them
to an IDE-side server over TCP. The server deserializes, processes,
and renders the events in a tool window (Figure 1, step 1.1). When
available, the client also emits the agent’s execution graph, which
the server renders in a dedicated Graph tab (Figure 1, step 1.2).
Tracing is enabled only for projects that the plugin classifies as AI
projects. The plugin indexes project files for imports of langchain
or langgraph; if any are found, it marks the project accordingly.
When the user clicks Run or Debug (Figure 1, step 1), the plugin
starts the local server and launches the user’s code under the Python
wrapper. The wrapper establishes the TCP channel and attaches
a LangChain CallbackHandler [18] to each Runnable [19]. The
handler captures events (node entry/exit, LLM and tool calls, and
other events), serializes them, and streams them to the server.
The tool window updates incrementally as events arrive, making
the tracer compatible with Debug execution: the view reflects the
run “so far,” enabling step-by-step debugging of agent behavior.
Unlike many existing solutions [17, 25, 31], we default to a Pretty
view that surfaces only the signal-bearing fields of each event. In
Figure 1, for example, the first agent node shows only the invoked
tool (read_schedule), while the second agent node shows only
the LLM’s last message. This preserves a readable narrative of the
run without the verbosity of full callback payloads.
For each node, the tracer displays execution time and token
usage, enabling quick profiling of latency and cost.
This design satisfies UX1, UX2, and UX3. For UX1, the tracer
appears exactly when the user clicks Run, and it activates only in
projects that already depend on LangChain [6] or LangGraph [16].
For UX2, we show immediate, visible results: the tracer tool win-
dow appears automatically, and it updates dynamically during the
agent’s run; it also displays the agent’s execution graph, helping
visualize the code-defined structure. For UX3, configuration is min-
imal – the plugin wraps the user’s code automatically and wires
callbacks to the frameworks – keeping activation energy low.
3.2
Evaluation: Unit-tests-like for AI
To make evaluation habitual for software engineers, we model it
after unit tests: developers declare data points as test cases (no hand-
written evaluation loops) and run them on the current code version
without code changes. In this model, a dataset is a table of inputs
with reference outputs; an evaluation executes the user’s code/agent
over each input, collects the generated outputs, and scores them
with a configurable set of evaluators. Evaluators include standard
metrics, LLM-as-a-judge [11], and lightweight custom code that
returns a run score (see step 4 in Figure 1).
From any captured trace in the AI Agents Debugger described
earlier, developers can promote the example directly into the dataset:
inputs are auto-extracted while reference outputs remain editable,
closing the manual-testing loop and providing a low-friction cura-
tion path – thus fulfilling DR2 (see Figure 1, step 1.3).
AI Toolkit then runs the user’s workflow through any in-IDE run
configuration, chosen during the Evaluation Configuration setup
(Figure 1, step 5.1). Under the hood, we instrument the Python
process at launch – monkey-patching the relevant I/O boundaries
– to propagate dataset inputs into the system without code edits,
aligning with UX3. The AI Toolkit captures the run’s trace, and
we reapply the output JSON path specified during dataset curation
(Figure 1, step 2.1) to automatically extract the generated outputs.
The results are presented in a bottom tool window analogous to a
unit-test runner (Figure 1, step 4), where each datapoint appears as
a test case. Developers can inspect the generated output, evaluator

3.3
End-to-end Flow Overview
Rather than pushing developers to external dashboards, we anchor
observability and evaluation to the IDE’s Run action, compressing
the informal “try–look into” routine into an in-IDE loop of trace →
inspect →curate to dataset →evaluate, with minimal setup and no
context switching, as illustrated in Figure 1:
(1) Run & explore traces. The user clicks Run to execute the
agent (1); the AI Agents Debugger shows the trace hierarchy
(1.1); optionally, the agent graph is displayed (1.2).
(2) Curate dataset from a trace. User can choose “Add to
Dataset” (1.3); then the “Add to Dataset” dialog opens (2); the
user configures JSON paths for input and output extraction
(2.1) – the same output path is later reused to automatically
extract generated outputs during evaluation – and verifies
or adjusts the expected output (2.2).
(3) The “Evaluation > Datasets” view is shown (3), followed by
the “Evaluation > Evaluation” view (4).
(4) Create Evaluation Configuration. User clicks the “+” but-
ton to start creating an Evaluation Configuration (4.1); the
“Create Configuration” window opens (5); the user selects
an existing agent Run configuration to execute evaluations
(5.1), chooses a name and input dataset (5.2), and configures
the LLM-as-a-judge [11] prompt and model (5.3).
(5) Run Evaluation and inspect. Back in the Evaluation view
(4), the user clicks Run to launch the evaluation (4.2); outputs
are shown per row (4.3); for any datapoint, the corresponding
trace can be inspected in the AI Agents Debugger (4.4).

release. At the moment, AI Toolkit only contained AI Agents De-

promo campaign included (i) the popup appearing after Run button

the AI Toolkit plugin (ii) description of the plugin in “What’s new“

Reach and interest. In the four weeks following the PyCharm
2025.2 release, we saw 970 new users of the AI Toolkit. The popup
window had a pretty high installation rate (58%), and generated a
one-third of all new users. The other two-thirds arrived from other
sources, mainly the “What’s new“ tab.
Activation. About 40% of those who installed via popup, captured
a first trace. However, only around 12% of users who installed via
“What’s new“, captured a first trace. The main stopper is the frame-
works coverage, not intent: most newcomers (particularly those
arriving from “What’s New”) did not use LangGraph in the current
project, and the released AI Agents Debugger implementation only
supported LangGraph-based agents. Users in unsupported setups
typically ignore the plugin rather than churn.
Retention and churn. By week four, 26% of all installers remained
active; among developers who actually made any runs in PyCharm,
week-four retention rises to 40%. Churn (disable/uninstall) remained
low at roughly 10–15%.
Takeaways. High conversion at Run, solid week-four retention,
and low churn indicate that the IDE-native, run-triggered workflow
is legible and valuable. The principal opportunity is to broaden the
framework support (beyond LangGraph).

Next Steps & Limitations
Next steps.
• Broader framework coverage. Early indicators suggest
that activation will be capped by framework support rather
than developer intent. To reach a broader user base, we
will release an updated plugin with planned support for the
Kotlin agentic framework Koog [13], PydanticAI [7, 24] and
OpenAI Python SDK [23]. Following these releases, we will
monitor usage signals and metrics to guide further devel-
opment and extend support to additional agent stacks as
demand evolves.
• Track the usage of AI Evaluation. We will monitor the
adoption of AI Evaluation by collecting usage statistics. Based
on feedback, we will prioritize UX improvements and tighter
integration between AI Agents Debugger and AI Evaluation.
Limitations and threats to validity.
• Narrow initial coverage. The reported activation reflects
a release that supported only LangGraph, leaving much of
the agentic-framework space uncovered; consequently, true
demand is likely under-estimated.
• Short observation window. Metrics summarize only the
first four weeks of a single release, with a sample of roughly
1,000 users. A larger sample and a longer observation win-
dow will yield more precise estimates.
• PyCharm-only sample bias. We have released a plugin
only for PyCharm [14], while a large portion of developers
use other IDEs [27], such as Visual Studio Code [21].

Related work
The rapid development of AI-based applications, particularly those
powered by autonomous or semi-autonomous agents, is transform-
ing the software landscape [9, 15, 22, 28]. As these AI agents grow
more complex and integrated into diverse workflows, the need for
effective tools to monitor, evaluate, and improve their behavior
becomes increasingly critical [4, 5, 8, 35].
There are several tools that provide agent observability fea-
tures at scale. Among them are LangSmith [17] by LangChain [1],
Weights & Biases [3, 31], MLflow [34], and LangFuse [25]. As men-
tioned earlier, these capabilities are typically offered as separate
tools or dashboards, designed for ML practitioners who already
know which controls to use and how to integrate them effectively.
Evaluation of AI agents is an active area of research [32, 33,
36] and industry [2, 12, 29]. Research efforts focus on develop-
ing general evaluation approaches (e.g., human-based, LLM-as-a-
judge [11]), assessing agent capabilities (e.g., planning, function
calling), and designing evaluation methods for particular domains
(e.g., software engineering agents, data agents) [32]. Industrial solu-
tions emphasize collaboration features and integration with existing
customer infrastructure. The main goal of this work is to provide a
simple, usable evaluation flow directly within the IDE.
A related initiative is the VS Code AI Toolkit by Microsoft [20]. It
follows a similar goal of improving the developer experience for AI
workflows but focuses on a different set of capabilities. Specifically,
it offers tight integration with Azure AI Studio for model fine-
tuning, deployment, and access to a model catalogue, while also
including basic evaluation and traceability features. In contrast to
our approach, which emphasizes lightweight, IDE-native evaluation
and observability, the VSCode AI Toolkit is designed as part of a
broader Azure ecosystem that streamlines the connection between
local development and cloud-based model management.
7
Conclusion
By meeting developers in the IDE and guiding tracing, evaluation,
and experimentation as a single workflow, the AI Toolkit lowers
cognitive overhead and makes disciplined AI development acces-
sible to non-ML specialists. This paper contributes: (i) empirical
evidence of developer needs around non-determinism and ad hoc
testing; (ii) an IDE-native design that ties observability and evalu-
ation to the Run/Debug moment, including run-triggered tracing
and a curated path from trace →dataset →evaluation; and (iii)
an implementation in JetBrains IDEs (AI Agents Debugger and AI
Evaluation) that delivers these capabilities with near-zero setup.
Early usage indicates that this approach is legible and valuable:
developers respond to in-context prompts at Run, successfully cap-
ture traces, and retain the tool at meaningful rates; the main activa-
tion bottleneck is framework coverage rather than intent. While
results reflect a short window and an initially narrow scope, they
provide actionable direction.
More broadly, we argue that bringing AI observability and eval-
uation into the IDE – rather than exporting developers to external
