Identification-Robust Testing in Endogenous Functional Linear Regression with
Weak or Irrelevant Auxiliary Variables
Won-Ki Seo∗
Abstract
We develop dimension-reduction-free tests for the slope function in functional linear regression
when the functional regressor may be endogenous or measured with error. The tests are based on a
functional moment condition induced by an auxiliary functional variable and do not require estima-
tion of the slope function. This feature is particularly useful in infinite-dimensional settings, where the
identification and regularization conditions needed for consistent estimation are often strong and dif-
ficult to verify. The proposed procedures remain asymptotically valid under weak or even failed rel-
evance of the auxiliary variable, and they are consistent against fixed alternatives that are detectable
through the moment operator. We establish the asymptotic null distribution, consistency against de-
tectable alternatives, and local power under drifting alternatives. We also derive the locally optimal
test within a class of weighted test statistics. Feasible critical values for implementation of the tests
are obtained from data. Simulations show reliable size control and competitive power, including under
weak relevance. We illustrate the method using a functional regression analysis of residential electric-
ity demand and temperature distributions in South Korea.

Keywords: endogenous functional linear regression; weak relevance; dimension-reduction-free infer-
ence; functional time series; identification-robust testing.

Introduction
The functional linear model (FLM) is a central tool for relating response variables to complex covariates
such as curves, images, and distributional objects. Important contributions to the study of FLMs include
Bosq (2000), Yao et al. (2005), Mas (2007), Hall and Horowitz (2007), and Imaizumi and Kato (2018); Ramsay
and Silverman (2005) and Horváth and Kokoszka (2012) provide comprehensive reviews of the topic. Much
of this literature operates under the exogeneity assumption that the functional regressor is uncorrelated
with the regression error. In practice, however, this assumption is frequently violated. Endogeneity in
functional regression can arise for a variety of reasons, such as omission of relevant variables, simultaneity
between the response and the regressor, and the regressor being observed only with error. The last issue is
particularly common in functional data analysis, since functional regressors are typically reconstructed by
smoothing discrete and noisy measurements or estimated from raw data such as empirical distributions;
see Chen et al. (2022) and Seong and Seo (2025) for representative discussions. Endogeneity of any of
these forms makes standard exogenous inference procedures unreliable, which has motivated a growing
literature on inference for the endogenous functional linear model, including Florens and Van Bellegem
(2015), Benatia et al. (2017), Chen et al. (2022), Babii (2022), Petrovich et al. (2024), and Seong and Seo (2025).

In this paper, we consider the FLM with a scalar response yt and a functional regressor Xt taking values
in a Hilbert space H,

where ut satisfies E[ut] = 0 and Θ : H →R is a continuous linear map. Although (1.1) is stated in a sim-
plified form to highlight the core components, the framework readily accommodates more general speci-
fications, including those with additional scalar control variables, as detailed in the subsequent sections.
Estimation and inference for the slope parameter Θ, the linear map that links the functional regressor
to the response, are central issues in the FLM literature. When Xt is endogenous, a standard strategy
is to introduce an auxiliary functional variable Zt that is correlated with the functional regressor but
uncorrelated with the regression error. Such a variable is often referred to as an instrumental variable
(IV) in the literature (see, e.g., Florens and Van Bellegem, 2015). The use of Zt yields a functional moment
equation that supports inference without requiring exogeneity of Xt. As we show below, our framework
subsumes the exogenous setting as a special case: when Xt is exogenous, one may set Zt = Xt, and the
procedure reduces to a test for the classical FLM. The framework therefore provides a unified inferential
approach that applies whether or not the regressor is exogenous.
Despite the empirical relevance of endogeneity in functional regression, endogeneity-robust hypothe-
sis testing remains underdeveloped relative to the extensive literature on exogenous FLMs (see, for exam-
ple, Cardot et al., 2003; Hilgert et al., 2013; Su et al., 2017; Dette et al., 2020; Lin and Lin, 2021; Yeon et al.,
2023a,b). To our knowledge, only a few studies, including Babii (2022) and Seo and Seong (2025), address
the testing problem for model (1.1) in the presence of endogeneity. Babii (2022) focuses on inference for
an upper bound of the parameter of interest, while Seo and Seong (2025) develops inference on the slope
parameter itself. As we detail in Section 2.3, however, the method of Seo and Seong (2025) relies on tech-
nical conditions that are stringent and difficult to verify, including spectral-gap and injectivity conditions
on certain covariance operators. These conditions can be overly restrictive in practice, and the same lim-
itation applies to existing function-on-function regression methods that could be adapted to the present
setup. Such restrictions are unattractive for practitioners who, for example, simply wish to test the basic
significance of the functional linear model (that is, H0 : Θ = 0) as a preliminary model check, without
committing to unverifiable assumptions.
A second concern in inference for the endogenous FLM is that, in the infinite-dimensional setting, the
auxiliary variable Zt may not be sufficiently correlated with the endogenous regressor Xt. The literature
commonly imposes regularity conditions on the cross-covariance operator CXZ of Xt and Zt, such as in-
jectivity or specific spectral properties. The injectivity condition, which is typically required for consistent
estimation, demands that CXZv ̸= 0 for every nonzero v of the entire space H or an infinite-dimensional
subspace. This is a restrictive requirement, since the auxiliary variable must be chosen so that CXZv ̸= 0
for infinitely many directions v. Moreover, the condition cannot be verified from finite-sample data.
Both the requirement of sufficient correlation between Xt and Zt and the technical conditions dis-
cussed above are typically essential for consistency of functional estimators and for the asymptotic valid-
ity of estimator-based inference. Since most of these conditions cannot be tested from the data, they im-
pose substantial costs in empirical work. We address these limitations by developing a testing framework

whose asymptotic validity does not rely on consistent estimation of the slope function or on the strength
of the auxiliary variable, a property we refer to as identification robustness.
Our contribution is threefold. First, we introduce a class of tests for the slope function constructed di-
rectly from a functional moment process. The tests do not require a consistent estimator of the slope func-
tion and therefore avoid the regularization and dimension-reduction choices inherent in many estimator-
based approaches. Second, we establish identification-robust validity. The asymptotic null distribution of
the proposed tests does not require the moment operator linking Xt and Zt to be injective or even strongly
informative, and weak or failed relevance affects power rather than asymptotic size. The tests are consis-
tent against any fixed alternative whose deviation from the null is detectable through the moment opera-
tor. Third, we develop a local power theory in which, within a broad class of weighted statistics, the effect
of the weight function on local power is summarized by a single normalized drift parameter, which leads
to an explicit optimality result.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces the notation and presents the
functional linear model with potential endogeneity. Section 3 develops the proposed tests and establishes
their asymptotic properties, including local power. Section 4 addresses the computation of feasible critical
values. Section 5 presents several extensions that broaden the scope of the proposed method. Section 6 re-
ports simulation evidence in support of the theory, and Section 7 applies the proposed tests to an empiri-
cal example concerning residential electricity demand and temperature distributions. Section 8 concludes.
All technical proofs are deferred to the Appendix.

It is commonly assumed in the FLM literature that the regressor Xt is exogenous, meaning that
CXu := Cov(Xt, ut) = E[(Xt −E[Xt]) ⊗(ut −E[ut])] = 0.
(2.3)
Under the present mean-zero assumption, CXu simplifies to E[Xt⊗ut]; we retain the centered form in (2.3)

the exogeneity assumption is frequently violated in practice (see, e.g., Florens and Van Bellegem, 2015;
Benatia et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2022; Petrovich et al., 2024; Seong and Seo, 2025). As noted by Seong and
Seo (2025), in many applications, the functional regressor of interest is often incompletely observed and is
typically constructed by smoothing a finite number of discrete samples, the number of which may not be
sufficiently large. Consequently, the regressor used in practice almost always deviates from the intended
true functional observation, which can give rise to endogeneity. Beyond measurement errors, certain
regressors considered by practitioners may be inherently endogenous; we refer the reader to Section 2.1 of
Seong and Seo (2025) for specific examples. A standard approach to address this is to introduce an auxiliary
functional variable Zt satisfying
CXZ := Cov(Xt, Zt) ̸= 0
and
CZu := Cov(Zt, ut) = 0.
(2.4)
The condition CXZ ̸= 0 is a minimal relevance condition on Zt; more stringent conditions, such as
CXZv ̸= 0 for all nonzero v in a sufficiently large subspace, are typically imposed in the literature for
identification and consistent estimation. While such stronger relevance is also desirable for power of our
tests, it is not required for the asymptotic validity of our tests under the null; one of the main features of
our procedure is that weak or even failed relevance affects power rather than size. The framework also
includes the classical exogenous case as a special case: when Xt is exogenous, one may take Zt = Xt, in
which case (2.4) reduces to the standard requirements CXX ̸= 0 and CXu = 0. The proposed procedure
therefore applies whether or not the regressor is exogenous.

Empirical application
Climate change has altered temperature distributions in ways that extend well beyond shifts in the mean:
extreme temperature events, including heat and cold waves, have become more frequent and severe, and
these tail occurrences are primary drivers of electricity demand for cooling and heating. This distributional
characteristic of temperature risk motivates replacing scalar temperature summaries (such as mean and
variance of temperature) with the full temperature distribution as a predictor of residential electricity
demand. We apply our proposed tests to the empirical framework of Nam and Seo (2026), who model
this distributional relationship, and provide the first formal statistical test of whether the temperature
distribution contributes explanatory power beyond what its standardized moments can capture.

The LHR specification presents a particularly instructive contrast. The LHR transformation, log pt(s)−
log(1 −Pt(s)), is the primary specification used by Nam and Seo (2026) to study how electricity demand

2%, so the null is clearly rejected. By contrast, when the endogeneity correction is dropped by setting
Zt = Xt, the LHR p-values rise to between about 10% and 33%, and the null cannot be rejected at
the 10% level in any configuration. This pattern is consistent with measurement error in the estimated
temperature distribution inducing bias that is especially consequential for tail-sensitive specifications such
as LHR: ignoring endogeneity can lead to the conclusion that high-temperature distributional effects are
insignificant, whereas the proposed auxiliary-variable approach restores statistical significance.
The QF specification stands apart: p-values are large in all configurations and especially high when
lagged auxiliary variables are used, indicating that the quantile-function predictor does not contribute
significant explanatory power once scalar controls and seasonal effects are included. As discussed above,
this likely reflects the qualitatively distinct interpretation of the QF model rather than a failure of the
distributional approach. As Nam and Seo (2026) discuss, the s-quantile of temperature varies seasonally,
so θ(s) in the QF model averages over these seasonal fluctuations and tends to be attenuated relative to
θ(s) in the other PDF-based specifications; their paper similarly finds slope parameters close to zero for
the QF specification.
Overall, the empirical evidence strongly supports the functional linear model for density-based repre-
sentations of temperature distributions. The conclusions for CLR, LRHR, LCDF, and PDF are robust across
the auxiliary-variable choices, while the LHR specification highlights the practical importance of correct-
ing for measurement-error endogeneity in tail-sensitive inference.

Conclusion
This paper develops identification-robust tests for the slope function in the functional linear regression
model with a potentially endogenous functional regressor. The proposed tests are based on a functional
moment condition induced by an auxiliary functional variable and require neither dimension reduction
nor consistent estimation of the slope. They remain asymptotically valid under the null regardless of the
strength of the linear relationship between the regressor and the auxiliary variable: weak or failed rele-
vance reduces power but does not distort size. A local asymptotic power analysis establishes that endpoint
evaluation is the locally optimal weighting within a natural family of weighted statistics, and a feasible
implementation based on a kernel estimator of the long-run covariance is provided and shown to preserve
the asymptotic properties. Monte Carlo experiments confirm good finite-sample size control and power
properties consistent with the asymptotic theory, and demonstrate that existing tests based on exogene-
ity or consistent estimation can break down when their underlying assumptions fail. An empirical appli-
cation to residential electricity demand and temperature distributions provides formal statistical evidence
that the full temperature distribution contributes explanatory power beyond scalar summaries, support-
ing recent approaches that model the distributional effects of climate change on energy demand.
