Abstract
Van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures, formed by stacking two-dimensional materials, offer
highly tunable electronic and optical properties, with the twist angle between layers acting as a
critical tuning parameter. While its impact on moir´e patterns, band structure, and correlated states
is well-established, the influence of twist angle on ultrafast charge transfer remains controversial.
Here, we employ time- and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (trARPES) to directly probe
ultrafast charge transfer in epitaxially grown WS2-graphene heterostructures with twist angles of
0◦and 30◦. Upon photoexcitation at ¯hω = 3.1 eV, we observe efficient charge separation at 0◦,
while at 30◦, electron and hole transfer occur at similar rates. Our results highlight the crucial role
of the twist angle in controlling charge separation efficiency, offering valuable insights for designing
vdW heterostructures for applications in photovoltaics and optoelectronics.
INTRODUCTION
Van der Waals (vdW) heterostructures are artificial materials created by stacking different
2D solids on top of each other, held together by weak vdW forces. This allows for the design
of materials with highly tunable electronic and optical properties, independent of lattice
matching constraints. In this context, the twist angle serves as a powerful tuning knob as
it influences the periodicity of the moir´e potential [1] and thereby moir´e excitons [2] and
phonons [3], band structure [4, 5], and electron-electron interactions [6]. At specific magic
twist angles, such as ∼1.1◦in twisted bilayer graphene, vdW heterostructures develop flat
electronic bands resulting in the emergence of strongly correlated states [7, 8].
The twist angle is also expected to influence ultrafast charge transfer processes across
different vdW interfaces [9–11] which are crucial for applications in light harvesting and
detection. In this context, literature provides a variety of experimental observations. A
coherent microscopic understanding, however, seems to be missing. For example, the rate of
ultrafast hole transfer from MoS2 to WS2 was found to be independent of the interlayer twist
angle [12, 13], while the rate of ultrafast electron transfer in WSe2-WS2 [14] and MoS2-WSe2
heterostructures [15] showed a strong variation with twist angle. Recent ultrafast electron
diffraction experiments suggest that charge transfer from MoS2 to graphene gets slower with
increasing twist angle [16]. Finally, efficient charge separation was found to occur in WS2-
graphene heterostructures with a twist angle of 0◦[17–19] while no indication for charge

To shed light onto this issue, we use time- and angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy

provide fundamental insights into controlling charge separation efficiency through the twist
angle, enabling the rational design of vdW heterostructures for advanced applications in
photovoltaics and optoelectronics.
METHODS
WS2 islands were grown by chemical vapor deposition from solid precursors [21, 22] on
top of hydrogen-intercalated epitaxial graphene on SiC(0001) [23, 24]. The WS2 islands
exhibit a triangular shape with edge lengths ranging from 300 nm to 700 nm [18, 19] and
grow mainly with twist angles of either 0◦or 30◦with respect to the underlying graphene
layer, as demonstrated using low-energy electron diffraction (LEED) in Fig. 1.
TrARPES experiments were performed at a repetition rate of 1 kHz using 3.1 eV pump
pulses with a fluence of 0.4 mJ/cm2 and 21.7 eV probe pulses. ARPES spectra were recorded
with a hemispherical analyzer that collects photoelectrons from the 300 µm-sized area on
the sample surface illuminated by the probe pulse, thus averaging over many different WS2
islands. The temporal and energy resolutions for the measurements presented in this work
were 200 fs and 350 meV, respectively.

a double-exponential decay with decay times of τ1 = 0.17 ± 0.05 ps and τ2 = 2.0 ± 0.5 ps,
respectively. Details about the exponential fits are provided in the Supplemental Material.
In Fig. 4a we present transient peak positions of the WS2 VB and CB for both 0◦and
30◦islands. Details about the data fitting are provided in the Supplemental Material. We
find that the bands of the 0◦islands shift up, while the bands of the 30◦islands shift down.
The transient band gap (see Fig. 4b) is obtained by subtracting the VB position from the
CB position. Both kinds of WS2 islands show a transient reduction of the band gap that
exponentially recovers on a timescale of a few picoseconds. After removing the contribution
of the transient band gap renormalization (subtracting ∆Egap/2 from the transient position
of the CB and adding ∆Egap/2 to the transient position of the VB, see [18, 19]), we find
that the bands of the 0◦WS2 islands exhibit a transient upshift of 169 ± 8 meV, while the
bands of the 30◦WS2 islands exhibit a transient downshift of 40 ± 2 meV with exponential
lifetimes of a few picoseconds (see Fig. 4c).

The gain above the equilibrium position of the WS2 VB follow a single-exponential decay

VB exhibits an up-shift for a twist angle of 0◦and a down-shift for a twist angle of 30◦.
DISCUSSION
Resonant excitation of the A-exciton of WS2 at ¯hωpump = 2.0 eV has been shown to be
followed by ultrafast hole transfer into the graphene layer, resulting in a charge-separated
state with a lifetime on the order of one picosecond [17–19, 27]. The following microscopic
picture for ultrafast charge transfer emerged: Despite the weak vdW interaction between
WS2 and graphene, density functional theory calculations showed that the two layers hy-
bridize, resulting in wave functions that are delocalized over both layers (“charge transfer
states”) [19], enabling efficient charge transfer between the layers [28–30]. Electrons and
holes then relax to the Fermi level located inside the Dirac cone of graphene by the emission
of WS2 A1g and graphene E2g phonons [31]. Hole transfer is faster than electron transfer
because the tunneling matrix element for holes is bigger than the one for electrons and the
potential barrier for holes is smaller than the one for electrons [18]. In addition to this
intrinsic tunneling channel, tunneling can also occur through sulphur vacancies [32, 33], a
common defect of monolayer WS2 [34]. Note that, in the present study, the WS2-graphene
heterostructures were excited at ¯hωpump = 3.1 eV rather than at the A-exciton resonance.
This elevated excitation energy opens an additional, highly efficient intrinsic charge-transfer
channel for holes in the WS2 valence band [35].
The data for the 0◦WS2 islands as well as for the graphene π-band are in perfect agree-
ment with this scenario [17–19]. Here, ultrafast charge separation is responsible for the

asymmetric population dynamics inside the Dirac cone in Fig. 3a and the charging shifts
in Fig. 4c and d, where excess electrons (holes) inside the WS2 (graphene) layer reduce
(increase) the binding energy of the WS2 (graphene) bands. The transient reduction of the
WS2 band gap in Fig. 4b is caused by light-induced free carriers that efficiently screen the
Coulomb interaction [36]. The gain above the equilibrium position of the WS2 valence band
in Fig. 3c contains contributions from a transient broadening and upshift of the WS2 valence
band.
As shown in Figs. 3c and 4c, the 30◦WS2 islands exhibit behavior distinct from their 0◦
counterparts. Unlike the 0◦WS2 islands, which become negatively charged, the 30◦islands
acquire a positive charge, matching that of the graphene layer (Figs. 4c,d). This suggests
that charge separation is unlikely to account for our observations. Furthermore, Fig. 3b
reveals that electrons leave the conduction band at the same rate for both twist angles.
The lack of charge separation in the 30◦islands therefore implies that the hole transfer rate
must be comparable to the electron transfer rate. We speculate that the transient downshift
of the WS2 bands observed in Fig. 4c may result from a back-gate effect induced by the
positively charged graphene layer. Finally, the shape of the pump-probe signal in Fig. 3c
can be attributed to two competing effects: instantaneous band broadening and a band
downshift with slower rise time (see Fig. 4a).
In summary, our results indicate that for a twist angle of 0◦, hole transfer is significantly
faster than electron transfer, whereas for a twist angle of 30◦, electron and hole transfer
occur at comparable rates.
These observations are in good agreement with previous trARPES data on MoS2-graphene
heterostructures with a twist angle of 30◦that also show no indications of charge separation
[20]. Further, Ref. [16] reports that charge transfer from MoS2 to graphene gets slower with
increasing twist angle. While a direct comparison with our trARPES data is difficult as Ref.
[16] does not distinguish between electron and hole transfer, this finding is consistent with
our observation that hole transfer is faster for a twist angle of 0◦than for a twist angle of
30◦.
In an attempt to understand the link between the twist angle and the presence or absence
of charge separation, we now compare our results to previously published theoretical work
that discusses MoS2-graphene [37] and WS2-graphene heterostructures [38] with twist angles
of 0◦and 30◦. The most obvious difference between the band structures corresponding to

the two twist angles is that for a twist angle of 0◦the K-points of both the transition
metal dichalcogenide (TMDC) and the graphene layer are folded onto the K-point of the
mini Brillouin zone of the heterostructure, while for a twist angle of 30◦the graphene K-
point is folded onto the Γ-point of the mini Brillouin zone. Further, both references report
different band alignments for twist angles of 0◦and 30◦. For a twist angle of 0◦the graphene
Dirac point is located close to the center of the TMDC gap at K. For a twist angle of 30◦
the graphene Dirac point moves closer to the TMDC conduction band. We also observe
this trend in our data (see differences in binding energy for negative delays in Fig. 4a).
The measured differences in band alignment are, however, much smaller than predicted
theoretically. Ref. [37] further predicts a transition from direct to indirect MoS2 band gap
when changing the twist angle from 0◦to 30◦that is difficult to verify based on the measured
band structures shown in Fig. 2.
Ref. [38] also plots the orbital composition of the states in the band structure, revealing
the presence of charge transfer states with delocalized wave functions for both twist angles.
Based on their calculations we can estimate the heights of the energy barriers that electrons
and holes need to overcome in order to reach the closest charge transfer state. We estimate

interpreted with caution due to the artificial strain applied to the graphene layer in the
calculations, the estimated energy barriers suggest that increasing the twist angle from 0◦
to 30◦should accelerate electron transfer and slow down hole transfer — consistent with the
experimental trend for holes but not for electrons. We would like to point out that tunneling
of electrons through sulphur vacancies [32, 33] is expected to be independent of twist angle
as the sulphur vacancy states are delocalized over the full Brillouin zone. Thus, the absence
of any twist angle dependence in Fig. 3b would be consistent with a scenario where electron
transfer is dominated by sulphur vacancies in this particular sample.
Estimating the magnitude of the tunneling matrix element from the calculations in [38]
is challenging, since the hybridization-induced avoided crossings are not distinctly resolved
in the band structure. However, [38] predicted that the interplay between interlayer and
intralayer excitons strongly varies with twist angle. Because of this, both the absorption and
the charge transfer between the layers were found to change. More precisely, efficient charge
separation with electrons localized in the WS2 layer and holes localized in the graphene

layer was found to occur for a twist angle of 30◦but not for 0◦[38]. Further, [39] combined
time-dependent density functional theory with nonadiabatic molecular dynamics to predict
ultrafast charge separation in WS2-graphene heterostructures with a twist angle of 30◦.
These predictions conflict with both our own trARPES measurements [17–19] and previous
work by others [20], which consistently show ultrafast charge separation at a twist angle of
0◦but not at 30◦. A microscopic understanding of the experimental data clearly requires
further theoretical investigations.
While band structures and charge transfer states in the model discussed above are mate-
rial specific, the underlying concept is more general. Therefore, we provide a short discussion
on the influence of the twist angle on ultrafast charge transfer in vdW heterostructures con-
sisting of two different monolayer TMDCs. There, Ref. [29] predicts a strong hybridization
between the bands of the individual layers in the VB at Γ. Since the Γ points of the two
layers coincide for any twist angle, the hole transfer rate should remain unaffected by twist
angle. For the CB, however, Ref. [29] predicts hybridization at momenta k > 0 only. Twist-
ing the two layers with respect to each other then increases the momentum offset between
the bands of the individual layers for any non-zero momentum away from Γ. Hence, hy-
bridization and thus electron transfer is expected to be highly sensitive to the twist angle
between the layers. This scenario is consistent with the findings of Refs. [12, 14, 15]. Ref.
[40], however, reports ultrafast (< 40 fs) electron transfer from WSe2 to MoS2, independent
of the angular alignment of the layers. We speculate that the temporal resolution of the
experiment of 190 fs may have been too poor to resolve the evolution of the electron transfer
rate with twist angle.
Finally, in addition to ultrafast charge transfer, ultrafast energy transfer is known to play
an important role in vdW heterostructures [41–43]. In contrast to ultrafast charge transfer
that requires the participating layers to hybridize, ultrafast energy transfer is mediated by
dipole-dipole interactions. Since the latter are independent of twist angle, ultrafast energy
transfer cannot explain the distinct behaviour of WS2-graphene heterostructures with twist
angles of 0◦and 30◦observed in the present manuscript.

SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK
We used trARPES to investigate how the twist angle affects ultrafast charge separation
in WS2-graphene heterostructures. Charge separation is observed at a twist angle of 0◦but
not at 30◦where electron and hole transfer proceed at comparable rates. We discussed the
role of energy barriers and the tunneling matrix element. A full microscopic understanding,
however, requires further theoretical investigations. Our findings provide crucial informa-
tion for optimizing the performance of vdW heterostructures for specific optoelectronic and
photovoltaic applications.
