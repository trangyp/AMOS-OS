Abstract
We study the problem of determining a matrix whose kth multiplicative compound is a prescribed
matrix M.
The cardinality of the set of matrices whose kth multiplicative compound equals M is
characterized in terms of rank(M).
On the one hand, if rank(M) ≤1, it is shown that there exist
infinitely many such matrices for which a complete characterization is determined. On the other hand,
if rank(M) > 1, then there exists a unique matrix — up to an overall sign — whose compound is M.
An algorithm for finding a matrix whose compound equals M is detailed, and its time complexity is
analyzed.

Introduction
Compound matrices are a classical object in linear and multilinear algebra, appearing in a range of ap-
plications, including geometry [10], graph theory [4, 9], algebraic topology [21], numerical analysis [1, 26],
and electric network theory [6]. More recently, they have proven useful in dynamical systems and control
theory [5]. Following the seminal works of Schwarz [32] and Li and Muldowney [22, 25], which introduced
compound matrices into this setting, there has been growing interest in their use for studying linear and non-
linear systems. System operators with so-called variation-diminishing and -bounding properties have been
studied in [11, 13, 15, 16] with applications to model order reduction [14], frequency domain analysis [12],
bounds on self-sustained oscillations [33], as well as sparse optimization [23]. Autonomous system analysis
brought forward new results on the global stability of systems with multiple equilibrium points [3, 37], with
applications ranging from power systems [7], to opinion dynamics [28] and neural networks [31].
All of these results follow a common paradigm: a classical condition formulated in terms of a matrix
(e.g., the Jacobian of the vector field of an autonomous system or the system operators associated with an
input-output system) is lifted to a condition on its kth compound. As a concrete example, a linear time-
invariant system x(t+1) = Ax(t) has been called k-positive if all entries of the kth multiplicative compound
of A have the same sign [2]. Since the signs of the entries of a matrix and its compounds are not invariant
under coordinate transformations, one may ask whether there exists a coordinate transformation that makes
a given system k-positive (see also [35] which studies a related problem in the continuous-time setting and
for a restricted class of coordinate transformations). This naturally leads to the question of whether a given
matrix M can be realized as the kth multiplicative compound of some matrix A, and, if so, how such a
matrix can be recovered from M.

Despite the long history of compound matrices, this inverse problem has not been considered before to
the best of the authors’ knowledge. Given a matrix M of appropriate dimensions, two fundamental questions
arise: (i) what are necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a matrix A whose kth multiplicative
compound is equal M? (ii) assuming such a matrix exists, how can one characterize and compute all matrices
A satisfying this relation?
In this paper, we address the second question. We provide a complete characterization of the preimage
of the kth multiplicative compound operator, showing in particular that the preimage is unique — up to
an overall sign — under certain conditions, and that otherwise it contains infinitely many matrices. We
then develop a constructive algorithm for recovering all matrices A whose kth compound equals M, and
show that the computational complexity of the algorithm is polynomial in the dimension of the matrix. A
MATLAB implementation of the proposed algorithms is available at https://github.com/debojyoti23/
inverse-compound.

Conclusion
In this paper, we presented an algorithm, which, given a compound-decomposable matrix M ∈R(
n
k)×(
m
k),
provably finds a matrix A ∈Rn×m such that the kth multiplicative compound matrix of A equals M. The

dimensions of M.
There are several directions for future work. First, the application of this algorithm to questions in
dynamical systems and control theory will be the focus of follow-up work. Second, the question of whether a
given matrix is compound-decomposable remains open. Finally, assuming a given matrix is not compound-
decomposable, it might still be useful to find matrices whose kth multiplicative compound is the best possible
approximation, and designing an algorithm to find such matrices will be done in future work.
