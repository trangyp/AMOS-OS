Abstract
Altermagnetism is a compensated magnetic phase characterized by zero net magnetization and exchange-driven
spin splitting. However, identifying altermagnets among collinear antiferromagnets usually requires full magnetic-
space-group or spin-group analysis, which is not intuitive. Here we formulate a simple real-space criterion based on
how the crystallographic operations of the host nonmagnetic structure permute the two opposite-spin sublattices.
For simplicity, we focus on collinear compensated antiferromagnets commonly described by type-III magnetic
space groups, whose magnetic primitive cell coincides with the host nonmagnetic crystallographic primitive cell. In
this class, altermagnetic spin splitting is generally allowed unless inversion operation exists which transfer between
the two opposite-spin sublattices. First-principles calculations on representative noncentrosymmetric and
centrosymmetric materials demonstrate these criterions. Similar rules can also be applied to low-dimensional
crystals or pseudocrystals. Our work reduces the identification of altermagnetism to a transparent real-space
symmetry test and provides a practical route for discovering altermagnetic crystals.


## Page 2


Altermagnetism has recently emerged as a distinct class of collinear magnetic order, in which fully compensated
antiparallel spin sublattices produce zero net magnetization while exchange-driven spin splitting persists even in the
absence of spin-orbit coupling (SOC)[1–6]. This unusual combination of zero global net magnetization and spin-
polarized band structure has opened a route toward spin functionalities unavailable in conventional ferromagnets or
spin-degenerate antiferromagnets[7–10]. Yet identifying altermagnets remains challenging and less intuitive,
because it generally requires knowledge of magnetic-space-group or spin-group analysis, often together with
corepresentation theory for antiunitary symmetries[11–13]. The resulting complexity hinders systematic searches or
design of new altermagnetic materials and motivates us to provide a more direct real space criterion[14,15].
Here we formulate such a criterion for collinear fully compensated antiferromagnets by examining how the
crystallographic operations of the host nonmagnetic structure (i.e., when the spin character is removed) act on the
two opposite-spin sublattice sites. In the absence of SOC, spin is a good quantum number. i.e., the band energies
can be labeled as εn(k,↑) and εn(k,↓), where k is the momenta. Because the system is globally antiferromagnetic, the
system must have equal number of crystallographically equivalent spin-up and spin-down sites, forming two spin-
sublattices. If the spin-cell is a supercell of the host nonmagnetic cell and the spin-up and spin-down sublattice is
connected simply by the host translational vector T, then T εn(k,↑) = εn(k,↓) because Tk = k , i.e., the spin is
degenerate for all k vectors[16,17] (see more detailed discussion below). Therefore, in this study, we only focus on
systems whose magnetic primitive cell coincides with the nonmagnetic crystallographic primitive cell, commonly
described by type-III magnetic space groups[13]; for brevity, we refer to them as type-III-MSG antiferromagnets
below.
Within this class, we show that the generic spin splitting can be classified by inspecting inversion-type operations
and their action on the two spin sublattices. If inversion is absent, or if inversion exists but preserves each spin
sublattice, exchange-driven spin splitting is symmetry-allowed at generic momenta. If inversion exchanges the two
opposite-spin sublattices, same-k spin degeneracy is enforced throughout the Brillouin zone. This simple real-space
criterion turns the identification of altermagnetism into a transparent symmetry test based on real-space sublattice
permutation. We demonstrate it using representative centrosymmetric and noncentrosymmetric type-III-MSG
antiferromagnets and discuss how little-group operations restore spin degeneracy at selected high-symmetry
momenta.
Real-space criterion for spin degeneracy—The criterion is most transparently formulated by tracking how
crystallographic operations of the host nonmagnetic structure act on the two opposite-spin sublattices. As stated
above, for a collinear antiferromagnet in the absence of SOC, spin remains a good quantum number, allowing the
band energies to be labeled as εn(k,↑) and εn(k,↓).

whether an inversion-type operation exists and how it acts on the two opposite-spin sublattices.
Real-space classification—According to the above criterion, type-III-MSG antiferromagnets can be divided into
three cases.
Case I: no inversion-type operation——The first case is that no inversion-type operation exists in the
nonmagnetic crystallographic structure. In this noncentrosymmetric case, no global inversion-based operation can
enforce same-k opposite-spin degeneracy throughout the Brillouin zone. Although other sublattice-exchanging
operations g−∈G−may exist, Eq. (4) shows that they generally map a state at k to an opposite-spin state at a
symmetry-transformed momentum Rg−k, which is not equivalent to k at a generic momentum. Therefore, same-k
opposite-spin degeneracy is not globally enforced, and exchange-driven spin splitting is symmetry-allowed at
generic momenta. This case corresponds to noncentrosymmetric altermagnets. Same-k spin degeneracy can still be
restored at special momenta where a sublattice-exchanging operation g−∈G−satisfies Rg−k = k + K, typically at
high-symmetry points, lines, or planes, as discussed below in terms of the little-group constraint.
Case II: sublattice-preserving inversion—The second case is that an inversion-type operation exists but preserves
each spin sublattice,
Ĩ ∈G+.
In this case, inversion belongs to the same-sublattice operation set defined in Eq. (1). According to Eq. (3), it only
imposes an equivalence within the same spin channel,
εn(k,σ)=εn(-k,σ), σ=↑,↓.
Because Ĩ does not exchange the two spin sublattices, it does not impose the opposite-spin relation in Eq. (4) and
therefore cannot enforce same-k opposite-spin degeneracy. Thus, same-k opposite-spin degeneracy is not globally
enforced, and exchange-driven spin splitting is symmetry-allowed at generic momenta. This case provides a
centrosymmetric route to altermagnetism. As in Case I, spin degeneracy may still be restored at special momenta if
a sublattice-exchanging operation g−∈G−also satisfies the little-group condition in Eq. (5).

Little-group constraint—The classification above determines whether opposite-spin degeneracy is enforced at
generic momenta. In the altermagnetic cases, namely Case I and Case II, no global operation enforces such a
degeneracy at generic momenta. However, spin splitting does not necessarily survive at every momentum. At high-
symmetry points, lines, or planes, the little group of k may contain a sublattice-exchanging operation that satisfies
the same-k condition in Eq. (5).

Therefore, even when altermagnetic spin splitting is allowed at generic momenta in case I and Case II, the
degeneracy can be locally restored at special momenta if the little group contains a sublattice-exchanging operation.
In contrast, if Eq. (10) is not satisfied, namely Gk∩G-=⌀, then no little-group operation at k exchanges the two
opposite-spin sublattices. All little-group operations preserve each spin sublattice and impose only same-spin
equivalences. Therefore, they cannot enforce
εn(k, ↑) = εn(k, ↓),
and same-k spin splitting remains symmetry-allowed at this momentum.
A sublattice-exchanging operation outside the little group may still relate the two spin channels through Eq. (4).
In that case, for g′′∈G- but g′′ ∉Gk, one obtains
εn(k, ↑) = εn(Rg'' k, ↓),
where Rg'' k is not equivalent to k. The opposite-spin counterpart of a state at k therefore appears at a symmetry-
transformed momentum rather than at the same k. This momentum-separated opposite-spin pairing is the
characteristic band-structure feature of altermagnets.

Conclusion—We have established a real-space symmetry criterion for altermagnetism in type-III-MSG
collinear compensated antiferromagnets following the Neumann’s principle, εn(k, ) = εn(Rg k, g). Since the
primitive spin-translation operation is absent in this class, global same-k opposite-spin degeneracy is normally
absent unless an inversion-type operation exchanges the two opposite-spin sublattices. In this case, the combined
spin-inversion operation restores the equivalence between the two spin channels and forbids altermagnetic splitting
in the absence of SOC. At high-symmetry momenta, the little group further determines whether the splitting
survives locally. Using MnS, Mn2SSe, Fe2B, and CrSb as examples, we show both noncentrosymmetric and
centrosymmetric routes to altermagnetism. Our analysis clarifies that altermagnetic spin splitting is governed by
real-space sublattice permutation rather than by centrosymmetry alone, providing a physically transparent and

practically useful symmetry principle for identifying, classifying, and designing altermagnetic crystals. Such
criterion can be easily extended to other systems such as low-dimensional materials and pseudocrystals.
