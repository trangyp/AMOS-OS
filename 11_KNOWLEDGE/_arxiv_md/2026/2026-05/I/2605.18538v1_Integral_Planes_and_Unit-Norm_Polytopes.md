INTEGRAL PLANES AND UNIT-NORM POLYTOPES
DANIELE CORRADETTI
Abstract. We introduce and study integral planes associated with crys-
tallographic and non-crystallographic integral systems in real composition
algebras. For an integral order O in such an algebra we define the plane
O2 with quadratic form Q(x, y) = N(x) + N(y), the axis shell, the bal-
anced shell, and the corresponding unit-normalised spherical polytopes.
For ten crystallographic orders we recover, in one uniform construction,
the orthogonal-direct-sum root systems 2A1, A2 ⊕A2, 4A1, D4 ⊕D4,
16A1, and E8 ⊕E8 (with classical-polytope realisations including the
square, the 16-cell, the 24-cell, and the Gosset polytope 421); for two
non-crystallographic orders we obtain H2 ⊕H2 (decagonal tegum) and
H4⊕H4 (600-cell tegum) over Z[φ]. We prove a rank-obstruction theorem
that closes, unconditionally and by a purely Coxeter-theoretic argument,
the existence question for an indecomposable rank-eight golden octonion
order: no such order can exist. On the balanced shell side, we identify
the genuine algebraic Hopf map hA(a, b) = (2a¯b, N(a) −N(b)) and prove
that its restriction to the balanced shell is a finite principal fibration of
the unit loop, valid both for the associative case and for the alternative
Moufang case.
Keywords: integral plane · composition algebra · root-shell system · icosian
ring · finite principal fibration · Moufang loop · rank obstruction.

1. Introduction and Motivation
Unital composition algebras, also known as Hurwitz algebras, are indispens-
able tools in geometry. Even more so the four real division Hurwitz algebras
that, up to isomorphism, are the reals R, the complexes C, the quaternions
H and the octonions O. Inside each of them lives, in a canonical sense, an
arithmetic skeleton: the Gaussian integers Z[i], the Eisenstein integers Z[ω],
the Hurwitz quaternions, and the Coxeter–Dickson integral octonions. These
four arithmetic skeletons are familiar to algebraists, lattice theorists and
crystallographers alike; they sit at the intersection of normed algebra, integral
quadratic-form theory, and root-system combinatorics.
A more recent development, in part motivated by aperiodic order and the
geometry of icosahedral symmetry (see Patera [14] for a survey), enlarges
the picture. If one is willing to let the coefficient ring grow from Z to Z[φ],
where φ = (1+
√
5)/2, then the non-crystallographic Coxeter systems H2, H3,
H4 enter the same arithmetic stage; the relevant orders are the cyclotomic
ring Z[ζ10] inside C and the icosian ring I inside H. Building on the recent
axiomatic framework of non-crystallographic integral root-shell systems over
Z[φ] established in [8], we ask in this paper a complementary question: what
spherical polytopes appear if, instead of treating O as an algebra in its own
right, one considers the integral plane O2 with the natural quadratic form
Q(x, y) = N(x) + N(y), and reads off the root system from the resulting unit
shell?
The present work proposes a single construction that organises these
crystallographic and non-crystallographic arithmetic skeletons into one shared
geometric object. Given an integral order O in a real composition algebra,
with quadratic norm N and polar form B, we consider the integral plane
O2 = {(x, y) : x, y ∈O},
Q(x, y) = N(x) + N(y).
This pair (O2, Q) is an integral quadratic R-module whose unit-norm shell
S1(O2) is a finite spherical polytope. The same definition simultaneously
yields the classical root-polytope doublings, 2A1 for Z, A2 ⊕A2 for the
Eisenstein integers, D4 ⊕D4 for the Hurwitz quaternions, E8 ⊕E8 for the
Coxeter–Dickson integral octonions, and their non-crystallographic counter-
parts H2 ⊕H2 and H4 ⊕H4.
In order to give an introduction we have to say that three themes run
through the paper.
The first is the doubling theme. The integral plane is a uniform construction
that produces, in one stroke, the orthogonal-direct-sum root systems R ⊕R
for the root systems R realisable as unit shells of integral orders. We separate,
throughout, the algebraic structure (root system over R) from the geometric
one (classical polytope, in the sense of Coxeter and Grünbaum). The unit shell
of the Gaussian order, for example, is the root system 2A1 (two orthogonal
copies of A1, eight elements through length-doubling and centrality); as a
polytope, its convex hull is the square, also known as the 2-cross polytope

2. Composition algebras and integral orders
Throughout the paper, A is a real composition algebra which means a real
algebra A equipped with a non-degenerate quadratic form N : A →R which

is multiplicative, i.e. N(xy) = N(x)N(y) for all x, y ∈A. The polar form is
B(x, y) = N(x + y) −N(x) −N(y) = x¯y + y¯x,
where ¯x = B(x, 1) · 1 −x is the canonical involution. By Hurwitz’s theorem
(see Schafer [15], ch. III, or Springer–Veldkamp [16], ch. 1) the unital real
composition algebras are, up to isomorphism, R, C, H and O.
In case of the real, i.e., A = R, one has trivially N(x) = x2 and ¯x = x
while for the complex case A = C, N(a + bi) = a2 + b2. In the quaternionic
case A = H, N(a + bi + cj + dk) = a2 + b2 + c2 + d2 while for the octonions
A = O, N is the standard Cayley-Graves form. In all cases the unit shell
{x ∈A : N(x) = 1} = Sd−1 is closed under multiplication, conjugation and
inversion; it is a Lie group for d ≤4 and the Moufang loop S7 for d = 8.
2.1. Integral orders and ranks. Given an algebra A, one might want to
introduce the notion of an integer order over it. Let R ∈{Z, Z[φ]}. By
an R-order in A we mean a finitely generated R-module O ⊂A satisfying
1 ∈O, ¯O = O, O · O ⊆O, N(O) ⊆R, and the bilinear form B restricting
to R-bilinear O × O →R. For an R-order O of R-rank r, the underlying
Z-rank is r if R = Z and 2r if R = Z[φ]. When the choice is not obvious
from the context we say "Z[φ]-rank r" or "Z-rank r" explicitly. By default
rank means Z-rank.
The orders we consider are listed below.
Example 2.1. The list of orders studied in this paper is:
(i) R: the order Z ⊂R, Z-rank one.
(ii) C: the Gaussian order Z[i], the Eisenstein order Z[ω] with ω = e2πi/3,
and the cyclotomic order Z[ζ10] with ζ10 = e2πi/10.
(iii) H: the Hamilton order Z⟨1, i, j, k⟩, the Hurwitz order, and the icosian
ring I ⊂H of Z[φ]-rank four (Z-rank eight).
(iv) O: the Cayley–Graves order Z⟨1, e1, . . . , e7⟩, and the Coxeter–Dickson
maximal order whose minimal vectors realise the E8 root system.
Hybrid intermediate orders (the so-called 2A2 in H, the 8A1 in O, and the
2D4 in O) are constructed inside the same H and O as direct sums of two or
four classical orders and appear in Table 1 for completeness.

Sm(O) = {x ∈O : N(x) = m}.

3. Integral planes and their shells
Having fixed the algebraic prerequisites, we now turn to the central con-
struction of the article. Given an integral order O, we form the integral
plane O2 with quadratic form Q(x, y) = N(x) + N(y) and analyse its unit
shell. Within S1(O2) we isolate two natural sub-shells, the axis shell and
the balanced shell, and prove the no-splitting criterion that forces the full
unit shell to coincide with the axis shell. This is the technical engine of the
doubling phenomenon that organises Sections 4–7.

integral plane of O is the R-module O2 = O ⊕O equipped with the quadratic
form Q(x, y) = N(x) + N(y) ∈R and the polar form BQ((x, y), (x′, y′)) =
B(x, x′) + B(y, y′).

3.2. The no-splitting criterion.
Lemma 3.6 (No-splitting criterion). Let O be an R-order in A such that
the only solutions of 1 = a + b with a, b ∈N(O) totally positive or zero in R
are (a, b) ∈{(1, 0), (0, 1)}. Then S1(O2) = A(O).
Proof. If N(x) + N(y) = 1 with both summands totally positive or zero, the
only possibility is (N(x), N(y)) ∈{(1, 0), (0, 1)}. By Hurwitz’s theorem the
real composition algebras have positive-definite norms, so N(z) = 0 forces
z = 0.
□
Lemma 3.7. In Z, the only decomposition 1 = a + b with a, b ∈Z≥0 is the
trivial one. In Z[φ], the only decomposition 1 = a + b with a, b totally positive
or zero is (a, b) ∈{(1, 0), (0, 1)}.
Proof. For Z[φ]: write a = a0 + a1φ, b = 1 −a. Total positivity of a and
1 −a in both Galois embeddings gives 0 < 2a0 + a1 < 2, so a1 = 1 −2a0;
substituting back forces a0 ∈(0.309, 0.724), an interval containing no integer.
Hence a ∈{0, 1}.

4. Crystallographic plane shells
We now apply the integral-plane machinery to the seven classical crystal-
lographic orders, ie., the integers, the Gaussian and Eisenstein orders, the
Hamilton and Hurwitz quaternionic orders, the Cayley–Graves and Coxeter–
Dickson octonionic orders, together with their three hybrid intermediates.
The no-splitting criterion of §3 reduces the unit shell of O2 to the axis shell,
and the doubling proposition identifies, line by line, the resulting root system
with the orthogonal direct sum recorded in Table 1.

6. A rank obstruction for non-crystallographic root-shell
systems
The natural question after Theorem 5.3 is whether a rank-eight indecom-
posable golden octonion order exists, in analogy with the Coxeter–Dickson
order in the crystallographic case.
The answer is negative by a purely
Coxeter-theoretic argument.
Theorem 6.1 (Rank obstruction). Let T be a finite root-shell system over
Z[φ] in the sense of Definition 2.3, indecomposable and non-crystallographic
(i.e. some Cartan coefficient does not lie in Z). Then
rank(T) ∈{2, 3, 4},
and the underlying reflection group W = ⟨rα : α ∈T⟩is one of H2, H3, or
H4.
Proof. By (R1) (T finite, central-symmetric) and (R3) (closed under reflec-
tions), the group W permutes the finite set T and acts on the real vector
space V = spanR T. Any w ∈W fixing T pointwise fixes V , hence is the
identity. The map W →Sym(T) is therefore injective and W is finite. Being
generated by reflections, W is a finite real reflection group.
Indecomposability of T corresponds to irreducibility of W on V .
By (R2), all elements of T have the same length: T is a single-length
(equilateral) root system, so W has a single W-orbit of root lengths.
The classification of finite irreducible real reflection groups (Humphreys [11],
Thm. 2.7 and Table 6.1; Bourbaki [2], ch. 4–6) lists, among single-length
groups:
• crystallographic: An (n ≥1), Dn (n ≥4), E6, E7, E8;
• non-crystallographic: I2(m) (m ≥5, m ̸= 6), H3, H4.
The non-crystallographic assumption excludes the crystallographic row.

Corollary 6.2 (Unconditional non-existence of a full-rank indecomposable
golden octonion order). There is no Z[φ]-order G3 ⊂O whose unit shell
SG3 = S1(G3) is an indecomposable non-crystallographic root-shell system
over Z[φ] in the sense of Definition 2.3 and has full R-rank 8.
Proof. By Theorem 6.1, every indecomposable non-crystallographic root-

dimR spanR T ≤4. Hence the hypothesis of a full R-rank-8 shell is incom-

□
The hypothesis that SG3 have full R-rank 8 is the natural requirement
in the present article: a non-crystallographic order in O whose unit shell
sits in a proper sub-octonion subalgebra would, by the doubling theorem
(Proposition 3.8) and the lower-rank classification (Theorems 5.1 and 5.3),
produce only an axis shell whose root system is H2 ⊕H2, H3 ⊕H3 (if such an
order existed in H), or H4 ⊕H4, already covered by the article. The genuinely
new structural possibility, an indecomposable rank-8 non-crystallographic
root-shell system inside O, is the case Corollary 6.2 excludes. The case
of intermediate R-rank 5, 6, or 7 is also excluded by Theorem 6.1. It is
also worth noting that the obstruction is purely Coxeter-theoretic: it does
not use the octonionic structure. The argument applies, mutatis mutandis,
to any composition algebra of dimension ≥5, to any non-crystallographic
indecomposable root-shell system over Z[φ], and to any extension of the
coefficient ring by a single quadratic surd.
Remark 6.3 (Alternative definitions that keep the question open). If one
weakens the root-shell-system axioms, the rank obstruction may no longer
hold and a "G3" of a different kind might exist. Concretely:
(a) Drop (R2): allow multiple root lengths; the resulting two-length golden
quasi-systems can have higher rank, but are no longer single-length
root-shell systems.
(b) Weaken (R3): require closure under a subset of reflections rather than
all rα, α ∈T; the configuration is no longer the root system of a Coxeter
group.
(c) Replace (R4): require Cartan coefficients in a larger ring, e.g. Z[ζ2m] ∩R
for m ̸= 5; the rank constraint then shifts.
(d) Drop the reflection-group structure entirely and require only multiplica-
tive closure (a finite IP Moufang sub-loop): this is the object of §7 below,
and admits the 240-element M240(E8) as a rank-eight example, although
the latter is crystallographic as a root system.
Each of (a)–(d) is a legitimate alternative pursuit; none is excluded by
Theorem 6.1. We pursue none of them in this article.

7. Balanced shells and finite principal fibrations
After analysing the axis shell, we turn to the second natural sub-shell of O2,

of this section is to clarify a recurrent terminological hazard in the literature:
the finite map of interest is not the topological Hopf fibration S2d−1 →Sd
but its discrete companion, the principal fibration π(u, v) = uv−1 of the unit
loop U = S1(O) over itself. We will see how the algebraic Hopf map restricts
to the balanced shell, factors through the equator of Sd, and there coincides
with π, both in the associative and in the alternative Moufang case.

9. Conclusions and Future Developments
We have introduced the integral plane O2 over an integral order in a real
composition algebra, defined its axis and balanced shells, and proved a uniform
classification of the resulting unit-norm polytopes: ten crystallographic orders
give the orthogonal-direct-sum root systems 2A1, A2⊕A2, 4A1, D4⊕D4, 16A1,
E8 ⊕E8; two non-crystallographic orders give H2 ⊕H2 and H4 ⊕H4 over Z[φ].
We have separated, throughout, the algebraic root-system identification from
the geometric polytope-name identification, correcting a small but recurrent
confusion in the literature between the cross-polytope βn (a polytope) and
the root system nA1.
We then have proved, by a purely Coxeter-theoretic argument, that no
indecomposable rank-eight golden octonion order can exist (Corollary 6.2).
On the balanced shell side, we showed that the algebraic Hopf map hA(a, b) =
(2a¯b, N(a) −N(b)) restricted to the balanced shell of an integral order takes
its image on the equator of Sd and there coincides with the finite principal
fibration π(u, v) = uv−1; the latter is a trivial principal U-fibration of U × U
over U, valid in the alternative Moufang case via Artin’s two-generated
associativity (Theorem 7.5).
Several directions are natural and we plan to address them in forthcoming
work.
First of all, beyond the analytic theorem, one would like a fully enumerative
confirmation of the Moufang principal fibration on M240(E8) in an explicit
convention for the twisted product. The script-level verification is conceptu-
ally straightforward but depends on a careful basis choice; we report this as
a follow-up reproducibility task. On the other hand, the rank-obstruction
theorem (Theorem 6.1) closes Definition 2.3 as a vehicle for indecomposable
golden octonion orders of rank ≥5. Nevertheless, our remark 6.3 listed
four legitimate ways to weaken the definition: dropping single-length (R2),
weakening reflection closure (R3), changing the Cartan-coefficient ring (R4),
or replacing the reflection-group structure with a multiplicative-only finite
inverse-property Moufang sub-loop. Each of these is a genuine open pro-
gramme. The most promising is, in our taste, the multiplicative-only setting:
it would describe finite IP Moufang sub-loops of the octonion unit loop over
Z[φ], of which the icosian shell H4 is the prototype; the Coxeter–Dickson
Moufang loop M240(E8) is its crystallographic counterpart. A classification
of such sub-loops over Z[φ] would be the natural next discovery.
A second alternative is to allow two root lengths and to ask whether
a rank-eight non-crystallographic quasi-system over Z[φ] can be realised.
Theorem 6.1 does not exclude this case (it relies on the single-length axiom).
The natural candidate would be a φ-modification of the F4 root system or of
a quasi-E8 configuration; we have not pursued the question.
Finally, the integral plane studied here is affine. Its projective companion,
P(O2), has been studied for crystallographic orders in connection with the

magic square and the exceptional Lie groups (Springer–Veldkamp [16], ch. 4);
the non-crystallographic counterpart is essentially absent from the literature.
All in all, we have shown that the integral plane O2, deceptively simple as a
definition, encodes both the doubling of root-shell polytopes and the principal
fibration of the corresponding unit loops, and that the existence question for
an indecomposable rank-eight golden octonion order is settled in the negative
by a clean Coxeter-theoretic argument. We hope the synoptic Table 1 and
the explicit rank-obstruction theorem will serve future investigators as a map
of a small but rich corner of the geometry of composition algebras.
