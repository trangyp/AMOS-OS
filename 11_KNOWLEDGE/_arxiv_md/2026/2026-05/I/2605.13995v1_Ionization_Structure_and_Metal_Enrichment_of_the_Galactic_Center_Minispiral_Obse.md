ABSTRACT
Sgr A* is the nearest quiescent supermassive black hole, and its proximity offers a unique opportunity
to study its surrounding fuel supply.
We leverage extensive spatial and spectroscopic information
provided by the JWST/MIRI MRS instrument to disentangle mid-infrared ionized gas structures in
the central 0.1 parsec of the Galaxy. The Galactic Minispiral’s Bar and Northern Arm are revealed by
their distinct morphological and kinematic signatures. Several compact (< 1′′) gas structures including
X7 also appear within ∼0.05 parsec of Sgr A* in the plane of the sky, moving with blue-shifted radial
velocities ≳600 km/s. Fine structure line measurements spanning ionization energies ∼7 −55 eV
are used to constrain the incident radiation field, metal abundances (neon, argon, sulfur, nickel, and
iron), and dust depletion/destruction for each identified gas structure. Overall, the Minispiral gas
metallicity is ∼1 −2.5 Z⊙, with a Wolf-Rayet star-driven ionizing radiation field, and significant
nickel and iron dust destruction. Increased flux at energies ≳41 eV suggests that the compact gas
structures experience an additional harder ionizing radiation source, which is most likely driven by
localized fast radiative shocks from stellar winds, a hypothetical Sgr A* outflow, and/or interactions
with the ambient medium.

thought to have electron densities ne ∼104 −105 cm−3

kinematics of the Minispiral encodes both present-day
inflow of material (e.g., T. Paumard et al. 2004; J.-H.
Zhao et al. 2009, 2010) and accumulated feedback from
the stellar population and potentially Sgr A* (e.g., D.
Lutz et al. 1993; F. Yusef-Zadeh & M. Wardle 1993; K.

such as carbon dioxide. In contrast, IR line emission in-

the latter probing the ionization structure and chemistry
of the Minispiral gas.
The ionizing radiation field that bathes the central
parsec arises through a combination of past/episodic
Sgr A* activity K. Koyama et al. (1996); M. Clavel et al.
(2013); A. Goldwurm et al. (2026), numerous O/B/WR
stars (e.g., T. Paumard et al. 2004; F. Martins et al.
2007), and shocks generated by the collisions of orbit-
ing gas parcels and winds (F. Najarro et al. 1997; J.
Moultaka et al. 2004; Q. D. Wang et al. 2013). Using
the Infrared Space Observatory (ISO) Short Wavelength
Spectrometer (SWS), D. Lutz et al. (1996) detect ions
such as [Ne II], [Ne III], [S IV], and [O IV] from the Min-
ispiral gas (see Table 1 for line properties). T. Paumard
et al. (2004) trace the Minispiral gas in He I and Br γ
emission, finding that it has a roughly uniform compo-
sition and an ionization state dependent on proximity
to nearby hot stars. J. P. Simpson et al. (2007) find
that gas located ∼20 parsec away from Sgr A* (in pro-
jection) is excited by photoionization, with a secondary
contribution from localized shocks.

With the JWST Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI)’s
Medium Resolution Spectrograph (MRS; M. Wells et al.
2015), we spatially map numerous fine-structure lines
emitted by the Minispiral gas at sub-parsec scales, build-
ing on earlier IR studies (e.g., D. Lutz et al. 1993, 1996;
J. C. Shields & G. J. Ferland 1994; U. Giveon et al.
2002; T. Paumard et al. 2004; P. Vermot et al. 2025).
This allows us to characterize the Minispiral gas’ ioniza-
tion structure and chemical composition, which is im-
portant for building an accurate picture of the Sgr A*
fuel reservoir.
The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2 we
outline our data reduction process for producing images
and spectra from the MIRI MRS cubes. Section 3 de-
scribes how we fit mid-IR spectral lines and use them to
estimate the physical properties of the Minispiral gas.
Section 4 presents measurements of key diagnostic line
ratios and element abundances for multiple identified
Minispiral gas structures. Section 5 leverages these re-
sults to characterize each Minispiral gas structure’s ion-
ization state and chemical composition in the context of
the GC stars, gas, dust, and Sgr A*. Finally, in Section 6
we summarize our key findings.
2. DATA CALIBRATION
2.1. Observations and Data Reduction
As part of JWST Program ID (PID) 4572 (D. Hag-
gard et al. 2023), we collected two sets of MIRI MRS ob-
servations of the GC central ∼0.1 parsec on 2024 April
8 and 9. The dataset consists of two full-spectrum, four-
point dither cubes (spectral channels 1–4 with the short,
medium, and long gratings; 22.2 minutes of exposure per
grating) and eleven longer exposure observations (spec-
tral channels 1–4 with the short grating; 22.3 hours per
channel). The full wavelength ranges covered by chan-
nels 1–4 (hereafter abbreviated Ch1–4) are: 4.9 −7.65
µm (Ch1), 7.51 −11.70 µm (Ch2), 11.55 −17.98 µm
(Ch3), and 17.70 −27.90 µm (Ch4) (see Figure 2). At
Sgr A*’s distance, 1 pc corresponds to roughly 25′′.
We calibrate the data using the JWST pipeline jwst
version 1.17 (H. Bushouse et al. 2025) with pmap ver-
sion 1322. In-depth calibration details are provided in
S. D. von Fellenberg et al. (2024), S. D. von Fellen-
berg et al. (2025, hereafter SVF25), and J. M. Michail
et al. (2026), all of which utilize the same dataset as this
work. In the Level 2 pipeline, we include the residual
fringe removal step13. We run each observation through
the Level 3 pipeline separately and incorporate pointing
offset corrections to mitigate astrometric errors. Sub-
sequent postprocessing steps include convolution (de-

2.2. Continuum Subtraction
To isolate line emission, we first model and subtract
the continuum.
For the native resolution cubes, we
subtract on a per-spaxel basis (producing a continuum-
subtracted line cube), while for the convolved cubes we
use aperture-extracted spectra (Section 3.1).
For each identified emission line, we extract a ∼0.15
µm-wide spectral subset centered on the line’s rest wave-
length. This is broad enough to encompass both contin-
uum and line emission for the Minispiral’s velocity range
in our field of view (FOV; see Section 1). To model the
continuum, we first mask out the 0.02 −0.08 µm-wide
“line region” (equivalently, a velocity range of ∼1000
km/s). The exact width depends on the line’s broad-
ness and the spectral resolution. In addition to the line
mask, we exclude prominent emission/absorption fea-
tures that could bias the continuum fit, and any pixels
with un-physical flux values (e.g., NaN pixels flagged by
the pipeline).
We define a polynomial model to describe the contin-
uum emission on either side of the masked line region.
The polynomial is of either first or second order depend-
ing on the spectral resolution and complexity of the lo-
cal continuum shape. The fitted model is interpolated to
span the full ∼0.15 µm width (including the line region)
and then subtracted from the spectrum. This results in
a continuum-subtracted spectral subset containing only
the line emission (see Figure 3).
Before fitting models to the continuum-subtracted
lines, we correct for residual fringing in the spectrum. In
addition to the JWST pipeline de-fringing mentioned in
Section 2.1, we apply a de-fringing procedure that is de-
scribed in detail in SVF25. The method combines Fac-
tor Analysis and Gaussian Processes to learn the fring-
ing profile spatially and spectrally around each emission
line. The fringe profile is subsequently interpolated and
removed to isolate the line emission.
3. ANALYSIS
We detect numerous emission and absorption features;
this work focuses on 13 forbidden fine structure emission
lines with peak signal-to-noise (S/N) ≳10 spanning a
range of ionization potentials (IPs) from 7.9 −54.93 eV
(Table 1).
The lines originate from iron (Fe), nickel
(Ni), sulfur (S), argon (Ar), neon (Ne), and oxygen
(O). These elements fall in one of two groups: (1) α
elements (sulfur, argon, neon, and oxygen), formed by
helium nuclei capture in massive stars and during core
collapse supernovae, and (2) iron peak elements (nickel
and iron), formed primarily during Type Ia supernovae.
We also report the Pfα 7.46 µm hydrogen recombination
line, which is useful for measuring absolute abundances
relative to hydrogen (Section 4.3).
The detected H2
lines (Figure 2) are the subject of a separate study, but
preliminary analysis indicates that they trace shocked
molecular gas that is primarily not co-spatial with the
Minispiral structures considered in this work.

3.2. Line Fitting
We construct models of the continuum-subtracted, in-
tegrated emission lines that consist of one to three Gaus-
sian components and fit them to the data using scipy’s
curve fit function. For each line, we select the model
whose reduced χ2 statistic is closest to 1 and whose com-
ponents are detected with at least 3σ confidence. In al-
most all cases, the spectra are best fit by double or triple
Gaussian models. An example model fit for [S IV] 10.51
µm is shown in Figure 3.
Ions whose lines are better fit with a single Gaussian
include [Fe II] and [O IV]. For [Fe II], the lack of ad-
ditional fit components is likely due to most of the de-
tectable iron emission being further ionized to [Fe III].
For [O IV] 25.89 µm, residual fringing prohibits a con-
fident detection of more than one fit component (see
N. Crouzet et al. 2025 for further discussion of fringe
modeling difficulties in Ch4). Likewise, we do not de-
tect [Ne V] 14.32 µm or 24.32 µm, which is unsurprising
given this ion’s large IP (97 eV).

3.3. Abundance Calculation
We convert the extinction-corrected line fluxes to ionic
abundances. These absolute abundances must be nor-
malized to a hydrogen recombination line or other mea-
sure of ionization. The most commonly used line is Hβ,
but it is far outside MIRI’s wavelength range. Instead,
we use the Pfα line assuming Case B recombination
with coefficients from P. J. Storey & D. G. Hummer
(1995). These give the appropriate Pfα and Hβ emis-
sivities (calculated with PyNeb; V. Luridiana et al. 2015;
C. Morisset et al. 2020; C. Mendoza et al. 2023). These
emissivities are combined with the measured Pfα flux to
infer the Hβ flux for each line fit component and aper-
ture, which are then used as a flux normalization to infer
each ionic abundance. The requisite atomic data are in-
cluded in PyNeb and supplemented with [Ni II] atomic
data from Chianti v11.0.2 (G. Del Zanna et al. 2021;
R. P. Dufresne et al. 2024).
All relevant ionic abundances are summed to estimate
the total element abundance (denoted as Xi for element
i, hereafter solar-normalized in log form). One version
of the Xi calculation uses fixed ne = 103 cm−3 and
Te = 7, 500 K (SVF25), and another version varies ne
and Te: ne = 105 cm−3 and Te = 13, 000 K for the Bar;
ne = 3 × 104 cm−3 and Te = 6, 000 K for the Northern
Arm; and ne = 4 × 104 cm−3 and Te = 7, 000 K for
BC structures. The varying ne,Te prescription is more
physically motivated based on previous measurements
(Section 1), but the fixed ne,Te prescription provides a
useful metric to assess how the assumed physical condi-
tions affect Xi.
When calculating the total elemental abundance, all
gas ionization states must be accounted for. If an ion-
ization state(s) is not observed, its ionic abundance
must be inferred using an ionization correction factor
(ICF). For neon and argon, the IP required to reach the
next ionization state is ≳60 eV, high enough that the
ICF is likely negligible14. In contrast, sulfur, iron, and
nickel may be more affected. The observed ion emission
strengths indicate that unobserved S+ (IP = 10 eV),
Fe3+ (IP = 30 eV), and Ni3+ (IP = 35 eV) may con-

Abundance statistical uncertainties are estimated us-

of each line (including both the fine-structure and Pfα
lines), we draw 100 random samples from Gaussian dis-
tributions defined by the component’s flux and uncer-
tainty. Ionic and elemental abundances are computed
for each sample using the approach described above.
These are converted to log space and normalized to so-
lar abundances by subtracting randomly sampled values
(drawn in log space from their reported uncertainties)
from M. Asplund et al. (2021), yielding distributions
of Xi. We adopt the 16th–84th percentiles of the MC
distributions as 1σ uncertainties. To account for the ad-
ditional uncertainty associated with unobserved ioniza-
tion states, we also compute the abundance spread after
multiplying the observed Xi by the minimum/maximum
of the chosen ICF range.

5.1.2. Iron Peak Elements
There is a relative enhancement of nickel compared to
iron in the Northern Arm (XNi/XFe ∼2 −4 depending
on their assumed ICFs), which is surprising given that
nickel and iron should be formed and depleted via sim-
ilar physical mechanisms (e.g., E. M. Burbidge et al.
1957; S. E. Woosley & T. A. Weaver 1995; A. Arcones
& F.-K. Thielemann 2023). D. Lutz et al. (1996) also
observe enhanced nickel-to-iron flux, though they do not
calculate absolute abundances. Our reported XNi/XFe
could be spurious if due to a biased abundance mea-
surement(s), or it could be due to a real physical en-
hancement (depletion) of gas phase Ni (Fe). Below, we
consider some of the most likely scenarios.
The main suspects for distorting XNi/XFe are the
atomic data and assumed physical conditions16.
In-
correct atomic data could specifically bias Ni3+ (J. E.
M´endez-Delgado et al. 2021). However, the de-reddened
[Ni III]/[Fe III] flux ratio is doubled in the Northern Arm
compared to the Bar (∼1.4 and ∼0.7, respectively; see
Table 4), prior to imposing atomic data assumptions.
Nickel should also not be skewed by our choice of ne
so long as ne < ncrit ∼106 cm−3 (Section 3.3 and Ta-
ble 1). Iron would also require a large ne ∼106 cm−3
for XNi/XFe to reach solar levels (Appendix D.2). Over-
all, we conclude that neither nickel nor iron should be
biased in a way that produces the observed XNi/XFe,

combined WR stellar field (F. Martins et al. 2007).

Balakrishnan et al. (2026b),
the inferred additional harder radiation is unlikely to
originate from Sgr A*’s accretion flow in its quiescent or
flaring state. Their CLOUDY simulations predict that the
peak mid-IR line fluxes arising from Sgr A*’s circum-
nuclear gas are too low to be detected by JWST MIRI.
After ruling out a radiative accretion flow contribution,
the next most likely scenario is radiative shocks.17 Fast
radiative shocks are expected to generate EUV/soft X-
ray radiation fields capable of enhancing high-ionization
species (e.g., M. G. Allen et al. 2008). In a MIRI MRS
study of low luminosity active galactic nuclei, L. Zhang
et al. (2025, 2026) model fast shocks moving at several
hundred km/s that display mid-IR line excitation indica-
tive of harder radiation fields. Given the BC structures’
vrad ∼500−600 km/s, they are likely to experience sub-
stantial ram pressure capable of generating these types
of shocks. In the present work we cannot distinguish
between shocks driven by interactions with the ambient
central parsec gas, the WR stellar winds, or a hypothet-
ical Sgr A* outflow (e.g., A.
Ciurlo & M. R.
Morris
2026). Future models of the shock-driven radiation field
that include the influence of WR stellar winds will be
useful for disentangling these scenarios and their influ-
ence on the BC structures.

5.3. Interpreting the Minispiral Gas
The Minispiral Bar, Northern Arm, and BC structures
are each described by a unique ionization state and com-
position. Table 3 summarizes a selection of these gas
structures’ traits, including their assumed ne, U, inci-
dent radiation hardness, Z, and dust depletion factor.
The Minispiral gas being mildly super-solar suggests
that it shares a common origin with the majority of the
surrounding GC material (Section 5.1), including the
NSC and young massive WR stars. This is consistent
with an in-situ star formation scenario (e.g., I. A. Bon-
nell & W. K. M. Rice 2008; A. Hobbs & S. Nayakshin
2009), in which a dense gas disk forms around Sgr A*
and subsequently collapses to produce the observed stel-
lar population. Although similar abundances could arise
if the stars and gas formed farther out in the GC and
migrated inward together, such scenarios are generally
disfavored for the young massive stellar population (see
e.g., S. F.
Portegies Zwart et al. 2003; T.
Paumard
et al. 2006; A. Feldmeier-Krause et al. 2015; S. D. von
Fellenberg et al. 2022).
Our measured metal abundances in the central ≲0.1
parsec can inform composition assumptions in future
gas dynamics and accretion simulations (e.g., J. Cuadra
et al. 2015; D. Calder´on et al. 2016; S. M. Ressler et al.
2020; D. Calder´on et al. 2025). While the α elements
have Z ∼1 −2.5Z⊙, the gas phase iron peak elements
are sensitive to dust and ICF assumptions. Dust deple-
tion and destruction may play an important role in gas
clumps’ ability to cool and fall on to Sgr A* (e.g., K.
Omukai et al. 2005; L. Ciotti & J. P. Ostriker 2007; D.

Namekata et al. 2014). D. Calder´on et al. (2025) find
that the Z ≈3Z⊙regime may represent a threshold that
regulates whether or not gas can cool efficiently into a
Sgr A* accretion disk. Future simulations that take into
account our varied gas phase metal abundances and dust
depletion may help elucidate the gas cooling efficiency.
6. CONCLUSION
In this study, we constrain the composition and ion-
ization state of three Minispiral gas features: the Bar,
Northern Arm, and fast-moving gas parcels that we call
blueshifted compact (BC) structures.
All these Minispiral structures have solar or mildly
super-solar metallicity. Variations between elements are
generally attributed to the assumed physical conditions
and/or dust depletion.
Depletion of gas phase nickel
and iron indicate that the Minispiral is both dusty and
shocked. Nickel is particularly enhanced in the Northern
Arm and BC structures compared to the Bar, which may
be due to a combination of differential dust depletion
and nucleosynthetic enrichment.
The inferred ionizing radiation field is generally ex-
plainable by the combined flux of the central WR stars
(F.
Martins et al. 2007).
Variations in this field as
a function of region position and gas structure can
be attributed to a combination of different gas den-
sities, distances/shielding from ionizing sources, and
shocks/other external radiation sources.
The BC structures are particularly intriguing, since
small gas clumps may be the main source of fuel for
Sgr A* accretion (G. Witzel et al. 2014; S. Gillessen
et al. 2012; D. Calder´on et al. 2025; S. Gillessen et al.
2026). The shared composition between the BC struc-
tures and the surrounding Minispiral gas suggest a com-
mon origin. However, the BC structures appear to ex-
perience a harder incident radiation field than the Bar
or Northern Arm, along with significant dust destruc-
tion. While Sgr A*’s accretion flow is ruled out as the
driver of this hard ionizing flux (M. Balakrishnan et al.
2026b), fast radiative shocks provide a natural pathway
for destroying dust and generating the harder radiation
field (D. Lutz et al. 1993; S. M. Ressler et al. 2018;
M. Balakrishnan et al. 2024b; L. Zhang et al. 2026).
These localized shocks may be generated by interactions
with the ambient medium, WR stellar winds, and/or
Sgr A* outflows.
Future higher S/N measurements of
IR line emission at IP ≳41 eV (e.g., [O IV] or [Ne V])
would help diagnose whether these small scale gas fea-
tures truly experience harder shock-driven radiation.

We generate a collection of images that map out superposed emitting structures moving at different vrad. Each

B. DATA ARTIFACTS
B.1. [Ar II] Cruciform Artifact
[Ar II] extracted from Regions 1 and 2 shows a small flux dip (≲5% of the peak flux) at red-shifted/longer
wavelengths (left panel of Figure 8). This absorption dip appears in both our science and the background observation
files, and so it cannot be easily corrected by background subtraction. This issue is identified as the ‘cruciform artifact’
(I. Argyriou et al. 2023)18.
The MIRI MRS point spread function (PSF) can scatter bright line features into neighboring slices. This stray
light signal is automatically removed by the straylight step in the JWST calibration pipeline. Very bright lines in
complicated, resolved fields, can have a slightly over subtracted artifact that then shows up as a ’dip’ in flux. Spectrally,
this dip manifests most prominently in the bright line’s wings. The cruciform artifact is most likely to appear in Ch1
and Ch2, and all other detected lines in those channels (Table 1) are too faint to be noticeably impacted (1 −2 orders
of magnitude fainter than [Ar II]).
To diagnose the impact of the cruciform artifact and its over-subtraction on our line fits, we re-run the calibration
pipeline without the straylight step enabled. In the corrected version (Figure 8 left panel), the over-subtraction
spatially appears as negative flux (denoted with red pixels), and spectrally appears as a flux dip in the redshifted tail
of the line. Meanwhile, in the un-corrected version ((Figure 8 middle panel), there is an artificial ‘stripe’ of enhanced
flux (blue pixels) due to the cruciform artifact’s influence on the PSF, and a corresponding bump in the spectrum.
For both the corrected and un-corrected data cubes, we fit a spectrum extracted from an aperture positioned over
the cruciform stripe using the procedure outlined in Section 2 (Figure 8 right panel). The cruciform artifact does
not significantly alter the resulting fit parameters. We recalculate XAr for each fit component using the procedure

B.2. [Fe III] and [Ni III] Rest Wavelengths
Compared to the other emission lines, [Fe III] shows a redshift offset of ∼60 km/s across all spectral fit components
and apertures (Figures 4 and 9). This is likely an artifact of a poorly constrained rest wavelength, since a physical
Doppler shift should vary spatially and between different fit components.
Based on the redshift, the line’s rest
wavelength seems closer to 22.93 µm, whereas the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) reports
22.925 µm. This redshift does not appear in other Ch4 lines, so it is unlikely a result of poor spectral resolution.
This would not be the first time that a rest wavelength needs to be updated. [Ni III]’s rest wavelength as given by
NIST is 7.349 µm but D. Van De Putte et al. (2024) correct it by ∼0.003 µm (the revised value, 7.352 µm, is used in
our work). The measured [Fe III] discrepancy of 0.005 µm is therefore not unprecedented, however, further spectrally
resolved observations would be helpful to clarify the origins of this apparent discrepancy.
B.3. [Ne II] Line Saturation
The bright [Ne II] 12.81 µm line requires a customized model fit. [Ne II] is bright enough to saturate the detector
in certain portions of the FOV. Regions 1 and 2 are positioned to avoid saturated pixels, but Region 3 has several
saturated pixels arising from the blue-shifted Northern Arm. These saturated pixels are masked out in the cube.
To account for the saturated wavelengths, we impose stricter fit priors for the Region 3 spectrum. First, we fit the
[Ne III] 15.56 µm and [S III] 18.71 µm lines; [Ne III] because it is the same element as [Ne II], and [S III] because
its IP is similar to [Ne II]’s (Table 1). [Ne II]’s µ and σ bounds are chosen to encompass the corresponding µ and σ
obtained from [Ne III] and [S III]. The saturated Northern Arm’s [Ne II] amplitude is constrained to be greater than
the unsaturated Bar component. We fit [Ne II] excluding the saturated wavelengths, and we report the flux of the
Northern Arm component as a lower bound.

D. ABUNDANCE VARIATIONS
D.1. Iron and Nickel Corrections for Unseen Ionization States
When calculating XFe, one must account for unseen Fe3+. Iron’s ICF has substantial scatter across models, influenced
by the atomic data properties and the assumed gas physical conditions (M. Rodr´ıguez & R. H. Rubin 2005; J. E.
M´endez-Delgado et al. 2021, 2024). We estimate lower and upper limits on the iron ICF range. For the lower limit, J. P.
Simpson et al. (2007) calculate the ICF as a function of the neon ionic abundances output from a CLOUDY simulation
grid. We use the models with assumed source distances r ∼0.01 −1pc, and select an ICF ∼2 that corresponds
to our inferred neon abundances for the Bar and Northern Arm. Meanwhile, the upper limits are calculated from
a recent study by J. E. M´endez-Delgado et al. (2024): they compare different ICF schemes tested against the true
measured Fe3+ abundance measurements for a sample of H II regions. For the galactic H II regions in their sample
with physical conditions comparable to our varied ne,Te prescription, they find ICF ∼2.4 −3.7. We choose an iron
ICF range = 2−3.7 that combines these two studies, with the corrected XFe,ICF given in Tables 2 and 5. This range is
validated by D. Lutz et al. (1993)’s study of the Minispiral mini-cavity. They employ ionization balance equations to
infer a mini-cavity ICF = 4 (their XFe,noICF and XFe,ICF are marked by black filled and empty squares, respectively,
in Figure 6). These ICFs imply that the majority of the gas phase iron is in the Fe3+ state.
For nickel, G. Delgado-Inglada et al. (2016) derive an ICF based on the measured O+ abundance. Though we
cannot measure O+ with our data, we reference their ICF as a benchmark. For H II regions with comparable ne,Te to
our varied prescription, their ICF ∼1.5 −2.
D.2. Density and Temperature Dependence
We recalculate Xi to test how it depends on the ne,Te prescription. In Tables 2 and 5, we find that increasing ne
beyond ncrit increases Xi, while increasing Te decreases Xi. Low ncrit ions are more sensitive to ne (Table 1); for this
reason, sulfur and iron both increase dramatically in the varied ne,Te prescription because the assumed ne is larger
than in the fixed prescription (Table 2). High ncrit elements are more impacted by changes in Te. This is most clear
for α elements in the Bar, where the varied prescription’s Te is almost double that of the fixed prescription’s (Table 2).
To further investigate Xi’s dependence on the assumed ne,Te, we test a series of ne,Te combinations that fall within
reasonable limits for the GC ionized gas (Section 3.3). For one set of calculations, we fix Te = 7, 500 K with varied
ne = [103, 106] cm−3(left panel of Figure 10). For another, we hold ne = 1 × 103 cm−3 with varied Te = [6000, 15000]
K (right panel of Figure 10). For the varied ne case, iron and sulfur experience the most pronounced change. Argon,
neon, and nickel have much higher ncrit and are minimally impacted, except at the highest ne ≳106 cm−3. Since the
identified gas structures all have an assumed ne ≲105 cm−3, those elements should be largely unaffected by ne. For
the varied Te case, Xi decreases with increasing Te. At the highest temperature (15,000 K), all abundances consistently
decrease by a factor of ∼1.5 −2. Altogether, ne has a bigger impact on the resulting abundances than Te once ne
surpasses ncrit.
