NDF+: JOINT NEURAL DIRECTIONAL FILTERING AND DIFFUSE SOUND EXTRACTION
Weilong Huang, Le Nhat Tam Huynh, Oliver Thiergart, Emanu¨el A. P. Habets
International Audio Laboratories Erlangen∗, Am Wolfsmantel 33, 91058 Erlangen, Germany
ABSTRACT
Neural directional filtering (NDF) has been introduced as a flexible
approach for reconstructing a virtual directional microphone (VDM)
with a desired directivity pattern for spatial sound capture. Building
on this idea, we propose NDF+, which enables joint neural direc-
tional filtering and diffuse sound extraction.
NDF+ reformulates
VDM estimation into two coupled subtasks: dereverberated VDM
reconstruction and diffuse sound extraction. This reformulation en-
ables NDF+ to manipulate diffuse components in the final recon-
structed VDM output. We evaluated NDF+ under reverberant con-
ditions and compared it with representative conventional baselines.
Results show that NDF+ consistently outperforms the baselines on
both subtasks while maintaining VDM reconstruction quality com-
parable to that of the original single-task NDF model. These findings
indicate that NDF+ introduces an additional degree of freedom for
diffuse sound control in the VDM reconstruction. In a stereo record-
ing application, NDF+ provides controllable inter-channel level dif-
ferences between left and right channels by adjusting the estimated
diffuse component.
Index Terms— Directional filtering, Microphone array, Diffuse
sound extraction
1. INTRODUCTION
Fixed beamformer (FBF) with an appropriate directivity pattern en-
ables precise spatial rendering of sound sources and preserves key
spatial cues, even in multi-source scenarios. However, conventional
FBFs, such as differential microphone array (DMA) [1, 2] and su-
perdirective beamforming [3], are fundamentally limited by a com-
pact array with a small size and a limited number of microphones [4].
Recently, neural directional filtering (NDF) has been proposed
as a data-driven alternative for reconstructing a virtual directional
microphone (VDM) with a desired directivity pattern [5–7]. By us-
ing a deep neural network (DNN) to learn the input-output behav-
ior of an ideal directional microphone, NDF achieves a frequency-
invariant target directivity pattern on compact arrays [7]. NDF fur-
ther allows flexible configuration of directivity patterns at inference
[8], making it attractive for spatial sound capture.
Existing NDF formulations mainly focus on VDM reconstruc-
tion accuracy and provide limited control over diffuse components.
In spatial recording scenarios, excessive diffuse sound can weaken
the listener’s perception of spatial cues and impair immersive audio
experiences [9, 10], underscoring the need for effective control of
diffuse components. To control the diffuseness, we propose NDF+,
a joint framework for neural directional filtering and diffuse sound
extraction. NDF+ reformulates VDM estimation as two coupled sub-
tasks: (i) dereverberated VDM reconstruction and (ii) diffuse sound

3. PROPOSED METHOD
3.1. DNN Architecture and Training Loss
The FT-JNF framework [14] used for the NDF task [5, 7] employs
two distinct long short-term memory (LSTM) networks to esti-
mate a single complex-valued mask and applies it to a reference
channel to estimate a wanted signal.
To accommodate estimates
for two distinct targets (Zcoh(f, t) and Zdiff(f, t)), the FT-JNF is
extended to a dual-mask architecture, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The
network input remains consistent with the original FT-JNF, repre-
sented by concatenated real and imaginary components with a shape
of [B, T, F, 2Q], where B is the batch size, T is the number of
time frames, and F is the number of frequency bins. This input is
firstly processed by a bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) network along
the frequency dimension. In contrast to the single unidirectional
LSTM (UniLSTM) used in the original FT-JNF, the proposed ap-
proach introduces two parallel UniLSTM branches to process the
BiLSTM output along the temporal dimension.
Each UniLSTM
output is then passed through a linear layer with a tanh activation to
produce a complex mask. These two estimated masks (Mcoh(f, t)
and Mdiff(f, t)) are subsequently applied to the same reference
signal, yielding two distinct estimated signals, i.e., coherent compo-
nent estimate bZcoh(f, t) = Mcoh(f, t)Y1(f, t) and diffuse sound

3.2. Training Strategy
A Jth-order Cardioid directivity pattern [7] is adopted as
Λ(θ, ϕ) = (0.5+0.5(sin ϕ sin ϕs cos(θ−θs)+cos ϕ cos ϕs))J, (8)
where θs and ϕs specify the target direction of the directivity pattern.
In this study, both a 1st-order Cardioid with J = 1 and a 6th-order
Cardioid with J = 6 are selected as target directivity patterns. The
maximum attenuation at the null position of the directivity patterns
is set to −30 dB to ensure robust training.
For simplicity, we assume that all microphones and sound
sources lie in the x-y plane. To learn the target directivity pattern
in a reverberant room, we simulate a random source-array setup
with up to three concurrent sources. The azimuth angle θn for the
n-th speech source with respect to the array is randomly selected.
Each speech source is assigned a random source-array distance. A
room with random dimensions and reverberation time is defined,
and the source-array setup is randomly positioned within the room.
Based on the positions of the microphones and sources, the corre-
sponding room impulse responses (RIRs) [15] are generated, and
the microphone signals are computed using (2).
The RIR for Hcoh,n(f; Λ) is approximated by windowing the
corresponding RIR of Hvdm,n(f; Λ), as illustrated in Fig. 2(a). The

4. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Configurations: A four-microphone array (Q = 4, diameter 3 cm)
was used, consisting of three microphones arranged in a uniform
circular array (UCA) and one positioned at the center as the refer-
ence microphone. The reference microphone signal served as the
first input channel for the NDF+ model. The target direction of the
directivity pattern (θs = 0 and ϕs = π
2 ) was assigned to a selected
element of the UCA, which was designated as the second channel
of input signals for the NDF+ model. The array position within
the room was determined using the Monte Carlo Room Impulse
Response simulation [16], ensuring a minimum distance of 1.2 m
from all walls. The source-array distance, room size (length, width,
and height), and RT60 were randomly sampled from the ranges
specified in Table 1.
Speech signals for the training and valida-
tion sets were sourced from the ‘train-clean-360’ and ‘dev-clean’
subsets of the LibriSpeech database [17], respectively.
For the
test sets, speech samples were drawn from the EARS dataset [18],
applying a minimum loudness threshold of −42dBFS [19].
All
signals were sampled at 16kHz, and L = 960 corresponded to
a 60 ms duration.
The candidate incident angles for the train-
ing and validation sets were set to θn ∈0◦, 5◦, . . . , 355◦and
θn ∈2.5◦, 7.5◦, . . . , 357.5◦.
For the test set, the candidate in-
cident angles were θn ∈1.25◦, 3.75◦, . . . , 358.75◦. To compute
bZvdm(f, t), we set β = 0.577 corresponding to a DI = 4.77 dB
for a 1st-order Cardioid target and β = 0.277 corresponding to a
DI = 11.14 dB for a 6th-order Cardioid target. All training stages
ran for up to 150 epochs. The training set comprised 50000 samples,
while the validation set included 6000 reverberant samples. Each
test set contained 3240 samples with two concurrent sources. Each
sample in all datasets lasted 4 s. Microphone sensor noise was added
at a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 30, dB. The batch size was set to
10. The STFT used a 512-point window and a 256-point hop size.
Performance measures:
We used the signal-to-distortion ratio
(SDR) [20] and perceptual evaluation of speech quality (PESQ)
[21, 22] to measure distance between estimated signals and target
signals. The obtained directivity patterns were estimated using the
method described in [7].

5.2. Stereo Recording Application
We simulated a reverberant room (RT60 = 0.5 s, 6 m × 4 m ×
3.5 m). A speech source moved clockwise from 180◦to 0◦over
12 s at a fixed 1.5 m distance. Two microphones of UCA faced 30◦
and 150◦(Fig. 5). The scene, generated by the Dynamic Acoustic
Scene Generator [26], evaluates NDF+ stereo recording.
Stereo recording can be made with the X-Y technique using
two coincident 1st-order Cardioids angled 90◦to 135◦[27]. In this
work, we realized the Cardiods using the NDF+ model, which was
trained with a fixed target direction toward a single UCA element
and λvdm = 0. By swapping the second and third input channels,
the same model was steered toward either 30◦or 150◦(see Fig. 5),
resulting in a 120◦angle. The corresponding NDF+ outputs, com-
puted via (7) under varying β, were assigned as the left and right
channels of the stereo recording. For comparison, we simulated the
X-Y technique with two ideal 1st-order Cardioid VDMs at the ar-
ray center, matching the NDF+ look directions. We compared the
segmental energy difference between the left and right channels for
NDF+ and VDM in Fig. 6. Results show that β = 0.577 gives NDF+
performance nearly identical to X-Y recording with ideal VDMs.
Adjusting β also allows direct control over diffuse sound energy for
NDF+, thereby tuning inter-channel differences in reverberant stereo
capture.
6. CONCLUSIONS
We introduce NDF+, a joint framework for neural directional filter-
ing and diffuse sound extraction. NDF+ splits the VDM estimation
into dereverberated VDM reconstruction and diffuse sound extrac-
tion. It consistently outperforms baselines on both tasks and matches
the single-task NDF model for VDM reconstruction. Joint optimiza-
tion maintains VDM reconstruction while enabling control over dif-
fuse components. In a stereo recording in a reverberant room, this
controllability allows adjustment of diffuse components and inter-
channel level differences.
