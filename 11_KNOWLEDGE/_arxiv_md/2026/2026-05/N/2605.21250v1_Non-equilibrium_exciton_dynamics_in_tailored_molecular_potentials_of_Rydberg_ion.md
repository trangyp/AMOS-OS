Non-equilibrium exciton dynamics in tailored molecular potentials of Rydberg ion
crystals
Simon Euchner,1 Mathias B. M. Svendsen,1 and Igor Lesanovsky1, 2

Trapped ions excited to high-lying electronic states combine strongly coupled collective vibrational
and electronic degrees of freedom with long-ranged interparticle interactions.
These ingredients
enable the quantum simulation of biochemical processes, associated with the dynamics of excitons
in non-perturbative parameter regimes. The key feature of such a quantum simulator are electronic-
state-dependent molecular potential surfaces which can be strongly coupled. This allows to shed
light on a variety of mechanisms underlying exciton transport. We illustrate this in a system of three
trapped ions, which is amenable to an ab initio treatment. Given that ion traps can be routinely
prepared with hundreds of ions, these quantum simulators can immediately realise scenarios which
are inaccessible by current numerical methods.
Introduction. Understanding and exploiting the trans-
port of excitation energy across molecules [1] is of sig-
nificant scientific and technological relevance [2–5]. Con-
crete examples are organic photovoltaics [6] and excitonic
wires [7], a tool to guide excitation energy, so-called ex-
citons, over long distances. Shedding light on their dy-
namics poses challenges due to the complex interplay of
molecular vibrations and electronic degrees of freedom.
Generally, a full analysis of this vibronic coupling ne-
cessitates the consideration of exponentially large state
spaces, making ab initio calculations intractable [8]. This
is especially the case in regimes where perturbative treat-
ments, such as adiabatic approximations [9, 10] or an
effective description via master equations [11], fail.
Analogue quantum simulation [12, 13] provides access
to these regimes.
Initial efforts explore highly excited
atoms, so-called Rydberg atoms, to study micrometer-
sized diatomic molecules [14–20], molecular instabilities
[21], and the Jahn-Teller effect [22–24].
Further, they
have been used to study excitation energy transport [25–
30], the formation of Rydberg aggregates and clusters
[27, 31], probed with the so-called van der Waals ex-
plosion [32], as well as the Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model
[33]. On the other hand, collective vibrations, native to
trapped ion setups, have been employed to study electron
transport dynamics [34] and vibrationally assisted exci-
ton transfer [35, 36], using phonon-mediated electronic
interactions [37, 38]. Trapped Rydberg ions [39, 40] com-
bine collective vibrational degrees of freedom with the
exaggerated properties of Rydberg states [41, 42]. Hith-
erto, Rydberg ions have been used to study electronic-
state-dependent conformational changes [43, 44], coni-
cal intersections [45–48], and spectral signatures of vi-
bronic coupling [49].
Importantly, all these phenom-
ena have in common that they are linked to physical
processes that take place in strongly-coupled electronic
state-dependent molecular potential surfaces — the elec-
tronic state-dependent potentials experienced by the nu-
clei of a molecule [8].
In this work, we illustrate how trapped Rydberg ions
allow to shed light on the physics of exciton transport
processes that rely on coupled molecular potential sur-
faces.
We first present the class of model Hamilto-
nians that are implementable on this quantum simu-
lation platform.
Subsequently we focus on a system
consisting of three ions.
Here, we showcase several
non-equilibrium processes, among them motional-state-
dependent excitonic transport, vibrationally assisted ex-
citon transfer [36], and excitonic relaxation induced by
a molecular conformational change.
These examples
highlight that quantum simulation with Rydberg ion
crystals may indeed serve to gather important insights
into fundamental physical mechanisms, which are of
cross-disciplinary relevance, for instance energy trans-
port across the Fenna–Matthews–Olson (FMO) protein
[50–52].
Quantum simulator architecture. We consider a chain
of N alkaline-earth metal ions of mass M confined in
the harmonic potential of a linear Paul trap with trap
frequencies ωx, ωy, and ωz in the respective spatial di-
rections; see Refs. [40, 53, 55] and Fig. 1(a). Each ion
is described by a two-level system with states |↑⟩and
|↓⟩encoded in a Rydberg S and P state, respectively, as
shown in Fig. 1(b).
Due to the interplay between the
trap confinement and the Coulomb repulsion, so-called
Coulomb crystals form at sufficiently low temperature
[56]. Here, the motion of the ions is described by small
displacements of the collective modes of the crystal. To
keep the presentation concise, we start by presenting the

Summary and outlook. Trapped Rydberg ions allow to
shed light on the physics of exciton transport processes
that rely on coupled molecular potential surfaces. Ulti-
mately, such a quantum simulator may be used to treat
technologically important systems of cross-disciplinary
relevance, such as organic photovoltaics [6], excitonic
wires [7], and light-harvesting complexes such as the
FMO protein [50, 51]. This is due to the variety of molec-
ular shapes that can be realised by exciting selected ions
from a larger two-dimensional ion crystal, which can be
confined in a Penning trap [61, 62]. Finite-temperature
effects in experiments [63, 64] further allow to study
temperature-dependent transport rates [65], as well as
controlled molecular dynamics out of thermal equilib-
rium.
Additionally, the modifications to the molecu-
lar potential caused by Rydberg excitation give rise to
geometrical distortions of the ion crystal. Such distor-
tions can support the formation of excitonic polarons —
a bound state between an exciton and the crystal dis-
tortions [66–68]. This shows that Rydberg ions offer a
promising route to probe their fundamental properties in
large molecular complexes.

Interpretation of state-dependent potentials
In this final Subsection we remark on an important
aspect concerning the Hamiltonian in Eq. (S72): gen-
