ABSTRACT
The degree of black-hole spin-orbit misalignment (“tilts”) in the astrophysical population could be a
powerful diagnostic to distinguish between binary formation in isolation, in dynamical environments,
or in hierarchical triples. However, robust population-level spin tilt measurements are complicated by
model misspecification as well as numerical and Poisson variance, ultimately owing to poor single-event
constraints on tilts. Motivated by reports of a possible peak in the spin tilt distribution, we analyze the
fourth LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA gravitational-wave transient catalog to test for preferred spin orientations
at different black hole masses. We find that a peak in spin tilts is not statistically significant nor model
independent. Since the data cannot be used to reliably identify subpopulations based on their spin
tilt properties, we also consider a complementary approach: measuring the spin magnitude and tilt
distributions at fixed mass scales. We find no confident correlation between mass and spin tilt, but we
do confirm a confident correlation between spin magnitude and mass, corroborating recent analyses.
1. INTRODUCTION
Gravitational-wave (GW) observations of merging
binary black holes (BBHs) have quickly become routine
since their discovery in 2015; the latest public LIGO–
Virgo–KAGRA (LVK) (J. Aasi et al. 2015; F. Acernese
et al. 2015; T. Akutsu et al. 2021) catalog now stands
at 204 candidates (A. G. Abac et al. 2025a). Together,
these data can be used to infer the population distribu-
tion of black holes in merging binaries (A. G. Abac et al.
2025b,c,d) which may belie their formation channels.
Black-hole binaries are characterized, in part, by their
masses and spin vectors. In particular, the orientation—
the tilt—of their spins relative to the binary orbit may
be a clear tracer of BBH formation histories. For exam-
ple, simulations of binary evolution in the field typically
predict BBHs with orbit-aligned spin tilts (V. Kalogera
2000; W. M. Farr et al. 2017; D. Gerosa et al. 2018; K.
Belczynski et al. 2020; V. Baibhav & V. Kalogera 2024).
Meanwhile, models of binary formation in stellar clus-

tilts (C. L. Rodriguez et al. 2016, 2022).
Black hole (BH) spin orientations may be even more
astrophysically interpretable if we can associate differ-
ent tilt distributions with different mass scales, e.g., field
formation may tend to produce BHs below the onset of
pulsational pair-instability at ∼45 M⊙(R. Farmer et al.
2019) while formation in clusters may yield heavier BHs
(D. Gerosa & M. Fishbach 2021; C. L. Rodriguez et al.
2022).
Merging BBHs may also be formed in hierar-
chical triples, where the inner BH binary is driven to
merger via dynamical interactions with an outer object.
The coupled dynamics of two BH orbits and spins along-
side a third body, their gravitational-wave emission, and
Kozai-Lidov oscillations leave a unique observational im-
print: the inner binary components tend to spin in the
orbital plane (F. Antonini et al. 2018; C. L. Rodriguez
& F. Antonini 2018; B. Liu & D. Lai 2018; H. Yu et al.
2020; Y. Su et al. 2021).

joint space of BH masses and spins (A. G. Abac et al.

BBH spin tilts via GWs; instead, we typically measure

population inference should be able to make a meaning-
ful measurement of spin tilts from the combination of
many poorly measured examples (S. Vitale et al. 2017b;
C. Talbot & E. Thrane 2017; S. J. Miller et al. 2024).
In practice and given current GW datasets, population
constraints on spin tilts are: sensitive to modeling as-
sumptions (S. Vitale et al. 2022; J. Golomb & C. Talbot
2023); sensitive to Poisson fluctuations in catalog mem-
bership (S. Vitale & M. Mould 2025; A. Corelli et al.
2026); and limited by systematic uncertainties in the
population likelihood (C. Talbot & J. Golomb 2023).
While some population analyses allow (but do not re-
quire) a preference for BHs with in-plane tilts, these
inferences may be spurious.

2. METHODS
2.1. Hierarchical inference
We infer the population-level properties of merging
BBHs via hierarchical Bayesian inference (C. Messen-
ger & J. Veitch 2013; I. Mandel et al. 2019; S. Vitale
et al. 2020). We take the confident (signal-to-noise ra-
tio > 10 through the LVK’s second observing run and
false-alarm rate < 1 yr−1 thereafter) BBHs in GWTC-
4 (R. Abbott et al. 2025a). For each event in O3 and
O4a, we use source parameter inference assuming the
NRSur7dq4 (V. Varma et al. 2019) waveform model
when available and the Mixed samples otherwise;7 in
O1 and O2 we use IMRPhenomXPHM (G. Pratten
et al. 2021; M. Colleoni et al. 2025). Selection effects
are estimated with publicly-available LVK data prod-
ucts (R. Essick et al. 2025; R. Abbott et al. 2025b,c).
Event selection, waveform choices, and sensitivity esti-
mation follow A. G. Abac et al. 2025d.
We estimate
the population likelihood via Monte Carlo integration
and limit the variance of this estimator to be less than
one during inference (V. Tiwari 2018; R. Essick & W.
Farr 2022; C. Talbot & J. Golomb 2023; J. Heinzel &
S. Vitale 2025). Inference is performed using dynesty
(J. S. Speagle 2020; S. Koposov et al. 2024) wrapped
by gwpopulation (C. Talbot et al. 2025); settings are
detailed in App. B.
2.2. Population models
We construct two population models designed to test
for the existence of subpopulations or correlations in the
joint space of black hole masses and spins, which we
call the Subpopulations and Correlation models,
respectively. Detailed priors for each model are listed in
App. A. We also designed more elastic models, although
their posterior geometeries were non-trivial to sample
so we reserve description of these model variations and
sampling challenges for App. B.
Subpopulations: For mass and redshift we adopt
the preferred parametric distribution from A. G. Abac
et al. 2025d: we model the primary masses as a mixture
between a broken power law tapered at low masses and
two Gaussian peaks (Broken Power law plus Two
Peaks), we model mass ratios as a power law, and we
model the differential merger rate density R as a power
law in redshift. For simplicity, we use the same mini-
mum BH mass for primary and secondary components.
The spin tilts follow J. Stegmann et al. 2026: above
a transition mass ˜m (“high-mass”) measured from the

4. DISCUSSION
The masses and spins of merging binary black holes
may hint at their formation history, and we may be
able to identify or falsify astrophysical formation chan-
nels by combining catalogs of BBH mergers observed via
gravitational waves. Analyses of GWTC-4 have identi-
fied potential signatures of a subpopulation of relatively
heavy (≳45 M⊙) BHs which prefer large, and possibly
isotropically-oriented spins, possibly indicating forma-
tion in a dynamical environment (C. L. Rodriguez et al.
2016; D. Gerosa & M. Fishbach 2021; C. L. Rodriguez
et al. 2022)—although note that some analyses only
model the effective binary spin (F. Antonini et al. 2025;
N. Guttman et al. 2026; H. Tong et al. 2025; C. Plunkett
et al. 2026; A. Ray et al. 2026), while others explicitly
model the component spins (S. Banagiri et al. 2025; E.
Berti et al. 2026; A. M. Farah et al. 2026). Simultane-
ously, the LVK’s analysis of GWTC-4 allows for BHs to
prefer to be oriented in-plane relative to their orbit as
opposed to isotropically distributed (A. G. Abac et al.
2025d), which could be indicative of an origin in hierar-
chical triples (e.g., F. Antonini et al. 2018). Recent work
by J. Stegmann et al. 2026 suggests that this possible
peak in the tilt distribution is associated with relatively
light (≲45 M⊙) BHs. However, as noted by S. Vitale
et al. 2022, measurements of the population distribution
of BH spin-orbit (mis)alignments are sensitive to the as-
sumed form of the population model (alongside other
concerns including numerical and Poisson variance).
In light of these challenges, we further investigated
population-level constraints on structure in the joint dis-
tribution of BH masses and spin tilts with two targeted
population models. In the first model, we augmented
the model from J. Stegmann et al. 2026—which models
three distinct subpopulations identified by their mass
and spin tilt distributions—allowing for additional flex-
ibility in the marginal primary mass distribution. We
used the same parametric model for primary mass as
the LVK’s default analysis of GWTC-4. In the second

aligned spin tilts between 0.9 and 3.6 with 90% credi-
bility. When we instead allow for a correlation between
mass and spin, the data limit the relative fraction of
in-plane spins to be at most a factor of 1.5 greater than
that of orbit-aligned spins, and the relative in-plane frac-
tion may be as low as 0.7 at 90% credibility. We found
the strongest—but still inconclusive—support for pref-
erentially in-plane spins for BH masses (primary or sec-
ondary) between 20 and 40 M⊙(cf. Fig. 3).
Is there a statistically-significant correlation between
BH masses and spin orientation? No. When modeling
subpopulations in mass and spin, we inferred a transi-
tion in the spin distribution at ∼45 M⊙; we enforced
an isotropic distribution of tilts above this threshold,
and below we inferred a spin tilt distribution consistent
with isotropy (cf.
Fig. 2).
When modeling a corre-
lation between mass and spin, we found that the spin
tilt distributions at all mass scales were consistent with
isotropy at 90% credibility (cf. Fig. 3). While there may
be hints that black holes between 7 and 20 M⊙prefer
orbit-aligned spin-tilts—consistent with typical models
of field formation—we cannot rule out that spins tend to
be randomly oriented at all masses, corroborating sim-
ilar findings by S. Banagiri et al. 2025, E. Berti et al.
2026 and A. M. Farah et al. 2026. Given that our results
are consistent with both isotropy and peaks in the spin
tilt distribution, in App. D we show that finite catalogs
drawn from isotropic spin populations can spuriously
yield a preference for in-plane spin tilts, complementing
S. Vitale & M. Mould 2025 which demonstrated that
spurious features in the tilt distribution can also arise
from preferentially orbit-aligned populations.
Is there a statistically-significant correlation between
BH masses and spin magnitudes?
Yes. When we al-
lowed a correlation between BH mass and spin, we found
that BHs above 40 M⊙may tend to have larger (≳0.6)
spin magnitudes and BHs below 40 M⊙confidently tend
to have smaller (≲0.2) spin magnitudes (bottom panel
of Fig. 4).
This corroborates findings by S. Banagiri
et al. 2025, E. Berti et al. 2026, and A. M. Farah et al.
2026. Notably, however, we did not find significant ev-
idence for distinct spin magnitude distributions at dif-
ferent masses when modeling subpopulations in primary
mass–spin tilt (top panel of Fig. 4).
Our work adds a cautionary tale to a growing body
of literature regarding population-level constraints on
BH spin orientations. Despite a more-than-doubling of
the catalog of BBHs since the end of the previous LVK
observing run, the observed data are still insufficient
to draw (or enforce) astrophysical conclusions based on
spin tilts alone. As demonstrated here, spin tilt popu-
lation inference is intimately related to population-level
assumptions in other sectors of the source parameter
space.
Given poor single-event measurements on the
spin tilts, it may be that population-level measurements
are ultimately driven by constraints on the spin tilts pro-
vided by some combination of the effective spin, compo-
nent spin magnitudes, and masses. To complicate mat-
ters, we also found that models that attempt to distin-
guish populations primarily from their spin properties
can present significant challenges during sampling (cf.
App. B).
While we focused on only two model variations, one of
our key conclusions—that spin tilts are essentially un-
constrained at all masses—is relevant when comparing
population models. Population models are often com-
pared by their Bayes’ factors, which include the prior
volumes associated with each model. That prior volume
is reduced if we fix the spin tilt distribution for all BHs or
a particular subpopulation, increasing the Bayes’ factor
relative to an analysis which makes no such assumptions
about tilts.
The lack of confident mass–tilt subpopulations or cor-
relations is also relevant when astrophysically interpret-
ing analyses that model the effective spins of binaries.
While multiple works have identified transitions in the
effective spin distribution(s) as a function of primary
mass (F. Antonini et al. 2025; N. Guttman et al. 2026;
H. Tong et al. 2025; C. Plunkett et al. 2026; A. Ray et al.
2026), different mass and component spin distributions
can yield the same effective spin distribution (S. J. Miller
et al. 2024). Instead, the component masses and spins
are easier to interpret in the context of the formation
history of each BH in a binary.
Our results corrobo-
rate a preference for lighter BHs to have smaller spins
which could be indicative of field formation, or alter-
natively first-generation mergers in dynamical environ-
ments (C. L. Rodriguez et al. 2019), although these in-
terpretations rely on other uncertain astrophysics like
angular momentum transport in massive stars (J. Fuller
& L. Ma 2019). Identifying those same BHs as nearly
always having at most a few degrees of spin-orbit mis-
alignment would lend additional credence to the identifi-

Here we describe the full functional form of the Correlation population model. The spin magnitudes and tilts
are written
p(χ1, χ2|m1, q) = p(χ1|m1)p(χ2|m2) ,
(A1)
p(cos τ1, cos τ2|m1, q) = p(cos τ1|m1)p(cos τ2|m2) ,
(A2)

model can be written
p(z)p(m1, q)p(χ1, χ2|m1, q)p(cos τ1, cos τ2|m1, q) ,
(A3)
where p(z) is derived from a power law in the differential merger rate density (M. Fishbach et al. 2018) and p(m1, q)
is the Broken Power Law Plus Two Peaks from A. G. Abac et al. (2025d).

B.2. Degenerate subpopulations
While testing the Subpopulations model, we noticed that changing the random seed provided to dynesty yielded
different marginal posteriors on the branching ratio ξ between the low-mass isotropic and Gaussian spin tilt distribu-
tions. In roughly half of analyses with different seeds, we recovered a single mode at ξ ∼1, and in the other half, at

similar likelihoods. We verified this result with an additional run with 104 live points; that same analysis is presented
in the main body for results under the Subpopulations model.
