Abstract: Empirical Bayes analyses routinely model noisy measurements of latent pa-
rameters as normal, justifying this by an informal appeal to the central limit theorem
(CLT). This paper puts this heuristic appeal on firmer analytical grounds. We show that
the denoising regret of the nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator (NPMLE)
and related sieve methods is controlled by the rate attained under exact normality,
plus a term reflecting the quality of the CLT approximation. The CLT need only hold
marginally for each coordinate, and moreover only on average, without needing high-
dimensional normal approximations. We identify two asymptotic regimes in which the
normal approximation is adequate and the empirical Bayesian prior remains informative,
and we show that our guarantees are robust to dependence and to variance estimation.
1. Introduction
Empirical Bayes (EB) methods (Robbins, 1956; Efron, 2010) are increasingly popular in
applied work in economics, statistics, and adjacent fields; see Walters (2024) for a survey in
labor economics and Koenker and Gu (2026a) for a recent monograph. A typical exercise has
the analyst observe noisy summaries X1, . . . , Xn for underlying parameters θ1, . . . , θn, e.g.,
teacher quality in the value-added literature (Gilraine et al., 2020), place effects (Bergman
et al., 2024), gene expression differences (Stephens, 2017), treatment effects from A/B tests
(Azevedo et al., 2019; Abadie et al., 2023), and seek to make decisions that involve θi.
A nearly universal modeling convention, at least in econometrics and economics, treats
Xi | θi, σi ∼N(θi, σ2
i ), with the standard deviation σi treated as known and equal to the
observed and estimated standard errors of Xi. This convention is often justified by a heuristic
appeal to the central limit theorem (CLT) applied to whatever micro-data that produced Xi.
Typically, Xi are estimates from certain well-behaved statistical procedures (e.g., regression
coefficients) estimated off the underlying micro-data and are asymptotically normal in the
size of the micro-data. Thus the analyst proceeds as if Xi were exactly normal.
This appeal to the CLT is pervasive enough to merit a catalog. In labor economics, this
normality assumption is equation (1) in the review by Walters (2024), treated as a starting
point of EB analyses. Kline et al. (2022), who study discriminatory hiring practices, invoke
an asymptotic approximation for estimates at the firm level “with a growing number of
jobs sampled for each firm.” Angrist et al. (2017), who study estimates of school quality
modeled as normal estimates, write “ subject to the usual asymptotic approximations, these
errors are normally distributed with a known covariance structure.” In e-commerce and A/B
testing, Abadie et al. (2023) model the treatment effect estimates as normal, “motivated by
approximate Gaussianity of the large sample distributions of many commonly used estimators

With rare exceptions, such statements are not rigorously justified.1 The natural question
is then—as put explicitly by Hirano (2023)—“In many cases, proceeding as if the data are
normally distributed may be quite reasonable, but can this appeal to approximate normality
be put on firmer analytical grounds?”
Resolving this question is important for the applicability of empirical Bayes, which makes
heavy use of the normality of the likelihood. If nonparametric EB methods turn out to be
sensitive to the normal approximation, that would severely limit their applicability. Answering
this question would also clarify the extent to which imposing normality directly at the micro-
data level—an assumption made by, e.g., Gu and Koenker (2017); Banerjee et al. (2020);
Ignatiadis and Sen (2025); Ho (2025); Gaillac (2025); Lee and Deb (2026); Song et al. (2026)—
is more restrictive than the normality assumption on Xi. If approximate normality of Xi
does suffice for nonparametric EB, then assuming micro-data normality is in fact a much
more substantive restriction that could be potentially relaxed. This question also appears
technically non-obvious. Since the empirical Bayes literature often studies a large number of
approximately normal estimates, it is not clear whether that would correspondingly require
tools from high-dimensional central limit theory (Chernozhukov et al., 2017).
This paper presents such a theory for nonparametric empirical Bayes methods, in particular
the workhorse nonparametric maximum likelihood estimator (NPMLE) (Jiang and Zhang,
2009; Kiefer and Wolfowitz, 1956) and related sieve maximum likelihood methods (Efron,
2016).

1.2. Further related work
Some authors have considered applying empirical Bayes to local parameters as in Theo-
rem 1.2 (Sen, 2000; Hansen, 2016); however, they consider asymptotics with fixed n and
apply James-Stein type shrinkage, rather than the more flexible rules afforded by nonpara-
metric priors. Zhong et al. (2022) is closest to the results we seek, proving that the empirical
Bayes regret converges to zero when normality does not exactly hold (nor independence).
Their results, however, are stated in a specialized random matrix theory setup and without
rates of convergence. Ghosh et al. (2026) provide a qualitative stability result for the NPMLE
under certain stochastic perturbations of the samples.
Our work, like the above, presupposes that the analyst proceeds by positing normality of
the summary statistics Xi. If one is doubtful about this assumption, one could work with
an appropriate context-specific likelihood (Kline and Walters, 2021), robustify the NPMLE
procedure (Koenker and Gu, 2026b), or consider likelihood-free alternatives that exploit
access to the micro-data (Coey and Cunningham, 2019; Deng et al., 2021; Ignatiadis et al.,
2023; Kline, 2025).

3. Robustness to dependence
So far we have assumed that X1, . . . , Xn are independent across units. This is often violated
in practice. As one example, Gu and Koenker (2022) fit a Bradley–Terry model and apply the
NPMLE to the components of the maximum likelihood estimator (X1, . . . , Xn), which are
correlated. In this section, we show that the empirical Bayes procedure of Section 2 remains
consistent under dependence and approximate normality.
To ground the discussion, suppose momentarily that θi ∼π⋆and Xi | θi ∼N(θi, σ2
i ), so
that normality holds exactly, but that the θi or the Xi (or both) are dependent across i.
Two conceptual issues arise. First, the objective in (1.1) is no longer the joint log-likelihood.
Instead, it is a type of “independence likelihood” (Chandler and Bate, 2007), a special case of
composite likelihood (Varin et al., 2011). Second, the true Bayes-optimal denoiser is Eπ⋆[θi |
X1, . . . , Xn], since under dependence all observations carry information about θi, beyond
their role in estimating π⋆. If the dependence structure is not explicitly modeled, however,
it is natural to benchmark ˆθi against the marginal oracle Eπ⋆[θi | Xi] instead, and we do so
below.3
