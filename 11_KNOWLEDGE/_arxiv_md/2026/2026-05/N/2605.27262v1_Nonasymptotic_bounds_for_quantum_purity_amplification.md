Abstract

of noise in quantum systems. Many quantum algorithms and protocols rely on the creation of delicately
prepared pure quantum states, and imperfections in the quantum computer, as well as interactions with the
environment, can perturb or even destroy these states. Several approaches for managing this error have been

Since this work, it has been an open problem to devise the optimal quantum purity amplification
algorithm for qudit rather than qubit states. The chief difficulty is that the algorithm of [CEM99] is heavily
representation theoretic, and the representation theory of quantum states, already challenging in the qubit
setting, becomes far more difficult in the qudit setting. A first step in this direction was made by Fu in his
master’s thesis [Fu16], where he identified and analyzed the optimal algorithm for purifying qutrit states in
the special case of depolarizing noise. This is the physically-motivated case when p1 = · · · = pd−1 < pd, and
the goal is to output |vd⟩. In this case, we can view the state ρ as being the result of passing |vd⟩through the

Related work
Quantum purity amplification has been studied in a variety of settings, yielding a suite of algorithms with
different tradeoffs. The representation theoretic algorithms we have discussed so far, and which we study in
this work, are able to achieve the information-theoretic optimal sample complexities. However, they tend to
be computationally more challenging to implement, and they require having access to all n copies of ρ up
front. An elegant alternative approach based on a tree of SWAP tests was proposed by Childs, Fu, Leung, Li,
Ozols, and Vyas [CFL+25]. Their algorithm operates in the streaming model, in which the copies of ρ come
in one-at-a-time, and only requires O(log(n)) qudits of memory. They consider the case of depolarizing noise,
and show a nonasymptotic bound which achieves the optimal bound from (1) up to constant factors, at least
in the case when pd ≥2/3 + 1/(3d); when pd is smaller, they show a bound of n = Op(1/δ). Following their
work, Grier, Leung, Li, Pashayan, and Schaeffer [GLL+25] extended the n = Op(1/δ) sample bound to hold
against all states ρ, not just in the case of depolarizing noise.

2.1
Young diagrams and Young tableaux
In the next few sections, we will introduce the combinatorics of random words and its relationship to the
representation theory which arises when studying quantum states. For a more thorough introduction to these
topics, see [Wri16].
A partition of n, denoted λ ⊢n, is a tuple of integers λ = (λ1, . . . , λd) satisfying λ1 + · · · + λd = n and
λ1 ≥· · · ≥λd ≥0. We write ℓ(λ) for the length of λ, which is the number of its nonzero entries. Partitions λ
are often visually represented as Young diagrams, which consist of boxes arranged into rows of lengths λ1, λ2,
and so forth. A standard Young tableau (SYT) S is a Young diagram whose boxes have been filled with the
numbers 1, . . . , n, with the property that each row is strongly increasing when read from left-to-right and
each column is strongly increasing when read from top-to-bottom. A semistandard Young tableau (SSYT) T
with alphabet [d] is a Young diagram whose boxes have been filled with numbers in [d], with the property that
each row is weakly increasing when read from left-to-right and each column is strongly increasing when read
from top-to-bottom. We illustrate these in Figure 1.

A word is a tuple w = (w1, . . . , wn) ∈[d]n. The type of w is specified by a histogram h = (h1, . . . , hd), in
which hi equals the number of occurrences of the letter i in w. The well-known Robinson–Schensted–Knuth
(RSK) correspondence establishes a bijection between words w ∈[d]n and pairs (S, T) with the following
properties: (i) S is an SYT, (ii) T is an SSYT, (iii) S and T have the same shape λ, and (iv) T has the
same type as w. The algorithm which computes this bijection is known as the RSK algorithm, and we write
RSK(w) = (λ, S, T) for the output of this bijection; although λ is implicit in S and T, we include it for
convenience. An observation first made by Schensted [Sch61], and later generalized by Greene [Gre74], is that
λ1 is equal to the length of the longest weakly increasing subsequence in w. A consequence is that λ1 ≥hd,
as the all-d’s subsequence is a weakly increasing subsequence in w.
