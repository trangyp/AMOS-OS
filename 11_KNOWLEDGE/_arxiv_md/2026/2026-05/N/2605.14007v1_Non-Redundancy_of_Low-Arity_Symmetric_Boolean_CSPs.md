Non-Redundancy of Low-Arity Symmetric Boolean CSPs
Amatya Sharma*
Santhoshini Velusamy †
Abstract
Non-redundancy, introduced by Bessiere, Carbonnel, and Katsirelos (AAAI 2020), has
emerged as a central structural parameter for Constraint Satisfaction Problems (CSPs): it un-
derlies kernelization and exact sparsification, and recent work shows that it also governs
approximate sparsification and exact streaming complexity. The non-redundancy NRD mea-
sures the largest size of a CSP instance that admits no smaller subinstance with the same set of
satisfying assignments.
In this paper we study the non-redundancy (NRDn(R)) for Boolean symmetric CSPs specified
by a symmetric r-ary relation R, that is, a relation whose value depends only on Hamming
weight. An instance of CSP(R) consists of n variables and m constraints, each given by an r-tuple
of variables, and a constraint is satisfied if and only if the resulting tuple belongs to R. This is a
natural and broad class containing Max-Cut, Max-k-SAT, etc. Our main result is a near-complete
classification of the growth of NRDn(R) for symmetric Boolean predicates of arity at most 5.
Using computational experiments together with algebraic upper- and lower-bound criteria, we
determine the asymptotic growth of NRDn(R) for every symmetric Boolean predicate of arity at
most 4. At arity 5, the framework resolves all predicates except two.
On the upper-bound side, we introduce a lifted, higher-degree version of the balancedness
notion of Chen, Jansen, and Pieterse (Algorithmica 2020), and show that it yields general
upper bounds on non-redundancy. More precisely, we define t-balancedness and prove that
it is equivalent to the existence of degree at most t multilinear polynomials “capturing” R,
implying that NRDn(R) = O(nt) for every t-balanced relation R. On the lower-bound side, we
use Carbonnel’s (CP 2022) framework, which gives a criterion for when a predicate admits a
special reduction from the k-ary OR relation; since OR already has non-redundancy Ω(nk), this
yields corresponding lower bounds on NRDn(R). While these determine the non-redundancy
of symmetric predicates up to arity 4 and nearly all predicates of arity 5, we discover two
exceptional predicates of arity 5 that are not characterized by this framework—we obtain an
Ω(n2) lower bound and an O(n3) upper bound. We further show that these remaining cases
reduce to natural extremal set-system questions.
Independent Work.
Very recently, Brakensiek, Guruswami, and Putterman [BGP26] used a
similar experimental viewpoint to study arity-4 Boolean predicates, and one of their remaining
cases connects to the symmetric arity-5 predicate with W(R023) = {0, 2, 3} that also appears
here.

Introduction
Constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs) are a central framework in theoretical computer science.
Formally, given a finite domain D and a constraint language Γ consisting of r-ary relations over
D, an instance of CSP(Γ) consists of n variables V := {x1, . . . , xn} together with constraints of the
form {R, ( ¯x)}, where R ∈Γ and ¯x ∈Vr. 1 The goal is to decide whether there exists an assignment
σ : V →D such that (σ(x1), . . . , σ(xr)) ∈R for every constraint. This framework captures many
familiar problems, including SAT, graph coloring, and systems of local equations, and has been
studied from the viewpoints of complexity theory [Sch78; JCG97; FV98; H˚as01; Kho02; BJK05;
Che06; Zhu20], approximation [KSW97; KSTW01; LLZ02; BFK03; ACMM05; CCJK06; KKMO07;
Rag08; KS09; AM09; GS11; MM15; FLP16; KTW14; CLRS16; GT17; AJT19; JQST20; BBK+21; JST21;
ALS25; ALMS25], (approximate) sparsification [KK15; FK17; BZ20; KPS24; KPS25b; KPS25a],
kernelization and exact sparsification [DV14; JP19; CJP20; LW20; BCK20; Car22; BG25; BGJ+25;
BGP25], and, more recently, streaming and sketching [KKS15; GVV17; KK19; CGV20; CGS+22;
Sin23; KPSY23; Vu24; CGSV24; STV25; FMW26a; FMW26b; ABF26; STV26].
Symmetric Boolean CSPs.
A relation R ⊆{0, 1}r is symmetric if membership in R depends only
on the Hamming weight of the input. Equivalently, there is a set W(R) ⊆{0, 1, . . . , r} such that
R = {x ∈{0, 1}r : |x| ∈W(R)}. Thus a symmetric predicate can be specified simply by its set of

accepted weights, and throughout the paper we will freely identify a symmetric predicate with its
weight set W(R).
Symmetric Boolean predicates already contain many natural and canonical problems. For
example, the r-ary OR predicate corresponds to W(ORr) = {1, . . . , r} and gives the usual r-SAT
constraints; the r-ary Not-All-Equal predicate corresponds to W(NAEr) = {1, . . . , r −1} and is closely
related to hypergraph 2-coloring and NAE-SAT; threshold predicates correspond to intervals of the
form {t, t + 1, . . . , r} and capture “at least t” type constraints; and parity predicates correspond
to the even or odd weights and encode systems of linear equations over F2. For these reasons,
symmetric predicates form a broad yet structured class, and they have been studied explicitly in
work on approximation, sparsification, sketching, and non-redundancy [ABM12; GL17; BHP+22;
BG21; BGS23; KPS25b].
Thus, we restrict our study to symmetric Boolean CSPs in this paper. We ask how large can an
instance be if every one of its constraints is non-redundant, that is, if no constraint can be removed
without changing the satisfying set. This leads to the notion of non-redundancy.
Non-redundancy.
Given a CSP instance, a constraint is redundant if deleting it does not change
the set of satisfying assignments. An instance is non-redundant if every constraint is non-redundant in
this sense. Following Bessiere, Carbonnel, and Katsirelos [BCK20], who introduced this viewpoint
in the context of learning CSP instances with few membership queries, and Carbonnel’s [Car22]
subsequent structural study, we define the non-redundancy dimension of a constraint language Γ
by
NRDn(Γ) := max{|I| : I is a non-redundant CSP(Γ) instance on n variables}.
When Γ = {R} consists of a single relation, we also write NRDn(R). Thus NRDn(R) asks a basic
extremal question: how many R-constraints can be simultaneously non-redundant in an instance
on n variables? At first sight, this is purely a combinatorial quantity. However, it turns out to be
closely connected to several algorithmic and structural questions about CSPs.
Connections of NRD to other problems.
One such connection comes from exact sparsification. The
goal is to reduce any n-variable instance in polynomial time to an equivalent subinstance of size
g(n), where equivalence means having exactly the same satisfying assignments. Exact sparsification
is closely related to kernelization, where one preprocesses an instance to an equivalent instance
whose size is bounded in terms of a chosen parameter. In our setting the parameter is the number n
of variables, and exact sparsification asks for the stronger property that the output is an equivalent
subinstance obtained by deleting constraints. On the positive side, several structured Boolean
CSPs admit exact sparsification by retaining only a small subset of constraints [JP19; CJP20; LW20].
Non-redundant instances are the canonical obstruction to such subinstance sparsification: any
equivalent subinstance must retain every constraint of a non-redundant input.
Another, more approximation-theoretic notion is approximate sparsification, where one seeks a
weighted subinstance of small size, with weights on constraints, that approximately preserves the
value of every assignment. This line of work originates in graph and hypergraph cut sparsification
and has since developed into a broad theory for CSPs [KK15; FK17; BZ20; KPS25b; KPS25a]. Very
recently, Brakensiek and Guruswami [BG25] proved that, for every unweighted predicate R, the
worst-case size of approximate sparsifiers is governed, up to polylogarithmic factors, by NRDn(R);
see also the recent follow-up on random CSP sparsification [BGP25]. Thus the same combinatorial
parameter that obstructs exact sparsification also controls approximate sparsification.
Recently, there has been a similar connection in the streaming setting as well. In very recent
work by Sharma and Velusamy [SV26], it was shown that, up to logarithmic factors, the single-pass

streaming complexity of deciding satisfiability and exact sparsification is characterized by non-

in one corner of the literature, but rather a structural invariant that governs exact sparsification,
approximate sparsification, and streaming decidability and streaming exact sparsification.
These connections motivate the problem studied in this paper:
Determine the growth of NRDn(R) for symmetric Boolean predicates R.
Our goal is both structural and computational: to identify algebraic criteria that certify polyno-
mial upper and lower bounds on NRDn(R), and to use these criteria to classify low-arity symmetric
Boolean predicates.
1.1
Our results
Our main result is a near-complete classification of the growth of NRDn(R) for low-arity symmetric
Boolean predicates. For arities at most 4, our upper- and lower-bound criteria always match, giving
the exact asymptotic growth. At arity 5, the same framework resolves all predicates except two.
Theorem 1.1. For every non-trivial symmetric Boolean predicate R, the following hold.
1. If R has arity at most 4, then the asymptotic growth of NRDn(R) is determined exactly.
2. If R has arity 5, then the asymptotic growth of NRDn(R) is determined exactly for all predicates
except, up to bit-flip symmetry,2 the two predicates R023 and R124 with W(R023) = {0, 2, 3} and
W(R124) = {1, 2, 4}. For each R ∈{R023, R124}, we obtain Ω(n2) ≤NRDn(R) ≤O(n3).
The exact exponents for all predicates covered by the classification are reported in Tables 1
and 2. The classification is obtained by combining an algebraic upper-bound criterion with an
algebraic lower-bound criterion. For a relation R, let u(R) denote the exponent certified by our
upper-bound criterion, and let ℓ(R) denote the exponent certified by the lower-bound criterion.
Whenever u(R) = ℓ(R), the two criteria give matching bounds NRDn(R) = Θ(nu(R)).
At arity 5; as suggested by the Table 2; our framework leaves exactly two unresolved predicates:
W(R023) = {0, 2, 3} and W(R124) = {1, 2, 4}. For both predicates, our lower-bound criterion yields
only an Ω(n2) lower bound, while our upper-bound criterion gives an O(n3) upper bound. Thus
these are the first symmetric Boolean predicates that fall strictly between the current lower- and
upper-bound frameworks. We further show in Section 3.3 that understanding these predicates
leads naturally to extremal set-system questions, which we view as a promising direction for future
work.
The classification is obtained by combining two algebraic criteria, one for upper bounds and
one for lower bounds.
Upper bounds via lifted balancedness.
On the upper-bound side, our main conceptual contribu-
tion is a lifted version of balancedness. The notion of balancedness was introduced by Chen, Jansen,
and Pieterse [CJP20] in their study of exact sparsification of Boolean CSPs, where it captures the
degree-1 polynomial phenomenon underlying linear sparsification. Our notion of t-balancedness
extends this viewpoint to higher-degree multilinear monomial lifts. We show that t-balancedness
exactly characterizes the existence of degree-≤t polynomial that captures the predicate, i.e., the
polynomial that separates falsifying tuples from satisfying tuples, and therefore yields general
O(nt) upper bounds on NRDn(R).

Theorem 1.2 (Upper-Bound criterion; informal Theorem 4.6). Let R ⊆{0, 1}r and let t ≥1. Then R
is t-balanced if and only if every falsifying tuple f /∈R is captured by a multilinear polynomial of degree at
most t over some commutative ring with identity. Consequently, every n-variable instance of CSP(R) has
an equivalent subinstance with O(nt) constraints, and hence NRDn(R) = O(nt).
Lower bounds via universal cubes.
On the lower-bound side, we use Carbonnel’s [Car22]
algebraic framework for proving lower bounds on non-redundancy. At a high level, this framework
gives a criterion for when a relation R is expressive enough to encode the k-ary OR relation. Since
ORk has non-redundancy Ω(nk), witnessed by placing one positive ORk constraint on every k-
subset of variables. Therefore, such an encoding immediately yields an Ω(nk) lower bound on
NRDn(R). For details see Section 5.
Robustness to instance-model choices.
Our classification concerns the exponent of NRDn(R)
and is unaffected by standard choices in the constraint model. In Section 6, we prove that allowing
unary maps on variables, constants, or repeated variables inside constraints does not change the
asymptotic non-redundancy, compared with the model where each constraint is applied directly to
a tuple of distinct variables. The equivalence holds up to constant-factor changes in the number of
variables. The intuition is that, for Boolean predicates of fixed arity, literals, constants, and repeated
occurrences can be simulated by replacing each original variable with a constant number of tagged
copies. Hence our results are independent of these modelling choices.
1.2
Organization
In Section 2, we introduce basic notation, the CSP model, symmetric Boolean predicates, and
the different instance models used in the paper. In Section 3.1, we present the computational
framework: the upper-bound criterion based on t-balancedness, the lower-bound criterion based
on the universal-cube test, the resulting classification up to arity 4, and the two exceptional arity-5
predicates. In Section 4, we prove the upper-bound criterion by relating t-balancedness to low-
degree capturing polynomials and then applying the sparsification theorem of [CJP20]. In Section 5,
we recall Carbonnel’s[Car22] lower-bound framework and proves the universal-cube lower-bound
criterion used in the computations. In Section 6, we prove the robustness of non-redundancy under
standard instance-model choices, such as allowing unary maps, constants, and repeated variables
in constraints. Finally, in Section 7, we conclude with some open problems.

Preliminaries and notation
2.1
Basic notation
For a positive integer n, we write [n] := {1, 2, . . . , n}. For a finite set S, we write {0, 1}S for the set
of all Boolean vectors indexed by S, that is, all functions x: S →{0, 1}. When S = [n], we identify
{0, 1}[n] with the usual Boolean cube {0, 1}n. For a vector x ∈{0, 1}r, we write |x| for its Hamming
weight, that is, the number of coordinates equal to 1. All asymptotic notation is with respect to the
number n of variables. The hidden constants may depend on fixed parameters such as the arity of
the relation under consideration.
Throughout, all rings are commutative rings with identity. When evaluating polynomials over a
ring E on Boolean inputs, we identify 0, 1 with 0E, 1E. Integer coefficients and signs are interpreted

2.2
CSPs
A constraint satisfaction problem is specified by a set of relations over a domain. Fix a finite
domain D and a set Γ of r-ary relations over D. An instance of CSP(Γ) consists of n variables
V := {x1, . . . , xn} and a set I of m constraints. A constraint C is defined by a pair {R, ¯x}, where
R ∈Γ is an r-ary relation and ¯x = (xi1, . . . , xir) ∈Vr is an ordered tuple of variables. Repetitions
inside ¯x are allowed unless explicitly stated otherwise. An assignment is a map σ: V →D. Such
an assignment satisfies the constraint {R, (xi1, . . . , xir)} if (σ(xi1), . . . , σ(xir)) ∈R. We write Sat(I)
for the set of satisfying assignments of an instance I.
Notice that above we assume that all relations in a fixed language Γ have the same arity r. This
is without loss of generality for fixed finite languages: if R ⊆Dk has arity k < r, we may replace it
by the padded relation R′ := R × Dr−k ⊆Dr, where the extra coordinates are dummy coordinates.
A constraint using R can then be viewed as a constraint using R′ by adding arbitrary variables in
the dummy positions.
Symmetric Boolean predicates and the OR relation.
From now on, when discussing Boolean
relations, the domain is {0, 1}. We use the terms Boolean relation and Boolean predicate interchange-
ably. Let R ⊆{0, 1}r be a Boolean relation. We say that R is symmetric if membership in R
depends only on Hamming weight. Equivalently, there is a set W(R) ⊆{0, 1, . . . , r} such that
R = {x ∈{0, 1}r : |x| ∈W(R)}. Throughout the paper, we identify a symmetric relation with its
accepted-weight set W(R).
For k ≥2, we write ORk ⊆{0, 1}k for the k-ary OR relation, that is, ORk := {0, 1}k \ {0k}.
Equivalently, (x1, . . . , xk) ∈ORk if and only if at least one input bit is 1. Equivalently, W(ORk) =
{1, . . . , k}.
Although one can define non-redundancy for arbitrary finite Boolean constraint languages,
it is enough, at the level of exponents, to understand singleton languages Γ = {R}. Indeed, if
Γ = {R1, . . . , Rs} is fixed and the singleton exponents are known, then the exponent for Γ is the
maximum of the singleton exponents. The lower bound follows by using only one relation, and the
upper bound follows by sparsifying the constraints of each relation separately. We therefore mostly
work with a single symmetric relation R ⊆{0, 1}r.
Equivalence and non-redundancy.
Two instances I and I′ are equivalent if they have the same set
of satisfying assignments, that is, if Sat(I) = Sat(I′). Let I be an instance and let C be a constraint of
I. We say that C is redundant if I \ {C} is equivalent to I, that is, if deleting C does not change the set
of satisfying assignments. We say that I is non-redundant if none of its constraints is redundant. The
main parameter studied in this paper is the non-redundancy of a language. For a finite language
Γ, define NRDn(Γ) to be the maximum number of constraints in a non-redundant instance of
CSP(Γ) on n variables. When the language consists of a single relation R, we write ΓR := {R},
CSP(R) := CSP(ΓR), and NRDn(R) := NRDn(ΓR).

We call a relation R ⊆{0, 1}r non-trivial if ∅̸= R ⊊{0, 1}r. The two trivial cases are easy to
handle separately: if R = {0, 1}r, then every R-constraint is always satisfied and hence redundant,
so NRDn(R) = 0; if R = ∅, then any instance containing at least one R-constraint is unsatisfiable,
and it is easy to see that NRDn(R) = 1. Thus, except where stated otherwise, we restrict attention
to non-trivial relations.
We also note that every non-trivial relation already has linear non-redundancy. Indeed, if a ∈R
and b ∈{0, 1}r \ R, then by partitioning the variables into ⌊n/r⌋pairwise disjoint r-tuples and
placing one copy of R on each block, we obtain a non-redundant instance with ⌊n/r⌋constraints:
to witness that a given block is essential, assign b on that block and a on every other block. Hence
NRDn(R) = Ω(n) for every non-trivial R.
Bit-flip symmetry.
For a Boolean relation R ⊆{0, 1}r, let R := {1 −x : x ∈R} be its image
under global bit-flip. If R is symmetric with accepted weights W(R), then R has accepted weights
r −W(R) := {r −w : w ∈W(R)}. The relations R and R have the same non-redundancy: replacing
every R-constraint by the corresponding R-constraint and complementing all Boolean assignments
gives a bijection between satisfying assignments, preserving equivalence of subinstances and
non-redundancy. Hence NRDn(R) = NRDn(R).
Literals, positive, and simple positive models.
We will use two closely related models of in-
stances, and one useful restriction of the positive model.
1. Literal model. Fix a finite set U of unary maps g : D →D containing the identity map.
Constraints may be applied to literals, i.e., unary substitutions of variables: a constraint has
the form {R, (g1(xi1), . . . , gr(xir))}, where R ⊆Dr and g1, . . . , gr ∈U. Constant maps may be
included in U, so this model also allows constants. We call this the literal model because, in
the Boolean case, taking U = {id, ¬, 0, 1} gives exactly the usual variables, negated variables,
and constants.
2. Positive model.
Constraints are applied only to variables:
a constraint has the form
{R, (xi1, . . . , xir)}, where the tuple (xi1, . . . , xir) is ordered and repetitions are allowed. It
is called simple if no constraint repeats a variable. Thus, in a simple positive instance, every
constraint has distinct variables.
By Theorem 6.1, for fixed finite domains and fixed arity, the literal, positive, and simple positive
models have the same asymptotic non-redundancy up to a constant-factor blow-up in the number
of variables. We will therefore move between them when convenient, explicitly indicating the
model when it matters. Unless stated otherwise, CSP(R) and NRDn(R) refer to the literal model.
3
Computing non-redundancy of symmetric predicates of small arity
3.1
Experimental methodology
Our computational approach studies the lower-bound and upper-bound mechanisms separately.
For each symmetric Boolean relation R, we compute one algebraic witness for a lower bound on
NRDn(R) and one algebraic witness for an upper bound on NRDn(R). When the two exponents
match, this yields the exact growth rate of the non-redundancy. The full proofs appear later in
Sections 4 and 5; here we state the statements and the intuition behind the two tests.

3.1.1
Upper-bound criterion
The upper-bound criterion is based on polynomial capturing. Let R ⊆{0, 1}r and let f ∈{0, 1}r \ R
be a falsifying tuple. A polynomial p over a commutative ring E with identity captures f with
respect to R if p(y) = 0 for every y ∈R, but p( f ) ̸= 0. Thus, a capturing polynomial separates one
particular falsifying tuple from all satisfying tuples of R.
Capturing polynomials are useful for non-redundancy because of the sparsification theorem of
Chen, Jansen, and Pieterse [CJP20]: if every falsifying tuple f /∈R can be captured by a polynomial
of degree at most t, then every instance of CSP(R) on n variables has an equivalent subinstance
with O(nt) constraints. Consequently, any non-redundant instance itself has size at most O(nt).
Therefore, to prove NRDn(R) = O(nt), it is enough to certify that every falsifying tuple of R has a
degree-≤t capturing polynomial.
We now introduce the degree-t lift, whose purpose is to turn degree-t multilinear polynomials
into linear polynomials. Fix t ≥1, and let Sr,t := {S ⊆[r] : 1 ≤|S| ≤t}. The degree-t lift maps
each tuple x ∈{0, 1}r to the vector νt(x) ∈{0, 1}Sr,t whose S-coordinate is νt(x)S = ∏i∈S xi. Thus a
degree-t multilinear polynomial in x1, . . . , xr is exactly a linear polynomial in the lifted coordinates,
together with a constant term.

polynomial p(x) = (x1 + x2 + x3 −1)(x1 + x2 + x3 −2) vanishes on all tuples of Hamming weight 1 or 2,
and is nonzero on tuples of Hamming weight 0 or 3. Thus this degree-2 polynomial captures both falsifying
tuples.

closure condition called t-balancedness. Let Xt := νt({0, 1}r) be the lifted Boolean cube and let

sums inside Xt: for every odd integer m, every a(1), . . . , a(m) ∈R(t), and every u := a(1) −a(2) +
· · · −a(m) ∈Xt, we also have u ∈R(t). The significance of this definition is the following theorem,
proved in Section 4.
Theorem 1.2 (Upper-Bound criterion; informal Theorem 4.6). Let R ⊆{0, 1}r and let t ≥1. Then R
is t-balanced if and only if every falsifying tuple f /∈R is captured by a multilinear polynomial of degree at
most t over some commutative ring with identity. Consequently, every n-variable instance of CSP(R) has
an equivalent subinstance with O(nt) constraints, and hence NRDn(R) = O(nt).

Implementation.
We now describe how the upper-bound criterion is implemented. Fix a sym-
metric relation R ⊆{0, 1}r and an integer t ≥1. We first form the degree-t lift νt(x) for every

R(t) = νt(R). The goal is to test whether R(t) is balanced relative to Xt.
Directly checking the definition of t-balancedness would require searching over all odd alter-
nating sums of tuples from R(t). Instead, we use the equivalent integer-span formulation proved in
Lemma 4.4. For a lifted vector v, write ext(v) := (1, v). Then R is t-balanced if and only if, for every
b ∈Xt \ R(t), the vector ext(b) does not lie in the integer span of the vectors {ext(a) : a ∈R(t)}.

3.1.2
Lower bound criterion
The lower-bound criterion is based on defining the k-ary OR predicate from R. The predicate ORk
has non-redundancy Θ(nk). For example, the instance containing one positive ORk constraint on
each k-subset of variables is non-redundant. Thus, if ORk is “definable” from R by a constant-
size gadget, then non-redundant ORk-instances can be translated into non-redundant R-instances,
giving NRDn(R) = Ω(nk).

the constraint
{NAE3, (x, y, 0)} is false exactly on (x, y) = (0, 0) and true otherwise. Thus it behaves as {OR2, (x, y)}.
Since OR2 has non-redundant instances of size Ω(n2), this gives an Ω(n2) lower bound.

The formal version of this gadget notion is Carbonnel’s [Car22] fgpp-definability. Informally,

Implementation.
We do not search directly for an fgpp-definition of ORk from R. Instead, we
use Carbonnel’s [Car22] equivalent preservation criterion: ORk ∈⟨R⟩fg if and only if a certain
partial operation, called the universal k-cube operation, does not preserve R. We denote this partial
operation by fk. Here “preserve” has the usual polymorphism meaning: if we arrange tuples from
R as rows of a matrix and apply fk coordinatewise, then the resulting tuple should again lie in R,
whenever fk is defined on every column. Thus, to prove a lower bound, the code searches for a
failure of preservation: input rows all lying in R whose coordinatewise image under fk lies outside
R.
Fix k ≥2, and let m = 2k −1. Index the m input rows by the nonzero strings v ∈{0, 1}k \ {0k}.
For each coordinate i ∈[k], define the column ci ∈{0, 1}m by (ci)v = vi, where vi is the ith bit of v.
The universal k-cube partial operation, denoted fk, is the partial operation of arity m whose domain
is {0m, 1m} ∪{ci, 1 −ci : i ∈[k]}, and whose values are given by fk(0m) = 0, fk(1m) = 1, fk(ci) = 0,
and fk(1 −ci) = 1 for every i ∈[k]. See Example 3.5.

of preservation is exactly the case where all input rows lie in R, but this output tuple lies outside R.

Upper Bound
In this section we prove the upper bound on the non-redundancy of t-balanced Boolean relations.
The proof has two ingredients.
First, in Section 4.1, we define t-balancedness via a degree-t monomial lift and prove that it
is equivalent to the existence of degree-≤t multilinear capturing polynomials over rings, see
Theorem 4.6. The proof reduces the degree-t statement to the degree-1 balancedness of [CJP20]
in the lifted space. The key intermediate step is a relative version of their linear-capture theorem,
stated as Lemma 4.5.
Second, in Section 4.2, we combine Theorem 4.6 with the general polynomial sparsifica-
tion theorem of Chen, Jansen, and Pieterse [CJP20], stated as Theorem 4.7, to obtain an O(nt)
sparsification for CSP(R) whenever R is t-balanced. The desired non-redundancy upper bound
NRDn(R) = O(nt) then follows immediately, since a non-redundant instance cannot be equivalent
to a proper subinstance, see Theorem 4.9.

algorithm that, given a set of R-constraints on n variables, outputs an equivalent subset of O(nt) constraints.

has an equivalent subinstance with O(nt) constraints.
Proof. By Theorem 4.6, for every tuple f ∈{0, 1}r \ R, there exists a prime-power q f and a mul-
tilinear polynomial p f (x1, . . . , xr) over Z/q f Z of degree at most t that capturesR f. Hence, by

straints.
Theorem 4.9. Let R ⊆{0, 1}r be t-balanced, with t ≥1. Then NRDn(R) = O(nt).
Proof. Consider any non-redundant instance I of CSP(R) on n variables. By Corollary 4.8, I has
an equivalent subinstance I′ with O(nt) constraints. Since I is non-redundant, no proper subset of
its constraints is equivalent to I. Hence I′ = I, and therefore I itself has O(nt) constraints. This
proves the claim.
Consequently, if u(R) := min{t ≥1 : R is t-balanced}, then NRDn(R) = O(nu(R)).

Lower Bound
In this section we recall the lower-bound framework of Carbonnel [Car22]. The role of this section
is to justify the lower-bound test used in Section 3.1. The framework has two ingredients. First,
fgpp-definability formalizes when constraints of one relation can be replaced by constant-size
gadgets over another relation while preserving non-redundancy lower bounds. Second, pattern
partial polymorphisms give an algebraic characterization of fgpp-definability. For our purposes,
the only pattern needed is Carbonnel’s universal k-cube pattern: if this pattern fails to preserve R,
then R inherits the Ω(nk) non-redundancy lower bound of ORk.
Before defining fgpp-definability formally, let us explain their role. In ordinary pp-definability
(famously used by Schaefer [Sch78] for P vs NP classification of problems), one expresses a target
relation by a conjunction of constraints over a base language. Fgpp-definability extends this by
allowing certain unary functional guards required for non-redundancy lower-bounds.

Conclusion
We studied the non-redundancy of symmetric Boolean predicates. On the upper-bound side, we in-
troduced t-balancedness, a higher-degree extension of the balancedness framework of Chen, Jansen,
and Pieterse [CJP20], and showed that it yields polynomial capturing representations and hence
upper bounds on NRDn(R). On the lower-bound side, we used Carbonnel’s [Car22] universal-cube
criterion to certify reductions from ORk and obtain matching lower bounds. Combining these two
criteria, we classified the asymptotic growth of NRDn(R) for every symmetric Boolean predicate of
arity at most 4, and for all but two predicates of arity 5.
The most immediate open problem is to resolve the two remaining arity-5 predicates,
W(R023) = {0, 2, 3}
and
W(R124) = {1, 2, 4}.
For both predicates, our current criteria give only Ω(n2) ≤NRDn(R) ≤O(n3). Closing this gap
would complete the arity-5 classification. It would also clarify whether the lifted balancedness
upper bound or the universal-cube lower bound can be strengthened, or whether these predicates
require genuinely different techniques.

A second direction is to understand non-redundancy beyond symmetric Boolean predicates.
Symmetry makes the problem amenable to a finite weight-set analysis, but this structure disappears
for general languages. A concrete benchmark is the ternary non-Boolean predicate BCK :=
{111, 222, 012, 120, 201} ⊆{0, 1, 2}3 from Bessiere, Carbonnel, and Katsirelos [BCK20], which has
been highlighted in later work as a predicate whose non-redundancy is not explained by the
standard universal-cube lower-bound machinery [BGJ+25]. Understanding such examples may
require new lower-bound mechanisms beyond reductions from ORk, and could be a step toward
a structural characterization of the exponent governing NRDn(Γ) for arbitrary finite constraint
languages.
