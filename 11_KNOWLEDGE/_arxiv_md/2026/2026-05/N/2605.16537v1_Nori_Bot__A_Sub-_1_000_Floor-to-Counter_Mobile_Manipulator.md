Nori Bot: A Sub-$1,000 Floor-to-Counter Mobile
Manipulator
Antonio Li, Sungjoon Park, Wen Ni Chew
Department of Computer Science, Columbia University
{asl2237, sp4050, wc2927}@columbia.edu
Abstract—Capable mobile manipulators remain expensive:
Mobile ALOHA at $32,000, Hello Robot Stretch at ∼$25,000,
PAL Robotics TIAGo above $200,000. Open-source efforts have
lowered the floor (XLeRobot at $660, AhaRobot at $1,000–
2,000), but every sub-$1,000 platform we know of shares three
limitations: arms at a single fixed height, control that only
fires when a human prompts it, and no protection against the
stall-induced burn-out that destroys the cheap Feetech servos
these platforms depend on. We present Nori Bot, a 17-DoF
dual-arm mobile manipulator at $947 in parts (∼3% the cost
of comparable commercial platforms) that addresses all three.
Three contributions: (1) a 600 mm linear-rail Z-axis lift driven
by a Feetech STS3215 servo on the same bus as the right arm,
giving floor-to-counter reach without a second controller board;
(2) a thin-client compute architecture, with a Raspberry Pi 4
(1 GB RAM) running only motor I/O and the robot exposed
to the OpenClaw proactive agent runtime as a skill manifest,
so cron jobs, hooks, and heartbeats can trigger physical tasks;
and (3) an onboard software stack that prevents Feetech burn-
out (stall detector, calibration-clamping fix, EEPROM backstops)
and recovers continuous grip-force feedback by mapping motor
current to a normalized [0, 1] signal on a soft TPU finger. A
7-inch touchscreen runs an animated face for HRI. The longer-
term goal is to lower unit cost and setup friction far enough that
identical units can be deployed widely and their teleoperation
data pooled server-side [4], [19]; this paper delivers the per-unit
foundation, not the pooling layer. We integrate with LeRobot
for ACT training and serving, demonstrate five tasks across the
Z-axis envelope (book re-shelving, trash-to-bin, laundry sorting
and folding, autonomous cron-triggered coffee), and report an
ACT policy trained on 30 demonstrations that grasps a small
DC motor from a desk. Code, CAD, and the OpenClaw skill
manifest will be released.
I. INTRODUCTION
Capable mobile manipulators are still too expensive for
most research and consumer use. Hello Robot Stretch [14]
sits at ∼$25,000, PAL Robotics TIAGo exceeds $200,000,
and even the cheapest academic dual-arm platform, Mobile
ALOHA [11], costs $32,000. Open-source work has driven
costs lower in two years: TidyBot++ [24] at $5,000–6,000,
XLeRobot [23] at $660, and AhaRobot [10] at $1,000–2,000.
Imitation learning has matured in parallel: ACT [26] attains
80–90% per-skill success from ∼50 demonstrations, and LeR-
obot [6] packages data collection, training, and gRPC serving
in one stack.
But three practical gaps remain at the sub-$1,000 tier. First,
vertical workspace: every cheap platform we know of has arms
at a single fixed height, which makes the same robot unable to
pick laundry off the floor and reach a kitchen counter. Second,
reactive-only control: existing low-cost manipulators act only
when a human prompts them, but useful household automation
(coffee at 8 a.m., pre-bedtime tidy at 9 p.m.) is a scheduled,
recurring problem. Third, servo fragility and missing force
feedback: the Feetech STS3215 servos that power XLeRobot,
AhaRobot, and SO-ARM strip their plastic gear in seconds
under sustained stall, and none of these platforms ship with
stall protection or grip-force feedback. We lost two servos this
way before adding any protection.
We present Nori Bot, a 17-DoF dual-arm mobile manipu-
lator (12 arm, 2 head, 2 wheels, 1 lift) costing $947 in parts,
∼3% the cost of comparable commercial platforms. Three
contributions address the gaps:
1) A 600 mm Z-axis lift on a single-bus motor stack. A
stock HGR20 / RM1605 ball-screw lift on the XLeRobot
cart, driven by a Feetech STS3215 servo on the same
half-duplex bus as the right arm. No extra controller
board, no second motor protocol, full floor-to-counter
reach.
2) Thin-client compute with OpenClaw orchestration.
The on-robot brain is a Raspberry Pi 4 with 1 GB of
RAM running only motor I/O and a thin WebSocket
bridge. Heavy compute runs off-board. The robot is
exposed as a skill manifest to the OpenClaw proactive
agent runtime [3], [18], so cron, hooks, and heartbeats
schedule physical tasks.
3) Onboard safety and sensorless force sensing. A stall
detector, calibration clamping, and persistent-register
(EEPROM) backstops prevent Feetech burn-out. A soft
TPU (thermoplastic polyurethane) gripper finger turns
the servo’s existing current register into a continuous
grip-force signal that ACT consumes as an observation
channel.
The 7-inch touchscreen running an animated face is not
decoration. It is part of the design (Sec. III-A0d): home robots
work in close human proximity, and the face surfaces task
progress, error states, and a confirmation/e-stop UI in ways
a few LEDs cannot. We treat it as a human-robot interaction
(HRI) choice, not a cosmetic one.
a) Broader motivation: The longer-term goal driving
this project is to make the unit cost and setup friction of a
capable manipulator low enough that many identical robots can
exist in the wild, and that the teleoperation data they collect
can be pooled server-side to train better generalist policies.
The motivation comes from recent work on generalist robot

II. RELATED WORK
a) Low-cost open-source mobile manipulators: Mobile
ALOHA [11] demonstrated bimanual whole-body teleopera-
tion and cotraining at $32,000. TidyBot++ [24] reduced this to
$5,000–6,000 with a holonomic base and vertical lift. XLeR-
obot [23] reached $660 with two SO-101 arms on an IKEA
cart, and we use this as our mechanical base. AhaRobot [10]
reached $1,000–2,000 with a custom horizontal arm mount
and is the only sub-$1,000 platform that reaches the floor
(via fixed arm geometry rather than active vertical travel). Our
hardware contribution is complementary: we keep the off-the-
shelf XLeRobot mechanical base and add an actuated 600 mm
Z-axis.
b) Proactive agent orchestration: LLM-driven robot or-
chestration to date has been reactive: SayCan [2], Inner Mono-
logue [12], Code-as-Policies [16], and CoPAL [13] all wait
for a user prompt. On the agent-runtime side, OpenClaw [18]
has emerged as a widely adopted open-source framework
that combines cron jobs, hooks, and heartbeats to make a
language-model agent proactive [3], [21], with a broader
skill ecosystem [7] and an emerging security literature [8].
Domain applications include digital twins for rare-disease
genomics [1]. To our knowledge, ours is the first work to plug
a low-cost physical manipulator into the OpenClaw runtime
as a cron-schedulable skill.
c) Servo protection and force sensing on cheap hard-
ware: Feetech STS3215 servos are the de facto standard for
low-cost research arms (XLeRobot, SO-ARM, AhaRobot), but
their plastic gear train fails under sustained stall. Published
platforms describe their motor wiring but, to our knowledge,
do not document a software-layer stall-protection stack. Our
protections are closest in spirit to bio-inspired grasping con-
trollers [15] that detect tactile contact through phase transi-
tions, except we have no dedicated tactile sensor.
Estimating contact force from motor current is well-
established in industrial robotics: Universal Robots and Robo-
tiq ship sensorless force estimation as standard on their cobots
and parallel grippers, computed from the same current-to-
torque relationship we use here. Research alternatives use
embedded air channels [17], vision-based deformation sens-
ing [20], external cameras [9], and discrete tactile arrays [25].
Several authors note that motor current alone is a weak
proxy on a rigid finger [9], since the signal blends inertial
transients with contact load. We agree for rigid fingers and
argue (Sec. III-C) that the same signal becomes useful and
continuous once the finger is compliant: a soft TPU finger
turns a current-vs-stall step into a current-vs-squeeze ramp.
III. METHODS
Nori Bot consists of (A) the hardware platform with the new
Z-axis, (B) a thin-client compute architecture with OpenClaw
proactive orchestration, (C) an onboard safety and force-
sensing software stack, and (D) a LeRobot/ACT imitation-
learning pipeline. Figures 1 and 2 show the assembled robot
and demonstrated tasks.
A. Hardware platform
We start from the XLeRobot v0.4.0 reference design [23]:
an IKEA RASKOG three-tier rolling cart, two SO-101 6-DoF
arms (Feetech STS3215 servos, ∼40 cm horizontal reach), a
2-wheel differential-drive base, a 2-DoF pan-tilt head, a 7-inch
touchscreen, and a head-mounted Logitech C920 webcam.
Including our 1-DoF lift, this gives 17 actuated joints across
two half-duplex Feetech buses driven by two USB CH343
control boards. Power is an Anker SOLIX C300 station
(288 Wh, 140 W per USB-C port).
a) Z-axis lift, mechanical: The lift is a stock CNC-style
kit: 2× HGR20 600 mm linear rails, four HGH20CA blocks,
an RM1605 ball screw (16 mm diameter, 5 mm lead) with
BF12/BK12 end supports, ball-nut housing, and a flexible
coupler. The dual-rail / four-block geometry constrains the
carriage in all four DoF transverse to travel, preserving the
kinematic context that ACT trains on across Z heights. (An
earlier single-screw single-rod prototype had visible rotational
play under arm moment load, which corrupted policies trained
at one Z height when executed at another.) The 600 mm of
travel takes the arm-mount platform from ∼0 mm above the
cart base to a height clearing a standard 36-inch (914 mm) US
kitchen counter at full forward reach.
b) Z-axis lift, actuator choice: The non-obvious choice
is what drives the screw. The textbook answer is a stepper
motor (NEMA-17) with a dedicated driver (TMC2209) and
controller board (Marlin or similar), the same parts as a 3D
printer. We deliberately did not do this. Instead, the ball screw
is driven by a Feetech STS3215 servo (motor ID 11) on the
same half-duplex bus as the right arm. The STS3215 has a
1:345 reduction and at 12 V can hold a steady ∼1 kg payload
through the ball screw’s mechanical advantage, sufficient for
the slow Z motion floor-to-counter reach requires. Three
practical advantages follow:
• One motor protocol, one calibration file. The lift appears
in the LeRobot dataset format as another joint, sharing the

B. Thin-client compute and OpenClaw orchestration
The architectural choice underlying contribution (2) is that
the on-robot brain is deliberately small. A Raspberry Pi 4
with 1 GB of RAM cannot run a 7 B-parameter VLM, an
ACT policy at 10 Hz, and a planner concurrently, and pushing
more silicon onto the robot would either inflate cost or shorten
battery life. We considered two alternatives: an on-robot Jetson
Orin Nano (more capable but adds ∼$500 and reduces battery
life by half), and tethering the robot to a workstation by USB 3
(constrains where the robot can go). Our choice is a thin client:
the Pi runs only motor I/O and a WebSocket bridge to an off-
board agent on the same network, which can be a workstation,

C. Onboard safety and sensorless force sensing
The Feetech STS3215 has a deterministic failure mode: a
goal position is commanded outside the joint range or against
an unexpected obstacle, the integral term ramps up, the motor
draws stall current, and the 1:345 plastic gear strips in seconds.
We lost two servos this way before deploying the protections
below, which work in three layers.
a) Calibration
clamping:

b) Software stall detector: Each control loop, for every
motor, we read Present_Current (register 69) alongside
Present_Position and the goal. We maintain a k = 15-

|∆position| ≈0 for the entire window. On stall, the gripper
drops torque to a GRIP_TORQUE of 200, holding the object
gently rather than crushing it; other joints retry once at full
torque, then drop to a STALL_TORQUE of 100 until a new
target is commanded. Torque restores when the goal changes.

The compliance is what makes the signal useful. On a
rigid finger, motor current produces a stall-like step that
is hard to disentangle from inertial transients [9]. On the
TPU finger, additional commanded gripper travel after contact
squishes the TPU rather than the object: the encoder continues
to move slightly under the deformation while current rises
monotonically with squeeze depth. This gives a continuous
current-vs-squeeze ramp instead of a discontinuous stall step,
which an imitation-learning policy can use directly as an
observation channel.

D. Imitation learning execution
We use ACT [26] as the imitation-learning model and
LeRobot [6] as the data, training, and serving stack. Each skill
is a separate ACT checkpoint; we did not co-train across skills,
since at our demo scale (tens of episodes per skill) shared
multi-task training would overfit faster than per-skill training.
Demonstrations are collected via leader–follower teleoperation
at 50 Hz with one of our STS3215 arms acting as leader.
The policy observes two RGB cameras (head-mounted
Logitech C920 and a wrist-mounted camera on the right arm),
the right-arm joint state (6 DoF), and the two gripper-force
channels (raw current and normalized ˆft). The action space
is 6 DoF target joint positions for the right arm and gripper.
The Z-axis, head pan/tilt, and base velocity are scripted by the
agent before the policy takes over, keeping each ACT check-
point small. We use the LeRobot ACT defaults: chunk size 100
(≈2 s of motion per forward pass at 50 Hz), n_action_steps 40
(re-query every ≈0.8 s), ∼80 M parameters. Training is 40,000
gradient steps on a single consumer-laptop GPU; the gRPC
PolicyServer streams target joint commands at ∼10 Hz.
IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
We report on five end-to-end task demonstrations (Fig. 2),
one trained ACT skill, and protection events logged across
four weeks of operation.
A. End-to-end demonstrations
Five tasks were demonstrated end-to-end, spanning the full
Z-axis envelope: re-shelving books at desk-mid height; trash-
to-bin pickup from the floor with the carriage fully lowered;
sorting laundry from a basket at cart height; folding a piece of
cloth on a tabletop; and an autonomous make_coffee task
at 8 a.m. on a weekday triggered by an OpenClaw cron job
with no human prompt. None of these are reachable from a
single fixed arm height, which is the point of the Z-axis. The
make_coffee demonstration is the proactive case: the robot
wakes itself, navigates to the coffee maker, and pours into a
cup without a user issuing a command. Per-task quantitative
benchmarking is in progress for the camera-ready.
a) Latency: End-to-end latency from cron fire to first
arm motion averages ∼2.4 s, dominated by OpenClaw context
assembly and the cloud-VLM call that selects the skill. Per-
step agent latency once the skill is running is ∼1.9 s (median);
ACT inference on the PolicyServer runs at ∼10 Hz indepen-
dently. For context, scheduled household tasks tolerate ∼10 s
startup latency, while reactive tasks typically require <500 ms.
Our latency budget is well-suited to the proactive (cron, hook,

B. ACT skill: pick_motor
We trained one ACT checkpoint on the right arm for a
representative pick-up skill: grasping a small DC motor off
a desk and lifting it clear. The training set is 30 leader–
follower demonstrations recorded at 50 Hz with the motor
placed in randomized starting positions. Training is 40,000
gradient steps on a single consumer-laptop GPU; deployment
positions the lift to a fixed Z, scripts a neutral pre-grasp pose,
and dispatches the ACT policy.
a) In-distribution success: With the motor placed inside
the trained spatial distribution under neutral lighting and a
clean background, the policy reliably closes on the motor and
lifts it. We have not yet logged a controlled n-trial sweep with
categorized failure modes; this is the most relevant benchmark
we plan to add for the camera-ready.
b) Out-of-distribution: Z-axis ablation (planned): Con-
tribution (1) claims the Z-axis is what makes floor-to-counter
manipulation possible on a single platform. To test that claim
we plan to evaluate the trained policy at three lift heights:
the trained Z0 (mid-height), Z0 + 10 cm, and Z0 −10 cm.
We expect success at Z0 to be highest and to degrade off-
distribution, since ACT learns implicit visual cues about Z
and a different lift height shows the same arm geometry from
a different camera angle. We saw this exact failure mode
on an earlier single-rod prototype, where rotational play in
the carriage (Sec. III-A0a) shifted Z subtly between training
and deployment and broke the policy. A clean version on the
production hardware is the next experiment on our list.

stall detector engages at Imax. In the slip case, current spikes briefly then

D. Force signal in deployment
Figure 3 plots a successful and a failed grasp during
pick_motor. In the successful trace (left), ˆft stays near zero
during the approach, rises continuously over ∼140 ms as the
TPU compresses against the motor, and saturates at ˆft = 1
when the stall detector engages and reduces Torque_Limit.
In the failed trace (right), the rise is truncated mid-squeeze,
the encoder catches up to the command, and the current drops
back to idle. The shape of that ramp is the point: a TPU finger
turns a current-vs-stall step into a current-vs-squeeze ramp,
which is what gives ACT something to read as an observation
channel instead of a binary contact bit.
The signal is monotonic in commanded squeeze on rigid
and semi-rigid objects but under-resolves on very compliant
ones (sponges, fabric) [9]. A grip-force-on/off ablation on
pick_motor is planned for the camera-ready to test whether
the channel improves grasp success.
V. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
We presented Nori Bot, a 17-DoF dual-arm mobile ma-
nipulator at $947 in parts, ∼3% the cost of comparable
commercial platforms. Three things distinguish it from prior
sub-$1,000 work: a 600 mm ball-screw Z-axis driven by the
same Feetech protocol as the arms (floor-to-counter reach
without a second controller board); a thin-client compute
architecture paired with the OpenClaw proactive agent runtime
for cron-, hook-, and heartbeat-triggered execution; and an
onboard software layer that prevents Feetech servo burn-out
and recovers continuous grip-force feedback from the gripper’s
existing current register on a soft TPU finger. Five tasks across
the Z envelope are demonstrated, an ACT policy trained on 30
demonstrations grasps a small DC motor from a desk, and zero
servos have been lost since the protections were deployed.
a) Future work: Six items, in priority order: (i) scale
the demo set per skill from 30 to 50+ episodes (the ACT
paper’s recommended floor) and measure how pick_motor
success improves with more data, alongside a grip-force-on/off
ablation; (ii) speed up the Z-axis (the STS3215-driven lift is
currently slow enough that make_coffee feels deliberate
rather than fluid); (iii) drive parts cost below $800 by tighten-
ing the parts list (BOM); (iv) grow the skill repertoire from one
trained skill to eight to ten, with behavior cloning at scale on
the larger corpus; (v) harden the on-robot software (formalize
the WebSocket protocol, extend the dead-man timeout, ship
a clean install path); and (vi) the broader pooled-data goal
motivating this project: a shared dataset specification for
matched Nori-Bot units, a privacy-preserving upload pipeline
that lands demonstrations in a common LeRobot-compatible
format, and a first cross-unit policy result showing that data
pooled from N ≥2 identical units improves a held-out unit’s
success on the same skill, in the spirit of [4], [19].
