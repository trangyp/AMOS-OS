Abstract. We generalize an efficient automata-based approach to string
constraint solving—the stabilization-based method behind the solver Z3-
Noodler—to support relational constraints represented by finite-state
transducers (useful, for example, for modelling replaceAll constraints).
We focus on an efficient treatment of length constraints by reducing the
need for expensive concatenation elimination, which is a major bottle-
neck in automata-based string solving. We also propose powerful heuris-
tics that significantly improve performance in practice. Implemented on
top of Z3-Noodler, our method vastly outperforms existing solvers on
benchmarks with relational constraints—it solves more instances and
runs orders of magnitude faster.

Introduction
String constraint solving has been studied intensively, with a number of compet-
ing approaches and solvers, e.g., [33,15,18,5,46,11,25,9,41,7,16,3,24,35,29,26]. As
the field matures, it finds practical applications (e.g., analysis of resource access
policies [45,6]) and approaches the point where it can fully fulfill its promises
in program analysis and verification. Automata-based approaches, originating
from works such as [49,4], have always excelled in their generality, the range
of handled constraints, and strong decidability guarantees (e.g., [5,18,15,29]).
Recent advances in the stabilization algorithm [13,21,30], implemented in the
solver Z3-Noodler [19,22], have further elevated these approaches beyond the
state-of-the-art in efficiency, as witnessed by the results of SMT-COMP in the
last two years [1,2] and by independent comparisons (e.g., [32,28,36]).
In this paper, we extend the stabilization algorithm to handle transducer-
represented relational constraints, which are essential for modelling functions
such as replaceAll, HTML encoding and decoding, character escaping and un-
escaping, and other transformations that arise in practical applications such as
software analysis and policy verification. We present a decision procedure for

the chain-free fragment of string formulae [5], the largest practical fragment
of string logic with transducers, equations, and length and regular constraints.
The main technical challenge we address is to efficiently integrate the stabi-
lization algorithm with transducer constraints, and its combination with length
constraints. In automata-based methods, length reasoning relies on eliminating
concatenation, a process that often causes severe combinatorial explosion due
to the explicit enumeration of alignments/boundary interleaving of variables on
opposing sides of equations/transducer constraints. For example, a transducer
constraint T(xx, uv) or an equation xx = uv would generate a case split of size
4, with cases having up to twice as many variables. Thus, a sequence of such
steps can generate an exponential number of cases, each exponentially larger in
the number of variables and the length of the concatenation terms. Stabilization
is much cheaper, but its output cannot be used to resolve the length constraints.
We therefore propose a low-level combination of the two, which uses stabilization
whenever possible and eliminates concatenation only from those sub-problems
where interaction with the length constraints is unavoidable. We further intro-
duce several heuristics that make solving transducer constraints efficient in cases
common in practice, which can (i) simplify certain “homomorphic” transducer
constraints to a conjunction of simpler ones, (ii) replace transducer constraints
with constant input/output by simpler equation constraints, and (iii) attempt to
conclude (un)satisfiability of generally undecidable cyclic transducer constraints.
Note that our handling of negated constraints appears to be more general
than in other takes on the automata approach. From [21], we take the ability to
handle unrestricted negated equations. We also observe that functional trans-
ducer constraints (the encoded relation is a function), covering nearly all known
practical uses of transducers in string solving, can also be negated. Specifically,
a functional transducer constraint T(x, y) can be rewritten as T(x, y′) ∧y′ ̸= y
where y′ is a fresh variable. Although this is technically simple, to our knowledge,
this observation does not appear to be widely known and has not been widely
exploited in existing string solvers: previous works treat transducers as non-
negatable (complements of rational relations are generally not rational [5,33]).
We have implemented our methods on top of the string solver Z3-Noodler
and evaluated them across all available benchmarks that involve transducer con-
straints. They outperform other solvers in almost all benchmark categories in
the number of solved instances and run orders of magnitude faster than the
second-best solver.
Related work. This work continues the branch [13,14,21,19,30,22] of the au-
tomata approach [4] behind one of the most efficient string solvers Z3-Noodler,
and adds an ability to handle transducer-definable constraints such as replaceAll.
Our decision procedure handles the chain-free fragment [5] of equations, trans-
ducers, regular, and length constraints, with a focus on an efficient combination
with length constraints. Our approach is inspired by the combination of the sta-
bilization method with elimination of concatenation from [21], which does not
consider transducers. The automata approach was extended with transducers
also in the branch of works [33,18,15,16,17,29] behind the solver OSTRICH

Preliminaries
Sets, strings and languages. N, Z, and B = {⊤, ⊥} are sets of natural numbers
(with 0), integers, and boolean values. Σ∗is a set of all finite sequences w =
a1 . . . an of symbols from Σ, called strings or words over Σ, with their length
defined as |w| = n and |ε| = 0 for the empty string ε ∈Σ∗. Σε = Σ ∪{ε}.
A concatenation of strings u and v is denoted u · v (uv for short); ε is the
neutral element. We use the same notation for concatenating tuples. We define
an iteration of a word w as w0 = ε and wk+1 = wk · w for k ∈N. A language L
over Σ is a subset of Σ∗. A concatenation of languages is defined as L1 · L2 =
{u · v | u ∈L1, v ∈L2}, where at the place of L1 or L2, we may write just w to
mean language {w}.
Relations. Given a tuple x, xi denotes its i-th item. A projection of an n-ary
relation R to the set of positions I ⊆{1, . . . n} is πI(R) = {(xi)i∈I | x ∈R}
and we write π−j(R) to denote π{1,...,n}\{j}, the elimination of a position j from
R. The synchronization of R with an m-ary relation R′ at positions i, j is the
relation R′ +(i,j) R = {π−i(x) · y | x ∈R, y ∈R′, xi = yj}. The composition of
relations is then R′ ◦(i,j) R = π−(n+j−1)(R′ +(i,j) R) (the synchronized position,
the original j-th track of R′, is eliminated from the result). When i is the arity
of R and j = 1 then we get the normal rational composition R ◦R′. For a set S,
R[S] = R ◦S is the image of S under R. We call the domain of a binary relation
R an input and the range of R an output, and its inversion is the relation
R−1 = {(b, a) | (a, b) ∈R}.

an alphabet Σ is a tuple A = (Q, Σ, δ, I, F) where Q is a finite set of states, δ a set

Chain-free String Constraints
A string theory solver in the CDCL(T) framework solves cubes. We concentrate
on the chain-free fragment of cubes [5]. We first define positive chain-free cubes,
and later discuss how negative atoms can be reduced to a positive chain-free
cube.
Positive chain-free cubes. We adapt the definition of chain-freeness from [13],
which is simpler than the original [5], and extend it with transducer constraints.
The definition is based on the notion of an inclusion graph Gφ = (V, E) of a
positive cube φ, which is also an integral part of our decision procedure. The
nodes V of the inclusion graph are the equations and the transducer constraints
of φ. The graph has an edge (c, c′) ∈E iff there is a shared variable between the
output term of c and the input term of c′.
We say a positive cube φ is directly chain-free iff (i) its inclusion graph is
acyclic, and (ii) no variable occurs multiple times in the inputs of constraints (it
can occur in only one input, with at most one occurrence). Note that any chain-
free cube can be transformed into an equivalent directly chain-free cube [13].
From now on, we presume that all chain-free cubes are in the directly chain-free
form. We define a dual variant of a formula as a formula obtained by replacing
some constraints by their duals, where a dual of an equation dual(s ≈t) = t ≈s
and a dual of a transducer constraint dual(T(s, t)) = T −1(t, s). We call a positive
cube chain-free iff it has a directly chain-free dual variant. Fig. 2 shows an
example of a (non-)chain-free cube and its graph.
We note that our definition of the inclusion graph differs from that in [13],
especially in the cyclic case: nodes on a cycle would have their duals in the
graph as well. The difference is related to technicalities and the presentation of
the decision procedure. It does not however influence the notion of chain-freeness.
Lemma 1. Our definition of a chain-free cube is equivalent to the definition of
a positive chain-free cube in [5].

provided that the negated transducers are functional. Hence, we can extend the
decidability result from [5] with the more general handling of negation.
Theorem 1. Satisfiability of a cube where negated transducer constraints are
functional and where the positive constraints are chain-free is decidable.

Combining Stable and Concatenation-free Form
Conceptually, we decide a positive cube φ in two steps. First, we derive a length
image of Strφ (the part without length constraints), in the form of a LIA formula
LenImg(Strφ). Second, we solve the LIA formula LenImg(Strφ) ∧Lenφ using a
LIA solver.
The length image LenImg(ψ) of a formula ψ is a LIA formula over variables
{lenx | x ∈Var(ψ)} that characterizes the lengths of the solutions of ψ: it has
a solution ν iff ψ has a solution µ where for each x ∈Var(ψ), ν(lenx) = |µ(x)|.
The generation of a length image LenImg(ψ) of a length-free formula goes
back to [4,33,5] and requires a concatenation-free form with acyclic transducer
constraints, possibly with trivial (solved) equations that do not influence satis-
fiability. The concatenation-free form is expensive. Its derivation requires gen-
erating alignments of variables on different sides, with variable splitting. That
may, in theory, add two additional levels of exponential explosion relative to the
stabilization of [13]. Therefore, we combine the stable and concatenation-free
forms: the concatenation-free form only for the part relevant to the length con-
straints and the cheaper stable form for the rest. We will first discuss the two
pure forms and then define the combination.

Stable form. A directly chain-free cube φ is stable when for each equation s ≈t,
Regφ(s) ⊇Regφ(t), and for each transducer constraint T(s, t), T[Regφ(s)] ⊇
Regφ(t). Stability together with the feasibility of regular constraints implies sat-
isfiability, similar to [13,21], where transducer constraints are not considered.
Theorem 2. A stable cube with satisfiable regular constraints is satisfiable.
There is a major difference between the implications of stability for trans-
ducer constraints and for equations. For only equations, a stronger version of
stability implies satisfiability even in the non-chain-free case: the inclusions must
also hold for the duals of constraints on the cycle; there are also additional condi-
tions on multiple occurrences of variables in the inputs [13]. This no longer works
for transducers, as witnessed by a cube T(x, x) ∧x ∈{a, b} where R(L(T)) =
{(a, b), (b, a)}. The cube is stable, has unconstrained and hence non-empty lan-
guages of variables, with both inclusions holding true (T[Σ∗] = T −1[Σ∗] = Σ∗),
but it is unsatisfiable. Thus, in this paper, we define stability only for the chain-
free formulae.
Concatenation-free form. We call a positive cube φ concatenation-free iff (i) con-
catenation appears only in solved equations (also called binding equations) of the
form x ≈tx where x has no other occurrence in φ and Regφ(x) ⊇Regφ(tx), and
(ii) the transducer constraints are chain-free (due to concatenation-freeness, it
just means acyclic).
The inclusions satisfied by the binding equations mean Regφ(x) does not
influence the satisfiability and the values of the variables of tx, and therefore
of any other variables of φ, although the value of tx may still put additional
constraints on the value of x.
Using the techniques discussed in [33,5] and in Section 6.3, a length image
can be extracted from a concatenation-free cube by composing the transducer
constraints into a single multi-tape transducer, extracting its Parikh image, and
then equating the length of x with the sum of the length of variables in tx for
every binding equation.

Noodlification for Equations and Transducer
Constraints
The core of our decision procedure is the transformation of the input cube into
the stable-concatenation-free form by a sequence of noodlification steps of two
9


## Page 10


kinds, introduced for equations in [13] and later extended to equations with
length constraints in [21]. They refine languages of variables to achieve stability;
one additionally splits equations and transducer constraints (and their variables)
to achieve concatenation-freeness. Noodlification steers the generation of possible
equation/transducer splits (alignments) only to the feasible ones. A split is a
splitting of variables into a concatenation of fresh variables where boundaries
between variables on both sides of an equation/transducer constraint match.
A feasible split is such where the fresh variables have non-empty languages.
We first recall the noodlification algorithms for the equations of [13,21], and
then describe both noodlification algorithms for transducers. The algorithms for
transducers are similar to those for equations. The extension from equations to
transducers is nevertheless nontrivial, especially in the case of noodlification that
eliminates concatenation.
To fit our decision procedure in Section 6, all noodlification variants use
a unified interface transforming a directly chain-free cube φ: Reg ∧η where η
is an equation or a transducer constraint to a disjunction of directly chain-free
cubes ψ: Regψ ∧inψ ∧outψ ∧Trψ where (i) Regψ are (refined) regular constraints
in a form closer to stability than Reg, (ii) inψ and outψ are input and output
binding equations of the form x ≈s and s ≈x, respectively, and (iii) Trψ are
(refined) transducer constraints. Note that the binding equations are not fully
concatenation-free, since x may occur in multiple equations, and the output
binding equations admit x on the right. A variable split is performed together
with splitting the variable’s languages in Reg. If Regψ(x) ⊇Regψ(s) for each
input binding equation x ≈s and output binding equation s ≈x and Trψ
is concatenation-free, we call ψ almost concatenation-free. The (input/output)
binding equations in almost concatenation-free cube are in the decision procedure
of Section 6, further processed, and turned into a fully concatenation-free form.
5.1
Noodlification for Equations
Although equations could be in theory treated as a case of transducer constraints
(the relation being identity), the noodlification specialized to equations can be
implemented more efficiently. We therefore use the procedures specialized for
equations from [21] whenever possible, also as a part of the workflow for pro-
cessing transducer constraints.
Stabilizing noodlification. We invoke the procedure L-noodlify from [21] by
calling EqToSt(Reg ∧s ≈t). It returns a disjunction of cubes of the form
ψ: Regψ ∧⊤∧⊤∧⊤. In conjunction with s ≈t, the disjunction is equivalent
to the original cube, and for each disjunct, s ≈t ∧Regψ is stable. Stabilization
treats an equation s ≈t as a pair of inclusions s ⊆t and t ⊆s and refines
only the variables on the left side of the processed inclusion. We use NFAs for
the languages of variables (from initial regular constraints) and perform only
regularity-preserving operations. For s = x1 · · · xn and t = y1 · · · ym, we con-
struct an ϵ-preserving product NFA for the delimited language w1 ◁· · ·◁wn with
each wi ∈Reg(xi) and w1 · · · wn ∈Reg(t), treating ◁as ϵ but preserving it in the

product language. Each noodle is cut at its delimiter transitions into segments,
one per occurrence of a variable in s. For each variable xi, the segments of its
occurrences are intersected to produce the refined language of xi. Noodles that
make some variable empty are discarded.

Stabilizing Noodlification for Transducers
In this section, we describe how to transform a conjunction φ = Reg ∧T(s, t) of
a transducer constraint with normalized regular constraints into the stable form,
without trying to eliminate concatenation. We can simplify our task of achieving
the stability of T(s, t) by rewriting it as an equivalent constraint T(s, z) ∧z ≈t

as it contains only a single variable in the output, and the equation z ≈t can
be stabilized by means of [13]. The T(s, z) is stabilized simply by setting the
language of z to T[Reg(s)].

T[Reg(s)]).

Substitution Rules
The substitution rules RSubst and LSubst transform an output/input binding
equation s ≈x or x ≈s, respectively, into a proper solved equation (moving
it from the almost concatenation-free to the fully concatenation-free part). The
transformation is constructed by applying the substitution σ = {x 7→s} induced
by the equation on all constraints. Application of the substitution σ on a con-
straint η (equation or transducer), denoted σ(η), returns the constraint where
each occurrence of x is replaced with the term s. We extend application of σ to
sets in the natural way.

Theorem 4. The presented decision procedure terminates on a directly chain-
free cube φ, and returns sat iff φ is satisfiable.

Proof (Sketch). Soundness and completeness are preserved by every rule appli-
cation: the refinement rules split the current cube into a finite disjunction of ex-
istential extensions, while the substitution rules apply an equivalence-preserving
substitution and only reinsert constraints whose stability or concatenation-freeness
may have been affected. Thus every branch of the worklist search represents a
satisfiability-equivalent refinement of the input cube. When the frontier becomes
empty, the remaining cube is in the stable-concatenation-free form, so by The-
orem 3 the satisfiability of the current branch is exactly the satisfiability of its
length image, which is what the rule LenImage checks.
For termination, the crucial invariant is acyclicity of the inclusion graph.
The procedure processes constraints in topological order, so in the absence of
substitutions it makes only one forward pass through the graph. The only rules
that can move work back in the graph are RSubst and LSubst by reinserting
the transducer constraint that became non-concatenation-free after the substitu-
tion. However, these reinserted constraints contain only a single variable on the
input. Substitutions generated from refining constraints of this form may affect
only outputs of constraints that are predecessors in the inclusion graph. Since
these predecessors are already processed, they have only a single input variable,
and so their processing affects only their predecessors, etc. Therefore reinsertion
can only traverse the acyclic graph backwards, and every backward traversal is
finite. Combining this with the finite forward pass yields termination of every
branch of the search tree, and thus termination of the whole procedure.

structure of the transducer constraints occurring in the benchmark) and explore
reachable configurations of T where we track the possible content of both tapes.
If for some configuration the tapes are not prefixes of the other, we can stop the
expansion of this node as we surely cannot reach a configuration with the same
string on both tapes. If for some configuration the length of some tape exceeds ℓ,
we report unknown. If we reach an accepting configuration with (w, w) on tapes,
the constraint is sat. Otherwise, we report unsat.

Conclusion
We extended the stabilization-based automata approach to the chain-free frag-
ment with transducer constraints, efficiently integrating them with length rea-
soning while avoiding variable splitting and monadic decomposition. We also
introduced several heuristics that proved highly effective in practice, and our pro-
cedure extends the framework with support for negations, allowing unrestricted
disequalities and negated functional transducer constraints. Implemented on top
of Z3-Noodler, our method significantly outperforms existing solvers on bench-
marks with relational constraints, often by orders of magnitude.

Theorem 2. A stable cube with satisfiable regular constraints is satisfiable.
Proof (sketch). Let φ be a stable and directly chain-free cube with all languages

Lemma 3. TrToCF(φ) is a disjunction of almost concatenation-free cubes, and
it is an existential extension of φ.
Proof (sketch).
Let φ = Reg ∧T(s, t) be the input formula. By construction,
TrToCF(φ) gives a solution iff φ has a solution. That is, T+, after delimiter sym-

noodles, a union of noodles is equivalent to T+ (no solution is lost, and no new
solution is added). Finally, the transformation of a noodle N into a conjunc-

side of the original constraint, (iii) concatenating all the left sides of the output
binding equations gives precisely the output side of the original constraint, and
(iv) substituting the input and output concatenated variables in the original
constraint by the right and left sides of the input and output binding equations,
respectively, gives precisely the original constraint T(s, t). We at most restrict
the existing languages of the variables of φ by eliminating words that do not
satisfy the constraint T(s, t). Indeed, every solution of TrToCF(φ) assigns values
to all the variables of φ, which at the same time satisfy φ. We can clearly see
that TrToCF(φ) preserves solutions of φ and adds no new solution, and that
TrToCF(φ) contains fresh variables. If all fresh variables are existentially quanti-
fied, we get precisely an existential extension of φ. Moreover, TrToCF(φ) is clearly
in the almost concatenation-free form, as for each noodle, all inclusions holds,
each transducer constraint in Tri is concatenation-free transducer constraint by
construction, and each input/output binding equation in ini and outi satisfies
the definition for almost concatenation-free form.
