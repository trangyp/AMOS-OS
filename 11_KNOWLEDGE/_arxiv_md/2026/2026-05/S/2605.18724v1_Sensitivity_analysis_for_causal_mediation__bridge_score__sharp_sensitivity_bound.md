Abstract
Causal mediation analysis decomposes the total treatment effect into a portion
operating through a hypothesized mediator and a residual direct portion. Identi-
fication of natural direct and indirect effects typically rests on the mediator stage
of sequential ignorability, which cannot be empirically verified and requires explicit
sensitivity analysis. We introduce the bridge score, a low-dimensional vector formed
from the two treatment-specific mediator densities at a common mediator value, and
show that it is a balancing score for the mediator stage of sequential ignorability.
Conditional on the bridge score, we then derive a sharp pointwise envelope on the
unidentified mediator-outcome confounding function in terms of two interpretable la-
tent confounding parameters. To make the bound operational for sensitivity analysis,
we further introduce two calibration approaches. The first is benchmark calibration
against an observed covariate, including a rank-based version that is invariant to
monotone re-expressions of the benchmark; the second is residual budget calibration
based on residual outcome variation. Finally, we show how the pointwise bound can
be operationalized for inference through a scalar functional reduction and a Bayesian
g-computation algorithm that propagates all sources of uncertainty into posterior
draws of the mediation effect estimates.
Keywords: causal mediation analysis; sensitivity analysis; Bayesian g-computation; bridge
score; sharp bound; benchmark calibration

Introduction
Causal mediation analysis decomposes the average (total) treatment effect into the portion
that operates through a hypothesized mediator (referred to as the natural indirect effect)
and the portion that works around the mediator (referred to as the natural direct effect).
Identification of the natural direct and indirect effects typically rests on the sequential ig-
norability assumption (Imai et al., 2010). That is, treatment assignment must be ignorable,
and the mediator must be ignorable given treatment and baseline covariates. The second
condition is rarely supported by design, and any unmeasured factor jointly driving the
mediator and the outcome leads to its violation. Hence, there remains substantial inter-
est in pursuing a calibrated sensitivity analysis for the no mediator-outcome confounding
assumption to strengthen the credibility of causal mediation analysis.
An emerging literature has developed sensitivity analysis for violations of sequential
ignorability.
Imai et al. (2010) introduced a parametric ρ-based parameter tied to the
residual correlation between mediator and outcome errors; Tchetgen and Shpitser (2012)
developed semiparametric, multiply robust identification with a sensitivity layer through a
user-specified additive confounding function; VanderWeele (2010) developed bias formulas
and effect decompositions, complemented by the sensitivity analysis (VanderWeele et al.,
2014) and bivariate normal latent confounder analyses (le Cessie, 2016; Lindmark et al.,
2018). Ding and Vanderweele (2016), as well as follow-up work (e.g., Sj¨olander and Waern-
baum, 2024), established risk ratio sharp bounds under interpretable parameters for the
strength of confounding; Bayesian treatments include McCandless et al. (2017); Daniels
et al. (2023), which emphasize prior factorization into likelihood and sensitivity.
Despite this progress, three statistical challenges remain for credible, nonparametric
sensitivity analysis of mediator ignorability on the additive effect measure scale, which is
arguably the most commonly used scale in scientific research. First, existing parameteriza-
tions face a tension between interpretability and localization. Scalar sensitivity parameters
are easy to report, but they collapse violations that may vary with the treatment arm,
mediator value, and covariate profile; fully localized sensitivity functions are more faithful
to the structure of mediator-outcome confounding, but are difficult to elicit, estimate, and
calibrate when the covariate is moderately high-dimensional. What has not been fully de-
veloped is a general low-dimensional reduction of the sensitivity object that preserves the
mediator information relevant to the cross-world natural effect functional without requiring
a full covariate specification. Second, although sharp nonparametric bounds on mediator-
outcome confounding under interpretable parameters for the strength of confounding exist
on the risk ratio scale and are most natural for binary outcomes (Ding and Vanderweele,
2016; Sj¨olander and Waernbaum, 2024), no analogous sharp additive (mean difference)
envelope is available. Additive sensitivity in the existing literature is handled either by
parametric bias formulas that require a specific latent confounding structure (VanderWeele,

2010; le Cessie, 2016; Lindmark et al., 2018) or by Manski-style bounds that use no sensi-
tivity parameters at all and are likely too wide and hence uninformative (Sj¨olander, 2009).
Third, operational benchmark calibration for sensitivity analysis on mediator ignorability
is fragile. Continuous benchmarks calibrated on their raw scale make the implied envelope
depend on the analyst’s units or support width, while the E-value style scale coherent sum-
maries (VanderWeele and Ding, 2017) collapse the full envelope into a single robustness
scalar and are generally more natural for binary outcomes.
We address these challenges through several contributions. First, we propose the con-
cept of the bridge score, which is a two-dimensional vector formed from both treatment-
specific mediator densities at a common mediator value. It places the identified outcome
regression and the unidentified mediator-outcome confounding correction on the same low-
dimensional scale, while encoding the arm asymmetry in the estimand. In contrast to the
density-based methods (Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2013; Hong et al., 2018), which collapse the
two arm-specific densities into a single inverse weight for natural effect estimation, the
bridge score retains them as a vector and uses them as a balancing score on which the
unidentified sensitivity function is defined. Second, we derive a sharp pointwise additive
envelope for the bridge score sensitivity function, parametrized by a residual selection ra-
tio and an outcome range parameter. The result is the additive scale counterpart of the
multiplicative risk ratio bound of Ding and Vanderweele (2016), providing, for any out-
come type, the sharp sensitivity envelope that the risk ratio bound provides for binary
outcomes. Third, we provide a benchmark-based calibration against an observed compara-
tor covariate, with a rank-based procedure that is invariant to monotone re-expressions of
the benchmark. As a complement, we also provide an alternative residual budget calibra-
tion based on bridge score residual outcome variation. Finally, we operationalize inference
through a scalar functional reduction and a Bayesian g-computation implementation that
propagates uncertainty in the bridge score, calibration quantities and sensitivity corrections
into posterior draws of natural direct and indirect effect estimates.
1.1
Positioning relative to existing sensitivity analyses
Among the prior work surveyed above, two families are widely used in applied mediation
sensitivity analysis and warrant explicit contrast with our framework. One is the paramet-
ric analysis based on ρ proposed by Imai et al. (2010); the other is the risk ratio sharp
bound family of Ding and Vanderweele (2016); Sj¨olander and Waernbaum (2024), together
with its scalar E-value reduction (Smith and VanderWeele, 2019). We position the bridge
score framework as a complementary tool rather than a replacement. The ρ-based analysis
of Imai et al. (2010) operates on the additive scale but requires fully specified paramet-
ric models for both the outcome and the mediator, a restrictive assumption that may fail
under model misspecification and that constrains the form of mediator-outcome confound-

ing the analyst can encode. Our bound is nonparametric. It imposes no functional-form
restriction on the outcome regression or the mediator law, and the sensitivity parameters
(ηa, γa) are interpretable features of an arbitrary latent confounder rather than residual-
correlation parameters in a working parametric model. The risk ratio bound of Smith and
VanderWeele (2019) is the natural sharp envelope on the multiplicative scale, especially for
binary outcomes, and we supply the missing additive counterpart for any outcome type,
equipped with a dimension-reduction property and a scale-coherent calibration that the
multiplicative framework cannot naturally accommodate. Given such considerations, our
framework has the following three differentiating features:
1. Scale. The sharp bound of Ding and Vanderweele (2016) operates on the risk ratio
scale and is most natural for binary outcomes, where the risk ratio is the canonical
effect measure; applying it to an outcome requires either dichotomization or a log-
linear approximation. Theorem 2 supplies an additive analogue on the mean difference
scale, so for any outcome type, the analyst no longer has to choose between scale
coherence and access to a sharp envelope.
2. Dimension reduction. Existing sensitivity analyses do not use the mediator-density
bridge score as the localization scale; in common implementations their sensitivity
parameters are global or attached to the full covariate/model scale. Proposition 2
shows that, for the additive bound, calibration on the bridge score can deliver a nu-
merically tighter envelope. The residual selection parameter at the bridge score level
is no larger than its worst-case full covariate counterpart within the corresponding
stratum, with an analogous outcome range comparison under the additional condition
stated in the proposition. The bridge score reduction is therefore not only conceptu-
ally cleaner but remains informative quantitatively, and it is what makes the additive
bound practical when the baseline covariate vector is moderately high-dimensional.
3. Scale coherent calibration. Operational calibration is where benchmark-based meth-
ods are most often criticized. Comparing latent confounding to a continuous ob-
served covariate makes the resulting envelope depend on the analyst’s choice of units
or transformation, and E-value style summaries trade scale dependence for a single
robustness scalar at the cost of the two-parameter structure of the underlying bound.
In comparison, the rank-based version of our benchmark calibration (Section 4.1) is
invariant to monotone re-expressions of the benchmark and stable under heavy-tailed
benchmarks, while preserving the separately interpretable ηa and γa inputs that the
E-value collapses. Among sensitivity analyses formulated on an additive scale, neither
Imai et al. (2010) nor the weighting-based approach of Hong et al. (2018) provides
scale-coherent benchmark calibration of this form.

4
Calibration of the sharp bound
The parameters in Theorem 2 are interpretable features of latent confounders but uniden-
tifiable.
We introduce two complementary calibration procedures for implementing the
proposed sensitivity analysis framework. The benchmark approach calibrates the latent
confounder against an observed covariate W. Specifically, auxiliary outcome regressions
and conditional laws of W provide observed analogues of the outcome range and residual
selection parameters. This makes the sensitivity inputs comparable to a known covariate
while preserving the two ingredients of the sharp bound. Another calibration approach,
residual budget calibration, instead scales ηa by bridge score residual outcome variation
and varies γa over a substantive grid for sensitivity analysis.

Discussion
This paper develops a new sensitivity analysis framework for mediator-outcome ignorabil-
ity that operates natively on the additive scale. The central object is the bridge score, a
two-dimensional vector formed from both treatment-specific mediator densities at a com-
mon mediator value, which serves simultaneously as a balancing score for the mediator
and as the localization scale on which the unidentified confounding correction is defined.
Building on this score, we derive a sharp additive envelope for the confounding function,
parametrized by an interpretable residual selection ratio and an outcome range parameter,
and show that calibration at the bridge score level is provably no looser than calibration
at the full covariate level. Two complementary calibration routes, benchmark-based and
residual budget, then translate these parameters into quantities an analyst can reason
about substantively. We then illustrate the operationalization of this framework through a
Bayesian g-computation algorithm that propagates all sources of uncertainty into posterior
draws of the natural direct and indirect effects. We formulate the framework under the
treatment randomization assumption, {Y (a, m), M(a′), X} ⊥⊥A, to emphasize the role
of the bridge score both as a balancing device for mediator ignorability and as the scale
on which mediator-outcome sensitivity is indexed. The same logic could be adapted to
the settings under conditional treatment exchangeability, {Y (a, m), M(a′)} ⊥⊥A | X, by
18


## Page 19


augmenting the bridge score with a balancing score for treatment assignment, such as the
propensity score. We leave this extension implicit to keep the present framework centered
on mediator-outcome sensitivity.
The framework is tailored to the cross-world natural effect estimand, where the asym-
metry between the two treatment arms is the defining structural feature, but the same
logic opens several natural directions for extension. First, causal pathways can operate
through multiple mediators simultaneously, whether structured into a causal sequence or
acting in parallel without a prespecified ordering (VanderWeele and Vansteelandt, 2013).
Extending the bridge score to this setting raises questions about whether a single joint
score can preserve the arm asymmetry that the natural effect functional depends on, or
whether the score must be defined separately for each mediator. Second, we have primarily
focused on independent observations, but in other applied settings (e.g., cluster-randomized
experiments) where causal mediation analysis is of interest, outcomes and mediators are
measured for units within clusters, and the bridge score sensitivity framework would need
to account for within-cluster dependence in both the mediator law and the confounding
correction (Cheng and Li, 2026). Furthermore, Ohnishi and Li (2025) have recently de-
veloped a Bayesian nonparametric approach for studying the natural indirect effect and
the spillover mediation effects in cluster-randomized experiments with multiple unstruc-
tured mediators, and it would be of interest to generalize the current framework to similar
cluster-correlated data settings.
