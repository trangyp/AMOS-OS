SHARP THRESHOLD FOR HAMILTON CYCLES IN RANDOMLY
PERTURBED SPARSE GRAPHS
GUORUI MA AND ZHIFEI YAN
Abstract. We determine the sharp threshold for Hamilton cycles in randomly perturbed sparse
graphs. For any α = α(n) = o(1), let Gα be an n-vertex graph with minimum degree δ(Gα) ⩾αn.
We prove that if
p ⩾(1 + ε)log(1/α)
n
,
then the union Gα∪G(n, p) is Hamiltonian asymptotically almost surely. This significantly strength-
ens a recent result of Hahn-Klimroth, Maesaka, Mogge, Mohr, and Parczyk by improving the leading
constant from 6 to the optimal value of 1. Crucially, we show that this bound on p is best possi-
ble when αn →∞, thereby establishing the exact probability threshold for Hamiltonicity in this
sparse regime. Our proof relies on a robust random expansion lemma, P´osa’s booster lemma, and
a sprinkling argument.
1. Introduction
A central theme in extremal graph theory is finding sufficient conditions for a graph to contain
specific spanning structures, such as perfect matchings or Hamilton cycles.
A classic result of
this type is Dirac’s theorem, which states that any n-vertex graph with minimum degree at least
n/2 contains a Hamilton cycle. On the other end of the spectrum, in the purely random setting,
a celebrated result of Korshunov, and independently P´osa [P´76], established that the binomial
random graph G(n, p) asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.) contains a Hamilton cycle when
p = log n + log log n + ω(1)
n
.
To bridge the gap between purely deterministic and purely random settings, Bohman, Frieze,
and Martin [BFM03] introduced the model of randomly perturbed graphs. In this framework, one
starts with a dense deterministic graph Gα satisfying a minimum degree condition δ(Gα) ⩾αn for
some α > 0, and adds random edges from G(n, p). Already in their seminal work, Bohman, Frieze,
and Martin demonstrated that when α > 0 is a constant, adding merely a linear number of random
edges is sufficient: Gα ∪G(n, p) is Hamiltonian a.a.s. provided
p ⩾C/n
for some constant C depending only on α. This O(1/n) probability threshold is a drastic reduction
from the O(log n/n) required in the purely random setting, powerfully illustrating how a small
random perturbation can fundamentally alter the threshold for the appearance of global structures.
While the dense regime is now well understood, determining the sharp threshold for Hamil-
ton cycles in sparse perturbed graphs, specifically when the density parameter α = o(1), has
remained a compelling open problem. Recently, Hahn-Klimroth, Maesaka, Mogge, Mohr, and Par-
czyk [HKMM+21] made significant progress in this exact setting. They proved that for an n-vertex
graph Gα with δ(Gα) ⩾αn, the perturbed graph Gα ∪G(n, p) contains a Hamilton cycle a.a.s.

By

point at which random perturbations overcome the deterministic bottleneck.
Beyond Hamilton cycles, the study of randomly perturbed graphs has evolved into a highly
active research program, expanding to a wide variety of spanning and extremal properties. For
instance, the threshold for embedding bounded-degree spanning trees was investigated by Kriv-
elevich, Kwan, and Sudakov [KKS17], and fully resolved by Joos and Kim [JK20].
This was
subsequently generalized to all spanning bounded-degree graphs by B¨ottcher, Montgomery, Par-
czyk, and Person [BMPP20]. There has also been extensive work on F-factors (tilings); notably,
Balogh, Treglown, and Wagner [BTW19] and Han, Morris, and Treglown [HMT21] explored the gap
between the Hajnal-Szemer´edi theorem and the Johansson-Kahn-Vu theorem. Very recently, this
line of research has culminated in exact threshold results: Cheng, Liu, Wang, and Yan [CLWY26]
determined the sharp threshold for near-perfect triangle packings, and Antoniuk, Kamˇcev, Reiher,
and Tukara [AKRT26] established the complete picture for clique factors. The model has also been
fruitfully applied to Ramsey properties, as demonstrated by Das and Treglown [DT20].
Proof Outline and Organization. The proof relies on splitting the random edges into two
rounds. In Section 2, we introduce the standard tools of P´osa boosters and a sprinkling lemma. In
Section 3, we prove a uniform random expansion lemma (Lemma 3.1) for G(n, p). In Section 4, we
expose the first round of random edges and use the expansion lemma to show that the resulting
union with Gα forms a robust, connected 2-expander. This guarantees a large reservoir of P´osa
boosters. We then expose a second, much smaller round of random edges (sprinkling) to hit these
boosters and close a Hamilton cycle. Finally, in Section 5, we provide the sharpness construction.

2. Two standard tools
In this section, we record two standard structural tools based on P´osa’s elementary rotation
technique. First we introduce a classical P´osa boosters lemma as follows.
Lemma 2.1 (P´osa boosters). Let G be a connected non-Hamiltonian graph on n vertices. Suppose
that for some integer k,
|NG(X) \ X| ⩾2|X|
for every X ⊆V (G), |X| ⩽k.
Then G has at least (k+1)2/2 boosters, where a booster is a non-edge e such that G+e is Hamiltonian
or has a path longer than a longest path of G.
Proof. This is the usual P´osa rotation argument. Let P = v0v1 . . . vl be a longest path of G. For a
fixed endpoint v0, let R(v0) be the set of endpoints obtainable from P by a sequence of rotations
that keep v0 fixed. P´osa’s elementary observation gives
|NG(R(v0)) \ R(v0)| ⩽2|R(v0)| −1.
If |R(v0)| ⩽k, this contradicts the assumed expansion. Hence |R(v0)| ⩾k + 1. For each x ∈R(v0),
repeat the same argument with x fixed. We obtain a set R(x) of at least k + 1 possible opposite
endpoints. For every y ∈R(x), the edge xy, if absent, is a booster: adding xy closes a cycle through
all vertices of the corresponding longest path, and since G is connected and non-Hamiltonian, this
either is a Hamilton cycle or can be extended to a longer path. Counting the resulting unordered
pairs gives at least (k + 1)2/2 boosters.
□

graph after the first i exposed edges have been added to G. As long as Gi is not Hamiltonian, it is
still connected and still satisfies the same expansion condition.
By Lemma 2.1, with k = ⌊n/4⌋, it has at least
(k + 1)2

it is at least n a.a.s. Each successful booster either creates a Hamilton cycle or increases the length

Hamiltonian a.a.s.

5. Sharpness of the constant
The leading constant 1 cannot be improved in the main range where the deterministic minimum
degree is genuinely growing, for instance when αn →∞.
Proposition 5.1 (Bipartite obstruction). Assume α = o(1) and αn →∞. For every fixed η > 0,
there is an n-vertex graph Gα with δ(Gα) ⩾αn such that, if
p = (1 −η)log(1/α)
n
,
then Gα ∪G(n, p) is not Hamiltonian a.a.s.
Proof. Let A, B be a partition of the vertex set with |A| = ⌈αn⌉and |B| = n −|A|, and let
Gα = KA,B. Then δ(Gα) ⩾αn. Let R ∼G(n, p), and let Y be the number of vertices of B that
have no R-neighbour inside B. Then
EY = |B|(1 −p)|B|−1 = (1 + o(1))nα(1−η)(1−o(1)).
The assumption nα →∞implies EY →∞, and a standard second-moment calculation for isolated
vertices in a binomial random graph gives Y = (1 + o(1))EY a.a.s. Moreover, since α = o(1),
EY
|A| = α−η+o(1) →∞.
Hence Y > |A| a.a.s.
Every vertex counted by Y has no edge to B in Gα ∪R; in any Hamilton cycle both of its two
cycle-neighbours would therefore have to lie in A. Since the vertices of A contribute only 2|A|
incident cycle-edges in total, at most |A| such vertices can be accommodated. This contradicts
Y > |A|. Therefore Gα ∪R is not Hamiltonian a.a.s.

Remark 5.2. If αn is bounded, the usual random-graph threshold around (log n)/n may dominate
the lower-bound example above. The theorem remains true because the case λ ⩾log n + 2 log log n
is already handled by the random graph alone.
