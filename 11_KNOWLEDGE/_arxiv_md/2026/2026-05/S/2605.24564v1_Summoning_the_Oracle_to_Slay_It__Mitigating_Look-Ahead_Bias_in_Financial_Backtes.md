Abstract
Backtesting large language models (LLMs) on
historical financial data is unreliable because
pre-training cuts off after the events happened.
An LLM trained in 2024 already “knows”
which way 2018–2020 stocks moved. We name
this failure parametric look-ahead bias and pro-
pose FinCAD, an inference-time adaptation of
Context-Aware Decoding that suppresses an
LLM’s memory of historical outcomes with-
out retraining. FinCAD pairs an adversarial
bias-discovery pipeline that learns a model-
specific memory-activating prior prompt with
an entity- and date-adaptive rule that scales
the CAD strength to per-(entity, date) memo-
risation, so the penalty fires on memorised in-
sample dates and decays to zero out-of-sample.
Across five 7–14B LLMs and five mega-cap eq-
uities, FinCAD cuts in-sample backtest returns
by up to −67.1% on memorised dates while
leaving 2025 out-of-sample returns within $8K
and Sharpe within ±0.10 of baseline, and pre-
serves general-purpose reasoning within ±1.7
pts. On an eleven-model leaderboard, it raises
the in-sample/out-of-sample Spearman corre-
lation from +0.779 to +0.846 (p<10−19), re-
covering rankings that genuinely predict out-
of-sample performance.

Introduction
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly
applied as autonomous financial agents that read
news, filings, and prices and decide whether to buy,
sell, or hold (Saha et al., 2025). In quantitative fi-
nance, backtests gate capital allocation, so a result
inflated by undetected bias translates directly into
capital misallocation and live-trading losses; stan-
dardised corrections exist for backtest overfitting
and multiple testing (Harvey and Liu, 2014; Bai-
ley et al., 2014; Bailey and López de Prado, 2014;
López de Prado, 2018), survivorship truncation
(Brown et al., 1992; Heckman, 1979), and point-
in-time data leakage (Li et al., 2025; Kong et al.,
2026). However, limited work has studied the bias
introduced by LLMs. A state-of-the-art LLM with
a 2025 cutoff has already seen how NVIDIA, Mi-
crosoft, and Netflix moved during 2010–2020, so
any backtest overlapping the training window inher-
its an additional bias that lives inside the model’s
weights and is invisible to data-pipeline audits. We
call this failure mode parametric look-ahead bias,
and its consequence is a trap: uncorrected long-
horizon backtests are systematically misleading,
whereas the only bias-free alternative, the short
post-cutoff period, is too limited to support statisti-
cally reliable conclusions (Li et al., 2025).
A natural starting point is Context-Aware De-
coding (CAD) (Shi et al., 2024), which sharpens
the contribution of the input by subtracting an off-
context prior at the logit level. CAD was originally
proposed to suppress unfaithful priors (hallucina-
tions); ours is the opposite regime, where the prior
the model carries about “NVDA in 2018” is not
a hallucination but an accurate memory of the fu-
ture that backtesting must explicitly reject. Two
challenges follow: xprior must reliably activate that
memory rather than answer the question, and α
must vary across entities and dates since memo-
risation is non-uniform on both axes, so a fixed
α would simultaneously under-correct memorised
cases and over-penalise context-grounded reason-
ing on out-of-sample dates.
We propose FinCAD, an inference-time de-
coding scheme that suppresses parametric look-
ahead bias without retraining: it first summons
the parametric oracle as an explicit prior, then
slays it by subtracting that prior from the context-
conditioned logits only where the memory is de-
tectable. An adversarial bias-discovery pipeline
performs the summoning: we first optimise a
model-specific instruction T ∗
prior that maximally ac-
tivates the model’s memorised future knowledge
on a held-out probe set, yielding an explicit sub-
tractable prior. An entity- and date-adaptive rule

Related Work
A growing line of work casts LLMs as autonomous
financial investors (Guo et al., 2024; Saha et al.,
2025), including a recent wave of dedicated trad-
ing agents (Yu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024;
Yang et al., 2024; Ding et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024;
Wang and Ma, 2024; Xiao et al., 2025; Li and Ma,
2025; Fatouros et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026) and
reinforcement-learning-enhanced variants (Ding
et al., 2023; Koa et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2025;
Deng et al., 2026), most of which evaluate on short
historical windows without bias control, leaving
open whether reported gains reflect genuine reason-
ing or contamination of the evaluation regime.
Input-side
protections
(survivorship,
data-
snooping, data-leakage look-ahead) are tracked by
FINSABER (Li et al., 2025), but none addresses
the bias that resides in the model’s weights them-
selves. This parametric look-ahead is documented
by Benhenda (2026), who show that LLMs recall
in-cutoff S&P 500 prices verbatim, and by Gao
et al. (2025), who provide a complementary statis-
tical test; concurrently, Merchant and Levy (2026)
proposes a decoding-time mitigation that fine-tunes
a pair of auxiliary models on labelled forget/re-
tain partitions, whereas FinCAD uses a single base
model with no auxiliary fine-tuning.
Our work connects to the broader literature on
LLM memorisation (Carlini et al., 2023a,b) and
machine unlearning for LLMs (Liu et al., 2024),
including parameter-editing methods (Meng et al.,
2022, 2023) and fine-tuning-based forget proto-
cols (Jang et al., 2023; Maini et al., 2024; Eldan
and Russinovich, 2023). All require curated for-
get sets, retraining cycles, or structured (subject,
relation, object) edits, and risk collateral damage
to neighbouring knowledge; they are operationally
impractical at the eleven-model leaderboard scale
of financial backtests, where each model would
need a bespoke unlearning pass per ticker per eval-
uation date. Decoding-side interventions instead
operate at inference without modifying weights (Li
et al., 2023; Chuang et al., 2024), and FinCAD
belongs to this family. We build specifically on
Context-Aware Decoding (Shi et al., 2024), whose
contrast between with-context and without-context
branches matches our setting: a trading agent’s
memory of how a stock actually moved should not
leak into its reading of the financial context pro-
vided at decision time.

3.2
Entity-Adaptive Penalty
A fixed penalty α applies the same subtraction
strength to every entity and time period.
This
is problematic for two reasons. First, parametric
look-ahead bias is non-uniform across entities: the
model has strong parametric recall for well-known
stocks (e.g., AAPL, NVDA) but near-random pre-
dictions for obscure or recently listed companies.
Applying a high α to the latter subtracts noise
from the context-conditioned logits, degrading out-
put quality. Second, memorisation is non-uniform
across time: the model has detailed knowledge
ofhistorical periods covered by its training data but
no memorised prices for dates beyond its knowl-
edge cutoff. A date-agnostic probe would assign
the same high α to a well-known entity regardless
of whether the backtest date falls inside or outside
the training window, penalising the prior even when
it carries no look-ahead information. We therefore
set α dynamically per entity and date, based on
how confident the model is in its own memory.

Experiments and Results
Our evaluation is structured around a central con-
cern: does FinCAD remove look-ahead bias with-
out damaging the model’s reasoning ability? A
naïve penalty that suppresses all parametric knowl-
edge would trivially reduce backtest returns, but it
would also cripple the model on tasks that require
genuine reasoning. The three experiments below
are sequential preconditions. Experiment 1 (§4.1,
reasoning preservation) verifies that applying each
model’s own backtest-calibrated ¯αIS to standard
reasoning benchmarks leaves accuracy intact, rul-
ing out destructive suppression as an explanation
for any subsequent return drop. Experiment 2
(§4.2, honesty drop) tests selectivity: the same
calibrated penalty should reduce returns on mem-
orised in-sample dates yet remain inert on post-
cutoff dates where no parametric memory exists,
the asymmetric pattern that distinguishes debiasing
from indiscriminate suppression. Experiment 3
(§4.3, ranking alignment) tests downstream utility:
under FinCAD, the in-sample LLM ranking should
agree with the strict out-of-sample ranking more
closely than the baseline does, restoring backtest-

4.4
Takeaway
The three experiments form a logical chain: rea-
soning is preserved (§4.1), the same calibrated
penalty is selective in-sample and inert out-of-
sample (§4.2), and as a consequence the in-sample
leaderboard becomes a more honest predictor of
the out-of-sample one (§4.3). We view FinCAD
as a contamination control conceptually parallel to
the deflated Sharpe (Bailey and López de Prado,
2014) and survivorship corrections (Brown et al.,
1992; Heckman, 1979) that the wider field has al-
ready standardised, providing a tool that lets future
evaluations re-run existing agent pipelines with the
parametric component removed and restores com-
parability across architectures and time windows.
5
Conclusion
To mitigate parametric look-ahead bias, we pro-
posed FinCAD, an inference-time adaptation of
Context-Aware Decoding that suppresses this bias
without retraining, by combining an adversarial
bias-discovery pipeline that learns a model-specific
prior prompt with an entity- and date-adaptive rule
for the CAD strength α.
Across multiple 7–14B LLMs, FinCAD sub-
stantially reduces in-sample returns on memorised
mega-caps, remains effectively identical to base-
line on strict 2025 out-of-sample dates, and pre-
serves general-reasoning accuracy. At the leader-
board alignment, the Spearman correlation be-
tween in-sample and out-of-sample Sharpe rank-
ings rises from +0.779 to +0.846 (p<10−19),
restoring backtesting’s intended role as a predictor
of out-of-sample performance.
More broadly, FinCAD is a complementary inter-
vention that integrates with existing trading-agent
evaluation pipelines, working alongside input-side
protections to restore the interpretability of in-
sample comparisons whenever training-cutoff over-
lap is unavoidable. FinCAD’s operational template
is not specific to finance: the same construction ap-
plies wherever a backtest or counterfactual study re-
quires the model to forget post-cutoff outcomes for
named entities at known dates, including clinical
decision-making evaluated against pre-publication
evidence, legal case-outcome prediction held out
of training, and so on. Extending the calibrator
to these settings, and characterising the entity and
time axes along which parametric memorisation
accumulates in each domain, is a natural research
direction opened by this work.

Limitations
First, FinCAD only partially mitigates paramet-
ric look-ahead bias: the calibrated logit subtrac-
tion reduces the contribution of memorised future
knowledge but does not guarantee its complete re-
moval. Residual memorisation may persist when-
ever the prior prompt fails to fully activate the
model’s parametric recall, or whenever memorisa-
tion is encoded in pathways that the chosen probe
does not detect. Treating the in-sample drop un-
der FinCAD as a lower bound on the true level
of look-ahead contamination is therefore the safer
reading of our results. Second, our evaluation is re-
stricted to instruction-tuned LLMs in the 7B–14B
parameter range. Whether the same calibration
mechanism transfers cleanly to substantially larger
models (e.g., 70B+ or frontier closed-source mod-
els) remains open. We could not run such a study at
the scale required to draw a reliable conclusion: the
eleven-model cross-validation in this paper already
required several thousand GPU-hours, and verify-
ing the same protocol on multiple frontier-scale
models was beyond the computational resources
available to us. We flag this as the most impor-
tant direction for follow-up work. Third, our back-
tests are restricted to US equities (mega-, mid-, and
small-cap names and SPY); whether the same cali-
bration mechanism transfers to non-US equity mar-
kets, fixed-income, FX, or cryptocurrency regimes
is an empirical question we did not test. The entity-
adaptive design is per-ticker by construction, so
we expect it to accommodate any market whose
directional memory can be probed with the same
completion template, but the magnitude of the bias
and the appropriate α-range may differ from what
we observe on US large-caps.
Despite these limitations, FinCAD provides the
first operational handle on parametric look-ahead
bias for LLM-based backtesting. The bias itself has
been documented qualitatively (Benhenda, 2026;
Gao et al., 2025) but lacks an agreed-upon ground
truth or a clean decomposition between memorised
and reasoned components, so a precise estimate of
its contribution to any specific backtest is unavail-
able today; under that uncertainty, the lower-bound
reading of our in-sample drop is itself a contribu-
tion to evaluation practice, since it gives the field a
concrete, model-by-model figure where there was
previously none.

A
Backtesting Framework
This appendix documents the single-stock back-
testing framework used in Experiments 2–4. The
choices below follow standard quantitative-finance
practice and are made deliberately to ensure that
any difference between baseline and FinCAD per-
formance reflects look-ahead-bias removal rather
than simulation artefacts.
Agent and signal.
At each trading date the LLM
acts as a quantitative analyst for a single ticker.
It receives a structured financial summary built
from price data only, with no news, filings, or
macro feeds: the latest closing price, trailing re-
turns over multiple windows (1m, 3m, 6m, 1y),
simple and exponential moving averages, annu-
alised realised volatility, the 52-week high/low and
the current price’s position within that range, the
count of positive days over the last 21 trading ses-
sions, and (when available) the 21-day average dol-
lar volume. Given this summary, the agent must
return a JSON object with three fields: action ∈
{buy, sell, hold}, quantity (integer share count),
and confidence (integer in [0, 100]). Outputs are
parsed with a lightweight tolerant JSON extrac-
tor; on parse failure the procedure retries with a
reduced α.
Execution timing (no intraday look-ahead).
The agent at rebalance date t sees only data with
11


## Page 12


timestamp strictly less than t (i.e., up to the previ-
ous day’s close). Trades are then executed at the
next trading day’s adjusted opening price, never the
same day’s close that produced the signal. Daily
portfolio values are marked-to-market using ad-
justed close. This ordering ensures the agent never
observes the price at which it trades, which is the
standard guard against intraday look-ahead.
Initial capital and portfolio.
Each backtest
starts with $100,000 of cash and zero shares. The
portfolio is long-only and consists of cash plus a
position in a single ticker; short selling, leverage,
and options are disabled. Cash earns no interest
in the simulation (the risk-free rate enters only via
Sharpe/Sortino computation, not via cash accrual).
Commission model.
Transaction costs are mod-
elled as 10 basis points (bps) of trade notional,
charged one-way on both buys and sells. This is a
central estimate for large- and mid-cap US equities
covering broker commission, half-spread, and mar-
ket impact (Novy-Marx and Velikov, 2016). The
model is deliberately notional-based rather than per-
share: split-adjusted prices can be orders of mag-
nitude smaller than nominal prices, so a per-share
fee would inflate effective commissions on heav-
ily split tickers. A notional fee is split-invariant.
Commission is deducted mechanically from the
portfolio cash and is not mentioned in the LLM
prompt, to avoid introducing asymmetric context
between the context and the CAD prior. Insuffi-
cient cash is handled by reducing the requested
share count until the order plus commission fits the
available cash.
Liquidity cap.
To prevent the agent from plac-
ing economically unrealistic orders, every order is
capped at 1% of the trailing 20-day average daily
volume (ADV) on the execution date. Orders re-
questing more than this cap are clipped to the cap;
the remainder is dropped (no carry-over). The cap
is disabled if volume data is unavailable for the
ticker.
Performance metrics.
We report total return,
CAGR, annualised volatility, Sharpe ratio, Sortino
ratio, maximum drawdown, and ending portfolio
value, computed from the daily mark-to-market se-
ries with 252 trading days per year. The risk-free
rate is fixed at 3% annualised (a long-run US T-
bill average over our evaluation window), used in
both Sharpe and Sortino. Each backtest is bench-
marked against a buy-and-hold reference: an all-in
purchase at the first execution open, held to the
final close, with the same commission charged on
the initial purchase.
Decoding configuration.
All backtesting experi-
ments use temperature T=1.0 with a fixed random
seed for reproducibility. We initially tried greedy
decoding (T=0) and found that it masks CAD’s ef-
fect: the logit subtraction shifts the full distribution
but rarely flips the argmax token, so a large fraction
of trading decisions remain identical between base-
line and FinCAD. At T=1.0 the distribution shift
propagates into sampled tokens, so CAD can mean-
ingfully alter the agent’s outputs. Experiment 1
(general-reasoning benchmarks) instead uses T=0
to match standard evaluation protocols on those
benchmarks.
