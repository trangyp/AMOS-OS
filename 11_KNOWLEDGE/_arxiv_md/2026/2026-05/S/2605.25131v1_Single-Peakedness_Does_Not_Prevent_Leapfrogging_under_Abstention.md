Single-Peakedness Does Not Prevent Leapfrogging under Abstention
Aman Ray
Madras School of Economics
ba24aman@mse.ac.in

Abstract
Parties in spatial competition rarely choose platforms that reverse their ideological order.
Mutual leapfrogging is the strongest form of reversal: each party locates beyond the other party’s
ideal point. In voting models without abstention single-peakedness rules out such reversals. We
show that this conclusion does not survive endogenous abstention. There is a spatial voting
model in which voter and party preferences are single-peaked, yet mutual leapfrogging occurs
in pure-strategy equilibrium. All voters participate at the equilibrium profile. The equilibrium
survives because some deviations change which voters participate. We prove that such equilibria
are impossible under a sufficient ordinal condition: parties agree on how to rank leftward and
rightward deviations from their ideal points. The condition is general enough to cover symmetric
single-peaked utilities and common translated utility shapes.

Introduction
Why do parties rarely reverse their ideological order and leapfrog one another?
Adams [2001]
finds that parties rarely leapfrog one another and that their policy movements are constrained by
ideology. Since Wittman [1983], models of ideological candidates have typically assume a concave
utility function for ideological preferences. These assumptions imply that each party has an ideal
point and ranks policies lower as they move away from it. This note asks whether this weaker
ordinal property of single-peakedness is enough to rule out leapfrogging.
We first show that mutual leapfrogging is impossible without voter abstention. More generally,
the same conclusion holds whenever the set of participating voters is independent of the parties’
positions. A party that has crossed beyond its opponent can move back toward its own ideal point
without losing electoral support, and the move is also ideologically better.
Abstention is therefore essential to the counterexample. We study alienation-style abstention: a
voter participates if at least one party platform is acceptable to her. Once participation depends on
the platforms, a party’s deviation can change which voters participate. This is a standard reason for
abstention in spatial voting models [Anderson and Glomm, 1992, Llavador, 2000], and alienation is
also an empirically relevant source of abstention [Adams et al., 2006]. Once participation depends
on the platforms, a party’s deviation can change which voters participate.

We give an explicit counterexample showing that single-peaked party preferences alone do not

common translated utility shapes, while remaining weaker than both.
Section 2 defines the model which is an ordinal version of alienation based abstention model
of spatial voting. Section 3 presents the example and shows why abstention matters. Section 4
presents the ordinal condition under which mutual leapfrogging is impossible.

Model
Let
X = {xj : j ∈I},
xj < xj+1,
where I ⊆Z is an interval of integers. Thus X may be finite or countably infinite. The integer
index records position in the linear order: xj+k is k steps to the right of xj, whenever j + k ∈I.
There are two parties, A and B, with ideal points τA = xp and τB = xq, where p < q. Each
party i ∈{A, B} has a weak preference order ⪰i over X, with unique ideal point τi. We write
x ≻i y when x ⪰i y holds while y ⪰i x fails.
Axiom 1 (Single-peakedness of party preferences). For each party i ∈{A, B}, the preference order
⪰i is single-peaked with peak τi: if xa < xb ≤τi, then xb ≻i xa; and if τi ≤xb < xa, then xb ≻i xa.
Axiom 1 is the standard single-peakedness condition on a line. It ranks alternatives on the
same side of the peak by proximity to the peak, but imposes no restriction on comparisons between
alternatives lying on opposite sides of the peak.
Remark 1 (Ordinal displacement notation). For later use, we introduce signed ordinal-displacement
notation. If τi = xc, define Ri(k) = xc+k and Li(k) = xc−k, whenever these policies belong to X.
Thus Ri(k) and Li(k) denote the policies k ordinal steps to the right and left of party i’s ideal point.
By Axiom 1, for each party i,
τi ≻i Ri(1) ≻i Ri(2) ≻i · · ·
and
τi ≻i Li(1) ≻i Li(2) ≻i · · · ,
whenever the corresponding policies belong to X. Axiom 1 imposes no restriction on comparisons
of the form Ri(a) versus Li(b).
A party’s strategy is a policy in X. We refer to the policies chosen by the parties as their
platforms. If party A chooses s ∈X and party B chooses t ∈X, we call (s, t) a platform profile.
Electoral outcomes
We use an ordinal version of Llavador [2000]’s alienation rule for a finite electorate. Instead of
requiring a party platform to lie within a fixed distance of the voter’s ideal point, we require a
party platform to lie in the voter’s acceptable interval. These intervals may differ across voters.

The electorate is a finite set V . Each voter v ∈V has an ideal point θv = xcv ∈X, strict
single-peaked preferences over X with peak θv, and an attraction interval Av ⊆X. We assume Av
is an interval in the policy order and contains θv.
Given platforms (s, t), voter v is active if s ∈Av or t ∈Av. If active, voter v votes for the
platform she strictly prefers.
If she is indifferent between the two platforms, she abstains.
In

Party objectives
Parties first rank electoral outcomes:
Win ≻Tie ≻Lose.
For party A, outcome A is a win and outcome B is a loss; for party B, outcome B is a win and
outcome A is a loss.
Conditional on the same electoral outcome, each party ranks profiles by its own announced
platform. Thus, if g(s, t) = g(s′, t′), then party A weakly prefers (s, t) to (s′, t′) if and only if
s ⪰A s′, and party B weakly prefers (s, t) to (s′, t′) if and only if t ⪰B t′.
This specification avoids any cardinal tradeoff between electoral success and ideology. Ideologi-
cal preferences are used only to compare profiles with the same electoral outcome. The specification
is nevertheless office-first: a win is always preferred to a tie, and a tie is always preferred to a loss.
A pure-strategy Nash equilibrium is a platform profile (s, t) ∈X × X such that neither party
has a strictly profitable unilateral deviation under this lexicographic ordering.
Definition 1. A platform profile (s, t) exhibits reversed order if t < s. It exhibits mutual leapfrog-
ging if each party crosses the other party’s ideal point, that is, if
t < τA < τB < s.
A Nash equilibrium exhibits reversed order, respectively mutual leapfrogging, if its equilibrium profile
does.
Since τA < τB, reversed order means that the left party chooses the right platform and the right
party chooses the left platform. The next proposition shows that any reversed-order equilibrium
must be mutually leapfrogged.
Proposition 1 (Order reversal implies mutual leapfrogging). Suppose Axiom 1 holds. Let (s, t) be
a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium with t < s. Then the election is tied at (s, t), and
t < τA < τB < s.

Proof. The election must be tied. If one party loses, it can copy the opponent’s platform and obtain
a tie, which is strictly better than losing. Since party A’s deviation to t gives a tie, equilibrium
requires s ⪰A t. If τA ≤t < s, then t is closer than s to τA on the same side, so single-peakedness
gives t ≻A s, a contradiction.
Hence t < τA.
Similarly, party B’s deviation to s gives a tie,
so equilibrium requires t ⪰B s. If t < s ≤τB, then s is closer than t to τB on the same side,
so single-peakedness gives s ≻B t, a contradiction.
Hence τB < s.
Since τA < τB, the result
follows.
Proposition 1 uses only the fact that copying the opponent’s platform produces a tie. The
existence result below uses abstention more substantively: the set of active voters can change after
deviations.
3
Single-peakedness alone is not sufficient with abstention
We first show that mutual leapfrogging is impossible when the set of participating voters does not
change with the pair of platforms. We then give a simple example in which mutual leapfrogging
occurs with four voters and seven policies: all voters participate at the equilibrium profile, but
some deviations change who participates.
Proposition 2. Suppose the same voters are active at every pair of distinct platforms. Then no
pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exhibits mutual leapfrogging.
Proof. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that (s, t) is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exhibiting
mutual leapfrogging. Then t < τA < τB < s.
The election at (s, t) must be tied. If party A loses, it can copy party B’s platform and obtain a
tie, which is strictly better than losing. The same argument applies to party B. Hence the election
is tied.
We claim that party A’s deviation from s to τA cannot reduce its number of votes. Let v be any
voter who votes for s against t. Since t < τA < s, single-peakedness of voter preferences implies that
v also prefers τA to t. Indeed, if v’s peak lies weakly to the left of t, then t ≻v s, a contradiction.
If the peak lies between t and τA, then τA ≻v s, hence τA ≻v t. If the peak lies weakly to the right
of τA, then τA ≻v t.
By assumption, the same voters are active at (τA, t) as at (s, t). Therefore every voter counted
for party A at (s, t) is still counted for party A at (τA, t). Since (s, t) is tied, party A obtains either
a tie or a win after deviating to τA.
If it wins, the deviation is profitable. If it ties, the electoral outcome is unchanged and τA ≻A
s, since τA is party A’s unique ideal point.
In either case party A has a profitable deviation,
contradicting equilibrium.
The example below shows what abstention changes. At the equilibrium profile all voters are
active, but deviations can change which voters are active. The example uses seven policies and four
voters.

Agreement on cross-side comparisons
Single-peakedness compares policies lying on the same side of a party’s ideal point. It does not
compare a rightward deviation with a leftward deviation. The counterexample exploits precisely
this freedom. We now impose an ordinal restriction on such cross-side comparisons. We use the
notation Ri(k) and Li(k) from Remark 1.
Axiom 2 (Agreement on cross-side comparisons). For all a, b ∈Z>0 such that the relevant policies
belong to X,
RA(a) ⪰A LA(b)
⇐⇒
RB(a) ⪰B LB(b),
and
LA(b) ⪰A RA(a)
⇐⇒
LB(b) ⪰B RB(a).
Axiom 2 says that the parties agree on the ranking of each cross-side pair: a rightward deviation
of size a and a leftward deviation of size b. It does not require left-right symmetry. It allows both
parties to prefer, say, a rightward deviation of size a to a leftward deviation of size b. It rules out
only disagreement between the parties over the same cross-side pair.
The axiom is ordinal, but it is implied by familiar cardinal restrictions.

Proposition 3. Axiom 2 is implied by each of the following assumptions.
(i) Symmetric single-peaked utilities. For each party i, there is a strictly decreasing function
ϕi : Z≥0 →R such that, if τi = xc, then vi(xm) = ϕi(|m −c|).
(ii) Common utility shape.
There is a function u : Z →R such that, if τi = xc, then
vi(xm) = u(m −c).
Proof. Under (i), for each party i,
Ri(a) ⪰i Li(b)
⇐⇒
ϕi(a) ≥ϕi(b)
⇐⇒
a ≤b,
and
Li(b) ⪰i Ri(a)
⇐⇒
ϕi(b) ≥ϕi(a)
⇐⇒
b ≤a.
Both conditions are independent of i, so the parties agree on every cross-side pair.
Under (ii), for each party i,
Ri(a) ⪰i Li(b)
⇐⇒
u(a) ≥u(−b),
and
Li(b) ⪰i Ri(a)
⇐⇒
u(−b) ≥u(a).
Again, the right-hand sides are independent of i. Hence Axiom 2 holds.
Axiom 2 is weaker than either sufficient condition above. Symmetric distance utilities imply
Ri(k) ∼i Li(k) for every party i and every k. Axiom 2 does not require this symmetry. It allows
both parties to prefer, for example, a rightward deviation of two steps to a leftward deviation
of one step, provided they agree on that comparison. A common translated utility shape is also
stronger: it imposes a common numerical representation over all signed deviations. Axiom 2 keeps
only agreement on the ordinal ranking of each cross-side pair.
We now show that this ordinal agreement rules out mutual leapfrogging.
Theorem 1. Suppose Axioms 1 and 2 hold. Then no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exhibits mutual
leapfrogging.
Proof. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that (s, t) is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exhibiting
mutual leapfrogging. Then t < τA < τB < s. By Proposition 1, the election is tied at (s, t). Since
party A’s deviation to t also gives a tie, equilibrium requires s ⪰A t. Since party B’s deviation to
s also gives a tie, equilibrium requires t ⪰B s.
Write τA = xp, τB = xq, t = xr, and s = xu, with r < p < q < u. Let δ = q −p > 0, a = u −p,
and b = p −r. Relative to party A’s ideal point, s = RA(a) and t = LA(b). Thus s ⪰A t gives
RA(a) ⪰A LA(b). By Axiom 2, RB(a) ⪰B LB(b).
Relative to party B’s ideal point,
s = RB(a −δ),
t = LB(b + δ).
By Axiom 1, RB(a −δ) ≻B RB(a) and LB(b) ≻B LB(b + δ). Therefore
s = RB(a −δ) ≻B RB(a) ⪰B LB(b) ≻B LB(b + δ) = t.
Hence s ≻B t, contradicting t ⪰B s. Therefore no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium exhibits mutual
leapfrogging.
Remark 2. Axiom 2 is sufficient, not necessary, for ruling out mutual leapfrogging. In the example
of Section 3, if every voter regards every policy as acceptable, then the same voters are active at every
pair of distinct platforms. Proposition 2 then rules out mutual leapfrogging. The party preferences
are unchanged, however, and Axiom 2 still fails.

5
Conclusion
Single-peakedness ranks policies on each side of a party’s ideal point, but not across sides. With
abstention, this is enough to permit mutual leapfrogging in pure-strategy equilibrium. Agreement
on cross-side comparisons rules it out. Single-peakedness alone is therefore not enough to prevent
party-order reversal.
