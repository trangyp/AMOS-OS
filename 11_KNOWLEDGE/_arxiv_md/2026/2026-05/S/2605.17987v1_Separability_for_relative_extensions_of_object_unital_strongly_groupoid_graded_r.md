1. Introduction
Let R be a ring. By this we mean that R is associative but not necessarily unital.
If R is unital, we denote its multiplicative unit by 1R and assume 1R ̸= 0. Suppose
that S is a subring of R. In that case, we say that R is a ring extension of S, and
we indicate this by writing R/S.
Recall that R/S is said to be separable if the multiplication map µ : R⊗S R →R,
defined by the additive extension of µ(r⊗r′) := rr′, for r, r′ ∈R, admits a section in
the category of R-bimodules, that is, if there is an R-bimodule map δ : R →R⊗S R
such that µ ◦δ = idR.
The concept of separability for ring extensions generalizes the classical notion of
separability for algebras over fields, which itself extends the notion of separability
for field extensions (see e.g. [4]). Separable ring extensions have in turn been gen-
eralized through the introduction of separable functors in [14]. For a comprehensive
overview of the historical development of the concept of separability, see [19].
Separable ring extensions have been studied by numerous authors (see e.g. [1, 2,
4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 13, 14, 15, 16, 18]). The reason for the intense interest in such ring
extensions is that important properties of the ground ring, such as semisimplicity
and hereditarity, are often automatically inherited by the larger ring.
Conditions for separability of ring extensions have been studied in various set-
tings. Let us briefly describe some of these results. The following results on rings
of matrices and group rings were proved by DeMeyer and Ingraham in [4, p. 41]:
Theorem 1. Suppose that S is a unital ring.
(a) Let n ∈N := {1, 2, 3, . . .} and let R := Mn(S) denote the ring of n×n matrices
over S. We consider S as a subring of R by identifying each element of S with
its corresponding diagonal matrix. Then R/S is separable.

Let Γ be a groupoid. By this we mean that Γ is a small category in which every
morphism is an isomorphism. We let Γ0 denote the set of objects of Γ, identified
with their identity morphisms, and we let Γ1 denote the set of morphisms of Γ.
The domain and range functions Γ1 →Γ0 are denoted by d and r, respectively.

this will often be indicated by writing σ : e →f. We identify each e ∈Γ0 with its
corresponding identity morphism e →e, thereby considering Γ0 as a subset of Γ1.
From now on we write σ ∈Γ when we mean σ ∈Γ1. We let the inverse of σ ∈Γ
be denoted by σ−1. We let Γ2 denote the set of ordered pairs (σ, τ) in Γ × Γ with
d(σ) = r(τ). If (σ, τ) ∈Γ2, then the composite of σ and τ is denoted στ.

case, we say that ∆is wide if ∆0 = Γ0. If e ∈Γ0, then Γ(e, e) is a group called the

of Γ is the groupoid having Γ0 as objects and ∪e∈Γ0Γ(e) as morphisms.

2.2. Modules. Let A be a ring. By a left A-module we mean an additive group
M equipped with a biadditive map A × M ∋(a, m) 7→am ∈M satisfying a(bm) =
(ab)m for a, b ∈A and m ∈M. Analogously, right A-modules are defined. We
let AMod denote the category having left A-modules as objects and left A-module
homomorphisms as morphisms. Let B be another ring. If M is both a left A-module
and a right B-module, then we say that M is an A-B-bimodule if (am)b = a(mb) for
a ∈A, b ∈B and m ∈M. Recall that if N is another A-B-bimodule, then a map
f : M →N is said to be an A-B-bimodule homomorphism if it is simultaneously a
left A-module homomorphism and a right B-module homomorphism.
Let M be a left A-module. If X ⊆A and Y ⊆M, we let XY denote the set of
finite sums of elements of the form xy, for x ∈X and y ∈Y . We say that M is
unitary if AM = M. We let AUMod denote the full subcategory of AMod having
unitary A-modules as objects. Recall that A is said to be idempotent if A is unitary
as a left module over itself, that is if AA = A.
Suppose that A/B is a ring extension where A is idempotent.
To this ring
extension we associate the
• restriction functor ResA/B : AUMod →BMod which to a left A-module M
associates the left B-module M, where B acts on M via A, and the
• induction functor IndA/B : BUMod →AUMod which to a left B-module
N associates the left A-module A ⊗B N, where A acts on A ⊗B N by left
multiplication, and B acts on A from the right via A.
Remark 7. (a) Since A is idempotent, ResA/B can be applied to A itself.
(b) The functor ResA/B does not always map unitary modules to unitary mod-
ules. Indeed, let F be a field and put A = F × F, B = {0} × F and M = F × {0}.
Let A and B act on M by coordinatewise multiplication. Then AM = M, but
BM = {(0, 0)} ̸= M. Therefore, M is unitary considered as a left module over A,
but not over B.
(c) The functor IndA/B is well defined, since A(A⊗BN) = (AA)⊗BN = A⊗BN.
