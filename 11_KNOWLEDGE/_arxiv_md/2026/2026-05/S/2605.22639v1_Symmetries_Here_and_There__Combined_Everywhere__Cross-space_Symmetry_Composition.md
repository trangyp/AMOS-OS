Symmetries Here and There, Combined Everywhere:
Cross-space Symmetry Compositions in Robotics
Loizos Hadjiloizou, Rodrigo P´erez-Dattari, and No´emie Jaquier
Abstract— Robots exhibit a rich variety of symmetries arising
from their mechanical structure and the properties of their
tasks. Although many robotics problems exhibit several symme-
tries simultaneously, existing approaches typically treat them in
isolation, failing to exploit their combined potential. This paper
introduces cross-space symmetry compositions, a framework for
learning robot policies that are jointly equivariant to multiple
symmetries across configuration and task spaces. Leveraging
the differential-geometric structure of the forward kinematics
map, we both descend symmetries from configuration to task
space and lift symmetries from task to configuration space,
enabling their composition within a unified representation
space. We validate our framework on simulated and real-world
experiments on a dual-arm robot, demonstrating that jointly
leveraging multiple symmetries yields improved generalization.
I. INTRODUCTION
Incorporating inductive bias is a key avenue to improve
sample efficiency and generalization in robot policy learning.
Among possible biases, those arising from symmetries are
particularly powerful as they enable a policy to generalize
from each training sample to an entire class of equivalent
observations and actions [1]. In robot policy learning, a
symmetry describes a transformation of the observation in-
ducing an associated transformation of the action, leading to
consistent behavior across symmetric situations. Symmetries
may arise from physical laws and mechanical structures [2]
or may be identified directly from data as invariance and
equivalence relations that a policy should respect [3].
Robots exhibit a rich variety of symmetries rooted both in
their mechanical structure and in the properties of their tasks.
These symmetries have been largely exploited by the robot
control and learning communities. In configuration space, the
Lagrangian of floating-based robots are invariant to transla-
tions and reorientations of their base [2]. By factoring out
these transformations from the equations of motion, dynamic
controllers and policies can be formulated or learned on
lower-dimensional spaces [4], [5], [6], [7]. Morphological
symmetries [8] arise from the interchangeability of replicated
kinematic chains such as the arms of a humanoid robot
or the legs of a quadruped. They were exploited for state
estimation [8] and policy learning [9], yielding enhanced
sample efficiency and stronger generalization compared to
symmetry-unaware algorithms. Symmetries in configuration
space were also shown to improve consistency and reduce
sample complexity of motion planning algorithms [10]. In
task space, symmetries typically capture equivalences in

generalize across unseen object poses and workspace config-

In many manipulation tasks, robots exhibit several of the
aforementioned symmetries simultaneously, as illustrated in
Fig. 1. Yet, the aforementioned works consider them in
isolation, thereby overlooking relevant inductive bias. For
instance, a dual-arm policy encoding only rotational symme-
try generalizes across rotated scenes but cannot execute the
task upon a reflection of the workspace. Conversely, a policy
encoding only morphological symmetries can exploit the
interchangeability of limbs, but fails under rotations of the
scene. Robust generalization across both variations therefore
requires jointly encoding both symmetries. Compositions of
symmetries have been explored by the machine learning
community. Kim et al. [17] proposed to weakly enforce
multiple symmetries through multiple regularizers in the
objective function. In a similar line, Li et al. [18] proposed
to learn dynamic models via latent flows that preserve
several symmetries simultaneously. However, in both cases,
the considered symmetries arise in a single space. In contrast,
robotics symmetries naturally emerge in different spaces:
Floating-base and morphological symmetries are defined in
the robot configuration space, while task isometries and task-
induced symmetries are expressed in task space. Therefore,
these symmetries cannot be composed directly and must first
be transferred in a common space.

principal families of symmetries arising in robotics, span-

symmetries between configuration and task spaces, together
with feasibility conditions; (3) A framework for composing
multiple symmetries within a common space, including a
characterization of the necessary compatibility conditions.
II. PRELIMINARIES
This section reviews the background on differential geom-
etry, groups theory, and Riemannian geometry necessary for
cross-space symmetry transfer and composition.
A. Differential Geometry
Intuitively, a smooth manifold M of dimension n is a
topological space that locally resembles the Euclidean space
Rn. Formally, there exists a neighborhood homeomorphic to
an open subset of Rn around each point p∈M. The tangent
space TpM at every point p∈M is an n−dimensional vec-
tor space composed of the first-order derivatives of curves on
the manifold passing through p. The Euclidean structure of
tangent spaces enables the use of tools from calculus, includ-
ing differentiation, integration, and vector fields. The tangent
bundle T M is the disjoint union of all tangent spaces.
B. Groups, Actions, and Equivariance
Symmetries are naturally represented through the math-
ematical structure of groups and their associated group
actions. A group G is a set equipped with an associative
binary operation ◦: G×G →G, an identity element e ∈G,
and an inverse g−1 ∈G for each element g ∈G [20].
A group acts on a manifold M via its action Φ, which
defines transformations parametrized by the group elements.
We focus on the left action, which is an associative map
ΦM : G×M →M that respects the identity transformation
ΦM(e, p)=p, ∀p∈M. Intuitively, a map h:M→N across

III. A CATALOG OF ROBOTIC SYMMETRIES
We review three principal families of symmetries com-
monly encountered in robotics: morphological, rotational,
and task-induced symmetries. We describe their associated
group action acting on either the configuration or task space.
A. Morphological and Floating-based Symmetries

ability of replicated kinematic chains with symmetric mass
distributions, such as the arms of a humanoid robot or
the legs of a quadruped. These symmetries arise from the
robot’s mechanical structure and induce equivariances in
both the state space and dynamics equations. Morphological
symmetries naturally act on the robot joints, i.e., on its
configuration space Q. Morphological symmetries of a rigid-
bodied robot with fixed morphology (i.e., with finitely-many
interchangeable kinematic chains) are characterized as a

C. Task-induced Symmetries
Task-induced symmetries [16] describe transformations of
x ∈X that preserve a given cost function c : X →R. Two
task-space states x1, x2 ∈X are considered symmetric if
c(x1)=c(x2). Therefore, task-induced symmetries describe
transformations within equivalence classes of states that
leave the task cost c unchanged.
Unlike morphological and rotational symmetries, task-
induced symmetries are not specified a priori through the
action of a known group. Instead, they emerge implicitly
from the structure of the task itself through the invariance
of c. Following [16], we focus on continuous task-induced
symmetries. Such symmetries are characterized locally by
the infinitesimal directions in TxX along which c remains
constant. These directions form the basis of the space of

B. Lifting Symmetries
After characterizing descending symmetries, we proceed
to study their lifting, i.e., the transfer of task-space symme-
tries to Q. We consider a continuous symmetry described by
the action ΦX :G×X →X of a Lie group G on the task space
X. This action relates each task-space state x ∈X to its
symmetric counterparts x′ =ΦX(g, x), ∀g∈G. Following the
same reasoning as in Section IV-A, lifting a symmetry would
require constructing a group action ΦQ :G×Q→Q such that
the inverse of the forward kinematics map is G-equivariant.
However, for redundant robots, this problem is generally ill-
posed due to the non-injectivity of the forward kinematics
map, leading to the non-uniqueness of its inverse. As mul-
tiple joint configurations correspond to the same task-space
state, a task-space symmetry does not uniquely determine a
corresponding transformation in configuration space. Hence,
directly constructing the lifted group action ΦQ through
inverse kinematics becomes an under-determined problem.
Next, we resolve this ambiguity by exploiting the additional
geometric structure of the forward kinematics map.

V. SYMMETRY COMPOSITION
In this section, we consider robot manipulation tasks
exhibiting several symmetries, originating either in the con-
figuration space Q or the task space X. All symmetries
are assumed to have been transferred to Q or X using the
transfer framework from Section IV. Our goal is to learn
a jointly-equivariant policy with respect to all symmetries
at hand. Formally, the policy must satisfy the equivariance
condition (1) simultaneously for a set of group actions
[Φ1, . . . , ΦM]. Next, we study the compatibility conditions
under which symmetries can be composed, and characterize
the resulting compositions based on their actions.
A. Direct Product
Two groups G1, G2 can be composed into a product group

B. Semi-Direct Product
When two group actions do not commute, applying one
action and then the other leads to a different transformation
than applying them in the opposite order. In this case, the two
groups can still be combined if the group G1 acts on G2 via
automorphisms. This action is described by a homomorphism
ρ : G1 →Aut(G2),
(31)
where Aut(G2) is the automorphism group of G2, i.e., the
group of bijections from G2 to itself that preserve the group
operation. This implies that each element g1 ∈G1 defines
a structure-preserving transformation of G2. Intuitively, ap-
plying an action from G1 modifies the action from G2.
Under this compatibility structure, the groups G1, G2 can
be composed via the semi-direct product G = G1 ⋉G2 [20],
yielding the composed action
Φ(g, q) = Φ1(g1, Φ2(ρ(g1)(g2), q)).
(32)
Unlike the direct-product case, the second action is explic-
itly transformed by the first through ρ, reflecting the non-
commutativity of the two actions.

VI. EXPERIMENTS
We experimentally validate the proposed cross-symmetry
compositions on a letter-writing task with a simulated dual-
arm planar robot, and on two real-world experiments on the
dual-arm humanoid robot RB-Y1.
A. Letter-drawing Task with a Simulated Planar Robot
We consider a planar dual-arm robot with two 4-DoF
arms with configuration space Q = Ql × Qr = T4 × T4 and
task space X =Xl × Xr =R2 × R2. The robot is tasked to
simultaneously trace the letters C and N with its left and
right end-effectors. Our objective is to learn a policy capable
of drawing these letters and symmetric variations thereof. We
consider three symmetries: (1) The bilateral morphological
symmetry of the arms, i.e., the cyclic-two group GQ
M = C2
acting on Q; (2) A rotational symmetry of GX
R = SO(2)
acting on the letters in X; and (3) A task-induced scaling
symmetry GX
S =S2 acting on the letters in X.
We train a policy π :Q×S →T Q that returns joint-space
velocities ˙q ∈TqQ based on the configuration q ∈Q. We
condition the network on a variable s ∈S that specifies the
symmetric variant of the task to execute. Each s = (θ, λ, σ)
is composed of a rotation angle θ ∈[−π, π], a scaling
factor λ ∈R>0, and reflection index σ ∈{1, −1}. The
network is trained via imitation learning as in [26] using
5 demonstrations.
We incorporate the symmetries into the policy through
data augmentation. The joint configurations and joint veloc-
ities of each demonstration are transformed via an action
ΦQ(g, q) and its differential dΦQ
g |q, respectively. Following
Sections IV and V, all symmetries are transferred to and
composed in Q. We generate the augmented dataset us-

symmetry. For the rotational symmetry, we discretize SO(2)
using rotations R(θ) with angular increment ∆θ, such that

predefined finite set of scaling factors λ ∈R>0. We use both
elements {e, gs}∈C2 of the morphological symmetry group.
Evaluation. We evaluate the symmetry composition by
considering four policies: (1) π, a base policy without
symmetry augmentation; (2) πGR, encoding only rotational

of morphological, rotational, and scaling symmetries.

Letter-drawing task. The first experiment extends the

VII. CONCLUSION
This paper introduces cross-space symmetry compositions,
a framework for learning robot policies that are jointly equiv-
ariant to multiple symmetries arising across configuration
and task spaces. Building on the differential-geometric struc-
ture of the forward kinematics map, we transfer symmetries
across spaces by descending configuration-space symmetries
to task space and lifting task-space symmetries to configura-
tion space. Moreover, we characterize the compatibility con-
ditions under which transferred symmetries can be system-
atically composed through direct and semi-direct products.
Our framework unifies several important symmetry families
commonly encountered in robotics, including morphological,
rotational, and task-induced symmetries, within a common
mathematical formalism. Through simulated and real-world
experiments on dual-arm manipulation tasks, we demon-
strated that jointly leveraging multiple symmetries substan-
tially improves policy generalization across task variations.
Future work will investigate the integration of the pro-
posed cross-space symmetry compositions into equivariant
neural architectures, to be enforce equivariance at the archi-
tectural level rather than weakly through data augmentation.
