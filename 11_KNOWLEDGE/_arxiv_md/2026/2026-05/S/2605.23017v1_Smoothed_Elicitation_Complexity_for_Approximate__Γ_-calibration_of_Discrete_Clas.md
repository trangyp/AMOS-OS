Abstract
One prominent method of evaluating machine learning model trustworthiness is
the notion of calibration. In the binary outcome setting, a probabilistic predictor is
calibrated if outcomes are realized according to a model’s distributional prediction,
conditioned on this prediction. Straightforward extensions of binary calibration def-
initions to probabilistic multiclass classifiers suffer from an exponential complexity
blowup as the space of predictions grows exponentially in the number of classes n.
As a remedy, Noarov and Roth [35] propose multiclass calibration with predictions
that are properties of the outcome distribution, reducing complexity from growing
in the number of classes n to the dimension d of the property, called its elicitation
complexity. Previous work on approximate property calibration is generally limited
to continuous scalar properties, despite many relevant properties of interest being
discrete, like the mode or rankings. We characterize the approximate property
calibration of discrete properties which are strongly orderable by using Lipschitz
continuous properties as an intermediary. This work is the first to our knowledge
to provide approximate calibration results for discrete properties. Along the way,
we characterize the Lipschitz elicitation complexity of strongly orderable discrete
properties by constructing algorithms for designing these Lipschitz properties,
which we prove can be post-processed to obtain the original discrete property.

Introduction
One prominent paradigm of evaluating machine learning models examines calibration, where a
model’s probabilistic predictions are roughly realized. That is, instead of conditioning on a feature
instance X = x (as desired in evaluating Bayes predictors), we condition on a model’s prediction
f(x) = p, and want to observe outcomes roughly aligned with p. Intuitively, this is typically
formalized as E[Y |f(X) = p] ≈p for all p ∈im(f). Empirically, continuous predictors must
have their predictions binned to obtain multiple instances of the same conditioning event. The
sample complexity of calibration is known to grow polynomially in the number of bins on the
space [10, 24, 45].
This poses a prohibitive challenge for multiclass calibration: when bins represent approximate
probabilistic predictions, the number of bins naïvely grows exponentially in the number of classes,
posing substantial computational and statistical challenges. To circumvent this, many works on
multiclass calibration reduce the granularity of the binned space [4, 23, 24, 28, 30, 45]. These
approaches generally combine full distributional predictions with some polynomial binning approach,
and often lack decision-theoretic guarantees for the task implicitly guiding the polynomial binning.

Derr et al. [11] outline a few possible approaches to defining multiclass calibration, consolidating
many of these approaches to what they call distribution calibration with respect to γ, where γ :
∆Y →R maps distributions to reports is called a property guiding the binning approach. In contrast,
they show distribution calibration with respect to γ is a stronger notion than an alternative called Γ-
calibration— initially introduced by Gneiting and Resin [22], Noarov and Roth [35], in which a model
estimates the property γ itself— rather than a distribution— while still providing decision-theoretic
guarantees.
Critically, results surrounding approximate γ-calibration assume the prediction target γ is continuous.
These continuous (calibrated) predictions are often used to make discrete decisions. This continuous-
to-discrete jump often poses challenges for obtaining decision-theoretic guarantees, as demonstrated
by Noarov and Roth [35]’s assumption of continuous properties and Derr et al. [11] only giving
exact calibration results for discrete properties. From a technical perspective, it is difficult to obtain
approximate calibration guarantees for discrete prediction tasks since discrete predictions do not
provide the opportunity to communicate a model’s uncertainty, which is critical to understand if a
model is “close to” calibration. More precisely, this tension emerges from the need for a calibration
error metric that is continuous in the prediction space, which is impossible if predictions themselves
are not continuous.
To bridge this gap, we propose using smoothed property elicitation to obtain a continuous predictor
of an intermediate continuous property Γ which can be post-processed to obtain the desired discrete
decision property γ. We can obtain approximate calibration guarantees with this smoothed property,
while bounding the probability of miscalibration in the discrete space. The dimension of this smoothed
property is an important object of study in its own right. If the prediction dimension d is significantly
lower than predicting a full distribution over n labels, this improves the computational complexity
of gradient-based optimization algorithms. Moreover, the number of bins evaluated in empirical
estimation of miscalibration grows exponentially in the prediction dimension d. Since sample
complexity grows in the number of bins, a prediction dimension d < n can significantly improve
computational and statistical complexity.
Figure 1 outlines the organization of the paper. In Section 3, we first characterize the discrete
properties that can be obtained by post-processing a continuous property Γ : ∆Y →R in 1 dimension.
Our characterization is constructive, providing two algorithms yielding smoothed properties. With
this characterization, we proceed to obtain bounds on the approximate Γ-calibration of probabilistic
predictors in Section 4.1, then bound the approximate discrete γ-calibration in Section 4.2 by using
the smoothed property as an intermediary.

2.1
Properties, identifiability, embeddings
The elicitation of discrete properties was first characterized by Lambert and Shoham [31], Lambert
et al. [33]. In essence, a property Γ : ∆Y →Rd or γ : ∆Y →R maps distributions over labels
to “reports” in some set. The primary question driving the property elicitation research is which
properties can be elicited by specific loss functions, as elicitation tends to be a simpler tool for analysis
than consistency [7, 44], which is necessary for establishing PAC learning bounds through empirical
risk minimization.† Lambert establishes a tight connection in the geometry of discrete properties and
their elicitability through power diagrams [2]; however, their characterization yields discrete losses,
which are usually not tractable to directly optimize. To circumvent this, the field has progressed in two
diverging directions: restricting to the study of continuous properties [17, 19, 35, 37, 42], or through
the design of surrogate loss functions [6, 15, 29, 39, 40, 43] whose surrogate predictions in Rd can
later be linked back to discrete decisions by a link function ψ : Rd →R. As one prominent example,
the categorical cross-entropy loss elicits the identity property Γ(p) = p, which is continuous. This
continuous property is then often “linked” to the discrete property of the mode ψ(u) = arg maxy uy.

notion of elicitation complexity for classes of loss functions or properties. This notion of complexity
is parameterized by the dimension d of inputs to the loss function. A lower dimension d can improve
computational costs for gradient-based optimization methods.

2.2
Calibration in multiclass settings
While calibration of predictors has been well-studied in binary outcome settings, definitions in
multiclass settings are not universally agreed upon, and results are generally definition-specific.
Recently, Derr et al. [11] categorize three primary approaches to defining calibration in multiclass
settings, though we focus on two: distribution calibration with respect to γ and Γ-calibration. For
intuition, consider calibration error which measures (a) conditioned on a piece of information, such as
a model’s prediction, the (b) distance between some prediction against a baseline. The computational
and statistical challenge posed by naïve extensions of multiclass calibration is that the granularity of
provided conditioning information in (a) grows exponentially in the number of classes n.
Derr et al. characterize the first set of multiclass calibration definitions as distribution calibration
with respect to γ, where γ : ∆Y →R is some given property representing the conditioning event (a),
with granularity |R| = poly(n) [4, 24, 45].
Definition 4 (Approximate distribution calibration with respect to γ). A distributional predictor
f : X →∆Y is ϵ-distribution calibrated with respect to γ if
EX,Y ∥f(X) −DY |{ˆx:γ(f(ˆx))=γ(f(X))}∥≤ϵ .

Moving one step beyond distribution calibration with respect to γ, Γ-calibration focuses on the
distance of predictions and baseline in (b), measuring distance in prediction space im(Γ) rather than
distribution space ∆Y. For discrete predictions and properties, this distance is not continuous in
function space, and approximate calibration is not empirically stable. To sidestep this issue, Noarov
and Roth [35] and Gneiting and Resin [22] assume properties Γ are continuous, Derr et al. [11]
only give exact Γ-calibration results for discrete properties, Hu et al. [28] focus on binary outcome
settings (emphasizing connections to calibration error metrics), and Collina et al. [10] study the
sample complexity of multicalibration for continuous scalar properties.
Derr et al. [11] discuss approximate Γ-calibration in terms of an arbitrary metric, but do not give
significant approximate results for discrete properties. With this insight, we use continuous properties
so that we can use Lp-norms as a distance metric, and bound the approximate calibration of discrete
properties through 0-1 error.

Characterizing 1-dimensional Lipschitz elicitable properties
In this section, we establish that orderable properties are refined by 1-dimensional Lipschitz properties
Γ : ∆Y →R, and as a consequence, we conclude that for any strongly orderable property γ,
elicCLip(γ) = 1. We later apply this characterization to establish approximate calibration guarantees
for such discrete orderable properties. Notably, our characterization is constructive, as Algorithm 1
and Algorithm 2 yield Lipschitz properties Γ refining γ. Algorithm 1 modifies the (piecewise linear,
convex, but not differentiable) surrogate construction of Finocchiaro et al. [13, Theorem 11] and
interpolates to “smooth” their surrogate; such an interpolation is not straightforward to ensure property
refinement. This approach is also very similar to the approach of Khurana et al. [29, Appendix H],
though they do not establish Lipschitzness in their work. We conjecture this approach might be
generalized to higher dimensions, but leave this to future work.
In contrast, Algorithm 2 uses the geometry of the discrete property γ directly, constructing a property Γ
which is a piecewise ratio of expectations, where the functions forming the numerator and denominator
are defined by the hyperplanes defining an orderable property. Crucially, it is not clear if it possible
to generalize this approach for d > 1. With the Lipschitz property Γ in both algorithms, we apply the
characterizations of Steinwart et al. [42] and Finocchiaro and Frongillo [12] to construct a surrogate
loss indirectly eliciting γ.

Application to Γ-calibration
The characterization of Lipschitz elicitation complexity, particularly with the construction of Lipschitz
refining properties in Algorithms 1 and 2, enables us to establish bounds on the approximate Γ-
calibration of both distributional predictors post-processed by direct computation of Γ (Section 4.1),
and from Γ-predictors to discrete γ-predictors (Section 4.2). The results of these sections can be
composed to obtain bounds on the probability of miscalibration with respect to a discrete property
when given a distributional predictor f : X →∆Y.

5
Discussion
We have established bounds on the approximate calibration of discrete properties, particularly for
strongly orderable discrete properties. This work is the first to our knowledge to establish such
approximate calibration bounds for discrete properties using norm-based calibration error metrics, in
contrast to the worst-case miscalibration error metrics in CDL [27] and CCE [41]. In order to establish
this approximate bound, we “smoothed” discrete properties into Lipschitz continuous properties
whose level sets can be mapped back to the discrete property, and established approximate calibration
of the smoothed properties.
Future work
Many directions of future work remain: first, our characterization of Lipschitz
elicitation complexity is limited to one dimension, as is Theorem 13, relying on strong orderability
of a property γ, seen in the presence of diam(γ, ψ) in the bound in Theorem 13. An interesting
immediate direction of future work is to extend our characterization to general d-dimensional bounds.
Another line of future work emerges from the observation that one advantage of distribution calibration
(with respect to γ) emerges from strong connections between (multi)calibration and to omniprediction,

where a model can be post-processed to yield decision-theoretic guarantees for a wide suite of
properties γ. We conjecture this characterization might explain some of the results of the efficiency
of the TreeCal algorithm of Fishelson et al. [16] also used by Peng [36].
Finally, one might consider modifications of our proposed algorithms which give the smoothest
possible properties, possibly by modifying interpolation points to not be midpoints in Algorithm 1 or
uniformly spaced in Algorithm 2, tightening the bounds derived in Section 4.
Broader impacts
While our work is theoretical in nature, our results make salient the deception of
“low calibration error” made possible by simply picking a property Γ that is quite smooth. We view
this as not unique to our work, but do advise proceeding with caution when drawing conclusions
based on low calibration error.

Omitted discussion of calibration error metrics
Calibration error metrics
It is worth discussing the emergence of calibration error metrics as a
unit of study in their own right. Rossellini et al. [41] pose axioms of calibration metrics of testability
— the ability to estimate a metric with finitely many samples — and actionability — obtaining
decision-theoretic guarantees from an approximately “calibrated” predictor. Haghtalab et al. [25]
discusses truthfulness of calibration metrics, lated extended by Qiao and Zhao [38], who apply their
results to decision-theoretic (actionable) calibration metrics. While expected calibration error [34] is
the “canonical” calibration error metric, it is not empirically testable with finite samples because of
a binning requirement. In contrast, Błasiok et al. [9] propose Distance to Calibration as a testable
calibration error metric, but it is notably not actionable. Other decision-theoretic calibration metrics
have emerged in binary settings, like Calibration Decision Loss [27] and Cutoff Calibration Error [41],

but for the purposes of this paper, we generally consider calibration error measured by an expected
norm. We leave it as a further line of inquiry to understand formally which calibration error metrics
are suitable for models which estimate properties Γ(p) instead of distributions p. Notably Bailie and
Derr [3] recently example the elicitation of “imprecise probabilities,” which may be helpful for this
problem.
