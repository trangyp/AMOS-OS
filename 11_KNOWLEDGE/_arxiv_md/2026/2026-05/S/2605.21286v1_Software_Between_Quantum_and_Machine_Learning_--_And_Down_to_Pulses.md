Abstract—Contemporary quantum computing platforms re-
main, in essence, programmable physical systems whose control
is typically mediated through unitary gate abstractions. While
such abstractions provide a uniform interface, they obscure
important aspects of the underlying hardware and may limit
the exploitation of its full capabilities. Direct operation at the
level of control pulses offers a more expressive and physically
faithful paradigm, enabling, for instance, the implementation of
tailored error-mitigation and optimisation strategies. However,
this increased expressivity comes at the cost of greater com-
plexity from the perspective of quantum software development,
necessitating structured and accessible tooling.
We present a software framework, integrated within the
QML-ESSENTIALS package, that extends quantum machine
learning (QML) methodologies to encompass pulse-level mod-
elling. By embedding quantum optimal control (QOC) techniques
within a QML setting, our approach enables the seamless
combination of gate-based and pulse-level representations in a
unified modelling paradigm. The framework provides a com-
prehensive suite of modelling and analytical capabilities. In
particular, we introduce composable ansatz constructions based
on interchangeable building blocks, alongside support for end-to-
end optimisation of pulse parameters. Motivated by the central
role of quantum Fourier models (QFMs), we further incorporate
a range of Fourier-analytic diagnostics, including the Fourier
fingerprint and the Fourier coefficient correlation (FCC), comple-
mented by extended measures of entanglement. All performance-
critical components are implemented in a high-performance
environment using JAX and supported by a dedicated quantum
simulator. Taken together, the framework facilitates reproducible
and systematic investigations in QML research, while bridging
the conceptual and practical divide between abstract circuit-
based models and hardware-aware pulse-level optimisation. It
provides a robust foundation for future developments at the
intersection of quantum machine learning and quantum control.
Index Terms—Quantum Machine Learning, Quantum Com-
puting, Quantum Optimal Control, Quantum Software Frame-
work
I. INTRODUCTION
Parameterised quantum circuits (PQCs) constitute the core
of contemporary quantum machine learning (QML), serving
as function approximators across a variety of supervised and
unsupervised machine learning (ML) tasks [1–7]. A Fourier-
analytic perspective [8, 9] allows studying their expressive
power (i.e., the classes of functions that can be learned).
*Equal contribution
By characterising PQCs and their data encodings through
their induced frequency spectra, quantum Fourier models
(QFMs) provide a structured approach to understanding how
quantum circuits realise function classes in terms of Fourier
coefficients and frequencies. At the same time, a growing
body of literature highlights the fundamental limitations in
PQCs. For instance, barren plateaus [10, 11], or trainability
degradation [12, 13] and restricted effective expressivity [14,
15] under realistic hardware constraints restrict the capabil-
ities of quantum computing approaches in ML. Despite this
increasing number of hardware-aware studies, we see a need
for systematic tools that enable investigations beyond idealised
quantum gates. In particular, while most QML studies assume
unitary gate decompositions [16, 17], quantum processing
units (QPUs), such as superconducting systems, operate at the
level of control pulses. However, based on Refs. [18–24], we
see a large potential in advancing QML research by directly
learning pulse-level control sequences instead of gate angles.
We therefore aim to bridge the gap between the high-level
circuit analysis metrics and the low-level pulse-level control
with a software framework that (1) provides a modular and
systematically composable QML model construction, (2) inte-
grates rigorous diagnostic tools tailored to the Fourier-analytic
perspective of QML, and (3) supports pulse-level modelling
grounded in quantum optimal control (QOC). Rather than
treating control pulses as an implementation detail, we expose
them as differentiable objects, enabling end-to-end optimisa-
tion of pulse parameters. This enables the study of expressivity,
trainability and other circuit properties, such as the entangling
capability, directly at the level of physically realisable controls.
Additionally, pulse-level learning paves the way for certain
error mitigation techniques [25], where control signals are
optimised to suppress coherent errors or reduce sensitivity to
noise [26–28].
In this work, we introduce software tools in Python that
provide a modular approach to variational ansatz design that
can be instantiated by conventional unitary gates or as implicit
pulse-level control sequences. Using this dual representation
allows controlled comparisons between circuit-level models
and hardware-aware implementations. Specifically, we extend
the QML-ESSENTIALS package, introduced in Ref. [29],
which already delivers some tools and metrics for circuit
analysis from a Fourier-analytic perspective.

Building on QFMs as a central theme, we incorporate

II. RELATED WORK
The current state of the art in QML software can be
roughly separated into three main categories, namely (1) gen-
eral libraries for quantum circuit simulation or real hard-
ware execution, at the core of established classical machine
learning frameworks, (2) QML-tailored frameworks, which,
in addition to quantum circuit execution, offer useful tools for
ML-research, such as predefined datasets and differentiable
programming, or (3) individual code setups accompanying
research articles for specific tasks or reproduction, often lever-
aging tools from the first two categories.

The second category contains more QML-tailored ap-

the capabilities of the quantum circuit simulation or hard-

instance, Refs. [45, 46], which orchestrate general quantum
experiments. The two frameworks, PennyLane and Qibo,
which are most similar to our work, additionally integrate
differentiable pulse-level control within a QML workflow,
enabling a full-stack approach from application to hardware or
simulator execution. Our work differs from these two in that
we particularly focus on the analysis of the interplay between
QFMs and their properties.
Since it has been established that PQCs can be represented
as truncated Fourier series [8, 47] (cf. Sec. III), the Fourier
spectrum poses an important tool to determine the expressivity
of a QML model. From a fundamental research perspective,
some relations between other properties of quantum circuits
and their Fourier spectrum are known, such as the express-
ibility [48, 49] (i.e., the ability of a model to explore the
Hilbert space; cf. Sec. VI-B), or dequantisability [50, 51]
(i.e., the ability to find classical alternatives to represent a
quantum model). Furthermore, a variety of publications [14,
15, 30, 52–56] investigate the Fourier spectrum to improve
QML algorithms, for instance, to find suitable circuit structures
or data encodings [57]. However, if code accompanies these
kinds of research articles, it mainly consists of individual code
setups, which may allow reproducing individual numerical
experiments, yet are difficult to build upon. Therefore, these
software packages fall into our third category of QML soft-
ware.
The QML-ESSENTIALS package, introduced in Ref. [29],
aims to close the gap between the categories (2) and (3): It
provides a set of standard tools, less general than category (2)
frameworks, but tailored to QFMs investigations and suited as
a backend for category (3) numerical experiments. However,
we find that the unitary-centred approach in Ref. [29] masks
the possibilities of lower-level quantum computations in QML
to directly access and learn the pulses of an operation. With
our QOC implementation, we aim to create a differentiable
interface for gradient-based pulse optimisation, such as algo-
rithms suggested in Refs. [58–60], or more recent approaches
that, for instance, use reinforcement learning [23].
Additionally, we propose further improvements to QML-
ESSENTIALS [29] to mature them into a software framework
that comprehensively tackles QFMs.

A. Model and Ansatz Overview

We refer to the set of angles corresponding to all rotational
gates in the circuit as the parameters of the model, which may
be subject to optimisation in a training process. For the ini-
tialisation of the parameters, we provide multiple approaches,
such as setting the parameters to specific values (zero or π),
or uniformly at random in a specific domain.
Following the data re-uploading mechanism from Eq. 3,
we support input encodings interleaved with the variational
layers, where encoding positions can be controlled with the
data_reuploading argument of the Model constructor. While
the default input encoding scheme is an angle encoding (using
a ˆRX gate), we also implemented the encoding schemes of
Ref. [61]: As introduced in Sec. III, the number of frequencies
(and therefore the number of coefficients) depends on the
chosen feature map. Hence, we provide an interface to choose
between different encoding methods in addition to providing
a custom feature map: (1) Hamming encoding (default, lin-
ear spectrum), (2) binary encoding (exponential spectrum),
and (3) ternary encoding (exponential spectrum). In addition,
trainable parameters can be applied in the feature map as
introduced in Ref. [54].
Additionally, we allow to view ansätze from a lower ab-
straction level than unitary gates, by providing the feature
to parametrise an ansatz directly with pulses, using pulse
parameters (cf. Sec. V), instead of rotational angles.
B. Execution with Input- and Parameter-Batching
Depending on the application or metric that is to be
evaluated for a model, different modes of execution may be
required. In QML-ESSENTIALS, this mode can be selected
by setting the execution_type argument when calling the
model. In particular, this value can be set to (1) "expval",
which gives the expectation value of an observable (e.g., the
default is ˆZ, acting on a specified set of qubits), (2) "density",
which gives the density matrix, (3) "state", which gives the
state-vector representation, and (4) "probs", which gives the
output probability distribution over a specified set of qubits,
using a finite number of shots.
To evaluate models with concurrent sets of inputs and
(pulse) parameters, we handle batch dimensions internally to
cover all possible combinations.
We utilise our own simulator to carry out the computation of
the circuit with the specified observables. This simulator and
all implementations of the metrics presented in this work are
entirely built upon the JAX [32] library for high-performance
array computations and utilisation of parallelisation where
possible. To solve the differential equation required for pulse-
level simulation, our framework supports adaptive Runge-
Kutta methods (e.g., Dormand-Prince of order 8 [64] via
Diffrax [65]) as well as commutator-free Magnus integrators
tailored for highly oscillatory Hamiltonian dynamics.

C. Other Features
The support for pulse-parameters and QOC is detailed in
Sec. V. Furthermore, the QML-ESSENTIALS package offers
circuit analysis metrics for computing the Fourier spectrum,
expressibility, or entangling capability. In Sec. VI we describe
further which metrics are part of the original framework and
how we contribute towards improving the existing state. In ad-
dition, we added other notable features, which are summarised
in this subsection.
1) Dataset Generator: To reduce the overhead required to
run simple trainings of QFMs, we provide a Fourier series
dataset generator. Given a model, its intrinsic frequencies are
inferred based on the encoding strategy chosen upon initial-
isation. Afterwards, corresponding coefficients are uniformly
sampled within a unit circle. The resulting Fourier series is
then used to obtain pairs of data points (x and F(x)), which
can then be used in a training environment.
2) Visualisation: To obtain a visualisation for a model’s
circuit, our framework offers an export of circuit diagrams
as text-based or Matplotlib figures. Additionally, we support
exporting to the Quantikz LATEX package [66]. The resulting
LATEX code can be easily copied into a paper or thesis. Pulse-
sequence schedules can also be visualised via the same API.
V. PULSE-LEVEL SIMULATION
Generally, unitary gates are used to train QFM models.
However, there exist lower-level parameters that allow control-
ling the exact pulse of each gate applied to a quantum system,
to which we refer as pulse parameters. These parameters are
usually subject to a fine-tuning process carried out by the
hardware provider in a process commonly termed QOC. In our
framework, we provide an interface to conduct experiments
that require access to the pulse parameters, therefore enabling
research that is more tailored to the actual quantum hardware.

B. Expressibility
In QML-ESSENTIALS, the expressibility is computed by
taking the inverse of the Kullback-Leibler (KL)-divergence
between states sampled from a given ansatz and the Haar
measure (cf. Apx. A for details). Therefore, the expressibility
metric introduced in Ref. [48] evaluates how well an ansatz
can approximate random states in Hilbert space. However,
expressibility does not reflect whether circuits are suitable for
learning settings, as functional constraints or input encodings
are not considered in this approach. As shown in Ref. [30],
the FCC addresses this limitation by revealing coefficient
dependencies that limit the effective number of learnable
frequency components.
The implementation of the expressibility based on the KL-
divergence from QML-ESSENTIALS and our complementary
FCC implementation allows us to measure and compare both
metrics for all available ansätze. In particular, we compute
the expressibility for 10 000 sample pairs and the FCC for an
ˆRY angle encoding and 10 000 samples of uniformly randomly
initialised parameters for each four-qubit ansatz. A comparison
between the KL-divergence-based expressibility and FCC is
shown in Fig. 6, also including the reference results for the
expressibility from Ref. [48]. As shown in the figure, the
KL-divergence from QML-ESSENTIALS and Ref. [48] match
closely, while the FCC yields rather different values for most
of the ansätze. This indicates that while some ansätze do
not seem to be very expressive (e.g., C8, C15, or C20), the
actual trainability might be better, as the Fourier coefficients
are not highly correlated, and vice versa, thus confirming the
discrepancy between these metrics as shown in [30].

we measured the runtime required to compute expressibility
and FCC for an increasing number of qubits. To this end, we
evaluated both metrics for 50 parameter samples (-pairs) on all
available ansätze with one and three variational layers on the

VII. CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK
The software library introduced in this work provides a
comprehensive environment for studying QML models from
both a Fourier-analytic and hardware-aware perspective. By
combining modular ansatz construction, pulse-level modelling,
Fourier-spectral diagnostics, entanglement analysis, and a dif-
ferentiable simulation backend, our software enables a repro-
ducible workflow for a broad range of controlled numerical
experiments within a single package.


## Page 10


This paves the way to investigate architectural ansatz
choices and physical constraints in the context of QML. At
the same time, we acknowledge that significant computational
costs are associated with training QML models, particularly
in pulse-level and noise-aware settings. While our framework
incorporates performance optimisations to mitigate compu-
tational overheads, the primary contribution of this work is
scientific rather than immediately application-driven.
In particular, we view the framework as a tool for the
systematic and reproducible investigation of pulse-level learn-
ing with QFMs. While both perspectives have independently
received considerable attention in the QML literature, with
pulse-level approaches for improving hardware control and
Fourier analysis for understanding the functional structure of
circuits, to our best knowledge, our software is the first to
allow for their combined investigation. With the foundation
provided in this work, we leave experiments and analysis to
connect the physical implementation of QML models with
their spectral properties to future work.
Our framework further provides the basis for a variety
of systematic studies. For instance, it can be investigated
to a greater extent how specific data encoding strategies
and ansätze, with their intrinsic entangling capability and
expressibility, influence trainability and set quantum models
apart from classical ML models. Additionally, the differen-
tiable pulse-level backend enables experiments for the direct
optimisation of pulse parameters in certain learning tasks, as
well as the exploration of error mitigation strategies. Notably,
our implementation allows combining pulse-level computation
with any of the available noise models, therefore enabling a
hardware-aware study of QML models. In future work, this
may include adding hardware-aware system dynamics, such
as damping due to hardware limitations. Ultimately, we aim
to provide a comprehensive toolbox for reproducible QML
research.
