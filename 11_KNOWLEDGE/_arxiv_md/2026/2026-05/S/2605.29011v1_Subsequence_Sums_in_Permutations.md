Abstract
A sequence of positive integers (a1, a2, . . . , ak) is called ℓ-additive if a1+a2+· · ·+ak = ℓa1 or
ℓak. In this paper, we prove that for all k ≥3, if n is sufficiently large, then every permutation
of {1, 2, . . . , n} has a 2-additive subsequence of length k. We also provide polynomial bounds for
the smallest n such that every permutation of {1, 2, . . . , n} has a 2-additive subsequence of length
k. When only monotone subsequences are considered, we show that 18 is the smallest n such
that every permutation of {1, 2, . . . , n} has a monotone 2-additive subsequence of length three.
Strong bounds are obtained for the minimum number of ℓ-additive subsequences of any length,
as well as monotone 2-additive subsequences of length three. Using techniques in arithmetic
Ramsey theory, we also show similar results for products and inverse sums.
Keywords: subsequences, monotone subsequences, permutations, sums, products, inverse sums

Introduction
In arithmetic Ramsey theory, the finitary version of the celebrated van der Waerden’s theorem says
that, for any positive integers k and r, if n is large enough, then every r-coloring of {1, 2, . . . , n}
has a monochromatic arithmetic progression of length k (see [14, Chapter 3] or [18, Chapter 2]).
In 1973, Entringer and Jackson [7] asked whether similar results hold for permutations. For any
positive integer n, let Sn denote the set of permutations of {1, 2, . . . , n} written in one-line nota-
tion. Entringer and Jackson asked whether every p = (p1, p2, . . . , pn) ∈Sn contains a monotone
subsequence (x, y, z) which is also a three-term arithmetic progression (3-AP), i.e., x + z = 2y.
Tom Odda (Ron Graham), R. C. Lyndon, and H. E. Thomas, Jr. [8] independently provided a
negative answer to Entringer and Jackson’s question. The main idea of Odda’s proof is that one can
build larger permutations without monotone 3-APs using smaller permutations without monotone
3-APs: if (p1, p2, . . . , pn) ∈Sn has no monotone 3-APs, then the permutation
(2p1, 2p2, . . . , 2pn, 2p1 −1, 2p2 −1, . . . , 2pn −1) ∈S2n
also has no monotone 3-APs.
Although arithmetic progressions can be avoided by permutations, are there subsequences, with
properties resembling results in arithmetic Ramsey theory, guaranteed to exist in every permutation

In this paper, we prove some Schur-Rado-type results for permutations. Our first result is that, for
a fixed k, if n is large enough then every p ∈Sn contains a subsequence of length k whose sum is
either twice the first term or twice the last term.
Theorem 1.1. For any positive integer k ≥3 and large enough n, every p ∈Sn has a subsequence
(x1, x2, . . . , xk) such that x1 + x2 + · · · + xk = 2x1 or 2xk.
In Theorem 1.1, a permutation does not necessarily have both a subsequence (x1, x2, . . . , xk)
such that x1+x2+· · ·+xk = 2x1 and a subsequence (y1, y2, . . . , yk) such that y1+y2+· · ·+yk = 2yk
simultaneously. For example, for any n, the identity permutation (1, 2, . . . , n) ∈Sn does not have
a subsequence (x1, x2, . . . , xk) such that x1 + x2 + · · · + xk = 2x1.
Our proof of Theorem 1.1 is purely combinatorial. The key observation is that any permutation
of a subset of {1, 2, . . . , n}, which contains the integers 1, 2, . . . , 2k −1 and a selective collection of
sums derived from these elements, is forced to contain a desirable subsequence. We also show that
the smallest n that satisfies Theorem 1.1 is between (k −1)(3k −4)/2 and 9(k −2)(k2 −k)2/4.
We call a sequence (a1, a2, . . . , ak) of positive integers 2-additive if a1 + a2 + · · · + ak = 2a1 or
2ak. Theorem 1.1 then says that, for any positive integer k ≥3, if n is large enough, then every
p ∈Sn has a 2-additive subsequence of length k. In general, we call a sequence (a1, a2, . . . , ak) of
positive integers ℓ-additive if a1 + a2 + · · · + ak = ℓa1 or ℓak. When k = ℓ, for any n, the identity
permutation (1, 2, . . . , n) does not have ℓ-additive subsequences of length k. When k = 3 and ℓ= 4
and when k = 4 and ℓ= 3, for any large enough n, every p ∈Sn has an ℓ-additive subsequence of
length k. See Section 4 for details about these cases and the exact thresholds. The general case is
still open.
Question 1.2. Let k, ℓ≥4 be positive integers with k ̸= ℓ. Does there exist n ∈N such that every

we prove that the minimum number of ℓ-additive subsequences of length k contained in any p ∈Sn
is Ω(n/ log n) (treating k and ℓas constants).
Using purely combinatorial arguments, we also

is O(nk−1) (treating k and ℓas constants). In the previous two sentences, standard asymptotic
notation was used: for functions f(n) and g(n), f(n) = Ω(g(n)) if there exist constants N and c
such that |f(n)| ≥c|g(n)| for all n ≥N; and f(n) = O(g(n)) if there exist constants N′ and C
such that |f(n)| ≤C|g(n)| for all n ≥N′.
The difference between additive subsequences and monotone 3-APs in permutations is not sim-
ply due to the fact that monotone 3-APs are required to be monotone. We show that Theorem 1.1
still holds for k = 3 when the 2-additive subsequence is required to be monotone.
Theorem 1.3. If n ≥18, then every p ∈Sn has a monotone 2-additive subsequence of length three.
In Theorem 1.3, 18 is the smallest n possible because there exists a permutation of {1, 2, . . . , 17}
which does not have monotone 2-additive subsequences of length three. Whether similar results
hold for monotone 2-additive subsequences of length greater than three is an open question.
Question 1.4. Is it true that, for any positive integer k ≥4, there exists n such that every p ∈Sn
contains a monotone 2-additive subsequence of length k?

Since the Erd˝os–Szekeres theorem [9] implies that every permutation of {1, 2, . . . , (k −1)2 + 1}
has a monotone subsequence of length k, a positive answer to Theorem 1.4 would be a generalization
of the Erd˝os–Szekeres theorem.
Theorems 1.1 and 1.3 can be used to obtain similar results for subsequence products and inverse
sums of subsequences. Using well-known arguments in arithmetic Ramsey theory, we show that for
any k, if n is large enough, then every p ∈Sn has a subsequence of length k whose product is either
the square of the first term or the square of the last term; and for any k, if n is large enough, then
every p ∈Sn has a subsequence (x1, x2, . . . , xk) such that 1/x1 + 1/x2 + . . . + 1/xk = 2/x1 or 2/xk.
When the subsequences are required to be monotone, both results hold for the case k = 3.
We note that arithmetic progressions behave differently in permutations of all positive integers.
For any positive integer k ≥3, we call a monotone sequence (x1, x2, . . . , xk) of length k a monotone
k-AP if there exists a positive integer d such that xi = xi−1 + d for all i ∈{2, 3, . . . , k} or xi =
xi+1 + d for all i ∈{1, 2, . . . , k −1}. Davis, Entringer, Graham, and Simmons [5] showed that
every permutation of the positive integers has a monotone 3-AP, while there exist permutations
of the positive integers without monotone 5-APs. However, despite some recent effort [1, 12, 20],
we still do not know whether there exists a permutation of the positive integers without monotone
4-APs. We also note that there are other studies on arithmetic progressions in permutations, such
as arithmetic progressions in random permutations [13], arithmetic progressions in permutations
of rational and real numbers [3], and permutations such that both the terms and the indices avoid
arithmetic progressions [6, 15, 16, 17, 24].
This paper is organized as follows. Some notation and preliminary results are presented in
Section 2. We prove Theorem 1.1 in Section 3 and provide polynomial bounds for the smallest n
such that every p ∈Sn has a 2-additive subsequence of length k. In Section 4, we study general
ℓ-additive subsequences and provide results for two easiest cases not covered by Theorem 1.1 and,
under the assumption that every p ∈Sn has an ℓ-additive subsequence, bounds for the minimum
number of ℓ-additive subsequences. In Section 5, using standard case analysis, we show that 18
is the smallest n such that every p ∈Sn has a monotone 2-additive subsequence of length three.
We also provide strong bounds for the minimum number of monotone 2-additive subsequence of
length three. In Section 6, we show similar results for subsequence products and inverse sums of
subsequences. Finally, we discuss possible future directions in Section 7.

Preliminaries
We first introduce some terminology which will be used in our proofs.
Definition 2.1. For all p = (p1, p2, . . . , pn) ∈Sn and s ∈{1, 2, . . . , n}, if pi = s, then we define
Lp(s) = {pj : j < i} and Rp(s) = {pj : j > i}.
That is, Lp(s) consists of all the terms to the left of s and Rp(s) consists of all the terms to the
right of s when we arrange the terms of p on a horizontal line. For example, for the permutation
p = (5, 1, 4, 3, 2) ∈S5, we have Lp(4) = {1, 5} and Rp(1) = {2, 3, 4}.
Definition 2.2. Let n ∈N, A ⊆{1, 2, . . . , n}, and p ∈Sn. The subpermutation of p on A is a
sequence obtained by deleting all the terms of p which are not in A, but keeping the relative order
of the terms that are in A.
For example, (5, 1, 3) is a subpermutation of (5, 1, 4, 3, 2) on {1, 3, 5}. If q is a subpermutation
of p on A, then we will simply call q a subpermutation on A when there is no confusion.
Now we state two simple observations which will be used to simplify our proofs.

Concluding Remarks
In this paper, we proved the existence of several subsequences with certain arithmetic properties in
large enough permutations. Any answer to Theorem 1.2 and/or Theorem 1.4 would provide more
insights on the behavior of sums of subsequences in permutations. Another possible direction is
to study weighted subsequence sums; that is, c1x1 + c2x2 + · · · + ckxk where (x1, x2, . . . , xk) is a
sequence of length k and c1, c2, . . . , ck are constants.

In Theorems 3.3 and 3.4, we provided polynomial bounds for the smallest n such that every
p ∈Sn has a 2-additive subsequence of length k. When k = 3, we showed that the smallest n is
5 in Theorem 3.5. This fact and Theorem 6.1 together imply that the smallest n such that every
p ∈Sn has a 2-multiplicative subsequence of length three is at most 25 = 32. In fact, 32 is the
smallest n such that every p ∈Sn has a 2-multiplicative subsequence of length three because
(1, 5, 10, 20, 7, 14, 28, 2, 26, 13, 22, 11, 21, 30, 16, 15, 8, 4, 24, 12, 6, 18, 3, 27, 9, 17, 19, 23, 25, 29, 31)
is a permutation of {1, 2, . . . , 31} which does not have 2-multiplicative subsequences of length
three. We have a similar result for 2-inverse-additive subsequences of length three. Theorem 3.5
and Theorem 6.4 together imply that the smallest n such that every p ∈Sn has a 2-inverse-additive
subsequence of length three is at most 60 (60 is the least common multiple of {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}); and,
on the other hand, if p ∈S59 such that the subpermutation of p on
{2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20, 21, 24, 27, 28, 30, 35, 36, 40, 42, 45, 48, 54, 56}
is
(27, 21, 14, 56, 45, 24, 18, 42, 15, 6, 10, 2, 7, 8, 40, 5, 4, 20, 3, 16, 12, 30, 9, 36, 48, 28, 35, 54),
then p does not have 2-inverse-additive subsequences of length three. It would be interesting to
study whether, as k approaches infinity, these general thresholds of n for multiplicative subsequences
and inverse-additive subsequences are significantly smaller than the upper bounds suggested by
Theorems 6.1 and 6.4. The work of Brown and R¨odl [4] and the first author [11] provided affirmative
answers to the counterpart of inverse-additive subsequences in arithmetic Ramsey theory; while it
is known that the counterpart of multiplicative subsequences in arithmetic Ramsey theory behaves
differently [2, Section 6].
