Abstract
Tropical cyclone (TC) trajectories are governed by large-scale steering flows with sensitive
dependence on initial conditions, raising the question of whether targeted perturbations can
induce track deviations. We demonstrate a case study within the Weather Jiu-Jitsu frame-
work, which exploits dynamical sensitivity at critical junctures to nudge extreme events, by
combining finite-time Lyapunov exponent (FTLE) diagnostics with idealized thermodynamic
perturbations in an AI weather forecast model. Applied to Hurricane Sandy (2012) six days
before landfall, FTLE-guided perturbations in the steering environment produce a track dis-
placement of 322.9 km, 3.3× larger than randomly placed perturbations of equal seeding en-
ergy (mean 97.6 km). The response is spatially specific: FTLE-guided sites in Sandy’s deep-
Caribbean steering environment produce a persistent positional offset of 36–50 km during the
first five forecast days, which is catastrophically amplified when Sandy recurves into the mid-
latitude baroclinic zone at day 5. Three control experiments—random placement (97.6 km),
backward FTLE targeting the subtropical ridge (91.5 km), and forward FTLE computed at
700 hPa within the TC warm core (55.0 km, below the random mean)—collectively suggest
that the larger response is associated with targeting the 500 hPa environmental steering-flow
boundaries using forward FTLE guidance. A supplementary experiment applying FTLE-guided
perturbations in the North Pacific 1 day before Sandy’s recurvature, when the storm has en-
tered the trough-bifurcation zone, produces 616.6 km, nearly double the Caribbean result,
demonstrating that trajectory sensitivity concentrates at the recurvature gate rather than be-
ing distributed across the 7-day forecast window. The required perturbation magnitude ex-
ceeds current operational engineering capabilities, so results represent a theoretical sensitiv-
ity analysis. These findings suggest that FTLE diagnostics may help identify flow-partition
boundaries within the TC steering environment where perturbations are associated with larger
downstream trajectory sensitivity.
1 Introduction
Tropical cyclones (TCs) cause disproportionate societal impacts relative to their frequency,
with track errors of even 100-200 km often determining whether a storm makes catastrophic
landfall or remains offshore. Hurricane Sandy (2012) exemplifies this sensitivity: a complex
interaction between Sandy’s tropical circulation, a deepening mid-latitude trough, and a Green-
land blocking anticyclone produced an unusual westward turn toward New Jersey, causing
$65 billion in damage and 233 deaths (Blake et al., 2013; Galarneau et al., 2013). This sen-
sitivity raises a fundamental question: can targeted perturbations to the large-scale atmo-
spheric circulation influence TC trajectories?
Early efforts to modify TCs, such as Project Stormfury, focused on disrupting inner-
core dynamics through cloud seeding (Willoughby et al., 1985), but these approaches proved
ineffective due to the rapid dynamical adjustment of the vortex. An alternative strategy is
to target the environmental steering flow, which governs TC motion at synoptic scales (George
and Gray, 1976). This perspective is grounded in the theory of sensitive dependence on ini-
tial conditions: small perturbations introduced at dynamically sensitive locations can grow
nonlinearly and influence large-scale flow evolution (Lorenz, 1963, 1969), with sensitivity con-
centrated at particular positions within the evolving flow rather than distributed uniformly.
Rather than opposing atmospheric dynamics, this approach exploits dynamical sensitivity at
critical junctures — the mid-latitude recurvature zone where TC tracks are highly sensitive
to upstream positioning — a concept we refer to as Weather Jiu-Jitsu (Huang et al., 2025).

Recent advances in low-order dynamical systems demonstrate that perturbations ap-
plied during positive-Lyapunov regimes can induce controlled trajectory deviations with min-
imal energy input (Liu et al., 2025a,b). In the full atmospheric system, finite-time Lyapunov
exponents (FTLEs) provide a practical diagnostic of flow structure by identifying Lagrangian
coherent structures that organize transport and delineate dynamical boundaries between flow
regimes (Haller and Yuan, 2000; Shadden et al., 2005; Haller, 2015; Garaboa-Paz et al., 2015).
This suggests a testable hypothesis: thermodynamic perturbations placed at FTLE-identified
locations within the TC steering environment should produce larger and more systematic track
deviations than perturbations applied elsewhere.
AI-based weather forecast models make such sensitivity experiments computationally
tractable. Recent models such as Pangu-Weather, GraphCast, GenCast, and Aurora demon-
strate skillful medium-range forecasts and enable rapid perturbation experiments (Bi et al.,
2023; Lam et al., 2023; Price et al., 2025; Bodnar et al., 2025). In parallel, emerging work shows
that sensitivity can be systematically exploited to probe extreme-event dynamics across both
AI-based and data-driven control frameworks (Hakim and Agrawal, 2026; Sawada et al., 2026;
Zhou et al., 2026). Peng et al. (2026) constructed a typhoon sensitivity testbed using the Pangu
model, applying wind perturbations of order 100 m s−1 to map track response as a function
of perturbation location and magnitude, confirming that AI-based models respond to imposed
forcing but also underscoring the large gap between numerically feasible perturbation ampli-
tudes and physically realizable interventions. Here, we use the Aurora model that has been
shown to have weather forecast skill superior to other AI-based models and matching pre-
dictability to physics-based models (Bodnar et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026) to investigate
whether FTLE-guided perturbations can influence TC trajectories in a high-dimensional, data-
driven forecasting system.
We apply this framework to Hurricane Sandy as a case study and address three ques-
tions: (1) Do FTLE-guided thermodynamic perturbations produce substantial TC track de-
viations? (2) Does FTLE-based site selection provide a systematic advantage over random
placement? (3) What physical mechanisms and atmospheric layers govern the response?
2 Methods
2.1 Aurora AI Weather Forecast Model
We use the Aurora 0.25◦deterministic model (Bodnar et al., 2025), a 1.3-billion-parameter
3D Swin Transformer trained on ERA5 and related Earth system datasets. The model op-
erates on a 721×1440 global grid with 13 pressure levels (1000–50 hPa) and a 6-hour timestep,
initialized from two consecutive ERA5 states (European Centre for Medium-Range Weather
Forecasts, 2019). Atmospheric state variables include zonal and meridional wind (u, v), tem-
perature (T), specific humidity (q), and geopotential (Z) at each pressure level; surface vari-
ables include mean sea-level pressure, 2-m temperature, and 10-m winds. Thermodynamic
perturbations are injected by modifying T and q fields in the initial batch at t = 0; Aurora
then propagates the perturbed state autoregressively through 168 hours without further in-
tervention.
As a data-driven forecasting system, Aurora does not explicitly enforce physical con-
servation laws. Perturbation responses are therefore interpreted as sensitivity experiments
within a learned dynamical system rather than strictly physical atmospheric trajectories. In
addition, the imposed perturbations may exceed the range of mesoscale anomalies represented

Its demonstrated short-range skill for TC track prediction justifies its use for probing steering-

required to capture environmental rather than vortex-internal Lagrangian structure.
The integration window τ = 48 h represents the time over which particle trajectories
are advected to compute separation rates; the FTLE field is evaluated at initialization (2012-
10-23 00Z) using ERA5 winds, and the perturbation is applied at that same moment—not
48 h in the future. In the convergent tropical Atlantic, FTLE values are uniformly negative
(−3.44 to −0.71 day−1); we target relative maxima at the 85th percentile (threshold: −1.51
day−1) as the least-convergent zones. These relative maxima delineate boundaries between
converging flow arms in Sandy’s Caribbean steering environment: locations where the dom-
inant steering-flow branch is most divided and where modest perturbations may preferentially
influence which steering-flow branch dominates downstream evolution. Positive FTLE val-
ues (Λ > 0) appear only in the mid-latitude jet stream (30–60◦N), where large-scale baro-
clinic instability supports genuinely divergent particle trajectories; these regions become dy-
namically relevant later in the forecast as Sandy recurves northward (see Section 4). Because
the tropical Atlantic flow over the chosen 48-hour integration window is uniformly conver-
gent, the negative FTLE structures identified here should not be interpreted as locally un-
stable manifolds supporting exponential perturbation growth. Instead, the FTLE field is used
heuristically as a diagnostic of flow organization, where relative maxima indicate boundaries
between competing steering-flow pathways. The downstream amplification observed in Sandy’s
recurvature phase therefore arises primarily from sensitivity of the later midlatitude inter-
action rather than from local exponential growth within the tropical steering region itself.

3 Results
3.1 Sensitivity to Perturbation Magnitude and Structure
Track deviation exhibits strong nonlinear dependence on perturbation magnitude. In-
creasing the perturbation radius from 10 km (0 km deviation) to 100 km (18.2 km) to 300 km
(∼323 km) reveals a clear threshold behavior. A similar response is observed for freeze effi-
ciency: increasing η from 10% to 60% results in a disproportionate increase in track devia-
tion (9.1 to 322.9 km), with a transition occurring near η ≈20–25%.1
Sensitivity to vertical structure shows that perturbations applied at 925/850/700 hPa
produce similar deviations (322.9 km) to those applied at 700/500/300 hPa (322.6 km). Per-
turbations at 700 hPa alone recover most of the response (302.7 km), while the contribution
from 300 hPa is negligible (∆T = +1.1 K, RH = 76.3%). This is physically expected: at
300 hPa, temperatures fall below −30◦C and the saturation specific humidity is extremely
low (qsat ≈0.1 g kg−1), so even moderately high relative humidity yields little freezable mois-
ture and minimal latent heat release. Effective perturbation energy is concentrated in the moisture-
rich lower troposphere (925–700 hPa), which provides idealized diabatic perturbation frame-
work.

3.3 Atmospheric Field Evolution
Figure 5 shows the evolution of seeded–baseline atmospheric anomalies in ∆Z500, ∆T700,
and ∆|wind250| over the 168-hour forecast.
The perturbation initially appears as a localized warm anomaly (∼+3 K) at 700 hPa
confined to the seeding sites, with negligible large-scale response. By 72 hours, weak ∆Z500
anomalies appear over the western Atlantic, but the 250 hPa wind anomaly remains ≲0.02 m s−1

After +120 h, as Sandy recurves northward, both ∆Z500 and ∆|wind250| grow rapidly, reach-

states (see Section 4.2). This period coincides with the rapid growth in track deviation (Fig-

subtropical ridge sites lie outside Sandy’s direct low-level steering flow and cannot sustain the
initial offset to the recurvature gate.

connect to Sandy’s steering environment, we apply Exp 7 with seeding at three lead times:

cast (Table 1). Each lead variant independently recomputes the forward 500 hPa FTLE at
its own perturbation hour over the North Pacific domain (25–65◦N, 130–250◦E), selects three

The lead-1d case (+144 h, 2012-10-29 00Z) produces the largest deviation in this study:

yet the selected sites at 161–219◦E, 43–64◦N, 5,400–8,400 km from Sandy produce a 223 km

of deviation is opposite to Exp 1: whereas the Caribbean-seeded track deflects southeastward
into the open Atlantic, the lead-1d Pacific-seeded track shifts approximately 7.8◦longitude
(∼580 km) westward relative to the baseline, moving Sandy toward the continental interior
rather than offshore. This near-instantaneous amplification contrasts with the gradual 5-day
tropical-phase build-up in Exp 1 and is consistent with the recurvature-gate mechanism (Sec-
tion 4.1): with Sandy already at the trough-bifurcation point, a remote flow modification trig-
gers the gate directly, bypassing the propagation delay required in early-forecast Caribbean
seeding.
4 Discussion
4.1 Amplification Mechanism: Recurvature-Gate Sensitivity
Analysis of the step-by-step forecast evolution reveals a two-stage amplification mech-
anism. During days 1–5 (+0 to +120 h), the thermodynamic perturbation directly modifies
the low-level (925–700 hPa) inflow arms that steer Sandy northwestward through the Caribbean.
This produces a persistent but modest positional offset of 36–50 km; the 250 hPa wind anomaly
between the seeded and baseline runs remains ≲0.02 m s−1 throughout this phase, ruling out
significant energy propagation to the jet stream.

the subtropical-ridge placement of the backward FTLE sites (both established in Section 4.1)

the temporal dimension. The lead-1d and lead-3d results bracket the FTLE predictability hori-

ture gate directly, while zero influence results when applied three days prior, despite lead-3d

neither necessary nor sufficient; the operative constraint is the finite time available for per-
turbation information to propagate to the gate. Taken together, Exp 1 and Exp 7 indicate
that recurvature-zone sensitivity is both spatial, concentrated at FTLE-identified steering-
flow boundaries, and temporal, peaking in the final 24 hours before trough capture. An in-
tervention targeting the recurvature window directly, rather than the tropical steering envi-
ronment at 5–7 day lead, could therefore achieve comparable deviations within a shorter cam-
paign window.
4.2 FTLE Advantage and Spatial Specificity
The sensitivity experiments reveal a threshold-controlled response in which perturba-
tions must exceed a critical amplitude in both spatial extent and efficiency to trigger large-
scale trajectory divergence. This threshold behavior is consistent with minimum-energy con-
trol regimes identified in low-order Lorenz systems (Liu et al., 2025a), where perturbations
must reach a critical size before the system crosses a bifurcation. In our experiments, the op-
erative bifurcation is not driven by local exponential perturbation growth diagnosed by FTLE
within the tropical steering region, where all FTLE values are negative and the flow remains
convergent. Instead, the dominant amplification mechanism arises from Sandy’s later inter-
action with the highly sensitive midlatitude recurvature regime described in Section 4.2.
Forward FTLE guidance provides a 3.3× advantage over random placement, but the
mechanism is spatial specificity rather than local perturbation growth. The initial track off-
set of 36–50 km is maintained by the proximity of the forward FTLE sites to Sandy’s active
low-level steering flow (925–700 hPa); the perturbation directly modifies the dominant inflow

the jet stream.
The backward FTLE control experiment (Exp 4) isolates the spatial specificity of this
advantage. Backward FTLE selects sites at 22–23◦N (subtropical ridge), which are outside
Sandy’s direct steering flow envelope; the result (91.5 km) is indistinguishable from the ran-
dom mean (97.6 km). Together, the random and backward-FTLE controls confirm that the
larger response associated with FTLE-guided placement appears to arise from perturbations
being located within Sandy’s active steering-flow envelope.
A third control experiment tested whether the FTLE vertical level matters. Comput-
ing FTLE at 700 hPa (the dominant seeding level) instead of 500 hPa selects sites at 3–17◦N
in the eastern Caribbean, where the 700 hPa flow is dominated by Sandy’s warm-core vor-
tex circulation rather than the environmental steering flow. The resulting track deviation is
only 55.0 km, 83% less than the 500 hPa result and, notably, lower than the random mean
(97.6 km). This occurs because the 700 hPa FTLE identifies boundaries within Sandy’s own
circulation rather than the environmental steering flow boundaries, directing energy to re-
gions swept into the TC’s outflow rather than the inflow arms that govern TC motion. The
500 hPa level, which lies above the TC warm core and correctly resolves environmental La-
grangian coherent structures (George and Gray, 1976), is therefore not an arbitrary choice
but an empirically validated necessity for TC applications. The three control experiments,
random placement (97.6 km), backward FTLE (91.5 km), and 700 hPa FTLE (55.0 km), col-
lectively suggest that the larger response depends on both the temporal direction and ver-
tical level used in the FTLE framework
4.3 Comparison with Independent Approaches
Our results provide a complementary perspective to recent studies of atmospheric sen-
sitivity by demonstrating that perturbations applied to the environmental steering flow can
induce substantial TC track deviations when placed within the TC’s active low-level steer-
ing environment. Hakim and Agrawal (2026) showed that optimized perturbations can pro-
duce substantial changes in cyclone evolution, highlighting the sensitivity of large-scale at-
mospheric flow. Sawada et al. (2026) used Ensemble Kalman Control to identify small-scale
perturbations that influence TC intensity through modifications to moisture and convection
near the storm. In contrast to approaches targeting inner-core dynamics, our results high-
light the effectiveness of modifying the large-scale steering environment, which avoids rapid
vortex adjustment. Similar sensitivity-maximizing behavior has been observed in CNOP-based
studies (Zhou et al., 2026), which identify perturbations that undergo the fastest nonlinear
growth in dynamical systems. In a complementary upstream approach, kwon (2025) tested
Gaussian temperature perturbations (∆T ≈2.5 K, σ = 8◦, levels 700–925 hPa) at 50 grid
points spanning the Pacific jet stream (27.5–57.5◦N, 125–250◦E), initialized one day later (2012-
10-24). Without FTLE guidance, this systematic survey shows minimal impact on Sandy’s
track on average (≲30 km), with a maximum single-site deviation of ∼160 km and modest
changes in recurvature timing. Together, these results indicate that Pacific jet perturbations
of physically realistic magnitude cannot efficiently teleconnect to Sandy’s steering environ-
ment within the 7-day forecast window, reinforcing that the effective perturbation region is
the deep-Caribbean low-level steering flow identified by the forward FTLE.

4.4 Real-World Feasibility Gap
At η = 60% over 300 km radius area, our perturbation exceeds current cloud seeding
capability: operational AgI campaigns achieve 5–15% precipitation enhancement over 1,000–
10,000 km2 (World Meteorological Organization, 2018; Cooper et al., 1997). At the near-realistic
η = 10%, the track deviation collapses to 9.1 km. The nonlinear threshold (η ≈20–25%)
lies well above real-world seeding efficiency, meaning the current technology falls on the wrong
side of the bifurcation.
These results establish the theoretical sensitivity upper bound and validate the FTLE
targeting principle, but they do not constitute a proposal for operational TC modification.
Whether emerging aerosol delivery systems, directed energy, or other technologies could ap-
proach FTLE-optimal scales is an open question (Huang et al., 2025). The same feasibility
gap is reported by Liu et al. (2026) for atmospheric river modification. A further structural
limitation of the present experiments is the single-impulse design: the perturbation is applied
once at t = 0 and propagated passively by Aurora’s autoregressive dynamics.
The present results of this study do not demonstrate deterministic controllability of trop-
ical cyclones, but rather indicate that certain steering-flow regions within Aurora’s learned
dynamics exhibit unusually high downstream trajectory sensitivity during Sandy’s recurva-
ture evolution. In practice, a sustained thermodynamic modification of Sandy’s steering en-
vironment would require repeated interventions over several days as FTLE-identified bound-
aries evolve with the forecast. Even at near-realistic seeding efficiencies (η ≈10%), an it-
erative strategy, applying multiple smaller perturbations at FTLE-refreshed sites across days 1–
3, could potentially compound small positional offsets without requiring the large single-impulse
magnitude demonstrated here. Designing and evaluating such iterative FTLE-guided cam-
paigns represents a natural and necessary extension of this work toward operational relevance.
5 Conclusions
We show that forward FTLE diagnostics at the environmental steering level (500 hPa)
identify steering-sensitive regions in Hurricane Sandy’s Caribbean steering environment where
idealized thermodynamic perturbations produce a 322.9 km track deviation—3.3× larger than
randomly placed perturbations of equal energy. The response follows a two-stage mechanism:
FTLE-guided seeding creates a modest but persistent 36–50 km positional offset during days 1–
5 by modifying Sandy’s active low-level steering-flow boundaries, which is then catastroph-
ically amplified when Sandy recurves into the mid-latitude baroclinic zone at day 5. Three
control experiments (random placement, backward FTLE, and 700 hPa FTLE level) collec-
tively confirm that this advantage requires both the correct temporal direction and the cor-
rect vertical level simultaneously; FTLE must target the environmental steering flow, not Sandy’s
inner circulation or the adjacent subtropical ridge. A supplementary Pacific delayed pertur-
bation experiment confirms that this sensitivity is equally concentrated in time: a perturba-
tion applied 1 day before recurvature, when Sandy has entered the trough-bifurcation zone,
produces 616.6 km with near-instantaneous amplification, while perturbations 2–3 days ear-
lier yield 121.5 and 0 km as the propagation window widens beyond the FTLE horizon. The
required perturbation magnitude exceeds current cloud-seeding capability by ∼1000×; these
results establish the theoretical sensitivity upper bound for single-impulse steering-flow mod-
ification and suggest that FTLE diagnostics may help identify both the spatial location and
optimal timing window for maximum trajectory sensitivity in the modeled flow.
