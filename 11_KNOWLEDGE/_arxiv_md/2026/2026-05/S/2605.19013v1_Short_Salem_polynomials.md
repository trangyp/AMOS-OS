SHORT SALEM POLYNOMIALS
JAMES MCKEE AND CHRIS SMYTH
Abstract. We give a complete classification of all Salem polynomials of length 5. For
length 6 we show that all but finitely many Salem polynomials lie in one of 12 infinite
families, and subject to Lehmer’s Conjecture we give a complete list of the 126 exceptions.
We provide a table of short polynomials for all known Salem numbers below the smallest
Pisot number.
1. Introduction and statement of results
A Salem number is a real algebraic integer τ > 1 such that all of the Galois conjugates
of τ (excluding τ itself) have modulus at most 1, and with at least one having modulus
exactly 1. For more about these numbers see the surveys [8] and [18].
For a polynomial P(z) ∈Z[z] that has degree d, we define P ∗(z) = zdP(1/z); its
coefficients are those of P written in reverse order. We speak of P ∗as being the reciprocal of
P (its zeros are the reciprocals of the nonzero zeros of P). We call P reciprocal (respectively,
antireciprocal) if P ∗= P (respectively, P ∗= −P). The length of a polynomial is the sum of
the absolute values of its coefficients. We use Φd(z) for the d-th cyclotomic polynomial; this
is the minimal polynomial of ωd := e2πi/d. It will be convenient to use the term cyclotomic
polynomial more generally to mean any product of irreducible cyclotomic polynomials Φd,
allowing repeated factors, and allowing the empty product 1.
The minimal polynomial of a Salem number τ is reciprocal, of even degree at least 4,
and all but two of its zeros lie on the unit circle (the exceptions being τ and 1/τ). A Salem
polynomial is defined to be the minimal polynomial of a Salem number, possibly (but not
necessarily) multiplied by a cyclotomic polynomial. The smallest known Salem number
is Lehmer’s number, λ0 = 1.1762808 . . . , a zero of z12 −z7 −z6 −z5 + 1. This length-5
polynomial is not the minimal polynomial of λ0, but is a multiple of its minimal polynomial
by the cyclotomic polynomial z2 −z +1. It is the shortest possible multiple of the minimal
polynomial of λ0 by a cyclotomic polynomial, and we say that λ0 has shortness 5, with
z12 −z7 −z6 −z5 + 1 being a short polynomial for λ0 (there is one other short polynomial
for λ0, namely z14 −z11 −z7 −z3 + 1).
1.1. Salem polynomials of shortness 5. It is easy to see that if a monic integer poly-
nomial is reciprocal and has length at most 4, then it is cyclotomic. Thus the shortness of
a Salem number is at least 5 (with λ0 illustrating that 5 is possible).
In this paper we give a complete list of all shortness-5 Salem numbers (Theorem 1).

this would be the case if, as is strongly suspected, the conjecture that λ0 is the smallest

Proposition 2.5. Let P(z) be an irreducible Pisot polynomial that is not equal to its
reciprocal polynomial, and Q(z) = P ∗(z). Take ε = ±1, and define, for n ∈N,
Pn(z) = znP(z) + εQ(z) .
Let r = (Q′(1) −P ′(1))/P(1), and let θ be the Pisot number that is a zero of P(z). Then:
(i) Pn(z) has at most one zero outside the unit circle. If there is one, it is in the real
interval (1, ∞).
(ii) If ε = 1, then Pn(z) has a unique simple zero ρn ∈(1, ∞), and all other zeros have
modulus ≤1.
(iii) If ε = −1 and n < r, then Pn(z) has all its zeros on the unit circle.
(iv) If ε = −1 and n > r, then Pn(z) has a unique simple zero ρn ∈(1, ∞), and all
other zeros have modulus ≤1.
(v) There exists n0 such that Pn(z) is a cyclotomic polynomial if n < n0, and such that
Pn(z) has a unique zero ρn in (1, ∞) if n ≥n0 (and ρn is either a Pisot number or a
Salem number). In the latter case, Pn(z) is the minimal polynomial of ρn multiplied
by a cyclotomic polynomial.
(vi) If εQ(θ) > 0 then {ρn}n≥n0 is a strictly increasing sequence. If εQ(θ) < 0 then
{ρn}n≥n0 is a strictly decreasing sequence. In either case, ρn →θ as n →∞.
(vii) There is a finite set of n for which Pn(z) has a repeated factor.
(viii) There is a finite set of d for which there exists n such that Φd(z) | Pn(z). If for
some nd we have Φd(z) | Pnd(z), then Φd(z) | Pn(z) if and only if n ≡nd (mod d).
(ix) There is a finite set of n for which ρn is a Pisot number; for all sufficiently large
n, ρn is a Salem number.
Before proving all of this, we make some remarks. Salem’s argument from [14] gives
(i), (ii), the final sentence in (vi), and (ix). He also shows that according to the sign of
εQ(θ) the ρn all lie on one or other side of θ, revealing that there are Salem numbers
converging to θ from both sides.
Boyd [1] comments on the monotonicity of Salem’s
sequences, establishes (iii)–(v), and shows that any square-free Salem polynomial arises
via Salem’s construction. One can compute the finite sets mentioned in (vii)–(ix) using
the technique described in detail for one case in Section 5. The hypotheses require that
P is an irreducible Pisot polynomial. More generally, given a Pisot polynomial P, one
can compute R = gcd(P, P ∗); then P/R is an irreducible Pisot polynomial to which the
proposition applies, and the sequence of polynomials produced by P is simply the sequence
produced by P/R with all the polynomials in the sequence multiplied by R; part (vii) and
the final sentence of (viii) might not hold for the sequence of polynomials produced by P.
A stronger version of part (vii) is given as Lemma 2.1 in [4], drawing together some of the
remarks in [1]. We need only the weaker statement here, which has an easy proof. The
first half of part (viii) follows from [4, Lemma 2.2], but we give an alternative proof that
is related to the method we use later for finding the claimed finite set, and is considerably
simpler than the proof of Chinburg’s lemma. Note that our argument for the proof of (ix)
also leads to a simpler proof of [4, Theorem 1].

Boyd [1], who also found four more soon afterwards [2]. Mossinghoff [12] found a further
four. These 47 Salem numbers, all less than 1.3, are collected in Mossinghoff’s table [11]
(five of them are in the sequence {τn}). Recently Sac-´Ep´ee [13] extended the search for
Salem numbers, finding 11 more Salem numbers between 1.3 and 49/37 > θ0 −0.0004 that
are not members of the sequence {τn}. Thus to the best of our current knowledge, the
Salem numbers less than θ0 consist of the sequence {τn} and 53 ‘sporadic’ ones σ1, . . . , σ53.
In our table we present the defining polynomials of the Salem numbers in ‘short’ form,
rather than by giving their minimal polynomials. Where more than one such short poly-
nomial exists, we give the one of least degree. For those Salem numbers less than 1.3,
El-Serafy and McKee [7] found these polynomials; as part of the current project we ex-
tended this computation to Sac-´Ep´ee’s 11 Salem numbers. We observed that, while the
minimal polynomial of σ48, of shortness 13, in fact divides a length-12 reciprocal polyno-
mial, namely z80 −z79 −z77 + z74 + z63 + z57 −z23 −z17 −z6 + z3 + z −1, the quotient
is not cyclotomic. For n ≥18, the generic polynomial shown for τn is indeed the unique
short polynomial for τn. Any other short polynomial would either be one of the sporadic
