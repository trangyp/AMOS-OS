Semiorthogonal decompositions for stacks
Chenjing Bu
Tudor Pădurariu
Yukinobu Toda
Abstract
We give a systematic construction of semiorthogonal decompositions of de-
rived categories of coherent sheaves on quasi-smooth derived algebraic stacks
over C, where the summands are subcategories defined by weight conditions,
and the inclusion functors are given by parabolic induction. The summands are
indexed by the component lattice of the stack, a central combinatorial structure
in intrinsic Donaldson–Thomas theory. As examples, we obtain semiorthogonal
decompositions for moduli stacks of semistable 𝐺-bundles or 𝐺-Higgs bundles
on a curve, and moduli stacks of de Rham or Betti 𝐺-local systems on a curve,
for reductive groups 𝐺not necessarily of type A.

1.1.3. Next, we explain our main motivations, and we review related prior work.
Our main motivation for our construction comes from Donaldson–Thomas (DT) theory,
which studies, among other topics, numerical, cohomological, and categorical structures asso-
ciated to (−1)-shifted symplectic stacks defined by Pantev, Toën, Vaquié, and Vezzosi [74].
Originally, Donaldson–Thomas theory [25; 81] focused on virtual counts of ideal sheaves
on complex threefolds, called Donaldson–Thomas invariants, and their relationship to curve-
counting invariants as conjectured by Maulik, Nekrasov, Okounkov, and Pandharipande [58;
59]. Later, Joyce and Song [50] and Kontsevich and Soibelman [54] defined invariants which
are virtual counts of semistable sheaves on Calabi–Yau threefolds and, more broadly, of semi-
stable objects in 3-Calabi–Yau abelian categories. They also defined invariants in the presence
of strictly semistable objects, which allowed for advances in wall-crossing formulas for DT
invariants and in the study of cohomological Hall algebras [55]. Although these invariants
can be rational, they are related through multiple cover formulas to integer-valued invariants
known as BPS invariants, named after Bogomol’nyi–Prasad–Sommerfield. In the absence of
stricly semistable objects, BPS invariants coincide with DT invariants.
It is now understood that DT invariants can be defined for more general stacks, namely
for certain (−1)-shifted symplectic stacks, not necesarily moduli of objects in a 3-Calabi–Yau
category, thanks to the new framework of intrinsic Donaldson–Thomas theory, developed in
[17–19].
Further, since the works of Behrend [11], Kontsevich and Soibelman [55], and Ben-Bassat,
Brav, Bussi, and Joyce [13], it is understood that BPS invariants are the Euler characteristic
of graded vector spaces obtained as the cohomology of certain perverse sheaves. This is the
subject of study of cohomological Donaldson–Thomas theory. A central result in this subject is
cohomological integrality, proposed by Kontsevich and Soibelman [55] and proved in increas-
ing generality by Efimov [29], Davison and Meinhardt [23], and more recently in the intrinsic
DT theory framework in [16; 44]. Next, we briefly recall this result.
For a (−1)-shifted symplectic stack X satisfying certain assumptions, as in [16; 44], includ-
ing symmetricity, orientability, and the existence of a good moduli space in the sense of Alper
[2], one considers a perverse sheaf 𝜑X on X obtained by gluing vanishing cycles sheaves for
functions on smooth stacks whose critical loci are local models of the stack X, see [13]. In

1.1.4. The current paper is concerned with a further refinement of these enumerative invariants,
which is the subject of study of categorical Donaldson–Thomas theory initiated in [82]. We are
interested in categorical analogues of the critical cohomology, which might be called critical
categories, and in semiorthogonal decompositions of such categories, whose summands are
window subcategories called quasi-BPS categories [64–72].
Such decompositions may be viewed as categorical analogues of cohomological integrality.
However, this relationship is less direct than one might hope. For instance, if one applies an
additive invariant directly to our decomposition, such as periodic cyclic homology, one does
not expect to recover the cohomological integrality decomposition. There are two reasons for
this.
First, additive invariants of stacks, including periodic cyclic homology, do not directly re-
cover the Borel–Moore homology of the stack; this is already evident in the example of the
classifying stack ∗/Gm. Second, even after applying topological or algebraic 𝐾-theory to the
semiorthogonal decomposition, the resulting decomposition is not as refined as the decompos-
ition appearing in BPS cohomology. One expects that a finer decomposition should instead
be constructed directly in rational topological or algebraic 𝐾-theory, which does not originate
from a categorical decomposition. We do not pursue this direction further here, and refer the
reader to [67].
New phenomena appear in 𝐾-theoretic and categorical DT theory, not visible in cohomo-
logy, such as the dependence on an auxiliary parameter 𝛿as in (1.1.1.2), which controls the
positions of the windows. This also affects the size of quasi-BPS categories, which has the
effect that only some of these categories (those of the correct size) recover the two-periodic
version of the BPS cohomology; see [69] for details in the case of symmetric quivers with
potential.

symplectic stacks, although they are known in some cases. Important progress towards the
general case has been made in recent works of Hennion, Holstein, and Robalo [45; 46]. In this
paper, we restrict to the following two situations, where a definition of the critical category is
available:
• When X = Crit(𝑓) is the derived critical locus of a function 𝑓: U →A1 on a smooth
stack U, with no critical values other than 0, its critical category (using its canonical ori-

1.1.6. Semiorthogonal decompositions for derived categories of coherent sheaves on stacks
were first constructed by Ballard, Favero, and Katzarkov [10] and Halpern-Leistner [40; 41].
There, the summands are supported on strata of Θ-stratifications introduced by Halpern-
Leistner [41], a generalization of the Kempf–Ness stratification from geometric invariant the-
ory.
On the other hand, the semiorthogonal decompositions constructed in this paper are dif-
ferent, in the following ways. First, the functors from the summands are given by parabolic
induction, instead of by repeating the procedure of adding terms corresponding to one Θ-strata
at a time. Second, although both types of semiorthogonal decompositions depend on the data
of a rational line bundle ℓand a positive-definite quadratic form 𝑞, they play different roles.
In [10; 40; 41], ℓplays the role of a stability condition, whereas for us, this data is denoted
by 𝛿, and is used instead to determine the positions of the windows for window subcategories;
see also §1.2 below. Compare also with Halpern-Leistner and Sam [43, Theorem 3.2], where
both 𝛿and ℓappear and play their respective roles.
The type of semiorthogonal decompositions discussed in this paper were first construc-
ted by Špenko and Van den Bergh [80, Proposition 7.4] in the case of linear quotient stacks,
and they also obtained coarser decompositions [80, Theorem 1.1.2] for more general smooth
quotient stacks. The summands in their semiorthogonal decompositions are twisted non-
commutative resolutions of singularities constructed in their previous work [79].

1.2
Main results
We now explain in more detail the main results of this paper.
1.2.1. We start with a quasi-smooth derived algebraic stack X over C, and along with some
mild assumptions, we put two main restrictions on X:
Firstly, we assume that X is quasi-compact, has affine stabilizers, and has a good moduli
space. These conditions are typically satisfied by moduli stacks of semistable objects in a linear
category, and are verified for a large class of examples in the work of Alper, Halpern-Leistner,
and Heinloth [5].
Secondly, we assume that X is quasi-symmetric in the sense of §3.2.1, meaning roughly
that at each closed point 𝑥∈X, the tangent complex TX|𝑥has symmetric weights as a rep-
resentation of the automorphism group of 𝑥. This condition is satisfied by many interesting
moduli stacks, as previously discussed in [16].
We also require the following two pieces of extra data on X.
Firstly, we require a quadratic norm on graded points of X in the sense of §3.1.9, denoted
by 𝑞. Roughly speaking, this is a compatible way to choose a Weyl-invariant, positive-definite
quadratic form on the cocharacter lattice of the automorphism group of every closed point
𝑥∈X. In most of our examples, there is a natural choice of such a quadratic norm.
Secondly, we require a linear function 𝛿: CLQ(X) →Q, where CLQ(X) is the rationalized
component lattice of X. This is used to determine the centre of the windows used to define
window subcategories.
The component lattice CLQ(X) can be seen as glued from the quotients (Λ𝑇⊗Q)/𝑊of the
rational cocharacter lattice Λ𝑇of the maximal torus𝑇by the Weyl group𝑊of automorphism
groups of closed points 𝑥∈X, and so 𝛿is a compatible way to choose a rational character of
the automorphism group of each closed point 𝑥∈X. In particular, every rational line bundle
on X defines such a linear function 𝛿.

1.2.7. Finally, we mention a few important questions which we do not address in this paper,
but which we hope to address in future work. First, we do not compare the topological 𝐾-
theory or periodic cyclic homology of quasi-BPS categories with the BPS cohomology of [16].
Second, we do not study the structure of quasi-BPS categories in more detail. For example, we
do not discuss when they are non-commutative resolutions of singularities of a relevant good
moduli space, or when they are equivalent to categories of coherent sheaves on semistable
open substacks of X. For the latter, see [10; 40; 41]. Third, it would be interesting to recover
at least some of the semiorthogonal decompositions of Ballard, Favero, and Katzarkov [10]
and Halpern-Leistner [40] from the semiorthogonal decomposition discussed in this paper in
examples where both methods are applicable.
1.2.8. This paper is organized as follows.
In §2, we prove the decomposition (1.2.2.1) for linear quotient stacks of the form 𝑉/𝐺,
where 𝐺is a connected reductive group and 𝑉is a quasi-symmetric 𝐺-representation.
In §3, we extend this decomposition to the case of smooth stacks with good moduli spaces,
using the intrinsic Donaldson–Thomas theory framework and a local-to-global argument, and
using the fact that such stacks are étale locally modelled on linear quotient stacks. After that,
in §3.5, we prove the decomposition (1.2.4.1) for matrix factorizations on smooth stacks.
In §4, we further extend the decomposition to the case of quasi-smooth derived stacks. We
deduce this from the decomposition (1.2.4.1) of matrix factorizations using a version of Koszul
duality, as in [82, Chapter 2], which relates matrix factorizations on a smooth stack to coherent
sheaves on the derived zero locus of a section of a vector bundle on a smooth stack. Using the
fact that quasi-smooth derived stacks with good moduli spaces are étale locally modelled on
such derived zero loci, we use a local-to-global argument similar to the smooth case to prove
the general versions of the decompositions (1.2.2.1) and (1.2.3.1).

useful technique is to use induction on the central rank of the stack, based on the idea that the
stack X𝜆is always ‘simpler’ than X measured by the central rank, except when it is the same
as X, and the induction allows us to we always assume that the property holds for all X𝜆that
are different from X.
Let X be a stack as in §3.1.4, and assume that X is connected.
An element 𝜆∈|CLQ(X)| is said to be central, if the forgetful morphism tot𝜆: X𝜆→X
is an isomorphism. By [17, Theorem 4.1.5], the locus of central elements in |CLQ(X)| is the
injective image of the maximal central face 𝛼0 : 𝐹0 →CLQ(X). There is also an integral
maximal central face Λ0 →CL(X), which can be similarly described.
The central rank of X, denoted crk(X), is defined as the dimension of the maximal central
face 𝛼0. Also, when X is quasi-compact, the rank of X, denoted rk(X), is defined as the
maximal dimension of a non-degenerate face of CLQ(X).
It follows from [17, Theorem 4.1.4] that for all 𝜆∈|CLQ(X)|, we have
crk(X) ⩽crk(X𝜆) ⩽rk(X𝜆) ⩽rk(X) ,
(3.1.6.1)
and the first inequality is strict unless 𝜆is central. Therefore, if we use the invariant rk(X) −
crk(X) to measure the complexity of X, then X𝜆is either strictly simpler than X (when 𝜆is
non-central), or isomorphic to X (when 𝜆is central).
