Scalarization of dark matter stars
Junya Tanaka1, ∗
1Independent Researcher, Tokyo, Japan
(Dated: May 2026)
We investigated scalarization in dark matter stars. In scalar–tensor theories, the coupling between
matter and a scalar ﬁeld generates an eﬀective mass, which is known to trigger the growth of the
scalar ﬁeld in stars and black holes (spontaneous scalarization). On the other hand, models in which
the scalar ﬁeld couples not only to baryonic matter but also to dark matter have been discussed in the
literature. Moreover, in models with self-interacting dark matter, it is known that dark matter can
form compact objects. Within the framework of scalar–tensor theories, we studied the parameter
ranges in which the growth of the scalar ﬁeld occurs in compact stars formed by self-interacting
dark matter. As a result, we found that scalarization occurs over most of the parameter region
with a negative coupling parameter βd. This parameter range is broader than that for scalarization
in conventional neutron stars. Since the dark matter parameters considered correspond to those
in which self-interacting dark matter resolves problems in galaxy formation, if dark matter stars
actually exist, they could serve as probes for investigating the relationship between the scalar ﬁeld
and dark matter.

INTRODUCTION
Scalar–tensor theory is an extension of general relativ-
ity (GR) in which scalar degrees of freedom are added to
the gravitational sector. Its general action is written in
the Jordan frame as follows [1]:

example, for Brans–Dicke theory, F = φ, Z = ω/φ, and

gravity and thus couples indirectly to matter.
On the other hand, the scalar ﬁeld is also suggested to
be the source of dark energy, which constitutes more than
70 percent of the energy content of the Universe. This
is modeled as quintessence [3]. If the scalar ﬁeld plays
a cosmological role in the evolution of the Universe, its
mass must be extremely small or nearly zero (of order H0,
the Hubble constant [4]). However, such a scalar ﬁeld is
ruled out by equivalence principle experiments and con-
straints on ﬁfth forces on Earth. This implies that the
scalar ﬁeld must be massive in terrestrial environments.
To resolve this apparent contradiction, various screen-
ing mechanisms for scalar ﬁelds, such as the chameleon
mechanism [4, 5], have been proposed.
However, these observational constraints mainly apply
to scalarization associated with baryonic matter. In the
current cosmological scenario, the existence of dark mat-
ter is indispensable for the formation of galaxies and the
large-scale structure of the Universe [6–11]. If dark mat-
ter is composed of particle-like matter, it may interact

with a scalar ﬁeld. In that case, the coupling to dark mat-

Introducing such interactions is rather reasonable from

There are also studies on trends outside of cosmology.
Dark matter stars coupled to scalar ﬁelds have been stud-

stars [15]. In this model, the scalar ﬁeld acts as an ad-
ditional force, deforming stellar structure and the mass-
radius relationship. In these models, dark sector inter-
actions deform the equilibrium state but do not induce
dynamic instabilities.
When the scalar ﬁeld couples to the trace of the en-
ergy–momentum tensor of matter or to curvature invari-
ants, an eﬀective mass is generated for the scalar ﬁeld.
Scalarization occurs when the square of this eﬀective
mass becomes negative. Scalarization refers to the phe-
nomenon in which the scalar ﬁeld takes a nontrivial con-
ﬁguration around objects such as neutron stars and black
holes, rather than a trivial one. At the linear level of in-
stability, this appears as a tachyonic instability, while the
ﬁnal state is determined by nonlinear eﬀects. Scalariza-
tion occurs only in regions where the scalar ﬁeld strongly
couples to matter or curvature, namely in strong-ﬁeld re-
gions. Scalarization was ﬁrst proposed for neutron stars
by Damour and Esposito-Far`ese [19, 20].
Since then,
scalarization of neutron stars has been studied in detail,
and the possibility of neutron-star scalarization in the
original DEF model has been almost ruled out [21]. How-
ever, the possibility of scalarization induced by structures
composed of dark matter still remains.
Self-interacting dark matter was proposed to resolve
the tensions between collisionless cold dark matter and
astrophysical observations [22–24] (such as the cuspy core
problem, the satellite galaxy problem, and the “too big

CONCLUSION
The relationship between the scalar ﬁeld and dark mat-
ter remains largely unknown. However, if dark matter
consists of massive elementary particles, it should inter-
act with scalar ﬁelds at least through gravity. The inter-
action between the scalar ﬁeld and dark matter may help

to circumvent some of the issues inherent in scalar-tensor
theories and provide insights into cosmological evolution.
Since dark matter interacts minimally with known mat-
ter, exploring the formalism of dark matter–scalar inter-
actions could oﬀer a clue to the dark sector.
In this work, we assumed a scalar-tensor theory frame-
work and considered the weak interaction between scalar
ﬁelds and dark matter, investigating whether scalariza-
tion occurs in dark matter stars.
We found that in
the parameter range where self-interacting dark mat-
ter is cosmologically relevant, dark matter stars undergo
scalarization for most of the negative coupling constant
range.
Given that observations of pulsars have nearly
closed the window for scalarization in neutron stars,
dark matter stars could reopen the pathway for matter-
coupling–induced scalarization.
On the other hand, it
would be diﬃcult to ﬁnd dark matter stars exhibiting
scalarization in the positive coupling constant range, as
such stars are likely unstable.
Moreover, in the negative coupling constant region, the
growth rate of the instability depends on the star’s com-
pactness and tends to be larger for less massive stars.
This mass range is close to the typical mass of neutron
stars. Dark matter stars are sometimes considered as al-
ternative compact objects to neutron stars; if the scalar
ﬁeld can be observed, they could provide a way to dis-
tinguish between dark matter stars and neutron stars of
similar mass.
