Abstract. Description logics (DL) are a family of formal languages for
representing and reasoning about structured knowledge in terms of con-
cepts and their relationships. A central reasoning problem in DL is con-
cept subsumption. Although this problem has been widely studied, im-
portant open problems remain for certain logics. The expressive power of
DLs depends on the constructors available for building complex concepts.
In this work, we investigate subsumption in the restricted logic FL⊥reg
and its related fragments FLreg, FL⊥, and FL0. These logics support
value restrictions over role names, where the subscript ⊥denotes the
presence of the empty concept and reg denotes the use of regular expres-
sions over roles. None of these logics includes concept negation.
We show that deciding subsumption between two concept descriptions in
FL⊥reg and FLreg is PSpace-complete. When subsumption is considered
with respect to a TBox (i.e., a set of axioms), the complexity increases
to ExpTime-complete. Our results are obtained via a novel reduction to
parity pushdown games.

Introduction
Description logics constitute a family of formal languages designed to represent
structured knowledge in terms of concept hierarchies and the relationships be-
tween them. They provide a rigorous framework for describing classes of objects,
their properties, and the relations that hold among them. A central aim of de-
scription logics is to support precise concept definitions and to enable automated
reasoning about class membership and inter-concept relationships.
A fundamental reasoning task in this context is the decision problem of
whether a subsumption relation holds between two given concept descriptions.

If subsumption is decidable in a particular description logic, it becomes possi-
ble to compute a concept hierarchy, which greatly facilitates the maintenance,
consistency checking, and effective use of ontologies.
In this paper, we present novel algorithms for the subsumption problem in
certain restricted description logics, with potential applications in ontology main-
tenance.
Furthermore, it is often desirable to restrict the class of admissible interpre-
tations of an ontology to those that satisfy a set of general constraints, while
preserving the decidability of key reasoning tasks such as subsumption. These
constraints can be formalized as a finite set of axioms describing relationships
between concepts, known as a TBox.
The subsumption problem, both with and without TBoxes, has been exten-
sively studied across a broad spectrum of description logics.
A particular description logic is characterized by the set of constructors al-
lowed for concept definitions. Individual logics therefore differ in their expressive
power, depending on the constructors provided.
In this paper, we focus on description logics that admit only conjunction
(⊓), the top concept (⊤), value restrictions (∀r.C), and, optionally, the bottom
concept (⊥), interpreted as the empty set. These constructors are used to build
complex concept descriptions from a given set of atomic concept and role names.
The logic comprising the first three constructors is denoted by FL0, while the
extension including ⊥is denoted by FL⊥. If value restrictions are generalized to
regular expressions over the alphabet of role names, the resulting logic is denoted
by FLreg, and its extension with the bottom concept ⊥is denoted by FL⊥reg.
For instance, in FL0, one can describe the concept of an undergraduate
student as a student who attends only undergraduate courses by the equivalence
UndergraduateStudent ≡Student ⊓∀attends.UGC,
where UGC is a concept name denoting an undergraduate course, and attends is
a role name relating a person and a course they attend.
In FL⊥, one can express the fact that professors do not attend any courses
by the subsumption axiom
Professor ⊑∀attends.⊥.
In FLreg, one can describe the more complex concept of a professor whose
all siblings are professors, all siblings of those siblings are professors, and so on,
for any number of steps through the sibling relation:
∀sibling∗.Professor.

The approach to constructing the new algorithms is to establish an equiv-
alence between an instance of the subsumption problem with TBoxes and a
suitably defined parity pushdown game associated with the instance, and then
to apply existing techniques from game theory to solve it.
Parity pushdown games arise from the combination of pushdown systems,
that is, transition systems equipped with a stack, and parity games—two-player
infinite-duration games in which the winner is determined by the parity of the
priorities visited infinitely often. This framework has found widespread appli-
cation in the verification of recursive programs and pushdown models [1,14], as
well as in interprocedural static analysis frameworks based on weighted push-
down systems [12].
Solving parity pushdown games, that is, deciding the existence of a winning
strategy for one of the players, is ExpTime-complete [14]. A variant of pushdown
games has been used to establish ExpTime-completeness of the subsumption
problem in the description logic FL0 with TBoxes [9]. Motivated by this result,

The logic FL⊥is a syntactically limited fragment of FL⊥reg, where we are
not allowed to use value restrictions ∀E.C for arbitrary regular expressions E,
but only ∀r.C for single role names r.
The logic FLreg is obtained by syntactically limiting FL⊥reg in another way:
we forbid the use of ⊥.
In order to obtain the logic FL0, one needs to apply both limitations: value
restrictions are limited to ∀r.C, and ⊥cannot be used.
The notions of subsumption and a TBox are defined uniformly for all de-
scription logics introduced above. In each case, concept descriptions are formed
according to the given logic, and interpretations assign meaning to the construc-
tors as specified.
Definition 2.1. A subsumption between concept descriptions C and D is writ-
ten C ⊑D, and we also say that C is subsumed by D. Such a subsumption holds
in an interpretation I if CI ⊆DI. When no interpretation is specified, C ⊑D
means that CI ⊆DI holds for all interpretations I. Concept descriptions C and
D are equivalent (written C ≡D) if C ⊑D and D ⊑C.
A TBox T is a finite set of axioms of the form C ⊑D, where C and D
are concept descriptions. Such axioms express inclusion (implication) between
concepts, stating that every instance of C is also an instance of D. For example,
Human ⊑Animal states that every human is an animal. Sometimes one also
allows axioms of the form A ≡B, expressing equivalence of two concepts. Such
axioms can be split into two subsumptions, A ⊑B and B ⊑A, hence we assume
that they are not present.
Subsumption can be relativized to a TBox as follows.
Definition 2.2. Let T be a TBox and let C, D be concept descriptions.
– An interpretation I is a model of T if all axioms (E ⊑F) ∈T hold in I.
– C is subsumed by D with respect to T (written C ⊑T D) if C ⊑D holds in
every model I of T .
The subsumption problem in the considered logic with TBoxes is defined as
follows:
– Input: a TBox T and concept descriptions C, D.
– Output: Yes, if the subsumption C ⊑T D holds, and No otherwise.
An instance of this problem is denoted C ⊑?
T D, with the question mark in-
dicating that the subsumption is the goal to be determined. We refer to such
expressions as goal subsumptions.
If the TBox is absent (empty), we talk about the subsumption problem with-
out TBoxes, with instances of the form C ⊑? D.

Subsumption in FLreg and FL⊥reg without TBoxes
In this section we solve the subsumption problem in FLreg and in FL⊥reg with-
out TBoxes.
Theorem 4.1. The following two problems are PSpace-complete:
– the subsumption problem in FLreg without TBoxes, and
– the subsumption problem in FL⊥reg without TBoxes.
Consider an instance C ⊑? D of the subsumption problem. As already men-
tioned, we may assume that C and D are in normal form; in particular these are
conjunctions of particles.
We first note that a subsumption C ⊑D1 ⊓· · · ⊓Dk holds if and only if the
subsumptions C ⊑Di hold for all i ∈{1, . . . , k} (cf. Formula (3)). By considering
each of the latter subsumptions separately, we may assume that the right side
of our goal subsumption consists of a single particle.
After the above step, the considered instance of the subsumption problem
in FL⊥reg is of the form either C ⊑? ∀G.A, where A is a concept name, or
C ⊑? ∀G.⊥; the left side C is a conjunction of particles involving different
concept names. Moreover, as explained in Section 3, we may assume that C
contains a particle involving ⊥, as well as a particle involving A (in the case
when the right side is ∀G.A for A ∈NC). In other words, the instance of the
problem, after preparatory syntactic transformations, is of the form either
– ∀E.A ⊓∀F.⊥⊓C0 ⊑? ∀G.A, where C0 does not use ⊥nor the concept name
A, or
– ∀F.⊥⊓C0 ⊑? ∀G.⊥, where C0 does not use ⊥.
As a next simplification, we observe that the part C0 can be removed from
these subsumptions, as stated in the following lemma, whose proof can be found

– ∀E.A ⊓∀F.⊥⊓C0 ⊑∀G.A holds if and only if ∀E.A ⊓∀F.⊥⊑∀G.A holds;
– ∀F.⊥⊓C0 ⊑∀G.⊥holds if and only if ∀F.⊥⊑∀G.⊥holds.

A proof of Theorem 5.1 is contained in Section B; here we only sketch the
main ideas. For the right-to-left implication, consider a model I of T in which
we want to check whether C ⊑D holds. As J we take a modification of I in
which B is interpreted as ∅, so in the same way as ⊥. It is then easy to see that
(KB)J = KI for every concept description K (possibly using ⊥, but not the
concept name B). It follows that J is a model of T B ∪R, so the subsumption
CB ⊑DB holds there by assumption, which implies that C ⊑D holds in I.
The left-to-right implication is a bit more involved. This time we start with
a model J of T B ∪R in which we want to check whether CB ⊑DB holds. Note
that J may interpret B as a nonempty set. We define I by removing BJ from
the universe of J , hence also from the interpretation of every concept name and
role name. One then has to check that KI = (KB)J \ BJ for every concept
description K using only concept names from ΣC and role names from ΣR. This
involves a suitable use of the axioms from R. Finally, it follows that I is a model
of T , so the subsumption C ⊑D holds there by assumption; it can be then

Parity pushdown games
A parity pushdown game is an infinite-duration two-player game played on the
configuration graph of a pushdown system. Positions of the game consist of a
control state together with a stack content, and are partitioned between the
two players, called Adam and Eve. Starting from a chosen configuration, the
players move a token along transitions induced by the pushdown system, thereby
generating an infinite play. The winner is determined by a parity condition on
the sequence of control states visited during the play.
Formally, a parity pushdown game is a tuple G = (PA, PE, Γ, ∆, Ω), where
– PA ⊎PE, which we denote P, is a finite set of control states, partitioned into
states of Adam and Eve;
– Γ is a finite stack alphabet;
– ∆⊆(P × Γ ∗) × (P × Γ ∗) is a finite set of transition rules;
– Ω: P →N is a priority function.
A configuration is a pair (p, w) ∈P ×Γ ∗. The transition relation, denoted →,
is a relation between configurations defined as follows: for every (p, α, p, β) ∈∆
and w ∈Γ ∗there is a transition
(p, wα) →(q, wβ).
Observe that the stack grows to the right (i.e., the top of the stack is the right-
most symbol of w). Note also that transitions in ∆may remove (pop) an arbi-
trary number of stack symbols in a single step. This more general model can be
effectively reduced to the standard one in which each transition removes precisely
one stack symbol.
A path is a sequence of configurations, (p0, w0)(p1, w1)(p2, w2) . . . , either fi-
nite or infinite, such that each consecutive pair follows the transition relation. A
path is called a play if it is maximal: either infinite, or ending in a configuration
(pn, wn) from which there is no transition. In a finite play, the winner is given by
the state of the last configuration: the player owning this state loses, as no move
is available. In an infinite play, the winner is determined by the parity condition
applied to the sequence of control states (pi)i≥0: Eve wins if the greatest priority
occurring infinitely often in this sequence is even; otherwise, Adam wins.
A (positional) strategy for Eve is a function defined on configurations (p, w) ∈
PE × Γ ∗with at least one available move, assigning to each such configura-
tion a valid successor. Strategies for Adam are defined analogously. A play
(p0, w0)(p1, w1)(p2, w2) . . . (finite or infinite) is consistent with a strategy σ for
Eve if, for every i such that pi ∈PE and (pi, wi) has at least one available move,
we have
(pi+1, wi+1) = σ(pi, wi).
We say that Eve wins the game G from a configuration c if she has a strategy

Conclusions
We have shown that the subsumption problem in FLreg extended with TBoxes
is ExpTime-complete. This result is obtained via a reduction from subsumption
to parity pushdown games, which are known to be solvable in exponential time.
Based on this reduction and existing algorithms for solving such games, a
decision procedure for subsumption in FLreg with TBoxes can be derived in
a straightforward manner. An implementation of this procedure is planned. In
particular, we intend to employ existing solvers for parity pushdown games, such
as PDSolver [8].
The approach also applies to FL⊥reg due to the reduction presented in Sec-
tion 5. Moreover, since FL0 is a sublogic of FLreg, the procedure is also appli-
cable to subsumption in FL0 with TBoxes.
An implementation, FL0wer, for subsumption in FL0 with TBoxes already
exists and is described in Baader et al. [6]. FL0wer is based on a different algorith-
mic approach. It is therefore of interest to investigate experimentally whether
the proposed implementation performs better or worse than FL0wer on sub-
sumption problems in FL0 with TBoxes. Nevertheless, the presented method
provides a general framework for deciding the corresponding reasoning problems
in the other logics considered in this work.
A further direction for future work is the extension of the presented tech-
niques to other reasoning tasks in FL0, FL⊥, FLreg, and FL⊥reg with TBoxes,
such as concept matching and unification.
