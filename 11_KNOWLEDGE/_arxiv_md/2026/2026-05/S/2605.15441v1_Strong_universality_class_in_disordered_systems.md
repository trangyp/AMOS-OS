Disordered systems are very rich laboratories for exploring complex systems. In particular, disor-
dered magnetic systems have been extremely important in the last five decades for understanding a
wide range of phenomena. In this work, we use the Edwards-Anderson Hamiltonian to obtain the
thermodynamic properties of disordered magnetic systems. In this way, we conduct a systematic
investigation of magnetization, correlation functions, order parameter, and fractal dimensions, in
function of disorder. In this context, the autocorrelation function for order–parameter fluctuations,
introduced by Fisher ( Journal of Mathematical Physics 5, 944322 (1964)), provides an important
mathematical framework for understanding the second-order phase transition at equilibrium.
However, his analysis is restricted to a Euclidean space of dimension d, and an exponent η is
introduced to correct the spatial behavior of the correlation function at T = Tc. In recent work,
Lima et al ( Phys.
Rev.
E 110, L062107 (2024)) demonstrated that at Tc a fractal analysis is
necessary for a complete description of the correlation function. We use Monte Carlo simulations
to validate analytical results and to show how disorder alters critical exponents , giving rise to
different universality classes.
On the other hand, there is a subgroup of critical exponents and
fractal dimensions that are invariant with disorder. This subgroup heralds a strong universality class.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Disordered systems are inherently fascinating in na-
ture. Disorder refers to the presence of imperfections or
randomness in a material’s structure. This can lead to
unique properties and behaviors. For a material repre-
sented by a structured lattice with one atom or molecule
at one site, the removal or addition of a new atom or
molecule at random locations can create a disordered ma-
terial. However, there are many ways a system can be
thrown into disorder. A simple way is to take a liquid
and quickly freeze it so that it does not form a regular
lattice, but rather an amorphous material, that is, an
arrangement of atoms where the distance or number of
connections with their neighbors is not fixed. Thus, we
have a large number of disordered systems, which for sim-
plicity we classify as: I- Disordered solids; II- Spin glasses
and III- Percolation transition (for reviews, see [1–4]).

Besides presenting important data for these exponents,
our main conclusion is the existence of two classes of
exponents: one that varies with σ and another that is
invariant. The latter is a good example of how scale in-
variance acts in geometric structures.

Hierarchy - Note that not all scaling relations have
the same strength; for example, the mean-field exponents
α = 0, β = 1/2, γ = 1, ν = 1/2 satisfy the Rushbrooke
(14), and Fisher (13) relations, but not the hyperscal-
ing relation, except for d = 4. The Rushbrooke relation
works if the exponents come from the same homogeneous
free energy, even when the free energy is not appropriate.
Universality - The universality class of a system is
typically defined by the dimension d, the symmetry of
the system, (Z2 for the two-dimensional Ising model with
σ = 0) and the range of interaction. Disorder σ alters
the symmetry and this may implies a change in univer-
sality, thus altering the set (β, ν, ...). Consequently, we
can say that every σ creates a system that may belongs
to a different universality class than that of the ordered
two-dimensional Ising model.
A Strong imposed geometric universality - The
results presented in Table I, in Figure 4a and in Figure
4b, however, point to a new reality. In Figure 4b, we see
that there is a linear relationship β = aν. The fit provides
a = 0.1254(2), and all points are on this line, within the
precision limit. The fact that the ratio β/ν = 1/8 and
γ/ν = 1.75 is not a coincidence; it only indicates that df,
given by equation (8), dR, given by equation (11), and η,
given by equation (10), remain constant. That is, they
are independent of σ. Thus, while the set (β, nu, ...) has
an universality for each σ, the set (η, df, dR, ...) belongs to
the same universality class as σ = 0, namely the ordered
two-dimensional Ising model.
The latter set reveals a
strong imposed geometric universality.
The scale invariance (17) characteristic of fractal clus-
ters is the most fundamental symmetry relation of the
system. In other words, this symmetry is the dominant
one among all possible conformal symmetries in two di-
mensions.
In the transition, the cluster does not dis-
tinguish the disorder resulting from the change in the
interaction Ji,j from the disorder resulting from the tem-
perature; therefore, the fractal dimension df is the same.
That from (8) imposes that β/ν is constant, which from
(11) explains the invariance of dR, and η from (10), as σ
changes. From (12) and (13), we observe that γ/ν and
α/ν must also be invariant, the deviation presented by
α = 0, are within the limit of precision of the parame-
ters within (13). The invariance of these proportions is
yet another indication of the strength of the Rushbrooke
relationship.
Note also that the two-dimensional Baxer-Wu model,
the Potts model with q = 4, has [56] β = 1/12 and ν =
2/3, resulting in β = ν = 1/8. That is, the set (β, ν, ...)
is quite different from the Ising model, but the subset
(η, df, dR, ...) belongs to the same universality class.
IV.
CONCLUDING REMARKS AND FUTURE
PERSPECTIVES
Concluding remarks - In this work, we discuss how
exact fractional differential geometry, used to obtain a
fractal mean-field theory
[36, 37], works even for dis-
ordered lattices. We recover exactly the same law, Eq.
(10), which states that the Fisher exponent η is the dif-
ference between d and the Riesz fractal dimension dR,
that is, η = d −dR = 1 −ζ, or equivalently, equal to the
difference between the unit and the fractional derivative
order ζ.
We use a precise computational method to obtain the
exponents Table I and the fractal dimensions Table II to
verify our claims. Furthermore, we show that (17) is the
strong scale that guaranties the invariance of the fractal
dimensions as the disorder changes.
Contrary to the common assertion that mean-field the-
ory describes critical behavior only for d ≥dc, we argue
that this is a geometric and, consequently, algebraic er-
ror.
The ”physical error” of neglecting fluctuations in
mean-field theories does not appear in (4), since it is
the correlation of the fluctuations itself. Discrete lattice
models, such as Ising, and continuous partial differential
