Abstract
Combinatorial counting problems pervade artificial intelligence, statistics, and discrete mathematics. Whether the
task is enumerating subsets, multisets, permutations, partitions, or compositions under structural and arithmetic con-
straints, solving it remains a stubbornly manual exercise. Closed-form derivations are powerful but brittle, while
naive encodings to propositional model counting or constraint satisfaction destroy the exchangeability that makes
counting tractable in the first place. We present Cofola (COmbinatorial counting LAnguage with First-Order logic),
a typed declarative language whose primitives are the combinatorial objects that recur in everyday counting ques-
tions, including sets, bags, tuples, sequences, circles, partitions, and compositions, together with natural relational
and arithmetic constraints over them. A denotational semantics maps every Cofola program to a well-defined com-
binatorial counting problem, and a three-phase compilation pipeline (preprocessing, decomposition, and symmetry-
preserving encoding) reduces this problem to a weighted first-order model counting (WFOMC) instance augmented
with coefficient-extraction constraints. To stay inside known domain-liftable fragments whenever possible, the en-
coding groups indistinguishable entities, breaks the symmetry of unordered groupings lexicographically, and encodes
sequences and circles via order axioms. On a suite of representative combinatorial counting problems, ranging from
textbook math problems to multi-object scenarios that the closest prior framework cannot express, Cofola produces
concise specifications and a uniform solving pipeline that is practical end to end.
1. Introduction
Combinatorial counting problems are everywhere in artificial intelligence, statistics, and discrete mathematics.
Every probabilistic normalising constant, every constrained-enumeration query in data analysis, and every textbook
combinatorics exercise asks the same question, “in how many ways can X be configured under constraints C”, over
an exponentially large space of candidate objects. Taking the following two problems as examples:
P1. Our water polo team has 15 members. I want to choose a starting team consisting of 7 players, one of whom will
be the goalie. In how many ways can I choose my starting team?
P2. On the refrigerator, “MATHCOUNTS” is spelled out with 10 magnets, one letter per magnet. If the Ts are
indistinguishable, how many distinct possible collections of 4 letters could be put in the bag such that the number of
vowels is less than the number of consonants?
Despite their innocent appearance, such queries already mix several modelling primitives, including multisets, ordered
arrangements, position patterns, and cardinality relations. They also resist any uniform evaluation strategy, since
exhaustive enumeration scales poorly in both the domain size and the number of constraints.

Weighted first-order model counting (WFOMC) [32, 17, 33] bridges the gap on the solving side. A growing

missing is a modelling layer. WFOMC is exposed to the user as a first-order sentence, and translating combinatorial
primitives such as multisets, partitions, circular arrangements, and sequences with positional patterns into a sentence
by hand is both tedious and error-prone. Naive encodings routinely cross known tractability frontiers.
This paper closes the gap. We propose Cofola (COmbinatorial counting LAnguage with First-Order logic), a
typed declarative language whose surface primitives are the combinatorial objects of everyday discrete mathematics,
including sets, bags, tuples, sequences, circles, partitions, and compositions, together with the relational, positional,
and arithmetic constraints that naturally accompany them. Unlike single-configuration languages [27], a Cofola pro-
gram may declare an arbitrary directed-acyclic dependency graph of objects, allowing multi-stage modelling such as
“choose a starting team, then choose a goalie from that team, then partition the bench by role”. A Cofola program
is given a denotational semantics that maps it to a well-defined combinatorial counting problem, and a compilation
pipeline reduces this problem to a WFOMC instance enriched with coefficient-extraction constraints. The compila-
tion is explicitly engineered to preserve domain-level symmetries through indistinguishable-entity grouping in bags,
lexicographic symmetry breaking on partition parts, and order-axiom encodings for sequences and circles, so that the
resulting instances stay inside lifted-tractable fragments whenever the input does. The whole pipeline runs on top of
off-the-shelf WFOMC solvers, inheriting their theoretical guarantees and engineering investment without modifica-
tion.
Concretely, this paper makes the following contributions:
• A typed declarative language for combinatorial counting. Cofola natively supports seven object types (sets,
bags, tuples, sequences, circles, partitions, and compositions) and a constraint language covering membership,
set or bag relations, position indexing, adjacency and precedence patterns, group-level quantification, and linear
arithmetic over cardinality and pattern-count atoms. Programs may compose objects through an arbitrary DAG
of dependencies. Every Cofola program is mapped to a well-defined combinatorial counting problem ⟨D, O,C⟩,
where O is a set of combinatorial objects with action functions and C is a set of constraint relations. The
semantics of compound constraints is the standard set-theoretic algebra on relations, giving the language a
precise mathematical foundation independent of any specific solver.
• A symmetry-preserving compilation procedure. The compilation proceeds in three phases: preprocess-
ing (entity analysis, max-size inference, optimisations, and sanity checks), decomposition (Shannon expan-
sion, size-range expansion, and independent-subproblem splitting), and per-type encoding (sets, bags, tuples,
sequences and circles, partitions and compositions, together with size constraints). The pipeline returns a
WFOMC instance together with a decoder that extracts the target coefficient from the WFOMC polynomial,
and the encoding rules are designed so that liftable inputs yield liftable instances.
• Implementation and empirical evaluation. We implement Cofola on top of state-of-the-art lifted WFOMC
solvers and evaluate the pipeline on a corpus of combinatorial counting problems, including textbook math
problems and instances that exceed the expressive reach of the closest prior framework. The experiments show
that Cofola delivers concise specifications and competitive end-to-end solving performance.

2. Related Work
Cofola sits at the intersection of two long lines of research: declarative modelling languages for combinatorial
problems, which describe what structures and constraints define a problem, and lifted inference, which counts assign-
ments to first-order theories without grounding to a propositional level. We discuss each in turn, and then position
Cofola against the closest prior work, CoLa/CoSo of Totis et al. [27], which directly targets the same class of combi-
natorial counting problems.
Declarative modelling for combinatorial problems. Constraint-programming languages have, for decades, provided
high-level vocabularies for describing combinatorial structures and constraints over them. MiniZinc [22] and its
predecessor Zinc [20] model finite-domain CSPs with sets, arrays, and global constraints, and compile to a wide range
of CP/SAT/SMT backends. ESSENCE [6] extends this style with high-level types such as multisets and partitions and
serves as the source language for the Conjure refinement pipeline. Answer set programming [7] and probabilistic
logic programming, e.g. ProbLog [4], similarly let the user state combinatorial problems declaratively over first-order
rules. None of these frameworks, however, is designed for the counting task: solvers are tuned for satisfiability or
enumeration, so counting reduces to either propositional model counting [24, 28] or to enumerating witnesses, both
of which scale poorly when the number of valid configurations is exponential in the domain. Moreover, even when a
language nominally supports multisets or partitions, the underlying solvers typically grind through propositionalised,
labelled copies of every entity; the exchangeability that makes a counting argument tractable on paper is lost during
solving, as Totis et al. [27] document in detail.
First-order and lifted model counting. A complementary line of work, originating in lifted probabilistic inference [3,
33], attacks counting at the first-order level by exploiting symmetries among indistinguishable individuals. Weighted
first-order model counting (WFOMC) [32, 11, 16] computes, in time polynomial in the domain size for the two-
variable fragment FO2 [9] and its counting-quantifier extension C2 [17, 15], the weighted number of models of a
first-order sentence. A series of follow-up algorithms extends the tractable fragment with cardinality constraints,
linear-order and tree axioms, and faster cell-graph procedures [25, 29, 30, 14]. Companion work has reused the same
machinery for first-order model sampling [34, 18] and for algebraic generalisations of model counting [12]. Despite
this growing toolbox, WFOMC is exposed to the user as a sentence-level interface: encoding everyday combinatorial
concepts such as multisets, ordered tuples, circular arrangements, partitions, or compositions requires careful first-
order modelling that easily breaks symmetry or crosses known tractability boundaries. A practical declarative front
end that compiles such concepts to WFOMC while preserving symmetry has so far been missing.
Lifted reasoning for combinatorial counting (CoLa/CoSo). The closest prior work is CoLa, a declarative language
for combinatorial counting problems, paired with the #CSP-based solver CoSo, recently proposed by Totis et al. [27].
CoLa supports sets, multisets, and a handful of configuration types, and CoSo implements lifted reasoning rules (mul-
tiplication, exchangeability, and case analysis) directly on the CoLa AST. Cofola shares CoLa’s high-level objective
of expressing combinatorial counting problems declaratively, but differs in three substantive ways. First, CoLa allows
a single combinatorial configuration per problem; Cofola supports an arbitrary directed-acyclic dependency graph
of objects spanning sets, bags, tuples, sequences, circles, partitions, and compositions, together with a richer con-
straint language that includes adjacency and precedence patterns, position indexing, group-level quantification, and
arithmetic comparisons of size atoms. Second, CoSo relies on a bespoke #CSP solver implementing its own lifted
rules; Cofola compiles to a generic WFOMC instance augmented with coefficient-extraction constraints, so it inherits
the theoretical guarantees and engineering investment of off-the-shelf lifted WFOMC solvers [32, 25, 14]. We also

3.4. Linear Order Axiom and Cyclic Order Axiom
Finally, we need the linear order axiom [26] and the cyclic order axiom to model the sequential relations among
entities in combinatorial counting problems. A linear order axiom is of the form Linear(R, Pred), where R and Pred
are binary predicates. It enforces the interpretation of R to be a linear (total) order on the domain elements, and the
interpretation of Pred to be a set of successive pairs in the order. Formally, an interpretation ω |= Linear(R, Pred) iff
ω satisifies the following formulas:
• reflexivity: ∀x : R(x, x),
• connectiveness: ∀x∀y : R(x, y) ∨R(y, x),
• anti-symmetry: ∀x∀y : R(x, y) ∧R(y, x) ⇒x = y,
• transitivity: ∀x∀y∀z : R(x, y) ∧R(y, z) ⇒R(x, z), and
• predecessor: ∀x∀y : Pred(x, y) ⇔(R(x, y) ∧¬∃z : (z , x ∧z , y ∧R(x, z) ∧R(z, y))).
Note that the last two formulas both contain three logical variables, and thus directly encoding them to the sentence
might make the FOMC problem intractable (recall that the FOMC problem for FO3 is #P1-complete).4 Thus we
introduce the linear order axiom as an extension rather than a primitive in the first-order logic.
Similar to the cardinality constraints, we write Linear(R, Pred) as an atomic formula, and connect it to the sentence
by the logical connectives. When Pred is not specified, we simply write Linear(R). With the linear order axiom, the
problem in Example 3 can be also modeled as Γ = Linear(Perm). Moveover, the linear order axiom can be used to
model the relative position constraints in combinatorial counting problems.
Example 7. Consider the combinatorial counting problem: How many ways can we rearrange two distinguishable
red balls and three distinguishable balls of other colors in a line such that the red balls are not adjacent? We can
model the problem as the FOMC problem of Γ = Linear(R, Pred) ∧∀x∀y : (Red(x) ∧¬Red(y) ⇒¬Pred(x, y)) with
the unary evidence {Red(ball1), Red(ball2), ¬Red(ball3), ¬Red(ball4), ¬Red(ball5)}.
We can further extend the linear order axiom to the cyclic order axiom Circle(Pred) to model a cycle of entities.
ω |= Circle(Pred) iff ωPred is a cyclic order on the domain elements and the predicate Pred interprets the successive
pairs in the clockwise order.

polynomial in the domain size |∆|, the size (and parameters) of the cardinality constraints, and the number of ground
literals in the unary evidence.
There are several WFOMC algorithms, e.g., FORCLIFT [32], FASTWFOMC [25], INCREMENTALWFOMC [26]

WFOMC problem. In this paper, we use FASTWFOMC and INCREMENTALWFOMC2 as our back-end solvers,
since they support all the extensions introduced above and are efficient in practice. The code of our implementation is
available at https://github.com/yuanhong-wang/WFOMC.

4. Overview of the Cofola Language
In this section, we give a brief overview of Combinatorial counting LAnguage with First-Order logic (Cofola) for
modeling combinatorial counting problems. The detailed syntax and semantics of the language are presented in the
following sections. The compilation of a Cofola program to a WFOMC problem is detailed in Section 8. Throughout
this section, we use the following combinatorial counting problem as a running example.
4.1. Syntax Overview
Cofola is a declarative language, whose abstract syntax is overviewed in Fig. 1. A Cofola program consists of a
set of object declarations and constraints. The object declarations specify the combinatorial objects involved in the
counting problem, while the constraints restrict the valid configurations of the objects.
An object can be defined in Cofola using the I = expr statement, where I is a new identifier representing
the name of the object and expr is an expression that defines the object. We require that all the identifiers in Cofola
programs are valid, i.e., they start with a letter or an underscore, followed by letters, digits, or underscores. The general
form of the expression expr is op(src, arg1, arg2, ...), where op is a symbol representing the operator that
forms the object, i.e., how the entities are organized, and src specifies the source of the entities, either a concrete
collection of entities or other objects.
Example 8. The problem P1 can be modeled by the following Cofola program:
1

2

3

The expression players = set(player1, player2, ..., player15) defines a set object named “players”
that contains the 15 members of the water polo team. The expression starting_team = choose(players, 7)
defines another object of type set that is formed by choosing 7 members from players without replacement.
Cofola provides seven types for objects: set, bag, tuple, sequence, circle, composition, and partition that cover
most common combinatorial configurations. In Cofola, each object has a fixed type specified when it is declared;
Cofola does not allow type casting either implicitly or explicitly. Set and bag are the basic types in Cofola that can
be directly defined by enumerating the entities they contain. The other five types are derived types that can only
be formed from existing basic objects using the operators provided in Cofola. For example, line = sequence(
starting_team) defines a sequence object that is formed by arranging the members in the set starting_team in

4.2. Semantics Overview
Before presenting the semantics of Cofola, we give the formulation of combinatorial counting problems that Co-
fola is designed to model. A combinatorial counting problem typically involves counting the number of combinatorial
objects (sets, multisets, ordered lists, etc.) formed by a set of entities while satisfying certain constraints. We formalize
this below.
Let D be a set of entities. The combinatorial universe U over D is the set of all combinatorial instances that can
be created from D, including all sets, multisets, ordered lists, circular lists, and their nested collections (e.g., sets of
sets, ordered lists of multisets). We may use Uset, UMset, Ulist, Ucircle, Usset, and UsMset to denote the subsets of U
that contain only sets, multisets, ordered lists, circular lists, sets of sets, and sets of multisets, respectively.

A combinatorial object over D is defined as a triple (v, (d1, d2, . . . , dk), f), where v is a designated variable for
the object, di is a variable representing the dependency of v, and f : Uk →2U is a function that specifies which
combinatorial instances the object can take given its dependencies. Given a set of entities D and a set of combinatorial
objects O, a combinatorial structure A of O over D is a mapping from each variable v in O to a combinatorial
instance A(v) ∈U, such that for every object (v, (d1, d2, . . . , dk), f) ∈O, A(v) ∈f(A(d1), A(d2), . . . , A(dk)). The
combinatorial base B of O over D is the set of all possible combinatorial structures of O over D.
A combinatorial constraint on O over D is a relation R(v1, . . . , vk) ⊆Uk on a tuple of variables (v1, v2, . . . , vk)
in O. A combinatorial structure A satisfies a constraint R(v1, . . . , vk) iff A(v1, v2, . . . , vk) ∈R(v1, . . . , vk). We often
write the relations R(v1, . . . , vk) using standard operators for combinatorial objects, e.g., v1 ⊆v2 for the constraint
R(v1, v2) = {(s1, s2) ∈Uset × Uset | s1 ⊆s2}.
Definition 3 (Combinatorial Counting Problem). A combinatorial counting problem is a triple ⟨D, O,C⟩, where D is
a set of entities, O is a set of combinatorial objects over D, and C is a set of combinatorial constraints on O over D.
The answer of the combinatorial counting problem ⟨D, O,C⟩, denoted by #⟨D, O,C⟩, is the number of combinatorial
structures in the combinatorial base B of O over D that satisfy all the constraints in C.

The semantics of Cofola is given by mapping a Cofola program P to a combinatorial counting problem ⟦P⟧=
⟨D, O,C⟩. The solution of the Cofola program P is defined as the answer of the corresponding combinatorial counting
problem ⟦P⟧.
Definition 4 (Solution of a Cofola program). Given a Cofola program P, let ⟦P⟧= ⟨D, O,C⟩be the corresponding
combinatorial counting problem. The solution of P is defined as #⟨D, O,C⟩.
The combinatorial counting problem ⟦P⟧= ⟨D, O,C⟩is obtained inductively from the statements in P. In brief,
an object declaration statement I = expr introduces a new combinatorial object to O while updating the domain of
entities D if necessary. For instance, the statement I = set(e1, e2, ..., en) updates D to D ∪{e1, e2, ..., en}
and adds a new object (vI, (), INIT((); {e1, ..., en})) to O. For another example, the statement I = choose(I’, 5),
where I’ is the identifier of an existing set with the variable vI′, adds a new object (vI, (vI′), SETCHOOSE(x; 5)) to
O. When an object declaration statement violates the typing rules of Cofola as specified in Fig. 2, the semantics is
undefined. We further disallow cyclic dependencies between objects for simplicity even though the corresponding
combinatorial counting problems can still be well-defined.6 Finally, a constraint statement adds a new combinatorial
constraint to C, and the logical connectives in compound constraints are interpreted as the standard logical operations
on relations.

6. Ordered Objects: Tuple, Sequence, Circle
This section covers the three ordered object types in Cofola: tuples, sequences, and circles. All three organize their
entities in a specific order, but they differ in the types of constraints they support: a tuple supports constraints on the
absolute positions of entities (i.e., position indexing), while a sequence and a circle support constraints on the relative
positions of entities (e.g., adjacency and precedence patterns). A sequence arranges its entities in a line, whereas a
circle arranges them on a ring. Cofola does not support a general notion of ordered list that can be constrained by both
absolute and relative position constraints. We leave it to future work.
The abstract syntax of the object declarations for tuples, sequences, and circles is shown in Figure 4. All three
types need to be formed from an existing set or bag; Cofola does not support declaring them directly from a concrete
ordered list of entities.

existing set or bag, respectively. Cofola also supports a compact syntax to simultaneously choose and arrange entities:

8. Solving Cofola Programs with WFOMC
Solving a Cofola program P amounts to computing #⟨D, O,C⟩for its combinatorial counting problem ⟦P⟧=
⟨D, O,C⟩(Section 4). Our approach reduces #⟨D, O,C⟩to a set of WFOMC instances, leveraging off-the-shelf lifted
WFOMC algorithms with both theoretical guarantees (Theorem 1) and strong practical performance. This contrasts
with existing methods such as CoSo for CoLa programs [27], which rely on specialized #CSP solvers.
Algorithm 1 outlines the overall solver. Given a combinatorial counting problem ⟨D, O,C⟩, the solver proceeds
in three conceptual phases that organize the rest of this section. Preprocessing (Section 8.1) is applied at two gran-
ularities, once on the whole problem (Algorithm 1) and again per subproblem produced by expansion and split (Al-
gorithm 1). Each call returns a simplified problem together with a read-only analysis record that the encoder will
query, or ⊥to signal unsatisfiability of the problem. In the decomposition phase (Algorithm 1), the input problem is
expanded into a set of subproblems that are more amenable to WFOMC solvers, which are then further split into inde-
pendent components (Algorithm 1) that can be solved separately and multiplied together. Finally, the encoding phase
(Algorithm 1) compiles each preprocessed subproblem into a WFOMC instance together with a decoding function
that maps the WFOMC output to the count of the subproblem, and the solving phase (Algorithm 1) runs the WFOMC
solver and applies the decoder to get the sub-count, which are then aggregated into the final answer.
Throughout this section, we often write a combinatorial object o = (v, f, d) ∈O as v = f(d) for better readability,
and we use the same name v to refer to the object and its name component interchangeably when the meaning is clear
from context.

8.1. Preprocessing
Preprocessing transforms a combinatorial counting problem ⟨D, O,C⟩into a simpler problem ⟨Dsimp, Osimp,Csimp⟩
that is easier to encode and solve, while also deriving information about the objects in O that the encoder can exploit. In
the following, we sketch the main components of preprocessing, and defer the detailed algorithms to Section Appendix
B.
Analyses. We perform several analyses to derive information, stored in an analysis record, that are useful for encoding
and optimizing the problem. Specifically, for each object v ∈O, the analysis record includes:
• pEnt(v) ⊆∆: the potential entity set of v, i.e., the over-approximation of entities that can occur in v;
• size(v) ∈N ∪{⊥}: the inferred exact cardinality of v when known (⊥otherwise)
• maxSize(v) ∈N: the inferred maximum cardinality of v;
• for every bag v, multv : pEnt(v) →N: the inferred upper bound on the multiplicity of each potential entity in v;
Note that we do not expect the analyses to be exact, so the derived potential entity sets and size bounds are over-
approximations of the true ones.
Given a problem ⟨D, O,C⟩, recall that we disallow cyclic dependencies between objects, and thus the object-
dependency graph is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) where each node is an object and each edge represents a depen-
dency from one object to another. The analyses operate by traversing this DAG in topological order, and inferring
information from the dependencies of an object according to its constructor. For instance, if v = SETUNION(v1, v2),
then pEnt(v) = pEnt(v1) ∪pEnt(v2), maxSize(v) = maxSize(v1) + maxSize(v2), and size(v) = size(v1) + size(v2)
if both are known. Then the inferred maxSize(v) and size(v) are further tightened by the max-size inference, which
takes into account the size constraints in C. For example, if C contains the constraint (|v1| + |v2| ≤5) ∧(|v1| ≥3), then
we can infer maxSize(v2) ≤2 by solving a linear program that encodes the size constraints and the max-size bounds
of all objects.

Optimizations. The optimizations are interleaved with the analyses to keep the problem representation as simple as
possible and to propagate derived information as early as possible. There are currently four optimizations:
• Constant folding replaces objects whose instantiation is fully determined by their inputs with an equivalent
initialized object (e.g., a union of two disjoint initialized sets becomes a single initialized set), and rewrites
constraints that reference the folded object accordingly;
• Identity simplification eliminates identical objects by substituting their definitions everywhere; for example, if

• Size-constraint folding substitutes each object’s known size into the size constraints in C;

Sanity checks. At the end of preprocessing, we perform a sanity check to detect any unsatisfiability or unsupported
features in the problem before invoking the encoder and WFOMC solver. The unsatisfiability triggers are designed to
be lightweight and mainly fall into two main categories:
• Size contradictions where the size constraints in C cannot be satisfied by any assignment of sizes to the objects;
• Out-of-range indexing where a tuple-index constraint references an index that is outside the maximum size of
the tuple.
Except for unsatisfiability, the sanity check also detects the features that are currently unsupported by the encoder,
raising an error to terminate the solver. For now, there are two cases:
• Unbounded tuples and sequences: If the size of a tuple or sequence object cannot be bounded by the analyses,
then the encoder cannot materialise the index entities required for their encoding. We therefore raise an error to
signal that the problem is currently out of scope.
• Multiple sequences: WFOMC only support a single linear order or cyclic order axiom7, so if there are multiple
sequence objects, we raise an error.

8.2. Decomposition
The decomposition phase transforms the input problem ⟨D, O,C⟩into a set of sub-problems that are more amenable
and efficient to solve by WFOMC solvers. A single expansion step (Algorithm 1) eliminates compound constraints
via Shannon expansion and fixes the cardinalities of ordered collections via size-range expansion, after which the
resulting branches are split into independent sub-problems (Algorithm 1) that can be solved separately and multiplied
together.
Size-range expansion. The encoder requires the size of each tuple and sequence object to be fixed to a constant, so if
the analyses fail to derive an exact size for such an object v, we perform size-range expansion by enumerating |v| = k
for k ∈{0, 1, . . . , maxSize(v)} (recall that we raise an error if maxSize(v) is unbounded), resulting in a compound
constraint WmaxSize(v)

Shannon expansion. The constraint set C of a combinatorial counting problem may contain compound constraints
built from atomic ones by the logical connectives ∧, ∨and ¬ (recall from Section 4 that these connectives are inter-
preted as the standard set-theoretic operations on the relations that atomic constraints denote). Compound constraints
cannot be directly compiled to WFOMC in our framework, which requires all elements of C to be atomic. Shan-
non expansion (the first sub-step of Algorithm 1) resolves this by enumerating all truth assignments to the atomic
sub-constraints appearing in each compound constraint, replacing each compound constraint with the corresponding
conjunction of (possibly negated) atomic constraints. In practice, we build a propositional formula over fresh symbols
representing the atomic sub-constraints of C and enumerate only its satisfying models via a SAT solver, so unsatisfi-
able assignments are skipped without invoking the encoder. This is analogous to the Shannon expansion applied to

8.3. Encoding
The encoder works on a preprocessed sub-problems. Generally, the encoder traverses the objects and the con-
straints of a sub-problem in topological order and populates a tuple (Γ, ∆, w, ¯w, A, overcount, Σ), where Γ, ∆, w, ¯w
are the components of the WFOMC instance to be solved, and A, overcount, Σ are the information for decoding the
WFOMC result back to the count of the sub-problem:
• A is a system of equations and inequalities over the symbolic variables in w and ¯w that characterizes the mono-
mial in the WFOMC output that corresponds to the count of the sub-problem;
• overcount is a numeric factor that accounts for any overcounting in the encoding;
• Σ is a set of term-dependent quotient corrections for residual symmetries that are not captured by overcount,
specifically for the encoding of partitions.
If there is no partition in the problem, then Σ is empty and the decoding is simply decode(f, A, overcount, Σ) :=
[A] f/overcount, where recall that [A]f is the coefficient of the monomial in f that satisfies the system A. The
decoding for partitions is more involved and is described in Section 8.3.5.
The following subsections describe the encoding rules by object type. Constraints that are naturally attached to a
particular object type (for example, position patterns on a sequence, or multiplicity comparisons on bags) are handled
together with the objects they constrain. The size constraints involving size atoms spreading across multiple object
types are encoded at the end of the encoding phase, and their encoding rules are described in Section 8.3.6.

9. Experiments
In this section, we evaluate Cofola on a suite of combinatorial counting problems, both real and synthetic. Through
this evaluation, we seek to answer the following questions:
Q1. Whether Cofola can encode real combinatorial counting problems.
Q2. How many of the encoded problems the lifted WFOMC backend can solve.
Q3. How the backend compares with existing modelling-and-solving pipelines.
9.1. Modelling Capability
We construct a real benchmark of 408 combinatorial counting problems, drawn from the MATH dataset [10] and
encoded in Cofola by hand. The construction process is described in detail below:
1. We first select the counting and statistics category from MATH, which contains 1245 problems;
2. the problems are filtered by the keyword “how many”, leaving 505 problems;
3. the remaining problems are filtered by hand for text-only combinatorial counting problems, as opposed to
problems that depend on figures, tables, diagrams, or other information not expressible in natural language,
leaving 408 problems;
4. finally, the problems involving set-theoretic word problems whose answer follows from elementary inclusion–
exclusion are removed, leaving 392 problems.
Of the 392 candidate counting problems collected after the text-only filter (a slightly stricter version of stage 3),
272 (69.4%) are encoded in Cofola and 120 (30.6%) are not. We illustrate each category below by a representative
problem from the corpus.
Arithmetic on entity-level numeric values (89). The largest unsupported class, by a wide margin, is the one in which
each entity carries an intrinsic numeric attribute (a position rank, a thickness, a denomination, a printed face) and the
question counts configurations whose attributes satisfy an arithmetic condition.
P7. In a certain cross country meet between 2 teams of 5 runners each, a runner who finishes in the nth position
contributes n to his team’s score. The team with the lower score wins. If there are no ties among the runners, how
many different winning scores are possible?
P8. In the United States, coins have the following thicknesses: penny, 1.55 mm; nickel, 1.95 mm; dime, 1.35 mm;
quarter, 1.75 mm. If a stack of these coins is exactly 14 mm high, how many coins are in the stack?
In both cases the constraint is a linear equation over per-entity numeric values, not over object cardinalities. Cofola
treats entities as opaque names: its size atoms are cardinalities and multiplicities of objects, not values attached to
entities, and there is no operator for “sum of w(e) over the entities of an object v” with w : entities →Q. Supporting
these problems would require counting SMT-style models with arithmetic constraints.

9.2. Reasoning Capability
Next, we evaluate the lifted WFOMC backend on the 272 real problems that Cofola can encode, and on a growing-
domain benchmark of 100 instances (five parameterised families of 20 variants each) that tests the expected lifted-
scaling behaviour. We also compare the backend with existing solving pipelines on both benchmarks:
• CoLa-CoSo [27], a recent framework for combinatorial counting that supports a fragment of Cofola and uses a
custom lifted #CSP solver. We use its official code.9
• ASP-Clingo [7], a state-of-the-art answer-set programming system. We use the toolchain described in the
CoLa/CoSo paper to translate CoLa problems into ASP, and use Clingo to solve the resulting ASP instances.
• ESSENCE-Conjure [6, 1, 8], a constraint modelling and solving pipeline that supports a wide range of combi-
natorial problems. We still use the CoLa/CoSo translation toolchain to translate CoLa problems into ESSENCE,
and use Conjure to solve the resulting ESSENCE instances.
• CNF-Ganak [23], a state-of-the-art exact propositional model counter. We use the same Cofola frontend as our
solver, then ground the generated WFOMC instance to propositional weighted CNF and invoke Ganak. Note
that Ganak natively supports weighted model counting with symbolic weights10, so the grounding step does
not need to extract coefficients or otherwise manipulate the counting problem; it simply replaces the first-order
variables with their domain elements and produces a propositional instance with the same structure and the
same symbolic weights.
The cofola implementation is available at https://github.com/lucienwang1009/cofola. All experiments were run
on a server with an Intel Xeon Gold 5218 CPU at 2.30GHz, 128 hardware threads, and 503GB RAM. We use a
per-instance timeout of 100 seconds.

9.2.3. Synthetic benchmarks
The real and growing-domain benchmarks probe what practitioners actually ask, but they say little about programs
that mix Cofola’s constructs in less stereotyped ways. We therefore add a synthetic suite of 100 randomly generated
Cofola programs whose structural difficulty is controlled by the generator rather than by the problem text.
Each synthetic program is built by sampling three structural parameters and then drawing operators and constraints

partition, ...) that build a DAG on top of the base object;
• constraint count nc ∈{0, 1, 2, 3}: the number of atomic constraints (membership, subset, disjointness, equality,
size atoms, position patterns, ...) on the resulting objects.

The synthetic suite separates two effects that the real and growing benchmarks couple. First, the lifted WFOMC
encoding scales smoothly with combinations of Cofola constructs: adding more operators or more constraints rarely
flips an instance from solved to not solved, it only moves the runtime inside the solved bucket. Second, the visible
gap to CoLa-CoSo on real problems is structural rather than a matter of tuning. As soon as a program declares more
than one combinatorial configuration, or uses a sequence, a circle, or a bag-side relational constraint, the CoSo-family
backends switch from solving to refusing to encode. This matches the modelling motivation of Cofola stated in
Section 4: the same DAG-shaped programs that let users describe “choose a team, then a goalie, then a bench” in one
specification are the programs that have no shortcut translation into the single-configuration CoLa fragment.

Appendix A.1. Combinatorics Notations
Table A.12 consolidates all combinatorial notations used in the paper. For a set S , the power set is 2S ; for a
multiset M, the power multiset is 2M := {N | N ⊆m M}. For multisets M and N, the additive union is M ⊎N := {e :
M(e) + N(e) | e ∈M ∪N}, distinct from the multiset union M ∪N which takes the max of multiplicities. The support
of a multiset M is the set Supp(M) := { e | M(e) > 0 }. Two multisets are equal, M = N, iff Supp(M) = Supp(N) and
M(e) = N(e) for every e ∈Supp(M).
For an ordered list L, we write e1 <L e2 (resp. e1 >L e2) to mean that e1 appears before (resp. after) e2 in L, and
(e1, e2) ∈L to mean that e1 and e2 are consecutive in L. Given an entity e and an ordered list L, we say that e is
together in L if all occurrences of e in L are contiguous (i.e., L can be written as [h1, . . . , hi, e, . . . , e, t1, . . . , t j] with
hp, tq , e). A set or multiset S is together in L if all elements of S occupy a single contiguous block of L (i.e., L can
be written as [h1, . . . , hi, m1, . . . , mk, t1, . . . , t j] with mp ∈S and hq, tr < S ).
A circular list is an ordered list whose first element is the successor of the last; we denote it by [e1, . . . , en] and
identify all rotations: [e1, . . . , en] ≡[e2, . . . , en, e1]. The predicate (e1, e2) ∈C extends to a circular list C by treating
the two ends as adjacent. A reflexive circular list [e1, . . . , en] is a circular list that is additionally identified with its
reflection: [e1, . . . , en] ≡[en, en−1, . . . , e1].

Appendix B. Preprocessing Algorithms
This section gives detailed pseudocode for the three analysis passes sketched in Section 8.1. All three passes
operate on a sub-problem P = ⟨D, O,C⟩and produce the analysis record (pEnt, maxSize, size, mult, Dis, Indis, S)
used by the encoder.
Appendix B.1. Entity Analysis
Entity analysis (Algorithm 3) walks the object-dependency graph in topological order, applying a constructor-
specific rule at each object to derive pEnt(v), maxSize(v), size(v), and multv. Literals contribute their entities and
cardinality directly; choosing operations inherit the source’s potential entities and cap the size by the choosing argu-
ment; binary set/bag operations combine the operands’ entity sets (∪, ∩, or set/multiset difference) and derive sizes
by the corresponding rule (sum, min, or element-wise max/min); and ordered collections are routed to the set or bag
type based on the replacement flag.
Appendix B.2. Max-Size Inference
Entity analysis yields only structural size bounds; it does not exploit the user’s explicit size constraints. Max-size
inference (Algorithm 4) closes this gap: it encodes all size constraints P
i ci |vi| ▷◁c as a system of linear (in)equalities
over variables xv = |v|, then solves, for each constrained object v, a maximizing LP and a minimizing LP to obtain

each entity’s multiplicity by its containing bag’s updated maximum size.

Appendix B.3. Optimizations
After entity analysis and max-size inference, the preprocessor runs the four optimizations described in Sec-
tion 8.1—constant folding, identity simplification, size-constraint folding, and simplification—to keep the problem as
small as possible before encoding. The four rules are interleaved and applied to fixed point, so that newly exposed
simplifications (e.g., a constraint that becomes vacuous after substitution) are caught in the same pass. Algorithm 5
shows the resulting loop. A rule is said to “fire” on (P, A) whenever it removes or rewrites at least one object or
constraint.
Appendix B.4. Sanity Checks
