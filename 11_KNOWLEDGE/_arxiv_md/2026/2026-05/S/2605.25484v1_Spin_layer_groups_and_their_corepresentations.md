Spin layer groups are the crystallographic symmetry groups with a periodic plane, and their
symmetry operations are inherited from three-dimensional (3D) spin space groups. However, the
direct application of 3D symmetry groups to two-dimensional systems is often inadequate due to
anisotropic axes and dimensional reduction. In this work, we systematically classify inequivalent
spin layer groups and analytically derive their irreducible corepresentations.
This classification
establishes a foundational framework for investigating symmetry-protected properties and novel
quantum states in low-dimensional magnetic materials.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Symmetry plays a central role in condensed matter
physics, with group theory providing its fundamental
mathematical language. When time-reversal symmetry
is incorporated into crystalline symmetries, the result-
ing groups are known as magnetic groups [1–3]. These
groups extend the ordinary point groups or space groups
and enable the description of a wide range of mag-
netic phenomena.
In magnetic groups, spin rotations
are locked to lattice rotations due to spin–orbit coupling
(SOC). Breakng this lock makes spin and lattice can
rotate independently, leading to the formation of more
complex symmetry groups known as spin groups [4–6].
Therefore, spin groups are particularly suitable for de-
scribing magnetic systems with negligible or weak SOC,
such as those composed of light elements or low-angular-
momentum electrons. The spin-group extension of space
group is called spin space group (SSG). Recently, three-
dimensional (3D) SSGs have been systematically inves-
tigated and classified into collinear, coplanar, and non-
coplanar types, each potentially hosting different phys-
ical properties [7–9]. Based on spin groups, novel phe-
nomena have been predicted systematically. Specifically,
218 novel quasiparticles unique to spin groups have been
identified [10], 498 unconventional magnon modes have
been reported [11].
More importantly, altermagnetism
has been identified as a novel magnetic class that ex-
hibits substantial momentum-dependent spin splitting in
the absence of SOC, which is naturally explained by the
collinear spin groups [12, 13].
In recent years, intrinsic two-dimensional (2D) mag-
netic materials have been synthesized, such as CrI3,
Cr2Ge2Te6, and Fe3GeTe2 [14–16]. These systems pro-
vide a versatile platform for exploring emergent quantum
phenomena. Notably, the altermagnetic phase is not re-
stricted to 3D bulk crystals but can be naturally inher-
ited by 2D systems. Within the spin group framework,

These

phases, particularly in low-dimensional systems.
From a theoretical perspective, the reduced dimen-
sionality of 2D systems imposes restriction on magnetic
materials.
According to the Mermin-Wagner theorem,
long-range magnetic order in two dimensions requires
strong magnetic anisotropy to suppress thermal fluctu-
ations [22]. In 2D materials, such anisotropy can arise
from both SOC and crystal field effects [23, 24], reflect-
ing the intricate interplay between spin and lattice de-
grees of freedom. More importantly, the reduction from
three to two dimensions breaks the equivalence of spatial
directions and introduces anisotropic axes, leading to a
restructuring of symmetry operations.
As a consequence, while 3D SSGs can in principle be
applied to 2D systems by incorporating a sufficiently
thick vacuum layer. However, they do not provide a natu-
ral or minimal description of the underlying symmetries.
In a 3D framework, symmetry operations irrelevant to
the 2D geometry are formally retained, leading to redun-
dancy and a lack of adaptation to reduced dimension-
ality. To address these issues, one must introduce spin
layer groups (SLGs), which extend ordinary layer groups
within the spin-group framework. Similar to the case in
conventional crystallography, where the 80 layer groups
can be mapped to the 230 space groups [25]. The symme-
try operations of SLGs can be inherited from 3D SSGs,
the correspondence is not one-to-one due to dimensional
reduction and anisotropy, such that distinct SLGs may
map to the same SSG. Consequently, a systematic clas-
sification of SLGs is essential for establishing a complete
and non-redundant symmetry description of 2D magnetic
materials. While the concept of SLGs has been applied

II.
CONSTRUCTION OF SLGs
Generally, there are two fundamental approaches to
constructing SLGs. The first proceeds from the 80 layer
groups by associating them with irreducible real repre-
sentations of the orthogonal group O(N) (N = 1, 2, 3)
and enumerating their normal subgroups or supergroups
[7–9].
The second approach is based on dimensional
reduction, recognizing that the symmetry operations
of SLGs descend naturally from three-dimensional spin
space groups. Specifically, SLGs are obtained by restrict-
ing 3D SSGs to systems with periodicity in two dimen-
sions [27]. In this work, we adopt the latter approach and
classify all inequivalent SLGs within this framework.
A.
Dimensional reduction
Generally, consider an SLG L. A corresponding SSG S
can be constructed from L as follows. Let N denote the
lattice translation group normal to the periodic plane of
L. The SSG is then obtained as the semi-direct product
S = N ⋊L.
(1)
Since N is a normal subgroup of S, we have
L ∼= S/N.
The corresponding isomorphic map ϕ : L →S/N is de-
fined by
ϕ : {S||R|t} 7→{S||R|τ}N,
where {S||R|t} ∈L, {S||R|τ} ∈S, and the translation
part t is given by the projection of τ onto the periodic
plane. The normality of N imposes a constraint on the
spatial rotation part R of S. For {E||E|t3} ∈N and
{S||R|τ} ∈S, one has
{S||R|τ}{E||E|t3}{S||R|τ}−1 = {E||E|Rt3} ∈N. (2)
This requires Rt3 = ±t3, i.e., each rotation R preserves
both the 2D subspace V2 of the periodic plane and the
one-dimensional (1D) subspace V1 orthogonal to it. Ac-
cordingly, the matrix representation of R takes a block-
diagonal form O(2) ⊕O(1), where O(2) acts on V2 (leav-
ing t3 invariant) and O(1) = {±1} acts on V1 (along the
direction of t3).
The above facts provide a practical route to construct
SLGs from SSGs.
Starting from a given SSG S, one
identifies 1D translation subgroups N that are normal in
S, and then constructs the corresponding SLG through
the quotient S/N. In practice, for each SSG, one can
generate symmetry-equivalent realizations by cyclic per-
mutations of the coordinate axes a, b, c →b, c, a →c, a, b,
yielding corresponding axes a′, b′, c′. For each such real-
ization, one examines whether the 1D translation sub-
group along the c′-direction forms a normal subgroup of
S according to Eq. (2). If this condition is satisfied, an
SLG can be obtained from S. The resulting SLGs may
still be equivalent under origin shifts or axis transforma-
tions. The final step is therefore to identify inequivalent
SLGs.

IV.
COREPRESENTATIONS OF SLGs
In principle, the coreps of SLGs can be obtained either
from little-group theory or from those of the correspond-
ing SSGs. Specifically, one approach is to compute the
small coreps of the little groups of SLGs, from which all
coreps can be constructed by induction [31].
Alterna-
tively, the coreps of an SLG L can be derived from the
coreps D of an SSG S satisfying S = L ⋉N. In this
case, the relevant coreps are those satisfing ker D ⊇N
in which ker D = {g ∈S | D(g) = I} [27, 32]. While the
coreps of SSGs have been computed in previous works
[7–9, 33], there remain two main limitations.
First,
existing databases are constructed using Hamiltonian-
based methods, and the results are largely numerical [33],
which hinders further analytical treatment. Second, in
Refs. [7, 9, 33], no publicly accessible database is avail-
able.
To overcome these limitations, we employ little-
group theory to derive all coreps of SLGs analytically
[2].

Single-valued and double-valued coreps of SLGs
The symmetry analysis of electronic systems with spin-
1/2 degrees of freedom requires the use of the double
groups of SLGs, namely the double covers of the corre-
sponding single SLGs. While the single SLGs are suffi-
cient for bosonic or artificial systems, the double groups
are indispensable for describing electronic band struc-
tures.
To derive the coreps of double SLGs, one need also first
determine the double-valued irreps of the double SLGs
and then calculate the corep types of the irreps. The key
distinction for double-valued irreps is that the character
of the 2π rotation (denoted as ¯E) satisfies the condition
χ( ¯E) = −χ(E). Furthermore, the time-reversal operator
must satisfy T 2 = ¯E for fermionic systems, in contrast
to T 2 = E in the single-valued case. In coplanar and
non-coplanar SLGs, which consist of discrete symmetry
operations, these double-valued coreps can be obtained
through standard procedure as described in Sec. IV B-
IV C.
However, the collinear case requires a more careful
treatment of the continuous group C∞, which is extended
to its double cover ¯C∞.
The rotation angle θ for the
element cθ ∈¯C∞spans the range [0, 4π). The isomor-
phism between C∞and ¯C∞(defined via the bijection

SUMMARY
The type of symmetry-enforced degeneracies in the
27,529 SLGs with Ik ≤8 are summarized in Table II.
Compared with magnetic layer groups, in which spin and
spatial rotations are locked, SLGs exhibit a significantly
larger variety of possible band degeneracies, particularly
high-fold degeneracies. The underlying group structure
plays a central role in determining the degeneracy types.
A comprehensive quantitative mapping between all de-
generacy types and the corresponding SLGs is provided
in S4 of the Supplemental Material [30].
It should be mentioned that the single-valued coreps
of collinear SLGs behave fundamentally differently from
those of standard space groups. In conventional single
space groups, the combination of spatial inversion P and
time reversal symmetry T does not enforce additional
band degeneracy, while double space groups always en-
force at least double degeneracy due to (PT )2 = ¯E. How-
ever, A single collinear SLG with {T ||P|0} symmetry
can indeed enforce double degeneracy. For example, con-

We then enumerate all subgroups of P. For each sub-

Appendix C: Irreducible representations of abstract
groups
In all cases considered in this work, the central ex-
tension of the little co-group is finite. Therefore, we can
always find a finite abstract group isomorphic to the cen-
tral extension. For groups of order less than 2000, ex-
cluding those of orders 512 and 1024, we directly use the
Small Groups Library in GAP, which contains the irreps
required in this work [35]. For the remaining cases, in-
cluding groups of orders 512, 1024, and 2048, we first con-
struct an isomorphic polycyclic group in GAP and then
compute its irreps using Dixon’s algorithm for polycyclic
groups [34].
