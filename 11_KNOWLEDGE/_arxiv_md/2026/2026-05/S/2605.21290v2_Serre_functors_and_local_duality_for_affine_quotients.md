1. Introduction
1.1. Overview. The purpose of this short note is to study Serre functors of categories of the form QCoh(Y)
where Y = Spec A/G is the quotient of a reductive group G acting on Spec A with a unique closed orbit.
1.1.1.
Recall, if C is a compactly generated DG category then there exists a continuous functor SeC : C →C
defined by the property that
HomC(x, y)∨≃HomC(y, SeC(x))
for all x, y ∈C compact. The functor SeC is known as the Serre functor of C.
Such functors have been studied since the classical theorems of Serre and Grothendieck and their generalisa-
tion to triangulated categories is seen in works such as that of Bondal–Kapranov [1]. Traditionally, in these
instances, it is required SeC be an equivalence but this assumption is often dropped in the DG setting where
such functors have been studied and used to great effect by, for instance, Beraldo in [2] and Gaitsgory–Yom
Din in [3].
1.1.2.
When C = QCoh(Y) for Y as above, the Serre functor is given by tensoring with an object SY ∈
QCoh(Y). We find that SY is the local cohomology of ωY at the unique closed orbit. Using this, we are able
to develop analogues of Matlis and local duality.
In the remainder of this introduction, we recall the classical theorems of local and Matlis duality in order to
highlight parallels with our results on affine quotients. After this, we briefly study the specific example of
Y ≃A1/Gm to illustrate these connections before stating our main results.
1.1.3.
Suppose (A, m, k) is a local ring and I is the injective hull of k in the abelian category of A–modules.
Then Matlis proves that, for an A–module M, if M is finitely generated, HomA(M, I) is Artinian. Conversely,
if M is Artinian, then HomA(M, I) is finitely generated. Furthermore, if M is either finitely generated or
Artinian, M ≃HomA(HomA(M, I), I).
One notes that, if M is finitely generated then its local cohomology modules Hi
mM are Artinian. Thus,
if we let Γm denote the derived local cohomology functor, the functor HomA(−, I) defines an involutive
equivalence between Coh(Spec A) and Γm(Coh(Spec A)).
The local duality theorem of Grothendieck then tells us that the functor HomA(Γm(−), I) is representable
by the normalised dualising complex ωA of A.

1.2. Main results. While the example of A1/Gm relied on the smoothness of A1, this assumption is not
necessary. What we really use is the existence of a unique closed orbit under the Gm action. Let us spell out
the more general case.
1.2.1.
Let G be a reductive group acting on the classical affine scheme Spec A of finite type. Consider the
stack Y = Spec A/G. The category QCoh(Y) is proper (that is, HomQCoh(Y)(F1, F2) has finite dimensional
total cohomology whenever F1 and F2 are compact) if Spec A has finitely many closed orbits under the action
of G. We focus specifically on the case where there is a unique closed orbit, in which case Y ≃Spec B/H
where Spec B has a unique closed orbit under the action of reductive H and, furthermore, this closed orbit
is a fixed point. Therefore, we will assume Spec A has a unique closed orbit which is a fixed point.
Not only is QCoh(Y) proper but its compact objects are dualisable so the Serre functor is given by tensoring
with some SY ∈QCoh(Y).
1.2.2.
Let i : BG ,→Y be the closed immersion of the fixed point and j : Y◦,→Y its open complement.
The full subcategory of QCoh(Y) given by the kernel of j∗is denoted QCoh(Y)BG. It is the subcategory of
quasicoherent sheaves set-theoretically supported at the fixed point.
The embedding QCoh(Y)BG ,→QCoh(Y) admits a right adjoint, denoted ΓBG and called the local cohomology
functor. Explicitly,
ΓBGF ≃ker(F →j∗j∗F).
By the projection formula, ΓBGF ≃ΓBGOY ⊗F.

1.2.3.
Our main result is an explicit description of SY:
Theorem A. We have SY ≃ΓBGΨ(ωY)[dim G].
The main idea behind the proof is showing that SY is supported entirely at the fixed point. From this, its
defining property allows us to characterise it as above.
We remark that this description for SY was found by Beraldo in [2] in the specific case where Spec A is the
nilpotent cone in the Lie algebra of G.
1.2.4.
Using this description, we are able to prove an analogue of Matlis and local duality. Writing
DMat = HomQCoh(Y)(−, SY) and DSe = HomQCoh(Y)(−, Ψ(ωY))
we prove:
Theorem B. For all F in Coh(Y),
DMat(ΓBGF) ≃DSe(F)[dim G] and DMat(F) ≃ΓBG(DSe(F))[dim G],
hence DMat defines involutive equivalences Coh(Y)op →ΓBG(Coh(Y)) and ΓBG(Coh(Y))op →Coh(Y).
The proof of this of course uses Theorem A. The main step aside from this is showing that, for F ∈Coh(Y),
DSe(j∗j∗F) ≃0 from which we can deduce that DMat(ΓBGF) ≃DSe(F)[dim G]. To do this, we show first
that DMat(ΓBGF) lands in Coh(Y) which forces DSe(j∗j∗F) ∈Coh(Y). We can then exploit Proposition 4.1.6
which tells us that HomQCoh(Y)(i∗OBG, −) is conservative on Coh(Y).
1.2.5.
We use both Theorem A and Theorem B to also calculate the Serre functor for QCoh(Y◦) in
Proposition 4.4.1. This application recovers classical Serre duality for projective varieties.

2. Preliminaries
In this section, we outline the notations and conventions of the paper as well as defining some basic notions
and recalling a few standard results that are worth highlighting. It is recommended the reader skip this
section and only refer back to it when necessary.
We always work over an algebraically closed field k of characteristic zero.
2.1. DG categories. As the existence of a Serre functor on compactly generated DG categories follows from
abstract nonsense, we provide here some of the basics of higher category theory needed in order to deduce
the existence of the Serre functor. We ignore all problems of a set-theoretic nature.

2.1.1.
For us, all categories are DG categories (i.e. stable ∞-categories with an action of Vect) except for a
few instances where we work with abelian categories (which we make clear). We generally assume DG
categories are cocomplete (i.e. contain arbitrary direct sums) unless stated explicitly otherwise or unless
they are of the form Perf(Y) or Coh(Y) for Y a prestack. We assume functors between categories are exact
and continuous (i.e. commute with arbitrary direct sums) unless explicitly stated otherwise or if they are
(internal) hom functors.
The category of exact functors between C and D is denoted Fun(C, D) and the full subcategory of continuous
functors is denote Funcont(C, D).
In general, hom-spaces are denoted by HomC(−, −) while internal homs (when they exist) are denoted by
HomC(−, −). When we do work in an abelian category, we use HomC(−, −) and HomC(−, −) instead.
2.1.2.
Let C be a category. An object c ∈C is compact if the functor HomC(c, −) is continuous. The full
subcategory of compact objects of C is denoted by Ccpt.
2.1.3.
A family of objects {cα} in C generates C if the family of functors HomC(cα, −) is a conservative
family, that is HomC(cα, c) ≃0 for all cα implies c ≃0. This means that the only cocomplete full subcategory
containing the cα is C itself. We say C is compactly generated if there exists a family of compact objects
generating C.
2.1.4.
Suppose C is a category which is not necessarily cocomplete. The cocomplete category Ind(C) is the
ind-extension of C. It is equipped with a functor C →Ind(C) and is characterised by the property that, for all
cocomplete categories D, the morphism
Funcont(Ind(C), D) →Fun(C, D)
is an isomorphism. The lift of a functor C →D to a continuous functor Ind(C) →D is called the ind-extension
of the functor. The category Fun(Cop, Vect) is a model for Ind(C)
2.1.5.
The functor C →Ind(C) is fully faithful and its essential image identifies with Ind(C)cpt. Moreover,
if C is cocomplete and compactly generated, then the functor Ind(Ccpt) →C is an equivalence.
2.1.6.
We say C is proper if x, y ∈Ccpt implies HomC(x, y) ∈Vectcpt. Recall, Vectcpt consists of objects with
finite dimensional total cohomology. We say C is reflexive if x, y ∈Ccpt implies HomC(x, y) is reflexive, that
is if each of its cohomologies are finite dimensional.

For a prestack Y, the category Perf(Y) is the full subcategory of QCoh(Y) consisting of dualisable

2.2.5.

We remark that QCoh(Y) is compactly generated by Perf(Y) and IndCoh(Y) is compactly generated by

We have a natural map π : Y →BG.
As π∗is conservative, QCoh(Y) is compactly generated by

2.3. Equivariant algebra. We will need to use some results on the category C = QCoh(Y)♡, that is the
abelian category of G–equivariant A–modules. These are G–equivariant version of many classical algebra
results which will be useful for us to reference.
2.3.1.
A G–ideal of A is a G–equivariant A–module contained in A. We say A is G–local if it has a unique
maximal G–ideal.
2.3.2.
The category C has enough injectives (since Spec A/G is algebraic with affine diagonal) and thus
every object has an injective hull. Recall that an essential extension of a G–equivariant A–module M is a
module N containing M such that every other G–submodule of N intersects M non-trivially. The injective
hull of M is then an injective essential extension.
2.3.3.
We say M ∈C is G-free if M ≃π∗V where V is a representation of G. Note that for every M ∈C
there is an epimorphism from a G–free module to M.
Suppose m is a maximal G-ideal of A. Then note that G acts transitively on Spec(A/m) so Spec(A/m) ≃
G/H for some subgroup H. As a result, Spec(A/m)/G ≃BH and we can conclude that every G–equivariant
A/m–module is G–free.
2.3.4.
Let us assume that A is G–local with unique maximal G–ideal m. If X ∈C is finitely generated and
mX = X then, by Nakayama, there exists m ∈m such that mx = x for all x ∈X. Note, this forces m ∈AG
so (m ∩AG)X = X. However, if A is G–local then AG is local so Nakayama then implies X = 0.
2.3.5.
As an application of this, suppose P ∈C is projective. Take F to be a G–free module with an
epimorphism F →P. Let K be the kernel in C. In the same way one proves it with ordinary local rings, we
see that K ⊆mF and since P is projective, we deduce that mK = K in C. Thus, K = 0. It follows that all
projective objects in C are G–free.
2.3.6.
Take M ∈C. The G–socle of M is taken to be
SocG(M) = HomC(A/m, M).
This is naturally a G–equivariant module over A/m and hence is G–free. We can also consider it as a
G–submodule of M. Hence, if M is G–Artinian, SocG(M) ≃A/m ⊗k V where V is a finite dimensional
representation of G. Note that if N is a G–submodule of M, N ∩SocG(M) ≃SocG(N) which is non-zero as
N is also G–Artinian (hence it contains a simple G–submodule which must be A/m). So M is an essential
extension of SocG(M). They thus have the same injective hull. Write E for the injective hull of A/m. Then
the injective hull of M is E ⊗k V.

3.1. Existence of Serre functors.
3.1.1.
Let C be a compactly generated category. Then
C ≃Ind(Ccpt) ≃Fun((Ccpt)op, Vect).
Thus, for all x ∈Ccpt the functor HomC(x, −)∨is representable by some SeC(x). By ind-extension, the
assignment x 7→SeC(x) defines a continuous functor SeC : C →C. By construction, it is characterised by the
property that
HomC(x, y)∨≃HomC(y, SeC(x))
for all x ∈Ccpt and all y ∈C although it is enough to check this property with y ∈Ccpt. When C is obvious
from context we just write Se instead of SeC.
3.1.2.
Suppose C is equipped with a symmetric monoidal structure with compact unit 1. Further assume
that all compact objects in C are dualisable. Observe that
HomC(x, y)∨≃HomC(1, x∨⊗y)∨≃HomC(y, Se(1) ⊗x).
Hence, Se is equivalent to tensoring by Se(1).
Notation 3.1.3. When C is as above, we write SC for Se(1).
3.1.4.
In the case when C is proper, note that, for x ∈Ccpt, the functor HomC(x, −) preserves compactness
and so has a continuous right adjoint T. See that T is determined by T(k) ∈C and
HomC(y, T(k)) ≃HomVect(HomC(x, y), k) ≃Hom(x, y)∨.
Hence, T(k) = Se(x). This provides another justification for the existence of the Serre functor when C is
proper.
3.1.5.
The Serre functor is fully faithful on compact objects if and only if C is reflexive [3, Proposition 1.2.6].
Thus, if C is reflexive; its compact objects are dualisable under some symmetric monoidal structure; and the
monoidal unit is compact, then
HomC(x ⊗SC, y ⊗SC) ≃HomC(x, y)
for all x, y ∈Ccpt.

4. Serre functors on affine quotients
4.1. Elementary properties of affine quotients. We now focus our study on the stack Y as defined in
Section 2.2.5. It is perfect so its Serre functor is given by tensoring with SY. We would like to consider cases
when QCoh(Y) is also proper.
Proposition 4.1.1. If Spec A has finitely many closed G–orbits then QCoh(Y) is proper.
Proof. It is enough to check that HomQCoh(Y)(OY(λ), OY(µ)) is compact for any λ, µ ∈ΛG. By dualisability,
it is enough to check HomQCoh(Y)(OY, OY(λ)) is compact for all λ ∈ΛG. Observe that this is the vector
space underlying p∗OY(λ), which is coherent by [6, Theorem 4.16(x)], and lands in the heart by t-exactness.
Thus it is a finitely generated AG–module. The claim is thus proven if AG is finite dimensional over k.
Closed orbits of Spec A correspond bijectively to points of Spec AG (this is [6, Theorem 4.16(iv)] or [7,
Proposition 0.1, Theorem 1.1]). Thus, if Spec A has finitely many closed orbits, Spec AG has finitely many
maximal ideals.
Note AG is a finitely generated k-algebra (this is Hilbert’s 14th problem which was proven by Nagata when
G is linearly reductive, or it is [6, Theorem 4.16(xi)]), thus it has finitely many maximal ideals if and only if
it is Artinian. Thus AG is Artinian and Noetherian so is indeed finite dimensional over k.
□
4.1.2.
It thus makes sense to consider when Spec A has finitely many closed orbits. From now on, we will
assume that, in particular, Spec A has a unique closed orbit. Equivalently, A is G–local. Also, note AG is
local.
Take a point x ∈Spec A in this orbit and let H be its stabiliser. By Luna’s slice theorem, there is a locally
closed affine subvariety Spec B of Spec A containing x such that {x} is the unique closed orbit of H acting
on Spec B and Y ≃Spec B/H.
Therefore, we assume from now on that Spec A has a unique closed orbit and that it is a fixed point. Note
that this is equivalent to the unique G–maximal ideal of A being maximal as an ordinary ideal.

4.2.5.
Our next minor result is how SY is the injective hull of i∗OBG in the abelian category QCoh(Y)♡

Proof. Since Hom(SY, SY) ≃OY, if SY were decomposable then OY would be too. If OY were decomposable,

so cannot contain a non-trivial idempotent.

Corollary 4.2.7. In the abelian category QCoh(Y)♡, SY is the injective hull of i∗OBG.
Proof. Since we identify i∗OBG with A/m where m is the unique, maximal G–ideal of A, we see that the
