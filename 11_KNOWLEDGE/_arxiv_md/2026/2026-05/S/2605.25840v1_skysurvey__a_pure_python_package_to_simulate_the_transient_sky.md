ABSTRACT
Context. Accurate simulation of astronomical observations is a critical element for any modern analyses, be it to measure event rates, analyses
population properties, validate or train pipelines, account for selection effects, or correct biases.
Aims. We present a novel pure python package named skysurvey made to enable the user to quickly simulate astrophysical transients as observed
by a survey. The package is structured to make the implementation of any complex population modeling fast and easy.
Methods. skysurvey relies on three core objects: a Target, that models how an astrophysical target exists in nature, a Survey, that specifies
how the sky has been observed and, a DataSet that combine these two to generate data as they would have been acquired. In addition, we present
a side stand-alone package named modeldag that contains the core structure that simplifies the parameter modeling.
Results. We present in this paper how skysurvey is structured and we clearly illustrate how the code can straightforwardly be used to simulate
complex populations, such as Type Ia Supernovae with varying color-brightness β term. We also illustrate how the package can be made to replicate
the rate and redshift distribution of the ZTF SNe Ia DR2 dataset.
Conclusions. The skysurvey package, already used in recent scientific publications, is now ready for general usage and paves the way for future
use of simulations such as simulation based inference.
Key words. Simulation ; Cosmology ; Supernovae
1. Introduction
Simulations are the foundation of many scientific analyzes, as
they allow scientists to validate methods on cases where the
ground truth is known. This is particularly useful when deal-
ing with selection effects that impact the vast majority of astro-
nomical and cosmological studies ; be it because of instrumen-
tal limits, because the Milky Way, or, say, of the availability of
follow-up instruments. Simulations are then used to validate in-
ference pipelines or to probe what has not been observed given
what has. The latter aspect is key to derive rates (e.g. Pain et al.
2002; Leaman et al. 2011; Perley et al. 2020), to study proper-
ties of the underlying population (e.g. Li et al. 2011; Vincenzi et
al. 2019; Dimitriadis et al. 2025) or to correct the selection ef-
fect when deriving cosmological parameters (e.g., Betoule et al.
2014; Kessler & Scolnic 2017; Scolnic et al. 2018; Riess et al.
2019; Brout et al. 2022). The use of massive realistic simulations
has furthermore been shown to be able to train machine-learning
methods to infer model parameters, a technique now known as
simulation based inference (e.g. Cranmer et al. 2020; Karchev
et al. 2023; Ho et al. 2024; Boyd et al. 2026, and references
therein). Finally, simulations are essential to plan future instru-
ments and surveys (e.g. Goobar et al. 2002; Kessler et al. 2010;
Bernstein et al. 2012; Nissanke et al. 2013; Hounsell et al. 2018).
In extra-galactic transient sciences, the SNANA package
(Kessler et al. 2009) has long been the central simulation soft-
ware. Among many other key successes, SNANA has been the
reference software used to prepare the upcoming Rubin Large
Survey of Space and Time (LSST, Ivezi´c et al. 2019) and the
cosmology program of the Roman Space Telescope (Rose et al.
2021, 2025). There, SNANA was most notably the software suite

used to simulate realistic transients as part of the PLAsTiCC and
ELAsTiCC classification challenges (Kessler et al. 2019; Hložek
et al. 2023), which have been extensively used to develop and
explore classification algorithms. Outside its use for transients
modeling, SNANA has been the central tool behind the last decade
of precision cosmological measurements from Type Ia Super-
novae (SNe Ia) (e.g. Scolnic et al. 2018; Brout et al. 2022; Vin-
cenzi et al. 2024; Popovic et al. 2025) via the Beams with Bias
Correction method (BBC, Scolnic & Kessler 2016; Kessler &
Scolnic 2017; Popovic et al. 2021).
But SNANA is not without downsides, mostly stemming from
its long legacy. SNANA is a complex combination of C, fortran
and python, making installation famously complex and largely
limited to a handful of high performance computing servers. Fur-
ther compounding the approachability problem is the legacy of
its 10+ years of development: the software contains hard-coded
numbers and structures that predate original analyses approach
to recently discovered issues. Notably, the SN-host correlations
(e.g., Sullivan et al. 2010; Rigault et al. 2013; Roman et al. 2018;
Kelsey et al. 2021; Rigault et al. 2020; Brout, & Scolnic 2021)
was not considered in the initial development of SNANA. This led
recent analyses to iterative fudging and new implementations of
the SN-to-host matching to recover observed distributions for
the treatment of SN-to-host correlation across, e.g., Smith et al.
(2020), Brout, & Scolnic (2021), Popovic et al. (2021), Popovic
et al. (2023), and Wiseman et al. (2022). Because of this long
development cycle, these new features are piped through pre-
defined structures such as libraries of host galaxies (HOSTLIBs)
and hard-coded probability distribution functions (WGTMAPs).
SNANA uses .input files, ideal for long-term storage and repli-
cability, but complex and spread across multiple fronts within
SNANA. The addition of the pippin wrapper (Hinton & Brout

a fast running pip installable library that would enable any
user to model (complex) simulations of any kind of astrophys-

this paper was being written, the LightCurveLynx survey sim-

simulated SN Ia populations (Dai et al. 2026), further illustrating
the need for alternative simulation codes to SNANA.
This paper presents skysurvey version 1.0. Ongoing or fu-
ture developments shall continue improving the code, both in
terms of speed and flexibility, while conserving the core aspect
of the package: an easy to use software made to enable the user
to simulate complex astrophysical targets and surveys. We in-
vite the user to refer to the online documentation that contains
many usage examples including those described in this paper.
The code is publicly available on GitHub and can be installed
using the pip install skysurvey command.
Since its initial development, the skysurvey package has
already been used to publish scientific analyses: to reproduce
an existing dataset to understand its characteristics (Amenouche
et al. 2025; Rigault et al. 2025), to simulate realistic Super-
novae data to validate an inference pipeline (Ginolin et al.
2025a,b), to study the origin of unexpected rate variations with
redshift (Gilles Lordet et al. 2026), to compare observed Super-
novae rate with expectations in early LSST data (Freeburn et al.
2025), to analyse the observations of strongly lensed supernovae
(Sagués Carracedo et al. 2024), to forecast measurements of H0
anisotropies with ZTF (Barjou-Delayre et al. 2026), etc. More
analyses are ongoing, such as a complex Type Ia Supernovae
modeling (Ginolin et al. in prep), a validation of dark energy
and large scale structure cosmological inference pipelines (Be-
toule et al. in prep, Kebadian et al. in prep.). The skysurvey
package is also particularly well suited to train simulation based
inference pipeline (Trigui et al. in prep), to derive core-collapse
Supernovae rate and luminosity functions (Qin et al. in prep) or
to validate strongly lensed supernovae analyses (Osman Hjortlun
et al. in prep.).

intuition, which in turns enables the user to easily build complex
(realistic) simulations thanks a straightforward user interface.
2. modeldag: build any parameter model
At the core of skysurvey’s parameter generation is the
modeldag1 package. This python package enables a user to
build complex models from a simple dictionary structure, form-
ing a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG). In this DAG, each node
represents a model parameter, and the edges represent dependen-
cies between them. Each parameter is associated to a function
that describes how this parameter is drawn. Thanks to the DAG
structure, the output of one parameter draw can be used as input
of the function that draws another. The strength of modeldag is
that this process is made straightforward by its simple python
dictionary structure.
This is fully illustrated in Fig. 1 for a simple model inspired
by the SNe Ia brighter-slower brighter-bluer relations (e.g., Tripp
1998; Riess et al. 1996): mi = α × x1,i + β × ci, with α and
β population parameters and mi, x1,i and ci individual SNe Ia
terms. As shown in the middle part of the left panel in Fig. 1,
The modeldag (nested-) dictionary structure is the following.
The first level keys (x1, c and m, Fig. 1) represent the param-
eters to be generated. The second level explains how to do so,
using only 2 entries: "func", which provide the information of
the function to be used to generate the parameter, and "kwargs",
which provide the function’s parameters. The core idea behind
modeldag is that one can use the symbol “@“ to use the result
of a parameter’s drawing as parameter input for another param-
eter, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In that example, x1 and c are drawn
from normal distribution, but m is generated by combining them
linearly ; notice the x1=’@x1’ and c=’@c’ in m’s kwargs.
2.1. Any function to generate parameters
Any function can be used to generate parameters. As illustrated
in Fig. 1 modeldag handles functions that return parameter(s)
or that "transform" them (i.e. generate one from others). To han-
dle functions returning multiple parameters generated simultane-
ously, the second level dictionary accepts a third keyword along
side func and kwargs: "as". This last keyword enables the user
to specify each returned parameter name. As illustrated in Fig. 2,
this is useful when several parameters have to be generated si-
multaneously, as when randomly drawing points uniformly dis-
tributed in a shell like sky-coordinates (RA, Dec). Alternatively,
as could be used to rename a single parameter.
In addition, modeldag accepts functions that return (n-d)
probability distribution functions (PDF). To be automatically
identified by modeldag, these functions must return two arrays:
’xx’ and ’pdf’. Then modeldag will sample the parameter by
randomly drawing xx values following the individual point prob-
ability given by pdf ; see online documentation for examples.
Across the code, the size argument must be accepted by
functions that returned drawn parameters, like draw_normal in
Fig. 1. Transformation functions do not need it as size will be
taken from the length of input parameters (like x1 or c, Fig. 1).
Functions returning PDF should accept xx as input argument

3. skysurvey’s structure and logic
The skysurvey package aims to simulate the transient sky in a
forward way. Following that logic, the code enables (1) to gener-
ate Target objects as given by nature, i.e. noise- and selection-
free (see Section 4) ; (2) to create Survey objects containing ob-
serving information (which filter has been used when to observe
where under which conditions ; see Section 5) ; (3) to combine
both objects to generate realistic lightcurves. This combination
is trivially handled by the DataSet object (see Section 6). This
is illustrated in Fig. 3.
In the following subsection, we detail how these three ob-
jects work. The skysurvey package has other objects that are
used internally and will be described when relevant within these
subsections.
4. Target
A skysurvey.Target represents the astrophysical objects to
be simulated. The key attributes of this family of objects are:
template and model.
The template corresponds to the spectro-photometric
model from which a lightcurve or a spectrum could be gen-
erated. In its released version, skysurvey relies on sncosmo
for handling templates (Barbary et al. 2016). This is handled
through the skysurvey.Template object that manages interac-
tions between other skysurvey objects and sncosmo.Models
(so sncosmo.Sources ; see sncosmo’s documentation).
As such, any sncosmo.Source (built-in or user-defined) can
be used in skysurvey.
The model attribute is a modeldag object (see Section 2)
made to simulate target’s parameters. These parameters are, at
least, those needed by the template and sky coordinates (RA
and DEC) needed to match the target with a survey and thereby
to simulate realistic observations of the target (see Section 6).

4.1. Simulating targets
Once the Target is loaded, one can use the draw() method
to generate a sample. This method internally calls the draw()
method of modeldag (i.e. of the model attribute) to generate a
pandas DataFrame where each row corresponds to an individual
target and the columns are the parameters specified in the model.
This dataframe is stored as a Target.data attribute.
Up on calling of the .draw() method, the user can alter
the model or the model’s parameters, such as the redshift range
of the targets and their corresponding time-range (correspond-
ing to sncosmo’s t0 source parameters). This functionality fully
exploits the modularity of the dictionary-oriented structure of
modeldag at the core of the skysurvey package.
Target redshifts are generated following the Target.rate
attribute which could either be constant or a function of redshift
or of any parameter such as the host properties (see detail in
Section 4.2).

4.2. rates and redshifts
The rate at which a transient occurs is a crucial input for any sim-
ulation, as it specifies how redshifts are drawn. The skysurvey
package relies on volumetric rates in target/Gpc3/yr which
could be specified in two ways: (1) as a constant (float) or (2)
as a function. The former assumes that the rate is the same re-
gardless of, say, direction or redshift. The latter enables a full
flexibility of the rate: it could depend of redshift (e.g. Frohmaier
et al. 2019), and/or host properties (e.g. Sullivan et al. 2006) etc.
In any case, the rate function is expected to return the target’s
volumetric rate in target/Gpc3/yr.
To draw redshifts, the code first defines redshift bins (flexible
size, but ∆z = 10−4 by default), second computes the Universe’s
volume (Gpc3) of this redshift shell given a cosmology, third
computes the volumetric target rate at the pivot redshift of the
shell. Fourth, the target redshifts are randomly drawn from these
redshift bins weighted by the number of targets per bin.
4.3. Collections of targets
The skysurvey package enables the user to build Target based
on any sncosmo template (e.g. SALT-like, or SN templates) or,
even more generally, any user-defined sncosmo.Source.
However, for most of transients, such as Type II Supernovae,
there is no generic template (unlike SALT for SNe Ia) but rather
a variety of templates based on well-observed objects. For in-
stance, sncosmo contains 23 templates for Type II Supernovae
as defined by Vincenzi et al. (2019).

4.5. Modifying a target
The rate and model Target attributes can be changed to mod-
ify the number of targets expected as a function of redshift, or
how the target parameters are drawn. These changes can be ei-
ther when instantiating the target (options of the .from_draw()
class method) or on a loaded target using the associated
.set_rate, .set_model or .update_model[_parameters].
The update_model method fully exploit the modeldag dictio-
nary structure as explained in Section 2. With this method you
only change how parameters are drawn and/or connected to-
gether, or even add new parameters. As explained in Section 2,
a new graph will automatically be built once the model is up-
dated. The .update_model_parameters method directly af-
fects the kwargs entries on the model to change the arguments
of the function used to draw a variable, but not the functional
form itself.
This is illustrated in Fig. 5. In that example, we change the
default SNeIa rate (a constant volumetric rate of 2.35 × 104
target/Gpc3/yr, Perley et al. 2020) to a redshift-evolving rate
as modeled by Frohmaier et al. (2019). In addition, we make the
beta standardization parameter of the SNe Ia Tripp’s relation a
normally distributed variable instead of a usual constant.
As illustrated in this Section, the skysurvey package,
thanks to the modeldag structure, has been designed to make
easy complex / realistic simulations. The user is only limited by
its imagination and ability to define how the target’s parameters
should be drawn and connected together, i.e. setting the model
and/or the rate’s function. The rest is automatically handled by
skysurvey.

4.6. Adding effects
skysurvey provides a flexible mechanism for adding various
physical effects to the simulation. This is handled by the Effect
class, which can modify both the intrinsic properties of the tran-
sients and/or the way their lightcurves or spectra are generated.
In practice, a skysurvey.Effect has two core attributes:
1. .effect, an sncosmo.PropagationEffect, which alters
how transient fluxes (mag) are generated, be it when getting a
lightcurve or a spectrum ; 2. .model, a modeldag dictionary
that will be used to update the target’s model to generate the
.effect parameters if any.
An effect can be added to any target either during instan-
tiation, using the effect= option of the .from_draw() class
method, or directly using the .set_effect() method. A target
can handle multiple effects simultaneously.
4.6.1. Milky Way
A good example of this is the handling of Milky Way dust extinc-
tion. skysurvey provides a pre-defined Effect easily accessi-
ble by calling mw = skysurvey.Effect.from_name("mw").

al. 1989, by default), which has two parameters, "mwebv"
and "rv" (3.1 by default). The "mwebv" parameter refers to
the intensity of the Milky Way dust along the line of sight
and hence depends on the target’s coordinates. Consequently,
mw.model has a modeldag dictionary with func a custom
made get_mwebv(ra, dec, which=’planck’) function (re-

5. Survey
The Survey objects in skysurvey represent a time-domain sur-
vey. A survey is defined by a spatial footprint of the camera in
the sky, and observing logs (or corresponding simulation), spec-
ifying what part of the sky has been observed when and under
what conditions. Under the hood, Survey objects are made both
highly modular while remaining extremely fast in matching ob-
serving logs with any sky location (hence any target) by a com-
bination of shapely3 and geopandas4 libraries and, for some,
by using the healpix decomposition 5.

5.1. Built-in Surveys
While skysurvey makes it easy to build any kind of surveys, a
few major surveys have been built-in and more will be added in
the future. We invite the user to see online documentation for an

5.1.1. ZTF: a GridSurvey
The ZTF survey acquires observations following two pre-defined
pointing grids (see e.g. Bellm et al. 2019; Masci et al. 2019). The
main grid has fieldid<1000 while fieldids>1000 are those
of the secondary grid. This griding corresponds to the camera
footprint in the sky as illustrated, for the main grid, in Fig. 7.
The secondary grid is offset by a quarter of camera footprint and
is used to increase the full sky coverage of the survey and for
calibration purposes. In practice, this secondary grid is only ac-
quired 10% of the time.

5.1.2. LSST: a Survey
The LSST survey does not follow a pre-defined pointing grid but

opsim simulations when this paper is being written) hence con-

6. DataSet
The DataSet object is the final product of a skysurvey simula-
tion. This object simulates the lightcurves by matching an input
target (or list of) with the survey logs. The lightcurves gen-
eration is detailed in Section 6.1 and key DataSet methods are
presented in section 6.2. We show in section 7 how the DataSet
object can be used to accurately simulate a survey like the ZTF
Type Ia Supernovae dataset (Rigault et al. 2025).
6.1. Generating lightcurves
The lightcurve generation is made at instantiation of the
DataSet using the .from_targets_and_survey() class
method. This method, the backbone of the DataSet object,
works as follows.
First, targets are associated with the survey’s logs using
the .radec_to_field() survey method, which takes a frac-
tion of second to provide the fieldid (i.e., the healpix pixel
id for generic survey) associated to each target. In case of a
GridSurvey, a target can have several fieldids as fields can
overlap, and this fieldid could be a multi-layer index, espe-
cially if the survey footprint is decomposed by detectors or
amplifier (see Section 5). At this stage, we have a way to select
the entry logs corresponding to any given target and we know
which target has no entry (e.g. simulated outside the survey sky-
footprint).
Second, we loop over each target and we build its
sncosmo.Model including its template parameter alongside

with any additional effect (e.g. the Milky Way dust extinc-

Third, once the target sncosmo.Model is loaded, we sim-

"band", "mjd" and "zp" survey logs entry, and we compute the
flux error given the "skynoise", this simulated "flux" and the
camera "gain". Optionally, this computation is only made in the
[−50, +200] d rest-frame phase range around the model’s t0 (ex-
plosion date or peak-date for SALT-like models). This data is
stored as a pandas dataframe and that of each target are con-
catenated into a multi-index pandas.DataFrame with the first
index for the target and the second for the survey log entry. This
multi-index dataframe then become the .data attribute of the
DataSet.
Altogether, on a laptop, this whole operation, from match-
ing each target with the survey logs to the storage of the massive
multi-index dataframe, takes about 5min for 100k targets ( 350
targets processed per second) for a 3π sky survey like ZTF or
LSST. The code supports dask parallel computing to distribute
this computation on a cluster (especially interesting for O(106+)
targets simulations) and work is ongoing to replace sncosmo for
the flux generation to enable vectorization; 10x speed up is ex-
pected with a aim of simulating 100k target in less than 1min.
6.2. Using a DataSet.
The skysurvey.DataSet object has three main attributes:
.targets, a pointer to the input (multi-)target object; .survey,
a pointer to the input survey; and .data, the multi-index
dataframe containing the simulated lightcurves (see Section 6.1).
Based on these, and mostly on .data, the DataSet has top level
functionalities to ease the user experience.
First, the .get_target_lightcurve() method enables the
user to directly extract the lightcurves of any observed target
while applying a variety of cuts (.e.g as a function of flux sig-
nal to noise or time) or while adding extra information (e.g. the
phase). Second, the .get_ndetection() method enables the
user to have access to top level statistics associated with each
lightcurve. As illustrated in fig. 9, this method returns the num-
ber of detections (flux/fluxerr > n ; with n = 5 by default) per
target (and per band, optionally), combining (optionally) same
day detections. As illustrated in the example analyses (section 7),
where we reproduce the ZTF SN Ia DR2 dataset (Rigault et al.
2025), this method is particularly useful when simulating the se-
lection function of a survey as one usually requests a target to
have at least n detections, including at least m pre-max and p
post-max and, altogether at least b bands.

7.1. Altering survey data
According to Rigault et al. (2025, b) and Amenouche et al.
(2025), the magnitude limits associated with ZTF science images
do not fully reflect the depth of difference images from which
ZTF SN Ia DR2 lightcurves are derived. They do not account
for errors in the reference image and thus under-estimate the
sky-noise. Following their recommendations, the skynoise de-
rived from science image magnitude limits should be increased
by 23%, 17% and 20% for g, r, and i ZTF bands, respectively.
We illustrate in Fig. 10 (top panel) how this is done
with skysurvey. Since the observing logs are stored as
pandas.DataFrame, one can use standard pandas procedures
to alter the survey.data, in that example, we group the
data per band, and we apply to the skynoise entry of each
group the corresponding multiplicative coefficient. As such,
ztf.data["skynoise"] is now the proper skynoise to con-
sider for the simulation.

8. Conclusion and Discussion
This paper presents the skysurvey package. A pure python li-
brary that enables the user to easily simulate complex astrophys-
ical populations as they would be observed by a survey. This
package has been designed to be fast, intuitive, and highly mod-
ular in its ability to build any astrophysical target that could have
any parameter modeling structure. This later aspect is made pos-
sible thanks to modeldag, a standalone package developed in
parallel to skysurvey and also presented in this article.
The modeldag python package provides a way to create a
direct acyclic diagram (DAG) between parameters using a triv-
ial python dictionary structure that only has three leafs: func
that defines how the parameters should be drawn, kwargs that
provides the function arguments, as specifies the name of the
returned parameter(s) – which is particularly useful when return-
ing multiple parameter simultaneously (e.g. coordinates). The
trick of modeldag is the introduction of the "@" keyword in
kwargs, such that "x=’@a’" is parsed by the code as "use the
output of the a parameter draw as x input to draw this other pa-
rameter". This way, modeldag automatically creates DAG be-
tween parameters while offering an entire flexibility to the user
to specify how parameters should be drawn as any function can
be used and any parameter connections are accepted.
The skysurvey package contains three main parts. The first
is Target that specifies how the astrophysical population ex-
ists in nature. The Target parameters are based on modeldag
and the spectro-photometric (time series) template that provides
the target flux as a function of time, wavelength or filter are
based on sncosmo. As such, any sncosmo template or model
(built-in or user-defined) are accepted by skysurvey. By default
skysurvey has many built-in Target such as any Supernovae
type (SNeII, SNeIa, SNeIbc etc.) or kilonovae. The second part
is Survey, that carries the information of how the sky has been
observed. A user can easily build any customed survey (see on-
line documentation) or use a list of predefined ones such as ZTF
or LSST. the third part is DataSet, which builds the expected
observations of Target (or list of) by the Survey. The user can
focus on complexifying the astrophysical population at will, they
may alter the Survey – for instance to introduce calibration un-
certainties, but the DataSet will still be able to handle them.
That is the strength of skysurvey: the package is intuitive, user
oriented, and designed to be highly robust and easy to use. It is
finally solely based on well supported python libraries such as
numpy, pandas and sncosmo, ensuring stability and long term
user supports and maintenance.
