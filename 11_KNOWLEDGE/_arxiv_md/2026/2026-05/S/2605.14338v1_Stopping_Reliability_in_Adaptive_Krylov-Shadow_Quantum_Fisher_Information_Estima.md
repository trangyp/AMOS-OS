Stopping Reliability in Adaptive Krylov-Shadow Quantum Fisher Information
Estimation
Erjie Liu1 and Yangshuai Wang1
1Department of Mathematics, National University of Singapore, 10 Lower Kent Ridge Road, 119076, Singapore
(Dated: May 15, 2026)
Adaptive quantum Fisher information (QFI) estimation requires a stopping rule that distinguishes
accuracy from apparent numerical stability. For Krylov-shadow QFI estimators, finite Krylov order
K produces truncation bias, while finite sample budget M produces finite-M sampling-side error. We
show that a width-only empirical stopping rule, based on interval width and local Krylov stability, can
declare convergence at small (K, M) even when the post hoc error exceeds the requested tolerance; we
call this event a false stop. The mechanism is a narrow empirical interval centered on a biased low-K
estimate. We give a two-component stopping analysis that separates the Krylov and sampling terms,
and we implement a guarded rule that permits a success declaration only after minimum thresholds
in K and M and a persistence condition are satisfied. On a five-level dephasing benchmark at
n = 4 qubits, the guarded rule suppresses the false success declarations produced by the width-only
empirical rule, whose false-stop rates range from 0.16 to 0.68 across the tested noise levels. Under
the main fixed resource limit, the guarded rule refuses to make success declarations rather than
accepting biased low-K estimates; a separate true-relative-tolerance sampling-budget sequence then
shows that, after Krylov and sampling recalibration, the same decision principle can make success
declarations without observed false stops. These results show that stopping reliability is a distinct
design requirement for adaptive QFI estimation: sampling precision at fixed K does not by itself
establish that Krylov truncation bias is controlled.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Quantum Fisher information (QFI) is the operational
quantity that sets the local precision limit in quantum
sensing and metrology [1–3]. For a parameterized state
ρθ, the quantum Cramér–Rao bound lower bounds the
mean-squared error of any locally unbiased estimator
by 1/[NF(ρθ)] after N measurement repetitions [4–8].
Estimating QFI is therefore a basic certification step
for quantum-enhanced sensing, but exact evaluation is
exponentially costly as the Hilbert space grows. This has
motivated randomized-measurement and shadow-based
estimators [9–13] that trade exact diagonalization for
controlled statistical estimation.
Efficient estimation is not enough by itself: an adaptive
routine must also decide when its current estimate may
be reported as accurate. This paper studies that stopping
decision. For Krylov-shadow QFI estimation, the issue is
especially simple to state. The Krylov order K controls
truncation error, while the sample budget M controls
finite-M sampling-side error. A stopping rule that mainly
monitors sampling width can therefore stop at low K
around a biased value. We call such an event a false stop:
the routine makes a success declaration, but the post hoc
error exceeds the user tolerance.
The problem is observable in the noisy mixed-state
benchmark used below. For example, a representative
width-only empirical run terminates at (K, M) = (2, 32)
with tolerance ε = 0.2 while the final error is more than
ten times larger than ε. Across the five-level dephasing
grid in Section V, the same rule has false-stop rates be-
tween 0.16 and 0.68. The mechanism is not a rare tail
event: at small K, the Krylov projection of the sym-
metric logarithmic derivative is biased, but the empirical
sampling interval can still be narrow.
We address this stopping problem for adaptive QFI es-
timation via Krylov-subspace shadow measurements [13],
which approximate the symmetric logarithmic derivative
on a Krylov subspace of order K before applying the
classical-shadow formalism [11, 14]. Our first contribu-
tion is a two-component stopping analysis. We decompose
the total error into a Krylov-truncation term and a sam-
pling term, and state sufficient conditions under which a
success declaration is valid when calibrated component
bounds are allocated so their sum is below tolerance (The-
orem IV.1). The theorem states the calibrated target
condition. The practical rule uses observable quantities
tied to the two components, and the numerical study tests
how those quantities behave relative to that condition.
Our second contribution is the guarded stopping rule.
AKS-QFI (Adaptive Krylov-Shadow QFI) adapts (K, M)
under a user tolerance ε and monitors separate truncation-
side and sampling-side stopping evidence. The guarded
variant permits a success declaration only after minimum
thresholds in K and M and a persistence condition are
satisfied. On a noisy mixed-state benchmark with five
dephasing levels and 50 independent replicates per noise
level at n = 4, guarded stopping has no observed false
stops, while width-only stopping has false-stop rates from
0.16 to 0.68. Under the default resource limit the guarded
rule makes no success declarations; the main benchmark
therefore measures false-success suppression under fixed
resources. It also reduces median absolute error by up to
a factor of 4.8, showing that the width-only rule’s lower
cost comes from early termination at a biased value.
We then add a separate relative-tolerance recalibra-
tion to test success declarations. At true relative toler-
ance εrel = 0.05, with full Krylov resolution and a larger

Shadow-based protocols for scalable observable es-
timation originate with shadow tomography [14] and
the classical-shadow formalism [11], and are surveyed
in the randomized-measurement toolbox [12]. Krylov-
subspace shadow methods extend these tools to QFI esti-
mation by introducing the explicit resolution knob K [13].
Resampling-based uncertainty assessment for shadow es-
timators has been explored independently [15].
More
broadly, AKS-QFI fits into a wider near-term quantum-
computing concern: variational routines, including VQE,
are constrained by finite-shot objective estimation, initial-
ization, and trainability of the optimization landscape [16–
23]. In that setting, separating apparent convergence from
reliable accuracy is as important as reducing nominal
quantum resources.
Adaptive stopping has a rich history in classical se-
quential analysis [24]. The anytime-valid inference and
e-process literature [25] provides confidence sequences
that remain valid uniformly over an adaptive trajectory,
offering a principled path to sequential stopping guar-
antees. Standard confidence-sequence applications most
directly control a single martingale-type error process. Us-
ing them for Krylov-shadow QFI estimation would require
a joint construction for the truncation component and
the sampling component, which shrink at different rates
and are controlled by different resources. We identify this
as an open direction in Section VI.
Adaptive stopping in quantum state tomography has
been studied by Ferrie and Granade [26] and Granade et
al. [27] in the self-guided and rejection-sampling methods.
These methods adapt resource allocation based on pos-
terior credible intervals, but they assume the estimation
target is well-approximated by the current posterior mode
and do not separate truncation-type bias from statistical
variance. The false-stop pathology studied here, a nar-
row uncertainty interval coexisting with large systematic
bias from insufficient Krylov resolution, is therefore not
addressed in the same form by posterior-width stopping
alone.
Adaptive resource allocation in multi-armed bandit
problems [28] addresses the exploration–exploitation
tradeoff in selecting which resource to query next; the
regret formulation there is concerned with cumulative
loss rather than with reliable stopping under a tolerance
constraint. Closest in spirit to our setting are best-arm
identification algorithms with a fixed-confidence guaran-
tee [29], which certify a decision with a given probability
after observing enough samples. The key structural dif-
ference is the additional truncation component: best-arm
identification requires enough samples to separate arms,
whereas AKS-QFI stopping additionally requires that
Krylov truncation bias has been suppressed, a condition
that cannot be inferred from sampling statistics alone.
The specific problem of stopping reliability under this
coupled truncation-and-sampling error structure has not,
to our knowledge, been treated explicitly in the literature.
The false-stop phenomenon documented here and the
guarded stopping rule appear to be new.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sec-
tion II reviews QFI and the fixed-(K, M) Krylov-shadow
estimation setting. Section III introduces AKS-QFI and
its two-component indicator. Section IV presents the
width-only and guarded stopping rules, states the main
theoretical results, and formalizes the false-stop pathol-
ogy. Section V reports benchmark evidence and reliability
plots. Section VI discusses broader implications, recali-
bration requirements, and next steps. Appendices collect
proofs, algorithmic details, benchmark configuration, and
additional checks.

B.
Krylov-shadow estimation with two resource
knobs
Reference [13] introduced a fixed-(K, M) Krylov-
shadow estimator for QFI: given a Krylov order K and
sampling budget M chosen a priori, it constructs a
randomized-measurement estimator with provable bias
and variance bounds. The present work does not modify
the estimator of Ref. [13]; instead, it adds three compo-
nents that are absent from that work: (i) an adaptive in-
terface that selects (K, M) online from observable checks,
terminating automatically when a user-specified tolerance
is met; (ii) a formal stopping analysis that identifies under
what conditions a stopping claim is justified by simulta-
neous control of truncation and sampling errors, and why
width-only stopping fails; and (iii) a guarded stopping rule
with theory-derived parameter thresholds that acts as a
transparent safety filter, preventing false success decla-
rations in the statistically powered benchmark studied
here, at the cost of no success declarations under the
default resource limit. These contributions are modu-
lar: the adaptive interface and stopping analysis apply
to any estimator with the two-component error structure
of Eq. (G1), and are not specific to the Krylov-shadow
construction of Ref. [13].

C.
Problem statement: adaptive stopping
Given a target tolerance ε > 0 and an optional risk
parameter δ ∈(0, 1), the goal is to construct an adaptive
interface that controls (K, M) based on the observed quan-
tities (dK, wM), terminates automatically, and returns a
summary that makes the stopping decision explicit. The
central difficulty is that the two error sources governed
by K and M are decoupled: the sampling-side width wM
may become small at low (K, M), and a local inter-order
change may also underestimate unresolved Krylov bias,
even when the true truncation bias BK remains large.
Thus a stopping rule without minimum-resource and per-
sistence guards can terminate prematurely in noisy mixed-
state regimes. Addressing this failure mode requires an
indicator IK,M that keeps both error sources in view si-
multaneously. Its construction is presented in Section III,
and Section IV derives assumption-explicit conditions un-
der which a stopping claim can be trusted when both
error components are controlled.
III.
AKS-QFI: ADAPTIVE INTERFACE AND
ERROR INDICATOR
AKS-QFI is a decision-layer wrapper around a fixed-
(K, M) Krylov-shadow QFI estimator. Its purpose is to
turn two internal resource knobs, the Krylov order K and
the sampling budget M, into a single user-facing routine
that (i) adapts (K, M) under a tolerance target ε, (ii)
terminates automatically, and (iii) reports the stopping
evidence at termination. The Krylov-shadow estimator
is treated as a modular subroutine: AKS-QFI does not
alter the estimator itself, but controls when and how it is
invoked and when it is permitted to declare success.

V.

We evaluate the stopping layer of AKS-QFI on a con-
trolled benchmark designed to probe the failure mode
identified in Section IV: premature termination at low
resources when empirical tests pass before calibrated error
components are controlled. The central question is the
degree to which guarded stopping suppresses this failure
mode, and at what cost in effective terminal shot budget.
The benchmark compares termination decisions and post
hoc errors while keeping the underlying estimator fixed,
so that all observed differences are attributable to the
stopping rule.

VI.
DISCUSSION
A.
What this work establishes
The paper addresses the following concrete question:
when an adaptive Krylov-shadow QFI run declares con-
vergence, is the reported estimate actually within the
requested tolerance?
In the tested noisy mixed-state
benchmark, the answer for the width-only rule is often no.
At small (K, M) the empirical sampling interval can be
narrow even though the Krylov-truncation error is still
large, so the routine can stop around a biased value.
Theorem IV.1 gives the corresponding two-component
requirement. A valid success claim needs calibrated con-
trol of both the truncation component I(trunc)
K
and the
sampling component I(stat)
K,M . The bootstrap width and
adjacent-order change are observable checks tied to these
two components. The bootstrap width measures finite-
sample spread at fixed K; Krylov resolution controls a
different error term. This is why the stopping problem
matters: a run can have a narrow interval around the
wrong value. The theorem states the condition needed
for a valid success claim, and the benchmark study mea-
sures whether the guarded stopping rule aligns with that
two-component requirement.
Guarded stopping changes only the stopping rule. It
keeps the same estimator, but requires minimum Krylov
order, minimum sampling budget, and persistence across
eligible rounds before a success declaration. On the sta-
tistically powered n = 4 benchmark this removes all
observed false success declarations. Under the default
resource limit every guarded run reaches the resource
limit; the primary benchmark therefore establishes false-
success suppression under fixed resources. The separate
relative-tolerance recalibration (Section V E) supplies the
complementary result: at pϕ = 0.03, full Krylov resolution
and a sampling-budget sequence with persistence gives
12 guarded success declarations in 20 runs at εrel = 0.05,
with no observed false stops, and all 20 guarded termi-
nal estimates lie within the 5% relative tolerance. The
matched width-only baseline declares success in 19 of 20
runs but makes 15 false success declarations.
The cost of the guard is larger terminal budget. At
the highest dephasing levels, guarded stopping reduces
median absolute error by a factor of up to 4.8 relative to
width-only stopping while using substantially more mea-
surements. This is the intended tradeoff of the method:
it avoids low-budget success claims that are not sup-
ported by both error components. The classical over-
head of the guard is modest in the present algorithm.
Each bootstrap evaluation uses B resamples, giving an
O(BM) classical post-processing cost at budget M. The
reported benchmark configurations use B = 32 for the
main grid and guard ablation, and B = 16 for the relative-
tolerance recalibration. The effective-budget summaries
count randomized-measurement shots, not bootstrap re-
samples.
B.
Separation of concerns in adaptive estimation
The estimator and the stopping rule play different roles.
The estimator produces a QFI value from a chosen pair
(K, M). The stopping rule decides whether the current
value should be reported as meeting the tolerance. The
false stops in this paper arise from the second step: the
estimator can still improve with larger K, but the stopping
rule has already accepted a low-K value.


## Page 11


11
This separation is useful in practice.
It allows the
same estimator to be paired with a stricter decision rule,
and it identifies the binding obstruction at termination:
Krylov resolution, sampling width, or the resource limit.
The closed-form expressions for Kstop
min and M stop
min in Ap-
pendix G 4 are therefore best viewed as recalibration
formulas for new regimes, not as universal constants.
C.
Open directions
The most immediate need is a broader empirical char-
acterization of the false-stop phenomenon across system
sizes.
At n ∈{6, 8} with the current resource limit
(Kmax = 8), the default guard parameters (Kmin =
4, Mmin = 128, P = 2) are insufficient: truncation bias
dominates at these system sizes, and the guard’s minimum
Krylov order is too low to suppress it (Appendix H). The
closed-form threshold expressions in Appendix G 4 pro-
vide the recalibration tool, but applying them at n ≥6
will likely require increasing Kmax beyond the current
budget, which raises its own resource-allocation questions.
Establishing at what system size the guarded rule remains
effective within a given budget, and how the guard pa-
rameters should scale with n, is a concrete open problem.
A second open question concerns the statistical cali-
bration of wM under adaptive stopping. The bootstrap
interval used here quantifies finite-sample spread at a cho-
sen K. A deployment-level certificate would also account
for the data-dependent stopping time and combine the
sampling statement with a Krylov-truncation statement.
A deeper open question unifies two related gaps: the
absence of Fref in deployment, and the need to turn the
observable checks (dK, wM) into calibrated radii usable
in the finite-horizon certificate of Proposition G.11. Theo-
rem IV.1 does not require Fref when calibrated component
bounds are available; the reference enters only in the post
hoc benchmark evaluation (Eq. (19)) to label stopping
decisions as true or false stops. In this work, false-success
suppression is evaluated in controlled benchmark settings
with a computable ground truth. The stopping criterion
itself uses only observable quantities; the reference value
is used after the run to audit whether a declared success
was correct. Connecting these quantities to the true er-
ror decomposition of Eq. (G1) tightly enough to support
online error certification without Fref is the next theo-
retical problem. Time-uniform confidence sequences [25]
provide the most principled path: they give probability
bounds that hold uniformly over an adaptive trajectory
and could replace the static sub-Gaussian bound of Propo-
sition G.3 with a sequentially valid accuracy statement
without preselecting a finite attempt budget.
VII.
CONCLUSION
This work identifies and tests a specific failure of adap-
tive Krylov-shadow QFI estimation: a run can declare suc-
cess because the empirical sampling width is small, while
the Krylov-truncation error still puts the estimate out-
side the requested tolerance. The proposed fix is equally
specific. Guarded stopping requires evidence from both
resource directions before reporting success: sufficient
Krylov order, sufficient sampling budget, and persistence
of the sampling-width pass.
The evidence has two parts. In the main n = 4 bench-
mark, guarded stopping eliminates observed false success
declarations, with no success declarations under the de-
fault resource limit. In the relative-tolerance recalibration,
the same guarded logic produces success declarations with-
out observed false stops after the Krylov and sampling
budgets are increased, whereas a matched width-only base-
line remains unreliable. For finite adaptive runs, the held-
out confirmation construction in Proposition G.11 gives a
route from calibrated component radii to a sequential stop-
ping certificate. The next theoretical step is to turn the
terminal sampling-width and Krylov-stability quantities
themselves into such calibrated radii, or into time-uniform
confidence statements for the two-component error. The
next practical step is recalibrating Kstop
min and M stop
min at
larger system sizes.

1.
Benchmark summaries
The numerical data used for Sec. V separate individual
trajectories from aggregate reliability summaries. Only
aggregate quantities needed for the scientific claims are
reported in the article: stop rates, false-stop rates, stop
precision, terminal budgets, and terminal errors. Indi-
vidual trajectories are used only for the representative
examples and the additional trajectory figure.
For interpretability, each terminal run is classified
into one of two outcomes: a success declaration or a
resource-limit termination. The guarded rule also tracks
whether the minimum-K gate, minimum-M gate, com-
ponent gates, and persistence requirement have passed.
These recorded checks are not part of the scientific result
by themselves; they are used to distinguish a conservative
non-declaration from an estimator that has genuinely met
all stopping requirements.
2.
Seed vector selection
The Krylov subspace KK(G, v0) is constructed by ap-
plying the Hermitian generator G iteratively starting from
an initial seed vector v0. In the numerical study, v0 is the
dominant eigenvector of the noisy density matrix, normal-
ized to unit norm. This choice is deterministic and cheap
to compute in the exact benchmark setting, but it is still
not guaranteed to have large overlap with the dominant
QFI-sensitive directions, which can increase the effec-
tive constant Ctrunc and delay bias decay (failure mode
(iii) of Appendix G, Section G 1). For the n = 4 noisy
mixed-state family tested here, the dominant-eigenvector
choice produces the decay rates reported in Section V H.
A systematic comparison of alternative initial vectors is
left for future work. Choosing v0 closer to the leading
QFI-sensitive directions (e.g., via a short warm-start) is
a natural improvement direction that could reduce Ctrunc
and lower Kstop
min (ε), particularly at larger system sizes
where slow convergence is the binding constraint.
3.
Code and data availability
The code and benchmark data needed to reproduce
the numerical results will be made publicly available in
a versioned repository, with a permanent archival copy
deposited upon publication. The release will include the
processed data used to generate the reported statistics and
figures. No quantum hardware is required to reproduce
the benchmark.

Appendix C: Status of Assumption-Explicit
Guarantees
This appendix clarifies the distinction between the
assumption-calibrated bounds used in the theoretical
statements and the empirical quantities used by the
present stopping rule.
Theorem IV.1 is a sufficient condition for reliable stop-
ping when the two indicator components are valid upper
bounds on truncation and sampling error at the terminal
resource pair. In the numerical rule, the corresponding ob-
servable quantities are the inter-order change dK and the
bootstrap width wM. The bootstrap width captures finite-
sample spread at the current K, whereas the inter-order
change is only a local measure of Krylov convergence.
Neither quantity alone is a distribution-free bound on
the total QFI error. This distinction is central to the
paper: width-only stopping fails because the sampling
width can be small around a biased low-K estimate while
the truncation-side error remains large.
The remaining theoretical step toward a fully certi-
fied online routine is to replace these terminal empirical
quantities by time-uniform, sequentially valid bounds for
both error components along the adaptive trajectory. The
present work instead evaluates the decision layer through
post hoc benchmark reliability metrics, where Fref is
available only for evaluation.

Appendix H: Scaling assessment at n ∈{6, 8} qubits
This appendix reports stopping reliability results at
n ∈{6, 8} qubits under the same noise and resource limit
protocol as Section V A, but with the relative tolerance
εrel = 0.05 (pdep = 0.03, (Kmax, Mmax) = (8, 512), 10
independent replicates per setting). These results are
separated from the main n = 4 benchmark because the
default guard parameters (Kmin, Mmin, P) = (4, 128, 2)
are insufficient at these system sizes, and the resource limit
Kmax = 8 does not provide enough Krylov resolution for
the truncation bias to decay to within the tolerance. We
report these data as a scaling assessment for recalibrating
guarded stopping at larger system sizes.
a.
Scaling results.
For both system sizes, width-only
stopping exhibits FSR = 1.00 at every noise level (95%
CI [0.72, 1.00], n = 10 runs), with all runs terminating
at K = 2, M = 16. The near-zero QFI estimates at
K = 2 have spuriously narrow bootstrap CIs, satisfying
the width-only stopping criterion immediately regardless
of εrel.
For n = 6, guarded stopping reduces FSR at moderate-
to-high dephasing (FSR = 0 at pϕ ≥0.12), but retains
residual false stops at low dephasing (FSR = 0.50, 95%
CI [0.24, 0.76] at pϕ = 0; FSR = 0.20, 95% CI [0.06, 0.51]
at pϕ = 0.06). The guarded runs have median effective
terminal budget 384 at pϕ = 0 and 512 at the remaining
tested noise levels. For n = 8, guarded stopping fails
entirely: FSR = 1.00 at all noise levels, with all runs
declaring success at K = 4, M = 256.
The guard’s
minimum Krylov order Kmin = 4 is reached before the
truncation bias has decayed sufficiently, so the guard
criterion is satisfied at a biased estimate.
b.
Slow Krylov convergence at scale.
Section V H
reports fitted decay rates ˆµ(BK) = 0.98 (n = 6) and
ˆµ(BK) = 1.00 (n = 8), compared to ˆµ(BK) = 0.89 for
n = 4. At ˆµ ≈1, the truncation bias |BK| ≈Ctrunc · µK
does not meaningfully decrease within K ≤Kmax =
8. The theoretical minimum Krylov order (Eq. (G9))
depends inversely on log(1/µ); as µ →1, Kmin diverges,
meaning the guard condition cannot be met within any
fixed budget.
Recalibrating the guard for n ≥6 therefore requires
either increasing Kmax substantially (with corresponding
increases in circuit depth and measurement cost), or iden-
tifying noise regimes where µ is sufficiently below 1. The
closed-form threshold expressions of Appendix G 4 pro-
vide the recalibration tool once µ and Ctrunc are estimated
from a short calibration run.
