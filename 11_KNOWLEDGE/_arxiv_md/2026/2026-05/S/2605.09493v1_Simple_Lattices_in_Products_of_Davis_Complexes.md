Simple Lattices in Products of Davis Complexes
Michal Amir and Nir Lazarovich
Abstract
Burger and Mozes [11] constructed the first examples of simple uniform lat-
tices in products of trees. In this paper, we construct simple uniform lattices
in products of certain Davis complexes. More precisely, we consider lattices in
products of trees and two-dimensional Davis complexes of the right-angled Cox-
eter group whose defining graph is an odd graph. As part of the proof, we define
an analogue of the Burger-Mozes universal groups in this setting, and provide a
local criterion for a vertex transitive group to be dense in the universal group.
1
Introduction
1.1
Background
Simple groups are often seen as the building blocks of group theory. While finite simple
groups were completely classified, finding infinite simple groups is still an active field
of research. Among them, the search for finitely presented infinite simple groups has
been of particular interest. There are only a few known families of such groups. The
first example of a finitely presented simple group was given by Thompson in 1965.
Thompson’s group T is a group of homeomorphisms of the circle. Generalizations of
this example, and of the simplicity of certain groups of homeomorphisms, were later
developed by Higman [21], Scott [36], Brown [10], Stein [37], R¨over [34], and more
recently, Lodha [27]. We refer the reader to the references, and references within for
more information.
A different family of examples arises from lattices in products. Recall that a sub-
group Γ of a locally compact topological group G is a lattice if it is discrete and G/Γ
supports a finite volume G invariant measure. Such a lattice is uniform if moreover G/Γ
is compact. We will say that a group is a lattice in a space X if it is a lattice in the group
of isometries of X. Since the spaces we will consider are complexes, we will denote this
group by Aut(X). Burger and Mozes [12] first constructed uniform lattices acting on
two regular trees. Independently, Wise [38] found an example of non-residually-finite
uniform lattice in products of trees. These groups have finite index in groups that act
simply transitively on the vertex set of a product of two regular trees. Such groups
are known today as BMW groups (after Burger, Mozes and Wise). More examples of
virtually simple BMW groups were found by Rattaggi [33] and Radu [32]. Moreover,

1.2
Main Results
The goal of this paper is to construct the first examples of uniform simple lattices acting
on three-dimensional spaces. These spaces will be products of two Davis complexes –
a regular tree and the right-angled Davis complex of an Odd graph.
Let d ∈N, the Odd graph Od is the following d-regular graph: The vertex set of Od
is the set ([2d−1]
d−1 ) of subsets of {1,...,2d −1} of size d −1. Two sets are connected by
an edge if they are disjoint. Let X = XOd be the right-angled Davis complex associated
to the graph Od. That is, X is obtained from the Cayley graph of the right angled
Coxeter group WL by collapsing bigons to edges, and filling in cubes whenever possible
(cf. §3). Denote by Tc the c-regular tree.
The main result of this paper is the following:
Theorem A. For infinitely many c,d there exists a finitely-presented, simple, uniform
lattice in Aut(Tc) × Aut(XOd).
The proof of this theorem follows a similar strategy to that of Burger-Mozes [12,
13]. The general strategy for proving such a theorem is finding a non-residually-finite
uniform lattice Λ′ ≤Aut(Tc) × Aut(XOd) whose projections on each factor are dense
in some simple topological groups. In our case, these simple topological groups are an
adaptation of the universal groups of Burger-Mozes (cf. Definition 3.5). Besides being
simple groups, the remarkable property of the universal groups is that in order to check
that a group (in our case, the projection of Λ′) is dense in them it suffices to check it
locally. Namely, to check that the restriction of a vertex stabilizer to the ball of radius
2 around this vertex matches that of the universal group. Burger and Mozes proved
this local-to-global property for the universal group of a regular tree, the following is
the analogous theorem for the Davis complex XOd (cf. Definition 3.5 for definitions):
Theorem B. Let 6 ≤d ∈N. Let G be a vertex transitive subgroup of the universal
group U = U(Alt2d−1) ≤Aut(XOd). Then, the following are equivalent:
1. G is dense in U.
2. G is non-discrete and Gv∣B1(v) = Uv∣B1(v) for some vertex v of XOd.
3. Gv∣B2(v) = Uv∣B2(v) for some vertex v of XOd.

Assuming that such a uniform lattice Λ′ exists, the proof proceeds as follows: Since
Λ′ is non-residually finite, its finite residue Λ = ⋂{H ≤Λ′ ∣∣Λ′ ∶H∣< ∞} is a non-
trivial normal subgroup, without any finite index subgroups. By Bader-Shalom [2], Λ
has finite index in Λ′ and is just-infinite, i.e., has no non-trivial infinite-index normal
subgroups. We conclude that Λ is simple. Note that since Λ has finite index in the
uniform lattice Λ′, Λ is itself a uniform lattice, and as such it must be finitely-presented.
We conclude this introduction with an outline of the paper: In Section 2 we cover
the basics of CAT(0) cube complexes, normal paths and our notation, in Section 3 we
define and discuss the Davis complex XOd, and define and prove relevant properties of
the universal group of XOd, in Section 4 we prove Theorem B. In Section 5 we show
how one can produce such lattices, and finally, we prove Theorem A in Section 6.

CAT(0) cube complexes.
2.1
CAT(0) cube complexes and hyperplanes.
We provide a brief introduction to the basic definitions of CAT(0) cube complexes and
their hyperplanes. For more details and examples, we refer the reader to [6,9].
Definition 2.1. For a (CW) complex X, the k-th skeleton of X, denoted by Xk is the
union of the cells of X of dimension ≤k.
Definition 2.2. A simplicial complex Σ is flag if every clique is the 1-skeleton of a
simplex in Σ
Definition 2.3. For a complex X, the link of a vertex v ∈X0, Lk(v,X), is the
simplicial complex corresponding to a small sphere around v, that is, for every cube of
dimension i ≥1 incident to v, there is a unique simplex of dimension i −1 in Lk(v,X).
Definition 2.4. A cube complex is a complex made up by gluing a collection of unit
cubes [0,1]d (of varying dimensions d) along isometric faces.
A finite dimensional cube complex is CAT(0) if it is connected, simply connected
and the link of each of its vertices is a flag simplicial complex.
Remark 2.5. We note that CAT(0) is a metric property of non-positive curvature
introduced by Gromov. He showed [19] that when endowing each cube with the Eu-
clidean metric, the obtained metric is CAT(0) if and only if the cube complex is CAT(0)
in the sense of the previous definition.
Definition 2.6 (Sageev [35]). A midcube of a cube C = [0,1]d is a subset of the form
{1
2} × [0,1]d−1.
A hyperplane in a CAT(0) space X, is the union of (non-empty) minimal collection
of midcubes of the cubes of X which is closed under inclusion in the following sense:
if M ⊆M ′ are midcubes and one of them is in the collection then so is the other.
Each hyperplane is itself a CAT(0) cube complex, and separates X into two com-
ponents which are called halfspaces.

The ℓ∞metric and normal paths
CAT(0) cube complexes can be considered with various metrics. When endowing each
cube with the ℓ∞metric, one obtains the so-called ℓ∞metric on the cube complex.
While geodesics in this metric are not unique, the following definition of “normal paths”
due to Niblo-Reeves [31, Definition 3.1] gives rise to a particular unique choice of ℓ∞
geodesics (i.e., a geodesic bicombing).
Definition 2.7. A cube path is a sequence {Ci}n
i=1 of cubes of dimension at least one
with a corresponding vertex sequence {vi}n
i=0, such that Ci−1 ⋂Ci = vi−1 for 2 ≤i ≤n,
v0 ∈C1 is in the opposite end of v1 and vn ∈Cn is in the opposite end of vn−1.
We say it is a normal cube path if in addition str(Ci−1) ∩Ci = vi−1 where str(C) is
the union of all closed cubes containing C.
Definition 2.8. A normal (vertex) path, [v ↗x], from v to x is the sequence of vertices
{v = v0,v1,...,vk = x} corresponding to a normal cube path from v to x. See Figure 1
(left).
Proposition 2.9 ( [31, Proposition 3.3]). For any two vertices v,x ∈X0, there is a
unique normal path [v ↗x] from v to x.
Intuitively, the normal path [v ↗x] is the path obtained by starting at v and at
each step go across the longest diagonal of a cube that decreases the distance to x.

Therefore, we consider the ℓ∞metric on X, and denote as usual:
d(v,x) = the length of the normal path [v ↗x].

Definition 3.1. For a finite graph L = (V,E), define the corresponding right-angled

edge appears in Lk(v,XL). For the other way around, an edge in the link corresponds
to a filled square, which is every 4-cycle, and the only 4-cycles are the ones of the form
{a,b,a,b}, which corresponds to an edge in L.

Constructing lattices in products
Recall the following definition:
Definition 5.1. A BMW group of degree (m,n) is a group Γ ≤Aut(Tm) × Aut(Tn)
that acts simply transitively on the vertex set of the product Tm × Tn of the m-regular
tree and the n-regular tree. A BMW group is involutive if it is generated by involutions
that reflect along edges incident to a fixed vertex.
For further background on such BMW groups, we refer the reader to [14, Section
4] and [24].
In this section we prove the following:
Theorem 5.2. For every involutive BMW group Γ of degree (m,n) with alternating
local actions, there exist c ≥m, d ≥n and a lattice Λ ≤Aut(Tc) × Aut(XOd) such that:

## Page 25


• The group Γ is a subgroup of a hyperplane stabilizer of Λ. In particular, if Γ is
irreducible then so is Λ.
• Λ acts on Tc × XOd simply transitively on vertices.
• The local actions of Λ are alternating.
Here is an outline of this section: In §5.1, using the uniqueness theorem of [23],
we will show that Tc × XOd is the unique CAT(0) cube complex all of whose links are
isomorphic to the join of a set of c points and the graph Od. Using this, in §5.2, we will
describe a combinatorial structure, called an “interlacing pair”, which defines a group
acting simply transitively on the vertices of Tc×XOd. In §5.3, given an involutive BMW
group Γ as in the theorem, we will construct a specific interlacing pair that “contains”
the defining data of Γ. Finally, in §5.4, we will finish the proof of Theorem 5.2 by
showing that the group corresponding to the interlacing pair constructed in §5.3 has
the desired properties.

All known examples of finitely presented

Question 7.1. Are there finitely presented simple group of cohomological dimension
other than 2 or ∞?
Since torsion free uniform lattices in the product Tc × XOd have cohomological

torsion.

Question 7.7. Is the commensurator of a uniform lattice in Aut(XOd) simple?

Corollary 7.8. G = CommAut(XL)(W) is almost simple.

G contains a simple group B which contains a finite index subgroup of W, and that
there is no non-trivial element z ∈G which centralizes a finite index subgroup of W.

Let z ∈G be an element that centralizes a finite index subgroup of W. Since L has
