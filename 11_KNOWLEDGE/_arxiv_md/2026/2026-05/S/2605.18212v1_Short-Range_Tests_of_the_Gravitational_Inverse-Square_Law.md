Abstract
Experimental constraints on the gravitational inverse-square law at short range are presented, employ-
ing a consistent formalism across a wide range of length scales. We provide comprehensive updates
from the past decade, building upon our previous review. This work facilitates the direct compar-
ison of experimental results with theoretical models that extend general relativity. Furthermore, a
comparison between various model parametrizations, including extra-dimensional models, is intro-
duced. Finally, results from tabletop experiments are compared with those from high-energy collider
experiments for both Yukawa and power-law potentials.
Keywords: short-range gravity, extra-dimensions, inverse-square law
1 Introduction
Testing gravity is testing spacetime. Newton’s
gravitational inverse-square law stands as the
founding monument of modern science; it remains
valid for most phenomena in our universe, except
in extreme conditions where modiﬁcations from
general relativity are necessary. The inverse-
square law has been tested with great precision,
to be sure, but such high-precision tests have
been performed only on planetary scales. On
the other hand, precision in short-range tests at
sub-laboratory scales remains poor. This paper
provides an updated review of testing the gravita-
tional inverse-square law at short range, following
our previous review from a decade ago [1].
Since the end of the last century, search-
ing for deviations from the inverse-square law
at microscales has attracted signiﬁcant inter-
est, especially in light of the possible hypoth-
esis of extra spatial dimensions beyond our
four-dimensional spacetime. Before then, several
claims, such as the ﬁfth-force hypothesis, also
prompted numerous experimental searches for vio-
lations of the inverse-square law from the 1970s to
today. A wide variety of experimental reports can
be found in such attempts, from the geographic
scale down to the quark scale. It is not always
straightforward to compare their results with the-
oretical models, as each experiment reports its
results using diﬀerent parametrizations. In this
paper, we aim to summarize the wide variety of
experimental results within the same parameter
spaces and using the same criteria.

3 Interpretation on Extra
Dimension
Most experimental attempts over this quarter
century have been motivated by theoretical pre-
dictions of violations of the gravitational inverse-
square law in the brane-world scenario. So it
should be helpful to discuss the parametriza-
tions in the context of extra-dimension models.
Among them, the large extra-dimension model
(the Arkani-Hamed–Dimopoulos–Dvali, or ADD
model) [5] and the warped extra-dimension model
(the Randall–Sundrum, or RS model) [6, 7] are the
two notable predictions. Indeed, both the ADD
and RS-II models predict power-law-type modiﬁ-
cations at short distances. Historically, the idea of
a compactiﬁed extra dimension was proposed in
the Kaluza–Klein (KK) theory a century ago [8].
Since then, the compactiﬁcation scale has been
assumed to be the Planck length,

It is also useful to discuss the ﬁnite-size eﬀects
of the test and source masses. In such cases, the
total potential must be integrated over the vol-

centers of mass and the gap distance d between
the nearest surfaces. Examples of these calcula-

to the behavior in absolute measurements (20).

longer exponential but follows a power-law behav-
ior, appearing as a straight line on a logarithmic
scale with α ∝λ−2, as seen in Figs. 3 (d) and (e).
A measurement involving ﬁnite-sized objects
can be treated as a superposition of contributions
from inﬁnitesimal volume elements at various sep-
aration distances. While the volume elements near
the gap surface provide an exponential constraint
for λ < d, elements deeper within the objects con-
tribute to the force at relatively larger distances.
This extends the eﬀectiveness of the constraint
into the d < λ region, up to the limit where
λ ∼rnear. Consequently, the characteristic behav-
ior α ∝λ−2 can be interpreted as a ‘dilution eﬀect’
arising from the spatial extension of the objects.

gravity-related phenomena more directly; how-

Standard Model background. Thus, the precision
of Standard Model predictions dictates the overall
sensitivity, a situation analogous to that found in
atomic systems.

6 Discussion
The α–λ plots (Figs. 4 and 5), along with the
n–λ (Fig. 6) and n–MD (Fig. 7) plots, clearly
demonstrate the signiﬁcant progress achieved over
the past decade. Both micrometer-scale preci-
sion experiments and high-energy collider studies
provide stringent constraints on the large extra-
dimension hypothesis. In particular, the increased
statistics enabled by the LHC’s high luminosity
have substantially improved the limits on α at the
quark scale.
Figures 6 and 7 illustrate that for the spe-
ciﬁc case of n = 2, micrometer-scale labora-
tory experiments can eﬀectively compete with
high-energy searches. For n > 2, however, high-
energy experiments remain the only eﬀective
approach to exploring deviations from the grav-
itational inverse-square law. Beyond these estab-
lished methods, numerous experimental advance-
ments have emerged this decade. Many have
successfully imposed new constraints on the α–λ
plane, further excluding the exchange of hypothet-
ical new bosons in these regimes. Several novel
experimental trials [4, 15, 16, 25–27] are also
underway; while they have not yet established the
most stringent limits, they represent promising
technologies for future exploration.
Regarding gravitational wave observations,
while the ﬁnite size of stellar objects may eventu-
ally allow for constraints at sub-planetary scales,
current reports remain relatively weak [28, 29].
The gravitational wave observations are unlikely
to achieve the precision required to test short-
range gravity at or below the laboratory scale.

7.1 Torsion Balance Experiments
Since Cavendish’s era, the torsion balance has
remained the most promising instrument for mea-
suring inﬁnitesimal forces in short-range gravity
experiments. As illustrated in Figs. 6 and 7, results
from the UW and HUST groups are uniquely com-
petitive with the LHC constraints in the n = 2
case.
The UW group has led this ﬁeld for decades,
with their latest results [11] obtained using the
apparatus shown in Fig. 8. They achieved mea-
surements with a minimum gap distance of d =
52 µm. Similarly, the HUST group has consistently
tested the inverse-square law using centimeter-
scale torsion balances. Their most recent result
[13], shown in Fig. 9, utilized a gap distance of

7.2 Casimir Force Experiments
At the sub-micrometer scale, Casimir force mea-
surements are primarily conducted to verify QED
calculations. By re-analyzing these results, we can
estimate the maximum allowable strength of grav-
itational couplings, even though the sensitivity
is often limited by the theoretical uncertainties
of the Casimir force itself. The Decca group at
Indiana-Purdue University [17] has been specif-
ically testing gravitational couplings using dedi-
cated devices designed to minimize the Casimir
contributions. Their latest result, obtained with
the setup shown in Fig. 10, provides the most
stringent constraint at around the 100 nm scale
(Fig. 5), representing a three-order-of-magnitude
improvement over their previous study [36].
The α–λ exclusion curves for the Casimir-type
experiments exhibit a characteristic power-law
behavior, α ∝λ−2. This feature, previously iden-
tiﬁed as Feature 4 in Fig. 3, can be attributed to
the ﬁnite-size eﬀects of the test and source masses.
Indeed, while the minimum gap distance in the
setup of Fig. 10 is d = 200 nm, the radius of the
test mass is 149.3 µm, which is much larger than
d. Thus, ﬁnite-size eﬀects are substantial in this
conﬁguration.
Mechanical fabrication at the micrometer scale
and below is increasingly challenging with current
technologies, making Casimir-type measurements
the practical limit for mechanical gravity tests.
Below the 10 nm scale, experimental eﬀorts must
shift to atomic, molecular, and sub-atomic sys-
tems, despite the dominance of electromagnetic
interactions in these regimes.

7.3 Atomic and Sub-Atomic
Experiments
Atomic systems serve as ‘micro-laboratories’ for
testing interactions between nuclei and orbital
electrons. Due to the extraordinary precision with
which atomic energy levels can be measured, cou-
pled with the ability to perform rigorous QED
calculations of the electromagnetic interactions,
these systems provide a unique platform for grav-
ity tests. The sensitivity to gravitational contri-
butions is determined by the interplay between
experimental precision and theoretical accuracy.
However, despite this remarkable precision, the
immense ratio of the Coulomb force to the gravita-
tional force limits the sensitivity to approximately
α < 1030.
As demonstrated in torsion-balance exper-
iments, suppressing electromagnetic forces sig-
niﬁcantly enhances sensitivity to α. While the
Casimir force cannot be shielded in experiments
like Decca’s, the gravitational signal can still be
extracted by designing sophisticated apparatuses
that eﬀectively cancel the Casimir contributions.
14


## Page 15


Without such mechanical means to suppress elec-
tromagnetic couplings, the sensitivity for gravity
tests inevitably degrades exponentially, as previ-
ously discussed. Figure 5 presents estimated con-
straints for several atomic systems. It should be
noted that most of these are not direct experimen-
tal reports but are instead our own estimations
derived from combining experimental and theo-
retical uncertainties (represented by δ), including
those of the relevant physical constants [1].
At the nanometer scale, a series of neutron
scattering experiments has established constraints
on the α–λ plane [18–21]; these results are summa-
rized in Fig. 5. In contrast to atomic spectroscopy,
these studies measure the neutron-scattering cross
sections of atomic targets. Utilizing the neutron
as a probe allows for signiﬁcant suppression of
electric interactions, which would otherwise dom-
inate the signals in atomic systems. Within a
wave-mechanical framework, these experiments
probe potential mass-coupling interactions, such
as non-Newtonian gravity. In this context, the
impact parameter b corresponds to the character-
istic distance d used in mechanical measurements,
while the spatial resolution is fundamentally lim-
ited by the de Broglie wavelength of the inci-
dent neutron beam. The coupling strength α is
thus derived from the observed scattering cross
sections. Notably, the constraint reported by the
NIST group provides the most stringent limit in
this regime [18]. In Fig. 5, we extend their reported
constraint into the shorter λ region by applying
the exponential growth function from Eq. (20) for
λ < wavelength (indicated by the dotted line,
Feature 2).
Consistent with Casimir force experiments, the
neutron-derived constraints exhibit a power-law
behavior reﬂecting ﬁnite-size eﬀects (Feature 4).
In the scattering process, the distribution of
impact parameters b can be interpreted as a dis-
tribution of distances between the source and
probe masses. Although the reported constraints
were derived using the Born approximation within
a wave-mechanical formalization, the emergence
of this common power-law feature across diﬀer-
ent experimental platforms strongly supports this
physical interpretation.
A series of experiments measuring isotope-
dependent eﬀects in atomic spectroscopy (isotope
shifts) has recently been conducted to search for
new bosons [25–27]. We have interpreted these
results in terms of the coupling α, as shown in
Fig. 5. Their current sensitivity to α remains rela-
tively limited (α < 1029), primarily due to uncer-
tainties in nuclear structure and the small mass of
the electron. However, applying this technique to
muonic or antiprotonic atoms, particularly those
with simpler nuclear structures, may signiﬁcantly
enhance gravitational sensitivity in the future.
These studies typically report their results as the
coupling strengths of a potential new boson to
the electron (ye) and to the neutron (yn). In this
work, we converted these parameters into α using
the relation α = yeyn/(4πGNmnme), where mn
and me denote the neutron and electron masses,
respectively. In this conversion, we assume that
the coupling strengths of the new boson to the
neutron (yn) and to the proton (yp) are identical.
At sub-atomic scales, nuclear or hadronic sys-
tems can be used to estimate potential gravita-
tional contributions [38]; however, the achievable
precision is fundamentally limited by the theo-
retical uncertainties inherent in hadronic physics.
While this precision is diﬃcult to improve signiﬁ-
cantly, the accessible length scale can be extended
by increasing the energy. Indeed, high-energy col-
lider data enable the estimation of gravitational
couplings between elementary particles at scales
as small as 10−19 m. Although LHC constraints
are typically reported in the n–MD parameter
space, they can be converted into the α–λ param-
eters using the relation in Eq. (24). We derived
(δ, λ, r) from the data in Fig. 7 and plotted the
corresponding α–λ curves in Fig. 5 using the expo-
nential growth function from Eq. (20). Over the
past decade, the LHC has substantially enhanced
its sensitivity through both increased integrated
luminosity and an energy upgrade from 7 TeV
to 13 TeV, thereby signiﬁcantly tightening the
constraints on α.
The LHC results can be compared with other
experimental systems across the α–λ (Fig. 5), n–λ
(Fig. 6), and n–MD (Fig. 7) plots. In the α–
λ parametrization used to search for new boson
exchange forces, the LHC provides the most strin-
gent constraints for λ < 10 pm, a regime where
atomic and nuclear tests are attempted. Regarding
the large extra-dimension model, Fig. 6 allows for
a direct comparison of the results. For most values
of n, the LHC establishes the strongest constraints
on λ. Even for the speciﬁc case of n = 2, the LHC

sets an upper limit of λ < 4 µm, surpassing the
HUST result of λ < 11 µm. In our previous review
[1], the UW constraint stood at λ < 23 µm, which
was stronger than the LHC’s limit of λ < 130 µm

A similar comparison is presented in the n–

HUST and the LHC provide lower limits of MD >
6.6 TeV and MD > 11.2 TeV, respectively. By con-

8 Conclusion
We have performed a comprehensive analysis com-
paring experimental results across uniﬁed parame-
ter spaces, speciﬁcally α–λ, n–λ, and n–MD. This
framework demonstrates how diverse experimen-
tal data can be interpreted within the context of
large extra-dimension models. Our analysis shows
that the latest LHC results provide the most strin-
gent upper limit on the extra-dimension scale for
n = 2, reaching λ < 4 µm, while the micrometer-
scale precision experiment by the HUST group
establishes a competitive limit of λ < 11 µm.
Notably, laboratory tests of the gravitational
inverse-square law remain competitive with LHC
constraints only for the n = 2 case at approxi-
mately the micrometer scale. Looking forward, the
development of novel experimental techniques or
signiﬁcant advancements in the theoretical estima-
tion of electromagnetic couplings will pave the way
for high-precision gravity tests at sub-micrometer
scales.
