ABSTRACT
The [12C II] emission at 158 µm is a key cooling line of the interstellar medium and serves as a unique tracer of gas kinematics in
spatially and spectrally resolved observations. Its spectral profile, however, is often modified by optical depth effects. The intrinsic
line shape can be reconstructed by comparison with emission from the less abundant singly ionized 13C isotope.
Due to the additional neutron spin, the [13C II] emission splits into three hyperfine structure (hfs) transitions. Laboratory measurements
have provided the [13C II] centroid frequency and the frequency of the strongest hfs component (F = 2 −1), whereas the two weaker
outer components (F = 1 −0 and F = 1 −1) have been inferred solely from quantum-mechanical calculations. Consequently,
the magnetic-dipole hfs constants, from which the individual transition frequencies can be derived, have not yet been determined
experimentally.
The high spectral resolution of observations with the upgraded German Receiver for Astronomy at Terahertz Frequencies (upGREAT)
on board SOFIA enabled the simultaneous detection of all three [13C II] hfs transitions. We thus determine, for the first time from
astronomical observations, the magnetic-dipole hyperfine structure constants Ahf
1/2 = 810.71(11) MHz and Ahf
3/2 = 162.18(5) MHz of
the [13C II] 2s2 2p 2P◦ground term with unprecedented precision. Combining these with the previously measured [13C II] centroid
frequency in the laboratory, we derive the rest frequencies of all three hfs lines. Using the observed [12C II] emission as a reference,
we also determine the [13C II] centroid frequency with improved precision from astronomical observations.
This work demonstrates that spectrally resolved astronomical observations can constrain fundamental atomic properties. High spectral
resolution and signal-to-noise ratio enable a level of precision in determining hfs constants that rivals laboratory measurements. The
approach is broadly applicable to other atomic and molecular transitions where laboratory data are difficult to obtain.
Key words. hyperfine structure – singly ionized carbon – high-resolution spectroscopy
1. Introduction
The [12C II] 158 µm fine-structure line (2P3/2−2P1/2) is one of the
most important cooling lines of the interstellar medium (Tielens
& Hollenbach 1985). The emission originates largely from pho-
todissociation regions (PDRs, Hollenbach & Tielens 1999), the
interfaces between hot ionized H II regions and cold molecular
clouds. In addition, recent studies show that [12C II] is a unique
tracer of gas kinematics, allowing to directly observe the gas ex-
pansion around H II regions (Pabst et al. 2019, 2020; Luisi et al.
2021; Tiwari et al. 2021; Beuther et al. 2022; Bonne et al. 2022;
Faerber et al. 2025).
However, the [12C II] line can be strongly affected by opti-
cal depth effects (Graf et al. 2012; Ossenkopf et al. 2013; Gue-
vara et al. 2020, 2024; Kabanovic et al. 2022) in dense, bright
PDRs. In particular, absorption by cold gas along the line of
sight (LOS) can alter the spectral profile, producing dips near the
bulk velocity that can create the appearance of multiple velocity
components. In order to disentangle optical depth effects from
gas kinematics, the rarer isotope [13C II] can be utilized. The low
abundance of the 13C-isotope (∼70 times less abundant in the
solar neighborhood, Wilson 1999) leads to optically thin tran-
sitions that simply integrate over the line-of-sight emission. The
coupling of the nuclear spin of 13C+ (I = 1/2), arising from its
unpaired neutron, with the electron angular momentum J splits
the [13C II] 2P3/2 −2P1/2 transition into three hyperfine structure
(hfs) lines: F = 2 −1, F = 1 −0, and F = 1 −1, where F is the
total angular momentum of the system. The corresponding hy-
perfine energy shifts for a level with quantum numbers (F, I, J)

The low relative abundance of 13C+ relative to 12C+ results in
weak lines, challenging the observational sensitivity require-
ments. The first detection of the F = 2 −1 component (in M 42)
was reported by Boreiko et al. (1988), using the Kuiper Air-
borne Observatory, followed by the first detections of the second
strongest [13C II] F = 1 −0 line by Stacey et al. (1991) and Bor-
eiko & Betz (1996). The improved sensitivity of the upgraded
German Receiver for Astronomy at Terahertz Frequencies (up-
GREAT; Heyminck et al. 2012; Risacher et al. 2016) on board
the Stratospheric Observatory for Infrared Astronomy (SOFIA;
Young et al. 2012) has enabled routine detections of [13C II] lines
alongside [12C II] in Galactic sources (Graf et al. 2012; Guevara
et al. 2020; Kabanovic et al. 2022). In addition, the first detec-
tion of [13C II] emission outside the Milky Way was reported by
Okada et al. (2019) in the Large Magellanic Cloud.
Analysis of [13C II] emission combined with [12C II] emission
allows us to derive the column density, optical depth, and ex-
citation temperature of C+ individually for each velocity com-
ponent (Kabanovic et al. 2022). For this analysis, the hfs lines
are shifted to a common velocity frame by correcting for their
relative frequency offsets and then averaged, weighted by their
relative intensities (Guevara et al. 2020), thereby increasing the
signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) of the observations. To apply the fre-
quency correction, it is essential to accurately determine the fre-
quency shifts between the different transitions. This is especially
important if the lines are only marginally detected.
Because of the highly reactive nature of C+ and the dipole-
forbidden character of the 2P3/2 −2P1/2 transition, observing this
transition under laboratory conditions is challenging. Neverthe-
less, Cooksy et al. (1986) successfully determined the [13C II] hfs
transition frequencies using laser magnetic resonance combined
with quantum-mechanical calculations. They experimentally de-
rived the [13C II] centroid and the combination of magnetic-
dipole hfs constants, (Ahf

hyperfine transition frequencies can be improved through these
more precise hfs constants. The updated theoretical [13C II] fre-

2. Astronomical observations
The only direct simultaneous measurements of all three hfs lines
are obtained with astronomical observations. An example spec-
trum with all three [13C II] hfs lines together with the [12C II]
line is shown in Fig. 1. The spectrum was observed in a single
pointing long integration towards the peak of the bright rim of
NGC 1977. Such an astronomical observation does not provide
direct access to the intrinsic line frequencies, since the observed
emission is Doppler shifted by the motion of the emitting source,
preventing a unique separation of the intrinsic frequency and the
source velocity. However, as all three [13C II] lines are well de-
tected, the frequency shifts between the hfs lines can be derived
from these high spectral resolution data.
To detect all three hfs lines, only bright sources with rela-
tively narrow [12C II] line widths are suitable. Therefore, we uti-
lize the deep integrations of the [12C II] and [13C II] lines across
Orion, including the Orion Bar, NGC 1977, and NGC 2024, tak-
ing advantage of the unprecedented sensitivity of upGREAT on
board SOFIA. More information on the observation and data re-
duction is given in Appendix B.

An alternative approach to determining the [13C II] frequen-
cies is to use the [12C II] line as a reference by performing a si-
multaneous fit to the [12C II] and [13C II] transitions in astronom-
ically observed spectra. The velocity shift between the [12C II]
and [13C II] lines obtained from the fit, combined with the known
[12C II] rest frequency, yields the absolute [13C II] frequencies.
This approach is straightforward if both the [12C II] and [13C II]
transitions are optically thin. However, the observed [12C II] tran-
sitions are often affected by self- and foreground absorption,
which distorts the line profile. This makes it unfeasible to de-
termine the systemic velocity with the high accuracy required.
By carefully examining each source, we find that only the
single-pointing observation of the Orion Bar exhibits a suffi-
ciently “intact” [12C II] profile. Nevertheless, the [12C II] emis-
sion from the Orion Bar is also optically thick (Ossenkopf et al.
2013), meaning that a simple Gaussian fit is not appropriate.
Therefore, to properly model the [12C II] emission, we fit the ra-
diative transfer equation assuming that the line emission can be
well described by a single excitation temperature. An in-depth
discussion of the radiative transfer model and its application to
the observed spectrum is provided in Appendix D.
The model fit yields a velocity shift between the [12C II] line
and the [13C II] F = 2 −1 transition of 11.357 ± 0.009 km s−1.
Combining this result with the known [12C II] rest frequency
(Cooksy et al. 1986), we determine the transition frequencies
of the individual [13C II] hyperfine components. The resulting
frequencies are summarized in Table 2. As before, the uncer-
tainty of the derived frequencies is dominated by the uncertainty
of the reference line; however, since the [12C II] line frequency
is known more accurately than the [13C II] centroid, the result-
ing [13C II] frequencies have correspondingly lower uncertain-
ties. Moreover, since this approach provides direct access to the
velocity difference between the [12C II] line and the [13C II] hfs
transitions, we can derive velocity offsets, which are stated in
Table 2, at the precision afforded by the astronomical data, inde-

these frequencies are derived independently of the [13C II] cen-
troid, providing an independent verification of the [13C II] cen-
troid frequency. The resulting centroid frequency (Table 2), de-
rived from astronomical observations, agrees well with the value
previously determined in the laboratory by Cooksy et al. (1986).

4. Summary and Conclusion
In a pioneering study, Cooksy et al. (1986) determined the
[13C II] transition frequencies. However, the laser magnetic res-
onance method used in that work did not allow for independent
measurements of both hfs constants, Ahf
1/2 and Ahf
3/2; the frequency
offsets between the three hfs lines therefore had to rely on nu-
merical quantum mechanical calculations to derive the hfs con-
stants. While these calculations have become more precise over
time (Jönsson et al. 1996, 2010), enabling a more accurate de-
termination of the [13C II] transition frequencies, no laboratory
measurements have been performed to independently validate
the computed values.
Significant technological advancements in submillimeter ob-
servations, such as with the upGREAT receiver (Risacher et al.
2016), allow for high spectral resolution observations of [12C II]
and all three [13C II] hyperfine transition lines. The directly mea-
sured frequency differences between the hfs lines enabled us to
determine both hfs constants with high precision, as summarized
in Table 1.
However, without independent knowledge of the systemic
velocity of the emitting gas, the absolute rest frequencies of the
individual transitions cannot be derived. A reference frequency
from laboratory measurements, where the rest frame is well de-
fined, is thus required. In this work, we applied two complemen-
tary methods to determine updated [13C II] transition frequencies:
1. Using the [13C II] centroid as a reference: By combin-
ing the centroid frequency with the hfs constants derived
from the astronomical measurements, we determined up-
dated [13C II] transition frequencies using Eq. (A.1). While
the hfs constants themselves can be determined with ex-
ceptional accuracy (Table 1), the uncertainty of the derived
transition frequencies is dominated by the uncertainty of the
laboratory-measured [13C II] centroid.
2. Using the frequency shift between [12C II] and [13C II] lines
from astronomical observations: This method relies on the
cleanest [12C II] spectra with minimal optical depth effects.
Its precision is limited by optical depth effects in [12C II] and
the uncertainty of the [12C II] rest frequency. Nevertheless,
it provides an independent determination of the [13C II] cen-
troid frequency.
The derived frequencies from both methods are summarized
in Table 2. Both approaches yield consistent frequencies with
comparable uncertainties and additionally provide an indepen-
dent verification of the [13C II] centroid frequency measured by
Cooksy et al. (1986).
In this work, we have demonstrated that spectrally resolved
astronomical observations can be used to infer atomic or molec-
ular structure, effectively using outer space as a spectroscopic
laboratory. The method presented here is not limited to the ion-
ized carbon lines analyzed in this study, but can be applied more
broadly to other atoms or molecules where laboratory measure-
ments are challenging. Whenever spectroscopic signatures allow
precise measurements of frequency differences (i.e., when two or
more lines share the same systemic velocity and are not strongly
affected by optical depth effects), this approach leverages the
unique synergy between laboratory science and astronomical ob-
servations to obtain highly accurate spectroscopic parameters.

When discussing error propagation, two types of uncertain-

the individual [13C II] hyperfine transitions, and the absolute fre-

be derived independently of any reference frequency. However,
when estimating the absolute frequencies of the three [13C II]
transitions, the uncertainty of the reference frequency must also
be taken into account.
Appendix B: Data reduction
The observations were performed using the hexagonal 2 × 7
pixel low-frequency array (LFA), in horizontal (LFAH) and ver-
tical (LFAV) polarization. The effective size of the dish is 2.5 m
resulting in a beam size of 14.1 arcseconds (Risacher et al.
2018). For this study, we examined the SOFIA archive for long-
integration [C II] observations that could be used for our analy-
sis. However, only four observations, spanning three different re-
gions in Orion, provided sufficiently high signal-to-noise (S/N)
in all three [13C II] lines with low contamination of the F = 2 −1
transition: (i) The Orion Bar was observed at its peak emission
using a single pointing, covering the emission peak and six ad-
ditional positions around it with the hexagonal array. These ob-
servations were obtained as part of the square-degree [C II] map-
ping project (PI: A.G.G.M. Tielens); see Higgins et al. (2021).
(ii) NGC 1977 was observed with a single pointing toward its
emission peak (PI: C. Guevara), and the entire bright rim of
NGC 1977 was mapped in [12C II] and [13C II] (PI: S. Kabanovic).
Although the two datasets could be combined, we keep them
separate to provide an internal consistency check of our results.
(iii) NGC 2024 was mapped in [12C II] and [13C II] with long inte-
gration times (PI: U.U. Graf), providing the third source for our
hyperfine analysis.

0.1 km s−1 (∼0.6 MHz) to reduce the noise level while preserv-
ing sufficiently high spectral resolution to obtain an accurate fit
to the hfs line positions. For the single-pointing observations,
all pixels of the array were averaged to produce a single high
S/N spectrum. For the spectral cubes, the data were convolved
to a spatial resolution of 60′′ and resampled onto a grid with a

Spectral baselines in astronomical observations are often af-
fected by standing waves, offsets, or power drifts between cali-
brations, which must be corrected prior to any analysis. A com-
mon approach is to mask the line emission and fit an m-order
(typically third-order) polynomial to the remaining baseline. The
fitted polynomial is then subtracted from the entire spectrum.
While this method works well in many cases, using a fixed
polynomial order has the disadvantage that complex baselines
may not be properly fitted. Residual baseline artifacts remain in
some spectra, potentially affecting subsequent analyses. In se-
vere cases, the affected spectra have to be discarded altogether,
leading to a loss of valuable observing time. Although higher-
order (m > 3) polynomials can improve the fit, they carry the
risk of over-fitting, i.e., introducing artificial features into the
baseline and the emission line.
To overcome this problem, we apply an adaptive polynomial
fitting procedure to each spectrum, minimizing the Corrected
Akaike Information Criterion (AICc):
AICc = n ln(σ2) + 2k + 2k2 + 2k
n −k −1 = AIC + 2k2 + 2k

where n is the number of fitted channels, σ2 is the variance of the
baseline, and k is the number of free fit parameters. The last term
of the AICc expression is a penalty for higher polynomial orders
for fits involving a small number of channels, thereby avoiding
over-fitting. We begin with a third-order polynomial fit and sub-
sequently test whether higher-order polynomials provide a sta-
tistically better fit according to the AICc.
If single-channel spikes are present in the spectra, they are
removed by replacing the affected channel values with a linear
interpolation between the two neighboring channels. However,
such spikes were only found to affect channels outside the sig-
nal region. In addition, each scan was inspected visually prior to
the averaging or gridding process to remove any potentially bad
spectra.

The uncertainties on the derived velocity separations of the
[13C II] hyperfine lines are obtained from the diagonal elements
of the covariance matrix returned by the non-linear least-squares
fit. The spectral noise (rms), measured from emission-free chan-
nels of the averaged spectrum, serves as the per-channel un-
certainty, ensuring that the covariance matrix yields properly
scaled 1σ statistical uncertainties. A reduced chi-squared value
of χ2

quately describes the data. All reported values and their uncer-
tainties are rounded following the “rule-of-m” convention with
m = 25, as described in Kramida (2011).
Beyond the formal statistical uncertainties, several potential
sources of systematic error were carefully considered. First, de-
spite a dedicated baseline treatment utilizing adaptive polyno-
mial fitting (Appendix B), small residual baseline undulations
cannot be entirely excluded, particularly across the relatively
wide velocity window (of 40 km/s) encompassing the [12C II]
line and the [13C II] F = 2 −1 component, which is masked
out during the baseline fitting. Second, the F = 2 −1 hyperfine
component is partially blended with the [12C II] emission, which
could bias the fitted line separation. To mitigate this, the [12C II]
line and all [13C II] hyperfine components were fitted simultane-
ously, explicitly accounting for the contamination in the model.
While these measures were taken to minimise systematic effects,
we cannot fully rule out systematic contributions. However, the
consistency of the derived hyperfine separations across indepen-
dent observations suggests that residual systematic effects do not
dominate the error budget.
The exceptionally high quality of the data from the long-
term single-pointing observation toward the peak emission in
NGC 1977 dominates the overall statistics. As a result, the other
sources have little impact on the combined results and primar-
ily serve as a consistency check. Although the sample size is
relatively small, the integration time per source is exceptionally
high, particularly toward NGC 1977.
