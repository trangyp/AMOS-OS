Abstract
Low-rank matrix completion is a widely studied problem with many variants. Inductive matrix com-
pletion (IMC) incorporates row and column side information to significantly narrow the search space.
Prior work falls into two regimes: methods that exploit this structure to achieve reduced sample com-
plexity but only in noiseless settings, and methods that handle noise but require sample complexity
matching the ambient matrix dimension—forfeiting the sample efficiency that side information should
provide. In this paper, we close this gap by studying noisy IMC with a nonconvex projected gradient
descent algorithm with spectral initialization. Our main technical contribution is establishing a regular-
ity condition for the IMC loss function that holds at the reduced sample complexity determined by the
effective problem size—scaling with the side information dimension a rather than the ambient dimen-
sion n. This directly yields linear convergence and an estimation error that both depend only on the
effective problem size rather than the ambient matrix dimension. We further extend our analysis to the
inexact side information setting, demonstrating that the reduced sample complexity is maintained and
the estimation error is order-optimal with respect to the inexactness of the side information. Extensive
simulations and real-world experiments on the MovieLens dataset validate our theoretical findings.

Introduction
Low-rank matrix completion is a fundamental problem in high-dimensional statistics and machine learning.
We consider a rank-r ground-truth matrix L⋆∈Rn1×n2 corrupted by an additive noise matrix E ∈Rn1×n2,
where each entry in [n1]×[n2] is observed independently with probability p. Let Ωdenote the set of observed
entries. Given the observed matrix
M = PΩ(L⋆+ E),
(1)
the goal is to estimate L⋆. This problem is now well understood in several canonical regimes. Let n denote
the larger of n1 and n2. Prior work shows that with eO(nr) observed entries, one can achieve exact recovery
in the noiseless setting when E = 0 [CT10, Rec11] and near-optimal estimation error in the noisy setting
[CLL20, CCF+20].
In the vanilla matrix completion problem, the only constraint on L⋆is that it is low rank. In some
settings, prior information on the column space and row space of L⋆is available. Consider the case where
we are given two orthonormal matrices X ∈Rn1×a1 and Y ∈Rn2×a2 which are called side information
matrices. As suggested by their names, the matrix X contains information for the column space of L⋆and
Y contains information for the row space of L⋆. In the case with exact side information, we can rewrite L⋆
as XZ⋆Y ⊤for some rank-r matrix Z⋆∈Ra1×a2 and the observed matrix as
M = PΩ(XZ⋆Y ⊤+ E).
(2)
The goal now is to recover the matrix L⋆= XZ⋆Y ⊤and this problem is sometimes called inductive
matrix completion (IMC) or subspace-constrained matrix completion. Since the unknown matrix is now

by the feature dimensions a1, a2 rather than the ambient dimensions n1, n2. For comparison, we refer to (1)
as the non-inductive matrix completion problem. Such side information is available in practice in various
problems. IMC and other models using side information have applications in many problems where known
structure or covariates can be exploited. It is used in multi-label learning [XJZ13, JD13], prediction for
gene-disease associations [ND14, CWQ+18, LYL+18, LZL+20], recommendation systems [SCLD15, JKN16],
link prediction [ZGD+22], semi-supervised clustering [CHD15, ZJZ17, NSB+18], etc.
In IMC, the restriction of the column and row spaces of L⋆means that the search space in the estimation
is narrowed. As a consequence, one would hope that this extra side information allows smaller sample com-
plexity or better estimation error. Equivalently, one expects both the sample complexity and the estimation
error to be governed by the effective problem size induced by the side information, rather than by the ambient
matrix dimension. Several works have explored this topic. On the one hand, [Che15, XJZ13] consider the
noiseless setting when E = 0 with nuclear norm minimization. They obtain exact recovery with significantly
fewer samples required than non-inductive matrix completion, with the sample complexity governed by the
lower-dimensional side-information structure. On the other hand, [CLM22] studies the noisy setting with
E ̸= 0 and proves a sharp estimation error guarantee, but with a sample complexity requirement at the
same scale as ordinary matrix completion. Prior theory leaves a clear gap: noiseless IMC enjoys reduced
sample complexity, while noisy IMC retains sharp error guarantees only at the larger sample size required in
the non-inductive problem. It is therefore natural to ask whether these two types of results can be unified.
This leads to the following central question:
Can one estimate L⋆in the presence of noise E using a sample size below that required for
ordinary matrix completion?
In Theorem 1, we resolve this question affirmatively. We show that one can retain the reduced inductive
sample complexity from [Che15, XJZ13] while simultaneously matching the noisy estimation guarantee of
[CLM22]. The main technical ingredient is a regularity condition for the IMC loss function that holds at the
scale of this effective problem size.
In addition to the sub-optimal sample complexity in the noisy setting, a major limitation of some prior
works [Che15, XJZ13] on IMC is that they require the side information to be exact, meaning that the
provided side-information subspaces exactly contain the true row and column spaces. This can be restrictive
in practice. This raises a second question:
Can one still estimate L⋆with reduced sample complexity when the side information is inexact?
In Theorem 2, we give a positive answer. We propose a way to quantify the level of inexactness of the side
information via principal angles between the true and observed side-information subspaces, and show that it
is possible to estimate XZ⋆Y ⊤with the same sample complexity requirement as in Theorem 1. Moreover,
we show that the estimation error is order-optimal with respect to the level of inexactness.

1.2
Related Work
Matrix completion without side information.
The classical matrix completion problem has been stud-
ied extensively over the past two decades. In the noiseless setting, pioneering convex approaches based on
nuclear norm minimization established exact recovery under incoherence and near-optimal sampling condi-
tions [CR09, CT10, Rec11]. In the noisy setting, subsequent work showed that one can achieve stable recovery
and near-optimal estimation error guarantees from partial noisy observations [CP10, NW12, CCF+20]. These
results provide the benchmark statistical rates for recovering a low-rank matrix when no side information is
available.
There is also a large literature on computationally efficient nonconvex algorithms for matrix completion,
including alternating minimization, spectral initialization followed by gradient refinement, and leave-one-out
analyses [KMO09, KMO10, JNS13, ZL16, MWCC18, DC20, TMC21]. Taken together, these works give a
sharp picture of both the statistical and algorithmic behavior of ordinary matrix completion.
Inductive matrix completion with exact side information.
Inductive matrix completion incorpo-
rates side information on the rows and columns to restrict the target matrix to a lower-dimensional subspace.
When the side information exactly spans the true row and column spaces, one expects recovery to require
substantially fewer observations than ordinary matrix completion, with the sample complexity governed
by the effective feature dimensions rather than the ambient matrix size. Early theoretical work confirmed
this intuition in noiseless settings. In particular, gradient-based and convex-style approaches were shown
to achieve exact recovery with significantly reduced sample complexity under suitable incoherence and con-
ditioning assumptions [XJZ13].
Related algorithmic developments include alternating-minimization-type
procedures and other efficient approaches tailored to the inductive structure [JD13, ZDG18].
More recent work has also studied nonconvex formulations of inductive matrix completion from an op-
timization perspective. For example, [ZN22] analyzes the landscape of a nonconvex IMC formulation and
shows the absence of spurious local minima under suitable conditions, while [ZDG18] develops a multi-phase
Procrustes flow method with favorable sample and runtime guarantees. These results clarify the optimization
landscape of IMC in exact-side-information settings.
Inductive matrix completion with noisy observations.
Compared with the noiseless exact-side-
information setting, the theory of inductive matrix completion is less complete when observations are noisy
or the side information is imperfect. One line of work studies noisy IMC and establishes estimation guarantees

under general side information models, but the resulting rates or sample requirements do not fully capture
the reduced effective dimension suggested by the inductive structure [CHD15].
More recently, [CLM22]
analyzes a nonconvex formulation of noisy IMC and proves a sharp estimation error guarantee. However, its
sample complexity scales at the level required for ordinary matrix completion, and therefore does not fully

[LAL+22] studies generalization guarantees with a Lipschitz-bounded loss function for IMC in a low-noise

Other approaches to matrix completion with side information.
Broader work on side informa-
tion for matrix completion considers settings where auxiliary covariates are informative but do not exactly
determine the target row and column spaces, as well as application-driven feature constructions in recom-
mendation, multi-label learning, and graph-based problems [SCLD15, ZSJD19, ZC19, SZT+21]. While they
address different formulations, they are closely related in spirit in that they incorporate side information
into matrix completion.

Problem Setup
We study the problem of recovering a low-rank matrix from partial, noisy observations when side information
about its column and row spaces is available. This section formalizes the model and assumptions, introduces
the notation used throughout the paper, and presents the projected gradient descent algorithm at the heart
of our approach.
2.1
Model formulation with exact side information
Recall that in inductive matrix completion we observe a subset of entries of a noisy low-rank matrix L⋆∈
Rn1×n2. More precisely, if Ω⊂[n1] × [n2] denotes the set of observed entries and E ∈Rn1×n2 is the noise

M = PΩ(L⋆+ E).

mation about the column and row spaces of L⋆2. The central idea is that if these side-information matrices

directly in the ambient space.

Inexact side information
In the setting with inexact side information, instead of knowing X⋆, Y ⋆exactly, we generate inexact side
information matrices X, Y with inexactness level δ as defined by (11).
Figure 4 shows the relative error for IMC at different inexactness levels, compared with MC across
different sampling rates. When δ = 0.2, at small sample sizes, IMC performs significantly better than MC.
However, as the sample size increases, the error of MC decreases significantly while the error of IMC plateaus.
This is consistent with our theory of IMC with inexact side information, which states that IMC is able to
estimate well with low sample complexity that is insufficient for MC, but its estimation error is limited by
the inexactness of the side information, so it is outperformed by MC when sufficient samples are available.
5.1.4
Interpolation Between MC and IMC
In the inexact side information setting, we have seen a visible trade-off between IMC’s low sample requirement
and MC’s better estimation error at larger sample sizes. The interpolation objective (17) introduced in
Section 4 offers a solution that tries to capture the best of both worlds.
We test this interpolation objective in the noiseless setting with inexact side information at level δ = 0.05,
using the same matrix dimensions as above. For various values of p and λ, we run gradient descent with
spectral initialization. Figure 6 shows that the interpolation can improve substantially over both MC and
IMC for an intermediate choice of λ. Such a choice of λ is larger when the sample size is small, meaning that
the interpolation is more aligned with IMC. This is consistent with our theory that IMC performs better
with low sample complexity that is insufficient for MC even when the side information is inexact.
In practice, the parameter λ can be selected by cross-validation. We implement a 5-fold procedure in
which the observed entries are split into five equally sized folds, with each fold held out in turn, and the
average validation error is used to select λ. Table 1 compares the resulting reconstruction error against MC
and IMC. The cross-validation procedure is able to take advantage of both MC and IMC and gives better
performance in both low- and high-sample-size regimes.
5.2
Application on the MovieLens Dataset
We perform experiments on the MovieLens 100k [HK15] dataset and compare IMC against non-inductive
matrix completion. The MovieLens dataset consists of 100,000 ratings by 943 reviewers on 1682 movies.
The ratings take integer values from 1 to 5. The dataset also includes demographic data of the users and
genre data of the movies as side information features that make inductive matrix completion possible. We
also augment the side information matrix with singular vectors of the training matrix. More specifically, we
have 29 features for reviewers (1 for intercept, 1 for gender, 7 for age groups, and 20 for occupations) and
30 features for movies (1 for intercept, 19 for genres, and 10 for top 10 right singular vectors of the training
matrix).
We consider the performance of IMC using our proposed method and non-inductive MC using vanilla
gradient descent at different sampling rates. At each trial, we randomly select m of the 100,000 samples as
the training set and the rest as the testing set. Figure 7 shows the RMSE of inductive and non-inductive
matrix completion on MovieLens 100k. We can clearly see that when sample size m is small, IMC performs
significantly better than non-inductive MC, and when m increases, non-inductive MC starts to gain advan-
tage. This is consistent with our findings in Theorem 2 on IMC with inexact side information, which states
that IMC is able to estimate well with low sample complexity that is insufficient for non-inductive MC, but
the estimation error is hindered by the inexactness of the side information and IMC is outperformed by
non-inductive MC when sufficient samples are available. For the MovieLens 100k dataset, m = 5000 can be
viewed as the aforementioned low complexity setting. In fact, over the 20 trials used for Figure 7, an average
of 92.6 users and 618.25 movies have no observation at all, which means non-inductive MC cannot provide
any non-trivial estimation for these users and movies. This is the scenario where IMC is powerful in utilizing
side information to provide good estimates even for the rows and columns that are completely unobserved.

6
Discussion
This paper shows that inductive matrix completion can retain its main statistical advantage beyond the
noiseless exact-side-information regime.
Under exact side information, we prove that noisy recovery is
possible at sample complexity governed by the side-information dimensions rather than the ambient matrix
size. Under inexact side information, we show that this reduced sample complexity can still be preserved,
with estimation error controlled by the level of inexactness. We also propose an interpolation scheme that
balances the low-sample efficiency of IMC against the robustness of ordinary matrix completion to side-
information mismatch.
Several important questions remain open for matrix completion with side information. We highlight a
few directions that seem especially worthwhile.
• The (un)necessity of the projection step. In our algorithm, a projection step is used at each
iteration to ensure the matrices of interest are incoherent.
Empirically, however, the projection is
inactive in our simulations; see Appendix F.2.
This raises the question of whether one can prove
comparable guarantees for Algorithm 1 without projection. A main technical difficulty that sets IMC
apart from non-inductive MC is that the subspace restriction onto col(X) and col(Y ) couples the row
and column dependencies induced by the observation pattern. This makes it difficult to decouple the
iterates in the way needed for the powerful leave-one-out arguments used in [MWCC18, CLL20] for
non-inductive MC.
• Entrywise guarantees and inference. Our paper establishes Frobenius-norm error guarantees. It
would also be interesting to develop entrywise error bounds, confidence intervals for individual entries,
and related inferential guarantees, in the spirit of the corresponding results for non-inductive MC
[MWCC18, CFMY19, CCF+20].
• Partial side information. In our model for exact side information, we assume the side information
15


## Page 16


covers the whole column and row space of L⋆. It would be interesting to understand settings where
the available side information is exact but incomplete, for example when only a subset of informa-
tive features is observed or when only part of the true subspace is known. Although our inexact-
side-information theory can technically cover such cases, it may not provide the sharpest or most
interpretable characterization of this type of structural information.

F.1
Detailed simulation setup
This subsection gives the details omitted from the simulation setup in Section 5.1.
The ground truth
matrix L⋆has dimensions n1 = n2 = n = 1000 and rank r = 10, and the side-information dimension is
a1 = a2 = a = 50. We first generate orthonormal matrices X⋆, Y ⋆∈Rn×a and a rank-r matrix Z ∈Ra×a
with spectral norm 1, and define
L⋆= X⋆ZY ⋆⊤.
The matrix L⋆is generated once and then kept fixed throughout the synthetic experiments. For each trial,
the observed index set Ωis resampled with independent entrywise sampling probability p. In the noiseless
setting, we observe
M = PΩ(L⋆),

while in the noisy setting we observe
M = PΩ(L⋆+ E),
where the entries of E are i.i.d. Gaussian with mean 0 and standard deviation σ = 0.001.
For experiments with inexact side information, we replace X⋆, Y ⋆by matrices X, Y with prescribed
inexactness level δ in the sense of Assumption 3. We describe the construction for the column side information
X; the row side information Y is generated analogously by applying the same procedure to (L⋆)⊤.
Let U ⋆be the left singular matrix of L⋆. We first choose target principal angles 0 ≤θ1 ≤· · · ≤θr =
arcsin δ, with the first r−1 angles drawn uniformly from [0, arcsin δ]. Next, we extend U ⋆to an orthonormal
basis of Rn and select orthonormal matrices U1 ∈Rn×r and U2 ∈Rn×(a−r) such that both are orthogonal
to U ⋆and U2 is also orthogonal to U1. We then define the first r columns of X by
X·,i = cos(θi)[U ⋆]·,i + sin(θi)[U1]·,i,
1 ≤i ≤r,
where [U ⋆]·,i and [U1]·,i are the i-th columns of U ⋆and U1, respectively. We then take the remaining a −r
columns from U2.
This construction ensures that the principal angles between col(X) and col(U ⋆) are exactly θ1, . . . , θr,
and hence ∥sin Θ1∥= δ. For each 1 ≤i ≤r, the vector Xi lies in the two-dimensional plane spanned by U ⋆
i
and (U1)i, and makes angle exactly θi with U ⋆
i . These planes are mutually orthogonal because the columns
of U ⋆and U1 are orthonormal, and the remaining columns from U2 are orthogonal to col(U ⋆). Therefore
X⊤U ⋆is diagonal in these coordinates, with diagonal entries cos(θ1), . . . , cos(θr). Hence the singular values
of X⊤U ⋆are exactly cos(θ1), . . . , cos(θr), which means the principal angles between col(X) and col(U ⋆) are
precisely θ1, . . . , θr.
