SUM OF CONSECUTIVE POWERS AS A PERFECT POWER
ANGELOS KOUTSIANAS AND NIKOS TZANAKIS
Abstract. In this paper we study the equation
xk + (x + 1)k = yn,
n ≥3,
when k ≡2 (mod 4). We prove that the only solutions are for x = 0, −1 when 6 ≤k ≤100
or for a k with odd prime factors congruent to 3 (mod 4). We use linear forms in logarithms,
the modular method and the resolution of Thue equations.
1. Introduction
A fundamental question in Diophantine equations is the determination of the sums of
consecutive perfect powers that are a perfect power.
For example, Euler [14, art.
249]
showed the very beautiful relation
63 = 33 + 43 + 53.
In the last few decades, many mathematicians have tried to get similar to Euler’s above
relation, with a variety of different techniques, by studying the equation
(1.1)
xk + (x + 1)k + · · · + (x + d −1)k = yn,
n ≥2,
for different values of k and d.
When k = 2 the cases 2 ≤d ≤10 have been solved by Cohn, Patel and Zhang [9, 28, 18].
The case k = 3 and 2 ≤d ≤50 has been resolved by many [26, 8, 23, 28, 4]. For k ≥4 less
are known. The case k = 4 and d = 2 is due to the deep results of Ellenberg about the image
of the Galois representations of Q-curves over imaginary quadratic fields and the resolution
of the generalized Fermat equation of signature (2, 4, n) [13, 2]. For k = 4, 5, 6 and d = 3 we
have the results of Zhang and Bennett-Patel-Siksek [28, 3]. Finally, for higher values of k,
the asymptotic theorem of Patel and Siksek states that equation (1.1) has no solutions for
almost all d ≥2 when k is even [19]. We recommend the survey [10] for a detailed exposition
of the known results about (1.1) and its generalizations.
Apart from the cases k = 2, 3, 4 there are no results for d = 2. This is not a coincidence
and has a very simple but deep explanation. For d = 2 the equation (1.1) is a special case
of the generalized Fermat equation of signature (r, r, p). From Darmon’s program [11] we
know the existence of the obstructions (0, 1, 1) and (1, 0, 1) that correspond to non-singular
Frey hyperelliptic curves with complex multiplication. We could isolate the two obstructions
if there was an analogous to Darmon-Merel results [12] for curves of genus greater than 1
but this is far beyond the current technology. The same obstructions, and their difficulties,
appear in our case for x = 0, −1 and |y| = 1.

In this paper, we consider the case d = 2 and study the equation
(1.2)
xk + (x + 1)k = yn,
n ≥3,
for k ≡2 (mod 4). To the best of our knowledge, it is the first attempt to solve (1.1) for
d = 2 and big values of k. Even though the mentioned obstructions still exist, we succeed in
overcoming all of the above difficulties by reducing the resolution of (1.2) to the resolution
of a finite number of Lebesgue-Nagell equations that we can solve. The main result of the
paper is the following theorem.
Theorem 1.1. Suppose k ≡2 (mod 4). If 6 ≤k ≤100, then the only solutions of the
Diophantine equation
(1.3)
xk + (x + 1)k = yn,
n ≥3,
result when x ∈{−1, 0}.
Note that, if (x, y, n) is a solution with x ≤−2, then (|x| −1, y, n) is also a solution,
therefore we may assume that x ≥1 and, consequently, y > 1 and odd. By changing the
variable x (and leaving unaltered y) we can express the consecutive positive integers x, x + 1
as (x −1)/2, (x + 1)/2, where x is an odd integer with x > 1. Therefore, our equation
becomes
(1.4)
(x −1)k + (x + 1)k
2
= 2k−1yn,
x, y ≥3 odd,
n ≥3,
which is symmetric on the left-hand side with respect to x around 0.
For the rest of the paper, we solve (1.4) instead of (1.2) for n = 4 or n is an odd prime.
In the study of (1.4) we use linear forms in logarithms to get an upper bound n0 of n, the
modular method to prove that there are no other solutions of (1.4) apart from those with
y = 1 and the resolution of Thue equations when n ≤7.
It is important to mention that the case k = 2 of (1.4) has been studied in [9, 1] and we
have the following result which we also use for k ≥6.
Theorem 1.2. The only solutions of the Diophantine equation
(1.5)

are (|x|, y, n) = (1, 1, n), (239, 13, 4).

Theorem 1.3. If k ≡2 (mod 4) with all its odd prime factors congruent to 3 (mod 4), then
the solutions of the Diophantine equation
(1.6)
xk + (x + 1)k = yn,

9. Conclusion
The methods and analysis we use to get Theorem 1.1 is, in principle, applicable to any
given value k ≡2 (mod 4) with k > 100. Therefore, it is quite natural to move our attention
to the cases k ̸≡2 (mod 4).
The case k ≡0 (mod 4) is a consequence of the work of
Bennett-Ellenberg-Ng about the generalized Fermat equation of signature (2, 4, n) that we
have already mention [13, 2]. Therefore, the remaining case is when k is odd.
For k odd the situation is quite different. The key Lemma 4.2 no longer holds; however,
equation (1.4) can be written as
(9.1)
xfk(x) = 2k−1yn,
where fk(x) ∈Z[x]. This implies that x is almost a perfect n-th power which is a very
restricted property for x. Even the case k = 5 is a very challenging problem.
