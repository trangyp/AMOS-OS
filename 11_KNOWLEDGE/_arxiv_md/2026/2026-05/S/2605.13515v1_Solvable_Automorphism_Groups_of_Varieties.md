SOLVABLE AUTOMORPHISM GROUPS OF VARIETIES
SERGE CANTAT, HANSPETER KRAFT,
ANDRIY REGETA, IMMANUEL VAN SANTEN
ABSTRACT. Let X be a variety of dimension n, and let Aut(X) be its auto-
morphism group. When X is quasi-affine, we prove that a solvable subgroup
of Aut(X) that is generated by an irreducible family of automorphisms con-
taining the identity is an algebraic subgroup.
Our main applications concern arbitrary varieties. First, every connected
solvable subgroup of Aut(X) is contained in a Borel subgroup and its derived
length is ≤n + 1. Second, the notion of solvable and unipotent radicals are
well defined for any subgroup of Aut(X). Third, if X is quasi-affine and
connected and B ⊆Aut(X) is a Borel subgroup of derived length n+1, then
X is isomorphic to the affine n-space An and B is conjugate to the Jonquières
subgroup.

1. INTRODUCTION
Let k be an algebraically closed field. By convention, algebraic varieties,
subvarieties, morphisms, etc. will always be defined over k. Throughout this
article, X denotes an algebraic variety, not necessarily irreducible, and Aut(X)
its automorphism group.
1.1. Algebraic families of automorphisms. A good example to keep in mind
is the affine space of dimension n which we denote by An. Thanks to Jung’s
theorem (see [Jun42, Lam25]), Aut(A2) is relatively well understood, but when
n ≥3, its properties are still rather mysterious.
Definition 1.1 (see [Ram64]). Let A be an algebraic variety. A map ρ: A →
Aut(X) is a morphism (or an algebraic family of automorphisms) if the map
A×X →X defined by (a,x) 7→ρ(a)(x) is a morphism of algebraic varieties. A
subset V ⊆Aut(X) is constructible if it is the image ρ(A) of a morphism. If we
can choose A to be irreducible, V is an irreducible constructible subset.
Definition 1.2. A subgroup G of Aut(X) is algebraically generated if there is
a morphism ρ: A →Aut(X) such that (i) the algebraic variety A is irreducible,
(ii) ρ(A) contains the identity idX, and (iii) G is generated by ρ(A) as a sub-
group of Aut(X). We write G = ⟨ρ(A)⟩.
Equivalently, G is algebraically generated if G = ⟨V⟩for some irreducible
constructible subset V of Aut(X) containing idX. We insist on assumptions
(i) and (ii). They are crucial and could be easily forgotten, because they are
implicit in the vocabulary “algebraically generated” (see Example 1.4 below).
Definition 1.3. A subgroup G of Aut(X) is connected if for every g ∈G there
is an algebraic family ρ: A →Aut(X) parametrized by an irreducible variety A,
such that ρ(A) ⊆G and ρ(A) contains idX and g.
The dimension dim(S) of a subset S ⊆Aut(X) is the supremum of dim(A)
over all injective morphisms ι: A →Aut(X) with ι(A) ⊆S. If dim(S) < ∞, then
S is finite dimensional. For instance, Aut(An) is finite dimensional if and only
if n ≤1. The next result endows finite dimensional subgroups of Aut(X) with a
canonical structure of an algebraic group. We refer to [Ram64] for the original
version of this theorem, and to [CKRvS25] for the generalization we state here
which includes the case of reducible varieties.
Ramanujam’s Theorem. Let X be an algebraic variety. Let G be a subgroup
of Aut(X) containing a connected and finite dimensional subgroup of finite
index. Then G carries the structure of an algebraic group which is uniquely
determined by the following universal property:

1.2. Solvable, algebraically generated groups. Recall that the derived sub-
group [G,G] of a group G is the subgroup generated by all commutators [g,h] =
ghg−1h−1 with g,h ∈G. Its derived series G(n) is defined recursively by G(0) :=
G and G(n+1) := [G(n),G(n)]. The group G is solvable if G(n) = {eG} for some
n ≥0, and the derived length dl(G) is the smallest such n.
Recall that a quasi-affine variety is an open subvariety of an affine variety.
Theorem A. If X is a quasi-affine variety, then every algebraically generated
solvable subgroup of Aut(X) is an affine algebraic subgroup. Thus, a con-
nected solvable subgroup is tempered.
This extends Theorem B of [CRX23] from the affine and commutative to the
quasi-affine and solvable setting. The proof is given in § 5.3.

1.3. Connected solvable subgroups. An element g ∈Aut(X) is said to be
algebraic if it is contained in an algebraic subgroup G of Aut(X). When X is
quasi-affine, G is automatically an affine algebraic group (see Lemma 2.11),
and we shall say that g is unipotent if g is unipotent as an element of G. Then
a subgroup U ⊆Aut(X), X again quasi-affine, is unipotent if every element
of U is unipotent. For instance the automorphisms (x,y) 7→(x,y + p(x)) with
p ∈k[x] form a unipotent subgroup of Aut(A2). We refer to § 2.6 for a more
detailed discussion of these notions.
An algebraic torus (shortly a torus) in Aut(X) is an algebraic subgroup iso-
morphic to (Gm)s for some s ≥1 where Gm denotes the multiplicative group
k× := (k\{0},×). The following result is our first application of Theorem A;
its proof is given at the end of § 6.1.
Corollary B. Let X be a quasi-affine variety, and let H ⊆Aut(X) be a con-
nected solvable subgroup. Let U ⊆H be the set of all unipotent elements in
H . Then U is a tempered unipotent subgroup and H is a semi-direct product
of U and an algebraic torus T ⊆Aut(X).
The normal subgroup U ⊆H is called the unipotent radical of H . Unipotent
and solvable radicals will be discussed in more generality in Section 6.4.

Theorem C. Let X be an algebraic variety of dimension n, and let H be a
connected solvable subgroup of Aut(X).
(1) The derived length of H is at most n+1.
(2) Assume in addition that X is connected and quasi-affine. If the unipotent
radical U of H has derived length ≥n, then X ≃An and H is conjugate
to a subgroup of Jonq(n).
The proof is given at the end of § 6.2. Assertion (1) should be compared with
analogous results obtained for Lie algebras of vector fields in characteristic 0
in [ET79, MP14]. In (2), X must be connected because Aut(An) = Aut(An∪Y)
for the disjoint union An ∪Y if Aut(Y) is trivial; and X must be quasi-affine, as
shown in Example 7.11.
1.5. Borel subgroups. Recall that a Borel subgroup of an algebraic group
G is a subgroup which is maximal among all connected and solvable sub-
groups of G. Over an algebraically closed field, there is a single conjugacy
class of Borel subgroups. For instance, the upper triangular matrices form a
Borel subgroup of GLn(k) of derived length 1 + ⌈log2(n)⌉(see [Sup76, §18,
Theorem 1]). By analogy, a subgroup of Aut(X) is a Borel subgroup if it is
maximal among connected solvable subgroups. We shall obtain the following
results (see Theorem C, Corollary 6.22, and Theorem 6.25).
• Any connected solvable subgroup of Aut(X) is contained in a Borel
subgroup and its derived length is ≤dim(X)+1.
• Any Borel subgroup of Aut(An) of derived length n+1 is conjugate to
Jonq(n).
• If a Borel subgroup of Aut(An) contains an n-dimensional torus, then
it is conjugate to Jonq(n).
These results suggest interesting similarities between automorphism groups
and linear algebraic groups, at least for Borel subgroups, but there are also
important differences, as shown in the next two items.
1.5.1.
For the plane, the analogy is excellent: all Borel subgroups of Aut(A2)
are conjugate to Jonq(2) (see Proposition 6.24 below, and [BEE16] when k =
C). But the situation changes drastically in dimension n ≥3. In that case
Aut(An) contains a Borel subgroup that is not conjugate to Jonq(n) (see Corol-
lary 1.5 of [RUvS25] and Proposition 7.1 below). In characteristic 0, we do not
know whether the number of conjugacy classes of Borel subgroups of Aut(An)
is finite or infinite. But in positive characteristic, we shall use Gupta’s coun-
terexamples to the “cancellation problem” to show that Aut(An) contains in-
finitely many conjugacy classes of Borel subgroups if n ≥4 (see Theorem 7.2).

1.6. Nested subgroups. If a subgroup G of Aut(X) is tempered, it contains
a lot of algebraic subgroups, but it is not clear whether it contains a countable
sequence G1 ⊆G2 ⊆··· of algebraic subgroups covering G, i.e., whether G
is nested. Conversely, if G is nested it is not obvious that every algebraically
generated subgroup is algebraic. However, the following equivalence will be
derived from Theorem A, see Theorem 6.10.
Theorem D. Let X be an algebraic variety. A subgroup of Aut(X) is tempered
if and only if it is nested by connected algebraic subgroups.
Our final application of Theorem A, given in § 6.6, is a proof of one of the
main results of [Per24], answering positively [KZ24, Question 4]. To state it,
recall that if X is affine, then Aut(X) is an ind-group with a natural topology
(see § 2.1 below).
Theorem E. Assume that chark = 0. Let X be an affine variety and let G ⊆
Aut(X) be a connected nested subgroup. Then G is closed in Aut(X), and
G = L ⋉U, with L a reductive algebraic subgroup and U a closed nested
unipotent subgroup of Aut(X).
The assumption char(k) = 0 is necessary as shown by Example 6.13.
1.7. Organization of the paper. Theorem A is proven by induction on the
derived length of the group and the dimension of X. It relies on a version of
Rosenlicht’s theorem due to Popov (see [Pop14, RUvS25]) which is described
in § 2. We also need a version of the Lie-Kolchin Theorem (see Theorem F in
§ 3.4). Then, Theorem A is proven in § 4 and 5. Corollary B, Theorems C, D
and E, and the main properties of Borel subgroups are obtained in § 6.

2.2. Nested and strongly nested ind-groups. Let G = S

Following the definition from § 1.1, G is nested if there is a countable ascend-
ing filtration G1 ⊆G2 ⊆··· ⊆G by algebraic groups. This does not imply that

2.6. Rosenlicht’s decomposition and the unipotent radical. Unipotent ele-
ments of affine algebraic groups are discussed in §15 of [Hum75]. An algebraic
group is unipotent if it is affine and consists of unipotent elements. In charac-
teristic zero a unipotent group is connected. In characteristic p > 0 an element
of an affine algebraic group is unipotent if and only if its order is finite and a
power of p.

The following result is due to Igusa who used it in his proof of [Igu73, Lemma 4].
Another proof can be found in [Kra84, II.3.4 Lemma and Bemerkung on p. 106].
Lemma 7.5. Let A,B be irreducible affine varieties. Let ϕ: A →B be a birational
morphism. Assume that B is normal and that codimB B\ϕ(A) ≥2. Then ϕ is an iso-
morphism.
Proof. Let r ∈k(B) be a rational function on B. If r is not regular on B, then r−1 van-
ishes on a hypersurface H ⊆B, because B is normal. Since B\ϕ(A) has codimension
at least 2 the inverse image ϕ−1(H) contains a hypersurface L ⊆A, and ϕ∗(r−1) van-
ishes on L. It follows that ϕ∗(r) is not regular on A. Hence, if the pull-back of a rational
function r on B is regular on A, then r is regular on B. Hence, ϕ∗: O(B) →O(A) is
surjective and thus an isomorphism.

7.3. Increasing sequences of algebraic subgroups.
Lemma 7.6. Assume char(k) = 0 or k is uncountable. Let G be an algebraic group
that is an increasing union of closed subgroups Hi, i ∈N. Then G = Hi for some i.
This lemma does not hold if k is countable and of positive characteristic. For exam-
ple, SL2(Fp) is the increasing union of the finite subgroups SL2(Fpn).
Proof. The statement follows from [FK18, Lemma 1.3.1] when k is uncountbale, so
we assume char(k) = 0. It suffices to show that G contains a finitely generated dense
subgroup, and we may assume G connected. Since Gaff/Ru(Gaff) is reductive, it is
generated by its root subgroups and a maximal torus (Theorem 26.3 in [Hum75]).
Since char(k) = 0, both Ga(k) and Gm(k) contain elements that generate a dense
subgroup. We deduce that both Ru(Gaff) and Gaff/Ru(Gaff) contain a finitely generated
dense subgroup, and so does Gaff.
Using the short exact sequence 1 →Gaff →G →G/Gaff →1, we may now assume
that G is an abelian variety. By Corollary 1 in [Mum08, Ch. IV, § 18] any abelian
variety is isogenous to a finite product of simple ones, thus we may assume that G
is simple. Then, again because char(k) = 0, there is an element of infinite order in G
(see [FJ74, Theorem 10.1]) and the claim follows.
□
