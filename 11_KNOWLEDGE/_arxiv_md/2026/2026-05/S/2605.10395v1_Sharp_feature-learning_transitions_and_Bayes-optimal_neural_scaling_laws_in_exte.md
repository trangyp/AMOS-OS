Abstract
We study the information-theoretic limits of learning a one-hidden-layer teacher
network with hierarchical features from noisy queries, in the context of knowledge
transfer to a smaller student model. We work in the high-dimensional regime
where the teacher width k scales linearly with the input dimension d – a setting
that captures large-but-finite-width networks and has only recently become analyt-
ically tractable. Using a heuristic leave-one-out decoupling argument, validated
numerically throughout, we derive asymptotically sharp characterizations of the
Bayes-optimal generalization error and individual feature overlaps via a system
of closed fixed-point equations. These equations reveal that feature learnability is
governed by a sequence of sharp phase transitions: as data grows, teacher features
become recoverable sequentially, each through a discontinuous jump in overlap.
This sequential acquisition underlies a precise notion of effective width kc – the
number of learnable features at a given data budget n – which unifies two distinct
scaling regimes: a feature-learning regime in which the Bayes-optimal generaliza-
tion error εBO scales as n1/(2β)−1, and a refinement regime in which it scales as
n−1, where β > 1/2 is the exponent of the power-law feature hierarchy. Both laws
collapse to the single relation εBO = Θ(kcd/n). We further show empirically that
a student trained with ADAM near the effective width kc achieves these optimal
scaling laws (up to a small algorithmic gap), and provide an information-theoretic
account of the associated scaling in model size.

Introduction
The performance of large neural networks often follows simple power laws in data, compute, and
model size [26, 31, 28]. A growing line of theory attributes this regularity to a hierarchical organiza-
tion of the target function: features carry geometrically decaying importance and a learner acquires
them roughly in order [29, 34, 2, 36, 22, 20]. In a teacher-student setting – the basic primitive behind
data-driven model distillation [11, 27, 25] – this hierarchy raises an immediate design question: at a
fixed data budget, how should one size the student?
Figure 1 exhibits two empirical regularities for an ADAM-trained student. (i) There is a critical width
beyond which the test error increases (middle panel); below this threshold, the error follows a power
law in model size with exponent 1 −2β for a power-law feature hierarchy of exponent β > 1/2.
(ii) At or near the optimal width, the error obeys two distinct power laws in n (right panel): a slow
regime with exponent 1/(2β) −1 and a faster regime with exponent −1. These are striking but
mechanism-agnostic: they could reflect properties of ADAM, or fundamental information-theoretic
constraints. To disentangle the two, we lift all computational constraints and adopt a Bayes-optimal
viewpoint, asking:
Q1. Given a fixed dataset, which features of the teacher are statistically recoverable?

Q2. How does the recoverable subset of features constrain the scaling law of the Bayes-optimal
generalization error εBO in the data size n?
We address Q1 and Q2 in a one-hidden-layer teacher–student model in the extensive-width regime
k = Θ(d), with k the teacher width and d the input dimension. This regime captures the large-but-
finite width of practical networks, lies strictly between the well-studied multi-index (k = Θ(1)) and
NTK (k ≫d) extremes, and is the minimal setting for non-trivial feature learning beyond the linear
data scale (n ≫d). Our analytical handle is a heuristic leave-one-out (cavity) decoupling argument,
validated numerically throughout.
Paper organization.

1.3
Additional related works
Multi-index models. Multi-index models (teacher width k = Θ(1) in the context of neural networks)
are a well-developed testbed for feature learning in SGD [3, 1, 18], Bayes-optimal [7, 4, 38], and
computationally-hard [16, 17] regimes. Despite these important insights, multi-index models remain
weakly expressive, and a recent substantial technical effort has been devoted to going beyond this
fixed-width setting [15, 13, 33, 36, 22, 6]. The present work contributes to this research line.
Neural scaling laws. Neural scaling laws have been studied from two broad perspectives. A first line
of work operates in the non-feature-learning regime – kernel methods, random feature models, or
linear regression – and derives scaling laws from spectral assumptions on the data covariance. Several
of these works obtain scaling laws in both data size and model size [5, 10, 32], or characterize how
the optimal model size scales with compute budget [35]. A second line of work introduces genuine
feature learning, either through abstract task models [29, 34, 23, 12] or through concrete neural
network models in the multi-index or sublinear-width regime [2, 36, 22, 20]. The quantization model
of [34] is phenomenologically close to our framework: it postulates that networks learn discrete units
of knowledge sequentially, in order of their contribution to loss, and that model capacity caps the
number of learnable units. These properties instead emerge from first principles in our model.
Our closest predecessor [20] derives data-size scaling laws in the multi-index regime k = Θ(1)
(numerically: k = 10, d = 1000, vs. k = 100, d = 200 here), using a hand-crafted rather than
standard generalization error, and restricted to activations with µ2 ̸= 0; ours are complementary,
covering µ2 = 0 and hence all odd activations. On the algorithmic side, the optimal scaling laws
in [20] are achieved by AMP, later extended in [19] to activations with µ2 ̸= 0 and even to even
functions with µ2 = 0: for AMP, the data-limited exponent acquires a multiplicative correction from
a noise-sensitive exponent defined therein, while the information-theoretic exponents are conjectured
to be unchanged. Despite all these differences, these three works obtain the same BO data-size
scaling exponent, suggesting universality across activation classes and width regimes.
Bayes-optimal learning of extensive-width networks. In the linear data regime n = Θ(d), the
BO problem reduces to linear regression [15, 13]. For quadratic activations at n = Θ(d2), [33]
characterized the BO error but found no feature recovery, due to rotational symmetry intrinsic to this
specific setting. The general case was resolved by [6] using the replica method from physics, for dense
readouts and multiple layers. Our work specializes to one hidden layer and µ2 = 0, but generalizes to

is arguably more direct and does not require familiarity with the replica formalism.

We emphasize that the analytic formula kc = Θ((n/d)1/(2β)) in the feature-learning regime provides
a first-principles prescription for the optimal student width directly from the data budget, without
retraining at each candidate model size – a concrete theoretical underpinning for hyperparameter
transfer of model width [39]: rather than fitting the optimal size empirically by training multiple
students, the exponent β of the feature hierarchy determines how to optimally scale the model width
ks as n varies, in the same spirit as compute-optimal scaling laws [28].

5
Conclusion and limitations
We studied the information-theoretic limits of learning a one-hidden-layer teacher network in the
extensive-width regime k = Θ(d), a setting that captures large-but-finite-width networks and sits
strictly between the multi-index and NTK/infinite-width extremes. We provided a heuristic leave-
one-out argument yielding closed fixed-point equations for the individual feature overlaps (qi) and
the Bayes-optimal error εBO. A single activation-dependent SNR threshold λσ governs feature
learnability: as n grows, features cross it one by one, producing a staircase of sharp phase transitions
in the learning curve. For a power-law hierarchy of features with exponent β > 1/2, the resulting
effective width kc ≤k unifies the two empirically observed scaling regimes via εBO = Θ(kcd/n):
a feature-learning regime in which kc grows with n, and a refinement regime in which kc = k is
saturated and the parametric rate n−1 is recovered. An ADAM-trained student near kc tracks these
Bayes-optimal exponents up to a small residual gap on the weakest features, consistent with an
algorithmic threshold λalgo
σ
> λσ.
The main limitations of our work are that derivations rest on unproved cavity assumptions – Gaussian
cavity fields and overlap concentration – and the restriction µ2 = 0. Tackling general activations in
the extensive-width regime k = Θ(d) is a technical challenge that has been considered only recently
[6]. Closing the µ2 gap [20, 33], proving the leave-one-out reduction, extending to multi-layer
architectures [6], and characterizing compute-optimal scaling [28] are natural next steps.

K.2
HMC simulation
The theoretical predictions for the overlaps are validated by HMC simulation. In general, sampling
from the posterior is difficult since the posterior landscape can be extremely complicated: with
random initialization, it can take exponentially long to reach the true equilibrium. However, if the
initialization lies in the same basin of attraction as the equilibrium state, convergence is achieved with
reasonable computational cost. We initialize such that the first kc rows coincide with the ground truth,
and the remaining rows are random. Note that the HMC does not validate the prediction of kc itself.
K.3

Directly evaluating the Bayes-optimal error from its definition is computationally expensive. Indeed,
computing E[y(x, W | D)] requires drawing many independent samples from the posterior distribu-

GPU. To overcome this difficulty, we compute the Bayes-optimal error through Gibbs error, defined

K.4
Training with SGD
We use the ADAM optimizer implemented in Pytorch to train the student network. We note here
some important points in the training
1. The student’s features are normalized after each epoch, preventing their norms from growing
excessively and resulting in more stable and well-behaved training dynamics.
2. Performance degrades when the student’s readout is fixed to match the teacher during
training, as the student is more likely to get stuck in local minima. In contrast, when the
student readout is learnable, it still recovers that of the teacher, and the possibility for the
readout to change during training helps the student escape local minima.
