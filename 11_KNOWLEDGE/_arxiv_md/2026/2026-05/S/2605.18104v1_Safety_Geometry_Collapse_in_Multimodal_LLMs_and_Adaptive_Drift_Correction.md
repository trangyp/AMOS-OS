Adaptive Drift Correction

Abstract
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) often fail to transfer safety capabili-
ties learned in the text modality to semantically equivalent non-text inputs, revealing
a persistent multimodal safety gap. We study this gap from a representation-
geometric perspective by analyzing a text-aligned refusal direction and a modality-
induced drift direction. We show that multimodal inputs compress the usable
separation along the refusal direction, making it no longer reliable for identifying
and refusing harmful inputs. We refer to this failure mode as Safety Geometry
Collapse. We quantify it through conditional refusal separability and show that
stronger modality-induced drift is consistently associated with weaker refusal
separability and higher attack success rates. We then validate the causal role of
modality-induced drift through a fixed-strength activation intervention: counter-
acting the estimated drift restores refusal separability and improves multimodal
safety. After drift correction, we further observe self-rectification, where the model
recovers its ability to recognize and refuse harmful multimodal inputs during
forward dynamics. This effect also provides an internal signal of the model’s
perceived harmfulness of each input. Motivated by this signal, we propose ReGap,
a training-free inference-time method that adaptively corrects modality drift using
self-rectification. Experiments across multiple multimodal safety benchmarks and
utility benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of ReGap, which significantly
improves the safety of MLLMs without compromising general capabilities. Our
findings highlight representation-level modality alignment as a crucial direction for
real-time safety improvement and for building safer, more reliable MLLMs.

Introduction
Recent progress in multimodal modeling, including vision-language (VL) [Bai et al., 2023, Liu
et al., 2023, Zhu et al., 2024], audio-language (AL) [Chu et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2025a], and omni-
modal systems [Yao et al., 2024, Li et al., 2025a, Xu et al., 2025a,b], has substantially expanded the
capabilities of foundation models. However, multimodal large language models (MLLMs) remain
less robust in safety alignment than their text-only counterparts [Achiam et al., 2023, Gong et al.,
2025]. Harmful intent expressed through images, audio, or mixed modalities can bypass refusal
behaviors that are reliably triggered by semantically equivalent text-only requests [Liu et al., 2024a,
Pan et al., 2025], posing a major obstacle to deploying multimodal systems in real-world settings.
A common explanation attributes these failures to insufficient multimodal safety supervision: harmful
multimodal examples are underrepresented during alignment training, leaving models poorly cal-
ibrated to unsafe non-textual inputs [Pi et al., 2024, Zong et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025]. While
important, this view does not fully explain why models with strong text-only refusal behavior still
fail on semantically equivalent multimodal inputs [Li et al., 2024a, Liu et al., 2025a,b]. This suggests

In this work, we study this failure mode from a representation-geometric perspective (§2). We
formulate multimodal safety through a two-dimensional subspace defined by a text-aligned refusal
direction and a modality-induced drift direction. The refusal direction captures the axis along which
harmful inputs are separated into refusal and compliance (§2.1), while the drift direction captures
how multimodal inputs are displaced from their text-only counterparts (§2.2).
Within this subspace, we identify Safety Geometry Collapse: a modality-induced degeneration of
refusal-separable geometry, where refused and complied harmful inputs become less distinguishable
along the text-aligned refusal direction. As shown in prior work [Li et al., 2024b, Zhao et al., 2025],
aligned LLMs exhibit a substantial representation separation between refused and complied inputs,
giving rise to a clear refusal direction (Figure 1, left). However, as illustrated in Figure 1 (middle), the
introduction of additional audio information induces representation drift away from this text-aligned
geometry, while image-only inputs exhibit an even larger displacement. This modality-induced drift
weakens the refusal geometry, causing the refusal direction to lose its ability to reliably separate
safety behaviors under multimodal inputs. We further quantify and analyze this phenomenon in § 3.1.
This phenomenon connects to prior findings on text-only jailbreaks, where adversarial prompts can
shift harmful requests toward benign or compliant regions in activation space, thereby blurring the
safety boundary [Lee et al., 2025, He et al., 2024, Lin et al., 2024]. However, Safety Geometry
Collapse differs in scope: rather than being induced by individually crafted adversarial inputs, it re-
flects a systematic degradation of the text-aligned refusal geometry under modality shift. Multimodal
jailbreaks therefore do not merely exploit isolated exceptions to a safety boundary; they expose a
broader failure mode in which the boundary itself becomes less reliable for non-textual inputs.
This geometry suggests a direct hypothesis: counteracting modality-induced drift can restore refusal
geometry and recover safety under multimodal inputs, indicating a potential path toward multimodal
safety alignment. We evaluate this hypothesis via a fixed-strength activation intervention that
subtracts the estimated drift (§3.2). Improved refusal on harmful multimodal inputs and restored
refusal-compliance separability validate the hypothesis, while larger drift-correction coefficients
degrade general multimodal capability, revealing a safety–utility trade-off under uniform intervention.
We further identify a layer-wise self-rectification phenomenon in the model’s forward dynamics
(§3.3). As the effect of partial drift correction accumulates over previous layers, inputs move toward
the refusal region along the refusal direction as the forward pass proceeds (Figure 1, right). Harmful
inputs exhibit substantially stronger self-rectification than benign inputs, suggesting that the model
recovers internal sensitivity to harmful inputs once modality-induced drift is partially corrected.
Based on this mechanism, we propose ReGap in § 4, a training-free adaptive inference-time method
for modality-gap correction. ReGap uses a normalized self-rectification signal to decide when stronger
correction is needed, and then counteracts the estimated modality-induced drift before generation.

future alignment research. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

• We propose ReGap, a training-free inference-time method that uses self-rectification signals to
adaptively correct modality drift, improving multimodal safety while preserving general capability.

Geometric Formulation of Multimodal Safety
We formulate multimodal safety through a two-dimensional diagnostic space defined by a text-
aligned refusal direction and a modality-induced drift direction. In § 2.1, we estimate the refusal
direction from text-only safety behavior. In § 2.2, we characterize modality-induced displacement
using semantically aligned multimodal pairs. In § 2.3, we combine these two directions to define a
low-dimensional space for analyzing safety behavior under modality shift.
Let hl(x) ∈Rd denote the hidden representation of input x at layer l, extracted at the final token
position following prior work [Zou et al., 2023a]. We estimate the text-aligned refusal direction from
harmful text-only instructions in AdvBench [Zou et al., 2023b], and estimate the modality-induced
drift direction from semantically aligned multimodal inputs in Omni-SafetyBench [Pan et al., 2025].
All calibration data are disjoint from evaluation samples, with details provided in Appendix F.

3
Mechanistic Analysis of Multimodal Safety Failure
Building on the multimodal safety space defined above, we analyze how modality-induced drift
affects safety behavior and motivates intervention. In § 3.1, we show that larger drift weakens
refusal separability, leading to Safety Geometry Collapse. In § 3.2, we test whether removing this
drift improves multimodal safety and examine the safety–utility trade-off. In § 3.3, we analyze
self-rectification dynamics through which modality-gap correction recovers refusal behavior.

ReGap: Self-Rectifying Modality-Gap Correction
The self-rectification dynamics observed in Section 3.3 suggest that modality-gap correction should
be input-dependent. ReGap operationalizes this idea by using self-rectification as a signal for adaptive
drift correction, rather than applying a fixed intervention strength to all multimodal inputs.
For an input x, ReGap first performs a lightweight probe forward pass. During this probe, a small
fixed correction with coefficient λprobe = 0.1 is applied to the input-side hidden states. The probe is
used only to measure the self-rectification response and does not require decoding. We then compute
the self-rectification score Sls(x) at a model-specific diagnostic layer ls in the middle-to-late layers,
where the refusal direction is stable and refusal-relevant dynamics are more explicit. Layer choices
and computational overhead are provided in Appendix F.
ReGap assigns a single input-level correction coefficient according to this self-rectification score:

where τ is a model-specific threshold and λ1 > λ2. The larger coefficient λ1 is used when the
probe reveals strong refusal-recovery dynamics, indicating that the input can benefit from stronger
modality-gap correction. The smaller coefficient λ2 is used when this signal is weak, reducing the risk
of disrupting benign multimodal processing. After α(x) is determined, ReGap applies modality-gap
correction to the input-side hidden states at all layers:
hl′(x) = hl(x) −α(x)gl.
(9)
The correction is applied only during the input forward pass; no intervention is applied during the
decoding stage. In this way, ReGap adaptively reduces modality-induced displacement without
directly adding refusal-direction activation, enabling the model to recover its existing refusal behavior
while limiting unnecessary interference with general multimodal capability.

Safety results.
ReGap substantially improves multimodal safety. Table 1 shows that ReGap
effectively reduces unsafe compliance across diverse multimodal inputs. Compared with post-training
baselines such as VLGuard-SFT and SPA-VL-DPO, which are mainly built around vision-text safety
alignment, ReGap shows stronger robustness under heterogeneous modality combinations. Although
these baselines can perform well on some pure-audio inputs, their effectiveness is less stable on
audio-text inputs, highlighting the difficulty of transferring safety behavior across modalities. This
also reflects a key limitation of post-training defenses: they rely heavily on high-quality safety data
covering each modality and modality combination. In contrast, ReGap directly corrects representation-
level modality drift and can recover latent refusal behavior without additional safety training.

Related Work
Safety alignment for multimodal language models.
Safety alignment has been widely studied
for text-only language models through supervised fine-tuning [Ouyang et al., 2022, Dong et al.,
2024], RLHF [Bai et al., 2022, Ji et al., 2023, 2025a], model editing [Gao et al., 2024, Zhao et al.,
2024, Wu et al., 2025b], and direct preference optimization [Rafailov et al., 2023, Xu et al., 2024].
In multimodal models, however, harmful intent can be embedded in non-textual inputs, such as
typographic instructions in images [Liu et al., 2024b, Gong et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2024a] or speech-
transformed harmful queries [Song et al., 2025, Chen et al., 2026], creating attack surfaces not fully
covered by text-centric safeguards. Existing defenses mainly rely on multimodal safety instruction
tuning, safety-aware data augmentation, or preference optimization over safe and unsafe multimodal
responses [Zong et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2025, Ji et al., 2025b]. Recent evaluations show that
such post-training methods improve benchmark-level safety but remain modality-dependent and
can degrade under out-of-distribution modality combinations, partly due to limited coverage of
high-quality multimodal safety data [Peng et al., 2025, Jeong et al., 2025, Lu et al., 2025].
Representation Engineering for Multimodal Safety.
Representation engineering [Zou et al.,
2023a] interprets and controls LLM behaviors via activation-space directions, and has been used
to probe safety representations [Arditi et al., 2024, Zhao et al., 2025, Zheng et al., 2024], analyze
jailbreaks [Zheng et al., 2024, Li et al., 2025c], and steer refusal of harmful inputs [Shen et al., 2024,
Sheng et al., 2025]. In MLLMs, existing methods mainly apply refusal-related vectors to suppress
harmful multimodal responses [Yang et al., 2026, Zhu et al., 2026], rather than explicitly diagnosing
the modality-specific representation failures underlying safety degradation. Although ShiftDC [Zou
et al., 2025] and VLMGuard [Liu et al., 2025c] show that modality gaps or activation shifts can
weaken safety representations, they largely use this observation to motivate refusal interventions.
In contrast, our work treats modality-induced drift as the central object of analysis: we estimate it
from paired text and multimodal inputs, quantify how it weakens the reliability of refusal-direction
separation on multimodal inputs, and correct the drift itself through adaptive modality-gap correction.
7
Conclusion
In this work, we study the multimodal safety gap in MLLMs from a representation-geometric
perspective. We identify Safety Geometry Collapse, a failure mode where modality-induced drift
weakens the text-aligned refusal geometry and makes harmful multimodal inputs harder to separate
and refuse. Through both geometric analysis and activation intervention, we show that this drift
plays a causal role in multimodal safety degradation. Motivated by these findings, we propose
ReGap, a training-free inference-time method that adaptively corrects modality-induced drift using
self-rectification signals. Experiments across multiple multimodal safety and utility benchmarks
show that ReGap improves safety without substantially compromising general capability. Our results
highlight representation-level modality alignment as an important direction for improving safety
transfer across modalities and building more reliable MLLMs.

A
Limitations
Unified modality-induced drift direction.
For diagnostic simplicity, our analysis uses a unified
modality-induced drift direction to characterize the dominant representation shift from text-only
inputs to multimodal inputs. This compact formulation enables a shared geometric view across
different modality combinations and supports a simple training-free correction mechanism. However,
modality shift may not be fully homogeneous across images, audio, video, and omni-modal inputs.
Modeling modality-specific drift directions, or even category-specific drift subspaces, may capture
finer-grained representation changes and further improve both safety and utility.
Dependence on calibration data and hyperparameters.
ReGap depends on calibration data
and model-specific hyperparameters. In particular, estimating the refusal direction, estimating the
modality-induced drift direction, selecting diagnostic layers, and choosing thresholds and correction
strengths all require a calibration procedure. Although our experiments show consistent effectiveness
across multiple models and benchmarks, further validation is needed to assess how robust these
choices are under different calibration sets, languages, domains, harmfulness taxonomies, and model
families.
15


## Page 16


Scope of evaluated models.
Our experiments focus on open-weight multimodal or omni-modal
models whose internal hidden states can be accessed and intervened on. This design is necessary for
representation-level analysis and activation-space correction, but it limits the immediate applicability
of ReGap to closed-source or API-only systems. Whether similar safety geometry and modality-gap
correction effects can be recovered in black-box settings remains an open question.
Granularity of adaptive correction.
Due to the computational cost of exploring dynamic interven-
tion policies, the current version of ReGap adopts a discrete adaptive strategy: it selects between two
correction strengths according to the self-rectification signal. This already captures input-dependent
correction and reduces unnecessary perturbation to benign inputs, but it may not be optimal for every
sample. A more fine-grained policy that dynamically estimates the most suitable correction strength
for each input could further improve the safety–utility trade-off, and we leave this direction for future
work.
B
Broader Impact
Positive societal impacts.
This work aims to improve the safety of multimodal large language
models by reducing unsafe compliance when harmful intent is expressed through non-text modalities
such as images, audio, or their combinations. As multimodal systems are increasingly deployed in
real-world applications, failures to transfer text-based safety alignment to multimodal inputs may
expose users to harmful, illegal, or otherwise unsafe content. By identifying a representation-level
failure mode and proposing a training-free inference-time correction method, our work may contribute
to safer deployment of multimodal assistants, especially in settings where harmful instructions can
be embedded in visual or auditory inputs. The proposed analysis may also help researchers better
understand why safety alignment fails across modalities and develop more robust safety mechanisms.
Potential negative societal impacts and misuse.
Because this paper studies the internal mecha-
nisms behind multimodal safety failures, it may also provide information that could be misused by
adversaries to design more adaptive multimodal jailbreaks. In particular, attackers might attempt to
construct inputs that avoid triggering the self-rectification signal or exploit modality-specific shifts
not fully captured by the unified drift direction. In addition, imperfect intervention policies, such
as fixed-strength correction or overly coarse adaptive correction, may lead to under-correction on
harmful inputs or over-correction on benign but safety-adjacent queries. This could either leave
harmful multimodal requests insufficiently mitigated or reduce the usefulness of multimodal systems
for legitimate users. Calibration bias is another concern: if the calibration data do not sufficiently
cover different languages, cultural contexts, domains, or accessibility-related input formats, the
resulting correction may be less reliable for some user groups or application scenarios.
Mitigation strategies.
We view ReGap as a complementary inference-time safeguard rather than
a complete safety solution. In practical deployments, it should be combined with other safety
mechanisms, including multimodal safety training, input and output moderation, adversarial red-
teaming, human oversight in high-risk settings, and continuous monitoring for distribution shift.
To reduce misuse risk, future releases should emphasize defensive evaluation and avoid providing
unnecessary operational details that directly facilitate attacks. Further work should evaluate ReGap
under adaptive adversaries, broader languages and domains, and more diverse multimodal inputs,
while also measuring both false refusals and missed refusals to better characterize the safety–utility
trade-off across user populations.

complex, multi-step reasoning.

benchmark targets the elicitation of objectionable content through two distinct evaluation

behaviors formulated as user instructions. It serves as a rigorous red-teaming testbed to

rather than correcting the underlying modality-induced drift itself.

E.4
Human Evaluation Protocol
We conduct a human evaluation to validate the reliability of the automatic ASR judge (gpt-5-mini;
Section E.2), rather than to replace it as the primary metric at scale.
Sampling.
We randomly sample 550 model outputs from the pool of safety evaluations used in
our experiments, together with the corresponding inputs (multimodal prompts and, when applicable,
generation settings). The sample is constructed so that it spans Omni-SafetyBench (including
multiple modality formats from the benchmark taxonomy), MM-SafetyBench, and AudioJailBench,
and includes a mix of evaluation conditions (e.g., base inference and representative inference-time
interventions such as ReGap and strong baselines), in order to cover diverse harmful topics and
non-textual attack surfaces.
Annotation procedure.
Each output is independently annotated by two trained annotators who
are blind to the evaluated model identity and experimental condition. Annotators judge whether
the model’s behavior corresponds to a successful attack under our ASR definition: the response
indicates that the model understood the harmful request and failed to refuse it with substantive,
intent-following content, consistent with the understanding-then-refusal logic used by the automatic
judge. Disagreements are resolved by discussion or adjudicated by a third annotator.
Annotation effort.
The human evaluation consists of 550 responses, each annotated independently
by two annotators. Annotating a single response takes a few minutes on average, resulting in
approximately a dozen hours of total annotation effort, excluding adjudication.
Agreement analysis.
We measure inter-annotator agreement and agreement between the consensus
human judgment and gpt-5-mini. The automatic judge achieves an overall agreement of 95.8%
with human judgments on the sampled items, indicating that it reliably tracks unsafe compliance for
multimodal harmful prompts in our evaluation setup.

F
Implementation Details
We implement main experiments with PyTorch and Transformers [Wolf et al., 2020] on a sin-
gle NVIDIA A800 GPU. For all models, the inference procedure follows the official model-
specific chat template or instruction template. We disable sampling during generation by setting
do_sample=False, and therefore use greedy decoding for all reported evaluations. This determinis-
tic decoding setup reduces variance across runs and ensures that observed changes in safety behavior
are attributable to representation intervention rather than sampling randomness.
Calibration data.
We use two calibration datasets for estimating the representation directions. For
the modality-induced drift direction, we construct a calibration split from Omni-SafetyBench, which
contains 972 harmful intents expressed across multiple modalities with semantically aligned content.
We randomly sample 472 harmful intents from Omni-SafetyBench and use their corresponding
inputs across all available modality formats for drift estimation. This ensures that the estimated
modality-induced drift is computed from modality variations of the same underlying harmful intents,
rather than from semantic differences across examples. For the text-aligned refusal direction, we use
AdvBench, which contains 520 harmful text-only instructions. We run the model on these text-only
inputs and estimate the refusal direction by separating examples according to whether the model
refuses or complies.
Hidden-state extraction.
We extract hidden states using vLLM [Kwon et al., 2023] together
with EasySteer [Xu et al., 2025c]. For each input, we run a forward pass under the official infer-
ence template and collect hidden representations from every transformer layer. Following prior
representation-engineering practice, we use the hidden state at the final input token position as
the sequence-level representation. This position is used for both refusal-direction estimation and
modality-drift estimation, since it summarizes the model’s internal state after processing the full input
context, including text, image, audio, or omni-modal information. Hidden states are extracted only
from the input-side forward pass; no hidden states from autoregressive decoding steps are used for
direction estimation.
Direction estimation.
All directions are computed independently at each layer using a difference-
in-means estimator. For the refusal direction, we first run the model on harmful text-only calibration
inputs and separate examples according to whether the model refuses or complies.
Activation steering.
We implement activation steering with EasySteer and vLLM. All steering
experiments apply layer-specific interventions to the input-side hidden states at every transformer
layer. Given a hidden representation hl(x) at layer l, drift correction is applied as
hl′(x) = hl(x) −λgl,
(11)
where λ denotes a global intervention strength shared across all layers. For ReGap, the fixed
coefficient λ is replaced by the input-dependent coefficient α(x) selected according to the self-
rectification signal. In all cases, interventions are applied only during the input forward pass before
generation begins. We do not intervene on hidden states during the autoregressive decoding stage.
This design ensures that the method corrects the model’s multimodal input representation while
leaving the subsequent generation dynamics unchanged.

To instantiate the adaptive decision rule in ReGap, we calibrate the
self-rectification threshold on a single source dataset and keep it fixed for all downstream evaluations.

Specifically, we use only the calibration split of Omni-SafetyBench, together with benign samples

solely on harmful prompts or on modality-specific artifacts.
For each candidate layer in the middle-to-late range, we perform the lightweight probe intervention
and compute the resulting self-rectification score. The threshold is then selected to separate inputs that
exhibit strong refusal-recovery dynamics, for which stronger correction is beneficial, from inputs with
weak self-rectification, for which weaker correction reduces unnecessary perturbation. Importantly,
this procedure does not train a task-specific classifier on final model responses; it uses only an
input-side representation response induced by a fixed probe intervention. As shown in Figure 5b,
harmful and benign inputs exhibit clearly separated self-rectification patterns after drift reduction,
supporting the use of this signal as an adaptive correction gate.

Baseline implementation.
VLGuard-SFT.
We reproduce VLGuard-SFT [Zong et al., 2024] by randomly sampling 1,000
instances from VLGuard, a vision-language safety instruction-following dataset of image–text queries
paired with target responses that encode safe instruction-following behavior. We perform supervised
fine-tuning on the sampled pairs with LoRA adaptation.
23


## Page 24


SPA-VL-DPO.
We reproduce SPA-VL-DPO [Zhang et al., 2025] by randomly sampling 1,000
instances from SPA-VL, where each example contains an image, a question, a chosen response that is
annotated to be both harmless and helpful, and a rejected response corresponding to a simple refusal.
We train with the DPO objective to prefer informative safe responses over plain refusals, using LoRA
adaptation. Both training baselines are reproduced on Qwen2.5-Omni-3B. All runs use one epoch of
optimization with a constant learning rate of 5 × 10−5.
Refusal Steering.
Refusal Steering is a training-free inference-time baseline that strengthens model
alignment with a text-derived refusal direction. We estimate a layer-wise refusal representation
from harmful text-only instructions in AdvBench using the same hidden-state extraction protocol
described in §2.1. During inference, this refusal direction is injected into the model activations at
the final token position of each layer on the input side before decoding. We implement steering
with EasySteer [Xu et al., 2025c] and vLLM [Kwon et al., 2023], and use greedy decoding for
evaluation. The global steering strength is tuned on the Omni-SafetyBench calibration split subject to
decoding-stability constraints: candidate strengths that consistently cause degenerate outputs, such
as repetitive, empty, or otherwise collapsed generations, are discarded, and the final coefficient is
selected from the remaining feasible range based on calibration performance.
ShiftDC.
ShiftDC [Zou et al., 2025] is an inference-time correction method that removes the
component of modality-induced activation drift aligned with the text-derived refusal direction. We
first estimate the refusal direction using the same protocol as in §2.1. For each evaluation example,
we then perform paired forward passes on the multimodal input and its aligned text-only counterpart
to estimate the modality-induced activation shift. ShiftDC suppresses the portion of this shift that lies
along the refusal axis, thereby reducing modality-driven deviations from the original text-aligned
safety behavior. We implement the method with the same EasySteer and vLLM framework used
for ReGap and apply interventions only during the input prefill stage. The intervention strength
is calibrated on the Omni-SafetyBench calibration split using the same decoding-stability filtering
procedure as for Refusal Steering. For multimodal prompts containing non-text inputs, the paired
text-only prompts are reconstructed using aligned chat templates together with synthesized captions
or audio descriptions when necessary.

Cost Analysis
H.1
Theoretical overhead.
We analyze the computational overhead of ReGap by separating the probe computation from final
response generation. Let Cprefill denote the cost of one standard input-side prefill pass, and let
Cdecode denote the cost of autoregressive decoding. Standard inference has complexity
O(Cprefill + Cdecode).
ReGap introduces two additional input-side computations before generation. The first pass obtains
the unmodified hidden state at the diagnostic layer ls, and the second probe pass measures the
self-rectification response under a small drift correction. Since the self-rectification score is computed
only at ls, these two passes only need to run up to the diagnostic layer rather than through the full
model. Let C≤ls denote the cost of an input-side forward pass up to layer ls. The final adaptive
pass is then a standard full prefill pass with the selected correction coefficient, followed by greedy
decoding.
The additional activation operation in ReGap is a layer-wise vector subtraction. For a hidden tensor
with T input positions and hidden dimension D, applying correction at L layers has an arithmetic-
work upper bound of O(LTD). However, this operation is a simple element-wise addition/subtraction
and is highly parallelized on GPU; in practice, its wall-clock cost is much smaller than the transformer
forward computation. Moreover, during the probe stage, correction is only needed up to the diagnostic
layer, so the corresponding vector-operation cost is bounded by O(lsTD) rather than O(LTD).
Therefore, the inference-time complexity of ReGap can be written as
O(2C≤ls + Cprefill + Cdecode + lsTD + LTD + D) ,
where the O(D) term comes from computing the self-rectification score at the diagnostic layer. Since
the vector operations are lightweight and parallelizable, the dominant additional cost is the two partial

We further evaluate the practical inference overhead of ReGap on a single NVIDIA A800 GPU

empirical inference cost.

I.1
Shared Refusal Directions Across Modalities
We further examine whether the refusal direction extracted from text-only inputs is shared across
different input modalities. For each model and each modality format in Omni-SafetyBench, we
independently estimate a modality-specific refusal direction using the same difference-in-means
procedure described in Section 2.1. We then compute pairwise cosine similarities between refusal
directions extracted from different modalities.
We report this analysis under two complementary layer settings. First, we evaluate the high-layer
regime where refusal directions have emerged and become stable. Specifically, we use the last 12
layers for Qwen2.5-Omni-3B and MiniCPM-o-4.5, each of which has 36 transformer layers, and the
last 16 layers for Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct. Second, we report the same similarity analysis
at the final layer, which is most directly associated with the model’s response behavior and refusal
decision.
In high-dimensional spaces, random vectors are expected to be nearly orthogonal due to concentration
of measure. MLLM hidden representations are high-dimensional, and different input modalities
introduce modality-specific information into this representation space. Therefore, if refusal behavior
were represented by modality-specific mechanisms, the refusal directions extracted from different
modalities would not necessarily be expected to align.
