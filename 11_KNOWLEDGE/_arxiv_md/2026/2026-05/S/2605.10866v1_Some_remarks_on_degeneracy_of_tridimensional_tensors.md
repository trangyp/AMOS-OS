SOME REMARKS ON DEGENERACY OF TRIDIMENSIONAL TENSORS
ALESSANDRO GIMIGLIANO AND MONICA ID`A
Abstract. We study tridimensional tensors on C from the point of view of hypermatrices, taking
into consideration the problem of determining whether they are degenerate or not, concise or not,
and what is their essential format if they are non-coincise; moreover, in some cases, their tensor
rank. We use a geometrical approach to these problems which, in part, goes back to Schl¨afli and
consists in studying certain determinantal schemes associated to the hypermatrix.

1. Introduction
The study of hypermatrices goes back to Cayley, whose definition of hyperdeterminant had been
mostly forgotten for a long time, until it has been deeply studied in the fundamental work [GKZ],
now a classical reference on the subject (see also [Gh]). Particularly, the tridimensional case has
received a lot of attention (e.g. see [K1], [K2], [F], [Ol], [TC], [T], [E]).
Here we consider tridimensional hypermatrices on C under various points of view (as associated
to tensors, to multihomogeneous polynomials in three sets of variables, to trilinear applications),
and we want to use a geometrical approach to their study which consists in considering three
determinantal schemes defined by the matrices L, M and N of linear forms in one of the three sets
of variables xi, yj and zk, associated to A = (Aijk), for i = 1, . . . , p, j = 1, . . . , q, k = 1, . . . , r.
This approach goes back to Sch¨afli [Sc] for particular formats, namely, in the tridimensional case,
he considered hypermatrices where two among p, q and r are equal, so one of the matrices of linear

We also consider other properties of A, as the tensor rank of the associated tensor and its conciseness
(i.e. whether the associated multi-homogeneous polynomial can be expressed using less variables).
The notion of concise tensor and of essential format have been much studied for symmetric
polynomials (see e.g. [Ca]); in the general case these notions, which corresponds to the minimal
numbers of variables that can be used to express the tensor, are what was classically called ”index
ranks” (e.g. see [TC]).
It is worth noting that the study of a projective determinantal scheme is often computationally
simpler than considering a tridimensional matrix (e.g. see [A1], [A2]).
Our main reference for our study are [GKZ], the very nice expositive paper [O], and [A1], [A2],
where the connection between degeneracy of A and singularities or unexpected dimension of the
associated schemes is studied. In section 3 we recall and reformat a result of [A2] and its proof for
the reader’s convenience, and we explain why the main result in that paper is not true, so that our
proposition 3.6 is a new result on this matter.
The structure of the paper is as follows: in section 2 we give preliminary definitions and properties
that we need for this study; these are mostly well-known facts, but we list them here to set up
notations and we prove some of them for the reader’s convenience (and particularly for who isn’t
acquainted with the matter); in section 3 we introduce Schl¨afli’s approach and its generalization,
much on the line of what is done in [T] and [TC] to study the (3,3,3) and (3,3,4) formats in order
to determine classes of tensors modulo linear changes of variables and permutations. We use this
approach for our main results (Proposition 3.6 and Theorem 3.8), which, as we said, give a new
corrected version of results in [A2]. In section 4 we study hypermatrices of format (2, 2, r), r ≥2
and, by using the associated determinantal schemes, we are able to give algorithms to determine
when they are degenerate and when concise and to find their tensor rank and essential format.

whose ideal is generated by the maximal minors of a matrix B of linear forms (hence as a set it

exp codimS = min{v −u + 1, n}.

3.
Schl¨afli’s approach and a generalization
The approach to the study of the hyperdeterminant of A via the associated schemes is originally due
to Ludwig Schl¨afli (1814-1895, [Sc]). Schl¨afli found that the hyperdeterminant of a hypermatrix
A (of any dimension) divides the discriminant of the hyperdeterminant of one of its associated
hypermatrices if such hyperdeterminant is defined (see [GKZ], Ch.14, Thm 4.1). In our case, since A
is trilinear, those are the matrices of linear forms L, M, N; hence in our case such hyperdeterminant
is a determinant which exists only for square matrices, i.e. when we consider formats (p, q, q) or
(p, p, r). In particular we have (notations as above; see [O]):

While Schl¨afli method uses only one associated scheme, our aim is to use all the three associated
schemes to get informations about A, mainly about its degeneracy. For example it is known, when
A is of boundary format, that A is degenerate if and only if the scheme L (or, equivalently, the
scheme M) possesses a degenerate point (again, see [GKZ]); here we try to exploit the relation
between degeneracy of A and degenerate points of L, M and N.
The work in [A1] and [A2] is aimed exactly to this target, but unfortunately, as we noticed in the
introduction, the main result there, namely Theorem 10 of [A2] which affirms the equivalence of

three schemes L, M and N is wrong, as the following counterexample shows; hence a more subtle
relation must be discovered.

• If there exists (P, Q, T) ∈KA, then P, Q, T are degenerate points for L, M, N, respectively.
• If N possesses a degenerate but non bi-degenerate point, then A is degenerate.
• If A is not interior (i.e. r ≥p + q −1), then A is degenerate ⇐⇒L is degenerate ⇐⇒
M is degenerate.
• If r < p + q −1 and there is a degenerate non bi-degenerate point in L or in M then A is
degenerate.

Algorithm 3 Tensor rank, degeneracy and essential format for (2, 2, r) tensors, r ≥4
Input: r a natural number ≥4, A = (aijk), i, j ∈{1, 2}, k ∈{1, ..., r}, aijk ∈C.
Output: State if A is degenerate, determine Fess(A), and compute Trk A.
1: STEP 1) Compute det N:
- If r = 4 and N is a smooth quadric, then A is non-degenerate, concise and
Trk A = 4. STOP
- If r ≥5 and N is a cone on a smooth quadric in P3, then A is non-
degenerate, Trk A = 4 and A is not concise, with Fess = (2, 2, 4) STOP
- If N is not as in the previous cases, A is not concise; GO TO NEXT STEP.
2: STEP 2)
If N is a cone on a smooth conic in P2 (i.e. the vertex of the
cone is a linear space of dimension r −4 in Pr−1), then A is non-degenerate,
Fess(A) = (2, 2, 3) and Trk A = 3 STOP
Otherwise A is degenerate; GO TO NEXT STEP.
3: STEP 3) If N is a cone on a conic in P2 made by two distinct lines (i.e. N
is made of two distinct hyperplanes) compute the minors of L; then:
- If L is a simple point in P1, then Fess(A) = (2, 2, 3) and Trk A = 3. STOP
- If L is made by two simple points in P1, then Fess(A) = (2, 2, 2) and
Trk A = 2. STOP
- If N is not as above GO TO NEXT STEP.
4: STEP 4) If N is a double hyperplane, compute the minors of L; then:
- If the minors of L define a double point in P1, then Fess(A) = (2, 2, 2) and
Trk A = 3. STOP
- If the minors of L vanish identically, i.e. L = P1, then Fess = (2, 2, 1) and
Trk A = 2. STOP
- If det N vanishes identically, GO TO NEXT STEP.
5: STEP 5)
When det N vanishes identically, i.e. N = Pr−1, compute the
minors of M and of L; then:
- If one between L and M is a double point and the other is all of P1, then

- If L = M = P1, i.e. all the minors of the two matrices are identically 0,
