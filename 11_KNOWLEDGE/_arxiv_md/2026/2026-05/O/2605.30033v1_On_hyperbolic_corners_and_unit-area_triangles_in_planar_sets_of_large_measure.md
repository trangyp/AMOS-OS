ON HYPERBOLIC CORNERS AND UNIT-AREA TRIANGLES IN
PLANAR SETS OF LARGE MEASURE
ALEKSANDAR BULJ AND VJEKOSLAV KOVAČ
Abstract. For large R, we consider measurable sets A ⊆[0, R]2 that avoid triples of points
of the form (x, y), (x + t, y), (x, y + 1/t) with x, y ∈R and t > 0, i.e., the vertices of upward-
oriented, axis-aligned right triangles of area 1/2. We prove that the measures of such sets
satisfy |A| = Oc(R2/(log R)c) for any constant c < 1/4.
An ingredient in the proof is a
hyperbolic variant of the two-dimensional trilinear smoothing inequality by Christ, Durcik,
and Roos. The aforementioned upper bound is complemented with an example of a set of
measure Ω(R log R) avoiding the same point configuration.
Next, we study measurable sets A ⊆[0, R]2 that avoid triples of points spanning a triangle
of a given fixed area and establish a sharpening of the aforementioned upper bound to any
c < 1/2. This makes partial progress on a question by Erdős, who conjectured an upper
bound O(1), and improves over a quantitatively weak o(R2) result by Graham. The latter
proof additionally uses induction on scales to interchangeably control the density and the
Riesz energy of the set A.

Finite-field results were being developed simultaneously

polynomial tools, such as the PET induction and the degree lowering of Gowers norms. The

paper by Bourgain [7] on the nonlinear Roth theorem in R. It is also fair to remark that Lim
[37] studied (1.3) for certain rational functions γ1, γ2, but in the finite-field setting F2
q, where
rational functions are close in spirit to polynomials.

The basic outline of the proof of the upper bound in (1.2) only superficially resembles those
in recent papers in geometric Ramsey theory [12, 16, 28, 15, 30].
The beginning is quite
standard: we define an appropriate pattern-counting form (2.1) associated with a parameter
λ > 0. In our case λ determines an approximate scale of eccentricity of the configuration
(1.1) that we aim to detect. A certain novelty (employed also in [30]) is that this parameter
does not represent any usual notion of configuration “size.” In particular, it makes no sense
to let λ →0+ or λ →+∞. In classical scaling arguments, letting λ approach 0 simply means
that one also detects arbitrarily small instances of the desired configuration, which are in fact
trivially found by the Lebesgue density theorem. Despite the absence of this interpretation,
we can still meaningfully decompose the counting form into the structured, error, and uniform
parts (2.4), just as in, e.g., [12, 28, 15]. Another notable difference here is that all values of λ
contribute equally to the overall pattern count. Thus, to control the error part, we can simply
integrate in λ over [1/R, R] and avoid using Bourgain’s trick of pigeonholing in the scales [6, 7].
This additional symmetry is responsible for the better upper bound in (1.2) compared with
[11], with a power of log R, rather than a power of log log R, in the denominator. The final
ingredient in the proof of Theorem 1 is to handle the uniform part by reducing its bound to
a hyperbolic variant of the trilinear smoothing estimate by Christ, Durcik, and Roos [11]; see
Lemma 5.
A trivial lower bound for M(R) is M(R) ≳R, and it is realized by a single skew strip of
width ∼1 intersecting the square [0, R]2. The proof of the lower estimate in (1.2) uses an
example made up of several strips of varying width. It is depicted in Figure 1, and the details
are presented in Section 3.
1.2. Sets with no unit-area triangles. Observe that the configuration (1.1) consists of the
vertices of an upward-oriented, axis-aligned right triangle of area 1/2. We turn to the study
of sets that avoid the vertices of any (possibly rotated and scalene) triangle of a fixed area.
