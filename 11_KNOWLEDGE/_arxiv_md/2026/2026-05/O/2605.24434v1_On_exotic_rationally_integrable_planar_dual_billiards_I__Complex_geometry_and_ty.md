On exotic rationally integrable planar dual billiards
I. Complex geometry and type of dynamics
Alexey Glutsyuk∗†‡§
May 26, 2026
Abstract
A planar dual billiard is a planar curve γ equipped with a family
(σP )|P ∈γ of projective involutions of the projective lines LP tangent
to γ at P that fix P. A dual billiard is called rationally integrable,
if there exists a rational function R(x, y) of two variables (called first
integral) whose restriction to each tangent line LP is σP -invariant. In
the previous author’s paper it was shown that rationally integrable
dual billiards exist only on conics punctured at k points, 0 ≤k ≤
4.
Their classification given there includes standard examples with
quadratic integrals, defined by conical pencils, and an infinite family
of exotic examples with minimal degree of integrals being any even
number greater than two. In the present paper we study a question
stated by Dmitry Treschev on dynamics and conservation laws in the
exotic examples. We study the dynamics acting on the two-dimensional
phase space: the complex algebraic surface consisting of pairs (Q, P),
where Q ∈CP2 \ γ, P ∈γ and the line QP is tangent to γ at P. We
show that the phase space is fibered by invariant algebraic curves along
which Q lies in a level curve of the integral of the dual billiard. For
each example we find the type of a generic level curve of the integral
and of the corresponding fiber. We show that a generic level curve is
rational in most of examples and elliptic in two examples. We present
formulas for an invariant area form on the phase space and for invariant
holomorphic differentials on invariant curves. This yields a formula for
holomorphic differentials on the elliplic level curves of the integral.

Consider a strictly convex closed planar curve C. The billiard reflection acts
on the oriented lines intersecting C. Namely, let us reflect from C an ori-
ented line L at its last intersection point with C with respect to orientation,
according to the standard reflection law: the angle of incidence is equal to
the angle of reflection; the reflected line is directed inside the convex do-
main bounded by C. Recall that a caustic of the billiard on C is a curve α
such that each tangent line to α is reflected from the curve C to a tangent
line to α. The billiard on C is called Birkhoff integrable, if a topological
annulus adjacent to C from the convex side is foliated by closed caustics,
and C is a leaf of this foliation. It is well-known that each elliptic billiard is
integrable: ellipses confocal to the boundary are caustics, see [36, section 4].
The Birkhoff Conjecture states the converse: the only Birkhoff caustic-
integrable convex bounded planar billiards are ellipses. It was studied by
many mathematicians including H.Poritsky, M.Bialy, S.Bolotin, V.Kaloshin,
A.Sorrentino, A.Mironov, I.Koval, the author and others.
Let us mention some of well-known results on the Birkhoff Conjecture.
In 1950 H.Poritsky [34], and later E.Amiran [1] in 1988 proved it under the
additional assumption that the billiard in each closed caustic near the bound-
ary has the same closed caustics, as the initial billiard. In 1993 M.Bialy [5]
proved that if the phase cylinder of the billiard in a domain Ωis foliated
by non-contractible continuous closed curves which are invariant under the
billiard map, then the boundary ∂Ωis a circle. See also [44] for another
proof. V.Kaloshin and A.Sorrentino [29] proved that every integrable de-
formation of an ellipse is an ellipse, under the condition that the family of
caustics extends up to a caustic tangent to triangular orbits. A generaliza-
tion of the result of Kaloshin and Sorrentino, without extension condition
but for a generic ellipse, was recently proved by I.Koval [31]. M.Bialy and
A.E.Mironov [12] proved the Birkhoff Conjecture for centrally-symmetric
billiards having a family of closed caustics that extends up to a caustic
tangent to four-periodic orbits. D.V.Treschev [39, 40, 41] stated and inves-
tigated an analogue of the Birkhoff Conjecture for so-called locally integrable
local billiards with reflection from pairs of germs of analytic curves and their
higher-dimensional analogues.
S.V.Bolotin [13, 14] suggested the following polynomial version of the
Birkhoff Conjecture. Let the billiard flow in the domain bounded by a

S.Tabachnikov introduced projective billiards [35] generalizing billiards
on space forms of constant curvature. He suggested a generalized version of
the Birkhoff Conjecture for projective billiards and for their projective-dual
objects: the dual billiards [37], see the next definition.
Let γ ⊂RP2 be a planar curve. For every point P ∈γ by LP we denote
the projective tangent line to γ at P.
Definition 1.1 A planar dual billiard is a planar curve γ equipped with a
family (σP )|P∈γ of projective involutions of the tangent lines LP that fix P.
Conjecture 1.2 (S.Tabachnikov [37]). Let γ be a strictly convex planar
curve equipped with an integrable dual billiard structure. This means that
there exist a topological annulus adjacent to γ from the concave side that
is foliated by strictly convex closed curves-leaves such that for every P ∈γ
the involution σP : LP →LP permutes the intersection points of the line
LP with each leaf. See Fig. 1. Then the underlying curve γ and the leaves
are conics forming a pencil.
A basic example of a dual billiard is an outer billiard, where the invo-
lutions are central symmetries with respect to the tangency points. It was
shown in [21] that if a dual billiard admits a polynomial first integral, i.e.,
a polynomial whose restriction to each tangent line is invariant under the
corresponding involution, then the underlying curve is a conic.
A partial positive answer to Tabachnikov’s Conjecture 1.2 was proved
by the author in [19], under the condition that the foliation admits a non-
constant rational first integral. This condition implies in particular that the
dual billiard is rationally integrable, see the following definition.

It is well-known that in the case, when the dual billiard is of conical

1.3
Background material: critical level curves and critical
points of rational functions in general sense
Let R be a meromorphic function on a smooth complex surface S. For every
λ ∈C we set Γλ = {R = λ}. Let A be its indeterminacy point and λ ∈C.
(The case, when λ = ∞is treated analogously.) Fix a local chart (z, w)
centered at A. For a δ > 0 set Bδ(A) to be the ball of radius δ centered at
A in the chart. Recall, see [26, p. 282], that the value λ is called typical
for the point A, if for every δ > 0 small enough there exists an ε > 0 such
that there is a homeomorphism of the set ∪|µ−λ|<ε(Γµ ∩Bδ(A)) and the
product {|µ −λ| < ε} × (Γλ ∩Bδ(A)) that commutes with the projection
(z, w) 7→µ = R(z, w). In the opposite case the value λ is called atypical,
or critical for the point A, and the indeterminacy point A is said to be a
critical indeterminacy point for the value λ. This motivates the following
definition.
11


## Page 12


Definition 1.13 A value λ ∈C is called critical for the function R, if at
least one of the two following conditions holds:
1) The curve Γλ contains a critical point of the function R that is different
from its indeterminacy points. Then it is a critical point of the map R and
is called a (true) critical point, and λ is called a true critical value.
2) There exists a critical indeterminacy point for the value λ.
A true critical point is Morse, if it is isolated and the corresponding
Hessian form of the function R is non-degenerate. Or equivalently, if it is a
double point: a transversal self-intersection of the corresponding level curve.
Remark 1.14 It is well-known that in the case, when the ambient surface
S is compact, the numbers of indeterminacies and critical values is finite,
see [26] and references therein.
Consider a critical level curve Γλ. Its critical intersection graph is the
following graph. Its vertices are its irreducible components. We connect two
components by an edge, if they contain the same true critical point, and we
mark this edge by the triple: the critical point and the pair of components.
If a component v contains a true critical point A that is not contained in
another component, then we connect v to itself by an edge marked by A.
Proposition 1.15 Let R be a meromorphic function on a smooth compact
complex surface S. Let for an infinite set of values λ the level curve Γλ =
{R = λ} be rational. Then the following statements hold.
1) Each non-critical level curve is rational.

Background material: δ-invariant of plane curves
In the proof of some of main results we use the following background material
contained in [23]. Let S be a compact two-dimensional complex manifold.
Let Γ ⊂S be a compact holomorphic curve, i.e., a purely one-dimensional
complex analytic subset. Let us recall the definition of δ-invariant δ(Q) of
a point Q ∈Γ, see, e.g., [23, section I.3]. Fix a local biholomorphic chart
(z, w) on S centered at Q. The intersection of the curve Γ with a small
ball B centered at Q is a union of topological disks identified with the local
branches of the germ at Q of the curve Γ. Each two of them intersect each
other only at Q. Let f(z, w) be a holomorphic function on B defining Γ:
Γ ∩B = {f = 0} and f has no curves of multiple zeros. The intersection
Γ ∩∂B is a finite disjoint union of topological circles: the intersections of
∂B with the local branches of the germ of the curve Γ at Q. Their number,
denoted by k ∈N, is equal to the number of local branches. For every ε ̸= 0
small enough the level curve γε := {f = ε} ∩B is regular and intersects the
boundary ∂B at k disjoint topological circles close to the above ones. Let
us take a sphere with k disks deleted and paste it to γε.
Definition 3.1 [23, section I.3]. The genus of the compact surface thus
obtained is called the δ-invariant of the singular point Q of the curve Γ.
Remark 3.2 The δ-invariant of a singular point is known to be always
positive. The δ-invariants of a double point of transversal self-intersection
of regular branches and of a cusp are equal to one. Conversely, if the δ-
invariant is equal to one, then the singular point is either a double point,
or a cusp. The δ-invariant is superadditive, or in other terms, upper semi-
continuous, in the following sense: if a singular germ of analytic curve at a
point p is analytically deformed to a curve with several singularities close to
