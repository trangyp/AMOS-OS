Abstract
Artificial intelligence is fundamentally changing
how health content is encountered and acted upon
across both the information and healthcare ecosys-
tems. AI systems now generate claims, curate infor-
mation, interpret symptoms, synthesize evidence,
and guide decisions, with significant opportunities
and risks for the public. Potential benefits include
improvements in access, comprehension, and conti-
nuity of care. At the same time, AI can introduce
inaccurate or manipulative content that is difficult
to distinguish from reliable guidance, and encour-
age automated decisions that affect care with little
transparency or recourse. We introduce a four-stage
framework to examine how these opportunities and
risks unfold as the public moves through the infor-
mation environment and into formal healthcare.
A Shifting Landscape
One of the authors was recently diagnosed with gall-
stones during a visit to the emergency room and
told to “watch his diet.”
So he googled “diet for
gallstones” and got an AI-generated summary that
was easy to understand and cited an article from the
Mayo Clinic describing a diet that allegedly would
get rid of gallstones.
However, the AI summary
failed to mention that the Mayo Clinic article actu-
ally debunked the remedy as ineffective and risky.
This highlights both the benefits and the risks of AI-
generated health information: while the information
was easy to digest, it was misleading. Users might
be enticed by an authoritative tone and believe the
claim in a summary without checking the original
source.
This is just one example of how AI sys-
tems are reshaping the way people encounter and
engage with health information, which extends be-
yond search. At the same time, AI is transforming
how patients interact with care providers, navigate
treatment decisions, and manage recovery.
Even before the recent AI explosion, digital tech-
nologies had become deeply embedded in every
stage of the health information journey[1]: social
media surfaced health content without active in-
quiry[2;3] and search engines returned ranked links
that users can navigate and synthesize[4;5]. Clini-
cal settings were also transformed. Electronic health
records, patient portals, and decision-support sys-
tems digitized documentation and portions of clini-
cal workflow[6;7]. Despite legitimate concerns about
the role of platforms and algorithms in shaping
health information well before generative AI[8;9;10],
most health information encountered online was
still produced by humans and evaluating it largely
remained the responsibility of clinicians and self-
directed patients.
This arrangement is now changing due to re-
cent advances in generative AI, particularly large
language models (LLMs).
As illustrated in our
opening example, Google now provides AI sum-
maries above the search results in response to
health-related queries[11]. At the same time, con-
versational AI systems (chatbots) such as Google’s
AI-mode,1
OpenAI’s ChatGPT, and Anthropic’s
Claude are increasingly used for health-related
questions[12;13;14]. More recently, OpenAI and An-

Four Stages of AI Exposure
Let’s trace an individual’s health information jour-
ney as it moves from the broader information en-
vironment into the healthcare system. We focus on
four stages: idle exposure, information seeking, ac-
tive care, and care management (see Figure 1 for
an illustration). This framework highlights analyt-
ically distinct health care settings in which gener-
ative AI systems increasingly mediate engagement
with health information, providing a lens for assess-
ing benefits and risks.
Idle Exposure refers to periods when individuals
are not actively seeking health information, but are
nonetheless exposed to it through routine engage-
ment with digital technologies. At this stage, gener-
ative AI systems shape baseline awareness and un-
derstanding of health issues by altering the produc-
tion, delivery, and reception of health content in the
broader information ecosystem.
Information Seeking begins when individuals in-
tentionally seek out health-related information,
such as symptoms, conditions, or care options. Here,
AI-mediated search engines and conversational sys-
tems increasingly guide sensemaking by synthesiz-
ing information, interpreting relevance, and sug-
gesting possible next steps.
Active Care marks the transition into the health-
care ecosystem and encompasses information ex-
changes and decision-making during formal clinical
encounters with medical professionals. In this stage,
generative AI systems are embedded within clinical
workflows, influencing documentation, communica-
tion, and decision support, even as clinicians retain
responsibility for medical decisions.
Care Management captures ongoing health man-
agement outside of formal clinical encounters. Dur-
ing this stage, AI-mediated tools may support medi-
cation management, symptom monitoring, and post-
care coordination, shaping longer-term engagement
with care within and beyond clinical settings.
Stage I: Idle Exposure
Idle exposure to health-related content is poised to
change rapidly as AI is integrated into everyday in-
formation systems, with the potential for societal
opportunities as well as risks.
The most immediate opportunity lies in produc-
tion. Generative AI expands who can produce health
information, how quickly it can be created, and
how precisely it can be tailored.
Institutions, ad-
vocacy groups, commercial actors, and individual
creators can now generate fluent, multilingual, and
audience-specific health content at minimal cost.
For public health agencies and healthcare systems,
this capacity enables communication that moves be-
yond generic advisories toward culturally adapted
and linguistically tailored messaging.
Such capa-
bilities may improve reach among historically un-
derserved populations and help overcome language
barriers. For instance, the World Health Organiza-
tion deployed AI-powered chatbots to provide real-
time guidance and counter misinformation during
the COVID-19 pandemic[29]; more capable genera-
tive systems could extend such efforts considerably
in future emergencies.
Generative AI also increasingly mediates which
health content people encounter and how they inter-
pret it. For instance, the AI system Grok, integrated
directly into the Twitter/X platform, responds to
user queries about posts in public feeds. Those re-
sponses are visible to other users who never asked
a question and never sought out health informa-
tion[30]. A user scrolling past a post about vaccine
safety may come away with a reinforced or revised
belief, depending on how the AI system responded.
Such systems can communicate accurate guidance
and surface corrections to dangerous misinforma-
tion, but they can equally launder false claims with
an appearance of polished authority[31;32]. As AI-
generated and AI-curated health content prolifer-
ates into more digital spaces, it will play an in-
creasingly consequential role in determining which
health claims people accept as true.

At the same time, researchers and developers have
begun to explore whether LLM-based systems can
rerank social media feeds to surface more accurate
and pro-social content, given their capacity to in-
terpret meaning and context[33]. Such tools could
take the form of browser extensions that sit on top
of commercial platforms, or conversational inter-
faces built directly into those platforms, allowing
users to actively shape what they encounter[34]. For
health information, the implications cut both ways:
a user who instructs such a system to prioritize high-
quality sources could construct an information en-
vironment substantially more accurate than what
commercial algorithms provide, while one nudged
toward untrustworthy content could accelerate the
adoption of fringe health beliefs through confirma-
tion bias[35].
Even with the best intentions, personalized gen-
eration and curation at scale strain the review pro-
cesses that have traditionally safeguarded health
communication.
Public health agencies that once
vetted a small set of advisories before release can-
not feasibly check the millions of individualized
variants AI systems might produce for different au-
diences, languages, and contexts; similarly, online
platforms cannot meaningfully audit the moment-
to-moment responses that feed-integrated systems
like Grok generate for consumption by a global user
base. Therefore, scalable personalization can pro-
duce scalable error: subtle factual inaccuracies, out-
dated guidance, or miscalibrated recommendations
that reach millions without ever being flagged by
the editors, clinicians, or moderators traditionally
responsible for catching them.
At the same time, generative AI dramatically low-
ers the cost and effort required to produce and
spread content, reducing barriers for malicious ac-
tors. These may include commercial operators pro-
moting unproven remedies and state-sponsored ac-
tors with strategic interests in weakening popula-
tion health, both of whom can flood digital spaces
with targeted messaging at minimal cost. Some so-
cial media accounts already consist largely or en-
tirely of AI-generated text, images, or video, giv-
ing rise to “AI influencers” whose authoritative-
sounding but synthetic health messages reach mil-
lions of users[36;37;38;39].
Autonomous AI agent
“swarms” can go further still, generating and dis-
seminating content across platforms continuously
and without human supervision[40]. These AI tools
create a new attack vector for state actors to inflict
harm on target populations, for example, by per-
suading them to forego vaccination during a pan-
demic.
The consequences are not hypothetical: antivac-
cine messaging has been shown to increase vaccine
hesitancy in experimental settings[41], with such
content measurably suppressing vaccination uptake
and contributing to increased disease burden dur-
ing COVID-19[10;42;43]. Repeated encounters with
fluent, personalized health messages during routine
digital activity can shape background beliefs about
risk, prevention, and treatment even without active
search[44]. Experimental evidence shows that large

language models can durably influence attitudes in
controlled settings[45], including on medical topics
such as HPV vaccination[46]. Such effects may accu-

In summary, generative AI systems update ex-
isting means of health content production and in-
troduce new ones, leading to substantial quantita-
tive and qualitative shifts of the information envi-
ronment. In this evolving landscape—where health
authorities coexist with individual creators, AI in-
fluencers, and autonomous agents—users may find
that health information is more accessible yet in-
creasingly overwhelming. They may also find it even
more difficult to distinguish credible medical exper-
tise from advocacy and automated output. As a re-
sult, the public will need to recalibrate how they
evaluate the credibility of health guidance and, ulti-
mately, develop new forms of digital and health lit-
eracy to navigate this environment.
Stage II: Information Seeking
When people start to actively seek out health in-
formation, they will find that search engines now
present “AI Overviews” above ranked results, syn-
thesizing information from the web to directly an-
swer their queries[47].
Chatbot systems such as
ChatGPT, Claude, and Google’s AI mode go even
further, replacing ranked links altogether with con-
versational interfaces[48].
Rather than retrieving
documents for users to read and evaluate, these
systems interpret and summarize them, provid-
ing users with easy-to-digest, synthesized responses.
Conversational AI tools are thus replacing tradi-
tional search engines as the first point of contact
when people seek health information[49].
Unsurprisingly,
people use AI interfaces for
health information and advice—about one-third of
adults as of March 2026, according to a recent
poll[50].
Some people are even using chatbots to
self-diagnose[51]. As AI systems blur the boundary
between information summary and care-oriented
judgment, they potentially shape symptom interpre-
tation and downstream decision-making[48;49]. This
shift comes with opportunities and risks.
A huge opportunity arises from the capacity
of LLM-based systems to synthesize across a far
broader range of sources than a person would typ-
ically consult, and to do so almost instantly—
compressing what might otherwise be hours of read-
ing into a single, accessible response. Authoritative
health content online is frequently written for clin-
icians, not patients, and physicians tend to rely on
jargon that leaves patients confused or reluctant to
ask follow-up questions[52;53]. AI systems can trans-
late such complex medical information into plain
language, calibrated to the user’s level of under-
standing. For the many people who leave clinical
encounters without fully understanding their diag-
nosis or treatment options, AI systems offer some-
thing genuinely new: a patient interlocutor that can
explain, re-explain, and adjust its language on de-
mand[54].
The interactive nature of conversational AI intro-
duces benefits that go beyond information access.
For many users, the absence of friction and social
pressure makes it possible to ask health questions at
any time, anywhere, and with no waiting or embar-
rassment. And unlike a hurried appointment at the
doctor’s office or ER visit[55], a well-functioning AI
system can deliver responses for users to consume at
their preferred pace.
These strengths come with corresponding fragili-
ties. AI systems can produce fluent, confident state-
ments that are factually wrong[56;57]. Because co-
herence is a property of the language model rather
than a guarantee of accuracy, users may mistake
polished language for reliability and forgo verifi-
cation when it matters most[58;49]. In health con-
texts, such errors can distort symptom interpreta-
tion or perceived urgency, especially when a sin-
gle synthesized answer is presented without un-
certainty cues[46]. The same push toward synthe-
sis can also compress nuance—collapsing qualified,
context-dependent guidance into simplified claims
that are easier to read but more likely to be misap-
plied.
AI systems also reflect knowledge cutoffs based
on when they are trained[59]. While much health
guidance is relatively stable, recommendations do
change; a system unaware of updated clinical guid-
ance may present outdated information with the
same confidence it applies to settled knowledge.
Even when AI systems use retrieval or web search,
the capacity to draw on many sources simulta-
neously does not guarantee that reliable medical
evidence will be prioritized[31;60;61].
The result-
ing synthesis can obscure disagreement or evidence
gaps[48]. Worse, AI systems may not be able to rec-
ognize unreliable sources. They are vulnerable to in-
tentional manipulation through so-called “poison-
ing,” the creation of websites with false claims that
are then used to train AI models[62;63;64;65].
These risks are compounded by inconsistency.
Performance varies across providers, models, and
even prompt phrasing,
so the quality of AI-
mediated health guidance is neither uniform nor
predictable[66]. This variability takes on an equity

dimension: cross-linguistic evaluations show that
non-English responses are substantially less accu-
rate and less consistent than their English counter-
parts[67]. The result is a new form of digital divide
in which the populations who stand to benefit most
from accessible health guidance (e.g., non-English
speakers, those with lower health literacy, and com-
munities with fewer healthcare resources) are pre-
cisely those most exposed to its least reliable out-
puts. When users act on such inaccurate guidance,
the downstream consequences extend well beyond
the search interaction itself, including delayed care,
inappropriate reassurance or escalation, and false
beliefs that persist into clinical encounters[47].
In sum, a major shift in active health informa-
tion seeking is driven by the integration of AI sys-
tems. While these systems streamline access and in-
terpretation, they risk oversimplifying complex is-
sues and obscuring errors, inconsistencies, and sys-
tem fragilities behind an appearance of interpretive
authority. Because active health information seeking
often informs downstream care decisions, this shift
from health information retrieval to interpretation
and prioritization makes evaluating the quality and
effects of AI systems increasingly critical.
Stage III: Active Care
Generative AI is rapidly moving from the periph-
ery of clinical care to its center.
In the United
States, healthcare delivery is marked by persis-
tent inefficiencies, including an understaffed work-
force that remains heavily burdened despite rising
levels of spending[68;69].
Generative AI tools are
well-positioned to target precisely these inefficien-
cies: they require minimal training to deploy, in-
tegrate readily into the mature information tech-
nology environments most healthcare organizations
already maintain, and can automate or augment a
wide range of routine tasks[17;70].
Economic pro-
jections suggest that deploying modern AI systems
could reduce healthcare spending by approximately
5–10%, largely by improving operations, adminis-
trative functions, and reimbursement processes—an
estimated $200–360 billion dollars per year[71;72].
On the user side, three in ten adults worldwide
now use a mobile health app, over 1,200 AI tools
have received FDA clearance[6], and 90% of US
health systems deploy some form of AI in radiol-
ogy[73]. This adoption is accelerating fast enough
that the JAMA Summit Report on Artificial Intelli-
gence now posits that “every patient-clinician con-
versation in the US may soon be accompanied by a
live interactive AI agent”[6] (p. 1651).
Understanding what the integration of generative
AI into clinical settings means for patients requires
distinguishing between implementations where AI
operates at low risk, such as automating administra-
tive tasks at the edges of care, and those where it
operates at high risk, mediating or shaping clinical
judgment itself.
Low-risk implementations have shown genuine
promise[74]. By 2025, 67% of a thousand surveyed
physicians reported using AI daily in their practice,
with 89% reporting at least weekly use, and 84% re-
porting that AI makes them better at their jobs[75].
Much of this adoption has focused on reducing ad-
ministrative and documentation burden.
For ex-
ample, ambient AI scribes leverage speech recog-
nition and language models to transcribe patient-
provider conversations and generate structured clin-
ical notes, producing meaningful reductions in
physician workload and burnout[21;22;23;76]. AI tools
have also helped overcome language barriers and
improve patient satisfaction[77].
Even within this
lower-risk tier, however, transparency obligations
apply: clinicians have an ethical duty to inform pa-
tients when AI is involved in their care[78].
AI applications are also beginning to be deployed
in situations that involve higher potential health
risks, depending on the specific context.
An ex-
ample is answering medical questions that would
traditionally be addressed by a doctor. LLMs can
draft responses to patient messages that compare fa-
vorably to those written by providers[27]. Patients
also rate chatbot responses higher in empathy than
those of real doctors[79;80;81;82], although this advan-
tage may partly reflect the greater length of AI re-
sponses and the use of clinicians, rather than pa-
tients, as raters[83]. Yet, expert assessments of popu-
lar LLM-based chatbot responses to health questions
have documented recurring issues, including incom-
plete or inaccurate information, weak or inadequate
citations, overlooked hereditary risks, and failure to
recommend consultation with a clinician[84;85].
In mental health settings,
a study of LLM-
supported therapeutic techniques to overcome neg-
ative thinking has positively impacted 67% of par-
ticipants[24].
There is also evidence that AI chat-
bots can be effective in treating depression and anx-
iety, a task traditionally reserved for psychothera-
pists[86;87].
However, long-term effectiveness has
not been demonstrated[88]. These applications also
raise significant ethical and legal concerns, for ex-
ample, whether AI chatbots should be allowed to re-
place licensed health practitioners[89;90].

interprets,
or surfaces
health recommendations for clinical decisions, a

LLM augmentation can also improve perceived ease-

Incorporating retrieval-augmented generation into
LLMs can significantly improve personalized diag-

assessments[97].
Yet even capable AI systems do
not straightforwardly improve human performance
in identifying medical conditions and choosing a
course of action. In controlled studies, LLMs alone
achieved 94.9% accuracy, while users assisted by
those same models achieved only 34.5%, no bet-

And when fam-
ily or friends minimized symptoms, AI triage rec-
ommendations shifted significantly toward less ur-
gent care[98], reflecting the sycophantic tendency of
LLMs to align with a user’s framing rather than chal-
lenge it[99].
Several challenges cut across applications with
varying risk levels.
Hallucinations remain partic-
ularly consequential in clinical settings[100], and
gauging real-world efficacy is harder than bench-
marks suggest[101;102;103;94]:
only 5% of medical
LLMs have been evaluated on actual electronic
health record data, and automated performance
metrics show weak or negative correlation with hu-
man clinical judgment. Moreover, concerns about
algorithmic bias, automation bias, and limited gen-
eralization persist[6].
Structural constraints com-
pound these problems: 81% of physicians report
dissatisfaction with how their organizations handle
AI adoption, and 71% report little influence over
which tools are selected[75].
The boundary between low- and high-risk is nei-
ther clear nor stable.
The same model that auto-
generates a clinical note can, depending on deploy-
ment context, interpret symptoms or shape triage
decisions. Understanding the risks inherent in any
given medical AI application is, therefore, a pressing
challenge in clinical care. Governance structures are
also needed to prevent low-risk AI tools from quietly
drifting into high-risk roles.
Stage IV: Care Management
Stage III examined AI’s role within formal clinical
encounters, where the presence of a clinician pro-
vides a clear locus of accountability for AI-mediated
decisions.
Stage IV concerns health care manage-
ment activities that do not involve direct interac-
tions with clinicians. Accountability is diffused in
this stage. Responsibility shifts to software devel-
opers and insurers whose reasoning is often opaque
to the patients affected by it. AI systems are now
rapidly expanding into this more ambiguous envi-
ronment.
AI can operate across a wide range of health care
management tasks that differ in their clinical stakes
and degree of autonomy.
At the low end, AI can
manage the administrative infrastructure of care
management: scheduling, billing inquiries, and ap-
pointment reminders that carry limited clinical con-
sequence[77]. At higher levels of risks, AI can me-
diate with increasing autonomy how patients un-
derstand their own recovery, providing conversa-
tional guidance on medication side effects, drug in-
teractions, and post-care[28;12]. At the highest lev-
els, AI systems can substitute for decisions once re-
served for clinicians and insurers: adjusting treat-
ment protocols, shaping medication adherence, and
determining coverage, often with limited human re-

fall most heavily on individuals least equipped to
detect or contest them.
Conclusion
Across both the information environment and
healthcare system, AI is introducing something cat-
egorically new: synthesizing health information and
shaping health decisions.
This carries both great
promise and substantial risk, with implications that
differ depending on where the public encounters it.
In the information ecosystem, AI has the potential
to democratize access to health information, reach-
ing people who lack access to professionals or face
language and literacy barriers. But synthetic con-
tent and AI influencers blur the line between exper-
tise and imitation, and AI-mediated search returns
confident syntheses that may compress nuance, ob-
scure uncertainty, or contain misinform. These ef-
fects are distributed across many systems, entangled
with individual behavior, and accumulate quietly
across large populations. The potential harms of AI
to public health are therefore difficult to attribute
and regulate.
In the healthcare ecosystem, AI has reduced doc-
umentation burden, supported clinical communica-
tion, and holds promise for extending care to un-
derserved patients.
But as its reach extends into
decisions about treatment, coverage, and care man-
agement, pressure to reduce clinician oversight will
grow.
The expanding role of AI creates an ethical ten-
sion between access and harm. Those who stand to
benefit most from AI-mediated health information
are also most likely to suffer from its failures: people
facing language barriers, limited health literacy, or
constrained access to care will rely most heavily on
these systems and therefore be most exposed to their
risks. Worse, deploying imperfect systems to under-
served communities may blunt incentives to expand
human-delivered care, widening the very disparities
AI is meant to close.
A further concern cuts across every stage: data
privacy. Health-related queries and interactions sur-
face highly sensitive information about symptoms,
diagnoses, and personal circumstances to platforms
that largely operate outside the regulatory regimes
7


## Page 8


governing clinical care. Disclosed data may be re-
tained, used for model training, sold to third par-
ties, or leaked in breaches, leaving users vulnerable
to insurance discrimination, blackmail, and identity
theft.
Addressing these challenges requires more than
technical improvements to individual systems[107].
We need longitudinal research designs capable of
capturing cross-stage effects, evaluation frameworks
that foreground differential impacts across popu-
lations, and governance structures that assign ac-
countability at each stage of the patient journey
rather than only within clinical walls.
Concrete
strategies have been proposed, but their implemen-
tation depends on incentive structures that cur-
rently favor commercial over public health goals[6].
Regulatory frameworks face their own challenges.
For instance, the FDA is actively grappling with how
to oversee generative AI tools whose outputs may
change from one moment to the next[108;109;110]. As
AI becomes further embedded in how the public en-
counters and acts on health information, the ade-
quacy of governance structures will matter as much
as the performance of the systems they oversee.
