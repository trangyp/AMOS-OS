EXTENSIONS, AND HIERARCHICAL HYPERBOLICITY
ERVIN HADˇZIOSMANOVI´C AND GIORGIO MANGIONI
Abstract. We prove that the outer automorphism group of a one-ended hyperbolic group is virtually
a hierarchically hyperbolic group (HHG), under mild orientability conditions on the associated JSJ
decomposition.
This is done by proving that a finite-index subgroup is a central extension of a
product of orbifold mapping class groups, and the extension has bounded Euler class.
Our theorem is sharp: we exhibit a surface amalgam whose fundamental group has full outer
automorphism group which is not a HHG. To prove this, the main technical tool is the fact that a
top-dimensional Abelian subgroup of a HHG is a standard flat.

Introduction
Outer automorphism groups of hyperbolic groups are of utmost importance in combinatorial and
geometric group theory. One of the many reasons is that this class includes both Out pFnq and map-
ping class groups of closed surfaces, which are ubiquitous in the study of low-dimensional topology.
Furthermore, a key step in the solution of the isomorphism problem for hyperbolic groups (see, among
others, [RS94, Sel97, DG11]) was a description of the outer automorphism group of a one-ended hyper-
bolic group G in terms of certain virtually Abelian extensions of mapping class groups. We improve
this description by showing that Out pGq is virtually a bounded central extension:
Theorem A (See Theorem 2.5). Let G be a one-ended hyperbolic group. Then Out pGq is virtually a
direct product Zq ˆ M, where q ě 0 and M fits inside a bounded central extension

If we further assume that G is torsion-free, then all orbifolds are genuine surfaces, and all finite
extensions are trivial. Hence in this case Theorem A has a simpler form:
Corollary B. Let G be a one-ended, torsion-free hyperbolic group. Then Out pGq is virtually a bounded
central extension of a direct product of finitely many mapping class groups of hyperbolic surfaces.
While the existence of the central extension from Theorem A follows from a careful inspection of
several, often implicit, results in the literature (see, among others, [Sel97, Bow98, Lev05, DG11]),
the fact that the extension is bounded is new. To the eye of a geometric group theorist, one of the
main consequences of boundedness is that the extension is quasi-isometrically trivial, meaning that it
is quasi-isometric to the direct product of the kernel and the quotient [Ger92]. In the case where all
orbifolds from Theorem A are orientable, we can dramatically improve our understanding of the coarse
geometry of Out pGq:
Theorem C (See Theorem 2.10). Let G be a one-ended hyperbolic group. Suppose that all quadratically
hanging subgroups appearing in the JSJ decomposition of G are orientable. Then Out pGq is virtually
a hierarchically hyperbolic group.
Hierarchically hyperbolic groups (HHGs), as first introduced in [BHS17b], provide a common framework
for mapping class group of surfaces, most cubulated groups, most 3-manifold groups, and more. As
such, techniques from low-dimensional topology and the world of CAT(0) cube complexes can be
exploited to prove a plethora of properties of (virtual) HHGs; a very non-exhaustive list includes
results on their asymptotic dimension [BHS17a], quasiflats [BHS21], and on being quasi-isometric to
CAT(0) cube complexes [Pet24].
We emphasise that the JSJ decomposition from Theorem A can be trivial: this happens precisely
when G is itself a finite extension of a cocompact Fuchsian group (that is, the fundamental group
of a closed, hyperbolic 2-orbifold). In this case, under the same orientability assumption, Out pGq is
genuinely hierarchically hyperbolic, without need to pass to a finite-index subgroup:
Theorem D (See Theorem 2.9). Let G be a finite extension of an orientable, cocompact Fuchsian
group. Then Out pGq is a hierarchically hyperbolic group.
As the full Out pGq is quasi-isometric to its finite-index HHG subgroup, it has the structure of a
hierarchically hyperbolic space, the non-equivariant analogue of a hierarchically hyperbolic group.
Unfortunately, being a hierarchically hyperbolic group might not pass to finite-index overgroups: in
other words, there might not be any equivariant structure (see [PS23, Corollary 4.5] for an explicit
counterexample). This is indeed the case in our setting, even under the assumption that G has no
torsion. For a concrete counterexample, let Σ “ S1

Outline of sections and arguments.
JSJ decompositions. The main tool for the study of outer automorphism groups of hyperbolic groups,
which also played a central role in the solution of the isomorphism problem for hyperbolic groups,
is Bowditch’s JSJ decomposition over virtually cyclic subgroups [Bow98]. This is a graph-of-groups
decomposition of a one-ended hyperbolic group with virtually cyclic edge groups, and whose vertex
groups are either:
‚ maximal virtually cyclic subgroups;
‚ “rigid” quasiconvex subgroups, which cannot be split further relative to the edge groups;
‚ or quadratically hanging subgroups, which are (finite extensions of) fundamental groups of
hyperbolic orbifolds.

Hierarchical hyperbolicity. Very roughly, a hierarchically hyperbolic group is a finitely generated group
admitting suitable “coordinate projections” to a collection of Gromov-hyperbolic spaces. The moti-
vating example is the mapping class group of a finite-type, orientable surface, which projects to the
curve graphs of its subsurfaces via subsurface projection. Combining this with the fact that bounded
central extensions of HHGs are themselves HHGs [FFMS26, Theorem 1.3], in Section 2.2 we deduce
Theorem C from Theorem A. Theorem D, which considers the cocompact Fuchsian case, is proven
along the same lines; the only difference is that, by carefully describing Out pGq as a finite extension
of a finite-index subgroup of a mapping class group, we do not need to pass to a finite-index subgroup
to get hierarchical hyperbolicity. We strived to make Section 2.2 as clear as possible for the reader
who is not an expert on HHG: the only prerequisites we need are given as self-contained blackboxes,
while a comprehensive background on HHGs is postponed to Section 3.1.
Standard periodic quasiflats in hierarchically hyperbolic groups. Section 3 starts with an overview on
HHGs. The main technical novelty of Section 3.1 is the following, which we believe to be of independent
interest to anyone working with hierarchical hyperbolicity:
Theorem F (see Section 2.2). Let G be a HHG of rank ν, and let F ď G be a subgroup isomorphic
to Zν. Then F is a standard flat, and in particular it is hierarchically quasiconvex.
Very roughly, the rank of G is the maximal ν for which G contains quasi-isometrically embedded copies
of Rν, which we call maximal quasiflats for short. Moreover, a standard flat is a maximal quasiflat
obtained by considering a maximal collection of unbounded coordinate spaces which are orthogonal
(roughly meaning that their coordinates can be chosen independently) and taking the product of one
quasiline in each coordinate space. Standard flats are canonical examples of hierarchically quasiconvex
subspaces, the natural notion of quasiconvexity in the hierarchical setting.
To prove Theorem F, we first notice that, since Abelian subgroups of HHGs are undistorted [HHP23,
Corollary E], the Quasiflat Theorem from [BHS21] covers F by a finite union of standard orthants (the
same as standard flats, but given by selecting rays instead of lines). Then we exploit the F-action by
translation to ensure that the orthants can be patched together to get a standard flat.
Remark G. In forthcoming work, Azuelos and Hagen [AH] independently prove a “flat torus theorem”
for virtually Abelian subgroups of HHGs, showing they preserve quasiflats satisfying a weak version of
hierarchical quasiconvexity. As a consequence, they recover part of Theorem F, namely that an Abelian
subgroup of maximal rank is genuinely hierarchically quasiconvex. We stress that our methods and
theirs are completely different: our proof relies on the Quasiflat Theorem and is specific to top-rank
Abelian subgroups, but has the advantage to describe them as standard flats; on the other hand,
their work pushes further techniques of cubical approximations of convex hulls in HHSs, and applies
to Abelian subgroups of any rank.
The counterexample. Finally, in Section 3.2 we construct the counterexample. Very roughly, assuming
that Out pπ1pKqq is a HHG, we use Theorem F to deduce that the p3, 3, 3q triangle group Z2 ¸ S3 ď
Out pπ1pKqq, generated by Dehn twists around the boundaries of the Σi’s and the homeomorphisms
permuting the three tori, is hierarchically quasiconvex. In analogy with quasiconvex subgroups in other
non-positively curved groups, hierarchically quasiconvex subgroups inherit a “HHG-like” structure
from the ambient group; we use this to deduce a contradiction, mimicking an argument of Petyt-
Spriano, who proved that the p3, 3, 3q triangle group carries no HHG structure (see [PS23, Corollary
4.5]).

As pointed out in Lemma 1.23, the mapping class group of an orbifold has finite index in the mapping
class group of the surface obtained by removing the singular locus; this surface might have some

group passes to finite-index subgroups, it would be enough to answer the following:
Question I. Is the mapping class group of a non-orientable surface (possibly with punctures and
boundaries) a hierarchically hyperbolic group?
To the best of our knowledge, this is unknown even for closed non-orientable surfaces.
A positive answer to Question I would follow from a more general result about centralisers in HHGs.
Indeed, let S be a non-orientable surface, let pS be its orientable double cover, and let i: pS Ñ pS be
the order-two deck transformation. By results of [BC72, Sze10, LGGM18], except in a few sporadic
cases, MCG˘ pSq embeds in MCG˘ppSq as the subgroup of orientation-preserving mapping classes that
commute with i. So we ask:
Question J. Let G be a HHG, and let F ď G be a finite subgroup. Is the centraliser of F a HHG?
The above is motivated by the principle that centralisers in non-positively curved groups should inherit
the geometric properties of the ambient group: for example, centralisers in hyperbolic groups are
quasiconvex, hence hyperbolic (see e.g. [BH99, Proposition III.Γ.3.9]), and centralisers in CAT(0)
groups are themselves CAT(0) [Rua01]. The analogue of Question J for centralisers of virtually Abelian
subgroup will be considered in forthcoming work of Azuelos and Hagen [AH].

1.2. Orbifold mapping class groups. We recall here definition and properties of the mapping class
group of a hyperbolic 2-orbifold, gathering notation and results from across the literature for improved
clarity. We refer to [MH75, Bow98, Fuj02, DG11] for the original treatments, and to [Mar25] for a
more recent, comprehensive introduction.
In what follows, let H2 be the hyperbolic plane, and let BH2 its boundary at infinity, which is
homeomorphic to a circle S1.
Remark 1.9 (Isometries of H2). An isometry g P IsompH2q is of one of 5 types. If g is orientation-

‚ elliptic if it fixes a point in H2;
‚ parabolic if it fixes exactly one point of the boundary BH2;

‚ loxodromic if it fixes exactly two ideal points of BH2 and acts as a translation along the unique
geodesic that connects them, called the axis of g.
If instead g reverses orientation, then it is either:
‚ a reflection along a geodesic;
‚ a glide-reflection along a geodesic, that is, the composition of a reflection along a geodesic and

The subgroup of IsompH2q fixing a point is isomorphic to the orthogonal group Op2q via the action on
the boundary BH2, seen as a circle with the standard round metric.
Definition 1.10 (Bounded Fuchsian group). A bounded Fuchsian group is a non-virtually-cyclic,
finitely generated, discrete subgroup G of IsompH2q without parabolics. Such a group acts properly
and cocompactly on the convex hull DG of the limit points ΛpGq Ď BH2 for the action. If moreover
DG “ H2 then G is called a cocompact Fuchsian group. A bounded Fuchsian group is orientable if it
lies in the index-two subgroup Isom`pH2q of orientation-preserving isometries.
Remark 1.11. The term “bounded Fuchsian group” has appeared in the literature with different
meanings. For example, in [DG11], Dahmani and Guirardel do not require the action on H2 to be
faithful, but only to have finite kernel; their bounded Fuchsian groups are therefore finite extensions
of groups satisfying Definition 1.10.
Their notion is taken from Bowditch’s paper [Bow98], which
further makes a distinction between bounded Fuchsian groups and cocompact ones. Classically (see
e.g. [Kat92]) Fuchsian groups are commonly understood as subgroups of Isom`pH2q, so we prefer to
exclude non-faithful actions. As a consequence, with our notation Fuchsian groups have no non-trivial
finite normal subgroups: see Lemma 1.12. On the other hand, we do include orientation-reversing
isometries: our cocompact Fuchsian groups are also known as non-Euclidean crystallographic groups
[Mac67]. Finally, in contrast with Bowditch’s notation, we consider cocompact Fuchsian group as
special examples of bounded ones, to simplify the notation later.
The following is well-known, but we provide a proof for completeness.
Lemma 1.12. Let G be a bounded Fuchsian group. Then G is Gromov-hyperbolic. Moreover, it has
no non-trivial finite normal subgroups, and in particular it has trivial centre.
Proof. Gromov-hyperbolicity follows from the fact that G acts properly and cocompactly on DG, which
is a convex subspace of H2 and is therefore hyperbolic. Therefore, since G is not virtually cyclic, it
admits a maximal finite normal subgroup K, which is the kernel of the action of G on ΛpGq “ BDG
(see e.g. [BJ25, Corollary 1.2], which is far more general). Now DG has more than two points at
infinity, as otherwise G would be virtually cyclic; hence every element k P K is a finite-order isometry
of H2 fixing at least three points at infinity, and is therefore trivial (this is because any finite-order
isometry fixes a point in H2, and an element of Op2q fixing three points on the circle is trivial). Hence
G has no non-trivial finite normal subgroups; in particular, G has trivial centre, as the latter is a finite
normal subgroup (see e.g. the far more general [DGO17, Theorem 6.14.(a)]).
□
