Observation of sine-Gordon-like solitons in a spinor Bose-Einstein condensate
Yannick Deller,1, ∗Alexander Schmutz,1 Raphael Schäfer,1 Alexander Flamm,1 Florian Schmitt,1
Ido Siovitz,1 Thomas Gasenzer,1 Panayotis G. Kevrekidis,2, 3 Helmut Strobel,1 and Markus K. Oberthaler1
1Kirchhoff-Institut für Physik, Universität Heidelberg, Im Neuenheimer Feld 227, 69120 Heidelberg, Germany

3Theoretical Sciences Visiting Program, Okinawa Institute of Science and Technology Graduate University, Onna, 904-0495, Japan
(Dated: May 13, 2026)
We experimentally generate sine-Gordon-like solitons in a spin-1 spinor Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC)
utilizing a robust and reproducible local phase-imprinting scheme. We find that the soliton velocity can be
tuned by the effective quadratic Zeeman shift. This enables the investigation of controlled soliton interactions,
in which we observe the characteristic elastic collision behavior of the integrable sine-Gordon model. The
spatial displacement —the so-called phase shift— between incoming and outgoing solitons, the signature of
their pairwise interaction, is found to be in quantitative agreement with numerical spin-1 simulations within
the error bars. These results establish spinor BECs as a highly controllable experimental platform for studying
aspects of the dynamics of sine-Gordon-like models.
Introduction—Solitons are shape-retaining wave packets
that can exist in nonlinear media when the dispersion is com-
pensated by the nonlinear interaction. They are found in a
variety of physical systems in nature, e.g., in water, clouds,
and even biological systems [1]. The sine-Gordon (sG) model
emerges as a prototypical integrable nonlinear model of soli-
ton dynamics that captures the dynamical behavior of a mul-
titude of physical systems as diverse as Josephson-junction
arrays, spin waves in magnetic materials, and lattice dislo-
cations in crystals [2]. In addition to its physical relevance,
even for systems as simple as coupled torsion pendula [3],
and mathematical importance, as on surfaces of constant neg-
ative curvature [4], the sG model is one of the cornerstones
of the theory of nonlinear waves and solitons. Examples in-
clude kinks and breathers, their elastic wave interactions, and
numerous perturbed variants of such solutions [5].
The sG model has also recently been shown to describe var-
ious phenomena in ultracold atomic systems, e.g., in coupled
condensates [6, 7], in particular solitons in these systems [8].
More concretely, the sG model emerges as the low-energy ef-
fective field theory of a weakly interacting ferromagnetic spin-
1 Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) [9] in a specific parameter
regime. Multi-component BECs have been developed towards
a pristine platform for the creation of nonlinear wave patterns,
including vector solitons [10, 11], magnetic solitons [12–14],
as well as Townes solitons [15], among others [16].
In this letter, we report on the experimental generation of
sine-Gordon-like solitons in a spinor BEC by employing a
robust and reproducible phase-imprinting scheme. In contrast
to the aforementioned experimental works, these solitons
correspond to magnetic domain walls, as can be seen in Fig. 1,
similar to the theoretical proposals introduced in [17–21].

Kink-anti-kink (K-K) collisions—The experimental control
over the velocity enables us to study K-K collisions. For this,
we imprint a rectangular phase profile with Gaussian edges at
the center of the condensate, which generates a K-K-pair at
a distance of ∼35 µm. As can be seen in Fig. 4, we observe
a gradual 2π change in the spinor phase during the collision.
This is in accordance with, but not limited to, the analytical
prediction for the sine-Gordon model [2], as such a gradual
phase change can be observed even in linear wave equations.
We simultaneously measure the transversal spin profile F⊥
and the spinor phase φS, see App. E for details.
Elastically colliding solitons experience a so-called phase
shift due to interactions during the collision, that is, a posi-
tional shift that the soliton experiences due to the interaction
during the collision, so that its trajectory deviates from the
one expected for a hard core collision. In order to extract this
phase shift, we determine the relative distance of the solitons
from a fit to the data of the transverse spin length, see Fig. 5,
where we choose q/h ∼0.3 Hz. We fit a linear function to
the relative distances both before and after the collision re-
gion, which we define as the time interval in which the soliton
profiles are separated by less than three times their width, and
where their individual positions are not well defined any more.

Discussion and outlook—We have deterministically gener-
ated sine-Gordon-like solitons in a 1D spin-1 Bose-Einstein
condensate and studied their dynamics, finding that the soliton
velocity can be controlled by adjusting the effective quadratic
Zeeman shift q. Furthermore, we have generated soliton pairs
and investigated their behavior upon collision. We were able
to extract a spatial phase shift, demonstrating that the experi-
ment’s stability and control are sufficient for studying the in-
teraction between the solitons. The experimentally extracted
value of the phase shift is in accordance with our numerical
spin-1 simulations.
In turn, this study paves the way for numerous extensions of
imminent interest, given in particular the versatility of the ex-
perimental system. One natural question associated with our
control of the wave speed concerns its potential limitations
analogous to the relativistic limit. The sG equation contains
both second-order spatial and time derivatives, reflecting the
non-dispersive sound excitations of the underlying non-linear
Schrödinger equation, with a linear relation ω ∼csk between
energy and momentum (for large values of k). It is natural to
inquire about the extension of our q-v-diagram near the limit
v →cs, and the associated relevance of the Lorentz factor.
Moreover, sG is unique among classical (homogeneous)
continuum field theories [29] since it has breather solutions.
Generating and examining breathers and their interac-
tions [30] is of particular interest in its own right.
Going
beyond the sG realm, it turns out that the spin system
provides a path towards non-integrable generalizations of the
sG description. Indeed, the spin-1 GP model is related to the
non-integrable double sG model [9] that has been the subject
of intense theoretical scrutiny in the nonlinear-waves com-
munity [31]. This is due to its numerous intriguing features
including kink internal modes and their intrinsic breathing,

In this Appendix, we provide further details on the experimental system, numerical simulations of the spin-1 system and
analytical calculations in both spin-1 and sG theory to support the results in the main text.
Appendix A: Experimental system
We prepare a quasi-one-dimensional BEC of ∼105 87Rb atoms in a box-like nearly homogeneous trapping potential. The
trap is realized by using a red-detuned laser beam with a wavelength of 1030 nm, resulting in a quasi-1D harmonic trap (wr =
2π × 170 Hz and wl = 2π × 1.6 Hz). The box walls are realized by two blue-detuned steerable laser beams with a wavelength
of 760 nm, see [11] for details. All experiments are performed in an actively stabilized homogeneous magnetic offset field of
B = 0.894 G, which gives rise to the quadratic Zeeman shift of q(B)/h ∼58 Hz. By applying off-resonant microwave dressing,
we couple, in leading order, the |F = 1, mF = 0⟩and |F = 2, mF = 0⟩levels. This allows us to effectively tune q into a regime
on the order of the spin-spin interaction, q ∼n|c1|. At the start of every experimental sequence reported here, we prepare the
BEC close to the easy-plane ground state of q = 0, which corresponds to an elongated transversal spin with |F⊥| ≈1. For this,
all atoms are first transferred to |F = 1, mF = 0⟩, followed by a π/2-radio frequency (RF) pulse which rotates the state around
the Fx-axis. In order to align the spin on the Fx-axis, we adjust the global spinor phase to φS = (0 mod 2π), which is defined
as φS = (φ1 + φ−1)/2 −φ0. This is implemented by two subsequent MW π-pulses between |F = 1, mF = 0⟩and |F = 2, mF = 0⟩
with a relative phase difference between the first and second pulse.
Appendix B: Experimental generation of solitons
In order to prepare a soliton in the spinor phase, we have to locally manipulate the phase difference between the mF = 0 and
mF = ±1 components. This is realized via sequential illumination of the BEC with a steerable focused laser beam at the tune-out
wavelength of 790.032 nm. By choosing this wavelength, the laser induces an additional local magnetic field ∆B(x) via the vector
Stark shift, without causing density excitations [33]. The additional magnetic field locally manipulates the phase evolution of
the mF = ±1 components, which would lead to a locally advancing Larmor phase φL = φ1 −φ−1. In order to utilize this concept
for local spinor phase rotations, we make use of the relation gF=1 ≈−gF=2, which is a property of the rubidium electronic ground
state (but also holds for other alkali species). Following the sequence shown in Fig. 6 (a), all atoms in the |F = 1, mF = −1⟩
component are transferred globally to |F = 2, mF = −1⟩by a microwave pulse and held there for a fixed duration, during which
the BEC is locally illuminated. Consequently, the condensate phases of |F = 2, mF = −1⟩and |F = 1, mF = 1⟩are advanced in
the same direction. As φ0 is unaltered, this effectively advances the spinor phase in a magnetic bias field B(x). During the hold
time τ, the spinor phase φS is locally advanced by ∆φS ∼∆B(x)τ. We use a setup of two crossed acousto-optic deflectors (AODs)
to steer the imprint laser beam over the atom cloud. In order to avoid interference, we turn off the driving signal of the AODs
between individual pulses, such that only one radio-frequency tone per pulse is present in each AOD crystal. In this way we
apply grids of up to 12 horizontal and 3 vertical sequential laser pulses. Consequently, the spatial profile of the spinor phase is
directly proportional to the sequentially applied intensity profile (Fig. 6 (b)), while the global offset value of the spinor phase is
adjusted by the hold time. After the local phase imprint we transfer all atoms from |F = 2, mF = −1⟩back to |F = 1, mF = −1⟩.
This method allows us to imprint any functional form in the spinor phase which can be approximated by a sum of Gaussians.

Appendix C: Density dependent q-shift
In the following, we give further details about the density dependence of the q-shift, which was observed for moving solitons.
Fig. 7 shows velocity data for different atomic densities. The velocities are not rescaled with the mean soliton width, therefore
the slopes do not coincide. We extract the density-dependent offset ∆q from the linear fits, and the results are shown in Fig. 8
with a linear fit to the data. The density-dependent offset is not captured in one-dimensional spin-1 mean-field simulations
and is also not present in the analytical calculations based on the spin continuity equation. We have also performed numerical
simulations in a three-dimensional trapping configuration close to the experimental one to exclude that the shift is due to the
finite transversal extent of the condensate. A possible source for the shift could be dipolar interactions or beyond-mean-field
effects, that were not included so far in the numerical simulations.
