Abstract
We consider the Minimum-(k, ρ)-Shortcut problem (min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut), where the goal is to
find the smallest set of shortcut edges such that every vertex in a given graph can reach its ρ closest
vertices using paths of at most k edges. This is a fundamental graph optimization problem used to
accelerate parallel shortest path algorithms.
It is well-known that the problem is trivially solvable for the cases k = 1 and k ≥ρ. While
recent work by Leonhardt, Meyer, and Penschuck (ESA 2024) showed that in undirected graphs
min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut is NP-hard for k ≥3 if ρ = Θ(nε), the boundary where the problem transitions
from polynomial-time solvable to NP-hard remained open.
In this paper, we narrow this gap significantly. We present a simpler and more direct reduction
from the Hitting Set problem which establishes that min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut is NP-hard for k ≥2
and ρ ≥k + 2 in both undirected and directed graphs. Complementing this, we use the symmetry
of the undirected case to show that ρ = k + 1 is solvable in polynomial time, a regime where the
directed version remains a candidate for NP-hardness. Therefore, we obtain an almost complete
characterization of the complexity of min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut, with the sole remaining open case being
ρ = k + 1 in the directed setting.

Introduction
The design of algorithms to solve the single source shortest path problem is one of the
fundamental challenges of computer science. Since the inception of the well-known Dijkstra [7]
and Bellman-Ford algorithms [2, 10], efficient practical sequential algorithms have emerged [12,
11]. In contrast, the progress of a parallel counterpart, namely the parallel single source
shortest path problem (PSSSP) on general graphs is limited.
Traditional PSSSP approaches often struggle to achieve low depth (i.e. high parallelism)
since this usually requires more work than the sequential Dijkstra’s algorithm. Recently, Cao
and Fineman [4] presented a novel algorithm that improved the theoretical upper bounds for
solving PSSSP on directed graphs. It achieves almost linear work e
O(m) and almost square
root depth n0.5+o(1). In practice, variants based on the stepping framework are used [15, 3, 8].
These variants exhibit superior experimental performance over Bellman-Ford achieving linear
depth in specific instances, although they do not provide theoretical guarantees for general
instances. The gap between theory and practice arises from intricate design and high constant
factors involved in novel theoretical algorithms.
To support the theoretical bounds for stepping based PSSSP algorithms, Blelloch, Gu,
Sun, and Tangwongsan [3] introduced a new graph notion alongside their Radius-Stepping
algorithm for undirected graphs. They call a graph a (k, ρ)-graph, if every vertex of the
graph can reach its ρ-closest vertices by using shortest paths with at most k edges. In this
setting, the algorithm’s work is almost linear in the number of edges ˜O(m) and its depth is

Any graph can be viewed as a (k, ρ)-graph for an appropriate choice of the parameters
k and ρ. Furthermore, for fixed values of k and ρ, any graph can be transformed into a
(k, ρ)-graph by adding shortcuts, that is, additional edges preserving all pairwise distances in
the original graph. This naturally leads to the following optimization problem:
▶Definition 1.1. The Minimum-(k, ρ)-Shortcut problem (min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut):
Input: A weighted graph G.
Problem: Find shortcut set of minimum cardinality for G such that, after adding the
shortcuts to G, it becomes a (k, ρ)-graph.
To provide a clear characterization in terms of fixed k and ρ, we analyze the decision
version of the min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut defined as follows:
▶Definition 1.2. The (k, ρ)-Shortcut problem ((k, ρ)-Shortcut):
Input: A weighted graph G and α ∈N.
Problem: Decide whether there is a shortcut set S for G with |S| ≤α such that after
adding the shortcuts to G, it becomes a (k, ρ)-graph.
Observe that the intractability of deciding whether a shortcut set of size α exists directly
implies that the optimization version of the problem is NP-hard.
In order to obtain graphs with small k and large ρ, reducing the depth of their algorithm
while maintaining almost linear work in the number of edges, Blelloch, Gu, Sun, and
Tangwongsan [3] provide a heuristic approach. However, they did not study the complexity
of finding the optimal shortcut set. Subsequent work by Dong, Gu, Sun, and Zhang [8] also
relies on the notion of (k, ρ)-graphs for their further improved algorithm. Their experimental
results underline the practical relevance of finding shortcuts efficiently for arbitrary k
and ρ. Therefore, characterizing the complexity of min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut has emerged as a key
theoretical challenge in the design of work-efficient parallel shortest-path algorithms.

Leonhardt, Meyer, and Penschuck [14] study the complexity of (k, ρ)-shortcutting, i.e. the
problem of finding a minimum cardinality set of shortcuts that converts a given undirected
graph with n vertices into an undirected (k, ρ)-graph. For k ≥3, they prove that finding such
a set of shortcuts is NP-hard if ρ = Θ(nε) holds for some ε > 0. In particular, this means
that ρ is not a constant but grows as a function of the number of vertices of the input graph,
which is not allowed in the more restrictive problem formulation that we consider, where
both k and ρ are fixed. Their construction relies on an intricate reduction from Vertex
Cover that requires pitchfork gadgets (star-like structures), and sophisticated blow up
lemmas to maintain the proof integrity. In this paper, we present a significantly simpler and
more general reduction from Hitting Set, extending the hardness to previously unexplored
regimes and providing a more direct insight into the problem’s fundamental complexity. In
addition, we expand the domain of the problem by also considering the directed setting.
1.1
Our Contribution
Our work focuses on characterizing the complexity of min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut on both, undirected
and directed graphs.
By definition, any directed graph with non-negative edge weights is a (k, ρ)-graph
when ρ ≤k. For k = 1, (k, ρ)-Shortcut is solvable in polynomial time for all ρ [14]. Therefore,
our work focuses on the case where ρ > k ≥2.
While our primary result focuses on the hardness of shortcutting, it is worth noting
that when multiple vertices are at the same distance from a source vertex, the set of “ρ
closest neighbors” becomes ambiguous. In such cases, deciding which specific vertices must
be reachable using paths of k edges can significantly alter the optimal shortcut set. This
challenge is captured by the (k, ρ)-Tiebreaker problem, in which given a graph, the task
is to select a specific set of ρ closest neighbors for each vertex, such that the selection is
compatible with an optimal solution to the min-(k, ρ)-Shortcut problem. A formal definition
of the problem is given in Definition 3.2. We show that (k, ρ)-Tiebreaker is a hard problem
on its own by providing a straightforward reduction from Hitting Set. In particular, we
show the following result in Section 3:
▶Theorem 1.3 (Tiebreaker hardness). For all k ≥2, ρ ≥k + 3, d-HittingSet is polynomial-
time Turing-reducible to (k, ρ)-Tiebreaker where d = ⌊(ρ −k + 1)/2⌋. In particular, the
(k, ρ)-Tiebreaker problem is NP-hard under Turing reductions for k ≥2 and ρ ≥k + 3. This
remains true when restricting the problem to only undirected graphs or to only directed graphs.
This raises the question of whether restricting to instances where the ρ-closest neighbors
are unique makes the problem more tractable. We show NP-completeness for both undirected
and directed graphs by providing a simple reduction from Hitting Set. Concretely, we
show the following in Section 3:
▶Main Theorem 1 (NP-hardness). For all k ≥2, ρ ≥k + 2, d-HittingSet ≤p (k, ρ)-Shortcut
where d = ρ−k. Thus, (k, ρ)-Shortcut is NP-complete for k ≥2 and ρ ≥k+2. This remains
true when restricting the problem to only undirected graphs or to only directed graphs where
the ρ-closest neighbors are unique.
This shows that the hardness is not a result of selecting the closest neighbors but it is
inherent to the structural optimization of the shortcuts. In the undirected case, our result
significantly improves on the hardness bound of Leonhardt, Meyer, and Penschuck [14] as we
do not require instances where ρ grows as a function of the number of vertices in order to

1.2
Related Work
Hub labeling is a related field of research. Cohen, Halperin, Kaplan, and Zwick [5] propose a
data structure for representing information about every pair of vertices in a graph that relies
on labeling vertices, which are then used for answering queries quickly and efficiently. For
example, the labels encode the (approximate) distance or the existence of a path between
each pair of vertices. They introduce 2-hop labels in different variants depending on the
information that is represented, and show that these problems can be solved by finding a
2-hop cover. This is a collection H of (certain) paths in the graph such that for every pair of
vertices u ̸= v, a path from u to v can be retrieved using at most two concatenated paths
from H. If there is no restriction on H, then 2-hop labels for answering reachability queries
are obtained. For obtaining 2-hop labels for answering distance queries, H is restricted to
contain only shortest paths. In this case, using our notion of distance-preserving shortcuts, H
is essentially a set of shortcuts such that within two hops, each vertex can reach every other
reachable vertex. As such, they consider a special variant of the (k, ρ)-shortcutting problem
where k = 2 and ρ is (e.g.) the number of vertices. For this problem, there are NP-hardness
results as well as bounds on the approximation factor if the restriction for the paths in H
has certain properties. There is also research considering the case where k ≥3. For a more
elaborate variant of the problem where vertices have a rank and potential hubs are further
restricted by these ranks, see [1].

We proceed with a formal definition of the problem. Let G denote a weighted graph with
vertex set V (G), edge set E(G), and edge weights we ≥1 for all e ∈E(G). G can be either
directed or undirected. For any vertices u, v ∈V (G), a shortest path from u to v is a path
from u to v with minimum total weight.
▶Definition 2.1 (Weight Distance, Hop Distance). Let u, v ∈V (G).

▶Remark 2.5. Adding shortcuts preserves the weight distance between any pair of vertices.
Note that when adding a shortcut set, a multigraph might emerge. However, any edge
parallel to a shortcut must have strictly greater weight than the shortcut itself as otherwise
the hop distance of the vertices would be 1. In particular, Remark 2.5 implies that the
parallel edge was not used in any shortest path before. Hence, we can remove such edges
from the graph to avoid the technicality of dealing with multigraphs.
Due to non-negative edge weights, every graph G is already a (k, ρ)-graph if ρ ≤k,
and the corresponding problems have trivial solutions. Another observation can be made
for k = 1, where min(1, ρ)-Shortcut is polynomial-time solvable for all ρ ∈N: Compute
a ρ-closest neighbor set Su
ρ for every vertex u ∈V (G), where ties are first broken in favor of
shorter hop distances, and second arbitrarily. As u needs to have a (1, ρ)-ball, it is sufficient
and necessary to insert a shortcut from u to v for each vertex v ∈Su
ρ with hopdist(u, v) > 1.
Thus, a (1, ρ)-shortcut set that has minimum cardinality can be computed in polynomial
time. Consequently, the corresponding decision problems are also polynomial-time solvable.
Hence, we focus on those problems where ρ > k > 1 holds.

Hardness of the (k, ρ)-Shortcut Problem
In this section, we prove Main Theorem 1 and Theorem 1.3 by giving a simple reduction
from d-HittingSet. Given a hypergraph H with vertex set V (H) and hyperedge set E(H),
a hitting set is a subset U ⊆V (H) such that U ∩e ̸= ∅for all e ∈E(H).
This is a
generalization of the vertex cover problem. Given a hypergraph H and a natural number α ∈
N, the HittingSet problem asks if H contains a hitting set of size at most α. This is one of
Karp’s classical NP-complete problems [13]. The size of the largest hyperedge in H is called
the rank of H. By restricting the rank of the input hypergraph to be at most d, one obtains
the problem d-HittingSet. For d ≥2, this restriction remains NP-complete.

seen as two nested covering problems: First, a ρ-closest neighbor sets must be selected for

Conclusion and Future Work
We have given a simple reduction proving NP-hardness that can be taught in class. Although
simple in nature, it is sufficiently expressive to improve on the prior state of the art by
reducing the prior constraint of k ≥3 for hardness down to k ≥2. In addition, we do not
rely on a polynomial dependency of ρ on n to exhibit hardness unlike previus work.
While we successfully settle the complexity for the undirected case, the directed case
for ρ = k + 1 remains open. Many of the technical lemmas used for the undirected case,
e.g., Lemmas 4.4 and 4.7, crucially rely on the ability to walk a path backwards. Thus,
the proofs inevitably break down when considering directed paths instead. Although the
structure in this setting is still highly constrained, any proof has to deal with the asymmetry
inherent to directed graphs. Several statements about G|φ
k,k+1 turn out to be false for its
directed analogue. For example, the directed variant of G|φ
k,k+1 is neither a forest nor a
pseudoforest. This suggests that the directed case for ρ = k + 1 may not be a mere technical
variant, but could instead belong to a different complexity class, or that at least it requires a
fundamentally different approach. The settlement of this case is a problem for future work.
Our tractability proof for the undirected case with ρ = k +1 relies on Courcelle’s theorem.
Although this approach allows us to prove that the problem is polynomial-time solvable, the
specific structure of the regime provides a clear opportunity for future work to design a more
direct and efficient combinatorial algorithm.
