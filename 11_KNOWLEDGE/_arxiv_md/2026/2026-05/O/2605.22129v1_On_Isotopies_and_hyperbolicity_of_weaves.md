ON ISOTOPIES AND HYPERBOLICITY OF WEAVES
KEN’ICHI YOSHIDA
Abstract. A weave is a type of textile that consists of vertical and horizontal
threads, and typically it has a periodic structure. In this paper, we regard a
weave as a link in the thickened torus with a diagram consisting of closed
geodesics.
As main results, we characterize isotopies and hyperbolicity of
weaves to determine them from diagrams. Moreover, we show that there does
not exist an essential Conway sphere for a weave. We use normal positions of
essential surfaces of weave complements to describe them.
1. Introduction
A weave is a type of textile produced by combining threads (called warps and
wefts) in two directions. The three types of weaves in Figure 1 are commonly used as
basic weaves. The plain weave is represented by an alternating diagram and has the
simplest structure. A twill weave has a pattern of diagonal ribs. A satin weave has
a structure in which most of the wefts (or the warps) are in front. Twill and satin
weaves have variations by parameters, and their structures were mathematically
investigated by Gr¨unbaum and Shephard [13]. Recently, there have been various
studies to create weaves from molecules [19, 26, 37].
Figure 1. Basic weaves: plain, twill, and satin
Mathematical research on textile structures, including weaves, using knot theory
was initiated by Grishanov, Meshkov, and Omel’chenko [12].
A typical textile
structure can be represented by the preimage of a link in the thickened torus T 2 ×I
by the universal covering map, which is called a doubly periodic tangle [8]. To
consider doubly periodic tangles, several invariants such as polynomial invariants
of links in T 2 × I have been computed.
However, a doubly periodic tangle is
represented by infinitely many links in T 2 × I. These are related by finite covers.
Nonetheless, consistency of finite covers and isotopies of links in T 2 × I was shown
in [25] using hyperbolic geometry. Hyperbolic structures on the complements of
weaves (especially the plain weave [4, 36]) have been investigated as interesting

In this paper, we regard a weave as a link in the thickened torus T 2 × I with
a diagram on T 2 that consists of closed geodesics. A weave can be regarded as a

In the following main theorems, we characterize isotopies and hyperbolicity of
weaves.
Theorem 2.1. If two weaves L0 and L1 in T 2 × I are isotopic links, then L0 is
isotopic to L1 via weaves. In other words, there is an ambient isotopy from L0 to
L1 in which each intermediate link is a weave.
Theorem 3.2. An m × n-weave L in T 2 × I for m, n ≥1 is hyperbolic if and only
if
• L is not layered, and
• L has no pair of parallel components even after interchanging adjacent com-
parable components.
Here, a weave is called layered if it is separated into two weaves. Consequently,
we can determine from diagrams whether two weaves are isotopic and whether a
weave is hyperbolic. For example, the three 8×8-weaves in Figure 1 are hyperbolic
and not mutually isotopic. In general cases, we have to be careful with the fact
that two adjacent components in a certain condition can be interchanged.
In Section 2, we prepare notions and show Theorem 2.1. In Section 3, we describe
the essential tori in a weave complement to show Theorem 3.2. We also discuss how
generic hyperbolic weaves are. In Section 4, we show that there does not exist an
essential Conway sphere for a weave. As a result, if L is a hyperbolic weave, the
thickened torus T 2 × I admits a hyperbolic orbifold structure with singular locus
L of a cone angle π. In Section 5, we ask questions on symmetries and hyperbolic
volumes of weaves. We also give remarkable examples of weaves.
2. Isotopies of weaves
In this section, we prepare notions and show a fundamental result on isotopies
of weaves. A link in a 3-manifold X is a disjoint union of circles embedded in
the interior of X. For two links L0 and L1 in X, an ambient isotopy from L0 to
L1 is a continuous map F : X × [0, 1] →X such that Ft = F(·, t): X →X is a
homeomorphism for each 0 ≤t ≤1, F0 is the identity map, and F1(L0) = L1. Two
links L0 and L1 are isotopic if there is an ambient isotopy from L0 to L1.
Let S1 = R/Z, T 2 = R2/Z2 = S1 × S1, and I = [0, 1]. Let p1, p2 : T 2 × I →S1
denote the projections to the first and second coordinates, respectively. For m, n ≥
0, an m × n-weave is a link in the thickened torus T 2 × I that is a disjoint union of
m warps and n wefts, where
• a warp (vertical component) is the image of an embedding ι: S1 →T 2 × I
such that p1 ◦ι: S1 →S1 is constant, and p2 ◦ι: S1 →S1 is a homeomor-
phism, and
• a weft (horizontal component) is the image of an embedding ι: S1 →T 2×I
such that p2 ◦ι: S1 →S1 is constant, and p1 ◦ι: S1 →S1 is a homeomor-
phism.
We often call it a weave by ignoring m and n. Note that a link isotopic to a weave
may not be a weave in our terminology.

3. Essential tori in weave complements
In this section, we characterize the hyperbolic weaves. A link L in T 2 × I is
hyperbolic if the interior of T 2 × I \ L admits a complete hyperbolic structure of
finite volume. Since each component of a weave in T 2 × I is homotopically non-
trivial, a weave complement is irreducible (i.e., every embedded sphere bounds
a ball).
A compact orientable surface other than a sphere properly embedded
in a compact orientable irreducible 3-manifold is essential if it is incompressible,
boundary-incompressible, and not boundary-parallel.
Theorem 3.1 follows from
Thurston’s hyperbolization theorem for Haken 3-manifolds [34]. The assumption
excludes exceptionally simple Seifert fibered 3-manifolds such as the product of a
3-punctured sphere and a circle, in which there does not exist an essential torus,
but there exists a π1-injective immersed torus.
Theorem 3.1. Let M be a compact orientable irreducible 3-manifold with boundary
consisting of at least four tori. Then the interior M admits a complete hyperbolic
structure of finite volume if and only if there does not exist an essential torus in
M.

T 2 × I is not hyperbolic. The weave shown in the left of Figure 4 is layered. For

Theorem 4.2. Let L be a hyperbolic link in a compact orientable irreducible 3-
manifold X with boundary consisting of non-empty incompressible tori. Suppose
that there does not exist an essential Conway sphere for L in X. Then L is π-
hyperbolic.
Proof. Consider the orbifold O of the underlying space X with a cone angle π at the
singular locus L. Thurston’s orbifold theorem states that O is decomposed along
(possibly empty) essential spherical and Euclidean 2-suborbifolds into pieces each
of which is a hyperbolic, Seifert fibered, Euclidean, spherical, or Sol orbifold. Since
the last two types are closed, we exclude them. A non-closed Euclidean 3-orbifold

is Seifert fibered. The closed Euclidean 3-orbifolds that are not Seifert fibered were

Since X is irreducible, a sphere embedded in X intersects L transversely at an
even number of points. In particular, there is not an embedded sphere with three
intersectional points of L, called a turnover. Moreover, since L is hyperbolic, there
is not an essential sphere with two intersectional points of L. Hence there is not an
essential spherical 2-suborbifold of O. A possible essential Euclidean 2-suborbifold
of O is a sphere with four cone points of cone angles π, called a pillowcase, which
is a Conway sphere.
Since there does not exist an essential Conway sphere for L, then O is hyperbolic
or Seifert fibered. Assume that O is Seifert fibered. Then O is geometric (see [5,
Theorem 2.50]). By [9, Theorem 1], the 3-manifold X is obtained by gluing (possibly
empty) solid tori to a Seifert fibered manifold, and the link L is a union of fibers and
Montesinos links in the attached solid tori. Since X has non-empty boundary, we
have L is a union of fibers of a Seifert fibered 3-manifold X, or there is an essential
torus in X \ L. This contradicts the assumption that L is hyperbolic. Hence O is
hyperbolic.

5. Further questions
5.1. Symmetries of weaves. The preimage of a link in T 2 × I by the universal
covering map R2 × I →T 2 × I is called a doubly periodic tangle. Actually, textile
structures such as weaves should be regarded as doubly periodic tangles, rather
than links in T 2 ×I. A layer group is a discrete group that acts R2 ×I cocompactly
and corresponds to an orbifold that is a quotient of T 2 × I. The symmetry of a
doubly periodic tangle is represented by one of the 80 layer groups, as explained
in [28]. According to De Las Pe˜nas, Tomenes, and Liza [7], there are precisely
50 layer groups that represent the symmetries of weaves. However, they classified
the symmetries for fixed weaving diagrams and did not consider isotopy classes
of weaves. Moreover, given examples of weaves with low symmetries often have
adjacent parallel components. Nonetheless, the Mostow rigidity theorem [31, 32]
implies that the symmetry of a hyperbolic weave L in T 2 × I up to isotopy is
represented by the finite group Isom(T 2 × I \ L) consisting of the isometries on the
hyperbolic 3-manifold T 2 × I \ L. For the doubly periodic tangle eL in R2 × I that
is the preimage of L, the group Isom(R2 × I \ eL) is a layer group.
Question 5.1. Can each of the 50 layer groups given in [7] be obtained from the
symmetry of a hyperbolic weave up to isotopy?
