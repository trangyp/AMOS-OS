We present new convergence estimates for the iterated penalty method applied to structure-
preserving discretizations of linear generalized saddle point systems. The method may be
viewed as an Uzawa iteration on an augmented Lagrangian formulation of the system. As
a by-product, we obtain sharper stability estimates for penalized/perturbed saddle point
problems. Three model finite element applications show agreement with the theory.
Keywords: iterated penalty method; augmented Lagrangian method; saddle point problem.
1. Introduction
We consider the numerical solution of generalized linear saddle point problems of the form:
Find u ∈V and p ∈Q such that
a(u,v)+(Dv, p)Q = F(v)
∀v ∈V,
(1.1a)
(Du,q)Q = G(q)
∀q ∈Q.
(1.1b)
Here, V and Q are real Hilbert spaces with inner-products (·,·)◦, norms ∥·∥◦, and dual spaces
◦′ for ◦∈{V,Q}; a :V ×V →R is a continuous bilinear form; D :V →Q is a surjective, bounded,
linear operator; and F ∈V ′ and G ∈Q′. Problems of the form (1.1) arise in a variety of
applications including incompressible flow, elasticity, liquid crystals, electromagnetism, and
constrained optimization (see e.g. (Benzi et al., 2005; Boﬀi et al., 2013)). The well-posedness of
(1.1) is given by classical Babuška–Brezzi theory (Babuška, 1971; Brezzi, 1974; Nečas, 1962).

We assume that (1.2) is well posed and that the discrete spaces are structure-preserving in
the sense that DV = Q. Structure-preserving schemes offer a number of advantages. As we
will see in Section 2 below, a discrete inf-sup condition necessary for the well-posedness of
(1.2) automatically holds. Additionally, one obtains improved error estimates (Boﬀi et al.,
2013, Theorem 5.2.4), (Ern and Guermond, 2021, Lemma 50.2 and Corollary 50.5), robustness
properties (Ern and Guermond, 2021, Remark 50.8) and more accurate representation of the
underlying physics of the problem; see e.g. (John et al., 2017) for the case of incompressible
flow. However, characterizing and constructing a basis for Q = DV is typically not a simple
task, and in many cases is an open problem.
One solution method that avoids the need for an explicit basis of DV is a simple Uzawa
iteration (Arrow et al., 1958) applied to an augmented Lagrangian formulation of (1.2), defined
precisely in (4.1) below. While the use of augmented Lagrangian methods dates back to Hestenes
(Hestenes, 1969) and Powell (Powell, 1969) in the nonlinear optimization literature, the first
use of these techniques with finite element methods appears to be Fortin & Glowinski (Fortin
and Glowinski, 1983) for incompressible flow problems (G = 0). In the context of structure-
preserving finite element methods (again for incompressible flow problems with G = 0), Scott
& Bagheri (Scott and Bagheri, 1990) appear to be the first to note that an explicit basis for
DV can be avoided with an Uzawa iteration applied to an augmented Lagrangian formulation;
the iteration scheme was dubbed “the iterated penalty method”. The method and its analysis
were extended to general saddle point problems in the first edition of (Brenner and Scott, 2008)
and to the case that G(q) = (Dv,q)Q in (Scott et al., 1996). In the context of incompressible
flow problems, variants of the iterated penalty method for high-order discretizations have been
developed in (Ainsworth and Parker, 2023) and for inhomogeneous boundary conditions in
(Eickmann et al., 2025; Scott et al., 1996).
Our main contributions are novel convergence estimates for the iterated penalty method and
an implementation scheme for the general case G ̸= 0. We show that under suitable assumptions
on the penalty parameters, depending on the structure of the bilinear form a(·,·), the iterated
penalty method converges at a geometric rate (Theorem 4.3 below). All of our estimates are
explicit in the typical stability parameters associated with the well-posedness of the discrete
saddle point system (1.2). As a by-product, we also obtain sharper stability estimates for
a penalized/perturbed formulation of (1.2) (Theorem 3.1 below) than those available in the
literature.
A detailed comparison with the current literature is given in Section 7 below, so we provide
a brief overview here. The case a(·,·) is symmetric positive definite (SPD) can be found in
(Fortin and Glowinski, 1983), a(·,·) is nonnegative and SPD on the kernel of D in (Brenner
and Scott, 2008), and a(·,·) coercive on the kernel of D in (Awanou and Lai, 2005; Huang et al.,
2024). Here, we only assume that a(·,·) satisfies an inf-sup condition on the kernel of D, which
appears to be novel. We also show that if a(·,·) is positive definite, or nonnegative and SPD on
the kernel of D, then the one-parameter variant of the iterated penalty method converges at a
geometric rate for any choice of penalty parameter, another result that appears to be new.

7. Relation to previous work
7.1. Penalty method
The stability of the perturbed saddle point problem or penalty method (3.1) has long been
known, with the first result seemingly being (Arnold, 1981, Theorem 5.1), which assumed (A1)
and showed existence and stability of solutions for all ε ≥0. However, the dependence of the
stability constants on Ma, βX, etc. was not explicitly given, as is the case in many standard
references e.g. (Girault and Raviart, 1986, p. 65, Theorem 4.3), (Brezzi and Fortin, 1991, p. 47,
Theorem 1.2), and (Braess, 2007, Theorem 4.11). When explicit constants do appear, e.g. (Boﬀi
et al., 2013, Theorem 4.3.2) assuming (A1), (Ainsworth and Parker, 2022, eq. (3.16)) for linear
elasticity, and (Bernardi et al., 2025, Lemma 7.1.1) for the Stokes equations, the dependence of
the constants in Table 1 are sharper, particularly as βX →0 and/or as ε →0. We also remark
that the paragraph after (Brenner and Scott, 2008, p. 359, Theorem 13.1.19) notes that the
arguments in (Brenner and Scott, 2008) are not suﬀicient to prove that ∥pX,ε −pX∥Q →0 as
ε →0, but Theorem 3.3 shows that this is always the case. Thus the last sentence in the
paragraph after (Brenner and Scott, 2008, p. 359, Theorem 13.1.19) is wrong.
