ON MINIMAL COLLECTIONS OF SEQUENCES FOR TESTING CONTINUITY
GYUHYUN LIM
Abstract. We study test sets: subfamilies of sequences converging to a point P that still suffice to
detect every discontinuity of real-valued functions at P. Ordered by inclusion, these test sets form
a poset. Under natural hypotheses at P, we prove that this poset has a minimal element. We also
analyze its maximal chains, showing that some have a least element, while others do not. Finally,
on the sequential fan we give a concrete realization in which the minimal test set produced by our
construction has strictly smaller cardinality than the full family of convergent sequences.

1. Introduction
In ordinary freshman calculus and introductory analysis, continuity at a point is often tested
using sequences: to verify that a function is continuous at P, one checks its behavior along every
sequence converging to P. While this criterion is correct in many familiar settings, it raises a natural
efficiency question: must one really inspect the entire family of convergent sequences, or can a much
smaller subfamily already detect every possible failure of continuity at P?
A familiar motivation comes from primality testing. The statement “n is prime” is equivalent to
“n is not divisible by any integer d with 1 < d < n.” Nevertheless, an equivalent formulation need
not be an optimal procedure. In practice, it suffices to test divisibility only for prime d ≤√n, and
this still yields a logically equivalent criterion with a drastically smaller search space. The guiding

idea of this paper is that sequential tests for continuity can exhibit the same phenomenon: one may
be able to discard many sequences while still detecting every discontinuity.
A first concrete hint appears on the real line. For f : R →R, continuity at a point P can be
decided by comparing two one-sided behaviors: if the left limit and the right limit exist and both
are equal to f(P), then f is continuous at P. Equivalently, it suffices to test f(Tn) →f(P) only for
sequences that approach P eventually from the left, and for sequences that approach P eventually
from the right. In this reformulation, highly oscillatory sequences are not examined directly, yet no
discontinuity is missed.
The purpose of this paper is to study this efficiency problem in an abstract topological setting.
Rather than asking only whether the full family of convergent sequences can be reduced, we ask
which smaller collections of convergent sequences still suffice to detect every failure of continuity at
P. To make this question precise, one is naturally led to look not only at all sequences converging
to P, but also at the smaller subfamilies along which a given discontinuous function actually reveals
its discontinuity. The problem then becomes one of finding compressed testing families that still
meet every such witness family. These notions will be formalized in Section 2; see in particular
Definitions 2.6 and 2.11.
Once those testing families are available, they may be compared by inclusion. This is the natural
order for the present problem: passing from a larger testing family to a smaller one corresponds to
discarding sequences while retaining the ability to detect every discontinuity. The resulting inclusion
structure, formalized as the poset (F, ⊆) in Definition 2.11, is the main object studied in this paper.
Two structural questions drive the paper.
Question 1.1. Does the inclusion-ordered family of test sets contain a minimal element?
Question 1.2. If minimal test sets exist, must they be reflected in every maximal chain of test
sets?
Question 1.1 asks whether there is an irreducible test set. Question 1.2 asks whether such minimal
objects are visible uniformly across the order structure of (F, ⊆). These questions are answered in
Theorems 1.1 and 1.2.
At the same time, the original motivation is one of efficiency. A test set that is minimal with
respect to inclusion need not have smaller cardinality than the full family SP . Thus, throughout
the paper, we distinguish inclusion-minimality from a genuine reduction in cardinality. The general
results establish the existence of inclusion-minimal test sets, while the sequential fan considered
later shows that such a reduction in cardinality can also occur.
Standing framework. All arguments are carried out at a fixed point P under four standing
assumptions:
• (X, P) is Fr´echet–Urysohn at P (Definition 2.1), so discontinuity at P is detectable by se-
quences; see Lemma 2.8.
• X is T1 at P, so the later set-theoretic construction can be carried out inside X \ {P}; see
Lemma 2.4 and Subsection 4.4.
• P is non-isolated, ensuring that there are genuinely nontrivial sequences approaching P.
• There exists a real-valued function discontinuous at P, excluding the degenerate case in which
every testing family is automatically sufficient; see Lemma 2.9 and Remark 2.10.

2. From continuity to test sets
The purpose of this section is to recast the continuity problem at P in a form that can be
studied combinatorially. Starting from the usual sequential criterion for continuity, we pass to witness
families of convergent sequences and then to the test sets that meet all such families. This produces
the inclusion-ordered family (F, ⊆) that will be studied in the rest of the paper.

2.2. Witnessing discontinuity and test sets. We now introduce the objects that translate
continuity at P into a problem about families of sequences convergent to P. The first step is to
record, for each discontinuous function, exactly which convergent sequences witness its failure of
continuity.
Definition 2.6 (SP , DP , and D(f)).
(i) The family of sequences converging to P:
SP := {T = (Tn)n∈N | Tn ∈X, Tn →P} .
(ii) The class of real-valued functions discontinuous at P:
DP := {f : X →R | f is discontinuous at P}.
(iii) For a function f : X →R in DP , the family of witnessing sequences:
D(f) := {T ∈SP | f(Tn) ̸→f(P)} .
Thus SP is the full sequential testing family at P, while D(f) ⊆SP singles out those sequences
along which the discontinuity of f is actually visible. The point of this language is that discontinuity
is no longer treated only as a local topological failure at P; it is now encoded by a concrete subfamily
of the convergent sequences to P.

Remark 2.10. If DP = ∅, then the condition of being a test set becomes vacuous: there are no

4. Constructing a minimal test set
Throughout this section we work under the standing framework of Section 1 at the fixed point
P: (X, P) is Fr´echet–Urysohn at P, X is T1 at P, P is non-isolated, and DP ̸= ∅.
The goal of this section is to construct a minimal test set. As a formal consequence, we will also
obtain a good maximal chain. A direct construction at the level of sequences is difficult, so we first
pass from injective convergent sequences to their ranges in X \ {P}. This reduces the problem to a
set-theoretic one, where the minimality question can be handled using almost disjointness.
4.1. Recasting the problem in terms of ranges. Because X is T1 at P, Lemma 2.4 gives
KP = {P}. Thus the relevant region outside the limit point is simply X \ {P}. We now isolate the
subsets of X \ {P} that behave like ranges of injective sequences converging to P.
Definition 4.1 (The family IP ). Define IP to be the family of sets M ⊆X \ {P} such that:
(i) M is countably infinite;
(ii) for every neighborhood U ∈N(P), the set M \ U is finite.
The point of introducing IP is that injective convergent sequences carry more information than
we need. For the argument below, what matters is not the order in which points appear, but only
which points appear and whether they still accumulate at P. The family IP records exactly that
information.

4.2. Building a minimal test set from a MAD family. We need a family inside IP with two
properties: maximality and almost disjointness. Maximality will be used to prove the test-set prop-
erty, and almost disjointness will be used to prove minimality. We therefore choose a maximal almost
disjoint family. Almost disjoint families and their role in topology are surveyed by Hruˇs´ak [Hru14].
Definition 4.5 (Almost disjointness ⊥P ). For A, B ⊆X \ {P}, we say that A and B are almost
disjoint if
|A ∩B| < ∞.
In this case, we write A ⊥P B.
By non-redundancy, we mean that no member of the candidate test set can be removed while
preserving the test-set property. Almost disjointness will be used to prove this non-redundancy.
When one sequence is removed, we will choose a discontinuous function supported on its range.
Since every other range meets that range only finitely often, the remaining sequences will eventually
fail to detect that function. Thus each sequence becomes indispensable, and this is what will force
minimality.
Definition 4.6 (Maximal almost disjoint family). A family M ⊆IP is called a maximal almost
disjoint family, or simply a MAD family, if
(i) for M1 ̸= M2 in M, one has M1 ⊥P M2;
(ii) for every M ∈IP , there exists N ∈M such that
|M ∩N| = ∞.
Condition (i) says that M is almost disjoint. Condition (ii) is the maximality condition: no
member of IP can be added to M while preserving pairwise almost disjointness. Indeed, if a witness
range M ∈IP met every member of M only finitely often, then M could be added to M without
destroying almost disjointness. This would contradict maximality. Hence every witness range M ∈
IP must meet some member of M infinitely often, which means that its witness role can be taken
over by a member of the consequent test set. This is exactly what will yield the test-set property.
