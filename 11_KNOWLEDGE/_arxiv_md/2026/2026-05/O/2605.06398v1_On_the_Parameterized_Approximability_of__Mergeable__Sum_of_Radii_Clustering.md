Abstract
The sum of radii problem (k-MSR) asks, given a metric space on n points, to place k
balls covering all points so as to minimize the sum of their radii.
Despite extensive study
from the perspectives of approximation and parameterized algorithms, the exact parameterized
complexity of the problem and the existence of efficient parameterized approximation schemes
remained open. We advance this understanding on both the hardness and algorithmic fronts.
We begin by showing that k-MSR is W[2]-hard parameterized by k, thereby pinpoint-
ing its location in the W-hierarchy.
Moreover, via our reduction, we rule out efficient pa-
rameterized approximation schemes (EPAS)—that is, (1 + ε)-approximations running in time
f(k, ε) · poly(n)—unless W[2] = FPT. Assuming the Exponential Time Hypothesis, we further
rule out such algorithms running in time f(k, ε) · no(k), strengthening recent lower bounds for
the problem.
On the algorithmic side, we study k-MSR under the framework of mergeable constraints,
which captures a broad class of clustering constraints, including fairness, diversity, and lower
bounds. We obtain an FPT ( 8
3 +ε)-approximation, improving upon the previous best guarantee
of (4 + ε). Moreover, given access to a suitable assignment subroutine, we achieve a (2 + ε)-
approximation, matching the best known bound for the unconstrained problem. This, in turn,
yields (2+ε) FPT-approximations for several important settings, including (t, k)-fair, (α, β)-fair,
ℓ-diversity, and private clustering.

Introduction
Clustering with the sum of radii objective has emerged as a natural alternative to classical center-
based formulations such as k-median, k-center, and k-means.
The unconstrained sum of radii
problem, abbreviated as k-MSR, admits a simple geometric interpretation: given a metric space
(X, d) on n points, the goal is to place k balls that cover all points while minimizing the sum of
their radii. A particularly appealing feature of k-MSR is that it mitigates the dissection effect
on underlying ground-truth clusters—a phenomenon that commonly occurs in its closely related
cousin, the k-center problem, which instead aims to minimize the maximum radius.
From a computational perspective, the approximability landscape of k-center is well understood:
it admits a simple 2-approximation, and this bound is known to be tight [31, 35]. In contrast, despite
more than two decades of study [16, 30, 9], the polynomial-time approximability of k-MSR remains
far less understood. The problem is known to be NP-hard and admits a QPTAS [30], thereby
suggesting the possibility of a PTAS. Recently, [14] improved the best known polynomial-time
approximation factor to (3 + ε), improving upon earlier results [27, 16].
Over the past two decades, FPT-approximation algorithms have achieved remarkable success
for clustering problems, obtaining approximation factors significantly better than those achievable
in polynomial time [4, 39, 19, 20, 2]. These algorithms run in time f(k)poly(n), and since the
parameter k is often small compared to n, they are particularly appealing in practice. Moreover,
this framework has proved effective even in constrained settings, including capacities, fairness,
diversity, and outliers; see, for example, [33, 34, 20, 12, 43, 29, 21] and references therein.
For k-center, the parameterized approximability landscape is again well understood: no (2−ε)-
approximation algorithm exists in FPT time unless W[2] = FPT. In sharp contrast, the parameter-
ized complexity of unconstrained k-MSR remains poorly understood. On the algorithmic side, the
best known FPT approximation guarantee is (2 + ε), due to Chen et al. [17], based on an extension
of the classical greedy framework of [35] for k-center. On the lower-bound side, Banerjee et al. [8]
ruled out no(k)-time algorithms under the exponential time hypothesis (ETH), although the precise
parameterized hardness class of the problem remained unresolved.
At the same time, under standard complexity assumptions, k-MSR is known to admit a PTAS,
which in turn implies the existence of a parameterized approximation scheme1 (PAS), even though
no explicit algorithm is currently known. Thus, from the perspective of parameterized approxima-
bility, the main remaining question is whether k-MSR admits an efficient parameterized approxi-
mation scheme (EPAS), namely a PAS whose running time is of the form f(k, ε)poly(n).
Question 1. Does k-MSR admit an EPAS?
More broadly, while recent lower bounds establish parameterized intractability under ETH, the
precise parameterized hardness class of the problem remained unknown.
Our first result resolves these questions. We show that k-MSR is W[2]-hard, thereby pinpointing
its location in the W-hierarchy.2
In particular, this rules out exact FPT algorithms under the
standard assumption that W[2] ̸= FPT, which is strictly weaker than ETH. Moreover, we rule out
EPAS, thereby answering Question 1 in the negative and essentially completing the parameterized
approximability landscape of k-MSR.

the gap between the yes and no instances is sufficient to rule out an EPAS under the assumption
that W[2] ̸= FPT.
We next turn to the design of FPT approximation algorithms for k-MSR. As discussed earlier,
the FPT framework has been highly successful not only in the unconstrained setting but also in
a wide range of constrained variants. For k-MSR, recent work has developed FPT approximation
algorithms under capacities [25, 36], fairness constraints [17, 15, 5, 8], and outliers [40, 28].
While these constraints are well motivated by real-world applications, they introduce significant
challenges in enforcing feasibility. More importantly, the rapidly growing variety of such constraints
makes it increasingly difficult to design algorithms tailored to each individual setting. For instance,
there are already more than a dozen notions of fairness constraints (see [1] and references therein),
and new variants continue to emerge.
Mergeable constraints.
To address this challenge, Arutyunova and Schmidt [3] introduced
a clean and general framework of mergeable constraints.
Informally, a constraint is said to be
mergeable if merging any two clusters of a feasible solution results in another feasible (possibly
larger) cluster.
This abstraction captures a broad range of commonly studied constraints [15],
including the following:
• Balanced Clustering [13]: Points are colored either red or blue, and each cluster must contain
equal numbers of red and blue points.
• Pairwise Fair Clustering [18]: Points are colored red or blue, and each cluster must maintain
a bounded ratio between the number of blue and red points, lying within [1/t, t]. This is also
known as (t, k)-fair clustering.
• Pairwise Exact with Multiple Colors [42]: Points have multiple colors, and each cluster must
preserve the same proportion of each color.
• ℓ-Diversity Clustering [22, 6]: Points have multiple colors, and no color constitute more than
a 1/ℓfraction of any cluster.
• Fair Representational [10, 11]: Points have multiple colors, and each color must lie within
specified lower and upper bounds in every cluster.
• (α, β)-Fair Clustering [11, 6]: A generalization of fair representational clustering in which a
point may have multiple colors simultaneously. It captures all of the above constraints.
• Private Clustering [42]: Each cluster must contain at least L points; this is also known as
clustering with a uniform lower bound.
In their work, Carta et al. [15] studied mergeable constraints for k-MSR and designed a (6 + ε)
FPT-approximation for k-MSR under mergeable constraints, yielding a unified guarantee across
all these settings.
They also obtained improved (3 + ε) approximations for special cases such

At a high level, our algorithm follows the blueprint of Bandyapadhyay and Chen [5], but in-
troduces key new ingredients. Their approach first computes an unconstrained clustering, then
expands the clusters, and finally merges them to obtain feasibility. In other words, it constructs
an unconstrained solution and subsequently projects it onto a feasible one, incurring losses in both
steps. In contrast, our algorithm operates entirely within the mergeable-constraints framework,
thereby avoiding this projection step altogether. This structural difference, together with a more
refined charging scheme and a careful analysis, leads to an improved approximation factor.
Our final result further improves the approximation to (2+ε), assuming access to an assignment
subroutine for the underlying constraint. Such a subroutine takes a set of at most k balls and assigns
each point to the center of a ball containing it, while respecting the constraint, provided such an
assignment exists.
Theorem 3. Suppose there exists an assignment subroutine AA with running time T(AA). Then
there is an algorithm that computes a (2+ε)-approximation for k-MSR under a mergeable constraint
in time 2O(k log(k/ε)) · n2 · T(AA).
This result matches the best known FPT approximation factor for unconstrained k-MSR [17],
where the assignment step is trivial. Moreover, it improves the best known guarantees for (α, β)-
fair k-MSR, which captures a broad class of fair clustering problems, improving upon the (3 + ε)
bound of [15] for several settings. To this end, we design an FPT-time assignment subroutine for
(α, β)-fair clustering based on ideas from [6].
Corollary 4. There is an algorithm for (α, β)-fair k-MSR that, for every ε > 0, computes a
(2+ε)-approximation in time 2O(k log(k/ε)) ·poly(n). In particular, this yields (2+ε)-approximation

private clustering, improving the previous factor of (3 + ε) [15].
Corollary 5. There exists an FPT (2 + ε)-approximation algorithm for private k-MSR running in
time 2O(k log(k/ε)) · poly(n).

Hardness implications and limitations of prior reductions.
Both of our hardness results—
W[2]-hardness and the non-existence of an EPAS—extend to the mergeable-constraint setting, since
the unconstrained version trivially satisfies the mergeability property. A result of [5] claims to rule
out algorithms with running time f(k) no(k) for fair-representational k-MSR, which would in turn

reduction in [5] contains a subtle flaw that invalidates the claimed hardness. In this light, our result
not only restores the intended hardness conclusion but also strengthens it by ruling out an EPAS.

Further Related Work
Polynomial-time regime.
Behsaz and Salavatipour [9] showed that k-MSR can be solved ex-
actly in polynomial time on unweighted graphs, provided that zero-radius clusters are disallowed. In
Euclidean metrics of constant dimension, [30] gave a polynomial-time exact algorithm, and showed
that the problem is NP-hard in metrics of constant doubling dimension. They further established
NP-hardness for shortest-path metrics induced by weighted planar graphs, while also presenting a
QPTAS for general metrics. Moreover, even when the locations of the optimal centers are known,
computing a valid assignment of points to centers remains NP-hard [30, 24]. For k-MSR under
pairwise fair constraints, [41] recently obtained an O(1)-approximation in polynomial time.
FPT regime.
For (unconstrained) k-MSR, [7] showed an EPAS in Euclidean metrics. When pa-
rameterizing by the treewidth of the underlying metric graph, [23] obtained an XP-time algorithm.
Chen et al. [17] obtained a (2+ε)-approximation for k-MSR in general metrics. Their approach
can be viewed as an extension of the classical algorithm of [35] for k-center: repeatedly selecting
a point among the remaining points, guessing the radius of its optimal cluster, and removing all
points within twice that radius. They also extend this approach to fair and matroid constraints,
incurring an additional loss in the approximation factor.
Under capacity constraints, the best known FPT-approximation factor is due to [25].
Liu
et al. [40] studied an extension of k-MSR with outliers, called colorful k-MSR, which imposes
separate budgets on the number of outliers for each color class, and obtained a (7 + ε) FPT-
approximation.
Recently, [28] considered k-MSR under simultaneous fairness (on centers) and
outlier constraints, and presented a (3 + ε) FPT-approximation that extends to objectives defined
by monotone symmetric norms of radii, beyond the sum-of-radii objective.
For mergeable constraints, [23] designed an EPAS for k-MSR in Euclidean metrics of arbitrary
dimension. This was subsequently extended to metrics of constant doubling dimension by [8].

a mergeable constraint. Towards this, in Section 3.1, we design an FPT algorithm (GBC) that

3.2
From Respecting Cover to Feasible Cover
In this section, we show how to transform a ball cover into a feasible cover using an approximate
radius profile. Since we do not know how to efficiently verify whether a given ball cover is a feasible
cover, our algorithm instead returns a family of covers that is guaranteed to contain a feasible one.
Theorem 8. There exists an algorithm that, given an instance I of k-MSR under mergeable con-
straints, a k-ball cover B for I, and a radius profile R′, runs in time 2O(k log k) · n and returns a
family F of k-ball covers such that the following holds:
For every optimal clustering Π∗for I with radius profile R∗, if B is a Π∗-respecting cover
obtained by the greedy ball cover (GBC) procedure using R′, and R′ is an ε-approximation of R∗,

4.1
Limitations of Previous Hardness Result
We briefly explain the issue with the lower-bound construction of Bandyapadhyay and Chen [5].
Their reduction is from Dominating Set. Given a graph G, the construction augments G by adding
two sets of k vertices and connecting each such vertex to every vertex of G. We refer to these as
auxiliary vertex sets, and to their elements as auxiliary vertices. The groups are defined as follows:
one group for each auxiliary vertex set and one group corresponding to the original vertex set of
G. The proportions are chosen so that, for each auxiliary group, αi = 1/(n + 3) and βi = 1, while
for the original group, α = 1/3 and β = 1, where n denotes the total number of vertices in the
constructed k-MSR instance. The metric is given by the shortest-path distances in the resulting
graph.
In the yes-instance, there is a feasible k-MSR solution of cost k, obtained by selecting the
vertices corresponding to a dominating set of G. For the no-instance, the argument in [5] relies on
the observation that singleton clusters violate the fairness constraints, and therefore concludes that
zero-radius clusters cannot occur. This is then used to argue that any feasible solution to k-MSR
can be used to construct a dominating set of size k.
The issue is that, while singleton clusters are indeed infeasible, this does not preclude the
existence of zero-radius clusters. In particular, empty clusters satisfy the fairness constraints and
have zero radius. Exploiting this, one can construct a low-cost solution as follows: choose a center
from one of the auxiliary sets, which—via the added edges—covers all points within distance 2.
This forms a large cluster that satisfies the fairness constraints. The remaining k −1 clusters can
then be opened at arbitrary vertices but left empty (their centers are assigned to the large cluster).
17


## Page 19


The resulting solution remains feasible and has total cost at most 2, contradicting the intended
hardness gap.
More generally, this highlights a limitation of reductions that introduce auxiliary vertices en-
abling shortcuts in the metric. Under mergeable constraints, such constructions can collapse into
a single large cluster, while the remaining clusters can be left empty without violating feasibility,
thereby yielding artificially low-cost solutions.
