On MMS, APS and XOS
Uriel Feige∗
Vadim Grinberg†
Abstract
We consider allocations of a set of m indivisible goods to n agents of equal entitlements
that have valuations from the class XOS. A previous sequence of works showed allocations
that obtain an α-approximation for the maximin share (MMS), for values of α that gradu-
ally approach 1
4 from below (the currently known ratio is
4
17). In this work we attempt to
obtain ratios better than 1
4, and manage to do so for sufficiently large n. Our methodology
is to first investigate the gap between the anyprice share (APS) and the MMS when all
agents have the same XOS valuations, for which we design an allocation algorithm and
prove that each agent receives at least α > 11
40 times the APS. Then, we derive inspiration
from this algorithm, and modify it so that it applies also when agents have different XOS
valuations. Using this modified version, we show that for some sufficiently large n0, there
is an α-MMS allocation (in fact, an α-APS allocation) for every n ≥n0.

Introduction
We consider fair allocations of m indivisible goods to n agents. Let M denote the set of m
items to allocate, and let the set of agents be N := [n]. Every agent i ∈N has a valuation
function vi defined on the subsets of M. We assume that vi satisfies vi(∅) = 0, and for every
S1 ⊆S2 it holds vi(S1) ≤vi(S2), i.e vi is monotone and non-decreasing. Every agent i may
have different entitlement bi > 0 to the goods M, and all entitlements bi for i ∈[n] satisfy
Pn
i=1 bi = 1. In this work, we shall always have bi = 1/n for all agents i ∈N.
An allocation of items M to agents N is a partition (P1, . . . , Pn) of M into n disjoint sets,
where agent i “receives” the set Pi.
We would like this allocation to be fair, for a certain
definition of fairness. In this work, we will focus on a share-based notion of fairness called the
maximin share, or MMS for short.
Definition 1.1. Let Pn be the set of all partitions (P1, . . . , Pn) of items M into n disjoint sets.
For an agent i with valuation vi and entitlement bi = 1
n, her maximin share is
MMS(M, vi, 1/n) =
max
(P1,...,Pn)∈Pn min
j∈[n] vi(Pj).
This notion of fairness was first considered in [Bud11]. A partition (P1, . . . , Pn) of M is
called an MMS-allocation if for every agent i ∈N it holds vi(Pi) ≥MMS(M, vi, 1/n). That
is, each agent i values the bundle Pi given to her at least as high as her maximin share. While
in general one may study MMS allocations for general monotone valuations vi, it is common
practice to classify vi according to the complement free hierarchy, first introduced in [LLN06].
Definition 1.2. Let v be an arbitrary monotone non-decreasing valuation, satisfying v(∅) = 0.
• v is additive if v(S) = P
e∈S v(e) for every set S ⊆M.
• v is submodular if v(S1 ∪{e}) −v(S1) ≥v(S2 ∪{e}) −v(S2) for every item e ∈M and
sets S1 ⊆S2 ⊆M.
• v is XOS if there exist t additive valuations v1, . . . , vt, such that v(S) = maxj∈[t] vj(S) for
every set S ⊆M. This class is equivalent to fractionally subadditive valuations ([Fei09]).
• v is subadditive if v(S1) + v(S2) ≥v(S1 ∪S2) for all sets S1, S2 ⊆M.
As was shown in [LLN06], each of these valuation classes is strictly contained in the one that
follows it. In this work, we will consider only valuations vi that are XOS.
In general, MMS-allocations need not exist, even if the valuations are additive [KPW18].
Because of this, it is reasonable to consider allocations in which every agent receives a fraction
of her MMS. In particular, for value α ∈(0, 1], we say that allocation (P1, . . . , Pn) is an α-MMS
allocation, if for every agent i ∈N it holds vi(Pi) ≥α·MMS(M, vi, 1/n). In this work, we focus
on showing the existence of α-MMS allocations for XOS agents, for certain values of α. Below,
we give a brief overview of known results on MMS for XOS agents. As for works on other classes
of valuations, we refer the reader to Section 3.
The allocations achieving α-MMS for XOS valuations were first considered in [GHS+22], who

1.1
Our results
We present a completely new approach to finding α-MMS allocations for XOS agents, which
allows us to improve over the previously known bounds as long as the number n of agents is
sufficiently large. Specifically, we prove the following theorem.
Theorem 1.1. There exists a constant α >
11
40 and number nα, such that for every n ≥nα
the following holds. Consider a fair allocation instance with items M and agents N, |N| = n,
where for every i ∈[n] valuation vi is XOS. Then, there exists an allocation (P1, . . . , Pn) of
M to N such that every agent i ∈N receives at least α-fraction of her MMS, i.e vi(Pi) ≥
α · MMS(M, vi, 1/n).
As nα grows, the value of the constant α converges to a solution to the equation 2(12α −
3) ln(3α) = (1 −3α)(3 ln 3 −4), which gives α ≈0.2767738. We alert the reader that to achieve
α ≥1/4, our proof uses nα > 100000.

prices, and the other based on fractional partitions. We present the latter definition, as this is
the definition used by our proofs.

Theorem 1.2. Let α, nα be as in Theorem 1.1.
For every n ≥nα, the following holds.
Consider a fair allocation instance with a set M of items, and a set N of n agents with equal
entitlements, where for every i ∈[n], her valuation vi is XOS. Then, there exists an allocation
(P1, . . . , Pn) of M to N such that every agent i ∈N receives at least α-fraction of her APS, i.e
vi(Pi) ≥α · APS(M, vi, 1/n).
In addition, we show that for every n and any XOS valuation v, the multiplicative gap
between the values of the APS and the MMS is less than 40/11.
Theorem 1.3. Let α be as in Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.2. For any n, M and any XOS
valuation v it holds that MMS(M, v, 1/n) ≥α · APS(M, v, 1/n).
Our approach for Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.2 originated from the work on the proof for
Theorem 1.3. We provide an overview of the proofs of our results in Section 2. For convenience,
in non-rigorous parts of our exposition, we may describe the approach either in terms of MMS
or in terms of APS. However, all the rigorous technical proofs all apply to APS.
The allocations for Theorem 1.1, Theorem 1.2 and Theorem 1.3 can be obtained in
polynomial time, as long as the MMS/APS partitions of all agents are provided in advance.
Computing the values of the MMS and the APS (and corresponding partitions) for XOS valua-
tions is APX-hard. This hardness result holds even for the more restricted class of submodular
valuations (see [BUF23]). Nevertheless, the value of the APS and an associated fractional parti-
tion can be computed in polynomial time for any monotone (including XOS) valuation v, if the
algorithm has access to the valuations not just via value queries, but also via what we refer to
as anyprice queries. The APS has two equivalent definitions. One is Definition 1.3. The other is
price-based, and postulates that when items have non-negative prices (scaled so that they sum
up to 1), then an agent with entitlement b (b = 1
n in our case) is entitled to any set of items
of her choice, of price at most b. In this context, the following anyprice queries are natural.
The query specifies nonnegative prices to the items, summing up to 1. The reply is the set S of
items that the agent desires, namely, the one maximizing v(S), subject to the sum of prices of
items in S not exceeding b. If we allow both value queries and anyprice queries, the value of the
APS can be computed using polynomially many queries (using the ellipsoid algorithm), and so
can an associated fractional partition (using techniques involving linear programming duality).
Consequently, given access to value queries and anyprice queries, our randomized allocation al-
gorithm can be implemented so that its expected running time is polynomial. Further details
on this aspect are omitted.

Overview of our proof
Throughout this overview all valuations are assumed to be XOS, and ρ serves as a generic
name representing some unspecified constant, strictly larger than 1
4.
Our goal is to show the existence of ρ-MMS allocations. We break this goal into two main
steps. One is the design of an allocation algorithm for which we have reasons to hope that it
5


## Page 6


produces ρ-MMS allocations. (At this stage, we do not require this algorithm to be efficient,
as we are mostly interested in the existence of ρ-MMS allocations, and treat the complexity of
computing them as a secondary issue.) The other is to analyse the designed algorithm and prove
that indeed it produces ρ-MMS allocations.
Our methodology for achieving the above two steps is to first change the problem as follows.
Recall that the currently best approximation ratio with respect to the MMS, namely
4
17, holds
also with respect to the APS. Hence it seems reasonable to expect that if there are ρ-MMS
allocations, then there also are ρ-APS allocations. Now we greatly simplify the goal of designing
ρ-APS allocations, by considering only allocation instances in which all agents have the same
XOS valuation function. We refer to this setting as that of identical valuations. Observe that
identical valuations makes no sense if we wish to design ρ-MMS allocations, because the MMS
partition with respect to the valuation function gives an MMS allocation, showing that ρ = 1.
However, for APS, identical valuations do make sense, because the APS fractional partition need
not be a legal allocation (bundles might overlap). Technically, the identical valuation setting is
equivalent to proving that for XOS valuations, the multiplicative gap between the APS and the
MMS is 1
ρ < 4. (Prior to our work, it was only known that this gap is at most 17
4 > 4.)
Handling the identical valuations setting already requires the development of new techniques.
The techniques developed in this process help overcome some of the difficulties also in the general
case in which valuations need not be identical, at least at the level of providing inspiration on how
to address the general case. This allows us to achieve the task of handling the general case using
two “leaps” of intermediate difficulty (one is that of developing techniques that provably handle
identical valuations, the other is developing only the additional techniques that are needed in
order to extend the results to the general case), rather than one very big leap.
2.1
ρ-APS allocations with identical valuations
Here we consider the setting in which there is an XOS valuation v with APS(M, v, 1/n) = 1,
and we need to prove the existence of a partition A1, . . . , An of M, with v(Aj) ≥ρ for every
j. (This is a reformulation of the problem of producing ρ-APS allocations when agents have
identical XOS valuations.) We refer to bundles of value at least ρ as acceptable. As is standard
in related settings, we may assume that no single item has value above ρ, because otherwise
we can give this item to one of the agents without decreasing the APS value for the remaining
agents.
We propose to use a greedy algorithm for the above problem. First, based on v, we set up a
potential set-function β, with the following properties.
1. β is monotone, namely, β(S) ≤β(S′) if S ⊂S′.
2. β(M) ≥1.
3. If β(S) ≥ρ, then S is an acceptable bundle.
4. Uniform and monotone lower bounds on transitions. There is some (non-increasing) func-
tion f : [ρ, ∞] →(0, 1] such that for every acceptable set S, there is an acceptable subset
T ⊆S satisfying β(S \ T) ≥(1 −f(β(S)))β(S). That is, the value β(S \ T) can be lower
bounded only as a function of β(S) (and beyond that, independently of the contents of S
itself).
Our greedy algorithm has n rounds. In each round r, a set Mr of yet unallocated items
is still available (with M1 = M). In round r we greedily select an acceptable Ar ⊂Mr that
maximizes β(Mr \ Ar). Then we update Mr+1 = Mr \ Ar, and proceed to the next round.

Inspired by arguments used in [GHS+22] and [FG25], for every S, we define DS to be the
distribution over acceptable bundles T ⊆S that minimizes pS, where pS is defined as follows.
Let p(e) for item e denote PT∼DS[e ∈T]. That is, p(e) is the probability that item e is included

For a given value t ≥ρ, we define pt to be the maximum pS over all those S that satisfy
β(S) ≥t. Observe that pt above is monotone non-increasing (the larger t is, the fewer S there
are that we can maximize over). That is, as β gets smaller, the respective pt grows.
For a given S with β(S) = t, if we select at random an acceptable set T from the distribution

following observation, which we prove rigorously in Lemma A.4.

for which the potential is no less than β(S \ T) ≥(1 −pt) · β(S).
This serves as property 4 for our choice of β.

rather by random sampling of an acceptable bundle from her associated distribution DMr at the

hope is that for every possible input allocation instance, the random process will offer positive
probability of succeeding, implying that a ρ-APS allocation exists.
Unfortunately, we do not know how to analyse the randomized algorithm when the number
of agents n is small. However, we propose a concrete plan (which we will successfully implement)
for how to analyse it when n is sufficiently large. Our plan is based on the known fact that a
sum of a collection of many random variables that are independent and bounded is concentrated
around its expectation. When n is large, the changes to the potential βi of agent i along the run
of the randomized algorithm are basically a sum of many small random changes, one change per
round of the algorithm. Hence we may expect at every given round that the potential functions
of all active agents will have roughly the same value. If these values were exactly the same,
this would mimic the analysis for the case of identical valuations, for which we could prove that

big difference to the analysis.

fluctuations from the former path do not start off a ripple effect that causes the latter path
to diverge greatly from the former path. The need to prove the attractor property places
an upper bound on how large c can be in the no large intersections property.
2. Removing the assumption that there are no large intersections. Without this assumption,
we lose the concentration results, and the analysis completely breaks down. To overcome
this difficulty, we modify the randomized algorithm, so that it gives special treatment to
sets that violate the large intersection property. Note that all sets in an MMS partition
of an agent might violate this property, each with respect to a different other agent.
Consequently, the treatment is “special” not in the sense of being applied only rarely, but
rather in the sense of differing from the treatment given to sets that do not violate the
large intersection property. Our modifications work only if c in the no large intersections
property is sufficiently large, as otherwise their effect on ρ becomes larger than what we
can tolerate. Luckily, there are “middle ground” values for c that allow us to both handle
the “easy” special cases, and to remove the assumption of no large intersections.
3. Removing the assumption that there are no large items. In most algorithms for finding
approximate MMS allocations (including [GHS+22]), this is the easiest step. If vi(e) ≥ρ
for some agent i and item e, simply give item e to agent i, remove i (as item e suffices from
her MMS approximation), and the MMS of the remaining agents in the remaining instance
does not decrease. Hence the allocation problem with n agents reduces to a seemingly easier
one with n −1 agents. However, in our case, we cannot afford to reduce the number of
agents, as our analysis requires large n. This seems problematic, because when there are
many large items, each of value 1 to all agents, it is without loss of generality that each
large item is allocated to a single agent, and the number of agents is reduced dramatically.
So is the situation hopeless? Not really, because we may choose the identity of the agents
that receive the large items. This way, even though we remain with an instance with few

Allowing for large intersections
We define the damage that a bundle B acceptable for an agent i can do to the other agents
as the sum of decrease in β values of all other agents caused by the removal of all items of B.
Using an appropriate choice of parameters, we classify bundles into three classes, those doing
little damage (at most c), those doing medium damage, and those doing large damage (greater
than D). For our choice of parameters c and D, those bundles that do little damage do not form
a problem, as they satisfy the no large intersection assumption. Those bundles that do large
damage also do not form a problem – it can be shown that they are rare, and simply not allowing
the agent to choose them does not significantly affect the run of the randomized algorithm.
The main difficulty is those bundles that do medium damage. There might be too many of
them, and so we cannot simply forbid choosing them. But, if they are chosen, some other agents
suffer a large drop in their β value, and we no longer have the martingale concentration results
that we need for our analysis.
Our fix for this is to depart from the greedy paradigm which states that once an agent i gets
an acceptable bundle B, this is an irreversible decision. Instead, we allow agents who suffered
large damage by the allocation of B to “steal” back items from B, if the need arises. To make

of B is still acceptable for i (for a new approximation ratio ρ′ that is smaller from ρ by a term
that tends to 0 as n grows).
