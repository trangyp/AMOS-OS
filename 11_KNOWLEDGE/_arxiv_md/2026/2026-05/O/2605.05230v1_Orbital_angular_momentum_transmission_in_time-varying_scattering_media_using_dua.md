ABSTRACT 
Orbital angular momentum (OAM) has been regarded as a potential dimension for optical communication and related fields. Despite several 
studies, the transmission of OAM beams through time-varying scattering media remains a challenge. In this paper, we report a method for 
OAM transmission through time-varying scattering media using dual orthogonal polarization channels, in which one channel carries a perfect 
vortex beam (PVB) carrying data, and the other serves as a reference plane wave. By calculating the second-order cross-correlation of speckle 
patterns generated by the PVB and the plane wave, the original data can be decoded. It is also shown that multiplexed PVBs carrying specific 
topological charges can produce interference patterns in the acquired cross-correlation images, thereby greatly enhancing transmission 
capacity. Experimental results show that the proposed method can transmit at least 6 bits per beam with 100% accuracy. Moreover, the 
proposed method is highly robust to variations in the receiving end's position in both radial and axial directions. The proposed method enables 
the practical deployment of OAM transmission in realistic optical communication and related applications.  
 
 
I. INTRODUCTION 
      Light, as an electromagnetic wave, carries both energy and 
momentum. Recently, optical beams carrying orbital angular 
momentum (OAM) have been studied as a potential means to 
broaden the capacity of optical communication systems.1 In theory, 
OAM can carry an infinite number of states, indicating enormous 
potential for optical communication.2, 3 
      However, in practical communication systems, it is inevitable 
that the transmitted beam will be interfered with by the 
transmission medium, such as scattering media and scaling factors.4 
The transmission through the scattering media can be further 
divided into two categories: transmission by compensating the 
distorted light5-8 and transmission with strongly scattered light.4, 9, 10 
Current typical methods for OAM transmission in strong scattering 
include the scattering transmission matrix methods,9 the vortex 
memory effect methods,11 and cross-correlation methods.12 
      The scattering transmission matrix-based methods recover the 
optical field by establishing transmission matrices, then perform 
inverse transformations to demultiplex OAM components from the 
light field.9, 13-15 This method provides great robustness against 
strong scattering, as well as high transmission capacity. The vortex 
memory effect method stores several speckle patterns generated by 
different OAM beams in advance for reference, rather than 
precisely measuring the entire scattering transmission matrix, 
thereby simplifying the calibration process.11 However, these 
methods need calibration to acquire the scattering transmission 
matrix or reference speckle patterns in advance. Thus, their 
applications in time-varying scattering scenarios are limited. 
       Cross-correlation methods focus on extracting information by 
calculating the cross-correlation of two speckle patterns.12, 16, 17 
These methods are based on the statistical properties of the 
distribution of fully scattered speckles.17-20 By measuring certain 
statistical parameters, the original information can be retrieved 
from optical speckles.21, 22 In 2021, Vanitha et al.12 mathematically 
proved that the cross-correlation image between two speckle 
patterns generated by two perfect vortex beams (PVBs) with 
different topological charges (TCs) would exhibit a Bessel function 
pattern. In 2024, Ma et al.16 proposed leveraging this phenomenon 
to achieve OAM-mode sorting and transmission. The advantage of 
this method is that it requires only one speckle intensity pattern for 
reference; thus, it has great potential for application in time-varying 
scattering scenarios. However, a change in distance on the receiver 
end would introduce a scaling effect on the acquired cross-
correlation images. Thus, their method requires a fixed camera 
axial distance during continuous transmission. In 2026, Zhang et al. 
23 proposed multiplexing two TCs to transmit OAM-encoded data. 
They sequentially transmit two specific multiplexed OAM beams 
and capture the corresponding speckle patterns. The acquired cross-
correlation image displays petal-like patterns. In this way, they can 
transmit 6-bit data with 95.8% overall accuracy using deep learning. 
However, the transmission accuracy of several states is lower than 
60%, limiting its application in practical systems. Furthermore, 
training deep learning needs a pre-acquired training set and incurs 
additional computational overhead.

Recent research on OAM transmission has opened the 
possibility of introducing vector fields into the transmission 
process.24-27 During the transmission through specific or thin 
scattering media, the polarization states of the incident light could 
remain unchanged.28 Thus, it is possible to utilize orthogonal 
polarization states as different channels, including circular 
polarization29 and linear polarization.25, 30, 31 This allows the 
simultaneous transmission of dual orthogonally polarized beams 
using polarized detectors. 
      In this paper, a dual-polarization enabled OAM beam 
transmission method via time-varying scattering media is proposed. 
It is shown that the mutual coherence function (MCF), which 
describes the correlation between two speckle light fields, also 
carries a vortex phase and can be interfered with. By multiplexing 
PVBs carrying specific TCs, the MCF interference can be observed 
by calculating the cross-correlation of the speckle patterns 
generated by the reference and signal beams. Based on this, OAM 
transmission leveraging MCF interference can be achieved. To 
demonstrate this, we combine the reference and signal beams into a 
dual-polarization beam and use a division-of-focal-plane (DoFP) 
polarization camera to collect speckle patterns through the time-
varying scattering media. Using the proposed setup, 6 bits of data 
can be transmitted per beam by multiplexing 4 PVBs, with no error 
occurring. The results show that the proposed method can achieve 
OAM data transmission using strongly scattered light and is robust 
to variations in detector position shifting, demonstrating great 
potential for optical communication and related fields.

II. METHOD 
A. Vortex phase in MCF 
      In ideal free-space scenarios, the transmission of OAM beams 
can be modeled as Fresnel diffraction from the original beam. 
However, in practical scenarios, the scattering effect from the 
transmission medium is usually unavoidable. Luckily, the 
information contained in the original beam would not be lost even 
after strong scattering. The strong scattering effect can be regarded 
as an unknown converter that projects the OAM space onto another 
measurable light field.11 Consequently, we can develop specific 
methods to retrieve the information carried by original non-
scattered OAM beams. Here, we take PVB as an example. PVB 
describes a set of OAM beams whose intensity distributions are 
independent of the TC.  
      Fig. 1 illustrates the process of PVB transmission through time-
varying scattering media. A plane wave serves as the reference 
beam, which is modulated to be S-polarized, while the multiplexed 
PVB carrying specific TCs serves as the signal beam, which is 
modulated to be P-polarized. The S and P light are combined to 
form a dual-polarized beam, which is then scattered by a rotating 
ground glass (RGG), which serves as a time-varying scattering 
medium. After propagation of the distance z , the S-polarized and 
P-polarized speckle patterns generated by the plane wave and PVB, 
respectively, are measured by a detector. The cross-correlation 
image between these two speckle patterns can then be calculated. 
By extracting features from the cross-correlation image, the 
information carried by the original PVB can be recovered. 
      We now start by proving that PVB carrying a single non-zero 
TC would result in a vortex phase and a ring-like pattern in the 
second-order cross-correlation.

      An experimental setup used to verify the proposed method is 
shown in Fig. 2(a). The laser (MGL-III-532, 532 nm, 200 mW) is 
first expanded and collimated, and the polarization angle of the 
beam incident on the SLM is rotated by the half-wave plate to 
adjust the intensities of S and P light. The spatial light modulator

digital hologram to generate the desired light field on the first-order 
diffraction of the modulated beam.37-40 Note that only the P light 
can be modulated by the SLM, while the S light cannot. Thus, the 
first-order diffraction of the P light carries the desired light field, 
while the S light carries a plane wave that has not been modulated. 
The Lens 1 and Lens 2 (focal length 200 mm) form a 4f system, 
while a Mach-Zehnder interferometer is put inside the 4f system to 
combine the S light and the first-order diffraction of the P light. 
The combined beam is then incident on the rotating ground glass 
(600 grit, rated speed of 0.25 rpm) and scattered. A DoFP 
polarization camera (MER2-503-36U3M POL, Daheng Image 
Vision) is placed 300 mm after the RGG to capture scattered 
speckle patterns at different polarization angles. RGG has no 
observable effect on the polarization state. (see Note 2 of the 
Supplementary material for details)

IV. CONCLUSION 
      In this paper, a dual-polarization enabled OAM-based 
transmission method via time-varying scattering media is proposed. 
By combining a linearly polarized multiplexed PVB with a plane 
wave orthogonally polarized to the PVB, serving as the signal and 
reference beams, respectively, 6 bits of data can be transmitted 
through time-varying scattering media per beam. Data decoding 
can be achieved by calculating the second-order cross-correlation 
between the signal and reference speckle patterns. The proposed 
method reaches 100% accuracy in transmitting an image with 4096 
6-bit data. The proposed method also demonstrates high robustness 
to the movement of the receiving end and varying beam sizes. The 
proposed method holds great potential in OAM transmission and 
optical communication through scattering media.
