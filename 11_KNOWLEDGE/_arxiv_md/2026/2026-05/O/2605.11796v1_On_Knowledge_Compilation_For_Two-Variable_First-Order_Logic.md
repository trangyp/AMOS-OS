Abstract
Knowledge compilation transforms logical theories into circuit representations that support efficient
reasoning. We study this problem for propositional groundings of FO2, the two-variable fragment of
first-order logic over finite domains. Given an FO2 sentence and a domain of size n, its grounding
yields a propositional theory over ground atoms. We ask whether such theories admit compact
representations in DNNF-based and related knowledge compilation languages, and whether these can
be constructed efficiently, both with respect to the domain size n for a fixed sentence. We show first
that compact compilation is impossible in general: there exists an FO2 sentence whose grounding
over a domain of size n requires DNNF size 2Ω(n). On the positive side, we develop a two-stage
compiler that exploits the symmetries inherent in the propositional groundings of FO2 sentences.
It branches on unary and binary types rather than individual ground atoms, in a similar spirit to
lifted inferences for probabilistic relational models. Moreover, it optimizes the compilation process
by efficiently identifying and caching residual subproblems that are equivalent with respect to future
extensions. Experiments show the practical efficiency of our approach, which often produces smaller
circuits and compiles faster than straightforward grounding-based baselines.

Introduction
Knowledge compilation (KC) studies how to transform a logical theory into a target repres-
entation that supports efficient downstream reasoning. Prominent target languages include
the decomposable negation normal form (DNNF), which admits tractable queries such as
satisfiability and conditioning, and its deterministic variant d-DNNF, which additionally
supports efficient model counting and related inference tasks [12, 8].
In this paper, we study knowledge compilation for groundings of first-order theories,
focusing on the two-variable fragment FO2 over finite domains. Given a fixed FO2 sentence
Γ and a finite domain ∆, grounding Γ over ∆yields a propositional theory whose variables
correspond to ground atoms. We ask two basic questions: whether these grounded theories
admit compact representations in DNNF-based languages, and whether such representations

Answering these questions has immediate practical value. For instance, d-DNNF admits
linear-time conditioning [12] and model sampling [15, 20], which naturally enables exact
conditional sampling to complete partially observed relational data while strictly respecting
universal and existential constraints. Furthermore, when the compiled circuit is organized as
a structured d-DNNF with respect to a fixed variable tree, it can be composed with other
structured circuits over the same variables, enabling modular integration with probabilistic
or neurosymbolic architectures [3, 2, 1]. Particularly, neurosymbolic learning systems usually
reason over complex relational domains, and their symbolic knowledge bases are inherently
specified in first-order logic (FOL) rather than propositional logic. Targeting the knowledge
compilation of the FO2 fragment, a well-studied subset of FOL with balanced expressivity
and decidability [14], offers a vital path to hybrid logic and learning pipelines at scale.
Knowledge compilation for first-order theories differs from both lifted inference [30, 4, 17]
and propositional compilation [11, 19]. Typically, lifted inference targets first-order theories
and performs inference without grounding. For instance, lifted inference for the full FO2
fragment is tractable in the symmetric setting, where the weight of models is invariant
under permutations of the domain elements, and it does not support conditioning on binary
evidence that breaks the symmetries [29]. In contrast, propositional compilation targets
propositional theories, where conditioning on arbitrary evidence is supported, but the input
theory is already grounded, so the symmetry and structural regularity present in the first-
order description are no longer explicit. Though there are a few works on exploiting the
implicit symmetries in propositional model counting [31, 27], how to leverage the symmetries
in knowledge compilation remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this paper lies between these
two viewpoints: we start from a first-order specification, but aim to compile its propositional
grounding into a standard propositional target language.
Our contributions are both negative and positive. On the negative side, we show that
polynomial-size compilation is impossible in general: there exists an FO2 sentence whose
grounding over a domain of size n requires DNNF size 2Ω(n). On the positive side, we develop
a specialized two-stage compiler for FO2 sentences. The compiler exploits the fact that FO2
models over finite domains can be organized via finitely many unary and binary types, and
therefore branches on types rather than on individual ground atoms. We further introduce
an optimization based on equivalent residual subproblems, allowing the compiler to reuse
compiled subcircuits when different contexts admit the same set of future completions. Our
experiments on several benchmark families suggest that this approach can produce smaller
circuits than straightforward grounding-based baselines.
The paper is structured as follows. Section 3 recalls the necessary background. Section 4
proves the exponential lower bound for FO2 compilation. Section 5 presents the two-stage
compilation procedure for FO2. Section 6 develops the optimization techniques, and Section 7
reports an empirical evaluation.

Related Work
Knowledge compilation studies how to trade succinctness for tractable queries by compiling
logical theories into target languages with polynomial-time support for selected operations.
The compilation map in [12] remains the standard reference for positioning such languages.
Among them, DNNF and d-DNNF are central because they strike a useful balance between
succinctness and tractable querying [8]. Other languages include OBDDs, FBDDs, and
SDDs [10], which are more restrictive but support additional queries such as equivalence

into variants of DNNF has been studied extensively, both from the algorithmic perspective [7,
11, 25, 19, 16] and from the perspective of lower bounds and separations [5, 13, 24], often
using communication-complexity techniques [6].
Moving beyond propositional theories, first-order knowledge compilation and lifted prob-
abilistic inference introduce lifted circuit representations, such as FO-d-DNNF, that operate
directly on first-order structure and support lifted inference [29, 30, 17]. These approaches
differ from ours both in the target representation and in the intended reasoning regime.
Our goal is to compile an FO2 sentence and a finite domain into a standard propositional
d-DNNF circuit, thereby retaining the query support of propositional d-DNNF.
Our work is also related to recent results on algorithmic tasks over FO2 models, such as
symmetric weighted first-order model counting [4], lifted first-order model sampling [32], and
model enumeration for two-variable logic [22]. These works show that FO2 admits unusually
strong algorithmic structure for counting, sampling, and enumeration. Our focus is different:
we study compilation into DNNF-based circuit languages, including both lower bounds on
representation size and a specialized compilation procedure. At the same time, these adjacent
lines of work help motivate our setting, since compiled d-DNNF circuits can serve as a basis
for exact reasoning tasks such as counting and sampling on grounded FO2 theories.

3.1
Propositional and First-Order Logic
We assume familiarity with standard notions from propositional and first-order logic [21].
In this paper, we work on the function-free finite-domain fragment of first-order logic. A
vocabulary is a finite set P of predicate symbols. An atom is of the form P(t1, . . . , tk) where
P/k ∈P and each ti is a variable or a constant. A literal is an atom or its negation. Formulas
are built from atoms using ¬, ∧, ∨and may be prefixed by quantifiers ∀x and ∃x. A formula
is a sentence if all variables are bound. We write Pφ for the vocabulary occurring in a formula
φ. A formula is ground if it contains no variables, i.e., it is propositional where ground atoms
are viewed as boolean variables. Given a first-order sentence Γ and a domain ∆, grounding of
(Γ, ∆) is the propositional formula obtained by instantiating all quantifiers in Γ over elements
from ∆with the usual semantics. Unless otherwise specified, the first-order formulas in this
paper are assumed to be constant-free, i.e., they contain no constant symbols.

(E(e1, e1) ∨E(e1, e2)) ∧(E(e2, e1) ∨E(e2, e2)) ∧¬E(e1, e1) ∧¬E(e2, e2),
where each ground atom, such as E(e1, e2), is a propositional variable.

3.2
Knowledge Compilation
Knowledge compilation (KC) is the process of transforming a propositional formula into a
representation such that various logical queries on the formula can be answered efficiently
by operating on the compiled representation. In this paper, we focus on compilation into
negation normal form and its variants [12].
A negation normal form (NNF) is a node labeled directed acyclic graph (DAG) with
internal nodes labeled by logical connectives (e.g., ∧, ∨), leaves labeled by boolean variables
or their negations (literals), and a unique sink node (outdegree 0) called the output. For an
NNF C, var(C) denotes its set of variables and |C| denotes its size, i.e., the number of edges.
Given an NNF C and an assignment f of truth values to its variables, the evaluation of
C under f, denoted C(f), is defined as the value computed at the output node when the
leaves are assigned according to f and internal nodes are evaluated according to their labels
in a bottom-up fashion. The assignments f such that C(f) = 1 are called the satisfying
assignments of C. Two NNFs are logically equivalent (or simply equivalent) if they have the
same set of satisfying assignments. An NNF C encodes a propositional formula φ if C and φ
have the same set of satisfying assignments.
An NNF circuit C is decomposable (DNNF) if for every ∧-node with children C1, . . . , Cr,
the boolean variable sets are pairwise disjoint: var(Ci) ∩var(Cj) = ∅for all i ̸= j. C
is deterministic if for every ∨-node with children C1, . . . , Cr, the disjuncts are pairwise
inconsistent, i.e., Ci ∧Cj is unsatisfiable for all i ̸= j. A circuit satisfying both properties
is a deterministic DNNF (d-DNNF). d-DNNFs are particularly of interest in knowledge
compilation since they support a wide range of queries that can be answered in time
polynomial in the size of the circuit [8, 12]. We also consider the more restrictive class of
structured d-DNNFs and OBDDs, which are defined with respect to a fixed variable tree and
variable order, respectively. We refer the reader to [12] for more details on these languages
and their properties.
We consider knowledge compilation for first-order logic, focusing primarily on d-DNNFs.
The same questions can also be asked for more restrictive target languages, and we provide
corresponding results for structured d-DNNFs and OBDDs.
▶Problem. Fix a first-order sentence Γ. Given a finite domain ∆, output a d-DNNF circuit
encoding the grounding of (Γ, ∆).
Given an algorithm that solves this problem, we mainly care about the size of the output
circuit and the time complexity of the algorithm, both w.r.t. the domain size |∆|.
3.3
Two-Variable Logic in Scott Normal Form
The two-variable fragment of first-order logic, denoted by FO2, is a well-studied decidable
fragment [23, 14]. FO2 exhibits particularly favorable properties for various computational
tasks, e.g., model counting for FO2 is in time polynomial in the domain size for a fixed
sentence, while adding another third variable renders the problem intractable [4]. Therefore,
we study knowledge compilation for FO2 as a first step towards understanding the compilation
of more expressive first-order fragments.
Any FO2 sentence can be transformed into Scott normal form (SNF) [26]:

Hardness of FO2 Compilation
In this section, we show that there exists an FO2 sentence whose DNNF representation
over a domain of size n cannot be in size polynomial (or even subexponential) in n. This
immediately implies the same lower bound for d-DNNF representations.
▶Theorem 2. There exists an FO2 sentence Γ such that for every n ≥1 and domain ∆
with |∆| = n, every DNNF circuit encoding the grounding of (Γ, ∆) has size 2Ω(n).
The proof relies on the transformation of DNNF circuits under minimization introduced
by Darwiche [8], and a lower bound on DNNF size for the Boolean function of permuta-
tion PERMn (or equivalently, the perfect matchings of the complete bipartite graph Kn,n)
established by de Colnet [13]. We recall these results below.
Given an NNF C, the minimization of C is an NNF whose satisfying assignments are
exactly those satisfying assignments of C with the minimum number of true variables.
▶Theorem 3 (Combined from [9] and [8, Section 2.5]). Given a DNNF circuit C, one can
construct a DNNF circuit Cmin encoding the minimization of C, in time polynomial in |C|.
The PERMn can be represented as a propositional formula over n2 boolean variables
{pij | i, j ∈[n]}, and its satisfying assignments of PERMn are exactly the n × n permutation
matrices, i.e., there is exactly one true variable in {pi1, . . . , pin} for each i ∈[n] and exactly
one true variable in {p1j, . . . , pnj} for each j ∈[n].
▶Theorem 4 ([13]1). Every DNNF circuit encoding PERMn has size 2Ω(n).

d-DNNF compilation for FO2
In this section, we present a compilation scheme for FO2. Throughout, we assume that the
sentence is in Scott normal form (cf. Section 3.3). Given a domain ∆= {e1, . . . , en}, our
goal is to compile the grounding of (Γ, ∆) into a propositional d-DNNF circuit. We also
show how the construction yields OBDD and structured d-DNNF representations.
The section is organized as follows. Section 5.1 introduces unary and binary types, which
describe the possible local truth assignments of elements and of element pairs, respectively.
These types provide the branching objects used in the compilation. Section 5.2 then presents
a two-stage procedure, where Stage I assigns unary types to elements, and Stage II assigns
binary types to element pairs. Section 5.3 then gives an efficient extendability test to prune
infeasible branches, which checks whether the current partial type assignment can be extended
to a full model.
5.1
Types
Let P be the set of unary and binary predicates in Γ. We say that a set of literals is maximally
consistent if it does not contain any contradictory literals and cannot be extended by adding
any other literal. Then we define unary and binary types as follows.
▶Definition 5 (Unary and binary types). A unary type τ is a maximally consistent set of
unary literals over P involving a single variable x (including the diagonal literals R(x, x) for
binary predicates R). A binary type π is a maximally consistent set of binary literals over P
involving distinct variables x and y. A type is either a unary type or a binary type.
We often identify a type with a formula obtained by conjoining all its literals, and write
τ(x) and π(x, y). For a structure A and an element a ∈∆, we say that a realizes a unary
type τ if A |= τ(a), and for two distinct elements a, b ∈∆, we say that the ordered pair (a, b)
realizes a binary type π if A |= π(a, b). The realization of types for an element or a pair of
elements is unique because types are maximally consistent. Therefore, a model of Γ over ∆
(equivalently, a satisfying assignment of the propositional grounding of (Γ, ∆)) can be viewed
as a type assignment that assigns a unary type to each element ei and a binary type to each
ordered pair (ei, ej) for i < j.
When dealing with types, we only need to consider those that are valid. A unary type τ
is valid w.r.t. Γ if τ(x) |= ϕ(x, x), where ϕ is from the universal conjunct ∀x∀y : ϕ(x, y) in
the Scott normal form of Γ. Given unary types τ and τ′, a binary type π is valid for (τ, τ ′)
if τ(x) ∧τ ′(y) ∧π(x, y) |= ϕ(x, y) ∧ϕ(y, x). Let UΓ be the set of valid unary types and, for
each pair (τ, τ ′) ∈UΓ × UΓ, let BΓ(τ, τ ′) be the set of binary types valid for (τ, τ ′). When the
sentence Γ is clear from context, we may omit the subscript and write U and B(τ, τ ′) instead.

5.2
Two-Stage Compilation
The construction of the d-DNNF circuit proceeds in two stages.
Stage I: unary type assignment. We process domain elements e1, . . . , en in order. At an
element ei we branch over τ ∈U, and proceed to ei+1. When all elements have been
processed, the path from the root to the current node corresponds to a complete unary
type assignment, and we then move to Stage II.
Stage II: binary type assignment. Based on a unary assignment, we process ordered pairs
(ei, ej) with 1 ≤i < j ≤n in lexicographic order of (i, j). At each pair (ei, ej) we branch
over π ∈B(τi, τj), and continue to the next pair until all pairs have been processed.
We implement this two-stage compilation as a recursive procedure, as shown in Algorithm 1.
Each call Stage-I(i, θ) returns the output node of a subcircuit whose satisfying assignments
are exactly the completions of the current context θ, where the context θ is formally defined
as follows.
▶Definition 7 (Context). The context θ is a propositional formula obtained by conjoining
the branch constraints accumulated along the current recursive call.
Intuitively, the context θ captures the constraints imposed by the type decisions made
so far and thus represents a partial type assignment. Although branching on valid types
ensures the universal conjunct ∀x∀y : ϕ(x, y), some branches may still violate the existential
constraints ∀x∃y : βk(x, y) in Γ. We prune such branches by checking whether their contexts
can be extended to full models of Γ over ∆. We call this the Extendable test and discuss
its details in Section 5.3.

Extendability Checks
At each branching step one must test whether the current context θ, which records the type
assignments made so far, can be extended to a full model of Γ over ∆of size n. We formalize
this as follows.
▶Definition 11 (Extendability). Let Γ be an FO2 sentence and let θ be a context during the
compilation over a domain ∆. We say that θ is extendable w.r.t. (Γ, ∆) if there exists a
model A over ∆such that A |= Γ ∧θ.
Intuitively, one can do the extendability check by reducing it to a satisfiability test of the
propositional grounding of (Γ, ∆) with the additional constraint θ. However, by this method,
we will lose the symmetry present at the first-order level, and more importantly, such tests
are generally intractable from the NP-completeness of SAT problems. Instead, we develop a
more efficient extendability test by exploiting the symmetry of FO2 models following the
approach of [22]. We first need the notion of configuration [4].
▶Definition 12 (Configuration). Fix an FO2 sentence Γ, and let U be the set of valid
unary types with an arbitrary fixed ordering. Let q = |U|. A configuration is a vector
n = (n1, . . . , nq) ∈Nq, where ni counts the number of elements realizing the i-th unary type.

realizing the i-th unary type is exactly ni for all i. It turns out that the satisfiability of
configurations has a monotonicity property, which allows us to compare configurations using
a partial order and characterize satisfiable configurations via finitely many bounded ones.

Checks in Stage II
We now consider extendability checks during Stage II. Recall that, at this point, the unary

elements of each unary type: we must also keep track of which binary types have already

Compilation Optimizations
In this section, we present optimizations for the compilation. The main idea is to identify and
merge equivalent branches returned from recursive calls to Stage-I and Stage-II (recall
that equivalent circuits have the same set of satisfying assignments). For instance, let Or(S)
be the ∨-node returned from a call to Stage-I(i, θ). If S contains two ∧-nodes τ(ei) ∧C
and τ ′(ei) ∧C′, such that C and C′ obtained at Line 8 in Algorithm 1 are equivalent, then
we can merge the two branches from (τ(ei) ∧C) ∨(τ ′(ei) ∧C′) to (τ(ei) ∨τ ′(ei)) ∧C, and
thus avoid the redundant recursive call and extendability check for C′.
Let us make the process formal. We call two circuits C and C′ returned from Stage-I(i, θ)
(or Stage-II((i, j), θ)) at the same level if they are returned for the same i (or the same
(i, j)) but possibly different contexts θ and θ′. It is clear that two circuits at the same level
have the same variable set. Then we have the following proposition.
▶Proposition 18. Let C and C′ be two equivalent circuits at the same level. Redirecting
all edges to C′ to C and removing C′ does not affect the correctness, determinism or
decomposability of the resulting circuit.
Proof. The decomposability is preserved since C and C′ have the same variable set. By the
equivalence of C and C′, they have the same set of satisfying assignments. Thus determinism
is also preserved. The correctness follows from the same reason: any satisfying assignment of
the original circuit also satisfies the modified circuit, and vice versa, since C and C′ have the
same set of satisfying assignments.
◀
▶Remark 19. Proposition 18 can also apply to the compilation of structured d-DNNFs and
OBDDs as in Proposition 10, since the sharing is between circuits at the same level, which
have the same variable set and thus are in the same vtree node for structured d-DNNFs and
at the same variable for OBDDs.
The pruning technique for equivalent circuits described above is not specific to our two-
stage compilation, but can be applied to any d-DNNF compilation algorithm that constructs
circuits by branching on different contexts. The advantage of our two-stage compilation is
that it provides clear criteria for identifying equivalent circuits from their contexts, without
the need to check the equivalence of circuits explicitly, which can be costly. In practice,
we maintain a hash table indexed by an abstraction of the current context for each level.

Conclusion and Discussion
We studied the compilation of FO2 sentences over finite domains into standard DNNF-based
target languages. On the negative side, we showed that there exists a fixed FO2 sentence
whose grounding over a domain of size n requires DNNF size 2Ω(n). On the positive side, we
presented a specialized compilation method by exploiting some characterization of FO2. Our
experiments show that this approach is effective on several representative sentences, improving
over baseline propositional compilation pipelines in both circuit size and compilation time.
Finally, we discuss some open questions and future directions.
It is well-known that the order of variable branching can significantly affect the perform-
ance of propositional compilers, such as c2d and cudd. In our proposed method, we use a
restricted processing order. It first branches unary types and then binary types. We remark
that this restriction is not merely an implementation choice but is crucial for the efficiency
of the method, more specifically for the efficiency of extendability checks. Interleaving unary
and binary type decisions would break the symmetry and make the extendability checks
even fall back to the #P-hardness of FO2 satisfiability with arbitrary binary evidence [29].
We however note that recent work on FO2 model counting with binary evidence that is
of bounded treewidth [18] may provide a promising direction for extending the method to
handle more flexible processing orders, that we leave for future work.
Another natural question is whether our method can be extended to FO3 ? Such an
extension is nontrivial. The feature of two variables is used in an essential way throughout
our method. First, models of FO2 can be organized by unary types and binary types, which
makes the two-stage decomposition possible. For FO3, constraints relate more elements
simultaneously, so pairwise type compatibility is no longer sufficient. Second, our extendability
checks rely crucially on the bounded configuration characterization of Theorem 14. This is
closely related to the classical boundary between the well-behaved two-variable fragment and
richer fragments with three or more variables. Therefore, instead of targeting unrestricted
FO3 directly, a promising direction is to study extensions of FO2 such as counting extensions
like C2 or FO2 with additional restricted axioms, e.g., tree or linear order axioms.
The last potential direction is to combine the type-level approach of our method with
finer-grained propositional compilation techniques, which is suggested by our experiments on
ΓP and u4-b2-fo. The current compiler operates primarily at the level of types, and may
therefore miss finer-grained propositional independence in some instances.

E.2
Correctness validation
To validate the correctness of our compiler, we performed both model enumeration and count-
ing cross-checks against independent baselines. For each input pair (Γ, ∆), we constructed (i)
the d-DNNF circuit produced by our compiler, and (ii) a propositional CNF encoding of the
grounding of (Γ, ∆).
For model enumeration, we compared the compiled circuit and the grounding in both
directions. First, we enumerated models of the compiled d-DNNF circuit and checked that
each enumerated assignment satisfies the grounding CNF. Second, on the SAT side, we used
MiniSAT as a SAT oracle on the grounding CNF, repeatedly calling solve(). After each
satisfying assignment A was found, we added a blocking clause excluding A, and continued
enumeration. In this way, we enumerated satisfying assignments of the grounding CNF
and checked that each of them satisfies the compiled d-DNNF circuit. Since the number of
models can become extremely large for larger domain sizes, exhaustive enumeration is often
infeasible; therefore, on each side, we capped enumeration at 100,000 models.
In addition to enumeration, we also validated correctness by comparing model counts
obtained from three methods: (i) counting on the d-DNNF compiled by our method, (ii)
counting on a d-DNNF compiled from the grounding by the compiler c2d [11], and (iii)
counting using the WFOMC algorithm for FO2 sentences [28]. All three methods returned
exactly the same counts on all tested instances.

E.4
Evaluation on OBDD
We further evaluate the OBDD representation induced by our compilation procedure. Al-
though our compiler is primarily designed for d-DNNF, the two-stage construction naturally
imposes a type-based block order on the propositional variables: unary blocks are processed
first, followed by binary blocks in lexicographic order. As shown in Proposition 10, for a
fixed sentence, the resulting circuit can be transformed into an OBDD respecting this order
with only a constant-factor overhead. Thus we also evaluated the OBDD compilation over
the same benchmarks.
We compare our approach with a generic grounding-based CNF-to-OBDD baseline
implemented using CUDD, a widely used BDD package. For each sentence and domain size,
as in the d-DNNF experiments, the CUDD baselines take as input the grounded propositional
encoding of the corresponding FO2 sentence.
Since OBDDs are canonical for a fixed boolean function and variable ordering, variable
ordering is one of the dominant factors affecting both OBDD size and compilation time. We
therefore evaluate two dynamic reordering strategies provided by CUDD. The first baseline,
denoted by cudd-WIN3, uses CUDD_REORDER_WINDOW3, a local window-based reordering
heuristic, and is intended as a more runtime-oriented baseline. The second baseline, denoted
by cudd-SIFT, uses CUDD_REORDER_SIFT, the standard sifting heuristic, and is intended
as a size-oriented baseline. All remaining experimental settings, including the hardware
configuration, and the timeout of 1800 seconds, are the same as in the d-DNNF evaluation.
The results are summarized in Table 18.
Overall, the results show that the type block order used by our compiler is also effective for
OBDD compilation. In particular, our method often scales to larger domain sizes and achieves
substantially lower compilation times than the generic CUDD baselines. This advantage is
consistent with the design of our compiler: instead of searching over propositional assignments
