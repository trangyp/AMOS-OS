On Ceva’s and Menelaus’s Theorems for a Möbius triangle
Ivan Livinsky
Abstract
We generalize the classical Ceva’s and Menelaus’s theorems to curvilinear triangles bounded
by circular arcs (Figure 1). We introduce trilinear coordinates associated with such triangles
and develop several geometric constructions. In particular, for any proper Möbius triangle we
define the incenter, excenters, and orthocenter. An interactive demo containing most of our
results is available at https://github.com/livinsky-research/Moebius
Figure 1: Ceva’s Theorem for a Möbius triangle gives necessary and sufficient conditions for three
cevians to belong to one pencil.

The Möbius plane
The main purpose of this paper is to show that Möbius geometry is no less rich than classical
geometries such as affine or hyperbolic. As we shall see, the results obtained here naturally yield
corresponding statements for spherical, hyperbolic, and Euclidean geometries, illustrating the uni-
fying role of Möbius geometry.
We begin by recalling the basic properties of the Möbius plane. The Möbius plane M is an
incidence structure of points and cycles. In its classical model we identify M with the Riemann

The standard constant-curvature geometries (spherical, Euclidean, and hyperbolic) are naturally
embedded into Möbius geometry, and every theorem in Möbius geometry becomes some result in
these geometries involving lines and circles.
• for spherical geometry we identify M with the sphere S2 via stereographic projection. The
cycles are then all straight lines and all circles. All are obtained as planar sections of the
sphere. The planes corresponding to the straight lines are the ones passing through the center
of S2. After projecting onto C the straight lines become the lines passing through 0 and the
circles relative to which the power of 0 is −1. In the matrix language these cycles correspond
to the matrices M with an additional property k + n = tr M = 0.
• for Euclidean geometry we consider C = M \ {∞} and once again the cycles are all straight
lines and all circles. The straight lines are identified by the condition k = 0.
• for hyperbolic geometry we consider the upper half-plane H ⊂C as the Poincaré model. The
straight lines are the half-circles orthogonal to R and vertical rays. These are exactly the
cycles defined by real matrices M. The remaining cycles that intersect H cover all hyperbolic
circles, horocycles, and equidistant curves depending on the number of intersection points
with R = R ∪{∞} (this is also the real projective line RP 1).

Points and cycles
The following property of Möbius transformations is well-known from Complex Analysis.
Proposition 2.1. For any two triples A, B, C and A′, B′, C′ of distinct points on M there exists a
unique orientation-preserving isometry σ ∈Isom+ M such that σ(A) = A′, σ(B) = B′, σ(C) = C′.
It means that any three distinct points on the Möbius plane are in general position. Hence, not
only there are no two-point invariants such as distance, but no three-point invariants either. Only
for four points we have some special cases and nontrivial invariants.

The space of cycles C has a geometry of its own. A pencil is a one-dimensional family of cycles
that naturally appears in Möbius geometry. Let a, b ≈M, N be two distinct oriented cycles. The
pencil C(a, b) = {c ∼xM + yN|x, y ∈R} is a family containing all non-oriented cycles that are
real linear combinations of a and b. If a ̸∼= b then M and N are not proportional and generate
a subspace W of dimension 2 in Herm(2). The pencil C(a, b) is a collection of cycles represented
by matrices contained in W. Pencils are lines in C. Three cycles a, b, c are collinear when they
belong to one pencil. Three pencils are concurrent when they share one common cycle. The action
of Isom M on Herm(2) is linear; therefore, it maps pencils to pencils.

Generalized cycles and extended pencils
Let a, b ≈M, N. In the hyperbolic case not every combination xM + yN defines a cycle, as the
corresponding matrix may be positive or negative definite or even degenerate. Thus, the hyperbolic
pencils (and parabolic as well) are not complete lines in the space C. Hence, in order to fully
embrace projective geometry we need to extend the pencils. And for this, in turn, we need to
generalize cycles a bit.
A generalized cycle is an equation of the form
kzz + lz + mz + n = 0,
where the matrix M = ( k l
m n ) ∈Herm(2) is nonzero and Hermitian, considered up to a nonzero real

a, b.

Triangles
A triangle ∆= △ABC is a part of the plane bounded by three cycle segments that pairwise join
three distinct points A, B, C and share no other common points. We write BC, AC, AB for these
segments and a, b, c for the cycles containing them. The points A, B, C are the vertices of the
triangle.
Unlike Euclidean geometry the triangles on the Möbius plane appear in extremely wide variety
of shapes and forms (Figure 8). For example, Reuleaux triangle, Ying-Yang curve, or an arbelos
are all Möbius triangles.
Figure 8: Various kinds ot Möbius triangles.
Since isometries are conformal the inner angles of a triangle are uniquely defined and are equal
for congruent triangles. There are no limitations for a single angle value in this case. If α, β, γ are
angles of a Möbius triangle, then α, β, γ ∈[0, 2π]. However, not all triples are feasible. The angles
must satisfy the triangle inequalities that hold for all triangles.
Theorem 9.1 ([6]). The angles α, β, γ of any Möbius triangle satisfy the inequalities
−π < β + γ −α < 3π,
−π < α + γ −β < 3π,
−π < α + β −γ < 3π.
Any triple of angles satisfying the inequalities above can be realized as internal angles of some Möbius
triangle.
Corollary 9.2. For any α, β, γ ∈[0, π) or any α, β, γ ∈(π, 2π] there exists a Möbius triangle having
α, β, γ as its internal angles.
Thus, the feasible triples of angles form a polyhedron filling exactly three quarters of the cube
[0, 2π]3. For example, there is no triangle with angles 0 and 2π at the same time, and no triangle
with angles 0, 0, π. The polyhedron is central symmetric, as the complement of every triangle is
also a triangle sharing same vertices and having angles 2π −α, 2π −β, 2π −γ.
Note that α = π does not mean that AB and AC lie on the same cycle; it only means that b
and c are tangent in A (Figure 8). A triangle degenerates into a digon when two angles are the
same and the third is π. When all angles are π the triangle becomes a half-plane. Such triangles
are degenerate. For non-degenerate triangles a, b, c are non-collinear.

Finally, we can also generalize the well-known partial transformation of isogonal conjugation to
Möbius triangles. Let na, nb, nc be three cevians satisfying the conditions of Ceva’s or Menelaus’s
Theorem. Then we can flip them around the corresponding bisectors and get new cevians ma, mb, mc.
But since (a, b; mc) = (a, b; nc)−1, and etc; we have that ma, mb, mc also satisfy the conditions of
the same theorem (Figure 18).
