Precision measurements based on optically detected nuclear magnetic resonance offer exquisite
sensitivity to absolute shifts in spin transition frequencies, with potential applications in fundamental
physics experiments and inertial sensing. We investigate 13C nuclear spins in diamond as a candidate
system for solid-state implementations, which hold the promise for high-fidelity readout of large
numbers of coherent nuclear spins in millitesla or lower magnetic fields. We demonstrate a technique
that allows for both optical polarization and readout of large ensembles of ∼1016 polarized nuclear
spins. Our method takes advantage of state-selective Landau-Zener transitions under microwave
frequency sweeping, which bidirectionally transfer spin polarization between Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV)
electron spins and remote 13C nuclear spins.
Using natural isotopic abundance diamonds with
nitrogen densities of ∼0.5–10 ppm, we perform optically-detected 13C Ramsey spectroscopy and
realize a nuclear-spin-dependent fluorescence contrast exceeding 0.5% peak-to-peak. We observe
nuclear spin dephasing times T ∗
2 ≈2 ms that only modestly improve with homonuclear dipolar
decoupling, indicating that they are limited by the longitudinal spin relaxation of nearby NV electron
spins. We study the magnetic field dependence of the optical readout and find comparable contrast
and dephasing times for magnetic fields in the range 8–20 mT. Our method can be interpreted as a
type of repetitive readout, where each NV center optically reads out the spin state of ∼100 nuclei
before nuclear spins depolarize.

Hyperpolarized solid-state spins are attractive for

However, the conventional nuclear
magnetic resonance (NMR) readout via radio-frequency
(RF) induction is fundamentally limited by Johnson
noise [13, 14]. At room temperature and low magnetic
fields (B0 ≲10 mT, corresponding to NMR frequencies
of order ≲100 kHz) this restricts the readout fidelity to
F ≪10−3 (Appendix I), limiting the attainable sensi-
tivity.
Optically-detected NMR (ODNMR) is an intriguing
alternative to inductive detection that can offer superior
readout fidelity at low B0 and ambient temperatures.
Typically, an optically detected electron-spin system is
used to transfer polarization to and from neighboring,
hyperfine-coupled nuclear spins [15]. The electron spin
system can simultaneously be used as a secondary sen-
sor to suppress the impact of environmental fluctuations
in a “co-magnetometer” configuration [6, 16]. In noble
gas/alkali-metal vapor mixtures, these techniques have
matured to the point where sub-nanohertz frequency

Precision ODNMR measurements based on Nitrogen-

ternative to alkali-metal vapor [7, 8]. Initial demonstra-
tions used ODNMR of the intrinsic 14N nuclear spins
associated with NV centers [27, 28]. This method pro-
vides a spin readout fidelity, F ≈10−3, but the spin
density is limited, as there is only one intrinsic 14N spin
for each NV electron spin.
We hypothesized that ODNMR measurements of the
abundant weakly-coupled 13C nuclei (spin I = 1/2)
in diamond could provide a larger polarized spin den-
sity. Numerous experiments have demonstrated room-
temperature hyperpolarization of bulk
13C spins by
transferring the near-unity optical polarization of NV
electron spins to nuclei via either the solid-effect [29–31],
pulsed [32] and microwave-swept [33–39] Landau-Zener
dynamics, or microwave [40] and magnetic-field [41–44]
tuned spin energy degeneracy. Long-lived 13C bulk po-
larization exceeding 5% has been reported for mm-sized
diamonds [30, 31, 37, 42]. However, bulk detection has
largely relied on post-polarization shuttling of diamonds
into a superconducting NMR magnet for inductive coil-
based readout.
This has two primary drawbacks: i)
the large magnetic field is impractical for some appli-
cations and is difficult to stabilize for absolute NMR
frequency measurements, and ii) the readout fidelity of
inductive detection is relatively poor, limiting sensitiv-
ity (Appendix I). This motivates the use of optical 13C
NMR detection techniques, but prior studies have fo-
cused on the low Np regime [41, 45–55].
Here, we demonstrate a method for low-field ODNMR
spectroscopy of 13C nuclei that are weakly coupled to
NV centers. Our technique uses state-selective Landau-
Zener transitions under microwave frequency sweeping,
which bidirectionally transfer spin polarization between
NV electron spins and remote 13C nuclear spins. This
enables a repetitive readout, where each NV center
is used to optically read out the spin state of ∼100
nuclei before nuclear spins depolarize.
We perform
optically-detected 13C Ramsey spectroscopy and realize
a nuclear-spin-dependent fluorescence contrast exceed-
ing 0.5% peak-to-peak. We study the 13C spin coher-
ence times of four diamonds, with nitrogen density in
the ∼0.5–10 ppm range, and find they are likely limited
by the ∼3 ms longitudinal spin relaxation of neighboring
NV electron spins. The results indicate the potential for
compact ODNMR devices, featuring relatively high spin
readout fidelity (F ≳10−3) of large ensembles of polar-
ized diamond 13C nuclear spins (Np ≳1016) at room
temperature and in magnetic fields down to ∼8 mT.

We focus our study on millimeter-sized
diamonds, grown by chemical vapor deposition, with
natural-abundance (1.1%) 13C, that have an initial ni-
trogen density in the range ∼0.5–10 ppm.
Approxi-
mately 10% of the initial nitrogen impurities were con-
verted to NV centers via electron irradiation and an-
nealing (Appendix II).
A Helmholtz coil pair is used to supply a uniform
external magnetic field, B0, in the range 2–12 mT along
the ˆz direction. The diamond is positioned such that one
of the four NV crystallographic axes is aligned along ˆz.
A copper-wire coil, wrapped around the diamond, is
used to deliver 20–130 kHz RF magnetic-field pulses
(∼50 µs duration), polarized along the ˆx axis, that ad-
dress the 13C nuclear spins. A second copper wire loop
delivers 2.9–3.2 GHz microwave magnetic fields polar-
ized along the ˆy axis that drive the ms = 0 ↔ms = 1
NV ground-state electron spin transition. The diamond
is mounted on a water-cooled aluminum nitride sub-
strate, and the entire assembly, excluding Helmholtz
coils and optics, is housed in an RF-shielding aluminum
box with holes for optical and electrical access.
Laser light (532 nm, 0.01–1.2 W) is amplitude modu-
lated with an acousto-optic modulator and shaped with
an anamorphic prism pair and lenses to a ∼0.5×2 mm2
beam that is incident on a side facet of the diamond.
An aspheric condenser lens (numerical aperture: ∼0.6)
collects NV fluorescence, which is then spectrally fil-
tered with a dichroic mirror and relayed by another lens
onto an amplified photodetector. Typically, the incident
peak laser power is ∼0.4 W, the collected fluorescence
power is ∼1 mW, and the NV fluorescence contrast (rel-
ative difference in fluorescence rate between ms = 0 and
ms = 1) is (∆F/F)pp,max ≈3%. For a more detailed
description of the experimental setup, see Appendix III.
Figure 1(b) depicts the ODNMR concept. Absorption
of green laser light polarizes NV electron spins to the
ms = 0 ground-state spin sublevel, denoted as |0⟩. The
frequency of a microwave field is swept about the NV
|0⟩↔|1⟩transition frequency, f+ = D + γnvB0, where
D = 2.87 GHz is the axial zero-field splitting parame-
ter and γnv = 28.03 GHz/T is the NV gyromagnetic ra-
tio. This process transfers NV electron spin polarization
to 13C nuclear spins, mediated by transverse hyperfine
coupling.
RF pulses encode the 13C NMR frequency,
fn = γnucB0, where γnuc = 10.7 MHz/T, into a change
in the 13C polarization. The resulting nuclear spin po-
larization is transferred back to NV electron spins via
a similar frequency-swept microwave transfer process.

B
RF encoding of the 13C NMR frequency
Following the polarization phase, RF pulses are ap-
plied that encode the 13C NMR frequency into changes
in the nuclear-spin polarization. For example, a Ram-
sey sequence can be applied using a pair of near-resonant
RF π/2 pulses with a detuning δ relative to fn. When
these pulses are separated by a free-precession interval
τ, the resulting nuclear-spin polarization is given by
⟨Iz⟩∝cos (2πδτ).
Before and during the RF encod-
ing phase, we typically leave the green laser light on for
∼10 ms, such that NV centers largely remain in the |0⟩
state where 13C spins are unaffected by hyperfine inter-
action.
C
Optical detection of the 13C spin state
Optical detection of ⟨Iz⟩is carried out using the detec-
tion phase of the sequence depicted in Fig. 1(c). Here,
microwave sweeping about f+ is performed with alter-
nating direction, and laser pulses are applied at the be-
ginning of each sweep segment for NV electron spin read-
out and (at least partial) repolarization. Consider the
case where the nuclear spin begins the detection phase
in state |↓⟩, Fig. 1(d). A laser pulse initializes NV cen-
ters to |0⟩. When microwaves are swept up in frequency
( ˙f > 0), the NV center undergoes AFP, and the final
state |1, ↓⟩results in a lower fluorescence rate. However,
when ˙f < 0, there is a significant probability of diabatic
hopping, resulting in a final state |0, ↑⟩that produces a
higher fluorescence rate. In this way, the fluorescence
rate alternates from low to high and repeats. If the nu-
clear polarization begins in the |↑⟩state, the fluorescence
instead alternates from high to low rates; sweeps with
˙f > 0 result in a lower fluorescence rate while sweeps
with ˙f < 0 result in higher fluorescence rate.
While the diabatic hopping flips the nuclear spin state
of one particular site, the cycle can repeat if another
polarized nuclear spin replenishes this site’s |↓⟩polar-
ization via spin diffusion or if the NV center interacts
with a different hyperfine-coupled nuclear spin site. This
provides a means for repetitive readout–each NV center
can be used to optically detect the spin state of numer-
ous 13C nuclei before spin relaxation processes dominate.
By repeating cycles of microwave sweeps of alternating
direction, the normalized amplitude of the resulting flu-
orescence oscillations, ∆F/F, retaining sign, provides a
direct mapping to ⟨Iz⟩.
III
Results
We performed optically-detected 13C Ramsey spec-
troscopy of diamond J5 (Appendix II) at B0 = 12 mT
to characterize the ODNMR readout. Figure 2(a) shows
the Ramsey pulse sequence. The default duration of the
pulse sequence is ∼1 s total: 0.5–1 s for the polarization
phase, ≲10 ms for the RF encoding phase, and ∼0.4 s for
the detection phase. The microwave sweep segments are
∼1 ms long and laser pulses during the detection phase
are applied for 100 µs. During the RF encoding phase,
the delay, τ, between two slightly detuned (δ ≈1 kHz)
nuclear-spin π/2 pulses is varied. For each value of τ,
the NV fluorescence time trace is typically averaged over
≥8 repetitions of the pulse sequence (Appendix V 2).

nal time trace for three different values of τ. Each point
on these plots, ∆F/F, corresponds to the average fluo-
rescence signal collected during a given laser pulse, nor-
malized to the mean fluorescence signal over the entire
time trace. For τ = 0 (top, red), the nuclear spin ensem-
ble has not precessed at all between the RF pulses and
experiences an overall π pulse, flipping the spins from
|↓⟩to |↑⟩. Oscillations in ∆F/F are observed, with an
initial contrast of ∼0.4% peak-to-peak, that persist for
detection times exceeding 0.2 s after the RF encoding
phase. For τ = 250 µs (middle, blue), the nuclear spin
ensemble precesses by ∼90° after the first RF pulse, such
that the second RF pulse (of the same phase) largely has
no impact and ⟨Iz⟩≈0. Consequently, no oscillations
in ∆F/F are observed. For τ = 500 µs (bottom, green),
the nuclear spin ensemble precesses by ∼180°, such that
the final π/2 pulse leaves the nuclei in approximately
the same state as the initial state, |↓⟩. Here oscillations
in ∆F/F are once again observed, except now the sign
of the oscillations is opposite to that in the τ = 0 case.
The ∆F/F oscillations decay with detection time, but
they do so markedly slower than a simple exponential
decay would suggest and much slower than the NV lon-
gitudinal spin relaxation rate [60]. This is an important
attribute, since each individual oscillation corresponds
to the readout of at most one 13C spin per NV center,
and thus the number of detectable oscillations dictates

into |0⟩during the RF encoding phase of the experi-

a different NMR frequency shift, owing to variation in
its hyperfine tensor, the ensemble-averaged effect is to
induce dephasing. This is a fundamental liability of our
ODNMR approach, as it relies on weak hyperfine cou-
pling to ground-state NV centers, but it may be miti-
gated in the future through NV microwave decoupling
protocols during the RF encoding phase [63, 64]. Al-
ternatively, future studies may explore the use of color
centers with ground-state spin singlet and metastable
triplet states [65–67].
IV
Discussion and outlook
We have introduced a method for ODNMR that al-
lows for probing a large number of diamond 13C spins.
Compared to prior diamond ODNMR experiments us-
ing the intrinsic 14N nuclear spin associated with NV
centers, our method allows for the measurement of a
larger number of nuclear spins and operation in a broad
range of magnetic fields. To characterize the relevance
of our approach to future precision measurements, recall
that the minimum detectable change in spin precession

sensitivity estimate would be nearly an order of mag-
nitude improvement over prior 14N ODNMR gyroscope
experiments [27], but there is much room for improve-

by ∼10x by using a larger diamond and higher laser
power (holding intensity constant). The readout fidelity
could be improved by ∼30x by improving the photon
detection efficiency and using a diamond with higher
NV spin-dependent fluorescence contrast [68].
These
relatively straightforward improvements would provide
δf ≈2×10−5 Hz for t = 1 s. More speculative improve-
ments may come from optimizing the photon budget,
through a better understanding of the readout-phase
optical power dependence, and potentially using the in-
trinsic 14N nuclear spin as a local memory [28]. Finally,
large gains in sensitivity would come from increasing T ∗
2 ,
potentially through microwave decoupling [63, 64] or the
use of ground-state spin singlet color centers [65–67].
In summary, we introduced a method for optical de-
tection of large ensembles of coherent 13C nuclear spins
in diamond. This method is based on microwave-swept
dynamics that bidirectionally transfer polarization be-
tween NV electron and 13C nuclear spins. We used it
to demonstrate ODNMR spectroscopy at low magnetic
fields (≲10 mT) and ambient temperature. With fur-
ther optimization, this approach may find application in
fundamental physics tests or in compact rotation sen-
sors.

A diagram of the experimental setup is shown in
Fig. 1(a). Here, we provide additional details.
An epifluorescence optical setup is used to illuminate
the diamond and collect and detect the resulting NV
fluorescence. For excitation, a 532 nm Lighthouse Pho-

Here, we describe the fitting procedure for the en-

presented in Fig. 2(b), which correspond to 13C in |↑⟩

We first subtract the persistent “offset oscillation”
from the data.
As discussed in Sec. III of the main
text and elaborated further in Appendix VII, this offset
oscillation can be attributed to AFP of the NV electron

Appendix VII
Ramsey offset
As discussed in Sec. III of the main text, the ODNMR
readout oscillations exhibit a constant-amplitude oscil-
lation in addition to the ⟨Iz⟩-dependent decaying oscil-
lation. We believe this offset oscillation is due to AFP of
the NV electron spin. This is evidenced by the observa-
tions that the constant-amplitude oscillation is present
regardless of the Ramsey free-precession time τ, it per-
sists indefinitely as a function of readout time t, and
it is much smaller in amplitude than the nuclear-spin-
dependent oscillations. The final reason is that this off-
set can be nullified by precise tuning of the microwave
central frequency.
As mentioned in the main text, we have two meth-
ods of analyzing the ODNMR readout oscillation time
traces.
The first involves fitting the envelope curves
of the ∆F/F(t) oscillations to a stretched exponen-
tial function, as described in Appendix VI. Prior to
implementing this method, we subtract the constant-
amplitude oscillation from the ∆F/F(t) data. This is
justified because the oscillation is indeed nearly constant
in amplitude regardless of t or τ. For instance, for an
initial value of ⟨Iz⟩that results in a ∆F/F(0) oscillation
of opposite sign of that of the persistent oscillation, the
overall oscillation of the ∆F/F(t) data flips sign once
the constant oscillation is larger than that of the ⟨Iz⟩-
dependent decaying ∆F/F portion. For an initial value
of ⟨Iz⟩that results in a ∆F/F(0) oscillation of the same
sign as that of the persistent oscillation, the signal never
dies off completely.
The other way we analyze the ODNMR readout os-
cillation time traces does not involve fitting.
This is
our primary method used throughout the main text, in-
cluding for Figs. 2(d-e), Fig. 3, and Fig. 4. Here, the
constant oscillation is not necessarily subtracted directly

processing the ODNMR readout curves in this manner,
the persistent constant-amplitude oscillation shows up
as a vertical shift of all the data in a ∆F/F(τ) Ram-

to Eq. (3) to extract the constant oscillation amplitude
from fits to Ramsey interferograms.

Appendix VIII
Coil data and NV data
In early versions of the experiments, we used an RF
coil in a single-sided transceiver mode to acquire bulk
diamond 13C NMR spectra. This allowed us to quan-
tify the degree of nuclear hyperpolarization and compare
the NMR spectra directly to the NV ODNMR detection
method. This was primarily done at higher magnetic
field B0 ≈20–50 mT, where the inductive NMR signal-
to-noise ratio is better.
Figure A14(a) shows the time-domain free induction
decay obtained by coil RF detection at B0 = 19.8 mT.
This curve is obtained by continuous laser excitation
and asymmetric microwave sweeping for a time Tpol =
200 s, which was found to be a characteristic satura-
tion time for coil-based detection.
The received coil
signal is mixed with a synchronized 217 kHz signal to
produce oscillations at ∼5 kHz.
The data are fit to
an exponentially-decaying sinusoidal function, revealing
T ∗
2 = 3.0 ± 0.1 ms. The phased Fourier transform of
these data, representing the NMR spectrum, is plotted
in Fig. A14(b). This curve is fit to a Lorentzian function,
revealing a fitted FWHM of 0.12 ± 0.01 kHz.
We also acquired an ODNMR signal at the same bias
field, B0 = 19.8 mT. For these data, we used an earlier
version of the detection protocol based on lock-in detec-
tion (see also Appendix XI). In this case, the polariza-
tion phase is identical to that in the main text, with du-
ration Tpol = 0.5 ms. However, unlike in the main-text
protocol, for the detection phase we follow a protocol
similar to the “Ramsey-Mz” method of Ref. [70]. The
laser is on continuously during the detection phase, the
microwave frequency is modulated sinusoidally about
f+, and the NV fluorescence signal is sent to a lock-
in amplifier and demodulated at the sweep rate (af-
ter a phase adjustment).
This allows us to extract a
relative amplitude of the readout oscillations, retaining
sign, similar to the pulsed protocol presented through-
out the main text.
We also periodically apply RF π
pulses to the 13C nuclei to rapidly (∼100 Hz) modulate
the sign of ⟨Iz⟩, suppressing the effect of uncontrolled
drifts in the signal.
By incrementing the free preces-
sion time during the RF encoding phase and recording
the detection-phase lock-in signal amplitude (retaining
sign) at each value of τ, we record the Ramsey inter-
ferogram shown in Fig. A14(c).
The data are fit to
an exponentially-decaying sinusoidal function, revealing
T ∗
2 = 1.5 ± 0.1 ms. The phased Fourier transform of
these data, representing the NMR spectrum, is plotted
in Fig. A14(b). This curve is fit to a Lorentzian function,
revealing a fitted FWHM of 0.26 ± 0.03 kHz.
The ODNMR and coil-based spectra are similar, in-
dicating that our method is largely addressing the same
bulk NMR signal. However there are some differences.
The NMR central frequencies differ by ∼100 Hz. We ten-
tatively attribute this to drift of the leading B0 field, as
the data were taken on different days. More significantly,
the T ∗
2 dephasing time in the coil based acquisition is ∼2
times longer than in the ODNMR case. A possible ex-
planation is as follows. To acquire the coil-based spectra
the polarization phase is much longer, Tpol = 200 s, so
there has been time for substantial spin diffusion to re-
mote 13C sites. Thus, a decent fraction of nuclei probed
in the coil-detection experiment have very small hyper-
fine coupling. In this case, the coupling may be small
enough (≲100 Hz) that any NV longitudinal relaxation
does not alter the precession frequency enough to cause
dephasing.
The observed 13C dephasing time in the coil-based
experiment is consistent with relaxation due to 13C-
13C dipolar coupling, T ∗
2 |dip ≈3 ms. The 13C dephas-
ing time observed during NV ODNMR is expected to
be influenced by a combination of 13C-13C dipolar de-
