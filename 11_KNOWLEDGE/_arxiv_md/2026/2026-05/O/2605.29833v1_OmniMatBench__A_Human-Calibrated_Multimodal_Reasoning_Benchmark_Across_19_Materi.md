Abstract
As multimodal language models play an in-
creasingly important role in scientific research,
materials science offers a critical testbed
due to its interdisciplinary, multimodal, and
application-driven nature. However, existing
materials benchmarks mainly focus on prop-
erty prediction, knowledge QA, or characteri-
zation understanding, leaving the broader rea-
soning process from materials knowledge to
application underexplored. To fill this gap, we
present OmniMatBench, a human-calibrated
multimodal reasoning benchmark for materials
science. OmniMatBench contains 3,171 expert-
curated QA and calculation problems across
19 materials-science subfields, spanning fun-
damental materials knowledge, structural and
engineering materials, materials processing and
manufacturing, and functional and applied ma-
terials. We evaluate 13 open-source and closed-
source MLLMs and find that the best model
achieves only a 0.372 overall score, revealing
a substantial gap in current materials-science
reasoning. Further analysis shows strong varia-
tion across subfields, fixed reasoning heuristics,
uneven materials knowledge, and limited high-
level knowledge application under formula-,
retrieval-, and code-assisted settings. Omni-
MatBench provides crucial insights into the
capabilities and limitations of current MLLMs
and establishes a foundation for reliable AI as-
sistants in materials-science research.

Introduction
Multimodal large language models (MLLMs)
are increasingly expected to assist scientific re-
search (Tang et al., 2026; Cui et al., 2025), yet their
reliability in domain-specific scientific reasoning
remains unclear. Materials science provides a par-
ticularly challenging and valuable testbed because
it is inherently interdisciplinary, multimodal, and
engineering-application (Lendvai and Pegoretti,
2024; Miret and Krishnan, 2025). However, ex-
isting materials-science benchmarks largely focus
on property prediction (Dunn et al., 2020; Tran
et al., 2023), knowledge QA (Weng et al., 2025;
Wu et al., 2025; Zaki et al., 2024), visual recogni-
tion (Cheung et al., 2025), characterization under-
standing (Alampara et al., 2025), or final-answer
accuracy (Zhang et al., 2025). However, existing
protocols lack systematic evaluation of materials-
science contexts spanning knowledge understand-
ing, structure–property interpretation, processing,
fabrication, and application decision making.
To address this gap, we introduce OMNIMAT-
BENCH, a human-calibrated multimodal reason-
ing benchmark for materials science. It contains
3,171 expert-curated QA and calculation problems
across 19 subfields, organized into four broad do-
mains: Fundamental Materials Science, Structural
and Engineering Materials, Materials Processing
and Manufacturing, and Functional and Applied
Materials. Each problem is paired with a proof
record documenting its source, formulation, and
solution logic, and is verified by materials-domain
experts for correctness and answer consistency.
This taxonomy reflects the Knowledge–Structure–
Processing–Application (KSPA) perspective, cov-
ering both foundational and engineering-oriented
topics, including hard-to-collect and important sub-
fields such as powder materials, gem materials and
gemology, welding technology and engineering,
and nanomaterials and technology.
OMNIMATBENCH adopts a fine-grained evalu-
ation design. QA items are annotated with expert-
curated key points covering concepts, reasoning el-
ements, scoring criteria, and common errors, while
calculation (CAL) items include structured answer
slots, formula traces, unit requirements, numerical
precision constraints, and output schemas. This
enables evaluation beyond final-answer matching
and reveals failures in concept identification, for-
mula selection, multimodal grounding, unit-aware
computation, and answer formatting.

Related Works
Materials Benchmarks and Evaluation Proto-
cols.
Materials benchmarks have evolved from
predictive modeling to language-based scientific
reasoning.
Early benchmarks such as Mat-
Bench (Dunn et al., 2020), Matbench Discov-
ery (Riebesell et al., 2025), OC20/OC22 (Chanus-
sot et al., 2021; Tran et al., 2023), and JARVIS-
Leaderboard (Choudhary et al., 2024) focus on
property prediction, stability screening, atom-
istic simulation, and materials discovery.
Re-
cent LLM and MLLM benchmarks, including
MaScQA (Zaki et al., 2024), MSQA (Cheung
et al., 2025), MatSciBench (Zhang et al., 2025),
MaCBench (Alampara et al., 2025), MatVQA (Wu
et al., 2025), MatQnA (Weng et al., 2025),
MatCha (Lai et al., 2025), and MATRIX (Mc-
Grath et al., 2026), further evaluate materials QA,
multimodal reasoning, and characterization under-
standing. However, most existing protocols em-
phasize knowledge recall, visual recognition, or
final-answer accuracy, offering limited insight into
formula selection, unit-aware computation, multi-
slot answers, and format-constrained execution.
To address these issues, OmniMatBench comple-
ments prior work by evaluating the knowledge-to-
execution gap with structured slots, formula traces,
retrieval settings, and fine-grained error analysis.

OmniMatBench is constructed through a multi-
stage, expert-involved data construction pipeline
in collaboration with materials-science specialists.
As shown in Figure 2, the source materials are se-
lected from classical and used materials knowledge
resources, and the resulting problems are organized
to cover 19 subfields aligned with the Knowledge–
Structure–Processing–Application perspective.
Experts first select, refine, and complete the do-
main knowledge from the source materials, and
then classify each candidate problem into its corre-
sponding subfield. For each problem, they identify
the core formulas, common error types, and key
concepts based on their domain expertise and ex-
perience, and distill them into scoring-oriented key
points. Meanwhile, the complete problem prove-
nance, adaptation rationale, and solution logic are
organized into the corresponding proof file. The
preliminary item pool and proof files are then
checked by an LLM verifier to ensure consistency
between the question and its visual, tabular, or for-
mula inputs, agreement between the solution and
the proof file, formatting correctness, redundancy
control, and overall item quality. Items that pass
this stage are further reviewed by materials-domain
experts, who verify disciplinary relevance, formula
correctness, reasoning validity, and answer con-
sistency. Only samples that pass both automatic
verification and expert validation are included in
the final OmniMatBench.
3.2
Dataset Statistics
OmniMatBench contains 3,171 expert-curated
questions across 19 materials-science categories,
spanning four major groups: Fundamental Mate-
rials Science (FMS), Structural and Engineering
Materials (SEM), Materials Processing and Man-
ufacturing (MPM), and Functional and Applied
Materials (FAM). To address the limited coverage
of specialized engineering topics in existing bench-
marks (Weng et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Wu
et al., 2025), OmniMatBench includes underrepre-
sented but engineering-critical subfields, such as
gem materials and gemology, powder materials,
welding technology and engineering, and nanoma-
terials and technology. These areas often require
specialized domain knowledge, engineering experi-
ence, and expert-verified solution procedures, mak-
ing them difficult to curate at scale. We therefore
collaborate with materials-domain experts to sys-
tematically collect and supplement representative
problems from these important subfields.
The benchmark includes 1,261 open-ended QA
questions and 1,910 calculation (CAL) questions.
QA items are annotated with expert-curated key
points covering core concepts, expected reasoning
elements, and scoring criteria for rubric-based eval-
uation. CAL items include structured answer slots
for evaluating numerical answers, formulas, units,
and output formatting. Detailed category statistics
are provided in Appendix Table 7.
Annotation team.
The benchmark was con-
structed by a team of 8 doctoral researchers, 60
trained annotators, and 5 materials-domain pro-
fessors, responsible for domain-level verification,
formula validation, and ambiguity resolution.
Difficulty distribution.
Each problem is as-
signed an expert-labeled difficulty level: easy,
medium, or hard. Experts determine the difficulty

Observation 2: Performance varies substan-
tially across materials-science subfields.
The
category-level results in Table 3 show that mod-
els perform better on some fundamental or well-
structured topics, such as metallic materials, ma-
terials chemistry, and welding technology and en-
gineering. In contrast, application-oriented and
engineering-intensive areas, such as metallurgi-
cal engineering, electronic information materials,
nanomaterials and technology, and composite ma-
terials molding, consistently receive lower scores.
This pattern suggests that models handle common
materials-science concepts better, but struggle with
specialized materials-engineering scenarios requir-
ing procedural knowledge, materials-specific for-
mulas, and application-oriented judgment.
Observation 3: Broad Coverage but Limited
Precision in Open-Ended QA.
Table 4 shows
a common pattern: many models achieve higher
Recall than Precision in open-ended QA. For in-
stance, Claude Opus 4.7 obtains an overall Recall
of 0.576 but a Precision of 0.481, suggesting that its
responses cover many materials-science key points
while still including unsupported or less relevant
content. This trend indicates that MLLMs can of-
ten generate broad materials-related answers, but
they still lack precise control over which scientific
points are truly relevant to the question. Thus, for
some materials QA tasks, models still struggle to
identify the most relevant scientific key points.

Limitations
Although OMNIMATBENCH covers 3,171 expert-
curated problems across 19 materials-science sub-
fields, it remains a bounded sample of materials-
science reasoning. We screened and balanced prob-
lems as much as possible while preserving eval-
uation quality, but variations in available domain
knowledge still lead to some imbalance in question
numbers and topic coverage. Some underrepre-
sented subfields, such as nanomaterials and tech-
nology, and gem materials and gemology, are often
overlooked by existing materials benchmarks but
remain important for materials research and engi-
neering applications. We therefore include these
areas while maintaining the best possible balance
under available high-quality sources.
OMNIMATBENCH focuses on expert-curated
QA and calculation-oriented CAL tasks with refer-
ence solutions, rubrics, formula traces, and struc-
tured answer slots. QA scoring key points are cross-
validated by materials-domain experts to improve
consistency and reduce ambiguity. However, these
expert-defined rubrics may still reflect subjective
judgments and may underweight valid reasoning el-
ements outside the annotated criteria. Future work
can extend OMNIMATBENCH to richer scientific
modalities and more flexible evaluation methods.
Ethical considerations
OmniMatBench was constructed with the participa-
tion of 73 materials-domain annotators and valida-
tors, including domain experts and trained graduate-
level contributors. All contributors were informed
of the purpose of the project and the intended use
of their annotations for research and benchmark
construction. They were compensated according
to local institutional standards and the amount of
work completed. The annotation process involved
problem extraction, domain adaptation, proof-file
construction, key-point annotation, and expert vali-
dation. No personal or sensitive information about
contributors is released, and the benchmark does
not contain private user data. The source mate-
rials were used only to construct expert-curated
problem–solution pairs and evaluation rubrics, and
all released items are reviewed to ensure scientific
correctness, answer consistency, and appropriate
use for academic evaluation. We further apply both
LLM-based verification and human expert valida-
tion to reduce noise, redundancy, and potential an-
notation errors.

Additional Findings on CAL
Table 8 further reveals two category-level patterns
that are less visible from the overall averages.
Performance is highly category-dependent.
CAL performance is not uniformly distributed
across the 19 categories. Models generally ob-
tain higher scores on chemistry- and processing-
centered categories, such as Materials Chemistry,
Welding Technology and Engineering, Metallic Ma-
terials, and Powder Materials. For example, sev-
eral strong models achieve their peak or near-peak
scores on Powder Materials, including GPT-5.5,
Gemini 3.1 Pro, and Qwen3.6 Plus. In contrast,
Metallurgical Engineering, Functional Materials,
Electronic Information Materials, and Composite
Materials Forming Engineering remain consistently
difficult. This suggests that CAL is especially chal-
lenging when the required slots involve implicit
process–property relationships, functional mech-
anisms, or application-specific roles, rather than
explicit material names or processing descriptors.
Similar average scores can hide different model
behaviors.
The category-wise results also show
that models with similar average CAL scores may
rely on different strengths. For instance, Claude
Opus 4.7 and Gemini 3.1 Pro obtain close overall
averages, but their category profiles differ: Gemini
3.1 Pro is stronger in several fundamental-materials
categories, whereas Claude Opus 4.7 shows ad-
vantages in multiple structural, processing, and
applied-materials categories. Likewise, Intern-S1
Pro is the best open-source model overall, but its
competitiveness is concentrated in selected cate-
gories rather than being uniform across all sub-
domains. These differences indicate that aggre-
gate CAL scores should be interpreted together
with category-level results, since the same average
performance may correspond to distinct forms of
materials-domain competence.

Detailed Benchmark Analysis
OmniMatBench evaluates not only whether a
model can answer a materials question, but also
whether it can complete a case-based reasoning
work-up: identify the relevant operation, apply it

Further Difficulty Analysis
E.1
Overall Difficulty-wise CAL Analysis
Figure 7 summarizes the overall CAL strict-slot
accuracy under different difficulty levels. The most
salient observation is the large gap between easy
examples and non-easy examples. Across all eval-
uated models, easy accuracy remains substantially
higher, ranging from 28.4% to 39.7%. In contrast,
medium and hard accuracy are much lower, with
most scores below 8% and all scores below 11%.
For example, Gemini 3.1 Pro achieves the highest
easy accuracy of 39.7%, but its medium and hard
accuracies drop to 7.6% and 9.4%, respectively. A
similar pattern appears for GPT-5.5 and Qwen3.6
Plus, whose easy accuracies are above 35%, while
their medium and hard accuracies remain around
6–7%. This suggests that current MLLMs can han-
dle simpler CAL instances to some extent, but their
strict slot-level reliability drops sharply once exam-
ples require more complex grounding, finer-grained
slot selection, or less explicit evidence.
A second observation is that the difficulty trend
is not strictly monotonic between medium and
hard examples. For most models, hard accuracy is
slightly higher than medium accuracy; Intern-S1
Pro is the clearest case, obtaining 6.0% on medium
examples but 10.9% on hard examples. This does
not imply that hard examples are intrinsically easier.
Rather, it suggests that strict-slot CAL difficulty
is affected not only by the nominal difficulty la-
bel, but also by the distribution of categories, slot
types, and textual cues within each split. Some
hard examples may contain more distinctive do-
main signals, while medium examples may involve
more ambiguous slot boundaries or require more
precise category-specific normalization. Therefore,
the difficulty-wise results indicate that CAL pri-
marily exposes a robustness gap in structured slot
grounding, rather than a simple monotonic degra-
dation from easy to hard.
