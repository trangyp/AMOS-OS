One-sided inverses in noncommutative infinitary
semigroups
Paolo Lipparini
Abstract. In a former paper we introduced partial infinitary noncommutative semi-
groups and showed, among other, that significant differences arise in comparison with
the commutative case, previously studied in the literature. For example, in the com-
mutative case we cannot have an infinitary identity e together with two elements
a ̸= e, b ̸= e such that ab = e, just under the assumption that the countable prod-
uct abababa . . . is defined. Here we show that this is possible in the noncommutative
case, actually, we can have an infinitary semigroup on a countable set with a complete
identity and such that the operation is defined for every indexed linearly ordered set.
1. Introduction
In [7] we introduced and studied the notion of a “noncommutative” infini-
tary semigroup. We have dealt with the case in which the infinitary operation
is partial and also with the case when the infinitary semigroup is complete,
namely, every product indexed by a linearly ordered sequence is defined. The
definitions generalize and encompass many previous notions introduced by var-
ious authors, including A. Tarski [10], C. Karp [6], J. H. Conway [3], D. Krob
[5], N. Bedon and C. Rispal [2], among many others. See for example [4], [8]
for a survey.
An easy argument from [7, Proposition 5.1] shows that we cannot have an
infinitary identity e together with two elements a, b such that ba = ab =
e, apart from the trivial case a = b = e.
There we stated that, instead,
we can have a complete infinitary semigroup with a complete identity if we
remove the request ba = e, namely, we just require the existence of a one-sided
inverse. Since the proof turned out to be unexpectedly long and complicated,
we decided to present it in a separate note. Here it is. This shows that the
noncommutative theory turns out to be much richer than the commutative
one, though sometimes also much more difficult.
Our main result here is the following theorem (undefined notions will be
explained in the next section).

3. Sketch of a simpler example
We first exemplify our proof by sketching the construction of a ≤ω-semigroup

case of ambiguity we will always specify whether we are dealing with string
concatenation or with a Q-product in some infinitary semigroup.
First, if σ is either a finite string, or an ω-indexed string and σ contains only
the symbols a and b, let the reduced form of σ be obtained from σ by computing
σ “as if we were in a semigroup in the ordinary sense”, with the clause ab = e,
e being a neutral element. Namely, we iteratively remove all adjacent pairs
of the form ab. This might involve removing infinitely many elements in an
infinite sequence; for example, if σ = bbaaabbaabababbabaaabaabbaaabbb . . . ,

4. Pseudonull sequences
We are now going to introduce the class of pseudonull sequences, which are
a broad generalization of those finite strings which reduce to e in a finitary
semigroup, under the assumption that ab = e. The “right” definition is far
from being straightforward, due to the difficulties hinted in Remark 3.1.
Definition 4.1. For arbitrary linearly ordered sets, we consider I-indexed
sequences whose elements, again, are only a and b.
The class of pseudonull sequences is the smallest class containing
(i) the empty sequence, and
(ii) any sequence (si)i∈I such that I can be partitioned into convex subsets
Ij (j ∈J) in such a way that, for every j ∈J, the subsequence (si)i∈Ij
is pseudonull, and
(iii) any sequence (si)i∈I such that I has a minimum i1, a maximum i2,
si1 = a, si2 = b and (si)i∈I∖{ i1,i2 } is a pseudonull sequence.
See Definition 4.2 below for a more formal definition.
In particular, by (i) and (iii), if I = { i1, i2 } and si1 = a, si2 = b, then
the sequence (si)i∈I is pseudonull. In what follows, we will denote the above
sequence by ab, namely we will consider the sequence as a string and we
will work up to reindexing. We will use a similar convention for countable
sequences. In fact, linearly ordered sequences are a generalization of strings
and some authors plainly call them strings.
In this and the next section,
juxtaposition always denotes string concatenation.
For example, the sequences ababab . . . (N-ordered) and . . . ababab . . . (Z-
ordered), hence also (aaababab . . . )bb and aa(. . . ababab . . . )bb are pseudonull.
On the other hand, . . . aaaaabbbbb . . . , is not pseudonull, essentially since in
(ii) we need to deal with a union of disjoint convex suborders. See below for
more explicit details. By the way, note that . . . aaaaabbbbb . . . is a “union” of
(not disjoint) pseudonull sequences.
In order to carry over appropriate transfinite inductions, we need an evalu-
ation of the “complexity” of pseudonull sequences. By the way, we get a more
formal definition of a pseudonull sequence.
Definition 4.2. We define the class of pseudonull sequences of degree ≤α,
for α an ordinal, by transfinite induction. The empty sequence, given by (i)
in Definition 4.1, is of degree ≤0. A sequence given by (ii) is pseudonull of
degree ≤α, where α is the smallest ordinal strictly larger than all the degrees

if τ is obtained from σ by removing a family of pairwise disjoint pseudonull
convex subsequences. As above, the sequence . . . aaaaabbbbb . . . witnesses that
(contrary to the example presented in Section 3) we do not always necessarily
have a canonical “total” reduction.
A sequence σ is regular if it has a (regularizing) reduction τ of the form
bnam, with n, m ≥0. In the case of a regular sequence, the reduction providing
bnam is “total” (this is intuitively clear, but a formal proof is quite tricky, see
items (f) and (h) in Lemma 5.1 below).
Note that in the above definition of regular we include the possibility that
n = m = 0, that is, τ can be the empty sequence. In particular, a pseudonull
sequence is regular. On the other hand, by considering clause (ii) in Definition
4.1, a sequence with an empty reduction is pseudonull. Thus a sequence is
pseudonull if and only if it has a reduction of the form n = m = 0.
For notational simplicity, it is convenient to refer to pseudonull, regular,
etc., indexes, rather than sequences.
Definition 4.4. If some fixed sequence (si)i∈I is intended and H ⊆I, we will
say that H is pseudonull if the subsequence (si)i∈H is pseudonull and we will
write “the degree of H” for “the degree of (si)i∈H”. Similarly, we say that H
is regular if (si)i∈H is regular. If this is the case, we will also say that H has a
reduction of the form (or that H reduces to) bnam if (si)i∈H has a reduction
of the form bnam.

7. Appendix I. A simpler proof for a special case of Theorem 1.1
In this appendix we present full details for the construction of a ≤ω-semigroup
with elements a, b, e satisfying the conclusion of Theorem 1.1, as sketched in
the example in Section 3.
We first need an analysis of finite strings containing only the symbols a, b,
with the intended idea that the juxtaposition ab reduces to the identity. We
let a pseudonull string be a finite string υ such that
(p1) υ contains an equal number of occurrences of a and b and, moreover,
(p2) for any initial substring η of υ, the number of occurrences of a in η is
≥than the number of occurrences of b in η.
Thus, for example, aaababbabb is a pseudonull string, but baab is not pseudonull,
since (p2) is not satisfied. From the point of view of (finitary noncommutative)
semigroups, pseudonull strings are strings which can be reduced to e under the
assumption ab = e, with e a neutral element.
Now consider a finite or ω-indexed string σ with only the symbols a and
b. The reduced form of σ is the (possibly empty) string obtained from σ by
removing all the pseudonull substrings (as usual, by a substring we always
mean a convex substring, not simply a subsequence). Note that the reduced
form of σ is a subsequence of σ, but not necessarily a substring. Also note
that in a reduction it might happen that infinitely many elements of the string
are removed, for example, if σ = bbaaabbaabababbabaaababab . . . , then the
reduced form of σ is bbaaa.
It is intuitively clear that no occurrence of the substring ab can appear in
the reduced form of σ, but a proof is needed, since, in principle, pseudonull
substrings could “amalgamate” in unexpected ways. To check the above state-
ment, and also for later use, we will prove the following properties of pseudonull
strings.
(α) A finite concatenation of pseudonull strings is pseudonull.
If ν is a
pseudonull string, then the concatenation aνb is pseudonull. More generally,
if σ is a finite string, υ is a pseudonull substring of σ and the string σ ∖υ, the
subsequence of σ obtained by removing υ, is pseudonull, then σ is pseudonull.
(β) A finite string υ is pseudonull if and only if (p1) holds and, moreover,
(p3) for any final substring η∗of υ, the number of occurrences of b in
η∗is ≥than the number of occurrences of a in η∗.
