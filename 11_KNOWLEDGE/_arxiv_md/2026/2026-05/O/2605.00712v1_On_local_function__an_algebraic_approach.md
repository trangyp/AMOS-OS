Abstract: The paper discuss the limit point concept of a subset in a group via ideal of the power set
ring. This idea along with anti-ideal give the topological structure in a group. Homomorphic images
of both ideal and anti-ideal are played the remarkable role to change the topological structure from one
system to another system.

Key words and phrases: Ideal, Anti-ideal, Group, Power set ring, Homomorphism

Introduction and Preliminaries
Group and ring theories are known to us from 19th century. The ideal on a set X is primarily defined by
Kurtatowski [12] and Vaidyanathaswamy [20]. They, and a good number of mathematicians [5, 2, 8, 6,
12, 13, 14, 16, 18] are discussed the ideals on the various field of mathematics [9, 6, 19, 15, 17, 11, 10].
However, for these discussion they used topology on the same set. The authors Das et. al [1] have
shown that this ideal is same the ideal on the power set ring. For a set G, (2G, ∆, ∩) or simple 2G,
the power set ring (means, the set 2G with two binary operation ∆and ∩, where ‘∆’ is defined as
A∆B = (A \ B) ∪(B \ A)). We through this paper, consider the local function upon a group via ideal
of the power set ring 2G, where G is a group. Homomorphism and kernel will take a major role of this
paper. Further we represent a new idea on a ring, which is called anti-ideal. Our another job through this
paper is, anti-ideal’s reflection on a group and its consequences.

Ideals on a group
Local function or action of an ideal on a group is considered as follows:
Definition 2.1. Let G be a group and A ⊆G be any set. Define A∝(I)(or simply A∝) = {g ∈G :
H ∩A /∈I, ∀H ∈G(g)}, where G(g) = {H ⊆G : H is a subgroup of G and g ∈H} and I is an
ideal on G.
Suppose x ∈A∝, then for every subgroup H containing x, H ∩A /∈I. That is, H ∩A ̸= ∅. One
can consider the concept; x is said to be a ∝-limit point of A if A intersects all subgroups containing x.
This is called local function associated with group.
Following are the properties and examples of this local function and its associated subjects.
We are starting with A∝may not be a group (even if A is a subgroup of G).
Example 2.2. Consider the Klein 4-group G = K4 = {e, a, b, c} and I = {∅, {c}, {e}, {e, c}}. Then
for the subgroup A = {e, a}, a ∈A∝but e /∈A∝.
Even, if I = Ifin on 2G, then A
∝is not a subgroup in general.
Example 2.3. Let G = Z and I = Ifin (collection of all finite subsets of G). Then for the subset
A = Ze, collection of even integers. Then A∝= Z \ {0} which is not a subgroup.
Example 2.4. Let G be any infinite group and I = Ifin. Take A to be any finite subgroup (or subset) of
G of size greater than 2 ( i.e {e, a} with a of order 2). Then A∝= ∅.
Theorem 2.5. Let G be a group and g ∈G. Then g ∈A∝⇐⇒< g > ∩A /∈I.
Proof. Let g ∈A∝. In particular < g >∈G(g) ⇒< g > ∩A /∈I.
Conversely, let < g > ∩A /∈I, for g ∈G. Then for any H ∈G(g), < g > ∩A ⊆H ∩A =⇒H ∩A /∈
I =⇒g ∈A∝.
Note that this property has been managed through the hereditary property of ideal.
Proposition 2.6. Let A be a subset of a group G. Then, for g ∈A∝=⇒gn ∈A∝, where n ∈Z.
Theorem 2.7. For two subsets A and B of a group G with A ⊆B, A∝⊆B∝.

Anti-ideal of the power set ring 2G has been characterized by the following lemma.
Lemma 3.4. aI is an anti-ideal on the power set ring 2G iff for A & B ∈aI, A /∈aI and for distinct
A, B ∈aI, A ∪B /∈aI.
Proof. Second condition of the anti-ideal can be characterized by the relation, [(A\B)∪(B \A)]∆(A∩
B) = A ∪B /∈aI.
For the first condition, suppose aI an anti-ideal. Let A & B ∈aI. If A ∈aI, then B = A ∪B ∈aI,
a contradiction. Thus A /∈aI. Next suppose A & B ∈aI implies A /∈aI. Now, if A∆B ∈aI,
then B \ A ∈aI. Since B \ A is a subset of B, then the last relation is a contradiction. Therefore,
A∆B /∈aI

Theorem 3.7. Let f : R →R′ be a ring isomorphism. Then, for an anti-ideal aI on R, f(aI) is an
anti-ideal on R′, where aI is an anti-ideal on R.
Proof. Let A′, B′ ∈f(aI). Then, f(A) = A′ and f(B) = B′, where A, B ∈aI. Now A′∆B′ =

A∆B /∈aI, then f(A∆B) /∈f(aI). Next, A ∪B = (A∆B)∆(A ∩B) /∈aI, and hence, f(A ∪B) =
