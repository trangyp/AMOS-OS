Abstract
We consider Subtraction Nim, where two players have exactly the same options,
but which is partizan in the sense that at the game ending, a partizan rule is
applied for the decision of the winner. We consider the following example: Let S
be the set of removable numbers, which is a non-empty finite subset of positive
integers greater than or equal to 2, applied for both players Left and Right. At
the end of the game, Left wins if the number of remaining tokens is even, and
Right wins if the number of remaining tokens is odd. We computed the outcomes
for many S, and found surprising phenomena that in most examples of S (almost
98% of some samples), the outcomes are L-positions for all large enough n. In
comparison, R-positions appear only occasionally. The main theorem explains
why this phenomenon occurs. We prove that n + 1 and n −1 are L-positions
when n is an R-position. Similarly, L-positions appear whenever P-positions or
N -positions appear. Only L-positions can last forever.
Keywords: Combinatorial Game Theory, Nim, Partizan Game

In both references (M.H.Albert et al. 2007) and (A.Siegel 2013), they prove fol-
lowing Theorem 1, explicitly or implicitly assuming that an impartial game is either
normal play or mis`ere play:
Theorem 1 If G is a position of an impartial game then G is in either N or P.
In this paper, we give examples of such rulesets that the condition of conventional
definition for impartial games is satisfied but the statement of Theorem 1 fails by
making the rule to decide the winner partizan, and we call these new games Ending
Partizan Games (Definition 4).
For example, consider LR-Subtraction Nim; let the set of removable numbers S
be a non-empty subset of positive integers greater than or equal to 2, which is applied
for both players Left and Right. At the end of the game, Left wins if the number of
remaining tokens is even, and Right wins if the number of remaining tokens is odd.
This game satisfies conventional Definition for impartial games, but Theorem 1 fails.
In LR-Subtraction Nim with S = {2, 3}, Left has a winning strategy regardless of
who moves first when there are 5k or 5k + 2 tokens where k ∈Z≥0. In contrast, Right
has a winning strategy only when there is 1 token. In the case S = {2, 3}, Left holds
an advantage over Right (Proposition 3).
Do there exist sets for which Right has an advantage? The answer is NO. On the
contrary, in the case S = {2, 3, 6}, Left has a winning strategy if there are more than
or equal to 8 tokens (Proposition 4). Moreover, it turns out that such cases, in which
Left consistently dominates, are rather common, if not all. Although it may initially
seem that the rule —Left wins if the number of remaining tokens is even, and Right
wins if the number of remaining tokens is odd— ensures fairness, a closer analysis
reveals that this is not so.
Theorem 5 explains why this phenomenon occurs. We prove that n + 1 and n −1
are L-positions when n is an R-position. We also prove that in a sequence of out-
comes {OS(n)}n∈Z≥0, P-positions consecutively appear min(S) −1 at most and both
sides of them are L-positions, when P-position appears. In addition, assuming that
S is finite, N-positions consecutively appear max(S) −1 at most and both sides of
them are L-positions, when N-position appears. Only L-positions can consecutively
appear forever. Professor Urban Larsson informed us that there exist references such
as (W.Johnson 2011), indicating that such examples are not unprecedented; however,
this is the only instance we have identified. This paper is the first example of Ending
Partizan Subtraction Nim.
Unless otherwise stated, we follow (M.H.Albert et al. 2007) for notation and
terminology.

2.2 Main Result
We computed the outcomes for many S (to be precise, 116794921 cases out of
134217728, almost 87%, examples of S with the minimum of S just 2 and the max-
imum of S less than or equal to 29, also 131500955 cases out of 134217728, almost
98%, examples of S with the minimum of S just 3 and the maximum of S less than
or equal to 30 ), and found that the outcomes are L-positions for all large enough n.
Theorem 5 and Lemma 6 explain why these phenomena occur.
Theorem 5 In LR-Subtraction Nim with S ⊂Z≥2, the following statements hold:
(1) If Right has a winning strategy from n, Left has a winning strategy from n ± 1.
That is,
OS(n) = R =⇒OS(n −1) = OS(n + 1) = L.
(2) If the previous player has a winning strategy in n, Left can win as the second player
in n ± 1. That is,
OS(n) = P =⇒OS(n −1), OS(n + 1) ∈{L, P}.
(3) If the next player has a winning strategy in n, Left can win as the first player in
n ± 1. That is,
OS(n) = N =⇒OS(n −1), OS(n + 1) ∈{L, N}.
(4) When P-position appears in the sequence of outcomes {OS(n)}n∈Z≥0, P-positions
consecutively appear at most min(S) −1 times and both sides of them are
L-positions.
(5) When N-position appears in the sequence of outcomes {OS(n)}n∈Z≥0, N-positions
consecutively appear and the left side is L-position. Moreover, the right side is L-
position when N-positions do not last forever. In particular, if S is a finite set,
N-positions consecutively appear at most max(S) −1 times and both sides of them
are L-positions.
To prove this Theorem, we first prepare the following Proposition. The basic idea
to prove Theorem 5 is a strategy stealing, as follows: If Right can win from n to finish

Lemma 6 In LR-Subtraction Nim with S ⊂Z≥2, the following holds:
(1) When Right can win as the first player from n, Left can win as the first player
from n ± 1. In other words,
OS(n) ∈{R, N} =⇒OS(n −1), OS(n + 1) ∈{L, N}.
(2) When Right can win as the second player from n, Left can win as the second player
from n ± 1. In other words,
OS(n) ∈{R, P} =⇒OS(n −1), OS(n + 1) ∈{L, P}.
Proof We prove both (1) and (2) simultaneously by induction on n. Let s = min(S).
Consider the case n < s. When Right can win from n < s, then n is odd and n + 1
and n −1 are even, so OS(n + 1) = OS(n −1) = L by the definition of LR-Subtraction
Nim except for the case n = s −1. However, when n = s −1, regardless of whether Left or
Right moves first, the only option in s is to take s tokens, which results in a win for Left, so
OS(n + 1) = L. Therefore, both (1) and (2) hold for n < s.
Now we assume that both (1) and (2) hold for all non-negative integers less than or equal
to n −1.
For proof of (1), suppose that in the position with n tokens, Right can win as the first
player. There exists s1 ∈S such that Right can win as the second player in n −s1 tokens.
Since OS(n −s1) ∈{R, P} and OS(0) = L, it follows that s1 < n, and hence s1 ≤n −1. By
the inductive hypothesis (2), Left can win as the second player in (n+1)−s1 and (n−1)−s1,
so in the position with n + 1 (and n −1) tokens, Left can win as the first player by taking
s1 ∈S tokens.
For proof of (2), suppose that in the position with n tokens, Right can win as the second
player. Then, for any s2 ∈S with s2 ≤n, Right can win as the first player in n −s2. By the
inductive hypothesis (1), Left can win as the first player in (n + 1) −s2 and (n −1) −s2, so
even if Right takes s2 tokens from n ± 1, Left can still win as the second player. The only
remaining case we need to consider is the case when n + 1 ∈S, we have to consider the
situation in which Right takes all n + 1 tokens from n + 1. Since the result with 0 token is
win for Left, which proves that Left wins as the second player from n + 1 and n −1.
Hence, the inductive step is complete, and the Proposition is established.
□

Remark 1 Proposition 6 does not hold if the roles of Left and Right are interchanged; this
asymmetry is the reason for the bias toward Left.
For example, let S = {2, 3}. As an example like (1), consider the case where OS(3) = N.
In this situation, Left wins if she is the first player. Furthermore, since OS(2) = L, Left also
wins when moving first. This occurs because Right is unable to remove three tokens from a
heap that only contains two.
As an example like (2), consider OS(2) = L. In this case, Left has a winning strategy as
the second player. However, since OS(3) = N, Right cannot win as the second player. This
is because Left can remove three tokens, leading to Right’s defeat.
Remark 2 In Theorem 5 (5), the finiteness assumption is essential. Suppose S = Z≥2. For
any n ∈Z≥3, from n tokens, Left can win as the first player by taking n tokens and Right can
win as the first player by taking n −1 tokens, so OS(n) = N and N-positions last forever.
Using Theorem 5, in LR-Subtraction Nim with a finite S, the following statements
on a sequence of outcomes {OS(n)}n∈Z≥0 hold:
• If R-position appears, the both sides of it are L-positions.
• P-positions appear consecutively at most min(S) −1, and both sides of them are
L-positions.
• N-positions appear consecutively at most max(S) −1, and both sides of them are
L-positions.
• No such restrictions for L-position.
Therefore, L-positions appear much more often than R-positions do. See Proposition
12 for more precise statement.

Definition 6 (p-symmetric) Let p ∈Z≥1. Non-empty subset S ⊂Z≥1 is p-symmetric if for
all s ∈S, p −s ∈S.
Notice that S is finite when S is p-symmetric.
Definition 7 (periodic) A sequence of outcomes {O(n)}n∈Z≥0 is periodic when there exist
A ∈Z≥0 and p ∈Z≥0 such that for all m ∈Z≥0 with m ≥A, the equality OS(m + p) =
OS(m) holds. We say that {OS(n)}n∈Z≥0 is periodic with a period p. We call {OS(n)}n∈Z≥0
is purely periodic when A = 0. Notice that if p is a period, multiples of p are also periods. In
particular, our period is not necessarily the minimum period.

Proposition 7 In Ending Partizan Subtraction Nim, the sequence {OS(n)}n∈Z≥0 has a
finite period if S is finite.
Proof Assume S = {s1, s2, . . . , sn} where s1 < s2 < · · · < sn. Since there are at most four
possible outcomes for each position, the number of combinations of an consecutive outcomes
that can appear in the sequence of outcomes is finite. Therefore, by the Pigeonhole Principle,
there exist positive integers x and y such that sn ≤x < y and
OS(x −sn) = OS(y −sn), OS(x −sn + 1) = OS(y −sn + 1), . . . , OS(x −1) = OS(y −1).
The outcomes of the position obtained from the position with x tokens is identical to that
of the position obtained from the position with y tokens, so it follows that OS(x) = OS(y).
Suppose that OS(x + k) = OS(y + k) holds for all k < t. Then, by the induction hypothesis,
OS(x+t−si) = OS(y +t−si) holds for any si ∈S. This implies that OS(x+t) = OS(y +t).
Thus, it is shown that the sequence of outcomes is periodic.
□

4.2 Nowakowski’s Normalization
During a discussion in Combinatorial Game Theory Colloquium V, Richard
Nowakowski made the following suggestion. LR-Subtraction Nim can be regarded as
a combinatorial game under normal play by the following; Positions where no moves
are available and the winner is Left are replaced by 1 = {0 |}, and those where no
move are available and the winner is Right are replaced by −1 = {| 0}.
By those transformation, each position in LR-Subtraction Nim can be assigned
not only an outcome class but also a game value in the sense of normal play.
In LR-Subtraction Nim, is the sequence of Nowakowski-normalized game values
periodic for every finite subtraction set S ? Our preliminary calculation shows that
when S = {2, 3}, the game values are periodic with the period 5, but when S =
{2, 3, 6}, the game values seem to be non-periodic, even additively.
To be precise, we write [n] for the game value of n tokens and −[n] for its negative
game value, OS([n + 9] −[n]) = P only for n ≡0, 2, 4, 5 mod 9 except for n = 4, and
the second difference OS([28 + 9k] −2[19 + 9k] + [10 + 9k]) is never P as far as we
computed.
