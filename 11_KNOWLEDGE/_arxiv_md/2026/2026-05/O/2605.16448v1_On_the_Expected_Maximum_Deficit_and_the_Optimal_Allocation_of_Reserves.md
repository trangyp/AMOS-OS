On the Expected Maximum Deficit
and the Optimal Allocation of Reserves
Pierre Zuyderhoff∗
Claude Lef`evre†
Abstract
This paper investigates risk measures derived from the expected maximum deficit in a
continuous-time framework and develops optimal reserve allocation strategies across
multiple lines of business. We formalize the expected maximum deficit and study its
associated distortion risk measures. Furthermore, we introduce implicitly bounded
risk measures based on the minimal capital required to meet prescribed fixed and
proportional risk tolerances, and propose approaches for optimal capital allocation
using line-specific distorted expected deficits. Theoretical results established include
static coherence and convexity properties, dynamic conditional extensions detailing
supermartingale time consistency over a fixed horizon and the evolution of capital
requirements across rolling horizons, and exact analytical optimizations of the aggregate
minimum reserve.
Keywords: Ruin probabilities, stop-loss transform, distortion risk measures, time consistency,
dynamic risk measures, optimal reserve allocation.
1
Introduction
Over the past decades, the concept of risk measures has become increasingly prominent
in the quantitative risk management literature (see, e.g., McNeil et al., 2015). Insurance
companies are traditionally required to hold capital that limits the probability of economic
ruin. However, classical baseline metrics like Value-at-Risk (VaR) present theoretical and
practical flaws. As established by Artzner et al. (1999), VaR is generally not a coherent
risk measure, failing to satisfy subadditivity and convexity. Consequently, it can penalize
diversification. Furthermore, point-in-time metrics fail to capture the actual severity of
adverse situations or the liquidity constraints that persist once a ruin threshold is breached.
To palliate these shortcomings, we develop path-dependent risk measures that capture and
mitigate the magnitude of distress.

In modern insurance risk theory, a key concern is not merely the occurrence of ruin, but
also the adequacy of reserves and the exact quantification of the potential deficit (or shortfall)
an insurer could face in an adverse scenario. The magnitude of such distress has been an
active area of research, notably since the introduction of the expected discounted penalty
function by Gerber and Shiu (1998), generalizing earlier works on the severity of ruin (e.g.,
Gerber et al., 1987). A related stream of literature studies the maximum severity of ruin (see,
e.g., Dickson, 1998; Thampi et al., 2007; Li and Lu, 2013). Although falling under the same
broad category of magnitude of distress, the maximum severity of ruin specifically evaluates
the worst-case drop strictly conditionally on ruin having occurred. This represents a distinctly
different quantity from the unconditionally evaluated expected maximum deficit studied in
this paper, which directly provides a comprehensive measure of the potential liquidity gaps
across all possible paths.
By accounting for the insured risk during the entire lifetime of the business or until a given
time horizon, researchers can design robust risk indicators (e.g., Trufin et al., 2011; Loisel
and Trufin, 2014). A natural method to address the magnitude of distress is to examine the
expected maximum shortfall, which yields a comprehensive measure of the potential liquidity
gaps in a portfolio.
Consider a continuous-time risk process starting with initial reserves u. The expected
value of the maximum deficit until any fixed time t is denoted by D(t)(u). Metrics measuring a
single-period expected shareholders’ deficit have been explored contextually by Sherris (2006)
and Kim and Hardy (2009); however, these strictly single-period models do not capture the
continuous path-dependent maximum deficit dynamics over time. The finite-time expected
maximum deficit was contextually examined by Jiang (2015), while Lef`evre et al. (2017)
utilized this specific deficit functional to establish stochastic comparison results for the
classical risk model. The primary purpose of this paper is to formally study risk measures
derived directly from this expected maximum deficit. Furthermore, as the management of
liquidity and capital risk across diversified portfolios has become a major concern, we propose
optimal capital allocation rules for distributing reserves among multiple lines of business.
Capital allocation has been extensively studied under various axiomatic risk measures (e.g.,
Tsanakas, 2009; Dhaene et al., 2012). We extend this literature by targeting the minimization
of the expected maximum deficit, an approach complementing the allocation strategies based
on the integrated surplus process explored by Loisel (2005).
The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 establishes the mathematical framework,
describes the classical risk model, and defines the expected maximum deficit, recalling
its consistency with stochastic orders.
Section 3 discusses static risk measures derived
from the expected maximum deficit, establishing their axiomatic properties—including both
fixed-tolerance convex and proportional-tolerance coherent implicit formulations. The formal
extension of monetary risk measures to unbounded c`adl`ag processes was rigorously established
by Cheridito et al. (2005). Section 4 extends this framework sequentially to formalize dynamic
risk measures, evaluating degrees of time consistency (Bielecki et al., 2025) and the evolution
of capital requirements across rolling horizons. Section 5 presents distinct approaches for
optimal reserve allocation utilizing line-specific distorted deficit functionals, demonstrating
how a global risk limit can be distributed to minimize overall capital needs. Finally, Section 6
provides concluding remarks.

Static risk measures
In this section, we propose static risk measures evaluated at time zero directly related to the
expected maximum deficit over the fixed horizon [0, t]. This maps the mathematical behavior
of the deficit into the axiomatic framework of path-dependent risk measures, providing robust
tools for static initial risk assessment.
Before detailing specific functionals, we first recall the formal definitions of convex and
coherent risk measures on the space of unbounded c`adl`ag processes H0, as established by
Cheridito et al. (2005). A functional ρ : H0 →R ∪{+∞} is called a convex risk measure if it
satisfies the following conditions for any X, Y ∈H0:
(i) Finiteness: ρ(X) ∈R for all X ∈H∞.
(ii) Translation Invariance: ρ(X + c) = ρ(X) + c for any constant c ∈R.
(iii) Monotonicity: If Xs ≤Ys almost surely for all s ∈[0, t], then ρ(X) ≤ρ(Y ).
(iv) Convexity: ρ(αX + (1 −α)Y ) ≤αρ(X) + (1 −α)ρ(Y ) for any α ∈[0, 1].
Furthermore, the functional ρ is a coherent risk measure if it is a convex risk measure that
additionally satisfies:
(v) Positive Homogeneity: ρ(λX) = λρ(X) for any λ ≥0.
Under positive homogeneity, convexity is mathematically equivalent to subadditivity, i.e.,
ρ(X + Y ) ≤ρ(X) + ρ(Y ).
We can deduce an interesting structural property regarding the scaling of risk measures.
Since the initial σ-algebra F0 is assumed to be trivial, the initial state X0 of any process
X ∈H0 is a deterministic real number. Thus, the functional strictly evaluating this initial
state, ρ0(X) = X0, safely maps to R and trivially satisfies the axioms of a coherent risk
measure. Because the class of coherent (respectively, convex) risk measures is closed under
convex combinations, we can scale down any arbitrary coherent or convex risk measure ρ by
interpolating it with this initial state functional. Specifically, for any scale factor γ ∈[0, 1], the
composite functional ˜ρ(X) = (1 −γ)X0 + γρ(X) remains a valid coherent (resp. convex) risk
measure. Consequently, when applied to the aggregate net loss process L, which intrinsically
starts at zero (L0 = 0), this composite functional reduces directly to ˜ρ(L) = γρ(L). This
demonstrates that any path-dependent coherent or convex risk measure evaluated on the
loss process can be validly scaled down by a factor γ ∈[0, 1] without violating its axiomatic
structure. Conversely, scaling up the risk measure (which would require γ > 1) applies a
strictly negative weight 1 −γ < 0 to the initial state X0, generally violating the monotonicity
axiom.

Concluding remarks
In this paper, we advanced the quantitative risk management of continuous-time insurance
portfolios by formalizing the expected maximum deficit as a robust, path-dependent risk metric.
Moving beyond binary ruin probabilities, this functional explicitly captures the financial
severity of liquidity shortfalls. By embedding this metric within the framework of distortion
risk measures, we introduced three distinct static risk evaluation tools: a coherent measure
operating at zero initial capital (ρ(t)
g ), a translation-invariant convex measure bounding the
absolute expected deficit to a prescribed fixed tolerance A (˜ρ(t)

Transitioning to inter-temporal risk evaluation, we established that dynamically updated
conditional expected deficits naturally satisfy supermartingale time consistency, mathemat-
ically guaranteeing prudent forward-looking capital provisioning. By decomposing rolling-
horizon evaluations into realized historical loss and static future expectations, we derived exact
boundary conditions for premium pricing. Specifically, we demonstrated that to maintain
premium-capital compatibility and avert dynamic regulatory arbitrage within internal models,
the continuous premium accumulation must strictly dominate the non-linearly distorted
expectation of aggregate claims, yielding explicit bounds such as c ≥EgP [S1] under discrete
annual evaluations.
Finally, we resolved the operational challenge of optimal reserve allocation across multiple
business lines through two distinct optimization criteria. Under the Marginal Summation
methodology, we demonstrated that the optimal capital distribution equates the marginal dis-
torted ruin probabilities, an algorithm that inherently prioritizes high-frequency, low-severity
lines to prevent imminent distress when capital is scarce. Conversely, under the Aggregate
Minimum Reserve methodology, we provided an exact analytical decomposition for indepen-
dent joint survival, revealing that this approach structurally overrides marginal constraints
to aggressively penalize and over-capitalize heavy-tailed components, thereby safeguarding
the portfolio against single-point systemic failures. Collectively, these methodologies supply
actuaries with comprehensive, mathematically validated instruments to optimize capital
efficiency while strictly controlling the magnitude of operational distress.
