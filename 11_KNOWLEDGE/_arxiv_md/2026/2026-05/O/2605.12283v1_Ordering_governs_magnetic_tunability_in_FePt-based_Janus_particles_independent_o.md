Abstract
Magnetic Janus particles enable remote actuation across biomedical, microfluidic, and materials applications. While
curvature-driven magnetic effects are well established at the nanoscale, their influence on magnetization reversal at
micrometer-scale particle sizes remains unresolved. Here, we combine experiment and micromagnetic simulation to
investigate curvature-dependent magnetism (i.e., diameter-dependent changes in magnetic response) in FePt-coated
Janus particles across diameters of 3–10 µm, and extended to 1–20 µm via micromagnetic simulations.
Structural
and crystallographic characterization confirms continuous coatings with near-equiatomic composition and partial L10
ordering. Magnetometry measurements reveal nearly invariant hysteresis behavior as a function of particle diameter
(µ0Hc = 1.13±0.05 T, pooled n = 8), with no statistically significant dependence of coercivity or remanence on diameter
(statistical analysis, p = 0.61 for Hc, p = 0.85 for Mr/Ms). To interpret these results, we introduce FunMaP, an open-
source micromagnetic simulation framework enabling direct comparison between experiment and idealized FePt caps.
Simulations show that curvature does not significantly influence magnetization reversal in this regime, consistent with a
locally planar limit (exchange length ℓex ≪R). In contrast, variations in chemical ordering produce substantial changes
in hysteresis shape and coercivity. These results show that, at micrometer scales, magnetic behavior in FePt Janus
particles is governed primarily by material ordering rather than curvature. This work defines a quantitative regime
boundary for curvature-dependent magnetism and provides design guidelines for programmable magnetic micro-systems.

Introduction
Asymmetric magnetic coatings on colloidal particles have enabled a wide range of functional systems, including field-
driven actuation, directed assembly, and biomedical microrobots1;2;3;4;5. Among these, Janus particles, characterized by
two chemically or physically distinct hemispheres, are particularly attractive due to their inherent structural anisotropy,
which allows precise directional control under external magnetic perturbations1. When coated with magnetic thin films,
these particles combine geometric asymmetry with functional programmability, enabling applications in targeted drug
delivery, microscale transport, and diagnostic imaging2;3;6.

Iron–platinum (FePt) based coatings are especially promising in this context due to their high magnetocrystalline
anisotropy (Ku ≈6.6 × 106 J/m3), thermal stability, and biocompatibility7;8;9. In its chemically ordered L10 phase,
FePt exhibits strong uniaxial anisotropy, making it a model system for studying magnetization reversal in confined and
curved geometries7;10;11;12;13.
As a result, FePt Janus caps have emerged as a platform for programmable magnetic
micro-agents2;14. Within this framework, two primary variables can, in principle, be used to control magnetic behavior:
geometry and material structure (here referring to chemical ordering and microstructure). In curved magnetic shells,
curvature is expected to introduce effective anisotropies and curvature-induced exchange terms, which can modify domain
wall structure, switching fields, and chirality15;16;17;18. This has led to the widespread assumption that curvature can act
as a tuning knob for magnetization reversal.
In parallel, the field of curvilinear magnetism has demonstrated that curvature can directly reprogram magnetic
behavior in nanoscale systems19;20;21;22.
When the radius of curvature (R = d/2, where d is the particle diameter)
approaches intrinsic magnetic length scales such as the exchange length (ℓex, the characteristic length scale over which
exchange interactions maintain spin alignment, defined as ℓex =
p
2A/µ0M 2s ), curvature introduces additional geometric
terms into the micromagnetic energy functional15;18.
These contributions manifest as effective anisotropies, modified
exchange interactions, and chiral spin textures, motivating the use of the thickness-to-radius ratio (t/R) as a design
parameter19;16;21. However, a key open question is whether curvature retains this role at the micrometer scale relevant
to most functional Janus systems. While curvature-driven effects are well established for radii in the tens to hundreds
of nanometers, most biomedical Janus particles operate in the 1–20 µm range1;6. For L10 FePt, the exchange length
is ℓex ≈3–5 nm, while the particle radii considered here span particle diameters of 1–20 µm (corresponding to radii
R = 0.5–10 µm). This yields ℓex/R ∼10−3–10−4, placing the system deep within the locally planar regime19;21. In
this limit, it remains unclear whether curvature continues to act as an independent control parameter for magnetization
reversal, or whether magnetic behavior is instead dominated by extrinsic factors introduced during processing.
Here, we investigate the interplay between geometric curvature and material ordering in FePt Janus particles across
experimentally accessible diameters (3–10 µm), extended to 1–20 µm in simulation. By combining superconducting quan-
tum interference device (SQUID) magnetometry and structural characterization (scanning electron microscopy (SEM),
X-ray diffraction (XRD)) with the open-source simulation toolkit FunMaP, we isolate the role of the thickness-to-radius
ratio (t/R) from the effects of chemical ordering. We demonstrate that magnetization reversal in ideal FePt caps is largely
insensitive to micrometer-scale curvature. Instead, deviations observed experimentally arise from diffusion-driven struc-
tural evolution, including incomplete L10 ordering and early-stage solid-state dewetting, which are indirectly influenced
by particle diameter during annealing23;24;25;26. In this context, it is important to distinguish between curvature as a
configurational constraint (setting anisotropy distribution) and curvature as a tunable parameter (producing diameter-
dependent behavior). These results establish that, in this regime, material structure, not curvature, acts as the dominant
tuning knob for magnetic functionality.

Results and Discussion
We establish a reproducible workflow for the synthesis of FePt-coated Janus particles, followed by structural and magnetic
validation using SEM, XRD, and SQUID magnetometry. As summarized in Figure 1, this workflow connects fabrication,
structural characterization, magnetic measurements, and micromagnetic simulations within a single framework.
This
integrated approach allows a direct comparison between ensemble-averaged experimental behavior and idealized single-
particle simulations, enabling intrinsic curvature effects to be separated from processing-induced structural variations27.
A central question in the field of curvilinear magnetism is whether geometric curvature can act as an independent control
parameter for magnetization reversal15;19;21;18. The following sections explicitly test this assumption at the micrometer
scale. Throughout this work, “curvature effects” refer specifically to diameter-dependent changes in magnetic response,
distinguishing them from curvature-imposed anisotropy configurations that are invariant across diameter.
2.1
Workflow for FePt Janus caps across curvatures
To probe curvature-dependent behavior, monodisperse SiO2 spheres with diameters of 3, 5, 8, and 10 µm were assembled
into near-monolayers via drop-casting of diluted 5% (w/v) suspensions.
The dilution factor was adjusted according
to geometric packing considerations to ensure comparable surface coverage across all particle sizes28.
As illustrated
schematically in Figure 1a, this ensures that curvature (via particle diameter) is the only geometric parameter varied,
while film thickness and packing density are held constant. All samples consist of FePt caps with a nominal thickness of
60 nm, ensuring that thickness remains constant while curvature (via particle diameter) is varied.
SEM imaging confirms that this protocol produces uniform particle monolayers with predominantly hexagonal packing
across all diameters (Figure 2a and Figure 4a). No systematic differences in packing density, ordering, or multilayer
formation are observed as a function of particle size, indicating that substrate morphology is effectively controlled. The
failure map in Figure 2b provides a comprehensive overview of process sensitivities. Multilayer formation, excessive film
thickness, columnar growth, and interparticle coalescence all arise from deviations in dilution, deposition flux, or adatom
mobility. During annealing, insufficient thermal energy preserves the disordered A1 phase, whereas excessive annealing
activates surface diffusion and promotes cap retraction23;26.
These competing mechanisms define a narrow processing window in which continuous FePt caps can be obtained while
enabling partial chemical ordering. Establishing this window is essential, as it ensures that any observed magnetic trends
are not artifacts of uncontrolled morphology and is particularly relevant for functional micro-scale systems29.
Weak features near the expected positions of the (001)fct and (110)fct superlattice reflections, observed around 23.2◦
and 41.2◦, are consistent with the onset of L10 chemical ordering, while the persistence of fundamental reflections such as
(111)fcc/(111)fct and (002)fct indicates coexistence of ordered (L10) and disordered (A1) phases30. Given the thin, partial-
coverage geometry of the FePt caps and the dominant substrate contribution, the XRD data are interpreted qualitatively
rather than as a quantitative determination of long-range order, consistent with previous reports on curved FePt-coated

anisotropy and introduces magnetically softer regions that, as shown below, play a dominant role in determining the

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) confirms near-equiatomic composition across all samples (Fe:Pt ≈49:51

Magnetic measurements (Figure 1e) show that the hysteresis response is effectively invariant across all particle diam-

simulations of ideal FePt caps (Figure 1f), indicating that curvature does not introduce a measurable contribution to
magnetization reversal in this regime.
2.2
SQUID magnetometry: absence of curvature dependence
The magnetic response was measured using SQUID magnetometry. As shown in Figure 3a, the normalized hysteresis loops
(M/Ms) for all particle diameters collapse onto a nearly identical curve, exhibiting sharp switching and high remanence.
This invariance is quantified in Table 1. Across the four diameters and two independent runs per sample, individual
coercivity values span a narrow range of µ0Hc = 1.03–1.21 T, with run-to-run variation within each diameter comparable
to the spread between diameter averages (∆Hc = 0.04–0.12 T). A one-way ANOVA on the eight individual measurements
treating diameter as the four-level grouping factor confirms no statistically significant dependence (F(3, 4) = 0.67, p =
0.61). The remanence ratio is similarly invariant, ranging from Mr/Ms = 0.917 to 0.976 across all measurements with
no systematic diameter trend (F(3, 4) = 0.27, p = 0.85). Hysteresis loss values lie between 89.0 and 110.2 J/kg, with
the corresponding ANOVA also non-significant (F(3, 4) = 4.26, p = 0.10); the limited sample size (n = 2 per diameter)
reduces statistical power for this comparison; with the observed scatter, the design has ∼80% power to detect a between-
diameter effect of ∼10 J/kg or larger, comparable to the observed ∼17 J/kg total spread. The pooled mean across all
eight measurements (µ0Hc = 1.13 T) lies within the run-to-run range of every individual diameter group, consistent with
diameter-independent reversal. Figure 3b shows that none of these parameters follow a systematic trend with particle
diameter.
Taken together, the data show no measurable influence of micrometer-scale curvature on the magnetization rever-
sal mechanism within experimental resolution. This result directly challenges the expectation, derived from nanoscale
curvilinear magnetism, that curvature should act as a primary tuning parameter15;19;22. For comparison, single-shot mea-
surements on as-deposited (A1-phase) FePt caps yield µ0Hc ≈3 −8 mT across the same diameter range (Section S9.3,
Table S3), more than two orders of magnitude below the annealed values and confirming that the high coercivity is a
consequence of the L10 phase transformation rather than an intrinsic property of FePt at this thickness and geometry.

2.3
Morphological evolution: curvature influences processing, not magnetism
SEM analysis after annealing (Figure 4) reveals that the FePt caps remain largely continuous while developing surface
roughness and grain-boundary grooving.
This morphology differs from the complete fragmentation often observed in
planar thin films and instead corresponds to early-stage solid-state dewetting23;24;25.
As illustrated in Figure 4c, the hemispherical geometry imposes a thickness gradient (t(θ) = t0 cos θ, with t0 ≈60 nm
at the pole), leading to a continuous reduction in thickness toward the cap perimeter. Consequently, the local thickness
approaches zero near the equator, where diffusion-driven evolution is fastest.
Consistent with this thickness dependence, morphological changes are localized at the rim, while the central cap region
remains comparatively smooth and continuous. The presence of interparticle bridges in the 3 µm samples (Figure 4b)
further indicates enhanced lateral mass transport at small interparticle separations.
However, despite these structural variations, no corresponding change in magnetic switching behavior is observed.
2.4
Micromagnetic simulations: intrinsic baseline
Micromagnetic simulations of ideal FePt caps provide a reference for intrinsic behavior. As shown in Figure 5c, simulated
hysteresis loops remain effectively invariant across all particle diameters from 1 to 20 µm. This invariance is consistent
with the locally planar limit (ℓex/R ≪1), where curvature-induced micromagnetic effects are negligible15;19;21.
This is reflected quantitatively in Table 1: at every fixed phase composition, simulated coercivity varies by less than 5%
across the full 1–20 µm diameter range. For fully ordered L10 FePt (A1 = 0%), µ0Hc spans 11.85 T (1 µm), 12.09 T (3 µm),
12.17 T (5 µm), 12.16 T (8 µm), 12.36 T (10 µm), and 12.35 T (20 µm), corresponding to a mean of µ0Hc = 12.17±0.18 T
(relative spread 4.2%). At the representative case shown in Figure 5c (A1 = 5%), the mean coercivity across six diameters
is µ0Hc = 5.64 ± 0.11 T (spread ∼2%) with mean remanence ratio Mr/Ms = 0.467 ± 0.010 (∼2%).
These residual
variations reflect numerical convergence rather than a curvature effect.
A subtle but important distinction emerges from the simulations. Curvature does play a role in the magnetic response,
but only by determining the spatial distribution of the easy axis: in a hemispherical FePt cap, the easy axis follows the
local surface normal, ˆeu(r) ∥ˆr, producing a distributed (radial) anisotropy configuration that differs qualitatively from
the uniform configuration of a planar film.
This distinction is quantitative. As shown in Figure 6a, simulations with a uniaxial easy axis (ˆeu ∥ˆz) at A1 = 5%
yield a square loop with µ0Hc = 11.62 T, while simulations with a radial easy axis on the same geometry and phase
composition yield a sheared loop with µ0Hc = 5.53 T, a 2.1× reduction in coercivity arising solely from the change
in easy-axis distribution. The experimental SQUID loops (Figure 3a) are reproduced only by the radial configuration,
confirming that curvature does shape the reversal mechanism through this geometric constraint.
Notably, the uniaxial A1 = 5% coercivity (11.62 T) is comparable to the fully ordered L10 baseline (12.17 T), demon-
strating that radial easy-axis dispersion provides the dominant initial reduction in coercivity relative to the fully ordered
uniaxial case, with chemical disorder introducing additional tuning in the curved geometry. Adding A1 disorder on top

the investigated regime.. Curvature therefore acts as a configurational rather than a tunable parameter in this regime: it
defines the anisotropy distribution but does not allow that distribution to be modified through diameter selection.
2.5
Material ordering as the dominant tuning knob
In contrast to curvature, simulations show a strong and systematic dependence on phase composition. As summarized in
Table 1 and Figure 6c, increasing the A1 fraction from 0% to 20% reduces the coercive field from µ0Hc = 12.17 ± 0.18 T
(mean across diameters at A1 = 0%) to 5.64 ± 0.11 T at 5% A1, 5.23 ± 0.10 T at 10% A1, and 3.81 ± 0.10 T at 20% A1.
The remanence ratio drops in parallel from Mr/Ms ≈1.0 (0% A1) to 0.47 ± 0.01 (5% A1), 0.41 ± 0.01 (10% A1), and
0.33 ± 0.01 (20% A1). The most pronounced change occurs at low A1 fractions: introducing 5% disorder reduces µ0Hc
by a factor of 2.2 (12.2 →5.6 T) and Mr/Ms by approximately 50% (1.0 →0.47). Increasing A1 fraction further (5% →
20%) reduces µ0Hc by an additional factor of 1.5 (5.6 →3.8 T), confirming that even small deviations from full L10 order
strongly modify hysteresis behavior.
The experimental coercivity (µ0Hc = 1.13 T pooled mean, range 1.03–1.21 T) is approximately 11× smaller than the
fully ordered limit (12.17±0.18 T) and ∼3.4× smaller than even the most disordered simulated case (20% A1, 3.81±0.10 T),
indicating that the experimental samples behave as if they were in a partially disordered state with additional softening
contributions beyond chemical disorder alone.
The strong sensitivity of Hc to A1 fraction has a direct micromagnetic origin. The disordered A1 phase exhibits
a vanishingly small magnetocrystalline anisotropy (KA1 ∼103 J/m3, four orders of magnitude smaller than KL10 ≈
6.6 × 106 J/m3), and therefore reduces the local effective anisotropy according to
Keff ≈(1 −f)KL10 + fKA1,
(1)
where f is the A1 volume fraction. Inserting f = 0.05 predicts a ∼5% reduction in mean anisotropy, yet the simulated
coercivity drops by ∼54%, indicating that the A1 grains’ contribution is not simply a linear average but an active reversal
mechanism.
In the mixed-phase microstructure, A1 grains play two coupled roles during reversal: (i) Soft nucleation sites.
Because their local anisotropy is negligible, A1 grains rotate reversibly under modest applied fields and act as low-barrier
nucleation centers. Reversal initiates within the soft regions and propagates outward into the harder L10 matrix. (ii)
Exchange-spring components. The A1 grains remain exchange-coupled to neighboring L10 regions through grain
boundaries31. As the field is reduced from saturation, A1 magnetization rotates first, producing the characteristic loop
shoulder near zero field visible in Figure 6c, pulling on neighboring L10 moments through exchange and progressively

2.6
Coupled role of ordering and morphology
Even at the highest simulated disorder level (20% A1, µ0Hc = 3.81 ± 0.10 T), the simulated coercivity exceeds the
experimental value (1.13 T pooled mean, range 1.03–1.21 T) by a factor of ∼3.4. This residual gap is too large to be
attributed to chemical ordering alone and is instead consistent with structural softening from the morphological evolution
observed in SEM (Figure 4).
Dewetting kinetics follow τn ∝h4/Ds 23;24, where h is the local film thickness and Ds the surface diffusion coefficient.
The fourth-power dependence on thickness implies that the rim region (where t →0 as θ →π/2, see Figure 4c) evolves
orders of magnitude faster than the apex. Consistent with this, SEM analysis localizes morphological changes (roughening,
grain-boundary grooving, and partial edge retraction) at the cap perimeter while the central region remains comparatively
continuous. These morphological features can influence magnetic behavior through two mechanisms. First, exchange
decoupling: grain-boundary grooving may reduce effective exchange coupling when characteristic feature sizes approach
or exceed the exchange length, rather than as a direct consequence of structural discontinuity. This distinction is important,
as structural fragmentation alone does not necessarily alter hysteresis behavior if exchange connectivity is preserved32.
Second, localized soft regions: the rim thickness gradient creates regions where the cap is sufficiently thin for finite-size
effects (e.g., reduced anisotropy due to surface contributions) to lower Keff, generating additional nucleation sites for
reversal.
Within this framework, curvature does not act as a direct tuning knob.
Rather, it influences magnetic behavior
indirectly by setting the thickness distribution t(θ) = t0 cos θ, which in turn biases dewetting kinetics and the density
of soft nucleation sites. This explains why experimental coercivity falls below the chemically-disordered simulation limit
while remaining diameter-invariant: the morphological softening is curvature-driven through t(θ), but the integrated
effect across the cap does not scale with R in the micrometer-scale curvature regime (R ≫ℓex) with fixed film thickness
(t ≈60 nm).
In FePt caps at micrometer-scale curvature (set by particle diameter) but nanometer-scale thickness (t ≈60 nm), three
effects must be distinguished. (i) Curvature configures the magnetic system by setting a radial easy-axis distribution, which
fundamentally alters loop shape relative to a planar film (Hc reduces from ∼12 T uniaxial to ∼5.6 T radial). (ii) Chemical
ordering tunes the magnitude of the anisotropy contrast within that configuration, scaling Hc inversely with increasing A1
fraction. (iii) Morphology modulates the residual response through exchange decoupling and localized softening, closing
the remaining gap between simulation (∼3.8 T at 20% A1) and experiment (∼1.1 T). What curvature does not do is
produce a diameter-dependent response in this regime.

Conclusion
We present a combined experimental and micromagnetic investigation of FePt Janus caps across micrometer-scale curva-
tures (3–10 µm), integrating synthesis, structural characterization, SQUID magnetometry, and simulation within a unified
framework (Figure 1). This approach enables a direct separation of intrinsic micromagnetic behavior from processing-
induced structural effects. Contrary to expectations derived from nanoscale curvilinear magnetism, we find that curvature
does not constitute an independent tuning parameter for magnetization reversal in this regime. Both SQUID measure-
ments (Figure 3, Table 1) and micromagnetic simulations (Figure 5) show that coercivity, remanence, and switching
behavior remain effectively invariant with particle diameter. This invariance is consistent with the locally planar limit
(ℓex/R ≪1), where curvature-induced contributions to the micromagnetic energy are suppressed.
Instead, the magnetic response is governed by material properties, specifically chemical ordering (which sets the mag-
netocrystalline anisotropy) and microstructural heterogeneity (which modifies exchange coupling and local anisotropy).
Simulations demonstrate a continuous and order-of-magnitude dependence of coercivity on A1 phase fraction (Figure 6,
Table 1), establishing that even small deviations from full L10 order strongly modify hysteresis behavior. The experimental
response is consistent with a partially ordered system, confirming that phase composition, rather than curvature, governs
the intrinsic reversal mechanism. The remaining discrepancy between idealized simulations and experimental measure-
ments is explained by diffusion-driven morphological evolution. SEM analysis (Figure 4) reveals thickness gradients and
grain-boundary grooving consistent with early-stage solid-state dewetting. These features introduce spatial heterogeneity
in anisotropy and exchange coupling, leading to reduced coercivity and broadened switching. Within this framework,
curvature influences magnetic behavior indirectly by modifying the thickness distribution and therefore the local diffusion
kinetics and ordering pathways during annealing, rather than through direct modification of the micromagnetic energy
landscape.
Taken together, these results establish a clear hierarchy of control parameters for FePt Janus caps at micrometer
length scales: (i) chemical ordering defines the intrinsic magnetic response, (ii) morphology modulates switching through
structural heterogeneity, and (iii) geometry configures the easy-axis distribution (radial vs. uniaxial), which fundamentally
shapes the hysteresis loop but does not introduce a diameter-dependent tuning parameter in this regime. This defines
a curvature-insensitive but processing-sensitive regime for magnetic Janus systems. The results provide practical design
guidelines for magnetic microrobots and functional colloids, shifting the focus from geometric engineering toward precise
control of phase transformation, diffusion kinetics, and thin-film morphology.
More broadly, this work establishes a
quantitative boundary for curvature-dependent magnetism, demonstrating that geometric effects become negligible when
the radius of curvature greatly exceeds intrinsic magnetic length scales (ℓex/R ≪1).

4
Methods
4.1
Particle Monolayer Preparation and FePt Deposition
Monodisperse SiO2 particles (3–10 µm diameter, microParticles GmbH) were assembled into near-monolayers on Si sub-
strates (5 × 5 mm2) via drop-casting of diluted aqueous suspensions. Dilution factors were determined from geometric
packing considerations to achieve close-packed coverage (Supporting Information). Substrates were cleaned by sequential
sonication in deionized water and ethanol/acetone (1:1), followed by oxygen plasma treatment (30 W, 10 min) to enhance
wettability. A 25 µL droplet was deposited immediately after plasma activation and dried under ambient conditions for
at least 12 h.
FePt thin films were deposited by molecular beam epitaxy via co-evaporation of Fe and Pt under high vacuum (<
1 × 10−8 mbar). Fluxes were adjusted to obtain near-equiatomic composition (50:50 ± 5 at.%), and a nominal thickness
of 60 nm was monitored in situ using a quartz crystal microbalance. Continuous substrate rotation ensured uniform
azimuthal coverage.
4.2
Thermal Processing and Structural Characterization
Post-deposition annealing was performed at 500◦C for 1 h under argon atmosphere to promote partial A1 →L10 phase
transformation while limiting excessive solid-state dewetting of the FePt caps. Preliminary experiments indicated that
FePt films on curved/spherical substrates exhibited the onset of diffusion-driven morphological evolution at lower tem-
peratures than comparable planar films, particularly near the cap perimeter where the local film thickness decreases.
These conditions therefore represent a compromise between chemical ordering and preservation of cap continuity during
annealing. Structural and morphological characterization was carried out using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss
GeminiSEM500, 2–8 kV) in both top-view and tilted configurations to assess cap continuity and curvature-dependent
morphology. Elemental composition was verified by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX, 25 kV, area-averaged
mapping), confirming near-equiatomic FePt. Phase identification was performed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using Cu Kα
radiation with cross-beam optics.
4.3
Magnetic Characterization
Magnetic hysteresis loops were measured using a Quantum Design MPMS3 SQUID magnetometer in vibrating sample
mode at 300 K with applied fields up to ±7 T. Diamagnetic background contributions from the substrate were subtracted
using linear fits to the high-field regions (Supporting Information), and magnetization was normalized to the saturation
value. The coercive field (Hc), remanence ratio (Mr/Ms), and hysteresis loss (Whyst) were extracted from the corrected
loops.

4.4
Micromagnetic Simulations
Micromagnetic simulations were performed using OOMMF via the Ubermag Python interface. FePt caps were modeled
as hemispherical shells with inner radius R = d/2 and thickness t = 60 nm, using material parameters representative of
L10 FePt (Ms = 1.0 × 106 A/m, A = 1.0 × 10−11 J/m, Ku = 6.6 × 106 J/m3). Unless otherwise stated, the easy axis was
aligned along the local surface normal to represent a radial anisotropy configuration. Hysteresis loops were computed via
quasi-static energy minimization by sweeping the applied field between ±18 T with a small transverse bias field to avoid
metastable states. Additional numerical details and geometry implementation are provided in the Supporting Information.
