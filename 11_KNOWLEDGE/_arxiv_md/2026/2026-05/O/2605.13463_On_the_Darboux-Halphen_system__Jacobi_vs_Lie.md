Abstract
Two constructions of the Darboux-Halphen system are discussed. In the Jacobi construction we
start with multi-valued functions which are fixed as the first integrals. In the Lie construction we
use single-valued representation of the simple algebra which is non-integrable in Lie’s terminology.

Introduction
The Darboux-Halphen system

determined in 1881 by Halphen [2, 3] and Brioschi [4] in terms of the elliptic modular function; that
is, the square of the elliptic modulus, viewed as a function of the ratio τ of the periods of the Jacobi
elliptic functions. The physical contexts in which these equations have appeared include dynamics of
magnetic monopoles pairs; theory of vacuum Einstein equations for hyperk¨ahler Bianchi-IX metrics,
similarity reductions of associativity equations on a three-dimensional Frobenius manifold [5].
There is a strong connection between the integrability of a system of ODEs and the Painlev´e
property, which asserts that all solutions of the system are single-valued in their domain of existence
where the movable singularities are either poles or isolated essential singular points. Classical Darboux-
Halphen system and its known generalisations are integrable in the sense that their solutions can be
expressed in terms of two linearly independent solutions of certain hypergeometric equations. However,
some generalizations do not possess the Painlev´e property in the usual sense because their solutions
admit a movable natural barrier instead of isolated singular points, see [5, 6, 7, 8] and references
within. In the context of modern topological mechanics, multi-valued functionals and non-exact closed
differential 1-forms are discussed in [9].
Our aim is to compute Darboux-Halphen type polynomial differential systems by using classical
Jacobi and Lie constructions of complete differential systems. Both construction are applicable to
complete differential systems with multi-valued first integrals. The main difference is that we start
with the multi-valued functionals in the Jacobi method, whereas in the Lie method we start with
single-valued realization of non-integrable infinitesimal transformation group (non-solvable Lie algebra
in modern terms).

The Jacobi theory involves functions fk(x1, . . . , xn) of any number of variables, the properties of
which are not explicitly specified. It is not even clear whether the variables are assumed to be real or
complex. Mathematicians in this period generally regarded variables as complex rather than real, as
this assumption allows for the existence of partial derivatives of these functions and makes frequent
use of the equality of mixed partial derivatives. It is far less certain whether Jacobi was fully aware of
the local nature of his results, but the reader should understand them as local results that are valid
in the neighbourhood of any point that satisfies the specified conditions. It was not until the 20th
century that mathematicians began to take the distinction between local and global results seriously.

Lie’s construction of complete equations

by the appearance of Jacobi’s theory is reflected in the publication of Imschenetsky monographic essay

Klein’s recollections, while he and Lie were together in Paris in 1870, Lie studied Imschenetsky’s essay
with great enthusiasm, to the point of satiety.

3
Conclusion
Fundamental contributions of Jacobi and Lie are the base of classical theory of complete and incomplete
systems of differential equations. Of course, we have to remember the key-stones of this theory to study
a complex behavior of physically important differential equations.
In this note we recover well-known Darboux-Halphen system of equations in the framework of
the Jacobi and Lie theories of complete Pfaffian equations. Our main aim is to prove that both these
theories can be useful to construct various generalisations of differential systems with known complete
solutions.
