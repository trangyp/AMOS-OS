ON PARTIAL GROUPS OF SMALL ORDER
PHILIP HACKNEY
Abstract. There are 123,650 partial groups of order at most 9 and 178,937,003
partial groups of order 10. We explain a computer enumeration of these results
and provide a complete list of indecomposable partial groups of order at
most 5. We also indicate results that were discovered through computational
experiments.
1. Introduction
Partial groups, and the well-behaved subclass of localities, were introduced by
Chermak in [Che13] as a part of his proof of the existence and uniqueness of centric
linking systems, a problem posed in [BLO03]. Localities are an encapsulation of
the idea of doing p-local analysis in finite group theory without the presence of
an ambient finite group. The other main implementation of this idea is via the
fusion systems of Puig [Pui06, AO16], which are closely related to localities (see e.g.
[CH22, Hen25]).
Our focus in this paper is not on localities, but on general partial groups, which
have excellent categorical properties [Che22, Sal23, HL25]. We anticipate a number
of ‘locality-like’ structured partial groups outside the bounds of p-local finite group
theory, with the first examples being the punctured Weyl groups from [HL]. Partial
groups also have a nice extension theory [BG], may be considered as generalizations
of the pregroups of Stallings [LM24, Sta71], and are flexible enough that each group
is the group of automorphisms of some partial group [DMV26].
Our core contribution is a complete enumeration of all partial groups of order
at most 10 (see Table 1 for orders up to 9, and Section 5). Two prior results make
such an enumeration feasible. First, a finite partial group is indeed a finite structure
[HM26] (it has only finitely many ‘nondegenerate’ simplices), even though it is not
part of the definition. Second, every partial group sits inside a canonical maximal
partial group determined by its underlying binary partial group [HLSb], which gives
the search a known ceiling. The enumeration is coupled with a collection of routines
for working with finite partial groups [Hac26b], written in the Julia programming
language [BEKS17]. Our aim is to enable experimentation: studying properties of
individual partial groups, constructing examples exhibiting desired behaviors, or
testing conjectures across a catalog.

2. Mathematical Background
We begin with necessary background, building on the point of view on partial
groups from [HL25]. The original definition of partial groups is due to Chermak in
[Che13], and the interpretation as simplicial sets appears in work of González [Gon].
2.1. Symmetric sets and partial groups. Let Υ be a skeleton of the category of
finite nonempty sets; for concreteness, we take as objects the sets [n] = {0, 1, . . . , n}
for n ≥0. A symmetric set is a functor X : Υop →Set whose value on [n] will be
denoted by Xn (the set of n-simplices) and whose value on α: [m] →[n] in Υ will
be denoted by α∗: Xn →Xm. We write Sym for the category of symmetric sets,
whose morphisms are natural transformations. Notice that Aut([n]) is the symmetric
group on n + 1 letters {0, 1, . . . , n}, so Xn naturally has a right Σn+1-action.
In particular, X1 is canonically an involutive set, via the nontrivial automorphism
of [1]. As the unique map [1] →[0] is split, the corresponding X0 →X1 is injective;
elements in its image, here called identities, always are fixed points for the involution.
Example 2.1. If S is a set, let Mat(S)n denote the set of (n+1)-by-(n+1) matrices
with entries drawn from S, i.e. the set of functions [n] × [n] →S. This naturally
assembles into a symmetric set: given α: [m] →[n] and a function [n] × [n] →S,
we can precompose by α × α to obtain a function [m] × [m] →S. In other words, if
M = (fij)0≤i,j≤n ∈Mat(S)n, then α∗M = (gab) where gab = fα(a),α(b).

embed into the category of symmetric sets. A symmetric set is isomorphic to the
nerve of a group if and only if it is reduced (i.e. X0 is a one-point set) and the Segal
maps are bijections for all n. This leads us to a definition of partial group [Che13],
based on Corollary 4.7 of [HL25].

(Bousfield-)Segal maps are injections. The symmetric set Mat(S) is not often spiny.
If X is any spiny symmetric set, then µX : X →Mat(X1) is a monomorphism. We
often use µX to write n-simplices as (n + 1)-by-(n + 1) matrices (fij). Other papers
in the partial groups literature consider ‘multipliable words’ in X1 as the primary
representation of n-simplices; such a word w = (g1, . . . , gn) then corresponds to
x ∈Xn satisfying En(x) = w.
2.2. Skeleta and Dimension. An n-simplex x in a symmetric set X is nonde-
generate if there does not exist a pair (y, ρ) consisting of an m-simplex y and a
noninvertible surjection ρ: [m] ↠[n] such that x = ρ∗y. If X is a spiny symmetric
set, x ∈Xn, and (xij) = µX(x), then the following are equivalent (see [HL, Lemma
2.13] and [HM26, Lemma 7]):
(1) x is nondegenerate.
(2) If xij is an identity (i.e. in the image of X0 →X1), then i = j.
(3) No row of (xij) contains a repeated element.
(4) Bn(x) consists of distinct, nonidentity elements.
Note: a degenerate element may have all nonidentity elements on its superdiagonal.
Definition 2.5. Let n ≥−1 be an integer. A symmetric set is n-skeletal if all
m-simplices for m > n are degenerate. The n-skeleton of a symmetric set X, denoted
skn X, is the smallest symmetric subset of X containing all k-simplices for k at
most n. A symmetric set is said to have dimension n if it is n-skeletal but not
(n−1)-skeletal.
If X and Y are finite dimensional partial groups, then their coproduct X ∨Y
has dim(X ∨Y ) = max(dim(X), dim(Y )).

4. Enumerating partial groups of small order
An approach to enumerating all partial groups is the following:
(1) Start with a fixed n-dimensional partial group X.
(2) Find all (n+1)-dimensional partial groups Y with the property that skn Y =
X.
(3) Reduce this collection by modding out by isomorphisms fixing the n-skeleton:
pick one representative from each isomorphism class.
(4) Increase n and repeat this process for each representative Y appearing in
the previous step.
If we start with the unique 0-dimensional partial group, then in the limit we will
obtain all partial groups up to isomorphism.
We wish instead to enumerate all partial groups of a fixed order. For order 1,
there is only the trivial group. Otherwise, we start with a 1-dimensional partial
group in (1), which is more or less the same thing as an involutive set.
4.1. The involutive set. Given a partial group X, one can consider the involutive
set I = X1 \id of nonidentity edges. Up to isomorphism, a finite involutive set is fully
specified by pair (free, fixed) = (a, b) of nonnegative integers, where a represents
the number of free orbits under the involution, and b represents the number of fixed
orbits. For concreteness, we always suppose that X1 is a set of integers of the form
X1 = [−a, a + b] = {0, ±1, . . . , ±a, a + 1, . . . , a + b}
where 0 is the identity, the involution acts via negation on [−a, a] and fixes [a+1, a+b].

6. The degree of a partial group
In this section we recall the (higher Segal) degree of a spiny symmetric set X,
following [HL, §4.2], and explain an implementation for computing degree. The
degree invariant is derived from the higher Segal conditions for simplicial objects,
which are based on the combinatorics of triangulations of cyclic polytopes and
have connections to higher algebraic K-theory [Pog], the Dold–Kan correspondence
[DJW19], and other areas; see [Dyc26] for a survey. In the context of partial groups,
the higher Segal conditions are closely related to intersection patterns of closed
subsets in closure spaces [HL], and to a form of associativity [HLSa, §3].
6.1. Computing degree. When computing degree, it is helpful to work with the
Bousfield–Segal map Bn and the associated starry word complex SX [HL, Definition
2.10]. Level-wise, SX is given by SX0 = X0, and for higher n
SXn = {(f1, . . . , fn) | d1f1 = · · · = d1fn} ⊆X×n
1
.
It is naturally a presheaf on the subcategory of Υ on those maps which fix 0 via
α∗(f1, . . . , fn) := (fα(1), . . . , fα(n)) with the understanding that f0 is an identity
with the same source as the edges f1, . . . , fn. In particular, for 1 ≤i ≤n, the face
map di simply deletes entry i and the degeneracy si duplicates fi, while s0 inserts
an identity at the beginning of the word. The Bousfield–Segal maps combine into
an injective map of presheaves B : X →SX.
Definition 6.1. A spiny symmetric set X has degree at most k (for k ≥1) if for each
n ≥k+1, whenever w ∈SXn has the property that dn−kw, . . . , dnw ∈Bn−1(Xn−1),
then w ∈Bn(Xn). The degree of X is the least positive integer k such that X has
degree at most k.
See [HL] for the definition of degree for more general symmetric sets, which is
based on an approach to the higher Segal conditions for simplicial objects from
[Wal20]. We note that a symmetric set has degree 1 if and only if it is isomorphic
to the nerve of a groupoid.
For w ∈SXn, we write w ∈SXm for the word where we have deleted all identities
and kept only the first instance of any non-identity fi appearing (if w consists entirely
of ida, then w is simply a ∈X0 = SX0). There is a surjection σ: [n] ↠[m] such that
w = σ∗w, and by choosing a zero-preserving section δ: [m] ,→[n] we have w = δ∗w.
We conclude that w ∈Bn(Xn) if and only if w ∈Bm(Xm). By our characterization
of nondegeneracy, if w = Bm(x) then x ∈Xm is nondegenerate. We call general
elements of SX which do not contain identities or duplicates nondegenerate.

Proposition 6.2. Suppose X is a spiny symmetric set and k ≥1. Then X has
degree at most k if for each n ≥k +1 and each nondegenerate word w ∈SXn having
dn−kw, . . . , dnw ∈Bn−1(Xn−1), we also have w ∈Bn(Xn).
Proof. One direction of the equivalence is clear. For the reverse, assume the condition
from the proposition and suppose w = (f1, . . . , fn) ∈SXn has dn−kw, . . . , dnw ∈
Bn−1(Xn−1). If for some n −k ≤i ≤n the element fi appears elsewhere in w, or
is an identity, then w is degenerate on diw, hence is in Bn(Xn). Assuming this
is not the case, then the reduced version w = (g1, . . . , gm) has the exact same
tail: gm−k = fn−k, . . . , gm = fn. Further, diw = di−m+nw for m −k ≤i ≤m.
As di−m+nw ∈Bn−1(Xn−1), we have diw = di−m+nw ∈Bm−1(Xm−1), so by
assumption w ∈Bm(Xm). This implies w ∈Bn(Xn).
□
Remark 6.3. One could likewise make a similar statement by instead working with
the Segal maps En, or with µX : X →Mat(X1). The important thing is to have an
ambient object to choose nondegenerate elements from. For the Segal maps this is
BP, where P is the underlying BPG of X, while for µX it is Mat(X1) itself.
The upshot is that computing degree for a finite partial group is a finite process,
and is one that works well with our existing implementation of finite partial groups
(Remark 4.7). Let us explain the basic algorithm.
For n ≥k + 1, start with some nondegenerate element x ∈Xn−1 ⊆SXn−1,
which we write as x = (a1, . . . , an−1). We then consider all extensions of this word
to w = (a1, . . . , an−1, an) where an ∈X1 \ {a1, . . . , an−1, id}. We then look to see
whether the faces dn−kw, . . . , dn−1w are in Xn−1 (we do not need to check dnw = x
is in Xn−1), and if they are, whether or not w ∈Xn. If some face is not in Xn−1,
or if all faces are in Xn−1 and w ∈Xn, then we continue the loop with the next
element of Xn−1, otherwise we stop since we’ve established that X does not satisfy
the condition. After processing all elements of Xn−1, we increase n and continue.
Remark 6.4. There is a small optimization that we include: we only process those
x ∈Xn−1 such that the substrings (a1, . . . , an−k−1) and (an−k, . . . , an−1) of x
are ordered. This is acceptable since the process from the previous paragraph is
insensitive to the action by Σn−k−1 ×Σk. For the corpus of partial groups of order 9,
this amounts to a 2.82-fold speedup (and will be more significant for larger partial
groups).

Appendix B. Partial groups of dimension two less than order
Recall from Theorem 2.6 that a partial group X of order n + 1 is a group if and
only dim(X) = n. We establish the following related result, which is also true for
order ≤5 by the explicit list in Appendix A.
Theorem B.1. Suppose that X is an indecomposable partial group of order n+1 ≥6
and dim(X) = n −1. Then X = skn−1 BG for a (unique) group G.
Proof. Consider a nondegenerate (n−1)-simplex M = (fij) in Xn−1, and let a be

Appendix C. Data format and usage
A companion to this paper is a data set consisting of all partial groups and BPGs
of order at most 10 [Hac26a]. These are both stored in the JSON Lines format
(see https://jsonlines.org/) for ease of processing. A reference importer (in
Python) is included alongside the data set. The associated Julia project [Hac26b]
also contains facilities for importing from the data set.
C.1. The BPG file format. The binary partial groups are split into two files: one
for all BPGs of order up to 9, and another, much larger file containing the BPGs of
order 10. Each line represents a BPG, and consists of four fields:
free: the number of free orbits in the underlying involutive set.
fixed: the number of nonidentity fixed points in the underlying involutive set.
bpg_hash: a string which gives a stable short identifier (derived from SHA-256).
mults: an array of nontrivial generating multiplications for the BPG.
The (free,fixed) pair determines the underlying involutive set (see Section 4.1).
Each generating multiplication in mults takes the form [a,b,c], which should be
interpreted as specifying a · b = c. The full BPG may be reconstructed as the union
of the 1-skeleton (specified by the involutive set) together with a collection of atomic
BPGs, one for each element of mults. As examples:
• [1,0,"f1_x0_200428d33786",[]] is the representation for the free partial group
on one generator.
• [1,0,"f1_x0_b50145ab2deb",[[1,1,-1]]] is the representation for C3 (consid-
ered as a BPG).
• [1,2,"f1_x2_7d59313238d9",[[1,1,-1],[1,3,2]]] is the BPG called P2 in
Appendix A.
We warn that the algorithm for computing bpg_hash is not isomorphism-invariant.

useful for finding which partial groups have the same underlying BPG, without
