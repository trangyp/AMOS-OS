Abstract
We study the problem of covering the maximum number of vertices in a graph by a collection of
vertex-disjoint stars, each with a number of satellites in a given interval [k, ℓ], where 1 ≤k < ℓand ℓcan

1.1
Other related work
The star packing problem belongs to the general H-packing framework, where H is a family of (non-
isomorphic) graphs. Given an H, an H-packing in an input graph G = (V, E) is a set of vertex-disjoint
subgraphs of G, each isomorphic to a graph in H. A vertex in G is said to be covered by the H-packing if it
is contained in one of the subgraphs in the packing. The H-packing problem aims to find an H-packing to
cover the maximum number of vertices.
For another example of H, we can look at an i-clique, typically denoted as Ki, which is the complete graph
on i vertices. When H = {K2}, i.e., it contains only a single 2-clique, the H-packing problem is equivalent to
the maximum matching problem. A maximum matching can be computed by several well-known algorithms
including the Blossom algorithm by Edmonds [1]. The Blossom algorithm is iterative, that it starts with
an empty matching and in each iteration it looks for an augmenting path (after contracting odd cycles),
which connects two uncovered vertices through an alternating path with edges alternately not in the current
matching and in the current matching; the algorithm terminates when no augmenting path is found, and
the achieved matching is shown to be optimal. The Blossom algorithm runs in O(|V |2 · |E|) time, that is,
the H-packing problem is polynomial solvable. When H = {K2, K3}, Hell and Kirkpatrick [7] discovered
three specific augmenting structures, and proved that any packing, in which none of the three structures

Approximating the [k, ∞)-Star Packing problem when k ≥2
The [k, ∞)-Star Packing problem is also referred to as k+-star packing in the literature [12, 11, 9, 10].
The state-of-the-art approximation algorithms are local search proposed by Hu et al. [9, 10], which have a
ratio of 1 +

2k+1 when k ≥3 and a ratio of 3

with a ratio of 4
3. We will highlight the major differences after we introduce the new local improvement
operations.
A local search algorithm is typically supported by a number of operations each is designed to improve
the current solution through searching a neighborhood in the solution space. Our algorithm is denoted as
Approx[k, ∞) and the operations are Collect and Trade defined below. The algorithm is iterative, and
during each iteration a feasible solution, referred to as the current solution or packing, is assumed at the
beginning, one of the operations is applied to update/improve the solution. Before the iteration ends, post-
processing is often necessary including collecting the solution information, updating the remainder graph,
and some other minor tasks to be detailed in each specific algorithm. If in an iteration none of the operations
is applicable, then the algorithm terminates and returns the current solution as the final solution.
Below we define the operations first, and for each operation we estimate its time complexity. Given a
feasible solution P for the input graph G = (V, E), we call each star in P an internal star. Let V (P) denote

the set of vertices covered by (the stars of) P, then V −V (P) is the set of uncovered vertices and the graph
R(P) induced on V −V (P), i.e., R(P) = G[V −V (P)], is the remainder graph with respect to P. A star in
the remainder graph is called an outside star.
Definition 1 (Operation Collect) [11, 9, 10] Given a vertex v in the remainder graph, if its degree in
the remainder graph is d ≥k, then the operation extracts the d-star centered at v with the d neighbors of v
as satellites from the remainder graph and adds it to the current solution P. (Comment: If d < k, then the
operation is not applicable at the vertex v.)
The algorithm starts with the empty solution, and in the first a few iterations Collect is repeatedly
applied on vertices of degree at least k in the remainder graph until impossible. Note that the degrees of
all the vertices can be pre-processed in O(|E|) time, and inside the algorithm their degrees in the remainder
graph can be updated in O(|E|) time too after applying a Collect operation. Each operation Collect
alone takes O(|V |) time but the iteration takes O(|E|) time due to post-processing. When no Collect
operation is applicable, the maximum degree of the remainder graph is at most k −1, which is stated in
the following Observation 1. We remark that an observation typically consists of straightforward statements
that do not need rigorous proofs, though we will provide some sample proofs below.
Observation 1 When no Collect operation is applicable,
1. the maximum degree of the remainder graph is at most k −1;
2. the center of an internal star is not adjacent to any uncovered vertex.
As we define more operations below, we remark that after applying any one of them in an iteration,
during the post-processing operation Collect is repeatedly applied whenever there is an uncovered vertex
of degree at least k in the remainder graph, or if there is an uncovered vertex adjacent to the center of an
internal star then the uncovered vertex is added to the star to become covered. Such post-processing at the
end of each iteration guarantees the two statements in Observation 1.
Definition 2 (Operation Trade-i) Given i internal stars S1, . . . , Si, where 1 ≤i ≤3, the operation
trades them to cover more vertices by first removing them from the current solution to make all their vertices
uncovered and then extracting one up to i + 1 feasible stars to cover more vertices. (Comment: Extracting
such one up to i + 1 feasible stars can be done by enumerating all subsets of i + 1 uncovered vertices as
the centers, checking whether these centers have sufficiently many satellites and whether each has at least
k distinct satellites by a maximum bipartite matching algorithm, in O(|V |i+1+1.5) time. If no combination
covering more vertices can be extracted, then the operation is not applicable on this subset of internal stars
S1, . . . , Si.)
To find an applicable operation Trade-i, one might need to enumerate all subsets of i internal stars.
Therefore, an applicable operation Trade-i takes O(|V |2i+2.5) time, for 1 ≤i ≤3.
Observation 2 When operation Trade-1 is not applicable, for every 1 ≤j ≤k, any j satellites of an
internal (k + j)+-star together with any k −j + 1 uncovered vertices do not form a k-star.
More observations can be made when operation Trade-i is not applicable, for i = 2, 3, and we will
do it whenever we need them. Using the above defined Collect and Trade-i operations, our algorithm
Approx[k, ∞) repeatedly applies them whenever possible to update the solution. When none of them is
applicable, the algorithm returns the achieved solution P as the final solution to the [k, ∞)-Star Packing
problem.

Definition 11 (Operation Collect) Given a vertex v in the remainder graph, if its degree in the re-
mainder graph is d ≥k, then the operation extracts an i-star centered at v, where i = min{d, ℓ}, from the
remainder graph and adds it to the current solution P. (Comment: Arbitrary i of the d neighbors of v in
the remainder graph are selected as the satellites of the extracted i-star; if d < k, then the operation is not
applicable on the vertex v.)
The algorithm, denoted as Approx[2, ℓ], starts with the empty solution, and in the first a few iterations
Collect (using k = 2) is repeatedly applied on vertices of degree at least 2 in the remainder graph until
impossible.

vertex and applying the Collect operation takes O(|V |) time, followed by updating the degrees of the
uncovered vertices in O(|V |) time (since at most ℓvertices are affected). That is, each operation Collect
and an iteration executes the operation takes O(|V |) time.

A truth assignment as stated in Lemma 3 is said canonical.
Similarly as observed by Xi et al. [17], given a [2, ∞)-star packing P in G, if one of the labeled vertex in
a clause gadget Uj is the center of a 2-star, then at least one private vertex will not be covered. One can
swap to use private vertices as centers to cover at least the same number of vertices. Next, another similar
observation holds that the two stars in P centered at the private vertices of Uj need at least one of the three
labeled vertices to be their satellite, or otherwise at least two private vertices would be uncovered. Consider
the gadget Vi for a variable xi which appears only twice in the clauses, say cj and cℓ. If for both Uj and Uℓ,
the stars in P need xi and ¯xi to be their satellites, then exactly one vertex in Vi is not covered; additionally,
if for both Uj and Uℓ, the stars in P need none of xi and ¯xi to be their satellites, then arbitrarily one of them
is made a satellite. Analogously, for the gadget Vi for a variable xi appearing three times (two positive and
one negative) in the clauses, if ¯xi and at least one xi are needed as satellites of stars centered at the private
vertices in the corresponding clause gadgets, then exactly one vertex in Vi is not covered; additionally, if ¯xi
is not needed, then both xi’s are made satellites, or if ¯xi is needed but the two xi’s are not, then only ¯xi is
made a satellite. The packing P is said canonical if all the uncovered vertices are in the variable gadgets and
for these variable gadgets all the corresponding labeled vertices in the clause gadgets are needed as satellites.
Lemma 4 A canonical truth assignment for the Max 3CNF-SAT-3 instance leaving ℓclauses unsatisfied
one-to-one corresponds to a canonical [2, ∞)-star packing in the graph G leaving ℓvertices uncovered.
Proof.
Given a canonical truth assignment, for each clause cj = xj,1 ∨xj,2 ∨xj,3, if a literal xj,z is true
then the vertex in Uj labeled by the literal is a satellite of a star center at the private vertex in Uj. This
gives rise to an initial [2, ∞)-star packing P0 in which all the vertices in satisfied clause gadgets and vertex
gadgets covered, but exactly 2ℓprivate vertices, two in each unsatisfied clause gadget, uncovered. Next,
for each unsatisfied clause cj, take an arbitrary literal and swap to cover the vertex labeled by the literal
inside Uj; by Lemma 3 this process will cover all the vertices in Uj but leave exactly one private vertex in
the corresponding variable gadget uncovered. Repeating this process, we achieve a packing P which leaves
exactly ℓvertices uncovered.
Conversely, given a canonical [2, ∞)-star packing with ℓvertices uncovered, these ℓvertices are in ℓ
distinct variable gadgets; and for each such gadget Vi, the corresponding vertices labeled by xi and ¯xi in the
clause gadgets are needed as satellites. We assign the variable xi true. For each variable gadget Vi containing
no uncovered vertex, if the corresponding vertices labeled by xi in the clause gadgets are covered as satellites,
then set xi true, or otherwise set xi false. This truth assignment makes exactly ℓclauses unsatisfied, and
thus proves the lemma.
