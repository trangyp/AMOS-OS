Gregory D. Scholes
Department of Chemistry, Princeton University, Princeton, NJ 08544, USA∗

Abstract
It is shown how to obtain state vectors associated with measurements on the separated
subystems of an entangled state, revealing how a single wavefunction encodes a set of statis-
tical measurement outcomes. The result explains why measurements on the subsystems give
deﬁnite outcomes and why measurements on one subsystem are correlated with those on the
other. It is therefore concluded that the theory of quantum mechanics, without nonlinearities
or ad hoc assertions, can explain both the mechanism of state vector collapse and the rea-
son for the paradoxical nonlocal correlations between separated subsystems.The theory also
explains how quantum correlations, including correlations that violate Bell’s inequality, are
read out by classical measurements.

I.
INTRODUCTION
In a remarkable paper[1] published over 90 years ago, Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen
(EPR) highlighted the diﬀerence between quantum and classical correlations. One of
the issues noted by EPR is that quantum mechanics makes the unsettling prediction
that correlations found in the measurement of entangled states appear to involve inex-
plicable long-range interactions. Famously described as a ‘spooky action at a distance’,
this property is known as nonlocality and has to date deﬁed physical explanation. Back-
ground details are found in many sources[2–10]. For example, the possibility that local
variables hidden in the theory explain nonlocal correlations[11] has been ruled out by
the Bell inequality[12] and related inequalities like the CHSH inequality[2, 5]. These
analyses predict that correlations in entangled states exceed the limits of those possible
for any local variable theory[13], which has since been veriﬁed by experiments[14, 15].
Yet, understanding the mechanism by which the correlations are revealed in joint
measurements of the separated subsystems associated with entangled states remains
unresolved. In particular, how do the correlations arise when the collapse model tells
us that the superpositions on each subsystem reduce randomly to give a measured
outcome? Or put another way, how does a measurement on subsystem A inﬂuence the
reduction of the wavepacket on subsystem B, given that the two subsystems do not
interact[16]? Fundamentally, the issue is that ‘collapse’ of the state vector—though an
appealing and intuitive notion—to date has no mechanistic explanation in the linear
theory of quantum mechanics. Although the presence of nonlocal correlations is beyond
doubt, elucidating of the mechanism enabling these correlations, which are evident even
over very large distances, would resolve a signiﬁcant gap in the fundamental theory
of quantum mechanics.
Moreover, clarifying the matter might suggest new ways of
exploiting or implementing nonlocal correlations.
The postulates of quantum mechanics include the foundational concept that an iso-
lated physical system is described by a wavefunction Ψ, or state vector |Ψ⟩belonging to
a Hilbert space H. Experimental observations are tied to the meaurement postulates,
which say the probability of obtaining an eigenvalue an of a Hermitian operator A is
given by the amplitude squared P(an) = |⟨an|Ψ⟩|2, and that if a measurement gives

eigenspace associated with an. This latter statement implies the ‘collapse’ rule, which
intuitively resolves the problem of obtaining a physical measurement outcome from a
superposition state. Further, it guarantees that a subsequent measurement on the state
also gives eigenvalue an.
Researchers have sought a microscopic explanation for collapse of the state vector
for a century[3, 17–20].

theory of quantum mechanics might be needed to explain collapse as well as fundamen-
tal notions of decoherence[21–23]. Other researchers have questioned whether collapse
is even a feasible concept. Dickson, Chapter 2.2 of Ref. [18] writes “What is it about
measurement, or observation (as opposed to other physical processes), that causes such
a collapse?”
Yet, if a plausible mechanism for collapse can be found, it would enable the perplex-
ing question about measurement to be answered intuitively based on the postulates.
We began to address this issue in a recent paper[24], where we proposed an insight
into the existing theory of quantum mechanical wavefunctions that provides a physical
basis for collapse of a superposition.
The details being brought to light are rooted
in the wave basis of quantum states. The present paper further explores this topic
and, in particular, gives much more technical detail concerning how measurements on
separated subystems of entangled states are naturally associated with collapse of local
vectors. The way collapse of these local vectors happens inherently leads to nonlocal
correlations in compatible measurements of both separated subsystems. The resulting
connection between collapse and nonlocality is examined here.
The paper is organized as follows. We start (Sec. 2) by deﬁning the tensor prod-
uct basis for composite quantum states and the concept of states of and measurement
of separated subsystems from an entangled state. We summarize in Sec. 3 some key
results from the prior paper[24]. In Sec. 4 we discuss how a single entangled wavefunc-

III.
SUMMARY OF THE CONTEXTUAL PHASE MODEL
We start with a brief summary of our prior work[24], where we proposed the con-
textual phase theory for quantum measurement of separated subsystems. A way to
associate entangled states in HA ⊗HB to states that represent the appropriate local
superpositions in the separated, non-interacting subsystems was posited. Those states,
in accord with von Neumann’s proposal, lie in HA and HB.
The previous paper highlights the following facts, which give intuition for the ap-
proach that is explained rigorously in the following sections. In terms of vectors in the
Hilbert space of subsystem A, phase is encoded in the coeﬃcients that deﬁne any nor-
malized state vector ψA = α|0⟩+ β|1⟩with respect to our chosen basis. It is convenient
to visualize the pair of complex coeﬃcients α, β, assuming ψA is normalized, as a point
on the sphere in four-dimensional real space, S3. The method shows the importance of
accounting for the possible ways that the phase of the superposition associates to these
vectors. The phase in the case of ψA does not matter, it is an arbitrary global phase.
Where the phase does matter is when it is the phase of a superposition state—that is,
the phase prefactor for the second term of the linear combination for a composite state.
This phase means that when we construct the tensor product space, we can visualize

IV.
WHY A SINGLE WAVEFUNCTION GIVES STATISTICAL MEASURE-
MENT OUTCOMES
Einstein struggled with the nature of the wavefunction and how it is probed by
measurements.
The fact that a single wavefunction can yield various measurement
outcomes according to how it ‘collapses’ when measured is the issue which opens the
possibility of a statistical interpretation for the wavefunction. Einstein thus wrote[35]:
The ψ function does not in any way describe a condition which could be
that of a single system; it relates rather to many systems, to “an ensemble
of systems” in the sense of statistical mechanics.
Einstein considered that this statistical view suggests an incompleteness in quantum
theory. Bohr, on the other hand advocated that the wavefunction describes a single
system. Here it is shown that a single wavefunction that describes a composite quan-
tum system intrinsically encodes a set of outcomes for measurements on the separated
subsystems. In this sense, the ensemble of measurement outcomes is ‘hidden in plain
sight’ and the probabilistic view of collapse is not required to explain measurements.
The following discussion is restricted to ﬁnite-dimensional vector spaces. The vector
spaces considered can be over any ﬁeld, but we use the explicit example where the ﬁeld
is the complex numbers (C). We begin with some technical details. Throughout, α, β
refer to scalars in our ﬁeld, C.
Deﬁnition 1 (Cartesian product of sets) Let S1, . . . , Sn be any sets. The Cartesian
product of sets, denoted S1 × · · · × Sn, is the set of all ordered n−tuples a1, . . . , an,
where ai ∈Si for i = 1, . . . n.
Deﬁnition 2 (Free vector space) A free vector space F(S) on a set S over a ﬁeld K is
a vector space comprising all ﬁnite formal linear combinations of elements in S, where
the elements of S = {e0, e1, . . . } form a basis.
The notion of formal linear combinations means that we have linear combinations of
the form α0e0+α1e1+. . . (αi ∈K), but no relations among the symbols (i.e. e0, e1, . . . )
are assumed. The construction of formal linear combinations of symbols, the elements

V.
MEASUREMENTS ON SEPARATED SUBSYSTEMS
At the heart of the EPR paper is the concept of measurements on subsystems of
an entangled state, where the subsystems are separated so that measurements can be
performed on each subsystem separately. That allows correlations to be revealed in the
measurement outcomes. Such measurements are possible because quantum subsystems
include electrons, atoms, photons, and so on—particles that can be entangled and
subsequently physically separated in space.
While trying to explain more clearly what he had in mind in the EPR paper, Einstein
wrote on June 19, 1935 to Schr¨odinger[37]:
Separation is the claim that whether a physical property holds for one
of the particles does not depend on measurements (or other interactions)
made on the other particle when the pair is widely separated in space.
So, the way we would like to think about measurements on separated subsystems is
that they provide a kind of projection of the composite state mediated by a subsystem.
That cannot be accomplished for general states in the tensor product space U ⊗V ,
where the basis for each subsystem has been converted to a new basis for the composite
system.
Instead, we need to explain measurements on the separated subsystems in
terms of expectation values of the subsystem states in their respective Hilbert spaces
HA and HB; the vectors in these spaces are precisely those associated with separated
subsystems. Using the developments described in the prior section of the paper, we can
capture this concept in the following way:

VI.
NONLOCAL COLLAPSE
Nonlocality lies at the heart of the EPR paradox[40]. The concept means that when
we perform measurements on two subsystems (A and B) that are far apart and equally
likely to yield either of two measurement outcomes, and we accept that measurement
of one subsystem does not disturb the other, the outcomes of these measurements are
correlated. As stated by Vaidman[41]: “Given entangled particles placed at a distance,
a measurement on one of the particles instantaneously changes the quantum state of
the other, from a density matrix to a pure state”. In this section we focus on how
22


## Page 23


contextual phase classes not only ensure measurements on each subsystem give deﬁnite
outcomes in random sequence, but they also enforce correlations between measurement
outcomes on the subsystems.
Kupczynski nicely summarizes three possible ways of rationalizing correlations of
the entangled pair of subsystems[40]:
1. Assume that all the pairs of subsystems are the same, and that the random
outcomes of measurements on each subsystem are correlated by some spooky
action at a distance.
2. Assume a statistical ensemble of diﬀerent pairs of subsystems, and for each pair
the results of all measurements are predetermined and correlated in the moment
of production.
3. Assume that the long-range correlations of the subsystem measurement results
are due to their particular preparation by a common source, but at the same time,
assume that the result of any single measurement is not predetermined.
But contextual phase theory suggests a fourth possibility:
4. Assume that all composite states are the same, and that there is a statistical
distribution of subsystem pairs owing to the contextual phases, which, in turn,
implies that the correlation between measurement pairs is statistically predeter-
mined.
This proposal does not imply hidden local variables, since the correlations arise solely
from the quantum state and its measurement context. The contextual phase comes from
the phase already present in the superposition. The way a single wavefunction in the
tensor product basis is associated to multiple classes of measurable separated states in
the free vector state representation may be the kind of ensemble view of a wavefunction
imagined by Einstein[16, 37].
The correlation between measurement outcomes depends on the measurement con-
ditions, so it is not possible to realize simultaneously all possible outcomes, as is well
known[42]. For example, Ψ+ when both subsystems are measured in the z−basis will
show anti-correlated outcomes. But measured in the x−basis the results are correlated.

correlated? (c) How are correlations that can exceed the classical limit exposed by
classical measurements on the separated subsystems? The developments reported here
build on prior work[24] that proposes how to map entangled states in HA⊗HB to states
that represent the appropriate local superpositions in the separated, non-interacting
subsystems. Those states, in accord with von Neumann’s proposal, lie in HA and HB.
The main result of the paper is accomplished by the contextual phase theory, Theo-
rem 2. The result gives a plausible mechanism for explaining collapse in the quantum
measurement postulates. Moreover, it guarantees that when a measurement is per-
formed, only one result is obtained and repeated measurements give the same result.
The theory makes these predictions without assuming any kind of ad hoc collapse.
The measurement postulates are generally thought to imply the ‘collapse’ rule, which
intuitively resolves the problem of obtaining a physical measurement outcome from
a superposition state. The random element of collapse is transferred to a statistical
distribution contextual phase class for the entangled state of the separated subsystems.
The well-deﬁned measurement outcome associated with any particular contextual phase
class also means that any subsequent measurement gives the same outcome. Collapse
appears to be random because the contextual phase class is statistically distributed
among the representatives of the entangled state in the covering free vector space. This
new viewpoint was enabled by proposing a theory for measurements on the separated
subsystems A and B of entangled states; essentially resolving what are the local vectors
in HA and HB and how they are associated to the entangled state.
What do contextual phases tell us about collapse? Have we simply moved the ran-
domness somewhere else? No, instead the randomness is replaced by a statistical basis
for the measurement outcomes because a single wavefunction Ψ in the tensor product
basis is associated, by the quotient, to multiple classes of measurable separated states
in the free vector space. Grouping the representatives for Ψ into diﬀerent contextual
phase classes shows how measurement outcomes expose, statistically, any one of those
classes. This analysis suggests how a single Ψ gives the kind of ensemble view of a
wavefunction imagined by Einstein[16, 37].

A contextual phase class applies to the entire composite system and therefore collapse
of a state vector for one subsystem in a composite state is correlated with the collapse
of the state vector of the other subsystem, without the need for any kind of inexplicable
long-range interaction after the subsystems separate. The correlations derive from the
fact that phases incorporated in the subsystem states are locked together by the phase
in the superposition of a composite state. Nonlocal correlations arise without any kind
of ‘spooky action at a distance’.
