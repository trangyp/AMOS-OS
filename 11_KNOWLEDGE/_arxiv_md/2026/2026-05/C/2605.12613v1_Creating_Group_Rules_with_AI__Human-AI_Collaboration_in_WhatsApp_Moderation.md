WhatsApp is one of the most widely used messaging platforms globally, with billions of users sharing
information in private groups. Yet, it offers little infrastructure to support moderation and group governance.
In the absence of platform-level oversight, group admins bear the responsibility of governing group behavior.
In this paper, we explore how WhatsApp group admins collaborate with AI tools to create, enforce, and
maintain group rules. Drawing on a two-phase speculative design study with 20 admins in India, we examine
how participants interacted with an AI assistant (Meta AI) to co-create rules and responded to a series of
probes illustrating AI-assisted moderation features. Our findings show that while admins appreciated the AI’s
ability to surface overlooked rules and reduce their moderation burden, they were highly sensitive to issues of
relational trust, data privacy, tone, and social context. We identify how group type and admin style shaped
their willingness to delegate authority, and surface the limitations of current chatbot interfaces in supporting
collaborative rule-making. We conclude with design implications for building moderation tools that center
human judgment, relational nuance, contextual adaptability, and collective governance.

Introduction
WhatsApp is the most widely used encrypted messaging platform globally, with billions of users
sharing information in groups that are often unregulated [7, 38]. In India alone, over half a billion
people use WhatsApp [55] as an essential digital infrastructure supporting everyday connections
among families, friends, neighborhoods, and workplaces [41, 55]. Due to end-to-end encryption,
WhatsApp has limited visibility into the content circulating in groups, placing the responsibility of
moderation on group admins. These admins are everyday users who create, manage, and informally
govern these groups [52].
Prior research shows that in formal WhatsApp groups—such as those for work or study—admins
tend to actively enforce rules to prevent unwanted content [52, 61]. In contrast, admins of public
or close-knit groups (e.g., family and friends) typically avoid moderation, either due to established
social hierarchies or the overwhelming volume of problematic content [39, 42, 52]. While the
presence of group rules can promote responsible behavior among members [15, 40], most platforms,
including WhatsApp, offer little to no infrastructure for rule creation or enforcement [52, 64].
Consequently, the process of defining group rules remains largely ad hoc and cognitively demanding
[9, 51, 52, 58]. Without structural support, admins rely on their own intuition, trial and error, or
simply copy rules from other groups [52, 64]. This lack of support often forces admins into a
reactive stance, where rules are codified only after problematic behaviors have already damaged
the group’s social fabric [8, 52]. For everyday users managing these groups, articulating group rules
proactively is a significant hurdle.

Although platforms like Reddit and Twitch have long used automated moderation tools, these
platforms typically rely on human moderators to define rules in advance [27]. Even with the
growing use of large language models for moderation, the emphasis remains on their ability to
detect violations of predefined rules, rather than supporting users in creating or adapting those
rules in the first place [24].
Given the limited platform support for creating group rules and the reluctance of many WhatsApp
admins to engage in active moderation, we examine whether AI assistance could help admins
establish data-driven group rules without adding to their burden and how admins might interact
with AI during this process. Specifically, we ask:
RQ1: How do WhatsApp group admins engage with AI to co-create group rules?
RQ2: How do admins perceive the benefits and limitations of using AI to create group rules?
To answer these questions, we conducted a two-phase speculative design study with 20 WhatsApp
admins in India. In phase 1, participants used the Meta AI1 chatbot within WhatsApp to co-create
rules for their groups. In phase 2, they interacted with design probes of different AI-assisted features
for creating and enforcing group rules.
Our findings show that the value of AI-generated, data-driven group rules varied across group
types, shaped by admins’ moderation styles and the relational dynamics within the group. Ad-
mins of large, formal or semi-formal groups—such as university or neighborhood coordination
groups—found AI support helpful for managing high message volumes, surfacing overlooked issues,
and drafting comprehensive rules with minimal effort. In these contexts, where admins often had
limited personal connection with members, AI-generated summaries, reports, and rule suggestions
were perceived as efficient and unobtrusive aids that reinforced, rather than undermined, their
authority. In contrast, admins of smaller, informal, and close-knit groups—such as those among
friends or family—anticipated that AI would offer little value. These admins relied on relational
trust, implicit group rules, and subtle social cues to maintain group order. AI-generated rules were
often perceived as unnecessary or even disruptive, risking harm to interpersonal relationships or
altering the group dynamics.
Across group types, we found that admins wanted to engage with AI in agentic and collaborative
ways, treating it as a partner rather than an authority. Admins preferred to selectively draft, edit, or
discard AI-generated rule suggestions based on their group’s needs, and emphasized the importance
of adjusting the tone and framing of rules to align with the group’s norms. Many of them also
expressed a desire for democratic governance features, pushing against AI systems designed for a
single-user model of decision-making.
We discuss admins’ expectations about control and decision-making with AI, showing how they
actively determine which aspects of moderation can be delegated to AI and which must remain
socially and relationally human. We then surface how these negotiations are shaped by boundary
work around privacy, cultural intelligibility, and accountability in end-to-end encrypted settings.
Finally, we translate these insights into design implications for AI tools that support contextual
customization and collective decision-making, rather than assuming a single-user, automation-
forward model.

Related Work
We situate our work within CSCW and HCI literature that examines the social and technical
practices of moderation in online communities. We focus on two strands of prior work: (1) how
group rules are created, maintained, and negotiated over time; and (2) how AI-driven tools are
being introduced to assist moderators.
2.1
Creating, Maintaining, and Negotiating Group Rules
Prior work has documented how moderators and group admins—across Reddit, Facebook, Twitch,
and WhatsApp—rely on a mix of formal and informal rules to guide group behavior. For instance,
Jiang et al. [28] identified 66 different types of rules in the community guidelines of different
social media platforms, addressing online harms, such as revenge porn and graphic violence.
Chandrasekharan et al. [10] categorized Reddit norms by adoption level, distinguishing platform-
wide macro rules (e.g., banning hate speech), meso rules that are common in large subreddits
(e.g., prohibiting spam), and subreddit-specific micro norms (e.g., banning Wikipedia links). More
recently, Fang et al. [19] extended this work by categorizing rules based on their impact on user
behavior, including those that reflect community values (e.g., mutual respect), restrict specific
content (e.g., no jokes), and impose structural regulations (e.g., minimum word limits). In addition
to formal group rules, Reddit and Twitch moderators—as well as WhatsApp group admins—often
also enforce implicit rules that guide moderation but are not formally codified [8, 64].
Rule creation is often ad hoc. Although group rules contribute to the stability of online com-
munities [15, 40], most platforms offer little support in creating these rules [64]. In the absence of
any platform support and scaffolding, group admins and moderators often draw on their common
sense, past experience, and personal values to create rules. Often, they just adopt rules from other
groups instead of making their own given the cognitive load involved in creating and enforcing
rules [9, 51, 52, 58]. Often, the rules they create are not data-driven and instead consist of generic
guidelines discouraging disrespectful attitudes and personal attacks toward other group members.
Usually, the group rules are made through trial and error [64], and new rules often emerge in
response to disruptive events, for example, misinformation during the COVID-19 pandemic [8],
or an influx of AI-generated content that admins perceive as low quality [37]. Sometimes, rule
design is shaped by technical constraints; for example, Reddit moderators introduce mandatory
image captions to support automation via AutoMod [27]. However, in most public and close-
knit WhatsApp groups, admins do not make an effort to implement rules against problematic
behaviors [52]. This lack of engagement, combined with limited platform support and the absence of
centralized moderation due to encryption, makes WhatsApp a particularly challenging environment
for community governance [52, 60].
Collaborative rule-setting varies across platforms and communities. In some contexts, rule
creation involves collective deliberation among moderators—via Slack, Messenger, or Discord [6, 27,
64]. In others, especially hierarchical communities, rule-making is more top-down [51]. On platforms
like Slashdot, rules are voted on by community members [35], but in Reddit and Twitch, involving
the wider community is often seen as a risk, especially due to potential rule manipulation [12, 29].
Rule maintenance is often an ongoing and collaborative process. Reddit moderators constantly
update their rules by adding new lists of undesirable phrases or domain names [9]. Rule changes
happen through reflections on emerging group behavior, especially when new members join the

While most prior work examines how human moderators create, maintain, and negotiate group
rules, far less attention has been paid to how these processes might be supported—or reshaped—by
AI systems, which we discuss next.
2.2
AI and Community Moderation
As moderation becomes increasingly complex, CSCW researchers have begun exploring how AI can
reduce the burden on volunteer moderators and scaffold decision-making in online communities.
For instance, to help Reddit moderators deal with large volumes of content, Chandrasekharan
et al. [9] developed an AI tool for flagging content that otherwise went unnoticed by AutoMod.
Schluger et al. [48] redesigned Wikipedia moderators’ dashboard to display which conversations
are likely to derail into antisocial behavior. Similarly, Choi et al. [11] designed an AI-augmented
version of Discord interface that allowed moderators to track activity and toxicity levels for
conversations across multiple channels and servers. Koshy et al. [32] also developed an AI-guided
review panel for Reddit to surface controversial cases so that multiple moderators could review
them to reduce inconsistencies in moderation decision. Khullar et al. [30] designed AI tools for voice-
based discussion forums to relieve moderators of mundane tasks like detecting and rejecting empty
recordings. However, most tools assume that group rules already exist, and focus on assisting
enforcement—not creation. As a result, the use of AI in upstream moderation work, such as
articulating, adapting, or negotiating group rules, remains largely unexplored.
Despite this momentum, there is still limited research on how AI can support moderators in
the creation of group rules, with one exception being Xiang et al. [66], who designed AI agents to
co-create rules in virtual reality communities. Moreover, most of the existing work on community-
driven group rules focuses on platforms like Reddit, Twitch, and Facebook. In contrast, very
few studies examine how rules emerge or are enforced in end-to-end encrypted platforms like
WhatsApp [8, 43].
Our work extends this line of inquiry by focusing on how group rules are co-created, adapted,
and negotiated in encrypted messaging environments with minimal infrastructure for moderation.
While prior research has analyzed how rules emerge through informal practices or are enforced
via AI tools, little is known about how admins might collaborate with AI in the actual process
of rule creation—especially in high-friction, relationally sensitive settings like WhatsApp which
are critically understudied. We contribute to this gap by studying how everyday group admins
engage with an AI assistant to draft and modify group rules, surfacing new dynamics of human-AI
collaboration in community moderation. In particular, we explore: (1) How do WhatsApp group
admins engage with AI to co-create group rules, and (2) How do they perceive the benefits
and limitations of using AI to create group rules.

Methodology
To examine how admins perceive the utility of AI assistance in creating and maintaining group
rules, we conducted a two-phase speculative design study with 20 WhatsApp group admins in
India. The study protocol received an exemption from the IRB at our institute.
Participant Recruitment. We used a combination of convenience and snowball sampling to
recruit participants. We advertised the study on social media groups in India as well as within
our immediate network of acquaintances. We selected participants who were at least 18 years old,
admins of at least one WhatsApp group, and had experienced problematic content in their groups.
To ensure diverse perspectives, we recruited admins from both public and private WhatsApp
groups with varying levels of rule enforcement. These ranged from groups enforcing explicit
rules to those where admins relied on implicit rules, expecting members to follow them without
active enforcement. We continued recruiting participants until the responses reached theoretical
saturation [44].
Participant Demographics and Group Details.Table 1 presents the demographic details of our
20 participants, including seven who identified as female and the rest as male, and aged between 20
to 40 years. All participants owned a smartphone and moderated at least one WhatsApp group.
Additionally, all had either completed or were pursuing a college-level degree.

Participants in our study were admins of a diverse set of WhatsApp groups that varied in size,
purpose, and social formality: formal groups (e.g., university coordination groups), semi-formal
groups (e.g., neighborhood associations, peer-support groups, and former colleague networks),
and informal groups (e.g., family and friends groups). This diversity of group types allowed us to
develop a more comprehensive understanding of how AI could support admins across different
group types, moderation styles, and social dynamics. Participants in our sample also had varying
levels of AI literacy, ranging from computer science students and data scientists who were familiar
with the use of AI and prompting, to those working in non-tech jobs with limited prior exposure.
Despite this variation in AI familiarity, we observed that participants’ core attitudes toward the
utility and risks of AI-assisted moderation—including concerns about privacy and consent, a desire
to retain human authority, and skepticism about AI’s ability to interpret social context—remained
consistent across the sample.
Speculative Design Study. We conducted a two-phase speculative design study with 20 WhatsApp
group admins to understand what support they might need in creating and deploying rules in
their groups. The study was conducted over Zoom either in Hindi or English, depending on
the preferences of each participant. After obtaining informed consent, we asked participants in
detail about their experiences of handling problematic content in WhatsApp groups, and their
motivations behind setting existing group rules or norms. Then, they engaged in a writing exercise,
where they co-created group rules using Meta AI (Phase 1) and shared their reflections on design
probes presenting data-driven approaches to rule enforcement (Phase 2). The sessions lasted for
approximately one hour and were recorded with the consent of the participants. Each participant
was compensated with a gift card worth 500 INR (5.76 USD) for their time and participation.
Phase 1: Co-creating Group Rules with Meta AI. To elicit nuanced responses from participants
regarding how they perceive the capabilities of AI in helping them make group rules, we conducted
a think-aloud writing exercise, where participants used Meta AI to create rules for their groups.
We used Meta AI chatbot because it is integrated within WhatsApp [62] and easily accessible to all
our participants. In this writing exercise, participants prompted Meta AI to suggest rules for their
groups, then, iteratively provided instructions to refine the rules. We asked participants to think
aloud as they interacted with Meta AI–describing their expectations, reactions to AI-generated
rules, and reasoning behind their modifications to the AI-generated rules. This exercise enabled
participants to critically engage with the capabilities of existing AI tools when making group rules
and reflect on their collaboration with AI in a tangible way.
Phase 2: Design Probes for Rule Creation and Maintenance. Technology probes are com-
monly used in HCI to prompt users to critically think about the possibility of new and emerging
technologies [25, 26]. We designed a set of interactive probes to help participants imagine and
critique various AI-assisted, data-driven approaches for creating, enforcing, and maintaining group
rules—scenarios that would have been difficult to grasp verbally. The probes were modeled after
the Meta AI chatbot already integrated within WhatsApp [62], ensuring continuity and familiarity,
as participants had already interacted with Meta AI in Phase 1 to co-create group rules.
The probe presented a scenario in which the Meta AI chatbot asked a WhatsApp admin
whether they would like to grant access to the group’s content to generate data-driven rules (see
Figure 1A). While platforms like Twitch, Reddit, Telegram, and Discord routinely use bots that scan
and moderate content in real time [2, 31, 50], WhatsApp lacks such capabilities due to encryption
constraints. Once granted access, the probe showed the bot generating a brief report on content
that might be inappropriate given the group’s context (see Figure 1B). This scenario was informed

Analysis. We collected around 22 hours of audio-recorded data across both phases, along with 132
pages of interaction logs from participants’ use of the Meta AI chatbot during Phase 1. All audio
recordings were manually transcribed and translated into English.
To analyze the interaction logs from Phase 1, we followed Kuckartz’s qualitative data analyses
method [33]. This structured coding process allowed us to explore different stages of rule co-creation
process, such as providing initial context about the group (e.g., group setting, group’s purpose)
and customizing the AI-generated rules to fit the group’s context. Our structural coding involved
multiple passes over the data. We applied open coding to develop 14 sub-themes that provided
us with a nuanced understanding of participants’ interactions with the AI-generated group rules.
These include aligning the tone and language to match the group’s context, removing irrelevant
group rules, among others.
To analyze the interview data collected in Phase 2, we conducted a reflexive thematic analysis [4].
The first author led the coding process, with all authors meeting periodically to review and refine
the codebook, merge overlapping codes, and identify emergent themes. Through multiple rounds of
analysis, we developed 107 codes, which were categorized into 12 sub-themes, such as expectations
from AI-assisted group rule enforcement and concerns around AI’s capabilities. We organized the
themes from our data across different stages in the life cycle of group rules, such as creating rules,
presenting rules, enforcing and maintaining rules. We provide the codebooks used in both phases
of analysis in the Appendix.
Positionality. Our analysis is shaped by the first-hand experience of inhabiting such WhatsApp
groups. Three of the authors are from India and one from Bangladesh. All authors have used
WhatsApp for over a decade, served as admins of multiple WhatsApp groups, and have firsthand
experience with problematic content in these spaces. The authors also have extensive fieldwork
experience in India and Bangladesh. Their sociocultural and educational backgrounds align closely
with those of the participants, allowing them to understand the underlying dynamics of these
groups and elicit nuanced insights.
We approached this work from an emancipatory action research mindset. We do not assume
that AI is a universal solution for moderation, nor do we treat automation as inherently neutral
or beneficial. Instead, our goal is to center the perspectives of WhatsApp admins as everyday
governance actors, and to critically examine the opportunities, risks, and tensions that arise when
AI tools are introduced into these grassroots forms of community management.

Findings
We begin by describing how participants created, enforced, and maintained group rules, and how
these practices varied across different types of groups (Section 4.1). We then present participants’
reactions to the idea of granting AI access to their group messages to generate contextualized
rules (Section 4.2). Finally, we describe how admins envisioned AI support across different stages
of the group rule lifecycle, including rule creation (Section 4.3), enforcement (Section 4.4), and
maintenance (Section 4.5).
4.1
Creating, Enforcing, and Negotiating Rules Across Group Types
Participants approached rule creation, enforcement, and maintenance in ways that were deeply
shaped by the social dynamics and purposes of their WhatsApp groups. Drawing on prior CSCW
literature on informal governance and relational moderation [27, 51, 58, 64], we identified three
broad categories of groups based on the formality of relationships among members: formal, semi-
formal, and informal. These categories strongly influenced how admins governed group behavior,
whether through explicit rule-setting or tacit social expectations.
Formal groups—such as university coordination groups—tended to have clearly defined, written
rules. These groups were usually managed by class representatives and, in some cases, included
professors. Rules focused on maintaining order, minimizing distractions, and ensuring productive
information exchange. Admins in these groups often created rules collaboratively with co-admins
and treated rule presentation as a visible and intentional act: posting rules periodically, pinning
them for easy access, and reminding members about them in case of violations.
Semi-formal groups included neighborhood associations, peer-support groups, and professional
alumni networks. While members occasionally shared personal updates, the tone of interaction
was largely bounded by rules of civility and relevance. Rules in these groups were often written,
but their enforcement depended on the admin’s relationship with the group. For example, one
admin of a 400-member housing society group in Chennai described enforcing rules that banned
political posts and misinformation while still allowing members to share community updates and
personal milestones. In another case, a peer-support group for people with albinism used a set of
shared rules to create a safe and respectful environment for members to discuss both structural
issues and personal experiences.
Informal groups, consisting mainly of family and friends, relied heavily on relational trust
and implicit norms. These groups—typically smaller in size—rarely maintained written rules, even
when conversations became heated or off-topic. Instead, admins described using soft interventions,
such as private messages or temporary removals, to manage conflicts without damaging interper-
sonal relationships. In one case, an admin privately messaged a family member who had shared
misinformation, gently explaining the issue and requesting they delete the post. Such examples
highlight the emotional and relational labor that admins in informal groups often undertake to
preserve group harmony.
While formal and semi-formal groups leaned on structured rule enforcement, including public
warnings, deletions, and even temporary group lockdowns (e.g., switching to admin-only mes-
saging), informal group admins avoided confrontational enforcement unless absolutely necessary.
Across group types, participants described rules not just as static directives, but as evolving arti-
facts—shaped through experience, group growth, and emerging issues. For instance, a university
admin introduced a rule requiring members to compile technical questions into PDFs after messages
became hard to track; another admin prohibited direct messaging between members to prevent
harassment, shifting all replies to the public group space.

the idea of introducing AI into these settings, specifically, their reactions to giving an AI assistant
access to group content in order to generate contextualized, data-driven rules.
4.2
Negotiating Data Access for AI-assisted Moderation
In Phase 2, we explored participants’ attitudes toward granting the Meta AI chatbot access to
their WhatsApp group content—such as messages, images, and shared links—in order to generate
contextualized, data-driven rules (Figure 1A). Participants’ responses revealed a complex set of
sociotechnical considerations grounded in group context, perceived privacy risks, and collective
responsibility.
Admins of large, formal groups—such as those used for academic coordination or workplace
logistics— were open to providing the AI with conditional access to group data, but only if key
privacy concerns were addressed. These groups typically focused on less sensitive topics, such as
academic queries, assignments, exam schedules, or organizational announcements, which admins
felt posed minimal privacy risk. However, despite their openness, many admins felt they did not
have the right to grant AI access to group data on their own and emphasized the importance of
member consent and collective data governance. Several participants felt uncomfortable making
unilateral decisions on behalf of their groups, and instead believed that any AI access to group
data should be subject to explicit, revocable consent from members. Participants also described
situations where access should be time-bound or reversible, such as during politically sensitive
periods (e.g., elections) or emotionally charged discussions. These concerns reflect how admins
understood group privacy as context-dependent, shaped by both the nature of the group and the
evolving sensitivities of its members.
Some participants expressed willingness to share group data if the AI’s data usage policies are
understandable and transparent. P4, the admin of a semi-formal, large neighborhood group echoed
broader concerns around data locality and reducing reliance on opaque cloud-based systems:
I would be less concerned about the data privacy if the entire processing is happening
on my device and the data doesn’t go out.
Across group types, admins also anticipated distinguishing between types of data they would or
would not share with AI based on perceived privacy risk. Almost all participants strongly opposed
sharing sensitive user-generated content such as messages about political views, health information,

Participants also considered group metadata, such as group’s size, purpose, or descriptions as
low-risk and sufficient for AI to provide relevant rules, without requiring additional access to group
messages. As P2, the admin of a large group of friends, explained:
I am okay with sharing some group info because we already have those in the group
description and group name. So maybe AI can read these and have some background
data[...] I guess I could give access to messages and links, but being an admin, I also
have to think about group members’ private images and videos. So I may not agree
with sharing that content with AI.
These findings show that participants’ willingness to grant AI access to group data is shaped by
their attitude towards member consent, perceived risk of sharing different types of data, and how
those data will be processed. This careful boundary work reflects the nuanced sociotechnical roles
that admins play as informal data stewards.
Next, we describe how participants envisioned the role of AI across the different stages in the
group rule lifecycle—creation, enforcement, and maintenance.
4.3
AI and Rule Creation
In Phase 1, participants interacted with the Meta AI chatbot to generate rules for their existing
WhatsApp groups. This process surfaced how admins from different group types engaged with
AI-generated suggestions, negotiated tone and relevance, and wrestled with the limitations of
current chatbot interfaces. Participants noted both the advantages and frictions of using AI to draft
group rules—highlighting where the tool aligned with their needs, where it fell short, and how its
usefulness varied across group contexts.
Expanded Rule Coverage, But Limited Specificity. During phase 1, most participants provided
minimal input when prompting Meta AI to create group rules, often only mentioning the group’s
purpose without further context about size, dynamics, or moderation preferences. Prompts such
as “Can you help me create rules for my college group?” or “I want some rules for friends’ group”
were common starting points. The initial AI-generated outputs were typically verbose, used formal
language, and contained 12 to 15 rules structured into segments like ‘General Rules’, ‘Posting
Etiquette’, ‘Content Guidelines’, ‘Admin Responsibilities’, and ‘Consequences’. Irrespective of the
group type that participants mentioned in their prompts, Meta AI suggested generic rules like
“Be respectful and inclusive,” “Members must follow admins’ instructions,” or “Members will be
removed after repeated violations.”
Participants appreciated that even with minimal input, the AI could generate not only expected
rules but also suggest new ones they hadn’t previously considered. These unexpected suggestions
surfaced rules that had been overlooked or were difficult to articulate, offering admins a way to
reflect on and formalize emerging concerns. For example, P7, the admin of a peer-support group for
people with albinism—a condition that affects vision and skin pigmentation—was impressed when
the AI suggested a rule related to audio messages: "Keep audio messages short and clear. Use a slow
and clear pace for easier comprehension." Although this rule was not part of existing group rules, P7
found it highly relevant given that group members frequently used audio messages. Similarly, P14,
who managed a formal university group, appreciated a rule about responsible emoji use. While her
group had not formalized such a guideline, members had previously misused emojis to insinuate

understand what I’m trying to say.

scaffolding or explicit nudges from the AI to capture existing group rules within suggested rules.
Iterative Editing and Tone Adjustment. Admins who were already active moderators—particularly
in formal and semi-formal groups—were more likely to engage critically with the AI’s output. They
refined rules through iterative prompting, asking the AI to incorporate existing rules or adjust
phrasing for clarity and authority. For example, P13, the admin of a large computer science student
group focused on technical discussions and internship opportunities, asked the AI to include a rule
about “no personal messaging within the group”–reflecting the group’s existing policy prohibiting
unsolicited direct messages to unknown group members. Similarly, P4, an admin of a neighborhood
group, revised a blanket ban on business promotions to allow those from within the community.
These examples show how experienced admins treated the AI as a writing assistant, iteratively
shaping its outputs to align with established community values. These contextualized inputs led to
more tailored and relevant rules in subsequent iterations.
Admins also refined the tone embedded in the rules. For example, admins of formal groups
instructed the AI to adjust the language to be more formal and strict, believing that the neutral,
default tone of AI-generated rules would not be taken seriously by group members. For instance,
P3, an admin of a university students group remarked that the initial AI-generated rules were “very
lenient.” He then asked the AI to“give much stricter rules along with consequences so that members
keep them in mind while sending messages.”
In contrast, admins of informal, close-knit groups preferred a casual tone and saw the rules as
merely suggestive. They often accepted the AI’s initial output with only surface-level changes—such
as making the language friendlier or shortening the list. Several admins voiced frustration over long,
redundant rule lists and asked the AI to “remove generic rules.” They requested shorter and more
concise rules capturing the essential points so that group members could review them easily. For
example, P18, who managed a small informal friends group, asked the AI to give only “three-four
one line rules” so that she can “add the rules to the WhatsApp group description.”
Almost all participants faced difficulty when editing AI-generated output due to its lengthy,
unstructured format. When participants wanted to edit a particular rule, they had to scroll through
the long output, locate the specific rule, and then refer to the rule using keywords if the outputs
were not numbered. Although occasionally having rule numbers made it easier for participants to
prompt, “change rule number X”—this did not always work when there were multiple sub-rules

participants expressed the need to be able to select a specific rule for modifying it directly. In

adapt, edit, and format the output to make it usable and appropriate for their groups. This process
was highly contextual and relational: admins had to consider tone, group dynamics, and how

4.4
AI and Rule Enforcement
Participants’ responses revealed that the perceived utility and risks of AI-mediated enforcement
varied across group types, moderation styles, and relational dynamics.
4.4.1
AI Overview of Problematic Content. In phase 2, we presented participants with AI-generated
reports summarizing potentially problematic content in their groups (Figure 1B). Admins, especially
those managing large, active groups, anticipated value in these reports as a way to monitor
alignment with group rules and assess whether conversations were drifting off-topic. For example,
P15, the admin of a semi-formal ex-employees group, said that if the AI flagged frequent political
discussions—off-topic in her group—she would use that insight to reinforce or introduce a rule
against political content. Similarly, P13, the admin of a large university group, noted that such
reports could help surface content like irrelevant promotions or ads that might otherwise go
unnoticed due to high message volume.
Admins also saw value in using AI-generated reports to track the frequency of rule violations and
identify new disruptive behaviors that might require intervention. Some noted that such summaries
might reveal areas where new rules were needed or where existing ones were too rigid. P3, the
admin of a formal university group, pointed out that if many members were posting a certain type
of problematic content that isn’t explicitly banned, it could indicate the need to either establish
a new rule or create a separate group for those discussions. Similarly, P8, the admin of a friends
group, expressed that the AI reports could inform whether the existing group rules are actually
relevant or overreaching, depending on how group members were actually using the group.
At the same time, some admins expressed concern that an AI-generated report that merely listed
the instances of problematic content, could be misleading without additional context. For example,
if the AI reported that the group received a large amount of problematic content of a certain kind,
admins wanted to know whether these messages were all posted on a single day or over a span of
days, as this would significantly affect how urgent the situation is. Some admins felt that the AI
should also show which group members were sharing problematic messages to help them identify
members with disruptive behavior.
Building on these concerns, many admins pointed out that the appropriate enforcement of rules
requires understanding the deeper context of the flagged content. They wanted the AI to do more
than just identify the number and categories of problematic content as shown in the probe. Many
also wanted the AI to go beyond categorizing content and instead interpret tone, intent, and cultural
nuance. For instance, they wanted the AI to distinguish between sarcasm and hostility, or between
casual political jokes and propaganda, though they also questioned the AI’s ability to accurately
assess these nuances, especially when the content was not in English, as was common in some of
the groups. P19 shared:
“Sometimes in my group, there are people who joke a lot, use sarcastic comments, and
AI can always detect that and may potentially flag it as spam or something like that.
Or when people don’t use English or use references to movies or memes, it might get
flagged as an offensive message or something. That could be an issue.”
In contrast, admins of smaller, informal groups found these reports less necessary and felt rule
enforcement to be manageable. As P8, admin of a friends group put it,

4.4.2
AI-Assisted Interventions for Rule Violations. We then explored how participants felt about AI
intervening directly after a rule violation, either by sending a group-wide warning or by privately
messaging the offender (Figure 1D).
Private Messaging. In close-knit groups, admins preferred to personally reach out to violators
rather than delegating the task to AI to preserve interpersonal relationships. Admins in these
groups worried that the AI-issued warnings would feel impersonal, lacking the nuance and warmth
needed to carefully handle violations caused by members they knew personally. As P10, the admin
of a small friends group, explained:
“I would like to have a better human touch to interact with my friends. It’s a friends
group. If I select the AI to DM my friend, it will be more of an AI censorship.”
This sentiment was especially strong when addressing minor or first-time violations. Admins
preferred drafting a personalized message with the help of AI over allowing the AI to directly reach
out to violators. They also wanted to review and edit any message before sending it, particularly in
cases involving friends or family. Admins also preferred sending the warning message themselves
via DMs when dealing with repeat offenders, seeing it as a more deliberate escalation before
considering harsher actions such as temporary or permanent removal.
In formal groups, some admins lacked confidence in the AI’s ability to convey the gravity
of rule violation to offenders. They worried that the AI would default to a neutral tone and its
warnings would be perceived as insincere and weak. They believed personal outreach from the
admin would signal more authority and would be taken more seriously by the offender. Moreover,
some admins didn’t trust the AI’s ability to correctly interpret the context of rule violations. Instead,
they preferred to directly engage with the violators to better understand their intent before taking
any action.
In contrast, admins of large, public, or loosely moderated groups welcomed the idea of AI-
led messaging—particularly when they did not know the violator personally or wanted to avoid
confrontation. This feature was particularly welcomed in groups where confronting offenders
might expose admins to harassment due to gender dynamics or social hierarchy. In these cases,
admins saw AI as a protective buffer between themselves and the offenders. For example, P13, a
female admin of a large group of computer science students shared:
“Most members in my group are male students who occasionally post adult humor
against the group rule. I could use an AI to message the violators I don’t know personally.
Because if I message them myself, they might harass me.”
In smaller, close-knit groups, a few admins also found it easier to offload rule enforcement to
the AI to avoid directly moderating people in their social circles. For example, participants felt
confronting a close friend for violating a rule might lead to awkwardness. Similarly, correcting
an elderly relative in a family group might make them feel disrespected by the admin. In such

Public Warning. Some admins managing large, formal groups preferred that the AI send public,

and when to delegate enforcement to AI. While some saw AI as a helpful assistant or buffer, others
viewed it as a potential disruption to group culture. Designing AI tools that can adapt to these
contextual differences—and support both soft and strict forms of enforcement—is critical to their
acceptance and effectiveness.
4.5
AI and Rule Maintenance
Beyond creating and enforcing group rules, participants also reflected on how AI could support the
ongoing work of maintaining and updating rules over time. This included interpreting whether
rules were working as intended, identifying new patterns of behavior that might warrant policy
changes, and helping admins adapt rules as group dynamics evolved. We explore two proposed AI
features—weekly moderation summaries (Figure 1E) and AI-suggested new rules (Figure 1F)—and
how participants engaged with their possibilities and limitations across different types of groups.
4.5.1
AI-generated Weekly Summary. In Phase 2, participants were shown probes depicting a
weekly AI-generated summary of moderation activity. These included data such as the number of
messages flagged for violating rules and trends indicating how group behavior changed following
rule implementation. Most admins who managed large, highly active groups, expressed strong
interest in using AI-generated moderation summaries. They described feeling overwhelmed by
message volume in their groups and saw summaries as a way to gain a high-level view of how the
rules were impacting group behavior, and identify emerging trends in rule violations.
However, even among those who found the summaries useful in theory, few planned to use
them actively. This was especially true for passive admins, who only engaged in moderation in
response to disruptive group behavior or when group members brought an issue to their attention.
In small, close-knit groups, where violations were rare and moderation was minimal, admins felt
such features added little value. They wondered if merely receiving data-driven insights was enough
for decision making. For example, P11, the admin of a university group remarked:
“What ultimately matters is whether the chat feels clean, not the raw statistics on how
many violations occurred since a rule was implemented.”
These observations suggest that apart from focusing on counts or trends, AI systems should
consider how to meaningfully convey the impact of rules in ways that resonate with WhatsApp
group admins’ intuitive judgments and decision-making styles.

AI-generated Suggestions for New Rules. We also presented AI-generated suggestions for new

emphasized the importance of human judgment, relational knowledge, and collective input when
deciding whether and how to update group rules. Until AI systems better support participatory
processes and offer contextual cues rather than prescriptive changes, their role in rule maintenance
may remain secondary to human-led governance.
5
Discussion
Our study examines how WhatsApp group admins perceive AI assistance when creating, enforcing,
and maintaining group rules. Through a combination of speculative design study and co-creation of
rules with Meta AI, we analyzed how admins’ perceptions of AI-assisted moderation vary depending
on their group dynamics, size, and moderation practices. Our findings reveal that admins compare
the efficiency and scalability of AI-assisted moderation against how such tools would interfere
with the group’s context, relational dynamics, and their agency. Rather than viewing the AI as a
one-size-fits-all solution, participants found it useful in some cases, such as when moderating large
formal groups or groups of strangers, but unnecessary or even disruptive for close-knit groups.
In this section, we critically reflect on these nuances and situate our findings within the broader
context of AI-assisted content moderation.
5.1
Negotiating Human-AI Collaboration in Online Governance
While AI-assisted moderation tools offer tangible benefits across different stages of the rule life
cycle, our findings surface several concerns around data privacy, trust, relational dynamics, and
human authority. These concerns highlight the complexities that a WhatsApp admin has to navigate
when deciding how—and to what extent—to involve AI in governing their groups
5.1.1
Concerns Around Data Privacy. Across different WhatsApp groups, admins voiced discomfort
with granting AI unrestricted access to user-generated content for making and enforcing rules,
particularly when the groups discussed sensitive topics. They viewed group metadata such as

18
Nayak et al.
size, purpose, and existing rules as sufficient for co-creating rules with AI. Granting AI access
to full group chat histories felt unnecessary and potentially invasive. This distinction reflects
a need to separate rule making from message-level surveillance. These concerns echo broader
critiques of automated moderation systems, which highlight how such systems often deepen
platform surveillance while limiting user agency and transparency [22, 23]. Consistent with calls
for visible, accountable moderation infrastructures [65], some admins in our study expressed a
strong preference for on-device processing, selective data sharing, and the ability to revoke AI access
when needed, even for rule making. Rather than imagining data access as a one-time incident of
giving consent, admins framed consent as dynamic, revocable, and context-sensitive—a governance
expectation also reflected in emerging work on privacy-preserving moderation systems [36]. Yet,
current AI-assisted moderation tools on social media platforms like Reddit, Facebook, TikTok and
Discord rarely support this level of granular control, suggesting a critical gap between community
expectations and existing privacy design paradigms.
5.1.2
Boundary Work and the Demarcation of Relational Authority . Perhaps the most consistent
boundary articulated by admins in our study, across all group types, was the desire to maintain
human authority over AI decisions. We interpret this insistence not merely as a preference for
control, but as a form of active boundary work [21]. Gieryn originally conceptualized boundary
work as the rhetorical efforts scientists use to distinguish their professional domain from "non-
science," thereby protecting their autonomy and authority. Drawing on this analogy, we observed
WhatsApp admins constructing a sharp boundary between informational tasks such as summarizing
violations or flagging trends, which they were willing to cede to AI, and relational enforcement,
which they fiercely protected as an exclusively human domain.
When admins rejected AI-mediated interventions in close-knit groups, they were prioritizing
the maintenance of existing social rules over technical efficiency. By insisting that enforcement
requires "human touch" and "emotional intelligence", admins effectively categorized these tasks as
outside the jurisdiction of AI. This demarcation serves a dual purpose: first, it mitigates the risk
of "impersonal" automation damaging social ties, and second, it preserves the admin’s status as
the group’s indispensable moderator, a role that would be threatened if AI were seen as capable of
managing the group’s social fabric independently.
The interaction with AI-generated rules highlighted the role of these artifacts as boundary objects
[56]. Star and Griesemer defined boundary objects as entities that inhabit multiple social worlds
and satisfy the informational requirements of each, yet require translation to maintain coherence
across them. In our study, the AI-generated rules originated in the technical world of the LLM,
characterized by generic, formal, and context-insensitive language. For these rules to function in
the social world of the WhatsApp group, they required significant translation work by the admins.
This explains why admins rarely adopted AI-generated rules without modification. Instead,
the admins themselves acted as an obligatory point of passage, reshaping the tone, removing the
"generic and formal" language when necessary and injecting local cultural cues to ensure the rules
would be legible and acceptable to group members.
Participants also drew clear boundaries around enforcement. Participants’ insistence on retaining
final authority over rule enforcement was not solely about preserving a human touch, rather,
it was rooted in a broader skepticism about AI’s capacity to understand context and nuance.
Across all group types, admins expressed concern that AI lacked the ability to interpret sarcasm,
multilingual content, and culturally specific references, raising doubts about its reliability in both
rule enforcement and decision-making. Even when AI-assisted moderation offered practical benefits
in large, highly active groups, participants consistently emphasized the need for human oversight in

socially sensitive situations, a concern echoed in broader literature, warning that excessive reliance
on AI may disempower human moderators and erode accountability [23, 27, 34].
This labor of translation and judgment confirms that successful AI-assisted moderation in close-
knit communities is not a matter of seamless automation or simple human-in-the-loop verification.
Rather, it is a process of collaborative negotiation where human admins must continuously re-
contextualize algorithmic artifacts to bridge the gap between the context-agnostic nature of the
LLM and the situated, relational nature of their WhatsApp group.
5.1.3
AI as a social buffer. Prior work has reported how friction and relational conflicts among
moderators leads to psychological distress and frequent turnover among volunteer moderators
[49]. Unlike task conflict or disagreements about specific rules, relational conflict damages the
social fabric of the moderation team, leading many to simply quit. In the context of WhatsApp,
where the co-admin is often a family member or a close peer, the stakes of such relationship
conflict are exceptionally high. An admin cannot simply quit a family group without causing
social estrangement. Cai and Wohn [6] observe a similar tension on Twitch, where moderators
often adopt an obliging conflict style, yielding to the streamer’s preference for leniency to avoid
damaging their standing with the channel owner. In both cases, the human moderator is forced
to choose between enforcing rules and preserving relationships. However, our findings suggest
that AI-assisted moderation may offer a third path which is that of buffering. Rather than quitting
(Schöpke-Gonzalez) [49] or obliging (Cai and Wohn) [6], our participants expressed a desire to use
AI to depersonalize the conflict. By delegating the bad cop role to an AI agent, admins hope to
enforce rules while protecting their personal relationships from the friction of moderation. This
suggests that the value of AI in high-context, socially hierarchical groups lies not just in efficiency,
but in emotional safety, providing a buffer that protects the admin from the relationship conflict
that Schöpke-Gonzalez identifies as a key threat to sustainability.
5.1.4
Accounting for Cultural Misalignment and Algorithmic Hegemony. One of the most persistent
concerns raised by our participants was AI failing to grasp the cultural references, relational nuances,
and linguistic fluidity that define their groups. Though our speculative design approach limited our
ability to audit specific model failures, the deep-seated skepticism participants expressed highlights
a critical sociotechnical barrier. For many admins, AI’s utility hinges on cultural intelligibility or
its ability to grasp the "vibe" of a non-Western, high-context environment. This anxiety reflects a
broader challenge in AI-assisted moderation: the normative gaze of Western-centric models.
As recent work on moderation in the Global South argues, AI tools often embody a colonial
impulse, enforcing Western sensibilities regarding speech, politeness, and harm as universal stan-
dards [14, 53, 54]. These models are predominantly trained on English-centric data, which encodes
distinct cultural values that often conflict with local norms [3, 53]. When exported to non-Western
contexts like India, such systems risk enacting a form of algorithmic hegemony, where local com-
munication styles are flagged as deviant simply because they do not align with Western definitions
of professional or safe speech [47, 53]. Empirical evaluations of LLMs confirm this systemic bias,
revealing that models consistently align closer to Western cultural values than those of other
nations, regardless of the model’s origin or the language used to prompt [57]. Consequently, these
Western-centric models frequently misinterpret local nuances [53].
This misalignment creates distinct risks in the moderation loop, particularly regarding the
technical failure of code-mixing. Indian digital communication is often characterized by code
mixing of regional languages and English. However, current NLP technologies are primarily
designed for English morphology and struggle to process code-mixed Global South languages
[53]. Although our participants interacted with Meta AI exclusively in English and did not share
real group messages during the study, they repeatedly raised concerns about how such systems

outlined in our Findings, participants worried that sarcasm and other culturally situated banter,
especially when expressed in non-English or mixed-language forms, could be misread as spam or
offensive content. If an AI assistant cannot distinguish between benign community banter and
actual harassment, it forces admins to perform constant error-correction, effectively increasing
their workload rather than reducing it.
Consequently, widespread reliance on such tools may pressure users to flatten their commu-
nication styles to avoid algorithmic censure, leading to homogenization of expression [1]. Our
participants expressed concern that if AI systems routinely misread culturally situated interactions,
members could potentially adapt by decreasing their overall engagement in the group. In a moder-
ation context, this anticipated adaptation reflects a broader governance risk: group members may
preemptively self-censor legitimate cultural expression to keep their messages acceptable to the
system, a critical consequence of automated governance [22].
5.1.5
The Conditional Value of AI-Assisted Moderation. Our findings strongly indicate that value
of AI-assisted moderation is not uniform, rather, it varies significantly depending on a group’s
size, formality, and interpersonal dynamics. In large, highly active WhatsApp groups, admins often
found tools like AI-generated summaries and rule suggestions valuable. The volume of messages in
such groups made manual oversight impractical, and AI was perceived as a useful triage system that
helped surface violations, track trends, and assist in rule enforcement. These findings align with
prior work that positions algorithmic tools as helpful aids in high-scale environments, enabling
moderators to focus their effort where human judgment is most needed [22, 51]. In contrast, admins
of small, close-knit groups, such as family or intimate friend circles, rarely felt the need of such
tools. They described such features as “nice to have,” but ultimately unnecessary. That said, our
study highlights points along the spectrum, such as semi-formal groups of former colleagues or
large informal friend groups, where admins grapple with challenges related to both scale and
interpersonal dynamics, sometimes compounded by their overlap, and see value in AI-assisted
moderation to help navigate these complexities.
5.2
Design Implications for AI-Supported Group Moderation
Building on our findings, we outline design implications for AI systems intended to support
group moderation and rule co-creation. Rather than proposing interface-level changes for specific
platforms, our recommendations focus on broader principles that can guide the development of AI
tools across a range of social contexts. We posit that AI is not a universal solution. It is important
to remember that our participants in informal groups often found human judgment sufficient and
AI interventions unnecessary or intrusive. Therefore, these implications emphasize the need for
flexibility, contextual sensitivity, and collaborative control, particularly in communities where
relational dynamics and informal governance are central.
5.2.1
Integrating Context through Structured Input Collection. In our study, many participants found
it difficult to provide the context needed for generating suitable group rules, in part due to the lack
of structured support for surfacing relevant information. This challenge was especially pronounced
among passive moderators, who often failed to recall or articulate existing rules or past incidents.
Without a systematic way to collect such input, AI assistance might risk overlooking community
norms by producing generic or irrelevant rules. Participants often provided only brief prompts at
first and added more context only after receiving generic, misaligned AI outputs, which made the
editing process feel unnecessarily tedious and frustrating. This observation reflects findings by
Briakou et al. [5], who show that minimal input often triggers LLMs to produce verbose, catch-all

responses because vague or under specified prompts lead models to overcompensate, burdening
users with the task of filtering out irrelevant or overly careful content.
Existing rule-based automation moderation tools on platforms like Reddit [45, 46], Discord [13],
and Facebook [16] generally rely on admins manually setting up filters or rules, but they offer
little structured support for iterative context collection. Tools such as Reddit’s AutoModerator[46]
or Facebook’s Admin Assist[18] require admins to specify rules in advance, often through fixed
templates or keyword lists, without prompting reflection on group structure, member dynamics, or
evolving needs. As a result, these systems risk reinforcing static or overly rigid rules.
To avoid the issue of misaligned outputs owing to lack of community input, we recommend that
when designing AI tools for creating group rules, online platforms should provide structure to
collect group context. This could include brief surveys for users, editable templates, or step-by-step
structured questions from a chatbot to elicit information about the group size, composition of
group members, level of formality, common problems, and existing rules. More importantly, context
sharing should not be a one-time setup. Admins should be able to revisit and revise their inputs
over time as group dynamics shift, ensuring that AI suggestions remain relevant.
5.2.2
Design for Human Authority, Discretion, and Collaborative Oversight. AI tools for moderation
should be designed to empower human admins without compromising their agency and control.
Rather than automating decisions regarding rule violations, platforms should give admins the option
to delegate tasks to AI when they choose, such as handling problems at scale or surfacing trends, and
let admins intervene directly when trust, nuance, or relational judgment is needed. This includes
offering options to review, edit, or discard AI outputs. Most existing moderation tools on platforms
like Reddit, Discord, and Twitch ensure human oversight by requiring moderators to configure
filters or review flagged content before any action is taken. Reddit’s AutoModerator [46], for
instance, acts only on rules explicitly defined by moderators, while Twitch’s AutoMod [59] queues
flagged messages for human approval. Although these systems preserve individual discretion, they
offer limited support for structured collaborative oversight. While multiple moderators may have
technical access to rule configurations, there are few built-in affordances for co-editing, discussion,
or reviewing moderation decisions collectively. As a result, coordination often happens informally,
outside the platform.
We therefore recommend that online platforms should explicitly support collaborative oversight
by allowing admins to share AI-generated rules with co-admins or group members for feedback
prior to adoption.
5.2.3
Reducing Interpersonal Burden of Rule Enforcement. In small, informal groups, participants
preferred to engage with members directly when violations occurred, to preserve relationships. In
contrast, admins of semi-formal groups were more open to AI-mediated intervention following rule
violation. Several participants described moments when they feared personal backlash, harassment,
or relational strain from enforcing rules themselves, and imagined that the AI could help diffuse
tension by acting as a neutral mediator.
While many platforms aim to reduce moderator workload through automation, few directly
address the interpersonal burden of enforcement. Tools like Reddit’s AutoModerator[46] or Dis-
cord’s AutoMod [13] can remove or block content automatically, thereby sparing moderators from
manual enforcement, but they do so without any relational framing or support for interpersonal
nuance. Additionally, none of these tools provide editable templates or writing support for admins
to send personalized warning to violators or the whole group.
Therefore, online platforms can provide admins with different customizable templates or AI-
assisted writing support that admins can use to reach out to violators or send warnings to the

5.2.4
Enabling Rule Edits and Sharing. Currently, most existing tools offer limited support for
customizing or sharing rule outputs in user-facing formats. While platforms like Reddit and Discord
allow moderators to define rules and filters in editable text formats (e.g., YAML files or keyword
lists), these are primarily designed for internal configuration, not member-facing communication.
Facebook Groups support pinned rules [17], but with minimal formatting and no support for
tailoring tone or output style.
If online platforms provide AI-assistance for rule creation, they should also make it easy for
admins to customize AI suggestions, both to fit the group’s needs and make the rules more accessible
to group members. This includes enabling direct edits to AI suggestions, offering controls for
adjusting tone (e.g., strict, formal, friendly), and allowing formatting options like bullet points,
bolding, or emojis. Since rules are ultimately meant to be communicated to group members, outputs
should be copy-ready or easily exportable in formats (e.g., text blocks or images) suited for sharing.
Making rule customization intuitive and presentation-friendly helps admins bridge the gap between
AI-generated rules and making them communicable.
5.2.5
Providing Privacy-Aware Defaults and Granular Data Controls. End-to-end encrypted plat-
forms like WhatsApp are uniquely constrained, as their architecture prevents server-side access
to message content [63]. Any potential AI moderation would require users to actively share data
externally, raising significant privacy trade-offs. In such contexts, we recommend that these po-
tential AI tools rely on locally available or admin-provided metadata and prioritize minimal data
use, transparent data usage terms, and informed, revocable consent. We recommend that systems
default to collecting only the data necessary for their specific function. For instance, for supporting
rule co-creation, structured inputs such as group size, purpose, or existing rules typically suffice.
Access to full chat histories is rarely justified and may erode trust, especially in sensitive settings.
More broadly, across platforms like Reddit and Discord, where content is publicly accessible, we
recommend that AI moderation tools incorporate granular consent controls and clear communica-
tion with group members. Where possible, we recommend using local or on-device processing to
preserve user privacy while maintaining functionality.
5.2.6
Designing for Cultural Competency and Local Language Customization. As detailed in our
discussion, Western-centric AI models frequently misinterpret non-Western communication styles,
flagging benign language as toxic [53] and may suggest rules that are either irrelevant or misaligned
with the local context. Therefore, we recommend that AI-assisted moderation tools explicitly
support local language customization, and rather than relying solely on pre-trained Western-
centric standards, allow admins to provide few-shot examples of acceptable local speech, such as
code-mixed slang, playful banter, or community-specific terms of endearment, to guide the rule
drafting and enforcement process. By allowing admins to define the boundary between toxicity
and camaraderie within their local context, designers can avoid silencing legitimate speech and
ensure the AI respects the community’s own values rather than enforcing Western standards.
5.2.7
Designing for Selective Non-Use and Scalable Intervention. Finally, we caution against a
one-size-fits-all or default-on approach to AI moderation. Our findings indicate that in small,
close-knit groups, human autonomy is often sufficient, and AI interventions can be perceived as
performative, unnecessary or even disruptive to the established social contracts. A key design
implication, therefore, is to avoid the overreliance on AI-mediated support in contexts where
relational governance is already effective. We recommend that platforms apply a principle of
progressive disclosure for AI moderation tools. Rather than offering the full suite of rule-creation

space and preventing the erosion of human accountability, unless decided otherwise by the group

5.3
Limitations and Future Work
Our study has several limitations that open avenues for future research. First, we used a design
probe to illustrate AI-assisted moderation features. While this method allowed participants to react
to realistic scenarios without privacy risks, it limited their ability to interact with the features
directly. As a result, our findings reflect anticipated rather than actual use, which is an inherent
limitation of design probes. This speculative approach was necessary given the ethical and practical
constraints of deploying a live AI assistant in encrypted WhatsApp groups.
Second, participants engaged in a rule co-writing exercise using the inbuilt Meta AI chatbot
(Llama 3.2) within WhatsApp. While this allowed us to study admins’ real-time interactions with
an LLM-chat bot for co-creating rules, the findings specific to this exercise are constrained by
the capabilities of this single model. We selected the Meta AI chatbot because it is inbuilt into
the WhatsApp ecosystem used by billions of people, thereby offering high ecological validity.
However, this also means that we cannot definitively distinguish whether observed frictions, such
as verbosity or rigid formatting, are inherent limitations of generative AI or specific artifacts of
this model’s implementation. Future studies should explore how alternative models and different
prompt-engineering strategies may shape admin experiences in co-creating rules.
Third, our participant sample skewed toward urban, college-educated users in India due to
recruiting limitations related to conducting remote research. Since WhatsApp is widely used across
diverse populations, future work should recruit a broader demographic, including rural users,
non-English speakers, and lower-literacy groups, to get the full picture of how AI moderation tools
are received across diverse social contexts.
Fourth, many groups in our study were governed through shared decision-making. However, we
recruited individual admins rather than admin pairs due to the logistical difficulty of coordinating
multiple moderators from the same WhatsApp group, a challenge that was present in public
groups but especially acute in smaller, private groups due to privacy concerns. Several participants
expressed discomfort with us interviewing multiple co-admins from a single family or close-knit
group, since doing so could increase the risk of identity exposure and reveal sensitive interpersonal
dynamics. As a result, our findings rely on participants’ self-reported descriptions of collaboration
rather than observed behaviors. This limited our ability to systematically examine how intra-
admin power dynamics such as conflicting moderation styles or disagreements over AI-generated
outputs—might shape joint decision-making.
A promising direction for future work lies in building an AI-assisted rule creation tool that
admins can integrate into their ongoing group governance practices. Given WhatsApp’s end-to-end
encryption and limitations around deploying external tools directly, future systems might explore
out-of-platform, user-controlled tools (e.g., a rule assistant app for rule drafting and copy-paste
workflows) that decouple governance from platform’s own infrastructure. Such tools could employ
a data donation model bulding on tools like WhatsApp Explorer [20], where admins can potentially
voluntarily contribute anonymized rule sets or group data to a community-managed repository.
This would allow moderators to generate rules using independent, community-aligned models
rather than relying solely on Meta’s internal AI, thereby circumventing concerns regarding the

6
Conclusion
Our study highlights how WhatsApp group admins across diverse groups perceive, adapt, and resist
AI assistance in group rule creation and moderation. While admins of large, formal and semi-formal
groups saw AI tools as useful for drafting rules, summarizing violations, and handling scale, admins
of smaller, close-knit groups often found such interventions unnecessary or relationally disruptive.
Across the spectrum, participants asserted the importance of preserving human authority, relational
sensitivity, and contextual judgment, especially in emotionally charged or ambiguous scenarios.
These findings challenge assumptions that automation universally eases moderation, showing
instead that AI-assisted moderation tools must flexibly adapt to different governance styles, group
dynamics, and expectations of control.
We offer design recommendations focused on providing structured mechanisms to collect relevant
group context from admins to generate appropriate rules, enabling flexible customization of tone,
length, and formatting as per the group culture, supporting privacy-conscious data sharing and
revocable consent, and building features that allow admins to collaboratively review and approve
AI outputs with co-admins and group members before enforcement. Ultimately, we advocate
for a flexible design where AI tools remain optional, allowing small groups to function without
unnecessary automation. As platforms begin exploring AI-driven tools for moderation in end-to-end
encrypted environments, our work underscores the need to design AI-assisted tools that support
admins’ relational practices, accommodate diverse group rules, and preserve human judgment,
especially in private, small-group settings.
