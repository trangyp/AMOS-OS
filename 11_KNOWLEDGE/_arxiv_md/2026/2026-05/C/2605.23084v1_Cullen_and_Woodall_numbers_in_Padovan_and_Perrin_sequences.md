Cullen and Woodall numbers in Padovan and Perrin sequences
Herbert Batte1,∗
Eric F. Bravo2
Florian Luca1,3
Abstract
Let {Pn}n≥0 and {Rn}n≥0 denote the Padovan and Perrin sequences, both satisfying the recurrence
Un+3 = Un+1 + Un, but with initial values P0 = P1 = P2 = 1 and R0 = 3, R1 = 0, R2 = 2, respectively.
A Cullen number is a positive integer of the form m · 2m + 1 for some integer m ≥1, while a Woodall
number is a positive integer of the form m·2m −1 for some integer m ≥1. In this paper, we determine all
Woodall numbers in the Padovan sequence and all Cullen numbers in the Perrin sequence. Specifically,
we prove that 1 and 7 are the only Woodall numbers in the Padovan sequence, and that 3 is the only
Cullen number in the Perrin sequence.
Keywords and phrases: Cullen number; Padovan number; Perrin number; Woodall number; 2-adic
valuation; Linear forms in logarithms.

Introduction
In 1905, Reverend James Cullen [6] published the first study on the primality of numbers of the form
m · 2m + 1. He realized that, apart from 1 · 21 + 1 = 3 and with the possible exception of 53 · 253 + 1, the
first hundred of these numbers are composite. A year later, Cunningham [7] published the finding that
5591 divides 53 · 253 + 1. The first non-trivial prime number of this form was discovered by Robinson
[14] nearly fifty year later, who confirmed Cunningham’s suspicion that 141 · 2141 + 1 was prime. These
numbers are now called Cullen numbers. We denote them by Cm for every positive integer m. A Cullen
number that is prime is called a Cullen prime.
In 1917, Cunningham and Woodall [8], inspired by Cullen’s work, published the first paper on the
primality of numbers of the form m · 2m −1. They proved that there were only three prime numbers of
this form in the range 1 ≤m ≤29, namely 2 · 22 −1, 3 · 23 −1, and 6 · 26 −1. These numbers are now
called Woodall numbers, and primes of this form are called Woodall primes. We denote them by Wm for
all m ∈Z+.
In the mid-twentieth century, mathematicians wondered how often Cullen and Woodall primes
appeared. In 1976, Hooley [9] showed that the natural density of positive integers m ≤x for which
Cm is prime is of the order o(x) when x →∞.
In other words, almost all of Cullen’s numbers are
composite. Hooley’s proof was reworked by Hiromi Suyama to show that it works for any sequence of
numbers of the form m·2m+a +b, where a and b are integers, and in particular also for Woodall numbers.
To date, sixteen Culllen primes and thirty-four Woodall primes are known.
The largest are C6679881
and W17016602. They were discovered through distributed computing and citizen collaboration via the
international PrimeGrid project.
In 2003, Luca and St˘anic˘a [11] began the search for Cullen and Woodall numbers in linear recurrence
sequences. They proved that there are only a finite number of them in any binary recurrence sequence
that satisfies certain conditions. They applied their result to the Fibonacci and Pell sequences. They
found that 1 and 3 are the only Cullen numbers that are also Fibonacci numbers, and 1 is the only
Woodall number that is also a Fibonacci number; while the only Cullen number and the only Woodall
number present in the Pell sequence is 1. Recently, Alahmadi and Luca [1] proved that there is no Cullen
number that is also a repunit number.
Regarding the presence of Cullen numbers and Woodall numbers in recurrent linear sequences of order
greater than 2, we have the recent results of B´erczes, Pink, and Young [2], and Bravo and Irmak [4].
B´erczes et al. [2] considered the k-generalized Fibonacci sequence, which is a linear recurrent sequence

For their part, Bravo and Irmak [4] considered a pair of linear recurrent ternary sequences: the Padovan
and Perrin sequences. The Padovan numbers {Pn}n∈Z are defined by the Fibonacci-like recurrence relation
Pn+3 = Pn+1 + Pn
with initial values P0 = P1 = P2 = 1. The Perrin numbers {Rn}n∈Z are defined by the same recurrence
relation as the Padovan numbers but with starting values R0 = 3, R1 = 0, and R2 = 2.
Bravo and Irmak [4] determined that 3, 9, and 65 are all the Cullen numbers appearing in the Padovan
sequence, and that 7 is the only Woodall number appearing in the Perrin sequence.
Their approach
combined a precise analysis of the 2-adic valuations of {Pn −1}n≥0 and {Rn + 1}n≥0 to obtain absolute
bounds, followed by a computational verification.
It is natural then to ask for the complementary results. What are all the Woodall numbers in the
Padovan sequence, and what are all the Cullen numbers in the Perrin sequence? In this paper, we answer
both questions completely. Our approach parallels that of [4], but requires a new and careful analysis of
the 2-adic valuations of {Pn +1}n≥0 and {Rn −1}n≥0, which are the objects relevant to Woodall numbers
in Padovan and Cullen numbers in Perrin, respectively. We prove the following results.
1.1
Main Results
Theorem 1.1. The only Woodall numbers in the Padovan sequence are 1 and 7.
Corollary 1.2. The only Woodall prime in the Padovan sequence is 7.
Theorem 1.3. The only Cullen number in the Perrin sequence is 3.
Corollary 1.4. The only Cullen prime in the Perrin sequence is 3.
The proofs of Theorems 1.1 and 1.3 combine an application of Baker’s theory of linear forms in three
logarithms of algebraic numbers and the 2-adic valuation of {Pn + 1}n≥0 and {Rn −1}n≥0, followed by
a computational verification.
