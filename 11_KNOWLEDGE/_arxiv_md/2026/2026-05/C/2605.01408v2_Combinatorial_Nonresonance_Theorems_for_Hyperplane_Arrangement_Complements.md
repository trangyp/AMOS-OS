COMBINATORIAL NONRESONANCE THEOREMS FOR HYPERPLANE
ARRANGEMENT COMPLEMENTS
BAITING XIE
Abstract. We study the nonresonance phenomenon for complex rank-one local systems on complements
of hyperplane arrangements. We refine the method of Cohen, Dimca, and Orlik and obtain a combinatorial
sufficient condition for nonresonance. As an application, we strengthen a theorem of Bailet, Dimca, and
Yoshinaga by removing one of its conditions. We also develop restriction and lifting techniques to prove a
nonresonance theorem for line arrangements.
1. Introduction
A classical problem in the theory of hyperplane arrangements is to determine which topological invariants
of their complements are governed by their intersection combinatorics. For example, Orlik and Solomon [12]
proved that the integral cohomology ring is combinatorially determined.
However, Rybnikov’s counter-
example [14] (see also [1]) implies that the fundamental group is not.
An important intermediate case
between them is the cohomology with coefficients in a complex rank-one local system. This is also related
to the question of whether the C-cohomology groups of the associated Milnor fibers of the arrangements are
combinatorially determined [4]. See [13] for a recent progress in this direction.
In studying such cohomology groups, the phenomenon of nonresonance, namely the vanishing of co-
homology outside the middle dimension, has received particular attention. A natural question is whether
this property is determined by the combinatorial data of the arrangement together with that of the local
system.

plement denoted by M(A). Let L be a complex rank-one local system on M(A). The pair (A, L) is said
to be nonresonant if Hp(M(A), L) = 0 for all p ̸= n. The combinatorial data of A are encoded in its
intersection lattice L(A). And a complex rank-one local system on M(A) is determined up to isomorphism
by its counter-clockwise monodromy around the hyperplanes, yielding the following one-to-one correspon-

2. Preliminaries: Hyperplane Arrangements
In this section, we recall some basic notion about combinatorics and geometry of hyperplane arrangement
complements. Our goal is to introduce Theorem 2.9 and prove Lemma 2.10.
Throughout this section, let A be a hyperplane arrangement in Pn and L a complex rank-one local
system on M(A) with monodromy map m: A →C×.
2.1. Combinatorics. For each subset S of A, let Z(S) be the intersection of hyperplanes in S. Denote
by V (S) the cone of Z(S) and r(S) = codimZ(S) the codimension of Z(S) in Pn. In particular, note that
V (S) = 0 and r(S) = n + 1 when Z(S) = ∅.
A subset F of A is called a flat if
F = {H ∈A | H ⊇Z(F)}.
The intersection lattice L(A) is then defined to be the ranked lattice consisting of all flats of A, ordered
by inclusion. The rank function of L(A) is given by r: L(A) →N. Furthermore, the monodromy map can
be extended to m: L(A) →C× as m(F) = Q
H∈F m(H).
A nonempty flat F is called irreducible if there do not exist proper subsets S1, S2 ⊊F such that
r(S1) + r(S2) = r(F) and S1 ∪S2 = F. Such a flat is called resonant (with respect to L) if in addition
r(F) ≤n and m(F) = 1. Denote by IF(A) the set of irreducible flats of A. Let RF(A, L) ⊆IF(A) be the
subset of resonant flats with respect to L. The following lemma implies that irreducible flats are building
blocks in L(A).
Lemma 2.1. ([5]) Every nonempty flat F can be written as a disjoint union F = F1 ⊔F2 ⊔· · · ⊔Fm of
irreducible flats such that r(F) = r(F1) + r(F2) + · · · + r(Fm). Furthermore, such decomposition is unique
and satisfies the following properties:
(1) Every irreducible flat G contained in F is contained in one of Fi.
(2) Given any flat Gi ⊆Fi, the disjoint union G1 ⊔G2 ⊔· · · ⊔Gm is still a flat of A.
The disjoint union F = F1 ⊔F2 ⊔· · · ⊔Fm in Lemma 2.1 is called the irreducible decomposition of
F, and the Fi are called irreducible components of F.
