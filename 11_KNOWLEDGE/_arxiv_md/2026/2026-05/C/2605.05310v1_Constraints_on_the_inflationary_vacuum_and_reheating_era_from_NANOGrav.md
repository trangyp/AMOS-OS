ary origin, we use latest NANOGrav 15-year dataset to constrain the inflationary parameters
e.g., tensor spectral index (nt), tensor-to-scalar ratio (r), and explore the implications for
the reheating phase through constraints on the reheating equation of state (ωre) and reheat-
ing temperature (Tre). We find the preference for an extremely blue-tilted tensor spectrum

Introduction
Decades of precise pulsar timing observations from millisecond pulsar arrays provide a plat-
form for detecting the stochastic gravitational wave background (SGWB) in the nHz fre-
quency range. Over the past couple of years, several pulsar timing array (PTA) collabora-
tions such as NANOGrav [1], EPTA [2], IPTA [3], PPTA [4], InPTA [5], CPTA [6] have
observed a common red noise spectrum with distinct angular correlations among the pul-
sars. This correlation is referred to as the Hellings-Downs curve [7], a hallmark signature
of the isotropic stochastic gravitational wave (GW) background. Recently, NANOGrav has
reported evidence for the stochastic GW signal at 3.5σ-4σ statistical significance after analyz-
ing the 15-year data set comprising observations of 67 pulsars along with their correlations.
However, the exact origin of such SGWB remains unsettled as a plethora of cosmological
sources such as inflation [8–10], first-order phase transition [11–23], cosmic defects [23–42],
supermassive black hole binaries [23, 43–45] etc. can mimic such SGWB. Later on, in a
companion work [46], the NANOGrav collaboration provided a comprehensive list of cos-
mological sources that can address the observed data. Among them, one of the intriguing
origins of SGWB is the inflationary GW sourced by the tensor perturbations originated
during inflation [47–51].
Inflationary interpretation of SGWB demands a blue-tilted gravitational wave spectrum,
i.e., the tensor spectral index, nt > 0 [8]. Typically, the GW spectral density depends on two
quantities: tensor spectral index (nt) and tensor-to-scalar ratio (r). The observed common
red noise spectrum is parametrized by the amplitude (A) and spectral index (γ) of the
PTA signal, which are related to the tensor spectral index and tensor-to-scalar ratio. As a
result, the allowed range of A and γ from the observed SGWB can constrain the inflationary
parameters as shown in Refs. [8–10]. It is noteworthy to mention that, although the blue-
tilted gravitational wave sourced during inflation explains the NANOGrav observation, it
cannot be accommodated within the conventional slow-roll inflationary paradigm, which
prefers nt < 0 (i.e., red tilted) since nt = −r/8 [52] and r > 0 from the data [53].
Besides constraining the inflationary parameters, the NANOGrav observation can also
constrain the reheating phenomenon. Usually, in this standard cosmological picture, one
assumes that the universe enters the radiation-dominated era right after the end of inflation.
In this standard picture, the tensor perturbations at nHz frequencies, a range that is the

primary focus of the PTA experiments, re-enter the horizon during the radiation-dominated
era, where the equation of state is 1/3. As a result, the GW relic density depends on r
and nt. In case of non-instantaneous reheating [54–62], the GW energy density depends on
the reheating equation-of-state parameter (ωre) and the reheating temperature (Tre). The

be radiation-dominated before big bang nucleosynthesis (BBN), which disfavors Tre below 4
MeV [63–66]. Thus, the permissible window of reheating temperature becomes quite narrow;
in particular, the viable range is approximately 4-50 MeV.
In standard treatments, the calculation of primordial GWs assumes the inflationary
vacuum to be of Bunch-Davies type [67]. However, the choice of the inflationary vacuum
is not unique due to the underlying de-Sitter symmetry. So, it is important to investigate
how the usual gravitational wave spectrum is modified when the inflationary vacuum is
non-Bunch-Davies. In literature, several studies have investigated the effect of non-Bunch-
Davies vacuum on the primordial power spectra [68–71], non-Gaussianity [72–84], and GW
spectrum [69, 85–87]. In this work, we compute the GW spectrum for such a vacuum, explore
its phenomenological implications, and, for the first time, place observational constraints on
the parameters characterizing this vacuum state.
GWs redshift as radiation after production in the early universe and contribute to
the radiation energy density as an additional component.
Such an extra contribution is
parametrized as an excess in the effective number of relativistic species, ∆Neff.
Cosmic
microwave background (CMB) experiments measure the quantity Neff with the following
precision Neff = 2.99 ± 0.17 [53]. Such a measured bound on ∆Neff puts an upper bound
on the energy density stored in the GW parametrized by ΩGWh2 ≤2 × 10−6, irrespective
of its origin. The SGWB, having an inflationary origin, has to be consistent with the CMB
observations. Unfortunately, the blue-tilted gravitational wave violates such a constraint,
giving rise to the so-called blue-tilted issue of the GW spectrum. However, several solutions
have been proposed to address the blue-tilted issue, such as early matter or primordial
black hole domination [88, 89], running of the tensor spectral index [10], inclusion of the
contribution from astrophysical sources of GWs [10], etc.
In this work, we use the NANOGrav 15-year data to probe the inflationary reheating
phase, considering that the observed SGWB has an inflationary origin. In this case, one can
express amplitude A and spectral index γ of the GW spectrum as functions of the inflationary
parameters r, nt, and the reheating parameters ωre, Tre. We perform a Markov chain Monte
Carlo (MCMC) analysis to determine the range of these parameters consistent with the
NANOGrav data. Next, we calculate the gravitational wave spectrum considering a non-
Bunch-Davies inflationary vacuum parametrized by αt and βt. In this scenario, the tensor
power spectrum depends explicitly on the vacuum parameters αt and βt, and consequently,
so does the GW spectrum. The smallness of the amplitude of the power spectrum constrains
the parameter βt to be 0. We perform an additional MCMC analysis to constrain the vacuum
parameter αt, thereby restricting its allowed range. Finally, we show that introducing a scale-
dependency on the vacuum parameter αt can address the blue-tilted issue before exceeding
the bound imposed by BBN and CMB.

5
Blue-tilted spectrum and frequency dependent alpha-vacuum
A blue-tilted GW energy spectrum, when extrapolated to high frequencies, can violate the
constraints imposed by big bang nucleosynthesis. In this section, we demonstrate that intro-
ducing a frequency-dependent parametrization of the vacuum parameter αt provides a viable
resolution to this issue.
Before introducing a frequency-dependent parametrization, we first study the behaviour
of the GW energy spectrum as a function of frequency. For illustration, we choose a bench-
mark from the 2σ contour of nt and ωre in Figure 2 to depict the GW spectrum. Figure 3
shows the GW relic density as function of frequency for ωre = 0.42, nt = 1.43, αt = 8.0 with
reheating temperature fixed at Tre = 50 MeV. Here, the gray shaded region is excluded due
to constraints coming from BBN. The red dots represent the NANOGrav 15-year data [46].
The evolution of the universe can also be understood by looking at the slope of the plot in
Figure 3, where changes in slope occur at f ∼10−17 Hz during the matter-radiation equality
and at nHz frequency during radiation-reheating equality as highlighted in Figure 3 inset. It
is obvious that after satisfying the NANOGrav observation, the GW spectrum hits the BBN
disallowed region i.e., the required blue-tilted spectrum violates the BBN bound.
According to the joint posterior distribution of ωre and nt in Figure 2, increasing ωre
results in smaller value of nt, and vice versa. It turns out that for such combination of ωre
and nt the GW spectrum will violate the BBN constraints at smaller frequency even before
as in Figure 3 and in some cases the GW spectrum will be so steep that it will hit the
BBN constraint before satisfying the NANOGrav data. Therefore, the parameter values in
Figure 3 are chosen such that the resulting spectrum is consistent with the NANOGrav data.
Also, a smaller value of αt makes the GW spectrum more steeper. Accordingly, the vacuum
parameter αt is chosen to ensure consistency with the data. Similarly, a smaller value of the
reheating equation of state ωre also implies that the GW spectrum saturates the BBN bound
at a lower frequency. Accordingly, we set ωre = 0.42 for which the GW spectrum saturates
the BBN bound at fS ∼10−5 Hz.

We demonstrate that a frequency-dependent alpha-vacuum provides a solution to the
long-standing blue-tilted problem when frequency dependency is introduced beyond a co-
moving scale kS. However, several solutions have been proposed in the literature to address
the blue-tilt issue.
These include incorporating astrophysical contributions to GWs [10],
running of the tensor spectral index [10], and the suppression of the GW spectrum due to
late-time entropy injection resulting from early matter domination [88, 89]. Notably, our
study presents a minimal solution to the so-called blue-tilted problem.
Finally, we analyse the cosmological consequences of this frequency dependent parametriza-
tion. We find that resolving the blue-tilted issue in the alpha-vacuum requires introducing
frequency dependency in the vacuum parameter αt beyond fS = 10−5 Hz.
This transi-
tion frequency corresponds to small scales during inflation. The scalar power spectrum is
known to be nearly scale-invariant (ns ≃1) in the CMB pivot scale (k∗= 0.05 Mpc−1) [53].
However, the behaviour of the scalar spectrum on smaller scales remains currently obser-
vationally unconstrained. Frequency dependency in vacuum parameters may also affect the
scalar power spectrum. If the vacuum parametrization for scalar and tensor modes is identi-
cal i.e., αt = αs, the scalar spectrum may exhibit a different scale dependence at small scales
compared to its near scale-invariant form at CMB scales.
So far, we have discussed solutions to the blue-titled issue, although it is important to
note whether such a large nt (∼2.20) value is realizable within inflationary models. In case
of the standard slow-roll single field inflation model, the spectra should be red-tilted (nt < 0)

6
Summary and Conclusion
Recently, NANOGrav and other pulsar timing array experiments (EPTA, IPTA, PPTA,
InPTA, and CPTA) have provided evidence for a common red-spectrum stochastic signal
across pulsars in the low-frequency regime. On top of that, a compelling evidence of the
Hellings-Downs inter-pulsar correlation has been observed, which provides the first detection
of the stochastic gravitational wave background in the nHz frequency range.
There is a
potential room for several possible explanations of the quest for the origin of such SGWB, for
example, the background of merging supermassive black hole binaries, cosmological origins,
etc. It is essential to scrutinize whether the cosmological explanation is consistent with the
observation because it helps to decide among the mentioned possibilities. In this work, we
test whether the SGWB has an inflationary origin, where cosmological GWs sourced during
the inflationary era constrain the inflationary reheating phase.
The primary result of our analysis, shown in Figure 1, is that the NANOGrav data prefer
an extremely blue-tilted spectrum with a large equation-of-state parameter for reheating. In
particular, by performing an MCMC analysis we have obtained nt = 2.20+0.36
−1.2 , ωre = 0.33+0.14
−0.36
in addition to placing an lower bound on tensor-to-scalar ratio, r > 3.16 × 10−11 for fixed
reheating temperature, Tre = 50 MeV. Our fit shows that the radiation-like reheating picture
(ωre = 1/3) is preferred; however, matter-like reheating (ωre = 0) is viable within the 1σ
range. Also, our analysis shows that realizing a nearly scale-invariant scenario (nt ≈0) is
excluded at more than 2σ confidence level.
Generally, most studies of inflationary GWs sourced by tensor perturbations assume
that the primordial vacuum is a Bunch-Davies vacuum. However, there is no solid evidence
for such a choice. In this work, we explore the possibility of a non-Bunch-Davies primor-
dial vacuum and examine the NANOGrav observations, suggesting that the SGWB has an
inflationary origin. We find that the NANOGrav 15-year data constrain the parameters α
and β that characterize the non-Bunch-Davies vacuum. Notably, observations from CMB
— particularly the small amplitude of the scalar power spectrum and the tensor-to-scalar
ratio — indicate that βt has to be zero. Therefore, the NANOGrav observations favour a
specific type of vacuum, namely the alpha-vacuum parametrized by αt. Furthermore, MCMC
analysis shows that the NANOGrav data significantly narrow the range of the parameter αt
by reducing its upper limit to αt < 11.5. For the first time, we provide a phenomenological
constraint on the parameters that define the alpha-vacuum.
We have previously noted that observation requires nt > 0; as a result, the GW spectrum
exceeds the Planck upper limit when extrapolated to higher frequencies. This issue is common
across all scenarios attempting to explain the observed SGWB as having an inflationary
origin.
In our setup, we have found that if the parameter αt, which describes the non-
Bunch-Davies vacuum, exhibits a specific frequency dependence above a threshold, the blue-
– 14 –


## Page 16


tilted issue can be resolved. Therefore, we choose the value of D so that the GW spectrum
begins to decline with increasing frequency after the threshold frequency. In summary, we
have investigated how 15-year NANOGrav data can constrain the inflationary reheating
phase and the primordial vacuum of inflation, and have shown that observations prefer a
specific non-Bunch-Davies vacuum, known as the alpha-vacuum. On top of that, we find
that a frequency-dependent parametrization of αt beyond a specific frequency provides the
simplest approach to resolve the long-standing blue-tilted issue. In addition, the resulting
GW spectrum will be further tested in future GW experiments such as LISA, DECIGO,
Cosmic Explorer, and the Einstein Telescope.
