COMMUTATIVE DECOMPOSITION OF INFINITE SYMMETRIC GROUPS AND
TRANSFORMATION MONOIDS
LUNA ELLIOTT AND ALEX LEVINE
Abstract. The commutative subgroup width of a group G is the smallest k such that there are abelian
subgroups A0, A1, . . . , Ak−1 ≤G with G = A0A1 . . . Ak−1. Commutative (inverse) submonoid width is
defined analogously.
In 2002, Ab´ert showed, rather surprisingly, that the commutative subgroup width
of the symmetric group on an infinite set is always finite. It was later shown by Seress that it is always
bounded above by 14. We answer a question of Seress and show that in fact the commutative subgroup
width of Sym(N) is at most 9. We improve the best known lower bound to 4. We also study standard
monoid analogues of the symmetric group; showing that the commutative submonoid widths of the full
transformation monoid NN, the partial transformation monoid PN and the symmetric inverse monoid IN are
exactly 3. We conclude by showing that the commutative inverse submonoid width of any infinite symmetric
inverse monoid is always infinite.
1. Introduction
A group G is said to have finite commutative subgroup width if there exists n ∈N such that G =
C1C2 · · · Cn, where C1, . . . , Cn are commutative subgroups of G. We write cgw(G) for the least such n if it
exists, otherwise we write cgw(G) = ∞. Commutative inverse submonoid width and commutative submonoid
width are defined analogously and are denoted by ciw and cmw respectively. As we will see later, these all
coincide in the case of a group, although ciw(M) and cmw(M) may differ for an inverse monoid M.
A related notion is that of bounded generation, also called finite cyclic width, which is the same definition
as having finite commutative subgroup width, except requiring that all of the relevant subgroups are cyclic,
rather than just commutative. Naturally, bounded generation implies finite commutative subgroup width.
There exist various studies of bounded generation, thus giving (finite) upper bounds for the commutative
subgroup width in a number of examples, including SLn(Z) for n ≥3 [2,4,6,10,13–15].
Ab´ert directly studied commutative subgroup width in symmetric groups, showing that if X is an infinite
set, then the symmetric group Sym(X) on X has finite commutative subgroup width [1] (of at most 289).
Seress drastically improved this upper bound to 14 [16]. Ito also proved that 3 is a lower bound for any
non-metabelian group [7], which of course includes Sym(X), as Seress also noted. Ab´ert also considers the
finite symmetric groups, providing upper and lower bounds in each case (the latter of which are generally
much sharper than 3).
Our purpose is to study the ‘standard’ analogues of the infinite symmetric group that occur in group and
semigroup theory, including the symmetric group itself. We begin with the symmetric group on the naturals.
We sharpen the upper bound to 9 and the lower bound to 4. This proves a conjecture of Seress [16].
Theorem A. 4 ≤cgw(Sym(N)) ≤9.
Additionally, we consider the three analogues of the symmetric group in semigroup theory: the full
transformation monoid NN, the partial transformation monoid PN and the symmetric inverse monoid IN. In
each case, we show that commutative submonoid width is exactly 3, contrasting with the symmetric group.
Theorem B. cmw(NN) = cmw(PN) = cmw(IN) = 3.
Unlike the case of groups where commutative subgroup and submonoid widths coincide, this is not the
case for inverse monoids. Indeed, we show that IN has infinite commutative inverse submonoid width, in
stark contrast with its commutative submonoid width of 3.
Theorem C. ciw(IN) = ∞.

Theorem to these monoids when viewed as topological monoids. We aim to make this accessible to non-
topologists, and all topology used is fully contained within Section 2. The use of the Baire Category Theorem

3. The Symmetric Group
This section covers our study of the commutative subgroup width of Sym(N) and Sym(X) for arbitrary
infinite sets X. We begin with a short proof of the fact that for a group, the commutative submonoid,
subgroup and inverse submonoid width all coincide. We thus hereafter use the subgroup width.
Lemma 3.1. If G is a group, then cgw(G) = cmw(G) = ciw(G).
Proof. First note that inverse submonoids of G and subgroups of G coincide, and so we need only show that
cgw(G) = cmw(G). As subgroups are submonoids, we trivially have cmw(G) ≤cgw(G). If cmw(G) = ∞,
then clearly cmw(G) ≥cgw(G).
Otherwise let k = cmw(G), and let C0, C1, . . . , Ck−1 be commutative
submonoids of G such that G = C0C1 . . . Ck−1. It follows that the same equality holds when we replace each
Ci with the subgroup it generates. As the group generated by a set of commuting elements is abelian, it
follows that k ≥cgw(G) as required.
□

5. Further questions
We have narrowed the possibilities for cmw(Sym(N)) to the values 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9. As such it is
plausible that an exact value can be found.
Question 5.1. What is the commutative subgroup width of Sym(N)?
Many results for Sym(N) and NN have been generalised to the automorphism groups and endomorphism
monoids of important structures such as Fra¨ıss´e limits [3,9,11,12]. It is not clear to what extent the results
