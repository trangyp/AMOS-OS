author with R. Spitler from split groups to arbitrary groups.

1.1. Motivation. Our interest in this concept stems from its implica-
tions for two rigidity properties of arithmetic subgroups of G: congru-
ence rigidity and profinite solitude. To explain this, recall that we ob-

yields the congruence completion G(Ok). Using the notation “≈” for
abstractly commensurable groups, it is natural to define that G(Ok) is
congruence rigid if for every l-group H, we have that G(Ok) ≈H(Ol)
implies G(Ok) ≈H(Ol).

profinite solitude. One obstacle is the longstanding problem whether
arithmetic groups with CSP are Grothendieck rigid [20, Problem 2].
Definition 3. A group Γ is Grothendieck rigid if no proper inclusion of
a finitely generated subgroup H ⪇Γ induces an isomorphism bH ∼= bΓ.
Though the problem was formulated decades ago, it seems that not
for a single arithmetic group with CSP has it been determined whether
it is Grothendieck rigid.

Assuming Serre’s conjecture, it thus remains to consider the case
that a given higher rank k-group G is congruence rigid among higher
rank groups, but there exists an l-group H locally isomorphic to G with
rank∞H < 2. Clearly, if rank∞H = 0, then H(Ol) is finite so that such
an H does not prevent G(Ok) from being profinitely solitary. So the
only remaining case is that there exists such an H with rank∞H = 1.
This case will be discussed in Section 2 below. At this point, let us
just say that the remaining case is rare: it cannot occur if G has type
1An for n ≥2, 1D2n for n ≥2, 3D4, E6, E7, E8, or G2 and in the other

strongly Grothendieck rigid arithmetic group G(Ok) in the remaining
case will still be profinitely solitary. However, in type Cn and F4, the
question depends on the well-known open problem whether lattices in
Sp(n, 1) and F4(−20) have CSP.

1.3. Criteria. It turns out that there are six different reasons that
may prevent a k-group G from being congruence rigid. Four of them
concern the field k only.
(I) The field k is not locally determined.
(II) The field k has Q-isomorphic quadratic extensions l1/k and l2/k
but no isomorphism l1 ∼= l2 restricts to an automorphism of k.
(III) The field k has quadratic extensions l1/k and l2/k with an iso-
morphism Af
l1 ∼= Af
l2 that restricts to an automorphism of Af
k.
(IV) The field k has too many real places.
(V) The group G reduces to non-isomorphic groups at too many
pairs of finite places v, w with kv ∼= kw.
(VI) The group G has an inner twin at too many finite places v:
a nontrivial inner kv-twist that is kv-isomorphic to G (Defini-
tion 54).
The precise meaning of “too many” depends in each case on the
Cartan–Killing type of G. The essence of our main results is that in
each Cartan–Killing type except D4, a subset of these properties, each
with appropriate refinements, is necessary and sufficient for congruence
rigidity. We decided to omit the triality type D4 from our discussion,
because already examining h becomes an unwieldy task if Sym ∆∼= S3.
