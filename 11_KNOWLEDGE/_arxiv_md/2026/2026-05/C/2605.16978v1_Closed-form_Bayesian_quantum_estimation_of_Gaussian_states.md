Bayesian quantum estimation provides a robust framework for quantum technologies, especially in scenarios
with limited data and minimal prior information. Yet, its application to continuous-variable Gaussian systems
has remained limited and largely numerical due to the complexity of the underlying parameter integrals. Here,
we introduce a variational framework reducing the optimisation over measurements and estimators to a finite-
dimensional linear problem and admitting closed-form solutions. This is achieved by restricting the analysis
to operators polynomial in the canonical quadratures, leading to solutions with a geometric interpretation as
orthogonal projections of the global optimum. We further derive a necessary and sufficient condition for global
optimality. Through single-shot examples, we show that the framework yields experimentally feasible strategies
based on Gaussian operations and quadrature measurements that are either optimal or near-optimal, and that
replacing the induced estimator with the posterior mean further improves performance towards the global op-
timum.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Quantum estimation theory (QET) underpins quantum met-
rology [1–4], where resources such as squeezing and entan-
glement enable precision beyond classical limits [5, 6]. Tradi-
tionally, QET has been formulated within a frequentist frame-
work [7–9], where performance is characterised via the Fisher
information and the Cramér–Rao bound. Such approaches of-
ten become effective only once the parameter has been suffi-
ciently localised, either through prior information or prelim-
inary measurements, thereby restricting their applicability in
genuinely global or finite-sample regimes [10–17].
The Bayesian formulation provides an alternative in which
prior information is incorporated from the outset and perform-
ance is defined globally through a mean loss [18]. Early works
by Personick showed that optimal measurements can, for the
mean square loss (MSL), be constructively derived from an
operator equation of Lyapunov type [19, 20], and more recent
developments have exploited this perspective in finite-sample
[11] or single-shot [21–24] settings, multiparameter schemes
[2, 12, 25–27], and symmetry-informed metrologies [28–30].
Despite this progress, Bayesian QET can be more demand-
ing computationally than its local counterpart, in part because
optimisation depends on integrated, prior-weighted state op-
erators rather than on local derivatives.
This difficulty is further exacerbated in continuous-variable
(CV) systems, which are central to quantum technologies [31–
35], due to their infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces. In this
context, Gaussian states are widely used due to their sim-
plicity and experimental accessibility, being fully character-
ised by first and second moments and generated by quadratic
Hamiltonians and linear operations. They underpin a range of
quantum platforms, including implementations in gravitation-
al-wave detectors [35–37]. But while Gaussian metrology has
been extensively developed within local QET [38–49], tech-
niques for its development within the Bayesian framework re-
main fragmented and largely unexplored.

Existing Bayesian approaches to Gaussian metrology can
be broadly grouped into (semi-)analytical solutions for spe-
cific systems [23, 24, 50–54]—which often do not readily ex-
tend to more general families of states or measurements—or
purely numerical approaches [11, 55–59]. This reflects the
aforementioned computational bottleneck. There is therefore
a need for a formulation of Bayesian optimisation in Gaussian
metrology that enables the problem to be treated in a system-
atic and, where possible, analytical manner.
Contributions.—We introduce a variational framework for
Bayesian quantum estimation in CV systems, reducing the
optimisation over measurements and estimators to a finite-
dimensional problem by restricting to operators polynomial
in the canonical quadratures. This approach yields a tract-
able linear system determining the optimal measurement–
estimator pair within the chosen subspace. We derive closed-
form expressions for the associated minimum MSL, establish
a geometric interpretation as an orthogonal projection of the
global optimum, and identify a necessary and sufficient con-
dition for global optimality within the subspace. We further
show that replacing the induced estimator with the posterior
mean (PM) yields a strict improvement whenever the con-
strained operator does not satisfy the original Lyapunov equa-
tion, bringing the performance closer to the global optimum
without altering the measurement. Applying the method to
displacement and squeezing estimation in Gaussian states, we
recover known optimal strategies and obtain near-optimal, ex-
perimentally feasible solutions in more general settings.

III.
CONSTRAINED OPTIMISATION OF THE MSL
Eq. (16) provides a complete solution to the search for op-
timal estimation strategies under the square loss criterion. In
practice, however, S is generically infinite-dimensional in CV
systems and its spectral measurement typically corresponds to
non-Gaussian POVMs that may require joint operators across
several subsystems, rendering their implementation experi-
mentally challenging [71]. Moreover, a numerical computa-
tion of S comes at the cost of increasing the dimension of
a truncated Hilbert space depending on the accuracy needed.
These challenges motivate a different approach.
Rather than optimising directly over POVMs, we now re-
strict M1 to a physically-motivated subspace of finite-degree
polynomials in the quadratures ˆq and ˆp, and minimise the
MSL Eq. (14) within this subspace. As we shall see, this
procedure yields a convex quadratic optimisation problem in
the expansion coefficients, whose solution is given by a linear
system of equations. As in the full SPM construction, the as-
sociated estimation strategy is defined by the spectral decom-
position of the resulting constrained (polynomial) analogue of
the SPM operator.

DISCUSSION
We have developed a general framework in which the op-
timisation of the MSL over measurements and estimators for
a CV system reduces to the solution of a finite linear system
within a chosen operator subspace V = span(B1, . . . , BN).
For Gaussian probe states, the required operator moments can
be computed in closed form. The resulting constrained estim-
ator admits a geometric interpretation as the orthogonal pro-
jection of the global optimum S onto V with respect to the
ρ0-weighted inner product in Eq. (25), while Theorem 1 char-
acterises when this projection is exact. Moreover, replacing
the constrained estimator with the PM as a post-processing
step yields a further reduction in the MSL at no additional ex-
perimental cost.

Since closed-form expressions for the MSL are often diffi-

as a benchmark or subroutine within numerical approaches

Our worked examples show that the resulting schemes are
either exactly optimal or near-optimal. For displacement es-
timation with a Gaussian prior, ρ0 is Gaussian and Theorem 1
implies that homodyne detection is exactly optimal for any
prior width, recovering the result of Ref. [54] by a different
route. In contrast, for a uniform prior the state ρ0 is no longer
Gaussian, so Theorem 1 no longer applies and the linear es-
timator ceases to be optimal. Nevertheless, it remains close
to optimal, while including higher polynomial basis elements
provides a systematic and tractable approximation to the full
Bayesian optimum.
For squeezing estimation of an undisplaced probe, the para-
meter is encoded in the covariance matrix, so that Tr(¯ρˆqϕ) =
0. Consequently, any estimator linear in the homodyne out-
come xϕ does not contribute to the MSL in Eq. (29), making
a quadratic operator the lowest-order nontrivial choice. For
the full quadratic subspace V = span(1, ˆq2, ˆp2), we obtained
closed-form expressions for the projected SPM operator SV
and the corresponding MSL L(SV) [Eqs. (59) and (60)], al-
though the associated PVM involves non-normalisable scat-
tering states and is experimentally challenging to implement.
Restricting instead to the subspace V = span(1, ˆq2
ϕ), corres-
ponding to homodyne detection at angle ϕ with an estimator
quadratic in the outcome, yields closed-form expressions for
both the estimator Eq. (62) and the MSL Eq. (67). While the
full quadratic basis achieves a lower MSL throughout, the ho-
modyne strategy remains directly implementable and provides
a practically accessible near-optimal scheme. In all cases, the
strategies converge rapidly to the global optimum as the prior
narrows, with the PM estimator providing a larger improve-
ment at broader prior widths.
Several limitations are worth noting. First, the linear sys-
tem Gα = b requires the traces Tr(ρ0BiBj) and Tr(¯ρBi)
to be finite. This is satisfied for polynomial bases and Gaus-
sian states with standard priors, but must be checked inde-
pendently in more general settings. Second, the spectral de-
composition of SV defines a physical projective measurement
only when SV is self-adjoint. While this is true for all ex-
amples considered here—including real polynomial bases in
a single quadrature and real quadratic forms in the canonical
quadratures—it is not guaranteed for higher-order bases mix-
ing quadratures and must therefore be verified case by case.
Nevertheless, even when a spectral decomposition is unavail-
able, L(SV) in Eq. (28) remains a valid lower bound on the
MSL achievable by any self-adjoint operator within V, al-
though the bound need not be attainable.
Looking ahead, the framework is not restricted to Gaus-
sian probes: the only requirements are that ρ0 and ¯ρ are well
defined and that the relevant operator moments exist, ensuring
that the Gram matrix G and vector b are finite. The optimisa-
tion therefore applies equally to non-Gaussian CV probes and
finite-dimensional systems; e.g., with Pauli-operator bases re-
