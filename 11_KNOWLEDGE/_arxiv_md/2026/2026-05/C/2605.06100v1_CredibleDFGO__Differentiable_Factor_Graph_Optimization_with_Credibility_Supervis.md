Abstract
Global navigation satellite system (GNSS) positioning is widely used for urban navigation, but the
covariance reported by the GNSS solver is often unreliable in urban canyons. Existing differentiable factor
graph optimization (DFGO) methods already learn measurement weighting through the solver, but they
still use position-only objectives. As a result, the mean estimate may improve while the reported covariance
remains too small, too large, or wrong in shape. In this work, we propose CredibleDFGO (CDFGO), a
differentiable GNSS factor graph framework that makes covariance credibility an explicit training target.
The Weighting Generation Network (WGN) predicts per-satellite reliability weights. The differentiable
Gauss–Newton solver maps these weights to a position estimate and posterior covariance, and proper
scoring rules supervise the East–North predictive distribution end-to-end. We study negative log-likelihood
(NLL), Energy Score (ES), and their combination. Results on three UrbanNav test scenes show consistent
gains in uncertainty credibility. Positioning accuracy also improves on the medium-urban and harsh-urban
scenes, and the mean horizontal error and 95th-percentile error improve on the deep-urban scene. On
the harsh-urban Mong Kok (MK) scene, CDFGO-Combined reduces the mean horizontal error from
13.77 m to 11.68 m, reduces NLL from 40.63 to 6.59, and reduces ES from 12.31 to 9.05. The case studies
link the MK improvement to better axis-wise consistency, more credible local covariance ellipses, and
satellite-level reweighting.
Keywords
differentiable factor graph optimization, GNSS positioning, uncertainty quantification, negative log-

INTRODUCTION
GNSS positioning in urban canyons can return an accurate position, but the reported covariance does not match the true position
error. This mismatch matters because the covariance is not just a diagnostic number. It directly controls downstream decisions.
The covariance controls how much fusion modules trust the GNSS position update and when integrity monitors raise an alert. A
miscalibrated covariance misleads both. Li and colleagues gave this problem a name: credibility (X. R. Li et al., 2012). Credibility
assesses whether the reported uncertainty is statistically consistent with the true position error. Position accuracy alone does not
tell us whether the reported uncertainty was credible. Yan and colleagues recently showed that two proper scoring rules, negative
log-likelihood (NLL) and the energy score (ES), can detect different kinds of covariance miscalibration in state estimators under
noise and model mismatch (Yan, Zhan, et al., 2025). These different kinds of miscalibration correspond to three concrete failure
modes. The reported ellipse may be too small, too large, or pointing in the wrong direction. In horizontal positioning, a tight ellipse
can still fail to cover the true East–North position. Two downstream systems show why this risk is not just theoretical. Joerger
and Pervan showed that sequential advanced receiver autonomous integrity monitoring (ARAIM) can exploit satellite motion
over time only when the uncertainty model stays credible (Joerger & Pervan, 2020). In driverless urban navigation, Nagai and
colleagues found that fault-free integrity requires credible GNSS uncertainty for fusion with other onboard sensors (Nagai et al.,

2024). Therefore, credibility cannot be inferred from the position error. It must be assessed directly from the reported covariance.
This requirement applies to any estimator family — classical weighted least squares, robust filters, or learned factor graphs.
Yet most work in urban GNSS targets position accuracy, not covariance credibility. Most methods work on the measurements
before they reach the solver. Zheng and colleagues predicted satellite visibility from a sky-plot graph using a graph transformer
(Zheng et al., 2024). Building on 3D city models, Wang and colleagues distinguished LOS from NLOS signals using shadow
matching (Wang et al., 2013). Ng and colleagues also extended 3D-mapping-aided GNSS to dual-frequency smartphone
pseudoranges (Ng et al., 2021). Li and colleagues learned NLOS-aware weights from signal features, so contaminated and clean
measurements are weighted differently (L. Li et al., 2023). Mohanty and Gao used graph convolutions to learn smartphone GNSS
corrections from inter-satellite relations (Mohanty & Gao, 2023). Smolyakov and colleagues adapted the stochastic model online
through multipath prediction and detection (Smolyakov et al., 2020). A more direct approach by Chen and colleagues learned
pseudorange corrections that reduce urban bias directly (Chen et al., 2023). Kanhere and colleagues trained a set transformer to
predict position corrections from pseudorange residuals and line-of-sight vectors (Kanhere et al., 2022). Zhang and colleagues
took a different direction by making measurement uncertainty itself the prediction target, using an LSTM to model its time-
varying behavior (Zhang et al., 2021). Medina and colleagues reviewed classical robust GNSS estimators under harsh conditions,
showing that standard least squares becomes unreliable when outliers and heavy tails dominate (Medina et al., 2019). Even a
dedicated urban M-estimator was found to be sensitive to the choice of the error model, and its performance depended on proper
design (Garcia Crespillo et al., 2020). Li and colleagues tuned the Huber M-estimator parameters using the logistic distribution
of GNSS errors, reducing the 2D RMSE and suppressing large error spikes (Z. Li et al., 2026). Yan and colleagues isolated multi-
constellation faults through incremental expansion of a fault-free basic set (Yan, Hu, et al., 2025). All of these methods target the
measurements or the robust estimator before the solver runs. The covariance the solver returns is never their supervision target.
The same gap exists within the factor-graph framework. Several methods address measurement faults and integrity monitoring,
but they still do not supervise the covariance. Wen, Hsu, and colleagues brought factor-graph optimization into robust GNSS
and RTK positioning (Wen & Hsu, 2021). They later applied graduated non-convexity to downweight pseudorange outliers
(Wen et al., 2022), and a related thread from the same group fused GNSS and LiDAR with self-adaptive Gaussian mixtures
(Wen et al., 2020). Yan and colleagues learned subspace-based adaptive GMM error models for fault-aware urban positioning
(Yan, Xia, et al., 2025). Knowles and Gao developed rapid fault detection and exclusion (Knowles & Gao, 2023). For long-term
integrity, Gallon and colleagues hardened orbit and clock error modeling (Gallon et al., 2022). Xia and colleagues came closest
to the covariance credibility question. Their integrity-constrained factor graph uses switch variables to reweight measurements
and derives a protection level from the modified weighting matrix, though their method does not involve learning (Xia et al.,
2024). Their work shows that the factor-graph framework can engage with covariance, but it needs a training signal that targets it.
Without such a signal, even general-purpose uncertainty quantification tools face the same limitation. Gal and Ghahramani’s
Monte Carlo dropout approximates Bayesian uncertainty by sampling subnetworks, but the factor-graph solver already provides a
posterior covariance from the inverse of the converged Hessian (Gal & Ghahramani, 2016). Levi and colleagues showed how to
calibrate regression uncertainty after training, but post-hoc calibration does not change which satellites receive high weight
during the forward pass (Levi et al., 2022). None of these approaches train the covariance to match the true position error.
A different route is needed to supervise the covariance. In a differentiable factor graph, the solver is unrolled so that gradients flow
from the loss back through the optimization to the factor weights. Because the posterior covariance is produced by the Hessian of
the converged solution, a credibility loss can directly penalize a miscalibrated covariance through the same backward pass.
This idea builds on decades of work on factor graphs for robot perception. Dellaert and Kaess formulated factor graphs and
smoothing as a sparse probabilistic framework that incorporates measurements, priors, and constraints inside one optimization
problem (Dellaert & Kaess, 2017). The iSAM2 and Bayes tree formulations from Kaess and colleagues made incremental
smoothing practical at navigation scale (Kaess et al., 2012), and Taylor and Gross recently framed the whole family as a navigation
generalization of Kalman filtering for systems that mix asynchronous sensors (Taylor & Gross, 2024). Several groups have also
tried to embed learning inside GNSS estimators. Mohanty and Gao connected a graph neural network to a backpropagation
Kalman filter so that the learned corrections directly affect the state estimate (Mohanty & Gao, 2024). Hu and colleagues
coupled deep bias and weight prediction with RTKLIB-style positioning (Hu et al., 2025). In these designs, the learning signal
reaches the estimator output and gradients flow through the estimator. However, the training loss still targets only the position
estimate. Mohanty and Gao note explicitly that they leave covariance learning for future work. These designs make the estimator
differentiable but do not unroll the factor graph optimization itself. Yi and colleagues addressed this by unrolling the optimization
steps and backpropagating through them (Yi et al., 2021), establishing DFGO as a training paradigm. The idea of differentiating
through a solved optimization problem is not limited to navigation. Amos and Kolter treated optimization as a neural-network layer
in OptNet and derived exact gradients through solved programs (Amos & Kolter, 2017). Xu and colleagues’ DFGO-ICov brought

this idea to smartphone GNSS, with a network predicting adaptive covariance scales for pseudorange factors and a differentiable
solver training these scales against ground-truth position (Xu et al., 2023). Their follow-up AutoW replaced ground-truth labels
with self-supervised priors from clustering and zero-velocity constraints, reporting gains over classical weighting schemes (Xu &
Hsu, 2024). But the training loss in these methods still acts on the position estimate, not the posterior covariance. A position-only
loss cannot distinguish a covariance that is tight and correct from one that is tight but misoriented, because both can yield the
same position estimate.
A loss that only sees the position error cannot judge whether the covariance is right. To evaluate both the position estimate
and the posterior covariance, the loss must act on the full predictive distribution that the solver outputs. Proper scoring rules
do exactly this. A strictly proper scoring rule attains its lowest expected value only when the predicted distribution matches
the true distribution. This means a covariance that looks precise but is poorly calibrated is penalized, not rewarded. For the
two-dimensional East–North prediction, scoring each axis independently fails to capture the correlation between the two axes
(Gneiting & Raftery, 2007; Gneiting et al., 2008; Scheuerer & Hamill, 2015). Yan and colleagues already used these scoring
rules as credibility metrics for state estimators (Yan, Zhan, et al., 2025). CDFGO uses the same rules and turns them into
training losses inside a differentiable solver. For the East–North Gaussian predictor used in GNSS, NLL penalizes a position
estimate that is far from the true position and a covariance ellipse that is too small, too large, or pointing in the wrong direction.
The energy score works differently: it measures the expected distance from predictive samples to the true position, minus half
the expected distance between pairs of predictive samples. Because the energy score does not assume that the errors follow a
Gaussian distribution, it remains informative when the urban pseudorange errors are heavy-tailed. Under a credible Gaussian
predictor, about 68% of axis-wise errors should lie within the reported 1-σ bound and about 99.7% within the 3-σ bound.
CredibleDFGO (CDFGO) implements this credibility supervision end-to-end. A Weighting Generation Network (WGN) maps
per-satellite features to reliability weights, which a differentiable Gauss–Newton solver (Pineda et al., 2022) uses as per-factor
information weights, and the gradients flow directly through the solver back to the WGN parameters (Amos & Kolter, 2017). We
study three variants. CDFGO-NLL optimizes the Gaussian NLL of the East–North posterior, CDFGO-ES optimizes the energy
score, and CDFGO-Combined trains against both. We evaluate CDFGO on three UrbanNav test scenes covering medium-urban,
deep-urban, and harsh-urban conditions. Credibility-driven weighting can suppress contaminated satellites that still contribute to
the geometry, so this sometimes results in weaker geometry that yields a covariance that better matches the true position error.
This geometry-credibility tradeoff is a core feature of the method, not a side effect.
The contributions of this paper are threefold:
• We show that proper scoring rules, when used as end-to-end training losses for a differentiable GNSS factor graph, make
the posterior covariance a supervised output rather than an unsupervised byproduct.
• We develop the CredibleDFGO framework and demonstrate on three UrbanNav scenes that credibility-driven training
improves uncertainty credibility while preserving or improving horizontal accuracy.
• We identify a geometry-credibility tradeoff inherent in credibility-driven weighting. A satellite-level analysis on the Mong
Kok scene shows how this tradeoff reshapes the posterior covariance and why it still improves both accuracy and credibility.

2.2
CDFGO Pipeline Overview
Figure 1 summarizes the CDFGO pipeline: the WGN, the differentiable Gauss–Newton solver, and credibility supervision.
During training, the WGN converts raw GNSS features into reliability weights. These weights define the pseudorange-factor
information used by the differentiable Gauss–Newton solver, which returns the state estimate and posterior covariance. Credibility
losses are then computed from the East–North position and covariance returned by the solver, and the loss gradients are
back-propagated through the solver to the WGN parameters ˜ϕ.
During inference, only the trained WGN and the solver are used. The runtime solver therefore stays the same as in a standard
FGO backend; only the factor weights are learned from data.

The WGN uses the four per-satellite features defined in Section 2.3: elevation angle, observed pseudorange, carrier-to-noise ratio,

inputs are normalized with training-set statistics only, and variable satellite counts are handled by padding and masking. The

Theseus (Pineda et al., 2022), and all reported results use the same pseudorange-only solver setting with a fixed five-epoch sliding
time window.
3.2
Metrics, Baselines, and Variants
In this experiment, point accuracy is evaluated with three horizontal-error metrics in meters: the mean horizontal error, the
median (50th percentile), and the 95th percentile. The mean reflects the overall operating level, the median the typical-case
performance, and the 95th percentile the tail behavior.
Uncertainty credibility is reported by two proper scoring rules, namely NLL and ES, both computed from the predicted EN
Gaussian distribution N(ˆpen, ˆΣen). NLL evaluates whether the reported covariance is statistically consistent with the realized
error under the Gaussian predictive model (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). ES evaluates distribution-level mismatch through sample
distances (Gneiting et al., 2008; Scheuerer & Hamill, 2015). Table 2 summarizes these five metrics and how each is interpreted in
the present study.

4
DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
The experiment results in Section 3 indicate that credibility training mainly changes how the solver’s covariance can be used
downstream. In the present setting, covariance is no longer a passive byproduct of the solver; it becomes an explicit training target.
This matters because downstream fusion and integrity modules act on covariance, not on horizontal error alone. In multi-sensor
fusion, covariance controls the update gain and therefore how strongly GNSS pulls the fused estimate (Wen et al., 2021). In
integrity-oriented processing, uncertainty quality is itself part of the decision logic (Xia et al., 2024). A GNSS backend that
reports more credible covariance is therefore a stronger candidate for downstream fusion and integrity integration, even though
those downstream systems are not evaluated in this work.
The credibility-oriented objectives also provide a direct training-time diagnostic. Because the uncertainty behavior is tied to an
explicit proper score, a model update can be judged not only by aggregate positioning error but also by whether the reported
covariance remains statistically consistent with realized errors, which is exactly the role of proper scoring rules such as NLL
(Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). The exceedance and coverage diagnostics in Section 3.4 serve the same purpose for credibility
checking (X. R. Li et al., 2012). They do not provide formal certification, but they do reveal whether the uncertainty behavior is
moving toward statistical consistency. This framing is closer to integrity-oriented evaluation than to optimizing geometry-oriented
losses alone.
At the same time, this study has clear limits. The loss balancing still uses fixed coefficients (α, β), although the calibration-
sharpness trade-off can vary with scene difficulty and mission profile. The posterior covariance is still extracted from a local
Laplace approximation, which is efficient but may be insufficient when the posterior is strongly multi-modal. These two points
suggest natural extensions toward adaptive loss balancing and richer uncertainty families.
The experimental scope is also deliberately narrow. The reported results use pseudorange-only factors. This isolates the mechanism
of credibility training, but it does not show how the same credibility-driven training objective behaves after coupling with
Doppler, carrier phase, or inertial constraints. Extending the framework to tightly coupled GNSS/INS or GNSS/vision graphs is a
necessary next step for studying downstream fusion and integrity behavior (Wen et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2024). The mechanism
interpretation in Section 3.6 is also built around a representative MK epoch. This selected epoch is useful for explanation, but it
does not establish the same causal pattern across all epochs and scenes. Larger-scale satellite-level statistics are still needed, and
developing such statistics is a clear direction for future work.

QIAN
The present evaluation is further limited by geography, hardware, and reference quality. UrbanNav covers several urban scenes
(Hsu et al., 2023), but its reference trajectories are tied to a single hardware setup and to high-grade RTK/INS post-processing
that may still retain some uncertainty under severe blockage. Because the credibility losses depend on reference quality, label
errors can transfer into the learned weighting behavior. End-to-end differentiable optimization with Monte Carlo ES estimation
is also more expensive than hand-crafted weighting rules, and gradient stability still depends on solver convergence. Broader
cross-domain experiments, more diverse hardware platforms, and more efficient training strategies are therefore needed before
deployment-oriented use can be claimed.
5
CONCLUSION
In this work, we introduced CDFGO, a differentiable GNSS factor graph trained with proper-scoring-rule losses on its East–North
predictive distribution. Experiments on the UrbanNav dataset across medium, deep, and harsh urban scenes confirmed that
credibility supervision produces a more reliable posterior covariance and improves horizontal accuracy. In contrast, the DFGO
(MAE) baseline with a position-only loss returns miscalibrated covariance even when its position estimate is accurate. This work
offers GNSS practitioners a way to train covariance as a primary estimator output, enabling direct use in downstream fusion and
integrity monitoring.
