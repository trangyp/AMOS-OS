STEPHEN STERN, AND RYAN WATSON
Abstract. Let R be a local or positively graded ring with a regular presentation R ∼
= Q/I where
I is a monomial ideal generated by n elements on a regular sequence.
In [BGP25] the authors
classify the cohomological support varieties VR(R) for n ⩽5. In this paper we extend their results
to classify the varieties that can occur as VR(R) for n = 6. Moreover, we provide two families of
rings, one realizing cohomological support varieties of unbounded codimension, the other realizing
an unbounded number of components. Finally, we answer a question of [Gin26] about the varieties
that occur as VR(R) where I is given by the edge ideal of a cycle.
Introduction
Inspired by Quillen’s geometric approach to modular representation theory [Qui71], Avramov im-
ported the theory of support varieties to commutative algebra to study local complete intersection
rings [Avr89]. This has now expanded to include all noetherian local rings through the work of various
authors [Jor02, BW15, Pol19, Pol21]. Most notably for this paper, Pollitz developed the theory of
cohomological support varieties over Koszul complexes which generalizes the theory of Avramov.
Suppose that R is a local or positively graded ring. Then R is said to admit a minimal regular
presentation, if one of the following holds:
• bR = Q/I with (Q, m, k) a regular local ring and I ⊂m2 in the local case, or
• R = Q/I where Q is a positively graded polynomial algebra over a field k and I is an ideal
generated by homogeneous forms of degree at least 2 in the graded case.
Assume that R has a minimal regular presentation given by Q/I, where I is minimally generated by
n elements. Let E be the Koszul complex on a minimal generating set of I. The ring of cohomological
operators S = k[χ1, . . . , χn] is a polynomial ring over k where each χi is a variable of cohomological
degree 2. For any finitely generated R-module M, the ring S acts on ExtE(M, k), making ExtE(M, k)
into a graded S-module. There are many different ways to define this action, but one is explained
as follows. The ring S is a graded k-subalgebra of ExtE(k, k), and the left action of ExtE(k, k) on
ExtE(M, k) defines an action of S on ExtE(M, k) (see [AB00a, Section 2]). The specific details of
how S acts on this ExtE(M, N) are not needed for this paper, so we refer the interested reader to
[Pol19, 3.2.6]. Due to our conditions on Q, it turns out that ExtE(M, k) is a finitely generated graded
S module [Pol19, 3.2.5].
The cohomological support variety of a finitely generated R module M, is defined to be
VR(M) := SuppS (ExtE(M, k)) .
As ExtE(M, k) is a finitely generated graded S module, we can and will view VR(M) as a conical
affine variety (i.e. an affine cone), living in An
k which we identify with mSpec(S). Geometric properties
of these varieties encode homological information of both the ring and the module. For example, they
see some of the structure of the lattice of thick subcategories in the derived category of R [Ste14, Pol19].
When the ring is a complete intersection, the theory of support varieties is generally well-understood
and has been studied extensively [Avr89, AB00b, Ber07, AI07, DS17]. However, moving away from
the complete intersection case, their behavior becomes much more mysterious. In [Pol21], the author
asks the following question known as the realization problem for cohomological support varieties:

Question 1. Given a noetherian local ring R, what conical affine varieties in An

the support variety of some finitely generated R-module?
This problem is solved when the ring is a complete intersection [Ber07, AI07], with the answer being
that every conical affine variety is realizable. However, when the ring is not a complete intersection,
much less is known. There are bounds on the dimensions of the varieties that can occur [BGP24,
BGP25], and certain varieties can be guaranteed to be realizable over certain rings in [Pol21, BGP22,
Wat25, Gin26]. Beyond these results, little is known in general. In fact, it is unknown what varieties
can arise as VR(R).
Some results in this direction include the following.

1. Preliminaries
Throughout this paper, we assume that R is either a local ring or a positively graded ring with
residue field k, admitting a minimal regular presentation Q/I. Moreover, we assume the ideal I is
minimally generated by the ordered list of monomials f = f1, . . . , fn on a regular sequence x1, . . . , xr
of Q. That is, each fi is a product of powers of the xj. Our object of interest is the support variety
Vf := VR(R). In this setting, [BGP25] furnishes a combinatorial construction of Vf in terms of two
graphs that are determined by f, the GCD graph Gf and the Taylor graph Tf. Moreover, for a fixed
number of generators, only finitely many Gf and Tf can be realized, hence only finitely many support
varieties can occur.
Definition 1.1. The GCD graph of f is the simple graph Gf with vertex set [n] = {f1, . . . , fn} such
that there is an edge {fi, fj} if and only if gcd(fi, fj) is not a unit. For a set of vertices σ ⊆[n] we
denote the neighborhood of σ as Nf(σ) = {fi : {fi, fj} ∈Gf, j ∈σ} .
Example 1.2. Consider two monomial ideals generated by
f1 = (bce, bd, cd, ae) and f2 = (de, ae, be, cd).
In both cases, Gf is a triangle on {f1, f2, f3} along with the edge {f1, f4}. Hence, Nf(f1) = {f2, f3, f4}.
This also illustrates that a fixed Gf can have multiple monomial ideals attaining it.
A priori, for a given Gf there are infinitely many monomial ideals to consider; our first step is to
reduce to considering a finite number of ideals. The first part of this reduction is given by [BGP25,
Remark 6.18], which argues that Gf and Tf (and hence Vf) are preserved by polarization. Thus we
always assume that f is a list of square-free monomials. Notably Vf depends only on I and hence is
not sensitive to the choice of the Q-regular sequence over which our monomials are defined. Thus, the
second step of our reduction is to show that, up to a choice of regular sequence, there are only finitely
many square-free monomial ideals with n generators. Moving forward, we adopt the convention that f
is an ordered list of n square-free monomials, none of which divide another. Hence [n] := {f1, . . . , fn}
is the set of minimal generators of a square-free monomial ideal.
Definition 1.3. Given a fixed list of square-free monomials f, for a regular element x, the type τ(x)
is the subset of minimal generators that x divides. That is, we identify τ(x) = {fi : x | fi} ⊆[n].
The image of this function appears as the construction of certain hypergraphs in [LM12, KTY09]
where it is used to compute the arithmetic rank and regularity of square-free monomial ideals. Towards
the second step of our reduction, we will consider ideals for which τ is injective. Suppose for some i ̸= j
that τ(xi) = τ(xj), then f can be realized on a regular sequence of length r −1 by removing both xi
and xj along with adding in their product xixj. Repeating until τ is injective, we have that r ≤2n as
well as a more insightful naming of our regular sequence: xσ = τ −1(σ). Note that, when appropriate,
we will abbreviate a set {fi1, . . . , fis} ⊆[n] with the concatenation i1 · · · is, for example x123 refers to
x{f1,f2,f3}. Note further that f may be determined by the image of τ by setting fi = Q

Let us now introduce some combinatorial objects that will be helpful to stratify the square-free
monomial ideals we will consider.
Definition 1.8. An abstract simplicial complex with vertex set [n] is a nonempty subset K ⊆2[n]
with the property that σ ∈K and θ ⊂σ implies θ ∈K. Elements of K are called faces and maximal
faces are called facets. The dimension of a nonempty face is one less than its cardinality. A simple
graph is a simplicial complex with facets of dimension at most 1, where the 0-dimensional faces are
called vertices and the 1-dimensional faces are called edges. The 1-skeleton of K is the simple graph
given by removing the faces of K with dimension more than 1. The GCD graph Gf can be identified
with 1-skeleton of the smallest simplicial complex containing the labels of the variables Xf.
Definition 1.9. A complete graph is a simple graph with every possible edge. If G is a simple graph
and W is a subset of its vertices, then the induced subgraph of G on W is the graph with vertex set
W and edges of G that use vertices in W. We call an induced, complete subgraph a clique.
Remark 1.10. Note that every induced subgraph of a clique is also a clique. Hence, the set of all
cliques of a simple graph forms a simplicial complex, which we call the clique complex. We denote the
clique complex of Gf by Kf. Note that if i, j ∈σ, then the presence of xσ witnesses the edge {i, j}
in Gf. In particular, Gf is the 1-skeleton of Kf. Moreover, we always have that the labels of variables
in Xf must be elements of Kf. It follows that the f satisfying G = Gf arise from selecting certain
subsets of 2K if K is the clique complex of G.

In the case that the support variety is V (χ1χ5) for 5 generated monomial ideals, the authors of
[BGP25] wrote out and argued about certain features of the images and kernels of two 16×16 matrices.
This exact strategy is not well suited to scale; in the case of 6 generators there would be more than just

5. Support Varieties for Cyclic GCD Graphs
The main objects of study in this section are the edge ideals of cycles and their cohomological
support varieties. Recall that [BGP25] and [Gin26] give examples of these ideals achieving support
varieties that are non-linear hypersurfaces. In fact, these are the only known support varieties of rings
defined by monomial ideals that are nonlinear hypersurfaces. In general, the edge ideal construction
associates to any simple graph a square-free monomial ideal with degree 2 generators. In particular–
and in contrast to GCD graphs–the vertices of the simple graph correspond to variables of a polynomial
ring and the generators of the edge ideal are the products of each of the variables corresponding to
edges. For n ≥3 let Cn denote the n-cycle, the simple graph with n vertices and n edges given by
a regular n-gon. These graphs have the remarkable property that they are the GCD graphs of their
own edge ideals. In particular, ideals with GCD graph Cn forms a family of ideals which contains the
edge ideal of Cn.
