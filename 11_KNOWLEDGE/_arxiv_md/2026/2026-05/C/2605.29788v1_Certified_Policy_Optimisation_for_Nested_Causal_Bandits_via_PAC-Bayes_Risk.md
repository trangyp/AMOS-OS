Abstract
Critical sequential decisions are rarely single-timescale: a strategic decision
causally shapes the context in which every subsequent tactical choice is made;
standard bandit and reinforcement-learning theory does not capture this causal
coupling between timescales. We formalise the problem class as Nested Contextual
Causal Bandits (NCCBs), a hierarchical SCM where each level’s action sets the
next level’s context distribution, and propose Nested Causal Thompson Sampling
(NCTS), which draws one mechanism-factorised belief per episode and acts recur-
sively under it. Our main theoretical result is a causal PAC-Bayesian excess-risk
bound that certifies any candidate deployment policy from historic data alone,
off-policy and anytime, answering the deployment question: can we trust this agent
here, and at what risk? Experiments on a hierarchical SCM show that, against
a matched RFF-GP joint regression on the same function class, the factorised
SCM-mechanism posterior transfers significantly better zero-shot under exogenous
distribution shifts, the recursive meta-to-inner commit significantly dominates the
joint-commit alternative in distribution, and the certificate significantly contracts
as offline data accumulates. Combining these results, we establish progressive
certified handover, a safe-deployment method: each timescale flips from a legacy
controller to NCTS when gains can be certified, independently of the others.1

Introduction
Critical sequential decision-making, treatment titration in healthcare, input allocation in agronomy,
batch configuration in process engineering, is still entrusted to humans because the learned-policy
guarantees on offer answer the wrong question. Frequentist regret bounds hold worst-case over all
environments; Bayesian regret bounds hold on average over a prior; neither speaks to this realised
environment, with this confounding, under this legacy controller. These domains are also naturally
hierarchical: a strategic decision generates the context for a tactical one, which generates the context
for an operational one. Progressive transfer of control must therefore proceed level by level, gated at
each level by an empirical bound meeting a practitioner-specified risk threshold. What is needed is a
(1−δ)-bound that holds for the realised environment, simultaneously over candidate policies, across
both the offline warm-start phase and the online deployment phase, and gates handover timescale by
timescale; no existing framework provides guarantees that are simultaneously timescale-resolved,
off-policy valid, and context-specific.

We introduce the Nested Contextual Causal Bandit (NCCB), an L-level hierarchical SCM in which
each level’s decision causally generates the context for the level below; the causal graph is given,
only the mechanism parameters are unknown. For this class we propose Nested Causal Thompson
Sampling (NCTS), which draws one belief per episode and acts on it at every nested level, and
AEGIS (Anytime Episodic Gain-Certified Inference Schedule), a host-agnostic deployment-time
wrapper that runs progressive per-level handover off any host with a Bayesian posterior and a
reward predictor. The analysis covers both algorithms: a single causal PAC-Bayesian excess-risk
bound (Theorem 1) certifies the realised environment, off-policy and anytime, with a regulariser that
decomposes along the causal mechanisms. Section 2 positions NCCB / NCTS against the closest
causal-bandit, hierarchical-bandit, and PAC-Bayesian off-policy lines.

excess-risk bound that, for any intervention scope I ⊆[L], factorises both the KL regulariser (over

Preliminaries
Causal models and interventions. A structural causal model (SCM) over endogenous variables
X specifies, for each X ∈X, a deterministic equation X = fX(Pa(X), ϵX; θ∗
X) with parent set
Pa(X) ⊆X and exogenous noise ϵX; the parent sets induce a DAG G, treated as known. Pearl’s hard
intervention do(X = x) replaces fX by X := x; a soft intervention do(X := h) generalises this
by replacing fX with a new mechanism h whose parents are non-descendants of X in G. A policy
intervention on an action A is the soft intervention do(A := π(· | X)) for an observable context
X ⊆Pa′(A) excluding descendants of A [20].
Contextual bandits. A contextual bandit is an SCM over context C, action A, reward R with
R = fR(C, A, ϵR; θ∗
R). A policy π : X →∆(A) induces a policy intervention do(A := π(· | C)),
with value V (π) := EC∼PC, A∼π(·|C)[R]. In the off-policy setting, data are collected under a
behaviour policy µ ̸= π, and V (π) is inferred via importance weighting wt = π(at | ct)/µ(at | ct)
or related estimators.
Thompson sampling. Thompson sampling maintains a posterior pk(θ) over the unknown parameters:
at episode k, draw one belief θ(k) ∼pk−1, act greedily under it, observe Dk, and Bayes-update
to pk. Drawing (rather than maximising) provides exploration with well-understood Bayes-regret
guarantees [24, 18].
PAC-Bayes for dependent data. Our off-policy analysis builds on the online PAC-Bayes framework
of Haddouche and Guedj [7]: for losses adapted to a filtration (Ft)t≥0 and an online predictive se-
quence of priors (Pt) (each Pt being Ft−1-measurable, P1 data-free), a change-of-measure argument
with an exponential supermartingale and Ville’s inequality yields concentration that holds anytime
over T and uniformly over posterior sequences (Qt), generalising the i.i.d. machinery of Seldin et al.
[25]. Theorem 1 uses a sharper specialisation [8, Thm. 3.2] with logarithmic rather than quadratic
dependence on the temperature λ; see Appendix C.
Notation. A complete summary of symbols used throughout the paper is provided in Appendix A.

that V (π; θ∗) remains identifiable. Outer levels carry at most one learnable mechanism each, so
within-level sharing is moot there. Cross-level confounding (a single latent acting on variables at
multiple levels) lies outside this model class; handling it would require non-backdoor identification
(e.g. instrumental or proximal variables), which we leave to future work.

I ⊆{1, . . . , L} (Definition 2) together enable a PAC-Bayesian excess-risk bound for arbitrary on-

7.1
Setup
All benchmarks instantiate a single parametric SCM (SCMunified) with meta-action M outer, inner-

7.2
Constructive Ablations
The primary hypothesis motivating NCTS is that constructing hierarchical bandits exhibits signifi-
cantly more rewarding behaviour than flat counterparts, for three reasons. First, replacing the flat
reward function with a factorised SCM-mechanism posterior endows the agent with a model of
why arms are rewarding, not just which arms are; this is the dominant axis of NCTS’s reward gain
(sec. 7.2.1). Second, given the SCM model, the recursive meta →inner structure of Algorithm 1,
which samples M once per meta-step then re-selects A per realised inner context C, strictly domi-
nates the natural alternative of committing (M, A) jointly at meta-step start (sec. 7.2.2). Third, we
can bound each level’s policy in isolation, then run the progressive certified handover workflow
of Section 6.1 where control transfers per level between policies according to a risk-adjusted gate
(sec. 7.2.3). The three benchmarks below address these claims in that order.
7.2.1
SCM factorisation gains significantly under exogenous shift with frozen function class.
To isolate the contribution of mechanism factorisation under distribution shift, we hold the function
class fixed across two bandits and vary only the reward model. flat_ts fits a single RFF-GP
regression on the joint reward T, M, C, A→R; flat_cts fits an RFF-GP posterior factorised over
the SCM mechanisms fC, fY , fR. Both are trained on the AMM-nonlinear source (Ksrc=100),
frozen, and replanned zero-shot onto three exogenously shifted targets (Ktgt=25). The factorised
bandit retains a substantial advantage on the two large shifts: under Shift-T (NT mean 0 →2) it
attains 113.6±4.9 versus 56.1±10.1 (∆= +57.5), and under Shift-NQ (σQ : 3 →6) it attains
194.7±4.9 versus 146.1±6.6 (∆=+48.6); both contrasts pass an aggregate Welch test at p<10−3.

On the milder Shift-U (λU :0.5→0.9), where U enters only via fR’s noise channel, the gain narrows
to ∆=+1.2 (116.1±5.0 vs. 115.0±4.3): at K=2000 the joint regression has accumulated enough
data to compensate on the mild shift, isolating the SCM factorisation’s benefit to genuinely large
exogenous shifts. The joint regression collapses by approximately 50 reward whenever the shift
exits the source support, whereas the factorised bandit holds: under the ICM principle, fC, fY , fR
are invariant and only the exogenous distribution moves. This furnishes the empirical sufficiency
complement to Richens and Everitt [22]’s necessity result, any distributionally robust agent must
internalise the causal structure , by demonstrating that being given the structure suffices for robustness
on these three shifts (Fig. 2a).
7.2.2
Recursive nested commit significantly dominates joint commit.
We next isolate the contribution of the recursive meta-to-inner commit by adding two further bandits
to those of sec. 7.2.1. joint_cts reuses the SCM posterior of flat_cts but commits A jointly with
M at meta-step start, conditioning on a representative inner context bC = E[C | T, M] and reusing
A for all I=20 inner steps; nested_cts is full NCTS (Algorithm 1), which re-samples A at every
realised C. Per-bandit reward across K=2000 episodes (RFF-GP throughout) is 101.5±3.1 for
flat_ts, 108.8±1.9 for flat_cts, 69.0±5.8 for joint_cts, and 113.6±1.9 for nested_cts,
decomposing cleanly into three contributions: causal factorisation alone contributes +7.3 reward
(p<10−4); committing (M, A) jointly then forfeits −39.8 relative to flat_cts (t=−20.7) be-
cause A can no longer adapt to the realised Ct; recursive nesting recovers and extends past this
baseline by +44.6 (p<10−10), for a total NCTS gain of +12.1 over the matched-class flat_ts
baseline (p<10−7). The two NCCB-specific contributions are orthogonal: nesting dominates in dis-
tribution, while factorisation dominates under shift (sec. 7.2.1). This attribution hinges on basis-class
control: against an unmatched NIG-linear baseline, the same flat_cts −flat_ts contrast inflates
by approximately 105 reward of pure function-class misspecification (Fig. 2b).

Conclusion
We introduced the NCCB framework, an L-level hierarchical SCM for progressive certified handover
across decision timescales, and proposed NCTS as a first concrete algorithm (recursive Thompson
sampling, one belief per episode, known graph). Theorem 1 is a causal PAC-Bayes excess-risk
bound at rate O(1/
√
K) in episode count, with a KL regulariser that decomposes along the causal
mechanisms; it is off-policy valid for linear and non-linear ANMs and certifies any intervention scope
I ⊆[L], exposing the safest deployment scope as the certificate-maximising one (Section 6.1). Two
structural assumptions stand out. The bound’s i.i.d.-inner-steps requirement (Assumption 2) means
that lifting the within-episode dynamics to an MDP or POMDP would introduce an extra H1/2 factor
through the value-function dependence on transitions; Girard et al. [5]’s self-normalised maximal
inequality applied within each episode is the natural avenue for that relaxation. The mechanism class
9


## Page 10


is also restricted to additive-noise models (linear with NIG, nonlinear with online Gibbs), with the
non-additive case exercised only as a misspecification stress test absorbed into the KL term rather
than modelled directly. NCTS is a baseline; the NCCB class admits a wider design space along
two axes. First, sharper bounds: PRISM’s multiplicative overlap penalty BI is sidestepped level by
level by the progressive workflow of Section 6.1 but remains loose under one-shot full deployment,
and SNIS [19], PAC-Bayes-Bernstein refinements on the per-mechanism variance structure, and
self-normalised maximal inequalities [5] are promising tighteners. Second, alternative NC-X hosts
indexed by the per-level rule, including NC-UCB [14], NC-IDS [23], NC-PB (direct PAC-Bayes
minimisation), NC-DOpt [4], and pessimistic warm-starts [2], all wrapped by AEGIS (Algorithm 2)
via its host-agnostic interface. Further open problems are collected in App. J.
