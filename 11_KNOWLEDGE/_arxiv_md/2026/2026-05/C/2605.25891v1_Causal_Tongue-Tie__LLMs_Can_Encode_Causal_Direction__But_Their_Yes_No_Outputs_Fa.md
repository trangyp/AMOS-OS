Causal Tongue-Tie: LLMs Can Encode Causal Direction, But Their Yes/No
Outputs Fail to Express

Abstract
We find a mismatch between what large lan-
guage models encode about a causal question
and what they answer. On anti-commonsense
CLADDER (Jin et al., 2023) items, a fixed linear
probe recovers the evidence-supported answer
from the model’s hidden state (accuracy ≈0.97),
while the spoken Yes/No reverts to the common-
sense one (accuracy ≈0.5). We call this ∆≈+0.5
gap Causal Tongue-Tie: a wrong Yes/No decom-
poses into two separable failure modes—no inter-
nal signal vs. a signal the verbal interface cannot
say. The implication cuts both ways for output-
only causal benchmarks: a benchmark “correct”
need not mean the model has understood, and
a benchmark “wrong” need not mean it cannot.
Sweeping claims about whether LLMs can do
causal reasoning, drawn from a single accuracy
number, deserve a second look.
1
Introduction
We find that on causal Yes/No questions, large
language models (LLMs) can encode an evidence-
consistent causal direction in their hidden states that
the ordinary Yes/No output does not say—a mis-
match we call Causal Tongue-Tie (Section 3 for-
malises “encode”).
Background.
Whether LLMs reason or merely
produce fluent causal language is contested (Ben-
der et al., 2021; Zeˇcevi´c et al., 2023). Benchmarks
like CLADDER (Jin et al., 2023) and CounterBench
(Chen et al., 2025) score models by Yes/No accuracy
on causal queries; probe studies report mixed evi-
dence about the hidden state (Chi et al., 2024; Geiger
et al., 2025).
*Corresponding author: xpzhang@ieee.org
Motivation.
On a CLADDER anti-commonsense
item stipulating “smoking prevents lung cancer”,
Qwen2.5-7B answers “No” but a linear probe on the
same hidden state returns “Yes”. The internal state
tracks the stipulation; the spoken answer reverts to
prior. This resonates with the Causal Parrots concern
(Zeˇcevi´c et al., 2023): fluent causal language need not
mean causal reasoning. But a wrong Yes/No alone
cannot tell us if the model lacks causal knowledge or
has it but cannot say it. Our contribution is Causal
Tongue-Tie: the empirical finding that hidden states
encode the evidence-aligned causal answer while the
spoken Yes/No reverts to commonsense (on anti-CS
CLADDER: Accprobe ≈0.97 vs. Accout ≈0.50). In
a 2×2 view of “wrong on a causal benchmark” by
internal signal × external Yes/No (Figure 1), this
dissociation occupies the off-diagonal cell (b) that
has not been systematically studied. Code, prompts,
hidden-state artifacts, and per-item probe logits and
Yes/No probabilities accompany the public release.

Related Work
Our finding sits at the intersection of two empiri-
cal lines: studies that compare hidden-state content
with the model’s text output, and the broader concern
that fluent causal language need not reflect causal
reasoning.
Hidden state vs. output.
Several studies report that
activations can contain information the textual an-
swer does not faithfully express: Contrast-Consistent
Search (Burns et al., 2023), inference-time interven-
tion and truth-geometry directions (Li et al., 2023;
Marks and Tegmark, 2023), and self-knowledge work
(Kadavath et al., 2022). The same probe–output gap
appears in clinical triage and hallucination detection
(Basu et al., 2026; CH-Wang et al., 2024), and chain-

Causal Parrots and causal benchmarks.

3
The Finding
The central finding is that a wrong Yes/No on a causal
benchmark splits into four states (Figure 1) by inter-
nal signal vs. external answer. Among these, the
cell we call Causal Tongue-Tie—(b), probe finds the
evidence-aligned answer, but the spoken Yes/No re-
verts to commonsense—is the failure mode output-
only accuracy hides: the hidden state knows, the
answer does not say so. Our paper is about (b).
The rest of this section fixes the setting and nota-
tion (Section 3.1), pins down what we mean by en-
code (Section 3.2), states how we quantify the gap
(Section 3.3), and lists the empirical claims we will
test in Section 3.4; a representative cell-(b) item is
illustrated in Figure 2.

3.2
What “encode” means here
With those pieces in place, we say precisely what we
mean when we claim the hidden state encodes the
evidence-supported answer on an item: on the same
frozen forward pass, (i) the linear readout recovers
that answer; (ii) on the counterfactual minimal pair
the same readout recovers the opposite direction (so
it tracks the causal direction, not the wording); and
(iii) both same-shape controls (sham and random
direction) sit at chance on (i)–(ii), so the readout
is specific to the cs causal direction, not anything
a 7B hidden state happens to support. The claim is
deliberately narrow—this is a hidden-state-vs.-output
gap, not human-style understanding—and we use it
throughout the paper.

3.4
The empirical claims
The rest of the paper tests three empirical claims,
each tied to a specific subsection of the evidence.
Claim 1. ∆is largest when the prompt evidence
points against commonsense: the hidden-state read-
out still matches the evidence-supported answer
while the Yes/No output falls back to a weak or mis-
leading response (checked in Section 5.1).
Claim 2. The readout direction Vcs is not random
variation: same-shape sham, random-direction, and
pair controls all support it as a stable, readable direc-
tion, even though it is not itself a switch that flips the
final answer (checked in Section 5.2).
Claim 3. ∆is not explained away by visible causal
words or candidate-token surface cues alone; those
cues can matter, but they do not account for when the
readout direction is recoverable and when the Yes/No
answer expresses it (checked in Sections 6.1 and 6.2).
Roadmap. Claims 1–3 map to Sections 5.1, 5.2, 6.1
and 6.2; a final scope check (Section 6.3) localises
the failure to the verbal interface rather than to one

How We Checked It
For each item, two readout paths branch off the same
single inference run of the model (a forward pass): a
linear probe on the hidden state, and the model’s own
lm_head—the language-model head, i.e. the final
linear layer that maps the hidden state to next-token
logits—producing the ordinary Yes/No token. We
compare them on the same item (Figure 2).
Paired questions and hidden states.
For each item
we use a matched causal-direction pair: the prompt

The internal answer scales; the spoken
answer does not
Finding.
Across the eight instruction-tuned models
we audit (Qwen 0.5B–72B, Mistral-7B, DeepSeek-
7B), and on 80 anti-commonsense CLADDER items—
items whose in-prompt evidence forces the an-
swer that contradicts everyday commonsense (e.g.
a fictional world in which smoking prevents lung
cancer)—a simple linear function of the model’s hid-
den state at the last prompt token recovers the correct,
evidence-supported answer with accuracy ≥0.969
on every model. The ordinary next-token Yes/No,
asked on the same items, lands at 0.350–0.525 across
the eight models. The gap ∆≈+0.5 is therefore not
specific to one model or one scale: from 0.5B to
72B the readable internal answer goes up while the
spoken answer does not (Figure 3).
Two everyday worries.
Two worries arise: (a) is
the probe just memorizing surface wording, so any
classifier of that shape would already score this high?
(b) is the low Yes/No really an error, or just imbal-
anced gold labels? We rule both out with four validity
checks.
(i) Is the wrong Yes/No just a label imbalance?
The gold-Yes rate on these anti-commonsense items
is 0.475, so the 0.35–0.53 output accuracy is not
what we would get from blindly answering “Yes”
(or “No”) on every item. (ii) Did we pick a flat-
tering layer? At two layers fixed before the per-
model best-layer search (layers 8 and 16) the probe
still reaches ≥0.919, so the gap is not a best-layer
cherry-pick. (iii) Is the readable signal already an
ordinary answer at that depth? A logit-lens check,
which sends the intermediate hidden state straight

(b) Pushing the direction:
scalar swap and
full-state injection.
The complementary test asks
whether moving the same direction flips the spoken
answer. The simplest version is a scalar swap: we
leave the hidden state otherwise alone but rescale its
component along Vcs to a more commonsense-like
value (and, as controls, along the sham direction Vns
and a Haar-random direction). Even pushed to +2σcs
none of the three reliably moves the spoken Yes/No
(accuracy shifts by at most +0.025, indistinguishable
from baseline). We label this a finite-power null—
“no detectable effect at this sample size and this size
of single-direction push”—rather than a strong nega-
tive.
A scalar swap touches one direction at one scalar;
the full-state counterpart is activation patching, the
standard interpretability procedure of copying a
whole layer’s hidden state from a donor prompt into
the recipient (Vig et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2022).
On the N=42 anti-commonsense items Qwen2.5-
7B-Instruct gets wrong, replacing the layer-27 last-
token hidden state with that of a matched common-
sense item lifts accuracy from 0 to 0.571, well above
random-hidden and self-injection controls (Figure 5;
Section N for the full table).

6
What It Is Not, and Where It Breaks
This section in one sentence.
The gap is not just a
reaction to causal vocabulary (Section 6.1), not just
statistical association between cause and effect words
(Section 6.2), and the failure point is the ordinary
verbal answer interface—not the internal direction
(Section 6.3). Together, these three exclusions narrow
what Causal Tongue-Tie can be: the readable internal
direction is real; what breaks is the plain-language
Yes/No commitment step.
6.1
It is not just causal wording
The wording control rules out a simple causal-
word explanation.
A skeptical reading of the gap
is that “the probe just follows surface causal words
(cause/affect/influence) in the prompt, not anything
causal”—a hypothesis raised in earlier critical work
on causal-LLM benchmarks (Zeˇcevi´c et al., 2023).
We control for it by varying the causal vocabulary
while keeping the underlying causal structure of the
item fixed, and ask whether the gap tracks the word-
ing or the structure.
Explicit causal words are neither necessary nor
sufficient for the observed accept/reject pattern. The

Discussion and Conclusion
We introduced Causal Tongue-Tie: hidden states of-
ten carry an evidence-aligned causal direction that the
ordinary Yes/No does not say (Sections 5.1 and 5.2;
controls in Sections 6.2 and 6.3), and full-state injec-
tion flips over half the wrong Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
items (Section N). A wrong Yes/No thus decomposes
into two failure modes—no internal signal vs. an
unexpressed one—not a single “failed” verdict.
Implication for benchmark-based claims.
The
gap warns against output-only evaluation: hidden-
state and Yes/No verdicts can differ by ∆≈+0.5,
while prompt form also matters (Section O). Single-
number claims conflate all three.

Limitations
Scope of the claim.
Causal Tongue-Tie is an empir-
ical diagnostic of a mismatch between hidden-state
evidence and the final Yes/No answer. Following
the operational definition in Section 3, we claim that
the model encodes causal direction only in that nar-
row sense, not human-like understanding, complete
causal reasoning, or a causal circuit; the direction
is causally sufficient on >50% but not all items the
ordinary interface gets wrong (Section N).
Scope of the finding.
Our gap is a hidden-state-vs-
output mismatch on textual causal questions (a short
story plus a Yes/No probe). It is not a finding about
causal discovery from raw observational data such as
physical sensor readings, time-series, or experimental
measurements; those tasks have very different inputs
and evaluation, and lie outside our scope. A local
contamination heuristic (Table 18) reports anti-CS
marker rate = 0.000 and ns / COUNTERBENCH
nonsense-entity coverage = 1.000, so the audited
items are unlikely to be verbatim pretraining text; a
rigorous n-gram contamination audit is left to future
work.
Direction-label confounding and wording sensitiv-
ity.
The original direction label in Section 5.1 is
partly confounded with question format, so the con-
trolled hard-negative diagnostic—not the Section 5.1
probe alone—anchors the readout claim. Even there,
the multi-template run exposes a brittle family, so the
recovered direction should still be read as tied to the
tested wording and syntax. The obs-matched hard-
negative families are template-generated; 5 hand-
written templates (Table 11, including a Chinese para-
phrase, passive, and indirect phrasings) all reproduce
the qualitative gap (per-template probe Acc ≥0.95,
gap ≥+ 0.26), but cross-template probe transfer
is fragile (Table 12, off-diagonal mean 0.672); the
recovered direction is template-conditioned in its pa-
rameterization. Full human-style paraphrases beyond
these 5 templates remain future work.
Limited interface repair and partial sufficiency.
Prompting narrows but does not close the interface
gap. The scalar Vcs swap (Section 5.2) is a finite-
power null on full Yes/No control; full-state activa-
tion patching (Section N) is causally sufficient on
>50% but not all of the items the ordinary inter-
face gets wrong, so we demonstrate a partially ex-
ploited internal direction, not a complete causal con-
trol path. The diagnostic is single-direction, single-
layer, and scalar; manifold or multi-mode geome-
tries, norm-preserving multi-layer lesions, and low-
rank or cone-shaped interventions could close the
remaining sufficiency gap. Forced-choice decod-
ing is not standardized across surface forms, but the
Yes/No vs. True/False vs. Chinese (Shi/Fou) vs. A/B-
reframe comparison on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Sec-
tion O) keeps ∆≥+0.595 across all four; a fully
multi-token-calibrated protocol over the entire model
suite is still left to future work.
Coverage of models and controls.
The evidence
is limited by the models and tests we could run. The
full diagnostic suite is centered on Qwen2.5-Instruct
and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3; DeepSeek-LLM-7B-
Chat supports the basic probe–answer gap but not
the full Sections 5.2 and 6.1 to 6.3 analysis. These
checks bound the claim but do not exhaust other
model families, layers, prompts, interventions, or
lexical shortcuts. Several controls use smaller per-
condition N (N ∈[20, 30] in Sections 6.1 and 6.2;
N=24 per hard-negative family), widening their CIs
relative to the headline anti-CS gap on N=80. All
eight backbones are instruction-tuned; auditing base
checkpoints would help separate interface failure
from RLHF answer policy.
Probe training set and cross-dataset transfer.
The probe in the main text is always fit on CLad-
der commonsense items. We report a preliminary
cross-dataset transfer evaluation in Section B: a
CLadder-trained probe transfers to CounterBench
at 0.844–0.913 accuracy (layer 24), and an anti-CS-
trained probe is nearly identical to a cs-trained probe
(symmetry gap 0.006). These results argue against
surface-marker overfitting and support cross-dataset
generalization of the direction readout. Cross-dataset
transfer is layer-conditioned: the headline 0.844–
0.913 requires layer 24, while layer 16 (the layer
used elsewhere in this paper) transfers only 0.506–
0.606. We report both to avoid layer-selection on
the transfer test. A comprehensive multi-benchmark
transfer study—testing the same probe weights on
CLadder, Causal-NLI, FaithDial, or other causal
benchmarks—remains an open empirical test for fu-

Cross-Dataset Probe Transfer
A natural concern is whether (i) training the direction
probe on anti-commonsense (anti-CS) items overfits
to surface lexical markers, and (ii) whether there is ev-
idence for cross-dataset generalization—specifically,
whether a probe fitted on CLadder commonsense
items transfers to CounterBench. We address both
questions through three held-out experiments using
already-cached hidden states (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct,
layer 24, the same model and layer swept in the main
analysis). No new GPU inference was required; the
transfer experiment runs only a logistic regression on
stored tensors.
Commonsense-only training: CLadder common-
sense →CounterBench transfer.
We fit a logistic
direction probe on all 160 CLadder commonsense
rows (80 fwd / 80 rev), freeze its weights, and eval-
uate it on the CounterBench adjacent (N=160) and
transitive (N=160) subsets. Table 3 summarises
the result: transfer accuracy is 0.844 (adjacent) and
0.913 (transitive)—both above the 0.80 generaliza-
tion threshold stated in our protocol and well above
the 0.50 chance baseline. In-distribution 5-fold CV
on CounterBench itself yields 0.981 ± 0.038 (adja-
cent) and 0.988±0.015 (transitive), giving a transfer
gap of only −0.138 / −0.075—negligibly small rela-
tive to the > 0.40 probe-output gap reported in the
main text.
The layer-sweep in Table 4 provides further con-
text: at layers ≤16 the cross-dataset transfer is near-
chance, but at layer 24 it peaks at 0.844 / 0.913. This
pattern is consistent with the known result that deeper
layers encode more abstract, entity-independent rep-
resentations: at early and mid layers, the probe di-
rection captures story-specific entity semantics (real-
world words in CLadder vs. nonsense words in Coun-
terBench); by layer 24, the representation has fac-
tored out entity identity and retains only the abstract
relational direction.

The commonsense-only condition
