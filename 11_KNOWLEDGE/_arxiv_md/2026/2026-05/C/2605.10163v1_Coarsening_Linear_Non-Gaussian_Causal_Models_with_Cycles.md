Abstract
Recent work on causal abstraction, in particular graphical approaches focusing
on causal structure between clusters of variables, aims to summarize a high-
dimensional causal structure in terms of a low-dimensional one. Existing meth-
ods for learning such summaries from data assume that both the high- and low-
dimensional structures are acyclic, which is helpful for causal effect identifica-
tion and reasoning but excludes many high-dimensional models and thus limits
applicability. We show that in the linear non-Gaussian (LiNG) setting, the high-
dimensional acyclicity assumption can be relaxed while still allowing recovery of a
low-dimensional causal directed acyclic graph (DAG). We further connect identifi-
ability of this low-dimensional DAG to existing results: LiNG models with cycles
are observationally identifiable only up to an equivalence class whose members
differ by reversals of directed cycles; our low-dimensional DAG, which is invariant
across all members of a given equivalence class, thus forms a natural representative
of the class. While existing approaches for learning this observational equivalence
class over high-dimensional variables have exponential time complexity, our low-
dimensional summary is learned in worst-case cubic time and comes with explicit
bounds on the sample complexity. We provide open source code and experiments
on synthetic data to corroborate our theoretical results.

Introduction
Causal discovery from observational data has predominantly focused on directed acyclic graphs
(DAGs) [32, 33]. Yet many systems of scientific interest contain feedback loops: gene-regulatory
networks [29] and dynamical systems [24] exhibit directed cycles. A line of work on cyclic causal
models has therefore developed, ranging from the structural-equation formulation of [34, 5] to the
linear non-Gaussian models of [19], which exploit standard ICA [16, 11] to recover the demixing
matrix W = I −B of a linear cyclic Structural Causal Model (SCM) up to row permutation and
scaling, from which the weighted adjacency matrix B—and hence the directed graph G—can be
recovered up to a distributional equivalence class. Additionally, other approaches have tried to
identify causal orderings among groups of variables: early extensions of linear non-Gaussian methods

[17, 10]. The GroupLiNGAM algorithm of [17] has worst-case complexity exponential in the number
of variables. More recently, this perspective has been revisited for models containing disjoint cycles
[9], with identification results that rely on second- and third-order moments and thus require the

standard ICA, does not require disjoint cycles, and runs in polynomial time. We provide an empirical
comparison with that line of work, even though their identification target (the full variable-level cyclic
graph under disjoint cycles) differs from ours.
The coarsening framework of [38, 21] characterizes when a DAG can be simplified by grouping
variables into clusters. We show that this framework extends naturally to directed graphs with cycles:
the strongly connected components (SCC) partition emerges as a structural floor on the coarsening
lattice—the finest partition whose quotient is a DAG—and the resulting cluster-DAG is exactly the
condensation Gsc of G in the classical sense of graph theory [4]. This is the identification target that
resolves the ambiguity in the equivalence class of [19].
Contributions.
We extend the coarsening framework to directed graphs that may contain cycles,
and connect it to the cyclic-SCM identification literature:
• The SCC floor (§ 4.1). The coarsening lattice acquires a structural floor: any valid DAG-
coarsening must group nodes in a common SCC, and the SCC partition is the finest valid
one.
• Condensation identifiability (Theorem 1, § 4.2). The condensation Gsc of a linear non-
Gaussian cyclic SCM is invariant across the distributional equivalence class of [19], and
is therefore identifiable from observational data alone, without invoking the stability and
disjoint-cycles assumptions of that work.
As a downstream payoff, identifying Gsc from observations alone supplies the prerequisite of the
C-DAG identification theorems of [2], reducing cluster-level causal-effect identification in a cyclic
system to the standard acyclic case.
The paper is organized as follows: § 3 fixes notation and recalls the coarsening lattice and cyclic-SCM
background; § 4 introduces DAG-coarsenings of cyclic graphs, proves the SCC-floor proposition,
presents the learning procedure, proves condensation identifiability and discusses the implication for
cluster-level causal-effect identification; § 5 reports the synthetic experiments1; § 6 concludes.

Related Work
Cyclic generalisations of structural causal models date back to [34], with rigorous foundations in
[5]. [19] showed that linear non-Gaussian cyclic SCMs with no latent confounders can be identified
via standard ICA: ICA recovers the demixing matrix ˆW, from which one obtains a candidate adjacency
matrix ˆB = I −diag(P ˆW)−1P ˆW for each admissible row permutation P, and thresholding yields
the graph. Later work has extended this to latent-confounded settings via overcomplete ICA [30, 7],
extensions of LiNGAM [22], and constraint-based methods using σ-separation [12]. [1] study
structure learning for linear cyclic SCMs including the Gaussian case, while [36] adapt linear non-
Gaussian models to learn graphs that are polytrees and [27] leverages interventional data. The
condensation also appears in recent experiment-design work for linear non-Gaussian cyclic models
[31]; our contribution is to place this invariance inside the coarsening lattice as its structural floor,
connecting it to the broader coarsening/abstraction literature and to C-DAG identification.
Another line of work grounds causal models in stochastic differential equations [13], rather than
standard structural equations. Graphical Lyapunov models [37, 8, 25, 26] encode directed causal
structure via a weighted adjacency matrix that solves either the continuous- and discrete-time
Lyapunov equation, while naturally allowing for cycles.
Coarsening of causal models has been approached from several angles: causal abstraction in the
SCM sense [28, 3], group-level structure under partial-order assumptions [38] and the partition-
refinement approach of [21] that we extend here. The C-DAG identification theorems of [2] charac-
terize which cluster-level interventional distributions are recoverable once an acyclic cluster-level

Background
3.1
Coarsening causal models
Let G∗= (V ∗, E∗) be a DAG. A coarsening [21] of G∗is a partition Π = {π1, . . . , πk} of V ∗such
that the quotient graph G′ = (Π, E′) with edges
E′ = {(χ(u∗), χ(v∗)) | (u∗, v∗) ∈E∗, χ(u∗) ̸= χ(v∗)}
is itself a DAG, where χ : V ∗→Π sends each node to its part. We call G′ the quotient graph and
the parts πi clusters.
The set of all coarsenings of G∗ordered by refinement forms a sublattice of the partition refinement
lattice (Theorem 3 in [21]); we generalize this construction to cyclic graphs in § 4.
3.2
Cyclic structural causal models
A linear cyclic SCM is defined by:
X = BX + ε,
solved as X = (I −B)−1ε,
(1)
where d = |V | is the number of variables, B ∈Rd×d is the weighted adjacency matrix of a directed
graph G = (V, E) (which may contain cycles), and the noise vector ε ∈Rd has mutually independent
components. The spectral radius ρ(B) = maxi |λi(B)| controls when (1) is solvable: existence and

Two matrices play distinct roles. The mixing matrix A = (I −B)−1 maps independent sources to
observations, X = Aε; its support encodes the transitive closure of G (i.e., total effects/reachability)
under no exact cancellations. The demixing matrix W = I −B = A−1 recovers sources from
observations via ε = WX; its off-diagonal support encodes the direct adjacency of G. Throughout,

Coarsening Directed Graphs with Cycles
We first generalize the notion of coarsening from DAGs to directed graphs that may contain cycles.
Definition 1 (DAG-coarsening of a directed graph). Let G = (V, E) be a directed graph (possibly
containing cycles), and let Π = {π1, . . . , πk} be a partition of V with associated surjection χ : V →
Π. The quotient graph is G′ = (Π, E′) with
E′ = {(χ(u), χ(v)) | (u, v) ∈E, χ(u) ̸= χ(v)}.
We call Π a DAG-coarsening of G when G′ is acyclic.
When G is a DAG this reduces to the original definition of [21]: the trivial singleton-per-node
partition has an acyclic quotient, and so does any coarsening of it. For graphs containing cycles the
acyclicity requirement is non-trivial and forces SCCs to be respected, as Proposition 1 below shows.

Proposition 1 (SCC floor). Let G = (V, E) be a directed graph. Nodes u, v ∈V in the same SCC
of G must lie in the same cluster of every DAG-coarsening of G. Consequently, the SCC coarsening
of Definition 2 is the finest DAG-coarsening of G, and its quotient Gsc is the structural floor of the
DAG-coarsening sublattice.
Proof. If u and v are in the same SCC, there exist directed paths u →· · · →v and v →· · · →u
in G. If χ(u) ̸= χ(v) in a coarsening, these paths induce directed paths χ(u) →· · · →χ(v) and
χ(v) →· · · →χ(u) in the quotient, forming a cycle and violating the DAG requirement.
Given G the SCC partition is computable via Tarjan’s algorithm [35].
Remark 1 (cyclic coarsenings). Definitions 1 and 2 requires the coarsened graph to be a DAG,
mirroring the original framework of [21]. A natural generalization drops this requirement and
asks only that the coarsening preserves the partial order over SCCs of G: any partition χ such
that the quotient graph (a) does not reverse a non-cyclic ancestral relation in G, and (b) does not
introduce a cycle between nodes that are not already in a common SCC, is a valid cyclic coarsening.
The interventional and observational coarsenings considered below are all DAG-coarsenings (and
hence cyclic coarsenings), so we focus on the DAG case throughout. Characterizing the general
cyclic-coarsening lattice is left to future work.

4.2
Observational coarsening via linear non-Gaussian ICA
We assume the linear cyclic SCM of (1) with mutually independent, non-Gaussian noise components
εi and no latent confounders, so ε has the same dimension as X. Under these assumptions, standard
ICA [11] recovers W = I −B up to row permutation and scaling—the regime exploited by [19].
Overcomplete ICA [20] is needed only when the number of independent sources exceeds the number
of observed variables, which arises under latent confounding [30, 7]. The interventional case is
discussed in Appendix A.1.

Markov on the condensation.
A second consequence of SCC aggregation is the validity of
conditional-independence reasoning at the cluster level.
Lemma 1 (Markov on the condensation). Under the linear cyclic SCM of (1), the marginal joint
distribution on cluster-level features is Markov to the condensation Gsc.
Linear cyclic SCMs are σ-Markov to G [34, 5]. Aggregating variables to their SCC clusters projects σ-
separation statements about G onto separation statements about Gsc; since Gsc is acyclic, σ-separation
collapses to ordinary d-separation, so the cluster-level distribution is Markov to Gsc. Furthermore our
identification does not invoke faithfulness: Algorithm 1 recovers bGsc by thresholding entries of ˆB,
not by conditional-independence testing, so faithfulness plays no role in Theorem 1. Nonetheless,
Corollary 2 of [38] shows it transfers cleanly under the SCC partition: σ-Markovian and σ-faithful G
implies the same for Gsc.

Proposition 2 (Two failure modes of thresholding). Finite-sample noise affects the recovered partition
in two opposite directions. (a) A true intra-SCC edge whose magnitude falls below τ is dropped;

Experimental Results
We illustrate Theorem 1 on synthetic linear non-Gaussian cyclic SCMs using the recipe of [19] from
§ 4.2. The interventional case (Appendix A.1) inherits the unmodified guarantees of [21] and is not
explored empirically. All experiments ran on a single CPU workstation (AMD Ryzen 9 9950X, 128
GB RAM); fit times are reported in Figure 4.
5.1
Setup
Data-generating process.
We generate linear non-Gaussian cyclic SCMs on d = 10 variables
with a controlled number κ ∈{3, 4, 5} of non-trivial SCCs and edge densities λ ∈{0.3, 0.5, 0.8}.
For each (d, κ, λ), the d nodes are randomly partitioned into κ non-trivial SCCs (each of size ≥2)
plus singleton nodes. Within each SCC a directed Hamilton cycle ensures mutual reachability and
additional intra-SCC directed edges are sampled at probability λ. Between clusters, edges that respect
a random topological order are sampled at probability λ. Edge weights are sampled in [0.5, 0.95]
with random sign. Noise components εi are mutually independent and Laplace-distributed. Sample
sizes n ∈[50, 105]; each cell is replicated across 10 random seeds.
Each configuration is run under two spectral-radius regimes: Stable (ρ(B) ≈0.9): the first-stable
filter can recover the true B. Unstable (ρ(B) = 1.5): the first-stable filter does not necessarily select
the right equivalence-class member; by Theorem 1 the condensation is the same.
Method (deliberately broader than Algorithm 1).
Algorithm 1 recovers Gsc from any single
admissible permutation. For the experiments we instead run the full ICA-LiNG-D enumeration of
[19] so that we can additionally report variable-level F1 and visualize the gap between the identifiable
cluster-level structure and the unidentifiable intra-SCC structure. We pick the first-stable candidate
(lowest-spectral-radius candidate if no stable one exists). By Theorem 1, this choice does not affect
the recovered bGsc. We use threshold τ = 0.1; sensitivity to this choice is reported in Appendix C.2.

5.2
Results
Figure 3 shows partition and edge recovery as functions of sample size for both regimes and all three
densities, faceted by κ. Three observations:
• Partition recovery. ARI rises with n and approaches 1 in both regimes and at all densities,
confirming consistency of LiNG-D + Tarjan under non-Gaussian noise.
• Cluster-DAG F1. Cluster-DAG F1 converges to 1 with n in both regimes, directly illustrating
Theorem 1: Gsc is identifiable regardless of the stability condition.
• Variable-level F1. In the stable regime (solid lines), the true B is the unique stable represen-
tative of its equivalence class, so the first-stable filter recovers it and variable-level F1 →1.
In the unstable regime (dashed), the true B has ρ(B) = 1.5, so the filter does not necessarily
select the right member of the equivalence class and variable-level F1 saturates below 1.
The gap is the unidentifiable intra-SCC structure.
The contrast between the two regimes is the key empirical message: the condensation (rows 1–2) is
robustly recovered even when the variable-level graph (row 3) is not. This is the practical motivation

Discussion
We have extended the coarsening framework of [21] to directed graphs with cycles, with the SCC
partition as the lattice floor and the condensation Gsc as the canonical identification target. Theorem 1
refines the cyclic-SCM identifiability picture of [19]: although the underlying micro-graph is identi-
fiable only up to a distributional equivalence class, Gsc is fully identifiable from the observational
distribution, supplying the prerequisite of the C-DAG identification machinery of [2].
The observational approach requires linear non-Gaussian noise and no latent confounders, but with
the practical advantage of requiring no interventional data. Hard do-interventions on a single variable
inside a non-trivial SCC sever the cyclic mechanism and are excluded throughout; only soft (shift)
interventions and hard interventions on entire SCCs are well-defined in the cyclic SCM setting (§ 4.3).
Open directions.
(i) Latent-confounder generalisations via overcomplete ICA [30, 7]: the cycle-
reversal argument carries over because cycle reversals are purely a graph-level operation, independent
of the source/observed dimension. (ii) Mixed-Gaussian extension—ICA’s rotational ambiguity in
the Gaussian subspace is global, not per-SCC: with two Gaussian sources in different SCCs, an
orthogonal rotation can mix them, introducing inter-SCC entries in the equivalent demixing matrix
and falsely merging the two SCCs in the recovered condensation. The condensation-invariance
argument therefore survives only when at most one noise source is Gaussian overall; lifting this
restriction would address the open problem of [19, § 6] on Gaussian/non-Gaussian mixtures and

B.2
Transitive closure and SCCs
For a directed graph G = (V, E), denote the existence of a directed path from vertex u to vertex v by the
reachability relation u ⇝G v. By convention, a vertex can always reach itself (paths of length 0), so u ⇝G u
for all u ∈V .
Definition 3 (Strongly connected component). Define a binary relation ∼G on V by
u ∼G v ⇐⇒(u ⇝G v) ∧(v ⇝G u).
The relation ∼G is an equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive). A strongly connected component
(SCC) of G is an equivalence class of V under ∼G.
The following result provides an alternative route to condensation identifiability via the mixing matrix A =
(I −B)−1 and its transitive-closure interpretation. While the main proof of Theorem 1 uses the more direct
cycle-reversal argument, the transitive-closure perspective is independently useful—for instance, it connects to
the path-rank lemma of [7] and to settings where one works with A rather than W.
Definition 4 (Transitive closure). The transitive closure of a directed graph G = (V, E) is the graph Gtc =
(V, Etc) with edge set
Etc = {(u, v) ∈V × V | u ⇝G v}.
Theorem 3 (SCCs are preserved under transitive closure). Let G = (V, E) be a directed graph and let
Gtc = (V, Etc) be its transitive closure. Then G and Gtc have the same SCCs.
Proof. It suffices to show u ∼G v ⇐⇒u ∼Gtc v for all u, v ∈V .
(⇒) Suppose u ∼G v, so u ⇝G v and v ⇝G u. By Definition 4, (u, v) ∈Etc and (v, u) ∈Etc, hence
u ⇝Gtc v and v ⇝Gtc u, i.e. u ∼Gtc v.
(⇐) Suppose u ∼Gtc v, so there is a path u = x0, x1, . . . , xk = v in G∗. Each step (xi, xi+1) ∈Etc implies
xi ⇝G xi+1 by Definition 4. Transitivity of ⇝G then gives u ⇝G v. Symmetric reasoning yields v ⇝G u, so
u ∼G v.
The two implications give equality of equivalence classes, hence equality of SCCs.
Remark.
Theorem 3 means that if one recovers supp(A) = adjacency(Gtc)—the transitive closure of
G—from the mixing matrix A = (I −B)−1, the SCC partition extracted from it is exactly the SCC partition of
G. This provides an alternative proof route for the SCC-partition part of Theorem 1, going through A rather
than W. The main text uses the cycle-reversal argument because it also immediately handles inter-SCC edges
without requiring a separate step.

The main scalability sweep of § 5.3 at density λ = 0.5, creates multiple overlapping cycles within each SCC
of size ≥3, violating the disjoint-cycles assumption of [9]. To isolate the effect of that violation from the
methodological gap, we run a complementary comparison whose data-generating process satisfies the assumption
exactly by construction.
Setup.
We fix d = 20 with κ = 5 non-trivial SCCs, inter-block density λ = 0.5, and zero intra-SCC chord
density: each SCC is generated as a single directed Hamilton cycle and no further intra-SCC edges are added.
Inter-block edges respect a random DAG ordering over the κ = 5 blocks. By construction, every directed simple
cycle in the resulting graph lies inside a single SCC and is the unique cycle of that SCC, so all simple cycles are
pairwise vertex-disjoint, which is the structural premise of [9]. We sweep n ∈[102, 105] with 10 seeds per cell,
in the stable weight regime (w ∈[0.5, 0.95], ρ(B) < 1), and use skewed exponential noise so that the baseline’s
third-moment tests carry signal. Both methods use the same fit configuration as the main scalability sweep.
Results.
Figure 6 reports ARI of the recovered partition, cluster-DAG F1, and fit time. Our method saturates
at ARI = 1 and cluster-DAG F1 = 1 from n ≥104, with fit time staying within ∼1 s across the full sweep. The
disjoint-cycles baseline plateaus at median ARI ≈0.13 and median cluster-DAG F1 ≈0.60 even at n = 105,
with fit time growing to 102 s.
Inspection of the baseline’s predicted partitions explains the gap: at n ≤103, every seed returns the trivial
all-singletons partition (the procedure’s third-moment cycle tests at α = 0.01 have insufficient power and
confirm no cycle); at n ≥104, the output either contains seeds where some 2–3-cycles are correctly recovered
and seeds where it merges most variables into a single cycle.
C.2
Threshold sensitivity and the two failure modes
Algorithm 1 requires a threshold τ > 0 on | ˆBij|. Proposition 2 characterises two opposite failure modes; this
subsection probes both empirically. We fix d = 10, κ = 4, λ = 0.5 in the stable regime (ρ(B) < 1) and sweep
τ across three orders of magnitude on a synthetic grid with 10 seeds per cell. The number of non-trivial SCCs is
exactly κ = 4 in every seed, but the singletons absorbed into non-trivial SCCs vary, so the true partition size |Π|
ranges over {4, 5, 6} across seeds (median 5).
Metrics.
Figure 7 reports three quantities. The first two are the same metrics used in § 5: ARI of the recovered
SCC partition (left) and F1 of the recovered cluster-DAG edges (middle). The third is the size |bΠ| of the
estimated partition (right) —the failure modes:
• |bΠ| = 1 (everything in one cluster): spurious entries above τ closed paths between distinct true SCCs
and merged everything (Mode (b) of Proposition 2).
• |bΠ| = d = 10 (every variable is a singleton): true intra-SCC edges fell below τ and every SCC
splintered (Mode (a)).
