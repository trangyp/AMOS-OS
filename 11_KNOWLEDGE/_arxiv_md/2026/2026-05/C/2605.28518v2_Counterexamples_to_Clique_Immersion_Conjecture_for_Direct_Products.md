Abstract
Let G and H be graphs, and let G × H denote their direct product. For a graph G, let im(G)
be the largest integer t such that G contains a Kt-immersion. Collins, Heenehan, and McDonald
conjectured that if im(G) = t and im(H) = r, then
im(G × H) ≥(t −1)(r −1) + 1.
We disprove this conjecture by constructing an infinite family of connected bipartite counterex-
amples.
Keywords: Immersion, Direct product

Introduction
Throughout this paper, graphs are loopless and simple. The degree of a vertex v in a graph G,
denoted by degG(v), is the number of edges incident with v. The maximum degree of G, denote by
∆(G). The chromatic number χ(G) is the minimum number of colours needed to colour the vertices
of G so that adjacent vertices receive different colours.
Let G and H be graphs. The direct product G × H is the graph with vertex set V (G × H) =
V (G)×V (H). Two vertices u = (u1, u2), v = (v1, v2) of G×H are adjacent if and only if u1v1 ∈E(G)
and u2v2 ∈E(H).
Let G = (V, E) be such a graph with vertex set V (G) and edge set E(G). Let G, H be graphs,
G is said to contain a weak immersion of H if there exists an injective function ϕ : V (H) →V (G),
satisfying the following:
• for every edge uv ∈E(H), there is a path in G, denoted Puv, with endpoints ϕ(u) and ϕ(v).
• the paths in {Puv|uv ∈E(H)} are pairwise edge disjoint.

The vertices {ϕ(v) : v ∈V (H)} are called the terminal vertices (also known as branch vertices)
of the immersion, and call internal vertices of the path Puv (where u, v ∈V (G)) the pegs of the
H-immersion. If terminal vertices do not appear as interior vertices on path Puv, we say that G
contains a strong immersion of H. This paper is only concerned with weak immersion, and from
now on we will omit “weak”, and simply say “immersion”. The immersion number of a graph G be
denoted as im(G), is the largest value t for which G has a Kt-immersion. Kt is a complete graph on
t vertices in which any two vertices are adjacent.
The immersion relation has received a considerable attention in recent years, especially after
Robertson and Seymour [6] proved that it is a well-quasi-order, just like the minor relation. Much
of this attention has been directed toward the conjecture of Lescure and Meyniel [5], which serves
as an analogue to Hadwiger’s conjecture [4] for strong immersion. It states that every graph G with
a chromatic number χ(G) ≥t contains the complete graph Kt as a strong immersion. Additionally,
Abu-Khzam and Langston [1] proposed a conjecture for weak immersion.
Haj´os’ conjectured that every graph G with a chromatic number χ(G) ≥t contains the complete
graph Kt as a subdivision. The counterexamples that Catlin [2] used to disprove Haj´os’ conjecture
are the lexicographic product of cliques and cycles.
Collins et al. [3] studied clique immersions in the four standard graph products: the lexicographic
product G◦H, the Cartesian product G□H, the direct product G×H, and the strong product G⊠H.
They first obtained general upper bounds on the immersion number of G∗H in terms of the maximum
degree of the product graph. For lower bounds, they considered the following natural question: if
im(G) = t and im(H) = r, is it true that im(G ∗H) ≥im(Kt ∗Kr)? In [3], the corresponding
lower bounds for the lexicographic product and the Cartesian product were completely determined.
However, the direct product and the strong product have substantially different structures, which
makes the corresponding lower-bound problems more difficult. For the direct product, Collins et al.
proposed the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1. (Collins, Heenehan, and McDonald [3]) Let G and H be graphs with im(G) = t and
im(H) = r, then im(G × H) ≥(t −1)(r −1) + 1.
They proved that Conjecture 1 holds in several special cases, including the case where H contains
Kr as a subgraph, and the case where the paths corresponding to the clique immersions in G and H
all have the same parity. For the strong product, Collins et al. [3] conjectured that im(G ⊠H) ≥tr
whenever im(G) = t and im(H) = r. Wu and Deng [7] verified this conjecture for several classes
of graphs admitting suitable clique immersions. In particular, they proved the conjecture when H
contains Kr as a subgraph.
In this paper, we construct a family of counterexamples disproving Conjecture 1.

and assume that

Set
k = (t −1)(r −1) + 1.
This is precisely the lower bound predicted by the direct product conjecture for im(G × H).
We first record a few key observations. Since both G and H are connected bipartite graphs,
their direct product G × H has exactly two connected components. Moreover, degrees in the direct
product satisfy
degG×H(x, y) = degG(x) degH(y).
Since p, q, s, u ≥2, we have t, r ≥4. Hence the only vertices of G × H attaining degree
(t −1)(r −1) = k −1
are those of the form (x, y), where both x and y are branch vertices. Indeed, every non-branch
vertex in either factor has degree 2, and therefore any product vertex with at least one non-branch
coordinate has degree strictly less than (t −1)(r −1).
Now every terminal of a Kk-immersion must have degree at least k −1. Consequently, if G × H
contained a Kk-immersion, then all its k terminals would have to be branch-by-branch vertices.
Furthermore, since all terminals in a clique immersion must lie in the same connected component,
these k branch-by-branch vertices would all have to lie in a single connected component of G × H.
It remains to count how many branch-by-branch vertices occur in each component. Let A ∪B
be the branch bipartition of G, and let C ∪D be the branch bipartition of H, where
|A| = p,
|B| = q,
|C| = s,
|D| = u.
The two connected components of G × H contain, respectively,
|A||C| + |B||D| = ps + qu
and
|A||D| + |B||C| = pu + qs
branch-by-branch vertices.
We now compare these quantities with k. Since
k = (t −1)(r −1) + 1,
we have
k −(ps + qu) = (t −1)(r −1) + 1 −(ps + qu) = (p −1)(u −1) + (q −1)(s −1),
and
k −(pu + qs) = (t −1)(r −1) + 1 −(pu + qs) = (p −1)(s −1) + (q −1)(u −1).
Because p, q, s, u ≥2, both right-hand sides are at least 2. Hence
ps + qu < k
and
pu + qs < k.
Equivalently,
max{ps + qu, pu + qs} < k.
