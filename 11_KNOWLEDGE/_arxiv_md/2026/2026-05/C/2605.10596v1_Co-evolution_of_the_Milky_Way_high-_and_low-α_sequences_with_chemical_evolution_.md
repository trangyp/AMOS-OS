ABSTRACT
Context. Observational data have revealed a clear dichotomy in the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] diagram of the Milky Way thick and thin disc
stars. Many recent studies have shown evidences of a co-evolution phase between the high- and low-α disc sequences as well as the
presence of very old low-α stars.
Aims. We aim to revise the parallel chemical evolution model that assumes two parallel histories of star formation for the two discs,
by considering a pre-enriched delayed second infall episode in our revised scenario. By means of our chemical evolution models, we
aim to explore the effects of a phase of co-evolution and the presence of old low-α stars, as recently observed.
Methods. We consider a new version of the parallel scenario for the Milky Way thick and thin disc formation, which consists into two
distinct infall episodes of slightly pre-enriched gas. The gas is considered to be extragalactic but possibly contaminated by chemically
enriched gas of a massive dwarf galaxy as Gaia-Enceladus, which merged with the Milky Way at least 10 Gyrs ago. Moreover, we
test in our model observationally derived star formation histories of kinematically selected thick and thin discs, suggesting that the
star formation is triggered by the passages of the Sagittarius galaxy.
Results. Our models can well explain the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] diagram from APOGEE DR17. The high-α sequence should have formed
fast (less than one billion years), whereas the low-α one on a much longer timescale in the solar vicinity (several Gyrs). A hiatus arises
as a period of low star formation between the formation of the high- and low-α sequences. We are able to predict the existence of
low-α stars older than 11 Gyrs, as found in the considered observational sample. Concerning the observationally motivated histories
of star formation, we find that the star formation rate for the thin disc can well reproduce the data also with the inclusion of bursts;
on the other hand, a prolonged star formation history for the thick disc is not compatible with its observed stellar age distribution of a
very old population.
Conclusions. Our revised chemical evolution model with a pre-enriched and delayed (roughly 1 Gyr) second infall episode, explains
not only the abundance patterns of high- and low-α stars but also stellar age distributions for the selected observational sample. We
predict a short co-evolution period in between the two phases and we can explain the observed old low-α stars, but still further data
for precise stellar ages would be needed to put more stringent constraints on their physical nature.
Key words. Galaxy: abundances – Galaxy: formation – Galaxy: evolution
1. Introduction
Galactic archaeology aims to reconstruct the history of forma-
tion and evolution of the Galaxy (Matteucci 2021 and references
therein). It is now well-established that the Milky Way has a
disc structure, with geometrically different thick and thin discs
(Gilmore & Reid 1983). It has been found that the Milky Way
thick and thin discs not only differ in their kinematical and phys-
ical structure, but also in chemical abundances with a clear di-
chotomy in the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] relation, with thick-disc stars
being generally α-enhanced with respect to the thin-disc ones
(Recio-Blanco et al. 2014; Hayden et al. 2015; Mikolaitis et al.
2017; Vincenzo et al. 2021). Also, they differ on the basis of stel-
lar ages, with thick-disc stars being generally older than thin-disc
stars (Silva Aguirre et al. 2018; Miglio et al. 2021a). Thus, the
Milky Way thick and thin discs can differ in structural, chemical,
kinematic and age properties. Different selection methods exist
to classify stars as thin or thick disc stars, each with its own ad-
vantages and limitations (see Alinder et al. 2025 for an in-depth
discussion on different definitions of thick and thin discs and
their applications). In this work, we will focus on the chemical
bimodality between the Milky Way thick and thin discs, and thus
investigate the Milky Way discs as chemically defined and refer
to them as high-α and low-α disc sequences, respectively.
In this context, chemical evolution models are powerful tools
to understand how our Galaxy formed and evolved, and put
strong constraints on the timescales of formation of the various
Galactic components (Matteucci 2001, 2012; Kobayashi et al.
2020). In the literature, many Galactic chemical evolution mod-
els have been developed to follow the chemical evolution of the
Milky Way and they have passed through different approaches:
i) stochastic approach (Argast et al. 2000; Cescutti 2008; Rizzuti
et al. 2025; Grisoni et al. 2025); ii) serial approach (Matteucci
& Greggio 1986; Matteucci & Francois 1989) iii) parallel ap-
proach (Ferrini et al. 1992; Pardi et al. 1995; Chiappini 2009;
Grisoni et al. 2017); iv) two-infall approach (Chiappini et al.
1997; Romano et al. 2010; Spitoni et al. 2019, 2024; Dubay et al.
2025); v) radial migration approach (Schönrich & Binney 2009;

2025). In the serial approach, it is assumed that the halo, thick
and thin disc form in sequence. In this case, the thick disc is just
a later phase relative to the halo, and the thin disc is a later phase
relative to the thick disc. The two-infall model belongs to the se-
rial approach, but it assumes that the halo and thick disc formed
out of a completely independent gas accretion episode relative
to the thin disc. Another sequential scenario similar to the two-
infall model is the three-infall one, assuming a further infall
episode forming the stellar halo (Micali et al. 2013) or occurring
during the thin disc formation (Spitoni et al. 2023). An alterna-
tive way to model the evolution of different Galactic components
is to follow their evolution not sequentially but in parallel (see
Ferrini et al. 1992; Pardi et al. 1995; Nykytyuk & Mishenina
2006; Chiappini 2009; Grisoni et al. 2017, 2018; Goswami et al.
2021). In particular, Grisoni et al. (2017) proposed the "parallel
model" specifically tailored to follow the evolution of the Galac-
tic thick and thin discs, which has been shown to reproduce well
the [Mg/Fe] vs. [Fe/H], and then also other abundance patterns,
from the light elements up to the heaviest ones (Grisoni et al.
2019, 2020a,b, 2021). In this model, it assumed that the Milky
Way thick and thin discs are formed through two distinct infall
episodes (namely, two separate one-infall models), that evolve
at different rates, with different star formation histories (SFH),
but they start forming stars at the same time. In this way, it is
possible to capture a period of possible co-evolution for the two
components.
In the last years, stellar ages have become another fundamen-
tal constraint for the chemical evolution of the Milky Way
(Soderblom 2010 for a review) and different methods have been
used, such as isochrone fitting (Haywood et al. 2013; Hayden
et al. 2017; Queiroz et al. 2023; Cerqui et al. 2025), asteroseis-
mology (Anders et al. 2017a,b; Silva Aguirre et al. 2018; Miglio
et al. 2021a; Willett et al. 2023, 2026; Warfield et al. 2024),
chemical clocks (Casali et al. 2023, 2025; Roberts et al. 2025).
In order to explain the data of Silva Aguirre et al. (2018) for
the stellar ages of Milky Way high- and low-α disc stars, Spi-
toni et al. (2019) applied the two-infall model and showed that
there should be an important delay for the second infall episode
(∼4.3 Gyr, see also Palla et al. 2020; Spitoni et al. 2021), as op-
posed to the delay of ∼1 Gyr found by Chiappini et al. (1997).
However, recent studies showed that there could be a time over-
lap between the formation of these two components (Beraldo e
Silva et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2023; Gent et al. 2024), thus suggest-
ing that there could be a period of co-formation that cannot be
explained by purely sequential chemical evolution models like
the two-infall (see also Dubay et al. 2025). On the other hand,
the co-evolution could be naturally interpreted in the framework
of a parallel chemical evolution model. Moreover, the presence
of a group of old low-α disc stars with ages older than 9 Gyr has
been found in several independent studies (Haywood et al. 2013;
Hayden et al. 2017; Silva Aguirre et al. 2018; Laporte et al. 2020;
Ciuc˘a et al. 2021; Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021; Queiroz et al. 2023;
Gent et al. 2024; Nepal et al. 2024; Casali et al. 2025; Borbolato
et al. 2025). This is also supported by the RR Lyrae stars possess-
ing thin-disc orbits and chemistry (Prudil et al. 2020; Crestani
et al. 2021; D’Orazi et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Bono et al.
2026).
Among the first studies finding the presence of old low-α stars,
Haywood et al. (2013) and Hayden et al. (2017) showed signif-
icant temporal overlap of the high-α and low-α components of
the Galactic disc. Silva Aguirre et al. (2018) also found a popula-
tion of old low-α disc stars in the APOKASC sample; however,
their age uncertainities were very large (with an uncertainity of
∼5 Gyr at an age of 12 Gyr) and no firm conclusions could be
drawn on that population. Using APOGEE and SEGUE data,
Laporte et al. (2020) also reported the existence of old low-α
stellar populations with ages between 8-12 Gyr in the Anticen-
ter Stream. Moreover, many other subsequent studies found old
low-α disc stars (Ciuc˘a et al. 2021; Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021;
Gent et al. 2024).We also note that old low-α populations defined
chemically (as already noted by Fernández-Alvar et al. 2025,
Ardern-Arentsen et al. 2024) can also be likely contaminated by
accreted populations, whose low-energy tail is interpreted as old
low-α (thin) disc (Viswanathan et al. 2025). Thus, the presence
of old low-α stars is currently matter of lively debate, and the
question of how and when do stable and dominant galactic stel-
lar discs form still remains a hot topic in the field (see e.g. Zhang
et al. 2024).
Recently, Nepal et al. (2024) using Gaia-RVS carried out a de-
tailed chrono-chemo-dynamical study of a large sample of stars
with precise stellar parameters, focusing on the oldest stars in
order to decipher the assembly history of the Milky Way discs
and found that the Galaxy, similar to the high-z galaxies ob-
served by JWST and by ALMA, has an old thin disc. In par-
ticular, they confirmed the existence of metal-poor stars in thin-
disc orbits: the majority of those stars are predominantly old
(>10 Gyr), with over 50% being older than 13 Gyr. More re-
cently, Borbolato et al. (2025) (hereafter B25) concluded that
there is an old chemically defined thin disc population with ages
>11 Gyr that indicates a period of co-formation between the
high- and low-α sequences of the Milky Way disc. Finally, re-
cently many efforts have been done in constraining the SFHs of
the Galactic thick and thin discs (Ruiz-Lara et al. 2020; Gallart
et al. 2024; Fernández-Alvar et al. 2025). In particular, Ruiz-
Lara et al. (2020) observed kinematically selected thick and thin
discs stars, and found a parallel evolution for these two Galactic
components. Recently Fernández-Alvar et al. (2025) presented
new results for the star formation histories of the kinematically
selected thick and thin discs of the Milky Way, showing also a
short phase of co-evolution between the two components.
In this paper, we propose a new chemical evolution model based
on the approach of Grisoni et al. (2017) to explain the recent
observational data for the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] diagram as well as
the age distributions of the high- and low-α disc populations
of the Milky Way. In fact, Spitoni et al. (2019), by analyz-
ing APOKASC data, showed that a pure parallel model, where
the two discs start forming exactly at the same time, predicts
too many old low-α stars. Thus a revision of such model ap-
pears necessary to best reproduce all the latest observational
constraints, including a co-evolution phase between the high-
and low-α disc sequences and the presence of old low-α stars
as found by B25.
The paper is structured as follows. In Section 2, we outline the
data to be compared with our chemical evolution models. In Sec-
tion 3, we present the chemical evolution models used in this
work. In Section 4, we show the results based on the compari-
son between data and model predictions. Finally, in Section 5 we
summarize our conclusions.

2.1. Spectroscopic data
We consider the APOGEE sample investigated in B25. Spec-
troscopic data are thus from the high-resolution spectroscopic
survey APOGEE (Majewski et al. 2017), in particular its latest
data release APOGEE DR17 (Abdurro’uf et al. 2022). APOGEE
is part of the Sloan Digital Sky Surveys (SDSS) and it oper-
ates using the du Pont Telescope and the Sloan Foundation 2.5
m Telescope (Gunn et al. 2006) at Apache Point Observatory.
Stellar parameters and chemical abundances are derived with the
APOGEE Stellar Parameters and Chemical Abundance Pipeline
(ASPCAP; García Pérez et al. 2016). Model atmospheres used
in APOGEE DR17 are based on the MARCS model (Gustafsson
et al. 2008, as discussed by Jönsson et al. 2020), and the line
list is described in Smith et al. (2021). In order to obtain stars
with high-quality abundance measurements, B25 applied quality
cuts, excluding sources that exhibited issues in the spectra, the
spectral fitting process and the estimated parameters (STARFLAG
== 0, see Jönsson et al. 2020). Moreover, they ensured accurate
estimates of the [Fe/H] and [Mg/Fe] abundances by selecting
sources with no flagged issues (namely, flagged == 0). They also
selected stars with a good signal-to-noise ratio (S/N > 50), which
allows precise estimates of chemical abundances, radial veloci-
ties, and stellar parameters (García Pérez et al. 2016; Jönsson
et al. 2020).
2.2. Stellar ages
Stellar ages in B25 were derived by Queiroz et al. (2023) us-
ing the StarHorse code, which is a Bayesian isochrone-fitting
code. In particular, in Queiroz et al. (2023) they used StarHorse
for the first time to derive stellar ages for main-sequence turnoff
(MSTO) and subgiant branch (SGB) stars, with age uncertainties
typically around 30%; the uncertainties drop to 15% for SGB
only stars. Age estimates are reliable for MSTO and SGB stars,
since isochrones of different ages are better separated in this re-
gion of the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram; however, the low lu-
minosity of both MSTO and SGB stars leads to an age sample
restricted to the solar neighborhood (d<2 kpc), which is good
to constrain our chemical evolution models in the solar vicinity.
In Queiroz et al. (2023), they presented results for stars listed in
both Gaia DR3 and various public spectroscopic surveys; as dis-
cussed in the previous subsection, we will focus on the APOGEE
DR17 case, which has the smallest nominal uncertainties. B25
restricted their final sample to stars with age uncertainty σAGE
< 1 Gyr, even if they comment that the qualitative results are
mantained if the age uncertainity cut is removed.
2.3. Final sample
After all the quality cuts both for chemical abundances and stel-
lar ages, the final sample contains 25,439 stars with APOGEE
DR17 chemical abundances and derived ages from StarHorse
(we address the interested reader to the B25 paper for further de-
tails on the observational sample).

3. Chemical evolution models
To model the chemical evolution of the Milky Way thick and thin
discs, we start from the parallel model of Grisoni et al. (2017).
The model has been extended to all the Galactic disc in Grisoni
et al. (2018) and tested on the abundance patterns of several
chemical elements, from the light ones (H, He, D, and Li) to
the heavy ones (α-elements, Fe-peak-elements, s- and r- process
elements, see e.g. Grisoni et al. 2019, 2020a,b, 2021; Grisoni
2024). Here, we will constrain our models also in the light of
data from stellar ages.

form via two distinct infall events starting at the same time and
evolving at different rates. In this article, we introduce a more
general formulation of the gas infall terms that allows for differ-
ent durations of the disc phases, which can be written as:

3.1. Nucleosynthesis prescriptions
In this work, we adopt nucleosynthesis prescriptions following
Grisoni et al. (2017), which are based on model 15 of Romano
et al. (2010) where an exhaustive description of the adopted
yields can be found. In particular, the nucleosynthesis prescrip-
tions are as follows. For single stars with mass 0.8–8 M⊙, we
adopt the nucleosynthesis prescriptions of Karakas (2010). For
SNe Ia, we take into account the nucleosynthesis prescriptions
of Iwamoto et al. (1999). For massive stars with masses M > 8

(2019), we consider a multiplying factor for SNIa by a factor

4.1. Star formation histories
In Fig. 2, we show the different star formation histories (SFH),
as predicted by the two models, both the classical and revised
parallel one, which are listed in Table 1. In the case of the classi-
cal parallel of Grisoni et al. (2017), we have two parallel events
which start forming at the same time (same tstart), but they evolve
at different rates (namely, with different star formation efficien-
cies and different infall timescales, faster in the thick disc than

4.3. Comparison with stellar ages
We now discuss in detail the predictions for the two models com-
pared not only to abundance patterns, but also to age distribu-
tions from the observational sample of B25, showing a phase of
co-evolution and the presence of old low-α stars that can be ex-
plained by a parallel chemical evolution model. However, as dis-
cussed in the Introduction, the need for a revised parallel model
resides in the fact that it predicts too many very old low-α stars.
In fact, Spitoni et al. (2019) had found that the classical parallel
model of Grisoni et al. (2017) with two discs starting forming
exactly at the same time could not reproduce the observed stel-
lar age distributions from APOKASC data of Silva Aguirre et al.
(2018).
In Fig. 4, we show the [α/Fe] vs. age for the sample of B25 com-
pared to the predictions of our chemical evolution models. In the
left panel, we compare with the predictions of our classical par-
allel model of Grisoni et al. (2017), with the two discs starting
forming exactly at the same time and evolving in parallel. How-

classical parallel model, with a predicted bulk of old stars in the
predicted age distribution, that are not present in the observed
age distribution. Also, the model curve of the thin discs (con-

explain low-α stars at old ages, and thus a revision is needed.

found in the age distributions of the classical parallel. Moreover,
we note that with the assumed pre-enrichment levels, the low-α
sequence starts in the low-α region as needed and not at too high
[Mg/Fe] levels, typical of the early evolutionary phases when the
pollution from Type Ia SNe is not yet present. In this way, this re-

only the observed abundance patterns but also age distributions.

Queiroz et al. 2023). On the other hand, in our chemical evolu-
tion model, the thin disc forms much more slowly. In the revised

mentioned above, the low-α sequence now starts at the required

4.6. Discussion
To summarize, in a parallel chemical evolution approach, the
two discs form in parallel but at different rates. This approach
is supported also by the work of Clarke et al. (2019) where the
two distinct sequences can be explained as disc fragmentation:
the high-α sequence is related to clumps with high star forma-
tion efficiency, while more distributed and less efficient star for-
mation produces the low-α sequence. Another interpretation of
the bimodality relies on the idea of a late second gas accretion
episode after a period of quenched star formation like has been
proposed in the works of Grand et al. (2018) and Mackereth
et al. (2018). Buck (2020) explain the bimodal α-sequence as
a generic consequence of a gas-rich merger at some time in the
Galaxy’s evolution: the high-α sequence evolves first in the early
galaxy extending to high metallicities, while it is the low-α se-
quence that is formed after the gas-rich merger (see also Calura
& Menci 2009). Another interpretation for the formation of the
bimodality in the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] plot is associated to radial
migration (Schönrich & Binney 2009 and more recently Sharma
et al. 2021; Prantzos et al. 2023), even if Minchev et al. (2013)
found a smooth density distribution in the [O/Fe]-[Fe/H] plane.
Palla et al. (2022) tested radial migration in both one-infall and
two-infall chemical evolution models, but they showed that they
have a small effect on the overall distribution functions, and that
peculiar histories of star formation are still needed to explain
the distribution of stars in the Galaxy (Mackereth et al. 2018;
Vincenzo et al. 2021; Khoperskov et al. 2021). In general, to
reproduce a bimodality in [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H], we propose a very
short timescale of formation for the thick disc and a much longer
evolution for the thin disc, and a period of low star formation be-
tween the peaks of the two phases (see also the quenching in
the star formation history of the Milky Way found by Haywood
et al. 2016). This reconciles with the idea of a hiatus in star for-
mation as proposed in Spitoni et al. (2024). Moreover, we can
explain also a phase of co-evolution between the two disc com-
ponents, as recently proposed (Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021; Gent
et al. 2024). Also, in our scenario we can interpret very old stars
in the low-α sequence, as recently found by B25. To conclude,
our chemical evolution models offer a unique opportunity to fol-
low in detail the evolution in the Milky Way thick and thin discs,
and can also naturally explain: i) a co-evolution phase between
the two disc components, ii) old low-α stars, as found by recent
observational data.
5. Summary and conclusions
We have studied the chemical evolution of the Milky Way thick
and thin discs by comparing detailed chemical evolution models
to recent observational data. We have started from the original
parallel chemical evolution approach of Grisoni et al. (2017),
i.e. two distinct one-infall models for the Galactic thick and thin
discs. Very recent observational studies (see e.g. Borbolato et al.
2025, and references therein) have indicated the existence of
very old low-α stars, invoking the need for a parallel chemical
evolution approach. Moreover, the parallel approach can account
for a phase of co-evolution among the two disc components
(Beraldo e Silva et al. 2021; Gent et al. 2024). However, Spitoni
et al. (2019) showed that two discs starting forming exactly at
the same time could be problematic to explain the stellar age
distribution of APOKASC. Therefore, we revised the parallel
approach, by considering pre-enriched and delayed infall of gas
in order to best reproduce the most recent observational data for
the high- and low-α disc stars. In addition, we have tested the
effects of the star formation histories derived by Ruiz-Lara et al.
(2020) for kinematically selected thick and thin discs, showing
a parallel evolution for the two components and the presence of
bursts of star formation.
Our conclusions, based on the comparison between our
chemical evolution models and the selected sample from Bor-
bolato et al. (2025), can be summarized as follows.
– Both the classical parallel model and the revised model with
a pre-enriched delayed second infall episode can explain the
two sequences in the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] relation observed in
the thick and thin discs (high- and low-α sequences, respec-
tively) from APOGEE DR17 data. However, to have a clear
bimodality in the [Mg/Fe] distributions, we need a very old
thick disc coupled with a pre-enriched delayed second infall
episode for creating the low-α sequence, and this is obtained
with the revised model. By considering pre-enriched and de-

mation rate for the thin disc can well reproduce the data also
with the inclusion of bursts; on the other hand, a prolonged
star formation history for the thick disc is not compatible
with its observed stellar age distribution of a very old popu-
lation of stars.
In summary, Galactic chemical evolution models are fundamen-
tal tools to follow in detail the chemical evolution of our home
Milky Way in comparison with the data for chemical abundances
and stellar ages. In particular, our proposed chemical evolution
scenario can explain i) a co-evolution phase between the Galac-
tic high- and low-α sequences, and ii) the presence of old stars
in the low-α sequence, as in the selected observational sample.
Nevertheless, further observational data are really fundamental
to put more stringent constraints on the timescale for the for-
mation of the Galactic disc, in particular in the earliest Galactic
phases, which still represent a hot topic in Galactic archaeology
(see e.g. Zhang et al. 2024 and references therein) To this pur-
pose, the high-precision of HAYDN (Miglio et al. 2021b) could
be fundamental. Other facilities such as a Wide-field Spectro-
scopic Telescope (WST, Mainieri et al. 2024) can help to con-
strain the formation timescale of the Galactic disc (in particular
see Bergemann et al. 2025).

Appendix A: Model for a massive dwarf galaxy
In our model for the Galactic thick and thin discs, by following
the approach described in Spitoni et al. (2024), we consider that
the Galactic disc has been built both from primordial gas and
pre-enriched one. In order to get our pre-enrichment levels, we
then run the chemical evolution of a massive dwarf galaxy which
is then accreted after a certain evolutionary time and provides
some level of pre-enrichment. We consider for such a galaxy
a one-infall model with parameters consistent with the ones of
Vincenzo et al. (2019), where they proposed a first model for
the chemical evolution of Gaia-Enceladus. This model is char-
acterized by a low star formation efficiency (ν=0.6 Gyr−1), short
infall timescale (τ=0.24 Gyr) and the presence of a galactic wind
(loading factor ω=2), which allows to reproduce the abundance
pattern as well as the metallicity distribution function of Gaia-
Enceladus stars (Vincenzo et al. 2019; Cescutti et al. 2020; Spi-
toni et al. 2024). In Fig. A.1, we show the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] pre-
dicted for our massive dwarf galaxy model, where we can then
obtain a good agreement between model predictions and obser-
vations.
Appendix B: Separate analysis for thick and thin
disc models
In Fig. B.1, we show the observed and predicted [α/Fe] vs.
[Fe/H] and [α/Fe] vs. age diagrams for the high- and low-α se-
quences, separately. In fact, the strenght of the parallel approach
is the possibility to follow the evolution of the two components
separately. We divide the data from Borbolato et al. (2025) into
high- and low-α sequences using a classical chemical separation,
as first suggested by Adibekyan et al. (2012), and we compare
to predictions for our chemical evolution models. In the upper
panels, we show the observed and predicted [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H]
diagrams for the high- (left) and low-α (right) sequences, sepa-
rately; we can see that the bulk of high- (left) and low-α (right)
data are reproduced, as well as their MDFs and [Mg/Fe] distri-
butions, with the thick disc being α-enhanced and more metal-
poor with respect to the thin disc. To reproduce the metal-rich
tail of the thin disc MDF, there can be some radial migration ef-
fects and stars coming from the inner part of the Galaxy. With
the thick disc model, we have also some thick-disc stars at the
higher metallicities, as discussed in Grisoni et al. (2017) even if
there are few thick-disc stars at those metallicities; the combined
MDF, as shown in Fig. 4, is well reproduced.
In Fig. B.2, we show the observed and predicted [α/Fe] vs.
age diagrams for the high- (left) and low-α (right) sequences,
separately; we can see that the bulk of high- (left) and low-α
(right) data are reproduced, as well as their age distributions and
[Mg/Fe] distributions, with the thick disc a very old population
peaked at 11-12 Gyr ago (as also suggested by several age stud-
ies, e.g. Miglio et al. 2021a; Queiroz et al. 2023). The thick disc
[Mg/Fe] distribution is reproduced, with few stars reaching also
low-[Mg/Fe] values at old ages; those can be the old low-α. Al-
ternatively, the old low-α stars can be explained with our chem-
ical evolution model of the thin disc; in fact, the age distribution
of the thin disc is well reproduced with our thin disc model.
