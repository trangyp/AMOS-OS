We investigate electronic instabilities in 2H-TaS2 and a self-intercalated variant, 2H†-Ta1+δS2. In conven-
tional samples, which we determine to be slightly hole-doped, spectral gaps and backfolded features are found
as fingerprints of the 3 × 3 charge density wave (CDW). Notably, the backfolded features emerge only at a
temperatures below T ≈65 K, substantially lower than the established CDW temperature of 78 K, suggest-
ing an incommensurate-commensurate lock-in transition analogous to the phenomenology of the 2H-TaSe2.

Tuning through different many-body ground states as a
function of band occupation is a hallmark of correlated
electron systems. Metallic transition metal dichalcogenides
(TMDs) provide a particularly fertile platform in this regard,
hosting intertwined charge density wave (CDW), and super-
conducting order that can be tuned through pressure, strain,
doping, and chemical substitution [1]. The advent of artificial
heterostructures has further expanded this landscape, enabling
interface-driven modifications of their electronic structure and
collective states [2]. However, there is increasing recogni-
tion that the actual band filling may not reflect the expecta-
tion based on the nominal sample stoichiometry [3, 4]. Subtle
adjustments of the band filling can drive Lifshitz transitions,
reshape the Fermi surface, and profoundly influence both su-
perconductivity and competing CDW instabilities [5, 6].
Bulk 2H-TaS2 is one of the canonical CDW systems. The
literature establishes a 3×3 CDW transition temperature ≈75-
78 K [1, 7]. Superconductivity has been reported with a Tc
of ≈1 K [8], but with a dome-shaped enhancement to over
4 K when the CDW is suppressed by doping [9, 10]. How-
ever, the extent of CDW suppression depends on the nature
of doping. For example, alkali and transition metal interca-
lation of 2H-TaX2 (X=S, Se) weakens the lattice instability
via electron doping [1, 11] merely due to chemical pressure
of the intercalant [12]. In contrast, self-intercalation does not
suppress the CDW order completely but stabilises other com-
mensurate orders such as 2 × 1, 2 × 2, and the

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Fig. 1(a) shows the crystal structure of 2H-TaS2 consisting
of two trigonal prismatically stacked layers which are alter-
nately rotated by 180◦, with the Ta atoms directly on top of
each other, following the 2Ha stacking [27]. The Fermi sur-
face measured in the high temperature undistorted phase at
102 K (left half of Fig. 1(b)) contains 3 closed quasi-2D Fermi
surfaces: a central “circular" pocket around Γ, twofold sym-
metric “dogbone” pockets centered at M, and a single closed
“barrel” around the K-points. Contrastingly, at a temperature
of 25 K, the K-barrel is completely gapped, and the dogbone
pockets develop partial spectral gaps, due to the formation of
the 3×3 CDW [20].
This normal state Fermi surface topology, although familiar
from the literature on bulk 2H-TaSe2 [3, 19, 28], differs qual-
itatively from the Fermi surface calculated by density func-
tional theory (DFT; see Methods). As shown in Fig. 1(c), DFT
instead predicts two closed Fermi surfaces around each of the
Γ and K points. Instead, the calculated constant energy map
taken 50 meV below the Fermi level in Fig. 4(d) reproduces
the dogbone topology qualitatively well.
The change in Fermi surface connectivity with band filling
is known as a Lifshitz transition, which in this case is linked
to whether a saddle point in the electronic structure found
near 1/2ΓK is above or below EF [6]. While the observed
"dogbone" band topology implies hole-doping in our samples
compared with the filling of exactly one electron per Ta in
the calculation, the data is in agreement with other ARPES
reports on 2H-TaS2 [22, 23], which also report the dogbone
topology. The data in Fig. 1 can be therefore be considered to
correspond to conventional 2H-TaS2 samples.
The temperature-dependent Fermi surfaces shown in
Fig.2(a-f) offer deeper insights into the spectral properties of
the 3×3 phase conventional 2H-TaS2. At low temperatures,
the 3×3 order leaves clear fingerprints in the Fermi surface.
First, the dogbone pocket becomes partially gapped. Second,
along the K −M −K line the dogbone is not gapped but in-
stead reconstructs into very small “triangular" pockets with
“half-moon" like intensity as indicated by black arrows in
Fig. 2(a). The center of these triangular pockets corresponds
to the equivalent of the K-point in the 3×3 mini Brillouin
zones, and their construction can be understood by consider-
ing backfolding of sections of Fermi surface by the wavevec-
tor 2/3ΓM (see Supplementary Figure 1). These triangles
are thus a hallmark of 3×3 order. Thirdly, the inner barrel
pocket around the K-point exhibits no spectral weight at all,
appearing to be completely gapped.
The most striking feature of our measurements in Fig. 2,
however, is not the low-temperature data, but rather the inter-
mediate temperature regime. The data at 65 K, though nomi-
nally in the CDW phase, is spectrally much more similar to the
normal state. Instead, the main spectral signatures of the 3×3
phase become only visually apparent in the Fermi surface at

exhibiting an identical intensity profile to that of the MDC at

another ARPES system, eliminating instrumental temperature
calibration as a possible explanation.
The mismatch in the transition temperature of 75-78 K
reported in the literature and the apparent onset temper-
ature in ARPES demands explanation.
Intriguingly, the
phenomenology is reminiscent of 2H-TaSe2 where there are
known to be two transitions: a higher temperature transition
at 122 K from a normal state to a slightly incommensurate
3×3, followed by the so-called lock-in transition at 90 K.
The former exhibits sharper anomalies in the heat capacity
and resistivity [29], whereas the lock-in transition is most
clearly seen in diffraction studies [16, 25]. Most pertinently,
Borisenko et al. [20] pointed out that the main electronic
reconstruction features as seen by ARPES occur only below
the lower 90 K lock-in transition.
Returning to the case of 2H-TaS2 in question here, while
the CDW is usually stated as remaining incommensurate, the
experimental evidence is sparse and mixed; a persistently
incommensurate scenario was proposed based on electron
diffraction [24, 30], but other techniques found it to be at
least locally commensurate at low temperatures [8, 31, 32],
which is also implied by the observation of quantum oscil-
lations [33].
A possible interpretation of our data, there-
fore, would be to support a previously unreported lock-in type
phase transition between 55 and 65 K in 2H-TaS2 analogous
to the sister compound 2H-TaSe2, becoming at least locally
commensurate.

at 78 K reported in the literature [34], and also confirmed
on our samples (Supplementary Figure 2), implies that there
should be some loss of carrier density, or gapping of states
at EF , already at TCDW. Interestingly, careful inspection of

The variety and tunability of ground states that appear in
the calculations motivates a search for possible alternative
charge density wave ground states in TaS2 samples at differ-
ent doping. In samples nominally of the 4Hb-TaS2 polytype,
we found several inclusion regions in our spatial map where
the stacking close to the surface was 2H-like. The occurrence
of such stacking faults is common, and has been reported in
X-ray diffraction and STM studies [37, 38]. Moreover, recent
photoemission studies involving some of the authors of this
work were able to identify regions having 2H-like stacking
beneath a T-layer on the surface of 4Hb-TaS2 [39].
In what follows, we shall focus on this inclusion region with
2H-like stacking (which we label as 2H†) that shows a remark-
ably different CDW reconstruction, shown in Fig. 4 (a), com-
pared to the regular 2H-TaS2. First, we justify the assignment
of the region based on the Ta 4f core levels shown in Fig.4(d).
The bulk of this crystal was 4Hb-TaS2, in which case both H-
and T- terminated regions can be observed [39–42]. 4Hb-H
terminations yield, in addition to the strongest doublet from
the H termination, a weaker split peak structure that is well-
understood to arise from the Star of David reconstruction of
the subsurface T layers [42, 43]. However, these are absent
on the 2H† region, implying an absence of T layers. Instead,
only sharp peaks with binding energy corresponding to the
trigonal prismatic (H) coordination are present. This leaves
the possibility of 2H or 3R stacking, but given that there is a
band splitting along the Γ −M direction in Fig. 4(f), which
is highly suppressed in the 3R stacking [44], we assign this
region as 2H stacking. We reproducibly found that crystals
from this batch exhibited similar inclusion regions at the few
percent level (Supplementary Figure 4).
The electronic properties of this 2H† region are quite unlike
the 2H-TaS2 data reported until now. It is apparent that the
normal/undistorted state of the 2H† phase would have a Fermi
surface with two barrels around the K point. The "dogbone"
topology is found only in constant energy maps well below the
Fermi level, such as in the constant energy contour 95 meV
below EF in Fig. 4(c). The Γ −K cut shown in Fig. 4(g) in-
dicates that both saddle points are occupied in 2H†, compared
with only one in with the conventional 2H sample (Fig. 4(k)).
This region is therefore clearly substantially electron-doped.
To quantify this, in Fig. 4(h,l) we extract the Fermi contours
from the data, interpolating across the regions showing spec-
tral gaps to access the effective band structure of an undis-
torted 1 × 1 phase. We fit these with symmetry-appropriate
functional forms (Supplementary Figure 5) and integrate the
areas for the Luttinger count. We find a filling of 1.16 e−per
Ta for the 2H†, contrasting with 0.97 e−per Ta for the con-
ventional 2H-TaS2 samples.
A hint at a chemical explanation for the electron doping
comes from a second look at the Ta 4f core levels in Fig. 4(d):
in contrast to the broader double-shoulder seen in the conven-
tional 2H region, for 2H† there is instead a single minority
peak. We suggest that this phenomenology could be due to
a partial filling of the Ta interstitial site as the primary defect
structures [45]. The sharper main peaks, together with the ex-
tremely low background in the ARPES measurements, would
align with this, since interstitial Ta would cause less in-plane
disorder compared with in-plane vacancies or inclusions. A
secondary effect of the interstitial filling may be to increase

SUMMARY AND OUTLOOK
To summarise, we have first shown that conventional 2H-
TaS2 with the familiar dog-bone Fermi surface is in fact
slightly hole-doped compared to a hypothetical stoichiometric
2H-TaS2, and exhibits 3×3 CDW order. Our temperature de-
pendent ARPES measurements present a compelling case for
an incommensurate to commensurate (lock-in) CDW transi-
tion that is accompanied by spectral gaps in the K-barrel and
the dogbone pockets. Our ab initio calculations align with
this argument, with an instability found along Γ −M close
to q = 2/3ΓM, although the leading q is strongly doping-
dependent. In contrast, our measurements on self-intercalated
and electron doped 2H†-TaS2 reveal a previously unreported
CDW, a 2
√
3×2
√
3R(30◦) phase corresponding to a 3q or-
der with qCDW = 1/2ΓK. We simulated this scenario by
assuming 2H-TaS2 with an elongated c-axis. In such a case,
we do diagnose an instability at q = 1/2ΓK, although in the
calculations it is stabilised with hole doping rather than the
electron doping found in the experimental sample. The pre-
diction of a CDW order along this wavevector has been found
in previous works in the form of soft LA phonon modes [55]
and peaks in the electronic susceptibility across a wide dop-
ing range [6] but has not received much attention since there
was no experimental confirmation of such a phase until now.
While the leading instability still lies along Γ −M in all
cases, this is not necessarily a matter of concern because at
low temperatures, the enhancement in the phonon self-energy
at q = 1/2ΓK may suppress other instabilities in other re-
gions of the Brillouin zone (see Ref. [56]), and for our sys-
tem, we leave this for future inquiry. Nevertheless, we high-
light that a new, and previously overlooked CDW order may
be anticipated by modifying the interlayer spacing, and dop-
ing. Importantly, the 2

Methods
Experimental Methods.
Samples of conventional TaS2
were purchased from HQ graphene, who used a chemical va-
por transport growth method. The crystals used to produce the
datasets in Fig. 4 of the main text, and Supplementary Figure
3 were also grown by chemical vapour transport, with iodine
as the transport agent. Stoichiometric amounts of tantalum
and sulfur were loaded in quartz ampules and vacuum sealed.
The 2H-TaS2 crystals were grown in a two zone tube furnace
with the hot/cold zones at 800◦and 700◦C, respectively, for
12 days. The furnace was then cooled to room temperature
at a rate of 0.1 C/minute. The procedures for the growth of
4Hb-TaS2 have been reported in Ref. [42]. ARPES measure-
ments were performed on the nano branch of the I05 beamline.
The typical energy resolution was 20 meV. TaS2 samples have
notoriously "baklava"-like surfaces, especially after cleavage,
with thin an often bent flakes appearing at the surface, ne-
cessitating the use of micro-ARPES combined with careful
searching for areas exhibiting sharp features.
Theory Methods.
The first-principle calculations were
performed using plane-wave density functional theory (DFT)
as implemented in the QUANTUM ESPRESSO package [57,
58]. The ion-electron interactions were described by fully-
relativistic norm-conserving pseudopotentials compiled from
the PSEUDO-DOJO library [59]. The exchange-correlation
functional was described by the Perfew-Becke-Erzenhoff
(PBE) paramterisation of the generalised gradient approxima-
tion (GGA) [60]. We used a kinetic energy cutoff of 100 Ry,
and a 12×12×4 Monkhorst-Pack [61] k-mesh for Brillouin
zone integration, and a smearing of approximately 0.136 eV.
To preserve the in-plane symmetries of 2H-TaS2, the Ta po-
sitions were kept fixed, whereas only the z-coordinate of the
S atoms was allowed to relax until all the Hellmann-Feynman
forces on the atoms were less than 10−5 Ry/Bohr. The ab
initio phonon dispersion was then calculated over q-grid of
6 × 6 × 2 points.
A tight-binding model was then constructed using maximally
localised Wannier functions projected over Ta-dx2−y2, dxy,
and dz2 orbitals (active subspace) to capture the essential
features of the DFT Fermi surface. The doping dependent
renormalised phonon dispersions were calculated using the
Calandra-Profeta-Mauri (CPM) scheme [62, 63] where the
phonon self-energy (Πqν) obtained from density functional
perturbation theory (DFPT) is patched by subtracting the
phonon self-energy of the active subspace (ΠA
qν) calculated
over a coarse k-mesh, and subsequently adding ΠA
qν calcu-
lated over a fine k-mesh of 84×84×28 points, and the de-
sired chemical potential µ. The phonon self-energy calcula-
tions were performed using Eq. 1 as implemented in the EPW
package [64],
