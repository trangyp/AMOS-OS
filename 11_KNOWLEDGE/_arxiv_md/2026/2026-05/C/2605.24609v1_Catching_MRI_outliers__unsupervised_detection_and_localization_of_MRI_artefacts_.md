Abstract  
Background and purpose: Artificial intelligence is increasingly integrated into 
radiotherapy workflows, yet such pipelines remain vulnerable to out-of-distribution image 
data that can introduce unexpected behavior in clinical tasks. Deep learning (DL)-based 
anomaly detection for pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) remains today unexplored 
and transparent evaluation for full automation feasibility is lacking. This study aims to develop 
and evaluate a fully automated, unsupervised anomaly-detection framework in radiotherapy 
for both pelvic and brain MRI.  
Materials and methods: A two-stage unsupervised DL-framework was trained on 
anomaly-free images from three public datasets: LUND-PROBE (pelvis), IXI and fastMRI 
(brain). The first stage compressed MRI slices into discrete tokens, and a second stage 
modeled the distribution of normal tokens. Anomaly evidence was obtained by combining 
perceptual image differences with token surprisal scores via negative log-likelihood 
estimation. 
Automated detection for pelvis, including synthetic global and real clinical anomalies and for 
brain, including clinically annotated anomalies from fastMRI+, was performed by evaluating 
sensitivity, specificity, and area under the curve (AUC). For both cohorts, a subset of normal 
cases was held out for false-positive analysis. 
Results: High robust performance across hidden evaluation cohorts was obtained, 
achieving an AUC of 0.97 (95% CI, 0.95–0.98) and 0.81 (95% CI, 0.74–0.87) on pelvic and 
brain images, respectively. Qualitative heatmap analysis showed strong spatial agreement 
between the detected anomalies and ground-truth locations, confirming localization accuracy. 
Conclusions: The framework demonstrated robust and generalizable anomaly detection 
performance across pelvic and brain MRI, supporting its potential for automated MRI quality 
control in radiotherapy. Heatmap-based visualization further improved model interpretability 
by spatially localizing detected anomalies, thereby enabling transparent and reliable 
assessment.

1 Introduction  
Artificial intelligence (AI) is rapidly being integrated into radiotherapy workflows to automate 
tasks such as synthetic image generation, segmentation, treatment planning, and clinical 
decision support [1–5]. By improving efficiency, reproducibility, and throughput while reducing 
manual workload, the use of AI-tools has the potential to further streamline clinical care [6,7]. 
However, safe implementation of such tools depends on robust image-based quality control 
(QC) that remains reliable not only under routine conditions, but also when exposed to 
unexpected inputs [8–11]. A limitation of deep learning (DL) models is their sensitivity to data 
distribution shift, whereby images that differ from the training data can lead to degraded or 
unreliable results [11–14]. Such out-of-distribution (OOD, or anomalous) cases may arise 
from artefacts in acquisition, anatomical variations, pathological findings, protocol deviations, 
or scanner differences. If left undetected, they may produce unreliable outputs and increase 
the risk of downstream clinical errors. Robust and automated image-level anomaly detection 
can thereby act as a QC safety layer for automated radiotherapy workflows. 
DL-based anomaly detection in magnetic resonance (MR) imaging has emerged as an active 
field of research, with most studies to date focusing on brain imaging [15–20]. Many of these 
approaches use unsupervised, normal-data-only training to learn a reference representation 
of healthy anatomy and then identify anomalies as deviations from the learned distribution. 
From a clinical perspective, this strategy is appealing, as it does not require collecting a 
dataset for every anomaly that could be potentially encountered in clinical practice, thus 
acting as a safeguard against both known and unknown anomaly sources.  
Despite previous efforts, important knowledge gaps remain. First, most published anomaly 
detection studies have focused on brain MR-images, leaving pelvic imaging, specifically for 
prostate cancer radiotherapy, largely unexplored. This gap is clinically relevant as prostate 
radiotherapy is undergoing two major shifts: an increasing reliance on pelvic MR-imaging 
(such as MRI-only workflows and MR-guided treatments) and a rapid transition toward DL-
driven care, with automated image reconstruction and generation, segmentation, and 
treatment planning solutions already deployed in clinical settings [21–27]. These advanced 
workflows depend entirely on the quality and superior soft-tissue contrast of MR images for 
accurate target and organ-at-risk delineation, making the lack of image-based anomaly 
detection a significant vulnerability. 
Second, available anomaly detection frameworks have rarely been assessed in the context 
of radiotherapy, where both local (region-specific) and global (whole-image) anomalies need 
to be reliably detected. In radiotherapy, unlike diagnostic radiology, multiple interdependent 
tasks exist and depend on the accuracy of each other. Consequently, anomalies must be 
found prior to all tasks, at the imaging stage. If undetected, errors can accumulate and 
propagate through subsequent clinical steps, introducing severe dosimetric uncertainties and 
compromising treatment delivery [28,29]. 
Third, limited attention has been directed towards assessing the false-positive burden and 
low specificity, despite their importance for evaluating automated anomaly detection 
frameworks. Methods that achieve high sensitivity at the expense of low specificity may 
generate excessive false alarms, increase the workload of clinical staff, and undermine the 
reliability and practical value of fully automated detection frameworks. 
To address the critical vulnerabilities, this study aimed to develop and evaluate an automated 
anomaly-detection framework for MR-guided radiotherapy, trained exclusively on normal 
data. The framework was comprehensively evaluated on pelvic MR-images from prostate

2 Materials and Methods  
2.1 Patient cohorts 
The LUND-PROBE dataset was selected for pelvis MRI analysis. The dataset comprise T2-
weighted (T2w) imaging data from a single 3T GE Healthcare MRI scanner from 467 prostate 
cancer patients, treated within an MRI-only radiotherapy workflow at Skåne University 
Hospital (Lund, Sweden).  
The LUND-PROBE cohort was randomly split into training (n=384), validation (n=29), and 
test (n=54) for framework development and evaluation. Cases with hydrogel rectal spacers 
(n=5) were extracted from the test cohort to include as clinical anomalies. Global MRI 
anomalies were synthetically generated on a copy of the anomaly-free test set, including 
noise, ghosting, Fourier k-space spikes, motion, and blur artefacts. The intensity of global 
artefacts was randomly generated, and simulation details are provided in Section 9 
(Supplementary) and public code repository (Section 2.8). Further details on all included 
anomaly categories are provided in Table 1. Furthermore, we additionally included a clinical 
cohort comprising retrospective pelvic MR cases with a variety of anomalies (n=31). This 
included: prostate cancer patients with hip implants (n=4 scanned with the same protocol as 
LUND-PROBE, and n=4 with different protocols); female cervical brachytherapy patients 
(n=4); and images with a cropped scanning field of view (n=14). To expand the range of 
clinical anomalies, MRI scans of a healthy volunteer (n=6; 5 with global anomalies, one 
normal) were also included, acquired with same or different acquisition protocol compared to 
LUND-PROBE.  
For brain MRI, we used two publicly available T1-weighted (T1w) datasets: IXI, fastMRI, and 
fastMRI+ annotations. IXI was used solely as a training cohort (n=581) and comprise 
volumetric brain scans from healthy participants acquired at three hospitals in London, 
United Kingdom. From the fastMRI, following the fastMRI+ provided annotations (bounding-
box, or global label) we identified and included both normal (n=172, for training) and 
anomalous scans (n=255, for testing). The included anomaly categories are listed in Table 1 
and based on the official fastMRI+ records. Normal cases were randomly split into training 
(n=112), validation (n=30) and test (n=30), whereas anomalous cases (n=255) were reserved 
exclusively for testing. The annotations in the fastMRI+ dataset were labeled by an expert 
board-certified radiologist. All included scans were acquired on either 1.5T or 3T MRI 
systems with variations in acquisition protocols and number of axial slices. A subgroup of 
global anomalies labeled small vessel chronic white matter ischemic change (n=30) was 
excluded as no bounding-box annotations were available, and lesion localization could not 
be reliably visually verified.

2.3 Deep learning framework 
A two-stage unsupervised anomaly detection framework was developed and trained 
exclusively on normal images. Stage 1 learned a compact, discrete, and condensed 
representation of normal images, whereas Stage 2 learned the distribution of normal images 
from the condensed information provided by Stage 1. At inference, images were flagged as 
anomalous if both representations and perceptual appearance deviated from the learned 
normal distribution. Two separate models were trained using the pelvic and brain data. An 
overview of the framework is shown in Figure 1; full architectural and configuration details 
are provided in Section 1 (Supplementary). Training data augmentation techniques and 
image preprocessing details for both the pelvis and brain experiments are provided in 
Sections 3 and 5 (Supplementary).   
2.3.1 Stage 1: Learning a discrete representation of anatomy   
In Stage 1, each MR-image was encoded by a residual vector-quantized variational 
autoencoder (RVQ-VAE) with a Vision Transformer (ViT) encoder (Fig.1a). The encoder 
mapped each slice to two hierarchical discrete token-codebooks, L1 and L2. The L1-
codebook captured coarse anatomical structure, while L2 captured fine residual texture 
details. The L1-and L2-codebooks were decoded through a convolution-based decoder to 
reconstruct the encoded image. In this way, the RVQ-VAE learned a compact discrete 
representation (tokens) of normal MR anatomy that was more efficient to model than raw 
image pixels 
2.3.2 Stage 2: Learning a distribution of normal reference tokens 
In Stage 2, the distribution of tokens from standard images was learned using a factorized 
bidirectional transformer (Fact-biT) via masked token modeling (Fig.1a), inspired by the 
MaskGIT framework [34]. During training, a subset of tokens was randomly masked while the 
remaining tokens were retained as context. The model learned to iteratively predict the 
masked tokens from their spatial arrangement and neighboring token information. Hence, 
anatomically abnormal regions were increasingly difficult to predict. Masking and prediction 
were applied hierarchically: masked L1-tokens were predicted from remaining L1-context, 
with L2 held fully masked, whereas L2-tokens were predicted conditioned on predicted L1. 
This factorization encouraged the separation of global anatomical structure (L1) from finer 
texture (L2) and allowed the framework to iteratively predict anatomically likely tokens at any 
masked location based on the learned distribution of normal images.

Fig. 1. Anomaly detection framework for Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)-based 
radiotherapy quality control (QC). The framework flags anomaly images in MR-scans before 
workflow tasks. Training (a) is self-supervised on anomaly-free images. In Stage 1, a residual 
vector-quantized variational autoencoder (RVQ-VAE) with a Vision Transformer encoder 
(ViT) compressed each slice into two discrete token codebooks (L1, L2) and reconstructed it 
through a decoder (Dec). In Stage 2, a factorized bidirectional transformer (Fact-biT) learns 
to recover randomly masked tokens. Inference combines two complementary signals. 
Pathway A (b), perceptual difference scoring: checkerboard-masked tokens are iteratively 
healed by Fact-biT and decoded into a healed image; the Learned Perceptual Image Patch 
Similarity (LPIPS) difference between input and healed image (or reconstructed, x̂ Reconstructed, 
depending on task) is converted into a Z-score map (Zscore-map) using per-pixel statistics 
calibrated on a normal reference population. Multiplying the perceptual difference by the 
binarized Zscore-map yields the anomaly localization map (ALM), later thresholded to give 
anomaly map A. Pathway B (c) token surprisal: tokens are repeatedly masked at random, 
and the Fact-biT negative log-likelihood map is averaged, resized, thresholded, and 
binarized. High-surprisal regions, after thresholding, form anomaly map B. (d) The slice-level 
anomaly score is the total pixel count of maps A and B. Further details on the model designs 
and contents of Anomaly maps (A) and (B) are provided in Sections 1 and 2 in 
Supplementary materials. 
 
2.4 Inference  
At inference, Stage 1 served two functions: (1) obtain the reconstructed image, x̂ , from the 
input image, x, and (2) generate the L1-and L2-levels, used as inputs to Stage 2. An anomaly 
score was then obtained from a combination of two complementary pathways (A and B) as 
shown in Fig.1b-d. 
2.4.1 Pathway A: Perceptual scoring 
The input was first encoded into tokens by Stage 1, tokens where then partially masked in a 
fixed checkerboard pattern and restored by Fact-biT (Stage 2), allowing it to generate a 
pseudo-normal version of the input, referred to here as the healed image, x̂ Healed (Fig.1b). 
Because Fact-biT was trained only on normal images, the perceptual differences between 
input and healed/reconstructed images represented regions that differed from the learned 
normal distribution. Perceptual differences were quantified using Learned Perceptual Image 
Patch Similarity (LPIPS) [35] using a Visual Geometry Group (VGG) backbone, yielding a 
perceptual difference map.  
We calibrated a per-pixel normal reference by computing the mean (μ) and standard 
deviation (σ) of LPIPS-values at every pixel across a held-out randomly selected cohort of 
normal slices from the validation set. At inference, the perceptual difference map was 
converted to a Z-score map, Zscore-map = (LPIPS − μ)/(σ). The Zscore-map was thresholded (using 
either a one- or two-sided thresholding criterion) and binarized, producing a Zscore-map-mask 
that isolated pixels whose perceptual error exceeded what was normal for that anatomical 
location. The anomaly localization map (ALM) was obtained by multiplying the perceptual-
difference map by the Zscore-map-mask, highlighting the region of the perceptual deviation. This 
spatial localization improves interpretability by enabling visual inspection of suspicious 
regions contributing to the anomaly score. Thresholding the ALM yielded the binary anomaly 
map A.

2.4.2 Pathway B: Token surprisal  
For each image, the L1-codebook went through a series of runs using random masking 
patterns (Fig.1c). In each run, a random subset of tokens was hidden, and Fact-biT predicted 
the full L1-token at every hidden position; L2 was held as masked context, forcing the model 
to reason from the L1-context alone. At each position, the negative log-likelihood (NLL) that 
the model assigned to the token in the input was recorded (a measure of how surprising the 
true token was based on the learned distribution). NLL-values were accumulated only at 
positions that were masked in each trial and averaged across trials. The resulting token 
surprisal map was bilinearly upsampled to the initial image resolution, thresholded and 
binarized to produce the anomaly map B. High-surprisal regions corresponded to tokens that 
the model found surprising despite surrounding context.  
2.4.3 Unified anomaly score 
Finally, the binary anomaly maps from pathways A and B were combined and summed into a 
unified slice-level anomaly score (Fig.1d). For patient-level decisions, slice scores were 
aggregated across all the selected slices of each scan. 
2.5 Experimental configuration: Pelvic MRI 
Pelvic scans in LUND-PROBE followed clinical protocol, centering scans around the pubic 
bone; 12 central slices (range 38-49) were selected for testing as they encompass the most 
clinically relevant information (e.g. prostate, bladder, rectum). Per-pixel LPIPS reference 
maps (μ, σ) and the token-surprisal threshold were calibrated on 15 randomly selected cases 
from the validation set. The perceptual heatmap was computed as LPIPS (x, x̂ Healed). 
Pathway A used a single-sided cut-off of Z > 2.0 on the smoothed Z-score map. 
Checkerboard masking used two complementary 2×2 block patterns, and test-time 
augmentation was performed via horizontal flipping, averaged into the perceptual difference 
map. Pathway B used 50 random-masking trials per slice at a masking ratio of 0.9 and an 
NLL threshold of 8.0. The patient-level score was the sum of all the 12 slice-level scores. 
2.6 Experimental configuration: Brain MRI  
For each patient and each anomaly category in the fastMRI T1w brain set, a single 
representative axial slice was selected, containing the largest radiologist-annotated bounding 
box, thereby capturing the most informative view of the lesion. In this way we reduce the risk 
of including edge-slices with partial volume effects and fair standardization of case difficulty 
with respect to size of anomaly.   
Per-pixel calibration baselines for Pathway A and the NLL threshold for Pathway B were 
estimated from 30 mid-axial slices of healthy cases drawn from the validation cohort. To 
reduce sensitivity to scanner- and protocol-dependent noise, the perceptual-difference map 
for brain MR-images was computed between the Stage-1 reconstruction and the healed 
image, LPIPS (x̂ , x̂ Healed), rather than LPIPS (x, x̂ Healed) as in the pelvic experiment. A two-
sided Z-score threshold (Z < −2.5 or Z > 6.0) was applied to the resulting map. Checkerboard 
masking used two complementary 4×4 block patterns, and test-time augmentation was 
performed via horizontal flipping, averaged into the perceptual difference map. Pathway B 
used T = 100 random-masking trials at a masking ratio of 0.8 and an NLL threshold of 5.0. 
The patient-level score was the single slice-level score.

2.7 Statistical analysis  
Discrimination of anomaly from the standard cases was assessed with receiver-operating-
characteristic (ROC) analysis across the full range of anomaly-score thresholds, trading 
sensitivity against false-positive rate (1 – specificity). Overall performance was summarized 
by the area under the ROC curve (AUC). We reported 95% percentile confidence intervals 
(CI) for AUC (2.5th to 97.5th percentiles) and a pointwise 95% ROC confidence band by 
evaluating stepwise true-positive rate (TPR) on a fixed false-positive rate (FPR) grid of 201 
points. The ROC-uncertainty was estimated using a nonparametric stratified bootstrap at 
patient level, resampling positive and negative cases separately with replacement for 2000 
replicates.  
2.8 Code availability 
All scripts used for anomaly simulation on LUND-PROBE, cohort filtering and case selection 
from IXI, fastMRI, fastMRI+, and for training and inference of the deep-learning framework 
are publicly available on our GitHub [36] for reproducibility and transparency. 
 
3 Results  
Results are reported for the pelvic and brain MRI datasets, with ROC–AUC over the full 
range of anomaly-score thresholds, in addition to qualitative anomaly heatmaps on held-out 
test sets. In all figures, the displayed heatmap is the combination (by addition) of the 
anomaly localization maps ALM (A) and ALM (B) (Fig.1b), normalized per-case to [0, 1] and 
overlaid on the input image.  
3.1 Pelvic MRI  
On the synthetic global and clinical test sets, the framework discriminated evaluated 
anomalies (Table 1) from normal images with an AUC of 0.97 (95 % CI 0.95–0.98; Fig. 2a). 
Qualitative heatmap overlays (Fig. 2b) demonstrate spatial accuracy on both synthetic and 
clinical test sets. Hip implants and brachytherapy applicators were correctly detected (red 
arrows). For global synthetic anomalies, the heatmaps were spatially diffused, consistent with 
anomalies that perturb wide regions of the image rather than a focal structure. From the 
ROC-curve, at a 5% false-positive rate, a sensitivity and specificity of 91.0% and 95.0% for 
synthetic and 90.9% and 95.0% for clinical sets was achieved respectively.

4 Discussion  
This study developed and evaluate an automated, unsupervised image-based anomaly 
detection framework for MRI QC in radiotherapy, trained exclusively on normal images and 
evaluated on publicly available, synthetic, and internal clinical datasets. To the best of our 
knowledge, this is the first application of DL-based anomaly detection for image-level MRI 
quality control in pelvic MRI for prostate radiotherapy workflows. Furthermore, we present the 
first comprehensive evaluation of the radiologist-annotated fastMRI+ and fastMRI T1w 
datasets in an automated anomaly detection setting. On the pelvic cohort, the framework 
achieved an AUC of 0.97 (95 % CI 0.95–0.98) across all global synthetic artefacts and 
clinical test sets. On the fastMRI, it achieved an AUC of 0.81 (95 % CI 0.74–0.87), 
preforming consistently despite variations of anatomical sites, multiple scanners, and 
heterogeneous imaging protocols.  
Our proposed framework is clinically relevant to radiotherapy, where workflows are moving 
towards end-to-end automation. With growing integration of synthetic CT (sCT) generation, 
auto-segmentation, automated treatment planning, and online MR-guided workflows, the 
safety of these pipelines depends critically on the quality and consistency of their input data. 
An MR-scan that deviates from the normal distribution on which downstream DL-models 
were trained may silently corrupt upcoming steps in the clinical workflow. Global image 
artefacts (motion, noise, etc.) and value-shifts (software or hardware updates) are potential 
sources of image quality degradations. Additionally, patients with implants, contrast agents, 
or uncommon anatomical morphology could also impose a challenge for DL-applications. 
Hence, an image-based anomaly detection framework that reliably flags such cases before 
reaching automated clinical tasks represents a practical safety layer for automated QC in 
MR-guided radiotherapy. 
In radiotherapy, anomaly detection has also been explored at other points in the clinical 
chain, including treatment-plan anomaly detection [37], automated contour quality assurance 
for MR-Linac adaptive workflows [38], and more recently image-level anomaly detection in 
Cone-beam CT-guided radiotherapy [39]. These studies support the broader view that 
anomaly detection can act as a safety mechanism in automated radiotherapy. 
Two design choices proved critical in this framework. First, the population-based Z-score 
calibration using only normal images provided a spatial prior to expected anatomical 
variability. This suppressed systematic false-positive activations in regions with naturally high 
variance (e.g. rectum and bladder filling in the pelvis, cortical folding in the brain), thereby 
only unusual variations were flagged. Second, the two scoring pathways (A and B) captured 
complementary views of normality. Token surprisal (Pathway B) was particularly sensitive to 
anomalies that introduced OOD token-patterns, such as hip implants on LUND-PROBE, and 
mass lesions on fastMRI+. Perceptual scoring (Pathway A) excelled at image-wide 
perturbations and diffuse findings, including motion, noise, and ghosting. Together they 
covered a spectrum of anomalies that neither pathway detected reliably on its own. 
Several limitations merit acknowledgement. First, the anomalies introduced into the LUND-
PROBE dataset were predominantly synthetic, and the clinical cohort was relatively small. 
Larger prospective clinical validation is therefore required before clinical use. Second, there 
is no universal anomaly-score threshold: site-, scanner-, and task-specific calibration is 
needed to set an operating point (acceptable false-positive rate decision). Third, all fastMRI+ 
annotations were provided by a single radiologist restricted to the axial plane view, which 
may introduce label uncertainty.

Moreover, earlier investigations of fastMRI+ T1w for anomaly detection restricted analysis to 
anomalies located at mid-axial slices, substantially limiting the clinical span of the evaluation 
[19,40]. In contrast, this study established a more comprehensive reference against which 
future methods may be benchmarked. 
The framework was flexible enough to accommodate diverse evaluation strategies, including 
heatmap-based segmentation of anomalies and hybrid schemes that combine global 
anomaly-scores with spatial heatmap information. Future extensions may include exploring 
vision–language models as a second opinion layer on flagged slices and evaluating the 
framework as an explicit automated QC-framework in downstream radiotherapy tasks to 
quantify error prevention in MR-guided radiotherapy workflows. 
In conclusion, we developed and evaluated an automated unsupervised MRI anomaly-
detection framework for radiotherapy applications trained exclusively on normal images. 
Across pelvic and brain MR-datasets, the framework achieved strong detection performance, 
underscoring its generalizability and highlighting its potential as an automated QC tool in AI-
enabled radiotherapy workflows.
