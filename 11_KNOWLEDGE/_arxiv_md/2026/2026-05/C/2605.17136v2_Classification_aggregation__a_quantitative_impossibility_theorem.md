Abstract
A group of individuals wishes to classify m objects into n categories in such a way that no class is
left empty, a condition known as surjectivity. The opinions of the individuals are aggregated separately
for each object using an aggregation function that can depend on the object.
Maniquet and Mongin showed that if the aggregation functions are unanimous and the outcome must
always be surjective, then the aggregation mechanism is dictatorial. Cailloux et al. showed that the same
holds even if unanimity is relaxed to citizen sovereignty (each object can be classified into any category).
We show that similar results hold even if we only require the outcome to be surjective with probability
1−ϵ (with respect to an arbitrary symmetric i.i.d. distribution), provided that the aggregation functions
are far from being constant.
On the way, we characterize all aggregation mechanisms whose outcome is always surjective without
any assumptions on the aggregation functions.
Our approach uses a general result of Alekseev and Filmus which has wider applicability. We illustrate
this by proving a similar impossibility result for aggregating equivalence relations.

Introduction
Arrow’s celebrated impossibility theorem [Arr50, Arr63] concerns elections among n ≥3 candidates in
which each voter has to rank all candidates, and the desired output is a ranking of all candidates. The
theorem states that the only voting mechanisms satisfying the two natural axioms IIA and Unanimity are
dictatorships.
One might hope that the issue goes away if we allow the outcome, encoded as a list of pairwise rankings
of the candidates, to be non-transitive (that is, not correspond to a ranking) with small probability (under
the impartial culture distribution). Kalai [Kal02] ruled this out for neutral mechanisms, showing that if
the probability of a non-transitive outcome is small, then the voting mechanism is close to a dictatorship.
Mossel [Mos12] replaced the assumption of neutrality by the assumption that the mechanism is far from
being constant. This assumption is necessary to rule out mechanisms which output a fixed ranking.
We prove a result in the style of Mossel in a different domain: classification aggregation. A group of
individuals wishes to classify m objects into n categories in such a way that no category is left empty, a
condition known as surjectivity. The opinions of the individuals are aggregated into a societal outcome on
an object-by-object basis, a constraint which mirrors the IIA axiom. This problem can be traced back to
Kasher and Rubinstein [KR97], who considered the case n = 2.
Maniquet and Mongin [MM14, MM16] showed that if m ≥n ≥3 and the mechanism satisfies Unanimity
(if all individuals classify object i into category j then the outcome also classifies i into category j) and
Surjectivity (the outcome is always surjective), then the mechanism is a dictatorship, up to a permutation
of the categories. Cailloux et al. [CHOS24] proved a similar result under the weaker assumption of Citizen
Sovereignty (for every object i and category j, there is an outcome which classifies i into category j), as well
as for m > n = 2. When m = n = 2, other mechanisms exist, and Cailloux et al. described all of them.

1.2
Impossibility domains without unanimity
Dokow and Holzman [DH09, DH10a] were interested in classifying predicates for which an impossibility
theorem in the style of Arrow holds.
Definition 1.7 (Impossibility domain). A predicate P ⊆{0, 1}m is an impossibility domain if the only unan-
imous polymorphisms of P are dictators. That is, if f1, . . . , fm : {0, 1}p →{0, 1} constitute a polymorphism
of P satisfying fi(j, . . . , j) = j for all i, j, then there exists k ∈[p] such that f1(x) = · · · = fm(x) = xk.
Theorem 1.8 (Dokow–Holzman, as refined by Szegedy–Xu). If a predicate P ⊆{0, 1}m is not an impossi-
bility domain then one of the following holds:
(i) x1 ⊕x2 ⊕x3, . . . , x1 ⊕x2 ⊕x3 is a (unanimous) polymorphism of P.
(ii) Maj(x1, x2, x3), . . . , Maj(x1, x2, x3) is a (unanimous) polymorphism of P.
(iii) There is a unanimous polymorphism f1, . . . , fm of P in which f1, . . . , fm ∈{x1 ∧x2, x1 ∨x2}.
Dokow and Holzman [DH10c] considered the case of larger alphabet under the stronger assumption of
supportiveness (fi(x1, . . . , xp) ∈{x1, . . . , xp}), but were unable to provide a complete classification. Szegedy
and Xu [SX15] used tools from universal algebra to complete the classification, and also to prove a similar
classification under unanimity.
Theorem 1.9 (Szegedy–Xu). If a predicate P ⊆Σm is not an impossibility domain with respect to support-
iveness, then this is witnessed by a polymorphism of arity p = 3.
If a predicate P ⊆Σm is not an impossibility domain with respect to unanimity, then this is witnessed by
a polymorphism of arity p = |Σ|.
Without unanimity, a new type of polymorphism arises: a certificate. In the case of Surjm,n, a polymor-
phism f1, . . . , fm is a certificate if for each j ∈[n], one of the fi’s is the constant j function.
Definition 1.10 (Trivial domain). A predicate P ⊆Σm is trivial if for every polymorphism f1, . . . , fm : Σp →
Σ, one of the following cases holds:
(i) f1, . . . , fm are dictatorial: There exists an index k ∈[p] and a permutation π on Σ such that
f1(x) = · · · = fm(x) = π(xk).
(ii) f1, . . . , fm are a certificate: There exists a partial mapping ρ from [m] to Σ such that
(a) ρ is a certificate: if xi = ρ(i) for all i ∈dom ρ then x ∈P.
(b) f1, . . . , fm conform to ρ: fi(x) = ρ(i) for all i ∈dom ρ and all x.
We say that P is p-trivial if the condition holds for a specific value of p.
Continuing our previous example, a certificate for Surjm,n is a partial mapping ρ from [m] to [n] which
is surjective. Functions f1, . . . , fm conform to ρ if fi is the constant ρ(i) function for any i ∈dom ρ.
Filmus [Fil26] proves a result in the style of Theorem 1.9 in this context. The result applies to predicates
satisfying two mild technical conditions.
Definition 1.11 (Degeneracy). A predicate P ⊆Σm is non-degenerate if
(a) P has full projections: for every i ∈[m] and σ ∈Σ there exists x ∈P such that xi = σ.
(b) P depends on all coordinates: for every i ∈[m] there are x ∈P and y /∈P which differ only on the i’th
coordinate.
Definition 1.12 (Vulnerability). A predicate P ⊆Σm is vulnerable if there exists i ∈[m] and σ ∈Σ such
that all x ∈Σm satisfying xi = σ belong to P.

Fishburn and Rubinstein [FR86] considered a different kind of classification problem, which also appears
later in Kasher and Rubinstein [KR97]. In this problem, a society of individuals is grouping a set of objects
into equivalence classes. Each individual encodes their classification by stating, for any two objects, whether
they are equivalent. The individual classifications are then aggregated by determining separately for any
two objects whether they are equivalent.

1.4
Related work
Theorem 1.1 falls under the umbrella of approximate judgment aggregation. Judgment aggregation [LP02,
LP09] arose from results such as the doctrinal paradox [KS86, LP02], which in our language states that all
unanimous polymorphisms of the predicate
P∧= {(a, b, a ∧b) : a, b ∈{0, 1}}
are oligarchies, that is, functions of the form f(x) = V
i∈S xi. The predicate P∧is truth-functional, meaning
that some coordinates are unconstrained, and the remaining ones are a function of the others.
There are several ways to relax the condition of polymorphicity. Theorem 1.1 relaxes consistency, which
states that the result of applying the aggregation mechanism always satisfies the predicate. There are several
papers analyzing Arrow’s theorem from this perspective [Kal02, Kel10, Kel12, Mos12]. Nehama [Neh13]
proved an approximate version of the doctrinal paradox, but the bounds in his result depend on the number
of voters. Filmus et al. [FLMM20] obtained an approximate doctrinal paradox in which the error parameters
are independent of the number of voters.
Chase et al. [CFM+22] proved a similar result for all truth-
functional predicates over {0, 1}, and Alekseev and Filmus [AF26] generalized this to arbitrary predicates
over {0, 1} and some predicates over larger alphabets.
Falik and Friedgut [FF14] considered a different relaxation, in which the aggregation mechanism is only
approximately coordinate-wise. They proved an approximate version of Arrow’s theorem and of the Gibbard–
Satterthwaite theorem [Gib73, Sat75] in this setting.
The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem assumes strategyproofness. Several papers prove approximate ver-
sions which relax this assumption [FKKN11, IKM12, MR15].

Discussion
Some impossibility theorems in the literature concern a situation in which the individual votes satisfy one
predicate, and the final outcome should satisfy a different predicate, potentially over a different alphabet.
Examples include:
• Gibbard [Gib69] considered the situation in which each individual specifies a weak order on [m], and
the desired outcome is a partial order on [m]. These correspond to the following predicates (in reverse
order):
– Partm ⊆{0, 1, ∗}(
m
2) is the set of partial orders, encoded as specifying, for any i < j, whether
i ≻j, i ≺j, or neither. We require transitivity:
i ≻j and j ≻k implies i ≻k.
– Weakm ⊆{0, 1, ∗}(
m
2) is the set of weak orders, which are partial orders specifying the additional
axiom
i ≻j implies i ≻k or k ≻j for each k ̸= i, j.
Gibbard shows that the only unanimous polymorphisms in this setting are weak oligarchies: there
exists a set S ⊆[p] such that

• Dokow and Holzman [DH10b] considered the situation in which each individual specifies a linear order
on [m], and the desired outcome is a partial order on [m]. We encode linear orders as:
– Linm ⊆{0, 1}(

They showed that the only unanimous polymorphisms in this setting are oligarchies: the outcome is
always a linear order, and there exists a set S ⊆[p] such that a ≻b iff a
k≻b for all k ∈S.
This extension of the concept of polymorphism appears in the literature on promise CSPs [AGH17, BG21,
BBKO21]. In this setting, we have two predicates P, Q, possibly on different alphabets, such that P is more
restrictive than Q, in the sense that there is a coordinate-by-coordinate mapping from P to Q. A (P, Q)
polymorphism is defined as in Definition 1.2, using P on the left and Q on the right.
Theorem 1.6 applies in this setting as well, where the flexibility condition is only needed for P (and is
required even if P is a predicate on {0, 1}). However, we don’t have an analog of Theorem 1.13. Indeed,
given the examples above, it seems that we should aim for oligarchies rather than dictators.
A different shortcoming of our framework is the unspecified dependence between ϵ and δ in Theorem 1.6.
In contrast to Mossel’s quantitative Arrow theorem [Mos12], where the dependence is linear, the dependence
in Theorem 1.6 is of tower type. We conjecture that the dependence can be improved to polynomial or even
linear.
