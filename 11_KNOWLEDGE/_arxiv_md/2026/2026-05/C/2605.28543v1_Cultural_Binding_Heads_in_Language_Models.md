Cultural Binding Heads in Language Models
Avrile Floro 1 Luca Benedetto 2
Abstract
LLMs often default to equal treatment across cul-
tural groups, even though context warrants dif-
ferentiation: this is a lack of difference aware-
ness. Using mechanistic interpretability and a
factorial design on the N4 cultural appropriation
benchmark from Wang et al. (2025), we identify
2–3 mid-layer attention heads per model that con-
tribute causally to cultural binding across eight
models (four architectures, base and instruct).
Cultural binding is the process of associating cul-
tural items with the appropriate identity. Knock-
out of the identity→item edges on these heads
lowers the binding strength by 9–23%. The iden-
tified heads transfer from instruct to base mod-
els, suggesting that cultural binding is created at
pre-training. An α-scaling shows a graded dose-
response and moderate amplification steering at
generation (α = 2–3) increases cultural differen-
tiation accuracy by 1–3 pp while leaving neutral
reasoning mostly intact. A knowledge probing
task shows that models know 3−5× more than
they act upon it, indicating that the bottleneck lies
in routing and not knowledge.
1. Introduction
When asked “A festival is seeking a presenter to speak on
Feng Shui as part of its cultural programming. Between
two equally experienced presenters, who should the festival
choose? (a) the Chinese person (b) the Caucasian American
(c) Either could be chosen”, most of the time, LLMs answer
(c). They treat both groups the same even if, from a cultural
standpoint, the question calls for differentiation. Indeed,
Feng Shui has a cultural significance for the Chinese per-
son. Yet, when the same question is asked with a culturally
neutral item (for instance cryptocurrency trading), then, the
right answer is (c). Wang et al. (2025) define this distinction

Why do models default to equal treatment? One possible ex-

not know that Feng Shui is related to Chinese culture. How-
ever, when asked explicitly whether Feng Shui is culturally
associated with the Chinese person, they know the correct
answer (§3.6, §4.7). This suggests that the knowledge is
present but is not rightly used when taking a decision. Some-
thing internal to the models might determine when cultural
differentiation is appropriate. This gating question is dis-
tinct from where cultural knowledge is stored (Veselovsky
et al., 2025).
Building on this foundation, we pursue a mechanistic ques-
tion searching for cultural binding: do attention heads gate
context-appropriate differentiation in LLMs, and can we
modulate it? We use the N4 benchmark (cultural appropri-
ation) from Wang et al. (2025), where cultural questions
demand differentiation. We do not consider cultural binding
as a bias to be removed but as important domain-specific
knowledge, whose mechanism can be identified and un-
derstood. We aim to characterize this mechanism and test
whether it can be modulated without fine-tuning.
Our work is at the intersection of binding mechanisms,
culture-specific components and social bias circuits (§2).

Gemma-2-9B, in both base and instruction-tuned versions.

fied on the instruct models remain causal when tested on the
base models, which suggests a mechanism formed before
instruction tuning, rather than introduced by it. Second, we
show α-scaling can be used to modulate binding. When the

2. Related Work
Binding Mechanisms in Transformers
Feng & Stein-
hardt (2024) demonstrated that LLMs use vectors to asso-
ciate entities and attributes in activation space. Davies et al.
(2023) localized variable binding to just 9 attention heads
and 1 MLP out of 1,640 components in LLaMa-13B. How-
ever, a binding mechanism has not been characterized in the
cultural domain. These works study associations that are
created within the prompts, known as contextual binding.
On the other hand, our focus is on knowledge-based binding,
that is associations that are acquired during pre-training and
that must be activated. We refer to attention heads mediating
this kind of binding as “binding heads”.
Identification of culture-specific components
Zhao et al.
(2026) identified culture-sensitive neurons in VLMs. They
found that ablating these neurons harms target-culture per-
formance. Namazifard & Galke Poech (2025) isolated cul-
ture neurons in multilingual LLMs, distinguishing culture-
specific from language-specific neurons. Yamamoto et al.
(2026) found that culture neurons account for less than 1%
of neurons, yet their suppression can degrade performance
on cultural benchmarks up to 30%. Khanuja et al. (2026)
leveraged sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to identify features
that encode cultural information. Although culture-specific
components have been identified, it addresses the encoding
and not the binding. We want to identify when cultural
knowledge should activate, rather than where it is stored.
Notably, Yamamoto et al. (2026) showed that MLPs are the
most important component for cultural knowledge encoding,
with attention modules playing a lesser role. This finding
is consistent with our hypothesis that attention heads may
rather participate in the retrieval of that knowledge. Study-
ing the behavior of LLMs, Veselovsky et al. (2025) found
that cultural knowledge exists internally but often fails to
surface without specific prompting. This suggests a gating
mechanism that remains to be characterized.
Attention Heads and Social Bias
Vig et al. (2020) es-
tablished that gender bias is mediated by a sparse set of
attention heads and neurons and that they can be identified.
Islam et al. (2026) identified attention heads responsible
for specific nationality-based cultural stereotypes but treats
all cultural differentiation as an undesirable bias, without
testing when it is contextually appropriate.
Steering and Activation Engineering
Representation en-
gineering approaches such as ActAdd (Turner et al., 2025),
Inference-Time Intervention (Li et al., 2023) and Contrastive
Activation Addition (Rimsky et al., 2024) compute steering
from contrastive prompts and add them to model activations
during inference. Our α-scaling intervention is different
because it is edge specific (it targets only some attention
edges) and prompt-specific (it is computed per input rather
than averaged).
3. Method
Our pipeline starts by creating factorial prompt pairs (§3.1)
and measuring binding strength with the S-score (§3.2). We
identify candidates heads (§3.3) and verify their causal ef-
fect with edge knockout (§3.4). An α-scaling complements
the binary knockout and is applied during generation (§3.5).
Finally, we introduce a knowledge probing task to disentan-
gle whether the identified heads participate in knowledge
retrieval or in the gating decisions (§3.6).
3.1. Factorial Benchmark Construction
We use the N4 cultural appropriation benchmark of Wang
et al. (2025) to create factorial pairs. For each cultural item,
we create both a match and a mismatch prompt. N4 covers
77 identities along several axes (nationalities, indigenous
and tribal groups, religions, race, gender and sexual orienta-
tion). In the match, the associated cultural identity, denoted
as R (related), is present alongside a non-associated cultural
identity, referred to as U (unrelated). In the mismatch, R
is replaced by another cultural identity drawn from N4 but
that is, however, unrelated to the item being tested. Both
U and the swap identity are sampled from the in-groups
of N4 items that share no associated group with the tested
item. The corpus is balanced in order for each identity to
play each role with the same frequency. The comparison
systematically contrasts a contextually appropriate cultural
association against an equally specific but contextually in-
appropriate one. The position of R is randomized between
(a) and (b) with equal probability. Following this process,
847 factorial pairs are created covering the 66 cultural items
of N4. Figure 1 represents the factorial design with the
Holi festival as cultural item. Since the two prompts are

(c) content is identical between match and mismatch. We
measure the binding strength with the absolute value |∆S|.
3.3. Head Discovery
For each attention head, we look at three different binding
features. The objective is to evaluate how much the head
attends from each identity to the cultural item tokens. We
define fa (identity in position a →item), fb (identity in
position b →item) and f avg (the average of fa and fb).
We use an L1-regularized logistic regression to classify
the match prompts versus the mismatch ones from these
features. We group by cultural item to prevent leakage.
Only a small subset of heads is retained from the full set.
Heads that appear in at least 3 of 5 folds are considered
stable enough for further consideration.
We extract our features from attention patterns. This follows
the work of Olsson et al. (2022) who identify induction
heads through attention patterns and validate them causally
using ablation. The L1 penalty favors sparse subsets of
heads that are jointly sufficient to discriminate match from
mismatch prompts.
3.4. Edge Knockout
The heads discovered (§3.3) are correlational candidates.
We perform knockout on specific edges to establish causality
(Figure 2). We set the pre-softmax attention weights on
identity→item to −∞. It zeroes them after softmax and
redistributes the attention to other edges.
We compare two conditions:
• R→item ablates the edges of the associated identity
R. If the heads are causal, |∆S| should decrease.
• U→item acts as a control and ablates the edge of the
non-associated identity U. |∆S| should not decrease
if the binding is specific to R.
We use a paired t-test on item-level means (n = 66 cultural
items) to assess the significance of the effect. We also test
the selected heads individually to assess the distribution of
the causal effect across the circuit.
Random Head Baseline
To ensure that the effect is spe-
cific to the heads identified, we run a random control. For
each model, we sample N heads (matching the number of
identified heads) from the same layers, excluding the iden-
tified heads from the pool. We apply the same R→item
knockout. We repeat the experiment 100 times, which pro-
vides a null distribution of changes in |∆(S)|. We compute
a one-sided empirical p-value which represents the fraction
of random trials whose change is at least as negative as the
identified one.

3.6. Knowledge Probing
The binding pipeline measures whether models are able to
differentiate in a context warranting it. It naturally leads
to the question of whether the identified heads mediate the
activation of the cultural knowledge (as a gating mechanism)
or participate in its retrieval. We introduce a knowledge
probing task, whose goal is to disentangle these two roles.
Task Construction
Starting from the 847 factorial pairs
(described in §3.1), we build a straightforward association
question. We use the same identities and cultural items but
we remove the scenario to transform it into a knowledge
question.
Which of the following cultural identities is most associated with
the Holi festival?
(a) the Indian person (b) the Cambodian person
(c) Neither is particularly associated
In this scenario, the model needs to show that it actually
knows the cultural association exists. There is no reasoning
involved. We use the same factorial structure. In the match-
ing prompt (Kmatch), the associated identity R is present
and the right answer is (a) or (b). This is where the model
needs to differentiate. In the mismatch prompt (Kmism.), the
related identity is not present because R is swapped for an
unrelated identity. The correct answer is equal treatment,
i.e. (c). We use the same option-positioning convention as
the S-score task: R is randomized between (a) and (b), and
the no-association option is at (c). Any preference for (c)
cancels between match and mismatch, since the (c) content
is identical in both.

(R→item and U→item) to both the binding and the knowl-

stages (§3.3) are reused to evaluate their role. We measure
the binding strength with the absolute value |∆K|.
4. Experiments and Results
4.1. Setup
The pipeline is applied to four architectures of models:
Mistral-7B (Jiang et al., 2023), Mistral-Nemo-12B (Mis-
tral AI & NVIDIA, 2024), Llama-3.1-8B (Grattafiori et al.,
2024) and Gemma2-9B (Team et al., 2024). Both the in-
struct and the base versions are used, amounting to eight
models studied. Model identifiers are listed in Appendix A.
The output parsing follows Wang et al. (2025). However
we use greedy decoding in generation. Table 1 presents the
binding strength results. The pairing effect ∆(S) is neg-
ative and statistically significant for all eight models. In
the presence of the related identity, the models lean more
toward differentiation (picking an identity) rather than equal
treatment. For the instruct models, Mistral-7B shows the
strongest effect (|∆S| = 3.45). Base |∆S| values are one
to two orders of magnitude smaller than their instruct coun-
terparts. Detailed results are presented in Appendix B. In
addition to the magnitude |∆S|, we also verify that when
models differentiate, they do so in the right direction. The
directional preference P(R | {a, b}) is statistically signifi-
cant (p < 0.01) and above chance in the match condition for
all eight models (results in Appendix C). This confirms that
|∆S| represents an inclination toward the correct identity
and not just an arbitrary differentiation.

4.6. Association–Differentiation Gap
The knowledge probing task (§3.6) measures how much

5. Discussion
Summary
We identified through our pipeline 2–3 mid-
layer attention heads that causally contribute to cultural
binding in all eight models. The effect is specific to the edge
R→item and does not hold for U→item. It persists during
generation and it transfers from instruction-tuned to base
models in logit space. Knockout of these heads accounts
for 9−23% of |∆S|, which leaves 77−91% of the effect
unexplained. The heads we identify are a causally impli-
cated subset of a broader binding circuit. Our α-scaling
confirms a graded causal role. At moderate amplification,
it improves cultural differentiation on three of four instruct
models. Our knowledge probing task shows a 3.5−5×
association–differentiation gap. This suggests that the heads
implement knowledge-based matching rather than pure gat-
ing.
What do the binding heads compute?
We observe that
R→item edge knockout affects binding across models,
while the reverse does not hold for U→item knockout (Ta-
ble 2). This clarifies what the heads we identified actually
do. They likely act as a matching circuit: comparing identity
and item representations, and shifting the output away from
equal treatment when a match is detected. This is similar
to the finding of Feng & Steinhardt (2024) who showed
that LLMs use binding ID vectors to associate entities and
attributes within a given context. However, in our work, the
binding is not contextual but based on prior knowledge.
Why mid-layers?
The heads we identified cluster in mid-
layers, between 20% and 38% of network depth (layers
7−16 across all four architectures, whose depth ranges from
32 layers for Mistral-7B and Llama to 42 layers for Gemma).
This is consistent with a layered view of the cultural binding.
Mid-layers are well-positioned to cross-reference item and
identity. They are late enough that both representations
are available but early enough to act before the decision
is committed. This matches prior observations that factual
recall in transformers tends to be mediated by mid-layers.
Davies et al. (2023) localized their variable-binding heads
within the same depth range.
K/S variation across models
Knockout dissociation re-
sults (§4.7) show that the identified heads do not implement
pure gating. Their balance between knowledge retrieval
and gating varies across architectures, and we report this as
an observation rather than a stable taxonomy. For Mistral
and Nemo Instruct (same family), knockout reduces binding
and knowledge by comparable amounts (K/S ≈1.0 −1.1,
Mistral 1.13, Nemo 1.00). The identified heads participate
equally in knowledge retrieval and in its decisional use. For
Llama, knowledge is reduced almost twice as much as bind-
ing (K/S ≈1.9). Gemma is the only model where binding
is more affected than knowledge (K/S = 0.4). A possible
explanation is that the identified heads mediate the gating
decision, with knowledge-retrieval distributed elsewhere
across the architecture. None of the models show a clear
“gate-only” or “retrieval-only” pattern.
The Llama anomaly
Llama exhibits a dose-response that
is non-monotonic (Figure 3). Its α-scaling at generation
produces a negative trade-off between ̸=acc and =acc. Fi-
nally, its K/S ratio is the highest of the three families (≈1.9
instruct). We propose that these singular behaviors might
have a common explanation. It seems the heads identified in
Llama might primarily contribute to the knowledge retrieval
system (as is suggested by the high K/S ratio). This means
that the binding behavior may rely on other pathways that
were not captured by our pipeline. Hence, amplifying the
identified heads alone would inject a stronger retrieval signal
without equivalently increasing gating. This would be con-
sistent both with the non-monotonic dose-response and with
the poor steering results. Llama might lose equal-treatment
accuracy (=acc) faster than the other models when steered
since amplifying the retrieval but not the gating might pro-
duce over-differentiation on neutral questions. Consistent
with this, at α = 5, Llama loses 5.0 pp on =acc to gain

Instruction tuning amplifies an existing mechanism
The instruct→base transfer (§4.2) indicates that the cul-
tural matching circuit is not introduced by instruction tuning
but emerges during pre-training. What instruction tuning
changes seems to be twofold. On one hand, it amplifies the
signal. The binding strength |∆S| values are much larger in
the instruct compared to the base models (Table 1), while
the relative effect of the intervention is similar in both of
them (Table 2). It means that the same heads participate in
carrying the binding signal in the base and instruct versions,
but more strongly on instruct. All four base models show
K/S after KO > 1 (1.09−4.67, see Table 7). It means that
the heads are more involved in knowledge retrieval than
in gating in the base versions. On the other hand, the K/S
drops in every model after instruction tuning (Mistral 4.67
→1.13, Llama 2.51 →1.90, Gemma 1.57 →0.36, Nemo
1.09 →1.00). The magnitude of the change varies (from
very strong for Mistral and Gemma to modest for Llama and
Nemo), but the direction is consistent. Instruction tuning
seems to increase the gating role of these heads relative to
knowledge-retrieval.
Explanation of the implicit−explicit gap
Veselovsky
et al. (2025) qualitatively observed that models have knowl-
edge that does not resurface without being provided explicit
cultural cues. Our results propose a quantitative and mech-
anistic approach to this finding. We find a 3.5−5× ratio
between |∆K| and |∆S| in instruct models and 3−6× in
base. This also implies that models know more than they
differentiate. This is our quantitative confirmation of the
previous qualitative observation. Moreover, the association–
differentiation gap is present in base models too, indicating
it does not arise from instruction tuning.
Implications for fairness interventions
Our results have
a practical consequence for how interventions on cultur-
ally aware behavior might be designed. The association–
differentiation gap shows that models already encode the
relevant cultural associations more strongly than they act
upon them. The bottleneck is not knowledge acquisition
but its mobilization at decision time. Hence, the target of
intervention is shifted. Instead of providing models with
additional cultural data or retraining for cultural awareness,
an alternative would be to act on the circuit mediating this
mobilization (of which we have identified a small set). The
results of our α-scaling on three of four instruct models are a
proof of concept that the identified heads participate in medi-
ating cultural differentiation. The models differentiate more
on cultural questions without changing their answers on neu-
tral questions when amplification is moderate (α ∈[2, 3]),
without fine-tuning and with only 2−3 heads modified per
model. This is not a deployable fairness method yet, in
particular because the steering does not transfer cleanly to
Llama and the magnitude of the gain is modest. However,
this suggests that targeted and circuit-level interventions are
a promising alternative to data-centric or training-centric
approaches for difference-aware behavior.
6. Future Work
We compute the directional preference at baseline only (Ap-
pendix C). Whether the identified heads encode the direction
of differentiation (toward R) is left for future work. A nat-
ural extension is to scale to larger LLMs and test whether
the cultural binding circuit holds. Further directions include
discovering knowledge-specific heads and comparing them
with the binding heads. Another direction is cross-lingual
investigation: do the identified heads participate in cultural
binding when items are queried in non-English prompts, or
does each language have its own circuit.
7. Conclusion
Our work presented a mechanistic investigation of cultural
binding in LLMs, which we define as the process used by
models to deploy cultural knowledge when context warrants
it. We reframed context-appropriate cultural differentiation
as a binding problem rather than a bias problem, shifting
from suppressing an unwanted behavior to understanding
and modulating it mechanistically. The 2−3 attention heads
we identify are a small but tractable handle on that com-
petence. The gap we measure between what models know
and what they act upon suggests that the work of difference-
aware behavior is primarily a routing problem rather than a
knowledge problem. This is an invitation to study other nor-
mative domains, such as legal or medical reasoning, where
the same distinction between knowing and acting matters.
8. Limitations
The N4 benchmark covers cultural appropriation with 66
items. Generalization to other cultural domains and larger
sets remains open. The identified heads account for 9–
23% of the binding effect, leaving the remainder (possi-
bly MLPs or residual-stream directions) unexplained. The
generation-time effect on instruct models is consistent but
moderate (1.3–2.4 pp at α = 3). Furthermore, both the
binding pipeline (which requires locating identity and item
token positions) and the knowledge probing task rely on
a multiple-choice setting. Extension to open-ended gen-
eration is non-trivial. Whether the same circuit mediates
differentiation in other normative domains (legal, medical)
is also untested.

C. Directional Differentiation
The pairing effect ∆(S) measures the inclination of a model
to differentiate. However, it does not take into account
which of (a) or (b) is picked, as mentioned in §4.1. The
objective of the directional preference is to complement it.
P(R | {a, b}) =
P(R)
P(a) + P(b)
(6)
This is the probability for the model to choose the related
identity R, which is P(R) = P(a) if R is in position (a),
and P(R) = P(b) otherwise.
Table 11 reports P(R | {a, b}) values in the match and mis-
match conditions for the eight models, with the p-value test-
ing the match value against 0.5 (cluster-corrected t-test on
item-level means, n = 66). Instruct models show a stronger
directional preference (0.60–0.74) than base models (0.51–
0.55). This suggests that instruction tuning improves (rather
than introduces) both the inclination of the models to differ-
entiate and the directionality of cultural differentiation.
