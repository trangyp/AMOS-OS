Abstract
The Muon optimizer has recently demonstrated remarkable empirical success
in training large language models. However, the theoretical understanding of its
mechanisms remains limited. Current convergence guarantees for Muon rely heavily
on smoothness assumptions, leaving its non-smooth convergence behavior largely
unexplored. In this work, we take a step toward bridging this gap by investigating
Spectral Descent (SD), a simplified variant of Muon, together with its truncated
counterpart, Truncated Spectral Descent (TSD). Under convexity, Lipschitz conti-
nuity, and sharpness conditions, we establish global linear convergence for both SD
and TSD in non-smooth convex formulations. We also study regularized variants
equipped with decoupled weight decay and derive sublinear convergence guarantees
through their connection with Frank-Wolfe methods. Finally, we apply our theoret-
ical framework to robust low-rank matrix recovery under mixed sparse and dense
noise regimes and provide rigorous recovery guarantees. Numerical experiments
support the theoretical findings and demonstrate the effectiveness of Muon-type
methods for non-smooth optimization.
Keywords: Muon, Spectral Descent, Non-smooth Optimization, Low-rank Ma-
trix Recovery, Weight Decay.

Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as a central paradigm in modern arti-
ficial intelligence, demonstrating strong performance across a wide range of tasks and
applications (Brown et al., 2020; Gemini Team et al., 2023).
However, the immense
computational overhead of large-scale LLM training makes computational efficiency a
primary practical concern. In this context, the choice of optimizer is particularly impor-
tant. Over the past few years, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and adaptive methods,
particularly Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2014) and AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017),
have become the primary choices for large-scale model training.
Recently, Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) has emerged as a highly promising alterna-
tive to these standard adaptive optimizers for large-scale training. Its core mechanism

Moreover, Ma et al. (2026) investigated Spectral Descent for specific tasks such as matrix

natural preconditioner to achieve a linear convergence rate independent of the condition
number.
However, the convergence behavior of Muon-type optimizers in non-smooth
optimization problems, which arise widely in data science and deep learning, remains
poorly understood.

Related Work
Spectral Descent and Muon.
The conceptual foundation of the Spectral Descent
(SD) optimizer can be traced back to the works (Carlson et al., 2016, 2015a,b), which
initially introduced it for machine learning applications. Fundamentally, SD operates as
4


## Page 5


steepest descent with respect to the spectral norm. Recently, Bernstein and Newhouse
(2024) showed that the Shampoo (Gupta et al., 2018) optimizer can also be understood
from this identical perspective.
Driven by these matrix-wise geometric insights, Jordan et al. (2024) proposed the
Muon optimizer; it incorporates a momentum term into SD and demonstrates superior
performance in large-scale model training. Inspired by the empirical success of Muon, a
proliferation of variants has recently emerged to improve its memory efficiency, stability,
and overall performance (An et al., 2025; Ahn et al., 2025; Pang et al., 2026). These
methods typically integrate Muon’s core orthogonalization step with standard adaptive
optimizers or other mechanisms (Liu et al., 2025b; Si et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025;
Wang et al., 2025).
A crucial line of research investigates the convergence of Muon-type optimizers (Ma
et al., 2026; Braun et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Sfyraki and Wang,
2025; Pethick et al., 2025).
We note that recent works have explored extending the
Muon optimizer to manifold optimization problems (Bernstein, 2025; Buchanan, 2025;
Su, 2025a,b; Yang and Lai, 2026).
Another line of research has recently explored the implicit bias of Spectral Descent
and Muon. Fan et al. (2026) proved that these algorithms converge to spectral norm max-
margin solutions in multi-class linear classification. This geometric bias was extended to
smooth homogeneous neural networks by Gronich and Vardi (2026), and further analyzed
in practical stochastic settings by Li et al. (2026).
Decoupled Weight Decay.
Decoupled weight decay, initially popularized by AdamW
(Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) to overcome the limitations of L2 regularization in adap-
tive methods, has become a standard technique in large-scale training. Recently, the
integration of decoupled weight decay into Muon has garnered special attention (Chen
et al., 2024, 2025; Liu et al., 2025a; Sfyraki and Wang, 2025; Pethick et al., 2025). Incor-
porating decoupled weight decay intrinsically alters the optimization dynamics, exposing
a structural equivalence with the Frank-Wolfe (FW) method over spectral norm con-
straints. Building upon this insight, we establish corresponding convergence guarantees
for Spectral Descent and Truncated Spectral Descent under decoupled weight decay in
non-smooth settings.
Sharpness.
Sharpness and weak sharpness conditions (Burke and Ferris, 1993; Luo and
Tseng, 1993; Studniarski and Ward, 1999; Burke and Deng, 2002; Ma et al., 2025) are
prevalent across many mathematical problems, serving as central tools for establishing
fast local convergence. Recently, sharpness has become a key ingredient in the analysis
of nonconvex low-rank matrix recovery (Li et al., 2020; Charisopoulos et al., 2021; Ding
and Wang, 2023; Xu et al., 2024) and phase retrieval (Duchi and Ruan, 2019; Davis et al.,
2020; Zheng et al., 2024). Motivated by these developments, we adopt sharpness as a key
structural assumption in our analysis of non-smooth problems.

This work: Spectral Descent
In our work, we focus on the momentum-free case (µ = 0) for both the Muon and MuonW
optimizers. In this regime, the Muon update simplifies to the following Spectral Descent
(Bernstein and Newhouse, 2024) step:
X(t+1) = X(t) −ηtmsgn(G(t)),
G(t) ∈∂f(X(t)).
(4)
The update removes the momentum term and directly uses the current subgradient to
compute the descent direction, thus serving as a simplified variant of Muon that retains
its core descent mechanism.

Main Results
In this section, we present our core theoretical results. We begin in Subsection 4.1 by
establishing the convergence guarantees for standard Spectral Descent (SD) (4) and Trun-
cated Spectral Descent (TSD) (5). By omitting the momentum term, we directly isolate
and analyze the core geometry-aware descent mechanism. Subsequently, Subsection 4.2
extends our analysis to regularized optimizers that incorporate decoupled weight decay.
Specifically, we prove the convergence of RSD-WD (6) and TSD-WD (7), theoretically
demonstrating that our proposed neighborhood selection mechanism effectively stabilizes
the optimization dynamics.
Let X ∗= {X ∈Rn1×n2 : f(X) ≤f(Z), ∀Z ∈Rn1×n2} denote the set of global mini-
mizers of f, and let f ∗be the optimal value of f. We consider the following assumptions
on the objective function f.
Assumption 4.1 (Convexity). We say a function f : Rn1×n2 →R is convex, if for any
X, Y ∈Rn1×n2 and θ ∈[0, 1], we have
f(θX + (1 −θ)Y ) ≤θf(X) + (1 −θ)f(Y ).

Assumption 4.2 (Lipschitz Continuity). A function f : Rn1×n2 →R is said to be
L-Lipschitz continuous if there exists a constant L > 0 such that
|f(X) −f(Y )| ≤L∥X −Y ∥F,
∀X, Y ∈Rn1×n2.
This is a mild and standard assumption in non-smooth optimization. In contrast to
the smoothness assumption (i.e., Lipschitz continuous gradients) which strictly requires
the function to be differentiable, Assumption 4.2 accommodates non-smooth landscapes.
Moreover, this condition ensures that the subgradients of f are uniformly bounded, i.e.,
∥G∥F ≤L for any G ∈∂f(X) and all X ∈Rn1×n2.
Assumption 4.3 (Sharpness). We say a function f : Rn1×n2 →R is µ-sharp with respect
to the optimal set X ∗if there exists a constant µ > 0 such that
f(X) −f ∗≥µ · dist(X, X ∗),
∀X ∈Rn1×n2,
where dist(X, X ∗) := infY ∈X ∗∥X −Y ∥F represents the distance from X to the optimal
set X ∗w.r.t. the Frobenius norm.
The µ-sharpness condition characterizes the local geometry around the optimal so-
lution set, naturally capturing the non-differentiable ”V-shaped” valleys in non-smooth
optimization landscapes. Utilizing this property is crucial for establishing rigorous con-
vergence bounds across various data science problems (Davis et al., 2018, 2020; Li et al.,
2020; Xu et al., 2024). Mathematically, this condition is directly satisfied by polyhedral
convex functions (e.g., L1-regularization and SVM hinge loss), spectral penalties like the
nuclear norm, as well as linear programming and linear complementarity problems (Burke
and Ferris, 1993).

Discussion
In this paper, we presented a rigorous theoretical framework for understanding the con-
vergence of Spectral Descent (SD) and its truncated variant (TSD) in non-smooth opti-
mization. By leveraging the geometric properties of convexity, Lipschitz continuity, and
sharpness, we established the global linear convergence of momentum-free Muon-type
optimizers. Furthermore, we introduced decoupled weight decay as a spatial stabilization
mechanism, explicitly connecting the regularized updates to the Conditional Subgradient
(Frank-Wolfe) method. This regularization theoretically circumvents stringent condition
parameter requirements, guaranteeing a sublinear O(1/
√
T) convergence rate. We in-
stantiated our framework on the robust low-rank matrix recovery problem via a least
absolute deviation (LAD) formulation, demonstrating that our algorithms can efficiently
recover the ground-truth matrix even in the presence of outliers and dense noise.
As the reader may note, our numerical experiments in Section 6 suggest that Spectral
Descent and its variants exhibit remarkable robustness and efficiency that often exceed
our theoretical worst-case bounds. For instance, in the linear programming experiments,
the algorithms successfully and rapidly converge even when the theoretical assumption
regarding the condition parameter is not strictly satisfied. This raises the interesting
question: whether tighter, problem-specific alignment conditions can be derived to narrow
the gap between deterministic worst-case bounds and empirical performance.
A critical direction for our future research is extending this theoretical framework to
highly non-convex and non-smooth landscapes. Modern machine learning architectures,
including transformers and fully connected neural networks with ReLU activations, in-
herently possess these complex geometries. While our current experiments empirically
validate the superiority of Spectral Descent in training ReLU networks, establishing rig-
orous convergence guarantees for such settings remains an open challenge.
