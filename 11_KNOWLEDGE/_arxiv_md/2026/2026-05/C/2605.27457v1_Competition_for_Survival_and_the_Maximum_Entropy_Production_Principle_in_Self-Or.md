Competition for Survival and the Maximum Entropy Production Principle
in Self-Organized Silver Particle Chains
Albert Han,1 Jiri Kataman-Kustwan,1 and Alexey Bezryadin1
Department of Physics, University of Illinois, Urbana-Champaign
(Dated: 28 May 2026)
The maximum entropy production (MEP) principle is a hypothetical law of physics which dictates that complex sys-
tems, far from equilibrium, evolve into an ordered dissipative structure (DS) which generates as much entropy per
second as possible, under given constraints. The MEP is a promising principle in non-equilibrium physics, but more
precise, quantitative experimental tests are needed for the MEP principle to become universally accepted. An impor-
tant problem is whether the natural competition for resources, well known in biology, limits the ability of dissipative
structures (DS) to achieve the maximum of the entropy production rate (EPR). Here, we investigate this competition
between DS experimentally, by performing high precision electrical measurements on suspensions of silver particles
under strong electric fields. To establish the hypothetical impact of competition between the DS on the MEP effect,
precise electrical measurements of the Joule heating effect are performed on two Ag suspension samples connected in
parallel, exposing them to the same voltage. The samples are able to self-organize, dissipate energy, generate entropy,
and they compete with each other for resources, i.e., electrical current, which is limited by a series resistor. We find that
usually only one of the two samples is able to develop a dissipative structure, i.e., a conducting chain of Ag particles.
Our findings are as follows. (1) There is a competition between the two samples, which prevents both systems from
achieving their maximum possible contribution to the global entropy production rate. (2) Due to this competition, we
find that only one out of two samples can self-organize, while the other deteriorates and approaches zero EPR. (3) The
global EPR, i.e., the entropy produced by the samples and the energy supply circuit, is also reduced from its possible
maximum due to the competition between the sub-systems. Based on these observations, we propose that the compe-
tition effect constitutes an essential constraint that must be incorporated into formulations of the MEP. This principle
parallels real-world phenomena, reflecting the competition for resources observed among species and individual organ-
isms in natural systems. We also examine the global implications of the MEP and propose that it serves as a driving
mechanism propelling the hypothetical ascent of civilizations along the Kardashev scale.
I.
INTRODUCTION
The notion of competition in biology is understood as an
interaction between organisms or species in which they re-
quire resources, the supply of which is limited. The func-
tioning, i.e., the entropy production, of one of the organisms
reduces the amount of the resource available to the other or-
ganism. Thus, "successful" organisms cause a strong entropy
production in the environment, while the "unsuccessful" ones
might go extinct or have their contribution to entropy produc-
tion minimized.
The thermodynamics of equilibrium systems is well under-
stood and has a governing extremum principle, namely, the
second law of thermodynamics. However, the thermodynam-
ics of systems far from equilibrium remains poorly under-
stood, and no universally accepted extremum principle ex-
ists for such systems. Several proposals have been put for-
ward, typically invoking the maximization of power dissipa-
tion and, consequently, entropy production1–7. Given the use-
fulness of the second law of thermodynamics and other ex-
tremum principles in physics, it is natural to look for an anal-
ogous principle that governs systems far from equilibrium, as
many phenomena of great interest take place far from equi-
librium. One can argue, for example, that life itself is a non-
equilibrium phenomenon8. The key idea discussed over the
last few decades is that systems which can self-organize do
so in such a way as to generate as much heat and entropy as
possible, given the constraints. Such maximum entropy pro-
duction (MEP) principles have been applied in many different
fields, including climate science, biology, and physics, and
has shown much promise.9 Examples are found in the Earth’s
climate, specific biological systems, selforganized electrorhe-
ological fluids4 and many other systems.10–13.
One notable application of the MEP includes the calcula-
tions of Paltridge, who showed that maximizing the entropy
production in Earth’s atmosphere produces temperature dis-
tributions that agreed surprisingly well with the measured
values.11,12 Similar calculations have been done for the atmo-
spheres of other planets like Mars and Saturn’s moon Titan14.
The MEP has also been successfully used to solve other prob-
lems in Earth science, such as mantle convection15 and ther-
mohaline circulation in the ocean16. In physics, the MEP has
been applied to problems such as fluid flow6, and Rayleigh-
Benard convection17 and self-organized motile ("wiggling")
clouds of carbon nanotubes18. In biology, the MEP has been
used to model photosynthesis19 and some hypothesize the
MEP could be extremely useful in understanding biologi-
cal evolution20. However, despite these successful applica-
tions of the MEP, it is still a heavily debated principle and
not generally accepted due to a lack of a high-precision, re-
producible experimental confirmations.
Many critics have
claimed to disprove the MEP21–23, while proponents argue
that the counterexamples provided are outside of the MEP’s
applicability24. These issues are caused by confliction formu-
lations of the MEP. Some formulations maximize the dynamic
steady state entropy production rate (EPR), while others re-
quire local maximization of the EPR.9,17,24–28

capable of self-assembly has hindered empirical support for

The absence of accurate entropy production measurements
has limited its acceptance within the broader scientific com-

a model system that exhibits compelling self-assembly behav-
ior and enables highly precise quantification of the heat gen-

In this paper, we focus on the competition concept. Com-
petition in biology is understood as an interaction between or-
ganisms or species in which both require resources, the sup-
ply of which is limited. Examples of such supplies include
food, water, territory, and sunlight29. According to Lang and
Benbow, competition reduces the fitness of the organisms in-
volved. This is because the functioning of one organism re-
duces the amount of the resource available to the other.30
Based on the hints provided by biological organisms, we
hypothesize that "successful" subsystems generate more en-
tropy within themselves and drive strong entropy production
in the environment, while "unsuccessful" subsystems might
become extinct or have their contribution to the entropy pro-
duction rate greatly reduced.
This factor may reduce the
global entropy production rate for the entire population of all
organisms or subsystems.
Here, we perform precise electrical measurements of the
entropy production rate by dissipative structures formed out
of silver (Ag) nanowires (and/or silver flakes) suspended in
isopropanol and subjected to a strong electric field bias. Non-
equilibrium conditions are imposed by placing two metallic
electrodes in the suspension and biasing them with a high volt-
age. The voltage and the current are precisely measured dur-
ing the experiments. The main goal is to clarify the impact of
the competition effect on the global entropy production rate
of the entire system. Therefore, the setup involves two self-
assembly subsystems arranged in parallel electrical connec-
tion within the circuit. From our precise measurements of the
current and the voltage, the Joule heating is calculated. The
entropy production rate is evaluated as the heat released by
the Joule heating divided by the temperature. Similar exper-
iments were done previously using carbon nanotubes and a
nonpolar solvent4,18,25, but there was only one DS in the pre-
vious experiments, and so the competition effect has not been
investigated until now.

succeed and contribute their potential maximum EPR to the
global EPR. Also, we conclude that the global EPR is not
maximized due to the competition. This last conclusion means
that if all subsystems were successful and achieved their max-
imum EPR contribution, then the global EPR would be higher.
II.
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
The setup is similar to previous experiments4,18. In this
section, first we describe the sample preparation procedures
and then discuss the circuit used to perform high-precision
electrical measurements. Isopropanol was used as the sol-
vent in all reported experiments. Novarials A100UL silver
(Ag) nanowires (100nm diameter, 150µm long) and Alpha
Aesar 80% < 20 micron Ag flakes were used to form electro-
rheological suspensions. Suspensions of Ag nanowires be-
tween 0.025 and 1.5 mg
ml were tested, and suspensions of Ag
flakes between 0.3 and 3 mg
ml were tested.
To prepare each sample, approximately 10 ml of solution
was placed into a glass vial equipped with two stainless steel
electrodes, 0.5 mm diameter, 20mm length. The electrodes, in
the shape of needles, were inserted through a rubber or plastic
cap into the solution. The distance between the electrodes was
6 mm. The electrodes allowed the samples to be connected to
a voltage source. The voltage source was connected in series
with the resistor to model the source’s internal dissipation and
limit the power that can be drawn from it. Such imitations
mimic real-life situations in which resources are limited and
the EPR cannot be set to infinity.
Before and between experiments, Ag Nanowires were sus-
pended in isopropanol by shaking the container until the so-
lution was visibly homogeneous. The Ag flake samples were
sonicated until visibly homogeneous. We have noticed that ul-
trasound sonication can break nanowires and change the sam-
ples qualitatively. Thus ultra-sonication was not used to re-
agitate samples with Ag nanowires. The concentrations tested
were chosen to ensure that significant self-assembly was pos-
sible, i.e, a dissipative structure (DS) could form between the
electrodes, but the initial concentration was low enough that
the DS significantly increased the entropy production. This
environment mimics biological systems, where organic mate-
rials are not overabundant.

Note that this EPR cannot be achieved since it would require
the resistance of the DS (Ag chain) to become zero, which is
not realistic. However, the sample can always evolve closer
and closer to this maximum since more Ag particles can join
the chain and make it more and more conducting over time.
Such process is frequently observed in the experiments.
The power dissipated within the sample only is Psample =
IVsample. This is done by setting a fixed source voltage (Vsource)
and by measuring the current in the circuit (I). The voltage

The entropy production rate (EPR) by the sample itself is
dSsample/dt = Psample/T. Interestingly, the entropy produc-

the resistance matching condition, namely when the resistance
of the dissipative structure (Rsample) equals the resistance of
the series resistor, i.e., when Rsample = Rseries, in which case
Vsample = Vsource/2.
Note that Vsample + Vresistor = Vsource,
where Vresistor = IRseries is the voltage drop on the resistor.
So, the maximum power which can be dissipated within the

This is a non-monotonic function with a maximum at the re-
sistance matching point, Rseries = Rsample. Based on this, we
can distinguish two stages of the evolution. In the first stage
Rsample > Rseries and in the second stage Rsample < Rseries.
The direction of the evolution is predominantly to decrease
Rsample. In the first stage the power dissipated by the same in-
creases with time (as long as Rsample goes down). In the sec-
ond stage of the evolution, when Rsample < Rseries, the power
dissipated in the sample decreases and the power dissipated in
the resistor increases, so the total dissipated power increases
as well.
Note again that, to simplify the discussion, we assume the
sample’s temperature equals the room temperature. This is
justified because the currents flowing through the sample are
usually very small, of the order of µA, which corresponds to
a low level of heating in general.

IV.
DISCUSSION
A.
Stages of evolution in single-sample experiments
Experiments show that as the system self-organizes it goes
through multiple stages. Initially the metallic suspended par-
ticles (silver nanowire or micro-flakes) are disorganized and
not connected to each other. Thus the conductivity of the fluid
and the current passing through it is very low. Therefore, the
entropy production rate is near zero. This is true for both
dSsample/dt and dSglobal/dt. Thus, by design of our experi-
ments, at the initial stages of evolution the entropy production
rate (EPR) is always much smaller than the allowed absolute
maximum.
Next, the system undergoes an evolution in which the con-
ducting particles orient themselves along the applied electric
field and form continuous and electrically conducting chains,
as seen in Fig. 3, Fig. 5b, and Fig. 10. Initially the chains are
short but with time they form bundles which eventually link
the electrodes and make the conductance of the sample much
higher compared to the initial state. If the concentration of the
metallic particles and their individual conductivities are suffi-
ciently high, and the applied electric field is sufficiently strong
to overcome destructive effects (e.g., gravity and thermal fluc-
tuations), then the particles organize into a dissipative struc-
ture which eventually achieves the maximum possible entropy
production rate (EPR) by the sample itself. In our case, this
means that the Joule heat released by the Ag chain itself is at
the maximum possible level.
A simple calculation shows that the maximum power dis-
sipated in the sample is achieved when the sample resis-


## Page 10


10
tance equals the series resistor, Rsample = Rseries. Under this
"impedance-matching" condition, the EPR of the sample itself
is its its maximum, i.e., dSsample/dt = (dSsample/dt)max, as de-
fined in Eq. 3. This is the end of the first stage of the evolution,
which occurs when the local EPR maximum is achieved, i.e.,
when the matching condition, Rsample = Rseries, is realized. At
the end of the first evolution stage the EPR of the sample is
(dSsample/dT)max = (Psample)max/T. This entropy is gener-
ated as the heat flows from the sample to the environment,
assumed to have the temperature T.
To characterize the evolution time scale quantitatively we
introduced the "evolution time", te, which was defined as the
time needed for the dissipative structure to achieve the local
EPR maximum, i.e. the time at which Rsample = Rseries for the
first time. (By our definition, see below, this point in time cor-
responds to the end of first evolution stage and the beginning
of the second and final evolution stage.) Our numerous tests
showed that such evolution time decays as function of the ap-
plied voltage, which is the driving force in these experiments.
This decay and be modeled by both a powerlaw trend or an
activation-law and exponential decay formula. The results are
summarized in Fig.8 and Fig. 9.
There are in fact two evolution stages. In the first stage,
when Rsample > Rseries, the EPR increases both within the sam-
ple as well as globally. In the second stage, when Rsample <
Rseries, the global EPR continues to increase while the sam-
ple EPR decreases, because the voltage now drops mostly on
the series resistor. As the system achieves the maximum EPR
within the sample, the evolution does not stop at this point,
although it might slow down since the voltage on the sample
at this point is reduced by 50%, due to the voltage drop on
the resistor. In general, the evolution continues as the system
attempts to maximize the global entropy production rate. The
global EPR includes both the heat generated by the sample
and the heat generated by the energy supply circuit. An impor-
tant fact is that after the local EPR maximum is achieved, and
if the evolution continues further (i.e., the flux or the current
allowed by the dissipative structure continues to increase), the
global entropy production rate continues to increase while the
local EPR begins to decreases. Thus the signature of the sec-
ond evolution stage is that the local EPR decreases while the
global EPR continues to increase. Thus, we observe a ten-
dency, which we suspect is quite general, that advanced dissi-
pative structures tend to shifts the heat generation from them-
selves to outer elements, i.e., to their energy supply system.
In our case, at the second evolution stage, more heat is gen-
erated in the series resistor than in the self-assembled silver
particle chain (see the derivation of Eq. 3 and Eq.4). Note
that such two stages of evolution have been observed previ-
ously in Ref.18 and it was also concluded that the first stage
is characterized by a series of avalanches, while the second
stage was much more stable.
In the second stage of evolution ( Rsample < Rseries and
so Vsample < Vseries), the global entropy production continues
to grow to its absolute maximum, which is set by the en-
ergy supplie circuit (Vsource and Rseries) and is given in Eq.2.
The global entropy production is maximized when Rsample
is exactly zero, however, this condition is never realized in
practice. Therefore, under favorable conditions, the systems
evolves to the global EPR maximum but cannot achieve it ex-
actly. Indeed, in the second (final) evolution stage we fre-
quently observe Rsample ≪Rseries (see Fig.5), provided that the
suspension has enough conducting particles. The Joule heat-
ing power produced by the supply circuit is Poutside = RseriesI2
and the heat power produced by the dissipative structure is
Pinside = RsampleI2. (The current, I, is the same in the sample
and in the resistor.) All this heat goes to the environment at
temperature T ≈295K, so the entropy production of the sam-
ple is Sinside/dt = RsampleI2/T and the EPR of the resistor is
Soutside/dt = RseriesI2/T. At the end of the second evolution
stage the EPR in the sample itself is much lower than the EPR
the dissipative structure causes or induces in energy source.
The analogy between our model systems and biological or-
ganisms is noteworthy. At earlier stages of evolution, organ-
isms primarily generate entropy within their own bodies. As
evolution progresses, however, a larger fraction of entropy
production shifts to external systems. Human civilization pro-
vides a clear example of this transition. With the develop-
ment of technologies to harness chemical energy (e.g., fire
and power plants), nuclear energy, and electrical energy (e.g.,
data centers), most entropy production associated with human
activity now occurs outside the body, within engineered in-
frastructures and energy networks.
A similar pattern is observed in our experiments. As Ag
particles evolve toward a highly conductive dissipative struc-
ture (DS) during the second stage of evolution, the effective
point of entropy production (EPR) shifts to the external en-
ergy supply circuit, specifically the series resistor. In contrast,
systems at an earlier evolutionary stage dissipate energy pri-
marily internally.
By
analogy,
animals—representing
an
earlier
stage—mainly dissipate energy through internal metabolic
processes such as digestion. Humans, representing a more
advanced stage, increasingly rely on external systems such
as factories, power plants, and data centers, which dominate
overall energy dissipation and entropy production.
Conse-
quently, the conversion of free energy (ultimately derived
from the Sun) now occurs predominantly outside the human
body, within industrial and technological systems. At present,
the rate of entropy production in these external systems far
exceeds that within human bodies.
B.
The competition effect in two-samples experiments
The main focus of this paper is on systems in which two
samples are connected in parallel. In such configurations, Ag
flake suspensions compete for a shared resource—namely, the
free energy supplied by the voltage/current source—which is
required for the formation of dissipative structures and the as-
sociated entropy production.
Initially, when both samples exhibit high resistance, the
voltage across each is approximately equal to the source
voltage, Vsource. However, once one sample undergoes self-
assembly and forms a dissipative structure with high electrical
conductance, its voltage drops sharply. Because the samples


## Page 11


11
are connected in parallel, the voltage across both must remain
equal. Consequently, the voltage across the second sample
decreases as well, suppressing its ability to form a dissipative
structure.
As a result, the system exhibits a symmetry-breaking effect:
the formation of a dissipative structure in one sample inhibits
its formation in the other, such that only a single sample de-
velops a dissipative structure under these conditions.
This is an example of a "winner takes all" scenario. This
winner is the sample in which the dissipative structure evolves
first. This sample takes the entire resource, which is the elec-
tric current, which is limited by the resistor. A strong voltage
(electric field, to be more precise) is needed to form a contin-
uous and conducting chain of Ag particles. The voltage po-
larizes the particles and then they, acting as electric dipoles,
tend to form chains. The current, on the other hand, allows
the chain to survive. Indeed, if a break develops, the current
creates a high concentration of opposite charges around the
break point, which produce a force to heal the break. Once a
structure forms it needs the current to sustain its functional-
ity. If the current is limited, only one dissipative structure can
exist, at least in our experimental settings.
Another interesting question is the effect of such "winner-
takes all" competition effect and the MEP principle. Assum-
ing that the conductivity of each Ag chain has an upper limit,
G1,max, one concludes that if the competition effect would be
absent and two chains are formed then the total conductivity
would be twice larger, i.e., 2G1,max. Then the total current
would be higher and more entropy would be produced per
second. In such simplified view the competition effect pre-
vents the system from reaching the global entropy production
rate. In the presence of such competition The local EPR is not
maximized either. To be more precise, it is maximized on the
winner sample, but it remains near zero in the sample which
does not win the competition. This suggests that in larger sys-
tems that consist of multiple dissipative structures, the MEP
may be acting as an additional constraint, that might reduce
the entropy production.
The hypothesis that the competition effect can prevent the
entire system from reaching the absolute global maximum
of the entropy production is in agreement with our experi-
ments. Typically if we measure just one sample then at the
end of the second stage of the evolution is current is not sig-
nificantly different from the maximum current Vsource/Rseries.
For example,in Fig.4, the resistance of the sample is less than
1% in comparison to the series resistor. Therefore the cur-
rent is less than the maximum current also by less than 1%.
Yet, in the experiments with two competing samples the fi-
nal current is roughly 3% to 5% less than the maximum pos-
sible current Vsource/Rseries. Indeed, in Fig.12, the series re-
sistor was 500kΩ, and the voltages were (Fig.12a): 100V,
(Fig.12b): 180V, (Fig.12c): 300V, (Fig.12d): 120V. The corre-
sponding maximum theoretical currents (corresponding to the
global maximum EPR) are 0.2mA, 0.36mA, 0.6mA, 0.24mA.
The experimentally achieved maximum currents are 0.19mA,
0.348mA, 0.58mA, 0.23mA. Thus it appears that the system
containing two competing samples could not approach the
(dSglobal/dt)max as closely as one-sample systems could.
Previously, theoretical models have been proposed31 in
which two types of dissipative structures compete for free en-
ergy. Now we report a similar behavior, although our sys-
tem has only one type of dissipative structure (i.e., Ag parti-
cle chains). This competitive behavior resembles natural sys-
tems, as animals and plants must compete for free energy in
terms of food and sunlight (for plants). Our experiments also
show that the "winners" and the "loosers" are not set forever.
For example in Fig.13 the "blue" sample develops a highly
conducting dissipative structure at t = 50, yet this structure
gets destroyed at t = 150s, when the "red" sample develops
a similar (in terms of its conductance) dissipative structure.
Extending the biological analogy, in 12 C shows that at very
high voltages (300V), both samples are able to self assemble.
This represents the case with an abundance of free energy, and
competition does not affect self assembly.
C.
Constraints and the MEP Principle
The question of constraints in formulations of the MEP
principle is somewhat difficult because of the possibility of
circular reasoning. Whatever is the entropy production rate,
one can always say that it is the possible maximum and that
the constraints are just such that the observed EPR has to be
considered as the maximum possible value. To avoid circu-
lar definitions, one needs an independent way to establish the
constraints on the EPR in any given system. A related ques-
tion is whether the maximum EPR is a constant or is it a func-
tion of time. We take the point of view that the EPR maxi-
mum, (dS/dt)max, is a constant for a given system. Then, an
immediate conclusion is that EPR is not always at the max-
imum possible level. There reason for this is that it takes a
finite time for the dissipative structure to form and to achieve
perfection. Therefore, at the beginning if evolution the en-
tropy production is always lower than the maximum possible
value allowed by the constraints. We find the evolution time
increases if the driving force (voltage) is reduced (Fig.8) fol-
lowing a power-law dependence. Currently, there many cur-
rent definitions for the MEP9,17,24,28 but the evolution time
is not typically considered as a constraint. Our current ex-
periments and similar previous experiments18,25, show the
maximum EPR cannot be achieved immediately, but can be
reached through the system’s evolution. Our work suggests
that the evolution time is accurately modeled by a power-law
trend, similar to previous works25. We also observe that an
activation-law and exponential-decay formula also accurately
models the evolution time as a function of the source voltage.
We will now comment on promising formulations of the
MEP in relation to our results. One promising formulation has
been suggested recently: "In a local physico-chemical system
with a thermodynamic condition far from equilibrium, a dy-
namic structure called dissipative structure (DS) would be sta-
bilized in chemical or physical space, which produces maxi-
mum amount of entropy among the possible modes, and keeps
itself in a low entropy state by disposing of the produced en-
tropy out into the reservoir."32 Our experiments suggest that
this formulation should apply to the global entropy produc-


## Page 12


12
tion, since not every sample forms the DS and, also, within
each sample, not all locations are occupied by DS and thus
the local EPR is not maximized at every segment of the sam-
ple. This matches biological organisms, where animals, which
dissipate large amounts of entropy, are concentrated to certain
locations, but usually are not evenly distributed. Thus, the
entropy production density is probably not maximized for ev-
ery point in space, and is very close to zero for most points,
while the global EPR might be maximized since the number
of animals might be controlled by the amount of food avail-
able globally. Additionally, the above definition uses a notion
of a critical parameter. We find that there are indeed some
critical parameters, namely the driving voltage and the con-
centration. If the voltage is too low the dissipative structure
cannot form. Similarly, if the concentration of Ag particles is
too low we don’t observe any dissipative structure. Also, the
gravitational force plays a role of a critical parameter since
not all silver particles have a chance to participate, as many
precipitate to the bottom of the sample glass bottle (vial).
One of the first formulations of the MEP was stated by
Ziegler in his orthogonality principle333. It is widely accepted
that Ziegler’s principle is valid in the linear regime, however,
some formulations of the MEP extend Ziegler’s principle be-
yond the linear regime5. Ziegler’s principle requires a fixed
force and elementary system. Our system is elementary since
it only has one flux and one force, namely, the current and
the voltage. If we consider just the sample, then Ziegler’s
principle wouldn’t apply, as the force, voltage, isn’t constant.
However, if we consider the entire circuit, the voltage is con-
stant. Our system is nonlinear due to the nonlinear nature of
the nanowire self assembly, which can be seen in the sharp
jumps in entropy production. This means that an extension
of Ziegler’s principle to the nonlinear regime would imply
that the entropy production of the entire circuit is maximized,
which we observe in the second stage of evolution. This sug-
gests that generalizations of Ziegler’s principle are promising
given that the second stage of evolution is the final stage we
observed. But, again, our observations suggest that DS does
not occupy every segment of the sample and therefore EPR
should be maximized globally, not locally.
Another constraint on the MEP is the competition effect.
When two samples are connected in parallel, they are ob-
served to compete for resources, which, at the end, lowers
the overall entropy production. This suggests that as the com-
plexity of a system increases the competition effect lowers the
entropy production from its maximum value, an affect formu-
lations of the MEP must take into account.
In a general sense the competition effect is present even
within one sample, but it is not simple to quantify. Indeed,
different areas of the same sample compete for the same re-
sources (silver particles, electric field, electric current) which
are needed to develop and maintain the dissipative structure.
Yet, at the end of the evolution process, only a small volume
of the sample is actually occupied by a functional dissipative
structure.

D.
Possible Global Implications: Kardashev scale and the
Maximum Entropy Production (MEP) Principle
The Kardashev scale classifies civilizations by their ability
to harness energy at increasing global scales: planetary (Type
I), stellar (Type II), and galactic (Type III)34. In this frame-
work, a Type I civilization utilizes the amount of energy ar-
riving from the Sun to Earth, a Type II civilization harnesses
the full energy output of the Sun (e.g., via a Dyson sphere or
a similar structure), and a Type III civilization exploits energy
on the scale of the Milky Way galaxy.
The human civilization is often estimated to be below Type
I, and more specifically K ≈0.7, as we do not yet utilize the
full energy budget of Earth, although its energy consumption
has historically grown rapidly, roughly exponentially. From a
thermodynamic perspective, biological life can be viewed as
a self-organized, non-equilibrium process driven primarily by
solar radiation and all the free energy sources it produces35.
The MEP suggests that such non-equilibrium systems tend
to evolve toward states that increase their entropy production
and approach the maximum set by constraints. Yet, according
to Kardashev scale, these constraints are not absolute as life
forms can, in principle, spread to other planets or even other
star systems. This perspective may imply a tendency for an
ever increasing energy utilization.
We notice an interesting analogy between the Kardashev
scale and the dissipative structures, as they tend to shift the
entropy production away from them. Indeed, in the first stage
of the evolution the newly formed chains of silver particles
generate heat inside themselves. Yet, in the second stage of
the evolution the heat and the entropy production is shifted
closer to the energy source, namely to the resistor. Similarly,
in Kardashev type of evolution the free energy usage shifts
from biological organisms to the planetary artificial structures
and then to the star (i.e., the Dyson sphere).
We conclude that the following formulation of the Max-
imum Entropy Production (MEP) Principle works the best:
with given free energy sources, a non-equilibrium system,
able to self-organize, will tend to develop competing and lo-
calized dissipative structures with tend to increase their en-
tropy production rate and tend to approach the maximum set
by the free energy sources.

Principle implies that the progression of technological civi-
lizations may reflect an increasing capacity to dissipate en-
