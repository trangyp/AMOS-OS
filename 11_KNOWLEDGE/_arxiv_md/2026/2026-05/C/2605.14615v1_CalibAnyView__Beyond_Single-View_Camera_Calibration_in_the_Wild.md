CalibAnyView: Beyond Single-View
Camera Calibration in the Wild
Boying Li1, Cheng Zhang1, Weirong Chen2,
Daniel Cremers2, Ian Reid3, and Hamid Rezatofighi1
1 Monash University

Abstract. Camera calibration is a fundamental prerequisite for reliable
geometric perception, yet classical approaches rely on controlled acquisi-
tion setups that are impractical for in-the-wild imagery. Recent learning-
based methods have shown promising results for single-view calibration,
but inherently neglect geometric consistency across multiple views. We
introduce CalibAnyView, a unified formulation that supports an arbi-
trary number of input views (N ≥1) by explicitly modeling cross-view
geometric consistency. To facilitate this, we construct a large-scale multi-
view video dataset covering diverse real-world scenarios, including mul-
tiple camera models, dynamic scenes, realistic motion trajectories, and
heterogeneous lens distortions. Building on this dataset, we develop a
multi-view transformer that predicts dense perspective fields, which are
further integrated into a geometric optimization framework to jointly
estimate camera intrinsics and gravity direction. Extensive experiments
demonstrate that CalibAnyView consistently outperforms state-of-the-
art methods, achieves strong robustness under single-view settings, and
further improves with multi-view inference, providing a reliable founda-
tion for downstream tasks such as 3D reconstruction and robotic percep-
tion in the wild.
Keywords: Camera calibration · Deep learning · Multi-view

ing [2], and robotic navigation [42]. At its core, calibration aims to estimate
intrinsic parameters that define the projection from 3D rays to image pixels,
including focal length, principal point, and lens distortion. Meanwhile, many
existing approaches for 3D understanding and pose estimation, such as visual
odometry, Structure-from-Motion (SfM), and SLAM, primarily recover relative
pose across views with respect to a reference frame, lacking a consistent notion
of absolute orientation. This missing component can be compactly represented
by the gravity direction. Despite decades of research in camera calibration, most

Motivated by the gap between dense reconstruction pipelines and single-
view learning-based methods, we ask: Can camera calibration be reliably solved
from sparse multi-view observations in the wild? To this end, we introduce
CalibAnyView (Fig. 1), a unified model that supports both single-view and
multi-view calibration, remaining robust where dense pipelines collapse. Our key
idea is to model cross-view geometric consistency within the learning architec-
ture, rather than treating it as a separate post-processing step. When multiple
views are available, the model aggregates complementary geometric evidence
across perspectives to resolve the intrinsic ambiguities of single-view estimation.
When only a single image is provided, the framework naturally reduces to robust
image-based inference. This unified design enables stable and physically consis-
tent estimation of camera intrinsics and gravity direction across diverse capture
scenarios, without requiring restrictive assumptions.

imagery or static scenes, making them unsuitable for learning from multi-view

Related Work
Single-image geometric calibration. Classical approaches exploit projective
geometry cues in a single image. Parallel lines converge at vanishing points (VPs)
from which camera roll, pitch, and focal length can be inferred [6,8]. VP detec-
tors include unconstrained line-clustering methods [25,36] and Manhattan-world
solvers built on three mutually orthogonal scene directions [10,26]. Radial distor-
tion can additionally be recovered from curved or covariant line segments [31,38].
These methods are geometrically precise in structured environments but break
down when relevant cues are sparse or absent, and they are restricted to the pin-
hole [10,26,36] or division model [31]. In contrast, our method imposes no such
structural requirements in single-view mode, and naturally generalizes across
camera models.

Learning-based single-image calibration. Deep learning has substantially
broadened the applicability of single-image calibration. Regression-based meth-
ods use CNNs or ViTs to directly predict focal length, distortion, or horizon
lines [5,19,33], but introduce no geometric constraints and generalize poorly to
unseen camera models [47,50]. Hybrid methods first predict an intermediate ge-
ometric representation and then recover camera parameters analytically or via
optimisation. Representatives include works that predict gravity-aligned surface
normals or classify line segments to obtain structured calibration cues [27, 57].
More recent approaches represent the image as per-pixel geometric quantities:
Perspective Fields [23] predict up-vectors and latitude per pixel, GeoCalib [50]
refines intrinsics from these maps through a differentiable Levenberg-Marquardt
solver, and AnyCalib [47] fits intrinsics in closed form from predicted per-pixel
ray directions, covering perspective, fisheye, and edited images. Single-image
calibration is nonetheless fundamentally ill-posed, and treating each frame inde-
pendently discards the geometric consistency present across views. We address
this limitation with an any-view formulation that handles both the single-image
case and sequences of arbitrary length within a unified model.
Multi-view calibration.
Classical multi-view methods divide into target-
based approaches [61] that use checkerboards or fiducial markers for bundle ad-
justment, and targetless self-calibration [37] based on epipolar geometry. Structure-
from-Motion [41] and SLAM [15] pipelines recover poses and intrinsics jointly,
though they require sufficient feature overlap between views. A line of learning-
based work bypasses this by performing self-supervised calibration from video,
jointly predicting depth or optical flow and camera parameters across pinhole,
radial, fisheye, and catadioptric models [12,14,15,49]. NeRF-based methods offer
another route, optimising flexible camera models together with scene geometry
during neural reconstruction [22, 56]. Moving to larger scale, Wang et al. [55]
incorporate a non-parametric camera model into a SfM pipeline, and ViPE [21]
recovers calibration, poses, and dense depth directly from in-the-wild video. Our
method differs in scope: rather than estimating full 6-DoF camera extrinsics,
it recovers intrinsics, distortion, and absolute gravity direction. These outputs
are complementary to pose, with gravity providing an orientation anchor not
tied to inter-view correspondence. Our any-view formulation is agnostic to the
input sequence length, offering reliable single-image calibration while effectively
leveraging temporal consensus across sparse views for further refinement.

3.1
Preliminaries
Camera Projection and Distortion Models Camera calibration fundamen-
tally models the transformation of a 3D point P = [X, Y, Z]⊤in the camera
coordinate system to a 2D pixel p = [u, v]⊤on the image plane. Under the
standard pinhole model, the 3D point is first projected onto a normalized image
plane as x = [X/Z, Y/Z]⊤. It is then scaled and shifted to pixel coordinates
as p = f · x + c, where f is the focal length, which directly determines the

camera’s Field of View (FoV), and c = [cx, cy]⊤represents the principal point.
Following common practice for images in the wild [23,50], we assume the image
crop is centered, and therefore fix the principal point c to exactly half the image
dimensions. This reduces the task to estimating the focal length f.
However, real-world images often deviate from the ideal pinhole projection
due to lens artifacts, which are typically addressed by specialized distortion mod-
els such as the Radial model [61] and the Unified Camera Model (UCM) [34]. By
accommodating these varied projection pathways, we unify the general intrinsic
calibration task as estimating a parameter set λ = {f, d}, where d ∈{k1, ξ}
encapsulates the specific lens distortion—with k1 for radial distortion and ξ for
UCM’s spherical projection.
The gravity direction in the camera frame is defined as a unit vector g =
[gx, gy, gz]⊤pointing towards the zenith. Specifically, it is related to the camera’s
pitch θ and roll ϕ via g = [sin ϕ cos θ, −sin θ, cos ϕ cos θ]⊤, which encapsulates
the absolute orientation of the camera. In our framework, we focus on the joint
recovery of the intrinsic parameters λ and the absolute orientation g across any
views.

two components: i) Up-vector field U ∈RH×W ×2, which predicts a unit vector
in the image plane pointing towards the projection of the zenith; ii) Latitude
field Φ ∈RH×W ×1, which predicts the angle between the viewing ray vp and
the horizon. Formally:

3.2
Unified Any-view Calibration Framework
We introduce CalibAnyView, a unified transformer-based framework designed
to accommodate camera calibration from an arbitrary number of views (N ≥1).
Given a sequence of input images I ∈RN×H×W ×3, our architecture captures
both view-specific visual context and inter-view geometric consistency through
an alternating attention mechanism. Building upon these latent representations,
the network estimates dense perspective fields (Ui, Φi) alongside a per-pixel con-
fidence map σi for each view i. The final calibration parameters, comprising the
shared camera intrinsics λ and the per-view gravity direction gi, are subse-
quently recovered through a joint geometric optimization process that enforces
shared intrinsic consistency across the entire sequence.
Any-View Geometric Latent Extraction. To extract comprehensive visual
features suitable for camera calibration, we first leverage DINOv2 [35] to ob-
tain dense patch-level representations from each input frame, which provides a
rich set of semantic priors by capturing objects with canonical orientations, such
as upright trees, human figures, or architectural structures, and scene layouts
that serve as implicit cues for camera parameters, including gravity and focal
length. Motivated by the 3D feed-forward method [52], we adopt the alternating-
attention mechanism to refine these representations through iterative geometric
reasoning. Specifically, the intra-frame self-attention blocks project these seman-
tic priors into a geometric manifold, effectively capturing structural constraints
such as gravity-aligned verticals and distorted perspective grids within each view.
Subsequently, the global cross-frame attention layers inject multi-view informa-
tion, allowing the model to establish spatio-temporal correspondences that en-
force shared intrinsic consistency across the sequence. This alternating process
effectively elevates low-level semantic features into a unified geometric represen-
tation, resolving scale and orientation ambiguities by aggregating structural cues
from diverse viewpoints.
DPT-based Universal Camera Head. To decode the high-dimensional geo-
metric latents into dense and interpretable representations, we utilize a predic-
tion head based on the Dense Prediction Transformer (DPT) architecture [39].
Recognizing that accurate geometric reasoning requires highly refined features
with substantial global context, we extract intermediate tokens from the latter
stages of the aggregator (specifically layers 15, 18, 21, and 23). Empirically, we
find that the representations from these deeper layers provide more consistent
structural priors than shallower stages, which is essential for capturing the sub-
tle geometric cues of camera calibration. These multi-scale features are fused
and progressively upsampled to generate perspective fields (U, Φ) at 1/4 of the
original image resolution to facilitate the extraction of holistic geometric pat-
terns and global structural cues, effectively filtering out pixel-level noise and
high-frequency local variations that might otherwise compromise the stability of
the subsequent optimization process.
Multi-view Geometric Optimization. Building upon the perspective align-
ment framework proposed in GeoCalib [50], we formulate the parameter recovery

Camera Motion Transfer. While panoramic spaces allow for arbitrary view
extraction, generating virtual trajectories from fixed view point [20] or through
synthetic sweeps [13] often yields unnatural motions that fail to capture the
complex dynamics of physical cameras. To emulate realistic camera behaviors,
we match camera trajectories extracted from CameraBench [30] to the 360◦clips
based on translation similarity [58]. To achieve this, we first run panoramic visual
SLAM [45] and ViPE [21] to extract camera trajectories from the 360◦clips and
CameraBench, respectively. Subsequently, we align each candidate perspective
trajectory to the panoramic clip’s translation using Umeyama fitting [48], con-
straining the alignment to vertical-axis rotation and scale normalization. The
top-K (K = 30) candidates with the lowest root-mean-square error (RMSE)
are then selected and their rotational dynamics are transferred to the virtual
camera. To further enrich the rotational diversity of the transferred trajectories,
we employ data augmentation strategies on the initial orientation and through
additive camera sweeping. Specifically, we first sample one consistent camera ro-
tation of yaw, pitch, and roll as a consistent offset. Then we sample two random

Multi-Camera Projection and Filtering. To inject diverse intrinsics and
lens distortions into the dataset, we project the equirectangular frames into vir-
tual cameras sampled from three distinct models: Unified Camera Model (UCM)
(50% probability), Pinhole (25%), and Simple Radial (25%). The UCM cameras
cover wide-angle to extreme fisheye lenses, with the horizontal field-of-view (FoV)
sampled between 105◦and 200◦and the distortion parameter ξ ∈[0.5, 2.3]. For
the Pinhole and Simple Radial models, the vertical FoV is sampled uniformly
from 20◦to 105◦, with the latter’s radial distortion coefficient k1 drawn from
a truncated normal distribution [50]. We then project the 360◦content onto
the image planes defined by these sampled models, yielding synthesized multi-
view videos with known ground-truth calibration. Finally, to ensure high visual
quality, we introduce a Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based filtering pipeline
utilizing Qwen2.5-VL [3]. We prompt the model to analyze a sample of frames
from each synthesized clip to detect artifacts such as black voids caused by in-
complete panoramas, text watermarks, artificial UI overlays, and unnatural CGI
content. To maintain dataset integrity, we enforce a strict quality control rule
that discards an entire panoramic source if over 10% of its derived clips exhibit
such artifacts. In total, the resulting dataset comprises ∼23.7K multi-view video
clips. Each clip spans 5 seconds (81 frames) at 16 fps, amounting to roughly 1.9M
frames in total with a resolution of 640×640.

Experiment
4.1
Experimental Setup
Training Datasets. Our framework is designed as a unified model capable
of calibrating camera parameters from an arbitrary number of views (N ≥
1). To demonstrate the effectiveness of our multi-view representation and the
impact of the proposed data pipeline, we evaluate two variants: Ours* and
Ours. The Ours* variant is trained exclusively on the OpenPano dataset [50],
which consists of pinhole images generated from static, gravity-aligned panora-
mas. This serves as a baseline to highlight the architectural improvements of
our Transformer-based aggregator over previous methods. In contrast, our pri-
mary model, Ours, is trained on a comprehensive mixture of OpenPano,
OpenPano-radial, and our newly proposed multi-view dataset (Sec. 3.3).
This full dataset significantly elevates the training challenge by incorporating
∼23.7K video clips and 1.9M frames, bridging the gap between static single-
view estimation and complex multi-view dynamics.

Our inference pipeline consists of two stages: (1) the network predicts dense
perspective fields from the input frames, and (2) a geometric optimizer recovers
the camera parameters from the predicted fields. To disentangle the contribu-
tions of the multi-view network and the multi-view optimizer, we design a 2 × 2
experimental protocol. On the network side, each batch is fed either as inde-
pendent single-image inputs (Single-view) without cross-view interaction, or as
a joint multi-view input (Multi-view) to activate cross-view attention, yielding
the per-batch perspective fields. On the optimizer side, the resulting fields are
then processed either with shared-intrinsic joint optimization (with shared in-
trinsics) or with independent per-frame optimization (without shared intrinsics).
This yields four configurations whose results are visualized in Fig. 4.
As shown in Fig. 4, for the network-predicted (Up and Latitude fields), and
gravity outputs (Roll and Pitch errors), the joint multi-view input consistently
yields substantially lower errors than the single-view counterpart. This demon-
strates that, through our alternating attention design and multi-view training
regime, the network acquires strong cross-frame geometric reasoning capabilities.
Examining the Relative Focal error and vFoV error trends reveals that Multi-
views consistently achieves lower errors than Single-view, regardless of which
optimizer configuration is employed. Moreover, within the Single-view and Multi-
view settings seperately, shared-intrinsic optimization consistently outperforms
independent per-frame optimization, with the gap widening as the number of
views increases. Overall, the calibration error drops sharply as the number of
views grows, demonstrating that our framework effectively resolves the inherent
geometric ambiguities of single-view calibration, confirming the benefit of our
joint optimization strategy.

5
Conclusion
In this work, we introduced CalibAnyView, a unified framework generalizing
camera calibration to an any-view paradigm. To bridge the gap between ide-
alized settings and complex real-world captures, we presented a large-scale, in-
the-wild multi-view video dataset. Our proposed transformer-based architecture,


## Page 18


18
B. Li et al.
combined with a physically consistent geometric optimization layer, effectively
leverages cross-view geometric consistency to resolve single-view ambiguities.
Extensive evaluations on multiple benchmarks demonstrate that our approach
not only surpasses prior state-of-the-art single-view methods but also achieves
substantial performance gains as more views are incorporated. Furthermore, our
unified model exhibits strong robustness to varying degrees of lens distortion,
eliminating the need for specialized radial-distortion variants. By providing sta-
ble and interpretable calibration in unconstrained environments, CalibAnyView
offers a robust foundation for downstream tasks such as 3D reconstruction and
robotic perception in the wild.
Limitations and Future Work..
Our method currently fixes the principal
point at the image centre and assumes shared intrinsics across the input se-
quence. These assumptions hold for most consumer cameras but may be violated
under zoom or asymmetric cropping scenarios, where per-frame single-view in-
ference serves as a natural fallback. Extending to off-centre principal points and
zoom-varying sequences remains future work.

Runtime Analysis
We evaluate runtime and peak GPU memory on TartanAir (N=10, RTX 4090,
GPU-synced) across four architecturally distinct baselines. CalibAnyView runs
at 6.6 FPS with 3991 MB peak GPU memory, compared with learning-SLAM-
based DroidCalib [15] (4.2 FPS / 5454 MB), ViT-based foundation model VGGT [52]
(7.5 FPS / 8662 MB), and CNN-based GeoCalib [50] (13.2 FPS / 511 MB). Our
method consumes only 45% of VGGT’s peak GPU memory and runs 1.6× faster
than DroidCalib, providing a favourable accuracy–efficiency trade-off across ar-
chitecturally distinct baselines.

D.1
Multi-Camera Synthesis and UCM Parameterization
To inject diverse intrinsics and lens distortions, we project the equirectangular
frames into virtual cameras sampled from three distinct models: Unified Camera
Model (UCM) (50% probability), Pinhole (25%), and Simple Radial (25%).
UCM Camera Categories and Sampling. For the UCM-based synthesis, we
categorize the virtual cameras into three lens-dependent groups based on their
field-of-view (FoV) and distortion intensity. We sample the horizontal field-of-
view (xFoV ) and the distortion parameter ξ uniformly within the following
ranges:
– Wide-angle: xFoV ∈[105◦, 140◦], ξ ∈[0.5, 0.95].
– Fisheye: xFoV ∈[140◦, 180◦], ξ ∈[1.05, 2.0].
– Extreme Fisheye: xFoV ∈[160◦, 200◦], ξ ∈[1.5, 2.3].
This categorical sampling ensures a balanced representation of various lens types,
from standard wide-angle optics to ultra-wide fisheye lenses typically found in
action cameras and security surveillance.
D.2
Quality Control and Automated Filtering
To maintain high visual fidelity, we employ a VLM-based filtering pipeline (Qwen2.5-
VL [3]) with the prompt detailed in Fig. 23. The model identifies artifacts such
as black voids from incomplete panoramas, artificial UI overlays, and unnatural
CGI content. We enforce a hierarchical rejection rule: an entire panoramic source
is discarded if more than 10% of its derived clips are flagged, ensuring the overall
integrity of the multi-view sequences.

panoramas, our pipeline effectively generates realistic training sequences with
diverse intrinsics and distortion patterns by re-projecting the panoramic content
onto augmented virtual camera trajectories.
