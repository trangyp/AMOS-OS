Abstract.
We present an algorithm for the simulation of three-dimensional spin glasses
deep in the low-temperature phase: Parallel Tempering enhanced with Houdayer moves
and with an entropic reservoir (PTHR). Although differences with the standard Houdayer
algorithm are small, PTHR allows us to equilibrate a large number of samples of L = 16
lattices with Gaussian couplings for temperatures T ≥0.2. We show that the computational
complexity displays better size scaling than standard Parallel Tempering. For finite sizes,
our method outperforms other cluster algorithms by a speedup factor of around 64. In close
analogy with standard Parallel Tempering, PTHR’s computational complexity strongly relates
to temperature chaos.
Keywords: cluster Monte Carlo methods, spin glasses, Parallel Tempering
1. Introduction
Spin glasses [1,2] are magnetic disordered alloys that have aroused the curiosity of physicists
from many points of view [3–6]. In particular, connections with computer science have been
emphasized, because finding the ground state of a spin-glass Hamiltonian in three spatial
dimensions —specifically, the Edwards-Anderson model [7, 8] with Ising spins— is an NP-
complete problem [9, 10]. Hence, simulating spin glasses at zero temperature, T = 0, is a
paradigmatic example of a hard computational problem. Yet, equilibrating three-dimensional

Boltzmann weight is more difficult at temperature T > 0 than at T = 0, there is empirical
evidence for it that we review next.
A review of modern approaches for sampling at T = 0 —i.e., finding the ground state—
can be found in Ref. [21]. Most relevant for us are the sizes reached in large-scale simulations
that found ground states for a significant number of problem instances —also named samples.
A sample is characterized by the set of couplings {Jij}, where Jij is the strength of the
pairwise interaction between spins σi and σj; see Eq. (1) below. In T = 0 simulations,
couplings are continuously distributed random variables (quite often the Jij are Gaussian-
distributed random variables, with zero mean and variance J; one typically chooses energy
units such that J = 1). Exact-solver simulations include Ref. [22], in lattices with linear sizes
L ≤8; Ref. [23], which reached L = 10 (although with open boundary conditions); and
Ref. [24], which reached L = 12 with periodic boundary conditions. On the other hand, we
have heuristic-solver simulations, i.e., simulations where the ground state was reached with
high probability. In this category, we find Ref. [25], which reached L ≤10, while Ref. [26]
went up to L ≤11. The absolute record to date is in Ref. [27], which obtained large statistics
using a genetic algorithm in lattices L ≤14. More recent work reaches good statistics for
L ≤12 and just some ten samples of L = 14 [28], so we think it is fair to say that the absolute
record still belongs to Ref. [27].
As for T > 0 simulations, the two main algorithms currently on the market are Parallel
Tempering (PT) [29,30] and Population Annealing (PA) [31–33], of which the latter is more
suited for massively parallel computations on a distributed architecture [34]. Both algorithms
are very sensitive to the minimal temperature reached in the simulation, Tmin, in the sense
that the lower Tmin the smaller the size L at which one manages to equilibrate. Consider, for
instance, the PT simulations of Refs. [35,36] with binary couplings (i.e., Jij = ±1 with 50%
probability). These authors went down to Tmin = 0.125 ≈0.11Tc on lattice sizes L ≤8,
where Tc=1.102(3) is the critical temperature [37].‡ For L = 12, however, they had to stop at
Tmin = 0.414 ≈0.38Tc and for larger sizes, using the Janus special-purpose computer [17],
they reached Tmin = 0.479 ≈0.44Tc (L = 16), Tmin = 0.625 ≈0.57Tc (L = 24), and
Tmin = 0.703 ≈0.64Tc (L = 32). At very low temperatures, Gaussian-distributed couplings,
which result in Tc = 0.95(4) [38], are usually regarded as preferable. For this version of the
model we have two PT simulations that went down to Tmin = 0.2 ≈0.21Tc (L = 8 [39]
and L = 10 [40]). On the PA side, we have Tmin = 0.01 ≈0.011Tc (L = 10 [41]),
Tmin = 0.333 ≈0.35Tc (L = 12 [42]), and Tmin = 0.42 ≈0.44Tc (L = 16 [43]). At least
in the case of PT [35, 44, 45] —but we strongly suspect this is also the case with PA— the
physical mechanism of temperature chaos, see Sec. 5, underlies the decline of the algorithmic
performance as L grows.

1.1. Computational challenge and simulation strategy
In this project we aim to equilibrate a large number of samples (≈2000) with Gaussian-
distributed couplings, down to temperature Tmin = 0.2 ≈0.21Tc, low enough to allow for a
smooth extrapolation to T = 0. Of course, the larger the L we reach, the better.
Our first attempts in this direction used the standard PT algorithm on more modern, faster
CPUs than the authors of Refs. [39,40] had access to. These standard PT simulations of ours
passed our equilibration test of choice, described in Sec. 2, down to Tmin = 0.2 for systems
of sizes L ≤12. Actually, we were also able to equilibrate a small number of systems with
L = 14. It was clear at this point, however, that some algorithmic improvement was necessary
if we were to expand the range of system sizes in our study [50].

An obvious candidate for an improved PT was adding Houdayer cluster moves to
the standard single-spin-flip Metropolis [47].
In this modified algorithm, two replicas
are simulated simultaneously because the cluster moves generate modifications in both
configurations. The method was originally proposed in 2D, where there is only a paramagnetic
phase as soon as T > 0 and clusters do not percolate. Indeed, the cluster move affords a very
significant speedup in the thermalization process on the square lattice [46]. Unfortunately, it
was clear from the outset [47] that the Houdayer clusters in 3D would percolate even at very
high temperatures. Conventional wisdom had it that the lattice would simply split into two
clusters.
It is well known that, when clusters are too big, the cluster move becomes a simple
symmetry transformation of the whole system, producing no algorithmic gain [51]. In the
case of spin glasses, when the Houdayer clusters are too big the two replicas simply exchange
their configurations. This probably explains why the Houdayer method was largely ignored
for simulations in space dimension D > 2, although Ref. [48] suggested that a significant
speedup can also be obtained in D = 3. We were initially skeptical, however, because the
authors of [48] were able to equilibrate only systems with L ≤12 down to temperatures
T ≥Tmin = 0.42 which was not an improvement over our own simulations with regular
PT [35, 45]. Nevertheless, having at our disposal a set of configurations equilibrated with
standard PT, we decided to study the geometry of the Houdayer clusters to decide whether an
algorithmic gain was achievable.
Let us recall that Houdayer’s clusters divide the lattice into connected components: two
neighboring lattice sites i and j belong to the same connected component if (and only if)
qi = qj [qi and qj are the respective site overlaps, see Eq. (2)]. Hence, if the global overlap
defined in Eq. (3) is q > 0 (q < 0), the majority of sites will belong to clusters with qi = 1
(qi = −1). Let us use majority to refer to the clusters with qiq > 0 (the minority will refer to
the clusters with qiq < 0), that is, the majority clusters have the same overlap sign as the full
lattice.
Now, see Fig. 1, we found that, at low temperatures, the largest cluster in both the
majority and the minority contained a significant fraction of the lattice.
Hence, flipping
the largest cluster, either from the majority or from the minority, essentially amounts to a
useless symmetry transformation. Interestingly enough, however, the second-largest cluster
in both sets contains a few dozen lattice sites whose flipping was potentially useful. This is
why we decided to opt for an all-cluster version of the Houdayer algorithm, analogous to the
Swendsen-Wang form [52] of the cluster algorithm for ferromagnetic systems; Refs. [47,48]
followed instead the analogue of the single-cluster approach [53].
A completely unexpected —and somewhat undesirable— consequence of the cluster
move, see Sec. 5 below, was that our favourite equilibration test [35,45,54] becomes useless
(the test focuses on the temperature diffusion of the different clones in the PT). This motivated
an additional modification of the algorithm, which we name the entropic reservoir and is
explained in Sec. 3. Thanks to this enhanced version of PT we have equilibrated systems
down to Tmin = 0.2 up to the size of L = 16 and nearly equilibrated L = 18 samples, see
Appendix A.

3. Description of the algorithm
Our procedure resembles the one proposed by Houdayer [47] for the 2D Ising spin glass, but
with a modification to the cluster move and the addition of an entropic reservoir on top of PT.
For the PT procedure we run NT simulations at uniformly spaced temperatures Tk. In
order to perform a cluster move, we need at least Nr = 2 replicas of the system for each
temperature. We can see this as having Nr groups of NT configurations. We refer to the

configurations in the same “replica group” as clones, while we will refer to configurations
belonging to different replica groups as replicas. Unless otherwise specified, when we refer
to replicas we mean configurations belonging to different replica groups but at the same
temperature.
An elementary Monte Carlo step (EMCS) develops as follows:
(i) 10 full-system updates with local moves at fixed temperature;
(ii) 1 all-cluster update (ACU) step;
(iii) 1 temperature exchange (or PT) step;
(iv) 1 entropy-reservoir (ER) step.
The full-system update at fixed temperature is performed independently for every replica
and for every clone. Every spin in the lattice is given a chance to be flipped during the full-
system sweep. In this work, a sweep consists in the sequential update of the whole lattice in
lexicographic order. A common choice is to use Metropolis updates for the spins, but other
algorithms such as a heat bath could be used. Most of our simulations were conducted with
Metropolis but we also employed Microcanonical Simulated Annealing (MicSA) [58], which
is slower in terms of convergence towards the equilibrium distribution but is faster in terms of
CPU time. The choice of 10 full-system updates comes from a balance between the individual
computational cost of the steps of the algorithm.
The all-cluster update (ACU) involves the two clones from replicas a and b that occupy
a given temperature Tk in the PT grid. One can use any number of replicas Nr and divide
them in pairs (a, b), but in this work we stick to Nr = 2. The move consists of the following
steps: First, we compute the site overlap [Eq. (2)] for each site i of the system. Second, the
lattice is divided into connected components (or clusters). Two lattice nearest neighbours i
and j belong to the same cluster if, and only if, qiqj > 0. Third, we independently choose
a random sign ηc = ±1 for every cluster, with 50% probability. All the spins belonging to
cluster c, in both replicas, are multiplied by ηc (if site i belongs to cluster c, then σi,a →ηcσi,a
and σi,b →ηcσi,b).
Let us now argue that the cluster move is symmetric in probability with its reversed
move. The crucial point is that the local overlaps are not modified by the cluster move,
qi →ηcσi,a ηcσi,b = qi. Now, the qi being identical, the lattice connected components are
the same for the original and transformed configurations. Thus, the probability of choosing
a set of signs {ηc} is identical for the direct and for the reversed cluster move. And, if one
chooses again the same set of signs {ηc} for the final configuration, the original configuration
is recovered.
Furthermore, the cluster move leaves the total Hamiltonian H = H(a) + H(b) (i.e., the
Boltzmann weight of the replica pair) unchanged. Therefore, being symmetric in probability,
the ACU step of our algorithm verifies detailed balance; see, for instance, Ref. [51].
In order to save computer time, we restrict the ACU move to clones occupying
temperatures T < 1. This choice relates to the temperature dependence of the size of the
second- and third-largest clusters. As shown in Fig. 1, these clusters reach a maximum size
for a sample-dependent temperature and then rapidly shrink as T increases above Tc. Our

Let us remark the two key differences with Houdayer’s original proposal [47]. First,
we flip clusters with both signs of the overlap while in the original algorithm only clusters
with negative overlap were considered. Of course, Houdayer was aware that positive-overlap
clusters can be flipped as well [47], but he wanted to keep the algorithm valid also when a
magnetic field is applied (in a field, the cluster move keeps the total energy unchanged only if
the overlap is negative). Second, in the original algorithm only one cluster is traced and then
flipped with probability one, in close analogy with Wolff’s single-cluster method [53]. We
think ACU is preferable in our case because whatever the speedup that the cluster move will
afford, it will come from the second- and third-largest clusters, which would rarely be picked
in a single-cluster update.
The PT step is conducted independently for each replica. Each clone in a replica occupies
a different temperature in the PT grid, which is described in Appendix A. In the PT step, the
clones that occupy neighbouring temperatures in the grid, Tk < Tk+1, attempt to exchange
their temperature, a move that is accepted with a rule that verifies detailed balance [29,30,59].
The rationale of the PT move is that every clone alternates time lapses of quick
decorrelation —when the clone occupies the highest temperatures in the grid— with
periods of exploration of the nearest free-energy minimum —when the clone occupies low
temperatures. For later reference, let us remark that every clone performs a random walk over
the temperature grid, which we shall refer to as the T random walk (TRW). In a sane PT
simulation the TRW should be ergodic over the temperature grid.
The ER step is carried out independently for each replica. This step involves the use
of additional clones at T = Tmax. When a clone in the PT hits the highest temperature
Tmax, it enters the ER, where it runs for a number of Metropolis steps high enough to get an
independent spin configuration. After its spin configuration has been refreshed at Tmax, the
clone is exchanged with the clone that occupies the highest temperature T = Tmax in the PT.
In this way, the clone that has just exited the ER could have its temperature lowered during
the PT step of the algorithm. Should the clone stay at Tmax, it would be pushed back into the
ER to be refreshed again.

6. Conclusions
We have obtained an effective cluster method for the simulation of three-dimensional
spin glasses down to very low temperatures by introducing some simple modifications of
Houdayer’s cluster algorithm [47].
Under some conditions, our algorithm outperforms
previous proposals [48] by a speedup factor ≈64.¶ We have related this large speedup to
the sizes of the second and third clusters —as ordered by their size— for both signs of the
overlap. These clusters are flipped with much larger frequency using the all-cluster scheme
advocated here than they are with single-cluster update schemes [47, 48]. An unexpected
feature of the algorithm is that the temperature random walk performed during the PT part of
the algorithm is extremely fast compared to plain PT simulations [35, 45, 54]. In particular,
the orders-of-magnitude sample-to-sample fluctuations for the mixing time of the temperature
random walk completely disappear.
We should mention two limitations. First, the sizes of the second and third clusters have a
maximum at temperatures T ≈0.4Tc. This is the temperature where the maximum speedup is
expected. Unfortunately, the sizes of the clusters diminish for smaller temperatures. Second,
although we have ascertained that equilibration time varies significantly from sample to

method is far better than it is in cubic lattices [55].

are harder to equilibrate. Temperature chaos has turned out to be a distinctive feature of hard

Appendix A.1. Multispin coding for Gaussian couplings through look-up tables
Multispin coding [49] is a very efficient technique to simulate several system in parallel,
by profiting from the fact that boolean operations (AND, XOR, OR, etc.)
are carried out
simultaneously and independently for all bits in a computer word. If one notices that our
spins σi = ±1 can take only two values, it comes naturally to identify a spin with a single bit
within the computer word [i.e., a binary variable bj = (1 + σi)/2 = 0, 1]. However, multispin
coding is most efficient when the couplings in the Hamiltonian are J = ±1, because in that
case the energy increment caused by a spin flip can take only a small number of values (for
a hypercubic lattice in D dimensions ∆E = 0, ±4, . . . ± 4D). In this simple setting it is
relatively straightforward to find a few boolean operations that implement exactly the (say)
Metropolis algorithm; see for instance Ref. [62]. The problem becomes more complicated
when the energy change ∆E is a real variable, as it is the case for Gaussian couplings.
The above difficulty has been efficiently solved in the past [46] using a LUT.
Unfortunately, the solution of Ref. [46] is quite specific to the square lattice’s connectivity 4:
the neighbourhood of the spin that is being updated is determined by just four binary
couplings, hence 24 = 16 different possibilities per lattice site. This results in short LUTs and
in a small number of boolean operations that decide whether or not the spin should be flipped.
In D = 3, the local neighbourhood of a site consists of 6 spins and hence 26 = 64 different
possibilities per site. Furthermore, the necessary time to equilibrate may vary significantly
from sample to sample, recall Section 5.1. Thus, we have preferred to avoid multispin-coding
solutions that implement different samples in a single computer word.

In its most ambitious form, our program stores in a LUT the 64 possible values of the
Metropolis acceptance probability min{1, exp(−∆E/T)}. The 8 systems in a computer word
compare their acceptance probability with 8 independent random numbers. Specifically we
use streaming extensions and 256-bit computer words to obtain a parallel implementation
of 8 independent xoshiro256++ pseudorandom number generators [63]. This approach
is extremely effective for small systems.

probabilities per site in the LUT (recall that NT is the number of temperatures in the PT
grid). The LUT size quickly grows with L because NT ∝LD/2. Furthermore, the 8 systems
coded in the same computer word are at a different temperature, hence the 64NT probabilities
in the LUT need to be accessed in a random order. It is then unsurprising that the efficiency
of this approach deteriorates with system size.

since all 8 systems in the reservoir are at the same temperature. Indeed, the reader will have
noticed that the entire ER can be used with a single computer word per lattice site.
