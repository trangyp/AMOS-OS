Abstract
Achieving practical quantum advantage on fault-tolerant
quantum computers (FTQC) is fundamentally constrained
by the substantial spatial and temporal overheads required
to map logical operations onto physical hardware. Exist-
ing compilation approaches typically adopt coarse-grained,
slice-based abstractions that overlook fine-grained microar-
chitectural effects, such as routing contention, leading to in-
efficient resource utilization and limited alignment between
algorithm structure and hardware capabilities.
This work presents a microarchitecture-aware compila-
tion approach that integrates algorithmic structure directly
with lattice surgery (LS) execution. By leveraging the commu-
tativity of C-Phase operations, the method transforms inher-
ently sequential gate sequences into concurrent multi-target
interactions, effectively removing artificial dependencies and
exposing significant instruction-level parallelism. To enable
this, we design a dynamic, event-driven scheduling strategy
that accurately models spatial layout and routing constraints,
allowing operations to overlap in time while minimizing con-
tention. Through improved coordination of computation and
communication, this approach substantially reduces idle re-
sources and achieves up to a 59.7× reduction in execution
time compared to standard baselines.

Background and Related Work
2.1
Quantum error correction and surface codes
Bridging the gap between the hardware’s physical qubit er-
ror rates and the logical error rates needed for large-scale
quantum algorithms requires Quantum Error Correction
(QEC) [13, 29]. The surface code is one leading QEC candi-
date due to compatibility with 2D nearest-neighbor archi-
tectures, encoding logical qubits as contiguous patches of
physical data and measurement qubits (Figure 3). However,
it introduces massive qubit overhead, making architecture a
first-order concern. Systems must carefully balance compu-
tation, communication, and auxiliary processes like magic
state distillation [4, 32] under tight resource constraints.
2.2
Lattice Surgery and Patch-Based Layouts
To perform logical operations between these logical qubits,
Lattice Surgery [17, 43] is the leading paradigm in surface-
code FTQC. Logical qubits are encoded as 2D data patches,
and computation proceeds via merge and split operations
that measure joint stabilizers (e.g., 𝑋𝑋, 𝑍𝑍). Because these
operations require physical proximity, performance depends
heavily on the layout of logical qubits. Operations are exe-
cuted by dynamically routing paths through intermediate
ancilla patches. As shown in Figure 4, LS operations span
multiple clock cycles (each requiring 𝑑syndrome measure-
ment rounds) involving sequential measurements and patch

LS execution follows one of two models: Pauli Product

bottlenecks. Consequently, near-term FTQC systems will
likely rely on the more resource-efficient Clifford+non-Clifford
model. We focus on this standard gate set, where achieving
high performance under strict spatio-temporal constraints
demands precise, globally aware compilation strategies.
2.3
Universal Gate Sets and Rotation Primitives
Fault-tolerant quantum computation relies on different uni-
versal gate sets depending on the regime. In fully fault-
tolerant (FFT) systems, Clifford+𝑇is standard, where ar-
bitrary rotations 𝑅𝑧(𝜃) are decomposed into Clifford+𝑇cir-
cuits (via Gridsynth [39]), increasing 𝑇-count and depth. In
early fault-tolerant (EFT) settings, rotations can instead be
executed via direct 𝑅𝑧(𝜃) injection (albeit at lower fidelity),
avoiding this decomposition overhead [1, 25, 45]. Within
the Clifford+𝑇model, high-fidelity |𝑇⟩states are generated
either via magic state distillation (MSD) [7, 33], which relies
on dedicated factories and routing-heavy infrastructure, or
via magic state cultivation (MSC) [15], a protocol consisting
of three stages (injection, cultivation, and escape), which
produces |𝑇⟩states within a single patch using iterative er-
ror suppression and reduced qubit overhead. These regimes
differ primarily in how non-Clifford resources are supplied,
leading to distinct compilation challenges in managing either
magic-state routing or rotation injection (more in appendix
Table 1). Across these settings, performance is ultimately de-
termined by how efficiently these resources are routed and
scheduled on a layout, motivating Qomet’s unified execution

2.4
Prior Compilation Work
Logical compilers for surface-code architectures typically
address the NP-complete Surface Code Mapping and Routing
(SCMR) problem by optimizing qubit placement and ancilla
routing [49]. State-of-the-art Clifford+𝑇compilers predomi-
nantly rely on slice-based abstractions, partitioning execu-
tion into discrete, synchronized time steps [35, 47]. However,
these rigid synchronization barriers and the assumption of
uniform operation latencies lead to routing underutilization,
stalls, and lost instruction-level parallelism. Furthermore,
existing models often employ simplified cost models that
neglect the non-trivial latencies of Clifford gates and re-
sets. They also frequently overlook algorithmic structures,
such as symmetry or multi-target interactions common in
QFT and QAOA, missing opportunities for execution paral-
lelism [16]. To address these inefficiencies, Qomet replaces
slice-based scheduling with a cycle-accurate, dependency-
aware execution model. By treating the lattice as a continu-
ous spatio-temporal resource, Qomet enables fine-grained
operation overlap and eliminates artificial synchronization,
significantly improving hardware utilization and execution.

Multi-Target CNOTs and Fine-Grained
Execution Scheduling
We propose a paradigm shift in how we route and sched-
ule logical operations to fundamentally reduce the execu-
tion time of quantum applications in FTQC. Current limita-
tions—specifically false dependencies and rigid synchroniza-
tion via wave-like slices—stem from a failure to leverage the
microarchitectural properties of LS. By bridging high-level
algorithmic symmetries with low-level hardware primitives,
we unlock a new dimension of parallelism.
Our approach is based on two key observations: Multi-
Target Parallelism, where we leverage an LS-supported
multi-target interaction mechanism that, combined with the
symmetry and commutativity of gate sequences, eliminates
false dependencies and increases Instructions Per Cycle (IPC),
and Cycle-Accurate Scheduling, where we replace coarse-
grained slices with a cycle-level scheduler that captures fine-
grained route-dependent costs, enabling temporal overlap
of operations that were previously serialized.

3.2
Fine-Grained Cost Modeling and Scheduling
We replace the logical time-slice abstraction with a cycle-
accurate scheduling model. Traditional compilers assume
uniform latency (typically one cycle per slice), leading to
significant inefficiencies. In practice, operations have hetero-
geneous costs: lattice surgery requires 𝑂(𝑑) cycles, Clifford
gates (H, S) and patch resets each take 𝑂(1) cycles, while
rotation injection latency depends on the chosen protocol
(EFT vs. FFT). Slice-based compilers enforce synchronization
to the slowest operation, causing unnecessary idling. In con-
trast, our scheduler tracks the grid at cycle-level granularity
and releases resources immediately upon completion. This
enables temporal overlapping (pipelining). For example,
once a 𝑇-state routed from a magic-state ancilla qubit to a
program qubit completes, the next dependent instruction
can begin immediately without stalling.
3.2.1
Support for Fault Tolerant protocols. The pro-
posed scheduling methodology is designed to work for all
cases, encompassing the distinct operational requirements of
both the Early Fault-Tolerant (EFT) and Fully Fault-Tolerant
(FFT) regimes. In the EFT context, the focus is on Continuous
Angle Injection [1, 25, 45], where arbitrary 𝑅𝑧(𝜃) rotations
are injected directly into the logical patch. While these oper-
ations typically involve lower latency than full distillation,
their high frequency in many quantum algorithms demands
precise, cycle-level coordination to prevent the data path
from stalling.
In the FFT regime, the scheduler must manage the trans-
port of magic states to data patches. When using MSD [7,
33], this involves long-range routing from centralized multi-
patch factories. When using MSC [15] , each magic state
can be grown in a patch adjacent to the target data patch,
eliminating routing overhead entirely but introducing per-
patch cultivation latency that must be overlapped with other
operations. In both cases, the scheduler treats magic states
as dynamic architectural resources rather than static gate
costs, routing or overlapping them through the 2D grid with
the same spatio-temporal precision as primary data gates.
By treating the layout as a continuous resource, our ap-
proach reduces qubit idling from routing and scheduling
delays, limiting decoherence from unnecessary wait times.
Qomet generates a compile-time schedule that resolves rout-
ing conflicts statically, enabling immediate activation of logi-
cal patches as resources free up, improving classical–quantum
scalability. Together with multi-target interactions and fine-
grained scheduling, this increases parallelism and resource
utilization, especially in structured workloads such as QAOA
and QFT. Overall, this shifts execution from rigid slice-based
abstractions to a high-throughput, LS-aligned pipeline under
fault-tolerant constraints.
4
Qomet: A Microarchitecture-Aware
Compilation Framework
Qomet is a microarchitecture-aware, C-Phase-aware com-
piler that directly optimizes the spatio-temporal execution of
logical circuits on FTQC architectures. Rather than treating
execution as a sequence of discrete steps, Qomet models the
2D logical layout as a continuously evolving resource, en-
abling fine-grained scheduling, reduced routing contention,
and improved hardware utilization.
At a high level, Qomet consists of three tightly coupled
stages: (1) multi-target grouping, which identifies paralleliz-
able interactions; (2) routing-aware packing, which maxi-
mizes utilization of available ancilla resources; and (3) rota-
tion realization, which implements logical rotations using
either direct injection or magic-state routing. Together, these
stages produce a layout-aware execution plan that is subse-
quently refined by a cycle-accurate scheduler.
In this section, we use QAOA as a representative work-
load, where the cost layer consists of a set of C-Phase op-
erations. Each C-Phase is decomposed into a CNOT–𝑅𝑧(𝜃)–
CNOT sequence ( Figure 5), requiring coordinated routing
and scheduling of both multi-qubit interactions and rotation
primitives.
4.1
Fine-Grained Latency Cost Modeling
Existing compilers typically assign uniform costs to LS oper-
ations, abstracting away important LS-level effects. In con-
trast, Qomet employs a fine-grained, LS-aware cost model
that captures the spatial and temporal characteristics of ex-
ecution on a 2D lattice. In particular, our model accounts
for factors such as routing directionality, patch orientation,
and the overheads associated with coordination and state
updates during execution. As illustrated in Figure 4, a logical
CNOT executed via lattice surgery is not a single-step event
but a sequence of discrete hardware-level instructions:
1. An initial ZZ measurement (1 clock cycle) to begin
the interaction.
2. A mandatory patch rotation stage (1 clock cycle [43])
to align ancilla boundaries with the routing path, fol-
lowed by a secondary ZZ measurement (1 clock cycle).
3. A final XX measurement stage (1 clock cycle) to com-
plete the CNOT sequence.
Each of these clock cycles represents 𝑑syndrome measure-
ment rounds, where 𝑑is the code distance. To accurately
estimate the cost of these logical operations, Qomet tracks a
persistent orientation map of the 2D lattice. This map is
updated dynamically after each route completion, allowing
the compiler to determine if a patch already possesses the

The Slice-Based version yields a slice-based execution plan

formation and routing-level optimization.
4.6
The Qomet Scheduler
The slice-based method already achieves substantial cycle
savings over prior slice-based compilers by replacing point-
to-point routes with multi-target fan-out groups. However,
it still inherits a key limitation of slice abstractions: a rigid
synchronization barrier between consecutive groups. No
new instruction can begin executing until the critical path
in the current group completes, even if resources free up
earlier. This barrier is not hardware-imposed but arises from
7


## Page 8


Dhanvi Bharadwaj, Siddharth Dangwal, Yuewen Hou, and Gokul Subramanian Ravi
conservative slice-based scheduling. Qomet removes this
constraint by treating the 2D ancilla grid as a continuous
spatio-temporal resource and issuing routing operations as
soon as resources become available. Our scheduler exposes
finer-grained timing and enables more efficient resource uti-
lization than slice-based baselines. The following subsections
describe the design of the scheduler.
4.6.1
Event-Driven Cycle-Accurate Execution. We re-
place slice-based execution with an event-driven scheduler
that models routing as a discrete-event simulation over the
physical grid. Each operation is scheduled as an event with
a precise duration, and a priority queue advances time to
the next completion, releasing resources and immediately
dispatching newly ready operations. This removes idle time
caused by slice boundaries and produces cycle counts that
reflect true execution under resource constraints.
Clock cycles are computed using the fine-grained cost
model from Section 4.1, calculated on the current state of
the layout. A persistent orientation map, that keeps tracks
the orientation of the patches in the layout, is updated after
each completed route, enabling reuse-aware cost estimation
that captures locality effects in dense layouts.
4.6.2
Three-Stage Pipelining Across Groups. Each C-
Phase group is decomposed into three stages (Figure 4): a
multi-target CNOT sweep (Stage A), a rotation layer (Stage
B), and a final CNOT sweep (Stage C). In slice-based execu-
tion, these stages are executed sequentially per group with
global grid resets enforcing strict slice boundaries.
When Stage A of group 𝑘completes, its Steiner-tree an-
cilla patches are immediately released. Group 𝑘then pro-
ceeds to Stage B, which uses only local ancilla resources
for rotation or magic-state injection and does not require
the fan-out routing region. This enables the scheduler to
opportunistically launch Stage A of group 𝑘+1 (or earlier
pending stages) on the freed resources. As a result, execution
becomes deeply pipelined: Stage A of one group overlaps
with Stage B of another, while Stage C of a previous group
completes elsewhere on the grid. This overlap keeps the lay-
out continuously utilized and eliminates idle gaps between
slices, yielding substantial reductions in total execution time
beyond grouping and packing optimizations.
To ensure progress under high contention, we also imple-
ment a specific dispatch priority policy. At each scheduling
timestep, the dispatcher prioritizes Stage C candidates over
Stages A and B. This ordering is a mechanical necessity for
maintaining grid throughput: completing a Stage C operation
immediately releases the locks on both control and target
data qubits, thereby expanding the pool of the next Stage
A instructions. Deferring Stage C in favor of new Stage A
work would allow qubit locks to accumulate, progressively
narrowing the scheduling window and risking deadlocks
or "lock-induced stalls" in congested layouts. By executing
Stage C instructions first, the scheduler maintains maximum
flexibility for subsequent dispatches and prevents resource-
contention bottlenecks from propagating forward through
the execution timeline.
4.6.3
Dynamic Search for Multi-target CNOT. Unlike
the slice-based method, multi-target groups are formed dy-
namically during execution in the scheduler. At the end of
each event (triggered by a route completion), the scheduler
re-evaluates all ready operations and re-runs multi-target
formation on the current grid state. This is necessary be-
cause readiness evolves continuously: completion of Stage
A enables Stage B, Stage B enables Stage C, and Stage C re-
leases data qubits that may unblock new Stage A operations.
As a result, optimal grouping depends on the instantaneous
availability of ancilla resources and cannot be determined
statically.
In practice, grouping proceeds greedily by incrementally
adding candidate gates that remain routable under current
occupancy constraints, forming the largest feasible Steiner-
realizable set. If conflicts prevent full fan-out formation, the
scheduler falls back to point-to-point routing for remaining
operations, ensuring progress while preserving parallelism
whenever possible.
4.6.4
𝑅𝑧(𝜃) Stage. In the EFT regime, Stage B requires only
a free ancilla patch adjacent to the target qubit. The scheduler
performs patch shuffling by selecting a neighboring ancilla
and reserving it for 𝜏𝑟𝑧(𝜃) cycles, making rotation scheduling
typically immediate with negligible routing overhead.
A similar mechanism is used in the FFT regime under MSC,
where Qomet allocates a nearby ancilla patch to generate a
magic state via MSC, which is then consumed through LS-
based injection. MSC preparation is treated as a background
process that proceeds in parallel with computation when lo-
cal ancilla resources are available. When multiple candidates
exist, the scheduler selects the nearest patch to minimize
routing distance and avoid contention with active fan-out
structures, decoupling state availability from the critical path
while respecting lattice constraints. This allows Qomet to
hide much of the preparation latency behind useful compu-
tation without dedicated factories or global synchronization.
For the MSD protocol, Stage B uses cost-aware selection
of MS patches based on the grid (more infornmation in ap-
pendix section 4.4.2) rather than static assignment. Under
pipelined execution, contention is resolved dynamically by
assigning each operation the nearest available patch and
rerouting to alternatives as needed, forming a contention-
aware T-state delivery mechanism that adapts to runtime
conditions.

DASCOT Baseline: The primary state-of-the-art (SoTA)
baseline is DASCOT, a dependency-aware compiler designed
specifically for magic-state distillation-based execution mod-
els. DASCOT formulates the SCMR problem with the ob-
jective of minimizing the total number of execution slices
through simulated annealing. However, its simulated anneal-
ing based search becomes computationally expensive for
large logical grids with high instruction-level parallelism.
Moreover, DASCOT is designed for magic-state distillation-
based fault-tolerant systems, where its optimization objec-
tive is fundamentally tied to slice minimization.
Greedy Compilation Baseline: While DASCOT pro-
vides a strong baseline for standard configurations, we ob-
serve that it does not perform effectively in non-traditional
layouts, high Magic State (MS) density, or workloads with a
large number of𝑇gates. In particular, even with a maximum
runtime budget of 48 hours on HPC systems, the simulated
annealing procedure often fails to converge to high-quality
solutions for large, constrained layouts (e.g., 20 program
qubits in QAOA with gridsynth precision set to 10−6). To ad-
dress this, we implement a custom Greedy Compiler that uses
a priority-based heuristic for pathfinding and resource allo-
cation. This baseline serves as a high-throughput alternative
when DASCOT becomes intractable, ensuring comparisons
against the best available heuristic under high-density and
routing-constrained regimes. We provide additional imple-
mentation details in Appendix B.

7
Discussion and Related Work
Most existing research in FTQC compilation is defined by
slice-based abstractions that fail to leverage the microarchi-
tectural nuances of LS. Frameworks targeting PPM architec-
tures face significant hurdles: the Lattice Surgery Compiler
(LSC) [47] lacks active program optimization and suffers from
high serialization, DASCOT [35] introduces dependency-
awareness via simulated annealing, but remain bottlenecked
by search times and a slice-minimization focus that ignores
heterogeneous latencies. Furthermore, while schedulers like
RESCQ [40] address resource management in EFT architec-
tures, they are not generalizable to the FFT regime. More
broadly, these prior efforts focus on orthogonal goals and
do not explicitly co-optimize quantum algorithms with the
underlying LS execution model, leaving substantial perfor-
mance opportunities unexplored. Current optimization ef-
forts also lack fine-grained, layout-aware cost models. High-
level analytical frameworks such as FLASQ [19] abstract
away the physical complexity of 2D lattices by assuming
"fluid" resource allocation, which fails to provide precise
costs required for executable compilation. Similarly, while
the EFT-VQA [10] framework provides a robust analytical
model for algorithms on EFT systems, it does not explicitly
calculate physical execution costs. Beyond cost modeling,
existing approaches also miss circuit-aware optimization op-
portunities that arise from jointly reasoning about gate struc-
ture, qubit layout, and routing constraints. In contrast, NISQ-
era compilation techniques emphasize co-design with circuit
rewriting [21], transpilation heuristics [24], and topology-
aware mapping [28] , but they are typically not designed
for fault-tolerant regimes and do not extend naturally to
large-scale LS execution.
Qomet addresses these gaps by integrating a cycle-accurate
cost model with hardware-native multi-target interactions,
while also enabling circuit-aware transformations that jointly
optimize algorithm structure and LS execution. This combi-
nation of flexibility in circuit construction and layout-aware
scheduling can further generalize to other domains such
as Hamiltonian simulation [8], where different physical im-
plementations may vary, but the need for co-optimization
between algorithm design and spatial execution remains
fundamental.
8
Conclusion
Realizing FTQC requires efficient logical compilation. How-
ever, current slice-based compilers ignore hardware laten-
cies, causing severe resource underutilization. We introduce
Qomet, a microarchitecture-aware compiler that exploits al-
gorithmic symmetries for multi-target interactions and uses
cycle-accurate scheduling to eliminate false-dependencies.
This unlocks hidden parallelism, achieving up to a 59.7×
speedup over baselines and scaling to complex workloads
like QAOA and QFT where prior methods fail. These results
aim to establish hardware–algorithm co-design as necessary
for practical quantum execution.

Dynamic Multi-target CNOT Formation
A key distinction between the scheduler and the static slice-
based version is that fan-out groups are not fixed at compile
time. At each scheduling decision point—triggered by any
route completion event—the scheduler inspects the full set of

continuously: a Stage A completion makes Stage B ready for

Discussion
The greedy compiler is layout-aware and parallelism-aware,
incorporating device-specific costs and MRV-based sched-
uling. However, it is fundamentally myopic: decisions are
made per round without global coordination, backtracking,
or cross-timestep optimization. This limitation motivates the
globally-aware strategies in our framework.
