COMPOSE: Composing Future Theorems from
Citations and Formal Structure
David Busbib
Michael Werman
Hebrew University of Jerusalem
Abstract
A plausible future mathematical claim must satisfy two constraints: it should follow
the direction of prior work and respect the formal dependencies that constrain what
can validly follow. Existing approaches typically model only one of these sources,
producing claims that are either weakly grounded or insufficiently motivated. We
introduce grounded future mathematical generation, where the goal is to generate a
plausible future theorem-like claim for an anchor paper using two complementary
sources of context: its scientific citation graph and aligned formal theorem depen-
dency graph. To address this setting, we propose COMPOSE, a dual-graph frame-
work that conditions a language model on both scientific citation context and formal
theorem structure. To support this setting, we construct a dataset of 108K paired
scientific-formal graph examples from arXiv and Mathlib, together with a bench-
mark of 47K future papers from 2024–2025. Experiments show that COMPOSE
outperforms strong baselines on retrieval to real future papers and achieves the
best overall performance under LLM-judge evaluation, producing more grounded
and mathematically richer outputs. These results show that future mathematical
generation benefits from combining scientific context with formal structure. Project
page is available at https://david-busbib.github.io/COMPOSE-page/.

Introduction
Large language models have shown growing promise in anticipating future scientific contributions
from existing knowledge across many domains [Si et al., 2024, Lu et al., 2024a, Wang et al., 2026].
However, in the mathematical domain, a plausible future result must both extend the research
trajectory of prior work [Lakatos, 1976, Davies et al., 2021] but also respect the logical dependencies
that determine what can validly follow from existing definitions, lemmas, and theorems [Yang
et al., 2023, Uskuplu et al., 2025, Cabral et al., 2025]. Thus, the task is not only to produce
fluent mathematical text, but to generate a result that is both scientifically meaningful and logically
grounded.
This challenge is reflected in two complementary sources of mathematical knowledge: scientific
papers, which capture how ideas develop across the literature and indicate which directions are worth
pursuing [Azher et al., 2025, Baek et al., 2025, He-Yueya et al., 2026], and formal theorem libraries
such as Lean’s Mathlib, which encode mathematical results together with explicit dependency struc-
ture. Combining the two is non-trivial because the same content appears differently in each source,
and because the two sources are organized by fundamentally different relational structures [Gao
et al., 2024a, Zhang et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2022]. Yet existing approaches tackle only one side:
models trained on scientific text alone can identify plausible research directions but may generate
claims that are not grounded in formal mathematical dependencies [Wang et al., 2024b, Radensky
et al., 2026, Baek et al., 2025, He-Yueya et al., 2026]; theorem-proving systems [Wang et al., 2025a,
First et al., 2023, Yang et al., 2023] reason over formal structure but lack the surrounding scientific
context needed to anticipate which directions are most promising; and structure-based methods

In this work, we propose COMPOSE, a system for generating plausible future mathematical claims
from an anchor paper. This is carried out by jointly modeling scientific context and formal math-

a scientific graph built from the anchor paper, selected cited papers, and extracted theorems, and
(ii) a formal graph built by aligning those theorems to corresponding formal theorems and their
dependency structure. These two graphs are encoded separately and fused into a shared representation
that conditions an LLM decoder on both the scientific direction of the papers and the formal structure
of the underlying mathematics. Figure 1 provides an overview of the full pipeline. To learn this
representation effectively, training proceeds in two stages: we first optimize the graph encoders
and fusion module with alignment objectives, and then fine-tune the decoder for graph-conditioned
generation. The model then generates a theorem-like mathematical text that represents a plausible

Related Work
Future Scientific Generation.
Prior work studies future scientific generation in several ways. One
line of work generates future research directions from prior literature using retrieval or iterative
retrieval-and-reasoning over related papers [Azher et al., 2025, Baek et al., 2025, Radensky et al.,
2026]. Another line of work uses citation structure or temporal supervision for the task [Wang
et al., 2026, 2024b]. GIANTS [He-Yueya et al., 2026] uses citation-based scientific context to guide
generation, although it targets future scientific summaries rather than theorem-like claims. Some
work also explores structure-aware scientific generation using external graphs or chain-of-ideas
frameworks [Gao et al., 2025, Xiong et al., 2024]. Across these settings, existing systems rely
on textual, citation-based, or structured scientific context, without combining the formal theorem
structure that constrains what can validly follow from prior results.
Formal Mathematical Reasoning.
Formal proof libraries such as Lean’s Mathlib [mathlib Com-
munity, 2020], Isabelle’s Archive of Formal Proofs [Paulson, 1994], and Coq-based libraries [Barras
et al., 1999, Rudnicki, 1992] encode large collections of verified theorems together with their formal
dependency structure [Li et al., 2024]. This dependency structure is not just a record of what has been
proved; it also helps determine what can be proved next. Retrieval-augmented and premise-selection
systems use this structure to identify relevant premises for proof search, as in ReProver [Yang et al.,
2023] and related work [Alemi et al., 2017, Wang et al., 2017, Mikuła et al., 2024]. Formal theorem
provers also use this library context to guide proof generation and search, including systems such as
Baldur [First et al., 2023] and DeepSeek-Prover [Xin et al., 2024], as well as more recent work [Wang
et al., 2025a, Dong and Ma, 2025]. Beyond proof search, formal libraries have also been used to
conjecture what should be proved in the first place: Lemmanaid [Alhessi et al., 2026] proposes
new lemmas by drawing analogies across existing theories, while related work studies conjecture
generation, verification, and blueprint construction from library context [Onda et al., 2025, Kasaura
et al., 2025, Liu et al., 2026, Zhu et al., 2026]. Together, these systems show that formal dependency
structure can inform not only how to prove but also what to prove. However, they operate entirely
within the formal world, requiring formal input and producing formal output, without modeling the
informal mathematical context found in research papers.
Scientific Informal-Formal Alignment.
In our setting, the alignment problem is to match an
informal theorem from a research paper to its corresponding theorem in Mathlib. A natural approach
is to translate informal mathematics written in natural language into formal proof-assistant syntax.
Early work used large language models to map informal theorems into formal representations Wu
et al. [2022], Azerbayev et al. [2023], Lu et al. [2024b]. Later work improved Autoformalization with

stronger training, retrieval-based methods, and math-specialized models, including Kimina [Wang
et al., 2025a], Draft, Sketch, and Prove [Jiang et al., 2023], and ProofBridge [Jana et al., 2026].
Despite these advances, coverage on real paper-level mathematics remains limited because of the
large vocabulary gap between informal exposition and formal syntax Zhang et al. [2025]. As an
alternative, FrenzyMath [Gao et al., 2024b] takes a different route by providing human-written
informal descriptions for over 136K Mathlib theorems, enabling informal-to-informal retrieval as
a pragmatic alignment strategy that avoids formalization altogether. Recent work also suggests
that linking informal theorem to formal libraries benefits from stronger retrieval and intermediate
structure [Lu et al., 2024b, Jiang et al., 2023, Jana et al., 2026]. Unlike these works, we do not treat
informal-formal alignment as the primary objective. Instead, we use the resulting aligned structure to
support grounded generation of future mathematical claims.
3
Method
Given an anchor paper, the goal is to generate a plausible future mathematical claim using its scientific
and matched formal context. Our approach has three components. First, we construct a dataset
that links scientific papers with formal theorem graphs 3.1. Second, we introduce COMPOSE, a
dual-encoder architecture that jointly models scientific and formal mathematical structure 3.2. Third,
we present our two-stage training 3.3.
Inference overview.
At inference time, given an anchor paper, the model constructs two graphs:
a scientific citation graph Gs, built from the paper and its contextually relevant cited papers, and a
formal theorem dependency graph Gf, built from Mathlib theorems aligned to extracted mathematical
theorems. Each graph is independently encoded by a dedicated GNN encoder. The two representations
are then fused via cross-attention. The fused graph embeddings are passed as conditioning to our
language model decoder, which generates a free-text theorem proposal.
3.1
Data Curation and Construction
Training our dual-graph model requires paired examples linking scientific context with formal
theorem-dependency structure. We construct this dataset by linking mathematical papers collected
via S2ORC [Lo et al., 2020] with formal theorems from Mathlib [mathlib Community, 2020].
On the scientific side, we begin with an anchor paper and construct outgoing citation edges for up
to two hops, retaining at most 5 cited papers at the first hop and at most 3 cited papers per node for
the second hop. Cited papers are selected by citation-context relevance, favoring citations that are
discussed in important parts of the paper or used directly in its proofs or main results, rather than
by including the full citation list; full selection details are in Appendix A.1. The graph contains
abstract nodes for all included papers, together with theorem nodes extracted from papers in the first
two citation hops. Each theorem node is connected either to its corresponding abstract node or to a
parent theorem node. We use typed edges to distinguish citation links, paper-to-theorem links, and
theorem-to-theorem links, allowing the graph to capture both external and internal citation context.
For each example, we also identify a target paper: a paper that cites the anchor and was published
after it, excluded from the input graph. Its main mathematical claim serves as the generation target.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental Setup
Data and Temporal Split. The 108K paired scientific-formal graph samples are split 80/10/10 into
training, validation, and test sets. To evaluate temporal generalization, we construct a future-paper
benchmark from a corpus of 47K mathematics papers published in late 2024-2025. We retain only
test-set anchor papers that are cited by at least one paper in the 47K future-paper pool, yielding 2K
test samples. For each anchor, future papers that cite it are treated as targets.
Retrieval Evaluation. Existing mathematical embedding models cluster future papers so tightly that
the similarity gap between true target papers and randomly sampled unrelated papers is too small to
reliably distinguish generation methods (see Appendix D.1). We therefore fine-tune a DeepSeek-Math
embedding model on the 47K benchmark papers using a margin-ranking objective that pulls together
papers with overlapping citation context and pushes apart unrelated papers. The resulting fixed model
is then used as a shared retrieval evaluator for all compared methods.
Metrics. We report Tgt-Sim, the mean cosine similarity between the generated text and its corre-
sponding ground-truth future papers; Neg-Sim, the mean cosine similarity between the generated
text and 500 sampled papers from unrelated subfields of the future pool; Gap = Tgt-Sim −Neg-Sim,
which measures how much closer the generated text is to the correct future papers than to unrelated
ones in the pool; and H@k, the fraction of examples for which a relevant future paper appears in the
top-k retrieved results. Exp-Sim measures similarity to the broader cluster of future papers that share
citation context with the target, capturing whether the generated text lands in the correct research
neighborhood rather than an unrelated area. We also report Novel Mathematical Claim Prediction
(NMCP) metrics for theorem-level grounding: Precision, the fraction of outputs aligned with the
formal theorem target derived from the future paper; Match, the fraction of outputs aligned with the
natural-language theorem target from the future paper; and Math, the ratio of mathematical tokens in
the output. To complement embedding-based metrics, we also evaluate generation quality using an
LLM judge (see Section D).
Internal Variants. We compare COMPOSE against several internal variants that remove or simplify
parts of the proposed system: (1) Paper-graph-only, which uses only the scientific graph Gs; (2)
Bag-of-Papers, which replaces the citation graph with a set of abstract and theorem nodes, removing
graph structure and bypasses the two graph encoders; (3) Text-only (LoRA), which fine-tunes the
decoder on our dataset using only a textual prompt, without any graph input; and (4) Prompt-only,
which uses a richer prompt but does not use graph input or fine-tuning.
We evaluate all variants with both DeepSeek-Math 7B and Mistral 7B decoders to demonstrate that
the gains from dual-graph conditioning are consistent across different backbone architectures.
Baselines. Since no prior work addresses exactly the same task, we compare against the closest
systems for future scientific generation and future-paper prediction. These include (1) GIANTS [He-
Yueya et al., 2026], which uses its own Qwen-based decoder; (2) DeepSeek-Math-7B-based systems,
including FutureGen [Azher et al., 2025], GoAI [Gao et al., 2025], and KG-CoI [Xiong et al., 2024],
which use the raw DeepSeek-Math-7B decoder; and (3) GPT-4.0-based variants of GoAI, CoI, and
ResearchAgent [Baek et al., 2025], which we include as API baselines. We also include a simple
retrieval baseline: Fixed NN, which retrieves by nearest-neighbor similarity over abstracts and main
theorems. For all external baselines, we use the authors’ released code and follow the prompts
described in their papers, adapting only the output request toward a mathematical claim.

Limitations and Future Work
Our method relies on approximate informal-formal alignment, which introduces noise into the formal
graph encoder. More importantly, generated claims are not verified by a proof assistant, so retrieval
and LLM-based evaluation remain proxy signals rather than direct tests of formal correctness. In
addition, the benchmark depends on automatically identifying a main claim from each future paper,
which may be noisy for papers with multiple results or less standardized theorem statements.
A natural next direction is to incorporate proof-aware verification so that generated claims can be
checked directly against a proof assistant. This framework could also support iterative conjecture
generation and proof attempts.

Conclusion
We introduced grounded future mathematical generation, a task that combines scientific citation
context with formal theorem structure to generate future mathematical claims. To support this setting,
we constructed a paired scientific-formal dataset and a temporally grounded benchmark over 47K
future papers, and proposed COMPOSE, a dual-graph framework for graph-conditioned generation.
Experiments show that COMPOSE produces outputs more similar to real future claims than the
baselines do, and LLM-judge evaluation supports this finding. Ablations further show that both
graphs are necessary: the scientific graph identifies relevant research directions, while the formal
graph keeps predictions grounded in valid mathematical dependencies.

A.1
Citation Subgraph Construction
Starting from the anchor paper, we follow outgoing citation edges hop by hop. We retain up to
5 cited papers at the first hop and up to 3 cited papers per node for the last hops. Candidate
neighbors are ranked by citation-context relevance rather than sampled uniformly. Nodes without
publication year metadata are discarded. Each paper node is represented by its abstract, encoded with
E5-large-v2 Wang et al. [2024a].
Since not all citations are equally informative, we rank candidate neighbors at each expansion
step relative to the current paper node. The ranking uses citation-context heuristics intended to
capture relevance to the surrounding scientific development, including appearances in the abstract,
introduction, main result claim, or proof-related passages; explicit dependency cues such as “using,”
“by,” or “follows from”; repeated technical mentions; and proximity to extracted theorem. At each
hop, we retain only the top-k candidates, yielding a compact citation subgraph that preserves the
most relevant local scientific context.

Citation Relevance Selection: Illustrative Example
To illustrate the difference between relevant and peripheral citations, we show two citations from the
same paper (ArXiv:1911.06307, Grifo-Ma-Schwede) and explain why one is selected as a neighbor
and the other is not.
High-relevance citation.
"arXiv:1702.06876, Grifo-Huneke" This paper is cited throughout the
introduction and proof sections with explicit dependency cues. For example: “In prime characteristic
p, Grifo and Huneke [GH19] showed that Harbourne’s conjecture holds for ideals defining F-pure
rings”, “the theorem above extends [GH19, Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 4.1]”, and “The containment
Q((h−1)(n−1)+1) ⊆Qn for all n ≥1 now follows by induction on n, as in [GH19, Theorem 4.1]”.
The paper’s main results are direct extensions of this work, and specific lemmas are invoked repeatedly
in proofs.
Low-relevance citation (not selected).
“Birational geometry of algebraic varieties” (Kollár-Mori).
This work does not appear anywhere in the body of the paper — it appears only in the citation
subgraph through indirect bibliographic links. It is not cited in the introduction, proofs, or any
technical passage, and has no connection to the symbolic power containment results developed in the
paper.
Citations that appear repeatedly in proof-related passages with explicit dependency language (“extends
[X]”, “as in [X, Lemma Y]”, “follows from [X]”) score significantly higher than works that appear
only through indirect bibliographic links with no direct textual dependency. This ensures the subgraph
captures the papers that directly shaped the mathematical content of the target paper.

A.2
Proof-Flow Graph Extraction
For papers within the first two citation hops, we extract a proof-flow graph from the full text.
We identify theorem nodes corresponding to five types: theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary,
and definition. Directed edges are added between theorem nodes when the text contains explicit
dependency references, detected with regular expressions matching patterns such as “by Lemma
2.1” or “using Theorem 3.4”. We filter out malformed statement spans, including nodes that begin
mid-sentence. Papers with fewer than three valid theorem nodes after filtering are discarded. The
resulting graph is a directed acyclic graph that approximates the proof-flow structure of the paper.

The matched theorem serves as the root of a local dependency subgraph extracted from Mathlib,
where nodes are formal Lean theorems and edges capture dependency relations between them. We
expand this subgraph up to 3 hops from the root, yielding between 8 and 15 theorems per sample.

We describe the retrieval implementation for each method in Table 7. Autoformalization and informal-

Autoformalization.
Each informal paper theorem is first translated into a Lean 4 formal stub using
either Kimina-7B [Wang et al., 2025a] or Gemini-2.5-Flash, prompted with five in-context examples.

• BM25 retrieval. We build a BM25 index over Mathlib theorem names concatenated with

• Namespace matching. We build a prefix index over all Mathlib theorem names. From the
generated stub, we extract identifiers and look up Mathlib theorems whose name shares the
longest matching prefix. Results are ranked by prefix specificity, top-5 returned.
• Dense retrieval. We embed the informal text as "query:
<text>" using e5-large-

A.5
Training Target Types
We use the same target-construction pipeline for both the training dataset and the future-paper retrieval
benchmark. For each paper, we first try to extract its main mathematical claim from the body text
using a set of heuristics, including regular-expression matching for phrases such as “we prove,” “we
show,” “Main Theorem,” and early numbered theorem statements. When no single main claim can
be identified reliably, we fall back to alternative targets such as the abstract or a supporting theorem
with clear local context. This yields a consistent target-construction process across both training and
evaluation, while handling different ways of stating the main claim.
Each training sample is paired with several target types derived from the same graph. These targets
expose the model to different forms of mathematical output from the same underlying context, helping
it learn richer graph representations instead of specializing to a single output style.
A Claim is the main mathematical result of the paper, extracted from sentences beginning with “we
prove,” “we show,” or identified as Theorem 1 or Main Theorem in the text. An Abstract target is
the full paper abstract. A Lemma in claim is a supporting lemma together with the neighboring
theorems it depends on, providing local proof flow context. Lean only targets consist of the formal

Lean signatures of the matched Mathlib theorems, and Informal + Lean combines the informal claim
with those signatures. Training on this mixture encourages the model to learn graph representations
that generalize across output types, which benefits Claim generation at evaluation time.

The LLM judge is based on five dimensions of mathematical quality: Mathematical Content, Technical
Depth, Novelty, Precision, and Specificity.
Mathematical Content measures whether the output contains substantive mathematical statements
rather than only prose. Technical Depth measures whether the claims are non-trivial and mathemati-
cally meaningful. Novelty measures whether the prediction introduces non-obvious ideas rather than
simple restatements. Precision measures whether assumptions, objects, and conclusions are stated
clearly and correctly. Specificity measures whether the output gives concrete mathematical details
rather than vague high-level descriptions. Together, these scores measure both the mathematical
content and the quality of the generated text. We report the average score for each dimension, along

Raw cosine similarity in mathematical embedding spaces is not a reliable retrieval signal in our
