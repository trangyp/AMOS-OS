Closing Trajectories: Equation-Free Cyclic Animation via Koopman
Surrogates
SHIXUN HUANG∗, University of Toronto, Canada
SIYUAN CHEN∗, University of British Columbia, Canada
YUE CHANG, University of Toronto, Canada

Cyclic animation is widely used in computer graphics and interactive content.
It supports seamless playback in games, VR, and interactive simulation,
where short clips must repeat smoothly over long durations. Achieving
physically plausible cyclic synthesis from an input sequence is challenging
because the endpoint states of the observed sequence rarely match exactly,
and the governing equations of the underlying system are often unavailable.
We therefore propose an equation-free framework that identifies a Koopman
surrogate from the observed trajectory and computes a cyclic trajectory by
applying a Fourier-parameterized, time-varying control force under a hard
temporal periodicity constraint. The resulting formulation reduces cyclic
synthesis to a linearly constrained quadratic program that can be solved
efficiently through a structured KKT system. Our method is applicable to
a diverse range of examples, including N-body systems, cloth, deformable
objects, shallow water, etc.
Additional Key Words and Phrases: cyclic animation, Koopman operator,
reduced-order modeling, physically plausible motion editing
1
Introduction
A cyclic animation is an animation sequence that repeats seamlessly
over time. It is valuable for interactive applications that must run
for long durations, because a short cyclic clip can be repeated in-
definitely without manual reauthoring. Cyclic sequences also serve
as reusable building blocks in animation systems, enabling consis-
tent behaviors across scenes and facilitating compositional editing.
As a result, the ability to synthesize high-quality cyclic animation
from general input sequences is practically important in computer
graphics and simulation workflows.
Formally, a cyclic animation is a trajectory in state space that
returns to its initial state after one period. However, synthesizing
such an animation from a general input sequence is challenging. A
visually closed loop is not necessarily physically meaningful: simply
forcing the last frame to match the first may introduce distortions
that break the natural temporal evolution of the physical system.
In other words, cyclicity is not only a geometric condition on the
trajectory, but also that the trajectory remain as close as possible to
a physically plausible natural temporal evolution.
Existing methods for synthesizing cyclic animation generally fall
into two categories. Interpolation-based methods such as blend-
ing [Kovar et al. 2002; Park et al. 2002] and frame stitching [Heck

but they typically rely heavily on access to governing equations,

optimization. This dependence limits their applicability in settings
where only a trajectory is given, such as precomputed simulation
outputs, legacy animation data, or captured motion sequences for
which the original simulator or physical model is unavailable.
In this work, we propose an equation-free framework for syn-
thesizing cyclic animation from an observed trajectory. We first
identify a Koopman operator from the observed trajectory and use
it as a surrogate for the unknown dynamics. We then solve for the
minimal control force that steers the trajectory toward a cyclic one.
This formulation seeks a cyclic trajectory that remains close to the
observed sequence while using only the minimal control needed
to enforce periodicity, which helps preserve a physically plausible
temporal evolution
Our approach is fully data-driven and does not require an ex-
plicit physical model. The resulting problem becomes a linearly
constrained quadratic program with a structured KKT system, mak-
ing the method computationally efficient. By editing the trajectory
through a learned surrogate dynamics rather than direct interpo-
lation, the method aims to preserve the temporal structure of the
observed sequence while enforcing exact cyclic closure.
In summary, this work makes the following contributions:
• We introduce a purely data-driven, equation-free framework
for synthesizing cyclic animation. Given an observed physi-
cal trajectory, our method fits a Koopman surrogate to the
input trajectory and computes a minimal control that steers
it toward exact cyclic closure, without knowledge of the
underlying dynamics equation.
• We show that the resulting formulation reduces to a linearly
constrained quadratic program that can be solved efficiently
through a structured Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) system.

Our method adopts Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD) [Schmid
2010] to approximate the Koopman operator. Interest in DMD has
grown within the graphics community in recent years. It has been
shown that DMD can be used for fast fluid simulation [Chen et al.
2025] and deformable simulation [Chang et al. 2026]. In both cases,
the authors leveraged the linearity of the operator to enable edit-
ing and inverse problems. However, none of the aforementioned
methods discuss how to generate cyclic animations using the ap-
proximated Koopman operator. We show, for the first time, that
the DMD framework can be applied to generate cyclic animations.
Relatedly, Dynamic Mode Decomposition with control extends this
framework to systems with prescribed input signals [Proctor et al.
2016]. Our method is closely related in spirit, but differs in that we
do not assume access to observed controls. We learn a DMD-based
surrogate directly from observed trajectories, without relying on ex-
plicit governing equations or prescribed controls. We then compute
a minimal corrective control force that steers the system toward a
closed, cyclic orbit.
Reduced-order modeling. Reduced-order models are widely
used to represent high-dimensional simulations in a compact space [Bar-
bič and James 2005; de Aguiar et al. 2010b; Kim and James 2009], and
have recently been extended through neural approaches [Chang
et al. 2026, 2025, 2023; Chen et al. 2023; Modi et al. 2024]. By project-
ing the full space configuration onto a small number of dominant
modes, these methods [Barbič et al. 2009, 2012; Chen et al. 2024]
make simulation and editing significantly more efficient while pre-
serving the main behaviors of the system. Prior work [Benchekroun
et al. 2025; Harmon and Zorin 2013; Lan et al. 2024; Neumann et al.
2013] has further extended this idea with local enrichment and non-
linear reduced representations to better capture complex deforma-
tion patterns. Our work is related in spirit because we also operate
in a low-dimensional subspace learned from the observed trajec-
tory. The key difference is that traditional reduced-order methods
typically rely on an explicit physical model or simulator, whereas
our setting is equation-free. We do not reduce a known simulation
model; instead, we learn a reduced surrogate directly from data and
use it to impose cyclicity while staying close to the original physical
evolution. Our interactive extension further shows that our method
can be combined with localized control bases for user-guided local
editing.
Data-only methods. Prior work has explored motion generation
from a single image [Tedla et al. 2025] or video [Kansy et al. 2025],
with some methods enabling personalization through generative
priors [Abdal et al. 2025]. Several approaches also operate directly
on 3D motion data, such as motion manifolds [Starke et al. 2022]
and motion generation from limited data [Li et al. 2022]. These
methods, however, do not impose structure motivated by under-
lying dynamics. In contrast, we introduce a structured temporal
prior by approximating the system evolution with a linear operator.
This assumption reflects the expectation that the data arises from a
coherent dynamical process, and may be complementary to existing
approaches.

4.1

acterized by closure under the underlying flow map. In practice,
however, we work with the learned discrete Koopman surrogate
rather than the unknown governing dynamics. If we simply roll

reproduce the original sampled evolution and does not, in general,
satisfy a cyclic closure condition. To synthesize a cyclic trajectory,

6
Results
We evaluate our method on several representative physical sys-
tems: N-body dynamics (Sec. 6.1), cloth simulation (pinned cloth,
Sec. 6.2), deformable solids (cute dragon and parrot, Sec. 6.3), and
shallow water (wave propagation, Sec. 6.4). N-body dynamics data
are generated by numerically integrating Newton’s equations using
the velocity-Verlet scheme, implemented in NumPy [Harris et al.
2020]; cloth simulation data are generated by Stiff-GIPC [Huang
et al. 2025]; deformable solids data are generated by stable neo-
hookean flesh simulator [Smith et al. 2018], and shallow water data
are generated by a 2D finite-difference solver based on the shallow-
water equations of de Saint-Venant (1871), implemented in NumPy
[Harris et al. 2020]. For all examples, experiments were conducted
on a desktop machine equipped with a 12th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM)
i7-12700KF CPU. Owing to the equation-free nature of our method,
the same pipeline applies across all examples with different underly-
ing dynamics. Across all examples, our method converts non-cyclic
input trajectories into seamless loops. Because the cyclicity is im-
posed as a hard constraint, the synthesized trajectories satisfy the
closure condition up to machine precision (on the order of 10−10).
In addition, we demonstrate an interactive local-control extension
in Sec 6.5.
For each system, we construct the state vector by stacking the
quantities that describe its dynamics: positions and velocities for N-
body, cloth, and deformable solids, and water height together with
horizontal velocities for shallow water. For a trajectory of length
𝑇+ 1, we fit the surrogate dynamics on the first 𝑇frames and re-
serve the final frame for evaluating cyclic closure of the synthesized
trajectory. Table 1 summarizes the state dimensions, the numbers
of input and fitting frames, the subspace dimensions, the control
basis dimensions, and the computation time for all examples.

Deformable Solids
6.3.1
Cute Dragon. We evaluate our method on a deformable se-

Instead of directly matching the last frame to the first, the added

first, producing a seamless loop that remains faithful to the original
deformable behavior.
6.3.2
Parrot. We consider a deformable parrot undergoing wing

the sequence is steered back toward the starting configuration near
the end, producing a cyclic result of the same duration.
Because the control force is introduced through the learned surro-
gate dynamics and distributed across time, the resulting loop retains
a natural repeated wing-flapping behavior and transitions smoothly
from the final frame back to the first.

7
Conclusion
We present a pipeline that converts non-cyclic animations into
cyclic ones while preserving the underlying dynamics. Our approach
learns a Koopman surrogate from the input sequence and enforces
periodicity through a minimal control force under a hard temporal
constraint. The linear structure of the surrogate leads to an efficient
linear solve for cyclic reconstruction, while remaining agnostic to
the governing equations of the input data. We demonstrate the gen-
erality of our method across a range of simulations, including cloth,
deformable solids, and fluids, consistently producing plausible cyclic
motions that respect both periodicity and characteristic dynamics.
Looking forward, an important direction is extending our frame-
work to more complex settings involving coupled systems, such as
fluid–solid interactions, where multiple physical processes interact
across different time scales. Another promising avenue is to expand
our editing capabilities to support richer and more localized con-
trol, enabling users to specify more complex temporal and spatial
constraints while maintaining dynamic consistency.
