Abstract. Motivated by Ziegler’s computability-theoretic characterisa-
tion of finite absolute presentability between groups, we prove an anal-
ogous theorem in symbolic dynamics. We introduce the notion of one
subshift being finitely determined over another and show that this rela-
tion is characterised by Ziegler reducibility between their co-languages.
We further investigate a notion of existential closure for subshifts.
Keywords: Subshifts · Ziegler reducibility · existential closure.
1
Introduction
The study of multidimensional subshifts—colourings of Zd that avoid a set of
forbidden finite patterns—has greatly benefited from the tools of computability
theory. A signature example is the simulation theorem by Hochman [4] and later
refinements by Aubrun–Sablik [1] and Durand–Romashchenko–Shen [3], which
state that every effectively closed shift is simulated by a subshift of finite type
(SFT) of higher dimension.
In this vein, we characterise in computability-theoretic terms when a subshift
is “completely determined” by another subshift using a finite amount of extra
information. Namely, we prove that this relationship coincides with Ziegler re-
ducibility, denoted ≤∗, (see Definition 8) between their co-languages.
The definition of ≤∗dates back to Ziegler’s analysis of the finitely generated
subgroups of existentially closed groups [10]. This fits into a growing “dictionary”
between notions and results in combinatorial group theory and in symbolic dy-
namics, as developed in [6,7]. We also raise an issue with extending the dictionary
further, to capture existentially closed groups. We show that the most natural
definition and construction of “existentially closed subshifts” (following the the-
ory for groups) does not satisfy Ziegler’s Theorem [10, Folgerungen III.1.8].

A characterisation of computable subshifts
The key definition is that of “finitely determined over” (Definition 7 below).
The definition should capture the intuition that from T, together with a finite
amount of extra information, one can determine S. So, what does it mean to
have access to T? How will S be presented? And what kind of finite information
are we allowed?
We identify T and S with their co-languages (and also implicitly with their
alphabets and dimensions). Thus, having access to T should correspond to “cod-
ing” the co-language of T (assuming that we know the alphabet and dimension of
T). This is captured by Jeandel–Vanier’s notion of full restriction (Definition 5).
Similarly, “determining S” will correspond to the resulting subshift having S as a
full restriction. Thus, “S is finitely determined over T” should have the following
flavour:
If T ⊑R and R satisfies (*), then S ⊑R,
where (*) is some finite set of conditions, not dependent on T or S.

Here the analogy with groups provides some insight. The relevant condition
in the group case is that of a group G being ∃1-isolated over another group H
([10, Bem. III.1.6.2]).
Definition 6. A group G is ∃1-isolated1 over H if there is an existential for-
mula φ(x, h) such that any F |= φ(a, h) with ⟨h⟩∼= H has ⟨a⟩∼= G.
Thus we should require that (*), in the framework above, enforces finitary
constraints on the language and co-language of R.
The next condition on R arises from the observation that the notion of “sub-
structure” in group theory (and indeed more generally in algebra or model the-
ory) bifurcates for subshifts: the first notion (that T is a substructure of R if the
atomic diagram of T is a subset of the atomic diagram of R) is captured by ⊑, and
this is what we have used so far. However, there is a second notion; namely that
there is a “structure-preserving” injection f : T →R. In subshift theory, the ana-
logue to this notion is that of a factor map; thus T is a “substructure” of R if there
is a surjective, shift-commuting map f : R →T (see Definition 4). Indeed, this
is borne out by the Simulation Theorems—shift-theoretic analogues of Higman’s
Embedding Theorem in group theory—proved by Durand–Romashchenko–Shen
(Theorem 1 of [3]) and Aubrun–Sablik (Theorem 3.1 of [1]). We shall need both
notions of substructure.
Finally, for this to all be well-defined, we need to fix the dimension of R.
Definition 7. Let S and T be subshifts. S is finitely determined over T if there
are:
– A finite alphabet Σ
– A dimension d
– A pair of finite sets of words (G, B) in Σ
– A block code ˆf : ΣD →∆, for some ∆⊇ΣS ∪ΣT
such that
1. There is some subshift R in dimension d and alphabet Σ such that G ⊆L(R),
B ⊆Lc(R), and T ⊑f(R).
2. Any such subshift R further satisfies that S ⊑f(R).
In this case, say that S is finitely determined over T by (Σ, d, G, B, ˆf).
If S is finitely determined over the empty subshift, we say that S is finitely
determined.

The proof relies heavily on the construction of a subshift which acts as an
oracle for the language of a given subshift. This oracle shift was introduced
in the proof of Theorem 23 in [7], their analogue of the relativised Higman
Embedding Theorem. The other key ingredient is the Simulation Theorem in
[3] and [1], which shows that every effectively closed shift of dimension d is the
d-dimensional slice of a factor of a d + 1-dimensional SFT.
The oracle shift Since it was sketched in [7], we provide details of the con-
struction here. It may be useful to also reference the proof of Theorem 23 in
[7]. The idea of the oracle shift is that, given a d-dimensional shift S in a finite
alphabet, it effectively produces a d + 1-dimensional shift OS that displays the
language of S in a uniform way. The forbidden words of OS can be chosen as
F ∪Lc(S), where the set F is c.e. Further, L(S) ≤1 OS—and in fact, a stronger
relation holds: S ⊑OS.
Let S be a d-dimensional subshift in the alphabet Σ and let ΣOS = Σ ∪{#}.
The subshift OS can be thought of as a “stack” of d-dimensional shifts, which
we, borrowing the terminology of the d = 1 case, call “rows”. Each d-dimensional
row in this stack will be associated with a number i ∈ω ∪{∞} called the type
of the row. Rows of type i consist of words w ∈L(S) which are on hypercubes
of length 2i −1, surrounded by a single layer of #s on each side. These rows will
be periodic, with period 2i · |Σ|2i in each of their d dimensions.
Further, the rows interact. Each of the rows of type i are identical, and appear
every 2i rows. Thus every other row is a row of type 1, and every other of the
remaining rows is a row of type 2, etc. Moreover, each connected word in Σ on
a row of type i appears as a subword of some word in the rows of type i + 1.
The construction of OS is then essentially the following: first O⟨Σ|⟩is con-
structed, which has c.e. set of forbidden words. OS is then obtained by further
forbidding Lc(S).
Say that a word d-dimensional word w on alphabet ΣOS is a valid pattern
of type i if its domain is a hypercube of length 2i + 1 where the hypercube of
length 2i −1 in the interior only has letters from Σ and it is wrapped by a layer
of #, which we call the boundary of the valid pattern. Each row of type i will
consist of only valid pattern of type i with shared boundaries. Let F be the set
of forbidden d + 1-dimensional words described below.
Building a grid: For each i, forbid the words on hypercubes of length 2i + 1
such that none of its rows is consistent with a valid pattern of type i.
Fixing the type: For each i, forbid the words that contain a valid pattern of
type i and something else inconsistent with a valid pattern of type i in the
same row.

Existential closure
Ziegler originally introduced ≤∗in the context of existentially closed groups. Say
that a group M is existentially closed (ec) if for any quantifier-free φ(x, m),
where m ⊆M, if there is N ≥M with N |= ∃xφ, then M |= ∃xφ.
Then Ziegler showed in [10] that for finitely generated groups G and H,
W(G) ≤∗W(H) iff G embeds into every ec group that H embeds into iff G
is ∃1-isolated over H (Definition 6; see also [8, Chapter 4] for more detail on
the background). Thus there is an equivalence between (1) relative effective
content, (2) relative embeddability in an algebraic structure, and (3) relative
∃1-definability for groups.
Notably, Belegradek [2] gave conditions for Ziegler’s theorem to hold in a
general algebraic context. In Theorem 2, we translate the equivalence between
(1) and (3) into the theory of subshifts. In this section, we introduce a notion of
existential closure for subshifts, namely being existentially closed for consistent
systems, in parallel to the definition for groups (Definition 10).
Subshifts which are existentially closed for consistent systems have some com-
monality with existentially closed groups (for example, both classes are amenable
to model-theoretic forcing arguments), but the class nonetheless fails to satisfy
