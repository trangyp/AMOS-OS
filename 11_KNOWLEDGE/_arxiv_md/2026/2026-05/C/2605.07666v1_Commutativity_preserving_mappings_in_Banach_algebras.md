COMMUTATIVITY PRESERVING MAPPINGS IN BANACH
ALGEBRAS
M. BREŠAR, G. M. ESCOLANO, A. M. PERALTA, AND A. R. VILLENA
Abstract. Let A and B be unital complex Banach algebras having
no quotients isomorphic to C or M2(C). Assume additionally that B
is semisimple.
If a surjective additive mapping Φ: A →B satisfies
[Φ(x2), Φ(x)] = 0 for all x ∈A, then there exist a surjective direct
sum of an additive homomorphism and an additive anti-homomorphism
Ψ: A →B, an invertible element λ ∈Z(B), and an additive mapping
ζ : A →Z(B) such that Φ(x) = λΨ(x) + ζ(x) for all x ∈A.
1. Introduction
Let A and B be algebras. We say that a mapping Φ : A →B preserves
commutativity if for all x, y ∈A, [x, y] = 0 implies [Φ(x), Φ(y)] = 0; here,
[x, y] stands for xy −yx. There is a vast literature on these mappings. For
a historical account, we refer the reader to [8, pp. 218-219] and to the recent
papers [15, 16] for a more updated information.
One usually assumes that a commutativity preserver Φ is linear or at
least additive, and also that it is bijective or at least surjective. Under these
conditions, one wishes to show that Φ is of the form
(1.1)
Φ(x) = λΨ(x) + ζ(x)
∀x ∈A,
where λ is an invertible element from the center Z(B) of B, ζ : A →Z(B) is
an additive map, and ψ : A →B is a Jordan homomorphism. Since we will
deal with additive (rather than linear) mappings, by a Jordan homomorphism
we will mean an additive (not necessarily linear) mapping Ψ : A →B that
satisfies Ψ(x◦y) = Ψ(x)◦Ψ(y) for all x, y ∈A; here, x◦y stands for xy+yx.
Note that 2×2 matrices over a field commute if and only if they are linearly
dependent modulo the scalar matrices.
It is therefore easy to construct
commutativity preserving linear mappings on 2 × 2 matrix algebras that are
not of the standard form in (1.1). At the end of the paper, we will provide
a different kind of nonstandard example on a certain C∗-algebra A that
contains a maximal ideal M such that A/M ∼= C (Example 4.2). Therefore,
when studying commutativity preservers between complex algebras, it is
natural to assume that these algebras have no quotients isomorphic to C or
M2(C).
The goal of this paper is to prove that a surjective commutativity pre-
serving additive mapping Φ : A →B is of the standard form in (1.1) if A

3. Examples
This section presents a wide range of natural examples to which our main
conclusion applies.
The culminating point is the case of surjective addi-
tive mappings preserving commutativity only in one direction between von
Neumann algebras with no central summands of type I1 and I2.
Example 3.1 (Simple unital Banach algebras). An obvious example of
semisimple unital complex Banach algebra which has no quotients isomor-
phic to C or M2(C) is a simple unital Banach algebra A with dim(A) > 4.
It should be noted that, for a unital complex Banach algebra A, it does not
matter if we think of simplicity in an algebraic sense, i.e. 0 and A are the
only ideals of A, or in a topological sense, i.e. 0 and A are the only closed
ideals of A. Classical examples are the matrix algebra Mn(C) for each n ∈N

Example 3.2 (Properly infinite unital Banach algebras). Let A be a com-
plex algebra. Two idempotents p, q ∈A are algebraically equivalent, written
p ≈q, if there exist a, b ∈A such that p = ab and q = ba. A unital complex
algebra A is properly infinite if there exist idempotents p, q ∈A such that
pq = 0 = qp and satisfy p ≈1 ≈q (cf. [13, Definitions 1.3.18 and 1.3.21]).
The Banach algebra B(X) of all bounded linear operators on a complex Ba-
nach space X is properly infinite if and only if X contains a complemented
subspace isomorphic to X ⊕X (see [19, Proposition 1.9]). The classical Ba-
nach spaces C([0, 1]), c0, ℓp and Lp([0, 1]), where 1 ≤p ≤∞, are isomorphic
to the direct sum of two copies of themselves. Hence, they are examples of
Banach spaces X for which B(X) is properly infinite. For a C∗-algebra A,
rather than considering idempotents, one considers projections. Further, the
natural equivalence of projections is that of Murray-von Neumann equiva-
lence, which says that two projections p, q ∈A are equivalent, written p ∼q,
if there exists u ∈A such that p = u∗u and q = uu∗(hence, u is a partial
isometry). Each idempotent is algebraically equivalent to a projection and,
moreover, given projections p, q ∈A we have p ∼q if and only if p ≈q
(cf. [13, Propositions 1.10.21(i) and 3.2.10]). By [14, Proposition 2.4], a
unital C∗-algebra A is properly infinite in the algebraic sense if and only if
it is properly infinite in the C∗sense, i.e. there exist mutually orthogonal
projections p, q ∈A such that p ∼1 ∼q.
Let A be a properly infinite unital complex Banach algebra. We claim
that A has no quotients isomorphic to Md(C) for any positive integer d. To
derive a contradiction, suppose that there exists a surjective homomorphism
π: A →Md(C) for some positive integer d. Let p, q ∈A idempotents such
that pq = 0 = qp and p ≈1 ≈q. Then π(p) and π(q) are idempotents of
Md(C), π(p)π(q) = 0 = π(q)π(p), and, π(p) ≈1 ≈π(q) (because π(1) = 1),
which entails that Md(C) is a properly infinite C∗-algebra, a contradiction.

phic to C or M2(C), then A/I has no quotients isomorphic to to C
or M2(C).
(ii) Tensor products. Let A and B be unital complex Banach algebras.
Then both A and B embed unitally into the projective tensor product
Ab⊗B through the mappings a 7→a⊗1B and b 7→1A⊗b, respectively.
Therefore, if any of the algebras has no quotients isomorphic to C
or M2(C), then Ab⊗B has no quotients isomorphic to C or M2(C).
However, it must be noted that the semisimplicity does not always
carry over from A and B to Ab⊗B.
(iii) Matrix algebras. An immediate consequence of the preceding exam-
ple is that the matrix algebra Mn(A) has no quotients isomorphic
to C or M2(C) for each unital complex Banach algebra A and each
n ≥3. Further, Mn(A) is semisimple whenever A is semisimple.
(iv) Banach algebras of vector-valued functions. Let A be a unital com-
plex Banach algebra and let S be a non-empty set. Then AS is a
unital complex algebra with respect to the pointwise operations. Let
B be a unital subalgebra of AS, and suppose that B contains all the
constant functions. Assume additionally that B is equipped with a
norm that makes it a Banach algebra. Then A embeds unitally into
B through the mapping a 7→(s 7→a). Consequently, if A has no
quotients isomorphic to C or M2(C), then the same is true of B.
Further, for each s ∈S, the evaluation mapping εs : B →A defined
by εs(f) = f(s) (f ∈B) is a surjective homomorphism and hence
εs(Rad(B)) ⊆Rad(A). Therefore, B is semisimple whenever A is
semisimple. This makes it possible for us to show, for example, that
any of the following unital Banach algebras of A-valued vector func-
tions have no quotients isomorphic to C or M2(C) provided that A
does not have them either:
• The Banach algebra ℓ∞(S, A) of all A-valued bounded functions
on the set S;
• The Banach algebra C(K, A) of A-valued continuous functions
on a compact Hausdorff space K;
• The Banach algebra Cn(I, A) of all n-times continuously differ-
entiable A-valued functions on a compact interval I, for each
n ∈N;
• The A-valued Lipschitz algebras Lipα(K, A) and lipα(K, A) for
each non-empty compact metric space K and each 0 < α ≤1.
Further, each of these algebras is semisimple given that A is so.

4. Lie homomorphisms
Recall that an additive mapping Φ from a ring A to a ring B is called a
Lie homomorphism if
(4.1)
Φ([x, y]) = [Φ(x), Φ(y)]
∀x, y ∈A.
Clearly, Φ preserves commutativity.
Obvious examples of Lie homomorphisms are homomorphisms and the
negatives of anti-homomorphisms.
A more general example is the direct
sum of a homomorphism and the negative of an anti-homomorphism, i.e.,
a mapping Θ : A →B for which there exists a central idempotent e ∈B
such that eΘ is a homomorphism and (1 −e)Θ is the negative of an anti-
homomorphism (i.e., (1 −e)Θ(xy) = −(1 −e)Θ(y)Θ(x) for all x, y ∈A).
Another standard example is any additive mapping ζ from A to Z(B)
that vanishes on commutators (i.e., ζ([x, y]) = 0 for all x, y ∈A). Observe
that the sum of an arbitrary Lie homomorphism and such a mapping ζ is
again a Lie homomorphism.
It is a classical problem to find conditions under which the above examples
are also the only examples of Lie homomorphisms. Obviously, we can tackle
it by using Theorem 2.7.

Example 4.2. Let H be an infinite-dimensional, separable Hilbert space,
let K(H) be the C∗-algebra of all compact operators on H, and let A be
the C∗-algebra obtained by adjoining a unity to the direct product M =
K(H) × K(H). Note that M is a maximal ideal of A and A/M ∼= C.
Define Φ : A →A by
Φ(λ1 + (a, b)) = λ1 + (a, −b∗)
∀λ ∈C, ∀a, b ∈K(H).
One easily checks that Φ is a Lie automorphism of A.
We will show not only that Φ does not satisfy the conclusion of Corollary
4.1, but that it is not standard in the sense of [3]. Suppose this is not true,
i.e., suppose Φ is standard. Then there exist a homomorphism ψ1 : A →A
and an anti-homomorphism ψ2 : A →A such that
ψ1(A) · ψ2(A) = ψ2(A) · ψ1(A) = {0},
and Φ coincides with ψ1 −ψ2 on [A, A].

ker ψ1 contains a nonzero element in F(H) × {0}. Since the algebra F(H)
is simple, we then have ψ1(F(H) × {0}) = 0. In particular, ψ1 vanishes on

ψ2((a, 0)) = −Φ((a, 0)) = −(a, 0),
for every a ∈[F(H), F(H)]. However, taking any a, b ∈[F(H), F(H)] such
that ab ̸= 0 and ba = 0, we arrive at a contradiction since
(ab, 0) = ψ2((a, 0)) · ψ2((b, 0)) = ψ2((b, 0)(a, 0)) = 0.
Therefore, ψ1 is injective on F(H) × {0}.
