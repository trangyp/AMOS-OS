CENTRALIZERS IN FINITE GROUPS AND DOMINATION NUMBER
OF THEIR COMMUTING GRAPHS
SUDIP BERA, HIRANYA KISHORE DEY, AND UMANG JETHVA
Abstract. The proper commuting graph C∗∗(G) of a finite group G is the simple graph
whose vertices are the noncentral elements of G and two distinct vertices are adjacent if they
commute. In this paper, we study the domination number and total domination number of
proper commuting graphs of finite groups. We first obtain general bounds for the domina-
tion number of proper commuting graphs. For finite nilpotent groups, we exploit a strong
product decomposition of commuting graphs to derive exact formulas for the domination
number. We further determine the exact domination number and total domination number
for proper commuting graphs of several well-known families of finite groups, connecting with
the centralizers of those groups.
1. Introduction
The study of graphs associated with various algebraic structures has been a topic of active
investigation during the last two decades. Such studies provide important insights into the
interplay between algebraic structures and graph theoretic properties. Several graphs have
been introduced to explore algebraic structures using graph theory, including commuting
graphs of groups, power graphs of groups and semigroups [2, 14, 21], the centralizer graph
[22], enhanced power graphs of groups [1, 10, 11, 12], among others.
In this paper, we focus on the proper commuting graph of a group. Let G be a finite group,
then the commuting graph of G, denoted by C(G), is the simple graph whose vertex set is
G, where two distinct vertices x and y are adjacent if and only if xy = yx. For a group G,
we denote by e the identity element of G and by G∗∗the set G \ Z(G), where Z(G) is the
center of the group G. The induced subgraph of C(G) with the vertex set G∗∗is called the
proper commuting graph of G and we shall denote it by C∗∗(G).
Commuting graphs and their variants have been widely investigated due to their strong
connections with group structure [6, 13, 16, 17]. In particular, graph-theoretic parameters
such as connectivity, diameter, and domination number have received considerable attention
[7, 15, 20, 23, 25].
Haji and Amiri [17] studied the domination number of proper commuting graphs and
established several notable results. Building on their work, we further examine domination
in proper commuting graphs, with an emphasis on how the algebraic structure of a group
governs these properties.

1.1. Basic Definitions and Notations. Let Γ be a finite simple graph with vertex set V
and edge set E. For a vertex v ∈V , the neighbourhood of v is the set N(v) consisting of
all vertices of Γ that are adjacent to v. The closed neighbourhood of v is the set N[v] =
N(v) ∪{v}. A vertex v ∈V is called a dominating vertex of Γ if its closed neighbourhood
is V , that is, N[v] = V . A subset X ⊆V is called a dominating set of Γ if for every vertex
v ∈V \ X, there exists a vertex u ∈X such that v is adjacent to u. Clearly, V itself is a
dominating set of Γ. If Γ has no edges, then V is the only dominating set of Γ. A dominating
set X of Γ is called a minimum dominating set if |X| ≤|Y | for every dominating set Y of
Γ. The cardinality of a minimum dominating set of Γ is called the domination number of
Γ, and it is denoted by γ(Γ). For a graph Γ, let Dom(Γ) denote the set of all universal
(dominating) vertices of Γ. Observe that Dom(Γ) ̸= ∅if and only if γ(Γ) = 1.
A set T ⊆V is called a total dominating set if for every v ∈V there exists u ∈T such
that uv ∈E. The minimum cardinality of such a set is called the total domination number
of Γ and is denoted by γt(Γ). In this paper, our goal is to contribute to the study of the
domination number and the total domination number of the proper commuting graph of
group.
Let Γ1 = (V (Γ1), E(Γ1)) and Γ2 = (V (Γ2), E(Γ2)) be simple graphs. The strong product
of Γ1 and Γ2, denoted by Γ1 ⊗Γ2, is the graph with the vertex set
V (Γ1 ⊗Γ2) = V (Γ1) × V (Γ2),
where two distinct vertices (g1, h1) and (g2, h2) are adjacent in Γ1 ⊗Γ2 if and only if at least
one of the following holds:
(1) g1 = g2 and h1 ∼h2 in Γ2;
(2) h1 = h2 and g1 ∼g2 in Γ1;
(3) g1 ∼g2 in Γ1 and h1 ∼h2 in Γ2.
Recall that the centralizer of x in G, denoted by C(x), is the subgroup of G consisting
of all elements that commute with x; i.e., C(x) = {y ∈G : xy = yx}. By cent(G), we
denote the set of all distinct centralizers of single elements of G. Researchers have studied
the classification of finite groups by counting their centralizers; see, for example, [3, 5, 8].
For a finite group G, a covering of G is a set Γ = {H1, . . . , Hn} of proper subgroups Hi of G
such that G = ∪n

are centralizers of some elements in G. Following the notation of [17], let σc(G) denote the
minimum possible cardinality of a centralizer covering of G. Clearly, σc(G) = γ(C∗∗(G)).

In this section, we first present the proof of Theorem 1.5.
Proposition 5.1. Let G be a finite non-abelian group. Then C∗∗(G) possesses a total dom-

Therefore, each C(x) contains at least two elements from G \ Z(G).
Now consider a set T obtained by taking two elements, namely x and any element y ∈C(x),

possess any total dominating set. This completes the proof.

In this section, we compute the domination number and total domination number of the
proper commuting graph of some well-known groups. For a group G, let cent(G) denote the
set of all centralizers of single elements of G, that is, cent(G) = {C(g) | g ∈G}. We start

In [17, Proposition 2.2], Haji and Amiri showed the following.
Proposition 6.1. Let G be any finite non-abelian group. Then, G is an AC-group if and
only if γ(C∗∗(G)) = |cent(G)| −1.
Proposition 6.2. Let G be a finite non-abelian group which is not generalized dihedral.
Then, γt(C∗∗(G)) = 2|cent(G)| −2 if and only if G is an AC-group.
Proof. Let G be an AC-group. Then, for a, b ∈G \ Z(G), we have ab = ba if and only if
C(a) = C(b). Let x, y ∈G\Z(G). If C(x) ̸= C(y), then we claim that C(x)∩C(y) = Z(G).

only if C(x)\Z(G) and C(y)\Z(G) are distinct components of the proper commuting graph
of G. Therefore, γt(C∗∗(G)) = 2γ(C∗∗(G)), completing the proof.
Conversely, if γt(C∗∗(G)) = 2|cent(G)| −2, we must have γ(C∗∗(G)) = |cent(G)| −1 and
thus by Proposition 6.1, G has to be an AC-group.
