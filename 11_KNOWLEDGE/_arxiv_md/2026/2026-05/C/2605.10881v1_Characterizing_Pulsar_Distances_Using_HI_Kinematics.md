Characterizing Pulsar Distances Using H I Kinematics

ABSTRACT
Distance measurements are fundamental to radio pulsars’ use as astrophysical probes of General Relativity
and the interstellar medium. One of the primary methods for determining pulsar distances is H I kinematics,
which leverages the radial velocities of H I absorption and emission features detected along pulsar lines-of-sight.
This method necessarily assumes a model for Galactic rotation, our knowledge of which continues to evolve in
both accuracy and precision. In this research note, we derive kinematic distances for 66 pulsars with archival
H I radial velocity measurements using a state-of-the-art Galactic rotation curve. The results and software are
provided in an online repository. Our kinematic distances differ by < 1σ from published parallaxes for nearly
all pulsars in the sample that have both types of distance measurement available. Comparison to the NE2025
Galactic electron density model shows general consistency between measured and predicted distances.
Keywords: Neutron stars – Astrometry – Galactic rotation – Interstellar medium
1. INTRODUCTION
Distance measurements are required for a wide range of
radio pulsar applications, including tests of General Relativ-
ity (Kramer et al. 2006), constraining neutron star formation
and velocities (Hansen & Phinney 1997; Verbunt et al. 2017),
and studies of the ionized interstellar medium (ISM; Cordes
& Lazio 2002; Yao et al. 2017; Ocker & Cordes 2026). How-
ever, less than 10% of the ∼4500 known pulsars have em-
pirical distance measurements (rather than distance estimates
based on a Galactic electron density model). Nearly a third
of published pulsar distances are derived using H I kinemat-
ics, which combines a model for Galactic rotation with radial
velocities of H I absorption and emission features observed
along pulsar lines-of-sight (LOSs; Frail & Weisberg 1990;
Verbiest et al. 2012). This method tends to deliver the least
precision when compared to, e.g., parallaxes determined with
Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) or distances de-
termined by association with globular clusters (Deller et al.
2019; Pan et al. 2021). Nonetheless, the sample of pulsar
distances is limited enough that robust determination of H I
kinematic distances remains important.
Previous catalogs of H I kinematic distances include those
published by Frail & Weisberg (1990) and Verbiest et al.

measured towards very bright pulsars observed with the
Parkes and Arecibo telescopes (e.g. Ables & Manchester
1976; Weisberg et al. 1987). As laid out in these studies, the
kinematic method relies on the detection of H I features that
place bounds on the pulsar distance: When H I absorption is
detected in the pulsar radio spectrum, it is attributable to gas
in front of the pulsar; the velocity of the furthest absorption
feature thus yields a lower limit on the pulsar distance. When
strong H I emission is detected beyond the furthest absorp-
tion feature in the pulsar spectrum, then an upper distance
limit can be inferred (though in practice, the inferred upper
limits depend on the observational sensitivity and the optical
depth of H I clouds; see Frail & Weisberg 1990 for further
discussion). In many cases, only a lower or upper distance
bound can be inferred due to a lack of detected absorption or
emission.
The kinematic method also suffers from uncertainties due
to peculiar motions, which are relevant to both the H I gas and
the assumed Galactic rotation curve. Previous pulsar kine-
matic distance catalogs adopted flat universal rotation curves
(e.g. Fich et al. 1989) with IAU constants, albeit with spe-
cial consideration of peculiar motions in the direction of the
Perseus arm (Frail & Weisberg 1990; Verbiest et al. 2012;
Jing et al. 2023). Since the publication of these catalogs,
Galactic rotation curve models have improved thanks to a
significant increase in parallax and proper motion measure-

2. THE SAMPLE
For this analysis, we consider all radio pulsars (including
radio-loud magnetars) with H I spectral line constraints in
the literature. The sample consists of 66 sightlines, drawn
primarily from Frail & Weisberg (1990) and Verbiest et al.
(2012) and references therein.1 More recent H I measure-
ments are drawn from Liu et al. (2025); Jiang et al. (2025);
Jing et al. (2023); Liu et al. (2021); van Leeuwen et al.
(2015); Kothes et al. (2018); Rudnitskii et al. (2014).
Pulsars in the sample are bright (median S1400 GHz =
8 mJy) and span a broad range in Galactic coordinates
(−116◦≤l ≤160◦; −9◦≤b ≤26◦) and DM (3.2 ≤
DM ≤787 pc cm−3). Thirty-seven of the sources have a de-
tection of both absorption and emission that give both a ve-
locity lower and upper bound on distance; the rest either have
only a single upper or lower velocity bound. Twenty-seven
pulsars in the sample have a velocity bound at the tangent
point.

3.2. Deriving Kinematic Distances from Radial Velocities
Kinematic distances are determined based on a pulsar’s
known Galactic longitude and latitude, the assumed Θ(R)
model, and reported H I radial velocities inferred from the
pulsar’s radio spectrum. Radial velocity constraints fall into
three main categories: (1) Lower and upper bounds; (2) sin-
gle lower or upper bound; and (3) tangent point velocities.
Tangent point velocities are those that lie at or above the max-
imum velocity expected for their sightlines. Figure 1 shows
three pulsars illustrating these cases.
Sightlines through the Perseus arm, between longitudes
100◦≲l ≲150◦, are well-known to be affected by signifi-
cant peculiar motions (Belfort & Crovisier 1984; Reid et al.
2019). The standard correction is applied for these sight-
lines (Vr,corr = Vr,obs/1.6) (Frail & Weisberg 1990; Jing
et al. 2023). Throughout the analysis we adopt standard ra-
dial velocity uncertainties of ±7 km s−1 to account for ran-
dom cloud motions (Frail & Weisberg 1990), except in the
direction of Perseus, for which this appears to overestimate
the errors (see e.g. Figure 1b) and we instead adopt 2 km
s−1 (the size of the smoothing kernel used in Weisberg et al.
2008).
4. ANALYSIS & RESULTS
4.1. Revised Kinematic Distances
The kinematic distances are provided in a Zenodo reposi-
tory (doi:10.5281/zenodo.19775798). Previous studies used
a variety of rotation curve models, all of which yield slightly
different distance results than the R19 model applied here.
On average, our distances differ by ≈10% from the distances
reported in Frail & Weisberg (1990), which are based on the
Fich et al. (1989) rotation curve. The most significant de-
partures between our distances and those previously reported
occur due to differences in rotation curves at R < 2.5 kpc
and ≈6 −7 kpc, where the circular velocity peaks in the
R19 model.

4.2. Comparison to Parallax Distances
Eight pulsars in the sample also have parallax distances.2
In all but two cases, the parallax distances agree to within
1σ with the H I kinematic distance; pulsars J2113+4644 and
J2018+2839 both have parallaxes that are > 1σ smaller than
the lower kinematic distance bounds, but they are still con-
sistent to within 2σ. None of these pulsars have parallax dis-
tances greater than the upper kinematic distance bounds. Two
of the examples in Figure 1 show comparisons to independent
parallax distances.

5. CONCLUSIONS
This research note provides an updated catalog of pulsar
kinematic distances based on analysis of archival H I radial
velocity measurements with a uniformly applied, modern ro-
tation curve model. There is significant room for improve-
ment in the analysis, including deeper assessment of sys-
tematic uncertainties associated with the H I measurements
themselves and the corrections applied for peculiar and ran-
dom motions. In the future we will explore methods to re-
fine distance bounds through consideration of both pulsar
and ISM properties, as well as technical advances in kine-
matic distance estimation based on Wenger et al. (2018) and
Reid (2022).
The distances inferred here are provided in
an online repository (doi:10.5281/zenodo.19775798), along
with a Python script for estimating pulsar kinematic dis-
tances from H I measurements (github.com/stella-ocker/psr-
HI-kinematics).
