Minimal Canonical Contracts*

Abstract
We study contracting with imperfect commitment and identify minimal canoni-
cal contract spaces that fully characterize equilibrium outcomes under general pref-
erences. Different from previous solutions, our framework accommodates infinite
agent type spaces (unlike Bester and Strausz (2001)), non-quasi-linear utilities (unlike
Skreta (2006)), and settings where the principal lacks the commitment power typically
assumed in information design (unlike Doval and Skreta (2022)). Moreover, our re-
sults apply to both single- and multi-principal environments, providing a unified and
tractable approach to contracting under limited commitment.

Introduction
Contracts under imperfect commitment are at the heart of many real-world economic re-
lationships. For example, a government agency contracting with a private firm to deliver
essential public services may guarantee payments for basic operations but cannot fully
commit to future pricing or service adjustments (Arve and Martimort (2016)). Similarly,
in corporate partnerships, firms may agree on immediate deliverables while retaining
flexibility over future decisions (Krishna and Morgan (2008)). These common challenges
highlight a fundamental question in contract design: how should contracts be designed
when full commitment is infeasible?
Traditional models in contract theory typically assume perfect commitment, whereby
parties are fully bound by their agreements. However, real-world contracts frequently
involve some degree of flexibility, making perfect commitment a strong assumption that
often fails to capture economic complexities. Recognizing this limitation, early research—
exemplified by the pioneering work of Hart and Tirole (1988) and Laffont and Tirole
(1993)—began to explore contracting under imperfect commitment.
Building on these insights, Bester and Strausz (2001) introduced a seminal frame-
work that has shaped our understanding of contracting under imperfect commitment.
In their model, the principal’s actions are divided into two categories: contractible and
non-contractible. Contractible actions can be formally enforced through binding agree-
ments, while non-contractible actions remain discretionary, allowing deviations after the
contract is signed. For instance, in long-term contracting, a principal may commit to an
initial compensation scheme but retains discretion over future payments or performance
incentives. Since these actions are non-contractible, agents anticipate renegotiation, dis-
torting their investment and effort decisions (Hart and Moore (1988) and Halac and Yared
(2022)). These insights underscore the limitations of the traditional revelation principle in
environments with imperfect commitment. To address this, Bester and Strausz (2001) pro-
pose a direct mechanism that accounts for the principal’s discretion. Unlike the standard
revelation principle, which assumes full commitment, their approach aligns discretionary
actions with truthful revelation, ensuring tractability and incentive compatibility while
simplifying equilibrium analysis. However, their approach applies only when the agent’s

private types are finite1—a restriction that significantly limits its applicability to real-world
settings where private information is often continuous.
Since Bester and Strausz (2001), little progress on the canonical model has been made
beyond the original finite-type solution, even though solutions to several modified ver-
sions of the canonical model have been proposed in specific environments. For instance,
Skreta (2006) extended the framework to a dynamic setting involving durable goods by
accommodating a continuous type space—albeit by relying on quasilinear utility assump-
tions. In regulatory contexts, Bester and Kr¨ahmer (2012, 2017) introduced adaptations in-
corporating exit options and serial correlation in types. More recently, Doval and Skreta
(2022) proposed an alternative approach using information design to transform unobserv-
able inputs into observable outputs, thereby facilitating contract formation. While this
method alleviates some commitment issues, it does so by introducing additional com-
mitment layers, potentially undermining the contract’s credibility if the principal retains
access to the original unobservable input.
Despite these advances, existing approaches remain tailored to specific economic
environments, lacking a general framework applicable to broader contracting problems.
In particular, no existing approaches fully characterizes equilibrium allocations in the
original model of Bester and Strausz (2001) when extended to environments with infinite
types.
Motivated by these limitations, this paper focuses on revisiting the original model
of Bester and Strausz (2001) but extends it to incorporate general preferences, deliber-
ately avoiding reliance on information design tools and without restricting the analysis
to either finite types or quasilinear preference. Moreover, our model accommodates any
finite number of principals,2 thus capturing the complexity of competitive contracting en-
vironments. In such principal-agent scenarios, where principals may adopt any arbitrary
contracts, a significant conceptual and technical challenge arises: how to rigorously de-
fine the problem when the set of all possible contracts is not clearly delineated. This issue
is analogous to challenges encountered in optimal auction design, where Myerson (1981)

Related literature
Up until Bester and Strausz (2001), only a small portion of the liter-
ature allowed for unrestricted general mechanisms to derive optimal contracts with im-
perfect commitment in specific settings, such as long-term contracts with renegotiation
in the sale of durable goods. Examples include Hart and Tirole (1988), who focused on a
binary quantity and finite time horizon, and Laffont and Tirole (1990), who considered a
continuum of quantities and two periods. In these models, the buyer’s type is finite.
Bester and Strausz (2001) made a path-breaking contribution to the literature by re-
viving the canonicity of direct mechanisms when the agent’s private information is mod-
eled as a finite type. This allowed Maestri (2017) to extend the analyses of Hart and Tirole
(1988) and Laffont and Tirole (1990) to the case of a continuum of quantities and an infinite
horizon, with the buyer having a binary type.
It has been elusive to derive canonical contract spaces with an agent’s continuous
type. Krishna and Morgan (2008) accomplished this in a specific setting that extends the
“cheap talk” model of Crawford and Sobel (1982). They introduced contractible monetary
transfers contingent on the information reported by the agent, establishing a modified
revelation principle with standard single-peaked utility, coupled with monetary transfers
4


## Page 5


in a quasilinear fashion. Skreta (2006) considered a finite time horizon where a seller
of a durable good can only offer short-term contracts that specify the current terms of
trade, but not future ones, if the good is not sold. She derived a general solution for the
case where the buyer’s type is continuous and both buyer and seller have quasilinear
preferences.
Separate from the effort to identify canonical contracts, contracting with imperfect
commitment has been studied across various applications. Gagnepain, Ivaldi, and Marti-
mort (2013) fit a structural principal-agent model with renegotiation to a set of contracts
for the French urban transport service, measuring the cost of renegotiation. They show
that welfare gains from improved commitment would be significant, though they would
mostly benefit the operators. In Arve and Martimort (2016), the private information of
the firm providing public utility is two-dimensional: the (continuous) cost of the basic
service, which is contractible in the first period, and the (finite) cost of additional services
provided in the second period. Battaglini (2007) identifies conditions under which the
optimal contract with commitment is renegotiation-proof, even when type realizations
over time are serially correlated and the type is binary.
Bester and Kr¨ahmer (2012, 2017) extend Krishna and Morgan (2008) by incorporat-
ing exit options. Bester and Kr¨ahmer (2012) demonstrate that pooling is unavoidable in
a model where the seller makes an unverifiable quality choice, the buyer has finite pri-
vate information about valuation, and the buyer can refuse to trade after observing the
quality of the good. Bester and Kr¨ahmer (2017) introduce a mediator, to whom either the
seller or buyer must pay a penalty if they prematurely terminate the deal. While in Kr-
ishna and Morgan (2008), delegation outperforms authority only if the conflict of interest
is sufficiently small, Bester and Kr¨ahmer (2017) show that, with exit options, delegation
is always optimal. As they note, for the case of unmediated, face-to-face communication,
there is no general revelation principle for their setting with a continuum of states.
Contracting with imperfect commitment is also an important consideration in the
design of unemployment insurance, as the government may not perfectly commit to the
allocations it will choose once the worker’s job opportunities are revealed (Golosov and
Iovino (2021)).

Motivating examples and key idea
In this section, we describe several economic examples3 where no tools in existing pa-
pers (e.g., Bester and Strausz (2001), Skreta (2006), Krishna and Morgan (2008), Doval
and Skreta (2022)) can be used to conduct equilibrium analysis. In all these examples, the
agent’s private information is parametrized by a continuous type instead of a finite type
(Bester and Strausz (2001)). In contrast to Doval and Skreta (2022), principals also cannot
commit to a message structure that stochastically converts the agent’s (unobservable) in-
put message to an (observable) output message, which then determines their contractible
part of actions. Even with the agent’s continuous type, we cannot extend a modified
Revelation Principle (Krishna and Morgan (2008)) nor use the standard technique that
eliminating one variable in the agent’s equilibrium utility by the virtue of the envelop
theorem, so the approach taken in Skreta (2006) is not applicable.
The strength of the minimal canonical contracts identified in this paper is that the
canonical property is established in a very general environment with no prior restrictions
on whether type and action are finite or infinite. Section 7 shows how the minimal canon-
ical contracts help us fully characterize equilibrium allocations in these examples.

Equilibrium notions without a fixed contract space
Because eG, the set of all possible contracts, is not well-defined, it is conceptually impos-
sible to define an equilibrium relative to eG. To overcome this difficulty, we define the

Different from Definitions 1 and 2, Definitions 3 and 4 allow principals to consider
any possible contracts.

8
Conclusion
This paper addresses the challenge of deriving equilibrium allocations in contracting en-
vironments with limited commitment, without relying on ad hoc restrictions on contract
spaces. By identifying minimal canonical contract spaces under general preferences, we
provide a unified framework that extends beyond existing models, which typically as-
sume finite types, quasilinear preferences, or access to information design tools. Our
findings demonstrate that canonical contracts can be systematically applied to various
economic settings, simplifying equilibrium analysis while maintaining generality.
Beyond its theoretical contributions, this work has important implications for con-
tract design in markets, regulation, and policy making, where commitment constraints
are common. Future research could explore extensions to dynamic environments, richer
strategic interactions between multiple principals, or settings with endogenous informa-
tion structures. By broadening the scope of contract theory to account for more realistic
commitment limitations, our results provide a foundation for more practical and flexible
approaches to contract design.
