Crystallographic symmetry is usually taken as a guide to electronic equivalence in crystals: atoms on 
the same Wyckoff position are expected to have the same charge, whereas atoms on different Wyckoff 
positions are expected to be electronically distinct. Here we show that both expectations can fail in opposite 
ways: crystallographically equivalent sites can become charge-inequivalent under compression, whereas 
crystallographically inequivalent sites can remain charge-equivalent at low pressure because of an emergent 
hidden symmetry. We develop a minimal Landau theory of pressure-induced charge transfer, in which com-
pression enhances the intersite Coulomb energy gained by charge redistribution until it overcomes the onsite 
charging cost and destabilizes the charge-equivalent state.  In BCC Na, all sites are charge-equivalent at 
low pressure, but compression drives charge transfer between neighboring sites, producing an electronically 
symmetry-broken CsCl-type state on an unchanged BCC ionic framework. In hP4 Na, the opposite anomaly 
occurs: two Na sites occupy distinct Wyckoff positions, yet remain charge-equivalent at low pressure be-
cause of an emergent gauge equivalence in the low-energy manifold, giving rise to near-Fermi doublets that 
appear accidental in conventional space-group analysis. Upon compression, pressure-induced charge trans-
fer breaks this hidden equivalence, splits the near-Fermi doublets, and drives a metal-insulator transition. 
These two complementary cases establish pressure-induced charge transfer as a mechanism by which elec-
tronic equivalence can either fall below or rise above what Wyckoff positions alone would suggest, showing 
that lattice symmetry constrains but does not uniquely determine the equivalence structure of the electronic 
state. 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Crystallographic symmetry is usually taken as a guide to electronic equivalence in crystals. In particular, 
atoms on the same Wyckoff position are expected to be electronically equivalent, whereas atoms on differ-
ent Wyckoff positions are expected to be distinct. This expectation is so natural that it is often taken for 
granted rather than examined directly. Yet crystallographic equivalence is defined by the atomic lattice, 
while electronic equivalence is a property of the self-consistent many-electron state. 
Pressure provides a direct way to test this assumption. Compression is known to drive rich structural 
and electronic reconstruction in simple elements, particularly in alkali metals, where it induces polymor-
phism and clear departures from the nearly-free-electron picture [1,7,29]. Representative examples include 
structural transitions in compressed alkali metals [2-5], broken-symmetry behavior in dense Li [6], metal-
semiconductor or insulating behavior in dense Li and Na [8,9], and pressure-induced charge transfer in 
elemental boron [30]. 
We find that the expected correspondence between Wyckoff equivalence and electronic equivalence 
can fail in two opposite directions. In one direction, crystallographically equivalent sites can become elec-
tronically inequivalent when pressure destabilizes the symmetric charge state and selects a lower-symmetry 
charge-transfer state. In the other direction, crystallographically inequivalent sites can remain electronically

equivalent when the low-energy electronic manifold possesses an emergent hidden symmetry beyond the 
crystallographic space group. These two possibilities overturn the usual Wyckoff-based intuition from op-
posite sides: electronic equivalence can fall below, or rise above, what is expected by the atomic lattice 
alone. To our knowledge, these two anomalies have not been understood within a unified conceptual frame-
work. 
In this work, we address this problem within a unified charge-transfer framework. We formulate a min-
imal Landau theory in terms of a charge-transfer order parameter. Under compression, reduced interatomic 
distances enhance intersite Coulomb interactions and can destabilize an otherwise charge-equivalent elec-
tronic state. The key competition is between the intersite Coulomb energy gained by transferring charge 
between neighboring sites and the onsite charging cost required to create local imbalance. When compres-
sion drives the quadratic coefficient through zero, the charge-equivalent state becomes unstable and a sym-
metry-broken charge-transfer state emerges. 
Here, we take BCC Na as an example. All Na atoms occupy the same Wyckoff position and are elec-
tronically equivalent at low pressure. Under compression, however, the symmetric charge state becomes 
unstable and a lower-energy self-consistent state develops finite charge imbalance between neighboring 
sites within the BCC-derived framework, acquiring a lower symmetry electronic charge density state (i.e., 
the CsCl-type state). 
The second example is Na in hP4 structure. The two Na sites belong to different Wyckoff positions, yet 
at low pressure they remain charge-equivalent within the low-energy electronic manifold. We trace this 
behavior to an emergent gauge equivalence among close-packed stackings [10], which makes the ABAC 
and ABAB descriptions effectively equivalent in the relevant low-energy subspace. This hidden equiva-
lence protects near-Fermi doublets that appear accidental from conventional space-group analysis. Upon 
compression, pressure-induced charge transfer also breaks the delicate hidden equivalence, splits the near-
Fermi doublets, and opens a band gap, thereby driving the metal-insulator transition. 
Together, these results show that pressure can reorganize electronic equivalence in two opposite ways: 
it can make equivalent sites electronically distinct, or it can support and then destroy an emergent equiva-
lence between inequivalent sites. Wyckoff positions define the symmetry of the atomic framework, but the 
electronic state may obey a different hierarchy of equivalences.

III. TWO CLASSES OF PRESSURE-DRIVEN ELECTRONIC EQUIVALENCE CHANGES 
We now show how this instability appears in first-principles calculations. The two examples are chosen 
to expose the two possible failures of Wyckoff-based intuition: in one case, equivalent crystallographic sites 
become electronically inequivalent; in the other, inequivalent crystallographic sites at first appear to be 
electronically equivalent associated with a higher symmetry and then split by pressure-induced charge 
transfer. In all calculations, the atomic framework is kept to be the same at each pressure so that the changes 
discussed below are pure of electronic origin.

A. Class I: Equivalent Wyckoff sites become electronically inequivalent 
In crystals where atoms occupy nominally symmetry-equivalent sites (i.e., the same Wyckoff position), 
standard crystallographic analysis would suggest that the sites are electronically equivalent. Under pressure, 
however, the symmetric electronic state can become unstable, and the system may spontaneously select one 
of a pair of symmetry-related lower-symmetry states, in which charge transfer develops between the sites 
even though the atomic framework remains unchanged. This is the spontaneous electronic symmetry break-
ing discussed here [31,32]. 
The anomaly is already visible in the simplest possible class: crystals with only one crystallographic 
Wyckoff position. In such systems, standard crystallographic reasoning would suggest that all sites should 
remain electronically equivalent. Yet beyond a critical compression, the symmetric charge state can cease 
to be the lowest-energy electronic state. The system can instead lower its energy by developing opposite 
charge deviations on neighboring sublattices while the ionic framework remains fixed. This raises the cen-
tral question for this class: how can crystallographically equivalent sites become electronically inequivalent 
without a structural symmetry reduction? 
BCC Na provides a clean benchmark for this question. The choice is motivated by the critical condition 
r(P) < Meff(P)R discussed in Sec. II. For the alternating two-sublattice charge pattern discussed here, the 
electrostatic lattice sum is that of the CsCl structure, which has a comparatively large Madelung constant, 
M ≃ 1.762675 [16,26,27]. Sodium is also an element with large valence atomic wavefunction that adopts 
the BCC structure under ambient conditions [5,13]. To keep the physical picture transparent, we take the 
effective charging radius to be pressure independent, R(P) = R, and interpret it as a capacitive radius set by 
the spatial extent of the valence charge around each site. For Na, this scale is naturally of the order of the 
tabulated single-bond covalent radius, ~1.55 Å [14,15]. 
Crystallographically, the BCC structure belongs to space group Im3̄m with point group Oh. The two 
atoms at A (0,0,0) and B (1/2,1/2,1/2) are symmetry-equivalent and belong to the same Wyckoff position.

B. Class II: Inequivalent Wyckoff sites become electronically equivalent 
We next turn to the complementary situation. In crystals with inequivalent Wyckoff sites, standard 
crystallographic analysis would suggest that the sites should have different charges and wavefunctions. This 
expectation can also fail, but in the opposite direction: the stable electronic manifold may acquire a higher 
hidden symmetry beyond that dictated by the crystallographic space group. Such a symmetry can make 
inequivalent Wyckoff sites electronically equivalent and protect near-Fermi surface degeneracies that would 
otherwise appear accidental. 
This complementary anomaly is already visible in the simplest possible class: crystals with two in-
equivalent Wyckoff sites. In such systems, Wyckoff inequivalence normally implies different local elec-
tronic environments. Yet at low pressure, the ground state electronic manifold can contain an additional 
gauge equivalence that relates the two sites, producing charge equivalence and near-degenerate bands even 
though the nuclear sites remain crystallographically distinct. Under compression, this hidden equivalence 
can break down, allowing charge transfer between the two sites and lifting the associated degeneracies. 
hP4 Na provides a clean example of this scenario. At low pressure, the two crystallographically in-
equivalent Na sites behave as electronically equivalent within the near-Fermi manifold; at higher pressure,

To elucidate the origin of the low-pressure charge equivalence between different Wyckoff sites and the 
near-Fermi band degeneracies in close-packed structures, we divide the symmetry analysis into the follow-

(i) In the AB (hcp) primitive cell of space group P63/mmc, the nonsymmorphic symmetry enforces 
twofold band sticking on the kz = π/c boundary plane, yielding robust double degeneracy along the boundary 
loop A–L–H–A (as shown in Fig. 5c). 
(ii) Upon constructing the ABAB supercell (as shown in Fig. 3), Brillouin-zone folding maps the AB 
boundary loop A–L–H–A exactly onto the ABAB basal-plane loop Γ–M–K–Γ, thereby transplanting the 
boundary degeneracy onto the Γ–M–K–Γ path of ABAB (as shown in Fig. 5b). 
(iii) Although hP4 (ABAC) is not a simple 2c supercell of AB, at low pressure—when the low-energy

nearly identical basal-plane dispersions, including the same folded degeneracy feature (as shown in Fig. 5a 
and Fig. 5b). This indicates that ABAC and ABAB share the same low-pressure wavefunction symmetries. 
Since inversion symmetry in ABAB enforces equal charges on Na1 and Na2, ABAC inherits this charge 
equivalence at low pressure, explaining the seemingly counterintuitive equal site charges.

equivalence protects the (Γ3− ⊕ Γ4+) pair (and likewise the K–Γ and Γ–M doublets) against splitting even

IV. CONCLUSIONS 
We have developed a minimal charge-transfer framework for pressure-driven changes in electronic 
equivalence. In this framework, the relevant order parameter is the charge imbalance ΔQ between crystal-
lographic sites. Compression enhances intersite Coulomb interactions; once the energy gained by transfer-
ring charge between sites exceeds the onsite charging cost required to create local imbalance, the  charge-
equivalent state loses stability . This provides a simple instability criterion for electronic symmetry breaking, 
even when the atomic lattice is held fixed in a given space group. 
The first consequence is that crystallographic equivalence does not necessarily enforce electronic 
equivalence. In BCC Na, all atoms occupy the same Wyckoff position and are equivalent at low pressure. 
Under compression, however, the symmetric charge state becomes unstable, and the system lowers its en-
ergy by developing a finite ΔQ between neighboring A and B sublattices. The ionic framework remains 
BCC, while the electronic density realizes a CsCl-like charge order on the BCC backbone. This provides a 
microscopic route by which crystallographically equivalent sites can become electronically distinct without 
an imposed structural symmetry reduction. 
The second consequence is that crystallographic inequivalence does not necessarily imply electronic 
inequivalence. In hP4 (ABAC) Na, Na1 and Na2 are crystallographically distinct, yet at low pressure an 
emergent gauge/charge equivalence in the (s,p_z)-dominated low-energy manifold makes them effectively 
equivalent and protects a set of near-Fermi doublets along K–Γ–M. Upon compression, charge transfer 
breaks this hidden equivalence by generating an onsite imbalance between Na1 and Na2. This imbalance 
lifts the protected doublets, separates the valence-band maximum from the conduction-band minimum, and 
drives the metal-insulator transition.

Taken together, these results show that Wyckoff positions do not by themselves determine the hierarchy 
of electronic equivalence. Electronic equivalence can fall below the equivalence imposed by the nuclear 
lattice, as in BCC Na, or rise above it through an emergent hidden symmetry, as in hP4 Na, before being 
destroyed by compression. Thus, pressure-induced charge transfer provides a unified microscopic mecha-
nism by which the self-consistent electronic state can reorganize equivalence relations beyond those spec-
ified by crystallography alone. More broadly, our results revise the usual symmetry-based intuition in solid-
state physics: the symmetry of the atomic lattice constrains, but does not uniquely determine, the symmetry 
and equivalence structure of the electronic state.
