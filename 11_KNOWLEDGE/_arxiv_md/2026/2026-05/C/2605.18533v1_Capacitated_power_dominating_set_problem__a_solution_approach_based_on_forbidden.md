Abstract
The optimal placement of measurement devices in electrical power systems is commonly modeled
through the power dominating set problem. However, in real-world applications, these devices have
limited capacities, leading to a capacitated variant of the problem that has received little attention in
the literature. In this work, we introduce forbidden propagation sets, novel combinatorial structures
that cannot occur simultaneously in any feasible solution. This notion enables a new class of integer
linear programming formulations. They combine infection-based variables with exponentially many
constraints, while avoiding big-M constraints. We derive structural properties, valid inequalities, and
redundancy-breaking constraints, and design an efficient lazy-separation procedure based on cycle detection.
Computational experiments on benchmark instances with up to 14,000 vertices show that the proposed
method achieves an average execution-time improvement of 1.7x over existing approaches adapted from
the literature. Moreover, the results indicate that performance depends not only on network size, but also
on capacities.

Introduction
Monitoring electric power systems is a fundamental control mechanism to ensure secure and reliable operations.
In recent decades, this area has received considerable attention, motivated by the widespread usage of
measurement devices with high sampling rate and accuracy, known as phasor measurement units (PMUs),
and by the global interest in smart grids in the context of clean energies (Joshi & Verma, 2021). The power
dominating set problem (PDS) has emerged as a fundamental model for determining the minimum number of
measurement devices, together with their optimal locations, required to monitor the entire network (Baldwin
et al., 1993). However, real-world applications often involve additional limitations and requirements that are
not adequately captured by standard models. Representative examples include limitations in the number of
channels (Korkali & Abur, 2009), connectivity settings (Fan & Watson, 2012), and fault-tolerant strategies

(Almasabi & Mitra, 2019). These considerations highlight the need for more realistic models that account for
such practical constraints.
As a graph optimization problem, PDS was first introduced by Haynes et al. (2002). However, the
definition most commonly used in the literature is due to Brueni & Heath (2005), who proposed a simplified
but equivalent problem. The objective is to find a subset S of vertices with minimum cardinality, representing
PMU locations, such that all vertices of the graph can be monitored through the sequential application of
two rules. First, each vertex in S monitors its closed neighborhood, as in the well-known dominating set
problem. Second, a monitored vertex can monitor one of its neighbors if all its remaining neighbors are
already monitored, a mechanism commonly referred to as propagation. Furthermore, the last rule can only
be applied at vertices satisfying the zero-injection property, meaning they have neither generation nor load.
The main drawback of this standard model is that the first rule assumes that a PMU has sufficient channels
(capacity) to monitor all of its neighbors, which is unrealistic in practice. Only a few studies address this
capacitated variant, called capacitated power dominating set problem (CPDS), which introduces additional
computational challenges: in contrast to PDS, it is necessary not only to decide the PMU locations, but also
which neighbors each PMU monitors.
Regarding computational hardness, PDS remains NP-hard even when restricted to bipartite or chordal
graphs (Haynes et al., 2002), as well as planar or split graphs (Guo et al., 2008). On the other hand,
linear-time algorithms have been developed for several classes, including trees (Haynes et al., 2002), block
graphs (Xu et al., 2006), circular-arc graphs (Liao & Lee, 2013), and graphs of bounded treewidth (Guo et al.,
2008). Concerning exact algorithms for arbitrary graphs, a moderately exponential-time algorithm with
running time O∗(1.7548n) was theoretically proposed in Binkele-Raible & Fernau (2012). From a practical
perspective, the most successful exact approaches are based on integer linear programming (ILP). Two main
modeling paradigms have been explored. Infection-based models explicitly track the application of the rules
over time, as in Brimkov et al. (2019); Jovanovic & Voss (2020). These formulations are compact, but use
integer variables and big-M constraints to model the timestep at which each vertex is monitored. In contrast,
fort-based models rely on a hitting-set characterization, where solutions must intersect the neighborhoods
of certain combinatorial structures known as forts (Bozeman et al., 2019). These formulations use a single
binary variable per vertex, but involve exponentially many constraints instead. A fort-based algorithm with
dynamic generation of constraints is the current state of the art, which can solve benchmark instances with
up to 82,000 vertices in a few minutes (Bl¨asius & G¨ottlicher, 2024).
As discussed previously, the incorporation of capacities has received little attention in the literature.
Carvalho et al. (2018) propose single-level and bilevel ILP formulations for both PDS and CPDS . However,
their model admits an additional propagation rule that allows a vertex to propagate to itself whenever all
of its neighbors are already monitored. To capture the capacity requirements, the authors introduce new
variables associated with the graph edges in order to control the individual application of the domination rule.
From a computational perspective, this approach exhibits limited scalability, as the two largest instances
solved contain 300 and 2007 vertices.
For these reasons, this work aims to advance the development of exact algorithms for CPDS, with a strong
focus on practical applicability. To this end, we introduce the notion of forbidden propagation sets, a novel
combinatorial structure grouping propagations that cannot be applied together because they create cyclic
temporal precedences. This concept enables ILP formulations that combine some infection-based variables
with exponentially many constraints, as in fort-based models, while avoiding big-M constraints that can
severely weaken linear relaxation bounds. To our knowledge, this approach has not previously been considered

• We introduce forbidden propagation sets, a novel concept in the power dominating set literature that

• We propose a new class of ILP formulations that rely on infection-based variables, but replace traditional

• We design a polynomial-time lazy-constraint separation that takes advantage of the derived structural

from the literature.
2
Capacitated power dominating set problem
In this work, we use standard definitions and notation from graph theory. Given a vertex v, δ(v) denotes
its degree, N(v) its set of neighbors, and N[v] = N(v) ∪{v}. In a digraph D, a (simple) cycle C is defined
as a sequence ((v1, v2), (v2, v3), . . . , (vn, v1)) of arcs of D, where vi ̸= vj for all i, j ∈{1, . . . , n}. In this case,
the cycle has length |C| = n and we may refer to it as an n-cycle. We say that an arc (vi, vj) ∈C if (vi, vj)
appears in the sequence that defines C. A chord of C is any arc of D that is not in C and whose endpoints
are both vertices of C. We denote by C (D) the set of cycles of D.
Let G = (V, E) be the graph that represents the electric power system, where the vertices are the buses
and the edges are the transmission lines. Let VP ⊆V denote the subset of zero-injection vertices. The PMU
locations are represented by a set S ⊆V . To encode the decisions arising from the channel limitation, we
introduce a function ρ : S →P(V ) such that, for each v ∈S, ρ(v) ⊆N(v). Since PMU locations can be
recovered from the domain of ρ, we usually denote Sρ = Dom(ρ). Monitoring obeys the following two rules.
Each u ∈Sρ monitors itself and all neighbors in ρ(u). Moreover, whenever a monitored vertex u has at least
δ(u) −1 monitored neighbors, its remaining neighbor also becomes monitored, and this rule can be applied
iteratively. Formally, we define the monitored set M(ρ) of ρ as the smallest subset of V satisfying:
Domination rule (DR). If u ∈Sρ and v ∈(ρ(u) ∪{u}), then v ∈M(ρ).
Propagation rule (PR). If u ∈VP ∩M(ρ), v ∈N(u) and N(u) \ {v} ⊆M(ρ), then v ∈M(ρ).

This work focuses on the following optimization problem.
Capacitated Power Dominating Set problem (CPDS)
Instance: A graph G = (V, E), a subset VP ⊆V , and an integer k ∈Z+.
Goal: Find a k-capacitated power dominating function ρ of (G, VP ) such that |Sρ| is minimum.
In particular, CPDS restricted to instances where k is at least the maximum degree of G is equivalent
to PDS, where the goal reduces to finding the set S ⊆V and defining ρ(u) = N(u) for every u ∈S. In
this setting, a set S such that M(ρ) = V is known as a power dominating set. Conversely, its restriction to
instances with k = 0 is equivalent to the zero-forcing problem (Bozeman et al., 2019). Consequently, CPDS is
NP-hard. Since CPDS can be solved independently on each connected component of G, it suffices to consider
instances where G is connected.
2.1
Propagations and precedence digraph
Given a k-capacitated function ρ, a calculation of M(ρ) consists of a finite sequence of applications of rules
(DR) and (PR) in a specified order such that, at the end, neither rule can be further applied to monitor any
remaining unmonitored vertices. A calculation of M(ρ) is proper when each applied rule increases the size of
the monitored set by exactly one, i.e., when the calculation has no redundant applications of the rules.

The propagation rule (PR) can potentially be applied at any vertex u ∈VP to monitor a neighbor
v ∈N(u), provided that the preconditions of the rule are satisfied. We represent each such possibility by the
pair (u, v), referred to as a propagation, more precisely, an outgoing propagation from u and an incoming
propagation to v. For convenience, we denote by AP = {(u, v) : u ∈VP , v ∈N(u)} the set of all potential
propagations. For example, the set AP associated with the graph in Figure 1 is given by the cyan propagations
shown in the left graph of Figure 2a. The propagations actually applied in the calculation of M(ρ) form a
subset of AP ; for instance, those used in the example in Figure 1 correspond to the cyan propagations shown
in the left graph of Figure 2b.
Applying the propagation rule (PR) imposes temporal precedences, requiring that certain vertices be
monitored before others. For example, in Figure 1b, the propagation (a, e) requires vertices a, b, and d to
already be monitored. Specifically, a propagation (u, v) ∈AP can be applied only if every vertex w ∈N[u]\{v}
is monitored before v. Such a requirement is represented by the pair (w, v), which we refer to as a precedence.
Observe that all precedences arising from a given propagation share the target vertex with that propagation.
The connection between propagations and precedences is formalized through the following mapping. Let ψ
be the function that assigns to each propagation (u, v) ∈AP the set of precedences imposed by its application,
formally ψ(u, v) = {(w, v) : w ∈N[u] \ {v}}.

in R is then DR = (V, ψ(R)). For simplicity, we write DAP = D. Note that D denotes the precedence

3
Forbidden propagation sets
We introduce a novel combinatorial structure, referred to as forbidden propagation sets, representing sets
of propagations that cannot be applied simultaneously in a proper calculation of the monitored set. The
definition of these structures is motivated by the following result.
Lemma 1. Let ρ be a k-capacitated function. If R ⊆AP is the set of propagations applied by rule (PR) in a
proper calculation of M(ρ), then the precedence digraph DR contains no cycles.
Proof. Let R ⊆AP be the set of propagations applied by rule (PR) in a proper calculation of M(ρ). Suppose
that DR contains a cycle C = (e1, e2, . . . , er). Since the calculation of M(ρ) is proper, it follows that R has
no redundant incoming propagations. Hence, by Remark 1, each arc ei = (vi, vi+1) ∈C is imposed by a
unique propagation in R, namely pi = φR(ei), with target vi+1, and this propagation does not impose any
other arc of C.
When pi is applied following the order of the calculation of M(ρ), vertex vi has already been monitored.
Since the calculation of M(ρ) is proper, monitoring vi is possible only if pi−1 (indices taken modulo r), which
is the propagation of R that monitors vi, is applied before pi. As this holds for all i ∈{1, . . . , r}, a cyclic
contradiction follows.
Lemma 1 motivates defining forbidden structures for sets of propagations in a proper computation of
M(ρ).
Definition 1. A subset F ⊆AP is called a forbidden propagation set (FPS) if the precedence digraph DF
contains at least one cycle, i.e. C (DF ) ̸= ∅.
The next key result follows directly and serves as the motivation for the ILP formulations introduced in
Section 4.
Remark 2. Let ρ be a k-capacitated function and F be an FPS. Any proper calculation of M(ρ) applies at
most |F| −1 propagations of F.
Observe that if F is an FPS, then every ˜F ⊆AP with F ⊆˜F is also an FPS, since every cycle in C (DF )
also belongs to C (D ˜
F ). Two distinct notions of minimality related to FPSs naturally arise.
Definition 2.
1. Let C ∈C (D). We say that F ⊆AP is an FPS that minimally imposes C if C ∈C (DF ) and, for
every p ∈F, C /∈C (DF \{p}).
2. We say that F ⊆AP is a minimal FPS if F is an FPS and, for every p ∈F, F \ {p} is no longer an
FPS.

4.3

The exponential number of constraints in FPS-IP and EFPS-IP motivates a lazy-constraint approach embedded
within a generic framework for solving ILP. For FPS-IP, starting from a (possibly empty) subset of FPS

polynomial-time separation procedure for FPS constraints, supported by the results introduced in the previous

are generated, and a minimal FPS is derived from each cycle.

For CPDS, we establish an analogous characterization based on forts for a k-capacitated power dominating

belong to Sρ to monitor some vertex in F through rule (DR); rather, u must actually decide to monitor a

if and only if, for every minimal fort F ∈F(G), Sρ ∩F ̸= ∅or there exists u ∈N(F) ∩Sρ such that

Proof. Let ρ be a k-capacitated power dominating function, and consider a proper calculation of M(ρ) = V .
For the sake of contradiction, suppose there exists F ∈F(G) such that Sρ∩F = ∅and, for every u ∈N(F)∩Sρ,
ρ(u) ∩F = ∅.
By its definition, a fort is nonempty, so F ̸= ∅. Let v be the first vertex in F that becomes monitored
during the calculation of M(ρ). Under our assumptions, v can only be monitored by applying rule (PR), say
through a propagation (u, v) for some u ∈N(v) ∩VP .
Since v is the first vertex in F to be monitored, it follows that u /∈F. But then u ∈N(F), and the
definition of fort ensures that u must also be adjacent to some other vertex w ∈F \ {v}. Therefore, the
propagation (u, v) imposes the precedence (w, v), but v is monitored before w, which leads to a contradiction.
To complete the proof, we demonstrate the converse implication. Let ρ fail to be a k-capacitated power
dominating function, i.e., M(ρ) ̸= V . Define F = V \ M(ρ) ̸= ∅. We first show that F is a fort.
If N(F) = ∅, then F is trivially a fort. Otherwise, let v ∈N(F). We claim that v must actually have
more than one neighbor in F; otherwise, an outgoing propagation from v could be applied to increase the
size of M(ρ), since v and all of its remaining neighbors (if any) lie in the complement of F and are therefore
already monitored. Hence, F is a fort.
Clearly, F contains some minimal fort F ′. Next, F ′ must satisfy Sρ ∩F ′ = ∅and, for every u ∈N(F ′)∩Sρ,
we must have ρ(u) ∩F ′ = ∅; otherwise, F ′ would contain a vertex that could be monitored by rule (DR), and
the size of M(ρ) could again be increased.

Computational experiments
In this section, we conduct computational experiments on 26 standard power network benchmark instances,
ranging from 200 to approximately 14.000 vertices, all extracted from the open-source tools pandapower
(Thurner et al., 2018) and matpower (Zimmerman et al., 2011). The names of the considered power networks
are shown on the x-axis of Figure 7 and usually contain the number of vertices as a substring, except for
Texas and Western, which have 2000 and 10024 vertices, respectively. For each power network, we define a
CPDS instance consisting of its underlying graph, its set of zero-injection vertices, and a capacity parameter
k. We vary k from 0 to a value k∗, which is the minimum capacity for which CPDS and PDS share the same
optimal value. Note that k∗may be lower than the maximum degree of the graph. As a result, a total of 262
instances are considered.
We use Gurobi 12.0.3 (Gurobi Optimization, LLC, 2026) to solve ILP, called through the C++20 API.
Formulations BRI-IP and JOV-IP are solved straightforwardly as black-box models, whereas user-defined
lazy-constraint callbacks are provided for FPS-IP, EFPS-IP, and FORT-IP. This approach differs from Bl¨asius
& G¨ottlicher (2024), who use Gurobi only to solve linear relaxations, while the remaining branch-and-bound
tree is handled separately. In addition, we do not incorporate their decomposition and reduction techniques.
We used a machine equipped with Ubuntu 22.04.5 LTS, 8 GB of RAM, and an Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-9700
CPU at 3.00GHz with 8 threads (all available). Gurobi is used with default parameters, except for a time
limit of 900 s per instance. Five runs (executions) are performed for each instance and model in order to
mitigate variability in computation times.
A concise summary of the computational results is reported in Table 1. Each row reports values averaged
over all instances and runs, aggregating a total of 1310 observations. The table reports, for each model, the
number of variables, the number of constraints initially in the formulation and the number of lazy-constraints
added, the percentage of runs that reach optimality, referred to as optimality rate, the execution time
(assigning 900 s to runs that hit the time limit), the cumulative time spent in lazy-constraint separation, and
the final relative gap (averaged over runs that reached the time limit, using the number of vertices as an upper
bound when no feasible solution was found within the time limit). Models EFPS-IP-InP, EFPS-IP-OutP,
and EFPS-IP-InP-OutP incorporate into EFPS-IP the incoming and outgoing propagation constraints (InP)
and (OutP) from Section 4.1, individually and jointly, while EFPS-IP-OutP-Init additionally includes the
initialization of EFPS constraints for 2-cycles described in Section 4.3. The results reported in each row will
be discussed incrementally in detail as each experiment is introduced.

EFPS-IP: model tightening and initial constraints
The second experiment focuses on evaluating the impact of the potential improvements for EFPS-IP discussed
throughout this work: specifically, the incorporation of valid inequalities, redundancy-breaking constraints,
and the initialization of the lazy-constraint generation framework.

and jointly. From Table 1, incorporating InP contraints appears to be counterproductive, as both the
optimality rate and the execution time deteriorate. In contrast, the results obtained with OutP constraints
stand out. Compared with EFPS-IP, the number of runs that reach optimality increases from 1191 (90.9 %)

constraints, and to only 134 with OutP constraints. Overall, the cumulative time spent in the lazy-separation
routine is negligible, remaining below 0.17 second on average. Combining InP and OutP constraints does not
provide any additional benefit beyond that obtained with OutP alone.

with real-world applications in power network monitoring. In this variant, the common assumption that a
vertex can monitor all of its neighbors is relaxed by introducing capacities, reflecting the limited number of
channels available in PMUs due to manufacturing decisions.
We introduce the novel concept of forbidden propagation sets (FPSs), which are sets of propagations that
cannot be applied together because cyclic temporal precedences are created. These structures are the core of
this work and give rise to new ILP formulations. Our formulations share some of the standard variables of
infection-based models, but do not rely on any big-M constraints. However, as in fort-based models, the

Based on these results, we conducted an additional computational experiment to evaluate the performance
of BRI-IP when incorporating OutP constraints. Although these constraints lead to some performance
improvement, the gains are rather limited, since our approach still achieves an average speedup of 1.36x in
execution time and outperforms it on 92.7 % of the instances.
We believe that the FPS-based approach can be further improved through a deeper structural analysis
aimed at identifying valid inequalities that strengthen the models. We also experimented with the use of
EFPS constraints to cut fractional solutions. The preliminary results were encouraging, but this approach
requires further investigation to become practically effective.
