ABSTRACT
We investigate the origin of the scatter in the supermassive black hole (BH) masses for galaxies
in the 𝐿★regime, using the ARTEMIS and EAGLE simulations. By classifying galaxies based
on their central BH / galaxy stellar masses ratios, we follow the evolution of galaxies with
the lowest and highest such ratios (denoted LBH and HBH, respectively). We find that the
properties of these two galaxy samples are comparable at 𝑧≈2 but diverge significantly towards
lower redshifts. Galaxies with less massive BHs were able to maintain higher gas fractions
and sustained star formation during their evolution, whereas those with more massive BHs
formed stars earlier, grew BHs faster and experienced more efficient feedback and subsequent
quenching. The simulations broadly match the observed scatter in the BH masses and galaxy
morphologies in the 𝐿★regime and explain the origin of this scatter in terms of differences
between merger histories. Galaxies with more active merger histories contain more massive
BHs at present time and tend to be elliptical, while galaxies with more quiescent histories
have less massive BHs and tend to be disc-like. Mergers enhance BH growth through higher
gas accretion rates onto central regions and direct BH-BH mergers. However, these channels
operate differently: in HBH galaxies, BHs grow primarily (≈90%) by gas accretion, whereas
in LBH they grow both through gas accretion (≈60%) and BH-BH mergers (≈40%). Our
results suggest that the different BH masses in MW and M31 could be explained by differences
in merger histories.

INTRODUCTION
Supermassive black holes (SMBH; BHs, hereafter) are ubiquitous
in the Universe, inhabiting central regions of galaxies ranging many
orders of magnitude in mass, from the brightest, most massive
galaxies in clusters to dwarf galaxies (Kormendy & Richstone 1995;
Genzel et al. 2010; Kormendy & Ho 2013; Taylor et al. 2025). The
properties of BHs and their host galaxies are closely connected, as
evidenced by a variety of scaling relationships, such as between BH
masses (𝑀BH) and bulge masses (𝑀sph), total stellar masses (𝑀★), or
stellar velocity dispersions (𝜎) (Magorrian et al. 1998; Ferrarese &
Merritt 2000; Häring & Rix 2004; McConnell & Ma 2013; Heckman
& Best 2014; Reines & Volonteri 2015). The 𝑀BH −𝜎relation is
one of the tightest BH scaling relations (Shankar et al. 2025), which
may be a consequence of 𝜎being correlated with 𝑀sph, or being
sensitive to the depth of the potential well in which BHs form

(Ferrarese & Merritt 2000). These observed trends suggest a strong
degree of co-evolution between BHs and their hosts galaxies.
The scatter in these scaling relationships may also contain
information about this co-evolution. For example, in the 𝐿★regime,
the Milky Way (MW) has one of the lowest-mass central BHs, Sgr
A*, of ≈4.3 × 106 M⊙(Gravity Collaboration et al. 2023), while
M31 has a central BH of ≈1.4 × 108 M⊙(Bender et al. 2005).
This is nearly a factor of 2 difference in BH masses for comparable
host stellar masses. However, M31 has an inner spheroid that is
more massive than the MW bulge (Reitzel et al. 1998), and shows
evidence of a massive accretion event at more recent times (Ibata
et al. 2001). Therefore, central BHs may retain information about
the assembly histories of galaxies, despite the vast differences in
spatial scales on which these two operate.
The connection between the growth of central BH and galaxy
properties can be established via one (or both) of two primary chan-
nels: BH growth via smooth gas accretion or induced by galaxy
mergers. Although the former channel appears to have been domi-

effectively operates, e.g., whether they trigger AGN feedback and
what their relevance is across many scales in galaxy mass (Ellison

The MW stellar mass is around the characteristic mass 𝑀★,c ≈
5 × 1010 M⊙where significant variations in the global properties of
local galaxies are observed. For example, some studies suggest that
the 𝑀BH −𝑀sph relation could be described by a double power law,
with a change in slope at ≃𝑀sph ∼5×1010 M⊙: a remarkably steeper
slope is obtained for galaxies below this mass than for galaxies
above it (e.g., Graham & Scott 2015; Davis et al. 2019). Below this
characteristic mass, galaxies are often spirals and actively forming
stars, while above they tend to be ellipticals with little or no star
formation (Kauffmann et al. 2003; Baldry et al. 2006; Savorgnan
et al. 2016; Comparat 2022; Graham & Sahu 2023).
The strong negative AGN feedback in massive galaxies is gen-
erally considered the primary mechanism driving these changes
in galaxy properties. AGN feedback regulates the gas supply, ef-
fectively shutting down star formation and transitioning galaxies
from the blue cloud to the red sequence. Either the heating of gas
produced by the energy released by the BH or its ejection via AGN-
driven outflows can suppress star formation1 in massive galaxies
(Croton et al. 2006; Cano-Díaz et al. 2012; Bischetti et al. 2022;
Chen et al. 2022; Lammers et al. 2023). AGN feedback affects even
the chemical properties of galaxies and their circumgalactic media,
phenomena that are intrinsically coupled to the evolution of gas-
phase reservoirs and the broader star formation history (Taylor &
Kobayashi 2015; Li et al. 2024; Villar Martín et al. 2024); in par-
ticular, AGN feedback may be responsible for the flattening of the
mass–metallicity relation at 𝑀★≳1010 M⊙(De Rossi et al. 2017).
MW-mass galaxies occupy a key transition regime between
two major feedback regimes: star-formation-driven outflows in low-
mass haloes and BH-driven quenching in massive systems. Bower
et al. (2017) showed that the baryonic flows of galaxies dramatically
change when they reach the MW-mass regime, specifically that the
gas buildup at the center is blocked by stellar-driven winds in any
galaxy halo less massive than ∼1012 M⊙. The star formation in
these galaxies is regulated by a balance between outflows triggered
by supernovae (SNe) and gas infall. However, as the halo mass in-
creases, a hot corona is formed (White & Frenk 1991; Birnboim &
Dekel 2003), which stalls the SN-driven outflows; as gas collects
into the core, the black hole undergoes a phase of rapid, nonlin-
ear growth. By heating the halo gas, the AGN feedback halts the

Cosmological hydrodynamical simulations routinely incorpo-
rate AGN feedback in galaxies that match the observed properties of
galaxies (e.g., Springel et al. 2005; Sijacki et al. 2015; Schaye et al.
2015; McCarthy et al. 2017; Nelson et al. 2018; Davé et al. 2019; Ni
et al. 2022; Huško et al. 2026; Schaye et al. 2025), with continuing
progress taking place in the modelling (Alexander & Hickox 2012;
Harrison 2017; Alexander et al. 2025). These simulations tend to
cover large volumes, encompassing orders of magnitude in galaxy
mass. Nevertheless, the 𝐿★regime has been extensively explored,
given its relevance to both SN feedback and AGN feedback. The
scatter in BH masses and the correlations between BH growth and
galaxy properties in this regime have begun to be explored more
recently, particularly in terms of the correlations with the mass and
formation times of galaxy haloes, or with the corresponding binding
energy (Davies et al. 2019, 2022, 2024; Roberts et al. 2026).
In this study, we focus on the origin of the scatter in the central
black hole mass – galaxy stellar mass relation in the 𝐿★regime. For
this, we use ARTEMIS, a suite of zoomed-in, cosmological simula-
tions of 42 MW-mass galaxies (Font et al. 2020), and samples of
MW-mass galaxies extracted from a series of runs from the EAGLE
project (Schaye et al. 2015; Crain et al. 2015). We therefore as-
semble a large sample of MW-mass galaxies with diverse merger
histories that have formed under different assumptions of SN and
AGN feedback. The ARTEMIS simulations successfully reproduce
the main observed properties of MW analogues and their satellites
(Font et al. 2021, 2022; Grimozzi et al. 2024, 2025), while EAGLE
simulations match a wide range of observables for galaxies on both
small and large scales (Schaye et al. 2015). The physical prescrip-
tions in these two simulations are very similar, with differences
that are complementary in nature. The EAGLE simulations model
larger cosmological volumes and so contain many more galaxies
in the MW-mass range. They also include runs with variations of
SN and AGN feedback, which will also be analysed here. ARTEMIS
has higher resolution and can better resolve smaller merger events.
Therefore, a comparison between these two simulations allows us
to examine in more detail the interplay of physical processes and
mergers in generating the scatter in BH masses at MW-mass scale.
This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we summarize
the main characteristics of the ARTEMIS and EAGLE simulations. In
Section 3, we analyze correlations between current BH masses and
key host galaxy properties (§ 3.1), and study the evolutionary paths
of galaxy populations at both ends of the scatter in BH masses
(§ 3.2) or in stellar spins, which are proxies for morphology (§ 3.3).
We also investigate whether the trends obtained from simulations
can be explained by differences in the merger histories, by focusing
on two galaxies that are representative of the two BH-mass popu-
lations (§ 3.4). We discuss our results in Section 4 and present our
conclusions in Section 5.

2.1
Subgrid models
Physical processes that cannot be resolved in the simulations are
implemented via subgrid models. ARTEMIS apply the same sub-
grid prescriptions developed for the EAGLE project and described
in Crain et al. (2015) and Schaye et al. (2015), including metal-
dependent radiative cooling in the presence of a photo-ionizing UV
background, star formation, stellar and chemical evolution, forma-
tion of BHs, stellar feedback from SN and stellar winds, as well as
AGN feedback.
Star formation is modeled with the prescription of Schaye
& Dalla Vecchia (2008), where gas with a density above 𝑛𝐻=
10−1 cm−3 (the critical density for onset of the thermo-gravitational
instability in hydrogen gas) can form stars. An effective equation of
state for the cold gas is also imposed in the subgrid physics. SNe
inject energy into the ISM using the kinetic feedback prescription of
Dalla Vecchia & Schaye (2008). The chemical evolution is tracked
independently for 11 elements (H, He, C, N, O, Ne, Mg, Si, Fe, S
and Ca), whose abundances depend on the nucleosynthetic yields
of AGB stars, stellar winds and core collapse SNe (Wiersma et al.

The black hole growth model is based on the Springel (2005)
formalism, with subsequent refinements from Booth & Schaye
(2009) and Rosas-Guevara et al. (2015). Black holes are seeded
with a mass of 105 M⊙ℎ−1 in haloes with a total mass above
1010 M⊙ℎ−1, by replacing the densest gas particle with a collision-
less black hole particle. Black holes can then grow by accretion of
gas or by merging with other black holes. The gas accretion rate de-
termines the rate at which energy is thermally injected into the ISM.
A fraction of the rest-mass energy of the gas is released, powering
the AGN feedback. Black holes accumulate energy at a rate that
is proportional to their gas-mass accretion rate, thus increasing the
feedback energy reservoir. Once a certain threshold is reached, the
AGN feedback operates by stochastically heating the neighbouring
particles. This threshold is equal to the amount of energy required to
increase the temperature of a gas particle by Δ𝑇AGN. When galaxies
merge, their central BHs also merge, provided that they meet certain
distance and velocity criteria.

the subgrid parameters were calibrated to improve the matches to
the observed 𝑧≈0 galaxy stellar mass function, the stellar mass–
size relation, and the normalization of the 𝑀BH −𝑀★relation.

2.2
Galaxy catalogues
All parameters used in this work come from the public EAGLE
database (McAlpine et al. 2016) and a similar database constructed
from the ARTEMIS simulations. These are physical parameters re-
lated to the BHs, such as masses or mass accretion rates; or param-

or dark matter masses, star formation rates, galaxy sizes, etc. The
identification of bound haloes and subhaloes was performed with
Friends-of-Friends (FoF) (Davis et al. 1985) and subfind algo-

were also used for the identification of galaxy progenitors and the
construction of merger trees of MW-mass galaxies.

specific stellar angular momentum, 𝜆★, defined as the stellar spin
per unit mass, as this is common to all databases and ensures a

respective parent populations. HBH galaxies tend to accrete more
mass via mergers than LBH systems, and also experience more
minor mergers along their whole formation histories. The richer
merger histories of HBH galaxies lead to an increase in their 𝑀BH via
BH-BH mergers, but more importantly, such interactions dissipate
the angular momentum of baryons, facilitating the growth of BHs
via gas accretion and preventing the formation of a stable disc.
4
DISCUSSION
4.1
Comparison with previous studies
Our study has shown that the scatter in the MBH −M★relation is
associated with intrinsic galaxy processes, largely driven by merg-
ers. This is reflected in the distributions of galaxy properties, such
as gas fractions, specific star formation rates, galaxy sizes, and stel-
lar spins (morphology), which are correlated with the central BH
mass. We have shown that these properties are comparable at 𝑧≈2,
which implies that the scatter in the MBH−M★relation of MW-mass
progenitors should be much smaller at high redshift. From 𝑧≈2
onward, these galaxies exhibit clear differences in their evolution-
ary paths. The two samples mainly analysed here, chosen based on
their current 𝑓BH (≈BH mass), exhibit strongly divergent paths until
𝑧≈0 in terms of their morphological properties, gas fractions and
star formation/quenching state. These results are not unexpected, as
many other studies, using both simulations and observations, have
found that black hole mass is a strong predictor of galaxy proper-
ties, including their quenching (e.g., Piotrowska et al. 2022; Bluck
et al. 2023; Goubert et al. 2024; Lim et al. 2025). The approach in
our study was to assemble a statistically representative sample of
MW-mass galaxies to investigate the inter-connection between the
growth of BHs and host properties.
A similar approach was taken recently by Roberts et al. (2026),
who used EAGLE to show that the scatter in the 𝑀BH −𝑀200 relation
correlates with morphology, specifically with the kinematical pa-
rameter 𝜅c𝑜. They also found that this scatter correlates even more
strongly with the halo binding energy, 𝐸bind, and that mergers (traced
by the global accreted fraction in these galaxies) are important in
determining the morphological evolution of the main galaxies. The
key role of the binding energy in the scatter of 𝑀BH at fixed halo
mass was also highlighted by Davies et al. (2019), who linked it to
the ability of 𝐿★haloes to expel gas through AGN feedback. The
role of mergers in the scatter of the 𝑀BH −𝑀200 relation in the 𝐿★
regime was also discussed by Davies et al. (2024) (see also Davies
et al. 2022) who used controlled simulations of a MW-mass galaxy.
Our results suggest that MW-mass galaxies may be affected by
multiple mergers and subsequent SN / AGN feedback during their
lifetime, and that it is the cumulative effect of these processes that
determines their evolutionary paths. The interplay between these
processes is, of course, complex and may not be easily disentangled.
At this galaxy mass scale, perhaps SN feedback is still dominant (see
Appendix A), although AGNs could become important for some
galaxies, particularly for those with more massive black holes.
Other cosmological simulations indicate that AGN feedback
can affect the morphological properties of galaxies, including those
of MW-mass (Torrey et al. 2020; Getachew-Woreta et al. 2022;
Irodotou et al. 2022; Getachew-Woreta et al. 2025; Sivasankaran
et al. 2025). For example, using the Horizon-AGN simulation,
Dubois et al. (2016) found that AGN feedback can lead to more
spherical galaxy shapes, including around the MW-mass range (al-
though MW is near the resolution limit for these simulations).
Our analysis also indicates that BH-BH mergers typically con-
tribute more to the BH mass in LBH galaxies in comparison to
those in HBH systems. This result agrees with that of Bravo et al.
(2025), who found that, at fixed galaxy stellar mass, BH growth in
galaxies with less massive BHs is merger driven, while in galaxies
with more massive BHs it occurs mainly via gas accretion.
4.2
Implications for MW and M31
Based on our classification scheme, MW could be identified as an
LBH galaxy, whereas M31 largely falls into the HBH category. Our
results indicate that this distinction is physically motivated, arising
primarily from differences in their merger histories. The MW has
experienced a major merger, Gaia-Enceladus/Sausage (GES), about
8−9 Gyr ago (Belokurov et al. 2018; Helmi et al. 2018), after which
it had an almost quiescent history (e.g., Wyse 2001; Ruchti et al.
2015). In contrast, M31 probably had a more active merger history,
as demonstrated by its thicker and more disturbed stellar disc, the
more prominent inner spheroid, and its more numerous satellites
and tidal streams (e.g., McConnachie et al. 2009, 2018). The last
accretion and disruption of a massive satellite galaxy occurred ≈
2 −3 Gyr ago (D’Souza & Bell 2018). This also appears to have
triggered a burst of star formation (Williams et al. 2015; D’Souza
& Bell 2018). Similar differences were noted between the HBH and
LBH samples: HBH galaxies have more active merger histories and
less rotational support as a result than LBH systems.
Although the simulations do not include exact matches of MW
and M31, they suggest that the differences in the BH masses of
these two systems could be explained by differences in the merger
histories. Taking the example of G25, a disc galaxy with low BH
mass like the MW, its merger history was fairly quiescent, including

could have experienced some AGN activity in the past. Although
its BH appears to be currently dormant, there are some indications
that brief AGN episodes occurred recently (e.g., Zhang et al. 2019).

CONCLUSIONS
In this study, we examined the scatter in the 𝑀BH masses at (nearly)
fixed 𝑀★mass, using samples of MW-mass galaxies from the
ARTEMIS and EAGLE simulations. Our goal was to determine the
origin of this scatter. For this, we classified the simulated galaxies
according to the 𝑓BH = 𝑀BH/𝑀★ratio, focusing on samples with
the 25% lowest and highest 𝑓BH, respectively. We found significant
correlations between the central BH masses and various galaxy
properties at 𝑧= 0, such as the galaxy stellar spin, mean stellar age,
and star-forming gas fraction, which motivated us to study the co-
evolution of BH mass growth and galaxy properties over time. This
has revealed the significant role of mergers and associated feedback
in determining the outcomes in terms of BH growth and galaxy
properties. We then investigated in more detail the merger histories
of two representative galaxies from the low- and high BH-mass cat-
egories and discussed the implications of this study for the growth
of BHs in MW and M31. We summarize in the following the main
conclusions:
• In both simulations, the BH mass at fixed 𝑀★correlates with
galaxy morphology, in qualitative agreement with the observed scat-
ter (e.g., Greene et al. 2020) (Fig. 2). Galaxies with less massive
BHs tend to have disc-like morphologies (higher stellar spin), and
those with more massive BHs tend to be elliptical (low stellar spin).
• BH masses correlate with other host galaxy properties (Fig. 3),
such as gas fractions and average stellar ages. Galaxies with more
massive BHs tend to form their stars earlier, consistent with an early
formation time of their halo. They also tend to have lower fractions
of star-forming gas, due to accumulated effects of feedback.
• We examined the co-evolution of BH growth and key galaxy
properties in the galaxy populations with lowest and highest 𝑓BH
ratios (which broadly correlate with BH masses in our narrow M★
range). We found that the galaxy properties in the two samples are
similar at 𝑧≈2 but diverge soon afterwards. Differences of about
an order of magnitude in BH masses (or 𝑓BH) at 𝑧= 0 result in
differences of at least a factor of 2 in sSFRs, cold and hot gas
fractions, or galaxy sizes (Fig. 4). For cases when BHs grow more
efficiently (HBH galaxies), the gas fractions undergo a steep decline,
leading to very efficient quenching by 𝑧≈0. Galaxies with less
massive BHs continue to form stars until present day. In contrast,
chemical abundances are similar for all galaxies, regardless of their
final BH mass. This is because metallicities tend to track closely the
growth in stellar mass which occurs early (Fig. 4), before most of the
BH growth takes place. These results are consistent with chemical
enrichment being determined mostly by SN feedback, which drives
the galaxy stellar mass – metallicity relation.
• By selecting galaxies according to their present-day stellar
spins, used as proxies for morphologies, we also found divergent
evolutions from 𝑧≈2. The evolution of galaxies broadly represent-
ing early/late types resembles that of galaxies with high/low BH-
masses (Fig. 5). However, the galaxy properties are not as strongly
separated as in the case of selection based on BH-mass (Fig. 4).
These results confirm that the morphological properties are closely
related to the evolution of their BHs, but also suggest that BH-
masses are stronger predictors of the gaseous-phase properties in
galaxies than morphologies.
• We investigated how galaxies with high and low BH masses
accrete mass, during or after mergers, in their dark matter haloes,
stellar and baryonic (stars + gas) components, and central BHs. We
found that, in terms of absolute values, galaxies with more massive
BHs tend to accrete more mass in each of these components than
those with lower BH masses (Fig. 6, upper panels). This is likely
due to their overall higher virial masses. When normalizing to the
maximum mass in each component (to account for the differences in
masses), we could determine the intrinsic contribution of mergers
to each category (Fig. 6, bottom panels). We thus found that mergers
contribute more to the stellar masses of host galaxies with massive
BHs (≈30%) than of galaxies with lower BH-masses (≈10%).
Mergers also tend to contribute more DM to galaxies that end up
having more massive BHs, although these fractions are typically
small (≲20%), most of DM halo formation having taken place at
higher redshifts (𝑧> 2).
• A reversed trend is seen in terms of the mass accreted directed
into BHs via BH-BH mergers (Fig. 6, bottom row, third panel). In
galaxies with less massive BHs, this channel is more important,
contributing ≈40% to the present-day mass, while in galaxies
with more massive BHs the contribution is ≲10%. This implies
that in HBH galaxies gas accretion is the primary channel for BH
growth (contributing ≈90% of the final BH mass), whereas in LBH
galaxies the two channels are more even (≈60% gas accretion,
≈40% BH-BH mergers).
• Overall, galaxies with more massive BHs tend to have more
active merger histories, as shown by various indicators (higher ac-
creted mass in total, higher fractional contributions to the final DM
and stellar mass, etc). More frequent/more massive mergers result
in a diminished rotational support by generating instabilities in the
gas motions that dissipate the angular momentum and enable gas to
migrate towards the central BH. Although mergers tend to subside
after 𝑧≈0.5 (see Fig. 6), HBH galaxies still cannot form discs, due
to their gas being depleted and low levels of star formation.
• We investigated further the satellite populations of the LBH and
HBH galaxies, by tracking their number across different redshifts.
We found that galaxies with massive BHs tend to have more satellites
at any given time, including more massive ones that host their own
BHs (Fig. 7). High mass satellites tend to accrete late (𝑧≲1),
by which time their BHs also had time to grow. The differences
between number of satellites in the two galaxy samples are quite
small in terms of the mass they can contribute through direct BH-
BH mergers. However these differences are a determining factor in
terms of the gas content these satellites could provide to the host
and the gravitational instabilities they can generate.

• We performed a more focused analysis of the merger histories

of angular momentum of the gas, enhancing the gas accretion rate
onto BH by factors of a few than in the more quiescent G25 (Fig. 9).
Our study has shown how the interplay between mergers and
internal galaxy processes, such as star formation and SN/ AGN
feedback (see Appendix A), can shape the evolution of 𝐿★galaxies
and ultimately determine the growth rate of central BHs and whether
a system develops into a star-forming disc galaxy or evolves into
a quenched spheroidal system. It has also highlighted how the two
main channels for the formation of central BHs in 𝐿★galaxies, gas
accretion and BH-BH mergers, operate differently depending on the
type of merger histories and how mergers are related to the scatter in
BH masses and galaxy properties. MW and M31, as representative
cases of observed scatter in the BH/galaxy properties in the 𝐿★
regime, could also have formed their central BHs through different
contributions from these channels. This scenario is plausible given
the known differences in the merger histories of these two galaxies.
