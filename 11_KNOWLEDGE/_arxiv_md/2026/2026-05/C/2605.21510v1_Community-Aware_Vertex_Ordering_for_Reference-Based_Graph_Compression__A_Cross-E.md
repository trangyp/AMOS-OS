Community-Aware Vertex Ordering for Reference-Based Graph Compression: A
Cross-Encoder Empirical Study
JIMMY DUBUISSON, Vantino, Switzerland
Reference-based graph compression encodes each vertex’s neighbor list relative to a recent vertex, exploiting locality and similarity to
reduce storage costs for large directed graphs. The dominant tool, WebGraph’s BVGraph, fixes a single encoding pipeline and relies
on a separately chosen vertex ordering—typically URL-lexicographic or Layered Label Propagation (llp). The interaction between
ordering and encoder is rarely measured.
We propose a two-stage Leiden+llp vertex ordering—global llp to seed labels, Leiden community detection over the resulting
locality structure, then per-cluster llp on each induced subgraph—and study how it interacts with reference-based compression. On
graphs whose initial vertex order is poor, reordering with Leiden+llp saves 0.3 to 5.4 bits per edge on every dataset and encoder
we measured. The size of that gain is largely insensitive to which reference-based encoder is run afterward: on four of five weakly
ordered datasets, four independently parameterised encoders agree on the Leiden+llp-vs-plain-llp gain within roughly ±0.04 bpe.
The exception is eat: our three larger-window encoders still agree within ±0.007 bpe, but BVGraph at 𝑤=7 shows almost no residual
gain—a window-size effect rather than a refutation of the pattern. On URL-ordered web crawls, where the distributed ordering already
encodes locality, our adaptive encoders still benefit from reordering, but encoders that depend on the URL-induced residual structure
(BV-HC, CG at 𝐾>1) are mildly hurt by it.
To quantify how much encoder choice matters once ordering is fixed, we contribute three reference-based encoders—BG, CS,
and CG—that perform per-vertex cost-optimal selection from up to 28 candidate decompositions. Each encoder is run under its own
best-tested ordering: Leiden+llp on weakly ordered graphs and on web crawls for the adaptive variants, original LAW order for CG at
𝐾=2 on web crawls. Under those configurations, the best of the three improves over BVGraph high-compression (BV-HC) by 2–9%
on every dataset tested, with the encoder-level gain consistently smaller than the ordering-level gain on weakly ordered datasets.
The encoder framework also yields a self-delimiting bitstream that supports low-overhead random access, presented as a practical
implementation choice rather than a primary contribution.
All algorithms, the ordering pipeline, generators, and benchmarking scripts are released as the Adjacently Julia library, with
deterministic reproduction scripts for every reported number.

Introduction
1.1
Motivation
Large directed graphs—web crawls, social networks, citation indices, knowledge graphs—are routinely too large to
store as uncompressed adjacency lists yet must remain accessible to in-memory analytics. The dominant compressed
representation is the BVGraph framework introduced by Boldi and Vigna [5], which exploits two structural properties
of web-style graphs: locality, the tendency of edges to connect vertices placed nearby in the chosen vertex order, and
similarity, the tendency of nearby vertices to share neighbors. BVGraph encodes each vertex’s sorted successor list
with a fixed pipeline of reference copying, interval detection, and residual gap encoding under 𝜁codes.
Both properties are properties of the ordering, not of the graph itself. A web crawl in URL-lexicographic order is highly
local because URLs cluster by host; the same crawl under a random permutation compresses far worse. Recognizing

The Role of Vertex Ordering
llp alone, however, leaves substantial structure on the table. Many real-world graphs exhibit strong community structure:
vertices form groups whose edges concentrate within the group. llp’s flat label-propagation objective tends to interleave
vertices from distinct communities, breaking the within-community contiguity that reference-based encoders rely on.
Modern community detection algorithms—most notably Leiden [15]—are explicitly designed to recover such groupings.
We study a simple two-stage pipeline that combines the two: global llp produces an initial locality-respecting
embedding, Leiden then partitions the graph using that embedding as input, and a final round of llp runs within each
Leiden cluster on the induced subgraph. The cluster orderings are concatenated to form the final permutation. The
encoded graph itself is unaltered; only the vertex labelling changes.
The empirical effect is substantial. Across seven real-world datasets—web crawls, citation, product co-purchase, and
word-association networks—reordering with Leiden+llp saves bits-per-edge on every dataset and every encoder we
measured. On graphs delivered in arbitrary or vertex-ID order (Amazon co-purchase, ArXiv citations, Wikipedia link
graph), reordering saves 1–6 bpe, frequently more than the gap between competing encoders, more than tuning the
reference window, and more than the gap between BVGraph default and BVGraph-HC. On URL-ordered web crawls the
picture is more nuanced. The adaptive encoders we contribute still benefit from reordering, but encoders that depend
on the residual structure URL ordering creates—BVGraph at small windows, BV-HC, and CG at 𝐾>1—are mildly hurt
by it and are best left in their original order.
A second observation, sharper than the first, is that the size of the gain is largely insensitive to which reference-
based encoder is run afterward. On four of five weakly ordered datasets we tested, four independently parameterised
encoders agreed on the Leiden+llp-vs-plain-llp gain within roughly ±0.04 bpe: Web-Google (0.34–0.40 bpe, ±0.04),
Arxiv-HEP-PH (0.22–0.25 bpe, ±0.015), Amazon-0601 (0.50–0.56 bpe, ±0.030), and enwiki-2013 (0.33–0.35 bpe, ±0.008).

shows almost no residual gain (−0.009 bpe); a plausible explanation is that BV’s small window cannot pick up the
fine-grained community contiguity Leiden+llp creates beyond what plain llp already provides. A practical reading is
that once a graph is poorly ordered, the extra gain from Leiden+llp depends mainly on the encoder having enough
window to see the community structure—not on the encoder’s particular decomposition strategy.

1.5
Scope and Non-Goals
This paper is an empirical algorithm-engineering study. We do not claim a new fundamental algorithmic technique:
Leiden, llp, and reference-based encoding are each well-studied. The contribution is the systematic measurement of
how they compose, evidence on five datasets that the ordering effect transfers across four reference-based encoders
(BVGraph default plus our three), and a public reference implementation that lets practitioners reproduce the results on
their own graphs. We do not address asymptotic bounds, lower-bound arguments, or theoretical analysis of why the
pipeline works; the relationship between graph community structure and compressibility is a natural follow-up but is
left to future work.
1.6
Paper Organization

Leiden+llp ordering pipeline—the central contribution—together with the cross-encoder ablation. Section 5 describes
the three encoders BG, CS, and CG. Section 6 presents the full experimental evaluation. Section 7 discusses findings,

Related Work
2.1
Vertex Ordering for Graph Compression
The role of vertex ordering in graph compression has been recognized since the earliest reference-based encoders. Boldi
and Vigna’s original BVGraph paper [5] relied on URL-lexicographic ordering for web crawls and reported substantial
degradation under random permutations, framing ordering as a precondition rather than a contribution. Subsequent
work has treated the ordering question more directly.
Apostolico and Drovandi [1] proposed BFS-based ordering, observing that traversal-derived permutations naturally
place connected vertices at nearby IDs and improve reference quality on social and citation graphs that lack a domain-
specific lexicographic order. Chierichetti et al. [10] explored shingle-based orderings that group vertices by Jaccard-
similar neighborhoods.
The current canonical preprocessing step is Layered Label Propagation (llp) [3], which optimizes a multi-resolution
label-propagation objective and consistently improves BVGraph compression on graphs that lack natural lexicographic
locality. llp has been the de-facto ordering for non-web datasets in the LAW corpus for over a decade. Variants and
successors include recursive graph bisection (RGB) [11], which directly minimizes log-gap encoding cost via a divide-
and-conquer min-bisection objective and reports strong results on inverted-index compression, and gap-minimizing
greedy methods that locally optimize neighbor overlap with the previous vertex.
The community-detection literature is largely separate. Leiden [15], the standard high-quality alternative to Lou-
vain [2], is widely used for analytics on community-structured graphs but has not been systematically evaluated as
a preprocessing step for compression. The combination we study—Leiden as a partition step followed by per-cluster
llp—has appeared anecdotally in benchmarking blog posts but, to the best of our knowledge, has not been the subject
of a published systematic study, and its cross-encoder transfer property has not been established. Our contribution is to
measure that composition end to end and to show that the resulting gain is largely a property of the labelling, not of
the encoder run afterward.

2.2
Reference-Based Graph Compression
BVGraph [5] introduced the reference-copy / interval / residual decomposition that defines the family. The LAW
repository distributes graphs in two variants: a standard format (𝑤=7, 𝑚=3) supporting efficient random access, and a
highly compressed (BV-HC) format (𝑤=16, 𝑚=∞) that achieves better compression at the cost of long reference chains
that make per-vertex random access impractical. Refinements include alternative integer codes [6], parallel encoding [4],
and the Elias-Fano-based representations [16] used for offset tables.
Outside the BVGraph family, 𝑘2-trees [7, 8] represent the adjacency matrix as a compact bit-tree, achieving 1.2–3.2 bpe
on large web graphs (3.11 bpe on CNR-2000) while intrinsically supporting both successor and predecessor queries
in a single structure. A forward-only BVGraph would need to store both the graph and its transpose to match this
bidirectional functionality, so the comparison is not direct.
2.3
Compression for Graph Analytics
A separate research line targets compression that supports efficient parallel analytics rather than minimizing bits per
edge. CompressGraph [9] uses rule-based compression with 4.6× ratio and fast parallel decompression. Laconic [17]
achieves 7.3× ratio with 11.7× faster processing than BVGraph. These systems optimize for query throughput, not
storage minimization, and are complementary to the present work; the ordering effects we report are likely to influence
them as well, but a quantitative study would require their own benchmarking infrastructure and is outside this paper’s
scope.

The pipeline structure is fixed and sequential—each stage processes the output of the previous one—but individual
stages are conditional: reference copying may find no suitable reference, interval detection may find no runs, and
residuals encode only what remains. The encoder applies a structured heuristic decomposition: it does not explore
alternative strategies such as applying intervals before reference copying, or adapting the minimum interval length per
vertex.

Stage 2 — Reference copying: offset 500 −498 = 2 (gamma: 3 bits). Walking 𝑁(498): positions 0–3 are copied (12, 13, 15, 20), position 4
skipped (33), positions 5–7 copied (45, 46, 47), position 8 skipped (88). Copy blocks: [4, 1, 3, 1], each gamma-coded. Seven of nine

Stage 4 — Residuals: gap-encode {14, 100} as [14, 86] using zeta-3 codes.
Observation: vertex 14 is part of the run {12, 13, 14, 15} that could form a 4-element interval, but the pipeline applies reference

list with intervals {12−15} + {45−47}, potentially saving bits — this is the kind of alternative that BVGraph’s fixed pipeline does

Community-Aware Vertex Ordering
This section presents the central contribution of the paper: a two-stage Leiden+llp vertex ordering pipeline whose
effect on compression is, on graphs with weak initial locality, larger than the gap between competing reference-based
encoders. We first review the role of vertex ordering in reference-based compression (Section 4.1), describe the pipeline
(Section 4.2), and then present the cross-encoder ablation that establishes the central empirical claim (Section 4.3).
Section 4.4 examines the mechanism, including the regimes where reordering does not help, and Section 4.5 gives
practical guidance.
4.1
The Role of Vertex Ordering
A reference-based encoder represents each vertex’s sorted successor list relative to a recently encoded vertex’s list. The
compressed cost depends on two ordering-dependent properties:
• Reference quality. The decoder benefits when a recent vertex shares many successors with the current vertex,
allowing a copy list to encode a large portion of the neighborhood with a single short reference plus a bitmask.
Reference quality is high when vertices that are close in ID order share neighbors.
• Residual locality. After copying and interval extraction, what remains is encoded as a sorted residual list
under gap codes. Encoding cost is minimized when those residual gaps are small, which holds when the vertex’s
remaining successors have IDs close to the vertex itself.
Both properties are properties of the permutation of vertex labels, not of the graph’s edge set. A web crawl in
URL-lexicographic order achieves both because URLs cluster by host and pages within a host link densely; under a
random permutation, the same graph compresses far worse with the same encoder. The llp algorithm of Boldi et
al. [3] is the standard tool for restoring locality on graphs with no natural lexicographic order, and improves BVGraph
compression substantially on social and citation graphs.
llp optimizes a single global label-propagation objective. For graphs that are essentially flat in their connectivity
(bipartite link graphs, web crawls), this works well. For graphs with strong community structure—vertices forming
groups with dense within-group edges and sparse between-group edges—the global objective tends to interleave vertices
from distinct communities at the boundaries, breaking the within-community contiguity that reference copying depends

4.2
The Leiden+llp Pipeline
The pipeline composes the two methods. Given a directed graph 𝐺= (𝑉, 𝐸), the ordering is computed as follows:
(1) Symmetrize and seed with global llp. Compute the symmetrized graph 𝐺sym and run global llp for a small
number of passes to produce an initial vertex permutation 𝜎0 : 𝑉→{1, . . . , |𝑉|}. This step injects locality
information that Leiden’s modularity objective can exploit.
(2) Leiden community detection. Run Leiden on 𝐺sym using 𝜎0 as the initial label assignment. The output is a
partition of 𝑉into clusters 𝐶1, . . . ,𝐶𝑘, each with strong internal edge density.
(3) Per-cluster llp on induced subgraphs. For each cluster 𝐶𝑖with |𝐶𝑖| > 2, extract the induced symmetrized
subgraph 𝐺sym[𝐶𝑖] and run llp on it for 5 passes, producing a within-cluster ordering 𝜎𝑖. Clusters of size at
most 2 retain their natural order.
(4) Concatenate. Order clusters by decreasing size and concatenate the per-cluster orderings to obtain the final
permutation 𝜎.
The relabelled graph is encoded with the chosen reference-based encoder under 𝜎; the encoded graph itself is
unchanged. The pipeline is deterministic given the seed used inside llp’s passes, and its output is independent of the
downstream encoder. Total runtime is dominated by the per-cluster llp step: on the largest dataset in our benchmark
(enwiki-2013, 4.2M vertices, 101M edges), the full pipeline completes in roughly 18 minutes on a single workstation,
comparable to global llp alone and a small fraction of total encoding time.
The conceptual point is that running llp within a Leiden cluster lets the local label-propagation objective operate
on a small, homogeneous induced subgraph where it converges to a much tighter locality solution than it can on the
global graph. The clusters themselves are typically fine-grained: on CNR-2000 we observe ∼34,000 clusters with median
size 9, on Amazon-0601 ∼30,000 clusters with median size 11.
4.3
Cross-Encoder Ablation
Two observations drive the rest of this section. First, on graphs delivered in an arbitrary or vertex-ID order, Leiden+llp
improves compression over both the original ordering and plain llp for every encoder we measured. Second, where we
have the full three-way ablation (Original / plain llp / Leiden+llp), the plain-llp-to-Leiden+llp step moves all four
reference-based encoders we benchmarked by similar amounts—a sign that the extra gain reflects something about
the labels we produce, not how a particular encoder consumes them. The ablation is now complete across five weakly
ordered datasets and four encoders—BVGraph default plus the three we contribute—giving twenty (encoder, dataset)
pairs with both endpoints measured.

4.4
Why It Works, and Why It Sometimes Doesn’t
Section 4.1 argued that reference-based compression depends on two distinct ordering-dependent properties: reference
quality (overlap among nearby vertices’ neighbor lists) and residual locality (smallness of post-copy gaps). The two are
not the same, and an ordering can improve one at the expense of the other.
Concretely, we examined the distribution of post-copy residual gaps on CNR-2000 under the LAW llp ordering versus
Leiden+llp. Under LAW llp, 66% of residual gaps are exactly 1 (consecutive successors after copying); under Leiden+llp,
this drops to 46%. The overall successor-gap entropy improves under Leiden+llp (from 2.88 to 2.54 bits), reflecting
better reference quality, but the loss of consecutive-gap structure costs encoders that have specifically optimized for it.
This is the mechanism behind the dagger-marked rows in Table 4: BV-HC’s encoding pipeline is optimized for the
residual-1 structure of URL-ordered web graphs, and disrupting that structure hurts more than the improved reference
quality helps. CG 𝐾=2 has the same property—multi-cluster encoding implicitly assumes a hierarchical residual layout.
BG and CS, which use different residual encoders less specifically tuned to consecutive-gap structure, do not show this
regression.
The diagnostic is straightforward: graphs delivered in a domain-specific lexicographic order with strong inherent
locality (URL-ordered web crawls being the prime example) are likely already in a near-optimal state for encoders like
BV-HC, and reordering is a small win at best. Graphs delivered in arbitrary or vertex-ID order, which is the case for
nearly all SNAP-style and citation datasets, are far from optimal and benefit substantially from Leiden+llp.

5
Reference-Based Encoders
The Leiden+llp ordering of Section 4 delivers the locality structure on which any reference-based encoder builds. This
section describes three encoders—BG, CS, and CG—that we contribute to extract value from that locality. They are not
the headline contribution; the ordering is. They are included for two reasons. First, to quantify how much encoder choice
matters once ordering is fixed: the cross-encoder rows of Table 4 are well-defined only against multiple independently
developed encoders. Second, to show that gains over BV-HC remain available after the ordering improvement, by
introducing per-vertex adaptive encoding choices that BVGraph’s fixed pipeline does not explore.
5.1
Per-Vertex Encoding Selection
The core design idea shared by all three encoders is per-vertex cost-optimal encoding selection. BVGraph processes
each vertex through a fixed sequential pipeline (reference copying →interval encoding →residual encoding) where
individual stages may produce no output but the overall decomposition strategy is not adapted per vertex. BVGraph
does not, for example, consider whether skipping reference copying and applying intervals to the full list might yield
fewer total bits, nor does it adapt the minimum interval length per vertex. Our encoders enumerate up to 28 candidate
decompositions per vertex and select the one of minimum total bit cost including header overhead—a form of local rate
optimization.
The action space is the cross product of a reference mode—none (encode the full neighbor list from scratch), ref (copy
shared neighbors from a reference vertex within a sliding window of size 𝑤, then encode the residual), or multi_ref
(copy from two reference vertices)—and an encoding type for the residual: delta (gap coding), interval (run detection
with minimum interval length MIL ∈{2, 3, 4, 5}), or RLE (hybrid run-length). Empty vertices (degree 0) are a standalone
action with a 3-bit code:

5.2
BG: Baseline Greedy Encoder
BG uses a merged variable-length code (VLC) header that encodes the encoding action and empty-vertex flag in a single
prefix code of 1–11 bits. The most frequent action (reference + stop-terminated delta, ∼53% of vertices on CNR-2000) is
assigned a 1-bit code.
Encoding proceeds in two levels. The outer level selects one of the 28 actions (reference mode × encoding type,
or empty). When a reference mode is selected, the inner level determines how to encode the copy positions for each
candidate reference vertex, choosing the cheapest among three representations by bit cost. This inner decision does not
expand the action space—it is resolved greedily during cost evaluation of each ref/multi_ref action.
Key features:
• 3-way adaptive copy (inner level): per-reference-vertex choice among copy-blocks, raw bitmap, and comple-
ment copy-blocks, selected by minimum bit cost with a compact 1–2 bit prefix.
• STOP-terminated delta lists: neighbor values encoded with continuation bits, eliminating the explicit count
prefix. Saves ∼0.16 bpe.
• Multi-reference encoding: each vertex can copy from up to two reference vertices (top-𝐾=5 candidates by
overlap, paired with all window candidates). Saves ∼0.02 bpe.
• Low-degree reference search: minimum degree threshold lowered from 3 to 1, allowing degree-1 and degree-2
vertices to benefit from reference copying. This is the single largest optimization (−0.30 bpe on CNR-2000).

5.4
CG: Clustered Greedy Encoder
CG supports two-level encoding with per-cluster vertex indexing: Leiden community detection partitions the graph into
𝐾clusters, vertices are relabeled to contiguous local IDs within each cluster, and intra-cluster edges are encoded under
those local IDs. Local IDs in principle enable tighter reference deltas within each community, at the cost of inter-cluster
overhead.
In practice, 𝐾=1 (a single cluster containing the entire graph) is the most useful operating point when paired with
the Leiden+llp ordering of Section 4. The ordering already groups community members into contiguous ID ranges,
providing the locality that multi-cluster encoding was originally designed to manufacture. With 𝐾>1, the cluster-
membership overhead and loss of cross-cluster references outweigh the benefit of local IDs in our experiments. The
exception is graphs that are kept in their original locality-rich ordering: on CNR-2000 with the LAW llp ordering,
CG 𝐾=2 reaches 2.33 bpe, the best result on that dataset under any encoder.
Automatic selection of 𝐾is not addressed in this paper. A practical heuristic for users wishing to explore 𝐾>1 is
to cap 𝐾by the number of Leiden communities containing at least 0.5% of vertices and try a few values; we have not
validated this rule beyond our own benchmarks.
At 𝐾=1, CG’s differentiator over BG/CS is a fixed-width reference header: the reference distance is encoded with a
fixed number of bits (1 + ⌈log2 𝑤⌉) rather than a variable-length code. This is efficient when reference distances are
near-uniformly distributed across the window, which holds for non-web graphs in our benchmark. On web graphs
where most references point to the immediate predecessor, BG/CS’s variable-length codes are more efficient. CG also
performs a full-model cost search that jointly optimizes intervals, LR-split, and tight-delta parameters per vertex.
Speed optimizations include analytical cost estimation (closed-form bit-cost expressions instead of trial-encoding
into a buffer) and the two-phase candidate pruning described in Section 5.1 above. Together these yield a ∼7× speedup
over the naïve approach with negligible impact on compression quality (< 0.01 bpe).

5.7
Self-Describing File Format and Random Access
The encoders share a common output format. A 12-byte self-describing header (MGS v3.2) encodes the algorithm
identity and all parameters, so decoders require no external configuration. Any compressed file can be decompressed
and verified without knowing which encoder or parameters produced it.
Two coding modes are supported. Children mode (default) writes vertex records back-to-back as self-delimiting
STOP-terminated bitstreams with no per-vertex offset table, supporting sequential access. Index mode prepends a
two-level sampled offset table storing absolute bit positions for every 𝑘-th vertex (default 𝑘=64), enabling 𝑂(𝑘) random
access at +0.036 bpe overhead on CNR-2000 (14 KB embedded), which is roughly 22× smaller than BVGraph’s separate
.offsets file. For CG, the sampled index applies within each cluster.
We present this format as a practical implementation choice rather than a primary contribution. BVGraph already
supports random access through its .offsets sidecar; the contribution here is the engineering choice to embed the
sample table in the same self-delimiting bitstream so that no separate file is required, at a small bpe cost. All bpe results
in this paper use children mode, consistent with the standard reporting convention (BVGraph’s separate .offsets file
is similarly not counted in reported bpe).

7
Discussion
7.1
When Does Each Method Win?
No single encoder dominated across all graph families. Instead, our experiments reveal a consistent interaction between
method design and graph structure:
• On high-locality web crawls with Leiden+llp ordering (cnr-2000, in-2004, web-google), CS achieved the best
compression, benefiting from its frequency-optimized prefix codes and large window (𝑤=256).
• On community-structured and non-crawl graphs (Amazon, EAT, Arxiv, LFR), CG led, exploiting intervals,
LR-split, and per-cluster encoding.
• On structureless random graphs (Erdős–Rényi at low density), BV was most effective, as its zeta-3 encoding
suits uniform gap distributions. With tuned parameters (zeta-5, 𝑖=2, 𝑚=−1), BV also led at very high density on
synthetic web graphs.
• On high-degree graphs (enwiki-2013, avg. degree 24.1), BG and CG are essentially tied (12.16 vs. 12.18 bpe,
within 0.02), with BG slightly ahead due to its multi-reference feature. CS (12.22) is a small distance behind.
Multi-reference provides modest gains on low-degree graphs (∼0.02 bpe on CNR-2000) and becomes more
valuable at higher degree, though not by enough to make BG dominate CG on this dataset.
7.2
The Convergence Phenomenon
On Arxiv-HEP-PH with Leiden+llp, CG (7.162), BG (7.252), and CS (7.269) are within 0.11 bpe of each other. A similar
pattern holds on Amazon (0.19 bpe spread) and EAT (0.22 bpe spread).
This convergence suggests that once the ordering provides sufficient locality, encoder-level differences become
second-order. A practical reading is that improving the ordering may matter more than fine-tuning the encoder, once
locality is already strong.
7.3
Ordering as First-Class Citizen
Leiden+llp ordering substantially improved compression on graphs with poor initial ordering in our experiments
(SNAP datasets: −1.7 bpe, LFR graphs: −3.5 to −5.4 bpe), but can hurt on graphs that already have strong sequential
locality.
On CNR-2000 with the LAW-distributed llp ordering, Leiden+llp slightly improves CS (−0.13 bpe) but degrades CG
𝐾=2 (+0.04 bpe) and BV (+0.34 bpe). Section 4.4 attributes this to a residual-locality property of the LAW ordering—the

7.4
Random Access vs. Sequential Reading
All bpe results in this paper measure sequential-access file size, following the standard convention in graph compression
research. However, practical deployments often require random access to individual vertices.
BVGraph supports random access through a separate .offsets file containing per-vertex bit positions, not counted
in the reported bpe. Random access requires following reference chains: to decode vertex 𝑣’s successor list, the decoder
may need to first decode the referenced vertex, which may itself reference another vertex, and so on. The maximum
chain depth is bounded by the maxRefCount parameter (default 𝑚=3), so random access costs decoding at most 𝑚+1
vertices.
The highly compressed variant (BV-HC) achieves better compression (2.448 vs. 2.898 bpe on CNR-2000) by setting
maxRefCount to unlimited (231−1) and increasing the window from 𝑤=7 to 𝑤=16. This allows arbitrarily long reference
chains, which improves reference copying (2.49M vs. 2.20M copied arcs) and reduces residuals by 0.30 bpe. An offsets
file can be generated for HC (in 137 ms on CNR-2000), so random access is technically possible. However, the average
reference chain depth is 301 hops (avgRef=300.979), meaning decoding a single vertex requires following ∼301 back-
references on average—each involving a seek and decompression. This makes per-vertex random access impractical,
which is why LAW distributes HC files for sequential access only.
Our encoders support an optional index mode that embeds random-access metadata directly in the compressed file.
We implement a two-level sampled index: fixed-width absolute offsets are stored for every 𝑘-th vertex (default 𝑘=64), and
the data section uses the same self-delimiting children-mode encoding (STOP-terminated lists). Random access to vertex
𝑣requires seeking to the nearest sampled offset (𝑂(1) lookup), then sequentially scanning at most 𝑘self-delimiting
vertex records to reach the target.
On CNR-2000, the sampled index adds only 0.036 bpe (14 KB embedded)—22× smaller than BVGraph’s separate
.offsets file (0.809 bpe, 318 KB). CS with sampled random access achieves 2.344 bpe, which is 4.3% smaller than
BV-HC’s sequential-only format (2.448 bpe). Our single self-contained file with embedded random access is smaller
than BV’s compression-only file that still requires a separate offsets file for random access (see Table 9).
For CG, the sampled index applies at the intra-cluster level: cluster-level offsets (small, ∼𝐾entries) remain full,
while per-vertex offsets within each cluster use sampling. All three encoders (BG, CS, CG) support sampled index with
identical overhead. All bpe results in the main benchmarks use sequential children mode for fair comparison with
BVGraph’s standard reporting convention.
7.5
Limitations
• Encoding speed and parallelism. BG and CS are one to two orders of magnitude slower than BVGraph in
single-threaded mode (depending on window size), due to per-vertex evaluation of all candidate encodings.
The fast cost model narrows their gap to 2–6× with modest bpe regression. CG is significantly faster thanks to
analytical cost estimation: only 2× slower than BV at 𝑤=8 and 12× at 𝑤=64 (Figure 4). All speed results in this
paper are single-threaded for both BVGraph and our encoders. BVGraph supports multi-threaded compression

(partition-compress-concatenate, available since WebGraph 3.5.0) with 2–7× speedup, but only when loading
from its native .graph format—not from ASCIIGraph, which is what our benchmarks use. Our encoders do
not yet implement parallelism, but the architecture is amenable to it: per-vertex encoding decisions write to

• Scale. Our largest experiment is enwiki-2013 (4.2M vertices, 101M edges). Billion-scale evaluation (e.g., uk-2007,
ClueWeb) would strengthen the claims.
• Decompression speed. Sequential decompression runs at 0.28–0.33 𝜇s/edge on CNR-2000 (best of 3 runs after
JIT warmup), which is 8–10× slower than BVGraph (∼0.034 𝜇s/edge). This gap reflects our Julia implementation
(including in-memory graph construction) vs. BVGraph’s optimized Java iterator. Systematic evaluation of
random-access latency remains future work.
• Ordering cost. Leiden+llp is a preprocessing step whose cost (𝑂(𝑚) per pass, typically 5–10 passes) is amortized
over repeated compressions of the same graph but significant for one-shot use.
7.6
Practical Recommendations
The complete practical guidance is in Section 4.5 (when to apply Leiden+llp); this subsection covers the encoder-level
choices that depend on it.
(1) Default configuration. Apply Leiden+llp (the data-origin heuristic of Section 4.5 indicates when to skip it),
then CG 𝐾=1 at 𝑤=64 for the best bpe/speed tradeoff in our benchmarks (12× slower than BVGraph at this
window with 20% better compression on CNR-2000), or CS at 𝑤=64–256 for a simpler format with competitive
bpe on web crawls.
(2) Speed-sensitive workloads. Use CG at 𝑤=8 (2× slower than BV, 14% better bpe), or CS with cost_model=1
(fast mode). BG is the slowest encoder of the three at small 𝑤.
(3) High-degree graphs (avg. degree ≳20, e.g. enwiki-2013). Use BG at 𝑤=64 with multi-reference, or CG 𝐾=1 at
𝑤=64 with intervals+lr-split; in our enwiki measurement these two are within 0.02 bpe of each other and both
outperform CS.
(4) Graphs already in a strong locality-rich order (URL-lexicographic web crawls). For CG, keep the original
ordering. The choice between CG 𝐾=1 and 𝐾=2 depends on the dataset’s community structure: in our bench-
marks, on cnr-2000 (Italian research-institute hub-and-spoke topology) CG 𝐾=2 at 𝑤=64 reaches 2.33 bpe and is
the best CG configuration, while on in-2004 CG 𝐾=1 at 𝑤=8 reaches 1.75 bpe and beats 𝐾=2. As a first try, use
𝐾=1 at 𝑤=64, then test 𝐾=2 if Leiden over the original ordering identifies a small number of large communities.
For BG and CS, prefer Leiden+llp ordering even on locality-rich graphs.
(5) Random access. Use the sampled index (𝑘=64, +0.036 bpe on CNR-2000) when single-vertex queries are needed
in a single self-contained file (Section 5.7).

Conclusion
We have presented an empirical study of vertex ordering as a first-class component of reference-based graph compression.
We found two things. First, on graphs delivered in an arbitrary or vertex-ID order, reordering with the two-stage

25
Leiden+llp pipeline (global llp to seed labels, Leiden community detection, per-cluster llp within each cluster) reduces
bits-per-edge on every dataset and every encoder we measured, by 0.3 to 5.4 bpe. Second, the size of that gain is
largely insensitive to the encoder: on four of five weakly ordered datasets, the four reference-based encoders we
benchmarked—BVGraph default and the three we contribute—agree on the Leiden+llp-vs-plain-llp gain within roughly
±0.04 bpe (Web-Google ±0.04, Arxiv-HEP-PH ±0.015, Amazon-0601 ±0.030, enwiki-2013 ±0.008). The exception is eat,
where our three larger-window encoders still agree within ±0.007 but BV default at 𝑤=7 shows almost no residual
gain—a window-size effect rather than a refutation of the pattern. On URL-ordered web crawls, the picture flips: our
adaptive encoders still benefit from reordering, but BVGraph at small windows, BV-HC, and CG at 𝐾>1 are mildly hurt
by it. In short, for poorly ordered graphs the question is usually not whether to reorder, but how much extra locality
the encoder can still exploit once the labels are in place.
To quantify how much encoder choice matters once ordering is fixed, we contributed three encoders—BG, CS, and
CG—that perform per-vertex cost-optimal selection from up to 28 candidate decompositions. Under each encoder’s
best-tested ordering (Leiden+llp on weakly ordered graphs and on web crawls for the adaptive variants; original LAW
order for CG 𝐾=2 on web crawls), the best of the three improves over BV-HC by 2–9% on every dataset tested, with
the encoder-level gain consistently smaller than the ordering-level gain on weakly ordered datasets. We also describe
a self-delimiting file format with low-overhead random access (sampled offset index, +0.036 bpe on CNR-2000) and
release all code and benchmarks as open-source Julia software.
We do not claim a new fundamental algorithmic technique—Leiden, llp, and reference-based encoding are individually
well-studied. The contribution is the systematic measurement of how they compose, the evidence on five datasets
that the ordering effect transfers across four reference-based encoders (BVGraph default plus our three), and a public
reference implementation.
Future work. A theoretical account of when and why community-aware ordering helps—ideally, a graph-statistic
that predicts reorderability without running the full pipeline—would close an obvious gap in the present study. Billion-
scale evaluation on uk-2007 and ClueWeb would test scalability beyond enwiki-2013. Multi-threaded encoding via
chunk-based partitioning with independent reference buffers is straightforward and would narrow the speed gap with
BVGraph. Finally, automatic selection of the cluster count 𝐾for CG on graphs where multi-cluster encoding is beneficial
remains open.
