Covering systems where the prime divisors of all moduli
are only 2, 3, or 5
Joshua Harrington, Jonah Klein, Joshua Lowrance, and Ognian Trifonov
May 19, 2026
Abstract
We try to find all quadruples of positive integers (m, a, b, c) with a ≥b ≥c such that
there exists a distinct covering system with minimum modulus m and least common
multiple of the moduli 2a3b5c. We obtain complete description of all such quadruples
when m = 2, 3, 4, 5, or 6, except when m = 6 and b = c = 1. We also show that if the
LCM of the moduli has only 2, 3, or 5 as prime divisors, then m ≤9 and contruct a
distinct covering system with m = 8, a = 8, b = 3, and c = 2. When a covering system
exsists for a quadruple (m, a, b, c) we provide an example. Nonexistence of covering
systems is established via integer programming or by using a new estimate on the
density of a set covered by a system of congruences.

Introduction
A covering system C is a set of congruences x ≡ri (mod ni), i = 1, . . . , k, such that every
integer satisfies at least one of the congruences.
We can assume that 1 ≤n1 ≤· · · ≤nk.
We will denote the least modulus n1 of the
covering by m, the largest modulus nk by M, and the least common multiple of all moduli
by L(C) = L.
A distinct covering (system) is a covering where all moduli are distinct
integers greater than 1. The following well-known distinct covering system was exhibited by
Davenport in 1952.
x ≡1 (mod 2), x ≡2 (mod 4), x ≡0 (mod 3), x ≡4 (mod 6), x ≡8 (mod 12)
(1)
In this covering system m = 2, M = 12, and L = 12.
The use of covering systems in number theory was initiated by Erd˝os in the early 1950s.
In 1971, Krukenberg [5], a student of John Selfridge, wrote a dissertation where he did an
extensive analysis of covering systems. Krukenberg investigated the question: If in a distinct
covering system the minimum modulus m is fixed, how small can its largest modulus M be?

Krukenberg [5] constructed distinct covering systems with m = 3 and M = 36; with m = 4

Krukenberg showed that if in a distinct covering system m = 2, then M ≥12; also, if
m = 3, then M ≥36. He claimed that he had a proof that if m = 4, then M ≥60; and he
conjectured that if m = 5, then M ≥108.
About fifty years after Krukenberg’s work, Dalton and the fourth author [2] showed that
if in a distinct covering system m = 4, then indeed M ≥60.
In July 2025, McNew and Setty [6] introduced the use of integer programming for covering
systems (they used the Gurobi optimizer) to check whether one can construct a distinct
covering with all moduli in a specific set. This allowed them to handle sets containing close
to one hundred moduli. Previously, even for certain sets containing twenty moduli, it was
not known whether one can construct a distinct covering system with all moduli in the given
set.
Extending the work of McNew and Setty, the second author [4] was able to show that if in
a distinct covering system m = 5, then M ≥108, confirming Krukenberg’s conjecture. While
very little work has been done to construct distinct covering systems with fixed m ≥6 and
M as small as possible, the second author [4] has conjectured that if m = 6, then M ≥168.

Open Problems
Problem 1. Establish whether or not there exists a distinct covering system with minimum
modulus 9 and an LCM of its moduli of the form 2a3b5c, either finite or infinite.
We expect that such a covering does not exist.
Problem 2. Establish whether or not there exists a positive integer a such that there exists
a distinct covering system with minimum modulus 6 and an LCM of its moduli 2a325.
We conjecture that such a covering does not exist.
Problem 3. Find all triples of positive integers (a, b, c) with a ≥b ≥c such that there
exists a distinct covering system with minimum modulus m = 8 and an LCM of its moduli
L = 2a3b5c.
Solving the above problem presents several difficulties. First, most of the covering systems
involved will contain more than one hundred congruences. Next, there is no analogue of
Theorem 1.2 in the case m = 8. Finally, solving Problem 3 using integer programming is
currently not computationally feasible.
Problem 4. Determine whether Theorem 1.9 holds without the condition L = 2a3b5c. If
not, find less restrictive conditions for the theorem to hold.
Currently, we can show that the condition L = 2a3b5c can be dropped if n = 3. The
proof of the theorem also works if one replaces 2, 3, 5 in the LCM condition with any three
distinct primes.

Notation
By the Chinese Remainder Theorem, if n1, . . . , nk are k pairwise coprime positive integers,
then a congruence modulo n1 · · · nk is equivalent to a system of k congruences with moduli
n1, . . . , nk respectively.
For example, since 180 = 4 · 5 · 9, the congruence x ≡11 (mod 180) is equivalent to
the system of congruences x ≡3 (mod 4), x ≡1 (mod 5), and x ≡2 (mod 9). A number
of authors have used a notation along the lines of [3, 1, 2], [4, 5, 9] (an ordered list of the
residues followed by an ordered set of the moduli of the congruences.)
Since the prime divisors of the moduli of the congruences we will be using are only 2,
3, or 5, we will use a simplified notation. We represent the congruence x ≡r (mod 2a3b5c)
as (k|l|m), where to obtain k we find the remainder of r when divided by 2a and write the
remainder in base 2 with the digits reversed; we proceed similarly for l, using 3b instead
and base 3; and 5c for m and base 5. We use a base 2 digits for k, b base 3 digits for l, and
c base 5 digits for m.
For example, [3, 2, 1], [4, 9, 5] becomes (11|20|1).
Also, if a = 0, we set k = ∗, if b = 0, then l = ∗, and if c = 0, then m = ∗.
For example, [2, 1], [4, 5] becomes (01| ∗|1).
Finally, for brevity, we skip the trailing ∗’s. For example, we write (0) for the congruence
x ≡0 (mod 2) rather than (0| ∗|∗).
