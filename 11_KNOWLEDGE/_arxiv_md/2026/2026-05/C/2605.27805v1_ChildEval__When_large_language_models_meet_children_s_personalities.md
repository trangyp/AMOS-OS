Abstract
While LLMs enable personalized chatbots,
their effectiveness in child-centered personal-
ization remains unclear, as systematic evalua-
tion of child-specific preferences is still lack-
ing. To address this gap, we introduce ChildE-
val, a benchmark for evaluating LLMs’ abil-
ity to infer and follow child-centered prefer-
ences in long-context conversations. ChildEval
contains 29K synthesized persona profiles of
children aged 3–6, providing relatively static
background information. Each persona is as-
sociated with a child preference—which may
align with, conflict with, or be independent
of the persona—expressed either explicitly in
a single sentence or implicitly through 6–10
turn dialogues. Explicit and implicit prefer-
ences are designed to reflect the same underly-
ing preference but differ in expression, captur-
ing dynamic aspects of preference expression
rather than changes in the static persona. The
benchmark spans five top-level and fourteen
sub-level categories covering children’s daily
lives and development. We further propose fine-
grained, child-centric evaluation protocols to
systematically assess open-source LLMs. Ex-
perimental results demonstrate how different
personalized representations affect LLM re-
sponses and suggest that finetuning on ChildE-
val can enhance child-centered performance.
Our code and dataset are available at https:
//github.com/ziyanluo/ChildEval.

Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) (e.g., ChatGPT
(OpenAI et al., 2024), Gemini (Gemini et al., 2025),
and Claude (Anthropic, 2024)) have achieved re-
markable success in effectively understanding and
generating human language, leading to a revolu-
tionary era in LLMs (Xu et al., 2025; Han et al.,
2025). Beyond generic dialogues, LLMs have been
utilized in a wide range of individual daily tasks
*The corresponding author.
(e.g., healthcare (Xu et al., 2024; Han et al., 2023),
finance (Easin et al., 2024) and other domains (Bai
et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025)) to deliver personal-
ized user experiences based on preferences (Kumar
et al., 2024). One promising direction is to provide
personalized responses tailored to children’s pref-
erences, fostering their development and learning
while supporting caregivers (Feng et al., 2024; Seo
et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2025a), rather than merely
returning generic correct answers.
Previous research on LLM personalization has
mainly focused on adult preferences and tasks. Qiu
et al. (2025) advance personalization by extract-
ing user preferences from historical profiles, while
other studies address the lack of benchmarks for
evaluating personalized capabilities (Salemi et al.,
2023; Jiang et al., 2025). However, the proposed
benchmarks focus on general preferences (e.g., the
number of dialogue turns) with generic tasks (e.g.,
ticket booking and restaurant recommendations)
for adults.
Several benchmarks target children
(Rath et al., 2025; Liu and Fourtassi, 2024), but
with limited scope: Rath et al. (2025) focus on
child safety without diverse child-centered tasks,
and Liu and Fourtassi (2024) examine style imita-
tion without considering developmental or learning
needs. Thus, whether LLMs can deliver personal-
ized responses aligned with child-specific prefer-
ences remains underexplored.
In particular, we identify two key gaps in existing
child personalization benchmarks for LLMs. (1)
Current benchmarks fail to capture children’s dis-
tinct preferences and developmental needs(Liu and
Fourtassi, 2024), limiting age-appropriate person-
alization (Chen et al., 2025b). (2) There is no com-
prehensive evaluation taxonomy tailored to child
personalization: existing studies largely focus on
adult-centered preferences (e.g., general preference
following) and overlook children’s specific needs,
such as generating developmentally appropriate
content and supporting creativity.

Facing these challenges, we introduce ChildE-
val, a benchmark for evaluating LLMs’ ability to
infer, interpret, and follow child-centered prefer-
ences in long-context conversations.
Targeting
preschool children (ages 3–6) with high demand
for LLM-based chatbot companionship, ChildE-
val comprises 29K synthesized children’s persona
profiles, with explicit preferences aligned to each
persona and their implicit expressions revealed
through multi-turn dialogues consisting of 6 to 10
turns. The preferences cover 5 top-level and 14 sub-
level topics that involve children’s daily lives and
development (Antle, 2008; Wang, 2013) according
to the guidelines published by the Ministry of Ed-
ucation of China (MOE, 2012). We also propose
fine-grained, child-centric evaluation protocols to
systematically assess open-source LLMs. Exper-
imental results demonstrate the influence of per-
sonalized representations on model responses and
suggest that finetuning on this dataset can improve
performance.

Related Work
Personalization Data and Evaluation. Person-
aMem (Jiang et al., 2025) and HiCUPID (Mok
et al., 2025) simulate multi-attribute personas and
multi-turn interactions to assess user-specific con-
sistency. Evaluation frameworks consider metrics
such as style alignment, preference fidelity, and
user satisfaction (Salemi et al., 2023). To address
the scarcity of personalized data, prior work ex-
plores synthetic dialogue generation (Braga et al.,
2024), profile summarization (Zhang, 2024), and
memory retrieval from past interactions (Qian et al.,
2025).
Child-centered personalization has recently
gained attention.
KidLM (Nayeem and Rafiei,
2024) introduces child-oriented corpora and train-
ing strategies, complemented by work on style sim-
plification (Valentini et al., 2023) and child-specific
safety evaluation (Rath et al., 2025). However, ex-
isting benchmarks remain largely general-purpose,
leaving it unclear whether LLMs can follow chil-
dren’s preferences in multi-turn settings.
LLM Personalization Methods. Methodolog-
ical advances in personalization broadly fall into
non-parametric and parametric approaches. Non-
parametric methods condition model behavior with-
out modifying parameters, including prompt-based
personalization using explicit or inferred traits (Liu
et al., 2025; Li et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024),
memory-based approaches that leverage past in-
teractions (Madaan et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2025;
Magister et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a), and
preference inference and conditioning that extracts
preferences to guide generation (Zhao et al., 2025a;
Ryan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025b). Parametric
approaches embed user traits into model parame-
ters via finetuning or reinforcement learning, in-
cluding parameter-efficient adaptations and reward-
based personalization (Tan et al., 2024; Huber et al.,
2025; Zhuang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Zhao
et al., 2025b). Despite progress, it remains unclear
whether these methods generalize to children, mo-
tivating child-specific evaluation.

3.2
Data Construction Pipeline
The data construction pipeline comprises three
steps, as shown in Figure 1(a).
Step 1: We generate 29K child personas using
Qwen2.5-72B via iterative generation and refine-
ment. Semantic duplicates are removed with FAISS
(Douze et al., 2024) based on text embeddings
(Xiao et al., 2023) to ensure diversity. All child
names and identifiers are eliminated through LLM
processing and human review to mitigate privacy
risks.
Step 2: For each child persona, we generate
two explicit preferences using persona-conditioned
prompts. Each preference may align with, conflict
with, or be independent of the persona, reflecting
context-dependent expression rather than changes
in the static persona. Preferences are expressed as
single first-person sentences, each grounded in a
distinct sub-level topic from a predefined taxonomy
(Table 1) covering children’s daily lives and devel-
opment (Antle, 2008; Wang, 2013; MOE, 2012),
and generated together with a corresponding child-
style utterance to initiate dialogue. This process
yields 58K explicit preferences, of which 46K are
retained after FAISS-based semantic filtering.
Step 3: Historical dialogues that implicitly re-
veal preferences are generated via prompt-based
generation conditioned on the corresponding ex-
plicit preference, with self-verification enforcing
predefined rules (Section A.4), resulting in 6–10-
turn child–LLM conversations. These implicit pref-
erences convey the same underlying preference as
their corresponding explicit ones, but differ in ex-
pression.

(1) Preference Consistency (PC). We evaluate
whether the model’s responses align with a child’s
explicitly stated or implicitly inferred preferences,
using established criteria from prior work (Zhao
et al., 2025a), which are released under the Creative
Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0 Interna-

(2) Child-Oriented Evaluation (COE). Beyond
preference consistency, child-centered dialogues re-
quire additional fine-grained evaluation dimensions
that differ from typical adult-oriented communi-
cation. Accordingly, we propose a set of novel
child-oriented evaluation metrics focusing on the
distinctive linguistic and contextual characteristics
of child-centered conversations, covering four sub-
dimensions.
Emotional Adaptation (EA). The LLMs should
be sensitive to the emotions expressed by the chil-
dren, providing empathetic, supportive, and age-
appropriate responses that help to maintain a posi-
tive atmosphere of interaction.
Interaction Scaffolding (IS). The LLM should
be able to scaffold effective child-centered con-
versation with prompts, clarifications, or playful
follow-ups in a natural conversational flow.
Developmental Appropriateness (DA). The
LLM’s responses should match the cognitive
and linguistic abilities of 3-to-6-year-old children,
avoiding overly complex vocabulary or reasoning
while providing informative and stimulating con-
tent.
Engagement (EG). The LLM should be able
to produce lively and appealing utterances, using
child-specific markers such as playful particles,
reduplication, or culturally grounded scenarios, to
keep children actively interested in the dialogue.

Experiments and Analyses
4.1
Experimental Setup
We evaluate five open-source LLMs (Qwen2.5-
3B/72B-Instruct, Qwen3-4B-Instruct, LLaMA3.1-
8B-Instruct, DeepSeek-R1-671B, and Mistral-7B-
Instruct-v0.3) using three child-preference adapta-
tion strategies: prompt-based (PBM), LoRA fine-
tuning, and our Persona Steer Model (PSM), which
injects child persona information from ChildE-
val via a pluggable Persona Steer Module (Sec-
tion A.5).
All experiments are zero-shot on a
bilingual (Chinese–English) dataset, with addi-
tional long-context multi-session inputs to assess
preference modeling under extended context (Ap-
pendix A.1). Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct serves as the
SFT backbone, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct is used for
evaluation, and ChildEval is split 8:2 into train/test
sets for a total of 46K samples. Fine-tuning and
evaluation were conducted on two and four A800
GPUs, respectively.
4.2
Reliability of LLM-as-a-Judge
To assess the reliability of machine-based evalua-
tion, we conducted a human evaluation on a subset
of the data and examined its consistency with LLM-
based judgments. Specifically, we randomly sam-
pled 600 examples covering all preference types
and manually annotated them, then computed the
agreement rates between human judgments and
LLM evaluations. As shown in Table 2, the agree-
ment rate reaches 88.83% for PC. While LLM-
based evaluation cannot fully substitute for human
review, this result indicates that it provides a reli-
able and scalable mechanism for assessing prefer-
ence consistency.

ble 2 indicate that these criteria are well-defined

Child-Oriented Evaluation
Personality preference consistency does not
align with child-oriented capabilities. Compar-
ing Table 4 and Figure 3 across models, consistency
accuracy may be similar (e.g., 74%–76%), while
child-oriented evaluation varies widely across di-
mensions, especially IS and DA, suggesting that a
high consistency score alone does not necessarily
reflect strong child-oriented personalization. No-
tably, Qwen3-4B-Instruct outperforms larger mod-
els (Table 4), indicating that performance may de-
pend not only on model scale but also on alignment
and instruction-following capabilities. Figure 7
shows a representative example.
LLMs show limited capability in Interaction
Scaffolding (IS). Across all models, performance
on the IS dimension is lower than on other eval-
uation dimensions. For example, on the explicit
dataset, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct achieves 35.8% ac-
curacy on IS. This substantial gap highlights a key
limitation of current approaches, as sensitivity to
subtle cues is critical for building engaging and
personalized child interactions.

4.6
Finetuning on ChildEval to Enhance
Child Personalization
Supervised Finetuning on ChildEval leads to
consistent improvements in children’s personal-
ization performance across open-source LLMs.
As illustrated in Figure 5, applying LoRA SFT,
both with and without persona injection, leads to
substantial gains in both preference consistency
and child-oriented evaluation compared with the
base models. Interestingly, LoRA SFT with per-
sona shows slightly lower improvements in pref-
erence consistency than LoRA without persona.
One possible reason is that adding persona signals
may introduce additional constraints, and the per-
sona itself may contain noise related to the explicit
and implicit preference expressions in the ChildE-
val dataset, which could slightly limit the model’s
ability to fully optimize for consistency and child-
oriented performance.

shifts from Preference Hallucinations in short con-

explicit datasets, LoRA remains proactive; PSM be-

5
Conclusion
We introduce ChildEval, a controlled evaluation
framework centered on underlying child-relevant
needs and preferences rather than surface-level ex-
pressions, enabling systematic assessment of pref-
erence alignment across realistic variations. Exper-
iments show that current open-source LLMs strug-
gle to maintain preference consistency in long inter-
actions, especially for implicit preferences. More-
over, targeted finetuning improves preference ad-
herence but may introduce new inconsistencies,
highlighting the trade-offs, potential, and limita-
tions of current approaches.

Limitations
Synthetic Data and Realism. A primary limita-
tion of this work is its reliance on a fully synthetic
dataset for evaluating child-centered preferences
and needs in conversational settings. Real-world
communication from children aged 3–6 exhibits
substantial variability in expression, context, and
developmental appropriateness, which is difficult to
faithfully capture through synthetic generation. Ac-
cordingly, the proposed benchmark does not aim
to fully reflect the complexity or distribution of
real-world child language use.
At the same time, collecting large-scale, open-
ended conversational data from young children
poses substantial ethical, legal, and privacy chal-
lenges, resulting in a lack of publicly available
and ethically compliant datasets. Under these con-
straints, synthetic data offers a practical and con-
trollable framework for reproducible evaluation,
while not substituting for real-world observations.
Moreover, as a fully synthetic benchmark, the
dataset may inherit stylistic biases from the genera-
tion pipeline. The benchmark should therefore be
interpreted as a controlled diagnostic framework
for systematically analyzing how LLMs align with
child-relevant needs and preferences, rather than
as a comprehensive simulation of child behavior or
an estimate of real-world distributions.
Scope of Safety Modeling. A second limita-
tion is that this work does not explicitly model
child-specific safety risks, developmental harms,
or safety violations. All benchmark instances are
constructed under a fixed and unified safety stan-
dard, with the goal of isolating and evaluating child-
specific preference consistency. As a result, the
benchmark does not capture scenarios in which
children’s misunderstandings, ambiguous expres-
sions, or exploratory behaviors may induce safety
risks in real-world deployments.
Addressing these limitations remains an impor-
tant direction for future work. In particular, future
research should aim to better capture the develop-
mental diversity of child-relevant needs and their
varied expressions in real-world contexts, while
moving beyond a unified safety standard to explic-
itly model age-dependent safety risks and the inter-
action between safety constraints and personalized
preference alignment. Advancing this direction
will likely require close interdisciplinary collabo-
ration with experts in child psychology, education,
and human–computer interaction.
Ethics Statement
This study involves no direct interaction with hu-
man participants and uses no real child-generated
conversational data. All benchmark instances are
fully synthetic and generated under existing content
safety constraints.
To address ethical, legal, and privacy concerns
associated with research involving young children,
no personal data, recordings, or transcripts from
children are collected, released, or used. Any real-
world materials referenced were used only at a high
level to inform child persona design and are not
included in the dataset or evaluation.
All synthetic data were manually reviewed to
comply with established content moderation and
safety guidelines, ensuring that the benchmark does
not expose children to risk or involve sensitive per-
sonal information.
All code and benchmark data will be released to
support transparency, reproducibility, and responsi-
ble use.

A.1
Long-Context Settings
To simulate realistic conversational dynamics, we
adopt a methodology similar to Zhao et al. (2025a).
We incorporate multi-session dialogue turns from
the WildChat-1M dataset (Zhao et al., 2024), which
contains real user and LLM interactions across di-
verse topics. For SFT training, we randomly se-
lect 3, 5, and 10 round conversations to construct
three training sets. For testing, we sample multi-
session contexts up to 21K tokens, interleaving
dialogue turns between the disclosure of children’s
preferences and the related utterances. Although
we initially considered extending dialogues to 50K
tokens, the backbone model supports at most 30K,
beyond which outputs became unstable. This setup
creates a challenging evaluation for LLMs to infer,
retrieve, and utilize children’s preferences in long
dialogues, especially when interspersed with unre-
lated topics. For dialogues of varying turn counts,
we randomly sample and fix their lengths, with
token statistics reported in Table 5.

A.5
Architecture of the Persona Steer Model
To assist in examining whether providing the child
persona information in our benchmark would con-
tribute to better LLM personalization, we propose
a persona steer model that leverages persona infor-
mation to guide the LLM’s outputs toward person-
alized behaviors. The architecture of our persona
steer model is depicted in Figure 15, whose core
is the Personalized Steer Module. While the pre-
trained LLM provides robust general language com-
prehension and generation, the Personalized Steer
Module enables effective user adaptation without
huge computational burdens.
Specifically, as shown in Figure 15, the Per-
sonalized Steer Module is designed to introduce
user-specific information into the language model
in a precisely controlled manner. A Persona En-
coder processes textual descriptions of children’s
personas, converting them into dense embeddings
that effectively encapsulate the semantic essence
of user characteristics. The persona embedding is
then aggregated with the output of a Steer Adapter
module, which contains information extracted from
the historical dialogue by the LLM. The aggregated
embedding eaggregate is then processed by a Gating
Network, whose gate value g(·) is dynamically cal-
culated based on hL. The final output embedding
e′
v before applying the Softmax function could
be obtained by:
e′
v = hLW T + g(hL)eaggregateW T ,
= hLW T + ∆(hL)W T
(3)
where W is the weight matrix employed by the
LLM. It should be noted that only the Steer Adapter
and the Gating Network used to calculate ∆(hL)
contain parameters learnable during the training,
while other parameters remain fixed.
To minimize the learnable parameters in the
Steer Adapter, we implement a parameterization
approach inspired by low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
(Hu et al., 2021). Specifically, rather than learning
a full-rank transformation matrix, we decompose
it into two low-rank matrices, as shown in Figure
15. This decomposition maintains the transforma-
tion’s expressive power while allowing efficient
integration of personalized information, seamlessly
merging it into the LLM’s representations to facili-
tate effective user adaptation and stable generation.
Additionally, it opens possibilities for incorporat-
ing more sophisticated personalized models into
LLM generation.

A.6.1
Effect of Inserted Context Length on
Preference Consistency Error Types
Figures 16 and 17 illustrate the changes in prefer-
ence consistency error types across different num-
bers of inserted irrelevant turns in the explicit and
implicit datasets, respectively. Under zero-shot
prompting without persona, Preference-Unaware
Violations become increasingly prominent as the
number of irrelevant turns increases, indicating that
LLMs struggle more to maintain awareness of user
preferences when exposed to longer irrelevant con-
text. With the introduction of various methods,


## Page 15


including finetuning approaches such as LoRA and
PSM, the proportion of Preference-Unaware Vio-
lations decreases, while Hallucination Violations
increase and Inconsistency Violations begin to ap-
pear, reflecting the challenges models face in gener-
ating responses that are both aligned with retrieved
preferences and free from hallucinated information.
On the explicit dataset, LoRA is more prone to
Inconsistency Violations across n-turn scenarios,
whereas PSM exhibits higher rates of Preference
Hallucination Violations in shorter contexts; how-
ever, as the number of irrelevant turns increases
beyond 30, the rate of Preference Hallucination
Violations in PSM decreases, while Unhelpful Re-
sponses become increasingly dominant. On the
implicit dataset, Unhelpful Responses constitute
the primary error type for PSM, indicating a ten-
dency to refuse or provide unhelpful answers rather
than attempt alignment with user preferences.
Overall, these results highlight the trade-offs be-
tween proactive preference-following and robust-
ness to irrelevant context. Notably, as the length of
irrelevant context increases, PSM becomes increas-
ingly conservative, producing unhelpful responses,
whereas LoRA is more proactive, continuing to
attempt responses aligned with user preferences,
although alignment issues remain.
A.6.2
Effect of Inserted Context Length on
Finetuning Results
Figure 18 illustrates how persona-informed finetun-
ing methods (i.e., PSM and LoRA) evolve with
increasing dialogue length on both explicit and
implicit datasets (i.e., datasets with historical dia-
logues that explicitly and implicitly reveal the child
preference). On the explicit dataset, PSM-based
models show a relatively sharp decline in prefer-
ence consistency as the number of inserted irrele-
vant dialogue turns increases, while LoRA-based
models exhibit a moderate decrease. Moreover,
results on the PC (Preference Consistency) dimen-
sion indicate that training with longer irrelevant
dialogues yields greater robustness on equally long
test dialogues than training with shorter ones. In-
terestingly, within the child-oriented dimensions,
most metrics remain relatively stable across dia-
logue lengths, whereas developmental appropriate-
ness (DA) exhibits the largest fluctuations, indicat-
ing its heightened sensitivity to contextual length.
On the implicit dataset, model trends largely
mirror those observed on the explicit dataset. In
the PC dimension, PSM-based models remain rel-
atively stable compared to LoRA-based models
on dialogues shorter than 30 turns (12K tokens)
and benefit more from inserted irrelevant dialogues.
However, in longer dialogues (e.g., 50 turns, PSM-
based models show a sharper decline, falling below
LoRA-based models. This pattern may be due
to the fact that PSM relies on the aggregation of
final-layer vectors to incorporate the persona in-
formation into an LLM, which works well when
the inserted irrelevant dialogue is short, but may
be negatively affected by accumulated noise when
the irrelevant dialogue is long. In contrast, LoRA’s
low-rank adaptation maintains greater stability in
extended contexts.
