Categorical (Co)Limits of Quantum Graphs
Jennifer Zhu

Abstract
We begin with the characterization of quantum graphs as left ideals in M ⊗eh
M (the extended Haagerup tensor product of M with itself) to avoid technicalities
surrounding representation dependence of quantum graphs. These left ideals roughly
correspond to a canonical complement of a quantum graph. Using these left ideals
and some operator space theory, we find a new, representation-free characterization
of a morphism of quantum graphs compatible with previous representation-dependent
morphisms.
A notion of categorical (co)limit of quantum graphs follows.
We also
briefly explore an alternative quantization of graphs as bimodules over C∗-algebras
(C∗-graphs), mostly to emphasize the point that a morphism of C∗-graphs is not a
morphism of C∗-correspondences.

Introduction
Many authors have explored the notion of quantum relations set out by Weaver in [Wea12]
from a variety of perspectives. A quantum relation by Weaver’s definition is an M′-bimodule
S ⊆B(H) (where M ⊆B(H) is a von Neumann algebra). When H is finite dimensional,
these are precisely the quantum relations in [MRV18, Section 7]. The diagrammatic calculus
(known by many names) used in [MRV18] has ben a fruitful avenue of research. Calcula-
tions using these diagrams can be elegant and intuitive, but they are fundamentally limited
by finite dimensionality (see Chapters 2 and 3 in [HV19] for an enjoyable explanation and
Appendix A of the author’s thesis for a brutally short summary). Thus Weaver’s quantum
relations provide a natural setting for infinite dimensional extensions and versions of these
diagrammatically defined objects. The motivation for this paper was to develop a notion of
limit for quantum graphs to build a (possibly) infinite quantum graph from finite quantum
graphs. At first blush, quantum relations seem representation dependent but Weaver im-
mediately shows that they only depend on the von Neumann algebra M, not the ambient
space B(H) ([Wea12, Theorem 2.7]). Generally, however, one works in a particular repre-
sentation and then appeals to the fact that the relevant properties of quantum graphs are
representation independent.

If we were to use this characterization of quantum graphs in to define a categorical limit,
however, it is unclear how a morphism of quantum graphs S1 ⊆B(H1) and S2 ⊆B(H2)
should behave on the ambient spaces B(H1) and B(H2). Furthermore, we may encounter
issues regarding the uniqueness of the categorical limit. We are thus pushed to avoid the
usual characterization of a quantum graph as an M′-bimodule S ⊆B(H), leading to a new
notion of morphism of quantum graphs in Definition 3.8. This new notion is equivalent to
a CP morphism of quantum graphs in [Daw24, Definition 7.1] and a classical morphism of
quantum graphs in [MRV18, Definition 5.4] under the appropriate assumptions. It also has
the advantage of giving a clean visualization of categorical limits: compare the diagrams in
Section 5 where we use this new notion of morphism and the ones in Theorem 6.5 where we
define the morphism on the level of bimodules.
Although this paper is not focused on the quantum information background or impli-
cations, we will see some comments sprinkled through the paper referring to the quantum
information theoretic roots. We will introduce the connection between quantum channels
and quantum graphs here, but the reader may wish to consult the original paper [DSW13]
for further explanation. The authors of [DSW13] are inspired by Shannon’s analysis of con-
fusability graphs of noisy classical channels in [Sha56]. We can consider such a channel as
a probabilistic function N : A →B between finite sets A and B where we allow for the
possibility that N does not always send an input a to a specified output N(a) (this is the
introduction of “noise” to the channel). We assume we know the probability N(b|a) of N
sending a to b for every a ∈A and b ∈B. Such a channel N gives rise to a confusability
graph.

The sections are laid out as follows. Section 2 is an overview of the conventions and
necessary operator space and category theory for the remainder of the paper. We will use
the framework of quantum relations in [Wea12, Definition 2.4], but we will depart from
Weaver’s naming convention and call them quantum graphs. A quantum graph according
to [Wea12, Definition 2.4] will be specified as a reflexive, symmetric quantum graph in this
paper. Section 3 presents the intuition behind our new morphism and defines it. Section
4 shows how some properties of quantum graphs (considered as operator space bimodules
over M′ for some von Neumann algebra M) are reflected in their annihilators (which are
left ideals in M ⊗eh M). Section 5 finally takes the categorical limit of a class of quantum
graphs. See Remark 7.5 for the categorical colimit. In Section 6 we take heavy inspiration
from [MT18] to explore the consequences of defining graphs as C∗-algebra bimodules and
characterize the morphism given in Section 3 on the level of operator spaces S. Ultimately,
however, it seems that the notion of morphism in Section 3 is more natural in the context
of (co)limits. Section 7 contains some reflections on the previous sections, and Section 8
collects some further questions and future avenues of research. In Appendix A the reader
will find the graphical calculus conventions and definitions needed to prove that a “classical
morphism of quantum graphs” defined in [MRV18, Definition 5.4] is equivalent to our new
definition of morphism under the more restrictive set of assumptions.

Preliminaries
By limits in this paper we refer to categorical limits (projective limits) and colimits (inductive
limits); see [Mac98, Chapter III.4], [Mac98, Chapter III.3]. Such limits are defined in purely
categorical terms (i.e., by objects and morphisms), so we need only identify the correct notion
of quantum relations and their morphisms to apply the definition of categorical (co)limit.
Our objects will of course be quantum relations as defined in Definition 2.1. As mentioned
in the introduction, we refer to Weaver’s quantum relations as quantum graphs. Weaver’s
quantum graphs will be specified as symmetric, reflexive quantum graphs.
4

A quantum graph in [Wea12] is given by a von Neumann algebra M (roughly, the quan-
tization of the vertices) and an M′-bimodule S ⊆B(H) , where B(H) houses a representa-
tion of M (roughly, the quantization of the edges). However, our morphism uses alternate
characterization of quantum graphs – keeping the same von Neumann algebra M as our
quantization of vertices, we will quantize the complementary edges of the graph instead.
These complementary edges live in M ⊗eh M, the extended Haagerup tensor product of M
with itself. We will introduce the extended Haagerup tensor product ⊗eh in Section 2.3 and
state more precisely which subspaces correspond to quantum graphs.
The symbol ⊗will denote the algebraic tensor product. We will be using both ∗and † in
this paper. The former will denote the involution in a ∗-algebra and the latter will denote
the adjoint of a bounded linear map between Hilbert spaces. At times these notions will
coincide, but the context will hopefully be clear enough to avoid confusion. When a predual
of a space X exists and is unique, we will denote it by X∗. We will denote the topology on
X∗induced by a space X by σ(X). The unit of an algebra A will be denoted 1A.

Operator Space Theory
As mentioned in the introduction, we will need some operator space theory in order to quan-
tize the complementary edges of a quantum graph. The full explanation of this quantization
is given in Subsection 3.1, but at the end of this subsection we will see how ⊗eh recovers the
complementary edges of a finite classical graph. We will also make use of the topologies laid
down here to ensure we have the correct continuity of various operations in Section 5.
Basic definitions from operator space theory are given below, but the following is by no
means an in-depth exploration or explanation. A few standard references for operator spaces
are [ER22], [Pis03], and [Pau02].
Definition 2.5. A concrete operator space is a norm-closed subspace X ⊆B(H) for some
Hilbert space H.

Operator Space Tensor Norms
The compatibility of the morphisms in the limit of quantum graphs relies heavily on a
particular operator space tensor product, the extended Haagerup tensor product ⊗eh (and
to some extent the normal Haagerup tensor product ⊗σh). Rather than introducing ⊗eh in
isolation, it may be more elucidating to present all three Haagerup tensor products (and
the operator space projective tensor product) together in relation to the map Φ introduced
below.
Given two operator spaces X and Y , there are many OSSs one can impose on X ⊗
Y under which the completion is an operator space (see [CDG25] for a slew of them).

and similarly if we replace ℓ1 by ℓ2. Therefore there exists aℓ1 such that πℓ1,∞(aℓ1) = πj1,∞(aj1)
and similarly for ℓ2. We claim that
πj1,∞(aj1) · sj2 = πℓ1,∞(aℓ1) · sj2
πk1,∞(ak1) · sj2 = πℓ1,∞(aℓ1) · sj2
and as before we only prove the first equation. Since ℓ1 ≤ℓ2,
πj1,∞(aj1) · sj2 = ej2,ℓ2(πj1,ℓ2(aj1) · sℓ2)

Further Questions
Here we collect sketch some questions inspired by the work above. Fuller expositions may be
found in the author’s thesis. Questions 8.1, 8.2, and 8.3 are some loose ends from the work
above. Question 8.4 has connections with quantum information theory. Question 8.5 pro-
poses that quantum Cayley graphs of quantum groups should be considered as left ideals in
some ⊗eh tensor product. Question 8.6 proposes that the ⊗eh tensor product characterization
of quantum graphs could extend the categorification of finite quantum graphs.
8.1
Transitivity of Quantum Graphs
A specter that has haunted many investigations of quantum graphs returns to the question of
transitivity. A quantum graph S on a von Neumann algebra M ⊆B(H) is transitive if S2 ⊆
S [Wea12, Definition 2.4]. In [Gon20, Remark 3.4(iii)], the author asks how one might obtain
Ann(S2) from Ann(S) ⊆M ⊗eh M. It also plays a role in abstractly defining a quantum
distance operator. Ideally, such an operator should have a categorical characterization as
adjacency operators do in Appendix A.
8.2
σ(M∗⊗h M∗)-Closed Left Ideals
Given the significance of σ(M∗⊗h M∗)-closed graphs in Section 5, it would nice to have
another characterization of such graphs. As noted in Corollary 5.7, all quantum graphs on
a von Neumann algebra of the form M = Q∞
i∈I Mni are examples.
8.3
Coproduct of Quantum Graphs
The categorical product of two finite classical graphs G1 and G2 is the so-called tensor product
of graphs, so-called because the adjacency operator of G1
Q G2 is the tensor product of the
53


## Page 54


adjacency operators of G1 and G2. The vertex set of this categorical product is V1 ⊗V2. A
naive quantization of the vertex set might be ℓ∞(V1) ⊗ℓ∞(V2).
In the category of quantum graphs, however, the “vertex set” of the categorical product
of G1 and G2 is the free product of von Neumann algebras (see Remark 2.60). Without going
into precise detail about how to form the free product, it is without a doubt much bigger than
ℓ∞(V1)⊗ℓ∞(V2). However, this is not so much an obstruction as an interesting feature of non-
commutative algebras. The coproduct in the category of commutative associative algebras
is the tensor product, which aligns with the classical case.
Allowing our algebras to be
non-commutative necessitates free product-like constructions in the coproduct, a byproduct
of which is that a product of quantum graphs must be a much larger object than in the
classical case.
8.4
t-Morphisms of Quantum Graphs
The morphisms explored in Subsection 3.3 are considered classical morphisms of quantum
graphs because no external quantum resources are involved in the morphisms. Allowing
the use of quantum resources naturally leads to the graph homomorphism game (see, for
example, [BGH22]). Non-local game theory distinguishes among the types of quantum re-
sources available to the players in the non-local game. [Mat24, Definition 4.1] generalizes
t-homomorphisms from [BGH22] for t ∈{loc, q, qa, C∗, alg} via W∗morphisms. Namely, a
t-homomorphism from (S1, M1) to (S2, M2) for finite dimensional von Neumann algebras
Mk is a unital ∗-algebra At and a unital ∗-homomorphism θ : M2 →M1 ⊗At satisfying
θ†(S1 ⊗1At)θ ⊆S2 ⊗At
and At is an algebra whose type depends on t.
(The notation θ† does not indicate the
adjoint of a map between Hilbert spaces; we refer the reader to [Mat24] for the definition.)
As of this writing there does not seem to be a translation for t-homomorphisms in terms
of annihilators, but following in the vein of Subsection 3.3 such a translation would yield a
compatible notion of t-homomorphism for infinite quantum graphs.
8.5
Quantum Cayley Graphs of (Profinite) Quantum Groups
A classical theorem states that a Cayley graph of a profinite group is always a profinite graph
[Rib17, Example 2.1.12]. The question of whether this statement still holds if “quantum”
is inserted before every noun is still open. As of this writing, there does not seem to be a
definition for the Cayley graph of a general quantum group, though we have one for discrete
quantum groups from [Was24]. In [Gon20], the author proposes a definition for the invariant
quantum relations (invariant quantum graphs, according to the conventions of this paper) of
a quantum group. The author proves that his proposed definition works in the case in which
the quantum group is a classical locally compact group and claims that the proof works the
same when replacing the relevant objects by their analogues in [JNR09].
We have a notion of profinite quantum graphs from Section 5. If one allows Cayley graphs
to be degenerate (i.e., suppose that the generators of the Cayley graph do not generate the
group), then G-invariant relations in G × G are precisely the Cayley graphs of G. We thus

have candidates for the quantum Cayley graphs of a profinite quantum groups. It remains
to see if the classical theorem holds in the quantum case.

A Taste of Categorification
The purpose of this appendix is to introduce just enough terminology to show that classical
homomorphisms of finite quantum graphs introduced in [MRV18, Section 5.2] are equivalent
to a morphism of finite quantum graphs in Definition 3.8.
The authors of [Rie03] quantize (finite) sets, functions, and (finite) graphs through cat-
egorification. Roughly, one characterizes the classical objects or properties via morphisms
in the category Set and interpret them in another category (FHilb, the category of finite
dimensional Hilbert spaces and linear maps, for instance) to obtain their quantum analogs.
Definitions will be accompanied by motivating examples. The authors in [MRV18] de-
velop quantum graphs by quantizing the adjacency matrix of a graph via string diagrams.
This is part of a broader trend of categorification: by rephrasing familiar objects and phe-
nomena in categorical terms, we achieve a new intuition of the categorified object. In this
appendix we will categorify undirected graphs on finitely many vertices. We will not intro-
duce the graphical methods here. For short overviews on graphical methods, see [MRV18]
and [Gol25, Chapter 4]. For an excellent textbook, see [HV19].
Our conventions: in this appendix, all Hilbert spaces are finite dimensional. If φ : H1 →
H2 is a map between Hilbert spaces, we denote its adjoint by φ† : H2 →H1.
Definition A.1. An algebra is a Hilbert space H with a multiplication map m : H⊗H →H
and unit map u : C →H satisfying the following equations:
(m ⊗IH) ◦(m ⊗IH) = (IH ⊗m) ◦(IH ⊗m)
(associativity)
m ◦(IH ⊗u) = IH = m ◦(u ⊗IH)
(unitality).
A coalgebra is a Hilbert space H with a comultiplication map δ : H →H ⊗H and a counit
map ε : H →C satisfying the following equations:
(δ ⊗IH) ◦δ = (IH ⊗δ) ◦δ
(coassociativity)
(IH ⊗ε) ◦δ = IH = (ε ⊗IH) ◦δ
(counitality)
As the names suggest, if (H, m, u) is an algebra then (H, m†, u†) is a coalgebra.
