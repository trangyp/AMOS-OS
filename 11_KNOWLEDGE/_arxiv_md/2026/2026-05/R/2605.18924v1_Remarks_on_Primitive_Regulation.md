PRIMITIVE REGULATION
Milan Rosko

Abstract
We prove, and mechanize in Rocq, an abstract obstruction theorem for
primitive closure predicates, defined as C : Form →Prop over the closed
implication-falsity fragment A, B ::= ⊥| A →B. Two structurally distinct
completeness principles for C enter the result. Evaluation completeness
Eval(C) is generative: every formula-valued behavior of codes admits a
representing code, up to closure equivalence A ≃C B ≜C(A →B) ∧
C(B →A). Excluded-middle completeness LEM(C) is decisional: every
formula is accepted, or its object-level negation is accepted.
Yet their
conjunction is obstructive: Eval(C) generates a reflective fixed-point B ≃C
¬B, which LEM(C) forces C to classify. Either branch collapses to C(⊥)
under modus ponens, and consistency converts the internal collapse into an
external contradiction. A Boolean decision implies LEM(C) and is therefore
obstructed in turn, whereas refutation imposes no coverage requirement and
is inhabited by the always-false classifier.

INTRODUCTION
1.1
BACKGROUND
Limitative results are typically established within their native semantics:
Incompleteness [Whitehead and Russell, 1927; Gödel, 1931; Rosser, 1936]
relies on the arithmetization of syntax; Undefinability [Tarski, 1933] pro-
ceeds by diagonalizing against a hypothesized truth predicate; Semidecid-
ability [Church, 1936; Turing, 1937; Kleene, 1952; Rice, 1953] presupposes
a fixed model of computation; Löb’s Theorem [Löb, 1955] depends on
the modal derivability conditions. The present paper isolates the diagonal
obstruction shared by these results under a minimal set of structural assump-
tions. Working in a constructive metatheory [Troelstra and van Dalen, 1988]
makes the role of bivalence visible: imposing the Law of the Excluded
Middle (LEM) on the closure predicate is the obstruction’s trigger.
1.2
INTUITION
We convey through a thought experiment, prior to any precise definition.
Consider a
regulator C
(1)
whose sole function is to record acceptance for formulas built from implication
and falsity. Three properties are at stake. Detachment: whenever C accepts
both A →B and A, it must accept B. Consistency: C does not accept ⊥.
And the load-bearing one, excluded-middle completeness: every formula A is
accepted, or its negation ¬A is accepted. Detachment and consistency are
the working hypotheses one would grant any reasonable closure predicate;
the third is the totality assumption that the rest of the paper traces back to
critique of unrestricted bivalence by Brouwer [1923].

C is to name its evaluative tableau. If every formula-valued behavior of
codes admits a representing code, C can be probed against the behavior
“Negate what the input would produce if applied to itself.”
Self-applying that code produces a formula B that, inside C, is identified
with its own negation, thence it is a fixed-point of negation at the level of
acceptance, not of syntax.
The informal reading is simple. To certify the question
“Is it true that LEM holds for every formula?”
one must already answer in LEM’s own form: either it holds, or it does not.
Impredicativity [Feferman, 1964] sits not in the answer but in the procedure
that produces one; the classifier deploys the very pattern whose unrestricted
availability it is being asked to certify.
“Pushing” the metaphor one step further: pressed for a verdict on every
formula, the regulator would quietly like one of them left off the list. The
certificate it would prefer to sign reads
“Every question checks out, except this one.”
But LEM(C) is precisely the refusal of that exemption: every formula, with
no clause excusing even one. The diagonal B is a formula like any other, so
the regulator is bound to decide it; and the only formula it cannot afford to
decide is exactly B. The sentence the regulator must utter to be complete
and the sentence it cannot utter without collapsing are the same sentence.
There is no “except this one” to retreat to: that retreat is a fourth principle,
weaker than LEM(C), and the obstruction is the proof that nothing weaker
will do and nothing as strong survives.
Gödel’s Second Theorem [Gödel, 1931] and the Feferman reflection
principles [Feferman, 1962] locate the obstruction one level up: a consistent
system that internalizes a survey of its own derivations cannot also certify
that survey, because the survey is itself one of the derivations it ranges
over. Eval(C) is that internalization at the propositional core, the regulator
naming its own evaluative behaviors, and it leaves no metalevel to retreat to:
a verdict withheld is still a verdict the survey records, so declining B is a
way of deciding B. In this layer the three principles divide cleanly: Eval(C)
performs the internalization, LEM(C) demands the certificate, and Cons(C)
is what makes the certificate unaffordable.
“Not answering the question answers it.”
The formal fixed-point makes that circularity mechanical. Eval(C) sup-
plies a formula B with B ≃C ¬B; LEM(C) then forces C to commit to B or
to ¬B. Either commitment rides the accepted equivalence, and detachment
alone propagates the chosen branch to C(⊥), which Cons(C) rules out. Pred-
icativity [Russell, 1908] is therefore the lever: a consistent, detachment-closed
regulator that names its own evaluative behaviors under closure cannot also
use excluded-middle completeness to certify that very closure.

Two local structural properties of C enter the obstruction. The first is closure
modus ponens MP(C), or detachment. The second is closure consistency
Cons(C) ≜C(⊥) →⊥,

the only bridge from internal acceptance of ⊥to external contradiction.
The first completeness principle is generative. An evaluation frame is
complete when every formula-valued behavior of codes has a representing
code up to closure equivalence:
Eval(C) ≜∀f. ∃c. ∀x. eval(c, x) ≃C f(x).
(4)
The codes form an arbitrary type rather than N; the right-hand side is closure
equivalence rather than syntactic equality. Representational completeness
here means naming behaviors, not deciding formulas. A single application
of Eval(C) to the behavior
f := x 7→¬eval(x, x),
(5)
evaluated at its own code, produces a formula B with B ≃C ¬B: a negation
fixed-point inside C. The diagonal step is Curry-style self-application
[Curry and Feys, 1958; Sørensen and Urzyczyn, 2006]; in the full-frame
presentation, Eval(C) also has the weak point-surjectivity shape familiar
from Lawvere’s Fixed-point Theorem [Lawvere, 1969], with closure
equivalence replacing equality.
The second completeness principle is decisional. Excluded-middle com-
pleteness asserts global bivalence for the regulator:
LEM(C) ≜∀A ∈Form. C(A) ∨C(¬A).
(6)
Eval(C) closes the regulator under reflection; LEM(C) demands a global
classification; their interaction under diagonalization is the obstruction:
Eval(C) ∧MP(C) ∧Cons(C) ∧LEM(C)
=⇒
⊥.
(7)
The boundary is sharp at LEM(C): a Boolean decision Dec(C) implies
LEM(C) and is obstructed along with it, whereas refutation imposes no
coverage requirement and survives.
The obstruction is hence excluded-
middle completeness itself, not negative information as such.
This is worth contrasting with classical Curry’s Paradox, where a syn-
tactic fixed-point B ↔(B →⊥) yields ⊥by assuming B and using it twice
inside one assumption block, a use of contraction that substructural logics
block [Ono, 2003; Restall, 2000]. Section 3 discharges no assumption: every
modus-ponens premise is a top-level accepted fact, and LEM(C) supplies
one disjunct of C(B) ∨C(¬B) as such a fact, freely reusable. Reuse is not
eliminated but relocated: an accepted fact of C is an ordinary metatheoretic
proposition, and the metatheory contracts hypotheses freely. What is avoided
is contraction within the object logic, so C need not be contraction-closed.

SUBSTRATE
2.1
OBJECT SYNTAX
Definition 2.1.1 (Regulator theory). A regulator theory is the syntactic
data that generates a concrete primitive regulator. It is a pair
R ≜(π, T),
(8)
where π is a regulator logic profile and T is an axiom set of object formulas.
A context Γ supplies local assumptions but is not part of the theory itself.
Together, R and Γ induce the closure predicate
CR,Γ(A) ≜R; Γ ⊢A,
(9)
where ⊢is checked derivability of A from Γ in R. Hence a “regulator theory”
serves as a checked syntactic source of accepted formulas, rather than as a
model, truth predicate, or semantic environment.
Definition 2.1.2 (Syntax). The object language is a minimal propositional
language, containing only falsity and implication.
A, B ::= ⊥| A →B,
¬A ≜A →⊥.
(10)
There are no atoms, no propositional variables, no quantifiers, no modalities,
and no substitution. We write Form for the type of formulas; formulas are
finite trees over {⊥, →}.
Lemma 2.1.3 (No formula is its own negation). For our regulator
∀A ∈Form,
A ̸= ¬A holds.
(11)
Proof. ¬A = A →⊥has A as a proper subterm. A finite tree cannot be a
proper subterm of itself.

2.2
PRIMITIVE REGULATION WITH CLOSURE
Definition 2.2.1 (Primitive regulator with closure predicate). A primitive
regulator on the formula language is a predicate
C : Form →Prop.
(12)
We write C▷A, or equivalently C(A), read “C regulates A” or “A is accepted
by C.” Primitive regulation is the bare acceptance layer: no semantic truth,

model satisfaction, arithmetic provability, or computation model is built in,
and C(A) is treated as a mere proposition.
Remark 2.2.2. We also call C a closure predicate when emphasizing closure
properties. Every closure-level result below is parametric in C and never
inspects its definition; regulator-level results follow by specialization.
Definition 2.2.3 (Closure equivalence).
A ≃C B ≜C(A →B) ∧C(B →A).
(13)
Remark 2.2.4. ≃C records what C itself accepts about the implication
arrows between A and B. By Lemma 2.1.3, A = ¬A never holds, whereas
A ≃C ¬A may, depending on C.
Definition 2.2.5 (Modus ponens, consistency, excluded-middle complete-
ness). The three structural properties of C are
MP(C) ≜∀A ∈Form. ∀B ∈Form. C(A →B) →C(A) →C(B),
(14)
Cons(C) ≜C(⊥) →⊥,
(15)
LEM(C) ≜∀A ∈Form. C(A) ∨C(¬A).
(16)
Remark 2.2.6. MP(C) is the only object-level inference rule the obstruction
uses: detachment alone, no deduction, cut, or further schema. Cons(C) is the
external guard against C(⊥), and LEM(C) is excluded-middle completeness:
C accepts every formula or its negation.
Definition 2.2.7 (Decision and refutation). A decision for C is a Boolean
classifier whose two branches are sound:
Dec(C) ≜∃d : Form →{tt, ff}.
∀A ∈Form. d(A) = tt →C(A),
∀A ∈Form. d(A) = ff →C(¬A).
(17)
A refutation is the negative-only counterpart, with no constraint on the false
branch:
Ref(C) ≜∃r : Form →{tt, ff}.
∀A ∈Form. r(A) = tt →C(¬A).
(18)
Remark 2.2.8. Boolean totality of d delivers LEM(C) pointwise, so
Dec(C) =⇒LEM(C), the only implication used below. The converse and
the weakness of Ref(C) are definitional observations, not formal separations.
Ref(C) is realizable by the always-false classifier; its certified non-obstruction
endpoint (Theorem 3.3.5) is stated at the regulator level.

themselves up to closure equivalence; self-application of such a code pro-
duces a formula with closure-level fixed-point status, without syntactic
self-reference or arithmetic encoding.
The obstruction arises at the interaction between two structures. Eval(C)
establishes reflection closure; LEM(C) imposes a binary verdict on every
formula. Because the diagonal B is built from C’s own evaluative content,
any LEM(C)-verdict on B propagates through the fixed-point arrows by
modus ponens, yielding C(⊥), which Cons(C) rules out.
The upshot is therefore neither an inconsistency in excluded middle nor
a defect in evaluation completeness, taken in isolation. Rather, bivalence is
incompatible with reflection in the presence of detachment and consistency,
once evaluation is internalized up to closure equivalence. The roles of the
assumptions separate sharply: Eval(C) is generative and structural; LEM(C)
is global and classificatory; MP(C) is local compositionality; Cons(C) enforces
external soundness. Only their interaction under reflection produces the
obstruction.
5.2
WITNESSING SCHEMATA
Read as witness patterns, Eval(C) and LEM(C) share a common ∀. ∃. profile.
Eval(C) is
∀f : Code →Form. ∃c. ∀x. eval(c, x) ≃C f(x),
(32)
which produces, for every formula-valued behavior, a code witnessing it up
to closure equivalence. LEM(C) (Definition 2.2.5) is the disjunction
∀A ∈Form. C(A) ∨C(¬A);
(33)
rewritten existentially, this is
∀A ∈Form. ∃ε ∈{A, ¬A}. C(ε).
(34)
Both schemata then have the same ∀. ∃. profile: ∀· . ∃· . (closure-relation).
They differ in the size of the witness set per index: Eval(C) draws witnesses
from the full code type Code, while LEM(C) draws from the binary set
{A, ¬A}. Among binary-witness schemas of this profile indexed over Form,
LEM(C) is the minimal informal choice that commits C on every formula.
Read this way, Eval(C) and LEM(C) are not unrelated assumptions but
two instances of the same totality discipline applied along complementary
axes. Eval(C) is reflective totality on the generation side: every behavior is
named. LEM(C) is reflective totality on the classification side: every formula
is committed. LEM(C) ranges over all formulas; Eval(C) ranges over all
formula-valued behaviors, including the diagonal behavior used to construct
B. Neither schema exempts the diagonal. The pairing analyzed here imposes
both totalities at once, and the diagonal collapse of Theorem 3.2.4 is its
formal cost.
This places the obstruction within a wider family. A trivial-witness
variant,
∀A. ∃ε ∈{A}. C(ε),
(35)
is inconsistent already at A := ⊥under Cons(C). The empty-witness variant,
∀A. ∃ε ∈∅. C(ε), is unsatisfiable. LEM(C) sits between these as the binary-

witness instance that imposes a real classification while remaining satisfiable
in isolation, and is the principle whose interaction with Eval(C) we analyzed.
5.3
REFUTATION ASYMMETRY
Ref(C), as a conditional principle rather than a totality, sits outside the
witnessing family of Section 5.2. Nothing forces r to fire on the diagonal
B, and the always-false classifier shows that consistency is compatible with
the principle inhabited vacuously. The asymmetry is therefore not about
negative information as such, but about the absence of a uniform existential
commitment indexed by every formula. What the obstruction rules out is
the totality discipline applied to classification; what it leaves untouched is
the implicational discipline of refutation.
5.4
CLASSICAL COMPARISONS
The result can be read as a generalization of classical diagonal phenomena,
stripped of their specific encoding mechanisms. The present closure layer is
purely propositional: its only primitives are a closure predicate, an evalua-
tion frame, and detachment. The common core is representability-induced
reflection together with global bivalence under detachment; consistency then
converts the resulting internal collapse into contradiction. Compared with
a purely categorical presentation, the formulation here is operational: the
interface is expressed through codes, evaluation, and a closure predicate.
Rather than a literal subsumption of the classical theorems, Theorem 3.2.4
obstructs LEM(C) as a hypothesis on the primitive regulator, not classical
excluded middle as a metatheoretic axiom.
5.5
OUTLOOK
The regulator-theory framing isolates a transferable acceptance interface,
and the natural next step is to ask whether the obstruction transfers along it.
A reduction from one regulator to another that preserves MP(C), Cons(C),
and the evaluation frame would carry the obstruction with it, so that
obstructedness becomes a property closed under such reductions rather
than a fact about one fragment. Mapping which reductions preserve each
hypothesis, and which break LEM(C) without breaking the rest, would chart
the boundary as a structure rather than a single point.
