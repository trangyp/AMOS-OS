Abstract
We introduce a new measure of robustness for statistical estimators, which we call empirical
sensitivity. An estimator ˆθ has bounded empirical sensitivity if, with high probability over a
dataset X = (X1, . . . , Xn) ∼D⊗n, for any dataset Y obtained by modifying at most ηn points
in X, we have that ˆθ(Y ) is close to ˆθ(X).
We study bounds on this quantity for the prototypical problem of Gaussian mean estimation.
We prove new lower bounds, showing that for any estimator ˆµ which achieves an optimal ℓ2-error
bound of O(
p
d/n), the empirical sensitivity is at least Ω(η +
p
ηd/n). The two terms arise due
to obstructions on the mean and variance (via an Efron-Stein argument) of such an estimator.
We show that this bound is tight up to logarithmic factors, by employing recent results for robust
empirical mean estimation.

Introduction
Robustness is a classic topic of study within Statistics [HR09]. It tries to understand and bound
how much an estimator can fluctuate when data diverges from our assumptions. Such divergences
could arise for innocuous reasons such as model misspecification, or more malicious reasons like an
adversary manipulating or poisoning the dataset.
A popular style of robustness ensures that an estimator maintains accuracy after an adversary
modifies a portion of the input dataset. In more detail, suppose we have an estimator ˆθ for a
parameter θ∗of some family of distributions Dθ. Let X = (X1, . . . , Xn) ∼Dθ∗, and let Y be any
dataset obtained by modifying ηn points in X. Robustness of the estimator ˆθ is then measured in
terms of ∥ˆθ(Y ) −θ∗∥, the distance between the estimator on the contaminated dataset and the true
parameter of interest.
While algorithms satisfying this definition are quite powerful, the notion does not capture
some natural properties we might hope for an estimator to possess. We illustrate by considering
the estimator ˆµ to be the median, and the dataset X to be sampled from a univariate Gaussian
distribution N(µ, 1)⊗n. For an uncontaminated dataset X, with high probability, the median is
well known to achieve the near optimal statistical rate: |ˆµ(X) −µ| ≤O(1/√n). Furthermore, the
median is the canonical example of a robust statistic. For any dataset Y , formed by changing ηn

Motivated by this deficiency, we introduce a new measure of robustness for statistical estimators,
which we call the empirical sensitivity. Informally, we would like for an estimator ˆθ to simultaneously

• Accuracy: Given a dataset X ∼Dθ∗, we have that ∥ˆθ(X) −θ∗∥is small.
• Bounded empirical sensitivity: For X ∼Dθ, and for any dataset Y obtained by modifying ηn

but Y is an arbitrary or worst-case contamination of X. As usual, obtainable guarantees will depend
on properties of the class of distributions Dθ. On the other hand, this can be seen as a stronger
type of robustness: by triangle inequality, an accurate estimator with bounded empirical sensitivity
will also enjoy a bound on ∥ˆθ(Y ) −θ∗∥, the traditional object of study in robust estimation.
The robustness of the estimated value to corruption has been considered before as a worst-case
constraint on a function, notably in the design of differentially private algorithms. To our knowledge,
ours is the first work that studies the interaction of empirical sensitivity with statistical efficiency,
and also the first to explicitly study the average-case notion. See Related Work (Section 1.2) for
more detail.

Related Work
Robust estimation is a vibrant area of study, particularly recent work with a focus on minimax
statistical rates and computational efficiency in multivariate settings, see, e.g., [DKK+16, LRV16]
and [DK22] for a textbook treatment. As already described, empirical sensitivity is stronger than
what is implied by traditional robustness guarantees. One recent work of Chen, Ding, Majid, and
McKelvie [CDMM26] studies differentially private Bayesian estimation, which they reduce to robust
estimation of an empirical mean. While not originally conceived as such, their robust algorithm
provides an estimator with bounded empirical sensitivity for a particular statistic that satisfies the
desired accuracy guarantee (i.e., the empirical mean).
Stability has long been an object of study in statistics and machine learning, see, e.g., [KR97,
BE00]. This generally focuses on understanding how the error of an algorithm changes if one removes
a single point. Our focus instead is on the effect of modifying a collection of points (and also on the
entire output, not just the error). The recent, contemporaneous work of Chakraborty, Luo, and
Barber [CLB26] explores minimax risk when an estimator must have low global sensitivity—that is,
worst-case empirical robustness with respect to a single corruption.
They suggest study of an distributional variant, where the dataset is sampled from a distribution
belonging to some family, as a direction for future work.
Empirical stability also relates to concepts considered in the literature on differential privacy
(DP) [DMNS06]. Many DP mechanisms operate by adding noise calibrated to the worst-case
sensitivity (called the global sensitivity) of a function. (This differs from our notion in that it is
for worst-case neighboring datasets and considers only a single corruption.) The literature also

2.1
Adversary Models
The robustness literature frequently studies the relative power of different adversaries [BLMT22,
BV25, BHMS26, BBKL23, LBK25]. We briefly discuss various adversary models, listed here in
increasing amount of strength.
Definition 2.4. Let a dataset X = (X1, . . . , Xn) ∼D⊗n, where D is a probability distribution. Fix
a corruption fraction η ∈(0, 1) and define k := ⌊ηn⌋to be the number of contaminated points.4
1. Resampling adversary. A subset I ⊆[n] of size k is chosen uniformly at random, and the
samples {Xi : i ∈I} are replaced by fresh independent samples from the same clean distribution
as X. This is the weakest adversary: both the removed points and the inserted points are
stochastic and drawn from the clean distribution.
2. Stochastic adversary. A subset I ⊆[n] of size k is chosen uniformly at random, and the samples
{Xi : i ∈I} are replaced by independent samples from a fixed replacement distribution Q
chosen before seeing X. The replacement distribution may differ from the clean distribution,
but it is not chosen adaptively after observing the realized dataset.
3. Adaptive adversary. After observing the clean dataset X, the adversary may choose both
the corrupted indices and the replacement values arbitrarily, subject only to the constraint
dH(X, Y ) ≤k. This is the adversary encoded by Sf
η (X).

3.2
Mean obstruction
We establish the mean obstruction by separating the analysis into two distinct regimes that
collectively cover the entire spectrum of corruption levels. In the low-corruption regime, where
η = O(1/√n), our argument relies on bounding the χ2-divergence between two distinct adversaries:
a local shift of exactly k random samples by a constant δ (which represents a valid adversarial
η-contamination), and a global shift of all samples by ηδ (which represents a genuine change
in the underlying parameter). Because the χ2-divergence between these two distributions scales
as O(k2δ4/n), choosing a sufficiently small δ renders the adversarial contamination statistically
indistinguishable from a true parameter shift. Since any MSE-accurate estimator is mathematically
forced to track the global shift to maintain its low average risk, this indistinguishability ensures the
estimator is similarly displaced by the local corruption, yielding the Ω(η) lower bound.
For the high-corruption regime, where η = Ω(log n/n), we shift to a coordinate-wise maximal
coupling argument. We couple two Gaussian distributions whose means are separated by exactly η.
By showing that samples drawn from these coupled distributions differ in at most k coordinates with
high probability in this regime, we show that an η-bounded adversary can seamlessly bridge the two
datasets. Since the estimator’s accuracy forces its mean response to be displaced by Ω(η) between
these two parameter values, the empirical sensitivity is similarly bounded below. Crucially, these
