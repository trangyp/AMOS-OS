1. Introduction
In arithmetic dynamics we typically study number theoretic properties of the polynomial or
rational function iteration. In this paper we study the arithmetic of the dynamics of algebraic
functions. Suppose F : P1 ⇝P1 is an algebraic function defined over Q. That is, suppose there is
a polynomial f(x, y) ∈Q[x, y] such that f(x, F(x)) = 0. If degy f ≥2, then F is technically not a
function of x, but we may view it as a multi-valued function. For example, consider F(x) =
√
x3 −1
which is defined by y2 −x3 + 1 ∈Q[x, y]. The presence of the square root in F suggests that even
if p ∈P1(Q), we typically cannot expect F(p) ∈P1(Q). And yet, miracles happen: for example,
F(1) = 0. Our goal is to address the following question.
Question. How many rational points can there be in the orbit of p ∈P1(Q) under an algebraic
function F?
To properly formalize the dynamics of algebraic functions we work with correspondences. A
correspondence F : P1 ⇝P1 defined over a field K is a (possibly reducible) K-algebraic curve
CF ⊆P1 × P1 for which the projections onto each component are finite and surjective. We may
view F(p) as the set of all points q such that (p, q) ∈CF . There is a natural way to compose
correspondences by taking fiber products; see Section 4 for definitions and constructions. Let F n
denote the nth iterate of the correspondence F. The degree of a correspondence is the pair (d, e)
of degrees of the two projection maps. Here is a summarized version of our main result.
Theorem 1.1. Let K be a finitely generated field of characteristic zero.
Let F : P1 ⇝P1 be
a correspondence defined over K with degree (d, e) such that d, e ≥2 and CF 12 is irreducible. If
there exists an n ≥12 for which there are infinitely many p ∈P1(K) such that F n(p) contains a
K-rational point, then F belongs to an explicit list of exceptional correspondences.
This is proved in two parts as Theorem 4.9 and Theorem 4.10; the latter result contains the
detailed list of exceptional cases. To give a sense of this classification, suppose that F is a cor-
respondence satisfying the hypotheses of Theorem 1.1. We show that the normalization of CF is
isomorphic to P1 and let f, g : P1 →P1 be the composition of the normalization with the coordinate
projections. Then after a change of coordinates, and with essentially finitely many exceptions, we
show that f must be a power map, a Chebyshev polynomial, or a (generalized) Latt´es map. The
rational function g must fit into a semiconjugation diagram with f, which highly restricts g.

In Theorem 4.10 we show that CF n is irreducible for all n ≥0, and that the normalization of CF n
has genus 0 for n ≤m, and has genus > 1 for n > m. Thus we may find infinitely many K-rational
points whose first m iterates are all in K, but Faltings’s theorem implies only finitely many will
have K-rational iterates beyond the mth iterate.
This example highlights how Faltings’s theorem allows us to translate infinitely many K-rational
iterates into the purely geometric hypothesis that CF n has an irreducible component of genus at
most one. The main source of tension that we exploit stems from how restrictive it is for a pair
of finite maps to P1 to have an irreducible fiber product with genus at most one. This tension
manifests as the following result.
Theorem 1.2. Let f : C →P1 be a finite degree d map from a smooth, complex, projective curve C
and let g : P1 →P1 be a degree e rational function. If e > 170d −84 and the fiber product of f and
g is irreducible with normalization of genus at most one, then the Galois closure of f has genus at
most one.
Theorem 1.2 is from Chapter 5 of the author’s Ph.D. thesis [Hyd19], which was joint work with
Michael Zieve. Theorem 1.2 is simplified for ease of notation; see Theorem 2.11 and Corollary 2.12
for the full statement and proof.
Maps f : C →P1 with a Galois closure of genus at most one are, in a sense, well-known and clas-
sified. This classification stems from the classification of finite groups of M¨obius transformations,
which are the Galois groups in the genus zero Galois closure case, and the theory of elliptic curves.
In Section 3 we carry out this classification in order to tabulate all the possible ramification types
of such maps (see Table 1 and Table 3) which we require to prove Theorem 4.10. Given how often
these maps arise as interesting exceptional cases, these tables may be of broader interest.
Another tool which we only use in a minor way but deserves to be better known is the following
theorem due to Michael Fried.
Theorem 1.3 ([Fri73, Prop. 2]). Let fi : Ci →D for i = 1, 2 be finite maps between curves. There
are decompositions fi = gihi such that g1 and g2 have the same Galois closure, and the fiber product
of g1, g2 has the same number of irreducible components as the fiber product of f1, f2.
Fried’s statement of this result is quite different but is essentially equivalent to Theorem 1.3.
We provide a proof as Theorem 2.5 via a translation to a result about finite G-sets. Theorem 1.3
gives us a starting point for handling reducible fiber products: If the fiber product of f1 and f2 is
reducible, then the maps f1 and f2 have nontrivial left factors with the same Galois closure.
1.1. Related work. The study of dynamics of correspondences has a long history. For example,
the arithmetic-geometric mean introduced by Lagrange and analyzed deeply by Gauss may be
viewed as the dynamics of the correspondence CF : 4xy2 = (x + 1)2. Within complex dynamics
there is a considerable body of work on correspondences; see for example [Bel23; BT02; Buc25;
Bul92; BP94; Par23; Got23; LM25] and the references therein.
The study of correspondences
from the perspective of arithmetic dynamics is more recent. See Patrick Ingram’s work on con-
structing canonical heights for correspondences [Ing19], studying analogs of post-critically finite
correspondences [Ing17], and on arboreal representations of correspondences [Ing18].
Ideally we would like to have a version of Theorem 1.1 which did not require the hypothesis
that CF 12 is irreducible. However, this requires an analysis of cases where CF n may be reducible
with components of genus at most one. When the projections from CF are polynomials, the recent
resolution of the Davenport-Lewis-Schinzel conjecture by Behajaina, K¨onig, and Neftin [BKN26]
should give one a foothold in this case, but we leave that for the future. See also the work of An
and Diep [AD13].

2. Foundations and fiber products
In this paper, K will always be a field of characteristic zero. With the exception of Theorem 4.9,
we assume K is algebraically closed and the Lefschetz principle allows us to assume without loss
of generality that K = C.
For us a curve C is a smooth, projective scheme defined over K of pure dimension one. Every
such curve is a finite disjoint union of irreducible curves.
Let C denote the category of all
curves with finite maps; we will only be working with finite maps and thus simply refer to them
as maps. The category C is dual equivalent, under the standard algebro-geometric dictionary, to
the category of finite products of finitely generated extensions of K with transcendence degree one
and K-algebra homomorphisms.
2.1. Fiber products. Let f1 : C1 →D and g1 : C2 →D be maps between curves. The fiber
product of f1 and g1 is the potentially singular curve
B := {(p, q) ∈C1 × C2 : f1(p) = g1(q)}.
Let eB →B be the normalization of B, and let g2 : eB →C1 and f2 : eB →C2 be the composition
of the normalization with the natural projections so that f1g2 = g1f2. The curve eB together with
the maps f2, g2 is the categorical fiber product of f1 and g1 in C . When we refer to the fiber
product of maps between curves, we mean this normalized fiber product in C . Abhyankar’s lemma
characterizes the points on a fiber product and their ramification. If f : C →D is a finite map and
p ∈C, let ef(p) denote the ramification index of f at p.
Lemma 2.1 (Abhyankar’s lemma). Let f1 : C1 →D and g1 : C2 →D be maps between curves
and let B denote the fiber product and let h : B →D be the map h := f1g2 = g1f2. Suppose that
(p, q) ∈C1 × C2 is a point such that f1(p) = g1(q). There are gcd(ef1(p), eg1(q)) points r ∈B such
that f2(r) = q and g2(r) = p and eh(r) = lcm(ef1(p), eg1(q)).
Proof. See, for example, Stichtenoth [Sti09, Thm. 3.9.1].
□
Suppose f1 : C1 →D and g1 : C2 →D are a pair of maps between curves. Even if all the curves
are irreducible, the fiber product may be reducible. The next lemma provides one simple sufficient
condition for the fiber product to be irreducible.
Lemma 2.2. Let f1 : C →D and g1 : C′ →D be a pair of finite maps between irreducible curves with
degrees d, e respectively. If d and e are coprime, then the fiber product of f1 and g1 is irreducible.
Proof. Let B be an irreducible component of the fiber product of f1 and g1, and suppose that f2, g2
are maps from B such that h := f1g2 = g1f2. Then deg h is divisible by both d and e, hence by de
since we assumed d and e are coprime. On the other hand, let h′ : B′ →D be the map from the full
fiber product of f1 and g1 to D. By construction, deg h′ ≤de. But h′ factors through h, implying
that deg h′ = de. Thus h = h′ and B = B′, which is to say that B′ is irreducible.
□
2.2. Galois maps. Let f : C →D be a map and define Deck(f) to be the group of all automor-
phisms g : C →C such that fg = f. Let G ⊆Deck(f) be a finite subgroup and let C/G be the
normalization of the quotient of C by G. We say f is Galois with Galois group G if there exists
an isomorphism g : C/G →D such that f = gh where h : C →C/G is the natural quotient map. If
C and D are irreducible, then G = Deck(f), but otherwise G may be a proper subgroup. The Galois
closure of f : C →D is the final Galois map ¯f : C →D which factors through f; the existence and
uniqueness up to isomorphism follows from standard Galois theory of field extensions via duality.
If ˜f : B →D is a Galois map which factors through f and G is the Galois group of ˜f, then
each g ∈G induces an isomorphism g : Cg →C. Then fg := g−1fg : Cg →D is the conjugate
of f by g. The Galois closure of f may be constructed as the component of the fiber product of
all the conjugates of f through which ˜f factors. The following lemma is a direct consequence of
Abhyankar’s lemma.

We say a map f : C →D is evenly ramified if for all p ∈D and all q ∈f−1(p) we have
ef(q) = mf(p). Lemma 2.3 tells us that Galois maps are evenly ramified. The following lemma
provides a converse for rational functions.
Lemma 2.4. A rational function f : P1 →P1 is evenly ramified if and only if f is Galois.
Proof. We only need to prove the forward direction. If f is evenly ramified, then so are all the
conjugates of f. Let ¯f : C →P1 be the Galois closure and suppose that ¯f = fg is the factorization
through f. Then Lemma 2.3 implies that g : C →P1 is unramified. The only unramified covers of
P1 are isomorphisms, hence g is an isomorphism and f is Galois.
□

Theorem 2.5 (Fried). Let fi : Ci →D for i = 1, 2 be maps between curves. There are decomposi-

Theorem 2.5 is the translation of Theorem 2.6 via Galois theory. The following corollary high-
lights how we may use Theorem 2.5 to analyze reducible fiber products.
Corollary 2.7. Let fi : Ci →D for i = 1, 2 be maps between curves. If the fiber product of f1, f2
is reducible, then there are decompositions fi = gihi such that g1, g2 have the same Galois closure
and both have degree at least 2.
Proof. Let fi = gihi be the decompositions provided by Theorem 2.5. Then g1, g2 have the same
Galois closure. If at least one gi had degree 1, then the fiber product of g1, g2 would be irreducible.
However, Theorem 2.5 implies that the fiber product of g1, g2 has the same number of components
as the fiber product of f1, f2, which, by assumption, has at least two components. Thus g1, g2 must
both have degree at least 2.
□
