Robustness of Persuasion to Receiver Preferences∗
Ronen Gradwohl†

Abstract
We study the robustness of Bayesian persuasion to uncertainty about the receiver’s
preferences. We analyze two conceptually distinct notions: continuity, in which only
the modeler lacks precise knowledge, but where the model’s predictions are nonethe-
less accurate; and robustness, in which the sender also lacks precise knowledge, but
where the outcome is insensitive to this ignorance. We model preference uncertainty
as infinitesimally small, non-probabilistic (Knightian) uncertainty, and the sender’s be-
havior as either minimizing the regret or maximizing the minimum utility. We show
that continuity holds if and only if robustness holds, and that both notions are generic.
Thus, while some instances of Bayesian persuasion are fragile, typical instances are
both continuous and robust with respect to a small amount of ignorance.

Introduction
Bayesian persuasion, introduced by Kamenica and Gentzkow [15], is one of the most widely
studied models in microeconomics. This is most likely due to its simplicity and its appli-
cability to various domains in economics, law, political science and more [14]. In its basic
form, there are two agents: one agent, the ‘sender’, has access to information about a state
of the world, but takes no action. Instead, the sender shares his information, partially and
strategically, with a second agent, the ‘receiver’. The receiver then takes an action that
determines the utilities of both agents. A main assumption in Bayesian persuasion is that
the sender can devise and commit to his information-sharing procedure before he observes

the realized state. The central observation, dating back to work by Aumann et al. [3] on re-
peated zero-sum games with incomplete information on one side, is that the maximal utility
of the sender is equal to the value of the concavification of his indirect utility function—the
implied utility function given that the receiver best-replies to his updated belief.
How robust is this observation, and to what extent does it depend on strong modeling
assumptions? In the so-called Wilson Doctrine [26], Wilson argues that “Game theory has
a great advantage in explicitly analyzing the consequences of trading rules that presumably
are really common knowledge; it is deficient to the extent it assumes other features to be
common knowledge, such as one agent’s probability assessment about another’s preferences
or information.” However, the Bayesian persuasion model does assume that the receiver’s
preferences are common knowledge. In this paper we ask whether this assumption is crucial
for the model’s main observation to hold, or whether the model is robust.
More specifically, we consider two conceptually distinct notions of robustness. The first
notion, which we call continuity, supposes that the sender and receiver do have complete
knowledge of all features of their interaction, but that the analyst or modeler lacks some
knowledge. The model is continuous if, despite the lack of complete knowledge, the modeler
can nonetheless accurately predict the outcome of the interaction [13].
The second notion, which we henceforth refer to as robustness, is more along the lines of
the Wilson Doctrine, and supposes that the sender also does not have complete knowledge.
The model is robust if, despite the sender’s lack of complete knowledge, the outcome of
the interaction is nonetheless close to the benchmark in which all features are common
knowledge.
Formally, we ask whether the concavification result is continuous and robust when the
precise preferences of the receiver are no longer common knowledge. We assume that the lack
of common knowledge manifests as infinitesimal ignorance—specifically, that for any action
and state, the receiver’s utility is only known to be within some small interval, without any
distributional assumptions (e.g., ambiguity or Knightian uncertainty). The Bayesian persua-
sion model is continuous if the maximal utility attained by the sender—the concavification
value—can be (approximately) predicted by the modeler even if the modeler only knows the
receiver’s utilities to within such small intervals.
In order to study robustness, we first elaborate on the sender’s strategy in light of his ig-
norance. To model this strategy we invoke two paradigms from decision theory [22], max-min
utility and regret minimization. A max-min sender chooses a strategy that maximizes her
worst-case utility. The sender’s regret is the difference in utility between the actual decision
made and what would have been the optimal decision in hindsight. Regret minimization is
the behavioral assumption that the sender chooses her signal policy in order to minimize the

worst-case regret. Under these two paradigms, the Bayesian persuasion model is then robust
if the maximal utility attained by the sender—the concavification value—is also (close to)
what she obtains when she only knows the receiver’s utilities to within small intervals, and
when she chooses her strategy to either maximize worst-case utility or to minimize regret.
Whether or not a particular instantiation of a Bayesian persuasion model is continuous
or robust depends on the parameters of the instance. For illustration, in Section 1.2 we
analyze two examples, one of which is both continuous and robust and the other of which is
neither continuous nor robust.
The examples illustrate that continuity and robustness need not hold. They also illus-
trate that, although the two notions are conceptually distinct, there appears to be a tight
connection between them. Our first result is that this is always the case: in Theorem 1 we
show that continuity holds if and only if robustness holds. Furthermore, in Theorem 2 we
show that continuity and robustness hold generically.
1.1
Related literature
The motivation for this work can be traced back to the literature on the robustness of equi-
libria in incomplete information games [13, 25, 21]. In this line of papers, the authors study
whether the introduction of a small amount of uncertainty about players’ higher-order beliefs
changes the modeler’s predictions about the equilibrium—what we call continuity. Conti-
nuity with respect to various model assumptions has also been studied in communication
games, and specifically in cheap talk games [6, 7, 2, 24]. In our study, we focus on a different
form of communication game, Bayesian persuasion [3, 15, 14].
Since its introduction, many variants of the Bayesian persuasion setting have been stud-
ied, including ones where the assumption that the sender’s knowledge is superior to that
of the receiver is relaxed. Several contributions can be interpreted as studying the conti-
nuity and robustness of Bayesian persuasion with respect to different relaxations, including
variations in the receiver’s private information, the receiver’s prior, the receiver’s level of
rationality, the sender’s credibility, and the receiver’s utilities.
For example, Matyskov´a and Montes [20] study an environment in which the receiver
can acquire private information at a cost. The information structure of the receiver’s private
signal is governed by a cost parameter which is known to the sender. The authors show that
as the cost tends to infinity—and so the receiver becomes almost uninformed—the sender can
attain the same expected utility as in the baseline Bayesian persuasion model. This provides
a continuity result with respect to the receiver’s private information. In Hu and Weng [11],
the receiver is privately informed about a binary state, but the sender does not know the
3

receiver’s information structure. They characterize the optimal information structure for an
ambiguity-averse sender with a max-min expected utility function. They show that, when the
receiver’s private information becomes arbitrarily weak, the sender can guarantee an expected
utility arbitrarily close to the baseline Bayesian persuasion model. This can be interpreted as
a robustness result with respect to the receiver’s private information. Finally, Gan et al. [10]
study the robustness of Stackelberg games (which are closely related to Bayesian persuasion)
to slightly sub-optimal receiver behavior, as well as algorithms for computing robust sender
strategies. Relatedly, Yang and Zhang [27] study the computational aspects of Bayesian
persuasion under such slightly sub-optimal receiver behavior.
Other papers also study variations of Bayesian persuasion in which the sender lacks some
information about the receiver. Dworczak and Pavan [8] assume that the sender is concerned
that her belief about the environment may be false. Specifically, the sender is uncertain
about the exogenous sources of information the receiver may learn from, and about strategy
selection. In Kosterina [16] the sender is ignorant about the receiver’s prior, and takes the
adversarial approach to evaluate each information structure by assuming the worst case of
the receiver’s prior. Zu et al. [28] study a repeated setting where both the sender and receiver
do not know the distribution over the payoff-relevant state. Instead, the sender learns this
distribution over time by observing the state realizations.
A different perspective on continuity and robustness in the Bayesian persuasion setting
is taken by Feng et al. [9], who depart from the fully rational paradigm. The authors first
show that, when the receiver is almost fully rational, an optimal policy for the fully rational
benchmark remains approximately optimal. They then study robust signaling schemes when
the sender is uncertain about the receiver’s rationality level. Another related paper is that of
Lin and Chen [17], in which the authors study generalized principal-agent problems (which
generalize Bayesian persuasion) and where the receiver uses no-regret learning algorithms to
respond to the sender.
In another related paper, Lipnowski et al. [19] study the robustness of persuasion models
to the tie-breaking rule. In addition, the authors show that their model can also be applied
to environments in which the sender has imperfect commitment power (Lipnowski et al. [18]).
The latter can be viewed as a continuity analysis of Bayesian persuasion with respect to the
sender’s credibility.
Finally, our work is related to two papers that also relax the assumption that the sender
knows the receiver’s utility function, and where the sender uses regret minimization in choos-
ing his strategy: Babichenko et al. [4] and Castiglioni et al. [5]. Specifically, Babichenko et
al. [4] study a setting in which the receiver has two actions and there are multiple states. The
authors show that the sender’s complete ignorance, and use of a regret-minimizing strategy,

A Bayesian persuasion model (BP model) is a tuple, G = {Ω, A, µ0, u, v}, where Ωis a finite
set of states, and a state is realized according to the full support prior, µ0 ∈∆(Ω).2 A is
a finite set of actions available to the receiver (he). Let u : A × Ω→R denote the utility

Results
3.1
Preliminary Results
Our first main result shows that sufficiently small ignorance always supports a weaker pre-
diction: the modeler can identify a valid upper bound on the outcome of arbitrary persuasion
mechanisms. In particular, for every type θ sufficiently close to θ0, the sender’s expected
utility at θ is no greater than her optimal expected payoff at θ0, up to an arbitrarily small
error. It follows that in any δ-BP model containing θ0, if δ is sufficiently small, then for
every type θ, the expected utility from any max-min or regret-minimizing policy is at most
only slightly higher than her optimal expected payoff at θ0. Note that the result applies to
arbitrary Bayesian persuasion models. Formally:

3.2
Continuity and Robustness are Equivalent
Our second main result is that continuity and robustness are equivalent:
Theorem 1. Let G = {Ω, A, µ0, uθ0, v} be a BP model. Then
• G is robust when the sender is regret-minimizing if and only if it is robust when the
sender is max-min.
• G is robust if and only if it is continuous.
To prove Theorem 1, we characterize necessary and sufficient conditions for continuity,
robustness when the sender is max-min and robustness when the sender is regret-minimizing.
We first establish sufficient conditions in the next subsection, and then develop the necessary
conditions. We show that the three notions admit the same characterization. Hence, they
are equivalent. The formal proof of Theorem 1 is deferred to Appendix D.4, after the proof
of the necessary conditions.

3.3
Continuity and Robustness are Generic
Our last result is that continuity and robustness hold generically. We treat utility values
{uθ0(a, ω)}a∈A,ω∈Ωas a set of random variables with values in [0, 1]. Let M := |A| be the
number of actions. The total space of utility profiles is thus the hypercube [0, 1]N×M, which
has dimension N × M.
Definition 10. Let U be a property of BP models. We say that U is generic if U holds
almost surely when each utility value uθ0(a, ω) is independently and uniformly distributed.
Note that if U is generic, then U also holds almost surely for every probability measure
P on [0, 1]N×M that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.
We now turn to investigate the genericity of the sufficient and necessary conditions for
robustness and continuity. For this we leverage recent results of Lipnowski et al. [19], who
consider a different property of BP models:
• U [LRS25] : every action which is receiver-optimal at some belief is uniquely optimal at
some other belief.
Lipnowski et al. [19] proceed to show that this property is generic (see their Proposition
2).
The following claim, therefore, suffices to argue that continuity and robustness are
generic:
Lemma 4. Let G = {Ω, A, µ0, uθ0, v} be a BP model. U [LRS25] holds for G if and only if, for
each action a ∈A with Bθ0(a) ̸= ∅, both U1 and U2 hold.
We defer the proof to Appendix E. Note that the sufficient condition is weaker, as it
needs to hold only for the actions induced by a single optimal policy and U1 can be replaced.
We conclude that:
Theorem 2. Generically, the Bayesian persuasion model is continuous and robust.
