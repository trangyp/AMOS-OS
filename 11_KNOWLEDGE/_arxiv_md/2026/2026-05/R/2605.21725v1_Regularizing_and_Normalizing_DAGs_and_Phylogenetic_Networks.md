Regularizing and Normalizing DAGs and Phylogenetic Networks
Marc Hellmuth1, Anna Lindeberg1,*, and Vincent Moulton2
1Department of Mathematics, Faculty of Science, Stockholm University, SE-10691 Stockholm, Sweden
2School of Computing Sciences, University of East Anglia, Norwich, NR4 7TJ, United Kingdom

Abstract
Phylogenetic networks and, more generally, directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) represent hierarchical
structure beyond trees, for instance in the presence of reticulate evolutionary events such as hybridiza-
tion or horizontal gene transfer. A central question is which parts of such graphs are essential with
respect to leaf-observable information, and which parts can be removed without changing this informa-
tion. Resolving this question can lead to principled simplification methods for phylogenetic networks,
such as the recent normalization approach of Francis et al.
In this paper, we study this question from three related perspectives: clusters displayed by a DAG
G, least common ancestors (LCAs) of subsets of its leaf set, and visibility, a path-based property
of vertices. We first introduce an LCA-based simplification procedure called i-regularization. For a
DAG G and i ≥1, the DAG REGi(G) retains precisely those vertices that occur as unique LCAs of leaf
subsets of size at most i, removes the remaining non-leaf vertices by a graph-editing operation ⊖, and
then deletes shortcuts. We show that REGi(G) preserves all such LCAs, is i-lca-relevant, and admits
a cluster-level description: it is regular, i.e., isomorphic to the Hasse diagram of the corresponding
lca-clusters.
We then compare LCA-based regularization with normalization. Using the same ⊖-operator, we
describe the cover construction underlying normalization, identify visible vertices that are neverthe-
less removed, and characterize when regularization and normalization coincide. Together, these re-
sults provide a unified framework for cluster-based, LCA-based, and visibility-based simplifications
of DAGs and phylogenetic networks.
Keywords: Phylogenetic network, Regular network, Normal network, Cluster, Least common ances-
tors

Introduction
Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and rooted phylogenetic networks are widely used to model hier-
archical structure in situations where purely tree-like representations are too restrictive. A classical
example arises in evolutionary biology, where reticulation events such as hybridization or horizontal
gene transfer cannot be captured by trees alone [18]. Beyond phylogenetics, DAG-based models also
appear in areas such as database theory [3], workflow analysis and process modeling [1, 2], or in the
theory of Bayesian and causal networks [19, 23].
A recurring theme in the study of such graphs is to determine which aspects of the underlying
topology can be recovered from “coarse” information derived from the leaves, and how one can sys-
tematically simplify a given DAG or network without destroying the leaf-implied structure of interest.
In phylogenetics, the leaves represent present-day species (or other taxa), while internal vertices en-
code ancestral ones. It is therefore natural to ask which internal vertices are structurally essential and
which are redundant with respect to a specified type of leaf-based information.

Three classical viewpoints on this question are central to this paper.
The first is cluster in-
formation:
every vertex v in a DAG G with leaf set X induces a cluster CG(v) = {x ∈X |
there is a directed path from v to x in G}, and the resulting set system C(G) records how leaves are
grouped by ancestral vertices. The second is least common ancestors (LCAs): for a subset A ⊆X,
its least common ancestors (when they exist) identify the “lowest” vertices that jointly explain the
ancestry of A. The third is visibility, a path-based notion that captures whether a vertex lies on all
root-to-leaf paths leading to at least one leaf. For example, in Figure 1, taking G to be the DAG N1,
the vertex u induces the cluster {a,b}, the LCA of the vertices a and c is ρ, and the vertex u is visible,
since all paths from the root ρ to the leaf a pass through u.
This leads to a fundamental question: given a DAG or network together with its induced leaf-based
information – such as clusters, LCAs, or visibility – which structural components are essential for
representing this information, and which are superfluous? In other words, can certain internal vertices
be removed or suitably transformed to yield a simpler directed graph that still faithfully captures the
prescribed leaf-based structure? From this perspective, simplifying a DAG or network means defining
a principled transformation that eliminates “irrelevant” substructures – those that do not contribute to
the chosen leaf-based information – while preserving the structural relationships among the leaves.
The main challenge is to formalize this notion of irrelevance and to design simplification procedures
that are canonical, well-behaved, and compatible with the underlying leaf-based representation.
In [11], Francis, Huson and Steel introduced a normalization procedure that transforms a network
N into a normal network NORM(N) by retaining only visible vertices, removing shortcuts, and sup-
pressing vertices of in-degree and out-degree equal to one. More recently, Lindeberg et al. [15, 20]
proposed an alternative simplification approach based on retaining only those vertices that serve as
least common ancestors for specified subsets of leaves. A third, conceptually related, construction is
to consider the Hasse diagram of the clustering system C(G), which yields a canonical DAG that is
minimal in the sense of representing all leaf-induced clusters. Note that there are other approaches
for simplifying DAGs and networks. For example, some focus on producing a tree from a given DAG
or network, see for example [6, 9, 13, 14, 17, 29], and further simplifications that do not necessarily
produce a tree are presented in [22, 27, 31].
Although the normalization, the LCA-driven and the Hasse diagram approaches produce a simpli-
fied version of the underlying DAG or network – essentially by removing vertices deemed “irrelevant”
and rewiring certain arcs – each of them rely on different criteria. Normalization is based on visibility
and thus on path-based properties, whereas the LCA-driven simplification retains vertices according
to their role in explaining leaf subsets. The relationship between a DAG G and the Hasse diagram
H(C(G)) of its cluster system has also been partially explored in [5, 15, 20]; in essence, H(C(G))
retains single vertices v for which CG(v) is contained in C(G) and its arcs reflect how such clusters are
related with respect to set inclusion. Our main objective is to understand the similarities and differ-
ences between these three approaches to simplifying DAGs and networks, and to place them within a
unified conceptual framework.
In the following, we begin with the LCA-driven perspective and develop a systematic “regular-
ization” approach based on the notion of lca-relevance. More specifically, for each integer i ≥1, we
consider the set of vertices that arise as the unique LCA, denoted lcaG(A), for some subset A ⊆X with
|A| ≤i, and we delete all other non-leaf vertices using a simple graph-editing operator ⊖(which gener-
alizes suppression and certain arc contractions). After subsequently removing shortcuts, this yields the
i-regularization REGi(G) of G. Intuitively, REGi(G) is a canonical simplification of G that preserves
the LCA structure of all leaf-subsets of size at most i. Interestingly, as we shall see in Theorem 3.4,
REGi(G) preserves all well-defined LCAs of leaf subsets of size at most i and is always i-lca-relevant.

Moreover, REGi(G) is regular, which means that it is completely determined by its set of clusters.
Note that regular DAGs have been shown to be the only DAGs that can arise from certain simple
models of evolution, see [4, 5] for details.
The fact that REGi(G) is regular next leads us to a cluster-level characterization of i-regularization
in terms of certain lca-clusters: clusters C for which lcaG(C) is well-defined and forced by some
subset A with |A| ≤i (cf. Theorem 3.7). In particular, we show that REGi(G) is isomorphic to the
Hasse diagram of these lca-clusters. This provides a direct link between LCA-based simplification
and the classical cluster/Hasse-diagram viewpoint. In particular, Theorem 3.11 characterizes when the
Hasse diagram H(C(G)) coincides with the fully regularized graph REG(G).
Having considered the i-regularization REGi(N) of a network N in terms of LCAs and clusters
we then look at how REGi(N) compares with the normalization NORM(N) of N. Note that these two
approaches are distinct: For example, for the networks N1 and N2 in Figure 1, the networks REG(N1)
and NORM(N1) coincide, whilst the networks REG(N2) and NORM(N2) do not. In particular, although
both approaches aim to remove “irrelevant” vertices, they rely on fundamentally different criteria:
normalization is based on visibility, a path-based notion that captures whether a vertex lies on all root-
to-leaf paths to some leaf, whereas i-regularization is driven by lca-relevance. To better understand
this, we present a unified framework to compare these two paradigms. Normalization, is based on
two steps. In Step 1, one constructs a directed graph COV(N). We show that COV(N) coincide
with N ⊖vis(N) with its shortcuts removed, where vis(N) is the set of non-visible vertices in N.
We then identify a subclass of visible vertices that are suppressed in Step 2 of normalization (so-
called dispensably visible vertices) and provide criteria for detecting them directly in N. This leads
to an ⊖-based characterization of normalization itself and enables us to characterize when REG(N)
and NORM(N) coincide. Along the way, we derive further structural consequences for normal and
strongly normal networks, highlighting connections between out-degree constraints, lca-relevance,
and properties under the ⊖-operator.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we recall the required background on
DAGs, shortcuts, the ⊖-operator, clusters, and LCAs. Section 3 introduces i-regularization, proves its
basic properties, and characterizes REGi(G) via lca-clusters and Hasse diagrams. Section 4 discusses
normal and strongly normal networks and relates their structure to LCA relevance. In particular, we
describe how normal networks behave under i-regularization, that is, under the transformation N 7→
REGi(N). In Section 5 we then develop the framework for the comparison of network regularization
and normalization, derive ⊖-based formulas for COV(N) and NORM(N), and identify conditions under
which regularization and normalization agree. Finally, in Section 6 we conclude with a brief discussion
of some future directions.

Preliminaries
In what follows, X will always be a finite non-empty set and X(i) the set of non-empty subsets of X
of size at most i. Moreover, A ∪· B denotes the disjoint union of two sets A and B. A collection T of
non-empty subsets of X is a hierarchy if X ∈T, {x} ∈T for all x ∈X, and for all A,B ∈T, we have
A∩B ∈{/0,A,B}.
A directed graph G = (V,E) is an ordered tuple with non-empty vertex set V(G) := V and arc
set E(G) := E ⊆V ×V. We sometimes use u →v to refer to the the arc (u,v) ∈E(G) and u ⇝
v to denote a directed uv-path in G. We put outdegG(v) := |{u ∈V : (v,u) ∈E}| and indegG(v) :=
|{u ∈V : (u,v) ∈E}| to denote the out-degree and in-degree of a vertex v, respectively. A vertex v
with outdegG(v) = 0 is a leaf of G and a vertex v with indegG(v) = 0 is a root of G. A hybrid is a
vertex v with indegG(v) ≥2. Note that leaves might also be hybrids. A directed graph G is phylogenetic
if it does not contain a vertex v such that outdegG(v) = 1 and indegG(v) ≤1.
Directed graphs G without directed cycles are called directed acyclic graphs (DAGs). If G is a
DAG with leaf set X, then G is a DAG on X. A network is a DAG with a unique root. A (rooted) tree is
a network that does not contain any hybrid vertices. A DAG G is separated if all hybrids v in G have
outdegG(v) = 1 [16].
Let G be a DAG. We write v ⪯G w if and only if there is a directed wv-path in G. If v ⪯G w and
v ̸= w, we write v ≺G w. If u →v is an arc in G, then v ≺G u and we call v a child of u and u a parent of
v. In a DAG G where u ⪯G v, we call u a descendant of v and v an ancestor of u. If u ⪯G v or v ⪯G u,

Two DAGs G and H are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism between G and H, i.e., a bijective
map ϕ : V(G) →V(H) such that (u,v) ∈E(G) if and only if (ϕ(u),ϕ(v)) ∈E(H). If this is the case,
we write G ≃H.
An arc e = (u,w) in a DAG G is a shortcut if there is a directed uw-path that does not contain
the arc e [8, 21]. A DAG without shortcuts is shortcut-free. If F ⊆E is the set of all shortcuts of
G = (V,E), then we put G−:= (V,E \F).
Lemma 2.1 ([20, L. 2.5] and [30, L. 7.2]). Let G be a DAG on X. Then, G−is a shortcut-free DAG
on X such that, for all u,v ∈V(G), we have u ⪯G v if and only if u ⪯G−v. Moreover, if G and H are
shortcut-free DAGs such that V := V(G) = V(H) and, for all u,v ∈V, it holds that u ⪯G v if and only
if u ⪯H v, then G = H.
We continue by summarizing important concepts from [20] where, in particular, the following
⊖-operator is studied.
Definition 2.2 ([25]). Let G = (V,E) be a DAG and v ∈V. Then G ⊖v = (V ′,E′) is the directed
graph with vertex set V ′ = V \ {v} and arcs (p,q) ∈E′ precisely if v ̸= p, v ̸= q and (p,q) ∈E,
or if (p,v) ∈E and (v,q) ∈E. For a non-empty subset W = {w1,...,wℓ} ⊊V, define G ⊖W :=
(...((G⊖w1)⊖w2)...)⊖wℓ. For notational reasons, put G⊖/0 = G.
In simple terms, the directed graph G ⊖v is obtained from G = (V,E) by deleting the vertex v
together with all incident arcs, and then adding an arc p →q for every parent p of v and every child
q of v. If v is a leaf or a root, we simply remove v and its incident arcs. If v has in-degree one and
out-degree one in G, then G⊖v is well-known as the directed graph obtained from G by suppressing
v. More generally, if v has a unique child u, then G⊖v can be viewed as contracting the arc v →u to
the single vertex u. Similarly, if v has a unique parent u, then G⊖v coincides with contracting the arc
u →v to u. Hence, the ⊖-operator unifies and generalizes several common graph-editing operations
on DAGs or networks.
It is also worth noting that the definition of G ⊖W is motivated by the fact that the ⊖-operator
is commutative. This fact is mentioned –– without proof –– in [20, 25]. For completeness, we shall
prove this in the appendix of the paper.
Lemma 2.3. For all DAG G and distinct vertices u,v ∈V(G) it holds that (G⊖v)⊖u = (G⊖u)⊖v.
Proof. The proof is straightforward but tedious and, therefore, outsourced to Appendix A.
Hence, the order of the vertices in W does not affect the result of G ⊖W := (...((G ⊖w1) ⊖
w2)...) ⊖wℓ. It is easily seen that G ⊖W remains a DAG that, in essence, has the same ancestor
relationships as G.
Lemma 2.4 ([20, Obs 5.3]). Let G be a DAG on X and W ⊆V(G)\X. Then, G⊖W is a DAG on X
that satisfies u ⪯G v ⇐⇒u ⪯G⊖W v for all u,v ∈V(G⊖W) = V(G)\W.
Let G be a DAG on X. For every v ∈V(G) we define the cluster of v as the set CG(v) := {x ∈X |
x ⪯G v}. The set C(G) = {CG(v) | v ∈V(G)} comprises all clusters of the vertices in G.
Just as every DAG G on X naturally gives rise to a set system on X, we can also associate to every
set system on X a corresponding DAG on X, as we now explain. The Hasse diagram H(C) of a set
system C on X is the DAG with vertex set C and arc set containing an arc A →B for A,B ∈C if and
only if (i) B ⊊A and (ii) there is no C ∈C with B ⊊C ⊊A. The Hasse diagram H(C) is also known
as the cover digraph of C [5]. Intuitively, DAGs that are indistinguishable from the Hasse diagram of
their set of clusters have much of their structure determined by these clusters. Such DAGs are called
regular, and are formally defined as follows.
Definition 2.5 ([5]). A DAG G = (V,E) is regular if the map ϕ : V →V(H(C(G))) defined by v 7→
CG(v) is an isomorphism between G and H(C(G)).
As we shall see, regular DAGs are closely related to properties involving so-called least common
ancestors, which we define next. For a given DAG G on X and a non-empty subset A ⊆X, a vertex
v ∈V(G) is a common ancestor of A if A ⊆CG(v) i.e. if v is an ancestor of every vertex in A. Moreover,
v is a least common ancestor (LCA) of A if v is ⪯G-minimal among all common ancestors of A. The
set LCAG(A) comprises all LCAs of A in G.

The following definition from [20] captures the notion of a DAG where every vertex is a k-lca
vertex for some k ≤i.
Definition 2.8. A DAG G = (V,E) on X is i-lca-relevant if, for all v ∈V, there is some A ∈X(i) such
that v = lcaG(A).
In other words, G is i-lca-relevant precisely if every vertex of G is a k-lca vertex for some k ≤i.
Hence, if G is i-lca-relevant for some integer i, then G is also j-lca-relevant for all j ≥i. As shown
by the DAG N′′ in Figure 2, the converse of the latter statement is not always satisfied: N′′ is 3-lca-

not. Clearly, all i-lca-relevant DAGs are, in particular, lca-relevant.

Theorem 2.9 ([20, Thm. 4.10]). A DAG G is regular if and only if G is lca-relevant and shortcut-free.

3
Regularizing DAGs
The aim of this section is to provide a systematic way to transform a DAG into a regular DAG while
preserving its least common ancestor (LCA) structure up to some prescribed level. Intuitively, we
would like to remove vertices that do not play any role as LCAs of “small” subsets of leaves. This
leads naturally to a filtration of the vertex set according to which vertices arise as LCAs of subsets of
bounded size, and ultimately to a canonical pruning operation that yields a regular, lca-relevant DAG.
We begin by introducing the necessary notation to make these statements precise.
Recall that X(i) denotes the non-empty subsets of X of size at most i. For an integer i ≥1 and a
DAG G, define the set
lcai(G) := {v ∈V(G) | v = lcaG(A) for some A ∈X(i)} and lcai(G) := V(G)\lcai(G).
In other words, lcai(G) contains each vertex of G that is a k-lca vertex for some k ≤i while lcai(G) is
the complement of that set. Note that X ⊆lcai(G) for all i, since every x ∈X satisfies x = lcaG({x})
for any DAG G on X.
Another simple observation stems from Lemma 2.7, namely, if a vertex v has out-degree one, then
it is not an LCA of any set of leaves. Consequently, vertices of out-degree one are in particular always
elements of lcai(G) for any integer i and any DAG G. The next lemma gives a slight extension of this
result which we will use later.

Regularizing Normal Networks
Normal networks are a class of phylogenetic networks that have grown to be very prominent in the
field [11, 28]; see [10] for a comprehensive overview. They are of particular interest in phylogenetics
because they appear to be more amenable to inference from empirical data while retaining strong
phylogenetic signals encoded through so-called visible vertices. In addition, normal networks enjoy
distinctive mathematical properties that are both structurally robust and biologically meaningful [10].
In this section, we consider what happens to a normal network when it is regularized. In particular, in
case N is a normal network, we show that REG(N) is also a normal network, and characterize when
REG(N) = N (see Theorem 4.11).
We begin by recalling the necessary definitions. Although our primary focus lies on phylogenetic
networks, several technical results are more naturally formulated in the broader setting of DAGs. For
this reason, we develop the theory at the level of DAGs and specialize to networks where appropriate.
Definition 4.1. A child u of v in a DAG G is a tree-child, if indegG(u) = 1. A DAG G on X is tree-child
if every vertex v ∈V(G)\X has a tree-child [7]. A DAG is normal if it is tree-child and shortcut-free
[28].
A common restriction in the phylogenetic literature is that hybrid vertices are required to have a
unique child which is denoted by calling them separated. This convention is used in particular in
the context of normal networks and is typically motivated by temporal considerations, distinguishing
the hybrid event from subsequent evolutionary processes. In our “time-point-free” setting such a
restriction is not required. Moreover, separated networks are structurally incompatible with regularity:
vertices of out-degree one cannot be lca-vertices and thus exclude the network from being regular (cf.
Lemma 2.7 and Theorem 2.9). Within our framework, such vertices are naturally eliminated by the
⊖-operator to obtain REG(N) although normal networks are allowed to contain such vertices. To avoid
the purely technical issue caused by separated vertices, we introduce the following class of DAGs:
Definition 4.2. A DAG is strongly normal if it is normal and does not contain any vertices that have
only one child.
Let N be a network on X with unique root ρ. A vertex v of N is visible (in N) if there exists
a leaf x ∈X such that every ρx-path of N passes through v. In phylogenetics, visibility captures
whether information about an ancestral species can, in principle, be inferred from present-day species
or, equivalently, whether the evolutionary signal associated with a vertex in the past remains “visible”
in the leaves, which constitute the observable entities of today [10].
Let vis(N) denote the set of visible vertices of N and put vis(N) := V(N)\vis(N). Observe that,
by definition, ρ and every x ∈X is a visible vertex of N. The following result provides an interesting
characterization of normal networks.
Theorem 4.3 ([7, Lem. 2]). A network is normal if and only if it is shortcut-free and all its vertices
are visible.

Normalizing versus Regularizing
In [11] the concept of normalization was introduced, that is, the transformation of a given network N
into a normal version NORM(N) of the network that aims to retain the underlying evolution informa-
tion whilst producing a network that has the desirable features of a normal network. Since clustering
systems naturally arise in network inference, REG(N) – by virtue of being regular – also encodes
useful phylogenetic signals through leaf-based information, since every vertex is the unique LCA of
some subset of leaves (cf. Theorem 3.4 and 3.5). It is therefore natural to investigate how REG(N) and
NORM(N) are related, as this could be useful information in practice when using these techniques.
One of the main goals of this section is to characterize those networks for which REG(N) =
NORM(N) holds. In particular, we show that NORM(N) can be fully expressed as (N ⊖W)−for a
particular subset W ⊆V(N) and thus, conceptually, falls within the same paradigm as REG(N). Be-
fore providing the definition of NORM(N), we emphasize that (implicitly), all networks in [11] are
separated, i.e., all hybrids v in N have outdegN(v) = 1. Moreover, no vertex v of the networks used in
[11] satisfies indeg(v) = outdeg(v) = 1. Hence, they are phylogenetic “up to” possibly the root having
out-degree 1. We thus slightly generalize the definition of NORM(N) to arbitrary networks N.
Definition 5.1 ([11, Sec. 2]). Let N be a network. The directed graphs COV(N) and NORM(N) are
constructed as follows.

and v ≺N u holds

(3) NORM(N) is obtained from COV(N) by suppressing all vertices v of COV(N) for which
indegCOV(N)(v) = outdegCOV(N)(v) = 1.

Discussion and Outlook
In this paper, we have introduced and explored a framework for simplifying DAGs, where the simpli-
fied DAGs have the form (G ⊖W)−for certain subsets W of the vertices of G. To be more precise,
in Section 3, we studied the i-regularization REGi(G) of a DAG G, where W is the set lcai(G) of
vertices that are not the unique least common ancestor of any subset of leaves of size at most i. In
particular, we noted that REGi(G) is both i-lca-relevant and regular (c.f. Theorem 3.4). We then pro-
ceeded to describe the clusters of REGi(G) in Theorem 3.7 which, due to regularity, fully determine
the structure of REGi(G). In Section 4, we studied regularizations of normal networks, in particular
showing that regularizations preserve the property of being a normal network (Theorem 4.11). Finally,
in Section 5, we studied the normalization NORM(N) of a network N and showed, in Theorem 5.6,
that NORM(N) = (N ⊖W)−for W = vis(N)∪dvis(N). In other words, the set W contains all vertices
of N that are not visible and all vertices of N that are visible but have in- and out-degree 1 in COV(N)
(where the latter vertices, in particular, can be determined by properties in N itself, see Lemma 5.3).
Moreover, Theorem 5.6 also characterizes those networks N for which NORM(N) and REGi(N) coin-
cide.
It could be worthwhile to investigate how different choices of subsets W affect the simplification
(G ⊖W)−of a given DAG or network G. By the results developed in [20], cf. Observation 2.10,
the DAG H := (G ⊖W)−always preserves the ⪯G-relation i.e. u ⪯H v if and only if u ⪯G v for
all u,v ∈V(H). Consequently, C(H) ⊆C(G), provided that W contains no leaves of G. Thus, the
simplification H never introduces clusters that were not already present in G.
In another more general direction, in [10, p.4], Francis states
“The normalization captures the key information in any given network: the informa-
tion that is in fact reconstructable, that is, the vertices and edges whose evolutionary signal
may be visible in the leaves. If one has data that evolved from a process on a network, re-
constructing the normalization of the network is the sweet spot: both the most information
reasonable to hope for, and the most that can practically be obtained.”
18


## Page 19


This provides a compelling argument for the normalization procedure in particular, and for normal
networks more generally. Although normalization is designed to remove vertices that are not visible,
visibility is a path-based notion of relevance that depends on the topology of the entire network. From
the perspective of data-driven simplification, it may therefore not be always clear which observable
data would directly support such a topological property, especially when the underlying network is
unknown. For example, depending on the data at hand, it is conceivable that one may be able to infer
the existence of a vertex v with a particular cluster C = CN(v), even if no vertex u with CN(u) = C is
visible in the network N. Such a cluster would disappear under normalization, since no vertex with
cluster C would be retained; cf. Theorem 5.5(3).
In general, such considerations could be very important when trying to take into account the data
when simplifying networks. For example, there is evidence that relationships between LCAs can, to
some extent, be inferred from so-called best hits [12, 26]. More precisely, pairwise similarities between
genomic sequences associated with leaves a, b, and b′, where a belongs to some species A and b,b′
belong to a species B distinct from A, may support conclusions of the form lcaN(a,b) ⪯N lcaN(a,b′)
even without prior knowledge of the structure of the underlying network N. The vertices lcaN(a,b)
and lcaN(a,b′) need not be visible, however, and may therefore be removed by the normalization
NORM(N). Thus, it may be useful to consider more general simplifications of the form (N ⊖W)−,
where W need not contain all non-visible vertices. Such a choice of W allows one to retain non-visible
vertices whose relevance is nevertheless supported by the available data, for instance because they
induce observed clusters or occur as unique LCAs of pairs, or more generally subsets, of leaves.
In summary, the main point is that, depending on the type of data under consideration, there may
be different notions of what constitutes the key information of a network and, consequently, of what
makes a vertex relevant for simplification. Thus, network simplifications should aim to retain those
vertices whose existence is supported, or witnessed, by the available data. If the relevant signal is
visibility, then this requires information about paths from vertices to leaves. If the data are clusters,
then vertices v with CN(v) among the supported clusters should be retained, since their relevance is
witnessed by the cluster system. Similarly, if the data support the existence or relative position of
LCAs of pairs of leaves, then the corresponding LCA vertices should be retained. In this sense, the
appropriate simplification of a network should be guided by the kind of evolutionary signal present
in the data, rather than by a single universal notion of vertex relevance and, indeed, this information
could also be useful for the more challenging problem of constructing a network in the first place.

Proof of Lemma 4.6. Let G be a normal DAG on X and put U := {v ∈V(G) | outdegG(v) = 1}. Let
v ∈U and denote with c the unique child of v. By Lemma 2.4, G ⊖v is a DAG on X. We start with
showing that G ⊖v remains normal. Since G is tree-child and since c is the unique child of v it must
be a tree-vertex, i.e., indegG(c) = 1, which implies that v is the unique parent of c.
We continue with showing that G⊖v remains tree-child. Let u ∈V(G⊖v)\X. We consider now
the following cases for u: (i) u = c and (ii) u is a parent of v and (iii) u ̸= c and u is not a parent of v.
Case (i): u = c. Since G is tree-child and u /∈X, u = c has a tree-child c′ in G. Since the children
of c in G⊖v are are precisely the children of c in G it follows that c′ is a child of c in G⊖v. Moreover,
as v is the unique parent of c and u ̸= v, it follows that c′ is not a child of (and since G is a DAG also
no parent of) v. Hence, the out-degree and in-degree of c′ in G is the same as in G ⊖v, and we can
conclude that c′ remains a tree-child of u = c in G⊖v.
Case (ii): u is a parent of v in G. If u is the unique parent of v in G, then u is the unique parent of c
in G⊖v, in which case c becomes the new tree-child of u in G⊖v. If u is one of at least two parents,
then v is not a tree-child of u in G since indegG(v) > 1. However, u must have a tree-child c′ in G. As
v is the unique parent of c it follows that c ̸= c′ and, therefore, c′ cannot be a child of v in G. Moreover,
c′ cannot be a parent of v in G as otherwise the arc u →v would be a shortcut. Hence, the out-degree
and in-degree of c′ in G is the same as in G⊖v, and we can conclude that c′ remains a tree-child of u
in G⊖v.
Case (iii): u is neither a parent nor a child of v. Since G is tree-child, u must have a tree-child c′ in
G. Since u is not a parent of v, it follows that c′ ̸= v and, therefore, c′ ∈V(G⊖v). Since u ̸= v and v is
the unique parent of c, we can conclude that c ̸= c′. It immediately follows, by construction of G⊖v
that the in-degree of c′ in G and G⊖v remain the same, i.e., c′ remains a tree-child of u. In summary,
all vertices u ∈V(G⊖v)\X have a tree-child and thus, G⊖v is tree-child.
