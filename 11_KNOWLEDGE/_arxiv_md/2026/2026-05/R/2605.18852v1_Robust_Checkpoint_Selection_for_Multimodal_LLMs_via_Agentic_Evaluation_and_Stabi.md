Abstract
Checkpoint selection for multimodal large language models (MLLMs) presents
significant challenges when performance differentials are marginal and evaluation
signals are prone to noise. Existing methodologies rely heavily on static bench-
marks or pointwise scoring, which frequently misalign with in-the-wild usage
and lack robust uncertainty estimation, particularly in OCR-heavy scenarios. In
this work, we formulate checkpoint selection as a robust decision problem under
evaluation uncertainty. We propose a multi-stage framework that integrates cu-
rated real-world data, structured LLM-based judgment, and multi-stage ranking
protocols. The evaluation system orchestrates progressive refinement via pointwise
filtering, listwise ranking, and pairwise comparison. To enhance reliability, we
introduce subsampling-based confidence estimation and a percentile-based scoring
formulation that captures distributional characteristics while penalizing tail failures.
Furthermore, we demonstrate that data quality, specifically OCR readability, is a
critical determinant of evaluation validity.
1
Introduction
1.1
LLM Evaluation Methods
Existing research on the evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs) can be broadly categorized
into three distinct paradigms.
Pointwise evaluation (scalar scoring). Pointwise methods assign a discrete scalar score to each
model output using either human judgment or automated evaluators. Recent studies, such as G-
Eval, demonstrate that rubric-guided prompting with advanced LLMs can significantly enhance the
correlation with human judgments in open-ended generation tasks (Liu et al., 2023). Similarly, the
Prometheus framework introduces trained evaluator models capable of fine-grained, rubric-based
assessment (Kim et al., 2023). Earlier research on neural text generation evaluation also emphasizes
the necessity of structured evaluation criteria to improve reliability (Gehrmann et al., 2023). While
efficient and scalable, pointwise approaches remain inherently sensitive to calibration bias and often
struggle to distinguish between models with closely matched performance profiles.

The example in Figure 1 highlights the constraints of pointwise scoring. Semantically equivalent
correct responses (e.g., various phrasings of “$15.12”) receive inconsistent scores, while incorrect
answers frequently obtain non-trivial scores (e.g., 0.40–0.55). Consequently, pointwise evaluation

comparison and final checkpoint selection.
Pairwise and listwise comparison. Relative evaluation methods compare multiple model outputs
under identical inputs. MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena demonstrate that pairwise comparison using
LLM judges can achieve high levels of agreement with human preferences and produce more stable
rankings than pointwise scoring (Zheng et al., 2023). Extensions to listwise ranking further improve
sensitivity by jointly ranking multiple candidates, which is particularly effective when performance
differences are subtle. The example in Figure 1 demonstrates that the listwise method could be more
effectively differentiate model checkpoints than the pointwise method without giving specific scoring
values.
LLM-as-a-judge frameworks. A growing body of work leverages LLMs as automated evaluators
for open-ended tasks (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023). These approaches enable
scalable evaluation across diverse tasks but remain highly dependent on prompt design, evaluation
protocols, and data distribution. Prior studies have identified systematic biases in LLM judges,
including position bias, verbosity bias, and self-preference effects, all of which can skew ranking
outcomes (Zheng et al., 2023).
1.2
Limitations of Existing Methods
Despite rapid progress, current evaluation approaches exhibit several fundamental limitations.
Weak correlation with real-world performance. Most benchmarks are constructed from curated or
synthetic datasets that do not accurately reflect in-the-wild usage patterns. WildBench demonstrates
that evaluation using real user queries leads to stronger alignment with human preferences compared
to traditional benchmarks (Lin et al., 2024). Similarly, recent work emphasizes that widely used
LLM metrics often fail to capture in-the-wild capabilities and user-centered performance (Miller et
al., 2025). This mismatch implies that checkpoint selection based on benchmark performance may
not translate into an improved practical user experience.
Sensitivity to evaluation noise and calibration. Pointwise scoring becomes unstable when perfor-
mance differences are small, while pairwise comparisons can be influenced by prompt design and
judge bias (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023). This issue is particularly pronounced in late-stage
checkpoint selection, where models exhibit only marginal differences.
Lack of uncertainty modeling. Most existing approaches rely on average scores or rankings without
quantifying their statistical reliability. Classical work on model selection shows that validation-based
criteria can be unstable under noisy evaluation signals (Prechelt, 1998; Prechelt, 2002). More
recent work further demonstrates that model selection decisions can vary significantly depending on
evaluation criteria and sampling variability (Apicella et al., 2026). However, similar uncertainty-aware
frameworks remain largely absent in LLM evaluation literature.
Misalignment in multimodal evaluation. For multimodal LLMs, existing benchmarks often fail to
capture in-the-wild complexity. Surveys highlight limitations such as narrow task coverage, a lack of
high-resolution inputs, and insufficient representation of OCR-heavy or multilingual scenarios (Liu et
al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Benchmarks such as MME-RealWorld and OCRBench demonstrate that
current models perform significantly worse on realistic tasks than on curated benchmarks, exposing a
gap between theoretical performance and practical usability (Zhang et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2025).

Objective and Proposal
Motivated by these limitations, we address the following practical research question:
How can we reliably select the optimal checkpoint when evaluation signals are noisy, benchmark
distributions are misaligned with real usage, and performance gaps between models are subtle?
Specifically, we focus on aligning evaluation with diverse real-world data distributions, improving
robustness under narrow performance margins, introducing confidence-aware decision mechanisms,
and supporting multimodal, OCR-heavy, and multilingual scenarios.
2.1
Approach and Novelty
Our methodology diverges from prior work along several key dimensions:
Real-world evaluation. Unlike prior approaches that rely on static benchmarks, we evaluate
checkpoints using the curated real-world data. This design choice is supported by evidence showing
that evaluation on in-the-wild usage data more accurately reflects practical model performance (Lin
et al., 2024; Miller et al., 2025).
Hybrid evaluation pipeline. We combine pointwise scoring for efficient filtering with listwise and
pairwise comparison for refined ranking. This integrates both absolute and relative evaluation signals,
building on prior work in rubric-based evaluation and comparative ranking (Zheng et al., 2023; Liu et
al., 2023; Kim et al., 2023).
Stability-aware checkpoint selection. We introduce repeated subsampling over curated real-world
data to estimate the robustness of checkpoint rankings. Rather than relying solely on average scores,
we measure how consistently one checkpoint outperforms another across various sampled subsets.
This connects model checkpoint selection with classical insights on model selection stability and
uncertainty (Prechelt, 1998; Apicella et al., 2026).
2.2
Problems Addressed
The proposed framework addresses several key gaps in the existing literature, including the mismatch
between benchmarks and real-world usage, instability under small performance differences, the ab-
sence of uncertainty-aware checkpoint selection, and insufficient coverage of in-the-wild multimodal
scenarios.
In summary, while existing LLM evaluation methods provide useful signals, they remain limited
by benchmark misalignment, sensitivity to noise, and a lack of uncertainty modeling. Our work
reframes checkpoint selection as a robust decision problem under in-the-wild usage, introducing a
stability-aware evaluation framework that improves both reliability and alignment with the observed
model behavior.
3
Methodologies
3.1
Overall System Design
We propose a multi-stage checkpoint selection framework that treats model selection as a sequential
decision problem under noisy and distribution-shifted evaluation signals. Rather than relying on a
single static evaluation protocol, the system dynamically allocates evaluation effort across stages of
increasing resolution.

A central evaluation orchestrator interacts between (i) input data, (ii) an LLM-based evaluation
engine, and (iii) ranking and confidence estimation modules (see Figure 2). At each stage, the
controller determines whether the current evidence is sufficient to select a checkpoint or whether
additional, more discriminative evaluation is required. This formulation reflects two core challenges
in multimodal LLM post-training. First, differences among late-stage checkpoints are often small
relative to evaluation noise, making direct comparison unreliable. Second, evaluation quality is
strongly dependent on the underlying data distribution, particularly for OCR-heavy and multilingual
scenarios where both perception and reasoning are required. The proposed framework addresses these
challenges by combining data-aware evaluation, relative ranking, and uncertainty-aware decision
making within a unified system.
3.2
Data Pipeline and Quality-Aware Curation
Evaluation is conducted on real-world-style data rather than static benchmarks to ensure alignment
with in-the-wild usage conditions. Real-world data are curated to better reflect practical usage
scenarios compared to standard open source (OSS) benchmarks (e.g., Chang et al., 2025). However,
raw data introduces significant challenges in multimodal settings, particularly due to the presence of
OCR-heavy inputs with highly variable quality.
Empirical analysis of the initial dataset reveals that, although a large proportion of samples contain
textual information embedded in images, many OCR regions are severely degraded. In numerous
cases, text is blurred, occluded, or distorted to the extent that even human evaluators cannot reliably
determine correctness. This creates a critical ambiguity: model responses may appear plausible, yet
cannot be verified as grounded or hallucinated. Under such conditions, both human judgment and
automated evaluation become unreliable.
To address this issue, we introduce a quality-aware curation strategy that preserves practical complex-
ity while ensuring evaluability. Specifically, the curated dataset maintains challenging characteristics
such as variable text styles, sizes, and layouts, multilingual content, occlusion, rotation, and scattering,
as well as long-tail visual patterns. At the same time, it filters out samples whose visual signal is too
degraded to support meaningful judgment. The goal is not to simplify the task, but to ensure that
evaluation reflects verifiable model behavior rather than noise induced by data corruption. This is
particularly important for OCR-dependent tasks, where the boundary between perception failure and
hallucination must remain observable.

3.3
Evaluation Framework and Rubric Design
Model responses are evaluated using a structured LLM-as-a-judge rubric tailored for multimodal
visual question answering. The rubric emphasizes dimensions that directly impact practical usability,
including: 1) content understanding and relevance; 2) factual accuracy and visual grounding; 3)
response clarity and completeness; 4) appropriate handling of unsupported requests; and 5) explicit
detection of hallucinations and errors.
A central design principle is that groundedness takes precedence over stylistic quality. When responses
are otherwise comparable, preference is given to the one more strongly supported by visual evidence.
This is particularly important in OCR and multilingual scenarios, where fluent but ungrounded
responses can otherwise dominate.
The rubric produces both structured judgments and scalar scores, enabling the evaluation framework
to support multiple ranking paradigms. Importantly, evaluation is not treated as a single-pass scoring
function; instead, it serves as a flexible signal generator whose outputs are interpreted differently
across ranking stages.

3.5
Implementation Details
The evaluation pipeline operates on structured multimodal data consisting of image inputs, textual
queries, and model responses. Each evaluation instance contains a checkpoint identifier, visual input
reference, textual query, and generated response. Evaluation is performed using batched LLM-based
judgment under a unified rubric framework.
4
Results and Analysis
We evaluate the proposed checkpoint selection framework in a multimodal LLM post-training setting
using curated real-world data. The candidate checkpoints are derived from fine-tuning variants of a
multimodal LLM-based model. Evaluation is conducted on approximately 2,400 samples, with a
particular focus on OCR-heavy and multilingual scenarios.
To assess robustness, we perform repeated subsampling. In each round, a subset of 500-800 samples
is evaluated, and results are aggregated across multiple runs. All methods use the same underlying
evaluation rubric to ensure a fair comparison.
4.1
OCR Quality and Its Impact on Reliable Evaluation
Evaluation of multimodal models on OCR-dependent tasks is fundamentally constrained by the quality
and verifiability of visual text. When OCR signals are highly ambiguous or unreadable, even human
annotators cannot confidently establish ground truth. Although modern vision-language models may
extract signals beyond what is easily readable, the lack of verifiable references makes it difficult
to determine whether a model’s response is correct or hallucinated. In such cases, models exhibit
divergent behaviors, as shown in Figure 3: GPT-5.3 instant adopts a more conservative strategy
(e.g., acknowledging uncertainty), while Gemini 3 Flash produces more detailed but potentially
hallucinated outputs. LLaMA-3-based multimodal models typically lie between these. As a result,
discrepancies across models often reflect differences in uncertainty handling or verbosity rather than
true capability, introducing noise and bias into evaluation.

Conclusion
In this work, we study checkpoint selection for multimodal large language models under noisy and
distribution-shifted evaluation conditions. We show that conventional approaches based on static
benchmarks and pointwise scoring are insufficient, particularly when performance differences are
small and evaluation signals are unstable. These limitations are amplified in multimodal settings,
where OCR-heavy and visually ambiguous inputs introduce additional uncertainty.
To address these challenges, we propose a comprehensive checkpoint selection framework that
integrates real-world data, structured LLM-as-a-judge evaluation, and a multi-stage ranking process.
An evaluation controller orchestrates progressive refinement through pointwise filtering, listwise
ranking, and pairwise comparison, enabling adaptive allocation of evaluation effort. We further
introduce subsampling-based, nonparametric confidence estimation and a percentile-based scoring
formulation to improve robustness and capture distributional characteristics of model performance.
Empirical results demonstrate that data quality is critical for evaluation reliability, particularly in
OCR-dependent scenarios, and that the proposed framework achieves improved stability and stronger
alignment with human judgment compared to conventional methods.
Overall, this work reframes checkpoint selection as a robust decision-making problem under uncer-
tainty and provides a practical framework for reliable model selection in multimodal LLM systems.
Future work includes extending this framework to broader multimodal tasks beyond OCR-heavy
scenarios, improving the robustness of LLM-based evaluators under adversarial conditions, and
integrating learned reward models or hybrid human–AI evaluation strategies to further enhance
decision reliability.
