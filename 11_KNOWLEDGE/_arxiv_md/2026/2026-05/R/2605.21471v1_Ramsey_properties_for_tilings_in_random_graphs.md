1. Introduction
Ramsey theory is one of the central topics in combinatorics. A fundamental problem in this
field is to determine the Ramsey number of a given graph H, denoted by R(H). This is the
smallest integer n such that every 2-colouring of the edges of the complete graph Kn contains
a monochromatic copy of H. A classical theorem of Ramsey [26] asserts that R(H) is finite for
every graph H, and in 1935 Erd˝os and Szekeres [16] established the first explicit upper bound
for Ramsey numbers. Later, in 1947, Erd˝os [15] gave an exponential lower bound for the case
H = Kk, marking the first application of the probabilistic method. This lower bound has since
only been improved by a constant factor [34]. On the other hand, the upper bound in the
case H = Kk has seen several substantial improvements over the years [12, 31, 35], although
an exponential improvement was achieved only recently by Campos, Griffiths, Morris, and
Sahasrabudhe [10]. Together with Balister, Bollob´as, Hurley, and Tiba [4], they also obtained
new upper bounds for multicolour Ramsey numbers. For further background on Ramsey theory,
we refer the reader to the surveys [13,23].
An interesting variant of the classical Ramsey problem concerns the study of Ramsey numbers
for graph tilings. Given a graph H, an H-tiling is a collection of vertex-disjoint copies of H, and
we write mH to denote an H-tiling of size m. One of the earliest questions in this direction,
posed by Erd˝os [14], asked for estimates of R(mKt) for all t ⩾3. In 1975, Burr, Erd˝os, and
Spencer [9] answered this question in a strong form by establishing the following theorem.
Theorem 1.1 (Burr, Erd˝os, and Spencer [9]). Let H be a graph on k vertices that contains
no isolated vertices and whose independence number is α. There exist constants c = c(H) and
m0 ∈N such that
R(mH) = (2k −α)m + c
for all m ⩾m0.

riving bounds for R(mH, nG) for arbitrary graphs H and G without isolated vertices. However,

The proof of Theorem 1.6 is inspired by the approach of Burr, Erd˝os, and Spencer [9]. To
obtain a large collection of monochromatic vertex-disjoint copies of H in any 2-colouring of the
edges of G(n, p), we search for structures that contain many monochromatic copies of H while
covering relatively few vertices. The main difficulty appears when H is not a clique, since in this
case the bow tie like structures considered by Burr, Erd˝os, and Spencer [9] are hard to find in a
2-colouring of the edges of G(n, p). To address this difficulty, for a graph H and a parameter η,
we introduce a new structure that we call (H, η)-clusters, see Definition 2.5. Considering these
structures is the key new idea in the proof for general H. Our argument relies on the method
of hypergraph containers, which allows us to establish a structural pseudorandom property
satisfied by all vertex subsets of size εn in G(n, p), under any 2-colouring of its edges. This
property, in turn, enables us to identify such (H, η)-clusters (see Section 2.3).
1.2. The method of hypergraph containers. The hypergraph container method, developed
by Balogh, Morris, and Samotij [6] and independently by Saxton and Thomason [32], is a pow-
erful tool for controlling the probability that a random set avoids some forbidden substructure.
In general, the basic container lemma asserts that the sets avoiding these substructures are
“clustered”, in the sense that they are contained in a small collection of vertex subsets C (the
containers), and moreover each C ∈C spans only a few copies of the forbidden structures.
The container method has proven highly effective in a wide range of problems in Ramsey
theory for random graphs. Of particular relevance to this work, it was used by Nenadov and
Steger [25] to provide a concise proof of the 1-statement in Theorem 1.5, and our argument
closely follows their framework. We first establish a container lemma, Lemma 2.11, and then
apply it to prove Theorem 2.7, showing that G(n, p) satisfies a certain property with high
probability.
In other applications, Schacht and Schulenburg [33] established the sharpness of the threshold
for Ramsey properties in certain classes of graphs, while R¨odl, Ruci´nski, and Schacht [30] de-
rived a quantitative version of Theorem 1.5, and Mousset, Nenadov, and Samotij [24] obtained
an upper bound on the threshold function for asymmetric Ramsey numbers. This last result
establishes the 1-statement of a well-known conjecture of Kohayakawa and Kreuter, as formu-
lated in [20]. The 0-statement of this conjecture was recently proved by Christoph, Martinsson,
Steiner, and Wigderson [11].
More recently, Friedgut, Kuperwasser, Samotij, and Schacht [17] developed a unified frame-
work for establishing sharp threshold results for a broad class of Ramsey properties, while
Alvarado, Kohayakawa, Morris, and Mota [1, 2] investigated canonical Ramsey properties of
random graphs. Finally, in a recent breakthrough, Arag˜ao, Campos, Dahia, Filipe, and Mar-
ciano [3] developed a new container theorem yielding an exponential upper bound on induced
Ramsey numbers, thereby resolving a conjecture of Erd˝os from 1975.

Before proceeding to the proof of Theorem 2.1, we first provide an overview of the proof.
Motivated by an idea of Burr, Erd˝os, and Spencer [9], in order to construct a large monochro-
matic K3-tiling in a 2-colouring of the edges of G ∼G(n, p) we first select a maximal family T
of disjoint bow ties, where by a bow tie we mean a subgraph consisting of two monochromatic
triangles of different colours that share a common vertex. The benefit of considering this type
of structure is that a bow tie contains a monochromatic K3 in each colour, while covering only
five vertices.
If this family of bow ties spans a large proportion of the vertices of the graph, we will be able
to find a large enough K3-tiling in both colours. If this is not the case, the next step is to show
that in the remaining vertices of G(n, p) we can find, in one of the two colours, a K3-tiling that
covers almost all of the remaining vertices.
To this end, the following lemma, applied iteratively to the remaining vertices of G(n, p),
yields many disjoint monochromatic copies of K3, not necessarily all of the same colour. We
observe that Lemma 2.2 can be derived from the results of R¨odl and Ruci´nski [27, 28, 29] and
also follows from Theorem 2.4, presented below.
Lemma 2.2. For every ε > 0, there exists C > 0 such that the following holds. If p ⩾Cn−1/2
and G ∼G(n, p), then with high probability, for every 2-colouring of the edges of G and every
U ⊂V (G) with |U| ⩾εn, there exists a monochromatic triangle in G[U].
Let B and R be the families of disjoint blue and red triangles, respectively, obtained by
iteratively applying Lemma 2.2. Recall that we set aside T , a maximal family of bow ties.
If one of these two families, together with the bow ties we set aside, provides enough disjoint

The difficulty here is to construct this new bow tie using the structural properties given by
families B and R. To deal with this, we prove that G(n, p) satisfies a certain property with high
probability. To make this precise, we first introduce the following definition.
Definition 2.3. Let s ∈N. We say that a graph G is (K3, s)-rich if for every 2-colouring of the
edges of G and every pair of disjoint s-sets X, Y ⊂V (G), at least one of the following holds:
(i) there exists a red triangle T in G[X ∪Y ] such that |V (T) ∩X| ⩾1;
(ii) there exists a blue triangle T in G[X ∪Y ] such that |V (T) ∩Y | ⩾1.
We now describe how to find a bow tie assuming this property. If each of the families B and
R spans at least s vertices and G(n, p) is (K3, s)-rich, then there exists either a red triangle
intersecting the vertex set spanned by B or a blue triangle intersecting the vertex set spanned
by R. Without loss of generality, let T be a red triangle that intersects the vertex set spanned
by B, and let T ′ be a blue triangle in B that intersects T. Since T and T ′ have different colours,
they intersect in exactly one vertex, and hence T ∪T ′ is a bow tie.

By adapting the proof of the triangle case to the general setting, we can similarly find families
B and R of disjoint monochromatic copies of H in blue and red, respectively, each of size Θ(n).
Let B be the vertex set spanned by B and let R be the vertex set spanned by R. We then seek
to find an H-tie using the structure of B and R, by finding either a red copy of H that intersects
B in at least α(H) vertices or a blue copy of H that intersects R in at least α(H) vertices. The
difficulty here is that, unlike the triangle case, finding such a monochromatic copy of H does
not guarantee the existence of an H-tie. For instance, if the new copy is red and intersects
B in at least α(H) vertices, these vertices may lie in several different blue copies of H in B.
Observe that this problem does not occur when H is a clique, since in this case α(H) = 1. In
fact, we can prove Theorem 1.6 when H is a clique by following the same steps as in the proof
of Theorem 2.1.
To address the difficulty discussed above for general H, rather than looking for individual
copies of H-ties that yield only one red and one blue copy of H, we introduce the following
broader and more flexible structure.
Definition 2.5. Let H be a graph on k vertices whose independence number is α, and η ⩾0

(H, η)-clusters.

2.3. Constructing an (H, η)-cluster. Unlike the triangle case, the difficulty here is that the
(H, s)-richness property does not directly yield a new (H, η)-cluster. We briefly sketch how
to construct such an (H, η)-cluster under the assumption of that property. For simplicity, we
assume η = 0 and that each of the families B and R above has the same size and spans 2m
vertices.
A cluster can be viewed as a 2-coloured graph F that contains both a red and a blue H-tiling
of size approximately v(F)/(2k −α). To construct such a set, let B and R be the vertex sets
spanned by B and R, respectively. We then reserve subsets B2 ⊂B and R2 ⊂R, each consisting
of the vertex set spanned by half of the copies of H in B and R, respectively. These reserved
copies will later ensure that the red and blue H-tilings in F have the correct size.
Next, we apply the (H, s)-richness property using the m-sets B1 = B \ B2 and R1 = R \ R2
to construct either a red copy of H intersecting B1 in at least α(H) vertices or a blue copy of H
intersecting R1 in at least α(H) vertices. If m is sufficiently large compared to s, this procedure
can be repeated (m−s)/k times, producing (m−s)/k disjoint monochromatic copies of H. For
simplicity, suppose that the process stops because fewer than s unused vertices remain in B1.
For the sake of brevity, we only analyze here the case where (m −s)/(2k) of these copies are
