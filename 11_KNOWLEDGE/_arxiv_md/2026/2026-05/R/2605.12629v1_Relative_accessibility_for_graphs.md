RELATIVE ACCESSIBILITY FOR GRAPHS
JOSEPH PAUL MACMANUS
Abstract. We relativise the Thomassen–Woess definition of accessi-
bility in graphs, defining what it means for a graph to be accessible rela-
tive to a peripheral system. In the case of locally finite, quasi-transitive
graphs, we characterise relative accessibility in terms of a certain sub-
ring of the Boolean ring of the graph, and apply this to show that our
definition agrees with the usual algebraic notion of relative accessibil-
ity in finitely generated groups. This implies, in particular, that rela-
tive accessibility is a quasi-isometry invariant amongst finitely generated
groups, when the quasi-isometry coarsely preserves the left cosets of the
peripheral subgroups. We also deduce a relative variant of Hamann’s
accessibility theorem on graphs with finitely generated cycle spaces.
1. Introduction
The definition of an accessible group is due to Wall [Wal71]. In the lan-
guage of Bass–Serre theory, a finitely generated group G is accessible if it
splits as a finite graph of groups with finite edge groups and vertex groups
with at most one end. Wall conjectured that every finitely generated group
is accessible, suggesting a natural extension of the Grushko–Neumann theo-
rem. Much progress was made on this conjecture, most notably by Linnell
[Lin83] and Dunwoody [Dun85], who verified Wall’s conjecture for groups
with bounded-order finite subgroups and (almost) finitely presented groups,
respectively.
Eventually, a counterexample was discovered by Dunwoody
in the 1993 [Dun93]. That same year, the idea of accessibility was re-cast
in an new, geometric light when Thomassen and Woess presented a purely
graph-theoretical definition of accessibility [TW93]. Their definition runs as
follows.
Definition 1.1 (Accessibility for graphs). Let Γ be a connected, locally
finite graph. We say that Γ is (edge-)accessible if there exists k P N such
that for every pair of distinct ends ω1, ω2 of Γ, there exists an edge-cut
consisting of at most k edges which separates ω1 and ω2.
Thomassen and Woess go on to prove that a finitely generated group is ac-
cessible if and only if some (and hence any) of its Cayley graphs are accessible
graphs. This geometric characterisation has the immediate consequence that
accessibility is a quasi-isometry invariant. Their work opened the door to
questions about how the classical study of infinitely-ended groups can be ap-
plied more generally to quasi-transitive graphs through purely combinatorial
means; see e.g. [HLMR22,Möl96,EGLD24,Mac23].
Within geometric group theory, there is an established algebraic notion
of ‘relative’ accessibility, described in e.g.
[GL17].

(1) Γ is accessible relative to H.
(2) BHpΓq is finitely generated as a G-module.

every vertex is contained in finitely many peripherals; see Definition 3.4. The
proof of Theorem A passes through the ‘cone-off’ of Γ over H, denoted pΓH;

pinning this down constitutes much of the hard work done towards proving
Theorem A.
Using Theorem A, we are able to prove that our definition of relative acces-

groups. This motivates that our definition is, in some sense, the ‘correct’

gave a combinatorial generalisation of Dunwoody’s theorem, in terms of the
cycle space of a quasi-transitive graph [Ham18]. Using our machinery, we
are able to deduce the following relative variant of Hamann’s theorem, by
considering the cycle space of the cone-off of our graph over its peripheral

Our standing assumption on any peripheral system will usually be that
it is thin. However, thinness alone is often not enough to immediately say
anything meaningful, as thin peripheral systems can still be wild. Much of
the hard work done in this section is to establish how we can take a thin
peripheral system H and substitute in its place a ‘nicer’ system H1. What
‘nicer’ here means is not obvious, and we will pass through several iterations
of ‘niceness’ before arriving at something well-behaved enough that we gain
some useful geometric control. The pay-off of all this is that, in the end, our
substitutions ultimately have no effect on the material content of our results,
and our main theorems will still only ask that the hypothesised peripheral
system be thin.
The first iteration of ‘niceness’ we will consider is the following strength-
ening of the thin property, which we call tameness.
Definition 3.5 (Tame peripheral system). Let Γ be a connected graph, then
a peripheral system H is called tame if for any elliptic cut b P BHpΓq we
have that only finitely many H P H intersect both b and b˚.

is thin, in the above sense. The converse is false, even for quasi-transitive

4. Finitely generated groups
We now discuss how our definition of relative accessibility relates to the
usual group-theoretic notion. Throughout this section, we let G be a finitely
generated group, and fix a choice of locally finite Cayley graph Γ. Let P be
a finite collection of subgroups of G, and let
H “ tgH : g P G, H P Pu
denote the set of left cosets of elements of P. Note that since P is finite, this
is a thin, G-invariant peripheral system of Γ.
4.1. Algebraic relative accessibility. We will need to briefly establish
some terminology relating to actions of G upon trees. Our conventions will
mostly follow those set out in [GL17].
Let T be a simplicial tree. We say that T is a G-tree if G acts on T by
automorphisms. We say that this action is without inversions if, given any
g P G, if g fixes an edge then it fixes the two endpoints of said edge. Note
that this condition is fairly inconsequential, as it can always be assured by
simply passing to the barycentric subdivision of T.
Call T non-trivial if
there the action has no global fixed point, and cocompact if T{G is compact.
A G-tree is minimal if there does not exist a proper G-invariant subtree. It
is a standard fact that, since G is finitely generated, every non-trivial G-
tree contains a unique minimal G-invariant subtree, and the action on this
minimal subtree is cocompact. If T is a minimal G-tree without inversions,
then we call T a finite splitting of G. Given two G-trees T1, T2, we say that
T1 dominates T2 if there exists a G-equivariant map f : V pT1q Ñ V pT2q. We
will call such an f a G-map, and often abuse notation by writing f : T1 Ñ T2.
We say that a subgroup H ď G acts elliptically on T if it fixes a vertex. It
is easy to see that T1 dominates T2 if and only if every vertex stabiliser of
T1 acts elliptically on T2. Also, domination is not affected by passing to
subdivisions, or replacing the Ti with G-invariant subtrees.
A G-tree T is said to be P-elliptic if for all H P P, we have that H acts
elliptically on T. The group is said to be one-ended relative to P if every
finite splitting of G is not P-elliptic. The splitting T is said to be terminal
