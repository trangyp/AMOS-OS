ROBUSTNESS OF VALLEY-HALL INTERFACE MODES AGAINST
SHARP BENDING
HABIB AMMARI AND JIAYU QIU
Abstract. It is well known that band inversion across a straight interface in a periodic
medium gives rise to interface modes that are localized near the interface and propagate along
it inside the bulk spectral gap. This phenomenon constitutes the key mechanism underlying
the valley-Hall effect. In this paper, we address the long-standing problem of the robustness
of such interface modes. We prove that, when the interface is bent through an angle of 2π
3 , the
interface modes persist for every frequency in the bulk spectral gap where the group velocity
is non-vanishing, except for a finite exceptional set. We also show that corner-localized modes,
if they occur, can appear only at these exceptional frequencies and have finite multiplicity.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first rigorous mathematical theory of the bending
immunity of valley-Hall interface modes.
Keywords. bent interface, sharp-bend robustness, interface mode, corner-localized mode,
valley-Hall effect, Dirac degeneracy, inversion-symmetry breaking, layer-potential theory on
unbounded interfaces

1. Introduction
1.1. Background. Recent developments in topological insulators have opened new avenues
for guiding waves robustly. For example, it is known from the bulk-edge correspondence (BEC)
principle that the interface between two insulating media with distinct topological invariants,
such as the gap Chern number or the Kane-Mele index, supports unidirectional propagating
modes [18,53]. As a consequence of their topological origin, such interface modes exhibit high
robustness against disorder and impurities present in the system as long as the topological
indices remain unchanged; therefore, these interface modes are referred to as topologically
protected. Remarkably, this unidirectional guiding of waves is also achieved in classical wave
systems, not only limited to condensed matter materials, such as photonic systems [51]. In
that case, wave propagation is governed by the Maxwell operator, or by its scalar reduction in
the case of plane-polarized waves, whose band structure still encodes the relevant topological
information.
However, the application of BEC to achieve robust guiding of waves is limited by the scarcity
of nontrivial topological indices in practical systems. For example, in photonic structures, the
emergence of a nonvanishing Chern number generally requires breaking time-reversal sym-
metry through magneto-optical effects, which in turn necessitates strong external magnetic
fields [51]. This difficulty has motivated the investigation of alternative mechanisms that may
produce robust interface modes without relying on conventional topological indices.
Remarkably, a mechanism of this type arises in the study of the (photonic) valley Hall effect.
Its basic structure may be summarized as follows:
(i) We begin with a periodic medium described by an elliptic operator L that is invariant
under a symmetry group S. The presence of symmetry may enforce special spectral
features, including degeneracies between adjacent Bloch bands. For instance, when
S is generated by R (2π
3 -rotation) and P (inversion symmetry), the band structure
may exhibit conical degeneracies, i.e. the so-called Dirac points, at high-symmetry
quasi-momenta of the Brillouin zone [30].
(ii) Next, if we perturb the system by a symmetry-breaking term by replacing L with
L + Lper,
where Lper does not commute with the inversion symmetry P, the degeneracy is lifted
and a local spectral gap opens in a neighborhood of the Dirac point (see Figure 2 for
an illustration). Importantly, for suitably chosen perturbations, the Bloch eigenspaces
associated with the upper and lower bands near the gap exhibit opposite symmetry
character for the operator L + Lper and L −Lper, as measured, for example, by the
rotation symmetry; see Figure 2(c) for an illustration. This phenomenon is commonly
referred to as band inversion [63].
(iii) Finally, we form a heterostructure by adjoining the two bulk media governed by L+Lper
and L −Lper along an interface, with the governing operator for the interface model
denoted by Lint. It is conjectured and has been supported by both formal and rigorous
analysis in specific settings that a weaker form of bulk–edge correspondence holds in
this context: namely, Lint possesses interface modes whose frequencies lie in the bulk
spectral gap and whose profiles are localized near the interface whenever the adjoining
media exhibit band inversion. In this regime, the interface states may be viewed as

Compared with the classical BEC mechanism based on nontrivial topological indices, the band-
inversion mechanism is often much easier to realize in concrete models. In photonic systems, for
example, it may be produced through suitable geometric deformations of a periodic structure,
without breaking time-reversal symmetry by external magnetic fields [51]. Such geometric
deformations are readily implemented in photonic crystals, making them a promising platform
for robust wave guiding. Remarkably, even though the resulting interface modes do not arise
from a global topological invariant in the usual sense, experiments show that they still possess
a striking degree of robustness, making them highly advantageous over the guided modes of
conventional waveguides. Two manifestations of this robustness are particularly important:
(i) symmetry protection: suppose that the interface operator Lint is invariant under
some symmetry O, then the corresponding interface modes are expected to be robust
against perturbations that preserve O [33].
(ii) bending immunity: even more remarkably, experiments indicate that interface modes
arising from band inversion remain robust when the interface is sharply bent. In other
words, when a straight interface is replaced by a bent one that forms a corner (see
Figure 4), the interface modes can pass through the corner with negligible reflection.
Of these two aspects, symmetry protection is comparatively well understood and was analyzed
rigorously by the authors in [11]. By contrast, sharp-bend immunity is much less understood.
Nevertheless, a complete understanding of this phenomenon is highly desirable, since it un-
derlies the proposed applications of valley-Hall-type photonic crystals, for example in chiral
quantum optics [17]. Despite a variety of informal explanations in the physics literature, there
is still no consensus on the precise mechanism responsible for sharp-bend immunity [23,45,65].
The purpose of this paper is to provide a rigorous mathematical theory for this robustness
under sharp bends. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first mathematical theory on the
bending immunity of valley-Hall interface modes.
1.2. Summary of Results. The main result of this paper is summarized as follows. We
begin with a straight-interface model that supports, for each frequency in the bulk spectral
gap, two linearly independent interface modes arising from band inversion. Then we consider
a bent-interface configuration in which the interface is bent by an angle of 2π
3 , forming a
corner at the origin. Our main result, Theorem 2.8, proves that:
(i) for every frequency in the bulk spectral gap I0 where the group velocity of straight-
interface modes is non-vanishing, except for a finite exceptional set Iexc
0 , there exist
exactly two linearly-independent interface modes propagating along the bent interface;
(ii) For every frequency in I0\Iexc
0 , there is no mode localized near the corner;
(iii) For every frequency in the exceptional set Iexc
0 , a corner-localized mode may appear,
but the multiplicity is at most finite.
Statement (i) shows that the number of propagating interface modes is unchanged by the
sharp bend, thereby establishing the bending immunity of valley-Hall interface modes. State-
ments (ii) and (iii) clarify the role of corner-localized modes. Such modes are known in the
physics literature to be a serious obstacle in applications where interface modes are used as
transport channels or cavities, yet, to the best of our knowledge, no rigorous mathematical
result addressing their existence or absence has previously been available. Our theorem gives a
definitive answer: corner-localized modes can occur only at exceptional frequencies, and even
there they form at most a finite-dimensional space. In this sense, they are generically scarce.

1.3. Relation to Previous Results. From a mathematical perspective, the analysis of in-
terface modes induced by band inversion originates in the pioneering work of Fefferman and
Weinstein. In [27], the authors establish the existence of interface modes in a one-dimensional
domain wall model, which smoothly interpolates between two periodic Schr¨odinger opera-
tors exhibiting band inversion near a Dirac point. Their multiscale analysis has subsequently
been extended to various two-dimensional settings, proving the existence of interface modes
bifurcating from Dirac points in honeycomb-type structures; see, for example, [24, 29, 42].
These works remain within the framework of domain wall models. In the context of chains
of subwavelength resonators, Ammari et al. have adapted the fictitious source method and
Toeplitz theory to study the interface modes [5,7]. More recently, Qiu et al. have developed
a layer-potential framework which applies to studying interface modes in sharp interface
models, in which two bulk media are directly adjoined without adiabatic modulation. This
approach applies to interface modes bifurcating from Dirac or quadratic degeneracies in one-
and two-dimensional systems [43,54,55].
We emphasize that a common feature of all the works above is that the interface is
assumed to be straight. This assumption is crucial for the use of Floquet-type techniques
in the analysis of interface modes. In fact, the straight interface case is by now rather well
understood: all straight interfaces with rational slopes are treated in [28,43], and the irrational-
slope situation is studied in [1]. The focus of the present paper is therefore no longer the
existence theory for straight interfaces, but rather the following challenging problem:
Are interface modes arising from band inversion robust under a sharp bend of the interface?
We need to point out that band inversion can, in certain special situations, give rise to a
nontrivial topological invariant. In such cases, it is well-known that the interface modes are
robust against arbitrary deformation of the interface geometry based on the (strong) BEC
principle. The mathematical theory of the BEC principle has been extensively developed in
a variety of settings, including Schr¨odinger operators [20–22, 34, 39, 46, 60], Dirac Hamiltoni-
ans [14,15], tight-binding Hamiltonians [10,12,13,19,25,26,35–37,40,57], and classical wave
operators [2–7, 9, 44, 48, 56, 62]; we also refer to the excellent monograph [52] and the recent
review [16] for comprehensive accounts. In contrast, the system considered in this pa-
per does not possess any nontrivial topological invariant; consequently, the BEC
principle is not applicable to account for the robustness of the interface modes
under sharp bends. To the best of our knowledge, there is currently no mathematical theory
addressing this question in such a non-topological setting.

• In Section 3, we present the main strategy of the proof of Theorem 2.8, which proceeds
in two steps. First, in Section 3.1, we reduce the bent-interface problem to a transmis-
sion problem across an auxiliary interface. More precisely, for any bent-interface mode
ubend, we decompose it into two parts, with the first part supported in the pre-bending
medium, denoted as ubend · 1ΩL, and the second part supported in the after-bending
medium, denoted as ubend · 1ΩR. By matching ubend · 1ΩL and ubend · 1ΩR across an
auxiliary interface separating ΩL and ΩR, we obtain a set of boundary integral equa-
tions. The number of solutions to this system is shown to coincide with the number
of bent-interface modes, up to the possible contribution of corner-localized modes; see
Proposition 3.3. Secondly, in Section 3.2, we solve the resulting boundary integral
equation by combining analytic Fredholm theory with layer-potential methods. This
step is highly nontrivial because the relevant layer-potential operators are built from
the out-going Green function defined through the limiting absorption principle,
rather than from the standard logarithmic kernel, and are posed on a non-compact
boundary. Establishing their regularity, Fredholm properties, and analyticity is one
of the main technical contributions of the paper; see Theorem 3.6 and Section 3.2 for
a detailed discussion.
• In Section 4, we discuss several related questions on the robustness of interface modes
that remain open for future study.
• In Section 5, we collect basic properties of the straight-interface modes that will be
used later in the analysis of the boundary integral equations obtained in Section 3.1.
• In Section 6, we introduce the out-going Green function for frequencies in the bulk
spectral gap, which forms the foundation of our layer-potential framework. Following
the lines of [32], the out-going Green function is defined via the limiting absorption
principle as presented in Section 6.1, in which we also list its basic properties, including
the far-field asymptotics. Then, in Section 6.2, we construct an analytic extension
of the out-going Green function with the frequency continued to the complex plane,
following the lines of [58], in order to prepare for the use of analytic Fredholm theory.
• In Section 7, we establish the Fredholm property of the layer-potential operator, which
is the final ingredient needed to apply the analytic Fredholm theory. The main difficulty
here comes from the fact that the layer-potential operators are defined on an unbounded
interface. To overcome this, we exploit the special bulk-interface-bulk geometry of the
model together with the spectral gap of the bulk operator. Roughly speaking, this
implies that the relevant Dirichlet modes remain concentrated near the interface, which
in turn leads to finite-dimensional kernel spaces for the layer-potential operator. See
Section 7.2 for the detailed argument.
