Abstract
We introduce a notion of residual derivative for elements of a preordered
set, a construction that generalizes both the Frattini subgroup in algebra
and the Cantor-Bendixson derivative in T1 topological spaces. For dual alge-
braic coframes with topologies compatible with order, we establish a partial
correspondence between the Cantor-Bendixson structure of the lattice and
the residual derivatives of its elements. Within this framework, we provide a
complete characterization of the first two Cantor-Bendixson levels in terms of
the lattice’s residual structure. This provides a unified lens through which to
study the Cantor-Bendixson structures of topological spaces across domains
ranging from algebra to functional analysis and dynamics, facilitating the
transfer of analytic techniques between them.

Introduction
Cantor-Bendixson structure
The Cantor-Bendixson derivative of a set X rel-
ative to a topology τ on X is the set X′ of the elements of X which are non-
isolated for the topology τ. For every ordinal α, we denote by Sα(X, τ) the αth
derivative of X, defined by transfinite induction, as follows: S0(X, τ) := X, for
any non-limit ordinal α, Sα+1(X, τ) := (Sα(X, τ))′, and for every limit ordinal λ,
Sλ(X, τ) := T
α<λ Sα(X, τ). The Cantor-Bendixson rank of X, when it exists, is
the smallest ordinal α such that Sα+1(X, τ) = Sα(X, τ). We denote it by r(X, τ).
Historically, this structure emerged from the work of Georg Cantor on a question
posed by Riemann: whether a trigonometric series that converges to zero every-
where must have all its coefficients equal to zero. Cantor proved that if a series
converges to zero outside a “thin” set , that is, a set whose Cantor-Bendixson
derivative vanishes after finitely many iterations, then the coefficients of the series
are zero. The idea of classifying such thin sets led to the introduction of transfi-
nite numbers, which quantify how many derivation steps are required before the
process stabilises. In this way, the Cantor-Bendixson process appears as one of
the earliest instances of a transfinite construction in analysis. This line of work
was further developed by Bendixson, who proved that every closed subset of R
admits a unique decomposition into a perfect set and a countable set. The latter
consists precisely of the points removed during the transfinite derivation process.
This result, now known as the Cantor-Bendixson theorem, was later extended to
more general topological spaces such as Polish spaces, and became a foundational
tool in descriptive set theory.
Applications of the Cantor-Bendixson process
More recently, the Cantor-
Bendixson process has found applications across several areas of mathematics, in-
cluding algebra, functional analysis, and dynamical systems. In algebra, it appears
naturally in the study of spaces of subgroups endowed with the Chabauty topol-
ogy. In this setting, it was used to classify the topological spaces that arise as
the space of subgroups of a countable abelian group [7], and studied as an ob-
ject of independent interest. It was computed for the Grigorchuk group [19] and
virtually metabelian groups with high Cantor-Bendixson ranks were constructed
[6]. In functional analysis, the Cantor-Bendixson structure of countable compact

Hausdorff spaces governs the structure of associated function spaces. The Banach
space C(K, R), the set of continuous functions from K to R, is determined, up to
isomorphism, by the Cantor-Bendixson rank of K together with the cardinalities
of its successive derived layers, leading to classification results for spaces of contin-
uous functions [4]. In model theory, it is used to prove, for instance, that certain
models are almost prime [3].
Multidimensional symbolic dynamics and the hyperspace of shifts
In
symbolic dynamics, the Cantor-Bendixson process has been used to analyze the
structure of multidimensional shifts [2], connecting it with a pre-order on config-
urations that compares their finite parts and with the notion of complexity. It
is also studied in the context of countable shifts, through the realization of high
Cantor-Bendixson ranks [17]. More recently, we have proved that in sharp contrast
with the case d = 1 [15] , the hyperspace of d-dimensional shifts with the Hausdorff
topology has infinite Cantor-Bendixson rank when d ≥2 [8]. In the same work, we
also characterized the isolated points in terms of maximal subsystems—a purely
order-theoretic notion.
The residual derivative
In the present document, we generalize this perspec-
tive to dual algebraic coframes equipped with topologies compatible with the order,
including in particular the Lawson topology and the Hausdorff topology on the hy-
perspaces of shifts. To capture the phenomenon that Cantor-Bendixson layers can
be expressed in purely order-theoretic notions at an abstract level, we introduce
a new order-theoretic operation: the residual derivative. While it can be defined
on any complete lower semilattice, the residual derivative finds a natural setting
in dual algebraic coframes, where it provides a powerful tool to investigate the
Cantor-Bendixson process. In particular, it allows us to characterize the second
layer of the Cantor-Bendixson process, that is, the set of elements of the poset that
become isolated once isolated elements are removed. Consider a complete lower
semilattice (L, ≤). Let x be an element of L. We denote by M(x) the set of max-
imal subelements of x, that is, the maximal elements of the poset {z ∈L | z < x}.

Significance
During the last decade, following the work of Mike Hochman [11]
[10], establishing a strong relation between multidimensional symbolic dynamics
and computability theory, most of research effort in the field has been devoted
to deepen this relation. However multidimensional symbolic systems are still not
well understood, and we believe that more conceptual structure is required for
this understanding. We hope that this research direction will contribute to this.
Recently, Van Cyr, Bryna Kra and Scott Schmieding [5] defined the notion of
chaotic almost minimal shift, which describes a family of shifts that contains the
Rudolph’s discrete version of the ×2 × 3 system on the circle [16], and proved that
Furstenberg’s ×2×3 conjecture does not hold on this family. Our framework offers
another way to explore shifts similar to Rudolph’s one, as it is contained in the
second layer of the Cantor-Bendixson structure of the hyperspace of bidimensional
shifts. The comparison with similar systems may ultimately shed some light on
the inner structure of Rudolph’s shift.
More broadly, while many natural topologies on ordered structures arise di-
rectly from the order and are studied through structural correspondences, the
Cantor–Bendixson process is defined independently of any order-theoretic consid-
erations. We show that, in a broad class of ordered topological structures, it can in
fact be recovered from a canonical order-theoretic construction, the residual deriva-
tive. This yields a conceptual framework linking topological and order-theoretic
stratifications and provides a unified perspective on the Cantor–Bendixson struc-
tures in diverse mathematical fields.
The remainder of this text is structured as follows. We provide some back-
ground on dual algebraic coframes and order-compatible topologies on posets in
Section 2 and detail some examples. Section 3 defines the residual derivative and
Section 4 presents the residual structure that follows from this definition. Section
5 and Section 6 contain the characterizations of the first two layers of the Cantor-
Bendixson structures of dual algebraic coframes, and Section 7 concludes with open
questions.

Background
A partially ordered set (poset) is a pair (L, ≤) where L is a set and ≤is a partial
order. Given a poset (L, ≤), for all x ∈L, we denote by ↓x the set {z ∈L : z ≤x}

{z ∈L : x ≤z, y ≤z}) admits a maximum (resp. minimum) denoted by x ∧y
(resp. x ∨y). We say that a lower (resp. upper) semilattice is complete when for
every subset S of L, the set {x ∈L : ∀s ∈S, x ≤s} has a maximum (resp. the set
{x ∈L : ∀s ∈S, s ≤x} has a minimum), denoted by V S or V

for all x, y, z ∈L, x ∧(y ∨z) = (x ∧y) ∨(x ∧z).

A directed set is a poset (D, ≤) such that for all d, d′ ∈D, there exists d′′ ∈D
such that d′′ ≥d′ and d′′ ≥d. A filtered set is a poset (F, ≤) such that for all
f, f ′ ∈F, there exists f ′′ ∈F such that f ′′ ≤f ′ and f ′′ ≤f. A net (resp. filter) is
a function whose domain is a directed (resp. filtered) set. Note that the image of
an increasing (resp. non-increasing) net is a directed (resp. filtered) set and that
the image of an increasing (resp. non-increasing) filter is a filtered (resp. directed)
set.

The notion of order-compatible topology is naturally related to multiple classi-
cal ideas at the interface of order and topology. Condition (ii) sets this structure
within the framework of topological semilattices [9]. The closedness of the order
in Condition (iii) is a standard in ordered topological spaces, in particular, in the
sense of Nachbin [14]. We notice that, under the additional assumption that the
space is Hausdorff, Condition (iii) is implied by Condition (ii): since x ≤y if and
only if x ∨y = y, the order is the equalizer of the continuous maps (x, y) 7→x ∨y
and (x, y) 7→y and is therefore closed whenever the topology is Hausdorff. Thus,
Condition (iii) is relevant in non-Hausdorff spaces. Finally, Condition (i) is a com-
patibility between topological and order-theoretic convergence which is involved in
the definition of Lawson topology [9]. To the best of our knowledge, these three
conditions have not been combined into a single definition in the existing litera-
ture. A canonical source of examples is the class of compact Hausdorff topological
semilattices. Theorem 2.6 then provides a more general framework than the afore-
mentioned class, as it has been designed to handle non-Hausdorff or non-compact
situations.

3
Residual derivative
In this section, we introduce the residual derivative, an operator that associates
to each element the meet of its maximal proper subelements. This construction
isolates the part of an element that cannot be recovered from its maximal approx-
imations. Iterating this operator yields a transfinite decreasing sequence, whose
stabilization defines a rank and a canonical core. This provides an intrinsic strat-
ification of the lattice, independent of any external topological construction. The
derivative and the subsequent residual stratification 4 are used to describe the
Cantor-Bendixson structures of dual algebraic coframes.
The section is organized as follows. We first define maximal subelements in
Section 3.1 and prove elementary statements about them. The residual derivative
itself is defined in Section 3.2. In Section 3.3 we see that the residual derivative
generalizes multiples concepts across algebra and topology.

3.3
Examples
Example 3.15 (Jacobson radical). Let R be a ring. The Jacobson radical of R
is the intersection of the maximal left ideals of R. The set of left ideals of R form
a lower semilattice for the inclusion preorder ⊂, and for all ideals I, J, the meet
of I and J is their intersection. The ring R itself is a left ideal, and its residual
derivative is exactly its Jacobson radical. See [12] for more details.
Example 3.16 (Frattini subgroup). Let G be a group. The Frattini subgroup of
G is defined as the intersection of its maximal subgroups. The set of subgroups of
G form a lower semilattice for the inclusion preorder, and for all subgroups H, H′,
the meet of H and H′ is H ∩H′. In this setting, the residual derivative of G is its
Frattini subgroup. See Section 10.4 in [13] for more details. In this context, our
definition of residual rank is reminiscent of the so-called Frattini series, obtained
by iterating the operator building the Frattini subgroup.
Example 3.17 (Cantor-Bendixson derivative of T1-spaces). Provided a topological
space X, its Cantor-Bendixson derivative, usually denoted by X′, is the topological
space which is obtained from X by removing its isolated points. When X is a T1-
space, meaning that every singleton of X is closed, it is also the residual derivative
of X in the lower semilattice of its closed subspaces. This comes from the fact that
a closed subspace is maximal if and only if it is of the form X\{x}, where x is
an isolated point in X. Indeed, it is straightforward that a subspace of the form
X\{x} is closed if and only if x is isolated. When it is, it is clearly maximal. On
the other hand, for any x, y ∈X such that x ̸= y, a closed subspace which does not
contain x and y is not maximal, because X ∪{y} is also closed, different from X
and strictly contains X.

Residual stratification in coframes
In this section, we assume that (L, ≤) is a coframe. We refine the structure induced
by the residual derivative by introducing the notion of residue, which captures
what is “left out” of a maximal subelement (Section 4.1). This leads to a canonical
decomposition of every element into its core and its residues (Section 4.2). Building
on this decomposition, we define the boundary poset of an element x as the set
of strongly co-irreducible elements above its core c(x) (Section 4.3), and show
that every element z such that c(x) ≤z ≤x can be reconstructed from the core
and elements of this boundary. The boundary itself carries a natural stratification
reflecting the successive stages of the residual process. As a structural consequence,
we prove that the residual derivative is a ∨-homomorphism (Section 4.4), paralleling
the behavior of the Cantor–Bendixson derivative. The framework developed in this
section will serve as the main tool for the analysis of the Cantor–Bendixson layers
in the subsequent sections.

7
Comments
The results obtained in this work show that the Cantor–Bendixson stratification,
although defined topologically, can be reconstructed from purely order-theoretic
structure in a broad class of structures. This suggests that similar correspondences
may hold in other ordered settings. In particular, it raises the question of identi-
fying minimal conditions under which a topological derivative can be represented
by an intrinsic order-theoretic operator.
We formulate below some questions that have arisen during the course of this
work, providing natural directions for further research.
Realization
The characterization of S1(L, τ) \ S2(L, τ) reveals that this set can
be divided into elements are isolated from above, ones that are isolated from below
and others. A natural question is whether each of them is non-trivial, in particular
for our motivating example:
Question 7.1. We know that there exist elements of S1(Hd, τ d
H)\S2(H, τ d
H) which
are isolated from above, as they are dually compact (ie shifts of finite type). On
the other hand, does there exist a non-finite-type shift (non isolated from above) in
S1(Hd, τ d
H) \ S2(H, τ d
H)?
More generally, one may ask whether elements in this family can exhibit iso-
lation from below, and how such properties can be detected in terms of residual
structure.

## Page 31


Special subsets
The objects introduced in this work - such as the core c(x)
and the boundary ∂x - define natural subfamilies of elements. For instance, one
may consider elements with trivial core, or those for which the decomposition
x = c(x) ∨∂x is degenerate in a prescribed way.
A systematic study of these
subfamilies could refine the understanding of the topological structure of the lattice,
in a similar way as characterizing lattices in which certain of these subsets are
empty.
Collapse of Cantor–Bendixson hierarchy
The interaction between residual
and Cantor–Bendixson derivatives raises the question of how Cantor–Bendixson
levels can degenerate. Having characterized the first levels, it is natural to ask
when some of them collapse entirely. For instance:
Question 7.2. Which dual algebraic coframes satisfy T0(L, ≤) = ε?
In such cases, non-isolated elements coincide with T∞(L), and under additional
assumptions (see below), the Cantor–Bendixson rank is 0 or 1. These extremal
cases provide simple models in which the relationship between residual structure
and Cantor–Bendixson decomposition becomes particularly transparent.
Perfect kernel
When K(L, ≤) is countable, the topology becomes first count-
able, and one obtains partial information over the perfect kernel. In particular,
T∞(L, ≤) ⊂Sr(L,τ)(L, τ). It would be of interest to determine to what extent this
relation depends on the countability assumption.
Another natural research direction consists in interpreting existing results com-
puting the Cantor-Bendixson rank of algebraic structures, such as the Grigorchuk
group [19] in terms of our framework. Furthermore, it would be interesting to see
how the Cantor-Bendixson structure varies depending on the order on the same
set. For instance, in the hyperspace of shifts, Nathalie Aubrun and Mathieu Sablik
[1] defined an order different from the inclusion, based on the algorithmic enumer-
ability of the language.
