Recognizing Level-k-Based Phylogenetic
Networks is NP-Complete
Takatora Suzuki
May 27, 2026
Abstract
Phylogenetic networks generalize phylogenetic trees by representing reticulate
evolution. Tree-based networks and their support trees have been extensively stud-
ied, but not all networks are tree-based. To measure how far such networks are
from being tree-based, Suzuki and Hayamizu (2025) formulated the problem of
finding the support network with minimum level of a given rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic network. They conjectured that this problem is NP-hard and provided
exponential-time algorithms. In this paper, we prove this conjecture by showing
that, for every fixed integer k ≥1, it is NP-complete to decide whether the mini-
mum level is at most k.

Introduction
Phylogenetic networks are graph-theoretical models for representing reticulate evolu-
tion of species, such as horizontal gene transfer [1,2] and hybridization [3]. Compared
with phylogenetic trees, which describe only branching evolution, they can represent a
wider range of evolutionary histories, but this also makes them more difficult to inter-
pret. To address this issue, various subclasses of phylogenetic networks have been pro-
posed, each capturing a biologically meaningful scenario while retaining mathematical
tractability (see [4] for a comprehensive review). Among these, two classes have at-
tracted particular attention. The first is the class of level-k networks [5], which were in-
troduced to make various computational problems on phylogenetic networks tractable
by restricting the local density of reticulations. Specifically, a network is called level-k
if each of its biconnected components contains at most k reticulations. Level-1 net-
works, originally defined as galled trees in [6], have been extensively studied [7–9],
and level-k networks in general have also attracted considerable attention [10,11]. The
second is the class of tree-based networks (e.g., [12–20]), which captures the perspec-
tive where evolution is tree-like with some reticulate events. More precisely, a network
is called tree-based if it has at least one support tree, defined as a spanning tree sharing
the root and leaf set.
Tree-based networks and their support trees have been the subject of active re-
search since their introduction by Francis and Steel [12]. Several studies have shown
that whether a given network is tree-based can be decided in linear time [12,13,16,20].
A particularly influential development is Hayamizu’s structure theorem for rooted bi-
nary phylogenetic networks (or more generally, for almost-binary networks) [16]. This
theorem provides a direct-product characterization of the family of support trees in a
given network by canonically decomposing the network into a unique set of subgraphs

called maximal zig-zag trails. This characterization yields optimal algorithms for var-
ious computational problems on support trees in a unified manner, such as counting,
enumeration, listing, and optimization. On the other hand, it has also been shown that
it is NP-hard to decide whether a given tree-based network has a support tree that is a

networks).
However, not all phylogenetic networks are tree-based. Several approaches have
been proposed to solve computational problems on such networks [15,16,21–24]. One
natural approach is to consider substructures that generalize support trees and to op-
timize how “close to a support tree” such a substructure is. Along this line, Suzuki
and Hayamizu [24] introduced the notion of support networks for rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic networks N, defined as spanning subgraphs of N sharing the root and the
leaf set. They then formulated two optimization problems: for a given rooted almost-
binary phylogenetic network N, RETICULATION MINIMIZATION asks for a support
network of N with the minimum number of reticulations, and LEVEL MINIMIZATION
asks for a support network of N with the minimum level. The minimum level among all
support networks of N is called the base level of N. For the former problem, they pro-
vided a linear-time algorithm. For the latter problem, they conjectured its NP-hardness
and developed two exponential-time algorithms: an exact algorithm and a faster heuris-
tic. These algorithms for the two problems are based on their extension of the structure
theorem [16] from support trees to support networks, which also serves as the theoret-
ical foundation of this paper.
In this paper, we prove that LEVEL MINIMIZATION is NP-hard. We introduce
its decision version, which we call LEVEL DECISION: given a rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic network N and an integer k ≥0, decide whether the base level of N is at
most k. We note that the case k = 0 coincides with the problem of deciding whether
N is tree-based, which is known to be linear-time solvable. We prove that LEVEL
DECISION is NP-complete for every fixed k ≥1; in particular, the NP-hardness of
LEVEL MINIMIZATION is a direct corollary of this result.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces pre-
liminaries, and Section 3 formulates LEVEL MINIMIZATION and its decision version
LEVEL DECISION. Section 4 recalls two key results from [16] and [24] and extends
them to the multiple-rooted setting, since our reduction uses multiple-rooted networks
as gadgets. Section 5 then establishes the NP-completeness of LEVEL DECISION for
k = 1 via a polynomial-time reduction from 3SAT, which immediately implies the NP-
hardness of LEVEL MINIMIZATION. Section 6 extends this reduction to establish the
NP-completeness of LEVEL DECISION for every fixed k ≥2. Section 7 concludes with
a discussion of open problems and directions for future work.

Preliminaries
2.1
Graph theoretical terminology
The graphs in this paper are finite, simple (i.e., having neither loops nor multiple
edges), acyclic directed graphs, unless otherwise stated. For a graph G, V(G) and
E(G) denote the sets of vertices and edges of G, respectively. For two graphs G and H,
G is a subgraph of H if both V(G) ⊆V(H) and E(G) ⊆E(H) hold, in which case we
write G ⊆H. Two graphs G and H are isomorphic, denoted by G = H, if there exists a
bijection ϕ : V(G) →V(H) such that (u,v) ∈E(G) if and only if (ϕ(u),ϕ(v)) ∈E(H)

for all u,v ∈V(G). A subgraph G of H is proper if G ̸= H. A subgraph G of H is a
spanning subgraph of H if V(G) = V(H).
Given a graph G and a non-empty subset S ⊆E(G), the edge-set S is said to induce
the subgraph G[S] of G, that is, the one whose edge-set is S and whose vertex-set is
the set of the ends of all edges in S. For a graph G with |E(G)| ≥1 and a partition
{E1,...,Ed} of E(G), the collection {G[E1],...,G[Ed]} is a decomposition of G, and
G is said to be decomposed into {G[E1],...,G[Ed]}. Here we recall that a partition of a
set is a collection of pairwise disjoint non-empty subsets whose union is the entire set.
For an edge e = (u,v) of a graph G, u and v are denoted by tail(e) and head(e),
respectively. For a vertex v of a graph G, the in-degree of v in G, denoted by indegG(v),
is the number of edges e of G with head(e) = v. The out-degree of v in G, denoted by
outdegG(v), is the number of edges e of G with tail(e) = v.
Let G be a graph. Subdividing an edge (u,v) of G means replacing it with a di-
rected path from u to v of length at least two. Smoothing a vertex v where indegG(v) =
outdegG(v) = 1 means suppressing v from G, i.e., the reverse operation of edge subdi-
vision. A graph G′ is called a subdivision of G if G′ is obtained from G by applying
edge subdivisions zero or more times. For two vertices u and v of a graph G, we call u
an ancestor of v, and v a descendant of u, if there exists a directed path from u to v.
An undirected graph is connected if there is a path between every pair of vertices.
A directed graph is connected if its underlying undirected graph is connected. For a
connected simple undirected graph G, a cut vertex (resp. cut edge) of G is a vertex
(resp. edge) whose removal disconnects G. A subgraph H of G is called a block of G if
H is a maximal connected subgraph of G that contains no cut vertex of H. In this paper,
a block of a directed graph refers to a block of its underlying undirected graph. A block
is trivial if it consists of a single edge or a single vertex; otherwise, it is non-trivial.
2.2
Phylogenetic networks
Throughout this paper, X represents a non-empty finite set, which can be interpreted as
a set of present-day species. We recall the definitions of rooted almost-binary phylo-
genetic networks and rooted binary phylogenetic trees from [16,24].
Definition 1. A rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on X is defined to be a
finite simple directed acyclic graph N with the following properties (P1)–(P3):
(P1) there exists a unique vertex ρ with in-degree 0 and out-degree 1 or 2 in N;
(P2) the set of vertices with in-degree 1 and out-degree 0 in N is identical to X; and
(P3) every vertex v ∈V(N)\({ρ}∪X) satisfies indegN(v) ∈{1,2} and outdegN(v) ∈
{1,2}.
When N has properties (P1), (P2) and (P4), N is particularly called a rooted binary
phylogenetic tree on X.
(P4) Every vertex v ∈V(N)\({ρ}∪X) satisfies indegN(v) = 1 and outdegN(v) = 2.
In Definition 1, we call the vertex ρ the root of N, each element of X a leaf of N.
We call a non-root and non-leaf vertex v a reticulation of N if indegN(v) = 2.
Let N be a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on X. If G is a spanning
subgraph of N and is a subdivision of some rooted binary phylogenetic tree on X, then
G is a support tree of N. If N has a support tree, then N is called tree-based.

Definition 2. Let N be a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on X and let G be
a spanning subgraph of N. If G is a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on X,
then G is called a support network of N. A support network G of N is called minimal
if no support network of N is a proper subgraph of G, and is called minimum if G has
the minimum number of edges among all support networks of N.
Since every support network of N = (V,E) has the same vertex set V, we shall
identify a support network G = (V,E′) of N with its edge-set E′ ⊆E throughout this
paper. We denote the families of all, minimal, and minimum support networks of N by
AN,BN and CN, respectively.
For a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network N, level(N) denotes the level of
N, i.e., the maximum number of reticulations contained in a block of N. The base level
of N, denoted by level∗(N), is the minimum value of level(G) over all support networks
G of N. If k ≥0 is the base level of N, then N is called level-k-based. We note that N is
tree-based if and only if level∗(N) = 0 holds since any support tree of N can be viewed
as a level-0 support network of N.

Problem Definitions and Known Results
In this section, we formulate the two problems whose computational complexity is
the focus of this paper, LEVEL MINIMIZATION (Problem 1) and its decision version,
LEVEL DECISION (Problem 2). We then recall a key lemma (Lemma 3) that will be
repeatedly used in our NP-completeness proofs in Sections 5 and 6.
Problem 1 (LEVEL MINIMIZATION, Problem 2 in [24]). Given a rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic network N on X, compute the base level, level∗(N), and find a support
network G ∈AN with level(G) = level∗(N).

Problem 2 (LEVEL DECISION). Given a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network

A key ingredient of our NP-completeness proofs in Sections 5 and 6 is the following
lemma, originally given in [24]. It shows that, for both Problems 1 and 2, the search
space can be restricted from AN to the (typically much smaller) subfamily BN without
losing optimality. We will invoke this restriction repeatedly throughout the remainder
of the paper.
Lemma 3 (the first statement of Theorem 19 in [24]). For any rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic network N on X, there exists at least one element G ∈BN which satisfies
level(G) = level∗(N).
As an algorithmic consequence, Lemma 3 guarantees that Algorithm 1 in [24],
which exhaustively searches BN in O(|E(N)| · |BN|) time, solves Problem 1 exactly.
Suzuki and Hayamizu [24] also presented a faster heuristic (Algorithm 2 in [24]) based
on an exhaustive search of CN, which runs in O(|E(N)|·|CN|) time but does not always
return an optimal solution. Both algorithms run in exponential time in the worst case.

An Extension to p-Rooted Networks:
Canonical Decomposition and
Characterizations of Support Network Families
In this section, we generalize two results from previous works: the maximal zig-zag
trail decomposition by Hayamizu [16]; and the direct-product characterization of the
families of all, minimal, and minimum support networks by Suzuki and Hayamizu [24];
from rooted almost-binary phylogenetic networks to a broader class of networks that
may have more than one root.
In what follows, we first define p-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic networks in
Definition 4, where p ≥1 denotes the number of roots, and restate the first result in this
setting (Theorem 5). We also recall a characterization of rooted tree-based networks
via the decomposition (Proposition 6). We then generalize Definition 2 to p-rooted
networks (Definition 7) and restate the second result (Theorem 8). In the proofs of
Theorems 5 and 8, we describe how the original proofs in [16] and [24] can be adapted
to the p-rooted setting. This p-rooted setting is essential for our NP-completeness
proofs in Sections 5 and 6, where almost-binary phylogenetic networks with more than
one root arise as intermediate objects. A similar multiple-rooted generalization has
also been considered in a different context [26].
Definition 4. Let p ≥1 be an integer. A p-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic net-
work on X is defined to be a finite simple directed acyclic graph N with the following
properties (P1’)–(P3’):
(P1’) there exist exactly p vertices with in-degree 0 and out-degree 1 or 2 in N;
(P2’) the set of vertices with in-degree 1 and out-degree 0 in N is identical to X; and

(P3’) every other vertex v of N satisfies indegN(v) ∈{1,2} and outdegN(v) ∈{1,2}.
In Definition 4, we call each of the p vertices of N described in (P1’) a root of N,
the set of all roots of N the root-set of N, and each element of X a leaf of N. We call
each non-root and non-leaf vertex v of N a reticulation of N if indegN(v) = 2. We note
that the case p = 1 coincides with Definition 1. Although any 1-rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic network on X must be connected, we note that a p-rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic network on X is not necessarily connected for p ≥2 since different roots
may belong to different components (see Figure 2(c) for an example).
Let p ≥1 be an integer and let N be a p-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network
on X. A connected subgraph Z of N with m ≥1 edges is called a zig-zag trail if there ex-
ists a permutation (e1,...,em) of E(Z), where either head(ei) = head(ei+1) or tail(ei) =
tail(ei+1) holds for all i ∈[1,m−1]. In this paper, we often identify Z and the permuta-
tion (e1,...,em). A zig-zag trail Z is maximal if no zig-zag trail contains Z as a proper
subgraph. A zig-zag trail is represented by an alternating sequence of (not necessarily
distinct) vertices and distinct edges such as (v0,(v0,v1),v1,(v2,v1),...,(vm,vm−1),vm).
Moreover, if we adopt the notation of each edge (vi,v j) as vi > vj or v j < vi, we can
more concisely express Z as v0 > v1 < v2 ...,vm−1 < vm or its reverse. We call |E(Z)|
the length of a maximal zig-zag trail Z.
Every maximal zig-zag trail is classified into one of the four types. A maximal
zig-zag trail Z is called a crown if Z has even number m of edges and can be written in
the cyclic form v0 > v1 < ...,vm−1 < vm = v0. A fence is a maximal zig-zag trail which
is not a crown. An M-fence is a fence with even number m of edges and of the form
v0 < v1 > ...,vm−1 > vm ̸= v0. A W-fence is a fence with even number m of edges and
of the form v0 > v1 < ...,vm−1 < vm ̸= v0. An N-fence is a fence with odd number m
of edges and of the form v0 > v1 < ...,vm−1 > vm.
As in the approach taken in [16, 17, 21, 24], we often consider a maximal zig-zag
trail Z as a sequence (e1,...,e|E(Z)|) of edges, ordered according to their appearance
in the trail. For any maximal zig-zag trail Z = (e1,...,e|E(Z)|) in N, any subset S of
E(Z) is specified by a 0-1 sequence ⟨b1 b2 ... b|E(Z)|⟩, where bi = 1 if ei ∈S and bi = 0
otherwise. For example, given a W-fence Z = (e1,e2,e3,e4), a subset {e1,e3,e4} of
E(Z) can be encoded as ⟨1011⟩.
The following Theorem 5 was originally proved for rooted binary phylogenetic
networks by Hayamizu [16], and was subsequently extended to rooted almost-binary
phylogenetic networks by Suzuki et al. [21], as noted in Section 6 of [16]. We further
extend it to p-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic networks.
Theorem 5 ([16], p-rooted almost-binary version). Let p ≥1 be an integer and let
N be a p-rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on X. Then, N is uniquely de-
composed into the set Z = {Z1,...,Zd} of its maximal zig-zag trails. Moreover, this
decomposition is computed in Θ(|E(N)|) time.
Proof. The original proof of Theorem 4.2 in [16] relies only on the facts that N is
almost-binary and finite, neither of which is affected by allowing multiple roots. Simi-
larly, Algorithm 5.1 in [16], which computes the decomposition by visiting each edge
exactly once, is unaffected. This completes the proof.
The maximal zig-zag trail decomposition yields the following characterization of
rooted tree-based networks. Equivalent results appear in [13,20].
Proposition 6 ([13,16,20]). Let N be a rooted almost-binary phylogenetic network on
X and let Z = {Z1,...,Zd} be the maximal zig-zag trail decomposition of N. Then, N
is tree-based if and only if no element Zi ∈Z is a W-fence.

NP-Completeness Proof of Level Decision for k = 1
In this section, we prove that Problem 2 is NP-complete even for k = 1 by giving
a polynomial-time reduction from 3SAT, a classical NP-complete problem ([LO2]
in [27]). Let ϕ = C1 ∧··· ∧Cℓbe a 3-conjunctive normal form (3-CNF) formula over
a set U = {v1,...,vn} of n Boolean variables, where each clause Ci = λi,1 ∨λi,2 ∨λi,3
consists of three literals λi,j ∈{vm,¬vm | m ∈[1,n]}. A truth assignment for U is a
map v : U →{T,F} assigning a truth value to each variable and v is said to satisfy ϕ
if every clause Ci of ϕ evaluates to T under v. Given a 3-CNF formula ϕ, 3SAT asks
whether ϕ is satisfiable, i.e., whether there exists a truth assignment for U that satisfies
ϕ.
Our reduction proceeds as follows. First, we introduce variable gadgets and clause
gadgets corresponding to the variables and the clauses of a 3-CNF formula ϕ, respec-
tively. We then construct the network N(ϕ) by connecting these gadgets to represent
the literal composition of each clause. Finally, we prove the correctness of the reduc-
tion by showing that ϕ is satisfiable if and only if N(ϕ) is level-1-based.

Conclusion
In this paper, we have settled the conjecture posed in [24] by proving that LEVEL MIN-
IMIZATION is NP-hard. In fact, we have established the stronger result that LEVEL
DECISION is NP-complete for every fixed integer k ≥1 (Theorem 16 and 18). As an
immediate consequence, the exact exponential-time algorithm for LEVEL MINIMIZA-
TION developed in [24] cannot be improved to a polynomial-time algorithm, unless
P = NP. Moreover, our hardness result contrasts with the fact that a closely related
problem, finding a support network with the fewest reticulations, can be solved in lin-
ear time [24].
A natural direction for future work is to investigate the parameterized complexity
of LEVEL MINIMIZATION. Our hardness result rules out fixed-parameter tractability
when parameterizing by the base level, unless P = NP, so future work should instead
consider parameters of the input network. Promising candidates are the treewidth and
its DAG-oriented variant, the scanwidth, both of which tend to be small on phylogenetic
networks arising from real biological data [29].
Another direction is to seek combinatorial characterizations, or at least necessary
conditions, of level-k-based networks. Tree-based networks, which coincide with level-
0-based networks, admit such a characterization through the maximal zig-zag trail
decomposition, namely via the absence of W-fences (Proposition 6). It would be of
interest to obtain analogous results for level-1-based networks and, more generally,
for level-k-based networks for each k ≥1. We note that our NP-hardness result rules
out characterizations as simple as the one for the tree-based networks. However, even
necessary conditions could lead to faster algorithms for LEVEL DECISION by further
restricting the search space beyond BN.
