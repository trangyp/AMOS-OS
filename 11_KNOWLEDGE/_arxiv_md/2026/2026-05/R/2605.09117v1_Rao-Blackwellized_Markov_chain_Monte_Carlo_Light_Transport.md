ABSTRACT
In light transport simulation, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are particularly effective at
exploring regions with complex lighting characteristics. However, estimator variance is a central concern
across Monte Carlo (MC) methods in general. In light transport, high variance directly manifests as increased
noise or, equivalently, longer rendering times at fixed image quality. Variance reduction techniques based
on RB have proven particularly effective. In practice, however, the RB approach traditionally used in light
transport, waste-recycling, can yield little to no measurable variance reduction, a fact we empirically confirm

Introduction
MCMC in light transport
In light transport, the computation of high-dimensional integrals is essential and is typically
performed using MC integration. Traditionally, this method involves generating independent samples within each pixel of
the image space. However, a major drawback of this approach is that samples are generated regardless of their contribution
to the final estimate. Even if a sample has no significant impact on the result, it is still drawn from a predefined importance
distribution, without considering the value of the target density at the sampled location. MCMC methods offer a way to address
this inefficiency. By constructing a Markov process, sample generation can be guided to better align with the target distribution,
allowing for a more structured exploration of the underlying space. Veach (1997) introduced the Metropolis-Hastings (MH)
algorithm, a widely used MCMC method, to the graphics community. Building on this pioneering work, numerous MH-based
MCMC light transport algorithms have since been proposed. Beyond this, Holl et al. (2025) recently introduced an alternative
continuous-time MCMC approach, known as Jump Restore Light Transport (JRLT).
Variance reduction
In MCMC-based light transport, estimator variance is a central concern, as high variance directly translates
into increased noise or longer rendering times at equal image quality. For general applications, a widely used variance reduction
technique is Rao–Blackwellization, which replaces an estimator by its conditional expectation given a sufficient statistic, thereby
producing an estimator that is at least as efficient and typically strictly superior. In light transport, a RB technique commonly
referred to as waste-recycling (Ceperley et al., 1977) is traditionally employed (Rioux-Lavoie et al., 2020, Section 4.6). While
this approach is computationally inexpensive, it does not guarantee variance reduction .
Contributions
We empirically confirm that waste-recycling does not necessarily lead to a significant reduction in estimator
variance in light transport (Section 7). Motivated by this lack of effective variance reduction, we consider a well-known
RB technique for the general-purpose MH algorithm, vanilla Rao–Blackwellization, which originally was computationally
prohibitive for practical use. We therefore derive a computationally efficient variant (Section 5.4) and empirically demonstrate
(Section 7) that it achieves substantial variance reduction and accelerated convergence across multiple standard metrics, under
both equal-sample-count and equal-rendering-time comparisons. Finally, we adapt the vanilla RB principle to the recently
proposed general-purpose Jump Restore algorithm (Section 6.3) and similarly demonstrate its substantial superiority .

Related work
Seminal work
The rendering equation (Kajiya, 1986) is traditionally solved using MC estimators such as ordinary path
tracing (Pharr et al., 2021) or its bidirectional extension (Lafortune and Willems, 1996; Veach and Guibas, 1995). While
effective, these estimators draw samples without regard to their eventual contribution, limiting their ability to capture challenging
transport effects. This motivated Veach (1997) to introduce MCMC-based rendering to light transport by adapting the MH
algorithm (Metropolis et al., 1953; Hastings, 1970). Later, Kelemen et al. (2002) replaced the path space representation by a
Euclidean primary sample space, significantly simplifying proposal design and making MH practical for rendering. Since then,
numerous MH variants have emerged.
Diffusion-based MH
Some of these variants correspond to the ordinary MH algorithm, but equipped with specific proposal
kernels. In the statistics community, many such proposal kernels are derived from time-discretized stochastic diffusion processes.
Among the most popular are Langevin-based (Roberts and Tweedie, 1996) and Hamiltonian-based (Duane et al., 1987) proposals,
which make use of gradient information to improve local exploration of the target distribution. In rendering, these ideas were
adapted by Li et al. (2015), who employed Hamiltonian dynamics with first- and second-order derivatives, and by Luan et al.
(2020a), who demonstrated that first-order gradients are already sufficient for producing guided proposals at lower computational
cost.
Bidirectional path tracing (BDPT)
Hachisuka et al. (2014) integrated BDPT into MH, enabling the algorithm to choose
among multiple sampling techniques via strategy-dependent proposal kernels, while producing estimates in a manner reminiscent

of multiple importance sampling. Most later MCMC-based rendering algorithms adopt this multiplexed MH variant as their

Stratification and global exploration
A key challenge in MCMC-based rendering is balancing efficient local exploration
with global discovery. Gruson et al. (2020) addressed this issue by spawning multiple chains across predefined strata, thereby
improving uniform convergence throughout the rendering.
The Restore framework
While MH has long been the standard for MCMC-based light transport simulation, Wang et al. (2021)
introduced with Restore a novel general-purpose continuous-time MCMC approach, in which fixed local dynamics terminate
after a finite runtime and are regenerated at new locations afterwards. McKimm et al. (2024) later added adaptive regeneration
mechanisms to minimize the number of eventual regenerations. Recently, Holl et al. (2025) generalized the Restore framework
substantially, allowing broader classes of local dynamics, state-dependent global dynamics (i.e., initial states from which local
explorations originate), and ensuring applicability of Restore in the specific context of light transport.
Variance reduction
While BDPT and stratification attempts also aim at variance reduction, there exist dedicated variance
reduction techniques for MH estimators in general-purpose applications. Among these, the class of RB methods (Robert and
Roberts, 2021) is the most relevant for our work. In light transport, a RB technique commonly referred to as waste-recycling
(Ceperley et al., 1977), is traditionally employed (Rioux-Lavoie et al., 2020, Section 4.6). A fundamentally different RB approach
was introduced as vanilla Rao–Blackwellization in Douc and Robert (2011). At the cost of substantial additional computational
overhead in the light transport setting, this RB technique can be shown to yield a strict variance reduction. The method proposed
in this work builds on this approach, but departs from it in a way that avoids these costs, while still achieving significant variance
reduction.

Rao–Blackwellization
A key challenge in MC integration is reducing the variance of estimators. RB is a classical and effective technique for this
purpose. RB is the practice of replacing an estimator by its conditional expectation given additional information. The neologism
originates from the celebrated Rao–Blackwell theorem (Rao, 1945; Blackwell, 1947), which states that conditioning an estimator
on a sufficient statistic improves estimation under any convex loss.

From a theoretical perspective, conditional expectation acts as an orthogonal projection in L2 and therefore cannot increase

due to extraneous randomness is averaged out, resulting in reduced or, in the worst case, unchanged variance.
In particular, replacing an estimator S by its conditional expectation with respect to a larger information set never increases
variance. This follows immediately by taking F to be the information provided by the estimator S itself. In Sections 5 and 6, we
exploit this principle to construct Rao–Blackwellized alternatives to the ergodic average estimator (36).

Local dynamics
The local dynamics of the Jump Restore algorithm are given by a user-defined Markov kernel κ. Conceptually,
κ represents the dynamics of a Markov chain (Mt)t∈N0 that is well suited for rapid local exploration of the state space. Such a
Markov chain may, for instance, be given by an existing MCMC method.
Embedding into continuous time
The Restore framework (Holl et al., 2025) operates in continuous time. For this reason, the
Markov chain (Mt)t∈N0 is embedded into continuous time by holding each state for a random duration. These holding times
must be chosen to be exponentially distributed in order for the resulting continuous-time process (Yt)t≥0 to remain Markovian.
Accordingly, the process remains in state x for a time distributed as Exp(h(x)), where h is a strictly positive holding rate. To
preserve the infinitesimal behavior of (Mt)t∈N0, one must choose h = 1 (Holl et al., 2025, Section 7.1). For clarity, however, we
retain a general holding rate h.
Killing
Each local exploration evolves according to the dynamics of (Yt)t≥0 and is killed (or terminated) after a finite time.
Termination is governed by a continuous-time clock that decays with a time-dependent exponential rate

Conclusion
Qualitative comparisons presented in Figures 1 and 3 to 7 and Section H of the supplemental demonstrate that our vanilla
RB estimators consistently achieve higher visual fidelity than standard estimation, while waste-recycling rarely yields a
perceptible improvement. These observations are corroborated by the quantitative results in Figure 2 and Sections G and H
of the supplemental, which provide detailed error tables at equal-sample-count and equal-rendering-time, as well as plots of
all evaluation metrics as functions of sample count and rendering time. Across all metrics, errors decrease substantially faster
when using our proposed vanilla RB estimators, establishing them as a generally applicable RB technique that consistently
outperforms the current state-of-the-art in rendering.

Given a finite measure π, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is a technique for estimating the integral

Markov process
A process is a state system evolving over time. Usually, the time domain T is either discrete, T = N0, or
continuous, T = [0, ∞). Informally, the process is said to be Markov, if at any fixed point in time, the evolution of the process
does only depend on the present state, but not on the past. For a general introduction to Markov processes, see Kallenberg (2021);
Çinlar (2011); Douc et al. (2018); Ethier and Kurtz (2009); Klenke (2020).
Invariance
A Markov process (Xt)t∈T is π-invariant if and only if once Xs is distributed according to π at a certain time point
s ∈T, every state Xt at a future time point t ∈T ∩(s, ∞] will be distributed according to π as well. That is, the distribution of
a state is stationary in time after it once coincided with π.

D
Implementation Details and Hardware Setup
Parameter choices
Across all considered Metropolis–Hastings variants — Metropolis, MALA, and H²MC — we used a large
step probability of ℓ= 0.3, a value known to perform well in practice. To account for burn-in, we discarded the first 10,000
iterations before collecting estimates.
For the corresponding Jump Restore methods — Metropolis Restore, MALA Restore, and H²MC Restore — we set c = 1 in
(29).
Hardware setup
All renderings were performed on a system equipped with two AMD EPYC 7702 processors, each providing
64 cores and 128 hardware threads. The CPUs operate at a clock speed of 2–3.3 GHz and are paired with 2048 GiB of DDR4
ECC memory running at 3200 MHz. All computations were executed entirely on the CPU. Since our hardware limited us to at
most 256 concurrent threads, the results reported in Section 7 may further improve on systems that support a higher degree of
parallelism.
