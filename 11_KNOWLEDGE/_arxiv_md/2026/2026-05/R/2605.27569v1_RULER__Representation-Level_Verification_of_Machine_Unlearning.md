Abstract. Machine unlearning aims to remove the influence of specific
training records from a deployed model without retraining from scratch.
Current protocols verify this at the output level through membership
inference, retain accuracy, and forget-set accuracy, but a model can sat-
isfy all three whilst still encoding forgotten records in its intermediate
representations. We introduce RULER, a set of representation-level ver-
ification metrics. The oracle-comparative metric M2 measures whether
forget-set records occupy the same representational position as in a model
retrained without them. The oracle-free metric M4 detects residuals
from the unlearned model’s internal similarity structure alone, without
retraining. Four approximate unlearning methods all pass output-level
evaluation, yet under a linear mixed-effects model M2 detects significant
residuals in 10 of 12 conditions (p < 0.05), with effect sizes growing as
the forget fraction increases. A fifth method, Bad Teacher, shows the
same residuals despite a different forgetting mechanism. M4 acts as a
pre-unlearning diagnostic across tabular, image, clinical text, and face-
identity settings: it detects identity-level memorisation in face recogni-
tion models where no tested method fully erases the signal.

Introduction
Machine unlearning removes the influence of a specified subset of training records
(the forget set) from a trained model without retraining from scratch, whilst pre-
serving the model’s behaviour on the remaining records (the retain set) [2,1]. Ap-
proximate methods such as gradient ascent, fine-tuning, and distillation achieve
this at a fraction of the computational cost of full retraining [9]. Determining
whether unlearning has succeeded requires an evaluation protocol; most existing
protocols rely on three output-level criteria: (a) membership inference attack
(MIA) accuracy close to the 0.50 chance level [10]; (b) preserved retain-set ac-
curacy; (c) forget-set accuracy matching a retrain oracle (a model trained from
scratch on Dr alone) [9]. These criteria assess predictions, not the internal repre-
sentations that produce them. A model can satisfy all three whilst still encoding

Related Work
Approximate unlearning and its evaluation. Approximate methods modify a
trained model without full retraining: gradient ascent on the forget set and fine-
tuning on the retain set serve as baselines [5], NegGrad+ [9] combines them,
and SCRUB [9] uses teacher-student distillation. Evaluation typically relies on
output-level criteria relative to a retrain oracle: MIA accuracy, retain accuracy,

and forget accuracy [5,9,10]. Hayes et al. [7] showed that unlearning MIAs can
overestimate privacy protection; Goel et al. [4] demonstrated that indistinguisha-
bility protocols are incomplete; Thudi et al. [11] argued that parameter-space
comparisons cannot certify unlearning. Wang et al. [12] propose evaluating via
model differences using influence functions. Chundawat et al. [3] introduce the
Zero Retrain Forgetting (ZRF) metric, which measures Jensen–Shannon diver-
gence between the unlearned model’s forget-set outputs and a randomly ini-
tialised teacher, alongside the Bad Teacher framework used in our evaluation.
Representation-level analysis. Golatkar et al. [5] introduced weight scrubbing,
observing that output metrics may not reflect whether information persists in
the network; feature spaces are known to encode structured information not
visible from outputs [8]. Existing metrics operate at the parameter or output
level: activation distance [6,3] compares softmax outputs (despite its name, not
an intermediate-layer measure); completeness [2] measures predicted-class agree-
ment; MIA [10] assesses membership leakage. None examine how a model inter-
nally organises information about individual records, or how forget records are
treated relative to retain records. RULER addresses this gap with per-record
penultimate-layer metrics calibrated against partition-aware nulls (M2, M4).
Tabular data. Much of the existing machine unlearning literature focuses on
image classification [5,9], whereas tabular data presents distinct challenges in-
cluding heterogeneous feature types, frequent class imbalance, and small forget
sets at low forget fractions that limit statistical power. We therefore use tabular
data as the primary experimental setting and extend the evaluation to image,
clinical text, and face-identity domains (Section 5.5).
3
Representation-Level Evaluation
3.1
Problem statement
We formalise RULER in the primary setting of binary classification on tabu-
lar data. The training dataset D consists of records (x, y) with x ∈Rd and
y ∈{0, 1}. The predicted class probabilities are σ(fθ(x)), where σ is the softmax
function and fθ : Rd →R2 is a model with parameters θ trained on D. The metric
definitions are label-free and do not depend on the number of classes; the diag-
nostic experiments (Section 5.5) therefore apply RULER without modification to
multi-class image, masked-language-modelling, and face-identity settings, with
fθ and the embedding dimension differing per architecture (Appendix Table 2).
We assume that the full training set is partitioned into a retain set Dr ⊆D
and a forget set Df := D \ Dr, and we define the forget fraction ff := |Df|/|D|.
Three models are defined:
– the original model θo trained on D;
– an unlearned model θu produced by applying an unlearning algorithm to θo;
– the retrain oracle θr trained from scratch on Dr alone.

The oracle serves as the gold-standard reference: a model with no knowledge of
Df. Starting from θo, the goal of machine unlearning is to produce an unlearned
model θu that behaves as if Df were never seen during training, matching the
oracle θr in outputs and internal representations, without incurring the computa-
tional cost of retraining from scratch. Reliable evaluation metrics are therefore
needed that verify erasure at the representation level, not only at the output
level.
3.2
Penultimate-layer cosine similarity
The proposed metrics use cosine similarity between penultimate-layer embed-
dings. Let hθ : Rd →Rp denote the penultimate-layer embedding function of a
model θ, i.e. the activation immediately before the task-specific output head gθ,
so that fθ = gθ ◦hθ. The embedding dimension p is fixed by the architecture;
in the primary tabular experiments (Section 4.1) p = 128, and the diagnostic
experiments use the analogous penultimate activation of each architecture (Sec-
tion 5.5). All embeddings are L2-normalised prior to any similarity computation.
Thus, for any two records x, x′ ∈Rd and models θ, θ′, the cosine similarity
in the embedding space is:
simθ,θ′(x, x′) :=
hθ(x)⊤hθ′(x′)
∥hθ(x)∥∥hθ′(x′)∥= hθ(x)⊤hθ′(x′),
(1)
where the second equality follows from L2-normalisation. The metrics compare
either the same record across two models (cross-model similarity, simθ,θ′(x) :=
simθ,θ′(x, x)) or two records within the same model (within-model similarity,
simθ(x, x′) := simθ,θ(x, x′)).
The penultimate layer is the final feature abstraction before the task-specific
output head; earlier layers share lower-level features across records, and the
output layer collapses representations into logits or per-token distributions, dis-
carding geometric structure. Cosine similarity operates per record (necessary
for small forget sets), is magnitude-invariant in L2-normalised space (enabling
cross-dataset comparison), and produces a scalar for statistical testing against
a calibrated null. Centred kernel alignment (CKA) [8] and mutual information
estimators produce matrix-level summaries that do not naturally decompose per
record, making them unsuitable for verifying individual erasure requests under
GDPR Article 17.
3.3
Lens 1: oracle-comparative metrics (M1, M2, M3)
Lens 1 metrics require access to the retrain oracle θr and measure how closely the
unlearned model’s representations of records in Df agree with those of the oracle.
All Lens 1 metrics use a paired-seed design: the original model θo and retrain
oracle θr share the same random initialisation seed, so that representational dif-
ferences reflect the unlearning procedure rather than geometric variation across
random initialisations.

Relationship between the two lenses. M2 measures repositioning relative to the
oracle and supports population-level inference across datasets, with negative
values indicating residual memorisation, positive values over-correction, and zero
values oracle-level erasure. M4 measures geometric indistinguishability within
the unlearned model alone, making it oracle-free but inherently dataset-specific.
We therefore report M4 as a per-dataset diagnostic rather than a population-

Experimental Setup
4.1
Datasets and model architecture
Ten tabular classification datasets are used: Adult Income, Diabetes 130-US,
Breast Cancer, Heart Disease, German Credit, Bank Marketing, Wine Qual-
ity, Phoneme, Magic Telescope, and Electricity. Nine are sourced from OpenML
and Breast Cancer from sklearn.datasets; three with multi-class targets are
binarised. Each dataset is split 80 %–20 % into training and held-out test par-
titions (stratified sampling, random state 999); features are standardised using
StandardScaler fitted on the training partition.
All models use a two-hidden-layer MLP (TabularMLP) with the fixed archi-
tecture shown in Fig. 2, hidden dimension p = 128 and dropout 0.2 after each
ReLU. The architecture is held constant across all datasets and all three model
types (θo, θu, θr). Training uses Adam with learning rate η = 10−3 and cross-
entropy loss for 50 epochs full-batch (no mini-batching), chosen to reduce gradi-
ent variance and isolate unlearning dynamics; robustness to mini-batch training
is verified in Appendix A.6.

4.2
Unlearning methods
Gradient Ascent (GA) maximises cross-entropy loss on Df for 5 epochs.
NegGrad+ (NG+) combines gradient ascent on Df with gradient descent on
Dr for 10 epochs, weighted by α = 0.6. Fine-Tuning (FT) continues gra-
dient descent exclusively on Dr for 10 epochs, relying on catastrophic forget-
ting. SCRUB distils from the frozen original (teacher) to a student copy for 10
epochs, maximising agreement on Dr and disagreement on Df [9], with α = 0.6
and temperature T = 2.0. All four start from θo and use Adam with unlearning
learning rate ηu = 5 × 10−4, held fixed across all datasets and forget fractions.
A fifth method, Bad Teacher [3], is evaluated separately (Section 5.4; implemen-
tation in Appendix A.11).
4.3
Evaluation protocol and statistical analysis
The oracle-comparative metrics operate on penultimate-layer embeddings; for
the retain-set baseline in M2, a subsample of min(500, |Dr|) records (random
state 42) is drawn per seed. M4 computes nearest-retain-neighbour similarities
across all Dr records, capped at 2,000 for memory. Output-level metrics are
computed in parallel: MIA accuracy (threshold-based attack using per-sample
cross-entropy loss, with balanced accuracy reported), retain, forget, and test
accuracy. Let MIA denote the mean MIA accuracy across the 10 training seeds
per (dataset, method, forget fraction) condition. The pass threshold |MIA −
0.50| < 0.05 accommodates dataset-level variation.

via restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to all N = 100 observations; the

test can overstate significance, so we also report the Wilcoxon signed-rank test
on N = 10 dataset-level means. The intra-class correlation ICC = σ2

Results
Key findings. All four methods pass output-level evaluation (Appendix Ta-
ble 3), but M2 detects significant residuals in 10 of 12 conditions under the
LMM (Table 1), with effect sizes growing with the forget fraction and direc-
tional consistency across datasets (Appendix Fig. 5). After correction, methods
are statistically indistinguishable on M2, M4, and MIA, indicating the discor-
dance is a property of the task, not any algorithm. Bad Teacher reproduces the
same negative M2 at every fraction despite a fundamentally different mechanism
(Section 5.4). M4 is dataset-specific (ICC = 0.89) and serves as a per-dataset
diagnostic, sensitive to identity-level memorisation (Appendix Fig. 7) and over-
displacement on large architectures.
5.1
Representation-level residuals persist despite output-level pass
All four methods pass standard output-level evaluation at every forget fraction,
with mean MIA accuracy within ±0.05 of 0.50 and retain-set and test-set ac-
curacy preserved (Appendix Table 3). Per-dataset MIA results show variation
masked by the aggregate (Appendix Table 4). On German Credit at ff = 1 %,
the oracle itself breaches the ±0.05 window (MIA = 0.700), indicating a dataset-
level characteristic rather than incomplete forgetting; Breast Cancer (0.560) sim-
ilarly breaches at the oracle level, whilst Heart Disease shows method-specific
breaches for Fine-Tuning and SCRUB (0.570; oracle = 0.520). Despite passing
all output-level checks on aggregate, the oracle-comparative metric M2 detects
residuals in 10 of 12 method-fraction conditions (p < 0.05, LMM; Table 1),
confirming that output-level evaluation is insufficient.
The oracle-comparative metric M2 is negative for every method at every
forget fraction (Table 1), meaning that after unlearning, forget-set records have
lower cosine similarity to the oracle than retained records. Under the LMM, 10
of 12 method-fraction conditions are significant at p < 0.05, with the two non-
significant conditions (Gradient Ascent at ff = 1 %, p = 0.136, and ff = 5 %,
p = 0.064) remaining directionally consistent.
The oracle-free metric M4 shows mean ranks above 0.50 in all 12 conditions.
Its variance is dominated by dataset identity (ICC = 0.89), which is why it func-
tions as a per-dataset diagnostic rather than a population-level test. The per-
dataset analysis shows that datasets with detectable memorisation (e.g. Breast
Cancer, M4 ≈0.60; Heart Disease, M4 ≈0.55) drive the aggregate signal, whilst
datasets with weak memorisation sit near the null for both unlearned and oracle
models. These gaps are small in absolute terms because L2-normalised penul-
timate embeddings place all records near similarity 1.0 under the paired-seed
design, so the calibrated gap rather than the raw similarity carries the signal.
They are nonetheless consistent in sign across methods, datasets, and forget
fractions (Appendix Fig. 5). Eight of ten datasets show a negative representa-
tion shift for all four methods at ff = 5 %. Heart Disease and German Credit
are exceptions: on German Credit, all four methods produce positive shifts at

5.2
Metric behaviour across forget fractions
M2 effect sizes increase with the forget fraction, reaching their maximum at
ff = 10 % (rrb = 0.90 to 0.96), with all four methods significant at p < 0.005
under the LMM (Table 1). M4 does not reach population-level significance at any
fraction (ICC = 0.89), with effect sizes decreasing from medium at ff = 1 % to
small at ff = 10 %. The per-dataset distribution of M4 (Appendix Fig. 6) shows
that within each dataset the four methods and the oracle baseline produce sim-
ilar distributions; deviations from 0.50 therefore reflect feature-space geometry
rather than residual memorisation specific to the unlearning algorithm, consis-
tent with the framing of M4 as a per-dataset diagnostic. The residual signal also
holds across unlearning learning rates: M2 < 0 and M4 > 0.50 for the same
datasets at ηu ∈{10−4, 5 × 10−4, 10−3} (Appendix A.7, Table 7).

Methods are interchangeable on representation metrics but

Post-hoc pairwise Wilcoxon tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across all
six method pairs and four metrics (24 tests) show that no pair is distinguish-

All four methods exhibit representation-level residuals of comparable magni-

5.4
Findings hold under a fundamentally different mechanism: Bad
Teacher
To check whether the discordance is method-specific or structural, we evalu-

Diagnostic role of M4 beyond tabular MLPs
Beyond verifying unlearning, M4 can indicate whether unlearning is necessary
at the representation level. Applied to the original model, it reveals whether
forget-set records are memorised or blend into the general distribution; when
memorisation is absent, applying unlearning may introduce artefacts. We eval-
uate this diagnostic across four settings: large-scale tabular data (Covertype,
Higgs; 100,000 samples; MLP, Residual MLP, FT-Transformer), image classifi-
cation (CIFAR-10, SVHN, CIFAR-100; three-layer CNN and ResNet-18), clin-
ical text (MTSamples with Bio_ClinicalBERT, masked language modelling),
and face-identity unlearning (LFW; ResNet-18 trained for gender classification).
Architecture diagrams are in Appendix Figs. 8 and 9.
Large-scale tabular datasets. Across Covertype (forest cover type, 7 classes)
and Higgs (binary signal-versus-background; 100,000 samples each), using MLP,
Residual MLP, and FT-Transformer with mini-batch training (batch size 256, 5
seeds, ff = 5 %), original-model M4 values are close to 0.50 for all three archi-
tectures (Appendix Table 12), indicating weak or no detectable memorisation.
Unlearning has little effect, with most methods remaining near 0.50. NegGrad+
is the exception, increasing M4 (e.g. 0.60 on Higgs with FT-Transformer), in-
dicating displacement rather than removal. In contrast to the primary tabu-
lar experiment, these results indicate memorisation depends on data scale and
structure, and when memorisation is weak or absent, unlearning methods can
introduce artefacts.
Image data. We repeated the analysis on CIFAR-10, SVHN, and CIFAR-100
(10,000 samples each, ff = 5 %) with a three-layer CNN and ResNet-18. Original
models again show M4 ≈0.50 (Appendix Table 13), indicating no or weak de-
tectable memorisation. After unlearning, behaviour depends on model capacity.
On ResNet-18, NegGrad+ and SCRUB produce substantial over-displacement
(M4 = 0.03 to 0.16 and 0.11 to 0.37 respectively); on the three-layer CNN most
methods remain near 0.50. Some methods therefore introduce representation-
level artefacts when memorisation is weak or absent, particularly in higher-
capacity models.
Clinical text. We next tested whether RULER applies to a non-classification
training objective. Bio_ClinicalBERT is a BERT-base model pre-trained on clin-
ical notes using masked language modelling. We evaluated it on the MTSamples
dataset using sentence-level representations (the CLS-token activation of the fi-
nal transformer layer), with forget and retain sets defined at the document level
and expanded to sentences (Fig. 4). The pre-unlearning M4 diagnostic showed
mean values of 0.537, 0.521, 0.501 at ff = 1 %, 5 %, 10 %. The 5 % and 10 %
values are near the null, indicating no detectable memorisation; the 1 % value
sits slightly above the null but remains modest given N = 5 seeds on a single
dataset. After unlearning, M2 remains small at 1 % for most methods but shows
increased variability at larger forget fractions, with both negative and positive
values observed (e.g. −0.048 for NegGrad+ and +0.033 for Fine-Tuning at 5 %).

6
Discussion and Conclusion
All four approximate methods pass output-level evaluation but leave statistically
significant residuals on M2. Fine-Tuning and SCRUB preserve retain accuracy
better than Gradient Ascent and NegGrad+, but this advantage does not extend
to representation-level metrics. Bad Teacher reproduces the same outcome de-
spite a different forgetting mechanism, indicating that the discordance reflects a
limitation of current approaches rather than any individual method. NegGrad+
and SCRUB on deeper architectures introduce geometric artefacts where none


## Page 14


14
G. Cosma and A. Finke
existed prior to unlearning. For practitioners, M4 provides information at two
stages. Before unlearning, values near 0.50 on the original model indicate un-
learning may be unnecessary. Values far above 0.50 (M4 = 0.73 to 0.94 on LFW)
confirm identity-level structure that no tested method fully erases. After un-
learning, deviations from 0.50 flag incomplete erasure or over-displacement that
output-level metrics miss. M4 requires only the model and the data partition.
For method developers, the consistency of residuals suggests future unlearning
objectives should incorporate representation-level constraints directly. Method-
ological limitations are detailed in Appendix A.2.

A.2
Methodological limitations
The following limitations and assumptions apply to the framework presented in
the main text. (1) The metrics are technical verification tools rather than le-
gal compliance tests under GDPR Article 17; they characterise representational
residuals, not regulatory adequacy. (2) Cosine similarity is not rotation-invariant,
so M2 requires the paired-seed design described in Section 3.3 to remain meaning-
ful. (3) Diagnostic experiments (large-scale tabular, image, clinical text, LFW)
use five seeds per condition, leaving open whether the over-displacement ob-
served for NegGrad+ and SCRUB on deeper architectures can be mitigated by
hyperparameter tuning. (4) A single unlearning seed is used per training seed to
separate variability from training initialisation from that of the unlearning pro-
cedure; sensitivity to the unlearning seed is untested for the four main methods,

Robustness to mini-batch training
The main experiments use full-batch gradient descent to isolate unlearning dy-
namics from optimisation noise. To assess robustness to stochastic optimisation,
we repeated the ff = 5 % experiment using mini-batch SGD (batch size 128, 5
seeds per dataset, N = 50) and applied the same LMM specification used in the
main analysis (Section 4.3).
For M2, the random-effect variance was not identifiable for Gradient Ascent,
Fine-Tuning, and SCRUB (singular REML fit) because within-dataset cluster-
ing was close to perfect at this sample size; we therefore report the Wilcoxon
signed-rank test as a fallback for these three methods and the LMM result for
NegGrad+. Under mini-batch training, M2 reverses sign for three of four meth-
ods (Table 6), confirming that the paired-seed calibration baseline is invalidated
when batch ordering introduces stochastic variation between the original model
and oracle. NegGrad+ is the only method for which M2 remains negative, but
its magnitude is small (−0.00195, rrb = +0.02) and does not differ significantly
from zero under either test.
M4 remains above 0.50 for all four methods (range 0.519 to 0.538). Under
the LMM, NegGrad+ is significant at p < 0.001 (ICC = 0.95); the other three
methods reach p ≤0.020 under the Wilcoxon test but only marginal significance
(p between 0.072 and 0.089) under the LMM, where very high within-dataset
clustering (ICC ≥0.97) absorbs most of the between-seed signal. The residual
signal on M4 therefore persists under stochastic optimisation, though the LMM
is conservative at this sample size.

The main experiments use a single forget set per (dataset, ff) condition. To check
that the reported results do not depend on this choice, we repeated the pipeline
on all ten datasets and all three forget fractions using five additional forget-set
seeds (999 to 1003), training a fresh oracle for each (N = 3,000 runs; Table 9).

30 cells. The 9 cells with at least one positive value involve the same exceptions

this independent sampling. The across-seed standard deviation (median across
methods) is largest on the smallest forget sets, reaching 0.0048 for M2 and 0.112

the forget-set sizes that arise from individual erasure requests under GDPR

A.11
Comparison with Bad Teacher
Gradient Ascent, NegGrad+, Fine-Tuning, and SCRUB all modify the origi-
nal model’s parameters directly. Bad Teacher [3] operates differently, training a
student to match a randomly initialised teacher on forget-set records. Testing
whether the same residuals appear under this mechanism indicates whether the
discordance is method-specific or structural.
Implementation. The implementation follows Chundawat et al. [3]. A randomly
initialised TabularMLP is frozen as the teacher. A student copy of the original
model is trained for 10 epochs using Adam at ηu = 5 × 10−4, α = 0.6, T = 2.0,
matching the SCRUB hyperparameters. On forget-set records the student is
trained to match the random teacher’s output distribution; on retain-set records
it minimises both the KL divergence to the random teacher and the cross-entropy
loss on true labels, following the loss formulation in [3] with the random teacher
in place of the incompetent teacher. A single unlearning seed (j = 100) is used,
matching the design of the main experiment. To assess sensitivity to teacher
initialisation, we repeated the analysis with two additional teacher seeds (101,
102). M2 remained negative for all ten datasets at all three forget fractions across
all three seeds. M4 values were stable across seeds, with maximum within-dataset
variation below 0.007. These results confirm that the findings are not specific to
the choice of teacher initialisation.
Results. Bad Teacher passes output-level evaluation at all three forget fractions.
M2 detects significant residuals throughout (p < 0.01; Table 10), meaning forget-
set records remain displaced from where the oracle places them. M4 does not
reach significance at any fraction, with mean ranks remaining above 0.50 (0.528,
0.514, 0.505 at ff = 1 %, 5 %, 10 %), consistent with the attenuation observed
in Section 5.2. Paired comparisons on M4 (Table 11) show that Bad Teacher
displaces forget-set records further from the retain set than all four approximate
methods at ff = 1 % (all p ≤0.01). At larger fractions no comparison reaches
significance. This stronger displacement does not achieve complete erasure: M2
remains negative at every fraction, confirming forget-set records still fall be-
low the retain-set baseline in the oracle’s representation space. The discordance
therefore holds across five methods spanning direct parameter modification and
distillation-based unlearning, indicating that current approximate methods are
optimised for output-level criteria that leave intermediate-layer representations
unconstrained.

A.16
Reproducibility
All code to reproduce the experiments, metrics, and figures is available at https:
//github.com/gcosma/RULER. All experiments use fixed random seeds propa-
gated to dataset splitting (random state 999), forget-set sampling (random state
999), training initialisation (seeds 0 to 9), and unlearning (seed 100). For each
training seed i ∈{0, . . . , 9}, the original model θo and retrain oracle θr are
trained with the same random seed. Same-seed oracle–original similarity has a
grand mean of 0.993 across ten datasets, compared with 0.431 for differently
seeded oracle pairs, confirming that shared initialisation produces similar repre-
sentational geometry. Original models and retrain oracles are cached as .pt files
after the initial training run, and all unlearning procedures operate from these
identical cached weights.
