Abstract
Survival analysis aims to model how covariates and time jointly shape the time-
to-event distribution under right censoring. Classical methods such as the Cox
model and generalised additive models (GAMs) require interactions and time-
varying effects to be manually specified, which is increasingly impractical on rich
clinical datasets. We introduce KAPLAN-HR, a B-spline Kolmogorov-Arnold
Network (KAN) for nonparametric estimation of the conditional hazard as a joint
function of covariates and time. A single-layer KAPLAN-HR model recovers a
GAM, while deeper architectures capture interactions and time-varying effects
through composition. We establish a convergence rate for the nonparametric KAN
hazard estimator that depends only on the smoothness of the underlying KAN
representation and not on the covariate dimension, thereby mitigating the curse
of dimensionality for KAN-representable targets. In evaluations over six clinical
benchmark datasets, KAPLAN-HR matches or exceeds the predictive performance
of established statistical and deep learning survival methods.

Introduction
Time-to-event data is ubiquitous in biostatistics, engineering, and business, but analysing it is
complicated because the events of interest are often only partially observed. Patients may leave a
study before their disease progresses, or a machine may be replaced before it fails, so only a lower
bound on the true event time is ever observed for these subjects. Survival analysis addresses this by
modelling the time-to-event distribution under this incomplete observation, called right censoring.
The Cox proportional hazards (PH) model is the de facto standard for survival regression [1], but it
requires careful manual work to accommodate interactions between features (covariates) and restricts
their relationship with time. Among the more flexible alternatives, spline-based approaches for
modelling the hazard have been studied for over three decades. For example, generalised additive
models (GAMs) with a survival likelihood fit a spline for each covariate’s effect on the hazard [2, 3].
HARE models the log-hazard as linear splines and selects main effects and pairwise interaction terms
by stepwise search [4]. However, these models are structurally limited in the way they represent
interactions, as GAMs only include them when interaction terms are specified manually, and HARE
cannot represent three-way or higher interactions.
This limitation is increasingly consequential, particularly in medicine and biostatistics, where modern
datasets such as UK Biobank [5] and MIMIC [6] are far richer than those available before, motivating
methods that capture interactions and time-varying effects without manual specification. Deep
learning approaches [7, 8] relax classical models’ structural restrictions and perform particularly well
on high-dimensional data such as imaging, but depart from the additive structure that makes classical
models simple to analyse and interpret.

learnable univariate function and each node sums its inputs. When the edge functions are splines,

6.2
Real-World Clinical Datasets
We benchmark KAPLAN-HR on six standard clinical survival analysis datasets. We evaluate predic-
tive performance in terms of discrimination, via Antolini’s C-TD [33]; survival function accuracy,
via the IBS [34]; and calibration, via the median event time ICI [35] and the D-CAL statistic [36].
Performance is compared against nine baselines spanning classical and deep learning approaches.
Appendix C.3 details our experimental protocol. Each dataset is split ten times into training, validation,
and testing sets 64 : 16 : 20. We report the mean and standard deviation across splits for C-TD, IBS,
and ICI, and the number of splits passing the D-CAL test at p > 0.05. We tune KAPLAN-HR and
all tunable baseline models via Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation to maximise C-TD.

Conclusion
We introduced KAPLAN-HR, a survival modelling approach that generalises established spline-
based models with KANs, able to capture covariate interactions and time-varying effects. Our
theoretical analysis shows that, when the true log-hazard follows the KAN structural assumption,
the KAPLAN-HR estimator attains a convergence rate that depends only on the smoothness of the
representation and not on the covariate dimension up to a logarithmic factor, mitigating the curse of
dimensionality. To our knowledge, this is the first formal convergence guarantee for a KAN-based
survival model. Simulation studies corroborate the predicted convergence rate and demonstrate
that KAPLAN-HR captures interactions that an additive GAM cannot represent. Together with
8


## Page 9


competitive performance on six clinical benchmarks, these results support that KANs are well-suited
for survival analysis.
Limitations
The convergence results we establish are informative about KAPLAN-HR’s statistical
properties but do not characterise finite-sample behaviour. Further, they rely on a structural assumption
about the true log-hazard that is consistent with prior KAN theory [9] but may not hold in all real-
world situations. Finally, while our evaluation showed KAPLAN-HR to be competitive with baselines,
the standard benchmarking practice in survival analysis using medium-scale clinical datasets has
known shortcomings [24], with reported performance varying across publications. Larger-scale,
standardised, and openly available benchmarks would benefit the survival analysis community.

Implementation Details
KAPLAN-HR is implemented with the pykan library v0.2.8 by Liu et al. [9] for the KAN layers.
Each edge is a B-spline plus a SiLU residual. We use the library’s default parameters throughout,
which we list in Table 4.
During training, the knot vectors are periodically re-fitted to the observed activation distribution via
pykan’s grid-extension at a tunable schedule of epochs U. Parameters are optimised with Adam
at a tunable constant learning rate using mini-batches of tunable size B. Training stops when the
validation NLL has not decreased below its running minimum for P consecutive epochs. The
complete training procedure is given in Algorithm 1.

evaluation (and validation during training) is initialised at K + 1 quantiles of the training observed

We define four synthetic DGPs:
DGP-1: g∗(x, t) = sin(2πx1) + 0.5 cos(2πx2) −0.3 sin(πt) + 0.2
DGP-2: g∗(x, t) = |2x1 −1| + 0.5 cos(2πx2) −0.3 sin(πt) + 0.2
DGP-3: g∗(x, t) = sin (π(x1 + 0.5x2)) −0.3t + 0.2
DGP-4: g∗(x, t) = x2
1 · t + 0.3 sin(πx2) −0.4
DGPs 1,3, and 4 are analytic, thus the smoothness exponent r for rate analysis is capped by the spline
order. We use cubic B-splines for all simulation experiments, so r = 4. DGP-2 contains the tent term
|2x1 −1| which admits only one weak derivative, yielding r = 1.
For each DGP we generate R = 100 independent training sets plus a shared testing set of 2000
observations. We further split each training set 80/20 and use the validation sub-set for early stopping.
We draw i.i.d. covariates x1, . . . , x5 ∼Uniform(0, 1) (x3, x4, x5 are intentionally left as noise) and
generate continuous event times by inversion from the conditional hazard λ(t | x) = exp(g(x, t))
on t ∈(0, 10]. Ground-truth survival S(t | x) = exp

C.3
Real-World Clinical Dataset Experiments
Datasets and Preprocessing
We evaluate on six standard survival analysis datasets: Molecular
Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) [40], Rotterdam tumor bank and
German Breast Cancer Study Group (RotGBSG) [41, 42], National Wilms’ Tumor Study (NWTCO)
[43], Assay Of Serum Free Light Chain (FLCHAIN) [44], and Study to Understand Prognoses
Preferences Outcomes and Risks of Treatment (SUPPORT) [45], with covariates selected to match
Kvamme and Borgan [46]; and Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) [6],
following the preprocessing of Purushotham et al. [47]. Each dataset is split 10 times stratified
on the event indicator into 64% train, 16% validation, and 20% test sets with random seeds 0-9.
Covariates and time are standardised using training set statistics at each split. Event times are
min-max normalised to [0, 1] prior to splitting. Rows with any missing values are dropped.
Baseline Models
We compare performance against: Cox proportional hazards, implemented
by lifelines [48]; a generalised additive model (GAM), implemented by the mgcv R package
[39]; HARE [4], implemented by the polspline R package; DeepSurv [17], CoxTime [18], and
DeepHit [19], implemented by pycox [18]; Deep Survival Machines (DSM) [49], implemented by
auton-survival [22]; CoxKAN [10]; and SuMo-Net [50].
Hyperparameter Tuning
All tunable models undergo Bayesian hyperparameter optimisation using
Weights & Biases [51] to maximise the time-dependent concordance index (C-TD) [33] on the
validation set in split 0 (out of 10), separately for each dataset, over 50 trials per (dataset, model) pair.
Table 5 gives the sweep ranges for each model hyperparameter.
Scoring
Survival predictions are evaluated on: the time-dependent concordance index (C-TD) by
Antolini et al. [33], implemented by pycox [18]; the integrated Brier score (IBS) [34], implemented
by scikit-survival [52]; the integrated calibration index (ICI) [35], implemented by lifelines
[48], measured at the median training set event time (per split); and the D-calibration p-value (D-CAL)
[36], implemented by SurvivalEVAL [53]. All metrics except D-CAL are averaged over the 10
data splits and reported as the mean ± standard deviation. The D-CAL is reported as the number of
splits (out of 10) with p > 0.05. Statistical comparisons between KAPLAN-HR and the best-scoring
baseline model on each dataset use the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the 10 per-split metric
values, with Holm-Bonferroni correction applied across the six datasets per metric.
