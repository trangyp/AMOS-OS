ABSTRACT
The Milky Way hosts a flattened nuclear stellar disc (NSD) that dominates the gravitational potential in the inner few hundred parsecs,
but whether the NSD is purely axisymmetric or contains a nuclear bar remains an open question. We test for the presence of a nuclear
bar with kinematic diagnostics, combining positions and line-of-sight velocities from the KMOS NSD survey with proper motions
from VIRAC2 to construct the (vℓ, vlos) velocity ellipse. We measure the vertex deviation lv and the anisotropy β of the velocity
ellipse for a number of sub-samples after applying quality cuts designed to minimise contamination from large-scale bar stars. For
our primary sample, comprising stars within |ℓ| < 0.9◦, −0.4◦< b < 0.25◦, [Fe/H] > −0.3, we measure a negative vertex deviation

1. Introduction
Bars are a prevalent structural feature in disc galaxies, with ap-
proximately 60% of disc galaxies hosting a large-scale bar (Es-
kridge et al. 2000). The Milky Way (MW) itself hosts a promi-
nent large-scale bar, whose major axis is oriented at an angle
of approximately 27◦relative to the Sun-Galactic Centre line,
and a semi-major axis ≈5 kpc (see Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard
2016, and references therein). Early studies of external galax-
ies based on photographic plates also identified the existence of
double-barred systems, where a barred galaxy contains a sec-
ondary, nuclear bar (de Vaucouleurs 1975; Sandage & Brucato
1979). Subsequent observational studies sought to quantify the
frequency and properties of nuclear bars, finding that roughly
20% of barred galaxies host a nuclear bar (e.g., Laine et al. 2002;
Erwin 2011, 2024). In addition, approximately 50% of barred
galaxies with MW masses and above (log(M⋆/M⊙) > 10.5)
are double-barred, with nuclear bar sizes typically a few hun-
dred parsecs (Erwin 2024). Studies also find that inner and outer
bars are dynamically decoupled, having orientations and pattern
speeds independent of each other (Buta & Crocker 1993; Friedli
& Martinet 1993; Maciejewski & Sparke 2000; Corsini et al.
2003; Debattista & Shen 2007; Du et al. 2015; Li et al. 2023).
There have been previous claims that the MW is double-
barred. Alard (2001) used H- and K-band star counts from
2MASS to derive the projected stellar density in the bulge re-
gion. They reported an overdensity at negative longitudes which
they interpreted as a signature of a nuclear bar. Rodriguez-
Fernandez & Combes (2008) derived the mass distribution of
the inner Galaxy by fitting the 2MASS star count map to a model
including a disc, bulge and nuclear bar. They then simulated the
gas dynamics in the deduced gravitational potential, recovering
an orientation of the nuclear bar of α ≈60◦-75◦, where α is mea-
sured from the Sun-GC line, with positive α in the positive lon-
gitude direction. Nishiyama et al. (2005) used red clump (RC)
stars at a latitude of b = +1◦to trace the main bar orientation as
a function of longitude (−10.5 < ℓ[◦] < 10.5). They reported a
change in the inclination angle for the Galactic bar at fields with
|l| < 4◦, which they interpreted as the effect of a distinct nuclear
bar. Gonzalez et al. (2011) later confirmed this result using RCs
from the VISTA Variables in the Via Lactea (VVV) survey to
trace the orientation of the bar at latitudes b = ±1◦. While the
works above appear to provide compelling evidence of a nuclear
bar, Gerhard & Martinez-Valpuesta (2012) used N-body simula-
tions to demonstrate that both the asymmetry in the star count
maps and the change in the bar orientation can be reproduced
by the radially varying axis ratio of the primary bar even in the

A significant challenge in assessing whether the MW is
double-barred stems from our edge-on perspective of the Galac-
tic centre, combined with the substantial, non-uniform extinction
throughout the nuclear region (e.g. Bally et al. 1988; Gonzalez
et al. 2012; Schultheis et al. 2014; Nogueras-Lara et al. 2021;
Henshaw et al. 2023). In particular, roughly 2/3 of the dust/gas
near the mid-plane within |ℓ| < 1.5◦resides at positive longitudes
(Bally et al. 1988). This complicates the use of star counts and
photometric methods by producing an apparent deficit of stars at
positive longitudes. If uncorrected, this bias could be misinter-
preted as evidence for a nuclear bar oriented with its near side at
negative longitudes, a configuration similar to that proposed by
Alard (2001). An alternative approach to detect a nuclear bar in
the MW is to use stellar kinematics. Using N-body simulations,
Fiteni et al. (2026) (henceforth referred to as Paper I) carried out
a systematic investigation into the potential kinematic signatures
of a nuclear stellar bar in the MW. They proposed two primary
diagnostics: (1) the vertex deviation, lv, of the (vℓ, vlos) veloc-
ity ellipsoid, and (2) the asymmetry in the µℓvs ℓdistribution.
Their analysis indicated that out of the two diagnostics, the ver-
tex deviation is the most robust, owing to its relative insensitiv-
ity to extinction, and the ability to combine stars from different
fields across ℓ−b space, which can improve the statistical signifi-
cance of the lv measurement. In particular, they find that N ≳500
stars when combining the inner (|ℓ| < 0.9◦, −0.4◦< b < 0.25◦)
fields in the KMOS survey fields (Fritz et al. 2021) could be suf-
ficient to reliably compute lv. In this paper, we apply the vertex-
deviation diagnostic established in Paper I to the currently avail-

2. The observational data
We employ the same dataset used by Sormani et al. (2022),
which includes line-of-sight velocity measurements from the
KMOS K-band spectroscopic survey of the NSD (Fritz et al.
2021), supplemented by proper motions from the VIRAC2
(Smith et al. 2025) astrometric and photometric catalogue, de-
rived from the Vista Variables in the Via Lactea (VVV) survey
(Minniti et al. 2010). Our initial dataset, consisting of stars hav-
ing µℓand vlos, has a total of 2533 stars.
Fig. 1 shows the Color-Magnitude diagram (CMD) for the
raw sample (gray markers). The survey of Fritz et al. (2021) se-
lects only stars in the region 6.6575 < Ks −1.37 × (H −Ks) <
9.1575 (i.e. between lines 1 and 2 in Fig. 1). Foreground stars
along the line of sight, originating from both the spiral arms
and the Galactic bulge, are subject to substantially different ex-
tinction compared to stars located in the central regions (e.g.,
Nogueras-Lara et al. 2021). Following Schultheis et al. (2021),
Nogueras-Lara et al. (2024), and Nieuwmunster et al. (2024), we
exclude stars with (H−Ks) > max(1.3, −0.0233Ks+1.63) (line 3
in Fig. 1). This effectively removes stars in the bulge field (field 5
in Fig. 2). All subsequent samples are constructed from stars sat-
isfying these selection criteria (red markers). We also note that
the VIRAC2 photometric catalogue saturates at Ks ≈10 mag,
truncating the bright end of our photometric sample.

5. Distance and Velocity Uncertainties
As outlined in Sec 2, we assumed that all stars lie at a Galacto-
centric distance of R = 8.2 kpc when converting µℓto vℓ. How-
ever, in reality stars will be distributed along the line of sight
within the nuclear structure, with observational selection bias-
ing our sample towards stars on the near side of the disc. To
assess how this impacts our results, we model the distance distri-
bution using a skew-Gaussian function1 (shown in Fig. 7) with
a shape ("skewness") parameter of −2. While the stars in the
samples likely have Galactocentric radii within a few hundred
parsec of the assumed distance, we adopt a large standard devia-
tion (σ = 1.5 kpc) of the skew-Gaussian function to account for
the possibility that some stars may be located at larger Galacto-
centric radii. The distribution is skewed towards the near-side of
the disc to reflect the higher likelihood of sampling nearby stars.
For each individual star in sample A, we randomly sample a
distance measurement from the skew-Gaussian in Fig. 7, and re-
calculate vℓ. Once this is done for all stars in the sample, we re-
compute lv. We repeat the process for a total of 50, 000 iterations.
The top panel of Fig. 8 shows the distribution of lv resulting from
the Monte-Carlo resampling of distances. We find that the distri-
bution has a standard deviation of σlv = 1.98◦, which is neg-
ligible when compared to the uncertainties associated with the
observational samples when bootstrapping is applied (Sec. 3).
We also assess how the uncertainties in vlos and vℓimpact
measurement of lv by propagating the errors through a Monte-
Carlo resampling. For each star in the cleaned sample A, we gen-
erate two independent Gaussian distributions with means equal
to the observed vlos and vℓand standard deviations equal to their
respective measurement errors. The velocity component for each
star is resampled for 50,000 iterations from its Gaussian, yield-
ing a perturbed velocity set. For every iteration, we recompute
lv, thereby obtaining its distribution.
The bottom panel of Fig. 8 shows the distribution of lv result-
ing from the Monte-Carlo velocity resampling. The distribution
is strongly peaked at lv ≈−64◦with a negative tail extending
up to lv ≈−68◦. The standard deviation is σlv = 1.76◦, again
indicating that the errors arising from velocity uncertainties are
negligible compared to the sampling uncertainties inferred from
the bootstrap method.

6. Impact of extinction on vertex deviation
The Galactic centre suffers from severe and spatially non-
uniform extinction, with significant variations occurring even
within individual KMOS pointings (see Fig. 9). To assess
how extinction impacts our results, we adopt a two-stage de-
reddening approach, prioritising individual stellar extinction es-
timates where possible and falling back to map-based values
where necessary.

6.2. Effect on the vertex deviation and anisotropy
While extinction does not directly affect the line-of-sight veloc-
ities or proper motions used to construct the velocity ellipse, it
enters our analysis through the photometric selection that de-
fines the kinematic sample. The Fritz et al. (2021) selection was
applied in the observed colour–magnitude plane (Fig. 1), and is
therefore sensitive to the spatially-varying extinction across the
KMOS pointings. To assess whether this biases our measure-
ments, we perform two complementary tests on the sample it-
self.
We emphasise that these tests are specifically designed to
identify extinction-dependent biases, that is, biases whose mag-
nitude varies with AKs across the field of view. The absolute com-
pleteness of the underlying KMOS+VIRAC2 spectroscopic se-
lection within the Fritz et al. (2021) CMD box is much lower
than unity, with only ∼few percent of photometrically eligible
stars in each field actually targeted. This overall undersampling
is set by the survey’s multiplexing rather than by any astrophys-
ical property of the targets, and we assume it is approximately
uniform within each field.

foreground-rejection cut introduced in Sec. 2 is least clean: in

nation of small samples and a target-selection bias whose impact
grows precisely in this regime precludes a reliable kinematic test
below AKs ≈2.0 with the present data.
We additionally note that the Galactic-centre extinction is
well known to be asymmetric in Galactic longitude, with ap-
proximately two-thirds of the dust within |ℓ| < 1.5◦concentrated
at positive longitudes (Bally et al. 1988; Henshaw et al. 2023).
In Paper I (their Sec. 3.4 and Fig. 11) we constructed a toy se-
lection function reproducing this longitude-dependent extinction
and applied it to an axisymmetric NSD model, finding that lv is
then shifted towards positive values, in the opposite direction
to our negative measurement. The longitude asymmetry of the
Galactic-centre extinction is therefore unable to account for the
observed signal.
We conclude that, although a saturation-driven photometric
incompleteness is detectable in the sample for AKs ≳1.9, the
resulting selection bias does not propagate into our kinematic
diagnostics. The vertex deviation and anisotropy measurements
reported in Sec. 4 are therefore decoupled from the extinction-
driven incompleteness, and the dominant uncertainty in lv re-
mains the statistical sampling uncertainty characterised by the
bootstrap approach of Sec. 3.

8.1. Elliptical nuclear disc or nuclear bar?
External galaxies often host NSDs that are not perfectly axisym-
metric, but rather slightly elliptical (e.g., Bittner et al. 2020),
with the NSD’s major axis oriented orthogonally to the primary
bar (e.g., Cole et al. 2014). Could the kinematic signature we
observe arise from an elliptical NSD oriented orthogonally?
The MW’s primary bar is oriented at about 27◦to the Solar-
GC line, with its near side at positive longitudes (Wegg et al.
2015). An elliptical orthogonally oriented NSD would therefore
have its near side oriented toward negative longitude. In Paper I,
we demonstrated that such a configuration produces a mildly
positive vertex deviation. In contrast, our observed lv is nega-
tive. Our measured lv is therefore inconsistent with being due to
an elliptical NSD.
8.2. Implications for the Milky Way
The presence of a nuclear bar would fundamentally alter the
gravitational potential of the inner R < 300 pc and hence, the
orbital structure of stars and gas in this region (Schultheis et al.
2025). This would require a significant re-evaluation of current
theoretical models for the three-dimensional structure of the cen-
tral molecular zone (CMZ) (Henshaw et al. 2023), necessitat-
ing updates to our understanding of the gas kinematics and star
formation in the Galactic centre. Moreover, the changed orbital
structure would call for a careful re-interpretation of longitude-
velocity (ℓ−v) diagrams (Sofue et al. 2025b,a), which are foun-
dational to our current models of gas flows and dynamics in the
CMZ.
The existence of a nuclear bar in the MW would also have
profound implications for our understanding of the growth his-
tory of the central super-massive black hole (SMBH), SgrA∗. In
galaxies without recent major mergers such as the MW, SMBH
growth is expected to proceed via secular processes. In single-
barred systems, gas inflow driven by the primary bar typically
stalls at a star-forming nuclear ring at radii of a few hundred par-
secs, acting as a barrier that suppresses substantial accretion onto
the SMBH (e.g., Fanali et al. 2015; Li et al. 2017; Tress et al.
2020). By contrast, nuclear bars have been proposed to torque
gas to smaller radii, channelling material toward the Galactic
centre and potentially contributing to fuelling the central SMBH
(Shlosman et al. 1989; Friedli & Martinet 1993; Maciejewski
et al. 2002; Namekata et al. 2009; Hopkins & Quataert 2010; Li
et al. 2023).
8.3. Formation scenarios for a nuclear bar
Numerical simulations show that in isolated systems, large-scale
bars can form through dynamical instabilities in pre-existing
discs (see Sellwood 2014, and references therein). The formation
mechanism of nuclear bars is less well understood but is thought
to be analogous, arising within a pre-existing nuclear stellar disc
(NSD) (see Schultheis et al. 2025, for a review). For instance, in
Paper I, the nuclear bar model evolved from the axisymmetric
NSD model of Sormani et al. (2022), which was mildly unstable
and spontaneously developed a nuclear bar. Other studies have
similarly reproduced long-lived nuclear bars through such insta-
bilities (e.g., Debattista & Shen 2007; Du et al. 2015; Saha &
Maciejewski 2013; Wozniak 2015), with external tidal pertur-
bations potentially accelerating the formation process (Semczuk
et al. 2024). Observationally, nuclear bars are found at arbitrary
orientations relative to the large-scale bar (Buta & Crocker 1993;
Friedli & Martinet 1993), suggesting independent rotation. Sim-
ulations confirm this decoupling, also revealing pulsating ampli-
tudes and pattern speeds as the nuclear bar rotates independently
through the large-scale bar (Debattista & Shen 2007; Li et al.
2023).
The relative timing of nuclear and large-scale bar forma-
tion also remains uncertain: simulations show nuclear bars can

gas inward to build the NSD, which could have subsequently
become unstable and formed the nuclear bar through dynami-

whether an age gradient would survive bar-formation and sub-

short dynamical times near the Galactic centre. This may explain
why Sanders et al. (2024) found weak or no evidence for an age
gradient when using Mira variables to trace the NSD’s stellar age
distribution.
There may also be the possibility that a nuclear bar existed
in the past but has now dissolved. However, simulations suggest
that nuclear bars dissolve when the central mass concentration
exceeds roughly 0.1% of the disc stellar mass (Du et al. 2017;
Nakatsuno & Baba 2024). The combined mass of SgrA∗and the
nuclear star cluster is reported to be below this threshold (Nakat-

nuclear bar in the MW.

independent fields within the region |ℓ| < 1◦and |b| < 0.5◦,
enabling measurement of radial velocities, metallicities and α-
abundances. The Roman Galactic Bulge Time Domain Survey
(Terry et al. 2023) will provide high-precision photometric and
astrometric measurements of ∼3.3 million stars over a 0.28
deg2 field centred on SgrA*, with proper motion precisions
of 2.5 −3.5 µas/yr. Lastly, JASMINE’s Galactic Centre survey
(Kawata et al. 2024) will cover a similar region −0.7◦< ℓ< 1.4◦
and |b| < 0.6◦, and is expected to provide accurate positions
and proper motion measurements for about 1.2 × 105 stars up
to Hw < 14.5 mag in the main survey area. A major step forward
will come from the JWST/NIRCam Legacy Survey of the Galac-
tic Center (Schödel et al. 2023), which will perform a large-area,
deep multi-epoch NIRCam imaging survey of the inner ∼100
pc, delivering unprecedented stellar catalogues, individual star-
by-star extinction measurements, and proper motion constraints
across the NSD.
In parallel, Vasini et al. (in prep.) are developing a tech-
nique to constrain the three-dimensional distribution of gas and
dust towards the Galactic centre. In addition to enabling future
kinematic analyses to account for depth-dependent extinction
and mitigate residual selection biases in low-extinction fields
(Sec. 6.2), this approach will also allow the detection of a possi-
ble nuclear bar directly from the gas distribution.
