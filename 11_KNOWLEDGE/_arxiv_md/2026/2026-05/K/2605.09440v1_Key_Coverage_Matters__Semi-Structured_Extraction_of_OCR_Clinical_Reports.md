Yu Wang, Yingyun Li, Ying Qin, Haiyang Qian*

Abstract
Clinical reports are often fragmented across healthcare institutions because privacy regulations and
data silos limit direct information sharing. When patients seek care at a different hospital, they often
carry paper or scanned reports from prior visits. This hinders not only electronic health record (EHR)
integration and longitudinal review, but also downstream healthcare applications that depend on more
complete patient records, such as patient management, follow-up care, real-world studies, and clinical-
trial matching. Although optical character recognition (OCR) can digitize such reports, reliable extrac-
tion remains challenging because clinical documents are heterogeneous, OCR text is noisy, and many
healthcare settings require low-cost on-premise deployment.
We formulate this problem as canonical key-conditioned extractive question answering over OCR-
derived clinical reports. Because the key fields are neither fixed nor known in advance, the key space
is open. We maintain a canonical key inventory through iterative key mining, normalization, cluster-
ing, and lightweight human verification, and introduce key coverage as a metric to quantify inventory
completeness. Using a 0.2B BERT-based model, experiments on real-world reports from more than 20
hospitals show that performance improves monotonically with key coverage. The model achieves F1
scores of 0.839 and 0.893 under exact match and boundary-tolerant matching, respectively, once the
Top-90 canonical keys are covered. These results show that key coverage is a dominant factor for end-
to-end performance. At Top-90 coverage, our model outperforms a fine-tuned Qwen3-0.6B baseline
under exact match. Although the annotated corpus used in this study is Chinese, the method is based on
the language-agnostic key–value organization of semi-structured clinical reports and can be adapted to
other clinical document settings given an appropriate canonical key inventory and alias mapping.
Keywords:
OCR-derived clinical reports, semi-structured information extraction, key-conditioned ques-

patient records, clinical document processing

clinicians of their medical history Mandl and Kohane [2012]. Figure 1 depicts this scenario. This practice
poses significant challenges for both clinicians and patients. For clinicians, paper reports are inconvenient to
read and cannot be integrated into EHR systems, limiting their usability for longitudinal review, structured
retrieval Hossain et al. [2023], and clinical decision support. Even after applying OCR, the resulting text

Clinical reports often follow an explicit layout: clinical concepts appear as field headers (keys) followed
by free-text content (values), separated by delimiters such as colons or layout cues Fu et al. [2020], Jaume

languages and across mixed-form clinical documents. Owing to wording differences across institutions, the
same clinical concept may appear under different surface-form keys, yielding a highly variable and long-
tailed key space in which previously unseen keys continue to emerge Wu et al. [2020].

Semi-structured clinical reports admit a natural extractive-QA formulation: field headers act as queries,
and the corresponding values are answers. In particular, bidirectional language models such as BERT are
well suited to this setting because they encode both the query and the document context, enabling robust
span extraction for free-text values.

However, standard QA-based extraction methods require a predefined and closed set of keys. This
requirement is difficult to satisfy in real-world clinical report practice, where the key space is open and
evolving: new surface-form keys continue to emerge across institutions and templates. As a result, extraction
performance is fundamentally heavily constrained by the coverage of key space.
To address this challenge, we propose a key-coverage extraction framework for semi-structured OCR
clinical reports. We formulate value extraction as key-conditioned QA over text derived from OCR. By
applying normalization and clustering, we maintain a canonical key inventory and semantically equivalent
variants. This inventory is maintained via an iterative refinement loop, in which uncovered keys from newly
observed reports are updated into the space, and the extraction model is retrained along the way. Experi-
ments demonstrate that extraction performance improves monotonically with increasing key coverage and
exhibits clear saturation at high coverage levels, confirming that key inventory expansion—rather than model
complexity—is the primary driver for handling emerging keys in real-world clinical reports.
Generalizable Insights about Machine Learning in the Context of Healthcare.
In semi-structured clin-
ical reports, end-to-end performance is constrained more by canonical key coverage than by model size
alone. When the same clinical field is expressed through institution-specific surface-form keys, canonical
key construction and alias expansion are part of the extraction task itself, because they determine which
fields the model can recognize and extract. The method is not specific to Chinese because its core induc-

surface form; adapting it to English therefore requires only an English canonical key inventory and alias

Related Work
Healthcare institutions still face challenges in sharing clinical data, even with ongoing interoperability
projects. As a result, patients often have to shoulder the burden of transporting their own records be-
tween providers. Recent studies point to specific obstacles in the real-world Health Information Exchange
(HIE)—namely fragmented systems and inconsistent data access—which creates a clear need for methods
that can structure patient-borne documents for reuse Manns et al. [2025].
In practice, transferred records often arrive as scanned or printed images, requiring OCR for digitization.
Li et al. (2024) demonstrated that OCR-derived clinical text is inherently noisy—issues such as character
confusion and line breaks significantly harm downstream extraction and integration pipelines Li et al. [2024].
Li, Wang, and Qian [2026] introduced MedStruct-S, a benchmark for semi-structured extraction from OCR-
derived clinical reports under open-world keys and OCR noise. This scenario matches our focus on page-
level OCR text: we need robust span extraction even when text rendering is imperfect, and minor boundary
shifts should not skew evaluation.
The query-based approach provides a reliable and universal method for extracting clinical information

from narrative texts in the real world (which have characteristics such as diversity, fragmentation, and style
variation). The EMRQA benchmark Pampari et al. [2018] has established question answering as a feasible
extraction paradigm, providing not only a methodology but also a public resource. Recent studies have
extended this approach to radiology reports, which contain clinical-based structures but lack standardized
patterns or markers Dada et al. [2024]. Biomedical language models such as BioBERT Lee et al. [2020] and
PubMedBERT Gu et al. [2021] have now become the core architectures for state-of-the-art medical question
answering and information extraction.
A major hurdle in working with semi-structured reports is the lack of standardized field headers, or
”keys”. These keys vary from one institution to another and change over time, creating a constantly evolving,
open-ended key space. This problem is analogous to biomedical entity normalization, where the goal is to
link different names to a single underlying concept Liu et al. [2021]. While previous normalization research
has largely targeted these biomedical entities, we turn our attention to the report keys themselves. Another
common limitation is that most existing data extraction methods assume a fixed, known set of keys. Our
approach is different: we start by quantifying how missing keys impact extraction performance, and then
leverage those findings to guide the maintenance and strategic expansion of our key set Lu et al. [2022],
Shen et al. [2022].
3
Methodology
In this problem, each page is treated as an independent input and the atomic object we discussed here is
page-level. Multi-page documents, if present, are handled through an aggregation stage and can also be
regarded as single page documents.
3.1
Dataset and Annotation
We construct a real-world corpus consisting of 16,144 OCR clinical reports collected from patients across
more than 20 hospitals, reflecting substantial institutional and template heterogeneity. The corpus covers a
diverse set of report categories, including outpatient medical records, biopsy pathology reports, examina-
tion records, postoperative pathology reports, genetic testing reports, admission records, and consultation
pathology reports. Among these, outpatient medical records are the most frequent category, accounting for
approximately 35% of all reports.
To protect patient privacy and comply with data governance requirements, all personally identifiable
information—including names, identification numbers, contact information, and addresses—was removed
prior to annotation and replaced with a uniform placeholder symbol (“**”). Only de-identified OCR text
was used in subsequent processing and analysis.
The corpus was manually annotated at the surface-form key–value (KV) pair level, requiring approx-
imately 430 human-hours in total. Compared with native electronic medical records, annotating OCR-
derived clinical text is substantially more time-consuming due to OCR noise, boundary ambiguity, and
layout inconsistencies. To reduce annotator workload while maintaining annotation quality, we adopted a
human-in-the-loop annotation strategy: candidate surface-form keys were first automatically identified us-
ing BGE-large-zh-v1.5, and then verified and corrected by expert annotators. This semi-automatic process
significantly improved annotation efficiency without introducing additional supervision bias.
The annotated corpus was split into training, validation, and test sets using a 7:1:2 ratio, with splitting
performed at the report level to prevent information leakage across sets. In total, the corpus contains 132,798
annotated surface-form key–value pairs, with 2,394 unique surface-form keys observed.
The distribution of surface-form keys exhibits a strong long-tailed pattern(Figure 4a-b): the top five keys
account for 23.7% of all key occurrences, while hundreds of low-frequency keys appear only sporadically.

3.3
Evaluation Method
In OCR-derived clinical reports, canonical key-value extraction errors arise from two primary sources. First,
OCR systems frequently introduce character-level noise, such as character substitutions between visually
similar symbols, missing or spurious whitespace, and erroneous line breaks caused by layout fragmentation.
For example, delimiters such as colons or newlines may be incorrectly recognized or omitted, leading to
slight shifts in the start or end positions of extracted value spans. Secondly, human annotation inevitably in-
troduces boundary-level imprecision, as annotators may differ in whether surrounding punctuation, whites-
pace, or line breaks are included in the annotated span. Importantly, both types of noise are typically
confined to a small number of characters near span boundaries and rarely alter the semantic content of
the extracted values. However, under strict exact string matching, such minor discrepancies can be over-
penalized, obscuring the practical usability of extraction results.

3.4
Training Method
We adopt a BERT encoder with a standard extractive QA head, in which two linear layers predict token-level
start and end logits. The model is fine-tuned on key-conditioned questions paired with OCR-derived text.
To avoid information leakage, data are split using a stable report-level hash, ensuring that pages from the
same clinical report never appear in different splits.
Long OCR pages that exceed the model’s input budget are handled via budget-based chunking. The
model predicts answer spans independently within each chunk, and candidate spans are merged across
chunks for each (report,key) pair.
The training loss is based on standard start/end cross-entropy, augmented with several regularization
terms to improve robustness under OCR noise and annotation variability. Specifically, we incorporate: (i)
label smoothing (ϵ = 0.08 for extraction and ϵ = 0.1 for canonicalization) to mitigate overconfidence;
(ii) an explicit no-answer margin, which enforces a separation between the [CLS] null score and the best
non-null span (margin 0.10/0.15 with weight 0.01/0.05 for extraction/canonicalization); and (iii) length
regularization to discourage over-extended spans (length reweighting weight 0.1, scale 2.0). To reflect the
clinical importance of short fields, we further apply a higher loss weight to short spans (2.0/2.5).
During inference, we enumerate the top-N start and end candidates (N = 20) and score spans by the
sum of their start and end logits. To prevent excessively long predictions, we apply a dynamic maximum
span length, retaining spans whose end positions cover up to 90% of the cumulative probability mass, with a
stricter cap for short fields. Predicted spans are post-processed by trimming leading and trailing whitespace,
removing trailing punctuation, and snapping to token boundaries. For long reports, the final prediction is
selected across chunks based on the highest span score.

Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work
We study the clinically important problem of extracting header–content fields from heterogeneous clinical
reports, a common scenario when patients seek care across multiple healthcare institutions. To address this
challenge, we formulate the task as key-conditioned extractive question answering over OCR-derived clin-
ical reports. Compared with contemporary large-scale language models, our BERT-based QA model (0.2B

## Page 12


parameters) is substantially more parameter-efficient. This design supports low-cost, on-premise deploy-
ment, making the approach practical under the regulatory, privacy, and infrastructure constraints commonly
faced by healthcare institutions. Beyond facilitating cross-institutional review during clinical encounters,
more complete and longitudinally coherent patient records can also support a broader range of health-
care applications, including out-of-hospital patient management, patient follow-up, real-world studies, and
clinical-trial matching.
We further propose a practical pipeline for constructing and maintaining a canonical key inventory by
combining automatic key mining, normalization, canonicalization, and lightweight human-in-the-loop ver-
ification. Through extensive experiments, we show that key coverage is a key determinant of end-to-end
extraction performance. In particular, end-to-end performance is already high when the Top 90 canonical
keys are covered, and further key expansion yields only marginal gains. Importantly, this level of coverage
can be obtained from a relatively small collection of clinical reports. These findings suggest that, for semi-
structured clinical report extraction, improving key coverage may be more effective than increasing model
capacity. This is particularly important in healthcare settings, where missing canonical keys can compro-
mise not only extraction accuracy but also the reliability, completeness, and actionability of downstream
clinical and operational workflows.
A notable characteristic of the current study is that all annotated data come from real-world Chinese
OCR clinical reports. We view this as an important strength of the work: it grounds the study in a realis-
tic and operationally relevant document setting, where OCR noise, cross-institution variability, and semi-
structured reporting practices genuinely affect healthcare data usability. At the same time, our claims re-
garding English reports are limited to the level of method design rather than empirical validation, and direct
evaluation on an annotated English corpus remains an important direction for future work.
Due to regulatory and privacy constraints, we are unable to publicly release the original clinical report
images or their OCR-derived text. To support reproducibility while respecting patient privacy and institu-
tional data governance, we provide, as supplementary material during the review stage, the training, infer-
ence, and evaluation code, together with the canonical key inventory and alias mappings; these materials
will be released through a GitHub repository in the camera-ready version.
We plan to extend this work in three directions with direct relevance to healthcare deployment. First,
we will explore adaptive boundary-tolerant matching, in which the tolerance parameter δ is dynamically
adjusted based on report characteristics such as layout structure and OCR confidence, so that evaluation
more faithfully reflects clinically acceptable extraction quality. Second, we will study how OCR-induced
noise and human annotation errors affect model training and system reliability, with the goal of improving
robustness in realistic hospital document pipelines. Third, we will investigate incorporating layout and OCR-
noise signals, such as page structure and noise patterns, into representation learning to improve extraction
robustness across more diverse real-world clinical reports and healthcare institutions.
