ABSTRACT
We present extensive optical and near-infrared (NIR) observations of the nearby Type II supernova
(SN II) 2023ixf in the nebular phase from +89 days to +749 days after explosion, supplemented with
NIR and mid-infrared (MIR) spectroscopy from the James Webb Space Telescope. The Hα emission
profile shows complex evolution, with the emergence of high-velocity components consistent with the

Hα profile at an intermediate epoch (around +375 d) can be reconstructed by scaling an earlier

intermediate-width component. This is consistent with the ejecta crashing into the initially aspherical

1. INTRODUCTION
Core-collapse supernovae (CCSNe) mark the explosive
demise of massive stars (≳8 M⊙; S. E. Woosley & T. A.
Weaver 1986) and represent one of the most fundamen-
tal processes that shape the Universe on every scale.
Understanding the explosion mechanism and nucleosyn-
thetic yields of CCSNe are longstanding goals in super-
nova (SN) science. Modern three-dimensional (3D) sim-
ulations reveal that many aspects of CCSNe are gener-
ically asymmetric24, including the pre-explosion stellar
envelopes (A. Chiavassa et al. 2009, 2010; J. A. Gold-
berg et al. 2022a,b, 2026), stellar cores (C. E. Fields &
S. M. Couch 2020, 2021; N. Yadav et al. 2020; F. Riz-
zuti et al. 2024), circumstellar material (CSM; B. T.-H.
Tsang et al. 2022; S. Orlando et al. 2024; J.-Z. Ma et al.
2025), and explosion mechanisms (H.-T. Janka 2012; A.
Wongwathanarat et al. 2015, 2017; A. Burrows et al.
2019, 2020; B. M¨uller et al. 2017; D. Vartanyan et al.
2019, 2022, 2025a,b; M. A. Sandoval et al. 2021). These
large-scale asymmetries have been confirmed observa-
tionally via a multitude of approaches, including strong
spectropolarimetric signatures (e.g., D. C. Leonard &
A. V. Filippenko 2001; D. C. Leonard et al. 2006), re-
solved imaging of SN 1987A (J. Larsson et al. 2019)
and SN remnant Cassiopeia A (D. Milisavljevic et al.
2024), and dynamical kicks measured in neutron stars
(G. Hobbs et al. 2005).
Despite major advances in self-consistent 3D simu-
lations, the detailed distribution of material in CCSN
ejecta remains a central unresolved problem.
Most
state-of-the-art 3D simulations of the core-collapse
mechanism do not calculate radiation hydrodynamics
beyond ∼1 s after core bounce, after which some

3D processes continue to synthesize heavy elements (T.

At epochs ≳100 days post-explosion, the opacity in
the SN ejecta drops significantly, and the spectra are
dominated by a combination of permitted and forbidden
emission lines powered by high-energy photons from the
radioactive decay chain of 56Ni→56Co →56Fe (D. K.
Nadyozhin 1994). These nebular lines help probe the
elemental distribution and asymmetries in the ejecta
through line strengths, shapes, and widths. Historically,
optical and near-infrared (NIR) emission lines like Hα,
[O I] λλ6300, 6364, [Ca II] λλ7291, 7324, [Fe II] 1.257,
1.644 µm, and Mg I 1.504, 1.711 µm have been used to
inform our understanding of the structure and chemical
distribution in CCSN ejecta (e.g., A. Elmhamdi et al.
2003; M. Modjaz et al. 2008; S. Taubenberger et al. 2009;
K. Maguire et al. 2010; D. Milisavljevic et al. 2010; A.
Jerkstrand et al. 2012, 2014, 2015b; S. Bose et al. 2019;
T. Szalai et al. 2019). Persistent asymmetries observed
in Hα and [O I] λλ6300, 6364 have been highlighted
as direct evidence for ejecta asymmetries and the pres-
ence of dust (J. E. Andrews et al. 2010; H. Kuncar-
ayakti et al. 2015; A. Bevan et al. 2017; A. Bevan 2018;
Q. Fang et al. 2022, 2024). Spherically symmetric one-
dimensional (1D) models have also been used to infer
He core mass, which is a direct tracer of the progeni-
tor’s initial mass (A. Jerkstrand et al. 2012).

Optical and NIR emission lines are subject to sig-
nificant blending with nearby species, telluric absorp-
tion (if taken from ground-based facilities), and atten-
uation from any newly formed dust within the ejecta,
complicating the interpretation of line widths and pro-
file shapes.
Synergy of optical and NIR spectra with
mid-infrared (MIR) observations, where emission lines
are more isolated and less attenuated, mitigates these
limitations. Such observations of CCSNe, however, are
few and far between, limited to very nearby events like
SN 1987A observed with the Kuiper Airborne Observa-
tory (D. H. Wooden et al. 1993), as well as SN 2004dj
(T. Szalai et al. 2011), SN 2004et (R. Kotak et al. 2009),
and SN 2005af (R. Kotak et al. 2006) observed with the
Spitzer Space Telescope.
Now, the superior sensitiv-
ity and resolution of the James Webb Space Telescope
(JWST), in conjunction with high-quality, high-cadence
ground-based optical and NIR observations, provide the
necessary information to infer the structure of CCSN
ejecta in unprecedented detail (J. Larsson et al. 2023;
D. Milisavljevic et al. 2024; L. Dessart et al. 2025b; K.
Medler et al. 2025, 2026).
In this work, we present new optical and NIR obser-
vations of the nearby Type II SN 2023ixf during its neb-
ular phase from +89 days to +749 days after explosion
and analyze its near-concurrent optical-to-MIR spectra
to constrain the ejecta geometry and elemental distribu-
tion. SN 2023ixf is one of the closest hydrogen-rich SNe
(SNe II) observed in the past few decades (D. A. Per-
ley et al. 2023), discovered on 2023 May 19 17:27:15.00
UTC (K. Itagaki 2023) in the Pinwheel galaxy (M101).

2.2. Optical Spectroscopy
In this section, we briefly describe the telescopes and
instruments used to obtain our optical spectra, as well
as their respective reduction processes.
• FTN 2 m:
We obtained 18 epochs of opti-
cal spectroscopy with the low-resolution cross-
dispersed FLOYDS spectrograph (T. M. Brown
et al. 2013) on Las Cumbres Observatory’s 2 m
Faulkes Telescope North (FTN) at Haleakal¯a Ob-
servatory (Hawai’i, USA) as part of the Global
Supernova Project collaboration (D. A. Howell
& Global Supernova Project 2017). All observa-
tions were taken with a 2.0′′-wide slit and a 235
lines mm−1 grating covering a spectral range of
∼3200 −10, 000 ˚A with a resolution of R ≈400–
700 depending on the wavelength. 1D spectra were
extracted, reduced, and calibrated following stan-
dard procedures using the FLOYDS pipeline (S.
Valenti et al. 2014).
• MMT 6.5 m: Multiple epochs of spectroscopy
were obtained with the 6.5 m MMT telescope,
with either the Blue Channel (6 epochs) spectro-
graph (J. R. P. Angel et al. 1979; G. D. Schmidt
et al. 1989) or Binospec (D. Fabricant et al. 2019;
8 epochs). The MMT/Blue Channel observations
were taken with a 1.0′′ slit, two of which using
the 1200 lines mm−1 grating (R ≈3340) cover-
ing a range of ∼5700 −7000 ˚A, and one using
the 300 lines mm−1 grating (R ≈740) covering
a range of ∼3600 −9000 ˚A.
Standard reduc-
tions for MMT/Blue Channel observations were
carried out using IRAF (D. Tody 1986), includ-
ing bias subtraction, flat-fielding, and optical ex-
traction of the spectra.
Flux calibrations were
achieved using spectrophotometric standards ob-
served at an airmass similar to that of each science
frame, and the resulting spectra were median com-
bined into a single 1D spectrum for each epoch.
The MMT/Binospec observations were taken with
a 1.0′′ slit using the 270 lines mm−1 grating
(R ≈1340) covering a range of ∼3850 −9150 ˚A.
All data were reduced either using the Binospec
pipeline (J. Kansky et al. 2019), which includes
an internal flux calibration into relative flux units
from throughput measurements of spectrophoto-
metric standard stars.
• LBT 2×8.4 m:
Eight epochs of spectroscopy
were observed with the Multi-Object Double Spec-
trographs (MODS; P. L. Byard & T. P. O’Brien
2000; R. W. Pogge et al. 2010) on the 2×8.4 m
Large Binocular Telescope (LBT). The data were
obtained in dichroic mode using both the G400L
(3500–5900 ˚A) and the G670L (5400–10, 000 ˚A)
gratings on the blue and red channels on each of
the two identical MODS1 and MODS2 spectro-
graphs, achieving a resolution of R ≈2000. Raw
data were first bias and flat-field corrected using
the modsCCDred package (R. Pogge 2019a,b), then
extracted and flux calibrated using IRAF (D. Tody
1986).
• Keck I 10 m: Three spectra were taken with
the Low-Resolution Imaging Spectrometer (LRIS;
J. B. Oke et al. 1995) on the 10 m Keck I telescope
and reduced using the LPipe package (D. A. Perley
2019).
• Bok 2.3 m: Five epochs of spectroscopy were
taken with the Boller & Chivens (B&C) spectro-
graph on the Bok 2.3 m telescope with a 1.5′′ slit
using the 300 lines mm−1 grating (R ≈700) cov-
ering a range of ∼4000−9000 ˚A. Standard reduc-
tions were carried out using IRAF (D. Tody 1986)
in a similar manner as MMT/Blue Channel obser-
vations.
• Lick 3 m: Three epochs of optical spectra were
obtained with the Kast spectrograph on the 3 m
Shane reflector at Lick Observatory. Kast spec-
tra were reduced using standard IRAF (D. Tody
1986)/PyRAF (Science Software Branch at STScI
2012) and Python routines for bias/overscan sub-
tractions and flat-fielding.

3. SPECTRAL EVOLUTION
The nebular-phase spectral evolution of SN 2023ixf is
shown up to +749 d in the optical in Figure 2 and up to
+695 d in the NIR in Figure 3. All spectra have been
shifted to rest-frame wavelengths assuming a redshift of
z = 0.000804 for M101 (G. de Vaucouleurs et al. 1995),

3.1. Hydrogen and Helium: Emergence of
Shock-Powered Emission
Emission from H dominates the nebular spectra of
SN 2023ixf, with a particularly complicated evolution
in the Hα line profile (see Figure 4).
Between +89
d and +139 d, Hα displays a broad, asymmetric, and
blueshifted emission profile. In SNe II, blueshifted Hα is
commonly interpreted as a consequence of optical-depth
effects associated with steep density gradients in the ex-
panding material (L. Dessart & D. J. Hillier 2005; J. P.
Anderson et al. 2014) or dust attenuation (L. B. Lucy
et al. 1989; A. Bevan & M. J. Barlow 2016; A. Bevan
et al. 2017). In the former case, the Hα emission peak
is expected to migrate toward zero velocity as the ejecta
continue to expand. In SN 2023ixf, however, we observe
a persistently blueshifted Hα line profile, in addition
to multiple peaks and a slightly slanted shape, which
has been suggested as evidence for asymmetric ejecta
of other SNe in the absence of dust (e.g., V. P. Utrobin
et al. 1995, 2021; A. Elmhamdi et al. 2003; N. N. Chugai
et al. 2005; L. Tomasella et al. 2013; S. Bose et al. 2015;
J. E. Andrews et al. 2019; S. Bose et al. 2019).
At +199 d, a blue flux excess appears at approxi-
mately −7500 km s−1, earlier than a similar feature first
reported in the +259 d spectrum by L. Ferrari et al.
(2024), who marked it as unidentified. Multiple works
have since interpreted this evolving feature as shock-
powered emission from the fast outer edge of H-rich
ejecta interacting with low-density CSM sitting farther
out from SN 2023ixf’s progenitor (∼2 × 1016 cm by
+199 d, assuming the outermost ejecta are expanding at
a velocity of 13, 500 km s−1 based on the high-velocity
absorption feature in Hα; A. Singh et al. 2024; G. Fo-
latelli et al. 2025; W. V. Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2025; A.
Kumar et al. 2025; G. Li et al. 2025; P. D. Michel et al.
2025; W. Zheng et al. 2025). This interpretation is con-
sistent with late-time X-ray, ultraviolet (UV), and radio
observations of SN 2023ixf (R. Timmerman et al. 2024;
K. A. Bostroem et al. 2026; Y. Iwata et al. 2025; W. V.

3.3. IMEs: Revealing the Inner Ejecta
The nebular-phase spectra of SN 2023ixf exhibit
prominent emission from IMEs such as O, Mg, Ne, Ar,
and Ca, which together trace the composition and struc-
ture of the inner ejecta. Emission from [O I] λλ6300,
6364 is one of the strongest features in the optical spec-
tra of SN 2023ixf, which emerges almost immediately af-
ter the drop from the plateau. Between +89 d and +113
d, the [O I] doublet appears largely blended, although
two individual peaks symmetric about 6300 ˚A are dis-
tinguishable. By +138 d, a clear double-peaked struc-
ture separated by ∼64 ˚A (corresponding with the wave-
length separation of the [O I] λ6300 and λ6364 lines)
appears, with a blueshift of ∼1600 km s−1 that persists
throughout the entire nebular phase. The blueshifted
[O I] λ6300 line itself has a distinct “notch”, which is
likely due to telluric absorption by O2 based on sev-
eral of our higher-resolution MMT/Blue Channel spec-
tra and the echelle spectra presented by N. Smith et al.
(2023). A short-lived, boxy emission at ∼6150 ˚A that
fades within the first 50 days of the nebular phase can
be attributed to Ba II λ6142. The early appearance of
[O I] may also point to a partially stripped progenitor
(A. Elmhamdi 2011) for SN 2023ixf, which is consis-
tent with previous studies of light-curve and radiative-
transfer modeling (e.g., Q. Fang et al. 2025; B. Hsu et al.
2025; L. Dessart et al. 2026a). Additional emission lines
of O, both forbidden and permitted, are also detected,
including [O I] λ5577, O I λ7774, O I λ8446, O I λ9264,
and O I 1.129 µm. However, many of these identifica-
tions are subject to significant uncertainties owing to
blending and line shifts. For example, the blueshifted
emission near [O I] λ5577 may instead arise from [Fe II]
or [Co II] (J. C. Houck & C. Fransson 1996; D. Milisavl-
jevic et al. 2010; A. Jerkstrand et al. 2014), O I λ7774
could be contaminated by telluric absorptions and K I
λλ7665, 7699 (R. Chornock et al. 2010; L. Dessart et al.
2013; J. M. Silverman et al. 2017), and O I 1.129 µm is
likely blended with [S I] 1.131 µm. Most of these weaker
O emission lines fade or become very weak by ∼+300
d.
Emission from Mg becomes prominent later in the
nebular phase compared to O. Beginning at +199 d,
Mg I] λ4571 emerges as a blueshifted feature with a
slanted profile and an extended red tail.
The Mg I]
λ4571 flux relative to the local continuum increases as
the ejecta expand.
At these early epochs, however,
the emission may still be partially blended with lines
from IGEs (K. Maeda et al. 2006; A. Jerkstrand et al.
2015a).
The line profile of Mg I] λ4571 decreases in
width between +248 d and +578 d, before fading by
∼+600 d. In the NIR, strong recombination emission
from Mg I 1.504 µm is detected at +200 d, although its
earliest emergence can be traced back to +81 d post-
explosion (G. Li et al. 2025).

at +1600 km s−1 is also visible in the Mg I 1.504 µm line

3.4. IGEs: Clues to Explosion Geometry and Mixing
Radiative-transfer models of SNe II predict a forest
of permitted and forbidden iron lines, primarily from
Fe I and Fe II. In the optical, no isolated Fe line is de-
tected. In the NIR, emission from [Fe II] 1.257 µm and
[Fe II] 1.644 µm are the strongest features, where [Fe II]
1.257 µm is blended with the red shoulder of Paβ. How-
ever, the observed intensity of [Fe II] 1.644 µm is much
stronger than that of [Fe II] 1.257 µm, which is in ten-
sion with the intensity ratio predicted by theory and
previous observations (I1.644 µm/I1.257 µm ≈0.7 −0.77;
E. Oliva 1987; H. Nussbaumer & P. J. Storey 1988;
N. Smith & P. Hartigan 2006). In addition, the emis-
sion complex around 1.6–1.65 µm consists of two nearly
identical line profiles consistent with [Si I] 1.606, 1.645
µm. We therefore conclude that [Si I] 1.645 µm makes
a nonnegligible contribution to the emission structure
at ∼1.66 −1.67 µm, contrary to model predictions (L.
Dessart 2025). A similar argument likely applies to the
multipeaked [Fe II] line profiles recently reported in the
nebular spectra of SN 2024ggi, given the mismatch in
velocity between [Fe II] 1.257 µm and [Fe II] 1.644 µm
(L. Ferrari et al. 2025; E. Hueichap´an et al. 2025). In
addition, we detect [Fe II] 1.321 µm, [Fe II] 1.534 µm,
[Fe II] 1.599 µm, Fe II 1.677 µm, and Fe II 1.809 µm.

5. LINE-PROFILE ANALYSIS
The opacity drops significantly during the nebular
phase, and emission-line profiles reveal the geometry and
composition of the ejecta. In the following section, we
follow the line-profile fitting procedure outlined by L. A.
Kwok et al. (2023) (using scipy.optimize.curve fit;
P. Virtanen et al. 2020) to fit the profiles of a few promi-
nent or interesting emission features, motivated primar-
ily by geometric configurations. If multiple lines from a
particular atom or ion fall within the fitted wavelength
region of interest (for example, the [O I] λλ6300, 6364
doublet), they are required to have the same parameters,
including offset, FWHM, and profile shape. As the main
goal of this work is to constrain the ejecta geometry and
elemental distribution, we focus the bulk of our analysis
on observations around +250 d and +375 d where ra-
dioactive decay still dominates the heating source and
where full optical-to-MIR coverage is available.
Note
that while several studies have implicated the presence
of dust in the ejecta of SN 2023ixf through CO emission,
strong thermal infrared (IR) excess, and time-dependent
asymmetries in emission-line profiles (W. V. Jacobson-
Gal´an et al. 2025; K. Medler et al. 2025; S. H. Park
et al. 2025; A. Singh et al. 2026), we do not model the
effects of dust absorption or scattering in this section,
as detailed radiative transfer is required.
We discuss
how dust formation affects the derived ejecta structure
in Section 6.3.

5.4. Multicomponent [Ca II] Emission
In SNe II, Ca II emission may arise from two spatially
distinct regions within the ejecta: primordial Ca in the
H-rich envelope (H. Li & R. McCray 1993; C. Kozma
& C. Fransson 1998; A. Jerkstrand et al. 2012; P. D.
Michel et al. 2025) or a more centrally concentrated
component produced by explosive nucleosynthesis (A.
Jerkstrand et al. 2015a; L. Dessart et al. 2021a; Q. Fang
et al. 2024). Because the two lines of the [Ca II] doublet
share a common lower level (the ground state of Ca II)
and have similar transition probabilities (D. E. Oster-
brock 1951; B. Warner 1968; J. H. Black et al. 1972; H.
Li & R. McCray 1993), their intensity ratio in the opti-
cally thin limit in LTE is set by the ratio of the statistical
weights of their upper levels, I7291/I7324 = 3/2 (J. Spy-
romilio et al. 1993). Motivated by the presence of both
blue and red flux excesses near zero velocity, which we
associate with [Ca II] (see Section 3.3), we explore two
geometric configurations: (1) a central narrow Gaussian
component combined with a broader asymmetric shell,
and (2) three distinct Gaussian components. The latter
configuration is partly motivated by the double-peaked
profiles observed in Na I, Mg I, and [Ni I] (see Sec-
tion 5.1). For both configurations, we fix the separation
between the doublet components to ∆v ≈1358 km s−1,
corresponding to a wavelength separation of ∆λ = 33 ˚A.
Here, we apply the modeling framework presented by
A. Jerkstrand et al. (2015b) to characterize the emis-
sion complex in the wavelength range 7000–7600 ˚A,
which has been applied to several CCSNe, including
SN 2023ixf, in previous studies (e.g., G. Terreran et al.
2016; C. P. Guti´errez et al. 2020; J. Sollerman et al. 2021;
S. J. Prentice et al. 2022; P. D. Michel et al. 2025). The
spectral features considered in this regime are [Ca II]
λλ7291, 7324, [Fe II] λλ7155, 7172, [Ni II] λλ7378, 7412,
and [Fe II] λλ7388, 7453. The relative intensity ratios
for [Fe II] and [Ni II] are adopted from A. Jerkstrand
et al. (2015b). We use the resolution-corrected FWHM
and velocity offset measured from a Gaussian fit to the
MIR [Ni II] 6.636 µm line to constrain the [Ni II] λλ7378,
7412 doublet. As no relatively isolated [Fe II] emission is
identified, we assume for simplicity that all [Fe II] lines
in this region share a Gaussian profile with the same
FWHM and velocity offset as the MIR [Co II] 10.521 µm
emission. This choice is motivated by the expectation
that [Fe II] and [Co II] trace similar spatial distributions
within the ejecta. We allow an additional uncertainty of
±500 km s−1 in both FWHM and velocity offset for all
lines constrained using NIR and MIR templates.
In either geometric configuration, the central Gaus-
sian component remains relatively unchanged and op-
tically thin throughout, with an intensity ratio of
I7291/I7324 = 3/2, consistent with theoretical expecta-
tions. In the asymmetric-shell configuration, the broad
component shows only a modest evolution in width
from FWHM ≈2700 km s−1 at +208 d to FWHM ≈
2500 km s−1 at +378 d.
The central cavity associ-
ated with the asymmetric shell also remains stable over
time, with vin ≈1600 km s−1 and vc ≈−100 km s−1
across all three epochs.
In contrast with the cen-
tral Gaussian core, the broad asymmetric-shell com-
ponents evolve from being marginally optically thick
(I7291/I7324 ≈0.93 at +208 d) to becoming increasingly
optically thin (I7291/I7324 ≈0.77 by +378 d). On the
other hand, the redshifted components grow progres-
sively optically thin in the multi-Gaussian configuration,
while the blueshifted component remains optically thin
throughout, possibly indicating a denser Ca-emitting re-
gion away from our line of sight.
We also attempted to fit [Ca II] using two skewed
Gaussian components, following the approach adopted
for other double-peaked emission lines.
However, we
find that no physically reasonable combination of pa-
rameters is able to reproduce the observed line profiles.
Given the multipeaked nature in other IMEs and also
several H transitions, we favor and show only the best-
fit [Ca II] profiles with multiple Gaussians in Figure 13.
Notably, while the red wing of the emission complex
around [Ca II] is well matched by four Gaussian-shaped
[Fe II] and [Ni II] emission lines, the blue wing has two
peaks around the zero velocity of [Fe II] λ7155. This
could mean that emission from [Fe II] also possesses
double-peaked profiles. On the other hand, telluric ab-
sorption by O2 (B band) near this wavelength range in-
troduce significant uncertainties in the continuum place-
ment, as well as the inferred amplitudes and profiles of
the [Fe II] λλ7155, 7172 lines. As a result, the empiri-
cal fits in this region should be regarded as approximate
rather than fully physically constrained. Although the
precise line-profile structure responsible for the extended
emission on either side of [Ca II] remains uncertain, mul-
tiple Ca-emitting regions are clearly required to repro-
duce the observed profiles.

6. DISCUSSION
6.1. Inferred Ejecta Structure
The diversity in line profiles analyzed in Section 5
suggests a highly nonspherical distribution of material
within the ejecta. Our findings are similar to the recent
study of SN 2024ggi by W. V. Jacobson-Gal´an et al.
(2026), another very nearby SN II with JWST cover-
age in the nebular phase, where similar double-peaked
profiles in [Ni I] and Mg I were identified. State-of-the-
art 3D hydrodynamic simulations consistently predict
asymmetric ejection of Ni-rich material in large-scale
structures composed of several plumes. These plumes
can evolve into Rayleigh–Taylor instability (RTI) “fin-
gers” as they interact with reverse shocks developed at
compositional interfaces (D. Vartanyan et al. 2025a,b).
A key outcome of these simulations is that Ni-rich
plumes can penetrate multiple layers with distinct com-
positions, reaching the base of, or even extending be-
yond, the H/He interface, thereby producing asymmet-
ric heating of the ejecta (see, e.g., A. Wongwathanarat
et al. 2015; M. A. Sandoval et al. 2021).
Within this framework, many of the line profiles an-
alyzed in Section 5 can be understood as the combined
result of an asymmetric explosion geometry and strat-
ified elemental distribution. In particular, the double-
peaked profile of [Ni I] 3.12 µm likely arises from dense,
localized (maybe even self-shielded) Ni clumps where re-
combination occurs more effectively (C.-Y. Wang 2005;
L. Dessart et al. 2018), while emission from singly ion-
ized IGEs such as [Ni II] and [Co II] trace warmer,
more diffuse, and volume-filling regions associated with
cavities carved out by the nickel-bubble effect. Emis-
sion from singly ionized IMEs ([Ar II] and [Ne II]) then
trace a region close to the nickel bubbles (or potentially
mixed within) to achieve their higher ionization poten-
tials (R. Kotak et al. 2006; L. Dessart 2025), consistent
with their comparable FWHMs to singly ionized IGEs.
By contrast, emission from neutral IMEs such as Mg I
and Na I, which primarily form via recombination (A.
Jerkstrand et al. 2015a), likely originates in compressed
regions around these Ni-rich cavities.
The strong evidence for intrinsic ejecta asphericities
inferred from our line-profile modeling is consistent with
late-time spectropolarimetric observations of SN 2023ixf
(up to ∼120 days post-explosion; A. Singh et al. 2024;
M. Shrestha et al. 2025; S. S. Vasylyev et al. 2026), which
showed an increase in polarization as the SN transitions
from the photospheric phase to the nebular phase. S. S.
Vasylyev et al. (2026) argued that the spectropolarimet-
ric evolution of SN 2023ixf can be explained by a bipo-
lar explosion, a scenario commonly invoked for strongly
interacting SNe exhibiting high polarization signals (L.
Wang et al. 2001, 2002; L. Dessart et al. 2021b; C. Bilin-
ski et al. 2024; S. S. Vasylyev et al. 2024). Several in-
dependent studies of SN 2023ixf have reached similar
conclusions based on comparisons of the [O I] λλ6300,
6364 and [Ca II] λλ7291, 7324 line profiles, suggest-
ing an axisymmetric structure in which an O-rich torus
surrounds bipolar lobes of explosively synthesized ma-
terial ejected along the jet direction (L. Ferrari et al.
2024; Q. Fang et al. 2025). A comparable interpretation
was proposed by W. V. Jacobson-Gal´an et al. (2026)
for SN 2024ggi, where pronounced polar enhancements
are required to reproduce the observed IGE and IME
line profiles. While an axisymmetric 56Ni distribution
in SN 2023ixf is plausible, 3D simulations indicate that
the number, morphology, and orientation of dominant
Ni plumes develop turbulently, rather than conforming
to a bipolar distribution with axial symmetry (V. P.

6.2. The CSM Geometry
As discussed in detail in Section 4 and previous stud-
ies (e.g., A. Singh et al. 2024; W. V. Jacobson-Gal´an
et al. 2025; A. Kumar et al. 2025; K. A. Bostroem et al.
2026; L. Dessart et al. 2026b), the broad CSM horns
shared by H and He emission features likely result from
reprocessing of X-rays by the CDS and photoionization
of the fast-moving ejecta. Through our profile decom-
position in Section 4.2, however, we have reasons to sus-
pect that an additional intermediate-width component
(−4000 km s−1 ≲v ≲4000 km s−1) exists and is re-
lated to the dense — and likely aspherical — CSM that
caused the early-time narrow features (W. V. Jacobson-
Gal´an et al. 2023; K. A. Bostroem et al. 2023; N. Smith
et al. 2023) and high polarization signals (S. S. Vasy-
lyev et al. 2023, 2026; A. Singh et al. 2024; M. Shrestha
et al. 2025).
Any disk-like or torus-like dense CSM
around SN 2023ixf’s progenitor must have had a lim-
ited radial extent given the lack of P Cygni absorption
from the unshocked CSM and the early disappearance
of narrow features (N. Smith et al. 2023; D. Dickin-
son et al. 2025). In this scenario, the fast ejecta engulf
the CSM, and any sustained interaction signatures are
hidden below the optically thick photosphere. As the
photosphere recedes, the post-shock CSM emerges and
contributes intermediate-width emission from the ejecta
crashing into the post-shock gas (see N. Smith et al.
2015).
Such an emergence of underlying emission from the
ejecta interacting with an aspherical CSM has also been
demonstrated by 2D radiation-hydrodynamic simula-
tions (P. Kurf¨urst et al. 2020, 2026).
By +749 d, it
is likely that all of the slowest moving CSM is already
swept up by the shock, given the lack of narrow emission.
The swept-up CSM disk is another location in addition
to the ejecta that is cool and dense enough for dust
growth, which would explain the lack of a redshifted
peak in the intermediate-width component.
Together
with our interpretations for an asymmetric ejecta, we
show our inferred ejecta and CSM composite structure
for SN 2023ixf in Figure 14. The picture described here
is essentially a combination of the respective evolution
suggested for PTF11iqb and iPTF14hls (N. Smith et al.
2015; J. E. Andrews & N. Smith 2018).
6.3. Effects of Dust
The presence of dust within the ejecta of CCSNe has a
profound impact on the interpretation of ejecta geome-
try based on nebular line-profile modeling alone. Visual-
wavelength emission-line signatures of dust formation
in CCSNe were first firmly established in the case of
SN 1987A (I. J. Danziger et al. 1989; L. B. Lucy et al.
1989), identified as a progressive blueshift in the [O I]
λλ6300, 6364 and [C I] λλ9824, 9850 profiles.
These
were attributed to attenuation of emission from the re-
ceding side of the ejecta. Similar behavior has since been
reported in numerous SNe (e.g., R. Kotak et al. 2009; K.
Maguire et al. 2010; N. Smith et al. 2008b, 2012; J. Rho
et al. 2018; N. Elias-Rosa et al. 2018; N. Smith & J. E.
Andrews 2020; D. Dickinson et al. 2024; S. Zs´ıros et al.
2024), establishing red-wing suppression as an indicator
of internal dust extinction. In SN 2023ixf, strong line-
profile asymmetries are clearly present, particularly in
multiple H transitions (see Section 3.1; A. Singh et al.
2024; W. V. Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2025; K. Medler et al.
2025; A. Singh et al. 2026), as well as in optical [O I]
and Mg I] emission.
In addition, emission of rotational-vibrational transi-
tions from CO was observed at +199 d for first-overtone
bands and at +253 d for fundamental bands (K. Medler
et al. 2025; S. H. Park et al. 2025).
Molecular emis-
sion signals efficient cooling of the ejecta and is often
a hallmark signature prior to the onset of dust forma-
tion. Analyses of the thermal IR emission and radiative-
transfer modeling of Hα by A. Singh et al. (2026) pro-
vided direct evidence for multicomponent dust growth
(with a cold silicate component and a cold graphite com-
ponent of ∼10−3 M⊙, each by +723 d). In particular,
A. Singh et al. (2026) found that a clumpy dust distri-
bution with modest total effective dust mass can reason-

7. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we present extensive optical and NIR
observations of SN 2023ixf in the nebular phase (+89 d
to +749 d after explosion). Together with supplemen-
tal JWST spectra, we empirically fit a few interesting
emission-line profiles to constrain the structure of the
ejecta and the CSM. We summarize our key findings
below.
• Our optical and NIR spectral series of SN 2023ixf
reveal a plethora of emission lines from atomic and
ionic transitions, including typical nebular emis-
sion lines seen in other SNe II like Mg I] λ4571,
[O I] λλ6300, 6364, Hα, [Ca II] λλ7291, 7324, and
the Ca II NIR triplet.

APPENDIX
A. EFFECTS OF MIRI/LRS RESOLUTION ON DOUBLE-PEAKED PROFILES
In this Appendix, we investigate whether the broad, symmetric line profiles from predominantly singly ionized IMEs
and IGEs — with the sole exception of [Ni I] 11.308 µm, which arises from neutral stable nickel — are due to resolution
effects or intrinsic geometric distributions. Specifically, we test the effects of resolution on our ability to identify any
potential double peaks in these longer-wavelength MIR lines. For each line, we smooth and resample a double-peaked
profile template to match the observed resolution at the central wavelength of the line under consideration. We use
the best-fit [Ni I] 3.12 µm model for [Ni I] 11.308 µm, [Ni II] 6.636 µm, and [Co II] 10.521 µm, and the best-fit Mg I
1.504 µm model for [Ar I] 6.985 µm and [Ne II] 12.813 µm. As shown in Figure A1, an intrinsically double-peaked
profile with a characteristic peak separation of ∼2800 km s−1 is only marginally resolvable at the lower resolution of
MIRI/LRS. Given the likely differing physical conditions (e.g., a lower density or a higher temperature; L. Dessart
2025; W. V. Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2026) required for these lines from mostly singly ionized IMEs and IGEs, it is
possible that they have a smaller peak separation, which becomes indistinguishable at a lower resolution.
