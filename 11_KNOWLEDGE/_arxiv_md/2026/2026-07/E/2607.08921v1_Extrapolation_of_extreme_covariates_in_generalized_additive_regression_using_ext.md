Extrapolation of extreme covariates in generalized additive regression
using extreme-value theory
Viviana Carcaiso∗, Sebastian Engelke†, Juliette Legrand‡, and Thomas Opitz∗
∗Unit´e Biostatistique et Processus Spatiaux, INRAE, France

Abstract
We propose methods to enhance the predictive performance of generalized additive models
(GAMs) in the context of covariate extrapolation, where predictions rely on covariates beyond
their observed range. When using predictive models such as GAMs, shifts in the covariate distribu-
tion between training and prediction datasets can occur. Ignoring this issue may lead to inaccurate
predictions in the tail of the covariate distributions. For example, this problem is particularly criti-
cal in climate-change scenarios, where covariates simulated from future climate scenarios are likely
to contain more extreme conditions. Our approach integrates GAMs for the bulk of covariate dis-
tributions with asymptotic models from multivariate extreme-value theory at high covariate values.
We consider binary responses based on a latent variable assumption, and also continuous responses.
For large values of the covariates, on a specific marginal scale motivated by extreme-value theory
the latent variable or continuous response is assumed to depend linearly on the covariates with
an additive error term, when using an appropriate link function. In an application to wildfires in
Europe, we explore how the new method can improve predictions, using environmental and mete-
orological covariates.
key words: covariate extrapolation; generalized additive models; multivariate extreme-value theory; pre-
dictive modeling.

Introduction
We consider a random vector (Y, X) composed of a response variable Y and one or several covariates
given by the random vector X, so that we aim to predict Y using information from X. We address the
problem of ensuring a reliable model when X is extreme, i.e., when covariates are located in the right
and left tails of their distribution, where only few observations are available for training the predictive
model.
Specifically, we seek model structures that allow for appropriate covariate extrapolation,
providing predictions that remain as accurate as possible when new covariates are more extreme than
those observed during training. In the random-design setting with X considered as a random vector,
the conditional distribution of Y given X can be viewed as the optimal predictive distribution, since
it uniquely minimizes the expected loss for proper scoring rules (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007).
Several issues for covariate extrapolation arise in commonly used prediction frameworks. Many
tree-based machine learning methods perform constant extrapolation, meaning that predictions re-
main unchanged when a component xj exceeds the range of the training data, which is unrealistic in
many applications. This behavior occurs in standard tree-based methods, including classification and
regression trees (Breiman et al., 1984), Random Forests (Breiman, 2001; Biau, 2012), and extreme
gradient boosting (xgboost) (Chen et al., 2015). By contrast, generalized linear models (GLMs) ex-
trapolate beyond the observed covariate range, except when the estimated coefficient is exactly zero,
but they are often too rigid to capture complex nonlinear behavior. Even when a GLM fits well over
the bulk of the covariate distribution, its behavior for extreme covariates can be unsatisfactory, and

especially for heavy-tailed covariates. Semi-parametric basis-function approaches, such as splines in
generalized additive models (GAMs), provide greater flexibility and non-constant extrapolation, but
the fitted curves are only weakly constrained near the boundaries of the training data, leading to high

too heavy- or light-tailed relative to the distribution of Y , implying an inappropriate specification of
the response distribution.
To define suitable model structures for the joint distribution of Y and extreme X, we draw on sev-
eral multivariate asymptotic frameworks commonly used for statistical modeling and tail extrapolation
of extreme events. To facilitate the construction and validation of predictive models with robust ex-
trapolation behavior, we assume a linear relationship between extreme covariates and the response on
appropriately chosen marginal scales. Such structures arise naturally in the multivariate extreme-value
frameworks we consider when marginal distributions are suitably standardized. Linear tail structures
provide monotone extrapolation and are relatively easy to fit, validate, and interpret. We therefore
propose models that remain flexible and nonlinear in the bulk of the covariate distribution (using
GAMs) while transitioning towards linear behavior in the tails. In our framework, reliable covariate
extrapolation hinges on the appropriate combination of three elements: marginal transformations of
X and Y , the response distribution used for the conditional distribution of Y , and the link function
relating the linear tail structure to a parameter of the response distribution. Figure 1 illustrates sev-
eral stylized scenarios for extreme covariates (see Appendix A for more details). It highlights that
the quality of tail extrapolation is highly sensitive to the interplay of these three elements, even when
the model provides a satisfactory fit over the bulk of the covariate distribution. Our work provides
recommendations for useful combinations of marginal scale, link function, and response distribution
supported by asymptotic theory.

clarifies how current approaches can be applied in extrapolation settings, offering a unified view of
current methodologies, and leads to the proposal of new models that capture both central and extreme
covariate behavior.
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review several frameworks for
multivariate extremes and adapt them to construct predictive models with linear structure in the tails
of the covariates. Section 3 discusses statistical aspects of these theoretical results, while Section 4
shows how to combine marginal scale, link function, and response distribution for each approach.
Section 5 presents a simulation study, Section 6 illustrates the proposed methods using wildfire data,
and Section 7 concludes with a discussion of the methodology, its limitations, and possible extensions.

Prediction based on frameworks of multivariate extremes
Different frameworks have been proposed to describe the dependence structure of multivariate random
vectors, including specific approaches for extreme events. We review, compare, and adapt them to the
setting where the vector (Y, X) is composed of covariates X and a response Y , with extreme events
4


## Page 5


arising specifically in X. Throughout, (Y, X) is assumed to have continuous marginal distributions.
Classical multivariate extreme-value theory relies on tail regularity assumptions formalized through
multivariate regular variation (De Haan and Ferreira, 2006; Resnick, 2008). Under asymptotic inde-
pendence, however, the limiting dependence structure is degenerate, preventing the construction of
useful statistical models directly from the limit (Huser et al., 2025). This has motivated the devel-
opment of more refined approaches that capture the rate at which joint tails converge to asymptotic
independence.
We focus on four frameworks commonly used for statistical modeling of dependence of extremes.
We show that, on appropriate marginal scales for (Y, X), each framework can induce a linear depen-
dence structure between the response and extreme covariates. Superscripts denote variables trans-
formed to a specific marginal scale; marginal transformations are discussed in Section 3.1. For sim-
plicity, we present the theory assuming that all covariates are continuous and subject to extrapolation.
2.1
Asymptotic dependence coefficient for the predictive setting
The strength of tail dependence between variables can be assessed with coefficients from multivariate
extreme-value theory. In our setting with linear extrapolation, we expect responses to become more
extreme when covariates become more extreme. A commonly used measure (Sibuya et al., 1960) is
the tail correlation, defined for two continuous random variables Z1 ∼F1, Z2 ∼F2 as the conditional
probability limit χ = limu→1 Pr(F1(Z1) > u | F2(Z2) > u) ∈[0, 1], if it exists. We here propose
a variant of this coefficient that is useful to evaluate the strength of association in the tail between
covariates and response. Indeed, in a predictive setting it is not obvious which pair of variables should
be used to compute χ. The exception is the case of a single covariate X, where the tail correlation
between X and the response Y is naturally a quantity of interest.
We define the point predictor of a model (if available, as for example in most common GAMs)
as the model’s best single-value prediction for the response at a given set of predictor values, which
in GAMs is the inverse link function applied to the estimated linear predictor. For instance, if the
parameter of interest is the mean, the point predictor is the estimated mean. We ask if extreme events
in the point predictor correspond to extreme Y , i.e., if the point predictor correctly predicts extreme
events. Denote the point predictor ˆY (X) and write ˆF for its distribution function (unconditionally of
X). We define the coefficient of extreme point prediction as

predictor.

3.3
Assessment of prediction performance
To assess model performance, especially for extreme covariates, we use several predictive measures,
including the logarithmic score (Good, 1952) and the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)
(Matheson and Winkler, 1976), two widely used proper scoring rules. More details on proper scoring
rules are given in Appendix D. Weighted proper scoring rules, such as the weighted CRPS of Gneiting
and Ranjan (2011), typically emphasize a specific region of the response distribution, for example
observations above a high threshold. Since our interest lies instead in the region of high values of the
covariates, we define
Sw(F, y, x) = I{r(x) > m}S(F, y),
where r is a risk function as previously, x is a realization of the covariate vector, and m is a high
threshold chosen to produce enough exceedances to guarantee that the computation of Sw(F, y, x) is
still reliable. Weight functions that allow a smooth transition between bulk and right tail of r(x)
could also be used.
For binary responses, we assess performance using the area under the ROC curve (AUC) (for more
details see, for instance, Fawcett, 2006) and the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC) (Saito
and Rehmsmeier, 2015), which is more appropriate for rare events. Both metrics aggregate model
performance across varying classification thresholds. Their weighted counterparts are defined as
Aw(ˆp, y, x) = I{r(x) > m}A(ˆp, y),
where A denotes either AUC or AUPRC, and ˆp is the predicted success probability.
While our focus is primarily on model improvements based on weighted measures, gains for extreme
covariates should not come at the cost of overall performance. As a complement to the unweighted
versions of proper scoring rules, AUC and AUPRC, the Akaike information criterion (AIC) is also
included to measure the overall predictive quality of different models.

The prediction of extreme wildfires is critical in the current European landscape, since their increasing
frequency is becoming animportant issue. In climate change scenarios it can be expected that the

environmental and climatic drivers of wildfires will increase in magnitude, thus covariate extrapolation

Discussion
In climate-change applications, future covariates often include more extreme conditions than those ob-
served before. This mismatch between training and prediction data can severely degrade performance
of predictive models such as GAMs, particularly in the tails of the covariate distributions. By lever-
aging multivariate tail representations, we provide recommendations for combinations of marginal
transformations, response distributions—both binary and continuous—and link functions that are
theoretically supported by multivariate extreme value theory.
GAM implementations such as those in the widely used mgcv package of the R statistical software
already put linearity constraints at the extreme values of the covariate training sample as a default
choice. In addition, we propose using linear extrapolation after first pretransforming the covariates
and response to specific marginal scales. Moreover, we allow for transitions to linearity already within
the range of the covariate training sample by considering the transition threshold as hyperparameter
for which several values can be checked to select the best one.
Our results indicate that model
performance improves when covariates and response (if continous) are pre-transformed to marginal
scales motivated by theoretical results. Additional gains can be achieved by imposing a linear structure
in the tails of the covariates prior to the linearity constraints at the boundary implemented in mgcv.
These improvements are not universal across all settings and datasets; however, the proposed approach
proves to be a useful tool in many cases. We therefore recommend considering its use as part of the
modeling strategy, particularly when tail behavior is of interest.
Constant extrapolation of covariate effects, as imposed by the structure of many standard Machine
Learning models, can be expected to be inappropriate in many applications. Nevertheless, it can be
an accurate choice in cases of saturation effects where further increasing a covariate beyond a large
threshold (or decreasing it below a small threshold) does not further modify the behavior of the
response. Therefore, including a model with constant extrapolation in the list of models to select
from can be useful when the modeling framework does not naturally impose or tend towards constant
extrapolation.
Our method targets moderate extrapolation of covariates when moving slightly beyond the ob-
served range of values. Statistical extrapolation of covariate effects to very extreme quantiles will
always be associated with very high uncertainties, except for cases where the true relationship be-
tween response and covariate actually is linear. Reliable extrapolation would require incorporating
expert knowledge about known mechanisms relating covariates and response into the model, in the

Proper scoring rules
We recall here some details about proper scoring rules (Gneiting and Raftery, 2007), which we use to
compare different models. A scoring rule is called proper if the expected score, calculated with respect
to a given distribution, is minimized when the forecast matches that distribution. It is strictly proper
when this minimum is achieved only when the forecast distribution exactly coincides with the true
distribution.
Proper scoring rules are commonly used in extreme-value theory to assess the predictive perfor-
mance of a model based on a forecast distribution F and an observation y. Indeed, as discussed for
instance in Lerch et al. (2017), common error-based metrics such as the MAE or MSE can be unreli-
able when comparing models based on extreme observations. Two widely used scoring rules are the
logarithmic score (LogS) (Good, 1952),
LogS(F, y) = −log f(y),
where f denotes the density corresponding to F, and the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS)
(Matheson and Winkler, 1976),
