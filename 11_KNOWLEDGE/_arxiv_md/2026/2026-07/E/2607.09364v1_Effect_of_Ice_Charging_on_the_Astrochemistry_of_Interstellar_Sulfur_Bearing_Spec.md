ABSTRACT
Context. Understanding where up to 99% of the expected sulfur is hidden in dense molecular clouds remains one of the long-standing
unresolved problems in astrochemistry. Binding energies (BEs) control desorption, diffusion, and residence times of molecules on
ASW mantles, and hence also determine these input parameters in gas–grain models. The effect of excess negative charge in ASW on
sulfur adsorption remains entirely unexplored.
Aims. We aim to derive statistically robust and physically interpretable BE distributions for atomic S and the sulfur-bearing molecules
H2S, SO2, and OCS on neutral and negatively charged ASW, and assess how excess negative charge alters their retention and potential
role in the sulfur reservoir of cold dense molecular clouds.
Methods. Basis set superposition error (BSSE) and zero-point energy (ZPE) corrected BEs of S, SO2, OCS and H2S, are calculated
by density functional theory (DFT), using the ORCA software. Molecule-specific DFT levels of theory were first selected from
benchmark calculations on neutral and charged small water complexes, against coupled-cluster reference energies. The selected
protocols were then applied to study adsorption on neutral and charged ASW clusters. A range of adsorption sites were sampled on
five independent amorphous ice clusters, yielding BE distributions that account for the site heterogeneity of ASW.
Results. Neutral ASW yields broad, site-dependent BE distributions consistent with previous water-ice estimates. On charged ASW,
three general cases are identified: the BE will either always increase due to electron transfer (S-atom, SO2), the BE increases slightly
without any electron transfer (H2S) or the BE remains the same unless an electron transfer occurs under specific conditions (OCS).
These findings are inherently linked to the molecular properties of these molecules.
Conclusions. Excess negative charge does not uniformly increase sulfur binding on ASW. Instead, it produces molecule-specific
adsorption regimes, showing that charged ASW must be treated explicitly in gas–grain models of sulfur chemistry.
Key words. Astrochemistry – ISM: clouds – ISM: molecules – Methods: numerical
1. Introduction
The interest in understanding the chemical evolution of the in-
terstellar medium (ISM) dates back to the late 1930s and early
1940s, when the unexpected optical detection of CH, CN, and
CH+ provided the first unambiguous evidence that space, despite
its extremely low densities and temperatures, harbors a chem-
ically active environment (Dunham 1937; Swings & Rosenfeld
1937; McKellar 1940; Douglas & Herzberg 1941).
However, the field changed decisively only three decades
later, when breakthrough advances in observational and spec-
troscopic capabilities enabled the detections of polyatomic
molecules (NH3 and H2O) and the organic molecule formalde-
hyde (H2CO) in the ISM (Cheung et al. 1968, 1969), overturning
the widespread belief that interstellar chemistry would remain
limited to diatomics (Snyder 2006).
To date, more than 340 molecular species have been detected
in the ISM and circumstellar medium (CSM) (Mondal et al.
2026; McGuire 2022). This inventory is dominated by acyclic
organic molecules or radicals with carbon-chain backbones,
whereas cyclic species remain comparatively rare. The wide va-
riety of organic molecules detected so far includes aldehydes,
alcohols, acids, amines, and carboxamides, that is, several of the
principal functional groups from which prebiotic chemistry can

emerge. The presence of such chemical diversity, together with
the detection of increasingly complex species, indeed leads to a
clear conclusion: gas-phase chemistry alone cannot account for
the observed molecular richness of the ISM (Herbst & Dishoeck
2009; Ceccarelli et al. 2017).
A significant step forward in our understanding of chem-
istry in the ISM came with the realization that, in cold and
dense interstellar clouds, the observed molecular inventory is in-
separable from the physics and chemistry of dust grains. It is
well known that molecular clouds are the coldest (∼10 K) and
densest (104 - 106 cm−3) regions of the ISM and are of par-
ticular importance because they are the sites where stars and
planets form. In these environments, dust grains with carbona-
ceous or silicate cores become coated by thick water-rich icy
mantles, generally described as amorphous solid water (ASW)
(Boogert et al. 2015; Hama & Watanabe 2013). These icy man-
tles are not passive condensates, but chemically active inter-
faces that can concentrate reactants, catalyse reactions by low-
ering activation barriers, and dissipate excess energy released in
exothermic processes (Cuppen et al. 2017; Minissale et al. 2022;
Ceccarelli et al. 2023). In this context, the binding energy (BE)
of a species to an ASW surface is not a secondary surface pa-
rameter, but one of the key quantities that determines whether
a molecule resides on the ice surface, undergoes surface diffu-
sion, participates in surface reactions, or desorbs into the gas

phase. However, recent work has also demonstrated that ad-
sorption on ASW cannot, in general, be represented by a sin-
gle characteristic such as the BE. Because ASW is structurally
heterogeneous, it presents a manifold of local binding envi-
ronments, so that the adsorption energy depends on the site,
the molecular orientation, and the surrounding H-bond pattern
(Ceccarelli et al. 2023; Ferrero et al. 2020; Perrero et al. 2022b;
Tinacci et al. 2022, 2023). For astrochemical modelling this is a
fundamental consideration: adsorption, desorption, and diffusion
rates depend exponentially on the BE, which means that even
moderate errors or oversimplified single-value representations
can propagate into large errors in predicted abundances, gas-
grain partitioning, and snowline locations (Cuppen et al. 2017;
Minissale et al. 2022; Ceccarelli et al. 2023; Ferrero et al. 2020;
Perrero et al. 2022b; Tinacci et al. 2022, 2023; Bariosco et al.
2024).
The presence of sulfur, the tenth most abundant element in
the Universe and one of the six CHNOPS elements essential
to terrestrial biochemistry, is to date a puzzling issue in astro-
chemistry. In diffuse clouds, sulfur is only mildly depleted and
remains close to its cosmic abundance, whereas in translucent
and dense clouds its observable gas-phase abundance drops by
one to several orders of magnitude (Rodriguez-Baras et al. 2021;
Vidal et al. 2017; Laas & Caselli 2019). The problem does not
disappear in warmer sources. Even in hot cores and hot corinos,
where water-rich icy mantles have sublimated, the sum of de-
tected gaseous sulfur-bearing species still falls far short of the
elemental sulfur abundance (Blake et al. 1994; Wakelam et al.
2004). This long-standing mismatch is well known as the sul-
fur depletion problem: sulfur is not absent from the ISM,
but a substantial fraction of it must be hidden in observa-
tionally elusive volatile, semirefractory, or refractory reservoirs
(Rodriguez-Baras et al. 2021; Vidal et al. 2017; Laas & Caselli
2019; Blake et al. 1994; Wakelam et al. 2004).
The most straightforward grain-surface expectation is that
atomic sulfur, once accreted onto cold grains, should hydro-
genate efficiently to H2S, in close analogy with the forma-
tion of water from atomic oxygen (Caselli et al. 1994). How-
ever, this simple picture fails observationally. To date, OCS
is the only sulfur-bearing molecule securely detected in in-
terstellar ices, while SO2 remains at best tentative; both are
far too scarce to close the sulfur budget (Palumbo et al. 1995;
McClure et al. 2023; Palumbo & Geballe 1997; Sturm et al.
2023; Boogert et al. 2022). H2S, despite being the obvious
hydrogenation product and a widely discussed candidate for
the major sulfur reservoir, has still not been securely iden-
tified in interstellar ices, even in recent JWST-era studies
(McClure et al. 2023; Boogert et al. 2022). The paradox be-
comes even sharper when compared with Solar System relics:
in comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, the summed sulfur in-
ventory approaches the solar sulfur abundance, H2S is one of the
dominant sulfur carriers, and ammonium hydrosulfide salts have
also been identified (Calmonte et al. 2016; Biver et al. 2021;
Altwegg et al. 2022). Sulfur chemistry on grains is therefore
clearly efficient, but the retention, transformation, and release of
sulfur-bearing species in interstellar ices are more intricate than
a simple freeze-out scenario would suggest.
In addition, laboratory studies have further revealed the com-
plexity of this chemistry. Irradiation and photoprocessing of
H2S-containing ices repeatedly yield H2S2, polysulfanes, sul-
fur allotropes, OCS, CS2, and refractory sulfur-rich residues
(Jiménez-Escobar & Caro 2011; Jiménez-Escobar et al. 2014;
Cazaux et al. 2022; Herath et al. 2025). However, the same ex-
periments also demonstrate the limits of the currently avail-
able diagnostics: FTIR spectra suffer from overlapping bands,
quadrupole mass spectrometry can fragment products in the ion
source, and a non-negligible fraction of the sulfur chemistry re-
mains unidentified (Herath et al. 2025). Moreover, temperature-
programmed desorption experiments provide essential con-
straints on BEs, but the extracted values depend on substrate
morphology, surface coverage, pre-desorption diffusion, and the
assumptions underlying the conversion of desorption traces into
BE values (Brown & Bolina 2007; Chaabouni et al. 2018). Al-
though experiment is essential, it does not provide a complete
microscopic picture of sulfur adsorption on realistic interstel-
lar ices. Hence, atomistic modelling has become a widely used
complement to experiment. Over the past decade, theoretical
studies have progressed from single-water proxies and small
clusters to periodic models, large amorphous clusters, ONIOM
approaches, and QM/MM frameworks (Ferrero et al. 2020;
Perrero et al. 2022b; Tinacci et al. 2022, 2023; Bariosco et al.
2024; Duflot et al. 2021; Bovolenta et al. 2022; Sameera et al.
2017; Perrero et al. 2022a). These studies consistently point to
three conclusions relevant to the present work. First, the real-
ism of the ice model has a strong effect on the computed BEs.
Second, amorphous substrates produce a distribution of BE val-
ues rather than a single characteristic value. Third, sulfur-bearing
species require careful treatment, as dispersion interactions,
molecular polarizability, and local site geometry all contribute
to the adsorption energetics (Perrero et al. 2022b; Bariosco et al.
2024).
However, nearly all computational studies have assumed an
electronically neutral ice surface, a convenient approximation
that may not be physically realistic. Interstellar grains are con-
tinuously exposed to cosmic rays, energetic particles, and low-
energy secondary electrons generated during irradiation of con-
densed matter. As a result, grain charging is regarded as an in-
trinsic aspect of interstellar grain microphysics rather than an
exceptional case. In the context of sulfur chemistry, negative
grain charge has been invoked as a factor potentially influenc-
ing sulfur accretion and subsequent solid-state chemistry dur-
ing translucent-to-dense cloud evolution (Laas & Caselli 2019;
Cazaux et al. 2022).
In our previous study of small interstellar molecules ad-
sorbed on neutral and negatively charged ASW, we showed
that the effect of surface charging is strongly species depen-
dent: it can enhance adsorption for some molecules, leave it
largely unchanged for others, and weaken it for yet others
(Vorsselmans & Neyts 2025). This raises the question of whether
sulfur-bearing adsorbates, which span a wide range of electronic
properties, respond to charged ASW in a similarly non-uniform
manner. Here, we present a computational study of the adsorp-
tion of S, H2S, SO2, and OCS on neutral and singly negatively
charged ASW using density functional theory (DFT). We dis-
cuss in detail the differences in BEs for the studied molecules
with statistical significance and compare calculated BE values
with experimental and/or theoretical data reported in the liter-
ature. These results have direct implications for astrochemical
modeling and could shed new light on the sulfur depletion.

Before any calculations were performed, a benchmark study
was performed to identify the best functional/basis set/ disper-
sion correction combination for each molecule. The benchmark
was performed by calculating the BE of a molecule on a water
dimer for a range of functionals, basis sets and dispersion correc-
tions and were tested against the CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ level
of theory (Purvis III & Bartlett 1982; Raghavachari et al. 1989;
Dunning 1989; Kendall et al. 1992). The full benchmark can be
found in Tables A.1-A.4 in Appendix A. Since not only the BEs
on both the neutral and charged ice clusters are important, but
also (and especially) the difference between these two values,
all three values were taken into consideration when choosing the
best functional/basis set/dispersion correction combination. Ta-
ble 1 shows the conclusion from the benchmark study. Thus, we
employ a distinct functional/basis set/dispersion correction com-
bination for every specific molecule so as to ensure optimal ac-
curacy per molecule. This implies that while we cannot directly
compare BEs between molecules, we obtain trustworthy differ-
ences in BE per molecule on a charged ice cluster vs on a neutral
ice cluster.
For every ASW cluster six binding sites were created by sim-
ply taking the positive and negative sides of the x-, y- and z-axes
in a Cartesian coordinate system. The starting configurations are
thus systematically created without any consideration of the ex-
act starting binding site to provide a random coverage of the
whole cluster. Using this strategy rather than manually selecting
binding sites, statistical insight into the BEs is obtained without
premediated bias in the setup of our calculations. This approach
corresponds to a stochastic hit-and-stick process of molecules
landing on a surface. Consistent with this approach, BE averages
are calculated as equal-weight averages (thus assuming no sur-
face diffusion) rather than Boltzmann-weighted averages (which
would assume diffusion and thermal redistribution of molecules
over the available binding sites).
The molecules to be placed on the ASW cluster are H2S,
SO2, S and OCS. These were chosen to have a diverse group of
molecular properties, where H2S can act as both H-bond donor
and acceptor, SO2, S and OCS act only as H-bond acceptors.
The multiplicities of all molecules are singlet, but the S-atom
in its ground state contains two unpaired electrons changing the
multiplicity to a triplet state. For the neutral complexes (ASW
cluster with a molecule), a 0 and 1 charge and multiplicity is
employed for H2S, SO2 and OCS. For S, 0 and 3 charge and
multiplicity is necessary. In the charged state, all complexes have
-1 and 2 charge and multiplicity.
The BE was defined as the negative of the interaction en-
ergy, such that positive values correspond to stable adsorption
and larger BEs indicate stronger molecule–surface interactions,
BE = [(Emolecule + Esur face) −Ecomplex] + ∆EBS S E + ∆EZPE
(1)
Here, Emolecule, Esur face, and Ecomplex denote the total energies
of the isolated adsorbate, the bare ASW cluster, and the opti-
mized adsorption complex, respectively. The basis set superpo-
sition error (BSSE) was corrected using the counterpoise scheme
of Boys & Bernardi (1970). Zero-point energy (ZPE) corrections
were obtained from harmonic vibrational frequencies and added
to the electronic binding energies. The reported BEs therefore
include both BSSE and ZPE corrections
3. Results
3.1. Benchmark and ZPE correction
The results from our benchmark are presented in Tables A.1-A.4
in Appendix A. For each molecule we calculated the BE of each
of the four molecules on a water dimer, testing a range of func-
tional/basis set/dispersion correction combinations by compari-
son to CCSD(T)/aug-cc-pVTZ. For each molecule we took into
consideration the error of the neutral BE (Error0), the error of the
charged BE (Error−1) and the error of the change in BE (Error∆).
For H2S, we found that B3PW91/def2-TZVPPD/D3(BJ) gave
the best correspondence to CCSD(T) with -1.15% Error0, -
3.46% Error−1 and -5.07% Error∆. The B3PW91 functional
slightly underestimates the BE for both neutral and charged com-
plexes. The error of the BE values are -14 K and -110 K for the
neutral and charged complexes respectively, which is well within
chemical accuracy (< 500 K). Next, the OCS benchmark gave
the ωB97M-D4REV/def2-TZVPPD as best option to calculate
the BE (Mardirossian & Head-Gordon 2016; Caldeweyher et al.
2019). Especially for the neutral complex it showed an Error0
of only -0.14%, corresponding to an underbinding of 3 K. The
charged BE was also good with a slight overbinding of 57 K,
corresponding to an Error−1 of 3.79%. The change in BE shows
an Error∆of -22.57%, which seems high but in absolute val-
ues it is only a difference of -59 K with the change in BE
calculated at the CCSD(T) level. Lastly, both S and SO2 have
RevPBE38/def2-TZVPPD/D3(0) as best performing combina-
tion (Zhang & Yang 1998; Santra et al. 2012). For S, this com-
bination showed the smallest error both the neutral and charged
BEs with an overbinding of 8.57% Error0 (149 K) and 3.50%
Error−1 (1330 K). The change in BE showed the second smallest
error of 3.25% Error∆(1181 K). Finally, for SO2 all function-
als overestimated the BE on the neutral dimer. Here, RevPBE38
gave the smallest error of 17.24% Error0 (627 K). This is slightly
above what would be considered chemical accuracy (> 500 K),
but it is the best performing functional we found and both the
charged BE and the change in BE where calculated accurately
with 0.23% Error−1 (69 K) and -2.16% Error∆(-558 K).

We stress that a benchmark as performed here is always
somewhat arbitrary. First, it is not feasible to test every possible
functional/basis set/dispersion correction combination. Second,
also the choice of the reference system, here the dimer with an
adsorbed molecule, is of importance. On larger ice clusters, the
balance of the various types of interactions between the molecule
and the cluster will change, which is already the case for OCS on
a charged tetramer versus a charged dimer. Clusters larger than
a few molecules are impractical at the high CCSD(T) level of
theory. Nevertheless, we show here that the neutral BE are accu-
rate when compared to both CCSD(T) and literature values for
both a dimer and tetramer water cluster. Since the change in BE
is also consistent with CCSD(T) on a water dimer, we are con-
fident that the presented method is robust enough to be used as
a proof of concept to investigate the potential change in BE of
sulfur containing compounds on charged ice surfaces.
The ZPE-correction is accounted for by multiplying the non-
corrected BE with a coefficient 1. This coefficient is determined
by a linear regression between the non-ZPE-corrected and ZPE-
corrected BEs for a test sample of species. From this regression,
it was found that at high BE regimes the ZPE-correction was
negligible while at lower BE regimes the ZPE-correction was
significant. Hence, only the low BEs are ZPE-corrected differ-
ently based on two categories: neutral BEs (ratio = 0.762) and
low charged BEs (ratio = 0.748).

3.2. H2S
The BE of H2S on the charged surface (2717 ± 732 K) is slightly
higher than on the neutral surface (2276 ± 625 K), correspond-
ing to an increase of 441 K (+19.4%) (Figure 2a, Table 3). A
Welch’s t-test yields p = 0.015 (< 0.05), indicating a statistically
significant difference between the mean values at this level of
significance. Nevertheless, the small difference in mean values
and the fact that the standard deviations of both groups are larger
than this difference shows that many data points in both groups
overlap.
For the neutral BEs, we observe that the lowest BEs are
found when H2S interacts with regions of the surface that lack
dangling H- or O-atoms, leading to a low BE (937 K). When a
dangling H from the surface (denoted as dH) interacts with the
S-atom (i.e., when a dH - - S interaction is established), a sig-
nificantly higher BE is found (1849 K), i.e., a difference of 912
K compared to the case where no interactions with dH or O are
present. Further increases in BEs are due to S-H - - O interac-
tions. This demonstrates that the positioning and orientation of
the water molecules around the H2S molecule is of great impor-
tance. When the S-H - - O interactions can be formed without
hindrance from other H-atoms from the ASW, the BE will in-
crease. The repulsion between the partially positive H-atoms on
the H2S molecule and the ASW is thus very important in these
higher BE configurations. For the charged BEs, we observe the
same trend as for the neutral BEs. Once a dH - - S interaction is
established, the BE increases. However, there are some config-
urations which interact with the extra electron alongside water,
as shown in Figure 3. The lowest BE found (1399 K) is a di-
rect interaction between a H-atom from H2S and the extra elec-
tron on the surface. This configuration is shown in Figure 3a and
yields a low BE since there is only one interaction between the
H2S molecule and the surface through this loosely bound elec-
tron, which is also interacting with two dH-atoms from the ASW
surface. Thus, the electron is localized in the pocket formed by
the three H-atoms and determines the BE of the H2S molecule
on the surface. The next interesting interaction we find between
H2S and the extra electron, shown in Figure 3b, has a BE of 4025
K. This value is already significantly higher than the average BE
on a neutral surface (2276 K). However, the maximum value on
a charged surface of 4553 K actually shows no interaction with
the extra electron on the surface.
3.3. SO2
The BE for SO2 on the charged surface (34992 ± 3803 K) is an
order of magnitude higher than on the neutral surface (3145 ±
819 K), corresponding to an increase by a factor of more than 10
(Figure 2b, Table 3). A Welch’s t-test yields p = 1.03x10−46 (<
0.05), indicating a statistically significant difference between the
mean values.
In the SO2 molecule, the S-atom is partially positively
charged. Because of this, we observe that the SO2 forms S - -
O interactions with the ASW. Additionally, both dH - - O and
H - - O interactions are established between dangling H-atoms
(i.e., H-atoms that are part of the H-bonded network) and an O-
atom of SO2. In Figure 4a the S - - O interaction and a dH - -
O interaction are shown to illustrate these interactions between
SO2 and the neutral ASW cluster. As is the case for the H2S
molecule, the configuration of the water around the binding site
is very important for the strength of the interaction. The highest
reported BE values are configurations where SO2 can form a dH
- - O interaction with one of its O-atoms and a H - - O inter-
action with its other O-atom apart from the S - - O interaction.
This configuration thus involves three interactions between the
SO2 and the ASW resulting in a high BE.

3.5. OCS
The BE for OCS on the charged surface (1429 ± 315 K) is nearly
equal to the BE on the neutral surface (1504 ± 580 K), the dif-
ference in mean values is only 75 K (-5%) (Figure 2d, Table 3).
A Welch’s t-test yields p = 0.577 (> 0.05), indicating that there
is no statistically significant difference between the mean values.
Both the neutral and charged complexes are missing some con-
figurations which either did not converge or the BE increased to
unreasonably high values multiple of magnitudes higher than the
main results.
OCS, similar to S and SO2, can act as H-bond acceptor but
can not act as H-bond donor. However, SO2 has a much larger
dipole moment than OCS. The H-bonds formed between OCS
and the ASW surface will therefore be much weaker than those
of SO2, as shown in Table 3. In fact, OCS shows the lowest mean
BE value (1504 K) of all molecules investigated in this study.
The strongest interactions found are indeed a dH - - O interac-
tion, while the weaker interactions are O - - S interactions be-
tween the O-atoms from the ASW cluster and the S-atom from
OCS. Indeed, we see that in configurations with low BEs, the
OCS-ASW interaction consists mainly of dispersion forces, with
a Grimme D3 dispersion contribution of 75% up to 85% of the
total BE. The high dispersion correction is a result from the high
polarizability of the S-atom on OCS, so it will form a dipole-
induced dipole interaction with the O-atoms from the cluster. In
configurations with higher BEs the contribution of the dispersion
correction drops to 65%, signifying a more electrostatic interac-
tion between OCS and the surface, through dH - - O interactions.
When the system is charged, we do not see any difference
in BE with the neutral system, as seen in Table 3. OCS will not
readily take on the extra electron like the S-atom or SO2 do, nor
will it interact with the loosely bound electron on the surface,
as H2S does. Since OCS has a lower dipole moment than H2S,
it will be less likely to interact strongly with the extra electron.
The OCS molecule indeed mainly binds to the surface through
dispersion interactions with its highly polarizable S-atom. How-
ever, we did find four cases where an electron transfer to OCS
occurs. In these cases, the molecule was initially placed close
to the place where the loosely bound electron resides on the
charged ASW surface, i.e., within 5 Å. When an electron trans-
fer occurs the BE increases significantly, up to 11000 K. Since
only a few cases were found where the electron transfer occurs
and since it only happens with specific starting conditions before
optimization, we opted to keep these out of the general results
shown in Table 3. It is, however, important to note that even if
it is unlikely that the OCS molecule takes on the extra electron,
there are certainly cases where it does happen, resulting in a sig-
nificant increase in BE.

mainly by dispersion and polarization, since an isolated S-atom

the excess electron tranfers to the sulfur atom, forming S−, and
the mean BE rises to approximately 43000 K. This more than an

dispersion-dominated adsorption to strong electrostatic stabi-

treated separately from the main neutral/charged comparison.

4.2. Electron transfer behavior
When a molecule adsorbs on a charged ASW surface, three
scenarios are possible: the electron may always transfer to the
molecule, the electron may never transfer to the molecule, or
the electron transfer may occur only under specific conditions.
The molecules discussed in this paper cover all three scenar-
ios: the S-atom and SO2 will always accept the electron, H2S
never accepts the electron and for OCS the electron transfer is
proximity dependent. The reasoning behind the behavior of the
different molecules is three-fold. First, there is the thermody-
namic driving force, which is defined by the electron affinity of
the molecule. Next, there is the kinetic barrier, which is the reor-
ganization energy required to accept an electron. Third, when an
electron transfer is impossible, the electrostatic properties of the
molecule, such as dipole moment and polarizability, will dic-
tate the BE. Table 5 shows these molecular properties, calcu-
lated by the method described in the method section, for the four
molecules presented in this paper.
The S-atom and SO2 have a high electron affinity, meaning
that electron capture is highly favorable, regardless of where
on the ASW cluster they are placed. SO2 shows a small struc-
tural reorganization due to the bond angle narrowing slightly
(from ∼120◦to ∼115◦) to accommodate the extra electron. H2S
has a highly negative electron affinity, thus, the H2S−anion is
highly unstable and prevents H2S from ever accepting the ex-
tra electron from the surface. Since H2S does not accept the
electron, it will fall back to its electrostatic properties interact-
ing with the surface with its moderate dipole moment and its
high polarizability. For OCS, we see a negative vertical elec-
tron affinity and an almost zero adiabatic electron affinity. The
OCS molecule will therefore not accept the electron in its neu-
tral geometry (EAvertical). In its anionic geometry (EAadiabatic),
however, it can accommodate the extra electron at the cost of a
high structural reorganization (Estructure). The necessity of OCS

4.3. Astrophysical implications
Infrared and microwave/submillimeter wave-based observations
have identified an ever-growing list of molecules throughout
what was previously believed to be an empty environment. How-
ever, the more certain we become as to which molecules are
present in interstellar space, the more we are faced with the ques-
tion of why other very similar molecules which “should” be there
are not. This question of the “missing” molecules is a primary
area of interest for extensive radioastronomical line searches,
hypotheses about blended lines, and laboratory syntheses to pro-
duce the elusive species that are expected to exist. A similar case
exists for molecules whose composition and chemistry suggests
that they should be relatively ubiquitous in the ISM, but in real-
ity are detected in very low quantities. It is certainly worth ques-
tioning whether the molecules that seem to be missing or in low
abundance in the gas phase are locked in reservoirs that have
evaded detection by the choice observational methods. Accord-
ing to our results, the interstellar ices present in cold molecular
clouds could be one such reservoir. A higher than anticipated BE
for these species on ice/dust grains would be a clear explanation
for their low abundance, or even absence, in the gas phase. The
failure of prior models to consider scenarios such as charged ices
may explain this discrepancy and the low number of detections
for these species.
One such case is the sulfur-depletion problem detailed in our
present work. It is curious that one of the most abundant atoms
in nature is present in so of the few molecular species detected in
the interstellar medium. It would stand to reason that the sulfur
is not in fact missing, but locked in solid-body reservoirs, such
as interstellar ice/dust grains. The lack of clear evidence for this
may be due to the failure of existing models to include the pos-
sibility that the ice surfaces are charged, which according to our
results has a noticeable effect on the BE of adsorbed molecules.
The unexpected lack of sulfur-bearing interstellar molecules
is evidenced by the observational results collected from cold
clouds – the star-forming nurseries where interstellar ices are
found. For instance, a 10-year long survey of the molecular
cloud Sagittarius B2N (Sgr–B2N) resulted in the detection of 73
molecules, only 15 of which contained sulfur (Halfen & Ziurys
2016). Of the 79 isotopic variants discovered, only 16 were
sulfur-bearing. Further, with the exception of OCS, every sulfur-
containing carbonaceous molecule detected was determined to
be present in amounts lower than those of their non-sulfur coun-
terparts by an order of magnitude. Perhaps most strikingly, thiols
make up only 0.2% of the functional groups present in the or-
ganic molecules detected in Sgr-B2N, while alcohols, their oxy-
gen analogue, comprise 62.3% (Table 6). This is certainly sur-
prising for such an abundant element, which also happens to be
one of the six (C, H, N, O, P, S) considered essential for life.
Our computational results show that the BE for H2S slightly
increases on a negatively charged surface. This small increase
might seem insignificant, but it has a big effect on the desorp-
tion rate. At the low temperatures (∼10 K) in dense molecular
clouds, the exponential relation of the desorption rate to the BE
will dominate (kdes ∝exp(−Ebind/T)) (Minissale et al. 2022).
This causes H2S to stick much longer on the negatively charged

Next, the electron accepting capabilities of OCS are linked

neutral and charged ASW means that it easily desorbs back into
the gas phase, which is indeed in accordance with observations
(Palumbo et al. 1995; McClure et al. 2023; Palumbo & Geballe
1997; Sturm et al. 2023; Boogert et al. 2022). Nevertheless,
when OCS accepts an electron, its BE will increase significantly,
essentially locking it into the ice cluster. The OCS−anion is also
much more reactive, leading to further processing into refractory
organosulfur or non-volatile compounds.
While SO2 has been detected in the gas phase of cold molec-
ular clouds, its abundance is much lower than in warm dense
clouds and star forming regions (Cernicharo et al. 2011). The
high BE of SO2 on charged surfaces due to the easy electron
transfer to SO2 provides clear rationale for the low gas phase
abundance. Additionally, when the SO−

readily react with neighboring species on the ice (such as H, O
or OH radicals) to create highly stable refractory sulfur com-
pounds, locking the sulfur onto the ice. The charged surface acts
in this case as a one-way valve for SO2, converting it into highly
bound species upon adsorption unable to desorb again.
Finally, it is known that sulfur in diffuse clouds is in the ion-
ized atomic form and it has been suggested that the S+ cations
freeze out onto negatively charged dust grains before the for-
mation of a dense cloud (Taillard et al. 2025). Following this
freeze out, they efficiently hydrogenate into H2S, analogously
to O-atoms (Caselli et al. 1994). However, if the grain still holds
a negative charge after the neutralization of S+, the neutral S will
promptly become negatively charged, making it much more re-
active than the neutral form. This opens up new pathways for
the S atoms, they do not just hydrogenate by reacting with in-
coming atomic H, but they can actually easily interact with any
open-shell species. The rapid charging and subsequent reaction
efficiently locks the atomic sulfur from the diffuse clouds in
the solid phase, significantly accelerating the depletion of sul-
fur from the gas phase.

The calculated low probability of sulfur-bearing molecules’
desorption to the gas phase on charged ices would have down-
stream effects throughout the stellar life cycle. With much of
the available sulfur locked in interstellar ices, sulfur-containing
molecules would remain adsorbed to these ices in the proto-
planetary disks which form from the accretion of dense molecu-
lar clouds (Das et al. 2018). Indeed, CS, SO, H2S, H2CS, and
SO2 are the only such gaseous molecules detected in a disk
(Keyte et al. 2024). Although this claim may be hindered by the
relative lack of survey data on these objects, it suggests a sig-
nificant fraction of the disk sulfur budget remains locked on dust
grains at the protoplanetary disk phase. From here, the associated
bodies progress to evolved stars, where sulfur-bearing species
may once more be detected – if they are not locked in planets or
other solar system bodies which may form around the star (note
that planetary disk matter may also give rise to comets and me-
teorites, further potential sources of sulfur loss) (Ziurys 2026).
As was the case for the Sgr-B2N, sulfur chemistry appears to
be relatively inactive in the gas phase at the evolved star stage;
a 2009 survey of the envelope of the Red Giant star VY Canis
Majoris determined a molecular abundance of CS of just 2-6 x
10−7 cm−2 compared to 0.4-5 x 10−4 cm−2 for CO (Ziurys et al.
2009). Finally, except in the case of very large stars which lead
to supernovae (rare in our galaxy), the end of the star’s evolved
phase marks the transition to a planetary nebula before returning
to become a diffuse cloud (Ziurys 2026).
In planetary nebulae (PNe), sulfur appears more frequently
in molecules, possibly due to stellar synthesis at this final stage.
Even so, S-bearing molecules in PNe are sparse compared to
their O-bearing analogues. A 2014 survey of five planetary neb-
ulae determined a column density for CS nearly 104 cm−2 lower
than that of its oxygenated counterpart, CO (Edwards & Ziurys
2014). Although a later survey found S-containing molecules
to be among the more abundant species in PNe (Edwards et al.
2014), this was in reference to SO and SO2, whose oxygen ana-
logues are either undetectable by rotational spectroscopy due to
having no permanent dipole (in the case of O2) or not currently
detected at all (O3). Later survey work confirmed the orders of
magnitude difference between CO and CS column densities for
various planetary nebulae (Smith et al. 2015). From the available
data, however, the low S/O ratio by this stage should not be sur-
prising. A high BE would keep sulfur-bearing molecules locked
in ices throughout the cloud and protoplanetary disk stages, with
sequestration on planets and other non-gaseous bodies a reason-
able pathway to keeping sulfur compounds undetected upon star
formation.
5. Conclusion
This work aimed to calculate statistically robust results for the
BEs of H2S, SO2, S and OCS on both neutral and charged ASW
surfaces, assessing how a single negative charge might affect the
BE distribution of these sulfur-bearing compounds. The BEs on
neutral surfaces show a site-dependent spread, which is in ac-
cordance with previous literature regarding the BE of interstellar
molecules on icy dust grains. On charged surfaces, three cases
were found for the behavior of the adsorbing molecule. First,
there is always a charge transfer due to the high positive elec-
tron affinities of SO2 and S. Second, there is no electron transfer
due to the negative electron affinity of H2S. Third, an electron
transfer can occur in specific cases where the structural reor-
ganization of OCS to accept the extra electron is favorable. It
can thus be concluded that the effect of a negative charge on
these grains is molecule-specific rather than a general effect. Due
to this molecule-specific change in BEs, an explicit approach
for each molecule is necessary in gas-grain models. Both SO2
and S will readily become charged when adsorbed on a charged
grain, making them much more reactive to form highly bound
species on the dust grains. OCS is less likely to accept the ex-
tra electron, and it desorbs easily into the gas-phase due to the
low BEs on both neutral and charged surfaces. However, when
OCS binds in such a way that it is favorable to overcome the
structural reorganization barrier, it will again become very re-
active and stay locked on the grains. The BE of H2S will in-
crease slightly due to the dipole interaction between H2S and
the loosely bound electron on the ASW surface. The increased
BE makes H2S stick longer to the grain than previously assumed
when only neutral surfaces were taken into account. These find-
ings support the idea that the missing sulfur is locked in icy dust
grains in dense molecular clouds and subsequently the proto-
planetary disks originating from these dense clouds.
