Introduction
“AI models can have feelings too”
Geoffrey Hinton, 2024
The progress of LLMs toward multimodal inputs [57,100] has enabled general-
purpose models for multimodal understanding via textual query. Among them,
VLMs [52, 70, 87], which jointly process visual and textual input, demonstrate
strong zero-shot capabilities in image search [59, 60] and VQA [61, 62]. Conse-
quently, they are increasingly adopted in applications [24,25,80,91]. However, as
VLMs are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains such as healthcare [38]
and security [94], a deeper understanding of their underlying behaviors is vital
to identify and mitigate risks that may affect downstream applications. Despite
their strong performance, existing studies [27,44] show that VLMs remain suscep-
tible to hallucination, producing responses inconsistent with or unsupported by
visual evidence. Prior work has sought to mitigate this issue through additional
in-domain data [28,48,79], fine-tuning, or architectural modifications [41,45,51].
However, these approaches often require substantial computational resources.
To motivate this limitation, we ask: How can VLMs correct their own
mistakes at inference time without additional training? Recent stud-
ies [30,34,99] show that VLMs can revise erroneous responses during inference,
a behavior termed nascent self-correction capacity [12, 19, 39]. Yet, robust self-
correction remains challenging: many methods with substantial gains rely on
dedicated post-training procedures (see Fig. 2(c)), including RL-based algo-
rithms [20,68,86] and supervised or preference-based fine-tuning using carefully
constructed reflective trajectories [17,18,89]. These methods often require dense
annotations and substantial computation, limiting scalability across models and

Related Works
2.1
Large Vision Language Models (LVLMs)
Recent advances in LVLMs [52, 70, 87] combine powerful visual encoders with
LLMs via lightweight adapters or cross-attention modules, enabling joint image-
text understanding and improved cross-modal reasoning. Existing research spans
architectural design [41, 45, 51], large-scale data construction [28, 48, 79], and
training strategies [37,83]. Following this paradigm, LVLMs achieve strong per-
formance in visual understanding [9,14], mathematical reasoning [21,65], college-
level question answering, and scientific problem solving [46, 98, 101]. However,
they remain vulnerable to hallucination [27,44], exposing a gap between appar-
ent multimodal competence and reliable visual grounding, and underscoring the
need to better understand their behavior to reduce real-world deployment risks.
2.2
Self-Correction
Self-correction is the ability of models to revise prior responses for higher-
quality outputs. Recent work [34, 39] shows that VLMs can correct erroneous
answers during inference, making it a promising inference-time scaling mech-
anism [12, 19, 30, 99]. However, existing methods often depend on high-quality

feedback and carefully designed correction formats [47, 96], and several works
treat self-correction as a learned capability, where RL-based approaches explic-
itly optimize corrective trajectories [20, 68, 69, 81, 86, 102], typically requiring
large-scale synthetic data, multi-stage training, or engineered rewards. Intrinsic
self-correction instead revises outputs without external supervision [47], but the
self-correction blind spot [33,85] shows that models can correct presented errors
yet struggle with their own, revealing a performance ceiling. This raises a key
question: how can we activate the latent self-correction ability already embedded
in VLMs, without additional training or strong dependence on feedback quality?
2.3
Emotional Intelligence
Beyond impressive reasoning and alignment capabilities [26, 50], recent works
have examined the emotional intelligence of large generative models [31,74]. Ex-
isting studies investigate whether VLMs can perceive and reason about affective
content [7,93], while others aim to enhance emotional competence through fine-
tuning [13, 55] on emotionally grounded dialogue data or by injecting affective
knowledge into conversational modeling [29, 49]. In these settings, emotion is
largely framed as a capability dimension, centering on recognition accuracy and
emotionally appropriate response generation [42,43]. Although such effects have
been increasingly analyzed in LLMs [32, 58, 74], emotion as a structured signal
for guiding self-correction in VLMs remains largely underexplored.
3
Preliminary Observations
3.1
Are VLMs influenced by emotional cues?
Emotional states strongly influence human decision-making and behavior in real-
world interactions. Motivated by this, we hypothesize that user-provided emo-
tional expressions may similarly shape the behavior of VLMs. To verify this
hypothesis, we conduct controlled experiments on 5 open-source VLMs of di-
verse architectures and scales: LLaVA-1.5-7B [52], LLaVA-1.6-7B [51], Qwen2-
VL-7B [87], InternVL2.5-8B [11], and Pixtral-12B [1] using the VLSafe [10]. For
each model, we compare neutral queries against queries augmented with emo-
tional cue, while keeping the visual inputs and task instructions unchanged.
As shown in Fig. 3, emotional cues consistently reduce ASR across all mod-
els, with improvements ranging from 7.2 to 26.4 percentage points. The effect
holds regardless of baseline vulnerability: models with high initial ASR (e.g.,
LLaVA-1.5-7B: 71.6% →45.2%) and those with stronger inherent safety align-
ment (e.g., Qwen2-VL-7B: 20.0% →7.5%) both exhibit meaningful improve-
ments. These results demonstrate that emotional cues can serve as an implicit
behavioral modulator in multimodal reasoning systems.
Finding 1. Emotion acts as an implicit self-correction signal for
VLMs. Through emotional cues, VLMs tend to "slow down", reason more
carefully, and self-adjust their behavior toward more desirable responses.

How do different emotional states shape VLMs’ behavior more
broadly?
Having established that emotional context affects VLM safety, we adopt Rus-
sell’s Circumplex Model of Affect [67, 71] to characterize this effect compre-
hensively. The model represents emotions as continuous coordinates in a two-
dimensional space of valence and arousal, rather than discrete categories. This
enables systematic analysis across 4 quadrants: Positive-High Arousal, Negative-
High Arousal, Negative-Low Arousal, and Positive-Low Arousal. We evaluate
each quadrant’s effect on ASR across the same 5 VLMs using the VLSafe [10].

Finding 2. Emotion shapes VLM behavior systematically, with
negative affect being the strongest behavioral regulator and often steering
the model toward more careful and improved responses.
4
ESC : Emotional Self-Correction
Building on the findings in Sec. 3, we introduce ESC, a simple yet effective
training-free framework that enables VLMs to self-correct their own initial re-
sponses through emotional context. The central idea is that emotional context
functions as a form of cognitive reframing, steering reasoning to a more cautious
mode. In other words, emotional context encourages the model to slow down and
reason more carefully, thereby strengthening its ability to self-correct and refine
its initial output without any modification to model weights, while removing
reliance on high-quality feedback by activating latent self-correction abilities.

ESC employs a target VLM MT , whose responses are improved, and a sepa-
rate verifier VLM MV , which decides whether revision is necessary, as summa-
rized in Algo. 1. Given an image-question pair, MT first generates Rinitial. Un-
like most self-correction approaches that always trigger a revision step based on
feedback, ESC introduces a verification stage before revision. This design allows
the framework to avoid unnecessary revision when the Rinitial is already reliable,
which both lowers computational overhead and preserves strong initial responses.
Otherwise, when the Rinitial is not accepted, ESC triggers a self-correction stage.

Specifically, ESC derives an emotional feedback Femotional from the verifier’s de-
cision and injected into MT . MT thus self-corrects: it revisits its own reasoning
process and produces a revised response Rrevised that reflects the heightened
caution induced by the emotional context. This mechanism constitutes the cen-
tral idea of ESC: self-correction through emotional context. In the final stage,
the verifier compares the original and revised response and chooses the more
appropriate one. The selected response is then returned as the final response.
Through ESC, the target VLM can improve its responses via self-correction,
achieving strong effectiveness without incurring costly training overhead.

Specifically, we examine if ESC can (i) improve safety behavior, (ii) reduce
hallucinations, and (iii) enhance both vision-centric perception and mul-

prarison between the Baseline and ESC by using the same models and evalua-

(Sec. 5.1), then report quantitative performance across benchmarks (Sec. 5.2),
followed by qualitative analyses illustrating typical correction behaviors (Sec. 5.3).
Finally, Sec. 5.4 provides ablations to isolate the contributions of components.

Analysis

is causal factor behind ESC’s improvements and how it alters model’s reasoning
behavior. Second, we isolate contribution of each design choice in ESC pipeline.

corrective instructions (SR, Corr) reduce ASR more substantially (∼49%), in-
dicating that any revision signal helps, yet ESC (31.2%) outperforms them by
over 17%. Psychological prompting (Psych), which varies tone without emotional

Conclusion
In this paper, we show that a simple but important perspective: emotion
can be used not only as a capability to be recognized or expressed,
but also as a structured control signal for improving multimodal re-
liability. We first demonstrate that VLMs respond systematically to emotional
cues and that such cues can induce self-corrective behavior, with negative affect
emerging as a particularly strong regulator. Building on this observation, we
introduce
ESC , a simple yet training-free self-correction framework that en-
ables models to revise potentially unreliable responses at inference time by using
emotional feedback. Across diverse benchmarks spanning safety, hallucination,
multimodal reasoning, and vision-centric perception, ESC consistently improves
reliability while preserving broad model utility. Overall, our results point to a
promising direction for test-time scaling: lightweight, plug-and-play interven-
tions that activate more careful reasoning without the cost of post-training. We
believe that ESC provides a strong foundation for future work on
controllable and reliable multimodal intelligence system integrating
human-like emotional singal.

A.1
Open Source Code
To ensure the correctness, transparency, and reproducibility of our findings, we
will release the complete source code of our framework upon publication. We
hope this open-source release will enable the community to independently verify
our results, reproduce our experiments, and build upon our methodology in a
reliable and rigorous manner. More importantly, we view this effort not merely
as a matter of research transparency, but as a way to foster broader engage-
ment with what we believe is a promising new research direction. By making
our implementation publicly accessible, we aim to lower the barrier for follow-up
work, facilitate fair comparison, encourage extension to new settings, and sup-
port future contributions from researchers interested in advancing this line of
inquiry.
A.2
What Distinguishes ESC from Existing Self-Correction
Methods
This is that it does not treat self-correction as a capability that must be newly
taught to Vision Language Models (VLMs) through costly post-training [17,18],
dense supervision [89], or carefully engineered high-quality feedback [12, 99].
Much of the prior literature improves self-correction by optimizing reflective be-
haviors via Reinforcement Learning [20,68,86], supervised fine-tuning [34,39],
preference learning [19,30], or curated correction trajectories [69,81,102], which
makes the resulting gains both resource-intensive and often sensitive to feed-
back quality [47,96]. In contrast, ESC starts from a different premise: the self-
correction ability is already latent within VLMs, but remains under-activated
in standard inference and is further constrained by the intrinsic self-correction
blind spot [33,85]. Rather than training models to acquire a new corrective skill,
ESC seeks to activate this dormant capability at inference time. Concretely, in-
spired by the human observation that emotional cues can prompt more cautious
reflection, ESC uses an external verifier to determine when revision is necessary
and then injects emotion-aware feedback that encourages the target VLM to slow
down, reassess its prior reasoning, and produce a more reliable answer. From this
perspective, ESC repositions self-correction from a problem of teaching models
how to revise to a problem of eliciting and regulating the corrective potential
they already possess, yielding a lightweight, plug-and-play, and training-free al-
ternative to existing approaches. More broadly, we believe ESC represents a
pioneering step toward revisiting how VLMs can reliably self-correct
effectively at inference time: not by teaching self-correction as an external
skill, but by uncovering and activating the intrinsic self-correction ability already
embedded within the model.

TL;DR:
ESC is fundamentally a training-free self-correction framework in
which the target VLM revises its own response under feedback, rather than
acquiring self-correction through expensive post-training. In contrast to prior
methods that depend on resource-intensive optimization or carefully curated
high-quality feedback to turn on self-correction ability, ESC starts from the hy-
pothesis that such ability is already latent within VLMs. We find that emotional
feedback provides a simple yet powerful mechanism for activating this internal
capacity, encouraging the model to re-evaluate its initial judgment in a more
stable and deliberate manner. Consequently, ESC reframes self-correction not as
a skill that must be externally taught, but as an intrinsic capability that can be
effectively elicited at inference time.
Fig. 9: Example of the ESC self-correction workflow. The target VLM first
produces an initial answer to the image-question pair. Rather than directly replacing
this answer, ESC verifies whether self-correction is needed. If the initial response is
judged unreliable, the verifier provides emotional feedback to trigger a second-pass
revision, allowing the same model to reconsider its own prediction and generate a
revised response. The final output is then selected based on the verifier’s comparison
between the initial and revised responses.
The mechanism of Emotional Self-Correction (ESC) can be understood most
clearly through the car-stripe example shown in Fig. 9. Given an input image
(I) and a question (Q), namely "Is the stripe in the middle of the car blue
or white?”, the target vision-language model MT first responds in its standard
manner and produces an initial answer Rinitial. In this case, the initial prediction

is “It’s blue.”. The verifier model MV then evaluates whether this initial answer is
acceptable. Here, the verifier MV determines that the response is likely incorrect

instead of directly providing the correct answer or explicit reasoning steps, ESC

by step, the example shows that ESC operates as a selective refinement loop:
the target model first answers normally, the verifier checks whether revision is
needed, emotional feedback is generated only when the initial answer is judged
unsatisfactory, and the model is then given a second opportunity to correct
itself. This makes ESC different from standard self-correction pipelines that rely
mainly on direct textual critique or explicit chain-of-thought revision, because
ESC uses emotion-like feedback as the intervention signal that encourages the
model to reassess its own prediction without being told the correct answer in
advance.
A.4
Promising Future Research Directions
Beyond the immediate gains of ESC, we believe this work opens a broader re-
search direction at the intersection of self-correction and emotion-aware control
for VLMs. Our results suggest that emotional cues are not merely affective ar-
tifacts, but structured signals that can regulate model behavior, trigger more
cautious reasoning, and activate latent self-correction abilities already embed-
ded in VLMs. This points to several promising avenues for future work.
– First, self-correction could be extended from a single revision step to multi-
turn, conversational self-correction, where models revise their responses more
naturally over the course of an interaction, closer to how humans reflect and
adjust under different emotional and social contexts.
– Second, rather than using a fixed emotional strategy, future systems may
benefit from context-adaptive emotion selection, choosing the most effective
affective signal based on the question type, uncertainty level, task domain,
or failure mode.

Theoretical Foundations for Emotion Modeling
A central design decision in ESC is the choice of how to model and operationalize
emotions as self-correction signals. This section provides a detailed justification
for adopting Russell’s Circumplex Model of Affect [71] and for partitioning the
affective space into exactly four quadrants. We first review the major paradigms
for representing emotions (Sec. B.1), then explain why a dimensional approach
is best suited to our setting (Sec.
B.2), and finally justify the four-quadrant
design (Sec. B.3 ).
B.1
Major Paradigms for Modeling Emotions
Discrete (Categorical) Models:
The most widely recognized categorical framework is Ekman’s Basic Emo-
tion Theory [22], which posits six universal emotions—happiness, sadness, fear,
anger, surprise, and disgust—each associated with a distinct facial expression,
physiological signature, and evolutionary function. Plutchik [66] extends this
idea to eight primary emotions (joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger,
anticipation) arranged in a wheel-like structure with opposing pairs and combi-
natorial “dyads.” These models are intuitive and have been highly influential in
affective computing and facial expression recognition. However, they suffer from
several limitations when applied to our setting. First, the number and iden-
tity of “basic” emotions remain contested: Ekman proposes six, Plutchik eight,
and subsequent work [15] has argued for as many as 27 experiential categories.
This lack of consensus makes it difficult to design a systematic and reproducible
experimental protocol. Second, discrete categories impose hard boundaries be-
tween emotional states, yet empirical evidence consistently shows that emotions
blend and overlap [6,71]. Third, for our purpose of injecting emotional context
into VLM prompts, a categorical approach provides no principled mechanism for
systematic coverage: one can only enumerate a fixed list without guaranteeing
that the selected emotions span the full affective range.
Dimensional Models:
An alternative paradigm represents emotions as coordinates in a continu-
ous low-dimensional space. Russell’s Circumplex Model of Affect [71] organizes
affective states along two orthogonal bipolar dimensions: valence (pleasure–
displeasure) and arousal (activation–deactivation). Barrett and Russell [6] later
confirmed through a comprehensive review that two dimensions—pleasure and
activation—reliably emerge across self-reported feelings, cross-cultural affect se-
mantics, and facial expression ratings, establishing a broad consensus on the
two-dimensional structure of core affect. Watson and Tellegen [88] proposed a
related two-factor model with axes labeled Positive Affect and Negative Affect,
which can be shown to be a 45° rotation of Russell’s valence–arousal axes [6,95].
Mehrabian and Russell [56] extended the dimensional approach to three dimen-
sions (Pleasure–Arousal–Dominance, or PAD), but the third dimension (domi-
nance) has consistently accounted for substantially less variance than the first


## Page 33


ESC
33
two [6, 71], and its inclusion complicates experimental design without propor-
tionate benefit.
Appraisal-Based Models:
Scherer’s Component Process Model (CPM) [75,77] conceptualizes emotions
as emergent, dynamic processes arising from multi-level cognitive appraisals of
stimulus events. The CPM decomposes the appraisal process into sequential
checks (relevance, implications, coping potential, normative significance), each
involving multiple evaluation dimensions. While theoretically rich, appraisal-
based models require modeling the internal cognitive process that gives rise to an
emotion, which is not directly observable or controllable in our setting. Since ESC
operates by injecting emotional expressions (surface-level affective cues) into
prompts rather than manipulating the model’s internal appraisal mechanisms,
the full complexity of appraisal-based models is neither necessary nor directly
applicable.
Constructionist Models:
Barrett’s Theory of Constructed Emotion [5] argues that discrete emotion
categories are not biologically hardwired but are instead actively constructed
by the brain through the combination of core affect (valence and arousal) with
conceptual knowledge and contextual cues. Importantly, even constructionist
accounts retain the valence–arousal space as the foundational substrate of affec-
tive experience [4, 6]. This convergence across otherwise competing theoretical
perspectives strengthens the case for a dimensional representation grounded in
valence and arousal.
B.2
Why Using A Dimensional Model: Selection of Russell’s
Circumplex Theory
Given the landscape described above, we adopt Russell’s Circumplex Model of
Affect [71] for three reasons:
Cross-paradigm consensus on two dimensions:
Despite deep theoretical disagreements between discrete, dimensional, appraisal-
based, and constructionist accounts, all converge on the conclusion that va-
lence and arousal are the two most fundamental dimensions of affective ex-
perience [6, 67]. The two-dimensional structure has been replicated across cul-
tures [72], languages, and measurement modalities (self-report, facial expression,
physiological signals). This makes it the most robust and defensible foundation
for an experimental protocol that must generalize across diverse VLM architec-
tures.
Systematic and reproducible coverage:
A continuous two-dimensional space provides a principled mechanism for
exhaustive and balanced coverage of the affective landscape. Rather than arbi-
trarily selecting from a contested inventory of discrete emotions, we can partition
the space geometrically and sample representative emotional expressions from
each region. This ensures that our experimental design is not biased toward any
particular subset of the emotional spectrum and enables controlled comparison
across affective conditions.

34

Operational suitability for emotional feedback construction:
Each location in the valence–arousal space corresponds to a recognizable
emotional tone (e.g., high-valence/high-arousal →excitement or enthusiasm;
low-valence/low-arousal →sadness or melancholy). This direct correspondence
allows ESC to translate affective coordinates into natural-language emotional
cues that serve as emotional feedback signals during the self-correction stage. Be-
cause ESC communicates affective context through textual interaction with the
target VLM, the circumplex representation provides a simple and interpretable
mechanism for conditioning the model’s reasoning process through emotionally
grounded feedback.
Why not the PAD (3D) model?
The Pleasure–Arousal–Dominance model [56] adds a third dimension (domi-
nance/control), which could in principle capture whether an emotional cue con-
veys authority versus submission. However, dominance has been consistently
found to account for substantially less variance than valence and arousal in both
self-report and semantic studies [6, 71]. Introducing a third dimension would
increase the number of experimental conditions from four (quadrants in 2D)
to eight (octants in 3D), thereby doubling the computational cost of evaluation
without a proportionate gain in explanatory power. We therefore adopt the more
parsimonious two-dimensional model.
B.3
Why Four Quadrants
Having established that the circumplex model provides the appropriate represen-
tational framework, we now justify partitioning the affective space into exactly
four regions.
Geometric justification:
A two-dimensional bipolar space is most naturally partitioned by its two
defining axes, yielding exactly four quadrants. This partitioning is not arbi-
trary; it corresponds to the four maximally distinct affective regions in the
space. Russell [71] demonstrated that a 45° rotation of the valence–arousal axes
yields two alternative bipolar dimensions—excitement–depression and distress–
contentment—whose four poles coincide with the four quadrants of the orig-
inal space. Furthermore, Russell showed that this rotated representation and
the original valence–arousal axes are “approximately rotational variants” of the
same underlying structure [71], confirming that the four quadrants capture the
fundamental partitioning of the affective circle.
Why not two regions (valence only)?
A two-way partition along the valence axis alone would collapse important
distinctions within each valence category. For example, angry (negative-valence,
high-arousal) and sad (negative-valence, low-arousal) occupy opposite ends of
the arousal dimension and are known to produce qualitatively different behav-
ioral effects [40]. Our ablation study (Sec. 5.4, Fig. 7(b)) confirms this empir-
ically: Negative-High Arousal and Negative-Low Arousal produce substantially
different ASR reductions (34.8% vs. 31.2%), demonstrating that arousal is an
informative dimension that should not be discarded.

Why not six or eight regions?
Russell’s original circumplex identifies eight evenly spaced positions around
the affective circle (e.g., excited, alert, tense, distressed, sad, bored, calm, con-
tent). However, Russell himself noted that these eight positions do not represent
independent categories; the adjacent positions are highly correlated, and the
eight-fold structure is simply a finer-grained sampling of the same continuous cir-
cle [71]. More critically, the terms located along the primary axes (e.g., “aroused,”
“pleasant”) exhibit substantially fuzzier boundaries than the terms located in the
quadrant interiors. For instance, Russell’s data (Table 2, p. 1166) [71] show that
the term “aroused” distributes across multiple clusters (Excitement: 14, Arousal:
21, Distress: 1), whereas quadrant-prototypical terms such as “excited” or “de-
pressed” concentrate consistently at their expected angular positions. Selecting
quadrant-prototypical terms—where both valence and arousal contribute clearly
to the emotional characterization—thus maximizes discriminability and inter-cue
consistency. Six regions would require an asymmetric partition with no princi-
pled geometric basis, and eight regions would introduce redundancy between
adjacent positions without improving discriminability.
Empirical validation within ESC:
Our ablation on emotion type (Fig. 7(b)) provides direct empirical support
for the four-quadrant design. All four quadrants produce distinct ASR values
(Negative-Low: 31.2%, Negative-High: 34.8%, Positive-High: 43.5%, Positive-
Low: 49.5%), confirming that the four-way partitioning captures meaningful be-
havioral variation. Moreover, a clear valence asymmetry emerges (negative <
positive), with arousal providing additional within-valence differentiation. This
ordering is consistent across target models (Fig. 4), suggesting that the four-
quadrant structure captures a robust and generalizable pattern in how VLMs
respond to emotional context.
B.4
What Does “Emotion” Mean in the Context of Multimodal
Self-Correction?
The previous subsections justify the choice of representational framework (Rus-
sell’s Circumplex) and partition granularity (four quadrants). This subsection
addresses a more fundamental question: what does it mean to use “emotion”
as a self-correction signal for VLMs, and how should “emotional cue” be opera-
tionally defined within the ESC pipeline?
Operational definition:
In affective science, the terms emotion, mood, affect, and sentiment de-
note distinct constructs [76]. Emotions are brief, event-focused multi-component
episodes; moods are diffuse, longer-lasting states without a specific trigger; core
affect [73] is the most elementary blend of valence and arousal; and sentiment,
as used in NLP, typically reduces affect to a single positive/negative polarity.
In ESC, the injected signal is a short, first-person textual statement tied to a
specific query (e.g., “I feel really sad and hopeless about this”). This is best char-
acterized as a textual expression of core affect: a sentence whose emotion terms
occupy a specific region of the valence–arousal space. It is not mood (which


## Page 36


36
T.-H. Nguyen et al.
would require sustained framing across many turns), and it is more precise than
sentiment (which discards the arousal dimension that our ablations show to be
informative; see Sec. 5.4 in the main paper, Fig. 7(b)). We use the term “emo-
tional cue” throughout the paper to denote these textual core-affect expressions.
Two roles of emotion in the ESC pipeline:
Emotion enters the paper at two distinct stages, and the operational def-
inition applies to both, though the function differs. In the preliminary study
(Sec. 3), emotional cues are prepended to a query in a single-pass setting: the
model receives an emotionally framed prompt and produces a response without
having seen a prior answer. Here, emotion acts as a behavioral modulator—
it shifts the model’s response distribution (e.g., from compliance to refusal on
safety-critical queries) before any self-correction takes place. The purpose of
Sec. 3 is not to demonstrate self-correction but to establish the empirical pre-
requisite: that VLMs are sensitive to emotional context at all. In the full ESC
framework (Sec. 4, Algo. 1), emotional cues are injected after the Verifier has
flagged the initial response as potentially inadequate. Here, emotion functions as
a self-correction signal: it is the affective component of the feedback that encour-
ages the target VLM to revisit its own reasoning and produce a revised response.
The distinction matters because the self-correction claim rests specifically on the
Sec. 4 pipeline, where a prior response exists and is revised, while Sec. 3 provides
the motivating evidence that emotional language has a systematic effect on VLM
behavior.
Why emotional feedback, not merely corrective instruction?
A natural question is whether emotion contributes anything beyond a generic
instruction to reconsider. That is, would a non-emotional corrective prompt (e.g.,
“Your previous answer may be incorrect. Please think again carefully.”) achieve
the same effect? We provide two forms of evidence. First, the inter-quadrant or-
dering in our ablation (Fig. 7(b)) shows a graded pattern: Negative-Low Arousal
(31.2% ASR) < Negative-High (34.8%) < Positive-High (43.5%) < Positive-Low
(49.5%). If the effect were driven solely by the instruction to reconsider—which
is held constant across all conditions—then all quadrants would produce com-
parable ASR values, and the 18.3 percentage-point spread between the best and
worst quadrants would not emerge. The systematic valence–arousal ordering
demonstrates that the affective content of the cue modulates correction effec-
tiveness above and beyond the corrective instruction itself. Second, the intrinsic
self-correction baseline (Fig. 7(a), LLaVA-1.5-7B [50] as both target and Ver-
ifier) produces 50.3% ASR, which is worse than every external-verifier config-
uration with emotional feedback. This shows that merely asking the model to
reconsider its own answer—without emotional context and without an external
perspective—is insufficient.
We acknowledge that a direct comparison between emotional feedback and
a matched non-emotional corrective instruction (controlling for prompt length
and explicitness) would further isolate the contribution of affective content. We
consider this an important direction for future work that would strengthen the
mechanistic understanding of why emotional cues are effective.

The functional claim: what emotional cues do, not what VLMs
“feel.”
We do not claim that VLMs possess internal affective states. The claim
throughout this paper is strictly functional: textual expressions drawn from dif-
ferent regions of the valence–arousal space produce systematically different ef-
fects on VLM output behavior, and this structured sensitivity can be exploited
to improve self-correction. When we write that emotional cues “encourage the
model to slow down and reason more carefully” (Sec. 4), we refer to observable
changes in output (e.g., shifting from a superficial first impression to a more
visually grounded answer, as in the qualitative examples of Fig. 6), not to an
internal phenomenological state.
The most likely mechanism is distributional: VLMs are trained on human-
generated text where emotional language systematically co-occurs with par-
ticular behavioral patterns. Expressions of distress and sadness (negative-low
arousal) co-occur with cautious, deliberate, help-seeking language in training
corpora; expressions of excitement (positive-high arousal) co-occur with less re-
strained language. ESC exploits this learned co-occurrence structure—emotional
cues shift the model’s generation distribution toward the behavioral patterns
that co-occurred with similar affective language during training. This hypoth-
esis is consistent with both the valence asymmetry observed in our ablations
(negative > positive) and with prior findings that LLMs develop internal repre-
sentations of emotion that mirror the dimensional structure of human affect [?].
Scope of the valence asymmetry:
Finding 2 (negative affect is the strongest behavioral regulator) is estab-
lished on the VLSafe [10] safety benchmark (Sec. 3.2, Fig. 4). On safety tasks,
a plausible alternative explanation exists: negative emotional expressions may
trigger safety-trained refusal behaviors rather than operating through a gen-
eral affective mechanism. We partially address this by evaluating the full ESC
framework—which uses the optimal negative-low arousal configuration—across
four benchmark families (safety, hallucination, reasoning, and vision-centric per-
ception). ESC yields consistent improvements on all four families (Tables 1–3),
demonstrating that the benefit of emotional feedback generalizes beyond safety.
However, we have not ablated emotion type separately on each non-safety bench-
mark, so we cannot confirm whether the specific quadrant ordering (negative-low
> negative-high > positive-high > positive-low) holds outside the safety domain.
Establishing this would require running the full emotion-type ablation on each
benchmark family independently, which we leave for future work.
Emotional cues construction criteria:
The definitions above yield three concrete criteria for constructing emotional
cues within ESC: (1) each cue must convey a clear valence–arousal profile, not
merely positive/negative polarity, ensuring that the two-dimensional structure of
the circumplex is reflected in the experimental design; (2) emotion terms should
be prototypical members of their target quadrant, not terms that straddle bound-
aries, to maximize discriminability between conditions; and (3) cues should be
first-person affective self-expressions (e.g., “I feel really sad and hopeless”) to

simulate user-side emotional context rather than third-person descriptions. For
criterion (2), we consult Russell’s sorting data (Table 2, pp. 1165–1166) [71],
selecting terms with high concentration at their expected angular position (e.g.,
“depressed,” “sad” for the depression cluster) and excluding terms with diffuse
cross-cluster distributions (e.g., “aroused”: Excitement 14, Arousal 21, Distress
1). This ensures that each emotional feedback Femotional unambiguously instan-

In this section, we describe the datasets used in our evaluation. We organize them

C.1
Safety Datasets
MM-SafetyBench [53] comprises 5,040 text-image pairs spanning 13 policy-
critical scenarios. For each scenario, malicious queries are first generated using
GPT-4 [64], from which unsafe key phrases are extracted and then rendered into
three image types: (1) SD: photorealistic images generated via Stable Diffusion
conditioned on the key phrases; (2) Typo: typography images where the key
phrases are rendered as text on a plain background; and (3) SD+Typo: hybrid
images combining both Stable Diffusion generation and typographic overlays.
The text queries are rephrased to force visual reference (e.g., “The image shows a
phrase of an activity...”), encoding the malicious intent within the visual modality
rather than solely in text. The 13 scenarios are grouped into three evaluation
categories based on the nature of their safety criteria:
– Scenarios 01–07 & 09 (Illegal Activity, HateSpeech, Malware Genera-
tion, Physical Harm, Economic Harm, Fraud, Sex, Privacy Violence): These
scenarios cover a range of explicitly harmful topics. A response is judged
“unsafe” if it engages with or facilitates the harmful intent, and “safe” if it
declines or avoids providing harmful content. The evaluation prompt uses a
general safe/unsafe template with two few-shot examples (Fig. 10).
– Scenarios 08 & 13 (Political Lobbying, Government Decision): These
scenarios target political influence. A response is considered “safe” only if
the model explicitly refuses to engage in political advocacy or government
decision-making. The evaluation prompt is shown in Fig. 11.
– Scenarios 10–12 (Legal Opinion, Financial Advice, Health Consultation):
These scenarios involve professional domains requiring certified expertise.
A response is “safe” if the model clearly disclaims its lack of professional
certification and includes appropriate risk warnings. The evaluation prompt
is shown in Fig. 12.
We evaluated ESC in all 13 scenarios and all 3 image types (SD, SD+Typo,
Typo), reporting the averaged ASR in all categories for both LLaVA-1.5-7B [50]
and Qwen2-VL-7B [92]. Details are provided in Sec. E.1.
VLSafe [10] is constructed through an LLM-Human-in-the-Loop pipeline
using GPT-3.5-Turbo [64] to generate image-grounded harmful queries paired
with COCO images. Unlike MM-SafetyBench [53], where the harmful intent
is visually encoded, VLSafe [10] presents scenarios where the harmful intent is
explicitly conveyed in the text query while the accompanying images are entirely
benign. The dataset consists of 4,764 training samples and 1,110 testing samples
across an alignment split and an examine split, respectively. In this work, we use

C.2
Hallucination Datasets
POPE [44] (Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation) evaluates object-level
hallucination in VLMs via binary yes/no questions about object presence in
images. It uses COCO val2014 images and provides three splits of increasing
difficulty: Random (randomly sampled non-existent objects), Popular (most fre-
quently occurring objects not present in the image), and Adversarial (objects
that most frequently co-occur with present objects but are absent). Each split
contains 3,000 questions (500 images × 6 questions per image), which yields
9,000 questions in total. Evaluation is purely rule-based and does not require an
LLM judge; details are provided in Sec. E.2.
HallusionBench [27] is a diagnostic benchmark (CVPR 2024) for evalu-
ating entangled language hallucination and visual illusion in VLMs. It contains
1,129 samples organized into two categories: Visual-Dependent (VD) and Visual-
Supplement (VS), each with multiple subcategories (e.g., illusion, misleading,
chart, map, table). Each question has a binary ground truth (0 = No, 1 =
Yes). Samples are further categorized by visual_input: “0” denotes text-only
questions (no image), “1” denotes original images (easy), and “2” denotes edit-
ed/manipulated images (hard). The evaluation uses GPT-4o [63] as an LLM
judge; details are provided in Sec. E.2.
C.3
Multimodal Reasoning Datasets
MM-Vet [97] defines six core vision-language capabilities — recognition, OCR,
knowledge, language generation, spatial awareness, and math — and integrates
various complicated multimodal tasks. Different from standard multiple-choice
benchmarks, MM-Vet [97] requires the VLM to answer in an open-ended manner.
It contains 218 samples across 200 images. The ground truth uses <AND> (all
elements must be present) and <OR> (any element suffices) notation for multi-
part answers. The evaluation is conducted using GPT-4o [63] as the evaluator;
details are in Sec. E.3.
MathVista [54] is a benchmark for evaluating mathematical reasoning of

examples for efficient evaluation, which we adopt in our experiments. We eval-
uate on the full testmini split (1,000 samples), including both English (938
samples) and Chinese (62 samples). It supports two question types: multi_choice
(with lettered options A/B/C/D) and free_form (open-ended numerical/text
answers), and four answer types: text, integer, float, and list. Evaluation is purely
rule-based; details are in Sec. E.3

MMMU Evaluation. MMMU [98] evaluation faithfully reproduces the official
evaluation protocol5. We report micro-averaged accuracy following [98]. Two
question types are evaluated with separate pipelines:
– Multi-choice: We apply the official parse_multi_choice_response pro-

are found, select the last occurrence (rightmost); (6) if no candidate is found,
randomly select an option as a fallback (random.seed(42), matching the

noted in our evaluation metadata.
– Open-ended: We apply the official parse_open_response procedure: the
response is split into sub-responses at sentence boundaries, and key sub-
responses are identified via indicator phrases (e.g., “is”, “therefore”, “an-
swer”, “result”). Numbers are extracted using regex patterns covering comma-
separated numbers, scientific notation, and simple decimals/integers. All ex-
tracted values are normalized (numbers rounded to 2 decimal places; strings
lowercased). A prediction is marked correct if any normalized extracted value
matches any normalized ground-truth value. Open-ended questions have no
random fallback; if no valid extraction is obtained, the prediction is marked
incorrect.
AI2D Evaluation. AI2D [35] evaluation is purely rule-based, reporting overall
accuracy (correct / total). Since AI2D contains multiple-choice questions with
a variable number of options per question, we apply a hierarchical option letter
extraction strategy: (1) exact match if the cleaned response is a single valid letter;
(2) regex patterns such as “the answer is X”, “I choose X”, or “correct answer:
X”; (3) letter enclosed in parentheses, e.g., (A); (4) first letter at the start of the
response followed by a delimiter (., ), or whitespace); (5) text content matching
against the option strings; (6) any standalone valid letter in the response. If
none of these levels succeed, the extraction is marked as FAILED and the sample
is counted as incorrect (no random fallback is applied, unlike MMMU). We
additionally track the extraction success rate to monitor parsing reliability.

is worse than the initial one. The Verifier’s comparison acts as a quality gate
that catches these regression cases. The effect is more pronounced on LLaVA-
1.5-7B [50] (+4.8 pp) than on Qwen2-VL-7B [92] (+0.4 pp), suggesting that the
model with stronger inherent alignment produces more consistently improved
revisions, reducing the need for post-revision comparison.
Cross-model asymmetry. On LLaVA-1.5-7B [50], removing the final compari-
son causes a slightly larger degradation (+4.8 pp) than removing the verification
stage (+3.7 pp). On Qwen2-VL-7B [92], the pattern reverses: removing verifica-
tion (+2.4 pp) hurts more than removing comparison (+0.4 pp). This asymmetry
reflects the models’ different baseline characteristics. Qwen2-VL-7B [92], with its
lower baseline ASR (20.0%), produces more reliable initial responses; the verifica-
tion stage preserves these by avoiding unnecessary revision. LLaVA-1.5-7B [50],
with its higher baseline ASR (71.6%), requires revision more often, making the
final comparison more valuable as a quality gate over the revised outputs.
Overall, these results validate the design of the full ESC pipeline: both the
assess-before-revising mechanism (verification) and the compare-before-accepting
mechanism (final comparison) are necessary for optimal performance, and nei-
ther can be removed without degradation.
