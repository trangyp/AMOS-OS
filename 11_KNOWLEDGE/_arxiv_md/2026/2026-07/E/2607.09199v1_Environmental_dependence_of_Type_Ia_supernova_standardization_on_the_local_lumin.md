ABSTRACT
Context. The dependence of Type Ia supernova (SNe Ia) standardized luminosity on host galaxy properties constitutes a significant
systematic error in cosmology. As next-generation surveys like LSST and Roman reduce statistical uncertainties, cosmological precision
will be dominated by astrophysical systematic errors. However, the widely used empirical mass step, acting as an indirect global proxy,
obscures the direct physical link to the progenitor’s environment, thereby limiting the precision of SNe Ia luminosity standardization.
Aims. We aim to identify the fundamental driver of these dependencies by comparing local luminosity-weighted age (LWA) against
global mass, specifically investigating whether the mass step is merely a proxy for progenitor age.
Methods. Using SDSS-MaNGA Pipe3D, we extract the local LWA within a 1 kpc aperture for 56 SNe Ia and perform a joint likelihood
analysis to disentangle the impacts of local age and mass on Hubble residuals.
Results. We find that SNe Ia in younger environments are significantly fainter than those in older ones, exhibiting a step amplitude of
0.163 mag (5.2σ) after standardization. While global and local mass steps are initially observed (0.071 mag, 2.0σ and 0.087 mag, 2.4σ,
respectively), both become statistically insignificant when accounting for age. Specifically, the global mass step decreases to 0.028 mag
(0.9σ) while the corresponding age step remains robust at 0.156 mag (4.9σ). Similarly, the local mass step becomes statistically
insignificant (0.012 mag, 0.3σ) whereas the age step persists at 0.157 mag (4.4σ). Furthermore, the Hubble residual dispersion (wRMS)
decreased from 0.1550 to 0.1376 mag after introducing the local LWA age step.
Conclusions. Our results provide strong evidence that approximately 50% −60% of the variance from the stellar mass step is due to
an environmental dependence on progenitor age. Crucially, a systematic bias in the dark energy equation of state parameter could be
introduced in our sample if the age-dependent luminosity evolution is neglected, highlighting the necessity of local age corrections for
next-generation cosmology.

1. Introduction
Type Ia supernovae, owing to the high consistency of their peak
absolute magnitudes, are widely used as distance indicators and
are thus referred to as standard candles, they occupy an irreplace-
able position in modern cosmology (Phillips 1993; Hamuy et al.
1995; Riess et al. 1996; Tripp 1998). Observations of SNe Ia re-
vealed the accelerating expansion of the Universe (Riess et al.
1998; Schmidt et al. 1998; Perlmutter et al. 1999a) and remain
core probes for constraining the nature of dark energy and its evo-
lution (Garnavich et al. 1998; Perlmutter et al. 1999b; Brout et al.
2019; Riess et al. 2022). With the imminent commencement of
fourth-generation large-scale survey projects, represented by
the LSST (Ivezi´c et al. 2019) and the Roman Space Telescope
(Spergel et al. 2015), the sample size of SNe Ia is set to expe-
rience explosive growth. To match the statistical precision of
future experiments, resolve the Hubble tension between current
local Hubble constant measurements and cosmic microwave back-
ground (CMB) inferred values (Planck Collaboration et al. 2020;
Di Valentino et al. 2021; Freedman 2021; Riess et al. 2022), and
achieve higher precision in cosmological distance measurements,

Despite the success of luminosity standardization (Phillips
1993; Tripp 1998; Riess et al. 1998; Perlmutter et al. 1999a),
SNe Ia still exhibit significant luminosity dispersion driven by
host galaxy properties. Crucially, these environmental dependen-
cies pose a risk beyond merely increasing the scatter in the Hub-
ble diagram. Since the properties of host galaxies—particularly
the stellar population age—evolve with cosmic time (known
as progenitor drift), any uncorrected dependence of SNe Ia lu-
minosity on its local environment will manifest as a redshift-
dependent systematic bias (e.g., Branch et al. 2001; Rigault et al.
2020; Son et al. 2025). Such biases could mimic cosmological
signals, potentially leading to significant shifts in the inference
of the dark energy equation of state parameter, w (Campbell et al.
2016; Lee et al. 2021). Therefore, identifying the fundamental
physical driver of these environmental effects is essential for
unbiased cosmology.
In current studies, observations indicate that the standardized
luminosity of SNe Ia is correlated with the global host galaxy
mass, an effect known as the mass step: SNe Ia in lower-mass host
galaxies are fainter than those in higher-mass host galaxies after
luminosity standardization (e.g., Kelly et al. 2010; Sullivan et al.

could lead to erroneous conclusions. Currently, the physical ori-
gin of the mass step remains debated (Thorp & Mandel 2022;
Wiseman et al. 2023; Popovic et al. 2025), and the understand-
ing of the dependence of standardized luminosity on host galaxy
properties is incomplete. Therefore, further research into the de-
pendence of SNe Ia luminosity on host galaxy environment is
necessary (Brout & Scolnic 2021; Briday et al. 2022).
From a physical perspective, these environmental depen-
dencies could be used to constrain the SNe Ia progenitor sys-
tems. The debate over progenitor channels—specifically between
single-degenerate (SD) accretion and double-degenerate (DD)
merger scenarios—remains unresolved (e.g., Hillebrandt et al.
2013; Maoz et al. 2014; Ruiter & Seitenzahl 2025). Theoretical
models predict that these channels lead to distinct explosion mech-
anisms, ranging from near-Chandrasekhar mass (MCh) delayed
detonations (Khokhlov 1991; Höflich & Khokhlov 1996) to sub-
MCh double detonations (Fink et al. 2010; Sim et al. 2010) or
super-MCh mergers (Pakmor et al. 2012). Crucially, these diverse
progenitor channels are expected to operate on distinct timescales,
meaning their contributions may evolve with the age of the stellar
population. This temporal connection is constrained by the SNe Ia
delay-time distribution (DTD): early “prompt + delayed” param-
eterizations (e.g., Mannucci et al. 2005; Scannapieco & Bildsten
2005; Sullivan et al. 2006; Aubourg, É. et al. 2008) and later re-
sults favoring a smoother ∝t−1 DTD (e.g., Maoz et al. 2014)
imply that observed samples mix progenitors spanning a wide
range of ages. It naturally produces young and old modes in
the progenitor-age distribution when convolved with galaxy star-
formation histories (Childress et al. 2014). This physical link
strongly suggests that the observed environmental dependencies
could be driven by the diversity of progenitor ages.
However, relying on global host galaxy properties as prox-
ies for the supernova environment is often insufficient. Massive
galaxies typically exhibit complex star formation histories, where
global properties could dilute the true physical signal at the spe-
cific explosion site. For instance, a supernova exploding in a local-
ized star-forming region at the edge of a passive massive galaxy
experiences an environment vastly different from the galaxy’s
global average (Rigault et al. 2013; Roman et al. 2018; Meng
2019). Consequently, recent research has shifted towards analyz-
ing local environmental parameters within a few kiloparsecs of
the supernova (Stanishev et al. 2012; Galbany et al. 2014). The
pioneering work demonstrated that corrections based on local spe-
cific star formation rate (LsSFR) significantly outperform global
mass corrections (Rigault et al. 2013, 2020), strongly suggest-
ing that the mass step is driven by local stellar population age.
However, establishing the robustness of this age-driven hypoth-
esis requires probing the stellar population age across multiple
dimensions using independent physical indicators.
Although LsSFR is an effective tracer of very recent star
formation and can broadly separate environments dominated by
young or old populations (Kennicutt 1998), it does not provide
a quantitative constraint on the age distribution of the SNe Ia
progenitor population—especially in passive galaxies where star
formation has largely ceased. To overcome this limitation, we
require an indicator derived from stellar population synthesis
models that can reconstruct the star-formation history and hence
constrain stellar population age even in the absence of ongoing
star formation (e.g., Conroy 2013). The Sloan Digital Sky Survey
Mapping Nearby Galaxies at Apache Point Observatory (SDSS
MaNGA) provides the necessary integral field spectroscopy
(IFS) data (Bundy et al. 2015; Drory et al. 2015; Yan et al. 2016;
Wake et al. 2017; Blanton et al. 2017). Leveraging the MaNGA
Pipe3D pipeline (Sánchez et al. 2016; Sánchez et al. 2018), we
adopt the local LWA, although it is also weighted towards lu-
minous populations, it is derived from full-spectrum fitting—
incorporating continuum shape and absorption features—which
allows it to effectively distinguish between young and old stellar
populations across the full evolutionary timeline.
In this study, we use local LWA derived from simple stellar
population (SSP) fitting to investigate the environmental depen-
dence of SNe Ia standardization in depth. We utilize a wide-field,
untargeted supernova sample provided by the Zwicky Transient
Facility (ZTF) (Bellm et al. 2019; Graham et al. 2019), combined
with the MaNGA Pipe3D stellar population products, to obtain
environmental parameters within a 1 kpc radius of the super-
nova explosion site. Following the general procedure for study-
ing the step effect (e.g., Kelly et al. 2010; Sullivan et al. 2010;
Rigault et al. 2020; Burgaz et al. 2025), we divide the SNe Ia
sample into young and old sub-samples based on their local LWA.
By comparing the Hubble residual step analyses of these two
groups, we aim to verify: (1) whether local LWA based on IFS
measurements can effectively separate SNe Ia populations of dif-
ferent luminosities; and (2) compare this with the local mass step
and global mass step to observe the dominant factor driving the
mass step. In Sect. 2, we describe the cross-matching of the Tran-
sient Name Server (TNS) catalog with the MaNGA survey and
detail the methodology for deriving local environmental metrics,
specifically the local LWA, as well as the global fit framework
used to minimize Hubble residuals. Sect. 3 presents our main
results regarding the correlations between Hubble residuals and
environmental properties, focusing on the comparison between
the mass step and the age step. In Sect. 4, we perform a series of
robustness checks to validate the stability of our findings. Finally,
we discuss the impact of our results on cosmology in Sect.5 and
summarize our conclusions in Sect. 6.

tify hosts (Sect. 2.1); correcting for Milky Way extinction and red-
shift effects (Sect. 2.2); acquiring and filtering forced photometry
from ZTF to construct high-quality light curves (Sect. 2.3); per-

2.1. Cross-matching the TNS and MaNGA
We first query the public database of the TNS and obtain a sample
containing 11,524 transient sources spectroscopically confirm
as normal SNe Ia (Branch et al. 1993). To obtain detailed host
galaxy properties for these supernovae, we cross-match this sam-
ple with the Value Added Catalog (VAC) of the SDSS MaNGA
project.
Host galaxy identification employs a joint strategy based on
spatial coordinates and redshift matching. Initially, we define
a conservative search radius of 40′′ around the coordinates of
SNe Ia provided by the TNS. This threshold is selected to suf-
ficiently encompass the spatial extent of the targeted galaxies.
Fig. 1 illustrates the spatial coverage of the SDSS MaNGA in-
tegral field unit (IFU) using the host galaxy of SN 2021bqv as
a representative example. For our full sample, the IFU bundles
range from 37 to 127 fibers, corresponding to field of view (FoV)
diameters between 17′′ and 32′′ (radii of ∼8.5′′ to 16′′). Since our
40′′ search radius significantly exceeds the maximum MaNGA
FoV radius, it ensures a complete inclusion of any SNe Ia that fall
within the effective IFU coverage, while the subsequent redshift
matching effectively filters out chance alignments.
Subsequently, to eliminate chance alignments along the line
of sight of the host galaxy, we introduce the directional light
radius (DLR) method (Sullivan et al. 2006; Gupta et al. 2016).
For each candidate host, we compute the normalized separation
between the SN and the galaxy center in units of the directional
light radius, dDLR, based on the galaxy effective size, axis ratio,
and position angle. When multiple candidates are present, we
retain the one with the smallest dDLR and require dDLR ≤5.4
for host assignment. Finally, we apply strict spectral redshift con-
sistency constraints. We require the redshift difference between
the supernova and the host galaxy to be |∆z| < 0.003, which cor-
responds to a velocity tolerance of about 900 km s−1, sufficient
to cover the peculiar velocity range of the host galaxy. After the
above screening, this selection yield a final sample containing 166
SNe Ia-MaNGA matched pairs. Notably, the redshift difference
of our final sample is generally less than 0.001 (∼300 km s−1),
further verifying the reliability of host galaxy identification.
After subsequent light curve quality filtering (Sect. 2.3), fit-
ting selection (Sect. 2.4), local parameter extraction (Sect. 2.5),
and Hubble residual fitting (Sect. 2.6), the final sample size for the
study of SNe Ia Hubble residuals and host galaxy local properties
is 56.
Given the established connections between SNe Ia prop-
erties and host-galaxy morphology (Hamuy et al. 1996, 2000;
Kang et al. 2016; Pruzhinskaya et al. 2020), it is critical to ver-

interval (0.02 < z < 0.08), the impact of the host galaxy’s peculiar
velocity on redshift measurement cannot be ignored. Consider-

redshift zcmb in the subsequent analysis. To statistically eliminate
the impact of uncorrected host galaxy peculiar velocities, we add
an additional uncertainty term σpec = 300 km s−1 (uncertainty
floor) to the final error, as done in previous works like Riess et al.

2.3. Raw Light Curves Data Quality Filtering and Baseline
Correction
We utilize the ZTF Forced Photometry Service (ZFPS) to obtain
the raw photometric data for our supernova sample (Masci et al.
2019; Masci et al. 2023). We emphasize that these data are forced-
photometry products generated by the ZFPS on archived ZTF
difference images. In this framework, the photometric measure-
ments are obtained through forced PSF-fit photometry at the fixed
supernova coordinates on the difference images, and the primary
host-galaxy background is suppressed through subtraction of a
reference image in the ZTF difference-imaging pipeline. This ap-
proach allows us to recover flux measurements below the nominal
single-epoch detection threshold and provides a continuous flux
history, which is critical for constraining the explosion epoch and
assessing the baseline of the differential-flux light curve.
However, the returned fluxes are differential fluxes relative to
the reference image rather than absolute host-subtracted source
fluxes. As noted in the ZFPS documentation, systematic errors
may remain owing to imperfect subtraction, contamination from
neighboring sources, non-photometric observing conditions, or
contamination of the reference image itself by transient flux. For
this reason, we therefore do not assume that the reference-image
subtraction completely removes all host-related or background-
related systematic errors; instead, the ZFPS products require
subsequent validation, quality filtering, and baseline correction
before cosmological use.
In addition, the ZTF survey officially commenced science
operations in March 2018. Although our parent sample sourced
from the TNS contains records predating this timeline, we exclude
targets discovered before 2018 because no ZTF photometric data
is available for these events. This cut ensures that all selected
targets fall within the ZTF observational window. With our data
cutoff set to October 2025, this constraint reduces our initial
cross-matched SNe Ia sample to 154 objects.
2.3.1. Light Curve Quality Filtering
Following the ZFPS user guide (Masci et al. 2023), we perform
strict quality filtering on the acquired raw light curves. This step
eliminates epochs with low signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) affected
by observing conditions (such as cloud cover, moonlight pol-
lution) or data processing anomalies. We perform strict quality
filtering on the acquired raw light curves to eliminate epochs
affected by poor observing conditions or processing anomalies.
Strictly adhering to the criteria defined in the ZFPS user guide
(Masci et al. 2023), we exclude measurement points that meet any
of the following conditions: 1. Photometric calibration anomaly:
infobitssci flag indicates serious problems with image process-
ing or instrument calibration (usually filtering infobitssci > 0
or specific high-order masks); 2. High background noise: The
background noise level scisigpix of the scientific image exceeds
the threshold (recommended value > 25 DN, where DN denotes
data numbers), which usually implies severe cloud occlusion or
moonlight interference; 3. Poor seeing: The seeing sciinpseeing
during observation is poor (recommended value > 4 arcseconds),
leading to severe point source diffusion.
Although this strict filtering strategy reduces the number of
sampling points in the light curve, potentially losing some early or
late data obtained in poor weather, it significantly reduces outliers
and systematic errors in photometric data. This is crucial for
accurately reconstructing supernova luminosity evolution using
the SALT2 model and precisely determining fitting parameters
(Sect. 2.4).
2.3.2. Light Curve Baseline Correction
Although the ZFPS pipeline has been preliminarily processed,
after completing quality filtering, we still find non-zero baseline
offsets in some light curves, typically originating from potential
transient source contamination in reference images or incom-
pletely removed systematic errors (Masci et al. 2019). Therefore,
we implement a strict set of baseline correction processes, fol-
lowing standard procedures for ZTF photometry (e.g., Yao et al.
2019).
First, to accurately separate transient signals from the back-
ground baseline, we use temporal S/N clustering technology.

Secondly, we define a clean baseline sampling window, select-

calculating the baseline flux, given that the uncertainty of differ-

advice adopted in Betoule et al. (2014) and Rigault et al. (2025)

corrections of approximately 2.5%, 3.5%, and 6.0% in the g, r,
and i bands, respectively.
Finally, to eliminate outliers we combine absolute me-
dian deviation with an iterative sigma clipping algorithm
(Ivezi´c et al. 2014) to clean the baseline data. Based on the
cleaned sample, we use the inverse variance weighting method
(Bevington & Robinson 2003) to calculate the average back-
ground flux and subtract it from the full time-series data to obtain
zero-point corrected light curves.
2.4. Light Curve Fitting
We employ the SALT2 (Guy et al. 2007) light-curves fitting
model to fit the SNe Ia light curves using the SALT2-T21 ver-
sion (Taylor et al. 2021). For a detailed description of the model
framework, we refer the reader to the original publications; here,
we avoid reiterating these details and focus on the parameters rel-
evant to our analysis. This model standardizes SNe Ia luminosity
using the peak apparent magnitude (mB), light curve stretch factor
(x1), and color parameter (c), where x1 is the light-curve stretch
parameter that correlates with the intrinsic luminosity, indicating
the brighter-slower relation (Rust 1974; Pskovskii 1977; Phillips
1993), and c is the color parameter reflecting SNe Ia intrinsic
color variations and host galaxy dust extinction.
Since the peak time t0 of the ZFPS data is not known a priori,
we implement a two-stage iterative fitting strategy to ensure the
robustness of parameter estimation:
The first stage applies Gaussian smoothing and S/N threshold
filtering to the raw light curve to roughly estimate the peak time
t0. Subsequently, centering on this peak t0, a time window of
20 days before and 50 days after the explosion is selected for
preliminary fitting to obtain a high-precision peak time t1st
0 .
In the second stage, we use the high-precision peak time t1st
0
obtained in the first stage as an anchor to further restrict the fitting
window to 10 days before the peak to 40 days after the peak. This
selection of time window follows Rigault et al. (2025) and aims
to exclude data from the very early phase (t < 10 days). The
SALT2-T21 model is known to have significant model bias in the
very early phase, and including too much very early data could
introduce non-physical systematic errors.
Fig. 3 shows a representative example of the fitting results for

and i bands.

2.5. Extraction and Processing of Local Environmental
Parameters
To investigate the physical drivers of SNe Ia luminosity varia-
tions, we leverage the spatially resolved capabilities of MaNGA
IFU data to derive local environmental properties. Unlike global
measurements, local parameter extraction requires careful consid-
eration of the physical resolution and S/N, and the specific nature
(extensive vs. intensive) of the physical quantities involved. In
this section, we first detail the definition of the local aperture and
the differentiated processing strategies for stellar mass and local
LWA (Sect. 2.5.1), with particular attention to determining the
effective physical resolution. Subsequently, we outline the dy-
namic threshold scanning (Sect. 2.5.2) strategy use to objectively
classify the SNe Ia sample into distinct populations based on their
local stellar population age.
2.5.1. Definition of Target Region and Parameter Extraction
Process
The local environment properties used in this study are taken from
the MaNGA Pipe3D VAC which contains pixel-level parameters,
rather than being re-fitted by us from the raw MaNGA spectra.
For the DR17 MaNGA Pipe3D products used in this work, the
released stellar-population decomposition is based on a MaStar-
based SSP library. The corresponding MaStar spectra cover 3622–
10354 Å, and the DR17 MaNGA Pipe3D VAC uses a template
set comprising 39 ages and 7 metallicities.

2.5.2. Classifying the SNe Ia Sample by local LWA
Recent studies such as Rigault et al. (2020) revealed a significant
correlation between SNe Ia Hubble residuals and LsSFR. This
observational fact strongly implies that the progenitor’s stellar
population age is likely the fundamental physical factor driving
the dispersion of Hubble residuals and affecting the precision of
cosmological distance measurements.
Consequently, this study utilizes the stellar parameter maps
provided by the MaNGA Pipe3D Value Added Catalog (VAC).
This data product is based on MaNGA Integral Field Spec-
troscopy (IFS) and generated by the Pipe3D pipeline through
SSPs(Sánchez et al. 2016). We adopt the derived local LWA as
a physical probe, aiming to divide the SNe Ia progenitor envi-
ronment into young and old physically, thereby clarifying the
physical driving mechanism of luminosity standardization disper-
sion.
Due to the limitation of the number of existing spectral sam-
ples, artificially specifying a fixed age threshold (such as the
median or a specific physical value) could introduce subjective
selection bias, thereby masking the true physical signal. To ob-
jectively determine the optimal physical boundary distinguishing
young and old stellar populations, this study adopts a dynamic
threshold scanning strategy.
Specifically, we scanned all possible split points within the
10% to 90% quantile range of the local age distribution. This trim-
ming of the sample boundaries ensures that both the young and
old subsamples contain a sufficient number of objects to provide
reliable statistical power and prevents the threshold search from
being dominated by extreme outliers at the distribution tails. For
each potential threshold, we divided the sample and calculated
the Hubble residual step and its statistical significance. We de-
termine the optimal threshold by maximizing this significance;
this approach effectively minimizes the residual sum of squares
of the model, which is statistically equivalent to a likelihood
ratio test for threshold models and yields the Maximum Like-
lihood Estimate (MLE) for the luminosity step (Hansen 2000).
To further ensure the robustness of our results and mitigate in-
terference from small-sample fluctuations, we imposed a strict
boundary constraint, requiring each subsample to contain at least
20 supernovae.

We examine the distribution characteristics of SNe Ia Hubble

the relationship between local LWA distribution and SNe Ia stan-

3.1. Light Curve Parameters
Tripp (1998) revealed the standardization of SNe Ia for cosmol-
ogy relies on the empirical correlations between peak luminosity
and light curve stretch factor (x1) and color (c). Therefore, under-
standing how x1 and c depend on the local environment is crucial
for identifying potential sources of bias. Specifically, correlating
these parameters with local LWA allows us to test whether the
progenitor age primarily affects the light curve stretch factor x1
or the dust/intrinsic color traced by c. In this section, we exam-
ine the dependence of SALT2-T21 parameters on local LWA, as
illustrated in Fig. 6.
3.1.1. Light Curve Stretch factor
The upper panel of Fig. 6 shows the relationship between the
SALT2-T21 stretch factor x1 and local LWA. We find that the
correlation between x1 and local LWA is very significant. The
Spearman rank correlation coefficient is rs = −0.61, with a sig-
nificance of 5.7σ. At the same time, we perform KS-test and
Student’s T-test (T-test) on the young and old groups grouped by
the threshold of 9.084 (see Sect. 2.5.2). The results show that the
x1 value of the young group is larger than that of the old group by
0.803 ± 0.236. The obtained KS-test p-value is 0.045, and T-test
p-value is 0.002. This indicates that the x1 distributions of the
young group and old group are statistically different.
This result indicates that SNe Ia in younger local environ-
ments tend to have broader, slower-declining light curves (larger
x1), whereas those in older environments show narrower, faster-
declining light curves. This trend is consistent with the long-
established environmental dependence of SN Ia light-curve width.
Previous studies already showed that younger stellar populations
host slower-declining and more luminous SNe Ia (Hamuy et al.
1996), while later studies demonstrated that light-curve width
or stretch is closely connected to host-galaxy stellar population
properties such as sSFR, host type, stellar mass, and progeni-
tor age (Sullivan et al. 2010; Lampeitl et al. 2010; Rigault et al.
2013, 2020).

stellar mass, with Spearman coefficients of rs = −0.37 (p =

both trends are weaker than the correlation between x1 and local

local stellar-population age and to identify the primary driver of
the luminosity step, we compare three standardization models: (i)
a baseline fit with no step term, (ii) a mass-step-only fit including

4. Robustness Checks
We have identified a significant correlation between local LWA
and SNe Ia Hubble residuals. However, as this study explores an
environmental effect rather than a fundamental parameter derived
from a physical law, it is imperative to determine whether the
appearance of this signal could be induced by systematic errors
or methodological artifacts. In this section, we scrutinized the
stability of our results to ensure the observed local LWA step
is not a consequence of dilution from PSF homogenization, nor
a byproduct of degeneracy with host galaxy dust. Furthermore,
we discussed the impact of mass threshold selection and verified
that the signal persists across different SNe Ia sub-populations
(classified by stretch factor and color) and remains statistically
significant under sub-sample resampling.
4.1. Dilution from PSF Homogenization
The extraction of all local environmental parameters in this study
is built on the framework described in Sect. 2.5: defining a target
region with a physical radius of 1 kpc. However, limited by the
spatial resolution of MaNGA data, what is actually measured
represents the mixed information of this target region and its sur-
rounding larger range (scale ≥2 kpc). This unavoidable dilution
effect could affect our results.
First, the most direct impact is the dilution of extreme local
environmental signals. If a supernova progenitor was born in a
young star cluster with a scale much smaller than 1 kpc, or located
on a boundary with a very steep age gradient, the local LWA we
measured will be closer to the average value of the larger scale
stellar population around it. This means that we could underes-
timate the extreme values of environmental parameters in the
sample, and the correlation coefficient between the observed en-
vironmental parameters and Hubble residuals could be physically
stronger than what we measured. In other words, our results could

4.2. Impact of host galaxy dust
Since the color coefficient, β, for SNe Ia originates physically
from host galaxy dust, recent studies have indicated that dust prop-
erties could vary with the environment (Brout & Scolnic 2021;
Popovic et al. 2021; González-Gaitán et al. 2021; Meldorf et al.
2023; Wojtak et al. 2023; Ginolin et al. 2025a). Variations in dust
properties with the environment could induce color biases in
supernovae, which in turn lead to biases in the standardized lu-
minosity. Furthermore, given that dust properties are correlated
with many host galaxy properties (Tremonti et al. 2004), the cal-
culation of luminosity-weighted properties, such as stellar mass
and age, relies on dust extinction corrections. Consequently, the
influence of dust must be treated with caution when observing
and interpreting the environmental effects on SNe Ia luminosity.
Although the impact of dust may not be ignored in standard-
ization, Childress et al. (2013) pointed out that the variation of
dust extinction with global host galaxy mass is too smooth to be
the primary driver of the mass step. Ginolin et al. (2025a) noted
that there is no significant evolutionary relationship between the
tracers of the environmental dependence of SNe Ia standardized
luminosity and supernova color. This implies that the environ-
mental dependence of standardized luminosity may not be simply
explained by the evolution of host galaxy dust properties. While
host galaxy dust properties are known to evolve with galaxy mass
and age, potentially biasing SNe Ia standardization, our results
suggest that dust is not the primary driver of the observed age
step in this sample. As demonstrated in Sect. 3.1 (Fig. 6), we find
no significant correlation between the local LWA and the SALT2
color parameter c (Spearman rs = 0.01). Since the parameter c
captures the combined effects of intrinsic supernova color and
dust reddening, its lack of correlation with local LWA implies
that the dust properties of the progenitor environments in our
sample are statistically decoupled from their stellar population
age. Consequently, the significant correlation observed between
Hubble residuals and local LWA cannot be attributed to a hidden
dust-age degeneracy. Based on this orthogonality, we focus the
subsequent analysis on the direct impact of stellar age, treating it
as an independent physical driver distinct from color-based dust
corrections.
We note that this interpretation is further supported by the
independent fits to the young and old subsamples presented in
Sect. 4.5. Although the best-fit nuisance parameters differ mod-
erately between the two groups (βyoung = 3.363, βold = 2.8985),
the age step remains significant at 0.1607 ± 0.0345 mag when
the two subsamples are fitted separately, arguing against a purely
dust-driven origin.

4.4. Robustness after Classification by Stretch factor or Color
Sullivan et al. (2010) proposed another way to test for potential
non-uniformity in the standardization which examined the varia-
tion of the brightness offset between SNe Ia in low- and high-mass
hosts when splitting the sample at c = 0 or x1 = 0 (Rigault et al.
2020).
To perform these tests, we classified samples by color and
stretch factor to observe the significance of the age step as in
(Sullivan et al. 2010). We obtain ∆A = 0.168 ± 0.055 for 21 SNe
with c < 0, and ∆A = 0.156 ± 0.034 in 35 samples with c > 0. In
39 samples with x1 < 0, we obtain ∆A = 0.177 ± 0.040, and in
17 samples with x1 > 0, we obtain ∆A = 0.144 ± 0.039. The age
step in samples with c < 0 is slightly larger than in samples with
c > 0, and the age step in samples with x1 < 0 is slightly larger
than in samples with x1 > 0.
The results demonstrate that the local LWA step clearly exists
on both sides of these dividing lines for both the stretch factor
and color parameter. Moreover, the size of the local-LWA step
is consistent between these subsets. This indicates that the envi-
ronmental dependence cannot be removed by the standard linear
SALT2-T21 corrections based only on x1 and c. We test this in-
terpretation more directly in Sect. 4.5 by allowing the young and
old subsamples to have independent standardization coefficients.

4.6. Robustness of the inferred mass and age steps
As an additional robustness test, we examined how the inferred
mass and age steps depend on both the light-curve-selection cri-
teria and the redshift prescription used to compute the Hubble
residuals. We considered two sample definitions: a conservative
one with a narrower colour range and a stricter fit-probability
requirement (−0.3 < c < 0.3 and fitprob > 10−3), and a broader
one with a wider colour range and a looser fit-probability cut
(−0.2 < c < 0.8 and fitprob > 10−7). The latter choice fol-
lows the baseline selection adopted in previous volume-limited
ZTF SN Ia DR2 analyses of environmental steps, in particular
Ginolin et al. (2025b). For each sample definition, we repeated
the analysis using both the CMB-frame redshift, zcmb, with a
conservative 300 km s−1 peculiar-velocity uncertainty, and the
peculiar-velocity-corrected redshift, zcor, derived from the 2M++
density field Carrick et al. (2015).

comparatively stable, changing from 0.163±0.031 mag to 0.148±

mass step is reduced, to 0.028 ± 0.033 mag for zcmb and 0.059 ±
0.035 mag for zcor, whereas the age step remains significant at
0.156 ± 0.032 mag and 0.128 ± 0.033 mag, respectively.

zcmb and 61 SNe for zcor. In this setup, the mass step is 0.112 ±

5. Discussion: Impact of local LWA on Dark Energy
Equation of State Parameter
Previous studies have shown that SNe Ia in younger environments
are systematically fainter than those in older environments after
luminosity standardization (Childress et al. 2013; Campbell et al.
2016; Rigault et al. 2020). This residual correlation indicates that
SNe Ia luminosities require additional corrections beyond the
standard light-curve standardization; their standardized brightness
retains a dependency on progenitor physics. This creates a critical
cosmological challenge due to progenitor drift: as we observe
higher redshift, the mean age of stellar populations decreases
significantly (Branch et al. 2001).
Recent work has quantified this effect. While Nicolas et al.
(2021) observed redshift-dependent evolution in light-curve pa-
rameters, Son et al. (2025) provided evidence (at the 5.5σ level)
that the standardized luminosity itself correlates strongly with
host galaxy age, confirming that SNe Ia from younger progeni-
tors appear systematically fainter. This mechanism is directly sup-
ported by our detection of a significant local age step (Sect. 3.2.2).
Because the fraction of young progenitor environments in-
creases with redshift (progenitor drift; Branch et al. 2001), a
redshift-dependent bias in the Hubble diagram (a spurious tilt
in µ versus z) could be introduced if ignoring an age-dependent
luminosity term. This systematic could shift the inferred dark-
energy equation-of-state parameter w by an amount of order
O(0.1–0.3), with the sign and amplitude depending on the sam-
ple redshift distribution and on the adopted step parameteri-
zation (Campbell et al. 2016; Kang et al. 2020; Lee et al. 2021;
Son et al. 2025). In our sample, accounting for the age step drives
the best-fit value towards more negative w (Sect. 5; Fig. 11). The
inferred w is shifted toward less negative values if the age step is
ignored.
To quantify the impact of this environmental dependence on
dark energy constraints, we perform a statistical test based on
MLE under the wCDM cosmological model. Given that our sam-
ple is confined primarily to the low-redshift regime, the redshift
coverage is insufficient to robustly constrain the time-varying
component of the equation of state wa. At low-redshift, the lumi-
nosity distance is relatively insensitive to higher-order variations
of w(z), and introducing wa would induce severe parameter de-
generacy. Therefore, we adopt a constant equation of state model
(wCDM). This framework captures the first-order impact of envi-
ronmental systematics on the inferred dark energy density while
ensuring the statistical robustness of the fit.

5.3. Impact of local LWA on Dark Energy Equation of State
Parameter
After establishing local LWA as a superior environmental indi-
cator, we examined its impact on cosmological parameter esti-
mation. To quantify this, we introduce the age step ∆A as a free
parameter in the wCDM model and perform a joint likelihood
analysis.
Fig. 11 presents the one-dimensional likelihood profiles (rep-
resented as ∆χ2) for the dark energy equation of state parameter
w. The baseline fit (black dashed line), which ignores the age
step, yields a value of wbase = −0.92 ± 0.38. This result is con-
sistent with the previous ΛCDM model assumptions (Sect. 2.6).
However, when the age step is simultaneously constrained, the
likelihood minimum shifts significantly. The corrected fit (red
solid line) yields wcorr = −1.21 ± 0.39, with a systematic shift of
∆w = −0.29. The comparison of these probability distributions
highlights that neglecting environmental dependencies introduces
a substantial bias in this dataset, shifting the best-fit value from
wbase = −0.92 to wcorr = −1.21 (i.e., towards more negative
values than −1).
While Fig. 11 quantifies the magnitude of this parameter shift,
Fig. 12 elucidates the physical mechanism driving it. The key
factor is the cosmic evolution of progenitor environments: the
universe is naturally younger at higher redshifts. Consequently,
the SNe Ia population evolves from a mixture of young and
old environments at low z to a population dominated by young
environments at high z.
As shown in the bottom panel of Fig. 12, the age correction
is not applied uniformly across redshift in our sample: the higher-
redshift bins contain a larger fraction of SNe classified as young,
and therefore receive a net positive luminosity correction (red
arrows), while the lower-redshift bins exhibit a smaller mean
correction. This redshift-dependent correction introduces a tilt to
the Hubble residuals, effectively altering the slope of the Hubble
diagram. Since the parameter w is sensitive to the expansion
history derived from this slope, the systematic steepening of the
residuals drives the best-fit w towards a more negative value. This
demonstrates that the Age Drift of progenitors could mimic a
non-standard dark energy signature if not properly accounted for.

6. Conclusion
This work utilize data obtain from the cross-matching of SDSS
MaNGA and ZTF forced photometry services to show a signifi-
cant statistical correlation between the environmental properties
within a 1 kpc physical aperture of the Type Ia supernova ex-
plosion site and the Hubble residuals. By constructing a joint
likelihood analysis containing the full covariance matrix, we find
that local LWA is a more fundamental physical driver than global
host galaxy mass and local stellar mass. Specifically:
Light Curve Parameters and local LWA: We find a significant
negative correlation between the SNe Ia light curve stretch fac-
tor (x1) and local LWA, confirming that younger environments
tend to host SNe Ia with broader, slower-declining light curves
as discovered previously(e.g., Hamuy et al. 1995; Howell 2001;
Lampeitl et al. 2010; Sullivan et al. 2010; Rigault et al. 2013,
2020). In contrast, no significant correlation is found between the
color parameter (c) and local LWA, suggesting that SNe Ia color
is not strongly driven by progenitor age in our sample.

local LWA Step: After standardizing the light curves, SNe Ia
in young environments are significantly fainter than those in old
environments. By incorporating an age step ∆A into the standard-
ization model, we detected a step amplitude of 0.163 mag with
a significance of 5.2σ. This inclusion significantly reduces the
dispersion of Hubble residuals (wRMS decreased from 0.155 to
0.138 mag, about 10%), demonstrating that local LWA is a more
effective parameter for standardization than mass.
Host Galaxy Mass Step: While a global mass step 0.071 mag
(2.0σ) is observed, it is largely explained by the age difference.
When both age step and global mass step are fitted simultaneously,
the global mass step decreased by approximately 60% (from
0.071 to 0.028 mag) and became statistically insignificant (0.9σ),
whereas the age step remained robust at > 4.9σ. This supports
the hypothesis that the widely used global mass step is primarily
a proxy for progenitor age(e.g., Galbany et al. 2014; Roman et al.
2018; Rigault et al. 2020).
Local Mass Step: The local stellar mass step is almost entirely
attributable to age. When accounting for local LWA, the local
mass step almost vanishes completely (from 0.087 to 0.01 mag,
0.3σ), while the age step signal remains strong. This indicates
that local mass does not provide additional constraining power
on SNe Ia luminosity once the local age of the stellar population
is known.
Impact on Dark Energy Equation of State parameter: We
quantify the systematic bias induced by neglecting the environ-
mental age correction on the dark energy equation of state pa-
rameter, w. As discussed in Sect. 5, the proportion of young
progenitors increases with redshift. When the age step correction
is applied to a baseline calibration, the inferred w is shifted by as
much as ∆w ≈−0.3, a bias that could lead to a misinterpretation

more robust predictor of SNe Ia luminosity than global host
galaxy mass. Our results suggest that the traditional mass step
acts largely as a proxy for these underlying age differences. There-

spatially resolved local environmental metrics over global host
galaxy properties. Adopting this approach is crucial for mitigating
redshift-dependent systematic biases and ensuring the precision
of SNe Ia cosmology in the era of next-generation surveys.
