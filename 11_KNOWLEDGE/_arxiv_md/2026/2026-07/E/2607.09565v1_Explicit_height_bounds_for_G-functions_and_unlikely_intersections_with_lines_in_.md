MARTIN ORR
Abstract. This paper computes explicit constants in Bombieri and Andr´e’s
height bound for points at which there are unexpected “global” polymomial
relations between values of G-functions. It applies these bounds for G-functions
to obtain explicit height bounds for unlikely intersections with lines in tori,
making explicit a weak version of the bounded height theorem of Bombieri,
Masser and Zannier and, in some cases, improving an explicit bound of Habegger.
1. Introduction
A G-function is a power series in Q[[X]] satisfying a differential equation and
a growth condition on its coefficients (for all absolute values on Q). They were
introduced by Siegel to study transcendence of numbers [SFZ14].
One of the
main results on polynomial relations between values of G-functions is Bombieri’s
principle of global relations, sometimes known as the “Hasse principle for values
of G-functions” [Bom81].
Andr´e applied Bombieri’s theorem to prove a height bound for what would later
become known as unlikely intersections (in a situation involving endomorphisms
of abelian varieties) [And89]. In recent years, Andr´e’s method of applying the
principle of global relations has become an important tool in the study of unlikely
intersections, being used to prove results on “large Galois orbits” and cases of the
Zilber–Pink conjecture. This includes work by Binyamini and Masser [BM21], Daw
and the author of the present paper [DO21], [DO25], Papas [Pap24], [Pap] and
Urbanik [Urb25], [Urb], among other papers by the same authors. For a summary
of the method and applications, see Andr´e’s recent survey [And25].
The primary goal of this paper is to establish explicit versions of the height
bound in the principle of global relations. As an illustration of the usefulness of
these explicit height bounds, we derive an explicit bound for a weakened version of
Bombieri, Masser and Zannier’s theorem on unlikely intersections in tori [BMZ99].
While the G-functions method for unlikely intersections has often been described
as “effective in principle”, it turns out that non-trivial work is required to compute

1.A. Height bound for relations between G-functions. Bombieri’s principle
of global relations deals with “non-trivial global” relations between values of G-
functions. Precise definitions of these notions involve some subtleties (see section 3)
but, essentially, a “global relation” between G-functions y1, . . . , yµ at ξ ∈Q is a
polynomial P with coefficients in Q such that the equation
P(y1(ξ), . . . , yµ(ξ)) = 0
holds v-adically, for every place v at which this equation makes sense (that is,
whenever ξ lies inside the v-adic radii of convergence of y1, . . . , yµ). We say that
P is “non-trivial” if it does not come from a polynomial relation between the
G-functions themselves.
Incorporating some improvements by Andr´e, Bombieri’s principle of global rela-
tions is as follows.
Theorem 1.1. ([And89, Thm. E], building on [Bom81, Thm. 4]) Let y1, . . . , yµ be
G-functions. All points ξ ∈Q at which there exists a non-trivial global polynomial
relation P between the evaluations at ξ of y1, . . . , yµ satisfy
h(ξ) < c1 deg(P)c2,
where c1 and c2 depend only on the G-functions y1, . . . , yµ.
[Bom81, Thm. 4] and [And89, VII, 5.2, Thm.] give an explicit value for c2.
[Bom81, Thm. 3] gives an explicit value for c1 in the case deg(P) = 1, subject
to finitely many exceptions (explicitly bounded in number). Neither [Bom81] nor
[And89] gives an explicit value for c1 in general, although they give explicit bounds
for some intermediate steps ([Bom81, Main Thm.] and [And89, VII, 3.5, Prop.]).
This paper proves several explicit versions of Theorem 1.1:
(1) Corollary 4.5: an explicit value for c1, with the same c2 as in [Bom81,
Thm. 4] and [And89, VII, 5.2, Thm.];
(2) Theorem 4.2 and Corollary 4.4: more refined bounds which take into ac-
count additional information about the relations between y1, . . . , yµ at ξ;
(3) Corollary 5.4: an explicit value for c1, alongside a better value for c2, when
the relation is “super-strongly non-trivial” (this improvement to c2, under a
slightly weaker condition called “strongly non-trivial”, was already included
in [And89, VII, 5.2, Thm.], building on [Bom81, Thm. 7 and 8]);

1.C.1. Comparison of Theorem 1.2 with Theorem 1.4. Theorem 1.2 is weaker than
Theorem 1.4 and [Hab17, Thm. 11] in the following ways:
(1) The height bound in Theorem 1.2 depends (polynomially) on [K(s) : Q],
while the height bounds in Theorem 1.4 and [Hab17, Thm. 11] are indepen-
dent of the field of definition of s.
(2) Theorem 1.2 only bounds the height of points in C ∩Σ2 (unlikely intersec-
tions), instead of C ∩Σ1 (unlikely and just likely intersections).
(3) In Theorem 1.2, the curve C must contain the identity point (1, 1, . . . , 1)
of Gn
m. (This could be weakened to saying that C must contain a torsion
point of Gn
m, but that does not gain anything significant.)
(4) In Theorem 1.2, the closure in An of C must be a line.
Restrictions (1), (2) and (3) appear to be essential to the G-functions method
for intersections in algebraic groups, even if one does not ask for explicit bounds.
Restriction (1) arises because we build a global relation as a product of v-adic
relations, involving a factor for each complex place of K(s), so that the degree of
the global relation may be proportional to [K(s) : Q]. Restrictions (2) and (3)
are needed to construct v-adic relations at archimedean places with coefficients
in Q – without these restrictions, we could only construct relations with coefficients
involving 2πi or logarithms of coordinates of a chosen base point on C.
Restriction (4) is not essential to the G-functions method, but it significantly
simplifies the argument and leads to stronger explicit bounds because it avoids
the need to pass to a cover of C with a “good local parameter”, as in [DO25,
Lemma 5.1]. Restriction (4) will be removed in a later paper, which will also apply
the same method to abelian varieties.
