galaxy hosting an ancient slow bar with an accreted bulge and

ABSTRACT
We applied the barred population-orbit superposition method developed and validated in Jin et al. (2025a,b) to construct 3D chemo-
dynamical models for the barred S0 galaxy NGC 1381 (FCC 170) in the Fornax cluster. Based on the properties of stellar orbits in
the models, we decomposed NGC 1381 into six components with distinct kinematics and morphologies: (1) a dynamically warm
nuclear disc; (2) a rigidly rotating, BP/X-shaped bar; (3) a dynamically hot, spheroidal bulge; (4) a dynamically cold thin disc; (5)
a vertically extended thick disc with slightly slower rotation than the thin disc; and (6) a dynamically hot, spatially diffuse stellar
halo. The luminosity fractions of dynamical components indicate that NGC 1381 is dominated by the bar ( fbar ∼30%) and thin
disc ( fthin ∼28%), with notable contributions from the thick disc ( fthick ∼16%) and bulge ( fbulge ∼17%), while the nuclear disc
(fnucl ∼5%) and halo (fhalo ∼5%) are minor. The nuclear disc, bar, and thin disc are metal-rich ([Z/H] ≳0), α-poor ([Mg/Fe] ≲0.2),
and old (∼13 Gyr), corresponding to in situ formation in the early Universe. The bulge, thick disc, and stellar halo are metal-poor
([Z/H] ≲0), α-rich ([Mg/Fe] ≳0.2), and younger than or comparable in age to the in situ components, suggesting their relations
with ex situ formation contributed by minor mergers. The flat metallicity and [Mg/Fe] gradients in the thick disc and stellar halo
indicate they are dominated by a similar population of ex situ stars. In contrast, the bulge exhibits a negative metallicity gradient
(∇[Z/H]bulge < 0) pointing to a more complex formation history: the bulge could be either predominantly ex situ or contain a non-
negligible mixture of in situ and ex situ stars. Our modelling also reveals the presence of a slow bar (R = 2.40+0.54
−0.27), with a bar

1. Introduction
The classifications of galaxy structures originated from their
morphologies and colours. For instance, the central regions
of galaxies were initially classified as either red, spheroidal
‘classical bulges’ or blue, flat ‘pseudobulges’ linked to secu-
lar evolution (e.g. Kormendy & Kennicutt 2004; Athanassoula
2005), while later detailed morphological analyses have demon-
strated the complexity of bulge regions (e.g. Buta et al. 2015).
Recent observations have revealed composite bulges in some
galaxies, where distinct structures such as a classical bulge,
a bar, and a nuclear disc can coexist (e.g. Erwin et al. 2021;
Tahmasebzadeh et al. 2024).
In standard models of galaxy formation, bars, nuclear
discs, and classical bulges have distinct origins: bars can form
through internal instabilities in rotation-dominated discs (e.g.
Hohl 1971; Ostriker & Peebles 1973; Efstathiou et al. 1982) or
external tidal perturbations (e.g. Noguchi 1996; Łokas 2018;
Yoon et al. 2019); nuclear discs assemble from gas inflow to-

Hopkins et al. 2010). However, how these formation mecha-
nisms collectively influence the assembly history of galaxies, in-
cluding the roles of in situ star formation and external accretion,
remains poorly understood.
A key to understanding different structures lies in decompos-
ing them using line-of-sight information. The traditional pho-
tometric decomposition techniques were widely used in the
past decades. However, these techniques rely on strong assump-
tions such as bulges described by Sersic (1968) profiles, discs
modelled by exponential profiles, and bars approximated as
prolate Ferrers bars (see Binney & Tremaine 2008). However,
these assumptions often deviate from reality. Previous studies
have shown that the surface brightness profiles of discs can
be down-bending or up-bending in their inner or outer regions
(e.g. (Méndez-Abreu et al. 2017; Breda et al. 2020; Ding et al.
2023), while real bars can be triaxial, exhibiting boxy, peanut, or
X-shaped (hereafter BP/X-shaped) structures (e.g. Shaw 1987;

Lütticke et al. 2000; Erwin & Debattista 2017; Li et al. 2017). A
recent study by Gadotti (2026) demonstrated that whether photo-
metrically decomposed bulges are identified as classical bulges
or nuclear discs depends on both the optimisation algorithms
used and the imaging data quality. All these evidences indicate
that traditional photometric decomposition techniques struggle
to reliably distinguish between structures of different physical
origins.
Compared to traditional photometric decomposition, dynam-
ical decomposition can better trace the physical origins of dif-
ferent galaxy structures, as demonstrated in cosmological sim-
ulations (e.g., Correa et al. 2017; Pillepich et al. 2019; Du et al.
2019, 2020; Pulsoni et al. 2020; Cristiani et al. 2024). In recent
years, multiple integral field spectroscopy (IFS) surveys (e.g.
Bacon et al. 2001, 2017; Cappellari et al. 2011; Sánchez et al.
2012; Bryant et al. 2015; Bundy et al. 2015) have delivered spa-
tially resolved spectra for thousands of nearby galaxies. From
these spectra, people can derive stellar kinematic and population
maps, enabling both dynamical decomposition of real galaxies
and subsequent analysis of their chemical properties.
The dynamical decomposition of real galaxies can be per-
formed using the Schwarzschild’s orbit-superposition method
(Schwarzschild 1979, 1982, 1993), which constructs 3D
galaxy models by fitting the luminosity distributions and stel-
lar kinematic maps. This method has multiple implemen-
tations (e.g. van den Bosch et al. 2008; Long & Mao 2018;
Vasiliev & Valluri 2020; Neureiter et al. 2021; Quenneville et al.
2022; Dattathri et al. 2024). The triaxial implementation devel-
oped by van den Bosch et al. (2008) (see also the publicly avail-
able version DYNAMITE 1; Jethwa et al. 2020) was initially
designed without accounting for bars. It has been validated in
simulations (e.g. Zhu et al. 2018a; Jin et al. 2019) and has been
used to analyse dynamical structures of galaxies in IFS sur-
veys (e.g. Zhu et al. 2018a,b,c; Jin et al. 2020; Santucci et al.
2022, 2023; Thater et al. 2023). Subsequently, this implementa-
tion was updated to support the modelling of stellar populations
(e.g. Poci et al. 2019, 2021; Zhu et al. 2020, 2022a,b; Ding et al.
2023; Jin et al. 2024) and to include bar structures for non-edge-
on cases (inclination angle θ ≲80◦; Tahmasebzadeh et al. 2021,
2022, 2024). The combination of dynamical modelling with stel-
lar populations enables a powerful way to uncover the assembly
history of galaxies (van de Ven et al. 2025). Our recent studies
(Jin et al. 2025a,b) further enhanced this method by simultane-
ously incorporating bars and stellar populations in the modelling
and extending it to handle edge-on (θ ≳80◦) galaxies. This im-
proved method was validated using simulated galaxies from the
Auriga simulations (Grand et al. 2017, 2024), which can esti-
mate the bar pattern speeds with a relative uncertainty of ≲15%
for both side-on and end-on bars (Jin et al. 2025a). This method
can also reliably characterise the luminosity distributions, ages,
and metallicities of various dynamical structures, including bars,
bulges, discs, and stellar halos (Jin et al. 2025b).
NGC 1381 (FCC 170) is an edge-on disc galaxy located
in the central dense region of the Fornax cluster, with an es-
timated stellar mass of 2.25 × 1010 M⊙(Iodice et al. 2019a)
and a distance of 19.57 Mpc (Spriggs et al. 2021; see also
Blakeslee et al. 2009 for a slightly different value). This galaxy
was classified as an S0 galaxy (Ferguson 1989). Subsequently,
both optical and near-infrared images (e.g. Lütticke et al. 2000;
Bureau et al. 2006; Venhola et al. 2018) revealed an obvious
boxy bulge in its central region, which indicates the possible
existence of a bar. The stellar kinematic maps confirm the pres-

ence of a bar by presenting an anti-correlated, correlated, and
anti-correlated again h3–V relation along the galaxy’s major
axis (Fig 2; see also Pinna et al. 2019a; Poci et al. 2021). The
positive h3–V correlation is recognised as a robust bar diag-
nostic (e.g. Bureau & Athanassoula 2005; Molaeinezhad et al.
2016; Li et al. 2018), while the negative h3–V correlation at
the innermost region is the signature of a bar-induced nuclear
disc (e.g. Chung & Bureau 2004; Bureau & Athanassoula 2005;
Gadotti et al. 2020; Fraser-McKelvie et al. 2025). This galaxy
likely fell into the Fornax cluster approximately 8–12 Gyr ago
(e.g. Iodice et al. 2019b; Ding et al. 2023). During the galaxy’s
infall, interactions with the intracluster medium stripped the hot
ionised gas from its stellar halo, cutting off the gas supply for
star formation and resulting in strangulation-dominated quench-
ing (e.g. Morokuma-Matsui et al. 2022; Martig et al. 2026) and,
consequently, old stellar populations in the BP/X-shaped regions
nowadays (e.g., Pinna et al. 2019a; Martín-Navarro et al. 2021).
This implies NGC 1381 probably formed its bar in the early Uni-
verse before falling into the cluster, with its internal structures
preserved as ‘fossil records’ until the present day.
The structures of NGC 1381 have been studied in previous
works, but the different components in the galaxy’s inner region
were not accurately distinguished. For example, based directly
on the projected 2D morphology, kinematics, and stellar popu-
lations, Pinna et al. (2019a) divided the galaxy into distinct re-
gions, each dominated by a different structure: a nuclear disc,
a boxy structure, a thin disc, and inner and outer thick discs.
Decompositions based on dynamical modelling were later per-
formed by Poci et al. (2021) and Ding et al. (2023); however,
their models did not explicitly include a non-axisymmetric bar
and not concentrate much on the nuclear disc. In this paper, we
modelled NGC 1381 using the barred population-orbit superpo-
sition method (Jin et al. 2025a,b), which can effectively disen-
tangle the bar and the dynamically hot bulge. This enabled a full
dynamical decomposition of the galaxy into a nuclear disc, a bar,
a bulge, a thin disc, a thick disc, and a stellar halo. We derived
the 3D spatial distributions, kinematics, and chemical properties
of these components, including the bar pattern speed and bar
length.
The paper is organised as follows. In Sect. 2, we introduce
the photometric and spectroscopic data we use. In Sect. 3, we
describe how we construct barred population-orbit superposition
models for NGC 1381, including the stellar-orbit-based dynami-
cal decomposition. In Sect. 4, we characterise the bar, including
its pattern speed, length, and corotation radius, as well as the
luminosity distributions and chemical properties of all decom-
posed components. We discuss our results in Sect. 5 and sum-
marise our conclusions in Sect. 6.
Throughout the paper, the coordinate system (x′, y′) repre-
sents the observing plane with the galaxy’s major axis aligned
with the x′-axis. The intrinsic bar’s rotating frame is denoted
as (x, y, z), where the x-axis and y-axis coincide with the bar’s
major and minor axes, respectively. The inertial frame (x0, y0, z)
coincides with (x, y, z) at the initial time, but deviates from it at
later times due to the figure rotation about the z-axis.

2.2. Stellar kinematics and populations
The stellar kinematic maps we adopted were derived fol-
lowing the same approach as Iodice et al. (2019b) but with
an improved signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) of 100. These maps
were generated by applying the full spectral fitting software
pPXF (Cappellari & Emsellem 2004; Cappellari 2017) to spec-
tra binned with the Voronoi 2D method (Cappellari & Copin
2003). The maps include the mean velocity (V), velocity dis-
persion (σ), higher-order velocity moments parametrised by
the Gauss-Hermite coefficients (h3 and h4; Gerhard 1993;
van der Marel & Franx 1993; Rix et al. 1997), and their corre-
sponding errors. Since our dynamical modelling is triaxial, we
further point-symmetrised the kinematic maps and their error
maps to reduce noise, and present them in Fig. 2.
We employed three versions of stellar population maps:
Version A originates from Martig et al. (2026), using Voronoi-
binned spectra with S/N = 60; version B is based on Pinna et al.
(2019a) but with S/N improved to 100 (S/N = 40 in the original
work); version C is from Martín-Navarro et al. (2021), also with
S/N = 100. All versions were derived by applying the pPXF fit-
ting to Voronoi-binned spectra, with stellar population synthesis
models (Vazdekis et al. 2010, 2015) based on the MILES stellar
libraries (Sánchez-Blázquez et al. 2006) and BaSTI isochrones
(Pietrinferni et al. 2004, 2006). Versions A and B are mass-
weighted and assume a constant Kroupa (2001) initial mass
function (IMF), using spectra covering 4750–5500 Å, with the
spatial coverage of maps similar to that of the kinematic maps.
In contrast, version C is light-weighted, allows for the variation
of IMF, and uses spectra covering 4800–6400 Å, which has a
smaller coverage because pixels with S/N < 5 were not included
during the Voronoi-binning process. Compared to versions A
and B, version C has an offset of ∼−0.3 dex in metallicity and
∼0.05 dex in [Mg/Fe] abundance due to different settings when
fitting the spectra, but the spatial trends in the maps are similar.
Figure 2 shows the maps of stellar age, metallicity, and
[Mg/Fe] abundance for version A, with their error maps derived
from the Monte Carlo simulations of perturbed spectra. The stel-
lar population maps for versions B and C are presented in the left
panels of Fig. F.1 and Fig. F.2, respectively. Versions B and C
did not include error maps, and we thus adopted fixed uncertain-
ties of 1 Gyr for age, ∼0.05 dex for metallicity, and ∼0.03 dex
for [Mg/Fe] abundance. These uncertainties generally agree with
those of version A, as well as with estimates from previous stud-
ies of NGC 1381 and other galaxies with similar MUSE data
(e.g. Pinna et al. 2019a,b; Martig et al. 2021; Sattler et al. 2023,
2025). We treated data version A as the default version in our
analysis. It will be shown later that the fixed uncertainties in ver-
sions B and C do not affect our main findings.
3. Constructing barred population-orbit
superposition models
We constructed the barred population-orbit superposition mod-
els in three major steps. First, we constructed barred orbit-
superposition models by simultaneously fitting the stellar lumi-
nosity distributions and kinematic data (Sect. 3.1). Then, we cat-
egorised the stellar orbits in our models into different dynamical
structures based on their orbital properties (Sect. 3.2). Finally,
we assigned ages and metallicities to the orbits by fitting the
stellar age and metallicity maps (Sect. 3.3). Our methodology
for the first step follows Jin et al. (2025a), but has been modified
to include a supermassive black hole. For the second and third
steps, it follows Jin et al. (2025b), with updated orbit classifica-
tion criteria during the decomposition.
3.1. Constructing barred orbit-superposition models
To construct a barred orbit-superposition model, the gravita-
tional potential is first built from four components: (1) a triaxial
stellar bar with constant figure rotation, Ωp; (2) an axisymmetric
stellar disc aligned with the bar’s major axis; (3) a spherical dark
matter halo; and (4) a central supermassive black hole.

3.2. Decomposing galaxies based on stellar orbits
We decomposed the galaxy into six components: a nuclear disc,
a bar, a bulge, a thin disc, a thick disc, and a stellar halo, based

and identified Rbar as the smallest radius at which fcold ≥0.5.

inner region (nuclear disc, bar, and bulge) from the outer region
(thin disc, thick disc, and stellar halo).
The radius of the inner Lindblad resonance, RILR, is defined

and/or perpendicular to the bar (e.g. Contopoulos & Mertzanides
1977; Athanassoula 1992a,b; Binney & Tremaine 2008), make
up the nuclear disc in our modelling. For the best-fitting model,
we derived RILR = 16.6 arcsec (1.57 kpc).

4.2. Luminosity distribution of dynamical components
Using the orbital weights and trajectories from the best-fitting
model, we reconstructed the 3D luminosity distributions for six
dynamical components. Fig. 7 displays these distributions pro-
jected onto the x-y, x-z, and y-z planes. The 3D morphologies
of different components are well distinguished: the nuclear disc
occupies the galaxy’s inner region (≲20 arcsec); the bar is
BP/X-shaped; the thin disc dominates the galaxy’s outer region
(≳25 arcsec); the bulge is spheroidal; the thick disc is vertically
extended; and the stellar halo is radially diffuse. We note that
the ‘holes’ in the thin and thick disc components arise from our
dynamical classification criteria: orbits with R ≤Rbar either fol-
low the bar’s rigid rotation, belong to the dispersion-dominated
bulge, or are rotation-dominated orbits within the inner Lindblad
radius that belong to the nuclear disc (see Fig. 5). Such ‘holes’
also emerged in our previous simulation tests, where our model
results match the truth well (Jin et al. 2025b). The substructures,
including the BP/X-shaped structure in the nuclear disc and ring-
like structure in the thick disc, are artefacts of model fluctuations,
and they change within the 1σ confidence level across different
models.
Similarly, we derived the luminosity distributions of each
component for each model within the 1σ confidence level and
plot their surface brightness profiles on the x-y, x-z, and y-z
planes in Fig. 8. The profiles of the galaxy’s inner components
(nuclear disc, bar, and bulge) exhibit similarly steep radial de-
clines. In contrast, the thin and thick discs display more com-
plex trends: The inner regions with r ≲20 arcsec (2 kpc) of both
discs exhibit ‘holes’ in the x-y plane, while they display approx-
imately constant gradients in x-z and y-z planes, with the thin
disc being less vertically extended compared to the thick disc.
The stellar halo profiles are approximately exponential across
all projections. These complex surface brightness profiles de-
rived from our dynamical models indicate that the underlying
structures (with different physical origins) might deviate signif-

decomposition methods.

NGC 1381 is very old (tnucl ∼13 Gyr), indicating its formation
in the early Universe. After the galaxy entered the cluster ap-
proximately 8–12 Gyr ago (e.g. Iodice et al. 2019b; Ding et al.
2023), the star formation of the galaxy might have ceased
due to strangulation-dominated environmental quenching (e.g.,
Morokuma-Matsui et al. 2022; Martig et al. 2026), which has
led to an ancient (tbar ∼13 Gyr) bar in the present day.

in Fig. C.2, the dark matter in NGC 1381 becomes dominant
at radii comparable to or smaller than the bar length. There-
fore, the dynamical friction between the bar and dark matter
could transfer the bar’s angular momentum to the dark mat-

5.2. The assembly history of NGC 1381
We analysed the physical origins of different structures to inves-
tigate the assembly history of NGC 1381. The mean metallicities
and [Mg/Fe] abundances shown in Fig. 12 demonstrate two dis-
tinct stellar populations in NGC 1381: (1) the metal-rich, α-poor,
and old nuclear disc, bar, and thin disc; and (2) the metal-poor
and α-rich bulge, thick disc, and stellar halo, which are younger
than or comparable in age to the first group.
Based on the metallicity and [Mg/Fe] abundance, the nuclear
disc, bar, and thin disc are consistent with an in situ origin. In
contrast, the distinct stellar populations in the bulge, thick disc,
and stellar halo may have formed either in situ at an early epoch
through a rapid process—similar to the thick disc formation
scenario proposed for the Milky Way (e.g. Lehnert et al. 2014;
Helmi et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2021)—or have been accreted later
via minor mergers. However, unlike in the Milky Way, the thin
disc and bar in NGC 1381 are older than or comparable in age
to the bulge, thick disc, and halo. This age relationship does not
support a purely in situ origin for the bulge, thick disc, and halo
at an early epoch.
We therefore suggest that a non-negligible fraction of the
stars in the bulge, thick disc, and halo of NGC 1381 were ac-
creted through minor mergers that occurred after the formation
of the thin disc and bar. Their low metallicity and enhanced α-
element abundances are consistent with an origin in lower-mass
systems like dwarf galaxies. Moreover, the fact that these ac-
cretion events did not disrupt the pre-existing thin disc and bar
further supports their interpretation as minor mergers.
In Fig. 13, only the bulge shows a negative metallicity gradi-
ent (∇[Z/H]bulge < 0 for all data versions). The metallicity gradi-
ents of other components, as well as all [Mg/Fe] abundance gra-
dients, are not significant. These results indicate that, apart from
the bulge, stars in each component share similar stellar popula-
tions, suggesting similar physical origins. In contrast, the bulge’s
negative gradient points to a more complex origin, which can ei-
ther arise from the main galaxy or from the accreted dwarf galax-
ies. Due to this negative gradient and the uncertainties in stellar
age, we cannot fully rule out the possibility of a primordial bulge
existing before bar formation.
Our findings are consistent with Pinna et al. (2019a), who
defined the thick disc of NGC 1381 directly from the projected
2D image and attributed its origin to the accretion of ex situ stars.
Compared to their results, our study performs a more physically
motivated 3D decomposition, successfully distinguishing struc-
tures with different origins, particularly the bar, bulge, and nu-
clear disc. We further reveal that ex situ stars are present not
only in the outer region (thick disc and stellar halo) but also in
the inner region (bulge) of the galaxy.
Based on the analysis in this subsection and Sect. 5.1, we
propose the following assembly history for NGC 1381: The
galaxy likely formed its bar from a rotation-dominated disc ap-
proximately 13 Gyr ago. Then, the nuclear disc formed from gas
inflow driven by the bar. The bar was fast initially but gradually
slowed down due to dynamical friction with dark matter. The
galaxy experienced a series of minor mergers, which built up its
thick disc and stellar halo. These mergers also led to the forma-
tion (or growth) of the dynamically hot bulge. After the galaxy
fell into the Fornax cluster 8–12 Gyr ago, the gas supply in the
outer regions of the galaxy was cut off (strangulation), resulting
in the galaxy being finally quenched but with its internal struc-
tures well preserved. This environmental quenching might have
further enhanced the bar’s slowdown by halting the supply of
angular momentum, leading to a slow bar with R > 2 at present.

servations of high-redshift galaxies. Such a comparative analysis
will advance our understanding of the evolution of barred galax-
ies from high redshift to the present day.

each component. The standard deviations of the resulting age distributions from these perturbations are taken as ∆tage. Assuming
the uncertainties contributed by kinematics and age maps are independent, the overall 1σ uncertainties for stellar ages (∆tall) were

In our dynamical modelling, the gravitational potential comprises contributions from stellar mass, dark matter, and a super-
massive black hole. This leads to a degeneracy between these three components, especially in the innermost regions (≲5 arcsec).
Martín-Navarro et al. (2021) reported that the IMF becomes more bottom-heavy toward the innermost regions, leading to a higher
M⋆/L there. Therefore, assuming a constant M⋆/L likely results in an underestimation of the stellar mass in the central regions.
However, this bias could be masked by models that adopt more cuspy dark matter halo and/or a more massive black hole.
The enclosed stellar, dark matter, and total mass profiles are shown in Fig. C.2. We compared our total mass profile with the
result from Ding et al. (2023), who constructed orbit-superposition models without considering the bar. Regardless of whether the
bar is included in the models, the total mass profiles, which are directly constrained by the kinematic data, are consistent.
