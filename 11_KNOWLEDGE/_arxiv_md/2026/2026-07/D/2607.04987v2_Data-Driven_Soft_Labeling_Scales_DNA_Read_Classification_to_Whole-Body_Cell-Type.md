Abstract
Cell-type deconvolution, the task of estimating the proportions of constituent cell
types in a heterogeneous biological sample, is a core problem in computational
biology. Methods that rely on epigenetic marks such as DNA methylation typically
operate on aggregated methylation estimates, discarding the pattern-level informa-
tion carried by individual DNA reads. Existing read-level approaches that exploit
this information are scarce, and all remain restricted to few-class settings; scaling
them further is an open problem because, at scale, non-discriminative reads domi-
nate and hard labels conflict with the many-to-many mapping between methylation
patterns and cell types, preventing classifier convergence. To overcome this, we
propose data-driven soft labels that estimate the conditional cell-type distribution
for each read, and integrate this scheme into Syto, a new modular framework for
read-level classification-based deconvolution. On a whole-body atlas of 39 human
cell types, Syto reduces MSE by 2.56× over SoTA, with gains transferring to
an out-of-distribution dataset spanning 16 tissues. Syto lays the foundation for
modeling increasingly large cell-type panels, with improved applications in biology
and healthcare. The proposed soft-labeling scheme is further translatable to any
setting with a many-to-many signal-to-label mapping.

proportion and its specific cellular profile, the narrower problems of tumor classification and purity

Existing methylome-based deconvolution methods typically operate on aggregated methylation mea-
surements (β [9], α-values [10], absolute counts of methylated CpGs [11]) or fragment-level counts
[12, 13, 14, 15], omitting the differences in methylation pattern at the level of the individual DNA
fragments (or reads) that are known to be important for tumorigenesis [16]. Conversely, incorporating
local omics signatures (potentially containing more than methylation) and the broader nucleotide
context may offer a more accurate representation of cell-of-origin and may improve detection capacity
[17]. Despite this potential, only three end-to-end classification-based deconvolution methods exist
[18, 19, 20] and only two of them preserve the within-read methylation pattern[19, 20]. These meth-
ods are either unfit to treat multi-class problems [18, 19], or wrongly apply the Bayesian framework
to uncalibrated outputs of neural networks, leading to highly biased deconvolution results [19, 20].
Furthermore, none of these methods has been demonstrated to work across dozens of different cell
types [13].
To address those limitations, we introduce Syto1: a modular framework for read-level pattern-sensitive
classification-based cell-type deconvolution that scales to dozens of cell types. Our main contributions
are as follows:
• We propose several labeling schemes to enable multi-class classification of omics-enriched DNA
reads with a large number of classes. Among these, we introduce a biologically inspired, data-
driven soft-labeling scheme – the maximum-likelihood estimator of the true conditional distribution.
This approach has potential applications in scenarios where the mapping from signal to class labels
is many-to-many rather than many-to-one.
• We propose a linear calibration method for regression models with probability simplex outputs: a
simple hyperparameter-free post-hoc correction that outperforms existing calibration baselines in
our setting.
• We propose a modular framework with a decoupled classifier, deconvolver and calibrator, allowing
independent improvement of each component. We extend three existing read-level pattern-sensitive
classifiers to operate within it and provide five deconvolvers.
• We conduct large-scale experiments on a 39 cell-type reference atlas [13] and on an out-of-
distribution dataset of 16 tissues [10]. Syto consistently improves deconvolution accuracy over the
SoTA.

Related Work
Read-level pattern-sensitive classification-based deconvolution methods.
CancerDetector [18]
is a tumor detection and fraction estimation method that models the joint methylation states of
CpG 2 sites in DNA reads using a Beta-Bernoulli distribution. For every cell-type specific genomic
region, the model learns two separate Beta distributions for tumoral and normal classes; these priors
are then used to estimate the tumor-derived cfDNA fraction via maximum likelihood. Dismir [19]
is another tumor fraction estimation method; it uses a CNN–LSTM architecture with both DNA
sequence and methylation state as input to assign each read a d-score representing the probability
that it originates from a tumor. The sample-level tumor fraction is then obtained by maximizing the
posterior probability over all d-scores. MethylBERT [20] is an adaptation of the BERT architecture
[21] to read-level classification. The model is first pre-trained on 3-mer tokenized reference genome
sequences, then fine-tuned by adding methylation embeddings encoding the CpG methylation state at
each position and by training a read classifier. Tumor purity is also obtained via maximum likelihood
estimation. MethylBERT is the only existing read-level pattern-sensitive classification-based model
that has demonstrated deconvolution with up to five cell types, using focal loss [22] to mitigate class
imbalance.
Label distribution learning and soft labeling.
Geng [23] has introduced label distribution learning
as a computational task of estimating the degree to which each label describes the instance. Xu
et al. [24] coined the term label enhancement (LE) for the task of constructing a label distribution
(or soft labels) from one-hot encoded labels (or hard labels). They proposed Graph Laplacian
Label Enhancement (GLLE) to learn the label distribution from hard labels by propagating label

Methods
3.1
Syto: a modular framework
Existing methods for read-level classification-based cell-type deconvolution follow three steps:
selection of informative genomic regions for training and inference, classification of individual reads
and estimation of target cell-type proportions. This common structure offers an opportunity for
harmonization. In this work, we propose Syto, a modular framework for read-level classification-
based deconvolution. Syto specifically subsumes the classification and proportions estimation steps;
it can be applied out of the box, regardless of the regions’ selection procedure.
Inference.
Syto operates on a sequencing library D := {(ri, gi, si)}1≤i≤N consisting of N DNA
sequences (or reads) ri obtained from a biological bulk sample. Each read ri belongs to a single
group of genomic regions (or GR group) gi ∈G := {1, . . . , G}. Differentially Methylated Regions
(DMRs) are a common choice for GR groups, and we use them in this work. Each read ri is also
assigned an omics signature si. In this work, we focus on methylation, and so the signature is the set
of pairs (position, methylation state) for each CpG site in the read. The methylation state is either 1
(methylated) or 0 (unmethylated). The omics signatures can be extended to contain information from
gene expression, histone marks, chromatin accessibility, etc. The first step is to classify all reads,
producing a vector of probability over the classes ui := (ui,1, . . . , ui,C)T ∈∆C−1 for every read
ri, where C ∈N is the number of cell types to deconvolve and ∆C−1 is the (C −1)-dimensional
probability simplex. The predictions are then aggregated into a feature matrix (or prediction matrix)
P ∈RG×C (cf. Section A.4.3 for further details):

Training.
Syto has three trainable parts: the read-level classifier, the deconvolver and the calibrator.
Each of them can be independently improved. The training dataset additionally contains, for each
read ri, the class label ci ∈C, which is the cell type the read originates from. To train the classifier,
we assign a label vector to each read. As we explain in Section 3.2, standard one-hot encoded
labels (or hard labels) are insufficient for model convergence. We propose several labeling methods,
namely hard labels with an additional background class, a smoothed version of these hard labels
(cf. Section 3.2.1) and a probabilistic labeling called data-driven soft-labeling (cf. Section 3.2.2). We
extend and modify the three existing read-level classifiers (CancerDetector, Dismir and MethylBERT)
and provide custom losses that enable convergence across different label types. Deconvolvers are
trained on a dataset of pseudobulks, i.e. pairs of (ground truth pseudobulk proportions, prediction
matrix) obtained by subsampling the original dataset with known proportions. The calibrators are
trained on the deconvolvers’ outputs, using the pseudobulk ground-truth proportions as targets. The

Hard labels with background.

3.2.2
Data-driven soft labeling
Biological rationale.
We argue that data-driven soft labels, constructed as probabilistic represen-
tations that capture the conditional distribution over cell types for each omics signature, are more
appropriate than hard labels for read-level cell type classification. The main reason is that the mapping
between omics signatures and cell types is not many-to-one, as in standard classification, but rather
many-to-many, because a single omics signature (and in particular a single methylation pattern) in a
given genomic region can be associated with multiple cell types. This is due, among several reasons,
to imperfect propagation and maintenance of DNA methylation patterns across cell divisions [30],
as well as high methylome variation across the cell cycle, with a sharp drop in average methylation
during the S phase[31]. Both issues are further compounded by technical noise: the purification of
cell types prior to sequencing is imperfect [13], and the techniques to infer the methylation status
of CpG sites are noisy [32]. This ambiguity in the mapping was previously resolved by selecting
highly discriminative regions that are hypomethylated in a specific cell type and methylated in all
others [33, 34, 13, 35]. However, as we consider an increasing number of cell types, it becomes
necessary to resort to less specific regions, where methylation patterns can only discriminate between
non-singleton cell-type groups [10]. In this case, assigning a different hard label to reads sharing the
same methylation pattern is not biologically meaningful and may impair the model’s ability to learn
the true conditional distribution of cell types.

3.3
Read-level classifiers
Extension of existing classifiers.
We adapted CancerDetector [18] to the multiclass setting by
extending the Beta-Bernoulli read likelihood model to all C cell types, with per-marker, per-class
parameter estimation by MLE (cf. Section A.2.3). We did a major refactoring of MethylBERT [20] to
harmonize it with the Hugging Face Transformers library [38] and enable faster fine-tuning. We also
substituted the original classification head with one with GR group attention (cf. Section A.2.4). The
original Dismir [19] implementation does not integrate GR group context, making it untrainable on
short reads with many classes, as the context cannot be inferred from the reads themselves. Therefore,
we also use the classification head with GR group attention for Dismir.
Lookup classifier as baseline.
To serve as a baseline, we introduced Lookup Classifier, a modifica-
tion of 1-NN. For an input read r with signature s, the predicted probability vector under soft labeling
corresponds to the soft label computed on the training data. Under hard labeling with background, the
prediction is instead the argmax over all reads sharing signature s, with ties resolved by averaging the
one-hot encoded labels. If s was never seen in the training data, for soft labels, the training signatures
at minimal Jaccard distance to s are aggregated, their normalized counts are pooled, and the resulting
soft label is returned. For hard labels, we return the average of the one-hot encoded labels of the
training signatures at minimal distance to s. If r belongs to no GR group in the training data, a
uniform distribution is returned for soft labels, and the background class is returned for hard labels.
3.4
Deconvolution models
All previous works relied in some way or another on maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) in order
to infer the mixture proportions from the classifiers’ outputs. However, preliminary experiments
showed that MLE methods applied on top of the classifier outputs fail in our large-scale setting
(cf. Section A.2.6). We therefore propose five distinct deconvolution models (or deconvolvers) to
learn the transfer function from the classifier output to the mixture proportions, which can all be
used in combination with every classifier. Three of those models are supervised machine learning
(ML) models, namely XGBoost [39] (referred to as XGB), and two neural networks: a multi-layer
perceptron with three hidden layers (referred to as MLP) and a multi-layer perceptron with one wider
hidden layer (referred to as shallow wide network or SWN), both implemented with PyTorch [40].
XGB is trained with RMSE loss, while the neural networks are trained with loss = MSE + 0.5KL.
The other two methods are the widely used Non-negative Least Squares or NNLS, and a variant
with sum-to-one constraint: Probability Simplex Least Squares or PSLS; both implemented with
cvxpy [41]. NNLS and PSLS are initialized on the C prediction matrices resulting from mixtures
with a single cell type. ML deconvolvers are fitted on the K prediction matrices obtained from
the aggregation of the classifier predictions. In each experiment, we have simulated K = 100,000
pseudobulk mixtures per split via the sampling procedure described in Section A.4.2. We describe
the models’ architectures and fitting parameters in detail in Section A.2.7.

Experiments
4.1
Experimental setups
Pseudobulk experiment.
We chose the largest available (by number of cell-types) DNA methy-
lation atlas of healthy adult human cell types for modeling [13, 43]. The dataset comprises 205
WGBS (Whole Genome Bisulfite Sequencing) libraries from 39 major somatic cell types, originating
from different donors, and is publicly available at GEO under accession number GSE186458 . We
preprocessed the data as described in Section A.3.1. We grouped the DMRs of Loyfer et al. [13] into
39 GR groups, each containing up to 25 DMRs specific to a single cell type. To fit the read-level
classifiers, we first filtered the dataset to keep only reads that overlapped with at least 4 CpGs in the
reference atlas. We split the data into training, validation and test sets following the procedure in
Section A.4.1, ensuring that every GR group has DNA reads in all splits while isolating biological
sources whenever possible. Resulting split ratios were 62:19:19. Of the 205 biological samples, 4

Limitations
As noted in [47, 48], the choice of genomic regions strongly affects deconvolution performance.
Most of our experiments use a relatively small set of regions (up to 25 per cell type), selected with
wgbstools [13]. A recent benchmark [49] included wgbstools, but did not draw firm conclusions on
its relative merit. In this work, we model from a single well-established WGBS atlas [13] and evaluate
OOD generalization on a single RRBS dataset [10], leaving open the question of how well data-driven
soft labeling transfers to other datasets and sequencing technologies. Among non-read-level methods,
only UXM was included for comparison, as it is recognized as one of the best-performing methods
on WGBS [49]. Although UXM is reported to lose accuracy on RRBS [49], analysis indicates that
this reduction is partially attributable to reference-selection issues (see Section A.3.2). A broader
benchmark that incorporates state-of-the-art methods from previous assessments ([50, 51, 47])
and extends target data to include Third Generation Sequencing platforms would provide a more
comprehensive evaluation of read-level classification approaches. The reported performance for
learnable classifiers (Dismir, MethylBERT) and deconvolvers (XGB, MLP, SWN) should be considered
a lower bound, as optimal hyperparameters were not pursued and the full prediction matrices could
not be utilized due to computational constraints. This study relies on pseudobulk mixtures, a
standard yet imperfect proxy, and uses TCS as an upper bound on deconvolution accuracy. Although
evaluation with samples containing known ground-truth proportions would be preferable, no such
publicly available alternative currently exists, and generating an independent reference would require
substantial effort.
6
Conclusion
This study extends existing read-level classifiers to support a broad range of cell types and introduces
learnable deconvolvers and post-hoc calibrators. Several labeling schemes are proposed to enable
convergence in large multi-class settings. Our data-driven soft-labeling approach outperforms all
other evaluated labeling strategies, including our generalization of hard labels with background,
achieving superior results in both pseudobulk and OOD scenarios, and surpassing the previous
SoTA UXM U25 by a substantial margin. The extension of the labeling-free CancerDetector ranks
second in performance, but does not offer the fine-tuning improvement potential observed in neural
classifiers. Applied to the OOD dataset, Syto achieves results comparable to UXM U250, despite
being trained on ten times fewer genomic regions and utilizing ten times less data during inference.
The promising results observed across 39 healthy cell types suggest the feasibility of progressively
expanding to larger and more complex reference panels. A primary extension involves incorporating
malignant cell types, enabling Syto to address tumor classification and purity estimation as specific
instances of deconvolution. Further extensions include integrating multi-omics signatures and using
third-generation long-read sequencing data, which would facilitate the capture of more complex
epigenetic patterns and enhance deconvolution accuracy.

A.1
Broader impact
Read-level classification-based deconvolution has been a niche area in the broader deconvolution
field, partly because earlier methods did not scale well as the number of cell types increased. Syto
enables the use of larger reference sets that include more normal cell types and, in future studies,
malignant cell types as well. Since tumor classification and estimating tumor purity or fraction can
be addressed through deconvolution, methods that handle many cell types could eventually take over
these specific tasks. They would also add read-level detail that aggregate-based methods cannot offer.
This could help us better understand local epigenetic factors and support clinical decision-making.
There are also some potential risks to consider. Deconvolution results based on a single reference atlas
might not apply equally well to all populations. If the proportions of rare cell types are inaccurate,
this could lead to misleading interpretations in clinical settings unless further validation is performed.
Methylation data can be identifying, so using methylation-based tools more widely brings up the
usual concerns about genomic privacy. Our work focuses on methodology. Using these methods in
clinical practice would need validation against actual proportions, which is beyond the scope of this
study.

A.2.3
Multiclass extension of CancerDetector
CancerDetector is a method by Li et al. [18] to estimate the proportion of reads originating from
tumor cells in a bulk sample of cell-free DNA (cfDNA).
CancerDetector for binary deconvolution.
In the original method, the training phase proceeds in
two steps. First, identifying methylation markers specific to the cancer under scrutiny by identifying
eligible markers — i.e. markers with sufficient coverage — and then keeping the markers whose
methylation levels can differentiate tumor samples from normal tissue samples and normal plasma
samples. Second, for each marker, for each class (tumoral or normal), the parameters of the beta
distribution of the α-values of the reads in that marker from the given class are computed by maximum
likelihood estimation (MLE).
In the deconvolution phase, for each read in the bulk sample, the likelihood of the read to belong to
each class is computed according to the beta distribution of the each class and the marker from which
the read originates. Finally, the proportion of each class in the mixture is computed by MLE.
Li et al. [18] also rely on the prior knowledge that they are deconvolving a mixture of normal and
tumoral reads where the tumoral reads are expected to be in low proportion. They apply a process
called “removal of ‘confounding’ markers” where they estimate the tumor proportion of each marker
and iteratively remove the markers whose tumor proportion is more than one standard deviation
above the mean tumor proportion, until convergence.

All deconvolution models were trained on the same feature-selected input matrices derived from

XGBoost (XGB).
A gradient-boosted tree ensemble was trained with 500 estimators, a maximum
depth of 15 and a learning rate of 0.05. Row subsampling was set to 0.7, with full-column sampling
per tree. Regularization was controlled via a minimum child weight of 5, L1 penalty α = 0.1 and L2
penalty λ = 1.0. Outputs were clipped and normalized to produce valid mixture proportions.
Single-layer Wide Network (SWN).
A single hidden layer of 1024 units with GELU activation and
20% dropout, followed by a softmax output layer. Training ran for up to 5000 epochs with a batch
size of 512, a learning rate of 5 × 10−5, weight decay of 10−3 and early stopping with a patience of
1000 epochs based on validation MAE. No learning rate scheduler was used.
Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
Two hidden layers of 512 and 256 units (each with GELU
activation and 20% dropout), followed by a projection back to the input dimensionality with 10%
dropout and a softmax output layer. Training used the same schedule as SWN: up to 5000 epochs,
batch size 512, learning rate 5 × 10−5, weight decay 10−3 and early stopping with patience 1000 on
validation MAE. No learning rate scheduler was used.

A.2.8

Calibration methods analysis.
In addition to the simple linear calibration scheme that we intro-

described in Section 3.5.
The parameters W and b from Equation (1) are optimized via Adam with cross-entropy loss between
the true and calibrated proportions.
The parameters are regularized with an ℓ2 penalty of strength λ in the case of temperature scaling and
vector scaling. For Dirichlet calibration, when µ is None, we apply ℓ2 regularization on all parameters

the off-diagonal parameters of W and with strength µ on b.

A.3
Data preparation for the experiments
A.3.1
Data preprocessing of .pat files
The raw data featured in our experiments is provided in ultra-compact *.pat format where reads with
the same covered CpG pattern are collapsed in single data record without aligned sequences [52].
Since the classification models expect a combination of read-level DNA sequence and methylation
pattern, we recovered this information as in the work by Jeong et al. [20]. As regards the cell-type atlas
[13], we downloaded all data in the aligned format using both reference assemblies, hg19 and hg38.
In the case of the tissue atlas [10], only the hg19 aligned format was available. For each alignment,
the reads were recovered from the genomic positions overlapping with the corresponding "top-25
unmethylated markers atlases" published by Loyfer et al. [13] (namely Atlas.U25.l4.hg19.full.tsv
and Atlas.U25.l4.hg38.full.tsv6). We initially trained MethylBERT on both sets and observed no
significant differences in the attained losses. Based on this observation and the fact that hg38 had
more overlaping reads, we disregarded hg19, and our training effort was done on hg38. For inference,
we used Atlas.U25.l4.hg38.full.tsv in the pseudobulk experiment and Atlas.U25.l4.hg19.full.tsv in the
OOD experiment; mixing assemblies was possible for Dismir and MethylBERT, because we united
DMRs into cell-type groups, and for those classifiers training and infering on different alignments
was possible. Lookup Classifier is not alignment agnostic, because constructed lookup keys are
position specific, hence, for this classifier only we used hg38 aligned reads for training in pseudobulk
experiment and hg19 aligned reads for training in ODD experiment.

A.4
Details of experimental setups
A.4.1
Split construction
To construct the training, validation and test splits, we follow a procedure that ensures every target
cell type is represented in all splits while isolating biological sources as much as possible. At the
beginning of the procedure, we aim for a 70:15:15 ratio between the splits. Target cell types do not
have an equal number of biological samples covering them. Therefore, we establish the following
rules, which we follow in the stated order, and in such a way that rule j is only applied in a way that
does not contradict all rules i < j:
1. If a target cell type is covered by a single sample, then the data from this sample is divided
across all three splits.
2. If a target cell type is covered by exactly two samples, one is allocated to train, and the other
is split between validation and test sets.
3. If a target cell type is covered by three or more biological samples, those samples must
not be individually broken down into separate splits, and the validation and test splits must
receive at least one full sample.
Applying the described procedure results in the final train/val/test ratios 62:19:19 with four samples
shared across all three splits (GEO GSM5652204, GSM5652218,GSM5652321, GSM5652358) and
two samples shared across validation and test split (GEO GSM5652199, GSM5652205) from a total
of 205 samples.
A.4.2
Pseudobulk generation procedure
Algorithm 2 describes the sampling procedure used to generate the pseudobulk mixtures employed
in this paper. The key parameter is the total size N of the pseudobulk, i.e. the number of reads
to subsample per split. Across all splits, the number of reads per originating cell type ranged
from 2,786 to 384,944, with a mean of 48,096. We used N = 475,000 across all experiments,
resulting in 475,000/39 ≈12,179 reads per GR group, per pseudobulk, per split. This value was
set heuristically to balance three competing objectives: sufficient representation of each cell type
in the resulting mixtures (for which a higher sample count is beneficial); useful sampling noise to
improve generalization of the trained model (for which sample counts that do not match the original
cell-type contributions are beneficial); and computational efficiency (for which a lower sample count
is beneficial). The generated pseudobulks are aggregated using Algorithm 3.
A.4.3
Predictions aggregation
Before deconvolution, we aggregate the model’s per-read predictions into a compact matrix, where
each row corresponds to a GR group and each column to a candidate cell type of origin. This matrix
summarises the cell-of-origin information (or prediction information) that the model extracted from
the mixture at the read level.
We aggregate using a weighted average, where each read’s weight is proportional to the number of
CpGs it covers. The intuition is that reads carrying more CpGs convey more evidence about their
cell of origin and are statistically more reliable. For long reads with many CpGs, this weighting is
essentially a no-op, but in our setting, most reads cover only a handful of CpGs (around four), so
down-weighting the shortest reads has a easurable effect.
A second issue arises at inference time. During training, every GR group is well represented, and
aggregation is straightforward. In real samples, however, it is common for no read to map to a
particular GR group, leaving an entire row of the matrix undefined. In the ODD dataset analyzed
in this work, 252 of 260 samples contain at least one GR group with zero reads, and 258 of 260
contain at least four GR groups with fewer than 30 reads in total. This is because the RRBS protocol
produces less output than the WGBS data used for training, and coverage gaps are likely due to our
employed atlas having only 25 DMRs per GR group (with each of our GR groups being specific to a
single cell type).

A.6
Experiments compute resources
The majority of experiments were conducted on a single workstation and are fully reproducible on
equivalent hardware. To accelerate batch jobs, pseudobulk generation and deconvolver fitting were
additionally offloaded to a high-performance computing cluster; however, the primary workstation is
sufficient to run the entire pipeline end-to-end.
Primary workstation.
An HP Z2 Tower G9 equipped with an Intel Core i9-12900K (16 cores, 24
threads, up to 5.2 GHz), 64 GB DDR5 RAM, an NVIDIA RTX A4500 GPU (20 GB VRAM) and
a 2 TB Samsung NVMe SSD, running Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. This machine was used for all stages of
the pipeline: data preparation (restoring reads from .pat files and constructing train/validation/test
splits), training and inference of read-level classifiers (Dismir, MethylBERT, Lookup Classifier),
pseudobulk mixture generation, deconvolver fitting and inference, and linear calibrator fitting and
inference.
Secondary workstation.
A Dell Precision 7540 laptop equipped with an Intel Core i7-9850H (6
cores, 12 threads, up to 4.6 GHz), 32 GB DDR4 RAM, an NVIDIA Quadro T1000 Mobile GPU
(4 GB VRAM) and a 512 GB Western Digital NVMe SSD, running Linux Mint 22.3 (Ubuntu 24.04
base). This machine was used in parallel to fit and evaluate the CancerDetector and Lookup Classifier
baselines, along with their corresponding calibrators.
HPC cluster.
Batch jobs for pseudobulk generation and deconvolver fitting were offloaded to an
HPC cluster.
All details about the compute resources and wall-clock time for each stage of the pipeline are provided
in the supplementary spreadsheet named ‘Compute Resources.ods‘.
