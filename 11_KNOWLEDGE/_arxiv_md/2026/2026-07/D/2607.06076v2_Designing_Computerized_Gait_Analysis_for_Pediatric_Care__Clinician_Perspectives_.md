Abstract
Computerized gait analysis (CGA) serves as an essential diagnostic
tool for evaluating neuromuscular, musculoskeletal, and neurologi-
cal disorders in children, from cerebral palsy to muscular dystrophy.
By enabling objective and comprehensive gait analysis, CGA sup-
ports timely clinical interventions that can significantly improve
pediatric mobility outcomes and quality of life. Yet pediatric gait
analysis introduces unique design considerations often underex-
plored in existing CGA research, as children’s ongoing development
shapes assessment requirements. To understand how CGA tech-
nologies can be designed for pediatric care, we conducted a quali-
tative study with 12 pediatric clinicians and one system designer
who routinely work with CGA. Participants identified child-specific
challenges including managing heightened sensory sensitivities
to wearable devices, accommodating body proportions in sensor
placement and calibration, and maintaining patient engagement
during data collection. Clinicians also articulated needs for work-
flow adaptations and expressed interest in extending gait analysis
beyond controlled laboratory settings into naturalistic environ-
ments such as playgrounds and schools, where children’s authentic
movement patterns emerge. Drawing from these clinician perspec-
tives, we present design recommendations for pediatric-centered
CGA that address sensing modalities suitable for sensory-sensitive
children and approaches for capturing gait data across diverse care
environments. Our findings contribute to the broader challenge of
adapting clinical technologies to meet the distinct needs of pediatric
populations.

Introduction
Gait analysis is a cornerstone of clinical evaluation across neuro-
logical, musculoskeletal, and cardiovascular medicine, playing a
critical role in managing conditions ranging from cognitive disor-
ders to pediatric neuromuscular and neurological conditions such
as muscular dystrophy and cerebral palsy [3, 13, 14, 23, 31, 37]. A

person’s gait reflects underlying neuromuscular function, joint me-
chanics, and disease progression, providing clinicians with valuable
diagnostic and prognostic insights.
Historically, gait analysis relied on visual observation by trained
clinicians [22, 39]. While instrumented approaches using sensors of-
fered more comprehensive data capture [34], modern computerized
gait analysis (CGA)—the use of computer-based technologies to
collect, process, analyze, and visualize gait data—has dramatically
expanded clinical capabilities. CGA utilizes vision-based systems,
ambient sensors, and wearable devices to enable three-dimensional
measurements, objective neuromuscular profiling, and longitudinal
tracking that exceed what visual observation alone can provide[31].
These computerized methods are particularly crucial for pediatric
patients, where comprehensive gait analysis and timely interven-
tion can substantially improve long-term mobility and quality of
life[15, 34].
Despite its clinical value, significant barriers hinder the inte-
gration of CGA into routine clinical workflows. Clinicians face
challenges related to the complexity of CGA systems, including
lengthy setup times, specialized technical expertise requirements,
and difficulties interpreting large volumes of data [20]. These barri-
ers are further compounded when working with pediatric patients
and their families, who may have difficulty with engagement and
compliance [20]. While prior research has documented general bar-
riers to CGA adoption, little is known about the specific invisible
barriers that emerge when conducting gait analysis with pediatric
populations—barriers that may be overlooked in technology design.
To address this gap, we conducted a qualitative interview study
supplemented by a structured survey with 13 experts who reg-
ularly engage with computerized gait technologies in pediatric
settings: 12 pediatric clinicians and one system designer. The pedi-
atric clinicians in our study provide healthcare to infants, children,
adolescents, and young adults across various neuromuscular and
neurological conditions. The system designer contributed expertise
in developing CGA technologies across multiple use contexts. We
categorize clinicians into two groups based on their roles in the gait
analysis process: medical experts (𝑛=7), who typically lead the anal-
ysis by coordinating technical procedures and facilitating patient
interactions, and physicians (𝑛=5), who interpret the resulting data
to inform patients and their families about diagnoses and treatment
plans.

With this work, we make the following contributions:
• Child-specific mismatches and clinician workarounds be-
tween current sensing systems and pediatric care, including
marker placement on small or variable bodies, sensory bur-
den, and the hidden coordination work required to make
analysis possible.
• HCI design opportunities for pediatric CGA, including adap-
tive calibration for small and variable bodies, child-centered
sensing configurations that treat contact reduction as a data
validity strategy rather than merely a comfort affordance,
family-mediated pre-visit preparation tools, and environment-

• Broader contributions for designing pediatric healthcare

required to make measurement possible reveals displaced in-
teraction design burdens that belong in system architecture
rather than in clinical workarounds.

Background and Related Work
In this section, we provide background on computerized gait analy-
sis (CGA) technologies and how they are used in clinical practice.
We also review related HCI research on clinical sensing and pedi-
atric health technologies.
2.1
Computerized Gait Analysis Technologies
CGA technologies increasingly support objective measurement of
physiological (e.g., heart rate) [30] and biomechanical (e.g., body
movement patterns) [30, 38] functions in healthcare. Within move-
ment science and rehabilitation, CGA technologies provide a sys-
tematic framework for analyzing locomotor (e.g., mobile and ki-
netic) biomechanics. These technologies include optical motion-
capture cameras, force plates, electromyography, and inertial sen-
sors that measure various spatial (e.g., stride length), temporal (e.g.,
gait speed), kinematic (e.g., step velocity), kinetic (e.g., ground re-
action forces), and muscle activity parameters [6, 29].
Clinical gait analyses are typically conducted in specialized gait
and motion analysis laboratories by multidisciplinary teams in-
cluding therapists, surgeons, and biomechanists [9]. Marker-based
optical motion capture combined with force plates remains the
laboratory reference standard for gait analysis, providing high-
resolution kinematic and kinetic data used to diagnose movement
pathologies and inform surgical or therapeutic planning [22, 34].
At the same time, wearable and portable sensing technologies are
expanding the contexts in which gait can be measured [31]. Inertial
measurement units (IMUs), pressure-sensing insoles, and optical
sensing systems have been applied to monitor gait in neurological
populations and support rehabilitation decision-making outside
specialized laboratories [18]. These advances enable more compre-
hensive objective insights into gait, which are particularly valuable
for informing specialized care in patients with complex or advanced
clinical needs. However, despite these advances, prior work has
found challenges in utilizing gait analysis technologies in practice.
2.2
Challenges in Utilizing Clinical Analysis
Technologies in Clinical Practice
Recent work has explored how gait analysis technologies may im-
prove the accessibility of movement analysis beyond specialized
laboratories. For example, OpenCap, a smartphone-based mark-
erless motion capture system, demonstrates that musculoskeletal
dynamics can be estimated at a fraction of the cost and time of
traditional laboratory approaches, highlighting the potential for
vision-based tools to expand access to movement analysis in clini-
cal and community settings [36]. However, successful adoption of
clinical technologies depends not only on technical capability but
also on usability and integration within clinical workflows. Studies
of healthcare technology adoption consistently show that perceived
usefulness and ease of use strongly influence whether clinicians
incorporate new tools into practice, while technical complexity,
unfamiliar interfaces, and lack of training often limit uptake [17].
Clinicians are also more receptive to technologies that address
problems encountered in daily practice; allied health professionals
have expressed skepticism toward systems perceived as commer-
cially rather than clinically driven [21]. Regarding CGA technolo-
gies specifically, utilization is hindered by high costs, specialized
training needs, and integration challenges [22]. Interviews with
clinicians highlight barriers such as limited technical skills, insuf-
ficient training, and lack of institutional support [20, 33]. While
clinicians recognize the potential of gait analysis to inform treat-
ment, its translation into routine workflows remains constrained.
Much of the work has emphasized technical development and insti-
tutional implementation, with comparatively little attention to how
users interact with these technologies, how they influence patient
experiences, and the role that human-centered design can play.
2.3
HCI Perspectives on Clinical Analysis
Technologies
Parallel work in the HCI community has examined how clinicians
interact with sensing technologies in general. For example, studies
show that even when sensing systems function reliably, the design
of data visualizations strongly influences whether clinicians can
extract actionable insights from sensor-derived data [31]. This dy-
namic has been documented across clinical AI and decision support
systems, where clinicians selectively integrate automated recom-
mendations based on contextual factors such as environmental con-
ditions, team dynamics, and institutional workflows [5, 35]. HCI
research has further shown that environmental conditions, team
dynamics, and institutional workflows shape how clinical technolo-
gies perform in practice [5], underscoring the need to study these
systems as sociotechnical assemblages rather than isolated tech-
nical artifacts. Clinicians are not passive recipients of automated

outputs; they actively interpret, contextualize, and sometimes resist
sensor-generated data depending on whether it aligns with their
professional judgment and workflow demands [27]. In movement
and rehabilitation contexts, HCI research with stroke rehabilitation
specialists has found that clinicians often require contextual and
experiential information, such as patient effort, fatigue, or compen-
satory strategies, that current sensing systems do not capture [1].
This finding highlights a persistent mismatch between what sensing
technologies measure and what clinicians need to make informed
clinical decisions. Work on wearable sensor systems for physical
therapy has similarly shown that clinician adoption depends not
only on accuracy but also on ergonomic design, interpretability of
outputs, and alignment with existing workflows [24]. Rehabilita-
tion specialists and biomechanists have identified data complexity,
limited normative reference data, and poor workflow integration
as persistent barriers to adopting quantitative gait sensing in prac-
tice [20, 28].
HCI researchers have also examined how automation reshapes
clinical roles and expertise. Rather than straightforwardly reduc-
ing burden, automated clinical systems introduce new forms of
interpretive work: clinicians must evaluate, calibrate, and selec-
tively trust algorithmic outputs in ways that draw on professional
judgment that systems cannot replicate [35]. This raises design
questions about how automated systems should surface outputs to
preserve rather than displace clinician expertise, questions directly
relevant to the emerging automation of gait data processing.
HCI researchers have also begun exploring the design of health-
care technologies for pediatric populations. Studies examining care-
givers of children with complex health conditions highlight chal-
lenges related to fragmented records, communication burdens, and
coordination across care teams that existing digital health tools of-
ten fail to support [32]. A recent systematic review of collaborative
health-tracking technologies for children and parents further em-
phasizes that effective pediatric health technologies must balance
children’s independence in self-management with active involve-
ment from caregivers and clinicians [11]. Pediatric health contexts
introduce distinct sociotechnical complexity: technology must ac-
count for developmental variability, family-mediated care relation-
ships, and sensory and cognitive differences that adult-oriented
systems are not designed to accommodate. Despite growing atten-
tion to clinical sensing technologies and pediatric health systems
within HCI, little research has examined clinician perspectives on
CGA technologies specifically within pediatric care contexts. As a
result, it remains unclear how existing gait analysis systems align
with the practical and interactional demands of working with child
patients, and what design opportunities, guidelines, and implica-
tions are needed to better support pediatric contexts. Our study
addresses this gap by examining pediatric clinicians’ experiences
using computerized gait technologies and identifying opportunities
for designing systems that better support clinical practice with this
population.

3.1
Participants
Participants were recruited from a university hospital gait labo-
ratory beginning in May 2025, following ethics approval. Recruit-
ment was conducted on a rolling basis via email invitations sent to
all eligible clinicians affiliated with the gait lab, supplemented by
word-of-mouth recruitment within this professional network. To
be eligible, clinicians were required to have (1) experience conduct-
ing gait analyses using computerized technologies and (2) direct
interactions with child patients. We included both medical experts
and physicians to capture complementary perspectives: medical
experts because of their specialized expertise in gait assessment,
rehabilitation planning, data collection, and biomechanical data
interpretation; and physicians because of their broader clinical
experience in diagnosis, treatment planning, and integrating gait
analysis findings into patient care. We also included the perspective
of a gait technology designer as a domain expert. Demographic and
background information about participants is provided in Table 1.
3.2
Procedure
Between June and October 2025, we conducted 13 one-on-one ses-
sions with seven medical experts, five physicians, and one designer.
At the beginning of the session, participants were introduced to the
research objective and completed an online consent form, which
included agreement to audio and video recording. Participants were
then presented with a scenario to contextualize the questions within
a pediatric context:
A pediatric patient is currently undergoing gait anal-
yses to guide intervention for cerebral palsy. As part
of this process, the attending clinician (medical expert
or physician) will choose a configuration of sensors to
perform the gait analysis. However, to ensure that the
process is robust, sensitive, efficient, accurate, and com-
fortable in the context of analyzing pediatric patients’

Participants then completed a quantitative survey, followed by a

surveys and interview questions were designed by the research
team. All sessions were conducted via Zoom and lasted approxi-

3.2.1
Complementary surveys. We conducted two complemen-
tary surveys to capture clinician perspectives on commonly used
and emerging CGA technologies. Each survey was tailored to the ex-
pertise of the two clinician groups (medical experts and physicians).
Medical experts often interact with the technical and biomechanical
aspects of gait analyses, so their survey focused on evaluating the
performance of the technologies and their applicability in various
contexts. Physicians evaluate the results of gait analyses and use
them to inform treatment planning. Therefore, their survey focused
on evaluating the importance of measuring various gait parameters
for decision-making. Both surveys also asked participants to priori-
tize performance criteria when selecting gait technologies. These
insights provided a foundation for understanding the perceived
strengths and applications of CGA technologies.
Medical expert survey. The survey for medical experts (𝑛= 7)
evaluated computerized gait technologies with an emphasis on sen-
sor functionality, accuracy, and clinical applicability. The survey
was structured into four main sections. In the first section, partici-
pants ranked the overall importance of seven evaluation criteria,
including functionality, accuracy, ease of use, comfort, affordability,
accessibility, and scalability, using a seven-point Likert scale (1 = not
important, 7 = extremely important) [16]. The second section cap-
tured perceptions and prior experience with various technologies.
Participants evaluated the technologies shown in Figure 1.
They rated each technology’s functionality for measuring five
gait parameters (temporal parameters, spatial parameters, joint
angles/kinematics, ground reaction forces/kinetics, and muscle ac-
tivation status), perceived accuracy for each parameter, ease of use,
patient comfort, affordability, availability, and scalability. Third,
participants evaluated each technology’s suitability for a special-
ized gait lab, local care center, and a patient’s home. The fourth
and final section invited open-text comments to provide additional
feedback on computerized gait technologies.
Physician survey. The physician survey focused on the clinical
decision-making value of measuring gait parameters. Physicians
evaluated temporal and spatial parameters, joint angles/kinematics,
ground reaction forces/kinetics, and muscle activation status. In the
first section, participants rated the importance of each parameter
as low, moderate, or high for decision-making. The second section
addressed the importance of these parameters across the three
clinical contexts—specialized gait lab, local care center, and patient
home—using the same three-level scale. The survey concluded with
an open-text section allowing physicians to provide qualitative
feedback regarding gait analysis parameters. For both surveys, all
questions also had an option for participants to select if they were
not familiar with the technology or parameter in question.
3.2.2
Semi-structured interviews. All participants took part
in a semi-structured interview lasting approximately 30 minutes.
The interviews supplemented the survey data by providing nu-
anced qualitative insights. The interviews began with a short self-
introduction, where participants described their role, background,
and workflow in child gait analysis. The second portion focused on
experiences with CGA technologies, including comparisons with
traditional manual methods, advantages and disadvantages, and
perceived impacts on clinical practice. The final portion of the in-
terview was the ideation session, where participants were asked
to envision design needs, discussing potential improvements to
analysis processes, clinical environments, and technology design,
as well as the roles of clinicians, children, and families in shaping
these systems.
3.3
Data Analysis
3.3.1
Quantitative data analysis. Survey responses were ana-
lyzed using descriptive statistics (mean and standard deviation) to
identify patterns in participants’ perceptions of CGA technologies
(RQ1) and how they prioritized performance and gait parameters.
Given the relatively small sample size, the analysis was intended
to highlight observable trends and areas of consensus rather than
produce generalizable inferences. The primary objective was to
capture expert perspectives to reveal both points of agreement and
divergence across participants.
To evaluate judgments on the scope of different technologies,
participants indicated whether each method could capture five
gait parameters: temporal parameters, spatial parameters, joint
angles/kinematics, ground reaction forces/kinetics, and muscle ac-
tivation status. These binary (yes/no) questions were summarized
using the mode response to reflect the most common participant
view. Responses regarding the likelihood of adopting each technol-
ogy for use in three contexts—gait laboratory, local care center, and
patient home were summarized using the top two approaches with
the most “definitely use” responses to show which methods were
viewed as most applicable in each context, highlighting if the set-
ting influenced adoption preferences. The top two approaches were
highlighted to reflect the greatest consensus and indicate those
most likely to be adopted in practice.
3.3.2
Qualitative data analysis. All interviews were video- and
audio-recorded with participants’ consent obtained via digital forms.
One researcher transcribed the recordings. Two researchers an-
alyzed the data using a thematic approach [8]. Initially, two re-
searchers independently took notes of the data to develop a pre-
liminary codebook. They then discussed individual codes, resolved
disagreements, and finalized a shared codebook, with interrater
reliability (IRR) exceeding 90%. Using this final codebook, one re-
searcher coded the remainder of the data, while an additional re-
searcher conducted a validity and reliability check on approximately
30% of the data (IRR >90%). The finalized codebook included themes
on perceptions of and experiences using computerized gait tech-
nologies with child patients, including challenges, benefits, and
barriers to implementation (RQ1) and participants’ envisioned de-
sign needs (RQ2). The most significant themes were then presented
as the findings and supported with quotes from participants.

3.4
Ethical Considerations
We recognize the sensitivity of conducting research with human
participants and took comprehensive precautions throughout the
study. The research protocol received institutional review board ap-
proval, ensuring full compliance with ethical standards. Before data
collection, all participants were informed about the study’s purpose,
procedures, and their rights, including the option to withdraw at
any stage without consequence. Informed consent was obtained
from participants digitally. Given the clinical context, particular
care was taken to safeguard any information relating to children.
Study sessions conducted via Zoom were recorded solely for re-
search purposes. To protect participant privacy, all audio and video
recordings were stored on an encrypted drive accessible only to
the research team.
4
Findings
The findings are organized into two parts. First, we present quanti-
tative survey results, detailing participants’ perceptions of 14 CGA
technologies (RQ1). Second, we present qualitative findings on par-
ticipants’ experiences using these technologies with child patients
(RQ1) and their design needs for future systems (RQ2). Across the
qualitative data, three patterns were especially important for design:
clinicians value CGA because it produces precise and standardized
evidence; pediatric care experience reveals mismatches between
sensing systems and children’s embodied variability; and future
systems need to reduce burden while supporting ecologically valid,
privacy-conscious, and clinically interpretable analysis.
4.1
RQ1: Perception of CGA technologies
This section explores clinicians’ perceptions of computerized gait
technologies. We report their ranking of technology performance
criteria and gait parameters, followed by medical experts’ analysis
of CGA technologies.

technologies
We present experiences utilizing CGA technologies from medical

4.2.1
Computerized gait technologies enable more standard-
ized and data-rich clinical decision-making.
Medical experts. Medical experts and the designer described
how computerized technologies have reshaped their interaction
with the gait analysis process. A central shift they highlighted is the
ability to “extract a lot more information than a lot of physicians were
traditionally used to having” (P13-Des). Similarly, P2-Exp noted,
“A lot has changed in the last five years... now we are able to get
pretty good [information] in the sagittal plane.” Reflecting on this
shift, P1-Exp emphasized how access to more comprehensive data
supports clinical decision-making: “Oftentimes the decisions that
are coming out of these gait analysis appointments are really life-
changing decisions, so to have a little bit more data, a little bit more
information to make those decisions is really important.”
Beyond enabling more detailed analysis, participants also de-
scribed these objective measures as critical for communicating
clinical value across stakeholders. P3-Exp explained that “quantifi-
able measures” help communicate progress not only to physicians
and families, but also to payers, thereby strengthening the case for
insurance coverage of interventions. In a similar vein, P5-Exp noted
that computerized methods support structured before-and-after
comparisons and provide a more scalable basis for evaluation, even
as visual interpretations remain important for clinical communi-
cation. P2-Exp further highlighted the shift introduced by these
systems, noting that computerized methods produce “objective”
data compared to traditional visual analysis, which can vary across
clinicians—thereby standardizing what was previously subjective
clinical judgment.

in clinical interpretation. P9 also stated, “I hope that there is slightly

Marker placement and embodied variability. As medical
experts primarily navigate conducting the gait analysis, they high-

configuration burdens. These issues expose a mismatch between
system design assumptions and the variability of real-world clinical
bodies. For example, P1-Exp reflected on anatomical differences in
children and their impact on placement and measurement accuracy:
“Sensor placement sometimes is difficult on a really small body or
individuals who have bony abnormalities2. She further stated, “There
are rotational deformities in bones, or different, harder-to-find bony
landmarks [which] can be tough. Maybe [for] patients who are a little
bit more overweight, it’s harder to find some of those bony landmarks.
So I think that can impact the accuracy.” P12-Phy, a physician and
director of the gait lab, also supported this perspective:
“Kids who have a lot of abnormality in their movement
or their bone structure can knock off these markers. Cur-
rent, state-of-the-art digital motion capture requires
markers, light reflective markers placed on bony land-
marks. And, you know, there’s noise and potential error
in the placement of those markers on bony landmarks.
Obviously, the amount of sub-tissue underneath them
makes a difference.”
Beyond anatomical variation, participants also emphasized how
developmental and sensory differences shape children’s interaction
with sensing equipment. P4-Exp described how some children with
cognitive delays may actively remove sensors, while others respond
strongly due to sensory sensitivities: “We get a lot of patients who
have sensory processing disorders, so having things put on their bodies
is really uncomfortable for them. [As a result], sometimes we have
sensors thrown across the room.” P1-Exp similarly elaborated:
“We get a lot of patients who have sensory processing
disorders, so having things put on their body is really
uncomfortable for them. Trying to help them during
that process can be really challenging. Sometimes we
have sensors thrown across the room when we put them
on, or just taken off, and we have to help them keep the
sensors on.”
These challenges also shape methodological decision-making.
P5-Exp explained that sensor choice depends heavily on patient
condition, noting that neurological populations in particular require
heightened sensitivity in system configuration: “For [the] pediatric
population, [it] depends on what condition they have. If it’s more like
orthopedic or sports-relevant injuries, there’s probably not too much
of a deal for the kids. But if it’s more like neurological conditions,
everything on their body can be really sensitive... You want to be as
minimal as possible and without interfering with the walking patterns
if you do a gait analysis.” At the same time, she highlighted an in-
herent constraint: certain clinically necessary measurements, such
as muscle activation, require specific sensors (e.g., EMG), limiting
flexibility in system design.

These perspectives reveal a design tension: while clinical validity
often depends on multi-sensor setups, pediatric patient tolerance
demands minimal, non-intrusive sensing. This finding creates a
gap between system requirements for comprehensive data capture
and the embodied realities of pediatric care. In response, clinicians

Marker stability under movement. Beyond placement chal-
lenges, medical expert participants described difficulties in main-
taining marker stability during dynamic movement. P5-Exp ex-
plained that pediatric analyses often involve activities beyond stan-
dard gait, such as jumping and landing, which increase the like-
lihood of markers becoming dislodged: “When they start to move
more, they start to sweat, and then the marker can fall off really easily.
We try tape, like FebriTape, and use those [adhesive] sprays so they
can stay better.”
Similarly, P6-Exp observed that markers “may not end up in
the exact same spot as they were when we first started,” introduc-
ing variability into the data and requiring technicians to balance
measurement precision with patient comfort and compliance. This
finding highlights a broader interactional tension: systems opti-
mized for accuracy often assume relatively stable and compliant
users, whereas pediatric gait analysis must accommodate continu-
ous movement and sensor displacement. P3-Exp further reinforced
this, noting that variability in adhesion across sessions can degrade
data quality. When markers fail, the entire workflow is disrupted,
requiring reapplication, recalibration, and repetition of data collec-
tion procedures.
Patient participation and interactional constraints. Finally,
medical expert participants emphasized that pediatric gait analysis
is not only a sensing challenge, but also a participatory one. P3-Exp
noted, “Working with kids and wearable sensors or getting them to
walk straight on a gait mat can be unpredictable at best,” adding that
even calibration can be difficult due to children’s limited ability to
remain still: “Just getting the kids, especially younger kids, to stand
still to calibrate the system that we were using at the time.” P1-Exp
further reflected on how patient endurance shapes the analysis
process itself: “Some of those markers do have to be put on in weight-
bearing positions... If our patient requires rest breaks every minute
or so, it just takes a lot more time... and the longer it takes and the
more energy they expend putting the markers on, the less energy they
have for the analysis piece.”
These findings illustrate how pediatric gait analysis is shaped
by technical sensing constraints and the embodied, sensory, and
behavioral variability of children. Rather than functioning as neu-
tral measurement systems, computerized gait technologies must be
continuously adapted to the realities of pediatric participation, re-
vealing a persistent mismatch between system design assumptions
and clinical use contexts.

mediated analysis. Therefore, technology relies on coordinated
human work across multiple roles to make children’s movement
measurement possible in practice.
4.3
RQ2: Clinicians surfaced design needs to
improve CGA technologies
All participants articulated visions for design improvements that
would better serve children while maintaining clinical utility and
increasing accessibility. In discussing future directions, P1-Exp em-
phasized the importance of advancing computerized gait technolo-
gies toward more personalized, accessible, and human- and patient-
centered models of care: “We get many parents who didn’t even know
this kind of resource existed. If we can help them recognize that our
services are available—-that we can provide support and ideally make
their appointments as comfortable and low-stress as possible-—that
would be my ideal outcome. I want both patients and parents to feel
heard, seen, and assured that this is an analysis designed for them,
where they remain our top priority.”
4.3.1
Low-burden and markerless gait analysis technologies.
Across all participants, they expressed a shared vision of shifting
from contact-based sensing systems toward more unobtrusive ap-
proaches. This envisioned transition reflects a broader attempt to
reduce the physical, sensory, and interactional burden placed on
pediatric patients during gait analysis.
Medical experts. Medical experts emphasized designing tech-
nologies and clinical environments that better support patient com-
fort during the analysis process. For technologies, they frequently
framed markerless or reduced-marker systems as a meaningful
shift in the patient–system interaction paradigm, particularly in re-
sponse to the challenges of pediatric analysis. As P1-Exp noted, “if it
could be something that has [as] minimum markers as possible to put
on their body. That would be wonderful,” emphasizing the increased
difficulty of applying sensors to anxious or sensitive children.
Building on this, P4-Exp described markerless motion capture as
“the gait analysis of the future,” highlighting its potential to reduce
“any type of medical trauma inflicted on these children.” He further
underscored a perceived data quality benefit of reducing physical
interference with the body:
“The less amount of touch that you have on these pa-
tients, I think the better data you’re probably going to
get... [because] a lot of these patients, if they’re not used
to having all these things on them, they’re not going to
walk as they typically do for your analysis.”
More broadly, medical expert participants viewed markerless
approaches as addressing a cluster of current limitations, including
patient tolerance, setup time, and issues related to sensor adhesion.
At the same time, P13-Des emphasized that certain physiological
signals remain difficult to capture without direct sensing modali-
ties, noting: “You have to measure the nerve. I don’t see a way you
would do that without an EMG-type sensor.” This perspective high-
lights a tension between aspirations for non-contact sensing and
the continued necessity of specialized sensors for specific clinical
measurements.
Overall, participants expressed a need for intuitive technologies.
As P3-Exp explained, simplicity is a core requirement in busy clini-
cal environments: “Simplicity is king. The simpler, the better. As a
care provider, we have a very limited amount of time with the patient;
we need something quick, easy, reliable, and consistent. We need it to
be a point-and-click solution. I need to be able to just press a button
and have it give me the information that I need.”
Participants’ preference for reduced-contact systems was also
shaped by recognition of the broader clinical environment as part
of the sensing context. P1-Exp explained that children often enter

4.3.2
Ecologically valid gait analysis. Across participants, fu-
ture CGA technologies were consistently envisioned as extending
beyond specialized laboratories and clinics into patients’ everyday
environments. Participants framed this shift as critical for capturing
more representative movement patterns, supporting longitudinal
analysis, and understanding patient behavior within real-world
contexts rather than controlled clinical settings.
Medical experts. Medical expert participants emphasized the
importance of contextual and performance-based analysis outside
the clinic, particularly for understanding how patients naturally
move in daily life. P2-Exp described the value of recreating more
ecologically valid environments for athletes:
“Having a space that recreates their sporting environ-
ment would be the best or most ideal... being able to
meet the patients in their environments would be better,
so portability of the systems to make that happen.”
This perspective reflects a broader shift away from laboratory-
constrained measurement toward sensing approaches capable of
capturing movement in the environments where patients naturally
perform activities. Extending this idea, P5-Exp highlighted the value
of observing patients across multiple everyday settings, including
“a school setting... an outdoor setting, [or] a playground.” Similarly,
P3-Exp contrasted the limitations of clinic-based snapshots with
the potential of continuous real-world monitoring:
“Data collected in [the] lab is going to be so much dif-
ferent than data collected out of [the] lab. A movement
library of them over time outside of the office would be
really interesting... versus the way they’re moving in
the office, which is kind of just a snapshot in time.”
These findings highlight ways to generate more representative
and longitudinal understandings of patient movement.
Physicians. Physicians similarly envisioned home and day-to-
day sensing as a means of improving clinical understanding of
patient performance, recovery, and safety over time. P12-Phy dis-
tinguished between the goals of laboratory analysis and home
monitoring:
“Physical and functional analyses [are] well-suited for
[the clinic and gait lab]. I think home monitoring has a
different goal, and that would be risk analysis for trips
and falls, to make sure that patients are safe in their
homes, [and activity monitoring]. Then you can see if
there’s a change in the gait pattern that’s sudden, and
that would be important information to know to avoid
trips and falls, but also to identify changes in health
status and neurologic status.”

4.3.3
Automated gait analysis technologies. Physicians en-
visioned automated gait analysis systems as a way to reduce the
technical and operational burden currently placed on clinical staff
while improving efficiency within the gait analysis workflow.
Physicians. Several physicians emphasized the substantial amount
of manual work required to process and interpret gait data under
existing systems. P8-Phy described the biomechanist role as partic-
ularly resource-intensive: “The biomechanist role [is] a lot of work.
Going through and cleaning up the data and all that... Those people are
very expensive, and the amount of time that’s required is incredibly
high.” Similarly, P9-Phy noted that automation could significantly
improve workflow efficiency: “If it were automated, it would actually
speed up the process quite a bit. I think the team, as far as I know,
spends a lot of time processing the data, which, candidly, won’t be
cost-effective for the hospital system.”
Beyond reducing processing time, participants also envisioned
automation as a way to support more continuous and scalable forms
of patient monitoring. For example, P12-Phy expressed interest in
an “automated report that the clinicians could use to keep monitoring
multiple times a year,” suggesting a desire for systems that could
streamline longitudinal tracking and clinical follow-up.
At the same time, P2-Exp, a biomechanist, recognized that in-
creasing automation could substantially reshape or diminish their
existing professional role:
“I’m going to say my role is going to be diminished,
especially with the advent of the video-based systems,
because there [are] less requirements for technical ex-
pertise to operate those [and] process the data, and some
of that’s becoming more automated.”
These findings highlight that automation in CGA is a sociotechni-
cal redesign: it redistributes cognitive and operational labor across
clinical roles, raises questions about how automated outputs should
be presented to preserve rather than displace clinician judgment,
and introduces new dependencies on system reliability in work-
flows where data quality shapes surgical and therapeutic decisions
for child patients.

5.1
Pediatric-Centered Sensing as a Validity
Requirement
Our findings show that pediatric-centered design is not only a
matter of improving comfort or satisfaction; it is central to the
validity of computerized gait assessment. Participants repeatedly
described how children’s bodies, sensory needs, fatigue, movement
patterns, and developmental differences shape whether data can
be collected at all and whether the resulting measurements reflect
typical movement. When a child removes sensors, walks differently
because markers feel uncomfortable, or expends limited energy
during setup before the assessment begins, the interaction between
child and technology becomes inseparable from the clinical data
produced.
This reframes pediatric gait assessment as a user–system fit prob-
lem. Many current gait technologies implicitly assume adult-sized,
stationary, cognitively typical, and sensor-tolerant users. Pediatric
clinicians must then compensate through informal workarounds:
preparing children with stickers or bandages, introducing playful
interaction strategies, involving child life specialists, asking care-
givers to help, or adapting the assessment when a child cannot com-
ply with standard calibration procedures. These accommodations
demonstrate clinical creativity, but they also reveal how current
systems externalize adaptation work onto clinicians, families, and
children.
Importantly, these challenges should not be framed as limitations
of pediatric patients. They are design mismatches between existing
sensing systems and the embodied realities of pediatric care. This
aligns with precedents in other medical domains, where pediatric
audiology required modifications for shorter attention spans, dif-
ferent anatomies, and developmental capabilities [4], and pediatric
cardiac monitoring required attention to higher heart rates, smaller
electrode placements, and movement artifacts [7]. Future gait tech-
nologies should similarly treat pediatric anatomical variability, sen-
sory accommodation, and movement unpredictability as first-order
design requirements. Adaptive calibration, flexible marker models,
minimal-contact sensing pathways, and child-facing preparation
resources should be designed into the system rather than handled
only through local clinical labor.
5.2
The Gait Lab as Part of the Sensing System
Participants’ accounts also suggest that the clinical environment
should be understood as part of the sensing system. The gait lab
is not a neutral backdrop where movement simply occurs. Its lay-
out, lighting, smell, privacy, noise, toys, staff, and perceived steril-
ity shape how children feel and move before any sensor begins
recording. Participants contrasted sterile adult-oriented spaces with

pediatric environments designed around familiarity and comfort,
echoing prior work showing that color, lighting, and child-centered
spatial design can reduce stress in pediatric clinical settings [12, 25].
For gait assessment, this environmental influence matters be-
cause movement is the object of measurement. If a child moves
differently because they feel watched, anxious, distracted, or con-
strained by the laboratory, the assessment may be technically pre-
cise but clinically incomplete. Participants’ interest in home, school,
sports, and playground-based assessment reflects a desire for eco-
logically valid data that captures children’s everyday mobility rather
than a short clinical snapshot. This ecological orientation aligns
with broader trends in pediatric health and rehabilitation, includ-
ing home-based physical therapy and attention to participation in
natural environments [2, 19].
At the same time, moving assessment beyond the lab introduces
new design tensions. Home and community sensing can produce
more representative longitudinal data, but it also raises questions
about privacy, occlusion, device placement, infrastructure, family
burden, and interpretability. Participants recognized that consumer
devices and smartphone-based systems may improve deployability,
while ambient or vision-based systems may reduce physical burden.
However, they also noted that privacy protections must work under
real movement conditions, not just idealized demonstrations. HCI
design for pediatric gait assessment should therefore consider a
spectrum of environments: child-centered clinical labs, portable
care-center setups, and carefully bounded everyday monitoring.
Each setting offers different tradeoffs among validity, privacy, bur-
den, and clinical oversight.
5.3
Automation Should Redistribute, Not Erase,
Clinical Expertise
Participants described automation as both desirable and consequen-
tial. Physicians saw automated processing and reporting as a way
to reduce expensive, time-consuming data cleanup and support
longitudinal monitoring. The designer and clinicians also noted
that video-based and consumer-device approaches could make gait
assessment more scalable. Yet the findings also show that comput-
erized gait assessment depends on expert interpretation, especially
when data are incomplete, movement patterns are complex, or
clinical recommendations remain uncertain.
This tension resonates with HCI research cautioning against
clinical systems that position experts as passive validators of auto-
mated outputs [35]. In pediatric gait assessment, automation should
not replace biomechanists, technicians, or physicians with opaque
summaries. Instead, it should selectively redistribute labor. Data
cleaning, parameter extraction, routine comparisons, and draft re-
ports may be appropriate targets for automation, while systems
should preserve clinician oversight over data quality, uncertainty,
context, and treatment relevance. Interfaces should make visible
how a measurement was derived, when the system is uncertain,
whether a trial was atypical, and how outputs relate to the child’s
functional goals.
This is especially important because pediatric care is collabora-
tive. Our findings show that clinicians, technicians, child life special-
ists, therapists, parents, and children all contribute to making gait
analysis possible. Automated systems that ignore this distributed
work risk optimizing a narrow analytic pipeline while undermining
the broader clinical process. Designing for pediatric gait assessment
therefore requires attention to team communication, family-facing
explanations, longitudinal reports, and tools that help clinicians
translate movement data into decisions that are understandable
and meaningful to families.
5.4
Design Implications
We summarize design implications for future computerized pedi-
atric technologies in Table 3. Participants revealed mismatches
between current gait technology design assumptions, such as adult-
sized, stationary, cognitively typical users, and pediatric interaction
requirements, including small anatomies, movement variability,
sensory sensitivities, and family-mediated participation.
5.5
Limitations and Future Research
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these
findings. First, our sample was drawn from a university-affiliated
clinical network, which may overrepresent well-resourced settings
with access to specialized gait laboratories. Future research should
include community hospitals, local care centers, and resource-
constrained settings where implementation barriers may differ.
Second, although our sample included complementary clinical roles
and a designer, the number of participants was modest; the survey
findings should be interpreted as descriptive expert perspectives
rather than generalizable estimates.
Third, this study focused on clinician and designer perspectives.
These perspectives are essential for understanding technical work-
flows and clinical decision-making, but they do not replace the lived
experiences of children and families. Future work should directly
involve child patients and caregivers, with age-appropriate and
accessible methods, to understand how gait assessment technolo-
gies are experienced before, during, and after clinical encounters.
Finally, our study relied on interviews, surveys, and scenario-based
ideation rather than direct observation of gait analyses. Observa-
tional and prototype-based studies could examine how specific
design interventions change patient comfort, clinician workload,
data quality, and family understanding in practice.
6
Conclusion
Computerized gait analysis can provide clinically valuable, objec-
tive evidence for pediatric diagnosis and treatment planning, but
its effectiveness depends on more than sensing accuracy. Through
a mixed-methods study with 12 pediatric clinicians and one gait
technology designer, we show that pediatric gait analysis is a so-
ciotechnical interaction problem shaped by children’s bodies, sen-
sory needs, families, clinician labor, automated processing, and
care environments. Participants valued data-rich and standardized
analysis yet identified persistent mismatches between current sys-
tems and pediatric practice. We contribute design implications for
low-burden sensing, clinically integrated workflows, environment-
aware analysis, ecologically valid longitudinal monitoring, and
automation that supports rather than displaces clinician interpreta-
tion. Designing gait analysis for children requires treating pediatric
needs as central system requirements, not special cases handled
after deployment.

A
Survey Instrument
Participants completed surveys tailored to their clinical role. The survey consisted of the following components.
Medical Experts and Designers
Participants completed three survey sections:
• Technology evaluation. Participants rated the importance of seven design criteria: functionality, accuracy, ease of use, comfort,
affordability, accessibility, and scalability using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not important; 7 = extremely important).
• Sensor perception. Participants evaluated sensing technologies according to functionality, accuracy, training requirements, patient
comfort, affordability, availability, and scalability using a three-level scale (low, moderate, high).
• Context-specific deployment. Participants indicated whether each sensing technology should be used in the gait laboratory, local care
center, or the patient’s home using one of three options: not use, maybe use, or definitely use.
Physicians
Participants completed three survey sections:
• Technology evaluation. Physicians rated the same seven technology evaluation criteria (functionality, accuracy, ease of use, comfort,
affordability, accessibility, and scalability) using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not important; 7 = extremely important).
• Clinical decision-making. Physicians rated the importance of temporal parameters, spatial parameters, joint angles, ground reaction
forces, and muscle activation using a three-level scale (low, moderate, high).
• Context-specific gait parameters. Physicians rated the importance of each gait parameter for use in the gait laboratory, local care
center, and patient’s home using a three-level scale (low, moderate, high).
