ABSTRACT
Early-time light curves of Type Ia Supernovae (SNe Ia) encode critical information about their pro-
genitor systems. We characterize the rise of normal SNe Ia using a volume-complete sample of 972
events from the Zwicky Transient Facility Data Release 2, an order of magnitude larger than any pre-
vious dataset for similar analyses. Fitting light curves up to 30% of peak flux with a power-law model
under a hierarchical Bayesian framework, we provide robust population-level constraints on the rise
time (trise; µ = 18.55 ± 0.08 days, σ = 1.42 ± 0.07 days), rise index (α; µ = 2.10 ± 0.04, σ = 0.48 ± 0.03
in ZTF r), and g −r color evolution (αg −αr; µ = 0.20 ± 0.02, σ = 0.17 ± 0.02). These power-law fits
are sensitive to the chosen truncation epoch if data beyond ∼40% of peak flux are included, but gener-
ally converge when restricted to earlier epochs. The relation between rise morphology and light-curve
width (SALT2 x1 stretch) bifurcates into two distinct regimes: high-stretch SNe Ia show clear trends
where a higher x1 correlates with shallower rises and more persistent blue colors, whereas low-stretch
SNe Ia lack such trends. While rise times correlate positively with x1 overall, this relation flattens
significantly within the high-stretch population. Searching for anomalies, we identify several normal
SNe Ia with unusually long rise times, which potentially exhibit short-duration (≲2 days) flux excesses
over a smooth rise. Long-duration (∼5 days) flux excesses appear common within the high-stretch
population and are tied to the shallow rises and early blue colors, pointing to widespread outward 56Ni
mixing. Multi-dimensional explosion models with more realistic progenitor setups are needed to fully
reproduce the observed dichotomy in rise morphology and stretch.

1. INTRODUCTION
Modern time-domain surveys have transformed Type
Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) from individual curiosities into
rich statistical samples, revealing a demographic com-
plexity that defies a single progenitor channel for the
thermonuclear explosion of a carbon-oxygen white dwarf
(WD) in a binary system (see Z.-W. Liu et al. 2023; A. J.
Ruiter & I. R. Seitenzahl 2025, for extensive reviews).
Although the remarkably standardizable light curves of
SNe Ia near maximum luminosity have cemented their
role as precise cosmological distance indicators (A. G.
Riess et al. 1998; S. Perlmutter et al. 1999; DES Col-
laboration et al. 2024), this standardization obscures the
diverse physical mechanisms driving their explosions.
The early-time light curves (≲1 week after the explo-
sion) of SNe Ia provide a unique opportunity to break
the degeneracy between different explosion channels and
progenitor properties. Observationally, early emission is
far more diverse in both morphology (R. E. Firth et al.
2015; W. Zheng et al. 2017; A. A. Miller et al. 2020) and
color evolution (M. D. Stritzinger et al. 2018; M. Bulla
et al. 2020; Y. Q. Ni et al. 2025) than the light curves
near peak brightness. Physically, early-time emission is
highly sensitive to the distribution of 56Ni in the outer
ejecta (A. L. Piro & E. Nakar 2013, 2014; M. R. Magee
et al. 2018, 2020), offering signatures of the underlying
explosion mechanism.
Beyond uncovering the structure in the outermost
ejecta, early-time observations are essential for anchor-
ing the explosion epoch and measuring the total rise
time to peak luminosity:
governed by the diffusion
timescale of photons from radioactive 56Ni decay, the
rise time is essential for estimating the total ejecta mass
and 56Ni yield (W. D. Arnett 1982).
Previous stud-
ies have inferred a broad progenitor mass distribution
characterized by a substantial fraction of sub-MCh ex-
plosions (R. A. Scalzo et al. 2014, 2019; N. Sarin et al.
2026). However, due to the historical scarcity of prompt
photometric observations, many of these studies relied
on empirical rise-decline correlations derived from small
samples (e.g., M. Ganeshalingam et al. 2011), poten-
tially biasing mass calculations.
Early-time light curves also uniquely encode signa-
tures of external interactions, where the expanding SN
ejecta collides with either a non-degenerate companion
(D. Kasen 2010) or circumstellar material (CSM; A. L.
Piro & V. S. Morozova 2016) to produce short-lived flux
excesses. Early excesses have been extensively searched
for in SNe Ia light curves (e.g., B. T. Hayden et al. 2010a;
R. P. Olling et al. 2015; J.-a. Jiang et al. 2018, 2020;
M. Deckers et al. 2022; M. R. Magee et al. 2022; M. M.
Fausnaugh et al. 2023; C. Ye et al. 2024; W. Wu et al.
2026; C. Rojas-Bravo et al. 2026) and confidently found
in a handful of normal events (e.g., W. Zheng et al. 2013;
G. H. Marion et al. 2016; G. Hosseinzadeh et al. 2017;
A. A. Miller et al. 2018; G. Dimitriadis et al. 2018; W.
Li et al. 2018; C. Ashall et al. 2022; H. Sai et al. 2022;
Q. Wang et al. 2024; A. Iskandar et al. 2025), providing
rare evidence for the binary companion and the mass-
transfer history of the progenitor system. Nevertheless,
most of these studies remain inconclusive regarding the
physical origin of the excesses.
Historically, early-time SN Ia studies focused on infer-
ring a global baseline model from aggregate data, defin-
ing a “standard” event through its mean rise time and
strict rise-fall correlations (e.g., A. G. Riess et al. 1999;
G. Goldhaber et al. 2001; A. Conley et al. 2006; M.
Ganeshalingam et al. 2011; S. Gonz´alez-Gait´an et al.
2011, see C. Liu & A. A. Miller 2026, hereafter Paper I,
for a comprehensive review). Modern analyses of large,
uniform datasets have shifted this paradigm, pulling the
focus towards rigorously quantifying the intrinsic scat-
ter around these universal templates. By modeling the
rises of individual SNe from large samples collected from
untargeted, all-sky surveys, a significant intrinsic scatter
in the rise parameters has been quantified (e.g., R. E.
Firth et al. 2015; W. Zheng et al. 2017; A. A. Miller et al.
2020). However, aggregating fitting results directly from
individual SNe, if not conducted carefully, can introduce
dramatic systematics. For instance, A. A. Miller et al.
(2020; hereafter M20) identified a spurious correlation
between the inferred rise time and the redshift of the
SN, which they attributed to Malmquist bias, noting
that higher-redshift SNe are more likely to be discovered
at later phases. While limiting the analysis to a small
subset of well-sampled SNe can mitigate this bias, it also
significantly reduces the sample size and may introduce
selection biases (e.g., preferentially selecting SNe with
longer rise times).
To overcome these challenges, Paper I developed a
hierarchical Bayesian framework that models a bulk
SN sample without dedicated early-time data quality
cuts, allowing for the robust inference of population-
level properties while properly accounting for measure-
ment uncertainty and sampling cadence. In this second
paper of the series, we apply this framework to a volume-
complete SN Ia sample from the Zwicky Transient Facil-
ity (E. C. Bellm et al. 2018; M. J. Graham et al. 2019;
R. Dekany et al. 2020) Data Release 219 (ZTF DR2; M.
Rigault et al. 2025a; M. Amenouche et al. 2025). After

We adopt the hierarchical Bayesian framework estab-
lished in Paper I, which simultaneously constrains indi-
vidual and population-level parameters. At the individ-

tivariate Gaussian distribution (the population prior),
whose mean, scatter, and covariance structure are them-
selves constrained simultaneously with the individual
fits. This joint inference naturally re-weights each SN’s
contribution to the population-level result according to
its data quality, allowing sparsely sampled events and
noisy detections to be included without bias. As demon-
strated in Paper I, the hierarchical approach dramat-
ically outperforms the traditional two-step method of
fitting individual SNe and then aggregating the results,
robustly recovering population-level parameters free of

the final 10–20% of the annealing iterations. The ad-

3. APPLICATION ON ZTF DR2
ZTF DR2 provides forced-photometry light curves de-
rived from difference images output by the IPAC pro-
cessing pipeline (F. J. Masci et al. 2018). The detailed
data reduction is described in M. Smith et al. (in prep.).
The full ZTF DR2 contains 3,826 spectroscopically con-
firmed SNe Ia. Of these, 2,667 pass the cosmology-ready
quality cuts defined by M. Rigault et al. (2025a), with
2,629 classified as spectroscopically normal SNe Ia. Here
the overluminous 91T-like SNe Ia (A. V. Filippenko et al.
1992a) are also considered normal. To ensure statisti-
cal completeness, we restrict our sample to z < 0.06
(M. Amenouche et al. 2025), yielding a final volume-
complete sample of 972 normal objects.
The key as-
sumption we make is that this volume-complete sample
accurately represents the underlying normal SN Ia pop-
ulation, whose early-time parameters can be described
by a unimodal, multivariate Gaussian distribution.
In our primary analysis, we refrain from applying fur-
ther quality cuts based on early-time data coverage,
retaining even those SNe with sparse sampling.
Cru-
cially, the hierarchical Bayesian framework naturally
down-weights events with a limited number of detec-
tions, while still leveraging their constraining power to
the population-level parameters. Finally, we neglect K-
corrections in this analysis despite the sample extending
to z = 0.06. As demonstrated in Appendix A, ignoring
K-corrections introduces only minor systematics to the
inferred population-level parameters, which remain well
within typical measurement uncertainties.
We apply the standard quality cuts described by M.
Rigault et al. (2025a) to individual photometric mea-
surements, removing any observations with bits 1, 2, 4,
8, or 16 set in their flag attribute. Additionally, we ex-
clude data points in the baseline (taken >25 days prior
to the SALT2 peak time t0) with an apparent signal-
to-noise ratio (S/N) above 10, as these typically indi-
cate significant, improperly subtracted baseline offsets.
Furthermore, we identify three SNe where >50% of the
baseline measurements within a single fcqfID are flagged
as outliers; for these events, we exclude all observations
from the affected fcqfID.
We simultaneously fit all the remaining measurements
up to three different truncation thresholds (50%, 40%,
and 30% of the peak flux) to evaluate the impact of
potential model misspecification for the simple power-
law rise. Ultimately there are ∼105 measurements from
−100 days to the epoch of the truncation threshold that
pass these quality cuts. Roughly 20 SNe have no mea-
surements before the truncation threshold, and are ef-
fectively excluded from the analysis.

Under the hierarchical Bayesian framework, the pos-
terior of an individual SN is determined by the likeli-
hood as well as the population prior that is learned from
the modeling process. As expected, this approach sig-
nificantly alleviates the spurious redshift-dependence of
parameters induced by the Malmquist bias (M20), such
that the inferred trise for individual events no longer cor-
relates with the redshift (ρ ≃0 and p-value ≃0.6). On
the other hand, for SNe with poor data quality, the pa-
rameter posteriors largely reflect the population prior.
Consequently, SNe in the distribution tails will be pulled
back towards the population mean. Any statistical infer-
ence based on a specific subset of hierarchically modeled
samples would require careful evaluation.
3.1. The Robustness of the Hierarchical Bayesian
Framework to Quality Cuts
Astronomical samples often rely on quality cuts to en-
sure reliable parameter inference. However, because the
thresholds for these cuts are often arbitrary and vary
significantly across studies, their systematic impact on
population-level inferences is rarely quantified. The hi-

exclusions by naturally propagating measurement uncer-
tainties and weighting individual events based on their
constraining power (Paper I). This statistical coherency
allows us to apply minimal quality cuts, thereby mitigat-
ing arbitrary selection biases while maintaining robust
constraints on the underlying population properties.

4.2. Rise Time
Figure 6 displays a strong positive correlation between
trise and x1 for ZTF SNe Ia in the volume-complete sam-
ple, consistent with previous studies (A. G. Riess et al.
1999; G. Goldhaber et al. 2001; A. Conley et al. 2006;
B. T. Hayden et al. 2010b; M. Ganeshalingam et al.
2011; S. Gonz´alez-Gait´an et al. 2011).
Notably, the
trise–x1 relation also exhibits a break: for SNe Ia with
x1 > xbrk, the average rise time increases more slowly
with x1 than for those with x1 < xbrk.
Applying the same Bayesian regression analysis de-
scribed in Section 4.1, we confirm a significant decrease
in slope across the break point at xbrk ≃0.2 in the trise–
x1 relation, broadly consistent with the break points
identified in the αr–x1 and (αg −αr)–x1 relations. This
structural break is most prominent for the 30% trunca-
tion threshold. Despite the clear slope transition, intrin-
sic scatter (ϵ ≃0.3 days) still dominates the uncertainty
for individual SNe Ia across the entire x1 range. Fur-
thermore, the Student-T likelihood strongly favors a low
degree of freedom (ν ≃2.6), indicating that significant
outliers drive heavy-tailed residuals with respect to the
broken linear model. The fitting results are summarized
in Table 4.
Part of this behavior, however, could arise from the
light-curve fitting process. In fact, both statistical un-
certainties and systematic errors in the inferred trise are
dominated by the extrapolation under the assumption
of a power-law rise.
Statistically, the degeneracy be-
tween trise and α in the power-law model elongates the
posterior distribution, which inflates the uncertainty in
the inferred tfl, and thus trise. This large uncertainty in
trise potentially masks its intrinsic correlation with x1.
Physically, for SNe with a significant dark phase (e.g.,
due to a highly confined 56Ni distribution; A. L. Piro &
E. Nakar 2013, 2014), the tfl inferred from a power-law
fit may poorly represent the true explosion time.

parametric models) to explicitly capture the multi-
modality in the early-time parameter distributions and
the correlation structure within each sub-population.
However, this approach demands a substantially larger
sample size to constrain the additional hyperparame-
ters and is beyond the scope of this work. Instead, we
perform a straightforward test by applying the hierar-
chical Bayesian framework separately to the two sub-
populations, divided at xbrk = 0.2, to independently in-

would no longer be pulled toward a single global popu-

For the x1 > 0.2 population, we consistently recover
significant negative correlations (ρ ≃−0.5) between x1
and the early-time parameters t2.5%,r, αr, and αg −αr.
In contrast, the x1 < 0.2 population shows moderate
positive correlations between x1 and both αr and αg −
αr (ρ ≃0.3), as well as a strong positive correlation
between x1 and t2.5%,r (ρ ≃0.7) that is weakly detected
in Figure 7. These findings confirm that the behavior
of early-time observables relative to the full light-curve
stretch is fundamentally distinct between low- and high-
stretch populations.
Alternatively, Paper I proposed fitting individual SN
light curves using a custom prior derived from the full hi-
erarchical population prior. This tailored prior preserves
the correlation structure of the key early-time parame-
ters but isolates the mean and scatter of the nuisance pa-
rameter ln A, minimizing the over-regularization of trise
and α.
In Appendix B, we apply this custom popu-
lation prior to the subset of 272 well-sampled SNe Ia.
We find that the structural breaks in the correlations
between x1 and the early-time parameters remain con-
sistent with those derived from the primary hierarchical
model, although the measurement uncertainties for in-
dividual SNe Ia are generally larger. This consistency
suggests that the observed correlations are not artifacts
of the population prior, but rather reflect genuine phys-
ical trends in the data.
5. DISCUSSION
5.1. Spectroscopically Peculiar SNe Ia
While our analysis has primarily focused on spectro-
scopically normal SNe Ia (including 91T-like events), our
hierarchical Bayesian framework can be readily applied
to other spectroscopic subclasses.
In this section, we
briefly explore three common subtypes of spectroscopi-
cally peculiar SNe Ia in the ZTF DR2 sample: 91bg-like
(A. V. Filippenko et al. 1992b), 02cx-like (W. Li et al.
2003), and 03fg-like events (D. A. Howell et al. 2006).
Rarer subclasses are excluded due to their limited sam-
ple sizes (<10) in ZTF DR2 (G. Dimitriadis et al. 2025;
A. Alburai et al. 2026).
Given the substantial internal diversity and smaller
sample sizes of peculiar SNe Ia, defining a volume-
complete sample for each subclass is nontrivial. For the
intrinsically bright, slow-evolving 03fg-like SNe, we as-
sume they are volume-complete within the same z <
0.06 threshold used for the normal sample, yielding 11
events. Conversely, the fainter, fast-evolving 91bg-like
and 02cx-like SNe are underrepresented at this distance.

03fg-like events display extended rise times (µtrise ≃
26 days) and much steeper rise indices (µαr ≃3.9),
whereas the latter exhibit significantly shorter rise times
(µtrise ≃14 days) and shallower rise indices (µαr ≃1.4).
As demonstrated in Paper I, modeling an early flux ex-
cess with a single power-law rise can artificially bias
the inferred early-time parameters:
a short-duration
(≲2 days) excess biases trise longer and α higher, while
a long-duration (∼5 days) excess biases trise shorter and
α lower. Consequently, the distinct parameters of 03fg-
like and 02cx-like events could be phenomenologically
attributed to the presence of short- and long-duration
early flux excesses, respectively.
Spiky early flux excesses are commonly seen among
03fg-like events (e.g., J.-a. Jiang et al. 2021; G. Dimitri-
adis et al. 2023; S. Srivastav et al. 2023; M. R. Siebert
et al. 2023). The leading explosion scenario involves a
violent double WD merger (R. Pakmor et al. 2012, cf.
S. Taubenberger et al. 2019; G. Dimitriadis et al. 2023;
L. A. Kwok et al. 2024 for independent observational
evidence). The tidally disrupted secondary WD leaves
dense, confined CSM in the vicinity of the SN (C. Raskin
& D. Kasen 2013; C. Raskin et al. 2014), and the conse-
quent ejecta–CSM interaction powers a prominent brief
early excess (A. L. Piro 2015; U. M. Noebauer et al.
2016; A. L. Piro & V. S. Morozova 2016). Our findings
provide additional statistical support for this scenario
and confirm that short-duration early flux excesses are
likely prevalent among 03fg-like events.
The short rise times and shallow rise indices of 02cx-
like events have been identified in previous studies (e.g.,
M. R. Magee et al. 2016; B. Barna et al. 2021; K.
Maguire et al. 2023; W. B. Hoogendam et al. 2025; M. R.
Magee et al. 2025; M. Singh et al. 2026). These proper-
ties could be well reproduced by a partial deflagration of
a near-MCh WD (M. Kromer et al. 2013; M. Fink et al.
2014), in which the 56Ni is well mixed into the outer
ejecta layers, leading to a shallow rise index of α ≲1.4
(U. M. Noebauer et al. 2017). The low ejecta mass fol-
lowing a weak deflagration, on the other hand, results
in a short diffusion timescale and thus a short rise time.
For the 91bg-like events, the mean αr remains poorly
constrained despite a larger sample size, driving a broad
spread in its posterior predictive distribution. Neverthe-
less, their rise times are consistently shorter than those
of normal SNe Ia (µtrise ≃15 days), which aligns with
findings from previous studies (W. Zheng et al. 2017).

5.2.1. Steep/Long Risers: Signatures of Companion/CSM
Interaction
As discussed in Section 5.1, ejecta–CSM/companion
collisions can produce short-lived (≲2 days) early flux
excesses, which manifest as steep rises and/or extended
rise times when fitted with a power-law rise (Paper I).
Within our volume-complete sample, however, we find
no SNe with αr values exceeding the median of their re-
spective x1 bins by 2σ (i.e., αr ≳3.2 for x1 > 0 and
αr ≳3.0 for x1 < 0). However, the population prior
applied to the individual α posteriors may artificially
suppress extreme outliers.
To test this, we refit the
light curves of the 03fg-like events within z = 0.06 (Sec-
tion 5.1), forcing the population priors to match those
of the normal SNe Ia. Despite presenting a population
mean of µαr ≃3.9 when these events are modeled inde-
pendently, only one 03fg-like event (SN 2018gdl) yields
an αr > 3 under the normal priors. Even when expand-
ing the sample to include all DR2 03fg-like events with
good baseline coverage and Nearly ≥2, SN 2018gdl re-
mains the sole event confidently exhibiting αr > 3. This
confirms that Bayesian shrinkage disproportionately af-
fects high-α SNe under typical ZTF cadences (Paper I),
making the inferred α an unreliable metric for identify-
ing early flux excesses in our sample.
In contrast, the rise time is less sensitive to the
Bayesian shrinkage effect. Among the eleven 03fg-like
events that pass our baseline and early detection cuts,
eight have an adjusted rise time of trise −t2.5%,r >
20 days, clearly separating them from the normal pop-
ulation (Figure 7).
Among the normal SN Ia sample,
we identify four SNe Ia with trise −t2.5%,r values that
are more than 3σ above the median of their respec-
tive x1 bins. These events, which include SN 2018ikr,
SN 2019fdc, SN 2019kg, and SN 2020adhi, are high-
lighted in Figure 9.24 Loosening the threshold to 2.5σ
reveals an additional event, SN 2020skp.
SN 2018ikr, SN 2019kg, SN 2019fdc, and SN 2020skp
all exhibit extended adjusted rise times of ∼19 days and
feature weak early-time color evolution (αg −αr ≃0.0–

these anomalously long rises do not stem from a fun-
damental shift in overall light-curve morphology.
In-

decoupled from later evolutionary phases. In fact, for
all but SN 2020skp, the SALT2 model systematically un-

5.3. Implications for Progenitor Demographics
In Section 4, we demonstrate that the relationships be-
tween early-time parameters (trise, α, and αg −αr) and
the full light-curve stretch x1 are nonlinear, exhibiting
a distinct break at some xbrk ≳0. Here, we explore the
physical implications of these correlations and the ap-
parent dichotomy between low- and high-stretch SNe Ia.
Specifically, we investigate whether these observations
point to distinct progenitor channels or can be explained
by a continuous distribution of progenitor properties.
The light-curve stretch, broadly reflecting the diffu-
sion timescale of the ejecta (W. D. Arnett 1982), is of-
ten interpreted as a proxy for the total ejecta mass. N.
Sarin et al. (2026) recently performed systematic light-
curve modeling for ZTF DR2 SNe Ia using the Arnett
model, finding that the bimodal distribution of x1 can
be readily reproduced with a smooth, single-peaked dis-
tribution of ejecta masses spanning a wide range (∼0.5–
2 M⊙). However, this continuous mass distribution does
not necessarily preclude the existence of multiple dis-
tinct explosion mechanisms.

and super-MCh (Mej > 1.5 M⊙) populations defined in
N. Sarin et al. (2026). In Figure 11, we display the cor-

basis. This further complicates the interpretation of the
inferred Mej values. Consequently, although the inferred
super-MCh population exhibits fundamental differences
in the light curves compared to the rest of the sample,
their progenitors are not necessarily super-MCh in real-
ity. The relative ordering of the Mej values among the
three groups should, however, be generally robust.
Self-consistent modeling of early-time multi-band light
curves requires realistic treatments of both the 56Ni dis-
tribution and the ejecta density profile. A smooth vari-
ation in 56Ni-mixing degrees should only produce a con-
tinuous trend between early emission and stretch; the
observed break therefore suggests a more complex physi-
cal reality. This could indicate distinct explosion mecha-
nisms, or alternatively, if a single mechanism dominates,
an abrupt structural change in the ejecta at a critical
progenitor condition or prominent ejecta asymmetries
producing complicated viewing-angle effects.
To evaluate these possibilities, we examine the pre-
dicted correlations from two detailed theoretical frame-
works: (i) near-MCh explosions spanning a grid of den-
sity profiles and degrees of 56Ni mixing (M. R. Magee
et al. 2020), and (ii) sub-MCh double-detonation models
featuring varying WD masses and viewing angles (K. J.
Shen et al. 2021). By leveraging the flexibility of these
models to produce a diverse range of light-curve shapes
and early color evolution trends, we assess whether a sin-
gle underlying mechanism could reproduce the observed
parameter space.
The relations between early-time parameters and full
light-curve shapes here serve as diagnostics for theo-
retical models.
However, the SALT2 stretch x1 lacks
a physical counterpart in synthetic light curves gener-
ated by radiative transfer simulations. To bridge this
gap, we propose the adjusted rise time trise −t2.5%,r27 as
a physically motivated, easily measurable proxy for x1
(see Section 4.2) in both real and synthetic datasets. Its
relations with αr and αg −αr exhibit a distinct break
at ∼16–17 days (corresponding to x1 ≃0–0.5), above
which anticorrelations with both parameters emerge.

Profiles and 56Ni Distributions
M. R. Magee et al. (2020) conducted a grid search for
near-MCh progenitors, varying the ejecta density pro-

tributions (total 56Ni mass and a scale factor govern-

Although the
parameter combinations in this extensive grid are not
strictly tied to specific, self-consistent explosion models,
they provide a valuable baseline for exploring the wide
array of light-curve morphologies and early-time behav-

For a statistically robust comparison between the
models and observations, we generate synthetic light

Specifically, we consider two sets of density
profiles (double power-law and exponential), and re-

EK > 0.5 × 1051 erg (yielding realistic rise times of
≲20 days).
For each model, we generate 50 synthetic
light curves, drawn from a uniform volumetric distri-
bution up to z = 0.06. The TURTLS models provide
synthetic light curves with a 1-day cadence in abso-
lute magnitudes, which we convert to fluxes (without
K-corrections).
To assign realistic flux uncertainties,
we randomly draw sky noise from the ZTF pointings
catalog adopted in REDBACK (N. Sarin et al. 2024) and
add a flat 2% of the model source flux in quadrature
to account for calibration uncertainties.
We also pad
the pre-explosion light curves with zero-flux data points,
sampled at a 2-day cadence with randomly drawn sky
noise out to 50 days before B-band maximum. Finally,
the fluxes are normalized to the peak flux in their re-
spective bands (Section 3) and fit to a power-law rise
using the population priors inferred from the ZTF DR2
sample to ensure a direct comparison with the observed
correlations.
Even for the same underlying model, different noise
realizations yield a wide range of inferred parameters,
particularly for the rise index α. To robustly estimate
the mean of each parameter across the 50 synthetic light
curves, we adopt the weighted bootstrap method from
Paper I. This approach reweights the parameters from
each light curve by the inverse variance of their posteri-
ors, downweighting poorly constrained noise realizations
to produce reliable posterior draws for the mean.

6. CONCLUSIONS
In this second paper of our series on the early-time
light curves of SNe Ia, we performed statistical infer-
ence on a volume-complete sample of 972 normal SNe Ia
from the ZTF DR2, an order of magnitude larger than
any previously used in similar studies.
We leveraged
the hierarchical Bayesian framework developed in Pa-
per I to incorporate sparsely sampled light curves, which
avoids the selection bias induced by applying strict qual-
ity cuts.
This approach yielded the first comprehen-
sive characterization of the early-time parameter space
for a large, homogeneous sample of SNe Ia, establish-
ing robust population-level constraints on the key ob-
servables: the rise time (trise; µ = 18.55 ± 0.08 days,
σ = 1.42 ± 0.07 days), rise index (α; µ = 2.10 ± 0.04,
σ = 0.48 ± 0.03 in ZTF r), and g −r color evolution
(αg −αr; µ = 0.20 ± 0.02, σ = 0.17 ± 0.02). There is
a weak positive correlation between trise and α, whereas
αg and αr are almost perfectly correlated.
Focusing on 272 SNe Ia with high-quality early-time
coverage, we identified a dichotomy in how early-time
parameters correlate with the light-curve stretch (SALT2
x1).
A break occurs near xbrk ≃0.2–0.5: for high-
stretch events, both α and αg−αr display significant an-
ticorrelations with x1, whereas the low-stretch popula-
tion lacks such correlations. Additionally, trise increases
more slowly with x1 for high-stretch events. These re-
lations are subject to significant intrinsic scatter, and
should be interpreted as broad trends rather than de-
terministic relations. Incorporating x1 directly into the
hierarchical framework in future work will place tighter
constraints on this structural break.
Beyond the normal population, peculiar subgroups oc-
cupy distinct regions in the parameter space, implying
diverse outer ejecta properties. Overluminous 03fg-like
events display significantly longer trise relative to nor-
mal SNe Ia, whereas subluminous 02cx-like and 91bg-
like events exhibit notably shorter trise. Furthermore,
the distinct rise profiles of these peculiar subclasses,
steeper for 03fg-like and shallower for 02cx-like events,
independently support violent mergers and partial de-
flagration as their respective physical origins.
Within the normal sample, we identified multiple ob-
jects with anomalous early-time properties, including
SNe Ia with exceptionally long rise times relative to their
stretch, and others with near-linear rises (α ≃1). The
extended rise times for the outliers with a normal stretch
(x1 ≃0) likely arise from short-lived early flux excesses
that bias the inferred time of first light, potentially
driven by CSM interaction. The linear risers, predom-
inantly high-stretch 91T-like SNe, do not form an iso-
lated subclass. Instead, they likely represent the tail of a
continuous distribution of early-time properties among
the most luminous, slow-evolving normal SNe Ia, sug-
gesting a diverse degree of enhanced 56Ni mixing appears
prevalent among the high-stretch population. Whether
this enhanced mixing drives the observed anticorrela-
tion between α and x1, or instead only contributes to
the overall scatter, merits further investigation.
These empirical findings offer new constraints on pro-
genitor scenarios. We compared our results to detailed
theoretical models, including 1D near-MCh models with
varying density and 56Ni profiles (M. R. Magee et al.
2020), and 2D sub-MCh double-detonations with varying
progenitor masses and viewing angles (K. J. Shen et al.
2021). While these models predict a wide diversity of
light-curve shapes, they generally fail to reproduce the
specific correlations with stretch.
This discrepancy is
likely due to a combination of two major factors: (i) un-
realistic 56Ni and ejecta density profiles arising from sim-
plified parameterizations or limitations in hydrodynamic
simulations, and (ii) radiative transfer calculations that
struggle to capture the multi-dimensional effects in the
extremely optically thick SN ejecta shortly after explo-
sion. Multi-dimensional explosion models with more re-
alistic progenitor setups are necessary to fully explain
the early-time diversity of SNe Ia. Furthermore, utiliz-
ing a large sample of bolometric light curves constructed
with multi-band, high-cadence photometry can signifi-
cantly reduce the systematics introduced by radiative
transfer modeling, providing more direct constraints on
the underlying physical parameters.
Spectroscopic follow-up across extended phases pro-
vides crucial diagnostics for understanding the physi-
cal origins of the observed correlations and anomalies.
For instance, pre-peak spectra reveal the ejecta velocity
structure and chemical composition of the outer layers,


## Page 23


23
while nebular-phase spectra probe the innermost ejecta
structure and nucleosynthetic yields. Connecting early-
time photometric properties to these spectroscopic diag-
nostics yields deeper insights into the explosion mech-
anisms shaping the observed diversity.
Additionally,
host-galaxy properties uniquely constrain progenitor de-
mographics and delay-time distributions, independently
informing the progenitor channels. In future work of this
series, we will systematically explore these connections
across the ZTF SN Ia sample.
The observed nonlinear correlations expose a multi-
modal parameter space that our current single multi-
variate Gaussian population prior cannot naturally cap-
ture.
Future hierarchical models should incorporate
more flexible population priors, such as Gaussian mix-
ture models or non-parametric frameworks. Although
these approaches require larger sample sizes, the rapidly
expanding ZTF dataset (∼104 SNe Ia in the upcom-

APPENDIX
A. IMPACT OF IGNORING K-CORRECTIONS IN
LIGHT-CURVE MODELING
K-corrections are usually ignored in early-time SN Ia
analysis due to the lack of a robust early-time spec-
trophotometric sample, which can introduce a system-
atic uncertainty in the rise index α of ∼0.1 (R. E. Firth
et al. 2015). Consequently, modeling an SN Ia sample
with a wide redshift distribution without accounting for
K-corrections could induce spurious scatter and corre-
lations in the inferred light-curve parameters.
To quantify this effect, we design simulations based
on the spectrophotometric time series of SN 2011fe (R.
Pereira et al. 2013), the gold-standard normal SN Ia with
the most complete early-time spectral coverage with
accurate flux calibration. We generate synthetic light
curves of SN 2011fe as observed at different redshifts in
the ZTF g and r bands, using the time-dependent spec-
tral energy distribution (SED) from R. Pereira et al.
(2013), and then fit these synthetic light curves using
the same hierarchical Bayesian framework.
We first model synthetic light curves of SN 2011fe at
two fixed redshifts: z = 0.01 (where K-corrections are
minimal) and z = 0.06 (the bound of the ZTF volume-
complete sample), fitting them up to 40% of peak flux
with the simple power-law model. For both sets of 300
synthetic SNe, the inferred scatter in α and trise is con-
sistent with zero, reflecting the single underlying SED.


## Page 24


24
However, the inferred αg at z = 0.06 is systematically
larger than at z = 0.01 by 0.10 ± 0.03, while differ-
ences in αr (0.01 ± 0.03) and trise (0.06 ± 0.07 days) are
not statistically significant. This bias in the g band is
driven by broad, rapidly evolving absorption features in
the rest-frame blue optical spectrum of SN 2011fe. Us-
ing a truncation threshold of 30%, where the early SED
is more strongly shaped by these features, the effect be-
comes more pronounced: the difference in αg increases
to 0.30 ± 0.05, and discrepancies in αr (0.18 ± 0.15) and
trise (0.46 ± 0.11 days) become non-negligible.
Next, we fit a realistic synthetic sample drawn from
a uniform spatial distribution out to z = 0.06.
Be-
cause the sample is dominated by objects near this outer
boundary, the inferred population means closely track
the z = 0.06 results. With a 40% truncation thresh-
old, we find only a marginal increase in the inferred
scatter:
σαg = 0.030 ± 0.005, σαr = 0.006 ± 0.003,
and σtrise = 0.017 ± 0.008 days.
These values remain
negligible compared to the intrinsic population scatter
measured in real data (see Section 3).
Even with a
30% truncation threshold, the induced scatter remains
small (σαg = 0.025 ± 0.006, σαr = 0.022 ± 0.008, and
σtrise = 0.012±0.007 days). This demonstrates that in a
volume-limited sample, the scatter induced by neglect-
ing K-corrections is consistently low regardless of the
truncation threshold, and any induced parameter corre-
lations are consistent with zero.
In conclusion, while ignoring K-corrections introduces
systematic shifts in the population mean of certain pa-
rameters (most notably αg), the resulting artificial scat-
ter and correlations are negligible relative to the intrinsic
distributions and typical measurement uncertainties in
current volume-limited SN Ia surveys.
We note, how-
ever, that this test relies entirely on the SED sequence
of SN 2011fe, which belongs to the early-red population
(M. D. Stritzinger et al. 2018).
Its early spectra are
characterized by a cool photosphere with strong absorp-
tion in the blue optical (overlapping the ZTF g band).
