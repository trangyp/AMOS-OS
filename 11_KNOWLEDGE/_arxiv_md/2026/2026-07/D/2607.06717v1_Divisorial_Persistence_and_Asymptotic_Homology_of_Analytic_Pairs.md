More broadly, many invariants of singular spaces arise from asymptotic
phenomena near degenerating analytic sets. Log canonical thresholds, di-
visorial valuations, poles of local zeta functions, oscillatory integrals, and
motivic volumes are governed by the birational geometry of log-resolutions
[Kol97, Kol13, KM98, Den92, Igu00]. A recurring principle is that analytic
asymptotics become monomial after resolution, and the resulting exponents
are encoded by divisorial data. This divisorial data feeds several parallel
lines of investigation — poles of local zeta functions, volume asymptotics of
sublevel sets, and, as pursued here, persistent asymptotic homology — each
asking a different mathematical question of the same underlying geometry;
the study of volume asymptotics from this divisorial viewpoint is developed,
from a different perspective, in related work [Gru26].

The present work continues the asymptotic birational program initiated
in [Gru26].
There the asymptotic expansion of sublevel-set volumes was
shown to determine the visible spectrum of the local zeta function through
an explicit reconstruction procedure: that layer of the theory records which
asymptotic exponents occur. The present article develops the homological
layer of the same geometry and addresses a different question — once the
divisorial asymptotic exponents are available, which of these levels actually
support persistent topological information, and how the divisors carrying
them are glued together. The two papers thus study different layers of one
asymptotic geometry: the first recovers divisorial exponents from the geome-
try of sublevel-set volumes, the second studies the asymptotic topology those
exponents can sustain. Accordingly, the analytic reconstruction of exponents
through Mellin asymptotics developed in [Gru26] is here regarded as estab-
lished, and the emphasis shifts from analytic reconstruction to asymptotic
topology.
Intersection homology [GM80, GM83] provides the original framework for
admissibility conditions in singular topology. Since then, several homologi-
cal theories have been developed to capture the topology of singular spaces
through analytic, metric, or Lipschitz information. Intersection homology
uses perversity conditions; vanishing homology [Val10] records the asymp-
totic behaviour of level sets; and metric homology [BB00, BB02] imposes
Lipschitz constraints. DAH is inspired by the general philosophy of these
asymptotic homology theories, but its construction is intrinsically divisorial
and birational.
From the perspective of topological data analysis, tracking homological
invariants along a one-parameter family of sublevel sets naturally leads to
the language of persistent homology [Car09, ZC05]. The present work re-
alizes this philosophy in the setting of analytic pairs, where the persistence
parameter is not continuous but reduces to a finite set of homological critical
exponents, each arising from divisorial data on a log-resolution.
The theory introduced here is distinguished by three features.
(1) Admissibility is governed by divisorial valuations arising from log-
resolutions rather than by metric or Lipschitz conditions.
(2) The filtration is indexed by a finite discrete spectrum
ΓH(X, I),
intrinsic to the asymptotic concentration phenomena of the pair and
independent of the chosen resolution.
(3) The resulting homology groups are invariant under the birational
constructions and energy equivalences considered in this paper and
are independent of the choice of generators and, in the surface case,
of the chosen resolution.

5.3. Why admissible homology becomes combinatorial. Before turn-
ing to the proof, it is worth isolating the idea that makes it work, indepen-
dently of any estimate.
In dimension two, the exceptional locus of a log-resolution is a curve.
Consequently:
• exceptional divisors are one-dimensional: each Ei is a compact curve,
contributing exactly one vertex to Γ(i);
• intersections of exceptional divisors are zero-dimensional: by the nor-
mal form of Section 5.1, two divisors meet in isolated points, con-
tributing exactly one edge each to Γ(i);
• there are no higher incidences: since at most two components pass
through any point of Exc(π), no point lies on three or more excep-
tional divisors simultaneously.
This truncation at pairwise intersections is what a graph can encode in full:
a graph has vertices and edges and nothing else. In dimension two there is
simply nothing more for the exceptional locus to record.
Consequently, once the asymptotic concentration of a chain near Exc(π)
is understood divisor by divisor (Section 5.4 below), the entire homological
content of the admissibility filtration is already determined by which divi-
sors are critical and how they meet — that is, by the combinatorics of Γ(i)
alone. No continuous or higher-order geometric information survives the lo-
calization; everything homological reduces to counting vertices, edges, and
the components they assemble into. The remainder of this section makes
this precise and proves it is an isomorphism, not merely an analogy.

5.6. Why dimension two is special. The proof of Theorem 5.8 given in

the incidence geometry of Exc(π) is completely recorded by a graph.
It
is worth making precise why this fact is special to dim X = 2, since this is
exactly the point at which the argument stops working in higher dimensions.
Let π : e

consider a non-empty intersection Ei0 ∩· · ·∩Eip of p+1 distinct components.
• Dimension two. Each Ei has dimension one, and normal crossings

of dimension zero. Triple intersections Ei ∩Ej ∩Ek are generically
empty. The incidence geometry of Exc(π) therefore consists of noth-

with no loss of information.
• Dimension three. Each Ei now has dimension two, and Ei ∩Ej
is generically a curve, not a point. A graph has no vertex-and-edge

can only record that two divisors meet, not how, i.e. not the genus
or connectivity of Ei ∩Ej itself. Triple intersections Ei ∩Ej ∩Ek
are now generically zero-dimensional and already carry information
a graph cannot see, since they correspond to 2-simplices, not edges.
• Dimension four. Each Ei has dimension three, pairwise intersec-
tions Ei ∩Ej are generically surfaces, triple intersections are generi-
cally curves, and quadruple intersections are generically points. The
incidence pattern now requires simplices of every dimension from 0
up to 3, each carrying its own homology.

7. DAH Within the Landscape of Admissibility-Based Homology
This section situates divisorial asymptotic homology within the family of
admissibility-based singular homology theories of analytic type. We prove
comparison theorems between DAH and two theories in the analytic-metric
regime: metric homology [BB00, BB02] and vanishing homology [Val10].
Intersection homology [GM80, GM83] serves as a theoretical backdrop, as
the founding theory of the admissibility paradigm. DAH is inspired by the
general philosophy of these asymptotic homology theories, including metric
and vanishing approaches, but its construction is intrinsically divisorial and
birational.

parison with metric homology (Section 7) is a complete theorem under the
hypothesis of normal embedding.
The comparison with vanishing homology (Section 7.1) should be regarded
as a conditional correspondence: it relies on the expected equivalence be-
tween projective thinness and divisorial admissibility (Proposition 7.9), to-
gether with a stability assumption for admissible chains under flat limits.
