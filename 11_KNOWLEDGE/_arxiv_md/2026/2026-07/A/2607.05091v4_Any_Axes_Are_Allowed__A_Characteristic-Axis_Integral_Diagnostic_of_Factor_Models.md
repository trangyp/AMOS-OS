Abstract
This paper extends the cap-axis integral diagnostic to general characteristic axes,
measuring factor-model pricing errors as bridge-alpha curves. A predetermined charac-
teristic order generates prefix portfolios; subtracting equal-exposure aggregate portfo-
lios yields zero-investment bridges indexed by cutoff p. The null is a zero-curve restric-
tion on the subspace generated by the order, not a pointwise decile test. In 1967–2024
CRSP data, adding a counterpart factor shifts each curve downward, but only HML
and CMA/IA overcorrect enough to be rejected, whereas RMW and UMD flatten their
axes. A size-split reconstruction traces the overcorrection to 2 × 3 factor construction
rather than the premium. Axis pricing errors relate weakly to maximum-Sharpe gains.

Introduction
Factor models summarize many cross-sectional return patterns with a small set of common
factors. When this works, the factor span expands the mean–variance opportunity set and
raises the attainable maximum Sharpe ratio. But a larger span and zero pricing errors on
a particular economic coordinate are not the same requirement: a low-dimensional SDF
approximation can be strong along some directions while leaving systematic alphas on a
restricted subspace. Standard GRS and characteristic-sorted portfolio tests ask whether a
model prices a selected set of test assets, but their conclusions depend on portfolio con-
struction and binning, while spanning and maximum-Sharpe criteria compare factor spans
without showing where pricing errors remain. This leaves a gap between model comparison
by factor span and restricted zero-alpha pricing on economically meaningful coordinates.
This paper fills that gap with a characteristic-axis integral diagnostic. Fix a characteristic
before looking at returns, sort stocks by it, and at each cutoff p form the prefix portfolio
and subtract an equal-exposure aggregate portfolio from the same valid subuniverse, giving
a zero-investment bridge. Regressing each bridge return on a factor model gives a cutoff-
specific alpha, and varying p turns these into a bridge-alpha curve, p 7→αx
m(p). A zero curve
means the model prices the prefix, tail, interval, and step-function portfolios generated by the
characteristic order; a nonzero curve shows the sign, size, and location of the pricing errors.
The diagnostic thus converts pricing errors into a functional object rather than a larger
collection of test portfolios: the signed area, IAE, ISE, and sup norm record direction,
total distortion, and largest local error in one coordinate system, and finite-grid differences
show where along the axis the curve is created, flattened, or reversed.
The construction extends the cap-axis diagnostic to general characteristics. Market cap-
italization is only one ordering variable; the same bridge applies to value, operating prof-
itability, investment, momentum, or any characteristic fixed ex ante. Once the axis is fixed
the cutoff portfolios and functionals are determined mechanically—the researcher does not
choose a few decile portfolios, the axis generates the whole path.
I apply the diagnostic to value, operating profitability, investment, and momentum axes
in 1967–2024 CRSP data. The four axes share a recurring sign pattern: models without
the counterpart factor tend to leave positive curves, consistent with undercorrection of the
high-characteristic side, while adding the counterpart factor shifts the curve downward. This

shift is not always a pass—HML and CMA/IA move the value and investment axes past zero
into significant opposite-signed errors, whereas RMW and UMD flatten the profitability and
momentum axes. There is no single model ranking: q5 is stable on value and momentum
but overcorrects on investment, FF5 and FF6 are flattest on profitability but rejected on
value and investment, and Carhart performs well on momentum but not on value. The same
model leaves different pricing-error fingerprints across coordinates.
These fingerprints are also distinct from maximum-Sharpe contributions: in a factor scan,
large ∆SR need not produce low axis IAE, and a factor whose construction is closer to the
tested axis often leaves lower distortion than the canonical counterpart factor. Axis-level
pricing therefore depends not only on whether a model contains a characteristic premium,
but on how that premium is implemented as a portfolio; replication, block-bootstrap, and
finite-sample calibration checks rule out merge, critical-value, and size-distortion explana-
tions. This is not a full SDF test—passing one axis means the model prices the restricted
subspace generated by that characteristic, not all anomaly, industry, or idiosyncratic direc-
tions. By fixing one economic coordinate ex ante, the diagnostic measures where a factor
model undercorrects, overcorrects, or genuinely flattens the pricing-error path at high reso-
lution within that coordinate.

Theoretical Background and Related Literature
Characteristics, factors, and pricing errors.
Many empirical asset-pricing factors orig-
inate from firm characteristics. Size and book-to-market became the core coordinates of
the Fama–French three-factor model (Fama and French, 1992, 1993); momentum was estab-
lished as an independent return-continuation pattern (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993; Carhart,
1997); and investment and profitability became central to investment- and profitability-based
models (Titman et al., 2004; Novy-Marx, 2013; Fama and French, 2015; Hou et al., 2015).
Whether these characteristics proxy for covariances with priced risks or directly describe
expected returns remains a long-standing issue (Daniel and Titman, 1997). This paper does
not resolve that debate; it fixes a characteristic as an economic coordinate and asks how a
factor model’s pricing errors are distributed along it. This matters because covariance fit
and characteristic-coordinate pricing need not coincide in a low-dimensional model: a model
can fit time-series covariances well yet still leave systematic average-return errors along a
3


## Page 4


characteristic order, and the results below show that adding the counterpart factor often
moves the bridge-alpha curve in the expected direction but can also move it past zero into
an opposite-signed error.
Model comparison and Sharpe-ratio criteria.
The growth of multifactor models has
made model comparison central. The Fama–French models (Fama and French, 1993, 2015),
the Carhart model (Carhart, 1997), and the q-factor family (Hou et al., 2015, 2019, 2020,
2021, 2024) explain overlapping return patterns from different economic motivations, and a
leading comparison criterion is factor spanning and the associated maximum Sharpe ratio.
Barillas and Shanken (2017, 2018) show that, for traded-factor models, common test-asset
alphas add little relative to the factor span; Fama and French (2018) discuss factor selection
from a mean–variance perspective, and Kozak et al. (2018) show how a few high-variance
directions can approximate the SDF. The present diagnostic is complementary—it asks a
different question, conditional on a chosen economic axis, where does a model leave pricing
errors?—so I treat maximum-Sharpe improvement and axis-level distortion, measured by
∆SR and IAE, as separate coordinates of model performance.
Test assets and characteristic-sorted portfolios.
Standard alpha tests evaluate pric-
ing errors on a selected set of test assets, including the GRS test (Gibbons et al., 1989),
Hansen–Jagannathan distance (Hansen and Jagannathan, 1997), and SDF-based evaluation
of linear factor models (Cochrane, 2005). Their conclusions can depend on how test assets
are formed: Lewellen et al. (2010) show that characteristic-sorted portfolios with a strong
factor structure can make tests weak or overly forgiving, while Giglio et al. (2025) emphasize
the joint role of test assets and weak factors. Repeated model search adds data-snooping
concerns (Lo and MacKinlay, 1990), and the reproducibility of the factor zoo is itself an
empirical issue (Jensen et al., 2023). The characteristic-axis diagnostic handles test-asset
choice differently: once the characteristic is fixed ex ante, the test assets—the full path
of prefix bridges and their functionals—are generated mechanically by the order and the
aggregate-exposure normalization, not chosen as a few deciles or a single spread. Because
these are zero-investment bridges, the aggregate component is removed before alphas are
estimated, so the test rests on a zero-curve restriction rather than on high R2 in individual
sorted-portfolio regressions.

Functional view of characteristic sorts.
The paper also relates to the functional view
of characteristic-sorted portfolios. Cattaneo et al. (2020) develop nonparametric estimation
and inference for portfolios indexed by characteristics; I use the same perspective for factor-
model diagnosis, estimating a bridge alpha αx

the curve by its signed area, IAE, ISE, and sup norm—direction, total absolute distortion,
quadratic concentration, and largest local error. The object is therefore not a pointwise
alpha test on a few bins, but a restricted zero-alpha condition on the subspace generated by
the selected axis.
Characteristics studied in this paper.
The empirical axes are value, operating prof-
itability, investment, and momentum. The value axis is tied to the value and anomaly-
decomposition literature (Fama and French, 2008); the investment axis follows the asset-
growth evidence of Titman et al. (2004) and the q-factor investment factor (Hou et al.,
2015); the profitability axis relates to gross profitability (Novy-Marx, 2013), RMW (Fama
and French, 2015), and the ROE and expected-growth block in q5 (Hou et al., 2021); and
the momentum axis follows Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) and Carhart (1997). The paper
does not claim these are new anomalies, but uses well-known characteristics as fixed coordi-
nates on which to compare where factor models under-correct, over-correct, or flatten pricing
errors.
Frequency, nonsynchronous trading, and prior diagnostics.
Daily factor regressions
can be affected by nonsynchronous and thin trading, which may distort betas and intercepts
(Scholes and Williams, 1977; Dimson, 1979), so I use monthly results as the baseline and
treat daily results as companion diagnostics rather than reporting lead–lag checks for ev-
ery axis. When a model passes the monthly bridge test but rejects in the daily diagnostic,
I report a Dimson lead–lag correction to separate a stable characteristic-axis pricing er-
ror from short-horizon timing mismatch; in the reported results this matters only for the
operating-profitability axis. Finally, the paper generalizes an earlier diagnostic: Shin (2026)
measures pricing errors inside the investible market as a full cap-axis bridge-alpha curve,
and the present paper extends the same object to general characteristic axes, since market
capitalization is only one ordering variable. The target is not full SDF validity, but pricing
on the restricted subspace generated by the chosen characteristic.

Remark 1 (Measure invariance and the canonical choice). Proposition 1 holds for every ad-
missible measure. The characteristic x fixes the ordering; the measure only fixes how the axis
is traversed and how the aggregate is weighted. Cap weighting (ρ = 1) is canonical because
the aggregate then coincides with the investable market return, so the gate αm(RA,x) = 0
is a statement about pricing the market. Under a general measure the aggregate remains
an implementable buy-and-hold portfolio but loses this market interpretation. Since a valid
SDF must price every implementable return, the diagnostic is well-defined for any admissible
µ, and equal weighting is the special case ρ = 0 rather than a separate construction. The
empirical sections use the canonical ρ = 1 measure for interpretability.
Remark 2 (Interpreting aggregate-gate failure). The equivalence in Proposition 1 depends
on the gate condition αm(RA,x) = 0. If the gate fails, a zero curve means pricing only the
zero-investment bridge subspace span{Dx(p)}, not the full space Vx. In particular, when
the valid subuniverse is defined by the CRSP∩Compustat intersection, aggregate alpha may
reflect coverage selection rather than model failure. Curve results for gate-failing models are
therefore interpreted conditionally on that gate.

3.5
Interpretation and relation to maximum-Sharpe criteria
Passing the diagnostic is a narrow but strong statement. If a model prices the axis aggregate
portfolio and has a zero bridge-alpha curve at all cutoffs, no systematic alpha remains in
any prefix, tail, interval, or step-function portfolio generated by that order. Passing the
13


## Page 14


value axis does not merely mean HML alpha is small; it means that, within the book-to-
market order, any cutoff-defined portfolio is priced. Where a conventional decile test is a
low-resolution inspection, the integral diagnostic traces the full pricing-error path by moving
the cutoff continuously. The statement remains local: it implies neither a valid SDF nor
pricing of all anomalies or industry directions, and with a valid subuniverse it is a statement
about that universe. A pass therefore means not that the model explains the market, but
that it leaves no visible pricing-error structure along the selected characteristic coordinate.
The diagnostic is also distinct from mean–variance performance. For a candidate factor
gt, define the maximum-Sharpe gain as
∆SR(m, g) = SR(m + g) −SR(m).
(30)
∆SR asks how much the factor expands the opportunity set; IAEx

increase ∆SR while leaving a bridge-alpha curve unchanged, and another can add little to
the frontier while sharply reducing axis distortion. I treat the two as complementary, not
competing, model-evaluation criteria.

Data and Methodology
I use daily CRSP stock returns (CRSP, 2026), Compustat annual fundamentals (Compustat,
2026), the Fama–French Data Library (French, 2026), q-factor provider data (Global-q.org,
2026), and Global Factor Data (Global Factor Data, 2026).
The empirical construction
proceeds through three nested universes. First, a month-end market-capitalization and liq-
uidity screen defines the CRSP investible universe. Second, a daily cumulative-capitalization
screen produces the production clean market used to construct the internal market return
cMKT and the common eligibility mask. Third, at each characteristic-formation date, the
production clean market is restricted to stocks with a valid value, profitability, investment,
or momentum signal. The bridge path for axis x is constructed on this characteristic-valid
formation universe.

4.4
Bridge-path implementation
At each formation date, the engine begins with the production clean market mask, applies
the characteristic-validity condition, and sorts the surviving stocks by the predetermined
characteristic. Ties are broken by descending formation-date market equity and then as-
cending PERMNO. Formation weights are proportional to market capitalization within the
selected aggregate mode. The characteristic order and portfolio membership are then frozen
for the holding period: annual accounting axes formed in June are held from July through
the following June, while the momentum axis is formed monthly and held during the next
month. Subsequent changes in the daily production clean mask do not force a stock out of
an already formed axis portfolio.
Within the holding period, positions evolve as start-of-day buy-and-hold holdings. At
each trading date the surviving holdings are normalized to obtain wealth shares wx

the characteristic-valid aggregate, bridge path, and rank-area return are computed from the
same weights. The baseline path contains 201 equally spaced target cutoffs on [0, 1]. Because
stocks are not split at cutoff boundaries, the engine selects the first whole-stock prefix whose
cumulative current wealth share reaches the target p and shorts the realized exposure st(p)
of the same characteristic-valid aggregate.

6
Robustness Checks
This section checks whether the main model-by-axis fingerprints reflect mechanical features
of the implementation or inference. I consider three possibilities: that the axes are distorted
by errors in the CRSP–Compustat merge, accounting timing, return accounting, or break-
points; that the nonlinear functional rejections depend on the finite-grid HAC-GP critical
values; and that the IAE, ISE, and SUP tests behave poorly in finite samples, either over-
rejecting the null or missing structured axis-local alternatives. Accordingly I reconstruct
HML, RMW, and CMA with the Fama–French 2×3 procedure (using the same merge and
return accounting as the main analysis), recompute the functional critical values with a
moving-block bootstrap, and calibrate finite-sample size and power. Exact factor replication
is not expected—small discrepancies remain from book-equity definitions, data revisions,
delisting treatment, breakpoints, and reinvestment rules—and for this paper the underlying
2×3 component portfolios matter more than the final long–short factor, since the axes sort
on the merged accounting variables and formation timing rather than the factor payoff. If
the replications are close, the bootstrap map matches the HAC-GP map, and the calibration
shows near-nominal size with rising power, the main fingerprints are hard to attribute to a
single implementation error, inference rule, or size distortion.

6.4
Summary
The robustness exercises address three mechanical explanations for the model-by-axis fin-
gerprints. First, the anomaly-factor replications validate the CRSP–Compustat merge, ac-
counting timing, breakpoint implementation, and return accounting used to construct the
characteristic axes. The replicated HML correlations are 0.962 daily and 0.971 monthly, the
corresponding RMW correlations are 0.941 and 0.959, and the CMA correlations are 0.945
and 0.959. Their regression betas range from 0.911 to 1.008, while the annualized replication
intercepts range from −28.8 to −9.2 bp. These residual differences preclude a claim of exact
factor replication, but the underlying 2×3 component portfolios—the objects most directly
determined by the merge, formation timing, and breakpoint rules—correlate 0.979–0.998
with their published counterparts. The bridge results are therefore difficult to attribute to
a gross error in the accounting merge or anomaly-portfolio implementation.
Second, the nonlinear functional conclusions do not depend on the HAC-GP approxi-
mation alone. The six-month moving-block residual bootstrap reproduces all 18 HAC-GP
model–functional decisions on the value, investment, and momentum axes. The only differ-
ences occur on the OP axis, where the borderline CAPM IAE and ISE p-values move from
45


## Page 46


approximately 0.06 under HAC-GP to approximately 0.05 under the bootstrap. FF5 and
FF6 remain the flattest OP-axis models, and the overall rejection fingerprint is unchanged.
Third, the finite-sample calibration provides no evidence of systematic over-rejection. Un-
der the zero-curve null, average rejection frequencies range from 3.6% to 5.4% under HAC-GP
and from 5.1% to 5.3% under the block bootstrap. The independently evaluated bootstrap
cell-level frequencies remain between 4.4% and 6.5%, while HAC-GP is mildly conservative
in some momentum cells. Average rejection then rises monotonically as the strength of the
model-specific empirical-shape alternative increases. This establishes approximate size con-
trol and local power against structured deviations proportional to the observed bridge-alpha
curves, without claiming uniform power against every possible alternative.
The bridge construction is verified separately by the accounting identities in Section 4.4:
each run checks endpoint closure, body–tail recombination, and agreement between the
grid-integrated bridge path and the midpoint rank-area return up to whole-stock boundary
approximation error. The momentum axis provides an additional empirical benchmark be-
cause it has nearly complete coverage and recovers the expected distinction between models
with and without UMD, while the cap axis is examined separately in Shin (2026). Taken to-
gether, the replication, alternative critical values, independent size calibration, and internal
accounting checks make the main fingerprints difficult to attribute to a single implementation
error, covariance approximation, Gaussian simulation step, step or test-size distortion.
7
Discussion
7.1
Interpreting overcorrection
A recurring empirical pattern is that adding the counterpart factor shifts the bridge-alpha
curve downward on its own axis. This shift sometimes flattens the curve, as with RMW and
UMD, but sometimes carries it past zero into a significant negative region, as with HML
and the investment factors. One interpretation is a mismatch between the factor portfolio
and the coordinate on which it is evaluated. Bridge-regression loadings are determined by
time-series covariances, whereas the diagnostic measures remaining mean pricing errors along
a cap-weighted characteristic-rank coordinate. A factor can therefore explain much of the
time-series variation in the axis payoff without eliminating its mean pricing-error curve. The
46


## Page 47


investment axis illustrates this separation: FF5, FF6, and q5 attain rank-area R2
Y between
0.70 and 0.78 while leaving significant negative rank-area alphas.
The size-split-free investment reconstruction provides evidence that this result is sensitive
to how the investment signal is packaged. Replacing CMA with CMA′, which uses the same
underlying asset-growth ordering without an explicit small–big portfolio split, moves the FF5
rank-area alpha from −20.1 to −1.9 bp and reduces IAE from 21.1 to 6.5 bp. At the same
time, rank-area R2
Y rises from 0.782 to 0.826. The reconstructed factor retains substantial
investment content—its loading on CMA is approximately 1.1–1.3—while its SMB loading is
small. Thus the negative investment-axis curve is not an unavoidable consequence of includ-
ing an asset-growth signal: a differently constructed portfolio carrying similar investment
exposure can leave substantially less axis distortion.
This comparison is informative but is not a one-parameter causal decomposition of the
size split. Removing the explicit small–big split also changes the cross-size aggregation and
stock-level weights of the factor portfolio. The experiment therefore identifies factor im-
plementation broadly, rather than size neutralization alone, as an important source of the
difference. The result is consistent with a size-split channel, but it does not establish that
the size split is the unique cause of the original negative curve. The factor scan gives comple-
mentary descriptive evidence: investment factors such as INV GR1 and NOA GR1A, which
are more directly aligned with the axis sorting variable, leave lower IAE than CMA or IA;
similarly, OPE/BE and intermediate momentum leave lower distortion than the representa-
tive profitability and momentum factors. These comparisons suggest that axis-level pricing
depends on portfolio construction and coordinate alignment in addition to characteristic
exposure.
The contrast across counterpart factors also shows that a size split is not sufficient to
generate significant overcorrection. RMW and UMD use size-balanced constructions related
to those of HML and CMA, yet their own-axis curves remain within the statistical noise
range. A possible explanation is that the effect of size balancing interacts with heterogeneity
in the characteristic premium along the rank coordinate: a correction calibrated to an aggre-
gate spread may flatten one axis while overshooting regions of another. The present bridge
evidence is consistent with this mechanism but does not identify it separately. Establishing
that channel would require comparable size-split-free reconstructions for value, profitability,
and momentum, or a direct decomposition of each factor into characteristic and cross-size
47


## Page 48


weighting components.
7.2
Meaning as a functional diagnostic
The main advantage of the diagnostic is that it turns pricing errors into a functional object
rather than a set of pointwise alphas. Where standard bin tests assign one alpha per bin, the
diagnostic moves the cutoff p and estimates the full curve αx
m(p), of which SA, IAE, ISE,
and SUP are different functionals rather than separate tests. This is more informative than a
rejection table: two models can share a signed area while leaving very different geometries—
one a smooth one-sided curve, another an oscillating curve with cancelling regions—and
whether a counterpart factor flattens the curve or pushes it past zero is visible only at the
curve level. The structure also suggests further uses: finite-grid differences locate where
errors are created or reversed; IAE or ISE can serve as an objective for factor construction,
though optimizing on a single axis may trade off distortion on the others, so a cross-axis
constraint is the more appropriate target; and a zero curve or low integral norm can act as
a model-selection constraint. The diagnostic is thus not only an ex post test but a way to
convert a model’s residual pricing-error geometry into a computable function.
7.3
Relation to existing tests
The diagnostic does not replace existing comparisons; it asks a different question. The GRS
test asks whether a model prices a given test-asset set, and maximum-Sharpe and span-
ning comparisons ask how much investment opportunity a factor span provides, whereas the
characteristic-axis diagnostic asks whether the zero-alpha condition holds on the restricted
subspace generated by a pre-specified characteristic. The empirical results show these an-
swers need not coincide: the rank correlation between candidate factors’ ∆SR and axis
IAE is essentially zero on value and only weakly negative on OP and investment, so the
largest-∆SR factor is not the smallest-IAE one. Against traditional decile or bin tests, the
difference is resolution: a coarse-bin test observes average alpha within each bin, while the
bridge-alpha curve observes the full path and detects positive and negative errors that cancel
within bins or local distortions concentrated in a rank region. The daily q5 result on OP
illustrates this—the signed area is weak but IAE reacts more strongly, flagging local curve
structure a single portfolio alpha would miss, which the lead–lag diagnostic then attributes to
48


## Page 49


short-horizon timing (Section 5.2.3). The functional view surfaces structure worth a second
look, and the conditional monthly–daily protocol classifies it.
7.4
What the rejection identifies
Because the size-split reconstruction traces overcorrection to factor construction, it is worth
stating precisely what a rejection claims.
The rank-area portfolio is cap-weighted along
the coordinate while the counterpart factors are size-balanced, so one reading is that a
rejection merely records a weighting mismatch rather than a pricing failure. I read it the
other way, but narrowly. A valid SDF must price every implementable return, including
the cap-weighted prefix and bridge portfolios generated by the axis (Proposition 1), so a
size-balanced factor that leaves systematic alpha on them has, by definition, failed to price
implementable returns along that coordinate. The rejection is therefore real, but its scope
is exactly this: it localizes where a factor’s implementation—how the premium is packaged
into a tradeable portfolio—fails to price a pre-specified cap-weighted coordinate, not that the
model is economically misspecified or the premium mispriced in the economy. This is why
a rejection and a large ∆SR can coexist: the factor expands the frontier while its size-split
packaging leaves a cap-weighted rank residual.
7.5
Scope of the test
The scope is limited in three ways. First, accounting-based axes are statements about the
CRSP∩Compustat valid subuniverse, not the entire investible market; the aggregate gate
makes this explicit, so a clean gate lets a bridge rejection be read within the valid subuni-
verse while a failed gate requires separating aggregate-level selection alpha from bridge-level
pricing error. Second, the verdicts are axis-specific: passing one axis does not imply passing
another—q5 is stable on value and momentum but rejected on investment, FF5 and FF6
flattest on OP but rejected on value and investment—so the diagnostic yields a model-by-
axis fingerprint, not a global ranking. Third, a pass is restricted to the subspace generated
by the axis: it covers the prefix, tail, interval, and step-function portfolios of that order,
a strong condition within the axis, but implies nothing about industry, other-anomaly, or
idiosyncratic directions, and conversely a rejection identifies a pricing-error structure on that
coordinate rather than a failure of the whole model. These restrictions are design features,
49


## Page 50


not defects: the strength of the diagnostic is completeness within a narrow scope, fixing
one economic coordinate and testing its entire bridge path instead of an arbitrary test-asset
set. The central empirical result is that, within these restricted spaces, factor models leave
residual pricing errors of different shape, sign, and magnitude across characteristic axes.
8
Conclusion
This paper proposes a characteristic-axis integral diagnostic for evaluating factor-model pric-
ing along a single pre-specified characteristic coordinate. The diagnostic sorts stocks by the
characteristic, forms a prefix portfolio at each cutoff p, and subtracts an equal-exposure
aggregate portfolio to obtain a zero-investment bridge; bridge alphas estimated over cutoffs
produce the bridge-alpha curve αx
m(p). The null is therefore not a finite list of portfolio
alphas, but a restricted zero-curve condition for the prefix, tail, interval, and step-function
portfolios generated by the characteristic order.
The empirical results yield no single global model ranking: q5 is stable on value and mo-
mentum but overcorrects on investment, FF5 and FF6 are flattest on operating profitability
but rejected on value and investment, and Carhart performs well on momentum but not on
value. A recurring pattern is sign reversal—models without the counterpart factor tend to
leave positive curves, consistent with undercorrection of the high-characteristic side, while
adding it shifts the curve negative, though not always to a pass. RMW and UMD flatten
the operating-profitability and momentum axes, whereas HML and CMA/IA carry the value
and investment curves past zero into significant opposite-signed errors. Passing therefore de-
pends not only on whether the model contains the characteristic premium, but on whether
the factor’s construction is aligned with the tested coordinate—and the size-split reconstruc-
tion on the investment axis shows this directly, tracing the overcorrection to CMA’s 2 × 3
construction rather than to investment information, with size neutralization one plausible
component.
The factor scan reinforces that maximum-Sharpe gains and axis distortion are different
objects: some factors expand the opportunity set while leaving large IAE, and factors more
directly tied to the sorting variable—OPE/BE on operating profitability, NOA GR1A and
INV GR1 on investment, intermediate momentum on momentum—often leave lower distor-
tion than the representative factors. Axis-level pricing thus depends on how a premium
50


## Page 51


is implemented as a portfolio. Replication, block-bootstrap, and finite-sample calibration
checks rule out merge, critical-value, and size-distortion explanations, so the diagnostic be-
haves as a functional test with approximate size control rather than a merely descriptive
curve.
The diagnostic is not a full SDF test. Passing one axis means the model prices the re-
stricted subspace generated by that characteristic—not all industry, anomaly, or idiosyncratic
directions—and the accounting-based axes concern the CRSP∩Compustat valid subuniverse.
These restrictions are intentional: by fixing one economic coordinate ex ante, the diagnostic
measures the direction, magnitude, location, and local concentration of pricing errors at high
resolution, and what a rejection identifies is correspondingly narrow—where a factor’s imple-
mentation fails to price a cap-weighted coordinate, not that the model is globally misspecified
or the premium mispriced in the economy. The diagnostic is therefore a complement to GRS,
spanning, and maximum-Sharpe comparisons, not a substitute: those ask whether a model
prices a test-asset set or how much its span expands the opportunity set, while this asks
where, and in what shape, pricing errors remain along a selected coordinate. Its advantage
is that the question is functionally closed—the bridge-alpha curve is a pricing-error function
admitting integrals, norms, differences, and optimization criteria. Low-dimensional factor
models can price the aggregate market or improve the maximum-Sharpe frontier while still
leaving systematic residual pricing-error geometry inside specific characteristic subspaces;
revealing that geometry as an axis-by-axis fingerprint is the main contribution of the paper.
