I.
INTRODUCTION
Muon-catalyzed fusion (µCF) provides a rare low-
temperature route to nuclear fusion by replacing an elec-
tron in a hydrogen isotope with a negative muon, thereby
shrinking the characteristic atomic or molecular length
scale by roughly the muon-to-electron mass ratio. In a
deuterium–tritium mixture, the dtµ molecule is the most
favorable catalytic species: intramolecular D–T fusion re-
leases an alpha particle, a 14.1 MeV neutron, and, in most
events, a muon that may initiate another cycle. The same
process also contains its intrinsic loss channel, because a
fraction of the emitted muons remain bound to the alpha
particle as an αµ state and are removed from the cycle un-
less reactivated. The basic catalytic cycle, together with
the dominant loss and reactivation channels, is illustrated
in Fig. 1. Since the early proposal and observation of
muon-induced nuclear reactions, µCF has been studied as
both a few-body atomic-nuclear problem and a potential
source of fusion energy or intense fusion neutrons [1–6].
Recent coupled-channel and T-matrix calculations of the
(dtµ)J=v=0 reaction have refined the microscopic inputs,
including the intramolecular fusion rate, the initial alpha-
sticking probability, and the emitted-muon spectrum [7, 8].

Lawson argument. Section III introduces the energy and
gain boundaries, including the conditional sticking no-go

and prospective cases onto the closure map. Section V
discusses the implications for energy-oriented µCF and
neutron-source applications.
II.
FROM LAWSON BALANCE TO
MUON-CYCLE CLOSURE
The classical Lawson criterion is not used here as a
variable-by-variable template for µCF. Its role is more
limited and more physical: it identifies what a closure
criterion must accomplish. In a thermonuclear plasma, a
large microscopic fusion cross section is insufficient unless
the reacting fuel remains available long enough, at suffi-
cient density and temperature, for the released energy to
compensate the losses [11]. A D–T µCF system has a dif-
ferent state variable, a different residence mechanism, and
a different dominant loss structure. The relevant object is
not a confined thermal plasma element, but the stochastic
life history of one negative muon as it cycles through
muonic-atom formation, dtµ formation, fusion, release,
possible αµ sticking, reactivation, and ordinary muon
decay [4–6]. Therefore the µCF closure criterion must
be derived from the renewal balance of this single-muon
cycle itself, not from a formal substitution of nτET by
another product. The assumptions made below are corre-
spondingly minimal: the many-cycle history of one useful
muon is represented by an effective cycle-completion rate
Λc, ordinary muon decay provides the dominant lifetime
scale λµ = 1/τµ, and all post-fusion alpha-sticking and
reactivation physics is summarized by a residual effective
sticking probability ωeff
S . Additional non-catalytic losses,
such as nuclear capture in a bound muonic atom, can be
absorbed into an effective loss rate without changing the
algebraic form of the reduced criterion.
Under these assumptions, the average fusion yield fol-
lows from the renewal balance of the active muon pop-
ulation. While a muon remains available for catalysis,
effective cycle completions occur with rate Λc; each com-
pleted cycle produces one D–T fusion event and returns
the muon to the active pool with probability 1−ωeff
S . The
same active muon is also removed from the active popu-

λµ = 1/τµ. Hence the total removal rate from the active
catalytic population is λµ + ωeff

production rate per active muon is Λc. The mean number

The map should therefore be read as a classification

formation, target compression, transport, and stopping
efficiency primarily change the effective cycle-completion
rate or the useful-muon cost [12–20]. At the same time,
sub-breakeven regions of the map are not irrelevant: a
system with Gµ < 1 may still be valuable as an intense
D–T neutron source if the objective is isotope production,
irradiation, or transmutation rather than net energy pro-
duction. The following discussion uses this separation to
assess which physical improvement is rate-limited, which
is sticking-limited, and which is primarily cost-limited.
V.
DISCUSSION AND OUTLOOK
The closure map should be understood as a reduced
diagnostic criterion, not as a complete reactor model. Its
main role is to reorganize the usual yield discussion into
three physically distinct quantities: the cycle strength Lµ,
the residual effective sticking probability ωeff
S , and the
energy-demand parameter NL = Ecost
µ
/(ηsysEuse). In this
