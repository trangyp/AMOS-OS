Abstract
We introduce a new notion of distance between two graph states |G⟩and |G′⟩on the same set of
qubits. This distance is the minimum number of ancilla qubits in a graph state | bG⟩from which both
|G⟩and |G′⟩can be “easily prepared”. (When preparing graph states, we are only allowed to use
one-qubit Clifford gates, one-qubit Pauli measurements, and classical communication.) We give a
graphical description of this distance through the lens of vertex-minors. We then show how this
distance yields quantum network analogs of many graph edit-distance problems.
Using this framework, we develop classical algorithms for identifying the “highly entangled
clusters” of a graph state |G⟩. The ancilla integrity problem asks, given a graph G and integer
k, for the minimum – over all graph states |G′⟩with distance at most k from |G⟩– of the maximum
component size of G′. Up to a factor of 2 in the number of ancilla qubits, this problem is equivalent
to rank integrity, where the distance between G and G′ is instead the minimum rank of the sum
of their adjacency matrices over GF(2). We prove that rank integrity is XP parameterized by k.
We also prove the complementary hardness result that rank integrity is W[1]-hard in k. Finally,
we give an explicit O(n6)-time algorithm for ancilla integrity when G has n vertices and k = 1.

Introduction
There is a vast literature of graph edit distance problems where the goal is to transform
a given graph G into a member of some target graph class by making as few changes as
possible. The most commonly studied changes are vertex and edge deletion and insertion.
This framework is very flexible and also includes other graph modification and connectivity
augmentation problems such as those studied in [17, 50]. How much more “connectivity” can
be added to a road network by building a few more lanes? How much can be destroyed by
putting a few lanes out of service? In this manner, graph edit distance problems can be
used to “cluster” a graph into “highly connected pieces” and to compute its “resiliency”,

Many different notions of edit distance, many different target graph classes, and many
different measures of connectivity have been considered; see [22, 36, 39] for surveys. Edit
distance problems such as feedback vertex set – can k vertices be deleted from G to
obtain a forest? – also form the foundation of modern parameterized complexity.
In this paper we introduce a new notion of edit distance for graphs/graph states which
is motivated by quantum networking. It captures whether a network provider can use a
few ancilla qubits to transform one distributed quantum network into another. To explain
this idea, consider two n-qubit graph states |G⟩and |G′⟩on the same set of qubits V . We
consider |G⟩and |G′⟩to be “similar” if a network provider can prepare a graph state | bG⟩on
the disjoint union of V and a small set of ancilla qubits A so that the following holds. When
the network provider keeps A and distributes V among n different parties, everyone can
collaboratively work together to prepare whichever of |G⟩, |G′⟩is desired (without requiring
any additional entanglement). Thus, by consuming a few ancilla qubits, we can choose
whether to prepare |G⟩or |G′⟩on the fly, that is, after the network has been distributed.
More formally, we define the distance d(|G⟩, |G′⟩) between two graph states on the same
vertex set to be the minimum number of ancilla qubits in a graph state | bG⟩from which each of
|G⟩and |G′⟩can be prepared deterministically using only one-qubit Clifford gates, one-qubit
Pauli measurements1, and classical communication. We add these additional restrictions
on the allowable operations in order to make the problem more manageable2. With this
definition in hand, we have a new notion of “edit distance” for quantum graph states.
We can obtain a completely graphical characterization of this new distance by applying
some known results about graph states [24, 27, 44]. This graphical characterization agrees
with the notion of “perturbations” which was recently introduced by [16] in the context of
vertex-minors. The vertex-minors of a graph G are the graphs that can be obtained from G
by taking induced subgraphs (i.e. deleting vertices) and by performing certain operations
called local complementations; locally complementing at a vertex v of G replaces the induced
subgraph on the neighborhood of v by its complement. That is, it “switches” edges and
non-edges within the neighborhood of v. Given two n-vertex graphs G and G′ on the same
vertex set, we define the distance d(G, G′) as the minimum integer k so that there exists a
graph on n + k vertices which contains both G and G′ as vertex-minors. Then a graph G′ is
a k-perturbation of a graph G if d(G, G′) ≤k.
It follows from [24, 27, 44] that these two notions of distance coincide.
▶Observation 1.1. For any graphs G and G′ on the same vertex set, d(|G⟩, |G′⟩) = d(G, G′).

To perform a flip on a graph G, we select an arbitrary set of vertices S of G, and replace
the induced subgraph on S by its complement. That is, we “switch” edges and non-edges
within S; see Figure 1. Note that flipping on S is the same thing as adding a new vertex v
with neighborhood S, locally complementing at v, and then deleting v. This operation is
also sometimes called partial complementation or subgraph complementation.
Various “flip-based” graph parameters have recently been used to efficiently decide first-
order properties of graphs [29, 35, 69]. Indeed, this area has many deep connections to model
theory which arise from the fact that performing a bounded number of flips is a quantifier-free
transduction; see [21, 63]. There is also a somewhat distinct line of recent research which
studies the time complexity of flipping to a target graph class; see [1, 2, 3, 32]. We use

then these problems have a nice quantum interpretation. Sen, Goodenough, and Towsley [67]
used a similar flip-based approach to distribute a graph state across a quantum network.
However, the connection to graph edit distance problems seems to be unexplored.
Let us explain this connection through the key example of ancilla integrity. The
k-ancilla-integrity of a graph state |G⟩is the minimum – over all graph states |G′⟩with
d(|G⟩, |G′⟩) ≤k – of the maximum component size of G′. (The size of a component is the
number of vertices it contains.) The ancilla integrity problem asks, given a graph G and
integer k, to compute the k-ancilla-integrity of G. The components of G′ can naturally be
interpreted as the “highly entangled clusters” of G; they form the optimal way to “disentangle”
the qubits of |G⟩using only k ancilla qubits. Indeed, the k-ancilla-integrity of |G⟩is small if
and only if G can “almost” be written as a tensor product of graph states which each only
have a small number of qubits. We show in Section 3 how this notion relates to another
previously studied parameter of graph states called Pauli persistency [13, 44].
Now, consider again the example in Figure 1 of a graph G′ which is obtained from G by
flipping. In general, given graphs G and G′ on the same vertex set V , we write G+G′ for the
graph on V whose edge set is the symmetric difference of the edge sets of G and G′. So in
Figure 1, this graph G + G′ is a clique plus some isolated vertices. The adjacency matrix of a
clique has minimum rank 1; the minimum rank of a graph is the minimum – over all ways of
filling in the diagonal of the adjacency matrix with 0s and 1s – of its rank over GF(2). It is
almost true that for any graphs G and G′ on the same vertex set, G′ can be obtained from
G by flipping on at most k sets if and only if G + G′ has minimum rank at most k. The
only difference is that sometimes one additional flip is required [51]; see also [14] and [26,
Lemma 4.1]. Since there are other nice connections between rank in the adjacency matrix
and entanglement in the graph state (see [44, 70]), we focus on the rank-based definition3.

So, we define the k-rank-integrity of a graph G to be the minimum – over all graphs G′ so
that G + G′ has minimum rank at most k – of the maximum component size of G′. We call
any such graph G′ a rank-k perturbation of G. Thus a rank-k perturbation of G is obtained
from G by adding a symmetric binary matrix of rank at most k to its adjacency matrix, and
then setting the diagonal to be 0. The rank integrity problem asks, given a graph G and
integer k, to compute the k-rank-integrity of G. We prove the following lemma in Section 3.
▶Lemma 1.2. For any graph G and non-negative integer k,
(2k)-rank-integrity(G) ≤k-ancilla-integrity(|G⟩) ≤k-rank-integrity(G).
Thus, rank integrity and ancilla integrity capture the same underlying notion, up to a
change in the parameter. Accordingly, translating between the two notions incurs at most a
factor-of-two change in the parameter k. Importantly, the factor of two appears only in the
parameter, not in a direct comparison of the resulting integrity values.
We note that the same relationship holds for any target graph class or target graph
parameter which is invariant under local complementation.
(Note that the maximum
component size of a graph is invariant under local complementation.) There are many such
invariants since cut-rank (or equivalently, Schmidt-rank of the corresponding graph state [43,
Proposition 10]) is invariant under local complementation; see [8, 44, 45]. Many such graph
parameters also have nice quantum interpretations, including twin classes [15], rank-width [71,
70], linear rank-width [54], and minimum degree up to local complementation [18, 47].
Our first main result (and the heart of this paper) is the following theorem.
▶Theorem 1.3. rank integrity is XP parameterized by k.
That is, there is an algorithm which takes as input an n-vertex graph G and integer k
and computes the k-rank-integrity of G in time nf(k) for some computable function f.
Even when k = 1 and we are looking for a flip G′ of G, the naive approach is to iterate
over all 2n subsets of the vertex set; obtaining a polynomial bound is surprisingly nontrivial.
However, note that for graphs G where the optimal flip G′ of G has exactly two components,
we are equivalently looking for a “most balanced split” of G. A split is a partition of the vertex
set of a graph into two parts so that the edges between them form the edge-set of a complete
bipartite graph. It is most balanced if the two parts are as equal in size as possible. Efficient
algorithms to find the “split decomposition” of a graph are well-known [20, 23, 25, 68]. The
split decomposition is a tree that “displays” all of the splits of a graph simultaneously. So,
at an intuitive level, the main idea of our algorithm is to first reduce to the two component
case, and then to do some dynamic programming using the split decomposition.
The approach for general k is somewhat similar, however we do not have access to
something like the split decomposition which displays all of the relevant information at once.
To explain our approach, let us say that a cut of a graph G is a partition (A, B) of its vertex
set into two parts. The rank (or cut-rank) of (A, B) is the rank over GF(2) of the submatrix
of the adjacency matrix with rows A and columns B. The most balanced cut-rank
problem asks, given a graph G and integer k, for the most balanced cut of rank at most
k. Very recently, Bojańczyk, Mi. Pilipczuk, Przybyszewski, Sokołowski, and Stamoulis [5,
Theorem 6.1] proved a theorem which implies that most balanced cut-rank is XP in k.
(This theorem has also been generalized to arbitrary connectivity functions [46].) Essentially,
this theorem shows that every cut of rank at most k is displayed in one of polynomially-many
(in the number of vertices n) partitions of the vertex set.
Thus our main contribution is handling the case where the optimal rank-k perturbation
G′ of G has many components. We note that even when G′ has exactly three components, it

That is, we cannot expect to find an algorithm which takes in an n-vertex graph G and
an integer k and computes the k-rank-integrity of G in time f(k)nc for some fixed constant
c and computable function f. (Doing so is at least as hard as determining if an n-vertex
graph has a clique of size k.) We reduce directly from clique. Our reduction is inspired by
the proof in [28] that component order connectivity is W[1]-hard parameterized by k.
In this problem, given a graph G and integer k, we wish to compute the k-order-integrity of
G. The k-order-integrity of G is the minimum – over all sets X ⊆V (G) of size at most k –
of the maximum component size of G −X.
We note that rank-integrity is really a “dense analog” of order-integrity in that for graphs
without a Kt,t-subgraph, they only differ in a change of the parameter (see Lemma 3.6). This
is an instance of a more general trend in the area of “structural sparsity”; we refer the reader
to the recent survey of Mi. Pilipczuk [63] for more examples. We note that Lemma 3.6 does
not seem strong enough to obtain Theorem 1.4 directly; we will have to work harder.
Finally, we give some evidence that computing rank integrity could be a useful
intermediate step towards obtaining an exact XP algorithm for ancilla integrity. We
take this approach to obtain an explicit algorithm for computing 1-ancilla integrity.
▶Theorem 1.5. The 1-ancilla-integrity of an n-vertex graph G can be computed in time O(n6).
We conjecture that, like rank integrity, the ancilla integrity problem is XP and
W[1]-hard in k. We note that this combinatorial framework for edit distance problems on
quantum networks leads to a whole host of interesting questions. For instance, one can ask
the following connectivity augmentation problem; what is the minimum integer k so that G
has a rank-k perturbation which is prime? (A graph is prime if it has no non-trivial splits.)
From the matrix viewpoint it is also natural to ask whether the following problem is XP in
k; given an n × m binary matrix M, what is the minimum – over all n × m binary matrices
N of rank at most k – of the maximum block size of M + N? (The size of a block is its
number of rows plus columns.) It also seems unlikely that replacing “maximum component
size” with “average component size” could change the complexity of the rank integrity
problem much. Can we capture all of these problems in one language and prove that every
problem which can be stated in that language is polynomial-time solvable?

2.3
Overview of the XP algorithm for rank integrity
As is standard for XP algorithms, we describe the algorithm in terms of a bounded number
of “guesses”, thus yielding a polynomial number of “guessed objects”. Formally, each guess
corresponds to a brute-force enumeration over all possibilities, and the algorithm returns
the best solution found over all non-rejected branches. A roadmap of our algorithm can be
found in Algorithm 1.
Recall that our goal is, given a graph G and integer k, to find a rank-k perturbation
G′ of G that minimizes the maximum component size of G′. It is convenient to introduce
looped graphs to store this perturbation more succinctly; a looped graph is a graph where
every vertex is allowed to have at most one loop. Thus we can equivalently describe a rank-k
perturbation G′ of G in the form G′ = G + bG where bG is a looped graph of rank at most k;
the rank of a looped graph is the rank of its adjacency matrix over GF(2). We now consider
the resulting graph G′ to also be looped; this does not change its maximum component size.
Fix a rank-k perturbation G + G∗of G that minimizes the maximum component size. To
design an XP algorithm, we would like to be able to describe G∗using only a bounded number
of vertices of G and bounded additional information. For this, we need some definitions.
In a looped graph H, the neighborhood of a vertex v is the set of vertices that are adjacent
to v in H. So we consider v to be in its own neighborhood if and only if there is a loop

The graph state picture
In quantum information, qubits are the quantum equivalent of classical bits, and an n-qubit
quantum state is identified with a complex vector with 2n coordinates. Graph states are
a family of quantum states that are in one-to-one correspondence with simple undirected
graphs. Given a graph G defined on the vertex set V , the corresponding graph state is
defined by |G⟩= P
X⊆V (−1)eX |1⟩X |0⟩V \X, where eX denotes the number of edges in the
subgraph induced by X. Graph states were originally introduced as a universal resource for
measurement-based quantum computing [12, 64, 65]. However, they have seen recent interest
as a simple but useful tool in quantum networking [41], as they are useful for cryptographic
protocols, a prime example being quantum secret sharing [4, 38, 48, 49, 56].
Typically, a graph state is prepared by a network provider, which sends a qubit to each
each party in the network. Then, only local quantum operations are allowed, for example one-
qubit gates or one-qubit measurements. It is standard to consider only the so-called Clifford
operations: one-qubit Clifford gates and one-qubit Pauli measurements. These are in general
simpler to implement than arbitrary local operations. Classical communication between
distant parties is also allowed; it is in general needed to correct according to the (probabilistic)
measurement outcomes. The graphical interpretation of these “simple” local operations,
given by the vertex-minor formalism, gives tools to easily manipulate entanglement across
the network [11, 19, 30, 34, 42, 55, 59], or to identify bottlenecks [40].
▶Proposition 3.1 ([24, 27, 44]). A graph H is a vertex-minor of a graph G if and only
if |G⟩transforms deterministically into |H⟩via one-qubit Pauli measurements, one-qubit
Clifford gates, and classical communication.

Discussion on ancilla-integrity
The k-ancilla-integrity of a graph state |G⟩defined on qubit set V is the minimum integer t
such that there exists a graph state | bG⟩defined on qubit set V ∪A such that bG −A = G,
and such that there exist one-qubit Pauli measurements on A that deterministically map | bG⟩
to a tensor product of quantum states with at most t qubits.
This definition is equivalent to the definition given in the introduction. Indeed, the one-
qubit Pauli measurements map | bG⟩to a quantum state that is a graph state up to one-qubit
Clifford gates, which do not modify entanglement (see for example [44]). Conversely, suppose
that |G⟩and |G′⟩can both be prepared from a graph state | bG⟩on qubit set V ∪A using
one-qubit Pauli measurements, one-qubit Clifford gates, and local communication. Then we
may switch the order of these operations so that bG −A = G and |G′⟩can be obtained from
bG by first performing one-qubit Pauli measurements and then one-qubit Clifford gates.

of 2 in the number of ancilla qubits. We say that two graphs G and G′ on the same vertex

complementations.

Comparison with Pauli persistency
We compare ancilla-integrity to another natural notion of integrity for graph states, inspired

▶Definition 3.3 ([13, 44]). The Pauli persistency of a graph state |G⟩is the minimum

G is locally equivalent to a graph G′ with a set X of at most k vertices so that G′ −X is an
edgeless graph. The definition of Pauli persistency requires that the target quantum state is
completely disentangled. We may ask a relaxed question: what is the smallest integer k such
that k one-qubit Pauli measurements on the qubits of |G⟩deterministically maps |G⟩to a

the graph state, and not on the ancilla qubits.

is much weaker: sometimes a Pauli measurement on a single ancilla qubit is enough to
transform the graph state into a union of disconnected quantum states with a small number
of qubits, but many Pauli measurements on the qubits of the systems are needed to do so.

1-ancilla-integrity ≤k + 1, but for which any k one-qubit Pauli measurements lead to a
quantum state with a connected component containing at least t qubits.

set in the partition contains exactly k + 1 vertices; so G is of order m(k + 1). An analysis
of the orbit of G by local complementations is given in [16, Lemma 7.3]. One can check
that deleting k vertices from any locally equivalent graph leads to a graph with a connected
component containing at least m vertices.
Conversely, adding a vertex a to G connected to every vertex, then locally complementing

Computing 1-ancilla-integrity
In this section we prove Theorem 1.5 about computing the 1-ancilla-integrity of a graph.
First we show how to reduce this problem to computing flip-integrity. Recall that the flip-
integrity of a graph G is the minimum, over all sets S ⊆V (G), of the maximum component
size of the graph G ◦S which is obtained from G by flipping on S. For convenience, given
a graph G, we say that an optimal flip set is a set S∗⊆V (G) so that the largest size of a
component of G ◦S∗equals the flip-integrity of G. Recall that, given a graph G and a vertex
v of G, we write G ∗v for the graph obtained from G by locally complementing at v.
▶Lemma 6.1. The 1-ancilla-integrity of a graph G equals the minimum, over all graphs G′
obtained from G by performing at most one local complementation, of the flip-integrity of G′.

Proof. First we prove that the 1-ancilla-integrity of G is at most the claimed value. There

It just remains to show that the claimed value is at most the 1-ancilla-integrity of G.
So, consider a graph G∗which is a 1-perturbation of G which minimizes the maximum
component size. Thus there exists a graph bG with one additional vertex so that both G and
G∗are vertex-minors of bG. By locally complementing in bG, we may assume that G is an
induced subgraph of bG, that is, there exists a vertex a so that G = bG −a. By Lemma 2.1,
the graph G∗is a vertex-minor of at least one of the following three graphs:
1. the graph obtained from bG by deleting a,
2. the graph obtained from bG by locally complementing at a and then deleting a, or
3. the graph obtained from bG by selecting an arbitrary neighbor u of a, locally complementing
on u then a then u again, and finally deleting a.

6.2
Finding a most balanced split
Recall that in a graph G, a cut is a partition (A, B) of the vertex set into two parts. We
consider non-oriented cuts, in the sense that we make no distinction between the cut (A, B)
and the cut (B, A). A cut (A, B) is a split if the edges between A and B form the edge-set
of a complete bipartite graph. Said differently, every vertex in A that has a neighbor in B is
adjacent to every vertex in B that has a neighbor in A. (If there are no edges between A
and B, then we also consider this to be a split.) The width of a cut (A, B) is max{|A|, |B|}.
Observe that a most balanced split in G is a split of minimum width.
This subsection is dedicated to proving the following result.
▶Theorem 6.5. There is an algorithm which takes as input a graph G with n vertices and
returns a split of G of minimum width in time O(n2).
To find a most balanced split of G, we rely on split decompositions, which were first
introduced by Cunningham [23]. Note that a graph may contain an exponential number of
splits; for instance if the graph is a clique or star, then every cut is a split. However, the
split decomposition is an object of polynomial size which “displays” all of the splits of G
simultaneously. To describe the split decomposition, we need some more definitions.
A split (A, B) of an n-vertex graph is trivial if it has width at least n −1, that is, if one
of A, B has size at most one. Otherwise the split is nontrivial. A graph is prime if it has no
nontrivial split. Two splits (A, B) and (C, D) cross if all four sets A ∩C, A ∩D, B ∩C, B ∩D
are nonempty. A split is strong if it does not cross any other split. Observe that every trivial
split is strong. In general, any collection of non-crossing cuts is arranged in a “tree-like”
fashion in a manner which we now explain for the special case of strong splits.
Let G be a graph and let T be a tree whose leaves are the vertices of G. Let e be an edge
of T, and let TA, TB be the two connected components of T −e. Let A be the set of vertices
of G corresponding to the leaves of T that are in TA, and define B analogously. We say that
the cut (A, B) is induced by the edge e of T.
The split decomposition of a graph G is the tree T whose leaves are the vertices of G and
such that each strong split of G is induced by a unique edge of T, and conversely each edge of
T induces a strong split. Its existence follows from the definition of strong splits. (It is a fairly
standard fact in graph theory that such a tree can be formed from any collection of pairwise
non-crossing cuts; this fact is used for constructing many different types of decompositions.
However, one can also just take the existence of T as a fact from [20, 23].)
Let x be an internal node of T and let T1, . . . , Tk be the connected components of T −x.
For each i ∈[k], let Vi be the set of vertices of G corresponding to the leaves of T that
are in Ti. The quotient graph at x is the graph Gx on vertex set {v1, . . . , vk} with an edge
between vi and vj in Gx if and only if there is an edge between Vi and Vj in G, for all
i ̸= j ∈[k]. The lift of a split (Ax, Bx) of Gx is the cut (A, B) of V (G) with A = S

the clique and the star are the only graphs with the property that every cut is also a split.

▶Theorem 1.5. The 1-ancilla-integrity of an n-vertex graph G can be computed in time O(n6).
Proof. By Lemma 6.1, we can compute the 1-ancilla integrity of an n-vertex graph by n + 1

extra factor of n. Now we consider computing the flip-integrity of an n-vertex graph G.
