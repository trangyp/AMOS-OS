A Calibration Audit of a Gaia XP White-Dwarf
Main-Sequence Binary Catalog: How Much BP-Band
Residual it Takes to Manufacture Contamination
Karan Akbari *
St. Xavier’s College, Mumbai, India
Abstract
A Gaussian-process classifier on Gaia DR3 XP spectra produced ∼30,000 white-dwarf main-sequence (WD–MS)
binary candidates [16], each with a probability but no likelihood or goodness-of-fit. The corrected XP BP band keeps
a local blue-flux residual of about 2% [13, 24], where a hot white dwarf also adds flux. We asked whether that
residual contaminates the selection at its realistic amplitude. It does not. The answer turns on one unit: 2% of total
flux, deposited in the narrow blue band, is a median 55% local excess on a red MS star, 27 times the same number read
locally. Injected as a 2% local excess it gives a spurious rate of 0.08 on a 0.05 baseline through the ∆χ2 threshold
standing in for the classifier, and an amortized posterior keeps 0.84 of its 90% coverage against a clean 0.88. The
selection fails only above a 10–20% local excess and reaches 0.96 near 50%, the raw uncorrected bias that correction
and the B < 18 cut remove. A model-comparison gate certifies binarity only above a WD flux share near 0.05; at the
catalog’s median share the statistics invert and the spurious carry the larger ∆χ2 improvement. The clean reliability
signal is off-cooling-sequence UV deficiency (GALEX FUV detection 0.19 against 0.50), robust to a distance control.
At the residual the correction leaves, the contamination hypothesis is a null; the open regime is the faint half, where
that residual is unmeasured. The audit gives the amplitude it would take, and the shape of the failure past it.

Introduction
White-dwarf main-sequence binaries are close pairs that have survived a common-envelope phase or will enter one,
and as a population they constrain that phase and feed the cataclysmic-variable and type Ia supernova progenitor
channels. Large samples turn individual systems into population statements, so a catalog of tens of thousands is useful
only insofar as its membership can be trusted. When the catalog is built by a classifier that returns a probability and
no likelihood, how many of its candidates are real, and which, is not a question the catalog answers about itself.
Gaia DR3 delivered low-resolution BP/RP (XP) spectra for about 220 million sources [8, 9], and white-dwarf
main-sequence binaries are one of the populations those spectra can isolate, because a hot white dwarf adds a blue
excess on top of an otherwise main-sequence continuum. Li et al. [16] trained a Gaussian-process classifier on this
signature and shipped a catalog of ∼30,000 WD–MS binaries, each with a probability prob_binary and a single-
versus-binary fit, cut at prob_binary > 0.8. What it does not give is a calibrated likelihood, or a test of whether the
model that generated the classifier’s training spectra actually describes the source. The same group has since compared
single against binary fits on XP spectra directly [15], and other Gaia DR3 white-dwarf and WD–MS catalogs have been
assembled from SED fitting, astrometry, and ultraviolet excess [10, 11, 19, 20, 22, 26]. This note audits one of these
catalogs.
The concern that motivated the audit is that a model systematic the classifier was never trained on could man-
ufacture candidates without lowering a per-object goodness-of-fit, and the XP BP band has such a systematic. The
blue-flux excess documented in the Gaia EDR3 photometric validation [24] is large for faint and red sources in the
raw spectra, and the corrected XP catalogs reduce it to a local residual of roughly 1–2% [13]. That residual lives in
the same band, and points the same way, as the hot-white-dwarf excess the classifier is built to find. The natural worry
is that a single-star spectrum carrying the residual imitates a genuine WD–MS binary in the band that decides the
classification.
How much residual that takes is set by one unit. At the realistic post-correction amplitude the BP systematic has
no measurable effect on the selection, and it only manufactures candidates once the local excess reaches the tens of
percent that Huang’s correction and Li’s B < 18 cut remove. An injection written as a fraction of a star’s total flux,

This is the optical counterpart of a test we ran on X-ray spectra [1], where a 3% detector gain shift slips past

We audit the catalog along three lines. We forward-model the BP-band injection into real Gaia XP single-star
spectra and measure the spurious rate as a function of the local excess, we test the catalog’s reliability against SDSS and
LAMOST spectroscopy and GALEX ultraviolet photometry, and we train an amortized neural posterior on the same
forward model and run simulation-based calibration across injection amplitudes. Section 2 describes the catalog and
the spectra, Sections 3–5 give the three audits, and Sections 7–8 set their scope. Every number below is reproducible
from configuration and a fixed seed.
2
Data
The shipped catalog has 30,131 rows, all with prob_binary > 0.8; this is the post-cut FaintQC sample, and the pre-
cut pool of 452,433 candidates is not distributed. Every statement we make is therefore certified within the 30,131-row
catalog, and says nothing about the population the cut was drawn from. The MS–MS contamination flag flag_MSMS
is set for 7,621 rows (25.3%), the authors’ own model-based suspicion that a system is two main-sequence stars. A
majority, 56.1%, have chi2_diff_renorm < 0, meaning the single-star fit is at least as good as the binary fit; this
is by construction, since the classifier targets MS-dominated systems where the binary model does not improve χ2.
The binary-fit reduced χ2 has median 1.02, so the fits are statistically reasonable where they are reported.
For the injection audit we built a data-driven forward model from real Gaia DR3 XP_SAMPLED spectra [7, 18]: 120
single main-sequence stars and 120 single white dwarfs pulled with astroquery (with has_xp_sampled = true),
fit on a 50+50 subsample. The model space is the template libraries themselves, following the classifier’s own con-
struction, and the injected BP-band excess is the only misspecification we introduce.
For the reliability audit we cross-matched the catalog to SDSS [21] and LAMOST [23] spectroscopic WD–MS
samples within 1.5′′, giving 159 unique spectroscopically confirmed WD–MS systems inside the catalog, and to
GALEX [4, 17] through the CDS X-Match, which covers 3,683 rows (12.2%) and supplies an ultraviolet detection
independent of the optical score.
3
The injection and the amplitude it takes
We inject the BP-band blue excess into single main-sequence spectra and refit them with the full template library,
varying the local amplitude of the excess. To rule out the dominant real-data confound, that a sparse fit library cannot
span the variety of real single-MS spectra and would manufacture false positives on its own, we fit each truth spectrum
with its generating template excluded (leave-one-out). The real MS templates carry a blue residual of only 0.1–0.2%
before injection, so they do not pre-carry the systematic, and a zero-amplitude injection reproduces the 0.05 baseline
spurious rate set by the 95th-percentile threshold.
The amplitude has to be stated carefully, because the whole result lives there. Huang et al. [13] report their
corrected-XP consistency as better than 2% in 336–400 nm, which is a local, per-wavelength number, so we inject a
local fractional excess of that size. The distinction matters, because injecting 2% of a star’s total flux into the blue band
is a different quantity. The BP profile is narrow and the blue flux of a red MS star is a small part of its total, so 2% of
the total, deposited in the blue, is a median 55% local excess across our templates (Appendix A.1), about 27 times a
2% local residual. Scaling to total flux would inject the raw uncorrected bias, a ∼55% local excess at what reads as
“the 2% residual”.
At the 2% local residual the effect is small. Running the production pipeline with a multiplicative 2%-of-local

the local excess, to 0.13 at 5%, 0.25 at 10%, 0.58 at 20%, 0.83 at 30%, and 0.96 near 50% (Figure 1). So the selection
only manufactures candidates in bulk once the local excess reaches the tens of percent, which is the raw uncorrected
Riello bias. Li et al. keep that bias negligible with a B < 18 cut (their Section 2.2), and Huang’s correction removes it
where it is applied. The dramatic rate belongs to a regime the correction and the cut already handle, and at the residual
that actually survives correction the BP systematic does essentially nothing to the WD–MS selection.
There is a caveat that keeps the mechanism live. Huang validate their correction only for 3 < G < 17.5, and the
released catalog reaches B ≈19.5, with about a quarter of candidates at B > 18. Roughly 6.5% of the catalog has
apparent G > 17.5, so by Huang’s own validation variable it is almost fully covered, but the blue-end residual is set
by BP signal-to-noise, and about half the catalog has BP > 17.5. For those faint sources the post-correction residual
is not measured. If it reaches the 10–20% local range there, the turn-on in Figure 1 says the selection would start to
fail. We cannot rule that out, and we cannot show it either, so the defensible statement is a conditional caution for the
faint tail, not a contamination claim on the catalog.
Two features of the failure, in the regime where it does manufacture spurious, say what a downstream cut can and
cannot do. First, the per-object ∆χ2, which is close to what the classifier scores on, is anti-informative for flagging the
spurious (AUC ≈0.0; 0.003 across three seeds, at a 20% local excess): the spurious singles keep a lower ∆χ2 than
genuine binaries, so a ∆χ2 cut ranks them below the genuine binaries. A plain binary-fit goodness-of-fit does regain
power as the excess grows (genuine-versus-spurious reduced-χ2 AUC ≈0.81 at a 20% local excess, 0.99 at 50%),
which is why a bright-source magnitude cut removes a gross systematic, but the classifier does not select on absolute
binary-fit χ2. Second, a model-comparison gate keeps power for the bright synthesized binaries, but its power is set by
the WD flux share. Comparing the full binary model against a single star plus one free nuisance flux-correction term,
by BIC, separates genuine from spurious at AUC ≈0.94 at a 20% local excess for WD flux shares of 0.05–0.23, and
that power comes from the comparison structure, so for those bright binaries it does not need to know the blue shape:
a flat, a red, or a random nuisance template all separate about as well, because a genuine binary needs a white-dwarf
shape a single free term cannot reproduce while a smooth excess is absorbed by almost any extra axis. The gate’s
genuine side falls off with the WD share, though. At share 0.05 the AUC is 0.73, and at the catalog’s typical share
(median 0.0096) it is at or below chance (0.48 at share 0.01), because the median gate statistic for a genuine binary flips
sign, from −28 in the bright band to +3 at share 0.01: the binary model is no longer preferred for the catalog’s typical
genuine binary at this SNR. A BIC-neutral gate then flags about 70% of the spurious but rejects 88% of the genuine
(pass rate 0.12 at share 0.01 against 0.93 in the bright band), so this is a completeness collapse, not a purity leak. The
∆χ2 statistic inverts across the same axis. Anti-informative at bright shares (AUC ≈0.0), it reaches 0.90 at share 0.01:
the injected spurious carry a larger ∆χ2 improvement than the catalog’s typical genuine binaries, so a ∆χ2-ranked
selection prefers the spurious to the faint real binaries it is meant to find. The one per-object check whose partial power
survives at every share is the plain reduced-χ2 goodness-of-fit (AUC ≈0.74), and the classifier does not select on it.
This quantifies, inside the injection framework, why the prior-driven majority of Section 4 is externally unverifiable:
at the catalog’s typical WD share no per-object statistic certifies binarity at this SNR, and the only retained power
is the goodness-of-fit flagging the spurious, not any statistic confirming a faint genuine binary. The gate/AUC run is
committed (three seeds, shares 0.005–0.05 plus the catalog distribution and the bright band, amplitudes 10–50%).
4
Catalog reliability versus external ground truth
The part of the catalog that external data can corroborate is the χ2-favoring minority. The 159 spectroscopically
confirmed WD–MS systems have a median renormalized ∆χ2 of +0.72, with only 11% below zero, whereas the full
catalog has median −0.018 and 56% below zero. The confirmed systems are the ones where the binary model already
wins on χ2. The classifier’s distinctive feature is the rest: the MS-dominated majority where ∆χ2 < 0 and the prior,
not the fit, drives the classification. Li et al. [16] themselves note that the classifier’s boundaries are shaped by the
training-data priors, affecting both completeness and reliability (their Section 4.4); the audit here quantifies which part
of the catalog external data can corroborate. That majority is externally unverified. The reach of the external data is the
reason: only 20 of the SDSS and 146 of the LAMOST spectroscopically confirmed WD–MS systems fall inside the
catalog, with the 201 matching LAMOST entries collapsing onto 146 distinct sources (159 unique across both surveys,
7 confirmed by both). The data do not show the majority is wrong. Enlarging the external sample does not change this,
and the channels carry different meanings, so we keep them separate (Table 1). A match to the Gaia EDR3 white-dwarf
catalogue [11] certifies only a catalogued white dwarf at that position, which for a single-white-dwarf catalogue can

Calibration of the amortized posterior
The BP residual is harmless to the selection at its realistic amplitude, but it is worth asking what it does to an amortized
posterior, both because such posteriors are starting to replace per-object fits and because the shape of the failure, when
the amplitude is large, is informative. We trained an amortized neural posterior (a neural spline flow, sbi [28]) on a
real-template PCA forward model and ran simulation-based calibration [27]. On clean data the posterior is calibrated:
across four seeds the clean 90% coverage runs 0.865–0.905 (mean 0.877). The correct null is not 0.90: the discrete
rank estimator at 50 posterior draws places 45 of 51 PIT ranks inside [0.05, 0.95] under perfect calibration, which
gives 0.882, verified analytically and with a conjugate-Gaussian self-test. So the clean flow sits on the target.
Coverage degrades with the injection amplitude in step with the selection. At the 2%-of-local residual the mean
90% coverage is 0.84, barely below the clean 0.88 and within the run-to-run scatter. It falls to 0.82 at 5%, 0.69 at 10%,
0.58 at 20%, and 0.28 near 50% (Figure 1, bottom). So the posterior, like the selection, holds at the amplitude that
survives correction and fails only toward the uncorrected one.
The shape of that failure is specific, and it is the part worth keeping. When the posterior does miscalibrate, the
damage is not spread evenly across parameters: it concentrates on the companion fraction. At a 10% local excess the
companion fraction’s 90% coverage drops to about 0.08 across four seeds while the leading main-sequence component
holds near the null, and the same holds at 20%. A blue excess is spectrally localized, so it aliases into the white-dwarf
and companion parameters that live in the blue, and it does this more than an equal-damage perturbation of another
kind. Matching a grey offset, a red-band excess, and extra noise to the same mean coverage drop as the blue injection,
the companion fraction still collapses far more under the blue residual (coverage 0.07 across three seeds) than under
matched noise (0.68) or a matched red excess (0.47). The collapse is a directed one: a blue distortion aliases into the
companion fraction in particular, while a noise perturbation matched to the same coverage damage degrades every
parameter about evenly. The failure is overconfidence about the companion fraction, not an inability to constrain
it. One caveat matters here: the leading MS component carries almost no blue weight by construction (0.055 of its
loading below 500 nm), so its apparent immunity is partly a property of the basis, and the load-bearing statement is
the blue-specific, damage-matched bias in the companion fraction, not a claim that the main sequence is physically
untouched.
6
Discussion
At the 2% local residual that Huang’s correction leaves, the injection manufactures spurious candidates at 0.08 against
a 0.05 baseline and the amortized posterior keeps 0.84 of its coverage, so the BP-band residual the audit set out to test
does not contaminate the Li WD–MS selection at its realistic amplitude. The mechanism is real but it needs a 10–20%
local excess to bite, which is the uncorrected Riello regime that a B < 18 cut and the Huang recalibration remove.
The one place it stays open is the faint tail beyond Huang’s G < 17.5 validation, where the residual is unmeasured,
and there the audit gives a conditional caution and no firm result.
The audit delivers something narrower and more transferable. The turn-on curve (Figure 1) puts a number on how
much residual it takes, which is the quantity a catalog builder actually needs to decide whether a given correction
is enough. The posterior test (Figure 3) shows that when a blue calibration residual does miscalibrate an amortized
posterior, it does so in a specific and deceptive way: it makes the companion fraction confident and wrong while the fit
quality on the rest of the spectrum stays clean, so a routine goodness-of-fit check does not flag it. That failure mode,
an untrained model systematic breaking an amortized posterior’s calibration while clean-data checks pass, is the one
studied in the simulation-based-inference robustness literature [6, 12], where SBC serves as the diagnostic and robust
or model-criticism methods as the fix [2, 30]. Neural posterior estimation for white-dwarf spectroscopy is already in
use [29], so the specific form the failure takes for Gaia XP is worth having on record for the amortized successors to
catalogs like this one, even though it does not bear on Li et al.’s Gaussian-process probability at the realistic residual.
A catalog user can still take a few things from the reliability audit, which does not depend on the injection. The
score ranks true white-dwarf presence, with FUV-confirmation rising across prob_binary terciles from 28% to
42% to 59%, so a higher probability does mean a higher chance of a real white dwarf. But even the top tercile is only
59% UV-confirmed, the distinctive MS-dominated majority is externally unverified, and the contamination flag worth
using is off-sequence white dwarfs.

Limitations
The injection is on real Gaia XP spectra under leave-one-out at SNR 30, with a 50+50 template fit library; the turn-on
rates are stable across three seeds. We do not convert the injection into a catalog-wide spurious count, which would
need the residual-versus-magnitude relation for the released sample, and in particular the unmeasured blue-end residual
for the BP > 17.5 half of the catalog. That is the main open piece: the conditional faint-tail caution in Section 3 stands
or falls on whether the post-correction residual there reaches the 10–20% local range, which Huang’s validation does
not cover. The selection statistic we score on is a 95th-percentile ∆χ2 threshold, a stand-in for Li’s Gaussian-process

classifier, and the released classifier itself is never run here, so the spurious rates describe that threshold and not the
exact catalog boundary.
Our single-MS truth sources are enforced by RUWE, non_single_star, and CMD position only, with no
spectroscopy. Any WD–MS contamination among them would dilute the injected effect, not create it, so the bias is
conservative.
The posterior-calibration test uses a real-template PCA forward model and an analog neural posterior, not the
catalog’s Gaussian-process classifier, so the coverage numbers describe the amortized methods replacing per-object
fits, not Li et al.’s released catalog. The spectral-specificity result is controlled for perturbation magnitude but carries
the basis caveat noted in Section 5. The synthetic binaries also span WD flux fractions of 0.05–0.30, while the catalog’s
own G-band WD flux fraction has a median near 0.0096, with about 64% of sources fainter than the 0.024 equivalent
of the injection, so the coverage numbers are measured on binaries several times brighter in WD fraction than the
catalog median. The gate and AUC re-run at catalog-representative fractions is in the committed record, and the share-
resolved result in Section 3 is measured there. Fractions in that run are the WD share of total grid flux, which for the
bluer WD sits slightly above the G-band share it brackets, so the catalog arm is a lower bound on the true grid share.
The samples that anchor the reliability audit are small and footprint-limited: 159 spectroscopic confirmations, 127
of them with GALEX coverage, and an off-sequence UV-deficiency that rests on the 383 off-sequence sources with
a GALEX AIS NUV match within 3′′ (FUV detection is then the measured outcome). GALEX covers 12.2% of the
catalog with a UV-bright, nearby bias, the magnitudes are observed rather than dereddened, and the FUV is at AIS
depth, so the UV indicator is demonstrated on that subset and reads absence of a hot white dwarf. The 47% purity
floor is for the NUV-bright GALEX-AIS-matched subset only.
8
Conclusion
• At the 1–2% local BP-band residual that survives XP correction, the systematic is harmless to the WD–MS
selection: the injection spurious rate is 0.08 ± 0.01 against a 0.05 baseline, and the amortized posterior keeps
0.84 of its 90% coverage against a clean 0.88. Both degrade only toward the raw uncorrected regime, reaching
a 0.96 spurious rate and 0.28 coverage near a 50% local excess, which Huang’s correction and Li’s B < 18 cut
remove. The amplitude has to be defined with care: a fraction of a star’s total flux, deposited in the blue, is about
27 times the same fraction read as a local residual.
• The selection turns on only above a 10–20% local excess (Figure 1). The one regime where this is not settled
is the faint tail beyond Huang’s G < 17.5 validation, where the post-correction residual is unmeasured; there
the result is a conditional caution. Where the systematic is large enough to make spurious candidates, the plain
goodness-of-fit partly flags them while the ∆χ2 prefers them (AUC 0.90 at share 0.01, the catalog’s median,
since the spurious carry a larger ∆χ2 improvement than the faint genuine binaries). The model-comparison
gate protects the selection only for binaries with a WD flux share above about 0.05; at share 0.01, the catalog’s
median, it is at chance and rejects most genuine binaries, so its protection does not reach the catalog’s typical
candidate.
• When a blue calibration residual does miscalibrate an amortized posterior, the failure is spectrally specific:
it drives a tight but biased companion fraction, whose 90% coverage falls to 0.07 under the blue residual at
matched coverage damage across three seeds, against 0.68 under matched noise and 0.47 under a matched red
excess, while the fit quality elsewhere stays clean, so a routine check does not see it. This bears on the amortized
posteriors replacing catalogs like this one, not on Li et al.’s Gaussian-process probability.
• Independently of the injection, the catalog’s external coverage is below 1%, the prior-driven majority (∼56%
with ∆χ2 < 0) is externally unverified, and the one clean, UV-validated contamination signal is off-cooling-
sequence sources (FUV-detected at 0.19 against 0.50, robust to a distance control). The probability ranks white-
dwarf presence without certifying it, and does not control FDR at q ≤0.2.
The probability per source is cheap to compute. The BP residual that motivated this audit does not threaten it at the
amplitude Huang’s correction leaves. What is left is the turn-on curve and the shape of the posterior failure past it, and
those transfer to the amortized methods coming after this catalog.
