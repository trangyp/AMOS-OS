Abstract
We prove a coarse analogue of the classic fact that every graph can be decomposed along its cut-
vertices into 2-connected components. Precisely, we prove that for every graph G and a positive inte-
ger d, G admits a tree decomposition whose adhesion sets have weak diameter at most 3d + 2 so that
no two vertices u, v lying in the same bag can be separated by a set of weak diameter at most d whose
distance from u and v is more than d. By the Coarse Menger’s Theorem for two paths, this condition
admits also a dual formulation, phrased in terms of the existence of two paths that are far from each
other and connect the vicinity of u with the vicinity of v.

Introduction
Coarse graph theory is a young and vibrant direction in structural graph theory that aims to understand the
structure of graphs viewed as metric spaces, by endowing them with the shortest-paths metric. As outlined
in the foundational work of Georgakopoulous and Papasoglu [12], the hope is that many classic results of
structural graph theory admit natural coarse analogues, obtained by replacing the conditions that objects
intersect or are disjoint by requesting that they are close or far from each other. Recent investigations have
revealed a mix of successes and failures. For instance, the Erd˝os–P´osa Theorem can be lifted to the coarse
setting [1, 2, 10], and so can also Gallai’s Theorem [9]. The coarse variant of Menger’s Theorem fails in
full generality [19, 22], but holds in various restricted settings [5, 7, 11, 12, 13, 15, 20, 21]. There are also
natural coarse analogues of pathwidth and treewidth [14, 16, 17]. For these, the coarse analogue of the
Excluded Tree Theorem holds [18], but the coarse analogue of the Excluded Grid Theorem fails [3]. The
Alon-Seymour-Tarsi Separator Theorem can be lifted to the coarse setting, at least to a large degree [8].
The coarse analogue of the Kuratowski-Wagner Theorem remains tantalizingly open [12].
In this paper we add another positive example to this growing list. One of the most basic results of
structural graph theory is the so-called block-cut tree: every graph can be decomposed along its cut-vertices
in a tree-like fashion into its 2-connected components. We prove the following coarse analogue of this fact
(see Section 2 for definitions):
Theorem 1. For every positive integer d and every connected graph G, there exists a tree-decomposition (T, β)
of G such that
• each adhesion set of (T, β) has weak diameter at most 3d + 2; and
• for any two vertices u, v ∈V (G) in a common bag of (T, β), there is no set S ⊆V (G) with
diamG(S) ⩽d and distG(S, {u, v}) > d + 1 that separates u and v in G.
Moreover, given G and d, such a tree-decomposition can be computed in time O(n(n + m)), where n and m
denote the vertex and the edge count of G, respectively.
Since the coarse analogue of Menger’s Theorem holds for two paths [5, 12], the second condition in
Theorem 1 can be reformulated to roughly the following: for any two vertices u, v lying in the same bag
and satisfying distG(u, v) ⩾Ω(d), there are two paths P1, P2 that connect the radius-O(d) balls around
u and v, respectively, and are at distance Ω(d) from each other. See Corollary 1 for a precise formulation.
The proof of Theorem 1 relies on a standard BFS-layering construction that has already been used
in multiple previous contributions in coarse graph theory. The technique was perhaps best exemplified
in the work of Berger and Seymour [6], who characterized graphs quasi-isometric to trees as those that
admit a tree decomposition with bags of bounded weak diameter, and as those that exclude a certain
obstruction called a loaded cycle. The construction of Berger and Seymour is the following. Assuming
without loss of generality that the considered graph G is connected, we partition the vertices of G into
layers L0, L1, L2, . . . according to the distances from some fixed vertex r. Each layer Li is subsequently
partitioned into blocks, where vertices u, v ∈Li are in the same block if they are connected in the graph
induced by the union of layers Li, Li+1, Li+2, . . .. Importantly, the adjacency relation between all the
blocks of all the layers induces a tree; this already provides a rough skeleton of a tree-decomposition.
Berger and Seymour observe that in the absence of a heavily loaded cycle, every block in every layer has a
bounded weak diameter, and thus the whole tree of blocks can be easily turned into a tree-decomposition
of G whose bags have bounded weak diameter. What we observe is that when the construction is applied

Preliminaries
All graphs considered in this paper are undirected, finite, and simple. For k ∈N, we write [k] := {1, . . . , k}
and [0, k] := {0, 1, . . . , k}.
Graphs.
We use standard graph notation. Given a graph G, we denote its set of vertices as V (G) and its
set of edges as E(G). An edge connecting vertices u and v is denoted as uv. For a subset of vertices A, we
write G[A] for the subgraph of G induced by A, which consists of all the vertices of A and all the edges
with both endpoints in A. The graph induced by the vertex subset V (G) \ A is denoted by G −A. We
say that a vertex subset S ⊆V (G) separates two vertices u, v /∈S if u and v belong to distinct connected
components of G −S.
Paths, distances, and neighborhoods.
A u-v path in a graph G is any path in G with endpoints u and v.
For two subsets of vertices S, T ⊆V (G), an S-T path in G is an s-t path in G for some s ∈S, t ∈T.
The distance metric in G, defined between pairs of vertices u, v of G as the length of the shortest u-v path
in G, will be denoted by distG(u, v). If A, B are vertex subsets or subgraphs of G, then distG(A, B) is the
minimum of distG(a, b) for a belonging to A and b belonging to B.
By N(v) we denote the open neighborhood of v in G, defined as the set of vertices of G which are
adjacent to v. This notion is extended to any vertex subset A ⊆V (G) as follows: N(A) := S
u∈A N(u)\A.

A coarse analogue of the block-cut tree
In this section we prove our main result, Theorem 1. We proceed to explain how to define the desired
tree-decomposition and subsequently we verify that it satisfies the desired properties.
BFS-layering trees.
Let us first recall the construction that originates from the work of Berger and
Seymour [6] on bounded-diameter tree-decompositions.
Suppose G is a connected graph and suppose that L := {L0, L1, . . . , Lh}, for some h ∈N, is the
BFS-layering of G from an arbitrarily chosen (root) vertex r ∈V (G).

we define relation ∼i on the set Li by declaring u ∼i v if there exists a u-v path in G[S

Proof. Since u and v belong to the same block of layer i, there exists a path connecting them in G[S
j⩾i Lj].

the same block. Then this block is the unique neighbor in layer Li−1 of the block of u, v, which implies

in TL, there is a path between them in G that avoids all the earlier layers.
Observation 2. Let B be a block of layer i and let B′ be a descendant of B in TL. Then, for any two vertices
u ∈B and v ∈B′, there exists a u-v path in G[S
j⩾i Lj].
Proof. Consider a shortest v-r path in G, say P. Noting that P must intersect layer Li, let w be the first

u-v walk in G[S

Towards the proof of Theorem 1.
To complete the proof of Theorem 1, it remains to show that no bag
of a d-coarse block-cut tree-decomposition contains a pair of vertices separable by a separator of small
diameter (second condition). To this end, we first identify two cases in which a vertex subset of small
diameter does not separate a vertex from the root of the BFS-layering.
For a given set of vertices S ⊆V (G), we define maxlayerL(S) to be the deepest layer containing a
vertex of S, i.e.,
maxlayerL(S) := max{i ∈[h] | Li ∩S ̸= ∅}.
Analogously, we define minlayer(S) as the minimum layer containing a vertex of S, i.e.,
minlayerL(S) := min{i ∈[h] | Li ∩S ̸= ∅}.
For a block B of L, we have maxlayerL(B) = minlayerL(B); we shall denote this common value by
layerL(B). For a vertex v ∈V (G), we define layerL(v) := i ∈[h], where v ∈Li.
We proceed by proving two important lemmas.
Lemma 2. Suppose v ∈V (G) and B is the block of L containing v. Suppose further that S ⊆V (G) is a
vertex subset such that diamG(S) ⩽d, distG(v, S) > d + 1, and maxlayerL(S) ⩾layerL(v) −1. Then there
exists a v-r path in G −S.
Proof. Let P be a shortest v-r path in G. If we enumerate the vertices of P starting from v, the i-th vertex
of P belongs to layer layerL(v) −(i −1). Since diamG(S) ⩽d, we have
minlayerL(S) ⩾maxlayerL(S) −d ⩾layerL(v) −(d + 1).
Consequently, S can intersect P only within the first d + 2 vertices of P, that is, within those vertices
whose distance from v is at most d + 1. However, since distG(S, v) > d + 1 by assumption, this is not the
case and P is a path in G −S.
Lemma 3. Let v ∈V (G) and B be the block of L containing v. Suppose that S ⊆V (G) is a vertex subset
such that diamG(S) ⩽d, distG(v, S) > d + 1, and maxlayerL(S) < layerL(B) −1. Suppose further that
B′ is the block of layer maxlayerL(S) + 1 on the unique B-{r} path in TL and B′ is (3d + 2)-wide. Then
there exists a v-r path in G −S.
Proof. Since B′ is (3d + 2)-wide, it contains two vertices at distance more than 3d + 2 from each other.
Since diam(S) ⩽d, one of these two vertices is at distance more than (3d+2−d)/2 = d + 1 from S. Denote
this vertex by v⋆. Recall that layerL(v∗) = layerL(B′) = maxlayerL(S) + 1. By Observation 2, there exists

A dual formulation
In this section we derive a dual formulation of Theorem 1, replacing the second condition by an equivalent
(up to constant factors) condition expressed in terms of the existence of scattered paths.
Towards this, we require the following result which provides a coarse analogue of Menger’s Theorem
for the special case where we ask for two scattered paths.
Theorem 2 ([5, 12]). Let G be a graph and S, T ⊆V (G) be subsets of vertices of G. Suppose that for some
d ∈N, there do not exist two S-T paths P1, P2 such that distG(P1, P2) ⩾d. Then there exists a vertex w of
G such that for every S-T path P, we have distG(w, P) ⩽129d.
The constant 129 is from the work of Albrechtsen et al. [5]; from now on we denote it by c := 129.
Definition 1. Let G be a graph, u, v ∈V (G) be two vertices of G, and d, ℓ∈N. We say that
• u and v satisfy the (d, ℓ)-cut condition if there is a vertex subset F ⊆V (G) such that diamG(F) ⩽d,
distG(F, {u, v}) > ℓ, and F intersects every u-v path; and
• u and v satisfy the (d, ℓ)-flow condition if at least one of the following assertions holds:
– distG(u, v) ⩽2ℓ; or
– in G there exist paths P1, P2 with endpoints u1, v1 and u2, v2 respectively, such that we have
distG(P1, P2) > d and distG(wt, w) ⩽ℓfor all w ∈{u, v} and t ∈{1, 2}.
The following lemma shows that thees two conditions are dual to each other in an approximate sense.
Lemma 4. Let G be a connected graph, u, v ∈V (G) be two vertices of G, and d, ℓ∈N. Then the following
implications hold:

• If u, v satisfy the (d, ℓ)-cut condition, then they do not satisfy the (d, ℓ)-flow condition.
• Assuming ℓ> 2cd, if u, v do not satisfy the (d, ℓ)-flow condition, then they do satisfy the (2cd, ℓ−
(2cd + 1))-cut condition.
Proof. First we show that if u, v satisfy the (d, ℓ)-cut condition, then they do not satisfy the (d, ℓ)-flow
condition. By definition of the cut condition, there exists a set F such that we have diamG(F) ⩽d,
distG(F, {u, v}) > ℓ, and F intersects every u-v path. Note that since the distance from F to both u and v
is larger than ℓ, it must be that distG(u, v) > 2ℓ. Now, suppose towards a contradiction that u, v satisfy the
(d, ℓ)-flow condition. Then, there exist two paths P1 and P2 with endpoints u1, v1 and u2, v2 respectively,
witnessing that the (d, ℓ)-flow condition is satisfied. Let us consider a shortest u-u1 path Q1 and a shortest
v-v1 path R1 respectively. Then P ′
1 := Q1 ∪P1 ∪R1 is a u-v walk. Analogously, we define Q2, R2, and P ′

have distG(P1, P2) > d, which contradicts the assumption that diamG(F) ⩽d.
Now, we prove that if u, v do not satisfy the (d, ℓ)-flow condition with ℓ> 2cd, then they satisfy the

distance more than d from each other. Hence, by Theorem 2, there exists a vertex w such that F := Ncd[w]
intersects every S-T path. Let P be the shortest Nℓ[u]-Nℓ[v] path in G. Since distG(u, v) > 2ℓ, it must be
that Nℓ[u] and Nℓ[v] are disjoint, and in particular, it must be that every vertex of P is at distance at least
ℓfrom both u and v. Let z be a vertex in P ∩F. As diamG(F) ⩽2cd, distG(u, z) ⩾ℓ, and distG(v, z) ⩾ℓ,
no vertex in Nℓ−(2cd+1)[u] and Nℓ−(2cd+1)[v] belongs to F. Therefore, distG(F, {u, v}) > ℓ−(2cd + 1).
Finally, observe that every u-v path in G contains an S-T subpath. Therefore F intersects every u-v path
in G and, as a result u and v, satisfy the (2cd, ℓ−(2cd + 1))-cut condition.

Corollary 1. For every positive integer d and every connected graph G, there exists a tree decomposition
(T, β) of G such that
• each adhesion set of (T, β) has weak diameter at most 3d + 2; and
• for any two vertices u, v ∈V (G) in a common bag of (T, β), one of the following assertions holds:
– distG(u, v) ⩽4(d + 1); or
– in G there exist paths P1, P2, with endpoints u1, v1 and u2, v2 respectively, such that we have
distG(P1, P2) > ⌊d/258⌋and distG(wt, w) ⩽2(d + 1) for all w ∈{u, v} and t ∈{1, 2}.
Proof. The second condition featured in Theorem 1 is equivalent to the following statement: For any two
