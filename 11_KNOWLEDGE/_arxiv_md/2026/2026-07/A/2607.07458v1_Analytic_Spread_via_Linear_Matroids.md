ANALYTIC SPREAD VIA LINEAR MATROIDS
STEPHEN LANDSITTEL AND ERAN NEVO
Abstract. We give a systematic analysis of the analytic spread of the determinantal ideal
JG,H arising from a pair of graphs (G, H). We give sharp bounds for this analytic spread,
and combinatorial conditions and obstructions for its maximality via a linear matroid. When
H is a single edge, JG,H is isomorphic to the binomial edge ideal JG and its analytic spread
is shown to equal the rank of G in Kalai’s 2-hyperconnectivity matroid.
1. Introduction
The analytic spread of an ideal in a local ring was defined by Northcott and Rees [42]
in studying reductions of ideals. Analytic spread has been of wide interest in commutative
algebra during the past three decades, for instance in studying algebraic properties of ideals
such as their cores and residual intersections, see for example [11, 12, 26]. It is well-known
that the analytic spread of an ideal in a local or standard-graded ring R is at most the
dimension of R, and this bound is sharp. Ideals of maximal analytic spread are of particular
interest in the theory of multiplicities, see for instance [1, 19]. In [34] the analytic spread
was studied using the theory of j-multiplicities, wherein they show that the analytic spread
of an ideal is maximal if and only if the ideal has positive j-multiplicity. The relationship
between analytic spread and the theory of integral closure was observed in [48, 7, 16], and
analytic spread was studied in relation to asymptotic depth in [40]. In the context of algebraic
geometry, analytic spread describes some aspects of the birational geometry corresponding to
blow-ups and their fibers, see [15, 27]. Some additional papers on analytic spread include [3,
41, 9, 6, 18, 19].
The analytic spread of an ideal I is, under mild assumptions, the minimal number of
generators of a reduction of I, see e.g. [42, Theorem 1], where J is a reduction of I if JIn =
In+1 for some n. Precisely, the analytic spread of an ideal I in a local ring (S, m) was defined
in [42] to be one more than the degree of the eventual polynomial P(n) := dimS/m In/mIn,
or equivalently, the Krull dimension of the special fiber ring ⊕n≥0In/mIn of I, and is denoted
by ℓ(I). An analogous definition of analytic spread is adopted when S is a standard-graded
ring over a field, where m is the graded maximal ideal. It was proven in [31, Chapter 8] that
if the field is perfect, then the analytic spread of I is the minimal number of generators of
I up to integral closure. We always have the analytic spread bounded between the height
of the ideal and the Krull dimension of its hosting ring: ht(I) ≤ℓ(I) ≤dim(S).

The history of when algebraic independence can be represented by linear independence
of derivations traces back to an 1841 paper of Jacobi [33, p.340, Propositio 1], in which
it was proven that the Jacobian of any real analytic functions (e.g. polynomials with real
coefficients) vanishes if they are algebraically dependent. Jacobi’s result precedes the modern
languages of matroids, derivations, and field characteristic. Around a century later, Van der
Waerden showed in [52] that algebraic independence forms a matroid. In 1957 Rado proved
that if a matroid is representable over a field of characteristic zero (e.g. is an algebraic
matroid over the rationals), then for p >> 0 it is representable over some, and hence infinitely
many, fields of characteristic p. See [45, Theorem 6]. Finally in [4, Theorem 8] it was shown
that over any field of either characteristic zero, or sufficiently high characteristic, an algebraic
matroid is linear. In the results of both [4, 45], the characteristic size requirement depends on
the particular algebraic matroid at hand. In [54], Zariski and Samuel reformulated Jacobi’s
result as a correspondence between separating transcendence bases and derivations in the
language of modern commutative algebra.

2.1. Graph Theory. We recall some notation and definitions following [21, 8].
An (undirected) graph is a pair G = (V, E) where V is a set and E is a family of doubletons
{u, v} with u, v ∈V . We write V (G) = V and E(G) = E. The elements of V are called
vertices and the elements of E are called edges.
G is called simple when u ̸= v for all
{u, v} ∈E(G). When |V (G)| is finite, we call G a finite graph and we call |V (G)| the order
of G. If v ∈V (G) and there does not exist e ∈E(G) with v ∈e, then we say that v is
isolated. Isolated vertices in graphs G and H do not contribute to the ideal JG,H. All graphs
in this paper are assumed to be finite and simple with no isolated vertices, in particular
|E(G)| < ∞. For convenience we write |G| := |V (G)| and e(G) := |E(G)|. For a positive
integer n, we write [n] := {1, . . . , n} and Call G a graph on [n] if V = [n], or a graph on
n vertices if |G| = n. Throughout this paper we consider graphs up to isomorphism, in
particular it applies to the definitions below of complete graph, star, path etc. The complete
graph G = Kn is the graph on [n] such that E(Kn) = {{i, j} | 1 ≤i < j ≤n}. For n ≥2,
the star of order n is the graph Stn whose edge set is E(Stn) = {{1, i} | 2 ≤i ≤n}. The star
of order four is also called the claw. For n ≥2, the path graph Pn is the graph on [n] with
E(Pn) = {{i, i + 1} | 1 ≤i ≤n −1}. Clearly Stn = Pn if and only if n ≤3. Given graphs
G = (V, E) and G′ = (V ′, E′), we say that G′ is a subgraph of G, or G contains G′, if V ′ ⊆V
and E′ ⊆E, in which case we write G′ ⊆G. A subgraph G′ ⊆G is called an induced subgraph
of G if E(G′) = E(KV (G′))∩E(G), i.e. G′ has every edge in G on the vertices of G′. For n ≥3,
the cycle G = Cn is the graph on [n] with E(G) = {{1, 2}, . . . , {n −1, n}, {1, n}}. We say
that a graph G has an induced cycle if Ck is an induced subgraph of G for some k ≥3. A tree
is a connected graph with no induced cycles. A unicycle graph is a connected graph G with
exactly one induced cycle. A maximal connected subgraph of G (in the sense of adding new
edges and vertices) is called a (connected) component of G. G is a forest if every component
of G is a tree. Given a graph G, we say that a graph G′ is obtained from G by adding a leaf to
G if V (G′) = V (G)⊔{v} and E(G′) = E(G)∪{{a, v}} for some a ∈V (G). Given a graph G,
we say that a graph G′ is obtained by adding a handle to G if V (G′) = V (G)⊔{v} and there
are vertices a, b ∈V (G) such that E(G′) = E(G) ⊔{{a, v}, {b, v}}. We say that a graph G′
is obtained by adding a generalized handle to G if for some s ≥2 and a, b ∈V (G), we have
V (G′) = V (G) ⊔{v1, . . . , vs} and E(G′) = E(G) ⊔{{a, v1}, {v1, v2}, . . . , {vs−1, vs}, {vs, b}}.

2.3. Matroids. Now we recall some elementary theory of matroids from [44] and [36] which
will also be used in the proofs of Sections 3, and 4.
Definition 2.3 (c.f. [44, p.7]). A matroid is a pair M = (E, I) consisting of a finite set
E, together with a family I of subsets of E such that ∅∈I, for every I ∈I and I′ ⊂I,
we have I′ ∈I, and for I1, I2 ∈I with |I1| < |I2|, there exists an element e ∈I2 such
that I1 ∪{e} ∈I. The elements of I are called independent sets. A basis is a maximal
independent set under inclusion. All bases of a matroid M have the same cardinality, and
this cardinality is called the rank of M, which is denoted by rank(M). Given two matroids
M1 = (E1, I1) and M2 = (E2, I2), a matroid isomorphism Φ : M1 →M2 is a bijection
ϕ : E1 →E2 such that A ∈I1 if and only if ϕ(A) ∈I2.
Whitney asked which matroids are isomorphic to linear matroids in [53, p.509], which we
define now.
Definition 2.4. A matroid M = ([n], I) is linear (or representable) if there exist a field
K and a matrix A over K with rows A1, . . . , An such that S ⊂[n] is in I if and only if
{Ai | i ∈S} is linearly independent (over K).
