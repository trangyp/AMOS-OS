Abstract
Cancer progression is driven by the ability of cells with identical driver
mutations to adopt biologically distinct adaptive phenotypes. Yet the
population dynamics implied by intratumour phenotypic heterogeneity
is poorly understood. Melanoma, a highly aggressive skin cancer, repre-
sents an excellent model to explore phenotype-switching, in part because
phenotypic identity is conferred by melanocyte-associated transcrip-
tion factor (MITF) activity. Here we develop and analyse a multiscale
phenotype-structured PDE model for melanoma cell populations in
the epidermis, progressing from subcellular MITF dynamics to well-
mixed and radially resolved population models. Numerical solutions
revealed that the model admits three distinct and stable long-term
population behaviours: a slow-growing melanoma of proliferative cells
and non-cycling, differentiated cells without invasive potential; a faster-
propagating melanoma with an invasive core; and a rapidly growing
melanoma with oscillatory core dynamics. More broadly, the analy-
sis also highlights that phenotype reversibility by individual cells does
not imply reversibility of phenotype distributions at the population
scale. Hence, properties at the single-cell level (e.g., reversibility of
invasive capacity) must be extrapolated with caution to populations

1 Introduction
Cell plasticity is a widespread biological phenomenon that refers to the ability
for cells to reversibly adapt their state, or phenotype, in response to envi-
ronmental cues [1]. This adaptive capacity gives rise to heterogeneity within
cell populations that obscures the understanding of numerous diseases [2–4].
Importantly, cell plasticity is universal to cancers and considered a barrier
to effective treatment due to its role in producing drug-tolerant tumour cells
[5, 6]. Several mathematical studies have been published concerning cell plas-
ticity in cancer, including analysis of structured individual-based models [7, 8],
stochastic branching processes [9, 10], structured partial differential equation
(PDE) models [11, 12] and multiscale models [13].
Melanoma is an excellent paradigm problem for cancer cell plasticity. It
is typically considered a skin cancer, but may also arise in the eye and
mucous membranes [14–16]. The disease is characterised by the deregu-
lated proliferation of melanocytes or their neural crest progenitors, which
are pigment-producing cells that reside in the dermal-epidermal junction (the
epidermis and dermis are the upper two layers of skin) [14]. Despite its
lower incidence relative to other skin cancers, melanoma is disproportionately
responsible for the majority of all skin cancer-related deaths [17]. Its clinical
importance has led to the establishment of a considerable number of mathe-
matical and computational studies (reviewed in [18]); the literature includes
ordinary differential equation (ODE) models for tumour-immune interactions
and virotherapy [19, 20], delay differential equation models for dendritic cell
therapy [21–23], PDE models for combination therapies [24, 25] and agent-
based models that capture tumour angiogenesis and genetic heterogeneity
[26, 27]. Importantly, several recent large-scale ODE models have also been
developed to capture phenotypic plasticity via subcellular gene regulatory
networks [28, 29], but lack explicit treatment of in vivo population dynamics.
Melanoma is a convenient problem to study cancer cell plasticity because
tumours show co-existence of multiple phenotypes [30]. Moreover, a mas-
ter regulator of phenotypic states has been identified and well-characterised,
namely the melanocyte-associated transcription factor (MITF) [30, 31]. The
connection between MITF and melanoma cell phenotype is captured by the
MITF rheostat model [32, 33]. In basic form, the theory asserts that pheno-
type reversibly transitions between three key states in a manner driven by the
activity of MITF protein. Here activity refers to the downstream transcrip-
tional and proteomic influence of MITF on cell state. High MITF activity gives
rise to a differentiated and cycle-arrested state (DIF), intermediate activity
promotes a proliferative state (PRO) and low activity generates an invasive
cycle-arrested state (INV). Importantly, MITF is inversely coupled to the inte-
grated stress response (ISR) [34]; stressful microenvironments are associated
with lower MITF expression that is at least partly attributable to elevated
transcriptional repression via the ISR effector ATF4 as well as decreased MITF
translation [35]. A summary is provided in Fig. 1.

The MITF rheostat is a theory of individual cell state. However, melanoma
tumours comprise a population of malignant cells that collectively influence
patient outcome via their coupled dynamics. This raises the research question
we consider in the present work:
Key question: What are the emergent population dynamics implied by the
MITF rheostat? More specifically,
- What is the phenotype distribution amongst melanoma cells?
- What long-term qualitative behaviours are to be expected?
We investigate the key question above using mathematical modelling. The
model is formulated as a multiscale and phenotype-resolved partial differential
equation that we develop and analyse incrementally. In Sect. 2, we first consider
subcellular dynamics for MITF RNA, protein and a downstream phenotype
variable. By exploiting a separation of timescales, we reduce the subcellu-
lar model to a homogenised phenotype flux. This flux informs the phenotype
dynamics of a spatially-homogeneous but phenotype-structured population
model in Sect. 3. Finally, we consider a radially-resolved extension to the pop-
ulation model in Sect. 4. The model is deliberately kept as simple as possible
throughout so that we may calibrate parameters to published data via Bayesian
inference. A schematic of the model and its development is provided in Fig. 2.
2 Subcellular MITF dynamics
We first consider the dynamics of MITF within individual melanoma cells.
The model is formulated as a stochastic system of differential equations which
couples the following key quantities:
• r(t) = MITF RNA concentration;
• p(t) = MITF protein concentration;
• ϕ(t) = MITF activity (∼phenotype).

3 Structured population model
We have presented thus far a mathematical model for the time evolution of
phenotype within individual melanoma cells. In reality, melanomas comprise
a population of malignant cells that collectively influence patient outcome via
their coupled dynamics. We address this discrepancy in the current section
by presenting a mathematical model for the time evolution of the phenotype
distribution amongst melanoma cells within a tumour.
The model is formulated as a partial differential equation (PDE) that
is non-locally coupled with respect to phenotype. The phenotype variable ϕ
informs the proliferation rate in a manner consistent with the MITF rheo-
stat. Importantly, by contrast to its usage as a given function or constant in
Sect. 2, the average transcription rate a(t) is coupled to the cell density as a
proxy for the integrated stress response. Spatial resolution is neglected here
for simplicity and explored in Sect. 4.

5 Discussion
We developed in this paper a new mathematical model to investigate the
emergent cell population dynamics of the melanoma MITF rheostat. The
model is formulated as a multiscale and phenotype-resolved partial differential
equation for melanoma cell density in the epidermis. We analysed the model
in three incremental stages, first considering subcellular MITF dynamics in
Sect. 2, then well-mixed population dynamics representative of the tumour
core in Sect. 3 and, finally, radially-resolved population dynamics in Sect.
4. The model was kept deliberately simple throughout in order to calibrate
parameter values to published data using Bayesian inference. We summarise
the key findings of each section below and discuss their biological and mathe-
matical implications.
Subcellular model.
We considered subcellular MITF dynamics as a stochastic system of differential
equations for the RNA concentration, protein concentration and phenotype.
The results draw attention to the heteroscedasticity evident in single-cell
sequencing data; the RNA variance increases with its mean. We saw that
this effect may be quantified using a power-law stochastic term in the RNA
dynamics (1). It may be reasoned that the variance is partly attributable
to population selection effects or spatial heterogeneity, rather than subcellu-
lar noise. However, the separation of timescales between the subcellular RNA

dynamics (half-life ≈2.5 hours) and selection effects (minimum doubling time
≈2 days, lifespan ≈1 month) indicates that the subcellular contribution domi-
nates. Moreover, similar heteroscedasticity is seen between melanoma cell lines
in which spatial heterogeneity is minimal [52].
From a biological perspective, the results of Sect. 2 highlight an important
nuance in the context of the MITF rheostat. Since MITF transcription is sup-
pressed by the integrated stress response, a more stressful microenvironment
produces cells with lower RNA heterogeneity and, by consequence, lower sub-
cellular heterogeneity in a phenotype variable coupled downstream of RNA.
Indeed, lower transcription rates select for narrower subcellular phenotype dis-
tributions within individual cells in our model (recall Fig. 7). It follows that
either MITF-low cells are functionally more similar to each other than the cell-
cell heterogeneity present in MITF-high cells, or phenotypic features relevant
to an MITF-low state (e.g., cell motility) have more finely tuned dependence
on MITF than features of an MITF-high state (e.g., pigmentation).
From a mathematical perspective, the derivation of the phenotype flux,
detailed in Appendix B, may serve as a template to incorporate single-cell
RNA sequencing data into the phenotype flux of future structured population
models. The derivation follows standard multiscale homogenisation arguments
for fast–slow stochastic systems [53] and relies on a separation of timescales
between RNA and protein dynamics and a slower phenotype variable. While
reduction to a single variable is not strictly necessary when considering the
dynamics of individual cells, the procedure considerably lessens the computa-
tional burden of carrying multiple structure variables at the population scale.
An interesting output of the homogenisation is that the individual magni-
tudes of phenotype drift and diffusivity are specified in terms of subcellular
parameters that are measurable. By contrast, previous structured population
models have introduced diffusivity either as an independent parameter or via
the upscaling of a discretised structure variable [54–56].
Population model.
In Sect. 3, we considered spatially homogeneous but phenotype-resolved
melanoma cell population dynamics. The model couples the average MITF
transcription rate inversely to the total cell density. In essence, we used the
total cell density as a proxy for cell stress, reasoning that higher densities pro-
mote increased nutrient competition, less oxygen per cell and higher levels of
mechanical confinement, the latter of which has been recently shown to itself
drive invasive gene programs [40]. After an initial transient, the model predicts
a prolonged period of balanced exponential growth that consists primarily of
differentiated and proliferative cells. Once the cell density grows large enough
to affect MITF transcription/translation, the model melanoma tends towards
one of three stable late-stage behaviours that may be resolved by their degree
of contact inhibition, κ. For high κ there is a low-density “PRO/DIF” steady
state balance between proliferative (PRO) and non-cycling, highly differenti-
ated (DIF) cells. Intermediate values of κ yield a high density “INV/PRO”

steady state balance between non-cycling, invasive cells (INV) and prolifera-
tive cells. Finally, low values of κ produce a cyclic solution that is periodically
dominated by either invasive or proliferative cells. We discuss the biological
interpretation of these late-stage behaviours below.
It is tempting to identify the PRO/DIF state with a non-cancerous popu-
lation of healthy melanocytes. However, melanocytes are typically longer-lived
(e.g., years [57]) than the months timescale derived from melanoma TUNEL
data (Appendix E). We therefore suggest that an appropriate interpretation
of the PRO/DIF state is either a non-cancerous but atypical melanocyte pop-
ulation with faster turnover on the scale of months, or a superficial spreading
melanoma that lacks metastatic potential without further genetic mutation.
The INV/PRO state may be interpreted as the late-stage behaviour of a
melanoma cell population with metastatic potential. In this state the cells
have grown to densities that yield stress-induced invasive phenotype switching,
consistent with nodular melanoma or superficial spreading melanoma that has
transitioned to vertical growth [58]. The cyclic state that the model produces
at minimal levels of contact inhibition is more difficult to place in biological
reality. We note that these density oscillations are distinct from those of cell
cycle synchrony previously identified in melanoma cell lines [59]. In our model,
the cell density may oscillate five-fold over a two month period at the lowest
value, κ = 0. Even if these cycles are restricted to a neighbourhood of the
core, as the spatial solutions indicate, we anticipate that such extreme density
changes would have been reported in a clinical study. Hence, it is likely that
the cyclic solutions are unrealistic and provide a lower bound κ > 0.57 for the
parameters used in this model.
The population model analysis also revealed an important nuance concern-
ing phenotype reversibility. Specifically, the region of bistability between the
PRO/DIF and INV/PRO states seen in Fig. 13 gives rise to hysteresis. Indeed,
as confirmed in the numerical experiment of Fig. 14, if the model melanoma
is initially in the healthier PRO/DIF state with 4.5 < κ < 10.5 and suffers a
mutation which sufficiently lowers contact inhibition, then it will tend to the
INV/PRO state. Importantly, subsequently reversing the degree of contact
inhibition to previous levels will not reverse the phenotype distribution back
into the DIF/PRO state. Hence, phenotype reversibility at the level of indi-
vidual cells, as is afforded by the MITF rheostat, does not yield phenotypic
reversibility at the population scale. This is because individual cell dynamics
are coupled to each other, in this model via the total cell density, and so aggre-
gating the behaviour of individual cells as if their dynamics are independent
will not, in general, produce a reliable assessment of population behaviour.
Spatially-resolved population model.
Finally, we considered a radially-resolved extension to the population model
in Sect. 4. The model assumes that cell motion is random but with phenotype-
dependent motility so that MITF-high differentiated cells are less motile than
MITF-low invasive cells. The spatially-resolved model melanoma grows in a

wave-like manner that eventually propagates with a constant radial speed. The
behaviour of the core is consistent with the predictions of the spatially homo-
geneous model and, in particular, depends on the degree of contact inhibition.
High contact inhibition yields a slowly propagating PRO/DIF state. Inter-
mediate levels of contact inhibition produce a faster wave with a PRO/DIF
wavefront and INV/PRO core. Low levels of contact inhibition give rise to the
fastest waves with a PRO/DIF wavefront and an oscillating core. Analysis of
the wave speed is consistent with a wave that is pulled by proliferation near
the front, rather than driven by nonlinear core dynamics.
The PRO/DIF wavefront present in all solutions is a direct consequence
of our use of cell density as a proxy for stress. The cells near the front are
subject to the lowest densities and, therefore, the highest MITF transcription
rates. While we believe cell density is a reasonable inverse proxy for stress
within the epidermis, due to its avascularity and lack of extracellular matrix
beyond the basement membrane, it is unlikely to be sufficient for vertical
growth melanoma. Indeed, vertical growth phase melanomas typically exhibit
an invasive front in the dermis [60].
The solution with oscillating core may be of further mathematical interest.
The oscillations about r = 0 neighbour a spatially homogeneous region with
identical phenotype distribution to the unstable continuation of the INV/PRO
state towards κ = 0. This feature suggests that cell motion in the spatially-
resolved model collapses the oscillations and stabilises the INV/PRO state in
the bulk of the solution. It would be interesting to explore this behaviour in
a more tractable model where an inner oscillatory solution may be matched
to a similar multi-peaked wavefront profile via asymptotic analysis.
Future work.
The model framework is readily extended to study vertical growth phase
melanoma. The key difficulty lies in adjusting the coupling of MITF expres-
sion to an appropriate proxy for cell stress in the dermis. To produce an
invasive front, it may be necessary to explicitly account for extracellular
matrix. Moreover, we are interested in the impact of various cells in the
melanoma microenvironment. While early non-invasive melanomas are pri-
marily surrounded by keratinocytes, as the tumours grow towards the deeper
subcutaneous tissue, they become surrounded by lipid-laden adipocytes.
Adipocytes participate in fatty acid signalling with melanoma cells that may
stabilise the invasive phenotype [61, 62]. Resolving these contributions by
extending the current model is a promising direction of ongoing work.
The subcellular dynamics of the model may also be adjusted to account
for possible subcellular bistability. To this effect, there are numerous subcel-
lular positive feedback loops that may yield bistability between an MITF-low
invasive state and an MITF-high differentiated state (e.g., between MITF
and BRN2 [63], or between MITF and the fatty acid saturation ratio [64]).
Understanding the population-level impact of cells with intrinsic phenotype
bistability is ongoing work.

Conclusions.
The mathematical model in this paper revealed that the MITF rheostat admits
three distinct and stable long-term population behaviours in the epidermis. In
order of decreasing contact inhibition, there is a slow-growing melanoma of pro-
liferative cells and non-cycling, differentiated cells without invasive potential,
a faster-propagating melanoma with a steady state invasive core, and a rapidly
growing melanoma with oscillatory core dynamics. In addition to nuances spe-
cific to MITF, the analysis highlights that phenotype reversibility at the level
of individual cells does not imply reversibility of phenotype distributions at
the population scale. These findings further the understanding of melanoma
population dynamics and the more general phenomenon of cell plasticity.
