ABSTRACT
Ultra-cool dwarf stars (UCDs) are a common source of contamination in high-redshift galaxy searches
as both sources are red and these early-forming galaxies can have sizes that are difficult to resolve even
with space telescopes. Standard selection techniques, including photometric redshift estimation and
color–color criteria, cannot fully eliminate this contamination. We develop Foreground Contamination
Evaluator of Nearby dwarf stars in high-Z photometrically selected Objects (FC-ENZO), a code that
predicts the number of dwarf stars misidentified as high-redshift galaxies for a given survey setup. FC-
ENZO models the number of UCDs and evaluates the fraction of synthesized dwarf stars that passes
user-specified selection methods. We compare two synthetic spectral energy distribution libraries and
find that the ELF OWL library, which relaxes the assumption of chemical equilibrium, predicts larger
contaminant fractions than the BOBCAT library, because of stronger absorption features around 1
µm. The contamination fraction increases with metallicity and also depends on the adopted stellar
number-density model. The dominant contaminants are T to early Y-type UCDs, which are most com-
monly misclassified as galaxies at z ∼8. Comparing deep surveys from different space telescopes, we
find similar overall contamination levels within the same redshift range. However, the contamination is
concentrated near the limiting magnitude of each survey. At brighter magnitudes, the relative contam-
ination is highest for HST (COSMOS), followed by Roman deep-tier survey, and JWST. Although the
predicted contaminant numbers remain sensitive to model assumptions, FC-ENZO provides a practical
tool for survey design and for identifying optimal fields for spectroscopic follow-up.

1. INTRODUCTION
High-redshift galaxy candidates are commonly iden-
tified using color–color selection techniques targeting
Lyman-break galaxies (LBGs), photometric redshift es-
timates, or a combination of both methods. The former
approach, often referred to as the Lyman-break galaxy
selection technique (e.g., C. C. Steidel et al. 1999), has
been widely used because it requires observations in
only a limited number of filters.
In contrast, photo-
metric redshift estimation has gained prominence over
the past decade, as it exploits information from all ob-
served bands, thereby maximizing the scientific return
from multiwavelength datasets. Photometric redshift es-
timation can be applied in conjunction with color–color
selection (e.g., G. Roberts-Borsani et al. 2022; R. J.
Bouwens et al. 2015) or used as a stand-alone selection
criterion (e.g., K. E. Heintz et al. 2025).
Photometrically selected candidates can be contam-
inated by two types of objects:
low-redshift galaxies
and ultra-cool dwarfs (UCDs) — stellar and substellar
objects of spectral types M, L, T, and Y with masses
M ≲0.1, M⊙and effective temperatures Teff ≲3000,K
(J. D. Kirkpatrick 2005).
Contamination from low-
redshift galaxies arises because their Balmer-breaks can
produces spectral features that resemble Lyα breaks
of high-redshift galaxies. Stellar contamination occurs
because, at faint magnitudes, point sources are often
difficult to distinguish morphologically from compact
extended objects, especially when the SourceExtractor
stellarity parameter CLASS STAR is used to quantify the
likelihood that an object is point-like (S. L. Finkelstein
et al. 2015; T. Morishita 2021). In addition, UCDs ex-
hibit colors similar to those of high-redshift galaxies. In
particular, M-type stars primarily contaminate R- and
V -band dropout selections (E. R. Stanway et al. 2008;

An example of such contamination was demonstrated
in G. Roberts-Borsani et al. (2025), who followed up
10 of the 26 photometrically selected z ∼8 candi-

survey, a pure-parallel imaging program on the Hubble
Space Telescope (HST). Of the ten targets, two were
confirmed to be low-redshift contaminants, and one was
identified as a T2 brown dwarf.
An accurate estimation of contamination is crucial for
at least two key areas of high-redshift galaxy studies.
The first is the determination of the galaxy luminosity
function (LF), which underpins a wide range of derived
quantities, including the cosmic star-formation rate den-
sity(e.g., S. L. Finkelstein et al. 2023; B. Robertson et al.
2024), the UV luminosity density (e.g., P. A. Oesch et al.
2018; Y. Harikane et al. 2024), and the neutral hydro-
gen fraction during the epoch of reionization (e.g., B. E.
Robertson et al. 2015; L. Whitler et al. 2025).
The
second is the measurement of galaxy clustering, which
links high redshift galaxies to their underlying dark mat-
ter halos through quantities such as the luminosity de-
pendence of galaxy bias (e.g., Y. Harikane et al. 2016;
N. Dalmasso et al. 2024b,a) and overdensity measure-
ments (e.g., R. A. Sutanto et al. 2026; K. C. Kreilgaard
et al. 2026).
Even a small fraction of contaminants
can significantly bias measurements at the bright end of
the galaxy population where number statistics are lim-
ited. This is especially relevant for wide-area surveys
including pure-parallel space telescope programs such
as BoRG, ground-based surveys (e.g., R. A. A. Bowler
et al. 2012), and the upcoming surveys with the Nancy
Roman Grace Telescopes.
In the literature involving high-redshift galaxy selec-
tion, there are two steps commonly taken to account
for UCDs contamination. The first step occurs during
candidate selection, where sources likely to be UCDs
are rejected. Most studies implement this by including
UCDs templates in spectral energy distribution (SED)
fitting and comparing the goodness of fit (χ2) between
the best-fit galaxy template and the best-fit stellar tem-
plate. Candidates that are better fit by stellar templates
are then excluded (e.g., R. A. A. Bowler et al. 2012; R. J.
McLure et al. 2013; R. J. Bouwens et al. 2015; S. L.
Finkelstein et al. 2022; M. Franco et al. 2025). Other
studies use color–color information to exclude the local-
ized region occupied by brown dwarfs, often in tandem
with size information such as stellarity (e.g., M. Ouchi
et al. 2009; R. J. Bouwens et al. 2011; S. L. Finkelstein
et al. 2015; T. Morishita et al. 2018). Studies target-
ing z ≳9, or those that rely primarily on JWST data,
may not include this step because UCDs contamination
is generally expected to be less significant at these red-
shifts (P. A. Oesch et al. 2013; A. Weibel et al. 2026), be-
cause photometry beyond 2 µm can help mitigate UCDs
contamination, or because the sources are spatially re-
solved with JWST (S. L. Finkelstein et al. 2024; N.
Leethochawalit et al. 2026).
The second step is to statistically account for the prob-
ability that each final galaxy candidate is a UCDs dur-
ing the LF calculation process.
This step is not in-
cluded in all studies in the literature.
Among those
that do account for it, some statistically estimate the
contamination rate based on a spectroscopic subsample
(C. C. Steidel et al. 1996), resample candidates accord-
ing to the probability that each source is a dwarf (N.
Leethochawalit et al. 2023), or use injection–recovery
simulations of brown dwarfs, either at the catalog level
(M. Ouchi et al. 2009) or directly in the imaging data
(R. A. A. Bowler et al. 2015; R. J. Bouwens et al. 2015).
In this paper, we follow the catalog-level injection–
recovery simulation method of M. Ouchi et al. (2009)
and develop a code to calculate contamination from
UCDs in photometrically selected high-redshift galaxy
samples,
Foreground
Contamination
Evaluator
of
Nearby dwarf stars in high-Z photometrically selected
Objects (FC-ENZO)4, which can be conveniently ap-
plied to a wide range of observational setups and selec-
tion methods.
In this code, we incorporate the most
up-to-date number density models and synthetic spec-
tral libraries of UCDs. We also investigate contamina-
tion rates across different redshifts and surveys. These
results can be used to aid observation planning, statis-
tically account for contamination, and inform the prior-
itization of follow-up observations and survey fields.

3. CODE STRUCTURE
FC-ENZO is designed to estimate the number of UCD
contaminants that are misclassified as high-redshift
galaxies under user-specified selection techniques within
a given observation field. The supported galaxy selec-
tion methods include photometric redshift estimation
using EAzY, as well as user-provided color–color selec-
tion. The output provides an estimate of the expected


## Page 4


4
number of UCD contaminants in the target field. We
provide an overview of the user input file and the calcu-
lation structure below, with further details available in
the documentation on GitHub.
3.1. User Input
Initial Setup and Image Quality Parameters —The initial
setup requires the user to specify the field name, as
well as its coordinate and field of view, output direc-
tory, and apparent magnitude bins in a reference fil-
ter over which the contamination will be calculated.
In addition, image calibration parameters must be pro-
vided for each filter used in the galaxy selection criteria:
PHOTPLAM, PHOTFLAM, EXPTIME, and the limiting magni-
tude (depths). These parameters vary across fields and
are used for estimating the noise of the simulated UCDs
(see details in Appendix A).
Selection Method Settings —The user can choose whether
the output should simply report the numbers of UCDs in
the given field or, alternatively, the number of UCDs ex-
pected to contaminate a given galaxy sample in a given
field. If the latter case, users must specify the stellar
model, stellar metallicity, and the galaxy selection crite-
ria. Currently, two options (or a combination of both)
options are supported: photometric redshift selection
with EAzY and color-color selection.
3.2. Step 1: Generate catalogs of UCDs at different
Magnitude Bins
For each apparent magnitude bin in a specified de-
tection band, we generate a catalog of all UCDs in the
stellar evolutionary model spectra. We shift the UCDs
so that their flux in the detection band matches the
value of the bin center, with fluxes in other filters scaled
proportionally. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in each
filter is computed by combining photon-counting (Pois-
son) noise with contributions from read noise, dark cur-
rent, and background noise (through 5σ limiting magni-
tude). The noise calculation requires calibration param-
eters such as PHOTFLAM, PHOTPLAM, and exposure time
to convert cgs fluxes into photon counts (see appendix
A for further details). Using these “true” shifted fluxes
and the SNR estimates, we then generate the “observed”
fluxes assuming a Gaussian noise.
3.3. Step 2: Misclassification Fractions
After generating the “observed” flux catalog, we ap-
ply galaxy selection criteria to evaluate the misclassifi-
cation fraction, i.e., the fraction of UCDs in each spec-
tral class that satisfy the galaxy selection criteria. The
implemented options are (1) photometric redshift esti-
mation using EAzY, and (2) the color–color selection
method. The result from EAzY is typically used in two
ways: first, by comparing the relative goodness-of-fit
between stellar and galaxy templates, and second, by
applying constraints on the redshift range and redshift
probability distribution p(z), often in combination with
color–color criteria. The color–color selection uses two
colors, usually constructed from magnitudes in three fil-
ters.
The criteria require (i) a strong flux decrement
due to absorption blueward of Lyα, and (ii) the object
not to have an excessively red continuum slope, to sup-
press contamination from low-redshift dusty or passive
galaxies. These criteria are further supplemented by the
requirement of non-detections in filters bluer than Lyα
and a minimum signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) detection
in the UV continuum. Based on the selection criteria,
we calculate the misclassification fractions, i.e. the frac-
tion of mock UCDs of each stellar type that satisfies all
galaxy selection criteria. We use the standard binomial
sampling uncertainty to estimate the uncertainties.
We note that morphology-based criteria, such as
CLASS STAR from SourceExtractor, are not included
since our analysis is performed at the catalog level.
Moreover, the magnitude ranges in which we assess con-
tamination typically correspond to regimes where ex-
tended sources cannot be reliably distinguieshed from
point sources (e.g. m > 25 in HST data; see T. Mor-
ishita et al. (2020)). Accordingly, we assume that point
sources and extended sources are indistinguishable in
this context.
3.4. Step 3: Number Density of UCDs in the observed
field
In this step, we estimate the number of UCDs from
late-M to early-Y types at each magnitude bin in the
observed field. We adopt the UCDs model distribution
from C. Aganze et al. (2022b) and E. J. Honaker &
J. E. Gizis (2025), as described in Section 2.2. Specfici-
cally, we use Equation 1 to compute the expected num-
ber of UCDs of spectral type SpT type in magnitude bin
mx, denoted N(SpT, mx). We conservatively determine
the minimum and maximum line-of-sight distances, dmin
and dmax, by accounting for both the intrinsic scatter in
UCDs luminosities and the width of the magnitude bin.
Specifically, dmin is defined as the lower 1σ limit of the
distances obtained by shifting UCDs of a given spectral
type to mbin +0.5∆m mag. Similarly, dmax is defined as
the upper 1σ limit of the distances obtained by shifting
the UCDs to mbin −0.5∆m mag.
To account for uncertainties in the stellar distribution
models, we sample the local density ρ⊙and scale height
H parameters 100 times, assuming a gaussian distribu-

4. RESULTS
We first present the results of testing the code on the
follow-up fields from G. Roberts-Borsani et al. (2025).
We then examine the robustness of the predicted con-
tamination rates under different evolutionary and num-
ber density models.
Finally, we discuss strategies to
mitigate UCDs contamination on the HST observations.
4.1. Demonstrating Our Pipeline for High-Redshift
Follow-Up Prioritization
We first check whether our contaminant estimation
could help flagging the fields with possible high con-
tamination rates from UCDs in real observations. G.
Roberts-Borsani et al. (2022) identified 26 z ∼8 high-
confidence candidates from 288 HST parallel fields com-
piled in the (Super)Brightest of Reionizing Galxies (Su-
perBoRG) data set (T. Morishita 2021).
For each
final candidate, they calculated the probability that
the source is a brown dwarf based on the χ2 com-
parison, adopting a uniform brown dwarf number den-
sity across the extragalactic sky as the prior.
Ten of
the highest-priority candidates were followed-up with
NIRSpec/JWST(GO 1747, G. Roberts-Borsani et al.
(2025)). Of these, seven were confirmed as high-z galax-
ies, two were z ∼2 interlopers and one was confirmed to
be a T2-type UCDs. Here we will apply our code to the
fields of the ten z ∼8 candidates and see if the priorities
would be altered.
We run FC-ENZO using the fiducial setting i.e., us-
ing the Milky Way distribution model from C. Aganze
et al. (2022b) and the Elf Owl stellar model. We im-
plement the same z ∼8 selection criteria as G. Roberts-
Borsani et al. (2022), including the Y098/Y105 −J125 and
J125 −H160 color–color cuts, photometric redshift selec-
tion with EAzY (G. B. Brammer et al. 2008) using the
V1.3 galaxy template set, and rejection of sources better
fit by brown dwarf templates from the SpeX Prism Li-
brary (A. J. Burgasser 2014). The best-fit photometric
redshift is required to satisfy 7.5 ≤zp ≤8.5 with P(z >
6.5) > 0.7. For each field, we model the UCD contam-
inants over the magnitude range 24.0 < H160 < 26.75,
corresponding to the candidate magnitudes reported by
G. Roberts-Borsani et al. (2022).

4.2.1. Dependence on Stellar Evolution Models
Here, we repeat the analysis described above, but now
using the Sonora Bobcat model to represent UCDs.
The Sonora Bobcat suite has been widely adopted in
the literature (e.g., M. S. Marley et al. 2021; B. Lacy
& A. Burrows 2023), and is an earlier generation of the
Elf Owl suite. The primary difference is that the Sonora
Bobcat models assume chemical equilibrium, while Elf
Owl does not. The grids span surface gravities of 3.25 ≤
log g (cgs) ≤5.5, effective temperatures from 200 K to
2400 K, and metallicities of [M/H] = −0.5, 0.0, and
+0.5. In total, the library contains 850 spectra, each
covering wavelengths from 0.6 to 20 µm.
Here we compare the contamination results using on
solar-metallicity UCDs ([M/H] = 0) from both model
suites on the same ten BoRG fields as in Section 4.1.
All other parameters and procedures remain consistent
with the previous setup.
We show the difference in
the contamination results in Table 2. The Sonora Elf
Owl model gives contamination rates up to seven times
higher than the Sonora Bobcat model in the fields with
higher contamination rates. In the field with confirmed
contaminant, the Bobcat model predicts approximately
0.02 UCD contaminants per field for the z ∼8 selection.
Four out of ten fields show zero UCD contaminants per
field.
The discrepancy in contamination levels can be at-
tributed to two key differences between the two model
grids. First is the number of stars in the model grids.
The Elf Owl models contain far more spectra at so-
lar metallicity (7,200 targets) compared to the Bobcat
model (429 targets), making Bobcat less sensitive in
the low-contamination regime, yielding zero contami-
nant.
Second, Bobcat assumes chemical equilibrium,
whereas Elf Owl incorporates disequilibrium chemistry.
As a result, some Elf Owl spectra exhibit enhanced fea-
tures beyond 1 µm (S. Mukherjee et al. 2023), producing
slightly redder SEDs. This difference translates into dis-
tinct distributions in the best-fit redshifts (zp) derived
with EAzY. In general, both evolution grids produce
bimodal distribution: one at 1.7 and another at z ∼7–
8.
However, the Elf owl grid yields a more complex
structure at higher redshift, with two peaks at z ∼7.4
and z ∼7.8, while Bobcat produces a single peak at
z ∼7.3. Because the photometric-redshift selection in
G. Roberts-Borsani et al. (2022) requires zp > 7.5, a
larger fraction of Elf Owl UCDs satisfy the galaxy se-
lection criteria compared to those from the Bobcat grid.
Given that the Elf Owl grid includes a larger number
of spectra and incorporates more up-to-date physics, we
adopt the Elf Owl grid as our fiducial model grid. All
subsequent analyses in this work are mainly based on
the Elf Owl models.
4.2.2. Dependence on Stellar Metallicities
In previous sections, we have used theoretical spectra
assuming solar metallicity, [M/H] = 0. In reality, Milky
Way stars span a range of metallicities. Halo stars are
metal poor with [Fe/H] ≲−1.2 (e.g., Y. S. Lee et al.
2017; C. Conroy et al. 2019). In the solar neighborhood,
most stars cluster around solar metallicity, with [Fe/H]
ranging from approximately −0.5 to 0.5 (M. Haywood
2001; S. Feltzing & M. Chiba 2013). The disk structure
is further complicated by corrugations and gradients in
both radial and vertical directions (e.g., C. Chiappini
et al. 1997; J. Bland-Hawthorn et al. 2019; S. E. Dietz
et al. 2020).
We explore how the contamination rate
changes when adopting different fixed stellar metallic-
ities, varying [M/H] between runs.
We use all avail-
able metallicities in the Elf Owl models which include
[M/H] = ±1.0, +0.7, ±0.5, and 0.0.
We present the results on five randomly-selected
BoRG fields with z ∼8 galaxy selection criteria in Fig-
ure 2. The figure shows the total contamination num-
bers, summed over the magnitude range 24.0–26.75, as
a function of metallicity. A clear trend is observed: the
contamination for z ∼8 galaxy selection strongly in-
creases with metallicity in all fields. Generally, the con-
tamination rate at [M/H] = +1.0 is 0.5-1 dex higher
than that at [M/H] = −1.0.
We note that each metallicity in the Bobcat and the
Elf Owl is associated with a range of C/O ratios, span-
ning 0.5–1.5 times and 0.5–2.5 times the solar value,
respectively. The latter range encompasses the full dis-
tribution of C/O ratios observed in nearby M dwarfs,
while the former covers most of the observed distribu-
tion (T. Nakajima & S. Sorahana 2016). In our default
analysis, we include all available spectra. To assess the
impact of varying C/O ratios, we repeated the analy-
sis using only spectra with a solar C/O ratio and found
that the results remain unchanged within 1 σ.
In the context of our contamination calculation, fully
incorporating these chemical inhomogeneities is non-
trivial. At a fixed apparent magnitude, potential UCD
contaminants originate from a wide range of distances,
and different spectral types probe different Galactic vol-
umes. For example, F160W = 26 mag corresponds to an
M9 dwarf located at approximately 3–10 kpc, while a Y0
dwarf at the same apparent magnitude would lie at 0.13
kpc from the Sun. These variations imply that a self-
consistent treatment would require a detailed Galactic
chemical-evolution model coupled to a 3D stellar-density

4.3.1. Contaminants in Different Redshift Selections
In the previous sections, we focused on contaminants

at other redshift ranges. We adopt imaging data from
the Cosmic Evolution Survey (COSMOS; R. E. Skelton
et al. 2014; N. A. Grogin et al. 2011; A. M. Koekemoer
et al. 2011). The original mosaic covers an area of ap-

from G. Roberts-Borsani et al. (2022). All criteria in-

4.3.2. Sources of Contamination and Dropout Filters for
z ∼8 Selection
In the previous section, we showed that the z ∼8
selection is the most susceptible to contamination from
UCDs. For HST, two commonly used dropout selections
for z ∼8 are the F098M- and F105W-dropout selec-
tions. Interestingly, the top three lines in Figure 2 cor-
respond to fields that use F105W-dropout criteria, while
the bottom two lines, which exhibit lower contamination
rates, correspond to fields that use F098M-dropout cri-
teria. We therefore compare the performance of these
two selection criteria presented in G. Roberts-Borsani
et al. (2022).
The two selections are identical except
for their color–color cuts: the Z8098 selection adopts
Y098 −J125 > 1.75, whereas the Z8105 selection uses
Y105 −J125 > 0.45.
We simulate synthetic UCDs based on the depths of
the BoRG-0409-5317 field, which includes both F098M
and F105W observations with comparable 5σ limiting
magnitudes, at 26.9 and 27.1 mag respectively. Other
filters used in the analysis are F606W, F814W, F125W,
F140W, and F160W with 5σ limiting magnitudes of
26.35, 25.55, 27.02, 27.09, 26.75, respectively.
In the
left panels of Figure 5, the gray points represent simu-

indicate those that satisfy all selection criteria, includ-
ing the photometric redshift constraint, signal-to-noise
requirements, and the χ2 comparison.
From the figure, it is evident that a larger fraction of
UCDs satisfy the Y105 color selection compared to the
Y098 selection. Most UCDs have similar magnitudes in
both F105W and F098M, indicating that the difference
in contamination rates is due to the criteria involving
the F098M/F105W−F125W color, where the threshold
in one case is more permissive than the other.
We further investigate the SEDs of UCDs that sat-
isfy all z ∼8F 105W galaxy selection criteria in the right
panels of Figure 5. We find that the contaminants are
predominantly ultra-cool dwarfs with spectral types L9
and later (Teff ≲1300K). These dwarfs show strong FeH

and F105W filters. However, for z ∼8 galaxies (top
panel), the F105W filter still includes part of the Lyman
continuum. As a result, the F105W−F125W dropout
criterion must be less stringent than the correspond-
ing F098M−F125W dropout criterion, allowing substan-
tially more UCD contaminants to enter the selection.

6. CONCLUSIONS
In this work, we present FC-ENZO, a code developed to
estimate the magnitude-dependent contamination from
ultra-cool dwarf stars (UCDs) that can mimic high-
redshift galaxy candidates.
The code can be used to
design observations or identify fields with potentially
high contamination rates prior to spectroscopic follow-
up observations. FC-ENZO operates at mock-catalog and
survey-characteristics level by combining UCD number
density models, synthetic spectral libraries, and galaxy
selection methods.
We test the robustness of the code against different
model assumptions. We also apply galaxy selection cri-
teria across a range of redshifts and to representative
surveys from HST, JWST, and the Nancy Grace Ro-
man telescopes. Our main findings are as follows.
(i) Absolute contamination rates are sensitive to as-
sumptions in the model, such as the adopted stel-
lar evolution models, metallicities, and stellar dis-
tribution models, leading to variations of up to
several tens of a factor in the predicted contami-
nation rates. However, the former sources of un-
