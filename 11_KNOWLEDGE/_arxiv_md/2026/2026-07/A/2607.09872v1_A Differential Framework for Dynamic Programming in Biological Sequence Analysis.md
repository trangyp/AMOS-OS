Kiyoshi Asai1,2∗

Background: Dynamic programming in biological sequence analysis computes probabili-
ties or partition functions by summing over exponentially many latent paths, alignments,
derivation trees, or RNA secondary structures. Their backward and outside quantities are
used model-specifically, but the relation between differential sensitivities and exact finite
sequence changes is rarely stated in a common framework.
Methods: We represent hidden Markov models, affine-gap alignment ensembles, stochastic
context-free grammars, and RNA secondary-structure ensembles as sum–product dynamic
programs, defining backward and outside quantities as adjoints of forward or inside
variables and sequence changes as finite replacements of sequence-dependent local factors.
Results: Posterior item marginals are normalized inside–outside products, local-event
posteriors additionally include the local factor and child inside terms, and expected
feature counts are logarithmic derivatives of the partition function. For HMMs, ordinary
SCFGs, and single-position substitutions in affine-gap alignment, the partition function
is multi-affine in position-specific factor groups, so a one-site change is recovered exactly
from first-derivative coefficients and multisite changes from mixed derivatives. In nearest-
neighbor RNA models a substitution alters overlapping loop, stacking, and multiloop factors
and boundary contexts, so exact mutation effects instead require context-dependent inside–
outside recombination, as in the Rchange algorithm. Numerical experiments reproduce
brute-force recomputation to machine precision.
Conclusions: The framework identifies when derivatives give exact finite sequence
effects and when broader recombination is required, providing a unified basis for posterior
marginals, expected counts, parameter sensitivity, mutation analysis, and sequence design.
Keywords: dynamic programming; adjoint variables; hidden Markov model; sequence
alignment; stochastic context-free grammar; RNA secondary structure; partition function;
finite sequence perturbation

Introduction
Dynamic programming underlies many biological sequence-analysis methods that sum
over exponentially many latent objects, including hidden state paths, alignment paths,
derivation trees, and RNA secondary structures. Although these models differ biologically
and geometrically, each evaluates a global probability or partition function by recursively
combining local factors.
Their forward or inside variables collect partial structures, whereas backward or outside
variables collect compatible contexts. These quantities are usually introduced separately
in HMMs, alignment, SCFGs, and RNA folding (Durbin et al., 1998), but they share a
common interpretation: a backward or outside quantity is an adjoint, namely the derivative
of the global partition function with respect to an intermediate forward or inside variable.
Posterior marginals are then normalized inside–outside products, and expected feature
counts are logarithmic derivatives with respect to local factors.
This interpretation is connected to semiring dynamic programming (Goodman, 1999),
sum–product message passing (Kschischang et al., 2001), network-polynomial differentia-
tion (Darwiche, 2003), reverse-mode automatic differentiation (Griewank and Walther,
2008; Baydin et al., 2018), and its application to structured prediction (Eisner, 2016).
Differentiable dynamic programming has also been developed as a trainable structured
layer (Mensch and Blondel, 2018). We take this adjoint interpretation as established
background; the contribution here concerns not the interpretation itself but its exact
relation to finite sequence changes.
That relation—between infinitesimal derivatives and discrete sequence changes—has
been treated less systematically than the adjoint identities themselves. A parameter
derivative describes infinitesimal sensitivity, whereas a nucleotide or amino-acid substitution
finitely replaces the local factors selected by the observed symbols. These coincide only
under specific algebraic conditions. After introducing position-specific factor groups, the
partition functions of HMMs and of ordinary SCFGs—those in which each derivation
emits exactly one terminal at each sequence position, with no context-dependent emission
factorization—are multi-affine in those groups; the same holds for a substituted position

in the affine-gap alignment ensemble considered here. A one-site replacement is then
recovered exactly from first-derivative coefficients, whereas multiple replacements require
mixed terms. This is an exact finite polynomial identity, not a first-order approximation.
Nearest-neighbor RNA thermodynamic models expose the boundary of this simple
position-wise factorization. One nucleotide can alter several overlapping hairpin, interior-
loop, stacking, dangling-end, and multiloop factors, as well as the boundary contexts of
dynamic-programming cells. Exact mutation effects therefore require context-dependent
recombination of inside quantities, mutant local factors, and outside contexts. Predicting
how mutations perturb the RNA structural ensemble has been addressed by a range of
tools (Waldispühl et al., 2008; Halvorsen et al., 2010; Sabarinathan et al., 2013; Churkin
and Barash, 2006); among these, the Rchange algorithm computes exact partition-function
changes by this context-dependent recombination (Kiryu and Asai, 2012).
This paper makes four contributions. First, we place HMMs, affine-gap alignment
ensembles (in the Boltzmann formulation of Miyazawa (1995), which relates alignment
to both pair-HMMs and RNA thermodynamics), SCFGs, and RNA partition-function
programs in a single sum–product and adjoint calculus, and within it we keep separate
three objects that are easily conflated: posterior item marginals, posterior local-event
terms, and expected feature counts obtained as logarithmic derivatives of the partition
function. Second, and centrally, we give a model-independent sufficient criterion—multi-
affinity of the partition function in position-specific factor groups—under which the adjoint
(first-derivative) coefficients reconstruct an exact finite sequence substitution (Section 2.3).
HMMs, ordinary SCFGs, and single-site substitutions in affine-gap alignment satisfy it,
so a one-site change is an exact polynomial identity in the outside quantities, whereas
multisite changes require the corresponding mixed derivatives. Third, we identify nearest-
neighbor RNA thermodynamics as the regime in which this criterion fails, and show that
the Rchange algorithm realizes precisely the finite inside–local–outside recombination that
the framework prescribes once a single derivative no longer suffices (Section 4). Fourth, we
verify the finite-replacement identities numerically against brute-force recomputation for
HMM, alignment, SCFG, and RNA-like models, obtaining machine-precision agreement

Extensions to Alignment and RNA Structure En-
sembles
Alignment and RNA folding instantiate the same adjoint, posterior, and logarithmic-
derivative identities as Section 2, but they also expose two important extensions. In
affine-gap alignment, a residue may be consumed without using any match factor, so the
relevant position group is affine but not generally homogeneous. In nearest-neighbor RNA
folding, one nucleotide can alter several overlapping factors and boundary contexts, so a
mutation is not localized to one position-specific factor group.

derivatives with respect to energy factors give expected structural features and first-order
rankings of candidate mutations; the exact effect of a substitution additionally inserts the
mutant factors, includes their joint products, and evaluates all affected boundary contexts.
The Rchange algorithm can thus be read as the finite-difference side of the same adjoint
framework: inside and outside quantities identify reusable structural contexts, while finite
replacement of the sequence-dependent thermodynamic factors produces the exact mutant
partition function.

Computational Implications
After one forward/inside and backward/outside pass, a candidate substitution is evaluated
by contracting stored coefficients with changed local factors, rather than rerunning the
global dynamic program. Table 3 summarizes representative costs. An HMM candidate
costs O(Q) after an O(LQ2) pass; an alignment substitution in x costs O(K) after O(LK)
preprocessing because all partners in y must be considered; and an ordinary SCFG
candidate costs O(N) after the cubic inside–outside computation. Thus an all-substitution
scan replaces repeated global runs by one global pass followed by local contractions. This
does not reduce the base DP complexity or make arbitrary multisite changes inexpensive:
the number of mixed terms can grow exponentially, and RNA requires context-dependent
recombination.

Discussion
7.1
Relation to established views
The adjoint interpretation of backward and outside quantities is established in semiring
dynamic programming, factor-graph message passing, network polynomials, and reverse-
mode automatic differentiation (Goodman, 1999; Kschischang et al., 2001; Darwiche,
25


## Page 26


2003; Griewank and Walther, 2008; Baydin et al., 2018; Eisner, 2016). Differentiable
dynamic programming has also been studied as a trainable structured layer (Mensch and
Blondel, 2018), and discriminative posterior models such as CONTRAfold exploit the
same inside–outside expectations (Do et al., 2006). The present contribution is to organize
these ideas for biological sequence models and to delimit when local derivatives reconstruct
exact discrete sequence changes.
Two distinctions are central. First, AuBu/Z is the posterior use of an item, whereas a
specific local rule has posterior Buϕr
Q
v Av/Z. Second, an infinitesimal derivative is not
generally a finite mutation effect. Proposition 1 shows that a one-site replacement is exact
under multi-affinity, while multisite replacements require mixed terms. Nearest-neighbor
RNA models mark the boundary of this simple position-wise factorization because one
nucleotide changes overlapping factors and boundary contexts; the Rchange algorithm
therefore uses context-dependent finite recombination (Kiryu and Asai, 2012), in contrast
to tools that quantify mutation-induced structural change by other means (Waldispühl
et al., 2008; Halvorsen et al., 2010; Sabarinathan et al., 2013; Churkin and Barash, 2006).
The reverse-mode interpretation developed here also points to a related optimization-
based extension. In max-plus or min-plus dynamic programs, such as Viterbi alignment and
minimum-free-energy RNA folding, the reverse computation propagates through optimizing
branches and is closely related to traceback. When the optimum is unique, the selected
optimal derivation can be viewed as a gradient of the optimal value with respect to local
scores, yielding optimal feature counts; when optima are tied, this interpretation requires
generalized derivatives or a set-valued subgradient. A full treatment of this connection,
including its relation to semiring dynamic programming and structured prediction, is
beyond the scope of the present work.
7.2
Implications for inference and design
The same dynamic program supports distinct queries: posterior marginals, expected
feature counts, infinitesimal parameter sensitivities, and exact finite sequence changes.
Keeping these objects separate avoids a common ambiguity in gradient-based sequence
26


## Page 27


analysis. Gradients can rank candidate changes or support continuous optimization, but
they equal a discrete edit only in the special multi-affine setting. For several sites, mixed
terms measure finite-site interaction; for nearest-neighbor RNA, exact evaluation also
requires mutant boundary contexts. In RNA analysis, posterior base-pairing probabilities
and their parameter derivatives already underlie applications such as the interpretation of
structural-probing data (Yamamura et al., 2025a) and the estimation of thermodynamic
parameters (Yamamura et al., 2025b).
Normalized HMM and SCFG probabilities and unnormalized alignment and RNA
Boltzmann weights share the same factor-level calculus. Probability-simplex constraints
or energy parameterizations enter only through an additional chain rule. Automatic
differentiation can generate the adjoints, whereas explicit reverse recursions reveal their
biological meaning and enable model-specific reuse and recombination.

7.4
Scope and limitations
The analysis assumes a finite acyclic sum–product computation with local factorization.
Adjoint identities extend algebraically beyond nonnegative weights, but posterior inter-
pretations require nonnegative object weights and Z > 0. Ratio and logarithmic forms
require care at zero factors. The exactness of the finite-replacement identities is also
confined to Z and its linear functionals: derived quantities such as log Z, posterior ratios
like base-pairing probabilities, and arg max structures remain nonlinear in a substitution
even in the multi-affine case.
Multi-affinity depends on the chosen factorization. It can fail when one position affects
several factors used simultaneously, when scores contain nonlocal couplings, or when
factors are tied nonlinearly. Occurrence-specific factors may restore polynomial structure,
yet a biological mutation can still coordinate many changes, as in RNA thermodynamics.
Efficient multisite algorithms must exploit model-specific transfer matrices, sparsity,
checkpointing, or recombination. Pseudoknots, tertiary interactions, cyclic grammars,
insertions, deletions, and black-box potentials may require enlarged state spaces or different
formulations.
7.5
Conclusion
Backward and outside quantities in major biological sequence dynamic programs are
adjoints of forward or inside quantities. This common structure explains item marginals,
local-event posteriors, and expected feature counts. The finite-replacement analysis further
specifies when derivative coefficients give exact mutation effects, when mixed terms are
required, and why nearest-neighbor RNA models demand context-dependent recombination.
The framework thereby separates posterior inference, parameter sensitivity, and finite
sequence change while deriving all three from the same dynamic-programming quantities.

S4.3
Context-indexed finite recombination
The displayed formulas use the notation of the RNA-like grammar specialized in the
following subsection. The use of context-indexed inside and outside copies, however, is the
part of the construction that extends to nearest-neighbor energy models.

the current dynamic-programming cell. In such cases, the ordinary inside and outside
variables are not sufficient, because a mutation at position j can change the boundary
conditions of nearby cells. Exact recombination is still possible by using context-dependent
inside and outside variables.

interactions between the two mutations are included through the inside and outside
variables α′ and β′. The same final partition function is obtained if the mutations are
applied in the opposite order, although different intermediate inside and outside variables
may be used. In implementations such as Rchange, many entries of α′ and β′ can be
