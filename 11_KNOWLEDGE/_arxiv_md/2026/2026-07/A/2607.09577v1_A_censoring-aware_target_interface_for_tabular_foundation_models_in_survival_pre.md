A censoring-aware target interface for tabular foundation models in
survival prediction
Yue Lyu, Steven H. Lin, Xuelin Huang∗and Ziyi Li∗
∗Correspondence: xlhuang@mdanderson.org & zli16@mdanderson.org
Abstract
Time-to-event prediction from tabular patient data is central to prognosis and biomedi-
cal decision support, but right-censored follow-up prevents direct use of ordinary regression
labels.
Tabular foundation models offer reusable prediction machinery for modest heteroge-
neous datasets, yet they generally assume fully observed outcomes.
We introduce SurvFM-
RMST, a censoring-aware target-interface framework that converts survival outcomes into jack-
knife pseudo-observation targets for restricted mean survival time, enabling multiple tabular
backbones to perform horizon-specific RMST regression without survival-specific fine-tuning.
In controlled simulations with known conditional RMST, SurvFM-RMST recovered restricted
event-free time accurately, and pseudo-RMST targets outperformed naive restricted observed-
time and event-only targets.
Across 36 eligible static SurvSet datasets, SurvFM backbones
were competitive with established survival and RMST-regression comparators, though relative
performance varied by endpoint, horizon and practical constraints. Predicted RMST further
stratified held-out patients into groups with ordered observed event-free time and event enrich-
ment. Overall, the results support pseudo-RMST target construction as a portable interface
between censored survival data and tabular foundation-model prediction.

Introduction
In many clinical and biomedical studies the question is not whether an event occurs but when,
and these times are routinely right-censored when individuals remain event-free at last follow-up.
Censoring makes survival prediction fundamentally different from ordinary supervised regression:
the observed follow-up time Y = min(T, C) is not a complete event-time label for censored patients.
Treating observed follow-up as the outcome can therefore mislabel censored observations, whereas
discarding censored patients reduces sample size and changes the target population. This distinction
is especially important for tabular biomedical prediction, where datasets are often modest in size
and censoring patterns vary across cohorts.
Classical and modern survival-analysis methods address this problem by incorporating censor-
ing into model fitting or evaluation. Cox proportional hazards models, random survival forests,
survival ensembles and neural Cox models encode risk sets, censoring structure, survival-function
assumptions or survival-specific objectives directly into estimation [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. These remain
strong, often ideal baselines for cohort-specific modelling, but they are trained de novo per dataset
and are survival-specific model classes rather than reusable tabular prediction engines.

In parallel, tabular foundation models and related in-context tabular learners have recently
become attractive for small to moderate structured datasets because they aim to reduce task-
specific modelling overhead [7]. This is appealing for biomedical survival datasets, which are often
modest in size, heterogeneous across cohorts and dominated by structured clinical, demographic
or molecular covariates. Yet standard tabular foundation models generally assume fully observed
classification or regression labels. Right-censored survival data therefore create a target-interface
problem: the covariates are tabular, but the outcome is not a directly observed regression target.
Recent work has adapted tabular foundation models to censored outcomes through several com-
plementary routes. Discretized survival-classification frameworks transform follow-up into time-
indexed binary tasks and use in-context classifiers to estimate survival probabilities [8]. Survival-
native pretrained and in-context methods instead learn censoring-aware priors, likelihoods or dis-
tributional outputs [9, 10, 11]. Concurrent target-side work includes TabSA, which places frozen
tabular regressors in a log-normal accelerated failure-time formulation: TabSA-PO uses a Kaplan–
Meier jackknife pseudo-observation at a reference time to initialize censored log-time targets,
whereas TabSA-BJ iteratively updates censored log-time targets through Buckley–James-inspired
imputation [12].
Representation-side adaptation provides another route by fitting trained Cox,
multi-task logistic regression (MTLR) or DeepHit-style survival heads to tabular representations;
Survival-Aware Adaptation specifically evaluates MTLR heads over pretrained TabPFN, TabDPT
and TabICL representations [1, 13, 14, 15].
These approaches are important and complementary to our target-interface question.
Our
main benchmark focuses on direct jackknife pseudo-observation targets for RMST at a prespecified
horizon, with a post hoc SurvivalPFN sensitivity analysis reported separately as supplementary
evidence. We ask whether censoring can be handled through this RMST target interface so that
existing tabular backbones can perform horizon-specific prediction without survival-specific fine-
tuning or newly trained survival-output heads.
Unlike TabSA’s AFT log-time imputation and
representation-side survival-head adaptation, SurvFM-RMST directly regresses a restricted-time
estimand and evaluates it against known conditional RMST in controlled simulations.
Restricted mean survival time (RMST) provides a natural target for this purpose. At a re-
striction horizon τ, RMST is the expected event-free time accumulated up to τ, equivalently the
area under the survival curve over [0, τ] [16, 17]. RMST is measured on the time scale of the orig-
inal outcome, does not require proportional hazards for its definition and yields a horizon-specific
prediction that is directly interpretable as restricted event-free time. However, individual RMST
values are not fully observed under censoring.
Jackknife pseudo-observations provide a bridge between censored-data estimands and regression
targets. By combining a full-sample RMST estimator with leave-one-out RMST estimators, pseudo-
observations convert a sample-level censored-data functional into constructed patient-level targets
[18, 19, 20]. Pseudo-observation RMST regression has been studied with conventional and ensemble
learners [21], suggesting a general route for linking censored survival outcomes to tabular regression
models.
We introduce SurvFM-RMST, a censoring-aware target-interface framework for tabular foundation-
model survival prediction.

Results
2.1
SurvFM-RMST converts censored follow-up into a pseudo-RMST regres-
sion interface
SurvFM-RMST separates the survival-specific problem of censoring-aware target construction from
the tabular prediction engine (Fig. 1). The input is a patient-level table containing baseline covari-
ates, observed follow-up time Yi = min(Ti, Ci), and event indicator ∆i. The central obstacle is that
Yi is not a complete event-time label for censored patients. Directly treating observed follow-up as
the regression target would mislabel censored observations, whereas discarding censored patients
would reduce sample size and alter the training population.
SurvFM-RMST addresses this label problem by constructing jackknife pseudo-observation tar-
gets for restricted mean survival time (RMST) at a prespecified horizon τ. These pseudo-RMST
labels provide censoring-aware patient-level regression targets while retaining censored patients in
target construction. The resulting context table contains training covariates and pseudo-RMST
labels; held-out query rows contain covariates only. A tabular backbone then predicts patient-level
RMST at horizon τ.
This target construction defines a shared regression interface. The main SurvFM-RMST back-
bones were SurvFM-TabPFN, SurvFM-TabICL, SurvFM-TabDPT, SurvFM-TabH2O and SurvFM-
MITRA. SurvFM-TabPFN denotes the current TabPFN-v3 backbone unless otherwise stated.
RMST-regression baselines and survival-model comparators were included to separate the con-
tribution of the censoring-aware target from the contribution of the predictive backbone.
The output is a horizon-specific prediction of restricted event-free time. Larger predicted RMST
corresponds to longer predicted event-free time up to τ.
For discrimination metrics, predicted
RMST was oriented as a risk score by reversing its sign, so shorter predicted RMST corresponded
to higher predicted risk. The framework therefore targets patient-level RMST at a chosen horizon,
rather than a full survival curve, dynamic prediction trajectory, competing-risk probability or

2.2
Controlled simulations support known-truth RMST recovery and validate
the pseudo-RMST target interface
We evaluated SurvFM-RMST in controlled simulations where the conditional RMST function was
known (Fig. 2). The simulation benchmark spanned 24 settings defined by signal structure, sample
size, feature count, censoring and noise regimes, with feature counts up to p = 99. The p = 99
feature setting preserved the intended high-dimensional p ≈100 regime while satisfying the hosted
TabH2O 100-column request limit when the target column was included. Tau80 and tau90 were
the main simulation horizons, and tau50 was retained as a reference horizon.
Oracle conditional-RMST RMSE measured recovery of the known conditional RMST function.
This endpoint is available only in simulation, where the data-generating mechanism is known. At
tau80, SurvFM-TabPFN and SurvFM-TabH2O formed the leading group for oracle conditional-
RMST RMSE, with nearly identical rounded mean errors (0.128 for both, across 120 oracle-
evaluable simulation blocks). SurvFM-TabPFN retained a small numerical aggregate advantage,
while RFReg-RMST was the leading non-SurvFM comparator at tau80 (0.134). A similar pattern
was observed at tau90: SurvFM-TabPFN had a small numerical aggregate advantage (0.176), fol-
lowed closely by SurvFM-TabH2O (0.177) and RFReg-RMST (0.182). At tau50, differences were
smaller: SurvFM-TabPFN and RSF had the same rounded RMST RMSE (0.0506), with RSF serv-
ing as the leading non-SurvFM comparator. SurvFM-TabPFN thus recovered conditional RMST
well, though SurvFM-TabH2O and the strongest non-SurvFM comparators were close in several
settings (Fig. 2a,c).
Discrimination from predicted RMST was evaluated using Uno’s C-index.
This metric ad-
dresses a different question from RMST error: RMST RMSE evaluates numeric restricted-time
prediction, whereas Uno’s C-index evaluates censoring-adjusted risk ordering. In the tau80 simu-
lation discrimination analysis, SurvFM-TabPFN was in the leading discrimination group by mean
block-level Uno’s C-index rank (mean rank 5.24; interquartile range 3–7 across 108 Uno-C-evaluable
blocks). SurvFM-TabDPT and SurvFM-TabH2O were numerically close, with mean ranks of 5.57
and 5.66, respectively, while RFReg-RMST was the leading non-SurvFM comparator by mean
Uno’s C-index rank (6.06; interquartile range 3–9). The discrimination ordering therefore differed
from the oracle-RMST-recovery ordering, indicating that performance depended on the evaluation
target (Fig. 2b,c).
To test whether the target construction itself was important, we performed a simulation target-
definition ablation using the same SurvFM-TabPFN v3 backbone with only the target definition
varied (Fig. 2d). Pseudo-RMST labels were compared with naive restricted observed-time labels,
min(Y, τ), and event-only restricted-time labels, min(T, τ), among uncensored events.
Pseudo-
RMST consistently reduced oracle conditional-RMST RMSE across tau50, tau80 and tau90. Mean
oracle conditional-RMST RMSE for pseudo-RMST was 0.050, 0.128 and 0.176, respectively, com-
pared with 0.063, 0.170 and 0.235 for the naive restricted observed-time target and 0.058, 0.171 and
0.238 for the event-only target. Discrimination also generally favored pseudo-RMST: mean Uno’s
C-index for pseudo-RMST was 0.570, 0.584 and 0.590 across the same horizons, exceeding the naive

2.3
Simulation-regime analyses show robustness across controlled data settings
We examined whether performance was concentrated in a narrow simulation regime or persisted
across controlled changes in sample size, feature/sample ratio, signal structure, censoring and
noise (Fig. 3). These analyses used tau80 oracle conditional-RMST RMSE as the primary regime-
summary metric. Ranks were computed against the full main comparator set, with rank 1 indicating
the lowest oracle RMST error.
Signal-structure summaries showed that the ordering of methods varied across linear additive,
nonlinear smooth, interaction and subgroup-mixture mechanisms (Fig. 3a). Regime heatmaps fur-
ther showed that performance patterns changed across sample-size strata, high feature/sample-ratio
settings, high censoring and high-noise sparse-signal settings (Fig. 3b). This variation is expected:
the grid deliberately spans settings favorable to different model classes. The relevant question is
therefore whether the pseudo-RMST interface stays useful across heterogeneous conditions, not
whether one model wins everywhere.
Top-three frequency across setting-replicate blocks gave a complementary robustness view (Fig. 3c).
SurvFM-TabPFN ranked among the top three by tau80 oracle RMST recovery in 62 of 120 blocks
(51.7%), followed by SurvFM-TabH2O in 51 of 120 blocks (42.5%). Other SurvFM backbones also
appeared among the top three, including SurvFM-TabICL in 34 of 120 blocks (28.3%), SurvFM-
TabDPT in 30 of 120 blocks (25.0%) and SurvFM-MITRA in 18 of 120 blocks (15.0%). Comparator
models also appeared among the leading methods in a substantial fraction of blocks: RFReg-RMST
ranked among the top three in 35 of 120 blocks (29.2%), RSF in 29 of 120 blocks (24.2%) and Deep-
Surv in 24 of 120 blocks (20.0%). SurvFM-RMST backbones were frequently in the leading group,
while established comparators remained important in parts of the simulation space. Full main-
comparator heatmaps, tau90 analogues and absolute RMST-error summaries are reported in the
Supplementary Information.

2.6
Predicted RMST supports retrospective group-level stratification and event
enrichment
We also asked whether predicted RMST had interpretable downstream survival meaning in held-
out patients (Fig. 5). Using SurvFM-TabPFN v3 predictions at the dataset/split-specific tau80
horizon, patients were divided into predicted-RMST tertiles. Low predicted RMST denotes shorter
predicted restricted event-free time, while high predicted RMST denotes longer predicted restricted
event-free time.
Illustrative Kaplan–Meier examples showed ordered observed event-free-time trajectories across
predicted-RMST tertiles (Fig. 5a,c). The figure reports observed high-minus-low Kaplan–Meier
RMST gaps and high-versus-low log-rank P values for selected datasets, while the cross-dataset
summary is shown in panel d.
Across the eligible benchmark subset at tau80, high-minus-low
observed KM-RMST gaps were positive in 35 of 36 datasets and 71 of 72 dataset/split blocks, with
53 of 72 split blocks showing a high-versus-low log-rank P < 0.05. The mean high-minus-low KM-
RMST gap was 24.3% of tau80 and the median gap was 23.2%, indicating that the high predicted-
RMST tertile generally had longer observed restricted event-free time than the low predicted-RMST
tertile (Fig. 5d).
Event enrichment offered a second read-out. For illustrative datasets, the event-share panel
showed how all observed events distributed across predicted-RMST tertiles (Fig. 5b). These bars are
not event-rate estimates; they show where observed events concentrate after held-out patients are
stratified by predicted RMST. Lower predicted-RMST groups were generally enriched for observed
events, consistent with their interpretation as higher-risk strata.
The analyses illustrate how the RMST-scale output can support retrospective group-level strat-
ification, event enrichment and observed event-free-time separation beyond a single discrimination
metric. At the same time, they do not establish individual-level calibration, treatment selection
or clinical deployment utility. Supplementary grouped RMST-agreement analyses showed that ob-
served Kaplan–Meier RMST increased monotonically across predicted-RMST tertiles in 57 of 72
tau80 dataset/split blocks, with median absolute normalized group error of 0.039. The grouped-
agreement analyses should likewise be interpreted as group-level diagnostics rather than individual
calibration.

Discussion
This study introduces SurvFM-RMST as a censoring-aware target-interface framework for tabular
foundation-model survival prediction. The central idea is to move the survival-specific component
from the model architecture to the outcome representation: right-censored follow-up is converted
into jackknife pseudo-observation targets for restricted mean survival time, and these pseudo-RMST
labels are then paired with tabular backbones for horizon-specific regression.
This target-side
adaptation allows existing tabular prediction machinery to be used for censored survival outcomes
without requiring survival-specific fine-tuning, risk-set losses or new survival-output heads. Emerg-
ing survival-native and in-context survival foundation models remain complementary to SurvFM-
RMST because they handle censoring through model-side survival objectives, output distributions
or discretized representations. The post hoc SurvivalPFN sensitivity analysis supported this po-
sitioning: under the submitted static SurvSet protocol, SurvivalPFN showed horizon-dependent
performance and did not change the main aggregate conclusions.
The controlled simulations support this target-interface view. When the conditional RMST
function was known, SurvFM-TabPFN showed one of the strongest aggregate simulation profiles
for oracle RMST recovery, while other SurvFM backbones and non-SurvFM comparators remained
competitive in specific settings. The target-construction ablation was particularly important: using
the same SurvFM-TabPFN v3 backbone, pseudo-RMST targets reduced oracle RMST error and
generally improved discrimination compared with naive restricted observed-time and event-only
targets. This indicates that the framework’s performance is not explained only by the backbone,
but also by the censoring-aware target construction. In other words, pseudo-RMST is not simply
a relabeling of follow-up time; it is the survival-specific interface that makes the regression task
meaningful under censoring.
The regime analyses further showed that performance depended on the simulation setting.
SurvFM backbones were frequently among the leading methods across signal structures, sample
sizes, feature/sample ratios, censoring and noise regimes, but no single method dominated every
setting. This heterogeneity is expected in tabular survival prediction, where signal form, sample
size, censoring support and feature dimensionality can change the relative strengths of model classes.
We therefore interpret the simulation results as evidence of robustness and portability of the pseudo-
RMST interface, rather than as evidence that one backbone should be universally preferred.
The public SurvSet benchmark led to a similar conclusion.
Several SurvFM backbones led
on discrimination or RMST-scale error within close performance bands, while RSF, AFT mod-
els, DeepSurv and the RMST-regression baselines remained strong in parts of the benchmark.
The pseudo-RMST interface is portable across backbones, but empirical performance is endpoint-,
horizon- and dataset-dependent.
This backbone-dependent pattern has practical implications. Tabular foundation-model-style
methods are often attractive because they reduce some task-specific modelling overhead, but sur-
vival prediction still requires careful validation within the intended data setting. The reduced-
training and feasibility analyses show that backbone profiles differ not only in discrimination and
RMST error, but also in coverage, runtime, hosted versus local execution and implementation con-
straints. For example, SurvFM-TabH2O provided hosted-backbone portability evidence but was

limited by a 100-column request limit in two high-column SurvSet datasets. SurvFM-MITRA was
feasible but more compute- and memory-sensitive. These practical profiles should be interpreted
as empirical guidance, not as prescriptive deployment recommendations.
A useful feature of the RMST target is that it produces predictions on a time scale. In the
held-out SurvSet stratification analyses, predicted RMST tertiles separated observed Kaplan–Meier
event-free-time trajectories and concentrated observed events in lower predicted-RMST groups, with
split-level and grouped RMST-agreement audits reported in Supplementary Fig. 4. These results
link the benchmark metrics to interpretable group-level summaries. However, they should not be
interpreted as individual-level calibration, clinical decision thresholds, treatment-selection evidence
or deployment-ready prognostic groups. The analyses are retrospective and group-level; they show
that predicted RMST carries meaningful survival ordering and event-enrichment information in
held-out data, not that the model is clinically validated.
Several limitations should be noted. First, SurvFM-RMST is a static tabular framework. It
uses baseline covariates and horizon-specific RMST targets, and does not model longitudinal co-
variate histories, dynamic landmark updates, recurrent events or competing risks. Second, RMST
predictions are horizon-specific. The choice of τ is part of the prediction question and depends on
follow-up support and the intended decision window; different horizons can change model rankings.
Third, pseudo-observation methods rely on censoring assumptions and finite-sample approxima-
tions. Although the target-ablation results support pseudo-RMST construction in our simulations
and public-data benchmarks, alternative censoring mechanisms, extreme censoring or very small
risk sets may require additional caution.
Fourth, the public-data evaluation used an eligible static SurvSet benchmark subset rather
than all SurvSet datasets.
This design made repeated multi-model benchmarking feasible and
aligned with the static tabular scope of the framework, but it excludes time-dependent, very large,
very high-dimensional or otherwise unsuitable datasets. A separate tau80 sensitivity on 11 scale-
excluded datasets showed that exact pseudo-RMST construction was computationally negligible
on those folds, but it covered only one horizon and retained model-specific feasibility failures;
it therefore does not replace the submitted benchmark. Fifth, not all backbones have identical
operational constraints. Hosted APIs, local GPU requirements, package availability and column
limits affect practical use and must be reported alongside performance. Finally, the benchmark used
fixed configurations rather than exhaustive hyperparameter tuning for every comparator, so the
results should be interpreted as a broad framework evaluation rather than a definitive leaderboard
for every model family.
We also evaluated several plausible extensions, including cross-fitted pseudo-RMST targets,
conditional-RMST pilots, target scaling, seed ensembling, residual or calibration adapters, stacked
adapters, TabPFN fine-tuning, scalable pseudo-RMST approximations and conformal RMST inter-
vals. These design-choice audits did not consistently improve both discrimination and RMST-scale
error enough to replace the simpler frozen-backbone pseudo-RMST workflow and are reported in
Supplementary Table 6.
The evidence scope for those audits is explicitly labeled because sev-
eral were earlier pilot analyses rather than current TabPFN-v3 full reruns. They are therefore
treated as supplementary evidence and future-work directions rather than main-method compo-

## Page 11


nents, consistent with much of the survival-specific gain in the present framework coming from the
censoring-aware target interface itself.
Future work could extend this target-interface principle in several directions. Dynamic landmark
versions could update pseudo-RMST targets as longitudinal information accumulates. Competing-
risk extensions could construct pseudo-observation targets for cause-specific restricted mean time
or cumulative incidence. Uncertainty quantification and conformal intervals could be revisited with
larger calibration sets and better censoring-aware interval diagnostics. High-dimensional biomed-
ical settings may require representation learning, feature filtering or embedding strategies before
applying tabular backbones. Finally, prospective or disease-specific validation will be needed before
RMST predictions can be used for clinical decision support.
In summary, SurvFM-RMST shows that censored survival prediction can be connected to tab-
ular foundation-model prediction through a target-interface strategy. By converting right-censored
follow-up into pseudo-RMST regression targets, the framework enables multiple tabular back-
bones to produce horizon-specific restricted event-free-time predictions.
The evidence supports
pseudo-RMST target construction as a portable bridge between censored survival data and tabular
foundation-model backbones, while emphasizing that backbone choice, horizon choice and practical
constraints remain empirical decisions.

Methods
4.1
Study design and manuscript scope
We developed SurvFM-RMST as a pseudo-RMST target-interface framework for static right-
censored tabular survival prediction.
The framework separates the survival-specific problem of
censoring-aware target construction from the tabular prediction engine. The workflow has four
stages: patient-level right-censored tabular input, jackknife pseudo-RMST target construction, tab-
ular backbone regression, and horizon-specific RMST prediction. The method is scoped to static
baseline covariates and fixed restriction horizons. It predicts patient-level restricted mean survival
time (RMST) at a chosen horizon τ; it does not estimate a full survival curve, dynamic landmark
trajectory, competing-risk probability or treatment effect.
The main empirical evaluation had five components. First, controlled simulations with known
conditional RMST evaluated recovery of the true restricted-time regression function and discrim-
ination from predicted RMST. Second, simulation-regime analyses assessed whether performance
was robust across sample size, feature/sample ratio, signal structure, censoring and noise settings.
Third, an eligible static SurvSet benchmark evaluated public-data discrimination and RMST-scale
prediction. Fourth, reduced-training and practical feasibility analyses characterized performance
under smaller training sets and implementation constraints. Fifth, held-out RMST stratification
analyses evaluated whether predicted RMST separated observed event-free-time patterns and en-
riched observed events at the group level.
Throughout the manuscript, SurvFM-prefixed labels denote a tabular backbone paired with the
same jackknife pseudo-RMST target interface. The main SurvFM-RMST backbones were SurvFM-
TabPFN, SurvFM-TabICL, SurvFM-TabDPT, SurvFM-TabH2O and SurvFM-MITRA. SurvFM-

4.8
Controlled simulation design
Controlled simulations were used to evaluate performance when the conditional RMST function was
known. The simulation benchmark included 24 settings defined by four signal structures and six
feature/censoring/noise patterns, with five replicates per setting. The four signal structures were
linear additive, nonlinear smooth, interaction and subgroup mixture. The design spanned sample
sizes n = 50, 100, 200, 500, feature counts p = 10, 50, 99, low/medium/high censoring regimes and
low-noise or high-noise sparse-signal settings.
The feature-count values p = 10, 50, 99 were chosen to represent low-, moderate- and high-
dimensional static tabular settings. The p = 99 feature setting preserved the intended p ≈100
high-dimensional regime while satisfying the hosted TabH2O 100-column request limit when the
pseudo-RMST target column was included in hosted API requests. High feature/sample-ratio set-
tings were defined where p ≥n. Censoring regimes were generated using setting-specific censoring
distributions with censoring maximum values 5.0, 2.5 and 1.4 for low, medium and high censor-
ing, respectively. High-noise sparse-signal settings reduced signal strength and added irrelevant
covariates to mimic harder biomedical tabular problems.
Most settings generated event times from Weibull proportional-hazards-type mechanisms. Lin-
ear additive settings used linear covariate effects; nonlinear smooth settings added smooth nonlinear
terms; interaction settings added threshold and interaction effects. Subgroup-mixture settings used
subgroup-specific outcome mechanisms to create heterogeneous response patterns. The complete
24-setting manifest, including sample size, feature count, signal structure, censoring regime, noise
regime and event-time mechanism for each setting, is provided in Supplementary Table 1.
The fully paired main controlled-simulation benchmark evaluated tau80 and tau90. Tau50 was
retained as a reference horizon in horizon-specific rank summaries and target-construction ablation
analyses. Oracle conditional-RMST RMSE was the primary simulation metric because the true
conditional RMST was known from the data-generating mechanism. In the audited simulation
generator, conditional RMST was computed for held-out test covariates from the known event-time
mechanism by numerical integration over 1024 grid points between 0 and τ. Simulation source files
store the setting/replicate seeds, and the target-ablation scripts used deterministic train/test split
seeds of the form 300000 + 17 × SettingID + Replicate. Uno’s C-index from predicted RMST was
used as a secondary discrimination metric.

4.18
Ranking, near-best summaries and missingness
Ranks were computed within comparable dataset/split/horizon blocks or simulation setting/replicate/horizon
blocks. Rank 1 indicates best performance. For error metrics, lower values were ranked better.
For Uno’s C-index, higher values were ranked better. Mean ranks were computed after block-level
ranking, not by ranking aggregate means only.
Within-0.02 frequency was computed for public-data Uno’s C-index by counting model-specific
evaluable dataset/split blocks in which a model was within 0.02 of the best evaluable model in that
same block. Top-three frequency was computed in simulation by counting setting/replicate blocks
in which a model ranked among the three lowest oracle RMST RMSE values. Denominators were
metric- and model-specific when evaluability differed.
Rows were treated as non-evaluable when model fitting failed, required predictions were missing,
Uno’s C-index could not be computed because of insufficient comparable event pairs, or IPCW
RMST RMSE could not be computed because of insufficient censoring support. Missing metric
rows were not imputed. Fit failures, evaluation-support failures, IPCW support limitations and
hosted API column-limit rows were recorded separately in the denominator audits. Supplementary
Tables 4 and 5 summarize evaluability, missingness, denominator rules and metric definitions.

they recompute aggregate means and ranks on blocks where both compared models were evaluable,

4.19
Supplementary design-choice ablation audits
Several design choices were evaluated as supporting audits rather than main-method replacements.
These included cross-fitted pseudo-RMST targets, conditional-RMST target pilots, target scaling,
seed ensembling, calibration or residual adapters, stacked adapters, TabPFN fine-tuning, scalable
pseudo-RMST approximations and conformal RMST intervals. These analyses are summarized in
Supplementary Table 6 with explicit evidence-scope labels. Several entries are earlier TabPFN-era
pilots or scope-limited audits rather than full current TabPFN-v3 reruns, and are therefore used
only to document design exploration and future-work priorities. The final workflow retained the
simpler frozen-backbone pseudo-RMST design because the source-backed submitted analyses did
not identify a consistently stronger alternative across both discrimination and RMST-scale error.
