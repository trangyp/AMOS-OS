A NOTE ON LEFT SEMIBRACES
SHOUFENG WANG
Abstract. A triple (S, +, ·) is called a left semibrace if (S, +) is a semigroup, (S, ·) is
a group and x(y +z) = (xy)+(x(x−1 +z)) for all x, y, z ∈S, where x−1 is the inverse
of x in the group (S, ·). In this note, we show that (S, +) is always a rectangular group
in this case, i.e., it is isomorphic to the direct product of a group and a rectangular
band, and obtain a structure theorem for general left semibraces by the generalized
matched products of right zero left semibraces and right cancellative left semibraces.
This improves and enriches some results obtained by Jespers and Van Antwerpen in
[Forum Math. 31 (2019) 241–263.]
1. Introduction
The quantum Yang-Baxter equation first appeared in theoretical physics in Yang
[25] and in statistical mechanics in Baxter [1] independently. Now, the Yang-Baxter
equation has many applications in the areas of mathematics and mathematical physics
such as knot theory, quantum computation, quantum group theory and so on. Finding
all solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation is presently impossible, so in 1992, Drinfeld
[12] posed the question of finding all the so called set-theoretic solutions of the Yang-
Baxter equation. Let S be a non-empty set. If a map r : S × S −→S × S satisfies
(r × idS)(idS × r)(r × idS) = (idS × r)(r × idS)(idS × r)
in the set of maps from S × S × S to S × S × S, where idS is the identity map on S,
then r is called a set-theoretic solution of the Yang-Baxter equation.
Set-theoretic solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation are investigated extensively in
recent years, see for example [7, 8, 13, 14, 17, 20, 21, 22]. In particular, Rump in-
troduced left braces in [21] as a generalization of Jacobson radical rings, and a few
years later, Ced´o, Jespers, and Okni´nski [8] reformulated Rump’s definition of left
braces. Since then, left braces have become the most used tool in the investigations
of non-degenerate involutive solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation. In order to study
non-degenerate solutions that are not necessarily involutive, Guarnieri and Vendramin
[15] introduced a generalization of left braces, namely, skew left braces. Let (S, +) and
(S, ·) be two groups. The triple (S, +, ·) is called a skew left brace if the following axiom
holds:
(1.1)
x(y + z) = xy −x + xz.
In [15, Theorem 3.1], Guarnieri and Vendramin have proved that every skew left brace
(S, +, ·) can give rise to some bijective non-degenerate solution.
To investigate left non-degenerate solutions of the Yang-Baxter equation, Catino,
Colazzo and Stefanelli [4] introduced left cancellative semibraces. Recall that a semi-
group (S, ·) is called left cancellative if for all a, b, c ∈S, ab = ac implies that b = c.
A triple (S, +, ·) is called a left cancellative semibrace if (S, +) is a left cancellative
semigroup, (S, ·) is a group and the following axiom holds:
(1.2)
x(y + z) = xy + x(x−1 + z).

One can show that (1.1) and (1.2) coincide in a skew left brace. Since groups are left
cancellative semigroups, it follows that skew left braces are left cancellative semibraces.
More results about left cancellative semibraces and left non-degenerate solutions of the
Yang-Baxter equation have been provided in [2, 3, 9, 11]. In 2019, Jespers and Van
Antwerpen [16] introduced and investigated general left semibraces. A triple (S, +, ·)
is called a left semibrace if (S, +) is a semigroup, (S, ·) is a group and the axiom (1.2)
holds. Among other things, Jespers and Van Antwerpen have shown that under a
natural assumption, a left semibrace (S, +, ·) can provide a solution (see [16, Theorem
5.1]). More results on general left semibraces can be found in [10, 23].
In [4], Catino, Colazzo and Stefanelli have shown that the additive semigroup (S, +)
of a left cancellative left semibrace (S, +, ·) is right group (i.e. a direct product of a
group and a right zero semigroup). In [5], Catino, Colazzo and Stefanelli have pointed
out that only partial results on additive semigroups are known for left semibraces.
More specifically, under some mild assumptions, for instance in the finite case, [16,
Theorem 2.8] states that the additive semigroup (S, +) of a left semibrace (S, +, ·) is
a completely simple semigroup (In fact, by [5, Theorem 2.3], (S, +) is a rectangular
group, i.e., it is isomorphic to the direct product of a group and a rectangular band).
In this note, we show that the additive semigroup of a left semibarce is always a
rectangular group and obtain a structure theorem for general left semibraces by the
generalized matched products of right zero left semibraces and right cancellative left
semibraces. This improves and enriches some results in [16].

To give the first main result of this note, we first recall the notion of rectangular
groups. Let (G, +) be a group with identity 0 and L and R be a left zero semigroup
and a right zero semigroup, respectively. Then M = I ×G×Λ forms a semigroup with
respect to the following addition:
(i, g, λ) + (j, h, µ) = (i, g + h, µ) for all (i, g, λ), (j, h, µ) ∈I × G × Λ.
Observe that
E(M, +) = {(i, 0, λ) | i ∈I, λ ∈Λ}.
A semigroup which is isomorphic to some semigroup constructed above is called a
rectangular group.
A semigroup (S, +) is called regular if for all a ∈S, there is a′ ∈S such that
a+a′+a = a. A regular semigroup (S, +) is called orthodox if E(S, +) is a subsemigroup
of (S, +). Rectangular groups are orthodox semigroups. A semigroup (S, +) is called
completely regular if for all a ∈S, there is a′ ∈S such that a + a′ + a = a and
a + a′ = a′ + a. A semigroup (S, +) is called simple if S + a + S = S for all a ∈S. A
completely simple semigroup is a simple semigroup which is also completely regular. An
element u ∈E(S, +) is called primitive if for all v ∈E(S, +), the fact u+v = v +u = v
implies that u = v.
