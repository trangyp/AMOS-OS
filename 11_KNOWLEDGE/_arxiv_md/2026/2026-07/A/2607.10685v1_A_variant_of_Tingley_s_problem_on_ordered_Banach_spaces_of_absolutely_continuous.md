1. Introduction
Tingley’s problem [18] asks whether every surjective isometry T : S(E) →S(F) between the unit
sphere of two Banach spaces extends uniquely to a surjective linear isometry between the Banach spaces
E and F. The problem has attracted considerable attention for function spaces [1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 19, 20,
21, 16, 17]. To date, no counterexample is known. In particular, Tingley’s problem has a positive answer
for Lp(µ)–spaces, when µ is σ–finite [16, 17]. However, results on absolutely continuous function spaces
remain limited [8, 12].
A related direction is to replace the whole unit sphere by its positive part. This is natural for ordered
Banach spaces whose positive cones generate the underlying real ordered structure; in the complex case,
the whole space is then obtained by complexification. Peralta [13] proposed a variant of Tingley’s problem.
An extended form of Peralta’s problem states as follows:
Problem 1. Let T : S(E)+ →S(F)+ be a surjective isometry between the positive unit spheres of two
ordered Banach spaces E and F with generating cones; namely
∥T(x) −T(y)∥= ∥x −y∥
(x, y ∈S(E)+).
Is there a surjective linear isometry Φ : E →F extending T?
Later, Leung, Ng and Wong [10] solved this problem affirmatively for Lp–spaces with 1 ≤p ≤∞. In
particular, this work is being applied directly to complex Lp–spaces and its extension is complex-linear. In

The proof of Theorem 3.1 highlights two features of the present approach.
First, we emphasize the separation into three cases. The three cases p = 1, 1 < p < ∞, and p = ∞
require different arguments because the relevant metric properties change at the two endpoints p = 1
and p = ∞. For p = 1, the radius function ρ used in Lemma 3.1 does not distinguish the constant
function 1 from the other elements of S(AC1(Ω))+. We realize AC1(Ω) as an L1–space instead. For
p = ∞, the restriction D|S(AC∞(Ω))+ loses injectivity, and hence we decompose Σ+ = P ∪Q. On P, the
scalar coordinate is fixed, and hence the derivative coordinate determines the point uniquely. The set Q
contains precisely the points for which the derivative alone leaves the scalar coordinate undetermined.
This decomposition therefore allows the two parts of Σ+ to be treated separately.
Second, we overcome the structural obstructions that prevent the argument of [3] from applying
to ACp(Ω). The proof replaces the convex–body argument by a different extension mechanism. For
1 < p ≤∞, we first apply the extension theorem for positive unit spheres of L1–spaces [10, Theorem 11]
and then use the metric separation properties established in Lemma 3.2. When 1 < p < ∞, Lemma 3.2(a)
shows that the resulting complex–linear isometric order isomorphism agrees with the induced isometry
on the whole positive unit ball. When p = ∞, Lemma 3.2(b) shows that the distance relations with
