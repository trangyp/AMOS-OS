ON THE LARGEST SIZE OF SUM-FREE SETS IN SYMMETRIC REGIONS
ANUBHAB GHOSAL AND DMITRY TSAREV
Abstract. A subset S of a group G is said to be sum-free (resp. ∆-free) if there are no solutions
to a + b = c (resp. a + b + c = 0) with a, b, c ∈S. For a convex region R ⊂Rd, let σ(R) denote the
maximal proportion of the volume of R that a sum-free subset of R can occupy.
We prove that σ([−1, 1]d) = 1/2.
Our proof employs a careful application of the Brunn-
Minkowski inequality.
Moreover, for the d-dimensional Euclidean ball Bd(0, 1), we show that
σ(Bd(0, 1)) ⩽1/2 + od(1). We present two arguments for this. The first combines some routine
harmonic analysis on the sphere with known bounds on values of the ultraspherical polynomials.
The second more elementary argument proceeds by establishing that the maximal ∆-free subset of
the unit sphere Sd−1 occupies 1/2 + O(d−1) of the sphere’s surface measure. This answers a question
raised by Bukh.
1. Introduction
A subset S of an abelian group G is said to be sum-free if there are no solutions to a + b = c with
a, b, c ∈S. Following the highly influential paper of Erd˝os [10] more than half a century ago, the
study of extremal properties of sum-free sets has received much attention. For example, Diananda
and Yap [7], and Green and Ruzsa [15] have determined the size of the largest sum-free subsets of
all finite abelian groups; see [19] for a survey on sum-free sets in groups.
A more recent line of inquiry has sought to determine the largest volume of sum-free subsets of
regions in Rd. For a convex region R ⊂Rd, let σ(R) denote the maximal proportion of the volume
of R that a sum-free subset of R can occupy. The (discrete version of the) problem of determining
σ([0, 1]2) was communicated by Aydinian to Serra and posed as an open problem at the 19th British
Combinatorial Conference in 2001 [4]. Note that the sum-free subset
{(x, y) ∈[0, 1]2 : 0.8 ⩽x + y < 1.6}
shows that σ([0, 1]2) ⩾0.6. Cameron [4, 5] conjectured that, indeed σ([0, 1]2) = 0.6, and this was
established in 2017 by Elsholtz and Rackham [9].
In higher dimensions, the problem of determining σ([0, 1]d) was also raised by Aydinian and
Cameron [5], and highlighted by Green [14]. To be completely accurate, the discrete version of this
problem was posed, which is strictly harder. However, the main difficulty in solving these problems
is captured well by the “nicer” continuous versions. Similarly as above, we have the lower bound

2. Proofs
We start by setting up some notation.
Definition 2.1. For a probability measure µ on Rd, let
σ(µ) := sup{µ(S) | S ⊂Rd is Borel and sum-free}, τ(µ) := sup{µ(S) | S ⊂Rd is Borel and ∆-free}.
For a bounded region R ⊂Rd, we let σ(R) and τ(R) denote the quantities σ(µR) and τ(µR), where
µR is the uniform probability measure on R. In particular, when R is a convex body in Rd, µR is
the normalised restriction of the Lebesgue measure λd to R; when R = Sd−1 is the unit sphere in Rd,
µR is the normalised surface measure, and when R is finite µR is the normalised counting measure.

3. Concluding remarks and open problems
3.1. Improving Theorem 1.4 via Conjecture 1.6. Recall that Conjecture 1.6 would imply a
stronger version of Theorem 1.4 if true. We believe that Hamming digraphs are good candidates to
witness the conjecture. Let V (G) = {0, 1}n, and include xy ∈E(G) if x and y differ in exactly ⌊αn⌋
coordinates for some α ∈(0, 0.5). After rescaling, this is a unit distance digraph. We believe that
for certain ranges of α, these digraphs should provide examples witnessing Conjecture 1.6. Indeed,
we believe it is possible to prove the weaker (undirected) version of this conjecture using spectral
methods, which would give an alternate proof of Proposition 1.3. However, we do not pursue this
here as the harmonic proof we present is much simpler.
3.2. A stronger version of Conjecture 1.2.
Question 3.1. Is it true that for any convex, centrally symmetric region R ⊂Rd, τ(R) equals a
half?
The lower bound of half follows by considering a half-space. If the answer to this question is in
the affirmative, it would imply Conjecture 1.2 by the following lemma.
Lemma 3.2. For any convex, centrally symmetric region R ⊂Rd, it holds that σ(R) ⩽τ(R).
Proof. By restricting to the affine span of R if necessary, we can assume that R is full-dimensional.
Let S be a sum-free subset of R. Let f denote an arbitrary non-zero linear functional on Rd and
let H denote the open half-space {x ∈Rd : f(x) > 0}. As R is full-dimensional, µ(R ∩ker(f)) = 0.
Replacing f with −f if necessary, we can assume that S′ := S ∩H has measure at least µ(S′) ⩾
0.5µ(S).
Note that H is ∆-free as f(a + b + c) = f(a) + f(b) + f(c) > 0 for a, b, c ∈H. Further, as S is
sum-free, S′ = S ∩H must be both ∆-free and sum-free. It follows that there are no solutions to
±a ± b ± c = 0 with a, b, c ∈S′.
Then S′′ := S′∪−S′ ⊂R must also be ∆-free and µ(S′′) = 2µ(S′) ⩾µ(S). Therefore τ(R) ⩾σ(R),
as we wished to show.
□
