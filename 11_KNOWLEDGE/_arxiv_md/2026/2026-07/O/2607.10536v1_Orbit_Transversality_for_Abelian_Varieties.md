ORBIT TRANSVERSALITY FOR ABELIAN VARIETIES
KAIWEN LU
Abstract. Let X/K be an abelian variety defined over a number field and let f : X →X
be a dominant morphism defined over K.
We show that (f, K) is strongly dense orbit
transversal. That is, up to replacing K with a finite extension, every set of representatives
for grand (f, K)-orbits is Zariski dense in X.
1. Introduction
Let X be a variety over a field K and let f : X →X be a morphism defined over K. A
central object of study in dynamics is the set of grand (f, K)-orbits of points in X(K), as
they encode the information of how points move under f.
Definition 1.1. The grand (f, K)-orbit of a point P ∈X(K) is the set of points whose
orbits eventually merge with the orbit of P:
Ogrand
f,K (P) := {Q ∈X(K) | f n(Q) = f m(P) for some n, m ≥0}.
A natural question to ask is then: how are the grand (f, K)-orbits distributed in X? We
know that the set of grand (f, K)-orbits in X(K) partitions X(K), so we need to pin down
a notion of distribution before we proceed. To precisely discuss this problem, Pasten and
Silverman [PS26] introduced orbit propagation principles, which measure the distribution
of grand orbits by the Zariski density of some sets of representatives. Using terminologies
introduced by Silverman, one set of representatives considered is the following:
Definition 1.2. An (f, K)-transversal of X is a subset S ⊆X(K) such that every (f, K)-
grand orbit of X(K) contains exactly one point in S.
With the above definition, orbit propagation principles (C2∃) and (C2∀) described in
[PS26] can be rephrased as follows:
Definition 1.3 (Weak DOT). We say that (f, K) is weakly dense orbit transversal (W-
DOT) if for some finite extension L/K, there exists a Zariski dense (f, L)-transversal of
X.
Definition 1.4 (Strong DOT). We say that (f, K) is strongly dense orbit transversal (S-
DOT) if for some finite extension L/K, every (f, L)-transversal of X is Zariski dense.
Pasten and Silverman proved orbit propagation principles of various strength for many
classes of self-maps on families of projective varieties. In the case of abelian varieties, they
proved strongly dense orbit transversality for simple abelian varieties:
Theorem 1.5 ([PS26] Theorem 6.1, rephrased). Let X/¯Q be a geometrically simple abelian
variety, and let f : X →X be an endomorphism of X (as an abstract variety) such that there
is a point in X(¯Q) with f-orbit that is Zariski dense in X. Then there is a number field K/Q
such that X and f are defined over K and (f, K) is strongly dense orbit transversal.

The main result of this paper is strongly dense orbit transversality for dominant endomor-
phisms of arbitrary abelian varieties.
Theorem 1.6. Let X/¯Q be an abelian variety, and let f : X →X be a dominant morphism.
Then there is a number field K/Q such that X and f are defined over K, and (f, K) is
strongly dense orbit transversal.
Theorem 1.6 strengthens and simplifies the proof of W-DOT for abelian varieties in the
author’s earlier unpublished paper [Lu26].
As another motivation for the defintion of (f, K)-transversals, Pasten and Silverman
proved that over a number field K and up to replacing K with a finite extension, if X(K)
is Zariski dense in X, then the existence of a dense (f, K)-transversal is equivalent to
X(K) ∖(Γ1 ∪· · · ∪Γr) being Zariski dense for every finite collection of grand (f, K)-orbits
Γ1, . . . , Γr. This gives more evidence for why the Zariski density of an (f, K)-transversal is
a reasonable notion for measuring the distribution of grand (f, K)-orbits.
One thing to note is that their proof for the aforementioned equivalence crucially relies on
the fact that K is countable. In fact, if K is uncountable, then we will prove that strongly
dense orbit transversality always holds for dominant morphisms of irreducible varieties; see
Proposition 3.1.
Henceforth we assume that K is a number field, and we may abuse notation when replacing
K with a finite extension and still call the extension K.

3. Varieties over uncountable fields

varieties over uncountable fields.

X(K) is Zariski dense in X, and let f : X →X be a dominant morphism. Then every
