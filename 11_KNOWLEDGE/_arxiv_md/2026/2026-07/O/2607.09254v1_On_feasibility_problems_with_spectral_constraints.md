On feasibility problems with spectral constraints
Shravan Mohan
17-004, Mantri Residency, Bangalore
Abstract—We study matrix feasibility problems “find X ∈K∩C”
where K is a closed convex matrix set and C = {X : σ(X) ∈S}
is defined by a convex constraint S on the ordered singular
values. Using the classical spectral transfer identity, projection
onto C reduces to an SVD plus a small quadratic program.
For seven natural polyhedral S we embed this projector in a
plain alternating projection (AP) loop. We experiment with two
concrete families of K – linear constraints (an affine subspace
intersected with an entrywise box) and ellipsoidal constraints
(a non-centered anisotropic Frobenius ellipsoid) – although the
method applies equally to more general convex constraints. The
experiments expose three regimes: rapid feasibility, slow tail
convergence, or informative infeasibility plateaus.
Index Terms—Alternating projections, convex feasibility, spectral
constraints, nuclear norm, condition number.

A convexity caveat. In every example above the singular-value
set S is convex (a linear inequality, a box, or the simplex on the
ordered nonnegative cone), so the vector projection PS – and
hence the matrix projection PC of Section II-C – is exact. The
induced matrix set C, however, is convex only for constraints
that upper-bound the largest singular values (the spectral norm,
the nuclear / Ky Fan norms, and weighted nuclear norms with
non-increasing weights). Constraints that bound the smallest
singular values from below – the condition number, the gain
lower bound, and the spread / Lipschitz caps – make C non-
convex, because σr and the intermediate σi are not concave
functions of X. In those cases the projection identity still holds
verbatim and we run AP as a heuristic. A full derivation of
each reduction is given in Appendix A.

III. POLYHEDRAL SPECTRAL CONSTRAINTS
We focus on the case where S is the intersection of the ordered
nonnegative cone with a polyhedron {s : Ls ≤b}; computing
PS is then a strictly convex QP in r variables, solved here
through CVXPY using the OSQP solver [7]. Throughout, we
impose the ordering and nonnegativity constraints s1 ≥· · · ≥
sr ≥0, and the seven cases below differ only in the additional
inequalities Ls ≤b.
The restriction to polyhedral S is purely for concreteness. The
spectral projection identity of Theorem 2 uses nothing about
S beyond the existence of its projector PS, so S may be any
closed convex set on the ordered singular values — a norm
ball, a quadratic or second-order-cone region, or any other
convex constraint — and the method is unchanged: one SVD
followed by the (now possibly conic) vector projection. The
polyhedral instances merely make PS a quadratic program.
1) Condition number. σ1 ≤κ σr, equivalent to a single
linear inequality σ1 −κσr ≤0.
2) Spectral spread. σ1 −σr ≤δ, again a single linear
constraint.
3) Singular value box. ℓi ≤σi ≤ui for i = 1, . . . , r. With
ℓ= 0 this reduces to clipping; in general the ordering
constraint makes it a non-trivial QP.

VI. RESULTS
A. Affine + box K vs ellipsoidal E
We run all seven spectral constraints on both convex sets,
sweeping each spectral parameter from loose to tight, delib-
erately into the infeasible regime. Figure 1 collects the seven
sweeps for the affine + box K and Fig. 2 the seven for the
ellipsoidal E. In every panel feasible runs are drawn as solid
lines (annotated with the early-stop iteration) and infeasible
runs as dashed lines whose legend reports the plateau value
reached and its gap to the target – i.e. exactly how close AP
got to the constraint.
For the affine + box K (Fig. 1), the condition-number, spread,
and Lipschitz sweeps reach every target within 100 iterations,
with the iteration count growing as the constraint tightens. The
remaining four cases expose informative infeasibility plateaus.
In the weighted-cap sweep (arbitrary positive weights wi
printed below the panel), integer caps down to c = 14 are
met, while c = 8 plateaus at w⊤σ ≈12.3 — an achievable
value in K that estimates, and upper-bounds, the best attainable
minX∈K w⊤σ(X). The two-sided box sweep (fixed lower
bound l = 0.5, tightening u) is feasible for u ≥2, but for every
u ≤1.5 the violation plateaus at a residual of σ1−u ≈0.12 at
u = 1.5; AP cannot push σ1 below ≈1.62 in this K (the lower
bound l is met throughout). The polyhedral sweep becomes
infeasible only at the tightest scale (τ = 0.2, reaching 0.25),
and the simplex equality becomes unreachable at the smallest
target sum (z ≈6.2, plateauing at ≈6.3).
For the ellipsoidal E (Fig. 2), AP converges far faster –
often in a single iteration – and every target, across all seven
constraints and all parameter values, is met. We attribute this
to the smooth, full-dimensional geometry of E: there are no
flat faces or corners for the iterates to wedge into, and the
ellipsoid intersects essentially any reasonable spectral set C.
The polyhedral affine K, by contrast, can force the iterate into
a low-dimensional slice where a tightened C is unreachable –
and where the plateau then quantifies the residual infeasibility.

VIII. CONCLUSION
We presented a uniform framework for matrix feasibility with
polyhedral spectral constraints, exploiting the classical spectral
transfer identity (6) to reduce the matrix projection to a small
QP on r singular values. Embedded in a plain alternating
projection loop, the framework solves a range of spectral
feasibility problems exactly (up to early-stop tolerance) on two
qualitatively different families of convex matrix sets. Future
