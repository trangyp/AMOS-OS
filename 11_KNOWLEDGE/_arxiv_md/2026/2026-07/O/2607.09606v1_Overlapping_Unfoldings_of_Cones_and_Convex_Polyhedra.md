Abstract
Research on D¨urer’s problem focuses on edge unfoldings
of convex polyhedra that avoid overlap. We invert the
goal and find unfoldings that overlap at some point to
any given thickness t.
We have two main results. The first is that, if we al-
low unfolding cuts that do not follow polyhedron edges,
then there is a convex polyhedron that can unfold with
overlap of any given thickness. The second result is that
for any given thickness, there is a convex polyhedron
with an edge unfolding that overlaps to that thickness.
1
Introduction
D¨urer’s problem [2, Open Problem 21.1] goes back to
a treatise by Albrecht D¨urer [3] from 1525, was fully
formalized by Shephard [8] in 1975, and has remained
unsolved. It asks whether every convex polyhedron has
an edge unfolding (i.e., an unfolding where only cuts
along edges are allowed) to a net, that is, to a simple
(i.e., nonoverlapping) planar polygon.
Among the strongest positive results is Ghomi’s proof
that every convex polyhedron can be stretched affinely
into a new polyhedron that has an edge unfolding to a
net. A type of inverse to Ghomi’s theorem is the re-
sult that every combinatorial convex polyhedron has a
metric realization that has an edge unfolding that over-
laps [7].
There are convex polyhedra for which every edge un-
folding provides a net—for example, the Platonic solids
[4]. On the other hand, there are polyhedra with over-
lapping edge unfoldings, for example a skinny tetrahe-
dron [6] (or see [2]), and a recently discovered family of
convex polyhedra with regular faces [9]. In these exam-
ples at most two layers overlap at any point.
In a general unfolding the cuts are not restricted
to edges of the polyhedron; the unfolded surface is still
required to be flat and connected; equivalently, the cuts

In this paper, we examine whether overlaps of larger
thickness are possible for edge or for general unfoldings
and, if so, what the limits on overlap thickness are. The
first question is answered positively by the unfolding
of a convex polyhedron in Fig. 1.
Before addressing
the second question, we define the concept of overlap
thickness more formally.
Given an unfolding of a polyhedron to the Euclidean
plane, define this unfolding’s thickness of overlap at
a point of the plane as the number of distinct, non-
boundary points of the surface unfolded to this point
(i.e., the number of layers or plies of the unfolding at
this point).
More precisely, thickness t at a point p
means that a line orthogonal to the plane pierces the
interior of the unfolded surface t times.
Thickness 0
at a point means that the line misses the interior of the
unfolding. The thickness of an unfolding is its maximum
thickness across all points. An unfolding of thickness 1
has no overlap, the goal of D¨urer’s problem for any given
polyhedron.

We have two main results: The first concerns gen-
eral unfoldings of a convex polyhedron, and the second
concerns edge unfoldings of convex polyhedra. Both are
established through explicit geometric constructions.
Theorem 1 There is a convex polyhedron P such that
for any t ∈N, P can be unfolded using general cuts (so
not restricted to edges) to have overlap thickness t.
Theorem 1 will follow from Lemma 3, which builds on
Lemma 2. Both lemmas concern an unbounded cone
C without polyhedral structure, i.e., just a single cone
vertex.
Lemma 2 For a cone C of apex curvature1 ω = λ · 2π,
with λ = a/b in lowest terms, a, b ∈N, there is a cut
path that unfolds the cone to have overlap thickness t =
b −a, and this is the maximum possible.
Lemma 3 For a cone C whose apex curvature is an
irrational multiple of π, there is, for any t ∈N, a cut
path that unfolds C to have overlap thickness t.
These cone unfoldings are not edge unfoldings.
Our
second main result concerns edge unfoldings:
Theorem 4 For any t ∈N, there is a convex poly-
hedron P of n = O(t) vertices with an edge unfolding
achieving overlap thickness of at least t.
The bound in the theorem is tight: to achieve overlap
thickness t with an edge unfolding the polyhedron must
have at least t faces, and thus Ω(t) vertices.

We now turn to Lemma 3. When C is a cone whose
apex curvature ω = λ2π is an irrational multiple of π,
we will create a cut path that produces an unfolding
with overlap thickness t for any given t ∈N. As in the
above case where λ = a/b, we identify t points starting
with the initial point p0 = 0. The angle of the surface
of the cone is (1−λ)2π, and we seek the remainder after
division by this expression. So when we roll the cone,
the first overlap with p0 is at p1 = (1 mod (1 −λ)) · 2π.
The ith point is pi = (i mod (1 −λ)) · 2π. Unlike for
λ = a/b, this sequence never repeats, so we can continue
to pt−1 for any t. As above, we can make a cut path
that spirals outward and visits p0, . . . , pt−1 in order.
Theorem 1 follows by creating a tetrahedron whose
apex has irrational curvature and extending the cut
path to cut all but one of the edges of the base.
3
Edge Unfolding Overlap
The construction in Fig. 1, which brings the tips of thin
triangles to triple overlap in the center of the unfold-
ing, can be extended to achieve larger overlap thickness.
Fig. 8 shows a “camera shutter” structure that results
in overlap of thickness t = 5.
It is plausible that generalizing this example to a k-
sided shutter will achieve overlap thickness t = k + 1.
However, there are two obstacles to proving Theorem 4
via this strategy. First, controlling the precise curva-
tures at the vertices that are leaves of the cut tree,
needed to produce overlap in the center of the shut-
ter, is quite delicate.
Second, the unfoldings are not
edge unfoldings because some vertices have zero curva-
ture and some edges have flat dihedral angles. It seems
difficult to make all vertices “real” (positive curvature)
and all cut edge dihedrals < π. Indeed, the triangula-
tion graph of the top square might not be “regular” [5,
p.55], which would imply it cannot be lifted to a convex
dome. Avoiding these complications, we prove Theo-
rem 4 by nesting overlap regions.

3.3
Nesting Analyzed
Now we argue that this plan of nesting overlap gadgets
is sound.
Focusing on i = 2, we need ω and the angle at
a2 of △2 to be such that the gadget does indeed create
overlap. And we need △2 placed so that P2 includes
the cut path that unfolds as illustrated in Fig. 11. More
precisely:
(1) As mentioned, ω can be selected arbitrarily small
by reducing the height z2 of △2 over △1. In the
example, ω ≈1.5◦at c2.
(2) The edges a1c1, a1p1, and p1b1 of P1 are all nonflat.
Extending their incident faces above the plane of
△1 creates a convex “canopy” under which △2 can
be placed. This placement, together with choosing
ω (see (1) above), determines z2.
(3) After altering P1 by installing △2 parallel to and
above △1 in the overlap region O1, and connecting
△2 to △1 by the edge p1b2, the new additions need
to be “retriangulated.” One can view this as taking
the convex hull of △1 and the four added vertices.
What we need to achieve for an edge unfolding is
that the cut path (p1, b2, a2, c2) follows edges of the
polyhedron P2.
For the generic situation, △i+1 sits over △i, and pi
lies just outside aibi. Then the convex hull always

the edges of △i+1 are included. Because the struc-

4
Conclusion
A natural next step is to determine the largest overlap
thickness achievable for specific polyhedra.
The Pla-
tonic solids do not have edge unfoldings that overlap [4],
but in a forthcoming paper we analyze the thickness
of general unfoldings of the Platonic solids and other
polyhedra.
For example, in related work Shiota and
Saitoh [9] found that the truncated dodecahedron has
an edge unfolding with overlap thickness 2.
Is that the
largest thickness achievable for that polyhedron?
