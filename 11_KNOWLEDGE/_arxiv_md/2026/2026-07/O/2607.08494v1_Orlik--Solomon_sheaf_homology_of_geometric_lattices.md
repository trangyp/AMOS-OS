1. Introduction
The Orlik–Solomon algebra is one of the fundamental algebraic invariants of a hyperplane arrangement.
If A is a complex hyperplane arrangement, Orlik and Solomon proved that the cohomology ring of the
complement is determined by the intersection lattice L(A) and is naturally presented by generators indexed by
the hyperplanes and relations indexed by dependent sets [OS80]. This construction extends without reference
to realizability: every geometric lattice, equivalently every simple matroid, has an associated Orlik–Solomon
algebra. Its graded pieces are free abelian groups with NBC bases, and the Hilbert series is the Poincar´e
polynomial of the lattice.
Thus the Orlik–Solomon algebra connects two central themes of arrangement
theory: the topology of complements and the combinatorics of geometric lattices.
Sheaf homology of posets provides another way to attach homological invariants to arrangements and
matroids. Everitt and Turner developed a theory of sheaf homology for graded atomic lattices and proved
deletion–restriction long exact sequences for such homology theories. They used this framework to compute
the sheaf homology of the intersection lattice of a hyperplane arrangement with coefficients in the natural
sheaf, recovering and extending a theorem of Lusztig [ET22a].
In subsequent work, they computed the
corresponding homology with coefficients in exterior powers of the natural sheaf, using Boolean covers and
cellular methods [ET22b].
These results suggest that natural algebraic coefficient systems on geometric
lattices often have computable and highly structured sheaf homology.
The purpose of this paper is to study the analogous coefficient system obtained from local Orlik–Solomon
algebras. Let L be a geometric lattice of rank ℓand let x ∈L be a flat. The principal ideal L≤x is again a
geometric lattice, so it has its own Orlik–Solomon algebra OS•(L≤x). If x ≤y, then L≤x ⊆L≤y. Besides
the natural inclusion of local Orlik–Solomon algebras, there is also a natural surjection
πy,x : OS•(L≤y) −→OS•(L≤x)

A lattice L is a poset in which the operations join and meet are defined for all pairs x, y ∈L. Recall that
the join x ∨y and the meet x ∧y represent the least upper bound and the greatest lower bound for x, y ∈L
respectively. If only meet (resp. join) is defined for all pairs, we shall call the poset a meet (resp. join)
semilattice. Note that in a lattice L, join and meet are associative so we write e.g. ∨{x, y, z} = x ∨y ∨z.
A lattice L has a bottom ˆ0 and a top ˆ1. We denote by A = A(L) the set of atoms of L, that are elements
covering ˆ0. We call a lattice L atomic if every element is a join of atoms, where we agree that an atom is the
join of itself and ˆ0 is the join of 0 atom.
A ranked lattice L is semimodular if the following semimodular inequality holds for all x, y ∈L,
rank(x) + rank(y) ≥rank(x ∧y) + rank(x ∨y).
Definition 2.2. A geometric lattice L is a ranked lattice, which is both atomic and semimodular. An element
of a geometric lattice is called a flat.
Example 2.3. Let A be a central hyperplane arrangement, the intersection lattice L(A) is a geometric
lattice ([Sta12] Proposition 3.11.2). More generally, a lattice is geometric if and only if it is the flat lattice
of a matroid ([Oxl11] Theorem 1.7.5). In fact, there is a bijection between geometric lattices and simple
matroids ([Oxl11] p.54).
There is also an affine analogue of geometric lattice. Let S be a ranked meet semilattice. The atoms
of S are the rank 1 elements. A set I of atoms of S is called independent if the join ∨I is defined and
rank(∨I) = |I|.
Definition 2.4. A geometric semilattice is a ranked meet semilattice, which is atomic and the collection of
independent sets of atoms form a matroid.
There is an alternative description of geometric semilattices.
Theorem 2.5 ([WW86] Theorem 2.1). A ranked meet semilattice S is geometric if and only if
(1) every interval of S is a geometric lattice, and
(2) whenever rank(x) < rank(∨I) = |I|, where x ∈S and I is an independent set of atoms of S, there is
an atom a ∈I such that a ̸≤x and a ∨x exists.
An important characterization of geometric semilattices is the following theorem.
Theorem 2.6 ([WW86] Theorem 3.2). A poset S is a geometric semilattice if and only if there is a geometric
lattice L and an atom a of L, such that
S = L \ L≥a.
Furthermore, L and a are uniquely determined by S. We call the pointed lattice (L, a) the completion of S.
To produce a geometric semilattice, we have a more flexible way.
Proposition 2.7 ([WW86] Corollary 4.5). Let L be a geometric lattice and x ∈L. Then L \ L≥x is a
geometric semilattice.
