ABSTRACT
We investigate whether galaxies identified as superthin in optical images remain superthin in the near-infrared (NIR),
and how their extreme disk morphology is related to environment. From a nearby volume-limited sample, we select
210 superthin galaxies using two-dimensional bulge/disk decomposition of SDSS r-band images, requiring the disk
component to have a major-to-minor axis ratio a/b > 9. We measure disk shapes from SDSS griz to UKIDSS
JHK bands. Both the major- and minor-axis scales decrease from the optical to the NIR, reaching ∼0.6 of their
r-band values in the K band, but the disk axis ratio remains nearly unchanged. Thus, optically selected superthin
galaxies remain superthin in the NIR, implying that the old stellar populations traced by NIR light do not form a
prominent thick disk. Reanalysis of our sample and a previous superthin sample shows that earlier reported NIR
thickening is mainly due to a magnitude- and band-dependent bias in one-dimensional fitting. We further compare
their environments with matched control samples using projected cross-correlations, reconstructed local overdensities,
and large-scale-structure classifications. Superthin galaxies show lower clustering on ∼0.1–1 h−1 Mpc scales and lower
overdensities at 1 h−1 Mpc, but no clear residual dependence on large-scale-structure type. These results suggest that
superthin galaxies are preferentially central galaxies in relatively low-mass dark matter halos, consistent with a picture
in which high host-halo spin helps build and preserve extended, vertically thin stellar disks.
Key words: superthin galaxies – bulge/disk decomposition – disk ellipticity – environment
1 INTRODUCTION
Superthin galaxies were first identified approximately six
decades ago as a distinct class of edge-on spiral galaxies ex-
hibiting exceptionally small disk axial ratios, with minor-to-
major axis ratios typically exceeding 1 : 9 and sometimes
reaching as low as 1 : 30 (Vorontsov-Velyaminov 1967; de
Vaucouleurs 1974; Goad & Roberts 1979, 1981). These galax-
ies are characterized by the absence of a prominent bulge
component and display strikingly flat stellar disks with mini-
mal vertical thickness (Matthews et al. 1999). Early spectro-
scopic studies revealed that superthins possess low emission-
line ratios indicative of low metallicity and slow star forma-
tion, alongside slowly rising rotation curves that imply mod-
est central mass concentrations (Goad & Roberts 1981).
The intrinsic shapes of galaxies can be inferred statistically
from their projected images by assuming a single ellipsoid and
random orientation of the line of sight. Such studies indicate
that the thickness-to-diameter ratio of typical galaxies fol-
lows a distribution with a mean of 0.21 and a scatter of 0.05
(e.g., Holmberg 1946; Sandage et al. 1970; Noerdlinger 1979;
Binney & de Vaucouleurs 1981; Padilla & Strauss 2008), cor-

minor-to-major axis ratio reduces to ∼1/8.9 (Guthrie 1992;
de Grijs 1998; Kregel et al. 2002; Mosenkov et al. 2015), yet
this remains thicker than that of the majority of superthin
galaxies. Superthins thus occupy the extreme flat tail of the
axis ratio distribution, reflecting their classification as very
late-type spirals (Heidmann et al. 1972).
Disk galaxies typically comprise two distinct stellar compo-
nents: a thin disk and a surrounding thick disk (e.g., Yoachim
& Dalcanton 2006; Comer´on et al. 2011; Mart´ınez-Lombilla
& Knapen 2019). The thick disk is characterized by a scale
height approximately four times larger than that of the thin
disk, along with older, more metal-poor stellar populations.
Superthin galaxies appear to represent an extreme case with
very weak bulges and little evidence for a prominent thick
disk, effectively constituting a nearly “naked” thin disk. This
unique morphology naturally invites comparison with low
surface brightness galaxies (LSBs), with which superthins
share many physical properties, differing primarily in incli-
nation: superthins are viewed edge-on while LSBs are ob-
served face-on (Banerjee & Bapat 2017; Narayanan & Baner-
jee 2022). Both classes exhibit high gas fractions, low star

The formation and persistence of superthin morphologies
should in principle provide interesting constraints and chal-
lenges for galaxy formation models. In the current theoretical
framework, galaxies form from condensed gas in the poten-
tial wells of dark matter halos (White & Rees 1978; Fall &
Efstathiou 1980; Blumenthal et al. 1984; Mo et al. 1998).
Angular momentum, acquired through tidal torques (Pee-
bles 1969; Doroshkevich 1970; White 1984), plays a crucial
role in determining disk sizes (Fall & Efstathiou 1980; Mo
et al. 1998). Energy feedback prevents overcooling and helps
preserve angular momentum in the stellar component (Weil
et al. 1998; Sommer-Larsen et al. 1999; Thacker & Couchman
2000). Subsequent hierarchical clustering reshapes galaxies
through mergers. While major mergers can drive morpho-
logical transformations (Toomre & Toomre 1972; Toomre
1977), even minor mergers can dynamically heat disks or
drive gas inflows, leading to thickening (Quinn & Goodman
1986; Quinn et al. 1993; Toth & Ostriker 1992; Walker et al.
1996; Velazquez & White 1999). In extreme cases, successive
minor mergers may even convert disk galaxies into ellipticals
(Bournaud et al. 2007). However, recent simulations reveal
that the outcome depends sensitively on gas fraction and or-
bital geometry; disk-dominant galaxies can survive even ma-
jor mergers when gas-rich and on prograde coplanar orbits
(Springel & Hernquist 2005; Athanassoula et al. 2016). By
tracking the evolution of superthin galaxies in the TNG100
simulation (Marinacci et al. 2018; Naiman et al. 2018; Nelson
et al. 2018; Pillepich et al. 2018; Springel et al. 2018), Hu et al.
(2024) found that the progenitors of present-day superthin
galaxies had similar morphologies to normal disk galaxies at
higher redshifts, developing their extended flat structures, in
terms of larger axis ratio a/b, since z ∼1 through frequent
prograde mergers that significantly increase dark matter halo
spin.
To unravel the nature of superthin galaxies, numerous ob-
servations have been carried out. Individual objects such as
UGC 7321 have been examined in detail (Goad & Roberts
1981; Matthews et al. 1999), revealing moderate ionization
and low metallicity. Karachentsev (1989) found a tight Tully-
Fisher relation for superthin galaxies, suggesting they may
serve as distance indicators. More recently, large samples
have enabled statistical investigations. Early efforts relied on
visual selection (Karachentsev et al. 1993, 1999). Kautsch
and collaborators (Kautsch et al. 2006; Kautsch 2009c,b;
Kautsch et al. 2009; Kautsch 2009a) constructed a sample
of 15,127 edge-on galaxies from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey
(SDSS; York et al. 2000) using an automated method, iden-
tifying a bulge-less (simple) disk fraction of 15% and finding
such systems in environments ranging from isolated fields
to moderate-density groups. Bizyaev et al. (Bizyaev et al.
2014) built another edge-on galaxy sample and applied a one-
dimensional photometric decomposition technique (Bizyaev
& Mitronova 2002, 2009) to measure disk radial-to-vertical
scale ratios. From this sample, they selected 85 superthin
objects with disk radial-to-vertical scale ratios exceeding 9
(Bizyaev et al. 2017, hereafter B17) and found that they
preferentially inhabit low-density environments with weaker
connections to filaments. Subsequently, 49 of these galax-
ies were observed in the near-infrared (NIR; Bizyaev et al.
2020, hereafter B20). Comparing optical and NIR structural
parameters, B20 found that the NIR scale length is signifi-
cantly shorter than the optical scale length, while the scale
height remains relatively comparable between bands. Follow-
up spectroscopy by Bizyaev et al. (2021) revealed that a large
fraction of blue superthin galaxies are dynamically undere-
volved, with low vertical velocity dispersions in both gas and
stars. More recent observations indicate that superthin galax-
ies typically reside in low-density field environments, where
major mergers are rare, allowing their thin disks to persist,
and that suppression of bar formation, enabled by the dom-
inance of dark matter in the inner regions, further prevents
the dynamical heating that would otherwise thicken the disk
(Aditya & Banerjee 2025).
The result from B20 that superthin galaxies appear thicker
in the NIR than in the optical raises a fundamental question:
are superthin galaxies identified in the optical still superthin
when observed in the NIR? This question directly addresses
the structure of the stellar disk. If NIR images show a thicker
disk, it would suggest that old stars (which dominate the NIR
light) are distributed over a larger vertical scale height than
younger stars, implying that dynamical heating has acted
to puff up the older population. Conversely, if the extreme
thinness persists in the NIR, it indicates that the thin disk
shape is independent of wavelength and that such dynam-
ical thickening has either not occurred or been effectively
suppressed—for example, by frequent prograde mergers as
seen in the TNG100 simulation by Hu et al. (2024).
In this work, we construct a new sample of superthin galax-
ies using two-dimensional (2D) bulge/disk decomposition ap-
plied to SDSS images. Unlike the one-dimensional method
used in B17, our 2D approach utilizes all pixels in the galaxy
image and yields more robust structural parameters. Using
multi-band imaging, SDSS griz bands and UKIDSS JHK
bands, we examine whether the superthin morphology is pre-
served in the NIR. As we demonstrate, using our 2D de-
composition approach, we find that the extreme thinness of
superthin galaxies persists in the NIR, indicating that the
stellar disk remains vertically thin regardless of wavelength.
Thus, our analysis directly tests the discrepancy noted by B20
and provides new insight into the vertical structure of stellar
disks in these extreme systems. Additionally, we investigate
the environmental dependence of superthin galaxies using a
suite of metrics spanning small to large scales (projected cor-
relation functions, overdensity of the local environment, and
large-scale structure classification), comparing our superthin
sample with a control sample matched in stellar mass and
colour.
This paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we describe
the galaxy samples and the optical and NIR imaging to be
used in this work. Section 3 explains our 2D decomposition
of galaxy images and analyses of the shapes of the superthin
galaxies. Section 4 examines the relation with environment.
We conclude with a discussion and summary in Section 5.

2 DATA
2.1 SDSS Edge-On Galaxies
We select our galaxy sample from the NASA Sloan Atlas
(NSA; Blanton et al. 2011)1 which includes both spectro-
scopic and photometric properties for ∼0.6 million galaxies
at z < 0.15 from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS; York
et al. 2000). For this work we restrict ourselves to relatively
nearby galaxies. We select a volume-limited sample with z-
band absolute magnitudes Mz < −18.6 and spectroscopically
measured redshifts 0.02 < z < 0.05. The absolute magnitudes
are calculated from the model magnitudes from the NSA as
estimated with a single S´ersic profile, corrected for Galactic
extinction and K-corrected to redshift zero. The sample con-
sists of ∼76,000 galaxies and serves as the parent sample from
which we select superthin galaxies as well as control samples
for comparison.
To select galaxies with a superthin disk, we further restrict
the parent sample to relatively edge-on galaxies, requiring
their r-band major-to-minor axis ratio a/b > 3. The axis ra-
tios a/b are taken from the NSA, where they are obtained
by fitting a two-dimensional S´ersic profile to the r-band im-
age of each galaxy. This selection yields a sample of ∼15,000
galaxies with relatively (but not extremely) thin shapes.
Figure 1 displays the diagram of colour index u −r versus
log10(M∗/M⊙) for both the parent volume-limited sample
(grey background contours) and the subsample of edge-on
galaxies with a/b > 3 (black dashed contours). The well-

Compared to the parent sample, the edge-on galaxy sample
misses the most massive galaxies with log10(M∗/M⊙)≳10.7

In Section 3 we perform careful bulge/disk decomposition
in multiple bands for each of these galaxies. This decompo-
sition allows us to measure the axis ratios of the disk and (if
present) bulge components separately. Our final sample of su-
perthin galaxies will then be selected based on unusually high
axis ratios of the disk component, minimizing contamination
from the central bulge.
2.2 SDSS and UKIDSS imaging data
We make use of both optical images from the SDSS and
near-infrared (NIR) images from the UKIRT Infrared Deep
Sky Survey (UKIDSS; Lawrence et al. 2007). SDSS images
were obtained with the Sloan 2.5-meter telescope located
at Apache Point Observatory (APO) in five bands, ugriz
(Fukugita et al. 1996; Smith et al. 2002; Ivezi´c et al. 2004),
using a large multi-CCD drift scan camera (Gunn et al. 1998).
Given the relatively low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the u
band, we consider only griz bands in this work, which cover
wavelengths from ∼4000 ˚A to ∼1.1 µm. The raw imaging
data are sky-subtracted and calibrated, both photometrically
(Hogg et al. 2001) and astrometrically (Pier et al. 2003).
Details of the SDSS data reduction pipelines and products
can be found in Stoughton et al. (2002). For each galaxy in
our parent sample, we retrieve the corrected image frames
in FITS format directly from the SDSS Data Archive Server
(DAS). Information necessary for the following analysis, such
as the parameter psf width, is fetched from the SDSS Cata-
log Archive Server (CAS).
UKIDSS images were obtained with the Wide Field
Camera (WFCAM; Casali et al. 2007) on the 3.8-meter
United Kingdom Infra-Red Telescope (UKIRT) in five bands,
ZY JHK (Hewett et al. 2006). We consider the JHK bands,
covering wavelengths from ∼1.1 to ∼2.5 µm. For each galaxy
and given band, we retrieve the stacked frame from the WF-
CAM Science Archive (WSA; Hambly et al. 2008)2. We note
that approximately half of the galaxies in our sample are not
covered by UKIDSS due to its limited sky coverage. For these
galaxies, we include them in the analysis of optical images
but exclude them when analysing NIR images. In Figure 1,
for comparison, the distribution of the edge-on galaxies in
our sample with UKIDSS data available is plotted as dotted
red contours, which are almost identical to the contours of
the full edge-on galaxy sample. This suggests that the former
can be considered a representative subset of the latter, thus
providing unbiased statistics despite the smaller sample size.

2.3 Reference and Random Samples
In Section 4 we investigate the clustering of our su-
perthin galaxies by estimating the projected two-point cross-
correlation function of the superthin galaxy sample with re-
spect to a reference sample, with the help of a random sam-
ple to account for the selection effects in the reference sam-
ple. Following Li et al. (2006a,b), we construct the reference
and random samples from the New York University Value-
Added Galaxy Catalog (NYU-VAGC)3, which was compiled
by Blanton et al. (2005) from the seventh data release of the
SDSS (DR7; Abazajian et al. 2009). The reference sample
3 http://sdss.physics.nyu.edu/vagc/
consists of approximately half a million galaxies with spec-
troscopically measured redshifts 0.01 < z < 0.2 and r-band
apparent Petrosian magnitude r < 17.6. The random sam-
ple is constructed to have the same selection effects as the
reference sample, but with a randomised spatial distribution
and a sample size ten times larger than that of the reference
sample.
2.4 Overdensity and Large-Scale Structure
Classification
In Section 4 we also investigate the local environment of the
superthin galaxies by considering two quantities: the den-
sity contrast (ρ/¯ρ) and the type of the large-scale structure
(LSS) of the local environment. We take the measurements of
ρ/¯ρ and LSS type from the SDSS density field reconstruction
of Wang et al. (2016), which fully covers the volume of our
galaxy sample. The density field was obtained by running a
constrained N-body simulation starting from the initial den-
sity field of the nearby universe, reconstructed to closely re-
produce the distribution of galaxy groups in the SDSS DR7.
The reconstructed density field is then smoothed on different
scales, and the resulting cosmic velocity and tidal fields are
used to classify each grid cell in the smoothed field into one of
four categories: void, filament, sheet, or cluster. In this work
we consider density field derived at smoothing scales of 1, 2,
and 3 h−1 Mpc, whereas the 2 h−1 Mpc version is adopted for
LLS type classification.

3.2 Selection of superthin galaxies
We select superthin galaxies based on the best-fit model in
the r band. Specifically, a galaxy is classified as a superthin
galaxy if its exponential disk has a major-to-minor axis ratio
a/b > 9. This selection yields a sample of 343 superthin galax-
ies, ∼0.45% of the parent volume-limited sample (see § 2.1).
We then visually examine the SDSS gri composite image of
each galaxy and exclude 37 galaxies with apparent problems
in the image data. Among the remaining galaxies, we find
that about 30% show prominent dust lanes or clear residual
structures after subtraction of the best-fit model. Given the
potential impact of these features on shape measurements, we
exclude these galaxies through visual inspection of the resid-
ual images. This leaves a final sample of 210 galaxies, which
are shown as blue or cyan triangles in Figure 1. When com-
pared to the parent sample of edge-on galaxies with a/b > 3,
the superthin galaxy sample is preferentially located in the
middle colour regime, thus lacking both the reddest and the
bluest galaxies at a fixed mass. The lack of bluest galax-
ies might be caused by dust reddening as mentioned above,
while the lack of reddest galaxies may indicate that low-level
star formation is still present in superthin galaxies.

4 CLUSTERING AND ENVIRONMENT
We now examine whether superthin galaxies occupy envi-
ronments that differ from those of otherwise similar galaxies.
We use three complementary diagnostics: the projected cross-
correlation function, the local overdensity, and the large-
scale-structure (LSS) classification. The reference and ran-
dom samples used for the correlation analysis are described
in Section 2.3, and the overdensity and LSS measurements
are described in Section 2.4; here we focus on the comparison
between the superthin samples and matched control samples.
4.1 Control samples
Galaxy environment depends strongly on stellar mass and
colour, so we compare each superthin sample with a control
sample matched in these quantities. The controls are drawn
from the parent volume-limited sample after excluding the
corresponding superthin galaxies. We use a kernel-based re-
sampling procedure to match the distributions of M∗and
g −r colour for the full superthin sample, and separately for
the “bulgeless” and bulge subsamples. For the LSS compari-
son, we additionally match the local overdensity smoothed on
1 h−1 Mpc, so that the LSS test probes any residual depen-
dence on cosmic-web type at fixed stellar mass, colour, and
smaller-scale density. For measurements involving the ELU-
CID density field, the control samples are constructed only
from galaxies with available overdensity and LSS measure-
ments.

of a fitted bulge.
4.3 Local overdensity
We next use the reconstructed density field from Wang et al.
(2016) to compare the local overdensities of superthin and
control galaxies. We consider smoothing scales of 1, 2, and
3 h−1 Mpc; for compactness, Figure 13 shows the results for 1
and 3 h−1 Mpc, while the 2 h−1 Mpc result lies between them.
The full superthin sample is shifted toward lower local
densities relative to its control sample, especially at the
1 h−1 Mpc smoothing scale. The difference becomes weaker
at 3 h−1 Mpc, indicating that the environmental dependence
is strongest on scales comparable to the sizes of dark mat-
ter halos. This is consistent with the interpretation from the
projected correlation function: superthin galaxies tend to be
central galaxies in relatively low-mass halos, and are therefore
located in low-density environments. Both their likely central
locations and their low overdensities indicate that they are
less affected by environmental processes than satellite galax-
ies in massive halos. Such a halo-scale preference also con-
nects naturally to the formation scenario proposed by Hu
et al. (2024), who found in the TNG100 simulation that su-
perthin galaxies develop their extended thin disks since z ∼1
as frequent prograde mergers increase the spin of their host
dark matter halos. Our environmental results therefore sug-
gest that the formation of superthin galaxies is closely linked
to the assembly and angular-momentum growth of their host
halos. The weak dependence of disk thickness on waveband
found in Section 3.3 is also consistent with this high-spin
halo picture: in a high-angular-momentum system, even the
old stars traced by NIR light need not be dynamically hot
enough to form a prominent thick disk. As in the correlation-
function analysis, the bulgeless and bulge subsamples show
similar trends, with no clear evidence that the presence of a
bulge changes the environmental preference.

5 SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION
In this work, we construct a sample of superthin galaxies se-
lected from their disk shapes in the SDSS r band, as measured
through two-dimensional bulge/disk decomposition. We use
SDSS griz and UKIDSS JHK imaging to test whether op-
tically identified superthin galaxies remain superthin in the
NIR, and we compare our measurements with previous 1D
analyses of the B17 comparison sample and the B20 mea-
surements. We also investigate the environments of the su-
perthin galaxies using projected cross-correlation functions,
reconstructed local overdensities, and LSS classifications, al-
ways comparing with control samples matched in the relevant
galaxy properties. Our conclusions are summarised as follows:
(i) From the optical to the NIR bands, the disk scale sizes
of superthin galaxies decrease along both the major and mi-
nor axes. The decreases along the two axes have roughly the
same amplitude (K/r ∼0.6), resulting in an almost invari-
ant disk shape. The presence of a bulge has no significant
influence on this result (Figures 4, 7, 8);
(ii) The apparent NIR thickening reported by B20 is
mainly associated with the combination of sample selection
and measurement method. In particular, the 1D method
shows a magnitude- and band-dependent bias in the mea-
sured radial scale length, which can artificially increase hs/rs
in the NIR for fainter galaxies. When the 2D method is ap-
plied, both our sample and the B17 sample show little wave-
band dependence in disk thickness (Figures 10, 11);
(iii) Superthin galaxies prefer relatively low-density envi-
ronments on halo scales. Their lower projected clustering am-
plitude on ∼0.1–1 h−1 Mpc scales and their lower overden-
sities at 1 h−1 Mpc suggest that they are preferentially cen-
tral galaxies in relatively low-mass dark matter halos. The
presence of a bulge has no significant influence on this trend
(Figures 12, 13);
(iv) After controlling for stellar mass, colour, and local
overdensity, superthin galaxies show no clear residual depen-
dence on large-scale-structure type (Figure 14). This indi-
cates that their environmental connection is mainly a halo-
scale effect rather than a direct dependence on the larger-
scale cosmic web.
Taken together, the structural and environmental results
suggest that the extreme thinness of these galaxies is linked
to the properties and assembly histories of their host dark
matter halos. The weak dependence of disk thickness on
waveband indicates that we find no evidence for a promi-
nent NIR-dominant thick component; even the older stellar
populations traced by the NIR light remain vertically thin.
Meanwhile, the preference for central locations in relatively
low-mass halos and low-density environments suggests that
superthin galaxies are less affected by environmental pro-
cesses such as satellite interactions or tidal heating in mas-
sive halos. This picture is consistent with the formation sce-
nario of Hu et al. (2024), in which superthin galaxies develop
their extended thin disks since z ∼1 as frequent prograde
mergers increase the spin of their host halos. In this inter-
pretation, mergers are not simply absent; rather, their or-

Looking further ahead, existing and future deep imaging sur-

systematic searches for superthin galaxies beyond the local
