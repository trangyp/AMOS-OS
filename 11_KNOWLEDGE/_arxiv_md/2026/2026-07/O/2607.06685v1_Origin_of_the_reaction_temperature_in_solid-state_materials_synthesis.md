Origin of the reaction temperature in solid-state materials synthesis

Abstract: Temperature plays a crucial role in solid-state materials synthesis, but there is currently 
no mechanistic theory to explain or predict which temperature is best to conduct a solid-state 
reaction. Reactions between powder precursors are conventionally assumed to be slow diffusion-
limited processes; however, recent in situ experiments show that solid-state reactions can complete 
in minutes above a critical onset temperature. Here, we present evidence that a transient liquid 
phase forms above the metastable eutectic temperature, and that this non-equilibrium liquid serves 
as a fast diffusion medium to intermix precursors and initiate a solid-state reaction. This 
thermodynamic principle is agnostic to the structure or chemistry of the reactants, and can be 
applied towards the synthesis and manufacturing of a wide range of complex materials.


## Page 2


Reaction temperature is arguably the most important processing parameter in the solid-state 
synthesis of inorganic materials. Despite the ubiquity of solid-state reactions in preparative inorganic 
chemistry, there is currently no theoretical understanding of what temperature is optimal for a solid-
state reaction (1, 2). Tammann’s rule is a century-old heuristic which suggests that solid-state reactions 
should be carried out between ½ and ⅔ of the melting temperature of the precursors (3). Unfortunately, 
the rule lacks a mechanistic foundation, and in fact there are many violations of Tammann’s rule (4, 5). 
For this reason, solid-state synthesis often requires many tedious ‘cook and look’ experiments to 
iteratively optimize the reaction temperature (6).  
Interdiffusion between powder precursors is the primary rate-limiting kinetic process in solid-
state synthesis reactions (7). Therefore, the onset temperature of a solid-state reaction should be the 
critical temperature at which interdiffusion becomes activated. In the synthesis of oxide ceramics, 
which encompasses important materials for Li-ion batteries, superconductors, and solid oxide fuel-
cells, it is generally assumed that high temperatures (>700°C) and long reaction times (>12 hours) are 
needed for significant interdiffusion (8–10). This intuition is supported by a simple estimate from an 
Einstein diffusion model, <𝑥2> = D/2t (11), where an optimistic oxide self-diffusion coefficient of the 
order D ~ 10−13 cm2/s would require tens of hours to complete interdiffusion between powder 
precursors that range from a typical x ~ 0.1 – 100 microns in size (12). 
Surprisingly, recent in situ XRD characterization experiments show that solid-state reactions 
can proceed very quickly. For example, elementary pairwise reactions can reach completion within ~1 
minute across a diverse range of systems, including NaxCoO2, YBa2Cu3O7-x, Li7La3Zr2O12, Li4Ti5O12, 
PbWO4, KBiS2, and Fe2SiS4 (13–19) at subsolidus temperatures on the phase diagram, where only 
solid-state diffusion mechanisms should be active. A theory to explain how fast, low-temperature solid-
state reactions proceed is essential towards building a fundamental understanding of solid-state 
interfacial reactivity. Moreover, a theory built on this mechanism would enable the rational design of 
temperature-time processing protocols for the synthesis and manufacturing of complex functional 
materials relevant to a variety of industrial technologies.  
The role of a metastable liquid eutectic 
Here, we hypothesize that solid-state reactions proceed in a non-equilibrium two-step sequence, 
where fast interdiffusion at low temperatures is mediated by a transient metastable liquid phase. 
Consider the relative free energy curves associated with a two-step reaction A + B  Liquid  AB, as 
illustrated in Fig. 1A. Although the liquid melt is the equilibrium phase only at high temperature, there 
are a range of temperatures where the liquid can be lower in free energy than the starting precursors A 
and B, but higher in free energy than the product AB. Within this temperature range, a liquid phase 
could spontaneously form at the interface between the precursors, while still being metastable (higher 
in free energy) with respect to the equilibrium solid phase AB.  
The minimum temperature for this non-equilibrium liquid to form is when the liquid has the 
same chemical potential as the precursors [A + B]. This occurs at the metastable eutectic temperature, 
which can be estimated by extending the liquidus curves on an equilibrium phase diagram from the 
precursors toward a metastable eutectic point, as illustrated by the dashed green lines in Fig. 1B.

Melting is generally more kinetically facile than solidification (20, 21). We therefore propose 
that solid-state reactions proceed in two stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1C. In Step 1, a metastable liquid 
forms at the interface between A and B powders, providing a fast diffusion medium to rapidly intermix 
precursors at the atomistic scale. However, the liquid is higher energy than the product AB phase, and 
is therefore metastable. Subsequently, in Step 2, the product AB phase nucleates from this liquid phase. 
The AB compound grows and consumes the liquid, potentially separating the precursors A and B from 
one another and terminating the rapid reaction, as shown in Fig. 1C (bottom). Such a process can only 
occur between contacting A and B particles, as a eutectic melt requires contact to initiate.

Two-step nucleation mechanisms are common in crystallization dynamics (22), and a similar 
metastable eutectic pathway has been identified in the rapid solidification in metal alloys (23). This 
mechanism derives from fundamental principles in thermodynamics and kinetics, and is agnostic to 
the structure or chemistry of the reactants and products. Therefore, this mechanism should be a broadly 
accessible pathway in solid-state interfacial reactions, so long as more kinetically-viable pathways 
(topotactic or martensitic transformations) are unavailable.  
In this work, we validate this metastable liquid eutectic hypothesis with a multi-modal 
computational and experimental study. First, we select a model system and evaluate its metastable 
eutectic temperature by combining first-principles density functional theory calculations with a 
Calculation of Phase Diagrams (CALPHAD) thermodynamic assessment. Next, we leverage three 
complementary experimental characterization techniques: 1) We use in situ synchrotron X-ray 
diffraction (XRD) to characterize a Temperature-Time-Transformation (TTT) diagram of the reaction 
progression, complemented with phase field modeling to simulate the competition between reactant 
melting and product solidification. 2) We use in situ Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) to 
directly observe the microstructural evolution of precursor melting. 3) We leverage nanotomography 
via Transmission X-ray Microscopy (TXM) to estimate reactant diffusivity during the solid-state 
reaction. These three perspectives offer complementary observations that, altogether, affirm the role of 
a transient liquid phase in facilitating solid-state reactions.  
To select a model system that is compatible with these three experimental techniques, we 
sought a reaction that satisfies multiple constraints: There should only be one product compound in its 
binary phase diagram; the predicted metastable eutectic temperature should be accessible by our in situ 
gradient heater (< 700°C); and the elements should have different X-ray attenuation lengths to 
distinguish the reactant and product phases in X-ray nanotomography. We used the Materials Platform 
for Data Science (24) to screen all pseudo-binary oxide phase diagrams in the ASM Phase Diagram 
database, and we chose Fe2O3 + 3 MoO3  Fe2(MoO4)3 as the best reaction for this study. 
Onset temperature of a solid-state reaction 
To determine the metastable eutectic temperature in the MoO3-Fe2O3 pseudo-binary system 
(24, 25), we used the ESPEI package (26) to perform a CALPHAD thermodynamic assessment, where 
Gibbs free energy models were constrained by solid-state formation energies as calculated by density 
functional theory using the SCAN functional (27). We computed the metastable eutectic temperature 
to be 390°C, which is determined by recalculating the phase diagram after excluding Fe2(MoO4)3 from 
the thermodynamic model (Fig. 2A).  
In situ synchrotron XRD was performed at beamlines 11-ID-B and 17-BM of the Advanced 
Photon Source, Argonne National Laboratory, to track the reaction progression of the Fe2O3 + 3 MoO3 
as a function of time and temperature with a time resolution of 1 second. We leverage our recently 
designed thermal gradient heater (28), which can simultaneously survey a continuum of temperatures 
by spatially varying the sample position in the beam (Fig. 2A, right). We then constructed TTT curves 
by monitoring nine isothermal reactions over the temperature range of 396°C to 651°C.

Figure 2B reveals the key phenomenology of solid-state reactions. First, we observe an 
apparent critical reaction onset temperature of 493°C, below which product formation was negligible 
(1% – 2%). Above this temperature threshold, the yield increased monotonically with temperature, 
reaching 76% completion at 650°C. These reactions proceed quickly, approaching an asymptotic 
reaction completion within 2 minutes. After 2 minutes, the reaction slows down dramatically; for 
example, at 651 °C, only < 6% additional completion was observed between 2 minutes and 15 minutes 
after reaching 70% completion at 2 minutes. We also conducted 48 hours ex situ experiments at 520°C, 
which increased the reaction completion by only 4% relative to the 15 min in situ yield (fig. S4). 
The observed phenomenology from the TTT curves in Fig 2B is consistent with our proposed 
two-step mechanism. Below the critical temperature, only a minor amount of product forms at the 
solid-state interface, due to solid-state diffusion being too slow to drive precursor mixing. Above the 
critical temperature, a transient metastable liquid offers a fast interdiffusion medium to mix Fe2O3 and 
MoO3. One apparent discrepancy is that the experimentally observed onset temperature (493°C) is 
higher than the predicted metastable eutectic temperature (390°C). However, this observation can be 
reconciled by considering the energetic competition between Step 1—melting of the precursors, and 
Step 2—crystallization of the product from the transient metastable liquid. Figure 2C shows the Gibbs 
free energies of the precursor phases, [Fe2O3 + 3 MoO3], the product phase Fe2(MoO4)3, and the liquid 
phase, as a function of temperature. At the precise metastable eutectic temperature, 390°C, the Gibbs 
free energies between the precursors and the liquid are equal, meaning that there is no driving force for 
liquid formation. One must superheat beyond the metastable eutectic temperature to provide driving 
force to generate some liquid.  
As superheating increases, the driving force for metastable liquid formation grows, while the 
driving force for solidification of the product diminishes. To simulate the kinetic competition between 
these two processes, we parameterized a phase-field model using our CALPHAD-assessed free 
energies. We initiate the simulation with a small liquid seed at the precursor interface, and then nucleate 
a product phase following its characteristic induction time, given by τ = 1 / JV, where J is its steady-
state nucleation rate and V is the volume of the parent liquid.  
Simulation results for mild superheating (468°C) and large superheating (651°C) are shown in 
Fig. 2D, represented using Cahn’s time-cone construction (29), where a nucleation event is represented 
by the vertex of a time-cone, and growth of the liquid (purple) or Fe2(MoO4)3 product (orange) is by 
the outward expansion rate of the cone. The middle panels of Fig. 2D visualize the corresponding 
phase evolution at 468 °C and 651 °C, with videos of the simulation in Supplementary video S1-S3. 
These phase fractions show how superheating shifts the timing of liquid formation relative to product 
growth, and can be semi-quantitatively compared to the experimentally-measured reaction progress.  
Our CALPHAD-parameterized simulations show that an interfacial reaction should indeed 
proceed in two steps through a non-equilibrium liquid melt. Notably, we find that the reaction extent 
depends mainly on how much liquid is produced before the solid phase nucleates and consumes it. 
With mild superheating, liquid formation is slow, and even when Fe2(MoO4)3 eventually nucleates it 
consumes the liquid quickly, separating the precursors and terminating the reaction prematurely. At 
higher superheating, faster melting provides more high-diffusivity liquid medium for intermixing, 
allowing for a larger fraction of Fe2(MoO4)3 to grow before the metastable liquid is consumed (fig. S5).

As another validation of this mechanism, we repeated our experiment by substituting the α-
Fe2O3 precursor with the metastable polymorph γ-Fe2O3. Since γ-Fe2O3 is higher in Gibbs free energy 
than α-Fe2O3 (30), the metastable eutectic transformation should occur at lower temperatures. In our 
CALPHAD modeling, replacing α-Fe2O3 with γ-Fe2O3 reduced the metastable eutectic temperature 
from 390 °C to 339 °C (fig. S6). Experimentally, we indeed observed a corresponding reduction of the 
onset temperature from 493°C to 421°C (fig. S7). This experiment further supports our thermodynamic 
interpretation of the metastable liquid phase, and opens up the opportunity to explore how more 
unstable (higher free energy) precursors can promote lower reaction temperatures (31). 
TEM observation of precursor melting 
We next use in situ TEM with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) to directly observe 
the morphological evolution of the solid-state reaction. While running control experiments, we found 
that MoO3 reduces to MoO2 in the TEM at approximately 500 °C (32), which is near the observed 
493°C onset temperature for α-Fe2O3 + 3 MoO3  Fe2(MoO4)3. For this reason, we characterized the 
γ-Fe2O3 + 3 MoO3 reaction, whose lower onset temperature of 421°C helps deconvolute MoO3 
reduction from the solid-state reaction in the TEM. Control experiments heating only MoO3 particles 
or only γ-Fe2O3 particles up to 450°C confirm that the isolated precursors are inert and maintain their 
original shape throughout heating (fig. S10). 
Fig. 3. In situ TEM observations on the synthesis of Fe2(MoO4)3. (A) In situ TEM images show the heating of MoO3 
+ γ-Fe2O3 particles at 400 °C; from 420 °C to 450 °C; after held at 450 °C for 2 mins. (B) EDX elemental mapping of 
Fe and Mo before and after the reaction. 
Fig. 3A shows the morphological evolution of a large MoO3 rod (~ 0.5 µm × 5 µm) in contact 
with γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticulate powders (~100 nm radius) when increasing temperature. First, to confirm 
the existence of a critical onset temperature, we held the hot-stage at 400°C for 30 minutes and saw no 
morphology change. Then, upon heating to 420°C, portions of the MoO3 rod that are in contact with 
Fe2O3 exhibit the breakup of MoO3 into rounded droplets, which morphologically shows a melt 
undergoing Rayleigh-Plateau instability to reduce surface tension (33) (Movies S4 and S5). The post-
reaction images and corresponding EDX (Fig. 3B) confirm the intimate mixing of Mo and Fe.

Another key observation from the TEM experiment is that nanoparticles of γ-Fe2O3 that are 
not in contact with MoO3 do not change shape, and importantly, retain their sharp corners and edges 
instead of becoming rounded. This eliminates a possible competing hypothesis that a liquid phase 
results from surface pre-melting, or a Gibbs-Thomson melting point depression of γ-Fe2O3 
nanoparticles (34), as the γ-Fe2O3 particles do not change shape unless they are in contact with MoO3. 
Moreover, melting point depression should only be a significant thermodynamic effect only when 
nanoparticle sizes are < 2 nm in size (35), whereas these Fe2O3 nanoparticles are >50 nm.  
The importance of precursor contact is anticipated by the metastable liquid eutectic hypothesis, 
in which interfacial contact between two solids is required for liquid eutectic formation. This further 
explains why repeated regrinding and annealing is often needed in solid-state reactions, which restores 
interfacial contact between powder reactants (36). As we showed in previous work, a high packing 
density also creates more interfaces between precursors (37), which would promote the formation of 
more metastable eutectic liquid.  
 
Diffusivity estimation from 3D nanotomography  
Finally we sought to quantify the effective diffusivity that enables precursor phases to intermix 
during Fe2(MoO4)3 formation. Inspired by recent studies that use 3D morphological evolution to 
quantify kinetic properties in heterogeneous materials (38, 39), we developed a new approach that uses 
transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM) to reconstruct a sample volume before and after a solid-state 
reaction, to estimate a lower-bound diffusivity between reactants and products. Here, our 
reconstruction volume spanned dimensions of 28 μm × 29 μm × 12 μm, with a voxel size of 20 nm3. 
TXM reconstructions were first acquired in a reactant precursor mixture at room temperature (Fig. 4A), 
then after annealing at 650 °C for 15 minutes (Fig. 4B).  
To extract diffusivity from the observed morphology change, we first converted the before-
and-after TXM reconstructions into stoichiometrically-normalized and volumetrically-comparable 
reaction units. Thus, one Fe2O3 cluster with 100 voxels and one MoO3 cluster with 311 voxels react to 
form one Fe2(MoO4)3 cluster with 577 voxels. The pre- and post-reaction volumes were aligned by 
applying a global translation correction to account for minor spatial shifts introduced during sample 
handling. The aligned 3D volumes were then discretized into fixed-size clusters chosen so that each 
unit represents an equivalent amount of reacting volume across the precursor and product states.   
With these clusters defined, we next related the pre-reaction precursor morphology to the post-
reaction product morphology, inferring which two precursor clusters most plausibly gave rise to each 
product cluster. Since this correspondence is not directly observable from the two reconstructions, we 
used a constrained local-matching algorithm, schematized in Fig. 4C. For each product Fe2(MoO4)3 
cluster, a k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) algorithm searches for nearby precursor Fe2O3 and MoO3 clusters. 
Each possible combination of one Fe2(MoO4)3 cluster, one Fe2O3 precursor cluster, and one MoO3 
precursor cluster was defined as a candidate triplet. We constructed self-consistent sets of triplets for 
the TXM reconstruction, where each precursor or product cluster only appear once. Based on our 
earlier in situ XRD measurements from Fig. 2B, at 650°C and 15 minutes we expect a reaction yield 
of ~75%, and unselected clusters were treated as residual precursors.

By ranking all the sets of candidate triplets, we identify which set of triplet results in the 
minimal summed diffusion distance between precursor to products, which we used to estimate a lower-
bound diffusivity on the solid-state reaction. For Fe3+ diffusivity, the Fe2O3 – Fe2(MoO4)3 distance 
distribution extracted from the selected triplets is shown in Fig. 4E. Due to the long tail of diffusion 
distances, we use a 95th percentile cutoff as a characteristic distance (although the precise cutoff 
percentile does not affect the order of magnitude of the computed diffusivity). Applying a one-
dimensional Einstein diffusion relation (11), D = x2/2t , where x is taken as the 95th-percentile distance 
and t is the reaction time, we estimate an effective Fe3+ diffusivity of 2.0 × 10-10 cm2/s. At 650°C, this 
value lies near the upper bound of reported Fe3+ diffusivities in oxide melts (40–42), and is 8 orders-
of-magnitude higher than vacancy-mediated Fe3+ diffusivity in solid Fe2O3 (Fig. 4F). This offers 
quantitative evidence for liquid-enhanced diffusion during the reaction.

Discussion 
Tammann’s rule, proposed in 1932, states that solid-solid reactions initiate around ~⅔ the 
melting point of a precursor. The typical explanation invokes solid-state Arrhenius processes, such as 
heightened lattice vibrations (43) or a surge in vacancy concentrations (4). Here we proposed a new 
mechanism: the formation of a transient liquid phase above the metastable eutectic temperature. This 
liquid phase, created by eutectic interaction rather than by melting within either precursor alone, 
transforms angstrom-per-hour solid-state transport into a liquid conduit, increasing diffusivity by eight 
orders of magnitude. This mechanism therefore establishes the reaction onset temperature as a well-
defined thermodynamic coordinate, rather than an empirical heuristic. Recent work has shown that 
incorporating liquid-like interfacial transport enable more accurate predictions of solid-state synthesis 
outcomes (44); our proposed mechanism further clarifies the thermodynamic origin these non-
equilibrium liquid-like interfaces. 
Here, we tested the metastable liquid eutectic hypothesis using three characterization 
techniques, each providing a different perspective of the reaction. In situ XRD mapped phase fractions 
as a function of temperature and time, finding a reaction onset temperature commensurate with the 
CALPHAD-predicted metastable eutectic temperature. In situ TEM tracked microstructural evolution 
of the solid-state reaction in real space, observing breakup of the Fe2O3/MoO3 interface into liquid-like 
droplets. X-ray nanotomography enabled measurement of a diffusivity that is on the order of oxide 
melts, and 8 orders of magnitude higher than solid-state diffusion. These three signatures align and 
altogether give decisive support for a transient metastable liquid intermediate.  
Our observations also allow us to rule out competing explanations (Supplementary Text). 
Surface diffusion cannot provide sufficient transport cross-sectional area to account for >70% reaction 
completion within minutes. Short-circuit diffusion along grain boundaries should follow Arrhenius 
behavior, whereas the TTT data gathered by in situ XRD show non-Arrhenius temeprature-dependence 
of reaction completion. Finally, an adiabatic self-propagating reaction is precluded by the reaction 
enthalpy in this system, which is insufficient to melt the reactants to their equilibrium liquidus 
temperature. 
From a practical perspective, the metastable eutetic mechanism offers a simple way to estimate 
the reaction temperature, even from the equilibrium phase diagram. Fig. 5A-C illustrates this approach 
in three representative pseudo-binary systems: NiO–Al2O3, CaO–ZrO2, and AlN–Al2O3. We can 
visually extrapolate the equilibrium liquidus curves down from the precursors to the metastable 
eutectic temperature, which aligns closely with the experimentally-reported reaction temperature (red 
line). A practical synthesis temperature can also be approximated by drawing straight lines from the 
precursor melting points to their nearest eutectic points and identifying their intersection. This linear 
extrapolation yields a higher temperature than the metastable eutectic, but this overestimation may 
improve synthesis success by balancing the competing processes of liquid melting and solid nucleation.

quite successful, as the metastable eutectic temperature is indeed often about ½ to ⅔ the melting 
temperature of the precursors. However, it also can explain some deviations from Tammann’s rule, for 
example in the case of CaZrO3. Here, the melting point of ZrO2 is around 3000K (2715 °C), the rule 
predicts an onset near 2000K (1800 °C), yet it was experimentally reported that CaZrO₃ can form at a 
far lower temperature of 950 °C (45), close to the extrapolated metastable eutectic. As shown in

Here, to elucidate the fundamental mechanism driving solid-state reactions, we chose a model 
system with only one possible product phase. However, in general, phase diagrams can include 
multiple competing product phases. Although these systems may be more complicated, the driving 
force to form a transient liquid above the metastable eutectic temperature will remain the same. In this 
context, one must evaluate the nucleation kinetics between competing product phases in metastable 
liquid eutectic. We previously showed that when there are multiple competing products, if the 
thermodynamic driving force exceeds 60 meV/atom then the phase with the largest reaction energy 
tends to form first (60). However, more than 80% of possible solid-state reactions have driving forces 
< 60 meV/atom (61). In these cases, surface energy can influence the nucleation barrier for each 
competing phase, thereby determining which phase forms first (62, 63). Moreover, if the first-phase to 
form does not match the net stoichiometry of the reactants, then the first phase may consume most of 
the reaction driving force, leading to slow subsequent reactions (64). While these situations require 
more investigation, the metastable eutectic mechanism established here provides the necessary 
foundation for understanding and predicting synthesis reaction kinetics in these complex systems. 
 
This mechanistic foundation carries broad implications. Elevating precursor free energies 
through metastable polymorphs, hydrates, or mechanical alloying, or slightly depressing the liquid free 
energy with flux additives, can drive reactions hundreds of degrees lower than conventional practice. 
Our mechanism also unites seemingly disparate synthesis acceleration strategies: including ultrafast 
joule heating, spark-plasma sintering, hot pressing, and repeated regrinding, which all succeed because 
they either create transient liquid rapidly or perpetually renew precursor contact. Looking ahead, DFT-
assisted CALPHAD assessments can enable the rational design of optimal precursors, temperatures 
and reaction times for solid-state synthesis. By reframing solid-state synthesis as a targeted excursion 
through a metastable liquid, we resolve the long-standing puzzle of reaction temperatures and lay a 
quantitative pathway toward the more predictive manufacturing of complex inorganic materials.

S1.4 Thermodynamics Parameters Generation and Assessment Using ESPEI 
The generation and assessment of CALPHAD model parameters are facilitated by the 
Extensible Self-optimized Phase Equilibria Infrastructure (ESPEI), a Python-based tool (26). This 
infrastructure is capable of generating CALPHAD model parameters, including binary and ternary 
Redlich-Kister interaction parameters, from either experimental or computationally derived 
thermochemical data, employing Muggianu extrapolation for this purpose. ESPEI is designed to 
perform a comprehensive optimization of all relevant parameters—encompassing formation 
enthalpy, entropy, and mixing parameters—against a backdrop of thermochemical and phase 
equilibrium data. This optimization process utilizes a Bayesian ensemble approach through 
Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques.  
Following the determination of the formation enthalpy for Fe2(MoO4)3 via DFT-SCAN 
methodologies, there remains a requisite to ascertain liquid mixing energy data for the generation


## Page 24


of Redlich-Kister interaction parameters within a substitutional solution model. The current 
absence of calorimetric measurements for liquid mixing energies within the Fe2O3-MoO3 system 
necessitates the computational estimation of these energies at invariant points within the phase 
diagram—specifically, the eutectic and congruent melting points—based on the principle of energy 
equivalence between solid and liquid phases at these junctures. Subsequently, ESPEI utilizes these 
calculated liquid mixing energies as inputs for the derivation of binary interaction parameters. The 
resultant thermodynamic database is characterized by 7 degrees of freedom, addressing both 
stoichiometric compounds and binary interactions. The optimization of these parameters through 
the MCMC method is based on thermochemical and phase equilibrium data, with normal priors 
assigned varying weight factors to reflect their respective degrees of reliability. 
fig. S1 shows calculated phase diagram after the optimization of 500 MCMC iterations. 
The experimental phase equilibrium data in fig. S1 are extracted from Materials Platform for Data 
Science (MPDS) Database (24), which has digitalized all ASM phase diagrams. The phase diagram 
was plotted using Pycalphad package which is a python library for computational thermodynamics

S4. TEM Characterization of the Liquid-facilitated Solid-state Reaction 
γ-Fe₂O₃ + MoO₃ solutions were created by dispersing γ-Fe₂O₃ and MoO₃ nanoparticles in 
methanol and sonicated for 30 minutes to ensure uniform mixing. The resulting suspension was 
drop-cast onto a copper TEM grid and allowed to dry under ambient conditions. 
In situ TEM heating experiments were conducted using a Gatan Model 652 bulk heating 
holder on a Thermo Fisher Talos F200X microscope (200 keV) equipped with a Gatan OneView 
camera. The sample was heated from room temperature to 450 °C—the onset of melting—at a 
ramp rate of ~10 °C/s in discrete steps. At each temperature step, the sample was allowed to 
thermalize, defined by the cessation of drift for approximately 2–5 minutes (except for the 30-
minute hold at 400°C). After reaching the maximum temperature, the sample was cooled back to 
room temperature and re-thermalized. 
Control experiments were conducted using isolated MoO₃ and γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles to 
verify this reaction occurs only when the two precursors are in contact with each other. In both 
cases, the samples were heated from room temperature to 520°C with the same procedure


## Page 30


described above. Before 500°C, both nanoparticles showed no structural changes. Upon heating 
up from 500°C to 520°C, the structure of the MoO₃ nanoparticles changed dramatically, 
redepositing material around the nanoparticle (fig. S8A). However, the γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles 
showed no structural changes after heating to 520°C and annealing back to room temperature (fig. 
S8B). Thus, structural changes only occur when the MoO₃ and γ-Fe2O3 nanoparticles are in contact 
and further occur at a temperature far below the reduction temperature for MoO₃. 
To further rule out extraneous factors such as heating effects due to the electron beam, two 
target reaction regions were identified before heating began. One was chosen to be observed during 
heating while the other was only imaged before and after heating, incurring minimal exposure to 
the electron beam (fig. S8C). In both cases, the nanoparticles reacted and mixed as expected (fig. 
S8D), ruling out heating effects from the electron beam as a factor in facilitating the reaction. 
Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) maps were acquired at room temperature 
before and after heating using the same Talos F200X microscope operated at 200 keV with a 
10.5 mrad convergence angle and a beam current of 200 pA. 
S5. Fe2O3 Diffusivity Estimation during Solid-state Synthesis 
S5.1 TXM Data Collection 
Fine, nanoscale particle agglomerates on the order of 40 μm in equivalent diameter were 
characterized during in situ heating via transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM), beamline 18-ID at 
Brookhaven National Laboratory's National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II) with 22 
nm/pixel resolution (87). The particle agglomerates were loaded into thin-walled quartz capillaries 
(10 μm wall thickness, 100 μm diameter). The capillaries were mounted atop a hollow alumina 
rod and secured using boron nitride paste and inserted in the beamline furnace. The assembly was 
attached to a kinematic mount on the beamline stage, which allows translation along the x, y, and 
z directions and rotation about the z-axis. In the tomographic frame of reference, the z-axis 
corresponds to the sample rotation axis, while x and y denote directions perpendicular to this axis. 
After aligning the rotation center of the sample so the agglomerate is fully in the field-of-view, X-
ray imaging is performed at 7.05 keV and 7.15 keV. After transmission through the sample, the 
beam was passes through a Fresnel zone plate (32.3x magnification) to form a magnified image 
on an Andor Neo 5.5 detector coupled with a 10x optical objective lens.  
In practice, the experiment was done by capturing a full tomography scan of the sample 
prior to heating, a stationary radiography scan during heating at 5℃/min, then a full tomography 
scan at temperature after heating to 650 ℃. Tomography scans contain projections of the sample 
from 0°to 180° at a rate of 3 °/s, with 0.1 s exposures taken throughout the rotation, resulting in a 
total of 900 projection images.  
In principle, the sub-K-edge energy provides distinct attenuation lengths for the three 
constituent phases and should therefore improve phase differentiation, whereas MoO3 and 
Fe2(MoO4)3 have more similar attenuation above the K-edge (fig. S9). However, the 7.05 keV


## Page 31


tomogram provided limited photon flux, reducing the separation between phase gray values and 
the background (fig. S10). We therefore used the 7.15 keV dataset. 
S5.2 Reconstruction and Segmentation 
The tomographic data was reconstructed in 3D using TomoPy, an open-source, Python-
based framework for tomographic data processing (88). We apply standard normalization using 
dark and bright field images. Ring artifacts were removed from the sinogram using Vo et al.’s stripe 
removal method. All radiographs were linearized by applying a negative logarithm before 
reconstructed into 2D slices using the Gridrec algorithm, a Fourier-based method incorporating a 
Parzen filter. 
To prepare the tomographic reconstructions for quantitative analysis, we implemented a 
segmentation procedure that removes noise and isolates the individual phases. MoO3 and 
Fe2(MoO4)3 have higher X-ray transmission and therefore appear brighter than Fe2O3. The 
segmentation algorithm exploits this relative brightness hierarchy to distinguish the three phases 
in the reconstructed volume. The full segmentation workflow is shown in fig. S11. 
In the pre-heating dataset, a threshold was first applied to highlight the MoO3 phase 
because it can otherwise appear with similar brightness as the Fe2O3 phase (fig. S11D), making it 
difficult to isolate Fe2O3 later. After thresholding MoO3, conventional morphological operations 
including dilation, hole filling, and noise removal are applied to clean and refine the segmentation 
(fig. S11E). Next, the MoO3 phase was rendered black, and a new threshold was applied to the 
tomographic greyscale volume to highlight the Fe2O3 phase (fig. S11B). Finally, similar 
morphological steps were repeated on Fe2O3, and the boundaries of both phases are then overlaid 
on the original slice to clearly distinguish them (fig. S11C). In the post-heating dataset, the same 
general procedure is carried out to segment Fe2O3 and Fe2(MoO4)3.

S5.4 Diffusivity Estimation based on Precursor-to-Product Distances 
 
To estimate reactant transport distances during the reaction, we analyzed the registered pre- 
and post-reaction tomographic volumes. The post-reaction dataset was first translated into the pre-
reaction coordinate frame using the global registration procedure described in S5.3. In the pre-
reaction dataset, the segmented MoO3 and Fe2O3 phases were treated as precursor phases. In the 
post-reaction dataset, the segmented Fe2(MoO4)3 phase was treated as the product phase. 
Each segmented phase was first separated into individual particles using 3D connected-
component labeling. Particles smaller than 1000 voxels were excluded to remove isolated 
segmentation noise and very small fragments; this removed 5.28% of MoO3 voxels, 5.36% of 
Fe2O3 voxels, and 1.09% of Fe2(MoO4)3 voxels. The remaining individual particles were 
subdivided into fixed-size voxel clusters using a space-filling-curve ordering. Specifically, the 
voxels within each connected component were ordered along a locality-preserving curve, and 
consecutive blocks of voxels along this ordering were assigned to the same cluster until the 
prescribed cluster size was reached. This approach avoids repeated nearest-neighbor searches over 
millions of voxels, while still grouping voxels that are generally close in 3D space. Any leftover 
voxels that did not form a complete fixed-size cluster were excluded from the matching analysis. 
The cluster sizes were chosen from the reaction stoichiometry and molar densities of the 
three phases. According to the MaterialsProject (90), molar densities are 3.22×10-2 mol/cm³ 
(Fe2O3), 3.11×10-2 mol/cm³ (MoO3), and 5.58×10-3 mol/cm³ (Fe2(MoO4)3). For the reaction, Fe2O3 
+ 3 MoO3  Fe2(MoO4)3, the corresponding cluster sizes were set to 100 voxels for Fe2O3, 311 
voxels for MoO3, and 577 voxels for Fe2(MoO4)3. Thus, one Fe2O3 cluster and one MoO3 cluster 
represent the precursor material required to form one Fe2(MoO4)3 product cluster. 
We then identified reacted precursor-product associations using a tripartite matching 
procedure. For each Fe2(MoO4)3 product cluster, k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) searches were used to 
identify nearby MoO3 and Fe2O3 precursor clusters based on centroid-to-centroid Euclidean 
distance (91). The k-NN searches were performed with k = 50. Here, k denotes the number of 
nearest precursor-cluster centroids returned by each k-NN query for a given Fe2(MoO4)3 product-
cluster centroid. Thus, each product cluster was associated with up to 50 nearby MoO3 candidate 
clusters and 50 nearby Fe2O3 candidate clusters, yielding up to 2500 candidate MoO3-Fe2O3-
Fe2(MoO4)3 triplets. Each possible triplet was scored using its perimeter. Candidate triplets were 
ranked by this score and selected subject to a one-to-one constraint: each MoO3, Fe2O3, and 
Fe2(MoO4)3 cluster could be used at most once. fig. S14 shows a simplified 2D schematic of the 
3D selection algorithm. Clusters not selected in the final assignment were treated as residual 
material. This procedure yielded 44,770 non-repeating MoO3-Fe2O3-Fe2(MoO4)3 triplets (fig. S15).  
The precursor-product distance from each selected triplet was used to quantify the ion 
diffusion distance. The resulting distance distribution was converted to physical length using the 
20 nm voxel size. Rather than using the maximum distance, which can be strongly affected by rare 
extreme values, we used the 95th percentile of the precursor-product distance distribution as the 
characteristic diffusion distance. The 95th-percentile distances were 1576 nm for MoO3-

Fe2(MoO4)3 and 2206 nm for Fe2O3-Fe2(MoO4)3. The distributions are shown in Fig. 4E and Fig. 
S16.

distance of 2206 nm gives an estimated effective Fe3⁺ diffusivity of 2.0 × 10-10 cm2/s. 
S5.5 Complimentary Diffusivity Estimation based on Precursor Particle Size 
For validation, we estimate diffusivity using an independent second approach.  Individual 
precursor particles were identified using three-dimensional connected-component labeling with 
26-voxel connectivity. This method groups voxels belonging to the same contiguous particle. The 
physical volume of each particle was determined by counting the number of voxels in the 
connected component and multiplying by the voxel volume. To avoid bias from particles that are 
only partially captured within the analyzed region, particles intersecting the boundaries of the 
sampled subvolume were excluded from the analysis. 
The resulting particle size distribution is highly skewed, with a large number of small 
particles present but contributing only a small fraction of the total precursor volume (fig. S17). 
Since these small particles are expected to be rapidly consumed during the reaction, the arithmetic 
mean of the particle size does not accurately represent the precursor population controlling the 
reaction kinetics. Instead, a volume-weighted mean particle volume was used to represent the 
characteristic precursor particle size. The representative particle diameter was then obtained by 
converting the volume-weighted mean particle volume into an equivalent spherical diameter.  
Using this approach, the mean Fe2O3 precursor particle volume was estimated to be 273109 
voxels with a 95% confidence interval of +111377/−127248 voxels, corresponding to an equivalent 
spherical diameter of 1609.94 nm (+194.43/−218.92 nm). For MoO3, the mean particle volume 
was 126025 voxels with a 95% confidence interval of +41475/−43798 voxels, corresponding to 
an equivalent spherical diameter of 1244.08 nm (+123.76/−165.05 nm). 
The uncertainty of the volume-weighted mean particle volume was estimated using 
bootstrap resampling of the particle population. Specifically, particles were resampled with 
replacement to generate bootstrap datasets of equal size to the original dataset, and the volume-
weighted mean particle volume was recalculated for each bootstrap sample. A total of 5000 
bootstrap iterations were performed, and the 95% confidence interval was obtained from the 2.5th 
and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution.
