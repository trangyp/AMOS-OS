Abstract
The conventional strong CP problem arises from the apparent tension between the existence of
a CP-violating θ term in the conventional topological formulation of QCD and the experimental
constraint that the corresponding parameter satisfies θ ≲10−10. The standard formulation assumes
a globally classified topological structure of gauge-field configuration space, leading to topological
sectors and θ-vacua. Within this framework one investigates the resulting physical consequences,
including possible resolutions of the strong CP problem. A logically distinct and prior question
is whether known physical principles or observables require the additional assumptions leading to
such a global topological classification of gauge fields in the first place.
In this work, we address the latter question. We distinguish between those aspects of QCD that
follow directly from local gauge invariance and causal locality, and those that rely on additional
assumptions leading to a global topological classification of gauge- field configurations. The former
include the local topological charge density, the topological susceptibility, the anomalous Ward
identity, the ’t Hooft vertex, the Witten–Veneziano relation, the Leutwyler–Smilga relation, and
the corresponding nonperturbative results obtained in lattice QCD.
We are not aware of any established physical observable that requires the additional assumptions
leading to a global topological classification of gauge-field configurations. From this perspective,
the conventional strong CP problem is contingent upon the adoption of additional global structure
rather than being a necessary consequence of presently known QCD dynamics. This observation
does not question the mathematical consistency or phenomenological success of the conventional
framework, but instead clarifies the logical assumptions underlying its formulation.

INTRODUCTION
Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD) is the non-Abelian gauge theory of the strong in-
teraction and forms a central part of the Standard Model of particle physics. The most
general local gauge-invariant Lagrangian density consistent with Lorentz invariance allows
a CP-violating term,
Lθ = θ(g2/32π2)F a

(1)

θ is a real parameter. Within the conventional formulation, the parameter θ is, a priori,
unrestricted. Since this term violates CP, experimental limits on the neutron electric dipole
moment imply the stringent bound θ ≲10−10 [1, 2]. This apparent fine tuning constitutes
the conventional strong CP problem [2–5].
There are two logically distinct lines of inquiry concerning this problem. One line of
inquiry assumes a globally classified topological structure of gauge-field configuration space
and investigates the physical consequences that follow. Within this framework one is natu-
rally led to topological sectors, θ vacua, and the conventional strong CP problem, together
with proposed resolutions involving, for example, axions, discrete symmetries, parity, or
more general ultraviolet constructions. This program has generated a large and important
literature, including for example Refs. [6–19]. For a recent discussion emphasizing the for-
mulation of strong CP violation directly in terms of the partition function and free energy,
see Ref. [20]. A logically distinct and prior question is whether any established physical
observable requires the additional assumptions leading to a global topological classification
in the first place. The present work addresses this latter question.
The distinction is important because physical observables in quantum field theory are
defined through correlation functions of local gauge-invariant operators and are associated
with measurements performed within finite regions of spacetime. In particular, the local
topological charge density,
q(x) = (g2/32π2)F a

(2)
is a local gauge-invariant operator, and quantities derived from its correlation functions,
such as the topological susceptibility, are physical observables. Their existence does not, by

distinguish the assumptions common to both formulations from the additional assumptions
required for the conventional global topological construction. No claim is made that this
terminology is standard; it is merely useful for the present purpose.
For brevity, we shall refer collectively to assumptions that go beyond those of minimal
QCD, such as the smoothness, boundary, and global topological assumptions discussed later,
as the additional assumptions.
It should be emphasized that the present work does not argue that an independent CP-

invariance, or renormalizability. Such a term is certainly compatible with these symmetries.
Rather, the question addressed here is whether any established physical observable requires
introducing such an additional independent coupling. In other words, the issue is not whether
such a term is permitted by symmetry, but whether it is required by known physics.

The central question addressed in this paper is therefore whether the exact QCD func-

the global structure required for an integer-valued topological charge, or whether these con-
ditions instead constitute additional assumptions leading to the conventional topological
formulation.
The purpose of the present work is therefore to identify precisely where these additional
assumptions enter the conventional formulation of the theory and to distinguish those results
that follow directly from local gauge invariance and causal locality from those that depend
upon additional global topological structure. To make this distinction transparent, we first
establish a broad class of standard nonperturbative results before introducing the additional
assumptions leading to the conventional topological formulation.
We then examine the
logical role of these additional assumptions in the construction of topological sectors, θ
vacua, and the conventional strong CP problem.
Our purpose is not to advocate one formulation over another, nor to question the math-
ematical consistency or phenomenological success of the conventional framework. Rather,
it is to identify the logical assumptions required to arrive at each formulation and thereby
clarify the relationship between the conventional strong CP problem and the additional
global structure upon which its formulation rests.
It is important to emphasize that the present work is not directed against the extensive
literature devoted to the strong CP problem and its proposed resolutions. Those works
investigate the consequences of the conventional global topological formulation of QCD, or
propose mechanisms within that framework to account for the smallness of the physical
parameter ¯θ. The present work instead addresses the logically prior question of whether es-
tablished physical observables require the additional assumptions needed to construct that
framework in the first place. Consequently, the conclusions reached here are logically com-
patible with analyses of neutron electric dipole moments, axion and Peccei–Quinn models,
Nelson–Barr constructions, lattice studies of topology, or other investigations performed
within the conventional framework. The present analysis neither challenges nor diminishes
those results; rather, it concerns the assumptions required before that framework is adopted.

Semiclassical approximation and instantons
It is important to distinguish the exact quantum functional integral from the semiclassical
approximations derived from it. Macroscopic observables are operator-valued distributions
that are effectively smeared over finite spacetime regions whose dimensions are large com-
pared with microscopic correlation lengths. For example,
OR[ϕ] =
1
VR
R
R d4x ϕ2(x),
(34)
depends only on the coarse-grained behaviour of the field over the region R, where VR is its
spacetime volume. In Euclidean field theory, fluctuations in widely separated subregions of R
are approximately uncorrelated, so OR receives contributions from many nearly independent
degrees of freedom. Consequently, the probability that OR deviates significantly from its
typical value is expected to be exponentially suppressed with the spacetime volume,
P(OR) ∼exp[−VRI/ℏ],
I ≥0,
(35)
schematically illustrating the concentration of measure [23–25]. In the macroscopic limit, the
expectation value of OR is therefore exponentially close to the minimum of the appropriate
coarse-grained (Wilsonian) effective action. This explains why macroscopic observables are
accurately described by classical or semiclassical saddle points, even though the underlying
functional integral is supported on rough, distributional field configurations.
Instantons are smooth finite-action stationary points of the Euclidean Yang–Mills action
that arise naturally within this semiclassical description. Their construction requires the
finite-action condition, which implies that the field strength vanishes asymptotically and
that the gauge field approaches a pure gauge at spacetime infinity. These conditions define
the classical saddle-point problem and are sufficient to identify instanton solutions and
organize a semiclassical expansion about them.
The assumptions used to define the semiclassical saddle-point problem should not, how-
ever, be confused with assumptions concerning the exact functional integral. Semiclassical
approximations identify the dominant stationary points of a given functional integral, but
they do not determine the space of gauge-field configurations over which the exact functional
integral is defined. Thus, although the boundary and smoothness conditions used to con-
struct instantons are sufficient to obtain the conventional topological classification of these

classical solutions, they do not by themselves establish that the exact quantum functional
integral must be formulated over gauge fields admitting a global integer-valued topologi-
cal classification. Instantons therefore provide a powerful semiclassical organizing principle
without, by themselves, establishing that the associated global topological framework is
required to account for the established physical observables associated with QCD.
The same observation applies to the ’t Hooft effective interaction. Its derivation uses the
fermion zero modes of a smooth instanton background and captures the local consequences of
the axial anomaly within the semiclassical approximation. The resulting effective interaction
is expressed entirely in terms of local operators and correlation functions. Its derivation
therefore relies on the existence of the instanton saddle point rather than on any prior
assumption that the exact gauge-field measure is fundamentally decomposed into globally
classified integer-valued topological sectors. The same distinction also underlies the Witten–
Veneziano relation, which is discussed separately in Sec. II E.
Instantons therefore illustrate how rich semiclassical physics can emerge from the exact
quantum theory without, by themselves, determining the additional global assumptions used
to define that theory.

IV.
CONCLUSIONS
The conventional formulation of the strong CP problem is based on a globally classified
topological framework in which the space of gauge fields is assumed to decompose into
integer-valued topological sectors. This leads naturally to the introduction of a physical
vacuum angle θ, the θ vacuum, and the local CP-violating operator θq(x). Within this
framework the physical parameter ¯θ = θ + arg det M must be extraordinarily small in order
to account for the observed conservation of CP by the strong interaction.
The present work instead asks the logically prior question, namely whether the known lo-
cal consequences of the topological charge density require the additional assumptions needed
to construct this global topological framework. We introduced the concept of minimal QCD,
consisting of the standard QCD Lagrangian together with local gauge invariance, causal
locality, and the corresponding functional integral, without initially assuming a global clas-
sification of gauge fields into integer-valued topological sectors.
Within this framework we showed that the known local consequences of the topological
charge density are recovered. These include the topological susceptibility, the anomalous
Ward identity, the semiclassical role of instantons, the ’t Hooft effective interaction, the
Witten–Veneziano relation, the Leutwyler–Smilga relation, and their corresponding nonper-
turbative realization in lattice QCD. In each case, the derivation relies on local operator
relations, semiclassical saddle-point methods where appropriate, or the large-Nc expansion,
but does not require the exact functional integral to be fundamentally restricted to smooth
gauge fields admitting a global integer-valued topological classification.
We then examined the additional assumptions under which the conventional global topo-
logical formulation is obtained. Restricting the functional integral to sufficiently smooth
30


## Page 31


finite-action gauge fields with the appropriate global structure leads to an integer-valued
topological charge, a decomposition into topological sectors, the relative weighting eiθQ, the
equivalent local θ term in the action, and the conventional θ vacuum. Within this enlarged
framework, θ acquires the interpretation of a physical vacuum angle and the conventional
strong CP problem emerges.
The principal conclusion concerns logical dependence. The known local consequences of
the topological charge density follow from minimal QCD, whereas the conventional strong
CP problem arises only after supplementing minimal QCD with additional assumptions
concerning the smoothness and global topological classification of gauge fields. At present,
we are not aware of any known physical phenomenon or experimental observation that
requires these additional assumptions for the successful description of established QCD
phenomenology. This observation does not exclude the possibility that future theoretical
developments or experimental discoveries may ultimately identify such a requirement.
Nothing in the present analysis questions the mathematical consistency or phenomeno-
logical success of the conventional global topological formulation of QCD, nor the important
roles played by instantons, the axial anomaly, topological susceptibility, or the extensive lit-
erature on axions and axion-like particles. Both minimal QCD and the conventional global
formulation are mathematically consistent frameworks. The conventional formulation sup-
plements minimal QCD with additional assumptions concerning the global topological struc-
ture of gauge-field configuration space and has proved to be an extremely fruitful framework
for understanding nonperturbative QCD. The purpose of the present work has been to dis-
tinguish carefully between those results that follow directly from the local structure of QCD
and those that rely upon these additional global assumptions.
Accordingly, the present
conclusions are logically compatible with any subsequent investigation performed within the
conventional framework. This includes studies of neutron electric dipole moments, axion
solutions, Peccei–Quinn symmetry, Nelson–Barr models, lattice investigations of topological
sectors, confinement-based analyses, and related nonperturbative studies. Such works ex-
amine the consequences of adopting the additional assumptions identified here. The present
work addresses the logically prior question of what established physical observables require
those assumptions to be introduced.
Thus the conclusions of the present work are entirely consistent with the possibility that
future theoretical developments or experimental evidence may show that the additional

## Page 32


assumptions are indeed required. Should such a requirement emerge, the extensive body of
work developed within the conventional framework would immediately apply.
The principal conclusion of the present work is therefore not to argue that the conven-
tional global formulation of QCD is incorrect. Rather, it is that we are not aware of any
established physical phenomenon that requires adopting the additional assumptions lead-
ing to a global topological classification of gauge-field configurations or, equivalently, to an
independent flavor-singlet CP-violating mass term. The conventional strong CP problem
arises only after those additional assumptions have been adopted. This conclusion should
not be interpreted as asserting that an independent CP-violating coupling is inconsistent
with the local symmetries of QCD. Rather, the present analysis asks whether introducing
such an additional independent parameter is required by any presently established physical
observable.
The local axial anomaly establishes that any independent CP-violating flavor-singlet
phase in the quark mass matrix is physically equivalent to a θ term through the invariant
combination ¯θ = θ + arg det M. The present analysis does not question this equivalence.
Once an independent CP-violating parameter is introduced, whether as a vacuum angle, a
flavor-singlet pseudoscalar mass term, or an equivalent combination of the two, the conven-
tional strong CP fine-tuning problem immediately arises through the corresponding value
of ¯θ. The question addressed here is therefore whether any established physical observable
requires the introduction of such an independent CP-violating parameter beyond minimal
QCD in the first place.
