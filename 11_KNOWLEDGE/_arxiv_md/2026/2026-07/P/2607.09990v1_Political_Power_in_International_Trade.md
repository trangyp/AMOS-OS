Political Power in International Trade

Abstract
Economic power in international trade is the capacity of one country to impose loss on an-
other by withdrawing from a trading relationship. This paper measures it. A model of the short
run represents each trade restriction as a pattern of barred entries in the world matrix of input
shares and maps it into a vector of losses by country and sector. The asymmetry between the two
countries’ losses under the same severance is the measure of power: a gap in substitution, since
a buyer’s dependence turns on how easily it finds another source and a seller’s on how easily it
finds another market. When a relationship is barred, buyers lean on alternative suppliers and
barred suppliers on alternative buyers already present in the benchmark network, under the
ceiling that no producer exceeds its pre-shock scale. The reallocation is a RAS balancing of the
disrupted matrix that lets both sides adjust together. Across 9,480 counterfactual severances
on the 2022 world input–output network, mutual trade dependence is anything but mutual.
The average bilateral asymmetry is 0.6 on a scale that runs from balance at zero to complete
lopsidedness at one. The United States holds the favourable side in all its relationships, China
in all but one—the United States. The same tilt runs far down the hierarchy: a severance with
Russia would cost Belarus more than a tenth of its economic activity, but Russia only half of
one percent. The asymmetry bears only a weak relation to bilateral trade imbalance but closely
tracks whether a country sits at the core or the periphery. Power is a property of network
position, not of deficits.

Introduction
The classical case for international trade is that it lets a country consume beyond what it could
make alone, the gains mutual. But trade also creates dependence, which is not mutual in the
same degree. A country that can be starved of an input it cannot replace, or denied a market it
cannot do without, has placed a part of its fortunes in the hands of whoever sits at the other end
of the relationship. The question of this paper is not how much trade enriches but how unevenly
it empowers. We ask how the structure of who buys what from whom distributes, across the
world economy, the capacity of one nation to injure another by the bare act of withdrawal. This
power originates not in the size of a flow but in what goes wrong when it is cut—a cost not even
proportional to the severed link’s value—which is why a ledger of bilateral balances cannot capture
it. What a severance destroys is not the shipment at the border but the production it fed, wherever
in the world that production sits, and the production that depended on that, up and down the
chain: the cost is drawn by the global production network. A country that buys little directly from
a partner can be devastated when that partner is cut, if what it buys has no near substitute and
feeds much of what goes on downstream. A country that buys a great deal can be barely touched, if
the same goods can be had elsewhere. Power, in such a world, is a property of the whole network
and not of any isolated pair within it.
We build a model of the short run that computes what a severance finally costs. We begin
from the observation that any restriction is ultimately a pattern of attenuated entries in the world
input–output matrix that records the flow of goods across country-sectors. A directional sanction
bars one country from supplying another; a bilateral rupture severs the relationship both ways;
the two differ only in which entries they strike. No restriction is watertight—evasion and indirect
routing leak some trade through—so each barred link is down-weighted rather than deleted. What
remains is to specify how the network adjusts to the disruption. When a relationship is barred,
both ends rebalance across the relationships that remain in the benchmark support. A Japanese
refiner whose Gulf crude relationship is restricted leans more heavily on Russian and West African
barrels. A Gulf producer whose European outlet is restricted leans harder on its Asian ones. The
first is a buyer, the second a supplier, and a model of the disruption must follow both at once. A
country’s dependence as a buyer turns on how easily it can find another source. Its dependence as a
seller turns on how easily it can find another market for what it can no longer place, and redirect its
idle resources at home. Power is the gap between these two abilities to substitute.
Rebalancing the matrix on both sides can be done in exactly one way that disturbs the bench-
mark pattern as little as possible. It is an old device, the biproportional balancing of the matrix,
the RAS adjustment of input–output analysis, here put to a use it has not before served.1 The

The balancing carries this back and forth to its limit, rescaling the retained flows by row and by
column in turn, until every buyer’s purchases and every seller’s sales settle at their targets. The
iteration is a way to compute an outcome, not an account of how buyers and sellers haggle; we read
its limit only as an approximation to where their mutual adjustment, pressed by the same capacity
constraints, would come to rest. The short run fixes the ceilings the balancing must respect, for
ports, refineries, and plants cannot expand within the horizon of a sanction. A fraction τ of the
part displaced by the attenuation is removed from the row and column targets and is simply lost;
the remaining target mass is allocated by the balancing over the retained benchmark support.
The rerouting forces producers to take their inputs in new proportions. At the level of whole
sectors these inputs are complements, not substitutes—abundant steel does not make up for missing
semiconductors—so a bundle in the wrong proportions supports a less efficient intermediate
composite than one in benchmark proportions. We capture that loss with a constant elasticity
of substitution aggregator, though not in its usual role. We take the pre-shock equilibrium to
be optimal: at benchmark prices, which the model holds fixed, the benchmark bundle is the
cost-minimizing input mix of every producer, and the table identifies it. A sanction bars part
of that mix, and the balancing is each country-sector’s attempt to work back to the optimum as
nearly as the sanction and every other party’s simultaneous adjustment allow. The RAS solution
makes this reading exact: it is the reconstitution of trade closest to the benchmark pattern that
respects the barred links and both sides’ capped margins, and where those constraints leave room it
restores benchmark proportions exactly. The CES enters afterward, mapping the achieved bundle’s
remaining distance from the optimum into the efficiency of the intermediate composite it supports.
It does not re-do the reallocation, and its curvature is not a free penalty but the complementarity
of intermediate production—the efficiency loss from an input that cannot be replaced. A second
aggregator of the same family maps that efficiency into gross output: the intermediate composite
and the non-intermediate block, value added together with out-of-network sourcing, combine
through an outer CES nest that is itself strictly complementary; this finite-shock mapping is
maintained rather than structurally derived. The benchmark table fixes both the proportions we
expand around and the benchmark output elasticity assigned to intermediates, which the outer
standard device for updating an input–output table to new margins (Stone and Brown, 1962; Bacharach, 1970).
Alternation is what makes it work: one row pass already meets every row target, so progress comes only from switching
sides, and on positive flows the iteration converges to the unique balanced matrix (Sinkhorn, 1964).

close to seven, while the United States would lose under one percent to either.
So the ability to disrupt economic life through international trade restrictions is very uneven.
But where lies the lever of this power? If bilateral leverage were the trade deficit weaponized,
as public debate takes it to be, the asymmetry would line up with bilateral imbalance. It does
not. Across all pairs the correlation is only 0.2, far below its alignment with position, whose rank
correlation with a continuous core score computed from the benchmark network alone is 0.9. This
gap survives fat-tail- and dyadic-robust re-estimation. Its sharpest illustration is the most familiar.

the bilateral ledger, so two countries with balanced trade can sit in a relationship tilted nine to one.

1.1
Related literature
The paper stands where three literatures meet. The first is the political economy of trade de-
pendence, which runs from Hirschman (1945) through Keohane and Nye (1977) and Baldwin
(1985) to the weaponized interdependence of Farrell and Newman (2019), and which supplies the
concept this paper measures. Its sharpest recent expression is the geoeconomics theory of Clayton,
Maggiori and Schreger (2026). There a hegemon converts other economies’ cost of losing access
to it, exercised largely as threats it need never carry out, into influence over their actions. That
theory takes the cost of severance as the primitive a hegemon exploits. This paper computes the
primitive. In Hirschman’s own terms, severing the entries of the world input–output matrix is
the interruption of trade, and the rerouting on both sides is each party’s search for substitutes.
The asymmetry the model reports is the influence effect itself, measured from the network rather
than posited. And states appear to act on it: realized coercion launches disproportionately from
its favourable side. The second is the production network literature, from Hulten (1978) through
Acemoglu et al. (2012) to Baqaee and Farhi (2019), which supplies the propagation apparatus, and
also the departure. Its classical experiments adjust one side of the market only, a convention that
is innocuous for productivity accounting but fatal for power measurement, where which side is
freed is precisely what must not be assumed. The third is the quantitative sanctions literature,
which evaluates realized episodes, in dynamic general equilibrium (Ghironi, Kim and Ozhan, 2025)
and econometrically across the universe of measures collected in the Global Sanctions Data Base
(Felbermayr et al., 2020). We answer the complementary, ex ante question of what any severance
would cost, for all 9,480 pairs under one calibrated mechanism. We use the realized 2022 episode
to check the mechanism rather than to study the episode itself, and the check is in-sample: the
same evidence helps calibrate τ and δ, and post-invasion flows fill most of the benchmark year.4

2.1.2
Trade restrictions
A trade restriction, in this model, is a change in the weights of the matrix of input shares A. It
forbids a set of sourcing relationships and drives the corresponding entries of A toward zero.
Everything the model then does with it follows from that single operation on A: the rerouting, the
input bundle mismatch, and the equilibrium that results.
The restrictions studied in this paper are country to country. A directional restriction, written
c′ ̸→c, bars every sector of country c′ from supplying any sector of country c: it designates the link
from seller i to buyer j as barred whenever C(i) = c′ and C(j) = c. We use “severed” as shorthand for
this policy designation; Assumption 1 attenuates, rather than deletes, the corresponding reference
entry. The model is in this sense a soft-restriction model: a barred link represents residual access
after imperfect enforcement, evasion, and indirect routing, not a hard legal ceiling on direct trade,
and the balancing below can allocate mass back onto an attenuated link (Remark 2). Because the
representation with buyers in columns is agnostic about which side of a transaction a policy names,
the same barred set is read equally as a sales ban on c′ or a purchase ban on c. The description from
the seller’s side is the description from the buyer’s side flipped. A bilateral restriction, written c′ ↮c,
is the two directional restrictions c′ ̸→c and c ̸→c′ imposed together. Let Ωcollect the admissible
restrictions: the directional and bilateral restrictions over the country pairs of interest, together
with their sector-level refinements. A refinement designates as barred, for an ordered country
pair, only the links originating in a named list of seller sectors, as in the sanctions programme of
Section 5.1.

2.2
Rerouting on both sides and the matrix of intended orders
When a relationship is barred and attenuated, both ends adjust: the buyer leans more heavily on
alternative suppliers and the barred supplier on alternative outlets. Freeing only one side means a
demand-oriented column rescaling or a supply-oriented row rescaling, the proportional-adjustment
analogues of the Leontief and Ghosh conventions (Dietzenbacher, 1997). Either choice makes which
side bears the loss an artefact of the accounting, precisely what a measurement of power must not
presuppose. We instead cap both sides, no buyer beyond its budget and no supplier beyond its
benchmark sales, and balance the disrupted matrix of transactions to the capped flow margins. The
transactions are the flows Zij = aijwj, the input shares scaled by each buyer’s benchmark input
spend wj = (1 −βj)sj. The result is the classical RAS (biproportional) adjustment: the unique
biproportional matrix on the retained benchmark support meeting both margins, equivalently the
relative-entropy projection of the disrupted reference onto the prescribed-margin set. Its columns
are then divided by wj to return the coefficient matrix Aω. The detailed within-sector supplier
composition generally changes, though the balancing preserves support and the prescribed buyer
and seller totals. Not all of the mass displaced by the attenuation is retained. The rerouting
friction τ ∈[0,1] is the fraction of that displaced mass removed from the buyer and seller targets,
symmetrically on both sides. The remaining share 1−τ is restored to those targets and allocated by
the biproportional balancing over the retained benchmark support. Because barred links remain in
that support with lower reference weights, this restoration is not constrained to unbarred partners.

3.5
Trade is recomposed, not just reduced
A severance does not scale the flow matrix down uniformly; it recomposes it. Because the model
lets both sides substitute, many unbarred links carry more trade after the cut than before. A buyer
facing an attenuated barred relationship redistributes intended orders over its retained sources,
while a barred seller redistributes its target sales over retained buyers. Many unbarred third-
country links therefore absorb more trade. Under the USA–China severance, for one, 31 percent of
all benchmark links end up above their pre-shock flow, the largest at 1.5 times (Canadian crude
oil pulled into China as its American-linked sourcing is cut). The pattern is general, not special
to the giants. Figure 4 pools the link flows across a random sample of forty bilateral severances.

Power in Trade Dependence
Section 3 constructs the Hirschman matrix from counterfactual bilateral losses. The object of
the present section is different. We do not construct a second measure of power, nor do we try
to compress the Hirschman matrix into a single country ranking. Instead, we ask how far the
asymmetries in the Hirschman matrix are already visible in the benchmark trade network.
The distinction is useful. The Hirschman matrix is a counterfactual object: it is built from the
losses countries suffer when bilateral trade relationships are severed. The matrix A, by contrast,
is the benchmark structure of trade. It records who buys from whom before any restriction is
imposed. This section therefore compares the bilateral asymmetries in H with structures computed
directly from the benchmark network: bilateral flow imbalance, bilateral trade intensity, centrality,
core–periphery position, and community membership. The purpose is diagnostic. If the Hirschman
asymmetries are almost entirely explained by direct trade flows, then the counterfactual model is
mainly recovering what is already visible in bilateral exposure. If they are not, then the model is
adding information from higher order propagation, sectoral position, input bundle mismatch, and
capacity constraints.

5
Validation
This section provides two empirical checks, each aimed at a different object. The first concerns
the reallocation operator, the RAS balancing of both sides that is the paper’s central device. It asks
whether the operator produces a pattern consistent with the redirection observed after the 2022
sanctions on Russia, and whether it does so more closely than the model’s one-sided proportional-
rescaling benchmarks. This is not an independent out-of-sample validation: evidence from the
same episode helps discipline τ and the attenuation scale δ, and the 2022 annual input–output table
contains post-invasion flows from most of that year. It is therefore a calibrated contemporaneous
reconstruction and a within-design comparison of allocation rules. The second asks whether the
power measure, the Hirschman matrix, carries descriptive behavioural content: whether states
that practise economic coercion tend to do so from positions the matrix calls favourable. Neither
exercise estimates the causal effect of any sanction.
5.1
The 2022 severance of Russia: a calibrated redirection check
The 2022 sanctions provide a large, sudden, and well-documented episode in which both margins
emphasized by the rerouting machinery were active at once. Russia searched for alternative
buyers for its displaced energy, the coalition for alternative suppliers of what it no longer bought,
each capped in the short run by the capacities of retained benchmark partners. We map the
measures onto the model as sector-partial severance legs between Russia and the thirty-seven
sanctioning economies in the table (the EU-27, the United Kingdom, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland,
the United States, Canada, Japan, Korea, Australia, and New Zealand). On the import side sit
Russian sales of coal, crude oil and gas, refined petroleum, iron and steel, and wood to coalition
buyers: the embargoes of 2022–2023, together with the de facto gas severance. On the export
side sit coalition sales of chemicals, electronics, electrical equipment, machinery, and transport
equipment to Russian buyers: the export-control packages. This gives seventy-four legs in all,
solved at the baseline calibration as one counterfactual equilibrium. The programme sits inside the
model without modification. The construction of Section 2 depends on a restriction only through
the entries of A it designates as barred (Section 2.1), and each leg designates a set of them: the
links from one country’s rows, in the named sectors, to the other country’s buyer columns. The
restriction is the union of the seventy-four barred sets, an element of Ωon the same footing as the
country-to-country severances, and the targets, the balancing, and the equilibrium are built from it
exactly as in Sections 2.2–2.4.16

The second test confronts the Hirschman matrix with the revealed behaviour of states. If H
measures leverage, then the practice of economic coercion, which is costly to the coercing country
as well as the target country, should originate disproportionately from the matrix’s favourable
side. A coercion dyad is one directed instance of a country pressuring another, so a coalition
sanctioning Russia enters the count as one dyad per coalition member. We assemble the directed
coercion dyads in force or imposed between 2010 and 2023 whose two ends both lie inside the
eighty-economy table. They comprise the sanctions on Russia, Belarus, and Myanmar with their full
coalitions, drawn from the official EU, OFAC, and UK programme lists. Next come Russia’s 2014
counter-sanctions, classified as retaliation, together with the United States’ measures against China
and T¨urkiye and China’s tariff retaliation. Last come the six documented episodes of informal
Chinese coercion: Japan 2010, Norway 2010, the Philippines 2012, Korea 2017, Australia 2020, and
Lithuania 2021 (for the practice generally, see Hufbauer et al., 2007). The full list of 154 directed
dyads, each with its source class, appears in Appendix F. The design is deliberately transparent:
every episode is public record, and the matrix entry Hts (t the target country, s the coercing country)
against which each is scored was computed in Section 4.7 with no reference to any of them.

Concluding Remarks
Eighty years ago, Hirschman (1945) argued that the gains from trade arrive with a shadow. Every
trading relationship distributes, alongside its mutual benefits, an unequal capacity to harm, and
the side that can better do without the relationship holds power over the side that cannot. The
observation has never been controversial. What it has lacked is a measurement. Dependence in a
modern economy is not a bilateral fact but a network fact. What a severance destroys is not the
shipment at the border but the production the shipment was an input to, wherever that production
sits. And so the influence effect of trade has remained, for most of a century, a concept in search of
a number.
This paper supplies the number. The instrument is a model of the short run. In it, every trade
restriction is the same kind of object, a pattern of barred entries in the world input-share matrix,
and both ends of every barred relationship adjust at once. Buyers and sellers reallocate intended
trade over their retained benchmark relationships, with barred links downweighted rather than
mechanically capped, and no producer exceeds its scale before the shock. Put simply, the model
lets every country-sector work its way back toward the trading pattern it preferred before the
shock, as far as the restriction and everyone else’s simultaneous adjustment allow. That reallocation
on both sides, capped by capacity, is the classical RAS balancing of the disrupted matrix, an eighty-
year-old device here put to a new use. Costly rerouting governs what reappears. A CES aggregator
registers the distortion of what arrives, and a second, strictly complementary CES nest against the
non-intermediate block passes that bundle-efficiency loss into output, with a pass-through that
steepens as bundles degrade. A fulfillment fixed point then propagates it through the network.
From each of 9,480 counterfactual severances on the 2022 world input–output table, the fixed point
selected from the pre-restriction benchmark returns a pair of losses, and from their asymmetries a
measurement of power.
Three findings deserve to be restated plainly. First, the world’s mutual dependence is profoundly
lopsided. The average bilateral asymmetry is 0.59. In the typical pair, one side loses close to four
times as large a share of its own activity as the other side loses of its. The United States holds the
favourable side of every one of its relationships. The worst exposures in the system face losses
twenty to eighty times smaller on the other side of the same relationship. They are over a tenth of
Belarusian activity against a severance with Russia, and a tenth of Vietnamese and near a tenth of
Congolese activity against one with China. Second, that severance has two sides is quantitatively
real, not a modelling nicety. A seller barred from a market bears a loss of the same order as the
buyer that bars it, a cost a demand-only model would set to zero. China forgoes about a third of one
percent of its activity when shut out of the American market, close to what the United States loses.
And a bilateral rupture is, to a close approximation, the sum of its two directional parts. Third,
and centrally, this power is not the bilateral statistic around which public argument is organized.
59


## Page 60


Its correlation with bilateral trade imbalance is only 0.19, a fraction of its 0.86 rank correlation
with core–periphery position in the benchmark network. Dependence tracks network position, not
trade deficit.18
It is worth being explicit about what these findings do not say. The losses are short-run quantity
losses, computed with prices held at benchmark. The terms of trade losses that real sanctions inflict,
such as Russian crude re-placed at a discount, and the relief that price movements eventually
provide are both outside it. The loss vector owes its nonnegative sign to the short-run capacity
ceiling, a discipline we have imposed as a modelling choice. Over horizons long enough for
capacity, relationships, and technologies to adjust, the asymmetries measured here would shrink,
and nothing in the exercise speaks to whether severance is ever worth its price to the severing side.
The model also treats only executed severance, not the threat of it, and so is silent on deterrence.
Within those limits the policy lesson is sharp, and it is a single one. Leverage lives in network
position, not in the bilateral ledger. For the sanction-designer, the relevant magnitude is not
how much the target country buys from you but how badly it re-sources and re-sells through the
network that survives. That is why the model finds American leverage over economies with which
America runs deficits, and why within-bloc hierarchy is as steep as between-bloc rivalry. The blocs
are not shelters but fractal structures of dependence. For the small open economy, the policy of
safety is not bilateral balance but position, since balance is uncorrelated with power. Safety is
multiple sources, multiple markets, and distance from single-hub dependence, the very exposure
the Hirschman matrix measures implicitly in every row. As for the measurement of economic
coercion, our exercise suggests that the objects worth monitoring are not trade balances. They
are the network statistics that carry nearly all of the counterfactual power: coreness and sourcing
centrality. Bilateral flows reveal little.

The balancing of both sides: programme, algorithm, and convergence
This appendix collects the computational and formal content of the rerouting on both sides of
Section 2.2. It states the block-wise programme the matrix of intended orders Aω solves, the

A word on provenance, since the device has been discovered repeatedly across fields. It
originates in statistics with Deming and Stephan (1940), who fitted a contingency table to known
marginals by alternately rescaling its rows and columns (later called “raking”). In matrix analysis,
Sinkhorn (1964) and Sinkhorn and Knopp (1967) proved that a positive matrix can be scaled to
prescribed margins uniquely and that the alternating iteration converges. In economics it entered
through Richard Stone’s Cambridge Growth Project (Stone and Brown, 1962), where it became the
RAS method for updating input–output tables and received its definitive treatment in Bacharach
(1970). The same fixed point reappears as the static Schr¨odinger bridge in probability and, since
Cuturi (2013), as the computational core of entropic optimal transport.

Calibrating the rerouting friction τ from disruption episodes
The model carries two free parameters beyond the benchmark input–output table: the CES
complementarity ρ, read from the short-run elasticity literature, and the rerouting friction τ ∈[0,1]
of Section 2.2. In the model, τ is the share of the mass displaced by barred-link attenuation that is
removed from the row and column targets. This target loss is not observed directly. We calibrate
it by the closest episode-based proxy, one minus the share of displaced trade that reappears with
unbarred partners after residual barred trade and demand destruction are separated. We read this
effective friction from the largest such episodes of the past decade, the 2022 sanctioning of Russia
and the standing sanctions on Iran, and find a sharp commodity split. For fungible, homogeneous
goods displaced trade reappears almost in full, so τ is small, of order 0.05–0.10 for crude oil.
For specific, relationship-bound inputs it reappears only in part, so τ is large, of order 0.5–0.8.
The same episodes inform the barred-link attenuation parameter δ of Assumption 1, but neither
parameter is structurally identified from a single post-restriction bilateral flow.

Netting out evasion: the δ channel
Sanctions episodes deliver the re-matching variation, but they also generate channel (ii) on a
large scale, and (79) is biased if it is not removed. Chupilkin, Javorcik and Plekhanov (2023)

to Russia mirrored by a surge in EU exports to Armenia, Kazakhstan, and the Kyrgyz Republic.
The drop is about 80 percent steeper for sanctioned goods, and re-exports of sanctioned goods
to those three countries rise an extra 30 percent. They estimate that over 2022–2023 the two
evasion strategies together offset roughly one third of the decline in European exports to Russia
caused by product-specific sanctions. That one third is residual barred trade rather than observable
switching to unbarred partners. It informs the attenuation scale and must be separated from the
displaced-flow proxy used to calibrate τ.
The Iranian oil trade is the same channel in its purest form. Under standing U.S. sanctions,
roughly 90 percent of Iran’s crude and condensate exports reach a single destination, China,
through ship-to-ship transfers and re-labeling as Malaysian-origin crude. China’s recorded imports

volume Malaysia does not produce (Congressional Research Service, 2025). The barred relationship
persists almost intact through evasion, so the Iranian crude episode points to weak attenuation (a
comparatively high δ) rather than a high rerouting friction. It informs δ but does not identify it
structurally. The lesson for (79) is that the displaced base (1 −bδ)X0, not the raw severed flow X0,
is the denominator. For fungible commodities, where bδ is large, this correction is of first-order
importance.

Specific inputs: a high τ
At the other pole, inputs that are differentiated and supplier-specific reappear only in part within
the sanction horizon. When Russia’s invasion halted Ukrainian production of semiconductor-grade
neon, there was no near substitute to switch to. Ukraine supplied roughly 70 percent of the world’s

old Soviet-era steel mills with gas-capture equipment. Russia’s roughly 40 percent share of the
palladium market posed the same problem for catalytic and electronic uses (Foreign Policy, 2022).

Sensitivity to the rerouting friction and the CES parameter
Figure 9 traces the bilateral vulnerabilities of the headline pairs across the full calibrated range
of the rerouting friction, τ ∈[0.05,0.80], and the admissible range of the CES parameter, ρ ∈
[−4,−0.25]. The rerouting friction does first-order work, as it should, it is the price of severance.
The American loss from a China rupture rises about sixfold as τ rises from 0.10 to 0.60 (0.17
to 1.01 percent of activity), and every headline pair climbs near-linearly across the range. Lost
rerouting is lost activity one-for-one before the network multiplies it, so the slope of each curve
is, to first order, the pair’s severed mass scaled by its multiplier. The ordering of the curves is
preserved throughout, no headline asymmetry reverses anywhere on the calibrated range, so the
power readings of Section 4.7 are not artefacts of the baseline τ.
The CES parameter, by contrast, moves these aggregate pairs only mildly over [−4,−0.25]. The
reason is in the calibration, not the model. At δ = 0.10 and τ = 0.30 the intended retention of every
affected buyer-sector stays above 1−τ(1−δ) = 0.73, so realized bundles remain close to proportional
and the composition penalty has little room to bite. As the shock deepens, larger τ(1 −δ), binding
capacity, losses drift toward their Leontief readings and the ρ-dependence steepens. Remark 5
gives the theory of this drift and reports a probe far outside the maintained interval (down to
ρ = −32), where convergence is unaffected and the United States’ loss against China rises from 0.53
percent at ρ = −8 to 0.67 at ρ = −32.
The outer curvature moves the headline pairs less still. Across ϱ ∈[−4,−0.25] the American loss
under the China severance runs from 0.499 to 0.502 percent, because at mild bundle degradations
the shadow share ej(κ) of (15) sits essentially at the benchmark cost share and the outer aggregator
agrees with its constant-share anchor to first order. Where ϱ earns its place is the deep-dependence
tail. Probing the Belarus–Russia severance across ϱ ∈{−0.25,−1,−3,−9} raises the Belarusian loss
from 11.2 to 11.5, 12.3, and 15.0 percent of activity, a thirty percent amplification, relative to
the baseline reading, at an outer elasticity of one tenth, with convergence untouched (terminal
ratios near 0.60 throughout). The pass-through steepens exactly where bundles degrade most, the
signature of the shadow share climbing toward one. The full-system counterpart is the pair of
ϱ ∈{−9,−3} rows of the sweep below.

Fat-tail- and dyadic-robust re-estimation of the alignments
The alignment statistics of Table 6 correlate a bounded outcome, the Hirschman entry Hcc′ ∈[−1,1],
with benchmark network gaps, and two features of the data recommend caution with a levels
Pearson coefficient. The regressors are heavy-tailed. A Hill index on the top decile returns tail
exponents below two for the raw bilateral imbalance (α ≈1.0), trade intensity (α ≈1.0), the
eigenvector-centrality gap (α ≈0.7), and the coreness gap (α ≈1.5), so their variances are effectively
unbounded and a levels covariance is dominated by a handful of extreme dyads. The outcomes
H and T , being bounded, are thin-tailed. And the pairs are not independent, each of the C = 80
countries appearing in 79 of them, so ordinary standard errors understate sampling uncertainty.
We re-estimate every alignment robust to both, on the same 3,160 pairs of E. Table 13 reports, for
each gap, the Hill tail index, the Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall coefficients, a standardized Huber
slope, and a two-way dyadic cluster-robust 95 percent confidence interval (the Aronow–Samii–
Assenova / Fafchamps–Gubert estimator, cross-checked against a delete-one-country jackknife).

Is the hierarchy heavy-tailed?
Section 4.7 describes trade power as a steep hierarchy rather than a field of equals. We ask here
whether that hierarchy is heavy-tailed in the formal sense, close to Zipf’s law. The paper’s own
advantage score is bounded in [−1,1] and cannot carry a power-law tail, so we put the question
to three positive, unbounded proxies for a country’s power. The first is its coreness, the network-
position measure that tracks H at a rank correlation of 0.86. The second is the total benchmark
108


## Page 109


activity it can destroy across all of its bilateral severances, a dollar measure of its power to harm.
The third, for reference, is its economic size, the classic Zipf object. We fit each tail by the maximum-
likelihood method of Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (2009), which estimates the power-law exponent
α together with the cutoff xmin above which the tail begins, and we test the estimate against the
Zipf value α = 2. Table 14 reports the fits and Figure 10 the rank-size and survival plots.
The distributions are heavy-tailed and Zipf-like. Coreness has α ≈2.3, within 1.3 standard
errors of the Zipf value, and economic size α ≈1.9, the textbook Zipf reading that serves as the
sanity check. The dollar power to harm is somewhat steeper at α ≈2.9, spread across a few more
countries than economic size is, so less top-heavy than GDP rather than more. Two facts keep us
from claiming a strict power law. With only N = 80 countries the fitted tails are short, and for none
of the three country measures can a power law be told apart from a lognormal, every likelihood-
ratio test a tie. Only the 6,320 dyadic loss shares Gkl carry enough data to discriminate, and there a
lognormal fits somewhat better than a pure power law, though the tail stays unmistakably heavy at
α ≈2.6. We therefore read the result as heavy-tailed and hierarchical, Zipf-like for economic size
and network position and steeper for the dollar power to harm, rather than as a strict power law.

The power statements across the calibration
The sensitivity above traces individual vulnerabilities across the calibrated parameter ranges. This
subsection asks the question that matters for the paper’s conclusions. Do the power statements
survive away from the baseline calibration? The power statements are the Hirschman matrix, the
asymmetry index, the ranking, and the structure-alignment findings of Sections 4 and 4.7. To
answer it we re-solve the entire bilateral severance experiment at every point of a grid spanning the
calibrated ranges, τ ∈{0.10,0.30,0.60} crossed with ρ ∈{−4,−1,−0.25} at ϱ = −1, δ = 0.10, together
with the outer curvature ϱ ∈{−9,−3} and the attenuation δ ∈{0.05,1/3}, each at the baseline of
the remaining parameters, thirteen grid points in all. Each requires all 3,160 bilateral severance
equilibria among the eighty economies of the table, 41,080 counterfactual equilibria in all.
Table 15 reports, for each grid point, the asymmetry index Ψ and the orientation-symmetrized
correlations of the Hirschman entries with direct trade imbalance and with the core-score gap.
It also reports the Spearman correlation of the trade-weighted power ranking with its baseline
counterpart, the share of country pairs whose asymmetry retains its baseline sign, and the top of
the ranking. Four facts summarize it. First, the ordering of power is, for practical purposes, not a
function of the calibration. The rank correlation with the baseline ranking stays above 0.999 at
every point of the grid. The sign of the bilateral asymmetry is preserved for 99.5 percent or more
of all 3,160 pairs, the few flips concentrated in near-balanced small-economy dyads, and the top

CES parameter is, for the power objects, numerically irrelevant. Within each τ block the three ρ

of the flat ρ-profiles of Appendix F. Third, the level of asymmetry is flat across the box. Ψ sits at
