Abstract 
Time-differential perturbed angular correlation (TDPAC or PAC) analysis is an ill-conditioned inverse 
problem in which site count, interaction type, correlated hyperfine parameters, damping, and 
initialization choices can lead to competing numerical solutions. This software paper presents PAC 
Studio ML, a human-in-the-loop Python desktop environment for physics-informed inverse analysis of 
PAC spectra. The software combines a Hamiltonian-based forward PAC model, user-defined synthetic 
training libraries, feature extraction, one-, two-, and three-site machine-learning predictors, direct 
parameter prediction, Auto sites model-family screening, ML-seeded nonlinear least-squares 
refinement, visualization, benchmarking, diagnostics, model-card reporting, and export tools in a single 
graphical workflow. The purpose of the ML component is not to replace conventional PAC fitting or 
expert interpretation, but to support the pre-fitting stage by accelerating parameter exploration, 
suggesting plausible initialization regions, comparing site-count hypotheses, and making repeated 
analyses more reproducible. Held-out synthetic tests provide proof of operation and show that global 
amplitude and baseline are generally more recoverable than site-resolved frequencies, angular 
variables, fractions, and damping terms, highlighting the non-identifiability of difficult PAC inverse 
problems. Selected BiFeO3 examples illustrate conventional, direct-ML, ML-seeded, and Auto sites 
workflows, but they are used as software demonstrations rather than as a complete experimental 
validation corpus. PAC Studio ML is therefore positioned as a supporting tool for expert PAC analysis: it 
can improve workflow speed, initialization, and diagnostic transparency, while final model choice, 
physical constraints, and materials interpretation remain the responsibility of the researcher. 
Comprehensive material-specific conclusions are reserved for a later physics-results paper after 
retraining, uncertainty calibration, repeated-start evaluation, and independent experimental benchmark 
assembly.

1. Introduction 
Time-differential perturbed angular correlation (TDPAC or PAC) spectroscopy is a local nuclear probe 
technique for studying the electric and magnetic environment of radioactive probe nuclei in solids and 
other condensed-matter systems. Through the perturbation of a gamma-gamma angular correlation 
during the lifetime of an intermediate nuclear state, PAC provides access to hyperfine quantities such as 
electric-field gradients, magnetic hyperfine fields, dynamic relaxation, site fractions, and local disorder. 
These features make PAC particularly valuable for investigating defects, phase transitions, magnetism, 
diffusion, and local structural environments that may be difficult to resolve by spatially averaged probes 
[Frauenfelder 1965; Butz 1989; Schatz 1996]. 
The physical basis of PAC analysis is well established. The measured ratio spectrum R(t) contains 
oscillatory and damping features determined by the perturbation factor G(t), which in turn depends on 
the hyperfine Hamiltonian acting on the nuclear spin. Pure electric quadrupole, pure magnetic dipole, 
and combined electric-magnetic interactions can all contribute, and the corresponding spectra depend 
on quadrupole frequency, asymmetry parameter, magnetic frequency, relative field orientation, 
damping, and amplitude terms [Abragam 1953; Butz 1989; Dang 2022]. For combined hyperfine

interactions, the Hamiltonian-matrix formalism provides a route to calculating the perturbation function 
for cases where analytic expressions are not sufficient [Hamiltonian-matrix formalism 1970]. 
Despite this strong theoretical foundation, extracting physical parameters from an experimental PAC 
spectrum remains a difficult inverse problem. A measured R(t) curve is a compressed projection of many 
possible microscopic configurations, and different combinations of site fractions, interaction types, 
angular parameters, damping distributions, baselines, and amplitudes can produce similar spectral 
shapes. The difficulty increases for noisy data, strongly damped spectra, overlapping frequencies, and 
multi-site samples, where a visually acceptable fit may not be unique. As a result, PAC fitting is often 
sensitive to the selected site model, parameter bounds, and initial guesses. 
Conventional nonlinear least-squares fitting remains the central quantitative tool for PAC analysis 
because it tests a physically specified model directly against the measured spectrum. However, in 
practice, the user may need to explore many starting values, interaction regimes, and site-count 
hypotheses before a stable and physically meaningful solution is obtained. This manual search is 
scientifically important, but it can be time-consuming and difficult to reproduce, especially when several 
local minima produce similar residuals. These challenges motivate computational tools that can support 
the pre-fitting stage without removing expert control from the interpretation. 
Machine learning offers one such route because synthetic PAC spectra can be generated from known 
forward-model parameters and then used to train predictors for the inverse direction. In this setting, the 
trained model does not discover the PAC physics independently; rather, it learns a numerical 
approximation to the mapping from simulated spectral features to the parameters used to generate 
them. This idea is consistent with supervised learning theory, where models are trained on labeled 
examples and evaluated on held-out data [Bishop 2006; Hastie 2009]. It also differs from approaches 
that apply machine learning only after conventional parameter extraction, such as post-fit automation 
of hyperfine-parameter analysis [da Silva 2026]. 
PAC Studio ML was developed for this pre-fitting and model-screening role. A physics-based forward 
solver generates synthetic one-, two-, and three-site spectra over user-defined parameter ranges; 
feature extraction converts R(t) and Fourier-domain information into numerical inputs; and trained 
regressors propose candidate PAC parameters. The resulting predictions may be inspected directly, used 
to initialize least-squares fitting, or compared across saved site-count databanks by the Auto sites 
assistant. Additional diagnostics, benchmark outputs, ablation options, model-card metadata, and 
export functions are included so that the analysis remains auditable rather than becoming an opaque 
black-box prediction. 
A central design principle of the software is human-in-the-loop analysis. ML can accelerate exploration, 
suggest plausible starting regions, expose alternative local minima, and make repeated comparisons 
more systematic, but it cannot determine by itself which site model, parameter range, interaction type, 
or physical interpretation is meaningful for a particular material. Those decisions require knowledge of

sample preparation, probe behavior, crystal structure, magnetic order, temperature history, and prior 
literature. This principle is consistent with broader human-AI interaction guidance, which emphasizes 
that automated systems should support human decisions while communicating uncertainty, limitations,

The present manuscript therefore has a deliberately defined scope. It reports PAC Studio ML as a

specific conclusions are not claimed here. A later physics-results paper will provide the curated 
experimental benchmark set needed to quantify speed, convergence, stability, site-count selection, 
uncertainty calibration, and parameter accuracy across independently validated PAC cases. 
2. Software Design and Implementation 
2.1 Software Architecture 
PAC Studio ML is organized as a modular Python application rather than as a single monolithic script. 
The graphical user interface sits above several coordinated software layers: core PAC computation, 
machine-learning modules, evaluation and documentation tools, and a portable project/release 
structure. This modular design makes the application easier to maintain, test, document, and extend, 
following common recommendations for sustainable scientific software development [Wilson 2014; 
Jimenez 2017]. 
The core PAC computation layer contains the forward PAC engine, nonlinear least-squares fitting, FFT 
diagnostics, and residual analysis. The machine-learning layer contains synthetic dataset generation, 
feature extraction, model training, parameter prediction, Auto sites analysis, and uncertainty 
calibration. A separate evaluation and documentation layer contains benchmarking, ablation analysis, 
model-card generation, system checks, and self-tests. The GUI coordinates these layers through 
background workers so that long calculations, fits, training jobs, predictions, Auto sites scans, 
benchmarks, and diagnostics remain interruptible by the user. 
The implementation uses the scientific Python stack, including NumPy, pandas, Matplotlib, SciPy, scikit-
learn, SymPy, Pillow, OpenPyXL, and Tkinter, with PyInstaller support for Windows executable builds. 
Source and executable distributions use the same portable Machine Learning PAC directory structure for 
trained models, datasets, example spectra, model cards, versioned artifacts, and installation 
documentation. Figure 1 summarizes this modular architecture.

2.2 Forward PAC Model 
The forward PAC model is the physics engine used to generate synthetic training spectra, calculate trial 
spectra in the GUI, and evaluate least-squares fits. For each crystallographic or hyperfine site, the user-
defined physical parameters specify the electric quadrupole interaction, magnetic interaction, 
orientation angles, damping behavior, and site fraction. These quantities define a combined electric and 
magnetic hyperfine Hamiltonian for the selected nuclear spin; the combined-interaction Hamiltonian 
matrix follows the formalism of Ref. [Hamiltonian-matrix formalism 1970] and standard PAC and 
hyperfine-interaction theory [Frauenfelder 1965; Butz 1989; Schatz 1996]. The same combined-
interaction framework is also consistent with the author's previous synthetic PAC simulations for 
combined hyperfine interactions [Dang 2022]. 
The code diagonalizes this Hamiltonian to obtain energy splittings and transition frequencies. Angular-
momentum amplitude terms are constructed using Wigner 3j coefficients, which provide the coupling 
factors required for the perturbation function [Edmonds 1957]. The result is a site-specific perturbation 
function, G22,i(t), describing the time evolution expected for that site.


## Page 7

The site perturbation functions are then modified by the selected damping model, such as Gaussian or 
Lorentzian broadening, and combined according to the site fractions. For a multi-site spectrum, the total 
perturbation function is the weighted sum of the one-, two-, or three-site contributions. The observable 
PAC signal is represented as R(t) = add_const + A22 G22(t), where add_const accounts for a vertical 
offset and A22 scales the effective anisotropy. FFT diagnostics are computed from the resulting R(t) after 
baseline removal, Hann windowing, and zero padding, following standard signal-processing practice 
[Oppenheim 2010]. Figure 2 summarizes the forward-model pipeline. 
 
Figure 2. Forward PAC model used for synthetic spectrum generation, fitting, and ML training. Physical site parameters 
define a combined electric and magnetic hyperfine Hamiltonian, which is diagonalized to obtain transition frequencies and 
angular-momentum amplitudes. Site perturbation functions are damped and combined according to site fractions to form 
G22(t), and the observable signal is calculated as R(t) = add_const + A22 G22(t). FFT diagnostics are computed after baseline 
removal, Hann windowing, and zero padding. 
2.3 Synthetic Dataset Design 
The ML models are trained from synthetic PAC databanks generated with the forward model described 
above. In the current PAC Studio workflow, these databanks are not treated as fixed, universal training 
domains. When the user presses Train ML, the GUI first asks for the number of spectra and then opens a 
training-range dialog in which the user defines the physical validity domain of the new models. Separate 
site-count families are generated for one-, two-, and three-site spectra so that the models can learn 
both single-site and multi-site behavior. Each stored site-count dataset is divided into an 80/10/10 split: 
80% of the spectra are used for training, 10% for validation during model selection and calibration, and 
10% as a held-out test set for estimating performance after model choices have been fixed. This 
separation follows standard supervised-learning practice, because the data used to tune or select a 
model should not also be used as the final unbiased test of predictive performance [Bishop 2006; Hastie 
2009].


## Page 8


8 
 
For each spectrum, the generator samples physical hyperfine parameters and global signal parameters 
within the ranges selected by the user. The default settings provide a practical starting domain, including 
tmax = 1000.0 ns, tstep = 1.0 ns, plot_time = 200.0 ns, noise standard deviation from 0.0 to 0.04, A22 
from -1.0 to 1.0, baseline from -0.2 to 0.2, vQ from 20.0 to 180.0 MHz, vM from 0.0 to 40.0 MHz, eta 
from 0.0 to 1.0, gamma from 0.0 to 360.0 degrees, beta from 0.0 to 180.0 degrees, lambdaj from 0.0 to 
0.03, delta from 0.0 to 0.08, and thetaR from 0.0 to 2.0. These values are defaults rather than universal 
physical limits. They should be adjusted according to the isotope, sample class, temperature regime, 
prior literature, and expected hyperfine interaction range. Predictions outside the selected training 
domain must be interpreted as extrapolations and should be retrained or independently validated. 
The user-defined training settings are written to a training_range_settings.json file and are also 
embedded in the generated model-card metadata. This makes the training domain explicit and 
reproducible: a later prediction can be interpreted together with the vQ, vM, damping, angular, 
baseline, noise, and time-grid assumptions used to train the corresponding models. In this way, PAC 
Studio treats ML training as a controlled scientific configuration step rather than as a black-box 
operation. 
The generator explicitly includes pure electric, pure magnetic, and combined interaction regimes. Pure 
electric spectra set vM, beta, and gamma to zero; pure magnetic spectra set vQ, eta, beta, and gamma 
to zero; and combined spectra allow both electric and magnetic contributions. For multi-site spectra, 
site fractions are sampled from a Dirichlet distribution and then sorted for label stability, so the same 
physical spectrum is represented consistently across the dataset [Kotz 2000]. These regime labels are 
retained so that prediction, Auto sites, and library filtering can apply physical constraints rather than 
treating all parameter combinations as equally valid. Figure 3 summarizes the synthetic dataset design, 
while Figure 4 shows how these synthetic databanks enter the broader physics-informed inverse-
prediction workflow.

2.4 Feature Extraction 
Feature extraction is the translation step between a PAC spectrum and the machine-learning model. The 
measured or simulated R(t) curve is not treated as a picture. Instead, it is converted into a fixed set of 
numerical descriptors. In machine-learning notation, these descriptors form the input feature vector x 
for one spectrum. The trained model then learns a mapping from x to the target PAC quantities, such as 
site count, vQ, eta, vM, damping parameters, site fractions, and uncertainty estimates. This follows the 
standard supervised-learning framework in which models are trained from paired inputs and target 
outputs [Bishop 2006; Hastie 2009; Pedregosa 2011].


## Page 10


10 
 
The extracted descriptors are grouped into four complementary families. First, time-domain descriptors 
summarize the R(t) curve directly in the time plot. They include quantities such as amplitude, integrated 
area, signal moments, zero crossings, slopes, and damping proxies. These values describe how strongly 
the spectrum oscillates, how rapidly it decays, and whether its shape is smooth, noisy, weakly 
modulated, or strongly damped. 
Second, frequency-domain descriptors are obtained from the Fourier spectrum. They describe the 
dominant oscillation frequencies, FFT peak amplitudes, centroid, bandwidth, and the ratio of high- to 
low-frequency power. These features are important because electric quadrupole and magnetic 
hyperfine interactions leave characteristic frequency signatures in PAC spectra. The use of Fourier-
domain information follows standard signal-processing practice for analyzing oscillatory signals [Cooley 
1965; Oppenheim 2010]. 
Third, shape descriptors preserve more of the full spectral profile. The program resamples and 
normalizes the time-domain R(t) curve and the FFT spectrum to fixed lengths, so spectra with different 
channel numbers can be compared by the same trained model. In the current implementation, this 
includes 160 normalized time-domain samples and up to 96 normalized FFT samples. 
Fourth, physics-informed descriptors add PAC-specific information that is not captured well by generic 
signal statistics alone. These include dominant and secondary angular-frequency proxies, peak 
separations, peak ratios, magnetic-interaction indicators, and damping indicators. These descriptors 
help the model distinguish, for example, pure electric from combined electric-magnetic interactions, or 
simple one-site spectra from spectra that may require multiple sites. 
Therefore, the feature vector x should be understood as a compact numerical fingerprint of the PAC 
spectrum. It combines general signal-shape information with PAC-informed physical clues. The ML 
model does not replace the physical model; it uses this fingerprint to propose plausible parameter 
regions that can then be checked, constrained, refined, or rejected by the user.

2.5 Machine-Learning Models 
After feature extraction, each PAC spectrum is represented by a numerical feature vector x. The role of 
the machine-learning model is to estimate the target PAC parameters y from this vector. In this 
manuscript, y may denote a single parameter such as vQ, eta, vM, delta, site fraction, or an interaction 
label, and the complete prediction is obtained by combining the outputs for all required targets. This 
follows the standard supervised-learning framework in which a model is trained from examples with 
known input features and known target values [Bishop 2006; Hastie 2009]. 
The training script therefore builds one predictor for each target quantity. When scikit-learn is available, 
the software can choose among tree-based regression models, including RandomForestRegressor, 
ExtraTreesRegressor, and HistGradientBoostingRegressor. These algorithms are well suited for tabular 
feature vectors because they can learn nonlinear relationships between spectral descriptors and PAC 
parameters without requiring a simple linear formula [Breiman 2001; Geurts 2006; Friedman 2001; 
Pedregosa 2011]. 
A random forest combines many decision trees and averages their predictions, which usually improves 
stability compared with a single tree. Extra Trees uses a similar ensemble idea but introduces stronger 
randomization during tree construction. Gradient boosting builds trees sequentially, with later trees 
correcting the remaining errors of earlier trees. In practical terms, all three methods are different ways


## Page 12


12 
 
of learning the mapping from spectral features to PAC targets, and the automatic choice allows the 
training script to select the model that performs best for a given target. 
The software also contains a NumPy-based k-nearest-neighbor fallback for environments where scikit-
learn is not available. In this case, prediction is made by finding synthetic spectra whose feature vectors 
are closest to the query spectrum and estimating the parameters from those neighbors. This nearest-
neighbor idea is a classical nonparametric learning method [Cover 1967]. 
Angular parameters require special treatment because angles wrap around. For example, 359 degrees 
and 1 degree are physically close, even though their ordinary numerical difference is large. To avoid this 
artificial discontinuity, angular targets are internally represented by sine and cosine components during 
prediction and are reconstructed as angles afterward. This is the same idea used in circular statistics for 
directional data [Mardia 2000]. 
In addition to the fitted regressors, the saved prediction bundle contains the feature list, target list, 
uncertainty thresholds, prediction specifications, and a compact synthetic reference library for each 
supported site count. This bundle is what the graphical interface loads during prediction. It allows the 
GUI to apply the same feature definitions and scaling rules that were used during training. 
Prediction first attempts a reference-library inverse solver. The experimental spectrum is converted into 
the same feature space as the training spectra, and the features are standardized using the training 
mean and standard deviation. The query vector is then compared with stored synthetic feature vectors. 
The closest physics-consistent neighbors are weighted by inverse squared distance, so very similar 
synthetic spectra contribute more strongly than less similar spectra. Scalar parameters are estimated by 
weighted averaging, angular parameters by circular means, and interaction classes by weighted voting. If 
the user supplies a physical prior, such as a known site count or a known interaction type, the library is 
filtered before prediction. 
Finally, the GUI applies three post-prediction safeguards. First, dominant-FFT-peak correction compares 
the main experimental and predicted frequency peaks and rescales positive vQ and vM values within a 
conservative range. Second, robust A22 and add_const calibration adjusts the predicted curve amplitude 
and vertical offset using median and percentile information. Third, residual-based reranking re-simulates 
candidate spectra and ranks them by their RMS mismatch to the experimental R(t) curve. These 
safeguards help ensure that the final displayed prediction is not only close in feature space, but also 
physically plausible and close in the time-domain spectrum. 
The resulting workflow is therefore human-in-the-loop. The ML model proposes a parameter region 
from learned synthetic examples, the reference library keeps the prediction tied to simulated PAC 
physics, and the GUI checks the result with frequency, amplitude, offset, and residual diagnostics. The 
user remains responsible for applying sample knowledge, literature constraints, and physical judgment 
before accepting the model or using it as the starting point for nonlinear least-squares refinement.

2.6 Auto Site-Count Assistant and ML Error Reporting 
The Auto sites command is designed to assist model selection when the number of PAC sites is not 
known with certainty. Starting from the selected experimental R(t) window, the software extracts the 
same numerical features used for ML prediction and compares the spectrum with the available synthetic 
databanks for one-, two-, and three-site models. In this sense, Auto sites is a candidate-generation and 
ranking tool: it searches for models that can plausibly reproduce the experimental spectrum, but it does 
not by itself prove the physical site structure. 
Two scan levels are provided. Quick mode evaluates a restricted set of feature-nearest candidates and is 
intended for interactive exploration. Full mode re-simulates all available candidates in the loaded 
databanks and is intended for final screening when the user can wait longer. For each site-count family, 
the software reports the direct time-domain RMS residual, the feature-space distance, the candidate 
index, and the number of candidates examined. It also reports a BIC-style score, which penalizes 
unnecessary parameter complexity so that a multi-site model must improve the fit enough to justify its 
additional degrees of freedom [Schwarz 1978]. 
The recommendation is not based only on the smallest residual. The assistant also reports weighted 
interaction votes for each proposed site, qualitative confidence, site separation information, and explicit 
ambiguity warnings. This is important because damped PAC spectra can often be described by more 
than one mathematically plausible model family, such as one combined interaction, two pure-electric


## Page 14


14 
 
interactions, or mixed electric and combined interactions. Therefore, the recommendation is shown 
before any model change and is applied only after user confirmation. 
The same section of the GUI also reports ML-derived parameter uncertainties. Existing trained bundles 
can provide raw dispersion estimates through columns such as std_A22, std_add_const, std_vQ1, 
std_eta1, and std_lambdaj1. These raw std_* values should be interpreted as indicators of model 
agreement or prediction spread, not as formal least-squares covariance errors. They are useful for 
comparing which predictions are stable or uncertain, but they should not yet be treated as fully 
calibrated experimental error bars. 
For this reason, the revised training pipeline supports empirical uncertainty calibration. After retraining 
and validation on cases with known target parameters, raw_std_* values can be compared with actual 
prediction errors. The pipeline can then retain the original raw_std_* values while also reporting 
calibrated_std_* values, approximate 95% prediction intervals, and reliability labels. This follows the 
general idea that probabilistic predictions should be checked and calibrated against observed errors 
before their uncertainty intervals are interpreted quantitatively [Gneiting 2007; Kuleshov 2018]. 
The practical interpretation is that Auto sites and ML error reporting should be used together. Auto sites 
proposes and ranks possible site-count and interaction models, while uncertainty reporting indicates 
how stable the predicted parameters are. The final decision remains human-in-the-loop: the researcher 
must apply literature knowledge, sample history, physical constraints, and least-squares refinement 
before accepting a model as a scientific interpretation.

2.7 Application-Level Functionality 
PAC Studio ML is implemented as a complete desktop analysis environment rather than only a 
command-line prediction script. The graphical interface connects the full PAC workflow: data loading, 
parameter editing, forward calculation, nonlinear fitting, visualization, ML-assisted prediction, 
diagnostics, and export. This application-level integration is important because PAC analysis is iterative: 
the user often alternates between inspecting the spectrum, changing physical assumptions, fitting 
selected parameters, checking residuals, and comparing conventional and ML-assisted results. 
The data-input layer accepts experimental R(t) data from Excel, CSV, DAT, and TXT files. It detects 
common time and signal columns, preserves optional error columns, and includes a compression 
command for reducing raw data before analysis. Users can select channel ranges and plotting windows, 
while t step and t max can be updated from the loaded data and selected interval. These operations are 
supported by the standard scientific Python data stack used for array and table handling [Harris 2020; 
McKinney 2010]. 
The workspace and computation layers allow the user to define the number of sites, edit global and site-
specific parameters, set parameter uncertainties, and choose which parameters are free or fixed during 
fitting. Forward PAC spectra can be calculated in single-core or multi-core mode, and long operations 
such as calculation, fitting, ML training, ML prediction, Auto sites, benchmarking, and ablation tests are 
executed through background workers that can be interrupted from the toolbar. 
The visualization layer provides live R(t), FFT, and residual previews. Users can normalize the FFT, 
include or exclude the DC component, apply log scaling or smoothing, and switch between diagnostic, 
smoothed-line, and publication-style filled FFT views. Individual site contributions can also be displayed 
for both R(t) and FFT spectra, while calculated and experimental traces can be hidden independently. 
The plotting and export behavior follows the role commonly served by Matplotlib in scientific Python 
applications [Hunter 2007]. 
The non-ML analysis layer remains central. Users can calculate forward spectra, perform nonlinear least-
squares fitting on selected experimental channels, inspect fitted values and uncertainties, view the 
reduced chi-square, and examine the correlation matrix. The Fit Report also derives PAC-relevant 
quantities such as omega_Q, omega_0, omega_L, Vzz, and Bhf when the nuclear quadrupole moment 
and magnetic moment are supplied, with propagated uncertainties where available. The nonlinear 
optimization workflow is consistent with standard numerical least-squares practice as implemented in 
scientific computing libraries such as SciPy [Virtanen 2020]. 
The machine-learning layer adds Train ML, Predict ML, Auto sites, Benchmark, Ablation, and Model card 
functions to the same user environment. These tools do not replace conventional fitting; instead, they

and documentation of model limits. Project persistence and export functions complete the workflow by 
saving parameter files, workspace files, selected data tables, fit reports, correlation matrices, and high-

2.8 Reproducibility and Evaluation Infrastructure 
PAC Studio ML includes an evaluation layer so that ML-assisted PAC analysis can be tested under 
reproducible conditions rather than judged only from individual examples. The key principle is that 
different starting strategies, correction options, and uncertainty estimates should be compared under 
the same experimental channels, parameter bounds, weighting scheme, free-parameter set, optimizer 
tolerances, and evaluation budget. This follows the broader expectation in machine-learning research 
that reported improvements should be tied to transparent and repeatable evaluation protocols [Pineau 
2021]. 
The first evaluation path is the baseline-initialization benchmark. It compares nonlinear least-squares 
fits initialized from randomized starts, literature or manually selected starts, neutral default starts, and 
ML-seeded starts. Because all starts are tested under the same model and fitting conditions, the 
exported results can be used to evaluate whether ML improves practical fitting behavior. Trial-level 
outputs include convergence status, runtime, number of function evaluations, residual statistics,


## Page 17


17 
 
reduced chi-square, fitted parameters, and repeated-start dispersion. For synthetic recovery spectra, 
the benchmark also reports parameter error relative to the known generating values. 
The second evaluation path is the ablation benchmark. This test asks which parts of the prediction 
workflow actually improve the result and whether any correction introduces bias. Three components 
are switched on and off independently: FFT frequency correction, robust A22/add_const calibration, and 
residual-based synthetic-library reranking. The software evaluates all eight on/off combinations and 
exports configuration-level trials, paired component effects, summary tables, and failure cases. Paired 
comparisons are important because they compare different settings on the same spectra, reducing the 
risk that apparent improvements are caused by differences in the test cases rather than by the 
component itself [Demsar 2006]. 
The third evaluation path is empirical uncertainty calibration. Raw ML dispersion values, reported as 
raw_std_* or std_* quantities, are first compared with validation residuals for each parameter. The 
calibration step estimates parameter-specific scale and floor terms so that the displayed uncertainty 
better reflects observed prediction errors. Untouched test spectra are then used to assess whether the 
resulting intervals achieve the intended 68% and 95% coverage. This is consistent with the general 
requirement that probabilistic predictions should be calibrated and checked against observed outcomes 
before being interpreted quantitatively [Gneiting 2007; Kuleshov 2018]. 
Until the one-, two-, and three-site models are retrained with finalized calibration maps, the manuscript 
treats displayed raw_std_* values as qualitative model-agreement indicators rather than formal 
confidence intervals. Figure 9 summarizes the three evaluation paths. Together, these tools allow PAC 
Studio ML to report not only whether a prediction looks good, but also whether it improves speed, 
convergence, stability, component robustness, and uncertainty reliability under controlled testing 
conditions.

2.9 Model Cards, Diagnostics, and Deployment 
The final software layer makes the limits of each trained model visible to the user. Each prediction 
bundle contains the fitted model or models, scalers, feature pipeline, and training metadata. Around 
this bundle, PAC Studio ML stores a model-card record that documents the feature definitions, target 
definitions, training parameter ranges, supported site counts, interaction regimes, time-grid 
assumptions, validation metrics, uncertainty-calibration status, and known failure modes. This follows 
the broader model-card idea that trained ML systems should be distributed with structured 
documentation of their intended use, performance, and limitations [Mitchell 2019]. 
The GUI uses the model-card metadata as an active diagnostic rather than only as static documentation. 
When a user analyzes a spectrum, the current configuration can be compared with the documented 
model scope. The software can then warn when a requested site count, interaction type, time grid, 
parameter range, or uncertainty interpretation lies outside the documented training regime. Model 
cards and parameter ranges can also be exported as JSON and CSV files, making the model assumptions 
inspectable outside the GUI. 
Some older model artifacts may not contain complete metadata because they were produced before 
the model-card system was finalized. In those cases, PAC Studio ML reconstructs partial cards where 
possible but visibly marks them as unverified metadata. This distinction is important: a reconstructed 
card can help users understand an old model, but it should not be treated as equivalent to a fully 
versioned and validated model card. The same documentation principle is related to dataset 
documentation practices, in which data origin, scope, limitations, and intended use are made explicit for 
downstream users [Gebru 2021]. 
The in-application System check supports deployment by reporting package versions, resource 
availability, write access, model bundles, and model cards. A cancellable self-test exercises the forward 
PAC engine, feature extraction, metadata loading, and optionally one real model prediction. These 
checks help users verify that the installed environment is functional before using ML-assisted analysis. 
They are deployment diagnostics, however, not a substitute for the automated unit, integration, and 
GUI tests planned for the public release. 
For the software-paper release, the distribution should archive the exact source revision, dependency 
specification, lightweight deterministic fixtures, example spectra, model cards, selected versioned 
model artifacts, and a public installation guide. This archive defines what is being claimed in the 
software article: a reproducible, documented, human-in-the-loop PAC analysis environment. Full 
retraining and broad experimental validation remain outside the claims of the present software paper

Table 2 reports two standard supervised-learning error metrics. The mean absolute error (MAE) is the 
average absolute difference between the ML-predicted value and the known synthetic value. It is easy 
to interpret because it has the same unit as the target parameter, for example MHz for vQ and vM. The 
root mean square error (RMSE) is the square root of the average squared error; it also has the same unit 
as the target but penalizes large mistakes more strongly than MAE. Therefore, when RMSE is much 
larger than MAE, the target likely contains occasional large prediction errors or difficult outlier cases. In 
the table, each entry is written as MAE/RMSE on the held-out synthetic test set. Table 2 is intentionally 
limited to selected representative targets in order to keep the table readable in the main text. 
Parameters such as eta, beta, gamma, lambdaj, delta, and thetaR are not absent from the evaluation; 
they are included in the full target-wise error summary shown in Figure 11. They are better compared 
graphically because they have different numerical scales and, for angular variables, periodic or 
orientation-dependent interpretation. The MAE and RMSE values reported here correspond to the 
specific synthetic training domain and model version used in this study; retraining with different 
parameter ranges, sample numbers, random seeds, or time-grid assumptions will change the numerical 
error values. 
The numerical values in Table 2 support the recoverability hierarchy described above. A22 and 
add_const have small errors because they control the overall signal scale and offset. The leading 
magnetic frequency vM1 is recovered with moderate accuracy, making it useful as an ML-generated 
starting value. The quadrupole frequency vQ1 has larger errors, showing that direct ML prediction alone 
should not be treated as a final fitted parameter. Instead, these synthetic results justify the intended 
workflow: use ML to provide plausible initialization and diagnostic guidance, then combine it with least-
squares fitting, physical constraints, and expert judgment.

3.2 Integrated Software Workflow 
PAC Studio ML is organized as an integrated analysis workflow rather than as a standalone prediction 
button. Figure 13 summarizes how the user supplies the experimental R(t) spectrum, selected channel 
window, site-count hypothesis, interaction constraints, and physical context, while the software 
coordinates forward PAC simulation, direct ML prediction, Auto sites databank scanning, least-squares 
fitting, benchmark diagnostics, model-card checks, and export. This organization follows the general 
principle of human-AI interaction that automated suggestions should remain inspectable, controllable, 
and situated within the user's scientific task [Amershi 2019]. 
 
Figure 13. Integrated PAC Studio ML workflow from experimental data loading to human-in-the-loop model validation and 
export. The workflow links user inputs, software actions, and scientific outputs. Direct ML prediction uses the selected site-
count model, Auto sites scans the saved one-, two-, and/or three-site databanks, and final interpretation remains controlled 
by physical constraints and expert judgment. 
The workflow has three practical layers. First, the user defines the experimental and physical context: 
the loaded R(t) data, channel range, site count, interaction family, and optional ML correction settings. 
Second, the software transforms this context into computational actions. Predict ML gives a direct 
parameter estimate for the selected site count; Auto sites loads the saved site-count databanks and 
compares candidate one-, two-, and three-site descriptions using residual and BIC-style scores; Fit R(t) 
then performs nonlinear least-squares refinement from either manual or ML-generated starting values.


## Page 25


25 
 
Third, the output layer reports parameters, fit quality, residuals, FFT comparison, site-count scores, 
warnings, and exportable figures or tables. 
This design makes the ML layer useful even when it is not accurate enough to replace fitting. ML can 
accelerate exploration, suggest plausible starting regions, expose alternative site-count hypotheses, and 
reduce manual searching in parameter space. However, the researcher remains responsible for judging 
whether the proposed site model, constraints, parameter ranges, and interpretation are physically 
meaningful for the material under investigation. 
3.3 Illustrative Experimental Applications 
Two experimental BFO examples are retained to demonstrate how the software supports real analysis 
decisions. They are intentionally presented as illustrative applications, not as a statistically complete 
validation corpus or as definitive materials-physics conclusions. Their purpose is to show the relationship 
among conventional fitting, direct ML prediction, ML-seeded optimization, Auto sites screening, and 
user-supplied physical constraints. 
3.3.1 111mCd Probe in BFO Nanoparticles 
A BFO nanoparticle spectrum measured with the 111mCd probe after TM = 100 C and TA = 500 C 
treatment illustrates a difficult initialization regime. The conventional/manual fit converged in 303 
function evaluations and 1.570 s, whereas the ML-seeded fit converged in 285 evaluations and 1.451 s 
and reached a lower reduced chi-square. Across 50 random starts, only 76% converged; the median 
random-start effort was 1590 evaluations and 17.60 s, and the median reduced chi-square was 
substantially poorer. In this example, ML seeding therefore identified a favorable numerical basin more 
efficiently than random initialization and modestly improved on the manual start. 
The comparison remains methodological. Synthetic recovery based on known generating parameters 
showed that several hyperfine quantities were still weakly identifiable, so improved convergence and 
residual quality cannot be interpreted as proof of more accurate physical parameters. Figures 14-16 
show the conventional, direct-ML, and ML-seeded stages of the software workflow.

3.4 Current Validation Scope and Planned Physics Paper 
The present article should be read as a software and workflow paper. The synthetic proof-of-operation 
and the two BFO demonstrations show that PAC Studio ML can execute the intended analysis chain: 
generation or loading of model families, feature extraction, direct ML prediction, Auto sites databank 
comparison, ML-seeded least-squares fitting, residual and FFT visualization, benchmark export, model-
card inspection, and human-in-the-loop interpretation. These results are sufficient to document the 
implemented capability of the software and to define a reproducible analysis protocol. 
The same evidence is not sufficient to claim general physical accuracy. The examples do not establish 
universal Auto sites accuracy, calibrated uncertainty coverage for experimental spectra, unbiased 
recovery of all hyperfine parameters, or superiority of ML initialization for all PAC datasets. They also do 
not remove the usual ambiguities of PAC analysis, especially when damped spectra can be represented 
by different combinations of site fractions, electric interactions, magnetic interactions, and damping 
parameters. Therefore, low residuals and visually plausible overlays are treated here as candidate-
model evidence rather than as final materials-physics proof. 
A full physics-results paper should use a curated benchmark corpus with independently justified ground 
truth or strong external constraints. That corpus should include one-, two-, and three-site cases; 
different probes and sample classes; a range of temperatures and annealing conditions; and spectra 
with known difficult cases such as strong damping, overlapping frequencies, weak fractions, and 
competing interaction assignments. For each spectrum, the benchmark should report 
conventional/manual fitting, direct ML prediction, ML-seeded least-squares fitting, repeated-start 
convergence, runtime, number of function evaluations, residual RMS, reduced chi-square, parameter 
uncertainties, Auto sites scores, and documented failure modes. 
The planned benchmark should also evaluate the supporting diagnostics introduced in the software. This 
includes calibration of ML-derived error estimates against empirical prediction errors, confidence 
calibration for Auto sites, the effect of Quick versus Full databank scanning, sensitivity to the selected 
training ranges, and ablation of FFT frequency correction, robust A22/baseline calibration, and residual-
based library reranking. These tests are necessary before numerical improvements in speed, 
convergence, or residual quality can be translated into claims about improved physical parameter 
accuracy. 
Several software upgrades are planned beyond the validation corpus. First, the current Hamiltonian 
module will be extended to include formulations appropriate for single-crystalline materials and explicit 
contributions from an external magnetic field. These upgrades mainly affect the forward Hamiltonian 
calculation; the surrounding PAC Studio ML workflow--synthetic data generation, feature extraction, 
model training, prediction, Auto sites comparison, fitting, diagnostics, and export--can remain largely 
unchanged. Second, future trained model families will extend beyond the present one-, two-, and three-
site limit, allowing more complex spectra to be treated within the same site-count selection framework.


## Page 31


31 
 
Third, larger synthetic databanks, exceeding the present 50,000 spectra per site-count family, will be 
generated with high-performance computing resources so that broader parameter ranges and more 
difficult interaction regimes can be sampled. Finally, the fitting interface will be expanded toward 
simultaneous analysis of multiple temperature-dependent R(t) spectra, enabling shared constraints and 
temperature-dependent trends to be fitted consistently across a series rather than spectrum by 
spectrum. 
Separating the software paper from the later physics-results paper therefore protects both 
contributions. The software paper documents what the platform does, how users operate it, what 
outputs are produced, and where the current claim boundary lies. The physics paper can then focus on 
material-specific PAC conclusions using a larger and independently justified experimental corpus. This 
separation also supports the human-in-the-loop philosophy of PAC Studio ML: machine-learning tools 
can accelerate exploration and reveal candidate hypotheses, but the final site model and physical 
interpretation must remain constrained by sample knowledge, literature, and expert judgment. 
4. Discussion 
PAC Studio ML demonstrates a practical route for coupling a PAC Hamiltonian solver with data-driven 
inverse prediction while preserving the conventional fitting workflow. The main contribution is therefore 
not an autonomous replacement for expert PAC analysis, but an integrated human-in-the-loop 
environment in which forward simulation, synthetic training libraries, ML-based initialization, site-count 
screening, least-squares refinement, benchmark diagnostics, and export tools are brought into a single 
reproducible workflow. This integration is important because PAC analysis is usually iterative: the 
researcher tests physically plausible site models, adjusts constraints, compares residuals and Fourier 
spectra, and interprets the fitted parameters in light of sample preparation, probe chemistry, 
temperature history, and prior literature. 
The inverse PAC problem is intrinsically difficult because different hyperfine-interaction models can 
generate visually similar time spectra, especially when the signal is strongly damped, noisy, or composed 
of several unresolved fractions. A one-site combined interaction, two overlapping pure-electric 
interactions, or mixed electric and combined interactions may all reproduce parts of the same 
experimental R(t) curve within a limited time window. In addition, angular parameters, damping terms, 
fractions, and site-resolved frequencies can be correlated, so a low residual does not automatically 
guarantee a unique or physically correct interpretation. These ambiguities explain why direct ML 
predictions and Auto sites results should be treated as candidate hypotheses rather than final answers. 
Within this boundary, ML is valuable because it can reduce manual trial-and-error and make the search 
process more systematic. A trained model can provide plausible starting regions for nonlinear fitting, 
expose alternative local minima, and rapidly compare one-, two-, and three-site model families through 
saved databanks. The least-squares stage then tests whether the ML-suggested parameters can be 
refined against the measured spectrum. In difficult initialization regimes, this workflow is expected to


## Page 32


32 
 
improve speed, convergence, and stability, particularly when compared with purely random starts or 
manual starting values chosen without systematic screening. 
The software also makes the analysis more auditable. Benchmark tools record direct ML predictions, 
ML-seeded least-squares results, repeated-start behavior, runtime, residual metrics, and comparison 
against baseline initialization strategies. Ablation options allow the user to examine how FFT frequency 
correction, robust A22/baseline calibration, and residual-based library reranking influence the final 
prediction. Model-card diagnostics document supported site counts, training ranges, interaction 
regimes, time-grid assumptions, and known failure modes. These elements are essential for publication 
use because they make it possible to distinguish a successful numerical fit from a robust physical 
interpretation. 
The BFO case studies included in this software paper illustrate both the promise and the necessary 
caution. They show that ML-assisted predictions can generate reasonable starting models and 
sometimes good agreement between calculated and experimental spectra. At the same time, cases 
where Auto sites predictions differ from literature-informed user models demonstrate the central role 
of expert judgment. The researcher must decide whether a site-count proposal is physically meaningful, 
whether a parameter range is compatible with the material system, and whether an apparently good 
residual is consistent with independent structural, magnetic, or temperature-dependent evidence. 
Several limitations remain. The most important are synthetic-to-experimental domain shift, incomplete 
empirical calibration of ML-derived uncertainty estimates, provisional metadata for legacy model 
bundles, and the need for broader automated testing and clean-machine deployment records. These 
limitations are typical for scientific ML workflows trained on synthetic data and applied to experimental 
spectra. PAC Studio ML addresses them by exposing diagnostics, warnings, user-controlled correction 
options, and benchmark outputs rather than hiding uncertainty behind a single automated result. 
For this reason, the present manuscript is best interpreted as a software paper. It documents the 
implemented platform, explains the computational workflow, defines the current validation boundary, 
and provides illustrative BFO examples. A later physics-results paper should use a curated experimental 
benchmark set to determine quantitatively whether the workflow improves speed, convergence, 
stability, site-count selection, and parameter accuracy across well-defined materials and probe nuclei. 
This separation keeps the software contribution clear while leaving final material-specific conclusions to 
a dedicated experimental validation study. 
5. Conclusions 
PAC Studio ML provides an integrated research-software environment for forward PAC simulation, 
physics-informed inverse prediction, Auto sites screening, ML-seeded least-squares refinement, 
uncertainty-aware reporting, benchmark diagnostics, visualization, and export. By connecting these


## Page 33


33 
 
functions in a single graphical workflow, the software reduces the practical gap between synthetic PAC 
modelling, machine-learning assisted initialization, and conventional expert fitting. 
The results presented here should be interpreted as software proof-of-operation and workflow 
validation rather than final materials-physics conclusions. Held-out synthetic tests and selected BFO 
examples demonstrate how the platform reports prediction errors, visualizes parameter recoverability, 
compares site-count hypotheses, and exposes non-identifiability in difficult spectra. They also show why 
low residuals or direct ML predictions alone are not sufficient evidence for a unique physical model. 
The central conclusion is therefore deliberately balanced: ML can accelerate exploration, improve 
initialization, support convergence, and make PAC analysis more reproducible, but it remains a 
supporting technique. Final interpretation requires the user's knowledge of the sample, probe nucleus, 
interaction regime, physically meaningful parameter ranges, site model, and literature context. A later 
physics-results paper will extend this software contribution with curated experimental benchmarks, 
retrained model families, calibrated uncertainty estimates, and independent validation of BiFeO3 case 
studies.
