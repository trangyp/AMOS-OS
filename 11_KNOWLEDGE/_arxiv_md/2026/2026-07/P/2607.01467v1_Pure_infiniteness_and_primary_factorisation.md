PURE INFINITENESS AND PRIMARY FACTORISATION
ANTONIO ACUAVIVA, BENCE HORV´ATH, AND TOMASZ KANIA
Dedicated to Niels Jakob Laustsen, whose mentorship shaped all three authors.
Abstract. We show that there is no real or complex indecomposable Banach space with
the primary factorisation property (PFP). We relate the PFP of a Banach space E to ring-
theoretic infiniteness of B(E) and of B(E)/ME, where ME denotes the set of operators not
factoring the identity on E, in the case it is the unique maximal ideal of B(E). For complex
E with the PFP, this quotient is purely infinite exactly when it is not scalar. We isolate
the quantitative gap relevant to ultrapowers, identify classical sequence spaces as positive
non-scalar cases, and show that Read’s space ER does not have the uniform PFP.
1. Introduction
The primary factorisation property is a strong operator-theoretic form of primariness.
A Banach space E has this property when, for every operator T ∈B(E), the identity on
E factors through T or through IdE −T. Thus every operator determines a factorisation
dichotomy. Such dichotomies are often used to prove that a Banach space is primary, but
they also have consequences for the ring structure of B(E) and of its Calkin-type quotients.
The starting point of this note is the ideal-like set
ME = {T ∈B(E) : IdE does not factor through T}.
Under the primary factorisation property this set is the largest proper two-sided ideal of
B(E). The quotient
AE := B(E)/ME
is therefore a unital Banach algebra. This object will be of central importance in the proof of
our first result, which establishes that the PFP and indecomposability are mutually exclusive
properties in both real and complex Banach spaces:
Theorem A. There is no real or complex indecomposable Banach space with the PFP.
As the first and third-named authors already noted at the beginning of [2, Section 2.2],
the PFP is intimately related to the primeness and primariness properties of Banach spaces.
Indeed, en route to proving primariness of a Banach space, the first step is often to show that
it has the PFP. It is thus worth contrasting the above theorem with a deep result of Gowers
and Maurey from [9], who constructed an indecomposable, prime Banach space.
It is natural to ask which ring-theoretic infiniteness properties of B(E) and AE are forced
by the factorisation dichotomy.

Our next result collects the elementary infiniteness consequences of the PFP. We use the
definition of pure infiniteness for unital complex Banach algebras adopted by Daws and the
second-named author: A is purely infinite when A ̸≃C and, for each non-zero a ∈A, there
are b, c ∈A such that bac = 1.
Theorem B. Let E be a Banach space.
(i) If E is infinite-dimensional and has the primary factorisation property, then B(E) is
Dedekind-infinite.
(ii) The preceding conclusion cannot in general be strengthened to proper infiniteness:
C[0, ω1] has the primary factorisation property, but B(C[0, ω1]) is not properly infinite.
(iii) If E is complex and has the primary factorisation property, then every non-zero ele-
ment of AE = B(E)/ME has the identity factoring through it. Consequently, AE is
purely infinite if and only if AE ̸≃C.
The proof of part (i) is very short: applying the factorisation dichotomy to a rank-one
projection forces the identity to factor through the complementary projection, and hence
produces an idempotent which is Murray–von Neumann equivalent to the identity but is
not equal to it. For part (ii), the obstruction is the Loy–Willis quotient. The third-named
author and Laustsen identified the corresponding codimension-one ideal as the set of operators
through which the identity does not factor, so B(C[0, ω1])/ME ≃C; proper infiniteness would
pass to this quotient, but C is not properly infinite.
Part (iii) is proved by passing any
factorisation of IdE through an operator representative to the quotient.

The final new test case is Read’s space ER. Read constructed ER so that B(ER) admits
a discontinuous derivation, and hence a discontinuous homomorphism into a Banach algebra.
The key point for the present paper is that Read’s codimension-one ideal has a large square-

if B(E) has a codimension-one ideal I and a closed ideal N ⊊I with I2 ⊆N, then E cannot

0 −→W(ER) −→B(ER) −→eℓ2 −→0,

2. Preliminaries
Throughout, E denotes a Banach space over K ∈{R, C}. The Banach space of bounded
linear operators from a Banach space E to a Banach space F is denoted by B(E, F). We
put B(E) := B(E, E), which is a unital Banach algebra with multiplicative identity IdE,
where IdE is the identity operator on E. We say that an operator S ∈B(E) factors through
T ∈B(E) if there are U, V ∈B(E) such that
S = UTV.
Definition 2.1. A Banach space E has the primary factorisation property, abbreviated PFP,
if for every T ∈B(E) the identity IdE factors through T or through IdE −T.
For C ⩾1, the space E has the C-primary factorisation property, abbreviated C-PFP, if
for every T ∈B(E) there are U, V ∈B(E) with
UTV = IdE
or
U(IdE −T)V = IdE
and ∥U∥∥V ∥⩽C. The space E has the uniform primary factorisation property, abbreviated
UPFP, if it has the C-PFP for some C ⩾1.

For a Banach space E, set
(2.1)

The following elementary observation is useful throughout.
Proposition 2.3. Let E be a Banach space. The set ME is closed in the operator norm.
The Banach space E has the PFP if and only if ME is the largest proper ideal of B(E). In
this case ME is the unique maximal ideal of B(E).
Proof. We first show that set ME is closed in the operator norm. Indeed, suppose Tn →T
in norm and T /∈ME. Choose A, B ∈B(E) such that ATB = IdE. Then ATnB →IdE, and
as the group of invertible elements in B(E) is an open set in the operator norm, ATnB is
invertible for all sufficiently large n ∈N. Consequently
IdE = (ATnB)−1(ATnB) = ((ATnB)−1A)TnB,
proving that IdE factors through Tn for all sufficiently large n ∈N.
Now suppose E has the PFP. Plainly IdE /∈ME. The set ME is stable under left and right
multiplication by arbitrary operators: if IdE factors through ATB, then it factors through T.
We check additivity. Let S, T ∈ME and suppose that S + T /∈ME. Then there are
A, B ∈B(E) such that
A(S + T)B = IdE.
Apply the PFP to ASB. Since
IdE −ASB = ATB,
the identity factors through ASB or through ATB, and therefore through S or through T.
This contradicts S, T ∈ME. Hence S + T ∈ME, and ME is a proper two-sided ideal.
Finally, if J is a proper ideal of B(E) and T ∈J, then T ∈ME; otherwise IdE would factor
through T, forcing IdE ∈J. Thus every proper ideal is contained in ME, so ME is the largest
proper ideal.
Conversely, suppose that ME is a maximal ideal. If the PFP failed, then for some T ∈B(E)
both T and IdE −T would belong to ME. Since ME is an ideal, it is a linear subspace, and
hence
IdE = T + (IdE −T) ∈ME,
contradicting properness.
□
We recall the ring-theoretic terminology used below. In a unital algebra A, the group of
invertible elements is denoted by invA. If A is a unital Banach algebre, then invA is an open
subset of A.
For two idempotents p and q in a unital algebra A, we write p ∼q when they are alge-
braically Murray–von Neumann equivalent, that is, when there are x, y ∈A such that p = xy
and q = yx. The idempotents p, q ∈A are orthogonal if pq = 0 = qp.

Definition 2.4. A unital algebra A is Dedekind-infinite if there are a, b ∈A such that
ab = 1A
and
ba ̸= 1A.
Equivalently, 1A is Murray–von Neumann equivalent to an idempotent different from 1A.

4. PFP and ring-theoretic infiniteness
We first prove the Dedekind-infiniteness observation.
Lemma 4.1. If the infinite-dimensional Banach space E has the PFP, then B(E) is Dedekind-
infinite.
Proof. Choose x ∈E and f ∈E∗with ∥x∥= ∥f∥= 1 and f(x) = 1. Put P = x ⊗f, so
that P is a rank-one idempotent on E. By the PFP, there are U, V ∈B(E) such that
IdE = UPV
or
IdE = U(IdE −P)V.
The first alternative is impossible, because UPV has rank at most one whereas E is infinite-
dimensional. Hence
IdE = U(IdE −P)V.
Define
bP = V U(IdE −P).
By Lemma 2.2, bP ∈B(E) is an idempotent operator with bP[E] ≃E and ker(IdE−P) ⊆ker bP.
On the one hand bP[E] ≃E implies bP ∼IdE. On the other hand Px = x by construction,

The preceding lemma cannot be strengthened to proper infiniteness.
Proposition 4.2. Let E = C[0, ω1]. Then E has the PFP, but B(E) is not properly infinite.
Proof. Loy and Willis constructed a codimension-one ideal in B(C[0, ω1]) [17]. The third-

impossible. Hence B(E) is not properly infinite.

Remark 4.4. For a non-scalar complex PFP space, Proposition 4.3 identifies AE as a simple
purely infinite Banach algebra. This places the quotients considered here close to questions
in Banach-algebra K-theory. For example, the third-named author, Koszmider and Laustsen
proved that K0(B(C[0, ω1])) ≃Z [11]. The scalar quotient B(C[0, ω1])/ME ≃C shows that
this example sits on the boundary of the present pure-infiniteness problem, but it suggests
that the K-theory of the non-scalar quotients AE may be worth studying separately.
Proposition 4.3 reduces pure infiniteness of B(E)/ME to excluding the scalar case. A stan-
dard geometric way to do this is to require E to contain two complemented copies of itself
inside a complemented subspace. Under this hypothesis B(E) is properly infinite, and hence
it cannot have a non-zero finite-dimensional quotient: proper infiniteness passes to unital
quotients, whereas finite-dimensional unital algebras are Dedekind-finite. The following ob-
servation is essentially the combination of [15, Proposition 1.9 and Lemma 1.11]; we include
the short proof for convenience.
Proposition 4.5. Let E be a Banach space which contains a complemented subspace isomor-
phic to E ⊕E. Then B(E) has no non-zero finite-dimensional quotient. In particular, if E
has the PFP, then B(E)/ME ̸≃C.
Proof. Let F be a complemented subspace of E with F ≃E ⊕E. The two coordinate copies
of E inside F give orthogonal idempotents p, q ∈B(E) whose ranges are complemented and
isomorphic to E. Hence
p ∼IdE ∼q,
so B(E) is properly infinite.
Suppose that θ: B(E) →B is a non-zero unital homomorphism onto a finite-dimensional
algebra B. Proper infiniteness passes to unital quotients, so B is properly infinite and hence
Dedekind-infinite. But every finite-dimensional unital algebra is Dedekind-finite: if ab = 1 in
such an algebra, then left multiplication by a has a right inverse, hence is invertible as a linear
map, and therefore ba = 1. This contradiction proves the first assertion. The final assertion
follows because B(E)/ME ≃C would be a non-zero finite-dimensional quotient.
□
Propositions 4.3 and 4.5 thus amount to the following result:
Corollary 4.6. Let E be a complex Banach space which contains a complemented subspace
isomorphic to E ⊕E. If E has the PFP, then AE = B(E)/ME is purely infinite.

Remark 5.1 (The geometry behind Cpi). In quotients of operator algebras, the constants
in (5.1) measure, in a precise way, how efficiently one finds complemented copies of the
underlying space inside ranges of operators. Suppose, for instance, that T ∈B(E) and that
UTV = IdE.
Then Q = TV U is a projection on E with Q[E] ⊆T[E] by Lemma 2.2. Moreover TV : E →
TV [E] is an isomorphism with inverse U|TV [E]. Thus a factorisation of the identity through T
gives a complemented copy of E inside T[E], with projection norm and isomorphism constants
controlled by the norms of U, V and T. Conversely, whenever a concrete Banach-space argu-
ment produces well-complemented copies of E inside the range of each operator representing
a quotient element, it gives estimates for Cpi.
This is the mechanism behind the constant-one result for the classical sequence spaces
below. It also explains why the non-scalar negative problem is delicate: one needs a PFP
space for which the qualitative dichotomy holds, while the constants for the complemented
copies forced by one side of the dichotomy can nevertheless escape to infinity.
