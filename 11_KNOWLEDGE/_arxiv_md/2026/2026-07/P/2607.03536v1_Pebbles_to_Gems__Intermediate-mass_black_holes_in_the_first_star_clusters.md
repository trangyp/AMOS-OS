ABSTRACT
The rapid assembly of supermassive black holes (SMBHs) observed at z ≳7 requires efficient seeding mechanisms operating in the
early Universe. Population III (Pop. III) star clusters have recently emerged as a promising pathway that may bridge the gap between
traditional light- and heavy-seed scenarios, producing intermediate-mass black holes (IMBHs) with masses up to ∼104 M⊙. In this
work, we investigate the properties and number densities of IMBHs forming in Pop. III star clusters with masses Mcl ∼103−4×105 M⊙,
and hosted in isolated dark matter minihalos, using a suite of direct N-body simulations. We adopt initial conditions motivated by
semi-analytical cosmological models and explore a broad parameter space spanning different stellar evolution prescriptions, binary
orbital parameter distributions, and cluster dynamical configurations. We find that, by z ∼19, the IMBH mass function consistently
peaks at mIMBH ∼200 M⊙, corresponding to number densities of nIMBH ∼0.2 −5 cMpc−3. If Pop. III stars formed in sufficiently
dense and massive clusters, IMBHs with masses > 103 M⊙could also be produced by z ∼19, with number densities of nIMBH ∼
10−4 −10−2 cMpc−3. The most massive IMBHs in our models reach ∼6200 M⊙, and originate from the collapse of very massive
stars assembled through repeated stellar collisions, a process that is particularly efficient in clusters with fractal initial conditions.
Lower-mass IMBHs are instead predominantly produced through single and binary stellar evolution, as well as binary stellar mergers.
We further find that models combining large stellar radii and tight binaries yield the highest IMBH abundances relative to isolated
Pop. III star evolution. Owing to the high retention fraction of IMBHs (≳88%), massive dense Pop. III star clusters can act as efficient
incubators of both light and heavy SMBH seeds, even if only a fraction of the overall Pop. III stellar population formed in such
environments.
1. Introduction
Supermassive black holes (SMBHs) with masses ≳106 M⊙are
now routinely observed at z ≳7 (e.g., Greene et al. 2020;
Maiolino et al. 2024; Akins et al. 2025; Greene et al. 2026;
Umeda et al. 2026). The discovery of luminous SMBHs already
in place at these redshifts implies that some of them have assem-
bled rapidly within the first ∼1 Gyr after the Big Bang (e.g.,
Alexander & Natarajan 2014; Inayoshi et al. 2020; Volonteri
et al. 2021; Spinoso et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2024). Explaining
how such massive objects assembled on such short timescales
remains one of the main challenges in models of black hole (BH)
formation and galaxy evolution.

A key open question concerns the nature of the initial “seeds”
from which SMBHs grow. Proposed formation channels are
commonly divided into “light” and “heavy” SMBH seeds.
Heavy seeds are expected to form with masses > 103 M⊙
through the direct collapse of massive gas clouds in atomic-
cooling halos (e.g., Bromm & Loeb 2003; Begelman et al. 2006;
Volonteri 2010; Regan et al. 2020; Regan & Volonteri 2024).
This scenario can naturally explain the rapid assembly of high-
redshift quasars, because the seeds are already sufficiently mas-
sive to alleviate the need for prolonged super-Eddington growth.
However, it requires rather specific environmental conditions to
suppress molecular cooling and provide large gas inflow rates,
including strong Lyman-Werner radiation backgrounds, large
streaming velocities between baryons and dark matter, and halo

mergers (e.g., Inayoshi & Tanaka 2015; Agarwal et al. 2016;
Habouzit et al. 2016; Trinca et al. 2022).
Light seeds, instead, are typically associated with the rem-
nants of Population III (Pop. III) stars, the first generation of
metal-free stars formed at z ≳20 (e.g., Madau & Rees 2001;
Tanaka & Haiman 2009; Natarajan 2021; Klessen & Glover
2023), or of early Population II stars. Such seeds are expected to
have masses ∼102 M⊙and to be relatively abundant (e.g., Regan
et al. 2020; Tanikawa et al. 2020, 2021a,b, 2022, 2023; Tanikawa
2024; Regan & Volonteri 2024). However, if Pop. III stars evolve
in isolation, i.e., as single stars or in multiples that do not expe-
rience significant dynamical interactions with other stars, their
remnants face several challenges as progenitors of high-redshift
SMBHs. First, their relatively small masses require sustained gas
accretion at, or above, the Eddington limit over extended peri-
ods (e.g., Volonteri & Rees 2005; Alexander & Natarajan 2014;
Natarajan 2021; Shi et al. 2024). Second, radiative and mechan-
ical feedback from both the progenitor stars and the accreting
BHs can strongly suppress gas inflow (e.g., Whalen et al. 2004;
Alvarez et al. 2009; Milosavljevi´c et al. 2009; Hosokawa et al.
2011; Hirano et al. 2014; Fukushima et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2024).
Finally, low-mass Pop. III remnants may experience inefficient
dynamical friction, preventing them from efficiently sinking to
the centers of potential wells and thus hindering their growth via
gas accretion and binary black hole (BBH) mergers (e.g., Pfister
et al. 2019; Ma et al. 2021).
In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to sce-
narios in which Pop. III stars form in clusters rather than in isola-
tion. Hydrodynamical simulations have shown that gas fragmen-
tation within primordial dark matter (DM) minihalos can lead to
the formation of multiple stars and compact stellar systems (e.g.,
Clark et al. 2011; Greif et al. 2011; Hirano et al. 2018; Sugimura
et al. 2020; Chon et al. 2021; Hirano et al. 2023). In particu-
lar, non-zero baryon-DM streaming velocities can amplify gas
asymmetries and fragmentation, potentially favoring the forma-
tion of relatively massive Pop. III clusters (Hirano et al. 2018;
Schauer et al. 2021; Hirano et al. 2023). Dense stellar clusters
provide a natural environment for the formation of intermediate-
mass black holes (IMBHs), with masses 102 −104 M⊙, through
repeated stellar collisions, compact object mergers, and BH-star
interactions. In metal-poor or metal-free environments, where
stellar winds are weak, repeated stellar collisions can efficiently
produce very massive stars (VMSs), which may subsequently
collapse into IMBHs (e.g., Portegies Zwart & McMillan 2002;
Mapelli 2016; Kremer et al. 2020; Rastello et al. 2020; Banerjee
2021; Rizzuto et al. 2021; Arca Sedda et al. 2023; Rastello et al.
2025; Paiella et al. 2026; Mestichelli et al. 2026).
Recent studies have suggested that Pop. III star clusters may
represent an efficient channel for the formation of SMBH seeds
with masses ≳103 M⊙. Several works have explored the impact
of runaway collisions, BBH mergers, and hierarchical cluster
assembly on the growth of primordial BHs (e.g., Wang et al.
2022; Mestichelli et al. 2024; Reinoso et al. 2025; Vergara et al.
2025; Rantala 2026; Wu et al. 2026). These studies indicate
that stellar dynamics may bridge the gap between traditional
light- and heavy-seed scenarios, producing IMBHs with masses
∼103 −104 M⊙already at very high redshifts. Nevertheless, the
initial conditions of Pop. III star clusters remain highly uncer-
tain, including their mass and density, as well as the initial mass
function (IMFs) and the orbital parameter distributions of pri-
mordial binaries.
Here, we address these uncertainties by performing a suite of
direct N-body simulations based on cosmologically motivated
initial conditions and exploring a broad range of stellar evolu-
tion models and cluster configurations. We extract cluster masses
and DM minihalo properties from the semi-analytical cosmolog-
ical models of Hartwig et al. (2022) and Ishiyama & Hirano
(2025), and evolve the clusters with the hybrid N-body code
PeTar (Wang et al. 2020a), including prescriptions for single and
binary stellar evolution through bseEmp (Tanikawa et al. 2020),
and assuming high primordial binary fractions (Moe & Di Ste-
fano 2017). We explore the impact of different stellar evolution
tracks, binary orbital parameters, and initial dynamical configu-
rations. Our main goal is to assess whether stellar dynamics in
the first star clusters can efficiently produce IMBHs that may act
as SMBH seeds. In particular, we study the relative importance
of stellar evolution, stellar collisions, VMS formation, and BBH
mergers in shaping the mass spectrum and number density of
dynamical IMBHs.
The paper is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the ini-
tial conditions and outlines the used numerical code. In Sec. 3,
we describe our main results. Section 4 considers the impact of
our chosen prescriptions and discusses the main implications of
our results on SMBH seeding. Finally, Sec. 5 summarizes our
conclusions.
2. Methods
In this work, we simulate Pop. III star clusters forming at the
center of DM minihalos, whose properties are extracted from
the semi-analytical cosmological simulations of Hartwig et al.
(2022, henceforth H22) and Ishiyama & Hirano (2025, IH25
from here forward).
Our models consist of two sets of N-body simulations based
on these cosmologically motivated initial conditions. We per-
formed the simulations using the N-body code PeTar (Wang
et al. 2020a), which includes prescriptions for single and bi-
nary stellar evolution through population-synthesis algorithms
(bseEmp; Tanikawa et al. 2020), as well as for external tidal ef-
fects (galpy; Bovy 2015).
2.1. Cosmological simulations
To initialize our cluster models, we extracted from H22 and IH25
the masses of both the star clusters and the host minihalos at z ∼
20. We assumed that the initial cluster mass, Mcl, corresponds to
the total Pop. III stellar mass formed within each minihalo.
Figure 1 shows the star formation rate densities (SFRDs) and
the distribution of masses of star-forming DM minihalos and
clusters at z ∼20, as derived from H22 and IH25. We started our
simulations at this redshift because the Pop. III SFRD is close
to its peak while still dominating over the Population II contri-
bution, and the DM minihalos are massive enough to host a star
cluster. We notice that, although the star-forming halos in IH25
are systematically less massive than those in H22, their hosted
clusters are typically more massive in the former case. The ori-
gin of this difference is discussed below.

onto which analytical prescriptions for the formation of indi-
vidual metal-free and metal-poor stars are applied (Ishiyama
et al. 2016; Griffen et al. 2016, 2018). The critical halo mass

2.1.2. IH25
Ishiyama & Hirano (2025) describe a semi-analytical framework
that also operates on DM halo merger trees, generated within a
comoving volume of L = 16 h−1 Mpc (Ishiyama et al. 2021). In
this model, the critical halo mass is defined as the minimum be-
tween Mcrit from Kulkarni et al. (2021) and the threshold for
atomic cooling from Feathers et al. (2024). Pop. III star for-
mation depends explicitly on halo properties, such as the gas
infall rate at the Jeans scale, following the results of hydro-
dynamical simulations (Hirano et al. 2014, 2015). Moreover,
the model takes into account the enhanced star formation effi-
ciency in rapidly collapsing clouds (> 0.01 M⊙yr−1, Toyouchi
et al. 2023). The Lyman-Werner radiation field is modeled self-
consistently, leading to a time-dependent regulation of star for-
mation (Johnson et al. 2013). Once the halo mass exceeds the
critical mass for star formation, it is assumed that a single star
forms instantaneously in the halo. It is important to note that
non-zero streaming velocities can enhance gas fragmentation,
increasing the likelihood of forming multiple Pop. III stars in a
minihalo (Hirano et al. 2018, 2023). Therefore, in this work we
adopt the results obtained in IH25 with a baryon-DM streaming
velocity of vBC = 1 σBC, to remain consistent with H22. The
model assumes cosmological parameters from Planck Collabo-
ration et al. (2020).
As shown in Fig. 1, the different treatment of star formation
efficiency and radiative feedback results in systematically larger
Pop. III stellar masses and, consequently, more massive clusters,
than in H22, for similar halo masses. In addition, the different
prescription for the critical halo mass allows Pop. III star forma-
tion to occur in smaller halos.
2.2. N-body code
We performed the simulations using the hybrid N-body code
PeTar (Wang et al. 2020a). Hybrid schemes rely on Hamil-
tonian splitting of long-range and short-range interactions. In
particular, PeTar implements the particle-tree particle-particle
(P3T; Oshino et al. 2011) algorithm together with the slow-
down algorithmic regularization method (SDAR; Wang et al.
2020b) to accurately and efficiently treat close encounters and
binary interactions. The use of OpenMP for parallel workload
distribution across CPUs, combined with GPU acceleration, en-
sures high computational efficiency and scalability. The code in-
cludes prescriptions for single and binary stellar evolution via the
population-synthesis code bseEmp (Tanikawa et al. 2020), while
the effect of the host DM halo is modeled using the galpy pack-
age (Bovy 2015).
2.2.1. Compact object mergers in PeTar
In dense star clusters with high primordial binary fractions, BH-
star collisions can occur frequently as a result of the combined
effects of binary evolution and dynamical interactions (e.g.,
Mapelli 2016; Arca Sedda et al. 2024; Rastello et al. 2025).
The outcome of such interactions remains uncertain (e.g., Kre-
mer et al. 2020; Schrøder et al. 2020). Nonetheless, dynamical
studies (Kıro˘glu et al. 2025a,b) indicate that repeated BH-star
collisions may drive significant BH growth. In this work, we as-
sumed that a fraction fc = 0.5 of the stellar mass involved in
such interactions is accreted onto the BH (Banerjee 2021; Riz-
zuto et al. 2021; Arca Sedda et al. 2024).
Gravitational-wave (GW) driven mergers are modeled by
evolving the semi-major axis and eccentricity of binaries follow-
ing Peters (1964). A binary is considered to merge when its GW
inspiral timescale becomes shorter than its integration timestep.
At that point, the system is evolved to the merger time, allowing
us to record the position of each BBH merger within the cluster.

We modeled the external gravitational potential using the galpy
package (Bovy 2015), assuming that each star cluster resided
at the center of its host DM minihalo. For H22, we randomly

2.3. Stellar and binary evolution
We modeled single and binary stellar evolution using the
population-synthesis code bseEmp (Tanikawa et al. 2020). All
clusters have metallicity Z = 10−10. Binary evolution processes,
including tides, mass transfer, common-envelope evolution, and
GW-driven orbital decay follow the prescriptions of Hurley et al.
(2002). We treated the common-envelope phase using the stan-
dard αCE −λCE formalism, where αCE is the fraction of orbital
energy used to unbind the envelope and λCE characterizes the
envelope binding energy. We adopted αCE = 1, while λCE was
computed following Claeys et al. (2014).
2.3.1. Stellar tracks
The bseEmp code provides two sets of stellar evolution fitting for-
mulae, referred to as the “M” and “L” tracks from the hoshi code
(Takahashi et al. 2016; Yoshida et al. 2019), calibrated using ob-
servations of stars in the Milky Way and the Large Magellanic
Cloud, respectively. The two models differ primarily in the ef-
ficiency of convective overshooting, with overshoot parameters
fov = 0.01 for the M model and fov = 0.03 for the L model
(Tanikawa et al. 2022). For Pop. III stars, these differences lead
to a markedly distinct mass-radius relation. M-tracks stars reach
radii of at most ∼100 R⊙even for 160 M⊙, whereas L-tracks
stars can exceed 103 R⊙for zero-age main sequence (ZAMS)
masses mZAMS ≥80 M⊙. The enhanced overshooting in the L
model enables more efficient mixing of fresh hydrogen into the
core, producing helium cores that are ∼20% more massive at the
end of the main sequence. This results in higher core luminosi-
ties and substantially larger post-main-sequence radii, and has
an impact on the (pulsational) pair-instability supernova (PISN)
mass range.
We sampled stars with ZAMS masses between 0.08 and
300 M⊙from a log-flat IMF (ξ(m) ∝m−1, Stacy & Bromm
2013; Stacy et al. 2016; Hirano et al. 2014, 2015; Susa et al.
2014; Wollenberg et al. 2020; Chon et al. 2021; Tanikawa et al.
2021b; Jaura et al. 2022; Prole et al. 2022; Klessen & Glover
2023). We point out that Pop. III clusters of different masses
and formed in different environments can have different stellar
IMFs, as shown in some hydrodynamic simulations and analyti-
cal models (Liu et al. 2024; Prole et al. 2024). Here, we adopt an
invariant Pop. III IMF for simplicity. In the following, we define
a VMS as a star with m∗> 300 M⊙, and an IMBH as a BH with
m ≥100 M⊙.

2.4. Initial conditions
We generated the initial conditions of our simulations using
mcluster (Küpper et al. 2011). We extracted cluster masses from
H22 and IH25, assuming that the total Pop. III stellar mass form-
ing in a halo at z ∼20 corresponds to the initial cluster mass. As
shown in Fig. 1, both distributions peak at ∼103 M⊙, but IH25
also produces a large fraction of clusters up to ∼4 × 105 M⊙. We
simulated clusters with Mcl ∈[103, 3 × 103] M⊙(N ∈[50, 80])
for H22 and Mcl ∈[103, 4 × 105] M⊙(N ∈[50, 1.5 × 104]) for
IH25.
Positions and velocities of particles in the clusters were sam-
pled from a King profile (King 1966) with central potential
W0 = 5, a commonly adopted fiducial value corresponding to
a moderately concentrated cluster (Portegies Zwart et al. 2004;
Giersz 2006; Kremer et al. 2020). We assumed two values for
the half-mass radius, rh = 0.5 and 1 pc, consistent with Marks
& Kroupa (2012). We note that this relation is applicable in the
local Universe, but it has not been specifically established for
Pop. III star clusters. However, Liu et al. (2024) find a rela-
tion connecting the initial half-mass radius of Pop. III clusters
to their mass, which reproduces values for rh that are consistent
or smaller than the ones used in this work.
We considered both monolithic and fractal initial conditions,
adopting a fractal dimension D = 1.6 to capture the expected
substructure of star-forming regions (e.g., Sánchez & Alfaro
2009; Küpper et al. 2011; Di Carlo et al. 2019). We denote
our models as M05 (monolithic, rh = 0.5 pc), M1 (monolithic,
rh = 1 pc), F05 (fractal, rh = 0.5 pc), and F1 (fractal, rh = 1 pc).
Figure 2 shows the initial densities at the half-mass radius ρh as
a function of cluster mass for the two cosmological models and
the four dynamical configurations. We notice that, while ρh is
the same for monolithic and fractal clusters, the local density in
fractal substructures can be higher.

3. Results
3.1. Number density of IMBHs
Figure 3 shows the number density distributions of IMBHs at
z ∼19, originating from star clusters that formed at z ∼20 and
subsequently evolved for 20 Myr. We present all model config-
urations and consider the two sets of cosmological initial con-
ditions (H22; IH25). We compute the cosmologically-weighted
number density of IMBHs by weighting each of them by the cos-
mological abundance of its host cluster. For each IMBH formed
in a cluster of mass Mcl, we assign a cosmological weight w =
nIII(Mcl) ∆log10 Mcl/Nsim(Mcl), where nIII(Mcl) is the comoving
number density of clusters with mass Mcl per dex of Mcl from the
cosmological simulations (H22; IH25), ∆log10 Mcl is the log-
arithmic width of the cluster mass bins, and Nsim(Mcl) is the
number of times that cluster mass was sampled across replica-
tions. This weighting scheme accounts for the cosmic abundance
of each cluster mass, yielding the expected comoving number
density of IMBHs as a function of their mass, nIMBH(mIMBH)
[cMpc−3]. We assume that only first-generation BBH mergers
could produce IMBHs (see Sec. 4.3). Moreover, we include

IMBHs that are expelled from their host cluster through dynam-

are unlikely to represent SMBH seeds.
For mIMBH < 200 M⊙, different assumptions on stellar evolu-
tion and orbital parameters result in noticeably different IMBH
mass distributions. In fact, the PISN mass range is broader on the
M tracks than on L tracks. Moreover, L-tracks stars expand more
and lose more mass, leaving lighter remnants. Binary interac-
tions can narrow this effective range, especially with the smaller
initial semi-major axes from S12, which boost mass transfer and
mergers.
In all configurations, the IMBH mass distribution peaks at
mIMBH ∼200 M⊙, corresponding to number densities of nIMBH ∼
0.2 cMpc−3 for H22 and nIMBH ∼5 cMpc−3 for IH25. This peak
arises because the dominant formation channel for IMBHs in
our simulations is the direct collapse of stars with masses above
the PISN mass gap. Such stars can form through single and bi-
nary stellar evolution, binary mergers or, less frequently, through
short chains of stellar collisions. The peak at mIMBH ∼200 M⊙
is produced both in H22 and in IH25, despite the different clus-
ter mass distributions (Fig. 1). The two models, in fact, favor the
formation of small clusters, while larger IMBHs can be produced
only in dense clusters with short relaxation times (see Sec. 3.2).
We will discuss the impact of the chosen IMF and maximum
mass on this result in Appendix A.
For mIMBH ≲500 M⊙, models based on H22 predict num-
ber densities over an order of magnitude lower than those using
IH25. This mainly reflects (i) the larger cluster masses in IH25,
which form more IMBHs, and (ii) their lower critical mass for
Pop. III star formation, allowing stars to form in smaller halos.
Different cluster mass distributions produce large discrepan-
cies at the high-mass end. The larger cluster masses in IH25
yield IMBHs up to an order of magnitude more massive than
those from H22, which reach mIMBH ∼500 M⊙for S12 and
≲400 M⊙for SB13. With initial conditions from IH25, the most
massive IMBHs form in initially fractal configurations (F05, F1),
where higher densities are reached during the first ∼2 Myr than
in the monolithic case (see Sec. 3.2). Here, IMBHs can exceed
2000 M⊙, with masses up to ∼6200 M⊙(M-tracks, S12, F05),
and number densities nIMBH ∼few × 10−4 −10−3 cMpc−3. Be-
cause of their lower density, none of the M1 models produce
IMBHs with mIMBH > 103 M⊙.
Overall, our results suggest that, if Pop. III stars commonly
formed in clusters with Mcl ≳103 M⊙, they could produce
IMBHs with masses up to 300 −500 M⊙at number densities
comparable to, or exceeding, those expected for light seeds from
isolated Pop. III stars (e.g., Madau & Rees 2001; Regan et al.
2020; Regan & Volonteri 2024; Mehta et al. 2026; Prole et al.
2026). Moreover, if dense massive Pop. III clusters were to form,
they would also create a non-negligible population of IMBHs
with masses above 103 M⊙already at z ∼19. In Sec 4.1 we
will compare our current results with previous studies on SMBH
seeds, while in Sec. 4.2 we will discuss the potential to grow of
our IMBHs.

4. Discussion
4.1. Comparison with previous works
In this work, we have investigated the number densities and
properties of IMBHs forming in Pop. III star clusters, assum-
ing a variety of different configurations, and starting from a set
of cosmologically-motivated initial conditions.
By z ∼19, we find that IMBHs with masses mIMBH ∼
200 M⊙reach number densities of nIMBH ∼10−1 −5 cMpc−3.
For higher masses, the number density decreases to nIMBH ∼
10−2 cMpc−3 at mIMBH ∼103 M⊙and nIMBH ≲10−3 cMpc−3
for mIMBH ≳5000 M⊙. These results are largely insensitive to
the adopted stellar evolution models and binary orbital parame-
ter prescriptions, but highly sensitive to the existence of dense
massive (Mcl ≳104 M⊙) Pop. III star clusters.
Previous studies have estimated the number density of light
SMBH seeds from Pop. III stars to be n ∼1 cMpc−3 for m ∼
O (102) M⊙at z ∼20−10 (e.g., Madau & Rees 2001; Regan et al.
2020; Regan & Volonteri 2024; Mehta et al. 2026; Prole et al.
2026). Our results are broadly consistent with these estimates,
but highlight a strong dependence on the clustering properties
of Pop. III stars. In particular, if stars formed preferentially in
massive clusters (IH25), the number density of IMBHs could be
enhanced by up to a factor of ∼5 compared to the isolated case.
Conversely, if Pop. III stars formed in smaller clusters (H22), the
resulting number densities would be comparable to, or smaller
than, those expected for isolated stars.
For more massive seeds, previous works relying on strong
Lyman-Werner backgrounds predict significantly lower abun-
dances, with n ∼10−10 −10−5 cMpc−3 at z ∼10 for m ≳103 M⊙
(e.g., Regan & Volonteri 2024; O’Brennan et al. 2025). How-
ever, alternative formation channels involving rapidly growing
halos can produce massive clusters (∼9 × 104 M⊙) by z ∼15,
yielding IMBHs of ∼6000 M⊙with number densities n ∼
10−5−10−1 cMpc−3 (Regan et al. 2020; Regan & Volonteri 2024).
Our simulations show that dense and massive Pop. III clusters
can form IMBHs up to ∼6200 M⊙already by z ∼19, with num-
ber densities up to ∼10−3 cMpc−3. Consequently, these environ-
ments provide a particularly efficient pathway for the formation
of high-mass IMBHs, primarily through the direct collapse of
VMSs. Importantly, in most cases (≳88%) these IMBHs remain
bound to their parent clusters.
4.2. Perspectives for growth and SMBH seeding
Our results provide new insight into the role of stellar dynamics
in shaping the population of light and heavy SMBH seeds. Only
a small fraction of the IMBHs we find (≲10−2 cMpc−3) would
be required to match the observed number density of SMBHs
at high redshift (e.g., Maiolino et al. 2024; Akins et al. 2025;
Greene et al. 2026; Umeda et al. 2026), and even today (Greene
et al. 2020). This implies that even if Pop. III stars clustered
with efficiencies comparable to those in the local Universe (∼
10%, Kruijssen 2012; Adamo et al. 2015), the resulting IMBH
population could still represent a viable seeding channel.
A key advantage of IMBHs forming via stellar evolution or
VMS collapse is that they are typically retained within their host
clusters. We recall that only 1−12% IMBHs with typical masses
≲500 M⊙are ejected from their host clusters. Because star clus-
ters are quite massive, they experience strong dynamical friction,
allowing them and their embedded IMBHs to sink efficiently to-
ward galactic centers (e.g., Antonini 2013; Smith et al. 2018;
Pfister et al. 2019; Torniamenti et al. 2026). Once in the galac-

survival, and the growth pathways of IMBHs.
4.3. Caveats
Our study relies on initial conditions motivated by the semi-
analytical models of H22 and IH25. To account for uncertain-
ties in Pop. III star formation, we have explored a wide range of
stellar evolution models (Tanikawa et al. 2020), binary parame-
ter distributions (S12; SB13), and initial cluster configurations,
including both monolithic and fractal setups. Our fiducial model
adopts a log-flat IMF, with alternative prescriptions discussed in
Appendix A.
A key assumption of our work is that the initial mass of
the star cluster corresponds to the total Pop. III stellar mass
formed in each mini-DM halo. In IH25, however, no multiplic-
ity is assumed and the resulting masses correspond to individual
Pop. III stars. Our approximation is uncertain because fragmen-
tation, particularly in halos with non-zero baryon-DM streaming
velocities, may redistribute the stellar mass among multiple stars
while also reducing the total stellar mass formed in a halo (e.g.,
Hirano et al. 2018; Liu et al. 2024; Hirano et al. 2026). Conse-
quently, the cluster masses inferred from IH25 should be viewed
as upper limits, implying that our most massive clusters likely
represent an optimistic scenario for SMBH formation.

We adopt half-mass radii of rh = 0.5 and 1 pc, consistent with
observations and models of lower-redshift star clusters (Marks
& Kroupa 2012), and comparable to, or larger than, the half-
mass radii predicted by hydrodynamical simulations of Pop. III
star clusters (Liu et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the structural prop-
erties of primordial star clusters remain highly uncertain. The
efficiency of stellar interactions and collisions in our simula-
tions depends sensitively on the initial cluster density. If typi-
cal Pop. III clusters were less dense, or had masses comparable
to (or smaller than) those predicted by H22, the resulting IMBH
population would likely be similar to, or even less abundant than,
that expected from isolated Pop. III stars. Conversely, if even a
modest fraction of minihalos hosted denser and more massive
clusters (e.g., with properties intermediate between those pre-
dicted by H22 and IH25) a substantial population of both light
and heavy SMBH seeds could already be in place by z ∼19.
Our N-body simulations do not include several physical pro-
cesses that may affect cluster evolution. In particular, we neglect
cluster rotation, which has recently been shown to accelerate
early mass segregation and potentially enhance the rate of stel-
lar collisions (Kamlah et al. 2022; Bianchini et al. 2026). We
do not model the presence of gas within the clusters, nor do we
follow subsequent star formation after the first supernova explo-
sions. Both gas accretion onto BHs and the formation of Popu-
lation II stars could significantly impact the growth of IMBHs.
However, we expect that our current star clusters do not retain
a large fraction of gas because of their low escape velocities
(vesc < 50 km s−1) compared to the typical velocities of stellar
winds and SN ejecta. We plan to investigate these effects in fu-
ture work.
Finally, the PeTar code does not include GW recoil kicks fol-
lowing compact object mergers. As a consequence, we assume
that IMBHs can only form from first-generation BBH mergers,
and that they are consequently ejected. This assumption is justi-
fied by the low escape velocities in our clusters. However, PeTar
still retains n-generation BH remnants, which could affect the
dynamics of the cluster.
5. Conclusions
We investigated the properties and number densities of IMBHs
forming in Pop. III star clusters at z ∼20, using the direct N-
body code PeTar, and cosmologically motivated initial condi-
tions from Hartwig et al. (2022, hereafter H22) and Ishiyama
& Hirano (2025, hereafter IH25). To account for the large un-
certainties in Pop. III stellar populations, we explored different
stellar evolutionary tracks, orbital parameter distributions, and
cluster dynamical configurations (Fig. 2).
Our main findings can be summarized as follows:
• Across all models, and for both sets of cosmological ini-
tial conditions, the IMBH number density, nIMBH, peaks at
mIMBH ∼200 M⊙. For IMBH masses ≲500 M⊙, number den-
sities are comparable to or larger than those expected from
isolated Pop. III stars, reaching nIMBH ∼0.2 −5 cMpc−3. In
the most massive and dense clusters from IH25, IMBHs with
mIMBH ∈[1000, 6200] M⊙form efficiently already by z ∼19,
with number densities nIMBH ∼10−4 −10−2 cMpc−3 (Fig. 3).
• The majority (≳65%) of IMBHs originate from the collapse
of stars with m∗≤300 M⊙forming via single and binary
stellar evolution or from stellar collisions (Table 2). In the
more massive and dense clusters of IH25, the very massive
star (VMS, m∗> 300 M⊙) channel becomes more impor-
tant (≲35%), while IMBH formation through BBH merg-
ers remains rare in all simulations (≲0.1%). The most mas-
sive IMBHs forming in our simulations are always produced
through VMS collapse (Fig. 4). Subsequent stellar collisions
(BBH mergers) can further increase IMBH masses in ≲10%
(≲1%) of the cases.
• Assuming different stellar tracks (with lower or larger over-
shooting) and different initial orbital parameter distributions
has a crucial impact on the total number of IMBHs formed
in Pop. III stellar clusters (Fig. 5). The combination of large
stellar radii and tight binaries (L-tracks stars with S12) pro-
duces the highest IMBH abundances because of more ef-
ficient binary interactions. Conversely, models combining
compact stars (M tracks) with the wider orbital distributions
of SB13 yield the smallest IMBH populations relative to iso-
lated Pop. III evolution.
Our results indicate that Pop. III star clusters represent ef-
ficient environments for the formation of both light and heavy
SMBH seeds, with massive star clusters forming IMBHs up to
∼6200 M⊙already by z ∼19. The vast majority of these IMBHs
remain bound to the parent clusters (≳88%), making them
also more likely to sink toward galactic centers and grow into
SMBHs. This conclusion is largely insensitive to the adopted
stellar evolution tracks, orbital parameter distributions, and clus-
ter dynamical configurations. Moreover, it remains valid even
if only a fraction (∼10%) of Pop. III stars form in sufficiently
dense and massive clusters. If Pop. III star clusters were only as
massive as Mcl ∼3000 M⊙, they could create a population of
light seeds comparable or smaller than the one due to isolated
Pop. III stars. In future work, we will investigate how the sub-
sequent growth of DM halos and the hierarchical assembly of
star clusters affect the evolution and growth of these dynamical
IMBHs.

Appendix A: Impact of the initial mass function
To assess the role of the IMF on the results shown in Fig. 3,
we compare our fiducial log-flat IMF with a “super” top-heavy
IMF (SB13; Jaacks et al. 2019; Liu & Bromm 2020; Costa
et al. 2023), defined as ξ(m) ∝m−0.17 exp (−m2
cut/m2), with
mcut = 20 M⊙. For both IMFs, we consider two values of the
maximum stellar mass, mmax = 150 and 300 M⊙. These simula-
tions were performed with a single realization, and considering
the most favorable configuration for IMBH formation, namely
cosmological initial conditions from IH25, fractal initial condi-
tions with rh = 0.5 pc (F05), and orbital parameters from S12.
Figure A.1 shows that adopting a more top-heavy IMF with
mmax = 300 M⊙leads to a modest increase in the number density
of IMBHs across the entire mass spectrum, relative to the fiducial
case. This behavior is expected, as the larger fraction of massive
stars enhances the probability of producing massive remnants
through the discussed formation channels.
When instead adopting mmax = 150 M⊙, both models retain a
peak at mseed ∼200 M⊙, but show a significantly different over-
all trend. In particular, the log-flat IMF produces approximately
one order of magnitude fewer IMBHs at the peak, while the top-
heavy IMF yields a reduction by a factor of ∼5. In addition, both
IMFs produce a deficit of objects in the range ∼200 −400 M⊙,
especially in the log-flat case. In fact, when mmax = 300 M⊙,
IMBHs form in this range of mass through single stellar colli-
sions. Instead, if mmax = 150 M⊙, that range can be filled only
through repeated stellar collision able to produce stars above the
PISN gap (Mestichelli et al. 2026).
Finally, both the log-flat and top-heavy IMFs are capable of
producing IMBHs with masses ≳103 M⊙. If mmax = 300 M⊙,
number densities reach up to nIMBH ∼few × 10−2 cMpc−3. With
mmax = 150 M⊙, instead, IMBHs above 103 M⊙form with num-
ber densities up to nIMBH ∼10−3 cMpc−3.

Appendix B: Properties of the most massive IMBHs
Appendix B.1: H22
Figure B.1 shows the median of the maximum IMBH masses
obtained across all realizations of the simulations adopting the
initial conditions from H22. We find that, for the relatively small
cluster masses predicted by H22, the maximum IMBH mass
shows little dependence on either the cluster mass or the initial
dynamical configuration. In almost all cases, the median maxi-
mum IMBH mass is ∼200 M⊙. In the case of M-tracks stars, the
median is slightly above this value, both because of the mass re-
tention of these stars due to their small radii, and because of the
larger PISN gap. When adopting the orbital parameter distribu-
tions from S12, the smaller initial semi-major axes increase the
probability of interactions between stars in binaries, even for sys-
tems evolving on M tracks. As a consequence, two effects occur.
First, the PISN mass gap can be partially populated, particularly
for stars evolving along the L-tracks. Second, binary interactions
can lead to the formation of IMBHs more massive than 300 M⊙,
through stellar mergers and the subsequent collapse of VMSs
(see Sec. 3.2).
Appendix B.2: IH25
Figure B.2 shows that, when we consider initial conditions from
IH25, the median maximum IMBH mass rises with cluster mass
Mcl, from ∼200 M⊙to ∼5000 M⊙. The PISN mass gap is most
populated in models combining L-tracks and S12, because mas-
sive stars on L-tracks reach larger radii and, in tighter binaries,
are more likely to undergo envelope stripping during interac-
tions.
At cluster masses comparable to those predicted by H22,
models based on IH25 produce IMBHs with similar masses
(Fig. B.1).
In models adopting orbital parameters from S12, the max-
imum IMBH mass remains in the range 200 −400 M⊙up to
Mcl ∼104 M⊙. In this regime of cluster masses and densities,
IMBHs form through a combination of single and binary stellar
evolution channels, and stellar collisions. When instead adopt-
ing the wider initial orbital separations from SB13, IMBHs form
primarily via single stellar evolution and have typical masses of
∼200 M⊙.
Across all configurations, the maximum IMBH mass re-
mains below ∼600 M⊙up to Mcl ∼5×104 M⊙. At these masses,
IMBHs can originate from binary interactions between massive
stars or from the collapse of VMSs. We note that, for models
adopting SB13, the least dense clusters in this mass range pro-
duce maximum IMBH masses below 300 M⊙.
Finally, for cluster masses ≳few×104 M⊙, the most massive
IMBHs exceed 600 M⊙and are typically produced through dy-
namical processes, such as the collapse of VMSs formed via re-
peated stellar collisions and, less frequently, through BBH merg-
ers. Above Mcl ∼4 × 104 M⊙, models with fractal initial con-
ditions (particularly F05) are the most effective at producing
IMBHs with masses exceeding 103 M⊙. Fractal initial conditions
can efficiently produce massive IMBHs in large clusters, even
when the stellar and binary initial conditions are otherwise unfa-
vorable (see Sec. 3.2).
