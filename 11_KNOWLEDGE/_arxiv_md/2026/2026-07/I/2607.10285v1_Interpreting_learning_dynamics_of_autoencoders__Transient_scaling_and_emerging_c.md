concepts of the Ising model

We study how unsupervised autoencoders trained on microscopic spin configurations from the
Ising model learn macroscopic, theory-relevant variables underlying the data-generating process.
Without embedding domain knowledge, we mimic a typical discovery setting: We quantify learning
across multiple spatial (coarse-graining) scales and reveal two distinct dynamical regimes controlled
by main hyperparameters (model depth, width, and learning rate) — a magnetization-dominated
regime and an energy-dominated regime characterized by trade-offs in their representation quality.
The first regime is a transitory state exhibiting dynamical scaling and fluctuations that follow
an ordering-to-scale; the second gradually shifts resolution towards smaller scales relevant for the
energy representation.
Deep models trained at moderate and fast rates become arrested before
reaching these regimes. With a novel analysis of recursive-dynamic trajectories, we demonstrate
that prediction errors induce flow fields that produce a common trajectory topology across all
representation spaces. A dynamical viewpoint of learning is established in which intrinsic properties
expose the effects of forced changes in representation during training. We utilize the intuition that
learning operates as a process driven far from equilibrium by fluctuations from the training data
and optimizer to provide an interpretive basis grounded in both the physical world and the machine
models that represent it.

I.
INTRODUCTION
Machines automate many physically demanding tasks,
such as farm or factory work; formal tasks, such as cal-
culations and thought experiments using computer simu-
lations; and, more recently, increasingly abstract or cre-
ative tasks common to the worlds of work and education.
In particular, machine learning enables the automation
of tasks without a formal scope – such as pattern recog-
nition [1] – and, with the advent of large language models
(LLMs), also creative tasks such as writing and planning,
albeit often to an inadequate standard [2, 3].
While we have a good understanding of linear meth-
ods like linear regression and related applications (e.g.,
PCA [4], LDA [5], SVD [6]), we lack a comparably
deep understanding of nonlinear (stochastic) methods.
This includes the methods used to train the neural net-
works (NNs) that underpin more recent advancements.
Encouragingly, many nonlinear systems in physics arise
from many interacting constituents forming a collective,
whose qualitative behavior we can describe well at the
macroscopic scale.
This motivates our attempt to use
well-established methods and theoretical principles from
physics to explain the nonlinear training dynamics of
commonly used models.
We focus on studying learning dynamics of a determin-
istic nonlinear auto-encoder [7], which is useful for find-

ing low-dimensional representations of high-dimensional
data.
Autoencoders can be seen as nonlinear analogs
of principal component analysis (PCA) [8]; heuristically,
they allow us to describe ‘principal components’ of the
data or generic extentions of these using nonlinear re-
lationships. The most informative (non-linear) principal
components are selected when optimizing the reconstruc-
tion loss — comparing input and output — while requir-
ing all information to pass through a bottleneck with
fewer dimensions than the input representation.
This
bottleneck limits the number of independent features,
driving the discovery of collective properties that reveal
strong correlations in the training data. From a phys-
ical perspective, this suggests that learning behavior in
autoencoders will involve coarse-graining operations to
reveal theoretically relevant concepts within the com-
pressed latent space.
The discovery of more complex
compressed representations of data leads to generalizing
abstractions being created, which are indispensable for
automating cognitive labor and advancing more capable
artificial intelligence. This explains why understanding
this process has become a prominent field of research
[1, 9–15].
A variety of post hoc methods have gained popularity
for explaining the final function of trained models [16].
However, these methods do not elucidate the formation
process of concepts that underlies the acquisition of gen-
eral abilities, which is our primary interest. Specifically,
we aim to understand the influence of commonly tuned
hyperparameters – such as model size (defined by depth,

Neural networks function as compositions of many
simple, parameterized building blocks (i.e., linear/affine
transformations). This structure allows for the adapta-
tion of functional properties by tuning these parameters
away from their usually random initialization. To define
the desired functional properties, a loss function is em-
ployed, which quantifies the mismatch between current
and target properties. The goal is to minimize this loss
function. The latter generally depends continuously on
the output values for given specific inputs and implicitly
on the model parameters. This continuous dependence
enables the calculation of the gradient direction, indicat-
ing how to adjust the parameters to minimize the loss
function locally, at least up to the first order. However,
vanilla gradient descent is a fragile method for solving
non-convex optimization problems, i.e., finding the pa-
rameter values that yield a low loss. The learning rate
acts as a scaling factor for the gradient vector, balancing
effective interactions and keeping training dynamics be-
tween two unwanted extremes. Very small learning rates
facilitate convergence towards (continuous) gradient flow,
ensuring that the model find the nearest (possibly subop-
timal) local minima, albeit at the expense of more com-
putation steps. In contrast, large learning rates can lead
to dominant discretization errors, enabling escape from
local minima but risking convergence issues and numer-
ical instability. Merely adjusting the learning rate is of-
ten insufficient to achieve low loss values.
A range of
techniques is employed in practice, including careful pa-
rameter initialization [17], training data normalization,
stochastic batches [18–20], and strategies based on ad-
justing learning rates over time [21] or parameter fluctua-
tions [22]. The interplay of all these components creates a
complex system (Sect. II A) whose dynamics are, in gen-
eral, neither normal nor regular without hyperparameter
tuning. Additionally, the different dynamical regimes ob-
servable throughout training can be chaotic with respect
to hyperparameters such as parameter initialization and
stochastic training batch dynamics [23].
Deep questions still linger regarding how to concep-
tualize learning as a dynamic process with a tendency
toward critical behavior [24, 25]. In the standard theory
of critical behavior, transitions are driven by collective
behavior at large scales [26, 27]. Understanding the ab-
stract process of machine learning in this physical manner
requires us to identify patterns within the complex dy-
namical details of the learning algorithm. The algorithm
governs the evolution of parameters, the model function,
and, ultimately, the local and global loss values for indi-
vidual data points or entire datasets (Sect. II B).
To explore this, we conduct a large-scale ablation study
of unsupervised training for autoencoder models using a
synthetically generated dataset (Sect. III A) that is phys-
ically motivated (based on the Ising model described in
Sect. II C). We vary the most commonly tuned hyperpa-
rameters and assess sensitivity to stochastic elements by
repeatedly training the same model with different initial-
izations or training data orders (Sect. III B, III C, III D).
While analyzing individual models guides our intuition
about fine-tuned details, examining ensembles of models
with similar hyperparameters reveals broader trends. We
observe a variety of dynamical regimes where different
runs converge during plateaus, exhibit greater fluctua-
tions during transitions, and, in extreme cases, become
arrested or chaotic (Sect. IV A).
The Ising model is a useful testbed for studying how
learning systems capture correlated structure. It is de-
fined through high-dimensional, temperature-controlled
distributions with nontrivial couplings across arbitrary
dimensions and topologies.
More importantly, it is a
physical model system grounded in a substantial body
of theoretical work. Notably, the inverse Ising problem is
now largely solvable with sample-efficient algorithms on
general graphs [28–30]. Previous research demonstrated
that both unsupervised and supervised methods can re-
cover phase transitions or critical temperatures [31–35].
However, these methodologies have typically focused on
global order parameters such as magnetization. Theo-
ries surrounding linear and shallow autoencoders have
linked representation learning to principal components
and specifics of dynamics caused by bottlenecks [23, 36–
41]. This background motivates us to study whether sam-
ple reconstruction losses can be decomposed into physical
features across training – especially near criticality, where
the Ising data spans broad ranges of magnetization and
energy.
The associated nonlinear dynamics that arise
can reveal how physical concepts are learned through
an abstraction process that, by rudimentary hypothesis,
should bridge scales [31, 42–45]. Physical theory funda-
mental to statistical descriptions of matter supports the
conception that collective quantities and dynamics are
central to critical behavior, and, indeed, in how to coarse-
grain or abstract it [26, 27, 46–49]. We employ this as
a conceptual basis to study the learning dynamics across
different spatial scales. By induction, this scale-bridging
is inherent to both the complex nature of the target
(Ising) system and, possibly, to how the complexity is
reduced or captured by the autoencoder. In Sect. IV B,
we examine the formation of physical concept representa-
tions by monitoring their development, as these are emer-
gent properties not explicitly optimized during training.
We find that learning dynamics can entail two distinct
dynamical regimes when analyzed by spatial scale. The
appearance of these regimes is controlled by main hyper-
parameters. We can attribute each of the two regimes
to the domination of one of the two most important con-
cepts governing the data distribution: magnetization and
energy.
To complement our observation of the autoencoder’s
externally-accessible properties, via input-output rela-
tionships, we study its internal behavior in the most com-
pressed (low-dimensional) latent space, referred to as the
bottleneck. We leverage the compatibility between an au-

viewpoints establish a foundation for grounding scientific
discovery processes in artificial intelligence in the physics
of emergence (Sect. V).
II.
BACKGROUND
A.
Relevant Components for NN Learning
The learning process for artificial neural networks
(NNs) can be decomposed into three main components,
each entailing a distinct physical interpretation:
1. The data-loader provides the structure of the
function to be learned and how it’s inputs are rep-
resented to the model for processing. It defines the
dynamics of the external environment (fluctuations
in the data distribution) driving the learning algo-
rithm.
2. The model class specifies the available processing
operations and how they must relate to each other.
The complexity of learning dynamics is shaped by
the relation between node activations and their
dependence on inputs and parameters.
Parame-
terized transformations (i.e., linear layers) specify
node connectivity that depends on how node acti-
vations depend on each other (i.e., on the previous
layer). This is determined by the evolving model
parameters, which enable an adaptation through
parameter changes and thus form the basis for
the self-organization process. Nonlinear activation
functions make the interaction strength between
activations both input- and parameter-dependent.
3. The optimizer determines how models of the
model class are updated in response to their inter-
actions with the dataset. This creates a nonrecipro-
cal feedback coupling between parameters and the
‘environment’ (specified by the data, data-loader,
and optimizer). Parameter dynamics can (locally)
incorporate past interactions (so-called momen-
tum), introducing memory that can ultimately ex-
tend interactions between activations over time,
dampen fluctuations, accelerate parameter drift,
and affect the model’s stability under external (dy-
namical) driving (i.e., during training on datasets,
which have intrinsic fluctuations).
These components are combined to produce a self-
organizing system that automatically finds relationships
to represent a system of interest (e.g., the Ising model
configurations and their macroscopic and microscopic de-
tails).
B.
Learning as Abstract Dynamical Process
At an abstract level, learning is a process resulting
from continual interaction between the model state, de-
fined largely by parameters and their effect on the model
output, and the time-dependent distribution of the train-
ing data batches. Changes in the batch distribution are
controlled by the data loader, while the interaction is ex-
ternally mediated by the optimizer, often with the intro-
duction of path-dependent rates. At the basic physical
level, learning is a process far from equilibrium, a ther-
modynamic condition in which any computation must
take place [50–52]. This is a fact due to the meta-stable
states necessary for deterministic computation, which re-
quire external driving to remain stable or undergo spe-
cific conditional transitions.
The exact relationship to
the underlying system’s physical properties depends on
the hardware and software implementation of the ma-
chine learning algorithm.
On top of this, there exists
the abstract effective dynamic, induced by the underlying
physical dynamics, which is invariant to implementation
details.
Today, training neural networks using Stochastic Gra-
dient Descent combined with a momentum-based op-
timizer is the most commonly used Machine Learning
method [21, 22]. The related effective dynamics are gov-
erned by a nonreciprocal interaction between the model
state and training batches.
The coupling between the model state dynamics and
the training batch dynamics is mediated by the opti-
mizer, which often introduces path-dependent effective
learning rates that control the strength of this inter-
action.
Through this interaction, the model is driven
throughout the parameter space defined by the architec-
ture.
The training dynamics are inherently stochastic
due to the changing data distribution across batches,
which is governed by independently seeded pseudo-
random number generators.
As a result, the training

batch dynamics, which drive the model state, are com-
pletely independent of the model’s state and evolution.
The model parameters (and, by extension, the data-
dependent activations) follow a discrete trajectory driven
by parameter gradients derived from the training batch
loss. The parameter gradients depend on both the data
points in the training batch and the model parameters,
making it likely that these gradients fluctuate during
training. The training batch determines where the model
is evaluated, which in turn defines where forces are ap-
plied to the model’s output function. The model state
defines how susceptible each parameter is to forces on
the output function (determined via error backpropaga-
tion).
Lastly, the optimizer (i.e., Adam [22]) modifies
this susceptibility depending on the past gradients for
each parameter. This can make the trained system in-
sensitive to fluctuations and more sensitive to persistent
drifts, helping it relax under external driving rather than
oscillate forever or drift asymptotically slowly. More gen-
erally, we can therefore decompose the model’s parameter
trajectory into a fluctuating part that vanishes on aver-
age over time and a consistent drift that remains. For
the model to become static, either the parameter forces
must vanish for all training data subsets (zero train loss)
or the parameters must become insusceptible to these
forces (zero parameter gradient).
A more reasonable definition of convergence to a stable
configuration is therefore based on whether the distribu-
tion of model parameters changes over an accessible time
horizon. In theory, infinite time is required to assess if
this distribution is stationary or whether some rare but
possible driving sequence might change the current dis-
tribution in the long term. For our interest, it will be suf-
ficient to specify the short-term behavior, to distinguish
between unstable, metastable, and potentially infinitely
stable states. We consider the model state unstable when
the parameter trajectory drift is non-vanishing (fluctu-
ations don’t average out) and thus the model changes
consistently beyond the usually present parameter fluc-
tuations. In contrast, we consider the model to be prob-
ably infinitely stable when all parameter fluctuations av-
erage out, and the trajectories’ probability distribution
becomes stationary for long times.
When fluctuations
average out only over short time scales, the model is con-
sidered to be in a metastable transient state, from which
it will eventually escape via fluctuations induced by the
stochastic driving forces. The model then becomes un-
stable and drifts, marking a new dynamical regime that
can evolve into another (meta-)stable state. The given
picture describes the model’s parameter dynamics, but
doesn’t describe the dynamics of the actual function it
represents, which is fundamental to learning. Therefore,
the parameters could drift consistently while the output
function fluctuates in the absence of any drift, and learn-
ing stops. Conversely, the function can’t drift if the pa-
rameters only fluctuate.
To relate the dynamics of a model’s output function
to learning, it helps to create an operational definition
of learning. According to Tom M. Mitchell,“A computer
program is said to learn from experience E with respect
to some class of tasks T and performance measure P if its
performance at tasks in T, as measured by P, improves
with experience E.” In the common machine learning
setting, the experience is the interaction with training
batches and the task is defined by the condition for which
the loss, measuring the performance, vanishes. Learning
(or unlearning) of a concept is thus defined as achieving
a lower (higher) loss with respect to that concept. As the
loss is usually an averaged quantity of microscopic losses
(i.e., across data points and output dimensions), it’s pos-
sible that this macroscopic loss remains constant, while
the microscopic losses and therefore the model’s output
function change.
This makes learning a dynamic pro-
cess operating on top of many dynamical abstractions:
(1) The physical dynamics implementing computation;
(2) The training data dynamics that are independent of
the following layers; (3) The parameter dynamics medi-
ated by the optimizer; (4) The functional dynamics de-
termined by the parameterization; (5) The absolute error
dynamics determined by the microscopic loss function;
(6) The learning dynamics defined by a macroscopic loss
function.
The dynamic picture presented at lower abstraction
layers carries over to the learning dynamics. Periods of
(un-)learning (consistently changing loss) can therefore
transition into (transiently) stable dynamics (stationary
loss) in which no learning occurs.
Stronger loss fluc-
tuations can be interpreted as increased inconsistency,
which is only advantageous for escaping a local minimum.
In contrast, fluctuations restricted to the underlying dy-
namics are generally desirable as they can be interpreted
as a search for relaxation pathways inside the parameter
space (towards lower losses). These relaxation pathways
correspond to a learning phase, usually transitioning to a
new (meta-)stable model state. The size of fluctuations
strongly influences which parts of the parameter space
(loss landscape) can be explored and in which attractors
(local minima) stable convergence is possible. This em-
phasizes that, in general, we cannot neglect the details
of the data or parameter dynamics when explaining the
learning dynamics.
Interestingly, large models trained
on large datasets appear to exhibit very consistent gen-
eralization behavior [53, 54], a pattern that hasn’t been
observed more generally. Besides some theoretical work
based on assumptions not necessarily supported by ex-
periments [23], it remains unclear when the learning dy-
namics become particularly dependent on initial condi-
tions, or, more generally, path-dependent (i.e., data and
parameter evolution).

METHODS
We study the learning dynamics of auto-encoders on
compression tasks. The goal of such tasks is to derive a
lower-dimensional representation from given input data
that is sufficient to approximately reconstruct the inputs.
The input data is a one-dimensional vector corresponding
to a two-dimensional grid, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The
output data has the same shape and can be interpreted
as a two-dimensional grid of spins.
The quality of the reconstruction is measured by the
mean-squared difference (MSD) between the input and
output pixels. The pixel-wise MSD is used for the opti-
mization and is referred to as (raw data) loss.
The analysis was made feasible by limiting it to the
following classes:

with a two-dimensional, globally uniform, correla-

B.
Model
The auto-encoder model is composed of two symmet-
ric components, an encoder and decoder [57]. The in-
put is connected to the encoder, which produces a latent
representation, which is then fed to the decoder, which
generates the final output used during optimization.
The encoder and decoder used in this study are com-
posed of sequential layers alternating between affine
transformations (linear layers) and the element-wise ap-
plication of nonlinearities multiple times (via the model
depth), after which a final affine transformation follows.
The architecture is visualized in Fig. 3. All layers in the
encoder and decoder have the same number of hidden di-
mensions (i.e., the model width) and use ReLU (rectified
linear unit) nonlinearities. ReLU is a popular choice for
deep non-linear autoencoders because it is simple, fast,
piecewise linear, and well studied [58]. The final layer of
the encoder and decoder has no nonlinearity.
The model’s size can be adjusted in three main ways
that affect its representational capacity and learning be-
havior:

2.
Model Width
We keep the architecture simple by using the same
width for all intermediate layers except for the bottle-
neck. This results in a uniform block structure for both
the encoder and decoder. Similar to the bottleneck, the
model width w influences the degree of linear com-
pression or expansion within any layer.
Theoretically,
a greater width provides greater parallel processing ca-
pabilities, e.g., affine transformation channels, and the
ability to retain more information, e.g., linear transfor-
mations of higher rank. Retaining information between
layers about a given subspace, i.e., the data distribution,
involves applying a transformation that is reversible in
this subspace. This is fundamental to the overall learn-
ing behavior, especially in deep neural networks with
many layers. In each layer, independent input features
that convey important information might be irreversibly
mixed with others, limiting the reconstruction quality
of auto-encoders.
Linear layers are reversible in their
entire input space whenever the weight matrix has full
rank (i.e., scaling, rotation, and mirroring transforma-
tions that retain the dimensionality). The ReLU nonlin-
earity is only reversible for positive inputs, as all negative
inputs are mapped to zero, removing fluctuations inside
this region. This can cause models with width w = k to
be on par with the PCA error using the top k/2 compo-
nents [40]. Theoretically, a sequence of layers (including
nonlinearities) remains reversible for datasets where all
activations are positive, and all weight matrices have full
rank. In the more general case with negative activations,
reversibility is a more difficult question, depending on the
independence of activations over the input domain.
Given the finite range of input activations (0,1), all
ReLU functions with a sufficiently large negative bias are
theoretically reversible. However, weights and biases are
initialized from a zero-centered distribution, likely lead-
ing to negative activations that are masked by the ReLU
layers. By adding two ReLU layer outputs whose inputs
are mirrored around the origin, a linear (identity) func-
tion can be obtained, which is reversible for any input.
Therefore, we focus our choice of model configuration
around the model class with a width of w = 512 dimen-
sions (2 x input dimension), which inherently includes
the purely linear model by design. Additionally, we se-
lected a thinner model with w = 256 dimensions that is
invertible only for half of the input domain, as well as
a wider model with w = 1024 dimensions. The wider
model can lead to greater variance in the projections,
potentially increasing the risk of overfitting to the train-
ing data. The widest w = 1024 models have more than
a million parameters per layer, resulting in high mem-
ory requirements for training checkpoints. However, sav-
ing these checkpoints is essential for analyzing the learn-
ing dynamics effectively. Because memory requirements
grow quadratically with the width, we don’t evaluate any
models larger than this.
3.
Model Depth
The depth of a model limits its capacity to perform a
sequence of operations to generate an output. Theoreti-
cally, the maximum number of piece-wise linear segments
in a ReLU model’s function grows exponentially with the
depth [59]. In contrast, this number increases only poly-
nomially with the width of the layers [59].
Therefore,
depth is expected to be the key factor influencing the
model’s ability to fit non-linear functions.
But as we
will see, this may not imply better generalization by the
model; this capacity may remain unused when the model
is stuck in the arrested state we have identified. Accord-
ing to Ref. [23], the depth alone is less descriptive of a
model’s behavior both during initialization and during
training. Instead, the authors claim that the depth-to-
width ratio (representing a ”relative depth”) is the main
factor controlling the non-Gaussianity of activations in-
side multi-layer perceptrons. This is used to distinguish
different dynamical regimes.
When the model is shal-
low compared to its width (not really deep), the learn-
ing dynamics are well described by a Gaussian model
that assumes independent activations. In an intermedi-
ate regime, the activations become dependent but can be
explained by a first-order perturbation with respect to
inverse layer width, making the depth-to-width ratio a
relevant order parameter [23]. When the model is overly
deep compared to its width, fine-tuned initialization is
required, and the model training becomes ‘chaotic’, re-
sulting in signals either blowing up or decaying. As the
theory is not tailored to models with small bottlenecks,
we intend to provide empirical evidence for this setting.
Therefore, we cover a broad range of depths to across all
these regimes.
Encoders and decoders are mirror-symmetric sub-
models, and the ‘depth’ refers to each part alone.
In
our setting, the model depth d indicates the number of
hidden layers and the number of nonlinearities in both
the encoder and decoder, as neither has a non-linear out-
put activation function.
A depth of 0 corresponds to
an encoder and a decoder that consist of a single linear
layer without activation functions. A depth of 1 means
that both sub-models have a linear layer followed by a
non-linearity, and then another linear layer (see Fig. 3).
To encompass all reasonable depths for a non-linear
model, we consider depths of 1, 4, and 16.
Models
with depths greater than 16 (34 linear layers) would lead

9
to lengthy gradient computations and evaluation times,
which are not feasible for this investigation.
We will
see that this range seems sufficient to reveal a regime
in which, for some hyperparameters, all input signals de-
cay regularly. Additionally, the performance of deeper
models appears to be equal to or significantly worse than
that of shallow models across a wide range of hyperpa-
rameters.
C.
Optimization Algorithm
We use Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with the
Adam optimizer [22] and a learning rate scheduler to
train the autoencoder models on Ising model configu-
rations sampled at transition temperatures. During this
process, we explore various hyperparameters, primarily
focusing on variations in batch size and learning rate.
Unless otherwise specified, the default PyTorch imple-
mentation, version 2.10, with its standard configurations,
was used [60].
1.
Batch Size
The batch size |B| refers to the number of sam-
ples processed simultaneously to calculate model param-
eter gradients during each optimization step. A larger
batch size reduces fluctuations in the driving forces across
epochs because each batch is more likely to contain sim-
ilar data points. This data sharing increases the number
of shared features across batches within the same epoch.
Together, these factors can gradually alter the optimiza-
tion landscape during training. In the case of full batch
gradient descent, the randomness (stochasticity) is elim-
inated, resulting in a static optimization landscape (in
parameter space).
For our investigation, we use batches of |B| = 30, 300,
and 3000 data points, that are sampled from a training
dataset of 300, 000 data points. These batch sizes ensure
that memory requirements remain low enough for par-
allel computation on our GPU, regardless of model size.
Consequently, the computational time primarily depends
on the number of optimization steps.
We are limiting the total number of steps to 100,000
across all batch sizes to ensure a fair comparison of com-
putational time. Additionally, this setup enables us to
examine how repeating the same data points 10, 100,
and 1000 times might affect training. However, this ef-
fect may be confounded with the learning rate scheduler,
which does not follow a repeating pattern.
We decided against cycling the scheduler because it
could significantly change the learning dynamics across
different batch sizes, making them less comparable. Ad-
ditionally, to ensure the integrated learning rate remains
consistent, especially during the initial linear learning
rate ‘warm-up’ phase after model initialization, we would
need to introduce an unusual learning rate schedule. This
approach could limit the applicability of our findings to
common practice scenarios.
2.
Learning Rate
The learning rate determines the size of the steps
taken during the optimization process. This factor af-
fects how effectively the model can avoid settling into
narrow, local minima: When the parameter fluctuations
are adjusted, naturally, they alter the course of the learn-
ing dynamics – such as the visitation of transitory and
metastable states – which directly affects how and which
physical concepts are learned by the autoencoder (as we
will see in Sect. IV). The learning rate can also be inter-
preted as the model’s coupling strength with the external
(driving) environment, as it controls how much the pa-
rameters change in response to the training data.
There are three main ways the effective learning rate
can be influenced:
• A global choice of the base learning rate at the ini-
tialization of the optimizer.
• Time-dependent adjustments to the current learn-
ing rate through a learning rate scheduler.
• Data-dependent
modifications
to
the
effective
learning rate via gradient accumulation and scal-
ing in the Adam optimizer, based on exponential
averages of the first and second moments.
In our implementation, the learning rate is scheduled
to increase linearly from 1% to 100% of the base learning
rate over 100 steps. This is followed by cosine annealing,
which reduces the learning rate from 100% at step 101
to 0% at step 100,000.
A common approach to selecting an appropriate base
learning rate λ is to use a learning rate finder. This
method begins training with very low learning rates and
then gradually increases them exponentially, all the while
monitoring the loss response. The loss response can theo-
retically be influenced by various hyperparameters, such
as model architecture, initialization, data batch size, and
the order of data presentation to the architecture. How-
ever, based on empirical observations, we find that the re-
sponse curves are generally quite similar across most hy-
perparameter settings, differing mainly at higher learning
rates where instability is anticipated (see Fig. 17). The
learning rate is typically selected at the point where the
gradient is lowest in order to avoid rates that are either
too slow (naive) or too fast (unstable).
In our hyper-
parameter testing, we found that the suggested learning
rate is typically around λ = 10−4 (see Fig. 17).
This
timescale tends to align well with the varying feature
sizes in the optimization landscape, e.g., peak and valley
widths, around the initialization.
To explore different learning regimes, we selected three
learning rates: one that may be too slow (λ = 10−5), one

that is close to optimal (λ = 10−4), and one that may be
too fast (λ = 10−3), according to the heuristic argument.
3.
Loss Function
The learning process is guided by an evaluation of the
mean-squared error loss, denoted LMSD (xb, ˆxb), which is
a feature defined externally to the neural network state
itself. The function measures the difference between the
training batch inputs, xb, and the outputs, ˆxb. This loss
quantifies how effectively the model reconstructs the ab-
solute values of the inputs and represents only one of
many learnable properties of the data.
The evolution of a loss function depicts learning dy-
namics in a basic way. A decrease in the loss indicates
that the model is learning the specific property better,
whereas an increase suggests that the model may be ‘un-
learning’ or ‘forgetting’ that property (with respect to
the samples over which the loss is averaged).
In the Sect. IV, we will explore various losses related
to different observables defined on the input space, which
typically correspond to physical concepts of the Ising
model.
D.
Statistics
The optimization algorithm has two sources of varia-
tion that can influence the learning dynamics, even when
all hyperparameters are unchanged.
Both sources are
determined by seeding a pseudo-random number gener-
ator.
Firstly, the model parameters are initialized by
sampling from a uniform distribution, as outlined in
[22].
This approach addresses depth-scaling issues as-
sociated with ReLU activations. Altering the model seed
changes the initial model parameters, thereby affecting
how they evolve under identical external influences. Sec-
ondly, training batches are created by permuting the
training dataset and then splitting it. Changing the data
loader seed changes the composition and order of data
points in training batches throughout the learning pro-
cess, thereby altering the external influences on training.
By independently varying both the model seed and the
data seed, we can estimate the effects of the initial model
parameters and the data ordering on the quasi-critical
learning dynamics. These effects contribute to the vari-
ance of the metrics calculated across runs (i.e., their loss
trajectories), which are analyzed in Sect. IV B.
IV.
RESULTS
We compare the evolution of the optimization loss
at the microscopic (pixel-wise) level with macroscopic
reconstruction errors computed from spatially coarse-
grained averages across multiple scales (3x3, 5x5, ...
blocks). These block-averaged observables are related to
natural order parameters of the Ising model data (see
Sect. II C) and provide a compact, physically grounded
description of the fields [46] the autoencoder is trying to
reproduce. Physically, coarse-grained averages suppress
fluctuations, so comparing scale-resolved errors pinpoints
the spatial scale at which fluctuations of the microscopic
error are largest. We emphasize how the learning rate λ
and model size (defined by width w, depth d, and bot-
tleneck b) correlate with these scale-dependent learning
trajectories, and how representation dynamics relate to
macroscopic observables of the Ising model.
The learning dynamics are analyzed by using three dif-
ferent coarse-graining steps. These summarize the repre-
sentation evolution during 972 runs across hyperparam-
eter variations of all 256 elements (pixels) making up
each of the 60/300k generated samples inside the train-
ing/validation dataset:
• Samples are described through physical observables
(e.g. magnetization, energy) that are real-valued
functions depending on the elements xi (pixels).
• Sample descriptions are averaged over the training
or validation dataset.
• Dataset averages of sample descriptions are com-
puted across multiple runs that share one or more
hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, model depth)
to reveal trends.
Training evolution plots show averages across all runs
that match the explicitly given variables, unless other-
wise stated.
First, the evolution of the mean squared deviation be-
tween raw inputs and outputs is analyzed in Sect. IV A,
which is directly connected to the optimization process.
Then, Sect. IV B introduces the relationship between
learning this ‘raw data’ and learning physical observables.
The latter is analyzed at different spatially averaged
scales of the Ising lattice and compared with principal
components obtained from the training dataset, which
represent a basis choice that maximizes the dataset’s
variance, captured by a set of linear components. Ad-
ditionally, the evolution of the joint output distribution
of magnetization and energy is measured in Sect. IV B 4.
Finally, the learning dynamics of physical observables are
connected to the evolution of compressed internal model
representations in Sect. IV C.

B.
Learning Viewed from Physical Observables
(Concepts)
The mean squared deviation (MSD) of the raw data
alone is not sufficient to capture the learning dynamics
of other observables. Therefore, we study in detail the
learning dynamics of physical observables that are theo-
retically important for the data-generating process.
We analyze how learning depends on the spatial scale
of coarse-grained physical observables, such as the mean

C.
Latent Representation Dynamics (Twofold)
Understanding the dynamics of learning cannot be re-
duced to input-output behavior measured solely by sam-
ple losses or distributional differences, as discussed in
Sect. IV B. Instead, it requires an analysis of the evolv-
ing sensitivity of a model’s internal representations to
differences in input-output pairs.
As in generic non-
equilibrium many-body systems, these are described as
susceptibilities [63].
Naturally, the driving forces generated by the training
process increase with the discrepancies between inputs
and outputs, and diminish when the inputs and outputs
are identical. The question is which of the model’s pa-
rameters, or which collective variables of the model’s pa-
rameters, are susceptible to these forces; only then are
the learning dynamics actually changed by the forces.
In the following sections, we will focus on the repre-
sentation dynamics of the most constrained part of the
model: the bottleneck. This will help us understand how
the compressed representation of the input space is gen-
erated, thereby providing an explanation for the evolving
reconstruction capabilities of autoencoders.
1.
Recursive (Self-Application) Dynamics in Latent Space
The recursive application of the auto-encoder model
to its own output creates a dynamical system, where
the output is considered the input of the next recur-
sion (time) step.
We call this ‘recursion dynamics’ in
the following.
This can be considered an intrinsic dy-
namic, as it is not dependent on any external degrees
of freedom — such as the evaluation and feedback of a
loss function, which defines training via the backpropa-
gation algorithm. However, the first step depends on the
prediction error used during training, i.e, the difference
between the input representation and the predicted out-
put representation. If we had no prediction error, there
would be no dynamics.
Importantly, these dynamics in the input/output space
can also be mapped to any layer between input and out-
put. This is done by mapping both the input and the
output using only the initial part of the autoencoder
model to obtain a latent-space representation (i.e., us-
ing only the encoder to obtain the bottleneck represen-
tation). We can think of the arrows pointing from the
representations at the input to those at the output as a
flow field that manifests within the respective represen-
tation space (i.e., the bottleneck space). This flow field
describes the changes between the input and output rep-
resentations, which is the main quantity that defines the
loss (acting as an order parameter of the learning sys-
tem). In this view, the learning dynamics correspond to
modifying this flow field, hence applying a force field to
it, i.e., external driving.
Our aim is to compare representational changes dur-
ing training to the evolving recursion dynamics at fixed
checkpoints.
The intrinsic recursion dynamics can say
something about the dynamical state of the model at
these checkpoints, i.e., fundamentally about the process
of learning. We sample the flow field, which is an intrinsic
dynamic of the system in the absence of external driving.
Alterations in the flow field during training reveal the ef-
fects caused by the force field. Thus, the dynamic self-
organization of model representations is illuminated via
a depiction of forced alterations to intrinsic flow topol-
ogy. In the following, we sample this flow field on vali-
dation samples to reveal such topological–dynamical fea-
tures associated with concept formation. For example,
fixed points and attractors relate to coarse-graining and
compression occurring inside the model.

Gen-

across all training data points. The norm must therefore
decrease during learning, i.e., the flow field must be at-

The latter also explains why the loss itself

In latent space representations, there is no direct con-
nection between the MSD value and the flow field mag-
nitudes due to the scale invariance and non-linearity of
ReLU networks.
However, it is possible to relate the
topologies of trajectories reflected by the flow field be-
tween any two representation spaces, as long as one of
them is a deterministic function of the other (e.g., be-
tween layers).
Theoretically, all recursion dynamics in a finite space
must relax/converge towards a limit cycle, where they
oscillate forever, or become stationary at one of possi-
bly multiple limiting fixed points. At each moment in
training, every input is thus either on a relaxation tra-
jectory towards such a limit, part of a limit cycle, or itself
the corresponding fixed point. The different dynamical
regimes observed in the input space necessarily translate
into the dynamics of internal model representations (i.e.,
the bottleneck representation). Although different inputs
may result in the same latent representation, the same
latent representation cannot result in different outputs
under a deterministic model. This is because any differ-
ence in the output must propagate through the preceding
layers. Therefore, when scanning across representation
layers, fixed points, cycles, and relaxing trajectories re-
main what they are under recursion. In the following, we
will refer to these without calling the ‘input’ or ‘latent’
space, as they are equivalent in this regard.
We choose to analyze these behaviors in the lowest-
dimensional latent space at the bottleneck, where the
network has the minimum width. As a result, the rele-
vant dynamics are all compressed into the few available
dimensions, making it easier to visualize a larger part.
The initial recursion dynamics, at step 0 in Fig. 12,
are dominated by immediate convergence of all inputs
towards a single fixed point.

learning.
In step 68, the representation drifts along one dimen-

data (orange line in Fig. 4) and the coarse-grained spin
averages (Fig. 6). The recursive dynamics move points

(large dots), these seem to be ordered with respect to
the input magnetization. There is a global drift toward
higher magnetization, while maintaining the topological
ordering. Thus, magnetization is not a stable ‘absolute’
concept; it is overestimated in the output.
One could
say that magnetization is a stable ‘topological’ concept,
as the corresponding neighborhood structure is preserved
in the last representation after the drift (from the crosses
to big circles). It remains an open question if the stabil-

After step 1000, the recursion expands in the second
principal component dimension, just after the trade-off
becomes visible in Fig. 6, i.e., indicating the beginning of
the energy-driven regime. As the scaling of the magneti-
zation’s learning dynamics is broken, the corresponding
recursion dynamics weaken the magnetization’s previous
topological ordering in latent space. The flow field be-
comes less confined, such that recursion traverses the la-
tent space in these two dimensions. A bit of representa-
tion drift remains. The loss of one-dimensional confine-
ment coincides with an expansion of the joint concept
distribution between magnetization and energy in out-
put space. This highlights the transferability of explana-
tions involving topology – from the latent space to other
spaces, such as the output.
In step 10 000, the drift disappears, and the trajecto-
ries expand along the second dimension. This happens as
additional symmetries are broken, which resolve increas-
ingly smaller spatial domains in the output representa-
tion (see Fig. 5). At the same time, outputs are shifted
to higher energies, which require this resolution (see Fig.
10).
In the final stage (step 100 000), loops begin to emerge
in the trajectories. We assume that the underlying flow
field again gets confined, limiting the spread of latent
representations (in the visible dimensions). The distri-
butions of the MSD between initial points and recursion
points (Fig. 31) seem to shift toward higher values, sug-
gesting that the loops might extend into the additional
latent dimensions.
Generally, over training time, the decoder starts re-
constructing more relevant input features from the en-
coded representation, i.e., more latent representations
can be differentiated, possibly enhancing reconstruction
loss.
An increasing number of dimensions is retained,
i.e., there is no collapse, resulting in more consistent
and stable representations under recursions. This implies
at least transient stability of these represented concepts,
which carves out the foundation for their emergence and
stabilization during learning.
Additionally, we conjecture that, during training, the
flow field norm decreases in large areas around the train-
ing data due to the smoothness and Lipschitz bounded-
ness of the input-output function. As a result, we expect
a slowing of recursion dynamics viewed in the output
space as the loss vanishes. The slowdown in output space
could be reflected in smaller relative steps in the latent
space (only relative, given the scale invariance of ReLU
networks).
2.
Stability of Ising Concept Representations under
Recursive Flow
From Fig. 12, we expect that, between steps 68-1000,
points closer together in latent space begin to corre-
spond to samples with more similar magnetization. This
relation should weaken after step 1000, as the energy-
dominated regime sets in, in which trajectories expand
into the second displayed dimension. However, the prior
analysis was limited to the visible dimensions.
The
Spearman rank correlation allows us to quantify this
‘topological ordering’ in all dimensions by measuring the
monotonicity of the relationship between the representa-
tion distance in latent space and the magnetization dif-
ference of the input samples. Whenever the topology of
samples in latent space reflects an ordering by a concept,
the correlation is high; this means that samples with the
same value of the observable are closer than those with
different values. We refer to the existence of such an or-
der as ‘topological ordering’. We can also measure the
topological ordering of other concepts, such as energy.
By analyzing this correlation along the recursion dy-
namics, one can distinguish the entangled effects of the
encoder and the decoder on the representation topology:
At recursion index 0, the encoder produces the represen-
tation, while the decoder has no influence on the resulting
correlation. With additional recursion steps, the decoder
can alter the structure of the latent space representation

and, therefore, its ‘topological ordering’ with respect to
the concept of interest (i.e., the input magnetization).
Together, these effects create the flow field that describes
the recursion dynamics. Thus, the Spearman correlation
across recursive steps can probe the topological changes
induced by the flow field at a fixed checkpoint.
Examining how the Spearman correlation varies across
training checkpoints enables us to understand how the
force fields modify the flow field and, consequently, the
recursion dynamics.
As described above, we can gain
insights into the interaction between the encoder and de-
coder as they work to evolve the latent representation
space.
We apply recursion for up to 20 times, on the pairs
sampled from the validation set. The results are visual-
ized in Fig. 12. Fig. 13 visualizes the Spearman corre-
lation between representation pairs and their magnetiza-
tion. Visible are drastic transitions in the latent space
encoding over training time, as well as under recursion
in the initial stages of learning. Initially, the Spearman
rank correlation is non-stable under recursion, visible in
the bottom-left corner of the plot area, before becoming
more stable under the recursive dynamics. This happens
concurrently with a drop in the optimized loss and the
related magnetization loss. It remains more stable dur-
ing the training period when the optimized loss plateaus,
indicating a transition state with stable topological or-
dering, at least with respect to the intrinsic dynamics of
recursion. As suggested by the confined flow trajectories
(the one-dimensional region in Step 68 of Fig. 12), the
magnetization is only learned after its topological order-
ing fully develops in latent space. Additionally, successful
learning of the magnetization concept comes after stabi-
lizing its latent space topology in an intrinsic sense (under
recursion). At the same time, the trade-off between av-
erages of spins on different scales becomes visible in Fig.
6; the correlation suddenly decreases just as the magne-
tization loss starts to increase.
After Step 68, the optimization loss decreases, as re-
construction on smaller scales continues to improve; the
energy-dominated learning regime begins at step 1000
(see Fig. 9), where the optimization loss becomes mini-
mal. At this transition, the magnetization-based topolog-
ical ordering destabilizes. Theoretically, this transition
requires additional degrees of freedom: the new regime is
dominated by a cascade of symmetry breaks, which are
necessary to resolve small-scale domains (see Fig. 5). A
more subtle dissolution of the topological ordering under
recursion is visible around step 10 000 in both the latent
space flow (a general spreading and final drift into the
upper right corner in Fig. 12) and the Spearman correla-
tion (a transitory recursion instability in Fig. 13).
The Spearman correlation with respect to energy (Ap-
pendix Fig. 37) is generally significantly lower compared
to the magnetization. It is only high in the regime where
the latent space is almost perfectly ordered with respect
to magnetization. Even in latent space, the magnetiza-
tion is an initial proxy for the energy, extending our find-
ing in Sect. IV B 4 to the internal representation space.
As the symmetry breaks occur, this proxy relation is
no longer valid, and the Spearman correlation of energy
drops rapidly, even becoming weakly negative. This sug-
gests that energy is not represented by a separate latent
space dimension, but rather by a collective property of
correlations at different scales.
V.
DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
In the preceding sections, we studied how unsupervised
autoencoders trained on microscopic spin configurations
from the Ising model learn macroscopic, theory-relevant
variables like magnetization and energy. These collective
observables not only describe the data-generating pro-
cess but also turn out to provide effective descriptions of
the learning dynamics. Generalizing on this, we propose
the idea that learning should become more understand-
able as a physical phenomenon when treating the ‘grand’
complex dynamical system coupling (and decoupling) dif-
ferent subsystems: (1) the data-generating and -feeding
processes, which cause the fluctuations in the struc-
tural and statistical properties of the data (and include
the data-shuffling and subsampling); (2) the optimizer,
which causes memory-dependent susceptibility; (3) and
the model state, which becomes dynamical through feed-
back in the update (backpropagation) process.
The analyses we proposed here reflect this idea by link-
ing the formation of physical representations with dy-
namical properties during learning, such as transition
states, scaling dynamics, and dynamical arrest.
In order to mimic a typical discovery scenario, we
embedded no prior domain knowledge into the model
parametrization or optimization algorithm, such as the
symmetries of the Ising model. Instead, any such knowl-
edge was used to analyze the learning of physically mean-
ingful observables (concepts) and to identify connections
between the physics and dynamics of the training data,
and the learning dynamics; these insights can potentially
generalize to settings where the generative process of the
data is unknown.
To enable a clearer attribution of visible effects to spe-
cific hyperparameters, we conducted an ablation study,
which revealed that specific effects occurred only with
sufficient depth and were influenced by model width and
learning rate.
We started by showing different dynamical regimes in
the optimization loss (as seen in plateaus and transi-
tions in Fig. 4) that depend on both model architecture
(depth, width, bottleneck size) and optimization hyper-
parameters, i.e., learning rate. There, we observed that
deeper models were less effective at learning good repre-
sentations (low validation loss) and often failed to resolve
input differences at higher learning rates. Additionally,
we found that larger widths and bottleneck sizes can ad-
versely affect the performance in this deep model regime.
We attributed this behavior to the increased mixing of

Thereafter, we visualized the evolution of reconstruc-

serve how generalization errors ‘look’; we observed over-
fitting behavior for small bottleneck sizes. We saw how
deep models get trapped in an arrested regime: They re-

resenting a dataset-average independent of the given in-
put (Fig. 22). We provided a heuristic explanation for
this arrested regime based on the ruggedness of loss land-
scapes and their effect on relaxation dynamics. This en-
riches the existing theoretical literature, which merely
marks this regime as ‘chaotic’, without specifying the
details that could induce this behavior.
For success-
fully trained models, we observed in model outputs that
learning begins by reconstructing globally uniform values
matching the pixel mode or mean before progressively
reconstructing smaller-scale features. We conclude that
collective features are learned prior to individual details,
but then involve trade-offs. Furthermore, we hypothe-
size that layer width and depth mix independent fluctu-
ations, which usually adds noise to small-scale features.
However, this analysis is limited to a few samples.
We quantified learning on multiple spatial coarse-
graining scales by using a microscopic definition of local
magnetization (spin) and energy (average spin gradient),
which we averaged across progressively larger square ar-
eas.
The resulting scale-dependent loss evolution re-
vealed two distinct dynamical regimes in which either
the magnetization or the energy losses decrease mono-
tonically across all scales. The transition between these
regimes is controlled by the model depth, width, and
learning rate.
These regimes were also identified with
learning the respective concepts: first, the magnetiza-
tion (an average scalar value), and then the energy (an

average gradient).
For shallow models, this transition
coincides with a representational trade-off, marked by a
global minimum of the large-scale losses. As this trough
is lifted with increasing bottleneck size, we conclude that
the bottleneck constraint is the cause of the trade-off.
The trade-off is between representing the large scales,
for approximating many samples using shared collective
features, and the small scales, for differentiating individ-
ual samples. Deep models trained at intermediate and
fast rates become arrested across all scales and fail to
reach these regimes, which are ultimately essential for
learning a generalizing energy representation. These rep-
resent highly chaotic dynamics, reflected in their path-
dependence and greater sensitivity to combined varia-
tions in initialization, training data ordering, and batch
size.
In contrast, the sensitivity of shallower models
was two orders of magnitude lower, while being espe-
cially high during transitions in the loss and low dur-
ing plateaus. Interestingly, the scale-invariant dynamical
regimes coincide with the generalization of the related
physical concepts. Lastly, the very deep models exhibit
largely the same run-to-run variance across all scales, in-
dicating a mode collapse. For the deep models that fail
to learn, the run variance completely vanishes in the final
stage, as they all predict the same dataset average.
To provide a more detailed picture of the representa-
tion of magnetization and energy in the output space,
we studied the evolution of each observable error via
joint input vs. output distribution. The distributions re-
vealed that the output energy is initially fully determined
by the magnetization and becomes degenerate only as
smaller scales are resolved. The symmetry-breaking re-
quired to reconstruct the energy only begins after the
magnetization is learned. These symmetry-breaking ef-
fects manifest as a shift to higher output energies. How-
ever, the output energies remain below an upper limit,
i.e., there is an energy cutoff, which we claim is influ-
enced by a bottleneck-dependent scale-resolution trade-
off. This resolution dependence is particularly evident
in the much higher error at the highest energies, where
small spin domains dominate. This interpretation is fur-
ther supported by the evolution of individual samples,
the scale-dependent losses, and the observation that the
energy cutoff shifted to higher energies when the bottle-
neck was expanded. In general, the evolution of these dis-
tributions appears to be highly dependent on the learning
rate and model depth.
Finally, we used a novel analysis of recursive-dynamic
trajectories to explain how prediction errors induce flow
fields that produce a common trajectory topology across
all representation spaces, e.g., latent and output, en-
abling explanations in one space to generalize to oth-
ers.
This flow field provides a physical intuition for
the prediction error, with the average norm across the
dataset corresponding to the loss; it is an effective or-
der parameter of the learning dynamics. We then im-
plicitly studied how the force field describing the learn-
ing dynamics changes this flow field during training via
recursive trajectories. The recursion dynamics revealed
topological stability of the magnetization in latent space,
which exhibited drift while the absolute concept was
still being learned. After the concept had been success-
fully learned, the drift became vanishingly small. In the
energy-dominated regime, trajectories expand into ad-
ditional latent-space dimensions, partially breaking the
magnetization concept. This leads to worse generaliza-
tion for magnetization and better generalization for the
energy.
The intrinsic dynamical properties generated by recur-
sive self-application provide a novel means to study learn-
ing as forces acting on flow fields. Systemic aspects, such
as stability and consistency, arise from properties of the
flow field and its responsiveness to forces (i.e., dynamic
susceptibility).
Other properties we have not yet dis-
cussed, such as memory, can also be investigated in this
framework, thereby offering an analogy to dynamical-
systems approaches to ML, such as reservoir computing
[64]. While the recursive dynamics require inputs and
outputs to be compatible, the idea of flow and force fields
generalizes beyond auto-encoders.
The Spearman correlation analysis highlights the hid-
den role of the intrinsic stability of concepts during re-
cursion dynamics. Though the latter differ significantly
from training dynamics, they reveal sharply transient and
metastable states visited during training and learning:
The formation of representations corresponding to the
magnetization is pinpointed with transitions from non-
or metastable to more stable representations, and the
energy-dominated regime sets in with a sharp drop in
the topological ordering of the energy concept under re-
cursion.
Such transitions are characteristic of complex non-
equilibrium phase transformation dynamics. These dy-
namical correspondences between machine learning and
physical phases form the basis for an analogy that
grounds artificial intelligence in physics. In principle, this
correspondence should extend to other complex systems,
as well, such as neuronal networks (brains). The emer-
gence of physical concepts in complex systems, as demon-
strated, can inspire cognitive architectures and lead to
new connections between human understanding and the
functioning of artificial intelligence.
Scaling dynamical behavior and insensitivity to certain
control parameters could suggest an affinity for a self-
organized critical point, for example [65–68]. Indeed, it
has been previously proposed that optimal computation
or operation in complex systems may be at the ‘edge of
criticality’ or ‘edge of chaos’ [69, 70]. Quantitatively, the
question of concept stability could be addressed through
the flow-field analysis we presented.
Although it is not clear up front what may be ‘optimal’
about a learning machine that exhibits scale-bridging be-
havior, we at least demonstrated an empirical connection.
On the one hand, it indicates the relevance of all scales for
accurate reconstruction. On the other hand, capturing a
broad bandwidth may signify how flexibility is an evolv-


## Page 29


29
ing functional property. The initial preference for large
scales could reflect the data distribution bias, which asso-
ciates high energies with a low probability of small-scale
features. Therefore, the energy is a proxy for the ratio
of small- to large-scale features, defining the represen-
tational trade-off between scales that characterizes the
learning dynamics. On a more general level, the associa-
tion of learning to physical concepts that define the data
distribution is a potentially powerful shift in perspective.
As we sought to identify hyperparameters that facil-
itate learning, we had to face the fact that there is no
such thing as a ‘free lunch’ [71]. As we fine-tune more
hyperparameters for a specific purpose (e.g., compressing
Ising model configurations), we should expect the class
of tasks for which the learning algorithm and the final
model perform well to shrink. We should therefore ask
ourselves whether we want a general algorithm/model
that works well across a broad set of tasks or a special-
ized one that performs very well.
The former enables
reusability across tasks and saves time/energy by avoid-
ing the need to recreate a tailored algorithm/model from
scratch. The latter saves time and energy per use at the
expense of reusability. As so often, the optimal choice
depends on context.
In this study, we built on the intuition that learning
often operates as a process driven far from equilibrium
by fluctuations in the training data, whose effects are
mediated by the memory-dependent susceptibility con-
trolled by the optimizer. We showed how this perspec-
tive explains the observed representation and optimiza-
tion dynamics, thereby justifying an effective descrip-
tion in terms of collective coarse-grained physical ob-
servables underlying the data-generating process. That
these observables are relevant underlines the collective
nature of the learning process [72]. Furthermore, this al-
lows insights to be transferred between domains, refram-
ing the machine learning opacity problem from a fixed
outcome (‘machine’) towards its dynamical origins (its
‘learning’ process). This is also useful for crafting algo-
rithms that more directly exploit physical mechanisms.
Lastly, our ideas suggest that automated scientific dis-
covery with artificial intelligence may rest on principles
of self-organization; Complex learning systems exposed
to data of the physical world can encounter relevant ab-
stractions of it. An extension to dynamical datasets rep-
resenting real processes is subject to future investigation.
