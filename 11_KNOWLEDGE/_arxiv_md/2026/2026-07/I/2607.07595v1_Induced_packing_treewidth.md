INDUCED PACKING TREEWIDTH
AMIR NIKABADI† AND PAWEŁ RZĄŻEWSKI∥
Abstract. In this paper, we introduce a framework that aims to unify classes defined
by forbidden induced subgraphs or induced minors with classes defined by the existence
of certain structured tree decompositions.
Let H be a fixed family of graphs.
We
define induced-H-packing treewidth, a tree-decomposition-based graph parameter that,
for each bag, measures the maximum number of pairwise anticomplete induced copies
of graphs from H intersecting that bag. This notion generalizes some previously studied
parameters: when H = {P1}, it is equivalent to tree-independence number, and when
H = {P2}, it is equivalent to induced matching treewidth.
We show that bounded induced-H-packing treewidth yields new algorithmic conse-
quences for a range of choices of H. In particular, we prove the following results for
graphs of bounded induced-H-packing treewidth.
• If H contains P4, then Maximum-Weight Independent Set (MWIS) can be
solved in quasipolynomial time.
• If H contains P5, then List 3-Coloring can be solved in quasipolynomial time.
• If H contains P3, then Odd Cycle Transversal and several other problems
can be solved in quasipolynomial time. Furthermore, there is a quasipolynomial-
time approximation scheme (QPTAS) for finding a largest induced subgraph of
bounded treewidth that satisfies a given hereditary CMSO2 formula.
• If H contains all cycles or any path, then MWIS admits a QPTAS.
These results partially answer and substantially extend a question of Bodlaender,
Fomin, and Korhonen [SODA 2026] on the tractability of MWIS for graphs of bounded
induced-H-packing treewidth for H = {P3} and for H equal to the family of all cycles.

1. Introduction
Among classic computational graph problems, Max Independent Set (and its
weighted variant) and 3-Coloring (and its list variant) are arguably two of the best
studied ones. In the Max Weight Independent Set (MWIS) problem, we are given
a graph G and a weight function w : V (G) →Q≥0, and the goal is to find an independent
set (i.e., a set of pairwise non-adjacent vertices) of maximum total weight. In the List
3-Coloring problem, we are given a graph G and a list assignment L : V (G) →2{1,2,3},
and the goal is to find a proper coloring of G such that each vertex v is colored with a color
from its list L(v). Both problems are among Karp’s 21 NP-hard [42] problems and remain
hard for many notions of hardness considered in complexity theory [13,19,26,36,41,43,47].
This persistent hardness naturally leads to a central question in algorithmic graph theory:
Which restrictions on the input instances make MWIS and List 3-Coloring tractable?
Forbidden induced subgraphs.
Over the years, significant attention has been given
to studying classes of instances defined by forbidding certain induced subgraphs. For
graphs G and H, we say that G is H-free if G does not contain an induced subgraph
isomorphic to H. For a family of graphs H, we say that G is H-free if G is H-free for
every H ∈H. A special role in the paper is played by classes excluding a fixed path, or,
more generally, a fixed linear forest, i.e., a forest of paths. By Pt we denote the path on t
vertices. For an integer k and a graph H, by kH we denote the disjoint union of k copies
of H.
Despite the extensive study of MWIS in H-free graphs, its complexity is still not fully
understood. It is known that the problem is NP-hard in H-free graphs unless H is a
forest of subdivided claws [4] – a subcubic forest in which every component has at most
one vertex of degree 3 (let us call this family S). On the other hand, polynomial-time
algorithms are known only for very restricted cases [11,33,49–51,57]. However, there is a
belief in the community that the problem is actually tractable once we exclude any graph
from S. This belief is supported by the existence of quasipolynomial-time algorithms.
Indeed, such algorithms were first found for the case that H is a linear forest [28,56], and
later extended to any H ∈S [29].
Note that the existence of a quasipolynomial-time algorithm for a given problem is a
strong indication that this problem is not NP-hard, as otherwise every problem in NP
could be solved in quasipolynomial time.
For List 3-Coloring, the situation is somewhat similar, though the boundary line
between easy and hard cases lies in a different place. On the one hand, List 3-Coloring
in H-free graphs is NP-hard unless H is a linear forest [24, 40, 45]. On the other hand,
polynomial-time algorithms are known only for restricted cases [10,15,44], but the prob-
lem can be solved in quasipolynomial time in H-free graphs, for every linear forest H [56].
Quite interestingly, the quasipolynomial-time algorithms for the MWIS and the List
3-Coloring in graphs excluding a fixed linear forest follow essentially the same ap-
proach. Furthermore, an extension of this approach can be used to solve a certain family
of wide generalizations of MWIS in the graph classes considered here [30].
For a fixed integer r and fixed CMSO2 formula1 ψ, by (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS we denote
the problem where we are given a graph G with vertex-weights, and we want to find a

This formalism captures a wide range of problems, including MWIS, Feedback Ver-

It turns out that in many cases tractability results for MWIS in graphs excluding a
linear forest can be generalized to (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS, for any r and ψ [2,16]. In particu-
lar, every problem expressible with this formalism can be solved in quasipolynomial time
in H-free graphs, for every linear forest H [30]. We remark that such a generalization is
not possible if H is not a linear forest, in particular for H ∈S. Indeed, already Feed-
back Vertex Set is NP-hard in claw-free graphs, where the claw is the three-leaf star
K1,3 [52].
Forbidden induced minors.
What makes classes excluding a linear forest so special?
It turns out that the answer to this question can be given in terms of induced minors. We
say that H is an induced minor of G if it can be obtained from G by removing vertices and
contracting edges, and G is H-induced-minor-free if H is not an induced minor of G. Note
that if H is a linear forest, then the classes of H-free graphs and H-induced-minor-free
graphs coincide, and this property holds only for linear forests.
Motivated by these observations, Gartland and Lokshtanov (see the doctoral thesis of
Gartland [27]) posed the following conjecture.
Conjecture 1.1 (Gartland and Lokshtanov [27]). Let H be a planar graph. Then, for
every fixed r and CMSO2 formula ψ, (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS and List 3-Coloring can be
solved in polynomial time in H-induced-minor-free graphs.
We remark that the analogous statement cannot hold for non-planar graphs H, as
MWIS and List 3-Coloring are both NP-hard in planar graphs, which exclude every
non-planar graph as an induced minor.
Conjecture 1.1 received significant attention
in the community, but it appears very challenging. Obtaining even a quasipolynomial-
time algorithm just for MWIS, or at least a quasipolynomial-time approximation scheme
(QPTAS), already seems to be a very ambitious goal. Such results are only known for
very restricted cases [7–9,14,29,53].
Decomposition-based approaches.
A somewhat different route, complementary to
forbidding induced subgraphs or minors, is to consider structural decompositions of
graphs. Arguably, the best known example of such a decomposition is tree decompo-
sition. Intuitively, tree decomposition aims to embed a graph into a tree by mapping
vertices of the graph to bags of vertices of the tree, in a way that preserves adjacency
and connectivity. If all bags are small, say of constant size, i.e., the graph has bounded
treewidth, then the graph “resembles a tree,” and many problems can be solved efficiently
by mimicking dynamic programming algorithms on trees. A drawback of such an ap-
proach is that its applicability is somewhat limited, as only sparse graphs may have tree
decompositions with all bags small.
As a remedy, one can study structured tree decompositions, where we allow bags to
be large, but we require that they have some structure that allows us to solve problems
efficiently. For example, bounding the independence number of bags leads to the definition
of the tree-independence number, introduced independently by Yolov [58] and Dallard,
Milanič, and Štorgel [21]. Classes of graphs with small tree-independence number have
very strong structural [18,22,38,46] and algorithmic [46,48,58] properties. In particular,

A further generalization of tree-independence number, that in particular captures bi-
cliques, is the notion of induced matching treewidth, first introduced by Yolov [58] under
the name minor-matching hypertreewidth. Here, for every bag of the decomposition, we
aim to bound the size of a maximum induced matching each of whose edges intersects
the bag (we emphasize that this matching is not required to be contained in the bag).
Yolov [58] showed that MWIS, List k-Coloring for any fixed k, and some other prob-
lems can be solved in polynomial time in graphs of bounded induced matching treewidth.
Later, these results were extended to more problems, including, in particular, Feedback
Vertex Set, by Lima, Milanič, Muršič, Okrasa, Rzążewski, and Štorgel [46]. They also
conjectured that for every fixed r and formula ψ, (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS can be solved in
polynomial time in graphs of bounded induced matching treewidth. This conjecture was
recently confirmed by Bodlaender, Fomin, and Korhonen [6].
In the conclusion of their paper, Bodlaender, Fomin, and Korhonen [6] discussed sev-
eral possible extensions of their result and, in particular, posed the following intriguing
question about possible further generalizations of tree-independence number and induced
matching treewidth.
Question 1.1 (Bodlaender, Fomin, and Korhonen [6]). Is MWIS polynomial-time solv-
able if the instance is given along with a tree decomposition where every bag is intersected
by a bounded number of pairwise disjoint and anticomplete P3s, or cycles?
1.1. Our contribution. We introduce a framework that aims to generalize both routes
discussed above: forbidding induced subgraphs and using tree decompositions with struc-
ture imposed on bags, and in particular to answer Question 1.1.
Induced H-packing treewidth.
Let H be a family of graphs. An induced H-packing
in a graph G is a collection of pairwise anticomplete induced subgraphs of G, each iso-
morphic to a graph in H. For a tree decomposition of G, its induced H-packing number
is the maximum size of an induced H-packing whose every element intersects a common
bag of the decomposition. Then, induced H-packing treewidth of G, denoted tree-πH(G),
is the minimum induced H-packing number over all tree decompositions of G. If H is a
singleton, say, H = {H}, we abbreviate the notation and simply write induced H-packing
and tree-πH(G).
Using the terminology just introduced, tree-independence number is exactly tree-πP1
and induced matching treewidth is exactly tree-πP2. Furthermore, Question 1.1 concerns
graphs of bounded tree-πP3 or tree-πC, where C is the family of all cycles.
On the other hand, the notion of bounded induced H-packing treewidth can be seen
as a “local relaxation” of forbidding certain induced substructures. Consider for example
H = {P3}, as suggested in Question 1.1. Graphs G with tree-πP3 ≤k −1 may have
many pairwise anticomplete P3s, but no k of them intersect a single bag of some tree
decomposition of G. Thus, these graphs are, in some sense, “locally kP3-free,” and they
generalize kP3-free graphs [25, 55]. Similarly, graphs G with tree-πC(G) ≤k −1 are a
local relaxation of kC3-induced-minor-free graphs [7,53].

We address Question 1.1 and more generally, we study the al-
gorithmic consequences of bounded induced packing treewidth for various choices of H.

Next, we show a number of algorithmic results in the case that P3 ∈H.

graph (G, w) and the goal is to find a minimum-weight set of vertices whose removal makes

We show the following result
concerning “locally kP3-free” graphs.
Theorem 1.4. Let H be a family of graphs containing a path with at most 3 vertices.
For every fixed k, given a vertex-weighted graph (G, w), in time nO(log2 n) one can either
solve OCT in (G, w), or correctly report that tree-πH(G) > k.
Next, using the technique of blob graphs [30], we show that for any fixed r and formula
ψ, unweighted (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS admits a QPTAS in graphs of bounded tree-πP3, pro-
vided that the property imposed by ψ is hereditary, i.e., closed under vertex deletion and
disjoint unions.
Theorem 1.5. Let H be a family of graphs containing a path with at most 3 vertices.
Let k, r ≥0, ε ∈(0, 1) be a real, and ψ be a hereditary CMSO2 formula. Given a graph
G, in quasipolynomial time one can compute one of the following outputs:
• a solution to (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS of size at least (1 −ε) times the optimum;
• a correct conclusion that no solution to (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS exists; or
• a correct conclusion that tree-πH(G) > k.
Dominated balanced separators.
Now, let us move to a somewhat more general
setting in which H contains a fixed path or all cycles, as in Question 1.1. We prove that
in both cases, every graph of bounded tree-πH has a small set X of vertices, such that
N[X], i.e., the set consisting of X and its neighbors, is a balanced separator.
Theorem 1.6. Let H be a family of graphs. Let G be an n-vertex graph with tree-πH(G) ≤
k. There is a set X ⊆V (G) such that every component of G −N[X] has at most n/2
vertices, where
(1) |X| = O(kt) if H contains a path on t vertices, and
(2) |X| = O(k3 log n) if H contains all cycles.

Computing a decomposition.
Finally, let us discuss the problem of computing a tree
decomposition of bounded induced packing treewidth. It turns out that a simple argu-
ment allows us to reduce this problem to the problem of computing a tree decomposition
with bounded independence number, which in turn can be done using the algorithm of
Dallard, Fomin, Golovach, Korhonen, and Milanič [20]. Summing up, we obtain the fol-
lowing result, which can be seen as a constant-factor XP-approximation algorithm under
the natural parameterization.
Theorem 1.8. Let H ̸= ∅be a fixed finite family of connected graphs. Given an n-vertex
graph G and an integer k, in time 2O(k2)nO(k) one can either output a tree decomposition
of G with induced H-packing number at most 8k, or correctly report that tree-πH(G) > k.
One may wonder whether Theorem 1.8 can be improved to, say, a fixed-parameter
or exact algorithm. This turns out to be unlikely, under standard complexity-theoretic
assumptions. Indeed, another simple reduction allows us to reuse known hardness results
for computing tree-independence number [20,21], yielding the following lower bounds.
Theorem 1.9. Let H ̸= ∅be a fixed finite family of connected graphs.
(1) It is NP-hard to decide whether a given graph G satisfies tree-πH(G) ≤4.
(2) It is W[1]-hard to approximate induced H-packing treewidth within any constant
factor.
(3) Assuming Gap-ETH, there is no f(k) · no(k)-time g(k)-approximation algorithm for
induced H-packing treewidth, for any computable functions f and g.

2. Technical overview
Let us give a high-level overview of our work.
The main message is that induced
packing treewidth provides a common language for two familiar sources of tractability in
algorithmic graph theory: excluding induced structures and imposing structure on tree
decompositions. This viewpoint yields quasipolynomial-time algorithms for several basic

problems as well as structural consequences, and the proofs are organized around a small
number of recurring ideas. In what follows, let G denote the instance graph and let n be
the number of its vertices.
Container lemmas.
The algorithmic core of the paper is a pair of “container lemmas,”
one for MWIS and one for List 3-Coloring, that allow us to clean the graph with
respect to a given bag of a tree decomposition of bounded induced-H-packing treewidth.
Conceptually, these lemmas isolate the difficult interaction with one bag and replace it
with a quasipolynomial-size family of residual instances that are easier to recurse on. For
simplicity, let us assume that H = {H}, where |V (H)| = h, but the lemma works for any
fixed finite family H of graphs.
Both container lemmas follow the same general idea, so let us describe the case of
MWIS, i.e., Lemma 4.1, in more detail. Consider a tree decomposition of G witnessing
that tree-πH(G) ≤k, where k is a constant, and let B be a bag of this tree decomposition.
We show that one can enumerate a quasipolynomial-size family Fα of pairs (I, F), where
I is an independent set and F ⊆V (G) \ N[I], such that:
(1) for each (I, F) ∈Fα, the graph G[F] has no induced copy of H that intersects
B ∩F, and
(2) for every independent set I∗in G there is a pair (I, F) ∈Fα such that I ⊆I∗⊆
I ∪F.
The second property implies that, in order to solve MWIS in G, it is sufficient to solve
MWIS for G[F], for every (I, F) ∈Fα. Indeed, denoting by IF the maximum-weight
independent set in G[F], the optimum solution has weight equal to the maximum weight
of a set I ∪IF, taken over all (I, F) ∈Fα.
Let us say a few words about the proof of Lemma 4.1. Essentially, the idea is inspired
by the quasipolynomial-time algorithm for MWIS in Pt-free graphs [56]: we try to guess
I∗by branching on carefully chosen vertices, and stop the search once the first property
is satisfied. The role of bounded induced packing treewidth is to provide a measure of
progress: we do not branch blindly, but rather branch so as to eliminate many induced
copies of H that still insist on touching the chosen bag B.
Consider the family U of vertex sets of all largest induced H-packings in G in which
every copy of H intersects B. Since B is a bag of a tree decomposition witnessing that
tree-πH(G) ≤k, we observe that every member of U has at most kh vertices, and thus,
U has size at most nhk.
Now, consider the vertex set X of an induced copy of H that intersects B. Note that
N[X], i.e., the set consisting of X and its neighbors, intersects every set U ∈U, as
otherwise we could find a larger H-packing. Since |X| = h, where h is a constant, there
is a vertex x ∈X such that N[x] intersects a constant fraction of all elements of U. We
branch on x, i.e., create one instance corresponding to putting x into I∗and the other one
corresponding to excluding x from I∗. Note that in the first branch we can remove N[x]
from the graph, thus shrinking U significantly. Since the size of U is polynomial in n,
we conclude that the recursion tree has nO(log n) leaves, and we return the corresponding
instances as members of Fα: I denotes the vertices guessed to belong to I∗, and F denotes
the vertices that were not discarded in the process.
The container lemma for List 3-Coloring, i.e., Lemma 4.2, is shown similarly, and
it enumerates a family Fχ of instances (G′, L′) of List 3-Coloring, where G′ is an
induced subgraph of G, such that

Summing up, both container lemmas reduce the problem to the case that no copy of H
intersects one chosen bag B. This yields a common recursive template: clean the graph
around B, solve recursively the components that lie away from B, and use additional
structure to handle the components that still touch B. From this point on, the whole
story is about understanding what this remaining structure can look like for different
choices of H and different optimization problems.
A well-known property of tree decompositions is that there is always a central bag B
such that every component of G −B has at most |V (G)|/2 vertices. Thus, we apply
the appropriate container lemma for the central bag B and consider each component
of a member of the returned family F (either Fα or Fχ, depending on the problem)
independently. We distinguish two types of such components: those that do not intersect
B, and those that do. By the choice of B, the components of the first type are small and
can be solved recursively. This leaves only the components that intersect B.
MWIS and List 3-Coloring in graphs with bounded tree-πP3.
The cleanest setting
for this template is bounded induced P3-packing treewidth, as suggested by Question 1.1.
In this case, dealing with components that intersect B is easy, as we can show that such
components are complete graphs (see Lemma 7.1).
Thus, bounded tree-πP3 serves as the warm-up case for our framework: once the com-
ponents touching B collapse to cliques, the recursion closes immediately. For MWIS, this
already resolves (in a weaker form, as we get a quasipolynomial-time, not a polynomial-
time algorithm) the P3 side of the motivating question of Bodlaender, Fomin, and Korho-
nen, and for both MWIS and List 3-Coloring it shows in the cleanest possible form
how the framework is meant to work. We can then solve the problem in quasipolynomial
time by applying the appropriate container lemma to the central bag B and solving each
component of the returned instances either recursively (if it does not intersect B) or
directly (if it does). The overall running time of both algorithms is nO(log2 n), where one
log n in the exponent comes from the recursion in the container lemma, and the other
log n comes from the recursion on components that do not intersect B. A similar running
time appears in all our main results, namely Theorems 1.2 to 1.4.
Going beyond P3: Theorems 1.2 and 1.3.
The real challenge is to make the same
paradigm work once the components of the returned instances that intersect B are no
longer trivial. This already happens for longer paths, and this is where the framework
has to interact in a serious way with graph structure rather than merely exposing it.
In particular, in the proof of Theorem 1.2, i.e., for the case of MWIS in graphs of
bounded induced P4-packing treewidth, we use modular decomposition, which allows us
to reduce the problem to the case that the graph is prime (see Section 5). Then, we use
the result of Hayward, Hougardy, and Reed [37] that if G is prime, then either it has a
simple structure (namely, it is a split graph) and thus MWIS can be solved in polynomial
time, or every vertex is contained in an induced P4. This is exactly the kind of dichotomy
that fits the container viewpoint: either the cleaned instance lands in a tractable class,
or induced P4’s remain ubiquitous enough to force further progress. Combining this with
the container lemma for MWIS, we conclude that the problem can be decomposed into

Back to P3: Odd Cycle Transversal (Theorem 1.4).
Returning to the case of
H = P3, we next consider the OCT problem. This is where the framework must cope
with a solution consisting of two interacting parts rather than one, so the warm-up case
no longer closes for free. Note that it is equivalent to finding a maximum-weight induced
bipartite subgraph of a given graph. In other words, the goal is to find two disjoint
independent sets X1, X2 of maximum total weight; we will adapt this viewpoint.
The actual proof is more delicate than in the previous P3-based arguments, because
we must control two independent sets simultaneously. In particular, the difficulty is no
longer to describe one good residual instance, but to synchronize two such descriptions.
We again apply the container lemma for MWIS to the graph G and the central bag B,
and we branch over pairs of containers, one for X1 and one for X2. For every such pair we
obtain a residual graph together with information, for each remaining vertex, on whether
it may still belong to X1, to X2, or to both. This yields two layers V1 and V2, where Vi
consists of vertices that may still be placed in Xi. In each single layer, each component
that intersects B is a clique, as in the previous P3-based arguments. However, the two
layers may interact in a nontrivial way, and the actual components of the residual graph
that intersect B may contain multiple components of each layer.
The first key observation is that each vertex in B is simplicial inside each layer, i.e.,
its neighborhood restricted to one layer is a clique. This yields a safe reduction rule for
a vertex from B that is already forced to one side: by comparing it with its same-layer
neighbors, one can simplify the instance while preserving the optimum value. The second
idea is that edges between vertices that are already forced to different sides can be safely
ignored, as they do not interfere with any feasible solution. Thus, we can break the
instance into layer-connected components – maximal subgraphs where any two vertices
are joined by a path that avoids cross-layer edges.
We show that, after exhaustive application of the reduction rule, each layer-connected
component that still intersects B is actually quite simple, since it can be covered by two
cliques. Consequently, such a component has only a few feasible choices for its intersection
with X1 and X2 and can be solved directly. As components that do not intersect B can
be solved recursively, we again obtain the running time of nO(log2 n). Conceptually, this
shows that the framework can also handle coupled solutions, not only single-set objectives
such as MWIS.

Dominated balanced separators.
We then turn to a structural consequence, namely
Theorem 1.6 on dominated balanced separators. Consider an n-vertex graph G and its
tree decomposition witnessing that tree-πH(G) ≤k.
Suppose first that H contains a path Pt.
Starting from the central bag B of the
decomposition, we take a maximum induced Pt-packing, where every path intersects
B. Let Y be the vertex set of this packing; we have |Y | = O(kt). If N[Y ] is a balanced
separator, then we are done. Otherwise, there is a unique large component G′ of G−N[Y ],
where large means that it has more than n/2 vertices. Clearly G′ intersects B, as removing
B leaves only small components. Furthermore, there is no induced Pt in G′ intersecting
B, as otherwise we could add it to the initial packing, contradicting its maximality.
Thus the only obstruction to having a balanced separator already in hand is a single
large component that still sees the central bag but no longer contains the relevant local
pattern through that bag.
We apply the well-known Gyárfás path argument [34] (see also [5]) to G′ starting from
a vertex v ∈B ∩V (G′). This yields an induced path X starting at v such that N[X] is a
balanced separator, i.e., every component of G′ −N[X] is small. Consequently, N[X ∪Y ]
is a balanced separator of G. As X has fewer than t vertices, we observe that X ∪Y has
at most O(kt) vertices. In this way, the local packing bound around one bag is converted
into a global separator by adding only one short induced path.
For the case that H contains all cycles, the overall strategy is similar, but already the
first step requires a different tool. Again, we start with the central bag B. However, at
this point we cannot take Y to be the vertex set of a maximum induced cycle packing,
as the cycles in the packing might be of unbounded length. Instead, we use the recent
result of Ahn and Kwon [3] concerning the induced Erdős–Pósa property for B-cycles, i.e.,
cycles that intersect B. They show that if we cannot pack k + 1 pairwise anticomplete
B-cycles (which is the case since B is a bag of a tree decomposition witnessing that
tree-πH(G) ≤k), then there is a set Y of O(k3) vertices such that N[Y ] intersects all
such cycles. This is exactly what we need from Y : in the cycle setting, induced Erdős–
Pósa provides a bounded hitting set where the path case used a bounded packing.

subexponential-time algorithms for List 3-Coloring [32], as well as for many problems

Computing decompositions (Theorems 1.8 and 1.9).
Finally, we address the al-
gorithmic prerequisite behind the whole framework: how to compute a tree decomposition
of bounded induced H-packing number. This is essential because all preceding algorithms
begin with such a decomposition (or at least its central bag) in hand.
First, we show that for any family H of connected graphs and any graph G, tree-πH(G)
is equal to the tree-independence number of the subgraph of G◦induced by all induced
copies of graphs from H in G. This allows us to reduce the problem of computing a tree
decomposition of bounded induced H-packing number to the already studied problem of
computing a decomposition of bounded tree-independence number. Furthermore, if H is
a fixed finite family, then the discussed induced subgraph of G◦has polynomial size. This
yields Theorem 1.8. This result is important for algorithmic applications, as we always
start with a tree decomposition witnessing that tree-πH(G) is bounded.
On the other hand, let G, H be graphs, where H is connected, and let G∗H be obtained
from G by adding, for each vertex v ∈V (G), a copy of H and identifying one vertex of
this copy with v. It is easy to see that tree-πH(G∗H) = tree-α(G). This simple reduction
allows us to lift known hardness results from tree-α to tree-πH, for any connected H. This
way we obtain Theorem 1.9. In particular, Theorem 1.8 is close to the best one can hope
for at this level of generality.

5.1. Dealing with (B, P4)-clean instances. Let us start with (B, P4)-clean graphs,

Modules and modular decomposition.
Let G be a graph. A nonempty set M ⊆
V (G) is a module of G if every vertex of V (G)\M is either complete to M or anticomplete
to M. A module is trivial if it is a singleton, or all of V (G), otherwise it is nontrivial. A
graph is prime if it has no nontrivial modules.
Let M = {M1, . . . , Mr} be a partition of V (G) into nonempty modules. The quotient
graph of G with respect to M, denoted G/M, is the graph with vertex set {q1, . . . , qr},
where qi corresponds to the module Mi, and where qiqj ∈E(G/M) if and only if Mi is
complete to Mj in G.
The theory of modules is quite rich, and we refer the reader to the survey of Habib
and Paul [35] for a comprehensive overview. We will only need a few basic facts about
modules and modular decomposition, which we summarize in the following lemma.
Lemma 5.1 (Modular decomposition tree). Given a graph G, in linear time one can
compute a rooted tree T, called the modular decomposition tree of G, whose nodes cor-
respond to nonempty modules of G, such that:
(1) the root of T corresponds to the module V (G), and the leaves of T correspond to
the singleton modules of G,
(2) for every non-leaf node M of T, the labels of its children form a partition of the
vertex set of G[M] into nonempty modules, and
(3) for every non-leaf node M of T, the quotient graph corresponding to the partition
of G[M] given by its children is either complete, edgeless, or prime.
Let us emphasize that for a graph G and a partition of V (G) into nonempty modules,
the quotient graph is an induced subgraph of G. Indeed, picking one vertex from each
module gives an induced subgraph of G isomorphic to the quotient graph. Thus, if G is,
say, H-free for some graph H, then so is the quotient graph.

It is well known that
modular decomposition can be used to reduce solving various problems to the case of
prime graphs [35]. Let us discuss it for the MWIS problem. Let (G, w) be a vertex-
weighted graph and let M = {M1, . . . , Mr} be a partition of V (G) into nonempty mod-
ules. Let G/M be the corresponding quotient graph and, as before, denote the vertex

G/M by setting w′(qi) = w(Ii) for each i ∈[r]. Independent sets of G/M correspond to

in a bottom-up fashion and solving the problem in the quotient graph of each non-leaf

computation time is polynomial times the time needed to solve MWIS in quotient graphs.
We remark that if the quotient graph is complete or edgeless, then the task of solving
MWIS in G/M is trivial. Thus, the only non-trivial case left is when G/M is prime.

Split graphs and modules of P4-free graphs.
A graph is split if its vertex set can
be partitioned into a clique and an independent set. We will make use of the following
result. It was shown by Hayward, Hougardy, and Reed [37], but we present an alternative
proof that is more consistent with the terminology of our paper.
Lemma 5.3. Let G be a prime graph. If G is not split, then every vertex of G belongs
to an induced P4.
Proof. Before proving the lemma, let us observe that its statement is invariant under
complementation. Let G denote the complement of G, i.e., the graph on the same vertex
set as G where two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are not adjacent in G. Then
G is prime if and only if G is prime, G is split if and only if G is split, and a vertex v
belongs to an induced P4 in G if and only if it belongs to an induced P4 in G, because
P4 is self-complementary.
Let G be a prime graph and let v be a vertex that does not belong to any induced P4
in G. We will show that G is split.
Claim 5.3.1. Non-neighbors of v form an independent set.
Proof of Claim: Let A = N(v) and B = V (G) \ (A ∪{v}). Consider a component C
of G[B]. First, note that every vertex a ∈A is either complete or anticomplete to C.
Indeed, suppose that a has both a neighbor b and a non-neighbor b′ in C. Since C is
connected, we can pick b, b′ so that they are adjacent. Then v −a −b −b′ is an induced
P4 that contains v, a contradiction.
We observe that C is a module in G. Indeed, v is anticomplete to C, every vertex from
A is either complete or anticomplete to C, and every vertex from B \ C is anticomplete
to C as C is a component of G[B].
Note that C must be a trivial module, as G is prime. As v /∈C, we conclude that C
has only one vertex. The choice of C was arbitrary, so indeed B is an independent set. ⌟

Solving MWIS in (B, P4)-clean prime graphs.
The following lemma combines the
above observations with the modular decomposition tree to show that MWIS can be
solved efficiently in (B, P4)-clean graphs, provided that components of G −B are “easy.”
Lemma 5.4. Let (G, w) be a vertex-weighted graph and let B ⊆V (G) such that G is
(B, P4)-clean. Assume that there is an algorithm Alg that, given an n′-vertex induced
subgraph of G −B, in time T(n′) computes a maximum-weight independent set in that
subgraph. Then a maximum-weight independent set in (G, w) can be computed in time
T(n) · nO(1).
Proof. By Lemma 5.2, it is enough to show how to solve MWIS in prime induced sub-
graphs of G. Thus, let G′ be such a graph on n′ vertices. Define B′ = B ∩V (G′). Clearly,
G′ is (B′, P4)-clean.
If G′ is a split graph, then we can compute a maximum-weight independent set in G′
in polynomial time. Indeed, in polynomial time we can find a split partition (C, S) of
G′, where C is a clique and S is an independent set. Then an independent set contains
at most one vertex of C, so we can exhaustively guess this choice, remove its neighbors,
and include all remaining vertices of S.
So, suppose that G′ is not split. By Lemma 5.3, every vertex of G′ belongs to an
induced P4. Since G′ is (B′, P4)-clean, this implies that B′ = ∅, i.e., G′ is an induced
subgraph of G −B. Thus, we can apply the algorithm Alg to the whole graph G′ to
compute a maximum-weight independent set in time T(n′).
Applying Lemma 5.2, we conclude that we can solve the instance (G, w) of MWIS in
time T(n) · nO(1), as claimed.

a balanced separator of (G, w).

10. Conclusion
Let us conclude the paper by pointing out some open problems and directions for future
research. First, it would be interesting to see if our quasipolynomial-time algorithms can
be improved to polynomial time.
Question 10.1. For what families H can MWIS and related problems be solved in poly-
nomial time on graphs of bounded induced-H-packing treewidth?
Next, it would be interesting to see how far the algorithmic tractability of graphs of
bounded induced-H-packing treewidth can be extended to generalizations of MWIS. In
particular, the following question is a natural “local” extension of Conjecture 1.1.
Question 10.2. Let H be a planar graph and let H be the family of all graphs that contain
H as an induced minor. Is it true that (tw ≤r, ψ)-MWIS and List 3-Coloring are
tractable in graphs of bounded induced-H-packing treewidth?
Recall that for MWIS there is also a broader family of graph classes obtained by
excluding a fixed forest of subdivided claws as an induced subgraph; in this setting,
quasipolynomial-time algorithms are known and polynomial-time algorithms are con-
jectured. It would be interesting to see if this picture can be extended to the “local”
setting. The following question addresses the smallest case, i.e., the case that H consists
of the three-leaf star K1,3.
Question 10.3. Is MWIS tractable in graphs of bounded induced-K1,3-packing treewidth?
Finally, it would be interesting to consider structural properties of graphs of bounded
induced-H-packing treewidth for various families H. First, recall that for every planar
graph H, H-induced-minor-free graphs are conjectured to admit balanced separators
dominated by few vertices [27]. Could this also be true in the “local” setting?
