ABSTRACT
Probing the chemical content of the interstellar medium (ISM) in nearby galaxies provides key
insight into their chemical evolution and informs our interpretation of galaxies at higher redshift.
However, nonlinear structure in the ISM, including density and temperature inhomogeneities, can
bias chemical abundance measurements and systematically affect empirical calibrations derived from
them. In this work, we investigate biases in Te-derived oxygen abundance determinations and explore
the physical properties that correlate with them. We combine [O II]λλ3726, 3729 measurements from
SITELLE with a full suite of optical emission lines obtained with MUSE. From auroral emission lines
([N II]λ5755, [S III]λ6312, and [O II]λλ7320, 7330) and nebular emission lines (including [N II]λ6584
and [S III]λ9069), we derive electron densities, temperatures, and chemical abundances for a sample
of H ii regions in five galaxies. We find that densities derived from the [O II] auroral-to-nebular ratio
are ∼103 cm−3, which is higher than the standard [S II] densities derived from nebular doublet ratios.
We demonstrate that combining the [N, II] electron temperature with the density inferred from the
[O II] auroral-to-nebular line ratio yields singly ionized oxygen abundances consistent with literature
expectations for a prescription insensitive to density inhomogeneities. We also find that the [S III]
temperature provides a reliable estimate of Te,[O III], enabling robust measurements of doubly ionized
oxygen abundances. Overall, these results indicate that the abundance discrepancy factor could be
higher in more chemically evolved H ii regions.

1. INTRODUCTION
The gas-phase chemical abundance, metallicity, of the
interstellar medium (ISM) is an important tracer of
galaxy and the chemical evolution. Metallicity is esti-
mated by taking the ratio of emission lines. H ii regions
often have bright emission lines due to the O and B type
stars (Osterbrock and Ferland 2006) that ionize the sur-
rounding gas.
Given the short lifetime of these stars,
the metallicity of an H ii region acts as a tracer for the
present-day chemical composition of the ISM. Metallic-
ity is a crucial parameter in stellar, galactic and cosmic

chemical evolution models (e.g., Pettini and Pagel 2004a;
Kewley and Dopita 2002; Kobulnicky and Kewley 2004),
hence accurate measurements of metallicity are pivotal
for theoretical modeling in astrophysics.
Elemental abundances in ionized nebulae are com-
monly derived using either recombination lines (RLs)
or collisionally excited lines (CELs). RLs are produced
when ions recombine with free electrons, while CELs
arise from collisional excitation followed by radiative de-
excitation. However, abundances derived from RLs and
CELs have long been known to disagree, with RLs typi-
cally yielding higher metallicities than CELs (Wyse 1942;
Peimbert 1967; Rubin 1986; Rodr´ıguez and Garc´ıa-Rojas

The dis-
crepancy between the abundances derived from CELs
and RLs is quantified by the abundance discrepancy fac-
tor (ADF; see, e.g., Garc´ıa-Rojas and Esteban (2007)).
Studies (Peimbert 1967; Peimbert and Costero 1969; Bi-
nette et al. 2012; Peimbert and Peimbert 2013; Berg
et al. 2015; Nicholls et al. 2020; Berg et al. 2020; M´endez-
Delgado et al. 2023b) have shown that density and tem-
perature inhomogeneities may be present within the dif-
ferent ionization zones of H ii regions, and are the most
likely cause of the ADF. In particular, CELs are exponen-
tially dependent on temperature while RLs are roughly
proportional to electron density and inverse electron tem-
perature (Osterbrock and Ferland 2006).
Thus, when
there are clumps of gas within an H ii region with dif-
ferent temperatures, the ratio of emission lines tend to
be biased to clumps with higher temperatures (M´endez-
Delgado et al. 2023a). These systematically larger elec-
tron temperatures from CELs subsequently cause un-
derestimated metallicities in comparison to RL-derived
metallicities (i.e., the ADF).
The ADF has been a long-standing problem in metal-
licity derivations (Peimbert 1967, 1971; Jamet et al. 2005;
M´endez-Delgado et al. 2023a,b; Rickards Vaught et al.
2024). To address this problem, M´endez-Delgado et al.
(2023a, hereafter MD23a) presented an empirical method
that relates the [N II] electron temperature to a RL-
derived metallicity (see Section 3.2.1). In this work, we
aim to study the density and temperature prescriptions
that yield a metallicity that best agrees with this RL-
derived empirical method. This will inform what phys-
ical quantities are most affected by inhomogeneities in
the gas, thus minimizing the ADF.
An additional difficulty in metallicity measurement
with either CELs or RLs is that both methods require
very faint emission lines (e.g., [N II]λ 5755, O II V1 RLs).
This has lead to the development of ‘strong line’ cali-
brations being employed to estimate metallicity (Kewley
and Dopita 2002; Kobulnicky and Kewley 2004; Pilyu-
gin and Thuan 2005; Dopita et al. 2016; Pilyugin and
Grebel 2016; Rosales-Ortega et al. 2026). These strong
line methods are calibrated using metallicities obtained
through the ‘direct Te’ method (Pettini and Pagel 2004b;
Marino et al. 2013; Pilyugin and Grebel 2016; Pilyugin
and Thuan 2005; M´endez-Delgado et al. 2023a; Rosales-
Ortega et al. 2026), theoretical models (McGaugh 1991;
Kewley and Dopita 2002; Kobulnicky and Kewley 2004;
Tremonti et al. 2004) or a combination of both (Zaritsky
et al. 1994; Dopita et al. 2016; Denicol´o et al. 2002). Dif-
ferent strong-line metallicity calibrations can show varia-
tions of ∼1 dex in metallicities (e.g., Kewley and Ellison
2008; Teimoorinia et al. 2021) derived for the same ob-
jects (e.g., H ii regions, emission-line galaxies).
With an increasing number of spectroscopic studies be-
ing done in both nearby and in high-redshift galaxies,
many different direct and strong line methods are be-
ing employed to estimate metallicity (Maiolino and Man-
nucci 2019; Kewley et al. 2019). Understanding the ADF
is a necessary first step toward accurately tracing metal-
licity evolution across galactic and cosmological scales.
In this work, we aim to prescribe a direct method as
well as a strong line method that produces the most
accurate estimation of metallicity.
Specifically, this is
done by comparing CEL derived abundances with the
RL derived relation from MD23a. Such a comparison will
demonstrate which electron density and temperature di-
agnostics are most affected by the density/temperature
inhomogeneities that give rise to the ADF.
In Habjan et al. (2026), observations of five nearby
galaxies (NGC 628, NGC 2835, NGC 3351, NGC 3627,
and NGC 4535) from Spectrom`etre Imageur `a Trans-

Rousseau-Nepton et al. 2018) are analyzed to obtain
blended [O II]λλ3726,3729 (hereafter [O II]λ3727) neb-
ular line fluxes. We pair these [O II]λ3727 fluxes with
Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE) data from
the Physics at High Angular Resolution in Nearby Galax-
ieS (PHANGS) survey (Emsellem et al. 2022). The suite
of emission lines from MUSE and SITELLE allow for di-
rect Te metallicity calculations in H ii regions that have
spatial resolution of ∼100 pc. We compare our derived
metallicities to those calculated using the RL-derived cal-
ibration derived by MD23a.

temperatures and densities are derived and presented in
Section 3. Oxygen and nitrogen abundances are calcu-
lated and studied in Section 4. The implications of these
results are outlined and explored in Section 5 and we
conclude in Section 6.

resulting in 23,244 H ii regions across 19 galaxies. Each of
the five galaxies observed with SITELLE were observed

2.2. SITELLE Data
SITELLE is an optical Fourier Transform spectrograph
with a field of view of 11 arcmin x 11 arcmin, large
enough to contain the full star-forming disk of each
galaxy.
This work makes use of data obtained using
SITELLE’s SN1 filter that covers 3650-3850 ˚A and in-
cludes the [O II]λ3727 emission line. Habjan et al. (2026)
describes the data reduction and emission line flux mea-
surements corresponding to the Nebular Catalog foot-
prints. Of the full set of nebulae covered by the SITELLE
data, 743 (8.4%) are detected in [O II] with S/N>3.
Based on the BPT cuts described above, 680 of these
are classified as H ii regions. Each of these line fluxes is
corrected for both foreground and internal extinction, ap-
plying the same E(B-V) reddening and O’Donnell (1994)
extinction law as was obtained from the Balmer decre-
ment in the MUSE data.
2.3. Auroral Line Fitting
The MUSE Data Analysis Pipeline (DAP) described
in Emsellem et al. (2022) provides a method based on
the gist code (Bittner et al. 2019), to use penalised
PiXel-Fitting (pPXF) (Cappellari and Emsellem 2004)
to fit stellar and emission line properties of the spec-
tra from the MUSE observations. However, after stel-
lar background continuum subtraction, a residual contin-
uum may be present at the location of faint lines, which
if ignored, may bias their measured intensity given their
intrinsic faint flux.
In order to more accurately mea-
sure auroral line fluxes in our H ii region sample, we re-
fit the [N II]λ5755, [S III]λ6312, and [O II]λλ7320,7330
(hereafter [O II]λ7325) auroral lines. This allows for line
measurement uncertainties and quality cuts to be made
consistently with the [O II] SITELLE fluxes while also
providing a direct comparison to the pPXF procedure
used in (Brazzini et al. 2024, hereafter B24).
This is done by fitting each auroral line with a Gaus-
sian and modeling the surrounding continuum (see Ap-
pendix A for exact wavelength ranges) with a straight
line. To model the continuum, which is approximately
flat immediately surrounding each auroral line, we first
fit a first-order polynomial to continuum regions on ei-
ther side of the line.
The resulting continuum model
is then subtracted from the spectrum, and the auroral
line is modeled with a Gaussian. Each of the four au-
roral lines are fitted using the curve fit method from
SciPy (Virtanen et al. 2020).
The amplitude, central
redshifted wavelength and velocity dispersion are taken
as free parameters during the fitting process.
An ini-
tial guess for the central redshifted wavelength for singly
ionized auroral lines (i.e.
N+ and O+) is made using
the [N II]λ6584 velocity from the Nebular Catalog. For
the doubly ionized auroral lines (i.e.
S2+) we use the
[O III]λ5007 velocity to estimate the central redshifted
wavelength.
Similarly, we use the [N II]λ6584 velocity
dispersion from the Nebular Catalog to estimate the
[N II]λ5755 and [O II]λ7325 Gaussian widths while we
use the [O III]λ5007 velocity dispersion to estimate the
width of [S III]λ6312. The estimated central redshifted
wavelength was given ±0.5 ˚A of freedom from the initial
estimate as the velocities from the Nebular catalog are
very accurate. The standard deviation of the Gaussian
σλ could vary in the range σλ,SL < σλ < σλ,SL + 0.5 [˚A]
where σλ,SL = λrest × (σv,SL/c), λrest is the auroral line
rest wavelength, and σv,SL is the strong line velocity dis-
persion from the Nebular Catalog. This restriction was
implemented for σ as the auroral line velocity dispersions
have a tendency to be larger than the strong line veloc-
ity dispersions, but we also did not want to include any
emission features that are unphysically broad. Examples
of each auroral line fit are shown in Figure 1.
We determine uncertainties using a Monte Carlo (MC)
method described below. A Gaussian noise distribution
is created with a mean of zero and a standard devia-
tion equal to that of the surrounding continuum (see
Appendix A). Random draws from this distribution are
made and added to the spectrum. Our fitting process
is carried out 1,000 times with added Gaussian noise,
and the standard deviation in the obtained auroral line
fluxes is taken to be the 1 σ uncertainty. We correct for
extinction using the same process described in Section
2.2.
In order to minimize the effects of sky contamination
in our auroral line fluxes, we only consider auroral line
fits that have signal-to-noise (S/N) > 5. The effect of
sky contamination for the galaxies in our sample af-
fects [N II]λ5755 to a smaller extent, however the spec-
tral ranges near the redshifted features of [S III]λ6312
and [O II]λ7325 show signs of contamination.
The
[O II]λ7320/λ7330 ratio is not strictly independent of
physical conditions, since the transitions originate from
levels with different collisional de-excitation rates; how-
ever, the extremely high critical densities involved en-
sure that it remains largely insensitive to density vari-
ations up to ne ≈105.5 cm−3. As a quality check, we
find that the [O II]λ7320/λ7330 ratio measured for our
sample is 1.27 ± 0.03. This is in good agreement with
the [O II]λ7320/λ7330 ratio found by Section 7.1.1 of
Rickards Vaught et al. (2024, hereafter RV24) and the

3. ELECTRON DENSITIES AND TEMPERATURES
In this section, we describe the derivation of electron
densities ne and temperatures Te using the extinction
corrected emission line fluxes discussed in Section 2. The
methods for each quantity are described first and then
followed by the results.
3.1. Electron Density and Temperature
3.1.1. Te and ne Methodology
We first derive the [S II] density ne,[S II] using the
[S II]λ6731/λ6716 diagnostic. This density is then com-
bined with the [O II]λ7325/λ3727, [N II]λ5755/λ6584,
and [S III]λ6312/λ9069 temperature sensitive line ratios
to derive the average electron temperature of each ion us-

and ne,[S II] using 103 Gaussian iterations by propagating
flux uncertainties. Each temperature derivation yields a
unique ne,[S II], thus we take ne,[S II] to be a weighted av-
erage; the weight is equal to the inverse square of the 1
σ uncertainty of each independently derived ne,[S II].

3.1.3. Te Results
The methods described in Section 3.1.1 produced 53
Te,[O II], 202 Te,[N II] and 27 Te,[S III] detections. We see
in Figure 2 that Te,[N II] and Te,[S III] follow a nearly lin-
ear relationship, where Te,[S III] ∼0.95 × Te,[N II] with a
Spearman rank correlation coefficient ρ of 0.93 ± 0.04.
The global trends found in Rogers et al. (2021), Zurita
et al. (2021) and RV24 show a steeper slope and superlin-
ear relationship for Te,[N II] – Te,[S III]. Each of the trend
lines from other studies in Figure 2 agree to within 1-σ
between the range of 0.6×104 K and 0.9×104 K, indicat-
ing that this Te – Te relationship agrees well with other
studies. In particular, the Te,[N II] and Te,[S III] relation-
ship from Figure 9 of RV24 shows that across their entire
sample that Te,[S III] is often larger than Te,[N II]. How-
ever, in NGC 2835 and NGC 628, the majority of H ii
regions from RV24 show that Te,[N II] ∼Te,[S III], which
can be seen in Figure 2. It should be noted that although
RV24 makes use of the same MUSE observations, the
H ii region boundaries are defined differently. Regard-
less, these findings indicate that the obtained Te,[N II] –
Te,[S III] relationship is strongly dependent on the galax-
ies in the sample.
We find that Te,[O II] is only weakly correlated with
Te,[N II] and Te,[S III], with ρ of 0.38±0.09 and 0.25±0.11,
respectively. We find an average Te,[O II] of 1.25 × 104 K
with a standard deviation of 0.20 × 104 K. The large val-
ues of Te,[O II] and the lack of correlation between Te,[O II]
with other Te in Figure 2 can be explained by either the
density dependence of the [O II]λ7325/λ3727 tempera-
ture diagnostic or contribution from the DIG (see Section
3.1.2).
The weak correlation between Te,[O II] and the
other Te shown in Figure 2 is contrary to what is found in
MD23b, which shows that Te,[O II], despite being overes-
timated due to density inhomogeneities, is still correlated
with Te,[N II]. The lack of correlation between Te,[O II]
and other Te in this work may be evidence that the DIG
or another mechanism is biasing Te,[O II] measurements.
Furthermore, the relations between Te,[O II] and other Te
in this work are found to be shallower than the relations
found by RV24, MD23b, Rogers et al. (2021), and Zurita
et al. (2021). Some of this variation may reflect differ-
ences in the galaxy samples used to derive each relation,
as sample-dependent differences in metallicity, ionization
conditions, and star formation environments can affect
the observed Te –Te trends. However, as shown by RV24,
for example, both the Te,[N II]–Te,[O II] and the Te,[S III]–
Te,[O II] relations exhibit substantial scatter, suggesting
an underlying mechanism that drives departures from a
simple linear relationship.

ne,[S II] has a tendency to underestimate the average ne

total oxygen abundance (MD23b). We present our O+
abundances in Figure 4, with our reference O+ plotted
against each of the other four O+ abundance estimates.

only emission lines from MUSE.

5. DISCUSSION
In this section, we discuss our findings in the context
of previous studies, with an emphasis on discussing how
temperature and density inhomogeneities contribute to
the ADF.
5.1. Galaxy Parameter Splitting
The galaxies across our comparisons of electron densi-
ties, electron temperatures, and abundances consistently
populate different regions of parameter space. For exam-
ple, in the case of ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727, NGC 2835 has an
average value of 700±200 cm−3, NGC 628 has an average
value of 1100±600 cm−3, and NGC 4535 has an average
value of 3200 ± 200 cm−3. A similar pattern is seen in
Te,[N II], with NGC 2835 having an average of 9000±800
K, NGC 628 7700 ± 900 K, NGC 4535 7700 ± 1700 K,
and NGC 3627 7600±1000 K. Similar trends are seen in
the literature (e.g., see Berg et al. 2020; Rickards Vaught
et al. 2024), where the Te – Te trends and various abun-
dance relations demonstrate similar galaxy segregation.
For Te,[N II], all galaxies except NGC 2835 have av-
erage values that agree within the standard deviations,
whereas the ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727 averages remain clearly
separated from one another across galaxies.
This re-
sult follows for Te , the oxygen abundances, and nitrogen
abundances. This result suggests that H ii regions within
the same galaxy tend to exhibit similar physical condi-
tions, while those conditions may vary substantially be-

tween galaxies, as seen for Te,[N II] and ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727
in our sample.
However, because our sample includes
only five galaxies and provides a limited number of detec-

of H ii region properties both within and among galaxies
is needed to better understand this apparent galaxy-to-
galaxy variation. The PHANGS-MUSE sample presents
an ideal dataset for such an analysis.
5.2. Density Inhomogeneities
In Section 3.1.2, we found a significant discrepancy of
∼103 cm−3 between ne,[S II] and ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727. It is
possible to attribute this difference to density inhomo-
geneities within H ii regions, which can be understood
in terms of the differing critical densities of the relevant
transitions. The [O II]λ3727 and [S II]λλ6716, 6731 neb-
ular lines have critical densities of order ∼103 cm−3
(Luridiana et al. 2022), whereas the [O II]λ7325 auro-
ral line remain density sensitive up to ∼105 cm−3. As
the gas density approaches or exceeds the critical density
of a given transition, collisional de-excitation reduces its
emissivity. If H ii regions contain dense clumps with ne
∼103–105 cm−3, then the [S II] nebular lines will prefer-
entially trace the lower-density gas, while the [O II] au-
roral lines can still retain sensitivity to denser material.
This picture is consistent with Figure 3, where ne,[S II]
is systematically biased toward lower values, often near
the low-density limit, and naturally explains why ne,[S II]
yields lower densities than ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727.
The
suppression
of
the
[S II]λλ6716, 6731
and
[O II]λ3727 nebular emission by density inhomogeneities
may have important consequences for the derivation of
Te,[O II]. When the [O II]λ7325/λ3727 ratio is combined
with the underestimated ne,[S II], the inferred Te,[O II]
becomes biased toward the hotter, lower-density gas
within the H ii region. By contrast, the nebular [N II]
and [S III] lines have critical densities of ∼105 cm−3
and ∼106 cm−3, respectively, such that Te,[N II] and
Te,[S III] remain largely insensitive to density inhomo-
geneities over the density range relevant for ionized
nebulae. This picture may naturally explain the trends
seen in Figure 2, where Te,[O II] > Te,[N II] ≈Te,[S III].
Although the [O II]λ7325/λ3727 ratio being unreliable
as a temperature diagnostic when used with ne,[S II],
the [O II] auroral-to-nebular ratio remains a powerful
density diagnostic (MD23b).
Following theoretical
predictions for regions with homogeneous ne and setting
Te,[O II] = Te,[N II], the resultant ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727 yields
an average electron density that is unaffected by density
inhomogeneities.
As discussed in Section 3.1.2, the DIG may impact
the observed fluxes of lower ionization lines such as
[N II]λ6548 and [O II]λ3727 (Zhang et al. 2017). While
both density inhomogeneities and DIG contamination
may contribute to the abundance offsets observed in this
work (e.g., Figure 4), the present analysis does not allow
us to quantify their relative importance. Additionally, it
remains unclear whether these offsets depend on galactic
environment. A follow up study will examine the relative
contributions of these effects in more detail.

5.3. Temperature Inhomogeneities
The [O III]λ4363 auroral line is outside the spectral
range of MUSE and SITELLE, however the use of
Te,[S III] and Equation 1 allow for Te,[O III] to be empiri-
cally calculated. Furthermore, this work does not make
use of RLs, but uses the empirically derived T0(O2+) us-
ing Te,[N II] and Equation 2. In Figure 5, we find that O2+
derived from either Te,[O III] or T0(O2+) generally agree
well with differences only up to ∼0.05 dex. Furthermore,
the elemental oxygen abundances derived with O2+ agree
well with the MD23a metallicity calibration. However,
we do find that there are systematic offsets between
MD23a and the metallicities derived from T0(O2+) and
Te,[O III] with these differences being more pronounced in
lower metallicity regions following the results of MD23a.
It is not clear what is the cause of the offsets seen in
the first and fourth panels of Figure 6, however it seems
that using Te,[O III] from B24 and T0(O2+) from MD23a
minimize the impact of temperature inhomogeneities on
metallicity. In future studies, a comparison between RL
derived abundances and abundances derived using mul-
tiple photoionization models (Marconi et al. 2024) may
reveal the cause of the discrepancies seen in Figure 6.
5.4. Proxy ADF
To further study the differences in the derived O2+
from T0(O2+) and Te,[O III], a proxy for the ADF is
defined as the ratio of O2+ derived from T0(O2+) and
Te,[O III]. We emphasize that this ratio is a proxy of the
ADF as T0(O2+) and Te,[O III] are empirically derived
temperatures.

against ne,[S II]. The Spearman rank correlation coeffi-
cient is positive (ρS ∼0.56), suggesting a possible mono-
tonic trend between the proxy ADF and ne,[S II]. How-
ever, because this trend is based on only four noisy mea-
surements, we interpret it with caution. A similar trend
between the proxy ADF and ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727 can be
found, which indicates this possible relationship is most

is found that NGC 4535 has the largest proxy ADF,
with values of ∼2.4, while NGC 628 has values of ∼1

enriched gas contributes to the proxy ADF. This does
not imply the existence of H-deficient clumps, however
this hypothesis can only be studied via spatially resolved
observations of H ii regions, similar to the study carried
out in MD23a, but for a sample of high metallicity (12
+ log(O/H) > 8.7) H ii regions.
6. CONCLUSION
In this work,
observations of five galaxies from
SITELLE are paired with PHANGS-MUSE observations
to study the properties of extragalacitic H ii regions. Var-
ious electron density, electron temperature and abun-
dance determinations are compared within this work and
to previous studies. The main findings in this work are
as follows:
• An [O II] density ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727 is derived us-
ing the [O II] auroral-to-nebular ratio in combi-
nation with the [N II] temperature.
On average
ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727 is found to be ∼103 cm−3 larger
than ne,[S II]. This substantial underestimation in
electron density from the [S II] diagnostic results in
a ∼0.1 dex underestimation in O+.
• We find that density inhomogeneities may under-
estimate electron densities when using the [S II]
nebular-to-nebular line diagnostic, which coincides
with results from MD23b and RV24. When ne,[S II]
is paired with the [O II] auroral-to-nebular line ra-
tio, then the [O II] temperature is overestimated by
∼0.3 ×104 K due to density inhomogeneities.
• The empirical temperatures T0(O2+) from MD23a
and Te,[O III] from B24 are found to produce O2+
abundances that minimize the effects of tempera-
ture inhomogeneities.
• The relation between N/O and N+/O+ is found
to deviate modestly from the commonly adopted
one-to-one approximation when ne,[OII]λ7325/λ3727is
adopted as the density prescription. For our sam-
ple, we find N+/O+ ∼(0.84 ± 0.01)N/O.
• A potential correlation is found between the proxy
ADF and properties that describe chemical enrich-
ment (i.e., metallicity and ne ).
These results
suggest that H ii regions that are more chemically
evolved may have larger ADFs. This result needs
to be rigorously tested with a direct calculation
of O2+ using both RL- and CEL-derived tempera-
tures.
The findings of this work imply that temperature and
density inhomogeneities remain a central issue in esti-
mating chemical abundances in photoionized nebulae.
However, this work demonstrates that the impact of
these biases can be reduced by adopting temperature and
density prescriptions that are less sensitive to tempera-
ture and density inhomogeneities. Any remaining biases
appear to be limited to a few tenths of a dex, substan-
tially smaller than the order of magnitude offsets that
have historically affected some direct abundance deter-
minations. Understanding the origin of these remaining
biases and their connection to the ADF remains a cen-
tral goal of studies involving direct metallicities in H ii
regions.

BACKGROUND CONTINUUM EXTRACTION FOR EMISSION LINE FITTING
Each H ii region in our sample has a unique galactic and rotational velocity. Thus, the spectral ranges in which
background continuum is extracted for emission line fitting varies for each H ii region in our sample. In the fitting
algorithm designed in this study, a list of known atomic transitions (Kramida et al. 2023) were used to designate where
background continuum should be extracted in the rest frame wavelength. After the fitting process described in Section
2.3 was carried out, a redshfited wavelength is measured and can be used to extract continuum background from
spectral ranges that do not contain known atomic transitions. In Table 4, the wavelength ranges in which continuum
background is extracted is shown.
