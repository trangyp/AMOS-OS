THE SPACE OF PREORDERS ON A COMMUTATIVE MONOID
JAN DRAISMA, GEORGE METCALFE, AND SIMON SANTSCHI
Abstract. For a finitely generated commutative monoid Π, we present a con-
structive description of all (total) preorders on Π that are compatible with the
monoid structure. Equipped with a natural topology, these preorders form an
irreducible spectral space, which we show can be covered by a countable union
of admissible sets: subsets of RN of the form A \ H where A is semialgebraic
and H is a countable union of hyperplanes, both defined over the rational
numbers. As a consequence of this description, we show that the universal
theory of commutative monoids with a total order is decidable. Our proofs
use a divide-and-conquer technique that requires establishing all of our results
in the greater generality of sets on which Π acts with finitely many orbits.
As a by-product, we find a new description of all monomoial orders on free
modules over a polynomial ring.
1. Introduction and main results
Let (Π, ·) be a commutative monoid. For most of this paper, we will require that
Π is finitely generated, but not quite yet. The reader may think of the monoid
Monn = {xα | α ∈Zn
≥0} of monomials in a polynomial ring K[x1, . . . , xn] over
an arbitrary field K; indeed, every finitely generated commutative monoid is a
quotient of Monn for some n ∈Z≥0. In particular, Π can be an abelian group such
as (Zn, +) for some n ∈Z≥0.
A (total) preorder on Π is a binary relation ≤on Π that is reflexive, transitive,
and total, and satisfies v ≤w ⇒uv ≤uw for all u, v, w ∈Π. In this case, we write
u < v to denote that u ≤v but not v ≤u, and u ≈v to denote that u ≤v and
v ≤u. If, additionally, ≤is anti-symmetric, then we call it an order on Π. We will
never require, as is often the case in computational algebra, that ≤is a well-order.
Let P(Π) denote the set of all preorders on Π. We equip P(Π) with the coarsest
topology in which, for all u, v ∈Π, the set {≤| u < v} is closed. Our main goal is
to derive a constructive description of the space P(Π) and its individual elements.
A first, preliminary description is as follows; we refer to Figure 1 for an illustration.
Theorem A. Let Π be a commutative monoid. Then P(Π) is an irreducible spec-
tral topological space. If Π is finitely generated, then P(Π) is also TD, i.e., every
singleton is open in its closure. Furthermore, for any n ∈Z≥1:
(1) P(Monn) is not Noetherian and has infinite Krull dimension.
(2) P(Zn) has Krull dimension n; it is Noetherian if and only if n = 1, and
this is also the only case where P(Zn) is pure-dimensional.
For the theory of spectral spaces, we refer to [Hoc69]; the separation axiom TD
was first introduced in [AT62].

It follow easily, using the strong distribution properties of the product and lattice
operations, that Theorem D is equivalent to the statement that the universal theory
of totally ordered commutative ℓ-monoids is decidable.
This theorem also implies the decidability of the universal theory of commutative
distributive ℓ-monoids, which form an equational class and have been studied in
some depth in the universal algebra literature (see, e.g., [Mer71, Rep83, AE84,
GH13, CGMS22]). We just require two further facts. First, an implication between
a finite conjunction of equations and finite disjunction of equations holds in an
equational class if and only if one of the quasiequations obtained by selecting a single
equation on the right of the implication holds in the class. Second, a quasiequation
holds in every commutative distributive ℓ-monoid if and only if it holds in every
totally ordered commutative ℓ-monoid [Mer71].
Corollary E. The universal theory of commutative distributive ℓ-monoids is de-
cidable.
Organisation of the paper and related literature. In §2 we introduce the key
notions discussed above in greater generality. Crucially, to prove Theorem B and its
consequences Theorems C and D by induction, we need to prove a generalisation of
this result for finitely generated Π-sets. In commutative algebra terms, we will prove
all the theorems above not just for preorders on the monomials in a polynomial ring
but also for preorders on the monomials in a finitely generated free module over
that polynomial ring.
In §3 we prove the first statement of Theorem A and its generalisation to finitely
generated Π-sets (Theorem 3.1).
In §4 we study preorders on finitely generated Π-sets, where Π is a finitely gen-
erated abelian group; see Theorem 4.20. Any such preorder gives rise to a tree
decorated with combinatorial data, which we call a preotree, along with numerical
data that plays the role of the matrix A in the description of orders on Monn men-
tioned above and is given by a vector of real numbers. (Moreover, if the preotree is
chosen to be minimal, then it is essentially canonical.) This generalises Robbiano’s
theorem to finitely generated Π-sets; the original result is recovered when the Π-set
is Π itself equipped with left multiplication. Our results in this section are close
to those of [RR97], which gives a recursive construction of all monomial orders on
a free module over a polynomial ring. However, for our subsequent application of
these results to monoids, we need finer control over the numerical data than that
provided by [RR97]. Around the same time as [RR97], similar classifications were
obtained in the Ph.D. thesis [Hor98] and in the study of Riquier orderings [CS99].
In §5 we establish several admissibility results for finitely generated Π-sets, again
under the assumption that Π is a finitely generated abelian group. For instance,
fix two preotrees τ and τ ′ for the same Π-set S, and let Σ, Σ′ denote their spaces
of numerical data. For p ∈Σ, let ≤p denote the preorder on S defined by (τ, p),
and similarly for p′ ∈Σ′ and τ ′. Then the locus of all (p, p′) ∈Σ × Σ′ satisfying
∀s, t ∈S : s ≤p t ⇒s ≤p′ t is admissible (Proposition 5.5). This result, and
variants of it, are of crucial importance in the next section.
Indeed, in §6 we
turn our attention to the considerably more difficult case where Π is any finitely
generated commutative monoid and S is a finitely generated Π-set. The main result
here is Theorem 6.11, which gives a description of an arbitrary preorder on S, now
using trees whose vertices are labelled by preorders on sets on which a group acts;
by Theorem 4.20 these in turn can be described by preotrees with numerical data.

3. Spectrality of the space of preorders
Let Π be a monoid and let S be a Π-set. The following theorem is a generalisation
of the first part of Theorem A to this setting.
Theorem 3.1. The space PΠ(S) is irreducible and spectral. Furthermore, if Π is
finitely generated and S is a finitely generated Π-set, then PΠ(S) is also TD, i.e.,

7. Closed points versus orders
If Π is a commutative monoid, then for each order ≤∈P(Π), the singleton {≤}
is closed: any preorder ≤′ in its closure must satisfy s < t ⇒s <′ t for all s, t ∈Π,

question as to when the converse holds; that is, for which commutative monoids
Π are all closed points in P(Π) orders? Since P(Π) is spectral, by Theorem 3.1,
every closed subset contains a closed point, and hence this question is equivalent
to asking when the closure of every preorder ≤∈P(Π) contains an order.

S′. Now Π′ and S′ are quotients of Monn and Monn ×[m], respectively, and we can
pull back the preorder. Hence in our model we may fix Π to be Monn (interpreting
each ui as the generator xi of Monn) and S to be Monn ×[m] (interpreting each sj
as the generator (1, j)), and only need to search for the preorder ≤.
To this end, we run two algorithms in parallel: one that looks for contradictions,
and a second that looks for such a preorder.
The first algorithm enumerates, for increasing N larger than all of the expo-
nents of the xi appearing in ψ, all binary relations ≤on Monn,≤N ×[m], where
Monn,≤N := {xα | ∀i : αi ≤N}, that satisfy transitivity, totality (v ≤w or w ≤v),
as well as (s ≤t ⇒us ≤ut) for all u ∈Monn,≤N and all s, t ∈Monn,≤N ×[m] with
us, ut ∈Monn,≤N ×[m]. For each such relation ≤, the algorithm checks whether ψ
is satisfied. If the first algorithm does not find such a relation ≤for a specific value
of N, then it outputs “no model exists”, signals the second algorithm to terminate,
and terminates itself. If it does find such a relation, then it increases N by 1 and
continues.
The second algorithm searches through all preorders on Monn ×[m], as follows.
Compute, for increasing i, the admissible set Ci = Ai \ Hi ⊆RNi and the map φi
from the Π-set version of Theorem B, and compute a semi-algebraic subset Bi ⊆Ai
from ψ by translating each clause s < t to the set A′
ist from Theorem B and by
translating boolean operations in the usual way to set operations. Now test whether
Bi \ Hi is nonempty. If so, the second algorithm outputs “a model exists”, signals
the first algorithm to terminate, and terminates itself. Indeed, then any p ∈B
defines a preorder ≤:= φi(p) on Monn ×[m] for which ψ is satisfied. If, however,
Bi \ Hi is empty, then the second algorithm increases i by one and continues.
The emptiness of Bi \ Hi can be tested by computing a cylindrical algebraic
decomposition of RNi adapted to Bi (see, e.g., [BPR06, Chapter 11]) and testing
whether (the span of) each cell in Bi is contained in some hyperplane in Hi.
By the Π-set version of Theorem B, if a model exists, then it is found after
finitely many steps by the second algorithm. On the other hand, if no solution
