We develop a statistical-mechanical theory of water in unsaturated soil whose outcome is a con-
tinuum field equation for the pore-occupancy g(r, x, t) ∈[0, 1]—the fraction of pores of radius r that
are water-filled at position x and time t. The theory is built by passing through three scales. At
the microscale, single inter-pore water transfers are set by Hagen–Poiseuille rates κ(r) and a driving
potential Φ(r, r′)—the difference of the pore-class chemical potentials, taken here in its capillary–
gravitational form but open to adsorptive, osmotic, or thermal refinement without change to the
theory. Averaging over a representative volume gives a mesoscale master equation whose gain and
loss terms relax the occupancy toward the equilibrium step geq = H(r∗−r) fixed by chemical-
potential minimization. Contracting the volume to a point promotes g to a field and yields the
continuum balance ∂tg + ∇· F = C[g] −E −T , with C[g] the redistribution operator and ∇· F
the transport divergence—the central equation of the theory, of which everything else is a limit, a
moment, or a boundary resolution.
The kinetic equation is an Onsager gradient flow: it descends the Gibbs free energy with a
state-dependent mobility, with dF/dt ≤0 a theorem for the isothermal, unforced system and mass
conservation as its zeroth moment.
A single dimensionless group, the pore-resolved Damköhler
number Da(r, x) = τredis(r)/τforcing(x), organizes the behavior and unifies phenomenologies long
modelled separately. A Chapman–Enskog reduction identifies Richards’ equation as the quasi-static
(Da →0) limit—with the matric potential ψ and the hydraulic conductivity K(θ) emerging only
there, K vanishing below the percolation threshold; capillary-bundle and critical-path conductivity
models are its diagonal and spectral limits, and dynamic capillary-pressure models its one-moment
approximation. Hysteresis is the holonomy of a forcing bundle over the space of boundary histories,
a geometric phase rather than per-pore bistability, with a falsifiable loop-area law H ∝I2 that
no rate-independent model reproduces.
Preferential flow is what the same equation does where
Da ≳1, so the long-standing dichotomy between Richards-equation and preferential flow becomes
a continuous, Da-controlled crossover within one framework. Out of the quasi-static limit g(r) is
the irreducible state variable and no scalar potential characterizes the system. All inputs—f(r),
C(r, r′), ¯L, ¯τ—are geometric properties of the pore network, measurable from micro-CT images and
calibrated against no macroscopic retention or conductivity data.
I.
INTRODUCTION
The dynamics of unsaturated porous media is inher-
ently irreversible [1]. Under realistic forcing, the system
does not evolve through a sequence of equilibrium states,
but through a succession of non-equilibrium configura-
tions. Capillary hysteresis is the most familiar manifes-
tation of this irreversibility: identical macroscopic states
such as the same water and energy content can corre-
spond to different internal configurations depending on
whether the system was reached by wetting or drying,
slowly or rapidly [2, 3].
Another long-standing difficulty in unsaturated flow
is the coexistence of two seemingly distinct behaviors.
When wetting is moderate or absent, water redistribu-
tion is slow, capillary-controlled, and close to local equi-
librium; flow is dominated by matric forces and is reason-
ably described by the classical continuum model known

as Richards equation [4–6]. In contrast, during intense or
rapid wetting events, water preferentially occupies larger,
well-connected pores, bypassing finer pores that would be
favored by equilibrium thermodynamics. This dual be-
havior has been emphasized in the seminal critiques of
Richards’ equation by Germann and Beven [7–9], who
argued that preferential flow and bypass mechanisms vi-
olate the assumptions underlying single-domain formula-
tions.
These observations have motivated the development
of dual-domain and dual-permeability extensions of
Richards’ equation, in which the pore space is parti-
tioned into interacting fast and slow domains [10]. While
successful in many applications, such approaches intro-
duce additional state variables and exchange terms phe-
nomenologically, without explaining how the two flow
regimes emerge from a common physical description. A
complementary, Lagrangian line of work represents soil
water as an ensemble of particles of unequal mobility [11].
That approach abandons the classical assumption that all
the water at a point moves with a single velocity, but the
fraction of pores of each size that is water-filled is still not

treated as a variable with its own dynamics. Promoting

From a physical perspective, the key issue is that
macroscopic variables such as water content are coarse-
grained descriptors.
Many distinct pore-scale configu-
rations correspond to the same macroscopic state, yet
differ in connectivity, accessibility, transport efficiency,
and energy content. When forcing is slow compared to
internal relaxation processes, such as film flow, vapor
transport, and interfacial rearrangement, the system re-
mains close to equilibrium and capillary ordering domi-
nates [12]. When forcing is rapid, relaxation cannot keep
pace, and water occupies geometrically accessible path-
ways rather than thermodynamically optimal ones. The
same porous medium therefore exhibits qualitatively dif-
ferent behavior depending on the relative timescales of
forcing and relaxation.
These dynamics generated the
phenomenon known as hydraulic hysteresis [13, 14] which
is not explained by Richards equation and usually treated
with ad hoc methods [15].
These considerations motivate a shift in perspective, in
which three elements play complementary roles. Thermo-
dynamics defines the equilibrium targets. Network topol-
ogy constrains which microscopic rearrangements are ge-
ometrically admissible. Relaxation dynamics governs the
rate at which the system can move through configura-
tion space. When relaxation is fast relative to forcing,
the system tracks equilibrium and classical matric flow
emerges; when forcing is fast, relaxation is incomplete,
flow bypasses the smallest pores, and preferential path-
ways appear. The transition between the two behaviors
is continuous and dynamically controlled, rather than im-
posed by the model structure.
The theory developed here explicitly tracks this ir-
reversible evolution through non-equilibrium states and
naturally unifies these behaviors within a single formal-
ism. Although motivated by water flow in porous media,
the framework is not specific to capillarity. It provides a
general description of systems in which local thermody-
namic equilibria exist, but macroscopic behavior emerges
from constrained, history-dependent transitions between
them.
Similar structures arise in plastic deformation,
magnetic hysteresis, and glassy dynamics, where irre-
versibility results from the interplay between thermody-
namic driving, geometric constraints, and finite relax-
ation times [16–18].
Phenomenological understanding,
together with over a century of measurements [2, 5, 19],
points to two distinct scales: an internal one, whose dy-
namics is governed by the topology and geometry of the
soil medium [20], and a larger one dominated by trans-
port. The natural mathematical representation of such a
two-scale system is a fiber bundle [21].
The remainder of this paper formalizes this perspec-
tive using tools from statistical physics and explores its
implications for transport and upscaling in porous me-
dia. Hysteresis, memory, rate dependence, and preferen-
tial flow emerge naturally as consequences of irreversible
non-equilibrium evolution, rather than as ad hoc modifi-
cations of equilibrium theory.

• Stage II evaporation (diffusion-limited): The liquid

then diffuses upward as vapor. This creates a pore

• Root uptake (plant-mediated): Roots extract water

are hydraulically connected to the root network.
This can remove water from pores that are inac-
cessible to both the gas-phase boundary and the
surface evaporation front, producing yet another
emptying sequence.
These mechanisms collectively tend to leave water in
more strongly-held configurations (more negative ψ)
compared to the non-selective filling during wetting. The
result: at the same water content θ, the drying path has
water in smaller pores (lower energy, more negative ψ)

firm this:

F.
The fundamental equation and its
morphological reading
Equation (42) is the fundamental non-equilibrium
equation and Eq. (48) is its mass-conserving moment.
The distinction is operational: Eq. (42) is closed—given
C(r, r′) and the single-pore conductances, both F and
C[g] are functionals of g, so g(r, x, t) determines its own
evolution—whereas Eq. (48) is not, since q is undeter-
mined by θ alone. The missing closure q[g] is precisely
the non-equilibrium content, and it is not a function of
θ alone. This is the statement that θ is an insufficient
state variable while g is sufficient—the origin of apparent
equifinality and of hysteresis. Mass conservation remains
exact and ansatz-independent, but for that reason it is a
constraint the dynamics must respect, not the dynamics
itself.
The insufficiency of θ has a morphological counter-
part. Beyond the volume fraction θ = M0 introduced
with the bulk budget above, the wetted phase carries
two further Minkowski descriptors, the interfacial area
M1 and the Euler characteristic M2 (connectivity), in
the three-functional 3D convention. Neither is idle: M1
is the capillary-surface contribution to F[g], and M2
is the topology that C(r, r′) and the percolation ac-
cessibility aw(r) encode, its sign change at threshold
marking field capacity. The matric potential is not it-
self a Minkowski functional; it enters through Young–
Laplace on the marginal radius, ψ = −2γ cos θc/(ρwg r∗).
The statement that θ underdetermines the state while g
closes it is then the geometric-state result of McClure
et al. [44]—the finding that, for two-fluid flow in porous
media, no single scalar (such as saturation) is a com-
plete state variable, whereas the triple of Minkowski
functionals {M0, M1, M2} (volume, interfacial area, and
Euler characteristic) is—here restated in pore-class lan-
guage: {M0} alone fixes neither retention nor conductiv-
ity, whereas {M0, M1, M2} does, and g(r) supplies that
same closing information resolved by pore radius.

VIII.
THE FORCING BUNDLE
The Chapman–Enskog reduction of Sec. VII collapsed
the kinetic equation onto Richards’ equation in the quasi-
static limit Da →0. The non-equilibrium content of the
theory—hysteresis—lives in the opposite direction: in the
dependence of g(r, t) on the history of the boundary forc-
ing, not on its instantaneous value. This dependence is
already contained in the dynamics of Eq. (42); to expose
it explicitly we recast that history dependence as a fiber
bundle over the space of forcing protocols, whose curva-
ture is the wetting–drying commutator [W, D] and whose
holonomy around a closed forcing loop is the hysteresis
loop itself (§VIII F).
When a REV is subjected to time-varying boundary
forcing—rainfall, drainage, evapotranspiration—its fill-
ing distribution g(r, t) evolves along a path that depends
on the forcing sequence, not just on its current value.
This path dependence is invisible to the equilibrium re-
tention curve θ(ψ), which assigns a single occupancy to
each water content; capturing it requires carrying the
configuration as a functional of the forcing history, which
is what the bundle below does.

X.
CONCLUSIONS
We have derived a continuum theory of unsaturated
soil water from the bottom up. Its outcome is a single
local balance [Eq. (42)],
∂tg(r, x, t) + ∇· F = C[g] −E −T ,
for the pore-occupancy field g(r, x, t)—the fraction of
pores of radius r that are water-filled at x.
The the-
ory is built by passing through three scales and is best
read as that passage. At the microscale, single inter-pore
transfers are governed by Hagen–Poiseuille rates κ(r) and
the capillary–gravitational driving potential Φ. At the
mesoscale, averaging over a representative volume turns
these into gain and loss operators whose difference is the
redistribution C[g], relaxing the occupancy toward the
equilibrium step geq = H(r∗−r).
At the continuum
scale, contracting the volume to a point promotes g to
a field and the inter-volume exchange to the transport
divergence ∇· F, giving Eq. (42). Everything else in the
paper—Richards’ equation, the conductivity, hysteresis,
preferential flow—is a limit, a moment, or a boundary
resolution of this one equation.
The continuum theory unifies phenomenologies that
have long been modelled separately. Richards’ equation
is its quasi-static (Da →0) Chapman–Enskog reduc-
tion, with the matric potential ψ and the conductivity
K(θ) emerging only in that limit, where local equilibrium
makes the map g →θ →ψ one-to-one; out of the limit

tial exists. Capillary-bundle and critical-path conductiv-

capillary-pressure models are its one-moment approxima-
tion. Hysteresis is the holonomy of the forcing bundle—a
geometric phase, not per-pore bistability—and preferen-
tial flow is what the same equation does where the lo-
cal Damköhler number Da(r, x) exceeds unity. The sin-
gle dimensionless group Da = τredis/τforcing organizes the
whole: Da ≪1 gives Richards’ equation, Da ∼1 gives
rate-dependent hysteresis, Da ≳1 gives preferential flow.
Whether Richards’ equation applies is therefore a prop-
erty of the soil–forcing combination at each point, not of
the soil alone.
Two features make the construction more than a re-
formulation. It is thermodynamically grounded: Eq. (42)
descends the Gibbs free energy as an Onsager gradient
flow, dF/dt ≤0 (Appendix D), with mass conservation
as the zeroth moment.
And it is constructive: every
ingredient—f(r), C(r, r′), ¯L, ¯τ, and hence κ, Φ, Da—
is a geometric property of the pore network, measurable
from micro-CT without calibration against retention or
conductivity data. The capillary–gravitational form of
Φ used throughout is a deliberate simplification, not a
limitation: because the theory uses the driving poten-
tial only through its values, the single point of entry
for new physics is Φ itself. Adding disjoining, osmotic,
or thermal terms to Φ carries the theory to adsorptive
films, saline soils, and coupled heat transport while leav-
ing the operator algebra, the gradient-flow structure, and
the Chapman–Enskog reduction unchanged.

A gas can sustain a

Darcy’s—the latter is a consequence of the CE expansion,
not its input.

vertical and horizontal profiles; quantify the dye-stained
fraction as a function of depth and pore size (via image
analysis of stained area vs. adjacent micro-CT of pore
geometry).
Key signature. Classical dual-porosity models im-
pose a fixed domain boundary; the theory predicts a con-
tinuously shifting crossover radius rDa ∝I. At low I,
staining should be uniform (matrix flow); at intermedi-
ate I, staining concentrates along macropores while the
matrix remains dry; at high I, nearly all pore space is by-
passed. The transition should be gradual, not a threshold
switch.
The gradualness of the transition presumes a con-
nectivity kernel C(r, r′) whose spectrum is continuous.

When the pore network is strongly bimodal—bodies and
throats separated by an order of magnitude, as in ag-
gregated soils—the spectrum of C(r, r′) separates into
distinct bands, and the crossover can acquire a genuine
threshold character rather than a smooth Da-controlled
shift:
the macropore band activates as a group once
the forcing exceeds the entry condition of the mediat-
ing throats. This spectral separation, and the conditions
under which it converts the continuous crossover into an
effective threshold, are analyzed in the companion pa-
per [9]; the dye-tracer protocol above can distinguish the
two behaviors by resolving whether activation is gradual
(unimodal) or stepwise (bimodal) in intensity.
Existing
evidence.
Dye-tracer
studies
at

show macropore activation above a pressure threshold
of approximately −3 cm.
The quantitative rDa ∝I

C.
K[g] non-uniqueness from micro-CT
Prediction. Two samples at the same θ but different
wetting histories have different K, by a factor of 10–100.
The theory predicts K as a functional of g(r), not just of
θ.
Experiment. Prepare matched pairs of undisturbed
columns from the same soil. Bring both to the same θ
via different paths (one by slow drainage from saturation,
the other by rapid wetting from dry).
Measure K by
steady-state flow at the target θ. Independently, image
both columns with micro-CT to extract g(r) (the pore-
by-pore filling state).
Compute K[g] from the theory
using the measured f(r), C(r, r′), and g(r); compare to
the measured K.
Key signature.
If K depends only on θ, the two
columns give the same K. If K = K[g], they differ, and
the theory predicts the correct ratio from the micro-CT-
observed g(r).
Existing evidence. Wildenschild et al. [32] demon-
strated micro-CT imaging of fluid configurations in
porous media. Recent work combining micro-CT with
pore network models [22, 33] provides the extrac-
tion pipeline (f(r), C(r, r′), κ(r)).
The prediction–
measurement comparison for K[g] at controlled g(r) has
not been performed.
D.
Field capacity as percolation threshold
Prediction. The residual water content θr should co-
incide with the water percolation threshold θc estimated
independently from network topology.
Experiment. For a given soil, determine θc from the
pore network extracted via micro-CT (compute the bond
percolation threshold on the actual network). Indepen-
dently, measure θFC by standard drainage to −33 kPa
and θr from the van Genuchten fit. The theory predicts
θr ≈θc.
Key signature. In classical models, θr is a fitting pa-
rameter with no structural origin. The theory predicts a
quantitative relationship between a topological quantity
(θc from network analysis) and a hydraulic one (θr from
the retention curve).

Heterogeneity penalty exp(−4σ2)
Prediction.
For log-normal pore-size distributions,
Ksat is reduced below the parallel-tube prediction by the
factor exp(−4σ2
ln r). This penalty depends only on the
width of the PSD, not on the median pore size.
Experiment.
Measure Ksat and the full PSD (via
micro-CT or mercury intrusion) for a suite of soils span-

Deviations indicate additional physics (spatial correla-

F.
Non-equilibrium water retention from dynamic
micro-CT
Prediction. During rapid infiltration, the pore-filling
state g(r) departs from the equilibrium step function
H(r∗−r): large pores that should be empty (by cap-
illary ordering) are partially filled.
Experiment. Time-resolved (4D) synchrotron micro-
CT during controlled infiltration at high I. Track indi-
vidual pore filling events; compare the measured g(r, t)
to the equilibrium prediction geq(r, θ(t)).
Key signature.
At low I, g(r, t) should track geq.
At high I, large pores fill before small pores have equili-
brated, producing g(r) > geq(r) for r > rDa. This is the
microscopic signature of non-equilibrium transport that
the kinetic equation describes.
Existing evidence. Dynamic synchrotron micro-CT
of water infiltration has been demonstrated at frame
rates of ∼15 s [25], resolving individual Haines jumps
and showing that macropores remain largely unsaturated
even under intense rainfall—consistent with the non-
selective filling mechanism of the theory.
G.
Relaxation spectrum from multi-frequency
measurements
Prediction. The redistribution timescale τ(r) ∝1/r
spans 3+ decades, from sub-second for macropores to
minutes for micropores.
Experiment. Apply step perturbations (sudden pres-
sure change) and monitor the time-dependent response of
θ at multiple frequencies using dielectric spectroscopy or
TDR at high temporal resolution (∼0.1 s). Fit the re-
sponse to a spectrum of relaxation times.
Key signature.
A stretched-exponential or power-
law relaxation (not single-exponential), with the spec-
trum width and shape predicted from the measured f(r)
and the κ(r) ∝r2 scaling. Near field capacity, the slowest
mode should diverge as (θ −θc)−3.76.
