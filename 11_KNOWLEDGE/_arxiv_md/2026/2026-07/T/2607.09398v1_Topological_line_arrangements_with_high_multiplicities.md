PAOLO ACETO AND MARCO GOLLA
Abstract. We investigate constraints on the existence of topological and smooth
realisations of combinatorial line arrangements and (nk)-configurations in the
complex projective plane CP2. By replacing complex lines with locally-flatly or
smoothly embedded 2-spheres, we explore the extent to which classical geomet-
ric results, such as Hirzebruch’s inequality, persist in the topological or smooth
category.
We introduce two classes of special line arrangements that we call odd and even.
We provide constraints for any smoothly realised, non-trivial, odd arrangement via
Furuta’s 10/8-Theorem. By looking at branched double covers and using the G-
signature theorem, we study topologically realised, non-trivial, even arrangement.
Finally, we establish a new lower bound for (nk)-configurations, showing that any
topologically realised configuration must satisfy n ≥k2 −5, which implies the
non-existence of topological realisations for finite projective planes.
1. Introduction
A classical topic in geometry is the study of line arrangements and configura-
tions (viewed as combinatorial objects) and their geometric realisations via points
and lines in the real or complex projective plane.
In this paper we investigate
topological realisations of arrangements and configurations with the general goal of
understanding how current techniques in 4-manifold topology compare with more
classical geometric results.
A line arrangement is given by a pair of finite sets (L, P) of lines and points and
an incidence relation I ⊂L × P such that for any pair of lines ℓ, ℓ′ ∈L there exists
a unique point p ∈P with (ℓ, p) = (ℓ′, p) ∈I. When (ℓ, p) ∈I we say that ℓis
incident to p or that p lies on ℓ. The multiplicity of a point is the number of lines
that are incident to it. For any k ≥2 we denote by tk(A) the number of points of
multiplicity k of the line arrangement A. We will omit A from the notation when
there is no risk of confusion.
A pencil of d lines is a line arrangement with td = 1 (and therefore tm = 0 when
m ̸= d).
An almost-pencil of d lines is a line arrangement with td−1 = 1, and
t2 = d −1. We will say that a line arrangement is non-trivial if it is neither a pencil
nor an almost-pencil.
A geometric realisation of a line arrangement A is a collection of lines in CP2
which realises the incidence relations of A. One way to study geometric realisations
is by looking at constraints on the tm’s. A classical result of Hirzebruch [Hir83]
states that, for any geometric realisation of a non-trivial line arrangement with d
lines one has

In particular, any such realisation has at least one double or triple point. Hirze-

the BMY inequality, and therefore its proof does not have an immediate topological
meaning.
By replacing lines with locally-flatly (respectively, smoothly), pairwise trans-
versely, embedded 2-spheres in CP2 we obtain the notion of topological (resp. smooth)
realisation of a line arrangement. In particular, each 2-sphere is necessarily homol-
ogous to a complex line.
It is natural to ask if in the more flexible topological setting one can have more
degenerate realisations. For instance: is it still true that one always has at least
one double or triple point?
In this generality, the problem seems to be out of
reach. However, constraints analogous to Hirzebruch’s inequality can be obtained
in particular cases. We call a line arrangement even if it contains an even number
of lines and tk = 0 whenever k is odd. An arrangement is odd if tk = 0 whenever k
is even.
Theorem 1.1. For any smoothly realised, non-trivial, odd line arrangement we have
t3 + t5 + t7 > 0.
This result is a consequence of a more general inequality in the style of Hirze-
bruch’s results (Theorem 4.1 below).
We consider the 4-manifold obtained by

neighbourhoods of the proper transforms of all the lines and glue back in suitable
positive-definite linear plumbings of spheres. This results in a closed, spin 4-manifold

we have to work in the smooth category. Along the way, a careful analysis of the

Let n, k be two positive integers. An (nk)-configuration is a combinatorial line
arrangement (P, L) with n lines, such that there exist P′ ⊂P comprising n points,
such that each point in P′ is contained in exactly k lines, and each line in L contains
exactly k points in P′.
Just like we did for line arrangements, we reserve the term “configuration” for the
combinatorial data. One can then talk about geometric, smooth, or topological real-
isations. A standard reference for configurations is Grunbaum’s monograph [Gr¨u09].
The main question we are interested in is: given k, what is the smallest n such that
an (nk)-configuration is realised?
It is very easy to see that for any (nk)-configuration (P, L), we have n ≥k2−k+1.
Pick a point in P′: each of the k lines that pass through it contains at least k −1
other points of P′, so we have at least k(k −1) + 1 points in P. Configurations

Organisation. The paper is organised as follows. In Section 2, we establish the
combinatorial foundations of line arrangements, fixing notation for multiplicities
and reviewing essential counting lemmas. In Section 3, we define the class of q-
divisible arrangements, which generalise even arrangements, and derive constraints
on their realisability by constructing branched covers and applying the G-signature
theorem. Section 4 is devoted to the study of odd arrangements; here, we employ
smooth 4-manifold techniques, specifically Furuta’s 10/8-Theorem and Heegaard
Floer correction terms, to obtain multiplicity bounds.
Finally, in Section 5, we
apply our results to (nk)-configurations and finite projective planes, establishing
new topological lower bounds and proving the non-realisability of finite projective
planes in the topological category.

A special case of configurations is given by finite projective planes.
Definition 5.3. A finite projective plane is a pair (P, L) where P is a finite set
(whose elements are called points) and L is a collection of subsets of P (called lines),
such that:
• any two lines intersect in one point;
• for every two points there exists a unique line containing them;
• there exist four points that are not in the same line.
Two finite projective planes (P, L) and (P′, L′) are isomorphic if there exists two
bijections π: P1 →P2 and λ: L1 →L2 such that λ(L) ∩λ(L′) = π(L ∩L′) for every
L, L′ ∈L.
It is known that if (P, L) is a finite projective plane, then P and L both have
cardinality q2 + q + 1 for some integer q, that each line contains q + 1 points and
that, dually, every point belongs to q + 1 lines. The integer q is called the order of
the finite projective plane. In fact, a pair (P, L) is a finite projective plane of order
q if and only if it is an ((q2 + q + 1)q+1)-configuration.
Example 5.4. Let q be a prime power and identify FqP2, the projective plane over
the finite field of order q. Then the lines of FqP2, form a finite projective plane of
order q. It is known that there exist finite projective planes of order 9 that are not
isomorphic to F9P2 [HSK59]. It is not known whether there exist finite projective
planes of order q for some q which is not a prime power.
Theorem 5.5. No finite projective plane can be topologically realised in CP2.
