THE C∗-ALGEBRAS OF LOCALLY FINITE UNDIRECTED GRAPHS: A
COMPLETE DESCRIPTION OF THEIR K-THEORY
D. PASK
Abstract. We study the C∗-algebra C∗(Γ) of a locally finite undirected (Serre) graph
Γ and compute its K-theory. The algebra is defined intrinsically, as the graph-of-groups
algebra with all groups trivial, and is shown to be independent of the choice of orientation.
Its structure is accessed through a passage to directed graphs: for every locally finite Γ there
is a row-finite directed graph EΓ with C∗(Γ) ∼= C∗(EΓ), and for essential Γ this identifies
C∗(Γ) with the Cuntz–Krieger algebra of the Bass–Hashimoto (non-backtracking) matrix
TΓ.
This makes the whole directed-graph K-theory machinery available and, unlike the
directed case, produces K-groups that read off the geometry of Γ: for a finite essential graph
of genus g ≥2 one has K0(C∗(Γ)) ∼= Zg ⊕Z/(g −1)Z and K1(C∗(Γ)) ∼= H1(Γ) ∼= Zg, and
in general K∗is governed by the genus, the number of ends and the number of dead-ends.
We record the resulting classification by genus: the algebras are finite-dimensional or AF at
genus 0, AT precisely for finite graphs at genus 1, and unital Kirchberg algebras for finite
essential graphs of genus ≥2.
1. Introduction
The K-theory of the C∗-algebra of a directed graph is dynamical in character: for a finite
graph it is the Bowen–Franks data of the associated shift of finite type, and it bears little
relation to the geometry of the underlying graph. The starting point of this paper is the
observation that for undirected graphs the situation is entirely different. To a locally finite
graph Γ in the sense of Serre we associate an intrinsically defined C∗-algebra C∗(Γ)—the
graph-of-groups algebra of [2] with all vertex and edge groups trivial—and its K-theory turns
out to be a direct transcription of the geometry of Γ: the genus g (first Betti number), the
graph valency γ (which, when finite, counts the infinite chains), and the number d of dead-
ends. The first computations of this kind are due to Cornelissen, Lorscheid and Marcolli [3]
for finite graphs without sinks, and to Iyudu [5] for infinite graphs of finite Betti number;
the purpose of this paper is to place those computations in an intrinsic framework and
to complete the classification across all genera, including the non-essential graphs and the
extreme genera g = 0, 1 lying outside the scope of [3, 5].
Our techniques are those of directed graph C∗-algebras, organised around a single con-
struction: to every locally finite Γ we associate a row-finite directed graph EΓ, built from
the reduced paths of length two with a tail adjoined at each dead-end, and prove that
C∗(Γ) ∼= C∗(EΓ) (Theorem 4.3). For essential Γ the graph EΓ is the undirected analogue of
the dual graph of Bates [1], and the realisation identifies C∗(Γ) with the Cuntz–Krieger alge-
bra of the Bass–Hashimoto (non-backtracking) operator TΓ (Theorem 4.5). The K-theoretic

Two combinatorial invariants organise everything below. The genus (first Betti number)
g = g(Γ) is the rank of H1(Γ), the number of geometric edges outside a spanning tree; g = 0
exactly when Γ is a tree; a finite tree cannot be essential. For an infinite graph of finite

graph valency γ = γ(Γ) is the sum of the valencies at the root and branching vertices of
those trees, in the following sense. Suppose we fix a finite subgraph of Γ with Betti number
g(Γ) (the definition does not depend on this choice). A root vertex is a root of one of the

vertex is a vertex of a tree with one incoming edge and more than one outgoing edge which
leads to an infinite path; if n is the number of such outgoing edges, the valency of that vertex
is n −1.

Moves on undirected graphs. Since the genus is the first Betti number, any two con-
nected graphs of the same genus are homotopy equivalent, and the genus-preserving moves
on Γ are generated by elementary expansions and collapses: subdivision of an edge, vertex
splitting, and the attachment or pruning of trees. The first two preserve the Morita class
of C∗(Γ): subdivision and vertex splitting are inverse to the contraction of a non-loop edge
whose endpoints are not dead-ends, which, by the proof of Theorem 5.10, is implemented
on EΓ by the collapse move of [15, Theorem 5.2] and so preserves the stable isomorphism
class; these moves preserve γ and d as well as g. Tree attachment, by contrast, preserves the
genus but changes the algebra: the bouquet Bg has K0 ∼= Zg ⊕Z/(g −1)Z (Theorem 5.10),
while Bg with a single pendant edge attached has K0 ∼= Zg+1 (Theorem 5.11 with γ = 0,
d = 1), and attaching an infinite ray instead gives Zg+1 through the graph valency. Thus
the Morita class of C∗(Γ) remembers precisely the triple (g, γ, d), together with essentiality
and finiteness, and not merely the homotopy type of Γ.
For finite essential graphs of genus g ≥2 the genus alone rules: γ = d = 0 is forced, the
algebras are unital Kirchberg algebras, and the Kirchberg–Phillips classification [9] shows
that every genus-preserving move between such graphs preserves the stable isomorphism
class. Isomorphism is finer: the class of the unit in K0 has order (g −1)/ gcd(g −1, |Γ0|)
[3], so subdivision changes the isomorphism class while preserving the stable one. Finally,
the in- and out-splittings of [15] and the Cuntz splice act on EΓ but in general destroy the
dual-graph form, so they have no undirected counterparts; whether the moves above generate
stable isomorphism within the class of C∗-algebras of undirected graphs, as the moves of [15]
do for simple graph algebras, is an interesting open question.
