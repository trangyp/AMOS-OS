TOPOLOGICAL FULL GROUPS AND STABLE RANK ONE
DAVID KERR AND SPYRIDON PETRAKOS
Abstract. We establish stable rank one for the reduced group C∗-algebras of the C∗-simple
topological full groups and dynamical alternating groups constructed by Kerr and Tucker-Drob.
The proof relies on both type II1 and type III phenomena, in the first case via the use of Følner
towers and in the second via Ozawa’s recent results on selflessness as applied to direct products of
free products.
1. Introduction
Despite the deep and productive analogies that take hold between von Neumann algebras
and C∗-algebras when these become highly noncommutative, the study of simple C∗-algebras
remains beset by many complications of a topological nature. The comparability and divisibility
of projections that are both automatic in factors of type II or III cannot even be guaranteed in
approximate form in simple unital C∗-algebras, and so one must postulate strict comparison and
almost divisibility as properties whose validity must be tested for in examples.
Even a “type II1” expression of zero-dimensionality like stable rank one (i.e., the density of
invertible elements) can fail in simple stably finite AH algebras [48]. All of these attributes would
seem (with the necessary restriction of stable finiteness for stable rank one) to be prerequisites
for the type of fine-scale analysis that a Connes-type structure and classification theory would
demand, and indeed if one limits one’s scope to the “II1 factor” setting of simple separable finite
C∗-algebras, as we do in this paper, then one can derive all of them from the McDuff-like property
of Z-stability that has become a cornerstone of the Elliott classification program [40]1.
Absent Z-stability, it becomes a challenge to assay a simple unital finite C∗-algebra for any of
the properties of strict comparison, almost divisibility, and stable rank one. In practice stable
rank one has been more amenable to verification but also plays a somewhat less central (if
sometimes surprising) role in issues around classification, while strict comparison has proven
especially recalcitrant outside the scope of nuclearity, although like stable rank one it can be
directly monitored and controlled in simple AH algebras and fails to hold for some examples
within this class [47] (see [42] for a survey). These differences are reflected in the partial two-out-
of-three sociology that has been observed among the three properties: for simple separable unital
nonelementary finite C∗-algebras, almost divisibility and stable rank one each follow from the
other two properties [45, Corollary 8.12][27, Corollary 1.3].
The present paper is concerned with stable rank and strict comparison in simple unital reduced
group C∗-algebras. These C∗-algebras possess a unique tracial state [3] and are never nuclear (the
simplicity is in fact equivalent to the nonexistence of amenable uniformly recurrent subgroups
[18]) and are thus natural and interesting test cases to consider as one crosses the threshold

For a large class of amenable acting groups, including elementary amenable groups and groups
of subexponential growth, almost finiteness is known to hold as soon as the action is free and
the phase space has finite covering dimension [21, 30, 4, 31], but the picture is most complete
for free minimal Zd-actions, where URPC is automatic (this was shown in [33] using a tiling
result from [13]) and almost finiteness, the small boundary property, and mean dimension zero
are all equivalent (for the equivalence of the first two combine [29] and Theorem 6.1 of [23] and
for the equivalence of the last two see [13]). Moreover, when the mean dimension is nonzero it
is possible for the crossed product not to have strict comparison [12]. This gives the impression
that stable rank one is a more robust condition than strict comparison, at least for simple crossed
products coming from actions on spaces, where the possibility remains open that the stable rank
is always equal to one. Going further in this direction, recent work of Bell, Geffen, and the first
author shows that Følner-like tower structure can also be applied through a Baire category lens
to establish stable rank one in some nonnuclear tracial crossed products, notably those coming
from a generic weakly mixing topological free minimal action of a free group on the Cantor set
preserving at least one Borel probability measure. What happens generically in the free group case
is that one can build Følner towers from one of the generators in a way that confines the freeness
of the relations with the other generators to much smaller dynamical scales, thereby ensuring the
approximate invariance of the towers with respect to all generators. This Baire category maneuver
allows one to bypass the unresolved problem of whether any of these actions have dynamical
comparison, which is a critical ingredient in (i) and (ii) above.
It would seem that these “type II1” Følner techniques should not be applicable in the setting of
simple group C∗-algebras if we understand C∗-simplicity to be a kind of saturation by nonamenabil-
ity. Indeed progress to date has relied in one way or another on group-geometric phenomena of a
type III flavour, specifically ones for which the prototype is precisely the freeness that we were
wanting to suppress in the dynamical framework above. The pioneering step in this direction was
Dykema, Haagerup, and Rørdam’s use of a rapid-decay-type argument to establish stable rank one
for the reduced group C∗-algebras of nonelementary free products, and in particular of free groups
[7]. The class of stable rank one groups was subsequently expanded so as to eventually come to
include all acylindrically hyperbolic groups [5, 11, 36]. Until very recently not much was known

Our aim in this paper is to address the family of C∗-simple groups constructed by the first
author and Tucker-Drob in Theorem 8.7 of [24] as topological full groups and dynamical alternating
groups of topologically free minimal subshift actions of amenable groups on the Cantor set. Many
of the dynamical alternating groups in this collection are simple and finitely generated, properties
that follow as soon as the group is finitely generated and the action is topologically free, minimal,
and expansive (i.e., a subshift over a finite alphabet) [32]. The assumption on the acting group
that supports the construction in [24] (a tiling property called property ID) incorporates an
entropy condition that is not directly relevant for us here but was included there so as to be
able to produce an uncountable infinity of examples for a given acting group by varying the
dynamical entropy, which is an invariant of continuous orbit equivalence and consequently also
of the dynamical alternating group. We will recall the construction in its more general form in
Section 4. It was inspired by the original Z2 example in [8], and, like that example, arranges for
nonamenability by using an idea of van Douwen that embeds the free product Z2 ∗Z2 ∗Z2 using a
ping-pong argument over a full shift along a copy of Z in the group [46]. See also [43] for a related
construction that geometrically replicates a full shift in a way that applies to all non-virtually
cyclic amenable groups. When the acting group is virtually cyclic, the presence (real or virtual) of
a “one-dimensional” full shift that unites all of these examples is incompatible with minimality,
and it is a remarkable fact that free minimal actions of virtually cyclic groups on the Cantor set
always produce an amenable topological full group [16, 43].
In [24] the first author and Tucker-Drob employed Følner towers and a probabilistic argument
inspired by [17] to show that, for every minimal topological free action of a countable amenable
group on the Cantor set, the group von Neumann algebras of the topological full group and
dynamical alternating group have property Gamma. Later the two authors of the present paper
discovered a non-probabilistic approach (but still using Følner towers) that strengthens the
property Gamma conclusion to McDuffness. We do not know however whether the reduced
group C∗-algebra of any of these groups is Z-stable. We also do not know whether these group
C∗-algebras are ever selfless or have strict comparison. On the other hand we have been able to
establish stable rank one for the C∗-simple examples from [24]. The underlying actions all possess
a property we call having a specification ridge (Definition 4.1), which is sufficient to reach the
conclusion:

Our strategy for establishing Theorem A is to simulate the dynamical property of square
divisibility from [2] in a way that will similarly yield stable rank one via a Rørdam-type argument
[38] that involves the near block diagonalization of zero divisors of a special form (in the spirit
of [26]) and unitary rotation to a nilpotent element. We proceed as in [24] by using a “striated”
Følner tower for the Γ-action to create a permutational Bernoulli structure whose phase space
lives within C∗
λ(G) as a spectral object (one may think of it as an “exponentiation” of the Følner
tower along the striation) and then applying some probability theory to produce a uniform near
partition of the Bernoulli space that is approximately invariant under a prescribed finite set of
elements in G. We require the near partition to be much finer than the two-member partition that
sufficed for the purposes of [24], and so we need to manufacture the Bernoulli structure within a
permutational wreath product whose base is a very large finite alternating group, like the ones
used in [22]. To produce the ersatz square divisibility some care is required in the construction,
manipulation, and double-indexing of the near partition, in particular to negotiate issues around
approximate invariance and zero division that are either not present or handled differently in [2].
The point where our situation critically departs from [2] is that we cannot replicate the
subequivalences in the definition of square divisibility in a dynamical way within the Bernoulli
structure, or even C∗-algebraically within the ambient wreath product. This is due to the presence
of the trivial representation of the ambient wreath product, which sits underneath one of the sets
in the Bernoulli space and cannot be budged within the group algebra of the wreath product,
where it appears as a one-dimensional direct summand. If the entire group G were amenable,
its trivial representation would also sit underneath the same set, which we could then not even
move within C∗
λ(G) itself, causing the whole argument to break down. This gives a nice dynamical
illustration of how, in the context of groups, nonamenability is related to the ability to move
around projections and other positive elements in the C∗-algebra. In its most permissive form this
transportability of positive elements is associated with C∗-algebraic simplicity (although strict
comparison still sometimes fails in this situation), and indeed it remains unclear how much we
can move around the trivial representation of the wreath product if we merely assume G to be
nonamenable.2 It is at this juncture that we invoke C∗-simplicity through the specific form of
the construction that was devised to produce it in [24]. Using a variation of the van Douwen
ping-pong argument, we embed into G a collection of nonelementary free products whose first
factors are the copies of the alternating group forming the base of the wreath product. These
embedded free products are configured so as to have pairwise disjoint supports in the topological
full group, so that their direct product G0 also forms a subgroup of G. The group C∗-algebra
C∗
λ(G0) will then be selfless by the previously mentioned work of Ozawa [34]. We can consequently
exploit the strict comparison that ensues from selflessness to realize the desired subequivalences

3. Preliminaries on towers and topological full groups
Let Γ ↷X be an action of a countable discrete group on the Cantor set (all such actions are
assumed to be continuous). The topological full group of the action, written F(Γ, X), is the discrete
group of all homeomorphisms h : X →X for which there is a clopen partition X = A1 ⊔· · · ⊔An
and s1, . . . , sn ∈Γ such that hx = six for all i = 1, . . . , n and x ∈Ai. This group is countable
given that Γ is countable and there are only countably many clopen partitions of X. The support
of a element h ∈F(Γ, X) is the clopen set of all x ∈X such that hx ̸= x.

By a tower for the action Γ ↷X we mean a pair (S, B) where S is a finite subset of Γ (the
shape) and B is a subset of X (the base) such that the sets sB for s ∈S (the levels) are pairwise
disjoint. The tower is clopen if B is clopen, which by the continuity of the action is equivalent to
all of the levels being clopen.
Canonically associated to a clopen tower (S, B) is the embedding σ 7→hσ of the symmetric
group over S into F(Γ, X) defined by hσx = σ(s)s−1x for every s ∈S and x ∈sB and hσx = x for
every x ∈X \ SB. We write S(S, B) for the subgroup of F(Γ, X) obtained under this embedding.
We also thereby obtain a copy A(S, B) ⊆S(S, B) of the alternating group over S. The subgroup
of F(Γ, X) generated by such embedded finite alternating groups over all clopen towers with three
levels is called the dynamical alternating group and denoted by A(Γ, X). This was introduced
by Nekrashevych in [32], where the following facts were established. If the action has no finite
orbits (in particular, if Γ ↷X is minimal) then A(Γ, X) is equal to the subgroup generated by
the embedded finite alternating groups arising from all clopen towers. The dynamical alternating
group is contained in the commutator subgroup of F(Γ, X) and is equal to it when the action is
almost finite (this relies on [28], as explained in Section 4 of [32]). If the action Γ ↷X is minimal
then A(Γ, X) is simple, while if Γ is finitely generated and Γ ↷X has no orbits of cardinality less
than 5 then A(Γ, X) is finitely generated.

5. Embedding free products
Lemma 5.1. Let q ≥4 and let Γ ↷X ⊆{1, . . . , q}Γ be a right subshift action with a specification
ridge. Let U ⊆X be a nonempty open set and d ∈N. Then there exist a clopen tower (S, B) with
|S| = d and SB ⊆U, and an involution g ∈A(Γ, X) supported in SB such that S(S, B) is free
with respect to g.

set in {1, . . . , q}Γ with X. Having a specification ridge guarantees that all such subsets are infinite.
The cylinder set is determined by a configuration z ∈{1, . . . , q}K over some window K ⋐Γ.
Take an infinite cyclic subgroup H = ⟨a⟩⊆Γ along which the action has a specification ridge.

specification ridge with E taken there to be K ∪{ak : 0 ≤k ≤|K| + 1}. A configuration x ∈X

in U. We also fix 0 ≤m1 < m2 ≤|K| + 1 with ami /∈K (these will be our control coordinates,
which we will be freely allowed to choose in {1, . . . , q}).

(1) ckx is stamped for 0 ≤k ≤d −1,
(2) x(am1) = 2,
(3) x(am1ck) = 1 for 1 ≤k ≤d −1.

7. Zero division
Recall that, to approximate a given noninvertible element a in the group C∗-algebra with
something invertible, the strategy is to produce a near block diagonal matrix modelling a that can
be unitarily rotated to a nilpotent element. We cannot do this for a itself but must first unitarily
rotate a so that it divides into zero on the left and right by a common nonzero positive element
(in fact projection) out of which we can create enough zeros on the matrix diagonal to facilitate
the desired rotation to nilpotence. In the crossed product setting of [26, 2] this positive element is
a function on the space, and here, analogously, it will be an indicator function in our embedded
permutational Bernoulli structure. In [26, 2] the positive element is delivered by Lemma 6.1 and
Proposition 6.2 of [26], but we cannot make direct use of these results or their techniques since
they do not presuppose that one is working at a fixed dynamical scale. In particular we need to
avoid the functional calculus methods used in [26], since these are fatal to the control we need on
the coefficients of approximations in the algebraic crossed product. It is the discreteness of our
phase space that saves us here. As in [26, 2], Lemma 3.5 of [38] will permit us to assume from the
outset, via a perturbation, that the element a to be approximated by something invertible is itself
already a two-sided zero divisor, which explains this hypothesis in the lemma below.
