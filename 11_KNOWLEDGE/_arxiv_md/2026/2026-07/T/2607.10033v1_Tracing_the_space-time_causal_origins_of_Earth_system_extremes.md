Identifying the causes of Earth’s extremes is challenging because counterfac-
tual experiments are not possible in the observed world, while numerical exper-
iments are computationally expensive and subject to biases. Data-driven causal
discovery offers a complementary path, but existing approaches can fail in under-
sampled, high-dimensional regimes, and may not recover multi-timestep, multi-
variate pathways leading to particular events. We introduce Tracer of Causal Evo-
lutions in Space and Time (TraCE-ST), a probabilistic Lagrangian approach that
produces event-conditioned causal trajectories in multivariate gridded data. In
synthetic experiments and real-world extreme events, TraCE-ST recovers known
causal drivers and estimates their relative contributions, while also highlight-

Introduction
Discovering causal dependencies in high-dimensional dynamical systems provides the mechanistic
foundation for explaining and predicting complex physical phenomena. This task is especially chal-
lenging in Earth systems, where weather and climate extremes emerge from multiscale interactions,
nonlinear feedbacks, and nonstationary dynamics (1,2), with broad socioeconomic, biological, and
ecological implications. Explaining why a particular event occurred requires more than detecting
statistical association (3); it requires identifying the variables and processes that shaped the se-
quence of states leading to a specific outcome (4, 5). To achieve this, we often rely on numerical
models, but they are computationally expensive and prone to biases (1). Causal discovery methods,
such as Granger causality, Peter–Clark Momentary Conditional Independence (PCMCI) and its
extensions, DYNOTEARS, and deep Koopman-based approaches, offer an alternative to address
this challenge by inferring directed causal dependencies from multivariate time series (1,2,6–12).
However, these methods are generally designed to recover causal structure under assumptions about
observability, sampling, temporal ordering, and, in many cases, stationarity, that make them chal-
lenging to use in high-dimensional settings with few temporal samples. Furthermore, they do not
produce event-specific explanations of the realized causal chain leading to an outcome (9).
Recent methods, including Causal Space-Time Stencil Learning (CaStLe) and its multivariate
version (M-CaStLe), extend causal discovery to high-dimensional gridded systems by exploiting
spatial regularities to estimate causal dependencies in relatively short temporal records (13, 14).
These methods substantially reduce the sample-complexity barriers that arise in Earth-system
data, where the number of spatial locations and variables can far exceed the number of temporal
observations. However, local causal discovery and event explanation are distinct tasks. M-CaStLe
identifies directed dependencies among variables and nearby states (𝑡−1 →𝑡), but it does not
directly explain how a particular event emerged through a realized sequence of interacting processes.
Earth system events often arise from extended, multi-step pathways in which local and remote drivers

## Page 4


interact across space, time, and variables. The remaining challenge is therefore to move from local
causal structure to event-conditioned causal pathways that connect upstream processes to a realized
event while quantifying the relative contributions of co-occurring and competing drivers. This
event-conditioned pathway framing is increasingly important for Earth system science as many
weather and climate extremes compound, intensify, or become more frequent under continued
warming (15,16).
To address this challenge, we introduce the Tracer of Causal Evolutions in Space and Time
(TraCE-ST), a Lagrangian-inspired framework for identifying event-conditioned causal pathways
among interacting Earth system variables. TraCE-ST is motivated by Lagrangian trajectory methods
used across Earth system science, which trace the histories of air parcels, water parcels, particles,
or other transported quantities backward or forward in time using spatiotemporal fields to identify
upstream sources, transport pathways, and process histories (17–22). Analogously, TraCE-ST traces
inferred causal influence backward through localized directed graphs rather than tracing physical
parcels through a velocity field: TraCE-ST recursively follows inferred causal parents across suc-
cessive time steps and outputs the multi-step chain of interactions that precede the realized state of
a target variable within the studied system. This framework can also flexibly accommodate differ-
ent causal discovery engines (e.g., PCMCI, DYNOTEARS, or other time-series graph discovery
methods), provided that they return directed temporal dependencies suitable for pathway tracing.
A probabilistic TraCE-ST formulation further enables estimation of co-occurring causal pathways
and their relative contributions. More generally, TraCE-ST provides a systematic way to trace cross-
variable causal pathways among interacting physical fields, identify remote influences mediated
by local interactions, and estimate the relative importance of distinct processes influencing a local
outcome.
We validate TraCE-ST hierarchically across systems of increasing complexity, including two
synthetic cases and three real-world scenarios: (i) the development of Tropical Storm Debby in
2006 (23,24), (ii) volcanic aerosol dispersion following the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption (25), and
(iii) the formation of the 2021 North American Pacific Northwest (PNW) heatwave (26). Across
these applications, TraCE-ST outputs causal pathways consistent with established mechanisms
while also shedding light on less-studied candidate drivers. By transforming causal discovery
from local graph estimation into dynamic, event-centered pathway tracing, TraCE-ST offers a new

Within M-CaStLe, an underlying time-series graph discovery algorithm (e.g., Granger causality,
PCMCI, or DYNOTEARS) identifies statistically significant causal parents based on conditional
independence tests or predictive power. Once the set of causal parents PW,R is identified, these
parents are grouped according to the variable, the sign of their inferred causal relationship to the
child, and their spatial direction relative to 𝑠(the center of the stencil B𝑟(𝑠)). The DBSCAN algo-
rithm (density-based spatial clustering) (28) is used to identify these parent groups (see Materials
and Methods). This parent-grouping step aggregates parents that represent coherent propagation
patterns in the stencil space. Here, “causal strength” refers to the magnitude returned by the chosen
causal discovery engine for each significant parent-child relationship (e.g., regression coefficient
or conditional-dependence measure).
One of the resulting parent groups from the set PW,R is then selected to define the next step of
the causal tracing procedure. The selection can be performed deterministically or probabilistically.
Deterministic selection would involve choosing the parent group with the largest aggregated causal
strength (favoring a consistent propagation pattern) or largest average causal strength (favoring
isolated but strong parent-child relationships) among its members (see Materials and Methods).
Probabilistic selection would involve using weights proportional to the causal strength (aggregated
or average) of each parent group. The latter formulation enables a Monte Carlo exploration of
multiple competing causal pathways. The variable associated with the selected parent group be-
comes the child variable for the next tracing step. The window is also shifted backward in time,
together with the trajectory; at the next step, the corresponding window is W = {𝑡−𝐿, . . . , 𝑡−1}.

Several hyperparameters impact the behavior of TraCE-ST, including those related to the track-
ing procedure, movement of the region, selection of causal parents, admissible causal dependencies,
and the underlying causal discovery algorithm (see Materials and Methods). Since different hy-
perparameter combinations can yield distinct plausible probabilistic causal origins for the target
child 𝐶( 𝑗0)(R0, 𝑡0), sensitivity to them should be explored and used to characterize uncertainty in

the obtained causal pathways. Given this sensitivity, it is important that each TraCE-ST application
defines physically motivated exploration ranges. In the Materials and Methods, we describe each
hyperparameter and provide guidance for selecting appropriate ranges. In general, the temporal
resolution and spatial grid spacing of the data, TraCE-ST time step, and stencil radius should be
chosen so that the relevant physical processes can be resolved at the scales of interest. Likewise, the
temporal window and region size should be selected to provide sufficient samples for M-CaStLe
while maintaining the assumptions of approximate temporal and spatial stationarity.
Moreover, when possible, application-specific criteria based on domain knowledge should also
be used to discard implausible trajectory ensembles and further constrain the explored hyperparam-
eter space. Such criteria may include requiring a minimum average trajectory length, a minimum
fraction of ensemble members that complete the prescribed tracing period, and qualitative consis-
tency across neighboring hyperparameter settings, as well as Earth-system-specific constraints such
as physically plausible trajectory lengths and avoidance of implausible causal transitions. Rather
than seeking a unique optimal hyperparameter configuration, the objective is to identify causal
pathways that remain qualitatively robust within a physically admissible region of parameter space;
lack of such robustness provides an a posteriori indication that the parameters may not be adequate
for the case considered. Accordingly, we restricted our following analyses to hyperparameter set-
tings that yielded stable causal discovery and trajectory behavior while avoiding frequent premature
termination or trajectory stagnation.
Controlled cases with prescribed causal trajectories
First, we evaluate TraCE-ST in two controlled synthetic cases. In the first controlled case, the
system consists of two variables, 𝑉1 and 𝑉2, in which a Gaussian perturbation with a prescribed
trajectory propagates across 𝑉1, while 𝑉2 is correlated with 𝑉1 but does not causally influence
it. Consequently, along the prescribed trajectory, the true causal parent of 𝑉1 at each time step
𝑡−𝑖is 𝑉1 at the previous lag 𝑡−(𝑖+1), despite the presence of cross-variable correlation. With this
configuration, we test whether TraCE-ST can correctly reconstruct the causal propagation of a
coherent feature in space and time while avoiding spurious attribution to a correlated variable.
In the second controlled case, the system contains three variables, 𝑉1, 𝑉2, and 𝑉3: two Gaussian

perturbations move independently along prescribed trajectories in 𝑉1 and 𝑉3 and collide spatially,
producing a linear response in 𝑉2. This setup mimics situations in which a target child arises from
multiple interacting causal parents, allowing us to evaluate whether the probabilistic formulation
of TraCE-ST can recover these parents and quantify their relative contributions.
Both experiments are conducted on a 200 × 200 Cartesian grid with grid spacing of Δ𝑥=
Δ𝑦= 1. Gaussian perturbations are chosen because they provide spatially coherent signals whose
propagation can be prescribed precisely while remaining representative of localized anomalies
commonly observed in geophysical fields. The perturbations have an amplitude 1 and a spatial
scale of approximately 20×20 grid cells, and propagate along deterministic trajectories for 30 time
steps prior to the event time 𝑡0. Note that the spatial size of the prescribed perturbations is large
enough to expand over multiple pixels, in line with the assumption of spatial homogeneity from
M-CaStLe (see Materials and Methods). To emulate non-causal variability, all variables include
spatially coherent autoregressive Gaussian noise 𝜈, AR(1), with temporal autocorrelation of 0.85
and amplitude of 0.1. Spatial coherence was introduced by applying a Gaussian spatial filter to the
Gaussian noise at each time step before the autoregressive update using a smoothing scale of 1.5
grid cells in both spatial dimensions. The characteristics of this background variability are meant
to represent the temporal autocorrelation commonly observed in geophysical fields.
In both cases, TraCE-ST trajectories are computed for 𝑁= 30 backward iterations starting
from the location of the target child at time 𝑡0. Although idealized, the spatial and temporal scales
of these synthetic experiments were selected to mimic the data characteristics encountered in
the subsequent Earth-system case studies: coherent features spanning multiple grid cells, multi-
step temporal evolution, and causal discovery performed over relatively short temporal windows.
This allows the synthetic benchmarks to evaluate TraCE-ST under conditions representative of its
intended applications while retaining fully prescribed ground-truth causal pathways.

based approach, where rather than relying on prediction error alone, it uses iterative conditional-
independence testing to determine whether dependencies persist after conditioning on selected past
variables and other relevant time-series parents.

Three real-world scenarios
We now evaluate TraCE-ST in three real-world cases where the dominant causal pathways are
sufficiently well understood to enable concise validation: (i) Tropical Storm Debby (2006), a
fast-evolving tropical system influenced by remote vorticity disturbances; (ii) the Mount Pinatubo
eruption (1991), which injected large amounts of gases into the stratosphere, where they were
transported circumglobally, formed aerosols, and drove significant radiative impacts; and (iii) the
2021 Pacific Northwest (PNW) heatwave, a deadly, extreme, compound event produced by a mid-
tropospheric ridge arising from the combined influence of local and remote drivers. Together, these
cases span distinct components of the Earth system, a wide range of spatiotemporal scales, varying
levels of dimensional complexity, and multiple datasets and geographic areas of interest. For each
case, we explore hyperparameter combinations over ranges chosen to avoid stagnant trajectories
and permit spatially coherent trajectory evolution, and analyze all resulting ensembles that satisfy
predefined trajectory-completion criteria. Additionally, variables are standardized to place different
physical quantities on comparable numerical scales before causal discovery, although alternative
preprocessing choices may be preferable for highly skewed or intermittent variables (31).
(i) Tropical Storm Debby (2006)
Debby developed over the eastern North Atlantic Ocean from an African easterly wave that moved
off the west coast of Africa on 20 August 2006, becoming a tropical depression by 1800 UTC
on 21 August (32). Debby subsequently intensified into a tropical storm at 0000 UTC on 23 Au-
gust while moving northwestward over the Atlantic. Beyond a favorable large-scale environment,
several studies have shown that Debby’s genesis and evolution were strongly influenced by the west-
ward propagation, merging, and organization of upstream vorticity disturbances over the African
continent during the 10 days prior to achieving tropical depression status (23, 24, 33, 34). These
disturbances included both dry and wet vortices, the latter promoting organized deep convection,
including mesoscale convective systems embedded within an African easterly wave, whose cold
cloud tops are reflected in brightness temperature observations. The evolution of these vorticity
disturbances culminated in a dry-wet vortex merger near the West African coast and contributed
to the development of Debby (24). Using precipitation occurring within the storm as the target

Using hourly data on a 0.25◦×0.25◦grid, we apply TraCE-ST to three variables: IMERG

timescales (38), and brightness temperature from NCEP/CPC is preferred over ERA5-derived cloud
variables because ERA5 can underestimate key aspects of mesoscale convective activity (39).
We acknowledge that other variables beyond precipitation could represent Debby, such as surface
pressure or wind anomalies, but we choose precipitation because it can be measured more accurately
at hourly timescales and is a consequence of the storm’s presence. Additional variables could also
be considered causal parents, including large-scale environmental fields such as SSTs and wind
shear, but we focus here on the demonstrative use of TraCE-ST in a case study supported by current
literature (23, 24, 33, 34) and leave a more in-depth analysis of Debby’s drivers for future work.

(iii) 2021 North American Pacific Northwest heatwave
In late June 2021, the Pacific Northwest (PNW) of North America experienced an unprecedented
heatwave, with many locations breaking all-time maximum 2 m ambient temperature records by
more than 5°C and causing severe societal and ecological impacts (26,46–48). The event’s excep-
tional magnitude has been attributed to interacting processes across variables and regions, including
a persistent blocking ridge, land-atmosphere feedbacks, upstream diabatic heating and moisture
transport, quasi-stationary circulation anomalies, a split polar-vortex configuration, and compar-
atively less explored ocean-atmosphere heat exchanges that reduced cloud cover and enhanced
incoming shortwave radiation over the PNW (49–56). The blocking ridge reduced cloud cover,
enhanced incoming shortwave radiation, and favored subsidence and warm near-surface conditions
over the PNW (49–51). Land-atmosphere interactions further amplified the event, as pre-existing
soil-moisture deficits altered latent and sensible heat fluxes and shifted the surface energy balance
toward sensible heating. Identifying these factors and quantifying their relative importance has
proven challenging, motivating multiple methodological approaches, including numerical model-
ing, reanalysis diagnostics, and air parcel tracking (49–54,56–60). Here, we test whether TraCE-ST
can identify these numerous processes in a data-driven manner by identifying the spatiotempo-
ral pathways through which different variables and regions contributed to the development and
amplification of the blocking ridge, and by estimating their relative causal contributions.

providing a data-driven estimate of their causal contributions over time (49,50,52,53).
Discussion
We introduced TraCE-ST, a framework that identifies event-conditioned multivariate causal path-
ways in space and time using gridded data. TraCE-ST builds upon recent causal discovery advances
that make it possible to estimate directed links between grid cells over short time windows (13,14),
but extends these local, single-step causal estimates into Lagrangian trajectories that trace the causal
drivers of a realized outcome of interest. Our approach provides a probabilistic formulation for em-
pirically estimating the relative causal contribution of multiple variables to a given event of interest,
rather than reducing causal explanation to a single pathway. This capability is important because
Earth system extremes often emerge from multiple interacting processes across variables, regions,
and timescales. Overall, TraCE-ST provides an interpretable bridge between local causal discov-
ery and event-scale process attribution, offering an objective and computationally efficient way
to formulate data-driven hypotheses that complement association analyses and targeted numerical
experiments.
We validated TraCE-ST across a broad range of spatiotemporal scales, levels of complexity, and
datasets. In synthetic controlled experiments, across a broad range of hyperparameter configurations
and using different underlying time-series causal discovery algorithms, TraCE-ST accurately re-
covered prescribed spatiotemporal propagation pathways, avoided attributing effects to correlated
but non-causal variables, and reproduced the imposed relative causal contributions of multiple
25


## Page 26


drivers. Furthermore, three real-world applications on extreme events show that TraCE-ST can re-
cover physically meaningful causal pathways in Earth system settings that differ in scale, dominant
physics, and complexity.
For Tropical Storm Debby, TraCE-ST identified mid-level vorticity merging as its main driver,
and connected the late-stage storm to a broader precursor network over West and North Africa,
consistent with previous work emphasizing the role of mesoscale convective systems and dry
vorticity cores over land (23,24,34), and pointing particularly to vortices produced over the Hoggar
Mountains in south Algeria. For Mount Pinatubo, TraCE-ST recovered a modeled large-scale
transport and chemical transformation pathway from the eruption region to the central Pacific
that was consistent with known post-eruption evolution of sulfur, sulfate, and aerosol optical
depth (45). Finally, when applied to the Pacific Northwest heatwave of 2021, TraCE-ST produced
a multivariate causal sequence, involving diabatic processes, moisture transport, wave activity, and
surface-atmosphere coupling in the development and amplification of the blocking ridge. TraCE-ST
trajectories largely agree with mechanisms proposed in the literature, provide estimates of their
relative causal contributions, and highlight less studied processes, including the possible role of
ocean-atmosphere fluxes over the Gulf of Alaska at subseasonal lead times (26,49,50,52–54,56,57).
We confirmed that TraCE-ST remains stable across a broad range of hyperparameter config-
urations and demonstrated its ability to work flexibly with different underlying causal discovery
algorithms, although the framework necessarily inherits strengths and weaknesses from the algo-
rithm used to estimate causality. In the experiments presented here, Elastic-Net was more skillful
than PCMCI and DYNOTEARS while remaining computationally efficient, suggesting that future
work should improve the compatibility of causal tracking with multiple causal-discovery engines
while retaining computational tractability, as well as explore the performance of TraCE-ST with
other underlying time series causal discovery algorithms across scientific applications. As with
other data-driven causal discovery approaches, the inferred causal pathways are conditional on
the variables included in the analysis. Omitted drivers, unresolved processes, confounders, or
insufficient spatial domains can therefore lead to incomplete or incorrect causal interpretations.
Additionally, temporally correlated errors, which are common in geoscience data, may lead to
spurious causal links. Likewise, observational reanalyses and numerical simulations that serve as
inputs for TraCE-ST may contain biases or uncertainties in transport, chemistry, and other physical
26


## Page 27


processes that can propagate into the obtained causal pathways, motivating careful interpretation
of the results. Applying TraCE-ST across multiple observational products or Earth system models
could help assess the robustness of results to observational uncertainty and differences in process
representation.
Robust TraCE-ST performance depends on informed variable selection, the inclusion of relevant
processes at scales resolvable by the input data, and sensible hyperparameter choices for the temporal
window length, search region size, and the causal stencil geometry. These choices should be viewed
as part of the model specification rather than as arbitrary or incidental tuning parameters, and
sensitivity to them should accompany interpretation. The physical principles used here to define
admissible hyperparameter ranges may also provide a basis for more automated future guidance
on hyperparameter selection across different Earth system applications. Several natural extensions
could further improve the framework. For example, allowing the time window to vary, allowing
the traced region to evolve in shape rather than remaining rectangular, and incorporating multi-lag
or multiscale causal estimation could improve representation of filamentary, deformable, or slowly
evolving causal structures.
Our results motivate several broader uses. TraCE-ST can serve as a hypothesis-generation tool
that narrows the set of candidate precursor regions, variables, and processes before more expen-
sive numerical experimentation, a capability that is particularly useful for rare and undersampled
extremes (61–63). When applied to sufficiently large event sets, TraCE-ST also enables comparing
causal structures across reanalyses, observations, and numerical, hybrid, or AI-based Earth system
models, offering a way to evaluate whether models reproduce the mechanistic pathways leading to
extremes (64–69). More generally, TraCE-ST could help address outstanding challenges in weather
and climate science by providing novel insights. For example, it could improve understanding and
prediction of tropical cyclone rapid intensification by clarifying the relative roles of environmen-
tal drivers and inner-core processes (41); complement recent ML-based studies investigating the
sources and limits of Earth system predictability (70–72) by helping identify the relative contribu-
tions of different Earth-system components and mechanisms; and help disentangle externally forced
change from internal variability by testing whether extremes arise through different causal pathways
across past, present, and future climate states (73). Furthermore, since TraCE-ST offers a way to
follow causal pathways without imposing field-specific thresholds, it complements conventional
27


## Page 28


feature-tracking methods (74–76).
Data-driven causal tracking does not replace physical reasoning or numerical experimentation;
rather, it complements them by providing an objective, event-conditioned framework for identifying
where, when, and through which variables causal influence is most plausibly propagated. Therefore,
we view TraCE-ST as an innovative and useful addition to the current set of scientific tools available
for studying Earth system phenomena and extremes.
Materials and Methods
M-CaStLe
To identify the causal parents of the current child 𝐶( 𝑗)(R, 𝑡) at each TraCE-ST iteration over the
region of interest R during the time window W, TraCE-ST relies on the multivariate Causal Space-
Time Stencil Learning framework (M-CaStLe) (14). M-CaStLe enables causal discovery in gridded
spatiotemporal data under sample-limited conditions, where the number of spatial degrees of free-
dom often exceeds the number of temporal observations available within an event-focused window.
The framework increases the effective sample size by exploiting spatial regularity and locality in
causal relationships. Specifically, it relies on four key assumptions that hold for smooth geophysical
fields over limited domains and periods of time: (i) temporal locality, whereby causal dependencies
are restricted to a single lag (𝑡−1 →𝑡); (ii) spatial locality, whereby causal parents of a grid cell
lie within a finite-radius neighborhood; (iii) temporal stationarity, whereby causal relationships are
approximately constant within the time window W; and (iv) spatial stationarity, whereby these
relationships are shared across locations within the analysis region R. These assumptions should
be interpreted as approximations rather than exact requirements, and their validity depends on
selecting analysis regions and time windows over which the dominant physical processes evolve
smoothly enough for neighboring samples to provide meaningful information about the underlying
causal structure. As these assumptions become progressively less valid, uncertainty in the ob-
tained causal relationships generally increases. Consequently, the hyperparameter ranges explored
by TraCE-ST must be selected to remain within regimes in which these assumptions are expected
to hold approximately.
28


## Page 29


Under these assumptions, M-CaStLe transforms the data within R and W into a locally encoded
dataset by extracting, for each valid spatial location 𝑠∈R and time step within W, the values of all
variables within the (2𝑟+ 1) × (2𝑟+ 1) Moore neighborhood B𝑟(𝑠) at lag 𝑡−1. To ensure that each
stencil is fully contained within R, only locations at least 𝑟grid cells away from the boundaries are
considered, yielding (𝑛−2𝑟)2 admissible central locations for a square region of size 𝑛×𝑛. Pooling
across space and time produces a design matrix with 𝑁samples = |W| × (𝑛−2𝑟)2 samples and
𝑁features = 𝑝× (2𝑟+ 1)2 features, where each sample represents a local spatiotemporal context and
each feature corresponds to a variable at a specific relative position within the stencil B𝑟(𝑠). This
transformation effectively converts the original spatiotemporal field into a dataset that leverages
both spatial replication and temporal evolution, substantially increasing the effective sample size
while constraining the dimensionality of the candidate parent set.
Causal parent identification is then performed by applying a time series causal discovery or
sparse predictive modeling method to this locally encoded dataset, targeting the dependence of the
central node 𝑋( 𝑗)(𝑠, 𝑡) on candidate predictors 𝑋(𝑎)(𝑠′, 𝑡−1) with 𝑠′ ∈B𝑟(𝑠) and 𝑎∈{1, . . . , 𝑝}. In
this work, we explored three causal discovery engines: DYNOTEARS (8), PCMCI (1), and Elastic-
Net regression (29), which represent complementary approaches based on structural equation
modeling, conditional independence testing, and sparse predictive modeling, respectively. The
output of M-CaStLe is a localized directed graph representing selected or statistically significant
𝑡−1 →𝑡relationships within the stencil B𝑟(𝑠) (e.g., see Fig. 1C,D in the main text). These inferred
links define the candidate causal parents used by TraCE-ST to transition across variables and
determine the spatial displacement of the region R at each iteration. Because causal relationships
are estimated with M-CaStLe at each TraCE-ST iteration using the current analysis region and
temporal window, the locality and stationarity assumptions apply only to the current tracing step,
allowing longer-range pathways to emerge through successive local causal transitions.

TraCE-ST hyperparameters
As shown above, TraCE-ST can exhibit stable behavior across broad regions of the hyperparam-
eter space, although its outputs vary across parameter choices. As with any data-driven method,
the successful application depends on selecting physically and computationally sensible ranges to
explore. The hyperparameters of the framework correspond to different components: the tracking
procedure, M-CaStLe, and the underlying causal discovery algorithm. We describe each of these
below. Additionally, Table 2 summarizes the hyperparameters explored for each case study in the
main text. These ranges were selected to exclude regimes that either produced unstable causal
discovery, stagnant results, or premature trajectory termination. We encourage users to assess and
report their sensitivity to these choices, as we do in our applications. The explored ranges are not
intended to imply invariance to arbitrary hyperparameter choices, nor to represent exhaustive opti-
mization. Rather, they characterize TraCE-ST behavior over admissible regions of parameter space
in which causal relationships can plausibly be resolved. Accordingly, the sampled combinations
should be interpreted as Monte Carlo samples from admissible regions of parameter space. Our
goal is therefore to assess the stability of qualitative inferences within this operating domain, not
to identify unique optimal parameter values.

Trajectory hyperparameters
Before applying TraCE-ST, the user must define the target child by selecting the starting latitude,
starting longitude, starting time, and target variable of the trajectories. The first, and likely most
important, modeling choices are the spatial grid spacing and temporal resolution of the input data.
Higher temporal resolution enables faster causal interactions to be resolved; conversely, relevant
drivers may be missed if the temporal resolution is too coarse relative to the timescale of the process
under study. For example, in the Mount Pinatubo eruption case, TraCE-ST did not resolve the SO2
→H2SO4 transformation because this chemical transition occurs faster than the daily temporal
resolution used in the analysis. Spatial grid spacing must also be fine enough to provide adequate
sampling within the region of interest. However, if the grid spacing is too fine relative to the process’s
displacement speed, a much larger stencil radius may be required to capture fast advective motion.
The Mount Pinatubo eruption case also illustrates this trade-off: at 4◦grid spacing, advection of
roughly 16◦day−1 can be captured with a stencil radius of 4 grid cells, whereas at 1◦grid spacing,
the same displacement would require a stencil radius of 16 grid cells. Such large stencils may
become expensive computationally or intractable for the underlying causal discovery algorithm.
To cluster the causal parents of the current child at each time step, the user also needs to define
the DBSCAN neighborhood radius 𝜖and the minimum number of samples 𝑁min required to form
a cluster. Larger values of these parameters make it more likely that multiple causal parents will
be grouped together. However, if these values are too large, distinct causal sources arriving from
different directions may be merged into the same group. In our applications, sensible values were
typically in the ranges 𝜖= 0.05−0.3 and 𝑁𝑚𝑖𝑛= 2−4. Other parameters control how the “winning
cluster” is selected and how the trajectory displacement is computed from its members. The scoring
rule determines whether clusters are ranked by their average causal magnitude or aggregated causal
magnitude. The 𝛼parameter determines the weights assigned to members of the winning cluster
(|𝛽𝑖|𝛼), when computing the weighted mean offset of the cluster relative to the center node of the
stencil B𝑟(𝑠). Finally, in the probabilistic formulation of TraCE-ST, the probability rule determines
whether the probability of selecting a cluster increases linearly with cluster strength or follows a
softmax function. In the latter case, a scaling parameter 𝛽𝑠𝑜𝑓𝑡𝑚𝑎𝑥controls how strongly the selection
probability concentrates on the strongest clusters.

M-CaStLe hyperparameters
The user must also select the size of the analysis region R, the length of the time window W, and
the radius of the stencil B𝑟(𝑠). The size of R and the length of W control the number of samples
available to the underlying causal discovery algorithm after local stencil encoding. A region or
time window that is too small may not provide sufficient samples for robust causal discovery.
Conversely, a region or time window that is too large may mix distinct dynamical regimes, violating
the spatial or temporal stationarity assumptions of M-CaStLe. Although the applications presented
here use relatively short time windows to study event-specific dynamics, longer windows are equally
compatible with the framework provided that the assumption of approximate stationarity remains
reasonable over the selected period. The stencil radius 𝑟must be large enough to capture the relevant
𝑡−1 →𝑡dependencies affecting the child variable. A radius that is too small may miss advective or
spatially displaced processes, whereas a radius that is too large increases the number of candidate
parents and can make the causal discovery problem computationally expensive or intractable for
the underlying causal discovery algorithm. More guidance on M-CaStLe hyperparameters can be
found in Nichol et al. (2026) (14).
Causal discovery core hyperparameters
Any time series causal discovery method within M-CaStLe can be used to infer directed links inside
the stencil B𝑟(𝑠), and each method introduces its own set of hyperparameters that can influence
the inferred local graph and, consequently, TraCE-ST trajectories. In the synthetic univariate case,
we explored Elastic-Net, PCMCI, and DYNOTEARS. For Elastic-Net, we varied the regularization
strength 𝜆𝑎and the ℓ1 ratio. For PCMCI, we explored different significance thresholds for both the
PC step and the final graph construction 𝛼𝑃𝐶, alternative conditional independence tests (ParCorr
and RobustParCorr), and optional false discovery rate correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg
procedure. For DYNOTEARS, we varied the regularization strength 𝜆𝑎, the maximum number of
iterations (100–3000), and the dependence threshold used to retain inferred links. These ranges
were selected to span regimes from sparse to dense causal graphs while maintaining numerical
stability and computational tractability.
