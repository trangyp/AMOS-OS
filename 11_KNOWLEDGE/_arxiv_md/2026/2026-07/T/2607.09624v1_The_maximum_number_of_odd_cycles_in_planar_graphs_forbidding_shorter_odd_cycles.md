Abstract
Given a graph H and a family of graphs F, the generalized planar Tur´an number
exP(n, H, F) is the maximum number of copies of H in an n-vertex planar graph that
contains no graph F ∈F as a subgraph. When only induced copies of H are counted,
we denote the corresponding generalized planar Tur´an number by exP(n, Hind, F). Gy˝ori
and Karim [10] determined exP(n, C5, {C3}). In this paper, we determine the exact value
of exP(n, C2k+1, {C3, C5, . . . , C2k−1}) for every k ≥3.
Since all shorter odd cycles are forbidden, every C2k+1 is induced. This problem is
closely related to the inducibility of odd cycles in planar graphs. Ghosh, Gy˝ori, Janzer,
Paulos, Salia and Zamora [7] (and independently Savery [22]) determined the exact value
of exP(n, Cind

k ≥3. Our result confirms their conjecture under the additional assumption that all
shorter odd cycles are forbidden.

5
Conclusion
After a long, sophisticated proof, we have proved Theorem 1. Although the proof is lengthy,
much of the work is technical; the main ideas can be summarized as follows.
• We work under the three properties introduced in Theorem 11. Property (1) is a standard
progressive-induction assumption, while properties (2) and (3) reflect symmetry consid-
erations. These ideas are widely used, but making them compatible in the present setting
requires the technical work in Section 3.
• In Section 4.1, we prove lemmas that identify empty regions and vertices of degree two.
This idea is inspired by the work of Ghosh, Gy˝ori, Janzer, Paulos, Salia and Zamora [7],
who determined the maximum number of induced copies of C5 in a planar graph. For C5,
this idea is already close to sufficient; for C2k+1, it is not.
• Section 4.2 is a key part of the proof. It treats vertices of degree two and special paths in
a unified way. One inherent difficulty is that the extremal graph is not unique: it contains
a forest, whose two natural extremes are a star and a path. The lemmas in Section 4.2
handle these two extreme cases.
• In Sections 4.3 and 4.4, we prove that ∆2 is large. This determines a large part of the
extremal graph and allows us to uncover the remaining structure. The inequalities in
these sections are the main reason the proof works.

• With these lemmas in place, the bootstrap argument in Section 4.5 is conceptually
straightforward: we build the whole structure step by step from a large ∆2.
We do not characterize the extremal graph in this paper. Although (9) in the proof of
Lemma 27 shows that a graph with properties (1), (2), and (3) attains the maximum number
of C2k+1 only when it is one of the extremal graphs described in Theorem 1, this only deter-
mines the extremal graphs within that restricted class. To obtain a full characterization of the
extremal graph, one would also need to track the operations in Section 3 that transform an
arbitrary graph into one with properties (1), (2), and (3). This seems promising, but for the
sake of brevity, we do not pursue it here.
The ultimate goal is to count induced copies after removing the assumption that all shorter
odd cycles are forbidden, as in Conjecture 2. Without this assumption, however, our method
fails entirely because Lemma 4 no longer holds.
