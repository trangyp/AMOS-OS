1. Introduction
Let V be a finite dimensional vector space over an algebraically closed field k of charac-
teristic 0 equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form ⟨, ⟩. The function
qi,j : V m →k,
(v1, . . . , vm) 7→⟨vi, vj⟩
is invariant under orthogonal group O(V ). The first fundamental theorem of invariant theory
states that these functions generate the ring of O(V )-invariant polynomial functions on V m.
The second fundamental theorem describes the algebraic relations between these functions;
in particular, if dim V ≥m then the qi,j (for i ≤j) are algebraically independent. These
theorems, and their analogs for other classical groups, have been extremely influential in the
development of invariant theory.
Recently, Harman and the second author [HS1] introduced a class of (typically infinite
dimensional) algebraic groups called linearly oligomorphic groups, and constructed examples
analogous to the classical groups. In this paper, we establish versions of the fundamental
theorems for them. We describe the simplest case here.
Let n be a positive integer.
A symmetric n-space is a vector space V equipped with
a symmetric multi-linear form ω: V n →k.
By [HS1, Theorem A], there is a universal
homogeneous symmetric n-space (V, ω) of countable dimension. “Universal” means that any
finite dimensional symmetric n-space W embeds into V , and “homogeneous” means that
any two embeddings from W differ by an automorphism of V . Remarkably, (V, ω) is unique
up to isomorphism.

2. Background
2.1. The ring of symmetric forms. Let V be a vector space. A symmetric d-form on V
is a function V →k that has the form v 7→ω(v, . . . , v) for some symmetric multi-linear form
ω: V d →k. For example, when d = 2 we recover the notion of quadratic form. We write
Pd(V ) for the space of such forms; this is naturally identified with the linear dual of Symd V .
We let P(V ) = L
d≥0 Pd(V ), which is naturally a ring; we call it the ring of symmetric forms.
Suppose V has basis {ei}i∈I. Let {xi}i∈I be the dual functionals, i.e., xi : V →k is the
linear map satisfying xi(ej) = δi,j.
Then an element of Pd(V ) can be represented by a
(perhaps infinite) k-linear combination of degree d monomials in the xi’s. In other words,
P(V ) is naturally isomorphic to the inverse limit, in the category of graded rings, of the
polynomial rings k[xi]i∈J as J runs over the finite subsets of I.
2.2. Tensor spaces. For a vector space V and a partition µ, a µ-form on V is a linear map
Sµ(V ) →k where Sµ denotes the Schur functor. Note that if µ = (d) then a µ-form can be
identified with a symmetric d-form, i.e., an element of Pd(V ).
Let λ = [λ1, . . . , λr] be a tuple of partitions; we often simply call λ a tuple. A λ-space is a
pair (V, ω), where V is a vector space and ω = (ω1, . . . , ωr) is a tuple where ωi is a λi-form
on V . A map of λ-spaces (W, η) →(V, ω) is an injective linear map i: W →V such that
i∗(ωi) = ηi for each 1 ≤i ≤r. We say that a λ-space V is finite or countable if dim V is.

2.4. GL-varieties. Let V = S
n≥1 kn and let GL = GL(V). A representation of GL is
polynomial if it decomposes into a sum of Sλ(V)’s. A GL-algebra is a commutative algebra
equipped with an action of GL by algebra homomorphisms under which it forms a polynomial
representation. A basic example of a GL-algebra is Rλ = Sym(Sλ(V)). A GL-algebra is
finitely GL-generated if it is a quotient of Rλ for some tuple λ. A GL-variety is the spectrum
of a reduced finitely GL-generated GL-algebra. We let Aλ = Spec(Rλ). This is the basic
example of a GL-variety: indeed, by definition, every GL-variety is a closed GL-subvariety
of some Aλ. See [BDES] for additional details.
The category of polynomial representations of GL is equivalent to the category of poly-
nomial functors, with Sλ(V) corresponding to Sλ. If M is a polynomial representation and
V is a vector space, we write M{V } for the value of the polynomial functor associated to M
on V . If X = Spec(R) is a GL-variety, we write X{V } for Spec(R{V }). Note that Aλ{V }
is the linear dual of Sλ(V ), i.e., the space of λ-structures on V . For this reason, GL-varieties
are closely related to λ-spaces. This connection is explored in detail in [HS2].

Proposition 3.3. Any natural transformation Aλ →Aµ is algebraic.
