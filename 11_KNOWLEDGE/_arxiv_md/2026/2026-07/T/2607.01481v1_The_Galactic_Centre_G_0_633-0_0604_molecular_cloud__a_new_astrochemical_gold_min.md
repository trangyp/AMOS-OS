ABSTRACT
Context. In the Central Molecular Zone (CMZ) environment, shocks play a key role in both triggering star formation and providing
chemical enrichment. The Sgr B2 complex is a prime template, hosting several massive protoclusters (N, M, S) and the northern
G+0.693 cloud, which exhibits shock-induced prestellar signatures.
Aims. We report on G+0.633-0.0604, a newly identified shock-dominated and chemically rich cloud at the southern edge of Sgr B2,
where the next star formation episodes have been proposed to occur. We characterise its physical properties and the shocks shaping it.
Methods. We analyse data from new high-sensitivity broadband spectral surveys using the Yebes 40m, IRAM 30m and APEX radio
telescopes, covering a ∼100 GHz aggregated bandwidth across the ∼31−275 GHz range. We present analyses on CH3CCH, CH3CN,
HC3N, HNCO and several isotopologues of CO to infer kinetic temperatures and H2 densities. We complementarily used 3 mm IRAM
30m mosaics (∼13′×13′, ∼32×32 pc2) of Sgr B2 in HC3N, HNCO and C2H5OH to probe G+0.633 environment.
Results. We identify three velocity components towards G+0.633: a narrow main one (C1, vLSR∼48.5 km s−1; FWHM∼10 km s−1),
and two broader, much fainter components at higher velocities, C2 (∼61 km s−1; ∼13 km s−1) and C3 (∼89 km s−1; ∼18 km s−1), all
showing similar properties (Tkin∼55−90 K, NH2∼(3−7)×1022 cm−2, nH2∼(0.5−2.5)×104 cm−3) and extended distributions. C1 delin-
eates G+0.633 physically, and corresponds to a peak in HNCO. This supports a shock-driven origin, likely rooted in the cloud–cloud
collision event shaping Sgr B2 and also traced by C2, which mainly extends north towards G+0.693. C3 is kinematically unlinked and
related to large-scale CMZ dynamics. Of the three, C1 may be consistent with a very early protocluster phase, yet to be confirmed.
Conclusions. G+0.633 emerges as a new shock-dominated CMZ cloud with physical resemblance to G+0.693, providing another
unique laboratory to investigate how shocks drive molecular complexity and regulate the onset of cluster formation in the CMZ.

1. Introduction
The Galactic Centre (GC) region of the Milky Way spans its in-
ner ∼300 pc around the Sgr A* central black hole, harbouring
∼5% of the total molecular gas reservoir of the entire Galaxy
(2−6×107 M⊙; Dahmen et al. 1998; Ferrière et al. 2007) within
just 0.1% of its surface area. This gas is asymmetrically dis-
tributed in a population of several Giant Molecular Cloud com-
plexes (with three times more mass concentrated at positive lon-
gitudes and velocities; Henshaw et al. 2023), which host ∼80%
of the dense gas in the Galaxy (>104 cm−3; Hüettemeister et al.
1993), and give shape to the so-called Central Molecular Zone
(CMZ; Morris & Serabyn 1996). However, despite such a large
reservoir of matter, star formation in the CMZ appears to be sup-
pressed in comparison to the Galactic disc, exhibiting star for-

mation rates at least one order of magnitude lower than expected
from the measured gas mass (e.g., Longmore et al. 2013; Licquia
& Newman 2015; Barnes et al. 2017). The origin of such defi-
ciency is still under debate, but hypothesised to be closely linked
to the extreme environmental conditions that prevail in the CMZ.
The high turbulence (velocity dispersions ∼2−53 km s−1; Hen-
shaw et al. 2016) as a consequence of low-velocity shocks (e.g.,
Martín-Pintado et al. 1997; Jones et al. 2012), intense UV radi-
ation (from nearby massive stellar clusters such as the Arches,
Quintuplet or Young Nuclear clusters; Lu 2018), X-ray emission
(e.g., Koyama 2018), and the enhanced cosmic-ray ionisation
rates (e.g., Goto et al. 2014; Le Petit et al. 2016; Yusef-Zadeh
et al. 2016; Oka et al. 2019; Rivilla et al. 2022a), make the CMZ
an extremely harsh environment for star formation and interstel-
lar chemistry, while offering a unique window into how these
processes operate under the most extreme galactic conditions.

Despite the generally reduced star formation activity ob-
served across the CMZ, this region hosts one of the most active
and massive star-forming sites in the entire Galaxy: the Sgr B2
complex. Located at a distance of 8.34±0.16 kpc from the Sun
and ∼100 pc east in projection from Sgr A* (Reid et al. 2014),
Sgr B2 contains ∼10% of the total CMZ dense gas. Its central
∼2 pc contain three high-mass protoclusters positioned along a
north–south ridge in equatorial coordinates and named accord-
ingly, Sgr B2(North), Sgr B2(Main) and Sgr B2(South), which
stand out as prominent sites of ongoing star formation activity.
These extensively studied massive protoclusters are surrounded
by a large envelope of 15.4′ in diameter containing >99% of
this complex total mass (Schmiedeke et al. 2016), and in which
some hints of star formation at very early stages, particularly to-
wards its southernmost Sgr B2(DeepSouth) region, have recently
been revealed (Ginsburg et al. 2018; Meng et al. 2019, 2022;
Jeff et al. 2024). The morphology and kinematic features identi-
fied in Sgr B2 have been interpreted as the result of a large-scale
cloud–cloud collision producing low-velocity shocks (Hasegawa
et al. 1994; Sato et al. 2000; Tsuboi et al. 2015; Zeng et al.
2020; Enokiya & Fukui 2022; Colzi et al. 2024), believed to
have sequentially triggered such intense star formation follow-
ing an “inside-out” pattern. Beginning at the most evolved Sgr
B2(M) core (de Vicente et al. 2000; Schmiedeke et al. 2016), the
sequence propagates outward “symmetrically” (Ginsburg et al.
2018; Jeff et al. 2024): north to Sgr B2(N) and south to Sgr
B2(S), eventually reaching the Sgr B2(DS) region (Fig. 1, left).
In support of this scenario, the G+0.693-0.027 molecular
cloud (hereafter G+0.693), located just ∼55′′ north-east from
Sgr B2(N), has become a particularly revealing case. This cloud,
unlike its neighbour Sgr B2(N), does not show any signposts of
ongoing star formation activity, such as ultra-compact (UC) HII
regions, H2O and Class II methanol masers, or dust continuum
sources (e.g., Ginsburg et al. 2018; Lu et al. 2019). However,
recent observations of dense condensations in a post-shock evo-
lutionary stage and with high levels of deuteration (Colzi et al.
2022, 2024), suggest G+0.693 may actually be the prestellar pre-
cursor of a new massive star-forming cluster within Sgr B2. In
fact, G+0.693 lies right at the proposed interface of the cloud-
cloud collision where the shocks are most intense (Henshaw
et al. 2016; Zeng et al. 2020), evidenced by the enhanced HNCO
emission in the maps from Jones et al. 2012 (Fig. 1, right). More-
over, these shocks appear to have played a double role in Sgr
B2, not only as triggers of its intense star formation activity, but
also as drivers of its remarkable chemical wealth observed, par-
ticularly towards Sgr B2(N) (e.g., Belloche et al. 2013, 2019,
2025) and G+0.693 (e.g., Rivilla et al. 2022b, 2023; Jiménez-
Serra et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2018, 2023; San Andrés et al. 2023,
2024; Sanz-Novo et al. 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). Together, these
findings establish Sgr B2 as an exceptional laboratory for ex-
ploring the interplay between shocks, star formation, and molec-
ular complexity in the CMZ, motivating the search for additional
shock-driven and chemically rich sources within its envelope.
Within this context, the Sgr B2(DS) region naturally repre-
sents the continuation of the “inside-out” star formation wave
in Sgr B2, making it a promising location to search for a po-
tential “southern counterpart” of G+0.693. Here, we report the
discovery of such a source: the G+0.633-0.0604 molecular cloud
(hereafter G+0.633). Located ∼2′ south of the Sgr B2(S) proto-
cluster, at the southern portion of Sgr B2(DS) (see Fig. 1, left),
G+0.633 occupies the next spot within the proposed sequence of
shock-triggered star formation across the complex. This source
was precisely selected due to its prominent HNCO emission,
the brightest within Sgr B2(DS) (Fig. 1, right), and very narrow
line profiles (similar to those of the nearby Sgr B2M (20,−180)
source studied in Martín et al. (2008)), making it well suited for
deep molecular surveys. In fact, our recent observations have re-
vealed an exceptionally rich chemistry, with more than 120 iden-
tified molecules, placing G+0.633 among the most chemically
rich sources in the CMZ (San Andrés et al., in prep.).
In this article, the first in a series devoted to G+0.633, we
focus on its physical characterisation. By analysing CH3CCH,
CH3CN, CO isotopologues and HC3N, we provide estimates for
the gas kinetic temperature and the H2 column and volume den-
sities. Moreover, we present an analysis on HNCO and the spa-
tial morphology of the molecular emission using HNCO, HC3N
and C2H5OH as tracers. The detailed study of the rich complex
chemistry of this cloud will be presented in forthcoming works.
This article is organised as follows. In Sect. 2, we describe
the observational survey conducted towards G+0.633. In Sect. 3
we present the analysis and results on the physical properties of
this cloud, along with the molecular emission maps. In Sect. 4
we examine these maps in detail to investigate the physical
structure of G+0.633 and the underlying mechanisms driving its
chemistry. Finally, in Sect. 5, we outline our conclusions.
2. Observations and data reduction
2.1. Single-dish surveys
We analysed data from the newly conducted ultradeep spectral
line survey of G+0.633, which combines single-dish observa-
tions from the Yebes 40m (Guadalajara, Spain), IRAM1 30m
(Granada, Spain) and APEX2 (Chajnantor, Chile) radio tele-
scopes. All of them were performed in position-switching mode,
centred at αICRS = 17h47m21.18s, δICRS = −28o25′36.99′′.
The off position (αICRS(OFF) = 17h46m14.92s, δICRS(OFF) =
−28o16′36.99′′) is the same as selected in previous surveys of
the neighbouring G+0.693 cloud (e.g., Rivilla et al. 2021; Sanz-
Novo et al. 2023), devoid of emission of abundant molecules
such as CS and HC3N. The line intensity of the spectra was
directly measured in antenna temperature (T ∗
A) units, since the
molecular emission towards G+0.633 is extended compared to
the primary beam of the three telescopes (Fig. 1 and Sect. 4.2).
2.1.1. Yebes 40m observations
We observed G+0.633 with the Yebes 40m radio telescope
(project 24A010; PI: San Andrés) for 25 different nights be-
tween February and April 2024, accounting for a total on-source
observing time of ∼28 hrs. We used the broad-band (18.5 GHz)
Nanocosmos Q-band (7 mm) HEMT receiver to cover the en-
tire Q-band (31.07−50.42 GHz) in two linear polarisations (Ter-
cero et al. 2021). The receiver was connected to 16 Fast Fourier
Transform Spectrometers (FFTS) as backends (eight different
with two linear polarisations each), providing a raw resolution
of 38 kHz (∼0.23−0.37 km s−1 across the Q-band). The observa-
tions were divided into two distinct frequency setups that were
usually alternated between each of the observing runs, centred
at 41.4 and 42.3 GHz, to ensure unambiguous line identification
against image sideband emission. The telescope pointing and
focus were checked every ∼1−2 hrs through pseudo-continuum
observations towards VX Sgr, a red hypergiant star close to
G+0.633. The half power beam width (HPBW) of the Yebes 40m
telescope varies from ∼55′′ at 31 GHz down to ∼35′′ at 50 GHz.

To perform the reduction of the Yebes 40m data, we exported
the raw spectra from the CLASS module within the GILDAS3
package directly into MADCUBA4 (Martín et al. 2019), where
we carried out the entire processing. In a first step, we fitted
and subtracted separate global baselines into each day datasets
by masking the most intense lines. Then, we combined and av-
eraged (noise-weighted) the different spectral scans along all
of the observing days, obtaining a single spectrum per spectral
setup, backend, and polarisation. As Tercero et al. (2021) already
stated, the signal from the Nanocosmos receiver shows a combi-
nation of ripples with distinct periods that produce a wavy-like
continuum, originating from internal reflections. The Yebes 40m
data of G+0.633 are also affected by such ripples. To correct for
them, we eliminated their associated frequency components in
the Fourier space, applying, separately for each of the result-
ing spectra in the previous step (thereby sweeping the entire
∼20 GHz range four times), a large set of Fast Fourier Trans-
formations (FFTs) over ∼50 MHz spectral windows. To avoid
accidentally removing any actual line when applying each FFT,
we simultaneously checking the consistency of the spectrum
for that particular individual FFT window with the other three
frequency-overlapping spectra. If coincident lines appeared, we
masked them before its application. In the last step, we com-
bined and averaged (noise-weighted) the resulting four corrected
spectra after the FFT treatment for each backend, comparing be-
forehand the two frequency setups to remove possible spurious
signals or any technical artefact. We note that we trimmed a
∼80 MHz portion in most of the raw spectra at ∼39.215 GHz,
since this range is severely affected by a military radar signal
near the observatory.

2.1.2. IRAM 30m observations
The single-dish IRAM 30m observations of G+0.633 were con-
ducted as a result of projects 058-21 (PI: Rivilla) and 155-24 and
055-25 (PI: San Andrés), aimed at covering the entire 3 mm win-
dow with exquisite sensitivity. In all projects, we used the broad-
band mm-wave heterodyne Eight MIxer Receiver (EMIR; Carter
et al. 2012) in combination with the Fast Fourier Transform
Spectrometer FTS200, to continuously cover the 3 mm band
(namely E090 in EMIR) with a raw channel width of ∼195 kHz
(∼0.5−0.8 km s−1 for E090). The observations on project 058-21
were carried out during the nights of 15, 16, and 18 Septem-
ber 2021, for a total time on source of ∼10.5 hrs. We employed
two different spectral setups, centred at 83.50 and 91.45 GHz, to
cover the lower part of the E090 window (∼72−103 GHz). The
observations on project 155-24 were conducted along five days
during the first fortnight of April 2025 and on the 31st May, 1st
and 2nd June 2025, achieving a total time on source of ∼10.5 hrs.

and 104.60 GHz (covering the ∼84−116.6 GHz range), complet-

ping part with 058-21 former setup. Finally, project 055-25 was
observed for 20 days (15 in June 2025, 5 in November 2025)
achieving a total time on source of ∼36.5 hrs. This observing run
was used to further integrate along the entire 3 mm band adopt-
ing the same spectral setups from projects 058-21 and 155-24,
to reach an homogeneous sensitivity across the whole survey. In

2.1.3. APEX observations
We conducted the APEX observations of G+0.633 (project ID
O-0109.F-9309A-2022; PI: Rivilla) in a total of 7 nights (5 be-
tween April and May 2022, 2 in October 2022), yielding a to-
tal time on source of ∼4.5 hrs. We used the 230 GHz-band New
First Light APEX Submillimeter Heterodyne (nFLASH2305) re-
ceiver connected to two FFTS backends, providing simultaneous
coverage through the 200−280 GHz range of two 7.9 GHz-wide
sidebands separated by 8 GHz, with a raw spectral resolution
of ∼250 kHz (∼0.27−0.38 km s−1 at these frequencies). We used
two setups centred at 222.3 and 262.5 GHz to cover four spec-
tral ranges: 209.92−218.33, 226.18−234.58, 249.92−258.83 and
266.17–275.08 GHz. Each setup was divided into two sub-setups
shifted by ∼0.5 GHz, to check for possible contamination of spu-
rious lines from the image sideband. The HPBW of these APEX
observations is ∼22−29′′ in the frequency windows observed.
We reduced the APEX observations following a methodol-
ogy similar to that applied to the Yebes 40m and IRAM 30m
data, with the absence of any FFT treatment but the inclusion of
an additional preliminary step. Specifically, we removed some
spectral channels at the lower and upper edges of each spectral
scan that were significantly distorted with respect to the contin-
uum profile. We smoothed the final reduced spectra to 1.0 MHz,
which translates into velocity resolutions of 1.1−1.5 km s−1 in
the spectral range covered. We achieved a rms ranging from 0.7
to 1.0 mK along the observed windows at this spectral resolution
(reaching a maximum of ∼1.5−2.1 mK at their edges).

3. Analysis and results
In this work, we focus on a set of molecular tracers probing
the physical conditions of the cloud. Specifically, we present the
analyses on several CO isotopologues (to derive the H2 column
density, NH2), HC3N (to retrieve the gas volume density, nH2),
CH3CN and CH3CCH (to infer the gas kinetic temperature, Tkin)
and HNCO (a shock tracer). The spectroscopic information and
details of their detected transitions are provided in Appendix A.
We carried out the identification and spectral modelling for
all of these species using the 27 May 2026 release of the Spectral
Line Identification and Modelling (SLIM) tool within the MAD-
CUBA package (Martín et al. 2019). For all molecules, SLIM
collected their available spectroscopic data from the Cologne
Database for Molecular Spectroscopy (CDMS, Endres et al.
2016) catalogue, which are based on different high-resolution
rotational spectroscopic works (see Appendix A), and gener-
ated a synthetic spectrum under the assumption of local ther-
modynamic equilibrium (LTE) conditions and accounting for
line opacity effects, to be compared with the observed one. We
derived the physical parameters describing all these molecules
emission using the automatic fitting routine SLIM-AUTOFIT,
which provides the best non-linear least-squares LTE fit to the
data using the Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm. The free param-
eters used for the LTE model are the column density (N), exci-
tation temperature (Tex), local standard of rest velocity (vLSR),
and full width at half maximum (FWHM). The beam-filling fac-
tor was set to unity for all components, as they trace extended
molecular emission relative to the telescope beams (see Sect. 4).
The spectral modelling of all these molecules indicates the
presence of three distinct velocity components along the line of
sight (see, e.g., Table 1 and Figs. 2, 3 and 4), rationalised in:
(i) A primary main component (C1), tracing gas with vLSR of
∼48.5 km s−1 and FWHM of ∼10 km s−1. This component
is the brightest and thus the one leading the molecular iden-
tification in G+0.633. It is displayed in red in all figures.
(ii) A secondary component (C2, depicted in green in all fig-
ures) of higher velocity (∼61 km s−1), exhibiting a slightly
broader FWHM of ∼13 km s−1 and usually appearing as a
small shoulder next to C1 profile. Generally, the C2 peak
is ∼3−5 times less intense than C1, making its contribution
traceable only for those molecules in which C1 is detected
with a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) in peak intensity ≳10.

3.1. G+0.633 physical properties
3.1.1. H2 column density (NH2)
Molecular hydrogen (H2) is the dominant component of inter-
stellar gas, and hence often used as the standard reference for
computing abundance ratios. However, its lack of a permanent
electric dipole moment prevents its direct observation at mm
wavelengths. As a consequence, H2 column densities are com-
monly inferred from CO, the second most abundant molecule in
the ISM, which emits prominently in the radio domain.
Nonetheless, CO emission is heavily optically thick in
G+0.633 (τ > 10), thus impeding its reliable analysis. For this
reason, we computed the CO column densities (and thus NH2)
using all detected isotopologues exhibiting optically thin emis-
sion. In this work, we analysed the lowest energy 1−0 transi-
tion of C18O, C17O, 13C18O and 13C17O, which is the only traced
for all of them. 13CO, although detected, was excluded since
it is optically thick (τ > 1.5). Fig. 2 shows the emission pro-
files of all these CO isotopologues in the three distinct velocity
components. We modelled the emission of each component by
fixing their FWHM to 10 km s−1 for C1, 13 km s−1 for C2 and
18 km s−1 for C3, as commonly found across the survey (see Ta-
ble 1). Moreover, since only one transition is covered for each
isotopologue, we fixed the Tex for all components to 8.8 K as
found in G+0.693 for C18O (Martín et al. 2008). This assump-
tion is well supported by the close agreement in Tex between
G+0.633 and G+0.693, with similarly close values across all ve-
locity components identified in G+0.633 (Table 1; San Andrés et
al., in prep.). The vLSR was left free for all components when per-
forming AUTOFIT in most cases, with their estimates shown in
Table B.1. The column densities derived for each isotopologue
and velocity component are listed in Table 2. Subsequently, these
were converted into CO column densities adopting the GC iso-
topic ratios X(16O/18O) = 250 and X(16O/17O) = 800 from Wil-
son & Rood (1994), together with X(12C/13C) = 40 obtained
in G+0.693 (Colzi et al. 2026). For each component, we com-
puted an average NCO from all isotopologues except 13CO, ob-
taining NCO = (2.6±0.5)×1018 cm−2, (2.9±0.8)×1018 cm−2 and
(1.2 ± 0.3) × 1018 cm−2 for C1, C2 and C3, respectively. These
NCO estimates were finally converted into NH2 using a CO-to-H2
conversion factor of ∼4.25×10−5. This factor was calculated as
X(16O/18O) × C18O/H2, adopting C18O/H2 = 1.7 × 10−7 from
Frerking et al. (1982) as done in G+0.693 (Martín et al. 2008).
For G+0.633, we obtained NH2 = (6.0 ± 1.2) × 1022 cm−2 for C1,
NH2 = (7±2)×1022 cm−2 for C2 and NH2 = (2.8±0.7)×1022 cm−2
for C3 (Table 2), which are ∼2.3, ∼1.9 and ∼5 times lower than
in G+0.693, respectively (see Table 1).
3.1.2. Kinetic temperature (Tkin)
CH3CN (methyl cyanide) and CH3CCH (propyne) are prolate
symmetric top molecules that serve as excellent tracers of the
kinetic temperature (Tkin) of the interstellar gas. This is because
their rotational energy levels (J) are subdivided into successive
K components (the projections of the total angular momentum
J onto the symmetry axis) that are slightly shifted in frequency,
producing a characteristic “K-ladder spectrum”. Since selection
rules for symmetric tops impede radiative transitions from occur-
ring between distinct K-levels, their population is expected to be
dominated by collisional excitation instead and thus thermalised.
In this sense, the Tex associated with the K-ladder spectrum of
each rotational transition of CH3CN and CH3CCH then provides
a measurement of the Tkin of the source (e.g., Yamamoto 2017).
In the current G+0.633 survey, we have detected the K-
ladders of the J + 1 →J rotational transitions with J = 1, 3, 4, 5
and 13 for CH3CN; and J = 1, 4, 5, 14 and 15 for CH3CCH.
These are shown in Fig. 3 (upper and lower panels, respectively).
We performed the fitting (adopting the three velocity compo-
nent model) of each rotational transition K-ladder spectrum sep-
arately to mitigate possible non-LTE effects, given that the very
low density found in G+0.633 makes the Tex, defined by the
same K within different J, to be much lower compared to the
Tkin, as generally found in the GC. For C1, we have identified
the complete progression of K-levels (K = 0, 1, ..., J) for each J
transition, with the exception of the most energetic ones (J > 13)
for which we only detected up to K = 6. In contrast, only the

3.1.3. Volume density (nH2)
HC3N (cyanoacetylene) is a linear molecule established as one
of the best probes for gas densities in GC clouds (e.g., Mills
et al. 2018) as demonstrated in G+0.693 (Colzi et al. 2024).
Its diagnostic power arises from its numerous accessible rota-
tional transitions at cm and mm wavelengths, evenly spaced by
∼9.1 GHz and connecting only adjacent energy levels given its
closed-shell electronic structure. Consequently, their intensities
are highly sensitive to excitation conditions, enabling the char-
acterisation of gas over a wide range of critical densities. Be-
sides, HC3N collisional coefficients are accurately determined
up to 300 K, suitable for GC conditions (Faure et al. 2016).
To provide an estimate for H2 volume density (nH2) in
G+0.633, we first conducted the LTE modelling of HC3N, de-
picted in Fig. 4. The detected lines span a range of energy lev-
els with upper energies of ∼4.4−34.1 K and 4 ≤Jup ≤126, all
of which are free from emission of other molecules. As Fig. 4
shows, the observed HC3N profiles also underline the three dis-
tinct velocity components identified in G+0.633. We conducted
their fitting simultaneously and allowing the four physical pa-
rameters to vary freely, with the exception of the FWHM for
C2 and C3 which were fixed to 13 and 18 km s−1, respectively.
The results of the fittings are summarised in Table B.1. We ob-
tained consistent Tex among components of ∼10 K, and derived
column densities of (2.414 ± 0.035) × 1014, (1.11 ± 0.04) × 1014
and (2.22 ± 0.34) × 1013 cm−2 for C1, C2 and C3, respectively.
These are translated into respective abundances relative to H2 of
(4.0 ± 0.8) × 10−9, (1.6 ± 0.5) × 10−9 and (7.9 ± 2.3) × 10−10.

3.2. HNCO emission
HNCO (isocyanic acid) is a prolate asymmetric top molecule
with a well-established role as a tracer of shock-heated regions.
This species has been observed across diverse astrophysical en-
vironments, its emission being associated with other key shock
tracers such as SO, SiO or CH3OH (e.g., Zinchenko et al. 2000;
Meier & Turner 2005). Furthermore, HNCO abundance is found
to be particularly enhanced in the presence of large-scale low-
velocity shocks (∼20 km s−1), making it a valuable diagnostic
tool to distinguish between kinematic regimes (e.g., Kelly et al.
2017).
The G+0.633 molecular cloud is precisely located at the peak
of HNCO (40,4 −30,3) emission in the southern part of the Sgr B2
complex, as seen in the MOPRA mosaic of the GC from Jones
et al. (2012) (Fig. 1). This can also be clearly discerned in the
HNCO velocity-integrated maps presented in Fig. 5, which were
generated following the same approach as for the HC3N 11−10
transition indicated above. The strong HNCO emission and its
corresponding physical parameters are also well constrained by
LTE in our single-dish survey, as detailed in Appendix E. In ad-
dition to the Ka = 0 transition of the J = 4−3 line targeted in the
maps, we detected the two Ka = 1 transitions of the same line
together with the Ka = 0, 1 transitions for J = 2 −1 and 5 −4.
No Ka > 1 transition is detected in G+0.633 (nor in G+0.693).
The line profiles of the Ka = 0 lines reveal the three velocity
components identified in G+0.633, whereas the Ka = 1 transi-
tions only display the C1 and C2 features (see Fig. E.1). We de-
rived total HNCO column densities of (1.85±0.11)×1015 cm−2,
(3.1 ± 0.4) × 1014 cm−2 and (6 ± 2) × 1013 cm−2 for C1, C2 and
C3, respectively. The corresponding abundances relative to H2
are (3.1 ± 0.7) × 10−8, (4.4 ± 1.6) × 10−9 and (2.1 ± 0.9) × 10−9.
Overall, the prominent HNCO emission observed towards
G+0.633 is a clear indicator of the presence of intense low-
velocity shocks, similarly to G+0.693 (Zeng et al. 2020). These
shocks could be producing an efficient sputtering of the inter-

4. Discussion
4.1. Comparison between the G+0.633 and G+0.693 clouds
The G+0.633 cloud shares several key physical characteristics
with its northern counterpart, G+0.693, making them a natural
pair for comparison. Beyond shocks, another remarkable feature
is the presence of multiple velocity components along the line of
sight. These exhibit significant differences in both clouds, pro-
viding important clues about their possible origin and nature.
In G+0.693, the dominant component (hereafter G+0.693-
C1) displays broad linewidths of ∼18–25 km s−1 (Zeng et al.
2018; Rivilla et al. 2022b), high Tkin of ∼70−140 K (Zeng et al.
2018; Colzi et al. 2024) and relatively low nH2 (∼2×104 cm−3;
Colzi et al. 2024). Combined with its extended emission, these
properties point to a turbulent gas reservoir largely supported
against gravitational collapse. In contrast, the secondary and
tertiary components observed in this cloud (G+0.693-C2 and
G+0.693-C3, respectively) exhibit much narrower line profiles
(∼7−9 km s−1), trace a much colder or denser gas (Tkin as low
as 30 K for G+0.693-C2, nH2 up to 4×105cm−3 for G+0.693-
C3), and exhibit more compact distributions embedded within
the extended G+0.693-C1 component (Colzi et al. 2024). Since
G+0.693-C1 likely arises from the large-scale low-velocity
shocks that shape Sgr B2 (Zeng et al. 2020), G+0.693-C2/C3
have been hypothesised to reflect the subsequent dissipation of
turbulence, fragmentation, and/or onset of prestellar cluster for-
mation in a post-shocked environment, given their enhanced lev-
els of deuterium fractionation (Colzi et al. 2022, 2024).
By contrast, all velocity components identified in G+0.633
display remarkably uniform physical conditions that closely re-
semble those of G+0.693-C1 (see Table 1), thus pointing to-
wards a similar shock-triggered nature rooted in Sgr B2 overall
dynamics. In particular, all of them show relatively low H2 den-
sities (< 3 × 104 cm−3) and moderately high, though lower, Tkin
of ∼55–90 K (Sect. 3.1.2), consistent with previous NH3-based
measurements across other CMZ clouds (∼50–120 K; Hüette-
meister et al. 1993; Krieger et al. 2017). In addition, gas and
dust are thermally decoupled, given the very low dust temper-
atures (Tdust) of ∼20 K (Etxaluze et al. 2013; Battersby et al.
2025). Along with their extended and spatially distinct mor-
phologies (see Fig. 5 and Sect. 4.2), these properties mainly in-
dicate a gas reservoir unlikely to undergo imminent fragmenta-
tion or collapse. Nonetheless, the particularly narrow linewidths
of G+0.633 main component (C1, ∼10 km s−1) close to those
of G+0.693-C2/C3, its comparatively greater nH2 among all
G+0.633 components (see Table 1), and its more spatially con-
fined distribution towards this cloud (Fig. 5), point to C1 trac-
ing a significantly denser and less turbulent gas that may be
more prone to gravitational collapse. Moreover, the location of
G+0.633 within SgrB2(DS), where tentative signs of very early
star formation have already been identified, supports a potential
prestellar nature for this component. However, further analysis
using established prestellar tracers, such as deuterated molecules
(e.g., Colzi et al. 2022), in combination with high angular reso-
lution data probing sub-pc scales, is required to clarify this point.
Collectively, these results suggest that G+0.633 may repre-
sent an even earlier evolutionary stage of shock-triggered CMZ
clouds than G+0.693, while exhibiting a comparable degree of
chemical enrichment. To gain deeper insight into the nature of
each of its three identified components, and address their inter-
play in shaping G+0.633 both physically and chemically, in the
next section we delve into their spatial characterisation.
4.2. The kinematic structure of G+0.633 velocity components
and the plausible origin for its chemical richness
To assess the physical origin of G+0.633 and the processes re-
sponsible for its chemical wealth, we examined the spatial distri-
bution of three diagnostic molecules: HNCO, as tracer of shock-
processed gas; HC3N, which outlines the density structure and
potential gas condensations; and ethanol (C2H5OH; here refer-
ring to the most stable ap isomer, see Rivilla et al. 2026b for
the updated nomenclature), as representative of the complex or-
ganic species detected in G+0.633. Since C2H5OH mainly forms
on dust grains and can be ejected into the gas phase through the
action of shocks (e.g., Jiménez-Serra et al. 2022; Agúndez et al.
2023), its distribution provides a great proxy for the spatial ex-
tent of chemically enriched gas, and thus more complex species.
The resulting velocity-integrated maps are shown in Fig. 5, and
were computed for C2H5OH mimicking the approach we fol-
lowed for HC3N and HNCO (Sects. 3.1.3 and 3.2, respectively).
Inspection of Fig. 5 maps reveal that among the three iden-
tified components, only C1 is directly associated with G+0.633.
In fact, although HC3N, HNCO, and C2H5OH display extended
emission across the mapped region, it only becomes prominent
towards the position of G+0.633 at C1 velocities, and peaking on
it for the latter two species (first column in Fig. 5). In contrast,
the spatial distributions of C2 and C3 (second and third columns
in Fig. 5, respectively) are not centred on G+0.633, but rather
on G+0.693 and the cores, indicating that none of these compo-
nents is intrinsically related to this cloud. Their overall extended
emission, unrelated to the C1 peak, results into a minimal contri-
bution towards the position of G+0.633 as observed in all trac-
ers, delivering the weak spectral features evident in the single-
dish data. Nonetheless, the spatial continuity and soft intensity
gradient in the C2 distribution from G+0.633 towards G+0.693,
particularly observed in HNCO and C2H5OH, suggest a phys-
ical link between C1 and C2. By contrast, the very faint and
spatially offset emission of C3 points towards a different origin.
Therefore, C1 outlines G+0.633 physically, C2 may represent
a gas interface between G+0.633 and G+0.693 (which peaks at
∼70 km s−1), whereas C3 would correspond to a separate feature.
The spatial profiles of the C1 and C2 components fit
naturally within the prevailing hypothesis explaining Sgr B2
morphology and kinematics: large-scale shocks induced by a
cloud–cloud collision event. The proposed scenario invokes a
high-velocity (∼60−80 km s−1) dense clump ploughing in the
north-east direction into a more extended and less dense cloud
with lower systematic velocities (∼40−50 km s−1; Armijos-
Abendaño et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2020; Colzi et al. 2024). Phys-
ical signatures supporting this scenario include a ∼5′ “hole” fea-
ture at these lower velocities (delineating the penetrating region),
previously identified in 13CO and other tracers (e.g., Hasegawa
et al. 1994; Sato et al. 2000), and apparent in Fig. 5 for C1; and a
cone-shaped structure in the position-position-velocity diagrams
of Sgr B2, with its tip found at ∼70 km s−1 and located close to
G+0.693 (e.g. Henshaw et al. 2016; Enokiya & Fukui 2022).

extending from the south towards G+0.633, together with the

Additional evidence supporting the cloud-cloud collision
origin for G+0.633 comes from the detection of exceptionally
bright Class I methanol (CH3OH) masers (see Fig. F.1). These
particular masers are pumped via collisions (e.g., Leurini et al.
2016) and are found to be mainly excited in a wide variety of
shocked environments, including molecular outflows from star-
forming regions, expanding HII regions, and cloud–cloud colli-
sions (e.g., Voronkov et al. 2006; Sjouwerman et al. 2010, re-
spectively). The relative intensities of different Class I methanol
maser transitions, particularly the 36 and 44 GHz lines, can also
provide insight into their physical origin. In star-forming re-
gions with protostellar outflows, the 44 GHz maser is gener-
ally stronger than the 36 GHz line (e.g., Voronkov et al. 2014;
Leurini et al. 2016). In contrast, shocked environments not show-
ing active star formation, such as cloud–cloud interaction zones,
tend to show the opposite behaviour (Sjouwerman et al. 2010;
Pihlström et al. 2011; McEwen et al. 2016). In G+0.693, Zeng
et al. (2020) reported that the 36 GHz methanol maser is more
than an order of magnitude brighter than its 44 GHz counterpart,
a strong indication of shock excitation without stellar feedback.
Not surprisingly, given their expected common origin, this exact
same pattern is also observed in G+0.633 (see Fig. F.1). More-
over, the absence of typical high-mass star formation tracers such
as OH, H2O, and Class II CH3OH masers (which are radiatively
pumped, Ellingsen 2005; Fontani et al. 2010) at G+0.633 posi-
tion, along with the lack of prominent dust-continuum sources
(e.g., Ginsburg et al. 2018; Lu et al. 2019; Meng et al. 2022;
Jeff et al. 2024) and Young Stellar Object (YSO) candidates (De
Buizer et al. 2025), argues against significant stellar feedback in
this region. In fact, Ginsburg et al. (2018) established that high-
mass star formation in the Sgr B2 complex is typically associ-
ated with NH2 ∼2 × 1023 cm−2, while no YSOs are found at
NH2 < 1023 cm−2. The NH2 we derive for the main C1 com-
ponent in G+0.633 is ∼2 times below this threshold (Table 1),
indicating that this gas is still in a stage prior to star formation.
Together, these results suggest that the shocks induced by
the cloud–cloud collision are the dominant process shaping the
physical structure of G+0.633 (C1 and C2) and driving its rich
chemical complexity. In this framework, G+0.633 may repre-
sent a chemically enriched yet dynamically young environment
where the initial stages of gas compression induced by the large-
scale collision are taking place, and potentially preceding the
formation of denser prestellar structures such as those observed
in G+0.693. We note, however, that although C2 also appears
chemically rich, its observational signatures in G+0.633 are
likely limited to the most abundant molecular species.
4.3. The potential role of Sgr B2(DS) in shaping G+0.633
In addition to the large-scale cloud–cloud collision likely setting
the global physical conditions for G+0.633, the enhanced molec-
ular emission observed in C1, along with the prominent Class
I CH3OH masers associated with this component, could com-
plementarily arise from local feedback from Sgr B2(DS). In the
continuum, Sgr B2(DS) appears as a bubble-like structure cen-
tred ∼20′′ north of G+0.633, with an outer diameter of ∼1.5 pc
(∼40′′), interpreted as an expanding HII region likely powered
by a deeply embedded O7 star (Meng et al. 2019). Sgr B2(DS)
exhibits a mix of compact thermal free-free and extended non-
thermal synchrotron continuum emission (up to ∼1′, delineated
by the golden dashed box in Figs. 1 and 5), alongside promi-
nent Hydrogen and Helium radio recombination lines (RRLs),
indicative of highly ionised gas. These RRLs display broad
profiles (∼30−40 km s−1) and velocity gradients from a central
∼70 km s−1 to ∼55 km s−1 at the outer edge, precisely where
G+0.633 is located. Consistently, numerous of these RRLs are
also detected across the G+0.633 survey (see Fig. F.2), exhibit-
ing linewidths and velocities identical to those reported by Meng
et al. (2019). Interestingly, the RRLs show a peculiar intensity-
frequency anti-correlation, pointing towards non-LTE excitation.
This behaviour was attributed by Meng et al. (2019) to the ex-
tended synchrotron radiation observed in Sgr B2(DS), likely
triggered by small-scale shocks resulting from the interaction be-
tween the expanding HII region and the denser surrounding ma-
terial (Padovani et al. 2019). These shocks could then be produc-
ing a local enhancement in the molecular abundances towards
Sgr B2(DS) and hence G+0.633, as seen in Fig. 5. However, we
caution that the significant differences in velocity and linewidth
between the RRLs and the rest of molecular lines observed in
G+0.633 (also markedly differing from any of its three identified
components; see Table 1), prevents establishing a direct physical
association with Sgr B2(DS), and the observed alignment may
just result from projection effects along the line of sight. Future
studies at high angular resolution will be essential to clarify the
morphology and possible connection between the molecular and
RRL emission observed in this region.
5. Summary and conclusions
We presented a physical study on G+0.633-0.0604, a newly dis-
covered shock-triggered and chemically rich molecular cloud at
the southernmost part of the Sgr B2 complex, and which stands
as the “astrochemical twin” of the extraordinarily rich G+0.693-
0.027 cloud located north in the complex. Using high-sensitivity
single-dish observations at 7, 3, and 1.3 mm from the Yebes
40m, IRAM 30m, and APEX radio telescopes, we analysed the
emission of CH3CCH and CH3CN (to probe the kinetic temper-
ature, Tkin), HC3N and several CO isotopologues (to constrain
the gas volume, nH2, and column densities, NH2), and HNCO
(a tracer of shocks). We complemented these analyses with
3 mm mosaicking observations of the Sgr B2 region in HNCO,
HC3N and C2H5OH, the latter marking G+0.633 complex or-
ganic chemistry. The data reveal three distinct velocity com-
ponents sharing comparable physical properties (Tkin∼55−90 K,
nH2∼(0.5−2.5)×104
cm−3 and NH2∼(3−7)×1022
cm−2). The
main component (C1), which displays vLSR of ∼48.5 km s−1
and very narrow linewidths (FWHM∼10 km s−1), dominates the
emission and delineates G+0.633 physically. Its spatial corre-
spondence with a peak in HNCO supports a shock-driven origin,
likely linked to the cloud–cloud collision event hypothesised to
be shaping the Sgr B2 complex. The second, weaker compo-
nent at ∼61 km s−1 (C2; FWHM∼13 km s−1) is also related to
the same interaction, but traces the more clustered gas associ-
ated with the shock wavefronts heading north of Sgr B2. The
third component (C3, vLSR∼89 km s−1, FWHM∼18 km s−1) is not
kinematically connected with Sgr B2, but related to the large-
scale CMZ dynamics. Of the three, C1 stands out as the densest
and least turbulent, with physical properties that may point to it
tracing the earliest steps towards a potential prestellar phase.
The discovery of G+0.633 as a new shock-triggered, chem-
ically enriched cloud in an early evolutionary stage represents
a cornerstone for understanding star formation in the CMZ. Its
properties identify it as the southern counterpart, within Sgr B2,
of the northern G+0.693 cloud, which already shows prestellar
signatures. Together, these two sources provide unique laborato-
ries to explore how shocks foster molecular complexity and set
the initial conditions for cluster formation in the CMZ.

Appendix C: CH3CN and CH3CCH rotational diagram analysis

Appendix F: Class I methanol masers and Radio Recombination Lines (RRLs) detected in G+0.633
Fig. F.1 displays the observational signatures of all Class I methanol masers identified in the current G+0.633 survey. All of them
exhibit very bright emission at the velocities of the main C1 component (peaking at ∼48.5 km s−1), highly contrasting with the
much fainter profiles from the C2 and C3 components (peaking at ∼61 km s−1 and ∼89 km s−1, respectively). The linewidths of the
C1 masers (∼7 km s−1) are reasonably similar to those of G+0.633 lines (∼10 km s−1) and identical for the other two components
(∼13 km s−1 for C2, ∼18 km s−1 for C3), indicating that the observed maser emission originates from the same gas reservoirs.
Fig. F.1: Spectra of the Class I methanol masers detected towards G+0.633. The dashed red, green and cyan vertical lines denote the
central velocities of G+0.633 C1, C2 and C3 components, respectively. Panel labels indicate the particular transitions being plotted.
Another distinctive feature of the G+0.633 molecular cloud is the detection of numerous very energetic Hydrogen and Helium
Radio Recombination Lines (RRLs), indicative of heavily ionised gas in the line of sight. This emission is found to originate locally
from Sgr B2(DS), previously interpreted as an expanding HII region (Meng et al. 2019). It appears unrelated to any of the three
velocity components identified in G+0.633, since the RLLs exhibit peak velocities of ∼55−65 km s−1 and very broad linewidths
of ∼30−40 km s−1. Fig. F.2 displays a representative subset of the detected RRLs, selected among the ∼100 identified across the
survey. All these span a wide range of energy levels (nup down to 29) and include large quantum leaps (∆n ≤8, α to θ-transitions, for
Hydrogen RRLs; and ∆n ≤2, α and β-transitions, for He ones). Each RRL found in the G+0.633 survey was fitted independently,
adopting a Tex of ∼8000 K (as in Meng et al. 2019) and leaving the remaining parameters free. This approach stems from RRLs
non-LTE excitation already reported by Meng et al. (2019), manifested in a peculiar intensity–frequency anti-correlation (Fig. F.2).
