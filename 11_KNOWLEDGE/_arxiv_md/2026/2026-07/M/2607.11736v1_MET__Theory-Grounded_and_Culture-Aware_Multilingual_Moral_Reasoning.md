Abstract
Language models are increasingly used for moral decision-making across
diverse linguistic and cultural contexts, yet existing work overlooks mul-
tilinguality on three aspects: 1) multilingual evaluation benchmarks use
direct translation, failing to adapt culture-specific items; 2) inference-time
methods for moral reasoning rely on static, English-centric scaffolds and
lack grounding in moral theory; 3) training methods for moral decision-
making typically require expensive supervision from stronger models or
human annotators. We address these gaps with three contributions. First,
we introduce MCLASH, a multilingual moral decision-making benchmark
to capture culturally situated moral intuitions and social norms across
languages. Second, we propose MET (Multilingual Ethics with Theory-
grounded reasoning), a two-step prompting method built on expert-curated,
theory-based grounds drawn from psychology and philosophy: the model
first selects situation- and culture-specific grounds, then reasons over them
in the native language of the user. Third, we introduce MET-D (MET-
Distillation), which enhances the second step through a self-distillation
training stage that requires no external supervision. MET-D improves
macro-F1 over the base model on all three models of different sizes and
families (Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Gemma3-4B), by an average of 3.71 points
on MCLASH and 4.23 on MMoralExceptQA, with a peak MCLASH gain
of 12.94 points for Malay on Qwen3-8B. We further reveal that MET-D
increases native-language reasoning by 62.13 points on average, and that
beneficial grounds differ systematically across cultures. Together, these con-
tributions open the path for culture-aligned, theory-grounded multilingual
moral reasoning.

Introduction
Language models are increasingly deployed in moral decision-making across high-stakes
domains such as law (Xiao et al., 2021; Nguyen, 2023) and healthcare (Singhal et al., 2023;
Hu et al., 2024), where users span diverse cultural backgrounds. Effective deployment
in such settings requires that models reason within the linguistic and cultural context
of the user, accounting for the local value systems that shape a sound moral judgment.
However, culturally aligned moral decision-making remains overlooked in both evaluation
and methods.
Evaluation: direct translation strips cultural context from moral dilemmas. Existing
multilingual moral benchmarks directly translate English scenarios (Kumar & Jurgens, 2025;
Farid et al., 2025) without adapting culture-specific items (Newmark, 1988) such as named
entities, institutions, currencies, and customs. When directly translated into Korean or
Hindi, a U.S. scenario set during “Thanksgiving” or at a “Wendy’s” can import improper

Method (inference): existing scaffolds lack theory grounding, culture- and situation-
specific adaptation, and multilingual support. Beyond producing the correct decision, the
reasoning that justifies it must be reliable and understandable for users to audit (Gilpin et al.,
2018; Besold & Uckelman, 2018). Theoretical grounding can improve reasoning quality in
moral decision-making (Chakraborty et al., 2025), yet most existing methods do not engage
with moral theories such as deontology or care ethics (Jin et al., 2022; AlKhamissi et al.,
2024). Even the theory-grounded work (Chakraborty et al., 2025) covers only a narrow
set of theories and applies them without cultural or situational adaptation. Research on
moral reasoning is also largely English-only; methods are developed and evaluated on
English benchmarks (Chiu et al., 2025), and multilingual reasoning techniques borrowed
from culture-agnostic tasks assume a language-invariant reasoning structure (Zhang et al.,
2025; Zhu et al., 2024; She et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2024), which does not
hold when moral frameworks vary by culture.
We address these gaps with MET (Multilingual Ethics with Theory-grounded reasoning), a
two-step prompting method in which the model first selects the grounds most relevant to
the given situation, then uses them to produce a grounded reasoning chain in the native
language of the user (Figure 1). The full, multilingual set of grounds from which the model
selects is developed by the authors including a domain expert1, drawing on psychology and
philosophy literature, and organized into six high-level dimensions (e.g., Value Systems,
Conflict Handling Strategies).
Method (training): training the model to ground its reasoning in moral theory suffers
from a lack of verifiable signals. Prompting alone is insufficient: even when the model
selects relevant grounds, the reasoning chain frequently engages with only a few (Figure 2),
leaving the rest unaddressed and producing shallow, one-sided judgments. However,
training the model to substantively engage with the grounds is hard because moral decision-
making is subjective and generally lacks the verifiable ground truth that enables rejection
sampling (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025); existing methods therefore rely on distillation

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• MCLASH, a multilingual moral decision-making benchmark of 1,852 long-form
scenarios across six languages, constructed by cultural adaptation rather than direct
translation.
• Expert-curated theoretical grounds for moral reasoning, organized into six high-
level dimensions and translated into all six languages.
• MET, a two-step prompting method that employs situation- and culture-specific
grounds, and MET-D, which extends MET with a self-distillation training stage
that requires no external supervision.

Moral Decision-Making Datasets
Most previous work in moral decision-making either probes the preferences of the models
rather than providing an answer, which makes it hard to assess their actual decision-making

multilinguality, thereby overlooking the cultural differences in making judgments (Lee et al.,

2.2
Inference-Time Methods for Moral Decision-Making
Most inference-time methods for moral decision-making rely on structured prompting
(AlKhamissi et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2022; Chakraborty et al., 2025), but apply the same
framework to every dilemma. This approach is insufficient, since the appropriate moral
framework varies with the context of each dilemma (Fletcher, 1966; Dancy, 2004). In
contrast, MoReBench (Chiu et al., 2025) addresses per-instance adaptation through rubrics
for evaluating moral reasoning. While the rubrics can be adapted as inference-time guidance,
they are coarse, atheoretical, English-only, and labor-intensive to construct. Closest to our
approach, Chakraborty et al. (2025) shows that theoretical scaffolds improve reasoning
quality, but uses a narrow, static, and English-only set of theories. MET combines theory
grounding with multilingual support and per-instance, per-language adaptation, enabling
culture-aligned reasoning in the native language of the end user.
2.3
Multilingual Training for Moral Decision-Making
Methods developed for conventional reasoning domains such as math and code treat non-
English reasoning as a translation of English reasoning, which assumes the same reasoning
structure across languages. Common techniques include aligning non-English representa-
tions toward the English subspace (Zhang et al., 2025), optimizing for similarity between
non-English and English reasoning chains (She et al., 2024), and bridging multilingual
encoders with English-trained reasoners that apply English reasoning patterns to inputs in
any language (Zhao et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2024). These approaches fail in moral decision-
making, where different cultures apply different moral frameworks reflecting their distinct
values (Hwang et al., 2025; Myung et al., 2024).
Existing training methods for moral reasoning rely on heavy external supervision via hand-
crafted rewards (Tennant et al., 2025) or stronger models (An & Du, 2026), and operate only
in English. In the multilingual space, prior work is largely evaluation-focused (Agarwal
et al., 2024; Kumar & Jurgens, 2025; Zhou et al., 2025), and training methods target cultural
commonsense rather than reasoning through moral dilemmas where values conflict, using
cultural surveys (Li et al., 2024a), multi-agent debates (Li et al., 2024b), fine-grained critique
(Feng et al., 2025), or culture-aware self-improvement (Xu et al., 2025). MET-D directly
targets moral decision-making and self-supervises via the character-paired structure of
CLASH.

Construction of MCLASH
CLASH (Lee et al., 2025) consists of 345 high-stakes situations paired with 3,795 character
descriptions, and tasks models with making a decision from the perspective of the character.
We adapt the original English situations into five languages (Chinese, Hindi, Korean, Malay,
and Spanish) and their corresponding cultures2 using Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025), selected
for its adaptation quality (Appendix A.1). Next, native speakers review and modify the
narratives with a rigorous checklist (Figure 11) to ensure cultural relevance. When a situation
seems entirely irrelevant to a given culture, we discuss with the inspectors and discard

4
Method
In §4.1, we describe how we curate the theoretical grounds. We then present 1) MET, our
two-step prompting method of selecting and applying the grounds, and 2) MET-D, which
constructs a training dataset and adds a rejection-sampling distillation stage on top of MET
in §4.2.
4.1
Theoretical Grounds
In collaboration with a domain expert, we curate a global set of theoretical grounds, trans-
lated into all six languages, for the model to select from. A total of 37 grounds are organized
into six dimensions, each paired with a description that helps the model understand the
ground and select the most relevant ones. This uniform structure makes the set easy to
extend: a new ground can be added simply by specifying its dimension and description
without changing the selection process. Figure 1 shows an example of these grounds and
their dimensions.
We explain each dimension below, with the full list of grounds and descriptions provided
in Appendix B.1. The Normative Authority dimension (e.g., Railton, 1986; Wood, 2008;
Reus-Smit et al., 2005) establishes the foundational premises for the reasoning process, while
Value Systems (e.g., Graham et al., 2013; Schwartz, 2012) identify the values that should be
taken into account. Ethical Theories (e.g., Alexander & Moore, 2007; Mill, 2016) provide
the normative structure guiding the reasoning, and Cognitive Reasoning Strategies (e.g.,
Wei et al., 2022; Freeman, 2010) outline its underlying cognitive structure. Finally, Conflict
Handling Strategies (e.g., Dancy, 2001; Musschenga, 2005) and Moral Uncertainty (e.g., Ess,
2006; MacAskill et al., 2020) address approaches for navigating dilemmas and managing
uncertainty in moral decision-making.
4.2
Guiding Reasoning using Grounds
MET. Given a situation, MET guides the target model in two steps (Figure 3): 1) Ground
Selection: The model is prompted with the dimensions, grounds, and descriptions, and
asked to select the grounds most relevant to the given situation, forming an instance-specific
framework. 2) Theory-Guided Reasoning: Given the selected grounds and the situation, the
model outputs the reasoning and the answer. The prompts are presented in Figures 14 to 16.
MET-D. Analysis of the initial results reveals that in the second step, models often engage
with only a subset of the grounds they select, leaving the rest unaddressed, thus producing
shallow reasoning (Figure 2). To enhance the utilization of the selected grounds in theory-
guided reasoning, we introduce additional training using a synthetic dataset that follows
the structure of CLASH. We refer to this training procedure, along with the subsequent
MET prompting, as MET-D (MET-Distillation). Moral dilemmas typically require human
annotation for ground-truth answers, especially “ambiguous” ones. CLASH circumvents
this by pairing each situation with well-defined character descriptions whose value priorities
determine the ground-truth decision, eliminating the need for human supervision. CLASH
also captures moral ambivalence through ternary labels (yes/no/ambiguous) rather than
clear-cut yes/no.

Analyses
We examine why our method works and what it reveals about multilingual moral reasoning.
We first verify that ground selection captures relevant grounds instead of random noise
(§6.1), then show that training increases engagement with the selected grounds (§6.2). We
also analyze how beneficial grounds differ across languages (§6.3). We further show that
training improves not only performance but also legibility, by raising the proportion of
native-language reasoning (§6.4), and finally analyze cross-lingual transfer (§6.5).

6.2
Training Increases Utilization of the Selected Grounds
We validate that training leads to improved utilization of selected grounds in theory-guided
reasoning. We sample 20 instances in English, Chinese, and Hindi and have annotators mark
whether each selected ground is used in the reasoning of Qwen3-4B both before and after
training. The per-instance utilization rate is the fraction of selected grounds used. Average
utilization increases from 52.10% to 61.19% in English, from 31.87% to 37.05% in Chinese,
and from 42.00% to 51.75% in Hindi.
However, we note that this metric primarily captures engagement, which refers to whether
the reasoning mentions and applies the given grounds. This is different from reconciliation,
which involves weighing and integrating these grounds to reach a conclusion. We observe
cases where the number of mentioned grounds increases but reconciliation is absent (Figure
18). Improving how models reconcile grounds is left to future work.
6.3
Beneficial Grounds are Different per Language
For each ground, we filter the instances where the ground is selected in ground selection
and compute the macro-F1 of our trained model on those instances with and without the
grounds included in the prompt for generating the reasoning chain. The difference between
the two F1 scores quantifies how much each ground contributes to the performance. Figure 4
reports the mean F1 gain per dimension and the top-six beneficial grounds per language.
Every dimension yields a positive average gain with variance across languages. Hindi
benefits the most broadly, likely because its low-resource status leaves the base model with
weaker structural priors for Hindi moral reasoning. The Moral Uncertainty dimension
shows cultural friction: it hurts F1 in Korean and Chinese, consistent with the Confucian
emphasis on relational obligations that prescribe a definite moral stance.
The top-six grounds per language further reveal culturally-grounded differences. English
gains most from Probabilistic Moral Uncertainty (+9.6), First-principles reasoning (+9.0),

6.4
Training Improves Native-Language Reasoning
Since native speakers naturally prompt models in their native language and expect responses
in the same language, we fix both the input and output language to the native language as
our default setting. Beyond input and output, we additionally enforce the internal reasoning
chain to be in the native language, motivated by legibility: end users can audit the model’s
reasoning process only if it is in a language they understand.
A natural question to pose is whether using native-language reasoning hurts task perfor-
mance. To answer this, we use MCLASH to compare four conditions that share the same
input/output language setting but differ in how the internal reasoning chain is handled: 1)
base model (Qwen3-4B) with no language enforcement on reasoning chains, 2) base model
with explicit prefill tokens to enforce native-language reasoning, 3) our trained model plus
prompting without enforcement, and 4) our full method, MET-D, with training, prompting,
and enforcement. We compare the performance and the percentage of the native language.
We use word-level language detection for non-Latin languages, where the unicode scripts
uniquely identify the language, and sentence-level language detection for Latin languages,
where per-word detection is unreliable.

Conclusion
We curate a culturally adapted evaluation dataset and propose a method that grounds moral
reasoning in psychological and philosophical theories. We carefully curate dimensions and
theory-relevant grounds, then introduce a two-step process consisting of ground selection
followed by theory-guided reasoning. In addition, we present a self-distillation training
strategy designed to improve the ability of models to adhere to the grounds during reasoning.
Our results demonstrate an average F1 improvement of 3.71 points on MCLASH and 4.23
points on MMoralExceptQA, along with enhanced legibility, reflected in an increase in
native-language usage of 62.13 percentage points. We further find that beneficial grounds
align with the actual characteristics of each culture, while cross-lingual transfer follows
linguistic typology rather than cultural similarity.

Limitations
Our approach has two main limitations. First, our training targets theory-guided reasoning
rather than ground selection, so ground selection still relies on the untrained base model.
Second, as discussed in §6.2, how models reconcile multiple selected grounds remains an
open question. Addressing both is a promising direction for future work.

• Truth-based Authority: Ground your reasoning in the assumption that there are ob-

• Reason-Based Authority: Ground your reasoning in principles or values that any

• Agreement-Based Authority: Justify the moral conclusion by appealing to what

• Institution-Based Authority: Justify the decision by appealing to established social

institutional legitimacy, and shared standards.
• Responsibility-Based Authority: Justify the conclusion in terms of authentic choice

• Relation-Based Authority: Justify the conclusion in terms of shared commitments
and endorsed values. Emphasize collective endorsement rather than objective truth.
• Human-Nature-Based Authority: Ground your reasoning in features of human

• Moral Foundations Theory: Identify which moral foundations are most relevant in

functioning.

Cognitive Reasoning Strategies
• Step-by-step reasoning: Break down the moral issue into clearly sequenced steps.
Make each assumption and inference explicit before reaching a conclusion.
• Harm-benefit analysis: Identify all foreseeable harms and benefits and use them in
the analysis.
• Stakeholder analysis: Identify all affected stakeholders. Analyze how each party’s
rights, interests, risks, and perspectives are impacted.
• Counterfactual reasoning: Consider how the moral evaluation would change if key
facts or minor details were different. Test the robustness of the conclusion under
alternative scenarios.
• Consequentialist framing: Frame the issue primarily in terms of outcomes and
systemic effects.
• First-principles reasoning: Begin from foundational moral assumptions and derive
the conclusion through structured logical reasoning.
Conflict Handling Strategies
• Context-sensitive resolution: Resolve the conflict by carefully analyzing the specific
relevant features of the case. Do not assume that general principles automatically
override contextual factors.
• Principle-based resolution: Identify the relevant principles and determine how they
rank or interact. Resolve the conflict by applying a structured hierarchy or balancing
process.
• Coherence-based resolution: Revise principles and case judgments iteratively until
they form a consistent and coherent moral position.
• Adversarial Testing: First construct the strongest arguments for each side. Then
evaluate which position withstands critical scrutiny.
• Perspective-based Resolution: Evaluate the conflict from the standpoint of each
affected party before reaching a conclusion.
• Spectator-based Resolution: Evaluate the conflict from the perspective of a spectator
who is not directly involved in the situation.
• Procedural Resolution: When principles conflict, determine what decision procedure
would be considered fair and legitimate by all affected parties.
Moral Uncertainty
• Monistic Commitment: Select the moral framework judged most defensible and
reason exclusively within it.
• Multi-framework Consideration: Evaluate the issue from multiple moral frame-
works and compare their conclusions without reducing them to a single metric.
• Probabilistic Moral Uncertainty: Assign relative credibility to competing moral
theories and choose the most probable answer.
• Optimization: Calculate in terms of expected value, and select an action that maxi-
mizes the expected value.
• Maximin Reasoning: Choose the action or principle that leads to the least harmful
outcome.
