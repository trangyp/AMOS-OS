Thatchaphol Saranurak‡

Abstract
The minimum degree spanning tree problem is a classic NP-hard problem whose optimal
approximation guarantee was established since the early 1990s: F¨urer and Raghavachari [FR92]
gave an ˜O(mn)-time algorithm that computes a spanning tree with maximum degree ∆⋆+ 1,
where ∆⋆denotes the optimum value. Whether similarly strong guarantees can be achieved in
near-linear time has remained open for over three decades.
We give the first near-linear-time algorithm that computes a spanning tree with maximum
degree ⌈(1 + ε)∆⋆⌉+ 1 in ˜O(m/ε2) time. Prior near-linear-time algorithms either achieved the
weaker bound ⌈(1 + ε)∆⋆⌉+ O(log n/ε2) [DHZ20] or required dense graphs with m ≥n7/4
[CQT21, BFW26].
Using the same framework, our algorithm can also compute a spanning tree with maxi-
mum degree ∆⋆+ 1 in ˜O(mn2/3) time, improving upon the recent ˜O(mn3/4)-time algorithm
of [BFW26]. These two results strictly improve all previous construction algorithms for the
minimum degree spanning tree problem.

Introduction
In the minimum degree spanning tree (MDST) problem, we are given an undirected connected graph
G = (V, E) and seek a spanning tree T minimizing its maximum degree, i.e., maxu∈V degT (u). Let
∆⋆:=
min
T: spanning tree of G max
u∈V degT (u)
denote the optimum value. The problem is NP-hard: deciding whether ∆⋆= 2 is equivalent to
deciding whether G contains a Hamiltonian path.
MDST is one of the most prominent examples of problems whose approximation guarantee was
essentially understood long ago, while its running time remained open for decades. In a landmark
result, F¨urer and Raghavachari [FR92, FR94] gave an algorithm that returns a spanning tree of
maximum degree at most ∆⋆+1 in ˜O(mn) time. This additive-one guarantee is best possible. Thus,
from the approximation viewpoint, the problem was already nearly settled in the early 1990s. The
main remaining challenge has been to obtain similarly strong degree guarantees in near-linear time.
Recent progress narrowed this gap only partially. Duan, He, and Zhang [DHZ20] gave the first
near-linear-time algorithm, but with the weaker degree bound (1 + ε)∆⋆+ O(ε−2 log n). Chekuri,
Quanrud, and Torres [CQT21] obtained a near-linear-time algorithm for estimating the optimum
value and constructing a sparsifier, but not for constructing the spanning tree itself. Bhattacharya,
Farokhnejad, and Wang [BFW26] later gave a ∆⋆+1 approximation in ˜O(mn3/4) time, breaking the
classical O(mn) barrier. Running this algorithm on the sparsifier of [CQT21] also yields a spanning
tree of degree ⌈(1 + ε)∆⋆⌉+ 2 in ˜O((m + n7/4)/ε2) time, which is near-linear only when m ≥n7/4.
Hence, before our work, no near-linear-time algorithm was known to construct a spanning tree with
maximum degree O(∆⋆); indeed, none achieved even an o(∆⋆log n) guarantee.
Our Results.
We show that strong degree guarantees are achievable in near-linear time by giving
a unified framework that strictly improves all prior construction algorithms for MDST.
Theorem 1.1. There exists a deterministic algorithm that, given an undirected graph and ε ∈(0, 1],
computes a spanning tree with maximum degree at most ⌈(1 + ε)∆⋆⌉+ 1 in ˜O(m/ε2) time.
This is the first near-linear-time algorithm that constructs a spanning tree with maximum
degree O(∆⋆), resolving an open question raised by [DP20, Pet16, Sar24]. In fact, it gives the
first near-linear-time algorithm with an o(∆⋆log n) guarantee. Note that for ∆⋆= polylog(n),
Theorem 1.1 gives a spanning tree with degree ∆⋆+ 2 in near-linear time by setting ε = 1/∆⋆.
The same framework also yields a faster additive-one approximation.
Theorem 1.2. There exists a deterministic algorithm that, given an undirected graph, computes a
spanning tree with maximum degree at most ∆⋆+ 1 in ˜O(mn2/3) time.
This improves both the classical ˜O(mn)-time algorithm of [FR94] and the recent ˜O(mn3/4)
bound of [BFW26]. See Table 1 for a comparison with previous results.
Broader Context.
MDST is arguably the most basic problem in degree-constrained network
design. The minimum-cost variant of MDST [RMR+93, KR00, CRRT05, Goe06, SL15] and its
generalizations—including bounded-degree Steiner tree, k-connected subgraphs [LS13, FNR15],
arborescences [BKN08], and dijoins [KLS12]—have played a central role in the development of
iterative rounding techniques [LRS11], which usually take large polynomial time.
In contrast,
the fast-algorithm side of this area remains far less developed.
Our work suggests that strong
degree guarantees in degree-constrained network design may be compatible with near-linear-time
algorithms.

Our Unified Framework.
Both Theorems 1.1 and 1.2 follow from the same core subroutine.
Suppose we are given a forest F of maximum degree at most k with f connected components. For
a parameter H ≥1, the goal is to reduce f quickly by repeatedly using augmenting chains of length
at most H, where an augmenting chain is analogous to an augmenting path in the maximum flow
problem. We show, formally in Theorem 3.1, how to reduce the number of components by Ω(f/H)
in ˜O(mH) time.
This improves the recent subroutine of [BFW26], which in ˜O(mH) time reduces the number of
components by only Ω(f/(θH)), where θ = n/f is the average component size. At a high level,
both algorithms rely on two ingredients: a progress step that merges forest components via an
augmenting chain, and an auxiliary step that merges reducible regions to help expose such chains.
Informally, a region is reducible if the forest can be locally rearranged inside the region so as to
make any prescribed node in the region slack, without violating the degree bound.
The algorithm of [BFW26] performs auxiliary merging aggressively as long as reducible re-
gions have size at most O(θ), and only then extracts augmenting chains of length at most H.
Each augmenting chain renders O(Hθ) vertices unusable, so their algorithm reduces the number of
components by only Ω(f/(θH)).
Remark 1.3. In Appendix B, we provide a comprehensive explanation of the barriers of the pre-
vious works. Specifically, in Appendix B.3, we show a concrete example that [BFW26] inherently
takes super-linear time, even if we allow O(∆⋆) degree bound. The intuition of our main object
(augmenting chain) and how it improves [BFW26], is explained in Appendix B.4.
In contrast, our algorithm prioritizes progress: for each threshold t = 1, 2, . . . , H, it first searches
for augmenting chains of length at most t, and invokes merging only when this search fails. Although
chains found in round t may render some regions unusable, we can restore all regions at the start
of round t + 1 in ˜O(m) time. Our new analysis, based on a relaxed notion of augmenting chains
called pseudo-chains, shows that after H rounds we reduce the number of forest components by
Ω(f/H) in ˜O(mH) total time.

Notations and Preliminaries
Let G = (V, E) be the connected input graph with |V | = n nodes and |E| = m edges. For any
subgraph K ⊆G, we let V (K) and E(K) respectively denote the set of nodes and the set of edges
of K. We denote by ∆⋆and k, the maximum degree of the optimal spanning tree of G and the
spanning tree of G returned by our algorithm, respectively. Thus, either k := ⌈(1 + ε) · ∆⋆⌉+ 1
for some arbitrarily small constant ε ∈(0, 1), or k := ∆⋆+ 1 (see Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.2).
Throughout this extended abstract, whenever we refer to a forest F of G, we assume that V (F) = V .
We say that a node v ∈V is k-saturated w.r.t. a forest F of G if degF(v) = k, and k-slack if
degF(v) < k, where degF(v) denotes the degree of v in F. Furthermore, we say that a forest F of
G is k-feasible iff degF(v) ≤k for all v ∈V , i.e., every node is either saturated or slack w.r.t. F.
Throughout this paper, k will be fixed by the algorithm, and hence we will usually omit k from the
terminology. We want to compute a feasible spanning tree of G.

an extra O(log ∆⋆) factor in the total runtime (see Appendix A for details).

Basic Building Blocks
In this section, we present an overview of two key objects that underpin our algorithm: a config-
uration (see Definition 4.1) and an augmenting chain (see Definition 4.3).
Definition 4.1. A configuration of the input graph G is a triple (F, B, C), such that:
1. F is a feasible forest of G.
2. B ⊆V is a subset of nodes. We refer to the nodes in B as junctions.
3. C is a subset of the collection of all (F, B)-regions, satisfying the property that every C ∈C
is reducible. We refer to the (F, B)-regions in C as untouched, and the remaining (F, B)-
regions as touched.
For every node v ∈V that belongs to a touched (resp. untouched)
(F, B)-region, we refer to v itself as touched (resp. untouched).

Running Time Analysis
Almost all parts of the algorithm are quite easy to implement with elementary data structures.
There are a few operations where we need to deal with the unique path between two given nodes in
a dynamic forest (after applying an augmenting chain the forest will change). For those operations,
we use the Top-Tree data structure [AHdLT05]. Equipped with this data structure, it is fairly
straightforward to implement all parts of every round of the algorithm in ˜O(m) time, except the
calls to the find-chain subroutine.
Time Spent on Find-Chain. To analyze the running time of the find-chain subroutine and
recursive calls to backward-search, we charge the time of every failed backward-search to
the increased value in the level of the object on which it is called. Since the levels of the nodes
and edges are bounded by H throughout the entire algorithm, the total time spent on all calls to
find-chain is bounded by ˜O(m · H) as well. We refer the reader to the full version (Part II) for a
comprehensive implementation and running time analysis.

Description of Backward-Search on a Node
The main purpose of the subroutine backward-search on a junction x is to find a partial sequence
(w0, z1, . . . , wr, zr+1) such that zr+1 = x and this sequence is potentially a subsequence of an
augmenting chain.
Description. We iterate over all edges (y, x) incident on x satisfying the following conditions;
• (y, x) ∈E(G) −E(F) (this is directly aligned with Property 7 of an augmenting chain).
• y is contained in a reducible region Cy ∈C (this is directly aligned with Property 3 of an
augmenting chain).
• L(y,x) ≤Lx −1 (this condition is for two purposes: first, having a fast running time, and
second, achieving Property 6 of an augmenting chain).
We refer to such an edge as a backward edge. If there exists at least one backward edge, we
find the backward edge (y⋆, x) with the minimum possible level and make a recursive call to the
backward-search(y⋆, x). There are two cases for this call;
• backward-search(y⋆, x) successfully returns a sequence (w0, z1, . . . , wr−1, zr, y⋆, x). In this
case, we say that backward-search on x is successful, and return this sequence as the
output.
• backward-search(y⋆, x) fails. In this case, we consider the next backward edge (y⋆
new, x).
As long as there exists at least one backward edge, we repeatedly call backward-search on such
an edge with minimum level. Every time that backward-search on an edge (y, x) fails, it will
increase the level of (y, x). As a result, this process will finish, and the algorithm eventually halts.
If at least one of these recursive calls is successful, we say that this backward-search on x is
successful as well.
If we end up in a situation where there is no more backward edge, we say
that backward-search on x has failed. Then, we increment the level of x, and terminate the
backward-search. Algorithm 4 summarizes this procedure.

Getting Rid of the Prior Knowledge of ∆⋆
In this section, we show how to run our algorithm without prior knowledge of ∆⋆. The idea is
simple: binary search. We consider a variable b ∈[1, n], to do the binary search.
A.1
Multiplicative Approximation.
The desired maximum degree of the spanning tree is k = ⌈(1 + ε) · ∆⋆⌉+ 1. It is straightforward
to see that in our algorithm and its analysis, we never use the fact that k is exactly equal to this
value. In other words, as long as k ≥⌈(1 + ε) · ∆⋆⌉+ 1, since there exists an spanning tree of
maximum degree bounded by (k −2)/(1 + ε), the analysis of our algorithm can be synchronized
with the spanning tree of bounded degree (k −2)/(1 + ε) instead of the actual optimal spanning
tree of maximum degree ∆⋆.
The Algorithm. Hence, we can do the following. We run our algorithm for finding a spanning
tree of bounded degree k = b. Moreover, we consider a timer that stops after ˜O(m/ε2) time steps

forest one by one, as described in Section B.1. This leads to their result.
B.2.1
Barriers of [BFW26] Algorithm
When the number of connected components of the forest becomes small, the [BFW26] algorithm
does not have any advantage over [FR92]. For instance, when f = O(√n), the size of the molecules
considered by the algorithm becomes too large, Ω(n/f) = Ω(√n). Hence, the procedure of [BFW26]
can destroy a molecule consisting of a large number of nodes, and the algorithm finds only one
augmenting chain. This issue remains even if we relax the approximation ratio to multiplicative
instead of additive. In the next section (B.3), we provide a precise instance that shows the [BFW26]
algorithm needs at least Ω(mn1/4) time to build a spanning tree of maximum degree 2∆⋆. This is
not necessarily the hardest instance for the algorithm, but it shows that the running time of their
algorithm is far from optimal even for O(∆⋆) approximation.

B.4
Our Techniques
Recall the degree reduction subroutine (Lemma B.1) as described in Section B.1. If the degree
reduction subroutine is applied on a node u w.r.t. region C, we refer to C as a touched region. For
a touched region C, the algorithm can no longer guarantee the degree reduction subroutine for any
v ∈C. Now, recall Steps I and II of the [FR92] algorithm in Section B.1. In the following, we
highlight the main ideas to improve [FR92] and [BFW26].
B.4.1
Potential Improvements Over the Main Steps of [FR92]
Discovering Step. In the discovering step, an edge (x, y) between two regions can provide a
potential improvement for the algorithm (by growing regions in Step II and discovering more
reducible nodes). A simple observation is that, for a touched region C, any node v ∈C whose
degree is already at most k −1 does not need any degree reduction. Hence, we consider a larger
set of potential edges in the discovering step. We refer to these edges as effective edges (formally
defined in Definition 9.6). A similar idea has been exploited in [BFW26] algorithm as well.

B.4.2
Augmenting Chains
We consider the same name ‘augmenting chain’ as in [BFW26] since our notion of an augmenting
chain is a non-trivial extension of the same object in [BFW26]. A high-level intuition of this object
is provided below. Assume that (x, y) is an effective edge. There are two cases for (x, y);
Case I: x and y are in different connected components of F.
We can just apply the degree
reduction subroutine on x and y separately, and then add (x, y) to F in order to reduce the number
of components of the forest. In this case, we call (x, y) an augmenting chain of length 1.

In essence, an augmenting chain is a sequence of non-forest edges (/∈E(F)) derived from the

Moreover, the above process that updates the forest according to an augmenting chain is called

Comparison to [BFW26]. The augmenting chains introduced in [BFW26] depend on the forest
decomposition in their algorithm. As explained in Section B.2.1, this decomposition of the for-
est introduces a hard barrier towards achieving fast algorithms (see Appendix B.3 for a concrete
example), which means that we need a stronger object independent of such decomposition. For
this reason, our definition of an augmenting chain described above is a non-trivial refinement of
[BFW26] to avoid creating cycles in the forest.
B.4.3
Incorporating Augmenting Chains In the Algorithm
Using our terminology, the [FR92] algorithm as described in Section B.1, simply keeps applying
the merging steps until an augmenting chain of length 1 appears. Thus, regions are often too large
when they become touched. Instead, our algorithm will prioritize finding longer augmenting chains
over merging. This encourages the algorithm not to merge regions too aggressively. On the other
hand, an augmenting chain of length h, touches Θ(h) many regions (see the intuitive description in
Section B.4.2). Hence, we need to deliberately choose the length h of the augmenting chains that
we apply since 1) if h is large, the number of touched regions becomes large, and 2) if h is small,
the size of the touched regions might be large. Both cases lead to little progress in the algorithm.
To address this, we run our algorithm in rounds, where the goal of round t is to find a large set of
augmenting chains, each of length ≤t.
Final Length of Augmenting Chains. Eventually, to achieve multiplicative (1+ε)-approximate
MDST, it is sufficient to run H = Θ(log1+ε n) many rounds of our algorithm to reduce the number
of components of the forest by Ω(f/H). To achieve a plus one additive approximate MDST, it is
sufficient to run H = Θ(n/f) rounds of our algorithm, to reduce the number of components of the
forest by Ω(f/H). These are aligned with the guarantees of Theorem 3.1 as desired.
B.4.4
Finding Augmenting Chains
Augmenting chains are complicated objects, where applying an augmenting chain can destroy or
create new augmenting chains since the forest is changing in a non-trivial way. As a result, searching
for augmenting chains is a non-trivial task due to the limited running time of the algorithm.
Levels of the Edges. We maintain an integer for each edge of the graph called the level of the
edge. If the level of an effective edge (u, v) is r, it intuitively means that the length of the minimum
augmenting chain whose last edge is (u, v) should be at least r.
Searching for Augmenting Chains. We provide a deliberate subroutine find-chain(h, (u, v))
that receives an integer h and an effective edge (u, v) and tries to find an augmenting chain of
length at most h. This subroutine searches for a sequence of effective edges whose levels are strictly
increasing, and upon any failure, it increases the levels of the corresponding edges.
In round t of the algorithm, we bound the level of the edges by t to make sure that find-chain
only finds augmenting chains of length ≤t. Moreover, the total time spent for finding augmenting
chains is proportional to the summation of the levels of all edges, which is O(m · H), aligned with
the guarantee of Theorem 3.1.
