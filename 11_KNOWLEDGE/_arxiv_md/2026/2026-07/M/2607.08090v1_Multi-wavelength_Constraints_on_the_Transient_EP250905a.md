ABSTRACT
Context. Fast X-ray transients (FXTs) are a diverse class of high-energy suggested origins, ranging from stellar explosions to compact object
mergers. The Einstein Probe (EP) satellite discovers approximately 100 FXTs per year.
Aims. We aim to constrain the physical origin of EP250905a.
Methods. We analyze X-ray, optical, near-infrared (NIR), and radio temporal and spectral properties of EP250905a. In addition, we assess the
possible role of weak gravitational lensing in shaping its observed characteristics.
Results. EP250905a fades rapidly in X-rays, and we detect no NIR or radio emission, but we detect early optical emission that rapidly fades
beyond the detection limits. Two nearby galaxies are identified for which we derive spectroscopic redshifts of z = 0.374 (G1) and z = 2.714 (G2).
Our analysis favors G2 as the host of the FXT EP250905a. The angular separation of 2.56′′ between the FXT’s optical counterpart and the center
of the G1 galaxy suggests the emission of the FXT might be moderately magnified by lensing effects (µ ≈3.9) given the inferred Einstein radius
of G1 (θE ≈1.9′′).
Conclusions. The data are best explained as an afterglow from a mildly off-axis structured jet at z = 2.714, providing a consistent broadband
interpretation that also allows for weakly lensed emission of EP250905a.

1. Introduction
Extragalactic fast X-ray transients (FXTs) are defined as bursts
of soft X-ray photons (in the ∼0.3–10 keV band) with durations
from seconds to hours (Heise & in’t Zand 2010). Over the last
two decades attempts have been made to systematically iden-
tify and characterize sources detected by the new generation
of X-ray satellites such as the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory
(Swift; e.g., Soderberg et al. 2008; Evans et al. 2023), the Chan-
dra X-ray Observatory (e.g., Jonker et al. 2013; Glennie et al.
2015; Irwin et al. 2016; Bauer et al. 2017; Xue et al. 2019;
Lin et al. 2022; Quirola-Vásquez et al. 2022, 2023), and the X-
ray Multi-mirror Mission - Newton telescope (XMM-Newton;
e.g., Novara et al. 2020; Alp & Larsson 2020; De Luca et al.
2021). The high spatial resolution of these observations has ro-
bustly established the population of extragalactic X-ray tran-
sients, in addition to X-ray outbursts from Galactic sys-
tems, i.e., strengthening their extragalactic nature and even
enabling the localization of the host galaxies of the tran-
sients (e.g., Lin et al. 2022; Eappachen et al. 2022, 2023, 2024;
Quirola-Vásquez et al. 2025).
The launch of the Einstein Probe (EP; Yuan et al. 2015;
Yuan et al. 2022, 2025) satellite on January 9, 2024, is enabling
a transformation of the field, discovering ≈220 new extragalac-
tic FXTs in the first two years of operations. EP has two instru-
ments onboard. The first instrument is the Wide-field X-ray Tele-
scope (EP-WXT), an imaging survey instrument operating in the
soft X-ray band (0.5–4 keV) with a field of view (FoV) of 3600
square degrees. It is ideally suited for the arcminute localization
and rapid reporting of FXTs. The second instrument onboard
is the EP Follow-up X-ray Telescope (EP-FXT). EP-FXT often
provides an X-ray position accurate to a few tens of arcseconds
on timescales of orders of an hour or even less when the EP-
WXT triggers an automatic slew to the EP-FXT instrument. The
rapid follow-up enabled by EP has led to the detection of the
optical, near-infrared (NIR), and radio counterpart to a subsam-
ple of tens of FXTs (e.g., EP240315a, EP240414a, EP240806a,
EP250108a, and EP250304a; Srivastav et al. 2024; Aryan et al.
2024; Li et al. 2024; Gillanders et al. 2024; Eyles-Ferris 2025;
Liu et al. 2025; Bright et al. 2025).
A variety of progenitor scenarios have been proposed to ex-
plain the X-ray light curves and spectra of extragalactic FXTs,
as well as the expected multi-wavelength emission of their coun-
terparts. These include:
– Shock breakout (SBO) associated with certain types
of core-collapse supernovae (CC-SNe), which can pro-
duce an FXT when the shock wave generated during
the explosion reaches the surface of the progenitor star
(e.g., SN2008D/XRF 080109 and in the last months
EP260321a; Soderberg et al. 2008; Waxman & Katz 2017;
Huang et al. 2026; Rastinejad et al. 2026; O’Connor et al.
2026; Wen et al. 2026; Chen et al. 2026; Yuan et al. 2026;
Martin-Carrillo et al. 2026);
– Models involving binary neutron star (BNS) mergers
that lead to the formation of a rapidly rotating, highly
magnetized neutron star (magnetar), capable of power-
ing a nearly isotropic X-ray signal observable as an
FXT (e.g., XRT 141001, XRT 150322, and EP 250207b;
Dai et al. 2006; Metzger et al. 2008; Yu et al. 2013; Zhang

The X-ray transient EP250905a was detected by the EP-
WXT instrument on 2025 September 5, at coordinates RAJ2000 =
4.441◦, DecJ2000 = 37.493◦, with a positional radius uncertainty
of 3′ at a 90% confidence level (Cheng et al. 2025). Subsequent
observations with EP-FXT provided an improved localization
with an uncertainty of ∼10′′ (Cheng et al. 2025). Following the
detection, multi-wavelength follow-up observations were con-
ducted to search for an optical/NIR counterpart (He et al. 2025;
Lipunov et al. 2025; Hall et al. 2025; Eyles-Ferris & Habeeb
2025; Bochenek & Perley 2025). Thanks to the rapid response
the ALT100C telescope reported a candidate optical counter-
part consistent with the EP-FXT localization (He et al. 2025).
However, this candidate has not been confirmed by contem-
poraneous observations (Lipunov et al. 2025; Hall et al. 2025;
Eyles-Ferris & Habeeb 2025; Bochenek & Perley 2025). In-
deed, a reanalysis found that the reported candidate was spuri-
ous.
In this paper, we report on our multi-wavelength dataset
of the FXT EP250905a. We present extensive imaging and
spectroscopic observations spanning from ∼1 to 150 days af-
ter the EP-WXT trigger. In Sect. 2, we describe the multi-
wavelength observations used in this work. In Sect. 3, we
present our main results. Sect. 4 provides an interpretation of
the EP250905a emission, and in Sect. 5 we summarize and con-
clude our work. Throughout this paper, magnitudes are reported
in the AB system, and we adopt a ΛCDM cosmology with H0 =
67.66 km s−1 Mpc−1 and ΩΛ = 0.69 (Planck Collaboration et al.
2020).

2.2. Swift-XRT
The Swift satellite (Gehrels et al. 2004) observed the location
of EP250905a starting on September 5 at 12:58:37 UTC (i.e.,
∼1.86 hours after T0) using the X-ray Telescope (XRT), for a to-
tal exposure time of ∼1.2 ks. Using the online data products gen-
erator provided by the University of Leicester (Evans et al. 2007,
2009)3, EP250905a was not detected. We estimate a 3σ count-
rate upper limit of ≈0.02 cts s−1. Assuming a Galactic hydrogen
column density of NH = 7×1020 cm−2 and a power-law spectrum
with photon index ΓX = 2.3, and using PIMMS v4.15, we derive
a corresponding flux upper limit of ≲7.9 × 10−13 erg cm−2 s−1
in the 0.5–10 keV band.

2.3. GTC/OSIRIS+ observations
2.3.1. Imaging
We obtained four imaging epochs of the field of EP250905a
using the upgraded Optical System for Imaging and low-
intermediate-Resolution Integrated Spectroscopy (OSIRIS+) in-
strument (Cepa et al. 2000) mounted on the Gran Telescopio Ca-
narias (GTC). Our observations were taken using the i-band fil-
ter. The first observation (E1) of the field started on September
6 at 02:01:39 UTC (i.e., ∆t ≈0.62 days, after the X-ray trig-
ger, and with a seeing of ∼0.81′′ FWHM) using an exposure
time of 9 × 60 sec. The second (E2) and third (E3) observations
were taken on September 25 and 26 at 03:40:03 and 22:13:34
UTC, respectively (i.e., ∆t ≈19.7 and 21.5 days after T0, and
with a seeing of ∼1.1 and 0.84′′ FWHM, respectively), and
with an exposure time of 9 × 60 and 19 × 150 sec, respectively.
Finally, a last epoch (E4) was taken on February 4 at 20:35:03
UTC (i.e., ∼152.4 days after T0, and with an optical seeing of
∼0.78′′ FWHM) with an exposure time of 9 × 60 sec. The data
were bias subtracted and flat-field corrected, adopting standard
PyRAF tasks (Science Software Branch at STScI 2012), and cos-
mic rays were removed using the LACosmic task (van Dokkum
2001). The world coordinate system information is implemented
using astrometry.net codes (Lang et al. 2010). Fig. 3, top
panels, depict the individual OSIRIS+ i-band images centered
on the EP-FXT position of EP250905a.
2.3.2. Spectroscopy
We obtained three spectroscopic observations of the host galaxy
candidates of the transient EP250905a (labeled as G1 and G2;
see Sect. 3.2), using the OSIRIS+ instrument (Cepa et al. 2000).
The first epoch, designed to get the spectrum of G1, was ob-
tained on September 25 at 04:18:56 UTC (i.e., ∆t ≈19.72 days
after T0) using an exposure time of 3×1200 sec, grism R1000R
(covering ≈5000-9500 Å), and a 1′′ wide slit placed at the paral-
lactic angle. The second observation was obtained on September
26 at 02:42:46 UTC (i.e., ∆t ≈20.65 after the X-ray trigger),
using an exposure time of 3×1200 sec, grism R1000R, and a
1′′ wide slit oriented to cover G1 and G2 simultaneously. Fi-
nally, a last GTC epoch was taken on October 1 at 01:44:18 UTC
(i.e., ∆t ≈25.61 days after T0), with a setting similar to the sec-
ond epoch, using the grism R1000B (covering ≈4000-8000 Å)
instead of the R1000R grism.
2.4. Nordic Optical Telescope (NOT)
We observed the target using the Andalucía Faint Object Spec-
trograph and Camera (ALFOSC) and the Nordic Optical Tele-
scope near-infrared camera and spectrograph (NOTCam), both
mounted on the NOT at the Roque de los Muchachos Observa-
tory (La Palma, Canary Islands, Spain). We observed the tran-
sient position using NOTCam at five epochs between September
5 and September 8 (i.e., ∆t ≈0.47, 0.52, 0.75, 1.73, and 2.59 days
after T0) using the J-band filter (program NOT 70-301 and 71-
812, PI: Jonker and Fynbo/Xu, respectively). Meanwhile, AL-
FOSC observed the field using the g and r bands on October
1 at 01:54:46.8 and 02:11:20.5 UTC (i.e., ∆t ≈25.6 days af-
ter the trigger; program NOT 71-805, PI: Jonker). All observa-
tions were carried out using a standard dithering pattern. The
data were reduced following standard procedures using the iraf
package (Tody 1986).
2.5. Las Cumbres Observatory (LCO)
EP250905a was observed with a fully robotic 1-m telescope
at the Teide Observatory located in the Canary Islands-Spain
on September 5 at 22:34:45.9 UTC (i.e., ∆t ≈0.49 days after
T0) using the r filter. The data were reduced using the BANZAI
pipeline (McCully et al. 2018) and standard procedures with
IRAF (Tody 1986).

3. Results
3.1. Optical and NIR counterpart
We performed image subtraction using the High Order Trans-
form of PSF and Template Subtraction code (HOTPANTS;
Becker 2015), adopting the OSIRIS+ E4 epoch as the host tem-
plate. We effectively, therefore, assume that no emission from
the transient was detected in that observation. The E4 image
is subtracted from the images of the E1, E2, and E3 epochs.
The resulting subtractions are shown in the bottom panels of
Fig. 3. The subtraction E1−E4 reveals a significant residual
within the EP-FXT error region, with a signal-to-noise ratio
of ∼5.9. The source is located at RAopt
J2000 = 00h17m48s.00,
Decopt
J2000 = +37◦28′19′′.6, with an astrometric uncertainty of
≈0′′.1, estimated using SExtractor (Bertin & Arnouts 1996).
The transient has an AB magnitude of mi = 23.23 ± 0.18, cali-
brated against Pan-STARRS.
We independently confirm this detection using the PyZOGY
package (Zackay et al. 2016), adopting an archival i-band im-
age from the Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope (CFHT) as the
reference template. Given the spatial and temporal coincidence
between this optical transient source and the X-ray detections of
EP250905a, we identify this target as the optical counterpart of
EP250905a. No significant residual is detected in the E2−E4 and
E3−E4 images (see Fig. 3, bottom panels). We derive 3σ upper
limits of mi > 23.5 and mi > 25.3 AB mag for these epochs,
respectively, confirming the fading of the counterpart discovered
in the E1−E4 image.
We applied the same procedure and template to the i-band
observations obtained with the ALT100C telescope. However, no
counterpart was detected in these observations. In the NIR, we
followed a similar procedure to search for a counterpart in the J-
band images obtained with the Nordic Optical Telescope, using
HOTPANTS for image subtraction. The final epoch, taken with
NOTCam (i.e., ≈2.6 days after T0), was used as the template.
No counterpart was detected in the residual images.
Spectroscopic observations obtained ∼20.7 days after T0
cover the position of the optical counterpart (see Fig. 4, left pan-
els), but no spectral features associated with the transient are de-
tected (see Sect. 3.2), consistent with the E2−E4 non-detection.

3.3. Optical/NIR light curves
Based on the redshifts of the candidate host galaxies, we derive
the absolute optical magnitude of the counterpart. Using obser-
vations obtained at ≈0.62 days after T0, we find Mi ≈−17.8

optical and NIR observations; however, we place deep upper lim-
its, particularly in the i-band (see Table F.1).
3.4. X-ray light curve
The EP-WXT light curve (see Fig.1) has a duration of ≈200 sec,
and does not show a single-pulse profile, but rather a multi-
peaked, slowly evolving one. In Figure 5 we show the 0.3–
10 keV X-ray light curve of EP250905a in the observer frame.
The first data point (black star) corresponds to the mean EP-
WXT flux (at the mid time of ≈100 s), followed by the EP-FXT
epoch 1 detection and Swift-XRT upper limits. There appears to
be a steep (∼3 orders of magnitude) decay in X-ray flux between
both the individually binned and mean EP-WXT flux points and
the flux at the start of the first EP-FXT observation. This drop
can be described by a power law with index ≈−3.8, which
we consider as an upper limit, since it could be even steeper.
Considering only the EP-FXT epoch 1 and epoch 2 observations
(δt≈10−2–100 days), the power law decay index is significantly
less steep, with an index of = −0.33 ± 0.06, indicating a sharp
break in the temporal behavior. The two power-laws are shown
in Fig. 5 by the cyan and green dashed lines. The 3σ upper limits
for epochs 3 and 4 remain marginally consistent with an extrapo-
lation of this power-law slope, although the strength of the epoch
4 limit hints at a possible late-time steepening decline.
3.5. Spectral Energy Distribution (SED)
We investigate the optical-to-X-ray SED of EP250905a dur-
ing our optical detection and the EP-FXT detection (mod-
ules A and B) closest in time (the time between both obser-
vations is ∆t ≈8.9 hours). First, we correct the photome-
try for line-of-sight Galactic extinction, adopting E(B −V) =
0.052 mag (Schlafly & Finkbeiner 2011) and the extinction law
of Calzetti et al. (2000). For the i-band filter, this corresponds
to a Galactic extinction of Aλ = 0.092 mag. Assuming a single
power law to the optical-to-X-ray data, we compute a broadband
spectral index of βOX = −0.80 ± 0.03, where Fν ∝νβ.
4. Discussion and interpretation
4.1. Host galaxy association and redshift assumption
Despite being the fainter of the two galaxies, the closer proxim-
ity of G2 to the optical counterpart position makes it less likely
than G1 to be a chance alignment. Moreover, the derived chance
alignment probabilities (see Sect. 3.2), in which the Pch of G2
is approximately one order of magnitude lower than that of G1,
further support G2 as the most likely host galaxy of the tran-
sient EP250905a. Therefore, throughout this work, we adopt the
transient redshift as zG2 = 2.714.

4.2. Counterpart interpretation
We first discuss the energetics of the event, taking into account
the upper limit in the γ-ray band we derived in Sect. 2.11. The
k-corrected isotropic-equivalent peak luminosity and total en-
ergy are ≲5.4 × 1048 erg s−1 and ≲8.9 × 1050 erg, respec-
tively. Comparing both parameters with a sample of GRBs (e.g.,
Tsvetkova et al. 2017), the isotropic-equivalent peak luminos-
ity is consistent with the GRB sample, although it lies in the
low-luminosity tail of the distribution. The high-energy SED of
the transient (see Fig. C.1) depicts the Fermi-GBM upper limits,
which are not very constraining. Indeed, they lie ∼1 dex above
an extrapolation of the best-fit photon index ΓWXT = 1.7 ± 0.4 to
the EP-WXT spectrum, and hence do not permit a clear identifi-
cation of any peak energy between ∼5–1000 keV, i.e., the source
might in principle have been significantly brighter in hard X-
rays/gamma-rays than in soft X-rays. This leaves considerable
uncertainty in EP250905a’s location on the so-called Amati re-
lation.
In the optical regime, Fig. 6 shows the i-band optical light
curve of EP250905a compared with several examples drawn
from the GRB, SN, jetted TDE, FXT, and LFBOT transient
classes. EP250905a occupies a region populated by multiple
classes of transients. At this redshift, the late-time upper lim-
its do not rule out the presence of an SN bump, for instance.
In a GRB context, at zG2 = 2.714, EP250905a falls within the
lower luminosity range of long GRBs and remains compatible
with their late-time evolution (see Fig. 6).

X-ray evolution resembles the canonical GRB X-ray afterglow
emission (see Fig. 5). In the context of GRB X-ray afterglows, an
early steep decay phase is commonly observed (Tagliaferri et al.
2005). This temporal decay slope is steep, typically with a

Comparing the single optical detection with the X-ray extrap-

The optical-to-X-ray SED at ≈1 day is well described by a
single power law with βOX ≈−0.8 (see Fig. 9, dashed line).
Given this βOX the source is not classified as an optically dark
burst (defined as βOX < 0.5 under Fν ∝ν−β; Jakobsson et al.
2004). This value is consistent with those typically observed in
GRB afterglows (e.g., Melandri et al. 2008), and likely implies
a non-thermal nature of the emission. The measured βOX during
the early epoch can be compared with predictions of canonical
forward-shock (FS) models (e.g., Sari et al. 1999; Granot & Sari
2002; Zhang et al. 2006; Gao et al. 2013). Here, we assume that
at ≈1 day after T0 the transient is in the decelerating relativistic
phase under the slow-cooling regime (see for instance Gao et al.
2013). In the spectral regime νm < ν < νc, where νm and νc are
the characteristic synchrotron and cooling break frequencies, re-
spectively, the spectral index follows β = (1 −p)/2 (where p
is the electron energy distribution index; Gao et al. 2013). Using
β = βOX ≈−0.80, we infer p ≈2.6, which lies within the typical
range observed in GRB afterglows (e.g., Panaitescu & Kumar
2002; Curran et al. 2010). This supports a synchrotron origin
consistent with a relativistic forward shock.
We note that the interpretation depends critically on the as-
sumed redshift; therefore, we also briefly discuss the scenario
in which the transient is located at zG1 = 0.374. At zG1, the
early optical detection (Mi ≈−17.8 AB mag), together with the
late-time upper limits, excludes the presence of an SN compo-
nent, as well as jetted TDEs and luminous LFBOTs. Further-
more, the radio upper limits rule out the presence of a power-
ful relativistic outflow, while the γ-ray upper limits are inconsis-
tent with typical GRB emission. Overall, under the zG1 redshift,
the multi-wavelength properties of EP250905a are not consistent
with the majority of known transient classes. In this scenario,
the low-redshift solution would require an unusually faint opti-
cal, X-ray, and radio counterpart. This disfavors the association
of EP250905a with galaxy G1. This conclusion is further sup-
ported by the ∼1 dex lower chance coincidence probability of
G2 relative to G1. (see Sect. 3.2). Thus, although the redshift am-
biguity prevents a definitive classification, the multi-wavelength
evidence strongly favors interpreting EP250905a as an afterglow
emission, with the zG2 = 2.714 scenario providing the most self-
consistent explanation of the observed properties.
To test the hypothesis of the afterglow nature of EP250905a
at redshift zG2 = 2.714, we modeled the multi-wavelength data
using redback (Sarin et al. 2024). We compared the radio, op-
tical, NIR, and X-ray data of EP250905a with those estimated

4.3. Gravitational Lensing posibility
The gravitational lensing effect in transients has been ro-
bustly observed in SNe (Rodney et al. 2021; Paynter et al. 2021;

Magee et al. 2023), while for GRBs, most cases remain lens-
ing candidates (Liao et al. 2022; Levan et al. 2025a). The con-
figuration of EP250905a is consistent with gravitational lens-
ing, as it lies in close angular proximity to a massive fore-
ground galaxy G1, providing the necessary potential to de-
flect and magnify background light. Its high inferred luminos-
ity, together with this alignment, might naturally be explained
by lensing-induced magnification rather than intrinsic bright-
ness alone. To explore this possibility, we adopt a singular
isothermal sphere (SIS) mass profile5, which predicts an Ein-
stein radius of θE = 4π (σ/c)2 (Dds/Ds) (Schneider et al. 1992;
Narayan & Bartelmann 1996), where σ is the velocity disper-
sion of the lens, Dds and Ds are the angular diameter distances
between the lens and source, and to the source, respectively, and
c is the speed of light. To estimate the velocity dispersion of
galaxy G1, we fit its GTC/OSIRIS+ spectrum using the package
BAGPIPES (Carnall et al. 2018, 2019) assuming an exponentially
declining star formation history, obtaining a posterior value of
σ = 300 ± 50 km s−1. To derive the Einstein radius and the mag-
nification we implement a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)
method, assuming uniform priors for σ and angular offset of
U∼[250; 350] km s−1 and U∼[2.2; 3.0] arcsec, respectively. We
derive an Einstein radius of θE = 1.9′′ ± 0.4′′ (at 68% confi-
dence level). Given the observed angular separation of ≈2.6′′,
the source lies outside the Einstein radius, corresponding to the
single-image regime of the SIS model, and indicating moderate
amplification without the formation of multiple images. In this
configuration, the expected magnification is µ = 3.9+5.7
−1.4 (at 68%
confidence level), showing a large uncertainty. Assuming the
lower magnification limit of µ ≈2.5, the intrinsic optical coun-
terpart of EP250905a would have a magnitude of mi ≈24.2 mag
or fainter. Therefore, although EP250905a may be a weak grav-
itational lensing source, the current data do not allow us to ro-
bustly establish this interpretation.
5. Conclusions
We have presented a comprehensive multi-wavelength analysis
of the fast X-ray transient EP250905a, combining X-ray, opti-
cal, NIR, and radio observations obtained from hours to months
after the trigger. The optical counterpart of EP250905a is identi-
fied in early imaging obtained with GTC/OSIRIS+ in the i-band
with mi = 23.23±0.18 AB mag. However, it remains undetected
in subsequent observations across optical, NIR, and radio wave-
lengths. The redshift of EP250905a was not directly measured;
instead, two galaxies (G1 and G2) located close to the transient
position were identified as potential host candidates. Their red-
shifts were determined through the identification of spectral ab-
sorption (zG1 = 0.374) and emission (zG2 = 2.714) lines. Based
on the lower chance-alignment probability, we find that the G2
galaxy is the most likely host galaxy, implying a fiducial redshift
of z = 2.714 for EP250905a.
The observational properties of the transient are best ex-
plained by an afterglow scenario, with modeling favoring a
mildly off-axis structured jet at redshift zG2 = 2.714, which pro-
vides a self-consistent interpretation of the broadband data. Al-
ternative scenarios at low redshift (zG1 = 0.374) are disfavored,
as they require unusually faint emission across multiple wave-
lengths and are less consistent with the observed constraints.
The close angular proximity of EP250905a to the foreground
galaxy G1 makes it an intriguing candidate for gravitational lens-

Einstein radius of θE≈1′′.9, placing the source outside the strong-

lensing nature of the transient.
Overall, EP250905a represents a compelling moderate-
redshift transient, potentially offering insight into both relativis-
tic jet physics and the role of gravitational lensing in shaping
the observed properties of distant explosive events. Future early
imaging and spectroscopic observations of new transients will
be crucial to constrain the lensing configuration and definitively
establish the nature of similar sources.
