Measurement Speed Limits for Quantum State Purification
Jiaxin Liu,1, 2, 3 Zuoxian Wang,1, 2 and Danyue Ma1, 2, 3
1School of Instrumentation and Optoelectronic Engineering, Beihang University, Haidian, Beijing 100191, China
2Institute of Large-Scale Scientific Facility, Beihang University, Beijing 100191, China
3Hangzhou Institute of Extremely-Weak Magnetic Field Major National Science and Technology Infrastructure, Hangzhou 310051, China
(Dated: July 13, 2026)
We derive three matched speed limits on the purification of a monitored system, valid in every finite dimension
and for every strategy and efficiency. All three are set by one functional, the square root of the state’s impurity,
whose maximal decay rate is a state-independent constant. For a qubit this reduces to the square root of the
determinant, whose protocol-independent decay at unit efficiency is a sharp null test. The three rate constants
stand in ratio two to four to eight, and attainment freezes at that square root, the half-moment. Numerics confirm
the bounds; for the qubit, a conservation law settles the local-versus-global optimality puzzle, and doubling
feedback spends exactly twice the minimum.
Measurement-based feedback can speed up the purification
of a monitored quantum system, a discovery that opened a
two-decade line of work on rapid purification[1–7]. Continu-
ous monitoring has since grown from a foundational tool into
a working resource on two experimental fronts. In many-body
systems it acts as a tunable probe whose competition with
entangling dynamics drives measurement-induced entangle-
ment phase transitions[8–11], distilling a weakly entangled,
rapidly purifying phase out of a volume-law-entangled one.
In the single-system laboratory it has become a routine diag-
nostic, tracking and steering the state of a superconducting
qubit, resolving individual quantum trajectories in real time
via the dispersive continuous readout[12–14]. In both arenas
the speed of purification is a shared concern, since rapid pu-
rification underlies the fast initialization and stabilization of
quantum information.
Jacobs showed that unitary feedback holding the state un-
biased with respect to the measured basis doubles the mean
purification rate of a qubit[1]; Combes and Jacobs extended
the rate speed-up to higher dimensions[2]; Wiseman and Ralph
found that the advantage reverses for the mean time to reach
a target purity, where no feedback is faster[3]; Wiseman and
Bouten proved both optimality statements at unit efficiency
by dynamic programming[4].
Scanning R´enyi-entropy ob-
jectives, Teo, Combes and Wiseman then exposed a puzzling
disconnect between locally and globally optimal feedback[7].
These results certify specific protocols within specific classes,
mostly at perfect detection.
What has been missing is the
other side of the problem: protocol-independent limits, valid
at every detection efficiency, that say how fast any strategy can
purify and which protocols exhaust them.

sion and show that each is exactly attained. For a system of

spectral half-spread ℓ, all under arbitrary record-dependent
