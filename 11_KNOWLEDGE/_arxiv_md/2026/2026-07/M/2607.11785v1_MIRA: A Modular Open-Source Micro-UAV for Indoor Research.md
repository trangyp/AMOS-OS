MIRA: A Modular Open-Source Micro-UAV for Indoor
Research

ABSTRACT
Indoor robotics research increasingly relies on
micro-UAVs whose airframe, electronics, and
control software are fully open to modifica-
tion. Off-the-shelf platforms rarely expose the
low-level access required for such modifications,
while building a custom alternative typically re-
quires substantial engineering effort before flight
testing can begin, leaving many laboratories to
work within constraints that limit the scope of
their research. We present MIRA (Modular In-
door Research Architecture)1, a low-cost, open-
source micro-UAV for indoor research built
around a replicable 3D-printed PLA airframe
and a containerized low-level software package
managing the companion-to-autopilot communi-
cation bridge via Micro XRCE-DDS. Designed
as a white-box architecture, core subsystems are
individually replaceable without firmware refac-
toring, supporting local fabrication and com-
ponent substitution from existing lab inven-
tory.
We characterize MIRA through manual
flight in position-control mode within an opti-
cal motion-capture volume, where the communi-
cation pipeline sustains a median companion-to-
autopilot latency of 0.02 ms and power spectral
density analysis confirms the structural vibration
energy stays concentrated in a narrow 90 to 110
Hz band, isolated from the sub-20 Hz control
bandwidth and within the autopilot safety thresh-
olds.

INTRODUCTION
Indoor aerial robotics research faces a persistent infras-
tructure bottleneck. Affordable commercial drones feature
closed ecosystems and payload constraints that restrict cus-
tom hardware integration, while open academic platforms of-
ten mandate rigid hardware configurations that escalate repli-
cation costs and limit component substitution from existing

lab inventory. This collective engineering and financial over-
head scales poorly with fleet size, creating a barrier to entry
for laboratories requiring replicable indoor testing baselines.
Although open-source flight stacks and ROS 2 middle-
ware have lowered software barriers, the hardware modular-
ity gap persists [1, 2]. The Crazyflie 2.1 is a reference swarm
vehicle, but its 27 g mass limits onboard processing and pay-
load [3, 4]. Conversely, larger academic platforms like Ag-
ilicious treat sensing, control, and compute as a jointly op-

platforms such as OmniNxt [6] and AgiPIX [7] remain bound
to specific, co-developed hardware configurations or costly
active sensing suites. A clear gap remains for a transparent,
standard-component baseline that can be fabricated, repaired,
and provisioned rapidly using common workshop tools.
This paper introduces a system, shown in Figure 1, that
addresses this practical bottleneck by focusing entirely on
providing accessible physical and digital infrastructure rather
than optimizing for extreme agility or specific perception
suites. The platform is simultaneously (i) optimized for in-
door, GPS-denied environments, (ii) easily replicable on stan-
dard desktop FDM printers using PLA, (iii) modular at the
component level to allow component substitution from exist-
ing lab inventory, and (iv) supported by a containerized low-
level software stack.

operate as strict design constraints throughout the system.

RELATED WORKS
The majority of recent open-source flight stacks [12, 1]
and robotics middleware [2] have lowered the barrier to build-
ing research UAVs, yet scaling a fleet reveals a persistent is-
sue: platforms optimized for single objectives are difficult
to replicate, repair, or adapt.
At the commercial end, the
Crazyflie 2.1 offers open firmware, but its 40 g payload limits
onboard processing, forcing reliance on external infrastruc-
ture [3, 4]. Conversely, the DJI Tello EDU provides more
thrust but imposes a closed firmware, confining research to
software layers operating entirely above the flight controller
ecosystem [13].
Academic platforms recover firmware transparency but
introduce rigid hardware constraints. Agilicious [5] provides
a compelling open baseline for agile flight but treats sens-
ing, control, and compute as a jointly optimized, custom-PCB
whole, rendering component substitution difficult.
Omn-
iNxt [6] enables omnidirectional perception, yet its compan-
ion computer and firmware form a fixed, co-developed con-
figuration. AgiPIX [7] improves software reproducibility via
containerized ROS 2 stacks but remains bound to hardware-
synchronized active sensing and specific GPU modules.
Consequently, while various open-source state estimation
pipelines are widely accessible, their adoption on custom
platforms is constrained by the lack of an adaptable low-level
communication framework that can ingest and transparently
map external pose data to the autopilot.
Thus, existing systems optimize openness along a sin-
gle axis, such as firmware, perception, or software, while
hardware-level interchangeability and local manufacturabil-
ity remain secondary.
This work addresses this gap by
proposing an indoor-oriented platform that is simultaneously:
(i) locally manufacturable via common desktop FDM print-
ers, (ii) modular at the component level to allow inventory-
driven substitution of flight controllers and companion com-
puters, and (iii) supported by a containerized low-level bridge
designed for seamless external state estimation ingest.
To illustrate the platform’s focus, a feature matrix com-
paring these existing systems with the proposed MIRA archi-
tecture is summarized in Figure 2, highlighting how MIRA
bridges the gap between hardware openness and low-cost lo-
cal manufacturability.

SYSTEM DESIGN
3.1
Architecture Overview
The platform follows a modular three-layer architecture
designed for hardware agnosticism, allowing individual lay-
ers to be maintained or entirely substituted based on user re-
quirements. The real-time control layer runs the PX4 au-
topilot on a flight controller to handle low-level control loops
and sensor fusion, while the perception and offboard layer
is hosted on a companion computer running ROS 2 for high-
level processing. Connecting these layers, the communica-
tion bridge utilizes the Micro XRCE-DDS protocol over a
serial link, completely decoupling flight execution from com-
pute processes to enable hardware swaps without refactoring
the core software stack. An overview of this hardware base-
line and low-level software architecture is illustrated in Fig-
ure 3.
3.2
Mechanical Design
The 3D-printed airframe consists of a central avionics
deck and four detachable arms. This modular interface acts
as a crash-mitigation mechanism, enabling damaged arms to
be rapidly replaced with standard fasteners without disrupting
internal electronics or the wiring harness.

RESULTS
4.1
Experimental Setup
Experiments are conducted in an indoor GPS-denied
environment equipped with an OptiTrack PrimeX optical
motion-capture system. This infrastructure serves a dual pur-
pose: it provides millimeter-level ground-truth trajectories for
validation and streams real-time pose estimates to feed the ve-
hicle’s EKF2 estimator. To evaluate the platform under real-
istic handling and operational conditions, the vehicle is oper-
ated in Position control mode via a manual remote controller.
Two core metrics are utilized to evaluate distinct and comple-
mentary dimensions of the platform: communication pipeline
latency and structural vibration characteristics.
4.2
Communication Pipeline Latency
A 112.5 s flight log with 123 495 messages was analyzed
to evaluate communication timing (Table 2). The motion-
capture input reached the FMU at 254.3 Hz, while the EKF2
published vehicle odometry at 86.5 Hz, a decimation

4.3
Structural Vibration Analysis
To validate the 3D-printed PLA airframe and its 15 %
honeycomb infill, a dual-domain analysis combining time-
domain metrics and frequency-domain Power Spectral Den-
sity (PSD) was conducted on flight-test inertial and actuator
logs. This evaluation ensures structural resonance does not
cause state estimator aliasing or control loop degradation.
Actuator outputs display strong symmetry across all chan-
nels during steady-state conditions, tracking a baseline hover
throttle of ≈20 %. This confirms a high thrust-to-weight
ratio and the absence of structural warping. Concurrently,
time-domain metrics (Figure 4) show stable mean vibration
levels of approximately 3.5 m/s2 (primary) and 8.0 m/s2 (sec-
ondary) accelerometers. Both channels remain safely below
autopilot thresholds, ensuring overall flight stability.
In the frequency domain, the acceleration PSD (Figure 5)
indicates that motor-induced energy is concentrated within
a narrow harmonic band between 90 Hz and 110 Hz, leav-
ing frequencies below 60 Hz highly attenuated. This isola-
tion is supported by the angular velocity FFT, which shows

DISCUSSION
The platform prioritizes local manufacturability and mod-
ular component reuse over specialized sensing or extreme
agility.
This design choice provides a transparent, eas-
ily repairable, and fleet-scalable baseline vehicle for indoor
robotics research.
Trade-offs. Printing the airframe in PLA trades struc-
tural stiffness for rapid, low-cost fabrication using standard
desktop FDM printers. Similarly, while a serial uXRCE-DDS
bridge offers less bandwidth than USB or PCIe interconnects,
the representative transport delay (P95 = 5.34 ms) is well
within acceptable limits for stable state fusion. This trade-
off successfully preserves a stateless, firmware-independent
interface without compromising real-time control.
Limitations.
Evaluation is currently confined to con-
trolled indoor environments.
The experiments revealed a
significant external bottleneck: the network-level motion-
capture bridge introduced a median delay of 554 ms and
clock misalignments, dominating the latency budget over the
drone’s internal pipelines. Additionally, formal closed-loop
tracking benchmarks, such as calculating absolute trajectory
error during autonomous flights, have not yet been performed.
Future work. Immediate next steps include fixing the ex-

ternal clock alignment and conducting multi-agent fleet de-
ployments. Crucially, future work will focus on migrating
state estimation entirely onboard via a VIO pipeline utiliz-
ing the RealSense stream. Since the camera processing in-

bypass external infrastructure delays.
Finally, we plan to
implement higher-level planning layers using the platform’s
modular framework.

CONCLUSION
We presented MIRA, an open-source modular micro-
UAV architecture for GPS-denied indoor research.
By
combining a locally manufacturable PLA airframe with a
hardware-agnostic PX4, ROS 2, and uXRCE-DDS low-level
communication bridge, the platform offers a transparent,
white-box baseline that is straightforward to repair, replicate,
and extend using existing laboratory inventory. Experimen-
tal results within an optical motion-capture environment con-
firm stable flight characteristics, excellent structural vibra-
tion isolation via tailored infill geometries, and a dependable
high-rate data pipeline. All design files, software configura-
tions, and documentation are released openly to support re-
producible indoor aerial robotics research.
