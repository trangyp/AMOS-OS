Abstract. Graphical small cancellation extends the classical small cancellation theory and
provides a powerful method for constructing groups with prescribed subgraphs in their Cayley
graphs. We prove that torsion subgroups of groups defined by possibly infinite C(4)–T(4) graph-
ical small cancellation presentations are finite. We also prove the corresponding result for groups
defined by C(6) graphical small cancellation presentations under the additional assumption that
the presentation is torsion-essentially C(6)-free. Both results follow from a more general result
on local-to-global fixed point properties for torsion groups acting by automorphisms on simply
connected graphical small cancellation complexes.
1. Introduction
Graphical small cancellation generalizes classical small cancellation theory.
It provides a
method for constructing groups whose Cayley graphs contain certain prescribed graphs, giving
rise to groups with interesting properties; see, for example, [Gro03, OW07, Osa20, CG19, Osa18].
Recently, graphical small cancellation was also used to show that universal Cayley graphs cannot
exist [TD26].
A graphical presentation of a group has the form G = ⟨f : Γ →Θ⟩, where Θ is a connected
graph, f : Γ →Θ is an immersion, and G is the fundamental group of Θ modulo the normal
subgroup generated by the images of all immersed cycles in Γ. The use of such presentations
goes back to the work of Rips and Segev, who constructed torsion-free groups without the unique
product property (see [RS87, Ste15]). Small cancellation theory over graphical presentations was
later used explicitly by Gromov [Gro03] in his construction of Gromov’s monster—a group that
contains an expander graph in a weak sense.
There are two 2-dimensional combinatorial complexes naturally associated to such a presen-
tation: the thickened graphical complex Xt, where 2-cells are attached to Θ to fill the cycles
coming from Γ, and the non-thickened graphical complex Xc, where cones over the con-
nected components of Γ are attached to Θ instead. We refer to the presentation together with
these two realizations as the associated graphical complex X. We call Θ the 1-skeleton of
X and denote it by X1. Each connected component Γi of Γ determines a graphical cell of
X. Both realizations have fundamental group G; in particular, we call X simply connected if
either, equivalently both, of its realizations is simply connected. Precise definitions will be given
in Section 2.
Our main focus is on torsion subgroups of groups defined by graphical small cancellation
presentations. This is motivated by the general philosophy that graphical small cancellation
complexes behave like nonpositively curved spaces, together with several conjectures and ques-
tions concerning torsion subgroups and locally elliptic actions on nonpositively curved complexes;
see, for example, [Swe99, HO21].
We prove the following finiteness results.
Theorem 1. Torsion subgroups of groups defined by C(4)–T(4) graphical small cancellation
presentations are finite.
Theorem 2. Not essentially C(6) torsion subgroups of groups defined by C(6) graphical small
cancellation presentations are finite. In particular, all torsion subgroups of groups defined by
torsion-essentially C(6)-free graphical small cancellation presentations are finite.
The terminology in Theorem 2 is meant to isolate the borderline case of the graphical C(6)
condition. More precisely, an essentially C(6) graphical cell is one containing an immersed cycle

Theorems 1 and 2 are consequences of a more general local-to-global fixed point theorem
for actions on simply connected graphical small cancellation complexes. Recall that an action
of a group on a metric space is called locally elliptic if every cyclic subgroup has bounded
orbits, and elliptic if the whole group has bounded orbits. Haettel and Osajda conjectured that
every locally elliptic action of a finitely generated group on a finite-dimensional nonpositively
curved complex is elliptic [HO21]. They proved this for several classes of complexes, including
locally finite simply connected graphical C(4)–T(4) complexes and not necessarily locally finite
graphical C(18) complexes.
For many nonpositively curved complexes, an automorphism fixes a point if and only if the
cyclic group generated by it has bounded orbit. However, as we show in Section 9, this equivalence
fails for non-locally finite C(p)–T(q) small cancellation complexes with q < 5. Nevertheless, we
prove in Section 9 that every finite order automorphism of a simply connected C(4)–T(4) or C(6)
graphical complex fixes a point. Thus torsion group actions in this setting provide a natural class
of locally elliptic actions to study.
Our main result is the following local-to-global fixed point theorem. It establishes a particular
case of the meta-conjecture of Haettel and Osajda [HO21] for torsion group actions on graphical
C(4)–T(4) and C(6) complexes, without assuming local finiteness of the complex or finite gen-
eration of the acting group, but under the assumption that the induced action on the 1-skeleton
is free.
Theorem 3. Let X be a simply connected C(4)–T(4) or C(6) graphical small cancellation com-
plex. Let G be a torsion group acting on X by automorphisms such that the induced action on
the 1-skeleton X1 is free. If X is C(4)–T(4), then G is finite. If X is C(6) and the action is not
essentially C(6), then G is finite. In particular, if X is torsion-essentially C(6)-free, then G is
finite. In either case there is a graphical cell of X stabilized by G.
Let us compare Theorem 3 with the existing results. The known theorems differ along several
independent axes: whether the complex is classical or graphical, whether local finiteness is
assumed, whether the acting group is finitely generated, and whether the action is free on the
1-skeleton. Duda proved analogous results for classical C(4)–T(4) and C(6) complexes under
the assumption that the action is free on the 1-skeleton [Dud25]. Haettel and Osajda proved
ellipticity of locally elliptic actions for finitely generated groups in several graphical settings,
including locally finite graphical C(4)–T(4) complexes and graphical C(18) complexes [HO21].
In the C(3)–T(6) case, corresponding results were obtained for finitely generated groups first
in the classical setting in [Dud25] and then in the graphical setting in [Gui26], both using the
fixed-point theorem for 2-dimensional CAT(0) complexes from [NOP22]. Theorem 3 extends this
type of result to not necessarily locally finite graphical C(4)–T(4) complexes, and to a subclass
of graphical C(6) complexes containing all graphical C(7) complexes.
The following question remains open.
Question. Can an essentially C(6) torsion subgroup of a group defined by a C(6) graphical small
cancellation presentation be infinite?
The freeness assumption in Theorem 3 cannot simply be removed for arbitrary torsion groups.
Indeed, by a theorem of Serre [Ser03, Theorem 15, Chapter I.6.1], every countable infinitely
generated group acts without a fixed point on a tree.
However, the finitely generated case
remains open.
Question. Does the conclusion of Theorem 3 still hold if the freeness assumption on the 1-skeleton
is dropped, but the torsion group G is assumed to be finitely generated?

2. Preliminaries
For fundamental notions such as CW-complexes, null-homotopy, and simple connectedness,
see Hatcher’s textbook [Hat00].
A map X →Y between complexes is combinatorial if its restriction to every open cell of X
is a homeomorphism onto an open cell of Y . A complex is combinatorial if the attaching map
of each cell is combinatorial after a suitable subdivision of the domain sphere. An immersion
is a combinatorial map which is locally injective.
Unless stated otherwise, all complexes and maps are combinatorial, and all attaching maps
are immersions. We mostly consider 2-dimensional combinatorial complexes, which we call 2-
complexes.
Starting from Section 5 we will often use square complexes. Square complexes are 2-complexes
whose 2-cells are 4-gons. In this case, instead of the usual terms, 0-cell, 1-cell and 2-cell, we will
use vertex, edge and square, respectively.
A graph is a 1-dimensional CW-complex with 0-cells called vertices and 1-cells called edges.
Distances between vertices in graphs are always measured by the standard graph metric which
is defined for a pair of vertices u and v as the number of edges in the shortest path connecting
u and v.
A graph Γ is simplicial if there is no edge in Γ with both endpoints attached to one vertex
and no two edges of Γ having their endpoints attached to the same unordered pair of vertices.
Among classes of simplicial graphs we distinguish the so called bipartite graphs: a graph Γ is
bipartite if the set of its vertices can be partitioned into two nonempty sets such that no edge has
both of its endpoints in the same set. A graph Γ is path-cycle extensible if every immersed
path in Γ factors through an immersed cycle.
Let Γ be a finite simplicial graph. A cone on Γ is the quotient space
Cone(Γ) = Γ × [0, 1]/Γ × {1}.
A simplicial cone is Cone(Γ) with a natural structure of a 2-complex.

When Θ is a wedge of circles, and each Γi is a cycle graph, the graphical presentation reduces
to a classical group presentation. One can then associate a 2-complex to such a presentation

cycle Γi. In the classical setting, this change does not modify the underlying geometric structure
significantly.
In the general setting, these two viewpoints of what a presentation complex is, lead to two
natural ways of constructing a 2-complex with fundamental group G = ⟨f : Γ →Θ⟩. First, let Θ
be the 1-skeleton. For every immersed cycle C →Γ, attach a 2-cell to Θ along the composition
C →Γ →Θ. The resulting complex is called the thickened graphical complex associated to
G, and we denote it by Xt. The term “thickened” comes from the fact, that for any connected
component Γi, we have a thick cell which is formed by gluing 2–cells along all immersed cycles
in Γi. Alternatively, for each connected component Γi of Γ, attach the simplicial cone Cone(Γi)
to Θ via the map f|Γi. The resulting complex is called the non-thickened graphical complex,
denoted Xc. Note that every 2-cell in Xc is a triangle.
Observe that in the general setting, the difference between the topology of thickened and non-
thickened graphical complexes becomes significant, as e.g. thickened cells may contain spheres,
while non-thickened ones cannot. Consequently, these complexes are used to obtain different
properties of a presentation.

Definition 3.4. For p, q ∈N+, we say the graphical complex X associated to a graphical
presentation G = ⟨f : Γ →Θ⟩satisfies
• the C(p) small cancellation condition if there is no immersed cycle C →Γ such that
C →Γ →Θ is the concatenation of less than p pieces;
• the T(q) small cancellation condition if for every reduced disc diagram D →Xt,
there is no interior vertex of D whose degree is greater than 2 and less than q.
In particular C(p)–T(q) denotes complex or presentation that satisfies both C(p) and T(q).

5. Quadric complexes
In this section, we first recall the definition of quadric complexes, then we describe the
quadrization of a non-thickened graphical complex, finally, we show that the quadrization of
a simply connected non-thickened graphical C(4)–T(4) small cancellation complex is quadric.
Definition 5.1. [Hod20, Definition 1.1.]
A locally quadric complex is a square complex Y
satisfying the following conditions.
(A) The attaching map of every square is an immersion.
(B) If there are two squares F1, F2 ∈Y that they share at least three edges, then ∂F1 = ∂F2.
(C) If there are two squares F1, F2 ∈Y such that ∂(F1 ∪F2) is a cycle of length 4, then there is
F ∈Y such that ∂F = ∂(F1 ∪F2).
(D) If there are three squares F1, F2, F3 ∈Y such that ∂(F1 ∪F2 ∪F3) is a cycle of length 6, then
there exist F, F ′ ∈Y such that ∂(F1 ∪F2 ∪F3) = ∂(F ∪F ′), i.e. this cycle has a diagonal
that divides it into two 4-cycles.
Conditions (C) and (D) are called rules of replacement. A quadric complex is a simply con-
nected locally quadric complex.

13. Automatic Continuity
In this section, we present an aforementioned application of Theorem 3 to automatic continuity.
For a group G and a metric space X, a group action Φ : G →Isom(X) is called proper if for
each x ∈X there exist a real number r > 0 such that the set {g ∈G|(Br(Φ(g)(x))∩Br(x)) ̸= 0}
is finite. The group action Φ is called cocompact if there exists a compact subset K ⊆X such
that Φ(G)(K) = X. We say that Φ is a geometric action if it is both proper and cocompact.
We say that a group G is systolic if it admits a geometric action on a systolic complex.
A group G is called artinian if any descending chain of subgroups G1 ⊃G2 ⊃. . . becomes
stationary, that is, Gn = Gn+1 = . . . from some n onwards.
Let G denote the class of all groups G with the following three properties:
(1) G does not include Q or the p-adic integers Zp for any prime as a subgroup;
(2) G does not include the Prüfer p group Z(p∞) for any prime as a subgroup;

(3) torsion subgroups in G are artinian.
