ABSTRACT
We report the discovery of a sample of little red dots (LRDs) at z ≈2 identified from deep JWST/NIRCam imaging and wide-field
slitless spectroscopy over 140 arcmin2 from the EIGER survey. With an improved blind broad-line identification algorithm, we select
19 sources at spectroscopic redshifts z = 1.55−3.18 identified via rest-frame near-infrared lines (Paschen-β, He i+Paγ and O i). Based
on a range of spectro-photometric criteria, we classify five of these sources as LRDs and the other 14 as classical active galactic nuclei
(AGNs). This classification is corroborated by some X-ray detections among the AGNs. Classical AGNs dominate the number counts
above optical luminosities M5100 < −22.5, whereas the LRD fraction among broad-line sources reaches 100 % at M5100 ≈−20. The
LRDs span the range in Balmer break strengths seen in the higher redshift populations. Blue-shifted He i absorption is detected in the
two reddest sources. The He i/Paγ ratio cleanly separates LRDs from classical AGNs and seems to anti-correlate with Balmer break
strength, likely tracing He i self-absorption at higher gas column densities. Our LRD sample has a similar optical luminosity range
as their high-redshift counterparts, corresponding to black hole masses of ∼106 M⊙at the Eddington luminosity. We measure LRD
number densities of ≈7 × 10−6 cMpc−3 at z = 1.9 −2.5, which indicates that LRDs represent ≲3% of the AGN population at these
epochs. Our results confirm the previously reported decline in the LRD number density with respect to z ≈5 based on photometric
surveys, although we find the decline to be more gentle than earlier emphasized.
Key words. Galaxies: active, high-redshift
1. Introduction
One of the most debated discoveries of the JWST has been the
nature of the population of objects nicknamed the ‘Little Red
Dots’ (LRDs; e.g. Labbe et al. 2025; Matthee et al. 2024; Koko-
rev et al. 2024; Akins et al. 2024; Pérez-González et al. 2026).
LRDs are primarily found at redshifts z ∼5−7 with number den-
sities ∼10−4 to 10−5 cMpc−3. Their red appearance in NIRCam
imaging is driven by a combination of strong Hα line-emission
and very red UV to optical colors, often associated with a strong
Balmer break (Setton et al. 2024). These compact, red sources
typically show broad Hα lines (Hviding et al. 2025), which has
been considered key evidence that they are powered by an active
galactic nucleus (AGN; see Inayoshi & Ho 2025 for a review).
Given that LRDs are far more abundant than quasars at z ∼5 and
have much lower luminosities, it is likely that we are witnessing
a newly identified, poorly understood phase in the formation of
supermassive black holes.
The unusual spectral energy distribution (SED) has been a
key fuel for the debate on LRDs. Besides the ‘V-shape’ in the
rest-frame UV to optical spectrum (with a relatively blue UV
slope and strong reddening from ∼0.3 −0.5 µm), the LRDs lack
the usual X-ray and hot dust emission expected for an AGN (e.g.

Yue et al. 2024; Maiolino et al. 2025; Xiao et al. 2025), nor do
they show significant optical variability on ≲10 yr time-scales
(Burke et al. 2025; Kokubo & Harikane 2025; Liu et al. 2026).
The Hα lines are very strong relative to the continuum (Yanagi-
sawa et al. 2026), and the Balmer lines show steep Balmer decre-
ments (Nikopoulos et al. 2025) and (in many cases) absorption
features (D’Eugenio et al. 2025; Matthee et al. 2026). These are
very rare in classical AGN populations (Shangguan et al. 2026),
but commonly seen in stellar phenomena (e.g. Martins et al.
2026). Models invoking obscuration by dense, partially excited
hydrogen gas with a high covering fraction (rather than obscura-
tion by dust) have been successful in explaining various of these
features simultaneously (e.g. Inayoshi & Maiolino 2025; Naidu
et al. 2025; de Graaff et al. 2025; Torralba et al. 2026a; Sneppen
et al. 2026). However, the nature of the LRDs and their role in
the formation of galaxies and supermassive black holes remains
unclear, with debate around the powering engine and the origin
and geometry of the dense gas (Santarelli et al. 2026; Brazzini
et al. 2026; Madau & Maiolino 2026; Roman-Garza et al. 2026;
Nandal et al. 2026).
Given that seemingly distinct physical scenarios can yield
similar spectra, additional constraints on the nature of LRDs
are highly valuable. Clustering measurements have shown that
LRDs typically reside in relatively low mass galaxies and halos

(Lin et al. 2024; Arita et al. 2025; Matthee et al. 2025). The steep
decline in the bright end of the LRD luminosity function (LF) at
z ∼5 favors a relatively low mass powering engine with a narrow
distribution of Eddington-level luminosities (Ma et al. 2025b).
Thus, similar to how key insights about galaxy formation have
been derived from the evolution of galaxy stellar mass functions
(e.g. Peng et al. 2010; Lilly et al. 2013), detailed measurements
of the redshift evolution of LRD LFs down to lower redshifts
promise to offer complementary constraints on their nature.
A challenge is that photometric selections of compact LRDs
(e.g. Kokorev et al. 2024; Akins et al. 2024) are difficult to
carry out uniformly across redshift as strong emission-lines and
(Balmer) breaks impact broad-band colors depending on the red-
shift in a complex way and high-resolution data are required to
remove interlopers (e.g. Euclid Collaboration et al. 2025; Ma
et al. 2025a). These studies find somewhat lower number of
LRDs at z ∼2 than at z ∼5−7, but they are currently still uncer-
tain without extensive spectroscopic follow-up. LRDs have been
identified in data from very wide spectroscopic surveys such
as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) and the Dark Energy
Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) survey in the z ≈0.5 Universe
(e.g. Lin et al. 2025a, 2026a; Park et al. 2026, see also Izotov &
Thuan 2008 for early studies). Their number density estimates
are ∼10−9 cMpc−3, orders of magnitude lower than at high red-
shift, but inherently uncertain due to the complex spectroscopic
selection function of these surveys.
Surveys using wide-field slitless spectroscopy (WFSS) with
JWST/NIRCam have been very efficient in identifying LRDs
through their broad Hα line emission at z ∼5 (Matthee et al.
2024; Lin et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025; Covelo-Paz et al.
2024; Lin et al. 2025b). In principle, emission-line surveys are
more straightforward to carry out uniformly over a larger redshift
baseline and WFSS on NIRCam covers Hα over z ≈2.8 −6.5.
However, both the sensitivity and the field of view of the NIR-
Cam grism declines significantly towards the bluest and reddest
wavelengths of the coverage. WFSS on NIRISS covers Hα at
cosmic noon, but its smaller field of view and lower resolution
challenges the identification of broad line emitters. Alternatively,
Loiacono et al. (2025) and Naidu et al. (2024) identified LRDs
at z ≈2.4 through broad He i and Paschen-γ (Paγ) emission. Al-
though these lines are significantly fainter than Hα (Hα/Paγ ≈9
in the z = 2.26 ‘Rosetta Stone’ LRD identified by Juodžbalis
et al. 2024), they are covered at z ∼2 in JWST’s most sensitive
WFSS wavelengths.
In this paper, we use deep NIRCam WFSS data from the
EIGER survey (Kashino et al. 2023) to specifically survey for
broad He i, Paγ and Paβ emission at z ∼1.5 −3. The search-
method builds upon the work of Matthee et al. (2024) who partly
used EIGER data to identify broad Hα, but here we use new,
optimised detection algorithms, more data and we can benefit
from the increased understanding of the broadband 1-5 micron
SEDs of LRDs. We first detail the data used in Section 2. We
explain the newly developed broad-line identification algorithm
and our means of separating LRDs from other broad-line sources
in Section 3. The properties of the identified LRDs at z ≈2 are
described in Section 4. In Section 5 we present the LF and com-
pare this to other measurements in the literature. In Section 6 we
discuss the number density evolution of LRDs. We summarize
our results in Section 7. Throughout this work we use a ΛCDM
cosmology as described by Planck Collaboration et al. (2020),
with ΩΛ = 0.69, ΩM = 0.31, and H0 = 67.7 km s−1 Mpc−1.

2. Data
Our analysis is primarily based on the EIGER survey (Kashino
et al. 2023; Matthee et al. 2023), which consists of imaging
and deep wide-field slitless spectroscopy obtained with NIRCam
(Rieke et al. 2023) on the JWST. EIGER (PID 1243, PI: Lilly)
observed a ∼4 by 4 arcmin2 mosaic centered on six luminous
quasars at z ∼6 −7 with the primary goals of studying the re-
lation between galaxies and the intergalactic medium (Kashino
et al. 2026), characterizing the environments of quasars (Eilers
et al. 2024), as well as investigating the relation between metal
absorption lines and foreground galaxies (Bordoloi et al. 2024),
including galaxies at cosmic noon. Among the various NIRCam
grism surveys that JWST performed (e.g. Oesch et al. 2023; Sun
et al. 2025; Kakiichi et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2026b), EIGER
stands out for its combination of depth (≈3 −10 hours of expo-
sure time per pixel) and area (143 arcmin2) over six independent
fields.
The spectral resolution of R ∼1600 combined with the sen-
sitivity of the deep NIRCam grism data in the F356W filter has
proven to be highly efficient in identifying relatively faint, broad
Hα emission lines at z ∼4 −5 that are serendipitously observed
in these fields (Matthee et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2024). Here we
extend this broad line search to lower redshifts by searching the
grism data for broad He i and Paschen lines in the rest-frame
near-infrared, that are redshifted into the 3.15 −3.95µm cover-
age at redshifts z ≈2 (see Fig. 1). In addition to grism data,
there is uniform deep NIRCam imaging available in the F115W
and F200W filter taken simultaneously with the WFSS data, as
well as direct imaging in the F356W filter. These data are used
to characterize the morphologies and the spectral energy distri-
butions of the identified sources. For a detailed description of the
observations, data reduction and photometric catalog production
we refer to Kashino et al. (2026). In this paper we use 2D grism
spectra extracted at the positions of all ≈120, 000 NIRCam-
detected sources identified across the six fields to search for
broad emission-lines.

3. Search method
3.1. Broad-line identification in grism data
Building
upon
the
methodology
developed
in
Matthee
et al. (2024), we identified candidate broad-line emitters
on continuum-subtracted emission-line spectra (‘EMLINE’)
extracted at the position of NIRCam-detected sources using
the code Allegro (Kramarenko & Matthee in prep.). Candidate
broad emission-lines are identified in these EMLINE spectra us-
ing Source Extractor (Bertin & Arnouts 1996). These candidate
lines should be within a distance ∆y = 1.5 (≈0.1′′) from the
center of the trace to reduce contamination by emission-lines
from other sources. We impose a signal-to-noise (S/N) threshold
of > 15 measured within a Kron radius (as opposed to a circular
aperture-based S/N threshold that is less optimal for broad lines)
and we impose an elongation criterion (A/B > 1.4, where A and
B are the major and minor axis derived from the lines intensity
distribution) with an angle |θ| < 10◦to only select for lines
that are extended along the spectral direction (θ = 0). Some of
the identified broad-lines are shown in Fig. 2, where we show
the EMLINE spectra used for line-identification, as well as the
original ‘SCI’ spectra (i.e. not continuum-subtracted) that we
used for fitting the line-profiles and fluxes. Some SCI spectra
are heavily contaminated by other sources (e.g. J159_6107), but
this does not prevent the line-identification.
The only photometric selection that we impose is that we
remove all objects for which the emission-line strength mea-
sured in the F356W grism data is too high for an objects
F356W magnitude, as this can only be due to contamination
(i.e. the emission-line originates from another object). Then,
we visually inspected the 834 candidate broad-lines using the
Specvizitor1 tool. The goal of this inspection is to remove
sources for which the identified emission-line is not real, or not
associated to the galaxy in which spectrum it was identified.
Most of the candidate broad-lines were identified as contami-
nation, such as residuals from the continuum-subtraction at the

spatial direction in the EMLINE spectra.
Other sources of contaminants to our broad-line sample are
galaxies with real emission-lines originating from H ii regions
that happen to be spatially extended along the dispersion direc-
tion. Specifically, for an extended source, we expect that the ob-
served line-width of a spectrally-unresolved line is the convo-
lution of the grism line-spread function (LSF; R = 1600) and
the spatial extent along the dispersion direction (where a NIR-
Cam long-wavelength pixel of 0.063′′ corresponds to ≈9.8 Å
in wavelength). We call this the extended-source LSF and we
used this to verify real broad lines. Typically we find that the ex-
tended LSF implies an effective resolution R ∼400 −600. To
distinguish velocity broadening from spatial broadening, we fit
the identified candidate broad-lines with the following models:
a single, narrow gaussian convolved with the extended-source
LSF, a combination of a narrow and a broad gaussian with the
point-source LSF, as well as a combination of a narrow gaus-
sian with extended-source LSF and a broad gaussian with point-
source LSF. We use the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC)
to determine whether line-profiles that require broad emission
(FWHM> 500 km s−1) beyond the spatial extent of the objects
along the dispersion direction are warranted (with a BIC differ-
ence of > 10 compared to models without broad emission). For
sources with closely separated He i + Paγ emission we modeled
independent broad and narrow components for both lines. We fit
the Hα + [N ii] profile exclusively with narrow [N ii] emission.

3.2. Redshift identification
In the F356W grism data used, we cover the following emission-
lines: Paschen-α at z = 0.68 −1.10, Paschen-β (Paβ) at z =
1.45−2.08, He i+Paγ at z = 1.91−2.64, [S iii] at z = 2.30−3.14,
O i8446 at z = 2.73 −3.68 and Hα at z = 3.80 −5.02. The redshift
identification is split in two steps. In the first step, we search
for secondary (i.e. fainter) lines in the grism coverage to yield a
unique redshift solution. All sources with He i in the sample are
also detected in the Paγ line. There are also redshift windows
where we additionally cover the relatively strong [S iii] line. In
some cases, we also detect faint [Fe ii] lines at z ∼1 −3.
The second step is to identify redshifts for single line emit-
ters. In most cases, this specifically means that we need to choose
whether a line is Hα (as [N ii] is usually not detected; Matthee
et al. 2024), Paβ or Paα. In one Paschen-β emitter we detect var-
ious faint [Fe ii] lines confirming the redshift. For the single-line
detections, we rely on the fact that the equivalent width (EW) of
the Hα line can reach much higher values than Paschen-series
lines (see e.g. Fig. 1) and the observed EW scales with (1+z),
such that we classify virtually all single line-emitters as Hα
lines. Our limited photometric data further supports these red-
shift identifications.
Out of the 54 broad-line sources, 26 are Hα emitters at
z = 3.86 −5.02 (including all sources identified in the EIGER
data in Matthee et al. 2024), 17 are identified based on their He i
emission (z = 2.04−2.50), 8 on [S iii] emission (z = 2.38−3.03)
and 2 on Paβ (z = 1.55 −1.93). One source that deserves special
attention is J0148_9325 at z = 3.177, for which the primarily
line in our spectrum is O i8446. Here, we determined the redshift
thanks to detections of Paschen-11, Paschen-10, Paschen-9 and
[Fe ii]9179 that we identified through their similar appearance to
the spectrum of the z = 3.5 LRD identified by Kokorev et al.
(2025). No broad Paα emission was identified. In the rest of the
paper we focus on the sources identified through Paβ, He i and
O i emission. The key properties of the sources studied in more
detail are listed in Table 1.

3.3. LRD classification
Having identified our sample of broad line-emitters at z ∼2, here
we detail how we separate LRDs from non-LRDs (i.e. classical
AGNs) among these sources. Usually, LRDs are defined based
on compactness, broad Hα lines and a ‘V-shape’, an inflection in
their continuum colors from the UV to the optical, although usu-
ally only two of these criteria automatically lead to satisfying the
third (e.g. Hviding et al. 2025). Alternative selections focusing
on extreme subsets (Weibel et al. 2026) or more tolerant colors
(Rinaldi et al. 2026) have also been explored.
At z ∼2, Ma et al. (2025a) previously explored a color selec-
tion mimicking a V-shape and a compactness criterion based on
ground-based data. As illustrated in Fig. 1, however, the strong
Hα emission in LRDs boosts the F200W band photometry at
z = 2.2, whereas F115W captures continuum flux around the
Balmer break and the F356W band contains relatively weak
lines. For somewhat higher redshifts, on the other hand, Hα no
longer boosts the F200W photometry and the F115W fully cov-
ers bluewards of the Balmer break, indicating that the NIRCam
colors are strongly redshift dependent within the z ≈1.5 −3 bin.

The challenge for our classification is that we lack broad-
band PRISM spectra from NIRSpec (i.e. we have no Hα spec-
troscopy or can not spectroscopically measure the continuum
colors) and our photometric measurements is limited to only
three NIRCam filters (HST/ACS photometry in the F606W,
F775W and F814W band is available for some sources, see
Kashino et al. 2026 and Table A.1). We therefore use template
fitting approaches, but we also explore our classification in the
context of He i and Paγ emission-line properties, and we discuss
the morphologies and X-ray data.
We explore the following templates:
– A set of ten dust-reddened quasar templates. These are based
on the empirical composite UV to NIR spectrum from Van-
den Berk et al. (2001) and Glikman et al. (2006). We cre-
ate a set of 10 templates with dust attenuation in the range
E(B −V) from 0 to 1, assuming the Calzetti et al. (2000)
reddening law.
– A set of seven template LRDs based on stacks of NIRSpec
PRISM spectra of high-redshift LRDs presented in Matthee
et al. (2026) that cover the rest-frame UV to optical. These
consist of four stacks in bins of the redness of the UV to opti-
cal color that capture the diversity in LRD spectra at z ∼3−7.
We interpolate between these four stacks to better sample the
variations in Balmer break strengths.
Each template is redshifted to the spectroscopic redshift of
the source and we calculate the χ2 based on synthetic photometry
in the available filters. The normalization is a free parameter. We
add a 5 % error to the photometry to account for uncertainties
in zero points, aperture corrections and possibly underestimated
correlated noise. We include all available photometry.
Out of the 19 sources at z = 1.55 −3.18, LRD templates are
preferred for 13 sources (|∆BIC| > 10, typically ≳50, whereas
the templates are inconclusive for J159_6127. Figures 3 and 4
show the SEDs of five LRDs and a transitionary source identi-
fied in this work. We show the SEDs of the other non-LRDs in
Figs. B.2 and B.3. In most cases at z ∼2, the LRD templates do
not cover the rest-frame near-infrared, resulting in the F356W
photometry being ignored in the fits. The key distinguishing fea-
tures between the best-fit dust-reddened quasar templates and the
LRD templates are the V-shape in the LRD spectra (e.g. Labbe
et al. 2025; Setton et al. 2024). Additionally, the high EWs of
the Hα or [O iii] lines in LRDs in many cases boost the photom-
etry in the F200W filter (see also Torralba et al. 2026b), which
is challenging to explain with quasar templates that have lower
EWs.
Upon inspection of the 13 sources that are best-matched with
LRD templates, we note that 9 of them show strong blue-skewed
wings in their emission-lines that we associate with powerful
outflows. In many cases, the outflow velocities extend up to
> 1000 km s−1, suggesting they are AGN-driven. Such strong
features of outflows are not seen in LRDs and we also notice
that these outflows are typically found in the sources that have
lower line EWs. Moreover, among the four broad-line sources
that are detected in the Chandra Source Catalog 2.1 (Evans et al.
2024), two are best-fitted by an LRD template, whereas LRDs
are known to be extremely X-ray faint (Maiolino et al. 2025).
These results suggest that our template fits alone are not suffi-
ciently capable of separating LRDs from classical AGNs, proba-
bly due to limitations in the templates as well as the low number
of available filters. By comparing the synthetic photometry in de-
tail (see Figs. B.2 and B.3), we note that the F356W photometry
matches the quasar templates particularly well, whereas in many
cases the scaled template of the Rosetta Stone LRD (which does
cover the rest-frame near-infrared) lies below the F356W flux.
We therefore speculate that the quasar templates could have been
preferred in more sources if our LRD templates extended into the
rest-frame NIR.

4. Sample properties
4.1. Colors and morphologies
Here we focus on characterizing the properties of the LRDs
that we identified. We use our best-fit templates to estimate the
Balmer break strength of our sources. The uncertainty that we
associate with these Balmer break strengths (fν,4050/ fν,3650) cap-
tures the range of Balmer break strengths within the samples
used to construct the templates. Our sample spans a wide range
in Balmer break strengths, from fν,4050/fν,3650 ≈1 −4.
Beyond the dominant point source that is powering the broad
emission-lines, we notice that most LRDs have nearby associ-
ated clumps, that can most clearly be distinguished in the F115W
imaging data (rest-frame ≈0.35µm) where the compact source
is usually less dominant. Specifically, J0148_9235, J1030_2545
and J159_6107 have faint, distinct clumps within 0.5′′, that are
probably associated regions of star formation (e.g. Rinaldi et al.
2025; Baggen et al. 2026). No separate clumps are seen around
J1030_2735, but its F115W morphology appears somewhat re-
solved. J1148_21539 similarly has a resolved morphology in the
F115W data and further has a companion at a separation of 1.2′′
(≈10 kpc).
4.2. Line-fitting
We obtain fluxes of the relevant emission lines (He i λ10830,
Paγ, Paβ) for our sources by fitting a multi-Gaussian model to
the extracted 1D ‘SCI’ spectra. While ‘SCI’ spectra may have
contamination by continuum emission from other sources (see
e.g. J159_6107 in Fig. 2 that shows clear contamination), we pre-
fer to model this continuum rather than the ‘EMLINE’ spectra,
where the broad wings of the line-profiles may be impacted by
the running median continuum-removal procedure, in particular
in the case of complex combinations of lines such as He i + Paγ.
Although high-quality spectra of LRDs suggest that their broad
components have exponential wings (e.g. Torralba et al. 2026a;
Kokorev et al. 2025; Brazzini et al. 2026), our spectra are not
sensitive enough to distinguish such shapes from simple Gaus-
sians. We therefore use a model consisting of a broad (conser-
vatively FWHM > 600 km s−1) and a narrow Gaussian compo-
nent. We allow small velocity offsets between both components
(±300 km s−1), and in the case of the sources with He i+Paγ we
impose the broad and narrow components of both lines to share
the same offset, respectively. Additionally, we model the possi-
ble blueshifted He i λ10830 absorption as an additional Gaussian
with negative flux. For every object with He i+Paγ coverage, we
fit a version of our model with and without the He i absorption
component, and find that the model including the absorption is
preferred for only two objects (∆BIC > 10; J1030_2735 and
J1148_21539, both classified as LRDs). The continuum is mod-
eled as a powerlaw (fλ,cont ∝λα). Some examples of the multi-
Gaussian line fits for LRDs are shown in Fig. 6. The Paβ profile
of J1148_21539 is shown in Fig. B.4 and the line-profiles of the
non-LRDs are shown in B.5.
For our sample of LRDs, we find typical Gaussian FWHM
for the broad components of 1400–2100 km s−1. The line-
profiles of the He i, Paγ and Paβ lines are all somewhat differ-
ent: we find a narrow-to-broad ratio of the fitted lines in the
range 0.1–1.0 for Paγ, 0.4–0.8 for He i and 0.3–0.6 for Paβ. The
He i lines tend to be somewhat narrower than the Paschen lines.

4.3. Bolometric luminosity and Balmer breaks
While we only have three-band photometry available for the ma-
jority of our sources and in many cases (some of) the bands are
impacted by strong lines as Hα or [O iii], we find that the pho-
tometry can distinguish well among the range of LRD templates
that we used. We use these templates to estimate the absolute
magnitude in the rest-frame optical M5100, as well as the Balmer
break (see Table 1).
The absolute magnitudes range from M5100 = −19.4 to
M5100 = −22.3, which is a similar luminosity range as LRD can-
didates identified at z ∼2 from wide-area ground-based surveys
(Ma et al. 2025a) and which also overlaps with samples at z ∼5
based on JWST data (e.g. Kokorev et al. 2024). In Fig. 5 we
show that the rest-frame optical luminosities of the most lumi-
nous LRDs overlap with those of the faintest classical AGNs that
we identified, but the LRDs dominate at luminosities fainter than
M5100 > −21. The bright-end of the LF appears to drop rather
steeply, as we did not identify LRDs brighter than M5100 ≲−22,
whereas we identified about a handful of more luminous, classi-
cal AGNs in the same covered volume.
The bolometric conversion in LRDs has been a key topic of
investigation (e.g. Loiacono et al. 2025; Greene et al. 2026), es-
pecially given their relative faintness in the X-rays and infrared
(e.g. Akins et al. 2024; Delvecchio et al. 2025). Here, for sim-
plicity, we assume the bolometric conversion Lbol/λL5100 = 5.4
based on empirical data on LRDs (Greene et al. 2026), which is
roughly half of the conversion in classical AGNs (Kaspi et al.
2000). This corresponds to log10(Lbol/erg s−1) = −0.4M5100 +
36. Our sample therefore spans bolometric luminosities from
(5.88 −80.34) × 1043 erg s−1. For reference, these luminosi-
ties corresponding to the Eddington rate for objects masses of
(4.7 −64) × 105 M⊙.
The sources show a diversity in SED shapes, with
J1030_2735 (see also Loiacono et al. 2025) and J1148_21539
showing strong Balmer breaks. These breaks coincide with ab-
sorption in the He i line (Fig. 6), reminiscent of the correlation
between the Balmer break strength and the presence of absorp-
tion in the Balmer lines seen in the high-redshift LRDs (Matthee
et al. 2026). J1030_2545, J0148_9325 and J159_6107 have rel-
atively weak Balmer breaks. While their overall SED shape is
relatively similar to the quasar template, we note that the prefer-
ence for the LRD templates is primarily driven by the excess in

4.4. Properties from the He i and Paγ emission-line ratios
The rest-frame near infrared emission lines in LRDs are sensi-
tive to the dust attenuation and the gas and ionization conditions.
For example, Chang et al. (2026) argue that Paschen-α emission
should be boosted relative to the other transitions due to radia-
tive decay in the process of Hβ scattering. Moreover, in addition
to Balmer line-ratios, the Paschen line ratios also provide com-
plementary constraints on the relative importance of dust atten-
uation and optical depth effects. The He i1087 line is a metastable
transition and therefore very sensitive to the helium column den-
sity (e.g. Wang et al. 2025).
As Fig. 7 shows, the LRDs (including the z = 2.26, 3.1 LRDs
identified by Juodžbalis et al. 2024 and Wang et al. 2025, respec-
tively) separate from the classical AGNs by their low He i/Paγ
ratio. All normal AGNs have He i/Paγ > 2.3, which happens
to correspond to the maximum line-ratio identified in HII re-
gions ionised by star-formation (Brinchmann 2023) since the
He i/Paγ is a tracer of the ionization parameter due to the dif-
ferences in the excitation energies. The LRDs are below this
line-ratio, highlighting that LRDs have a relatively soft ioniz-
ing spectrum (see also Wang et al. 2026a). For LRDs, we find
that the He i/Paγ line-ratio tentatively anti-correlates with the es-
timated strength of the Balmer break (Pearson P-value ≈0.06
propagating flux uncertainties). While this could imply that the
LRDs with weaker breaks have a somewhat higher ionization
parameter and/or harder spectra, we interpret this trend as be-
ing primarily caused by an optical depth effect: objects with
stronger Balmer breaks have higher gas column densities, which
particularly manifests in stronger He i self-absorption, lowering
the He i/Paγ ratio. Since star-forming galaxies tend to show rela-
tively low He i/Paγ ratios and an increasingly strong host galaxy
contribution is expected to weaken the Balmer break strength
(Barro et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2026), we note that this correlation
likely traces the properties of the central engine rather than the
relative contribution of the host galaxy emission.
Due to the relatively narrow wavelength coverage of the
grism data, we usually only detect a single Paschen line for each
LRD. However, thanks to the fortunate redshift of J1148_21539,
we cover He i + Paγ as well as Paβ, for which we measure total
Paγ/Paβ flux ratio of 0.42 ± 0.05. Within the sensitivity of our
data (S/N per pixel at the line half maxima ≈10 and 5 for Paβ
and Paγ, respectively), the line-profiles of these lines are con-
sistent with each other, with a broad-to-total flux ratio of ≈80
%. The observed line-ratio is significantly below the case B ex-
pectation for a 104 K gas with electron density of 100 cm−3 and
could indicate a strong attenuation of AV ≈4. However, optical
depth effects that could have stronger impact on the lower order
Paβ line could lead to a lower intrinsic ratio (‘case C’), which
would mimic the effect of attenuation. This is not implausible
given the detection of blue-shifted He i absorption (see Fig. 6)
and the strong estimated Balmer break (the strongest in our sam-
ple), but follow-up spectroscopy of higher order Paschen and of
Balmer lines could differentiate these scenarios conclusively.
The stacked spectra of the LRDs and the non-LRDs fur-
ther illustrate the relative weakness of the He i line in LRDs. As
shown in Fig. 8, we also detect the fainter Paδ line in our stack.
These stacks are made in the rest-frame, correcting for differ-
ences in luminosity distance but without further normalisation.
For LRDs, the Paδ/Paγ ratio is ≈0.6, which is in line with case B
recombination expectations indicative of little dust attenuation.

Additionally, the evolution of the shape of the LF can pro-
vide key insights into the physical properties of LRDs. Recently,
Ma et al. (2025b) argued that the steep drop of the bright end
of the LRD LF at z ≈5 suggests that LRDs are a population
of relatively low mass AGNs with uniformly high Eddington ra-
tios (see also Tucci & Volonteri 2017 and Zhang et al. 2023 for
similarly steep bolometric LFs for the sub-set of AGN powered
by low mass SMBHs in their models). At z ≈2, the knee of
the galaxy stellar mass function has evolved significantly com-
pared to z ≈5 (Leja et al. 2020; Chaikin et al. 2026), while the
number density of galaxies with stellar masses ≈3 × 108 M⊙
(the typical host galaxy masses of LRDs; Maiolino et al. 2024;
Matthee et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2026) is relatively constant. This
evolution of the stellar mass function can explain the evolution
of the X-ray AGN number density: if X-ray AGNs are primarily
hosted by relatively massive galaxies with relatively high (≈108
M⊙) black hole masses that span a large range in Eddington ra-
tios, we expect a relatively flat AGN LF whose normalization
increases rapidly within this redshift interval (as observed).
The fact that there are fewer LRDs at z ≈2 than at z ≈5,
while there could in principle be similar number of galaxies host-
ing them, suggests that stellar mass is not the key driver of the
presence of LRDs. As can be seen in Fig. 9, we find indications
that the faint-end of the LF evolves less than the bright end. At
z ≈2, LRDs have a ∼4 times lower number density at faint
magnitudes than at z ≈5, whereas the difference is ≈10 times
at M5100 ≈−21. Although this could be due to possible incom-
pleteness issues in measurements of the faint-end at z ≈5, it
could imply a decrease in the characteristic luminosity of LRDs
at lower redshifts, which could be linked to the available (metal-
poor) gas content to fuel LRD activity (e.g. Baggen et al. 2026;
Maiolino et al. 2026). A more detailed investigation of such
changes requires better constraints of the faint and bright-ends
of the z ≈2–5 LRD LF than currently are available. The upcom-
ing Roman telescope is excellently suited to measure the bright
end of the LRD LF at z ≈1–2 and constrain the rapid evolution
from z ≈1–2. JWST spectroscopy will be required to improve
constraints on the faint end, either by extending an analysis sim-
ilar to ours to lensing cluster fields, or by surveying specifically
for the intrinsically stronger Hα line, which at z ≈1–2 can be
identified in medium-band data with NIRCam (such as available
through the MINERVA survey; Muzzin et al. 2025) or NIRISS
slitless spectroscopy (e.g. Iani et al. 2026).
7. Summary
In this paper we used deep JWST/NIRCam imaging and wide
field slitless spectroscopic data from the EIGER survey (Kashino
et al. 2023) to identify a sample of little red dots at z ≈2. Our
main results are the following:
– Out of a sample of 54 blindly identified broad-line emit-
ters across redshifts z = 0.8 −5.0, we here focus on the 19
broad-line emitters at z = 1.55 −3.18 selected based on the
rest-frame near-infrared (Paβ, OI and He i) lines. The clas-
sification between LRDs and non-LRDs is partly based on
the spectral energy distribution (with limited 3-5 band cov-
erage) and the He i and Paγ line-profile. Five of these are
classified as LRDs at z = 1.93 −3.18, whereas the others are
AGNs with classical (obscured) quasar-like characteristics.
The non-LRDs generally are more luminous, are more spa-
tially extended and some show X-ray detections. [Section 3,
Table 1, Figs. 2 and 5]
– Based on template-fitting, we estimate that the LRDs have
Balmer break strengths encompassing the range observed at
high-redshift, fν,4050/fν,3650 ≈1−4. The broad-lines originate
from a point-source, but separate clumps and a resolved rest-
frame UV morphology are found in the majority. [Section
4.1, Figures 3 and 4]
– We find that the broad wings in the He i and Paγ line-profiles
(in one case also Paβ) have typical gaussian FWHM of
1400 −2100 km s−1, with broader lines found in the Paschen
lines and blue-shifted He i absorption detected in the two red-
dest sources in the sample. Similar to other studies, the He i
absorbing gas has stronger blue-shifts than the shifts usu-
ally found in Balmer absorption lines. We also show that the
He i/Paγ line ratio clearly distinguishes LRDs from classical
AGNs, with a lower He i/Paγ ratio associated to a stronger
Balmer break strength, probably tracing stronger He i self-
absorption in higher column density regimes. [Sections 4.2
and 4.4, Figures 6 and 7]
– The LRDs span a similar optical luminosity range as high-
redshift LRDs with M5100 ≈−21. Above luminosities of
M5100 ≲−23 virtually all broad-line sources are classical
AGNs within our surveyed volume, suggesting that the LRD
fraction among broad-line sources is strongly luminosity-
dependent. Assuming recently derived bolometric conver-
sions applicable to LRDs, our sample spans bolometric lu-
minosities from (2.3 −33.8) × 1043 erg s−1 that corresponds
to masses of (2 −27) × 105 M⊙at the Eddington luminosity.
[Section 4.3]
– We measure the luminosity function of the LRDs and non-
LRDs and we find number densities ≈7 × 10−6 cMpc−3 at
z = 1.9−2.5. These independently confirm the reported drop
in the number density of LRDs compared to z ≈5, albeit
with a somewhat more gentle pace. At fixed bolometric lu-
minosity, LRDs only span a small (≲3 %) sub-set of the
AGN population at z ≈2. [Section 5, Table 2, Figures 9, 10
and 11]
Our discovery of a new sample of emission-line selected
LRDs at z ≈2 is an important step towards better understanding
the nature of these sources. In the future, follow-up spectroscopy
of these sources will enable more precise characterization of
their basic properties as Balmer break strengths and Balmer
line luminosities. The opportunity to cover the rest-frame near-
infrared with sensitive NIRSpec spectroscopy enables detailed
studies that are much more challenging at z ∼5, such as mea-
surements of gravity-sensitive features like CaT absorption (Lin
et al. 2025a) as well as joint studies of Paschen and Balmer
decrements (e.g. Reddy et al. 2026) that would enable us to
distinguish radiative transfer effects from dust attenuation (e.g.
Chang et al. 2026). Our results also motivate the extension of
this methodology to the faint and bright regimes using data over
lensing clusters and wide areas, respectively. The current data
suggest a very rapid evolution below z < 2 that can be con-
strained better with slitless spectroscopy with the Roman Space
