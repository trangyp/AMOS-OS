Abstract. We develop the basic notions of commutative algebra and algebraic geometry over the
field with one element F1, working within the Connes-Consani framework, which models F1-algebras
as monoid objects in the category of Γ-sets. In this setting, F1-algebras generalize commutative
rings by encoding the algebraic structure functorially, using machinery originating in homotopy
theory. Our main contribution is a theory of localization for F1-algebras and the construction of
prime spectrum Spec A for a commutative F1-algebra A. We then prove that Γ(X, OX) = A for
any absolute affine scheme X = Spec A and establish an anti-equivalence between the category of
commutative F1-algebras and the category of absolute affine schemes.

scheme Spec R consists of the prime spectrum | Spec R| (the set of prime ideals with the Zariski
topology) equipped with a structure sheaf OR of commutative rings. The contravariant functor
Spec : CRingop →AffSchZ establishes an anti-equivalence of categories.

In [Lur18], Lurie further extends this picture to spectral algebraic geometry, where commutative

• OA is a sheaf of E∞-rings obtained by localizing A with respect to elements in π0A
Our goal in this paper is to develop an analogous theory for absolute algebraic geometry—the
geometry over the “field with one element” F1, following Connes and Consani’s framework [CC16],
which models F1-algebras as monoid objects in the category of Segal’s Γ-sets, or discrete Γ-spaces.

In spectral algebraic geometry, π0(−) extracts the underlying commutative ring from an E∞-ring.
In absolute algebraic geometry, the functor −(1+) extracts the underlying pointed commutative
monoid from an F1-algebra. This analogy guides our construction.
For a commutative F1-algebra A, we define the prime spectrum as a locally F1Alg-ed space:
Spec A := (| Spec A|, OA)
where:
• | Spec A| is the prime spectrum of the underlying pointed commutative monoid A(1+) in
the sense of Deitmar [Dei05]
• OA is a sheaf of F1-algebras obtained by localizing A with respect to elements in A(1+)
Two key technical challenges arise in developing this theory. First, we must carefully choose
the correct notion of prime ideal. Unlike in classical ring theory—or even in hyperring theory—we
disregard any additive closure conditions in our definition of prime ideals. This is necessary when
working with F1-algebras since the additive structure encoded by a Γ-set is far looser than that of
a ring or hyperring, and imposing additive closure leads to pathological behavior (see Remark 5.11
for a detailed discussion). Our approach thus follows Deitmar’s monoid-theoretic spectrum, which
considers only the multiplicative structure of A(1+).
Second, constructing the structure sheaf requires developing a theory of localization for F1-
algebras. Unlike classical localization of rings, where one simply inverts elements, localizing an
F1-algebra is more delicate: the algebra A is not merely a monoid but a Γ-set—a functorial object
carrying coherent information across all levels. The localization process must therefore respect
this entire structure, inverting elements of A(1+) while preserving the Γ-set functoriality at higher
levels.

B(1+).

2. Background
The concept of a geometry over the hypothetical “field with one element”, can be traced back to
the work of Jacques Tits, through the study of spherical buildings [Tit56]. Since then, a number
of approaches have been proposed to make this notion precise, notably Deitmar’s monoid schemes
[Dei05], Lorscheid’s blueprints [Lor12], Connes-Consani’s Γ-sets [CC16, CC21].
In this section, we review the necessary background on Γ-sets and F1-algebras, as well as Deit-
mar’s theory of monoid schemes.

base point 0).

• A Γ-set A
• Multiplication µ : A ∧A →A
• Unit η : F1 →A
satisfying associativity and unit axioms. An F1-algebra is commutative if multiplication commutes
with the symmetry isomorphism.

algebraic objects for absolute algebraic geometry, or algebraic geometry over F1, in precise analogy
with the role of commutative rings in classical algebraic geometry. The anti-equivalence allows us
to translate between algebraic questions about F1-algebras and geometric questions about absolute
affine schemes.
Example 6.4 (The absolute point). The simplest absolute affine scheme is the absolute point
Spec F1. We have:
| Spec F1| = SpecDeitmar(F1(1+)) = SpecDeitmar({0, 1}) = {p0}
where p0 = {0} is the unique prime ideal of the pointed monoid {0, 1} (with 0 as absorbing element
and 1 as identity).
The structure sheaf satisfies:
OF1({p0}) ∼= F1.
The absolute point Spec F1 is the terminal object in AffSchF1: for any absolute affine scheme
Spec A, there exists a unique morphism Spec A →Spec F1, corresponding to the unit map F1 →A.

7. Base change to classical schemes
In [Xu26a], an adjunction between −⊗F1 Z and H was established through an exposition on the
associativity of the additive operation in a Γ-set. Explicitly, −⊗F1 Z is the functor given by:
A ⊗F1 Z := Z[A(1+)]/R
where R is the ideal generated by the following relations:
(i) (Basepoint relation) [∗] = 0.
(ii) (Additivity relations) [a] + [b] = [c] for all a, b, c ∈A(1+) with c ∈a ⊕b. (See Remark 5.11
for the operation ⊕)
and morphisms canonically determined by universal property. One can interpret the Eilenberg-
Maclane functor H as a forgetful/restriction of scalars functor, and −⊗F1 Z as an extension of
scalars functor from F1 to Z.
Via the anti-equivalence of Theorem 6.2, this further induces an adjunction at the level of affine
schemes:
Theorem 7.1. The extension of scalars functor defined above induces a base change functor:
−⊗F1 Z : AffSchF1 →AffSchZ
defined by:
Spec A 7→Spec(A ⊗F1 Z)
for any commutative F1-algebra A.
This functor is right adjoint to the restriction functor induced by the Eilenberg-MacLane functor:
H : AffSchZ →AffSchF1,
Spec R 7→Spec(HR).
Explicitly, there is a natural bijection:
HomAffSchZ(Spec R, Spec(A ⊗F1 Z)) ∼= HomAffSchF1(Spec(HR), Spec A).
Proof. By Theorem 6.2, morphisms of affine schemes correspond to morphisms of their coordinate
algebras (contravariantly). Therefore:
HomAffSchZ(Spec R, Spec(A ⊗F1 Z)) ∼= HomCRing(A ⊗F1 Z, R)
∼= HomF1Alg(A, HR)
∼= HomAffSchF1(Spec(HR), Spec A).
□

Example 7.2 (Base change of the absolute point).
(Spec F1) ⊗F1 Z = Spec(F1 ⊗F1 Z) = Spec Z.
The absolute point becomes the terminal object Spec Z under base change.
