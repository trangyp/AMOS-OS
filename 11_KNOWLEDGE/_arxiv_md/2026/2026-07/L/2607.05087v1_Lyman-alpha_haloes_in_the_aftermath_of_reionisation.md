ABSTRACT
We present a comparative study of Lyα haloes (LAHs) around low-luminosity (LLyα ≲1042 erg s−1) Lyα-emitting galaxies (LAEs) at
very high redshifts z ≥6 and a reference sample at z ∼3 covering a similar Lyα luminosity and host galaxy stellar mass range. Using
data from the Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE) at the ESO VLT, we extracted the samples such that at the different redshifts
we obtain the same intrinsic surface brightness sensitivity, accounting for cosmological dimming. We detect extended Lyα emission
around 6 out of 18 high-z LAEs in the MUSE eXtremely Deep Field (MXDF), more than doubling the number of known such objects
at z ≥6. We obtain an only slightly higher individual LAH detection fraction of 40% among the lower redshift comparison sample.
Yet the typical exponential scale lengths at z ≥6 are three times smaller than those at z ∼3. Stacking the LAEs with undetected haloes
gives again drastically different results for the two samples, with a highly significant halo detection at z ∼3 but no trace of extended
Lyα emission at z ≥6. We also find the Lyα spectral line widths of the high-z sample to be ∼2.5 smaller in comparison to the lower
redshift objects. We discuss the potential mechanisms driving such strong changes. In a reionisation-driven scenario the higher neutral
fraction in the intergalactic and circumgalactic media might lead to substantial scattering losses of escaping Lyα radiation, leaving
detectable only emission from the vicinity of the star-forming regions. In an alternative scenario the LAH properties might be linked
more closely to the evolution of their host galaxies than previously thought.

1. Introduction
The Lyα emission line is one of the principal tracers of star-
forming galaxies at high redshifts(Partridge & Peebles 1967).
It is now well established that this emission is not confined
to the sites of actual star formation, but often extends far
beyond the stellar components of galaxies. Typically more
than half, and sometimes even more than 90% of the total
Lyα luminosity of a galaxy emerges from this circumgalac-
tic region (Wisotzki et al. 2016, Leclercq et al. 2017 hereafter
cited as LW16 and FL17, respectively). The physical mecha-
nisms to produce these Lyα haloes (LAHs) can be diverse, but
the dominant process is probably recombination radiation fol-
lowed by resonant scattering at H i clouds in the circumgalac-
tic medium (CGM) (e.g., Verhamme et al. 2006, Kusakabe et al.
2019, Mitchell et al. 2021, Byrohl et al. 2021). LAHs are there-
fore valuable tracers of the cool and partly neutral CGM at high
redshifts.
Recent years have seen a tremendous push of the observa-
tional frontier towards earlier cosmic epochs and into the epoch
of reionisation. The visibility of Lyα in galaxies at very high
redshifts is now one of the key probes of the rapidly evolving H i
content of the Universe (e.g., Ouchi et al. 2020, Napolitano et al.
2024, Runnholm et al. 2025). While the internal production of
Lyα radiation depends mainly on the star formation rate, its es-
cape and propagation along the line of sight can be greatly re-

duced through scattering. Predicting the shape and the spatial
extent of Lyα emission during reionisation is challenging (e.g.,
Ahn & Shapiro 2021, Melia 2024, Kageura et al. 2025 and ref-
erences therein), but qualitatively, an immediate consequence of
a more neutral medium surrounding a Lyα -bright galaxy would
be to disperse its line emission, both spatially and spectrally. A
large neutral fraction in the CGM or in the adjacent intergalac-
tic medium (IGM) would therefore cause the solid angle of Lyα
emission to spread out and render the emission essentially invis-
ible (unless the system resides in a substantial ionized bubble;
e.g., Witstok et al. 2025). On the other hand, an only moderately
increased abundance of neutral gas in the outskirts of a galaxy at
the end of reionisation might lead to just a more extended LAH,
still observable albeit with lower surface brightness.
Measurements of the prominence and properties of Lyα
haloes could thus provide additional constraints on the reion-
isation process and topology, especially its late stages at
z ∼6–7. Observational data on LAHs at these redshifts are
however scarce, with currently only very few known haloes

faint LAEs at z ≥6 and measure the properties of such emis-

z ∼3, taking great care to eliminate possible biases arising from
redshift-dependent differences in the sensitivity to intrinsic sur-

2. Data and sample construction
Since our goal is to compare the properties of Lyα haloes at two
very different redshift ranges, cosmological surface-brightness
dimming is a major issue, with a factor ∼10 loss in effective
sensitivity between z ∼3 and z ∼6. The typical exponential
scale length of a LAH with luminosity LLyα ∼1042 L⊙at z = 3
is only ∼4 kpc (FL17), comparable to the size of the ground-
based point spread function (PSF) even under good conditions.
Observing a halo of the same intrinsic properties but at z ≥6,
using the same instrumental setup and depth, would lead to a
2× smaller isophotal radius and complete noise dominance over
any real extended emission at >∼1–2 scale lengths. These effects
taken together could make all the difference for the detectability
of Lyα haloes and must therefore be taken into account.
In the following we describe our approach to address this is-
sue by comparing two samples at different redshifts that were
extracted from observational data matched in intrinsic (i.e., cor-
rected for cosmological dimming) surface brightness sensitiv-
ity. For both samples we use observations conducted with the
Multi-Unit Spectroscopic Explorer (MUSE; Bacon et al. 2010)
mounted on the ESO Very Large Telescope (VLT)). MUSE is a
panoramic integral field spectrograph with an instantaneous field
of view of 1′ × 1′ at 0′′.2 × 0′′.2 sampling and a wavelength range
from 4700 Å to 9350 Å. The corresponding redshift range in Lyα
is z = 2.87 to 6.67. One MUSE spectral pixel is 1.25 Å and the
spectral resolution is ∼2.6 Å, depending weakly on wavelength.
All MUSE datasets used in this study benefit from improved im-
age quality from Ground-Layer Adaptive Optics (GLAO), result-
ing in a PSF full width at half maximum of typically better than
0′′.5.
2.1. High-redshift (z ≥6) sample
We selected our high-redshift sample from the MUSE eXtremely
Deep Field (MXDF, Bacon et al. 2023), a blind spectroscopic
survey consisting of many exposures in a single pointing in-
side the Hubble Ultra Deep Field (HUDF). The MXDF has a
coadded exposure time of 141 hours over a circular field of
view of 1′ in diameter and reaches a 3σ surface brightness
limit of ∼1.0 × 10−19 ergs−1 cm−2 arcsec−2 in a 1′′ × 1′′ aper-
ture for an unresolved emission line around 7300 Å. Our sam-
ple was drawn from the AMUSED catalogue and database by
Bacon et al. (2023)1, comprising altogether 2221 objects in the
HUDF with spectroscopically measured redshifts. The location
of the MXDF inside the HUDF implies that extensive space-
based imaging data is also available (see Sect. 2.3).

2.3. Rest-frame UV data
In addition to the MUSE observations we used space-based
imaging at rest-frame UV wavelengths to estimate the stellar
continuum properties of the galaxies in our study. For the high-
redshift sample we exploited the JWST Advanced Deep Ex-
tragalactic Survey (JADES Eisenstein et al. 2023, Bunker et al.
2024, D’Eugenio et al. 2025), which covers the entire footprint
of the MXDF with 11 filters ranging from F090W to F444W.
Among the available filters we chose the NIRCam F115W filter
as it is free from Lyα contamination and provides the deepest
observations in the rest-frame UV at z ∼6. For the z ∼3 sample
in the MUSCATEL-SF A2744 field we employed HST/ACS ob-
servations from the HFF project (Shipley et al. 2018), selecting
the ultradeep F814W band as the main reference for similar rea-
sons. For each dataset we also consulted the associated source
catalogue with photometric redshift estimates.
For each Lyα emitter in both samples we identified by visual
inspection the most likely UV counterpart (in 11/18 objects of
the MXDF and all of the MUSCATEL-SF sample), or decided
that no such counterpart could be detected (7/18 and 0/90, re-
spectively). For 8 objects in the lower-redshift sample and for
2 objects in the z ≥6 sample we found a clear UV source (al-
beit rather faint, mlim
F814W = 29.1 mag and mlim
F115W = 30.5 mag)
spatially coinciding with the Lyα emission, which however had
no record in the corresponding published catalogue. In all those
cases we assumed the uncatalogued UV source to be the coun-
terpart. For 7 of the 18 high-redshift LAEs we deemed the angu-
lar and/or photometric redshift distances between LAE centroid
and the closest UV source as too large (more than 5.5 kpc in all
cases), so that no counterpart was assigned. For the z ∼3 sample
we encountered no such case. We found a mean offset of 0′′.25
between Lyα and UV centroids for both samples, only slightly
more than one MUSE spaxel and in excellent agreement with
previous measurements (Claeyssens et al. 2022).
We used the available broadband photometric data also to
estimate stellar masses (M⋆) and star formation rates (SFR) by
fitting the spectral energy distributions (SEDs). As a baseline
we employed the FAST code (Kriek et al. 2009) with models
from the Bruzual & Charlot (2003) stellar library, assuming a
Chabrier IMF (Chabrier 2003) and an exponentially declining
star-formation history. FAST also estimates the dust attenuation
acting on the starlight, assuming a Calzetti et al. (2000) extinc-
tion curve. We run the SED fitting routine using all available
HST and JWST photometric data for all objects.
The choice of a specific star formation history (SFH) can sig-
nificantly influence SED derived stellar mass and SFR by alter-
ing the mass-to-light ratio (e.g. Lower et al. 2020). To explore
the effect of the adopted SFH on the estimated galaxy prop-
erties, we conducted the following study using Prospector.
Prospector is a Bayesian forward-modelling code developed
to derive the physical properties of galaxies from photometric
or spectroscopic data using Markov chain Monte Carlo sam-
pling. It uses FSPS stellar population models (Conroy et al.
2009, Conroy & Gunn 2010) with Chabrier IMF (Chabrier
2003), self-consistent nebular emission generated with Cloudy
as described in Byler et al. (2017), and dust attenuation law
with Calzetti et al. (2000) extinction curve as a default. The
main advantage of Prospector is a very flexible treatment
of SFHs: the built-in functions allow for various paramet-
ric models (such as τ and delayed-τ) and five non-parametric
ones (continuity, continuity_flex, continuity_psb,
dirichlet, and stochastic), which mainly vary in the result-
ing smoothness of the reconstructed SFH.

ies that uniformly probe the MUV distribution in each sample.

Prospector assuming six different star formation histories: τ
and delayed-τ, continuity, continuity_flex, dirichlet,
and stochastic2. We report that at z ≥6 the stellar masses in-

3. Detection of Lyα haloes
In order to study individual Lyα haloes we need to distin-
guish between objects with and without significantly detected
extended emission. In accordance with most previous work (e.g.,
LW16, FL17) we base this decision on a comparison with the
spatial extent of the stellar light distribution: If the SB distri-
bution of the Lyα emission is consistent with the distribution of
starlight (within the error bars and after accounting for PSF blur-
ring) we adopt the null hypothesis that the observed Lyα emis-
sion originates mainly in the ISM of the host galaxy, with no
evidence for a circumgalactic component due to scattered light
or added emission. Only if this null hypothesis is significantly
rejected do we take the Lyα halo as detected. As in our previous
LAH studies using MUSE (e.g. LW16 and FL17), a key ingredi-
ent to this test are the deep high-resolution space-based images
available in our fields, allowing us to constrain the stellar light
distribution much more accurately than possible from the MUSE
data alone.
3.1. Image construction
3.1.1. Lyα pseudo-narrowband images
For each object we extracted a 6′′.6×6′′.6 (33×33 MUSE spaxels)
pseudo-narrowband (NB) image from the corresponding MUSE
datacube. The image size was chosen so that we could detect
and model the extended Lyα emission of all objects of interest
while limiting the impact of nearby sources. We used the median
filter subtracted cubes (see LW16 for details) to approximately
remove the continuum of target galaxies and back-/foreground
sources.
The size of each narrowband was chosen manually to incor-
porate most (but not strictly all) of the Lyα flux in the band (see
example in Fig. 1). Since the blue peaks of Lyα emission lines
are increasingly lost to IGM absorption towards high redshifts
(e.g., Mason & Gronke 2020, Hayes et al. 2021) we treated our
z ∼3.2 objects similarly and deliberately excluded any blue
peak emission also in those. The resulting typical NB width for
both samples was 9 MUSE spectral pixels (11.25 Å). Examples
of constructed Lyα pseudo-narrowband images are shown in the
left column of Fig. 3.
3.1.2. UV continuum images and model fits
A typical Lyα emitter detected in MUSE is a low-mass star-
forming galaxy identified by its bright emission line, but with
a very faint and often tiny continuum counterpart. Using the
deepest available reference images (see Sect. 2.3) we extracted
matching 6′′.6 × 6′′.6 cutouts (220×220 pixels at the 0′′.03
sampling of the HST and JWST data). All sources except
the assigned UV counterpart were masked. We used GALFIT
(Peng et al. 2002, 2010) to model each object as a linear com-
bination of standard functions (point source, exponential, Sérsic
profile) convolved with the PSF. For the MXDF sample we used
a simulated JWST NIRCam PSF from the JWST PSF simula-
tion library3. For the HST data of the MUSCATEL sample we
assumed a symmetric Gaussian PSF with FWHM adopted from
Shipley et al. (2018). Single component models were sufficient
to fit the UV counterpart of all the z ≥6 objects. Of the lower
redshift sample 61/90 objects could be modelled with just one
component, 20 required two, 7 were modelled as three- and 2 as
four-component systems. The 7 high-z objects undetected in the
continuum were treated as point sources in the remaining anal-
ysis. Second and third columns of Fig. 3 show examples of the
UV continuum images and models after resampling to MUSE
resolution and convolving with the MUSE PSF.
3.2. Detection procedure
We now describe our LAH detection test procedure. In contrast
to previous studies, our null hypothesis test for the presence of
extended Lyα emission is based on a spaxel-to-spaxel evalua-
tion of the light distribution, combined with an empirical self-
calibration of the noise properties using empty regions. For each
object we performed the following steps:
1. We determined the centroid and flux scaling amplitude of
the resampled and convolved UV continuum model to best
match the Lyα NB data. For the centroid we fitted the
NB image with a 2D Gaussian using the photutils package
(Bradley et al. 2025). We then calculated the scaling factor
for the continuum model placed at this location by χ2 min-
imisation and record the corresponding value of χ2
2. Next we defined a list of empty fields in the NB image of the
full datacube. To do so we first smoothed the image with a
Gaussian kernel with σ of 1.5 pixels and constructed a binary
mask to hide extended sources. Recall that the NB images
were already continuum-subtracted using the prior median-
filtered datacube, so this masking step mainly marked emis-
sion line sources (in particular by foreground [O ii] emitters),
but also cases of poor continuum subtraction. We projected a
rectangular grid with a spacing of 16 pixels onto the image,
and at each location we tested if a small (3′′.3 × 3′′.3) NB im-
age centred on this position intersected with the mask. Only
if there was no such intersection did we record the location
as an empty region. The resulting number of empty regions
per object was around 300 in the MXDF and between 1500
and 2000 in the MUSCATEL-SF footprint.
3. To perform the null hypothesis test, we required a robust es-
timate of the noise inside each of the investigated apertures.
While the “effective noise” approach (Urrutia et al. 2019)
provides a good approximation for large-aperture measure-
ments, it overestimates the noise level for the spaxel-by-
spaxel comparison within small apertures around our LAEs.
We therefore self-calibrated the goodness-of-fit test by the
following iteration over the grid of empty regions, object
by object: We inserted a copy of the UV continuum model
scaled to the observed Lyα flux at each grid point and then re-
determined the best centroid and matching scale factor as de-
scribed above. After that we subtracted the scaled UV model
from this mock field. Combining the residuals in a single
empty region provided a χ2 value (note that the noise level
for faint objects in MUSE is entirely dominated by the back-
ground with a minor contribution of detector readout noise;

3.3. Results
3.3.1. Radial profiles
To visualise the spatial distribution of Lyα emission, we ex-
tracted azimuthally averaged surface brightness profiles in con-
centric circular annuli for the objects in our sample. Our annuli
have radial increments of 1 MUSE pixel (0′′.2) to properly sam-
ple the inner LAH regions which are strongly affected by PSF
blurring. Taking advantage of the procedure outlined in Sect. 3.2,
we also estimated self-calibrated error bars for the SB profiles by
constructing SB profiles of the empty fields using the same an-
nuli and calculated the interquartile range (IQR) of all profiles
in each radial bin. The values of IQR/1.34 were then adopted as
1σ uncertainty at each radius. We used the IQR statistic rather
than standard deviations as uncertainty estimator because of its
greater robustness against outliers. The thus derived error bars
are typically 1.2–1.7 × smaller than the ones formally propa-
gated from the effective variances, but for objects located near
strong sky emission lines (relevant for several of the z ≥6 ob-
jects) this ratio increases up to 5.
3.3.2. LAH detection fractions
Before the final assessment of which objects have significantly
detected LAHs we performed a visual inspection of all Lyα NB
images and SB profiles to check for the integrity of the formal
detections. We found and discarded four z ∼3.2 objects with
high relative χ2 values which however appeared to be driven by
sky or continuum subtraction residuals. After this last cleaning
step we count 40/90 ((44 ± 5)%) detected LAHs at z ∼3.2 and
6/18 ((33 ± 11)%) at z ≥6. As our study was designed to com-
pensate for cosmological SB dimming, we a priori expect that
the detection rate should depend mainly on the intrinsic promi-
nence of LAHs in these two redshift domains. The fact that the
LAH fractions are not statistically significantly different might
be taken as indication that also the typical LAH properties are
similar. We demonstrate in Sect. 4 below that this is however not
the case.
At face value our LAH detection rates are much lower than
the ∼80% reported in LW16 and FL17. For the redshift z ∼3.2
comparison sample this can be easily explained by the reduced
sensitivity of our current data compared to the much deeper ex-
posures used for those studies. FL17 demonstrated in their Ap-
pendix how a factor 3 in exposure time already makes a big
difference for measuring LAH properties. Our MUSCATEL-SF
data have a 6× shorter exposure time than the UDF-Mosaic and
20× shorter than the UDF-10 and HDFS used by FL17 and
LW16. Our stacking analysis (see Sect. 4.3) indeed demonstrates
that the non-detections among our z ∼3.2 objects typically have
perfectly normal extended LAHs, which are just a little bit too
faint to be detected individually at the sensitivity of the MUS-
CATEL shallow fields.
For our high redshift sample the comparison to LW16 and
FL17 is not so easy to perform. On the one hand the effects
of cosmological dimming make the intrinsic SB sensitivity (see
Fig. 2) of the MXDF at z ≥6 lower than that of the UDF-10 at
z < 5.5 and of the UDF-Mosaic at z < 4, implying that a some-
what lower detection fraction should be expected. On the other
hand, there could also be real differences in the properties of
LAHs at these highest redshifts. In the following we show that a
more detailed comparison of the LAH properties in our two sam-
ples, including a stacking analysis, strongly suggests that this is
indeed so, and that even deeper data than the MXDF would prob-
ably not result in a much higher LAH detection rate at z ≥6.
4. Lyα halo properties
4.1. Surface brightness profiles and modelling
We now proceed to determine the main properties of our detected
LAHs. In line with previous LAH studies (LW16, FL17) we ap-
proximate the Lyα emission as the sum of a central component
that follows the spatial distribution of the stellar UV continuum
and an extended halo component that is usually well described
by an exponential surface brightness profile.
For the central component models we adopted our HST- and
JWST-based UV continuum fits from Sect. 3.1.2. For each object
we reconstructed the stellar light distribution in the UV, con-
volved with the MUSE PSF at the corresponding wavelength,
keeping all parameters fixed except for the total Lyα flux of
the component (Fc). The halo component was taken as a single
exponential with four parameters: scale length (rs,H), axis ratio
(qH), position angle (PA) and the total flux (FH). To fit the model
to the Lyα NB images, we again used GALFIT, with the addi-
tional constraint of forcing both components to have the same
centroid. The fit was performed within a circular aperture of 10–
14 pixels in radius, depending somewhat on the extent of the
halo, plus a mask to avoid projected contaminating objects if
required. For 6 LAHs in the z ∼3 sample we had to force circu-
larity of the halo for the fit to converge. In the case of the z ≥6
objects, all halo fits converged with q kept free (but recall that
only 6/18 objects show a detectable LAH). From these fits we
calculated the halo flux fractions, fH = FH/(FH + Fc).
Fig. 4 compares the main halo properties of the z ∼3 and
z ≥6 samples. Evidently, the two sets of objects have very
similar distributions of fH and qH; a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test
gives p values of 0.98 and 0.64 that both are drawn from the
same parent distribution. (See Appendix Fig. B.1 for a version
of the left plot that includes upper limits for the individually un-
detected LAHs). There is a slight excess of relatively dim LAHs
(fH <∼0.5) in the z ∼3 sample, which comes primarily from ob-
jects where halo circularity was forced, indicating a potentially
unreliable fit. On the other hand, Fig. 4 reveals a clear difference
in the resulting halo scale lengths between the two samples: The
LAHs at high redshifts are much more compact than their z ∼3
counterparts, with the latter having typical scale lengths ∼3 times
larger than the former. Moreover, 39 out of 40 z ∼3 LAHs have
scale lengths larger than the mean (or median) of the z ≥6 ob-
jects in the MXDF halo, and 29/40 are more extended than any
of the high-redshift haloes.
Comparing our lower redshift measurements with the re-
sults of LW16 and FL17 – obtained with much deeper data –
we find that the recovered distributions of LAH properties at
z ∼3 are in full agreement (see also Fig. 8 below). Our median
halo scale length for the MUSCATEL sample is 5.3 ± 1.8 kpc
(with the error bar representing the standard uncertainty of the
median), while that value is 4.7 ± 0.6 kpc in the LW16 and
4.2 ± 1.5 kpc in the FL17 samples, for the same redshift inter-
val 3 < z < 3.4. These values are statistically consistent with
each other, although slightly higher in our data. That is, however,
naturally explained by the lower sensitivity of MUSCATEL-

4.2. Characteristics of Lyα haloes at z ≥6
The six spatially resolved LAHs in our high-redshift sample
more than double the number of known individual LAH detec-
tions around star-forming galaxies at z > 6. We present them in
Fig. 5 and give their main structural parameters in Table 2. In
the following we briefly comment on each source separately, in
order of increasing redshift:
– MXDF#8211 (z = 6.00) has the lowest Lyα luminosity
among all known LAHs to date. It shows no continuum coun-
terpart in any of the available deep JWST images, implying
a high Lyα equivalent width suggestive of a very metal-poor
stellar population, a dust-free interstellar medium, and/or
possibly contributions from other power sources than central
photoionization (e.g., Cullen et al. 2020, Saxena et al. 2023).
– MXDF#8145 (z = 6.04) is located at the edge of the MXDF
footprint and therefore has a significantly lower signal-to-
noise ratio. In fact, a 1D SB profile test would have classified
this object as not extended despite it showing clear signs of
extended emission. Our self-calibrated 2D test reveals a Lyα
halo around this source (p0 = 0.03). It also shows no de-
tectable counterpart in JWST. Notably, our image modelling
with GALFIT prefers a single exponential with no separate
central component.
– MXDF#7610 (z = 6.09) has a very similar Lyα scale length
as VR7 (Matthee et al. 2020b), but at a ∼40× lower Lyα
luminosity. The UV counterpart of VR7 is even ∼300×
brighter and 2.4× more extended that that of MXDF#7610.
This highlights the diversity of the relation between galaxy
and LAH properties. The inferred stellar metallicity of
the UV counterpart (both with FAST and Prospector) is
amongst the lowest in the z ≥6 sample.
– MXDF#7614 (z = 6.31) is located at the edge of the MXDF
full exposure region, and its redshift places the Lyα line
very close in wavelength to a night sky emission line. Due
to these restrictions the halo is only detected with our self-
calibration method. The halo is notably elongated. This is
the most extreme high-z halo detected in the study. It is
very similar to CR7 in terms of both line width and halo
size (but not the Lyα luminosity). Moreover, this object is
the only z ≥6 LAH similar to the average z ∼3 halo
in the MUSCATEL sample. This is the only object in our
z ≥6 sample with available rest-frame spectroscopy from the
JADES DR4 (Curtis-Lake et al. 2026, Scholtz et al. 2026).
This means we can measure its Lyα velocity offset from sys-
temic redshift ∆vLyα = c (zLyα −zsys)/(1 + zsys), where zLyα is
the redshift estimated from the Lyα peak position, and zsys –
from rest-optical non resonant lines. We obtain the value of
220 ± 55 km s−1. Assuming Case-B recombination we calcu-
late the Lyα escape fraction (f Lyα

UV magnitude (e.g., Saxena et al. 2024, Tang et al. 2024,

– MXDF#7699 (z = 6.63) has the most compact halo detected
in our sample despite being one of the most luminous ob-
jects. With a scale length of 1.2 kpc it is only marginally
resolved, helped by the small error bars due to its relative
brightness. The UV counterpart resembles an elongated U-
warped edge-on galaxy, making the Lyα to UV size ratio one
of the lowest recorded.
– MXDF#8214 (z = 6.63) is our highest redshift detected
LAH. The NB image suggests a second emission source
∼2′′ away, which was however masked and therefore did
not influence the Lyα halo detection (indicated by the grey
band in the rightmost panel of Fig. 5). The secondary source
is too faint for an independent classification, but if it is Lyα,
then it would have a velocity offset of ≲300 km s−1 from
MXDF#8214. It also has a weak JWST counterpart with

companion at a projected distance of ∼12 kpc. The extended
LAH of MXDF#8214 may thus be enhanced by gravitational
interaction with its companion.

5. Spectral properties

halo differences, we investigated the Lyα emission line spec-

known to correlate with Lyα halo properties, both from a the-

empirically (FL17, Leclercq et al. 2020) We fitted the red peak

line widths (defined as the full width at half maximum, FWHM)

broadening removed.

6. Discussion
In the previous sections we presented evidence that the Lyα halo
and also the spectral properties of our two samples of LAEs are
very different between z ≥6 and z ∼3. We now discuss the
implications of this finding in comparison with the literature and
consider possible explanations.
6.1. How different are Lyα halo scale lengths at different
redshifts?
We start with a brief review of the available evidence for
and against major changes in the observed properties of Lyα
haloes within the redshift range 3 <∼z <∼6. We focus here
on LAHs around normal star-forming galaxies, leaving out the
much brighter, much larger, and often AGN-related Lyα “nebu-
lae” and “blobs”. Figure 8 shows a compilation of measured Lyα
halo scale lengths from the literature plotted against redshift, in-
cluding our new data. This diagram illustrates the conflicting
trends seen in previous LAH observations: The stacking study
by Momose et al. (2014) based on several thousands of LAEs in
Subaru NB images found that the typical scale lengths stay es-
sentially unchanged between z = 2.2, 3.1, and 5.7, with values
around ∼5–10 kpc, and rise to rs,H ≃12.6 kpc at z = 6.6. In con-
trast, Wu et al. (2020), using similar data but a 3× larger sample,
measured a much smaller mean scale length of only ∼2 kpc at
z = 5.7. Kakuma et al. (2021) did not determine scale lengths,
but reported no systematic differences in the stacked Lyα radial
profiles of LAEs between z = 5.7 and 6.6 in their Subaru-HSC
images.
The advent of individual LAH measurements with MUSE
did initially not bring much more clarity. The first such study by
LW16 of 21 LAHs in the Hubble Deep Field South suggested
a tentative evolutionary trend that Lyα haloes might evolve to-
wards gradually smaller sizes with increasing redshifts up to
z <∼5.7, but this trend was not deemed significant given the
small sample. And indeed the nearly 10× larger follow-up study
by FL17 based on the MUSE-MOSAIC sample (Bacon et al.
2017) did not confirm such a trend, but concluded that LAH scale
lengths do not change systematically within 3 <∼z <∼6. Guo et al.
(2024b) found the same behaviour with stacking MUSE LAEs in
bins of redshift from 3 to 6. That study also presented the first
z ≥6 LAH measurement with rs,H ≃3 kpc. More recently, tar-
geted MUSE observations of the outstandingly bright z = 6.6
Lyα emitter CR7 (Matthee et al. 2020a) and of the UV-luminous
Lyman Break Galaxy VR7 at z = 6.5 (Matthee et al. 2020b) gave
similar rs,Hvalues of around 3 kpc.
A problem with all previous investigations is that results at
different redshifts were obtained with roughly similar apparent
SB sensitivities. After accounting for cosmological dimming this
implies vastly different intrinsic SB detection limits between dif-
ferent redshift domains, which could have led to z-dependent
biases in the estimation of LAH properties. Other possible ob-
stacles to a proper comparison of results between studies are
differences in the angular resolution of the data, as well as in-
compatible analysis methods.
Here we aimed at accounting for all these effects in a consis-
tent way. In particular, as explained in Sect. 2.1 our data at z ∼3
and z ≥6 are fully matched in intrinsic surface brightness sen-
sitivity (at the expense of giving up on sensitivity for the lower
redshift range). The GLAO-improved PSF of the MUSE instru-
ment provided the currently sharpest available view of LAEs,
critical for our ability to constrain LAH scale lengths.
Figure 8 reveals that our results are strongly inconsistent
with the null hypothesis of no evolution of LAH scale lengths
from z ≥6 to z ∼3. Instead, we find that z ≥6 LAHs are much
smaller, by nearly a factor 3 in the mean, than their lower redshift
siblings. In the right panel of Fig. 4 we compare the distributions
of scale lengths of the detected haloes between our two samples.

shape and peak location. A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test rules out

artefact of drawing our LAE samples from very different parts of
the galaxy population, but physically real and highly significant.
6.2. Are Lyα haloes at z ≥6 smaller because of reionisation?
Since our high-redshift sample reaches into the late stages of
the cosmic reionisation epoch (Planck Collaboration et al. 2016,
Mangena et al. 2020, Umeda et al. 2026) which is known to af-
fect the visibility of Lyα (Dijkstra 2014), it is conceivable that
the changing conditions in the intergalactic and circumgalac-
tic media associated with this fundamental phase transition also
lead to different emerging Lyα haloes.
But how does a more neutral IGM and correspondingly
higher Lyα optical depth change the observed properties of
LAHs? For Lyα sources surrounded by a more or less homoge-
neous ambient medium following the Hubble flow the main ef-
fect will be to scatter the centrally emitted Lyα photons until they
are redshifted out of resonance (Dijkstra 2014). For the modest
optical depths of the post-reionisation period one would there-
fore expect that LAH scale lengths and Lyα line widths should
get systematically larger with increasing neutral fraction and
thus with redshift. When the reionisation epoch is reached this
mechanism should result in very extended Lyα haloes stretching
over intergalactic scales, with a strongly broadened line profile

(Loeb & Rybicki 1999, Park et al. 2021). Such haloes are proba-
bly unobservable individually due to their extremely low surface
brightnesses (but might be detectable in large-scale line intensity
mapping experiments; Padmanabhan & Loeb 2024), so that the
scattered Lyα photons would be essentially lost from the obser-
vational budget.
This simple picture changes however when taking into ac-
count that Lyα emission from galaxies in an incompletely reion-
ized Universe is detected mainly from sources in ionized bubbles
(e.g., Trapp et al. 2023, Fujimoto et al. 2024, Neyer et al. 2025).
The appearance of extended Lyα haloes will depend greatly on
the properties of their host bubbles, in particular their sizes,
which are still poorly constrained by observations. Numerical
simulations have so far only provided a few indications of what
to expect. In an early study, Smith et al. 2019 found that account-
ing for the evolving IGM transmission at 5 < z < 7 actually
steepens the emerging Lyα SB profiles and reduces their scale
lengths by about 20% compared to the no IGM model. From
their radiative transfer simulations, Park et al. (2021) concluded
that large bubbles with radii of several comoving Mpc facili-
tate easier escape of Lyα photons than much smaller ones where
some of the radiation would still diffuse to IGM scales and get
effectively lost. However, their simulations lacked the resolution
required to properly model the circumgalactic Lyα emission and
make actual predictions for Lyα halo properties.
In the light of these theoretical arguments it seems that our
observational results could indeed be interpreted, at least quali-
tatively, as driven by reionisation. In this scenario, most galaxies
would reside in ionized bubbles large enough to allow the cen-
tral emission to at least partly escape, but too small (or not suffi-
ciently ionized) to avoid the diffusion of a large fraction of Lyα
photons into the surrounding neutral regions, effectively sup-
pressing the formation of detectable Lyα haloes. The few cases
where we actually observe a resolved halo could then correspond
to a somewhat larger host bubble.
Furthermore, recent deep observations of Lyα haloes study-
ing radial evolution of the spectra discovered that the peak of
the red wing of the Lyα line progressively moves towards the
systemic velocity at larger radii (Gallego et al. 2021, Guo et al.
2024a, Mukherjee et al. 2026, Kozlova in prep.). Guo et al.
(2024a) found tentative evidence that beyond 20 kpc the red peak
even shifts blueward of the systemic velocity. If this behaviour is
real and ubiquitous at high-redshift, it would also affect the Lyα
halo sizes in the epoch of reionization. As the IGM transmission
sharply drops to zero blueward of the systemic velocity, most of
the blueshifted Lyα photons would get absorbed and/or scattered
away thus decreasing the observed SB at larger radii. This would
naturally produce more compact haloes at redshifts where IGM
is still significantly neutral. However, due to the lack of robustly
measured systemic redshifts and statistical nature of the study
such a scenario remains mostly speculative.
This scenario seems particularly plausible if for z < 6 the
properties of LAHs do not evolve much with redshift, at least
down to z ∼3, as suggested by the majority of previous studies
(Momose et al. 2014, FL17, Kakuma et al. 2021 see Sect. 6.1).
Note that this would imply a substantial amount of intrinsic evo-
lution to counteract the strong population evolution of galaxies.
Byrohl et al. (2021) investigated this question in a cosmologi-
cal context using the TNG50 simulation and found that self-
regulated feedback balancing inflows and outflows produces
nearly redshift-invariant LAH sizes. In such a framework, a sud-
den change in halo sizes for galaxies at z ≥6 would indeed have
to be attributed mainly to the effects of reionisation.
Could the observed narrow Lyα line widths of the LAEs in
our z ≥6 sample also be an effect of the late stages of reionisa-
tion? A qualitatively similar phenomenon was already noted by
Songaila et al. (2022) and Songaila et al. (2024) in a large spec-
troscopic follow-up study of bright LAEs selected from narrow-
band imaging. They showed that at fixed Lyα luminosity, the
Lyα line widths are on average smaller at z = 6.6 than at z = 5.7,
and that this difference is stronger for objects of lower Lyα lumi-
nosities. Since our LAE sample is much fainter than theirs, by a
full order of magnitude, we cannot compare directly, but extrap-
olating the trend from Songaila et al. (2024) between LLyα and
FWHMLyα to the luminosity range of our z ≥6 objects would
yield a good agreement with our measured line widths.
To explain this fast change in FWHM from z = 6.6 to 5.7,
Songaila et al. (2024) suggested that the Lyα line widths expe-
rienced different amounts of truncation of their blue wings due
to the rapidly evolving intervening Lyα Gunn-Peterson trough
and damping wing. If this is correct then the same should ap-
ply to our sample. We find, however, much smaller line widths
for our high-redshift objects, a decrease by a factor ∼2.5 com-
pared to z ∼3.2 (see Fig. 7). To achieve such a strong reduc-
tion in the emergent line width would require quite extreme con-
ditions, with negligible Lyα line offsets from systemic redshift
as well as very small ionized bubble size, which seems some-
what unlikely (Verhamme et al. 2018, Prieto-Lyon et al. 2023,
Saxena et al. 2024, Lu et al. 2024).
6.3. Or do Lyα halo sizes change in lockstep with galaxy
evolution?
As an alternative scenario, we now consider the hypothesis that
our finding of very different LAH sizes at z ∼3 and z ≥6 is just
a reflection of the overall evolution of galaxy properties between
these redshifts and not specifically connected to reionisation. In
that case the effects of LAH evolution should be observable not
just at z ≥6 but at all redshifts, contradicting some – but not all
– of the above mentioned studies. However, as already discussed
in Sect. 6.1, the many pitfalls and potential biases arising from
data quality and analysis aspects could so far have obscured the
true evolutionary behaviour.
As a starting point for such a halo co-evolution scenario,
we consider one of the most robust measured scaling rela-

tween Lyα and UV continuum scale lengths, rLyα

galaxies grow in size by a factor 1.7–2.3 over the redshift range

find, but close enough to explain the bulk of the halo size growth.

could thus simply be a consequence of the above correlation be-

However, the growth of the galaxies’ stellar bodies is directly
driven by and strongly coupled to the assembly of their dark mat-

properties (its extent most importantly) are tightly linked to the
dark matter halo and its virial parameters (e.g., Werk et al. 2014,
Bordoloi et al. 2018, Lochhaas et al. 2020, Cook et al. 2025).
Therefore, the growth of the dark matter haloes from z ≥6 to
z ∼3 would naturally explain the increase in the Lyα halo sizes.
To test this hypothesis, we estimated the virial radii of galax-
ies in both samples based on the halo masses derived in Sect. 5.
We find that at z ∼3 the typical virial radius in our sample is
2.6 times larger than at z ≥6 (35 kpc and 13 kpc, respectively), a
difference remarkably similar to the difference between the Lyα
scale lengths. We find the ratio between LAH scale length and
virial radius to be 0.16 across both samples. If our estimates of
the halo masses (and hence virial radiii) are correct, this simi-
larity would naturally imply that the growth of the Lyα haloes
is naturally directly tied to the assembly of the dark matter halo
reinforcing the gradual evolutionary scenario.
Since the spatial extent over which Lyα gets scattered by cir-
cumgalactic gas depends strongly on the H i fraction, we can
try to link the expected evolution of Lyα haloes to the general
H i content of the Universe. The latter is traced directly by the
incidence rate of damped Lyα absorbers (DLAs) that can be
observed in quasar spectra up to z ∼5 (but not beyond due
to the ever increasing obscuration by the Lyα forest). Earlier
measurements suggested that the cosmic H i density increases
steadily towards higher redshifts (Bird et al. 2017, Ho et al.
2021, Rhee et al. 2023; see also the review by Péroux & Howk
2020). In the absence of other effects, this would imply that Lyα
haloes would be larger at earlier cosmic times (see Sect. 6.2),
contrary to the size evolution trend of galaxies. Recently, how-
ever, Oyarzún et al. (2025) reported a maximum in the DLA in-
cidence rate at z ∼4 −4.5 and a decline beyond. The authors
interpret this as evidence in support of a gradual build-up of the
baryonic content of galaxies over cosmic time. If confirmed, then
this should result in a likewise gradual growth of the Lyα scat-
tering medium, in lockstep with their central galaxies.
7. Conclusions
In this paper we took advantage of the unprecedented sensitivity
of the MUSE Extremely Deep Field to perform the first system-
atic investigation of the properties of Lyα haloes around Lyα
emitters at redshifts z ≥6. We find remarkably strong ensem-
ble differences to a comparison sample at lower redshifts around
z ∼3.2, carefully selected to be observed at similar intrinsic sur-
face brightness sensitivity after accounting for cosmological SB
dimming. Our results can be summarized as follows:
(i) We identify significantly extended Lyα emission around 6
individual high-redshift LAEs from our sample of 18 ob-
jects. This more than doubles the number of known Lyα
haloes beyond z = 6 and provides the first statistical set of
LAHs associated with low-luminosity, low-mass systems
at these redshifts (Fig. 5).
(ii) The sizes (scale lengths) of these 6 high-z Lyα haloes are
much smaller than those of typical lower redshift LAEs
with similar Lyα luminosities and stellar masses, by about
a factor 3 in the mean. Their Lyα halo flux fractions are
however similarly high as at lower z with values around
0.6 (Fig. 4).
(iii) Stacking the remaining 12 LAEs with individually unde-
tected Lyα haloes does not lead to the detection of a mean
LAH signal, not even a tentative trace (Fig. 6). Thus, nearly
70% of LAEs at these high redshifts have either no Lyα
halo at all, or only a very weak or small one.
(iv) In stark contrast, combining our z ∼3.2 objects without in-
dividual LAH detections recovers a highly significant Lyα
halo in the stacked data (Fig. 6), indicating broadly similar
LAH properties for the entire sample. Overall, our results
for the lower redshift comparison sample are in excellent
agreement with those of previous studies.
(v) Comparing our z ≥6 measurements with the results of pre-
vious observations for this redshift range is not straightfor-
ward as the few available studies show a large dispersion.
Our data rule out any previous suggestions that LAHs do
not evolve at z ≥6 or are systematically larger (Fig. 8).
(vi) We also measure substantially lower Lyα line widths of the
z ≥6 LAEs, by a factor ∼2.5 compared to similar lower
redshift objects (Fig. 7). The lines of the high-z LAEs are
often so narrow (but still resolved by MUSE) that the in-
ferred amount of broadening by radiative transfer effects
must be quite modest relative to the predicted intrinsic
virial line widths based on stellar masses from SED fitting.
Our results provide important constraints on the propagation
of Lyα photons during the late stages of reionisation, and on the
evolution of the CGM in general. However, the question about
the physical origin for the observed differences in LAH proper-
ties must for the moment stay open. We considered two possible
broad scenarios, one in which the transition from a partly neu-
tral to a highly ionized IGM results in a rapid change of Lyα
scattering and visibility behaviour, and an alternative scenario
of “slow” co-evolution between the general population of star
forming galaxies and their Lyα haloes.
In the reionisation-dominated scenario (see
Sect. 6.2) it
would be tempting to build a direct connection between the small
sizes and frequent indetectability of Lyα haloes and the mea-
sured narrow Lyα line profiles, both of which are believed to
be strongly shaped by resonant scattering. It could be that the
Lyα radiation that we receive from low-luminosity LAEs is com-
posed mainly of those Lyα photons that escaped with a relatively
modest scattering history, both spatially (escaping closer to the
sites of star formation) and spectrally (escaping closer to sys-
temic redshift). The remainder of the total escaping Lyα radi-
ation would then be suffering much stronger scattering than at
lower redshifts, producing a vast but probably unobservable halo
due to its low surface brightness and strongly broadened line pro-
file.
Such effects would also provide a natural explanation for
the apparent non-evolution of the Lyα luminosity function at
3 ≤z ≤6 and its rapid decrease beyond z ≈6 (Umeda et al.
2025). A substantial loss of Lyα photons to the still partly neu-
tral IGM would result in systematically lower recovered Lyα lu-
minosities at z ≥6. Another relevant finding of this study is that
the two main drivers of LAE detectability – halo size and spec-
tral line width (Pharo et al. 2024) – show drastic redshift evo-
lution, affecting the LAE selection function and hence strongly
influencing the Lyα luminosity function. Thus, the prominence
and sizes of Lyα haloes has the potential to serve as an addi-
tional powerful diagnostic for the properties of the ionized re-
gions where LAEs are found, especially in conjunction with
high-resolution radiative transfer simulations.
On the other hand, it is also possible that the previously
held view, supported by a number of studies (e.g., Momose et al.
2014, FL17), of more or less unevolving Lyα halo properties be-
tween z ∼3 and z ∼6 is incorrect, and that Lyα halo sizes grow

more or less in lockstep with their host galaxies (Sect. 6.3). This
is fortunately a testable hypothesis, as data of sufficient quality
exist. A systematic investigation of Lyα halo properties over the
full redshift range accessible to MUSE, using improved anal-
ysis methods and taking advantage of the latest generation of
GLAO-supported MUSE deep fields including the MXDF and
the MUSCATEL survey, should firmly establish how the relation
between galaxies and their surrounding LAHs evolve. We have
started with such an analysis and aim to report on the outcome
in a future paper.
Such a galaxy-LAH coevolution framework would certainly
have the appeal of not requiring any special modelling treatment
for LAHs at the highest redshifts. The question is whether this
is physically plausible under the changing conditions during the
late stages and aftermath of reionisation. Another possible prob-
lem with this scenario is that in general one should expect the
minimum Lyα line widths to scale with the cosmologically grow-
ing host masses. The fact that our two LAE samples display very
different FWHMLyα values, at similar stellar mass distributions,
is therefore not in obvious agreement with the simplest coevo-
lution scenario. However, we emphasize that the two scenarios
sketched above are by no means mutually exclusive. It is possible
that both are required for a full explanation of our observations, a
global evolutionary trend and changes in Lyα escape properties
that are specific to the late stages of reionisation.

Another possible effects that might influence the observed scale
length distributions are a change in PSF size and angular di-
ameter distance (DA) evolution. As DA decreases with redshift
beyond z = 1.6, objects of the same physical size become ap-
35
parently more extended. In the context of this study, this con-
stitutes a factor 1.4 increase in the apparent size of the objects
at z ≈6 compared to z ≈3, which increases the detectabil-
ity of compact haloes. The change of the MUSE PSF size with
wavelength also introduces a dependence of the LAH detection
40
threshold on redshift. As the detection procedure compares the
UV continuum model convolved with the MUSE PSF at the
observed wavelength, this dependence is linear in PSF FWHM
to first order. A typical ratio between PSF FWHM at 5105 Å

50
PSF broadening and angular size decrease. We then applied our
