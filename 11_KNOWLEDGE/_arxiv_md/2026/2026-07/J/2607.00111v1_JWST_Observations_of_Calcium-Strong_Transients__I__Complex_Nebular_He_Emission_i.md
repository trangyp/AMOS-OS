ABSTRACT
We present the first JWST observations of a Calcium-Strong Transient (CaST), SN 2024uj, a rare
class of supernovae (SNe) with observable properties that are consistent with both thermonuclear
explosions of white dwarfs (WDs) and the core collapse of massive stars.
SN 2024uj is offset by
∼6.6 kpc from its host and exhibits a double-peaked light curve consistent with shock cooling of
nearby circumstellar material. At early times, its optical spectra resemble those of normal SNe Ib,
but strong [Ca II] λλ7291, 7324 emission emerges between +2 and +17 days after maximum light.
Radiative-transfer models of a massive stripped He star cannot reproduce this early forbidden Ca

1. INTRODUCTION
Calcium-Strong Transients (CaSTs) are a subclass of
supernovae (SNe) that have puzzled observers since their
first detections.
The first members of this class were
identified by Filippenko et al. (2003) on the basis of un-
usually strong (“and perhaps suspect”) calcium emis-
sion lines in their spectra.
In early analyses (Perets
et al. 2010; Kasliwal et al. 2012), the defining charac-
teristics of this class came to be (i) peak magnitudes
fainter than ∼−16.5 mag, (ii) quick evolution to the
nebular phase (∼1 −3 months), and (iii) “strong” cal-
cium emission, which was later quantified as an inte-
grated flux ratio [Ca II] / [O I] > 2 (Milisavljevic et al.
2017). Although they were originally thought to syn-
thesize large amounts of calcium (“Ca-rich” SNe), Ca is
a very efficient coolant, so high abundances may not be
necessary to reproduce the Ca line strengths (see mod-
els from Polin et al. 2021), and/or these systems may
be weak in oxygen. Thus, we use the label “Ca-strong”
(Shen et al. 2019) in this work.
CaSTs are often found at considerably large offsets
from their host galaxies.
In a sample of all CaSTs
observed before 2025, C.G. Touchard-Paxton 2026 (in
prep.) find an average host-SN offset of 5.76±0.75 kpc,1
where ∼25% are offset by more than 20 kpc. In combi-
nation with the lack of star formation at the sites of
CaSTs (Lyman et al. 2014), this suggests that CaSTs
originate from old stellar populations, disfavoring mas-
sive star progenitors (Lunnan et al. 2017), or that they
are not formed in situ (Lyman et al. 2014; Foley 2015).
Furthermore, CaSTs also typically have small ejecta and

Despite some evidence for a thermonuclear origin,
CaSTs also have properties that instead suggest a mas-
sive star origin. Most CaST optical spectra near maxi-
mum light resemble those of Type Ib/c SNe, which are
tied to the core collapse of massive stars. These opti-
cal spectra have helium emission lines, as do the small
number of published near-infrared (NIR) CaST spectra
(SN 2025coe, Kumar et al. 2026; SN 2021gno, Jacobson-
Gal´an et al. 2022; SN 2019ehk, Jacobson-Gal´an et al.
2020a; iPTF 15eqv, Milisavljevic et al. 2017). The pres-
ence of He in these spectra could naturally be explained
with a massive star progenitor, but there are also ther-
monuclear scenarios that could produce He emission
(e.g., a He-rich WD, Waldman et al. 2011; Dessart &
Hillier 2015, or double detonations with a WD and a He-
rich companion, Boyle et al. 2017; Callan et al. 2025a).
The low ejecta and 56Ni masses from CaSTs could also
be consistent with low-mass progenitors undergoing core
collapse (Kawabata et al. 2010; De et al. 2021); however,
these progenitor models are ruled out when the masses
are computed from the very late-time bolometric light
curve (e.g., Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2021).
It may be the case that several progenitor channels
and/or explosion mechanisms contribute to the CaST
population.
In a study of CaST host galaxies, Dong
et al. (2022) find that at least some CaSTs must come
from thermonuclear WD explosions, leaving multiple
channels as a possibility.
For example, CaSTs in re-
mote environments may be WD explosions, while those
at smaller host-SN offsets and/or in star-forming galax-
ies may originate from massive stars (Prentice et al.
2020; De et al. 2021).

SNe (Ca-Ia) and Type Ib/c SNe (Ca-Ib/c), with a rela-

classes can be explained with the same progenitor sys-

Touchard-Paxton et al. (2025) proposed that the
CaST SN 2023xwi originated from a thermonuclear ex-
plosion in an AM Canum Venaticorum (AM CVn) sys-
tem. They also find that this system can explain ob-
servables seen in all CaSTs (e.g., the presence of forbid-
den transitions at very early times). AM CVn systems
belong to a class of variables where a WD accretes mat-
ter from a He-rich semi/fully degenerate donor (Roelofs
et al. 2007). As explored by Bildsten et al. (2007), the
helium accreted from the donor onto the WD forms a
He shell, which can produce a He-shell thermonuclear
flash (He-nova) in cases of unstable burning at high ac-
cretion rates. These flashes in AM CVn systems occur
periodically and can ultimately result in a faint ther-
monuclear SN, where a “last flash” with sufficient mass
becomes dynamical and ejects radioactive material and
fully unbinds the WD (Bildsten et al. 2007).
To disentangle proposed CaST progenitors, many
groups have developed radiative-transfer models for
both core-collapse and thermonuclear CaST channels
(for reviews, see Shen et al. 2019 and Zenati et al. 2023).
Perets et al. (2010) first identified the prototype CaST
SN 2005E and proposed it was the product of a He-
shell detonation on a WD. Waldman et al. (2011) mod-
eled this scenario specifically for WDs with low-mass
carbon-oxygen (C/O) cores, and in cases where there
is strong carbon enrichment, found good agreement be-
tween the simulated and early-time observed CaST spec-
tra. Dessart & Hillier (2015) adapted the Waldman et al.
(2011) model to a non-local-thermodynamic-equilibrium
(NLTE) time-dependent framework with the CMFGEN
code (Hillier & Dessart 2012). More recently, Dessart
et al. (2021) simulated the core collapse of low-mass he-
lium stars (He-stars).
Their NLTE radiative-transfer
simulations replicate many spectral features seen in
CaSTs, but also present strong Ti II lines that have
yet to be observed in a CaST. Lastly, we highlight the
work of Zenati et al. (2023), who model the merger of a
low-mass C/O WD and a hybrid helium-C/O (He/C/O)
WD. In particular, through a mix of multidimen-
sional hydrodynamical-thermonuclear (FLASH; Fryxell
et al. 2000) and one-dimensional (1D) NLTE radiative-
transfer (CMFGEN) simulations, they model the full dis-
ruption of the C/O WD by the He/C/O WD and the
following detonation of the material accreted onto the
He/C/O. Notably, the progenitor CSM profile used in
this model configuration is consistent with the CSM con-
straints made from X-ray CaST observations (Jacobson-
Gal´an et al. 2020a, 2022; Kumar et al. 2026). However,
the spectra from these simulations lack helium emission
lines seen in most CaSTs (Ca-Ib/c).
While the sample of observed CaSTs has grown
to ∼
50, only three have NIR spectra taken be-
yond ∼+10 days post-peak (iPTF 15eqv, Milisavljevic
et al. 2017; SN 2019ehk, Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2020a;
SN 2025coe, Kumar et al. 2026). Only iPTF 15eqv has
a late-time (>+50 days) NIR spectrum, making neb-
ular NIR observations of CaSTs a key underexplored
frontier. In the late-time nebular phase, the SN ejecta
becomes optically thin, revealing the innermost mate-
rial that is obscured at earlier times. This enables in-
ferences of the geometry and kinematics of the ejecta
via the shapes of nebular emission lines (for a review,
see Jerkstrand 2017). Nebular spectroscopy is especially
fruitful in the NIR and mid-infrared (MIR) which host
strong, relatively isolated lines tracing a range of ioniza-
tion stages and nucleosynthetic groups. The distribution
of different elements within the ejecta provides critical
clues to the explosion mechanism and progenitor sys-
tem (DerKacy et al. 2023, 2024, 2026; Kwok et al. 2023,
2024b, 2025a,b; Medler et al. 2023, 2025; Ashall et al.
2024; Dessart et al. 2025; Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2025,
2026; Mera et al. 2026).
We present an analysis of the first JWST observations
of a CaST, SN 2024uj.
These data, taken at a phase
of +150 days after the second r-band peak, include a
medium-resolution spectrum (R ≈1000) from the Near-
Infrared Spectrograph (NIRSpec; Jakobsen et al. 2022,
B¨oker et al. 2023) covering 1–5 µm and F1000W pho-
tometry from the Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI; Rieke
et al. 2015, Wright et al. 2015). Paired with ground-
based optical observations, these JWST data provide a
more complete picture of the mechanisms behind this
CaST and the class as whole.

3.3. Optical and Ultraviolet Photometry
We obtained high-cadence follow-up photometry of
SN 2024uj using a network of worldwide 1 m and 2 m
robotic telescopes within the Las Cumbres Observatory
(Brown et al. 2013). The observations were triggered
through the Global Supernova Project (GSP; Howell
2019) and photometric data reduction was done with the
lcogtsnpipe5 (Valenti et al. 2016) pipeline. The pack-
age lcogtsnpipe uses standard point-spread-function
(PSF) fitting techniques to calculate instrumental mag-
nitudes. Observations in the gri filters are calibrated to
the AAVSO Photometric All Sky Survey (APASS) cat-
alog (Henden et al. 2016), while UBV filter photometry
is calibrated to a local Landolt catalog (Landolt 1992,
2009) constructed using standard fields observed with
the same telescope/instrument on the same night as the
science observations. As SN 2024uj is significantly off-
set from its host galaxy (6.6 kpc) it has no significant
background contamination, and thus we do not perform
difference imaging for photometry.
The Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory (Swift; Gehrels
et al. 2004) monitored SN 2024uj with its UV-Optical
Telescope (UVOT; Roming et al. 2005) across ultravi-
olet (UV) filters UV W2, UV M2, and UV W1. These
data were reduced using the High-Energy Astrophysics
Software (HEASoft). We chose a source region centered
at the position of the SN with an aperture radius of
3′′ for photometry. A background was chosen from a
source-free region with an aperture radius of 5′′.
We
adopted zero-points for the photometry from Breeveld
et al. (2010) and used the latest updates to the time-
dependent sensitivity corrections in 2020.
The multiband extinction-corrected UV and optical
light curves of SN 2024uj are shown in Figure 3 and Ap-

SN 2019ehk, SN 2021gno, and SN 2025coe are ∼16, 30,

4. PHOTOMETRIC ANALYSIS
To quantify the nature of the light curve evolution,
we first construct a bolometric light curve of SN 2024uj
with the Light Curve Fitting package (Hosseinzadeh
et al. 2023) using optical and UV photometry. For each
telescope-night, if there are at least three photometry
points, we fit a blackbody Planck function to the ob-
served spectral energy distribution (SED; UV—optical)
using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) routine to
calculate the bolometric luminosity. We find that the
bolometric luminosity estimated from SED fitting is re-
liable only until ∼40 days after the second peak in the
optical light curve. Due to the rapid evolution from the
photospheric to nebular phase in SN 2024uj, the peak of
the SED quickly shifts from the UV to the IR, and with
no IR photometry, the blackbody temperatures are left
unconstrained at epochs beyond 40 days. Moreover, as
the SN evolves into the nebular phase, [Ca II] and other
emission lines strengthen, breaking down the blackbody
assumption. The constructed bolometric light curve is
presented in Figure 4.
The bolometric light curve shows an early rapid de-
cline from a peak luminosity of ∼1.4 × 1042 erg s−1 be-
fore rising to a second peak (∼9 days after discovery)
with a luminosity of ∼4 × 1041 erg s−1. We note that
there are no UV observations at the time of the first
observed peak, so the corresponding bolometric lumi-
nosity is a lower limit. The double peaked nature of the
light curve is similar to several other CaSTs discovered
early, where the rapid first peak arises from the cool-
ing of a shock-heated envelope and/or shock interaction
with ambient circumstellar material (CSM), while the
slower second peak is powered by the radioactive decay
of 56Ni (De et al. 2018; Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2020b,
2022; Ertini et al. 2023; Crawford et al. 2025; Ravi et al.
2026).
We adopt a combination of the shock-envelope cool-
ing formalism of Piro et al. (2021) with the radioactive
decay of 56Ni to explain the double-peaked bolometric
light curve of SN 2024uj. Physical scenarios leading to
the excess shock-cooling luminosity are degenerate, but
must involve a gravitationally bound compact envelope,
as opposed to, or in combination with, an ambient CSM
around the progenitor. In the Piro et al. (2021) envelope
cooling model, extended material of mass Me within a
radial extent of Re is imparted an energy of Ee as the
shock from the explosion passes through. The resulting
cooling of the extended envelope adds extra luminosity
to the expected contributions from radioactivity alone
leading to the first peak. Homologous expansion is as-
sumed and the luminosity due to a cooling envelope is
found to be proportional to the initial envelope radius
(Piro et al. 2021).
To determine the other physical parameters associated
with the explosion of SN 2024uj, we fit the bolometric
LC with the luminosity expected from radioactivity at
photospheric and nebular phases. In the photospheric
phase (t ≲30 days from explosion), the luminosity is
driven by photon diffusion time which is a function of the
ejecta mass Mej, the photospheric ejecta velocity vph,
and the opacity, assuming spherical symmetry (Arnett
1982). If we assume that at the radioactively-powered
second peak of SN 2024uj the rise time is comparable to
the photon diffusion time, we can infer Mej, the nickel
mass MNi, and the explosion kinetic energy Ek. We fix
the optical opacity (κopt) at 0.1 cm2 g−1, a value con-
sistent with electron scattering in singly ionized helium

5.3.1. Gaussian Fits to Calcium
We present our fits to the [Ca II] λλ7291, 7324 line
profile as it evolves in the right top panel of Figure 7.
Multiple components are required to capture the asym-
metry in the two clear [Ca II] peaks and the way in
which they evolve. We model this feature as three sets
of Gaussian pairs representing independently evolving
concentrations of [Ca II] in the ejecta, and a linear com-
ponent for the local pseudo-continuum.
We use a simple weighted least-squares algorithm to
find a three-component model of [Ca II] with maxi-
mized likelihood.
We start by fitting the fully nebu-
lar +135 d emission line with Gaussians pairs at fixed
∆λ, allowing any value for the means (velocity off-
set of the emitting material v) of each pair.
To en-
sure our fit captures physical features in the emission
profile rather than narrow low S/N features, we set
a lower bound of ∼600 km s−1 for the Full Width at
Half Maximum (FWHM), or, the velocity dispersion of
the emitting material.
Using the best-fit parameters
from the +135 d [Ca II] λλ7291, 7324 line profile (set-
ting v+49d = v+134d ± 1500 km s−1 and FWHM+49d =
FWHM+134d±1800 km s−1, and leaving amplitudes un-
constrained), we fit the +51 d profile.
In our resulting fits, the broad ∼0 km s−1 Gaus-
sian pair and the Gaussian pair near ∼2500 km s−1
are nearly static (similar amplitudes) between epochs.
The other narrower Gaussian pair near ∼700 km s−1,
becomes more pronounced relative to the other compo-
nents between epochs, giving rise to the double peaked
structure. Our multicomponent fit parameters are given
in Table 3 and we discuss physical interpretations in Sec-
tion 5.3.3.
5.3.2. Gaussian Fits to Helium
The highly redshifted and asymmetric He I profiles
in SN 2024uj, along with our multicomponent fits, are
shown in the bottom right panel of Figure 7. The He I
profile is more complex than that of [Ca II], and with
only one epoch, we can run the risk of overfitting this
feature with an overly complicated model.
For this
reason, we utilize nested sampling in our fits to He I.
The primary goal of nested sampling is to estimate the
Bayesian evidence Z (the marginal likelihood), which
represents the probability of the data given a specific
model. These evidence values quantify how well a given
model explains the data while penalizing unnecessary
complexity. When comparing different models, selecting
the one with higher evidence guards against overfitting.
In our fits to He I, we model the 1.083µm and 2.058µm
features simultaneously. Our He I 1.083 µm model is
comprised of a linear pseudo-continuum and three Gaus-
sian components that represent distinct regions of neu-
tral helium within the SN ejecta. The choice of three
Gaussians, rather than two, is a result of model com-
parison: the ratio of evidences, or, the log Bayes factor
= 114, in favor of the triple-Gaussian model. We include
an additional broad Gaussian for the blueshifted “shoul-
der” (non-zero flux between [-8000, 0] km s−1). Lastly,
because He I 1.083 µm is blended with [S I] 1.082 µm,
and there is also an isolated neighboring [S I] 1.131 µm
line, we include 2 Gaussian components in our model for
these [S I] lines. The fit to the isolated [S I] 1.131 µm
line sets the amplitude, v, and FWHM of the [S I]
1.082 µm Gaussian, based on the theoretical intensity
ratio of these two transitions.
Our He I 2.058 µm fit is the same as our He I 1.083 µm
fit, but without the broad blueshifted “shoulder” Gaus-
sian component or the nearby [S I] components. When
fitting both He I 1.083 µm and He I 2.058 µm pro-
files simultaneously, the three shared Gaussians have
the same velocity parameters, and we allow their am-
plitudes to vary slightly between each profile. We use
the nested sampling package dynesty (Speagle 2020) to
find the solution with a maximized posterior probability.
For more details on prior selection and the model setup,
see Appendix B. Figure 7 (bottom right panel) shows
the fit using maximum a posteriori (MAP) values of the
samples for each parameter. MAP values and credible

6. DISCUSSION
6.1. Progenitor Scenarios
6.1.1. SN 2024uj: CCSN?
Some properties of CaSTs resemble those of stripped
envelope SNe (Ib/c), suggesting a possible massive star
origin.
In SN 2024uj, the early time spectra, particu-
larly near the second r-band peak (17 d post-explosion),
closely match the stripped He star he5 model (top panel
of Figure 8), largely due to the presence of He absorp-
tion. At late times, CCSNe show prominent He emis-
sion, as does SN 2024uj. Binary systems that give rise
to many stripped-envelope supernovae (SESNe) can be
asymmetric, viewing-angle dependent, and shrouded by
aspherical CSM (e.g., Chandra et al. 2020; Baer-Way
et al. 2025).
This is consistent with the significantly
asymmetric nebular lines in the nebular SN 2024uj spec-
tra;
however, the complex He
I line extending to
≳5000 km s−1and peaking at +1500 km s−1 does not
closely resemble the He profiles seen in CCSNe, even
asymmetric SESN. Finally, SN 2024uj displays a shock-
cooling first light curve peak, and molecular and dust
emission, both of which are typically associated with
CCSNe due to pre-explosion mass loss in massive stars.
There are, however, rare SN Ia subtypes that also ex-
hibit early light curve bumps: 02es-like (Cao et al. 2015;
Miller et al. 2020; Xi et al. 2024) and 03fg-like (Jiang
et al. 2021; Srivastav et al. 2023; Dimitriadis et al. 2023),
with the latter potentially arising from shock-cooling.
There are three key observables in SN 2024uj and
other CaSTs that are difficult to reconcile with a massive
star progenitor system: the early emergence of [Ca II],
the redshifted He I component (v=5740 km s−1), and a
remote environment. In an experiment with the massive
He-star radiative-transfer model he5, we are unable to
reproduce early [Ca II] emission even after adding un-
physically large amounts of Ca (χCa=0.01) to the star’s
outer layers, which would require a level of mixing that is
not expected in a massive star scenario. We encourage
future exploration of this point with a broader range
of massive star progenitor models and configurations.
Next, while the highly redshifted peak velocity of He I at
5740 km s−1 could be tied to previous mass-loss episodes
or a high bulk velocity, it is unclear how a CCSN sce-
nario could produce the peculiar shape of the He I line,
with bulk redshift and wings extending to the broad-
est velocities, while [Ca II] and [O I] remain centrally
located (Figure 7). Finally, the lack of local star for-
mation and the large host-galaxy offset for SN 2024uj
and other CaSTs makes a young, massive star progeni-
tor less likely. However, the remote location alone does
not preclude a massive star origin (e.g., the SN Ic-BL
SN 2002ap was observed 10 kpc away from its host, at a
site with no local star formation; Gal-Yam et al. 2002;
Modjaz et al. 2008b).

6.1.2. SN 2024uj: AM CVn-like?
An AM CVn-like progenitor system modeled after
V445 Puppis could explain a number of features in the
SN 2024uj observations. AM CVn systems can undergo
successive He novae, episodes of helium burning and
mass ejection, which have also been proposed to ex-
plain the helium observed in some Type Iax SNe (e.g.,
Jacobson-Gal´an et al. 2019). These He novae can give
rise to lobes with He, Ca, and O at extended radii
(Woudt et al. 2009).
Within an AM CVn-like CaST
progenitor scenario, these low-density Ca-rich regions
formed pre-SN could help explain the early emergence
of [Ca II] emission (Touchard-Paxton et al. 2025). The
bipolar lobes (or similarly complex structure) of a V445
Puppis-like system would also likely be able to pro-
duce the multicomponent Ca and He profiles, distinct
regions of Ca and O, He moving at ≳5600 km s−1, and
overall asymmetry of the nebular emission lines seen in
SN 2024uj. The presence of dust and CO in SN 2024uj
(Figure 1) is also broadly consistent with V445 Puppis-
like system, as Ashok & Banerjee (2003) identify a dust
shell in V445 Puppis that was formed ∼1 month after
the He-nova outburst.
Lastly, the remote location of
SN 2024uj is compatible with an AM CVn-like progeni-
tor composed of a double degenerate WD binary system
(e.g., the V445 Puppis-like CaST model in Touchard-
Paxton et al. 2025), although it is unclear whether this
channel can reproduce the distribution of large host-SN
offsets that define the CaST class.
Based on the bipolar lobe geometry of V445 Puppis,
the AM CVn-like CaST progenitor in Touchard-Paxton
et al. (2025) also predicts spectral ‘transition features’:
emission lines dominated by the external regions at early
times (profiles with strong high-velocity components)
and by the central SN at later times (profiles with strong
components near v ≈0 km s−1). In our multicomponent
fits to [Ca II] in SN 2024uj we identify a central compo-
nent (v ≈750 km s−1 between +51 and +135 d) which
grows over time, which may be tied to the SN overpow-
ering external material from pre-SN activity.
We en-
courage further exploration into ‘transition features’ in
the CaST class at large, especially with high resolution
spectra taken with high cadence.
There is also a compelling similarity between the He I
profiles of SN 2024uj and V445 Puppis.
Woudt et al.
(2009) identify four distinct peaks in the He I lines at
7065 ˚A and 2.058 µm, two with negative velocities and
two with positive, which they attribute to blue- and red-
shifted material distributed asymmetrically in each lobe.
In SN 2024uj we see similarly peaked asymmetric struc-
ture (Figure 7), but all peaks have positive velocities
v > 1400 km/s, besides the blue “shoulder” only present
in He I 1.083 µm. While we cannot directly attribute
the He I in SN 2024uj to a bipolar shell structure like
the one in V445 Puppis due to a lack of similar spatial
information, this possibility should be investigated in
future high-resolution NIR samples of CaSTs and three-
dimensional models. We note that of the other CaSTs
with ≥+35 d NIR spectra (SN 2019ehk and iPTF 15eqv,
Figure 6) only SN 2024uj has He I profiles that resem-
ble those in V445 Puppis. A larger sample would help
determine whether this is a viewing angle effect or a
shortcoming of the model.
Although aspects of this model are promising, it has
yet to be modeled with radiative-transfer simulations,
and may struggle to explain other CaST properties.
In particular, it is unclear whether the lobes in the
V445 Puppis-like CaST model (Touchard-Paxton et al.
2025) can reach high enough densities to generate the
shock-cooling first light curve peak and associated X-ray
emission seen in several well-observed CaSTs (Jacobson-
Gal´an et al. 2020a, 2022; Kumar et al. 2026). Addition-
ally, De et al. (2020) note that the rate of AM CVn
systems is well below their estimate of the CaST volu-
metric rate, but they do not rule out that some CaSTs—
particularly some members of the Ca-Ib subclass—could
originate from AM CVn systems.
6.1.3. SN 2024uj: Low-mass binary WD merger?
Finally, we consider a WD merger scenario with at
least one He WD or hybrid He/C/O WD, which we re-
fer to as a low-mass WD binary. We note that the AM
CVn-like and low-mass WD binary scenarios are likely
not mutually exclusive. It may be that an AM CVn-
like progenitor explodes terminally in a low-mass WD
merger, and we encourage modeling to explore this pos-
sibility.
The late-time optical spectra of SN 2024uj and other
CaSTs reasonably resemble model spectra of binary WD
systems (see COp45HEp2 and fca1 in Figure 8), which
predict strong late-time [Ca II] and also the early emer-
gence of [Ca II]. There are several possible low-mass WD
merger models which explode in various ways. The fca1
model (Zenati et al. 2023) follows the accretion of C/O
material onto an He/C/O-WD that causes a detonation
of the He shell, burning away most of the He while leav-
ing a hot remnant C/O core that may radiate X-rays as
it cools. This causes the fca1 model not to produce sig-
nificant He features (Zenati et al. 2023). Mor´an-Fraile
et al. (2024) simulated the merger of a C/O WD and He
WD where the He WD is disrupted into a massive He
shell around the C/O WD and the He ignites, triggering
a double detonation. Callan et al. (2025b) showed that
full NLTE radiative transfer of this model does produce

He absorption at early times and He emission at later
times in both the optical and NIR, validating that He
lines can be produced in thermonuclear explosions.
Merger systems can be quite asymmetric and view-

2024b; Pakmor et al. 2026), which might provide an
explanation for the asymmetric He I and [Ca II] pro-
files in the +150 d NIRSpec spectrum of SN 2024uj.
The nebular He I also has a strong redshifted peak at
∼+1500 km s−1, which could potentially be attributed
to a shocked helium-rich companion. Wong & Bildsten
(2025) predict narrow nebular He emission lines (1000–
4000 km s−1) for double-degenerate SNe Ia involving a
low-mass He WD companion that is flung off at its or-
bital velocity of 1000–3000 km s−1. The higher-velocity
(5740 km s−1) He I in SN 2024uj might also be connected
to material that was stripped prior to merger.
Several other characteristics of WD mergers of low-
mass WDs can naturally account for the observed low
energy and ejecta masses of SN 2024uj and other CaSTs.
Thermonuclear explosions produce higher kinetic energy
to ejecta mass ratios than CCSNe because they produce
56Ni more efficiently and have less additional envelope
mass to lift off, enabling the SN ejecta to cool and be-
come optically thin quickly. This is consistent with the
observation of early forbidden Ca emission and rapid
transition to the nebular phase in CaSTs.
In a WD
merger scenario, material can be stripped off during the
inspiral, providing a potential avenue for nearby CSM;
however, it is unclear whether such material would be
of sufficient density to produce the shock cooling and X-
rays associated with CaSTs. Unburned C and O from a
merger system, either stripped pre-explosion or left over
if the explosion does not fully disrupt the WD (as in the
fca1 model; Zenati et al. 2023), could potentially form
CO and seed dust formation, in line with the CO and
dust detections in SN 2024uj (see Figure 1 and S. Hall
2026b, in prep.) Finally, the remote locations of most
CaSTs are suggestive of WD progenitors.
Some characteristics of SN 2024uj are not fully con-
sistent with our current set of low-mass binary WD
merger models.
Future hydrodynamical simulations
and radiative-transfer models should investigate the pre-
merger mass loss and include physics for molecule and
dust formation.
Multidimensional models should also
investigate whether such systems give rise to ejecta dis-
tributions that are asymmetric enough to reproduce the
distributions of He, Ca, and O seen in SN 2024uj (Fig-
ure 7).
Although still difficult computationally, such
models should follow the pre-merger inspiral, such as
was done by Pakmor et al. (2026), to help determine
whether these systems can produce dense enough ma-
terial prior to explosion.
Lastly, we note that a WD
progenitor system, one which could exist at many lo-
cations throughout a given host galaxy, does not fully
explain why CaSTs consistently explode at large host-
SN offsets.

To date, He-rich (Ib-like) CaSTs have been inter-

low-mass C/O (or hybrid) white dwarf by a more mas-

2021). In this work, based on a remote explosion site,
early [Ca II] emission, small 56Ni and ejecta masses, and
complex asymmetric nebular lines, particularly the He I
emission, we favor the interpretation that SN 2024uj is
the product of a thermonuclear explosion. Specifically,
we find that a binary WD merger, with at least one
He WD or hybrid He/C/O WD, potentially within an
AM CVn-like system is the most likely progenitor sys-
tem. However, we cannot rule out a CCSN origin and
encourage future modeling to further explore both av-

offsets, exhibit early [Ca II] emission, show double-peak
light curves, and feature strong He I emission, similar
to SN 2024uj, so we suggest that our interpretation may
extend to the larger subclass. CaSTs as thermonuclear
explosions would reinforce that low-mass WDs with He
can produce strong spectroscopic He signatures, and
likely explode via a double detonation or He shell burn-
ing mechanism.
Finally, our detection of molecular CO emission in the
+150 d NIRSpec and MIRI observations of SN 2024uj
is the first such detection for a CaST and thermonu-
clear SN in general.
In S. Hall 2026b (in prep.), we
analyze the dust and CO in SN 2024uj. These results
may suggest that faint thermonuclear SN can be dust
producers.
We encourage future hydrodynamical and
radiative-transfer models of low-mass WD binaries with
He, as well as investigating pre-explosion mass loss, and
molecule and dust formation in these systems. Larger
samples and multi-epoch late-time NIR and MIR ob-
servations of CaSTs will be essential to improving our
understanding of this class of SN and their diversity.

B. MODEL SETUP FOR HE I
Here, we outline our modeling procedure for the He I 1.083 µm and He I 2.058 µm profiles in our nebular SN 2024uj
observations. This information, and the software used to perform this modeling, can also be found in the GitHub
repository associated with this paper: github.com/saarahhall/SN24uj
1. Define the data to be fit:
(a) He I 2.058 µm: we model the profile from -10000 to 9000 km/s. We extend to -10000 km/s to verify the
absence of a blue “shoulder” (justify the modified model for He I 1.083 µm) and to help define the baseline
for this feature.
(b) He I 1.083 µm: we include the spectrum between -10000 and 16000 km/s, capturing the “shoulder” on the
blue end and also the [S I] [S I] 1.131 µm line on the red end. The choice of where to cut off this spectrum,
and how to represent the baseline, has a non-negligible impact on the amplitudes in resulting fits. This
does not affect our interpretations, however, because we only gleam physical information from the means
and standard deviations, not the amplitudes.
2. Define the model: We model the He I 1.083 µm and He I 2.058 µm features simultaneously. This requires a
complex model with 21 parameters in total.
(a) 12 of the parameters define the three Gaussians that are common to both He I features: {µ1, FWHM1, µ2,
FWHM2, µ3, FWHM3, A1,1.083µm, A1,2.058µm, A2,1.083µm, A2,2.058µm, A3,1.083µm, A3,2.058µm}. Note: the
means (velocities) and FWHM (velocity dispersions) of these three Gaussians are the same in our models
for He I 1.083 µm and He I 2.058 µm, but the amplitudes for each Gaussian are free to vary, since these
values can differ due to differences in the linear baseline.

(b) 4 of the 21 parameters define the linear baselines in each feature: {m1.083µm b1.083µm, m2.058µm, b2.058µm}.
(c) 3 model parameters define the Gaussian “shoulder” present only in He I 1.083 µm: {µshoulder, FWHMshoulder,
Ashoulder}.
(d) The final 2 parameters define the Gaussian contributions from [S I]: {FWHM[S I] 1.131µm, A[S I] 1.131µm}.
The 1.131µm [S I] line is isolated, thus we know the value of µ[S I] 1.131µm. The Gaussian fit to this isolated
feature then defines the [S I] 1.082 µm Gaussian term (which has the same FWHM value, and the amplitude
is scaled based on the transition probability ratio of these two lines).
3. Define the priors: We set uniform priors on all model parameters, and define their bounds carefully to be
physically-informed without introducing bias Priors on all amplitudes are wide, and we ensure positive values
with a lower limit of 0. We also set wide, uninformative priors for the slopes and intercepts of the linear baselines
in each profile (m1, b1, m2, b2).
(a) Velocities µ: The velocities defining the 3 Gaussians shared between He I 1.083 µm and He I 2.058 µm, µ1,
µ2, and µ3, have uniform priors between [0, 9000] km/s. The lower bound is set to avoid doubly-modeling
the shoulder feature, and the upper bound is defined by the right edge of the He I 2.058 µm data we are
fitting. The prior on µshoulder is [−6000, 4000] km/s.
