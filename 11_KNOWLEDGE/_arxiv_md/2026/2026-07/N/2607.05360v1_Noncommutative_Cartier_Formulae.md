Abstract. We prove, for every E1 algebra A, a formula describing the interaction of the action of the
cap product on topological Hochschild homology of A with the cyclotomic structure map, as well as a
variant of this result relative to a ring R. Specializing to R = Fp gives a noncommutative analog of a
formula of Cartier which describes the conjugation of interior product action on differential forms by
the Cartier isomorphism, and which computes the p-curvature of the Getzler-Gauss-Manin connection
in terms of an equivariant cap product.
The motivation for this formula comes from symplectic
geometry, where (in the case R = Fp or a Novikov analog) the symplectic analog of this formula
explains the interaction between the cyclotomic structure on symplectic cohomology and the quantum
Steenrod operations. We prove, under standard transversality and nondegeneracy assumptions on
the Fukaya category, that for a Calabi-Yau symplectic manifold with rational symplectic form, the
p-curvature of the quantum connection computes the Quantum Steenrod operations. In particular,
the p-curvature of the quantum connections of projective Calabi-Yau hypersurfaces, and many other
examples in mirror symmetry, can be interpreted in terms of Z/pZ-equivariant genus zero Gromov-
Witten invariants.
1. Introduction
The purpose of this paper is to establish a noncommutative analog of a formula of Cartier connect-
ing the interior product and the Cartier isomorphism, in the form of an analogous result for all E1-ring
spectra A and a relative variant for all E1-ring spectra A over an E∞-ring spectrum R. The arguments
and results require only homotopical algebra; however, the motivation arises from symplectic geome-
try, and the author discovered the analog of these formulae first in that setting, where they admit a
simple pictorial interpretation (and ‘picture proof’). The formulae describe the relation between the
cap product action of Hochschild cohomology THC on Hochschild homology THH, and the cyclo-
tomic structure on topological Hochschild homology. One can formulate an expected generalization of
these formulae, articulating the compatibility conditions between the E2-structure on THC and the
cyclotomic structure on THH; we refer to this as the cyclotomic Deligne conjecture (Appendix A).
In the setting of symplectic topology, this formula corresponds to a result describing the relationship
between the quantum product, the equivariant quantum product (or ‘quantum Steenrod operation’),
and the cyclotomic structure on symplectic cohomology, thus clarifying the algebro-geometric inter-
pretation of the quantum Steenrod operations under mirror symmetry, and giving an explanation of
the relationship between the quantum product and the quantum Steenrod operation that has been
established in various special cases in previous works.

We now explain the application of these algebraic results to symplectic topology. Symplectic ge-
ometry and complex geometry are each, in their own right, a source of interesting linear ordinary
differential equations.

Manin connections define the parallel transport of cycles in the cohomology bundle H∗(X/S) on a
smooth proper family of complex varieties X over a base S. The underlying differential equations can

2. Background and Conventions
In this section, we give a terse review of the homotopical background required for this paper, with a
focus on the various models of homotopical categories and derived functors we use as well as discussion
of how we handle various models of the equivariant stable category and its localizations.
2.1. Model categories. We will describe the equivariant stable category in terms of presentations
of homotopical categories given by model categories. This is a convenient formalism for computing
derived geometric fixed-point functors, in particular.
We will only need formal properties of model categories; see [57, 55] for textbook references. It
suffices to know that a model category structure on a category C is given by specifying classes of
morphisms called cofibrations, fibrations, and weak equivalences, satisfying certain axioms. The model
structure axioms ensure that we have control on the homotopy category Ho(C), which depends only
on the weak equivalences of C. The cofibrations and fibrations can be thought of as scaffolding helping
us calculate derived functors, notably homotopy colimits and limits.
We will work in the context of model categories for which cofibrant and fibrant replacements are
functorial; that is, we will be given cofibrant and fibrant replacement functors which we denote c and
f, respectively. These are equipped with natural transformations
c →id →f with cX →X a cofibration and X →fX a fibration for any X ∈C.
Writing ∅for the initial object of C and ∗for the terminal object, an object X in C is cofibrant
if ∅→X is a cofibration and fibrant if X →∗is a fibration.
Many of the model categories we
work with are pointed, in the sense that the initial and terminal objects coincide. An elementary
argument shows that cofibrant replacement preserves fibrant objects, and vice versa.
The model
structure provides control on the homotopy category: if X is cofibrant and Y is fibrant, then the
localization map C(X, Y ) →Ho(C)(X, Y ) is the universal map given by quotienting by the equivalence
relation of homotopy equivalence of morphisms. There is a more precise version of this statement in
terms of the underlying ∞-category associated to C, which we discuss below.
Most of the model categories we consider are symmetric monoidal; this means that there is a
symmetric monoidal structure on the category C that is compatible with the model structure in a precise
sense (namely that the unit axiom and the pushout-product axioms are satisfied). One consequence of
this is that under mild hypotheses the model structure on C lifts to a model structure on Alg(C), the
category of algebra objects in C. The situation is slightly more complicated for commutative algebra
objects, but in the cases we care about there will also be an induced model structure on CAlg(C). We
give more detail on this below in the discussion on (equivariant) commutative ring spectra.
2.2. ∞-categories and higher algebra. Although we use model categories of equivariant spectra
for technical convenience, our results are naturally formulated at the level of ∞-categories. In this
paper, we mostly work model-agnostically, but to be concrete we will use quasicategories as our model
for the theory of (∞, 1)-categories unless otherwise specified. Associated to a model category C is
an underlying ∞-category N(C)[W −1] that lifts the homotopy category Ho(C). Here N denotes the
nerve functor and N(C)[W −1] denotes the localization in quasicategories at the weak equivalences.
The homotopy limits and colimits in C model limits and colimits in N(C)[W −1], and derived functors
in model categories compute functors between ∞-categories.
When C is a symmetric monoidal model category, a variant of this construction gives rise to a
symmetric monoidal ∞-category. For any symmetric monoidal ∞-category C, there is an ∞-category
E1(C) of E1-algebra in C, and similarly an ∞-category E∞(C) of E∞-algebras in C. These generalize the
notion of commutative/associative algebras to the setting of ∞-categories. Similarly, given A ∈E1(C)
there is an ∞-category of A-modules, which we will write either as A-Mod or as ModA.

7.3.1. Mixed complexes and S1-actions. To handle S1-actions algebraically, we use the theory of mixed
complexes [63, 73]. A mixed complex (in k-modules) is a graded k-module equipped with a pair of
operators d, B of degree −1 and +1 respectively such that d2 = B2 = db + Bd = 0. In other words, it
is a module over the exterior algebra H∗(S1, k) = k[B] in chain complexes, with the algebra structure
on H∗(S1) given by the Pontrjagin product on S1.
The category of mixed complexes has a symmetric monoidal structure induced from the coalgebra
structure on H∗(S1) coming from the diagonal map S1 →S1 × S1; explicitly, one sets
BM1⊗M2 = BM1 ⊗1 ± 1 ⊗BM2
with the sign given by the Koszul sign rule. The homotopy theory of mixed complexes comes from
the quasi-isomorphisms; i.e., ∞-category of mixed complexes k[B]−mod arises by inverting the quasi-
isomorphisms.
Classically, mixed complexes arise from cyclic objects in the category of k-modules; for exam-
ple, [47] constructs a functor from cyclic k-modules to mixed complexes, see also [73, Section 2.5]. Put
slightly differently, the ∞-category of k-modules with S1-action is the functor category F(BS1, Modk),
or equivalently, a module over the simplicial commutative algebra k[S1] (see [11, Lemma 3.9] for a
detailed proof, or [33] for an old simplicial argument). Here the diagonal map on S1 makes k[S1]
into a commutative coalgebra in simplicial commutative algebras, and this coalgebra structure makes
ModR[S1] ≃F(BS1, ModR) into a symmetric monoidal ∞-category. Realization then provides a func-
tor from cyclic objects in a stable ∞-category to S1-equivariant objects (e.g., [90, B.5]).
Now, there is an equivalence of E1-algebras R[S1] ≃H∗(S1, R); this follows from the homotopy
transfer theorem for A∞-structures, as there is no possibility for higher order terms in the A∞equations
on H∗(S1, R). Thus, the ∞-categories R[B]-mod and R[S1]-mod are equivalent. It is a fundamental
fact, however, that the above equivalence of ∞-categories does not extend to a symmetric monoidal
equivalence, although it does when Q ⊂k. In this case, there is an equivalence of E∞-bialgebras (i.e.
E∞-algebras in E∞-coalgebras) k[S1] ≃H∗(S1, k) [119]. Moreover, when additionally A/k is smooth,
the HKR map extends to an equivalence of S1-equivariant cdgas (i.e. of E∞−S1-algebras). Away
from characteristic zero, when considering properties related to En −k-algebras with S1-action for
n ≥2, computations become more delicate.

Remark 7.20. The additional difficulty when working with the rings R = k[[x]], k((x)) is that when-
ever one takes a tensor product of R-modules over k, one should use the completed tensor product
(with respect to the x-adic topology) rather than the usual tensor product. Homotopically, one should

Remark 7.21. Geometrically, while many Fukaya categories are smooth and proper over Novikov
fields like k((x)), they almost never have extensions to smooth and proper dg categories over k[[x]],
nor are they in any straightforward manner the completions of categories over k[x, x−1] or other bases
of finite type. The reason for the appearance of rings like k((x)) is two-fold: first, infinitely many
powers of x are required to keep track of infinitely many homology classes of holomorphic curves, and
second, Floer homology of Lagrangian submanifolds is Hamiltonian isotopy invariant when defined

12.4. Domains of definition of Fukaya category. There are several choices that go into the defi-

• The Lagrangian submanifolds {Li}i∈I to be considered as objects;
• The ring R over which fundamental classes of moduli spaces of holomorphic curves are defined,
and
• A large amount of non-canonical data P, the most important of which is an almost complex
