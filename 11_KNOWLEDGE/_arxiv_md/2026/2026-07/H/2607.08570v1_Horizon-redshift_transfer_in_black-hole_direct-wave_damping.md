Abstract
Direct waves from black-hole mergers may probe horizon dynamics, but their observed en-
velopes need not decay at the Kerr surface-gravity rate. We compute the complex-frequency
spin-−2, ℓ= m = 2 Teukolsky response driven by a redshift-stretched near-horizon source.
Through Kerr screening and source convolution, the calculation maps the local surface-
gravity scale κ into the finite-window envelope damping γeff measured at infinity.
For
GW250114, this calculation gives γeff/κ ≃0.6, or γeff ≃0.4 ms−1, consistent with QNM-
subtracted residuals and a joint H1–L1 residual analysis. An instantaneous-source control
recovers the impulse-response damping near κ, whereas finite-duration plunge-source, test-
particle and radial-normalized source realizations give γeff < κ.
A residual-level check
in GW231226 favours the same finite-window damping prediction. These results identify
direct-wave envelope damping as a horizon-redshift transfer observable rather than a direct
measurement of surface gravity.
Introduction
Gravitational-wave ringdown spectroscopy normally infers black-hole horizons indirectly. The
standard observables are Kerr quasinormal modes, whose complex frequencies test the remnant
mass, spin and no-hair structure, but whose excitation and damping are largely governed by the
light-ring potential barrier rather than by the event horizon itself [1–13]. Direct waves have been
proposed as a different window on the merger: a prompt, non-quasinormal component sourced
by the plunge, screened by the remnant potential, and partly captured in Green-function and
Backwards One Body (BOB) descriptions [4, 14–22]. Here and below, by the envelope we mean
the slowly varying amplitude of the direct-wave strain after factoring out a chosen carrier phase.
Recently, an analysis of GW250114 reported a post-merger direct-wave component after
subtracting ordinary quasinormal-mode ringdown [22]. The residual favours a direct-wave in-
terpretation with a phase evolution compatible with the remnant horizon frequency. At the
same time, numerical-relativity and BOB studies argue that direct-wave carrier frequencies are
not generically correlated with the horizon frequency, that the GW250114 agreement may occur
near an accidental spin crossing, and that the filtered-strain damping can evolve rather than
track the surface gravity across comparable-mass simulations [21, 23]. This makes it essential
to separate the carrier-frequency question from the envelope-damping question. Although the
GW250114 analysis in Ref. [22] interpreted the direct-wave envelope as a probe of the Kerr
surface gravity, its Fig. 4 contains finite-window damping support around 0.4–0.5 ms−1, below
the bare Kerr value near 0.6 ms−1. This is not a small bookkeeping issue. It asks whether
the observed envelope should be compared with κ itself or with the screened, source-convolved
signal that reaches the detector.
We argue that the reduced envelope damping is a physical consequence of the same near-
horizon structure that makes direct waves interesting. In a horizon-guided channel, the Kerr pole
supplies a natural carrier scale, but the strain at infinity is not the screened impulse response

Numerical calculation of direct-wave envelope damping
We first compute the damping channel before introducing a compact analytic relation. The
complex-frequency Teukolsky calculation supplies the screened impulse response, denoted I(t),
before source convolution.
Applying the same finite-window log-envelope projection to this
response alone gives γimp ≃0.59 ms−1 for the GW250114 remnant scale, close to the bare
surface-gravity rate. We then drive this response with a redshift-stretched near-horizon plunge
source, denoted S(t), and apply the same projection to the source-convolved direct wave. This
numerical operation gives γeff ≃0.39–0.41 ms−1, well below κ.
The reduction is supported by a sequence of controlled tests summarized in Table 1. In the
instantaneous-source limit, an intentionally nonphysical control in which the source duration
is much shorter than the horizon damping time κ−1, the same fitting pipeline recovers the
screened impulse-response damping, showing that the analysis does not artificially force signals
toward 0.4 ms−1. As the source duration is increased, the measured damping moves smoothly
downward; the fiducial plunge-source point gives γeff ≃0.41 ms−1. As a separate recovery check,
injected impulse-response and source-convolved waveforms are recovered at their input damping
rates. A near-horizon test-particle source gives γeff ≃0.40 ms−1, and a radial-normalized source
model gives γeff ≃0.39 ms−1. The agreement across these tests indicates that the low damping is
caused by source convolution rather than by a fitting bias, and that the tested source projections
stay in the redshift-locked band near p ≃1.

Physical mechanism of the reduced damping
The lower rate arises only after three ingredients are kept separate: the horizon pole sets
the complex frequency scale, the Teukolsky calculation supplies the screened impulse response

post-peak window. Figure 3 summarizes this response-level picture. A real-frequency template
scan or a single time-domain damped-sinusoid fit can test whether a waveform resembles the
data, but it cannot by itself identify which part of the observed damping comes from the horizon
pole, which part from Kerr propagation through the potential barrier, and which part from the
redshift-stretched plunge source and fitting window.

Discussion
The main result is a separation between the carrier-frequency question and the envelope-
damping question. Recent numerical-relativity and BOB analyses argue that the direct-wave
carrier frequency is not a population-universal horizon-frequency observable, that the agree-
ment with mΩH near the GW250114 spin may be accidental, and that instantaneous direct-
wave damping in filtered numerical-relativity strains need not track κ [21, 23]. Our calculation
does not require a universal carrier-frequency identification or a direct κ measurement. Instead,
it asks a narrower question: when a horizon-guided carrier-frequency channel is tested, what
damping rate should be compared with the observed direct-wave envelope?
The answer is not generically the bare surface-gravity rate. The damping measured in the
strain envelope is the observable envelope damping γeff, produced by source convolution and
measured over a finite window.
Equivalently, γeff/κ is a horizon-redshift transfer factor: it
measures how the local surface-gravity redshift scale is mapped, after Kerr screening, source
convolution and finite-window projection, into the observed direct-wave envelope. This dis-
tinction explains why the GW250114 direct-wave damping can sit near 0.4–0.5 ms−1 while the
underlying remnant surface gravity is near 0.6 ms−1, and why the GW231226 damping profile is
compatible with the finite-duration plunge-source prediction from Eq. (4) rather than with the
bare-κ band. In this sense our result is complementary to the claim that direct waves are not,
by themselves, clean horizon-frequency tests: it shows that the damping channel also should
not be interpreted as a direct measurement of κ.
The instantaneous-source limit clarifies what the number γeff/κ ≃0.6 does and does not
mean. Here instantaneous means short compared with κ−1 in the observed horizon-time coor-
dinate; it does not mean spatially pointlike. This is a useful but nonphysical control, because a
real merger plunge is redshift-stretched near the horizon. The ratio is not a universal replace-

This is why GW250114 and GW231226 can give comparable values near 0.6, while different

drive the fitted slope back toward the screened Teukolsky impulse-response damping near κ. An
extreme-mass-ratio plunge, however, is not automatically impulsive: in Boyer–Lindquist time
it remains redshift-stretched near the horizon, so it may provide an especially clean test of the
finite-window damping relation rather than a guaranteed return to bare surface-gravity decay.
The GW231226 carrier-frequency profile is the main caution in the present draft. In the un-
derlying two-dimensional residual map, the single best point sits above the parameter-estimation
median for fH, but the posterior-locked theory point remains inside the approximate profile 1σ
region. This behaviour is not naturally explained by bad strain data: the event and noise seg-
ments are finite, the covariance requires no diagonal loading, the off-source background is low,
and the signal is not carried by a single detector. It is more plausibly a sign that the carrier-
frequency coordinate is currently degenerate with the residual subtraction, the plunge-source
phase history and the allowed time offset. The GW231226 result should therefore be described
as a damping consistency check, and a final measurement should sample the remnant posterior
and the source model jointly instead of reading the frequency from the likelihood maximum
alone.
Several caveats remain before this becomes a discovery claim. The current GW250114 and
GW231226 analyses use fixed or partially locked remnant parameters for many stages and should
be extended to posterior marginalization over Mf, χf, sky location, polarization, source-shape
parameters and calibration/PSD uncertainty. The present result should therefore be read as a
theory-guided damping-relation test and a reproducible analysis path, not yet as a completed
multi-event population measurement.
Methods
Complex-frequency impulse response
We solve the spin-−2, l = m = 2 homogeneous radial Teukolsky equation using the analytic-
series numerical method of Jiang and Han [24]. The calculation is performed in the complex-
frequency plane because the central object is an impulse response, not only a real-frequency
template. This response-level view follows the recent interpretation of direct waves as prompt,
screened radiation from plunging sources [19, 20]. Evaluating the Kerr transfer function at
complex frequency keeps the damping direction inside the Green-function response and lets us
identify the screened impulse-response scale before any source convolution or finite-window fit
is applied.
For each complex ω we construct the two standard homogeneous radial solutions.
The
ingoing solution Rin is purely ingoing at the outer horizon and becomes a superposition of
ingoing and outgoing waves at infinity; the upgoing solution Rup is purely outgoing at infinity
and becomes a superposition at the horizon. Following the Jiang–Han scheme, the local solutions
are represented by Frobenius or asymptotic series near the singular points and by ordinary-
point power series between them. Recurrence relations propagate the series coefficients, and
matching on overlapping radial intervals returns the asymptotic amplitudes and the Wronskian.
This avoids a direct long-range radial integration for every frequency and permits the same
boundary-amplitude construction to be evaluated at complex, not only real, frequencies.

Near-horizon plunge source
The time-domain impulse response is convolved with a source whose detuning from the horizon
frequency decays along the near-horizon plunge. The source weight includes the leading direct-
wave screening and the redshift shutoff of the outgoing channel, consistent with prompt-response
calculations in which plunge radiation is modulated by source motion before being decomposed
into prompt, quasinormal and tail pieces [20]. In the minimal analytic model this redshift-
stretched plunge source has a single decay rate λs = pκ, where p is the effective near-horizon
projection power.
In the numerical checks it is represented either by a finite-duration phe-
nomenological source, a near-horizon test-particle source, or a radial-normalized source proxy.
All versions are evaluated with the same envelope-fitting operator used for the controlled tests
summarized in the main text.
Source-projection sensitivity
The source-projection power p is not fitted to the residual data in the fiducial calculation. We
set p = 1 as the leading redshift-locked prescription and then vary it to measure how strongly
the envelope damping depends on the source channel. For the GW250114 window, κt = 1.26–
5.04, and the screened impulse-response rate αimp = 0.936, Eq. (4) gives γeff/κ = 0.623 at p = 1.
Varying p over 0.9–1.1 gives 0.573–0.667. As a more conservative boundary check, varying it
over 0.7–1.3 gives 0.459–0.740. These checks show that the reduced damping is not obtained by
tuning p to a single value, but they also prevent over-stating γeff/κ ≃0.6 as a universal number.

GW231226 residual profile
The GW231226 residual test uses the public 4096 Hz H1 and L1 GWOSC strain files and the
public parameter-estimation samples to set the event time, detector response and remnant pos-
terior. The locked template uses Eq. (4) to convert each Kerr posterior sample into a predicted
envelope damping, so the profiled damping rate is γeff. For each grid point in (fH, γeff) we
morph the complex direct-wave mode, project it into H1 and L1, and compute the normalized-
basis evidence after marginalizing over a discrete time-offset grid and profiling over a complex
amplitude. Off-source windows are generated by shifting the same construction away from the
event time. For Fig. 5, the two-dimensional surface is profiled over fH and replotted as a one-
dimensional profile in η = γeff/κ. The curve is used as a residual-level consistency check, not
as a full posterior credible interval or a multi-event hierarchical likelihood.
