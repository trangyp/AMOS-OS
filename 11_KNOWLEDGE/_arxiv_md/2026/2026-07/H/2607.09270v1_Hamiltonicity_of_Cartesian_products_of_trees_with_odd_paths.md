Abstract
A {P2, P3}-factor in a graph G is a factor of G in which every component is
a path on two or three vertices. Let T2Pn be the Cartesian product of a tree T
and a path on n vertices. Kao and Weng [9] proved that T2Pn is hamiltonian if
T has a path factor and n is a sufficiently large even integer. In this article we
prove that, for every odd n, there exists a tree T of maximum degree 4 that has a
{P2, P3}-factor such that T2Pn is not hamiltonian, thereby refuting a conjecture
given in [9].
Key words: Cartesian product, hamiltonian graph.
AMS subject classification (2020): 05C45, 05C76.

Introduction
There exist several degree conditions which guarantee hamiltonicity of a graph. One
of the most widely known is Dirac’s condition [5], which states that a graph of order n
and minimum degree at least n/2 is hamiltonian. Several generalizations of this result
were obtained later; see [2, 3, 6, 10].
Contrary to the (rather natural and intuitive) restrictions on minimum degree of a
graph, hamiltonicity of Cartesian products depends on the maximum degrees and the
orders of factors. Batagelj and Pisanski [1] characterized hamiltonian products T2Cn,
where T is a tree and Cn is a cycle on n vertices.
Theorem 1.1 [1] Let T be a tree of maximum degree ∆. Then T2Cn is hamiltonian
if and only if n ≥∆.
For product of a tree T with an even path Pn a sufficient condition for hamiltonicity
of T2Pn is given in [9]. If T2Pn is hamiltonian, then T has a {P2, P3}-factor (see [7]),
and hence any sufficient condition for hamiltonicity of T2Pn must assume that T has
a {P2, P3}-factor. The condition given in [9] bounds maximum degree ∆(T) in terms
of n.

Theorem 1.2 [9] For any tree T the Cartesian product T2Pn is hamiltonian if one
of the following holds:
(a) T has a perfect matching and n ≥∆(T)
(b) T has a {P2, P3}-factor, n is an even integer, and n ≥4∆(T) −2.
In [7] the authors proved that the bound n ≥4∆(T) −2 in (b) is best possible.
Both theorems above assert the following: if maximum degree of one factor of G2H is
sufficiently small compared to the order of the other factor, then G2H is hamiltonian.
It is interesting that the converse is also tacitly true — as follows from Theorem 1.3.
Let ∇(G) be the minimum k such that G has a spanning tree of maximum degree
k. In [4] (see also [8]) the authors have proven the following.
Theorem 1.3 [4] Let G and H be connected graphs. If G2H is hamiltonian, then
∇(G) ≤|V (H)| + 1 and ∇(H) ≤|V (G)| + 1.
Hence hamiltonian products G2H have the property that the maximum degree of a
spanning tree of G is bounded by the order of H (and vice versa).
The proof of Theorem 1.1 is based on the fact that {x}2Cn, which is isomorphic to
Cn, is a hamiltonian graph. To construct a Hamilton cycle in T2Cn we first construct
a Hamilton cycle in each {x}2Cn, where x ∈V (T). If ∆(T) ≤n we can then connect
together all these cycles to obtain a Hamilton cycle in T2Cn.
If T has a {P2, P3}-factorr and n is even, we use a similar idea to prove that T2Pn is
hamiltonian. Here it is essential that both P22Pn and P32Pn are hamiltonian graphs,
and to construct a Hamilton cycle in T2Pn we start with a {P2, P3}-factor ∪Gi of T,
where each Gi is a P2 or a P3, and then we construct a Hamilton cycle in each Gi2Pn.
If ∆(T) is sufficiently small we can then connect all these cycles together to obtain a
Hamilton cycle in T2Pn.
This approach does not work when n is odd, for the simple reason that P32Pn is
not hamiltonian. As we shall see hamiltonicity of G2Pn does not depend on maximum
degree of G, contrary to the conjecture raised in [9].
Conjecture 1.4 [9] Let G be a graph with a {P2, P3}-factor and n ≥4∆(G) −2. If
G2Pn is balanced bipartite, then G2Pn is hamiltonian.
In particular, if n is odd, T2Pn might be non-hamiltonian even if n is arbitrarily
large relative to ∆(T). In this article we refute the above conjecture by proving that
for every odd integer n there exists a graph G of maximum degree four, that has
{P2, P3}-factor, such that for every odd i ≤n the graph G2Pi is not hamiltonian.
Theorem 1.5 For every positive integer n there exists a balanced bipartite graph G of
maximum degree four, that has a {P2, P3}-factor, such that G2Pi is not hamiltonian
for every odd i ≤n.

In the rest of the introduction we give the notation and the terminology. Let Pn be
the path on n vertices. The length of a path is the number of edges it contains. A path
with one vertex is called a trivial path. A set of pairwise disjoint paths P in a graph
G is called a path cover of G if S
P∈P V (P) = V (G) (we allow that P contains trivial
paths). Hence the union of paths in P is a path factor of G – a spanning subgraph
whose components are paths. We use the notation P to denote a path cover or a path
factor, and it is clear from the context which of the two we are referring to.
If P is a path cover (or a path factor) of a graph G, then we denote by E0(P) the
set of endvertices of trivial paths in P and by E1(P) the set of endvertices of nontrivial
paths in P. We define E(P) = E0(P) ∪E1(P).
If P is a path cover of a graph G, and R is a subgraph of G, then let PR =
{P ∩R | P ∈P}.
Let G = (V (G), E(G)) and H = (V (H), E(H)) be graphs. The Cartesian prod-
uct of graphs G and H is the graph, denoted as G2H, with vertex set V (G2H) =
V (G) × V (H) where vertices (g1, h1) and (g2, h2) are adjacent in G2H if g1 = g2 and
h1h2 ∈E(H) or g1g2 ∈E(G) and h1 = h2.
Let G be a graph and H a subgraph of G. If x and y are vertices of G that are
adjacent in H we say that x is an H-neighbor of y.
For any positive integer n we define [n] = {1, . . . , n}. We assume (throughout the
paper) that V (Pn) = [n] and hence V (P32Pn) = [3] × [n]. Let G = P32Pn, and let E
and O be subsets of V (G) defined as follows
E = {(i, k) | i ∈[3], k is even} and O = {(i, k) | i ∈[3], k is odd} .
