Abstract
A graph class is k-WQO if its k-labeled graphs are well-quasi-ordered under label-preserving induced
subgraph embeddings. We show that every hereditary graph class that is 2-WQO has bounded clique-
width. Combined with the recent result of Dumas and Lopez, this confirms a long-standing conjecture
of Pouzet: A hereditary graph class is 2-WQO if and only if it is k-WQO for all k ⩾2, if and only if it is
∀-WQO, that is, its labeled graphs are well-quasi-ordered for every possible well-quasi-ordered label set.
Our proof builds on a recent structure/non-structure dichotomy for the model theoretic notion of
monadic dependence by Dreier, M¨ahlmann, and Toru´nczyk. Through the non-structure characterization
by forbidden induced subgraphs, we show that every hereditary 2-WQO graph class is monadically
dependent. Leveraging the Ramsey-theoretic structural properties provided by monadic dependence,
we then establish bounded clique-width by ruling out the existence of large well-linked sets, which are
the canonical obstructions for clique-width.

Introduction
A graph class C is well-quasi-ordered (WQO) if it contains no infinite antichain under the induced subgraph
(embedding) relation. Further, if k is a positive integer, we say that C is k-WQO if the class of all vertex-
labelled graphs from C with labels in [k] is well-quasi-ordered under label-preserving embeddings.
For example, the class of cycles is not WQO, while the class of paths is WQO, but it is not 2-WQO, since
labelling the two endpoints with a distinguishing label creates an infinite antichain. On the other hand, the
class of linear orders (viewed as directed graphs) is k-WQO for all k ⩾1 by Higman’s lemma, and so is the
class of cographs [4] (see also [2, Thm. 1]).
The notion of k-WQO was studied by Pouzet around 1972, who observed that already 2-WQO is a very
strong assumption and asked whether for classes of relational structures which are hereditary (that is,
closed under taking induced substructures) 2-WQO implies k-WQO for all k ⩾1. This is a rephrasing of a
question posed in [11, §2.4], which has been repeated by Fra¨ıss´e in [7, Chap. 12, §3.1]. In the setting of
graphs, this has been repeated by Daligault, Rao, and Thomass´e [3] in the modern formulation, as follows:

Conjecture 1.1 ([11], [3, Conj. 1]). For hereditary graph classes, 2-WQO implies k-WQO for all k ⩾1.
In fact, Pouzet [12, Problems 9 (1)] conjectured that for hereditary classes, 2-WQO even implies ∀-WQO1,
which means that the class remains WQO under embeddings which preserve or increase the labels, when
the vertex labels are drawn from an arbitrary fixed well-quasi-order (another variant of this conjecture
appears in [13, Problem 3]).
Daligault, Rao, and Thomass´e [3, Conj. 5] proposed a route towards Pouzet’s conjecture, by posing the
following structural conjecture:
Conjecture 1.2 ([3]). Every hereditary 2-WQO graph class has bounded clique-width.
Clique-width is one of the central width parameters for hereditary graph classes. It can be seen as
an extension of tree-width to dense graphs. Roughly, graphs of bounded clique-width can be encoded
in trees in a simple fashion (namely, by a fixed MSO formula). Thus, the conjecture of Daligault, Rao,
and Thomass´e gives an unexpected connection between a purely order-theoretic assumption and a strong
structural tameness property of hereditary graph classes. This is reminiscent to the connection of minor-
closed classes—which are famously well-quasi ordered by the minor relation—and tree-width discovered by
Robertson and Seymour.
Thanks to the very restricted structure of graphs of bounded clique-width, and their close relationship
to trees, Dumas and Lopez [6, Thm. 4] were able to confirm Pouzet’s conjecture for all graph classes of
bounded clique-width, using methods from automata theory. Thus, to confirm Pouzet’s conjecture for
hereditary graph classes, it remains to establish the conjecture of Daligault, Rao, and Thomass´e.
Our main result does exactly this.
Theorem 1.3. Every hereditary 2-WQO graph class has bounded clique-width.
Combined with the result of Dumas and Lopez [6], this proves the following.
Theorem 1.4. The following conditions are equivalent for hereditary graph classes:
1. 2-WQO,
2. k-WQO for all k ⩾1,
3. ∀-WQO.
This confirms two conjectures of Pouzet, in the setting of graphs: Conjecture 1.1 and the conjecture
about ∀-WQO [12, Problems 9 (1)]. Also this implies that hereditary 2-WQO graph classes have exponential
speed—contain at most 2O(n) graphs with n vertices up to isomorphism—answering a question of Pouzet and
Sobrani (2003), see [13, Problem 5]. This follows from Theorem 1.3, since classes of bounded clique-width
are well-known to have at most exponential speed [1].
Note that by [9], there are hereditary WQO graph classes with unbounded clique-width, so the assump-
tion of 2-WQO is indeed necessary for our result2.
To prove Theorem 1.3, we introduce a family of forbidden induced configurations, which we simply call
patterns. These are a generalization of the path-like structures that were recently used by M¨ahlmann [10]
to characterize graph classes of unbounded shrub-depth. On the one hand, the patterns are robust and easy
to exhibit, on the other hand, it is easy to show that every hereditary 2-WQO class is pattern-free: from
arbitrarily large patterns one can directly construct a labelled antichain. This is the first step of the proof;
see Figure 1 for a diagrammatic overview of the proof.

As a next step, we connect pattern-freeness to a general model-theoretic notion of tameness, namely
monadic dependence, introduced by Shelah [14]. Monadically dependent graph classes have been recently
characterized in terms of forbidden obstructions by Dreier, M¨ahlmann, and Toru´nczyk [5]. We observe that
each of the forbidden obstructions to monadic dependence contains arbitrarily large patterns. It follows
that every pattern-free graph class is monadically dependent.
The final step is to prove that monadically dependent, 2-WQO classes have bounded clique-width.
The insulation property developed in [5] provides a Ramsey-theoretic notion of structure in monadically
dependent graphs. Combining the insulation property with 2-WQO, we are able to distribute every
sufficiently large vertex set across a long linear partition whose intermediate blocks act as separators of low
cut-rank. This lets us exclude the existence of large well-linked sets, which are obstructions characterizing
bounded rank-width by the result of Oum and Seymour [8]. Since bounded rank-width is equivalent to
bounded clique-width, this completes the proof of Theorem 1.3.
Conceptually, our result connects three lines of work that had so far evolved largely in parallel: labelled
well-quasi-ordering, structural width measures such as rank-width and clique-width, and the recent model-
theoretic structure theory of monadically dependent graph classes. In particular, it shows that a seemingly
weak labelled order-theoretic assumption already forces one of the central structural tameness properties
of hereditary graph classes.

Preliminaries
Graphs.
We consider finite, simple, undirected graphs. A graph class is hereditary if it is closed under
taking induced subgraphs.
Well-quasi orders.
A quasi-order is a reflexive and transitive binary relation ⩽on some domain. A well
quasi-order (WQO) is a quasi-order ⩽such that for every infinite sequence q1, q2, . . . of elements from the
domain, there exist i < j such that qi ⩽qj. Equivalently, there are no infinite antichains and no infinite
descending sequences.
For a positive integer k, a graph class C is k-WQO if the class of k-labeled graphs from C is WQO
under label-preserving induced subgraph embeddings. More precisely, C is k-WQO if for every infinite
sequence (G1, ℓ1), (G2, ℓ2), . . . where each Gi ∈C and ℓi : V (Gi) →[k] is a vertex-labeling, there exist
i < j and an injective φ: V (Gi) →V (Gj) such that
• uv ∈E(Gi) ⇔φ(u)φ(v) ∈E(Gj) for all u, v ∈V (Gi), and
• ℓj(φ(v)) = ℓi(v) for all v ∈V (Gi).

Rank-width.
Instead of bounding the clique-width of hereditary 2-WQO classes, we will bound their
rank-width, a parameter that is functionally equivalent to clique-width:
Theorem 2.1 ([8, Prop. 6.3]). A graph class has bounded clique-width if and only if it has bounded rank-width.
The single property of rank-width we will use is its characterization by Oum and Seymour in terms of
forbidden obstructions which we now describe. The rank of two disjoint vertex sets A, B of a graph G,
denoted rkG(A, B), is defined as the rank, over the two-element field, of the (0, 1)-matrix with rows A
and columns B, where the entry at row a ∈A and column b ∈B is 1 if ab ∈E(G) and 0 otherwise. For a
single set A ⊆V (G) we define rkG(A) := rk(A, V (G) −A). (This is also known as the cutrank of A.)
A set W of vertices of G is well-linked, if for every bipartition X, Y of W and every set Z with
X ⊆Z ⊆V (G) −Y , the cut-rank of Z satisfies rkG(Z) ⩾min(|X|, |Y |).
Theorem 2.2 ([8, Thm. 5.2]). Every graph of rank-width greater than k contains a well-linked set of size k.
We will also need the following observation.
Observation 2.3. If W is well-linked in G, then also every subset W ′ of W is well-linked in G.
Proof. Consider a bipartition X′, Y ′ of W ′ and a set Z with X′ ⊆Z ⊆V (G) −Y ′. Now for X := W ∩Z
and Y := W −Z we have
rkG(Z) ⩾min(|X|, |Y |) ⩾min(|X′|, |Y ′|)

Pattern-free implies monadically dependent
The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.
Theorem 4.1. Every pattern-free graph class is monadically dependent.
Monadic dependence originates in model theory [14] (it is also called monadic NIP) and is defined
through first-order transductions. We will instead use the combinatorial characterization through forbidden
induced subgraphs given by [5], which we will now recall.
For r ⩾1, the star r-crossing of order n is the r-subdivision of Kn,n (the biclique of order n). More
precisely, it consists of roots a1, . . . , an and b1, . . . , bn together with r-vertex paths {πi,j : i, j ∈[n]} that
are pairwise vertex-disjoint (see Figure 3). We write π(i, j, t) for the tth vertex of path πi,j, for t ∈[r],

The clique r-crossing of order n is the graph obtained from the star r-crossing of order n by turning the

Bounding the rank-width
In this section we prove our main theorem: every hereditary 2-WQO graph class has bounded rank-width
(equivalently: bounded clique-width). We have previously established that hereditary 2-WQO classes are
pattern-free, and pattern-free classes are monadically dependent. For the proof of our main theorem, we
distill the relevant structure of pattern-free classes derived from monadic dependence into the following
Ramsey-theoretic lemma, whose proof is sketched in Section 6. The full proof can be found in Section D.
Lemma 5.1 (Separator Lemma). For every pattern-free graph class C , there are f : N →N and k ∈N such
that for every graph G ∈C and set X ⊆V (G) of size at least f(m) there exists a size m partition P1, . . . , Pm
of V (G) such that:
1. X intersects each of the sets P1, . . . , Pm,
2. for every 1 ⩽α ⩽m:
rkG(L, R) ⩽k,
where L := P1 ∪. . . ∪Pα−1 and R := Pα+1 ∪. . . ∪Pm.
Informally, the lemma states that in any pattern-free graph class, a large set X can always be “spread
out” across a partition of the vertices into ordered parts P1, . . . , Pm, such that each part Pα acts as low-rank
separator between the parts P1, . . . , Pα−1 left of it and the parts Pα+1, . . . , Pm right of it. The rank bound k
is a constant depending only on the graph class, not on the graph or the size of X. We will use Lemma 5.1
now, but explain its proof later in Section 6.
In order to prove the main theorem, we use one more lemma, which is a standard WQO argument. We
will later use the small size of H to argue that one of the parts from the partition in Lemma 5.1 is disjoint
from H, effectively separating it.
Lemma 5.2. For every hereditary 2-WQO graph class C , there is a function f : N →N such that for every
k ∈N and graph G ∈C , if G contains a set W ⊆V (G) of size k that is well-linked in G, then G contains an

An insulator is an Oh(1)-flip H of the graph G, in which the neighborhood of the set A is well-controlled.
An example is depicted in Figure 7 (a). The vertices of A are all marked with a star symbol. In the insulator,
the neighborhood of A is organized in a grid like fashion. Each column of the grid contains a vertex of A in
its bottom cell, and the number of rows of the grid is equal to the height h of the insulator. The union of
the leftmost column, the rightmost column, and the top row of the grid form its boundary, while the rest of
the grid is called the interior. The insulator controls the interior of the grid as follows. In the graph H:
1. No vertex outside the grid is adjacent to any interior vertex.
2. Interior vertices in non-consecutive rows are non-adjacent.
3. There is an Oh(1)-coloring of V (H) such that adjacency between two interior vertices u and v in
non-consecutive columns depends only on their colors and on the left-to-right order of u and v in
the grid.
4. Every interior vertex has
• at least one neighbor in the cell directly below it, and
• no neighbors in the row below that are strictly to its left.
A complete formal definition of an insulator is given in Definition D.2 in the appendix. Crucially the
insulator controls all edges except:
• edges from outside vertices towards the boundary, and
• edges between vertices in neighboring cells.
We have marked such exceptional edges in red in Figure 7 (a). Intuitively, the greater we choose the height h
of the insulator, the farther we push away the boundary from the set A, and the more control we gain. (The
cost of this is the rising complexity of the Oh(1)-flip H and the Oh(1)-coloring.)
So far, we have just applied the insulation property. The key insight is that inside an insulator of
height h, if many of its top cells are occupied, then we find an (ℓ, h −1)-pattern, where ℓis proportional to
the size of A. Such a pattern is highlighted in Figure 7 (b). The layers of the pattern embed into the rows of
the insulator, and we choose the pattern in non-consecutive columns. By pigeonholing over the parts of the
Oh(1)-flip and over the Oh(1) colors, we assure that each layer is a clique or an independent set.
We assume A to be large and the graph class that we work in to be pattern-free. Hence, we know that
for some fixed h ∈N, we can build insulators in which most of the top cells must be empty. For this proof
sketch we assume all the top cells are empty. (Otherwise, we have a large pattern, as argued above.) This is
depicted in Figure 7 (c). Now vertices from outside the grid can only connect to the leftmost and rightmost
column of the insulator. The interior columns C1, . . . , Cm of this insulator form the partition P1, . . . , Pm of
V (G) (each column forms its own part), where we put all the vertices from outside the interior into the part
P1. By construction, each part contains a vertex from X. Now if we ignore any two columns/parts Pα and
Pβ, this cuts out a middle part M := Pα+1 ∪. . . ∪Pβ−1 that is completely in the interior of the insulator
and whose neighboring columns are exactly the ignored parts Pα and Pβ. Therefore, the adjacency of
L ⊎R := V (G) −(M ∪Pα ∪Pβ) towards M is completely controlled by the insulator. This is visualized in
