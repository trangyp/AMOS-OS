Abstract:
We develop a data-driven framework based on Neural ODEs that learns the
bulk metric functions and the U(1) gauge potential of a static, planar-symmetric black
hole from boundary fermionic spectral functions. After validating the framework on the
Einstein–Maxwell and Gubser–Rocha models with high accuracy, we apply it to the strange
metal phase of the cuprate (Pb, Bi)2Sr2−xLaxCuO6+δ within a semi-holographic setting,
taking as input the spectral data generated from the extended power-law liquid (PLL)
model calibrated by angle-resolved photoemission measurements.
At low temperatures
and near-optimal doping, we show that the normalized extended PLL model can be well
described by an effective representative of a conformal class near AdS2×R2 black holes with
a nearly vanishing gauge potential (qAt ∼10−4 eV). A key structural observation is that
our probe fermion is massless and therefore insensitive to the conformal factor Ω(z). This
further implies that fixing the macroscopic thermodynamics such as the electronic specific
heat would require independent input beyond the fermionic spectra. We also delineate
the applicability boundaries of our framework: the learned effective model remains viable
across doping at low temperatures, with the loss increasing mildly as the samples move away
from the marginal Fermi liquid point toward the overdoped side; the principal limitation
is temperature, where both the loss and qAt grow substantially.

Introduction
Strange metals, observed in the normal state of cuprate high-temperature superconduc-
tors, exhibit anomalous properties that defy the Fermi liquid paradigm [1–3]: a linear-in-
temperature resistivity ρ ∝T [4], a Hall angle cotangent cot θH ∝T 2 implying two distinct
scattering rates [5, 6], and a logarithmic specific heat C/T ∝ln(1/T) signaling quantum
critical behavior [7–9]. These point to Planckian dissipation with τ −1

and an intimate link between strange metallicity and superconductivity [13, 14]. Various
theoretical frameworks have been proposed, including quantum critical scenarios [15, 16],
the marginal Fermi liquid theory [17, 18], and solvable SYK-type models [19–22], yet a
complete microscopic understanding remains elusive.
AdS/CFT correspondence [23–25] offers a non-perturbative approach to strongly cor-
related systems by mapping a d-dimensional conformal field theory to a (d+1)-dimensional
gravitational theory in anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetime, where the radial direction encodes
the renormalization group flow [26–29]. The strong-weak nature of the duality has en-
abled holographic methods to address problems in condensed matter [30, 31], QCD [32–
34], and quantum information [35, 36]. Combined with linear response theory [37], holo-
graphic methods have yielded key results for fermionic response in charged black hole
backgrounds [38–46].
The Reissner-Nordström-AdS (RN-AdS) black hole is a natural
arena, as its low-temperature near-horizon geometry develops an AdS2 × R2 factor that
furnishes a gravitational realization of quantum criticality [42, 47]. Among the various
holographic constructions, the Gubser–Rocha model [48]—an Einstein–Maxwell-dilaton
(EMD) theory—has emerged as one of the most successful: its dilaton coupling produces
a vanishing ground-state entropy, and its near-horizon geometry remains conformal to
AdS2 × R2 [49, 50], successfully reproducing ρ ∝T [51–53]. Blake and Donos [54] further
argued within EMD-Axion theory that both ρ ∝T and cot θH ∝T 2 can be obtained
simultaneously, a conclusion reproduced via the memory-matrix formalism [55].1
The
Gubser–Rocha model has also been applied to plasmons in layered strange metals [57].
Purely holographic models typically require the large-N limit and exact conformal
symmetry—conditions not fully realized in real materials. The semi-holographic frame-
work [58–61] bridges this gap by coupling a weakly coupled UV electron to a strongly corre-
lated holographic IR sector, retaining the predictive power for infrared physics while relax-
ing the requirements of pure holography. Recent studies have shown that semi-holography
can reproduce ρ ∝T over a wide temperature range and cot θH ∝T 2 above the Fermi
temperature [62–64].
Of particular relevance to this work, the high-precision angle-resolved photoemission
spectroscopy (ARPES) measurements by Smit et al. [65] revealed non-Lorentzian nodal
spectral lineshapes in the cuprate (Pb, Bi)2Sr2−xLaxCuO6+δ that cannot be captured by
the momentum-independent power-law liquid (PLL) model [66], a framework that provides
a unified description of the nodal self-energy across the entire cuprate doping phase dia-
gram with a single smoothly varying exponent α. The observed spectral asymmetry is well
reproduced by promoting the PLL scaling exponent to a momentum-dependent function,
a modification motivated by the Gubser–Rocha model within the semi-holographic frame-
work. Building on this insight, Mauri et al. [67] constructed a detailed semi-holographic
model that captures the lineshape asymmetry across doping levels at low temperatures.
All the approaches discussed above address forward problems: computing boundary
observables from a given bulk theory. Growing attention has been devoted to the comple-

of fundamental significance for spacetime emergence and quantum gravity [68, 69] as well
as for bottom-up model building. Various methods include smearing-function reconstruc-
tion for bulk operators [70, 71], geodesic probes via boundary two-point functions [72, 73],
inverse scattering from scalar and vector correlators [74, 75], and the derivation of the
(1+1)d geometry using generalized free fields but without assuming symmetry match-
ing [76, 77]. Quantum information-theoretic approaches have also proven fruitful, includ-
ing entanglement wedge reconstruction [78–81], holographic entanglement entropy [82, 83],
complexity [84–87], and tensor networks [88–92]. Pole-skipping points of boundary Green’s
functions provide yet another window into the near-horizon geometry [93–95]. While these
methods have significantly advanced our understanding of holographic encoding, they often
rely on specific assumptions about boundary observables or the bulk geometry.
Machine learning has opened new avenues for holographic inverse problems. Hashimoto
et al. [96] established the AdS/DL correspondence by interpreting the radial coordinate
as network depth.
Neural ODEs [97], which parameterize the derivative of a hidden
state with a neural network, are naturally suited to holographic radial evolution, while
Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [98] complement this by incorporating equa-
tions of motion into the loss function. These techniques have been applied to reconstruct
bulk geometries from QCD observables [99–105], shear viscosity [106, 107], equations of
state [108, 109], optical conductivity [110–112], and entanglement entropy [113, 114]. Ex-
tensions beyond asymptotically AdS spacetimes have also been explored recently [115].
In this work, we develop a data-driven framework based on Neural ODEs that simul-
taneously learns the metric functions and the gauge potential of a static, planar-symmetric
black hole from boundary fermionic spectra. As emphasized in Chapter 9 of Ref. [30], the
single-fermion Green’s function, directly accessed by ARPES, is a uniquely powerful probe
of strongly correlated electron matter: unlike the collective bosonic responses that underlie
previous holographic machine-learning studies, it carries the imprint of fermionic quantum
statistics, resolves the full (ω, k) dependence rather than only the small-momentum hy-
drodynamic regime, and directly registers the Fermi-surface structure of the finite-density
ground state.
To establish a baseline, we first validate our algorithm on two analytically tractable
models—the Einstein–Maxwell theory and the Gubser–Rocha model—and demonstrate
sub-percent reconstruction accuracy in both cases. Then we apply the framework to the
cuprate strange metal phase within a semi-holographic setting. A key technical ingredient
is a boundary extraction scheme adapted to conformal-to-AdS2×R2 asymptotics, where the
response coeﬀicient is subleading and the standard prescription fails: we extract the spectral
function directly from the conserved radial flux of the Dirac field, which bypasses the sub-
leading response coeﬀicient entirely. Using spectral data generated from the extended PLL
model [65, 67], we investigate the learned effective bulk geometry, its gauge-invariant char-
acterization and the temperature degeneracy, the near-vanishing gauge potential and the
particle-hole symmetry, the spectral invisibility of the conformal factor and the decoupled
thermodynamics, and the applicability boundaries of the conformal-to-AdS2 description
across temperatures and doping levels.

3
Semi-Holographic Power-Law Liquid
The purely holographic calculation reviewed above yields the Green’s function of a com-
posite operator, not the physical single-electron spectral function measured in ARPES. To
bridge this gap, we employ the semi-holographic framework together with the phenomeno-
logical PLL model for strange metals [66]. As shown in Ref. [67], semi-holography supplies
the key ingredient for extending the PLL model to a momentum-dependent scaling.
3.1

5.3
Training Protocol
We now specify the numerical integration scheme and the optimization strategy. We employ
the Tsitouras 5(4) method [118], an explicit adaptive Runge-Kutta solver well suited to
non-stiff problems, with absolute and relative tolerances set to 10−6.
To optimize the parameters eﬀiciently, we adopt a multi-stage training protocol that
accelerates convergence by exploiting a diversity of good initializations. The procedure
consists of four stages:
Stage 0: Seed Model Selection. We initialize 10 candidate models with distinct ran-
dom seeds and train them in parallel using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate
of 10−3 and a batch size of 64. Training is halted for all models once the first model
completes 500 epochs, and the 5 models with the lowest loss are selected as seed
models.
Stage 1: Variant Generation and Fine-Tuning. For each of the 5 seed models, we
retain the original and generate 3 additional variants by injecting 10% Gaussian noise
into the network weights, yielding 20 models in total. These models then undergo a
rapid fine-tuning phase with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 10−4, batch size 128,
100 epochs), after which the top 10 models are retained.
Stage 2: Deep Optimization. The 10 surviving models are trained further to settle into
deeper loss minima, using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 10−5 and a
batch size of 256 for up to 400 epochs. The top 5 models are selected for the final
stage.
Stage 3: Precision Polishing (BFGS). The remaining 5 models undergo second-order
optimization via the BFGS algorithm to achieve high numerical precision. The opti-
mization proceeds for up to 2000 steps or until both relative and absolute tolerances
of 10−9 are satisfied.
This progressive strategy significantly reduces the overall training time while eﬀiciently
locating high-quality solutions in parameter space. The tabulated loss and mean relative
error (MRE) below are evaluated using the model with the lowest loss among the five
candidates from the final training stage. For the bulk-field figures with confidence bands,
we first compute the averages and pointwise 95% confidence intervals on a common z
grid. The curves shown in the gauge-fixed u coordinate, and in Appendix E also in the
temperature-mapped v coordinate, are then obtained by transforming this averaged z-frame
representative.

6.3
Application to Cuprate Strange Metals
The extended PLL scaling is inspired by the IR Green’s function of the Gubser–Rocha
model in the semi-holographic framework. The related Dirac equation reduces—as shown
in Section 3.3 and Appendix A—to that of a geometry conformal to the AdS2 × R2 black
hole. This leads us to adopt the second ansatz, Eq. (4.6), which parameterizes a class of
geometries asymptotically conformal to AdS2 × R2. For the training data, two options
present themselves: the analytical IR Green’s function of the Gubser–Rocha model, or the
phenomenological extended PLL model. The former elegantly grounds the momentum-

tribution to the self-energy at elevated temperatures when fitting experimental data [67].

description of the finite-temperature self-energy over a much broader range, as the target
spectral data. This choice turns the problem into an inverse test: rather than assuming
a known bulk dual, we use Neural ODEs to explore whether, and over what ranges of
doping and temperature, this compact ARPES-calibrated self-energy admits an effective
geometric realization beyond the fixed analytical Gubser–Rocha benchmark.

Conclusion and Discussion
In this work, we developed a data-driven framework based on Neural ODEs to learn the ef-
fective bulk metric functions and the U(1) gauge potential from frequency- and momentum-
dependent fermionic spectral functions. After demonstrating sub-percent accuracy on the
Einstein–Maxwell and Gubser–Rocha models, we applied the framework to the extended
PLL model of the cuprate strange metal phase in the semi-holographic setting.
At low temperatures and near-optimal doping, our work reveals that the normalized
extended-PLL model can be described by a family of geometries conformal to near-AdS2 ×
R2 black holes with a nearly vanishing gauge potential (qAt ∼10−4 eV). This near-zero
gauge potential is consistent with the particle-hole symmetry inherent in the PLL model.

Our study highlights two key insights. First, fermionic spectra alone cannot uniquely
determine the full bulk geometry.
A massless Dirac fermion is insensitive to the con-
formal factor Ω(z). Consequently, predicting macroscopic thermodynamics—such as the
entropy density or the electronic specific heat—requires independent experimental inputs
to fix Ω(1, T) at the horizon. Notably, this perspective is in line with recent studies of
quantum-critical metals, where quantum Monte Carlo simulations reveal that non-Fermi-
liquid signatures in self-energy and transport need not coincide with a broad enhancement
of C/T [119], and Luttinger–Ward–Eliashberg analyses further show that, in the controlled
normal-state approximation, the thermal fermionic self-energy cancels from the electronic
free-energy contribution, leaving the quantum-critical part of the specific heat encoded
in the dressed bosonic propagator rather than directly inferable from single-particle spec-
tra [120].
Second, we identified clear applicability boundaries for the conformal-to-AdS2 geome-
try within the assumed semi-holographic decomposition and minimal Dirac-probe dynam-
ics. This description, with a near-vanishing qAt, is robust across all doping levels at low
temperatures; among the available samples, the loss is smallest for the one closest to the
marginal Fermi liquid point (α = 1/2). At elevated temperatures, by contrast, the loss
increases; the learned blackening factor F(u) deviates from the fitted AdS2 baseline; qAt
becomes non-negligible and develops unnatural oscillations; the final-candidate profiles be-
come more dispersed; and the reconstructed spectrum develops a sizable ω-odd component

when extrapolated to unoccupied states (ω > 0). Since the Neural ODE already explores
a broad family of conformal-to-AdS2 geometries, these persistent residuals suggest that
refining the dual geometry within this conformal class alone cannot capture the target
phenomenology at elevated temperatures.
These findings point to several concrete directions for future work. A natural next step
is to relax the assumptions that define the present inverse problem: the strict conformal
AdS2 ×R2 boundary condition, the minimal massless Dirac dynamics of the probe fermion,
and the clean factorization of the non-holographic contributions G0(ω) and gk. Comple-
mentary observables, such as macroscopic thermodynamics and Hall-angle scaling, could
then be incorporated to constrain the conformal factor and test whether the same effective
geometry controls both single-particle spectra and transport. Applying the framework to
other strongly correlated systems, including heavy fermion compounds, would further clar-
ify the scope and limitations of holographic and semi-holographic methods in condensed
matter physics.
