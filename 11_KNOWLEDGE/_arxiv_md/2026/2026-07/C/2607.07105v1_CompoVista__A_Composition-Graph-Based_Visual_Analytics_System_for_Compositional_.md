Traditional Chinese Paintings

Abstract—Composition
in
Traditional
Chinese
Paintings
(TCPs) carries spatial, narrative, and cultural-aesthetic mean-
ing. Systematic compositional analysis is therefore important
for understanding their visual language and artistic meaning.
Traditional compositional analysis is mainly qualitative and
interpretation-driven. It supports close reading of individual
paintings, but it is difficult to discover, compare, and verify
compositional patterns across large painting collections. To better
understand these challenges, we conducted a literature review
and in-depth interviews with two art historians. Based on these
findings, we introduce the Composition Graph, a scene-graph-
based representation for TCP composition. It models a painting
through four layers: entities, relations, void space, and context.
Based on this representation, we develop CompoVista, a canvas-
based visual analytics system for composition-oriented explo-
ration of TCPs. CompoVista allows art historians to construct
and revise format-aware painting cohorts through visual queries
and context queries. It also supports cohort-level inspection of
entity distributions and relations, comparison of compositional
differences across cohorts, and tracing aggregate patterns back
to painting-level evidence. We evaluated CompoVista through
a task-based user study with 12 domain-aware participants,
two case studies, and expert interviews. The results show that
CompoVista supports composition-oriented cohort construction,
pattern discovery, iterative refinement, and evidence inspection.
The evaluation also reveals future needs, including clearer result
explanations, fuzzier composition queries, and stronger explo-
ration history management. Our work contributes a composition-
specific structured representation and an integrated visual ana-
lytics workflow for studying TCP composition at collection scale.

methods are mostly designed for general art-historical images

identify visual content or organize external knowledge.
Scene graphs provide a promising entry point for addressing
this gap because they encode the semantic structure of an
image through objects, attributes, and relations [17], [18]. For
TCPs, such a representation can make pictorial entities and
their spatial or semantic relations explicit, thereby making
composition-oriented evidence more searchable and compa-
rable. However, generic scene graphs mainly encode object-
relation semantics. They do not capture the composition-
specific structure that TCP compositional analysis requires:
format-aware spatial organization, void structures, and links
back to individual paintings. More importantly, art-historical
composition research is not only a retrieval problem: structured
representations must be connected to the analytical workflow
of art historians, who move between close reading, cohort
comparison, hypothesis refinement, and evidence verification.
It is therefore necessary to understand how art historians
formulate compositional questions and how such evidence can
be visually inspected, queried, and compared.
To this end, we conducted a formative study with two
domain experts specializing in TCPs, comprising multiple
rounds of semi-structured interviews and a review of literature.
Synthesizing these sources, we identify two challenges (C1–
C2) that make composition-oriented analysis of TCPs difficult
to support at scale. C1. Composition analysis relies on
visual evidence that is hard to define computationally. Art
historians read composition through where pictorial elements
sit, how they relate, and how void is reserved within a for-
mat [1], [2], [19]. Manual readings can preserve this structure
but do not scale, while scalable proxies such as context or
global image similarity discard the structure that makes the
analysis compositional. C2. Compositional claims require
repeated close reading and cross-painting comparison. A
tendency often becomes visible only in the aggregate, yet
becomes trustworthy only when traced back to the paintings
behind it [20]–[22]. The groups worth comparing and patterns
worth trusting emerge during analysis, rather than being fixed
in advance. Grouping, comparison, and verification proceed
together and are revised as understanding grows.
To address these challenges, we introduce the Composition
Graph, a structured representation that makes pictorial ele-
ments, spatial relations, void space, and context information
explicit and queryable (C1). Based on this representation, we
develop CompoVista, which supports an iterative workflow for
forming format-aware cohorts, comparing aggregate patterns,
and tracing them back to individual paintings for verification
(C2). Through structured queries based on keywords, entity
positions, and structural relations, art historians can con-
struct composition-oriented cohorts, interpret retrieval results,
examine emerging observations, and trace related composi-
tional patterns. We evaluated CompoVista through a task-
based user study, two case studies, and expert interviews.
The user study assessed workflow usability and perceived
usefulness with domain-aware participants; the case studies
demonstrated how the system supports composition-oriented
analysis in representative scenarios; and the expert interviews
examined the domain validity and limitations of cohort con-
struction, comparison, and painting-level evidence inspection.
The results suggest that CompoVista provides a workflow
for connecting structured composition retrieval, cohort-level
visual summaries, and painting-level evidence inspection. The
primary contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
• We identify challenges and design requirements for
composition-oriented TCP analysis, and introduce the
Composition Graph for structured composition query
and comparison.
• We develop CompoVista, a canvas-based visual analytics
system built on a scene-graph-based composition mod-
eling and retrieval pipeline, with coordinated views of
entity distributions, relations, and cohort differences.
• We evaluate CompoVista through a task-based user study,
two case studies, and expert interviews, demonstrating its
support for pattern discovery, result interpretation, and
cross-cohort comparison.
II. RELATED WORK
Our research is closely aligned with computational research
on Chinese paintings, scene-graph-based representation and re-
trieval, and visual analytics for cultural heritage. Accordingly,
we review the body of literature surrounding these areas to
clarify the research context, technical foundation, and system-
level positioning of our work.
A. Computational Research on Chinese Paintings
Computational research on Chinese paintings has grown
rapidly across style, authentication, and semantic understand-
ing. For style analysis, methods range from low-level feature
engineering to deep convolutional networks and dedicated
architectures such as MTFFNet [23]–[25], while brushstroke
analysis extracts stroke-level geometric features from digitized
paintings [26]; in authentication, classifiers trained on statis-
tical or deep features support computer-assisted connoisseur-
ship [16]; for semantic understanding, object detection models
identify recurring elements within paintings and are combined
with knowledge graphs for cross-modal retrieval [15]. These
directions, however, engage with visual content at the level
of style, attribution, or object presence rather than the spatial
arrangement of elements.
In contrast, computational work on composition—the spatial
arrangement of pictorial elements—remains markedly sparse.
Among ninety-two papers surveyed by Zhang et al. [7], only
four address compositional analysis directly, and even these
focus on specific tasks such as void detection rather than
supporting broader composition-oriented workflows. Yet com-
position has been a core art-historical methodology for over a
millennium, from Xie He’s Six Principles to Western formal
analysis [3], [4], [27]. We build computational support for this
established methodology through scene-graph representation
and interactive visual analytics.
B. Scene-Graph-Based Representation and Retrieval
Representing pictorial elements and their spatial relations
is a prerequisite for computational composition analysis.

Content-based image retrieval uses global embeddings [28],
[29] that collapse spatial arrangement into flat similar-
ity, leaving compositionally distinct paintings indistinguish-
able [15]. Knowledge graphs for Chinese paintings such as
WuMKG [15] structure external attributes and subject-level
semantics but not spatial relations. Scene graphs [17], [30]
explicitly encode objects, attributes, and spatial relations, with
subgraph matching enabling retrieval by structure [17], [31].
In the TCP domain, semantic annotation [15] and element
detection [32] provide building blocks, but these efforts an-
notate what elements are present rather than modeling spatial
relations as a queryable structure.
Recent surveys note that existing scene graph methods
are designed for natural images with limited object vocab-
ularies and relation types [18], making direct application to
TCP domain non-trivial. Studies on painting-specific scene
graphs [32] and layout-based retrieval [33] begin to address
artwork-specific structures, but their focus remains on generic
fine-art images rather than TCP-specific composition concepts
such as void regions and format-aware placement. Further-
more, existing subgraph matching formulations [31] require
a fully-specified query graph, offering little support for the
partial, exploratory querying typical of art-historical analysis.
Scene graphs thus provide the right representational core, but
realizing their potential for TCP composition analysis requires
a composition-specific vocabulary and flexible interaction.
C. Visual Analytics for Cultural Heritage
Visual analytics has been widely applied to cultural her-
itage research [34]. Existing systems operate at three lev-
els: collection-level systems (VIKUS [35], SeMap [36],
MuseKG [37]) handle metadata browsing without engaging
visual content; inter-work systems (ScrollTimes [38], stylistic
influence networks [39]) analyze relations between paintings
but not structure within each work; intra-work systems visu-
alize detected elements, brushwork, and saliency [26], [32],
[40] but stop short of treating spatial relations as a queryable
analytical primitive.
Windhager et al. [34] identify the lack of integration across
data levels as a key gap in cultural heritage VA. While
collection-level tools provide overview [35] and intra-work
tools provide detail [32], no existing system connects com-
position query with cohort-level comparison and painting-
level inspection in a single workflow. Systems such as Image
Composition Canvas [14] model compositional structures for
general art images, but focus on retrieval rather than interactive
analysis. What remains underexplored is an integrated VA
workflow for composition-centered analysis of TCPs, where
entities, relations, regions, formats, and void space are queried
and interpreted together. Our work fills this niche by operating
at the intra-work composition level and connecting it to
collection-scale retrieval through scene-graph matching.
III. BACKGROUND
This section introduces the relevant background informa-
tion, including compositional analysis in TCP and its related
concepts.
A. Compositional Analysis in Traditional Chinese Painting
When art historians read a single painting, they look not
only at individual motifs but at how the whole picture is
put together. In Chinese painting theory, this is what we call
jingying weizhi or zhangfa: the deliberate arrangement of parts
within the whole [2], [7]. So composition is about how motifs
relate, where they sit and how big they are, how figure and
ground (solid and empty) play off each other, and what format
the picture is in. A few recurring devices shape this order.
Guo Xi’s three distances—the high, deep, and level views—
build a sense of depth [27]. Balancing solid and empty makes
void space an active part of the picture, not just an empty
background [41], [42]. Opening and closing, and host and
guest, spread emphasis across the picture [2]. The format
matters too: a hanging scroll, a right-to-left handscroll, or
an album leaf puts the same elements in a different spatial
setting [19] [43].
Art-historical reading is also comparative. Scholars put
paintings side by side to see how the same kinds of problems
are handled across artists, periods, formats, and subjects [41],
[42]. This turns up recurring composition types—the “great
mountain, great water” of Northern Song landscape, the “one-
corner” and “half-side” of Ma Yuan and Xia Gui, and Ni Zan’s
“one river, two banks” [19], [44]. However, this is still mostly
manual—an expert reads works one by one, spots the patterns
by eye, and slowly groups similar ones. This is hard to scale
once a collection holds hundreds or thousands of digitized
paintings.
B. Concepts in Compositional Analysis
Compositional analysis in TCP involves several kinds of
structure that later become the layers of our Composition
Graph (Sec. V-A). We introduce them here not as a general
taxonomy of painting concepts, but as the analytical vocab-
ulary that must be made explicit and computable: entities,
relations, void space, and context.
• Entities. Entities are the recurring pictorial units through
which composition is organized. We follow the VisTCP
semantic taxonomy [45], which groups entities into
human, natural scenery, animal, and artifact. Because
our work focuses on compositional analysis, especially
landscape composition, we refine natural scenery into
vegetation,
landform, and
water. This gives
six entity categories in our corpus:
human figures,
vegetation,
landform,
water,
animals, and
artefacts.
• Relations. Relations describe how pictorial entities are
spatially arranged and semantically connected. We follow
VisTCP’s distinction between location and event rela-
tions [45]: the former encode spatial placement, such
as along, behind, in, and on, while the latter encode
semantic interactions, such as holding, sitting on, and
watching. These relations provide the structure that allows
compositions to be compared beyond object presence.
• Void (white space). Void, or white space, is a defining
feature of TCP composition. It is not merely the un-
painted background, but an active compositional structure

[46]. We therefore treat white space regions as first-class

• Context. Composition is interpreted together with contex-
tual information such as artist, period, subject, format,
medium, and collection information [43], [46]. These
fields help define a meaningful scope for comparison. In
our model, context is integrated as data that helps define
meaningful scopes for query, grouping, and comparison.
IV. INFORMING THE DESIGN
Building on the two challenges (C1 and C2) identified
in the Introduction, this section reports the formative study
underlying CompoVista and derives the design requirements
(R1–R2) and tasks (T1–T6) that the system must satisfy.
A. Cooperating with Art Historians
We conducted three rounds of in-depth semi-structured in-
terviews with two art historians (A1 and A2) who specialize in
TCP. A1 is a university professor whose research focuses on
Chinese art history and traditional painting studies, with over 9
years of experience. A2 is a museum professional who works
with Chinese painting collections and curatorial practice, with
over 15 years of experience. Their joint expertise covers both
scholarly research and collection-facing interpretation, the two
contexts in which compositional analysis is typically practiced.
The collaboration spanned about 6 months. The interviews
focused on participants’ current workflows, the barriers they
encountered, and their expectations of computational support.
The interviews were conducted in three rounds. The first
round focused on understanding experts’ workflows, analytical
practices, and pain points in composition research. The second
round focused on validating emerging design concepts and
gathering feedback on query representation, cohort construc-
tion, and visual analysis strategies. The third round focused on
evaluating the completed system through expert exploration,
reflection, and discussion of its strengths, limitations, and po-
tential research applications. All sessions were audio-recorded
with consent and transcribed; two researchers independently
coded the transcripts using thematic analysis [47], iteratively
reaching consensus on a shared codebook.
We complemented the interview findings with a synthesis
of 46 papers across TCP computational analysis, scene graph
retrieval, and art-historical methodology [2], [7], [19], [21],
[44], treating the literature as a check on whether interview
themes were idiosyncratic or systemic. The convergence of
these two sources is presented in Sec. IV-B.
B. Requirements and Task Analysis
Both A1 and A2 described compositional analysis as an
iterative process: formulating a compositional hypothesis, ex-
amining patterns within a working set of paintings, contrasting
that set with comparable categories, and returning to individual
paintings for verification. This workflow is consistent with
cultural analytics research on distant viewing [20], [21], but
existing tools provide limited support for it. They do not

explain paintings as structured compositional objects, do not
summarize spatial and relational patterns within a selected
group, and do not help art historians revise cohorts as their

tion research often requires examining large collections rather
than isolated paintings. Existing workflows rely heavily on
manually browsing works one by one, making it difficult to ob-

patterns, and compare differences across artists, dynasties, or
thematic groups. A2 emphasized the limitations of existing
metadata-driven access methods. Retrieval is typically orga-
nized around artist, title, dynasty, or subject labels, making it
difficult to find paintings based on their compositional content,
such as works containing particular entities, spatial arrange-
ments, or layout structures. Together, these limitations show
that existing systems provide limited support for composition-
oriented discovery and analysis. Building on C1 and C2
(Sec. I), we derive two design requirements for supporting
composition-oriented TCP analysis and refine them into six
concrete tasks (T1–T6).
R1 Represent compositional evidence as structured and
inspectable data. The system should make composition-
relevant visual evidence explicit before it is queried, summa-
rized, or compared. Art historians need to see how entities,
spatial positions, relations, void space, and context jointly
shape composition, because these structures are not exposed
by context, global image similarity, or close reading alone [2],
[19]. The resulting evidence should remain inspectable at the
painting, cohort, and cross-cohort levels.
T1 Represent composition as structured visual relations.
Represent each painting’s composition in a structured,
comparable form that captures its TCP-specific entities
and their placement, the relations among them, the
reserved void space, and the contextual information.
T2 Summarize compositional distributions and rela-
tionships within a cohort. Help art historians examine
where entities tend to appear, which entities frequently
occur together, and what relations organize a cohort.
T3 Compare compositional tendencies across compara-
ble cohorts. Help art historians compare how spatial dis-
tributions and relational structures differ across cohorts
defined by artist, dynasty, theme, format, or query.
R2 Support interactive and recursive exploration of com-
positional patterns. Although compositional analysis rests on

V. COMPOSITION MODELING AND ANALYSIS
We transform digitized TCP images, annotations, and con-
text into a Composition Graph and analyze it through
structure-aware retrieval and cross-cohort comparison (Fig. 5).
A. Composition Graph
Informed by R1 and R2, we introduce the Composition
Graph, a structured representation of the composition of a
painting that underpins the structure-aware retrieval (Sec. V-B)
and cross-cohort comparison (Sec. V-C) developed later. An-
alyzing TCP at the level of composition requires capturing
not merely which pictorial elements appear, but how they are
placed, related, and balanced against the reserved void within
a given viewing format [7]. We build on the scene graph [17],
[30], which records objects and their semantic relations, and
lift it to the elements that make TCP composition legible: a
void space layer for the reserved void and a context layer that
situates a painting for valid comparison.
1) Four-layer representation:
For each painting p, the

[30]. We adapt this backbone to TCP composition by using
a TCP-specific entity vocabulary and relation types derived

and context layers extend the scene-graph structure to capture
composition-specific evidence that is central to TCP analysis

B. Structure-Aware Retrieval and Cohort Construction
Recursive, composition-oriented exploration (R2) starts
from a cohort that the user can build and rebuild (T4, T5).
Locating paintings that share a compositional structure, rather
than a keyword or an overall visual look, requires matching
the structure itself [49], [50], which neither metadata filters nor
global image similarity capture [15], [28], [29]. We therefore
retrieve over Composition Graph: a user expresses what to
look for as a composition query, and the matches form a
reusable cohort.
1) Composition query: A user expresses what to look for

CompoVista is built with JavaScript (Vue 3 and D3.js) on

used by the coordinated views.
VII. EVALUATION
We evaluated CompoVista through a task-based user study,
two representative case analyses, and post-study expert in-
terviews with the same participants. Together, these evalua-
tions assess the usefulness and usability of CompoVista for
composition-oriented analysis of TCPs.

C. Expert Interview
After the task-based user study, we conducted one-on-one
semi-structured interviews with the same 12 participants to
better understand how they perceived CompoVista in rela-
tion to TCP compositional analysis. Each interview lasted
approximately 45 minutes to 1 hour and was audio-recorded
with participants’ consent. The interviews focused on three
aspects: whether the system helped participants construct
and revise composition-oriented cohorts, whether the spatial
distributions, relational structures, and painting-level evidence
supported compositional interpretation, and what limitations
or improvements they identified for research or teaching use.
We transcribed the recordings, reviewed the interview notes,
and summarized the open-ended feedback using thematic
analysis [47]. The findings are organized around perceived
analytical value, workflow fit and usability, and limitations
and suggestions.
Perceived analytical value. Participants generally found
that CompoVista helped turn broad art-historical interests
into inspectable visual evidence. P5 noted that the cohort-
based workflow made it easier to “first see what kinds
of paintings are being compared.” P8 emphasized that the
Distribution View helped move beyond individual examples
and judge whether a visual tendency was broader: “When I
look at paintings one by one, I may remember a few typical
examples, but I cannot easily see whether this is a broader
tendency. The distribution view gives me a first sense of the
pattern.” P10 further stressed that overview patterns still need
to be checked against painting-level evidence: “The overview
is useful, but I still need to click back to the actual paintings.
Only after seeing the examples can I judge whether the pattern
really makes sense.” Together with P3’s and P4’s workflows,
these comments suggest that CompoVista supports hypothesis
development across cohort construction, overview analysis,
comparison, and close inspection.
Workflow fit and usability. Participants described the
workflow as close to familiar practices of comparison and
close looking in art-historical analysis. P2 noted that keeping
each cohort as a separate node helped them remember “which
group I am looking at and why it was created.” P6 valued the
link between overview summaries and painting-level evidence
because it supported visual checking: “I do not want to stop at
a statistical pattern. I want to see the paintings behind it and
check whether the visual evidence is convincing.” Participants
also showed different entry preferences. P9 preferred to start
from the Distribution View when examining landscape com-
positions. In contrast, P11 found the
Relations View
more
natural for motif-based questions because it foregrounded
recurring entity associations: “For motif questions, the relation
between things is often more important than the object itself.

Seeing which entities repeatedly appear together helps me
think about the scene.” This echoes the two case workflows. P3

Limitations and suggestions. Experts also identified sev-
eral directions for improvement. First, the system should
explain more clearly why particular paintings are retrieved or
ranked, especially when a match depends on partial spatial
similarity. For experts, this is not only about understanding
system results, but also about judging whether these results
can serve as evidence for further compositional analysis. As
P7 noted, “Although I can see that the result is related to the
query, I still want to know which part of the painting makes it
match the query. This would help me conduct a deeper analysis
of the relevant part.” Second, the system should support more
abstract and fuzzy composition queries. Experts pointed out
that art historians often reason about approximate regions,
visual balance, density, openness, or compositional tendencies
rather than exact object locations. P1 explained that experts
often think in terms of “a general feeling of balance, density,
or openness” rather than specific object positions. Third, the
system needs stronger history management to support longer
exploration sessions. P12 suggested that the system should
help scholars “return to the groups created at the beginning
of the exploration and remember why a comparison was made
in the first place.” The motivation behind each exploratory step
is especially important for sustained research processes.
Experts also noted several limitations. They mentioned a
learning cost for interpreting comparison encodings, empha-
sized that results depend on annotation quality, and suggested
caution when comparing paintings across formats. They also
noted that handscrolls are currently excluded from normalized
spatial analysis because their sequential viewing structure
differs from hanging scrolls, album leaves, and round fans.
VIII. DISCUSSION
Based on the task-based user study, the two case studies, and
the expert interviews, this section discusses what CompoVista
suggests for composition-oriented visual analytics of TCPs.
We first summarize the key findings, then derive design
implications and outline limitations and future work.
A. Key Findings
Inspectable compositional evidence. The evaluation sug-
gests that the value of CompoVista is not to automatically
determine art-historical meaning, but to help scholars inspect
compositional evidence. Traditional compositional analysis
concerns where entities appear, how they relate to each other,
how white space is organized, and how these structures work
within different painting formats [1], [2], [19]. Through the
Composition Graph, CompoVista represents entities, relations,
void space, and context as structured evidence that can be
queried, summarized, and inspected. The two case studies
illustrate this role. P3 used the system to examine how
mountains, rocks, trees, and white space were distributed
across dynasties and artists, and further connected Ma Yuan’s
recurring man–attendant relation to imperial inscriptions and
courtly album paintings. P4 used relations among figures,
boats, and water to understand the fisherman motif as a compo-
sitional structure, rather than a simple object label. These cases
suggest that scene-graph representation can support expert
interpretation when it remains connected to painting-level
visual and contextual evidence.
Cohorts as provisional evidence spaces. The findings
also suggest that cohorts should be understood as provisional
evidence spaces. In CompoVista, a cohort is not a fixed
category or a final answer. It is a temporary working set created
by a query, context condition, motif, or emerging hypothesis.
Participants constructed cohorts, inspected aggregate patterns,
revised the cohorts, and returned to individual paintings to
judge whether the patterns were meaningful. This behavior
matches the iterative nature of compositional analysis. A
scholar may begin with a broad question, such as the difference
between Northern Song and Southern Song landscapes, or with
a motif, such as a fisherman in a boat. The relevant cohort is
often not known in advance. It is formed and adjusted during
analysis. Therefore, visual analytics systems for art history
should not treat retrieval as a one-time operation. They should
support movement among query, cohort construction, overview
analysis, comparison, and close inspection [22], [48], [55].
Comparison as an interpretive bottleneck. Cross-cohort
comparison remained a difficult part of the workflow. Com-
pared with cohort construction and evidence inspection, par-
ticipants found it harder to interpret compositional differences
between cohorts. This is not only a usability issue. It also
reflects the complexity of art-historical interpretation. Visible
differences do not automatically become art-historical argu-
ments. Similar layouts may have different meanings across
themes, periods, artists, or formats, while different structures
may express related ideas. The current
Comparison View
shows differences in entity frequency, spatial placement, and
relation strength, but it provides limited support for interpret-
ing those differences. Future systems should not only display
differences, but also help scholars understand their basis,
reliability, and possible meaning. For example, a system could
highlight the entities or regions that cause a difference, link a
comparison pattern to the paintings that contribute most to it,
and show whether a pattern is supported by many paintings
or only by a few examples.
B. Design Implications
The expert interviews point to four design implications for
composition-oriented visual analytics systems.
Retrieval and ranking explanations. Systems should ex-
plain retrieval and ranking results more clearly. For scholars,
this is not only about system transparency [62]. It also helps
them judge whether a match can serve as evidence for further
analysis. When a painting is retrieved because of partial spatial
similarity, users need to know which region, entity, or relation
makes the match meaningful.
Fuzzy composition queries. Systems should support more
abstract and fuzzy composition queries. Experts often reason

about balance, density, openness, visual weight, and approxi-
mate regions. These ideas are central to compositional inter-
pretation, but they are difficult to express through exact object
positions or fixed bounding boxes. Future query mechanisms
should allow scholars to express uncertain regions, rough
layout tendencies, and higher-level spatial qualities.
Exploration history. Longer exploration requires history
management [48], [63]. The canvas and query provenance
preserve the analytical process, but scholars need to record
why a cohort was created, why a comparison was made, and
how an interpretation changed over time. Future systems could
treat exploration history as an analytical object, including
saved queries, cohort versions, comparison paths, notes, and
links between observations and supporting paintings.
Uncertainty and reliability cues. Systems should present
uncertainty and reliability more explicitly [34]. Cohort sum-
maries can look persuasive, but their strength depends on
cohort size, annotation quality, and the amount of supporting
evidence. Showing cohort size, support counts, annotation
confidence, and outliers would help scholars judge whether
a pattern is stable evidence or a tentative clue.
C. Limitations and Future Work
Corpus expandability, relation sparsity, and cohort re-
liability. The corpus supports the design and evaluation of
CompoVista, but it does not cover the historical and stylistic
range of TCPs. The corpus contains 960 paintings, 7,587 entity
instances, and 1,197 relation instances. Relation evidence is
much sparser than entity evidence, with about 1.25 relations
per painting on average. This can affect the Relations View
and relation-level comparison. Some relations may be absent
because they were not annotated, rather than because they
have no visual or historical meaning. Small cohorts may
affect reliability. When a query or filter returns only a few
paintings, spatial distributions, relation support, and cross-
cohort differences may be influenced by individual works.
Future work should expand the corpus, involve multiple expert
annotators, and support user-uploaded digitized paintings with
semi-automated annotation to enable scholars to analyze their
own materials within the system, and show cohort size, support
counts, and confidence cues more directly, helping scholars
judge the evidential strength of a pattern.
Format-aware modeling. The current system uses format-
aware spatial normalization and excludes handscrolls from
normalized spatial comparison. This is a necessary simplifica-
tion, but it also limits the scope of analysis. Hanging scrolls,
album leaves, and round fans can be compared more directly
through normalized pictorial space. Handscrolls involve se-
quential viewing, inscriptions, seals, and colophons, and their
composition cannot be fully captured by a single coordinate
space [19], [38]. Future work should model the viewing logic
of different formats more explicitly, especially the right-to-left
sequential structure of handscrolls.
Evaluation scope. Our evaluation combines a task-based
user study, two representative case studies, and expert inter-
views, providing evidence for the usefulness of CompoVista in
composition-oriented analysis. The current evaluation mainly
focuses on short-term tasks and representative scenarios. It
involved 12 domain-aware participants, and each session lasted
about 45 minutes. Therefore, the results mainly show the
usability and analytical potential of the system in a controlled
setting. Future work could include longer deployments with
art historians, museum researchers, and educators in real
research or teaching contexts. Such studies could further
examine how scholars use cohort history, comparison results,
and painting-level evidence, and how system-supported obser-
vations develop into research questions, teaching materials, or
art-historical arguments.
IX. CONCLUSION
We present CompoVista, a canvas-based visual analytics
system for composition-oriented analysis of Traditional Chi-
nese Painting collections. Drawing on art-historical concepts
of entities, relations, void space, and context, we propose
the Composition Graph, a structured representation that
makes painting composition explicit, queryable, and com-
parable. CompoVista instantiates this representation through
structure-aware retrieval, visual query, cohort construction, and
coordinated views for distribution, relation, comparison, and
painting-level evidence inspection. The Composition Graph
serves as both a computational model for organizing com-
positional evidence and an analytical scaffold for scholars
to move between cohort-level patterns and close reading of
individual paintings. A task-based user study with 12 domain-
aware participants, two case studies, and expert interviews
show that CompoVista supports composition-oriented pattern
discovery, cohort refinement, and evidence inspection in rep-
resentative TCP analysis scenarios. We hope this approach,
which connects structured visual representation with expert in-
terpretation, can support computational art history and extend
to other visual-cultural collections where spatial organization
is central to meaning.
