Abstract.
The two-point correlation function completely describes a Gaussian random
field, but nonlinear gravitational growth, halo bias, and redshift-space distortions drive
the late-time halo field strongly non-Gaussian, moving a substantial part of the cosmo-
logical information into higher-order correlations. We quantify the information content of
the configuration-space two-, three-, and connected four-point correlation functions of Qui-
jote dark-matter haloes at z = 0 and fixed number density. We build Fisher forecasts for
{Ωm, Ωb, h, ns, σ8, Mν} in real and redshift space from ∼38,000 GPU-accelerated N-point
measurements. Treating the statistics as a ladder, 2PCF →+3PCF →+ζ(4)
conn, we report the
information gained at each rung. The 3PCF supplies most of the accessible higher-order infor-
mation: it tightens every parameter, most strongly σ8 and Mν, whose degeneracy it partially
breaks, with per-parameter gains consistent with those of the Fourier-space halo bispectrum
on the same simulations. The connected 4PCF adds a further ∼1.4–1.5×. This rung-to-rung
increment is stable against derivative-sample noise and compression regularization, whereas
the absolute constraints remain limited by the finite simulation ensembles and are reported
as preliminary.
We validate the measured 3PCF against a tree-level perturbation-theory
model, recovering a linear bias consistent with the 2PCF. The configuration-space ladder
thus offers an independent and complementary route to the higher-order information probed
by the Fourier-space poly-spectra.

Introduction
The two-point correlation function, and its Fourier transform the power spectrum, have long
been the primary tools of large-scale-structure cosmology [1]. They encode the complete
statistical content of a Gaussian random field, and the primordial density field is Gaussian
to high precision. However, nonlinear gravitational growth, galaxy and halo biasing, and
redshift-space distortions drive the late-time field strongly non-Gaussian, depositing a large
fraction of the cosmological information into higher-order correlations that two-point statis-
tics cannot reach [2, 3].
Extracting this information is becoming increasingly important.
Stage-IV surveys such as DESI [4], Euclid [5], and SPHEREx [6] are mapping the clustering
of tens of millions of objects, with a precision at which the higher-order sector becomes mea-
surable, and for parameters such as the summed neutrino mass [7] it may even be decisive.
The natural step beyond two points is the three-point function and its Fourier coun-
terpart, the bispectrum. The bispectrum breaks the degeneracy between linear bias and the
clustering amplitude [8] and has been measured in spectroscopic surveys [9]. In configura-
tion space its redshift-space form has been used to discriminate modified-gravity models that
two-point statistics alone cannot separate [10]. Its constraining power has been quantified
extensively, most recently with the Quijote simulation suite [11], where the halo bispectrum
roughly doubles the constraint on the summed neutrino mass relative to the power spec-
trum alone [12, 13].
A broad programme has used Quijote to forecast many alternative
non-Gaussian summaries, among them the marked power spectrum [14], the wavelet scatter-
ing transform [15], and nearest-neighbour distributions [16]. Much of this effort traces back
to HADES [17], a dedicated suite of massive-neutrino N-body simulations and Quijote’s di-
rect predecessor from the same group. HADES is built around the σ8–Mν degeneracy: each
massive-neutrino cosmology is paired with a massless one whose σ8 is lowered to mimic the
neutrino-induced suppression of clustering, so that any statistic separating the two breaks
the degeneracy on an equal-amplitude footing. Prior work used HADES to show that a range
of higher-order and configuration-space statistics do exactly this, including the redshift-space
halo bispectrum [12], the marked power spectrum [14], and the void size function combined
with halo and matter clustering [18]. With few exceptions, all of these analyses are performed
in Fourier space.
Configuration space offers a complementary route to the same information, and in sev-
eral respects an advantageous one. The N-point correlation functions are local in pair sepa-
ration, so the baryon-acoustic feature and the scales of interest are cleanly localised. Survey
boundaries, masks, and selection effects are handled transparently through the random cat-
alogue. The large-scale signal also connects directly to analytic perturbation theory. The
main obstacle has been computational. Naively, an N-point count scales as O(N n
g ) in the
number of objects. However, a number of algorithmic advances have got around this: the
edge-corrected estimators of [19], the O(N2
g ) multipole decomposition of the 3PCF [20, 21],
its four-point generalisation [22], and graph- and FFT-based estimators [23, 24]. Together
these have made the 3PCF [25, 26] and, more recently, the 4PCF measurable on survey-scale
catalogues.
So far, the four-point function has attracted most attention as a probe of parity violation,
for which the parity-odd part of the 4PCF is a uniquely clean signature [27–29]. Its role as
a carrier of standard, parity-even cosmological information has been comparatively little
explored, especially in configuration space, and this is the gap we address here. Fourth order
is also the first at which a qualitatively new feature appears. A connected statistic separates

in the nearest-neighbour analysis of [16]. The random catalogues are drawn uniformly within
the periodic box.

4.3
Finite-sampling corrections
A Fisher matrix built from a finite number of derivative simulations is itself a noisy estimate,
and this noise biases the forecast errors [34]. We mitigate it in two ways. First, in redshift
space we measure every derivative simulation along all three Cartesian lines of sight and
average them, which triples the effective number of derivative realizations entering ∂µ/∂θi.
Second, we confirm that the marginalised errors have converged by recomputing them as the
number of derivative simulations is increased (§8).

7
The information ladder
We summarize the cosmological information carried by each rung of the ladder in Fig. 4 and
Table 1. We anchor the two-point baseline, quantify the gain delivered by the three-point
function and the breaking of the σ8–Mν degeneracy, compare with the Fourier-space bispec-
trum, and set out the status of the connected four-point rung, which the present covariance
does not yet support (§8). Throughout, redshift-space results refer to the monopole of the
anisotropic statistic, and all marginal errors include the Percival covariance-noise correction.
7.1
The two-point baseline
The first block of Table 1 gives the constraints from the 2PCF alone. For a single (1 h−1Gpc)3
volume the amplitude-like directions are poorly constrained: the 2PCF determines σ8 only
to ≃0.41 and Mν to ≃0.81 eV in real space. These directions are limited by the degeneracy
between the clustering amplitude, the linear halo bias, and the small-scale power suppression
from massive neutrinos, which two-point clustering cannot separate. In redshift space the
σ8 and Mν constraints do not improve.
Instead they weaken to ≃0.20 and ≃0.62 eV,

7.4
Comparison with the Fourier-space bispectrum
It is useful to set these gains beside the Fourier-space halo bispectrum forecast of [12] on the
same simulation suite. For Ωm, Ωb, h, and ns our 3PCF-over-2PCF factors (∼2–9) are larger
than but qualitatively track their bispectrum-over-power-spectrum factors (1.9–3.6), and we
reproduce the qualitative pattern, with the three-point statistic helping σ8 and Mν the most.
Our σ8 and Mν factors (∼14 and ∼9–11) exceed theirs (2.6 and ∼5).
There are a few
reasons for the difference. One is our fine configuration-space binning down to 10 h−1Mpc.
Another is the weaker two-point baseline of the configuration-space monopole compared with
the Fourier power spectrum out to kmax = 0.5 h Mpc−1, since a weaker denominator inflates
the ratio. This baseline is moreover itself un-converged—its large six-parameter marginal
errors are mirage-sensitive (§8.2)—so we compare with [12] at the level of the qualitative
pattern and the σ8–Mν degeneracy break rather than the precise gain factors, and take the
baseline-independent relative ladder gain as the robust point of contact. The finite-sample
effects noted above also contribute. We therefore present the configuration-space ladder as an
independent and complementary route to higher-order information, not as an improvement
on the Fourier bispectrum.

Binning
The absolute gains depend on the binning of each statistic, so we attach no significance to

8.4
Compression of the connected four-point rung
We adopt score (MOPED) compression [49, 50] as our primary estimator for the connected-
four-point rung, and use the direct Fisher as a cross-check. The motivation is conditioning,
not invertibility: once degenerate tetrahedra are excluded from the data vector (zero random
count, RRRR = 0; §3), the direct 2+3+ζ(4)
conn covariance (Nd = 1484) does invert, but
at the current covariance size it carries large debiasing corrections (Hartlap 0.70, Percival
m1 = 1.42; §8.1) that both inflate the parameter errors and inject covariance-estimation
noise. Compression sidesteps them: each realization is reduced to one score per parameter,
ta = P
ij Da,i Wij (xj −µj), and only the resulting 6 × 6 compressed covariance is estimated
from the simulations and inverted. Its Hartlap factor is ≈1 and no appreciable Percival
inflation is needed, so the compressed Fisher is well-conditioned where the full Nd×Nd inverse
is noisy. We take the weighting matrix to be a shrinkage-regularized inverse covariance [51, 52,
applied to cosmological covariances by], W = [(1−λ) ˆC +λ diag ˆC]−1. This regularization is a
separate ingredient from the compression itself, and it is essential, because the unregularized
sample precision ˆC−1 reintroduces the very covariance noise we are trying to avoid. The
compression is lossless for a well-estimated covariance, and we verify that it reproduces the
direct 2PCF and 2+3 constraints; the recovered connected-four-point information gain is
stable against the regularization, varying by less than 10% as λ ranges over [0.02, 0.5], and
we adopt λ = 0.1. As the decisive cross-check, the direct 2+3+ζ(4)
conn Fisher—now that the

to a uniform factor ∼1.42 across all six parameters, precisely the Hartlap–Percival penalty
that compression avoids and that itself relaxes toward unity as the fiducial ensemble grows.
That the offset is a single global scaling, not a parameter-dependent distortion, confirms that
compression discards no parameter information.

9.1
An independent route to higher-order information
The configuration-space ladder reaches the same physics as the Fourier-space bispectrum
by an independent route: the breaking of the bias–amplitude and σ8–Mν degeneracies. To
within the expected differences, its gains track those of [12] (§7.4). Working in separation
space brings three practical advantages. First, the baryon-acoustic feature and the scales
that carry the signal are local in pair separation, so the information is not spread across a
window-convolved range of wavenumbers. Second, survey boundaries, masks, and selection
functions are absorbed transparently through the random catalogue, rather than through a
window-matrix deconvolution [19, 23, 24]. Third, the large-scale signal connects directly to
analytic perturbation theory. Our tree-level validation of the 3PCF demonstrates this (§5).

9.2
Diminishing returns and the value of fourth order
A clear feature of the ladder is that the largest step is the first: the 3PCF delivers most of
the accessible higher-order information, tightening σ8 and Mν several-fold over the 2PCF.
The incremental value of the connected 4PCF over the 2+3 combination is the natural next
question. Although the absolute constraints at fourth order have not converged, the incre-
mental gain is a ratio of rungs, and the convergence test of §8.2 shows that this ratio is stable:
the common finite-sample mirage cancels between the numerator and denominator, so the
connected four-point function adds a real ∼20–50% tightening rather than a manufactured
one. That this increment is modest is itself informative: it shows that the three-point func-
tion already captures most of the non-Gaussian information available at these scales and this
number density, and it sets a quantitative ceiling on the cost/benefit of measuring a 632-
dimensional four-point data vector. Whether the increment grows into a more decisive gain
at lower redshift, higher number density, or fourth-order theory that we do not yet model
remains an open question.
9.3
Comparison with previous studies
Several earlier studies have quantified the cosmological information carried by higher-order
statistics, and it is useful to place our configuration-space results in that context. Using the
Quijote N-body suite and Fisher forecasts, [12] and [13] established the constraining power
of the Fourier-space bispectrum, with particular attention to the summed neutrino mass,
and both found that adding the bispectrum to the power spectrum tightens the constraints
on the cosmological parameters in general and on Mν in particular. [12] showed that in
redshift space the halo bispectrum responds differently to Mν and σ8, so that varying the
two leaves distinct imprints and the bispectrum breaks the degeneracy that limits the power
spectrum. Quantitatively, at kmax = 0.5 h Mpc−1 they found the bispectrum to tighten the
constraints on {Ωm, Ωb, h, ns, σ8} by factors of 1.9, 2.6, 3.1, 3.6, and 2.6 relative to the power
spectrum, and that on Mν by a factor of 5. [13] extended this analysis to a halo-occupation
galaxy-bias model, computing the galaxy bispectrum into the nonlinear regime in redshift
space; combined with the power spectrum it tightened the same parameters by 2.8, 3.1, 3.8,
4.2, and 4.2, and Mν by 4.6. Even under Planck priors the galaxy bispectrum delivered on
average a factor of ∼2 improvement over the power spectrum, with the gain concentrated in
the nonlinear regime, kmax > 0.2 h Mpc−1.
Whereas those analyses were performed on simulations, [53] applied the same approach
to data, combining the DESI DR1 LRG bispectrum with DR2 BAO measurements. Following
[12] and [13], they worked with the redshift-space bispectrum monopole, and by consistently
modelling the cross-covariance between the two datasets they showed that the DESI DR1
bispectrum sharpens the sensitivity to the neutrino mass. In combination with CMB data
the constraint tightens substantially, reaching Σmν < 0.059 eV at 95% confidence. They
further reported that the bispectrum helps break the degeneracies among the cosmological
parameters, the growth rate f, and σ8, and that it shifts the posteriors toward ΛCDM,
weakening the evidence for time-varying dark energy relative to the power-spectrum-only
result. Although their parameter set differs from ours, this illustrates the practical value

## Page 22


of higher-order clustering for survey data and motivates extending our configuration-space
ladder to higher redshift and to extended cosmological models.
Closest in spirit to the present work is [54], who studied the neutrino-mass sensitivity of
the three-point function directly in configuration space. Using 2000 Quijote N-body realiza-
tions at three redshifts and four neutrino masses, they measured the connected and reduced
3PCF with the estimator of [20], binning the 2PCF out to 150 h−1Mpc and the 3PCF out to
145 h−1Mpc. With a methodology and simulation set close to ours, they examined how the
neutrino imprint varies with triangle shape and scale, finding that elongated triangles—which
trace the filamentary structures of the cosmic web—carry most of the neutrino sensitivity,
with complementary information from right-angled configurations in the reduced 3PCF. On
this basis they concluded that the configuration-space 3PCF, and not only the bispectrum
studied previously, can break the Mν–σ8 degeneracy, consistent with what we find here.
9.4
Caveats
Several limitations should be kept in view. The forecast is a Fisher forecast, so it assumes
a Gaussian parameter likelihood and locally-linear derivatives, which can be optimistic for a
high-dimensional, manifestly non-Gaussian data vector; a simulation-based-inference treat-
ment would be needed to test that assumption. The covariance and derivative estimates carry
residual noise, which we quantify and correct as far as possible in §8. The σ8 amplitude con-
straint is nearly converged (a residual systematic of order 15%), but the absolute constraints
on the weak directions (Ωb, h, ns) and on Mν are still rising at the largest derivative set and
should be read as lower bounds rather than forecasts. The neutrino-mass response is the
most sensitive of all: it is obtained by finite differencing the massive-neutrino simulations,
and the resulting σ(Mν) both fails to converge at the Quijote ceiling and varies by ∼2.3×
with the finite-difference scheme, with the noisier scheme yielding the tighter error—the sig-
nature of a finite-sample mirage rather than of genuine constraining power. We therefore
treat the absolute Mν error as a scheme-dependent lower bound; the relative Mν gains along
the ladder, which cancel this mirage, are robust. The analysis is of dark-matter haloes at a
single redshift (z = 0) and a fixed number density. Holding ¯n fixed isolates clustering from
abundance (§3), but it treats the halo bias as effectively known. The absolute constraints
are therefore optimistic relative to a galaxy survey that must marginalize the halo–galaxy
connection. The relative gains along the ladder are far more robust to this. Finally, the mea-
surements are made in a periodic box, free of the survey window, mask, and fibre-collision
effects of a real catalogue, and so far only the 3PCF, not the connected 4PCF, has been
validated against an analytic model. These are the natural targets of the extensions set out
in §10.

Conclusions
We have measured the configuration-space two-, three-, and connected four-point correlation
functions of the Quijote dark-matter halo field, in both real and redshift space at z = 0
and a fixed comoving number density, and we have used them to forecast the cosmologi-
cal information available on {Ωm, Ωb, h, ns, σ8, Mν}. We separate the connected four-point
function from its disconnected Gaussian part on the fly, so the genuinely non-Gaussian four-
body signal is measured directly rather than by subtraction. A tree-level perturbation-theory
model reproduces the measured 3PCF over the fitted range, with a galaxy bias consistent
with that inferred from the 2PCF (§5), which establishes analytic contact for the lowest
– 21 –


## Page 23


higher-order rung. Adding the 3PCF tightens every parameter, and most strongly σ8 and
Mν, through a configuration-space breaking of the degeneracy between clustering ampli-
tude, halo bias, and neutrino free-streaming. The pattern of per-parameter gains tracks the
Fourier-space halo bispectrum [12], with the three-point statistic helping σ8 and Mν most,
which confirms that configuration space offers an independent and complementary route
to the same higher-order information.
Two results survive our full convergence analysis
and carry the paper. The first is a reportable amplitude constraint: the 2+3 rung reaches
σ(σ8) ≃0.02–0.03, nearly converged, with a residual convergence systematic of order 15%;
the full ladder tightens this to σ(σ8) ≃0.017 (real) / 0.022 (redshift space). The second, and
central, result is the information-content ladder itself—the ordered, several-fold gains from
2 →2+3 →2+3+ζ(4)
conn. These relative gains are robust: the finite-sample tightening (the
Fisher’s mirage) is common to the rungs it connects and cancels in the ratio (§8.2), so the
connected four-point function genuinely adds information—including on Mν—even where the
absolute errors have not converged. The neutrino mass is the honest exception at the level of
an absolute forecast. Its relative gains along the ladder are real, but its absolute σ(Mν) does
not converge even at the maximal Quijote derivative set and is strongly scheme-dependent,
varying by ∼2.3× between finite-difference schemes; the apparent tightness of the highest-
order scheme is largely a finite-sample mirage that grows with derivative noise, so we quote
σ(Mν) as a lower bound (≳0.1 eV) rather than a forecast. We therefore report the 2 and
2+3 amplitude constraints and the full information-gain ladder as our robust findings, and
present every absolute Mν constraint, together with the weak, still-rising directions (Ωb, h,
ns), as un-converged and hence conservative.
This analysis opens several extensions.
The most immediate is to move from dark
matter halos to mock galaxies. Populating the haloes with a halo-occupation model and
marginalizing its parameters would convert the present halo forecast into a survey-realistic
galaxy forecast, and moving from periodic boxes to lightcones with realistic windows, redshift
distributions, masks, and fibre collisions would exercise the configuration-space estimators
under survey conditions. An analytic or emulated model for the connected 4PCF (so far only
the 3PCF is theory-validated) would extend analytic contact to fourth order, and a joint
configuration-plus-Fourier analysis would combine the complementary strengths of the two
spaces in a single inference. Likewise extending to anisotropic clustering in redshift space, if
only at the 2PCF level, would offer significant advantages over the monopole alone. Stage-IV
surveys are now mapping the non-Gaussian regime, so a configuration-space ladder of this
kind, which is easy to interpret and connects to theory, is a useful addition to the higher-order
toolkit.
