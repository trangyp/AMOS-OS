OF A PARAMETRIZED VARIETY
BENJAMIN BIAGGI, JAN DRAISMA, FULVIO GESMUNDO, AIDA MARAJ,
AND MAGDALÉNA MIŠINOVÁ
Abstract. We prove that the symmetry Lie algebra of a parametrized variety can be de-
termined directly from the parametrization, without computing the vanishing ideal of the
variety. We derive a practical polynomial-time Monte Carlo algorithm for computing the
symmetry Lie algebra of a parametrized variety. We discuss applications to testing the bi-
nomiality of the ideal of a parametrized variety after changing coordinates, and test this
property on varieties arising from staged tree models and colored Gaussian graphical models.
Finally, we discuss symmetries and binomiality after changing coordinates for rational curves
and give a characterization of the symmetries of many secant varieties.
1. Introduction
In applications of algebraic geometry, one is often given a polynomial map or, more generally,
a rational map φ: Cm 99K Cn, and wants to compute properties of the image closure
X := {φ(p) | p ∈Cm and φ is defined at p}.
The closure and other topological notions throughout the paper refer to the Zariski topology;
in this setting, the closure coincides with that in the Euclidean topology. This framework
yields a large class of (irreducible) algebraic varieties, called unirational varieties.
Let GLn := GLn(C) and gln := gln(C) be the general linear group and its Lie algebra,
respectively. An important invariant of the embedded variety X is the group
GX := {g ∈GLn | gX = X}
of linear symmetries of X, often called the symmetry group or the linear preserver subgroup
of X. Knowledge of GX can be exploited in the computation of other properties of X. For
instance, the vanishing ideal of X in the polynomial ring C[y1, . . . , yn] is a GX-module, and thus
knowledge of the representation theory of GX may help in determining (interesting subspaces
of) that vanishing ideal.
By construction, GX is a Zariski closed subgroup of GLn, and hence the union of finitely
many connected components, which by standard algebraic group theory are also irreducible.
Let G0
X be the connected component containing the identity element Idn ∈GLn. This is a
closed normal subgroup of GX, and GX/G0
X is a finite group; see, e.g., [Bor91, §1.2]. The
identity component G0
X is uniquely determined by the Lie algebra gX of GX. We call gX the
symmetry Lie algebra of X.
The problem of determining the symmetry group of an algebraic variety appears in several
contexts [GLŠ00, LP01], in particular in the setting of varieties of matrices, tensors, or op-
erators with special structure [LT92, TW03, Joh11]. The symmetry Lie group of varieties of

The following paragraphs briefly highlight the main contributions of this paper.
Computing the symmetry Lie algebra. We characterize the symmetry Lie algebra of
the parametrized variety X as the algebra of linear maps that send each point of X into its
tangent space at that point (Theorem 2.2), and consequently, into the span of the columns of
the Jacobian of φ (Corollary 2.3). For dimensionality reasons, only a finite number of points
of X are needed for the computation. We implement this in the probabilistic Algorithm 1 and
the deterministic Algorithm 3.
In all our algorithms we assume that φ is defined over the field of rational numbers Q;
in this case, gX is the C-linear span of gX,Q := gX ∩gln(Q), and our algorithm computes a
basis for gX,Q. This restriction on the coefficients is very mild: on the one hand, in many
applications φ is indeed defined over Q; and on the other hand, the algorithms can easily be
extended to computations in a fixed finite-degree extension of Q. They can also be extended to
transcendental extensions, but they may no longer run in polynomial time, as rational-function
coefficients can become super-polynomial in size.
Theorem 1.1 (Algorithm 1 and Proposition 3.1). For any ϵ > 0 there exists a polynomial-
time Monte Carlo algorithm that, on input a list of rational functions φ = (f1, . . . , fn) ∈
Q(x1, . . . , xm)n, outputs a basis for gX,Q with probability ≥1 −ϵ. Here X ⊆Cn is the image
closure of φ regarded as a rational map Cm 99K Cn. With probability ≤ϵ, the output is either
“fail” or a basis of a subspace of gln(Q) different from gX,Q.
The input φ can be given in either of two ways:
(1) the standard encoding, where each fi is given as an explicit rational function ai/bi,
with ai and bi polynomials in Q[x1, . . . , xm] represented as a list of pairs (cα, α) with
α ∈Zm

its coefficient;
(2) the circuit encoding, where each fi is given as an algebraic circuit in the sense of [SY10]:
nodes with in-degree 0 are labelled by a constant or a variable xi, nodes with in-degree

that is identically 0. Such an algebraic circuit would not define any rational function.
The running time of Algorithm 1 is polynomial in the bit length of the input φ, given in either
of the two encodings, and in log(1/ϵ) (Proposition 3.2).

Testing GL-binomiality. In [MP26, KV25], the problem of determining the symmetry Lie
algebra is studied within the context of toric varieties, particularly to establish necessary
conditions for a variety X ⊆Cn to have a GL-binomial ideal—that is, for there to exist
matrix g ∈GLn such that gX is the vanishing set of an ideal generated by polynomials with
at most two terms. The condition dim gX ≥dim X is presented as a necessary one in [MP26].
This result is generalized in [KV25], which presents an algorithm for deciding whether X has
a binomial ideal after a coordinate change. This algorithm requires access to the defining
ideal I(X): first it computes the symmetry Lie algebra gX, then identifies the Lie algebra of
a maximal torus T within GX, and finally verifies whether T has a dense orbit in X. We
discuss a Monte Carlo variant of this approach which does not require access to the defining
ideal I(X) and it is applicable directly on a parametrization of X.
Corollary 1.2 (Algorithm 2 and Proposition 4.2). For any ϵ > 0 there exists a polynomial-
time Monte Carlo algorithm that on the same input as in Theorem 1.1 decides whether X has
a GL-binomial ideal, and whose output is correct with probability ≥1 −ϵ.
We give a deterministic variant of this algorithm in Algorithm 4.
Applications to staged tree models. Staged tree models are discrete models that represent
dependency relations among events and are realized by a staged tree, that is, a rooted directed
tree with colored nodes and labeled edges. Algebraically, the model is the image closure of
the polynomial map in (6.1) given by the product of edge labels along root-to-leaf paths.
Binary one-stage tree models are all GL-binomial by [GMN22, Theorem 6.5]. However,
not all binary staged trees have GL-binomial ideal by [MP26, Example 25] of a binary tree
model with 4 stages whose ideal is not GL-binomial, and not all ternary one-stage trees have
GL-binomial ideal by [MP26, Example 26] which shows that the ternary one-stage tree C3,4 of
depth 4 (the first tree in Table 2) does not have GL-binomial ideal.
We prove (Example 6.1) that GL-binomiality breaks as soon as two stages are involved: we
test this property for all 127 binary staged tree models with 2 stages of depth at most 3, and
find that the 4 models in Table 1 are not GL-binomial. We also tested GL-binomiality for
the 33 extensions of the stage tree C3,4 by at most two internal nodes (Example 6.2): exactly 16
of them, listed in Table 2, are not GL-binomial.
Applications to colored Gaussian graphical models. These are generalizations of Gauss-
ian graphical models, with symmetries in the entries of the concentration (precision) matrix,
encoded in colorings of the graph. Its reciprocal variety is the image closure of the parametriza-
tion in (6.2) given by maximal minors of these symmetric matrices. We say that a model is
GL-binomial when its reciprocal variety has a GL-binomial ideal.
Experimental results of [KV25, Section 6] on graphical Gaussian models, imply that Gauss-
ian graphical models are GL-binomial if and only if the graph is a block graph if and only if
it is binomial in the original coordinates. Other works [BDM26, CMMS23, CMR26] provide
classes of colored Gaussian graphical models on block graphs that are binomial in the original
coordinates. In particular, in [BDM26] is proven that a colored Gaussian graphical model is
binomial in the original coordinates if and only if the graph is vertex regular, edge regular,
and vertex triangle regular.

Symmetry Lie algebras of curves. We treat the case of parametrized curves, that is,
when φ = (f1, . . . , fn) is an n-tuple of univariate rational functions. In this case, Luroth’s
Theorem guarantees that the curve is rational: in particular, up to reparameterization, the
generic fiber of φ is a single point.
We completely classify the possible symmetry Lie algebras of X in Theorem 7.2. More
precisely, we show that gX is either trivial, the span of a diagonalizable matrix, or a 2-
dimensional parabolic subalgebra of sl2.
In the last case, X is an affine rational normal
curve. In particular, whenever gX ̸= {0}, X has a GL-binomial ideal, and up to a linear
change of coordinates, the components of the parametrization of X are powers of a fixed
rational function.
Symmetry Lie algebras of secant varieties. Let X ⊆Cn be an irreducible variety that is
closed under scalar multiplication, that is, λq ∈X for q ∈X and λ ∈C. The r-th secant variety
of X is σr(X) := {q1 + · · · + qr | qi ∈X} ⊆Cn. Note that dim(σr(X)) ≤min{n, r dim X}.
The symmetry Lie group of X is contained in that of σr(X) and this containment may be
strict: for instance, if X is not a linear space and r is large enough that σr(X) is the linear
span of X.
We show (Theorem 8.1) that when σr+1(X) has the expected dimension (r + 1) dim X,
the symmetry Lie algebras of X and σr(X) coincide, and consequently so do the identity
components of their symmetry Lie groups. The proof of Theorem 8.1 does not require X to be
unirational. It relies on the natural “parametrization” from Xr to the r-th secant variety σr(X)
of X, sending an r-tuple of points to their sum.

2. A geometric characterization of the symmetry Lie algebra
We provide a characterization of the symmetry Lie algebra of a variety X as the algebra of
linear maps that map each point of X into its tangent space to X. Geometrically, this means
that elements of the Lie algebra define vector fields on X, and the corresponding complex-
analytic one-parameter subgroups will define the integral curves of such vector fields, hence
mapping X to itself.
In order to stay within the purely algebraic framework, rather than working with integral
curves, we prove the result using a different characterization of the symmetry group and hence
of its Lie algebra. We recall the following result which is a combination of [GHL25, Lemma
3.1] and [Bor91, Lemma 7.4]; see also the discussion of [GHL25, §3.1].
Proposition 2.1. Let X ⊆Cn be an algebraic variety and let gX be its symmetry Lie algebra.
Let d ≥0 be an integer such that I(X)≤d generates the vanishing ideal I(X) ⊆C[y1, . . . , yn]
of X. Then
gX = {A ∈gln | A.f ∈I(X)≤d for all f ∈I(X)≤d}
= {A ∈gln | A.f ∈I(X) for all f ∈I(X)}.
Here A.f stands for the usual action of gln on the polynomial ring C[y1, . . . , yn] via deriva-
tions; explicitly, (A.f)(q) is the coefficient of ϵ in f((Idn −ϵA).q); see also [MP26, Proposi-
tion 4.2]. Using this characterization at the level of ideals, we obtain the following infinitesimal
characterization in terms of tangent spaces.
Theorem 2.2. Let X ⊆Cn be an algebraic variety, let S ⊆X be a Zariski-dense subset, and
let TqX denote the tangent space of X at q. Then gX = {A ∈gln | A.q ∈TqX for all q ∈S}.
Proof. If A ∈gX, then A.f ∈I(X) for all f ∈I(X) by Proposition 2.1. Therefore 0 =
(A.f)(q) = f(q −ϵA.q) mod (ϵ2). This means precisely A.q ∈TqX. Conversely, if A.q ∈TqX
for all q ∈S, then (A.f)(q) = 0 for all f ∈I(X) and q ∈S. Since S is Zariski dense in X,
A.f vanishes on X, so A.f ∈I(X), and therefore A ∈gX by Proposition 2.1.
□

For each p ∈Uφ, the condition that A.φ(p) lies in the column space of Jφ(p) defines a system

observation is used to design Algorithm 1 in the next section.
The characterization of gX in Theorem 2.2 applies to any variety X. Special geometric
features of X can however simplify the computation considerably. We record two such cases:
when X is linearly degenerate, that is, contained in a proper linear subspace of Cn, and
when X is a cylinder, that is, X = π−1π(X) where π: Cn →Cn/L is the quotient by some
linear subspace L.
Proposition 2.4. Let X ⊆Cn be an algebraic variety contained in a linear subspace U ⊆Cn.
Let KU ⊆gln be the subspace of linear maps A: Cn →Cn with A|U = 0. Then KU ⊆
gX. Moreover, if U is spanned by X, then gX is the preimage in gln of the symmetry Lie
algebra gX,U of X in gl(U), under the natural map gln →gln/KU ∼= Hom(U, Cn).
Proof. For the inclusion KU ⊆gX, note that any A ∈KU satisfies A.q = 0 ∈TqX for
all q ∈X ⊆U, so Theorem 2.2 gives A ∈gX.
For the second statement, note that gln/KU ∼= Hom(U, Cn), and gl(U) ⊆Hom(U, Cn)
consists of those maps sending U to itself.
It suffices to show that for any A ∈gX, the
restriction A|U maps U into U. By Theorem 2.2, for every q ∈X we have A.q ∈TqX ⊆U,
since X ⊆U implies TqX ⊆U. Thus A.q ∈U for all q ∈X, and since A is linear and U = ⟨X⟩,

Remark 3.3. In a practical implementation of Algorithm 1 one may first try to guess k :=

defined at p and rk Jφ(p) = k. Furthermore, instead of iterating the loop n2 times, one may
iterate it until L stabilizes, or stabilizes for a number of steps. In this manner, the output of
the algorithm is never “fail”, and moreover, if the initial guess for k is correct, then the output
is always a subspace containing gQ. The running time becomes a random variable, but its
expected value remains polynomial.

not know whether this problem is in RP (randomized polynomial time) or in co-RP. The point
here is that Algorithm 1 can output both an over-estimate of gQ (e.g. if the points sampled
satisfy rk Jφ(p) = dim X but are collectively in too special position) or an under-estimate
of gQ (e.g. if all sampled points p satisfy rk Jφ(p) = 0 < dim X).
Remark 3.5. If X is either linearly degenerate or a cylinder, Proposition 2.4 and Proposi-

the case of cylinders, the linear space L over which one should quotient X can be computed by

efficiently by sampling a point q ∈X and then a random hyperplane containing TqX. These
procedures return L and ⟨X⟩with high probability. In practice, an efficient implementation
of Algorithm 1 should first check whether X is linearly degenerate or a cylinder, and reduce
to the non-degenerate case accordingly.

potentially exponential in n and can no longer be done in polynomial time.

In this section, we use Theorem 2.2 and material from Section 4 to characterize when a
rational curve X given by a parametrization has gX ̸= {0}.
We first establish some terminology.
The leading coefficient of a nonzero rational func-
tion r = a/b for polynomials a, b ∈C[t] is by definition the leading coefficient of a divided
by that of b. If the leading coefficient of r equals 1, we call r monic. The degree of r equals
deg(a)−deg(b) ∈Z, the order of the pole at infinity, and we set deg(0) := −∞. These notions
behave similarly to the case of polynomials. In particular, if r, s are rational functions of dif-
ferent degrees, then deg(r + s) = max{deg(r), deg(s)} and if r, s are monic rational functions
of the same degree, then deg(r −s) < max{deg(r), deg(s)}. Also, if r is a monic rational
function of degree d, then its derivative r′ has degree d −1 and leading coefficient d, unless
d = 0, in which case deg(r′) is a value between −∞(if r is a constant) and d −2.

8. Secant varieties
Let X ⊆Cn be an algebraic variety invariant under rescaling, that is if q ∈X then λq ∈X
for all λ ∈C. Note that in this case TqX = TλqX for all λ ̸= 0. The variety
σr(X) = {q1 + · · · + qr | qi ∈X}
is called the r-th secant variety of X. It is immediate that GX ⊆Gσr(X). There are examples
for which the inclusion is strict, see, e.g., [GHL25, Prop. 4.1]. In this section, we provide a
sufficient condition for which, instead, equality holds at least at the level of the Lie algebra.
Note that a standard parameter count provides
dim σr(X) ≤r dim X;
we say that X has the expected dimension if equality holds. Terracini’s Lemma describes the
tangent space to σr(X) at a point z = q1 + · · · + qr with qi ∈X sufficiently general as:
Tzσr(X) = Tq1X + · · · + TqrX.
Note that σr(X) has the expected dimension if and only if the sum above is a direct sum. In
particular, if σr(X) has the expected dimension, the same holds for σs(X) if s ≤r.
Theorem 8.1. Let X be an algebraic variety invariant under rescaling such that σr+1(X) has
the expected dimension. Then gσr(X) = gX.

We point out that in many cases the symmetry Lie algebra of an r-th secant variety coincides
with that of the base variety, even if the (r +1)-th (and often even the r-th) secant variety has
dimension smaller than expected. A classical example are secant varieties of Segre varieties
with two factors, that is, varieties of matrices of bounded rank, which have the expected
symmetry Lie algebra whenever they do not fill the ambient space [GLŠ00]. The Segre varieties
of 3 factors of dimension 3, and of 4 factors of dimension 2 have defective secant variety
for r = 4 and r = 3 respectively; however, the results of [GHL25, Sec. 4, Sec. 9] show that all
secant varieties of these Segre varieties have the expected symmetry group. In fact, the only
examples that we know for which a secant variety of a Segre-Veronese variety has symmetry
Lie algebra larger than the one of the base variety are pencils of matrices [GHL25, Prop. 4.1].
