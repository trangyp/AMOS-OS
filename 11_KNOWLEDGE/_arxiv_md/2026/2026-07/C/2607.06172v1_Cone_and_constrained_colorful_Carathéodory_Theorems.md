CONE AND CONSTRAINED COLORFUL CARATH´EODORY THEOREMS
PAVLE V. M. BLAGOJEVI´C, ROMAN N. KARASEV, AND B´ALINT ZSIGRI
ABSTRACT. Holmsen proved in 2016 a generalization of the classical colorful Carath´eodory the-
orem in which a matroid imposes additional constraints on the desired colorful transversal. His
approach also works in the more general setting of oriented matroids, rather than relying directly
on convex hulls.
In this paper, we extend these ideas in several directions.
First, we study which colorful
Carath´eodory-type results remain valid when convex cones replace convex hulls, as well as anal-
ogous modifications in the oriented matroid setting. Second, we consider variants in which the
additional constraint on the transversal is not encoded by a matroid. This leads to new extensions
of the classical Tverberg theorem.
Our approach is topological, following the methods of Holmsen, and Kalai and Meshulam, on
which it builds. The key idea is to analyze homology groups of simplicial complexes that encode
colorful Carath´eodory-type phenomena, such as the support complex of an oriented matroid. In
particular, one shows that these complexes are (near-)d-Leray. We extend this analysis by carrying
out more detailed homology computations for these complexes, with the aim of enabling further
and more refined applications of the method.
1. INTRODUCTION AND STATEMENT OF CENTRAL RESULTS
Since its introduction, the colorful Carath´eodory theorem of B´ar´any [2] has become one of
the central results in combinatorial convexity. Its significance was firmly established through
its role in the celebrated proof of the classical Tverberg theorem [24] via Sarkaria’s tensor trick
[21], as well as through numerous applications, including colorful analogues of Fenchel’s and
Kirchberger’s theorems [3]. Efforts to further generalize this result have continued ever since,
yielding extensions by Arocha, B´ar´any, Bracho, Fabila, and Montejano [1], Holmsen, Pach, and
Tverberg [14], Kalai and Meshulam [15], Holmsen [13], and, most recently, [7]. We review
these developments in Section 1.1 before presenting our new contributions in Section 1.2.
1.1. Generalizations of the colorful Carath´eodory theorem so far. In this section, we review
all known versions of the colorful Carath´eodory theorem; a summary of these results is provided
in Theorem 1.11 and Table 1.
A collection of vectors in Rd, indexed by the elements of a finite set V , can be formally
represented as a function A: V →Rd. In this language, the original colorful Carath´eodory
theorem of B´ar´any [2, Thm. 2.1] claims the following.
Theorem 1.1. Let V be a finite set, d ≥0, and let A: V →Rd be a function. Suppose that r ≥d+1
and that U1, . . . , Ur ⊆V are pairwise disjoint subsets satisfying
0 ∈conv(A(U1)) ∩· · · ∩conv(A(Ur)).
Then there exist elements u1 ∈U1, . . . , ur ∈Ur such that
0 ∈conv(A({u1, . . . , ur})).

Both Theorem 1.3 and Theorem 1.4 modify the original Theorem 1.1 by altering which subsets
U ⊆V are assumed to satisfy 0 ∈conv(A(U)) and what type of subset U′ ⊆V is sought
satisfying 0 ∈conv(A(U′)).
Kalai and Meshulam [15, Cor. 1.4] discovered another colorful
Carath´eodory theorem of this kind: their formulation uses a matroid to encode both the subsets
U ⊆V for which 0 ∈conv(A(U)) is assumed and the class of subsets U′ ⊆V among which we
look for one with 0 ∈conv(A(U′)).
Recall that a matroid on a finite set V is a nonvoid abstract simplicial complex M on the
ground set V satisfying the following exchange property: whenever σ, σ′ ∈M with |σ| < |σ′|,
there exists an element v ∈σ′ −σ such that σ ∪v ∈M. The faces of M are called its independent
sets. The rank ρM(U) of a subset U ⊆V is the cardinality of a largest independent set contained
in U, and the rank of the matroid M is defined by rank(M) := ρM(V ). Terminology and notation
concerning simplicial complexes will be reviewed in Section 3.1.
For example, let U1, . . . , Ur ⊆V be pairwise disjoint subsets, each regarded as 0-dimensional
simplicial complex with vertex set Ui. Their join U1 ∗· · · ∗Ur is then a matroid on the ground

Another way to generalize B´ar´any’s colorful Carath´eodory theorem is to replace the function
A: V →Rd with its associated oriented matroid, a combinatorial abstraction of A, as was done
by Holmsen [13, Thm. 1.2]. The necessary theory of oriented matroids is reviewed in detail
in Section 4, but for the purposes of the result below it suffices to note that, for any subset
U ⊆V , one has 0 ∈conv(A(U)) if and only if U contains a positive circuit of the oriented
matroid associated with A. Oriented matroids capture enough of the affine geometry of A for
the theorem below to remain valid, while at the same time providing a discrete and more general
framework.
Theorem 1.6. Let V be a finite set, d ≥0, and let O be an oriented matroid on set of elements V
of rank at most d. Suppose that M is a matroid on ground set V of rank at least d + 1 such that U
contains a positive circuit of O for every subset U ⊆V with ρM(V −U) < d. Then there exists an
independent subset U′ of M containing a positive circuit of O.
In the original phrasing of this result [13, Thm. 1.2], it is implicitly requires that 1 ≤rank(O).
Indeed, all oriented matroids in that paper are assumed to be loop-free, and rank(M) ≥d+1 ≥1
implies that V is nonempty. Nevertheless, it is straightforward to verify — as we do in Section 6
— that the theorem remains valid when rank(O) = 0.
The final variant of the colorful Carath´eodory theorem we wish to mention is the so-called
constrained colorful Carath´odory theorem [7, Thm. 1.6]. However, it is instructive to first pro-
vide some context, so we postpone its statement to Section 1.2.

complexes (see [13, Thm. 1.4]), thereby yielding colorful Carath´eodory Theorems 1.1 and 1.6.

The combined work of Kalai, Meshulam, and Holmsen was further developed in [7] in two
directions, both of which we pursue here. First, observe that the previous approach relied on
only a single essential property of matroids. This permits a generalization of the method to
more general simplicial complexes, leading to colorful Carath´eodory theorems in which K is
no longer required to be a matroid. Second, [7] carried out extensive homological compu-
tations for the complex C arising from F(U) =“0 ∈conv(A(U))”, going substantially beyond
the mere verification that C is near-(d −1)-Leray. We restructure and extend these compu-
tations, and perform a comparable analysis for several additional variants of C beyond those
introduced at the beginning of this section. Besides enabling — and indeed yielding — new col-
orful Carath´eodory theorems, these calculations are intended to serve as groundwork for future
developments. The central tool in the method of Kalai and Meshulam is the Homological Nerve
Theorem, which may, for instance, be derived from the more powerful Leray spectral sequence.
Combined with the computations presented here, this perspective may permit extensions of col-
orful Carath´eodory theorems even in settings where the original formulation of the method, as
developed by the previous authors, no longer applies.
The work of Holmsen, as well as that of Kalai and Meshulam, will be discussed in detail
in Section 3. For the remainder of this section, however, we shift our focus to the new color-
ful Carath´eodory theorems arising from our results. Like the colorful Carath’eodory theorems

(B) The function A: V →Rd is replaced by its associated oriented matroid, and both the
assumptions and the conclusions of the colorful Carath´eodory theorem are formulated

(C) Convex hulls are replaced by other operators; for example, instead of considering “0 ∈
conv(A(U))”, we study conditions such as “A(v0) ∈cone(A(U))” or “A(v0) ∈aff(A(U))”.
Recall that Theorem 1.2 follows the description in (C), Theorems 1.3, 1.4, and 1.5 are general-
izations falling under scheme (A), while Theorem 1.6 constitutes a generalization in the sense
of both (A) and (B).

explained in Observation 2.1.
Theorem 1.7. Let V be a finite set, d ≥0, and let A: V →Rd be a function. Suppose that
r ≥max{d + 1, 2}, U1, . . . , Ur ⊆V are pairwise disjoint subsets of V with the property that
0 ∈conv(A(U1)) ∩· · · ∩conv(A(Ur)), and that K is a simplicial complex on the ground set V of

Then there exists a face σ ∈K such that 0 ∈conv(A(σ)).

strengthening of Tverberg’s theorem, especially in the context of ordered Tverberg partitions.
For further details, see Section 2, in particular Corollary 2.8.

3. OUTLINE OF THE METHOD
In this section we reformulate the method – originally due to Kalai & Meshulam [15] and
extended by Holmsen [13] – for more general complexes than just matroids, in order to apply it
in Section 6 to prove all of our central results. The presentation here intends to highlight some
interesting points where more complicated tools could be substituted in in order to extend the
computations to less well-behaved settings.
Recall from the previous section that each colorful Carath´eodory theorem is parametrized by
a logical formula F(−) and two collections U and U′ of subsets of V , in terms of which the
theorem states that “if F(U) for all U ∈U then F(U′) for some U′ ∈U′ too”. The following
CF := {U ⊆V : F(U) = false}
is an abstract simplicial complex for each of the four F(−) that we consider:
(1) F(U) =“0 ∈conv(A(U))” for a fixed function A: V →Rd,
(2) F(U) =“A(v0) ∈cone(A(U))” for a fixed function A: V ⊔{v0} →Rd,
(3) F(U) =“U contains a positive circuit of O” for a fixed oriented matroid O of rank at
most d on set of elements V ,
(4) F(U) =“v0 ∈conv(U)” interpreted in a fixed oriented matroid O of rank at most d on
set of elements V ⊔{v0}.
In [13] the CF of point (3) was called the support complex of the oriented matroid O. We will
refer to the CF of points (1), (2), and (4) as the zero-avoiding, vector-avoiding, and element-
avoiding complex respectively.
As CF is an abstract simplicial complex, replacing U′ in a colorful Carath´eodory theorem with
the smallest abstract simplicial complex K containing it gives the equivalent statement “if F(U)
for all U ∈U then F(U′) for some U′ ∈K”, which can be further rephrased as “if U ∩CF = ∅
then K ⊈CF”. Statements of this last form have already been established by Kalai & Meshulam
[15, Theorem 1.6] and Holmsen [13, Theorem 1.4], where K is demanded to be a matroid and
CF satisfies one of the following two properties:
Definition 3.1. A simplicial complex C is near-(d −1)-Leray (with coefficients in the field F) if
˜Hn(C; F) = 0 for all n ≥d and ˜Hn(linkC(σ); F) = 0 for each simplex ∅̸= σ ∈C and n ≥d −1.
C is (d −1)-Leray (with coefficients in the field F) if ˜Hn(linkC(σ); F) = 0 for all n ≥d −1 and
∅⊆σ ∈C.
Here linkC(σ) denotes the link of σ in the abstract simplicial complex C, which is the subcom-
plex of C defined as
linkC(σ) := {τ ∈C : τ ∩σ = ∅, τ ∪σ ∈C}.
With coefficients in a fixed field F, the cohomology Hn(X) of any space X is naturally isomor-
phic to the dual Hn(X)∗of the homology Hn(X) [19, Corollary 53.6], so there is an isomor-
phism Hn(X) ∼= Hn(X) whenever the latter is finite dimensional. From this it easily follows that
˜Hn(X) ∼= ˜Hn(X) whenever the latter is finite dimensional. In particular, near-(d −1)-Lerayness
and (d −1)-Lerayness could have been defined using reduced homology groups in place of re-
duced cohomology groups, like it is done in e.g. [13]. We frame the definition differently here,
because this is the version which we will always use.
[13, Proposition 1.3] states that the support complex of an oriented matroid O is near-
(rank(O) −1)-Leray. From this it follows that zero-avoiding complexes are also near-(d −1)-
Leray, see [13, p. 4]. We prove that vector- and element-avoiding complexes are (d−1)-Leray in
Section 5. In fact, we will study the (co)homology groups of these complexes more completely
than demanded by the (d −1)-Leray property, in order to pave the road for future applications
of the method described in this section by enabling more detailed computations.

APPENDIX A. NERVE THEOREMS FOR UGLY COVERS
Given a regular CW complex X, call a subset A ⊆X a cellular subset if it is a union of
some open cells of X, such that if σ and σ′′ are open cells in A, and σ′ is an open cell of X
with σ ⊆Cl(σ′) and σ′ ⊆Cl(σ′′), then σ′ ⊆A. Here Cl(τ) stands for the closure of τ in X.
Equivalently, a cellular subset is an intersection of a subcomplex and an open union of open
cells of X. In particular, if a union of open cells of a cellular subset is closed or open within
this subset, then it is itself a cellular subset, in which case we call it a closed (respectively open)
cellular subset. Say that a collection (Ai)i∈I of cellular subsets of X is ugly if whenever i ̸= j ∈I,
σi an open cell of Ai, σj an open cell of Aj, and σi intersects the closure of σj we have σi ⊆Aj
or σj ⊆Ai. Collections of open cellular subsets and collections of closed cellular subsets of a
fixed cellular subset X′ are always ugly. If A is a cellular subset and Z is a subcomplex of X,
then A ∩Z is closed in A, so Theorem 5.11 is a consequence of the more general Theorem A.1
below:
