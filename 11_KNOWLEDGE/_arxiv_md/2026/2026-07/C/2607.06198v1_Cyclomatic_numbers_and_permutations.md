Cyclomatic numbers and permutations
Bridget Eileen Tenner∗
and
Vincent Vatter†
Abstract
We show that the inversion graph of a permutation governs several apparently dif-
ferent aspects of the permutation: the gap between its Coxeter and reflection lengths,
the number of repeated letters in its reduced words, and its cycle structure, and it also
bounds the number of 321 and 3412 patterns. The mechanism is that a reduced word
orders the edges of the inversion graph, with the edges from first-occurrence letters
forming a spanning forest and the edges from repeated letters accounting for the rest.
The case where the inversion graph is a forest unifies several classical characterizations
of these permutations, due to Edelman, Tenner, and Petersen and Tenner. We also give
a new proof that every connected acyclic inversion graph is a caterpillar.
Several apparently disparate results about permutations, spread across the literature on
Coxeter groups, reduced words, permutation patterns, and cycle structure, admit a common
explanation: they are all governed by the inversion graph. This paper develops that unifying
viewpoint.
Our quantitative results are the following. The cyclomatic number β (the dimension of a
graph’s cycle space) of the inversion graph Gπ of a permutation π equals the exact count of
repeated letters in any reduced word for π. The “cycle surplus” (the excess of permutation
cycles over sum components) is bounded above by the cyclomatic number. Combining these
sandwiches the difference between the Coxeter and reflection lengths:
β(Gπ) ≤ℓ(π) −ℓ′(π) ≤2β(Gπ).
The mechanism behind these statements is that a reduced word for π orders the edges of Gπ
one at a time, with the edges from the first occurrence of each letter forming a spanning
forest and the remaining edges coming from the repeated letters.
As a special case of these quantitative statements, the forest case β(Gπ) = 0 recovers
several classical characterizations of the permutations in Av(321, 3412), each previously es-
tablished by different methods. Edelman [7] described these permutations as the direct sums
of unimodal cycles of intervals: permutations of an interval that can be written as a single
cycle whose entries, after cyclic rotation, strictly increase to a maximum and then strictly
decrease. Tenner [17] characterized them as the permutations avoiding the patterns 321 and

permutations because their principal order ideals in the Bruhat order form boolean lattices.
The avoidance condition π ∈Av(321, 3412) falls out from a folklore fact: no inversion

C4, the graph Gπ is a forest if and only if π avoids both patterns. Edelman’s description

is established by showing it equivalent to (i), and each equivalence is the forest case of one
of the motivating quantitative results of this work.

Organization
In Section 1, we introduce the permutation-centric objects and properties that we unify
in this work.
The length of that section reflects the range of results it brings together.
Section 2 collects the foundational facts about inversion graphs that we draw on throughout
the article: the identification of sum components with connected components of Gπ, the
placement of permutation cycles within sum components, and the absence of long induced
cycles in Gπ. Section 3 develops the reduced-word viewpoint, proves the spanning-forest
theorem that underlies the repeated-letters identity, and characterizes when the pattern
bound on repeated letters is sharp. Section 4 proves the surplus bound and obtains the gap
decomposition by substitution. Section 5 establishes the correspondence between trees and
unimodal cycles, recovers Edelman’s description by taking direct sums, and shows that every
connected acyclic inversion graph is a caterpillar. We conclude with some perspective on
this work, in Section 6.
1
Permutation context
1.1
Inversion graphs and permutation patterns
The inversion graph Gπ of a permutation π ∈Sn, also known in the literature as the
permutation graph of π, has vertex set [n] = {1, 2, . . . , n}, with an edge ab whenever the
values a and b form an inversion of π, meaning that the larger value appears to the left of
the smaller in one-line notation1. Thus we label the vertices of inversion graphs by value,
while some other works label them by position; the choice is purely cosmetic, but the reader
should bear this convention in mind.
The vertex labels exist for convenience of reference (so we can say, for instance, that
two specific values form an inversion), but inversion graphs themselves are unlabeled in the
graph-theoretic sense: we identify two inversion graphs that differ only by a relabeling of
their vertices. At times we work more closely with the identification of vertices as values, as
in “the vertex 1” or “the values of a cycle of π”; which sense is intended will always be clear
from context. Likewise, in figures we draw the vertex π(i) at coordinates (i, π(i)), so that
two vertices are adjacent precisely when one lies southeast of the other; this embedding is
merely a convenience for diagrams, and inversion graphs themselves do not come equipped
with such an embedding in the plane.
There is another natural way to view Gπ. A permutation π ∈Sn defines a poset on [n]
of order dimension at most 2, realized by the two linear orders 1 < 2 < · · · < n and
π(1) ≺π(2) ≺· · · ≺π(n); the inversion graph Gπ is the incomparability graph of this
poset, with order dimension exactly 2 unless π is the identity, in which case the poset has
dimension 1. Dushnik and Miller [6] proved that a graph arises as an inversion graph if
and only if both it and its complement are comparability graphs of posets (equivalently, if
they are both incomparability graphs), and Gallai [8, 13] gave a forbidden induced subgraph

The standardization of a sequence of k distinct integers is the permutation of [k] obtained
by replacing the entries with 1, 2, . . . , k in increasing order. Standardization preserves relative
order, and hence preserves inversions. A permutation π contains a permutation σ as a pattern
if some subsequence of the one-line notation of π standardizes to σ, and π avoids σ otherwise.
We write Av(B) for the set of permutations avoiding every pattern in the set B. This set is
a permutation class because it is closed downward under the containment order; we refer to
Vatter’s survey [20] for more background on permutation classes.
By definition, |E(Gπ)| = inv(π), the number of inversions of π. Let κ(Gπ) denote the
number of connected components of Gπ. The cyclomatic number of Gπ, denoted β(Gπ), is
the dimension of the cycle space of Gπ, equivalently the minimum number of edges that must
be removed to obtain a forest, and hence
β(Gπ) = |E(Gπ)| −|V (Gπ)| + κ(Gπ).
1.2
Reduced words and inversion graphs
A simple reflection si = (i, i + 1) acting on a permutation π either creates or destroys a
single inversion, so any product expression for π requires at least inv(π) simple reflections.
We define the Coxeter length ℓ(π) to be the minimum number of simple reflections in such
an expression, so ℓ(π) = inv(π) = |E(Gπ)|, and a reduced word for π is an expression of π
as a product of ℓ(π) simple reflections. We write reduced words as sequences of subscripts:
the word w = i1i2 · · · im represents the product si1si2 · · · sim, and reduced words for π have
exactly |E(Gπ)| letters.
The cycle space of a graph G is the F2-span of its (graph) cycles, viewing each cycle as
a set of edges and using symmetric difference for addition. Its dimension is the cyclomatic
number β(G): if G has n vertices, then any spanning forest of G has n −κ(G) edges, and
each of the remaining β(G) edges, when added back to the forest, creates a unique cycle.
These cycles form a basis for the cycle space.
A reduced word w = i1i2 · · · im for π orders the edges of Gπ one at a time. Set πt =
si1si2 · · · sit, so π0 = e is the identity and πm = π. At step t, the simple reflection sit swaps
the entries in positions it and it + 1 in the one-line notation of πt−1. Because w is reduced,
this swap increases the Coxeter length by one, so it creates precisely one new inversion,
namely the inversion between the two values in positions it and it + 1; the corresponding
edge of Gπ joins those two values. Writing ft for this edge, the m letters of w, taken in order,
correspond bijectively to the edges f1, f2, . . . , fm of Gπ.

Before the letter i has appeared for the first time (reading left to right), the values in
positions [1, i] of the current permutation are exactly [1, i] as nothing has yet crossed that
boundary. Then multiplying by the transposition si creates an edge joining a value in [1, i]
to a value in [i + 1, n]. We show in Section 3 that the first-occurrence edges form a spanning
forest of Gπ, and so the remaining edges, contributed by repeated letters, account for the
β(Gπ) edges that lie outside this spanning forest.
Let supp(π) denote the support of π, the set of simple reflections appearing in any reduced
word for π. This set is independent of the choice of reduced word, since by Matsumoto’s
theorem [15] any two reduced words are connected by braid and commutation relations,
both of which preserve the set of generators used. Then |supp(π)| is the number of distinct
letters in any reduced word, and the number of repeated letters in w, meaning the number
of occurrences past the first occurrence of each distinct letter, is ℓ(π) −|supp(π)|.
The
spanning-forest description identifies this count with the cyclomatic number.
Theorem 1.1. Any reduced word for π contains exactly β(Gπ) repeated letters; that is,
ℓ(π) −|supp(π)| = β(Gπ).
The numerical identity in Theorem 1.1 also follows by combining ℓ(π) = |E(Gπ)| with
the standard identity |supp(π)| = n −κ(Gπ). What the theorem contributes is the struc-
tural mechanism behind this equality: a reduced word orders the edges of Gπ so that first
occurrences form a spanning forest, while repeated letters are precisely the edges outside
that forest.
Tenner [17] showed that this count is zero precisely for the permutations avoiding 321 and
3412, and Daly [4] later characterized the permutations with precisely one repeated letter.

Tenner [18] later proved a general upper bound: the number of repeated letters in a reduced
word for π is at most the total number of copies of 321 and 3412 in π. Theorem 1.1 sharpens
the picture by identifying the repetition count exactly: it equals β(Gπ) for every reduced
word. This turns Tenner’s upper bound into an immediate graph-theoretic consequence of
two facts: induced cycles span the cycle space, and inversion graphs have no induced cycles
of length five or more (folklore, presented here as Proposition 2.3). Thus copies of 321 and
3412 in π, corresponding to induced triangles and induced 4-cycles of Gπ, account for all
induced cycles.
Corollary 1.2 (cf. Tenner [18, Theorem 3.2]). The number of repeated letters in any reduced
word for π is at most the total number of copies of 321 and 3412 in π.
1.3
Permutation cycles and the length gap
The cycle space of Gπ is also closely tied to the cycle structure of π, via the sum components
of π (the indecomposable summands in its direct sum decomposition; see Section 2 for a
formal definition). Writing κ(π) for the number of sum components of π, the first fact we
need, established in Proposition 2.1, is that the sum components of π correspond to the
connected components of Gπ, so κ(π) = κ(Gπ).
Every cycle of π is contained in a single sum component (Proposition 2.2), so c(π) ≥κ(π).
We call the nonnegative difference c(π)−κ(π) the cycle surplus of π, since it counts the excess
of permutation cycles over sum components.
For example, the permutation 321 has cycle structure (1, 3)(2), so c(321) = 2, and its
inversion graph G321 = K3 is connected, so κ(321) = 1 and the cycle surplus is 1. We
computed β(G321) = 1 above, so the cycle surplus and cyclomatic number agree here.
Our larger example π = 243179586 = (1, 2, 4)(3)(5, 7)(6, 9)(8) from Figure 1 has c(π) = 5,
and Gπ has κ(π) = 2 connected components, giving a cycle surplus of 3, which again equals
β(Gπ) = 3.
These two quantities need not agree in general. Take π = 4321 = (1, 4)(2, 3), so c(4321) =
2, and the inversion graph G4321 = K4 is connected, so κ(4321) = 1 and the cycle surplus
is again 1. But |E(G4321)| = 6, so β(G4321) = 6 −4 + 1 = 3, strictly larger than the cycle
surplus.
Theorem 1.3. For every permutation π,
c(π) −κ(π) ≤β(Gπ).
The proof, in Section 4, rests on a parity argument: between any two disjoint π-invariant
subsets, the number of edges of Gπ joining them is even. Two distinct permutation cy-
cles within the same sum component therefore contribute at least two edges between their
supports, and since each support is connected in Gπ, this ensures a graph cycle in that
component.
The reflection length ℓ′(π) of a permutation π ∈Sn is the minimum number of arbitrary
reflections needed to express π. Decomposing π into its c(π) disjoint cycles and expressing
each m-cycle as a product of m −1 transpositions gives an expression for π using n −c(π)
reflections, so ℓ′(π) ≤n −c(π).

1.4
Schubert varieties
The cyclomatic number β(Gπ) also appears in the Schubert geometry of the flag variety. To
each permutation π ∈Sn is associated a Schubert variety Xπ, a classical algebraic variety on
which a torus T acts; the codimension of a generic T-orbit is called the T-complexity of Xπ,
denoted cT(Xπ). We do not develop this setting here, but we record what known results say
about it from our viewpoint.
In type A, Donten-Bury, Escobar, and Portakal [5, Theorem 5.8 and Corollary 4.16]
proved that
cT(Xπ) = ℓ(π) −|supp(π)|,
and Gao and Hodges [10] subsequently established the same formula type-uniformly. Com-
bined with Theorem 1.1, the right-hand side equals β(Gπ), so we obtain the following iden-
tification.
Theorem 1.5. For every permutation π ∈Sn, we have cT(Xπ) = β(Gπ).
The forest case, β(Gπ) = 0, is the case where Xπ is a toric variety (a variety on which T
acts with a dense orbit); this special case was established earlier by Karuppuchamy [12].

Sum components and induced cycles
This section collects the structural facts about inversion graphs that we draw on throughout
the paper. We first set up the direct-sum decomposition of a permutation and identify the
sum components of π with the connected components of Gπ. Then we show that inversion
graphs have no induced cycles of length five or more, and identify their induced triangles
and induced 4-cycles with copies of 321 and 3412.

2.1
Sum components and connected components
The direct sum of permutations π1 ∈Sm and π2 ∈Sn is the permutation π1 ⊕π2 ∈Sm+n
whose one-line notation is obtained by concatenating the one-line notation of π1 with the
one-line notation of π2, with each entry of the latter increased by m. A permutation is sum
indecomposable if it cannot be written as the direct sum of two nonempty permutations;
otherwise it can be written uniquely as α1 ⊕· · · ⊕αk with each αi sum indecomposable, and
these summands are the sum components of π.
The foundational fact of this section identifies these sum components with the connected
components of Gπ. The result is folklore; we include a proof for completeness.
Proposition 2.1. The connected components of Gπ are the sets of values of the sum com-
ponents of π. In particular, a permutation π is sum indecomposable if and only if Gπ is
connected.
Proof. Suppose a < b < c and ac is an edge of Gπ. Then c appears to the left of a in the
one-line notation of π. If b appears to the right of a, then c appears to the left of b, so
cb is an edge. If b appears to the left of a, then ba is an edge. Thus every value between
the endpoints of an edge lies in the same connected component as that edge, and so every
connected component of Gπ is an interval of values.
Let I = [a, c] be a connected component of Gπ. Since no edge joins I to its complement,
every value less than a appears to the left of every value in I, and every value greater than c
appears to the right of every value in I. Hence the values in I occupy precisely the positions
a, a + 1, . . . , c, so I is a sum component of π.
It follows that π is the direct sum of its restrictions to the connected components of Gπ,
ordered by value. Each of these restrictions is sum indecomposable, since a further direct-sum
decomposition would disconnect the corresponding graph component. Thus these restrictions
are exactly the sum components.
We now turn to the basic interaction between the cycle structure of π and its sum
decomposition. For a subset I ⊆[n], write Gπ[I] for the subgraph of Gπ induced on I.
Proposition 2.2. Every cycle of π is contained in a single sum component, and if I ⊆[n]
is the set of values of a cycle of π, then Gπ[I] is connected.
Proof. Each sum component is π-invariant: the values in the ith sum component occupy a
contiguous interval of positions, and π maps this interval to itself. A cycle of π therefore
stays within a single sum component.
For the second claim, restrict π to I and standardize. The resulting permutation is a single
cycle, hence sum indecomposable, so by Proposition 2.1 its inversion graph is connected.
Standardization preserves inversions, so this graph is isomorphic to Gπ[I].
In particular, c(π) ≥κ(π), where κ(π) denotes the number of sum components of π, so
the cycle surplus c(π) −κ(π) is indeed nonnegative.
By Proposition 2.1, κ(π) = κ(Gπ). We use the two notations interchangeably from this
point on.

2.2
Induced cycles of inversion graphs
Inversion graphs cannot contain arbitrary induced cycles: their induced cycles are restricted
to lengths three and four. This fact is folklore; we include a proof for completeness.
Proposition 2.3. No inversion graph contains an induced cycle of length five or more.
Proof. We prove an equivalent statement: any induced cycle in an inversion graph of length
at least four has length exactly four.
Suppose Gπ contains an induced cycle on vertices π(i1), . . . , π(ik) with k ≥4. Choose
i1 minimal among the indices of the cycle. The vertex π(i1) has two neighbors in the cycle;
let i2 be the index of the one whose position is closer to i1, and label the remaining cycle
vertices π(i3), . . . , π(ik) in cycle order, so that
π(i1)π(i2) , π(i2)π(i3) , . . . , π(ik−1)π(ik) , π(ik)π(i1)
are edges of Gπ. By minimality of i1 and choice of i2, we have i1 < i2 < ik, and the point
(i2, π(i2)) lies southeast of (i1, π(i1)). Since π(i3)π(i2) is an edge and π(i3)π(i1) is not, using
that k ≥4 and the cycle is induced, we have i1 < i3 < i2 and π(i2) < π(i1) < π(i3).
Now consider (ik, π(ik)). Since ik > i2 and π(ik)π(i1) is an edge, the point (ik, π(ik))
lies east of (i2, π(i2)) and southeast of (i1, π(i1)). These constraints force (ik, π(ik)) to lie
southeast of (i3, π(i3)), giving an edge π(ik)π(i3). Since the cycle is induced, this forces
k = 4.
The induced cycles of Gπ are in bijection with copies of two small patterns.
Proposition 2.4. The inversion graph Gπ is a forest if and only if π avoids the patterns
321 and 3412. More precisely, induced triangles of Gπ are in bijection with copies of 321
in π, and induced 4-cycles of Gπ are in bijection with copies of 3412 in π.
Proof. A triangle of Gπ requires each pair of the corresponding entries to be an inversion.
The only way this is possible is if the three entries form a copy of 321. The converse direction
is obvious: every copy of 321 in π corresponds to an induced triangle in Gπ.
An induced 4-cycle in Gπ consists of the join of two copies of K2. Each K2 must corre-
spond to a noninversion of π, while every pair of entries with one endpoint in each K2 forms
an inversion. Thus the two larger entries must appear first, in increasing order, followed by
the two smaller entries, also in increasing order. The four entries therefore form a copy of
3412. Conversely, every copy of 3412 in π corresponds to an induced 4-cycle in Gπ.
By Proposition 2.3, induced cycles of Gπ have length three or four, so Gπ is a forest if
and only if it contains no induced triangle and no induced 4-cycle, which by the previous
paragraphs is if and only if π avoids 321 and 3412.

Reduced words and inversion graphs
This section proves Theorem 1.1, the identity ℓ(π) −|supp(π)| = β(Gπ), and derives the
pattern-count result, Corollary 1.2, as a consequence. The argument is structural: a reduced
word for π orders the edges of Gπ one at a time, and we identify the edges contributed by
9


## Page 10


first occurrences of letters as a spanning forest of Gπ. The repeated letters are then precisely
the non-tree edges, of which there are β(Gπ).
As in Section 1.2, let w = i1i2 · · · im be a reduced word for π, and set πt = si1 · · · sit, so
π0 = e and πm = π. At step t, applying sit swaps the entries in positions it and it +1 of πt−1.
Let at < bt be the values in those positions just before the swap, so that the swap creates
the inversion (at, bt) of πt. We write ft = atbt for the corresponding edge of Gπ.
Proposition 3.1. The edges f1, f2, . . . , fm are distinct and account for every edge of Gπ.
Proof. Since w is reduced, the inversion set of πt has size t for each t. The swap at step t
adds the inversion (at, bt), which was not previously an inversion, so the inversion set of πt
is the inversion set of πt−1 together with the new inversion ft. Iterating, the m inversions
f1, . . . , fm are distinct and constitute the inversion set of π, which has the same size as
E(Gπ).
3.1
First occurrences and spanning forests
Fix a permutation π and let w = i1i2 · · · im be a reduced word for π. Say that the tth letter
of w is a first occurrence if it /∈{i1, . . . , it−1}, and let F(w) ⊆E(Gπ) be the set of edges
created by first occurrences in w.
The next proposition is the core structural claim. It says that F(w) is always a spanning
forest of Gπ, regardless of which reduced word is chosen. The example of π = 243179586
in Figure 1 illustrates the construction concretely. The two reduced words w and w′ given
there produce different spanning forests F(w) and F(w′), but each has n −κ(Gπ) = 7 edges,
with three on the connected component {1, 2, 3, 4} and four on the connected component
{5, 6, 7, 8, 9}. The proposition says this is no accident.
The proof of Proposition 3.2 tracks two pieces of information across the steps of w. The
first is a partition of [n] into intervals, refined as letters are first encountered: at each step,
two values lie in the same interval if and only if every letter strictly between them has
already appeared in w. The second is the location of the values themselves: at each step, the
values in any interval I occupy exactly the positions of I in the current permutation. They
have been shuffled by the reflections applied so far, but have not crossed into a neighboring
interval.
Proposition 3.2. For every reduced word w for π, the set F(w) is a spanning forest of Gπ.
Proof. For t ≥0, let At ⊆[n −1] be the set of letters appearing among i1, . . . , it, and let Pt
be the partition of [n] into the maximal intervals obtained by cutting between i and i + 1
for each i ∈[n −1] \ At. That is, if [n −1] \ At = {j1 < j2 < · · · }, then the partition is
[1, j1]⊔[j1+1, j2]⊔· · · . Let Ft = {fs : s ≤t and is is a first occurrence}, so that Fm = F(w).
We prove, by induction on t, that:
(i) Ft is an acyclic subgraph of Gπ whose connected components are exactly the parts of
Pt;
(ii) for each part B of Pt, the values occupying the positions of B in the one-line notation
of πt are exactly the values in B.

Both invariants hold at t = 0: we have A0 = ∅, the partition P0 consists of singletons,
F0 = ∅, and π0 = e.
Suppose the invariants hold at time t −1. If it ∈At−1, then At = At−1, Pt = Pt−1, and
Ft = Ft−1. Positions it and it+1 lie in the same part B of Pt−1, since no cut of Pt−1 separates

two values of B within positions of B. Both invariants are preserved, and we note for later
use that both endpoints of the edge ft lie in B.
If instead it is a first occurrence, then Pt is obtained from Pt−1 by merging the two parts

swap exchanges two values between the positions of BL and those of BR, so the values of
BL ∪BR still occupy the positions of BL ∪BR in πt; this preserves invariant (ii). In this case
both endpoints of ft lie in the part BL ∪BR of Pt.
In either case, the endpoints of ft lie in a single part of Pt. Since the partitions only
coarsen as t increases, every edge of Gπ has both endpoints in a single part of the final
partition Pm. On the other hand, invariant (i) shows that each part of Pm is connected
in Gπ, indeed already connected using only the edges of Fm. The parts of Pm are therefore
precisely the connected components of Gπ, and F(w) = Fm is an acyclic subgraph of Gπ
whose components are these parts, hence a spanning forest of Gπ.
The spanning forest description also recovers a standard fact, phrased through the view-
point of inversion graphs as |supp(π)| = n −κ(Gπ). For every reduced word w, the set F(w)
contains exactly one edge per first-occurrence letter, hence one per element of supp(π), and
as a spanning forest of Gπ on n vertices with κ(Gπ) components, it has n −κ(Gπ) edges.
3.2
Repeated letters and the cyclomatic number
It follows that the number of repeated letters in a reduced word is a graph invariant. A
reduced word w for π has ℓ(π) = |E(Gπ)| letters, one per edge of Gπ by Proposition 3.1.
The first-occurrence letters contribute the n −κ(Gπ) edges of the spanning forest F(w), by
Proposition 3.2. The repeated letters therefore account for the remaining edges, giving the
following theorem.
Theorem 3.3. Every reduced word for π contains exactly β(Gπ) repeated letters.
The permutations with no repetition in any reduced word are easy to identify. By The-
orem 3.3, no reduced word for π contains a repeated letter precisely when β(Gπ) = 0, that
is, when Gπ is a forest.
Corollary 3.4. No reduced word for π contains a repeated letter if and only if Gπ is a forest.
Combined with the avoidance characterization of forests given by Proposition 2.4, this re-
covers Tenner’s theorem [17] that the permutations whose reduced words contain no repeated
letter are precisely those avoiding 321 and 3412.

The number of repeated letters can also be bounded above by pattern counts.
The

Corollary 3.5. The number of repeated letters in any reduced word for π is at most the total
number of copies of 321 and 3412 in π.
Proof. The induced cycles of a graph span its cycle space: any cycle with a chord is the
symmetric difference of two shorter cycles, so induction on length expresses every cycle as a
sum of chordless cycles. The number of induced cycles is therefore at least the cyclomatic
number. By Proposition 2.3, every induced cycle of Gπ has length 3 or 4, and by the proof of
Proposition 2.4 these correspond to copies of 321 and 3412 in π, respectively. The corollary
follows from Theorem 3.3.
Let [321, 3412](π) denote the total number of copies of 321 and 3412 in π. We have
just seen, via Corollary 3.4, that when [321, 3412](π) = 0, every reduced word for π has
zero repeated letters. Daly [4] showed the analogous statement at the next value: when
[321, 3412](π) = 1, every reduced word for π has precisely one repeated letter. The cycle-
space viewpoint extends this one step further. If [321, 3412](π) ≤2, then Gπ has at most two
induced cycles, and any two distinct cycles are linearly independent in the cycle space, so in
this case β(Gπ) = [321, 3412](π) and hence every reduced word for π contains [321, 3412](π)
repeated letters. The conclusion fails for the next value: the permutation π = 34512 has
inversion graph K3,2, with three induced 4-cycles and no triangles, so [321, 3412](34512) = 3
while β(G34512) = 2.
The general sharpness condition for Corollary 3.5 is the question of when the induced
cycles of Gπ form a basis of the cycle space. By Theorem 3.3 and Proposition 2.4, both
sides of the bound depend only on Gπ (the repetition count is β(Gπ); the total 321 and 3412
count is the number of induced triangles and induced 4-cycles), so sharpness is an invariant
of the inversion graph. Tenner [18] characterized this invariant by exhibiting a list of ten
permutation patterns whose avoidance is necessary and sufficient. Their inversion graphs
are the four small graphs

inversion graph of the example 34512 above. The graph-theoretic version of the sharpness
theorem could be obtained by classifying these minimal obstructions directly; we do not

Unimodal cycles and caterpillars
Edelman [7] studied the minimum number of inversions among permutations with a fixed
number of cycles. He showed that
min{ℓ(σ) : σ ∈Sn and c(σ) = k} = n −k,
and that this minimum is attained precisely by the direct sums of unimodal cycles of intervals.
Since ℓ′(π) = n −c(π), achieving Edelman’s minimum is the same as satisfying ℓ(π) = ℓ′(π).
Edelman’s theorem therefore says that ℓ(π) = ℓ′(π) if and only if π is a direct sum of
unimodal cycles of intervals. In this section we recover this direct-sum description from the
forest viewpoint, and as a structural bonus we show that every connected acyclic inversion
graph is a caterpillar.
A unimodal cycle of an interval 2 is a permutation of an interval of integers that can be
written as a single cycle whose entries, after cyclic rotation, strictly increase to a maximum
and then strictly decrease. After standardizing the interval to [n] and rotating the cycle to
end with its minimum value (namely 1 in the standardized form), the cycle has the form
(a1, . . . , ar, n, b1, . . . , bs, 1) where a1 < · · · < ar < n and n > b1 > · · · > bs > 1. The cases
r = 0 and s = 0 are allowed, as are a singleton cycle (a fixed point) and the empty cycle.
For instance, take the permutation γ = (3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 7, 6, 2, 1), written in cycle notation.
The entries of this cycle increase to the maximum 9 and then decrease to 1, so γ is a unimodal
cycle of [9]. Its one-line notation, γ = 314582697, is not unimodal in any obvious sense. The
inversion graph Gγ, shown in Figure 2, is a caterpillar: its leaves are 1, 4, 5, 6, and 9, and
after removing them we obtain the path 3, 2, 8, 7.
2The terminology is potentially confusing: the unimodal cycles considered here are not the unimodal
permutations studied elsewhere. A permutation π = π1 · · · πn is unimodal if its one-line notation increases
to its maximum and then decreases, equivalently if π ∈Av(213, 312); these permutations are enumerated
by 2n−1. This permutation class has been studied from several perspectives, including its cyclic structure
and connections with one-dimensional dynamics by Gannon [9], its cycle enumerator by Thibon [19], and
its relationship with almost-increasing cycles by Archer and Lauderdale [1], who give a bijection between
almost-increasing cycles on [n] and unimodal permutations on [n −1]. The unimodal cycle terminology is
also distinct from Chung’s study of long unimodal subsequences in arbitrary permutations [3].

this shows immediately that unimodal cycles avoid 321, because the middle entry of a copy

5.1
Trees and unimodal cycles of intervals
If Gπ is a tree, then ℓ(π) = n −1 and |supp(π)| = n −1, so every reduced word for π
uses each of s1, . . . , sn−1 exactly once. The permutations whose reduced words use each
simple reflection exactly once are the Coxeter elements of Sn, and the following proposition
is the standard classification of Coxeter elements of Sn (and, by restriction, of the parabolic
subgroup generated by sa, sa+1, . . . , sb−1).
The result is well-known; we include a proof
because the inductive construction tracks the cycle structure explicitly, which we use below.
Proposition 5.1. Let a ≤b be integers, and let γ be a permutation of [a, b]. Then γ is a
unimodal cycle of [a, b] if and only if it has a reduced word in which each of sa, sa+1, . . . , sb−1
appears exactly once.
Proof. We apply induction on b −a, removing the smallest value a from the cycle and the
generator sa from the word at each step.
If b −a = 0, then [a, b] = {a} and γ is the identity, the singleton unimodal cycle, with
the empty reduced word.
If b −a = 1, then γ is a unimodal cycle of [a, b] if and only if γ = (a + 1, a) = sa, whose
only reduced word, sa, uses sa once. So the result holds.

once. The generator sa commutes with every sj for j ≥a + 2, so among the letters of the
word it fails to commute only with sa+1. Since sa+1 occurs just once, we may commute sa
past everything on one side of it, moving sa to the far left or the far right according to
whether it occurs before or after sa+1:

unimodal cycle of [a + 1, b].
Since a is fixed by δ, we may write δ in our normalization as (c1, . . . , ck, a + 1), with
the entries increasing to b and then decreasing to a + 1.
Multiplying on the left gives,

following a in the cyclic order. Multiplying on the right gives δsa = (c1, . . . , ck, a + 1, a),
again a unimodal cycle of [a, b], this time with a + 1 immediately preceding a.

γ = (c1, . . . , cr, b, d1, . . . , ds, a).

side it equals c1 and immediately follows a, and if it lies on the decreasing side it equals ds
and immediately precedes a. Multiplying γ by sa = (a, a + 1) on the left in the first case,
and on the right in the second case, removes a from the cycle and leaves a unimodal cycle of
[a + 1, b]. By induction, that smaller cycle has a reduced word using each of sa+1, . . . , sb−1
exactly once, and reinserting sa on the appropriate side expresses γ using each of sa, . . . , sb−1
exactly once.
This expression is reduced. For each i with a ≤i ≤b −1, the cycle γ does not preserve
[a, i], since γ is a single cycle on [a, b] and [a, i] is a proper nonempty subset. An expression in
the simple reflections that avoids si never moves a value across the cut between i and i + 1, so
it preserves [a, i]; since γ does not, every expression for γ contains si. Therefore ℓ(γ) ≥b−a,
and the constructed expression, of length b −a, is reduced.
The tree statement follows.
Proposition 5.2. An inversion graph Gπ is a tree if and only if π is a unimodal cycle of [n].
Proof. Suppose Gπ is a tree. Then ℓ(π) = |E(Gπ)| = n−1 and κ(π) = 1, so |supp(π)| = n−1.
Every reduced word for π has length n −1 and uses n −1 distinct simple reflections, so each
of s1, . . . , sn−1 appears exactly once. By Proposition 5.1, π is a unimodal cycle of [n].
Conversely, suppose π is a unimodal cycle of [n]. By Proposition 5.1, π has a reduced
word using each of s1, . . . , sn−1 exactly once, so ℓ(π) = n −1 and hence |E(Gπ)| = n −1.
Since π is a single n-cycle, it is sum indecomposable, so Gπ is connected by Proposition 2.1.
A connected graph on n vertices with n −1 edges is a tree.
Taking direct sums gives the following.
Corollary 5.3. The inversion graph Gπ is a forest if and only if π is a direct sum of unimodal
cycles of intervals.

5.2
Connected acyclic inversion and caterpillars
A caterpillar is a tree in which every nonleaf vertex lies on a single path, called the spine;
equivalently, removing all leaves yields a path.
We use the following elementary characterization. A tree T is a caterpillar if and only
if its line graph has a Hamiltonian path; equivalently, if and only if the edges of T can be
listed
e1, e2, . . . , em
so that consecutive edges share an endpoint. Indeed, if such an ordering is given, let xi be
the endpoint shared by ei and ei+1. After deleting repetitions from
x1, x2, . . . , xm−1,
we obtain a path in T, and every edge of T is incident with this path. Thus every vertex off
the path is a leaf. The converse is immediate by listing the edges of a caterpillar along the
spine, inserting the leaf edges incident with each spine vertex when that vertex is reached.
By Proposition 5.2, every connected acyclic inversion graph is the inversion graph of a
unimodal cycle of [n], so it suffices to prove the caterpillar conclusion for inversion graphs
of unimodal cycles. This result seems to have been first explicitly noted by Brualdi and
Dahl [2].
Proposition 5.4. If π is a unimodal cycle of [n], then Gπ is a caterpillar.
Proof. The cases n ≤2 are immediate, so assume n ≥3. By Proposition 5.2, Gπ is a tree,
and π avoids 321.
We define lists L and M as follows. Let L be the values of the left-to-right maxima of π,
listed from left to right, and let M be the values of the remaining entries, again listed from
left to right. We think of these as lists of vertices of Gπ. Because π avoids 321, both lists
must be increasing. Because of this monotonicity, every edge of Gπ joins a value of L to a
value of M.
For each ℓ∈L, its neighbors in M form a consecutive block of the M-list. If ℓis adjacent
to two entries of M, then ℓlies to the left of the first and is larger than the second; every
entry of M between them sits between them in position and value, hence is also inverted
with ℓ.
Two vertices of L cannot share two neighbors in M, and the neighbor blocks move
rightward as ℓmoves rightward. Suppose ℓ, ℓ′ ∈L with ℓbefore ℓ′, and m, m′ ∈M with m′
before m. If ℓm and ℓ′m′ are both edges of Gπ, then since both L and M are increasing, ℓm′
and ℓ′m are also inversions, and ℓ, m, ℓ′, m′ span a cycle in Gπ, contradicting that Gπ is a
tree. Applied to a shared pair of neighbors, this rules out overlap of size two; applied to a
leftward-crossing pair, it rules out any block moving backward.
17


## Page 18


Consecutive neighbor blocks therefore either are disjoint or meet in a single entry of M.
They cannot be disjoint, since the cut between them would disconnect Gπ: earlier values of L
have all their neighbors on one side of the cut, later values on the other. Hence consecutive
blocks meet in exactly one entry of M.
List the edges of Gπ block by block: first all edges incident with the first value of L,
in the order of their endpoints in M; then all edges incident with the second value of L;
and so on. This lists every edge exactly once. Consecutive edges within a block share their
endpoint in L, and consecutive edges across a join share the unique endpoint in M where
the two blocks meet. Thus this edge ordering is a Hamiltonian path in the line graph of Gπ,
and the characterization above implies that Gπ is a caterpillar.
The same ordering can be described by sorting the edges by the sum of their endpoint
values: within a block the endpoint in L is fixed and the endpoint in M increases, while
across a join the endpoint in M is fixed and the endpoint in L increases, so the endpoint
sums strictly increase along the path.
6
Concluding remarks
We have shown that the inversion graph of a permutation π is closely tied to several appar-
ently different aspects of π: the gap between its Coxeter and reflection lengths, the number
of repeated letters in its reduced words, and its cycle structure. The unifying quantity is the
cyclomatic number β(Gπ), which equals the exact repetition count, bounds the cycle surplus
above, sandwiches the length gap between β(Gπ) and 2β(Gπ), and provides an upper bound
on the number of 321 and 3412 patterns π contains.
Reflection length and its connection to the inversion graph raise a broader question:
does an analogue of reflection length make sense for arbitrary simple graphs, beyond the
inversion-graph setting? In forthcoming work with Mandrick [14], we introduce an operation
on simple graphs called edge reflection, modeled on the effect of a permutation reflection on
its inversion graph, and study the resulting length statistic. Here again, forests appear as
extremal objects: just as ℓ(π) −ℓ′(π) = 0 precisely when Gπ is a forest, the corresponding
length gap in the graph setting vanishes precisely when the graph itself is a forest.
