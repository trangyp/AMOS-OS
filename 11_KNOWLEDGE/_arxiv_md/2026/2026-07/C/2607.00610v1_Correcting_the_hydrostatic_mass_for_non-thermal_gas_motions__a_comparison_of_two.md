ABSTRACT
An accurate estimation of the mass of galaxy clusters is key to precisely and unbiasedly constraining cosmological parameters through
their number count. The hydrostatic mass, estimated from the properties of the intracluster medium (ICM) assuming hydrostatic
equilibrium, sphericity, and thermal-only pressure, is known to be biased by 10 to 20%, most likely due to non-thermal pressure
support from gas motions. Two corrections have been proposed: i) replacing the thermal pressure by the total pressure Ptot = Pth+Pnth,
or ii) adding effective mass terms derived from the gas momentum equation. We compare these approaches using a numerical replica
of the Virgo cluster as a case study, estimating corrected masses from 3D radial profiles in different cluster regions and from projected
sightline velocities mimicking XRISM observations. We find that the two methods do not yield the same results in 3D: the non-thermal
pressure correction increases the mass by a growing amount with radius (from a few per cent in the core to ∼40% at the virial radius),
whereas the effective mass terms provide a correction that varies less with radius. When estimated from projections, the two methods
agree to within a few per cent for a given sightline, but the non-thermal pressure fraction is underestimated by about a factor of 2
compared to the 3D case. Furthermore, projection effects can change the inferred non-thermal pressure fraction by up to a factor of 2,
particularly when the sightline is aligned with cosmic filaments.

1. Introduction
The population of galaxy clusters and its evolution in mass and
redshift directly depend on the Ωm and σ8 cosmological param-
eters, making them powerful cosmological probes (see e.g. La-
hav & Liddle 2022, for a review on cosmological parameters).
These objects are indeed located at the nodes of the cosmic web,
corresponding to density peaks in the Universe’s matter distri-
bution. They originate from primordial overdensities that have
continuously accreted matter since the earliest stages of cosmic
evolution (see e.g. Liddle 2015; Peebles 2020). The key step in
using galaxy clusters as cosmological probes through their num-
ber counts is to calibrate their mass in an unbiased way (see,
e.g., Allen et al. 2011; Pratt et al. 2019, for reviews). Several
methods can be used to compute the cluster mass, among which
are scaling relations based on the self-similar model of Kaiser
(1986) (see e.g. Giodini et al. 2013 for a review and Aymerich
et al. 2024,2025 and references therein for recent works), the
spatial and velocity distribution of cluster galaxies (see for ex-
ample Caustic in Diaferio & Geller 1997 or MAMPOSSt in Ma-
mon et al. 2013), or weak lensing (see Hoekstra et al. 2013 for a
review and Okabe & Smith 2016, Applegate et al. 2014, Umetsu
et al. 2016, and Herbonnet et al. 2020 respectively for the Lo-
CuSS, WtG, CLASH and CCCP catalogues).
Another way is to estimate the total mass of galaxy clus-
ters from the properties of the intracluster medium (ICM) by

assuming hydrostatic equilibrium, that is, the balance between
the thermal ICM pressure and the cluster gravitational potential,
assuming spherical symmetry (see, e.g., Ettori et al. 2013, for a
review). This hydrostatic mass has been estimated from obser-
vations in the X-rays (e.g. Pointecouteau et al. 2005; Vikhlinin
et al. 2006; Vikhlinin et al. 2009; Pratt et al. 2009; Ettori et al.
2019) but has been shown in simulations to be biased by 10–
20% due to non-thermal pressure support (see e.g. Rasia et al.
2006), a result that has been confirmed and further quantified
observationally (e.g. Eckert et al. 2019, and references therein).
This hydrostatic mass bias is nowadays calibrated from simu-
lations and accounted for in the cosmological analysis, though
with values distributed around (1 −b) ≃0.8 among the differ-
ent studies (see comparisons in e.g. Biffi et al. 2016; Gianfagna
et al. 2021; Lebeau et al. 2024b). Moreover, the value proposed
in Salvati et al. (2018) to reduce the tension between constraints
on Ωm and σ8 from Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) and
cluster number counts from thermal Sunyaev–Zel’dovich (tSZ,
Sunyaev & Zeldovich 1972) observations is (1−b) = 0.62±0.07,
which is in disagreement with most of the values calibrated from
simulations and weak-lensing. There is thus a need for a better
understanding and correction of the physical sources of devia-
tion from hydrostatic equilibrium in the ICM.
As a matter of fact, various non-thermal processes (see e.g.
Ferrari 2012, for a review) could contribute to push the ICM
out of hydrostatic equilibrium, such as magnetic fields (see e.g.
Dolag et al. 2005, 2008) and cosmic rays (see e.g. Van Weeren

et al. 2024; Romero et al. 2023, 2024; Lovisari et al. 2024; Adam
et al. 2025). Moreover, an important predicting work is being
carried out in prevision of the future with NewAthena sattelite
(e.g. Clerc et al. 2019; Beaumont et al. 2024; Molin et al. 2025).
In that context, correction methods to account for gas mo-
tion in the mass estimation of clusters have been proposed. On
the one hand, one can directly correct the hydrostatic mass by
adding the non-thermal pressure contribution to the total pres-
sure support (as in e.g. Nelson et al. 2014; Pearce et al. 2020;
Gianfagna et al. 2021; Ettori & Eckert 2022). On the other hand,
one can account for gas motion by adding additional effective
mass terms derived from the Euler gas momentum equation (as
in e.g. Lau et al. 2009, 2013; Suto et al. 2013). In this work, we
compare these two approaches using a numerical replica of the
Virgo cluster (Sorce et al. 2021) as a case study. We first provide
a concise description of the simulation in Sect. 2 and present the
hydrostatic mass and its correction accounting for the velocity
field in Sect. 3. We then estimate these masses and their bias
with respect to the total mass using 3D radial profiles in Sect. 4.
Using projected quantities, we explore possible estimates of the
hydrostatic mass and its correction in the case of XRISM ex-
posures in Sect. 5. We finally discuss our results in Sect. 6 and
conclude in Sect. 7.
2. The Virgo cluster simulated replica
The present study is based on a constrained hydrodynamical
zoom-in simulation of the Virgo cluster embedded in its large-
scale environment; full details of its construction and valida-
tion are provided in Sorce et al. (2021), which was extensively
used in Lebeau et al. (2024a,b, 2025, 2026); Sorce et al. (2026).
We here summarise the main characteristics of this numeri-
cal replica of the Virgo cluster. The simulation is part of the
CLONE (Constrained LOcal and Nesting Environment) project,
in which the initial conditions of the local Universe are recon-
structed from observed galaxy positions and peculiar velocities
taken from the Cosmicflows-2 catalog (Tully et al. 2013) via a re-
verse Zel’dovich approximation following the method described
in Sorce et al. 2016. Among the 200 dark matter replicas of the
Virgo cluster produced by Sorce et al. (2019) within this con-
strained environment, the one that best reproduces the statistical
properties and merging history of the full ensemble was selected
as the basis for the hydrodynamical run (Sorce et al. 2021). The
resulting simulation reproduces the observed local environment
of Virgo remarkably well, including the foreground and back-
ground cosmic filaments along our line of sight and the group of
galaxies currently infalling onto the cluster.
The code employed for the simulation is the adaptive mesh
refinement (AMR) solver RAMSES (Teyssier 2002), run within
a 738 Mpc (500 Mpc h−1) local Universe box. The zoom-in
region centred on Virgo is a sphere of 30 Mpc diameter re-
solved with an effective number of 81923 dark matter parti-
cles, each of mass mDM = 3 × 107 M⊙, and an AMR gas grid
reaching a finest cell size of 0.35 kpc in the cluster core. The
adopted cosmological parameters follow Planck Collaboration
(2014): H0 = 67.77 km s−1 Mpc−1, Ωm = 0.307, ΩΛ = 0.693,
Ωb = 0.048, σ8 = 0.829, and ns = 0.961.
The baryonic physics is modeled through sub-grid prescrip-
tions for radiative gas cooling and heating, star formation, and
kinetic feedback from type II supernovae and active galactic
nuclei (AGN), following the Horizon-AGN implementation of
Dubois et al. (2014, 2016). The AGN jet direction is set by the
spin of the central black hole (Dubois et al. 2021), which pro-
duces a more physically motivated feedback geometry than a
fixed axis. Both the dark matter-only and the hydrodynamical
runs are in good agreement with observations of the Virgo clus-
ter and its local environment (Sorce et al. 2021; Lebeau et al.
2024b).

4. Mass corrections from 3D radial profiles

derivatives, which give access to the true, unprojected quanti-

6. Discussion
6.1. Comparison of the two correction approaches
We have shown that the two approaches investigated in this
work, namely the non-thermal pressure correction (Mα) and the
effective mass terms correction (Meff), do not yield identical re-
sults when applied to the 3D radial profiles in the different re-
gions of our Virgo replica. Specifically, Mα increasingly exceeds
MHE with radius, driven by the rise of the non-thermal pressure
fraction α in the outskirts, whereas Meff shows a correction that
varies less with radius, with the contributions of Mdisp and Mrot
partially compensating each other. This difference is expected
from a physical standpoint: Mα accounts for the overall non-
thermal pressure support regardless of the nature of the under-
lying gas motion, whereas Meff decomposes it into distinct kine-
matic components (random motion and bulk rotation), which can
have opposite signs. As a consequence, Mα tends to overestimate
the correction in the outskirts, where the non-thermal pressure
fraction is high but where the effective mass terms partially can-
cel out.

entire cluster, it remains close to unity in the core (R < 500 kpc)
and increases outwards, though not monotonically, reaching

inferred non-thermal pressure fraction by up to a factor of two.
6.2. Comparison with previous works and impact on the
mass bias
The non-thermal pressure fraction profiles obtained in the 3D
case are in good agreement with previous simulation-based stud-
ies. In particular, the increase of α from below 10% in the core
to ∼22% at R500 and ∼33% at Rvir is consistent with the results
of Nelson et al. (2014), Angelinelli et al. (2020), and Pearce
et al. (2020), though with a significant scatter among the dif-
ferent regions, reflecting the complex and anisotropic dynamics
of Virgo’s local environment. The values of α in the filament re-
gions rise above 0.3 beyond Rvir, which is higher than the typical
values found in large cluster samples but consistent with those
found in disturbed systems in Angelinelli et al. (2020). This is

likely due to the enhanced turbulence in the filaments connected
to Virgo, as shown in Lebeau et al. (2025).
We note, however, that we define the non-thermal pressure as
Pnth = ρσ2
3D/3, using the velocity dispersion computed in spher-
ical shells without multi-scale filtering to separate bulk from tur-
bulent motions. This is the same approach as in Nelson et al.
(2014) and Pearce et al. (2020), but differs from that of Vazza
et al. (2018) and Angelinelli et al. (2020), who showed that the
turbulent velocity filtered from bulk motions yields lower non-
thermal pressure fractions. Our values of α should thus be con-
sidered as upper limits on the turbulent contribution, and fu-
ture comparisons using filtered velocities would help to disen-
tangle the respective roles of bulk and turbulent motions. Sim-
ilarly, the simulation does not include magnetic fields and cos-
mic rays, both of which could affect the dissipation of turbulent
motions and the resulting non-thermal pressure support (see e.g.
Simionescu et al. 2019). These simplifications do not affect the
comparison between the two correction methods, which rely on
the same underlying velocity field, but they do affect the absolute
values of α and, consequently, the corrected masses.
Regarding the analytical modeling of α, Shi & Komatsu
(2014) proposed a model based on the mass accretion history
of clusters that predicts a radially increasing non-thermal pres-
sure fraction, in qualitative agreement with our results. Shi et al.
(2016) further showed that this model can be used to correct the
hydrostatic mass bias, similarly to the Mα approach used in this
work. However, their model assumes a universal profile for α
that does not account for the anisotropic environment within in-
dividual clusters, which we have shown has a significant impact
on the non-thermal pressure fraction.
The effective mass terms approach has been less widely used
in the literature than the non-thermal pressure correction, but our
results are qualitatively consistent with Lau et al. (2009) and Lau
et al. (2013), who found that the velocity dispersion term can be
either positive or negative depending on the radial profile of σr
relative to σt, and that the rotation term provides a roughly con-
stant positive contribution. The estimation of Mrot from projec-
tions using the quadrant estimator (Appendix D), following Ota
et al. (2018), yields lower values than in the 3D case, suggest-
ing that this estimator may underestimate the ICM bulk rotation.
Alternative techniques for estimating vt from projected velocity
maps would be worth exploring in future work.
These methodological considerations directly affect the re-
sulting mass biases, which we provide in Tab. F.1. The 3D hy-
drostatic mass bias of 0.95 at R500 is consistent with the upper
end of the (1 −b) ≃0.8–0.9 range found in large simulation
samples (e.g. Nagai et al. 2007; Biffi et al. 2016; Pearce et al.
2020; Gianfagna et al. 2021), likely reflecting the specific dy-
namical state of this relatively low-mass and unrelaxed cluster.
The overcorrection by Mα to 1.13 at this radius is consistent with
the finding of Biffi et al. (2016) that the object-to-object varia-
tion is large and that a correction based on α does not necessarily
bring the bias closer to unity for individual clusters. At Rvir, the
3D bias of 0.62 is significantly lower than the typical values,
which is expected given the complex dynamics in the outskirts,
including strong accretion flows and turbulence in the connected
filaments. For the projected cases, the z-sightline stands out as
the most favorable case, with corrected biases of 0.85 (Mα,ew)
and 0.83 (Meff,ew), closer to one than the uncorrected value of
0.71.
6.3. From projections to observations
The comparison between the 3D and projected mass corrections
has direct implications for the interpretation of XRISM obser-
vations. As shown in Sect. 5, the non-thermal pressure fraction
estimated from projections is systematically lower than its 3D
counterpart by about a factor of two, and certain sightlines are
strongly affected by the local filamentary environment. For a
cluster like Virgo, XRISM measurements of the sightline veloc-
ity dispersion would thus only partially capture the non-thermal
pressure support, and the resulting mass correction would be in-
sufficient to fully account for the hydrostatic mass bias. In addi-
tion, the strong departure from isotropy quantified by β, see bot-
tom panel of Fig. 3, implies that the assumption σ2
1D = σ2
3D/3
used to recover the 3D velocity dispersion from line-of-sight
measurements is itself a source of systematic error, which con-
tributes to the underestimation of the non-thermal pressure frac-
tion from projections.
Moreover, the deprojected hydrostatic mass is already signif-
icantly overestimated at R500 due to the model-free geometrical
deprojection method used in Lebeau et al. (2024b). Adding mass
through either correction method further increases this overes-
timation, highlighting that correcting the hydrostatic mass for
non-thermal pressure support is only meaningful if the hydro-
static mass itself is accurately estimated. The strong dependence
of the results on the sightline further complicates the picture:
the cen sightline, aligned with the filaments connected to Virgo,
yields a very different velocity dispersion and thus mass correc-
tion compared to the other sightlines. In practice, the correction
of the hydrostatic mass from velocity measurements should thus
be interpreted with particular caution for clusters whose sightline
intersects prominent filaments of their large-scale environment,
as is the case for Virgo.
It is also important to recall that this study is based on a sin-
gle simulated cluster, and the results are specific to the dynam-
ical state and local environment of the Virgo replica. Extending
this analysis to a larger sample of clusters with different masses,
formation histories, and environments would be necessary to as-
sess the generality of our findings. Furthermore, the projected
quantities used in this work are not mock observations: they do
not include instrumental effects such as the point spread func-
tion, energy resolution, or photon noise, which could further de-
grade the accuracy of the velocity dispersion measurement. We
plan to address both of these points in dedicated studies using
mock XRISM observations and additional clusters from larger
constrained simulations of the local Universe.
7. Conclusion
In this work, we compare two approaches to correct the hydro-
static mass of galaxy clusters by accounting for non-thermal gas
motions, using a constrained hydrodynamical zoom-in simula-
tion of the Virgo cluster as a case study: the non-thermal pressure
correction (Mα) and the effective mass terms correction (Meff).
We estimate these corrected masses both from 3D radial profiles
in four distinct regions of the cluster and from projected sightline
velocities mimicking XRISM-like measurements.
We show that the two correction methods are not equivalent:
Mα increasingly exceeds MHE with radius due to the rising non-
thermal pressure fraction, whereas Meff provides an almost radi-
ally constant correction due to the partial compensation between
the velocity dispersion and bulk rotation terms. Both approaches
increase the estimated mass by up to 25 to 40% in the outskirts,
but neither fully removes the hydrostatic mass bias. When es-

This case study does not aim to provide universal pre-
scriptions for correcting the hydrostatic mass bias, but rather
to demonstrate how the local environment, dynamical state,
sightline orientation, and projection weighting schemes affect
the mass correction in a detailed, well-characterized numerical
replica. As XRISM already delivers increasingly precise mea-
surements of the ICM velocity field, and as NewAthena (Cruise
et al. 2025) will push these measurements further in the late
2030s, the correction methods investigated here will become di-
rectly applicable to observations. In future work, we plan to ap-
ply them to mock observations of this Virgo replica, including
realistic instrumental effects, and to extend the analysis to other
clusters in larger simulations of the local Universe.

Appendix D: Estimating vt from projections
The tangential velocity, vt, cannot be measured directly. To over-
come this, we make the assumption, following Ota et al. (2018),
that if the cluster has a global bulk rotation, it induces a slightly
higher sightline velocity in a region of the cluster and a smaller
one in the opposite region. We thus divide the sightline velocity
maps into eight sectors, as shown in Fig. D.1, numbered from
1 to 8 in trigonometric order, each with an aperture of π/4 in
the [0,2π] range. We thus have four pairs of opposite sectors: 1-
5, 2-6, 3-7 and 4-8. Therefore, the maximum absolute velocity
difference among the pairs, max(∆vLOS), is assimilated to vt.
