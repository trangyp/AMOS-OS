Horizon Structure and Quasinormal Mode Spectra

In realistic settings, black holes are expected to be embedded in astrophysical environments.
These environments, including possible dark matter distributions, can modify observable properties
of black holes and leave imprints on their quasinormal mode spectra. In this work, we model the
environment as matter with anisotropic pressure, and we consider a charged black hole embedded in
it. The resulting spacetime is described by the Kiselev metric. We first analyze its horizon structure.
We then investigate the quasinormal modes of a massless charged scalar field propagating on this
background.
For this purpose, we develop a nontrivial extension of Leaver’s continued fraction
method to incorporate the effects of the surrounding matter, and we combine this framework with
automatic differentiation techniques. We also compare our results to those obtained with the sixth-
order Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin approximation.
We find that the surrounding matter modifies the oscillation frequencies and damping rates and
leads to the appearance of long-lived modes. We also identify avoided crossings regions and reor-
ganization of the modes in the spectra. Our results demonstrate the importance of incorporating
surrounding matter when modeling realistic black holes. The numerical framework we developed
here provides a tool for studying quasinormal modes in non-vacuum spacetimes and can be extended
to a broad class of black-hole geometries embedded in matter fields.

I.
INTRODUCTION
In real astrophysical scenarios, black holes (BHs) are
not isolated, they are embedded in environments com-
posed of surrounding matter or energy. While the ma-
jority of binary BH models or studies on BH pertur-
bations have focused on vacuum solutions, precision
gravitational-wave observations [1–4] now motivate un-
derstanding the imprints of these environmental effects.
How does surrounding matter affect the observables
that we can link to BHs? To answer this question, an
environmental distribution and corresponding spacetime
geometry must be known. Anisotropic fluids provide a
phenomenological yet controlled way to model the in-
fluence of a BH environment. For example, it has been
shown that the presence of anisotropic matter could alter
the shape and size of the BH shadow, which can poten-
tially be observed by future Event Horizon Telescope ob-
servations [5–7]. The presence of ultralight dark matter
may yield similar modifications of the BH shadow [8–
11]. A rotating BH surrounded by an anisotropic fluid
may act as a particle accelerator [12]. Other phenom-
ena studied in this context include superradiance and BH
(in-) stabilities [13], the Penrose process [14], quasinor-
mal modes [15, 16], or the photon sphere [17, 18]. The
environment may also measurably alter the quasi-normal
mode (QNM) spectrum [19, 20] or the phase of gravita-
tional waves of extreme-mass-ratio inspirals that will be
measured with LISA [21, 22].
In this work, we model a single BH immersed in an
environment using the Kiselev metric [23]. The Kiselev
metric is an exact, static and spherically symmetric so-
lution of Einstein’s equations with an (effective) energy-
momentum tensor that resembles that of an anisotropic
fluid.
The effective energy-momentum tensor is com-
posed of a superposition of matter or energy contribu-
tions that interact only gravitationally. Thus, the Kise-
lev spacetime provides a flexible framework to incorpo-
rate matter or energy into BH spacetimes; special cases
include, for example, the Schwarzschild and Reissner-
Nordström (RN) metrics, and RN–de Sitter or –anti-de
Sitter spacetimes. It was also shown that the anisotropic
stress tensor can be decomposed into a perfect fluid plus
an electromagnetic field [24].
Here, we focus on a two-component Kiselev model that
represents a charged BH embedded in an anisotropic fluid
which is characterized by a linear equation of state with
coefficient w = −1/3 [25]. We display a schematic rep-
resentation of the setup in Fig. 1. This (effective) fluid
can mimic dark matter distributions [26], a condensate
of cosmic strings [27–29], or a Barriola–Vilenkin global
monopole [30, 31]. In the latter, the geometry is locally
Schwarzschild-like but globally exhibits a deficit solid an-
gle, yielding a conical spacetime of mathematical interest.
There is extensive observational evidence for the exis-
tence of dark matter [32] that constitutes more than 80%
of all gravitating matter. Some authors have explored
connections between anisotropic fluids described by the
Kiselev metric and galaxy rotation curves [26, 33]. This
connection was used to explain the asymptotic flatness
of some rotation curves [26]. Fluid halo models with an
anisotropic equation of state have been fitted to observed
rotation curve data [34] and used to derive weak-field cir-
cular velocities in “reduced” Kiselev spacetimes [24].
The anisotropic fluid we consider may also be inter-
preted as a condensate of non interacting, radially ori-
ented, one-dimensional cosmic strings.
The effective
energy-momentum tensor can be derived as an ensemble
average over the strings [27], and describes field theories
that generalize action-at-a-distance interactions [35, 36].
It was shown that the spacetime around an infinite
straight string remains locally flat but is globally coni-
cal [37]. This geometric effect may lead to characteristic
observational signatures.

After analyzing the horizon structure of the two-
component Kiselev spacetime, we determine its response
to external perturbations as illustrated in Fig. 1. This
response is determined by the QNM spectrum [38–42]
and computed within perturbation theory. QNMs encode
key information about the underlying spacetime geome-
try, making them powerful tools for BH spectroscopy and
studies of their (mode) stability. Most of the QNM liter-
ature focuses on the spectral properties of vacuum BHs,
a clear idealization.
In realistic astrophysical settings,
massive BHs at galactic centers interact with dense envi-
ronments, including accretion disks, dark matter struc-
tures and halos, all of which shape galactic and BH dy-
namics.
Mergers involving such systems generate de-
tectable gravitational waves.
Furthermore, interstellar
plasma obscures low-frequency electromagnetic signals,
making gravitational waves a unique probe of these en-
vironments.
Understanding how environmental effects

In this work, we consider a massless, charged scalar

For this pur-

comparative perspective. In our setup, the WKB effec-
tive potential is explicitly frequency dependent, and the
treatment developed here applies generally to WKB po-
tentials with this property.
Although we focus on the analysis of an anisotropic
fluid around a BH, the methods developed here can be
applied to a broad class of non-vacuum BH spacetimes.
Our results reveal novel features in the QNM spec-
trum of charged BHs surrounded by an anisotropic fluid,
including the presence of long-lived modes, asymmetric
structures in the spectrum and regions of avoided cross-
ings. These findings contribute to the broader effort to
model realistic BH environments.
This paper is structured as follows. In Sec. II, we in-
troduce the Kiselev spacetime and the two-component
model we consider, and we discuss its physical and geo-
metrical properties. In Sec. III, we present the dynam-
ics of a charged scalar field in this background, reducing
the Klein–Gordon equation to a Schrödinger-like form
and deriving the effective potential. In Sec. IV, we de-
velop a nontrivial extension of Leaver’s continued fraction
method, including both the full recurrence relation for
this background and a numerical implementation based
on automatic differentiation. Here, we also describe the
sixth-order WKB method that we employ for compari-
son. In Sec. V, we apply this framework to compute the
QNM spectrum and discuss the physical implications of
our findings. Finally, in Sec. VI, we conclude with a sum-
mary of our contributions.
Throughout this paper, we use geometric units, c =
8πG = 1, and the metric signature (−+ ++). Symbols
and their definitions are summarized in App. A.

C.
Automatic Differentiation for the Continued
Fraction
We can find the roots of the characteristic equation
defined in Eq. (71) using traditional root finders. Tradi-
tional solvers are efficient and reliable for small to inter-
mediate values of the BH charge Q and the fluid param-
eter K. However, as the parameters approach their ex-
tremal values, or when the electrostatic coupling, qQ, be-
tween the scalar field and the BH charges becomes large,
closely spaced roots may arise. Then, conventional root-
finding algorithms may fail to distinguish nearby roots or
converge to spurious solutions. Therefore, we reformulate
the problem as a minimization task to find the desired fre-
quencies and solve it using the limited-memory Broyden–
Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (L-BFGS) algorithm together
with automatic differentiation [67].
L-BFGS is a quasi-Newton optimization method. In
our case, it constructs an approximation to the inverse
Hessian matrix of the function under consideration using
information from previous frequency updates and gradi-
ent evaluations [68]. This approximation is then used to
determine an efficient update of the candidate frequency
associated with Eq. (71).
Instead of solving the characteristic equation in
Eq. (71) directly, we introduce the real, non-negative ob-
jective function [69],
J(ω) = |F(ω)|2 ≥0 ,
(73)
where F(ω) is defined in Eq. (71). The minima of the ob-

ima of this objective function is mathematically equiv-
alent to solving the characteristic equation in Eq. (71).
This reformulation converts the root-finding problem into
a nonlinear problem for a real, non-negative function, and
we can employ gradient-based optimization methods.

real space (ωR, ωI) spanned by the real and imaginary
parts of the frequency. To minimize the objective func-

Wolfe line search implemented in PyTorch [70]. The op-
timizer iteratively updates the frequencies using gradient

We compute the QNM spectra using the methods de-

we analyze their characteristic patterns as we scan the
parameter space consisting of the BH charge Q and fluid

electrostatic potential is below (positive branch) or above
(negative branch) a critical value. That is, for large elec-
trostatic interactions, the scalar perturbation ceases to
oscillate and decays monotonically in time. This behav-
ior is analogous to that of a damped harmonic oscillator
that transitions from the underdamped region in param-
eter space to an overdamped region.
In our problem,
the electrostatic potential appears to play the role of the
damping parameter yielding modes with zero oscillation
frequency above a critical value. We verify these “zero-
frequency” modes numerically using N = 2, 000 terms in
the continued fraction method 6.
As we switch on the fluid parameter, both branches
in the spectrum continue to exist and, in contrast to
K = 0, the oscillation frequencies are non zero for all val-
ues of the electrostatic interaction; see the left panel of
Fig. 8. Their magnitudes increase approximately linearly
with that of the electrostatic interaction. This increase
is largest for small fluid parameters, and it is suppressed
as the fluid parameter grows.
Furthermore, we observe a distinctive spectral feature:
The two branches 7 cross from positive to negative oscil-
lation frequencies below a critical value of the interaction.

Next, we analyze the imaginary part of the QNM fre-

Frequency Spectrum
Here we examine representative examples of the QNM
frequency spectrum of the monopole, including the fre-

Representative results are shown in the contour plots in

for charged scalars with interaction strength qQ = 1 in

contour maps in Fig. 9 display log10 |F(ω)|. The dark
purple regions indicate the smallest residuals, where the

In the top panels of Fig. 9, in which we set qQ = 0,
the spectra are symmetric with respect to the imaginary
axis (i.e., about Re(ω) = 0). This behavior is expected
when the interaction between BH and scalar field charges
is zero. Next, we compare the spectrum obtained around
a BH surrounded by an anisotropic fluid with K = 0.2
(top right panel) with that of a RN black hole (K = 0,
top left panel). While the spectrum remains symmetric
about the imaginary axis, we see that the fluid reduces
both the oscillation frequency and the damping rate of
all overtones. This is a consequence of the decrease of the
potential barrier in the presence of the fluid; see Fig. 4
and discussion in the following section V D.
In the bottom panels of Fig. 9 we show the QNM spec-
trum for non zero interaction between the BH and scalar
field charges, qQ ̸= 0. Due to this non zero interaction,
the spectrum becomes asymmetric, i.e., the oscillation
frequencies are no longer symmetric about the imaginary
axis and are shifted towards positive real part of the fre-
quency. We also see that the damping rates, indicated
by the imaginary part of the frequency, are spaced dif-
ferently for the two branches.
Comparing the bottom left and bottom right panels
of Fig. 9 reveals that increasing the fluid parameter K
changes the spectral structure.
In particular, the two
monotonic branches deform into a “W”-shaped pattern
visible in the bottom right panel. This pattern may be
interpreted either as a migration of the original branches
or as the appearance of a new branch of modes. The left-
most branch appears to move closer to the real axis. As
a result, one of its modes may become the fundamental
mode. We see a similar pattern in the branches of the
l = 1 and l = 4 modes, and for all values of the fluid
parameter K that we considered.
Finally, we find no QNMs with a positive imaginary
part in the parameter range explored here. This provides
evidence that nonextremal charged BHs surrounded by
the anisotropic fluid remain (mode) stable under mass-
less, charged scalar field perturbations.

VI.
CONCLUSION
Astrophysical BHs are not isolated objects.
They
are surrounded by complex environments that can leave
observable imprints on their dynamics.
In this work,
we have modeled environmental effects through an
anisotropic fluid described within Kiselev’s framework,
and we have analyzed the BH’s response to perturba-
tions by computing the QNM spectra of a charged scalar
field. In particular, we have considered a two-component
Kiselev model (cf. Eq. (10)) comprising a charged BH
surrounded by an anisotropic fluid that may represent
dark matter or a cosmic string network.
We have analyzed the horizon structure of the two com-
ponent Kiselev metric, and showed that it always con-
tains a Cauchy and an event horizon in the physically
allowed region of the parameter space.
To investigate the mode stability of the spacetime and
its response to perturbations, we have computed the
QNM spectra of a massless, charged scalar field prop-
agating on the two component Kiselev metric.
Therefore, we have developed a nontrivial extension
of Leaver’s continued fraction method that accounts for
the anisotropic fluid around the BH. This method re-
sults in a characteristic equation whose roots are the
complex QNM frequencies that we are seeking. In ad-
dition to standard root finders, we have implemented a
new algorithm using automatic differentiation in the Py-
Torch framework. This new algorithm has proven cru-
cial in challenging regions of parameter space, such as
near-extremal spacetimes, where conventional root find-
ers failed. To our knowledge, this is the first application
of automatic differentiation techniques to BH perturba-
tion theory.
We have corroborated our results using a sixth-order
WKB approximation extended to consistently account
for a frequency-dependent WKB effective potential. We
find that the WKB approximation captures the qualita-
tive behavior of the QNM spectrum.
We have analyzed how the scalar’s QNM spectra de-
pend on the BH charge, the fluid parameter and the in-
teraction between the BH’s and the scalar’s charges. In
summary, we find the following results:
- For all parameters in the physically allowed range that
we have considered, we found no evidence of QNM fre-
quencies with a positive imaginary part. That is, we
only found decaying QNMs which strongly indicates
mode stability of the two component Kiselev metric un-
der massless, charged scalar perturbations.
- For an uncharged scalar field, increasing the BH charge
increases the oscillation frequency and decreases the
damping rate of the scalar’s fundamental modes. In-
creasing the fluid parameter decreases both the oscilla-
tion frequency and the damping rate. The surrounding
fluid therefore yields longer lived scalar modes.
- For a charged scalar field at fixed electrostatic inter-
action strength, increasing the BH charge increases the
oscillation frequency. This effect becomes weaker as the
fluid parameter increases. The surrounding fluid there-
fore suppresses the influence of the electromagnetic in-
teraction on the QNM spectrum.
- For a charged scalar field at fixed BH charge, the elec-
trostatic interaction changes both the oscillation fre-
quency and the damping rate. For nonzero fluid param-
eters, the magnitude of the oscillation frequency grows
as the magnitude of the interaction is increased, while
this growth is weakened for larger values of the fluid
parameter.
The damping is weakest near vanishing
interaction. Increasing the magnitude of the interac-
tion increases the damping over the interaction range
considered.
For certain values of the interaction we
also observe avoided crossings, where the two frequency
branches approach each other but do not intersect.
- For both charged and uncharged scalar fields, increas-
ing their angular multitpole number increases the os-
cillation frequency and slightly decreases the damping
rate. These trends persist as the BH charge and the
fluid parameter are varied.
- We have also analyzed the full QNM spectrum includ-
ing overtones. For uncharged scalars, the spectrum is
symmetric about the imaginary part of the frequency.
This symmetry is broken when the electromagnetic in-
teraction is nonzero.
Increasing the fluid parameter
qualitatively reorganizes the spectrum. Some spectral
branches deform into a “W”-shaped structure. We have
found that the ordering of the modes can change as
the parameters vary. In particular, certain overtones
can exhibit a smaller magnitude of decay rate than the
original fundamental mode.
Taken together, these results support mode stability in
the parameter range studied and show that the surround-
ing fluid tends to suppress both the oscillation and the
decay rate of the scalar modes, to reduce the electromag-
netic imprint on the frequencies, and to reorganize the
spectral branch structure.

Appendix G: Numerical Results and WKB
Comparison
In this section, we present the numerical results for the
QNM frequencies obtained with Leaver’s continued frac-
tion method and the sixth-order WKB approximation.
In Tables IV and VI, we list the results of the scalar’s
l = 1 mode, computed only with Leaver’s method. In
Tables V and VII and Figs. 13 and 14, we present the
results of the l = 4 mode obtained with both Leaver’s
and the WKB methods. We discuss the results for the


## Page 29


29
uncharged and charged scalar fields separately in the two
subsections below.
For vanishing fluid parameter, K = 0, the WKB ap-
proximation shows excellent quantitative agreement with
Leaver’s method for the full range of BH charges Q. As
the fluid parameter increases, K > 0, we find visible
deviations of the WKB results from those of Leaver’s
method. That is, the sixth-order WKB approximation
reproduces only the qualitative dependence of the QNM
frequency spectrum on the BH charge Q. Similar method
dependent, quantitative differences have been reported in
QNM calculations of BHs surrounded by matter, where
WKB results are commonly checked against Prony, time-
domain, or Leaver-type computations [101, 102].
It has been shown that for frequency-independent
WKB potentials, Padé resummations of the sixth-order
and higher WKB approximations agree very well with ac-
curate numerical QNM frequencies for Schwarzschild and
RN BHs [103].
For Schwarzschild BHs, the numerical
benchmark frequencies were taken from Ref. [104] which
used a modified Newman-Thorson method.
Ref. [103]
found that for low-lying modes the Padé-improved WKB
results are comparable to, and in some cases better than,
phase-integral results used as an independent benchmark.
This suggests that a Padé improved WKB treatment
may help reduce the discrepancy with Leaver’s method
that we find. However, in our setup the WKB effective
potential depends explicitly on the frequency, so the stan-
dard Padé construction does not apply directly. Adapt-
ing Padé improved WKB methods to the frequency-
dependent case would be an interesting direction to ex-
plore in future work.
1.
Uncharged Scalar Field
For an uncharged scalar field, q = 0, the sixth-order
WKB approximation reproduces the qualitative increase
of the real part of the frequency with the BH charge Q, in
agreement with Leaver’s method. Fig. 13 shows excellent
agreement between the two methods when the fluid pa-
rameter vanishes (yellow dashed line). For example, for
the l = 4 fundamental frequency of an uncharged scalar
scattering off a BH with charge Q = 0.489898 we find a
relative error of ≲9×10−7 in the real part and ≲5×10−6
in the imaginary part of the frequency; see Table V.
For nonzero fluid parameter, K > 0, visible devia-
tions emerge and increase with increasing fluid param-
eter, reaching a relative error ∼O(10−1) in the extremal
limit, K →1. This behavior reflects the inherent differ-
ences between the two techniques.
This trend is consistent with the sensitivity analysis of
the sixth-order WKB formula, Eq. (82); see App. D for
details. The analysis shows that the WKB spectrum is
most sensitive to the fluid parameter K and the angular
index l, while its sensitivity to the BH charge Q and the
field charge q is weaker.
2.
Charged Scalar Field
Here we consider a charged scalar field and focus on
the case where the interaction between charges is qQ = 1.
As shown in Table VII and Fig. 14, the sixth-order WKB
approximation continues to reproduce the qualitative de-
pendence of the spectrum on the BH charge Q, including
the monotonic increase of the real part of the frequency
along the fixed charge interaction qQ family.
When there is no fluid surrounding the BH, we find
excellent agreement with Leaver’s method over the full
range of the BH charge parameters; the relative errors
are of order O(10−7) in the real and O(10−6) in the imag-
inary parts of the frequencies.
When we include the surrounding fluid, the differences
between the two methods increase systematically with
increasing fluid parameter K. The relative errors in the
frequencies grow to O(10−1). This behavior follows the
same pattern as in the uncharged case and is consistent
with the sensitivity analysis.
