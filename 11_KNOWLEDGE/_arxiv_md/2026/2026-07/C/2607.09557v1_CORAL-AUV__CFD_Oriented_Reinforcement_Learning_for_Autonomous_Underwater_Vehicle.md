Abstract: Fine grain control and positioning of autonomous underwater vehicles
(AUVs) is critical for sampling, maintenance, and survey applications. Tradi-
tional control methods for AUVs are labor intensive and are not robust to changes
in the vehicle configuration or environmental conditions. Reinforcement learning
(RL) promises rapid controller development while handling a range of deploy-
ment parameters via domain randomization (DR). However, DR is still limited
by the capacity of the underlying simulation to model real physics. In particular,
drag physics are difficult to model and are a large contributor to sim-to-real gaps.
Meanwhile, computational fluid dynamics (CFD) provides high fidelity drag mod-
els but is challenging to leverage within reinforcement learning frameworks due
to its computational overhead. Thus, in this paper we exploit the idea of training
surrogate approximations of CFD models of a given vehicle, enabling fast infer-
ence within RL pipelines. We are the first to successfully deploy a zero-shot RL
policy on a 6-DOF AUV in which policy training is performed on surrogate drag
models (SDMs) trained on CFD data. We find 31% lower energy usage compared
to a controller using simplified physics while traversing between waypoints 11%
faster with 19% less error. Our SDM based RL controller better predicts zero-shot
transfer and is more robust across reward shaping design choices. When using
DR to complete a task with perturbed parameters, we find that the CFD policy
is the only controller that successfully transfers. The policies are evaluated in
a controlled tank environment and in the field providing extensive testing of the
policies’ capabilities.
Keywords: Sim-to-real transfer, Reinforcement learning for physical robot con-
trol, CFD, AUVs

Introduction
Autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) have been used for a variety of underwater environmen-
tal research interests, from the study of plankton [1], tracking of mobile animal species [2, 3], and
surveys of coral reefs [4]. These tasks often require robots to navigate challenging terrain and con-
ditions [5, 6]; for example, a vehicle may need to operate within sub-meter ranges of a fragile
coral species to obtain high-resolution visual data [4, 7], or it may need to use multiple sensors to
avoid obstacles [8]. Maintaining close proximity to obstacles and reacting quickly to marine life
requires fine-tuned control of the dynamics of the vehicles. A mission may require multiple sensor
configurations, making controller development extremely time-intensive and challenging due to the
changes in the hydrodynamics. For example, Hawkes et al. curtailed one of their AUV experiments
prematurely when they added a stern-facing camera to their AUV, causing unintended impacts to
their hydrodynamics and thus controller failures [9]. Traditional controllers such as PID may re-
quire manual re-tuning, fail to generalize to new tasks, or degrade under changing environmental

conditions. Recent works have turned to reinforcement learning (RL) based controllers to address
these limitations. Two common approaches to dynamics modeling involve: 1) using high-fidelity
computational fluid dynamics (CFD) solvers directly in the RL training loop to obtain an accurate
controller [10, 11, 12, 13, 14], or 2) using analytical low-fidelity models to obtain a controller within
minutes [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. For a 6-DOF vehicle, exhaustive CFD sampling quickly becomes
computationally prohibitive. If each component of the linear and angular velocity is sampled n
times over an expected operating range, the resulting CFD dataset scales as O(n6). Each compu-
tation could take dozens of seconds or even minutes for to solve a transient flow case. While the
CFD approach produces an accurate controller in a zero-shot manner, it is challenging to rapidly
adapt to new vehicle parameters or update the controller design without expensive re-training. In
this work, we effectively distill complex CFD models with neural network approximations for fast
inference. We refer to these approximations as surrogate drag models (SDMs), which can be incor-
porated into RL frameworks to rapidly create new controllers while benefiting from CFD. We also
fit a drag model with System ID to enable investigation into a more general question: what does a
better dynamics model buy the RL model?
Our contributions are as follows:
1. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to successfully deploy a zero-shot RL policy
on a 6-DOF AUV in which policy training is performed using surrogate drag models trained
on CFD data. For our tasks, RL training is accomplished in 15 minutes or less.
2. Analysis on the interplay between reward shaping, domain randomization, and drag model
fidelity. Our work suggests that the CFD based controller is less sensitive to reward de-
sign choices and better predicts sim-to-real transfer. Using SDMs with RL successfully
produces a policy that is robust to an additional 2 lbs on the stern, while the other policies
failed to transfer successfully.
3. Extensive testing of sim-to-real transfer for each RL controller in both tank settings and
real-world settings. We demonstrate the learned controller on a scientifically rich coral reef
site: Yawzi Reef, located in St. John, USVI.

Related Works
2.1
Reinforcement Learning for AUV Control
Various control paradigms exist for AUVs: PID, model predictive control [21], lead-lag [22], and
sliding mode [22]. However, these methods are often time-intensive, must be re-tuned for a new
payload configuration, and can lead to sluggish behavior. Recent work has turned to reinforcement
learning to rapidly learn controllers and handle perturbations through domain randomization (DR).
On CUREE, an AUV capable of 6-DOF control, Cai et al. [15] deployed a zero-shot RL policy that
successfully station keeps while recovering from disturbances. Swim4Real [16] deployed a position
controller on the MOLA 6-DOF AUV, performing extensive in-water position and orientation ex-
periments and demonstrating a 39% reduction in energy usage compared to PID. Tuncay et al. [17]
evaluated three RL algorithms on a 6-DOF vehicle and compared to an MPC baseline, and Fosso et
al. [18] trained a BlueROV2 Heavy within 3 minutes and corrected steady-state errors in the con-
troller with an integral term. However, many of these rely on simplified hydrodynamics models that
may prevent controllers from learning higher performance or more energy efficient maneuvers.
2.2
Computational Fluid Dynamics with RL for AUV Control
Besides domain randomization, another strategy to close the sim-to-real gap is to leverage CFD
models. Zhu et al. [10] used the lattice Boltzmann method with a deep recurrent Q-network to
simulate a fish robot reaching goal poses in a flow field. Lidtke et al. [11] used Partially Averaged
Navier Stokes with the KSKL turbulence model to train an RL controller on a 2D version of the
BlueROV2 Heavy, which enabled vehicle control in simulations of various flow conditions. Cui et

DOF fish robot. However, none of these methods enable full 6-DOF control, nor enable domain
randomization with highly parallel training across AUV environments.
3
Method
Most prior works simplify the drag model of an AUV using simple shapes, such as MuJoCo’s equiv-
alent inertial box [24], to estimate the drag at each time step. We hypothesize that this results in
poor sim-to-real transfer in some cases, especially when a vehicle has coupling along various con-
trol dimensions. We seek to bridge this gap by experimenting with two models of higher fidelity
than the inertia box. In previous literature, many do not enable 6-DOF vehicle control, and perhaps
more importantly, incorporate a full CFD solver in the RL training loop, which can be prohibitively
computationally expensive, especially when modeling robots with higher degrees of freedom. To
gain the majority of the benefits of a CFD model while avoiding expensive computations, we fit
SDMs on steady-state CFD data of our vehicle. Steady-state flow CFD is cheaper than transient
flow CFD, and lightweight MLPs used as SDMs enable rapid RL policy learning in simulation. As
outlined in Figure 1, we modify Cai et al.’s [15] training pipeline to incorporate these new models.
We investigate how the following drag model assumptions affect sim-to-real transfer:
1. The equivalent inertial box drag model (diagonal drag model)
2. System ID on real vehicle data (diagonal drag model)
3. Surrogate drag models that approximate the steady state CFD data (coupled model)

3.1.3
Computational Fluid Dynamics
To generate steady-state CFD data, we use OpenFoam [25] with our vehicle’s CAD model (Figure
2). In contrast with the previous models, the data captures coupling between axes which further
closes the sim-to-real gap for RL training. We randomly sample a vehicle orientation and a linear
or angular velocity. We use RANS K-ω SST [26] as our turbulence model and solve for the steady-
state wrench with the PIMPLE algorithm. This produces two datasets where the features are a
linear velocity with an associated wrench or an angular velocity with an associated wrench. We
obtain ∼183,000 examples for linear velocities and ∼90,000 examples for angular velocities. We
fit an MLP on each dataset. During inference, the outputs of the MLPs are summed together to
produce the estimated wrench at each time step. The splitting of linear and angular velocities reduces
the computational complexity of generating the CFD dataset by orders of magnitude at the cost of
simplifying the steady-state dynamics. Each MLP has 1024 units in their three layers and uses MSE
loss, Adam, ReLU, and a 80/10/10 training split. We selected the best parameters based on the
validation set to mitigate overfitting. These lightweight surrogates enable an RL controller to be
developed in 15 minutes or less.

3.2
Domain Randomization
We seek policies that are robust to small parameter perturbations. Following the work of [27, 15],
we utilize domain randomization (DR) on the volume and COB-COM offset of the vehicle. COB
is the center of buoyancy and COM is the center of mass. For field experiments, we use a small
DR range of 0.01 m for the COB-COM offset and ±1% of the volume of the vehicle. With these
DR parameters, a random vector sampled from a sphere of radius 0.01 m is added to the default
COB-COM offset and the volume is set within 1% of 0.025977 m3. Motivated by the problem of
handling an unexpected scientific instrument placed on the vehicle, we calculate the expected shift
in these parameters when placing weights on the stern of the vehicle. To handle 1 lb in weight we
use 0.01 m for COB-COM offset DR and ±1% volume DR. For handling 2 lbs in weight we use
0.02 m for COB-COM offset DR and ±2% volume DR.
3.3
Training Approach and Reward Shaping
Each policy is trained in IsaacSim with 10, 000 environments learning in parallel. Each vehicle
spawns with a random pose within 5 m of a goal position. The vehicle is tasked with achieving
both the goal position and the goal orientation in 10 seconds. We train the policy with on-policy
implementation (PPO) [28]. At each time step, the vehicle receives an observation of its target
orientation, current orientation, body-frame linear velocity, body-frame angular velocity, and offset
from the goal position. We implement two reward shaping approaches.

3.3.2
General Task: Reward Shaping for General 3D Pose
The second reward shaping method is simpler but applied to the harder 3D pose task. We again trace
the same U-shaped pattern in Section 3.1.1, except the vehicle must achieve a particular 3D pose at
each corner. The segments are 1.7 m in length. The stability term, the yaw gating on the position
reward, and the additional position reward terms are all removed. Moreover, in training, we reward
attaining the full goal orientation. We feed orientations that solely vary yaw so the DVL maintains
bottom lock. The reward function is:
rtotal = λprp + λqrq + λvrv + λara
where rp = e−||p||2, rq = e−||q||2, rv = e−||v||2, ra = e−||a||2. A practical use of zero-shot methods
is obtaining a reasonable controller quickly in sim and fine-tuning it on hardware. We simulate
an engineer tuning a controller that handles an unexpectedly added scientific instrument during a
mission. The IsaacSim simulator is configured with the parameters in Table 1 (appendix). We
generate three sets of reward coefficients in an iterative fashion, generating the next set of coefficients
after testing the previous set in the tank. The first set attempts to produce a conservative controller.
The second set aspires to balance the behavior by rewarding pose attainment more than rewarding
lack of motion. Seeing success with the inertia box and CFD models, the third set encourages more
aggressive maneuvering. The best coefficient set for each drag model assumption is then used to
train the policy with domain randomization. We use the 2 lb DR range described in Section 3.2. The
weights are added, and we evaluate the best three discovered policies on the same U-shaped pattern
task.

Tank Results
A waypoint is considered completed when the vehicle simultaneously satisfies ||p|| ≤0.20 m and
||q|| ≤25◦. The first set of coefficients from the tuning experiment outlined in Section 3.3.2 pro-
duced controllers that often reached the correct positions but frequently failed to rotate to the correct
yaw. Occasionally, the controller seemed to prefer not to move until error accumulated. The second
set of coefficients led to promising controllers from the inertia box and CFD methods, but waypoint
completion time was high. The final set of coefficients aimed to reduce transit times by encouraging
more thrust. We find that the inertia box based controller was highly sensitive to the reward shap-
ing choices. The System ID based controller achieved mediocre performance across the three sets,
completing more waypoints than the inertia box method with coefficient sets 1 and 3 but less with
coefficient set 2. The CFD method is the most robust over the reward shaping choices. Comparing

5
Conclusion
We proposed a method for distilling CFD drag models into lightweight models to enable rapid train-
ing of RL controllers for AUVs. Compared to the other models studied, our results suggest that
our CFD based RL training pipeline more accurately predicts zero-shot transfer and is less sensi-
tive to reward shaping, while not introducing prohibitive computational cost into the RL training
loop. Future work includes building on this pipeline to learn agile behavior, developing new au-
tonomous scientific data collection capabilities, or further investigating the sim-to-real mismatch
that this pipeline fails to capture. Studying the effectiveness of training these lightweight models on
transient CFD results is another future topic of investigation.
6
Limitation
The System ID model has similar performance to the inertia box model in the field. Both are diagonal
models for a roughly rectangular vehicle, so observing significant differentiation with the System
ID approach is challenging. This is even harder to observe using a feedback controller in a damped
environment. Beyond this, to estimate the System ID coefficients, we manually drove the vehicle
to an initial velocity, cut the thrusters, and fit the drag coefficients on the resulting coast-down
data. A limitation of this approach is that the coefficients are fit on data that may not contain the
velocity magnitudes experienced during a mission when thrusters are active. We hypothesize that
this, combined with the diagonal drag assumption, leads to similar performance in the field.

A.2
Tank Results, Cont.
Figure A.1 displays the metrics for each drag model obtained on the U-shaped task in the tank. As
described in Section 3.3.2, reward shaping started Conservative and was iterated to the Balanced
and Aggressive policies based on zero-shot deployment performance. The three sets of reward
coefficients are:
Cconservative = {λp = 0.20, λq = 0.50, λv = 0.05, λa = 0.20}
Cbalanced = {λp = 0.40, λq = 0.90, λv = 0.00, λa = 0.20}
Caggressive = {λp = 0.20, λq = 1.0, λv = 0.00, λa = 0.08}
Reward shaping can lead to satisfactory policies for a lower-fidelity drag model, as seen with the
inertia box with the Balanced Policy configuration. A higher-fidelity drag model leads to more
consistent transfer over the three choices; the System ID model performs mediocre across all pol-
icy choices, and the SDM based model performs the best overall. We hypothesize that the cross-
coupling embedded in the CFD dataset (Figure A.2) is largely responsible for this gain by lowering
the sim-to-real gap.
Figures A.3-5 show the reward curves achieved in IsaacSim when using domain randomization to
handle a 1 lb and 2 lb weight. In the CFD and System ID cases, the controller successfully learns
policies that achieve a similar reward with and without domain randomization. The inertia box
model fails to learn behavior that achieves similar reward levels. Because the System ID and inertia
box drag models have the same diagonal structure, this suggests that learning is highly sensitive to
the drag coefficient values and ratios. Future work may investigate this relationship more fully.
Figures A.3 and A.5 may lead one to expect similar results to those in Figure 5 when the vehicle
is loaded with 2 lbs on the stern (Figure A.6). However, we find that the CFD based policy is the
only policy to transfer (Figure 6). This provides evidence that the use of SDMs in the RL training
pipeline leads to more accurate predictions of zero-shot transfer.
