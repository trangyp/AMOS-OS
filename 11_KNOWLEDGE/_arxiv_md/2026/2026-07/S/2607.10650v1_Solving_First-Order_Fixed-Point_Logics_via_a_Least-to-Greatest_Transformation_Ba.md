Solving First-Order Fixed-Point Logics via a Least-to-Greatest
Transformation Based on Game Semantics
SATOSHI KURA, Waseda University, Japan
HIROSHI UNNO, Tohoku University, Japan
Fixed-point logics provide an expressive intermediate framework for reasoning about temporal properties
of programs. One of the key approaches to solving their validity checking problem is via transformations
from least fixed points to greatest fixed points (𝜇-to-𝜈transformations), which generalizes a reduction from
termination verification to safety verification studied in binary reachability analysis. In this paper, we introduce
game-semantic interpretations of 𝜇-to-𝜈transformations. We first introduce a new 𝜇-to-𝜈transformation
based on parity relations. We show that solving 𝜇-to-𝜈-transformed fixed-point equation systems corresponds
to finding winning strategies in the game semantics of the original fixed-point equation systems. We apply the
same game-semantic framework to interpret two existing 𝜇-to-𝜈transformations, one by Kobayashi et al. and
the other by Unno et al, and show that they admit analogous game-semantic interpretations. Furthermore, we
show that the game introduced by Tsukada et al. corresponds to an alternative characterization of the winning
condition. On the implementation side, we propose optimization techniques for efficiently solving our new
𝜇-to-𝜈transformation. We implement these techniques in a fixed-point logic solver, compare our approach
with existing solvers, and demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed optimizations through experiments.
Additional Key Words and Phrases: Fixed-point logics, Parity games, Game semantics

Introduction
Fixed-point logics have been widely studied as a foundation for reasoning about temporal properties
of programs. While much of the early work focused on finite-state systems, such as those captured by
the modal 𝜇-calculus, recent research has addressed verification of infinite-state programs [Beyene
et al. 2013; Bjørner et al. 2015; Cathcart Burn et al. 2018; De Angelis et al. 2022; Kobayashi et al. 2019,
2018; Nanjo et al. 2018; Unno et al. 2023, 2013, 2021]. For example, properties such as the weakest
precondition and weakest liberal precondition of a loop can be characterized as the least and greatest
fixed points, respectively, and are thus naturally expressible within a fixed-point logic [Blass and
Gurevich 1987]. Numerous program verification methods have been developed through reductions
to solving constrained Horn clauses (CHCs) [Beyene et al. 2013; Bjørner et al. 2015; Cathcart Burn
et al. 2018; De Angelis et al. 2022; Unno et al. 2013], which can be seen as a fragment of a first-order
fixed-point logic without 𝜇and ∃. Allowing alternation of least and greatest fixed points further
increases the expressiveness of fixed-point logics. For instance, model checking of infinite-state
while-programs against modal 𝜇-calculus specifications can be reduced to the validity checking
problem for a first-order fixed-point logic with fixed-point alternation [Kobayashi et al. 2019].
Other applications of fixed-point logics include game solving and reactive synthesis [Heim and
Dimitrova 2024, 2025a,b; Maderbacher and Bloem 2022; Samuel et al. 2021; Schmuck et al. 2024];
implementability of global protocols [Li et al. 2025a,b]; and semantics-guided synthesis [Murphy
et al. 2025]. These various applications highlight the role of fixed-point logics as a powerful
intermediate formalism for program verification and synthesis. Thus, efficient solvers for fixed-
point logics are a key component towards practical verification and synthesis tools.
The validity checking problem of fixed-point logics can be solved by applying transformations
from least fixed points to greatest fixed points [Kobayashi et al. 2019; Unno et al. 2023]. We call such
transformations least-to-greatest fixed-point transformations or simply 𝜇-to-𝜈transformations. The
idea of such 𝜇-to-𝜈transformations was inspired by, and generalizes, the reduction of termination

Our 𝜇-to-𝜈transformation can be naturally interpreted in terms of game semantics of fixed-point
logics [Baldan et al. 2019, 2020; Bradfield and Walukiewicz 2018], which interprets a fixed-point
equation system as a parity game such that the validity of the equation system corresponds to the
existence of a winning strategy. Similarly to the example above, our 𝜇-to-𝜈transformation inserts
parity relations into a given fixed-point equation system, which ensures that the parity condition is
satisfied for any infinite play in the corresponding parity game. Therefore, if there exists a solution
for the constraint solving problem obtained by our transformation, then there exists a memoryless
winning strategy in the parity game.
Game semantics also provides a unified perspective on the 𝜇-to-𝜈transformations of [Kobayashi
et al. 2019; Unno et al. 2023] and the game introduced in [Tsukada et al. 2025]. Specifically, these
works can be uniformly interpreted as equipping the original parity game with auxiliary partial
information about the history and then rephrasing the winning condition in terms of the auxiliary
information. By characterizing the winning condition in terms of well-founded relations, we obtain
a game-semantic interpretation of [Unno et al. 2023]. Solving the 𝜇-to-𝜈-transformed equation
system of [Unno et al. 2023] corresponds to constructing a winning strategy in the augmented parity
game. Similarly, the game introduced in [Tsukada et al. 2025] can be viewed as a reformulation of
the winning condition in terms of disjunctively well-founded relations. Likewise, the transformation
in [Kobayashi et al. 2019] can be understood in terms of a game augmented with counters that
bound the number of unfoldings of least fixed points. This game-semantic analysis also reveals
that the original game of [Tsukada et al. 2025] imposes an unnecessary restriction on the winning
condition.
We implement our new 𝜇-to-𝜈transformation on top of the existing solver MuVal [Unno et al.
2023] for first-order fixed-point logics. Our implementation generates constraints over predicate
variables that involve (unknown) parity relations. To synthesize parity relations, we provide
templates for parity relations and implement them in the existing constraint solver PCSat [Unno
et al. 2021]. We further develop several optimization techniques based on dependency analysis,
which reduce the size of the generated constraints. We compared our implementation with the
existing solver MuVal [Unno et al. 2023] and MuStrat [Tsukada et al. 2025] on benchmarks from
the literature. We also evaluated the effectiveness of our optimization techniques.

• We present a novel and simple 𝜇-to-𝜈transformation for first-order fixed-point equation

complete reduction of the validity checking problem for first-order fixed-point logics to a
constraint solving problem.
• We discuss how the existing methods [Kobayashi et al. 2019; Tsukada et al. 2025; Unno
et al. 2023] can be understood from a uniform game-semantic perspective. In particular,
we interpret the transformation proposed in [Unno et al. 2023] in terms of well-founded
relations, and the game introduced in [Tsukada et al. 2025] in terms of disjunctively well-
founded relations. We also show that the transformation proposed in [Kobayashi et al. 2019]
admits a game-semantic interpretation using counters. This game-theoretic perspective
provides a unified view of these methods and our own.

Related Work
Fixed-point logics and parity games. It is well-known that the model-checking problem for modal
𝜇-calculus can be reduced to solving parity games (see, e.g., [Bradfield and Walukiewicz 2018]),
which can be done in quasi-polynomial time [Calude et al. 2017]. Various extensions of this result
have been studied. One direction is to consider fixed-point logics with more general truth values.
The reduction to parity games is extended for continuous complete lattices [Baldan et al. 2019] and
for complete lattices [Baldan et al. 2020]. Another direction is to consider more general transition
systems based on coalgebraic frameworks [Hasuo et al. 2016; Hausmann et al. 2024; Hausmann
and Schröder 2019].
Transformations of first-order fixed-point logics. Transforming fixed-point equation systems is
a common technique for solving them [Kobayashi et al. 2020, 2019; Neele and Van De Pol 2024;
Unno et al. 2023]. Among them, our focus is on 𝜇-to-𝜈transformations. In [Kobayashi et al. 2019],
a 𝜇-equation 𝑋( ˜𝑥) =𝜇𝜙is transformed to a 𝜈-equation 𝑋′(𝑦, ˜𝑥) =𝜈𝑦> 0 ∧𝜙[𝑋′(𝑦−1, −)/𝑋] by
adding an extra argument 𝑦that upper-bounds the number of unfoldings of 𝑋. Then, it follows
that the solution 𝑋′∗for 𝑋′ satisfies 𝑋′∗(𝑦, ˜𝑥) = J𝜙K𝑦(⊥). Thus, for any term 𝑡, 𝑋′∗(𝑡, ˜𝑥) is a lower
approximation of 𝑋∗( ˜𝑥). This idea was later generalized to higher-order setting in [Kobayashi
et al. 2023]. Another transformation is proposed in [Unno et al. 2023], which we have discussed
in Section 4. There are other kinds of transformations. Fold/unfold transformations are studied
in [Kobayashi et al. 2020], and swapping and substitution that preserve the solution are studied
in [Neele and Van De Pol 2024]. These transformations themselves do not directly solve fixed-point
equations, but they can be used to simplify the equations before solving them.

Conclusions and Future Work
We have presented a novel 𝜇-to-𝜈transformation for 𝜇CLPs based on parity relations. We proved
that the transformation is sound and complete and established its game-semantic interpretation.
We also showed that the existing 𝜇-to-𝜈transformations [Kobayashi et al. 2019; Unno et al. 2023]
and the game introduced in [Tsukada et al. 2025] can be understood from a unified game-semantic
perspective. Finally, we implemented our transformation together with optimization techniques,
including SCC-wise parity relations and priority reassignment, and demonstrated their effectiveness
through experiments on benchmark suites. As future work, we plan to develop advanced methods
for exchanging information between primal and dual 𝜇CLP solving.
